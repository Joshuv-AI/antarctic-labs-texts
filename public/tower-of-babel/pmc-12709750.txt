Impact of multimorbidity on health-related quality of life: the mediation role of intrinsic capacity – evidence from the WHO ICOPE pilot program in Lianyungang of China (2024)
Authors: Yiying Yang, Jiacheng Dong, Peiyuan Qin, Yu Guo, Hengchao Ge, Degui Li, Zhen Wu, Lulu Xiao, Linlin Hu, Yan Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth-related Quality of life (HRQoL) is associated with multimorbidity, but the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study aimed to explore the association between multimorbidity and HRQoL, focusing on intrinsic capacity (IC) as a mediator.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted under the World Health Organization (WHO) Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) pilot program in Lianyungang, China, between April and July 2024. Multimorbidity was classified by clinicians’ experience, Charlson Comorbidity Index, and Cumulative Illness Rating Scale for Geriatrics. HRQoL was assessed using EQ-5D-3L. IC was assessed based on the WHO’s guidelines for ICOPE. Logistic regression was used to calculate the correlation between multimorbidity and HRQoL. Mediation model was further constructed to assess the mediating role of IC.ResultsThis study recruited 468 adults aged ≥ 60 years (207 males [44.2%]). In logistic regression, the risk of reduced HRQoL increased by 22% per additional condition (adjusted for gender, age, IC, marital status, education, nursing home resident status). Specifically, compared to Q1 (multimorbidity number = 0), the risk of poor HRQoL increased by 148% (95% CI: 1.39–4.43, p = 0.002) in Q2 (number = 1), 172% (95% CI: 1.48–5.01, p = 0.001) in Q3 (number = 2–3), and 194% (95% CI: 1.57–5.50, p < 0.001) in Q4 (number ≥ 4), respectively. Additionally, mediation analysis showed that IC mediated 25.90% of the multimorbidity-HRQoL association (p < 0.001).ConclusionOur findings suggest that multimorbidity was a risk factor for poorer HRQoL. IC was a useful indicator for identifying HRQoL and mediated the multimorbidity-HRQoL association.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01791-1.Keywords: Intrinsic capacity, Multimorbidity, Heath-related quality of life, Health aging, Mediation analysis

Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines quality of life (QoL) as individuals’ perception of their position in life in the context of the culture and value systems in which they live and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards and concerns [1]. Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) narrows this broad construct by focusing specifically on the utilities associated with health [2]. Global aging is intensifying, by 2030, the population aged ≥ 65 years is projected to surpass 1 billion [3]. This demographic shift poses profound challenges to healthcare systems worldwide, making healthy aging a critical priority. As a key criterion for assessing the level of healthy aging, HRQoL has garnered growing attention [4].

Reduced QoL is a major consequence of multimorbidity [5], commonly defined as the coexistence of two or more diseases in an individual [6]. In China, multimorbidity is highly prevalent among older adults, with rates ranging from 44% to 76% [7]. Individuals with multimorbidity often face restricted social and physical activities due to disease burden, frequently leading to sedentary behavior. This increases their risk of physical functional decline, depression, and ultimately reduced HRQoL [8, 9]. Thus, investigating the factors and pathways linking multimorbidity to HRQoL is critical for shaping health policies and improving elderly health outcomes. The relationship may be mediated by multiple factors, including activities of daily living (ADL), instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), loneliness, and depressive symptoms. Notably, many of these established mediators map directly onto the domains of intrinsic capacity as defined by the WHO ICOPE framework [10].

Multimorbidity also adversely affects intrinsic capacity (IC) [11], a concept introduced by WHO in its 2015 World Report on Ageing and Health to describe an individual’s biological capabilities, distinct from physical impairments [12]. IC, defined as the composite of physical and mental capacities, comprises six key dimensions—visual, hearing, nutrition, cognition, mobility, and depression—and is a robust predictor of health outcomes [13]. Multimorbidity accelerates the decline of IC, which in turn is associated with poorer HRQoL among older adults [14]. Moreover, each dimension of IC differentially impacts HRQoL [15].

Therefore, we hypothesize that IC mediates the relationship between multimorbidity and HRQoL in older adults. Including it as a mediator will help clarify the direct and indirect effects of multimorbidity on HRQoL.

However, relevant research remains scarce in current studies. To examine this mediating relationship, this study administered questionnaire surveys to 509 older adults as part of the WHO Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) pilot program implemented in Lianyungang, China. Statistical analysis was performed to investigate the association between multimorbidity and HRQoL, as well as the mediating role of IC.

Methods

Data source and study design

This cross-sectional study utilized data from the WHO ICOPE pilot project in Lianyungang, China (n = 509). The research data came from the WHO’s integrated care pilot project in Lianyungang, China, between April and July 2024. The study population was older adults from Lianyungang communities and nursing homes, who were identified by the Civil Affairs Bureau and street offices using a multistage sampling method. This process resulted in a response rate of 25.45%. Inclusion criteria were: age ≥ 60 years, clear consciousness and ability to cooperate in completing all assessment items, and voluntary provision of informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: age below 60 years, presence of severe mental disorders or critical illnesses, and individuals with missing key variables. Missing data were handled by listwise deletion, yielding 468 analyzed participants (Fig. 1). The study complied with the STROBE guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by the Ethics Committee of The first people’s Hospital of Lianyungang (QT-20221118001-02). Written informed consent was obtained, and data were anonymized prior to analysis.

Outcomes: HRQoL

The outcome variable, HRQoL, was measured using the EQ-5D-3L scale developed by the EuroQol Group. This scale assesses HRQoL across five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. It has demonstrated good reliability and validity across various populations [16]. Based on EQ-5D-3L scores and guided by previous literature, statistical balance, and clinical experience, participants were divided into two groups. Those with a score of 0 were classified as the low EQ-5D-3L level group, indicating a higher HRQoL. Those with a score of 1–3 were classified as the high EQ-5D-3L level group, indicating a lower HRQoL.

Exposure: multimorbidity

The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) is widely used to measure multimorbidity due to its strong applicability [17]. The Cumulative Illness Rating Scale for Geriatrics (CIRS-G) is a standardized assessment tool designed to quantify health status and disease burden in older adults [18]. In this study, multimorbidity was measured based on integrating clinician expertise, CCI, and CIRS-G (Supplementary table 2). To optimize statistical power and prevent spurious results from small subgroups, Multimorbidity was assigned as: multimorbidity number = 0 (1), multimorbidity number = 1 (2), multimorbidity number = 2 or 3 (3), multimorbidity number ≥ 4 (4).

Measurement of IC

WHO released the ICOPE guidelines (2017) and the ICOPE handbook (2019) to support IC assessment [13]. This study evaluated older adults’ IC using the ICOPE framework across six domains: cognition, mobility, nutrition, vision, hearing, and depression [19]. As per WHO recommendations, the assessment occurs in two steps: an initial screening (using brief self-reports and basic objective tests) followed, if abnormalities are detected, by a detailed assessment with standardized scales [20].

The study employed the following detailed assessment tools. Cognitive function was evaluated using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), a widely used and validated screening tool [21]. Scores were assigned as normal (0) or abnormal [1] based on education level. Mobility is assessed via the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB). A score of 9–12 indicates normal mobility (assigned as 0), while a score of 0–8 indicates impairment (assigned as 1) [22]. Nutritional status is measured using the Mini Nutritional Assessment-Short Form (MNA-SF), with scores ≥ 11 classified as normal (assigned as 0) and ≤ 10 as malnutrition (assigned as 1) [23]. Vision, hearing, and depression were all assessed based on clinical expert consensus: the vision assessment items include “difficulty reading, walking, or watching TV”, “feeling obstructed or having blind spots when looking at objects” and “seeing objects as distorted or warped”, each item response “yes” is scored 1 point, a total score of ≤ 1 indicates “good vision” (assigned as 1), 2 points indicates “poor vision” (assigned as 2), and 3 points indicates “very poor vision” (assigned as 3); hearing assessment items include “others often complain that you turn up the volume of the TV or radio too loud”, “frequently needing others to repeat what they said”, and “finding it difficult to hear on the phone or mobile”, a total score of ≤ 1 indicates “good hearing” (assigned as 1), 2 points indicates “poor hearing” (assigned as 2), and 3 points indicates “very poor hearing” (assigned as 3); depression was assessed using the question: “In the past two weeks, have you been bothered by any of the following problems?” The response options included “feeling down”, “depressed”, or “hopeless” and “little interest or pleasure in doing things”, if at least one response was “yes”, the participant was classified as having depression (coded as 1), otherwise, they were classified as not having depression (coded as 0).

The total IC score was calculated based on the above dimensions, with higher scores indicating more severe impairment. Guided by previous literature, statistical balance and clinical experience, the IC score was categorized as follows: <2 (good, assigned 3), 2–3 (normal, assigned 2), and > 3 (low, assigned 1).

Covariates

The covariates were determined based on previous studies [24], including sociodemographic characteristics such as age, gender, marital status, education, and nursing home resident status. The coding schemes were as follows: gender (1 = male, 2 = female), age (1 = 60–79 years, 2 ≥ 80 years), marital status (0 = never married/windowed/divorced, 1 = married/living with partner), education (1 = no schooling, 2 = primary school, 3 = middle school, 4 = high school or above), and nursing home resident status (1 = no, 2 = yes).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed with the statistical software packages R (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) and Free Statistics software versions 2.1. For baseline data analysis, multimorbidity was categorized into three groups: low (multimorbidity number = 0), medium (multimorbidity number = 1), and high (multimorbidity number ≥ 2). Chi-square tests were employed to compare differences in baseline characteristics among older adults across different multimorbidity groups. To examine the relationship between multimorbidity and HRQoL, both univariate and multivariate regression analyses were performed. Four logistic regression models were constructed: non-adjusted model; model 1 (adjusted for gender and age); model 2 (further adjusted for IC); and model 3 (fully adjusted for gender, age, IC, marital status, education, and nursing home resident status). Potential non-linearity was examined using restricted cubic splines (RCS) with full covariate adjustment. Robustness was further verified through interaction tests and stratified subgroup analyses. Furthermore, following the bootstrap mediation testing framework proposed by Preacher & Hayes, we assessed the mediating effect of IC by examining the 95% confidence interval for the indirect effect based on 5,000 bootstrap samples using the ‘mediation’ package in R.

Association of multimorbidity with HRQoL: univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses

Four logistic regression models were constructed to examine the relationship between multimorbidity and HRQoL. In the non-adjusted model, there was a positive correlation between multimorbidity and EQ-5D-3L level, and the effect values were higher than in the other models (OR, 95% CI: 1.26, 1.14–1.38, p <0.001). Additionally, the study employed RCS to further analyze the relationship between multimorbidity and HRQoL. Results revealed a negative linear correlation between multimorbidity number and HRQoL in older adults (Supplementary fig. 1).

In the fully adjusted model, the risk of reduced HRQoL increased by 22% per additional condition (95% CI: 1.10–1.35, p <0.001). When multimorbidity number was categorized into four groups and trend was tested, the odds of poorer HRQoL were significantly higher in Q2 - Q4 compared to Q1 (Q2 vs. Q1: OR, 95% CI: 2.48, 1.39–4.43, p = 0.002; Q3 vs. Q1: OR, 95% CI: 2.72, 1.48–5.01, p = 0.001; Q4 vs. Q1: OR, 95% CI: 2.94, 1.57–5.50, p <0.001) (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis revealed that after full covariate adjustment, multimorbidity was significantly positively associated with EQ-5D-3L level (OR, 95%CI: 1.82, 1.18–2.81), with stable results. No significant interaction was observed between stratification factors and the multimorbidity–HRQoL association (p for interaction > 0.1) (Fig. 2).

Mediation by IC

This study employed mediation analysis to investigate the mediating role of IC in the relationship between multimorbidity and HRQoL. The results demonstrated that IC partially mediated this association (β, 95% CI: 0.0163, 0.0083–0.0260, p < 0.001). The total effect was 0.0615 (β, 95% CI: 0.0615, 0.0451–0.0750, p < 0.001), with a direct effect of 0.0452 (β, 95% CI: 0.0452, 0.0261–0.0616, p < 0.001). The proportion of the total effect mediated by IC was 25.9% (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study demonstrates a significant negative correlation between multimorbidity and HRQoL after full covariate adjustment (age, gender, IC, marital status, education, and nursing home resident status). The risk of reduced HRQoL increased by 22% per additional condition. The RCS indicates a linear correlation. Subgroup analysis confirms result stability with no significant interactions. In addition, IC had a mediating effect in the negative association of multimorbidity on HRQoL (total effect = 0.0615; direct effect = 0.0452; indirect effect = 0.0163), with a 25.9% mediating proportion.

A prospective surgical study in elderly patients revealed significantly higher HRQoL scores in those without multimorbidity compared to individuals with multimorbidity [25]. Moreover, a systematic review involving eight studies also demonstrated multimorbidity’ s negative association with HRQoL [26]. Notably, this association similarly observed in Chinese population [27]. Regarding the mediating role of IC, a study conducted in the UK demonstrated that IC serves as a significant mediator in aging research [28]. Most of the previous studies were conducted in the United States, followed by Western European countries, with only a small fraction focusing on Chinese populations [29]. Our study is primarily based on the ICOPE pilot in Lianyungang, China. The evaluation strictly adheres to the WHO ICOPE methodology and framework, focusing on the overall functional status of older adults. In addition to examining the direct effects of multimorbidity on HRQoL, this study investigates the mediating role of IC in this relationship. Furthermore, whereas prior research has predominantly focused on primary care and clinical settings [29], our study specifically targets older adults in communities and nursing homes.

Multimorbidity affects HRQoL mainly by impairing physical function and causing psychological disorders. For instance, multimorbidity may imbalance the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems [30, 31], potentially leading to gastrointestinal motility disorders and depression [32]. Additionally, individuals with multimorbidity face significant socioeconomic burdens which may diminish HRQoL, including complex treatment regimens and increased healthcare utilization [33, 34].

By mediating 25.9% of multimorbidity’s effect on HRQoL, IC represents a key intervention target; strengthening it could alleviate nearly one-third of the related HRQoL decline. Therefore, integrating IC interventions with disease treatment into a multidimensional strategy is valuable, and understanding how multimorbidity contributes to IC decline is crucial for designing them effectively. Multimorbidity is associated with the decline of IC, and several potential pathways may underlie this association: (1) Multimorbidity causes multi-organ interactions, reducing IC (e.g., cognitive decline, depression). Examples: Heart failure, renal failure, and brain dysfunction exacerbate organ damage through hemodynamic changes and renin – angiotensin - aldosterone system (RAAS) dysregulation. As multisystem functional reserve declines simultaneously, older adults lose the inter-organ compensation seen in single-organ diseases. This leads to an additive reduction in IC, accelerating the progression to disability [35]. (2) Multimorbidity often leads to polypharmacy, which can trigger drug–disease interactions and increase adverse reaction risks [36, 37]. For example, in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and inflammatory arthropathy, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) used for joint inflammation inhibit prostaglandin, prostacyclin, and thromboxane synthesis, potentially causing pre-renal acute kidney injury (AKI). This acute renal impairment elevates levels of uraemic toxins, inflammatory mediators, and anemia, further reducing intrinsic capacity and creating a vicious cycle of “pain–drug–renal injury–functional decline” [38]. (3) Multimorbidity exacerbates systemic inflammation, leading to elevated proinflammatory markers. These markers can contribute to the decline of IC domains—such as muscle mass, cognition, and organ function—by suppressing Insulin-like Growth Factor 1 (IGF-1) synthesis and disrupting neurotransmitter metabolism [39–41].

The multimorbidity-IC association was subsequently linked to poorer HRQoL through two primary pathways: (1) functional impairments in activities of daily living (ADLs), including increased disability/fall risks and reduced independence/mobility [42–44]. (2) psychological consequences, such as depression, diminished well-being and cognitive-emotional dysfunction, which may precipitate somatic symptoms [45, 46]. These impacts are interconnected and are collectively associated with diminished HRQoL.

In summary, this study establishes IC as a pivotal mediator in the relationship between multimorbidity and HRQoL. This finding encourages an integrated understanding of other reported mediators. For instance, existing research has identified ADL, IADL, and depression as key mediators, whose importance varies across factors such as age, education, and gender [10]. It is noteworthy that within the WHO’s “IC–functional ability” framework, depression is a direct manifestation of the psychological domain of IC, while ADL/IADL limitations represent core consequences of declined functional ability resulting from its impairment [47]. Consequently, IC can be regarded as a more upstream, comprehensive core mediator, whose changes encompass and influence multiple downstream pathways. Building on this, future research should aim to develop and test comprehensive chain mediation models—such as “multimorbidity → IC → functional ability/depression → HRQoL”—while incorporating sociodemographic factors to more fully delineate the complex trajectory of healthy ageing.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the temporal relationships among multimorbidity, IC, and HRQoL in older adults. The absence of longitudinal follow-up also prevents observation of IC trajectories or HRQoL changes, thereby impeding identification of critical windows for early intervention. Second, information bias may exist due to reliance on self-reported data. Third, the sampling strategy predominantly recruited participants from urban districts of Lianyungang, with inadequate representation from rural areas, potentially limiting the generalizability of findings to other populations. Future cohort studies with more diverse samples are needed to validate these results. Furthermore, this study did not adjust for potential confounding factors such as socioeconomic status. Subsequent research should investigate how these variables moderate the multimorbidity-IC-HRQoL pathway to facilitate development of targeted interventions. In conclusion, while our findings support an association between multimorbidity and HRQoL mediated by IC, these results require confirmation through rigorous longitudinal studies.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study including 468 patients aged ≥ 60 years from Lianyungang, a WHO ICOPE pilot program in China, multimorbidity was negatively associated with HRQoL. The risk of reduced HRQoL increased by 22% per additional condition. In addition, IC significantly mediated the association between multimorbidity and HRQoL, explaining 25.9% of the total effect.
