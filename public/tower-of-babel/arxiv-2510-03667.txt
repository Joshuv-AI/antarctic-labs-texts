Abstract
. Sycophancy, the tendency of LLM-based chatbots to express excessive enthusiasm, agreement, flattery, and a lack of disagreement, is emerging as a significant risk in human-AI interactions. However, the extent to which this affects human-LLM collaboration in complex problem-solving tasks is not well quantified, especially among novices who are prone to misconceptions. We created two LLM chatbots, one with high sycophancy and one with low sycophancy, and conducted a within-subjects experiment ( n = 24 n=24 ) in the context of debugging machine learning models to isolate the effect of LLM sycophancy on users’ mental models, their workflows, reliance behaviors, and their perceptions of the chatbots. Our findings show that users of the high sycophancy chatbot were less likely to correct their misconceptions and spent more time over-relying on unhelpful LLM responses. Despite these impaired outcomes, a majority of users were unable to detect the presence of excessive sycophancy.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are rapidly becoming prevalent
decision-support
tools, but their tendency to appease their users has raised alarm. This trait, known as
sycophancy
, manifests as
overly enthusiastic support for the users’ ideas,
and a tendency to avoid contradicting, debating, and disagreeing with users when they express incorrect or limited beliefs—exactly when they would stand to gain from critical feedback
(
Sharma et al. 2023
)
. Given a user question that contains hints of the user’s opinions, beliefs, or emotions, LLMs may explicitly reinforce these values through direct validation, or they may implicitly reflect them by not suggesting alternative or potentially better ideas. While this has been recognized as a significant safety problem in human-LLM interactions
(
Carro 2024
;
Cheng et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2024a
;
Fanous et al. 2025
;
Moore et al. 2025
;
Ibrahim et al. 2025
)
, sycophantic behaviour seems to be troublingly pernicious and deeply embedded in the dominant training paradigm of instruct-finetuned LLMs—using reinforcement learning to maximize user satisfaction
(
Christiano et al. 2017
)
—and is likely not easily reversed. In April 2025, OpenAI retracted an update to GPT-4o that was intended to make the model more intuitive, but instead it resulted in excessive sycophancy
(
OpenAI 2025
)
. As such, while we lack the appropriate technical solutions to control and suppress this undesirable trait, there is a need to conduct a deeper investigation to better quantify how LLM sycophancy impacts end users.

Prior studies have documented that LLMs can inadvertently create echo chambers of opinion,
such as when used for information search

(
Spatharioti et al. 2023
;
Si et al. 2023
)
.
Question-answering sycophancy benchmarks also show LLMs often agree with objectively wrong facts if they are expressed by the user

(
Sharma et al. 2023
)
. However, more complex problem-solving tasks like data analysis, programming, and debugging represent a
non-trivial
proportion of LLM-assisted tasks
(
Chatterji et al. 2025
;
Handa et al. 2025
)
, but are not represented well in sycophancy literature. Interactions in such tasks may involve open-ended problem-solving and multi-turn conversation, which encode risks beyond
explicit agreements
with the user in standalone, single-turn responses. The impact of LLM sycophancy on how users
form beliefs, explore alternative ideas, and rely on LLM advice
in problem-solving tasks is an important yet understudied area.

LLM chatbot during a problem-solving task. We measure how

We targeted novices as they are likely to have misconceptions about the task that a sycophantic LLM will amplify
. To quantify the effect of LLM sycophancy, we created two chatbots that are different on the sycophancy spectrum but expected to behave similarly on all other dimensions (like accuracy and
sentiment
). The

chatbot was designed to validate users’ incorrect beliefs, while the

Summary of Findings.

We conducted a within-subjects, mixed-methods study with
n=24
undergraduate students, using the two LLM chatbots to debug two isomorphic ML models and improve their F1 score (Section
3
). We found that using the
High

Sycophancy
chatbot resulted in
less improvements to the user’s overall mental model, especially in misconceived beliefs
(Section
4.3
).
This suggests that sycophancy can affirm existing beliefs, even in open-ended tasks. This risk is particularly elevated when the user group is novices who are more prone to holding misconceptions, and would benefit from more direct corrections and critical feedback. Users of the
High

Sycophancy
chatbot relied on the LLMs to a similar degree as users of
Low

Sycophancy
, but
more frequently over-relied on
irrelevant
or
incorrect
generated advice, which led users towards suboptimal task performance outcomes
(Section
4.4
).
Alarmingly, users rated their subjective perceptions about the chatbot equally across categories like
helpfulness
and
reliability
, and a majority (71%) verbally expressed detecting no differences between the two chatbots. This indicates that in spite of the clear discrepancy in mental model, behaviour, and performance outcomes,
users may remain unaware of being impacted by sycophancy.
(Section
4.5
).

Based on these findings, we discuss the implications of the impact of sycophancy on real-world, open-ended tasks, which opens up the risks beyond simplistic, fact-based question answering tasks (Section
5
).
We pose that the consequences are most significant for learners who are still grasping the fundamentals of their tasks, including younger users who may be more vulnerable to the influence of LLMs. Given that sycophancy remains difficult to control, we also discuss the fundamental value tensions of LLMs and potential pathways to mitigate the risks of sycophancy. We advocate for stronger research in evaluating sycophancy in real-world tasks, monitoring the longitudinal effect of LLMs on learners at scale, and further consideration of design choices that encourage mindful LLM usage.

2.
Related Work

We position our work in relation to existing literature in human-AI interactions. Specifically, we are concerned with how a ubiquitous property of LLMs, such as sycophancy, poses risks via amplifying errors in novice-LLM workflows, where the user is
not an expert
in the task that they are trying to perform.

2.1.
LLM Sycophancy

Sycophancy is a property of LLMs that makes them highly agreeable to their users, which, in the worst cases, can manifest as reinforcing a user’s incorrect beliefs
(
Sharma et al. 2023
;
Fanous et al. 2025
)
or always validating a user’s perspective
(
Cheng et al. 2025
;
Moore et al. 2025
)
.
Instruction-tuned LLMs exhibit sycophancy due to being trained to maximize positive user feedback
(
Christiano et al. 2017
)
. While the full impact of sycophancy on LLM users is still not fully understood, it is already present in many consumer-facing chatbots. In April 2025, OpenAI rolled back an update to
GPT-4o
—the default model for many users of ChatGPT—due to excessive and flagrant sycophancy. In a blog post
(
OpenAI 2025
)
, they attributed the cause of the problem to the introduction of a new reward signal based on user thumbs-up/down feedback, extrapolating that this change
“weakened the influence of our primary reward signal, which had been holding sycophancy in check."
This public incident highlighted the urgency to better evaluate the downstream impact of sycophancy on end users, many of whom were exposed to this sudden amplification of sycophancy.

Current efforts in AI research have targeted benchmarking
(
Sharma et al. 2023
;
Cheng et al. 2025
;
Fanous et al. 2025
)
or reducing
(
Wei et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024a
)
the level of sycophancy in LLMs. However, these evaluations primarily target single-turn question answering, which do not reflect complex and multi-step problem-solving tasks in real user conversations with chatbots. Works on sycophancy in HCI primarily analyze the effect on the subjective perceptions of trust towards the LLMs
(
Carro 2024
;
Sun and Wang 2025
)
, but the current knowledge landscape lacks understanding into how sycophancy affects the cognition and behaviour of users in complex tasks. As eliminating sycophancy entirely is not yet possible, this study compares the effect on users of an LLM that always echoes the user’s beliefs against an LLM that corrects the user’s misinformed beliefs.

2.2.
Risks in Human-LLM Interactions

Sycophancy funnels into a broader class of safety risks within human-LLM interactions. From hallucinations
(
Huang et al. 2025
)
, to deception
(
Hagendorff 2024
)
, to encoded biases
(
Wan et al. 2023
)
—such concerns are actively investigated within AI ethics, safety, and alignment research. As LLMs become increasingly used as advisers, decision-makers, and emotional confidants, the question of how to promote appropriate usage becomes crucial
(
Chatterji et al. 2025
)
. Wrongful usage of AI can risk suboptimal decision making
(
Bo et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2025
)
, reduce creativity and divergent thinking
(
Kumar et al. 2025
)
, and potentially even reduce cognitive activity
(
Kosmyna et al. 2025
;
Singh et al. 2025
)
.

While most human-LLM interactions research has not specifically controlled sycophancy as an independent variable, it can be deduced that sycophancy was likely present in all commercial LLM-based chatbots. The results of the following studies are framed around LLMs in general, but we postulate that sycophancy is a specific property that contributed to the outcomes measured. Compared to traditional search engines like Google, searching for information with LLMs is more likely to lead to asking confirmatory questions and over-relying on incorrect results
(
Spatharioti et al. 2023
;
Si et al. 2023
)
. LLMs amplify subjective and polarizing opinions that the user already holds, leading to echo chambers of (mis)information
(
Sharma et al. 2024
)
. In emotionally-charged contexts, users who are looking for validation may receive LLM responses that are not appropriate for rehabilitating their mental states
(
Moore et al. 2025
)
. Therefore, sycophancy has the potential to interact with the emotional response, decision-making, and cognitive state of the user, which makes it both complex and vital to investigate.

2.3.
Novices and LLMs

Lastly, we pose that the effects of LLM sycophancy are particularly harmful to novices, especially if the task involves problem-solving skills that rely on experience, heuristics, and domain knowledge. Adapting LLM responses to diverse end-users with different skill levels in the task is still an open area of development
(
Chen et al. 2024b
;
Díaz-Pace et al. 2024
;
Nguyen et al. 2024
)
. For example, prior research in the LLM-assisted coding domain has documented the meta-cognitive struggles that novices face in prompting and verifying LLMs, leading to ‘rabbitholes’ of over-reliance
(
Kazemitabaar et al. 2023
;
Tie et al. 2024
;
Prather et al. 2023
;
Lucchetti et al. 2024
;
Prather et al. 2024
)
. Our domain of focus, machine learning, represents a particularly challenging form of problem-solving.
In ML, the relationship between input hyperparameters and output metrics is not easily predictable
(
Amershi et al. 2019
;
Krishnan and Wu 2017
)
. There is not one direct path towards the optimal solution, but a complex and iterative process that involves constant verification
(
Nahar et al. 2023
;
Ashmore et al. 2021
;
Arteaga Garcia et al. 2024
)
. The execution gap between experts and novices means that while LLMs have the potential to supply novices with relevant advice
(
Arteaga Garcia et al. 2024
;
Cao et al. 2023
)
, this is often unsuccessful due to the novice user’s inability to prompt good questions, filter relevance results, and accurately verify the outputs
(
Bo et al. 2025
)
. Due to these additional risks, we motivate our study to focus on novices who are prone to misconceptions and are likely to use LLMs for help in the task.

3.
Methodology

We conducted a within-subjects study to investigate the effect of LLM sycophancy on an open-ended problem-solving workflow. To isolate the effect of sycophancy, we first created
High

Sycophancy
and
Low

Sycophancy
LLM chatbots and computationally verified that they validate user queries to a maximally differentiable degree while being otherwise similar. In this section, we describe the design and coverage of the tasks, the process for creating the chatbots, the procedure of the user study, and detailed measures for our research questions.

We center our experiment on debugging machine learning models as the task, and self-identified ML novices as the participants. Novices refer to learners in the task who are familiar with basic concepts of ML (such as knowing the purpose of training and testing datasets, reading model performance metrics, and describing failure modes like over-fitting and under-fitting), but may not be fluent or experienced in all aspects of ML theory or implementation. We developed two scripts for training binary classification ML models using concepts familiar to an introductory audience:
Random Forest
(
RF
) with the Adult Income prediction task
(
Becker and Kohavi 1996
)
and
Logistic Regression
(
LR
) with the Wine Quality prediction task
(
Cortez and Reis 2009
)
. We further modified the datasets to support the goal of creating ‘debuggable’ training code, such as changing the magnitudes of the features in Wine Quality to induce feature scaling issues and dropping some features to reduce the complexity of the Adult Income dataset.

Each task was intentionally planted with four conceptual errors that affect the performance of the models. The errors were purposely screened and selected such that they differ between the two tasks to reduce potential learning effects in a within-subjects setting. Whenever possible, we strived to ensure that each error contributes
distinct
deterioration in performance. See Table
1
for the description of the errors and the proposed solutions. Appendix
A
further shows the full assignment code and the proposed code fixes. Note that there can be multiple ways to solve an error, such as applying
class_weights=‘balanced’
in the model parameters or resampling the data with the SMOTE algorithm
(
Chawla et al. 2002
)
to fix data imbalance issues. We would consider either solution to be conceptually correct.

For each debugging task in the study, participants were given a description explaining the context of why the model is trained. For example, we motivated the
RF
task as
‘Your goal is to identify all of the high-income citizens, without mistakenly identifying any low-income citizens’
.
To introduce some natural ambiguity, we did not explicitly say which metric out of the ones provided (accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and confusion matrix of both the training and testing datasets) they should optimize.
Given that the
RF
task’s Adult Income dataset is highly imbalanced, this implies that the appropriate metric to optimize is the F1-score of the
high-earning
class, rather than the overall accuracy. See Figure
A.1
of the Appendix to see the custom
get_performance()
function provided to the participants.

We further verified these two tasks through multiple pilot studies with ML learners and practitioners of various levels who were unaffiliated with the research team and who were further excluded from the main participant pool. In the pilots, we made improvements to the comprehensibility of the task instructions and checked that the conceptual issues and solutions were of an appropriate skill level for novices.

3.2.
Creating
High

Sycophancy
and
Low

Sycophancy
Chatbots

In order to measure the effects of LLM sycophancy, we designed two chatbots that differ in their sycophancy towards users, but are otherwise similar. Before deploying the chatbots in our experiment, we conducted computational evaluations to validate that the
High

Sycophancy
and
Low

Sycophancy
chatbot conditions behave as expected.

Chatbot Behaviour Goals.

We define a sycophantic LLM as one that reflects and supports the user’s mental model of the task, irrespective of the correctness of their hypotheses and beliefs. To study the effects of sycophancy, we seek to
emphasize the difference in model response behavior when the user has the wrong mental model.
Specifically, we define the following goals:

(D1)

Maximally differentiated validation characteristics when the user query contains a misconception — high validation of the user’s beliefs for
High

Sycophancy
and low validation for
Low

Sycophancy
.

(D2)

Similar performance in the ML task when the user query does
not
contain any misconceptions.

(D3)

Similar sentiments in the tone of the response.

It should be noted that the design goal
(D1)
necessitates revealing the task solutions to the chatbot system, which were provided to help the LLM infer if the user’s beliefs were misconceived or not
2
2

2

This design choice makes the LLMs more similar to an “oracle” AI chatbot rather than a generic AI chatbot, which would not always have the relevant task context. This is appropriate, as oracle AI chatbots (e.g. those with access to homework answers) are used in educational contexts.

Sycophancy

Sycophancy

High

Sycophancy
chatbot to amplify detected misconceptions.

The

The misconception inference step also naturally encourages the underlying

Computational Evaluation.

To benchmark the chatbots, we compiled and adapted real user queries asked in the two ML tasks from previous pilot studies. These computational evaluations are not comprehensive and should be interpreted only in the context of our ML debugging tasks. Due to the system prompts encoding task-specific instructions, it was not possible to conduct generalized benchmark evaluations.
We compare the two conditions across the previously specified characteristics and report the results:

(1)

Differentiated Validation Behaviour
: We aim to achieve maximally different validation behaviour of the LLM when the user has misconceptions. To measure this, we sampled 15 user queries from each ML task that contained a misconception (30 total) and prompted each chatbot to respond. For evaluation, we used an
LLM-as-Judge
evaluation set-up with
GPT-4o

(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
, which was presented with randomized chatbot responses in a blinded setup (i.e., without knowledge of which chatbot generated which response). This enabled us to assess the pairwise win rate of which chatbot
validates the user’s perspective
more. Averaged across
n=3
judging trials per query,
High

Sycophancy
was rated to be more validating 75.6% of the time,
Low

Sycophancy
7.8% of the time, and the conditions were equal 16.6% of the time. Using a binomial test and dropping the equal predictions, the winrate of
High

Sycophancy
was significantly higher than chance (
p<.0001
).

(2)

Equal Performance in Task
: To understand if the system prompt may have impacted LLM’s performance — and therefore the quality of the advice they offer — we collected 15 open-ended and conceptual user queries from each task (again 30 total). In the same format, the LLM chatbots were prompted and their answers were judged in a blinded, pairwise fashion. Averaged across
n=3
judging trials per query,
High

Sycophancy
was rated to be more accurate 23.3% of the time,
Low

Sycophancy
35.6% of the time, and the conditions were equal 41.1% of the time. While a slight difference between conditions was observed, a binomial test shows the difference was not statistically significant (
p=0.16
).

(3)

Equal Sentiment
: Lastly, sentiment can often be seen as an indication of sycophancy. We therefore aimed to evaluate whether sentiment differences existed between the two chatbots.
We used the pre-trained sentiment classifier TweetEval
(
Barbieri et al. 2020
)
to predict the probability of
positive
,
neutral
, and
negative
sentiments in the tone of the chatbot’s response. We evaluated sentiment on both datasets of
misconceived
and
open-ended
queries from the two previous evaluations. We find that
Low

Sycophancy
’s predicted negative sentiment was only significantly higher than
High

Sycophancy
by the Mann-Whitney U-test (
p<.0001
)
when responding to misconceptions
. There was no significant difference when the questions were open-ended and conceptual, which is desirable. The graphical results can be found in Figure
C.1
in the Appendix.

To summarize the computational tests, we confirmed that our two conditions achieve significantly different validation behaviour when asked misconceived questions, but do not differ significantly in performance or tone when asked neutral and open-ended questions. Therefore, the conditions exhibited satisfactory performance for our main experiments in the context of our ML debugging tasks.

3.3.
RQs and Measures

We describe the three research questions and their measures, originally summarized in Section
1
, in detail here.

RQ1: Mental Models.

The first objective centers around measuring whether excessive sycophancy affects users’ mental models of the tasks. We operationalize as the change in the confidence of their beliefs about the task. The literature around measuring mental models is not very standardized, with many studies relying on capturing qualitative themes from interview data
(
Liu et al. 2023a
;
Thompson et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
. While this approach captures appropriately rich descriptions and individual differences, it does not support quantitative analyses of
changes
in mental models. Instead, we follow the style of prior research that uses standardized quiz questions to measure knowledge in the task
(
Nourani et al. 2021
;
Collaris et al. 2022
;
Kulesza et al. 2013
)
. We further aim to discriminate between
strong
and
weak
beliefs, which helps quantify if interacting with the chatbots affects the
strength
of users’ beliefs.

Based on these requirements, we developed a quiz comprised of 12 true or false hypotheses about the task — for example, ‘random state
does not significantly affect model performance’
and
‘The data loading code contains a misstep’
. The statements were generated using the beliefs and misconceptions captured from pilot study participants as a guideline. Both tasks shared the same set of statements. For each task, 6/12 statements were correct and 6/12 were incorrect, and only 2/6 of the correct statements and 2/6 of the incorrect statements overlapped between the two tasks. During the study, participants rated their confidence in each statement with a slider spanning -100 to 100, where -100 is a confident belief that is is
false
, 0 is
unsure
, and 100 is a confident belief that it is
true
. See Appendix
E
for the statements in the mental model quiz and how they apply to each ML task.

To understand the impact of sycophancy on participants’ mental models in the debugging task, we evaluated the changes in their confidence ratings in the
Mental Model Quiz
pre- and post-chatbot use.
While each statement in the quiz could be either true or false, we normalized the directions such that a
positive confidence
in a statement is always a correct belief, while a
negative confidence
is always a wrong belief. Accordingly, we can compute the following metrics:
(A)
confidence-weighted accuracy, which describes the mean accuracy of their beliefs weighted by their self-rated confidence; and
(B)
count-based accuracy, which only accounts for the number of correct beliefs.
In the following equations,
P
is the number of participants,
N
is the number of questions, and confidence can be either pre- or post-chatbot (the pre-post difference is be computed as the change):

(A)

Confidence-Weighted Acc. =
\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\text{confidence}_{p,n}

(B)

Count-Based Acc. =
\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\!\left(\text{confidence}_{p,n}>0\right)

RQ2: Workflows and Reliance.

To understand users’ behaviour and reliance on the LLM, we thematically analyzed the workflows of participants in each task using a codebook approach. This is a standard approach in analyzing participant actions and synthesizing findings on LLM interaction patterns
(
Yen et al. 2025
;
Gu et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2023b
)
.
The types of event in our user workflows are:
User

Query
,
Chatbot

Response
, and
Code

Change
. We assigned each event with two labels — the
detailed action
, which breaks down the event into further sub-categories, and the
outcome
, which describes the correctness or relevance of the action. For example, a
Code

RQ3: Subjective Perceptions.

Lastly, we captured participants’ self-reported perceptions of their interactions with the chatbot, including the effect of the chatbot on their mental models, learning, and engagement in the task. We used a mixed-methods approach by combining a Likert survey after using the chatbots, think-aloud while completing the surveys, and an additional semi-structured interview after the conclusion of both tasks. In the interview, we asked participants to contrast the experiences they had with the two chatbots in the experiment. We also inquired into their strategies and feelings on using chatbots in real life, with an emphasis on learning and education scenarios. The

These topics were related to our research question and have been defined based on pilot study findings.
We grouped recurring codes into broader themes and triangulated them with the survey results to describe richer findings and strengthen the interpretation of the results. The research team discussed their interpretation of the interview results and negotiated disagreements until resolution.

3.4.
User Study

We describe the recruitment and procedure of the user study.

Recruitment and Inclusion Criteria.

We first conducted a power analysis to estimate the number of participants needed as 24.
The sample size was calculated using G*Power and based on
\alpha=0.05
,
power=0.8
, and a medium effect size of Cohen’s d
dz=0.6
, using the statistical test:
‘Means: Difference between two dependent means (matched pairs)’
.

We targeted recruitment primarily from students who were currently enrolled, or had recently taken an undergraduate Introduction to Machine Learning course at our research institution.
Due to the limited class size, we also employed a number of other recruitment techniques, including snowball sampling from the existing participants, and posting the recruitment on various university communication channels, including class forums, Slack channels, and student clubs’ social media. To narrow in on the target population without introducing unnecessary friction in the registration process, we required participants to self-declare that they were
“familiar with basic machine learning concepts but not an expert, such as having taken <Introduction to ML>, self-studied ML, or implemented small-scale ML projects"
. We did not impose any additional requirements on skill level. The experiment lasted a maximum of 90 minutes, and participants were compensated with a $25 CAD ($18 USD) gift card, which is in the range of the local minimum wage.

Experiment Procedure.

The procedure of the experiment is visualized in Figure
2
and described as follows:

(1)

Participants filled out the intake form, which included a consent form and asked about basic demographics information. They also took a
Knowledge Screening Quiz
consisting of five multiple-choice questions (see Appendix

), where they selected both the answer and their confidence in their answer. This provided us with information about their baseline theoretical knowledge in machine learning.

(2)

(3)

Participants were assigned to the first task-chatbot pairing, e.g.

-

(4)

Forming hypotheses was intended to prime participants into ‘‘information-searching" mode with targeted goals, which would help them ask more structured and specific questions from the chatbot as opposed to offloading the cognitive load of the task to the chatbot

4.1.
Participants Details

Out of the 24 participants recruited to the experiment, 7 identified as women, 16 as men, and 1 did not self-disclose. They range between 20-23 years of age (
\mu=21.0
,
\sigma=0.93
) and were all undergraduate students. 21 participants took an introductory ML course at the institution, while three had self-studied with online resources. In terms of baseline AI usage, 15 disclosed that they used AI chatbots daily, while six used them weekly, and three used them infrequently.

As part of the intake, participants also answered a 5-item, multiple-choice

designed to test proficiency on theoretical basics in machine learning. We computed both the accuracy and the Brier score for their performance in the quiz, which factors in both their accuracy and confidence. The lowest Brier score of zero indicates accurate answers and well-calibrated confidence, while higher scores up to 2 indicate poor confidence calibration (being unconfident in correct answers or over-confident in incorrect answers).
The overall range of Brier scores was 0.00-1.23 (
\mu=0.69
,
\sigma=0.32
) and the number of correctly answered questions ranged from 1-5 (
\mu=2.95
,
\sigma=1.19
). Only one participant achieved perfect knowledge calibration (100% confidence with all correct answers). See Figure
D.2
in the Appendix for the distribution of participants’ Brier scores.
These knowledge screening results demonstrate that most participants had appropriate pre-existing domain knowledge about ML, but were predominantly non-experts, as evidenced by the Brier scores and accuracy. This is crucial as we expect participants to be familiar with ML basics, but demonstrate having some level of misconceptions due to incorrect or missing knowledge.

RQ1: Sycophancy Reinforce Misconceptions in Mental Models

To understand the impact of sycophancy on participants’ mental models of the debugging tasks, we evaluate the changes in their beliefs about the task, as recorded in the
Mental Model Quiz
administered pre- and post-chatbot use. As specified in Methods, we compute the changes in the
(A)
confidence-weighted accuracy and
(B)
the count-based accuracy of the participant’s beliefs.

For
(A)
, we fit a linear mixed-effects ANCOVA-style model to assess the effect of the presence of LLM sycophancy on the post-chatbot confidence-weighted accuracy, treating the pre-chatbot confidence-weighted accuracy, the participant’s domain knowledge (Brier score on the Knowledge Screening Quiz), the task (
LR
or
RF
), and the order of tasks (
High

Sycophancy
or
Low

Sycophancy

\displaystyle\mathbf{(A)}\ \text{Post-Conf}_{ij}=\

\displaystyle\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\,\text{Sycophancy}_{ij}+\beta_{2}\,\text{ML Knowledge}_{ij}

\displaystyle+\beta_{3}\,\text{Order}+\beta_{4}\,\text{Task}+\beta_{5}\,\text{Pre-Conf}_{ij}

\displaystyle+\beta_{6}\,(\text{Sycophancy}_{ij}\times\text{Baseline Usage}_{ij})+\epsilon_{ij}

We employ a similar ANCOVA style analysis for
(B)

\displaystyle\mathbf{(B)}\ \text{Post-Counts}_{ij}=\

\displaystyle\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\,\text{Sycophancy}_{ij}+\beta_{2}\,\text{ML Knowledge}_{ij}

\displaystyle+\beta_{3}\,\text{Order}+\beta_{4}\,\text{Task}+\beta_{5}\,\text{Pre-Counts}_{ij}

\displaystyle+\beta_{6}\,(\text{Sycophancy}_{ij}\times\text{Baseline Usage}_{ij})+\epsilon_{ij}

We further expand beyond their perceptions of our experimental chatbots and discuss how they observe and mitigate sycophancy in chatbots in real life.

Users attribute differences in chatbot responses to prompting styles and task properties.

5.
Discussion

Key Findings.
We uncover that sycophantic LLMs have the potential to induce
a disconnect between how people think and behave with how they perceive their interactions with the LLM
.
In
RQ1
, we find that sycophancy can reinforce misconceptions in novices’ beliefs about the task, but the negative effects may be diluted with higher levels of baseline LLM usage. In
RQ2
, we find that reliance actions that users take are more likely to result in unhelpful code changes (over-reliance on the LLM). Both findings are mechanisms that contribute to the inferior task performance when using the
High

Sycophancy
chatbot. However, in
RQ3
, 71% of our participants were unobservant to the underlying sycophantic properties of the LLM. Furthermore, a majority of the subjective perception categories were rated without significant differences between the chatbots. In exploring the qualitative data, we uncover conflicting preferences towards the agreeableness in the tone of LLMs and the mitigation strategies against sycophancy in real-world usage.

We synthesize our key findings on the impact of LLM sycophancy on users in open-ended problem-solving tasks, which expands upon the standard set of tasks that have been studied in LLM sycophancy research. We discuss the repercussions on task learners as a group that is particularly vulnerable to negative cognitive impacts of sycophancy. Finally, we draw attention to the tension between how immediate user desires and long-term cognition-preserving strategies may arise in the current training paradigm of LLMs, and an outlook for interaction strategies and interventions that moderate the effect of sycophancy.

LLM Echo Chambers in Complex Tasks.

Sycophancy is originally conceptualized and evaluated based on inducing LLMs to output factually false answers that align with the opinions of their users
(
Sharma et al. 2023
)
. It is commonly benchmarked through simple question-answering datasets
(
Fanous et al. 2025
;
Cheng et al. 2025
)
, although recent efforts have expanded towards multi-turn conversations too
(
Hong et al. 2025
)
. We explore user interactions in a more ecologically valid, open-ended task, demonstrating that sycophancy can present itself in a less detectable way that does not always mean being
factually wrong
. Simply by echoing and enforcing the user’s existing mental model of the task, irrespective of correctness or relevance, LLMs can contribute to a distorted sense of perception
(
Morrin et al. 2025
)
. While diminished performance presents a legitimate risk to human-LLM collaboration, the user further misses out on the opportunity to engage with alternative approaches, critically reflect on their mental model of the task, and calibrate the confidence in their own knowledge. Such harms are more difficult to quantify and may surface as ramifications in downstream engagements, rather than in immediate outcomes. These factors are not easily measured in static benchmarking tests, as they derive from the nuanced behaviours of real people.

Our findings strike parallels with the body of research on LLM persuasion
(
Rogiers et al. 2024
;
Breum et al. 2024
)
. Prior works have found that LLMs can impart strong influences on personal beliefs of their users, creating echo chambers of thought which can be particularly troubling when concerning sensitive, political, or extremist issues
(
Sharma et al. 2024
;
Krügel et al. 2023
;
Danry et al. 2025
)
. LLMs that are further capable of deception, which we did not evaluate, extend these risks by creating opportunities where the user can be intentionally misled
(
Hagendorff 2024
)
. At larger scales across society, this raises the question of whether and how the consequences of the dark patterns of LLM design can be detected and mitigated
(
Kran et al. 2025
)
. Our results in the open-ended ML debugging task also call upon sycophancy evaluation tests to consider more realistic and complex user interaction scenarios, which may include brainstorming, reasoning, and automation domains. Investigating the impact of sycophancy in a wider range of applications can inform the development of model guardrails that can lessen harms
(
Malmqvist 2025
)
.

Implications for Novice-LLM Interactions.

Our findings that sycophancy may reinforce false beliefs are especially troubling when contextualized within the population of novices who are learning new information and skills
(
Liao et al. 2024
;
Brachman et al. 2024
)
. People are turning to AI tools to enhance the speed at which they can learn new skills, including coding and machine learning, but the pedagogical effectiveness of these approaches generally relies on substantial external scaffolding
(
Ma et al. 2025
)
. What makes novices particularly vulnerable to sycophantic AI? While experts may be able to leverage LLMs using precise prompting and seasoned verification, novices lack the knowledge and first-hand experience to supervise their interactions with the LLM
(
Kazemitabaar et al. 2023
;
Margulieux et al. 2024
;
Bo et al. 2025
)
. As echoed in our interviews with participants, many of them perceive LLMs to be a knowledge equalizing tool that is more generally beneficial than harmful, especially in resource-limited contexts where they don’t have access to a better learning alternative (e.g., their professor). A further concern specific to novices is the impact of cognitive offloading to the LLM on the learning process.
People develop mental models iteratively over time through synthesizing their interactions with the system and their learned conceptual knowledge
(
Kieras and Bovair 1984
)
. Without such practices, novices may never learn the necessary mental models to become self-sufficient, even if they can generate AI-assisted outputs at a high level of perceived expertise
(
Krupp et al. 2024
;
Blake 2025
)
. Our participants mention this as a concern with respect to increasing reliance on LLMs, and some even divulge personal strategies for learning with LLMs, but there lacks systematic recommendations for effective engagement.

Beyond novices in tasks, our results also have broader implications for the general demographic of young people in an increasingly AI-driven society. ChatGPT and most other LLM-based chatbots are not age-restricted for teenagers, who are particularly vulnerable to persuasive online influences
(
Rath et al. 2025
)
. A pressing concern is the potential erosion and under-development of foundational cognitive skills like reading, writing, reasoning, and problem-solving
(
Kosmyna et al. 2025
;
Lee et al. 2025
)
. Sycophantic LLMs that are agreeable, efficient, and extremely accessible, compounded with a lack of awareness of the vulnerability of particular users, can irreparably alter the cognition of young users. We call for longitudinal research to better understand the impact of LLMs on learning. Furthermore, we advocate for the development and evaluation of cognitive-preserving AI designs, which include cognitive forcing UI designs
(
Buçinca et al. 2021
;
Collins et al. 2024
)
, proactive interactions
(
Liu et al. 2025
;
Deng et al. 2025
;
Pu et al. 2025
)
, and the promotion of meta-cognitive practices
(
Tankelevitch et al. 2024
;
Margulieux et al. 2024
)
.

Sycophancy as a Value Tension in LLM Design.

Sycophancy is recognized as a key challenge in LLM safety, yet represents the tension between what
users think they want
, and what may end up being
harmful to them
in the long term
(
Ibrahim et al. 2025
)
. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) has been the main driver of success in the LLM age of chatbot technology
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
. Learning from the reward signal of satisfying the user — which by proxy encodes qualities of helpfulness, agreeableness, and trustworthiness — has been able to radically upscale the capabilities of these systems. However, we now recognize that such training paradigms can develop undesirable traits that may be hidden from naive evaluations. For certain user archetypes, like people who seek to outsource deep thinking or people who react positively to emotional validation, their preferences may be reflected as positive ratings for LLM outputs that inadvertently embody sycophancy
(
Williams et al. 2024
)
. While more critical users may recognize that a “yes man" chatbot limits their underlying usefulness, or even may find the output style placating and annoying, evidence points to the sycophantic characteristic of LLMs being core to how they are trained and is non-trivial to eliminate
(
Malmqvist 2025
)
. Therefore, addressing the inherent tension between user satisfaction and long-term safety remains a salient problem.

For the time being, sycophantic behaviour may be an inevitable consequence of training LLMs on human feedback. Addressing dark patterns of technology has been a topic of research in HCI far pre-dating LLMs, spanning social media
(
Cara 2019
)
, website design
(
Gunawan et al. 2021
)
, and payment systems
(
Luguri and Strahilevitz 2021
)
. Within LLMs, these dark patterns often manifest as deception over the user
(
Hagendorff 2024
;
Kran et al. 2025
)
. Design tensions often manifest when there are opposing values represented by the stakeholders — in the case of LLM chatbots, it is often between revenue generation and the well-being of the users. Our results indicate that an unintended consequence of maximizing helpfulness may lead to chatbots that reinforce bad ideas and lead to poor outcomes
(
Ibrahim et al. 2025
)
. With a better understanding of how LLM sycophancy can impact users in open-ended tasks, model developers may want to consider including these inadvertent impacts in their design requirements. They may also be incentivized to perform more comprehensive evaluations, which include multi-turn user tests with diverse end users, rather than just using simple benchmarks and unit tests.

Lastly, we recognize that individuals and societies can co-evolve alongside the changes in technology
(
Shen et al. 2024
)
— this is demonstrated by our preliminary finding that more frequent LLM users were less impacted by sycophancy. Through AI literacy education, users can be taught critical limitations of LLMs, promoting better awareness of appropriate use cases
(
Bender 2024
)
. For example, lay end users were largely unaware of sycophancy before this year. But due to improved public awareness, including the transparent post-mortem analysis from OpenAI
(
OpenAI 2025
)
and increased visibility on parasocial chatbots in the news
(
cbs 2025
;
Reiley 2025
)
, more and more of the public have the opportunity to learn about and discuss their awareness of this risk. While obviously challenging (and not absolving model developers of their responsibility), systemic structures like educational institutions and regulatory bodies can also adapt their policies to meet the opportunities and challenges posed by Generative AI
(
Hacker et al. 2023
)
. The AI and HCI research communities have an incentive to collaborate on the development, maintenance, and evaluation of value-aligned, cognition-preserving AI tools.

Limitations.

Finally, we present the limitations and assumptions made in our experimental setup that may have impacted the ecological validity of our results. While we intend to create a chatbot that is realistically sycophantic, it is impossible to compare its performance to the now retracted
GPT-4o

(
OpenAI 2025
)
— a real chatbot with high sycophancy. As a workaround, we introduced an intermediate misconception inference step that explicitly lists out the user’s likely beliefs. This technique results in the default LLM becoming more corrective, which may be unrealistic. Therefore, our
High

Sycophancy
and
Low

Sycophancy
chatbots may represent an artificially amplified effect of LLM sycophancy.

In order to maximize opportunities where
High

Sycophancy
and
Low

Sycophancy
chatbots would respond differently (e.g., when the user query contains misconceptions), we emphasized in our experiment protocol that participants should always lead the queries with their hypotheses, which was not the natural pattern of prompting for some participants. In unrestricted workflows, users may ask more open-ended questions that do not contain clear leads and misconceptions, which reduces the negative impact of sycophancy.

The length of time participants spend with each chatbot is approximately 15 minutes, which allowed most to complete 2-10 rounds of questions. However, this is not a substantial amount of time to learn the idiosyncrasies of the chatbots, and may have led to an under-estimation of how many people could identify the difference between them. Many participants explicitly stated that they didn’t notice a difference at first, but when probed to reflect more deeply, they began brainstorming ideas based on observations they had made but didn’t have enough evidence to support.

Mental models are difficult to capture and lack standardized methods. We operationalize this measure as a confidence rating over a constant set of statements, which enabled us to perform quantitative analyses. However, this method does not capture nuances, edge cases, or beliefs that are otherwise not represented in the set of statements. Since participants also saw correct statements ahead of their problem-solving, this may have biased them to use them as hints to guide their problem-solving. We attempt to mitigate this by having them brainstorm their own hypotheses at the start, and warning them in the quiz not to rely on the statements.

The generalization of our results beyond ML debugging requires further assessment. While we motivate our experiment design as a problem-solving task where users can contribute their own ideas and alongside LLM advice, variations between collaboration styles across different tasks can alter the outcomes. We also recruited a specific group of participants who are young university students, which limits the understanding of how sycophancy can affect other population segments. Our computational evaluations are also constrained to the task and do not offer generalized results on how the system prompting may have affected the LLMs.

6.
Conclusion

We investigate the effect of sycophancy on novice-LLM interactions in open-ended problem-solving. We find that sycophancy can reinforce existing misconceptions in users and drive them into over-relying on the LLM. However, most users indicated that they did not notice the characteristics of sycophancy, suggesting that it can be difficult for end users to enact mitigation strategies.

Which of the following best describes the impact of a data distribution shift between the training and testing datasets in a machine learning model?

Participants’ performance on the Knowledge Screening Quiz is shown in Figure
D.2
. The Brier score distributions are plotted, separated by the number of questions that participants got correct, where it is possible to get the same Brier score even with different numbers of correct answers, depending on the confidence rated. The overall range of Brier scores is 0.00-1.23 (
\mu=0.69
,
\sigma=0.32
) and the number of correctly answered questions ranged from 1-5 (
\mu=2.95
,
\sigma=1.19
). The equation for the multi-class Brier score is given as:

\text{Brier Score}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bigl(p_{i,k}-y_{i,k}\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\text{Number of questions},

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=\text{Number of multiple-choice answers per question},

\displaystyle p_{i,k}

\displaystyle=\text{Confidence that question $i$'s answer is $k$},

\displaystyle y_{i,k}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{If question $i$'s answer is truly $k$},\\
0&\text{Otherwise}.\end{cases}

.
Distribution of Brier scores calculated from the accuracy and confidence calibration of participants in the

We specify the finalized codebook used to code the workflows. Codes are applied at both the event-level and the chunk-level.

Table F.2
.
Codebook for the actions and reliance behaviours in the task workflows.

Code Category

Label

Definition

User Query
- Detailed Action

Correct Lead

The query contains a helpful or conceptually correct lead, like
“I believe the model is overfitting"
for Random Forest.

Misconception Lead

The query contains a irrelevant or incorrect lead, like
“Should I scale features?"
for Random Forest.

No Lead

The query does not contain any leads, such as asking for a definition.

Chatbot Response
- Detailed Action

Confirmatory

Affirms and validates the user’s existing ideas.

Corrective

Corrects existing misconceptions, redirects users to other ideas.

Neither

Response to an open-ended or conceptual question.

Chatbot Response
- Outcome

Helpful

Suggests conceptually correct advice.

Leads Astray

Suggests irrelevant or incorrect advice.

Mixed

Suggests both correct and incorrect advice.

Code Change
- Detailed Action

No Change

Running or re-running the code without any changes from its prior state.

Major Change

Adding a new parameter, function, or feature to the code.

Minor Change

Changing a parameter detail, like tuning the hyperparameter or debugging an error.

Code Change
- Outcome

Improve

Implements a conceptually correct change (adding a function or tuning a parameter) – usually the test F1-score is expected to improve (but not always).

Worsen

Implements a conceptually incorrect solution. Usually the test F1-score is expected to decrease or stay the same.

Same/Error

Performance is not substantially changed, or cannot be evaluated — such as, altering a piece of conceptually incorrect solution, altering a conceptually correct solution in an ineffective way, implementing a correct solution but at the wrong step, adding an unnecessary analysis or plot, or there is an error (unless it is clear that the change is conceptually incorrect, in which case it is Worsen).

Reliance
- Chunk-Level Code

Over-Reliance

The user made code changes that were conceptually bad as a result of recommendations made by the LLM.

Under-Reliance

The user did not implement code changes suggested by the LLM that were conceptually correct and would have been helpful. If the LLM suggests multiple things and the user only implements one, that does not count.

Appropriate Reliance on LLM

The user made code changes that were conceptually good as a result of recommendations made by the LLM.

Appropriate Reliance on Self

The user did not implement code changes suggested by the LLM that are irrelevant or conceptually wrong (such as as a result of responding to a misconceived query).

Conceptual

The user asked conceptual or definition questions, and did not modify the code.
