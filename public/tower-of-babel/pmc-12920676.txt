The gut-lung axis in celiac disease: a narrative review of pulmonary manifestations and pathogenic mechanisms
Authors: Ahmed A Abdelmoaty, Maha E. Alsadik, Khaled A. Abdel-Sater
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aim:This narrative review synthesizes current evidence on the association between Celiac disease (CD) and pulmonary disorders, explores underlying mechanisms, and highlights clinical implications and future research directions.Background:CD is a chronic autoimmune enteropathy triggered by gluten ingestion in genetically predisposed individuals. Although it primarily affects the small intestine, emerging evidence implicates extraintestinal involvement, particularly of the respiratory system, suggesting a potential gut–lung axis.Methods:This narrative review was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, covering literature published in English up to October 2025. Search terms combined ‘celiac disease’ OR ‘coeliac disease’ with (‘lung disease’ OR ‘pulmonary’ OR ‘respiratory tract’ OR ‘asthma’ OR ‘bronchiectasis’ OR ‘COPD’ OR ‘interstitial lung disease’ OR ‘pulmonary hemosiderosis’). Inclusion criteria were peer-reviewed human studies reporting pulmonary manifestations in celiac disease. Exclusion criteria included non-English language, in vitro or animal studies, abstracts without full text, and insufficient clinical or mechanistic data. This was not a systematic review, and therefore no PRISMA flow diagram was generated.”Results:Large-scale registry studies in Scandinavia and case-based evidence across Europe and Asia support a spectrum of pulmonary manifestations in celiac disease, ranging from asthma and chronic cough to rare but life-threatening idiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis. Asthma and chronic cough were the most commonly observed associations, with population-based studies reporting an elevated risk. Case reports and small cohorts described co-occurrence with bronchiectasis and interstitial lung disease, while rare cases confirmed links to idiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis (Lane–Hamilton syndrome). Proposed mechanisms include systemic immune activation, increased intestinal and pulmonary permeability, micronutrient deficiencies, IgA deficiency, and chronic inflammation. Notably, several studies reported symptom improvement or resolution following a gluten-free diet (GFD).Conclusion:Pulmonary manifestations of CD, though relatively uncommon, are clinically significant and often reversible with dietary intervention. Greater awareness, early recognition, and mechanistic research are essential to optimize patient outcomes.

Introduction

Celiac disease (CD) is a chronic immune-mediated disorder triggered by gluten ingestion in genetically susceptible individuals, affecting approximately 1% of the global population. While its gastrointestinal manifestations—such as diarrhea, malabsorption, and abdominal pain—are well-characterized, CD is a systemic condition with diverse extra-intestinal presentations (1). Among these, pulmonary involvement remains under-recognized. However, accumulating clinical case reports and emerging hypotheses concerning a shared gut-lung axis necessitate a contemporary synthesis of the evidence (2).

This narrative review aims to synthesize the current evidence, explore pathogenic mechanisms, and highlight the clinical relevance of pulmonary manifestations in CD.

Methods

This narrative review was conducted by searching the electronic databases PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science for articles published in English up to October 2025. Search terms included combinations of: 'celiac disease' OR 'coeliac disease' AND ('lung diseases' OR 'pulmonary disease' OR 'respiratory tract diseases' OR 'asthma' OR 'bronchiectasis' OR 'COPD' OR 'interstitial lung disease' OR 'pulmonary hemosiderosis').

The inclusion criteria were peer-reviewed human studies (original research, observational studies, case reports, and systematic reviews) that explicitly addressed pulmonary manifestations in patients with celiac disease.

The exclusion criteria were non-English publications, animal or in vitro studies, editorials, commentaries, and studies lacking sufficient clinical or mechanistic data.

While a PRISMA diagram was not created due to the narrative nature of this review, the screening process was systematically tracked. From an initial pool of 99 identified records, 50 full-text articles were assestudies after duplicate removal and title/abstract screening, yielding 21 studies for inclusion.

Pulmonary manifestations of CD

1- CD and bronchiectasis: The association between CD and bronchiectasis is supported by several case reports and small cohorts. A retrospective UK review found that approximately 6% of patients with bronchiectasis had serological or histological evidence of CD (3). Clinicians should include CD in the differential diagnosis of unexplained bronchiectasis, particularly in patients presenting with gastrointestinal symptoms or evidence of malabsorption (4).

2- CD and COPD: The evidence for an association with COPD is mixed. A large Swedish population study indicated a modestly increased risk of COPD among CD patients (Hazard Ratio ~1.2) (5). In contrast, a Danish registry analysis reported no significant increase in COPD incidence (6). This discrepancy highlights the need for further research, controlling for confounders like smoking, to clarify this potential relationship. Furthermore, a Mendelian randomization study by Ma et al. (7) indicated that individuals with autoimmune diseases, including CD, have a higher likelihood of developing COPD.

3- CD and interstitial lung disease: Case reports document the coexistence of CD with various ILD subtypes, including cryptogenic organizing pneumonia (COP), lymphocytic interstitial pneumonia, and non-specific interstitial pneumonia (8). In some instances, serological markers for CD have been identified in patients presenting primarily with ILD, underscoring the importance of screening in atypical respiratory presentations.

4- CD and bronchial asthma: Population-based studies consistently demonstrate a bidirectional relationship between CD and asthma. A large cohort study reported that CD confers a 1.6-fold increased risk of asthma development (9). Conversely, children with asthma, particularly those with a family history, may have an elevated risk of CD, suggesting shared genetic or inflammatory pathways (10). Chronic cough is also a frequently reported symptom, which often improves with a gluten-free diet (GFD).

5- CD and idiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis: The co-occurrence of CD and idiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis was first reported by Lane and Hamilton in 1971. Since then, fewer than 100 cases have been reported worldwide. The syndrome is rare but important due to its clinical implications. Lane-Hamilton syndrome is a rare condition characterized by the concomitant presence of CD and idiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis (11). Pulmonary hemosiderosis is a rare disorder marked by repeated alveolar bleeding and subsequent iron deposition. Despite its rarity, recent case reports confirm the association between the two conditions (12).

In patients with Lane-Hamilton syndrome, early diagnosis and strict gluten avoidance significantly improve outcomes. Delay in diagnosis or poor adherence to GFD may lead to recurrent pulmonary haemorrhages and permanent lung damage. All pediatric patients with idiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis should undergo screening for CD. Low serum ferritin in patients with idiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis could be suggestive of coexisting CD. Strict GFD should be considered as initial therapy (13).

Underlying mechanisms connecting CD and pulmonary disorders

The precise mechanisms linking CD to pulmoelucidated and remain poorly understood. Current hypotheses point toward a multifactorial interplay involving systemic immune activation, increased mucosal permeability, micronutrient deficiencies, chronic inflammation, and selective IgA deficiency, all of which may converge on the lung as a target organ.

1. Systemic Immune Activation: The gluten-induced immune response in the gut may not be localized. Activated T cells and pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ, IL-21, TNF-α) can enter the systemic circulation, potentially triggering or exacerbating lung inflammation (14).

2. Mucosal Permeability: Increased intestinal permeability ("leaky gut") is a hallmark of active CD (15). This may allow gluten peptides and microbial antigens into the bloodstream, provoking a systemic immune response. Notably, increased lung permeability has also been observed in CD patients, suggesting a generalized defect in mucosal barriers (16).

3. Immunoglobulin A (IgA) Deficiency: Selective IgA deficiency is more common in CD patients and is a known risk factor for recurrent respiratory infections, which could predispose to chronic airway damage (17).

4. Micronutrient Deficiencies: Malabsorption in CD can lead to deficiencies in vitamins (e.g., D, B12) and minerals (e.g., zinc, iron), which are crucial for immune regulation and lung tissue integrity (18).

5. Chronic Inflammation: Persistent, low-grade systemic inflammation in CD may contribute to progressive tissue damage in the lungs, potentially driving the development of ILD or bronchiectasis (19).

6. Pulmonary Hemosiderosis: In Lane-Hamilton Syndrome, the pathogenic mechanism is believed to involve an autoimmune response that damages small blood vessels in the lungs, leading to recurrent bleeding. This response may be linked to the immune response to gluten in CD (11).

These hypotheses suggest that the relationship between CD and pulmonary disorders is multifaceted, involving immunological, inflammatory, and nutritional mechanisms. However, further research is still needed to fully elucidate these mechanisms and identify the factors that increase the risk of developing pulmonary manifestations in CD patients.

Diagnosis and management

The diagnosis of pulmonary manifestations of CD requires a high index of suspicion due to their atypical presentation. Clinicians should be aware of this association, particularly in patients with unexplained respiratory symptoms or lung diseases unresponsive to conventional treatments. A thorough evaluation should include a detailed medical history and physical examination, with attention to family history of CD or other autoimmune disorders, as well as respiratory symptoms such as chronic cough, wheezing, shortness of breath, or pulmonary hemorrhage (2).

Serological testing for CD, including tissue transglutaminase IgA and endomysial IgA antibodies, is essential to confirm or exclude the diagnosis. When serology is positive, duodenal biopsy remains the gold standard for detecting characteristic mucosal damage (20). Pulmonary assessment should include pulmonary function tests to identify obstructive or restrictive patterns, and chest imaging — including X-ray and high-resolution computed tomography — to detect structural changes such as bronchiectasis, interstitial fibrosis, pulmonary nodules, or pulmonary hemosiderosis (2). In selected cases, additional investigations such as bronchoalveolar lavage or lung biinvolvement.

Management requires a multidisciplinary approach involving gastroenterologists, pulmonologists, and nutritionists to ensure optimal care. Strict adherence to a GFD remains the cornerstone of therapy for CD. It has been associated with improvement in gastrointestinal and certain respiratory symptoms, including chronic cough, asthma, and pulmonary hemosiderosis (21). In Lane–Hamilton syndrome, a GFD can be curative (21). Refractory or severe pulmonary involvement may necessitate adjunctive therapies such as corticosteroids or bronchodilators tailored to the specific pulmonary condition.

Limitations, translational challenges, and future directions

Current evidence is limited by small sample sizes, retrospective design, lack of control groups, and absence of interventional studies assessing the impact of a gluten-free diet on pulmonary endpoints. To date, no randomized controlled trials have specifically investigated pulmonary outcomes in patients with CD. Most data are derived from case reports and retrospective cohorts. Diagnostic misclassification and incomplete adjustment for confounders (e.g., smoking in COPD studies) further complicate interpretation. Translational challenges include the lack of validated biomarkers, inconsistent screening practices, and difficulties extrapolating pediatric findings to adults. Future research should focus on prospective cohort studies, controlled dietary intervention trials, and biomarker discovery to clarify the gut–lung axis and optimize therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

CD is a systemic autoimmune condition with clinically relevant respiratory sequelae. Although uncommon, pulmonary involvement is increasingly recognized and frequently amenable to dietary intervention through strict adherence to a gluten-free diet. Acknowledging the gut-lung axis in CD is vital for comprehensive patient care. Future research is essential to unravel the underlying mechanisms and improve outcomes for patients with coexisting celiac and pulmonary diseases.
