Collaboration in team outpatient eating disorder care: clinician’s perspectives
Authors: Megan Bray, Gabriella Heruc, Olivia R L Wright
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clinical practice guidelines recommend team outpatient eating disorder treatment, yet the organisation and delivery of team care vary considerably in practice. This qualitative study explored clinicians’ attitudes toward and experiences of team outpatient eating disorder treatment, including multidisciplinary and interprofessional care. Nineteen Australian clinicians (dietitians, mental health professionals, and general practitioners) with at least two years’ experience working in outpatient eating disorder teams participated in semi-structured interviews. Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis. Three themes and three sub-themes were identified. Theme 1, Preference for Interprofessional Ways of Working, captured clinicians’ preference for highly integrated and collaborative approaches, including perceived benefits for team functioning, clinician wellbeing, and client care; the related sub-theme Dismantle Systemic Hierarchies to Foster Trust, Respect, and Collaboration highlighted structural barriers to collaboration. Theme 2, Interprofessional Education (IPE) as the Foundation for Collaborative Practice, emphasised IPE as a key enabler of collaborative capacity; while its sub-theme IPE Develops Shared Philosophies and Values described the role of IPE in developing shared conceptual and philosophical foundations for collaborative care. Theme 3, Communication as the Strategy for Sustaining Collaborative Practice, underscored communication as central to day-to-day coordination; its sub-theme Leverage Role Overlap Through Effective Communication illustrated how communication supported role integration. Overall, clinicians described highly collaborative team care as desirable, and shaped by education, communication infrastructure, and systemic constraints. Introduction

Clinical practice guidelines for eating disorders recommend team outpatient treatment from health professionals representing the disciplines of medicine, psychology, and dietetics at minimum [1, 2]. Accordingly, team-based approaches are widely implemented in eating disorder care. Although team approaches vary considerably, different models of team care are commonly described along a continuum from multidisciplinary to interprofessional care [3–5].

Multidisciplinary care involves professionals from multiple disciplines contributing discipline-specific expertise toward treatment goals, typically supported by some level of communication and care coordination. The degree of teamwork and goal alignment within multidisciplinary teams can vary, and while shared goals and collaborative interaction may be present, clinical assessment, decision-making, and professional accountability generally remain situated within individual disciplines. As such, collaboration often occurs in parallel rather than through shared responsibility for treatment processes and outcomes [3–5]. Multidisciplinary approaches are widely used across healthcare and have been associated with improved clinical and economic outcomes [6]. However, evidence suggests that the presence of multiple disciplines alone does not guarantee improved patient outcomes. Rather, the effectiveness of multidisciplinary teams appears to depend on how collaboration is enacted in practice, including the quality of communication, coordination, and shared clinical processes within the team [7].

Interprofessional care represents a more integrated model of teamwork compared to multidisciplinary treatment and is characterised by intentional collaboration across professional boundaries. Interprofessional Collaborative Practice (IPCP) provides a framework for understanding and strengthening collaborative processes within teams. Within interprofessional models, Interprofessional Collaborative Practice (IPCP) refers to the enactment of interprofessional work through specific collaborative principles, including shared values and ethics, teamwork, high quality communication, and role clarity. Interprofessional treatment also involves explicit negotiation of responsibilities, shared decision-making, and collective accountability for outcomes [8]. Across diverse healthcare contexts, IPCP has been associated with improvements in clinician wellbeing, job satisfaction, and efficiency, alongside reductions in burnout and staff turnover [9].

Despite the widespread use and growing recognition of varied team approaches in eating disorder treatment, research in this area remains limited. A recent scoping review of team outpatient eating disorder treatment found that existing studies often lack methodological rigour, and that explicit examination of collaborative processes is sparse [10]. Whie emerging evidence suggests that coordinated team approaches are preferred by individuals with lived experience of eating disorders [11], clinicians’ perspectives on team outpatient eating disorder treatment remain underexplored.

This gap is particularly salient given the substantial challenges clinicians face when delivering eating disorder treatment. Clinicians must routinely navigate patient ambivalence, illness severity, and variable treatment outcomes, which contribute to frustration and high rates of burnout [12, 13]. Systemic factors such as poor team coordination and ineffective communication can further compound these challenges, adversely affecting both clinician wellbeing and treatment delivery [12, 13]. Accordingly, better understanding clinicians’ perspectives on team-based care may help identify opportunities to strengthen team functioning and support clinicians working in this complex field.

The aim of this study was to explore clinicians’ attitudes toward and experiences of team outpatient eating disorder treatment, including both multidisciplinary and interprofessional care. By capturing rich, contextual insights, the research addresses gaps in the literature regarding clinician experiences of team outpatient eating disorder treatment and contributes to emerging evidence on collaborative processes in this setting. The findings aim to inform future research to optimise team outpatient eating disorder treatment, support clinician wellbeing, and ultimately enhance care delivery for individuals with eating disorders.

Methods

This inductive, exploratory qualitative study was guided by Deweyan pragmatism, which emphasises that knowledge is grounded in experience and that the meaning of ideas is determined by their practical usefulness [14]. This stance informed the study design, data collection, analysis, and reporting, with a focus on generating actionable insights into clinicians’ attitudes and experiences regarding team outpatient eating disorder treatment [15]. Ethics approval was obtained from The University of Queensland (2020/HE003118).

Reflexivity and positionality

The lead author (M.B.) is a researcher-clinician with 10 years of experience delivering outpatient eating disorder treatment in multidisciplinary and interprofessional teams across varied settings. M.B.’s extensive experience collaborating with professionals from diverse disciplines, including medicine, psychiatry, psychology, and allied health, informed their understanding of the complexities of team-based eating disorder care. During analysis, M.B. reflexively engaged with the data, balancing their insider knowledge to ensure the findings were grounded in participants’ perspectives rather than their own experiences. Co-author G.H. brought over 20 years of experience as a clinician and researcher in eating disorder treatment. G.H.‘s depth of clinical experience, particularly with complex and varied team structures and clinical presentations, added a valuable dimension to the reflexive discussions. Co-author O.W., an experienced qualitative researcher, provided methodological expertise and an external perspective, supporting analytical rigour and adherence to qualitative standards. Together, the team brought a balance of professional and methodological expertise to the analysis.

Recruitment and sample size

Participants were recruited between June 2021 and May 2022 through convenience and snowball sampling, using professional networks and study advertisements on social media platforms targeting Australian eating disorder professionals. Eligibility was confirmed through an online screening questionnaire. Eligible participants were dietitians, mental health professionals, and general practitioners who self-identified as currently working within an eating disorder treatment team, had delivered outpatient care for at least two years, and reported an average caseload of five or more clients with eating disorders per week. Experience within a formally identified interprofessional team was not required. All participants were required to hold current registration with their relevant governing body. Exclusion criteria included regulatory practice restrictions (that is, conditions or limitations placed on professional registration by a national regulatory authority) and insufficient English proficiency to participate in interviews.

Sample size was informed by principles of information power [16]. Given the study’s focused aim, the specificity and expertise of the participant group, and the generation of rich, in-depth interview data, a moderate sample size was considered appropriate.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured interviews (range: 27–71 min duration, average: 48 min) conducted by M.B. via Zoom between June 2021 and May 2022. Semi-structured interviews were chosen for their ability to elicit rich, participant-driven insights while remaining guided by a flexible framework [17]. The interview guide used Interprofessional Education Collaborative’s core competencies for collaborative practice (values and ethics, roles and responsibilities, communication, and teamwork) as an organising framework (Supplementary Materials 1) [8]. This framework was selected because it provides a well-established set of competencies for describing collaborative processes in healthcare teams, enabling systematic exploration of team approaches to outpatient eating disorder treatment. The interview guide also incorporated broad, open-ended questions that allowed participants to draw on experiences across different team models, including explicit discussion of multidisciplinary and interprofessional approaches to treatment.

The guide was reviewed by a panel of qualitative researchers and piloted with co-author G.H. Participants were provided with definitions of multidisciplinary and interprofessional treatment, including IPCP, both before and at the start of the interview to ensure consistent and accurate use of terminology throughout the discussions. Transcriptions were generated using Otter.ai and manually checked for accuracy against the recordings by M.B. The full interview guide is included as Supplementary File 1.

Data analysis

The transcribed data were uploaded to NVivo (version release 1.7) (09/2022 Lumivero, CO). Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) where researchers: (1) familiarise, (2) generate codes, (3) generate initial themes, (4) develop and review themes, (5) refine, define and name themes, and (6) write the report [18]. RTA is a theoretically flexible approach that captures both the latent (underlying) meaning, as well as semantic (surface/explicit) meanings of each participant, and the whole dataset [18]. This allowed nuanced exploration of clinicians’ perspectives on team-based outpatient eating disorder treatment. Familiarisation involved M.B.’s repeated reading and listening to transcripts and documenting analytic insights. Coding involved M.B. identifying key data segments, focusing on both semantic and latent patterns, which were iteratively organised into themes. These were refined through collaborative discussions with O.W., ensuring alignment with the data. G.H. provided clinical expertise, contextualising themes within the broader landscape of eating disorder care. Trustworthiness [19] was supported through transcript validation, reflexive journaling, and peer debriefing [18]. This approach ensured that findings were both methodologically robust and grounded in participants’ experiences.

Results

Participant demographics

Of the 19 clinicians interviewed, 47% (n = 9/19) were dietitians, 37% (n = 7/19) were mental health professionals, and 16% (n = 3/19) were general practitioners. Participants averaged 8 years of experience delivering outpatient eating disorder treatment (range 2–25 years). Their caseloads predominantly comprised individuals diagnosed with Anorexia Nervosa, Bulimia Nervosa, and Other Specified Feeding or Eating Disorder, particularly Atypical Anorexia Nervosa. Binge Eating Disorder presentations were less frequent, with Avoidant Restrictive Food Intake Disorder noted as rare. Participants reported working primarily with adolescents and young adults, though clients ranged from adolescents to older adults.

Main themes

Three main themes and three sub-themes were identified. Theme 1, Preference for Interprofessional Ways of Working, reflects clinicians’ expressed preference for more integrated and collaborative approaches to team outpatient eating disorder treatment, with sub-theme Dismantle Hierarchies to Foster Trust, Respect, and Collaboration highlighting the perceived need to address systemic barriers for effective collaboration. Theme 2, Education as the Foundation for Collaborative Practice, emphasises the role of education in supporting clinicians’ capacity for collaborative practice, with sub-theme Interprofessional Education (IPE) Develops Shared Philosophies and Values identifying the importance of shared conceptual and philosophical foundations for interprofessional care developed through IPE. Theme 3, Communication as the Strategy for Sustaining Collaborative Practice reflects the essential role of communication in sustaining collaborative team functioning in practice, with the sub-theme Leverage Role Overlap Through Effective Communication, illustrating how effective communication optimises role integration. Figure 1 presents a thematic map illustrating the interrelated nature of the themes and sub-themes. Consistent with the pragmatist lens, results prioritise practical and actionable insights. Where participant quotes are included, they are accompanied by the participant code and discipline, denoted as ‘DT’ for dietitians, ‘MHP’ for mental health professionals, and ‘GP’ for general practitioners.

Thematic map

Theme 1: preference for interprofessional ways of working

Participants described a preference for highly collaborative ways of working within team outpatient eating disorder treatment, reflecting features commonly associated with IPCP. As one participant stated, highly collaborative approaches are “the most rewarding and safest way to work with eating disorders” (P15, DT). When reflecting on experiences across different team configurations, clinicians described collaborative treatment as “much more streamlined” (P11, DT), facilitating “sharing the load” (P17, GP), “sharing and lessening risk” (P2, MHP), and fostering “shared understanding” (P9, DT) among team members. High levels of cohesion and collaboration were perceived as supporting more consistent and coordinated care, reducing “confusion” and “splitting” (P4, MHP), and promoting a unified approach to patient care.

Clinicians perceived positive impacts on their professional experiences when working in more collaborative team configurations, particularly in relation to reduced workload pressures and increased professional support. Participants reported feeling “better supported, less likely to burn out” (P12, DT), “a whole lot more confident” (P4, MHP), and described collaborative working arrangements as “a whole lot less stressful” (P11, DT). As one participant noted, “It probably takes a bit of pressure off, to be honest… I feel supported in their care and not left on my own” (P16, GP).

Informal supervision and peer collaboration within coordinated teams were frequently cited as beneficial, with one participant highlighting, “There’s informal, regular supervision between clinicians, which supports continuous learning and reflective practice” (P15, DT). Collaborative team environments were also seen as beneficial for setting boundaries. As one participant explained, “My boundaries are better… it’s that sharing, genuine sharing of the load… which helps me not try to do too much” (P8, DT). Additionally, highly collaborative approaches were viewed as time-efficient, with one participant stating, “I don’t have to work as hard… that saves me time” (P7, GP). Collectively, these features were described as contributing to a more fulfilling and sustainable practice environment.

Participants also described perceived benefits of highly coordinated and collaborative team approaches for clients, particularly in fostering a sense of containment and perceived improvements in outcomes. “The client feels better supported” (P12, DT) and “better understood” (P8, DT), noted participants, with one emphasising, “interprofessional approaches provide more containment for the client” (P18, MHP). Participants linked this sense of containment to perceived improvements in symptom trajectory. As one participant explained, “When I’ve worked with clients within those kinds of spaces, I see a quicker reduction in symptoms” (P4, MHP). Summarising the overall impact, one participant stated, “Ultimately, we have better outcomes for our clients… eating disorders require a whole treatment team… they require this wraparound approach” (P14, MHP).

Sub-theme 1a: dismantle systemic hierarchies to foster trust, respect, and collaboration

Flat hierarchies were viewed as important for fostering the trust, respect, and collaboration required for effective collaborative practice within teams. By reducing power imbalances, flat hierarchies created an environment where team members felt valued and empowered to contribute meaningfully. As one participant noted, “Any sort of hierarchy reduces trust… [flat hierarchies] make you feel grounded… and more confident to disagree or propose something different” (P8, DT). However, participants identified systemic and structural barriers that promoted systemic hierarchies in practice.

Manualised treatment models, such as Enhanced Cognitive Behaviour Therapy (CBT-E) and Family Based Treatment (FBT), were cited as reinforcing hierarchical dynamics. While these models were acknowledged as important and evidence-based, they were perceived to elevate mental health professionals to lead roles, marginalising other disciplines. One participant explained, “CBT-E is that kind of classic individualised treatment, and probably how we ended up with some of these silos in the first place” (P18, MHP). Another added:

I feel as though the CBT-E model completely discounts the role of the GP, the role of the dietitian, and the role of an exercise physiologist in an eating disorder team and sort of empowers the mental health clinician to be doing it all. (P17, GP)

Beyond specific treatment models, broader professional hierarchies and funding structures were seen as limiting high quality collaboration. One participant noted, “Some medical professions… [say] ‘This is what’s happening, and this is what’s best’… that certainly leaves me, as a practitioner, feeling overlooked,” adding “I think it’s systemic… funding models… may have a role to play” (P13, MHP). These findings highlight the systemic nature of hierarchies within healthcare and their impact on collaborative practice within team eating disorder treatment.

Theme 2: interprofessional education as the foundation for collaborative practice

Participants described IPE as foundational to supporting more collaborative and integrated approaches to team-based outpatient eating disorder care. IPE refers to occasions when two or more professions learn about, from, and with each other in order to enable effective collaboration and improve health outcomes [20]. IPE was viewed as facilitating the development of shared knowledge, skills, and ways of thinking that supported collaboration practice across disciplines. However, participants also highlighted that inconsistent understanding and application of collaborative models remained a key challenge. In particular, the blurred or interchangeable use of the terms multidisciplinary and interprofessional was perceived as a barrier to recognising and enacting more collaborative forms of practice. As one participant observed, “We’re just still so much in that multidisciplinary space” (P6, DT). Another noted, “I think we use both words kind of interchangeably, which to me is not really the correct approach because it outlines two completely different ways of practising” (P1, DT).

To address this, participants emphasised the need for targeted strategies to promote interprofessional practice, with education identified as essential. Calls were made to integrate principles of IPCP into university curricula, as one participant explained, “If you look at a university education of anyone working with eating disorders… [it should] foster the idea of an interdisciplinary approach from the start and build that into the education of those clinicians” (P15, DT). Beyond university training, ongoing collaborative professional development was considered beneficial. “Collaborative professional development is incredibly important… instead of siloing our learning… we need interdisciplinary professional development” (P1, DT). Similarly, interprofessional supervision was also highlighted as a key enabler for embedding IPCP. “You need supervision… that brings you together as a team” (P18, MHP). Together, these education-based strategies were seen as foundational to supporting more integrated and collaborative forms of team eating disorder treatment.

The importance of embedding an interprofessional ethos across multiple levels of education and practice was summarised by one participant in stating, “If it comes through in terms of training at university… in the manualised approaches… [and] Credentialing, that this is our best practice… you’re constantly reflecting and thinking… Am I working this way? Or am I working in a silo?” (P14, MHP). In this context, ‘Credentialing’ refers to the Australia and New Zealand Academy of Eating Disorders’ Credential, an Australian program that formally recognises clinician competency in evidence-based eating disorder treatment through an accreditation process.

Sub-theme 2a: interprofessional education develops shared philosophies and values

IPE was identified as essential for developing the shared treatment philosophies and values that supported collaborative practice. Establishing a shared philosophy required team members to align their understanding and approach to eating disorder treatment. As one participant explained, “We need to be on the same page about the way we formulate, understand, conceptualise, and treat eating disorders before we can do interprofessional treatment” (P6, DT). To achieve this, participants emphasised the importance of a “baseline shared level of training and understanding” to facilitate collaboration from the outset (P18, MHP).

Shared philosophies provided a cohesive framework that facilitated consistent care and reduced misunderstandings. Examples included the interprofessional application of evidence-based treatment models, trauma-informed care, and Health at Every Size (HAES) perspectives. Participants also highlighted that alignment in values and treatment approaches was vital for effective collaboration. As one participant explained:

It’s important to find people who have a similar value system and way of working to you… It’s so much harder to work when there are different people saying different things… or their approaches and values of working with people are different… It’s not going to be helpful for that person that you’re seeing to be in that team. (P17, GP)

Theme 3: communication as the strategy for sustaining collaborative practice

This theme describes how participants identified communication as a central strategy for sustaining collaborative team functioning in practice. Effective communication was characterised as proactive, frequent, dynamic, and respectful. Structured communication at pivotal junctures, such as the beginning, middle, and end of treatment, as well as during moments of stagnation or significant change, was considered essential. Regular updates on client progress, acuity, risk, and treatment plans were seen as beneficial for fostering collaborative problem-solving and maintaining treatment alignment. Communication frequency was seen as adaptable, increasing during periods of heightened client needs and decreasing as treatment stabilised.

Colocation and integrated documentation systems were considered optimal for efficient communication, allowing clinicians to stay informed, coordinate care, and engage in spontaneous collaboration. One participant explained:

It’s just a lot easier to do it when you’re all together… in a shared setting, where you have shared notes… you can look at the last session they might have had with another health professional and very clearly see what’s been spoken about, where they’re up to in treatment, and how you might best complement what they’re already doing… that, I think, is a huge advantage (P9, DT).

Team meetings were also seen as highly beneficial for fostering interprofessional collaboration and aligning care. One participant suggested teams should “have a team meeting each week to discuss the clinical cases, the waitlist, all of the client-centred stuff… progress reports, where we’re up to… does anyone need help? Do we need to have any joint sessions?… That sort of thing” (P18, MHP).

Informal communication channels, such as phone calls, emails, and impromptu conversations in shared spaces, were highly valued for their accessibility and capacity to strengthen treatment planning. As one participant noted:

It might be a conversation, a catch-up, a phone call, an email… just checking in on… where that person’s up to… [it offers] a sounding board… it’s more informal in that way, but definitely, extremely, extremely helpful to get a sense of how a client is doing. (P2, MHP)

In contrast, formal letters, while necessary for professional and regulatory compliance, were not described as sufficient on their own for fostering meaningful communication.

Despite its importance, participants cited barriers to communication, including time constraints, lack of remuneration, and the absence of secure communication platforms. One participant remarked, “Finding time, mutual time that works for everyone, can be hard” (P12, MHP) while another highlighted financial constraints: “We’re not paid for our time communicating, we’re not paid for our collaboration… that is a massive limitation” (P1, DT). Concerns about appropriate information-sharing systems were also raised, with one participant stating, “The method of communication is a real problem… I don’t think that there’s a secure way of sharing information.” (P7, GP).

Sub-theme 3a: leverage role overlap through effective communication

Participants identified role overlap between disciplines as a strength when supported by effective communication. As one participant explained:

There’s gonna [sic] be overlap. [Within interprofessional teams] no one person gets cranky about that overlap… because of the increased communication, they know exactly what’s happening… whereas [in] multidisciplinary [teams]… another clinician might get cranky that their turf has been trodden on” (P15, DT).

Overlapping roles, when underpinned by strong communication, was viewed as beneficial for clients. As one participant noted, “If we’re all on the same page, and they’re constantly getting the same message, I think that’s really helpful” (P11, DT). Another added, “Some level of overlap is actually a good thing… we’re all kind of giving the same message” (P2, MHP). Overall, effective communication was framed as the key strategy that transforms role overlap from a challenge into an asset, reinforcing more integrated and coordinated approaches to team-based care.

Discussion

This study qualitatively explored clinicians’ attitudes toward and experiences of team outpatient eating disorder treatment. Findings indicated that clinicians valued highly integrated and collaborative ways of working, describing features consistent with principles of IPCP, while also identifying systemic hierarchies as a barrier to effective collaboration. IPE was described as foundational to building collaborative capacity, particularly through the development of shared treatment philosophies, while communication emerged as the primary strategy for sustaining collaborative practice in day-to-day care.

The perceived benefits of collaborative team approaches identified in this study extend perspectives reported by individuals with lived experience of eating disorders [11]. Together, these findings suggest that collaborative treatment approaches incorporating IPCP principles may strengthen team outpatient eating disorder treatment for both clinicians and individuals with lived experience.

Despite valuing collaborative practice, clinicians described systemic hierarchical structures and manualised treatment models as a significant barrier to more integrated team care. Addressing this challenge may require greater consideration of how principles of interprofessional practice can be integrated within evidence-based psychological treatments. Emerging work, including an interprofessional adaptation of CBT-E [21], and interdisciplinary guidance for FBT [22] provide potential pathways for integration. Beyond treatment models, longstanding power structures in healthcare, such as the dominance of medicine over allied health, and funding models that prioritise certain disciplines were also perceived to constrain collaboration. These deeply embedded systemic factors may limit the feasibility of sustained interprofessional practice in outpatient eating disorder care [23].

To strengthen collaborative capacity in the eating disorder workforce, participants identified IPE as important. These strategies align with existing frameworks for interprofessional collaboration which position IPE as foundational to building a collaborative practice-ready workforce [24]. Early evidence from IPE programs in eating disorder treatment demonstrates promising benefits, including improved attitudes toward teamwork, enhanced communication, and greater confidence in collaborative practice [25–27]. With IPE recognised as an essential mechanism for IPCP, and early research showing promise, further investment is likely important [28].

Participants noted that an important outcome of IPE is the development of shared treatment philosophies and values within teams. Broader research confirms a unified approach enhances teamwork and clinical decision-making [29], while inconsistent conceptual frameworks across disciplines have been linked to fragmented eating disorder care [30]. Thus, targeted IPE to support shared treatment philosophies and values within eating disorder teams should be explored.

Effective communication was consistently recognised as the primary strategy for sustaining collaborative team functioning in practice. Findings closely align with lived experience perspectives, where communication was identified as central to collaborative care, enhancing engagement and treatment progress [11]. Moreover, the finding that colocation and integrated documentation systems are key facilitators of coordination is consistent with evidence that shared workspaces and electronic health records improve team coordination and decision-making [31]. Overall, results reinforce communication as a foundational component of collaborative team eating disorder treatment and suggest that strategies used to optimise communication practices in other healthcare settings may be applicable.

Despite the importance of communication, participants identified significant barriers in practice. Similar challenges in other healthcare settings arise from administrative burdens, high caseloads, and funding models that prioritise direct client contact over collaboration [32]. Similarly, broader research highlights clinicians’ dissatisfaction with existing communication systems, often leading to the use of insecure platforms which raises security and privacy concerns [33]. It is likely important to explore secure, low-burden communication systems which promote efficiency and responsiveness, while also considering professional documentation obligations that may vary across disciplines and regulatory contexts.

Clinician-identified strategies to support collaborative team care

Taken together, these findings highlight several practical considerations that may support more collaborative and sustainable practice in team outpatient eating disorder care. These include engaging in interprofessional education through university training, continuing professional development, and supervision; intentionally forming teams with shared values and treatment philosophies; and actively addressing professional hierarchies that may influence collaboration. Working in ways that uphold the principles of Interprofessional Collaborative Practice may further support effective teamwork. In addition, regular, timely, and flexible communication between clinicians, including informal exchanges alongside formal documentation, may help facilitate coordination, shared understanding, and the management of role overlap within teams.

Future research

This study highlights clinicians’ emphasis on collaborative team functioning in outpatient eating disorder treatment, alongside systemic barriers that may constrain more integrated approaches. Future research should examine the role of IPE in preparing clinicians for collaborative practice and explore how collaborative principles can be meaningfully integrated within established treatment models such as CBT-E and FBT, including assessment of feasibility and clinical impact. Further research is needed to evaluate the effects of more integrated collaborative approaches on clinician wellbeing, team functioning, and client outcomes, using longitudinal and quantitative designs. Given the central role of communication identified in this study, future work should also investigate structural and technology-based supports for collaboration, including secure documentation and messaging systems, colocation, and structured team meetings. At a systems level, research examining policy and funding mechanisms that enable and incentivise collaboration is warranted.

Strengths and limitations

This study’s reflexive thematic analysis, guided by a pragmatist lens, generated nuanced insights into clinicians’ experiences of collaborative processes in team outpatient eating disorder treatment and identified practice-relevant considerations to support effective team functioning. Inclusion of clinicians from multiple disciplines and with varied levels of experience strengthens the transferability of findings. However, several limitations should be noted. The moderate sample size (n = 19) was weighted toward dietitians and mental health professionals, with fewer general practitioners and no representation from other disciplines, which may limit broader applicability. Participation was limited to clinicians working within a team, potentially favouring perspectives more supportive of team approaches. Practice setting was not systematically collected as a participant characteristic; although most participants described working primarily in private practice, with some experience in publicly funded services, the absence of formal categorisation limits interpretation of how service context may have shaped perspectives. Despite these limitations, the study offers valuable insights into clinician perspectives on collaborative team care and identifies key areas for strengthening team outpatient eating disorder treatment across settings.

Conclusion

This study explored clinicians’ experiences of team outpatient eating disorder treatment, highlighting a clear preference for more interprofessional ways of working. IPE and communication were identified as central to establishing and sustaining collaborative team care, supporting shared treatment philosophies, role integration, and flatter team hierarchies. However, systemic and structural barriers continue to constrain the implementation of more integrated approaches. Together, these findings underscore the importance of strengthening educational, organisational, and communication structures to support sustainable and effective collaborative practice in team outpatient eating disorder care.
