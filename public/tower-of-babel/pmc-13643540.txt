Percutaneous Tricuspid Valve-in-Valve Implantation With Melon Seeding During Deployment
Authors: Alexander M Sellers, Shreyas Ravat, Jayme Bennetts, Ross Roberts-Thomson, Ehsan Khan, Jean Engela, Jayne Langran, Ajay R Sinhal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBioprosthetic tricuspid valves are prone to structural deterioration over time. In patients with multiple prior sternotomies and functioning left-sided prostheses, repeat surgery may carry prohibitive risk, prompting consideration of transcatheter alternatives.Case SummaryA 42-year-old patient with rheumatic heart disease and a history of 3 prior sternotomies for mitral, aortic, and tricuspid valve replacement presented with recurrent admissions for diuretic-resistant right heart failure complicated by congestive hepatopathy (Child–Pugh B cirrhosis). Imaging demonstrated severe stenosis of a bioprosthetic tricuspid valve, with preserved left-sided valve function. The patient underwent successful percutaneous tricuspid valve-in-valve implantation, resulting in marked symptomatic improvement and significant reduction in transvalvular gradients on echocardiography.DiscussionCurrent guidelines support reintervention for symptomatic bioprosthetic valve dysfunction. In high surgical risk patients, percutaneous tricuspid valve-in-valve implantation represents an effective and less invasive alternative to repeat surgery.Take-Home MessagePercutaneous tricuspid valve-in-valve replacement is a viable therapeutic option for failed tricuspid bioprostheses in carefully selected high-risk patients.Key words: rheumatic heart disease, tricuspid valve, valve replacement

History of Presentation

In 2023, a 42-year-old Aboriginal Australian man with a complex history of rheumatic heart disease with multiple prior aortic, mitral and tricuspid valve replacements presented to a remote clinic in the Northern Territory of Australia with a 1-month history of NYHA functional class III dyspnea, progressive abdominal distension and leg swelling. Before presentation, the patient had been nonadherent with medications and fluid restriction and disclosed ongoing heavy alcohol use.

Clinical examination was remarkable for elevated jugular venous pressure with prominent a wave and a positive hepatojugular reflux. There was a mechanical S1 and S2 with a loud ejection systolic murmur heard through the precordium. There was reduced air entry and dullness in the base and tense ascites with a palpable liver and spleen edge. There was pitting edema to the level of the midshins.

Past Medical History

In 2001, the patient underwent their first operation for rheumatic heart disease, receiving an aortic (23 mm Carpentier Edwards Perimount Magna Valve), mitral (27 mm Carpentier Edwards Pericardial Valve), and tricuspid valve (29 mm Carpentier Edwards Pericardial Valve) replacement. Intraoperatively, rheumatic involvement of the tricuspid valve was noted with a fixed septal leaflet that was fibrosed with minimal mobility, leaving a fixed central orifice.

The patient presented again in 2009 with exertional dyspnea, with echo showing degeneration of all 3 valves. A redo valve replacement of all 3 valves was performed, with implantation of a new aortic (21 mm On-X mechanical valve), mitral (25/33 mm On-X mechanical valve), and tricuspid (29 mm St Jude Biocor Porcine Prosthesis) valves.

In 2012, the patient developed staph aureus prosthetic aortic valve infective endocarditis complicated by an aorto-atrial fistula with dehiscence noted posteriorly within the left coronary cusp and extending down to the aorto-mitral curtain posterior to the mitral prosthesis and left atrium. This required extended antimicrobial therapy and a further redo aortic valve replacement (21 mm St Jude Medical Masters Valve) (Table 1).

Other comorbidities of note included hypertension, type II diabetes mellitus, permanent atrial fibrillation, hepatitis B, and prior pulmonary meliodosis complicated by septic shock.

Differential Diagnosis

Presentation was consistent with right heart failure (RHF) secondary to severe tricuspid stenosis with a mean gradient of 23 mm Hg with elevated pulmonary pressures (estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure of 62 mm Hg). The right ventricle was of normal size with mild dysfunction with an RVs’ of 8 cm/s. Left ventricular function was preserved, the mitral valve was functioning well, and there were increased gradients noted across the aortic valve with a mean gradient of 34 mmHg. There was moderate right atrial (RA) dilatation with a RA area of 32 cm2.

Gastroenterology reviewed and diagnosed the patient with Child–Pugh class B cirrhosis, felt to be predominantly driven by RHF, with alcohol misuse a contributing factor.

Investigations

The patient’s 12-lead electrocardiogram showed rate-controlled atrial fibrillation. Their N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide was elevated at 3,699 ng/L. The patient was diuresed and had a therapeutic abdominal paracentesis drain inserted, which removed 10 L of fluid. Following diuresis, the gradients across the tricuspid valve improved to 11 mm Hg. A right heart catheterization showed elevated RA pressures (V 29, mean 24 mm Hg), severely elevated pulmonary pressures (pulmonary artery [PA] 76/28, mean 44 mm Hg) with elevated mean pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (24 mm Hg) and a high normal cardiac index (3.79 L/min/m2) with elevated transpulmonary gradient (20 mm Hg). Despite over 3 weeks of aggressive intravenous diureses, the patient had refractory RHF.

Management

A multidisciplinary Heart Team determined that the patient was failing medical therapy and required intervention on the tricuspid valve for symptom relief. The elevated gradient across the aortic prosthesis (mean 34 mm Hg) and the potential need for double-valve intervention were considered; however, in the absence of structural valve degeneration or symptoms attributable to left-sided dysfunction, this was not deemed the primary driver of the presentation. Combined surgical reintervention was considered high risk, as this would represent a fourth sternotomy in the context of extensive adhesions. The predicted operative mortality was 22% according to the TRI-SCORE model; therefore, a percutaneous approach was favored.

Several uncertainties remained. First, would treating the tricuspid stenosis relieve symptoms? Second, would relieving the stenosis worsen the pulmonary pressures and pulmonary arterial hypertension? Last, given the right ventricular (RV) dysfunction on echocardiography, would relieving the tricuspid stenosis and increasing RV filling overload the right ventricle and precipitate calamitous RHF? After extensive discussion and detailed explanation of the risks to the patient, it was decided to proceed with the percutaneous tricuspid valve-in-valve replacement.

An Edwards SAPIEN 3 Ultra 29 mm was selected. Preprocedural computed tomography demonstrated unfavorable inferior vena cava–RA angulation, making transfemoral venous access unsuitable. A right internal jugular vein (RIJV) approach was therefore selected to achieve a more coaxial trajectory across the tricuspid prosthesis, which had been easily crossed via this route during the prior right heart catheterization.

Left femoral artery and left femoral vein sheaths were inserted as a contingency access for Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation support in the event of calamitous RHF. A 6-Fr sheath was inserted in the RIJV under U.S. guidance. The St Jude Biocor 29 mm tricuspid valve was crossed with a 5-F multipurpose catheter, and the 0.035 wire was exchanged for Safari via a pig tail catheter. The 6-Fr sheath was then upgraded to a 14-Fr eSheath, which was advanced to the level of the right atrium.

Balloon valvuloplasty was performed using a 12 × 40 mm balloon under rapid ventricular pacing to facilitate valve crossing and improve compliance of the stenotic prosthesis. The balloon-valve assembly was inserted with the Edwards logo facing upward. Initial attempts to deliver the balloon-valve into position were unsuccessful because coaxial alignment could not be achieved. The balloon-valve assembly was therefore retracted into the sheath and realigned with the valve immediately beyond the sheath tip. The SAPIEN frame was fully advanced across the bioprosthesis before retracting the pusher.

During deployment, forward displacement (“melon-seeding”) was observed with initial inflation. This required a double inflation during the same pacing run, with a partial inflation initially, followed by a realignment of the balloon and a second full inflation to secure the valve (Video 1). The valve was subsequently postdilated with an additional +2.0 mL to optimize prosthesis expansion (Video 2).

The SAPIEN 3 Ultra 29 mm valve was successfully deployed, with no complications as a consequence of the melon seeding. The RIJV access site was closed with a figure-of-8 suture and manual compression. Postdeployment, the mean gradient improved from 9 mm Hg to 3.5 mm Hg on transesophageal echocardiogram (Figure 1). There was only trivial transvalvular tricuspid regurgitation with an estimated pulmonary artery (PA) systolic pressure of 25 mm Hg + right atrial pressure (Video 3). Invasive PA gradients were 65/7 mm Hg with a mean of 27 mm Hg postvalve deployment.

The patient had an uneventful postoperative course and reported marked symptomatic improvement. Postoperative transthoracic echo demonstrated only mild RV dysfunction with a tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) of 1.3 cm and RVs’ 6 cm/s. Estimated PA systolic pressure was 44 mm Hg, which represented an improvement from the invasive gradient seen on the right heart catheterization preprocedure. The right ventricle coped well and likely improved in contractility with increased filling pressures.

Discussion

Relief of tricuspid stenosis in the presence of pulmonary hypertension and RV dysfunction presents important hemodynamic considerations. As highlighted by the procedural uncertainties in this case, elimination of a fixed inflow obstruction may lead to an acute increase in RV preload, with the potential to precipitate RV dilation and decompensation in a ventricle already operating near the limits of its reserve. This risk may be further amplified in the setting of elevated pulmonary vascular resistance, where increased forward flow could exacerbate pulmonary pressures and RV afterload. Conversely, relief of tricuspid obstruction reduces RA pressure and systemic venous congestion, which may improve hepatic congestion. In carefully selected patients, these competing physiological effects may result in a net clinical benefit. In the present case, the patient demonstrated sustained symptomatic and hemodynamic improvement following intervention.

Surgical bioprosthetic valves have a finite lifespan before developing valve deterioration. Current European Society guidelines give a class I indication for reintervention for patients with bioprosthetic failure in whom there are symptoms and echocardiographic evidence of valve dysfunction not attributable to thrombosis.1 Similar recommendations are reflected in the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines, which support intervention for prosthetic valve dysfunction.2 Surgical reintervention remains the standard for durable valve replacement, particularly in younger patients. However, in this case, the cumulative risk associated with a fourth sternotomy, extensive adhesions, and comorbid cirrhosis rendered surgery prohibitively high risk. In such scenarios, less invasive transcatheter approaches have emerged as an alternative strategy.

Percutaneous tricuspid valve-in-valve implantation for bioprosthetic deterioration is a relatively less common procedure compared with left-sided intervention such as aortic valve-in-valve implantation, and consequently the evidence base is far less robust. There are case reports and case series that have shown encouraging outcomes, demonstrating that the procedure is technically feasible and that it may provide patients symptomatic relief.3,4 Although large-scale randomized clinical trials and long-term outcome data specific to tricuspid valve-in-valve procedures remain limited, recent randomized clinical trial evidence demonstrating improved symptoms and quality of life with transcatheter tricuspid intervention compared with medical therapy in patients with native tricuspid regurgitation has prompted the European Society to upgrade its recommendation for transcatheter tricuspid valve-in-valve implantation in bioprosthetic valve failure at intermediate or high surgical risk from class IIb to class IIa.5,6

This case highlights the importance of the individualized Heart multidisciplinary team approach. At the age of 42, this patient had already had 3 sternotomies with significant adhesions as a consequence. Their comorbidity placed them at considerable operative risk. Percutaneous tricuspid valve-in-valve implantation improved this patient’s symptoms and allowed them, at the very least, to avoid a fourth sternotomy for now. Although RV failure was a threat, the patient tolerated the implant well from a hemodynamic perspective.

Follow-Up

The patient was discharged on day 5 and subsequently transferred to a local health center for convalescence. He remained well at 12-month follow-up without further cardiac hospitalizations.

Conclusions

Percutaneous valve-in-valve implantation is a technically feasible option in the management of failed tricuspid bioprosthesis. It can be an attractive option in patients for whom surgical intervention is prohibitively high.
