Efficacy and safety evaluation of intravenous esketamine in alleviating perioperative depressive symptoms in Parkinson’s disease: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Wei Tan, YuanYuan Gao, Dan Su, Lin Ji, Shu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDepressive symptoms frequently afflict individuals with Parkinson’s disease (PD). The current study was consequently conducted to determine the efficacy of low-dose intravenous esketamine in managing postoperative depressive symptoms, thereby exploring its potential mechanisms of action in PD patients subjected to non-intracranial surgery.MethodsIn this randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, patients were allocated to receive either low-dose esketamine (0.25 mg/kg IV bolus at induction followed by 0.125 mg/kg/h infusion) or placebo. The primary outcome was the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) score assessed preoperatively and on postoperative days 1, 3, and 7. Secondary outcomes included serum levels of 5-HT, norepinephrine (NE), IL-6, and TNF-α, postoperative pain (VAS), and adverse events.ResultsA total of 53 patients were enrolled, including 27 patients (mean age:72.93 ± 6.59, 14 males, mean BMI 22.87 ± 2.71) in the control group (C group), and 26 patients (mean age: 70.46 ± 7.85, 11 males, mean BMI 23.32 ± 3.40) in the esketamine group (ES group). Compared to the control group, ES group showed significantly lower SDS scores at all postoperative time points (p < 0.01), with sustained antidepressant effects through day 7. Esketamine selectively increased 5-HT (p = 0.0005) but not NE levels. It also significantly reduced proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6, p < 0.001; TNF-α, p < 0.001) and VAS scores (p < 0.001). The incidence of adverse events was comparable between groups.ConclusionLow-dose intraoperative intravenous esketamine safely mitigates postsurgical depressive symptoms in PD patients following non-intracranial surgery, potentially through synergistic modulation of serotoninergic activity and neuroinflammatory responses.Trial registrationhttp://www.chictr.org.cn, ChiCTR2500098063, Registration date: 3 March 2025. Retrospectively registratered.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder whose neuropathological hallmarks are accompanied by a heterogeneous spectrum of non-motor symptoms. Among these, Depression affects 40% to 50% of individuals with PD, as reported by epidemiological studies [1, 3, 11], and it significantly diminishes their quality of life while potentially accelerating disease progression. During the perioperative period, the incidence of depressive symptoms in patients with PD is further heightened due to the combined effects of surgical trauma, physiological stress, and anesthesia. This exacerbation significantly impairs both postoperative recovery and long-term quality of life. Postoperative depression is particularly common in this population, likely resulting from the interplay of surgical stress, neuroinflammatory responses, and further dysregulation of dopaminergic and serotonergic systems [1, 25]. Current clinical management of perioperative depression in PD remains challenging. Although first-line antidepressants such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) show some efficacy, they are limited by a delayed onset of action—often requiring several weeks to take effect—which fails to meet the urgent need for rapid mood stabilization in the perioperative setting. Moreover, their potential cardiovascular and metabolic side effects warrant particular caution during this critical period [26].

Esketamine has emerged as a promising therapeutic agent due to its rapid-onset antidepressant action [13]. It thus shows significant promise for clinical application in areas such as treatment-resistant depression (TRD) and perioperative depression management [18, 20]. Esketamine exerts its effects through a complex mechanism, which entails regulating glutamate-mediated neurotransmission, enhancing BDNF expression, and engaging in intricate crosstalk among dopamine, serotonin, and norepinephrine systems [16, 22, 23]. Esketamine offers significant advantages over conventional antidepressants, primarily due to its rapid antidepressant efficacy. In contrast to conventional antidepressants, which have a delayed onset of action spanning several weeks, esketamine exerts its therapeutic effects within hours, offering new hope for patients with TRD. Furthermore, esketamine has received FDA approval specifically for TRD, establishing its unique position in the therapeutic landscape [9]. Otably, its rapid efficacy profile has been successfully translated into other clinical settings requiring swift intervention. A systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that, as an adjuvant to electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), esketamine significantly increased the 24-hour remission rate and reduced depression scale scores compared to placebo, with no significant difference in discontinuation rates, providing moderate-certainty evidence for its short-term efficacy [4]. This established role in augmenting another rapid-acting somatic therapy further underscores its therapeutic potential in time-sensitive contexts like the perioperative period.

However, despite the established antidepressant efficacy of esketamine in patients with TRD [9] and its emerging promise in perioperative settings [6, 17], a significant research gap remains regarding its application in specific subgroups—particularly for the prevention and treatment of perioperative depression in patients with Parkinson’s disease. The underlying pathophysiology of PD, including dopaminergic neurodegeneration, neuroinflammation, and dysregulation of neurotransmitter networks, may interact with the pharmacodynamic profile of esketamine. To date, high-quality clinical studies directly examining the use of esketamine for postoperative depression in PD patients are limited. Most mechanistic insights are extrapolated from animal models or non-PD human studies, leaving the efficacy, safety, and precise mechanisms of action of esketamine in this context unclarified. This article aims to evaluate the potential value and mechanistic foundations of esketamine in managing perioperative depression in individuals with PD.

Methods

Ethical approval and trial registration

This study was designed as a randomized, double-blind clinical trial. It received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Yancheng Third People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2024-92; Date: 1 November 2024) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial protocol was prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration ID: ChiCTR2500098063). Prior to enrollment, written informed consent was obtained from every participant. The study was conducted and reported according to the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines.

Patient selection and eligibility

From November 2024 to August 2025, 58 patients diagnosed with Parkinson’s.

disease and scheduled for elective non-intracranial surgery under general anesthesia at Yancheng Third People’s Hospital were enrolled as shown in Fig. 1. All patients met the Chinese Diagnostic Criteria for Parkinson’s Disease, and the diagnosis was confirmed by at least one board-certified neurologist.

Flow chart for patient enrollment

Inclusion criteria included

(1) Age ranging from 60 to 90 years; (2) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) I-III; (3) Diagnosed of Parkinson’s disease; (4) Scheduled for non-intracranial surgery under general anesthesia; (5) Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. (6) Hoehn & Yahr stages I–III in Parkinson’s disease.

Exclusion criteria included

(1) History of severe depression (SDS standard score > 72), or other psychiatric disorders interfering with depressive symptom assessment (including use of antidepressant medications); (2) Severe preoperative cardiac, hepatic, or renal dysfunction (Cardiac: NYHA functional class III or IV; Hepatic: Child-Pugh class B or C; Renal: eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²); (3) BMI > 35 kg/m²; (4) Communication barriers or inability to cooperate; (5) Uncontrolled hypertension (baseline > 180/100mmHg); (6) Known allergy or contraindication to esketamine.

Withdrawal criteria included

(1) Participant request or non-compliance; (2) Intraoperative severe hypertension or tachycardia necessitating discontinuation of esketamine; (3) Postoperative transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU); (4) Loss to follow-up.

Randomization and blinding

Participants who met the inclusion criteria were randomly assigned, utilizing a computer-generated sequence, to either the ES group or the control group in a 1:1 ratio. An independent researcher generated the random allocation sequence and safeguarded concealment by utilizing sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. After concealment was secured, the study drugs were prepared by an anesthesia nurse according to the predetermined randomization list; the nurse had no subsequent role in patient care or outcome assessment. For the ES group, esketamine (Hengrui Pharmaceuticals; 50 mg/2 mL) was diluted with saline to 20 mL, while controls received 20 mL of saline, both prepared in identical syringes. All syringes were labeled with patient name, date, and administration route. To maintain blinding, patients, attending anesthesiologists, postoperative assessors, and data analysts were all kept unaware of group assignments. Emergency unblinding envelopes were prepared for use in the event of serious adverse events.

Intervention

A standardized anesthetic protocol was applied to all patients. After the induction of anesthesia, patients in the ES group were administered an intravenous bolus of 0.25 mg/kg esketamine, which was immediately followed by a continuous infusion at 0.125 mg/kg/h maintained until 30 min before the end of surgery. Patients in the control group received a volumetrically equivalent infusion of normal saline adhering to the same schedule. The study medications were prepared and delivered via infusion pumps that were masked to the attending clinical team.

The common adverse reactions associated with esketamine primarily involve transient central nervous and cardiovascular effects, such as dissociative symptoms, drowsiness, elevated blood pressure, and tachycardia during initial administration. Through standardized management measures—including slow infusion, close monitoring, and symptomatic treatment (e.g., short-acting antihypertensives or antiemetics)—these adverse events are generally dose-dependent, transient, predictable, and manageable. Severe or persistent sequelae are rare when the medication is used appropriately.

Anesthetic management

After standard preoperative fasting, all patients underwent continuous monitoring of electrocardiography, invasive arterial pressure, pulse oximetry, temperature, and bispectral index (BIS). Anesthesia was induced according to a standardized protocol using intravenous sufentanil (0.4 µg/kg), remimazolam (0.05 mg/kg), propofol (1–2 mg/kg) or etomidate (0.3 mg/kg) at the anesthesiologist’s discretion, along with rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg) to facilitate tracheal intubation via video laryngoscope. Mechanical ventilation was initiated with the following settings: FiO₂ 100%, fresh gas flow 1.5–2 L/min, respiratory rate 12 breaths/min, tidal volume 6–8 mL/kg, I:E ratio 1:2, PEEP 5 cmH₂O, and end-tidal CO₂ maintained at 35–45 mmHg. Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane (1-1.5%), propofol (2–4 mg/kg/h), and remifentanil (0.01–0.05 µg/kg/min), titrated to sustain a BIS value between 40 and 60. Neuromuscular blockade was maintained with rocuronium to achieve a train-of-four (TOF) count < 1. Hemodynamic stability was managed per protocol, aiming to keep mean arterial pressure within ± 20% of baseline and heart rate between 60 and 100 bpm, using vasoactive agents such as phenylephrine, ephedrine, urapidil, atropine, and esmolol as needed. All anesthetic agents were administered by a consistent anesthesia team. Sevoflurane was discontinued 30 min before the end of surgery, and all anesthetics were ceased at skin closure. Neuromuscular blockade was reversed with sugammadex (1–2 mg/kg) upon return of spontaneous respiration or response to commands. Extubation was performed once patients were conscious, with adequate airway reflexes and tidal volume > 5 mL/kg. Postoperative analgesia was provided via a patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) pump containing sufentanil (2 µg/kg) and nalbuphine (40 mg), diluted with normal saline to a total volume of 100 mL, set to a background infusion of 2 mL/h, bolus dose of 5 mL, and a 15-minute lockout interval. Rescue analgesia with intravenous flurbiprofen axetil (50 mg) was available for pain scores > 4 on the postoperative pain (VAS), and postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) was treated with intramuscular metoclopramide (10 mg).

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the Self-rating Depression Scale score, measured before surgery and on postoperative days 1, 3, and 7. The SDS is a self-reported instrument with 20 items, each rated from 1 to 4 based on symptom frequency, providing a total score range of 20–80. SDS scores were categorized according to the Chinese normative data: normal (< 53), mild (53–62), moderate (63–72), and severe (> 72) depression (Duan, Q. Q., & Sheng, L, 2012). For analysis, depressive symptoms were dichotomized as present if the SDS score was ≥ 53.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes included: Serum biochemical markers: Levels of 5-HT, NE, IL-6, and TNF-α measured preoperatively and at 24 h postoperatively using commercial ELISA kits. Pain intensity: Assessed on postoperative day 1 (POD 1) with a 10-cm visual analog scale (VAS), where 0 represented “no pain” and 10 indicated “the worst pain imaginable.” Scores were categorized as 0 (none), 1–3 (mild), 4–6 (moderate), 7–9 (severe), and 10 (excruciating). Other safety outcomes: Adverse events recorded within the first 24 h after surgery.

Statistical analysis

Sample size

A power analysis using PASS 2021 software was conducted to determine the sample size, with the postoperative SDS score as the primary endpoint. Based on preliminary data, 18 patients with PD undergoing surgery were randomized to receive either low-dose intravenous esketamine or saline. The mean ± standard deviation of the SDS score change at postoperative day 7 was 38.31 ± 9.54 in the ES group and 46.00 ± 1.58 in the control group, and setting α = 0.05 (two-tailed), power = 0.8, and a 1:1 allocation, the analysis yielded a requirement of 26 subjects per group. To accommodate a 10% attrition rate, the final enrollment target was set at 58 patients (29 per group).

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed continuous data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous data are expressed as the median (first quartile, third quartile) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data are presented as frequency (%) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

For longitudinal measures involving repeated assessments (including SDS scores, intraoperative mean arterial pressure, heart rate, and various serum indicators), data were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models. The models specified each measurement as the dependent variable, with fixed effects for Group, Timepoint, and their interaction, and included a random intercept for subjects. The models handled missing data directly under the missing-at-random assumption. Between-group comparisons at specific time points were based on model estimates and adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value < 0.05.

Results

Participants

As shown in Fig. 1, of the 65 individuals screened, 7 were excluded for not meeting all inclusion criteria. Among the 58 randomized participants, 5 (8.6%) were lost to follow-up prior to the primary endpoint assessment at postoperative day 7, resulting in 53 participants being included in the per-protocol efficacy analysis. All 58 randomized participants who received the allocated intervention were included in the safety analysis.

Baseline characteristics

The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the two groups are summarized in Table 1. The C and ES groups were well-matched, with no significant differences observed in age (p = 0.325), height (p = 0.368), weight (p = 0.965), body mass index (p = 0.573), ASA physical status classification (p = 0.778), or sex distribution (p = 0.585) and preoperation medication (p = 0.885), MMSE score (p = 0.385), SDS score (p = 0.784), ensuring baseline comparability following randomization. The types of surgical procedures performed were comparably distributed across the two groups (p = 0.897, chi-square teste). Orthopedic and general surgeries were the most common procedures in both cohorts. An intergroup comparison revealed no significant differences in the categories of surgery performed.

Primary outcome

Table 2; Fig. 2 showed no significant difference was detected in preoperative SDS scores when comparing the ES and C groups (p = 0.676), confirming comparable baseline mood status prior to intervention. SDS scores decreased progressively and significantly from baseline to postoperative day 7 (POD 7) in the ES group (p < 0.001). In contrast, the C group exhibited no significant change in SDS scores across the postoperative period (p = 0.249). Between-group comparisons revealed that the ES group had significantly lower SDS scores than the C group at all postoperative assessment points: POD 1 (95% CI: 4.37 – 7.94, p < 0.001), POD 3 (CI༚7.25 – 10.40, p < 0.001), and POD 7 (95% CI༚8.19 – 11.06, p < 0.001). The magnitude of the between-group difference increased over time, suggesting a sustained and potentially enhancing beneficial effect of esketamine on depressive symptoms during the first postoperative week. These results indicate that intraoperative esketamine administration significantly improves postoperative depressive symptoms in PD patients undergoing non-intracranial surgery. The progressive reduction in SDS scores beyond the immediate postoperative period suggests that the antidepressant effect of esketamine is not merely transient but sustains and potentially strengthens during the critical first week after surgery.

Secondary outcome

Table 3; Fig. 3A, B showed preoperative serum levels of 5-HT and NE were comparable between the ES and C groups (5-HT: 95% CI [-33.80–21.17], p = 0.650; NE: 95% CI [-0.94–0.75], p = 0.826). A significant increase in serum 5-HT levels from preoperative baseline to postoperative day 1 was observed exclusively in the ES group (p < 0.001). The C group showed no significant alteration in 5-HT levels (p = 0.162). The between-group comparison at POD 1 confirmed that a significantly larger rise in 5-HT levels was seen in the ES group versus controls (95% CI [-78.13 – -23.16], p < 0.001). Regarding NE levels, no statistically significant within-group changes were found from the preoperative period to POD 1 in either the C group (p = 0.562) or the ES group (p = 0.864). Consistently, the between-group comparisons showed no significant differences in NE levels either preoperatively (95% CI [-0.94–0.75], p = 0.826) or on POD 1 (95% CI [-1.21–0.49], p = 0.409). These results suggest that intraoperative esketamine administration significantly enhances serotoninergic activity, as indicated by the elevated serum 5-HT concentration postoperatively. However, it does not appear to exert a significant effect on the noradrenergic system in the early postoperative period.

Table 4; Fig. 3C, D showed Baseline serum concentrations of IL-6 and TNF-α were comparable between the ES and C groups (IL-6: 95% CI [-11.62–12.14], p = 0.965; TNF-α: 95% CI [-2.58–3.07], p = 0.866), indicating mparable baseline inflammatory status. As expected, surgery triggered a significant inflammatory response. Both groups exhibited a substantial increase in IL-6 and TNF-α levels on postoperative day 1 (POD 1) compared to their respective preoperative baselines (p < 0.001 for within-group comparisons). However, the magnitude of this increase was markedly attenuated in the ES group. Relative to controls, the ES group showed marked reductions in IL-6 (95% CI [14.97–38.73], p < 0.001) and TNF-α (95% CI [5.23–10.89], p < 0.001) on POD 1. These findings demonstrate that intraoperative esketamine administration significantly mitigated the surgery-induced increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines, suggesting a modulatory effect on the neuroinflammatory response in the early postoperative period.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of intraoperative low-dose intravenous esketamine on perioperative depressive symptoms in a real-world cohort of patients with Parkinson′s disease and elucidated its potential mechanisms of action. The key findings demonstrate that an intravenous bolus of 0.25 mg/kg at induction followed by a continuous infusion of 0.125 mg/kg/h until 30 min before the end of surgery significantly reduced SDS scores in PD patients postoperatively, with the antidepressant effect sustained through postoperative day 7. This improvement was accompanied by elevated serum serotonin levels, attenuated inflammatory responses (reduced IL-6 and TNF-α concentrations), and decreased pain perception. Furthermore, esketamine administration was associated with maintained hemodynamic stability during surgery, with no significant increase in postoperative adverse events, underscoring its favorable safety profile. These results indicate that the use of esketamine during general anesthesia in PD patients can provide stable hemodynamic control, exert preventive antidepressant effects, and does not raise the risk of postoperative complications.

In our study, administration of esketamine led to significantly reduced Self-Rating Depression Scale scores at postoperative days 1, 3, and 7, indicating an improvement in perioperative depressive symptoms among PD patients. This symptomatic improvement may be attributable to both the potential therapeutic effect of the surgical procedure on the primary disease and the rapid antidepressant action of esketamine [26]. Although some studies have reported no significant reduction in Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) scores by postoperative day 3 or 7 after esketamine use in patients undergoing abdominal tumor surgery, emerging evidence suggests that patients with higher preoperative depression scores—as measured by validated rating scales—are at greater risk for postoperative depression and may derive greater benefit from ketamine treatment [6].Various depression rating scales are used in clinical and research settings, which can be broadly categorized into self-report and clinician-administered instruments. Common examples include the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD/HDRS), the Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS), the Self-Rating Depression Scale, and the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) [18]. Among these, the SDS is widely adopted in clinical practice due to its brevity, ease of administration, and established reliability and validity. It effectively captures four core domains of depressive symptomatology: affective symptoms, somatic disturbances, psychomotor changes, and psychological distress. Notably, the SDS has been validated for assessing depressive symptoms in PD patients and demonstrates good reliability [24].

Consistent with previous findings in non-PD populations—such as studies by Han et al., [5] and Wen et al., [21] which reported reduced perioperative depression incidence with esketamine in postpartum and oncology patients—our results suggest that its antidepressant effect may extend to patients with neurodegenerative disorders. This extension is further supported by high-level evidence: a recent large-scale meta-analysis (67 RCTs, n = 11,553) concluded that intravenous administration, the route used in our study, is the most effective for improving depressive symptoms and preventing postoperative depression, though it may carry a higher risk of dizziness [7]. By applying this optimized route to a novel and specific population (PD patients with postoperative depression), our study provides preliminary evidence for esketamine’s utility in a previously understudied comorbidity context, underscoring the need for future large-scale trials in this group. Nonetheless, these findings must be interpreted with caution given the limitations of our primary assessment tool, the SDS, including its inability to differentiate etiologies of depression, susceptibility to subjective bias, and potential insensitivity to mild or atypical symptoms [10].

Serotonin, a key inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system, plays a crucial role in mood regulation. Reduced 5-HT signaling, commonly observed in patients with depression, forms the rationale for conventional antidepressants such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), which aim to elevate synaptic 5-HT concentrations and alleviate symptoms. However, growing evidence indicates that depression is not merely a “5-HT deficiency disorder.” Multiple neurotransmitter systems—including norepinephrine, dopamine, GABA, and glutamate—are deeply involved, reflecting a complex, multifactorial etiology [1, 25]. Emerging evidence further suggests that dopamine D2 receptors may participate in emotional regulation via the gut–brain axis, offering a novel systems-level perspective on esketamine’s antidepressant mechanisms [22]. In the present study, esketamine administration specifically increased perioperative serum 5-HT levels in Parkinson’s disease patients, while norepinephrine concentrations remained unchanged. This finding suggests that its antidepressant effect could involve selective modulation of the serotonergic system. Such a mechanism aligns with the complex dysregulation of dopamine, 5-HT, and norepinephrine systems implicated in PD-related depression [1]. While previous research has emphasized esketamine’s role as an NMDA receptor antagonist—exerting rapid antidepressant effects through modulation of glutamatergic transmission, enhanced synaptic plasticity, and promotion of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression [14]—converging evidence points to the lateral habenula (LHb) as a pivotal hub. Crucially, conditional knockout of NMDARs in the LHb completely abolishes the antidepressant effects of esketamine and blocks its elevation of hippocampal serotonin and BDNF levels, identifying the LHb as a primary target [2]. Furthermore, enhanced 5-HT transmission from the raphe nuclei to the LHb has been shown to mediate psychological resilience, suggesting that 5-HT regulation of LHb activity is integral to the antidepressant process [15].

In this context, our data highlight an alternative hypothetical pathway. It is plausible that esketamine could potentiate glutamatergic projections from the prefrontal cortex to the dorsal raphe nucleus, which might subsequently lead to activation of 5-HT neurons and potentially promote 5-HT release [2, 12]. Collectively, these findings support a model where esketamine’s rapid action may involve both direct modulation of the hyperactive LHb (via NMDAR antagonism) and indirect regulation through ascending 5-HT pathways, ultimately converging to restore emotional circuit homeostasis.

The absence of significant change in norepinephrine levels may be explained by the advanced degeneration of the locus coeruleus–noradrenergic system in PD, which likely limits its compensatory capacity [21]. Consequently, we hypothesize that activation of the serotonergic system could be one contributing mechanism underlying the therapeutic effect of esketamine observed in PD-associated depression in this study.

Beyond potential serotonergic modulation, esketamine might also improve mood through indirect mechanisms involving the dopaminergic system. Preclinical studies suggest that esketamine increases extracellular dopamine concentrations in limbic regions by modulating glutamate and opioid receptors [16]. We speculate that a similar process could occur in our clinical context, potentially compensating for PD-related dopaminergic deficits and thereby indirectly influencing 5-HT and norepinephrine neurotransmission.

Moreover, the significant reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α observed in the esketamine group is compatible with the proposal that anti-inflammatory mechanisms may contribute to its antidepressant action. However, given the limited biomarker sampling, this association remains speculative and requires further validation. Neuroinflammation is a core pathological feature of PD and comorbid depression. Pro-inflammatory cytokines can disrupt monoamine synthesis and release and activate the indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) pathway, thereby reducing 5-HT biosynthesis [1]. By inhibiting microglial activation and attenuating peripheral inflammatory responses, esketamine may help restore serotonergic neurotransmission [8, 16, 19].

Our study also observed that PD patients who received esketamine showed improved postoperative pain scores compared to the control group, an effect that may have contributed synergistically to their overall mood recovery. Beyond the established NMDA receptor antagonism, the underlying mechanism may also involve modulation of monoaminergic pathways within descending pain-modulatory circuits.

Regarding safety, although transient adverse effects such as dissociation and visual disturbances have been reported in the literature [17], in the present trial all adverse reaction symptoms resolved spontaneously or with basic supportive measures within 24 h. Furthermore, throughout the entire observation period, no dissociative symptom events reaching clinical concern or requiring intervention were reported in either group. We hypothesize that this may be related to the preemptive, low-dose, slow intravenous infusion strategy adopted in this study. These safety observations are consistent with several recent systematic evaluations [1, 8], indicating that when administered under controlled medical supervision, esketamine demonstrates an acceptable safety and tolerability profile in surgical PD patients.

Limitations

This study has several potential limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings and generalizability. (1) Selection bias: The exclusion of high-risk PD patients, such as those with severe preoperative depression may limit the applicability of our results to the population most likely to benefit from preventive intervention. (2) As an intervention with prominent psychoactive properties, the observed treatment effects may include a contribution from placebo and contextual factors. Although our controlled design helps mitigate this, it cannot fully dissociate the specific pharmacological effect from nonspecific therapeutic elements. Future studies employing active placebo controls or more sophisticated blinding methods could further elucidate this aspect. (3) Sample size: The relatively small sample size (53 analyzed patients) may restrict statistical power to detect subtle differences in secondary outcomes or rare adverse events. (4) The follow-up period was limited to 7 days, focusing on the acute phase. Consequently, these findings cannot inform the longer-term sustainability of the antidepressant effect, the relapse prevention potential, or the delayed safety profile of esketamine in this population. Studies with extended follow-up (e.g., 4 weeks, 3 months) are warranted. (5) Evidence gap: Existing related literature is based on animal models or studies in non-PD populations (Vecchia et al., 2021), and high-quality clinical research focusing specifically on postoperative depression in PD patients remains scarce. (6) An additional limitation of this study is the retrospective trial registration. Although the study design and data collection were conducted following rigorous methodological standards independent of the registration timing, retrospective registration may introduce potential selection bias. (7) The primary outcome relied on a single, patient-reported depression scale (SDS) without cross-validation with a clinician-rated instrument (e.g., HAM-D). While the SDS is a validated and sensitive tool for capturing subjective symptom burden, the inclusion of an objective clinician assessment in future studies would strengthen the reliability of efficacy evaluations (8) This study did not perform subgroup analyses based on the severity of Parkinson’s disease motor symptoms. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to explore this aspect to clarify the consistency of efficacy across different disease stages. Consequently, future studies should prioritize rigorously designed, targeted clinical trials to clarify the optimal dosing, timing, and long-term efficacy of esketamine in this specific population. Additionally, integrating neuroimaging and molecular assays will help elucidate its mechanisms of action and interaction with PD pathophysiology.

Conclusion

Our findings establish that low-dose intraoperative esketamine produces preliminary evidence of short-term antidepressant efficacy in surgical PD patients without increasing adverse events. This effect is likely attributable to a multimodal mechanism, integrating serotonergic enhancement, dopaminergic modulation, anti-inflammatory activity, and analgesic properties. This work thereby identifies a novel therapeutic strategy for managing perioperative neuropsychiatric symptoms in Parkinson’s disease. While our findings suggest the potential efficacy of esketamine in this setting, they must be considered exploratory and warrant validation in larger, multicenter trials with longer follow-up.
