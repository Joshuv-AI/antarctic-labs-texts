Abstract
This paper deals with the global control properties of the Cahn-Hilliard equation posed on the d d -dimensional flat torus 𝕋 d \mathbb{T}^{d} . We first prove that the system is small-time globally approximately controllable using a control supported on finitely many Fourier modes, following an approach based on geometric control theory techniques. Next, we show that the corresponding linearized problem is null-controllable with a localized control, where the control region is an arbitrary measurable set of positive Lebesgue measure. Our analysis is based on quantitative propagation of smallness estimates for the free solution. Furthermore, when d ∈ { 1 , 2 , 3 } , d\in\{1,2,3\}, we ensure the local null controllability for the nonlinear system via a fixed-point argument. Finally, by combining these two results, we establish the global null controllability of the Cahn-Hilliard equation. In the first phase, the control is localized in Fourier modes, whereas in the second phase, it is spatially localized on a set of positive Lebesgue measure.



Keywords:
Global controllability, Geometric control theory, Agrachev-Sarychev method, Observability from measurable sets, Propagation of smallness, Lebeau-Robbiano approach, Source term method.

1.1
Motivation

The Cahn–Hilliard equation is a fundamental model for describing phase separation in binary mixtures and other segregation-driven processes. Originally introduced by Cahn and Hilliard
[
9
,
11
]
, it provides a thermodynamically consistent description of spinodal decomposition, a mechanism by which an initially homogeneous material becomes unstable and separates into distinct phases. Beyond this classical setting, this equation is also used to describe various pattern-formation phenomena, including nucleation and growth
[
10
,
6
]
. Over the years, its range of applications has broadened substantially, with Cahn–Hilliard type models appearing in contexts as diverse as copolymer morphology
[
53
]
, image inpainting
[
7
]
, tumor-growth modelling
[
52
,
29
]
, biological applications
[
21
,
22
]
, two-phase fluid flows
[
41
,
1
,
36
]
, interacting populations
[
16
]
, astronomical structures
[
60
]
, and thin-film dynamics
[
59
]
. This diversity of applications has established the Cahn–Hilliard equation as a central tool for studying segregation-like behaviour across disciplines. General overviews of its modelling foundations and mathematical theory can be found in the surveys
[
51
]
and
[
45
]
.

In its classical form, the Cahn–Hilliard equation describes the evolution of an
order parameter
u
, representing the difference in concentration between the
components of a binary mixture. On the periodic domain
\mathbb{T}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
),
the system takes the form

\begin{cases}u_{t}=\kappa\Delta\mu&\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d},\\
\mu=-\alpha\Delta u+f(u)&\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d},\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
T>0
is a fixed time horizon,
\mu
is the chemical potential,
\kappa>0

is the mobility,
\alpha>0
is related to the surface tension at the interface,
and
f
is the derivative of a double-well potential
F
whose minima correspond
to the stable phases of the mixture. A typical (and thermodynamically relevant)
choice is

F(s)=\tau(s^{2}-1)^{2},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{so that}\hskip 20.00003ptf(s)=4\tau(s^{3}-s),

(1.2)

where
\tau>0
controls the strength of the phase-separation tendency.
1
1

1

In physical models, one often normalizes the concentrations of the two components in the
mixture so that each lies in
[0,1]
and their sum is identically equal to
1
. Under this
convention the order parameter
u
is defined as their difference, and therefore takes values
in
[-1,1]
. More refined thermodynamic descriptions use a singular (logarithmic) potential

whose domain is
(-1,1)
, which enforces this constraint at the level of the model.
From both the physical and mathematical viewpoints, this logarithmic potential is the
relevant one, but its singular nature makes the analysis substantially more delicate. For this
reason, the regular polynomial approximation (
1.2
) is widely used in the
mathematical literature, even though the resulting Cahn–Hilliard dynamics do not prevent
solutions from leaving
[-1,1]
; see, e.g.,
[
44

.

Periodicity is commonly adopted in the modelling of phase separation (see, e.g.,
[
26
,
50
,
44
]
), both for physical and practical reasons. On one hand, it is consistent with the mass-conserving character of the equation: in the absence of boundary fluxes, we have

\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}u(t,x)\,\textnormal{d}x=0,

which reflects the preservation of the total mass of the mixture. On the other hand, periodic domains eliminate the need to prescribe boundary behaviour whose physical relevance may be limited in bulk models, while offering numerical and computational advantages such as the use of efficient Fourier-based methods and the avoidance of artificial boundary layers without altering the essential phase-separation dynamics
[
17
,
39
,
31
]
.

From a mathematical viewpoint, the Cahn–Hilliard system is a nonlinear fourth-order parabolic equation for the order parameter
u
. In order to obtain a well-posed evolution problem, it must therefore be equipped with appropriate initial data. In this work, we consider an initial condition of the form

u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),

(1.3)

posed on the periodic domain introduced above.

Beyond the modelling aspects, our interest lies in the analysis of the Cahn–Hilliard equation from the perspective of control theory. Roughly speaking, to control a dynamical system governed by a PDE means to act on its trajectories, typically through a forcing term, a boundary input, or another external mechanism, in order to reach a established goal. More precisely, a fundamental question in PDE control is whether one can guide the system to a desired target (either
exactly
or
approximately
) within a finite time, or in particular whether the system can be driven exactly to zero (
null controllability
). Classical references on these notions include the works
[
43
,
18
,
61
]
.

From the control viewpoint, the two notions that we consider in this work are

approximate controllability
and
null controllability
. To introduce these notions, we consider
a controlled version of the Cahn–Hilliard system (
1.1
) in which an internal control
h=h(t,x)
acts on a region
\omega\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d}
(usually an nonempty open subset or even the full domain
\mathbb{T}^{d}
)

\begin{cases}u_{t}=\kappa\Delta\mu+\mathbf{1}_{\omega}(x)\,h(t,x)&\text{ in }(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d},\\
\mu=-\alpha\Delta u+f(u)&\text{ in }(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d},\end{cases}

(1.4)

with the initial condition (
1.3
). In (
1.4
),
\mathbf{1}_{\omega}
stands for the indicator function of the set
\omega
.

Formally,
approximate controllability
asks whether, for any prescribed target profile

u_{\mathrm{tar}}
and any
\epsilon>0
, one can choose a control
h
so that the
solution of the controlled system (
1.4
) satisfies

\|u(T,\cdot)-u_{\mathrm{tar}}\|_{X}<\epsilon,

for a suitable functional space
X
. In other words, the question is whether the evolution starting from
u_{0}
can be steered
arbitrarily close to a desired configuration at the final time.
2
2

2

In physical models, this may be interpreted as the possibility of influencing the evolution from the initial state
u_{0}
so that the configuration at time
T
comes close to a prescribed distribution of phases.

The notion of
null controllability
concerns the particular case in which the target is
the zero state, that is, whether there exists a control
h
such that

u(T,x)\equiv 0\,\text{ in }\,\mathbb{T}^{d}.

Thus, one seeks to determine whether the dynamics starting from
u_{0}
can be driven all the way to the zero at time
T
.
3
3

3

In models for phase–separating mixtures, the state
u\equiv 0
corresponds to a spatially uniform distribution of the two components. For this reason, steering the system to the zero state is often interpreted as reaching a homogeneous configuration.

The global controllability of fourth-order systems, and in particular of the Cahn–Hilliard equation, remains largely open and challenging. Existing contributions, such as
[
20
]
,
[
32
]
,
[
35
]
, and
[
40
]
, typically rely on restrictive assumptions concerning the spatial dimension, the structure of the nonlinearity, or the choice of boundary conditions. Among these works, only
[
32
]
addresses the Cahn–Hilliard equation (
1.4
) in one spatial dimension with the physically relevant nonlinearity
f(s)=u^{3}-u
, whereas the remaining papers study control properties of fourth-order parabolic equations with different and more tractable nonlinearities (globally Lipschitz or power-type with good sign). We also note that the result in
[
32
]
is local in the sense that it requires small initial data, while the other works achieve global controllability under specific assumptions on the on the nonlinear terms. Altogether, these limitations highlight the intrinsic difficulties of controlling nonlinear fourth-order dynamics globally, particularly in higher dimensions.

To the best of the author’s knowledge, no existing result addresses global controllability properties of the Cahn–Hilliard equation (
1.4
). In particular, the analysis of small-time global controllability properties for such systems remains largely unexplored, which motivates the present study.

1.2
Global approximate controllability

Building on the discussion above, we now introduce a more precise formulation of the approximate controllability problem. To simplify the exposition, we rewrite the controlled Cahn–Hilliard equation as

\begin{cases}u_{t}+\Delta^{2}u+\Delta u=\Delta(u^{3})+\eta&\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d},\\[2.84526pt]
u(0)=u_{0},&\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d}.\end{cases}

(1.5)

This formulation corresponds to choosing
\kappa=\nu=1
and
\tau=1/4
in (
1.1
)–(
1.2
). We note that a similar analysis can be performed for general values of the positive constants
\kappa
,
\nu
and
\tau
.

In (
1.5
),
\eta=\eta(t,x)
corresponds to a control applied on the whole domain
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. With this function, our aim is to steer the solution from any given initial data
u_{0}
arbitrarily close to a target function
u_{1}
in a suitable norm. In more detail, for any nonnegative integer
k
, let us denote by
H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
the usual Sobolev space defined on the
d
-dimensional torus (see
Section

2.1
) and consider the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by

\mathcal{K}=\left\{\left(1,0,\ldots,0\right),\left(0,1,\ldots,0\right),\ldots,\left(0,\ldots,0,1\right)\right\}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

Let us denote the linear space

\mathscr{H}_{0}=\spn\{1,\sin(x\cdot k),\cos(x\cdot k)\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}.

(1.6)

Our interest is the following notion of approximate controllability.

Definition 1.1
.

The Cahn–Hilliard equation (
1.5
) is
globally
H^{k}
-approximately controllable
at time
T>0
by an
\mathscr{H}_{0}
-valued control if the following holds: for any
u_{0},u_{1}\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a control

\eta\in L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathscr{H}_{0})

such that the corresponding solution
u
to (
1.5
) satisfies

\|u(T)-u_{1}\|_{H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}<\epsilon.

If this property holds for every arbitrarily small time horizon
T>0
, we say that the Cahn–Hilliard equation (
1.5
) is
small-time globally
H^{k}
-approximately controllable
.

Our first main result reads as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
be such that
k>d/2
. Then, the Cahn-Hilliard equation (
1.5
) is small-time globally
H^{k}
-approximately controllable.

Theorem
1.2
represents an important advancement in the study of controllability for the Cahn–Hilliard equation and, to the best of our knowledge, provides the first global control result for (
1.5
). The theorem highlights several significant aspects. First, the control acts only on finitely many Fourier modes. More precisely, the result holds on a
d
-dimensional domain, with a control space of dimension
2d+1
that is independent of the system parameters as well as the initial and target states. On the other hand, we establish the global approximate controllability without any restriction on the control time
T>0
, in fact,
T
can be taken arbitrarily small.

The methodology we use to prove Theorem
1.2
follows ideas from the geometric control approach. This strategy was introduced for Navier-Stokes and Euler equations on the torus in the works of Agrachev and Sarychev
[
2

3
]

[
56
]
, compressible and incompressible Euler equations
[
48
,
49
]
, the viscous Burgers equation
[
57
,
58
]
, the Boussinesq equations
[
46
]
.

There has also been substantial progress for nonlinear scalar equations on periodic domains.
In particular, the approach in
[
47
]
plays an important role in our arguments: the overall structure of our proof is inspired by their strategy, suitably adapted to the fourth–order setting of the Cahn–Hilliard dynamics. Further advances include results for the Benjamin–Bona–Mahony equation
[
33
]
, the Kadomtsev–Petviashvili–I equation
[
34
]
, the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation
[
28
]
, the Korteweg–de Vries equation
[
13
]
, the Kawahara equation
[
4
]
, the Zakharov–Kuznetsov–Burgers equation
[
14
]
, and the Camassa–Holm equation
[
15
]
. Furthermore, for small-time global controllability in the framework of bilinear control, we refer to
[
23
]
for the Schrödinger equation,
[

[

1.3
Global null controllability

Building on the approximate controllability result presented in the previous section, we now turn to an application: global null controllability. The argument relies essentially on this property, combined with a local null controllability result that allows us to reach the null state exactly.

Let
d\in\{1,2,3\}
. Our second main result can be stated as follows.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
T>0
and
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).
Then there exists a control
\eta\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
such that equation (
1.5
) satisfies
u(T,\cdot)=0
in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Moreover, the control is of the form

\eta(t,\cdot)=\begin{cases}0&t\in(0,\epsilon),\\
\eta_{1}(t,\cdot)&t\in(\epsilon,\delta),\\
\mathbf{1}_{\omega}\eta_{2}(t,\cdot)&t\in(\delta,T),\end{cases}

(1.7)

where
\epsilon
and
\delta
are arbitrary positive constants satisfying
0<\epsilon<\delta<T
,
\eta_{1}(t,\cdot)\in\mathscr{H}_{0},
and
\eta_{2}\in L^{\infty}(\delta,T;L^{\infty}(\omega))
where
\omega\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
is any measurable set with positive Lebesgue measure.

Theorem
1.3
establishes small-time null controllability for (
1.5
). This property is obtained by combining the global approximate controllability result from the previous section with a local null controllability argument that acts on a fixed subdomain. The overall strategy is organized in three stages: an initial waiting time, a finite-dimensional control acting on the whole torus, and finally a localized control that drives the solution exactly to zero. A detailed description of this construction is provided in
Section

1.4
.

To address the null controllability strategy for the Cahn–Hilliard equation, we rely on the extensive body of work developed over the last two decades on spectral and observability estimates for parabolic equations on measurable sets with positive Lebesgue measure, see, for instance,
[
54
,
5
,
27
,
62
]
. These results show that the classical geometric requirement that the control region be open can be significantly weakened, thanks to recent advances in spectral geometry and quantitative unique continuation. More precisely, for linear parabolic equations (such as the heat or Stokes equations
[
12
]
), a comprehensive theory now exists: observability holds from sets of the form
E\times\omega
with
E\subset(0,T)
and
\omega\subset\Omega
of positive Lebesgue measure.

More recently, observability for the heat equation has been established even from control sets of zero measure, provided they have positive fractional Hausdorff content, see
[
8
,
30
]
. This yields the sharpest geometric conditions currently known. In line with these developments, we extend our global null controllability results to controls supported on certain sets of zero measure. For clarity, recall that the
s
-Hausdorff content of a set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is

\mathcal{C}^{s}_{\mathcal{H}}(E)=\inf\Big\{\sum_{j}r_{j}^{\,s}:E\subset\bigcup_{j}B(x_{j},r_{j})\Big\},

and its Hausdorff dimension is defined as

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(E)=\inf\{s:\mathcal{C}^{s}_{\mathcal{H}}(E)=0\}.

We may then state the following global null controllability result:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
d\in\{1,2\}
,
T>0
,
0<\epsilon<\delta<T,

m>0
and
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).
Then the solution of the system (
1.5
) satisfies
u(T,\cdot)=0
in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
by means of a control
\eta\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{-2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
of the form (
1.7
)
where,
\eta_{1}\in L^{\infty}(\epsilon,\delta;\mathscr{H}_{0})
,
\eta_{2}\in L^{\infty}(\delta,T;H^{-2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
is supported in
(\delta,T)\times\omega
,
\omega\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
is any closed measurable set satisfying
\mathcal{C}^{d-s}_{\mathcal{H}}(\omega)>m
for some
0<s<1
.

1.4
Strategy of the proof of main results

We begin this section by presenting the overall strategy used to establish our two main controllability results.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
relies on three key ingredients:
stability estimates (
2.4
), asymptotic property (
3.2
), the saturation property (
3.5
). Using these properties, we establish small-time global approximate controllability of (
1.5
). The proof technique is inspired by the approach in
[
47

. A brief idea is that, thanks to the asymptotic property, the system can be driven over sufficiently small time intervals from

to an arbitrarily small neighborhood of any point in

By iterating this procedure together with using the saturation property, we reduce the approximate controllability problem to steering the system toward targets lying in a finite-dimensional affine subspace translated by the initial data. The key step is the use of large controls over short intervals, exploiting a specific asymptotic property of the nonlinear dynamics. Finally, continuity of the solution in time, together with stability estimates (see
2.4
), allows us to conclude global approximate controllability at any prescribed time.

•

To prove global null controllability, we use a strategy that combines global approximate controllability with local null controllability; see

. More precisely, we first prove small-time global approximate null controllability by which we steer the trajectory arbitrarily close to the origin, and then we apply the local null controllability result to reach the origin exactly.

The main idea behind the proof of local null controllability is to reduce the nonlinear problem to the linearized one and to establish the null controllability of this linearized system. Our controllability result applies when the control region is a measurable set of positive Lebesgue measure, or even for certain control sets of zero measure. The overall structure of the argument is inspired by the works
[
8
]
and
[

which combine a propagation of smallness of free solution of the linearized system and a Lebeau–Robbiano type strategy
[

]
. Furthermore, We establish an appropriate control cost estimate which enables the application of source term method of

Preliminaries

This section is devoted to preliminaries concerning the functional setting and several key well-posedness results.

2.1
Functional framework

Consider
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}):=L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R})
as the collection of real-valued
square-integrable functions on the torus. For
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, its Fourier coefficients are given by

u_{k}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}u(x)\,e^{-ix\cdot k}\,dx,\hskip 20.00003ptk\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},

and we write the corresponding Fourier series expansion

u(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}u_{k}\,e^{ix\cdot k},

where the series converges to
u
in the
L^{2}
sense. For real-valued functions we also have

u_{-k}=\overline{u_{k}}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

For
p\in[1,\infty]
, we denote by
L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
the usual Lebesgue spaces on the

d
-dimensional torus, endowed with the norms

\|u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}:=\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|u(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{1/p},\;\;1\leq p<\infty,\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}:=\esssup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}|u(x)|.

For
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the spaces

H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d}):=\left\{u=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}u_{k}\,e^{ix\cdot k}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\;:\;\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|u_{k}|^{2}\,(1+|k|^{2})^{s}<\infty\right\}.

The induced norm is
\|u\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|u_{k}|^{2}\,(1+|k|^{2})^{s}
.

Remark 2.1
.

For brevity, and whenever the underlying domain is clear from the context, we
omit the explicit indication of the domain in the norms.
For instance, we write
\|u\|_{L^{p}}
or
\|u\|_{H^{s}}
instead of

\|u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}
or
\|u\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}
.
Whenever the domain plays a role or ambiguity may arise, we will instead specify it explicitly.

The homogeneous operator
(-\Delta)^{s/2}
acts as

(-\Delta)^{s/2}u=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|k|^{s}\,u_{k}\,e^{ix\cdot k},

with norm

\|(-\Delta)^{s/2}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|k|^{2s}\,|u_{k}|^{2}.

For
s>0
, the
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
-norm defined above satisfies the equivalence

\|u\|_{H^{s}}\simeq\|u\|_{L^{2}}+\|(-\Delta)^{s/2}u\|_{L^{2}}.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the operator defined as follows:

\begin{cases}\mathcal{A}u=-\Delta^{2}u-\Delta u,\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}),\\
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}):\mathcal{A}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\right\}.\end{cases}

(2.1)

It is easy to see that,
\mathcal{A}
generates a strongly continuous semigroup

\{\mathcal{S}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
on the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.
For any
s\geq 0
and
T>0
, we define the space

X_{T}^{s}=C([0,T];H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{s+2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))

endowed with its natural Hilbert norm

\|u\|_{X_{T}^{s}}=\|u\|_{C([0,T];H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{s+2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}.

Flow, asymptotic and saturation properties

This section presents several technical results that play a central role in proving our main theorems. We begin with a standard result concerning the properties of the nonlinear semigroup.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume

,
\varphi\in H^{k+2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and

\eta\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,\infty;H^{k-2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))

1.

Let

. The flow property holds:

\mathcal{R}_{\delta}(u_{0},\varphi,\eta)=\mathcal{R}_{\delta}(u_{0}+\varphi,0,\eta)-\varphi.

(3.1)

2.

Let us define

\eta(s)=\begin{cases}\eta_{1}(s)&s\in[0,t_{1}),\\
\eta_{2}(s)&s\in[t_{1},t_{1}+t_{2}),\\

(3.2)

Proof.

Let us denote
v=u+\varphi,
where
u
is the solution of (
2.2
). It can be checked that

satisfies the following equation

\begin{cases}v_{t}+\Delta^{2}v+\Delta v=\Delta(v^{3})+\eta,\\

By uniqueness of the solution, we have

This implies
\mathcal{R}_{\delta}(u_{0}+\varphi,0,\eta)=\mathcal{R}_{\delta}(u_{0},\varphi,\eta)+\varphi.

Let

and let

denote the solution of (

In particular,

By the uniqueness of solution, for any

we have

(3.3)

Using (

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}(t_{1}+t_{2}+t_{3},0,u_{0},\eta)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\!\left(t_{3},\,0,\,\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\left(t_{1}+t_{2},t_{1},\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\left(t_{1},0,u_{0},\eta_{1}(\cdot)\right),\,\eta_{2}(\cdot)\right),\,\eta_{3}(\cdot+t_{1}+t_{2})\right)

.

This asymptotic property (

belonging to the affine space

(3.6)

\gamma^{\delta}>1

\delta^{-1}\mathcal{T}^{\delta}>1.

Therefore, solution
v
of (
3.9
) is well-defined in
[0,1]
and finally from (
3.17
) we have

\displaystyle\|v(1)\|^{2}_{H^{k}}\leq C_{1}\delta^{\frac{1}{3}}\longrightarrow 0\text{ as }\delta\to 0^{+}.

Hence, we have proved that solution
u(t)
of (
3.4
) is well-defined in
[0,\delta]
and the following limit holds

u(\delta)=\mathcal{R}_{\delta}(u_{0},\delta^{-\frac{1}{3}}\varphi,{\delta}^{-1}\eta)\to u_{0}+(\eta+\Delta(\varphi^{3}))\text{ in }H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\text{ as }\delta\to 0^{+}.

∎

3.2
Saturation property

This section is devoted to the so-called saturation property, which serves as a key ingredient together with
3.2
for proving Theorem
1.2
.
Let us recall the finite dimensional subspace
\mathscr{H}_{0}
of
H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
defined in (
1.6
). We now define a non-decreasing sequence of finite dimensional subspaces as follows

\mathscr{H}:=\mathscr{H}_{0},\hskip 20.00003pt\mathscr{H}_{j}:=\mathcal{F}(\mathscr{H}_{j-1}),\ j\in\mathbb{N}^{*},\hskip 20.00003pt\mathscr{H}_{\infty}:=\cup_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathscr{H}_{j-1}

(3.18)

where

\mathcal{F}(G):=\spn\left\{f_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta(f_{i}^{3}):f_{0},f_{i}\in G,\,\textnormal{for some }m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\right\}

(3.19)

for some set
G
.

Definition 3.4
.

We say that
\mathscr{H}
is saturating if
\mathscr{H}_{\infty}
is dense in
H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.

For any subset
I\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, let us denote the set of trigonometric functions

A_{I}:=\{\sin(x\cdot p),\cos(x\cdot p):p\in I\}.

(3.20)

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the set of indices

I_{N}=\left\{p=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\sum_{i=1}^{d}|p_{i}|\leq N+1\right\}.

Observe that

\mathscr{H}_{0}=\spn A_{I_{0}}.

The goal of this section is to prove the following result.

Proposition 3.5
.

The space
\mathscr{H}_{0}
is saturating.

The proof relies on a characterization of certain elements in the subspace
\mathscr{H}_{j}
defined in (
3.18
). This characterization is stated in
3.9
. In what follows, we will make use of the following auxiliary lemmas, whose proofs can be obtained by direct computations.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
p,q,r
be real-valued scalar functions. Then the following identity holds

\begin{split}pqr&=\left[\left(\frac{p}{2}+\frac{q}{3}+\frac{r}{4}\right)^{3}-\left(\frac{p}{2}+\frac{q}{3}-\frac{r}{4}\right)^{3}-\left(\frac{p}{2}-\frac{q}{3}+\frac{r}{4}\right)^{3}+\left(\frac{p}{2}-\frac{q}{3}-\frac{r}{4}\right)^{3}\right]\\
&=:f_{1}(p,q,r)^{3}+f_{2}(p,q,r)^{3}+f_{3}(p,q,r)^{3}+f_{4}(p,q,r)^{3}\\
&=:Q(p,q,r).\end{split}

Lemma 3.7
.

For any real-valued scalar functions
p,q,r
, we have
Q(p,q,-r)=-Q(p,q,r)
.

Hereinafter, for any nonnegative real numbers
a<b
, we define the set
\left\llbracket a,b\right\rrbracket:=[a,b]\cap\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

For any subset
I\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, if
p,q,r\in A_{I}
, then
f_{i}(p,q,r)\in\spn A_{I}
for
i\in\left\llbracket 1,4\right\rrbracket
. Additionally, we have

\Delta Q(p,q,r)=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\Delta\left(f_{i}(p,q,r)^{3}\right).

We are in position to prove the following result.

Proposition 3.9
.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the following holds

\spn A_{I_{N}}\subset\mathscr{H}_{N}.

Proof.

We proceed by induction.

– Case
N=1
. From the notation introduced in (
3.20
), note that

A_{I_{1}}=\left\{\sin(x\cdot p),\cos(x\cdot p):\sum_{i=1}^{d}|p_{i}|\leq 2\right\}.

By construction (see (
3.18
) and (
3.19
)), we already have
\spn A_{I_{0}}\subset\mathscr{H}_{1}
. Therefore, to prove that
\spn{A_{I_{1}}}\subset\mathscr{H}_{1}
, it
suffices to focus on the functions whose indices satisfy
\sum_{i=1}^{d}|p_{i}|\leq N+1=2
. For these elements, we will show that each of them can be generated (up to a constant) by means of the ansatz (cf. (
3.19
))

f_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta(f_{i}^{3})\textnormal{ with }f_{0},f_{i}\in\mathscr{H}_{0}\textnormal{ and some }m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}.

(3.21)

Using the symmetry properties of the sine and cosine functions, it suffices to consider the cases with
p=e_{i}\pm e_{j}
, where
e_{i},e_{j}
are vectors in the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, since all other functions can be generated from these by symmetry.

•

The single angles
. Let us generate
\sin(2\theta)
for
\theta=x_{i}
,
i\in\left\llbracket 1,d\right\rrbracket
. Using Lemma
3.6
, we have that

\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta)=Q\left(\sin(\theta),\cos(\theta),1\right).

Applying the operator
-\Delta
on both sides of the above expression, we have

\displaystyle 4\sin(2\theta)=

\displaystyle-\Delta Q(\sin(\theta),\cos(\theta),1).

By Lemma
3.8
and recalling the ansatz (
3.21
), we deduce that
\sin(2\theta)
for
\theta=x_{i}
belongs to
\mathscr{H}_{1}
. To generate
\cos(2\theta)
for
\theta=x_{i}
,
i\in\left\llbracket 1,d\right\rrbracket
, we argue as follows. We have that

\cos^{2}(\theta)=Q(\cos(\theta),\cos(\theta),1).

Using that
\cos(2\theta)=2\cos^{2}(\theta)-1
and the above expression, we have that

\frac{1}{2}\cos(2\theta)=Q(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta),1)+\frac{1}{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle 2\cos(2\theta)=-\Delta(Q(\cos(\theta),\cos(\theta),1)),

and, by Lemma
3.8
,
\cos(2\theta)
for
\theta=x_{i}
belongs to the vector space
\mathscr{H}_{1}
.

•

The sum of angles
. Using Lemma
3.6
, we can write

\sin x_{i}\cos x_{j}=Q(\sin x_{i},\cos x_{j},1)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\cos x_{i}\sin x_{j}=Q(\cos x_{i},\sin x_{j},1),

which lead to

\sin(x\cdot p)=\sin(x\cdot(e_{i}\pm e_{j}))=\sin(x_{i}\pm x_{j})=Q(\sin x_{i},\cos x_{j},1)+Q(\cos x_{i},\sin x_{j},\pm 1),

where we have used Lemma
3.7
. Applying the operator
-\Delta
in both sides of the above expression we have

2\sin(x_{i}\pm x_{j})=-\Delta(Q(\sin x_{i},\cos x_{j},1))-\Delta(Q(\cos x_{i},\sin x_{j},\pm 1))

so
\sin(x_{i}\pm x_{j})\in\mathscr{H}_{1}
by recalling Lemma
3.8
and (
3.21
). On the other hand, we have that

\cos x_{i}\cos x_{j}=Q(\cos x_{i},\cos x_{j},1)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\sin x_{i}\sin x_{j}=Q(\sin x_{i},\sin x_{j},1),

which in turn yield

\cos(x\cdot p)=\cos(x_{i}\pm x_{j})=Q(\cos x_{i},\cos x_{j},1)+Q(\sin x_{i},\cos x_{j},\mp 1).

Similarly applying
-\Delta
on both sides of the above expression, we have that

2\cos(x_{i}\pm x_{j})=-\Delta Q(\cos x_{i},\cos x_{j},1)-\Delta Q(\sin x_{i},\cos x_{j},\mp 1),

and thus
\cos(x\cdot p)\in\mathscr{H}_{1}
.

With this, we have proved that
\spn A_{I_{1}}\subset\mathscr{H}_{1}
.

– The inductive step
. Assume that
\spn{A}_{I_{N}}\subset\mathscr{H}_{N}
. Then, by the construction described in (
3.18
) and (
3.19
), we have that
\spn A_{I_{N}}\subset\mathscr{H}_{N+1}
. Therefore, our task is reduced to showing that for any
p\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}|p_{i}|=N+2
, the corresponding elements of the set
A_{I_{N+1}}
can be generated by means of the ansatz

f_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta(f_{i}^{3})\textnormal{ with }f_{0},f_{i}\in\mathscr{H}_{N}\textnormal{ and some }m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}.

(3.22)

•

The single angle
. We analyze the cases when
p\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is equal to
\displaystyle(0,\ldots,\underbrace{N+2}_{i-\text{th}},\ldots,0)
. Let
\theta
be equal to
x_{i}
,
i\in\left\llbracket 1,d\right\rrbracket
. We note that

\sin(x\cdot p)=\sin((N+2)\theta)=\sin((N+1)\theta+\theta)=\sin((N+1)\theta)\cos(\theta)+\cos((N+1)\theta)\sin(\theta)

By the induction hypothesis
\sin((N+1)\theta)
and
\cos((N+1)\theta)
belong to
\mathscr{H}_{N}
, hence using Lemma
3.6
, we can write

\sin((N+2)\theta)=Q(\sin((N+1)\theta),\cos(\theta),1)+Q(\cos((N+1)\theta),\sin(\theta),1)

and by applying the differential operator
-\Delta
we have

(N+2)^{2}\sin((N+2)\theta)=-\Delta Q(\sin((N+1)\theta),\cos(\theta),1)-\Delta Q(\cos((N+1)\theta),\sin(\theta),1)

which shows that
\sin((N+2)\theta)\in\mathscr{H}_{N+1}
for
\theta=x_{i}
,
i\in\left\llbracket 1,d\right\rrbracket
, by recalling Lemma
3.8
and (
3.22
). On the other hand, we have that

\cos((N+2)\theta)=\cos((N+1)\theta+\theta)=\cos((N+1)\theta)\cos(\theta)-\sin((N+1)\theta)\sin(\theta).

Hence, using Lemma
3.6
and Lemma
3.7
, we can write

\cos((N+2)\theta)=Q(\cos((N+1)\theta),\cos(\theta),1)+Q(\sin((N+1)\theta),\sin(\theta),-1),

whence

(N+2)^{2}\cos((N+2)\theta)=-\Delta Q(\cos((N+1)\theta),\cos(\theta),1)-\Delta Q(\sin((N+1)\theta),\sin(\theta),-1),

and thus
\cos((N+2)\theta)\in\mathscr{H}_{N+1}
.

•

The sum of angles
. Let
p\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}|p_{i}|=N+2
, and
|p_{i}|<N+2,
for all
1\leq i\leq d
be arbitrary. Then
p
can be written as
p=l\pm q,
where
l,q\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}|l_{i}|=N+1
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{d}|q_{i}|=1
. Using trigonometric identities,

\displaystyle\sin(x\cdot(l\pm q))=\sin(x\cdot l)\cos(x\cdot q)\pm\cos(x\cdot l)\sin(x\cdot q)

\displaystyle\cos(x\cdot(l\pm q))=\cos(x\cdot l)\cos(x\cdot q)\mp\sin(x\cdot l)\sin(x\cdot q),

along with
Lemma
3.6
and Lemma
3.7
, we have

\displaystyle\sin(x\cdot p)

\displaystyle=\sin(x\cdot l)\cos(x\cdot q)\pm\cos(x\cdot l)\sin(x\cdot q)

\displaystyle=Q\left(\sin(x\cdot l),\cos(x\cdot q),1\right)+Q\left(\cos(x\cdot l),\sin(x\cdot q),\pm 1\right)

Note that by the construction (
3.18
)–(
3.19
), the functions
\pm 1
,
\sin(x\cdot l),\cos(x\cdot q),\cos(x\cdot l),\sin(x\cdot q)
belong to
\mathscr{H}_{N}
. Hence, by taking
-\Delta
on both sides of the previous inequality and Lemma
3.8
give

\displaystyle|p|^{2}\sin(x\cdot p)

\displaystyle=-\Delta Q\left(\sin(x\cdot l),\cos(x\cdot q),1\right)-\Delta Q\left(\cos(x\cdot l),\sin(x\cdot q),\pm 1\right)

and hence
\sin(x\cdot p)
with
\sum_{i=1}^{d}|p_{i}|=N+2
, and
|p_{i}|<N+2,
belong to
\mathscr{H}_{N+1}
. Analogously,

\displaystyle\cos(x\cdot p)

\displaystyle=\cos(x\cdot l)\cos(x\cdot q)\mp\sin(x\cdot l)\sin(x\cdot q)

\displaystyle=Q(\cos(x\cdot l),\cos(x\cdot q),1)+Q(\sin(x\cdot l),\sin(x\cdot q),\mp 1),

from which we deduce that

\displaystyle|p|^{2}\cos(x\cdot p)

\displaystyle=-\Delta Q\left(\cos(x\cdot l),\cos(x\cdot q),1\right)-\Delta Q\left(\sin(x\cdot l),\sin(x\cdot q),\mp 1\right)

and thus
\cos(x\cdot p)
belongs to
\mathscr{H}_{N+1}
.

∎

4
Proof of global approximate controllability

In this section, we will prove the main global approximate controllability result Theorem
1.2
. Our argument follows the approach used in
[
47
, Theorem 3.3]
. The strategy is to first prove small-time approximate controllability to any point of
the affine space
u_{0}+\mathscr{H}_{N}
by combining
3.2
with an induction argument
in
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Using the saturation property
3.5
, we then deduce small-time approximate controllability to every point in
H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Finally, global approximate controllability in any time
T
is obtained
by steering the system close to the target
u_{1}
in a short time and then keeping it
near
u_{1}
for a sufficiently long interval. Let us begin by proving global approximate controllability to
u_{0}+\mathscr{H}_{N}
in very small time.

Proposition 4.1
.

Equation (
1.5
) is globally
H^{k}
-approximately controllable in arbitrarily small time
T>0
. More precisely, for any
u_{0}\in H^{k+2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),

u_{1}\in u_{0}+\mathscr{H}_{N}
, and
\sigma,\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta\in(0,\sigma)
and a control
f\in L^{\infty}(0,\delta;\mathscr{H}_{0})
such that the solution of (
1.5
) satisfies

\|\mathcal{R}_{\delta}(u_{0},0,f)-u_{1}\|_{H^{k}}<\epsilon.

Proof.

Let us first take
u_{0}\in H^{k+2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.
We shall establish the approximate controllability in small time from
u_{0}
to
u_{0}+\mathscr{H}_{N}
by induction, along with the asymptotic property from
3.2
. More precisely, our goal is to show the following for all
N\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\text{For any }u_{0}\in H^{k+2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\ u_{1}\in u_{0}+\mathscr{H}_{N},\ \sigma,\epsilon>0,\text{there exists}\,\delta\in(0,\sigma)\text{ and a}

(
\mathcal{P}_{N}
)

\displaystyle\text{control }f\in L^{\infty}(0,\delta;\mathscr{H}_{0}),\ \text{such that the solution of \eqref{eq_main} is well-defined in $[0,\delta]$ and }

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{\delta}(u_{0},0,f)-u_{1}\|_{H^{k}}<\epsilon.

Step 1. Controllability from
u_{0}
to
u_{0}+\mathscr{H}_{0}
.

For
u_{1}\in u_{0}+\mathscr{H}_{0}
, there exists
\eta\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
such that
u_{1}=u_{0}+\eta
.
Thanks to
3.2
,

\mathcal{R}_{\delta}(u_{0},0,\delta^{-1}\eta)\to u_{0}+\eta,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{as }\delta\to 0^{+}.

Hence, for any
\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta\in(0,\sigma)
such that the solution of (
1.5
) is well-defined in
[0,\delta]
and

\|\mathcal{R}_{\delta}(u_{0},0,f_{1})-u_{1}\|_{H^{k}}<\epsilon,

where the constant (in time) control

is of the form
f_{1}(t,\cdot)=\tfrac{1}{\delta}\eta(\cdot)
.
Thus, equation (
1.5
) is small-time approximately controllable from
u_{0}
to
u_{0}+\mathscr{H}_{0}
by means of an
\mathscr{H}_{0}
-valued control.

Step 2. Controllability from
u_{0}
to
u_{0}+\mathscr{H}_{N}
.

Assume that (
1.5
) is approximately controllable from
u_{0}
to
u_{0}+\mathscr{H}_{N-1}
using an
\mathscr{H}_{0}
valued control. Let
\epsilon>0
and
\sigma>0.
Assume that
u_{1}\in u_{0}+\mathscr{H}_{N}
. Then there exist
\eta,\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{m}\in\mathscr{H}_{N-1}
such that

u_{1}=u_{0}+\eta+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta(\varphi_{i}^{3}),

for some
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. We proceed by induction on
m
. Consider
m=1.
Then
u_{1}=u_{0}+\eta+\Delta(\varphi_{1}^{3}).

Using
3.2

we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\delta}(u_{0},\delta^{-\frac{1}{3}}\varphi_{1},0)\to u_{0}+\Delta(\varphi_{1}^{3}),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ in }H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\text{ as }\delta\to 0^{+}.

From the flow property (
3.1
),
\mathcal{R}_{\delta}(u_{0}+\delta^{-1/3}\varphi_{1},0,0)-\delta^{-1/3}\varphi_{1}=R_{\delta}(u_{0},\delta^{-\frac{1}{3}}\varphi_{1},0)

\mathcal{R}_{\delta}(u_{0}+\delta^{-1/3}\varphi_{1},0,0)-\delta^{-1/3}\varphi_{1}\to u_{0}+\Delta(\varphi_{1}^{3})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in }H^{k}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}),\text{ as }\delta\to 0^{+}.

\epsilon^{\prime}>0

\delta_{2}\in(0,\sigma/3)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\|\mathcal{R}_{\delta_{2}}(\mathcal{R}_{\delta_{1}}(u_{0},0,f_{2}),0,0)-\mathcal{R}_{\delta_{2}}(u_{0}+\delta_{2}^{-1/3}\varphi_{1},0,0)\|_{H^{k}}

. We have that

\displaystyle I_{1}=

\displaystyle-\sum_{|\alpha|=k+1}\frac{(k+1)!}{\alpha!}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\partial^{\alpha}v|^{2}\varphi^{2}-\sum_{|\alpha|=k+1}\frac{(k+1)!}{\alpha!}\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha\atop\beta\neq\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\partial^{\alpha}v\partial^{\beta}v\partial^{\alpha-\beta}(\varphi^{2}).

Proof.

To prove it, it is enough to perform integration by parts and apply Leibniz formula to the term
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}u\partial^{2\alpha}u\varphi^{2}
. More precisely,

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v\partial^{2\alpha}v\varphi^{2}=(-1)^{k+1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial^{\alpha}v\partial^{\alpha}(v\varphi^{2})

(B.3)

whence

\partial^{\alpha}(v\varphi^{2})=\varphi^{2}\partial^{\alpha}v+\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha\atop\beta\neq\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\partial^{\beta}v\partial^{\alpha-\beta}(\varphi^{2}).

(B.4)

Putting together (
B.3
) and (
B.4
) and replacing in
I_{1}
yields the desired result.
∎

Lemma B.3
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
be such that
|\alpha|=k+1
. For any multi-index
\beta\leq\alpha
,
\beta\neq\alpha
, we have

\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial^{\alpha}v\partial^{\beta}v\partial^{\alpha-\beta}(\varphi^{2})\right|\leq C\|\varphi\|_{H^{2k+2}}^{2}\|v\|_{H^{k}}^{2}.

Proof.

Since
|\alpha|=k+1
, we note that
0\leq|\beta|\leq k
. In this way, the most difficult case is to estimate the one that corresponds to
|\alpha|=k+1
and
|\beta|=k
. We note that in this case we can write
\alpha=\beta+e_{i}
where
e_{i}
is a canonical vector from the basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then

\displaystyle{\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial^{\alpha}v\partial^{\beta}v\partial^{\alpha-\beta}(\varphi^{2})}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial_{i}\partial^{\beta}v\partial^{\beta}v\partial_{i}(\varphi^{2})=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\partial^{\beta}v|^{2}\partial_{ii}(\varphi^{2}).

Therefore

\left|{\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial^{\alpha}v\partial^{\beta}v\partial^{\alpha-\beta}(\varphi^{2})}\right|\leq C\|\Delta\varphi^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|u\|_{H^{k}}^{2}

for all
|\beta|=k
.

Next, we focus in the case when
|\beta|\leq k-1
. Note that
\alpha
can be written as
\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}+e_{i}
for some canonical vector from the basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and such that
|\alpha^{\prime}|=k
. Arguing as above

\displaystyle{\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial^{\alpha}v\partial^{\beta}v\partial^{\alpha-\beta}(\varphi^{2})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial_{i}\partial^{\alpha^{\prime}}v\partial^{\beta}v\partial^{\alpha-\beta}(\varphi^{2})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial^{\alpha^{\prime}}v\partial^{\beta+e_{i}}v\partial^{\alpha-\beta}(\varphi^{2})-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial^{\alpha^{\prime}}v\partial^{\beta}v\partial^{\alpha-\beta+e_{i}}(\varphi^{2}).

So

\displaystyle\left|{\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial^{\alpha}v\partial^{\beta}v\partial^{\alpha-\beta}(\varphi^{2})}\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\|\partial^{\alpha-\beta}\varphi^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\partial^{\alpha^{\prime}}v\|_{L^{2}}\|\partial^{\beta+e_{i}}v\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial^{\alpha-\beta+e_{i}}\varphi^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\partial^{\alpha^{\prime}}v\|_{L^{2}}\|\partial^{\beta}v\|_{L^{2}}.

for all multi-index
\beta
such that
|\beta|\leq k-1
. Thus,
|\beta+e_{i}|\leq k
and
|\alpha-\beta+e_{i}|\leq k+2
and therefore

\displaystyle\left|{\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial^{\alpha}v\partial^{\beta}v\partial^{\alpha-\beta}(\varphi^{2})}\right|\leq C\|\varphi\|^{2}_{H^{2k+2}}\|v\|_{H^{k}}^{2}.

This ends the proof.
∎

Lemma B.4
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
be such that
|\alpha|=k+1
. For any multi-index
\gamma\leq 2\alpha
,
\gamma\neq 2\alpha
, we have

\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v\partial^{\gamma}v\partial^{2\alpha-\gamma}(\varphi^{2})\right|\leq C\|\varphi\|_{H^{3k+2}}^{2}\|v\|_{H^{k}}^{2}.

Proof.

We separate in several cases according to the length of the multi-index
\gamma
.

–
Case 1:
|\gamma|\leq k
.
First, we note that for any
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
such that
|\gamma|\leq k
, we have by Hölder and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities

\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v\partial^{\gamma}v\partial^{2\alpha-\gamma}(\varphi^{2})\right|\leq\|\partial^{2\alpha-\gamma}\varphi^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|v\|_{L^{2}}\|\partial^{\gamma}v\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|\varphi\|_{H^{3k+2}}^{2}\|v\|_{H^{k}}^{2}.

–
Case 2:
k+1\leq|\gamma|\leq 2k
.
Let
m=|\gamma|
, we can split
\gamma=\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}
with
|\gamma_{1}|=k
and
|\gamma_{2}|=m-k\geq 1
, so by Leibniz formula

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v\partial^{\gamma}v\partial^{2\alpha-\gamma}(\varphi^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v\partial^{\gamma_{1}}(\partial^{\gamma_{2}}v)\partial^{2\alpha-\gamma}(\varphi^{2})=(-1)^{|\gamma_{1}|}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial^{\gamma_{2}}v\,\partial^{\gamma_{1}}\left(v\partial^{2\alpha-\gamma}(\varphi)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\sum_{\beta\leq\gamma_{1}}\binom{\gamma_{1}}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial^{\gamma_{2}}v\,\partial^{\beta}v\partial^{2\alpha-\gamma+\gamma_{1}-\beta}(\varphi^{2}).

We note that
|\gamma_{2}|\leq k
,
|\beta|\leq|\gamma_{1}|=k
and
|2\alpha-\gamma+\gamma_{1}-\beta|\leq 2k+1
, so by direct computations using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we get

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v\partial^{\gamma}v\partial^{2\alpha-\gamma}(\varphi^{2})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\beta\leq\gamma_{1}}\binom{\gamma_{1}}{\beta}\|\partial^{2\alpha-\gamma+\gamma_{1}-\beta}\varphi^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\partial^{\gamma_{2}}v\|_{L^{2}}\|\partial^{\beta}v\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\varphi\|_{H^{3k+1}}^{2}\|v\|_{H^{k}}^{2}.

–
Case 3:
\gamma\leq 2\alpha
,
\gamma\neq 2\alpha
, and
|\gamma|=2k+1
. We first observe that if
\gamma\leq 2\alpha
and
|\gamma|=2k+2
, then necessarily
\gamma=2\alpha
, so the condition
\gamma\neq 2\alpha
excludes that case. Now, since
\gamma\leq 2\alpha
and
|\gamma|=2k+1
, it follows that
|2\alpha-\gamma|=1
and hence
\gamma=2\alpha-e_{i}
for some
i\in\left\llbracket 1,d\right\rrbracket
with
\alpha_{i}\geq 1
, where
e_{i}
is the
i
-th canonical basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Thus, we may write
\gamma=\zeta+\xi
with
|\zeta|=k+1
and
|\xi|=k
(for instance,
\zeta=\alpha
and
\xi=\alpha-e_{i}
). The specific decomposition does not matter in what follows.

By Leibniz formula

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v\partial^{\gamma}v\partial^{2\alpha-\gamma}(\varphi^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v\partial^{\zeta}(\partial^{\xi}v)\partial^{2\alpha-\zeta-\xi}(\varphi^{2})=(-1)^{|\zeta|}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial^{\xi}v\partial^{\zeta}\left(v\partial^{2\alpha-\zeta-\xi}(\varphi^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k+1}\sum_{\beta\leq\zeta}\binom{\zeta}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial^{\xi}v\partial^{\beta}v\partial^{2\alpha-\xi-\beta}(\varphi^{2}).

(B.5)

On the other hand

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v\partial^{\gamma}v\partial^{2\alpha-\gamma}(\varphi^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v\partial^{\xi}(\partial^{\zeta}v)\partial^{2\alpha-\zeta-\xi}(\varphi^{2})=(-1)^{|\xi|}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial^{\zeta}v\partial^{\xi}\left(v\partial^{2\alpha-\zeta-\xi}(\varphi^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\sum_{\beta\leq\xi}\binom{\xi}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial^{\zeta}v\partial^{\beta}v\partial^{2\alpha-\zeta-\beta}(\varphi^{2}).

(B.6)

Adding up (
B.5
) and (
B.6
), we see that the terms corresponding to
\beta=\zeta
and
\beta=\xi
cancel out, leading us to

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v\partial^{\gamma}v\partial^{2\alpha-\gamma}(\varphi^{2})=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{2}\left(-\sum_{\beta\leq\zeta\atop\beta\neq\zeta}I_{\beta}(\zeta,\xi)+\sum_{\beta\leq\xi\atop\beta\neq\xi}I_{\beta}(\xi,\zeta)\right),

(B.7)

where we have used the notation

I_{\beta}(\Psi,\Upsilon):=\binom{\Psi}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial^{\Upsilon}v\partial^{\beta}v\partial^{2\alpha-\Upsilon-\beta}(\varphi^{2}).

We observe that the first term in the right-hand side of (
B.7
) (namely, the one containing
I_{\beta}(\zeta,\xi)
) can be bounded easily by following Case 1 above since
|\xi|\leq k
and
\beta\leq\zeta
with
\beta\neq\zeta
implies that
|\beta|\leq k
, so

\left|\sum_{\beta\leq\zeta\atop\beta\neq\zeta}I_{\beta}(\zeta,\xi)\right|\leq C\|\varphi\|^{2}_{H^{2k+2}}\|v\|^{2}_{H^{k}}.

(B.8)

For the last term in (
B.7
), we observe that
|\zeta|=k+1
but
\beta\leq\xi
with
\beta\neq\xi
imply that
|\beta|\leq k-1
, which falls into Case 2 above where we can pass first-order derivatives to the lower order terms. With some direct computations and arguing as in Case 2, we can prove that

\left|\sum_{\beta\leq\xi\atop\beta\neq\xi}I_{\beta}(\xi,\zeta)\right|\leq C\|\varphi\|^{2}_{H^{2k+2}}\|v\|_{H^{k}}^{2}.

(B.9)

Thus, using (
B.8
)–(
B.9
) in (
B.7
) yield that

\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v\partial^{\gamma}v\partial^{2\alpha-\gamma}(\varphi^{2})\right|\leq C\|\varphi\|^{2}_{H^{2k+2}}\|v\|^{2}_{H^{k}}.

Lastly, combining Cases 1 to 3 yields the estimate for the term
I
.
∎

Replacing
v
by
w
leads to the same estimate, therefore the proof is complete.
∎

B.2
Proof of Lemma
5.8

Proof.

We split the proof into two cases.

Case 1
:
b\leq c
.
Choose
\epsilon\in(0,1]
by balancing the two terms

\epsilon=\Big(\frac{b}{c}\Big)^{N/(N^{2}+1)}\leq 1.
For this value of
\epsilon
and choosing
\theta=\frac{1}{N^{2}+1}
, we compute

\epsilon^{-N}b=b^{1-\frac{N^{2}}{N^{2}+1}}c^{\frac{N^{2}}{N^{2}+1}}=b^{\theta}c^{1-\theta},

\epsilon^{1/N}c=b^{\theta}c^{1-\theta}.

Thus both terms coincide, and using the hypothesis, we obtain

a\leq M\Big(\epsilon^{-N}b+\epsilon^{1/N}c\Big)=2M\,b^{\theta}c^{1-\theta}.
Hence the desired inequality holds with
C_{0}\geq 2M
.

Case 2
:
b>c
.
From the assumption
a\leq C_{1}c
, and since
b>c
, we have

b^{\theta}c^{1-\theta}=c\,(b/c)^{\theta}>c,

so it follows that

a\leq C_{1}c\leq C_{1}\,b^{\theta}c^{1-\theta}.
Thus the desired inequality holds with
C_{0}\geq C_{1}
.
Combining both cases, take
C_{0}:=\max\{2M,C_{1}\}
, which yields the result.
∎
