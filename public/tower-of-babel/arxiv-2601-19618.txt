The pretraining domain outweighs the training objective in setting the privacy-utility trade-off of differentially private medical image analysis
Soroosh Tayebi Arasteh ∗, Mina Farajiamiri, Mahshad Lotfinia, Behrus Hinrichs-Puladi, Jonas Bienzeisler, Mohamed Alhaskir, Mirabela Rusu, Christiane Kuhl, Sven Nebelung, Daniel Truhn

Abstract
Differential privacy limits how much each training image can influence a medical imaging model but lowers its diagnostic accuracy. Pretrained weights reduce this loss. It is unknown whether the pretraining objective or domain improves accuracy more. Here we train chest radiograph classifiers under differential privacy from five starting points on 591,884 radiographs from five datasets. We show that supervised pretraining on chest radiographs ranks first in 24 of 25 combinations of dataset and privacy setting. Measured by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, its lead over supervised pretraining on natural images is 2.6% without privacy and 15.3% at the strictest budget. From that starting point, changing the domain improves this area 2.2 to 3.7 times more than changing the objective. Private pretraining on chest radiographs lowers this area by about 5% under privacy but outperforms the three starting points without patient data at the strictest budget. Under privacy, supervised chest radiograph pretraining leads in the worst-performing demographic subgroup.

Introduction

Deep learning models for medical imaging improve with the size and diversity of their training data. However, medical images are among the most sensitive patient data
[
19
]
. Training images can also be reconstructed from the released weights of a model
[
4
]
. Differential privacy (DP) bounds how much any single training example can influence a model
[
9
]
. For deep networks, it is realized by differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD), which clips per-sample gradients and adds calibrated noise
[
1
]
. DP-SGD has been applied in medical imaging
[
59
,
46
]
, but it lowers diagnostic accuracy. A recent review of 74 studies found that models remain clinically useful at moderate budgets near
\epsilon=10

[
33
]
. The same review reported that accuracy commonly decreases at strict budgets near
\epsilon=1
and decreases most in small and heterogeneous cohorts. Reconciling privacy with accuracy remains a main obstacle to deploying private medical artificial intelligence (AI)
[
58
]
.

Among the methods that reduce this loss, the initialization has been among the most effective. Beginning private training from pretrained weights instead of from scratch recovers much of the lost accuracy, an effect documented on natural images
[
7
,
24
]
, in language
[
26
]
, and in radiography
[
46
]
. Self-supervised pretraining produces representations that transfer more broadly and are more robust than those of supervised pretraining
[
6
,
36
,
14
]
. It has proven useful in medical imaging
[
23
,
2
,
45
,
29
]
. Features from self-supervised pretraining also improve accuracy under DP
[
50
]
. Recent DINOv3 models provide self-supervised pretrained weights for convolutional backbones
[
43
]
. Separately, pretraining on the target modality is long-standing practice in chest radiography, although its benefit over generic pretraining is contested without privacy
[
20
,
21
,
31
]
. Foundation models for chest radiography are pretrained on radiographs, either with self-supervision
[
37
,
54
]
or with expert labels
[
30
]
. It has not been established whether the objective or the domain drives performance under privacy.

Here we contrast each property while holding the other fixed. Using a fixed ConvNeXt backbone
[
27
]
and a common DP-SGD pipeline, we compare five initializations that differ in the pretraining objective and the pretraining domain (Fig.
1
). Three of them are random initialization with the He scheme
[
13
]
, supervised ImageNet pretraining
[
8
]
, and self-supervised DINOv3 pretraining on natural images
[
43
]
. The other two are supervised pretraining on chest radiographs from MIMIC-CXR
[
17
]
, once conventionally and once under DP-SGD. Supplementary Table
1
defines every term of the study. Pretraining under DP-SGD addresses a second problem. Private finetuning conventionally treats the pretraining corpus as public. This assumption is hard to defend when the pretraining corpus consists of patient data
[
51
]
. Private pretraining extends the guarantee to the pretraining data and has been studied on hundreds of millions of web images
[
57
]
. We evaluate every initialization at four privacy budgets and without privacy on 591,884 radiographs from five datasets
[
34
,
16
,
53
,
5
,
21
]
. These datasets come from four countries and from routine and intensive care. Further experiments compare three finetuning schemes, including low-rank adaptation (LoRA)
[
15
]
, and two model capacities. We also test generalization to unseen institutions by holding out one dataset at a time. Finally, we measure performance within demographic subgroups, in which the effect of privacy is known to be uneven
[
3
]
. To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare the pretraining objective with the pretraining domain under DP at this scale, and the first in medical imaging to measure the downstream cost of protecting the pretraining corpus.

We expected the noise added to every gradient to penalize a model in proportion to how much it still has to learn. An initialization that already encodes the target modality should then lose the least. We test this expectation on diagnostic accuracy, on the privacy spent before a model converges, on transfer to institutions not seen in training, and on the demographic subgroup with the lowest performance. Pretraining on chest radiographs leads in every dataset at every privacy budget. The pretraining domain contributes more to this lead than the pretraining objective. Making that pretraining private keeps it ahead of generic pretraining in most private settings. Under privacy, the domain-matched initialization also converges with less privacy spent and gives the highest performance in the worst-performing subgroup. Supplementary Table
2
summarizes the findings. These findings indicate that encoders pretrained on the target modality, including encoders pretrained under DP, may be a more effective route to private medical AI than generic self-supervised encoders.

In-domain pretraining sets the privacy-utility frontier

He initialization trains the network from scratch. Its starting weights are drawn from the He (Kaiming) normal distribution instead of being taken from a trained model. ImageNet was pretrained on natural images with labels, and DINOv3 was pretrained on natural images without labels. InDomain-stand was pretrained with labels on chest radiographs from MIMIC-CXR, and InDomain-priv was pretrained in the same way under DP. On five chest radiograph datasets, we finetuned a ConvNeXt-Small classifier from each of these five initializations at four privacy budgets and without privacy. InDomain-stand had the highest macro AUROC in 24 of the 25 combinations of dataset and privacy setting (Table
1
, Fig.
2
a–e). The exception was UKA-CXR without privacy, where InDomain-stand and DINOv3 had the same macro AUROC (88.8 each,
p=0.562
).

\epsilon\leq 1
, its leads were 15.3% over ImageNet and 8.8% over DINOv3 (Fig.

\epsilon\leq 1
lowered the macro AUROC by 5.1% on average for InDomain-stand, 7.3% for InDomain-priv, 12.5% for DINOv3, and 17.8% for ImageNet (Fig.

\epsilon\leq 1
and by 5.8% without privacy. On the four datasets with 77,870 to 137,902 training radiographs, its lead was 9.7% to 14.5% at
\epsilon\leq 1

The pretraining domain outweighs the training objective

The pretrained initializations differ in their pretraining objective, which is supervised or self-supervised, and in the domain of their pretraining images, which is natural images or chest radiographs. ImageNet, DINOv3, and InDomain-stand cover three of the four combinations of objective and domain (Fig.
3
a). No public model pretrained with self-supervision on chest radiographs was available for the ConvNeXt backbone. The fourth combination was therefore not evaluated. We compare the two properties through two pairwise contrasts that share supervised ImageNet pretraining as their reference. The objective contrast is DINOv3 minus ImageNet, which were both pretrained on natural images. The domain contrast is InDomain-stand minus ImageNet, which were both pretrained with a supervised objective. Neither contrast is a factorial main effect. With three conditions, the interaction between objective and domain cannot be estimated.

A domain-matched initialization converges with less privacy spent

The converged epoch of a private run could come long before its training stopped at the end of the budget. At
\epsilon\leq 1
, the converged epoch averaged 18.6 for InDomain-stand, 22.2 for InDomain-priv, 37.0 for DINOv3, 38.4 for He initialization, and 39.4 for ImageNet (Fig.
4
a). At every budget, the two in-domain initializations converged before DINOv3, and DINOv3 converged before He initialization and ImageNet. At
\epsilon\leq 6
and
\epsilon\leq 9
, InDomain-priv converged before InDomain-stand. Seven of the 100 private runs of the main experiment kept the model of their first epoch. All seven started from InDomain-stand or InDomain-priv, which begin finetuning with a classification head already trained on the same five findings. These models therefore start as working chest radiograph classifiers. We attribute their early converged epoch to this starting point. Under privacy, further training can move such a model away from it faster than the clipped and noisy gradient improves it.

Private in-domain pretraining outperforms generic pretraining under privacy

In-domain pretraining needs a corpus of medical images. Such a corpus is patient data and has the same privacy requirements as the data used downstream. InDomain-priv was pretrained on the same MIMIC-CXR radiographs with the same supervised objective. Its pretraining ran under DP-SGD and satisfies a guarantee of
\epsilon=7.93
at
\delta=6\times 10^{-6}
. Private pretraining lowered macro AUROC by about 5% at every privacy budget. InDomain-priv trailed InDomain-stand by 4.9% at
\epsilon\leq 1
and by 5.0% to 5.1% at the looser budgets. Without privacy downstream, the difference was 2.7%. All 25 comparisons of InDomain-priv with InDomain-stand had
p<0.0001
(Table
3
).

Low-rank adaptation improves the pretrained initializations under privacy

DP-SGD adds Gaussian noise to the update of every trainable parameter. The norm of the noise therefore grows with the number of trained parameters. We compared full finetuning with two parameter-efficient schemes, head-only finetuning, which trains only the classification head, and LoRA (Fig.
5
a–d,h). The comparison covered He initialization, ImageNet, and InDomain-stand on VinDr-CXR and CheXpert at
\epsilon\leq 1
and
\epsilon\leq 6
. LoRA exceeded full finetuning in 11 of the 12 configurations (Fig.
5
e). The mean difference over all 12 was 4.3% macro AUROC, and the largest difference was 16.3%. The exception was He initialization on VinDr-CXR at
\epsilon\leq 1
, where LoRA was 1.3% lower. Head-only finetuning exceeded full finetuning in 10 of the 12 configurations (Fig.
5
f). Its mean difference over all 12 was 2.1%. The other two configurations were He initialization on CheXpert, where head-only finetuning was 0.8% lower at
\epsilon\leq 1
and equal at
\epsilon\leq 6
.

The initialization advantage persists at unseen institutions and exceeds the effect of model capacity

Deployment usually means running a model at a new institution. To test this setting, we held out each dataset in turn, trained on the other four, and measured macro AUROC on the held-out dataset. At
\epsilon\leq 1
, the macro AUROC of the four pretrained initializations on a held-out dataset was 3.9% to 6.6% lower than after training on that dataset. He initialization lost 1.1%, because it was close to chance in both evaluations (Fig.
6
a,c,d and Supplementary Table
5
). At
\epsilon\leq 1
, InDomain-stand led on four of the five held-out datasets. The held-out averages were 76.5 for InDomain-stand, 70.6 for InDomain-priv, 67.3 for DINOv3, 62.9 for ImageNet, and 51.0 for He initialization (Fig.
6
a,e). Without privacy, the averages of the four pretrained initializations were 78.5 to 80.3. InDomain-stand led on three of the five held-out datasets, had the same macro AUROC as DINOv3 on VinDr-CXR (90.1), and was 0.2% below DINOv3 on ChestX-ray14 (Fig.
6
b,e). With UKA-CXR held out at
\epsilon\leq 1
, the macro AUROC was 67.4 for ImageNet, 66.2 for InDomain-stand, and 61.7 for DINOv3 (Fig.
6
f). UKA-CXR is the only dataset collected primarily in intensive care and the only dataset labeled by structured clinical reporting. The in-domain benefit may therefore depend on how closely the pretraining corpus resembles the deployment setting, beyond the imaging modality.

Discussion

We asked whether, in the differentially private finetuning of chest radiograph classifiers, the benefit of a pretrained initialization comes from its pretraining objective or from its pretraining images. On five datasets, InDomain-stand had the highest macro AUROC in 24 of the 25 combinations of dataset and privacy setting. At
\epsilon\leq 1
, the average macro AUROC was 82.1 for InDomain-stand, 73.2 for DINOv3, and 66.8 for ImageNet. The domain contrast exceeded the objective contrast in 24 of the 25 combinations. Under privacy, the mean domain contrast over the five datasets was 2.4 to 3.7 times the mean objective contrast. Pretraining on the same radiographs under DP lowered macro AUROC by about 5% and left InDomain-priv ahead of DINOv3 and ImageNet in every dataset at
\epsilon\leq 1
. Generic pretraining still helped under privacy, since ImageNet and DINOv3 exceeded He initialization by 14.7% to 22.5% macro AUROC on average at every budget. Pretraining on the imaging modality of the task, however, reduced the loss of accuracy under private finetuning more than a self-supervised objective on natural images did.

Without privacy, ImageNet pretraining transfers well to chest radiographs
[
20
]
. On large medical datasets, its benefit over training from scratch can be small
[
39
]
. Under privacy, the lead of InDomain-stand over ImageNet at
\epsilon\leq 1
was about six times its lead without privacy. DP-SGD clips every per-sample gradient and adds Gaussian noise to their sum
[
1
]
. The privacy budget limits the number of these noisy updates. The error added by the noise does not grow with the number of parameters when the gradients concentrate in a few directions
[
25
]
. Public pretraining has also been proposed to help private training by selecting a good basin of the loss landscape, which is hard to do with private data
[
10
]
. The private data then refine the model within that basin. In this interpretation, an initialization pretrained on chest radiographs to recognize the same five findings needs smaller changes to fit the target task. The same noise then disturbs these changes less. The two in-domain initializations also converged before DINOv3 and ImageNet at every budget. In the main experiment, every private run that kept the model of its first epoch started from one of these two initializations.

Self-supervised pretraining has made medical imaging models more robust and more data-efficient in standard training
[
2
,
23
]
. Self-supervised features also improve private learning
[
50
]
. In our study, DINOv3 exceeded ImageNet in every dataset at every privacy setting. Under privacy, however, changing the objective improved macro AUROC less than changing the domain. Pretraining on radiological images instead of ImageNet has improved transfer most clearly on small target datasets
[
31
]
. DP lowers accuracy most in small cohorts
[
33
]
. Our largest in-domain advantage, on VinDr-CXR with its 12,000 training radiographs, is consistent with both observations. On natural-image benchmarks, private finetuning of models pretrained on large public image collections approaches non-private accuracy
[
7
]
. Whether such benchmarks measure how well public pretraining transfers to sensitive domains has been questioned
[
51
]
. We provide this measurement for chest radiographs. There, pretraining on natural images, with or without labels, left a larger loss of accuracy under privacy than pretraining on radiographs.

Reports of DP in medical imaging often state their privacy parameters incompletely
[
33
,
58
]
. A practical guide asks for the unit of privacy, the definition of neighboring datasets, the accountant and its assumptions, and the guarantee of the final model
[
38
]
. We report each of these, with the radiograph as the unit of privacy. Beside the budget of every run, we also report its achieved
\epsilon
, which is the budget spent up to the converged epoch. The guarantee of a final model remains that of its whole run, because the converged epoch was chosen after training had ended. At
\epsilon\leq 1
, InDomain-stand converged after spending two thirds of the budget on average. An initialization matched to the modality may therefore reach its best accuracy within a smaller budget than a generic initialization.

Private finetuning conventionally treats the pretraining data as public. This assumption is difficult to defend when the pretraining data are patient radiographs
[
51
]
. Pretraining under DP removes it, because the guarantee then also covers each radiograph of the pretraining corpus. In our study, InDomain-priv exceeded DINOv3 in 18 of the 20 combinations of dataset and privacy budget and trailed it without privacy in every dataset. Its advantage therefore appeared only when the finetuning was private as well. Developers of open medical foundation models
[
30
]
could release encoders pretrained under DP with a stated guarantee, for hospitals that finetune them privately.

On natural images, very large batches, long schedules, and very large public pretraining corpora recover much of the accuracy lost under DP-SGD
[
7
,
24
]
. A hospital that trains a model on its own patients rarely has these resources. In our study, the size of the backbone changed macro AUROC by 1.7% on average between ConvNeXt-Tiny and ConvNeXt-Small. Within one backbone, the initialization changed it by up to 20.4%. Matching the initialization to the modality needed no additional data from the target hospital, no additional compute, and no change to the privacy accounting. Chest radiograph classifiers lose accuracy at institutions that did not contribute training data
[
21
,
44
]
. When one dataset at a time was held out, InDomain-stand still led on four of the five held-out datasets under strict privacy.

Under privacy, LoRA improved on full finetuning in 11 of the 12 configurations, and head-only finetuning did so in 10. Parameter-efficient finetuning is known to improve the differentially private training of language models
[
56
,
26
]
. On natural images, linear models trained privately on fixed features outperformed end-to-end private training at moderate budgets
[
50
]
. In private few-shot classification, parameter-efficient adapters performed about as well as training the whole network
[
49
]
. Our results extend these observations to chest radiograph classification. The gain from LoRA was largest for ImageNet and smaller for InDomain-stand, which already starts close to the target domain. He initialization gained 0.3% on average and remained close to chance with every scheme. LoRA did not change the order of the initializations. Parameter-efficient finetuning therefore complemented a domain-matched initialization without replacing it.

Privacy can amplify differences in accuracy between groups
[
3
]
. A recent review found that few medical studies examine subgroup performance under DP and that several of them report wider subgroup gaps
[
33
]
. On a large chest radiograph cohort, private training largely did not increase disparities by age or sex
[
46
]
. In our study, the disparities were larger between age bands than between the sexes. Chest radiograph classifiers are known to perform unevenly across age groups also without privacy
[
42
]
. Under strict privacy, the two narrowest age gaps belonged to InDomain-stand and to He initialization, whose worst subgroups differed by 28.4% macro AUROC. A gap statistic does not distinguish these two cases, because a uniformly poor model and a uniformly good model both have a small gap. He initialization also had the lowest ECE at
\epsilon\leq 1
, although its macro AUROC was close to chance. A model can be well calibrated without separating the radiographs with a finding from those without it. Reporting the macro AUROC of the worst subgroup beside the gap would remove this ambiguity in evaluations of private medical models. A ranking metric reported beside a calibration metric would do the same for calibration.

Several limitations qualify these conclusions. First, the comparison of objective and domain lacks a self-supervised initialization pretrained on chest radiographs, because no public model of this kind was available for the ConvNeXt backbone. Self-supervised radiograph encoders exist for other architectures
[
48
,
54
]
. Pretraining such an encoder for ConvNeXt would complete the four combinations of objective and domain and allow their interaction to be estimated. Second, under privacy, the full finetuning of the two in-domain initializations used half the learning rate of the other initializations, since they were already adapted to chest radiographs. The comparison under privacy therefore does not separate the initialization from the learning rate. Repeating these private runs at the common learning rate would separate them. Third, at
\epsilon\leq 1
, InDomain-stand did not lead when UKA-CXR was held out. UKA-CXR was collected primarily in intensive care, while the pretraining corpus consists of the radiographs of patients who presented to an emergency department
[
17
]
. Pretraining corpora from several care settings would allow us to test whether the in-domain benefit depends on the care setting. Fourth, we studied one privacy mechanism, DP-SGD, with one backbone family at two capacities on one imaging modality. Because the in-domain initializations exist for ConvNeXt-Small only, the comparison of all five initializations is limited to this backbone. Repeating the design with vision transformers, with three-dimensional imaging, and with federated, synthetic-data, and hybrid approaches
[
19
,
29
]
would show whether the initializations keep their order in these settings. Fifth, we analyzed fairness by sex and age only, because race and ethnicity are not recorded in the curated lists of these datasets. Intersectional and finer subgroups would need cohorts that record these attributes. Sixth, the guarantees bound the influence of each radiograph and not the influence of each patient, who can contribute several radiographs. Opacus samples and clips single training examples, and VinDr-CXR records no patient identifiers. A patient-level guarantee would require sampling and clipping all radiographs of a patient together. We leave this guarantee to future work. Finally, the datasets are retrospective and were labeled by expert annotation, by automated labeling of the radiology reports, or by structured clinical reporting, without a common reference standard. A prospective validation in clinical use would be needed before deployment.

Under DP, the domain of the pretraining images affected diagnostic accuracy more than the pretraining objective or the size of the backbone. A domain-matched initialization gave the highest macro AUROC in the worst subgroup and reached its best validation performance with less of the privacy budget. With LoRA, it recovered about half of the accuracy lost under strict privacy on the two datasets tested. Pretraining on the target modality under DP still outperformed the generic initializations in most private settings. With such pretraining, the guarantee covers the pretraining data as well as the finetuning data. For a hospital that trains a model on its own patients, choosing such an initialization improves private accuracy without additional data from its own patients. Openly released encoders that were pretrained under DP on medical images may help private medical AI more than generic self-supervised encoders.

Network architecture and initializations

Every model was a ConvNeXt classifier
[
27
]
. Its four stages of ConvNeXt blocks have 96, 192, 384, and 768 channels. ConvNeXt-Small is the main backbone and has 3, 3, 27, and 3 blocks in these stages. ConvNeXt-Tiny is used in the capacity experiment and has 3, 3, 9, and 3 blocks. The last feature map is averaged over its spatial positions and layer-normalized into a 768-dimensional embedding. A linear head maps this embedding to one logit for each of the five findings. With the head, ConvNeXt-Small has 49.5 million parameters, and ConvNeXt-Tiny has 27.8 million.

He initialization is the random initialization of this study. By random, we mean that none of its weights comes from a trained model, and not that the weights are arbitrary. Every convolutional and linear weight of the network and of its head was drawn from the He (Kaiming) normal distribution
[
13
]
. This standard initialization for training deep networks from scratch sets the variance of the weights of a layer with
n_{\mathrm{in}}
inputs to
2/n_{\mathrm{in}}
. All biases were set to zero. Every layer normalization was reset to unit scale and zero shift. Every block multiplies its residual branch by a learnable vector called the layer scale. In He initialization, each layer scale was set to
10^{-6}
, as in the original ConvNeXt initialization
[
27
]
. We applied this reinitialization to the ImageNet checkpoint of each backbone with the seed fixed at 42. No parameter kept its pretrained value. He initialization therefore has the architecture and the classification head of the ImageNet initialization and differs from it only in its starting weights.

ImageNet initialization used the ConvNeXt weights pretrained with supervision on ImageNet-1k at
224\times 224

[
8
]
. DINOv3 initialization used the self-supervised DINOv3 ConvNeXt weights, which were distilled from a vision transformer pretrained on about 1.7 billion web images
[
43

For the in-domain private initialization (InDomain-priv), we carried out the same pretraining under DP-SGD. The two in-domain initializations exist for ConvNeXt-Small only. ImageNet and DINOv3 received a new head with the default initialization of PyTorch. The two in-domain initializations kept the head pretrained on the five findings in the same order.

(1)

where
\mathcal{B}
is a batch of radiographs,

z_{ic}

is its label,

is the number of these that are positive

[

Non-private models were trained for 20 epochs on shuffled batches of 128 radiographs. The last incomplete batch of each epoch was dropped. Every model was validated after each epoch. Differentially private training

DP bounds the influence of any single training example on the model. A randomized mechanism
\mathcal{M}
satisfies
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP if, for every pair of datasets
D
and
D^{\prime}
that differ by the addition or removal of one example and every measurable set of outputs
S
,

\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D)\in S]\;\leq\;e^{\epsilon}\,\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D^{\prime})\in S]+\delta.

(2)

In this study, one example is one radiograph, both in the finetuning on each target dataset and in the private pretraining on MIMIC-CXR. Because one patient can contribute several radiographs, the guarantees bound the influence of each radiograph on the model and not the influence of each patient.

We trained with DP-SGD
[
1
]
as implemented in Opacus
[
55
]
. At each step, the per-sample gradients of a sampled batch
\mathcal{B}
were clipped to a maximum
L_{2}
norm
C
, and Gaussian noise proportional to
C
was added to their sum, giving the privatized gradient

\tilde{g}=\frac{1}{L}\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\frac{g_{i}}{\max\!\left(1,\,\lVert g_{i}\rVert_{2}/C\right)}\;+\;\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\,\sigma^{2}C^{2}\mathbf{I}\right)\right),

(3)

where
g_{i}
is the gradient of the loss of radiograph
i
with respect to all trainable parameters together,
\sigma
is the noise multiplier, and
L
is the number of training radiographs divided by the number of steps per epoch, rounded down. Each training radiograph entered a step independently with probability
q=1/\lfloor N/128\rfloor
for a training set of
N
radiographs. An epoch had
\lfloor N/128\rfloor
steps on every training set except VinDr-CXR, where it had 92 steps, one fewer than
\lfloor N/128\rfloor
. The divisor
L
was therefore 128 on every training set except VinDr-CXR, where it was 130. The privacy loss was tracked with the Rényi differential privacy accountant
[
32
]
of Opacus, which used the reciprocal of the number of steps per epoch as the sampling rate. This rate equals
q
on every training set except VinDr-CXR, where it was 1/92. For each run,
\sigma
was calibrated by the binary search of Opacus from a target
\epsilon
,
\delta=6\times 10^{-6}
,
q
, and a schedule of 150 epochs. With the calibrated
\sigma
, the budget computed for 150 epochs at the rate
q
would be at most the target
\epsilon
and within 0.01 of it. The clipping norm was
C=4
for ConvNeXt-Small and
C=3.5
for ConvNeXt-Tiny. Each sampled batch was processed in physical batches of at most 64 radiographs. The noise was added once per sampled batch. ConvNeXt contains no batch normalization. The network therefore needed no modification for per-sample gradients. The Gaussian noise was drawn from the standard pseudo-random generator of PyTorch, and the secure mode of Opacus was not enabled.

We used the target values
\epsilon=2
, 5, 8, and 11, which correspond to the privacy budgets
\epsilon\leq 1
,
\epsilon\leq 3
,
\epsilon\leq 6
, and
\epsilon\leq 9
, respectively. The spent budget was computed after every epoch. Training stopped once it was at least 0.99, 2.99, 5.99, or 8.99, just below the upper end of the respective budget. The achieved
\epsilon
of a run is the budget spent up to its converged epoch. The converged epoch was chosen after training had ended. The final model therefore satisfies Eq.
2
with the
\epsilon
spent by the whole run and
\delta=6\times 10^{-6}
. Rounded to one decimal, as reported throughout, both the achieved
\epsilon
and the budget spent by the whole run are at most the upper end of the budget of the run.

The reported budgets quantify the privacy of the finetuning stage with respect to the training radiographs of the target dataset. He initialization uses no pretraining, ImageNet and DINOv3 were pretrained on public natural images, and InDomain-stand was pretrained on MIMIC-CXR without a privacy guarantee. For these four initializations, the guarantee therefore covers the finetuning alone. The pretraining of InDomain-priv used the same Opacus implementation of DP-SGD and satisfies a separate guarantee of
\epsilon=7.93
at
\delta=6\times 10^{-6}
for each MIMIC-CXR radiograph. Both its pretraining and its finetuning are therefore differentially private. Supplementary Table

Experimental design

The main experiment comprised 125 runs. Each finetuned ConvNeXt-Small from one of the five initializations on one of the five datasets, at one of the four privacy budgets or without privacy. Each run trained on one graphics processing unit (GPU). The 125 runs took 1,616 GPU hours in total. A private epoch took about twice as long as a non-private epoch on every dataset (Supplementary Table
10
). For each epoch time
t
of a run, we counted the epoch times within
t
plus or minus 5% of the median epoch time. The time per epoch of the run is the mean of the epoch times in the window with the largest count.

The parameter-efficient finetuning experiment compared full finetuning with two schemes that train far fewer parameters. It was run for He initialization, ImageNet, and InDomain-stand on VinDr-CXR and CheXpert at
\epsilon\leq 1
and
\epsilon\leq 6
. Head-only finetuning froze the backbone and trained the 3,845 parameters of the head, with a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-3}
. LoRA
[
15
]
froze the backbone and added a trainable low-rank adapter to each of the 72 pointwise linear layers, 2 in each of the 36 blocks of ConvNeXt-Small. Each adapter replaced the output
W_{0}x
of a frozen layer by
W_{0}x+(\alpha/r)\,B\,\mathrm{dropout}(Ax)
. The matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}
was drawn uniformly from
[-1/\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{in}}},\,1/\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{in}}}]
, and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}
was initialized to zero. We used
r=16
,
\alpha=32
, and a dropout probability of 0.05. LoRA trained 2,169,605 parameters including the head, with a learning rate of
5\times 10^{-4}
. The 24 runs of the two schemes were compared with the 12 corresponding runs of the main experiment with full finetuning.

The leave-one-dataset-out experiment held out each dataset in turn, trained on the pooled training data of the other four, and tested on the held-out dataset, for all five initializations at
\epsilon\leq 1
and without privacy. Checkpoints and thresholds were selected on the pooled validation data of the four training datasets. The held-out dataset was therefore used only as the test set. In the four folds that trained on VinDr-CXR, the official split of VinDr-CXR was used. Its 15,000 training radiographs were used for training. Its 3,000 test radiographs served as validation data, because the test set of those folds came from another dataset. The pooled training sets contained 288,025 to 410,927 radiographs. At
\epsilon\leq 1
, the models of the folds holding out VinDr-CXR were trained for one epoch, because the budget was spent within the first epoch on their pooled training set of 410,927 radiographs. The models of the folds holding out PadChest, ChestX-ray14, CheXpert, and UKA-CXR were trained for 2, 3, 7, and 10 epochs, respectively. The experiment comprised 50 runs.

The capacity experiment comprised 12 runs, which repeated He initialization, ImageNet, and DINOv3 with ConvNeXt-Tiny on CheXpert and VinDr-CXR at
\epsilon\leq 1
and without privacy. The four experiments comprised 211 runs in total.

Evaluation metrics

Each final model was evaluated once on the test set of its dataset, or on the held-out dataset in the leave-one-dataset-out experiment. For each finding, we computed eight metrics: the AUROC, the average precision, the area under the precision-recall curve, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, the Brier score, and the ECE. Each metric was then averaged over the five findings. We report the average of the average precision as the mean average precision. Accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity used the validation thresholds of the model. The other five metrics do not depend on a threshold.

The average precision is the sum of the precision at each rank of the radiographs ordered by predicted probability, weighted by the increase in recall at that rank. The area under the precision-recall curve was computed by trapezoidal integration. The Brier score is the mean squared difference between the predicted probability and the label
[
11
]
. The ECE was computed over
M=15
equal-width bins of the predicted probability as
\sum_{m=1}^{M}(n_{m}/n)\,\lvert\bar{y}_{m}-\bar{p}_{m}\rvert
. Here,
n_{m}
of the
n
radiographs fall in bin
m
, and
\bar{y}_{m}
and
\bar{p}_{m}
are their fraction of positive labels and their mean predicted probability
[
12
]
.

Statistical analysis

Uncertainty was estimated by a nonparametric bootstrap of the test set with 10,000 resamples drawn with seed 42
[
22
]
. For each dataset, one set of resamples was drawn over the ordered test set and reused by every run. All comparisons on that dataset are therefore paired. In each resample, as many units as the test set contains were drawn with replacement. A unit drawn several times contributed all of its radiographs that many times. The unit was the patient for CheXpert, ChestX-ray14, PadChest, and UKA-CXR and the radiograph for VinDr-CXR. Each metric was summarized by the mean and the standard deviation of its 10,000 bootstrap values. This standard deviation, computed with the denominator 9,999, is the bootstrap standard error. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap values form the 95% CI. When a resample contained only one class of a finding, the AUROC, the average precision, and the area under the precision-recall curve of that finding were treated as missing in that resample. The macro average of the resample was then taken over the other findings. For CheXpert, the metrics of atelectasis and pleural effusion were computed over the radiographs with a label for that finding, in the same resamples. All metric values are reported as percentages with one decimal place. Every reported difference, mean over datasets, ratio, rank, and count was computed from these rounded values. The
p
-values were computed from the unrounded bootstrap values.

Initializations were compared on macro AUROC with a two-sided paired bootstrap test. We compared every pair of the five initializations in every dataset and privacy setting of the main experiment. There are 250 such comparisons. For a pair, the 10,000 paired differences of macro AUROC were centered by subtracting their mean. The
p
-value is the fraction of centered differences whose magnitude is at least the magnitude of the mean difference. With 10,000 resamples, the smallest
p
-value that can be resolved is 0.0001. Smaller values are therefore reported as
p<0.0001
. Other
p
-values below 0.001 are given in scientific notation and the rest with three decimals. The
p
-values were not corrected for multiple comparisons, and the significance level was 0.05. Differences are interpreted by their size and by the CIs of the two compared values. A
p
-value indicates statistical reliability and not clinical importance.

For one dataset and privacy setting, let
A_{\mathrm{ImageNet}}
,
A_{\mathrm{DINOv3}}
, and
A_{\mathrm{stand}}
be the macro AUROC of ImageNet, DINOv3, and InDomain-stand, so that the objective contrast is
\Delta_{\mathrm{obj}}=A_{\mathrm{DINOv3}}-A_{\mathrm{ImageNet}}
and the domain contrast is
\Delta_{\mathrm{dom}}=A_{\mathrm{stand}}-A_{\mathrm{ImageNet}}
. Because all initializations are evaluated on the same bootstrap resamples, the paired comparison of InDomain-stand with DINOv3 tests whether the domain contrast exceeds the objective contrast, since in every resample

\Delta_{\mathrm{dom}}-\Delta_{\mathrm{obj}}=A_{\mathrm{stand}}-A_{\mathrm{DINOv3}}.

(4)

Demographic subgroups were defined by sex, male or female, and by three age bands, from above 0 to below 40 years, from 40 to below 70 years, and from 70 to below 100 years. For each subgroup, seven of the eight metrics were computed, all except the area under the precision-recall curve. They used the validation thresholds of the model and the same bootstrap resamples, restricted to the radiographs of the subgroup. This conditional bootstrap keeps each subgroup analysis paired with the analysis of the full test set. The age gap of a model is the macro AUROC of its best age band minus that of its worst age band. The sex gap is the absolute difference in macro AUROC between male and female patients. The worst subgroup is the subgroup with the lowest macro AUROC among the five. The subgroup sizes, the per-finding prevalence, and the number of radiographs excluded for a missing attribute are reported in Supplementary Table
7
. These exclusions applied only to the subgroup analyses. Every other evaluation used the complete test set of each dataset. The excluded fraction was below 1% for every dataset except VinDr-CXR. Of its test radiographs, 58% lack a recorded sex and 84% lack an age. The paired tests cover the comparisons of initializations in the main experiment. The comparisons in the parameter-efficient finetuning, leave-one-dataset-out, capacity, and subgroup analyses are descriptive.

Supplementary Note 1: Dataset curation and label harmonization

Label harmonization

Each dataset was reduced to five findings: atelectasis, cardiomegaly, pleural effusion, pneumonia, and no finding. ChestX-ray14
[
53
]
names pleural effusion as effusion. Pneumonic infiltrates in UKA-CXR
[
21
]
were counted as pneumonia. A healthy examination in UKA-CXR was counted as no finding. UKA-CXR grades cardiomegaly for the whole heart and grades atelectasis, pleural effusion, and pneumonic infiltrates separately for each side of the chest. Each of these findings was counted as present at grade 3 or 4. A finding graded per side was counted as present when either side had one of these grades. VinDr-CXR
[
34
]
, ChestX-ray14, and PadChest
[
5
]
provide binary labels.

CheXpert
[
16
]
labels come from a rule-based report labeler that marks each finding as present, absent, uncertain, or not mentioned. A finding not mentioned in the report was counted as absent. The curated CheXpert list does not contain the 39,323 training radiographs of the release that have an uncertain label for cardiomegaly, lung opacity, lung lesion, edema, or pneumonia. Of these radiographs, 33,351 are frontal. Cardiomegaly and pneumonia therefore have no uncertain label in this study. For atelectasis and pleural effusion, a radiograph with an uncertain label was excluded from the training loss, the validation, the decision threshold, and the evaluation of that finding alone. This applied to 15,330 training, 1,641 validation, and 3,597 test radiographs for atelectasis and to 5,143, 597, and 1,387 radiographs for pleural effusion.

Projection filtering

CheXpert and PadChest were filtered by projection before any split was used. The filter therefore applies to the training, validation, and test data alike. Of the 184,325 radiographs of the curated CheXpert list, we kept the 157,878 frontal radiographs and discarded the 26,447 lateral ones. Of the 160,704 radiographs of the curated PadChest list, we kept the 110,525 posteroanterior, anteroposterior, and horizontal anteroposterior radiographs and discarded the other 50,179, of which 49,538 are lateral. VinDr-CXR, ChestX-ray14, and UKA-CXR were not filtered by projection.

Splits

The curated lists of CheXpert, ChestX-ray14, PadChest, and UKA-CXR assign every patient to one of the training, validation, and test splits. We used these assignments unchanged. ChestX-ray14 keeps its official test split. In each of these four datasets, the validation split contains 10% of the radiographs outside the test split. No patient appears in two splits. VinDr-CXR was released with 15,000 training and 3,000 test radiographs, without a validation split and without patient identifiers. We drew 3,000 of its training radiographs as the validation set, stratified by the combination of the five labels with seed 42. The official test split was kept unchanged. Supplementary Table
8
lists the size of every split.

Demographic subgroups

Sex is recorded as male or female. A radiograph with another or no recorded sex belongs to no sex subgroup. VinDr-CXR records sex for 8,608 of its 18,000 radiographs. Age was grouped into three bands, from above 0 to below 40 years, from 40 to below 70 years, and from 70 to below 100 years. VinDr-CXR records an age of 0 for 13,766 of its radiographs. These radiographs fall into no age band. A radiograph outside a subgroup still contributes to every other result. Supplementary Table
7
lists the size of every subgroup of every test set and the number of test radiographs without a sex or an age band.

Contrasts

Objective contrast (
\Delta_{\mathrm{obj}}
)

Macro AUROC of DINOv3 minus that of ImageNet. Both initializations were pretrained on natural images.

\Delta_{\mathrm{dom}}

Macro AUROC of InDomain-stand minus that of ImageNet. Both initializations were pretrained with labels.

Difference of the contrasts

\Delta_{\mathrm{dom}}-\Delta_{\mathrm{obj}}

4
).

Privacy

Differential privacy (DP)

A guarantee that adding or removing one training radiograph changes the probability of any output by at most a factor of
e^{\epsilon}
, plus
\delta
(Eq.
2
).

Differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD)

Training in which the gradient of each radiograph is clipped to the norm
C
and Gaussian noise of standard deviation
\sigma C
is added to their sum
[
1
]
(Eq.
3
).

Privacy budget

The upper end of the
\epsilon
of a private run, written
\epsilon\leq 1
,
\epsilon\leq 3
,
\epsilon\leq 6
, or
\epsilon\leq 9
, at
\delta=6\times 10^{-6}
.

Target
\epsilon

The value of 2, 5, 8, or 11 for which the noise multiplier
\sigma
was calibrated over 150 epochs.

Converged epoch

The epoch with the highest validation macro AUROC. Its model is the final model of the run.

Achieved
\epsilon

The privacy budget spent up to the converged epoch, given at one decimal.

Finetuning

ConvNeXt-Small and ConvNeXt-Tiny

The main backbone with 49.5 million parameters and the smaller backbone with 27.8 million, which is used in the capacity experiment.

Full finetuning

Training of every parameter of the network and its classification head.

Head-only finetuning

Training of the 3,845 parameters of the classification head of a frozen backbone.

Low-rank adaptation (LoRA)

Training of low-rank adapters
[
15
]
on the 72 pointwise linear layers of a frozen backbone, together with the head, 2,169,605 parameters in total.

Leave-one-dataset-out

Training on the pooled training data of four datasets and testing on the fifth.

Metrics

Macro AUROC

The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) averaged over the five findings, in percent.

Sex gap

The absolute difference in macro AUROC between male and female patients.

Age gap

The macro AUROC of the best minus that of the worst of the age bands from above 0 to below 40, from 40 to below 70, and from 70 to below 100 years.

Worst subgroup

The subgroup with the lowest macro AUROC among male patients, female patients, and the three age bands.

Reporting

Mean
\pm
standard deviation [95% confidence interval (CI)]

The mean, the standard deviation, and the 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles of a metric over 10,000 bootstrap resamples of the test set, drawn with seed 42.

Resampling unit

The patient for CheXpert, ChestX-ray14, PadChest, and UKA-CXR, and the radiograph for VinDr-CXR.

Paired bootstrap test

A two-sided test of the 10,000 paired differences in macro AUROC at the 0.05 level, without correction for multiple comparisons. The smallest
p
