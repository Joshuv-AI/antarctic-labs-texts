Obstructive fibrinous tracheal pseudomembrane complicated with bronchial mucus plug: A rare post-intubation complication case report
Authors: Kairui Shi, Hong Yu, Jiangfeng Shen, Yumin Xu, Zheng Gu, Lin Jiang, Chun Yang
Published: 2026

Background

Obstructive fibrinous tracheal pseudomembrane is a rare but potentially life threatening complication following endotracheal intubation. Its clinical presentation often overlaps with more common post extubation airway conditions, making timely diagnosis challenging. Elderly patients undergoing thoracic surgery are particularly vulnerable to both airway mucosal injury and secretion retention. We present a case of concurrent obstructive fibrinous tracheal pseudomembrane and bronchial mucus plug following double lumen tube intubation for left upper lobectomy, aiming to raise awareness of this rare combination and to discuss its possible pathogenetic mechanisms and preventive strategies.

Case presentation

An 80-year-old female patient (height 152 cm, weight 58 kg) was admitted to the hospital following the identification of a pulmonary nodule in the left lung during a routine physical examination. The patient was a non-smoker but with a medical history of hypertension and coronary atherosclerotic heart disease.

Preoperative coronary computed tomography angiography revealed calcified and non-calcified plaques in the proximal segments of the right coronary artery and the left anterior descending artery, resulting in mild luminal stenosis. Echocardiography demonstrated left atrial enlargement along with mild mitral and aortic valve regurgitation. The patient reported good functional capacity, being able to walk 200 m at a normal pace without limitation.

Preoperative airway assessment was unremarkable (Mallampati class 2, mouth opening >3 fingerbreadths, normal neck range of motion, no neck mass), and the patient was edentulous. Room-air arterial blood gas analysis prior to surgery showed a PO2 of 83.2 mmHg and a PCO2 of 44.9 mmHg.

The patient underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) for left upper lobectomy. Anesthesia induction was uneventful. Direct laryngoscopy revealed a Cormack-Lehane grade 1 view with normal vocal cords. During insertion of a left-sided 35-Fr double-lumen bronchial tube, resistance was encountered immediately below the glottis. The tube was gently rotated and advanced into the bronchus without excessive force. Correct positioning in the left main bronchus was subsequently confirmed by fiberoptic bronchoscopy. The double-lumen tube remained in place for one hour, and the surgical procedure proceeded smoothly. Pathological examination of the left upper lobe specimen confirmed adenocarcinoma with acinar, lepidic, papillary, and micropapillary growth patterns.

Following complete metabolization of anesthetic agents, the patient was extubated and placed on supplemental oxygen via face mask. Upon arrival in the ward, her tidal volume and oxygen saturation were normal, with an Aldrete score ≥ 9.

Forty-eight hours postoperatively, the patient developed chest tightness and shortness of breath in the ward, without dysphagia. Physical examination revealed clear breath sounds bilaterally with no audible wheezing. While receiving oxygen via nasal cannula at 4 L/min, her SpO2 was 99%. Despite treatment with antispasmodic, anti-asthmatic, and anti-inflammatory medications (including methylprednisolone and diprophylline), her symptoms showed no improvement. Emergency arterial blood gas analysis revealed hypoxemia and hypercapnia (PO2 62.5 mmHg, PCO2 63.9 mmHg). Contrast-enhanced chest CT was performed, ruling out acute pulmonary embolism or acute infection.

Six hours later, the patient developed tachypnea with visible suprasternal retraction at rest, suggestive of grade II–III laryngeal obstruction. Emergency fiberoptic laryngoscopy identified subglottic mucosal edema with localized adherent white viscous secretions, causing narrowing of the subglottic tracheal lumen. Airway obstruction due to post-intubation laryngeal edema was considered unlikely. The patient was subsequently transferred from the general ward to the intensive care unit (ICU) for further management.

Nine hours later, physical examination revealed marked inspiratory stridor and the presence of three-sign retractions, consistent with upper airway obstruction. An urgent neck CT scan demonstrated membranous structures within the tracheal lumen (Fig. 1A). Repeat arterial blood gas analysis showed a PO2 of 62.6 mmHg and PCO2 of 59.1 mmHg. Under intravenous sedation and oropharyngeal topical anesthesia with lidocaine, immediate bronchoscopy was performed. Inspection revealed a white pseudomembrane-like substance adherent to the subglottic region, extending in a valvular manner (Fig. 1B). Using biopsy forceps, the material, which was not firmly attached to the airway wall, was removed. Extraction of multiple irregular grayish-white tissue fragments resulted in immediate relief of the patient’s respiratory distress (Fig. 1C, D). The largest fragment measured 2.0 × 0.9 × 0.3 cm and exhibited a tubular bronchial shape (Fig. 1D).

Discussion

Obstructive fibrinous tracheal pseudomembrane (OFTP) is a relatively rare but potentially life-threatening complication following endotracheal intubation, with an incidence that remains poorly defined.1 The precise etiology of OFTP is not fully elucidated. One hypothesis implicates the vulnerability of the subglottic mucosa—the narrowest segment of the adult larynx—to intubation-related trauma.2 Injury to this region may lead to accumulation of sloughed epithelial cells, contributing to the formation of a membrane-like structure. Traumatic intubation, particularly when compounded by aspiration of gastric contents, has also been proposed as a contributory factor. A previously reported case described the development of OFTP 72 h after extubation in a patient with a history of vomiting during a difficult intubation, despite maintenance of cuff pressure at 15 cmH2O.3 Another theory suggests that OFTP may result from ischemic injury secondary to elevated cuff pressure.4, 5 However, cases have been reported even with high-volume, low-pressure cuffs, implying that systemic factors such as hypoperfusion or hypotension may induce relative mucosal ischemia.6 Collectively, mechanical trauma, excessive pressure, or inadequate perfusion can induce ischemia and inflammation of the tracheal mucosa and submucosa. This process triggers dysregulated growth factor secretion, impaired epithelial regeneration, prominent polymorphonuclear infiltration, and fibrinous exudation, ultimately progressing to tracheal stenosis or, in severe instances, complete luminal occlusion.7

Bronchial mucus plugging and retention of secretions are frequently encountered in elderly patients.8 The aggregation of viscous endobronchial secretions, combined with impaired mucociliary clearance—often due to prolonged immobilization, pain-related suppression of cough, and limited expectoration ability—prevents effective mucus elimination.9, 10 Retained secretions accumulate and may consolidate into mucus plugs, which can incorporate necrotic debris and evolve into partial or complete mucus casts.11 When a mucus plug partially obstructs the airway, its incomplete adhesion to the bronchial wall can create a ball-valve effect.8 During the respiratory cycle, airflow mobilizes the plug without fully dislodging it, resulting in a dynamic and potentially progressive obstruction that may culminate in localized airway closure if not promptly relieved.10

In the present case, one of the extracted foreign bodies was identified as a bronchial cast. Based on bronchoscopic and histopathologic findings, we concluded that the patient developed both OFTP and a bronchial mucus cast concurrently. Resistance encountered during intubation, coupled with the absence of post-extubation bronchoscopic evaluation, left open the possibility of undetected mucosal injury. Following left upper lobectomy, surgical trauma and endotracheal tube irritation likely precipitated a marked inflammatory response, leading to the influx of fibrin-rich exudate into the airway. This material accumulated locally, forming a pseudomembrane and mucus cast that overwhelmed normal tracheociliary clearance mechanisms. Concurrently, postoperative pain restricted diaphragmatic and intercostal muscle function, further compromising the expulsion of viscous secretions and exacerbating airway compromise.

Several factors may explain why these two pathologies developed simultaneously in our patient. Resistance encountered during its insertion immediately below the glottis suggests mucosal trauma at the subglottic level, which is the narrowest segment of the adult larynx, and this injury may have initiated the inflammatory cascade leading to pseudomembrane formation. The larger outer diameter and relative rigidity of double-lumen tubes compared to standard endotracheal tubes may increase the risk of such injury, particularly in elderly patients with more fragile airway mucosa.2 Beyond this mechanical insult, the left upper lobectomy itself created a perfect storm for mucus retention, as postoperative pain restricted effective coughing, surgical disruption of pulmonary architecture impaired mucociliary clearance, and the patient’s advanced age further diminished her physiological reserve to clear secretions.9 Thus, the convergence of traumatic intubation as a nidus for pseudomembrane formation with the impaired clearance mechanisms related to surgery and age likely explains why both lesions manifested together.

From a broader pathophysiological perspective, the concurrent development of OFTP and mucus plugging raises intriguing questions about their potential interactions beyond mechanical factors alone. Airway epithelial injury resulting from traumatic intubation, cuff-related ischemia, or surgical manipulation triggers a cascade of inflammatory responses that may predispose to both pseudomembrane formation and mucus hypersecretion.12 Following epithelial damage, injured ciliated cells and goblet cells release pro-inflammatory cytokines, which recruit neutrophils and promote fibrin exudation into the airway lumen.12, 13 This inflammatory exudate, rich in fibrinogen and plasma proteins, can coalesce with sloughed epithelial cells to form the fibrinous pseudomembrane characteristic of OFTP. Simultaneously, epithelial injury disrupts normal mucociliary clearance mechanisms, and ciliary dysfunction combined with inflammation-induced mucus hypersecretion leads to accumulation of tenacious airway secretions.14, 15 Studies have shown that inflammatory mediators such as neutrophil elastase and reactive oxygen species released during airway inflammation can directly stimulate MUC5AC and MUC5B expression in goblet cells, thereby promoting mucus hypersecretion and hyperconcentration.16, 17, 18 The resulting viscous mucus, when combined with impaired clearance, creates an environment conducive to mucus plug formation.19, 20 Notably, the relationship between OFTP and mucus plugging may be bidirectional, as the fibrinous pseudomembrane itself may act as a nidus for mucus accumulation while retained secretions and their associated inflammatory mediators may exacerbate local epithelial injury and perpetuate pseudomembrane formation. This synergistic cycle—where epithelial injury triggers inflammation, and inflammation simultaneously promotes both fibrin exudation and mucus hypersecretion, with each lesion potentiating the other—offers a compelling explanation for the simultaneous presentation observed in our patient.

Most reported cases of OFTP present with stridor, wheezing, or respiratory failure shortly after extubation.21 These manifestations are nonspecific and can mimic more common entities such as laryngeal edema, asthma, or laryngospasm (Table 1). The interval from extubation to symptom onset is highly variable, ranging from 3 h to 9 days in the literature.4 In this patient, symptoms manifested 48 h post-extubation.

Definitive diagnosis of OFTP relies on endoscopic visualization and histopathological confirmation.4, 7 Bronchoscopy remains the gold standard, typically revealing a tubular or patchy white-to-grayish pseudomembrane adherent to the tracheal wall. Cross-sectional imaging such as CT or plain radiography is less sensitive, though it may suggest airway abnormality. Spontaneous expectoration of pseudomembranes has been documented anecdotally,22 but bronchoscopic removal is generally required to relieve obstruction.2 Rigid bronchoscopy is often preferred as it permits maintained ventilation during intervention.5, 21 Although recurrence is uncommon, pseudomembranes may reform2; therefore, follow-up with neck soft-tissue imaging or repeat bronchoscopy is advisable to ensure resolution and monitor for recurrence.

Mucus casts are directly identifiable during bronchoscopy.23 Imaging modalities, including chest CT and radiography, aid in evaluating the extent and severity of airway involvement.8, 24 Management strategies include bronchoscopic clearance techniques (e.g., suctioning, forceps extraction, cryoextraction), nebulized mucolytics, appropriate antimicrobial therapy when infection is present, and optimized airway humidification. In refractory cases, surgical intervention may be necessary.

OFTP represents a rare but critical differential diagnosis in patients with post-extubation airway obstruction. Given its potential for rapid clinical deterioration, a high index of suspicion should be maintained in patients who develop stridor or dyspnea shortly after extubation. Prompt diagnostic evaluation and therapeutic intervention are essential, and clinicians should be prepared to escalate respiratory support, including reintubation or tracheostomy, as clinically indicated.

Several limitations of this case report should be acknowledged. First, while histopathological examination of the subglottic specimen revealed fibrinous exudate with necrotic debris containing acute inflammatory cells and scattered bacterial colonies, microbiological analysis was not performed. Consequently, the specific bacterial species involved remain unidentified, and their potential role in the pathogenesis of OFTP and concomitant mucus plug formation could not be definitively determined. Although bacterial colonization may represent a secondary phenomenon rather than a primary etiologic factor, the possibility that infection contributed to the inflammatory response and subsequent pseudomembrane development cannot be entirely excluded. Future reports involving similar cases would benefit from routine microbiological assessment of bronchoscopically removed specimens to better elucidate the relationship between bacterial infection and OFTP formation. Second, as with any single case report, the generalizability of our findings is limited, and further accumulation of similar cases is warranted to establish more definitive conclusions regarding optimal prevention and management strategies.

Looking toward clinical practice, several preventive measures warrant consideration for similar high-risk patients, particularly elderly individuals undergoing thoracic surgery with double-lumen intubation. Meticulous intubation technique with optimal tube size selection should be emphasized to minimize mucosal trauma, while strict cuff pressure monitoring is essential to avoid overinflation. Postoperative airway clearance protocols should be enhanced to include chest physiotherapy, mucolytics, and adequate humidification. A low threshold for early bronchoscopic evaluation is advisable for patients who develop unexplained respiratory distress or fail to respond to conventional therapy. Additionally, routine post-extubation airway inspection via flexible laryngoscopy may be considered in high-risk populations, though further study is needed to establish its risk-benefit profile.

Conclusions

Obstructive fibrinous tracheal pseudomembrane is a rare but critical cause of post extubation airway obstruction and can occur simultaneously with bronchial mucus plugging in elderly patients after thoracic surgery. The use of a double lumen tube may increase the risk of subglottic mucosal injury, while impaired secretion clearance due to surgery and advanced age predisposes to mucus retention. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion when patients develop progressive respiratory distress unresponsive to conventional therapy. Prompt bronchoscopic evaluation is essential for both diagnosis and treatment. Preventive measures including meticulous intubation technique, cuff pressure monitoring, and enhanced postoperative airway clearance should be considered in high risk populations.
