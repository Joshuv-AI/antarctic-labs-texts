Radiofrequency ablation for colorectal liver metastases: an evolving alternative or complement to resection?
Authors: Yang Sun, Siyu Jiang, Ruolin Wu, Bo Zhou
Published: 2025

Colorectal cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with the liver being the most common site of metastatic spread. Surgical resection has long been considered the gold standard treatment for colorectal liver metastases (CRLM), offering 5-year overall survival rates ranging from 25% to 47% in selected patients (1,2). However, only approximately 20–25% of patients with CRLM are candidates for surgical resection due to strict eligibility criteria, anatomical constraints, underlying liver disease, or comorbidities (3). This significant limitation has driven the development of alternative local treatment modalities, particularly thermal ablation techniques.

Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) has emerged as a minimally invasive treatment option for patients with CRLM who are not candidates for surgical resection or as a complementary approach in combination with resection (1,2). The technique utilizes localized heat to induce protein denaturation in tissue cells, resulting in coagulative necrosis and cell death (4). Recent years have witnessed a substantial increase in the application of RFA, especially for patients with technically inoperable disease or those with poor tolerance to major surgery (5). The expanding role of RFA occurs against the backdrop of rapid advancements in systemic therapies, including chemotherapy, targeted agents, and immunotherapy, raising important issues about how local ablative treatments integrate with systemic approaches in modern multidisciplinary cancer care (6,7).

RFA operates on the principle of converting radiofrequency energy into heat through ionic agitation, leading to coagulative necrosis of tumor tissue (2). The procedure can be performed percutaneously, laparoscopically, or during open surgery, with imaging guidance typically provided by ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), or magnetic resonance imaging (8). The effectiveness of RFA is influenced by several technical factors, including tumor size, location, and proximity to major blood vessels, which can cause heat-sink effects that reduce ablation efficacy (9). The comparative efficacy between ablation techniques and surgical resection has been extensively studied. An umbrella review synthesizing evidence from eleven meta-analyses concluded that surgical resection remains superior to RFA for resectable CRLM in terms of overall survival and disease-free survival (4). However, RFA demonstrated advantages in perioperative outcomes, including lower complication rates and mortality. Importantly, in matched cohort studies, overall survival rates between RFA and liver resection were comparable, though RFA was associated with higher intrahepatic recurrence rates (4). These findings suggest that while resection may offer superior oncological outcomes in ideal candidates, RFA provides a valuable alternative with reduced invasiveness for selected patients.

The integration of RFA with systemic chemotherapy and targeted agents has shown promising results in patients with initially unresectable CRLM. A retrospective study comparing RFA combined with systemic chemotherapy plus targeted therapy versus systemic therapy alone demonstrated significantly improved median progression-free survival (17.16 vs. 8.35 months, P<0.01) and overall survival (34.9 vs. 21.1 months, P<0.01) in favor of the combination approach (5). These findings suggest that RFA can potentiate the effects of systemic therapy, particularly in the first-line setting for advanced disease. Combination strategies also extend to surgical approaches, where RFA is used complementarily with resection to treat multifocal disease. A systematic review and meta-analysis comparing combined thermal ablation and liver resection versus resection alone found no significant differences in 1-, 2-, and 3-year overall survival between the approaches (10). This suggests that while combination therapy expands treatment possibilities for patients with extensive disease, patient selection and technique choice significantly influence outcomes.

Technical success and local tumor control are highly dependent on achieving adequate ablative margins. Multiple studies have consistently demonstrated that a minimum ablative margin (MAM) of ≥5 mm is critical for optimal local control (11,12). A multicenter study using deformable CT registration and artificial intelligence-based autosegmentation found that 3-year local disease progression rates decreased to 0% for margins ≥5 mm compared to 79% for 0 mm margins (11). Similarly, research evaluating two different confirmation software packages showed no local tumor progression in CRLM ablated with MAM ≥5 mm (12).

Appropriate patient selection is paramount for optimizing outcomes following RFA for CRLM. Several clinical and tumor-related factors have been identified as significant predictors of treatment success and survival. Tumor size remains a critical determinant, with lesions <3 cm generally achieving better local control (1). Perivascular tumor location has been associated with increased risk of residual tumor [odds ratio (OR) =6.673] and poorer local tumor progression-free survival (13). Additionally, baseline carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level >30 ng/mL, multiple tumors, and positive nodal status of the primary tumor have been identified as independent predictors of overall survival (14). Frailty assessment has emerged as an important consideration in patient selection for percutaneous ablative therapies. A nationwide analysis demonstrated that frail patients (as defined by Hospital Frailty Risk Score ≥5) undergoing percutaneous ablation for CRLM had significantly increased risks of complications (adjusted OR =4.80), longer hospital stays, and higher total hospital charges (15). This underscores the need for comprehensive patient assessment beyond tumor characteristics alone.

Despite significant advancements, several challenges and limitations persist in the application of RFA for CRLM. Local recurrence rates remain higher compared to surgical resection, particularly for tumors >3 cm and those located near major blood vessels (4,13,16). The lack of high-quality randomized controlled trials comparing RFA to other treatment modalities represents another significant limitation, with most available evidence derived from retrospective studies or single-center experiences (8). Besides, molecular factors such as KRAS mutations present both a challenge and opportunity for personalized treatment approaches. The finding that KRAS-mutant tumors require wider safety margins for adequate local control suggests that ablation parameters might need to be adjusted based on tumor biology (17). However, the mechanisms underlying the association between KRAS status and treatment response remain incompletely understood, warranting further investigation.

The integration of RFA with immunotherapy represents a particularly promising future direction. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that RFA induces an anti-tumor immunity that is insufficient to prevent recurrences but can be potentiated by intratumoral immunotherapy (18). In mouse models, RFA combined with intratumoral granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) and Bacillus Calmette-Guerin resulted in complete metastases regression. A trinity nano-vaccine system with spatiotemporal immune effect combined with RFA showed enhanced abscopal effects and improved survival in preclinical models of colorectal cancer liver metastases (19). Bibliometric analysis has identified immunotherapy as an emerging niche field in local treatment of CRLM, with preclinical studies showing enhanced anti-tumor immunity and survival when combining RFA with anti-PD-1 agents (6).

RFA has firmly established itself as an indispensable component in the multidisciplinary management of CRLM. While surgical resection remains the gold standard for resectable disease, RFA offers a minimally invasive alternative with comparable survival outcomes in carefully selected patients, particularly those with smaller tumors (<3 cm) and favorable biology (1,4,9). The technique’s value extends beyond standalone therapy to include combination approaches with resection for multifocal disease and integration with systemic therapies for advanced cases (5,10). The evolution of RFA technology, particularly the development of microwave ablation, has addressed some limitations of traditional RFA, offering larger ablation zones and reduced heat-sink effects (9,20). Concurrent advancements in imaging guidance, particularly MR-guided ablation and fusion imaging techniques, have significantly improved procedural precision and local tumor control. The establishment of ≥5 mm minimal ablative margin as a technical goal and the development of tools to quantify this margin intraprocedurally represent significant steps toward standardizing ablation procedures and optimizing outcomes (11,12). Molecular characterization of tumors, including KRAS mutation status and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) monitoring, provides opportunities for personalized treatment approaches and improved patient selection (17). The emerging evidence on the interaction between ablation and the immune system opens exciting possibilities for combining local and immunotherapeutic strategies (19). Future research directions should prioritize prospective randomized trials, refinement of combination strategies, development of predictive biomarkers, and exploration of novel therapeutic combinations.

In conclusion, RFA should be viewed as complementary rather than inferior to surgical resection in the management of CRLM. Its role continues to evolve within the context of multidisciplinary care, offering a valuable tool for expanding treatment options and improving outcomes for patients with liver-limited disease. As technology advances and our understanding of tumor biology deepens, RFA is poised to remain an important component of comprehensive liver-directed therapy for colorectal cancer metastases.
