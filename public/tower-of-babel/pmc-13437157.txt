Efficacy of Intraligamentous Steroid Injection in Pain Reduction and Knee Motion Improvement in Patients with Mucoid Degeneration of the Anterior Cruciate Ligament
Authors: Sang Yoon Kim, Byung Sun Choi, Du Hyun Ro, Hyuk-Soo Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study assessed the efficacy and safety of fluoroscopy-guided intraligamentous steroid injections in managing mucoid degeneration of the anterior cruciate ligament (MDACL) in patients resistant to oral medication and intra-articular steroid injections.MethodsA retrospective review of 294 knees diagnosed with MDACL via magnetic resonance imaging between October 2014 and June 2023 was conducted. After applying exclusion criteria and excluding patients who were asymptomatic or showed improvement with conservative treatment, 82 knees were included in the final analysis. Intraligamentous steroid injections were performed under C-arm fluoroscopy by a single orthopedic surgeon. Clinical data collected included the duration of the pain-free period, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs; pain numeric rating scale, the International Knee Documentation Committee subjective score, Lysholm score, and Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score), range of motion, and other radiological measures. Symptom recurrence rates were determined using the Kaplan-Meier survival analysis.ResultsThe average age at the time of injection was 64.0 years (range, 39–83 years), and the mean follow-up period was 19.5 months (range, 12–90 months). Pain improvement was noted in 78 of 82 knees (95.1%), with an average effect duration of 7.8 months (standard deviation, 9.1). Improvement of limitation of knee motion was observed in 49 of 50 cases (98.0%). Kaplan-Meier analysis indicated symptom recurrence rates of 19.5% at 3 months, 39.0% at 6 months, and 48.8% at 1 year. All PROMs significantly improved after injection for 6 months (p < 0.01) and then slightly declined. No patients reported post-procedure instability.ConclusionsFluoroscopy-guided intraligamentous steroid injection demonstrated short-term efficacy in relieving pain and improving knee motion in MDACL patients resistant to conventional treatments, although further studies are warranted to confirm these findings.Keywords: Anterior cruciate ligament, Mucoid degeneration, Knee joint, Glucocorticoids, Fluoroscopy

Mucoid degeneration of the anterior cruciate ligament (MDACL) involves a chronic degenerative process that results in an enlarged ACL, potentially accompanied by synovial cysts, which could lead to impingement within the femoral condylar notch. Histologic studies have revealed mucoid and/or chondroid material intermingled within the ACL fibers.1) Although the precise cause of MDACL remains unclear, it is hypothesized that inflammation following minor injuries or due to osteoarthritis could significantly contribute to ACL degeneration.2) Previous literature indicates that the prevalence of MDACL, as identified via magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), ranges from 1.8% to 5.3%. However, the occurrence of symptomatic MDACL, which requires clinical intervention, is much less frequently reported, with a 0.4% prevalence.3,4) The most common symptoms include a gradual onset of posterior knee pain and a limited range of motion (ROM), particularly affecting terminal knee flexion or extension.5,6) MRI is the gold standard for diagnosis, revealing a distinctively striated signal in an enlarged ACL on T2/proton density-weighted images, a phenomenon filling the entire intercondylar notch, commonly known as the “celery stalk sign.”7,8) Common arthroscopic findings include loss of the ACL synovial lining, increased ACL volume with continuous fibers, and yellowish-colored material within the ACL substance.9)

While conventional management of MDACL focuses on pain medication such as oral nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and intra-articular steroid injections, the effectiveness of these treatments remains limited and uncertain. For cases unresponsive to conservative treatments, surgical intervention via arthroscopic decompression, involving either partial or complete resection of the ACL, is often considered the next step.10,11) However, this method has its drawbacks, as excessive debridement poses a risk of causing knee instability. Through a comprehensive literature review, 1 study was identified that employed ultrasound-guided non-surgical decompression procedures, including cyst fluid aspiration and intraligamentous steroid injections.12) However, this study primarily involved cases with cystic lesions of the ACL, which accounted for 76% of its subjects. Additionally, the procedure was performed through the posterior side of the knee. Six months post-procedure, 17.4% of participants expressed dissatisfaction with the outcomes.

In this study, we aimed to assess the efficacy and safety of fluoroscopy-guided intraligamentous steroid injections in reducing pain and improving knee motion in MDACL patients resistant to oral medication and intra-articular injections. We hypothesized that this intervention would lead to significant improvement in patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and ROM, providing symptomatic relief in this refractory population.

METHODS

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committees and the 1975 Helsinki Declaration, as revised in 2000. Ethics approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. H-2504-186-1641). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Board due to the retrospective nature of the study and anonymization of patient data.

Patient Selection and Diagnostic Criteria

A retrospective analysis was performed on a cohort of patients who were diagnosed with MDACL via knee MRI from October 2014 to June 2023 (n = 294). Patients without MDACL-related symptoms or who showed symptom improvement with oral medication or intra-articular injections (n = 181) were excluded, leaving 113 knees for intraligamentous injection. Resistance to conservative treatment was defined as persistent posterior knee pain with a numeric rating scale (NRS) score > 7 and limited ROM despite at least 3 months of oral NSAID therapy and at least 2 documented intra-articular steroid injections, as recorded in patient charts. Further exclusion criteria included inadequate follow-up (< 1 year), concomitant posterior cruciate ligament injections, use of injection during ACL reduction-plasty, and multiple injections in the same knee. After exclusion, 82 knees were analyzed in the final cohort (Fig. 1).

The chief complaint of all patients was a gradual onset of pain in the posterior knee area, exacerbated by flexion or extension, without any notable traumatic event. In most cases, patients were referred for further intervention after insufficient symptom relief with NSAIDs or physiotherapy over a 2- to 6-month period. The radiological diagnostic criteria for MDACL on MRI findings were as follows: overall high signal intensity of the ACL in T2-weighted images without discontinuity, increased ACL diameter (> 10 mm), and poorly visualized, thickened ACL fibers (Fig. 2).6,13)

Injection Technique

All procedures were performed by a single experienced orthopedic surgeon (HSH). Prior to the injection procedure, patients were positioned supine with the knee flexed at 30°–45°. Local anesthesia was achieved by injecting 2–3 mL of 2% lidocaine into the anteromedial skin and infrapatellar fat pad of the affected knee. Using fluoroscopy (C-arm), a 22G spinal needle was advanced from the tibial side toward the femoral side, aligned with the ACL fibers and targeting the femoral insertion site. Needle guidance was aided by referencing the Blumensaat line on lateral fluoroscopic views.

Because the intercondylar notch is densely occupied by the cruciate ligament fibers, advancing the needle until it gently contacted the femoral insertion reliably indicated that the tip was fully embedded within the ligamentous substance (Fig. 3). From this confirmed intraligamentous position, the needle was then slowly withdrawn while 40 mg of triamcinolone acetate was injected, allowing the steroid to disperse along the ACL fibers within the substance of the ligament. This technique minimized the possibility of extra-ligamentous injection and promoted uniform intraligamentous distribution. No additional NSAIDs, physiotherapy, or specific ROM or activity restrictions were prescribed after the procedure. Patients were monitored for 1 hour before discharge.

Follow-up and Outcome Measures

All patients were asked to visit the outpatient clinic at post-injection intervals of 3 weeks, 6 weeks, 6 months, and 1 year, and thereafter for symptom recurrence monitoring. Clinical data assessed at pre-injection and each follow-up included pain characteristics (duration, location, and triggering or aggravating factors), ROM, and PROMs such as the NRS, the International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) subjective score, Lysholm score, and Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS). To aid in the interpretation of pain reduction, changes in pain NRS were compared with the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 1.4 points reported by Bensa et al.14) for patients undergoing intra-articular corticosteroid injections for knee osteoarthritis. ROM improvement was determined qualitatively based on outpatient chart documentation (e.g., resolution of terminal flexion or extension limitation), rather than standardized goniometric measurements. Radiological assessments included Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) osteoarthritis grade from standing knee radiographs and combined intra-articular lesions from knee MRIs. Because this study did not include a control group, the observed changes in PROMs were further contextualized using previously reported MCID thresholds for pain NRS referenced from disease- and intervention-specific literature.

Statistical Analysis

For pre- and post-injection functional scores, pairwise comparisons using a paired t-test were conducted, followed by Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Survivorship analysis with recurrence of symptoms as an endpoint was performed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves. All results were analyzed using IBM SPSS ver. 22.0 (IBM Corp.). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patient Demographics and Baseline Characteristics

The demographic and pre-injection data are summarized in Table 1. The average age of the subjects at the time of injection was 64.0 years (range, 39–83 years), with a mean follow-up period of 19.5 months (range, 12–90 months). Among the subjects, 62 (75.6%) were female and 20 were male. The distribution of KL grades was as follows: grade 0 in 26 cases (31.7%), grade 1 in 16 cases (19.5%), grade 2 in 26 cases (31.7%), grade 3 in 12 cases (14.6%), and grade 4 in 2 cases (2.4%). Additionally, cystic changes in the ACL occurred in 19 cases (23.2%). Associated MRI findings of intra-articular lesions included a medial meniscus tear in 29 cases, predominantly degenerative tears, medial meniscus posterior root tears in 17 cases, and lateral meniscus tears in 6 cases.

Clinical Efficacy and Knee Motion Outcomes

Of the 82 injections administered, posterior knee pain was relieved in 78 cases (95.1%). Post-injection, 49 cases achieved complete pain resolution, 29 experienced partial improvement, and 4 showed no discernible improvement. The average duration of pain recurrence was 7.8 months (standard deviation, 9.1) for the final 82 subjects. In 59 of 82 cases (70.5%), the treatment provided relief for more than 6 months. In a Kaplan-Meier analysis with recurrence of symptoms as the endpoint, the recurrence rates at 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year were 19.5%, 39.0%, and 48.8%, respectively (Fig. 4). A statistically significant difference in time to pain recurrence was noted between knees with meniscal lesions and those without, at 7.7 months and 8.3 months, respectively (p < 0.001).

A Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrating the recurrence rate of symptoms at various time intervals.

Prior to the injection, 50 of 82 knees (61%) exhibited limited knee motion (LOM), with 46 cases showing flexion limitations and 4 cases with extension deficits. Following the fluoroscopy-guided intraligamentous steroid injection, LOM resolved in 49 of the 50 cases, with only 1 patient showing no significant improvement.

PROMs and Subsequent Management

At the 3-week follow-up, improvements were observed across all PROMs. Although PROM scores decreased slightly after 6 months, they remained higher at 12 months post-injection than at baseline (Fig. 5). The reported MCID for the pain NRS is a 1.4-point reduction in patients undergoing intra-articular corticosteroid injections for knee osteoarthritis. In our cohort, the mean NRS decreased by 3.4 points at 3 weeks (from 5.1 to 1.7).

After recurrence of pain, 16 patients received repeat injections, which were administered between 3 and 18 months after the initial treatment. In addition, 8 patients underwent arthroscopic ACL reduction-plasty during follow-up. No patient reported post-procedure instability during the follow-up period. There was 1 instance of transient facial flushing after injection. No cases of infection, hemarthrosis, or neurovascular complications were observed.

DISCUSSION

The most salient findings of this study highlight that fluoroscopy-guided intraligamentous steroid injections are highly efficacious in managing pain and resolving limitation of knee motion for MDACL patients, achieving a 95.1% response rate with minimal adverse reactions. Furthermore, 61.0% of cases reported symptom relief for more than 6 months. Knee ROM improved in 98% of patients after the injections. PROMs, including pain NRS, IKDC, Lysholm, and KOOS, improved markedly at 3 weeks and, although a modest decline was observed after 6 months, remained higher at 12 months after injection than at baseline, suggesting short- to midterm symptomatic and functional benefit within the first year of follow-up. In particular, the mean pain NRS score decreased from 5.1 at baseline to 1.7 at 3 weeks, corresponding to a 3.4-point reduction, which exceeds the reported MCID of 1.4 points for knee pain by more than twofold. This magnitude of change supports that the analgesic effect of intraligamentous steroid injection is likely to be clinically meaningful for most symptomatic MDACL patients. Nevertheless, these results should be interpreted cautiously, as the study lacked a comparator arm, was based on retrospective observational data, and some patients ultimately required repeat injections or arthroscopic reduction-plasty, indicating that the effect may not be uniformly durable.

These clinical findings should be interpreted in the broader context of the epidemiology and pathophysiology of MDACL. Although the precise nature of MDACL remains ambiguous, literature reviews have indicated that its prevalence in MRI studies can reach up to 5.3%.3,4) Data from the Osteoarthritis Initiative revealed that among baseline MR images of the right knees of 1,899 individuals, 37 exhibited mucoid degeneration.15) Despite its relatively high prevalence, MDACL is seldom symptomatic3,4) and its pathogenesis remains elusive.6) Previous studies have explored various theories for MDACL pathogenesis, including traumatic, synovial, and degenerative theories.6,10,16) It is generally accepted that impingement of the hypertrophied ACL on the narrow intercondylar notch causes these symptoms.5,17) Another study on conservative treatment reported that the most common symptoms were posterior knee pain (77.4%) and limited terminal flexion (58.1%).16) Moreover, 77.8% of patients treated conservatively reported ongoing knee pain and regular analgesia use at a mean 4-year follow-up. In our study, 38.4% of MDACL patients identified through MRI exhibited symptoms severe enough to necessitate intraligamentous ACL injections.

Histologic studies indicate that inflammation in the ACL substance and ligament sheath increases with age.2) Inflammation in the ACL manifests as leukocyte infiltration and neovascularization in both the ligament sheath and the ACL substance. The ligament sheath is more susceptible to inflammation than the ACL substance, and the inflammation in the ACL substance often follows that of the ligament sheath (Fig. 6). There is a positive correlation between ACL inflammation and ACL degeneration. In normal ACLs, inflammatory cells are primarily found in perivascular areas of the ligament sheath. Conversely, few inflammatory cells are present between collagen fibers in the ACL substance. However, ACLs with degeneration exhibit increased infiltration of inflammatory cells not only in perivascular regions but also between collagen fibers within the ACL substance. Nonetheless, chronic and advanced stages of mucoid degeneration demonstrate diminished infiltration of inflammatory cells. Given that steroids exert anti-inflammatory effects, we hypothesize that intraligamentous steroid injections may alleviate symptoms by reducing localized inflammation in early to mid-stage degeneration and potentially contributing to a reduction in ACL volume, thereby leading to clinical improvement. The observed variation in the duration of effectiveness of intraligamentous steroid injections in our study is likely due to this factor. We are uncertain whether the lack of response is because the case has advanced beyond the inflammatory phase of ACL degeneration, necessitating further research.

There is no consensus on the treatment of MDACL, and evidence is scarce. The primary management of MDACL typically involves arthroscopic debridement combined with notchplasty in cases of impingement with the lateral wall of the notch.5,18,19) Morice et al.11) proposed an alternative intervention called reduction-plasty, which reduces the hypertrophic ACL to its normal size without removing all mucoid substances. A systematic review indicated that arthroscopic debridement of the ACL offers satisfactory pain relief and improves knee outcome scores.20) Most studies show postoperative pain relief ranging from 53.8% to 95%; however, the study by Kim et al.21) observed that only 53.8% of patients achieved complete pain relief following arthroscopic surgery. There was an improvement in postoperative ROM and outcome scores (Lysholm and IKDC scores and the KOOS). A high incidence of postoperative knee laxity, marked by a positive Lachman test, was noted in most studies, with up to 6% of patients experiencing symptomatic instability requiring delayed ACL reconstruction.18) However, many studies also included subjects who had undergone concurrent arthroscopic procedures to address accompanying pathological knee conditions. MRI and arthroscopic evaluations in a specific cohort identified ganglion cysts around the ACL as the most common pathology.22) The complexity of combined procedures and co-existing pathologies makes it difficult to attribute symptomatic improvement solely to ACL debridement. In contrast, our study aims to assess exclusively the effectiveness of intraligamentous steroid injections without concurrent procedures or medications.

The use of corticosteroids within ligamentous tissue remains controversial, and there is limited evidence specifically supporting intraligamentous steroid injection in MDACL. However, MDACL is a degenerative condition with limited regenerative potential and no clearly established, universally effective treatment; current options are largely confined to conservative management with oral NSAIDs or intra-articular steroid injections on one hand, and arthroscopic reduction-plasty or debridement on the other. In this context, we explored intraligamentous steroid injection as a minimally invasive intermediate intervention aimed at directly reducing intraligamentous edema and impingement-related symptoms. Triamcinolone acetate was chosen for its intermediate- to long-acting depot characteristics, which allow sustained anti-inflammatory activity suited to reducing intraligamentous edema in MDACL. Given its established safety in musculoskeletal soft-tissue applications and the minimal risk associated with single-dose administration, it represents an appropriate agent for this intervention.

Few studies have documented the outcomes of conservative treatment for MDACL.12,17) One such study described an ultrasound-guided decompression procedure that included intraligamentous steroid injections.12) This case series utilized a posterior approach with inherent risks of neurovascular complications. Conversely, we adopted an anterior approach using C-arm fluoroscopy to reduce these risks. Additionally, we targeted the proximal femoral attachment in all cases, given the predominantly posterior location of pain in our study population. We generally preferred a longitudinal access aligned with the ligament’s direction and length. While we believe the anterior fluoroscopic approach offers improved safety and accuracy, future comparative studies directly evaluating this technique against ultrasound-guided methods would strengthen this assertion.

This study has several limitations. It is a retrospective case series with predominantly Asian patients, raising concerns about ethnic variability and the challenge of generalizing results to a broader population. Additionally, the absence of a control group limits the robustness of the conclusions. As this was a retrospective single-center study, the possibility of selection bias cannot be completely excluded. Because the study relied on outpatient clinical records, knee ROM was documented qualitatively rather than measured with standardized goniometric methods, which limits the precision of our ROM analysis. Furthermore, the lack of MRI follow-up to assess structural changes in relation to symptom recurrence restricts deeper interpretation of long-term outcomes. Nonetheless, we included patients who were unresponsive to other conservative treatments, and no effective non-surgical alternatives comparable to our method were available. Moreover, we included a relatively large cohort of MDACL patients compared to previous studies.

In conclusion, fluoroscopy-guided intraligamentous steroid injections appear to provide meaningful short- to midterm symptom relief and functional improvement in MDACL patients with posterior knee pain and restricted knee motion, especially among those unresponsive to conventional treatments such as oral NSAIDs and intra-articular steroid injections. However, given the absence of a control group, relatively short follow-up, and variable duration of treatment effect, our findings should be interpreted cautiously, and further controlled studies are warranted.
