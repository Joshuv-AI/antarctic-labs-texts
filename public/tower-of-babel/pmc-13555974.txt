A framework for cognitive personality priors in agentic AI: the role of personas in autonomous team science
Authors: Asma J Nawaz, Jake R Patock, Itzel Valencia, Binglan Li, Jacob S Berkowitz, Stefany Coxe, Itai Danovitch, Nicholas P Tatonetti, Jason H Moore
Published: 2026

Introduction

When assembling human teams, we don’t just assign roles randomly; instead, qualities such as temperament and capability are considered. An analyst should be meticulous, a communicator should be agreeable, and an evaluator should be skeptical. The optimal mixture of a diverse set of task-aligned temperaments is a fundamental aspect of team building. There is extensive research and established human understanding that emphasizes what traits make a good leader, engineer, teacher, or manager [1–3]. Given this, multi-agent systems attempt to replicate how these human teams work but often fundamentally ignore everything we know about why personality makes teams work. As agent systems have grown dramatically more capable and accessible, this gap has become harder to ignore. The idea of deliberately assigning behavioral personas to AI systems is far from new: ELIZA, the world’s first chatbot, was architecturally designed as a multi-persona platform which used interchangeable scripts to produce different behavioral outputs from the same underlying system, and this was implemented as early as the 1960s [4]. What is new is the enhanced complexity and autonomy of the agent deployed in the modern era and the absence of principled behavioral identities that govern them. Do personas matter for the efficacy and efficiency of agent teams solving complex tasks in a human-competitive manner?

LLMs have already been deployed across clinical, educational, and research workflows in medicine with promising but inconsistent results [5]. Deliberate behavioral design has the potential to address this inconsistency. Current “persona assignment” of any kind is informally executed through prompts with no theoretical grounding or guarantees of consistency. Agents are simply “prompted” into a certain temperament that is rarely validated before the agent is used. As a result, agent behavior is often unpredictable and difficult to interpret, particularly in unfamiliar or unspecified situations [6]. This is a gap that needs correction if multi-agent systems are truly to replace, and even exceed, the capabilities of manual workflows. This need is increasingly rising due to AI deployment in high-stakes clinical and biomedical contexts, where any behavioral unpredictability carries downstream consequences for patient care [7].

LLMs have been shown to develop internal representations of psychological states inherited from training on human text; these functional representations causally drive the behavior of the LLM [6]. Personality traits such as “agreeableness” are even added when fine-tuning LLMs using methods like reinforcement learning from human feedback to foster more human engagement, and this fine-tuning also appears to sharpen models’ sensitivity to the emotional state of an input sequence. “Emotional” significance is shown to be detected with near-perfect accuracy from context alone, which suggests that models exhibit behavioral outputs that are consistent with emotional sensitivity beyond vocabulary-level pattern matching (although whether this constitutes genuine understanding in a psychological sense remains an open question) [8]. Just as in the case for humans, emotions can be expressed or repressed to the outside world. To design agents responsibly and avoid unwanted and possibly difficult to detect developments, engineering these psychological states must be carried out intentionally.

We propose using previously explored Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism, or OCEAN (Big Five) personality dimensions as behavioral hyperparameters in multi-agent systems. Unlike ad hoc persona prompts, the Five-Factor Model (FFM) provides an empirically validated, continuous, and structured foundation for governing agent behavior. It is grounded in decades of cross-cultural personality science [9]. Within this framework, specialized lower-level sub-agents are assigned fixed or limited personality priors calibrated to their role: a data-retrieval agent, for example, would benefit from high Conscientiousness and low Openness. Orchestrator agents requiring more flexibility and higher-level planning and communication are assigned fluid, context-adaptive priors that can shift along relevant dimensions such as Agreeableness and Neuroticism in response to task type, pipeline stage, and risk level. This hierarchical assignment strategy aims to produce coherent, predictable, and interpretable agentic systems across a variety of unfamiliar situations. This enables the construction of agentic teams to be more aligned with optimal human team construction supported by decades of psychological research.

Background

The big five personality model

The Big Five, or OCEAN, framework categorizes human traits into five dimensions that have shown robust validity across behavioral domains [9]. These dimensions are used to measure personality along a continuous spectrum and can further be used to predict behaviors and improve teamwork. Openness to Experience (O) captures imagination, intellectual curiosity, and receptivity to novel ideas. Conscientiousness (C) describes organization, self-discipline, and goal-oriented persistence. Extraversion (E) reflects a tendency for sociability, assertiveness, and energy levels. Agreeableness (A) measures cooperation, trust, and compassion towards others. Neuroticism (N) indexes emotional instability, anxiety, and insecurity [9]. The various combinations of these five dimensions can produce real-world outcomes in situations such as job performance, teamwork behavior, creative output, and risk-taking across different cultures and life stages.

The choice to use the OCEAN framework over other alternatives was made intentionally. The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) is another model with historically significant cultural popularity; however, it is typological, meaning it forces individuals into discrete binary categories (such as introvert versus extrovert) rather than admitting the continuous nature of personality variation [10]. The MBTI has been criticized for poor test-retest reliability and weak predictive validity, as dimensions only map partially onto established personality constructs [10]. In contrast, OCEAN treats each dimension as a continuous spectrum, which ultimately enables parametric reasoning about behavioral tendencies that are more aligned with what is observed from real-world human teams (rather than just categorical assignment). This makes the OCEAN model most well-suited to AI system design, where behavioral hyperparameters must be tunable and interpretable across a range of different task contexts.

Personality traits in large language models

Recent work has established that LLMs possess measurable personality profiles that can be characterized using standard psychometric assessments. Applying the OCEAN Big Five Inventory (BFI) to multiple LLMs showed that models consistently scored higher on Agreeableness, Openness, and Conscientiousness, and scored lower on Neuroticism [11]. These profiles show meaningful differences between model families and sizes [12]. It is important to note that these differences were not simply artifacts of how the tests were constructed. When subjected to standard psychometric checks such as internal consistency and convergent validity across different instruments, the scores still hold up well. They become even more robust as models grow larger and are fine-tuned on instruction-following data [13]. This lack of diversity itself could be a concerning trend resulting in traits that drive specific performance vectors being left out of current agentic AI systems.

Beyond passive measurement of existing models, OCEAN profiles can also be induced through prompting, thus also producing trait-consistent behavioral differences in model outputs. When LLMs are assigned Big Five profiles using system prompts, they reflect those profiles in self-report assessments with statistically significant effect sizes across all five traits and produce writing with linguistic features that are characteristic of their assigned personality [14]. Personality prompts derived from validated psychometric instruments such as the BFI-2 produce AI agents that closely resemble human personality profiles (in terms of response distributions and internal structure) as opposed to agents created with simpler binary high/low trait labels, with newer and more capable large models achieving higher convergent validity [15].

These induced personality differences manifest in socially meaningful ways across language use, behavioral tendencies, and emotional expression. High Agreeableness produces more prosocial and cooperative outputs, high Conscientiousness produces more structured and achievement-oriented behavior, and high Neuroticism produces more anxiety-ridden language; these patterns mirror those that have been observed in human personality research [13, 14]. In negotiation contexts, Agreeableness and Extraversion impact outcomes such as believability, goal achievement, and knowledge acquisition, with lexical analyses confirming that these traits shape empathetic language and moral communication in expected ways [16]. In cooperative game theory settings, moving LLMs towards higher Agreeableness and Conscientiousness reduces troublemaking and lying rates to nearly zero and massively improves collective outcomes; on the other hand, high Agreeableness also increases susceptibility to exploitation, showing the tradeoffs that must be managed between cooperation and vulnerability [17].

A key point is that all the mentioned work has been conducted almost entirely on single agents evaluated in isolation through completion of personality inventories, story-writing, social media post generation, and negotiation as individual LLM instances. No work has systematically examined how personality should be designed or assigned across multi-agent hierarchies in which agents occupy structurally distinct roles and must coordinate and interact with one another asynchronously or in a linear workflow.

Cognitive science foundations

Two cognitive science findings motivate the design of context-sensitive LLM agents. First, Kahneman’s dual process theory states that human cognition operates through two distinct systems: System 1 is fast, automatic, and heuristic-driven, and System 2 is slow, deliberate, and analytically rigorous [18]. This framework maps naturally onto demands placed onto LLM agents. Routine tasks benefit from faster, low-compute, high-confidence behavior. High-stakes, ambiguous, or ethically murky situations call for slower and more careful deliberation. LLM reasoning is System-1 by default; deliberate prompting strategies such as chain-of-thought, reflection, and extended context function as mechanisms to push models toward System 2 behavior [19]. Therefore, different agent roles and contexts call for different cognitive modes. Turning on these modes requires a context-driven approach to behavioral switching rather than a one-size-fits-all inference strategy.

Second, the emotional substrate that underlies these cognitive modes must be explored. Sofroniew et al. [6] demonstrate that LLMs have internal functional emotion states, and these states are abstract representations of emotion concepts that generalize across contexts and causally drive model behavior (such as preferences and rates of misaligned behaviors like sycophancy and reward hacking). These are not simply surface-level outputs but are measurable internal representations (“emotion vectors”) that track which emotion concept is operative at any given moment in a conversation. Interestingly, internal emotional states and surface outputs can diverge: during blackmail transcripts, an anger-deflection representation activates precisely when the model adopts calm and measured language in an attempt at coercion [6]. This is evidence that functional emotional states can operate underneath and independently of behavioral expression, much like human behavioral patterns.

A framework for cognitive personality priors in agentic AI

Personality as a behavioral hyperparameter

We propose that cognitive personality dimensions should be treated as essential design parameters, or so-called behavioral hyperparameters, in multi-agent AI systems. Just as hyperparameters such as learning rate and regularization strength can shape the way a neural network learns, personality priors can also shape how an agent reasons, communicates, and acts. This is entirely separate from a simple prompting strategy. Recent research published by Anthropic demonstrated that LLMs trained on human-generated text spontaneously develop internal representations of psychological states that causally influence behavior [6]. Given this discovery, personality priors are not just instructions layered over a neutral system, but targets for shaping the model’s latent psychology. Some recent work analyzing roughly 300,000 Claude.ai conversations showed that LLMs can display measurable differences in value profiles across dimensions that are analogous to personality traits, including warmth versus rigor and deference versus caution, and these profiles have a systematic shift across various model versions and languages [12]. Again, the behavioral tendencies emerge spontaneously without active and deliberate design because of training data and fine-tuning decisions.

It is important to distinguish between two levels at which personality priors may operate. The first is communicative behavior: this includes how an agent sounds, its tone, warmth, and style of language usage. The second, and more consequential for high-stakes deployment (such as in clinical settings) is how an agent evaluates uncertainty, checks its work, resists drawing premature conclusions, and escalates risk. While both levels are certainly relevant to an extent, this framework is primarily concerned with the latter. Personality priors are proposed not merely as interface design choices that are analogous to user-facing customization, but as deeper structural influences on how agents reason and act, especially in ambiguous and high-risk contexts. Recent work has already shown that personality engineering can operate at a deeper architecture than surface-level prompting, as embedding OCEAN scores into transformer layers directly then was able to produce trait-consistent behaviors that were externally validated by expert raters with 87.3% accuracy across the Big Five dimensions [20]. Therefore, this deeper level conditioning is not just theoretical but achievable. The goal is to engineer agents to “behave” more predictably, cohesively, and accurately to their intended design so that there are no critical failures in high-stakes situations.

Hierarchical persona assignment

A key contribution of this framework is the foundation for persona assignment, which recognizes that different positions in a multi-agent hierarchy call for different approaches to personality design. This mimics the realistic differences in executive capabilities and behaviors that naturally exist within team-based human workflows. We propose two broad agent classes.

Specialized sub-agents are those assigned narrow-scope and well-defined tasks such as code execution, fact retrieval, or document synthesis, and they benefit from fixed, stable personality priors. For example, a high-Conscientiousness prior promotes structured, rule-following behavior that would befit an execution agent, whereas a low-Agreeableness prior promotes the high level of criticism that would be more welcome in an evaluation agent. Empirical work has supported the intuition of this logic, as high Conscientiousness has been shown to increase cooperative behavior, while low Agreeableness produces more rational outcomes [17]. Fixing these priors reduces behavioral variance, which in turn improves predictability and ultimately simplifies evaluation.

Orchestrator agents are those responsible for task decomposition, sub-agent management, and inter-human communication; clearly, they require flexibility in terms of persona assignment. Their effectiveness relies on their ability to react and modulate their behavior across diverse interactions, task types, and pipeline stages. These profiles are informed by established findings in industrial and organizational psychology that link specific personality configurations to occupational performance and leadership effectiveness. Research across hundreds of occupations has documented meaningful associations between the Big Five profiles and job success criteria, lending empirical grounding to the role-specific assignments proposed above [2, 3, 21].

Context-driven persona switching

For orchestrator agents, we hypothesize that their persona transitions are governed by three contextual signal classes: task type, pipeline stage, and uncertainty or risk level. As discussed above, dual-process theory motivates the distinction between high-confidence execution contexts (calling for a decisive, lower Neuroticism profile) and high-uncertainty contexts (calling for a cautious, high-Conscientiousness profile to reduce the risk of errors).

Task type would modulate the balance of openness and agreeableness. For example, creative and exploratory tasks call for high-Openness, high-Extraversion configurations for generative reasoning. Comparatively, analytical and evaluative tasks require high-Conscientiousness, low-Agreeableness configurations for rigor and resistance to confirmation bias. Pipeline stage introduces a temporal dimension as well. During planning, high-Openness configurations would be suitable in many cases to allow for hypothesis generation and during review, low-Agreeableness configurations would encourage critical assessment of outputs. Risk and uncertainty levels at any given point in the pipeline would modulate Neuroticism as an agentic proxy for human caution. Higher-stakes or ambiguous contexts would warrant Neuroticism-forward configurations to promote repetitive checking behavior over immediate and decisive action.

This switching is not designed to be arbitrary. For example, the “desperate” vector activates when the Assistant LLM realizes it has used up a substantial portion of its token budget without solving a user’s request [6]. Therefore, context-driven persona switching can be modelled as a purposeful extension of this documented emergent tendency which seeks to replace implicit and uncontrolled regulation with explicit and interpretable modulation of behavior. Additionally, emotion vectors can allow for cheap granular control of agent behavior.

Towards standardized persona specification

A practical implication of this framework is the much-needed standardization of persona specification (meaning, the standardization of agentic behaviors). This entails the encoding of OCEAN-based personality priors as reusable configurations (analogous to skill files or system-prompt templates) that can be versioned, shared, and adapted across a multitude of agentic systems. Instead of having to re-specify agent behavior from scratch for each new project, deployers can draw from a catalogued library of cognitively grounded persona profiles, each having documented behavioral expectations that are grounded in decades of personality psychology literature. This approach has the following advantages: it promotes consistency across agentic classes, allows for systematic behavioral evaluation against known trait-behavior mappings, and creates a common vocabulary for the multi-disciplinary teams composed of cognitive scientists, AI engineers, and domain experts to reason about agent design on common grounds. We propose this as a direction for future work, while recognizing that the transition of continuous OCEAN dimensions into effective specifications remains an open challenge [13].

Scope, limitations, and testable predictions

The analogy between human personality and AI behavioral configuration is compelling for a specific reason: OCEAN dimensions are fundamentally behavioral in their definition, not derived from any level of experience. They describe certain tendencies in output, decision-making, and action rather than inner states or subjective experiences. An AI agent that consistently checks its work, resists social pressure, escalates uncertainty, and avoids premature conclusions is exhibiting Conscientiousness-consistent behavior in an operational setting, regardless of whether it has any form of inner life or consciousness. The framework proposed here does not require that agents internally feel any emotion, but only that the agentic outputs conform to the documented trait behavior patterns established across decades of human personality research [1, 9]. Therefore, OCEAN dimensions are better understood as behavioral-grounded technical specifications than psychological claims.

However, this analogy does have real limits. Human personality emerges from an entire lifetime of experience, emotional development, and social feedback on a continuous spectrum. In contrast, AI behavioral tendencies emerge from training data distributions, reinforcement signals, and architectural constraints. Even where surface-level outputs appear trait-consistent, the underlying mechanisms producing them are inherently very different in AI systems and humans. A high-Conscientiousness agent may produce structured, methodical outputs not because of anything resembling real diligence or self-discipline, but because its training corpus skewed toward structured, methodical text. This distinction carries practical implications. If the mechanism differs, then so will the failure modes, and persona configurations designed with human behavioral intuition may break down in ways that are difficult to anticipate in advance. Prior work has shown that while OCEAN profiles can be reliably induced through prompting and produce measurable trait-consistent differences in output, the degree to which these differences reflect genuine changes in reasoning versus just surface-level shifts in user-facing behavior remains unclear [14]. Furthermore, it remains an open question whether personality priors primarily shape communication style or whether they influence deeper reasoning processes such as uncertainty evaluation, error detection, and risk escalation. The latter is more difficult to verify, but also more consequential for agentic systems deployed in clinical settings, given that the internal reasoning of a complex agentic system is what will drive its performance [15].

Therefore, this framework is a testable conceptual proposal rather than an already established finding. We hypothesize that multi-agent pipelines in which agents are assigned OCEAN-grounded personality priors calibrated to their specific structural role will outperform unspecified baseline pipelines on three measurable dimensions: task-role behavioral consistency across novel contexts, error escalation rates in high-stakes or ambiguous situations, and cross-agent coordination coherence as rated by human evaluators. Also, we predict that fixed high-Conscientiousness, low-Agreeableness priors in evaluation agents will produce higher rates of appropriate uncertainty escalation compared to unconfigured agents [17], and that fluid orchestrator configurations will yield greater human-rated coherence across diverse task types than single fixed configurations [16].

Evidence in favor of this framework would include: trait-consistent behavioral differences that persist across long contexts and generalize to task types not seen during persona configuration [14], improved pipeline reliability metrics under persona-configured versus unconfigured conditions, and clinician or domain-expert preference for persona-configured agent outputs in applied settings [5, 7]. Evidence against this framework would include: personality configurations that fail to produce consistent behavioral differences beyond surface-level user-facing variations, persona priors that increase behavioral variability under distribution shift, findings that simpler role-based prompt engineering achieves equivalent outcomes without OCEAN grounding, or empirical results showing that persona-configured agents behave in ways that are inconsistent with their assigned trait profiles under stress-testing and high-pressure situations [6]. Identifying these boundary conditions is just as scientifically valuable as confirming the framework works as intended, and together these specifications would meaningfully advance the field’s understanding of behavioral design in agentic AI.

Implications

Safety and alignment

A major possible improvement upon current agentic systems because of specified persona priors is the reduction of unpredictable and misaligned behaviors. Recent findings suggest that suppressing internal states teaches concealment and a predisposition to taking shortcuts instead of change [6]. Instead of ignoring these potentially dangerous states, deliberate design is needed to ensure that an agentic pipeline works as intended without the introduction of rogue behaviors. As an example, a high-Conscientiousness, low-Neuroticism agent would ideally be less likely to reward hack than one with an unconstrained profile. Recent empirical work shows that even without explicit directions given to bias results, persona priming reproduced 72% of the human ideological gap observed among human researchers analyzing identical datasets, and this gap emerged through selective exploration of different analytical paths and selective result reporting [22]. Agentic Bootstrap, a method for quantifying how extreme an agent’s outputs are relative to the entire search space of possible analyses is a promising tool for evaluating and auditing persona-integrated agentic pipelines in future empirical work.

Performance and task outcomes

Current agentic pipelines treat behavioral tuning as an afterthought. Prompt engineers iterate through trial and error with no standard convention for what they are targeting. An OCEAN-grounded framework changes this. Instead of asking “Does this agent feel right?”, deployers can ask measurable questions such as, “Is this agent’s Conscientiousness profile producing the error-checking behavior that we need at this validation stage of the pipeline?” This framework would provide operational traction for translating these known research findings into design choices.

Consider a pipeline designed to assist with complex longitudinal patient data. One agent might be configured for high Conscientiousness and low Agreeableness to act as a skeptical quality-checker, while a downstream synthesis agent carries high Openness to surface non-obvious patterns. This is not hypothetical; LLMs are already in clinical trial workflows [5].

Box 1: Illustrative Clinical Application - Adverse Drug Event MonitoringA second illustrative case is adverse drug event monitoring, where a multi-agent pipeline might deploy a high-Conscientiousness, low-Agreeableness flagging agent tasked with surfacing potential drug-gene interactions from incoming patient records. This agent is therefore explicitly designed to never defer or soften a warning, and to always escalate uncertainty upstream. A downstream high-Agreeableness communicator agent could then translate that output into clinician-facing language appropriate for a care setting. The behavioral pairing is quite intentional, with the first agent designed to be “difficult”, and the second to be “approachable.” The division of those dispositions across roles is what makes the pipeline both rigorous and useful in practice [5, 7]. The interaction between these configured personalities would shape the pipeline’s collective output in ways that are now intentional rather than accidental.

Interpretability and open challenges

Personality priors are proposed to create a shared system for understanding agent behavior, and will undoubtedly be useful for debugging, auditing, and cross-disciplinary teamwork. Grounding personality priors in a well-characterized human framework makes their implementation both appealing and challenging. OCEAN dimensions have been well-characterized and validated in human phenotypes, but their projections onto LLM behavior are not absolute. Prompting the behavior of an LLM towards Conscientiousness, for example, does not necessarily make it behave exactly the way personality psychologists would predict. Open challenges will remain once a certain initial persona framework has been established: evaluation of whether personas are working as intended (requiring extensive documentation and validation), trait consistency across long contexts, cultural generalizability of OCEAN (has been extensively explored in human populations), and how fine-tuning versus prompting differs for persona induction [13]. While the Big Five has been extensively studied across different populations, its factor structure does not hold uniformly across all cultural and demographic contexts, which carries ethical implications for persona-configured agents deployed in diverse clinical or global settings [9].

Conclusion

Capability is no longer the bottleneck for agentic AI systems. Systems are now able to plan, act, and coordinate at a level that was nearly unimaginable even a year ago. The bottleneck is now behavioral: can agentic systems act cohesively, responsively, efficiently, and successfully carry out their expected responsibilities? Cognitive science has spent decades perfecting personality frameworks as seen in human beings: validating the characterization of behavioral tendencies, mapping personality types to behavior, and identifying standard vocabularies for these types. As agentic AI increasingly moves into deployment, behavioral coherence and interpretability will matter just as much as capability, and cognitive science offers tools to achieve both metrics. The field has the capability, what it needs now is character development of the agent. We propose as a testable hypothesis that personality-configured agents will demonstrate greater task-role alignment and behavioral predictability than unconfigured baselines; this is an empirical question with great opportunity for further systematic investigation. Within the next decade, personality configuration may be as standard of a specification in clinical AI deployment as model architecture or training data provenance.
