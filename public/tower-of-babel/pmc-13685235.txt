DNA methylation defines lineage identity yet converges among functionally related cells and is cell‑specifically remodeled during in vitro passaging
Authors: Lanlan Sun, Dongmei Chen, Xin Zhang, Hailang Fan, Yaqian Zhao, Yingdi Huang, Yang Wu, Yanni Lou, Dake Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDNA methylation (DNAme) is used to define cellular identity, but its ability to distinguish closely related subpopulations and its stability during in vitro culture remain to be systematically evaluated.ResultsLineage-scale whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) analysis of 40 cell types across 13 organs showed that DNAme separates major lineages but converges among functionally related populations. Endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and smooth muscle cells from different organs clustered primarily by lineage rather than tissue of origin, whereas epithelial cells retained stronger organ-associated methylation signatures. Lineage-restricted analysis of endothelial cells identified 700 tissue-specific differentially methylated regions (DMRs) across seven tissues that were masked in global comparisons, indicating residual tissue-of-origin information persists within highly similar lineages.In kidney cells, in vitro passaging induced systematic methylation remodeling without erasing lineage identity. Primary and subcultured glomerular endothelial and tubular epithelial cells formed separate subclusters within their lineage groups. Genome-wide analysis identified extensive differential methylation (~ 1.88 M differentially methylated CpG sites [DMCs] in glomerular endothelial; ~2.64 M in tubular epithelial), with promoters overrepresented among hypermethylated features. Shared hypermethylated promoters were enriched for core homeostatic functions; hypomethylated promoters were enriched for differentiation and tissue-specific pathways. Motif analysis showed enrichment of bZIP and GATA factor motifs in endothelial hypomethylated regions and ETS-family motifs in epithelial cells.Cross-cell-type array analysis confirmed reproducible methylation drift across fetal skin fibroblasts, adult vascular endothelial cells, and neonatal foreskin fibroblasts, with 7,434 shared hypomethylated DMCs and 1,347 shared hypermethylated DMCs. Drift onset was cell type-dependent: adult skin fibroblasts and vascular smooth muscle cells diverged by passage 3, whereas neonatal and fetal cells remained stable through passage 10. Shared hypomethylated DMCs were enriched in partially methylated domains (PMDs) and late-replicating domains (LRDs). Cross-platform comparison detected only 44 common hypomethylated DMCs and 24 common hypermethylated DMCs between array and WGBS data, with negligible regional overlap.ConclusionsDNAme robustly distinguishes major lineages but converges among closely related cell types, limiting fine subpopulation resolution. Lineage-restricted analysis recovers tissue-of-origin signals otherwise obscured, whereas in vitro passaging induces cell type-dependent remodeling and reproducible methylation drift that preserves lineage identity while attenuating tissue-specific programs. No universal passage threshold ensures phenotypic stability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13224-6.

Introduction

Epigenetic modifications can be mitotically heritable, enabling their transmission through cell division and thereby exerting durable effects on gene regulation and chromatin organization [1, 2]. DNA methylation (DNAme)—the covalent addition of a methyl group predominantly to cytosine residues in DNA—has attracted sustained interest because of its relative biochemical stability and its pervasive role in gene regulation [3]. As a key component of the epigenetic regulatory network, DNAme, together with chromatin accessibility and histone modifications, constitutes a multilayered system governing gene expression. DNAme provides a relatively stable molecular memory that contributes to the maintenance of core cellular identity and developmental history [4, 5], whereas chromatin accessibility and histone modifications confer high dynamicity and environmental responsiveness in gene activity regulation [6].

During embryogenesis, totipotent cells progressively give rise to pluripotent and subsequently lineage-restricted cell states, a process accompanied by extensive remodeling of DNAme landscapes [7]. Notably, different cellular lineages undergo organ-specific specialization to the diverse extent [8]. Each cell type carries a characteristic methylation “fingerprint”, and profiling such patterns can help infer the cellular sources of DNA molecules in a mixture [9].Therefore, the combination of stability and cell-type specificity makes DNAme an attractive biomarker for cell-of-origin inference in complex tissues and liquid biopsies [8, 10–13]. Since whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) is often regarded as the “gold standard” for DNAme measurement owing to its genome-wide coverage and single-base resolution [14], in 2023, Loyfer et al. [8] generated a single-base-resolution human methylation atlas by WGBS across diverse purified cell types from multiple healthy tissues, providing an important reference for cell type deconvolution in methylation profiles of bulk tissues.

However, it remains unclear to what extent DNAme retains sufficient power to distinguish cell subpopulations that share developmental origins, closely related functions, or a common tissue microenvironment. Emerging evidence indicates that some closely related cell types can exhibit highly similar methylation patterns. For example, the atlas by Loyfer et al. reported substantial similarity among several epithelial and podocyte subpopulations in the kidney [8], consistent with observations in the study by R. You et al. [15]. In the nervous system, single-cell methylation maps delineate many cell clusters yet also reveal continuous spatial gradients of DNAme among excitatory neurons in specific mouse brain regions (for example, cortex and hippocampus), rather than strictly discrete boundaries [16]. Together, these findings suggest that methylation-based resolution for fine-grained subpopulation distinction may be limited in certain contexts and warrants systematic evaluation.

Beyond cell type, DNAme can also be shaped by environmental exposures [17]. Cell lines and primary cells expanded in vitro are indispensable for basic and translational research yet the epigenetic alterations that accumulate during passaging are often overlooked. These changes arise from non-physiological culture conditions, including loss of native three-dimensional architecture, repeated enzymatic dissociation, and elevated oxygen tension. For example, prolonged culture of human bone marrow- and adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) leads to reproducible methylation changes at specific CpG sites, likely reflecting epigenetic drift and/or culture-driven selection [18]. Recent work further indicates that passage-associated DNAme changes are not entirely stochastic but may preferentially affect certain genomic regions [19, 20]. Determining the generalizability of such drift across cell types and experimental platforms remains an active area of investigation. Accounting for these changes supports experimental reproducibility and translational relevance. Therefore, documenting passage number and quantitatively assessing methylation identity shifts provide a robust basis for cross-laboratory and cross-platform comparisons.

In this study, we first systematically evaluated the ability of DNAme to distinguish closely related cellular subpopulations and assessed methylation similarity among cells of the same lineage across different organs. We then compared methylation alterations between primary and subcultured cells and their generalizability across diverse cell types. By jointly examining in vivo lineage-associated epigenetic organization and in vitro culture-induced epigenetic plasticity, this study aims to define the subpopulation-level resolution achievable by DNAme and to characterize the extent and consistency of DNAme feature loss during cell culture across different cell types.

Result

DNAme signatures define major cell types and reveal organ-specific programs through lineage-restricted analysis

To evaluate the resolution of DNAme in distinguishing cell subtypes, we integrated our data with a public dataset (GSE186458) comprising 77 primary cell types [8]. After applying selection criteria based on systemic classification, organ origin, and morphofunctional features, we retained 40 representative cell types spanning 13 organs (Table S1). This panel enabled cross-system analyses (e.g., digestive and immune-lymphoid systems) and allowed comparison of morphologically similar cells from different organs, such as smooth muscle cells.

We selected the top 100 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) by absolute mean methylation difference (|Δβ|≥0.48, FDR ≤ 0.05), each containing ≥ 10 CpG sites and displaying a binary, cell-type-restricted pattern: hypermethylated (or hypomethylated) in one focal cell type and the reciprocal state in all others. Every selected region showed an absolute methylation difference > 50% compared with all other cell types (Fig. 1b).

ChromHMM 15-state enrichment analysis using corresponding Roadmap Epigenomics reference models showed that DMRs from B cells and NK cells were significantly enriched in all three enhancer-associated chromatin states (EnhG, Enh, and EnhBiv), while CD8⁺ T cells DMRs were enriched in EnhG and Enh (adjusted P < 0.05, Figure S1c). When enhancer-associated states were combined, DMRs from all three cell types remained significantly enriched in enhancers relative to non-enhancer states (adjusted P < 0.05, Figure S1c), with greater fold-enrichment for enhancer-associated states.

Given the strong enhancer enrichment of cell type-specific DMRs, we examined the methylation patterns of all analyzed cell types. Most cell-type-restricted DMRs were hypomethylated in their corresponding target cell types. Glomerular epithelial cells, tubular epithelial cells, and podocytes displayed highly concordant DNAme profiles (Fig. 1c). This concordance extended to endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and smooth muscle cells sampled across organs, each showing strong lineage-consistent similarity. Macrophages, monocytes, granulocytes, and T cell subsets (CD3⁺, CD4⁺, CD8⁺) also exhibited high intra-lineage concordance. Consistent with shared intestinal origin, colon and small intestine epithelial cells showed strongly aligned methylation profiles (Fig. 1c).

We quantified cross-organ methylation convergence within four major lineages (epithelial, endothelial, fibroblast, smooth muscle) using pairwise methylation distances (Euclidean and correlation metrics). Epithelial cells showed consistently higher inter-organ distances compared with the other three lineages (Figure S1a). These differences were significant for epithelial versus endothelial and epithelial versus smooth muscle (P < 0.001), while epithelial-fibroblast comparisons did not reach significance (P > 0.05). Heatmaps of mean inter-organ distances confirmed this pattern, with epithelial pairs showing higher values while endothelial, fibroblast, and smooth muscle pairs displayed lower divergence (Figure S1b–c). Thus, methylation convergence across organs varies by lineage, with epithelial cells retaining more pronounced organ-associated signatures.

Together, these results indicate that DNAme provides stable, cell type–associated epigenetic signatures that separate major cellular identities. At finer resolution, however, functionally similar populations across organs, immune subtypes, and microenvironment-defined cellular subgroups exhibit highly convergent DNAme profiles, limiting the ability to distinguish closely related populations. Nevertheless, other epigenetic layers may capture such tissue-specific regulatory variation. To assess tissue-resolving chromatin accessibility within conserved endothelial methylation programs, we integrated lineage-specific DMRs with sci-ATAC-seq data from the Descartes Human Developmental Cell Atlas [22]. Of 700 DMRs across seven endothelial subtypes (Fig. 1c), matched scATAC-seq data were available for five subtypes from four organs; thus, 500 DMRs and 15,034 endothelial cells from lung (n = 4,352), liver (n = 7,720), kidney (n = 2,802), and pancreas (n = 160) were retained. Of these DMRs, 377 overlapped accessible chromatin peaks. Although DMR-associated accessibility profiles showed limited tissue separation (Figure S8a), chromatin accessibility exhibited significantly greater cross-organ variability than DNAme at matched regulatory region (Figure S8b). Subtype-level analysis showed preferential accessibility of subtype-associated DMRs in their cognate organs, with the strongest enrichment for liver sinusoidal endothelial cells(Liv_SEC), lung endothelial cells (Lun_ENC), and renal tubular endothelial cells(Kid_TENC) DMRs and moderate enrichment for glomerular endothelial cells(Kid_GENC) and pancreatic endothelial cells(Pan_ENC) DMRs (Figure S8c-d). Thus, these findings demonstrate that, beyond DNAme, chromatin accessibility harbors organ-associated regulatory variation within these conserved endothelial DMRs. As these DMRs were defined by DNAme, we next tested whether lineage-restricted analysis could similarly uncover tissue-specific methylation patterns masked by dominant lineage effects.

Lineage-restricted analysis reveals organ-specific methylation programs in endothelial cells

To test this, we performed a lineage-restricted analysis on endothelial cells, the most extensively represented lineage, and identified tissue-specific DMRs across seven tissues.

For each endothelial subpopulation, we selected the top 100 tissue-specific DMRs, which were consistently hypomethylated in their corresponding populations (Figure S1e). Unlike the convergence seen in the previous 40-cell-type analysis, lineage-restricted analysis separated endothelial subpopulations by organ of origin, with residual similarity mainly between anatomically and functionally related endothelial types, such as glomerular and tubular endothelial cells.

Projection of these 700 DMRs onto the global methylation landscape confirmed that they distinguished endothelial cells from all other lineages and retained organ-level structure within the endothelial lineage. These findings suggest that top DMRs from global analyses capture predominantly lineage-defining features, whereas lineage-restricted analysis reveals tissue-specific variation otherwise masked by stronger inter-lineage signals.

Genomic annotation of endothelial lineage DMRs showed enrichment in intronic (48%) and intergenic (31%) regions, with smaller proportions in promoters (14%) and exons (7%) (Figure S1f). As with global DMRs, these regions were strongly associated with enhancers: 64.9% overlapped dELS and 15.0% pELS, totaling 79.9% enhancer-associated, while 20.1% lacked enhancer annotation (Fig. 2e and Figure S1g).

Functional enrichment analysis further revealed that genes linked to these DMRs were enriched in actin filament-based movement and cytoskeletal organization (Fig. 2d). These results indicate that organ-specific endothelial specialization may be mediated in part through DNAme-dependent regulation of cytoskeletal and contractile functions, supporting distinct physiological roles across tissue microenvironments.

Passaging-associated DNAme drift preserves lineage identity and reveals culture-induced plasticity across cell types

In the global analysis, tubular epithelial cells showed modest separation from other kidney epithelial populations. This separation coincided with the inclusion of subcultured cells in the tubular epithelial group, raising the question of whether in vitro expansion contributes to systematic epigenetic remodeling. To test this, we stratified kidney-associated subpopulations by culture status and analyzed their methylation profiles within and across lineages.

Using the top 100 DMRs (|Δβ| ≥ 0.5, FDR ≤ 0.05) across glomerular endothelial cells (primary and subcultured), human renal mesangial cells, tubular endothelial cells, glomerular epithelial cells, tubular epithelial cells (primary and subcultured), and podocytes, hierarchical clustering resolved three groups corresponding to endothelial, epithelial, and mesangial identities (Figure S2a-b). Within the endothelial and epithelial clusters, primary and subcultured cells were distinguishable: primary and subcultured glomerular endothelial cells formed clearly separated subclusters (Figure S2b: Pri_GENC vs. Sub_GENC), while primary and subcultured tubular epithelial cells showed a similar but less pronounced separation (Figure S2b: Pri_KTEPC vs. Sub_KTEPC). These results indicate the in vitro passaging induces systematic shifts in DNAme, yet the cells remain clustered by lineage, indicating that lineage-level identity is preserved.

To quantify these passaging-associated changes, we conducted genome-wide differential methylation analysis (Figure S6). In glomerular endothelial cells, primary versus subcultured comparison identified 961,670 hypermethylated differentially methylated CpG sites (hyperDMCs) and 920,977 hypomethylated differentially methylated CpG sites (hypoDMCs), corresponding to 115,938 hypermethylated differentially methylated regions (hyperDMRs) and 130,075 hypomethylated differentially methylated regions (hypoDMRs). Promoters were more frequent among hypermethylated features (hyperDMCs 11% vs. hypoDMCs 7%; hyperDMRs 9% vs. hypoDMRs 7%) (Figure S2c-d). Tubular epithelial cells exhibited a comparable magnitude: 1,253,595 hyperDMCs, 1,385,692 hypoDMCs, 217,054 hyperDMRs, and 300,029 hypoDMRs (Figure S2e-f), with promoter fractions of 10%, 6%, 12%, and 6%, respectively (Figure S2e-f). Thus, both cell types undergo extensive methylation remodeling during culture, with a consistent bias toward promoter hypermethylation.

Functional annotation of promoter-level differentially methylated regions further linked these changes to distinct biological processes. In endothelial cells, the small GTPase signaling and cytoskeletal organization in both directions, with hypermethylation biased toward cell adhesion/structural maintenance and hypomethylation toward activation-related programs (cell differentiation, neurodevelopment, synaptic activity, and muscle-specific functions) (Fig. 3a-b). In tubular epithelial cells, hypermethylation was associated with metabolic and transmembrane transport functions, and hypomethylation with differentiation and signaling (Fig. 4a-b). These data indicate that passaging perturbs different functional modules in endothelial versus epithelial cells, although differentiation-related pathways are hypomethylated in both.

A direct comparison of the two cell types identified a subset of shared methylation changes: 395,741 common hypoDMCs and 248,695 common hyperDMCs (Fig. 5a), plus 72,450 common hypoDMRs and 29,465 common hyperDMRs (Fig. 5b). Cross-cell-type and cross-platform assessment of culture-associated methylation drift

To evaluate whether these findings generalize beyond kidney cells, we analyzed public DNAme array datasets from multiple primary human cell types.

We first performed a pilot analysis to define primary and subcultured sampling. Passages 1–3 were initially treated as “primary” under the working assumption that early passages minimally perturb cell identity, and subcultured samples were collected every tenth passage starting at passage 10. For cell lines with limited passage capacity, passage 10 and the final passage were used. Because the passage-10 neonatal foreskin fibroblasts 2 (NFF2) sample clustered with passages 1–3 (Figure S3a-b), subcultured sampling for NFF2 was shifted to begin at passage 20 (Table S2).

The pilot analysis revealed that the onset and rate of passaging-associated DNAme drift vary by cell type: adult skin fibroblasts(ASF) and adult vascular smooth muscle cells (AVSMC) showed identity shifts beginning at passage 3, whereas neonatal foreskin fibroblasts (NFF), fetal skin fibroblasts (FSF), adult vascular endothelial cells (AVEC), and neonatal foreskin keratinocytes (NFK) remained consistent until approximately passage 10 (Figure S3c; Figure S4a-b); Based on these patterns and sample-size requirements for differential methylation testing, we selected FSF, AVEC, and NFF for downstream analysis, defining passages 1–9 as primary and the last nine passages as subcultured (Table S2).

In all three cell types, PCA and hierarchical clustering separated primary from subcultured samples (Figure S5; Fig. 6), confirming that passaging-associated methylation drift is reproducible and not restricted to renal cells. Using |logFC| ≥ 0.1, we identified 7,434 shared hypoDMCs and 1,347 shared hyperDMCs across the three cell types (Fig. 7a–b). The existence of shared sites indicates that a component of the drift is generalizable across distinct cell types.

We next examined whether these shared hypoDMCs reflect the progressive demethylation of partially methylated domains (PMDs) and late-replicating domains (LRDs) reported in previous serial passaging studies [23]. Fisher’s exact test revealed significant enrichment of cross‑tissue hypoDMCs within common PMDs (OR = 3.29, FDR < 0.05). Strong enrichments were also observed for LRDs from HUVEC (OR = 4.43, FDR < 0.05) and BJ (OR = 4.59, FDR < 0.05) (Figure S7a). Permutation analysis confirmed these patterns, with fold enrichments of 3.08 (PMDs), 3.95 (HUVEC LRDs), and 4.18 (BJ LRDs), all with adjusted P < 0.05 (Figure S7a–b). These data indicate that cross‑tissue hypoDMCs preferentially accumulate within both PMDs and LRDs compartments.

Temporal analysis showed that the number of DMCs and the magnitude of methylation change both increased with passage number in FSF, AVEC, and NFF cells (Figure S7c–d), demonstrating a progressive accumulation of epigenetic drift during prolonged culture.

Finally, we assessed the cross-platform portability of these signatures. Comparing array-based DMCs with WGBS data from kidney-related cells recovered 44 common hypoDMCs and 24 common hyperDMCs (Fig. 7c–d; Table S5). At the regional level, DMR analysis across the three array datasets yielded 273 shared hyperDMRs and 42 shared hypoDMRs, but array–WGBS comparison recovered almost no shared DMRs (Fig. 7e). These results show that while common passaging-associated DMCs can be identified across cell types using array data, the locus-level overlap between array and WGBS platforms is limited and depends on assay coverage and analytical definitions.

Discussion

DNAme contributes to stable, cell-type-associated transcriptional states and identity maintenance [24–27]. Our lineage-scale analysis confirms that bulk WGBS captures broad lineages but provides limited resolution for subpopulations with shared origins, functions, or microenvironments [8]. Quantitative assessment of cross-organ methylation convergence revealed that epithelial cells retain stronger organ-specific signatures, while stromal lineages—endothelial cells, fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells—are more conserved. This divergence aligns with the early, multi-germ-layer specification and deep epigenetic imprinting of epithelia, in contrast to the later, less extensive organ-specific differentiation of mesenchyme-derived stroma [28].

This resolution gap reflects the division of labor within the epigenetic regulatory network: DNAme encodes stable lineage identity, whereas tissue-specific functional specialization is governed by dynamic layers, including chromatin accessibility and histone modifications [6, 29]. Enhancer marks H3K27ac/H3K4me1 respond rapidly to environmental cues, and chromatin accessibility reflects transcription factor binding [30–32]. These modifications engage in bidirectional crosstalk with DNAme—through MBD-mediated compaction [33], H3K36me2/3-guided de novo methylation [34], and H3K4me3-mediated CpG island protection [35]. From a technical perspective. In addition, bulk WGBS/array profiles reflect population averages, which can mask subtle substructure and reduce sensitivity to small subpopulation-specific shifts [36]. Thus, the dominant lineage signals in global DNAme analyses reflect stable identity encoding, while fine-scale, tissue-specific variation remains largely embedded in dynamic regulatory layers.

Independent single-cell ATAC-seq studies have identified endothelial-specific chromatin accessibility signatures absent from other cell types and have shown that the tissue microenvironment imprints unique accessibility landscapes across organs [22, 37, 38]. Integration of WGBS-derived endothelial DMRs with the Descartes developmental sci-ATAC-seq atlas revealed greater cross-organ heterogeneity in chromatin accessibility than in DNAme at the same regulatory regions. However, averaged DMR-associated accessibility profiles showed limited tissue separation, likely because population-level averaging masks cellular heterogeneity and these regions were defined by DNAme rather than accessibility differences. Accessibility enrichment was weaker for Pan_ENC and Kid_GENC DMRs, potentially owing to limited pancreatic cell numbers and dilution of the specialized glomerular endothelial signature within the broader renal endothelial population, respectively. Collectively, these findings indicate that DNAme alone is insufficient to distinguish closely related subpopulations across distinct tissues, and chromatin accessibility serves as a complementary epigenetic layer with finer tissue-resolving power.

Despite these inherent limitations in subpopulation resolution, DNAme retains unique advantages for liquid biopsy: its biochemical stability, tissue specificity, and intra-individual conservation make it the gold standard for tissue-of-origin deconvolution [39]. Our findings support a hierarchical DNAme architecture in which dominant lineage signatures drive global clustering, whereas tissue-specific signals emerge after lineage-restricted analysis. This framework supports stratified cfDNA deconvolution that first assigns lineage and then resolves tissue of origin, improving precision without additional data. Integrating DNAme with chromatin accessibility and histone modifications may further improve resolution of developmentally related cell populations.

Beyond in vivo lineage stability, we show that in vitro culture induces pronounced, yet incomplete, DNAme remodeling that preserves lineage identity while attenuating tissue-specific programs. Consistent with prior work [19, 20], primary kidney epithelial and endothelial cells exhibited extensive differential methylation upon passaging, with preferential promoter hypermethylation, consistent with transcriptional repression [40]. Shared hypermethylated promoters were enriched for core homeostasis, and hypomethylated promoters for differentiation and tissue-regulatory functions, suggesting a transition toward a culture-adapted state.

Motif analysis identified bZIP/GATA motifs in endothelial cells and ETS-family motifs in epithelial cells, given their established roles in proliferation, stress responses, differentiation, and morphogenesis [41–44], nominating these TF families as candidate regulators of culture-associated remodeling. Given the known sensitivity of the epigenome to environmental perturbations, a parsimonious interpretation is that in vitro stressors (e.g., altered matrix cues, serum exposure, repeated dissociation) engage cell type–specific TF circuits that coincide with promoter-centric methylation remodeling.

Cross-cell-type analysis of public array datasets confirmed that primary and subcultured states are separable across multiple cell types, with cell-type-dependent kinetics. Shared DMC/DMR signatures support a partial common passaging module, yet locus-level overlap between array and WGBS data remains limited [15]. This limited transferability likely stems primarily from differences in biological context, particularly variations in cell Subculturing history. In this study, EPIC array profiling was conducted on cells with strictly documented passage numbers, whereas the WGBS datasets lack precise passage information. This uncertainty introduces biological variability that obscures consistent methylation patterns. Methylation differences induced by serial subculturing and captured by the EPIC platform may appear insufficient or confounded in WGBS samples due to the unclear passage status. Furthermore, platform intrinsic technical factors, including disparate CpG coverage, divergent DMR demarcation criteria, and inherent variations in statistical power between sparse probe arrays and WGBS may compound the observed lack of overlap.

More fundamentally, passaging-associated methylation changes exhibit intrinsic biological selectivity. We observed a progressive accumulation of DMCs with increasing passage number, consistent with the established phenomenon of epigenetic drift during in vitro culture [45]. This process is not stochastic but preferentially targets genomic regions enriched for repressive histone marks (H3K27me3/H3K9me2) and Polycomb-associated domains, which substantially overlap with PMDs [19, 20]. PMDs and LRDs showed partial spatial overlap (Table S6), consistent with prior reports [23]. This overlap may be underestimated because the EPIC array provides sparse and uneven genomic coverage, and methylation and replication-timing datasets differ in resolution. Nevertheless, shared hypoDMCs were significantly enriched in both CommonPMDs and independently defined LRDs, indicating that culture-associated methylation loss preferentially occurs in regions associated with PMD organization and late replication.

Together with lineage-specific methylation changes, these findings support a two-layer model of culture-associated epigenetic remodeling. First, PMD-associated chromatin architecture and late replication create a shared genomic susceptibility to methylation loss across cell types. Second, lineage-specific TF networks may shape the loci and pathways preferentially affected within this susceptible background. Although these associations do not establish causality, this model explains the coexistence of conserved PMD/LRD-associated methylation erosion and lineage-specific regulatory remodeling in cultured cells.

Heterogeneity in proliferative kinetics introduces a temporal mismatch in drift across cell types [28]: identical passage numbers can mask substantial differences in effective replication histories. Our data show that methylation drift can be detectable as early as passage 3 in some adult cell types, while neonatal or fetal cells remain stable past passage 10. Therefore, no universal passage number can guarantee phenotypic stability. Researchers should document population doublings, monitor methylation identity using lineage- or tissue-specific DMR panels, and align stability thresholds with experimental goals—tissue-specific functions may be attenuated earlier, whereas core lineage identity is more robust.

Additionally, the heterogeneity in proliferative kinetics across cell types of distinct tissue origins [28] constitutes another major factor underlying the limited overlap of shared differential loci across tissues and platforms. Cell types differ substantially in cell cycle length, tolerance to replicative stress, and senescence thresholds—parameters that directly determine the actual number of DNA replication events and the magnitude of epigenetic remodeling experienced under nominally equivalent passage numbers. Consequently, even when nominal passage levels are comparable, the effective replication histories of different lineages are not equivalent, thereby undermining the reproducibility of stable methylation markers across tissues. This lineage-specific proliferative disparity introduces a temporal scale mismatch in passaging-associated methylation drift across cell types, ultimately reducing the detection rate of shared signals across data platforms.

Several limitations should be noted. Bulk measurements cannot distinguish uniform reprogramming from clonal expansion [36]; WGBS sample sizes for subcultured cells are limited and motif-based TF predictions remain uncorrelated; methylation changes were not directly linked to gene expression or functional phenotypes.

Conclusion

DNAme robustly distinguishes major cell lineages but shows diminished resolution for closely related subpopulations, as reflected by methylation convergence among functionally similar cells across organs. Organ-specific programs remain detectable through lineage-restricted analysis. In vitro passaging induces reproducible, cell-type-dependent methylation identity drift without erasing lineage identity, arguing against a universal passage threshold for stability. Shared and lineage-specific remodeling programs collectively bias cells toward a convergent culture-adapted epigenetic state.

Method

Data collection

Acquisition of cell subpopulations and DNA extraction

Human renal tubular epithelium, Human renal glomerulus endothelium, and Human renal mesangial cells were commercially obtained from Shanghai Zhaorui Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Primary human hepatic sinusoidal endothelial cells, hepatocytes, and hepatic stellate cells were purchased from China Primary Cell Bank (Liveo Technology Co., Ltd.). DNA was extracted from these cells using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and quality of the extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 8000 spectrophotometer.

Public data

WGBS β-value data for various human primary cell types were obtained from the GSE186458 dataset [8]. The data were converted into BED format files using the wgbstools software [46], and high-confidence CpG methylation sites with a sequencing depth of ≥ 5× were retained. The resulting data were organized by genomic coordinates and exported as standardized bedGraph files. In addition, raw Illumina Infinium MethylationEPIC array data for six primary cell types were retrieved from the GSE197512 dataset [23]. These samples represent serially subcultured cultures of primary cells that had not undergone any experimental treatment.

WGBS data processing

Library construction and sequencing

Genomic DNA (500 ng) from each cell sample was randomly sheared using a Covaris ultrasonicator. Lambda DNA (0.5% of total DNA) was spiked in as an internal control. End repair, A-tailing, and ligation of methylation-specific adapters were performed using the Bio Scientific library preparation kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The adapter-ligated fragments were purified with magnetic beads and subsequently subjected to bisulfite conversion using the EZ DNA Methylation-Gold™ Kit, which converts unmethylated cytosines to uracil while preserving methylated cytosines. The bisulfite-converted DNA was PCR-amplified using the KAPA HiFi HS Uracil+ ReadyMix. The amplified products were purified again with magnetic beads to yield the final WGBS libraries. Qualified libraries were then subjected to paired-end sequencing on an Illumina platform to generate raw WGBS data.

WGBS data preprocessing and determination of site-specific methylation levels

For the our WGBS data, raw sequencing reads were first subjected to comprehensive quality assessment using FastQC (v0.12.1). Adapter sequences and low-quality bases were subsequently trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.39) [47]. The filtered paired-end reads were then aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using Bismark (v0.24.2) [48]. PCR duplicates were removed with the deduplicate_bismark command, and overlapping bases between paired-end reads were clipped using the bamUtil (v1.0.15) [49] clipOverlap function to ensure data accuracy.

DNAme information was extracted using the bismark_methylation_extractor command. For each CpG site, methylation levels were calculated by merging data from both strands according to the formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Methylation\:level=\frac{m}{m+u}$$\end{document}

where m represents the number of reads reporting cytosine (C) at that site, and u represents the number of reads reporting thymine (T).High-confidence CpG methylation sites with sequencing coverage ≥ 5× were retained. The final processed data were sorted by genomic coordinates and exported as standardized bedGraph files.

Identification of differentially methylated CpG sites (DMCs)

Custom scripts were developed to organize the preprocessed methylation data into tab-delimited text files compatible with the methylKit R package (v1.20.0) [50]. Each line corresponded to a single CpG site and included the site ID, chromosome, genomic position, strand orientation, total read coverage, methylated proportion (%), and unmethylated proportion (%).Differential methylation analysis was performed using methylKit as follows:

First, methylation data were imported using the methRead function, with the minimum coverage threshold set to 10. CpG sites with coverage within the 10th–99.9th percentile range were retained using the filterByCoverage function to eliminate low-coverage and excessively covered sites that may introduce bias. Next, median normalization was applied to correct for coverage differences among samples. The data from all samples were then merged, and CpG sites with a methylation proportion standard deviation greater than 2% were retained as variable sites.Finally, differential methylation analysis was conducted using the calculateDiffMeth function. CpG sites with a q-value < 0.01 and an absolute methylation difference ≥ 25% were identified as DMCs, which were further classified as hypermethylated or hypomethylated sites.

Identification of differentially methylated regions (DMRs)

Two complementary strategies were employed to identify DMRs.First, based on all WGBS data, Metilene (v0.2–8) [51] was used to detect cell subpopulation–specific methylated regions. The selection criteria were as follows: each region contained at least 10 CpG sites, exhibited an average methylation difference of ≥ 0.1 (absolute value), and had an adjusted p-value < 0.05 after multiple testing correction. Subsequently, by calculating the methylation differences between each target cell type and all other cell populations, the top 100 regions showing the most significant methylation differences were selected as specific molecular signatures of that cell subpopulation.Second, for kidney tubular epithelial cells and glomerular endothelial cells with both primary and subcultured culture conditions, DMRs were identified using the methylKit R package. The tileMethylCounts function was applied with a sliding window of 500 bp in length and a step size of 500 bp to segment the genome, aggregating CpG methylation counts within each window to the regional level. Differential methylation analysis between primary and subcultured samples was then performed using the calculateDiffMeth function. DMRs were defined as regions with an FDR-adjusted q-value < 0.01 and an absolute methylation difference ≥ 25%.

Functional annotation of differential methylation

DMCs and DMRs was performed using the annotateWithGeneParts function in the methylKit R package, based on ENSEMBL transcript annotations (BED format) retrieved from the UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu/cgi-bin/hgTables) for the hg19 reference genome.

For functional enrichment analysis, annotation files were downloaded from the Ensembl database (GRCh37/hg19, Release 115; https://ftp.ensembl.org/pub/grch37/release-115/gtf/homo_sapiens), and a correspondence table between transcript IDs and gene names was constructed. The getAssociationWithTSS function was used to determine the association of each differential site or region with transcription start sites (TSSs), after which sites/regions were annotated to their corresponding genes using the previously generated mapping table.Based on the resulting gene set, Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.10.1) [52]. Enriched terms or pathways with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered statistically significant, thereby revealing biological processes and signaling pathways associated with differential DNAme.

To further characterize the chromatin state context underlying cell type-specific methylation differences, candidate cis-regulatory elements (cCREs; hg19 assembly) were downloaded from SCREEN [53] (https://screen.wenglab.org/downloads). pELS and dELS were strictly retained to build an enhancer-specific annotation set. Each DMR was overlapped with these annotations to determine whether it fell within an enhancer region and to further classify it as pELS, dELS, or non-enhancer.

Since the cCREs annotations are not specific to the cell types under investigation. To obtain more cell type-resolved chromatin state information, 15-state ChromHMM models from the Roadmap Epigenomics Project [29, 54](https://egg2.wustl.edu/roadmap/data/byFileType/chromhmmSegmentations/ChmmModels/coreMarks/jointModel/final/) were utilized. Because most Roadmap models are derived from tissues rather than isolated primary cells, representative models with the closest biological correspondence were selected for annotation, including E032 (Primary B cells from peripheral blood), E046 (Primary Natural Killer cells from peripheral blood), and E047 (Primary CD8 + naive T cells from peripheral blood). DMRs identified in B cells, NK cells, and CD8 + T cells were respectively annotated using the corresponding ChromHMM model. Overlaps between DMRs and chromatin states were determined using BEDTools(v2.30.0) [55]intersect, and the number of overlapping base pairs was quantified for each state. Enrichment was evaluated using a hypergeometric test by comparing observed overlap with the expected overlap based on genome-wide chromatin-state proportions. P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg(BH) procedure, and enrichment fold changes were calculated as the ratio of observed to expected overlap frequencies. In addition, enhancer-associated ChromHMM states (Enh, EnhG, and EnhBiv) were merged into a unified enhancer category to assess overall enhancer enrichment using the same statistical framework.

In addition, to explore potential transcription factor binding motifs within differentially hypomethylated regions, the top 1,000 most significant hypomethylated regions were analyzed using the HOMER software (v5.1) [56] with the findMotifsGenome.pl command for motif enrichment analysis.

Methylation distance analysis across cell lineages

To quantify inter-tissue methylation divergence, genome-wide tissue-specific DMRs were extracted from the methylation matrix for four major cell lineages, including endothelial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and smooth muscle cells. Samples were automatically stratified into lineage groups and annotated by tissue of origin based on metadata-derived keywords, and non-target lineages (e.g., immune and neural cell types) were excluded.

For each lineage-specific DMR set, methylation distances were computed across all inter-organ sample pairs within each lineage. Two distance metrics were adopted: Euclidean distance, calculated directly from the methylation level vectors to reflect absolute differences, and correlation distance, defined as 1 minus the Pearson correlation coefficient to capture dissimilarity in methylation patterns.

Only pairs involving distinct organ sources were retained, excluding same-organ comparisons. Differences in methylation distances among the four lineages were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test, followed by Dunn‘s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. P-values were adjusted using the BH method to control the false discovery rate. For lineage-specific analyses stratified by organ diversity, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were applied when only two organ groups were present, whereas Kruskal–Wallis tests followed by Dunn’s post hoc comparisons were used when three or more organ groups were included.

Chromatin accessibility analysis of tissue-specific endothelial DMRs

To investigate whether chromatin accessibility provides additional resolution for detecting tissue-specific endothelial functional states beyond global DNA methylation patterns, we integrated DMRs identified from five tissue-derived endothelial subtypes with single-cell chromatin accessibility landscapes, including pancreas endothelial cells, glomerular endothelial cells, renal tubular endothelial cells, lung endothelial cells, and liver sinusoidal endothelial cells.

Publicly available human developmental single-cell combinatorial indexing assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing version 3 (sci-ATAC-seq3) datasets were obtained from the Descartes Human Chromatin Atlas (https://descartes.brotmanbaty.org/) [22]. Vascular endothelial cells from four solid organs (lung, liver, kidney, and pancreas) were retrieved based on curated cell-type annotations. Both high-confidence and low-confidence endothelial subpopulations were included to ensure sufficient cell numbers for robust cross-tissue comparison. Peak-by-cell sparse matrices from each tissue were merged using a consensus set of accessible peaks shared across all four organs. Relative count normalization (scale factor = 10,000) was applied for quantitative accessibility estimation to correct for sequencing depth differences across cells. All preprocessing and downstream analyses of chromatin accessibility data were performed using the Seurat (v5.3.0) and Signac (v1.16.0) R packages.

Genomic overlaps between endothelial DMRs and accessible chromatin peaks were determined using the GenomicRanges (v 1.46.1) package. For DMRs overlapping multiple accessible peaks, the mean normalized accessibility of all overlapping peaks was computed as the regional accessibility value for the corresponding DMR. To enable matched cross-tissue comparison between the two epigenetic layers, methylation levels of glomerular and tubular endothelial cells were averaged to represent the bulk renal endothelial population, consistent with the cell annotation in the Sci-ATAC-seq3 dataset.

To quantify the magnitude of cross-tissue variation for each epigenetic layer, the coefficient of variation (CV) across the four organs was calculated for each DMR at both the DNA methylation and chromatin accessibility levels, defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:CV=\frac{SD\left(x\right)}{Mean\left(x\right)}$$\end{document}

where x represents the methylation level or chromatin accessibility value of a given DMR across the four organs. Differences in CV distributions between the two epigenetic layers were evaluated using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.

For each set of tissue-specific endothelial DMRs, the enrichment ratio of chromatin accessibility in the corresponding organ was calculated to evaluate the concordance of tissue specificity between DNA methylation and chromatin accessibility, defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Enrichment\:ratio=\frac{Mean\:accessibil\mathrm{i}\mathrm{t}{y}_{target\:organ}}{Mean\:accessibilit{y}_{other\:organs}}$$\end{document}

An enrichment ratio greater than 1.2 was considered as evidence of tissue-enriched accessibility.

Methylation EPIC array analysis

The analysis of DNAme EPIC Array data were conducted using the ChAMP R package (v2.28.0) [57, 58]. Raw fluorescence intensity data, stored in IDAT format, were imported and converted into β-value matrices using the champ.load function. The β-values range from 0 to 1, representing the continuum from fully unmethylated to fully methylated states, thereby reflecting the methylation level of each CpG site.During the quality control (QC) phase, the champ.QC function was used to generate multidimensional scaling (MDS) plots, β-value density distributions, and hierarchical clustering dendrograms to comprehensively evaluate sample quality. Samples showing abnormal β-value distributions were excluded to ensure data reliability.Following QC, data normalization was performed using the champ.norm function with the BMIQ algorithm as the default method. The champ.SVD function was employed to identify major sources of variation in the dataset (e.g., batch effects or group differences), and the champ.runCombat function was used to correct potential batch effects, thereby minimizing non-biological variability.Subsequently, differential methylation analyses were carried out using the champ.DMP and champ.DMR functions to identify DMCs and DMRs, respectively. During DMR identification, the maxGap parameter was set to 500 bp.Visualization of results was achieved through ChAMP’s graphical user interface (GUI) functions for intuitive data interpretation and presentation.

Enrichment analysis in partially methylated domains and replication timing domains

To investigate the genomic distribution patterns of broadly shared hypoDMCs across tissues, PMDs annotations were obtained from the publicly available PMD database (https://zwdzwd.github.io/pmd), specifically the list of Infinium EPIC probes located in multi-tissue common PMD regions. In addition, genome-wide replication timing profiles were obtained from ENCODE UW Repli-seq WaveSignal tracks for HUVEC (GSM923452) and BJ fibroblast (GSM923444) cell lines. The WaveSignal track represents a wavelet-smoothed, single-value replication timing metric integrated from six cell cycle-fractionated sequencing signals, where lower values correspond to later replication during S phase. LRDs were defined using a quintile thresholding strategy aligned with prior work linking late replication to DNA hypomethylation [23]. Specifically, genomic intervals with WaveSignal values falling below the 20th percentile (the lowest quintile) of the autosomal genome-wide signal distribution were designated as late-replicating bins. Adjacent bins were merged into continuous domains, and domains shorter than 10 kb were filtered out to remove spurious short fragments. For the BJ cell line, this procedure was performed independently on two biological replicates, and the intersection of their outputs was retained as high‑confidence LRDs. For HUVEC, the single available replicate was processed using the same workflow. The proportion of common PMD probes residing within each LRD set was then determined. Specifically, high‑confidence LRDs were intersected with the PMD probe set, and the number of overlapping probes was counted. Overlap fractions were computed as the ratio of overlapped probes to the total number of autosomal common PMD probes.

Enrichment of hypoDMCs in PMDs and LRDs was evaluated using two complementary statistical approaches. First, a two-sided Fisher’s exact test was performed on 2 × 2 contingency tables, with the full set Infinium EPIC probes serving as the global background, to calculate odds ratios (OR) and raw P-values. Second, to validate the robustness of results and eliminate probe distribution bias, we further conducted 10,000 permutation tests. For each permutation, an equal number of probes to the hypoDMCs set were randomly sampled without replacement from the entire EPIC probe set, and the number of overlaps with the target region was calculated to construct an empirical null distribution. Enrichment fold change was calculated as the ratio between the observed overlap count and the mean overlap count obtained from the random permutations. All P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the BH method, and adjusted FDR < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Dynamic accumulation of DMCs during cell passaging

Two complementary strategies were systematically applied to analyze the dynamic changes of differentially methylated sites with increasing cell passage number (i) Baseline mean difference count analysis: the average methylation level across all samples with passage ≤ 9 was defined as the baseline. For each sample, the absolute difference between its methylation level and the baseline was calculated for every significant DMC, and the number of sites with an absolute difference > 0.1 was recorded as the total DMC count. Hypermethylated DMCs were defined as sites with a difference > 0.1 among hypermethylation trend sites, and hypomethylated DMCs were defined as sites with a difference < -0.1 among hypomethylation trend sites. (ii) Out-of-early-range count analysis: for each DMC, the minimum and maximum methylation values observed in the passage ≤ 9 samples were taken as the “early normal fluctuation range.” For each sample, the number of sites exceeding this range was tallied: hypermethylated DMC count (above the early maximum), hypomethylated DMC count (below the early minimum), and total DMC count (sum of both). Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess the relationship between passage number and each of the three indices, calculated separately for all samples and for the subcultured samples only. Differences between primary and subcultured groups were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Integrated methylation analysis and cross-platform consistency filtering

To identify robust epigenetic signatures, a multi-step intersection strategy was implemented across EPIC array and WGBS datasets. All genomic coordinates were standardized to the human reference genome hg19 (GRCh37), with EPIC probe annotations facilitated by the IlluminaHumanMethylationEPICanno.ilm10b4.hg19 package(v 0.6.0). DMCs were first identified by intersecting site-specific data across all datasets, requiring consistent methylation directionality and strict statistical significance (FDR < 0.05 for EPIC; adjusted q-value < 0.01 for WGBS). Subsequently, DMRs were integrated using the GenomicRanges (v 1.46.1) [59] and IRanges R packages (v 2.28.0). A stringent 50% reciprocal overlap threshold was mandated for both intra-platform and cross-platform DMR comparisons to ensure high genomic conservation. Finally, the confirmed methylation features were annotated using the genomation R packages (v 1.26.0) for downstream functional interpretation.

Statistical analyses

R software (version 4.1.2) was used for all statistical analyses. Statistical significance was defined as a p value < 0.05 (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001).
