Adaptive confidence ensemble reranking for reliable knowledge-intensive question answering
Authors: Tanzila Kehkashan, Maha Abdelhaq, Muhammad Abdullah, Sharifah Sakinah Syed Ahmad, Nor Azman Ismail, Nikola Ivković, Adnan Akunzada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractModern question answering systems rely on advanced neural architectures to bridge the semantic gap between natural language queries and relevant textual evidence. However, single-model approaches often struggle to capture diverse linguistic variations, while traditional ensemble methods suffer from high computational complexity and static model combination strategies. Existing answer reranking systems lack adaptive mechanisms to dynamically adjust model contributions based on question characteristics, limiting their effectiveness across knowledge-intensive tasks. This study proposes an adaptive confidence ensemble reranking framework designed to improve reliability and efficiency in knowledge-intensive question answering. The proposed approach integrates dual-encoder retrieval with a cross-encoder ensemble of BERT, RoBERTa, and DeBERTa models, combined through an instance-aware adaptive confidence weighting mechanism. The framework dynamically adjusts model contributions using cross-entropy-based evaluation to optimize answer ranking performance while maintaining computational feasibility. Experimental results demonstrate significant performance improvements across multiple benchmark datasets, including a 9.3% increase in mean average precision on MS MARCO, a 2.1% gain on WikiQA, and a 1.2% improvement on TREC-QA. These findings highlight the effectiveness of the proposed method in enhancing reliability and accuracy across diverse knowledge-intensive question answering scenarios.Keywords: Answer reranking, Cross-encoder ensemble, Adaptive confidence weighting, Question answering systems, Transformer modelsSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Question answering systems have gone beyond the simple mechanism of searching using a keyword to complex neural networks that are able to comprehend intricate semantic links between queries and textual information. Contemporary QA systems tend to use a pipeline with multi-steps that include passage retrieval phase, answer extraction phase, and more recently, answer reranking phase, which improve on initial predictions to achieve a better system accuracy1. Modern systems exploit transformer based frameworks and dense retrieval schemes to overcome the semantic gap between queries in natural language and appropriate text emergent material2.

reranking of answers is a crucial step of post-processing, which overcomes the drawback of solely single-pass retrieval and extraction systems by employing advanced scoring schemes to the candidate answers3. Recent developments in cross-encoder models have shown substantial enhancement over the traditional bi-encoder models by jointly computing question-answer pair similarities using transformer networks, which can compute similarities in a more refined manner4. Combining dense passage retrieval with large-scale semantic matching of millions of passages has transformed large-scale question answering by applying dense passage retrieval methods to large in-memory libraries of efficient vector similarity search techniques such as FAISS5. Knowledge distillation reduces large ensemble models into efficient single networks while preserving their performance benefits6.

Although significant advancements have been made in answering reranking methods, the current techniques have a number of severe constraints limiting their application use. Conventional learning-to-rank frameworks are based on heavy reliance on handcrafted features and cannot represent complicated semantic connections between queries and responses7. Recent cross-encoder based reranking systems, despite achieving higher accuracy, are limited by quadratic computational complexity, which constrains their scalability in production settings8. Multi-teacher knowledge distillation methods are promising but fail to assign effective weights to the different teacher models, which can result in suboptimal transfer of knowledge9. In addition, existing ensemble approaches lack mechanisms to adapt model contributions based on question type or complexity10. Computational efficiency versus accuracy trade off is not fully determined, especially in the case of real time application where response time is required in the sub-second range11.

The key problem with the modern question answering systems is that it is difficult to create an answer reranking framework at once being highly accurate and being computationally efficient enough to be deployed at large scales. Current single-model methods cannot capture diverse linguistic patterns in real-world questions, while existing ensemble techniques lack instance-sensitive aggregation schemes, leading to suboptimal answers for complex queries12–14. Our approach differs from related paradigms along three specific technical dimensions: (i) Unlike standard mixture-of-experts (MoE) frameworks, which apply static or input-independent gating functions, our aggregator conditions ensemble weights jointly on cross-encoder score vectors and a 4-dimensional question-type one-hot encoding making weighting instance-aware at the query level rather than at the input-feature level. (ii) Unlike multi-teacher knowledge distillation12, which compresses ensemble knowledge into a single student model and discards model diversity at inference time, our architecture retains all three cross-encoders during inference and dynamically adjusts their per-query contributions without any knowledge compression. (iii) Unlike RankT515, which is a single sequence-to-sequence reranker with a fixed encoder architecture, our framework explicitly combines three architectures (BERT16, RoBERTa17, DeBERTa18) with distinct pre-training corpora and attention factorizations DeBERTa’s disentangled attention and BERT’s bidirectional encoding capture complementary linguistic patterns that no single architecture can replicate. Formally, the problem addressed in this work is: given a question Q and a set of K candidate answers retrieved by a dual-encoder, how to optimally combine relevance scores from multiple cross-encoder models through an instance-aware weighting mechanism that adapts to question characteristics, overcoming the limitation of static ensemble strategies that apply fixed weights regardless of question type or complexity.

This study introduces a new ensemble-based answer reranking model to address the computational limitations of current methods while improving accuracy through adaptive model combination. Unlike existing fusion strategies such as simple score averaging, fixed linear combination, or model stacking, our approach introduces an instance-aware confidence-weighting mechanism that conditions ensemble weights on question-level characteristics. Compared to multi-teacher knowledge distillation methods12, which compress ensemble knowledge into a single student model and lose model diversity at inference time, our architecture retains all cross-encoder models and dynamically adjusts their contributions per query. The confidence-weighting mechanism measures cross-entropy between the outputs of teacher models and ground truth distributions, reducing erroneous knowledge transfer and enhancing ensemble performance. The suggested architecture has shown significant advancements in ranking accuracy across benchmark datasets including MS MARCO, WikiQA, and TREC-QA, while maintaining computational efficiency suitable for production deployment. Moreover, our extensive ablation experiments with results reported over three random seeds provide empirical evidence for the effectiveness of each architectural component, offering clear design guidelines for ensemble-based question answering systems.

Literature review

This literature review explores the development of answer reranking algorithms, starting with traditional machine learning methods, moving on to innovations of deep learning, and finally looking at transformer-based architectures.

Machine learning foundations in answer reranking

The paradigms of the foundational methods of answer reranking were based on the old paradigms of information retrieval, in which early systems used rule-based and feature-engineering methods to enhance answer selection19. Initial applications were based on lexical matching methods, and TF-IDF and BM25 scoring systems were used to generate candidate answers. Although such sparse approaches to retrieval were computationally efficient, they proved limited to a considerable degree in the ability to represent a semantic relationship between questions and candidate answers20.

The idea of supervised learning as an answer candidate ordering method has been introduced through a range of traditional learning-to-rank systems, which form the conceptual basis of modern reranking systems21. Usually, these methods used support vector machines (SVM) and random forest algorithms and relied on handcrafted features, like lexical overlap, lexical matching of known entities, and syntactic similarity values22.

The shift of the sparse to the dense representations was a crucial development in retrieval efficiency. Dense passage retrieval (DPR) proposed two-encoder systems that showed spectacular increases on the traditional sparse systems and reached an 9-19% overall increase in top-20 passage retrieval accuracy23. This theoretical breakthrough laid the groundwork of more state-of-the-art reranking methods with the ability to compute semantic similarity in continuous vectors spaces with efficient indexing algorithms such as FAISS2.

Deep learning integration and neural architectures

Answer reranking was reinvented by the introduction of more advanced representation learning abilities through the integration of deep neural networks24. Recurrent neural networks (RNN) and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks were the first networks able to make significant gains in terms of modeling orderly dependencies among pairs of questions and answers. These architectures dealt with the sequential property of textual data and was efficient computationally but was restricted by the inherent sequential processing that such architectures provided1.

Convolutional neural networks (CNN) provided some complementary benefits as local n-gram pattern has been presented and the hierarchical feature representation25. The hybrid CNN-RNN architecture in hybrid models also exhibited superior functionality in a variety of QA benchmarks, especially in cases where both local pattern recognition and sequential modeling properties were needed26.

Transformer-based models and cross-encoder architectures

Transformer architectures were introduced and impacted answer reranking capabilities with self-attention and bidirectional context modeling in it fundamentally27. BERT-based cross-encoders showed better results than the traditional two-encoder models because they operated equally on pairs of question-answer and allowed a more subtle similarity calculation. Cross-encoders are more successful than bi-encoders because they can encode both input sequences at the cost of higher computational cost28.

Recent trends in the optimization of cross-encoders are directed to the efficiency gains without compromising those. Methods like knowledge distillation can be used to reduce the size of large cross-encoders into smaller and more efficient models, with results of 2.4x model size and 7x runtime reduction and similar accuracy29. These optimizations overcome the main drawback of cross-encoders, which is their computational intensity within production settings8.

Multi-encoder ensemble methods have also been integrated which has increased the effectiveness of reranking. Multiple transformer model systems (BERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTa) with adaptive confidence combination are uniform in their gain according to benchmark datasets30. These ensemble techniques exploit diversity of models in order to enhance robustness and overcome the weaknesses of single-model techniques to a variety of question types31. Recent work has further demonstrated the effectiveness of disentangled attention mechanisms for improving transformer-based language understanding32.

Contemporary developments and emerging paradigms

New studies have discussed the integration of large language models (LLMs) in reranking systems, and methods such as RankGPT have shown competitive performance using prompt-based reranking strategies11. Nevertheless, recent empirical evaluations demonstrate that fine-tuned cross-encoders and sequence-to-sequence rerankers remain competitive with LLM-based approaches while achieving substantially higher computational efficiency15.

The idea of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) integration is an important modern trend, with the reranking elements playing a crucial role as the links between retrieval and generation stages15. The current RAG systems use advanced reranking algorithms in order to guarantee the quality of retrieved context, which has a direct effect on the generation accuracy and the factual consistency33.

Research gaps

In spite of the significant progress, a number of significant gaps remain in the present-day answer reranking methods. The trade-off of computational efficiency versus accuracy is still a major problem, especially in real-time computing where response time must be less than one second. Although cross-encoders are more accurate, they can be scaled only quadratically with the size of the candidate set, and their use in the production setting is constrained.

The other important problem is the domain adaptation as the majority of the existing approaches require a substantial amount of fine-tuning to succeed in the specialized domains. The lack of transfer learning mechanism across domains makes the reranking systems in practice constrained to a limited application. This weakness is especially highlighted on low-resource areas where the availability of training data is scarce.

The methodologies of evaluation remain a challenge due to the fact that the normal metrics used perhaps cannot be that effective in reflecting the subtle needs of the modern systems in QA. The elaboration of newer and improved evaluation systems whereby the answers are evaluated with regard to completeness of the answers, factuality and the contextual applicability is a very important research direction.

Methodology

This methodology deals with reranking answers in question answering (QA) systems. Our solution is to have a two-step pipeline as the candidate answers are produced, and then reranked to enhance the accuracy. In the following, we detail the baseline for comparison, our model selection rationale, data preparation, feature engineering, mathematical formulation, architecture, and implementation specifics.

Baseline method

To benchmark our approach, we adopt a standard retrieve-then-read pipeline as the baseline, which combines BM25 or dense passage retrieval (DPR) with a single BERT-based reader model27. In this baseline pipeline, a question is passed to the retriever to fetch candidate answer passages, which are then fed directly to a cross-encoder reader that scores each candidate independently. The baseline does not perform ensemble-based reranking of answers, candidate answers are ranked solely by the single reader’s confidence score. This often leads to suboptimal final answers when the top-ranked passage is not truly the best source, as a single model cannot capture the full diversity of linguistic patterns across different question types. In contrast, our proposed pipeline inserts an answer reranking module that employs adaptive instance-aware weighting rather than static score fusion or knowledge compression into a single model.

Model selection

We proposed ensemble architecture that integrates three complementary components to achieve robust question-answer reranking.

Dual-encoder layer

We adopt Sentence-BERT (SBERT-base)28 to embed questions and answer sentences independently. Dense vectors are compared by cosine similarity; the top-K (= 20) candidates are passed downstream. SBERT offers millisecond-level retrieval while preserving semantic recall.

Cross-encoder ensemble

Three fine-tuned transformers are BERT-base16, RoBERTa-base17 and DeBERTa-base18 form the deep interaction layer. Each cross-encoder receives the concatenated [CLS] question [SEP] answer [SEP] sequence and outputs a scalar relevance score. Model diversity (different pre-training corpora and attention factorisations) guards against over-fitting to any single linguistic style.

Adaptive confidence aggregator

Inspired by mixture-of-experts9, we train a two-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP) that ingests the three cross-encoder scores and a one-hot vector for question class (factoid, list, yes/no, descriptive). Question types are determined using a lightweight rule-based classifier that analyses interrogative words and syntactic patterns (e.g., “how many” → list; “is/are/does” → yes/no). On a manually labelled subset of 500 questions, this classifier achieves 91.3% accuracy. A softmax layer returns weights that are applied to the raw scores before a final sigmoid.

Data collection

To comprehensively evaluate our methodology, three widely-adopted datasets were selected:

MS MARCO v2.1

Provided by Microsoft34, MS MARCO contains approximately one million real user-generated questions paired with candidate answer passages from web documents. The dataset is devoted to open-domain QA cases, which offer linguistic diversity. Our model can easily generalize because it is a vast size (8.8 million passages). It is obtained directly out of the Bing search logs and for that reason, it is perfect in validating pragmatic reranking capacities owing to the existence of various plausible solutions to a query.

WikiQA

This dataset was introduced by Yang et al.35, having 3,047 natural questions based on Bing search queries, and 29,258 candidate answers sentences of Wikipedia articles. The main advantage of WikiQA is the realistic questions and the short and situation-specific responses that render it quite suitable in investigating the accuracy of reranking in simple yet noisy environments.

TREC-QA

TREC-QA is based on the Text Retrieval Conference QA track, and it consists of more than 1,200 factoid questions with short-answer sentences taken out of news articles36. This dataset is controlled and its answers are short in nature which would enable us to test the effectiveness of our model in identifying accurate factual information within short textual conditions.

All of these datasets reflect various QA situations, which also allow us to fully validate our reranking strategy.

Data preprocessing

For each dataset, we followed tailored preprocessing procedures to ensure data readiness for transformer-based embedding and ranking tasks. For MS MARCO, we used the official dev set as our test set and held out 15% of the training set for validation, consistent with standard practice in the literature. For WikiQA and TREC-QA, we used the official train/dev/test splits as provided by the dataset authors (Yang et al.,35; Voorhees & Tice,36). All hyperparameters were tuned exclusively on the validation set, and test results were reported strictly on unseen test data.

MS MARCO

Raw data required normalization by lowercasing all text, removing redundant spaces, and tokenizing text using WordPiece tokenizer . Empty passages were discarded. No augmentation was performed given the dataset’s inherent variability.

WikiQA

Text normalization similar to MS MARCO was applied, with additional steps to remove irrelevant symbols and special characters. Answers with missing labels or ambiguous content were dropped. Minor data augmentation involved shuffling negative candidate answers during training to reduce bias towards positional features.

TREC-QA

Similar normalization and cleaning steps were conducted. Given its limited data size, additional data augmentation included question paraphrasing using back-translation techniques, thus increasing the robustness of the reranking model. Feature selection

In developing the reranking model, we also investigated which features of the question-answer pairs are most predictive of correct answers. We employed SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis as a post-hoc explainability tool to interpret which input characteristics correlate with the ensemble’s ranking behavior. Note that these features are not direct inputs to the neural reranker but serve as explainability proxies computed via a surrogate model. The model inherently uses a deep representation, but for explainability we considered several indicative features derived from each question-candidate pair: e.g., semantic similarity between question and answer text, the number of named entities in the answer that also appear in the question, answer length, the original QA model’s confidence score for that candidate, etc. We used a gradient-boosted tree surrogate model (XGBoost with 500 estimators) to approximate the cross-encoder ensemble’s ranking decisions and computed SHAP values for features. The surrogate model achieves R of 0.87 on MS MARCO, 0.91 on WikiQA, and 0.89 on TREC-QA, indicating high fidelity to the actual reranker’s behavior. This analysis validates the aggregator’s input design: the two highest SHAP-ranked features across all three datasets semantic similarity (cosine score from SBERT) and named-entity overlap are directly captured by the DeBERTa and BERT cross-encoders respectively (see Table 2), providing post-hoc empirical justification for including these complementary architectures in the ensemble. The SHAP analysis serves as a post-hoc explainability tool and is not a component of the reranking pipeline itself. The following feature set is consistently computed across all three datasets: semantic similarity (cosine score from SBERT), named-entity overlap, answer length, BM25 score, and cross-encoder confidence. Model architecture

Our proposed ensemble architecture integrates three complementary components to achieve robust question-answer reranking as shown in Fig. 1.

Proposed ensemble model architecture for answer reranking. This figure illustrates the proposed ensemble model architecture integrating dual-encoder retrieval using SBERT and FAISS, cross-encoders including BERT, RoBERTa, and DeBERTa, adaptive aggregation, and score fusion for ranked answer prediction.

Stage 1: dual-encoder retrieval

Sentence-BERT (SBERT-base)28 independently encodes the question Q and each corpus sentence into 768-dimensional dense vectors. Candidate answers are ranked by cosine similarity, and the top-K () candidates are passed downstream. Approximate nearest-neighbour indexing via FAISS2 enables sub-30 ms retrieval over corpora of 5M+ sentences.

Stage 2: parallel cross-encoding

Each candidate pair is fed independently into three fine-tuned cross-encoders: BERT-base16, RoBERTa-base17, and DeBERTa-base18. Each model processes the concatenated [CLS] question [SEP] answer [SEP] sequence and produces a final [CLS] representation, which is passed through a task-specific linear head to obtain a scalar relevance score for the m-th encoder.

Stage 3: question type classification

A lightweight rule-based classifier analyses interrogative words and syntactic patterns of Q to assign a question type label from {factoid, list, yes/no, descriptive}. The classification rules map interrogative patterns as follows: “who/what/when/where” → factoid, “how many/list” → list, “is/are/does” → yes/no, and remaining → descriptive. To validate the classifier, 500 questions were randomly sampled from the MS MARCO training set and manually labeled by two annotators (Cohen’s kappa = 0.88), yielding 91.3% rule-based classification accuracy. For comparison, a learned alternative BERT-base fine-tuned on the same 500 annotations via 5-fold cross-validation achieves 93.1% accuracy, a marginal 1.8% difference. When substituted into the full pipeline on MS MARCO, the learned classifier yields MAP of 0.4021 vs. 0.4012 for the rule-based system, a 0.2% gap within one standard deviation across seeds. Given this negligible performance difference and the rule-based classifier’s practical advantages no additional training data, no GPU dependency at classification time, and full interpretability of decision rules, we retain the rule-based approach as the default. This label is encoded as a one-hot vector and concatenated with the cross-encoder scores for the aggregation stage.

Stage 4: adaptive aggregation

The score vector and the question-type one-hot are concatenated to form a 7-dimensional input vector and fed into a two-layer MLP (hidden size = 64, GELU activation, dropout = 0.1). A softmax output layer produces instance-aware weights satisfying . The fused score is computed via Eq. (1), followed by a sigmoid to yield a final relevance probability. Candidates are sorted in descending order of this probability. The entropy regularisation term (Eq. (3)) prevents weight collapse during training.

Stage 5: output

The highest-ranked answer is returned to the user. A configurable confidence threshold can trigger a “no-answer” fallback when the top-ranked candidate’s probability falls below the threshold.

Because aggregation is instance-aware, the system adapts its weighting per question type. On the MS MARCO validation set, the mean learned weights are (0.42, 0.31, 0.27) for factoid questions, (0.28, 0.33, 0.39) for descriptive questions, and (0.35, 0.38, 0.27) for yes/no questions. This confirms that the system leans on BERT for short factoid answers while allocating more weight to DeBERTa for verbose descriptive answers, consistent with DeBERTa’s strength in modelling long-range dependencies through disentangled attention.

Implementation details

Implementation details are given below:

Frameworks and libraries

All experiments were conducted in PyTorch 2.2 with Hugging Face Transformers 4.41 for model fine-tuning, FAISS 1.8 for dense-vector indexing, scikit-learn 1.5 for evaluation utilities, and SHAP 0.45 for post-hoc feature attribution. Mixed-precision (FP16) training was enabled throughout. Runs were executed on a single NVIDIA A100 (40 GB) GPU, seeds were fixed to 42 and deterministic cuDNN paths forced for exact reproducibility. For all experiments, hyperparameter tuning was performed solely on the validation split, and final results were computed on the held-out test split to prevent data leakage.

Cross-encoders: three models (BERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTa) each trained three epochs; learning-rates , , and respectively; batch 24 pairs (grad-accumulated to 48); label-smoothing 0.1; gradient-clip 1.0.

Experiments and results

The experimental validation of our proposed answer reranking methodology was conducted through comprehensive evaluation across multiple benchmark datasets. Our experiments include the ablation case to explain the architectural decision, quantification to compare our model with the state of the art approaches and play by play of the performance metrics that surrounds the computational efficiency.

Ablation studies

We performed systematic ablation studies to test the contributions of the individual components presented in our proposed architecture by ablating, or randomly altering, important components. These experiments provide empirical support for our design decisions and demonstrate additive benefits of every component through comprehensive ranking evaluation metrics across all benchmark datasets.

We conducted the ablation experiments on all three datasets to have a full assessment and verify the effectiveness of the component in a variety of question-answering situations. Individual components were tested in isolation while keeping remaining architectural elements fixed, ensuring fair comparison across standard information retrieval metrics.

The result of the ablation indicates that each of the components is a significant contributor to the overall performance of both ranking evaluation metrics as indicated in all the three datasets in Table 3. Most importantly, in the absence of dual-encoder retrieval, the performance decreases substantially (0.3473 MAP, 0.3487 MRR).

Quantitative analysis

We evaluated our proposed methodology against established state-of-the-art approaches across three benchmark datasets using standard information retrieval ranking metrics. It compares recent transformer-based models and the standard learning-to-rank methods and gives full coverage of the existing approaches with prioritized emphasis on ranking-conscious evaluation.

We further contextualize our results against recent strong baselines from different reranking paradigms. ColBERTv28 is a late-interaction retrieval model that achieves state-of-the-art MRR@10 as a standalone retriever on the MS MARCO passage ranking task. Our model achieves MRR of 0.3970 on the same benchmark. It is important to note that ColBERTv2 operates as a full-corpus retriever whereas our framework is a reranker applied to pre-retrieved top-K candidates—the evaluation settings are not directly equivalent, and a controlled comparison under identical candidate sets would require re-running ColBERTv2 in a reranking-only configuration, which is beyond the scope of the current study.11 (GPT-4 based listwise reranker) incurs multi-second per-query latency versus our 54.9 ms with parallel cross-encoder execution, making the latency trade-off a primary distinguishing factor. Neither ColBERTv2 nor RankGPT reports results on WikiQA or TREC-QA; a cross-benchmark comparison under identical settings remains an open direction for future work.

When cross-encoders are executed in parallel across three GPU streams, end-to-end latency is 54.9 ms per query, which is feasible for near-real-time applications. Peak GPU memory consumption is 6.8 GB with parallel execution and FP16 inference, well within the capacity of modern inference hardware. At batch size 32, throughput reaches 185 queries/second.

To quantify the cost-vs-gain trade-off, a single BERT cross-encoder achieves 0.3671 MAP on MS MARCO with approximately 50.5 ms latency (SBERT/FAISS retrieval 8.4 ms + BERT cross-encoder 42.1 ms), while our full ensemble achieves 0.4012 MAP with 54.9 ms latency under parallel execution a 9.3% accuracy gain for only 8.7% additional latency. The peak GPU memory of 6.8 GB (FP16) fits within a single consumer-grade GPU such as the NVIDIA RTX 3090, where our measured latency is 71.3 ms per query 30% overhead vs. the A100 but within interactive response thresholds. For CPU-only deployment (Intel Xeon E5-2670, 16-core), sequential cross-encoder execution requires approximately 1,840 ms per query; applying INT8 quantization via Hugging Face Optimum reduces this to approximately 620 ms, suitable for batch or asynchronous QA pipelines. A single-encoder fallback (DeBERTa-only, no ensemble) further reduces CPU latency to approximately 310 ms at a cost of 2.4% MAP on MS MARCO. For scalability, increasing the candidate pool from K=20 to K=40 approximately doubles cross-encoder latency to 10 ms on GPU, remaining within interactive response times.

Entropy regularisation sensitivity

We evaluated entropy regularisation parameter across all three datasets. On MS MARCO: λ = 0 causes weight collapse to a single model (MAP 0.3891); performance peaks at λ = 0.02 (MAP 0.4012); over-regularisation at λ = 0.1 forces near-uniform weights (MAP 0.3934). On WikiQA: the same pattern holds—peak MAP at λ = 0.02 (MAP 0.8731), degrading to 0.8684 at λ = 0.1 and 0.8663 at λ = 0. On TREC-QA: peak MAP occurs at λ = 0.01 and λ = 0.02 (MAP 0.925 and 0.924 respectively), confirming that λ = 0.02 is robust across all three datasets. Through our overall experimental analysis, it is demonstrated that the proposed ensemble-based answer reranking method achieves state-of-the-art performance with computational efficiency acceptable for real-world deployment. The systematic ablation experiments confirm the contribution of every architectural element, whereas quantitative comparisons demonstrate consistent improvements over current techniques on a variety of benchmark datasets.

Discussion

The experimental results show that ensemble-based answer reranking is effective across the three benchmark datasets evaluated, spanning open-domain web questions (MS MARCO), Wikipedia-based QA (WikiQA), and factoid QA from news (TREC-QA). Generalization to other domains such as biomedical or legal QA requires further validation. The proposed methodology achieves 9.3% MAP and 7.6% MRR improvement on MS MARCO over BERTLargeBM25CAT, indicating meaningful progress in ranking quality among the compared baselines. This is because the combination of dual-encoder retrieval, cross-encoder ensemble processing, and adaptive confidence aggregation do so synergistically and overcome the shortcomings of single-model methods.

The ablation experiments demonstrate the important information about the component contributions. Elimination of the adaptive confidence aggregator leads to overall performance loss (3.9% MAP loss on MS MARCO, 1.9% on WikiQA, 1.8% on TREC-QA) that shows the relevance of instance-based model weighting. More importantly, the cross-encoder ensemble provides the most significant performance improvement (8.5% MAP increase on MS MARCO) as it confirms the hypothesis that diversity of models improves the accuracy of answer selection by complementary linguistic pattern recognition.

These differences in the performances of the datasets are interesting displays of the nature of the data they represent. The enhancements of ensemble methods on the difficult large-scale web-derived data are the largest on MS MARCO, which indicates that the different model attitudes would be especially useful to noisy real-world data. The smaller improvement on TREC-QA (1.2% MAP) is explained by its controlled factoid structure, where strong single-model baselines already achieve high precision, leaving less headroom for ensemble gains.

The improvement in intermediate performance (2.1% MAP) of WikiQA can be attributed to the fact that it is neither too complex nor too simple in its approach to semantic understanding because the ensemble diversity is useful and the computation overhead can be controlled. The greater difference in MRR scores between the MAP scores in all the data sets prove that the proposed system can be successfully used to place correct answers at the top positions that are essential in the context of practical implementation where a user is likely to focus mainly on the initial results.

However, despite the strong performance, several limitations should be acknowledged. The quadratic complexity in cross-encoder processing constrains scalability for extremely large candidate sets. With parallel GPU execution, the end-to-end latency of 54.9 ms per query (Table 6) is feasible for near-real-time applications but may be prohibitive for latency-critical deployments requiring sub-10 ms response times. Additionally, the rule-based question classifier may misclassify ambiguous question types; the evaluation is limited to English-language QA benchmarks; the framework has not been tested in domain-specific settings such as biomedical or legal QA; and the full three-encoder ensemble requires a modern GPU for sub-100 ms latency CPU-only deployment increases end-to-end latency to approximately 620 ms with INT8 quantization, and a single-encoder DeBERTa fallback is recommended for strict resource-constrained settings. Further limitations include: the framework assumes pre-retrieved candidate sets and does not address end-to-end retrieval quality, and the ensemble approach requires fine-tuning three separate transformer models, increasing training time and storage requirements compared to single-model approaches.

The SHAP surrogate model (R = 0.87–0.91) confirms that the features most predictive of correct ranking align with the encoder strengths exploited by the adaptive aggregator: BERT’s higher mean weight (0.42) for factoid questions corresponds to its strength on entity-anchored retrieval, which is reflected in named-entity overlap being the second-highest SHAP feature (Table 2); DeBERTa’s higher mean weight (0.39) for descriptive questions corresponds to the greater importance of answer length and semantic coherence signals in those question types, as observed in the SHAP analysis across all datasets.

Conclusion and future recommendations

The study has been able to show that answering questions by using ensemble-based answer reranking with adaptive confidence weighting can greatly improve the quality of performance of question answering but is also computationally feasible. The suggested methodology has shown impressive gains in both benchmark datasets, as 9.3% improvement in MAP on MS MARCO and almost perfect 0.979 MRR on TREC-QA demonstrate new records of improvement. The systematized ablation experiments validate that every architectural component is beneficial to the overall system performance, and the cross-encoder ensemble offers the largest benefits, and the adaptive confidence aggregator makes sure that the model is fully utilized. The mathematical model which includes entropy regularization appropriately helps to avoid the over-concentration of models and allows the dynamic weight adjustment to the properties of questions. These results confirm the hypothesis that intelligent ensemble integration can be used to overcome the constraints of single-model methods in a non-prohibitive manner and provide high-end reranking to be practically implemented in large-scale question answering systems.

Despite these gains, the framework has limitations including quadratic cross-encoder complexity for large candidate sets, dependence on a rule-based question classifier, and evaluation restricted to English-language benchmarks. Future studies need to investigate adaptive ensemble pruning to save on computational expenses whilst keeping the accuracy intact and incorporate multimodal information source on complex queries.
