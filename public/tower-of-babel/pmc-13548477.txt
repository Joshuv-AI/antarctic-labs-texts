Construction of ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposites for enhancing the visible-light-driven photocatalytic degradation of alizarine yellow R dye
Authors: Muhammad Jamshaid, Atef El Jery, Moutaz Aldrdery, Mohammad Ashraf Hossain, Maha Sharif, Huma Sadiq, Narzikul Maxmudov, Alisher Babamuratov, Shamil Mahmudov, Hossam S. El-Beltagi, Ahmad Samir Shahsamandy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimThe current research study focuses on the construction of effective zinc oxide/tin dioxide (ZnO/SnO2) nanocomposites The synthesized nanocomposites were subsequently implemented as photocatalyst materials for the elimination of toxic alizarine yellow R (AYR) in aqueous solution under visible light.MethodThe synthesis of ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite photocatalyst was carried out via hydrothermal method combined with ultrasonic-assisted calcination with some modifications.ResultsVarious analytical methods, such as XRD, SEM-EDS, XPS, and UV-DRS analysis, validated the effective fabrication of the nanocomposites. The findings exhibited the enhanced elimination of AYR with 98.07% degradation using the ZnO/SnO2 photocatalyst, significantly surpassing the degradation rates of pure ZnO (63.10%) and SnO2 (52.47%) over a 60-minute timeframe under optimal conditions. The rate constant for ZnO/SnO2 (0.0651 min⁻¹) was markedly superior to that of pure ZnO (0.0170 min⁻¹) and SnO2 (0.0125 min⁻¹). The reduced bandgap energy of the ZnO/SnO2 photocatalyst relative to pure ZnO and SnO2 enhanced its degradation potential for AYR degradation through enhancing the visible light absorption. The research examined the influence of various reaction variables and intervening anions on AYR degradation. Additionally, the radical scavenging experiments confirm the presence of reactive radicals involved in the presence of AYR degradation.ConclusionThe ZnO/SnO2 photocatalyst showed remarkable reusability and preserved its photocatalytic effectiveness after undergoing five consecutive cycles that further confirmed the significant potential of the synthesized ZnO/SnO2 photocatalyst in eliminating AYR-contaminated water.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13065-026-01907-w.

Introduction

The proliferation of highly toxic and enduring pollutants from various industries, such as dyeing, printing, textiles, leather, and pharmaceuticals, infiltrating water infrastructure, constitutes a major environmental issue that cannot be omitted due to their detrimental effects for living organisms [1]. The harmful pollutants emitted from various industrial processes typically consist of synthetic dyes and heavy metals, which severely hinder biological degradation due to their complex structure, substantial molecular weight, highly aromatic nature, and enduring stability [2]. A negligible amount of dyes accumulates in environments, exacerbating the water crisis and posing harmful effects on aquatic life and human populations [3]. Notwithstanding many chemical alterations, these substances disrupted the functions of aquatic life, obstructing sunlight from penetrating the whole system and ultimately eradicating it [4].

Amongst various dyes, Alizarin Yellow R (AYR) leaked into water settings from the textile sector and is employed as a colorant in the dyeing industries [5]. It is a derivative of salicylic acid, relatively solvable in water, has great resistance to bio-degradation, and exceedingly stubborn owing to its intense coloration and azo bonding within its molecular structure [6]. Furthermore, it exhibits resistance to deterioration caused by lighting and heating, causing carcinogenic/mutagenic activities [7]. These organic pollutants also inflict significant harm on aquatic ecosystems, necessitating the development of effective methods for their degradation and removal. Consequently, it is indispensable to eliminate such effluents from various businesses prior to their release into the water [8].

A range of diverse techniques, including adsorption, oxidation, precipitation, membrane filtration, electrochemical treatment, bio-degradation, and photocatalysis, have been developed to enable the removing of potentially harmful contaminants [9–12]. Each method has certain advantages and disadvantages, with its effectiveness dependent on factors like dye properties and concentration [13]. Photocatalytic technology is an extremely cost-effective and efficient technique for eliminating hazardous pollutants from wastewater, since it is non-polluting, economically viable, and operates at mild operating settings [14–16]. Photocatalysis, which uses sunlight to break down organic pollutants like dyes and antibiotics into harmless inorganic small molecules under ambient conditions [17], is a popular area of research for creating environmentally friendly ways to clean up water pollution [18]. Due to the swift electron transitions between different energy bands, exposure to UV light having energy equal to or exceeding the photocatalyst band gap can be absorbed on the surface, leading to the generation of predominant active species that promote dye breakdown [19]. Ultraviolet radiation comprises around 5–6% of sunlight energy, although visible light represents about 45% of solar light energy [20]. Consequently, the development of photocatalysts for visible light harvesting has emerged as a prominent area of interest within the scientific community [21].

The aggregation of two or more semiconductor metal oxide nanoparticles is increasingly recognized for its enhanced properties relative to individual nanoparticles and its capacity to tailor material characteristics to meet specific application requirements [22]. Although semiconductor metal oxide nanoparticles along with their wide band gaps are identified as promising candidates for environmental cleanup and several other applications but unfortunately their limited light absorption and greater pace of photoinduced charge carrier recombination impede their real-world utilization [23]. This drawback can be resolved by the morphology control, elemental doping, and fabrication of heterojunction nanostructures between the two metal oxides with well-aligned energy bands [24–26]. The nanocomposite formation appears to be a viable approach among the various strategies investigated owing to improved light harvesting and charge separation ability [27]. Nanocomposite engineering with narrow band gap demonstrate superior properties compared to their individual photocatalysts by establishing an intermediate energy level among them and ultimately inhibit the recombination of photogenerated charge carriers, which is advantageous for the intended applications [28].

Among numerous semiconductor metal oxides, ZnO and SnO2 exhibits notable crystallographic compatibility and a range of Fermi level values [29]. ZnO has been widely utilized as an operative photocatalyst owing to its non-toxicity, stability, low-cost, exceptional efficiency, and easy to synthesize [30, 31]. However, owing to its broad optical band gap of approximately 3.36 eV, it is restricted to ultraviolet wavelength [32], accounting for less than 6% of the solar spectrum [33]. Therefore, the primary goal is to narrow its bandgap to facilitate effective absorption of visible light, which accounts for nearly 43% of the solar spectrum [34]. Similarly, SnO2 with a wide bandgap of approximately 3.6 eV, were also examined for these applications owing to their numerous advantages, including abundance and inexpensive nature, and low toxicity [35, 36]. It possesses notable advantages, including excellent chemical stability, higher electron mobility, appropriate energy level configuration, and a straightforward fabrication process [37]. Though, in real-world applications, SnO2 face several limitations, with a greater ability of charge-carrier recombination and stimulation solely under UV light [38]. To address this, nanocomposites among two metal oxides with appropriate energy bands alignment are engineered to enhance photocatalytic performance further.

Based on the aforementioned precedent, this study synthesized a highly efficient ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite photocatalyst via hydrothermal method combined with ultrasonic-assisted calcination [39]. The as-prepared nanocomposite was fully characterized with different physicochemical methods to provide a correlation between their synthesis and photocatalytic potential. The photocatalytic performance of ZnO, SnO2, and ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposites by using AYR, a model anionic azo dye, in an aqueous solution under visible light. The structure-property relationships and the mechanism of photocatalysis were assessed and attributed to the nanocomposite structure of the ZnO/SnO2, resulting in enhanced separation efficiency of electron-hole pairs and increased generation of active radicals, thereby significantly improving photocatalytic activity.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

The following reagents were involved in the fabrication process of ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite photocatalyst: Zinc acetate (Zn(CH3CO2)2), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), ethanol (C2H5OH), tin(II) chloride dihydrate (SnCl2·2H2O), trisodium citrate (Na3C6H5O7), ethylene glycol (C2H6O2), silver nitrate (AgNO3), disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetate (EDTA-2Na, C10H14N2Na2O8), isopropyl alcohol (IPA, C3H8O), and p-benzoquinone (p-BQ, C6H4O2). The chemicals were all very pure and were utilized as they were received, used without further purification.

Photocatalyst preparation

The synthesis of ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite photocatalyst was carried out in three steps using a previously reported method with slight modifications, such as the quantity of reactant [40]. In the first step, a weighed quantity of 0.825 g of Zn(CH3CO2)2 and 0.8 g of sodium hydroxide was added to a flask in the presence of 30 mL of ethanol and stirred for 1 h using a magnetic stirrer until the solution is clear. The aforementioned solution was transferred into the reaction vessel and placed it in an oven at 180 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the sample was rinsed with deionized water and ethanol thrice time and dried in an oven, resulting in the acquisition of ZnO white granules.

The second step was initiated with the incorporation of 0.188 g of SnCl2·2H2O and 2.45 g of Na3C6H5O7 into a flask in the presence of 20 mL of deionized water and 30 mL of ethylene glycol. The resulting solution was vigorously stirred through a magnetic stirrer for one hour to obtain a transparent solution. The obtained solution was kept in an autoclave and heated at 180 °C for 24 h. After calcining the sample, it was then rinsed with deionized water and ethanol three times followed by drying in an oven. At last, faint yellow-colored SnO2 nanoparticles were produced.

In the third step, the fabrication of ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite photocatalysts material was carried out by combining SnO2 in 50 mL of deionized water with a mass ratio of 1:1. Subsequently, the resulting mixture was ultrasonicate for 2 h to attain a homogeneous mixture. Later on, it was dehydrated at a constant temperature of 80 °C followed by calcination in furnace at 350 °C for 2 h. The resultant material was named as ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite material and stored for subsequent application in photocatalytic degradation studies. Characterizations

An array of various advanced analytical tools has been employed to examine the synthesized ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite photocatalysts. An investigation on the crystalline configuration of the ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite materials was conducted using X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8) having Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å) over the 2θ range of 10–80° at a scanning pace of 0.02°/minute. Using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS Sigma 300, Germany), the morphology of ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposites was examined. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was conducted to perform a quantifiable analysis of the elements. The valence states of the elements in ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposites were characterized through analysis with Thermo ESCALAB 250XI X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). A UV–Vis spectrophotometer (JASCO, V-750) in diffuse reflectance mode was used to measure the optical properties of the prepared materials within the wavelength range from 200 to 800 nm. Finally, a UV–Vis absorption spectrometer (UH4150 Spectrophotometer (Integrating Sphere)) to quantify the light absorption to figure out the efficiency of the degradation process.

Evaluation of photocatalytic effectiveness

The photocatalytic efficiency of the prepared materials was evaluated through the degradation of AYR under visible light. A Xenon arc lamp with intensity of 350 W with an intensity of 45 mW/cm2 functioned as the source of light. The illumination source was situated 12 cm above the reactor. In a standard experiment, a 24-ppm AYR solution was provided with 32 mg of the photocatalyst within a 500 mL vessel. The suspension was subjected to magnetic agitation in darkness for approximately one hour to ensure the establishment of adsorption–desorption equilibrium, and subsequently exposed to visible irradiation [41]. Following an appropriate irradiation period, 2–3 mL aliquots were collected at 10-minute intervals and subsequently centrifuged to eliminate distributed photocatalyst. The removal of the dye was assessed by monitoring the change in absorption of the aliquot solution through a UV–Vis absorption spectrometer (UH4150 Spectrophotometer), with distilled water serving as the standard. Photocatalytic decontamination of AYR

The photodegradation of AYR was examined under visible light exposure to evaluate the potential of pure ZnO, SnO2, and ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite photocatalysts. The equilibrium was achieved by placing the solutions, consisting of dye solution and the photocatalyst separately, in the dark prior to completing the degradation experiments. The remaining AYR concentration was determined through UV-visible analysis at determined time frames to evaluate the percentage degradation after the solution was kept under visible light. Utilizing Eq. (1), the proportion of dye degradation was determined by comparing the quantity of degraded dye to the control dye (Fig. 6a). Based on the results, it was observed that the maximal removal percentage was 98.07% for AYR dye, achieved with the ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite photocatalyst after 60 min. Nevertheless, for ZnO, the degradation decreased to 63.10% for AYR dye. The ongoing decline in degradation persisted, with 52.47% of AYR achieved through the use of pure SnO2 nanoparticles. Without the presence of a catalyst, 4.30% of AYR was eliminated following 60 min of reaction, confirming the pivotal role of ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite photocatalyst in AYR degradation.

Effect of parameters

Effect of photocatalysts concentration

The applied quantity of ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite photocatalyst for AYR degradation determines the pace at which the degradation reaction occurs under visible light illumination. Consequently, to determine the optimum quantity of the photocatalyst desirable for AYR degradation, the photo-degradation experiments were accomplished by altering the photocatalyst ranging from 8 mg to 48 mg, as illustrated in Fig. 7a. The results revealed that the elimination rates of AYR were reached to 47.69%, 64.93%, 87.41%, 98.07%, 94.54%, and 89.38% for the photocatalyst loading of 8 mg, 16 mg, 24 mg, 32 mg, 40 mg, and 48 mg, respectively. While the k values were measured in the range of 0.0108 min− 1 to 0.0651 min− 1 for the photocatalyst dosage increased from 8 mg to 48 mg (Fig. S1). It can be confirmed from the presented results that the AYR elimination rate significantly promoted with the increment in photocatalyst dosage and reached at maximum level for a dosage of 32 mg. The maximum elimination rate of AYR can be assigned to the enhanced approachability of active sites and substantial surface area of the photocatalyst [58]. However, it is also apparent that the pace of degradation increased initially until the photocatalyst loading of 32 mg and after that limit it tends to decline. The primary reason behind this is the excessive presence of photocatalyst that can cause the accumulation of particles and obstruct the permeation of light to the photocatalyst surface. What’s more, the turbidity of the solution also slowed down the degradation process, scattering light across the appropriate amount of photocatalyst rather than aiding its absorption [59].

Effect of pollutant concentration

Using the optimum photocatalyst dosage of 32 mg, the rate of AYR degradation in the presence of ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite was measured within the AYR concentration range of 6–36 ppm. The degradation rates for the AYR dye were observed to be 87.41%, 90.68%, 95.21%, 98.07%, 93.7%, and 83.42% for an initial concentration of AYR at 6 ppm, 12 ppm, 18 ppm, 24 ppm, 30 ppm, and 32 ppm, respectively (Fig. 7b). Although the k values were determined in the range of 0.0318 min− 1 to 0.0651 min− 1 for the initial dye concentration increased from 6 ppm to 36 ppm (Fig. S2). The findings show that the degradation of AYR increased as its concentration augmented to 24 ppm, then declined with further increment in the concentration to 30 ppm and 36 ppm. In actuality, the photocatalyst surface produced the most radicals, and the interaction between reactive radicals and AYR molecules enhanced the elimination process. This degradation rate trend may be described as follows: as the AYR concentration in solution raised, so did the amount of dye molecules to be removed by the ZnO/SnO2 photocatalyst, increasing the rate. An increase in dye concentration such as 30 ppm and 36 ppm reduce the absorption of visible light. As a result, it is prevented from penetrating the depths of the reaction mixture, as well as being absorbed by the ZnO/SnO2, leading to a reduction in AYR abatement. Another important explanation of this decrease in degradation is the permanent attachment of AYR molecules on the surface of ZnO/SnO2, limiting the active sites reachable for the absorption of light and consequently the decreased the abatement of dye [60].

Effect of pH

Changes in the pH of wastewater might have a substantial effect on the photodegradation process of dyes. Figure 7c illustrates the effect of pH on the photocatalytic performance of the synthesized ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposites in the photodegradation of AYR dye. The pH can affect on both the charge of the photocatalyst and the nature of target pollutant [61]. Hence, we performed the degradation of AYR dye in a wide pH range (4–9) by adjusting it with the inclusion of HCl and NaOH. The data showed that the dye degraded at a faster pace in slightly acidic pH levels (98.07% at pH 5) and tend to dropped slowly until the pH level reached alkaline. This better photoactivity is due to the electrostatic attraction between the positively charged ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite and the anionic AYR [62]. The degradation efficiency decreased at 61.31% as the pH raised towards alkaline levels (pH 9). This decrease is probably because the negatively charged ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite and AYR molecules develops repulsive interactions [63]. Also, at greater pH, the generation of hydroxyl radicals, which are an important active specie in the photodegradation process, is hindered, which slows down the overall degradation rate even more. Furthermore, the photodegradation reaction at various pH followed the pseudo-1st -order kinetics with k values in the range of 0.0165 min− 1 to 0.0651 min− 1 for the solution pH increased from 4 to 9 (Fig. S3).

Effect of temperature

The role of reaction mixture temperature is also a key parameter that can impact photocatalytic degradation of dyes; therefore, the influence of different temperature on catalytic potential of ZnO/SnO2 was explored comprehensively. The photodegradation of AYR over ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite photocatalyst was studied for four different temperatures, particularly at 25 °C, 30 °C, 35 °C, and 40 °C (Fig. 7d). While performing the degradation process, the catalytic dosage of 32 mg, AYR concentration of 24 ppm, and pH of solution were kept constant at 5. The results of temperature effect undoubtedly disclose that temperature variations had a modest influence on the photocatalyst’s effectiveness, while photo-induced AYR degradation reached at 93.66%, 98.07%, 89.68%, and 82.98%, for the solution temperature of 25 °C, 30 °C, 35 °C, and 40 °C, respectively. Additionally, the photodegradation process at different temperature levels observed the pseudo-1st-order kinetics with k values ranging from 0.0303 min− 1 to 0.0651 min− 1 in the temperature range of 35–40 °C (Fig. S4). The variation in degradation rates was attributable to the interaction among photocatalyst with different kinetic energy at different temperature and the pollutant molecules that can results in collisions among photocatalyst and target pollutant, thereby affecting degradation rate.

Effect of anions and HA

The degradation of dyes in the presence of light can be significantly influenced by inorganic anions. This is contingent upon the quantity and type of anions that are present in the reaction. Consequently, this investigation explores the impact of various inorganic anions on the degradation efficacy of photocatalysts by subjecting an 24 ppm AYR solution to a variety of salts (NaCl, NaNO3, and NaHCO3), each at a concentration of 15 mM (Fig. 8a). The degradation process is slightly impacted by chloride ions (Cl⁻), which prevent the formation of •OH radicals, thereby preventing the passage of these ROS to break down contaminants. In the presence of Cl⁻, the degradation of AYR reached at 94.42%. This reduction in photocatalytic activity is the result of competing adsorption, radical quenching, and interaction with the photocatalyst. Bicaarbonate ions (HCO3⁻) can significantly slow down the degradation of dye to 86.02% by participating in several mechanisms that are detrimental to the process. HCO3⁻ serves as a quencher for •OH radicals, thereby reducing the quantity of these potent oxidative species that are crucial for degradation processes. Verification for reactive species

A scavenging test was conducted to evaluate AYR degradation by the ZnO/SnO2 catalyst under visible-light irradiation, utilizing AgNO3, EDTA-2Na, IPA, and p-BQ as specific scavengers for quenching photo-generated electrons, holes, superoxide radicals, and hydroxyl radicals, respectively (Fig. 8b) [64]. The presence of AgNO3 and EDTA-2Na minimally influenced the AYR degradation, which reached 94.26% and 89.64%, respectively, indicating the smallest contribution of electrons and holes in the degradation process. The significant reduction in AYR degradation was observed in the presence of IPA and p-BQ, which attained 43.87% and 52.46% degradation rate, respectively, thereby indicating the substantial role of •OH and O2•− in AYR degradation. Relying on the results from scavenging studies, the degradation of AYR was primarily governed by •OH and O2•− radicals, with the overall contribution of species to AYR degradation varying as follows: 43.87% •OH > 52.46% O2•− > 89.64% h+ > 94.26% e−.

The photocatalytic process starts when visible light strikes ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposites photocatalyst, causing the excitation of electrons. The electrons then moved to the CB, where they create holes in the VB. After that, the e− from the CB of ZnO transferred to the CB of SnO2, while the h+ from the VB of SnO2 move to the VB of ZnO. So, electrons build up in the CB of SnO2 and h+ build up in the VB of ZnO, which causes the partitioning of carriers in the nanocomposite. The separated carriers can take part in a redox reaction. The e− in the CB of SnO2 combine with the O2, which produces O2•−. The h+ in the VB of ZnO also combine with −OH brought from physically absorbed water on the surface of the photocatalyst and produces •OH radicals. The O2•−radicals can also react with water molecules and produce active hydroperoxyl radical that can generate additional •OH radicals. Lastly, the AYR disintegrated into CO2, H2O, and other products when they combine with the reactive radicals.

Reusability of ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite photocatalyst

The assessment of photocatalyst’s reusability is an important consideration both in terms of economic viewpoint and environmental sustainability. To explore the reusability and effectiveness of ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite photocatalyst, it was employed in five progressive cyclic runs under the determined optimum circumstances (Fig. 11a). For this purpose, the photocatalyst was carefully extracted from the solution, repetitively washed with deionized water and ethanol, and dried in an oven for 24 h to be use in the preceding runs. The experimental findings elucidated that the AYR degradation was reached at 98.07%, 94.10%, 91.32%, 86.15%, and 82.19% after five successive cycles, unveiling moderate reduction in degradation efficiency. These results indicated outstanding ability and sustained effectiveness of ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite photocatalyst following five photocatalytic reactions. The moderate reduction in AYR decontamination might be due to the pore blockage induced by AYR degradation intermediates, as well as the deteriorating of ZnO/SnO2 particles all through the retrieval process [67]. The post-degradation structural analysis of the employed photocatalyst was also studied through XRD analysis which indicated that the crystalline structure of the ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite photocatalyst remained unaltered after the reaction (Fig. 11b), indicating that the synthesized photocatalyst is suitable for reuse in pollutant removal. Finally, ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite photocatalyst confirms its effectiveness in terms of degradation, highlighting its enormous potential in visible-light-driven photocatalytic effluent treatment.

Conclusion

In the present investigation, ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposites were fabricated through a hydrothermal method combined with ultrasonic-assisted calcination. The synthesis of the materials was established through characterization with various microscopic and spectroscopic methods, including XRD, SEM-EDS, XPS, and UV-Vis-DRS. Additionally, the band gap energy of all the materials was determined through a Tauc plot and determined to be 3.24 eV for ZnO, 3.31 eV for SnO2, and 3.19 eV for ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite. Among all the prepared materials, ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite demonstrated superior degradation effectiveness of 98.07% compared to the ZnO (63.10%) and SnO2 (52.47%) in 60 min during the photodegradation of AYR. The kinetics of the degradation process have also been examined; it was determined that the photodegradation of the AYR dye adheres to pseudo-1st -order kinetics. The AYR degradation process was additionally studied under the influence of various reaction parameters that validated the maximum degradation of AYR using 32 mg of ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposite photocatalyst, 24 ppm of AYR initial concentration, a pH of 5, and a temperature of 30 °C. A scavenging study indicates that •OH and O2•− radical species participate in the photodegradation process, and, relying on their participation, a reliable photodegradation process has been anticipated. Furthermore, ZnO/SnO2 nanocomposites demonstrate excellent stability and reusability, as confirmed by the recyclability study. Finally, the developed ZnO/SnO2-based nanocomposite photocatalyst demonstrates significant potential as a photocatalyst material for environmental cleanup applications.
