Comparative bioactivities of Annona squamosa seeds and fruits: phytochemical insights and pharmacological evaluation
Authors: Marwah S. Joudah, Hossam M. Hassan, Wesam R. Kadhum, Riyadh J. Almaliki, Ahmed Ibrahim Fathelrahman, Nur Azzalia Kamaruzaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Annona squamosa has been traditionally used and has demonstrated promising therapeutic properties. However, there remains a lack of comprehensive comparative evaluation of the phytochemical composition and biological activities among its different fruit parts. The present work aimed to examine the chemical compositions of seed (MSS), pulp and peels (JSW) and assess antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer activities of the Annona squamosa fruits procured from the Iraqi market. The antioxidant capacity using DPPH, ABTS, 5-LOX, and ORAC assays and the anti-inflammatory COX1/COX2 inhibition assays were determined. In addition, anticancer potential against HepG2 (liver), MCF-7 (breast), and Colo-205 (colon) cell lines were investigated via MTT- assay, flow cytometry for cell cycle distribution, and apoptosis analysis. Phytochemical profiling was also conducted using LC-HRESIMS. Among the extracts, MSS exhibited the strongest antioxidant activity. JSW on the other hand demonstrated higher activity in DPPH and ABTS assays. Furthermore, MSS displayed significant cytotoxic effects against cancer cells. Overall, the findings underscore the pharmacological potential of A. squamosa seeds and fruits (pulp and peels) extracts, particularly MSS, as valuable sources of antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer agents. This study highlights the promise of A. squamosa as a sustainable candidate for drug development and nutraceutical applications, while also emphasizing the health benefits of fruit waste utilization.

Introduction

The family Annonaceae, commonly known as the custard apple or sugar apple family represents one of the largest families of tropical plants, comprising nearly 130 genera and over 2300 species of trees and shrubs1. These plants are distributed across tropical and subtropical regions worldwide and are well recognized for producing exotic fruits with nutritional and therapeutic significance. Examples include Annona muricata (soursop or graviola), Annona crassiflora (araticum-do-cerrado or marolo), and Annona squamosa (sugar apple or sweetsop)2. Annona squamosa Linn., locally known as “Apple Keshta” in Iraq, is one of the most commercially cultivated species of the family. Beyond its nutritional value, this plant has been traditionally employed in folk medicine across Asia, Africa, and the Americas for the treatment of diverse ailments, including diabetes, ulcers, infections, and chronic inflammatory conditions3,4.

The fruits of A. squamosa are widely consumed in the tropics and are commonly used to prepare drinks, ice creams, and confectioneries3. Although the pulp is edible and commercially valuable, the seeds are usually discarded as waste despite being a rich source of bioactive secondary metabolites5. Previous phytochemical investigations have revealed that the seeds contain acetogenins, alkaloids, flavonoids, and other compounds associated with a wide range of biological activities, including antifungal, antiparasitic, and anticancer effects6,7. Such findings underscore the potential of fruit waste valorization as a sustainable approach to nutraceutical and pharmaceutical development.

Medicinal properties of A. squamosa and related Annona species have attracted increasing scientific interest. Ethnopharmacological evidence shows their applications in traditional systems of medicine, where different plant parts—leaves, bark, roots, and seeds—are used to manage conditions such as dysentery, diarrhea, cough, wounds, and skin diseases8. In particular, alkaloid-rich extracts of Annona plants have demonstrated antibacterial activity, providing therapeutic benefits against bacterial ulcers, dysentery, and boils9. Furthermore, several members of the Annonaceae family are documented for their anticancer10, antiinflammatory11, antimicrobial4, and antiviral activities, including anti-HIV potential10. These diverse pharmacological properties are attributed to a unique chemical repertoire, especially annonaceous acetogenins, which are polyketide-derived metabolites with mitochondrial inhibitory and apoptosis-inducing effects12.

Cancer remains a global health challenge, and the exploration of plant-derived agents with multitargeted activities such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cytotoxic effects, has become a promising strategy in anticancer drug discovery13. Reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated oxidative stress and chronic inflammation are both linked to the initiation and progression of malignancies, highlighting the therapeutic importance of natural products that can modulate these pathological pathways14. Studies on A. squamosa have suggested cytotoxic effects of its seeds and leaves against various tumor cell lines8. Despite accumulating evidence for the therapeutic relevance of A. squamosa, a comprehensive comparative evaluation of its different fruit parts (peel, pulp, and seeds) cultivated in Iraq remains lacking. This knowledge gap is particularly important, given the role of environmental factors in shaping the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites. Moreover, the underutilized seeds and peels, often discarded as agricultural waste, may represent a sustainable reservoir of pharmacologically active compounds.

Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the phytochemical composition of A. squamosa extracts (peel, pulp, and seeds) using LC-HRESIMS and to evaluate their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cytotoxic potential through a series of in vitro assays. This work provides insights into its value as a sustainable source of therapeutic candidates, while contributing to fruit waste valorization strategies in drug discovery and nutraceutical development.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

The A. squamosa fruits were collected from Al-Kut’s local market (Al-Kut, Wasit/Iraq) as shown in Fig. 1. The fruits were gently washed with distilled water to remove dust or surface contaminants, then dried and peeled. The fruit pulp was cleared from the seeds by hand, and the pulp and peels were chopped into pieces. The samples were then placed in glass containers for extraction process.

Fig. 1Representative samples of A. squamosa (sugar apple) obtained from the local market of Al-Kut city.

Representative samples of A. squamosa (sugar apple) obtained from the local market of Al-Kut city.

Crude extract preparation

Seeds (MSS) (40 g), peels and pulp (JSW) (700 g) were collected from four fruits and extracted by percolation (cold extraction) using 70% EtOH (3 times x 3 L). The collected extracts were evaporated separately using rotary evaporator at 60 °C until the solvent was completely removed (Fig. 2). The crude extracts were collected and stored in amber vials, then kept in refrigerator to be subjected for further evaluations.

Anti-inflammatory activity

5‑Lipoxygenase inhibitor screening assay

5-Lipoxygenase (5-LOX) is a non-heme iron-based dioxygenase that transforms unsaturated fatty acids to epoxides. 5-Lipoxygenase (5-LOX) Inhibitor Screening Assay was performed using the fluorometric-based BioVision 5-LOX Inhibitor Screening Kit (Catalog #K980-100), designed to detect the inhibition of 5-LOX enzyme activity in vitro. The assay was conducted in a 96-well white plate, with Zileuton utilized as a positive control. Test inhibitors were dissolved in anhydrous DMSO. In each well, 2 µL of test compound (solvent control) was mixed with 38 µL of LOX Assay Buffer. A 40 µL reaction mix—containing LOX Assay Buffer (34 µL), LOX Probe (2 µL), and 5-LOX Enzyme (4 µL)—was then added. The reaction mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 min to allow pre-binding of inhibitors to the enzyme. The LOX Substrate (supplied at 12500X concentration) was diluted first 1:25 in buffer to make a 500X intermediate solution, then 1:100 to prepare a 5X working solution. Following incubation, 20 µL of the 5X LOX Substrate was added to initiate the reaction. Kinetics of the enzymatic activity were monitored by recording the fluorescence increase every 30–60 min. Fluorescence measurements were taken using a microplate reader (Bioline Elisa Reader, Bioline Technologies, India) with excitation/emission wavelengths set to Ex/Em = 500/536 nm. The initial reaction rates (slopes) were used to calculate percent inhibition compared to the enzyme control (no inhibitor). All assays were performed in triplicate, and results were averaged. IC50 values were derived from inhibition curves using multiple concentrations (100 µM to 0.01 µM).

COX-1/COX-2 inhibitor screening assay

The enzyme cyclooxygenase (COX), often referred to as prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase, is in charge of producing the vital biological mediators known as prostanoids, which include thromboxane, prostacyclin, and prostaglandins. COX-1 and COX-2 inhibitor screening assays were conducted using fluorometric assay kits, which were obtained from BioVision (COX-1: K548-100; COX-2: K547-100). Enzyme reaction mixtures were prepared in 96-well white opaque plates. Test inhibitors were dissolved in DMSO and diluted to appropriate concentrations (100 µM to 0.01 µM). Each assay well received a reaction mix containing COX enzyme (1 µL), probe (1 µL), cofactor (2 µL), buffer (76 µL), and test compound. Reactions were initiated by addition of diluted Arachidonic Acid/NaOH solution 10 times by adding 90 µL ddH2O. Fluorescence was recorded over 5–10 min at 25 °C. Kinetically, the slope of ΔRFU/ΔT was used to calculate enzyme activity and % inhibition. Fluorescence measurements were taken using a microplate reader (Tecan Spark, Tecan, Switzerland) with excitation/emission wavelengths set to Ex/Em = 500/536 nm. All assays were performed in triplicate, and results were averaged. IC50 values were derived from inhibition curves using multiple concentrations.

Antioxidant activity

DPPH radical scavenging assay

The 1, 1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay was utilized to assess free radicals scavenging by A. squamosa samples15. To prepare DPPH solution, 0.004 g of DPPH reagent was dissolved in 100 mL of methanol. This solution was then added to various A. squamosa extract concentrations (50, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300, and 350 ug/mL). The absorbance at 517 nm was measured following a 15-minute incubation period at 25 °C. To provide a positive control, Trolox was used. The Trolox calibration curve was used to calculate the concentration of sample needed to scavenge 50% of the Trolox (IC50). ABTS antioxidant assay

OxiSelect™ Trolox Equivalent Antioxidant Capacity (TEAC) Assay Kit measures the total antioxidant capacity of biomolecules from a variety of hydrophilic or lipophilic samples. The TEAC Assay is based on the conversion of oxidized ABTS·+ radical to ABTS via SET or HAT antioxidant mechanisms. Briefly, a volume of 25 µL of the diluted Trolox standards or samples was added to a 96-well microtiter plate (Bioline Elisa Reader, Bioline Technologies, India). The 50X ABTS reagent was diluted at a 1:50 ratio in either 1X Assay Buffer (for hydrophilic samples) or 75% ethanol (for lipophilic samples), and mixed thoroughly using a vortex mixer. Then, 150 µL of the diluted ABTS reagent was added to each well and the contents of the plate were mixed thoroughly. Timing of the reaction was initiated immediately. The plate was incubated for 5 min on an orbital shaker. Absorbance was read immediately at 405–415 nm. Each Trolox standard and sample was assayed in triplicates. A freshly prepared standard curve was used for each assay run16.

Oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC) assay

In this experiment, AAPH (2,2ˊ-azobis (2-amidinopropane) dihydrochloride) produces free radicals which cause fluorescein to lose its fluorescence due to oxidation15. Briefly, all reagents were prepared using phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and Trolox (5 g/mL, final concentration of 20 M) was utilized as a reference. Next, 2.2 mg/mL of AAPH, A. squamosa extracts at 2.5–50 µM concentrations, and fluorescein (16.7 nM) were added to a final amount of 200 µL test solutions in each well of a microplate, and shaken for five seconds. For 110 cycles, fluorescence was monitored at 60-second intervals using excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 and 535 nm respectively. ORAC values were calculated using the area under the curve (AUC) method and expressed in mol Trolox equivalent (TE)/g for all fluorescence investigations carried out at 37 °C. Fluorescein, AAPH, and phosphate buffer (pH 7) were employed as a blank.

Anticancer activities against human liver, colon, and breast carcinoma

The MTT method was used in the evaluation of in vitro cytotoxicity. Cancer cell lines HepG2 (human hepatoma), MCF7 (human breast adenocarcinoma), and Colo-205 (human colorectal adenocarcinoma) cells were utilized for this study. Cultures were removed from the incubator and placed into a laminar flow hood as a sterile workspace. Each vial of MTT [M-5655] used was reconstituted with 3 mL of medium lacking phenol red and serum. The reconstituted MTT was then added at 10% of the culture medium volume. Cultures were returned to the incubator for 2–4 h, depending on the cell type and maximum cell density. After incubation, cultures were removed from the incubator, and the resulting formazan crystals were dissolved by adding a volume of MTT Solubilization Solution [M-8910] equal to the original culture medium volume. Gentle mixing was conducted using a gyratory shaker to enhance the dissolution process. Absorbance was measured spectrophotometrically at 570 nm with background absorbance measured at 690 nm using a microplate reader. Assays were conducted in triplicates, and replicated in three independent studies, with inhibition percentage calculated.

Cell cycle analysis and apoptosis

The stages of the cell cycle and the triggering of apoptosis are crucial in the development and/or treatment of cancer. Thus, the effect of A. squamosa fruit and seed extracts on cell cycle profile and apoptosis was investigated. MCF7 cell line, which was selected due to its high sensitivity to the assessed sample, was used to disclose the apoptotic power and the cell cycle profile of A. squamosa seed and fruit extracts. Annexin-V-FITC and the propidium iodide (PI) staining test method, as outlined by Hassaballah et al. (2024), were utilized to assess apoptosis17.

LC-HRESIMS-assisted chemical profiling

Samples were subjected to LC–ESI–QTOF–MS analysis as formerly described by Abdelmohsen et al. (2014). Firstly, 1 mg/mL of ethyl acetate soluble fraction in MeOH was injected and analyzed using LC-QTOF system (6200 Series TOF/6500 Series Q-TOF, Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with ESI source. Chromatographic separation was performed using HPLC column (an ACE C18, 75 mm × 3.0 mm, 5 µ column (Hichrom Limited, Reading, UK)). The gradient elution was carried out at 300 µl min-1 for 30 min using purified water (A) and acetonitrile (B) with 0.1% formic acid in each mobile phase. The gradient program started with 10% B, increased gradually to 100% B, and continued isocratic for 5 min before linearly decreasing back to 10% B for 1 min. The total analysis period for each fraction was 45 min. The injection volume was 10 µL and the column temperature was maintained at 20 °C. an LC system configured for a 45-minute acquisition run. A total ion chromatogram was acquired in negative and positive ESI modes with a fragmentor voltage of 130 V, covering a mass range up to m/z 3200. The obtained MS data were processed using the data mining software Mzmine 2.10 (Okinawa Institute of Science and Technology Graduate University, Japan) for deconvolution, peak picking, alignment, deisotoping and molecular formula prediction prior to dereplication. Dereplication and metabolites identification for the positive and negative ionization mode datasets were carried out using the DNP database. The structures of the chemical formulas were drawn using the ChemBioDraw Ultra 14.0 software.

Results

Anti-inflammatory activity

5‑Lipoxygenase inhibitor screening assay

The inhibitory activity of the two tested samples, MSS and JSW, was evaluated against 5-Lipoxygenase (5-LOX) using a fluorometric assay, as results are displayed in Figs. 3 and 4. Zileuton, a known 5-LOX inhibitor, was used as a positive control (IC50: 0.38 ± 0.012 ug/mL). MSS showed higher 5-LOX inhibition with IC₅₀ value of 0.991 ± 0.032 ug/mL while JSW displayed strong inhibition, with an IC₅₀ of 4.021 ± 0.13 ug/mL.

COX-1/ COX-2 inhibitor screening study

The inhibitory activity of the two tested samples, MSS and JSW, against COX-1/ COX-2 was evaluated. Results as presented in Fig. 5 revealed that MSS demonstrated selective COX-2 inhibition with an IC₅₀ of 0.341 ± 0.01 µM, compared to 27.8 ± 0.9 µM for COX-1, indicating a strong preference for COX-2; surpassing standard drugs indomethacin (0.527 ± 0.02 µM) and celecoxib (0.961 ± 0.03 µM) in potency. Similarly, JSW showed higher selectivity towards COX-2 (2.061 ± 0.07 µM) over COX-1 (40.66 ± 1.31 µM).

Antioxidant activity

DPPH, ABTS, and ORAC assays

The antioxidant capacities of MSS and JSW were evaluated using three complementary in vitro assays DPPH, ABTS, and ORAC, as results are presented in Table 1; Fig. 6. In the DPPH assay, JSW exhibited a lower IC₅₀ (66.5 ± 2.47 µg/mL) than MSS (71.22 ± 2.65 µg/mL), indicating stronger radical scavenging activity. A similar trend was observed in the ABTS assay, where JSW showed greater potency (116.98 ± 4.35 µg/mL) compared to MSS (154.09 ± 5.73 µg/mL); with standard antioxidant ascorbic acid (DPPH: 27.23 ± 1.01 µg/mL; ABTS: 44.09 ± 1.64 µg/mL). However, in the ORAC assay, which measured the ability to neutralize peroxyl radicals, MSS exhibited a higher antioxidant capacity with a Trolox Equivalent (TE) value of 28.370 ± 0.95 µM/L, compared to JSW, which showed a lower TE value of 20.824 ± 0.69 µM/L. Interestingly, both MSS and JSW demonstrated greater ORAC activity than quercetin, a known potent antioxidant, which had a TE value of 13.349 ± 0.45 µM/L. This suggests that, under the ORAC assay conditions, MSS in particular may possess strong peroxyl radical scavenging potential, outperforming quercetin.

In vitro anticancer activity

The cytotoxic activity of MSS and JSW extracts were evaluated against HepG2, MCF7, and Colo-205 cell lines, as displayed in Fig. 7. MSS exhibited greater potency than JSW across all cell lines, with the lowest IC₅₀ observed in MCF7 cells (7.58 ± 0.24 µg/mL), indicating highest cytotoxicity followed by HepG2 (13.01 ± 0.42 µg/mL) and Colo-205 (29.44 ± 0.95 µg/mL). In contrast, JSW showed higher IC₅₀ values, indicating lower cytotoxicity, particularly against Colo-205 (49.85 ± 1.61 µg/mL).

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Lower IC₅₀ values indicate higher cytotoxic potency. Staurosporine was used as a reference drug.

Apoptosis analysis

Given high sensitivity to the assessed compounds, MCF7 cell line was employed to disclose the apoptotic power and cell-cycle profile of MSS18. The induction of apoptosis in the MCF7 cell line was evaluated using a flow cytometry method that employed propidium iodide (PI) and annexin-V-FITC labelling. As demonstrated in Table 2; Fig. 8, MSS treatment significantly increased apoptosis in MCF7 cells compared to the untreated control. The total apoptotic population reached 27.21%, comprising 5.42% early and 17.58% late apoptotic cells, alongside 4.21% necrosis. In contrast, control MCF7 cells exhibited minimal cell death, with only 2.76% total apoptosis (0.47% early, 0.23% late) and 2.06% necrosis. These results indicate that MSS primarily induces late-stage apoptosis in MCF7 cells.

Cell cycle analysis

Based on the results demonstrated in Table 3; Fig. 8, For the specific cell cycle analysis we used concentrations related to the extract’s half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50). MSS treatment markedly altered the cell cycle distribution in MCF7 cells, inducing a strong G0-G1 phase arrest. The percentage of cells in G0-G1 phase increased to 81.08%, compared to 59.43% in the untreated control. Concurrently, S phase and G2/M populations were significantly reduced (16.49% and 2.43%, respectively), indicating that MSS impedes cell cycle progression beyond G1 phase.

LC-HRESIMS metabolomics analysis

Using both positive and negative ionization modes, LC-HRESIMS analysis led to the putative identification of 18 metabolites, as outlined in Table 4; Fig. 9. The dereplication process employed a chemotaxonomic filter, specifically selecting hits associated with the genus, A. squamosa to ensure taxonomic relevance. The detected metabolites include several chemical classes, including alkaloids, flavonoids, acetogenins, cyclic peptides, and diterpenoids. Notably, acetogenins were the predominant constituents identified in the seed extract.

Discussion

Anti-inflammatory activity

The anti-inflammatory potential of A. squamosa MSS and JSW extracts was evaluated through enzymatic inhibition assays targeting 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX) and cyclooxygenase (COX-1/COX-2). These enzymes play pivotal roles in the biosynthesis of pro-inflammatory mediators, making them crucial pharmacological targets for inflammatory diseases27.

MSS exhibited superior inhibitory activity against 5-LOX, with an IC₅₀ of 0.991 ± 0.032 ug/mL, closely approaching the potency of the reference drug Zileuton (0.38 ± 0.012 ug/mL). In contrast, JSW, though significantly active, displayed a weaker 5-LOX inhibition profile (IC₅₀: 4.021 ± 0.13 ug/mL), suggesting a more moderate effect on leukotriene biosynthesis pathways. These findings indicate a more pronounced lipoxygenase-targeting efficacy in MSS, potentially attributable to its higher content of various secondary metabolites such as cyclic peptides, flavonoids and alkaloids, which have been previously reported to inhibit 5-LOX28. On the other hand, when assessed for cyclooxygenase selectivity, both extracts showed a remarkable preference for COX-2 over COX-1, which is a desirable trait in anti-inflammatory therapeutics29. MSS demonstrated the strongest COX-2 inhibition (IC₅₀: 0.341 ± 0.01 µM), outperforming standard drugs such as indomethacin (0.527 ± 0.02 µM) and celecoxib (0.961 ± 0.03 µM). JSW also displayed notable COX-2 selectivity, albeit with lower potency (IC₅₀: 2.061 ± 0.07 µM vs. 40.66 ± 1.31 µM for COX-1), which may still be pharmacologically relevant. From literature, it was reported that COX-2 gene is highly responsive to inflammatory mediators. For example, interleukin-1 (IL-1), tumor necrosis factor (TNF), and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) can induce COX-2 expression, leading to increased prostaglandin production. Consequently, COX-2-specific inhibitors have been widely used to alleviate symptoms of inflammation, such as those seen in osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and musculoskeletal pain29. Taken together, these results provide compelling evidence that MSS is a potent dual inhibitor of 5-LOX and COX-2, with efficacy surpassing or rivaling standard drugs suggests potential utility as a natural anti-inflammatory agent. The aforementioned results align with previous studies on Annona species, which have often reported anti-inflammatory activity associated with their distinctive secondary metabolites, especially acetogenins, flavonoids, and alkaloids. For instance, present LC/MS analysis identified the cyclic peptide cyclosquamosin D (4); Yang et al. (2008) demonstrated the anti-inflammatory activity of cyclosquamosin D, the predominant cyclic peptide found in the seeds of A. squamosa. The findings suggest that cyclosquamosin D can inhibit the production of TNFR and IL-6 in J774A.1 macrophage cells stimulated with LPS and Pam3Cys22. Additionally, the alkaloids methylarmepavine (16) and isocorydine (18) significantly reduced ulcer formation, suggesting a notable cytoprotective effect; this activity may be mediated through modulation of prostaglandin pathways, further supporting their therapeutic potential in inflammatory conditions28.

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant capacities of A. squamosa (JSW) and (MSS) extracts were comprehensively evaluated using three distinct in vitro assays, DPPH, ABTS, and ORAC to capture a comprehensive profile of radical scavenging potential. These assays, though complementary, differ in radical types and mechanisms of action, providing a multidimensional view of antioxidant behavior30–32. In both the DPPH and ABTS assays, JSW consistently exhibited lower IC₅₀ values compared to MSS (DPPH: 66.5 vs. 71.22 µg/mL; ABTS: 116.98 vs. 154.09 µg/mL, respectively), suggesting that JSW contains compounds effective in single-electron transfer reactions. These results place JSW as a moderately more efficient antioxidant in direct radical quenching under hydrophobic and hydrophilic conditions. However, both extracts were less potent than the standard antioxidant ascorbic acid used as a reference drug. Conversely, the ORAC assay, which reflects antioxidant action through hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) to neutralize peroxyl radicals, revealed a significantly higher TE value for MSS (28.37 µM/L) than JSW (20.82 µM/L). Remarkably, both extracts outperformed quercetin (13.35 µM/L), a benchmark flavonoid antioxidant. This suggests that MSS harbors constituents that are more effective in HAT-based antioxidant defense, a mechanism closely associated with physiological oxidative stress mitigation32. These differential trends may reflect the chemical composition disparities between MSS and JSW. The predominance of acetogenins and specific flavonoids in MSS, which have been reported to possess reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging activity, may explain its superior ORAC activity33. In support of this, Panda and Kar (2007) demonstrated that isoquercetin (quercetin-3-O-glucoside) (15) may have therapeutic potential in the management of diabetes mellitus and tissue lipid peroxidation, likely through its free radical scavenging properties25. On the other hand, the better IC₅₀ values of JSW in DPPH and ABTS assays may reflect compounds with rapid electron-donating capacity. These observations highlight that MSS and JSW exhibit distinct antioxidant profiles depending on the radical type and reaction mechanism. This underscores the value of using multi-assay platforms when evaluating natural antioxidants, as single methods may underrepresent certain mechanistic contributions.

Cytotoxic activity

The cytotoxic potential of A. squamosa MSS and JSW extracts was evaluated against three human cancer cell lines: MCF-7 (breast adenocarcinoma), HepG2 (hepatocellular carcinoma), and Colo-205 (colorectal adenocarcinoma). Both extracts demonstrated dose-dependent cytotoxicity; however, MSS exhibited substantially greater potency across all cell lines, as reflected by its consistently lower IC₅₀ values. The highest cytotoxic activity of MSS was observed against MCF-7 cells, with an IC₅₀ value of 7.579 ± 0.24 µg/mL, followed by moderate activity against HepG-2 cells (IC₅₀ = 13.01 ± 0.42 µg/mL). These values indicate a strong cytotoxic potential, particularly against breast cancer cells.

These findings are in agreement with previous reports. For instance, Shehata et al. investigated two cultivars of A. squamosa (Balady and Hindi) seeds and pulp and demonstrated notable cytotoxic effects across multiple cancer cell lines. Their results showed that the seeds are more effective than the pulp, as the Balady cultivar seed extract exhibited the highest inhibition against HepG-2 (50.14 ± 0.32%), followed by MCF-7 (49.30 ± 0.07%) and Caco-2 (48.81 ± 0.16%). Similarly, the Hindi cultivar seed extract showed the highest activity against MCF-7 (38.99 ± 0.37%), followed by Caco-2 (33.05 ± 0.30%) and HepG-2 (32.67 ± 0.33%)34. In contrast, JSW exhibited weaker cytotoxic responses, indicating that the cytotoxic constituents are more concentrated or more active in the seed extract. To further investigate the mechanism underlying the cytotoxic effect of MSS on MCF-7 cells, flow cytometry was employed to assess apoptosis and cell cycle progression. MSS treatment significantly induced apoptosis, with a total apoptotic population of 27.21%, including 5.42% early and 17.58% late apoptotic cells, as well as 4.21% necrotic cells. In contrast, the untreated control exhibited negligible cell death (2.76% total apoptosis). These findings suggest that MSS exerts its antiproliferative effect primarily through the induction of late-stage apoptosis. Additionally, MSS profoundly altered the cell cycle profile of MCF-7 cells. Flow cytometry revealed a marked G0–G1 phase arrest, with the proportion of cells in G0–G1 increasing to 81.08%, compared to 59.43% in untreated cells, which indicates that MSS interferes with DNA synthesis and cell cycle progression, preventing cells from transitioning beyond the G1 checkpoint35. These combined effects, late apoptosis induction and cell cycle arrest at G0–G1, highlight a multi-targeted cytostatic and cytotoxic action of MSS.

Evidence suggests that cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) plays a central role in tumor development and progression, in contrast to COX-1, which is largely involved in maintaining physiological homeostasis. Overexpression of COX-2 in cancer cells contributes to evasion of apoptosis, enhanced cancer invasiveness, and sustained tumor growth. These tumorigenic effects are primarily driven through three interlinked processes: (1) Induction of angiogenic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), (2) Inhibition of programmed cell death (apoptosis), and (3) Promotion of malignant transformation. Both tumor cells and tumor-associated macrophages are known to produce prostaglandin E₂ (PGE₂) via the COX-2 pathway. This PGE₂, in turn, stimulates VEGF expression, thereby enhancing tumor vascularization and supporting tumor progression. This pro-tumorigenic mechanism is not limited to cancer but is also observed in chronic inflammatory conditions, such as carrageenan-induced inflammation, reinforcing the close link between inflammation, COX-2 activation, and cancer. Therefore, future studies employing in vivo models of inflammation, including carrageenan-induced edema, alongside molecular evaluation of COX-2 and VEGF expression, would be valuable to further validate these proposed mechanisms29,36. Given the pivotal role of COX-2 in supporting tumor survival and angiogenesis, the strong COX-2 inhibitory activity demonstrated by the MSS extract in this study may represent a key mechanism underlying its cytotoxic and anticancer effects, particularly against MCF-7 and HepG2 cell lines. By downregulating COX-2, MSS may reduce PGE₂ synthesis and subsequent VEGF-mediated angiogenesis, thereby disrupting tumor vascular support and restoring apoptotic pathways.

This mechanistic insight is supported by flow cytometry data showing that MSS induces late-stage apoptosis and causes G0–G1 cell cycle arrest in MCF-7 cells. Collectively, these findings suggest that MSS exerts its anticancer effect not only through direct cytotoxicity but also by targeting critical pro-tumorigenic signaling pathways such as COX-2–PGE₂–VEGF, offering a dual anti-inflammatory and anticancer therapeutic potential.

The superior performance of MSS suggests the presence of bioactive compounds with potent antiproliferative properties, possibly acetogenins, which are well-known for mitochondrial complex I inhibition leading to apoptosis23,24,37, and other COX-2 inhibitors. These findings align with the phytochemical profile obtained from LC-HRESIMS, which identified several acetogenins, and alkaloids widely recognized for their cytotoxic and apoptotic activities. This has been further supported by previous phytochemical and pharmacological studies which identified Annonaceous acetogenins (ACGs) as the primary bioactive constituents of A. squamosa seeds. These polyketide-derived metabolites exhibit encouraging anticancer properties, attributed to their potent cytotoxic effects, mitochondrial complex I inhibition, and ability to induce apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, and autophagy in various cancer cell lines21–23,37–40.This selective cytotoxicity highlights MSS as a promising candidate for further investigation as a natural anticancer agent, particularly for breast and liver cancer therapy39,40.

Despite the promising bioactivities observed in this study, it is important to consider the potential toxicity associated with certain phytoconstituents of A. squamosa, particularly annonaceous acetogenins, which are well documented for their potent mitochondrial inhibitory effects, specifically targeting complex I, potentially leading to ATP depletion and associated toxicological effects6,39. The present work was primarily designed as a preliminary evaluation of phytochemical composition and in vitro bioactivities, therefore detailed toxicity assessments were beyond its current scope. In this context, previously published findings have reported a relatively favorable safety profile of Annona extracts on normal cell lines. For instance, Shehata et al. demonstrated that extracts from different Annona species exhibited high EC₁₀₀ values in normal human lung fibroblast (WI-38) cells, suggesting low cytotoxicity toward non-cancerous cells34. While these observations indicate a degree of selectivity, further investigations are required to confirm their safety, particularly in terms of systemic and long-term toxicological effects, to support their potential application in nutraceutical and pharmaceutical development.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that A. squamosa seed extract (MSS) exhibits superior antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cytotoxic properties compared to the fruit extract (JSW), supported by multi-assay profiling and mechanistic insights. MSS showed potent 5-LOX and COX-2 inhibition, surpassing standard drugs in selectivity and efficacy. Antioxidant assays revealed its strong peroxyl radical scavenging capacity in the ORAC assay, while JSW was more effective in DPPH and ABTS assays. Notably, MSS exerted pronounced cytotoxicity against MCF-7 and HepG2 cells, inducing late-stage apoptosis and G0–G1 cell cycle arrest, mechanisms consistent with the presence of acetogenins, alkaloids, cyclic peptides and flavonoids. These findings highlight MSS as a promising source of bioactive compounds for natural anti-inflammatory and anticancer therapeutic development. Overall, MSS represents a rich candidate for drug discovery especially in inflammation- and cancer-associated disorders. Further studies incorporating broader concentration ranges, diverse cancer models, and targeted molecular and toxicological evaluations, including relevant in vivo approaches, are essential to elucidate mechanisms, confirm safety, and support potential therapeutic use.
