Evaluation of root and root canal morphology of mandibular and maxillary canine teeth in Turkish subpopulation by cone beam computed tomography with using two classification systems
Authors: Guldane Magat, Sultan Uzun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKnowledge of root and canal morphology is a prerequisite for effective nonsurgical and surgical endodontic treatments. The aim of this cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) study was to evaluate the root canal morphology of permanent canines in a Turkish subpopulation using two classification systems.MethodsA total of 2,000 canine teeth were examined. The number of roots was identified, and root canal configurations were classified according to Vertucci’s classification and a new system for classifying root and canal morphology. Fisher’s exact test was used for statistical analysis, and the level of significance was set at 0.05 (p = 0.05).ResultsThe canal configuration type I was the most common in all canines, while type III was the second most common. According to the new system, the code 1C1−1 was the most common, whilst 1C1 (A1) codes were the second most common for all canines. In this study, more than 10% morphology that did not fit the Vertucci classification was detected. In the categorization of accessory canals based on the new classification, the most common type in all canines was 1C1,1 (A1). Age had an effect only on the morphology of the left maxillary canine (p < 0.05).ConclusionCanines in the Turkish subpopulation showed a relatively high degree of variation in root and canal anatomical variations. Clinicians should be aware of where canals converge or diverge to avoid missing canals. The new system for classifying canal morphology describes root and canal configurations more accurately and practically compared to the Vertucci classification.

Introduction

The prominence of the canines, considered the “cornerstone” of the dental arches in the labial region, is a cosmetic feature of both maxillary and mandibular canines. They assist in maintaining consistent facial expressions at the corners of the mouth. The shape and position of the canines significantly influence intercuspal placement through “canine guidance” [1]. Proper endodontic and restorative treatment is necessary due to the significance of canine cosmetic and occlusal guidance.

Endodontics’ primary goals include biomechanical cleaning of the pulp cavity and root canal, as well as preparation of this space for receiving filling material and full hermetic closure of the apical and coronal regions [2]. Proper understanding of dental morphology, as well as the ability to accurately interpret it and have enough access to its sources, are essential for an effective treatment of root canals and for determining the treatment outcome [3, 4]. There are several ways for classifying root canal and accessory canal morphology [5, 6]. In the literature, these systems are classified as Weine et al. [7] (classify root canal configurations into four various types with using cross-sectional and radiographic techniques), Vertucci et al. [8] (utilized the cleaning procedure to figure out internal root canal structure and developed a more complicated classification with eight configurations), and Versiani et al. [5] (reported 37 different root canal configurations throughout a single root utilizing micro-CT technology). Ahmed et al. [9, 10] developed a more complete approach for the classification of root, root canal, and accessory canal patterns due to the increasing variety of anatomical variances and obvious inadequacies of existing methods. Further, the system has wider applicability and offers more detailed categorization, allowing for better classification of anatomical variations compared to previous systems [11].

A detailed understanding of root canal morphology and its variations is an absolute requirement for treatment success. Many roots have many canals and different canal configurations. Occasionally, a rupture in Hertwig’s epithelial root sheath occurs during root development, resulting in a small gap. This causes “accessory canals” to develop anywhere in the root, resulting in periodontal-endodontic communication [2]. Studies on root canal morphology and its variations have been widely recorded throughout history [3, 4, 12]. As a result, the final results of root canal treatments are dictated by a thorough understanding of root canal morphology and its aberrations [8]. The morphological study of root canals can be performed in a variety of ways, including staining and tooth clearing, tooth sectioning, conventional radiography, digital radiography, and conventional computed tomography (CT) [13, 14]. The ideal technique is one that is accurate, simple, non-invasive, and capable of in vivo application [13]. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) has proven to be an effective method for investigating root canal anatomy [15, 16]. According to Neelakantan et al. [17], CBCT is as precise as modified canal staining and clearing and is the gold standard in determining root canal anatomy. The key advantages of this technology are the ability to produce three-dimensional images versus conventional radiography, the fact that it is non-invasive, the reduction of superimpositions in intraoral and extraoral anatomies and the adjacent structures, and the use of lower radiation doses and costs in comparison to conventional CT [18–20].

The root canal morphology and accessory canal structures of maxillary and mandibular canines in the Turkish subpopulation vary according to demographic factors such as age and gender. The increasing anatomical complexities and the inadequacies of existing systems necessitate more detailed classification of root, root canal, and accessory canal morphology. Ahmed et al. [10] and Vertucci [6] classification systems may differ in terms of reliability, usability, and accuracy in clinical and educational applications. In this context, data obtained through CBCT are critical for ensuring accurate diagnosis and improving the success rates of endodontic treatments. This study aims to evaluate the root canal morphologies of mandibular and maxillary canines, along with accessory canals, in the Turkish subpopulation using CBCT images, categorized by age and gender. Additionally, it seeks to assess the reliability, usability, and precision of two root canal classification systems for educational and clinical applications. The null hypothesis (H₀) of this study is that the root canal morphology and accessory canal structures of mandibular and maxillary canines do not vary according to age or gender in the Turkish subpopulation. Furthermore, Ahmed et al. [10] and Vertucci [6] classification systems perform similarly in terms of reliability, usability, and accuracy in both clinical and educational settings.

Materials and methods

Data collection

This cross-sectional retrospective study evaluated the root and root canal configurations of 2000 permanent maxillary and mandibular canines, characterized by intact or minimal carious lesions/restorations and complete root development, from 250 females and 250 males aged 16–70 years. High-quality diagnostic 500 CBCT images were utilized for each group (right-left, maxillary-mandibular). Conversely, canine teeth that underwent root canal therapy and/or had post-core placements, as well as those with restorations, crowns, resorptive defects, internal calcifications, and/or fractures were excluded.

The sample size was determined using the G-power 3.1.9.4 software tool to be at least 188 individuals at a 95% confidence level, = 0.05, power (1-) = 0.95 based on the differences between two independent proportions [21].

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) and image processing

CBCT data were gathered using a CBCT machine (J Morita MFG. Corp., 3D Accuitomo 170, Kyoto, Japan) with exposure parameters of 90 kVp and 5 mA for a scanning period of 17.5 s, a voxel resolution of 0.250 mm. Image analysis was conducted using the i-Dixel One Data Viewer imaging program (J Morita MFG. Corp., Kyoto, Japan). To ensure proper visualization and interpretation of the images, contrast and brightness adjustments were made. These adjustments helped enhance the visibility of morphological structures and facilitated manual analysis. The number of roots and the morphological features of the root canals were manually determined using coronal, axial, and sagittal planes. The image processing techniques applied in this study were limited to basic contrast and brightness adjustments, and no advanced image processing algorithms or automatic detection methods were employed. The images were viewed on a 27-inch UltraSharp LED TFT display (Dell Inc., Round Rock, TX, USA) with a resolution of 2560 × 1440 and 3.7 MP.

Calibration

An oral and maxillofacial radiologist with seven years of experience (SU) classified canines’ root canal configurations. The expert received instructions to examine 100 CBCT radiographic images from the axial, sagittal, and coronal views. The anatomy of the root canals was described using the classifications defined by Vertucci et al. [22] (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4; Table 1) and Ahmed et al. [9, 10] (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4; Tables 1 and 2). To assess reproducibility, repeat measurements of the canine root and canal designs were performed twice, two weeks apart. The reference observer was assigned as the other observer (GM) with 14 years of experience and inter-observer reliability was assessed. Intra- and inter-observer reliability were assessed for 100 random images. Cronbach’s alpha analysis revealed high intra- and inter-observer agreement (0.891–0.948).

Root canal analysis

The images were categorized based on the patient’s age (20–30, 31–40, 41–50, and over 51 years) and sexes (female and male). The morphology of the root canals of the permanent maxillary and mandibular canines was assessed individually using the classification techniques developed by Vertucci et al. [8] (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4; Table 2) and Ahmed et al. [9, 10] (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4; Tables 2 and 1).

Statistical analysis

For data entry and statistical analysis, SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) software was used. Frequency, mean, and standard deviation were determined as descriptive statistics. The chi-square test was used to analyze the data, with the significance threshold set at 0.05 (p < 0.05).

Results

The distribution of all evaluated CBCT images according to age groups and gender is shown in Fig. 1. A total of 2000 permanent maxillary and mandibular canines were examined in 500 CBCT images. The mean age of the participants was 36.95 ± 15.02 years. The predominant age group among the female participants was 21–30 years (n = 75), whereas for male participants, it was those aged 51 years and older (n = 68). There was no statistically significant difference in the gender distribution among the age groups (p = 0.284).

Table 3 shows the distribution of the number of roots of permanent canines according to age and gender. Most of the canines had one root. The presence of two-rooted canine teeth was more common in the mandible (2.2% on the right and 2.8% on the left) (Table 3). There was no statistically significant difference in the number of roots based on gender and age groups (p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

Understanding any variations in the shape of canals is essential for endodontic treatment [23]. It helps us foresee potential complications and enables us to successfully remove necrotic material and pulp tissue without endangering the tooth’s or root’s structural integrity [24]. The current study focuses on the root canal morphology of permanent maxillary and mandibular canines to overcome potential problems related to root canal treatment and enhance the clarity of canal morphology classification.

In all, there were not any statistically significant differences between males and females regarding the number of roots in maxillary and mandibular canine teeth, respectively, and combined (p > 0.05). Mashyakhy et al. [25] reported similar findings for both the maxillary and mandibular teeth as a whole. Both studies found that canine teeth often have a single root. A higher prevalence of two-rooted canines was noted in the mandible. We found that two-rooted canines were present in 0.3% of the upper jaw and 2.5% of the lower jaw in our research. Supporting these data, Mashyakhy et al. [25] found two-rooted canines in 2.2% of the maxilla and 5.2% of the mandible. In a study conducted in 2022 in the Saudi population [26], the rate of detection of two roots in mandibular canines was found to be 5%. A review of the literature reveals that the prevalence of two-rooted canine teeth varies between 0% and 15.1% across different ethnic groups [26–31].

In this study, based on the Vertucci classification, the most common root canal patterns in maxillary canines were Type I (right maxilla: 96.4%; left maxilla: 95.6%), followed by Type III (right maxilla: 1.6%; left maxilla: 2.0%) and Type V (right maxilla: 1.4%; left maxilla: 1.6%). Mashyakhy et al. (81.6%) [25], Vertucci (100%) [22], Pineda and Kuttler (100%) [32], Calışkan et al. (93.48%) [33], and Sert and Bayırlı (91% male and 96% female) [3] reported similar findings in the literature, which identified Type I as the most prevalent root canal pattern in maxillary canine teeth. However, while the third most common canal morphology in our study was Type V, Type V canal configuration was not reported in the study by Pineda and Kuttler [32], Sert and Bayırlı [3], and Vertucci [22]. Mashyakhy et al. [25] showed the presence of a Type V canal configuration in the fourth place with a rate of 2%. If we classify these data according to the Ahmed et al. [10] classification, the most frequently observed canal configuration is 1C1 (right maxilla: 80.8%, left maxilla: 41.2%). This is followed by the configuration 1C1–2−1 (right maxilla: 1.6%, left maxilla: 1.8%). The effect of age on dental anatomy has not been extensively studied. One study found that individuals over 65 years of age exhibited a higher proportion of more complex root canal structures compared to younger groups [28]. Similar findings were reported by Karataslioglu and Kalabalik [34] in a Turkish cohort and by Martins et al. [35] who studied age-related changes in root canal configurations using CBCT. However, previous studies in Turkish and Chongqing populations found that younger individuals more commonly had multiple canals compared to older individuals [27, 36]. In the present study, root canal morphologies were not found to vary according to the system of Vertucci et al. [8] and Ahmed et al. [10].

Although there are various differences between the studies on mandibular canine teeth based on the Vertucci classification, the most cet al. [anal morphology was observed as Type I, similar to our study [22, 25, 37]. The second most common canal morphology in our study was Type III. Despite Mashyakhy et al. [25] and Çalışkan et al. [33] having similar results, Vertucci [22] reported the second most common canal type as Type II. While it was not reported by Mashyakhy et al. [25], Type IV canal morphology was observed at 2% in the current study. Further, Pecora et al. [37] reported Tip IV canal morphology as 1.2% in their study. When assessed by the Ahmed et al. classification, the most common canal morphology is 1C1 (right mandibula: 82.0%, left mandibula: 81.6%), followed by 1C1–2−1 (right mandibula: 2.6%, left mandibula: 4.6%), respectively. For all canines, more than 10% morphology that did not fit the classification of Vertucci et al. [22] was detected in this study.

The effect of gender on root number and morphology, this study also found that the number of roots did not differ according to gender, similar to some studies in the literature [25, 26, 31, 38]. In this study, 1C1 morphology was detected at similar and high rates in both males and females. However, there are also studies in the literature indicating that canine teeth with two roots are more dominant in males [39, 40]. In three studies conducted in the same population as this study [3, 41, 42], it was stated that the root morphology of mandibular canine teeth did not vary according to gender. Geduk et al. [41], also conducted in the same population, observed significant gender-related differences in the root canal morphology of mandibular canines, where the root canal morphology exhibited greater diversity in females compared to males, which is not fully consistent with our findings. In line with Karobari et al. [43], females showed an additional 1MI2−1−2−1 classification. These discrepancies in findings may be due to differences in sample size, study methodologies, or genetic factors among the studied populations. Variations in the outcomes of studies may be due to factors like the size of the sample, the methods used, or the genetic characteristics of the participants in the study.

Age has been identified as a significant factor influencing the morphology of canine teeth, particularly in terms of root canal configurations. There are publications in the literature that age significantly affects the morphology of permanent teeth [24]. However, in this study, age was found to have no significant effect on the root morphology of canine teeth evaluated using both classification systems, except for the left maxillary canines. In the left maxillary canines, the “other” type root morphology was statistically more common in individuals aged 21–30 years compared to those aged 31–40 years, classified as more complex in the Ahmed et al. [9] system (p < 0.05). These results in consistent with the studies conducted in Chinese [36], Turkish [27, 44] and Malaysian [31] populations in terms of complexity. This may suggest that younger individuals in this population exhibit a greater degree of root canal complexity, which could be due to ongoing root development or other age-related anatomical changes that gradually simplify with age. The higher prevalence of complex root morphologies in the younger age group aligns with the idea that anatomical variations are more prominent in early adulthood and may decrease as the root system stabilizes over time. Consistent with this study, Almohaimede et al. [28] found also that the number of roots did not vary according to age groups.

The Vertucci classification is a long-standing and user-friendly approach that we utilized in this study to evaluate categorization systems [22]. Nevertheless, the Vertucci categorization has drawbacks. These failures involve not reporting intricate root-canal structures such root number, auxiliary canals, root fusion, and dental anomalies [10, 44]. We found that the Vertucci categorization is insufficient for categorizing root-canal configurations within the “other” category. The term “other” in this study refers to accessory canals and complex morphology. The Ahmed et al. et al. [9] classification contributes to the reporting of the localization and number of accessory canals observed under the heading “other” to give knowledge before treatment. With the only coding system used by Ahmed et al. et al.‘s classification, it can identify root and canal structures, dental anomalies, and anatomical variations [9, 10]. Although this classification system is quite explicit and accurate in defining complex morphologies, it has the limitation of creating a greater number of unique codes and categories compared to previous classifications, which makes it complex [44, 45].

This study, while providing valuable insights into the root canal morphologies of the Turkish community, is limited by its single-center design, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. A multi-centric approach with a broader sample size would be more appropriate to capture a more comprehensive representation of the variations in root canal morphology across different regions and demographics within the Turkish population. Additionally, the use of CBCT for assessing intricate structures like root canal morphology presents limitations, particularly in terms of spatial resolution when compared to advanced imaging techniques such as micro-CT and nano-CT. This reduced spatial resolution could potentially influence the accuracy of the morphological assessments and, consequently, the study’s outcomes. Furthermore, while the new classification method proposed by Ahmed et al. is pivotal for enhancing the understanding of root canal morphology, its integration into undergraduate and graduate dental education remains a challenge. The lack of widespread adoption of this novel system may hinder the comprehension of complex subjects related to root and canal morphology, which could lead to increased postoperative complications in clinical settings. These limitations underscore the need for further research employing diverse methodologies and educational frameworks to improve the understanding and management of root canal systems in dental practice.

Conclusion

The study found a high rate of complicated crown morphologies in canine teeth, particularly in the 21–30 age group. Given the canines’ position in the dental arch, successful root canal treatments are crucial for occlusal guidance and aesthetics. Therefore, understanding complex root canal structures is essential prior to treatment. For clinicians seeking clarity on accessory canals, dental anomalies, and complex canal structures, the new classification system by Ahmed’s classification is superior to Vertucci’s classification.
