Seizure Freedom Despite Piriform Cortex Sparing: Structural Connectivity Remodeling After SEEG‐Guided Mesial Temporal RFTC
Authors: Stéphane Jean, Paul Edisie Donkor, Xiaoqiang Wei, Yihai Dai, Jifeng Gong, Qingfang Xiao, Weitao Chen, Weihong Liu, Xinrong Fang, Shiwei Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo investigate whether selective radiofrequency thermocoagulation (RFTC) targeting mesiotemporal structures, while sparing the piriform cortex (PC), can achieve seizure freedom in drug‐resistant mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE), and to assess whether said seizure freedom is associated with an indirect disconnection of the PC within the epileptogenic network.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on 32 patients (mean age 27.81 ± 13.37 years) with drug‐resistant MTLE who underwent stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG)‐guided RFTC. Volumetric and structural connectivity (SC) analyses were conducted using pre‐ and postoperative T1‐weighted (T1W) and diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI). The regions of interest (ROIs) targeted included the hippocampus, amygdala, rhinal cortex, and parahippocampal cortex, while the PC was spared. Network metrics assessed pre‐ and postoperative SC changes.ResultsAt a mean follow‐up of 40.63 ± 16.36 months, 25 patients (78.1%) became seizure free (SF). SF patients had significantly greater ablation volumes in the amygdala (p = 0.026) and rhinal cortex (p = 0.023) compared to non‐seizure‐free (NSF) patients. SF patients showed significant postoperative reductions in SC across all mesiotemporal ROIs, including the spared PC (p ≤ 0.020). NSF patients exhibited no significant SC changes.ConclusionsSeizure freedom following SEEG‐guided RFTC is associated with widespread disruption of SC in mesiotemporal networks, including the spared PC. These findings support the hypothesis that seizure freedom following the ablation of key mesiotemporal ROIs can induce an indirect disconnection of the PC through its connected nodes, offering a safer therapeutic option for MTLE.Keywords: DWI, MTLE, piriform cortex, SEEG‐guided RFTC, structural connectivity

Introduction

Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) is among the most prevalent and pharmacoresistant forms of epilepsy [1, 2, 3]. Despite the growing adoption of minimally invasive techniques such as laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT) and stereo‐electroencephalography (SEEG)‐guided radiofrequency thermocoagulation (RFTC), conventional resective surgery remains the benchmark for achieving durable seizure freedom [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. In mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE), traditional anterior temporal lobectomy typically yields seizure‐free rates of approximately 60%–80% [10]. Recent evidence suggests that SEEG‐guided RFTC may provide comparable seizure outcomes, ranging from 50% to 70%, while offering substantial advantages in the preservation of cognitive and visual functions, particularly in procedures involving the dominant hemisphere [11]. These developments highlight the need to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the differential outcomes of SEEG‐guided interventions to further optimize therapeutic efficacy.

Among the key structures implicated in MTLE, the piriform cortex (PC) has emerged as a pivotal hub in seizure initiation and propagation [3, 4, 5, 6]. Situated at the intersection of mesiotemporal and orbitofrontal networks, the PC maintains extensive reciprocal connections with major epileptogenic regions, including the hippocampus, amygdala, and parahippocampal cortices [10, 11]. Converging evidence from functional neuroimaging and electrophysiological studies demonstrates aberrant PC activation during interictal discharges, even when anatomically distant from the primary seizure‐onset zone (SOZ) [12]. Furthermore, inclusion of the PC within resection or ablation fields has been consistently associated with superior postoperative seizure control, underscoring its critical role within the broader epileptogenic network [4, 5, 6].

However, direct ablation of the PC via RFTC poses considerable technical challenges and procedural risks due to its proximity to major cerebral vessels and its oblique rostrocaudal orientation, which complicate both electrode implantation and thermocoagulation. To mitigate these risks, the present study employed RFTC limited to adjacent mesiotemporal structures—namely, the amygdala, hippocampus, rhinal (entorhinal and perirhinal cortex combined), and parahippocampal cortices—while deliberately sparing the PC. Notably, seizure freedom was achieved in some patients despite the absence of direct PC ablation, suggesting that therapeutic efficacy may be associated with some structural connectivity alterations within the ablated mesiotemporal structures, which may have extended to the spared PC.

This interpretation is consistent with recent findings indicating that seizure‐free patients exhibit greater postoperative reductions in structural connectivity than those with persistent seizures, supporting a network‐level mechanism of seizure suppression [13, 14, 15, 16]. Collectively, these insights reinforce the notion that seizure control may depend more on structural disconnection of epileptogenic hubs than on their complete anatomical removal.

Building on this framework, this study investigated following selective RFTC targeting mesiotemporal structures, while sparing the PC, whether seizure freedom was associated with indirect PC's disconnection within the epileptogenic network. To our knowledge, this represents the first study specifically designed to examine this mechanism. We hypothesize that selective thermocoagulation of mesiotemporal regions induces sufficient network reorganization to structurally isolate the PC, thereby interrupting epileptogenic initiation or propagation without necessitating its direct ablation. Confirmation of this mechanism would establish mesiotemporal RFTC as a safer yet equally effective strategy for indirect PC disconnection, minimizing surgical risk while maximizing seizure control in patients with MTLE.

Methodology

Patient Selection

A retrospective cohort study was conducted on MTLE patients meeting the drug‐resistant definition of the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) [17]. This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital (Approval No. 2025KY540). Written informed consent was obtained from all adult participants and from the parents or legal guardians of minors.

Patients who underwent SEEG‐guided RFTC between October 2019 and October 2024 were consecutively included. Eligibility criteria were as follows: (1) failure to achieve seizure control with at least two adequately chosen antiseizure medications (ASMs); (2) suspicion of unilateral MTLE based on VEEG demonstrating ictal temporal lobe epileptiform discharges; (3) seizure semiology consistent with focal MTLE; (4) confirmation of unilateral onset based on SEEG‐defined ictal and interictal abnormalities; and (5) ≥ 1 year of postoperative follow‐up. Patients with any prior neurosurgical intervention were excluded.

Of 42 screened individuals, 32 (17 males and 15 females) met all criteria and were included in the final analysis. Ten were excluded due to insufficient follow‐up (n = 4), missing postoperative MRI (n = 4), or absence of preoperative DWI (n = 2). All the patients underwent comprehensive preoperative noninvasive evaluations for epileptogenic zone (EZ) localization, including seizure semiology, scalp VEEG, neuropsychological testing, positron emission tomography (PET), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). An additional MRI scan was obtained post‐procedure to assess structural changes.

MRI Acquisition

MRI data were acquired on a 3T MAGNETOM Prisma scanner (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) using a 64‐channel head coil. Structural imaging included sagittal T1‐weighted MPRAGE (voxel size = 0.94 × 0.94 × 0.9 mm3), axial T2‐weighted fast spin‐echo (FSE), and axial FLAIR sequences.

DWI was performed using a grid‐sampling scheme with 128 q‐space samples across 21 b‐values (200–3000 s/mm2), with 3–20 diffusion directions per shell. Additional parameters were: TR = 2400 ms, TE = 87 ms, slice acceleration factor = 3, GRAPPA = 2, FOV = 230 × 230 mm2, voxel size = 2.5 mm isotropic, and acquisition time = 5 min 27 s. A bipolar diffusion pulse sequence minimized eddy‐current artifacts.

Electrode Implantation and SEEG Recording

All patients underwent SEEG implantation targeting mesial temporal structures: hippocampus, amygdala, rhinal cortex (entorhinal and perirhinal cortex combined), and parahippocampal cortex. A dense electrode configuration ensured comprehensive coverage of the ROIs, enabling optimal SOZ localization and maximizing potential RFTC coverage.

Trajectory planning was performed using the Sinovation SinoPlan robotic system mounted on a Mayfield or Dora 3‐pin frame. Electrodes (0.8 mm diameter; 8–16 contacts; 2 mm contact length; 3.5 mm intercontact distance; 1.5 mm insulation) were inserted to optimize sampling of targeted structures while avoiding major vasculature. Postoperative CT scans co‐registered to preoperative MRI verified placement accuracy and excluded hemorrhagic complications.

Chronic SEEG monitoring was conducted over 1–4 weeks, with ASMs reduced to facilitate seizure capture. Recordings were obtained using a 256‐channel Neuvo Amplifier (Neuroscan Compumedics, Australia) at 10000 Hz with a 0–2500 Hz bandpass. Bipolar signals were obtained between adjacent contacts. Ictal events were identified by low‐voltage fast activity or periodic rapid discharge of spikes, and interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs) including spikes, spike‐and‐wave complexes, polyspikes, or polyspike‐and‐wave complexes were annotated. Electrical stimulation mapping was performed after 2–3 spontaneous seizures; electrically elicited seizures replicating habitual semiology were deemed localizing.

Preoperative Image Processing and Parcellation

Preoperative T1‐weighted images were processed using FreeSurfer version 7.4.1 (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) following the standard recon‐all pipeline for skull stripping, segmentation, and cortical parcellation. Each output was visually inspected, with manual corrections performed when necessary. Pre and postoperative MRI were co‐registered using SPM12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/) to ensure accurate alignment for volumetric and connectivity analyses.

Manual Segmentation of Post‐RFTC Cavities

Postoperative T1‐weighted images were manually segmented by two independent raters in 3D Slicer version 5.8.1 (http://www.slicer.org). Ablation cavities were delineated slice‐by‐slice across all orthogonal planes to ensure accurate 3D reconstruction (Figure 1). Segmentation was performed independently by two raters, S.J and P.E.D., with a third rater, S.S., resolving any disagreements. Inter‐rater reliability was assessed using the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) in 3D Slicer, utilizing the Segment Comparison module. A Dice coefficient of at least 0.8 between raters was considered acceptable for agreement.

Choice of ROIs and RFTC Volumetric Analysis

Four ROIs, the hippocampus, amygdala, rhinal cortex, and parahippocampal cortex, were selected based on the side of ablation. For each ROI, the ablated volume was quantified by intersecting the preoperative FreeSurfer parcellation with the manually segmented RFTC cavity. The Logical operators in 3D Slicer was used to subtract the ablated region from the preoperative structure, yielding postoperative residual volumes (Figure 1).

Manual Segmentation of Piriform Cortex

The piriform cortex was manually segmented in 3D Slicer (Figure 1) independently by two raters, S.J. and P.E.D., with a third rater, S.S., resolving any disagreements (if DSC was < 0.8 between the two raters), following a previously validated protocol based on anatomical landmarks and histological correlation [6, 18]. A methodology summary is provided below.

MRI scans were reoriented so that coronal slices were perpendicular to the long axis of the hippocampus, with any residual tilt corrected. The frontal portion of the piriform cortex was defined as 50% of the distance between the entorhinal sulcus fundus (laterally) and the olfactory sulcus (medially), visible on anterior slices and tapering posteriorly. The gray–white matter interface above the upper bank of the entorhinal sulcus served as a reference for cortical thickness. For the temporal portion, the medio‐lateral extension corresponded to 30%, 50%, 75%, and 100% of the distance between the entorhinal sulcus and gyrus semilunaris apex on the first four coronal slices, respectively. From the fourth slice posteriorly, superior–inferior limits were bounded by the temporal white matter and the amygdala or sulcus semiannularis, maintaining a constant 2 mm thickness. The limen insulae defined the anterior boundary, while the posterior limit was the penultimate slice showing full cerebral peduncle connection and a visible interpeduncular cistern.

Structural Connectivity Workflow

Structural connectivity (SC) analysis was performed using each patient's pre‐ and postoperative DWI data. Connectivity matrices and graph‐theoretical measures were computed in DSI Studio (February 8th 2023 build) (https://dsi‐studio.labsolver.org/doc/gui_t3_whole_brain.html) (Figure 1).

Diffusion data were reconstructed using generalized q‐sampling imaging (GQI) and registered to T1‐weighted space for tractography. Whole‐brain deterministic fiber tracking was conducted with the following parameters: (1) a total of 1,000,000 tracts were seeded per brain, and the step size for tract propagation was set to 0; (2) the angular threshold was set to 0 degrees, allowing for randomization in tract direction, which enhances reproducibility and minimizes directional bias across the brain; (3) tracks shorter than 30 mm or longer than 200 mm were excluded based on predefined length constraints. Fiber tracking was terminated once these length thresholds were met. To improve tract quality, topology‐informed pruning (TIP) with a value of 2 was applied, removing poorly defined tracks. The default thresholding settings were used, with a diffusion sampling length ratio (DSLR) of 1.25 for the reconstruction of orientation density functions (ODFs).

For our connectivity analysis, we generated the connectivity matrix using the streamline count as the matrix value, representing the number of tracts connecting two ROIs. The pass region option was selected, counting all streamlines passing through each region, regardless of their start or end points. A threshold of 0.001 was applied to filter out matrix entries with a small number of connecting tracks, expressed as a ratio to the maximum number of tracks in the matrix.

The residual ROIs, in addition to the non‐ablated ROIs and the manually segmented piriform cortex, were exported as individual NIfTI (Neuroimaging Informatics Technology Initiative) files for each ROI in native space, using each patient's respective T1 data. These files were then imported into DSI Studio and were processed with their respective postoperative T1 and DWI. The preoperative non‐ablated ROIs, derived from FreeSurfer parcellation in addition to the piriform cortex, were processed similarly using their respective preoperative T1 and DWI data.

Both preoperative and postoperative ROIs (residual structures) were included in the complete analysis. Weighted network metrics were used, as they provide a more detailed representation of connectivity compared with unweighted (binary) measures [19, 20]. Global metrics included: (1) average clustering coefficient, (2) characteristic path length, (3) small‐worldness, and (4) global efficiency; and local metrics included (1) clustering coefficient and (2) local efficiency.

RFTC Procedure

RFTC was performed under awake conditions using a radiofrequency generator (R2000B‐M1, BNS, Beijing). Lesions were created with 7.5 W for 40 s between adjacent electrode contacts demonstrating ictal onset, interictal discharges, or electrically elicited seizures. To reduce the risk of visual field deficits, only contacts placed at least 3.5 mm from the optic radiation were ablated [21]. All electrodes were removed 1–2 days post‐RFTC under general anesthesia.

Post‐RFTC Outcome and Data Analysis

Clinical outcomes were evaluated at 1‐, 3‐, and 6‐month post‐procedure, and then annually. At the 1‐year follow‐up, patients were classified as seizure‐free (SF; Engel Class IA) or non‐seizure‐free (NSF; Engel≠IA). No discontinuation of ASM occurred following RFTC.

Statistical Analysis

Ablation volumes were quantified in 3D Slicer, and statistical analyses were performed in SPSS version 27. Significance was set at two‐tailed p < 0.05 with 95% Hodges‐Lehmann confidence interval (CI). Between‐group comparisons of ablation volume and clinical outcomes were assessed with the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher's exact test. Hemispheric asymmetry and pre‐ to postoperative SC changes were evaluated using Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests.

Results

Patients Demographics and Outcomes

Thirty‐two MTLE patients (17 males and 15 females) were included, with a mean age of 27.81 ± 13.37 years (range: 6–57). Seizure onset was right‐sided in 17 patients (53.12%) and left‐sided in 15 (46.87%). MRI abnormalities revealed hippocampal sclerosis (HS) in 12 patients (37.50%), while 20 (62.50%) were MRI‐negative. The mean clinical follow‐up duration was 40.63 ± 16.36 months. No cases of hemorrhage, infection, or visual field deficits were observed in the cohort following the procedure. A total of 8 patients (25%) reported slight memory decline postoperatively.

At final follow‐up, 25 patients (78.12%) were SF, whereas 7 (21.88%) remained NSF (6 Engel IIA, 1 IVA). All instances of seizure recurrence were concentrated within the first year post‐RFTC. No seizure occurrences were observed following RFTC up to the last follow‐up in the SF patients.

Among SF patients, 13 (52%) underwent right‐sided ablation and 12 (48%) left‐sided; in the NSF group, 4 (57.14%) had right‐sided and 3 (42.85%) left‐sided ablations, with no significant laterality difference (p > 0.05). HS was identified in 8 SF (32%) and 4 NSF patients (57.14%), but the association between MRI abnormality and outcome was not significant (p > 0.05). Sex distribution differed numerically (more females in SF; more males in NSF), but not statistically (p > 0.05). Mean age did not differ between SF (27.08 ± 12.18 years) and NSF groups (30.42 ± 17.91 years; p = 0.523). Follow‐up duration was significantly shorter in the SF group (35.91 ± 16.60 months) compared with NSF (52.85 ± 10.54 months; p = 0.020, CI [3, 32]) (Table 1).

Ablation Volumes Relative to Outcomes

Ablation volumes within the hippocampus did not differ significantly between SF and NSF groups (p = 0.324). In contrast, SF patients had significantly larger ablation volumes in the amygdala (24.85 ± 10.94 vs. 12.75 ± 12.84), p = 0.026, CI [−22.73, −1.466] and rhinal cortex (10.24 ± 8.50 vs. 1.88 ± 2.66), p = 0.023, CI [−15.67, −0.37]. Parahippocampal ablation was slightly greater in SF patients but not statistically significant (p = 0.688). No patient underwent piriform cortex ablation. Overall, these findings indicate that more extensive amygdalar and rhinal cortex ablation was associated with seizure freedom, whereas hippocampal and parahippocampal ablation volumes did not significantly influence outcome. Preoperative ROIs Hemispheric Asymmetry

Preoperative analysis of mesiotemporal structures volumes revealed several hemispheric asymmetries. The right hippocampus was slightly larger than the left, though not significantly (p = 0.278). The right amygdala was significantly larger than the left (1911.65 ± 246.71 compared to 1771.80 ± 224.99), CI [41.5, 222.92], p = 0.003. No significant asymmetry was found in the rhinal cortex (p = 0.304). The parahippocampal cortex exhibited significant leftward asymmetry (2005.13 ± 331.36 vs. 1857.86 ± 298.87), CI [−255.495, −43.9], p = 0.009. The piriform cortex showed pronounced rightward asymmetry (428.72 ± 55.42 vs. 376.91 ± 55.97), CI [37.87, 70.005], p < 0.001 (Table 2).

Between Groups Structural Connectivity Differences

Pre‐ and postoperative global SC metrics did not differ significantly between SF and NSF patients. Preoperatively, there were no between‐group differences in clustering coefficient (p = 0.346), characteristic path length (p = 0.123), small‐worldness (p = 0.207), or global efficiency (p = 0.532) (Figure 2A). Postoperatively, group differences remained non‐significant for clustering coefficient (p = 0.242), characteristic path length (p = 0.789), small‐worldness (p = 0.420), and global efficiency (p = 0.624) (Figure 2B). Thus, global graph‐theoretical metrics did not distinguish patients based on seizure outcome.

Within Group Structural Connectivity Alterations Following RFTC

The SF group exhibited marked postoperative changes in global network topology (Figure 3A). Clustering coefficient decreased significantly (from 0.09 ± 0.02 to 0.07 ± 0.02) (p = 0.001), CI [−0.04, −0.015], reflecting reduced local interconnectedness. Characteristic path length increased (p = 0.001), indicating a shift toward diminished global integration. Global efficiency declined significantly (0.28 ± 0.04 to 0.02 ± 0.06) (p < 0.001), CI [−0.10, −0.04], suggesting reduced parallel information transfer. Small‐worldness also decreased (0.018 ± 0.007 to 0.01 ± 0.007) (p = 0.001), CI [−0.012, −0.005], signifying a disruption of the balance between segregation and integration. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that seizure freedom was associated with substantial reorganization of global SC, characterized by reduced clustering and efficiency and increased path lengths.

In contrast, the NSF group showed no significant change in clustering coefficient, path length, global efficiency, or small‐worldness (all p > 0.05; Figure 3B), indicating preservation of preoperative network architecture. The absence of measurable network disruption in NSF patients suggests insufficient interruption of pathologic connectivity.

In contrast, the NSF group demonstrated no significant postoperative alterations in either graph metric (Figure 4C,D). Across hippocampal and amygdalar regions, changes were uniformly nonsignificant (left hippocampus: clustering coefficient p = 0.499, local efficiency p = 0.398; right hippocampus: p = 0.310 and p = 0.398; left amygdala: p = 1.000 and p = 0.866; right amygdala: p = 0.499 and p = 0.735). Similarly, the rhinal and parahippocampal cortices remained stable, with only marginal, non‐significant trends toward reduction in the right rhinal (both p = 0.063). The piriform cortex also showed no meaningful postoperative change (left: clustering coefficient p = 0.866, local efficiency p = 0.735; right: p = 0.063 and p = 0.128).

Collectively, these findings reveal pronounced and widespread postoperative disruption of local network integration and clustering in the SF group, particularly within the hippocampus, amygdala, rhinal, and parahippocampal cortices bilaterally, with significant changes extending to the unablated piriform cortex. In contrast, the NSF group maintained preserved local connectivity topology.

Discussion

This study evaluated whether SF following SEEG‐guided RFTC in drug‐resistant MTLE patients may also be associated with indirect disruption of the PC network as opposed to its reported direct ablation and resection. By comparing ablation extent and postoperative changes in SC between SF and NSF patients, we found that greater ablation of the amygdala and rhinal cortex, regions tightly interconnected with the PC, was strongly associated with seizure freedom, whereas hippocampal and parahippocampal ablation did not differ between groups. Furthermore, only SF patients exhibited significant postoperative alterations in SC involving multiple mesiotemporal ROIs, including the PC itself. These findings support the hypothesis that targeted ablation of mesiotemporal structures can indirectly disconnect the PC and can contribute to seizure control without direct PC ablation.

Prior studies have shown that seizure outcomes in MTLE depend heavily on the degree of mesiotemporal tissue removal [22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. Sagher et al. [24] reported that resections encompassing ≥ 90% of the amygdala, hippocampus, and entorhinal cortex achieved SF rates of 86%–90% across different surgical techniques. Keller et al. [25] similarly demonstrated that larger resections of the amygdala and entorhinal cortex were associated with better outcomes, while Gleichgerrcht et al. [26] found that optimal seizure control typically followed combined anterior resections of the hippocampus, amygdala, piriform, entorhinal, and perirhinal cortices. Because these structures were usually removed together, determining the independent contribution of each region has remained challenging. Our results are in line with these findings in showing that greater mesiotemporal ablation, particularly within the amygdala and rhinal cortex, is associated with SF even when using the more spatially selective approach of RFTC. Whether isolated ablation of these regions is sufficient, or whether seizure freedom requires the cumulative ablation of multiple mesiotemporal nodes, remains an open question.

Volumetric analyses in our cohort revealed consistent preoperative asymmetry of the PC, with larger right‐sided volumes, paralleling findings by Iqbal et al. [27]. A similar rightward asymmetry was detected in the amygdala. However, without a healthy control group or longitudinal preoperative imaging, it was not possible to determine whether these asymmetries represent pathological hypertrophy or normative anatomical variation. Prior studies of amygdala morphology in TLE have largely focused on volumetric enlargement, rather than asymmetry, making direct comparisons challenging [28, 29, 30, 31]. For this reason, we interpreted these findings conservatively as volumetric asymmetries rather than evidence of disease‐related enlargement.

A large body of evidence identifies the PC as a critical structure whose resection is strongly predictive of seizure freedom. Galovic et al. [6] showed that removal of ≥ 50% of the PC was necessary for seizure control, while Borger et al. [4] demonstrated that favorable outcomes following selective amygdalo‐hippocampectomy were associated with significantly greater PC resection (51% vs. 13%). Similarly, Kim et al. [5] reported that higher percentages of PC ablation during LITT correlated with complete seizure freedom, and Hwang et al. [32] demonstrated that PC ablation percentage independently predicted seizure freedom at both 6 months and 1 year. In contrast to these studies, the PC was intentionally spared in our cohort. Yet SF patients nonetheless exhibited pronounced postoperative reductions in SC of the PC, suggesting that its indirect disconnection may replicate the therapeutic benefits traditionally attributed to its direct ablation or resection.

These observations align with prior network‐based investigations showing that postoperative reorganization of structural connectivity is a key determinant of surgical outcome [13, 14, 15, 16, 33]. Bernhardt et al. [13] found that increased clustering coefficient and path length were associated with poorer outcomes. Bonilha et al. [14] identified pathological increases in medial temporal and insular connectivity in MTLE, while Taylor et al. [33] demonstrated, using connectomic modeling, that SF patients exhibit greater predicted reductions in overall connectivity. Ko et al. [15] extended these findings to LITT, reporting that greater disruption of mesial, anterior, and inferior temporal connections predicted favorable outcomes. While their studies inferred postoperative network alterations using preoperative diffusion imaging and postoperative structural scans, our study directly measured pre‐to‐postoperative SC changes using diffusion imaging at both time points. Moreover, the use of DSI tractography, shown to reduce false‐positive fiber reconstructions relative to conventional DTI [34], provides a more reliable estimate of fiber‐level architectural changes. Consistent with prior work, we observed significant SC reductions only in SF patients, indicating that seizure freedom may require surpassing a threshold of network disruption that was not achieved in the NSF group.

The PC plays a central integrative role within the limbic network. It receives dense projections from the amygdala and rhinal cortices, and its highly convergent excitatory architecture facilitates rapid synchronization and propagation of epileptiform activity across mesiotemporal structures. In SF patients, more extensive ablation of the amygdala and rhinal cortex likely severed these crucial afferent and efferent projections, thereby diminishing the PC's capacity to act as a synchronization hub. By contrast, the smaller ablation volumes in NSF patients may have preserved key PC pathways, enabling continued intra‐limbic and interhemispheric propagation.

Taken together, these findings reinforce the pivotal role of the PC in seizure initiation and propagation [3, 4, 5, 6, 32]. Although direct PC ablation remains technically challenging and carries procedural risk, our study demonstrates that seizure control can be achieved through selective ablation of adjacent mesiotemporal structures which in turn indirectly disrupt PC connectivity. The observed connectivity reductions may be related to a better SOZ coverage in the SF group, which in turn resulted in larger cumulative RFTC volumes. The improved SOZ coverage likely led to a more effective disruption of epileptogenic network connections, extending beyond the mesiotemporal ROIs to the piriform cortex. This suggests that the therapeutic effects of mesiotemporal surgery arise not solely from resecting or ablating focal epileptogenic tissue, but from strategically disrupting critical nodes and pathways within the broader epileptogenic network. Consequently, targeted thermocoagulation of mesiotemporal regions that constitute the primary input and output pathways of the PC may represent a safer yet comparably effective alternative to direct PC ablation in the surgical treatment of MTLE.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, its retrospective design introduces potential bias. Second, the sample size is relatively small, limiting statistical power. Third, manual segmentation of the piriform cortex, while necessary for precise sparing, is operator‐dependent and prone to variability, which could affect connectivity metrics despite efforts to standardize the process. Fourth, structural connectivity analyses relied on diffusion‐weighted imaging, which provides indirect measures of white matter tracts and may not fully capture dynamics or synaptic changes underlying epileptogenesis. Finally, the study focused exclusively on MTLE, limiting generalizability to broader epilepsy types. Prospective, multicenter studies with larger cohorts are needed to address these issues.

Conclusions

Clinically, these findings support SEEG‐guided RFTC as a viable, minimally invasive option in refractory MTLE patients. By leveraging the network‐level effects of targeted mesiotemporal ablation, seizure suppression can be achieved without the elevated risks associated with direct PC intervention. Future studies integrating advanced tractography and longitudinal analysis are warranted to further delineate the specific pathways responsible for indirect PC disconnection and to optimize individualized therapeutic targeting.
