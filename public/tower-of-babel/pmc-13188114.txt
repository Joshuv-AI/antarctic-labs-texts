Regulation of water and oil absorption of whey protein aerogels by gel parameter control: Exploring structure-function relationship
Authors: Lu-yao Pan, Zhen-shan Zhang, Ying Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Whey protein aerogels were prepared via supercritical CO₂ drying to investigate the effects of gelation conditions on their water/oil absorption behavior, and the structure–function relationship. Gelation temperature, pH and protein concentration all significantly affected the absorption capacities (p < 0.05), with pH being the most influential factor. Accordingly, hydrophilic (HPAG) and oleophilic (OPAG) aerogels with tailored adsorption selectivity were constructed. Their overall preparation conditions were similar, whereas OPAG required lower gelation pH and temperature. Their adsorption kinetics fitted well to exponential models: biexponential for water absorption and monoexponential for oil absorption. Compared with OPAG, HPAG exhibited a more open porous structure with larger specific surface area (160.17 vs. 78.50 m2/g), pore volume (0.78 vs. 0.32 cm3/g), and average pore diameter (18.96 vs. 15.61 nm), as well as higher protein denaturation extent. These results demonstrate that gelation conditions regulate adsorption performance by synergistically modulating protein conformation and pore structure.

Introduction

Aerogels are a type of low-density solid material with a high specific surface area that retain a three-dimensional porous network after removing solvents from gel matrices (Alavi & Ciftci, 2023). With their high porosity, large specific surface area, and excellent adsorption capacity, aerogels hold broad application prospects in food preservation, active ingredient delivery, and controlled lipid release (Abdullaha et al., 2023; Cui et al., 2025). However, traditional inorganic aerogels (e.g., silica aerogels) have poor biocompatibility and are difficult to apply in food systems, whereas bio-based aerogels prepared from natural polymers have attracted increasing attention due to their good biocompatibility, degradability, and safety (Abdullaha et al., 2023; Morales-Herrejón et al., 2025).

As a by-product of cheese and casein processing, whey protein is abundant, low-cost, and renewable, with excellent gelling properties, making it an ideal raw material for the preparation of food-grade aerogels (Kleemann et al., 2018). The molecular structure of whey protein contains both hydrophilic and hydrophobic groups, which can form stable three-dimensional network under thermal induction, providing a structural basis for the preparation of functional aerogels (Plazzotta et al., 2020). Compared with other bio-based aerogels (e.g., polysaccharide-based aerogels), whey protein aerogels exhibit significant advantages in adsorption capacity, structural adjustability, and functional controllability, making them a research focus in the field of food functional materials (Kleemann et al., 2018; Kurd et al., 2025).

Currently, extensive research has been conducted on whey protein aerogels as adsorbents and carriers for active substance encapsulation. For instance, Zhou et al. (2024) reported that whey protein aerogels exhibited high water absorption capacity and rapid uptake, enabling effective retention of meat exudates, highlighting their potential in water-rich food systems. Kleemann et al. (2020) further confirmed that whey protein aerogels could load fish oil up to three times their own weight and achieve controlled release during in simulated digestion, demonstrating their potential for lipophilic active substance delivery. Moreover, Plazzotta et al. (2020) showed that whey protein aerogels dried via supercritical CO₂ formed strong, plastic oleogels with rheological behavior comparable to conventional fats, further expanding their application in lipid-based food systems. Existing research has confirmed their excellent amphiphilicity, allowing the loading of both hydrophilic and lipophilic substances. The adsorption capacity, pore structure, and stability of aerogels are highly dependent on the preparation route, among which the drying method plays a critical role in determining the microstructure (Abdullaha et al., 2023). Manzocco et al. (2022) reported that supercritical CO₂ drying better preserved the integrity of the gel network compared with freeze-drying, resulting in aerogels with a larger specific surface area, more regular pore structure, and superior adsorption performance. Therefore, supercritical CO₂ drying was employed in this study.

Despite encouraging progress in the preparation and application of whey protein aerogels, critical research gaps still exist. Most studies focus on fabrication processes and macroscopic property characterization, with insufficient mechanistic understanding of how gelation conditions regulate protein conformation and network evolution, thereby governing pore structure and adsorption behavior. While gel parameters such as pH, protein concentration, and heating temperature are known to influence aerogel performance (Schmidt et al., 2019), the underlying mechanisms linking protein conformational changes, pore formation, and water/oil adsorption behaviors remains unclear. Furthermore, most studies focus on non-specific aerogels and rarely tailor their affinity toward aqueous and lipid phases through gelation conditions, limiting precise control over adsorption behavior.

To address these research gaps, this study aims to clarify the structure–function relationship of whey protein aerogels and reveal the intrinsic mechanism by which gelation conditions regulate their adsorption performance. The effects of key gelation parameters (pH, protein concentration, and temperature) on the structural and adsorption properties of supercritical CO₂-dried whey protein aerogels were systematically investigated. Accordingly, hydrophilic (HPAG) and oleophilic (OPAG) aerogels were prepared, and their pore structure, protein secondary structure (characterized by circular dichroism, CD spectroscopy), water/oil adsorption kinetics, and related physicochemical properties were comprehensively compared. This work not only provides a new insight into the regulatory mechanism of gelation conditions on whey protein aerogels but also lays a solid theoretical foundation for the precise design, controllable preparation, and targeted application of food-grade aerogel materials in functional loading systems.

Materials and methods

Materials

Whey protein isolate (WPI, 80% protein content, w/w) was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Refined soybean oil, manufactured by Yihai Kerry, was obtained from a local commercial supermarket. Anhydrous ethanol was purchased from Tianjin Zhiyuan Reagent Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). Liquid carbon dioxide (CO₂, purity >99.99%) was purchased from Henan Keyi Gas Co., Ltd. (Zhengzhou, China).

Preparation of hydrogels

Whey protein hydrogels were prepared following the method described by Betz et al. (2012). Whey protein stock solutions with concentrations of 10%, 12%, 14%, 16%, and 18% (w/w) were prepared by dissolving whey protein powder in deionized water, followed by magnetic stirring (400 rpm) at room temperature for 2 h to ensure complete dissolution. The pH of protein solution was then adjusted to the desired values (5.5, 6.5, 7.5, 8.5, 9.5) using 1 M HCl or NaOH solutions. Then whey protein solution was aliquoted into 20 mL plastic syringes (internal diameter: 20 mm) and heated in a water bath at a predetermined temperature (75 °C, 80 °C, 85 °C, 90 °C, 95 °C) for 30 min to induce protein denaturation and gelation. After gelation, the samples were cooled to room temperature naturally and stored at 4 °C for 24 h to stabilize the gel network prior to subsequent experiments.

Preparation of alcogel

Alcogels were prepared according to the method of Alavi and Ciftci (2023) with minor modifications. Hydrogels were demoulded from the syringes and cut into cylindrical sections with a length of 15 mm. The water in the hydrogels was replaced with ethanol via gradient solvent exchange. The detailed procedure was as follows: the hydrogel sections were first immersed in 30% (v/v) ethanol solution for 6 h, then transferred to 70% (v/v) ethanol solution for 12 h, and finally soaked in absolute ethanol (100%, v/v) for 24 h on three successive times. Throughout the entire solvent exchange process, the mass-to-volume ratio of hydrogel to ethanol solution was consistently maintained at 1:5 (w/v).

Preparation of aerogels

Whey protein aerogels were prepared using supercritical carbon dioxide (CO₂) drying method based on the techniques reported by Andlinger, Bornkeßel, et al. (2021) and Manzocco et al. (2022). The drying process was conducted in a 1000 mL high-pressure reactor. The pressure was ramped to 11 ± 1 MPa at a rate of approximately 1 MPa/min, followed by supercritical CO₂ drying at 45 °C with a continuous flow rate of 3.5 NL/min for 4 h. After completion of drying, the pressure was slowly released to atmospheric pressure at a rate of 0.1 MPa/min while maintaining a constant temperature of 45 °C to avoid structural collapse. The obtained aerogels were immediately transferred to a desiccator containing P₂O₅ and stored at room temperature until further characterization.

Determination of water-holding capacity (WHC) and oil-holding capacity (OHC)

The WHC and OHC of aerogels were determined referring to the method of Plazzotta et al. (2020). Approximately 0.3 g of aerogel particles (accurately weighed, P₁) was dispersed in 300 mL of distilled water or refined soybean oil. The mixture was vortexed (XW-80 A, Kylin-Bell Lab Instruments Co., Ltd., China) three times for 1 min each with an interval of 2 min. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 11,200 ×g for 5 min at room temperature. The supernatant was carefully discarded, and the precipitate was recentrifuged under the same conditions to remove residual unbound solvent. The centrifuged sediment was accurately weighed (P₂) after gently blotting the surface with filter paper. WHC and OHC were calculated as the mass of retained water or oil per gram of dry aerogel particles using Eq. (1):(1)WHCorOHCg/g=P2−P1P1

The volumes of alcogel and aerogel samples were calculated based on their diameters and heights measured using a digital caliper (accuracy: 0.01 mm) according to the method of Dogenski et al. (2020). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observation

The microstructures of whey protein and aerogels were observed using a Quanta FEG scanning electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). Aerogel samples were cut into small pieces using a blade, and whey protein powder was evenly spread on the sample stage. All samples were mounted on aluminium stubs with double-sided conductive tape and sputter-coated with a thin gold layer (∼10 nm) at 20 mA for 1 min under vacuum conditions prior to observation. The microstructure was observed at an accelerating voltage of 3 kV, and images were captured at different magnifications.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) observation

CLSM observation was performed following the method of Plazzotta et al. (2020) with slight modifications. Proteins and oil in the samples were stained with 0.2% (w/v) aqueous solutions of Nile Blue and Nile Red, respectively. The stained samples were placed on a glass slide, covered with a coverslip and observed using an FV3000 laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) at 40× magnifications.

Water and oil absorption kinetics

Water and oil absorption kinetic experiments were conducted at room temperature (25 ± 1 °C). Aerogel samples (m₀) were placed in 250 mL beakers containing 125 mL of distilled water or refined soybean oil. At predetermined time intervals, the samples were removed, and surface water or oil was gently blotted dry with filter paper. The samples were then weighed (mₜ) immediately. The weighing process was repeated until three consecutive measurements showed a mass variation of less than 0.001 g (i.e., reaching adsorption equilibrium). The adsorption capacity at time t (yt, g/g) was defined as the ratio of the mass increase of the aerogel at time t to its initial mass, calculated as yₜ = (mₜ − m₀)/m₀.

The water and oil absorption kinetic data were fitted to an exponential model (Manzocco et al., 2017; Plazzotta et al., 2019) to analyse the kinetic behavior, as shown in Eqs. (4), (5):(4)yt=yfast1−e−kfastt+yslow1−e−kslowt(5)ymax=yfast+yslowwhere yt is the adsorption capacity at time t (g/g); yfast and yslow are the asymptotic adsorption capacities of the fast and slow adsorption stages, respectively (g/g); kfast and kslow are the rate constants of the fast and slow adsorption stages, respectively (min−1); and ymax is the maximum adsorption capacity when the adsorption reaches equilibrium (g/g), which is the sum of yfast and yslow.

Specific surface area, pore volume, and pore size

Nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms of aerogels were measured using a specific surface area analyser (NOVA 2200e, Quantachrome Instruments, Boynton Beach, USA). Approximately 150 mg of aerogel sample was accurately weighed and degassed under vacuum (<1 mPa) at 60 °C for 15 h to remove adsorbed moisture and impurities. The specific surface area (SSA) was calculated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method (ISO 9277: 2022). The pore volume and average pore diameter were determined using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method based on the desorption branch of the isotherm.

Textural analysis

Textural properties of the aerogels were determined using a TA. HD Plus C texture analyser (Lotun Science Co., Ltd., China) with a 36R flat probe. The measurement parameters were set as follows: pre-test speed = 1.0 mm/s, test speed = 0.1 mm/s, post-test speed = 1.0 mm/s, compression strain = 50% relative to the original sample height, and trigger force = 5 g. Each sample was tested in triplicate. Textural parameters including hardness, adhesiveness, cohesiveness, and stickiness were derived from the force-time curves generated by the texture analyser.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis

FTIR spectra of whey protein, whey protein aerogel, water-adsorbing aerogel, and oil-adsorbing aerogel were recorded using a Nicolet 6700 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). Samples were mixed with potassium bromide (KBr) at a mass ratio of 1:100, ground into fine powder, and pressed into transparent pellets. The spectra were collected in the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1 with a spectral resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 co-added scans (Plazzotta et al., 2020). Background correction was performed using pure KBr pellets before sample testing.

Thermogravimetric (TG) analysis

The thermal decomposition behavior of aerogels was investigated using a Q50 thermogravimetric analyser (TA Instruments Ltd., USA) according to the method of Alavi and Ciftci (2023). Approximately 2–3 mg of aerogel sample was placed in an open aluminium crucible. The sample was heated from 50 °C to 600 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen atmosphere with a flow rate of 20 mL/min. An empty aluminium crucible was used as the reference.

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy analysis

Far-ultraviolet (far-UV) CD spectra of whey protein and its aerogel were measured using a MOS-450 circular dichroism spectropolarimeter (Bio-Logic Science Instruments, Seyssinet-Pariset, France). Samples were dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.0, 50 mM) and filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter to remove insoluble impurities, resulting in a final sample concentration of 0.1 mg/mL. CD measurements were performed in the far-UV region (190–260 nm) at 25 °C using a 1 mm path length quartz cuvette. The scanning speed was set to 1 nm/s, and the bandwidth was 1 nm. Three scans were accumulated for each sample, and the background spectrum of PBS buffer was subtracted to correct for baseline drift.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three replicates, and the results were expressed as mean values ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis and Pearson correlation analysis were conducted using SPSS 24.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's multiple range test was used to evaluate significant differences between groups, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. Non-linear regression analysis of water/oil absorption capacity versus time was performed using Origin 2018 software (Origin Lab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA) to fit the kinetic models.

Results and discussion

Effects of gelation conditions on properties of aerogels

The functional properties of whey protein aerogels are inherently determined by the molecular conformation and intermolecular interactions of whey protein during gelation. Herein, we systematically investigated the regulatory effects of protein concentration, gelation temperature, and pH on aerogel properties (WHC, OHC, shrinkage, density) and clarified the underlying molecular mechanisms by correlating with protein structural changes.

Whey protein concentration

Fig. 1 shows the variation of aerogel properties with whey protein concentration. Statistical analysis confirmed a significant regulatory effect of protein concentration on aerogel properties (p < 0.05). Specifically, WHC and OHC increased proportionally with protein concentration up to 14%, reaching 10.47 g/g and 5.34 g/g, respectively, and then decreased significantly at concentrations above 14%. Meanwhile, volume shrinkage decreased gradually (from 23.19% to 20.22%) and bulk density increased monotonically (from 0.12 to 0.26 g/cm3) with increasing protein concentration. This trend was rooted in the concentration-dependent regulation of whey protein intermolecular interactions. At concentrations below 14%, the increased protein content enhanced molecular collision probability, promoting the formation of disulfide bonds (–S–S–) and strengthening hydrophobic interactions (Pei et al., 2025). This accelerated gelation and constructed a continuous, moderately cross-linked three-dimensional network, which provided sufficient pores for water/oil retention. Notably, WHC showed a more significant improvement than OHC, as the denser network with small pores facilitated water penetration via hydrophilic interactions (between water molecules and protein surface amino/carboxyl groups) and hydrogen bonding. In contrast, oil molecules with larger molecular weight and higher viscosity encountered greater steric hindrance and weaker hydrophobic affinity with the protein matrix.Fig. 1Effect of whey protein concentration on aerogel properties.Fig. 1

Effect of whey protein concentration on aerogel properties.

When protein concentration exceeded 14%, excessive molecular cross-linking led to an overly compact network, which restricted the entry and retention of water/oil molecules, resulting in decreased WHC and OHC. Although high concentrations enhanced structural integrity via reinforced cross-linking, the diminishing marginal gains in adsorption properties (WHC/OHC) indicated that 14% (w/v) was the optimal concentration, balancing molecular network stability and functional adsorption capacity.

Gelation temperature

Fig. 2 presents the effect of gelation temperature on aerogel properties. WHC increased continuously with temperature, peaking at 11.18 g/g at 90 °C, and then slightly decreased to 10.99 g/g at 95 °C. OHC exhibited a similar trend but peaked earlier at 85 °C (5.32 g/g). In contrast, volume shrinkage (from 21.77% to 25.77%) and bulk density (from 0.14 to 0.17 g/cm3) increased monotonically with temperature (p < 0.05).Fig. 2Effect of gelation temperature on aerogel properties.Fig. 2

Effect of gelation temperature on aerogel properties.

The temperature-dependent variation of aerogel properties was rooted in the thermal denaturation and cross-linking behavior of whey protein. Within 80–90 °C, elevated temperature promoted the unfolding of whey protein molecules, exposing internal hydrophobic groups and sulfhydryl groups (–SH), which facilitated the formation of disulfide bonds and intermolecular cross-linking. (Andlinger et al., 2021a; Plazzotta et al., 2020). This process constructed a porous network with uniform pore size and moderate density, enhancing capillary action and hydrophilic interactions, thereby improving WHC (Manzocco et al., 2022). When the temperature reached 90 °C, protein denaturation and cross-linking approached completion, and the network structure achieved the optimal state for water retention. However, excessive temperature induced vigorous molecular motion, leading to protein over-aggregation and partial micropore collapse, which compressed the water storage space and caused a slight decrease in WHC. Xia et al. (2022) also reported that excessively high temperatures can lead to a decrease in WHC.

For OHC, the peak at 85 °C was attributed to the moderate denaturation degree of whey protein at this temperature. The formed network had a dense microporous structure, and the appropriately exposed hydrophobic groups on the pore walls enhanced the affinity with oil molecules. When the temperature exceeded 85 °C, the enhanced cross-linking made the network overly compact, increasing the steric hindrance for oil molecule penetration, thus reducing OHC (Guo et al., 2023). Additionally, higher temperatures strengthened the cross-linking of whey protein molecules, resulting in a more rigid and less elastic aerogel network. Although supercritical CO₂ drying minimizes capillary pressure-induced shrinkage, the rigid network could not buffer the slight volume change during solvent removal, leading to increased volume shrinkage rate and bulk density. In summary, when the gelation temperature was controlled within the range of 80–90 °C, the whey protein aerogels achieved optimal WHC and OHC. Therefore, this temperature range was ideal for preparing high-performance whey protein aerogel.

Gelation pH

Fig. 3 illustrates the effects of gelation pH on the WHC, OHC, shrinkage rate, and bulk density of aerogels. There was a significant pH-dependent differences in aerogel properties (p < 0.05). WHC increased sharply from 3.13 g/g (pH 5.5) to a maximum of 11.20 g/g (pH 8.5) before slightly decreasing to 11.03 g/g (pH 9.5). OHC peaked at 5.33 g/g (pH 7.5) and gradually declined with further pH adjustment. Shrinkage rate and bulk density decreased monotonically with increasing pH, falling from 25.43% and 0.18 g/cm3 (pH 5.5) to 20.18% and 0.16 g/cm3 (pH 9.5), respectively.Fig. 3Effect of gelation pH value on aerogel properties.Fig. 3

Effect of gelation pH value on aerogel properties.

These trends were dominated by the effect of pH on whey protein charge distribution and conformational changes. The isoelectric point of whey protein is 4.5–5.2. At pH 5.5 (near the isoelectric point), protein molecules carried minimal net charge, with hydrophobic interactions becoming dominant (Plazzotta et al., 2021). This led to rapid aggregation and the formation of a dense, low-porosity network structure. This can explain why WHC and OHC were at their lowest at pH 5.5. As pH increased to 7.5, protein molecules acquired moderate negative charge, enhancing electrostatic repulsion and inducing moderate unfolding. Hydrophobic interactions remained dominant, forming a dense microporous structure with enriched hydrophobic groups on pore walls, resulting in the highest OHC.

When pH reached 8.5, enhanced electrostatic repulsion drove complete protein unfolding, promoting the formation of flexible long-fiber structures via –SH/–S–S– exchange reactions (Betz et al., 2012). This constructed a loose, interconnected three-dimensional network with large pores and fully exposed hydrophilic groups (amino/carboxyl), maximizing WHC. Further increasing pH to 9.5 did not improve WHC, excessive molecular unfolding disrupted partial pore integrity, slightly reducing water/oil retention. Subsequent CD and FTIR results also indicated an extensive unfolding of protein molecules. Additionally, elevated pH strengthened electrostatic repulsion, rendering the network looser and elastic, which mitigated shrinkage during the drying process. Andlinger, Rampp, et al. (2021) indicated that altering gel pH can modulate protein-protein interactions. Thus, the optimal gelation pH range was determined as 7.5–8.5: pH 7.5 for maximum OHC and pH 8.5 for maximum WHC.

Structural characterization of whey protein aerogels

Based on single-factor experiments, the optimal conditions were determined as the levels yielding the maximum WHC or OHC of the aerogels. Under these conditions, hydrophilic aerogel (HPAG, 14% protein, 90 °C, pH 8.5) and oleophilic aerogel (OPAG, 14% protein, 85 °C, pH 7.5) were prepared. Multi-dimensional structural characterization was performed to establish the relationship among molecular structure, microstructure and functional properties, which is critical for the precise design of bio-based aerogels.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Fig. 4 shows the microstructures of raw WPI, OPAG, and HPAG. Raw WPI exhibited irregular granular morphology (5–55 μm) with smooth but uneven surfaces (Figs. 4A–B). In contrast, both OPAG and HPAG formed uniform submicron porous networks (Figs. 4C–F), confirming that gelation induced protein denaturation and cross-linking to form interconnected honeycomb structures. Notably, HPAG displayed larger and more interconnected pores than OPAG, which was attributed to the higher pH (8.5) during HPAG preparation. Elevated pH enhanced electrostatic repulsion, inducing more extensive protein unfolding and flexible long-fiber formation (Betz et al., 2012), while OPAG (pH 7.5) formed a denser network due to moderate unfolding and dominant hydrophobic interactions. This structural difference directly explained the superior WHC of HPAG and OHC of OPAG.Fig. 4SEM images of raw WPI, HPAG and OPAG.Fig. 4

After water absorption, HPAG (Fig. 5E) showed significant pore expansion due to strong hydrophilicity. This occurred as water molecules penetrated rapidly via hydrophilic interactions and hydrogen bonding, inducing network swelling and merging of small pores into large channels. In contrast, OPAG (Fig. 5B) maintained its dense structure without obvious expansion. After oil absorption, OPAG (Fig. 5C) formed larger oil droplet aggregates than HPAG (Fig. 5F). (2019) observed a comparable phenomenon during the encapsulation of fish oil in protein aerogels as the matrix.

Water and oil absorption kinetics

To elucidate the dynamic adsorption behaviors of HPAG and OPAG and their intrinsic correlation with microstructures, water and oil absorption kinetic tests were conducted, and the experimental data were fitted with exponential models. As shown in Fig. 6, both aerogels exhibited distinct adsorption preferences: HPAG showed superior water absorption performance, while OPAG outperformed in oil absorption. All adsorption processes were well fitted by exponential models with coefficients of determination (R2) greater than 0.98, indicating high fitting reliability, and the relatively large standard deviations of individual rate constants are mainly attributed to the inherent structural heterogeneity of the porous aerogels (Table 1). Notably, water absorption followed a biexponential model (with fast and slow stages), whereas oil absorption conformed to a monoexponential model (single rapid adsorption stage), reflecting the fundamental difference in interaction mechanisms between aerogels and polar/nonpolar solvents. The water absorption process could be divided into two sequential stages. In the fast adsorption stage (0–10 min), HPAG exhibited a significantly higher rate constant (kfast = 0.325 ± 0.121 min−1) than OPAG (kfast = 0.180 ± 0.103 min−1). This was attributed to the larger pore size and interconnected network structure of HPAG, which provided stronger capillary driving force and unobstructed channels for rapid water penetration (Khosravi & Azizian, 2016). In the slow equilibrium stage (10–1000 min), the rate constants of both aerogels decreased sharply (HPAG: kslow = 0.003 ± 0.058 min−1; OPAG: kslow = 0.002 ± 0.026 min−1), and HPAG still maintained a slightly higher rate. This stage was dominated by pore volume: after the saturation of large mesopores, residual water permeated into narrow micropores, and the swollen protein matrix of HPAG (induced by hydrophilic interactions) caused mild steric hindrance, which was weaker than that of the dense OPAG network (Manzocco et al., 2017). Correspondingly, HPAG achieved a higher maximum water absorption capacity (ymax = 11.189 ± 0.097 g/g) than OPAG (ymax = 10.246 ± 0.067 g/g), which may be consistent with its larger total pore volume.

In contrast, the oil absorption of both aerogels was completed within 2 min, showing a typical single-stage rapid adsorption characteristic. OPAG exhibited a higher oil absorption rate constant (kfast = 3.499 ± 0.131 min−1) and maximum capacity (ymax = 5.315 ± 0.032 g/g) than HPAG (kfast = 2.552 ± 0.154 min−1; ymax = 4.962 ± 0.056 g/g). This may be attributed to the denser porous structure and smaller pore size of OPAG, which facilitate more effective oil absorption and retention (Jung et al., 2023). Additionally, the single-stage adsorption indicated that oil molecules only interacted with the hydrophobic groups on the pore walls without inducing swelling of the protein network, which was consistent with the negligible pore expansion of OPAG observed in CLSM images (Fig. 5B) and the results reported by Plazzotta et al. (2019).

Specific surface area, pore volume and pore size

The nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms of HPAG and OPAG were classified as type IV isotherms with H3-type hysteresis loops according to the IUPAC classification, indicating both aerogels possessed mesoporous structures with broad pore size distributions and irregular pore morphologies. Quantitative pore structure parameters (Table 2) showed significant differences between HPAG and OPAG. HPAG exhibited a much higher BET specific surface area (ABET = 160.17 m2/g) than OPAG (ABET = 78.50 m2/g). These structural differences originated from the regulatory effect of gelation conditions on whey protein molecular unfolding and cross-linking. HPAG was prepared at higher pH (8.5) and temperature (90 °C), which enhanced electrostatic repulsion between protein molecules and promoted extensive denaturation and unfolding. This contributes to the formation of a loosely connected three-dimensional network with high pore volume (Betz et al., 2012). In contrast, OPAG was fabricated under moderate pH (7.5) and temperature (85 °C), leading to moderate protein unfolding and dominant hydrophobic interactions. This induced the formation of a denser network with smaller pores and lower pore volume. The smaller average pore diameter of OPAG was consistent with its superior oil-holding capacity, as reported by Jung et al. (2023) that smaller mesopores can enhance capillary force for oil retention.

Texture profile analysis (TPA)

Texture properties are critical for evaluating the mechanical stability and practical application potential of aerogels. TPA results (Table 2) showed that OPAG exhibited superior mechanical performance over HPAG, including higher hardness, cohesiveness, elasticity, and adhesiveness. The differences in texture properties were inherently determined by the molecular cross-linking degree and network structure of the two aerogels. The gelation temperature of 85 °C for OPAG promoted thiol-disulfide bond exchange reactions, enhancing intermolecular cross-linking and forming a rigid and dense porous network. This dense structure provided strong mechanical support, resulting in higher hardness and cohesiveness. For HPAG, the higher gelation temperature (90 °C) induced vigorous molecular motion, which reduced the compactness of the cross-linked network. Meanwhile, the elevated pH (8.5) increased electrostatic repulsion between protein molecules, leading to more extensive molecular unfolding and the formation of a loose three-dimensional network. This loose structure weakened the mechanical integrity of HPAG, resulting in lower texture parameters. These findings are consistent with those reported by Andlinger, Rampp, et al. (2021). The distinct texture and structural characteristics endow the two aerogels with different application potentials. The superior mechanical properties of OPAG make it more suitable for load-bearing application scenarios such as controlled drug release systems and food packaging materials. In contrast, HPAG, with its loose porous structure and excellent water absorption capacity, is well-suited for applications requiring high water retention and moisture regulation, such as food moisture-preserving materials and wound dressing substrates (Athamneh et al., 2023).

For aerogel-oil composites, some typical triglyceride peaks appeared, including 2853 cm−1 and 2922 cm−1 (C—H stretching of methyl/methylene groups), 3007 cm−1 (=C-H stretching), 1740 cm−1 (CO stretching), and 1160 cm−1 (C—O stretching) (Plazzotta et al., 2021). Notably, OPAG exhibited stronger oil-related peaks than HPAG, correlating with its higher OHC and dense microporous structure that enhances oil capillary retention.

For water-absorbed aerogels, the ∼3280 cm−1 broad peak was significantly broader than that of dry aerogels, attributed to hydrogen bond formation between water molecules and N-H/O-H groups on the aerogel scaffold, which increased O—H content and strengthened intermolecular hydrogen bonding (Fang et al., 2022). No new peaks or shifts were observed, confirming water absorption via physical interactions (hydrogen bonding and capillary action) rather than strong chemical bonds. HPAG exhibited more obvious peak broadening than OPAG, consistent with its higher WHC and larger pore volume that provides more hydrogen bonding sites with water.

Except for the intensity of water- and oil-related peaks, no significant spectral differences existed between HPAG and OPAG. This indicated that both aerogels shared the same physical adsorption mechanism with water/oil, and their functional differences (WHC/OHC) originated from structural characteristics (pore size, network density) rather than interaction types.

Thermogravimetric analysis

Thermal stability is a critical property for aerogel applications in biomedicine and related fields, as they typically require high-temperature dry heat sterilization (ca. 160 °C for 2 h) (Alavi & Ciftci, 2023). Thermogravimetric (TG) and differential thermogravimetric (DTG) analyses were conducted to assess the thermal decomposition behavior of HPAG and OPAG (Fig. 8).Fig. 8Thermogravimetric and derivative thermogravimetric curves of HPAG and OPAG.Fig. 8

Thermogravimetric and derivative thermogravimetric curves of HPAG and OPAG.

As shown in Fig. 8, both aerogels exhibited analogous TG/DTG curves with two distinct mass loss stages, consistent with the thermal decomposition characteristics of protein-based materials reported by Alavi and Ciftci (2023). The first stage (60–130 °C) involved a minor mass loss (≈ 3%), which was attributed to the evaporation of physically adsorbed moisture within the aerogel pores (Rosseto et al., 2025). The second stage, a sharp mass loss starting at approximately 225 °C, which can be attributed to protein degradation. Notably, distinct thermal stability differences were observed between HPAG and OPAG. HPAG showed a higher total mass loss rate (65.60% vs. 63.26%) and reached the maximum mass loss rate at a lower temperature (309.88 °C vs. 320.68 °C), indicating inferior thermal stability compared to OPAG. This difference originated from the molecular cross-linking degree and network structure of whey protein in the two aerogels. OPAG formed a denser network via strong hydrophobic interactions and disulfide cross-linking (as confirmed in Section 3.2.4 and 3.2.6), reducing protein exposure to heat and oxygen. In contrast, HPAG had extensive protein unfolding, weak cross-linking, and loose pores, facilitating heat transfer and pyrolysis (Wang et al., 2021).

These results demonstrated that the thermal stability of whey protein aerogels is inherently determined by the molecular structure and cross-linking state of whey protein. The superior thermal stability of OPAG ensures its structural integrity during dry heat sterilization, supporting its potential application in biomedical and tissue engineering fields.

These differences arise from the gelation conditions: HPAG was prepared under higher temperature (90 °C) and pH (8.5), which enhanced the thermal motion of protein molecules and electrostatic repulsion between amino acid residues, promoting extensive protein unfolding (Wang et al., 2021). In contrast, OPAG was fabricated under milder conditions (85 °C, pH 7.5), allowing the preservation of a more ordered protein structure. These results further corroborate the conclusion presented above that excessive unfolding of protein molecules occurs at pH 9.5.

The conformational changes of whey protein directly affected the formation of aerogel networks. The increase in disordered structures (especially random coil) exposed more functional groups (e.g., thiol groups, hydrophobic groups) on the protein surface, facilitating intermolecular cross-linking and the formation of porous three-dimensional networks within protein molecules (Alavi et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2023). However, excessive disordered structures (as in HPAG) may lead to lose network packing, while appropriate retention of ordered structures (as in OPAG) contributes to the formation of a more compact and stable network. This is consistent with the results of pore structure characterization (Section 3.2.4) and mechanical property analysis (Section 3.2.5).

In summary, the CD spectroscopy results confirmed that gelation conditions regulate the secondary structure composition of whey protein by affecting the degree of protein unfolding. The difference in secondary structure between HPAG and OPAG is the intrinsic factor leading to their differences in network structure and functional properties.

Conclusions

This study investigated the effects of protein concentration, gelation temperature, and pH on the water-holding capacity (WHC) and oil-holding capacity (OHC) of whey protein aerogels. Optimal preparation conditions were identified, and the intrinsic relationship between the structure and properties of the aerogels was clarified. The results showed that during the gelation process, OPAG and HPAG had the same protein concentration, but the gelation pH and temperature for OPAG were slightly lower. Compared with OPAG, HPAG exhibited a larger pore size, a higher specific surface area, and a looser three-dimensional network structure. This phenomenon might be attributed to the more intense unfolding of α-helices and β-sheets in HPAG, which also served as the key factor underlying its faster water absorption rate and greater WHC. In contrast, the denser structure of OPAG could effectively enhance its oil-locking ability, thereby resulting in a stronger OHC. Experiments confirmed that adjusting gelation conditions could effectively tune the WHC and OHC of whey protein aerogels by modulating their microscale structure and protein secondary structure.

Notably, all samples in this study were prepared using identical solvent exchange and supercritical CO₂ drying procedures, ensuring consistent treatment and reliable comparability among different samples. However, multi-step solvent exchange and high-pressure supercritical drying process may restrict large-scale production, and the potential structural effects induced by ethanol replacement require further verification. Future research may focus on systematically evaluating the influence of ethanol exchange on aerogel microstructure, exploring more scalable drying strategies, or further improving the WHC and OHC via structural modification.
