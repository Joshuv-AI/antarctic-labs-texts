Genotype × environment interaction studies in monoecious and gynoecious bitter gourd (Momordica charantia L.) genotypes for stability using multi-model based approach
Authors: Durga Prasad Moharana, D. R. Bhardwaj, Kartik Pramanik, Vidya Sagar, Sarvesh Pratap Kashyap, Indivar Prasad, K. K. Gautam, Pragnya Paramita Mishra, Pallavi Mishra, Anand Kumar Singh, Biswaranjan Paital, Dipak Kumar Sahoo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bitter gourd is an important cucurbitaceous crop grown in the summer and rainy seasons. However, its improvement requires genotypes with high yield and stability across the seasons. We evaluated 22 (gynoecious and monoecious) lines across four environments (spring–summer and rainy seasons over 2 years) for number of fruits per plant (NFP), fruit length (FL), fruit circumference (FC), fruit weight (FW), and yield per plant (YP), with the goal of determining the genotype-by-environment interaction (GEI) for yield and yield-contributing traits. Analysis of variance indicated a highly significant variation among the genotypes, environments, and GEI for all the traits under study. The environmental influence on yield accounted for 70.38% of the total variation. The presence of a strong environmental effect on complex traits such as yield indicates a substantial difference among different growing seasons. AMMI, WAASBY/ASV, and YSI all identified broadly stable genotypes; G3, G5, and G8 (notably high NFP), with G8 and G7 combining stability and high YP. Best performers included G16, G19, and G7 for FL; G9, G2, and G11 for FC; and G13, G16, and G21 for FW, with G12 expressing the most FW. GGE biplots identified two mega-environments; E2 and E3, which were the most discriminative for NFP and YP, respectively, with environment-specific “winners” allowing for targeted deployment. BLUPs and BLUEs were consistent across environments, with G20, G8, and G7 consistently leading in YP. The high-yield group (BLUP ≥ 1.79 kg: G14, G20, G9, G5, G6, G4, G8, G7) outperformed the grand mean. These findings support a need for a dual breeding strategy to advance broadly adapted, high-YP lines (G20, G8, G7, and G3, G5) for wide release and hybrid production, while exploiting specific adaptation for market-defining traits (FC and FW) using E2/E3 as primary selection sites. Integrating stability indices with BLUPs accelerates genetic gain and makes gynoecious lines more suitable for hybrid seed production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29704-w.

Introduction

Bitter gourd (Momordica charantia L., 2n = 2x = 22) is an important cucurbitaceous vegetable in terms of nutritive value, particularly for bioactive compounds like vitamins, minerals, alkaloids, polypeptides, steroidal saponins, and volatile aromatic oils1. It is primarily grown in Asia and Africa, with Asia accounting for more than half of global production of bitter gourd. The total production of bitter gourd in India is ~ 1330 thousand metric tons from an area of 109 thousand hectares. Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, Bihar, and Uttar Pradesh are the major producers of bitter gourd2. This crop is being grown in a variety of environments, and genotypes exhibit varying levels of trait expression under different environments.

The major goal of the cucurbit breeding programs is to develop high yielding genotypes with stable performance across environments. To achieve this in countries like India, where a wide range of climatic conditions exists, most cultivars are evaluated in different environments before being recommended for a specific one3. This evaluation is critical for identifying high-yielding cultivars with stable performance and broad adaptation across a wide range of environments. Cultivars with less fluctuation in response to changing environmental conditions are more stable and have a higher possibility of similar performance over a range of environments4,5. The performance of bitter gourd cultivars is often influenced by genotype × environment (G × E) interaction, making it challenging to select stable cultivars suitable throughout the region6.

The phenotype is a cumulative outcome of genotype (G), environment (E), and G × E; making G × E a crucial factor in the success of most breeding programmes7. Thus, understanding GE interaction allows for a true assessment of the impact of selection and ensures the consistency of genotype productivity in a specific location or environment group8,9. Several researchers have emphasized the importance of GEI, but only a few studies have been conducted on bitter gourd to determine the GEI. Among the various methods used for analyzing GEI, Additive Main Effects and Multiplicative Interaction (AMMI) analysis and Genotype Main Effect plus Genotype by Environment Interaction (GGE) biplots are frequently used.

AMMI analysis determines the effect of genotype and environment as additive effects and GE as a multiplicative component, allowing for the simple graphic representation of genotypes and environments in a multivariate dispersion diagram10. In principal component analysis, the best cultivar is one with a high score for PC1, indicating a high average yield, and a low PC2 score, indicating a lower GEI and greater stability. AMMI analysis can be used to effectively select better-growing sites and genotypes with higher mean performance11. However, more than two significant principal components resulted in multi-environments trials (METs) may not be sufficient to elaborate the GEI in finding stable genotypes by using AMMI and GGE biplot. Thus, other parametric methods such as AMMI Stability Index (ASI), AMMI Stability Value (ASV), Modified AMMI Stability Index (MASI), and Modified AMMI Stability Value (MASV)12 have been used to get a better insight into GEI and stability. Researchers have used Best Linear Unbiased Prediction (BLUP) in genotype screening to identify stable, high-yielding genotypes that have adapted to adverse environments.

Cucurbitaceous crops, including bitter gourd, require stability analysis because they are grown in multiple seasons (summer and rainy) in various agro-climatic zones12. The current experiment evaluated the G × E interaction for yield and related traits in 22 bitter gourd genotypes. The goal was to identify broadly or specifically adapted lines for future breeding programs and farmer adoption. Instead of relying upon a single model to analyze the GEI, the current study employs a multi-model based approach that includes analyses such as AMMI, GGE biplot, best linear unbiased prediction (BLUP), weighted average absolute scores (WAAS), and multi-trait stability index (MTSI) analysis to obtain a more convincing result in bitter gourd10 and recommend the best stable genotype for cultivation.

Experimental site and design

All the genotypes (Coded G1 to G22) were raised in the experimental farm (latitude 25.3176° N and longitude 82.9739° E) of the ICAR-IIVR, Varanasi and evaluated for yield and related traits for four consecutive growing seasons, viz., spring–summer (February–June) of 2017 and 2018 (E1 and E3); and rainy (July–October) of 2017 and 2018 (E2 and E4). The soil is characterized as alluvial soil and the surface soils exhibited a pH range between 7.2 and 8.5, with an average value of 7.9, reflecting their alkaline characteristics. Soil electrical conductivity (EC) was within normal limits, varying from 0.18 to 0.50 dS m−1, and averaging 0.30 dS m−1. Organic carbon content ranged from 3.5 to 9.4 g kg−1, with a mean concentration of 5.7 g kg−1. The available nutrient contents of nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) ranged from 110 to 298 kg ha−1 (mean 186), 9 to 63 kg ha−1 (mean 26), and 144 to 360 kg ha−1 (mean 232), respectively. A randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications was used in the experiment. The experimental plots are of 5.0 m × 3.0 m size with a spacing of 1.5 m between the rows and 0.5 m between the plants. The seeds were sown in the pro-trays, and the four-week-old seedlings were transplanted in the main field. All the recommended cultural practices including need-based chemical application for disease and pest management were followed to maintain a good crop stand.

Weather parameters

The monthly weather data recorded at experimental location for the year 2017 and 2018 have been separately annexed as supplementary Table S1. From February to June (E1 and E3), Varanasi experiences a progressive rise in temperature, with average monthly values increasing from approximately 19.7 °C in February to 33.5 °C in May across both years. Relative humidity declines steadily during this period, reaching its lowest levels in April (30–32%) before increasing slightly in June due to pre-monsoon activity. Rainfall remains minimal from February to May (typically < 20 mm/month), but June marks the onset of monsoon with a sharp increase to over 130 mm. Diurnal temperature variation is pronounced in the pre-monsoon months, with maximum temperatures frequently exceeding 38 °C in April and May. July to September (E2 and E4) constitute the core monsoon period in Varanasi, characterized by high humidity (> 78%) and substantial rainfall exceeding 250 mm/month. Average temperatures during this phase stabilize around 28–29 °C, with reduced diurnal variation due to persistent cloud cover and precipitation. October marks the retreat of the monsoon, with rainfall declining to ~ 40 mm and humidity levels dropping to ~ 70%. Despite reduced rainfall in October, residual moisture and moderate temperatures (~ 25.8 °C) maintain a transitional climate before winter onset.

Observations recorded

Observations were recorded for five quantitative traits, viz., number of fruits per plant (NFP), fruit length (cm) (FL), fruit circumference (cm) (FC), fruit weight (g) (FW), and yield per plant (kg) (YP) for each genotype in each growing season. Five randomly selected plants from the middle rows were chosen for recording the observations. Further, five fruits, attaining the edible maturity, were harvested from five randomly selected plants (excluding the ones at border rows) for data collection.

Statistical analyses

Analysis of variance

Analysis of variance was performed following the standard procedure as described by Singh and Choudhary in 198513. Bartlett’s test was used to test the homogeneity of error variances across environments, and combined analysis was performed with the transformed values where error variances were heterogeneous. Tukey’s test was performed to compare mean data of different traits using CropStat (software version 7.2) (IRRI, Philippines).

AMMI analysis

The AMMI analysis was performed to estimate the magnitude of genotype (G), environment (E), and GEI effect on fruit yield and its associated traits using R package by following formula;

IPCA scores and residual values were extracted from the sum of squares for G × E Interaction (GEI). IPCA I score and main effects of the genotypes were considered for construction of AMMI I biplot to identify the stable genotypes.

After testing the significant of GEI for the concerned traits, the ASV was calculated by the proposed formula to determine the stable and high yielding genotypes.

Yield stability index (YSI)

The YSI was determined for each genotype by summing ranking based on ASV (RASV) and ranking based on yield (RY) following the formula proposed by Kang14. The genotypes observed with low YSI and high yield are considered as stable and desirable15.

GGE biplot

GGE biplot was constructed to reveal the interaction and adaptation pattern of genotypes to environments as per proposed model11. The model is based on singular value decomposition (SVD) of the first two principal components (IPCA I and IPCA II) is:

WAASB is based on singular value decomposition of BLUP matrix to explain the GEI effects generated by a linear mixed-effect model (LMM)17. WAASB × trait mean (Y) biplot is adopted to identify productive environments and stable genotypes which has broad adoption. It is being recent trend of using the WAASB index in different species, such as soybean18, and barley19,20.

Results

The ranks of the breeding lines in different environments change as a result of crossover interactions caused by genotype and environment interactions, reducing genetic gain realization and breeding efficiency. Breeders are frequently faced with the dilemma of determining the best environment for carrying out the selection and then deciding on type of yield trials to select the winning genotypes. The crops like cucurbits, including bitter gourd, are grown in contrasting environments, such as harsh hot summers and mild humid rainy seasons in the northern plains of India. As a result, selecting bitter gourd genotypes for adaptations to both the seasons is necessary for improved yield realization in the farmers’ field. Understanding genotype-environment interactions could be a first step in the breeding for improvement of widely-adapted bitter gourd genotypes.

Mean performance

The pooled mean values for the traits (Table 2) across the seasons demonstrated that G7 was found to be the best for the traits like NFP (30.53) and YP (2.79 kg), while the genotypes G14, G16, and G20 were found to be superior for the traits FL (22.51 cm), FC (16.03 cm), and FW (108.8 g), respectively. The wide range of mean values for all the traits across environments indicated the presence of a large variation, which is highly desirable for screening in the multiple environments.

Combined analysis of variance

All traits were subjected to a combined analysis of variance across environments (Table 3). According to the analysis of variance for pooled data across all environments, the mean sum of squares due to the G, E, and GEI were significant for all the traits under study, indicating that the environments had a significance impact on genotype trait expression, which could be useful for future research and bitter gourd Improvement. However, using simple ANOVA makes recommending varieties for different environments difficult. Therefore, genotypic evaluation is useful if mega environments are defined and genotypes are identified in relation to them. The genotypic responses to different environments differ significantly, indicating a significant variability among the genotypes21.

Discussion

In this work, we characterized the 22 bitter gourd genotypes across four seasons under contrasting environments. The multi-model approach was used for identification of best performing genotypes with high stability for five traits in bitter gourd.

Analysis of AMMI model

The AMMI ANOVA analysis gives straightforward interpretation on GEI or residual matrix in multi-locational or multi-seasonal datasets22 which in not well understood in conventional ANOVA, regression analysis, or PCA. In AMMI, the main effects of genotype and environment are first extracted using a two-way additive ANOVA model (additive main effects), and PCA is then applied to the ANOVA residuals, which include the genotype × environment interactions (GEI; multiplicative interaction). The G and GEI are evaluated independently, which is important because plant breeders face various challenges and opportunities11. The AMMI analysis revealed highly significant genotype, environment, and GEI effects for all traits, confirming ample exploitable genetic variability as well as differential responses of bitter gourd genotypes to seasonal changes. In terms of yield per plant (YP), genotypic effects predominated (70.34%), followed by a moderate environmental effect (25.77%) and a small GEI (3.89%), indicating a largely consistent genotype ranking across the spring-summer and rainy season. This pattern indicates that selection for high yield can be effective across environments, and superior lines, particularly gynoecious types, can be advanced as broadly adapted parents for hybrid development and stable cultivars for diverse production environments.

Traits expression varied in their sensitivity to the environment. FL was largely under genetic control (91.82%) and had a low GEI (4.46%), as was NFP (4.68). These traits are thus reliable for early-generation selection and can be used as strong indirect criteria to improve YP. FC and FW had significantly higher GEI (22.66% and 10.38%, respectively), indicating greater plasticity and potential crossover responses between seasons. Breeders should prioritize multi-season testing for these market-defining characteristics with near-zero interaction scores for uniformity across the environments and specifically adapted lines for niche markets that require season- or region-specific fruit size/shape. Our results corroborated with the findings of Dia et al.23 who reported significant genotype, environment, and G × E effects on yield-associated traits, viz., percent culls, fruits per plant, early yield, marketable yield, and total yield in cucumber. Iwo and Odor24 revealed contribution of genotype by environment interaction of total variation created in bitter gourd for traits such as number of fruits per plot, fruit length, fruit weight, and total fruit yield which implied discrimination among genotypes under multi-location. Our study was in conformity with findings of Pramanik et al.25 who explained the significant role of G, E, and GEI accounted to total variation in French bean due to differential responses across different growing seasons. Lower value of residual for all the traits showing greater accuracy of the model except FW where a substantial value of the residual interaction was observed indicating unexplained variation (noise). The finding was in conformity with the outcomes of Pramanik et al.25 who reported the unexplained variation in protein content in stability analysis of French bean under multi-environments.

Identification of stable genotypes

Estimation of IPCA score, AMMI stability value, and WAAS score

The genotypes estimated with low IPCA score or close to zero are regarded as the stable performers for the concerned traits26,27 and it positioned close to interaction axis in biplot. However, the genotypes having a high mean value and IPCA score near to zero are suitable for inclusion in the future breeding program. Genotypes placed with long vector from center of interaction point are highly responsive towards environmental changes and may not be suitable for cultivation and/or to be used in crop improvement28 and that performs better under favorable environments only. The genotypes with negative IPCA score are suitable for adverse environments other than optimum climatic condition and the performance may not improve in favorable environments. AMMI biplots (Fig. 1a–e) and stability metrics (Tables 6 and 7) consistently revealed genotypes with broad versus specific adaptation across seasons. Small absolute IPCA1 scores indicated high stability, as evidenced by low ASV/AASV and WAASBY. The most stable genotypes were G11 and G16 for NFP, G3, G21, and G17 for FL, G19, G10, and G9 for FC, and G20 and G3 for FW. For YP, G10, G3, and G22 had low IPCA1, indicating broad stability. Notably, multi-index consensus (low ASV/AASV, low WAASBY, favorable YSI) identified G3, G8, and G5 as stable, with each combining desirable stability and high NFP. In contrast, G7 showed maximum NFP but high ASV and WAAS, indicating specific adaptation; however, for YP, both G7 and G8 achieved the highest means with favorable stability ranks, implying compensatory effects among yield components.

Trait-specific patterns indicate breeding opportunities. FL demonstrated multiple stable, high-mean donors (e.g., G16, G19, G7; and promising G13, G8, G5, G9) that are suitable for early-generation selection due to its high genotypic control. For FC, G9, G2, and G11 combined stability with high means, whereas G16 combined superior FC with favorable stability ranks; genotypes such as G3, G4, G19, and G21 provided high FC but with specific adaptation, which was useful in targeted environments. G13, G16, and G21 were relatively stable in terms of FW, while G12 combined the highest FW with favorable stability ranks.

The broadly stable, high-YP genotypes (G3, G10, and G22) are excellent candidates for varietal release and as parents of widely-adapted hybrids. The high-performing lines (e.g., G7 for NFP, G12 for FW, and high-FC lines) can be tailored to seasonal windows and market niches. Integrating WAASBY and YSI with AMMI biplots allows a balanced selection on mean and stability, while deploying gynoecious lines among these stable donors can boost hybrid seed production and genetic gain. The current results corroborate with the findings of Koundinya et al.29 in brinjal and Pramanik et al.25 in French bean who also identified stable genotypes using the stability indices. The IPCA1 and IPCA2 were considered for interpretation due to maximum significant cumulative effect (> 80%) of GEI which many workers have justified about its consideration in the earlier studies30–33. Purchase et al.34 proposed a numeric index known as ASV to identify phenotypic stable genotypes across multi-environments. In early findings, many earlier workers, viz., Bose et al. in 201415 in rice, Koundinya et al.29 in brinjal, Mwale et al.35 in tepary bean, and Pramanik et al.25 in French bean considered genotypes as stable having low ASV, YSI, WAASBY score, or less than equal to AASV. The results are in accordance with the findings of Mwale et al.35 who highlighted consideration of stability value and indices for screening of stable accessions of tepary bean for seed yield attributing traits in multi-environments.

Construction of AMMI 1, GGE, and WAASB biplots

Biplots such as AMMI 1, GGE, and WAASB are important studies for classifying genotypes and environments in identifying stable genotypes and productive environments highlighted by many workers16,25,35. The biplot has four quadrants indicating the adaptability and stability of different genotypes to multi-environments and gives a straight interpretation. AMMI biplot was constructed with IPCAI score and genotypes main effects whereas, WAASB biplot is plotted with trait value and WAASBY scores for the trait to find out the stable genotypes and adaptive environments. GGE biplots, termed as “which-won-where”, depict the best performing genotypes in (an) environment(s) and identify the mega-environments for experimentation. Consideration of different biplots demonstrated greater accuracy of our study on bitter gourd. In biplots, the environments E1, E2, E3, and E4 were joined with long vectors or far from center indicating presence of high GEI. First quadrants presented genotypes placed above the horizontal interaction line, and the left side of the vertical mean line indicted positive interaction with environments and having trait value less than mean and their performance reduced under adverse conditions. Likewise, genotypes congregated in second quadrants which are present above interaction vector and right side of mean value vector explaining positive interaction with higher trait value and genotypes were well performer in suitable environments. The third quadrant depicted the genotypes having negative interaction with environments and having less trait value. Similarly, genotypes scattered in fourth quadrant revealed adaptability to adverse environments but their performance is awful in congenial environments conditions.

AMMI 1 biplots

AMMI 1 biplots revealed patterns of adaptation and stability. E2 and E4 performed well in terms of NFP. G16, G22, G2, G19, and G10 were adapted to E2/E4 but below the mean; G4, G6, G9, and G7 were highly responsive but risked stress-related losses. E1/E3 had a negative impact, but G20 and G14 remained resilient. Genotypes near the origin (G3, G5, G8) remained stable, with G5 and G8 showing high NFP levels. For fruit length, E3/E4 were the most discriminative, while G16, G19, and G7 remained stable. Strong GEI was evident in fruit circumference; G9, G2, and G11 were superior, while G4, G16, G5, G6, and G19 performed well under adverse conditions. G13, G16, and G21 remained stable in terms of fruit weight. The yield per plant was affected by the environment; G8 and G7 combined close proximity to the origin with high means. Breeding should advance stable parents, capitalize on specific adaptations, and use E3/E4 for selection and E1/E3 for stress screening. In our experiment, a few genotypes outperformed others in gaining plant value when these received a favourable environment. But their performance was affected by adverse climatic conditions indicating instability. Environments E2/ E4 and E1/ E3 found distinct as favourable and adverse climates, respectively, which differentiate the performance of genotypes. Our results were in accordance with Pramanik et al.25 who reported stable genotypes in French bean by using AMMI 1 biplots.

GGE biplot

Genotype Main Effects and Genotype by Environment Interaction Effects [G + (G × E)] biplots present a straight forward interpretation of best performing genotypes in adaptive environments and mega-environment(s) for consideration. It explains “which-won-where” means adaptability of genotypes to the environments forming polygons and sectors with interaction vectors. GGE biplots divided the testing environments into two mega-environments and revealed clear differences in genotype performance across traits. For NFP, E2 and E3 were the most informative target environments; G7 performed well in E2, while G12 and G17 proximity to E3 indicated strong adaptation. Polygon vertices (e.g., G13, G12, G7, G10, G6) revealed the best NFP performers, confirming E2 and E3 as effective screening and general cultivation sites, respectively. In FC, G20 (E2), G18 (E3), G12 (E1), and G5 (E4) were environment-specific winners, with G10, G5, G1, G20, G15, G18, and G12 performing well across the environments. FW demonstrated strong environmental discrimination (obtuse E1-E2 and E3-E4 angles); multiple vertex genotypes (e.g., G10, G21, G12, G18, G14, G13, G19, and G4) were superior. For YP, G12, G17, G21 (E3); G1, G14, G20, G18 (E1); G19, G6, G9, G4 (E4); and G2, G10, G16, G5 (E2) were winners, whereas G8 and G7 near the origin showed broad stability. Many workers highlighted the validation of GGE biplots in screening of different genotypes and identifying suitable genotype(s) for particular environments29,36. AMMI and GGE biplots were employed in identifying the stable and preferable genotypes in spine gourd37. GEI for marketable fruit yield in pickling cucumber was studied by constructing AMMI and GGE biplots23.

WAASB biplot

Trait mean (Y) × WAASB biplot is an efficient model to interpret stability of genotypes and trait productivity jointly. The biplot has four quadrants composing of genotypes and environments under study and is useful to identify stable genotypes and productive environments16. For NFP, G12, G17, and G21 (quadrant I) interacted positively with E1/E3, but they were unstable and below the grand average. G7, G6, G4, G9, and G14 (quadrant II) showed high NFP and were adapted to E2/E4. Quadrant III genotypes were stable but below-mean, whereas G8 and G5 (quadrant IV) exhibited the highest stability (low WAASBY) and higher NFP; G15 and G20 were also stable. For FL, G14, G19, G11, G8, and G13 were less responsive and above the mean; however, G2, G12, G4, G15, and G1 were unstable due to strong E1/E2 interactions. Long environment vectors showed strong discrimination for FC; genotypes near the vertical axis in quadrants III/IV remained stable, with high FC and low WAASBY in G16 (0.10), G10 (0.14), G4 (0.14), G19 (0.08), G21 (0.09), G9 (0.05), and G6 (0.18). FW had little environmental impact overall, with E1 being the least productive. In terms of breeding, G8 and G5 are ideal broadly adapted donors for NFP; stable, high-FL lines (G14, G19, G11, G8, G13) and stable, high-FC lines can be used for ideotype refinement, whereas E1 is unsuitable for FW selection. Mwale et al.35 identified high yielding stable tepary bean for cultivation and screening under drought-prone environments using WAASB score and biplots. WAASB stability index was employed to isolate cotton lines for affected areas38. Significant genotype by environments interaction was estimated and considered for identifying wider adapted lettuce varieties using WAASB model39. Da Silva et al.40 highlighted efficacy of BLUP and WAASB model over AMMI in screening and identifying stable accessions in soybean.

BLUPs and blues analysis for fruit yield among 22 bitter gourd genotypes

The BLUPs analysis in our present study found reliable which corrected the environmental effect and enhanced the genotypic effect for fruit yield of bitter gourd genotypes across environments. BLUPs and BLUEs for yield per plant exhibited strong concordance both within and across environments, supporting the reliability of genotype ranking (Table 7). In E1, G20 attained the highest yield (BLUP 1.94 kg; BLUE 2.02 kg), succeeded by G8 (1.71; 1.87) and G7 (1.65; 1.82), while G19 and G17 exhibited the lowest performance. Significantly, G19 exhibited the lowest performance in E2 and E3, while G17 was the lowest in E4, suggesting restricted adaptation. The strong correlation between BLUP and BLUE rankings across various sites and seasons suggests that high-yield performance is consistent and only slightly influenced by environmental factors, thereby affirming the validity of selection based on BLUPs. Secondly, G20, G8, and G7 are optimal candidates for extensive adaptation and can function as progenitors/testers in hybrid breeding as well as elite lines for deployment. The G20’s dominance in E1 indicates potential benefits for production periods susceptible to stress. The extensive collection of above-mean genotypes (G14, G9, G5, G6, G4) enhances the donor pool for heterotic crosses and regional application. Combining these yield BLUPs with existing AMMI/WAASBY stability data facilitates balanced selection—favoring G20, G8, and G7 for extensive cultivation, while allocating other high-BLUP lines to designated target environments and integrating them with trait donors for fruit size and shape to align with market specifications. Higher heritability implies a significant genetic contribution and a lower impact of environmental variance on phenotypic expression of bitter gourd genotypes. The total variation markedly contributed with genotypic effect followed by GEI. The results were in accordance with the findings of Mwale et al.35 who revealed the importance of BLUPs and BLUEs estimation in tepary bean for seed yield across multi-environments.

MTSI

The MTSI is an advanced statistical tool to screen out stable genotypes with high trait values under multi-environments set up17. It considers both fixed and random effect model on multiple traits to measure the distance between genotype and ideotype to generate factor analysis score to identify elite genotypes based on mean performance and stability (MPE) in METs. The genotype with lowest MTSI score, having similar ideotype, contributes to its overall performance. In the present study, the genotypes, G8, G7 and G5 were found to be highly stable for expression of different traits, indicating that these genotypes can be grown in summer and rainy season without losing the yield. However, to maximize the bitter gourd yield, cultivation of genotypes specifically adapted to summer and rainy season will be more rewarding. The MTSI was used for selection of heat stress tolerant genotypes of vegetable pea for early and late planting during the season. Our results corroborated with earlier findings Olivoto et al.17. Stable cultivars were identified among 46 soybean genotypes under drought and salinity conditions based on MTSI41. The GEI was studied in tepary bean for seed yield and stable lines were revealed based on WAASBY and MTSI model35. Multiple traits were considered in screening of stable performing genotypes or cultivars with high trait value in different vegetable crops under METs based on MTSI. Some of such reports are noted by Koundinya et al.29 in brinjal; Alam et al.42 in sweet potato; Taleghani et al.43 in sugar beet, Begum et al.44 in Solanum khasianum, and efficacy of the model was highlighted.

Eco-genomic studies hint about better genotypes for cultivation of various economically important crop species44. Therefore, identifying such genotypes has paramount importance in agricultural sciences45. Using multiple parameters and statistical approaches, such identified species can be useful to increase yields46–51. Therefore, the genotype G7, G8, G4, G5, G6, and G14 identified in the current study are supposed to have better agricultural values52–54. Few stable genotypes identified in this programme (e.g., VRBTG-10) are already in pipeline for commercial release as listed in IIVR Annual Report published by Indian Institute of Vegetable Research in 202252.

Conclusions

A major goal of any plant breeding programme is to identify stable genotypes; however, identifying genotypes suitable for multiple environments necessitates screening in suitable environments over multiple seasons. In the current study, 22 bitter gourd genotypes were evaluated over four seasons across two years. The multi-model analysis, including AMMI model, revealed that the gynoecious genotypes Gy-318 (G7), Gy-323 (G8), Gy-144 (G4), (Gy-2116 (G5), Gy-2135 (G6), and VRBTG-10 (G14) were the best performers across all the seasons. The rainy season of 2018 (E4) was discovered to be the most discriminating environment for bitter gourd cultivation. The environment contributed the most to total variation, followed by genotype and GEI. The current study will assist bitter gourd breeders in selecting appropriate genotypes for breeding during specific seasons. Further evaluation of these genotypes across multiple locations and seasons is recommended as a follow-up study.
