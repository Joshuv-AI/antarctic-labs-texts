On the Sign of the Casimir Force Between Two Bodies Coupled to a Dirac field
Aitor Fernández, César D. Fosco, Guillermo Hansen

Abstract
We apply the result of Kenneth and I. Klich, Phys. Rev. Lett. 97 , 160401 (2006) to derive a theorem for two objects coupled to a Dirac field. We demonstrate that a sufficient condition for the Casimir interaction to be repulsive , is for their geometric configuration to be odd under the same reflection operation considered in that work.



1
Introduction

The study of the relation between the properties of the Casimir
effect
[
1
]
and the geometry (i.e., shapes and positions)
of the bodies involved is a captivating area of research, with significant
implications to both experimental and theoretical physics. Understanding
this relation is crucial for Casimir experiments; indeed, one could, for
instance, be interested in discovering
the optimal geometric configuration to maximize the Casimir force, as this
could have applications in nanotechnology and microelectromechanical
systems (MEMS)
[
3
]
. Additionally, identifying shapes that
exhibit stability under small deformations is vital for ensuring the
reliability and robustness of devices that exploit the Casimir
effect
[
4
]
.
Furthermore, investigating the impact of surface corrugations and other
irregularities on the Casimir force is essential for predicting its
behavior in real-world scenarios
[
5
]
.

On the theoretical front, an important challenge lies in developing
effective methods to cope with the geometries that often arise in Casimir
effect studies. Perturbation theory, a commonly used tool in theoretical
physics, is generally not suitable for tackling non-trivial
shapes
[
6
]
, except
in specific cases, like an expansion in powers of the deformation of the
geometry with respect of a highly symmetric one, which is exactly solvable.
A related but different approach is the derivative
expansion
[
7
]
.

This calls for the development of alternative theoretical frameworks and
computational techniques that can accurately model the Casimir interaction
for a wide range of geometric configurations. In this context, there is an
important theoretical result regarding the relation between geometry and
the Casimir force: a sufficient condition for this force between
two dielectric or conducting objects to be attractive is for the geometry of the system to have a reflection symmetry with respect to a
plane
[
8
]
. In other words, when the function representing
the electromagnetic properties of the two media as a whole is invariant
(i.e., even) under the symmetry operation, the force between them is
attractive.

In this work, we apply that theorem to derive another one, this
time for objects coupled to a Dirac field. The result may be phrased as
follows: a sufficient condition for the Casimir interaction to be
repulsive
, is for their geometric configuration to be
odd
under the
same geometric operation considered in the bosonic case.

This paper is organized as follows: since our approach to the Dirac field
case relies heavily upon the scalar field result, we briefly review the
latter in Sect.
2
, albeit in the notation and
conventions we afterwards use for the Dirac field. Besides, we focus here
on the properties which are more relevant to the Dirac field case, which
is dealt with in Sect.
3
, where we derive the result about
the sign of the force in that kind of system.

In Sect.
4
we present some remarks about the general result
and its application, and a brief summary of our conclusions.

2
Scalar field

We shall begin by introducing the main concepts and definitions for the
case of a real scalar field, briefly reviewing the known result for the
nature of the force, in terms of our approach, notation, and conventions.
We deal with a vacuum massless real scalar field
\varphi(x)
, in
d+1

space-time dimensions, in the presence of two objects, denoted by
L
and

R
.
To obtain the Casimir interaction energy, we shall work in the context of
the functional integral framework, using the imaginary-time formalism;
hence, it is convenient to introduce the system in terms of its Euclidean
action. This object is a functional
{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{S}}(\varphi;U)

denotes a function that accounts for the relevant properties
of the two bodies. We assume
{\mathcal{S}}
to have the structure:

{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{S}}^{(0)}\,+\,{\mathcal{S}}^{(I)}\;,

(1)

{\mathcal{S}}^{(0)}

{\mathcal{S}}^{(0)}(\varphi)\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\,\int_{x}\partial_{\mu}\varphi(x)\partial_{\mu}\varphi(x)\;,

(2)

summation over repeated indices has been assumed. Indices from the
middle of the Greek alphabet run from
0

space-time coordinates. We shall use

to denote
these coordinates,

{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})

The

between the field and the two bodies, and is given by:

{\mathcal{S}}^{(I)}(\varphi;U)\,=\,\int_{x_{0},x^{\prime}_{0},{\mathbf{x}}}\varphi(x_{0},{\mathbf{x}})U(x_{0}-x^{\prime}_{0};{\mathbf{x}})\varphi(x^{\prime}_{0},{\mathbf{x}})\;.

(3)

coordinate, of the objects involved

(16)

We now introduce some notation to distinguish an operator from its

\widehat{K}_{AB}

K_{AB}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}})=\langle{\mathbf{x}}|\widehat{K}_{AB}|{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}\rangle

L

R
.

Using standard formulae for the evaluation of Gaussian integrals, and

\widehat{K}_{LL}
and
\widehat{K}_{RR}

\frac{{\mathcal{Z}}_{\omega}(\widetilde{U})}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{\omega}(0)}\;=\;[{\rm det}(\widehat{K}_{LL})]^{-1}\,[{\rm det}(\widehat{K}_{RR})]^{-1}\,\Big({\rm det}\big[\widehat{I}_{L}-(\widehat{K}_{LL})^{-1}\widehat{K}_{LR}(\widehat{K}_{RR})^{-1}\widehat{K}_{RL}\big]\Big)^{-1}\;,

(17)

where
\widehat{I}_{A}
is the identity operator for functions with support

A

Finally, using the assumption that each function
\widetilde{U}_{A}

\widehat{\widetilde{U}}_{A}

\langle{\mathbf{x}}|\widehat{\widetilde{U}}_{A}|{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}\rangle=\widetilde{U}_{A}({\mathbf{x}})\delta({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}})

\widehat{T}_{A}=\big(\widehat{I}_{A}+\widehat{\widetilde{U}}_{A}\hat{G}^{(0)}_{AA})^{-1}\widehat{\widetilde{U}}_{A}\;,

\frac{{\mathcal{Z}}_{\omega}(\widetilde{U})}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{\omega}(0)}\;=\;({\rm det}\widehat{K}_{LL})^{-1}\,({\rm det}\widehat{K}_{RR})^{-1}\;\big({\rm det}[\widehat{I}_{L}-\widehat{T}_{L}\hat{G}^{(0)}_{LR}\widehat{T}_{R}\hat{G}^{(0)}_{RL}]\big)^{-1}\;.

E_{I}\;=\;\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,{\rm log}{\rm det}[\widehat{I}_{L}-\widehat{T}_{L}\hat{G}^{(0)}_{LR}\widehat{T}_{R}\hat{G}^{(0)}_{RL}]\;=\;\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,{\rm Tr}{\rm log}[\widehat{I}_{L}-\widehat{T}_{L}\hat{G}^{(0)}_{LR}\widehat{T}_{R}\hat{G}^{(0)}_{RL}]\;.

(20)

On has

\widehat{\mathcal{P}}|{\mathbf{x}}\rangle\,=\,|{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbf{x}})\rangle\;.

3
Dirac field

Let us now deal with a vacuum massless Dirac field
\psi
,
\bar{\psi}
, in

d+1
space-time dimensions, in the presence of two objects,
L
and
R
, which
impose boundary conditions on the field fluctuations. The regions are
assumed to be disjoint, as in the scalar case.

We shall first relate the calculation of the vacuum energy to the case of a
real scalar field, and then study the role of the reflection transformation
in this case.

The Euclidean action is now a functional
{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{S}}(\bar{\psi},\psi;V)
where
V
denotes a function that accounts for the
two bodies. We also assume
{\mathcal{S}}
to have the structure:

{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{S}}_{0}\,+\,{\mathcal{S}}_{I}\;,

(31)

where
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
denotes the free action for the Dirac field,

{\mathcal{S}}_{0}(\bar{\psi},\psi)\;=\;\int_{x}\bar{\psi}(x)\not\!\partial\,\psi(x)\;.

(32)

Dirac’s
\gamma
matrices are assumed to be Hermitian,

\gamma_{\mu}^{\dagger}=\gamma_{\mu}
, and to satisfy the Clifford algebra

\{\gamma_{\mu}\,,\,\gamma_{\nu}\}=2\delta_{\mu\nu}
.
Also,
\not\!\partial\equiv\gamma_{\mu}\partial_{\mu}
.

The
{\mathcal{S}}_{I}
term, on the other hand, is given by:

{\mathcal{S}}_{I}(\bar{\psi},\psi;V)\,=\,\int_{x_{0},x^{\prime}_{0},{\mathbf{x}}}\bar{\psi}(x_{0},{\mathbf{x}})V(x_{0}-x^{\prime}_{0};{\mathbf{x}})\psi(x^{\prime}_{0},{\mathbf{x}})\;.

(33)

Introducing time Fourier transformations,

\psi(x_{0},{\mathbf{x}})=\int_{\not\omega}e^{i\omega x_{0}}\tilde{\psi}(\omega,{\mathbf{x}})\;,\;\;\bar{\psi}(x_{0},{\mathbf{x}})=\int_{\not\omega}e^{-i\omega x_{0}}\bar{\tilde{\psi}}(\omega,{\mathbf{x}})\;,\;\;V(x_{0};{\mathbf{x}})=\int_{\not\omega}e^{i\omega x_{0}}\widetilde{V}(\omega,{\mathbf{x}})\;,

(34)

the action decomposes again
into an integral over independent modes

{\mathcal{S}}\,=\,\int_{\not\omega}\,{\mathcal{S}}_{\omega}\;,\;\;\>{\mathcal{S}}_{\omega}\,=\,\int_{\mathbf{x}}\,\bar{\tilde{\psi}}(\omega,{\mathbf{x}})\big[\not\!\nabla+i\gamma_{0}\omega+\widetilde{V}(\omega,{\mathbf{x}})\big]\,\tilde{\psi}(\omega,{\mathbf{x}})\;.

(35)

The properties of the two objects, are
again determined by the form of
V
:

\widetilde{V}({\mathbf{x}})\,=\,\widetilde{V}_{L}({\mathbf{x}})\,+\,\widetilde{V}_{R}({\mathbf{x}})\,.

(36)

A crucial difference between this case and the bosonic one is that the

\widetilde{V}_{A}
functions do not need to be positive. Indeed, on physical
ground, the bosonic
\widetilde{U}
plays the role of the square of a mass,
unlike

\widetilde{V}
.
We do assume, however, that the
\widetilde{V}_{A}
functions do not vanish
inside the respective regions,
A=L,R
, irrespective of their sign.

Then we see that, as in the bosonic case:

e^{-\Gamma(V)}\;=\;\frac{{\mathcal{Z}}(V)}{{\mathcal{Z}}(0)}\;,

(37)

where

{\mathcal{Z}}(V)\,=\,\int{\mathcal{D}}\bar{\psi}{\mathcal{D}}\psi\,e^{-{\mathcal{S}}(\bar{\psi},\psi;V)}\;.

(38)

Thus,

E(V)\,=\,-\big[\frac{\Gamma(V)}{T}]_{T\to\infty}\,=\,-\,\int_{\not\omega}\log\frac{\det\big(\not\! \nabla+ i \gamma_0
\omega+ \widetilde{V}\big)}{\det\big(\not\! \nabla+ i \gamma_0
\omega\big)}\;.

(39)

Here, we have used the notation
\not\!\!\nabla\equiv\gamma_{j}\partial_{j}
, and the determinant is meant to be over functional as well as
Dirac space.
In (
39
), we take advantage of the fact that the energy is real,
and that the determinant of the adjoint of an operator is the conjugate of
the determinant of the original operator, to make

\displaystyle E(V)\,=\,E^{*}(V)\,=\,-\,\int_{\not\omega}\log\frac{\det[-\not\!\nabla-i\gamma_{0}\omega+\widetilde{V}\big]}{\det[-\not\!\nabla-i\gamma_{0}\omega\big]}\;,

(40)

and then

\displaystyle E(V)

\displaystyle=\,-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\not\omega}\log\frac{\det[(-\not\!\nabla-i\gamma_{0}\omega+\widetilde{V})(\not\!\nabla+i\gamma_{0}\omega+\widetilde{V})\big]}{\det[(-\not\!\nabla-i\gamma_{0}\omega)(\not\!\nabla+i\gamma_{0}\omega)\big]}

\displaystyle=\,-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\not\omega}\log\frac{\det[-\nabla^{2}+\omega^{2}+\widetilde{V}^{2}-(\not\!\nabla\widetilde{V})\big]}{\det(-\nabla^2 + \omega^2)}\;.

(41)

This expression is identical
except for an important global factor

(-\frac{1}{2})
to the one of the multi-component scalar field considered
in the previous Section.
The number of components depends, of course, on the dimension of the
representation of the Clifford algebra generated by the Dirac matrices.

In the mapping to the scalar field case, we make the
identification:

\widetilde{\mathbb{U}}\;=\;\widetilde{V}^{2}-(\not\!\nabla\widetilde{V})\;.

(42)

Hence, the resulting
\widetilde{\mathbb{U}}

is Hermitian and has support on the same regions
L
and
R
defined by

\widetilde{V}
.

Regarding the positive definiteness of
\widetilde{\mathbb{U}}
it is
determined by the relative magnitude of the norms of

\widetilde{V}^{2}
and
(\not\!\!\nabla\widetilde{V})
. We shall assume

\widetilde{V}
, or what is the same, each one of its components

\widetilde{V}_{L}
and
\widetilde{V}_{R}
are such that the
\widetilde{\mathbb{U}}
is positive.

In the Dirac case, we then have:

E_{I}\;=\;-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\not\omega}\,\log\det\,[\widehat{I}_{L}-\widehat{T}_{L}\hat{G}^{(0)}_{LR}\widehat{T}_{R}\hat{G}^{(0)}_{RL}]\;=\;-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\not\omega}\,\,{\rm Tr}\log\,[\widehat{I}_{L}-\widehat{T}_{L}\hat{G}^{(0)}_{LR}\widehat{T}_{R}\hat{G}^{(0)}_{RL}]\;,

(43)

with
\hat{G}^{(0)}_{AB}
diagonal in Dirac space, and
\widehat{T}_{A}

identical to
the ones of the (multicomponent) bosonic case, with
\widetilde{\mathbb{U}}_{A}
determined by
\widetilde{V}_{A}
, as in
(
42
).
Note the important difference posed by the global
(-\frac{1}{2})
factor,
which is crucial for determining the sign of the force.

Let us now consider the issue of symmetry under reflection. Albeit the
conclusion will be identical we need to distinguish between two cases:

d=1
and
d>1
:

3.1
d=1

For
d=1
it is quite clear that if

\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\widetilde{V}_{L}\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\dagger}\,=\,-\widetilde{V}_{R}\;\;,\;\;\;\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\widetilde{V}_{R}\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\dagger}\,=\,-\widetilde{V}_{L}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\widetilde{V}\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\dagger}\,=\,-\widetilde{V}\;,

(44)

then:

\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbb{U}}}_{R}\,=\,\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbb{U}}}_{L}\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\dagger}
.
Thus, under this antisymmetry assumption for
\widetilde{V}
, we may apply
the same arguments of the bosonic case for an
U
which is parity
symmetric. Therefore:

{\rm sign}(\lambda_{n}\partial_{a}\lambda_{n})\,=\,-1
,
\forall n
.

Thus, because of the
-\frac{1}{2}
in (
43
):

{\rm sign}(F)\,=\,+1\;,

(45)

which is a repulsive Casimir interaction. Again, note that the conclusion
does not change if the Dirac field has many flavors and therefore the
previous antisymmetry is realized for the resulting Hermitian matrices

\widetilde{\mathbb{V}}_{L}
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{V}}_{R}
.

3.2
d>1

For
d>1
we postulate the same anti-symmetry as for
d=1
, namely, that
(
44
) holds. This yields, for
\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbb{U}}}
:

\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\gamma_{d}\widetilde{\mathbb{U}}_{L}\gamma_{d}\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\dagger}\,=\,\widetilde{\mathbb{U}}_{R}\;\;,\;\;\;\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\gamma_{d}\widetilde{\mathbb{U}}_{R}\gamma_{d}\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\dagger}\,=\,\widetilde{\mathbb{U}}_{L}\;.

(46)

Since
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\gamma_{d}\widetilde{T}_{L}\gamma_{d}\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\dagger}\,=\,\widetilde{T}_{R}
we find:

E_{I}\;=\;-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\not\omega}\,{\rm log}\big[{\rm det}\big(\widehat{I}_{L}-\widehat{Y_{d}}^{2}\big)\big]\;,\;\;\widehat{Y_{d}}\,\equiv\,\sqrt{\widehat{T}_{L}}\hat{G}^{(0)}_{LR}\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\gamma_{d}\sqrt{\widehat{T}_{L}}\;.

(47)

Now, following the same procedure as for the real scalar field
case, we introduce
|\phi_{n}\rangle
, now the normalized eigenvectors of

\widehat{Y}_{d}
(note that they cannot be chosen to be, simultaneously,
eigenvectors of
\gamma_{d}
) and arrive to the study of the sign of

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{n}|\widehat{Z}_{d}|\phi_{n}\rangle\,\langle\phi_{n}|\partial_{a}\widehat{Z}_{d}|\phi_{n}\rangle\;,

(48)

with
\widehat{Z}_{d}\,=\,\widehat{Z}\,\gamma_{d}
, where

\widehat{Z}=\hat{G}^{(0)}_{LR}\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
.

Again, we introduce
|\psi_{n}\rangle\equiv\sqrt{\widehat{T}_{L}}|\phi_{n}\rangle
,
and also assume that a representation has been used for the Dirac matrices
such that
\gamma_{d}
is diagonal, each block having
+1
or
-1
as
eigenvalue. Denoting by
|\psi_{n}^{(\pm)}\rangle
the component of the
respective block of

\gamma_{d}
, we get:

\displaystyle{\rm sign}(\lambda_{n}\partial_{a}\lambda_{n})

\displaystyle=\,{\rm sign}\big[\langle\psi_{n}^{(+)}|\widehat{Z}|\psi_{n}^{(+)}\rangle-\langle\psi_{n}^{(-)}|\widehat{Z}|\psi_{n}^{(-)}\rangle\big]

\displaystyle\times\,{\rm sign}\big[\langle\psi_{n}^{(+)}|\partial_{a}\widehat{Z}|\psi_{n}^{(+)}\rangle-\langle\psi_{n}^{(-)}|\partial_{a}\widehat{Z}|\psi_{n}^{(-)}\rangle\big]\;.

(49)

Then we notice that the structure of each one of the averages above is as
follows (see
[
8
]
):

\langle\psi_{n}^{(\pm)}|\widehat{Z}|\psi_{n}^{(\pm)}\rangle\,\equiv\,I_{n}^{(\pm)}(a)\,=\,\int\frac{d^{d-1}\mathbf{k}_{\perp}}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\,\frac{e^{-a\sqrt{\mathbf{k}_{\perp}^{2}+\omega^{2}}}}{2\sqrt{\mathbf{k}_{\perp}^{2}+\omega^{2}}}f_{n}^{(\pm)}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp},\omega)\,,

(50)

where
f_{n}^{(\pm)}
is a positive function (determined by

|\phi_{n}\rangle
) which form we do not need here.

Then,

{\rm sign}(\lambda_{n}\partial_{a}\lambda_{n})\,=\,{\rm sign}\big[I_{n}^{(+)}(a)-I_{n}^{(-)}(a)\big]\times{\rm sign}\big[\partial_{a}I_{n}^{(+)}-\partial_{a}I_{n}^{(-)}\big]\;.

(51)

Assuming
I_{n}^{(+)}(a)>I_{n}^{(-)}(a)
, and noting that, if that is
the case, then, for
\varepsilon>0
,

I_{n}^{(+)}(a+\varepsilon)\,-\,I_{n}^{(-)}(a+\varepsilon)<I_{n}^{(+)}(a)-I_{n}^{(-)}(a)\;.

(52)

Hence,
\partial_{a}I_{n}^{(+)}(a)-\partial_{a}I_{n}^{(-)}(a)<0
.
Therefore,
\lambda_{n}\partial_{a}\lambda_{n}
is negative.

On the other hand, if
I_{n}^{(+)}(a)<I_{n}^{(-)}(a)
the same argument,
after
(+)\leftrightarrow(-)
, leads to identical conclusion about the
sign.

Since, for the Dirac field, the force in terms of the eigenvalues

\lambda_{n}
is:

F=-\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\sum_{n}\frac{\lambda_{n}\partial_{a}\lambda_{n}}{1-\lambda_{n}^{2}}\;,

(53)

we again conclude that the force is positive, i.e., repulsive.

4
Discussion and Conclusions

It is appropriate to discuss here at least one example of a
\widetilde{V}

function, in order to show how the resulting condition of the positivity
for the operator
{\mathbb{U}}
resulting from (
42
) is
realized.
Positivity of
{\mathbb{U}}
is equivalent to positivity of both its
L

and
R
components. Therefore it is sufficient to consider it for a single
body.

Let us do that for a situation that has been extensively studied:
that of singular
\delta
-potentials
[
11
]
, which may be used
to enforce bag-model boundary conditions.

A regularized version
\widetilde{V}_{\varepsilon}
of
\widetilde{V}
for a
body located at
x_{1}=0
, say, is:

\widetilde{V}_{\varepsilon}(\omega,x_{1})\,=\,\frac{g(\omega)}{\varepsilon}\theta(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}-|x_{1}|)\;,

(54)

such that
\widetilde{V}_{\varepsilon}\to\widetilde{V}
for
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Then, for a normalized, continuous
\psi
:

\langle\widetilde{\mathbb{U}}_{\varepsilon}\rangle\,=\,\langle\psi|\widetilde{\mathbb{U}}_{\varepsilon}|\psi\rangle\,=\,\big(\frac{g}{\varepsilon}\big)^{2}\,+\,\frac{g}{\varepsilon}\big[(\psi^{\dagger}\gamma_{1}\psi)(\frac{\varepsilon}{2})-(\psi^{\dagger}\gamma_{1}\psi)(-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})\big]

(55)

an expression which is clearly positive for a sufficiently small

\varepsilon
.

For this kind of potential, one can use the natural generalization of the
method used in
[
11
]
to prove that an odd potential leads to
a repulsive Casimir interaction.
As an explicit realization of this framework, we may consider the case of
two bodies modeled by
\delta
-like potentials with opposite coupling
constants
g
and
-g
, separated by a distance
a
along the
x_{d}

direction:

V(x)\;=\;g\left[\delta(x_{d})-\delta(x_{d}-a)\right]\,.

(56)

This potential satisfies the required oddness condition for

\widetilde{V}
and allows for an exact computation of the interaction
energy. The result can be written as an integral over the frequency and
momentum components perpendicular to
x_{d}
, denoted by
\mathbf{k}_{\perp}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d-1})
. For spatial dimensions
d=1,2,3
, the vacuum
energy is given by:

\displaystyle E(a)

\displaystyle\;=\;-\,2^{\left\lfloor\frac{d+1}{2}\right\rfloor-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{d^{d-1}\mathbf{k}_{\perp}}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\,\log\left(1-\frac{16\,g^{2}}{(4+g^{2})^{2}}\,e^{-2a\sqrt{\mathbf{k}_{\perp}^{2}+\omega^{2}}}\right)\,

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{a^{d}}\,\frac{2^{\left\lfloor\frac{d+1}{2}\right\rfloor}}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{d+1}{2}\right)\text{PolyLog}_{d+1}\left(\frac{16\,g^{2}}{(4+g^{2})^{2}}\right)

(57)

which is a positive, monotonically decreasing function of the separation

a
, leading to a repulsive Casimir force.

We conclude by summarizing some of the aspects of the result we present in
this article. First, we wish to remark that the property of the media being
described by an odd function is a sufficient condition for repulsion,
although not a necessary one. For instance, one also has repulsion in one of the
systems considered in
[
12
]
, which corresponds to an atom facing a
wall which imposes bag boundary conditions. The force is repulsive, but
there is no odd function
\widetilde{V}
.

A relevant comment we wish to convey is about the relation of the
result we present, and its dependence on the known result for bosonic
fields: that dependence is at the level of the proof, namely, the
formal analogy between two objects (functional determinants) is what
makes it possible to use one result to prove the other. It is worth
pointing out that the different statistics is what leads to repulsion
(rather than attraction) when relating both functional determinants.

We also wish to elaborate on another important aspect of the approach
we followed: note that we have expressed the Casimir energy for a
Dirac field in the presence of space-dependent mass (the function

\widetilde{V}
in (
35
) ) from the determinant of a
fluctuation operator
{\mathcal{H}}
, defined as follows:

{\mathcal{H}}\equiv{\mathcal{D}}^{\dagger}{\mathcal{D}}\;\;,\;\;{\mathcal{D}}\;=\;\not\!\nabla+i\gamma_{0}\omega+\widetilde{V}\;.

(58)

However, one could certanly have considered the operator

{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}\equiv{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{D}}^{\dagger}
, which has the
same spectrum as
{\mathcal{H}}
, except for the
existence of zero modes. Indeed, that is the content of the
Atiyah–Patodi–Singer index theorem
[
13
,
14
,
15
]

which can be used to relate the spectral asymmetry between
{\mathcal{H}}
and
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
to topological properties of the Dirac
operator.
In cases where such asymmetry exists, in order to evaluate the
contribution of the fermionic modes, one should take the average of the
contributions to the energy due to the operators
{\mathcal{H}}
and

{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}

[
16
,
17
,
18
]
. For the
system we are considering, that would correspond to taking the average
of the energies due to two systems, defined with two values of
the object defined in (
42
), namely:

\widetilde{\mathbb{U}}^{\pm}=\widetilde{V}^{2}\pm(\not\!\nabla\widetilde{V})
. Equivalently, one should take the average of the
energies resulting from
L
and
R
in there original positions, and
the energy when they are reflected.
For the kind of background we are considering, a
space-dependent mass could produce zero modes for domain-wall mass
profiles and in an odd number of spacetime dimensions, as in the Callan
and Harvey mechanism
[
19
]
.
Note, however, that that would require a rather different kind of
configuration: the mass we are considering here
has two spatial lumps (since we are dealing with a Casimir interaction
phenomenon) and it vanishes except for the region occupied by the
bodies.

We conclude by pointing out an interesting problem regarding this kind
of result, about the sign of the Casimir interaction:
for the bosonic version, it would be to know how
far a departure from a perfectly symmetric configuration can go without
changing the conclusion, i.e., attractive force. And its equivalent for the
Dirac field case and departures from a perfectly odd configuration.
