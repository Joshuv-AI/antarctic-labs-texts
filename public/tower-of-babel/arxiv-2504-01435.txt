Relativistic quantum Otto heat engine using a three-level Unruh-DeWitt detector
Tomoya Hirotani, Kensuke Gallock-Yoshimura

Abstract
In this study, we explore a relativistic quantum Otto heat engine with a qutrit as the working substance interacting with a quantum scalar field in curved spacetime. Unlike qubits, which extract work by simply expanding or shrinking a single energy gap, qutrits allow multiple energy gaps to be adjusted independently, enabling more versatile work extraction in the quantum Otto cycle. We derive a general positive work condition in terms of the effective temperature that each pair of energy levels perceives. Moreover, we discuss additional subtleties that are absent when using a qubit, such as the generation of coherence terms in the density matrix due to interactions.

Relativistic Quantum Otto engine

II.1
Three-level UDW detector

Let us introduce a three-level UDW detector.
Unlike two-level detectors, three-level UDW detectors (and more generally, any
d
-level detectors) can be modeled in various ways, such as employing the spin-1 representations of
SU(2)
or the Heisenberg-Weyl model
[
45
]
.
These models differ in the allowed transitions.
In this paper, we focus on the qutrit model using the spin-1 representations of
SU(2)
.
1
1

1

In quantum optics, this model corresponds to the
\Xi
-type atom (also known as the ladder or cascade type).

For the sake of generality, consider a pointlike three-level UDW detector traveling along an arbitrary timelike trajectory in an
(n+1)
-dimensional globally hyperbolic spacetime.
The total Hamiltonian in the Schrödinger picture
\hat{H}_{\text{S,tot}}
is written as

\hat{H}_{\text{S,tot}}=\hat{H}_{\textsc{S,d}}+\hat{H}_{\text{S},\phi}+\hat{H}_{\text{S,int}}\,.

(1)

Here,
\hat{H}_{\textsc{S,d}}
is the free Hamiltonian of the detector given by

\hat{H}_{\textsc{S,d}}=\epsilon_{0}\ket{e_{0}}\bra{e_{0}}+\epsilon_{1}\ket{e_{1}}\bra{e_{1}}+\epsilon_{2}\ket{e_{2}}\bra{e_{2}}\,,

(2)

where
\ket{e_{0}}
is the ground state, and
\ket{e_{1}}
and
\ket{e_{2}}
are the first and second excited states, respectively.
Each eigenstate
\{\ket{e_{0}},\ket{e_{1}},\ket{e_{2}}\}
has the respective energy eigenvalue denoted by
\epsilon_{0}
,
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
.
In what follows, we express the detector’s free Hamiltonian (
2
) using the energy gaps
\Omega_{01}\equiv\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{0}
,
\Omega_{02}\equiv\epsilon_{2}-\epsilon_{0}
, and
\Omega_{12}\equiv\epsilon_{2}-\epsilon_{1}
:

\hat{H}_{\textsc{S,d}}=\Omega_{01}\ket{e_{1}}\bra{e_{1}}+\Omega_{02}\ket{e_{2}}\bra{e_{2}}\,,

(3)

where
\epsilon_{0}=0
was assumed as there is no loss of generality.
Note that if we set
\Omega_{01}=\Omega_{02}(\equiv\Omega)
then the free Hamiltonian corresponds to
\hat{H}_{\textsc{S,d}}=\Omega(\hat{J}_{z}+\mathbbm{1})
, where
\hat{J}_{z}
is the
z
-component of the spin-1 angular momentum operator.
In this case, each of our energy eigenstate
\{\ket{e_{0}},\ket{e_{1}},\ket{e_{2}}\}
corresponds to the eigenstate of
\hat{J}_{z}
:
\{\ket{-1},\ket{0},\ket{1}\}
, where
\ket{m}\equiv\ket{j=1,m}
.

\hat{H}_{\text{S},\phi}
is the free Hamiltonian of the quantum scalar field, and the interaction Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{S,int}}
is given by

\hat{H}_{\text{S,int}}=\lambda\chi(\tau/\sigma)\hat{J}_{x}\otimes\hat{\phi}({\bm{x}})\,,

(4)

where
\lambda
is the coupling constant between the qutrit and the field
\hat{\phi}
, and switching function
\chi(\tau/\sigma)
determines the time dependence of coupling with
\sigma
being the typical time scale of interaction.
This interaction Hamiltonian is a natural extension of the two-level UDW model by replacing the Pauli matrix
\hat{\sigma}_{x}
with the spin-1 angular momentum operator,

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{e_{1}}\bra{e_{0}}+\ket{e_{0}}\bra{e_{1}}+\ket{e_{2}}\bra{e_{1}}+\ket{e_{1}}\bra{e_{2}})\,,

(5)

which determines how the qutrit transitions through the interaction.
As shown in Fig.
1
, this represents transitions to adjacent energy levels, such as between
\ket{e_{0}}\leftrightarrow\ket{e_{1}}
and
\ket{e_{1}}\leftrightarrow\ket{e_{2}}
, but not to one skipped level, such as between
\ket{e_{0}}\leftrightarrow\ket{e_{2}}
.

Figure 1:
The qutrit transitions represented by the spin-1 operator.

In the interaction picture, the interaction Hamiltonian (denoted by
\hat{H}_{\textsc{I}}
) reads

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\textsc{I}}(\tau)=\lambda\chi(\tau/\sigma)\hat{J}_{x}(\tau)\otimes\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x}(\tau))\,,

(6)

where

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{x}(\tau)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e^{\mathsf{i}\Omega_{01}\tau}\ket{e_{1}}\bra{e_{0}}+e^{-\mathsf{i}\Omega_{01}\tau}\ket{e_{0}}\bra{e_{1}}

\displaystyle\qquad+e^{\mathsf{i}\Omega_{12}\tau}\ket{e_{2}}\bra{e_{1}}+e^{-\mathsf{i}\Omega_{12}\tau}\ket{e_{1}}\bra{e_{2}})\,.

(7)

Here,
\tau
is the proper time of the UDW detector, and
\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x}(\tau))
is the field operator defined along the detector’s timelike trajectory
\mathsf{x}(\tau)
.
The time evolution operator is expressed using the time ordering
\mathcal{T}_{\tau}
with respect to the proper time
\tau
:

\hat{U}_{\textsc{I}}=\mathcal{T}_{\tau}\exp\left(-\mathsf{i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau\,\hat{H}_{\textsc{I}}(\tau)\right).

(8)

Assuming the coupling constant is small, we perform the Dyson series expansion so that

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\textsc{I}}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}+\hat{U}_{\textsc{I}}^{(1)}+\hat{U}_{\textsc{I}}^{(2)}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{3})\,,

(9a)

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\textsc{I}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau\,\hat{H}_{\textsc{I}}(\tau)\,,

(9b)

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\textsc{I}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\Theta(t(\tau)-t(\tau^{\prime}))\hat{H}_{\textsc{I}}(\tau)\hat{H}_{\textsc{I}}(\tau^{\prime})\,,

(9c)

where
\Theta
denotes the Heaviside step function.

II.2
Cycle

Based on the above setup, we now review the relativistic quantum Otto engine (RQOE) depicted in Fig.
2
and calculate the work and heat exchanged in each stroke.
The RQOE consists of two subsystems: a working substance and a bath. In this study, the qutrit serves as the working substance, while the quantum field acts as the bath. The bath that interacts with the qutrit in the first isochoric process (step 2) is referred to as bath I, and the one that interacts with the qutrit in the second isochoric process (step 4) is referred to as bath II.
Below, we assume that the initial state of the joint systems is the product

\rho_{\text{tot,0}}=\rho_{\textsc{d,0}}\otimes\rho_{\phi,0}\,,

(10)

where
\rho_{\textsc{d,0}}
and
\rho_{\phi,0}
are the initial states of the detector and the field, respectively.
Here, the detector is initially prepared in a diagonal state in the basis
\{\ket{e_{2}},\ket{e_{1}},\ket{e_{0}}\}

\rho_{\textsc{d,0}}=\begin{bmatrix}p_{2}&0&0\\
0&p_{1}&0\\
0&0&1-p_{1}-p_{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}\,,

(11)

with
p_{1},p_{2}\in[0,1]
.
We note that the qutrit’s initial state need not be a thermal state. The initial energy gaps are set to
\Omega_{ij}^{\textsc{ii}}
[see top-left in Fig.
2
(a)], where II named after ‘bath II’, which the qutrit interacts at the end of the cycle.
We start with such energy gaps because the cycle must be closed at the end.

The initial state of the field
\rho_{\phi,0}
is assumed to be a quasifree state, which is a particular class of quantum states whose corresponding
n
-point correlation functions (i.e., Wightman functions)
\braket{\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x}_{1})\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x}_{2})\ldots\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x}_{n})}_{\rho_{\phi,0}}\coloneqq\Tr[\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x}_{1})\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x}_{2})\ldots\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x}_{n})\rho_{\phi,0}]
are expressed by two-point correlation functions only.
Examples include the vacuum, squeezed, and Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) thermal states.

(
step-1
)
Quantum adiabatic process
:

The quantum adiabatic process is the process where the energy gaps change very slowly as
\Omega_{ij}^{\textsc{ii}}\to\Omega_{ij}^{\textsc{i}}
without making a contact with a quantum field.
We note that
\Omega_{02}^{\textsc{ii}}(=\Omega_{01}^{\textsc{ii}}+\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{ii}})\to\Omega_{02}^{\textsc{i}}(=\Omega_{01}^{\textsc{i}}+\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{i}})
, and the qutrit’s free Hamiltonian during this process is time-dependent:

\hat{H}_{\textsc{d}}(\tau)=\Omega_{01}^{\textsc{ii}}(\tau)\ket{e_{1}}\bra{e_{1}}+\Omega_{02}^{\textsc{ii}}(\tau)\ket{e_{2}}\bra{e_{2}}\,.

(12)

Moreover, each energy gap can be either enlarged or shrunk, and we will consider each energy gap configuration later.

If the Hamiltonian is changed slowly enough, the quantum adiabatic theorem
[
49
]
ensures that this process can be performed without changing the detector’s state
\rho_{\textsc{d,0}}
.
Thus, the work done on the qutrit,
\braket{W_{1}}
, and the heat it receives,
\braket{Q_{1}}
, during this process are given by

\displaystyle\langle W_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\text{d}\tau\Tr\left[\rho_{\textsc{d,0}}\frac{\text{d}\hat{H}_{\textsc{d}}(\tau)}{\text{d}\tau}\right]

\displaystyle=(p_{1}+p_{2})\Delta\Omega_{01}+p_{2}\Delta\Omega_{12}\,,

(13)

\displaystyle\langle Q_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=0\,,

(14)

where
\Delta\Omega_{01}\equiv\Omega_{01}^{\textsc{i}}-\Omega_{01}^{\textsc{ii}},\Delta\Omega_{12}\equiv\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{i}}-\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{ii}}
are the change in each energy gap.

(
step-2
)
Quantum isochoric process
:

During this process, the qutrit interacts with a quantum field [bath I in Fig.
2
(a)] while its energy gaps remain fixed at
\Omega_{ij}^{\textsc{i}}
.
Note that the detector’s free Hamiltonian reads
\hat{H}_{\textsc{d}}=\Omega_{01}^{\textsc{i}}\ket{e_{1}}\bra{e_{1}}+\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{i}}\ket{e_{2}}\bra{e_{2}}
throughout this process, thus no work is done:
\braket{W_{2}}=0
.
On the other hand, the interaction alters the qutrit’s quantum state as
\rho_{\textsc{d,0}}\to\rho_{\textsc{d,1}}
, which leads to the exchange of heat defined by

\displaystyle\braket{Q_{2}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int\text{d}\tau\,\Tr\left[\hat{H}_{\textsc{d}}\frac{\text{d}\rho_{\textsc{d}}(\tau)}{\text{d}\tau}\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr[\rho_{\textsc{d,1}}\hat{H}_{\textsc{d}}]-\Tr[\rho_{\textsc{d,0}}\hat{H}_{\textsc{d}}]\,,

(15)

Let us obtain the post-interaction density matrix
\rho_{\textsc{d,1}}
of the three-level UDW detector.
We first note that the time dependence of the interaction is specified by a compactly supported switching function
\chi_{\textsc{i}}(\tau)
.
In particular, we will assume that the switching function is symmetric in
\tau
for simplicity (e.g., rectangular function), and we write
\chi_{\textsc{i}}[(\tau-\tau_{\textsc{i}})/\sigma_{\textsc{i}}]
, where
\sigma_{\textsc{i}}
is the interaction duration and
\tau_{\textsc{i}}
is its midpoint.
Thus, its support reads
\text{supp}(\chi_{\textsc{i}})=[\tau_{\textsc{i}}-\sigma_{\textsc{i}}/2,\tau_{\textsc{i}}+\sigma_{\textsc{i}}/2]
.

The density matrix
\rho_{\textsc{d,1}}
is obtained by evolving the total system over time and tracing out the field degrees of freedom:

\displaystyle\rho_{\textsc{d,1}}

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\phi}[\hat{U}_{\textsc{I}}\rho_{\text{tot,0}}\hat{U}_{\textsc{I}}^{{\dagger}}]

\displaystyle=\rho_{\textsc{d,0}}+\Tr_{\phi}[\hat{U}_{\textsc{I}}^{(1)}\rho_{\text{tot,0}}\hat{U}_{\textsc{I}}^{(1){\dagger}}]+\Tr_{\phi}[\hat{U}_{\textsc{I}}^{(2)}\rho_{\text{tot,0}}]

\displaystyle\quad+\Tr_{\phi}[\rho_{\text{tot,0}}\hat{U}_{\textsc{I}}^{(2){\dagger}}]+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4})\,,

(16)

where
\hat{U}_{\textsc{I}}^{(1)}
and
\hat{U}_{\textsc{I}}^{(2)}
are given in Eqs. (
9b
) and (
9c
), respectively.
Since we are assuming that the field’s initial state
\rho_{\phi,0}
is quasifree, only the even-point correlation functions survive in
\rho_{\textsc{d,1}}
, leading to its dependence on the even-power in the coupling constant
\lambda
.

Let us define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{i}}(\pm\Omega^{\textsc{i}}_{ij})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sigma_{\textsc{i}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\chi\left(\frac{\tau-\tau_{\textsc{i}}}{\sigma_{\textsc{i}}}\right)\chi\left(\frac{\tau^{\prime}-\tau_{\textsc{i}}}{\sigma_{\textsc{i}}}\right)e^{\mp\mathsf{i}\Omega^{\textsc{i}}_{ij}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}W(\mathsf{x}(\tau),\mathsf{x}(\tau^{\prime}))\,,

(17a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\textsc{i}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sigma_{\textsc{i}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\chi\left(\frac{\tau-\tau_{\textsc{i}}}{\sigma_{\textsc{i}}}\right)\chi\left(\frac{\tau^{\prime}-\tau_{\textsc{i}}}{\sigma_{\textsc{i}}}\right)e^{-\mathsf{i}(\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{i}}\tau+\Omega_{01}^{\textsc{i}}\tau^{\prime})}W(\mathsf{x}(\tau),\mathsf{x}(\tau^{\prime}))\,,

(17b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{\textsc{i}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sigma_{\textsc{i}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\Theta(t(\tau)-t(\tau^{\prime}))\chi\left(\frac{\tau-\tau_{\textsc{i}}}{\sigma_{\textsc{i}}}\right)\chi\left(\frac{\tau^{\prime}-\tau_{\textsc{i}}}{\sigma_{\textsc{i}}}\right)e^{-\mathsf{i}(\Omega_{01}^{\textsc{i}}\tau+\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{i}}\tau^{\prime})}W(\mathsf{x}(\tau),\mathsf{x}(\tau^{\prime}))\,,

(17c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\textsc{i}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sigma_{\textsc{i}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\Theta(t(\tau^{\prime})-t(\tau))\chi\left(\frac{\tau-\tau_{\textsc{i}}}{\sigma_{\textsc{i}}}\right)\chi\left(\frac{\tau^{\prime}-\tau_{\textsc{i}}}{\sigma_{\textsc{i}}}\right)e^{\mathsf{i}(\Omega_{01}^{\textsc{i}}\tau+\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{i}}\tau^{\prime})}W(\mathsf{x}(\tau),\mathsf{x}(\tau^{\prime}))\,,

(17d)

where
\Omega^{\textsc{i}}_{ij}\in\{\Omega_{01}^{\textsc{i}},\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{i}}\}
,
W(\mathsf{x}(\tau),\mathsf{x}(\tau^{\prime}))
is the pullback of the two-point Wightman function along the detector’s trajectory, and
\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{i}}(\pm\Omega^{\textsc{i}}_{ij})
is the response function.
The response function determines the probability of transition between two energy levels of the qutrit.
For example,
\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{i}}(\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{i}})
reflects the excitation probability from
\ket{e_{1}}
to
\ket{e_{2}}
when their energy gap is
\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{i}}
, while
\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{i}}(-\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{i}})
is for the deexcitation
\ket{e_{2}}\to\ket{e_{1}}
.
Similarly,
\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{i}}(\pm\Omega_{01}^{\textsc{i}})
is the probability of transition between
\ket{e_{0}}\leftrightarrow\ket{e_{1}}
.

The density matrix for the qutrit after the interaction is then
[
45
]

\displaystyle\rho_{\textsc{d,1}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}p_{2}+\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{2}&0&\mathcal{C}_{\textsc{i}}\\
0&p_{1}+\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{1}&0\\
\mathcal{C}_{\textsc{i}}^{*}&0&1-p_{1}-p_{2}-\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{1}-\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{2}\end{bmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4})\,,

(18)

where

\displaystyle\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma_{\textsc{i}}}{2}[p_{1}\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{i}}(\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{i}})-p_{2}\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{i}}(-\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{i}})]\,,

(19a)

\displaystyle\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma_{\textsc{i}}}{2}[(1-p_{1}-p_{2})\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{i}}(\Omega_{01}^{\textsc{i}})-p_{1}\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{i}}(-\Omega_{01}^{\textsc{i}})+p_{2}\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{i}}(-\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{i}})-p_{1}\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{i}}(\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{i}})]\,,

(19b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\textsc{i}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma_{\textsc{i}}}{2}[p_{1}\mathcal{X}_{\textsc{i}}-(1-p_{1}-p_{2})\mathcal{Y}_{\textsc{i}}-p_{2}\mathcal{Z}_{\textsc{i}}]\,.

(19c)

In the case of qutrit-type UDW detectors, an off-diagonal term
\mathcal{C}_{\textsc{i}}
appears, though it is absent in two-level UDW detector models within perturbation theory.
It was shown in
[
45
]
that the off-diagonal coherence term
\mathcal{C}_{\textsc{i}}
vanishes in the adiabatic long-interaction limit if a qutrit is initially prepared in a diagonal state (
11
).
2
2

2

While this statement holds for smooth switching functions such as Gaussian switchings, the term
\mathcal{C}_{\textsc{i}}
can be divergent if one employs sudden switchings, even in the long-interaction limit.

However, this term is nonvanishing as long as the interaction duration is finite, and it leads to some subtleties in the relativistic quantum Otto engine, which we will address later.
Nevertheless, since only the diagonal terms in
\rho_{\textsc{d,1}}
contribute to the heat in (
15
),
\braket{Q_{2}}
is independent of the off-diagonal coherence term:

\braket{Q_{2}}=(\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{2}+\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{1})\Omega_{01}^{\textsc{i}}+\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{2}\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{i}}\,.

(20)

(
step-3
)
Quantum adiabatic process
:

Detach the qutrit from the field and restore the energy gaps to its original width:
\Omega_{ij}^{\textsc{i}}\to\Omega_{ij}^{\textsc{ii}}
.
The heat and work during this process result in

\displaystyle\braket{Q_{3}}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(21)

\displaystyle\braket{W_{3}}

\displaystyle=\int\text{d}\tau\,\Tr\left[\rho_{\textsc{d,1}}\frac{\text{d}\hat{H}_{\textsc{d}}(\tau)}{\text{d}\tau}\right]=-(p_{1}+p_{2}+\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{1}+\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{2})\Delta\Omega_{\textsc{01}}-(p_{2}+\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{2})\Delta\Omega_{12}\,.

(22)

(
step-4
)
Quantum isochoric process
:

Another isochoric process (i.e., the qutrit interacts with the quantum field) is implemented using
\chi_{\textsc{ii}}[(\tau-\tau_{\textsc{ii}})/\sigma_{\textsc{ii}}]
for the switching function.
To ensure that the support of the interaction region does not overlap with the previous isochoric process, we ensure that the supports of the two switching functions are disjoint by implementing
\tau_{\textsc{i}}+\sigma_{\textsc{i}}/2<\tau_{\textsc{ii}}+\sigma_{\textsc{ii}}/2
.
The state of the qutrit evolves as
\rho_{\textsc{d,1}}\to\rho_{\textsc{d,2}}
, and
\rho_{\textsc{d,2}}
can be calculated following the same procedure as
\rho_{\textsc{d,1}}
:

\displaystyle\rho_{\textsc{d,2}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}p_{2}+\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{2}+\delta p^{\textsc{ii}}_{2}&0&\mathcal{C}_{\textsc{i}}+\mathcal{C}_{\textsc{ii}}\\
0&p_{1}+\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{1}+\delta p^{\textsc{ii}}_{1}&0\\
\mathcal{C}_{\textsc{i}}^{*}+\mathcal{C}_{\textsc{ii}}^{*}&0&1-p_{1}-p_{2}-\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{1}-\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{2}-\delta p^{\textsc{ii}}_{1}-\delta p^{\textsc{ii}}_{2}\end{bmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4})\,,

(23)

where
\delta p^{\textsc{ii}}_{2},\delta p^{\textsc{ii}}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{\textsc{ii}}
, and
\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{ii}}(\pm\Omega^{\textsc{ii}}_{ij})
take the same form as Eqs. (
19a
), (
19b
), (
19c
), and (
17a
), respectively, replacing the subscript ‘I’ with ‘II’.
Therefore, the heat and work exchanged during this stroke are

\braket{Q_{4}}=(\delta p^{\textsc{ii}}_{2}+\delta p^{\textsc{ii}}_{1})\Omega_{01}^{\textsc{ii}}+\delta p^{\textsc{ii}}_{2}\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{ii}}\,,\quad\braket{W_{4}}=0\,.

(24)

Finally, to close the cycle, we impose the condition
\rho_{\textsc{d,2}}=\rho_{\textsc{d,0}}
, which implies

\displaystyle\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{1}+\delta p^{\textsc{ii}}_{1}=0\,,

(25a)

\displaystyle\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{2}+\delta p^{\textsc{ii}}_{2}=0\,,

(25b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\textsc{i}}+\mathcal{C}_{\textsc{ii}}=0\,.

(25c)

As we will discuss in more detail in Sec.
IV
, it should be noted that, in order to close the cycle, these coherence terms
\mathcal{C}_{\textsc{i}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\textsc{ii}}
must be eliminated, which may require additional energy.
Although such energy must be accounted for to compute the extracted work properly, its estimation is complicated.
Hence, we focus solely on Alicki’s definition of work
[
50
]
[see, e.g., Eq. (
13
)] and examine the PWC.

III.1
General expression for the PWC

From (step-1) to (step-4), the total amount of extracted work
\braket{W_{\text{ext}}}
reads

\displaystyle\braket{W_{\text{ext}}}

\displaystyle=-(\langle W_{1}\rangle+\langle W_{3}\rangle)

\displaystyle=(\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{2}+\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{1})\Delta\Omega_{01}+\delta p^{\textsc{i}}_{2}\Delta\Omega_{12}\,,

(26)

and the positive work condition (PWC) is expressed as
\braket{W_{\text{ext}}}>0
.
Furthermore, we impose (
25
) to close the thermodynamic cycle.
By following the calculation given in Appendix
A
, the PWC for a qutrit-type RQOE reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\Omega_{01})S(\Omega_{01})\Delta\Omega_{01}+\mathcal{A}(\Omega_{21})S(\Omega_{21})\Delta\Omega_{21}>0\,,

(27)

where
\Omega_{21}^{\textsc{i,ii}}=-\Omega_{12}^{\textsc{i,ii}}
,
\Delta\Omega_{21}=-\Delta\Omega_{12}
, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\Omega_{ij})

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{A}(\Omega^{\textsc{i}}_{ij},\Omega^{\textsc{ii}}_{ij})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left[\dfrac{1}{\sigma_{\textsc{i}}\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{i}}(\Omega^{\textsc{i}}_{ij})}+\dfrac{1}{\sigma_{\textsc{ii}}\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{ii}}(\Omega^{\textsc{ii}}_{ij})}\right]^{-1}(>0)\,,

(28a)

\displaystyle S(\Omega_{ij})

\displaystyle\equiv S(\Omega^{\textsc{i}}_{ij},\Omega^{\textsc{ii}}_{ij})\coloneqq e^{\Omega^{\textsc{ii}}_{ij}/T^{\text{eff}}_{\textsc{ii}}(\Omega^{\textsc{ii}}_{ij})}-e^{\Omega^{\textsc{i}}_{ij}/T^{\text{eff}}_{\textsc{i}}(\Omega^{\textsc{i}}_{ij})}\,.

(28b)

Here,
T^{\text{eff}}_{j}(\Omega)
,
j\in\{\text{I},\text{II}\}
, is an
effective temperature with respect to the two levels gapped by
\Omega
defined as
[
25
]

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{T^{\text{eff}}_{j}(\Omega)}\coloneqq\dfrac{1}{\Omega}\ln\dfrac{\mathcal{F}_{j}(-\Omega)}{\mathcal{F}_{j}(\Omega)}\,.

(29)

The effective temperature defined above is well-posed for two-level quantum systems in the sense that it reduces to the bona fide temperature of the environment,
T^{\text{eff}}\to T
, if the environment is in the thermal state and the two-level system reaches thermal equilibrium.
To be precise, the effective temperature becomes the bona fide temperature if the pullback of the Wightman function satisfies the KMS conditions and the adiabatic long-interaction limit is taken
[
51
,
52
]
.
We note, however, that for general multi-level quantum systems with inhomogeneous energy gaps, the effective temperature is not unique as it depends on which pair of energy eigenstates we are looking at.
For our qutrit system, we have two notions of effective temperature,
T^{\text{eff}}_{j}(\Omega_{01})
and
T^{\text{eff}}_{j}(\Omega_{12})
, and they are inequivalent unless the energy gaps are equal
[
10
]
.
For this reason, we need to specify a pair of energy eigenstates to talk about the effective temperature.
Nevertheless, if the multilevel system reaches thermal equilibrium, every effective temperature coincides with the environment’s bona fide temperature.

We note that the notion of “closing a cycle” is subtle in quantum thermodynamics.
In classical and quantum thermodynamics, it is typically assumed that heat baths remain constant and thereby, one closes a cycle by bringing back the state of a working substance to its initial state.
However, if one considers environments that are subject to change, then closing a cycle must mean bringing back the whole system to their initial state.
This is crucial in quantum thermodynamics, especially when a working substance entangles with the environment, or when one is interested in the non-Markovian behavior of the environment.
In our case, the qutrit is entangled with the quantum field, and so we need to add some extra work to disentangle them at the end.
Although we impose the closing cycle condition only for the working substance in this work, the state of the environment and the correlation developed should be taken into account if one wishes to investigate work extraction in the strict sense.

III.2
Case study of various energy gaps

Analyzing QOEs using two-level systems is very simple since there is only one energy gap.
In particular, the qubit-PWC in terms of
S(\Omega)
defined in (
28b
) can be expressed as
S(\Omega)>0
if the energy gap is assumed to be enlarged during the first adiabatic process.
In contrast to the qubit-type QOE, qutrit-type QOEs have various ways to manipulate each energy gap.
For example, during the first adiabatic process, one could enlarge the gap
\Omega_{01}
between the states
\ket{e_{0}}
and
\ket{e_{1}}
while reducing the gap
\Omega_{12}
between
\ket{e_{1}}
and
\ket{e_{2}}
.
Moreover, the qutrit-PWC (
27
) consists of
S(\Omega_{01})
and
S(\Omega_{21})
influencing each other.
Our aim here is to examine each scenario and compare them to the well-known PWC for the qubit-type QOE.

To begin with, let us consider the simplest scenario of an evenly gapped qutrit,
\Omega_{01}=\Omega_{12}\equiv\Omega
.
It is straightforward to check that the PWC (
27
) in this case is written as
S(\Omega)+S(-\Omega)>0
and is equivalent to that for the two-level system:

\displaystyle\dfrac{T^{\text{eff}}_{\textsc{ii}}(\Omega^{\textsc{ii}})}{\Omega^{\textsc{ii}}}<\dfrac{T^{\text{eff}}_{\textsc{i}}(\Omega^{\textsc{i}})}{\Omega^{\textsc{i}}}\,,

(30)

where we used the fact that
T^{\text{eff}}_{j}(-\Omega)=T^{\text{eff}}_{j}(\Omega)
and
\cosh x>\cosh y\Rightarrow x>y(>0)
.
As described in
[
25
]
, if the pullback of the Wightman function satisfies the KMS condition and the detector interacts with the field for a sufficiently long time, then the effective temperatures become the bona fide temperatures of the field, yielding the well-known PWC in the literature
[
5
,
6
]
,
T_{\textsc{ii}}/\Omega^{\textsc{ii}}<T_{\textsc{i}}/\Omega^{\textsc{i}}
.

Let us now examine various cases where
\Omega_{01}\neq\Omega_{12}
.
We can categorize the three-level quantum Otto cycle by the sign of
\Delta\Omega_{01},\Delta\Omega_{12}
, and
\Delta\Omega_{02}
.
Recalling that
\Omega_{02}=\Omega_{01}+\Omega_{12}
and so
\Delta\Omega_{02}=\Delta\Omega_{01}+\Delta\Omega_{12}
, we have six scenarios to consider.
For an explanation, consider a triple
(\Delta\Omega_{01},\Delta\Omega_{12},\Delta\Omega_{02})
, where each slot can be either
+
or
-
depending on the sign.
For example,
(+,-,+)
means
\Delta\Omega_{01}>0
,
\Delta\Omega_{12}<0
, and
\Delta\Omega_{02}>0
.
Noticing that it is impossible to have
(+,+,-)
and
(-,-,+)
due to the constraint
\Delta\Omega_{02}=\Delta\Omega_{01}+\Delta\Omega_{12}
, we have the following six cases:
(+,+,+)
,
(+,-,+)
,
(-,+,+)
,
(+,-,-)
,
(-,+,-)
, and
(-,-,-)
.
These are depicted in Fig.
3
.
Furthermore, swapping
\Delta\Omega_{ij}\leftrightarrow-\Delta\Omega_{ij}
and the order of the isochoric strokes

yields an equivalent cycle with a different starting point.
For this reason, we mainly focus on the three cases,
(+,+,+)
and

IV
Conclusion and Discussion

In this paper, we considered an RQOE that employs a qutrit-type UDW detector as its working substance and derived the PWC (
27
) in terms of the response functions and the effective temperature.
Here, the effective temperature is understood as the temperature perceived by a specific pair of energy eigenstates of the qutrit.
Thus, unless the energy levels are equally gapped, each level experiences a different temperature.
Nevertheless, once the qutrit reaches thermal equilibrium, these effective temperatures merge into a unique bona fide temperature of the field.
Precisely, this bona fide temperature emerges when the pullback of the Wightman function of the quantum field satisfies the KMS conditions and the qutrit interacts adiabatically with the field for an infinitely long time
[
51
,
52
]
.

While the well-known qubit-PWC can be expressed simply in terms of the ratio of the effective temperature and the energy gap
[
5
,
7
,
25
]
, the qutrit-PWC exhibits influences among the three levels, making the expression complicated.
Moreover, qutrits allow for various manipulations of the energy gaps.
This leads to a rich variety of qutrit-PWC.

As summarized in Fig.
4
, the qutrit-PWC can be compared to the qubit-PWC for each pair of energy eigenstates.
As reported in
[
9
]
, we observed that the qutrit-PWC is looser than the qubit-PWC when all the energy gaps increase during the first adiabatic process.
Nevertheless, we additionally found that qutrits can still extract work even when one pair of energy eigenstates fails to satisfy the qubit-PWC, though the set of parameters that realizes such work extraction is restricted.
These aspects are less studied in the literature.

While qubits are the simplest working substance to deal with, they often exhibit exceptional characteristics that are absent in general multilevel systems.
Examining qutrits provides us insight into the quantum thermodynamic properties of generic quantum systems.
In this regard, we close this section by addressing several subtleties that multilevel systems encounter in QHEs.

The first subtlety is the existence of an off-diagonal coherence term in the density matrix.
For a given QOE, the density matrix of the working substance does not have off-diagonal elements provided that the system thermalizes at each stroke.
Even when thermalization does not occur (e.g., due to a finite-time interaction), a qubit does not acquire an off-diagonal coherence term at the leading order in the coupling constant within perturbation theory.
However, three-level systems inevitably acquire off-diagonal elements when thermalization is not guaranteed.
This is problematic for closing a cycle, as the newly acquired coherence term must be reset at the end, which requires additional energy.

Another subtlety, related to the aforementioned coherence terms, is the definition of work and heat in quantum thermodynamics.
In the literature, Alicki’s definition of work
[
50
]
, which is essentially the expectation value of the change in the system Hamiltonian
\braket{W}=\Tr[\rho\Delta\hat{H}]
, is commonly employed.
Under this definition, the off-diagonal coherence terms in the system’s density matrix
\rho
are not accounted for in the work.
Nevertheless, several approaches exist for handling coherence terms.
For example, in
[
53
]
, the contributions of coherence are explicitly incorporated into the definitions of work and heat, and these contributions cancel out at the end of the cycle, yielding a first law of thermodynamics that excludes coherence terms.
In other words, the coherence received from the heat bath is directly returned as work.
When applied to our model, this means that coherence naturally vanishes after one complete cycle.
Furthermore, the presence of coherence allows for additional work extraction, thereby relaxing the PWC compared to the case without coherence.
However, there remains ongoing debate regarding whether coherence should be included in the definitions of work and heat.
For example, Ref.
[
54
]
defines the first law of thermodynamics to include a coherence term, instead of modifying the definitions of work and heat as in
[
53
]
.
In such a case, the coherence term in the first law must be eliminated at the end of the cycle if the coherence is initially absent in the system.
In any case, future research in both RQI and quantum thermodynamics must place great emphasis on the existence of off-diagonal coherence terms.
