Effects of phospholipase D1-inhibitory peptide on the growth and metastasis of gastric cancer cells
Authors: Dongju Kim, Mee-Sup Yoon, Junwon Lee, Shin-Young Park, Joong-Soo Han
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhospholipase D1 (PLD1) contributes to cancer development and progression through its effects on cell proliferation, survival, invasion, metastasis, angiogenesis, drug resistance, and modulation of the tumor microenvironment. Its central role in these processes makes it a promising target for novel cancer treatments aimed at inhibiting its activity and disrupting the signaling pathways it regulates. In this study, we aimed to investigate the effect of PLD1 inhibition on gastric cancer cell growth using a novel peptide inhibitor, TAT-TVTSP. PLD1, which plays a role in cancer progression, catalyzes the conversion of phosphatidylcholine into choline and phosphatidic acid through hydrolysis. To effectively target PLD1 in cells, we engineered TAT-TVTSP by fusing a PLD1-inhibitory peptide (TVTSP) with a cell-penetrating peptide (TAT). We observed that TAT-TVTSP effectively inhibited PLD1 activity in AGS gastric cancer cells. Moreover, TAT-TVTSP significantly inhibited the mammalian target of the rapamycin signaling pathway, including the phosphorylation of key downstream targets such as S6K1, AKT, S473, glycogen synthase kinase-3b, and forkhead box O1. TAT-TVTSP did not induce cell death, but it triggered cell cycle arrest by activating p21 and p27 via AKT phosphorylation. Functional assays revealed that TAT-TVTSP significantly impaired the colony-forming ability of AGS cells, thus inhibiting cell proliferation. Transwell and wound-healing assays revealed that this peptide disrupted the cellular behaviors critical to cancer progression, such as migration and invasion. In vivo, TAT-TVTSP significantly reduced tumor growth in the xenograft model of gastric cancer without any toxicity. Overall, our results suggest that TAT-TVTSP is a novel therapeutic agent for PLD1-mediated cancers.Keywords: Cell signaling cascade, Gastric cancer, Peptide inhibitor, Phospholipase D1, Mammalian target of rapamycin signaling

INTRODUCTION

Gastric cancer ranks as the fifth most common cancer and is the third-highest cause of cancer-related mortality globally (Bray et al., 2018, Li et al., 2023). Surgical resection is mainly used to treat gastric cancer, with radiation therapy and chemotherapy used as adjuvant and neoadjuvant therapies (Schirren et al., 2015). Gastric cancer is treated with various medications, such as 5-fluorouracil, cisplatin, and leucovorin, and different chemotherapy protocols. However, these regimens exert side effects and show diminished efficacy in chemotherapy-resistant patients with gastric cancer (Van Cutsem et al., 2016). Therefore, alternative strategies are needed to treat these patients.

Phospholipase D (PLD) catalyzes the conversion of phosphatidylcholine into choline and phosphatidic acid (PA) through hydrolysis (Cho and Han, 2017). PLD has 2 main isoforms, PLD1 and PLD2, which exhibit distinct activities and cytoplasmic localization (McDermott et al., 2020). PLD1 and its product, PA, regulate various cellular processes, such as vesicle trafficking (Morris, 2007, Roth, 2008), cytoskeletal rearrangement (Cross et al., 1996), endocytosis, exocytosis, secretion (Brown et al., 1998, Dohke et al., 2002, Roth, 2008, Siddhanta et al., 2000), apoptosis (Jiang et al., 2016), and neural stem cell differentiation (Park and Han, 2018). Additionally, PLD1 is involved in many diseases and conditions, including cancer (Cho et al., 2008, Foster and Xu, 2003), Alzheimer’s disease (Cai et al., 2006, Liu et al., 2009), and diabetes (Hammond et al., 1995). PLD1 expression and activity are increased in a range of cancer types, indicating its roles in regulating cancer proliferation, growth, survival, invasion, migration, and angiogenesis (Bruntz et al., 2014, Cho and Han, 2017). Therefore, targeting PLD1 is a potential therapeutic strategy for cancer.

The mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) is a serine/threonine protein kinase from the PI3K-related kinase family and is known to form 2 distinct protein complexes: mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) and mTOR complex 2 (mTORC2) (Jhanwar-Uniyal et al., 2019).

mTORC1 is composed of 3 key elements: mTOR, Raptor (a regulatory protein associated with mTOR), and mammalian lethal with Sec13 protein 8 (mLST8, also known as GßL) (Kim et al., 2013). In addition to these 3 core components, mTORC1 contains 2 inhibitory elements: a proline-rich Akt substrate of 40 kDa and a DEP domain-containing mTOR-interacting protein (Kim et al., 2013). Similar to mTORC1, mTORC2 contains both mTOR and mLST8. Additionally, mTORC2 consists of DEP domain-containing mTOR-interacting protein and 2 inhibitory elements, mSin1 and Protor1/2 (Oh and Jacinto, 2011). However, instead of Raptor, mTORC2 contains the rapamycin-insensitive companion of mTOR (Rictor), which plays similar roles as Raptor (Oh and Jacinto, 2011). mTORC1 regulates the production of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids necessary for cell growth and division and controls the balance between anabolism and catabolism in response to environmental conditions (Saxton and Sabatini, 2017). mTORC1 regulates protein synthesis via the phosphorylation of 2 major substrates, p70 S6 kinase 1 (S6K1) and eIF4E-binding protein, and regulates new lipid synthesis via the transcription factor sterol-responsive element-binding protein (Saxton and Sabatini, 2017). mTORC1 regulates cell growth and metabolism, and mTORC2 mainly regulates cell proliferation and survival via phosphorylation of the AGC (PKA/PKG/PKC) family of protein kinases (Betz et al., 2013). mTORC2 mainly regulates Akt phosphorylation and activation (Oh and Jacinto, 2011). Akt promotes cell survival, proliferation, and growth by phosphorylating various substrates, such as the transcription factor forkhead box (Fox)-O1/3a, metabolic regulator glycogen synthase kinase (GSK)-3β, p53 inhibitor MDM2, p21, and p27 (Kang et al., 2024, Lu and Hunter, 2010, Saxton and Sabatini, 2017). Previous studies have indicated an association between PLD and PA and the activation of mTOR (Fang et al., 2003, Frias et al., 2023, Sun et al., 2008). Rapamycin forms a complex with FK506-binding protein 12 (FKBP12), which inhibits mTOR by binding to its FKBP12 rapamycin-binding (FRB) domain (Yang et al., 2013). PA competitively binds to the FRB domain and competes with rapamycin for binding (Fang et al., 2001). PA stabilizes the mTOR complex (Toschi et al., 2009). However, the action mechanisms of PLD1 and mTOR in gastric cancer remain unclear.

TAT-TVTSP is a PLD1-inhibitory cell-permeable chimeric peptide synthesized from TAT, a cell-permeable peptide (CPP), and TVTSP, a PLD1-inhibitory motif (Lee et al., 2018). CPP is a cationic peptide that binds to genes, proteins, and nanoparticles and delivers them to cells. TAT is a peptide derived from human immunodeficiency virus-1 that is internalized via clathrin-mediated endocytosis and micropinocytosis (Zhang et al., 2012). TVTSP is a PLD1-inhibitory peptide derived from AP180 (Cho et al., 2011). AP180, a clathrin assembly protein involved in the formation of clathrin-coated vesicles that mediate protein and lipid transport between intracellular organelles, binds to PLD1 and inhibits its activity (Lee et al., 2000). TVTSP is an AP180 motif that binds to PLD1 (Cho et al., 2011). We previously demonstrated that TAT-TVTSP inhibits PLD1 activity in bronchial epithelial cells (Lee et al., 2018).

In this study, we aimed to investigate the effects of TAT-TVTSP against gastric cancer using AGS gastric cancer cells and a mouse xenograft model. We observed that TAT-TVSTP inhibited PLD1 activity in AGS cells and reduced cancer cell growth, migration, and invasion. Furthermore, TAT-TVTSP inhibited tumor growth in the mouse xenograft model. This study suggests TAT-TVTSP as a new candidate for gastric cancer treatment.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

TAT peptide (GRKKRRQRRR) and TAT-TVTSP (GRKKRRQRRRTVTSP) were synthesized by Peptron. PA was purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids Inc. Torin1, an mTORC1/C2 inhibitor, was purchased from Selleck Chem. Antibodies against β-actin (#4970S), p70 S6 kinase (#9202S), p-p70 S6 kinase (T389; #9205S), AKT (#9272S), p-AKT (S473; #9271S), FoxO1 (#2880S), p-FoxO1 S256 (#9461S), GSK-3β (#9315S), p-GSK-3β (S9; #9323S), and p21 (#37543) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. Antibody against p-p21 (T145; #SC-20220-R) was purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. Antibody against p27 (#610242) was obtained from BD Life Sciences. Antibody against p-p27 (T157; #AF1555) was purchased from R&D Systems. Antibody against calnexin (#ADI-SPA-860-F) was purchased from Enzo Life Sciences.

Cell Culture

AGS gastric cancer cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection. AGS cells were cultured in the Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 medium (Cytiva) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Cytiva) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Invitrogen) at 37℃ in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Western Blotting Assay

Whole-cell extracts were prepared using the radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP40, and 0.25% Na-deoxycholate) with a complete mini protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche Diagnostics), phosphatase inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (Sigma-Aldrich). Protein samples (10-30 μg) were loaded onto the acrylamide gel and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Cytiva) after electrophoresis. After blocking with 5% nonfat dried milk for 1 hour, the membranes were first incubated with primary antibodies for overnight at 4℃ and then with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-mouse IgG [#7076] and anti-rabbit IgG [#7074]; 1:2,000; New England Biolabs). Specific bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific). ImageJ software (v1.53e, NIH, http://imagej.nih.gov/ij) was used for the densitometry of the resulting bands.

PLD Activity Assay

The PLD activity assay was performed using the Amplex Red PLD assay kit (#A12219; Invitrogen). AGS cells were cultured in a serum-free medium overnight at 37℃ before treatment with the peptide. The cells were pretreated with 100 μM TAT or 100 μM TAT-TVTSP for 2 hours, stimulated with serum (10% fetal bovine serum [FBS]) for 30 minutes, and lysed with 1× reaction buffer with 1% Triton X-100 for 3 quick freeze-thaw cycles. Then, 100 μl of the diluted sample containing 50 μg of total whole lysate was used to perform the assay. Fluorescences were measured with a microplate fluorometer (Thermo Scientific Fluoroskan FL; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at an excitation wavelength of 540 nm and an emission wavelength of 595 nm.

Cell Viability Assay/3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-Diphenyltetrazolium Bromide Assay

AGS cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 10 units/well. After 24 hours, the cells were treated with 100 μM TAT or 100 μM TAT-TVTSP in a culture medium and incubated for 48 hours at 37℃. 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) (Duchefa) was dissolved at 5 mg/ml in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Cytiva). The stock solution was filtered through a Millipore filter (pore size: 0.22 µm), added to the culture medium at a dilution of 1:10, and incubated at 37°C for 4 hours. Formazan crystals, which were formed via the reduction of tetrazolium by the mitochondria of living cells, were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (Sigma-Aldrich). Subsequently, the sample optical density was measured at 595 nm with a microplate reader (BioRad).

TdT-Mediated dUTP Nick-End Labeling Assay

AGS cells were treated with 100 μM TAT or 100 μM TAT-TVTSP for 48 hours and subjected to the TdT-Mediated dUTP Nick-End Labeling (TUNEL) assay using the TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit (#KTD102-EN; Abbkine, Shanghai, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For positive cell apoptosis induction, the Apoptosis Inducer A (supplied by the manufacturer) was added into the culture medium according to the volume ratio of 1:1,000. Positive cell apoptosis was observed after 48 hours, concurrently with the experimental group of cancer cells. Fluorescence microscopy (Nikon Instruments) was used to identify the TUNEL-positive cells, indicating DNA fragmentation.

Cell Cycle Assay

AGS cells were cultured in the serum-free medium overnight at 37℃ before treatment with the peptide. The cells were treated with 100 μM TAT or 100 μM TAT-TVTSP in the culture medium for 12 hours and fixed with 70% ethanol in PBS. The fixed cells were centrifuged at 1500×g for 3 minutes at 4°C, washed with PBS, and stained with 50 μg/ml propidium iodide (Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.5 μg/ml RNase A (Worthington Biochemicals). The cell cycle populations were sorted via fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACSCanto II; BD Biosciences) at 488 nm.

Colony Formation Assay

AGS cells (5 × 10³) were seeded into a 6-well plate. After 24 hours, the cells were treated with 100 μM TAT or 100 μM TAT-TVTSP, cultured for 2 weeks in the medium, and the peptides were replaced 3 times weekly. Then, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and stained with 0.05% crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich). Finally, the number of colonies was counted using the ImageJ software.

Wound-Healing Assay

AGS cells (5 × 10⁵) were seeded in a 6-well plate and incubated at 37℃ until 90% confluency. The cells were mechanically disrupted using a sterile 100-μl tip, washed with PBS, and cultured for 48 hours with 100 μM TAT and TAT-TVTSP in a growth medium containing 1% FBS. Wound-healing images were acquired at 0 and 48 hours. Wound migration was assessed using the ImageJ software.

Migration and Invasion Assays

Cell migration and invasion were assessed via transwell assay (#3422; Corning Life Sciences). For the migration assay, AGS cells were treated with 100 μM TAT or 100 μM TAT-TVTSP and seeded in the transwell at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells/insert in 500 μl of serum-free medium. The transwell was then placed in a 24-well plate in the culture medium and incubated at 37℃ in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 for 24 hours. Then, the transwell was removed, and the inner surface of the well was wiped with a cotton tip to remove the nonmigratory cells. Migratory cells on the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with 4% PFA and stained with 0.05% crystal violet. Cell migration was assessed using the ImageJ software. For the invasion assay, the inner surface of the transwell filter was coated with Matrigel (#356230; Corning Life Sciences). Matrigel was diluted 1:5 in the serum-free medium, added to the transwell chambers, and incubated at 37℃ for 5 hours. AGS cells were treated with 100 μM TAT and TAT-TVTSP and seeded in the transwell at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/insert in 500 μl of serum-free medium. The transwell was then placed in the 24-well plate in a culture medium and incubated at 37℃ in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 for 48 hours. Then, the transwell was removed, and the inner surface of the well was wiped with a cotton tip to remove the nonmigratory cells. Migratory cells on the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with 4% PFA and stained with 0.05% crystal violet. The invaded cells were assessed using ImageJ software.

Establishment of a Tumor Xenograft Model

All experimental animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Hanyang College of Medicine (Seoul, Korea) (approval number: 2021-0040). Eight-week-old female nude BALB/c mice were purchased from Orient Bio (Daejeon) and randomly divided into 3 groups: saline, 1 mg/kg TAT, and 1 mg/kg TAT-TVTSP groups. After a week of acclimatization, 1 × 10⁶ AGS cells in ice-cold PBS were mixed with 100 μl of Matrigel (#354248; Corning Life Sciences), and the mixture was subcutaneously injected into the right flank of 4 mice in each group. After 1 week, saline, 1 mg/kg TAT, or 1 mg/kg TAT-TVTSP (n = 4 in each group) were intraperitoneally administered to the mice 3 times weekly for 21 days. Subsequently, the animals were sacrificed, and the tumors were removed and weighed.

Statistical Analyses

Quantitative data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean. All measurements were performed in triplicate for each experiment. One-way analysis of variances followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons post hoc test were used when comparing more than 2 groups. Significance was set at *P < .05, **P < .01, and ***P < .001.

RESULTS

TAT-TVTSP Inhibits PLD1 Activity in AGS Cells

Here, we synthesized the peptide, TAT-TVTSP, using TAT (CPP) and TVTSP (PLD1-inhibitory motif; Fig. 1A). We previously reported that TAT-TVTSP inhibits PLD1 activity in BEAS-2B, a human bronchial epithelial cell (Lee et al., 2018). To confirm whether TAT-TVTSP inhibits PLD1 activity in gastric cancer cells, AGS cells were stimulated with serum after pretreatment with 100 μM TAT or 100 μM TAT-TVTSP for 2 hours. As shown in Figure 1B, PLD1 activity increased in the serum and serum + TAT groups compared to that in the control group. However, TAT-TVTSP reversed this effect. Therefore, TAT-TVTSP effectively inhibits PLD1 activity in AGS cells.

(B) PLD activity was measured using the Amplex Red PLD assay kit. The cells were cultured in a serum-free medium overnight at 37℃ before stimulation with the serum and peptide. The cells were pretreated with 100 μM TAT or 100 μM TAT-TVTSP for 2 hours and stimulated with serum (10% FBS) for 30 minutes. Data are represented as the mean ± standard error of the mean of 3 independent experiments. **P < .01.

TAT-TVTSP Regulates mTOR Signaling

To determine whether PA, a PLD1 product, regulates mTOR signaling in AGS cells, we treated the cells with serum or PA. According to previous study, treatment with serum alone can activate mTOR signaling (Nobukuni et al., 2005); therefore, serum was used as a positive control in our experiments. And we confirmed the phosphorylation of p70S6K1 at T389, a substrate of mTORC1 (Magnuson et al., 2012), and AKT at S473, a substrate of mTORC2 (Sarbassov et al., 2005) (Fig. 2A-C). Moreover, mTOR complex activity was increased in the serum and 300 μM PA-treated cells compared to that in the control cells, indicating that PA regulates mTOR activity in AGS cells. Next, we investigated the effect of TAT-TVTSP on mTOR signaling in AGS cells. The cells were pretreated with 100 μM TAT, 100 μM TAT-TVTSP, or 500 nM Torin1, a mTOR inhibitor, for 2 hours and then treated with serum or 300 μM PA. As shown in Figure 2D, serum or PA increased the mTORC1 activity (phosphorylation of p70S6K1), mTORC2 activity (phosphorylation of Akt S473), and phosphorylation of FoxO1 S256 (Rena et al., 1999) and GSK-3β S9 (Zhou et al., 2013), which are regulated by Akt (Fig. 2D-H). However, TAT-TVTSP and Torin1 inhibited the mTOR complex activity and phosphorylation of FoxO1 S256 and GSK-3β S9 (Fig. 2D-H). These results suggest that TAT-TVTSP effectively inhibits mTOR signaling in AGS cells.

TAT-TVTSP Inhibits Cell Growth and Survival

To investigate the effects of TAT-TVTSP on cancer cell survival and growth, we performed colony formation assays. AGS cells (5 × 103) were seeded in a 6-well plate and exposed to TAT and TAT-TVTSP. Colony quantification was performed 2 weeks after treatment (Fig. 3A and B). TAT-TVTSP inhibited cell survival and growth. To assess the cytotoxicity of TAT-TVTSP, MTT and TUNEL assays were performed (Fig. 3C-E). The results from both assays demonstrated that TAT-TVTSP treatment did not induce cell death or affect cell viability. These findings suggest that the inhibition of cell growth induced by TAT-TVTSP is not due to cytotoxic effects.

p21Cip1 and p27Kip1 are p21Cip family inhibitors (cyclin-dependent kinase [CDK] inhibitors), and their expression is crucial for the regulation of CDK activity (Nakayama and Nakayama, 1998). The stability of p21Cip1 and p27Kip1 is controlled via proteasome-mediated degradation and ubiquitination (Lu and Hunter, 2010). Akt phosphorylates p21Cip1 T145, leading to its cytoplasmic localization. Phosphorylation by Akt disrupts the binding of p21Cip1 to the proliferating cell nuclear antigen, CDK2, and CDK4, consequently inhibiting DNA replication (Li et al., 2002, Rossig et al., 2001, Zhou et al., 2001). To determine whether TAT-TVTSP regulates p21 and p27 protein expression, AGS cells were pretreated with 100 μM TAT or 100 μM TAT-TVTSP for 2 hours and then treated with serum for 2 hours. As shown in Figure 3F, TAT-TVTSP increased the p21 and p27 levels, confirming the effect of TAT-TVTSP on intracellular p21 and p27 protein levels (Fig. 3G-H). Next, we investigated the effect of TAT-TVTSP on p21 and p27 phosphorylation in AGS cells. The cells were pretreated with 100 μM TAT, 100 μM TAT-TVTSP, or 500 nM Torin1 for 2 hours and then treated with serum for 5 minutes. TAT-TVTSP and Torin1 inhibited p21 T145 and p27 T157 phosphorylation compared to that observed in the control and TAT-treated cells (Fig. 3I-K). Therefore, TAT-TVTSP inhibited the phosphorylation of p21 T145 and p27 T157 by inhibiting AKT phosphorylation and upregulating p21 and p27 protein levels, thereby causing cell cycle arrest.

Cell cycle arrest is an important target for cancer treatment (Chu et al., 2008, Huang et al., 2016). To determine the effect of TAT-TVTSP on the cell cycle, propidium iodide cell cycle analysis was performed. The G1 phase cell population increased in the group treated with TAT-TVTSP, indicating that TAT-TVTSP causes cell cycle arrest (Fig. 3L). Therefore, TAT-TVTSP suppresses cancer cell growth without inducing apoptosis via cell cycle regulation.

TAT-TVTSP Inhibits Cell Migration and Invasion

Cancer cell metastasis leads to an aggressive phenotype and adversely affects cancer treatment (Massague and Obenauf, 2016, Suhail et al., 2019). PLD1 is involved in cell mobility in various carcinomas (Chen et al., 2012, Roth and Frohman, 2018). In this study, we performed wound-healing and transwell assays to determine the effects of TAT-TVTSP on cancer cell migration and invasion. In the wound-healing assay, we scratched the plate filled with cells at the bottom and treated the cells with TAT or TAT-TVTSP in a medium supplemented with 1% FBS for 48 hours. TAT-TVTSP effectively inhibited cell migration compared to that in the control and TAT-treated groups (Fig. 4A and B). Furthermore, cells treated with TAT and TAT-TVTSP were placed inside each transwell chamber. After 12 hours, the lower layer of the chamber was stained to confirm the extent of cell migration. As shown in Figure 4C and D, TAT-TVTSP significantly inhibited cell migration compared to that in the control and TAT-treated groups. To check the effect of TAT-TVTSP on invasion, the upper layer of the transwell was coated with Matrigel, and cells were treated with TAT or TAT-TVTSP. After 24 hours, the lower layer of the chamber was stained to confirm the extent of cell invasion (Fig. 4E and F). TAT-TVTSP inhibited cell invasion compared to that in the control and TAT-treated groups. Therefore, TAT-TVTSP inhibits the migration and invasion of AGS cells.

TAT-TVTSP Inhibits Tumor Growth in the Xenograft Model

To investigate the anticancer effects of TAT-TVTSP in vivo, we constructed the AGS xenograft model using BALB/c nude mice. Saline, TAT, or TAT-TVTSP was administered 3 times weekly for 3 weeks. Then, the mice were sacrificed, and tumors were excised (Fig. 5A). To determine the effects of TAT-TVTSP in mice, the weights of mice were measured each time the peptide was administered. No significant differences were observed in the body weights of the experimental groups, suggesting that TAT-TVTSP exerts low toxicity at the tested doses (Fig. 5B). Additionally, TAT-TVTSP significantly decreased the tumor size (Fig. 5C) and weight (Fig. 5D) compared to those in the saline- or TAT-treated groups. These results suggest that TAT-TVTSP effectively suppresses tumor growth without any toxicity.

DISCUSSION

Expression and activity of PLD1, an oncogene, are elevated in various cancer types, facilitating cancer cell growth, survival, and metastasis (Yao et al., 2020). Therefore, targeting PLD1 is a prospective therapeutic strategy for cancer. Although suppression of PLD1 disrupts crucial cell signaling pathways associated with cancer cell proliferation and survival (Hwang et al., 2022, Noble et al., 2018), PLD1-specific inhibitors have not yet been integrated into anticancer treatments.

Peptides often show lower affinity and shorter half-lives compared with antibodies in the body (Fosgerau and Hoffmann, 2015). However, peptides can selectively target and bind to critical cell surface receptors and proteins, including immune checkpoints, receptor kinases, and hormone receptors (Wang et al., 2022). This ability enables them to inhibit or mimic biological functions, which is particularly valuable in the development of multifunctional agents for cancer therapy (Vadevoo et al., 2023). In this study, we developed a novel peptide that inhibits PLD1 and conducted a comprehensive study to assess its therapeutic effects against gastric cancer. This research explores how the unique capabilities of our peptide can be harnessed to improve treatment outcomes in this challenging disease context.

In this study, TAT-TVTSP effectively inhibited PLD1 activity in AGS cells. PA modulated the mTOR complex activity in AGS cells. However, TAT-TVTSP effectively inhibited mTOR complex activity. mTOR signaling plays a major role in regulating basic cell behaviors and affects many cellular functions (Laplante and Sabatini, 2012, Saxton and Sabatini, 2017). PLD and PA mediate cell signaling upstream of mTOR (Frias et al., 2023). PLD and PA are necessary for mTOR activity and the stabilization of mTOR complexes (Nguyen et al., 2018). Elevated PLD activity confers rapamycin resistance by binding to the FRB domain of mTOR between PA and rapamycin-FKBP12 competitively (Frias et al., 2023). Furthermore, PLD1 acts as an effector of RHEB, an mTORC1 activator, in the mTOR pathway (Frias et al., 2020). Here, TAT-TVTSP inhibited mTOR activity, thereby acting as a potential cancer therapeutic. In addition, mTOR plays an important role in tumor angiogenesis. mTOR is involved in the regulation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling, a major pathway of angiogenesis (Wang et al., 2016). VEGF is a major angiogenic factor that contributes to the formation of new blood vessels by promoting endothelial cell proliferation and migration (Shibuya, 2011). mTORC1 may also affect angiogenesis by regulating the stability of hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha, a transcription factor that plays an important role in the response to hypoxia (Karar and Maity, 2011). Under hypoxia, mTORC1 inhibition can lead to the stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha, which induces the expression of proangiogenic factors, including VEGF. This promoted angiogenesis in response to low oxygen levels. However, further studies are necessary to determine the effect of TAT-TVTSP on angiogenesis.

G1 arrest hinders DNA repair and prevents progression to the S phase in cancer cells. This regulatory effect is mediated by cyclins and CDKs. Here, TAT-TVTSP increased the p21 and p27 levels in AGS cells. p21 and p27 are key regulators of cell cycle progression that bind to and inhibit CDKs (Karimian et al., 2016). p21 is a member of the CDK inhibitor family. p21 forms complexes with cyclin A/CDK2, E/CDK2, D1/CDK4, and D2/CDK4, thereby preventing pRB phosphorylation and inhibiting cell cycle progression (Choi et al., 2014, Karimian et al., 2016). Furthermore, p21 plays a crucial role in regulating the expression of genes associated with cell cycle progression (Soria and Gottifredi, 2010). p27 inhibits the catalytic activity of the cyclin D-, E-, A-, and B-CDK complexes by interacting with cyclin and CDK subunits (Abbastabar et al., 2018). To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to show that TAT-TVTSP regulates the cell cycle but does not induce cell death. Here, TAT-TVTSP arrested the cells in the G1 phase. Our results suggest that TAT-TVTSP inhibits cancer cell growth via G1/S-phase cell cycle arrest by modulating the cell cycle regulatory proteins.

In this study, we identified several key questions that remain to be addressed. While we demonstrated that TAT-TVTSP regulates mTOR activity by inhibiting PA, the specific mechanisms through which PA modulates mTOR remain unclear. Additionally, although our findings suggest that TAT-TVTSP can influence the expression of cell cycle regulators p21 and p27, the underlying mechanisms have yet to be elucidated. Furthermore, our use of MTT assay and TUNEL staining has confirmed that cell death is not induced by TAT-TVTSP; however, a deeper understanding of its effects on cellular viability is necessary. Further studies should address these intriguing aspects to fully elucidate the roles and regulatory mechanisms of TAT-TVTSP in cell biology.

In this study, we demonstrated that TAT-TVTSP, a PLD1-inhibitory peptide, regulated mTOR signaling (mTOR/S6K1/AKT/FOXO1/GSK3b) and inhibited cell growth and metastasis in gastric cancer. Moreover, TAT-TVTSP effectively inhibited tumor growth in a mouse xenograft model. Overall, this study highlights the potential use of TAT-TVTSP as a novel cancer treatment agent.
