On-demand design of controlled-release systems using an expert-mimic AI framework
Authors: Ying Qin, Rushuang Zhou, Yali Ming, Bohao Li, Yuning Hu, Donglin Sui, Jingxuan Liu, Hang Wang, Guanghui Ma, Yuedong Yang, Yining Dong, Yufei Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNavigating a vast formulation-preparation design space that is highly sensitive to perturbations makes trial-and-error approaches inefficient and costly for diverse clinical needs. Here, we introduce an expert-mimic AI framework called E-MAF that integrates a process-aware surrogate with an optimized genetic algorithm to emulate coarse-to-fine reasoning, autonomously generating lab-ready designs tailored to desired release profiles. We show that E-MAF delivers on-demand release and shortens design cycles from 6-12 months to approximately 1 hour. We demonstrate that in vitro release, in vivo pharmacokinetics and therapeutic effects match or surpass commercial benchmarks across 7 active pharmaceutical ingredients. We show that E-MAF also enables release profiles that are hard to achieve by conventional approaches, exemplified by ≤2% risperidone burst within 1 h and near-zero-order release over ~28 days in vivo. These results demonstrate E-MAF’s potential to accelerate polymeric controlled-release system development and drug-delivery industrialization.Subject terms: Biomedical engineering, Drug delivery, Drug delivery, Machine learning, Data mining

Introduction

Chronic diseases, from diabetes to cancer, demand therapeutic strategies that maintain efficacy while reducing treatment burden1. Biodegradable polymer-based controlled release systems (CRS), particularly microparticle formulations, were developed to demonstrate time-dependent structure evolution for drug release, sustaining therapeutic effects over weeks to months from a single dose2. Despite notable clinical successes, e.g., Lupron Depot® and Bydureon®, translation from an active pharmaceutic ingredient (API) to a CRS product remains slow and costly, with fewer than 20 products approvals worldwide3.

A major barrier lies in the highly nonlinear and poorly resolved process-structure-performance triad governing CRS preparation and efficacy. Formulation composition and preparation parameters jointly dictate particle morphology, internal microstructure, and degradation–diffusion pathways, such that even subtle perturbations (e.g., ± 0.5% surfactant, ± 40 rpm shear) can shift particle morphology and produce divergent release profile4–7. These sensitivities are further compounded by diverse clinical spatiotemporal-release requirements3,8–10. Analgesics may benefit from an initial burst followed by slow maintenance release, whereas narrow-therapeutic-index agents require stringent control of the initial burst. Together, these factors force CRS development into slow and costly trial-and-error cycles involving fabrication, quality control, in vitro release and in vivo evaluations11,12. Navigating optimal solutions within this high-dimensional and tightly coupled design space is highly challenging. In practice, experienced engineers manage this complexity through a coarse-to-fine strategy. They first use empirical knowledge and mechanistic intuition to narrow the formulation and processing space to a feasible range, after which candidates are refined through targeted experiments guided by release behavior and mechanistic feedback. Although the working loop remains slow, labor-intensive, and highly dependent on expert experience, a key question raises: can expert heuristics and mechanistic reasoning be computationally formalized to enable an information-aware, self-evolving design paradigm that replaces empirical iteration.

Here, we developed an expert-mimic AI framework (E-MAF) that implements a coarse-to-fine search workflow with embedded controlled-release mechanisms (Fig. 1a). Trained on 4246 records from polymeric controlled-release systems spanning 36 APIs, including drug-loading, in vitro release, and pharmacokinetic (PK) data, E-MAF learns formulation-process-performance relationships that capture the spatiotemporal coordination of polymer degradation and drug diffusion, enabling prediction of drug loading and in vitro/in vivo release. Given a target release profile, E-MAF encodes formulation and preparation as dual genetic representations and emulates expert coarse-to-fine reasoning through random exploration followed by genetic-algorithm-driven self-evolution toward viable candidates. It delivered on-demand CRS, with in vivo pharmacodynamics and therapeutic effects matching or surpassing commercial benchmarks. The design timelines were compressed from 6–12 months to ~ 1 h (Fig. 1b) and generate actionable formulation-preparation protocols for profiles traditionally requiring extensive optimizations, such as minimal initial burst and a stable maintenance release (Fig. 1c). Collectively, this workflow establishes a de novo, process-aware design strategy for polymeric CRS and provides a framework for formulation systems governed by tightly coupled process-structure-performance relationships, with potential applications in drug delivery and advanced pharmaceutical manufacturing.

Results

Formulation-preparation-performance database for model training

Data were collected via a regulatory-aligned preparation, characterization, and curation workflow. APIs were encapsulated in microparticles using clinically adopted routes (W1/O/W2, S/O/W, and O/W), followed by solvent removal and solidification. Each batch was logged with composition details and process parameters. As clinically critical quality attributes that determine the dosage feasibility, regulatory acceptability, and therapeutic consistency, drug loading and in vitro/in vivo release profiles were selected as performance indicators. Drug loading was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using API-specific, validated methods. In vitro release profiles were acquired under pharmacopeial conditions (37°C, PBS) and under API-tailored protocol (Supplementary Note S1). In vivo pharmacokinetics of the microparticles were further collected in rats to capture key biological effects such as absorption, distribution, and clearance.

To ensure mechanistic fidelity, feature selection retained experimentally validated descriptors that influence drug loading and in vitro/in vivo release, including formulation variables, preparation variables, and study-context variables where applicable (Supplementary Data 1). Formulation variables, such as polymer molecular weight, LA:GA ratio, and surfactant concentration, represent the thermodynamic attributes of the system by governing viscosity, phase behavior, and polymer-drug affinity. Preparation variables, such as shear speed, duration, and solidification strategy, impart kinetic via shaping transient microstructures and imprinting them onto the matrix. For release-prediction tasks, study-context variables, such as release method, dosage, and injection site, were additionally included to condition the specific in vitro testing or in vivo administration scenario.

The dataset comprises 4246 records collected from the literature and our in-house studies, spanning 36 APIs and covering clinically relevant material combinations, formulation strategies and preparation routes. It encompasses release profiles ranging from rapid burst release to slow, sustained release. Data distributions (Supplementary Figs. S1–S3) illustrate the highly nonlinear and coupled dependence of performance on both formulation and preparation parameters. t-SNE-based feature-space visualization (Supplementary Fig. S4) indicates the broad and diverse spread of the dataset.

Predicting in vitro and in vivo release of de novo process

First, we built a process-aware surrogate that captures process-structure-performance interplay to predict the release profile and replace the experimental evaluation (Fig. 2a). Mean absolute error (MAE) was used as the primary evaluation metric, as it preserves the 0–100% scale of the outputs (encapsulation efficiency and drug release in vitro/in vivo) and directly reports percentage-point deviation. Candidate algorithms were screened and cross-validated using a nested leave-one-out framework (Supplementary Data 2), and LightGBM was ultimately selected for its consistently high predictive accuracy across all tasks (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Subsequently, the model was applied to de novo formulation-preparation combinations, and its predictions were evaluated against in vitro and in vivo release experiments. For the controlled release of a peptide drug, semaglutide, the prediction and experiments showed close agreement. The AE of loading efficiency was 7.35% (experimental 92.65% vs predicted 100.00%; Fig. 2b). In vitro release showed MAE of 7.68%, with R2 = 0.9619 (Fig. 2c), and serum drug concentrations in Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats yielded MAE of 39.16 ng·mL−1 (R2 = 0.8882). Conversion of concentration-time profiles to cumulative in vivo release using the Wagner-Nelson method gave an error of 3.99% (Fig. 2d).

A similar trend was observed for the small-molecule drug, donepezil. Prediction errors were 3.51% for drug loading, 3.79% for in vitro release (R2 = 0.9761), 0.52 ng·mL−1 for serum concentrations in SD rats (R2 = 0.9961), and 0.94% for cumulative in vivo release (R2 = 0.9989) (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Interpretable process-structure-performance analysis and validation

Beyond a black-box predictor, we interrogated the prediction models using SHapley additive exPlanations (SHAP)13,14. The five most influential input descriptors were displayed, where positive (red) and negative (blue) SHAP values indicated their promoting or suppressing effects (Fig. 2e–g).

Across drug loading, in vitro and in vivo release, several descriptors emerged as dominant contributors, including poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) concentration in the oil phase (PLGA_Conc, formulation), microparticle sizes (Size, representing an integrative outcome of multiple process parameters, preparation), and theoretical drug loading (Theoretical DL%, formulation). Mechanistically, these are diffusion-related determinants. PLGA_Conc, Size, and Theoretical DL% are associated with the mass-transfer barrier, diffusion path length, and concentration driving force, respectively. Increasing PLGA_Conc and Size while lowering Theoretical DL% would be expected to increase internal diffusion resistance and reduce the outward driving force, thereby improving loading efficiency and slowing the release rate15.

In addition, the rest feature attribution patterns align with prior studies. For example, the rapid solidification method (preparation) enhances drug-loading efficiency by accelerating solvent removal and matrix consolidation; Higher polymer Mw (formulation) typically suppresses in vitro release by slowing degradation and impeding diffusion; and higher dosing levels (Dosage) yield increased in vivo serum concentrations. Notably, these findings were not imposed as priors but emerged from the model’s interpretation analysis, underscoring the potential of E-MAF to identify process-aware and mechanism-consistent variables.

E-MAF-guided optimization

To evaluate whether SHAP-based interpretation could provide experimentally actionable guidance, we randomly selected a formulation-preparation combination for goserelin (details in Supplementary Note S2). Under this condition, goserelin exhibited a biphasic release profile with an initial lag followed by accelerated, near-sigmoidal release. The model prediction closely matched the experimental trajectory (MAE = 5.50%, R2 = 0.9716; Fig. 2h, i). Time-resolved SHAP analysis at 1, 2, and 4 days showed that internal drug concentration was the dominant positive modulator during both burst and sustained-release phases (Fig. 2j).

Based on this insight, we hypothesized that lowering the internal drug concentration can suppress both early burst and later-phase release. To test this, we decreased the internal drug concentration from 125 mg mL−1 to 62.5 mg mL−1 while keeping all other parameters constant, yielding microparticles with comparable morphology and size (Fig. 2k). The SHAP-modified formulation (SHAP-opt) showed a ~ 55% reduction in burst release at 2 h (1.55 ± 0.13 %) compared with the original (3.39 ± 1.04 %), and a lower cumulative release in the subsequent phase (Fig. 2l). These findings illustrate how interpretable predictors can generate experimentally testable and actionable formulation-adjustment indication for CRS optimization.

Expert-mimic AI workflow for the design of controlled release systems

A major challenge in on-demand design is to automatically optimize formulation-preparation pairs and identify the most suitable and actionable candidates that achieve a target release profile. We treat formulation and preparation as dual genetic codes that synergistically govern the thermodynamics and kinetics in drug loading and release. Building on this concept, we developed an Expert-Mimic AI Framework (E-MAF) that integrates an optimized genetic algorithm to emulate coarse-to-fine expert reasoning and generate lab-ready protocols.

The workflow embedded the prediction model as a scoring engine and proceeds through four stages (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. S7). (i) Generation (coarse-screening): Random search within expert-defined bounds, generates > 1000 formulation-preparation pairs ranked by MAE, yielding a “coarse” feasible region. (ii) Evolution (refining advantaged pairs): An optimized genetic algorithm creates distinct settings by varying mutation rates, and refines candidates using MAE to generate ~ 60 top-fitting pairs per setting. (iii) Convergence (narrowing the settings): Scoring by feature importance and fluctuation for the optimal setting. (iv) Constraint-guided candidate prioritization: the experimentally executable candidate is prioritized based on release-profile fitness and predefined feasibility constraints for laboratory validation.

Benchmarking on-demand design with commercial CRS

Risperidone, a dopamine D2 and 5-HT2A receptor antagonist used for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, requires stable and sustained serum concentrations. To avoid excessive early peaks that may trigger extrapyramidal symptoms, drowsiness, or hypotension16, initial burst must be minimized (< 4% release within 2 h), followed by a near-zero-order release to maintain steady-state exposure and a gradual tapering phase to support discontinuation or switching (Fig. 3b).

We translated this clinical requirement into a target in vitro release profile and applied E-MAF to generate actionable formulation and preparation parameters (see Supplementary Note S3 for full workflow). During the design process, diagnostic tracking demonstrated effective exploration and robust convergence: population fitness increased monotonically across generations (Fig. 3c-I), the number of newly generated formulations declined as the search advanced (Fig. 3c-II), and the population distribution evolved from a dispersed cloud into a compact optimum (Fig. 3c-III), indicating the self-revolution and refinement toward the optimal candidate.

Prepared according to the E-MAF-generated protocol, the resulting risperidone microspheres exhibited smooth and spherical morphology with uniform size distribution (Fig. 3e). Under accelerated testing at 45 °C, the experimental release profile aligned closely with both the model prediction (R2 = 0.9809) and the desired long-acting profile (MAE = 5.13%) (Fig. 3d). Details of the accelerated release method are provided in Supplementary Note S1.

We further evaluated the in vivo pharmacokinetics of AI-designed risperidone microspheres (RIS-M) against the commercial CRS Rykindo® (Fig. 3f, g). RIS-M showed only 0.71 ± 0.50% release at 1 h, fulfilling the design objective of minimizing early burst. At day 1, the fraction released was 9.21 ± 2.65%, significantly lower than Rykindo® (16.13 ± 6.56%). From day 2 to day 14, RIS-M maintained an approximately constant release rate, followed by a gradual slowdown through day 28, matching the intended near-zero-order to tapering release pattern. Compared with Rykindo® at the same dose, RIS-M demonstrated higher bioavailability and extended therapeutic coverage.

To elucidate why RIS-M outperformed the commercial formulation, and to show that E-MAF generates designs based on experimentally grounded principles rather than arbitrary combinations, we conducted a feature attribution analysis (Fig. 3h). SHAP analysis suggested that the enhanced in vivo performance of RIS-M likely resulted from several distinct parameters, including higher polymer lactide content and molecular weight, a different Polymer_End base choice (ester-terminated), a lower Surfactant_Conc (0.86%), and a reduced Drug_Conc in the oil phase (80.57 mg·mL−1).

Physicochemical characterizations validate the rationality of the E-MAF-guided design. In vivo release kinetics indicated that RIS-M release was predominantly governed by matrix degradation, with diffusion acting as a secondary contributor (Supplementary Fig. S8). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images further supported this trend. Minimal surface pores and slow erosion indicated degradation-driven release, whereas progressive matrix deformation suggested internal drug diffusion (Fig. 3i). Moreover, after 28 days, RIS-M retained a substantially higher fraction of its initial normalized number-average molecular weight Mn (approximately 42%) than Rykindo® (approximately 21%) and exhibited a markedly smaller increase in 1/Mn, indicating relatively slower hydrolytic polymer-chain scission (Fig. 3j). Furthermore, FTIR and Raman imaging using the characteristic risperidone absorption band at 1655 cm−1 revealed a more homogeneous drug distribution throughout the microsphere matrix in RIS-M, whereas Rykindo® exhibited pronounced drug enrichment near the particle periphery (Fig. 3k). The combined effects of “reduced diffusion driving” and “slowed degradation” contribute to the superior release performance of RIS-M.

To test the framework in a peptide-drug setting, we applied the framework to goserelin, a gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonist for hormone-dependent cancers. Because excessive early exposure may trigger a transient “tumor flare”17, an ideal long-acting CRS should suppress initial release to allow gradual absorption, and then accelerate release once receptor downregulation occurs, yielding a characteristic sigmoidal, biphasic profile. Guided by this design rationale, we applied E-MAF to design goserelin microspheres (GOS-M) and evaluated their in vivo performance against the marketed ZOLADEX®.

In rats, GOS-M reduced early systemic exposure, showing a lower serum concentration of 2.22 ± 1.06 ng·mL−1 (accumulative release of 0.12 ± 0.03%) at 1 hour post injection, relative to ZOLADEX® (3.65 ± 1.25 ng·mL−1, 0.16 ± 0.05%). Notably, GOS-M maintained serum levels through day 28 (0.84 ± 0.62 ng·mL⁻¹), whereas ZOLADEX® declined to 0.01 ± 0.02 ng·mL−1, demonstrating controlled onset with prolonged maintenance (Supplementary Fig. S9a, b).

Unlike hydrophobic small-molecule drug encapsulation (RIS-M), goserelin is a hydrophilic peptide whose release is more strongly governed by diffusion-related behavior. For this system, E-MAF pinpointed the parameters that critically shape diffusion-driven behavior, such as PLGA_Conc, Size, and Homo_speed (Supplementary Fig. S9c). These observations are consistent with kinetic modeling and the time-ordered evolution of porosity observed by SEM (Supplementary Fig. S10). These findings show that E-MAF does not explore the design space blindly; rather, it converges on a mechanism-informed strategy that enables drug-specific on-demand design.

Advanced therapeutic effects in vivo

To further validate the applicability of E-MAF and its potential for accelerating the clinical translation, we applied it to three therapeutically and physiochemically distinct drugs, semaglutide (amphiphilic peptide), goserelin (hydrophilic short peptide), and paclitaxel (hydrophobic small molecule), and compared their pharmacodynamic performance with the corresponding commercial CRS in relevant disease models.

In a type-2 diabetes model (db/db mice), a single subcutaneous injection of AI-designed semaglutide microspheres (SEMA-M; target and in vitro release profiles in Supplementary Fig. S11) was compared with four weekly doses of Ozempic® (commercial benchmark). Fasting blood glucose was measured after an 8-h fast at each sampling (Fig. 4b). A single SEMA-M dose produced a pronounced and durable glycemic reduction (862.1 mmol·L−1·day), lowering overall glycemic burden by ~36% versus a weekly dose of Ozempic® (1352.9 mmol·L−1·day) and by ~ 62% versus the saline group (2291.0 mmol·L−1·day). Time-in-range (FBG < 11.1 mmol·L−1) nearly doubled with SEMA-M (8.33 ± 1.21 days) relative to Ozempic® (4.33 ± 1.03 days; n = 6 per group). In addition, SEMA-M maintained glycaemic control for nearly three months after a single injection, substantially longer than Ozempic® (4 weeks), with body-weight recovery parallelling improved glycaemia (Fig. 4c).

In SD rats, a single intramuscular dose of GOS-M (target and in vitro release profiles in Supplementary Fig. S12) produced rapid and durable suppression of serum testosterone (Fig. 4d, e). Within the GOS-M group, absolute serum testosterone concentrations decreased from a mean pre-dose level of 7.83 ± 0.90 ng·mL−1, reaching a nadir of 1.36 ± 0.28 ng·mL−1. Because pre-dose testosterone concentrations differed among groups, between-group pharmacodynamic comparisons were performed using baseline-normalized testosterone values. No statistically significant differences in baseline-normalized testosterone AUC0-28d were observed between AI-designed GOS-M and either ZOLADEX® or self-developed GOS-M, indicating that all three formulations achieved sustained testosterone suppression over the 28-day study period. Notably, the final GOS-M formulation was identified through the E-MAF-guided design workflow in less than 10 min, whereas conventional experimental optimization of self-developed GOS-M required nearly 6 months.

In a C57BL/6 melanoma model (Fig. 4f), a single peri-tumoral injection of AI-design paclitaxel microparticles (PTX-M; Supplementary Fig. S13) matched the efficacy of daily paclitaxel dosing for 10 days. By day 10, tumor growth inhibition was 58.50% for PTX-M, comparable to 53.0% with daily dosing, with terminal volumes of 757.89 ± 307.72 mm3 versus 851.32 ± 326.06 mm3, respectively, compared with 1737.11 ± 289.76 mm3 in the saline group (Fig. 4h, i).

Together, these findings show that, expert-mimic design can translate target release profiles into robust in vivo efficacy across APIs with distinct physicochemical properties, while substantially accelerating polymeric CRS development.

Generalization and feedback-guided refinement across unseen APIs

Beyond the main validation examples, we further evaluated E-MAF using APIs that were not included in the original training dataset. Norfloxacin and ibuprofen were selected as unseen small-molecule APIs with physicochemical properties similar to those represented in the training data. For norfloxacin, E-MAF generated a solid-in-oil-in-water microsphere formulation targeting biphasic release, which closely matched the target profile (MAE = 5.88%, R2 = 0.9647; Supplementary Fig. S14). Similarly, for ibuprofen, an oil-in-water microsphere formulation targeting approximately zero-order release over one week also showed strong agreement with the target curve (MAE = 4.01%, R2 = 0.9907; Supplementary Fig. S15). These results suggest that E-MAF can be extended to unseen small-molecule APIs when their physicochemical and formulation characteristics remain related to the learned polymeric microparticle formulation-preparation space.

Furthermore, imiquimod was selected as an unseen hydrophobic small-molecule API and a challenging release profile comprising an initial ~ 60% release within 2 h followed by sustained release over 7 days was designed as the target profile. This case was used as a stress test for E-MAF to evaluate its feedback-guided adaptive optimization capability. The initial design showed a relatively large deviation from the target release profile; after incorporating this experimental result into the feedback loop, the prediction error was reduced by approximately 59% (Supplementary Fig. S16). These additional cases were included to more clearly define the current applicability and limitations of E-MAF, while also demonstrating its potential for adaptive improvement through experimental feedback when encountering underrepresented APIs or release patterns.

Discussion

In summary, we established E-MAF to tackle the longstanding challenge of reconciling the vast formulation-preparation design space while meeting diverse industrial demands. Departs from traditional one-factor-at-a-time optimization, E-MAF operates as an interpretable engineering system that both generates and rationalizes controlled-release designs. Mechanistically, unlike conventional strategies that mainly focus on the local optimization of a limited number of preselected factors, such as DoE and RSM, or mechanistic and semi-empirical equations that are primarily used to analyze experimentally obtained release profiles, E-MAF jointly explores the nonlinear coupling between formulation features (what to make) and preparation parameters (how to make) to automatically generate experimentally feasible designs according to release requirements in practice.

This allows E-MAF to generate on-demand solutions that meet stringent release constraints, exemplified by RIS-M achieving ≤ 2% burst release within 1 h and sustained near-zero-order release for ~ 28 days in vivo, while remaining compatible with realistic manufacturing conditions. In doing so, E-MAF not only reduces development time and cost but also lowers the barriers for non-experts in CRS design and development.

E-MAF also demonstrates translational performance across multiple therapeutics. Across seven clinically relevant drugs, E-MAF-generated controlled-release systems achieved in vivo outcomes comparable to or exceeding commercial benchmarks, while accessing release profiles that are difficult to reach through conventional trial-and-error optimization. Notably, this framework accelerated the development of a long-acting glycemic-control CRS for our industrial collaborator, Huili Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., supporting its progress toward IND approval in China (2025LP02563), highlighting the framework’s industrialization potential.

Challenges still remain for the vast clinical applications of E-MAF. For more complex clinically required release profiles, such as multi-pulse release or profiles involving conflicting requirements across different stages, the model may not readily converge to an ideal solution. In such cases, E-MAF may support a decomposition-based design strategy, in which simpler CRSs with distinct release profiles are designed separately and then blended at appropriate ratios to obtain the desired multi-stage release behavior. Another important consideration for future translation is how pre-clinical in vivo predictions can be related to human pharmacokinetic outcomes. The current in vivo model, trained on experimentally measured rat data, provides a standardized pre-clinical setting for formulation development and validation. Further clinical translation will likely benefit from establishing clinically relevant cross-species mapping relationships as paired pre-clinical and clinical datasets continue to accumulate.

In addition, generalizability to biomacromolecule delivery systems remains limited when these modalities are insufficiently represented in the training data, such as protein therapeutics (Supplementary Figure S17). Extending E-MAF to emerging nucleic acid therapies, including siRNA and mRNA, may require a broader design scope. The development objectives for these modalities may extend beyond sustained release to include transmembrane delivery efficiency, intracellular trafficking, burst release, expression level, as well as stability evaluations. Accordingly, the design objectives of E-MAF may need to be redefined, together with modality-specific descriptor systems and dedicated datasets.

Given their established clinical use and strong process-structure-performance coupling, polymeric CRS served as a representative and practically relevant platform for developing and validating E-MAF in this work. More broadly, E-MAF offers a potential strategy to replace trial-and-error pipelines by integrating formulation and process parameters, as well as expert heuristics within a self-evolving model. With additional formulation variables, process parameters, platform-specific descriptors, training data, and experimental validation become available, this framework is extensible to other delivery systems, where structure-property relationships arise from tightly coupled formulation and process variables, including lipid nanoparticles, responsive polymer networks, and materials systems whose functional performance is governed by complex processing procedures. As such, E-MAF establishes a data-driven, process-aware, and generative framework for on-demand formulation design.

Data collection of polymer microsphere-based controlled release systems

The dataset was primarily constructed from our group’s previously published studies and proprietary research data provided by our industrial collaborator, Huili Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. In addition, a portion of the dataset was supplemented with data from published studies by other research groups25–34. External literature-derived data were curated to supplement the in-house formulation-process database and to improve the diversity and verifiability of the dataset. Literature records were retrieved from the Web of Science database using broad keyword combinations related to biodegradable polymer-based controlled release, including “controlled release”, “sustained release”, “PLGA”, “PLA”. This broad search strategy was used to identify potentially relevant biodegradable polymer-based controlled-release studies before applying feature-consistency and data-completeness criteria.

Studies were considered eligible when they met the following criteria. First, the reported formulation and preparation parameters needed to be consistent with the descriptor format used in our in-house dataset. Third, time-resolved in vitro release, in vivo release, or pharmacokinetic data needed to be obtainable from tables, text, or digitizable figures. When release profiles were extracted from figures, only plots with clearly defined axes, units, and sampling time points were digitized using GetData Graph Digitizer.

Forward prediction model training and hyper-parameter selection

In this study, the forward models for in vitro drug release, in vivo drug release, and drug-loading capacity prediction were developed using a separate model-development and external-validation procedure. Leave-one-formulation-out cross-validation (LOOCV) was conducted within the dataset and was used for model screening and hyperparameter selection, without an additional nested cross-validation procedure. Therefore, the reported LOOCV results reflect internal model-development performance. Independent external-validation formulations were completely excluded from LOOCV, model screening, and hyperparameter selection, and were subsequently used to assess generalization performance on de novo formulation-preparation combinations.

Specifically, one formulation in the development dataset was selected as the validation set, and all the remaining formulations were used as the training set. For release-prediction tasks, LOOCV was performed at the formulation/release-curve level rather than at the individual time-point level. Therefore, all time-point records belonging to the held-out formulation or release curve were excluded from the training set and used only for validation, thereby preventing different time points from the same release curve from appearing simultaneously in the training and validation sets.

Generally, there were three types of forward models in our study, including the in vitro drug-release model, the in vivo drug-release model, and the drug-loading model. All of them were trained and validated using the LOOCV approach within the development dataset. For example, the in vitro drug-release model first learned how to predict the corresponding in vitro drug release for the formulations in the training set. Then, its prediction error on the held-out validation formulation was calculated and recorded. The above training-validation process was repeated until all formulations from the development dataset had been validated once. The optimal hyperparameters of the forward models were determined according to their performances during the LOOCV process within the development dataset.

Heuristic pipeline for expert-mimic AI framework(E-MAF)

The intelligent and generative design of controlled-release systems tailored to clinical delivery requirements remains a critical yet unresolved challenge in the field. Inspired by biological evolution and expert reasoning workflows, we conceptualized the formulation-preparation parameters synergy of CRS as a thermodynamic and kinetic “genetic code” governing their performance. Building on this principle, we developed an expert-mimic artificial intelligence framework (E-MAF) that integrates random search and genetic algorithms to emulate the coarse-to-fine reasoning process of human experts, thereby enabling the intelligent generation of experimentally actionable formulations and processes based on target release profiles and drug properties.

Specifically, E-MAF comprises three modules: a LightGBM model V(⋅) for in vitro drug release prediction, a LightGBM model D(⋅) for drug loading capacity prediction, and a heuristic searching method. The drug loading prediction model D(x) served as a feasibility-oriented constraint. Candidate formulations were first required to meet an acceptable predicted drug-loading criterion before release-profile optimization. On the other hand, fitness is defined as the reciprocal of MAE(V(x),E(x)), which quantifies the closeness between the predicted and the expected drug release. Considering the practical development and translational logic underlying E-MAF, in vitro release matching was used as the primary basis for fitness evaluation during evolutionary optimization because it can be evaluated under standardized conditions, thereby providing a reproducible and robust metric for iterative genetic optimization. The in vivo prediction model was positioned as a subsequent evaluation for assessing the in vivo performance of candidate formulation-preparation combinations. Building on this framework, future extensions of E-MAF could incorporate the outputs of the in vivo prediction model as an additional soft term in the fitness function, thereby supporting the joint optimization of in vitro release and in vivo performance.

Under this fitness definition, the generation of candidate designs with high fitness is of great importance to the inverse design process. Here, we generate a large population of candidate designs using a naïve random search approach. First, the feasible set for the c-th microsphere feature of an arbitrary design x is defined as Zc. Here, the design x is represented by a C dimensional vector randomly sampled from C feasible sets, which can be defined as x=[x1,x2,…,xc],xc∈Zc. Subsequently, the predicted drug loading D(x) of the randomly generated candidates were ranked, and the 80th percentile was used as loading-feasibility threshold. The top 20% of designs that satisfied the constraint were accepted as the initial candidate pool for the evolutionary release optimization.

In E-MAF, each candidate formulation-preparation design was encoded as a mixed-type genome rather than as a purely continuous vector. Each searchable feature was assigned a predefined feasible range or category set according to its variable type and experimental constraints. Continuous variables, such as polymer concentration, surfactant concentration, homogenization speed, and emulsification time, were searched within predefined bounded numerical ranges. In contrast, categorical variables without numerical-order interpretation, such as encapsulation method, polymer end-group type, and solidification method, were handled through replacement within their corresponding allowed category sets rather than numerical perturbation.

In our implementation, the genetic algorithm operated within these variable-specific feasible value sets. The mutation probability was set between 0.01 and 0.10 to maintain exploration while reducing excessive disruptive changes in the mixed formulation-preparation parameter space. Crossover was applied at the gene level, allowing each offspring to inherit valid feature values from parent candidates. After crossover and mutation, generated candidates were decoded into complete formulation-process inputs and checked to ensure consistency with predefined formulation constraints, such as the relationship between theoretical drug loading, drug/polymer feed ratio, and encapsulation method. Candidates were then evaluated by the drug-loading model and release-prediction model, and low-fitness candidates were progressively removed through genetic selection.

The proposed genetic algorithm was implemented using the PyGAD library47. After 100 or 500 generations, the designs with the top H=60 fitness in the latest design pool were selected for further analysis, while the remaining designs were removed.

Stability-aware strategy for the automatic selection of design pools

In practice, the final profile of the design pool processed by the heuristic searching method was determined by many initial conditions. Firstly, the accuracy of the forward models for in vitro drug release and drug loading capacity predictions determined the reliability of the generated designs. Secondly, the search process was controlled by the number of parents M and the mutation probability τ. A large M and τ encouraged the algorithm to fully explore the possible designs, but might result in an unstable optimization trajectory. Consequently, we had various design pools by setting different initial conditions for the search process, and each pool had H designs with high fitness. If the errors of the forward models were nearly zero, we could simply select the design with the highest fitness among all the pools for experiments. However, due to the limited sample size of the training dataset, it is difficult to develop forward models with ignorable prediction errors. Thus, the above naïve strategy is risky.

Here, we propose a stability-aware strategy for the automatic selection of design pools. First, various design pools were generated by the proposed heuristic searching method under different initial conditions. Each pool contained H candidate designs. We note that this stability-aware strategy is a robustness-oriented candidate-pool selection procedure, but it is not equivalent to formal predictive uncertainty quantification. In the current implementation, the LightGBM surrogate models provide deterministic predictions without confidence intervals. After the optimal design pool was identified based on the stability score, the final candidate was selected from this pool according to release-profile fitness and predefined feasibility constraints. These constraints included loading feasibility, particle-size requirement, preparation-route compatibility, polymer/material availability, and manufacturability. Human supervision was limited to confirming whether the model-generated candidate could be practically executed under available laboratory conditions, rather than performing open-ended formulation selection.

To evaluate the accuracy and reliability of the E-MAF framework and demonstrate its practical applicability, we designed validation tasks under multiple clinical delivery scenarios. Representative drugs included the hydrophilic short peptide goserelin, the amphiphilic peptide semaglutide, hydrophobic small molecules such as risperidone, paclitaxel, norfloxacin, ibuprofen, and imiquimod, as well as the macromolecular protein model drug ovalbumin (OVA). According to the clinically relevant release requirements of each drug, distinct target release profiles were defined, including: (i) first-order release with minimal initial burst (risperidone, semaglutide); (ii) biphasic release featuring a low burst followed by rapid release (goserelin, norfloxacin); (iii) zero-order release (ibuprofen, paclitaxel, OVA); and (iv) high-burst first-order release (imiquimod).

The workflow was as follows: first, drug-specific physicochemical properties, such as molecular weight, XLogP, hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor count, were encoded as compositional descriptors, with all data retrieved from the PubChem database. The desired release kinetics were then encoded as time-series cumulative-release sequences serving as optimization targets. Additional task constraints, including required particle size or fixed polymer type, were provided to prevent E-MAF from generating experimentally infeasible formulations or process conditions. After E-MAF output the optimized formulation-preparation parameters prescriptions, the corresponding microspheres were prepared according to the predicted parameters. Detailed formulation and process strategies for these validation microspheres are provided in Note S3.

Characterization of in vitro release performance

To efficiently construct and validate the E-MAF framework, in vitro release kinetics of the prepared microspheres were systematically evaluated. For short-term release designs (≤ 7 days), conventional in vitro release testing was performed under physiological conditions. The release medium consisted of 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.2-7.4, sterile), maintained at 37.5 °C with an orbital shaking speed of 80 rpm. For water-soluble drugs (e.g., ovalbumin, OVA), the total release volume was 10 mL. At each predefined sampling point, the suspension was centrifuged at 8928 x g for 5 min, and 1 mL of supernatant was collected as the sample solution. An equal volume of fresh medium was then replenished to maintain a constant total volume. For poorly soluble drugs (e.g., paclitaxel), the total release volume was adjusted to 7 mL; at each sampling point, 6 mL of supernatant was withdrawn after centrifugation (8928 x g, 5 min), followed by replenishment with the same volume of fresh medium.

For long-acting formulations, customized accelerated release methods were developed according to the physicochemical characteristics of each drug to realistically simulate in vivo release behavior and compress the testing duration. The detailed accelerated protocols and their established in vitro-in vivo relationships (IVIVR) are provided in Supplementary Note S1.

In vivo drug release model fitting

To comprehensively elucidate the in vivo drug release mechanisms of the microspheres and verify the scientific rationality of the E-MAF design process, four classical kinetic models, Korsmeyer-Peppas48, Weibull49, Duvvuri50, and Hopfenberg51,52, were jointly applied. These models describe distinct yet complementary aspects of drug release kinetics.

The Korsmeyer-Peppas model differentiates diffusion and erosion-controlled transport in polymeric systems through the release exponent (n), allowing identification of Fickian diffusion, anomalous transport, and case-II erosion mechanisms. The Weibull model provides a flexible empirical description of the overall release curve shape and time scale, characterized by the shape factor (β) and characteristic time (α). The Duvvuri model captures degradation-driven, multi-phase release kinetics, while the Hopfenberg model represents surface erosion processes in geometry-constrained systems, aiding in the identification of complex erosion behaviors. Detailed model equations and parameter interpretations are provided in Supplementary Note S7.

Each mouse was subcutaneously administered 0.1 mL of the prepared suspension.

Testosterone suppression model

As a gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonist, the serum testosterone level serves as a direct pharmacodynamic indicator of goserelin efficacy. Therefore, male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were selected as the animal model to evaluate the pharmacodynamic performance of the E-MAF-designed goserelin microspheres (GOS-M). A total of 18 SD rats (male, 250–300 g, 4-6 weeks) were obtained from Hengrong Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and randomly divided into three groups (n = 6). The positive control groups received either the commercial goserelin implant ZOLADEX® or the self-developed goserelin-loaded microspheres prepared by our laboratory (Self-developed GOS-M), while the experimental group received the E-MAF-designed GOS-M. All formulations were administered at an equivalent dose of 2.4 mg·kg−1. Microsphere formulations were injected intramuscularly, and the dosing calculation and administration procedures were identical to those used in the pharmacokinetic study.

After administration, approximately 1 mL of blood was collected from the orbital sinus at 1 h, 4 h, 6 h, 24 h, 96 h, 168 h, 264 h, 336 h, 432 h, 504 h, 600 h, and 672 h. Samples were centrifuged at 15845 x g for 15 min, and the separated serum was stored at − 80 °C. Serum testosterone concentrations were quantified using a radioimmunoassay (RIA) method.

Subcutaneous melanoma xenograft model

Murine melanoma B16-F10 cells (CL-0319; RRID: CVCL_0159) were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Procell, Wuhan, China). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (100 U·mL−1 penicillin, 100 μg·mL−1 streptomycin). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells were subcultured at approximately 80-90% confluence using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA.

Female C57BL/6 mice (6-8 weeks old, 18–22 g) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. and acclimated for one week before experimentation.

B16 melanoma cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested, washed twice with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended to a final concentration of 1 × 107 cells/mL. Using a 26 G needle, 100 μL of the cell suspension (1 × 106 cells per mouse) was injected subcutaneously into the right axillary region of each mouse. Tumor volume was monitored on days 0, 2, 4, 6, and 10 after treatment initiation and was calculated as V = (L × W2)/2, where L and W represent the tumor length and width, respectively. The maximal tumor volume permitted under the approved animal protocol was 2000 mm3. Animals whose tumor volume exceeded this predefined humane endpoint were euthanized in accordance with the approved ethical protocol. For longitudinal tumor-volume analyses, values unavailable at subsequent scheduled time points because of euthanasia were assigned the protocol-defined maximal tumor volume of 2000 mm3.

When the tumor volume reached 50–100 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 6 per group): Negative control group: received a single peritumoral injection of 0.1 mL PBS; Positive control group: received paclitaxel injection at a dose of 8.57 mg·kg−1, administered peritumorally once daily (0.1 mL per injection); Experimental group: received a single peritumoral injection of E-MAF-designed paclitaxel-loaded microspheres (PTX-M) at a dose of 60 mg·kg−1. Specifically, 185.47 mg of PTX-M powder was accurately weighed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube; prior to administration, 1 mL of suspending agent was added and mixed thoroughly to form a uniform suspension, followed by 0.1 mL peritumoral subcutaneous injection.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using individual animal-level data. For pharmacokinetic analyses, AUC0-28d was calculated from individual plasma concentration-time profiles using the trapezoidal method. For pharmacodynamic analyses, fasting blood glucose AUC, baseline-normalized testosterone AUC0-28d, and tumor-volume AUC were calculated from individual animal profiles.

Data are presented as mean ± s.d. Between-group comparisons were performed using two-tailed unpaired Welch’s t-tests. Test statistics with degrees of freedom, P values, effect sizes (η2), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the differences between group means are reported in Supplementary Data 3. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
