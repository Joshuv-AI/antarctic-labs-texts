Cosmic Lockdown: When Decoherence Saves the Universe from Tunneling
Robson Christie, Jaewoo Joo, Greg Kaplanek, Vincent Vennin, David Wands

Abstract
We investigate how quantum decoherence influences the tunneling dynamics of quantum fields in cosmological spacetimes. Specifically, we study a scalar field in an asymmetric double-well potential during inflation, coupled to environmental degrees of freedom provided either by heavy spectator fields or by short-wavelength modes as they cross out the Hubble scale. This setup enables a systematic derivation of both Markovian and non-Markovian master equations, along with their stochastic unravelings, which we solve numerically. We find that, while decoherence is essential for suppressing quantum interference between vacua, its impact on the relative vacuum populations is limited. Fields heavier than the Hubble scale relax adiabatically toward the true vacuum with high probability, while lighter fields exhibit non-adiabatic enhancements of false-vacuum occupation. Once the system has decohered, quantum tunneling between vacua becomes strongly suppressed, effectively locking the system into the stochastically selected local minimum. This “cosmic lockdown” mechanism is a manifestation of the quantum Zeno effect: environmental monitoring stabilizes enhanced false-vacuum occupation for light fields by preventing them from tunneling.

Introduction

The stability of vacuum states in quantum field theory has long been a concern in cosmology. Scalar fields with multiple minima naturally give rise to the possibility of false vacua, in which the universe becomes trapped in a metastable state with relatively higher energy density. The modern understanding of false vacuum decay began with the work of Coleman and Callan
Coleman:1977py
;
Callan:1977pt
, who showed how tunneling between vacua can be described semi-classically through instanton solutions in field theory. Their formalism was later generalised to gravitational and cosmological settings, first by Coleman and De Luccia who demonstrated how gravitational effects can modify vacuum decay rates
Coleman:1980aw
, and later by Hawking and Moss who analysed transitions in de Sitter space driven by thermal fluctuations over the potential barrier
Hawking:1981fz
. These results established a physical mechanism that could in principle have serious implications for the large-scale evolution of the universe. Related treatments of barrier crossing within the stochastic-inflation framework have also been explored more recently
Noorbala:2018zlv
;
camargo2023phase
;
rigopoulos2023computing
;
miyachi2024stochastic
.

A further example comes from the Standard Model itself. The vacuum expectation value of the Higgs field defines the familiar electroweak vacuum, which gives masses to Standard Model particles and determines the structure of low-energy physics. If one assumes that no additional particles or interactions enter the theory up to very high energies, the renormalization-group flow of the Higgs quartic coupling is fixed by measured Standard Model parameters such as the Higgs mass and calculable loop corrections (dominated by the top quark). With these inputs, the Higgs’ quartic coupling becomes negative and its effective potential develops a second, deeper minimum at very large field values
Sher:1988mj
;
Isidori:2001bm
;
Degrassi:2012ry
;
Buttazzo:2013uya
;
Andreassen:2014gha
. In the Standard Model, the associated tunneling rate to the deeper vacuum is typically calculated to be extraordinarily small, so that the electroweak vacuum lifetime vastly exceeds the age of the universe
Isidori:2001bm
;
Espinosa:2007qp
;
Chigusa:2018uuj
;
Markkanen:2018pdo
(see however
Branchina:2013jra
). Nevertheless, it can be unsettling to realize that our universe could, in principle, transition abruptly into a radically different state—one in which the structure of matter and the forces governing it are altered, effectively erasing the world as we know it.

Reassuringly, the parameters of our universe make such an event extraordinarily unlikely. However, even if the parameters suggested a greater tunneling probability, it would still be important to recognize that these calculations are highly idealized. They are typically performed in Euclidean signature and treat the quantum fields as perfectly isolated, so all evolution occurs simply under a Hamiltonian (and so is unitary). However, in realistic cosmological settings, all fields inevitably interact with a multitude of environmental degrees of freedom, including gravitational perturbations, other matter fields, and possibly hidden sectors. Importantly, in a universe dominated by gravity, its universal coupling ensures that no field is entirely isolated in cosmology. This motivates the central question of this work: how decoherence induced by environmental interactions modifies the standard semiclassical picture of false-vacuum decay and whether it can suppress coherent transitions to influence the fate of cosmological vacua. It also raises the issue of vacuum selection, which instanton calculations often provide little insight about.

Our setup allows us to derive both Markovian and non-Markovian master equations that describe the reduced dynamics of the scalar field in a quartic potential. Since analytic solutions of these equations, even just for the zero mode, are intractable, we solve them numerically using a truncated Fock basis to explore how environmental interactions influence vacuum selection in an inflationary background. Appendix
C

These master equations provide an ensemble-averaged description of the reduced density matrix, which captures the coarse-grained evolution in phase space, represented by the Wigner function. This is contrasted in our results with the dynamics of individual stochastic trajectories, which can exhibit more varied behavior. In this sense, master equations, in particular Lindblad, offer a natural description of vacuum selection under decoherence at the level of averaged, physically observable quantities.

Our analysis reveals several key results:

•

First, the adiabaticity parameter
\tilde{\mu}
controls the relative vacuum populations. If the Hubble rate is small compared to the mass scale set by the potential, the evolution is adiabatic: the field follows the instantaneous ground state and becomes localized in the true vacuum at late times. By contrast, when
\tilde{\mu}
is sufficiently small, the evolution is rapid and non-adiabatic, and the system relaxes to a mixed state with comparable populations in both minima, with a non-adiabatic enhancement of the false-vacuum occupation.

•

Second, we find that decoherence itself doesn’t have much influence on
which
vacuum is chosen. Introducing a nonzero coupling to the environment (
\underline{\lambda}\neq 0
) does not significantly affect the relative probability of ending in the true or false vacuum. Instead, the principal role of decoherence is to suppress quantum interference between vacua, ensuring that once a vacuum is selected the system cannot tunnel through the barrier.

•

Third, the time-dependence of the de Sitter background means that the potential acquires a growing prefactor with time (coming from
\sqrt{-g}
required on the grounds of covariance), which increases the energetic separation between minima and sharpens the wells in phase space at late times. This stretching of the potential in phase space helps to reinforce the outcome of vacuum selection.

•

Fourth, at late times the system has decohered into a definite local minimum, quantum tunneling between vacua is strongly suppressed. Even if the field settles into a false vacuum, continuous environmental “monitoring” or “measurement” induces a quantum Zeno effect
itano1990quantum
;
li2021quantum
, suppressing any subsequent tunneling back to the true vacuum. In this sense, decoherence gives rise to something we call a
cosmic lockdown
mechanism, stabilizing whichever vacuum the system has reached and preventing further evolution through tunneling.

The total Lagrangian

is defined by
S=\int{\hbox{d}}t\,L
, which gives

L(t)=\mathtt{vol}\cdot a^{3}\Biggl\{\frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}-V(\phi)-\frac{1}{\mu}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential m\,\Bigl[\frac{1}{2}\dot{\chi}_{m}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\chi_{m}^{2}+\underline{\lambda}\,G(m)\,\phi\chi^{3}_{m}\Bigr]\Biggr\},

(2.25)

where the homogeneous zero mode
\phi(N)
serves as the system (with dimensions of
\mathrm{mass}^{+1}
), while the continuum of heavy spectator zero modes
\{\chi_{m}(N)\}
acts as the environment (also with dimensions of
\mathrm{mass}^{+1}
in our conventions). We assume that these spectator modes begin in their ground state in the infinite past.

2.2
Stochastic inflation master equation

Coupling to an external bath is not the only mechanism that generates open-system dynamics. In de Sitter space, quantum field modes are continually stretched by the cosmic expansion. Coarse-graining over a physical patch of radius
(\sigma H)^{-1}
with
\sigma\ll 1
defines an effective long-wavelength field, while modes crossing the coarse-graining scale act as stochastic sources that randomly kick the field value with variance
H^{2}/4\pi^{2}
, as described in App.
B
, provided the field mass is small compared to the Hubble scale
Starobinsky1986
;
NambuSasaki1988
;
StarobinskyYokoyama1994
;
Finelli2009
;
Vennin:2024yzl
. This framework, known as
stochastic inflation
, describes the coarse-grained (over a Hubble patch) dynamics of a light field and its canonically conjugate momentum through the Langevin process

\displaystyle\differential\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-3N}}{\mathtt{vol}\,H}\,\pi_{\phi}\,\differential N+\frac{H}{2\pi}\,\differential W\,,

(2.28)

\displaystyle\differential\pi_{\phi}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathtt{vol}\,e^{3N}}{H}V^{\prime}(\phi)\,\differential N\,.

(2.29)

In App.
B
we show that there exists a unique normalised SSE

\differential\ket{\psi}=-i\hat{K}_{S}(N)\ket{\psi}\differential N-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{\pi}\,\hat{\pi}_{\phi}^{2}\ket{\psi}\differential N-i\sqrt{\Gamma_{\pi}}\,\hat{\pi}_{\phi}\ket{\psi}\,\differential W_{\pi},\quad\text{with}\quad\Gamma_{\pi}=\frac{H^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}

(2.30)

which, at the level of field expectation values, reproduces the Langevin equations
(
2.28
) and (
2.29
):

\displaystyle\differential\expectationvalue*{\hat\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-3N}}{\mathtt{vol}\,H}\,\expectationvalue*{\hat\pi_{\phi}}\,\differential N+\frac{H}{2\pi}\,\differential W\,,

(2.31)

\displaystyle\differential\expectationvalue*{\hat\pi_{\phi}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathtt{vol}\,e^{3N}}{H}\,\expectationvalue*{V'(\hat\phi)}\,\differential N\,.

(2.32)

For a double-well potential, these relations describe the stochastic dynamics of the homogeneous component of a test field in a quasi-de Sitter background, provided that the characteristic field mass scale
\mu
remains small compared to the Hubble rate. In the present work we identify this coarse-grained long-wavelength field, averaged over a Hubble patch, with the zero mode of the spectator field used in Sec.
2.1
, so that both the bath-induced GKLS channel and the stochastic-inflation channel act consistently on the same effective degree of freedom.

Averaging the SSE (
2.30
) over noise realisations yields the GKLS equation with a single
\hat{\pi}_{\phi}
Lindblad term,

\partial_{N}\hat{\rho}=-\,i\bigl[\hat{K}_{S}(N),\hat{\rho}\bigr]-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{\pi}\,[\hat{\pi}_{\phi},[\hat{\pi}_{\phi},\hat{\rho}]].

(2.33)

Here
\differential W_{\pi}
is a real Wiener increment. The SSE (
2.30
) can be viewed as a Schrödinger equation with a random, Hermitian Hamiltonian perturbation proportional to
\hat{\pi}_{\phi}
. Equivalently, since
\hat{\pi}_{\phi}
generates translations, it describes a random translation of the state along
\hat{\phi}
. In other words, the evolution is unitary but driven by stochastic Hamiltonian noise, and it reproduces the standard stochastic-inflation Langevin dynamics at the level of expectation values.

2.3
Spectator dynamics with bath decoherence and stochastic inflation

We combine the bath-induced decoherence derived in App.
A
(from the
\phi\,\chi^{3}
interaction) with the stochastic-inflation contribution of App.
B
to obtain a single GKLS equation for the spectator field. The combined effects of environmental interactions and stochastic inflation have been discussed in stochastic variable formulations in
haba2018stochastic
;
haba2019stabilization
, the formulation we present here is quite different in providing a GKLS master equation and explicit pure-state unravelings.

Throughout this subsection we assume the light-field regime
\tilde{\mu}\ll 1
required for stochastic inflation. The resulting GKLS equation is

\partial_{N}\hat{\rho}=-\,i\bigl[\hat{K}_{S}(N),\hat{\rho}\bigr]-\frac{131\pi\lambda^{2}e^{6N}}{512\,\mu^{5}\,\mathtt{vol}}\,[\hat{\phi},[\hat{\phi},\hat{\rho}]]-\frac{H^{2}}{8\pi^{2}}\,[\hat{\pi}_{\phi},[\hat{\pi}_{\phi},\hat{\rho}]],

(2.34)

which governs the coarse-grained dynamics of the spectator field in de Sitter when both the
\chi_{m}
bath and stochastic coarse-graining effects are included. In the notation of (
2.8
) this corresponds to two Lindblad operators,

\hat{L}_{1}\propto\hat{\phi},\qquad\hat{L}_{2}\propto\hat{\pi}_{\phi},

(2.35)

with decoherence rates proportional to
\lambda^{2}e^{6N}
and
H^{2}
, respectively.

In order to see trajectory-wise effects such as localization and random translations when both channels are present, we unravel (
2.34
) with a normalised Itô Stochastic Schrödinger Equation (SSE)
belavkin1989nondemolition
;
petruccione
that combines the structures of (
2.27
) and (
2.30
):

\differential\ket{\psi}=-i\hat{K}_{S}\ket{\psi}\differential N-\frac{131\pi\lambda^{2}e^{6N}}{512\mu^{5}\mathtt{vol}}\Big(\hat{\phi}-\langle\hat{\phi}\rangle\Big)^{2}\ket{\psi}\differential N-\frac{H^{2}}{8\pi^{2}}\hat{\pi}_{\phi}^{2}\ket{\psi}\differential N\\
+\sqrt{\frac{131\pi\lambda^{2}e^{6N}}{256\mu^{5}\mathtt{vol}}}\big(\hat{\phi}-\langle\hat{\phi}\rangle\big)\ket{\psi}\;\differential W_{1}-\frac{iH}{2\pi}\hat{\pi}_{\phi}\ket{\psi}\;\differential W_{2},

(2.36)

where
\differential W_{1,2}
are real Wiener increments with
\mathbb{E}[\differential W_{i}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[\differential W_{i}\differential W_{j}]=\differential N\delta_{ij}
. Ensemble averages of these pure state trajectories over noise realisations yield the solution of the combined GKLS equation (
2.34
). In terms of the general structure in (
2.9
) and (
2.10
), (
2.36
) is the special case with two Lindblad operators
\hat{L}_{1}\propto\hat{\phi}
(treated in a nonlinear, measurement-like form) and
\hat{L}_{2}\propto\hat{\pi}_{\phi}
(treated as stochastic Hamiltonian noise), with the corresponding rates fixed by (
2.34
).

4
Late-time spectator dynamics with bath decoherence and stochastic inflation

In the previous section, we showed that non-adiabatic effects can significantly enhance the false-vacuum occupation of the light spectator-field zero mode during the transition from a kinetically-dominated to a potential-dominated epoch. The natural question is what happens afterwards, over the many e-folds that may still remain until the end of inflation. In this section, we argue that the combination of strong bath-induced decoherence, growing as
\propto e^{6N}
, and Hubble friction drives the spectator into the standard Starobinsky overdamped regime. In this limit the late-time false-vacuum decay is well described by an effective Arrhenius-type hopping rate
ref:chandlerbook
from the false vacuum
F
to the true vacuum
T
.
As discussed in §
2.3
, the coarse-grained spectator dynamics is governed by the normalised stochastic Schrödinger equation

\differential\ket{\psi}=-i\left(\frac{e^{-3N}}{2H\mathtt{vol}}\,\hat{\pi}_{\phi}^{2}+\frac{e^{3N}\mathtt{vol}}{H}\,V(\hat{\phi})\right)\ket{\psi}\,\differential N-\frac{H^{2}}{8\pi^{2}}\hat{\pi}_{\phi}^{2}\ket{\psi}\,\differential N-\frac{iH}{2\pi}\hat{\pi}_{\phi}\ket{\psi}\;\differential W_{2}\\
-\frac{131\pi\lambda^{2}e^{6N}}{512\mu^{5}\mathtt{vol}}\big(\hat{\phi}-\langle\hat{\phi}\rangle\big)^{2}\ket{\psi}\,\differential N+\sqrt{\frac{131\pi\lambda^{2}e^{6N}}{128\mu^{5}\mathtt{vol}}}\big(\hat{\phi}-\langle\hat{\phi}\rangle\big)\ket{\psi}\;\differential W_{1},

(4.1)

where
\differential W_{1,2}
are independent real Wiener increments. For large
N
, the potential term and strong
\lambda
-induced decoherence drive the state towards an approximate eigenstate of
\hat{\phi}
, so that the wavepacket becomes very sharply localized in
\hat{\phi}
,

\langle\hat{\phi}^{2}\rangle-\langle\hat{\phi}\rangle^{2}\approx 0.

(4.2)

We may drop the decoherence-induced noise terms proportional to the
\hat{\phi}
deviation from the mean
(
\hat{\phi}-\langle\hat{\phi}\rangle
) recovering Eq. (
2.30
) and hence the stochastic expectation value equations (
2.31
) and (
2.32
). Due to localization in
\hat{\phi}
we may replace
\langle V^{\prime}(\hat{\phi})\rangle\approx V^{\prime}(\langle\hat{\phi}\rangle)
, and Eqs. (
2.31
) and (
2.32
) reduce to

\displaystyle\differential\langle\hat{\phi}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-3N}}{\mathtt{vol}\,H}\,\langle\hat{\pi}_{\phi}\rangle\,\differential N+\frac{H}{2\pi}\,\differential W,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\differential\langle\hat{\pi}_{\phi}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathtt{vol}\,e^{3N}}{H}\,V^{\prime}\!\big(\langle\hat{\phi}\rangle\big)\,\differential N,

(4.4)

The associated delocalization in the
\hat{\pi}_{\phi}
basis is not problematic here, because the Eq. (
2.31
) is linear in
\hat{\pi}_{\phi}
. When the field mass scale is sufficiently small compared to the Hubble scale, the dynamics is overdamped and the evolution of the expectation value is described by the one dimensional Langevin equation in
N
,

\differential\expectationvalue*{\hat\phi}=-\frac{V^{\prime}\big(\expectationvalue*{\hat\phi}\big)}{3H^{2}}\,\differential N+\frac{H}{2\pi}\,\differential W.

(4.5)

The standard Eyring–Kramers formula for overdamped escape in a multi-well potential
ref:chandlerbook
then yields the mean first-passage time from the false vacuum to the true vacuum,
4

Summary

We have derived a Caldeira–Leggett–type open-system description for the zero mode of a spectator scalar field, together with a light-field stochastic inflation model in de Sitter space, in order to study the interplay between non-adiabatic dynamics and decoherence in an asymmetric double-well potential. This setup captures generic behavior that should also arise in realistic cosmological models but is often overlooked. A finite Hubble rate induces non-adiabatic evolution, exciting the instantaneous ground state and enhancing false-vacuum occupation, especially for light fields. Decoherence then reduces the purity until the state approaches a classical mixture of well-localized configurations in each potential well. The fastest purity decay occurs when the potential and kinetic contributions to the effective Hamiltonian are comparable, so that interference generated during the barrier switch-on is promptly decohered.

After decoherence, inter-well tunneling is strongly suppressed. Once off-diagonal elements in the field pointer basis are erased, sustained tunneling would require coherences that the Lindblad channel continually destroys. In our finite simulation window the false-vacuum occupation saturates with no drift under GKLS evolution, whereas small coherent oscillations persist in the closed system case. After the barrier switch-on the field is left in an excited state with enhanced false-vacuum occupation. As the system Hamiltonian transitions from a kinetically dominated to a potentially dominated regime, the underlying pure-state trajectories become increasingly localized, and any remaining transitions between wells are driven by diffusive stochastic-inflation kicks as UV modes cross into the IR. Over-the-barrier hops from the false to the true vacuum can in principle relax this excited state, but they are exponentially rare and must occur before the end of inflation; in the parameter ranges we explored, relaxation is inefficient and the zero mode typically exits inflation with non-adiabatically enhanced false-vacuum populations.

A.1
Single-oscillator bath in de Sitter

We begin with the four-dimensional action

S=-\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\sqrt{-g}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\phi}+\mathcal{L}_{\chi}-\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}^{(k)}\right)

(A.1)

with the system and environment Lagrangian densities

\mathcal{L}_{\phi}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial\phi)^{2}-V(\phi)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{L}_{\chi}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial\chi)^{2}-\frac{m^{2}}{2}\chi^{2}\,,

(A.2)

and system potential given in eq. (
1.3

(A.5)

To pass to Hamiltonian language we define canonical momenta in the
usual way,

\pi_{\phi}\equiv\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{\phi}}=a^{3}\dot{\phi},\qquad\pi_{\chi}\equiv\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{\chi}}=a^{3}\dot{\chi},

For simulations
N

and therefore

=H{\phi}^{\prime}

We can diagonalise the bath Hamiltonian
which leads to the compact expression for the bath
\chi
oscillator Hamiltonian

\hat{K}_{\rm B}^{(\chi)}(N)=\omega_{\chi}\Bigl(\hat{b}^{\dagger}(N)\hat{b}(N)+\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigr),\quad\text{with}\quad\omega_{\chi}\equiv\frac{m_{\chi}}{H}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{b}(N)=\sqrt{\frac{m_{\chi}\mathtt{vol}e^{3N}}{2}}\chi+\frac{i}{\sqrt{2m_{\chi}\mathtt{vol}e^{3N}}}\hat{\pi}_{\chi}.

If we assume that the system and bath are coupled at

(A.11)

We have split the interaction term
\hat{S}_{I}^{(k)}\otimes\hat{B}_{I}^{(k)}

\hat{S}_{I}^{(k)}(N)=\frac{\mathtt{vol}e^{3N}}{H}\,\hat{\phi}_{I}^{4-k}(N),\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{B}_{I}^{(k)}(N)=\underline{\lambda}\,\hat{\chi}_{I}^{k}(N)

with higher correlators

\phi^{3}\chi

\widehat{W}(k,N)=\Theta\!\left[k_{c}(N)-k\right],\qquad k_{c}(N)=\sigma e^{N}H,\qquad 0<\sigma\ll 1.

(B.1)

Long/short operators are defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{L}(\mathbf{x},N)

\displaystyle=\!\int\!\frac{\differential^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\widehat{W}\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\,\hat{\phi}_{\mathbf{k}}(N),

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{S}(\mathbf{x},N)

\displaystyle=\!\int\!\frac{\differential^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,(1-\widehat{W})\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\,\hat{\phi}_{\mathbf{k}}(N),

(B.2)

so that
\hat{\phi}=\hat{\phi}_{L}+\hat{\phi}_{S}
. For the conjugate momentum (prime is
\partial_{N}
),

\hat{\pi}_{\mathbf{k}}=e^{3N}H\,\hat{\phi}^{\prime}_{\mathbf{k}},\qquad\hat{\pi}_{L}(\mathbf{x},N)=\!\int\!\frac{\differential^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\widehat{W}\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\,\hat{\pi}_{\mathbf{k}}(N).

(B.3)

Differentiating the projected operators generates injection sources from shell-crossing modes,

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x},N)

\displaystyle=-\!\int\!\frac{\differential^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,(\partial_{N}\widehat{W})\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\,\hat{\phi}_{\mathbf{k}}(N),

(B.4)

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}_{\pi}(\mathbf{x},N)

\displaystyle=-\!\int\!\frac{\differential^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,(\partial_{N}\widehat{W})\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\,\hat{\pi}_{\mathbf{k}}(N),

(B.5)

with
\partial_{N}\widehat{W}=(\partial_{N}k_{c})\,\delta\!\big(k-k_{c}(N)\big)
. Below we suppress
\mathbf{x}
. The coarse-graining source for the field is

\hat{\eta}_{\phi}(N)=\!\int\!\frac{\differential^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,[-\partial_{N}\widehat{W}(k,N)]\,\hat{\phi}_{\mathbf{k}}(N).

(B.6)

Using

\langle\hat{\phi}_{\mathbf{k}}(N)\hat{\phi}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(N^{\prime})\rangle=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime})|\phi_{k}(N)|^{2}

and converting
\delta\!\big(k_{c}(N)-k_{c}(N^{\prime})\big)
to
\delta(N-N^{\prime})
, one finds

\langle\hat{\eta}_{\phi}(N)\hat{\eta}_{\phi}(N^{\prime})\rangle=\frac{k_{c}^{3}(N)}{2\pi^{2}}\,|\phi_{k_{c}}(N)|^{2}\,\delta(N-N^{\prime}).

(B.7)

For a massless scalar in de Sitter, the superhorizon mode function gives
|\phi_{k}(N)|^{2}\simeq H^{2}/(2k^{3})
, and evaluating on the shell
k=k_{c}=\sigma e^{N}H
yields

|\phi_{k_{c}}(N)|^{2}\simeq\frac{H^{2}}{2k_{c}^{3}},\qquad\Rightarrow\quad\langle\hat{\eta}_{\phi}(N)\hat{\eta}_{\phi}(N^{\prime})\rangle=\left(\frac{H}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\delta(N-N^{\prime}),

(B.8)

which is independent of
\sigma
. Defining analogously

\hat{\eta}_{\pi}(N)=\!\int\!\frac{\differential^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,[-\partial_{N}\widehat{W}(k,N)]\,\hat{\pi}_{\mathbf{k}}(N),\qquad\hat{\pi}_{\mathbf{k}}=e^{3N}H\,\hat{\phi}^{\prime}_{\mathbf{k}},

(B.9)

one obtains

\langle\hat{\eta}_{\pi}(N)\hat{\eta}_{\pi}(N^{\prime})\rangle=\frac{k_{c}^{3}(N)}{2\pi^{2}}\,|\pi_{k_{c}}(N)|^{2}\,\delta(N-N^{\prime}).

(B.10)

For a massless field on superhorizon scales
\phi^{\prime}_{k}=\mathcal{O}\!\big((k/(e^{N}H))^{2}\phi_{k}\big)
, so on
k=k_{c}=\sigma e^{N}H
,

|\pi_{k_{c}}|^{2}=e^{6N}H^{2}\,|\phi^{\prime}_{k_{c}}|^{2}=\frac{e^{6N}H^{2}}{2}\,\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{4})\,\frac{H^{2}}{k_{c}^{3}},

(B.11)

and therefore

\langle\hat{\eta}_{\pi}(N)\hat{\eta}_{\pi}(N^{\prime})\rangle=\frac{e^{6N}H^{4}}{4\pi^{2}}\,\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{4})\,\delta(N-N^{\prime}).

(B.12)

Since this is subleading in
\sigma
, we neglect momentum injection and retain only

\langle\hat{\eta}_{\phi}(N)\hat{\eta}_{\phi}(N^{\prime})\rangle=\left(\frac{H}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\delta(N-N^{\prime}).

(B.13)

We now seek a unitary stochastic evolution that reproduces (
B.13
) at the level of expectation values. We consider the Stratonovich stochastic Hamiltonian

\hat{K}_{\text{stoch}}(N)\,\differential N=\hat{K}(N)\,\differential N+\hat{L}(\hat{\phi},\hat{\pi}_{\phi};N)\circ\differential W,\qquad\hat{L}=\hat{L}^{\dagger},

(B.14)

where
\hat{L}
is kept arbitrary for now. The unitary increment is defined by

\differential\hat{U}=-i\Big(\hat{K}\,\differential N+\hat{L}\circ\differential W\Big)\hat{U},\qquad\differential\hat{U}^{\dagger}=i\hat{U}^{\dagger}\Big(\hat{K}\,\differential N+\hat{L}\circ\differential W\Big),

(B.15)

so that, for any operator
\hat{O}
, the corresponding stochastic Heisenberg equation is

\differential\hat{O}=i[\hat{K},\hat{O}]\,\differential N+i[\hat{L},\hat{O}]\circ\differential W.

(B.16)

Applying (
B.16
) to
\hat{\phi}
and
\hat{\pi}_{\phi}
gives

\differential\hat{\phi}=i[\hat{K},\hat{\phi}]\,\differential N+i[\hat{L},\hat{\phi}]\circ\differential W,\qquad\differential\hat{\pi}_{\phi}=i[\hat{K},\hat{\pi}_{\phi}]\,\differential N+i[\hat{L},\hat{\pi}_{\phi}]\circ\differential W.

(B.17)

We take the system Hamiltonian to be the coarse-grained scalar Hamiltonian

\hat{K}(N)=\frac{e^{-3N}}{2\,\mathtt{vol}\,H}\,\hat{\pi}_{\phi}^{\,2}+\frac{\mathtt{vol}\,e^{3N}}{H}\,V(\hat{\phi}),

(B.18)

for which the drift commutators are

i[\hat{K},\hat{\phi}]=\frac{e^{-3N}}{\mathtt{vol}\,H}\,\hat{\pi}_{\phi},\qquad i[\hat{K},\hat{\pi}_{\phi}]=-\frac{\mathtt{vol}\,e^{3N}}{H}\,V^{\prime}(\hat{\phi}),

(B.19)

using
[\hat{\phi},\hat{\pi}_{\phi}]=i\hat{\mathds{1}}
. Hence the most general stochastic Heisenberg system generated by (
B.14
) is

\displaystyle\differential\hat{\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-3N}}{\mathtt{vol}\,H}\,\hat{\pi}_{\phi}\,\differential N+i[\hat{L},\hat{\phi}]\circ\differential W,

(B.20)

\displaystyle\differential\hat{\pi}_{\phi}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathtt{vol}\,e^{3N}}{H}\,V^{\prime}(\hat{\phi})\,\differential N+i[\hat{L},\hat{\pi}_{\phi}]\circ\differential W.

Taking expectations in a normalised state yields

\displaystyle\differential\langle\hat{\phi}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-3N}}{\mathtt{vol}\,H}\,\langle\hat{\pi}_{\phi}\rangle\,\differential N+\langle i[\hat{L},\hat{\phi}]\rangle\circ\differential W,

(B.21)

\displaystyle\differential\langle\hat{\pi}_{\phi}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathtt{vol}\,e^{3N}}{H}\,\langle V^{\prime}(\hat{\phi})\rangle\,\differential N+\langle i[\hat{L},\hat{\pi}_{\phi}]\rangle\circ\differential W.

Stochastic inflation (with momentum injection neglected) requires an additive, state-independent noise of amplitude
H/2\pi
in the
\phi
equation and no noise in the
\pi_{\phi}
equation. Since
\differential W
is common to all trajectories, this means that the operator coefficients themselves must satisfy

i[\hat{L},\hat{\phi}]=\frac{H}{2\pi}\,\hat{\mathds{1}},\qquad i[\hat{L},\hat{\pi}_{\phi}]=0.

(B.22)

The first condition implies that the commutator of
\hat{L}
with
\hat{\phi}
is a c-number. For Hermitian
\hat{L}(\hat{\phi},\hat{\pi}_{\phi};N)
this is only possible if
\hat{L}
is at most linear in
\hat{\pi}_{\phi}
and independent of
\hat{\phi}
up to an additive c-number, i.e.

\hat{L}=\alpha_{1}(N)\,\hat{\pi}_{\phi}+\alpha_{2}(N)\,\hat{\mathds{1}}.

(B.23)

The second condition in (
B.22
) is then automatic, while the first fixes
\alpha(N)=H/2\pi
. The additive term
\ell(N)\hat{\mathds{1}}
has no effect on the dynamics and may be dropped, leaving the unique choice (up to an irrelevant c-number)

\hat{L}=\frac{H}{2\pi}\,\hat{\pi}_{\phi}.

(B.24)

Substituting (
B.24
) into (
B.20
) yields

\displaystyle\differential\hat{\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-3N}}{\mathtt{vol}\,H}\,\hat{\pi}_{\phi}\,\differential N+\frac{H}{2\pi}\,\hat{\mathds{1}}\circ\differential W,

(B.25)

\displaystyle\differential\hat{\pi}_{\phi}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathtt{vol}\,e^{3N}}{H}\,V^{\prime}(\hat{\phi})\,\differential N,

and therefore, for expectation values,

\displaystyle\differential\langle\hat{\phi}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-3N}}{\mathtt{vol}\,H}\,\langle\hat{\pi}_{\phi}\rangle\,\differential N+\frac{H}{2\pi}\,\differential W,

(B.26)

\displaystyle\differential\langle\hat{\pi}_{\phi}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathtt{vol}\,e^{3N}}{H}\,\langle V^{\prime}(\hat{\phi})\rangle\,\differential N.

In the classical limit
\langle V^{\prime}(\hat{\phi})\rangle\simeq V^{\prime}(\langle\hat{\phi}\rangle)
, and eliminating
\pi_{\phi}
in the overdamped/slow-roll regime reproduces the standard stochastic-inflation Langevin equation for the coarse-grained field. Finally, for an initial pure state the corresponding Stratonovich SSE is

\differential\ket{\psi}=-i\left(\hat{K}\,\differential N+\hat{L}\circ\differential W\right)\ket{\psi},\qquad\hat{L}=\frac{H}{2\pi}\hat{\pi}_{\phi},

(B.27)

which in Itô form reads

\differential\ket{\psi}=\left(-i\hat{K}-\frac{1}{2}\hat{L}^{2}\right)\ket{\psi}\,\differential N+\hat{L}\ket{\psi}\,\differential W.

(B.28)

Averaging over noise realisations yields the GKLS master equation

\partial_{N}\hat{\rho}=-\,i\bigl[\hat{K},\hat{\rho}\bigr]+\hat{L}\hat{\rho}\hat{L}-\frac{1}{2}\hat{L}^{2}\hat{\rho}-\frac{1}{2}\hat{\rho}\hat{L}^{2}=-\,i\bigl[\hat{K},\hat{\rho}\bigr]-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{H}{2\pi}\right)^{2}[\hat{\pi}_{\phi},[\hat{\pi}_{\phi},\hat{\rho}]]\,,

(B.29)

i.e. a single
\hat{\pi}_{\phi}
Lindblad operator implementing stochastic translations in
\phi
.

Appendix C
Adiabatic theorem for an oscillator in de Sitter

Consider the time-dependent quadratic Hamiltonian

K_{S}(t)=\frac{\pi_{\phi}^{2}}{2\,e^{3Ht}}+\frac{e^{3Ht}}{2}\,m^{2}\phi^{2},

(C.1)

with
N\equiv Ht
. This can be written in the general form of a parametric oscillator,

\hat{K}_{S}(t)=\frac{\hat{\pi}^{2}}{2M(t)}+\frac{M(t)}{2}\,\omega^{2}\hat{\phi}^{2},\qquad\text{where }M(t)=e^{3Ht},\;\;\omega=m.

(C.2)

The time derivative in the Schrödinger picture is

\dot{\hat{K}}_{S}(t)=-\frac{\dot{M}}{2M^{2}}\hat{\pi}^{2}+\frac{\dot{M}}{2}\omega^{2}\hat{\phi}^{2}=\frac{\dot{M}}{2M}\omega\left(\hat{a}^{2}+\hat{a}^{\dagger 2}\right),

(C.3)

where
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
are the instantaneous ladder operators associated with
M(t)
and
\omega
. A sufficient condition for adiabatic evolution is given in
comparat2009general
as

\frac{\left|\langle m(t)|\dot{\hat{K}}_{S}(t)|n(t)\rangle\right|}{\left[E_{n}(t)-E_{m}(t)\right]^{2}}\ll 1\qquad\text{for all }m\neq n,

(C.4)

Where
n
and
m
are labelling the eigenstates with energies
E_{n}
and
E_{m}
. The nonvanishing matrix elements of
\dot{\hat{K}}_{S}
occur only between states differing by two quanta. Evaluating these gives the adiabatic parameters

\frac{\left|\langle n+2|\dot{\hat{K}}_{S}|n\rangle\right|}{\left(E_{n}-E_{n+2}\right)^{2}}=\frac{3H}{8m}\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)},\qquad\frac{\left|\langle n-2|\dot{\hat{K}}_{S}|n\rangle\right|}{\left(E_{n}-E_{n-2}\right)^{2}}=\frac{3H}{8m}\sqrt{n(n-1)},

(C.5)

and zero for all other
m\neq n
.
In particular, the ground-state (vacuum) mixing parameter is

\frac{\left|\langle 2|\dot{\hat{K}}_{S}|0\rangle\right|}{\left(E_{0}-E_{2}\right)^{2}}=\frac{3\sqrt{2}}{8}\frac{H}{m}.

(C.6)

Hence, the evolution is adiabatic provided that

\frac{m}{H}\gg 1\,.

(C.7)

A convenient way to realise a genuinely non-Markovian dynamics is to couple the spectator field to a
single
massive environment field
\chi
via the cubic interaction
\phi\chi^{3}
, and then apply the non-Markovian quantum state diffusion (NMQSD) formalism of Diósi, Gisin and Strunz
diosi1998non
;
diosi2014general
. In this case the reduced dynamics of the system field is unraveled by the nonlinear, norm-preserving NMQSD equation,

\frac{\partial}{\partial N}\,\ket{\psi(z^*)}=\biggl(-\,i\hat{K}_{S}(N)+\frac{\lambda e^{3N}}{H}\bigl(\hat{\phi}-\expectationvalue*{\hat\phi}\bigr)\,z_{N}^{*}-\frac{\lambda e^{3N}}{H}\bigl(\hat{\phi}-\expectationvalue*{\hat\phi}\bigr)\!\int_{N_{0}}^{N}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{N}\;C(N,\bar{N})\,\frac{\delta}{\delta z_{\bar{N}}^{*}}\biggr)\,\ket{\psi(z^*)},

(D.2)

where
z_{N}\equiv z(N)
is a complex Gaussian process with zero mean and coloured correlations

\mathbb{E}\!\big[z_{N}\big]=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}\!\big[z_{N}z_{\bar{N}}\big]=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}\!\big[z_{N}z_{\bar{N}}^{*}\big]=C(N,\bar{N}).

(D.3)

The same kernel
C(N,\bar{N})
therefore governs both the non-local memory term and the noise correlations.

The non-local memory kernel
C(N,\bar{N})
is fixed by the spectator-field correlators. Writing the total interaction as

\hat{H}_{\rm int}(N)=\tilde{\lambda}_{3}\,\hat{\phi}\,\hat{\chi}_{I}^{3}(N)
, one finds

C(N,\bar{N})\;=\;\tilde{\lambda}_{3}^{2}\,\Big(\big\langle 0_{N_{0}}\big|\hat{\chi}_{I}^{3}(N)\,\hat{\chi}_{I}^{3}(\bar{N})\big|0_{N_{0}}\big\rangle-\big\langle 0_{N_{0}}\big|\hat{\chi}_{I}^{3}(N)\big|0_{N_{0}}\big\rangle\big\langle 0_{N_{0}}\big|\hat{\chi}_{I}^{3}(\bar{N})\big|0_{N_{0}}\big\rangle\Big).

(D.4)

Applying the renormalization scheme in Eq. (
A.36
) yields a stationary correlator that depends only on the e-fold difference
\Delta N=N-\bar{N}
,

C(N,\bar{N})=\frac{3\lambda_{3}^{2}}{128\,H^{3}\,\omega_{\chi}^{3}}\Bigl[9\cos\bigl(\omega_{\chi}\Delta N\bigr)+\cos\bigl(3\omega_{\chi}\Delta N\bigr)\Bigr],\qquad\omega_{\chi}\equiv\frac{m_{\chi}}{H},

(D.5)

so that (
D.4
) provides an explicit non-local kernel
C(N,\bar{N})
for use in the NMQSD equation and the corresponding non-Markovian master equation. In the the Wigner snapshots of Fig.
11
we can see that the periodic non-Markovian driving can lead to periodic transitions between vacua.
