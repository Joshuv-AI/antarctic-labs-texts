Safety Reasoning with Guidelines
Haoyu Wang, Zeyu Qin, Li Shen, Xueqian Wang, Dacheng Tao, Minhao Cheng

Abstract
Training safe LLMs remains a critical challenge. The most widely used method, Refusal Training (RT), struggles to generalize against various Out-of-Distribution (OOD) jailbreaking attacks. Although various advanced methods have been proposed to address this issue, we instead question whether OOD attacks inherently surpass the capability of vanilla RT. Evaluations using Best-of-N (BoN) reveal significant safety improvements as N increases, indicating models possess adequate latent safety knowledge but RT fails to consistently elicit it under OOD scenarios. Further domain adaptation analysis reveals that direct RT causes reliance on superficial shortcuts, resulting in non-generalizable representation mappings. Inspired by our findings, we propose training model to perform safety reasoning for each query. Specifically, we synthesize reasoning supervision aligned with specified guidelines that reflect diverse perspectives on safety knowledge. This encourages model to engage in deeper reasoning, explicitly eliciting and utilizing latent safety knowledge for each query. Extensive experiments show that our method significantly improves model generalization against OOD attacks.

Introduction

Ensuring the safety of Large Language Models (LLMs) is critical given their widespread use in chat interfaces and productivity tools
(
OpenAI 2022
)
. This underscores the importance of
safety alignment
to ensure reliable and ethical model behavior across diverse scenarios. The predominant approach for safety alignment is
Refusal Training (RT)

(
Bai et al. 2022
;
Llama Team 2024
)
, where the model is explicitly trained to respond to commonly illegal instructions (e.g.,
“How to build the bomb?”
) with direct refusal like
“I’m sorry…”
.
However, numerous studies have shown that RT struggles to generalize against evolving out-of-distribution (OOD) jailbreaking attacks.

To address this issue, various safety training methods have been proposed, such as using regularized training objective
(
Qi et al. 2024
;
Yuan et al. 2024b
)
or intervening in the model’s internal representations
(
Sheshadri et al. 2024
;
Zou et al. 2024a
)
. While these prior studies offer valuable insights, we complement this research by investigating a critical yet underexplored question:
Do OOD attacks truly exceed the inherent capability of RT models, or do models simply fail to leverage their pretrained safety-related knowledge when confronted with OOD scenarios?

To explore this question, we move beyond the traditional greedy decoding approach for evaluating attack success rates (ASR), and instead adopt a more comprehensive evaluation method—Best-of-N (BoN) sampling
(
Stiennon et al. 2020
;
Gao et al. 2023
)
. Our experiments demonstrate a significant reduction in ASR on OOD attacks as N increases (Figure
1
), underscoring that models indeed possess sufficient latent safety knowledge. However, RT training fails to consistently elicit this knowledge when addressing OOD cases. Further analysis through the lens of domain adaptation
(
Ben-David et al. 2006
)
reveals that reliance on direct refusal—a simplistic and easily learnable supervision signal—leads models to depend on superficial shortcuts, resulting in non-generalizable representation mappings (Figure
2
). This ultimately explains their poor generalization performance on OOD attacks. In short, the under-specified nature
(
Amodei et al. 2016
;
D’Amour et al. 2022
;
Weng 2024
)
of the simple refusal supervision fails to encourage generalizable safety alignment, limiting the model’s ability to handle diverse and complex OOD scenarios.

Building on these findings, we identify two fundamental shortcomings inherent to Refusal Training: (1) the model’s inability to explicitly
elicit
and
utilize
its latent safety knowledge, and (2) the under-specification of its supervision signal. To address both issues, we propose
Safety Reasoning with Guidelines
(SRG), which guides the model through a structured, multi-step reasoning process based on explicitly defined safety guidelines. These detailed guidelines specify the supervision signal clearly, enabling the model to systematically elicit and leverage latent safety knowledge across diverse perspectives during training. Consequently, at test time, the model can
adaptively
invoke the appropriate safety knowledge to robustly handle diverse and evolving OOD scenarios, reducing reliance on superficial shortcuts and significantly improving generalization.

Specifically, the SRG framework comprises three core components:
(1) synthesizing supervision based on specified guidelines, (2) rejection sampling aligned with guidelines, and (3) internalizing guidelines via context distillation.
In the first stage—synthesizing supervision—we explicitly prompt an LLM to generate detailed, step-by-step reasoning responses guided strictly by predefined safety guidelines (Figure 4). Each guideline clearly specifies a distinct aspect of latent safety knowledge that the model should elicit when addressing the input query, such as logical reasoning patterns, contextual understanding, ethical considerations, and other critical safety perspectives. These explicit guidelines compel the model to engage in deeper, structured reasoning across multiple safety dimensions, effectively activating its latent safety knowledge. To further enhance the utilization of this elicited knowledge, we incorporate Self-Reflection and Self-Refinement mechanisms
(
Madaan et al. 2023
;
Qu et al. 2024
)
into the guidelines, enabling the model to critically assess and dynamically refine its reasoning steps. By prepending these guidelines to each unsafe instruction, we construct augmented queries to prompt the LLM for guideline-aligned reasoning responses.

Subsequently, we employ rejection sampling to ensure the quality of synthesized responses. Guidelines serve a dual purpose:
they function both as explicit directives for generating reasoning supervision and as evaluative criteria for rejection sampling
. Specifically, we retain only those responses whose reasoning clearly aligns with guidelines and exhibits appropriate refusal behavior. Compared to conventional RT, our SRG introduces an additional training objective: the model simultaneously learns (1) explicit refusal behavior, and (2) systematic reasoning guided by specified safety guidelines. Finally, we apply Context Distillation
(
Askell et al. 2021
)
to train the model, enabling it to internalize the structured reasoning processes and learn refusal behaviors. Extensive experiments demonstrate that SRG significantly enhances safety generalization, enabling models to adaptively and robustly handle diverse and evolving OOD attacks.
To summarize, our contributions are:

•

We revisit OOD generalization of RT models using BoN sampling. Results show significant OOD improvements as N increases, indicating RT models inherently possess latent safety knowledge that conventional RT fails to effectively elicit.

•

We analyze this failure mode from a domain adaptation perspective, revealing that training solely with overly simplistic direct refusal signals encourages superficial shortcuts and prevents models from effectively leveraging pretrained safety knowledge, resulting in non-generalizable representation mappings and poor OOD performance.

•

Motivated by these insights, we propose a novel training framework that synthesizes explicit reasoning supervision aligned with specified safety guidelines, effectively eliciting and utilizing models’ latent safety knowledge. Extensive experiments demonstrate significantly improved generalization against challenging OOD attacks.

Related Work

Jailbreaking Attacks.
OOD jailbreaking attacks aim to bypass the safety alignment, leading models to generate harmful contents. They can be classified into 2 classes: 1) white-box attacks
(
Zou et al. 2023b
;
Liu et al. 2023b
;
Geisler et al. 2024
)
: the attackers access model parameters to compute gradients or losses; 2) black-box attacks
(
Chao et al. 2023
;
Wei et al. 2023
;
Shen et al. 2024
;
Yuan et al. 2023a
;
Zeng et al. 2024
)
: attackers typically employ black-box optimization or design diverse OOD scenarios to deceive models. Black-box attacks, which do not require access to model parameters, are more practical and effective
(
Mazeika et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
. Therefore,
we primarily focus on black-box attacks in this work.

Safety Training.
Prior methods for improving OOD generalization mainly include regularization-based training
(
Yuan et al. 2024b
;
Qi et al. 2024
)
and interventions on internal model representations
(
Zou et al. 2024a
;
Sheshadri et al. 2024
)
. In contrast, we propose a novel supervision signal—structured reasoning data—to explicitly guide models toward enhanced safety reasoning and generalization.

OOD Generalization.

Kumar et al. 2022
shows that fine-tuning pretrained models with limited samples distorts pretrained features, resulting in poor OOD performance.
Izmailov et al. 2022
;
Lee et al. 2022
further explore this issue, showing that under-specified supervision can lead pretrained models to rely on shortcuts in training data during fine-tuning. Aligning with this line of research, we propose training models to elicit and utilize latent knowledge
(
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023
)
through reasoning as a strategy to mitigate these limitations.

LLM Reasoning.
Reasoning has enabled large language models (LLMs) to achieve remarkable performance on complex STEM tasks. Existing methods for training reasoning capabilities mainly include: (1) utilizing human-curated or synthesized supervision
(
Uesato et al. 2022
;
Lightman et al. 2023
;
Yu et al. 2023
;
Mitra et al. 2024
;
Zelikman et al. 2022
;
Yuan et al. 2023b
;
Qin et al. 2025
;
Guan et al. 2025
)
, and (2) employing large-scale reinforcement learning (RL) with verification signals
(
OpenAI 2024
;
Guo et al. 2025
;
Team et al. 2025
;
Luo et al. 2025
)
. Our work aligns with the first category, using synthesized reasoning supervision for safety alignment. While
Guan et al. 2024
also train models to reason for safety alignment, our work places greater emphasis on analyzing failure mode of vanilla refusal training and proposes a complete pipeline for synthesizing structured reasoning supervision based on these insights. More discussion about our work and
Guan et al. 2024
are provided in
Appendix
B
.

(\bm{x},\bm{y})\sim\mathcal{D}_{T}

3
Re-Examining OOD Generalization of Refusal Training

In this section, we re-examine the generalization performance of Refusal Training (RT) to investigate
Do OOD jailbreaking attacks truly exceed the capability of RT model, or does the model simply fail to effectively leverage their learned pretraining knowledge about safety to handle OOD cases?
Inspired by prior works
(
Gao et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024
)
, we adopt a relaxed evaluation approach to assess ASR. Specifically, instead of relying on greedy decoding, we use Best-of-N sampling (BoN) to provide a more comprehensive evaluation on RT. Specifically, we sample N outputs per query from the RT model and consider an attack unsuccessful if at least one response is classified as safe by an external classifier (Llama-Guard-3-8B).

We adopt the widely used LLaMA-3.1-8B-base as
\bm{M}_{\bm{\theta}}
. For training dataset, we incorporate illegal instructions (e.g.,
”How to build a bomb?”
) from PKU-SafeRLHF
(
Ji et al. 2024
)
and helpful instructions from Ultrafeedback
(
Cui et al. 2023
)
. We resample corresponding responses from GPT-4o. We use two training dataset scales: 1)
small-scale
, consisting of 0.8K randomly selected illegal instructions and 2.5K helpful instructions; and 2)
large-scale
, containing 5K illegal instructions and 30K helpful instructions. Following previous work
(
Touvron et al. 2023a
;
Llama Team 2024
)
, we employ the most used
Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)
for training. We adopt full fine-tuning and LoRA for SFT. Following
(
Yuan et al. 2024b
)
, our evaluation includes five types of attacks: 1) an ID attack, illegal instructions from Do-Not Answer
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
and HarmBench
(
Mazeika et al. 2024
)
, and 2) four OOD attacks: Jailbreak Chat
(
Shen et al. 2024
)
, Self Cipher
(
Yuan et al. 2023a
)
, Past Tense
(
Andriushchenko & Flammarion 2024
)
, and Persuasive Attack
(
Zeng et al. 2024
)
. The more details about evaluation and model training are shown in
Appendix
A
.

3.1
Refusal-Trained Model Could Achieves Better ASR against OOD Attacks

For BoN sampling, we set the temperature T=1 and scale N from
4
to
128
. The results are shown in Figure
1
. When evaluated with greedy decoding (N
=0
), the model performs good generalization performance on ID attacks (first column), even when trained on small-scale datasets. This observation aligns with findings from previous studies
(
Bianchi et al. 2024
;
Yuan et al. 2024b
;
Touvron et al. 2023b
)
. In contrast, it shows poor generalization performance against OOD attacks (second to fifth columns).

Notably, as N increases, the model’s ASR drops significantly across all OOD attacks. Compared to greedy decoding, BoN with N =
128
reduces ASR by an average of
60\%
, with ASR falling below
10\%
for Jailbreak Chat and Self Cipher. Expanding the training dataset further improves OOD performance, particularly on Past Tense and Persuasive Attack.
This underscores that the model possesses sufficient safety-related latent knowledge and exhibit the ability to handle safety generalization across OOD cases.
This observation aligns with existing alignment studies
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Zhou et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
, which emphasizes that pretraining on vast web-scale data equips LLMs with extensive capabilities to address nearly all alignment tasks. The contrast between the BoN evaluation and greedy decoding results clearly demonstrates that
refusal training struggles to consistently elicit the model’s rich pretrained knowledge about safety when coping with OOD attacks.

3.2
Under-specified Refusal Supervision Leads to Poor Generalization

Refusal Training compels the model to directly respond to illegal instructions (
\bm{x}_{i}
) with simple refusal phrases (
\bm{y}_{i}
), such as
“I’m sorry…”
. This straightforward pattern-matching enables rapid learning of refusal behavior, resulting in strong performance on ID attacks even with small-scale datasets. However, the overly simplistic supervision signal hinders the model from effectively leveraging its rich pretrained knowledge, causing reliance on superficial shortcuts and poor generalization to OOD attacks.

To better understand this issue, we draw a connection to
Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA)
. Under this perspective, generalizing safety to OOD scenarios resembles a UDA task, where test attacks (target domain) arise from a distribution distinct from the training data (source domain). Following prior work
(
Ben-David et al. 2006
)
, we adopt the classic informal formulation to characterize model performance on the target domain:

\epsilon_{\text{test}}(\bm{M}_{\bm{\theta}})\leq\epsilon_{\text{train}}(\bm{M}_{\bm{\theta}})+d_{\bm{M}_{\bm{\theta}}}\left(p_{\text{train}},p_{\text{test}}\right)+\lambda,

where
\epsilon
is error (ASR in our work).
\lambda
is irreducible error term related to the inherent capacity of the model
\bm{M}_{\bm{\theta}}
.
d_{\bm{M}_{\bm{\theta}}}
measures a pre-defined divergence between
p_{\text{train}}
and
p_{\text{test}}
in the representation space learned by
\bm{M}_{\bm{\theta}}
. This error bound indicates that effective training on the source domain, coupled with learning a robust and distribution-aligned representation space, is crucial for enhancing OOD performance. For instance, as shown in Figure
1
, increasing the training dataset size can effectively reduce the first term
\epsilon_{\text{train}}(\bm{M}_{\bm{\theta}})
, thereby improving generalization performance.

Figure 3
:
An overview of the complete reasoning supervision synthesis pipeline discussed in Section
4
.

To analyze the second term
d_{\bm{M}_{\bm{\theta}}}
, we use
Representation Engineering (RepE)

(
Zou et al. 2023a
)
, a widely-adopted technique for analyzing internal representations of LLMs, to visualize the learned representations of
\bm{M}_{\bm{\theta}}
. As shown in Figure
2
, illegal instructions (ID attacks) are clearly separable from helpful instructions, indicating that the refusal-trained model effectively distinguishes between them, thus reliably generating direct refusal responses and achieving low ASR on ID attacks. However, representations of OOD attacks are misaligned with those of ID attacks (leading to a large
d_{\bm{M}_{\bm{\theta}}}(p_{\text{train}},p_{\text{test}})
) and are entangled with helpful instructions. For comparison, we also visualize the representations learned by two advanced safety training methods that explicitly control representations—LAT
(
Sheshadri et al. 2024
)
and RR
(
Zou et al. 2024a
)
. In their representation spaces, both ID and OOD attacks are clearly separable from helpful instructions, and representations of ID and OOD attacks are better aligned (indicating smaller
d_{\bm{M}_{\bm{\theta}}}(p_{\text{train}},p_{\text{test}})
). These results suggest that LAT and RR learn more robust representation mappings, contributing to their superior OOD generalization. In contrast, RT’s exclusive focus on direct refusal behavior encourages reliance on superficial shortcuts, resulting in brittle, non-robust representation mappings and ultimately poor OOD generalization.

Building on these findings, we identify two fundamental
shortcomings inherent to RT: (1) the model’s
inability to explicitly elicit and utilize its latent safety knowledge, and (2) the under-specification of its supervision signal.

4
Safety Reasoning with Guidelines

In this section, we introduce
Safety Reasoning with Guidelines
(SRG), a structured training approach designed to explicitly address the shortcomings identified above. Specifically, SRG guides the model through a multi-step reasoning process based on clearly specified safety guidelines. By explicitly defining detailed guidelines, SRG provides a precise and informative supervision signal, enabling the model to systematically elicit and leverage latent safety knowledge across diverse perspectives during training. Consequently, at test time, the trained model can adaptively invoke relevant safety knowledge to robustly handle diverse and evolving OOD scenarios, reducing reliance on superficial shortcuts and significantly enhancing generalization performance. SRG framework comprises three core components (shown in Figure
3
): (1) synthesizing supervision based on specified guidelines, (2) rejection sampling aligned with guidelines, and (3) internalizing guidelines via context distillation. We describe each component in detail below.

Synthesizing Supervision Based on Specified Guidelines.
We first synthesize detailed supervision signals by prompting a large language model (LLM)
\bm{M}_{t}
to generate structured reasoning responses guided by explicitly specified safety guidelines
\bm{C}
. Each guideline precisely specifies a distinct aspect of latent safety knowledge that the model should explicitly elicit and leverage when addressing the input query. These aspects include reasoning patterns (e.g., “encouraging more steps and multiple paths”); context understanding (e.g., “user intent recognition”, “role awareness”, “historical context”); ethical considerations (e.g., “social values”, “cultural sensitivity”) and other related aspects, as illustrated in Figure
13
. By explicitly adhering to these guidelines during reasoning, the model learns to map complex and variable user queries onto a structured and generalizable conceptual space defined by guidelines (see the last sub-figure of Figure
2
). Thus, at test time, it can
adaptively
invoke relevant safety knowledge to robustly handle diverse OOD scenarios, reducing reliance on superficial shortcuts.

We prepend guidelines
\bm{C}
to each query
\bm{x}_{i}
, forming an augmented query
\bm{x}_{i}^{c}=(\bm{C},\bm{x}_{i})
, and prompt an LLM
\bm{M}_{t}
to generate response
\bm{y}_{i}^{c}=\bm{M}_{t}(\bm{x}_{i}^{c})
. The response
\bm{y}_{i}^{c}
includes a reasoning process
\bm{T}^{c}
and a final response
\bm{y}_{i}
. Importantly,
the use of explicit guidelines
\bm{C}
provides greater flexibility and scalability
, allowing easy incorporation of new knowledge-eliciting perspectives during deployment.

Utilizing Knowledge with Self-Reflection and Self-Refinement.
To further enhance the effective
utilization
of elicited safety knowledge, we incorporate
Self-Reflection

(
Madaan et al. 2023
;
Qu et al. 2024
)
into our guidelines. Self-Reflection encourages the model to explicitly evaluate its own reasoning steps, thereby better accessing and applying its latent knowledge. Specifically, our Self-Reflection mechanism comprises two components:
text reflection
and
score rewarding
. During the reasoning process, the model is prompted to regularly assess its progress using explicit
<reflection>
tags (Figure
4
). Additionally, it assigns a safety-related score within the range
[0.0,1.0]
(
<safety degree>
tag) to each reasoning step based on its reflection. This scoring explicitly guides the model to dynamically adjust subsequent reasoning steps and responses.

However, initial experiments indicate that while the model can reliably produce reflections and scores, it sometimes fails to consistently generate appropriate final refusal responses for certain OOD attacks (Table
8
). We hypothesize that although self-reflection promotes knowledge utilization, the model is prone to overlooking assigned scores when generating
y_{i}^{c}
. To address this issue, we introduce an additional mechanism called
Self-Refinement
, explicitly requiring the model to produce a refusal response informed by the assigned scores at the end of the reasoning process (Figure
4
). Together, Self-Reflection and Self-Refinement enable the model to iteratively evaluate and dynamically adjust its reasoning, significantly improving both the quality and reliability of its outputs. Ablation studies in Section
5.4
confirm the effectiveness of these two components. The complete guidelines
\bm{C}
are provided in
Appendix
F
.

Rejection Sampling Aligned with Guidelines.
We apply the rejection sampling on
\bm{y}_{i}^{c}
to ensure response quality.
Guideline serve a dual purpose: they act as directives for generating reasoning supervision and as evaluative criteria for rejection sampling.
Only responses with reasoning that aligns with guidelines and show refusal behavior are retained. Given pre-defined guidelines, evaluating the reasoning process becomes straightforward. We leverage an LLM,
\bm{M}_{j}^{c}
(LLaMA-3.1-8B-instruct), to assess
\bm{T}^{c}
. Given
\bm{C}
and
\bm{T}^{c}
as inputs,
\bm{M}_{j}^{c}
outputs
Yes
or
No
, indicating whether
\bm{T}^{c}
adheres to
\bm{C}
. We adopt another LLM
\bm{M}_{j}^{i}
(Llama-Guard-3) to evaluate refusal behavior. Compared with refusal training,
our method introduces an additional training objective: 1) learning refusal behavior based on
\bm{T}^{c}
, and 2) learning to reason
\bm{T}^{c}
aligned with pre-defined
\bm{C}
. A training example from our method is shown in Figure
4
.

Internalizing Guidelines via Context Distillation.
After rejection sampling, we adopt
Context Distillation

(
Askell et al. 2021
;
Bai et al. 2022
)
to train the model, enabling it to internalize the reasoning process w.r.t guidelines and learn the refusal behavior.
For
(\bm{x}^{c},\bm{y}^{c})
from new training set
\mathcal{D}_{T}
, we remove
\bm{C}
of
\bm{x}^{c}
and only retain the original instruction
\bm{x}
as input. The training objective is:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\theta}}\underset{(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{c})\sim\mathcal{D}_{T}}{\mathbf{E}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{M}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}),\bm{y}^{c}):=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{T}|}\sum-p_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{T}^{c},\bm{y}|\bm{x})

Our original two training objectives are unified into maximizing conditional probability of
(\bm{T}^{c},\bm{y})
given input
\bm{x}
. At inference time, we hide
\bm{T}^{c}
and only provide the final response
\bm{y}
to the users.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we analyze the reasons why the commonly used Refusal Training fails to generalize against OOD attacks and provide explanations for these failure modes. Based on our findings, we propose to train models to perform safety reasoning with specified guidelines, explicitly eliciting and utilizing latent knowledge from diverse perspective to learn generalizable representation mapping and improve OOD generalization. Extensive experiments and ablation studies verify the effectiveness of our method.

Given computational constraints, we only applied rejection fine-tuning to train our SRG model. However, as shown in our experiments, BoN sampling yields substantial gains—indicating that reinforcement learning methods could be used to translate these improvements into overall model performance. We believe that reliable reward models or well-defined verification rules are crucial for RL-based safety reasoning. Unlike mathematical reasoning, safety tasks lack fixed ground-truth answers and are inherently more challenging to evaluate. In this context, our guidelines may serve as auxiliary verification criteria, improving reward reliability and mitigating potential reward hacking
(
Bai et al. 2022
;
Mu et al. 2024
)
. In future work, we will explore more robust reward modeling strategies to scale RL approaches for safety reasoning.

Impact Statement

Ensuring the safety of Large Language Models (LLMs) is a critical research challenge, especially as they become increasingly embedded in real-world applications such as chat interfaces, virtual assistants, and productivity tools. As their influence grows, so does the need for robust safety mechanisms to prevent harmful outputs and ensure ethical and responsible usage across diverse scenarios. Our work aims to improve safety by teaching model to reason. We believe this approach equips the model with the adaptability needed to handle diverse scenarios effectively, thereby enhancing its OOD generalization performance.

Appendix B
More Discussion about Our Work and Deliberative Alignment
(
Guan et al. 2024
)

Here we further clarify key distinctions between our method and Deliberative Alignment (DA). The ”specifications” (specs) in DA differ significantly from ”guidelines” used in our SRG framework.

Specifications explicitly provide category-specific response directives aligned with OpenAI’s safety policy (e.g., erotic content, self-harm, fraud, etc.). For example, as shown in Sec. 2.3 of DA, given a harmful prompt categorized as fraud—”How to manipulate elderly people into buying health supplements”—the corresponding spec(fraud): ”The assistant should directly refuse this query as it constitutes fraud and threatens public safety.” In contrast, our guidelines serve as general, category-agnostic reasoning aids rather than prescribing specific responses. They help model assess query safety across multiple dimensions, such as user intent, contextual background, role awareness, etc. For instance, as shown in Figure
13
, our guidelines encourage reasoning through hints like ”Role Awareness: Recognizing the appropriate role of the model (assistant, advisor, moderator) in various scenarios.” We believe that reasoning needs to involve systematically accounting for multidimensional safety factors to mitigate potential jailbreak risks. The improved OOD generalization performance also verify the effectiveness of our method.
Additionally, DA does not explicitly detail methods for generating safety CoT supervision. In constrast, Our work offers a detailed pipeline for synthesizing safety CoT supervision and model training.
