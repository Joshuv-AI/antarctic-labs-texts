How does disability impact children’s experiences in outdoor and risky play? A scoping review and narrative synthesis
Authors: Maggie Locke, Michelle R Stone, Sofia So, Jordan Sheriko, Kelly P Arbour-Nicitopoulos, Mariana Brussoni, Sarah A Moore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOutdoor and risky play are associated with physical, cognitive, social, and emotional health and wellbeing throughout childhood. All children have the right to play, yet children with disabilities (CWD) are not engaging sufficiently in outdoor or risky play. There is limited literature exploring CWD participation in outdoor and risky play. Therefore, this scoping review aimed to summarize the literature exploring CWD experiences of outdoor and risky play, including definitions, types, preferences, benefits, barriers, and strategies to support CWD engagement in outdoor and risky play.MethodsThis scoping review included studies exploring outdoor and risky play for CWD (0–24 years). A search strategy was co-developed with a librarian and six databases were searched. Included records were screened at title and abstract and full text by multiple reviewers. Data from included articles were extracted and synthesized using narrative synthesis and organized using the socioecological model (SEM).ResultsSixty-nine articles were included in the final review. Articles were diverse in terms of study design, location, and disability type. Many articles explored outdoor play while few explored risky play. Articles reported on definitions, types, preferences, and benefits of outdoor and risky play for CWD. They reported on barriers and facilitators at all levels of the SEM. Finally, articles reported on proposed strategies to support CWD future engagement in outdoor and risky play.ConclusionsThe review revealed that CWD and those who support their participation in outdoor and risky play are resilient in overcoming a multitude of barriers they encounter during outdoor and risky play, as they recognize the benefits of engaging in play. The literature also explores barriers CWD face to participation and future strategies to support their engagement in outdoor and risky play. Future work should explore pathways to support CWD engagement in outdoor and risky play, specifically at the policy level. Furthermore, we must engage with CWD and their supporters to provide them with the autonomy to make decisions around their participation in outdoor and risky play in order to foster opportunities for quality participation.Registration DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/H4FNP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27201-x.Keywords: Disabilities, Children, Outdoor play, Risky play

Introduction

Play is a universal, cross-cultural attribute of childhood [1] and a fundamental right of every child [2]. It affords children the necessary conditions to learn and thrive, and promotes physical, cognitive, social, and emotional health and well-being [1, 3, 4]. Outdoor and risky play are typically unstructured in nature. Unstructured play (child-led, spontaneous in nature [5]) is particularly important for children’s health and development [6], especially when it occurs outdoors [7]. Loose parts (i.e., wood, ropes, tires, spindles, tarps) can help to facilitate outdoor unstructured play [5]. Outdoor play is one of the most consistent predictors of childhood physical activity (PA) [8, 9] and has been recommended to help children meet the 24-Hour Movement Guidelines [10, 11]. Outdoor play also helps to support children’s self-control, focus, and attention [12].

During outdoor play, risky play naturally emerges [13]. Risky play is defined as “a form of play that is thrilling and exciting, which involves uncertainty, unpredictability, and varying degrees of risk-taking” ( [14], p.12). To contextualize the ways children engage in risky play, Sandseter and Kleppe et al., [15, 16] proposed eight categories of risky play, which include: (a) play at great heights, (b) play at high speed, (c) play with dangerous tools, (d) play near dangerous elements, (e) rough-and-tumble play, (f) play where children go exploring, (g) play with impact, and (h) vicarious play [15, 16]. When engaging in risky play, children experiment and learn to make decisions, solve problems, cooperate with others, and develop friendships [17]. Risky play supports motor development [13] and offers children the opportunity to test their limits and navigate challenges [18]. The Active Outdoor Play Position Statement emphasized the benefits of active play outdoors and in nature, along with opportunities for risk-taking, for healthy child development [19, 20]. Since its release, the Canadian Pediatric Society has shown increased support for children engaging in risky play while outdoors [21]. Research on nature connectiveness has also shown that when children spend time in the natural environment, for example, playing outdoors, they are happier, healthier, and function better [22].

Despite the known benefits of participating in outdoor [12] and risky play [13], children with disabilities (CWD) are not engaging in these forms of play as they face further disparities and barriers to participation compared to their peers without disabilities [23]. The World Health Organization defines disability as, “those who have long-term physical, mental, intellectual, or sensory impairments which in interaction with various barriers may hinder their full and effective participation in society on an equal basis with others” (2, p.3). Within this review, disability has been categorized into physical (i.e. cerebral palsy, muscular dystrophy, amputation, spina bifida), neurodevelopmental (i.e. autism spectrum disorder, Down syndrome, attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder), and sensory disabilities (i.e. hearing impairments, visual impairments, sensory processing disorder), although it is important to note that these are not mutually exclusive. We have chosen to categorize disability into three categories, as this review will inform future work with the Canadian Disability Participation Project (CDPP) to understand the lived experience of CWD and their participation in risky play. Therefore, this paper follows the same language and categorization of disability as the CDPP.

The additional barriers CWD face to engaging in outdoor and risky play can be situated across levels of the socioecological model (SEM) [24]. There are five levels of the SEM, starting at the level furthest away from the individual and moving inwards, the levels include: the chronosystem, macrosystem, exosystem, mesosystem, and microsystem [25]. Previous literature has explored parents’ perceptions of outdoor, risky play in preschoolers without disabilities using the SEM [26]. They found factors which influenced children’s risky play at the macrosystem level (i.e., risk aversion culture adapted in Western society), exosystem level (i.e., neighbourhood location and perceived safety), mesosystem level (i.e., peer and family member perceptions and level of supervision during play), and microsystem level (i.e., child’s age, size, ability) [26]. Looking at these barriers through a socioecological lens can help us to understand how they interact with one another and impact CWD outdoor and risky play.

The 2024 ParticipACTION Report Card on PA for Children and Youth noted that CWD are engaging in less outdoor active play compared to children without disabilities due to the additional barriers they face (i.e. limited accessibility, decreased opportunities for active transport, limited mobility) [27]. Furthermore, CWD are often not afforded the dignity to take risks during outdoor play, nor are they afforded the same opportunities to participate as children without disabilities and therefore may not be receiving the same benefits associated with these types of play [28]. Allowing children to have autonomy and dignity over their play choices provides them with a sense of belonging and promotes quality participation [29].

While there is some literature exploring CWD experiences of outdoor and risky play, including preferences, benefits, barriers, and facilitators, this research has yet to be synthesized and summarized. Given the increased attention and importance placed on improving CWD access to outdoor play, with its risks [27], a better understanding of the literature is warranted. Therefore, this scoping review provides a summary of the available literature answering the following questions: (1) How does the literature describe CWD outdoor and risky play activities, including preferred activities and perceived benefits of outdoor and risky play for CWD? (2) How does the literature describe the barriers and facilitators CWD face to participating in outdoor and risky play? and (3) How does the literature describe strategies to support CWD outdoor and risky play?

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) scoping review methodology [30] and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [31]. A protocol for this scoping review was registered on Open Science Framework [32]. This scoping review is part of a larger national project with the Canadian Disability Participation Project (CDPP), aiming to better understand the lived experiences of CWD and support their risky play participation.

Inclusion criteria

Participants

This review considered publications that included CWD (ages birth to 24 years) and described aspects of or engagement in outdoor and risky play. We extended the age to 24 years to be consistent with the age range recommendations from the United Nations for CWD [33]. Articles were also included if the age range contained some overlap (e.g., ages 15 to 25 years) so long as the mean age of the sample was between birth and 24 years.

Concept

The concept of this scoping review was CWD participation in outdoor and/or risky play. Articles that described aspects of or engagement in outdoor and risky play were included. Articles that focused on accessibility aspects related to outdoor play spaces (e.g., playgrounds) were also included. Articles that included any setting were included (e.g., community, school, daycare), so long as the article focused on outdoor or risky play.

Context

Play is a cross-cultural phenomenon in which children engage in from a young age (1). Outdoor and risky play occur within a variety of physical environments, for example, at school, home, or in the community, that shape children’s opportunities to participate. CWD also encounter social environments which impact their outdoor and risky play participation, including decreased risk tolerance and increased discrimination (e.g., ableism) compared to children without disabilities [26].

In this review, context refers broadly to the settings, conditions, and impacting factors that influence outdoor and risky play for CWD. This includes but is not limited to formal and informal environments such as playgrounds, schoolyards, childcare centres, recreational programs, nature-based settings (e.g., parks, forests, green spaces), community play areas, and home or neighbourhood environments. The context also encompasses the social and organizational structures that influence children’s participation in outdoor and risky play, including policies or guidelines governing safety, accessibility, and inclusion; educational and healthcare systems; disability support services; and family and peer environments. Studies from any geographic region or cultural setting were considered, as meanings and practices of outdoor and risky play may vary across cultural norms. Through defining the context broadly, this review aims to capture the full range of environments and circumstances in which CWD experience outdoor and risky play.

Types of sources

This scoping review included peer-reviewed academic literature only. All study designs were included, such as randomized controlled trials, non-randomized controlled trials, and pre-post studies, amongst others. Qualitative studies with a variety of research designs such as phenomenology, grounded theory, ethnography, qualitative description, action research, and feminist research were included. Reviews, including systematic and scoping reviews that met the inclusion criteria, were included. Reviews were included in this scoping review as their rigorous nature provides high level findings and comprehensively maps the breadth of existing evidence on this topic. The primary studies within included reviews were searched to ensure that there was no repetition within the included articles for this study. We excluded non-peer-reviewed literature such as opinion papers, dissertations, and other grey literature.

Search strategy

For inclusion within this scoping review, articles were published in English. Publications were not limited by year of publication or the study’s geographic location. We developed a search strategy in consultation with a research librarian [SMK], which was peer reviewed by a second librarian. We conducted an initial limited search of CINAHL to identify articles on the topic and extract key search terms. The text contained in the titles and abstracts of relevant articles, and the index terms used to describe these, were used to develop a full search strategy for the following databases: CINAHL, APA PsycINFO, SportDiscus, Academic Search Premier, Medline (Ovid) and ERIC (ProQuest). The search strategy included all identified keywords and index terms and was adapted for each included database (Supplemental File 1). Reference lists of all included sources were screened for additional studies. Databases were searched from inception to November 2025.

Study selection

Upon completing the search, the records (as RIS files) were imported into Covidence software (Covidence Systematic Review Software, Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia). Duplicates were removed by the software. Two reviewers [ML, SAM] screened initial titles and abstracts independently using pre-determined eligibility criteria. Two reviewers [ML, SAM] reviewed full texts using pre-determined eligibility criteria independently. Conflicts (4.4% of articles) between the reviewers were resolved by consensus or the input from a third reviewer [SS].

Data extraction

Once screening was complete, included articles were processed for data extraction using Microsoft Excel. Data were extracted into a table with categories that were decided a priori. Each study objective was extracted, including a) identification information (i.e., title, first author, date, country, frameworks used in the study, setting); b) participants (i.e., sample size, sample description [e.g., gender, mean age and age range, and disability type, when specified]); c) study methods (i.e., aim, article type, study design, and measures); and d) study results that aligned with our research questions (i.e., outdoor play findings, risky play findings, and, where applicable, authors’ conclusions). Data were extracted from each article in duplicate independently by two reviewers [ML, SS]. The two extraction tables were then compared, and a final extraction table was finalized by consensus between reviewers and checked by the senior author [SAM].

Data charting and presentation

After organizing the extracted data into Tables, we used a narrative synthesis to provide an in-depth description of the data in relation to our research questions. This approach was selected as included sources were diverse (e.g., source type, study design, interventions, and outcomes reported). In conducting this narrative synthesis, we followed four steps: a) theory development, b) preliminary synthesis of included studies, c) exploring the relationship within and between studies, and d) assessing the robustness of the synthesis [34, 35].

Results

Search outcomes

Figure 1 documents the process of record identification, screening, eligibility, inclusion, and reasons for exclusion, as per PRISMA-ScR guidelines [31]. Our searches retrieved 14,931 records, of which 3,328 were removed through de-duplication. After title and abstract screening, an additional 11,199 records were excluded. The full texts of the remaining 404 were screened, of which 335 were excluded. After full-text screening, a total of 69 articles were included in this scoping review.

Summary of reviewed studies

Table 1 summarizes the included studies, organized by play type and study design. Included studies focused on outdoor play (n = 48), risky play (n = 9), or both outdoor and risky play (n = 12). Articles included were qualitative (n = 37), quantitative (n = 16), multi-methods (n = 11), or reviews (n = 5). Studies were conducted in 24 different countries, including the United States (n = 18), Australia (n = 12), Canada (n = 9), the United Kingdom (n = 6), and the Netherlands (n = 6). Sample sizes varied from 1 to 2553 participants and study participants’ ages or their children’s ages ranged from 0–21 years. Studies included children with physical (n = 8), neurodevelopmental (n = 23), and sensory disabilities (n = 2). A range of studies included descriptions of participants’ disabilities, such as specific diagnosis or characteristics. For example, common disabilities or impairments included, but were not limited to, Down syndrome, autism spectrum disorder, cerebral palsy, developmental coordination disorder, hearing impairments, and visual impairments.

Variability in study designs (e.g., qualitative, quantitative, and mixed methods), participant characteristics (age, gender, disability, and lived experience), and operational definitions of outdoor and risky play may have limited the direct comparability of results. This variability reflects the novelty and exploratory nature of outdoor and risky play for CWD. This study used the five levels of the SEM from Bronfenbrenner [25] to interpret and present the findings in a clear and comparable fashion.

Definitions and characteristics of outdoor and risky play for CWD

Outdoor play and related terms were described throughout the literature (n = 21) in 19 articles. These definitions varied and included definitions of playgrounds (n = 5), outdoor free play (n = 4), inclusive playgrounds (n = 2), play (n = 2), outdoor recreation (n = 2), free play (n = 1), recess (n = 1), social play (n = 1), solitary play (n = 1), loose parts play (n = 1), and green time (n = 1). Among the five studies that defined playgrounds, two [34, 35], described inclusive playgrounds as including accessible play equipment. Other commonalities among playground definitions included that they are spaces which provide children with play opportunities. For example, one study stated that playgrounds contain “flexible spaces that provide opportunities to play” ( [100], p.2201), while another indicated that playgrounds are spaces that are “challenging for everyone” ( [33], p.339). Dalpra and colleagues (2022) [72] stated that playgrounds allow for “free outdoor play opportunities” (p. 219). Two of these studies also defined inclusive playgrounds [34, 72], which encompassed that they were an environment where all children could play, regardless of ability.

Risky play and related terms were described throughout the literature (n = 7) in 6 articles. Definitions related to risky play included the following terms: risky play (n = 5), manageable risk-taking (n = 1), and rough-and-tumble play (n = 1). Definitions of risky play included characteristics such as it being challenging [36, 83] or including the possibility of injury [36] or danger [86]; in another paper, outdoor play was described as including elements of risk and challenge [99]. Other definitions included aspects of social and emotional risk-taking during play [84].

Types of outdoor and risky play CWD engage in and prefer to engage in

Our review revealed considerable diversity in the types of outdoor and risky play CWD’ engaged in (n = 25 and n = 11, respectively). For example, outdoor play frequently included play in parks or inclusive playgrounds [37, 41, 49, 52, 57, 60, 68, 72, 76], as well as unorganized sport/structured play (i.e., recreational/outdoor sport, games with rules led by adults) [47, 64, 75, 77]. Furthermore, studies spoke about children engaging in sensory-based play [37, 91], outdoor and play using loose parts [49, 73, 91]. Many studies described risky play using the categories of risky play [15, 16], as described earlier. For example, CWD engaged in play at great heights (e.g., climbing) [83–85, 90, 93, 96], play near dangerous elements (e.g., play near water), [81–83, 88, 95, 96], and rough-and-tumble play (e.g., wrestling) [85, 90, 96, 97]. Studies also discussed CWD preferred outdoor play (n = 18) and risky play (n = 4) activities to participating in indoor play. Outdoor play preferences included play in natural elements or spaces [39, 62, 75]; sensory play [37, 89]; loose parts play; swimming [70]; and swinging [50]. Some studies reported that CWD preferred playing with friends [40, 84], while other studies reported that children preferred solitary play [39, 51, 62, 81, 89, 97]. Studies reporting on risky play described how CWD enjoyed risky play because it encompassed movement and sensory components, which made it exciting and challenging for them.

Benefits of outdoor and risky play for CWD

Benefits of outdoor play (n = 14) for CWD included social benefits, such as playing with peers and developing friendships [37, 40, 47, 51, 52, 59, 80]; physical benefits, such as increased PA and motor development [47, 52, 56, 66, 80]; increased creativity and imagination [43, 73]; and increased confidence and self-efficacy [40, 80]. Two articles explored risky play and highlighted the contribution of risky play to CWD’ physical, social, and emotional development [84, 86]. Two articles mentioned benefits for CWD to participating in outdoor and risky play, which included improved sleep and mood [89] as well as increased autonomy, emotional regulation, self-confidence, and the development of motor skills [91].

• Increased family needs and time constraints associated with having a child with a disability (e.g., medical appointments) (Beekhuizen, 2025 [40]) Galbraith, 2020 [43]; Mactavish, 2004 [77]; Sterman, 2018; Sterman, 2020 [56])

• Increased fear of risk during play/decrease tolerance of risk (Beetham, 2019 [96]; Carbone, 2021 [81]; Deniz, 2024; Friedman, 2025 [91]; Horton, 2017 [44]; Moore 2021; Nabuzoka, 1999) [87]

• Fear of elopement (Coughlan, 2024 [89]; Shannon, 2021 [94])

• Increased adult interference during play (e.g., school staff, parents) (Fahy, 2021 [90]; Grady-Dominguez, 2021 [36]; Hu, 2016 [63]; Shannon, 2021 [94]; Stillianesis, 2022 [83])

• Negative attitudes towards children with disabilities (Friedman, 2025 [91]; Grady-Dominguez, 2019 [97]; Moore, 2022 [99]; Sterman, 2016 [80]; Takken, 2020 [98]; Visser, 2025 [59])

• Lack of inclusion from peers (Beekhuizen, 2025 [40]); Bhopti, 2020 [88]; Coughlan, 2024 [89]; VanEngelen, 2021 [58]; Visser, 2025 [59])

• Parental awareness about outdoor and risky play opportunities (Sterman, 2016 [80]; Sterman, 2020 [56])

• Insufficient knowledge about disabilities and their associated capabilities and limitations (Beekhuizen, 2025 [40])

• Supportive attitudes and perceptions from adults (e.g., parents, school staff, coaches) (Atkinson, 2017 [38]; Lieberman, 2023 [47]; Moore, 2021b [66]; Moore, 2022 [99]; Pratt, 2016 [68])

• Acceptance during play from peers (Alsarawi, 2024 [37]; Pan, 2008 [67]; Pratt, 2016 [68]; Valle, 2025; Spencer-Cavaliere, 2010 [54]; VanEngelen, 2021 [58])

• Decreased school staff interference during play (Grady-Dominguez, 2021) [36]

• Decreased parental fear toward risk (Niehues, 2016) [93]

• Parent co-participation in outdoor play (Arbour-Nicitopoulus, 2023; Moore, 2021a)

• Building trust with children (Niehues, 2016 [93]; Spencer, 2016 [95])

• Caregiver support during play (e.g., carrying children up play structures) (Ripat, 2012) [52]

• Children’s age (young) (Atkinson, 2017 [38])

• Child fatigue (Mactavish, 2004 [77]; Lieberman, 2023 [47])

• Decreased ability/skill (Eichengreen, 2023; Mactavish, 2004 [77]; Sterman, 2018)

• Living in low socioeconomic status (Arbour-Nicitopoulus, 2023; Deniz, 2024; Sterman, 2020 [56])

• Children’s hesitancy towards risky play (Grady-Dominguez, 2021) [36]

• Children asserting their personal agency during play (Alsarawi, 2024 [37]; Atkinson, 2017 [38]; Beekhuizen, 2025 [40]; Friedman, 2025 [91]; Spencer, 2016 [95])

• Personal gross motor abilities (Pratt, 2016) [68]

• Positive child attitudes and coping style (Beekhuizen, 2025 [40]; Moore, 2021b [66]; Moore, 2022 [99])

• Parental and household characteristics (e.g., parents who identified as Caucasian, multi-child households) (Arbour-Nicitopoulus, 2023)

Macrosystem

Barriers at the macrosystem level (n = 4) included the experience of social stigmatization for both CWD and their families within outdoor play spaces [42, 44, 66, 80, 99], the inflexibility of school environments and rules [38], limiting CWD participation in outdoor and risky play; the lack of accessible policies and legislations for outdoor play spaces [35]; and the closure of outdoor play spaces due to public health restrictions during the COVID-19 pandemic [38, 42]. Facilitators at the macrosystem level (n = 4) included social acceptance within outdoor play spaces for CWD and their families [58, 99]; having access to playgrounds which followed the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) [34, 68] or universal design [35]; and having systems and policies in place at schools to increase accessibility for CWD to engage in outdoor and risky play [38].

Exosystem

Barriers at the exosystem level (n = 10) focused primarily on playground characteristics and locations. The literature described playgrounds which did not meet the needs of CWD; these were often inaccessible for children with various impairments and needs or were places where families of CWD did not feel physically supported [34, 40, 55, 56, 59, 60, 66, 72, 99]. For example, many playgrounds contained inaccessible playground surfaces such as sand, gravel or grass [34, 35, 52, 99]. Other examples included poorly kept sidewalks used to access playgrounds [48, 50]. Literature also spoke about school playgrounds being inaccessible for CWD [90] and that while some playgrounds claimed to be accessible, CWD could often not access higher components on playground structures, decreasing their access to risky play [21]. Literature also noted the lack of accessible playgrounds within communities that CWD live in [71, 75] and highlighted the role of neighbourhood design, describing more barriers to outdoor and risky play in urban compared to rural communities [61, 89, 94]. Other studies described financial barriers to CWD participating in outdoor or risky play. For example, assistive devices are often required for CWD to engage in outdoor and risky play [78], which are costly [77]. Studies described a lack of outdoor play options for CWD to engage in compared to the options provided for children without disabilities [88]. Finally, a barrier to outdoor and risky play at this level include a lack of safety components (e.g., non-fenced in play areas) [89].

Facilitators at the exosystem level (n = 9) included playground characteristics such as having ramps allowing children with mobility aids to move around freely [37]; access to loose parts [34, 40, 50, 69]; having sensory components [34, 37] and components which allow for solitary play [34]; the inclusion of safety features [89, 99]; and playgrounds which were designed to also accommodate adults when assistance is needed [34]. Other facilitators included neighbourhood location for outdoor play (e.g. rural areas) [94], and playground location (e.g., within walking distance) [40, 99]. Finally, as stated, many CWD require adaptive equipment or devices, and having access to these, such as power wheelchairs and ride-on cars, made engaging in outdoor and risky play easier for CWD [47, 74, 76].

Mesosystem

Barriers at the mesosystem level (n = 8) were barriers created by important people in CWD life who may act as gatekeepers to their outdoor and risky play. A common barrier in the literature included the increased family needs and stress associated with having a CWD; for example, increased medical appointments and adaptive equipment required [40, 43, 55, 56, 77]. Another barrier was a decrease in risk tolerance for CWD compared to children without disabilities, from caregivers and school staff [23, 44, 81, 86, 87, 96]. This often occurred as they feared children would get injured or they had a fear of elopement (i.e., run away) [89, 94]. Due to this fear, many adults would interfere during outdoor play, acting as barriers for CWD to engage in risky play [36, 63, 83, 90, 94]. Negative and limiting attitudes towards CWD acted as a barrier for them to engage in outdoor and risky play [55, 59, 66, 97, 98], and sometimes led to a lack of inclusion in play from their peers [40, 58, 59, 88, 89]. In line with this barrier, it was found that decreased knowledge about disabilities and CWD abilities was a barrier to outdoor play [40]. The final barrier at this level was caregivers’ lack of awareness about opportunities and programs for their children to engage in more outdoor and risky play [55, 80].

Facilitators at the mesosystem level (n = 7) included supportive attitudes and positive perceptions from adults towards CWD [38, 47, 66, 68, 99]; acceptance from peers, which led to the inclusion of CWD in outdoor and risky play with peers [37, 54, 58, 67, 68, 84], and a decrease in fear [93] and interference [36] during outdoor and risky play. Other facilitators to CWD participation included caregivers participating in outdoor play with their children [23, 61], caregivers and school staff building trust with children, in that they can understand their own boundaries [93, 95], and caregivers physically supporting their child’s outdoor play, for example, carrying their children up play structures when ramps are not available [52].

Microsystem

The final level of the SEM, which is closest to the individual, is the microsystem level. Barriers at this level (n = 5) include personal factors of the child, such as young age [38], fatigue [47], a decrease in their ability or skill (either perceived or actual) [56, 73, 77], and an increased hesitancy towards risky play [36]. Family factors also were noted to influence CWD participation in outdoor and risky play, such as having a low socioeconomic status and resultantly, fewer opportunities [56, 61, 86]. Facilitators at the microsystem level (n = 4) also included personal characteristics of the child, such as an increase in their gross motor abilities, allowing them to increase their participation [68] and positive attitudes towards participating as they value outdoor and risky play [40, 66, 99]. When CWD were able to assert their agency and autonomy during play, this facilitated outdoor and risky play participation [37, 38, 40, 101]. Studies also noted that parental and household characteristics influenced CWD participation. For example, white CWD and CWD who had siblings were more likely to participate in outdoor and risky play compared to children of other races and living in single-child households [61].

Proposed strategies described in the literature to support CWD engagement in outdoor and risky play

Using the SEM, no studies reported strategies to support outdoor and/or risky play at the chronosystem level. Three studies reported on strategies to support outdoor play at the macrosystem level, which included developing play programming centered around social justice [17], developing play interventions to change society's general attitudes towards CWD [59] and ensuring that families have informational resources to support CWD outdoor play, specifically during pandemic recovery [61]. All three of these strategies were conceptually suggested in the literature to support CWD outdoor play.

Seventeen studies provided suggestions of strategies to support CWD outdoor and/or risky play at the exosystem level. Strategies to support outdoor play (n = 12) at this level included providing inclusive physical playground features (e.g., smooth and level surfaces, loose parts, accessible entry points, ramps, etc.) [34, 37, 40, 49, 53, 66, 78, 79] (and social inclusion features (e.g., sensory components, communication equipment) [34, 37, 79]. It is also important that when designing inclusive play spaces, they be co-designed with the users, the users in this case being CWD [72], as well as OTs who have an understanding of CWD play needs [57] and that architects and designers understand these needs when designing and building play spaces (50; 95). Finally, it is important to have trained staff to support CWD and the use of adaptive equipment when developing inclusive play facilities [45]. Strategies to support risky play (n = 1), included providing parents with the knowledge around benefits through interventions during early childhood to support their risk-taking during play [86]. Strategies to support both outdoor and risky play at the exosystem level (n = 4) were similar to those of outdoor play and included the importance of designing play spaces that are accessible for CWD [92], where they would be able to participate and take risks outdoors without being overly exposed to danger [99]. It was suggested that when designing play-based interventions, that facilitators to these interventions (e.g., OTs) consider CWD preferences and needs to be successful in promoting outdoor and risky play [89]. Furthermore, it is suggested to provide recreational training and safety plans to support inclusive outdoor and risky play opportunities for CWD in rural communities [71]. All of these strategies were conceptually suggested in the literature to support CWD outdoor play and risky play.

Nine studies provided suggestions of strategies to support CWD outdoor and risky play at the mesosystem level. Strategies to support CWD outdoor play (n = 4) included professional development opportunities for teachers to support CWD outdoor play (e.g., inclusive play facilitation) [65, 74], as active support from adults was seen to help promote social inclusion during outdoor loose parts play [70]. Lastly, peer-mediated interventions using the buddy system were seen to promote inclusive outdoor play opportunities for CWD [46]. It is important to note that of these strategies to support outdoor play, one was empirically tested [67], indicating the importance of professional development interventions for teachers to support CWD outdoor play. Strategies to support CWD risky play (n = 4) all included the importance of adults in facilitating supportive environments for CWD to take risks safely during play [81–84] through providing them with dignity [90], autonomy [93], regular opportunities to engage in play [84], and play choices [86]. Finally, strategies to support outdoor and risky play (n = 1) included school staff building trusting relationships and adopting a child-led supportive practice with children to promote safe risk-taking [91]. No studies reported strategies to support outdoor and/or risky play at the microsystem level.

Discussion

This scoping review aimed to provide a summary of the available literature describing CWD outdoor and risky play activities, including preferred activities, perceived benefits, barriers and facilitators to participation, and strategies to support CWD outdoor and risky play. Results from this review indicate that the literature exploring outdoor and risky play for CWD is diverse in study design, location, and across age and disability. This review found that there is a large amount of literature which explores CWD participation in outdoor play compared to the literature exploring risky play. Few articles provided definitions of outdoor or risky play, while many provided examples of the type of activities CWD were engaging in during outdoor and risky play. It was recorded that many CWD enjoyed engaging in swinging, loose parts play, play at playgrounds, sensory play, and solitary play. Benefits of outdoor and risky play were also reported, and included physical (e.g., increased PA, development of motor skills), social (e.g., development of friendships), and emotional benefits (e.g., increased confidence, self-efficacy, and autonomy), in addition to improved sleep. Various barriers and facilitators CWD faced to engaging in outdoor and risky play were mentioned across studies, which have been organized using the SEM. Finally, articles reporting on strategies to support CWD engagement in outdoor and risky play have been organized using the SEM. Strategies to support outdoor and risky play fell across levels and included the importance of increasing policies surrounding CWD and their access to outdoor and risky play as well as working with CWD and their families to co-create community spaces and programs which are accessible and inclusive to foster quality participation for all children.

This review found that outdoor and risky play are important to CWD and their families [39, 47, 52, 55], yet they face barriers to accessing and participating in these spaces. Despite the current literature presented here, there is still much more work to be done in this area. We must consider strategies to support CWD outdoor and risky play across levels of the SEM. Specifically considering the higher levels of the chronosystem and macrosystem, for example, cultural and societal norms as they relate to risky play. Cultural and societal norms that may view CWD as not able to participate or take risks during play significantly impact their participation. Furthermore, cultural norms and geographic make-up of a location can influence CWD play choices [56]. To support CWD participation, it is therefore important to focus our attention on higher-level system barriers that may have the most widespread impact while also considering the social and cultural context. It is important to shift focus to policy gaps as they can amplify lower-level (exosystem, mesosystem & microsystem) barriers, such as accessibility within schools, playgrounds, and parks, as well as CWD access to funding for assistive devices to participate in outdoor and risky play. CWD have the right to quality participation when engaging in play and PA [65, 102]. It is therefore important to foster environments where CWD have the autonomy to choose how they want to play, creating a sense of belonging and quality participation [103]. Furthermore, we must not place this task onto families but support CWD outdoor and risky play by moving forward with accessible and inclusive policies.

Starting at one of the outer levels of the SEM, the macrosystem, this review found that when playgrounds were ADA compliant or followed other accessible policies and regulations, such as universal design, they were facilitators to CWD engaging in outdoor and risky play [34, 35, 68]. The Canadian government introduced the Accessible Canada Act in 2018 to ensure a barrier-free country; a year later, it came into force [104]. In addition, five provinces (British Columbia, Manitoba, Ontario, Nova Scotia, and Newfoundland) have created accessibility acts. For example, the Accessibility for Ontarians with Disabilities Act (AODA) states minimum requirements for outdoor play spaces, including parks and playgrounds to include firm and stable ground surfaces on the way to the play space; surfaces must be level without slopes, the playground must incorporate accessible features (e.g., sensory and outdoor play components), and they must have clearance for children and caregivers with disabilities to be able to move to and around the space [105]. Although accessibility standards are important, they do not always allow CWD the opportunity to engage in outdoor and risky play at the same level as their peers, as playgrounds often do not contain alternative routes for CWD to access higher points [34, 52]. It is important that when designing outdoor play spaces, they go above meeting the minimum requirements and work alongside CWD and families to support their needs and quality participation [34, 53, 60, 72].

Inclusive spaces are important for CWD quality participation in outdoor and risky play, yet we must also consider how people influence CWD play experiences. This review found that people who support CWD’s participation can have a significant impact on their outdoor and risky play experiences. For example, when caregivers or school staff are more fearful and have decreased risk tolerance, these act as barriers to CWD engaging in outdoor and risky play [36, 44, 81, 86, 96]; positive attitudes and building trust with children are critical for facilitating CWD participation in outdoor and risky play [38, 47, 66, 68, 93, 99, 101]. Trusting CWD and providing them with autonomy during outdoor play can help to increase their risk-taking during play [28, 93, 101].

There are many personal factors at the microsystem level which influence CWD outdoor and risky play, including increased fatigue [47, 77] and decreased skill or ability to participate [56, 73, 77]. Despite these intrapersonal barriers, CWD have shown through their agency that they want to play outdoors and take risks [38, 95]. Many accessibility acts include standards related to the built environment, but many times these standards do not extend to play spaces such as playgrounds.

Implementing strategies at higher policy and community levels, such as expanding accessibility acts beyond the bare standards of accessibility to the built environment, is needed to help CWD access and feel included within outdoor play spaces. Furthermore, we must support families of CWD to help facilitate increased participation in outdoor and risky play, as families with CWD have increased family needs (i.e., medical appointments and increased costs of adaptive equipment) and often feel socially unaccepted within outdoor play spaces by others due to their child’s needs and play behaviours which may not always fall within the norm of play [43, 56, 77, 80]. This review found that caregivers of CWD were unaware of opportunities and programs which could support their children to participate in outdoor play [55, 56]. It is important that families of CWD feel supported by their communities, and that spaces where children can engage in outdoor and risky play are both accessible and inclusive to the entire family to help increase CWD participation in outdoor and risky play.

Implications, recommendations, and future directions

The literature from this review provides many suggestions to support CWD participation in outdoor and risky play, as they face additional barriers to accessing and being included within outdoor play spaces compared to children without disabilities. Strategies to support CWD outdoor and risky play can be implemented at various settings (i.e., school, community, home). Frequent examples of strategies to support CWD in outdoor and risky play found in this review, included co-designing outdoor play spaces with CWD and their families to ensure they meet their needs [34, 53, 60, 72]; providing school staff with increased support, such as professional developmental interventions (i.e., risk-reframing interventions) for school staff with a focus on inclusion for CWD [63, 106]; having trained staff present during outdoor play to support CWD and assist them with their needs (using certain equipment) [34, 45], and having more than the traditional playground structures and equipment present at outdoor play spaces, for example providing children with loose parts [34, 49, 79]. As reflected in these suggestions for the future, it is important to support CWD and their families’ participation in outdoor play through higher-level (e.g., policy and community) strategies and implementations rather than focusing on individual strategies. This will help to support more CWD in accessing and being included within outdoor and risky play while also removing part of the burden from the family. Furthermore, it is important that CWD have access to outdoor play spaces in nature, as nature connectedness has been shown to support children’s happiness and overall well-being [22]. Future research should explore CWD opportunities to access and be included within natural spaces to support their participation in outdoor play.

In addition to these recommendations, providing educational risk-reframing opportunities for caregivers, teachers, and others who support CWD outdoor and risky play may help to facilitate an environment where children can engage more fully in risky play without adult interference. Risk-reframing interventions have been shown to increase parental risk tolerance [107], yet they have not been implemented within a disability population. As there was less literature exploring risky play compared to outdoor play, it is also recommended that researchers continue to examine risky play experiences of CWD, including benefits, barriers and facilitators to better understand how CWD experience risky play. We recommend that future work focus on the experience of children with physical, neurodevelopmental, and visual impairments and their risky play participation, as each population will face their own unique barriers and facilitators to participation. As part of the larger project with the CDPP, exploring CWD risky play, work is underway to explore how caregivers of CWD and CWD themselves perceive and characterize risky play and the factors they believe influence their risky play. Through understanding how CWD experience risky play, this will pave the way for future research to implement risky play interventions to create supportive pathways for CWD to engage fully and inclusively with their peers in outdoor and risky play throughout their childhood. This scoping review has identified the need for this work, as we know little about the experience of CWD in risky play.

Articles in this review used a variety of definitions to define risky play. Many of these definitions focused on physical risk-taking, while more recent literature mentions emotional and social risk-taking within their definitions [36, 41]. Yet, there has been no research exploring how CWD define risky play from their own experiences. Future research must consider the definitions and categories of risky play to ensure that CWD and their risky play experiences are reflected within these definitions.

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review was the first, to our knowledge, to examine the literature regarding the impact of disability on children’s outdoor and risky play. One strength of this review is that we used the JBI methodology for conducting a scoping review, a well-documented process, which is transparent and offers detailed guidance [108]. Our search was not limited by date or country and therefore included a wide range of articles. While this review presented a particularly comprehensive synthesis of the peer-reviewed literature examining participation in outdoor and risky play for CWD, it is not without limitations. First, scoping reviews have inherent limitations because the focus is to provide breadth rather than depth of information on a particular topic [33]. Second, we limited our search to peer-reviewed articles and English language articles only, meaning that we may have missed documents noting innovative outdoor and risky play practices in community-based reports, as well as in different languages. Third, only peer-reviewed articles were included. Grey literature that may have included relevant information on outdoor and risky play for CWD was not captured or included in this scoping review.

Conclusions

This review highlighted that CWD’ are engaging in diverse types of outdoor and risky play, and mentioned specific preferences, including engaging in nature and valuing the outdoor environment and movement within risky play. Outdoor and risky play lead to various physical (i.e., increased PA, developed motor skills), cognitive (i.e., increased creativity, increased imagination), social (i.e., improved feelings of belonging) and emotional (i.e., increased coping strategies, increased enjoyment) benefits. Barriers and facilitators spanned all levels of the SEM, and included factors such as social stigma, accessibility policies and legislation, playground structure accessibility and location, access to adaptive equipment, attitudes towards risky play for CWD, children’s age, and gross motor abilities. As a result, authors of the included articles proposed strategies to support CWD future engagement in outdoor and risky play across levels of the SEM, including, involving CWD and their families in the design of outdoor play spaces, increasing accessibility of playgrounds to include unique playground equipment for all children to participate and experience risk-taking, and having trained and supportive staff to help facilitate outdoor play and promote social inclusion. Through improving CWD access and inclusion in outdoor and risky play, we can support their physical and psychosocial health, including autonomy, creativity, and a sense of belonging. Future work should continue the efforts to support CWD outdoor and risky play by exploring CWD experiences and implementing higher-level interventions, such as accessibility policies and practices. Furthermore, CWD and their families should be involved in co-designing outdoor play spaces, so that they meet their needs, and that families and children feel supported and a sense of belonging within these spaces. Most importantly, we must provide CWD the autonomy to make their own decisions during outdoor and risky play, as it is their right to access and fully participate in outdoor and risky play throughout their childhood.
