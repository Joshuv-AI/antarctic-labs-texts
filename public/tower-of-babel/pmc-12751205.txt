Development and validation of the HWARNER models for predicting atrial tachyarrhythmias recurrence after AF ablation: a retrospective cohort study with emphasis on HbA1c
Authors: Min Xu, Ruikun Jia, Juan Chan, Hanlin Wang, Qidong Gao, Sitai Liu, Dayong Zhang, Yunfeng Di, Kaijun Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPredictive models for recurrence following atrial fibrillation (AF) radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) have potential value in minimizing the risk of recurrent atrial tachyarrhythmias (ATAs). This study aims to develop and validate models to optimize patient selection and post-ablation management.MethodsA retrospective analysis of consecutive AF patients undergoing RFCA from January 2021 to August 2023 identified risk factors for ATAs recurrence through regression analysis, leading to the development of two predictive models, HWARNER 1 and HWARNER 2, which were preliminarily externally validated.ResultsSeven risk factors were identified: Weight > 77.5 kg, HbA1c > 5.75%, RA (dimension / > 46.5 mm), NT-proBNP > 650.18 pg/ml, Early AF recurrence, Redo procedures, and Height > 171.5 cm. Based on these factors, the HWARNER models showed high predictive accuracy with AUCs of 0.775 and 0.773, outperforming established models. Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed significant differences in ATAs recurrence between low- and high-risk groups (HWARNER 1: 93.0% vs. 59.1%, P < 0.001; HWARNER 2: 94.5% vs. 67.9%, P < 0.001) with an median follow-up of one year. In subgroup analysis, HbA1c showed stronger predictive value in patients with BMI > 26.7 (OR = 7.191, 95% CI: 1.375–37.594, P = 0.019). In paroxysmal AF (PAF) patients, the pulmonary vein isolation plus low voltage zones-stepwise (PVI + LVZs-Stepwise) protocol showed no benefit over PVI alone.ConclusionThe HWARNER models have the potential to predict ATAs recurrence after RFCA, providing a valuable tool for patient selection and management. HbA1c is an important independent risk factor.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05369-1.

Introduction

Radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) has emerged as the first-line therapy for atrial fibrillation (AF), with substantial evidence demonstrating its benefits in maintaining sinus rhythm and improving quality of life [1–3]. However, the recurrence of AF following the procedure remains high, particularly in non-paroxysmal AF cases. The estimated rates of sinus rhythm maintenance at 5, 10, and 15 years after the initial RFCA are as follows [4]: for paroxysmal AF (PAF), the rates are 67.8%, 56.3%, and 47.6%; for non-paroxysmal AF (NPAF), the rates are 46.6%, 35.6%, and 26.5%; and for long-standing persistent AF (LsAF), the rates are 30.4%, 18.0%, and 3.4%. Additionally, patients who underwent multiple procedures showed slightly improved outcomes: for PAF, the rates reached 80.3%, 72.6%, and 62.5%; for NPAF, the rates reached 60.1%, 50.2%, and 42.5%; while for LsAF, the rates showed 43.4%, 32.0%, and 20.6%.

How can we optimize the selection of suitable patients for RFCA and post-procedure management? It would be beneficial to identify risk factors for AF recurrence and establish an accurate and generalizable predictive model.

Our study analyzed clinical data from the pulmonary vein isolation plus low voltage zones-stepwise (PVI + LVZs-Stepwise) protocol in RFCA and identified a few interesting risk factors associated with late recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmias (ATAs). Based on these findings, we developed two predictive models to estimate ATAs recurrence following RFCA. These models can help optimize patient selection and serve as a valuable reference for post-procedure management.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective observational study included data from all patients diagnosed with symptomatic drug-refractory AF who underwent RFCA at Mianyang 404 Hospital (from January 2021 to August 2023). AF was categorized as PAF if it spontaneously terminated within seven days, and as NPAF if the episode persisted for more than seven days or required pharmacological or electrical cardioversion.

Inclusion criteria: (i) Patients eligible for AF ablation; (ii) RFCA performed; (iii) Follow-up exceeding 6 months.

Exclusion criteria: (i) Previous cardiac surgery; (ii) Left atrial appendage (LAA) isolation; (iii) Right atrial ablation except for tricuspid isthmus or superior vena cava (SVC) isolation; (iv) Participation in other intervention studies.

The protocol adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board of Mianyang 404 Hospital on August, 2024. After verbal consent by phone, we obtained written consent from all participants by mail.

Electrophysiological study and mapping

All antiarrhythmic drugs except beta-blockers were stopped for at least five half-lives before the procedure. Under deep anesthesia, a decapolar mapping catheter (DECANAV™, Biosense Webster, CA, USA) was placed in the coronary sinus and a quadrupole electrode catheter was inserted into the right ventricle via the right femoral vein. Then, an atrial septal puncture was performed. The CARTO 3 System was used to reconstruct 3D electro-anatomical maps with a multipolar PentaRay catheter.

Catheter ablation protocol

Ablation was initiated from PVI using ST or STSF Catheters (Biosense Webster, CA, USA), chosen at the operators’ discretion. If AF persisted following ablation, electrical cardioversion was performed. Subsequently, a systematic modification of the LVZs restricted to the left atrium, characterized by bipolar voltage amplitudes < 0.3 mV in sinus rhythm, was implemented and referred to as PVI plus LVZs-Stepwise. Instead of complete substrate modification, a linear isolation strategy was employed to construct a tailored pathway through the core of LVZs (bipolar voltage amplitudes < 0.05 mV) to the anatomical barriers (Fig. 1A). However, the “box” approach was conducted for LVZs located in the posterior wall of LA (Fig. 1B) to minimize the risk of esophageal injury. The Ablation Index (AI) was performed to determine the optimal lesion depth, with the following AI settings: for right wide antral circumferential ablation (WACA) on the anterior/top wall, 500–520; for left WACA on the top/crest wall, 450–480; and for the anterior wall/mitral isthmus, 550.

A comprehensive electrophysiological study was conducted to induce and treat atrial tachycardia using active mapping and entrainment, with an additional assessment of bidirectional conduction block during sinus rhythm for linear ablation lesions.

AADs administration

Antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs) were given during the 90-day blank period post-procedure, depending on the sinus node recovery time (SNRT). AADs were prescribed as follows: Amiodarone or Dronedarone for SNRT < 1500 ms, Dronedarone for 1500 ms ≤ SNRT < 2500 ms, and no AADs for SNRT > 2500 ms, considering contraindications.

During the blank period, patients received oral anticoagulants like Warfarin and NOACs (Rivaroxaban, Dabigatran), with low-risk patients discontinuing therapy based on CHA2DS2-VASc scores.

Clinical follow-up

Patients were followed up post-discharge, with initial monthly visits at our AF clinic for the first three months, followed by tri-monthly visits thereafter. Routine assessments included 12-lead ECG and 24-h Holter monitoring, with seven-day Holter monitoring for suspicious symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was also performed.

Recurrent ATAs were defined as episodes of atrial fibrillation, flutter, or tachycardia lasting for 30 s or longer. Recurrence within 90 days post-procedure (blanking period) was defined as early recurrence, and after 90 days as clinical recurrence.

Statistical analysis

After conducting the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk normality tests, continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD if normally distributed, or as Interquartile Range (IQR) if not. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages.

For two independent samples in our study, the appropriate statistical tests were selected based on the data distribution and variance: one-way ANOVA was used for normally distributed data with equal variances, Welch’s t-test was employed for normally distributed data with unequal variances, and the Mann–Whitney U test was conducted for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests, with Fisher’s exact test employed for smaller sample sizes.

A part of variables were dichotomized based on the optimal Youden index and subsequently analyzed using binary logistic regression to identify predictors of recurrence risk. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) and Precision-Recall (PR) curves were employed to assess the predictive ability of the models. Patients were divided into high/low risk groups, and differences were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves and log-rank tests.

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0.1 and R 4.4.1, with a thorough cleaning process addressing missing values, outliers, and maintaining consistency across all study analyses.

Results

ATAs recurrence prediction models

A total of 586 patients were initially screened, and 337 patients (63.2% with PAF and 36.8% with NPAF) were ultimately included in the analysis (Fig. 2). Specifically, 279 patients were in the ATAs-free group, while 58 patients were in the ATAs recurrence group.

The average age was 66 years, with a balanced gender distribution. Patients had a mean weight of 65 kg, a BMI of 24.8, and an eGFR of 61.9 ml/min/1.73 m2. Nearly half of the patients showed mitral or tricuspid regurgitation, and approximately one-third had aortic regurgitation. Echocardiography revealed normal left and right ventricular dimensions and cardiac function in the majority of patients. Voltage mapping revealed that the percentage of anterior wall LVZs was approximately 22.3%. The median PVI time was 46 min, averaging 21 min for LPVs and 25 min for RPVs. The overall ATAs recurrence rate was 17.2%. Notably, NPAF patients demonstrated a significantly higher recurrence rate compared to PAF patients (25.2% vs. 12.2%, P = 0.004), indicating an inherent difference in arrhythmogenic substrate between the two AF subtypes.

Continuous variables were dichotomized using optimal cut-off points determined by ROC analysis based on the Youden index. Univariate logistic regression identified eight significant predictors of ATAs recurrence from these dichotomized variables. Multivariate analysis subsequently established HbA1c > 5.75%, NT-proBNP > 650.18 pg/ml, and height > 171.5 cm as independent risk factors (Table 2).

In the aforementioned analyses, we observed a significant association between HbA1c levels and FFATAs. This relationship was further examined using KM analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2), which revealed that patients with HbA1c levels above 5.75% had a significantly lower rate of maintaining sinus rhythm compared to those with lower HbA1c levels (75.6% vs. 93.6%, P < 0.001). In the subgroup with BMI > 26.7, HbA1c showed a stronger predictive value for ATAs recurrence (OR = 7.191, 95% CI: 1.375–37.594, P = 0.019). Conversely, in patients with BMI < 26.7, the predictive ability of HbA1c was less pronounced (OR = 2.860, 95% CI: 0.882–9.275, P = 0.080).

A significant association was observed between elevated HbA1c and ATAs recurrence. KM analysis revealed that patients with HbA1c > 5.75% had lower FFATAs compared to those with lower HbA1c (75.6% vs. 93.6%, P < 0.001). This predictive effect was more prominent in patients with BMI > 26.7 (OR = 7.191, 95% CI: 1.375–37.594, P = 0.019) than in those with BMI < 26.7 (OR = 2.860, 95% CI: 0.882–9.275, P = 0.080).

The effect of PVI + LVZs-Stepwise protocol in PAF

Among 213 patients with PAF, propensity score matching (PSM) produced matched cohorts for the PVI + LVZs-Stepwise and PVI-only groups (Supplementary Table). Rigorous baseline analysis with KM curves and log-rank tests found no significant differences in freedom from AF (FFAF) and FFATAs between groups, both in the blanking period and clinically (Supplementary Fig. 3).

The incidence of atrial tachycardia/flutter (AT/AFL) was only observed once in each group. No significant differences were observed in cardiac chamber, function, or valvular regurgitation of the aortic, mitral, and tricuspid valves before and after the procedure.

Additionally, there were two instances of post-procedure pericardial effusion > 2 mm in the PVI + group and one instance in the PVI group, with no statistically significant difference. No other severe adverse events (SAEs), including peri-procedural death, cardiac tamponade, myocardial infarction, thromboembolic events, stroke, atrio-esophageal fistula formation, pulmonary vein stenosis, or atrioventricular block, were observed. Thus, the PVI + LVZs-Stepwise strategy demonstrated a comparable safety profile to PVI alone.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and externally validated two novel predictive models—HWARNER 1 and HWARNER 2—for ATAs recurrence following RFCA in AF patients. These models outperformed traditional scores such as APPLE [5], DR-FLASH [6], CHA2DS2-VASc [7], HAS-BLED [8], and demonstrated strong predictive power across both PAF and NPAF subgroups. This performance advantage may be attributed to three key innovations: a modified ablation strategy, regionally tailored modeling, and the inclusion of glucose metabolism parameters.

First, our study employed a novel ablation strategy, specifically the PVI + LVZs-Stepwise protocol. This approach constitutes an adaptation of low-voltage zone modification and offers a distinct variation compared to protocols used in other studies. While this approach did not significantly improve outcomes in PAF—likely due to statistical limitations and the fact that PAF recurrence stems primarily from PV reconnection rather than substrate abnormality—it may hold greater promise in NPAF patients, whose arrhythmias are more dependent on a diseased atrial substrate.

Secondly, the HWARNER models were developed using a regionally specific (Southwest Chinese) cohort, incorporating local clinical and anthropometric traits. This design captures critical nuances for this population that are often absent in models derived from other ethnicities. Consequently, while this enhances local relevance, the model's generalizability to other populations requires further external validation across diverse ethnic and geographic groups.

Thirdly, our inclusion of HbA1c enhances the clinical relevance of the models. The role of glucose metabolism in AF recurrence is supported by several studies: The Framingham Heart Study reported a 34% increased AF risk in diabetics [9], and elevated HbA1c has been linked to higher AF incidence with an OR of 1.14 per 1% increase [10]. Our findings align with these, identifying HbA1c > 5.75% as an independent predictor of ATAs recurrence. Notably, this association was stronger in patients with BMI > 26.7, suggesting a synergistic effect between obesity and glucose dysregulation. This emphasizes the need for glycemic control, particularly in obese patients, as a strategy to reduce recurrence risk.

Unlike SCALE-CryoAF [11], ALARMEc [12], and ATLAS [13], which require complex assessments such as coronary angiography, cardiomyopathy diagnosis process, and specialized software for indexed left atrial volume, the HWARNER models incorporate more readily obtainable clinical factors, thereby enhancing model sensitivity and accuracy.

In addition to metabolic markers, NT-proBNP and height emerged as significant independent predictors. Elevated NT-proBNP—an indicator of cardiac wall stress—showed stronger predictive ability than LVEF in our cohort. This might be explained by the predominance of HFpEF patients in our sample, as AF is highly prevalent in HFpEF (up to 50% [14]) and NT-proBNP better reflects diastolic burden in these cases. Our identification of height (> 171.5 cm) as a recurrence risk factor corroborates previous findings from the Copenhagen City Heart Study [15] and the British Regional Heart Study [16], possibly reflecting a link between body size, atrial dimensions, and conduction heterogeneity.

Interestingly, while previous studies highlighted left atrial size and fibrosis as predictors of AF recurrence, the DECAAF study demonstrated a significant link between the presence of left atrial LAZs and the recurrence of arrhythmias [17–20], we found that right atrial diameter had greater discriminatory power in our model. In addition, the CAPLA study and the other studies confirmed a substantial correlation between the presence of LAZs in the posterior wall and the recurrence of arrhythmias [17, 18, 21, 22]. This could be due to our LVZs-stepwise modification in the left atrium, which may have attenuated the predictive role of LA diameter. The observed link between right atrial enlargement and recurrence suggests a possible pathophysiological role of right atrial cardiomyopathy or volume overload, raising the hypothesis that volume management, including diuretic therapy, might benefit select patients post-ablation.

Regarding ablation strategies, existing literature (e.g., STABLE-SR-II) [23], DECAAF [19]) indicates that extensive LA fibrosis correlates with higher recurrence. Although DECAAF II did not find significant benefits with MRI-guided fibrosis ablation [19, 20], it showed promise in patients with < 20% fibrosis. These discrepancies may stem from aggressive, uniform ablation strategies that prolong procedure time and increase complication risk. The VOLCANO trial, for example, found that extensive ablation within LVZs significantly increased AT risk (71% vs. 32%, P < 0.0001) [24, 25]. Importantly, our method—focusing on the core of LVZs rather than complete homogenization—differs from traditional strategies, potentially offering a balance between efficacy and procedural safety. Additionally, studies like Jongmin Hwang et al. [26] have linked LVZs with CFAEs, suggesting that these regions harbor specific arrhythmogenic substrates. However, consistent replication of these results remains challenging, and further multicenter trials are needed.

Our study does have limitations. The sample size, while adequate for model development, may limit generalizability. The absence of continuous rhythm monitoring may have underestimated asymptomatic recurrences. Additionally, the PVI + LVZs-Stepwise protocol was only assessed in PAF patients, and matching between groups based on LVZ characteristics was suboptimal. External validation, although attempted, remains limited in scale and scope. Finally, while our models performed well across both PAF and NPAF, future studies should further explore whether these AF subtypes exhibit distinct pathophysiological trajectories in relation to our risk factors.

Limitations

This study has a few limitations. Its retrospective, single-center design may introduce selection bias. Although preliminary external validation was performed, prospective, multicenter studies are needed to confirm generalizability. The 12-month follow-up period limits assessment of long-term outcomes, as atrial tachyarrhythmias are known to recur beyond this timeframe; thus, our findings represent intermediate rather than long-term results. Future studies should include extended follow-up of 3–5 years. Furthermore, only 22% of patients underwent extended rhythm monitoring at 6 months, which likely led to under detection of asymptomatic recurrence. Finally, the PVI + LVZs-Stepwise protocol was assessed only in PAF patients, and its comparison with the PVI-only group was limited by sample size and unmeasured confounders.

Conclusion

The HWARNER model accurately predicts the risk of ATAs recurrence following RFCA, considering factors such as weight > 77.5 kg, HbA1c > 5.75%, RA (dimension/> 46.5 mm), NT-proBNP > 650.18 pg/ml, early AF recurrence, redo procedures, and height > 171.5 cm. Compared with PVI alone, patients with PAF do not benefit from the PVI plus LVZs-Stepwise Protocol.
