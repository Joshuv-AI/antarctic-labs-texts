Experimentally characterising the dynamical landscape of an active MEMS cantilever
Authors: Seigan Hayashi, Chris Cameron, Stefanie Gutschmidt, Rua Murray, Bernd Krauskopf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicro- and nano-electromechanical systems (M/NEMS) are in high demand for cutting-edge future technological solutions. Their strongly nonlinear nature is regarded as beneficial for optimising performance metrics for a wide range of engineering applications. As model-free experimentation remains limited to forward time observations, important dynamic features can be left uncharted, resulting in an incomplete dynamical landscape. Here we address this issue by employing experimental continuation — a model-free technique for constructing bifurcation diagrams by tracking steady-states and/or periodic responses directly in a physical experiment. This approach is unexplored for investigating micro-scale systems with fast timescales. Our state-of-the-art experiment investigates an active MEMS cantilever operated as a self-oscillator with a natural frequency of 100 kHz; the fastest timescales of a mechanical system probed with experimental continuation. We explore the cantilever’s nonlinear responses to external, periodic excitation via a sequence of one-parameter response curves. By experimentally mapping out the MEMS cantilever’s stable and unstable periodic orbits, we expose the dynamic landscape by rendering a multi-valued response surface across a range of forcing frequencies and amplitudes. An atypical, non-Duffing-like bifurcation structure is revealed.Subject terms: Mathematics and computing, Mechanical engineering, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

There exists a plethora of well-established tools for deducing the governing relationships that determine a structure’s motion. Such methods typically involve measuring the structure’s response subject to a series of input signals, generally assuming that the motion follows a linear relationship. Despite the prevalence of nonlinearities in engineering applications, no such ‘standard tools’ exist for evaluating behaviours beyond the linear range. Over the last ~ 30 years, researchers have developed tools that address the prevalence and importance of nonlinear effects in engineering structures1,2.

Owing to their technological importance and strongly nonlinear nature, micro- and nano-electromechanical systems (M/NEMS) are the subject of a rapidly growing body of nonlinear dynamics research. Their small scale provides attractive benefits in terms of compactness, energy consumption, sensitivity, and mass-manufacturability3. As these micro- and nano-systems operate at large amplitudes and feature strong nonlinear coupling between modes and external influences, the dynamics of M/NEMS generally sits firmly in the nonlinear regime. While nonlinear effects may be detrimental to the operation of the device, some nonlinear qualities exhibit desirable characteristics that can be exploited for practical use4,5.

Actively operated M/NEMS, in particular, have gained attention as a means to shift the paradigm towards embracing nonlinearities for enhanced performance in engineering applications6. Unlike their passive counterparts, active M/NEMS utilise nonlinear feedback and coupling mechanisms that enable a wider range of complexities. The idea is to leverage knowledge about bifurcations and multi-stability to make use of nonlinear concepts — parametric excitation, frequency mixing, mode-locking, and coupled resonances. Benefits of this approach are to allow precise control and tunability7,8, resulting in increased sensitivities in applications such as mass and gas detection9–11, bandpass filtering5, frequency stabilisation12,13 and ensuring synchronisation14.

In this endeavour, but also when one wishes to avoid unwanted nonlinear effects, there are great benefits in applying appropriate techniques to accurately capture the governing dynamics. When a mathematical model is available or can be obtained, for example, by finite element modelling15, then simulations and established analytical tools, such as perturbation methods16 or numerical continuation17 can be applied to predict responses in a selected parameter range. However, model-free experimentation of a given system, such as a M/NEMS device, remains limited to forward-time observations and frequency analysis, especially when subject to stochastic/noise inputs. Only stable behaviour can be detected in this way, so regions of different types of instabilities are left uncharted; see, e.g., Younis et al.18,19. Unstable solutions can be important features in the topology of responses — knowledge thereof can assist with identification of basins of attraction for multi-stable systems, allowing discovery of important dynamical behaviour that is otherwise inaccessible by other methods20.

This issue is addressed by the technique of experimental continuation20,21. The term broadly refers to a collection of methodologies for tracing out a nonlinear response by directly investigating a physical experiment subject to changes in a system parameter. Experimental continuation embraces the essence of numerical continuation by tracing steady-state and periodic solutions, irrespective of their stability, to construct a curve of solutions that describes an aspect of the system’s experimental dynamical landscape. Such (one-parameter) bifurcation diagrams are able to identify, for example, ranges of multi-stability and associated hysteresis loops. In particular, experimental continuation has gained substantial attention in the mechanical and structural engineering community for constructing the nonlinear frequency response curves (NFRCs) and nonlinear amplitude response curves (NARCs) of nonlinear oscillators. So far, it has been demonstrated almost exclusively for macro-scale systems; see the review article by Raze et al.20 as an entry point into the literature. Not only do macro-scale demonstrations of experimental continuation operate at slow timescales, but their nonlinearities also arise through passive mechanisms. Consequently, they result in well-studied Duffing-like nonlinear response curves and bifurcation diagrams that illustrate the system’s bistability22–26.

The motivation behind our work is to showcase cutting-edge tools for investigating nonlinear dynamical systems by extending experimental continuation to broader engineering applications, such that they may eventually become integrated into standard engineering practice. The class of M/NEMS serves as an excellent platform for this research by enabling two primary objectives: first, to apply experimental continuation to fast-timescale dynamics; and second, to display nonlinear behaviours not yet probed by experimental continuation.

Specifically, we employ experimental continuation to characterise the dynamic response of a micro-system in the form of an actively operated MEMS cantilever. This MEMS cantilever was chosen for its industrial relevance in scanning probe and atomic force microscopy (SPM/AFM) and its unique multi-physics characteristics, combining mechanical, electrical and thermal elements. Specifically, we drive the MEMS cantilever periodically and perform many experimental (one-parameter) continuation runs in the forcing amplitude for different values of its forcing frequency. This allows us to build up the dynamic landscape of the MEMS cantilever: the surface of periodic solutions that exist for any combination of the driving amplitude and frequency. We also identify fold curves on this surface where the stability of periodic solutions changes. The main challenge in this work is the fast timescales in MEMS. The natural frequency of the MEMS cantilever is just under 100 kHz, which is several orders of magnitude larger than typical operating frequencies of 100 Hz to 102 Hz one encounters in previously considered macro-scale systems. These lower timescales are sufficiently slow for modern digital data acquisition and real-time controllers (RTC) required for experimental continuation, but these cannot be applied reliably at the timescales of MEMS cantilevers. We overcome this difficulty with very fast acquisition hardware to implement the required local real-time control of the MEMS cantilever.

A system with similarly fast timescales was recently considered by Böttcher et al.27, who applied a different flavour of experimental continuation to find the surface of periodic solutions of a driven probe for AFM with a natural frequency of around 56 kHz. Namely, these colleagues employed an approach known as a phase-locked loop (PLL), where feedback and control is achieved by tracking the oscillator’s phase response. PLLs have also been applied to investigate the frequency response of beams28,29 and to track backbone curves30. Phase feedback is a common feature in industrial lock-in amplifiers, originally for electrical signal synchronisation, and is useful for identifying Duffing-like frequency responses in other M/NEMS devices; it has been employed in MEMS applications as early as the 1990’s, for example, by Greywall et al.31, as well as more recently by Sobreviella et al.32, Mestrom et al.33, Zega et al.34, and Tsai and Li35. However, there are drawbacks to phase feedback: (i) detectable changes in phase deteriorate further from resonance, resulting in low phase responsivity, (ii) each measurement is localised to a single mode, and (iii) an ideal phase parameterisation is required. A further notable difference to the work by Böttcher et al.27 — and, in fact, with practically all previous studies of driven oscillators with experimental continuation — lies in the nature of the solution surface of the MEMS cantilever we study. Owing to the coupling to a thermal element, the nonlinearity of our MEMS cantilever is different from previous studies using experimental continuation.

Results

We consider a single MEMS cantilever with embedded self-sensing and self-actuation capabilities. The device was originally invented for non-conventional AFM and future nano-lithography for pre-deformed structures36,37. Recent numerical works by Lam et al.38, Hayashi et al.39, and experimental work by Lenk et al.40 have investigated its nonlinear properties. The composite structure comprises a base silicone layer, a silicon dioxide middle layer, and an aluminium top layer.

We now introduce the operation of the active MEMS cantilever in the context of the present investigation. We motivate the need for employing a control-based testing methodology by characterising its open-loop response. Data acquisition and control is implemented on a Zynq-7000 Field-Programmable Gate Array (FPGA) onboard a RedPitaya STEMLAB 125-14. More details on the MEMS cantilever and the associated instrumentation can be found in the Methods section.

Open-loop characterisation

We fix the parameters associated with the active coupling law (2) by tuning the system past the onset of a Hopf bifurcation38 so that the cantilever acts as a self-oscillating unit. To investigate the open-loop nonlinear interactions between the self-sustained oscillations and external excitation, we employ a traditional sine-sweep by linearly varying the excitation frequency, f. In this way, we induce and observe entrainment. This phenomenon is a special case of synchronisation and refers to a system following the frequency of the external driving source42; it is also known as mode-locking or injection locking43–45.

The time-domain response in Fig. 2a can be understood by considering two key features of the frequency response in Fig. 2b: First, a diagonal line at fres=f, where the response frequency matches that of the excitation, with the largest magnitude of the spectra indicating resonance at f ≈ 99 kHz. Notice that the peak in Fig. 2a aligns with the largest magnitude of the spectra in Fig. 2b. Second, a horizontal frequency component at fres≈99kHz, which exists for f ∈ (95, 98.7) ∪ (99.3, 101) kHz and corresponds to the natural frequency of the MEMS cantilever. Its appearance (or disappearance) indicates the transition, via a two Neimark-Sacker (or torus) bifurcations, between frequency locking with fres=f and a two-frequency quasiperiodic response. Thus, the frequency range 98.7 kHz < f < 99.3 kHz represents the entrainment region for the chosen forcing parameters. Moreover, enlargements of the timeseries response confirm quasiperiodic motion outside the entrainment region (Fig. 2c) and a periodic response within it (Fig. 2d).

We conclude from Fig. 2 that there exists an unknown curve of periodic solutions that lose stability at two points of torus bifurcation. Our goal is to capture accurately the nonlinear response of the MEMS cantilever by finding this type of curve directly in the experiment, even when the periodic response is unstable. As we show next, this can be achieved with experimental continuation.

Constructing the MEMS response surface

We employ the simplified control-based continuation algorithm, as termed in the review article by Raze et al.20, and we will refer to it simply as CBC from now on. This type of experimental continuation was originally demonstrated by Barton and Sieber22 and has been applied to investigate harmonically forced mechanical oscillators where the response is typically determined from varying forcing strengths and frequencies22,24,25,46. We implement CBC for the MEMS cantilever to track a curve of periodic orbits, regardless of their natural (open-loop) stability. Details of the control strategy required and implementation details can be found in Control law for CBC implementation and ref. 26.

We use CBC to compute a NARC for a given forcing frequency f over a suitable range of the forcing amplitude u^. By performing this experimental continuation for fixed f over the relevant f-range from 97.8 kHz to 98.4 kHz in steps of Δf = 50 Hz, we obtain 121 equally spaced constant-frequency NARCs. These curves are then post-processed by smoothing and remeshing so that they have the fixed number N = 2000 of data points up to the selected maximal excitation strength of maxu^=0.2V; see Experimental procedure and post-processing for specific details. The resulting 121 curves are then used to construct and render the response surface as a triangulated mesh over the plane of excitation frequency f and amplitude u^, where the response is represented by the response amplitude q^f of the fundamental Fourier harmonic. This approach to obtaining the response surface in a three-dimensional space has been introduced and tested by the authors in ref. 26 for a macro-scale nonlinear beam experiment of the Duffing type.

Characterisation of the MEMS response surface

The surface in Fig. 3c,d rendered from 121 NARCs is very finely resolved and smooth, and it brings out the key features of the non-Duffing-like response of the MEMS cantilever. The surface is a function over the (f,u^)-plane when the forcing frequency f is sufficiently far from the resonance frequency 98.12 kHz. Nearer this frequency, the MEMS response surface protrudes back to lower values of u^, owing to the S-shaped nature of the NARCs; this is illustrated in Fig. 3c,d with the two views that show the lower-frequency and the higher-frequency side of the surface, respectively.

This experimental continuation methodology disregards the natural stability of the open-loop system due to the employed closed-loop stabilisation and, hence, does not detect bifurcation points directly. However, saddle-node bifurcations of periodic solutions are readily identified geometrically in post-processing as fold points with respect to q^f on a given NARC. Given the high number of NARCs that are continued experimentally, this allows us to render the locus of saddle-node bifurcations as two curves that meet at two cusp points. They are shown in Fig. 4a in projection onto the (f,u^)-plane and in Fig. 4b on the response surface itself. The region with co-existing periodic solutions in the (f,u^)-plane is bounded by two curves of folds that meet at a cusp point on the left (lower value of f) and a cusp point on the right (higher value of f). On the response surface, on the other hand, the entire locus of folds is a single closed and smooth curve; compare Fig. 4a and b. Moreover, each of the two fold curves in the (f,u^)-plane has a local minimum, and these two points correspond to critical points of the response surface: the local minimum on the upper fold curve corresponds to a saddle point, and the local minimum on the lower fold curve, very close to u^=0, to a local extremum — the ‘resonance tip’ of the protrusion of the response surface at f = 98.12 kHz. Extracting NFRCs from the response surface

Figures 3c, d and 4b represent the dynamical landscape of our actively operated MEMS cantilever. It is decidedly different from that of other systems that have been investigated with experimental continuation to-date, including those in Barton et al.22, Renson et al.23, Bureau et al.24, Böttcher et al.27 and Hayashi et al.26, which all feature nonlinearities stemming primarily from passive elements that result in a Duffing-like behaviour with bistable characteristics. In particular, a Duffing-like response surface can be found from NFRCs by performing experimental continuation runs in the frequency f for a sufficient number of fixed values of the amplitude u^ of the forcing. This is not possible for the non-Duffing case of the active MEMS cantilever studied here, because the protruding part of its response surface, for u^ in the range 0.0026 to 0.0594 V between the extremum and the saddle point, would be missed; namely, only the lower part of the NFRC would be found for this range of forcing amplitudes.

However, the complete NFRC of the active MEMS cantilever for any fixed value of u^ within its range can be found to good accuracy by intersecting the 121 smoothed NARCs on the response surface with planes of constant u^. This is illustrated in Fig. 5 with four representative examples of NFRCs. Here, Fig. 5a shows how the respective NFRC is rendered from the respective intersections points in (f,q^)-plane, while Fig. 5b shows the resulting smooth curves on the response surface in (f,u^,q^f)-space. The example for u^=0.16V represents a typical frequency response near the primary resonance for large stimulus strength, when the NFRC is a single curve that is a function over f. As u^ is decreased below the value of the right cusp point in Fig. 4a, the response shows a slight hardening behaviour with a pair of fold points above the resonance frequency; the curve for u^=0.10V in Fig. 5 is a representative example. When further decreasing u^, the left cusp point is also passed and a second pair of fold bifurcations is observed to the left of the resonance peak; see the NFRC for u^=0.06V. Subsequently, the saddle point in Fig. 4b is reached, where the two lower left and lower right fold bifurcation points collide; for lower values of u^, the plane for constant u^ intersects the response surface of the active MEMS cantilever in two disjoint curves that make up the NFRC: a lower one that is a function over f and an upper closed isola with two fold points; see the case for u^=0.04V in Fig. 5, which is representative for the situation all the way down to the extremum where u^ is practically zero. One can readily observe the transition of qualitatively distinct NFRCs in Fig. 4a over varying drive conditions. In particular, an isola exists for frequency and forcing conditions in the part of the shaded region (bounded by the fold curve) that lies below the saddle point at u^=0.0594V; see also Table 1.

Discussion and conclusions

Many of the prior works on CBC considered mechanical systems with passive elements which result in geometric nonlinearities that feature a Duffing-like, cubic restoring force as the dominant feature22–24,26,27. While such geometrical nonlinearities also exist in a variety of MEMS systems3, our active MEMS cantilever applies a self-excitation law between the measured response and its thermal actuator, thereby creating a thermo-mechanical system with self-oscillating behaviour. Unlike the single-degree-of-freedom Duffing oscillator, which demonstrates bistable characteristics, this device displays self-sustained oscillations, multi-valued responses, and frequency entrainment in the presence of external forcing. Its response features different kinds of bifurcations, including a fold locus with a pair of cusp points, a transition through a saddle point and Neimark-Sacker bifurcations, where periodic solutions become unstable and quasiperiodic motion arises.

In spite of the very fast timescales of the MEMS cantilever, of around 100 kHz, we obtained its underlying response surface of periodic solutions by performing an unusually large number of experimental continuation runs. This was achieved with a simplified CBC algorithm that focuses on the harmonic component and employs a position-only local control law. This allowed us to render the response surface and accurately identify its geometric features — fold curves, cusp points and critical points. We remark that the Neimark-Sacker (or torus) bifurcation points that bound the actual locking range of the active MEMS cantilever (explored in Open-loop characterisation) cannot be identified during experimental runs of by post-processing. However, we stress that these curves bounding the stability regions of the open-loop system are well beyond the fold curve we identified on the surface; compare Fig. 2 with the Fig. 4a. Hence, the majority of the periodic solutions on the surface depicted in Figs. 3–5 are naturally unstable. This is in contrast to bistable Duffing-like structures, whose region of instability is bounded exactly by the locus of fold points.

The type of response surface we found for the active MEMS cantilever has not been found directly in an experiment before. However, numerical investigations of mathematical models have shown that it also arises in other mechanical systems; see, for example, Li et al.47, Ponsioen et al.48 and Habib et al.49. In fact, these theoretical works, as well as our own modelling of the MEMS cantilever39, confirm that the observed nature of the response can be attributed to its nonlinear damping.

Our work contributes to the field of experimental continuation by successfully characterising the response surface of an actively operated MEMS cantilever by means of CBC. As such, we showcase the feasibility of this technique for a wider range of devices with complicated, large and generally unknown nonlinearities, which operate on a timescale of hundreds of kHz — substantially faster that those of macro-scale mechanical experiments. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, the MEMS cantilever considered here has the fastest timescales, with a self-sustained oscillation frequency of just under 100 kHz, of all mechanical systems to which CBC has been applied. A very close second is the recent AFM experiment by Böttcher et al.27 with operating frequencies around 56 kHz and a Duffing-like response. Both systems operate about four orders of magnitudes faster than the macro-scale systems.

While the implementation by Böttcher et al.27 employs a PLL for stabilisation, our work shows that the nonlinear response of a MEMS system can also be stabilised and tracked with a local control law. Specifically, we used a proportional-only controller and only the harmonic part of the response to reduce the computational effort required for successful real-time stabilising control to such an extent that the strict real-time requirements can be satisfied with the available RTC, in our case, the RedPitaya’s FPGA. More generally, the control law implemented on the RTC needs to act 5–10 times faster than the maximum operating frequency of the device under study. Hence, in fast-timescale applications, implementing more involved control strategies, such as the prediction of the successive point, online harmonisation of the forcing signal with state estimation50,51, or adaptive methods capable of identifying optimal control52,53, is secondary to achieving the required stabilisation of the response.

The work presented here demonstrates that experimental continuation is a viable tool for investigating a wide range of dynamic systems, including micro-scale systems with very fast timescales. We focused on finding the surface of periodic solutions, which are effectively all unstable past a locus of Neimark-Sacker bifurcations. Identifying this locus directly in an experiment would be a natural next step, but this is a considerable challenge irrespective of the timescales involved. In contrast to fold points, the Neimark-Sacker bifurcation is not related to geometric properties of the solutions surface. Rather, it is defined exclusively by a stability change of a periodic solution. The (in)stability of periodic solutions on the response surface could be quantified experimentally by checking for divergence when the control is switched off, as done by Renson et. al.46. However, this is not a practical method of finding and then tracking the actual stability change. How to achieve this in a practical way is a challenge of considerable interest to the experimental continuation community20. A related and even harder challenge is the identification and (experimental) continuation of quasiperiodic motion on invariant tori that arises from a Neimark-Sacker bifurcation, which is a difficult task, even in a mathematical model54.

In summary, we showcased experimental continuation as a feasible standard tool for the investigation of dynamic systems with fast timescales. In this context, we envision that greater accessibility to RTC hardware and its associated advancements in terms of computational power will allow experimental continuation to be applied to a large range of systems — including those that operate at even faster timescales (such as NEMS), show enhanced susceptibility to nonlinear influences, and have stricter stabilisation requirements.

Methods

Figure 6 illustrates the physical realisation of our MEMS experiment in block-diagram form. It is partitioned into three key components: (a) the active MEMS cantilever; (b) an analogue signal conditioning circuit; and (c) a RedPitaya 125-14 signal acquisition and RTC board.

Schematic diagram of the experimental setup.

a MEMS cantilever; b signal conditioning board; c RedPitaya digital controller.

The signal conditioning board interfaces the MEMS cantilever with the RTC. Firstly, the measured deflections are amplified by an instrumentation amplifier and an active high-pass filter, which removes low-frequency components related to initial deflections and expansions caused by slow-timescale temperature drifts; the filtering component was revealed to be vital to the cantilever’s active operation39. Secondly, the thermal actuation command voltage is low-pass filtered to remove power-supply coupling present in the real-time control unit’s output; it is then converted to the thermal supply current by a transconductance amplifier. Finally, a constant current source powers the strain sensor.

Data acquisition and control algorithms are implemented on the Zynq-7000 FPGA on the RedPitaya STEMLab 125-14 signal acquisition board. The RTC features two input and two output channels with a signal range of ±1 V and a 14-bit ADC/DAC resolution. Signals are generated and sampled at 125 MSa/s. Data are digitally decimated and low-pass filtered before being saved at 2.5 MSa/s. We utilise a single input channel corresponding to the filtered measurements of the cantilever deflections. The cantilever signal is used for control and self-actuation; additionally, it is recorded for further offline analysis. The two output channels are used for external actuation u(t) and thermal actuation UH, as described in The actively operated MEMS cantilever.

We proceed in this section by describing the CBC algorithm, as introduced by Barton and Sieber22 and implemented previously by the authors26, with a focus on the requirement to achieve real-time, noninvasive control.

Control law for CBC implementation

A crucial ingredient is a closed-loop feedback law, uCL, that acts to locally stabilise the system dynamics that one wishes to track, which are periodic solutions in the case of the active MEMS cantilever. The chosen reference trajectory r(t) acts as the driving mechanism that replaces the open-loop harmonic forcing, but it also parameterises the system such that each input maps to a unique response; the choice of r(t) is elaborated below. In contrast to the quite common use of a proportional-plus-derivative controller, this choice avoids the need for velocity information, which would require an additional sensor or be estimated through finite differencing or filtering21. Hence, position-only control minimises computational effort, which is a key requirement in light of the fast timescales of the MEMS cantilever. The proportional-only controller must overcome the instability caused by the positive feedback (2), which is achieved for large values of Kp. We empirically tuned the experimental system by iteratively selecting a large, positive gain Kp that provides a wide measurement range for the ADC/DAC over the experimental operating range.

Experimental procedure and post-processing

We perform sweeps in r^ for fixed values of the external forcing frequency f to obtain 121 NARCs over the relevant f-range from 97.8 to 98.4 kHz. As we explained in Control law for CBC implementation, we only take into account and find the fundamental Fourier harmonic during these continuation runs. Hence, we do not employ an iterative process (such as fixed-point iterations22) to update the non-fundamental Fourier coefficients in an online fashion; see also Abeloos et al.51 for this simplified approach. This is justified by a series of tests that we performed a priori, which show that the MEMS cantilever experiment has a sufficiently fast transient decay for the chosen parameter sweeping rate to ensure tracking of the controlled periodic solution. A fixed experimental period of T = 1 s was chosen to maximise accuracy and repeatability while minimising experimentation times and datafile sizes. We confirmed our choice of sweep rate by empirical means, verifying that the recorded responses are consistent with those obtained with longer experimental times. An added advantage of the sweeping methodology is that it reduces the experimentation time, compared to performing iterations for every recorded point. Hence, it is feasible to perform a large number of experimental continuation runs with minimal parameter drift (due to slower external influences, such as thermal effects). We further minimise these slow-timescale influences by performing several sweeps that ensure the experimental setup reaches an equilibrium state. The overall experimental procedure for performing CBC in this way is summarised in Algorithm 1.

Algorithm 1

The experimental procedure for control-based continuation

1: r^←0

2: r^max←1

3: f ← finit

4: while f≤fmax do ⊳ Compute constant-frequency NARCs.

5: while t ≤ T, in real-time do ⊳ Apply real-time control.

6: Measure q(t)

7: r^←r^max⋅t/T

8: r←r^cosΩt

9: Compute u←Kpr−q

10: end while

11: r^←0

12: Save measurements q(t) and u(t) for t∈0,T

13: f ← f + Δf

14: end while

Once the experimental sweeps are concluded, we analysed the MEMS cantilever response and control signal by applying a short-time Fourier transform. In this way, we extract the Fourier amplitudes of the signal at the forcing frequency and its integer multiples. The noisy-amplitude data for each NARC is interpolated with smoothing splines to obtain the curves used to generate the response surface; see Fig. 3. Specifically, we use Method B from ref. 26 with smoothing parameter p = 0.999 for the majority of the curves, including those with turning points. However, the NARCs near the extremum where u^ approaches zero for frequencies close to resonance have very high curvature (much higher than one encounters in Duffing-like systems22–26); this leads to inappropriate fitting with large residuals, where effectively this part of the NARC is ‘smoothed off’.
