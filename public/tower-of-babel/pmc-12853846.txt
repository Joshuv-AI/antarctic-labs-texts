Night-time road traffic fatalities disproportionately affect male pedestrians in geographic hotspots
Authors: Simpiwe Sobuwa, Benjamin De Waal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRoad traffic accidents (RTAs) are a major public health concern, disproportionately affecting low- and middle-income countries. Despite having fewer vehicles per capita, Africa experiences the highest global road traffic fatality rates. This study examines demographic, temporal, and geographic patterns of fatal RTAs in Cape Town to inform targeted safety interventions.MethodsA retrospective analysis of Western Cape Government Health and Wellness Emergency Medical Services (WCGHW EMS) data (January 2021–May 2024) was conducted. Data included patient demographics, location, time, and accident type. Geospatial mapping using QGIS identified hotspots, and statistical analyses assessed relative risk and mortality trends.ResultsOf 784 RTA cases, 167 resulted in fatalities. Males accounted for 70.7% of deaths, and pedestrians comprised 65.3% of fatalities. Findings underscore the urgent need for improved pedestrian infrastructure, enhanced street lighting, and optimised emergency medical response strategies. Targeted safety interventions in high-risk areas can significantly reduce RTA fatalities. Further research should explore behavioural and environmental factors over longer timeframes to inform policy and practice.Graphical abstract Keywords: Emergency medical services, Geospatial analysis, Nighttime fatalities, Pedestrian safety, Road traffic accidents

Introduction

Road traffic accidents (RTAs) represent a significant global concern, with projections suggesting they will rank as the seventh leading cause of death by 2030 [1]. In 2018 alone, RTAs caused 50 million injuries and 1.35 million fatalities worldwide, disproportionately affecting individuals aged 5 to 29 years [2]. The rapid modernisation of transport networks has contributed to the increase in RTAs, often resulting in serious injuries and fatalities [3]. Low- and middle-income countries possess less than 60% of the global motor vehicle population, yet bear over 90% of the world’s road traffic deaths [1, 3]. Notably, Africa, despite having the fewest vehicles per capita and the smallest road network, has the highest rate of road transport fatalities globally [4]. One ongoing challenge across the continent is the inadequate documentation of RTAs, with police records often remaining the primary information source [4].

South Africa (SA) exemplifies these challenges. RTAs are a leading cause of death and disability, particularly among children and young adults. Since 2000, road deaths among children under five have ranked ninth nationally, with even higher rates among older children [5]. Among children aged 5 to 14 years, RTAs emerged as the primary cause of death, with a rate that was double the global average [5]. The sharp rise in motorised vehicle ownership, doubling over the past two decades, has contributed to a 25% increase in national road deaths from 1990 to 2011 [6]. The economic burden is substantial, with RTAs costing SA approximately USD 11.2 billion, or 2.7% its GDP [7].

Multiple factors contribute to the country’s high RTA rates. Behavioural issues, including speeding, impaired driving, fatigue, and poor compliance with traffic laws, are leading causes [8–12]. In addition, infrastructure deficiencies such as limited signage, deteriorated roads and a lack of pedestrian crossings or adequate lighting further elevate risk [13]. Many vehicles are unroadworthy, and both drivers and pedestrians frequently ignore road regulations [14, 15]. For instance, jaywalking accounted for nearly a quarter of fatal accidents in 2023, making pedestrian safety a critical area for intervention [7]. Mobile phone use has also emerged as a significant contributor to RTAs [10, 16].

Beyond their immediate health consequences, RTAs have far-reaching impacts on public health and the economy [17]. There are substantial long-term impacts on productivity, household income, business operations and national economic growth [10]. Families losing a breadwinner to an RTA face significant financial and emotional hardship [2].

Emergency Medical Services (EMS) play a vital role in mitigating the severity of injuries and fatalities associated with RTAs [2]. By providing rapid response and medical care, EMS can reduce mortality and morbidity. In the Western Cape province, and particularly Cape Town, the Western Cape Government Health & Wellness Emergency Medical Services (WCGHW EMS) serves as the primary prehospital system. Its effective deployment is essential given that the region consistently reports the highest number of pedestrian injuries and deaths in South Africa.

Cape Town stands out for its persistently high rates of RTA-related fatalities, particularly among pedestrians. Between 2021 and 2023, Cape Town reported more pedestrian accidents than any other South African city [17]. Furthermore, the Western Cape province recorded the third-highest prehospital deaths from RTAs nationally between 2007 and 2019 [17]. These statistics underscore the urgency of addressing road safety challenges within this region.

To address these challenges, a better understanding of the spatial and demographic patterns of fatal RTA incidents is required. Identifying high-risk areas, or “hotspots,” and understanding the factors, such as age, sex, time of day, and infrastructure conditions, associated with fatal incidents are critical for designing effective interventions. Such spatial analysis can inform targeted enhancements to road safety infrastructure, strategic EMS deployment, and public education campaigns.

Recent international research increasingly leverages geospatial information systems (GIS), kernel density estimation, and spatial analysis to identify road traffic accident hotspots and evaluate the influence of environmental and infrastructural risk factors [2–4, 18, 19]. Studies conducted in urban areas across Africa, Asia, and beyond frequently use retrospective analyses of EMS or police data, combined with mapping techniques, to reveal patterns in crash severity, pedestrian vulnerability, and the impact of factors such as street lighting and road quality. By adopting these recognised methodologies, this study ensures both comparability with global evidence and the application of proven analytic approaches to address Cape Town’s unique road safety challenges.

Accordingly, this study uses EMS data to analyse the geographic, demographic, and temporal patterns of fatal RTAs in Cape Town. By highlighting key risk factors and high-burden areas, this research aims to support targeted interventions to reduce fatalities and inform ongoing policy and practice.

Methods

Research design

This study employed a retrospective descriptive analysis of data collected from RTA victims in which the Western Cape Emergency Medical Service was involved in responding to incidents in Cape Town.

Study setting

The study was conducted in the City of Cape Town, which had an estimated population of 4.7 million inhabitants in 2022 [20]. Cape Town covers an area of approximately 2 455 km² and has a coastline of 294 km [20]. Cape Town’s population density rose from 1 529.3/km² in 2011 to 1 951.7/km² in 2022, representing a 27.6% increase [20]. The decline of the rail service in Cape Town has led to a steep increase in road usage. This has resulted in serious gridlock during peak periods, with a shift of passengers to the road network.

The WCGHW EMS has approximately 253 ambulances and 1633 operational personnel. They do between 400 000 and 620 000 cases a year, with assaults being the most common cause of trauma emergencies, accounting for 50% of the EMS caseload [21, 22]. Ambulances operate 24 h a day throughout the year, with day and night shifts from 07h00–19h00 and 19h00–07h00, respectively.

Data management

The study included all patients involved in RTAs between 01 January 2021 and 31 May 2024. The WCGHW EMS uses a Computer-Aided Dispatch (CAD) system to send prehospital teams to emergencies during regular operations. The case would be sent to each emergency resource via an electronic Patient Care Report (ePCR) system. This allows the prehospital teams to document the pertinent information on each patient managed. After completion, this data is synced with the WCGHW EMS database. For medico-legal, quality assurance, and health planning purposes, only the WCGHW EMS Information Management Department has access to this extensive database, which is constantly updated and monitored. However, each practitioner has access to their own completed patient care documentation. The CAD system collects all non-clinical information related to the physical resources that EMS assigns to each call it manages. The prehospital assessment and diagnosis, medical interventions given, comorbidities, patient acuity, patient demographics, type of emergencies requiring intervention, and receiving medical facility are all collected by the ePCRs from information entered by the attending practitioner. Practitioners are still required to complete the ePCR for medico-legal purposes, even if the patient is dead on arrival.

Sampling and inclusion/exclusion criteria

All ePCRs for patients involved in RTAs in the Cape Town Metropole between 01 January 2021 and 31 May 2024 were included in this study. Cases where patients were deceased on arrival are highlighted as code blue in the ePCRs following emergency response. Any patients not involved in RTAs and not managed by the WCGHW EMS within the specified period were not included in this study.

The EMS data variables for this study included call reference number, shift, time of the accident, type of vehicle involved, incident suburb, incident suburb longitude and latitude, incident longitude and latitude, patient’s sex, age, and triage colour.

Validity and reliability

Before data analysis, the data was evaluated for completeness. Although no forecasting analysis was planned as part of this study, the possible impact of trends and/or seasonality on data was also considered. It was plausible that time-related trends in RTA frequency could impact the internal validity of the analysis and might introduce a selection bias, given that the data collection period only spanned 3 years and 5 months. The time series data for the frequency of RTA and RTA-related mortality (deaths and non-deaths) was analysed using the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test (ADS) and the Kwiatkowski-Phillips-Schmidt-Shin (KPSS) test as well as time series plots. The results indicated non-stationarity in most time series, and a small but consistent increase in RTA deaths appeared over the study period. Thus, potential bias from trends and seasonality was considered in the interpretation.

Data analysis

The data was analysed to establish the patterns and trends of fatal incidents in Cape Town (i.e., accident location, car or pedestrian, and accident time). Descriptive analysis was performed using descriptive statistics. Post hoc analysis was performed to analyse relationships observed in the data during the descriptive analysis. Relative risk measures (calculated using 2 × 2 tables) and χ² statistics were used to assess relationships between exposures and outcomes.

RTA and RTA-related mortality geospatial frequency distributions over time were mapped using QGIS 3.34.9. Heatmaps for geospatial frequency distributions were created using the QGIS heatmap function, utilising a Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) algorithm to create a density raster of the GPS locations of individual RTA cases. Higher frequencies and closer relative geospatial proximities are reflected as ‘hotspots’ on the heatmap raster output. This technique assisted the researchers in assessing the risk-spread areas of accidents. In addition, the heatmap raster outputs were used to identify specific “geohotspots” for RTAs which involved fatalities. These geo-hotspots were categorised at a suburb level if a suburb was contained within or overlapped by a hotspot area in the mapping output. To evaluate the relationship of RTA location to road infrastructure, a GIS road map layer of Cape Town was imported into QGIS, and both imported digital maps and GIS data frames were aligned to the WGS1980 UCS coordinate system. All non-patient GIS data was obtained from the City of Cape Town Open Data Portal.

Ethical considerations

The Western Cape Department of Health provided permission to extract the necessary data after the Durban University of Technology granted full research ethics approval (IREC 228/24). There was no direct patient interaction throughout this evaluation of secondary data. There was no direct patient interaction throughout this evaluation of secondary data. No patient-identifying information was obtained or reported; data analysis was performed on anonymised records only.

Results

There were 784 patients who were involved in road traffic accidents (RTAs) between 2021 and May 2024, with 167 fatalities, or patients who died on the scene or in the ambulance before reaching the hospital (Table 1). The mean age of the accident victims was 34 years. Male patients had a higher mortality rate (70.7%) compared to females (22.8%). Pedestrian accidents had the highest mortality rate (65.3%) compared to all other accident types. Most fatalities occurred during nighttime hours (51.5%). The data also indicates that 38.9% of all fatalities occurred in geo hotspot locations (Fig. 3).

Male patients had a higher relative risk of death compared to females (Fig. 1) (RR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.063–2.05, χ² = 5.70, p = 0.017). Cases in hotspot areas had a two-fold higher relative risk of death than those in the rest of Cape Town (RR = 2.09, 95% CI: 1.61 to 2.72, χ² = 28.79, p < 0.001). Nighttime accidents were also associated with an increase in the risk of death compared to daytime accidents (RR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.22–2.08, χ² = 11.58, p < 0.001). Pedestrian incidents exhibit a fourfold greater risk of mortality as compared to all other accident types (RR = 4.16, 95% CI: 3.14–5.51, χ² = 115.43, p < 0.001). Light motor vehicle accidents (MVAs) were associated with a decreased risk of mortality as compared to other RTA types (RR 0.36, 95% CI: 0.26 to 0.51, χ² = 41.59, p < 0.001).

When considering possible hypotheses for the increased risk of mortality associated with specific geographical hotspot areas, it was considered that ambulance shift changes in certain time periods may play a role. As noted in Fig. 2, many RTA incidents and fatalities appear to occur within these time periods (06:00 to 08:00 and 18:00 to 20:00). During these periods, ambulances return to their bases, which results in fluctuating levels of resource availability. This factor was considered because it highlights a potential temporal gap in emergency coverage that coincides with peak traffic periods, potentially resulting in delayed response times.

The analysis of mortality and survival by time of day revealed peak incident volumes occur during early evening hours (19:00), with approximately 58 total cases, of which most (74%) survived. While overall cases are lower during early morning hours (2:00–5:00), these periods show proportionally higher mortality rates.

When considering all RTA cases, the data shows that most RTAs were in the Cape Town central business district, with a wide and relatively consistent distribution mainly following the major roads (highways, arterial roads) throughout the city (Fig. 3). However, most deaths occurred in the Geo hotspot areas, mostly located in the eastern part of the City, which includes the suburbs of Gugulethu, Nyanga, Mitchells Plain and Khayelitsha (Fig. 4). It was also noteworthy that many of these fatal cases occurred on smaller suburb-level roads or major roads within suburbs as opposed to highways.

Discussion

We examined demographic, temporal, and geographic patterns of fatal RTAs in Cape Town. This study revealed significant gender disparities in RTA mortality rates in Cape Town. Males accounted for 72.5% of fatalities, suggesting a higher vulnerability to fatal RTAs. This finding aligns with global trends, as reported by the World Health Organization (WHO, 2018), which consistently shows higher road traffic fatality rates among males. Similarly, a study investigating RTAs in Nigeria found that 73% of those affected were males [2]. National data further reinforces this pattern, as males constituted 75% of road traffic fatalities in 2022 and 76.5% in 2023. Additionally, long-term South African road transport accident death data (2007 to 2019) indicate that the total number of male deaths from RTAs was three times the total number of female deaths during this period [17]. The reasons for this disparity may include higher risk-taking behaviour, more frequent travel, and potentially different vehicle use patterns between genders [23, 24].

Both fatal and non-fatal accident victims were predominantly young adults. This finding is particularly concerning as it highlights the significant impact of RTAs on the most economically productive age group, potentially leading to substantial socioeconomic consequences for families and communities. This supports earlier findings that accident victims are mostly young individuals [17, 24–26].

Pedestrians emerged as the most vulnerable group, a pattern consistent with findings across the African continent [27]. This vulnerability shows distinct temporal and spatial patterns in Cape Town. Despite lower overall incident volumes at night, pedestrian mortality rates increase significantly during these hours, possibly due to poor lighting conditions in accident-prone areas [19, 28, 29]. While this broader pattern aligns with regional trends, this study provides unique insights into Cape Town’s specific challenges, accounting for local infrastructure, traffic patterns, and socioeconomic conditions. These findings emphasise the urgent need for targeted pedestrian safety interventions, particularly focusing on improving lighting and infrastructure in high-risk locations.

Implications for policy and practice

The high number of pedestrian fatalities necessitates urgent improvements to pedestrian safety, including better infrastructure such as well-lit crosswalks, pedestrian bridges, traffic calming in high-risk areas, and public awareness campaigns. The increased nighttime fatalities require targeted interventions such as enhanced street lighting, stricter nighttime enforcement, and improved public transport options to decrease impaired driving. The identified accident hotspots should be prioritised for infrastructure upgrades, increased law enforcement, and community-based safety programs. Finally, emergency medical services should strategically allocate resources to these hotspots, especially at night, to minimise response times and improve survival rates.

In addition to general awareness campaigns, user-focused interventions that address the psychological and behavioural determinants of risky road use are crucial. Evidence from international literature highlights that programs built on behavioural science, such as tailored education, motivational social marketing, and community engagement, are more effective at shifting road user attitudes, intentions, and habits than generic information alone [30, 31]. Incorporating theory-driven strategies that target risk perception, compliance, and decision-making, and that are culturally adapted to high-risk populations, can deliver sustained reductions in road traffic injuries and fatalities [32]. Such user-side interventions should be integrated into any comprehensive road safety strategy for Cape Town.

Limitations

While this study provides valuable insights, it has several limitations. The study did not have access to traffic volume or exposure data, which could provide context for the observed patterns. The study did not investigate the role of factors such as alcohol use, speeding, or road conditions, nor did it objectively measure lighting conditions associated with RTAs. The study accounted only for prehospital deaths and not those which occurred in-hospital. It is, therefore, likely that fatalities are under-represented in this study. Although the WCGHW EMS is the largest EMS service provider, there are several private EMS services for which this study did not obtain data. The lack of this data could act as a possible confounder in that private EMS services operated in more affluent areas of the city (which tended to be non-hotspot areas). It is plausible that some fatal RTA incidents may have been attended to by these services and not included in this study. As discussed earlier, we also noted some seasonality and trend stationarity in the data. This may imply that a longer timeframe should be used in future research to reduce the influence of seasonality and other trends in future analysis. Future research should address these limitations by conducting longitudinal studies to identify trends over time and investigating the role of behavioural and environmental factors in RTAs.

Conclusion

This study provides an overview of fatal road traffic accidents in Cape Town, highlighting the particular vulnerability of pedestrians, the increased risk during nighttime hours, and the presence of geographical hotspots for fatal RTA incidents. These findings have important implications for policy and practice, particularly regarding targeted interventions for pedestrian safety and strategic allocation of emergency medical resources. To address these challenges, practical measures such as improving pedestrian infrastructure, implementing targeted traffic calming strategies in high-risk zones, and optimising EMS deployment during high-risk periods are recommended to enhance safety and serviceability management. Additionally, improved inter-agency communication can strengthen serviceability management and reduce response delays. Integrating real-time data analytics and predictive mapping could further support the dynamic deployment of EMS resources and proactive accident prevention. By addressing these critical areas, there is potential to significantly reduce the burden of road traffic fatalities in Cape Town.

Future research should include longitudinal studies capturing a broader timeframe to identify long-term trends and seasonal variations in RTA fatalities. Further studies are also needed to investigate behavioural risk factors, traffic volume, and environmental conditions such as road quality that may influence RTA outcomes.
