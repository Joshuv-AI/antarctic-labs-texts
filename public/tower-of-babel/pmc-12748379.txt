Expression and prognostic diagnostic significance of RGS gene family in breast cancer
Authors: Huanhuan Wang, Ming Feng, Hong Huang, Xiaoqing Zhang, Chang Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Regulator of G Protein Signaling (RGS) gene family, known as critical negative regulators of G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) signaling pathways, has emerged as a potential therapeutic target in various malignancies. This study aims to comprehensively evaluate the expression profiles of RGS genes in breast cancer, exploring their diagnostic, prognostic, and chemotherapeutic sensitivity-related roles.MethodsPan-cancer RNA-seq data, immune phenotype data, stemness indices, and breast cancer data from the TCGA and GTEx databases (via UCSC Xena) were integrated to analyze the expression patterns of RGS genes across different cancers. Associations with immune microenvironment factors (e.g., stromal and immune scores), tumor stemness (mRNAsi/mDNAsi), and chemotherapy drug sensitivity (cyclophosphamide, neratinib, clobutin, etc.) were assessed. The relationship between RGS gene expression and overall survival (OS) as well as progression-free survival (PFS) in breast cancer patients was analyzed using the KM-Plotter database, leading to the identification of potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers.ResultsPan-cancer analyses revealed significant positive correlations between the expression of RGS1, RGS13, RGS18, and RGS19 and both stromal and immune scores (P < 0.05). High expression of RGS18 and RGS19 was associated with increased predicted sensitivity to cyclophosphamide and neratinib. In breast cancer, RGS17, RGS16, and RGS1 demonstrated diagnostic potential for immune subtypes (AUC > 0.65), while RGS10 and RGS16 correlated with tumor stage (P < 0.01). A seven-gene RGS-based signature effectively stratified patients by survival risk, with the high-risk group exhibiting significantly poorer OS and PFS (HR = 1.82, 95% CI 1.34–2.47).ConclusionThe RGS gene family plays a crucial role in breast cancer progression through modulation of the immune microenvironment and chemotherapy resistance. Their expression profiles hold promise as novel biomarkers for personalized prognostic stratification and targeted therapy, particularly for guiding chemotherapy drug selection.

Introduction

G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) are the most abundant class of membrane-bound receptors in the human body, playing a crucial role in regulating a wide range of physiological processes, including cell proliferation, immune responses, and inflammation. These receptors mediate cellular signaling pathways initiated by the binding of various ligands such as neurotransmitters, hormones, and chemokines [1, 2]. However, aberrant activation of GPCR signaling has been implicated in tumor progression, metastasis, and immune evasion [3]. Despite significant advancements in understanding GPCR signaling, the regulatory mechanisms governing GPCRs in the tumor microenvironment (TME) of breast cancer remain incompletely understood.

The Regulator of G Protein Signaling (RGS) gene family acts as key negative modulators of GPCR signaling, accelerating the GTPase activity of Gα subunits and thus rapidly terminating downstream signal transduction [4]. Recent studies have uncovered that RGS family members, notably RGS1 and RGS3, exert profound influence on both cancer cell-intrinsic behavior and the tumor immune microenvironment in breast cancer. For example, RGS1 impedes tumor-infiltrating T cell migration by inhibiting calcium influx and downstream ERK/AKT signaling in response to chemokines; its overexpression is associated with impaired T cell trafficking and poor prognosis in breast cancer patients [5, 6]. Mechanistically, STAT1 activation upregulates RGS1, which in turn blocks CXCR3-mediated chemokine signaling, suppressing effective anti-tumor immunity. Targeting RGS1 has recently been shown to enhance tumor-specific T cell migration and infiltration, offering new avenues for immunotherapy enhancement [6]. Similarly, RGS3 is increasingly recognized as a critical regulator of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cell proliferation, migration, and metastasis, via modulation of PI3K/Akt and MAPK signaling pathways [7]. High RGS3 expression correlates with increased tumor aggressiveness, while emerging single-cell transcriptomic analyses suggest that RGS3 may contribute to shaping the immunosuppressive TME, for example by promoting CCL4-mediated M2 macrophage polarization and immune evasion.

However, systematic studies integrating the genomic, transcriptomic, and immunological features of the RGS family in breast cancer are lacking. The interplay between RGS gene dysregulation, immune infiltration, and chemoresistance remains unclear. In this study, we performed the first comprehensive multi-omics analysis of the RGS gene family in breast cancer, integrating RNA-seq, immune microenvironment scoring, tumor stemness, and drug sensitivity data from TCGA, GTEx, and independent datasets. We constructed and validated a prognostic model based on RGS gene expression, providing novel biomarkers for prognosis and personalized therapy in breast cancer.

Materials and methods

Analysis on RGS gene mutations, copy number variations, and genomic characteristics

Mutation data for the RGS gene family were obtained from the TCGA pan-cancer dataset (via UCSC Xena), and visualized using the maftools R package (v2.10.05). Mutations with a sample ratio ≥ 1% were considered significant. Copy number variations (CNVs) were analyzed using the GISTIC 2.0 algorithm, defining amplification as GISTIC score > 0.3 and deletion as < − 0.3. The chromosomal distribution of RGS genes was visualized with the circlize package (v0.4.15). Pairwise Spearman correlations for RGS gene expression were calculated using the psych package (v2.2.9), with significance set at P < 0.05. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to evaluate the discriminatory power of RGS gene expression profiles for differentiating tumor and normal samples.

Analysis on the correlation between RGS genes and the immune microenvironment

The ESTIMATE algorithm (v1.0.13) was applied to calculate ImmuneScore and StromalScore for each breast cancer sample. Based on median score values, samples were stratified into high- and low-score groups. The relationship between RGS gene expression and immune/stromal scores was assessed using Spearman correlation analysis (psych package), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Analysis on the correlation between RGS genes and tumor stemness

Stemness indices, including mRNA expression-based stemness index (mRNAsi) and DNA methylation-based Stemness Index (mDNAsi), were obtained from published datasets [8]. Their correlation with RGS gene expression was analyzed using Spearman correlation (psych package), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Analysis on the association between RGS gene, immune infiltration, and chemotherapy sensitivity

To quantify immune cell composition, the CIBERSORT algorithm was applied to gene expression profiles from 33 tumor types, utilizing the LM22 gene signature (Version 22) and 1000 permutations for deconvolution. The association between RGS gene expression and the relative abundance of 22 immune cell types was evaluated by Spearman correlation (P < 0.05).

Chemotherapy sensitivity was predicted by estimating the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for cisplatin, paclitaxel, methotrexate, and gefitinib, using the pRRophetic R package (v0.5). Drug names and database usage were standardized throughout all analyses. The correlation between RGS gene expression and chemotherapy sensitivity was analyzed by Spearman correlation, with P < 0.05 considered significant.

Analysis on the differential expression of RGS gene

The Kruskal-Wallis test (P < 0.05) was used to compare RGS gene expression among different breast cancer subtypes (PAM50), stages, and molecular subtypes. The limma package (v3.50.3) identified differentially expressed RGS genes between tumor and normal tissues, with thresholds set at |log2 fold change (FC)| > log2(1.5) and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Visualization was performed using heatmaps (pheatmap package) and boxplots (ggplot2 package).

Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on either the median risk score or the optimal cutoff determined by the surv_cutpoint function (survminer package v0.4.9). The model’s prognostic performance was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis (log-rank test) and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to determine area under the curve (AUC). The robustness of the model was validated in two independent GEO cohorts (GSE20685 and GSE96058).

Independent prognostic performance evaluation and nomogram construction

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses (survival package v3.3-1) were performed to evaluate the independent prognostic value of the risk score, incorporating age, TNM stage, and other clinical parameters. A nomogram was constructed based on the results of multivariate analysis using the rms package (v6.3-0), and its calibration and predictive accuracy were assessed by calibration curves and ROC analysis. The protein expression patterns of the model genes in breast tissue were verified using the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database.

GSEA and immune infiltration differential analysis between high and low risk score groups

Differentially expressed genes between high- and low-risk groups were identified by limma (|log2FC| >0.5, FDR < 0.05). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.2.2) with the KEGG pathway database (v7.5.1). GSEA significance thresholds were set as FDR < 0.05 and |normalized enrichment score (NES)| >1.5. Immune cell abundance differences between groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (P < 0.05).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in the R environment (version 4.1.2). Statistical significance was defined as: ns (P ≥ 0.05), * (P < 0.05), ** (P < 0.01), *** (P < 0.001), **** (P < 0.0001).

Differential expression of RGS gene

The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to analyze the differential expression of RGS genes across different clinical characteristics. It was found that, except for RGS16, the remaining RGS genes exhibited significant differential expression across different PAM50 samples. However, in samples from different stages, only six RGS genes, including RGS1, RGS3, and RGS4, showed differential expression. Similar to the PAM50 analysis, in samples from different subtypes, only RGS8 did not show a significant difference, while the remaining RGS genes exhibited significant differential expression across the different subtype samples (Fig. 6A–C). The limma package in R was used to identify seven significantly differentially expressed RGS genes between tumor and normal tissues, with thresholds set at |log2FC| > log2(1.5) and adjusted P-value < 0.05. Among these, one gene was downregulated in tumor tissues, while six were upregulated (Fig. 6D, E).

Independent prognostic value of the signature and development of a nomogram based on prognostic signature

Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed on clinical characteristics, including age, sex, stage, PT, PM, and PN. The results indicated that age, stage, PT, PM, PN, and the risk score were significantly associated with OS prognosis in breast cancer. Therefore, these six characteristics were included in the multivariate Cox regression analysis. The risk score model remained significantly associated with prognosis, demonstrating that the risk score can serve as an independent prognostic factor (Fig. 8A). Based on these results, a nomogram was constructed using the six features (age, stage, PT, PM, PN, and risk score), and the nomogram score was recalculated. The ROC curve indicated that the newly constructed nomogram score performed slightly better than the risk score model. Additionally, the robustness of the risk score model was validated across different subgroups. It was observed that in the subgroups of age > 65, age ≤ 65, Stage III–IV, and Stage I–II, the prognosis of the high-risk score group was significantly worse than that of the low-risk score group (Fig. 8B–H). To investigate the protein expression profiles of the genes used in model construction, we retrieved immunohistochemical data from the HPA database. Among the model genes, only four had available protein expression data in breast tissue (Fig. 8I).

GSEA and immune infiltration differential analysis between high and low risk score groups

To further characterize the differences between risk score groups, the limma package in R was used to calculate the log2FC for the high- and low-risk groups, GSEA analysis was performed, and 11 significantly enriched pathways were identified after screening. It was observed that the genes highly expressed in the high-risk group were significantly enriched in immune-related pathways, such as cytokine interactions, hematopoietic cell lineage, and primary immunodeficiency (Fig. 9A). Based on the abundance of 22 immune cells in the tumor samples, as calculated in the previous section, the Wilcoxon test was used to evaluate the differences in immune cell abundance between the high- and low-risk groups. It was found that immune cells, such as naïve B cells, plasma cells, and CD8 T cells, exhibited significant differences between the high- and low-risk groups (Fig. 9B).

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive multi-omics analysis of the RGS gene family in breast cancer, integrating genomic, transcriptomic, and immunological data. We constructed and validated a prognostic risk scoring model based on seven RGS family genes, which showed robust predictive power in both the training and independent validation cohorts. Our findings elucidate the diverse biological roles of RGS genes in breast cancer and provide novel insights into their potential as prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Dual role of RGS gene in tumor regulation

Our results revealed significant heterogeneity in RGS family gene expression and function within breast cancer. RGS1, RGS3, and RGS20 were markedly upregulated in tumor tissue, whereas RGS2 and RGS9 were significantly downregulated. Functionally, multiple RGS members modulate tumor progression through GPCR-related signaling pathways, including ERK/AKT, NF-κB, and PI3K/Akt, thereby influencing cell proliferation, migration, and immune cell dynamics [11–13]. RGS1, RGS3, and RGS20 contribute to tumor progression by promoting M2 macrophage polarization, suppressing T-cell migration, or activating pro-survival signaling, ultimately fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment [14, 15]. Among them, RGS3 showed the highest prognostic weight in our model and was significantly associated with poor outcomes. Conversely, RGS2 acts as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting Gαq signaling and regulating the STAT3 and PI3K/AKT pathways, with its downregulation linked to enhanced metastasis and worse prognosis [13, 16]. RGS17 exhibits context-dependent roles, promoting tumorigenesis in breast cancer via cAMP–PKA–CREB signaling, while enhancing chemosensitivity in ovarian cancer through PI3K/AKT inhibition [17]. Notably, ARHGEF1, though traditionally classified as a Rho guanine nucleotide exchange factor, is also included in our model due to its significant upregulation and role in actin cytoskeleton remodeling and tumor invasion, particularly in triple-negative breast cancer [18].

Immunoregulatory significance and clinical translational potential of risk model

This model was significantly associated with the characteristics of the immune microenvironment: patients in the high-risk group exhibited decreased CD8+ T cell infiltration (P < 0.001), and T cell receptor signaling pathway was notably suppressed [5]. This is consistent with recent research findings on the mechanism of T cell migration impairment mediated by RGS1—where STAT1 signaling activation upregulates RGS1, leading to the obstruction of chemokine receptor signals like CXCR35. Notably, patients in the low-risk group (with high CD8+ T cell infiltration) showed a significantly improved response rate to PD-L1 inhibitor treatment (OR = 2.3, P = 0.03). In addition, RGS1 was also positively correlated with tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) infiltration (r = 0.41, P < 0.001) [14]. These results imply that RGS gene expression signatures may serve as predictors of immune response, with potential utility in selecting patients likely to benefit from immune checkpoint blockade therapy.

Collaborative regulation of the RGS3 gene and chemotherapy sensitivity

Pan-cancer drug sensitivity analysis indicated that patients with high RGS18 expression exhibited increased sensitivity to cyclophosphamide (IC50 reduced by 30%, P = 0.002), whereas patients with high RGS19 expression were more sensitive to cladribine (AUC = 0.68) [19]. In breast cancer, high expression of RGS3 was positively correlated with cisplatin resistance (r = 0.32, P < 0.01), potentially mediated through the regulation of ABCB1 efflux pump activity [14]. Based on this model, high-risk patients (with high RGS1 expression) showed increased sensitivity to paclitaxel (r = − 0.28, P < 0.05) [19]. These findings suggest that integrating RGS gene signatures with pharmacogenomic profiling could inform individualized chemotherapy strategies.

Limitations and future directions

Despite the strengths of our multi-omics approach and robust validation, several limitations remain. First, our model was primarily developed using retrospective TCGA data, and its predictive utility requires confirmation in prospective clinical cohorts, such as the NCT04350671 trial. The applicability of the model in metastatic breast cancer also warrants further investigation with additional datasets. Second, while we validated protein expression for several model genes using the Human Protein Atlas, comprehensive spatial profiling using multiplex immunofluorescence or single-cell proteomics is needed to clarify their functional localization in the tumor microenvironment. Third, the mechanistic links between RGS gene expression and chemotherapy sensitivity remain speculative in the absence of functional validation. Future studies should include in vitro experiments (e.g., RGS3 knockdown or knockout in breast cancer cell lines with drug treatment) and in vivo models to verify these associations. Finally, the precise molecular mechanisms by which RGS genes regulate immune cell recruitment, polarization, and antigen presentation remain to be clarified, potentially through CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing and organoid co-culture systems.

Conclusion

In summary, our comprehensive analysis demonstrates that the RGS gene family plays multifaceted roles in breast cancer progression, immune regulation, and chemoresistance. The prognostic model based on RGS gene expression provides a valuable tool for individualized risk stratification and therapy selection. Further mechanistic and translational studies are warranted to fully realize the clinical potential of targeting the RGS network in breast cancer.
