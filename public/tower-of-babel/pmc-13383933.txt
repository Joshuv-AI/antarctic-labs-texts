Surgical Treatment of Injury to the Posteromedial Meniscocapsular‐Extra‐articular Attachment Associated With Rupture of the Anterior Cruciate Ligament
Authors: Jonas Eck, Tayfun Yilmaz, Hagen Schmal, Kaywan Izadpanah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConcomitant injuries are often present in the knee affected by anterior cruciate ligament injuries. Certain injuries are routinely identified, whereas others may be inadvertently neglected. Such injuries include lesions to the posteromedial meniscocapsular‐extra‐articular attachments. This can result in instability not only of the medial meniscus but also of the knee joint as a whole. Consequently, it is crucial to identify and manage these concomitant injuries during the assessment and treatment of anterior cruciate ligament injuries to enhance patient outcomes and ensure knee stability. Our technique offers a straightforward approach for the identification of such injuries through the application of the “Medial Pseudo‐Hiatus Sign” and shows an effective treatment protocol. This technique is applicable universally by surgeons.

Injuries of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) are often associated with concomitant injuries of the injured knee. 1 Especially, meniscal tears and injuries of the medial collateral ligament (MCL) present themselves with high prevalences. 1 , 2 , 3 The MCL consist of two components the deep and the superficial part. Trauma mechanisms often include valgus stress and femorotibial rotation of the knee joint. Biomechanical studies support the hypothesis that isolated ACL injuries are rare, due in part to the nature of the trauma mechanism. 4 It is therefore essential to specifically investigate for concomitant injuries in cases of ACL rupture.

Some injury patterns are difficult to identify. The medial meniscus (MM) and the medial joint‐capsule are attached to extra‐articular structures like the MCL and the posterior oblique ligament. 5 , 6 Moreover, an anatomical connection exists between the joint capsule and the semimembranosus muscle. 6 , 7 The posteromedial stabilizers as well as the connections between the joint capsule and these extra‐articular structures (meniscocapsular‐extra‐articular attachments, MEA) can be concomitantly injured in patients suffering from ACL injury and can lead to relevant meniscal instabilities. 8 Unfortunately, this kind of injury is often missed in clinical practice as they can be missed on magnetic resonance imaging and in clinical examination. Failure to identify and address these concomitant injuries can lead to suboptimal treatment outcomes and increased long‐term complications for patients. This underscores the importance of comprehensive assessments in ACL injury cases to ensure all associated injuries are diagnosed and managed effectively.

Injury to the MEA must be evaluated independently from so called ramp lesions, owing to the specific anatomical structures involved and their precise localization. Ramp lesions conventionally describe a rupture occurring between the meniscal base and the articular capsule, whereas the previously mentioned injury pertains to the capsular attachment to the extra‐articular anatomical structures. 9

The purpose of this article is to raise awareness, to show an easy way to recognize such a combined injury, and to show the surgical treatment through a representative case.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Preoperative Planning

The preoperative planning should involve a thorough assessment of all associated injuries to ensure optimal surgical outcomes and to address the complexities of concomitant injuries effectively. A precise patient history is essential to elucidate the mechanism of injury and previous injuries or operations. Potential knee joint instabilities should be specifically assessed during clinical examination. In addition to the Lachman test and the classic anterior drawer test in neutral rotation, the anterior drawer should also be performed in external and internal rotation. An increase in anterior translation in external rotation (and stabilization in internal rotation) is indicative of a concomitant injury to the (postero‐)medial secondary stabilizers. It can be useful to repeat the clinical examination under anesthesia. In this case, Lachman test and anterior drawer were positive. The anterior translation was increasing in external rotation and decreasing in internal rotation (Video 1, 0:57). A magnetic resonance imaging should be available for radiologic assessment. Surgical Procedure

At the beginning of the procedure, a diagnostic arthroscopy is performed via a standardized anterolateral viewing portal. During meniscal evaluation, meticulous inspection is essential. Equally important is mechanical probing using a hook probe introduced through the anteromedial portal. In the present case, this revealed an instability of the MM, despite an intact appearance on visual inspection (Figure 2). Notably, the pars intermedia and the transition zone to the posterior horn were affected. The meniscus shows this considerable instability despite remaining entirely affixed to the joint capsule. This technique shows how the tip of the probe fully sinks into a cleft between the meniscus and the capsule, thereby clearly identifying a lesion of the MEA. We are calling this a positive “Medial Pseudo‐Hiatus Sign.”

Therapeutic intervention consists of refixation using an all‐inside suture device (in this case FAST‐FIX FLEX, Smith & Nephew). To optimize access, a portal switch is performed: the arthroscope is placed anteromedially, while the suture device is introduced via the anterolateral portal, allowing precise access to the lesion. A single horizontal suture is sufficient to restore meniscal stability (Figure 3). Adequate fixation is confirmed through probing (Video 1, 3:12).

Subsequently, ACL reconstruction is carried out using an autologous hamstring graft. Hybrid fixation is performed using a femoral button (ACL TightRope II Implant, Arthrex) and tibial interference screw (FastThread BioComposite Interference Screw, Arthrex) with back‐up fixation via suture anchor (SwiveLock SP Anchor, Arthrex).

An additional examination under anesthesia is performed to confirm the achieved stability of the knee joint (Video 1, 3:35).

DISCUSSION

The main message of this article is to underscore the significance of evaluating concomitant injuries in individuals presenting with an ACL injury. Understanding these associated injuries can lead to improved treatment strategies and better patient outcomes following ACL reconstruction. Frequent associated injuries are meniscal tears and lesions of the MCL. 1 , 10

These injury patterns frequently manifest in preoperative magnetic resonance imaging evaluations and, consequently, can be effectively managed by the majority of experienced surgeons. Injuries to the MEA frequently present challenges in preoperative identification and consequently are prone to oversight. The technique we have described for diagnosing such injuries is straightforward and can be executed by any surgeon, showing a high sensitivity in identifying instabilities within the MEA. It reiterates the significance of tactile assessment of all intra‐articular components during each diagnostic arthroscopy procedure. This approach not only enhances the accuracy of diagnosis but also ensures comprehensive management of ACL injuries, ultimately contributing to improved surgical outcomes. The presence of the “Medial Pseudo‐Hiatus Sign” should be easily noticeable in all diagnostic arthroscopies, provided that substantial injury to the MEA has transpired. The recognition of the pathological implications associated with a positive “Medial Pseudo‐Hiatus Sign” should be incorporated into the surgical guidelines governing knee arthroscopy. It should be noted that the “Medial Pseudo‐Hiatus Sign” may be absent in the presence of concomitant meniscal injuries (Table 1). In such cases, MEA injuries should be additionally considered. The test may be repeated once treatment of the meniscal lesion has been completed.

The MM serves a vital function in the stabilization of the knee joint, as it substantially contributes to the reduction of anterior tibial translation. 11 A stable MM can consequently be regarded as a crucial factor in diminishing the incidence of failure rates associated with ACL reconstructions. Particularly, concomitant tears of the meniscus have been correlated with the laxity of grafts in ACL reconstruction. 12 It is advisable to address concomitant meniscal lesions at the time of ACL reconstruction, particularly those resulting in meniscal instability.
