Euclidean interval objects in categories with finite products
Martín H. Escardó

Abstract
Based on the intuitive notion of convexity, we formulate a universal property defining interval objects in a category with finite products. Interval objects are structures corresponding to closed intervals of the real line, but their definition does not assume a pre-existing notion of real number. The universal property characterises such structures up to isomorphism, supports the definition of functions between intervals, and provides a means of verifying identities between functions. In the category of sets, the universal property characterises closed intervals of real numbers [ a , b ] [a,b] with endpoints a < b a<b . In the category of topological spaces, we obtain intervals with the Euclidean topology. We also prove that every elementary topos with natural numbers object contains an interval object; furthermore, we characterise interval objects as intervals [ a , b ] [a,b] of real numbers in the Cauchy completion of the rational numbers within the Dedekind reals.

The characterisation of interval objects in elementary toposes provides a versatile tool for

2
Interval objects

We define an interval object in a category
\mathcal{C}
with finite products
to be an initial two-pointed, iterative and cancellative midpoint
algebra in
\mathcal{C}
. Midpoint algebras in
\mathcal{C}
and the cancellation
axiom are defined in Section
2.2
, generalising the
discussion of the introduction, and the crucial iteration axiom is
defined in Section
2.3
, again motivated by the discussion
of the introduction. The notion of interval object is defined and
studied in Section
2.5
, following the
development of cancellative iterative midpoint objects, called
midpoint-convex bodies, in Section
2.4
. In particular, in
Section
2.5
we define negation and
multiplication on an interval object, and we also prove their expected
properties, using the universal property that defines interval objects,
where for multiplication we need a stronger notion of a

parametrised
interval object, also defined in that section.

2.1
Preliminaries

We briefly review the notation and terminology we use for
categories.

We write
\mathbf{1}
for the terminal object in
\mathcal{C}
and

X\xleftarrow{\pi_{1}}X\times Y\xrightarrow{\pi_{2}}Y
for the binary product
structure. We say that an object
X
is
exponentiable
if the
functor
(-)\times X
has a right adjoint
(-)^{X}
. The object
Y^{X}

can be thought of as a function space internalising the hom-set

\mathcal{C}(X,Y)
. The category
\mathcal{C}
is said to be
cartesian closed

if every object is exponentiable.

Lawvere’s notion of natural numbers object, see e.g.
[
LS86
, Chapter I.9]
, already mentioned in
Section
1
, abstracts the characterising properties of
the set of natural numbers.

2.1 Definition

A
natural numbers object (nno)
in
\mathcal{C}

is an object
N
together with
two maps
0\colon\mathbf{1}\longrightarrow N
and

(-)\!+\!1\colon N\longrightarrow N
such that,
for any object
X
and maps
\mathbf{1}\xrightarrow{a}X\xleftarrow{f}X
,
there exists a unique map
g\colon N\longrightarrow X
satisfying

\displaystyle g(0)

\displaystyle=a,

\displaystyle g(n+1)

\displaystyle=f(g(n)).

We have written the equations above using ordinary algebraic notation.
Indeed, we shall use such notation freely throughout the paper.
In order to understand such notation in the general setting of an
arbitrary category with finite products, the variables must be
interpreted as
generalised elements
, i.e. as maps targeted at
the object in question but sourced at an arbitrary object
Z
. For
example, the equation
g(n+1)=f(g(n))
above, states that,
for all objects
Z
and generalised elements

n\colon Z\longrightarrow N
, it holds that

g\circ((-)\!+\!1)\circ n=f\circ g\circ n
. In this case, as in many others, the condition simplifies to
an unquantified equation between maps, namely,
g\circ((-)\!+\!1)=f\circ g
.

In the presence of finite products alone, the notion
of nno is not sufficient to implement
definition by primitive recursion in
\mathcal{C}
. Indeed, even
addition need not be definable. This is rectified by using
the following parametrised notion of nno instead
[
PR89
]
.

2.2 Definition

A
parametrised nno

is given by an object
N
together with
two maps
0\colon\mathbf{1}\longrightarrow N
and

{(-)\!+\!1}\colon N\longrightarrow N
such that,
for any objects
X,P
and maps
P\xrightarrow{a}X\xleftarrow{f}Z\times P
,
there exists a unique map
g\colon P\times N\longrightarrow X
satisfying

\displaystyle g(p,0)

\displaystyle=a(p),

\displaystyle g(p,n+1)

\displaystyle=f(p,g(p,n)).

Here
P
is to be thought of as an object of parameters.

It is easily shown that any parametrised nno is an nno,
and that if a parametrised nno exists then any nno is
parametrised. It is not in general the
case that any nno is parametrised, though the following
sufficient condition for this is well known.

2.3 Proposition

If
\mathcal{C}
is cartesian closed then any nno is parametrised.

2.2
Midpoint objects

We begin the reformulation of the notions from Section
1

by defining a notion of
midpoint object
based on
the laws of the binary midpoint operation.

2.4 Definition

A
midpoint object
in
\mathcal{C}
is a pair
(A,m)
, where

A
is an object of
\mathcal{C}
and
m\colon A\times A\longrightarrow A

is a map
satisfying the
idempotency, commutativity and transposition equations of
Section
1
.
A midpoint object is said to be
cancellative
if it also satisfies
the cancellation law (
1
) of Section
1
.
If
(A,m)
and
(B,m^{\prime})
are midpoint objects then
a
homomorphism
from
(A,m)
to
(B,m^{\prime})
is a map

h\colon A\longrightarrow B
satisfying
h(m(x,y))=m(h(x),h(y))
.

Observe that the transposition law is equivalent to saying that the
midpoint operation
m:A\times A\longrightarrow A
is itself a homomorphism
with respect to the induced midpoint operation on
A\times A
.

Henceforth, we often use the same notation for a midpoint object and
its underlying object
A
, in which case we
conventionally
use the letter
m
for the associated midpoint operation.
We use
map
to refer to arrows in the underlying category

\mathcal{C}
, and
homomorphism
for homomorphisms of midpoint objects.
We also adopt the convention of referring to midpoint objects in
categories of particular interest by replacing the word “object”
with the name of the objects of the category. Thus a
midpoint set

is simply a midpoint object in the category of sets,
and a homomorphism of midpoint sets is a function preserving
the midpoint operation. Similarly,
a
midpoint (topological) space
is a midpoint object in the
category of topological spaces (thus
m
is required to
be continuous), and a homomorphism between midpoint spaces is
a continuous function preserving
m
.
Similar naming conventions will be applied to all other notions
of algebraic structure we consider.

Examples of midpoint sets abound. Every convex subset of a vector
space over
\mathbb{R}
(or
\mathbb{Q}
) is a midpoint set with

m(x,y)=(x+y)/2
. Similarly, every convex subset of a topological
vector space is a midpoint space. Moreover, one can obtain further
examples by taking arbitrary subsets (subspaces) of midpoint sets
(spaces) that are closed under the operation
m(x,y)
. Thus, for
example, the (dyadic-)rational points in any convex subspace of
Euclidean space form a midpoint space. These examples are all
cancellative. Semilattices provide examples of midpoint sets that are not
cancellative.

As discussed in Section
1
, we shall define an interval
object as a free midpoint-convex body on two generators. To motivate
the need for considering a notion of convexity, let us consider the
analogous free objects in the categories of midpoint sets and
spaces. It is easy to show that the free midpoint set over two
generators
0
and
1
is the
set
D=\{\frac{m}{2^{n}}\!\in[0,1]\mid m,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
of
dyadic rationals in the closed unit interval endowed with the midpoint
structure
m(x,y)=(x+y)/2
. Explicitly, this says that for any
midpoint set
A
and any
a,b\in A
there is a unique midpoint
homomorphism
h:D\to A
with
h(0)=a
and
h(1)=b
.
In the category of topological spaces,
the free midpoint
space over
\{0,1\}
is the set
D
with the

discrete
topology.
Here the discrete topology arises because, when all
operators are finitary,
free topological algebras
over discrete spaces
always have discrete topologies.

By considering the example of topological spaces, one sees that the
notion of midpoint space falls short of characterising the interval

[0,1]
on two counts. First, the points are wrong — the object
D

contains only dyadic rational points. Second, the topology
is wrong — it is the discrete rather than Euclidean topology.
The iteration property solves both problems at once.

2.3
Iterative midpoint objects

One formulation of
iteration was given in Section
1
.
This formulation involves an infinitary operation
M
,
which is not directly interpretable in an arbitrary
category with finite products.
We circumvent this issue by giving here a
concise reformulation of iteration, phrased
in terms of finite products alone.
Moreover, whenever
\mathcal{C}
has enough structure to interpret
infinitary operations, the new
formulation of iteration is equivalent to that
of Section
1
.

A midpoint object
(A,m)
is said to be
iterative
if for every
map
c\colon X\longrightarrow A\times X
there is a
unique map

making the diagram below commute.

Using category-thoretic terminology, the above definition
says that

is iterative if the

(A\times(-))
-algebra

is
final with respect to coalgebra-to-algebra
homomorphisms from abitrary
(A\times(-))
-coalgebras.

To understand the above definition intuitively, one should
consider the map
c\colon X\longrightarrow A\times X
as exhibiting

as an
object of sequences over

. Indeed,
any such map has the form

for some

and

. Think of

as the map returning the head

of a sequence

, and of

as the map returning the tail
a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\dots

The definition above simply says that
A
is iterative if
for every such object of sequences,

,
there is a unique map
u\colon X\longrightarrow A
satisfying
u(x)=m(h(x),t(x))
.
The map

implements
the operation

on sequences

in

.
The equation

corresponds to the unfolding property of

. The uniqueness of

corresponds
to the canonicity property of

To establish a precise correspondence between the definition
of iterativity above and the iteration axioms of Section

we need to assume additional structure on

sufficient for

An

is then given by a map
M\colon A^{N}\longrightarrow A
.

In what follows, we shall use the notational convenience of working
with generalised elements of

as if they were simply
sequences
(x_{i})
of generalised elements of
A
. Indeed,
if
M
is an infinitary operation, then we shall
often write
M(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\dots)
or (more honestly)

for

iteration axioms of Section

sense whenever
\mathcal{C}
has an exponentiable parametrised nno.

2.6 Proposition

Suppose that
\mathcal{C}
has an exponentiable parameterised nno
N
.

1.

A midpoint object
A
is iterative if and only if
there exists an infinitary operation
M:A^{N}\to A
on
A

satisfying the following properties:

(Unfolding)

\displaystyle M_{i}\,x_{i}\,=\,m(x_{0},M_{i}\,x_{i+1}),

(Canonicity)

if
y_{i}=m(x_{i},y_{i+1})
for all
i
, then
y_{0}=M_{i}\,x_{i}
.

Moreover if
A
is iterative then there is a unique
M

satisfying unfolding.

2.

If
A
and
A^{\prime}
are iterative then any midpoint
homomorphism
f:A\longrightarrow A^{\prime}
is also a homomorphism with respect to
the unique associated infinitary operations; i.e. for all sequences
(x_{i})
,

f(M_{i}\,x_{i})=M^{\prime}_{i}\,f(x_{i}).

Proof.

For statement
1
, suppose that
A
is iterative.
We derive
M\colon A^{N}\longrightarrow A
.
Define
h\colon A^{N}\longrightarrow A
by
h((x_{i}))=x_{0}
,
and
t\colon A^{N}\longrightarrow A^{N}
by
t((x_{i}))=(x_{i+1})
.
This determines
(h,t)\colon A^{N}\longrightarrow A\times A^{N}
.
So, by iterativity, there is a unique

M\colon A^{N}\longrightarrow A
such that
M((x_{i}))=m(h((x_{i})),M(t((x_{i}))))
,
i.e. such that
M_{i}\,x_{i}\,=\,m(x_{0},\,M_{i}\,x_{i+1})
.
Thus there is a unique infinitary operation
M
satisfying unfolding.

To show that this unique
M
also satisfies canonicity, let

(x_{i})
and
(y_{i})
be sequences satisfying
(y_{i})=(m(x_{i},y_{i+1}))
. Consider the map
c\colon N\longrightarrow A\times N
defined by
c(n)=(x_{n},n+1)
. Then
n\mapsto y_{n}\colon N\longrightarrow A
and
n\mapsto M_{i}\,x_{i+n}:N\longrightarrow A
, if taken as

,
both make diagram (
2
) commute. Thus these two maps are
equal, i.e.
y_{n}=M_{i}\,x_{i+n}
. Thus, in particular,
y_{0}=M_{i}\,x_{i}

as required.

Conversely, suppose that there exists
M:A^{N}\longrightarrow A
satisfying
unfolding and canonicity. To show iterativity, take any

c=(h,t)\colon X\longrightarrow A\times X
. We must show that there is a unique
map
u\colon X\longrightarrow A
such that
u(x)=m(h(x),u(t(x)))
. Such a
map is defined by

\displaystyle u(x)=M_{i}\,h(t^{i}(x))=M(h(x),h(t(x)),h(t(t(x)),\dots)).

For statement

\displaystyle(h(M_{j}\,x_{i+j}))\>=\>(h(m(x_{i},\,M_{j}\,x_{i+j+1})))\>=\>(m(h(x_{i}),\,h(M_{j}\,x_{i+j+1}))).

Note that the proof above does not

of an iterative binary operation.

2.7 Remark

Proposition
2.6
can be reformulated as follows. Construct a category whose objects are triples
(A,m,M)
, where
(A,m)
is a midpoint object and
M
satisfies the equations in statement (
1
), and whose morphisms are midpoint homomorphisms preserving
M
. Then the evidently faithful forgetful functor into the category of midpoint objects is full, its image on objects consists of exactly the iterative midpoint objects, and the resulting functor onto the category of iterative midpoint objects is an isomorphism of categories.

We end this subsection with the remark that
there are two different ways of making sense of
infinitary operations in even greater generality than above.
The first is to drop the assumption that

N
is exponentiable. At this level of generality, an infinitary operation is
given by a family of functions

M_{X}\colon\mathcal{C}(X\times N,A)\to\mathcal{C}(X,A)

natural in
X
. In the special case that the object
N
is
exponentiable, this notion coincides with the one given above.
It is possible to formulate the iteration axioms using this
more general notion of infinitary operation, and to establish the counterpart of
Proposition
2.6
.

A second way of generalising the notion of infinitary operation is
to drop the requirement that
\mathcal{C}
have an nno, and require instead
that each functor
X\times(-)
have a final coalgebra
\mathrm{Seq}(X)
.
With this assumption, one formulates an infinitary operation on
A
as
a map
M\colon\mathrm{Seq}(A)\longrightarrow A
. Again, in the special case that

\mathcal{C}
has a parametrised
natural numbers object
N
, the new definition coincides with the one
given, as then
N
is exponentiable with
A^{N}
given by
\mathrm{Seq}(A)
.
At this level of generality, one can again formulate the iteration axioms.
However, it appears that the counterpart of Proposition
2.6
does

not
hold, as, in the absence of the object
N
, the unfolding and canonicity axioms
seem strictly stronger than iterativity.

2.4
Midpoint-convex bodies

2.8 Definition

A

, or

a cancellative iterative midpoint object.

By Proposition

Many examples of m-convex bodies will be presented in Section

Here we just give the following non-example, showing that the cancellation property of
m-convex bodies is not implied by the midpoint and iteration axioms.

2.9 Example

Consider the midpoint set

defined by:

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(x,y)\in[0,1]\times[0,1]\mid\mbox{$x=1$ or $y=1$}\}

\displaystyle m((x,y),\,(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(1,\,y\oplus y^{\prime})&\mbox{if $x=x^{\prime}=1$}\\
(x\oplus x^{\prime},\,1)&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

This is iterative, as is
easily verified by defining

and
checking the unfolding and canonicity properties.
The failure of cancellation is shown by

The cancellation property of m-convex bodies will serve many technical
purposes in the sequel. One of them will be established in
Proposition

equivalent to a useful approximation property of the infinitary
operation

. A further consequence of cancellation is that it
allows iterativity to be obtained via an equationally axiomatized
iterated midpoint operation

below).

2.10 Definition

Suppose that
\mathcal{C}
has an exponentiable parametrised nno

.
A

\displaystyle M(x,x,x,\dots)

\displaystyle=x,

(Commutativity)

\displaystyle M(x,y,y,y,\dots)

\displaystyle=M(y,x,x,x,\dots),

(Transposition)

\displaystyle M_{i}\,M_{j}\,x_{ij}

\displaystyle=M_{j}\,M_{i}\,x_{ij},

(Unfolding)

\displaystyle M_{i}\,x_{i}

\displaystyle=M(x_{0},\,M_{i}\,x_{i+1},\,M_{i}\,x_{i+1},M_{i}\,x_{i+1},\,\dots).

2.5

We define the notion of interval object by specifying its universal property.

the midpoint operation is a homomorphism, we conclude that so is the
map
h(x)=x\oplus-x
. Since
h(-1)=h(1)=0
, the universal
property shows that
h
must be the constant function with
value
0
.
∎

The universal property of an interval object is not strong enough
to allow us to define functions of two or more variables such as
multiplication. The situation is exactly analogous to that for
natural numbers objects, discussed in §
2.1
.
As in that case, the solution is to define a stronger

parametrised
notion of interval object.

2.20 Definition

A
parametrised interval object
is an m-convex body
[a,b]

together with two global points
a
and
b
such that for every
object
P
and every m-convex body
A
with maps
x_{a},x_{b}:P\to A
there is a unique map
h:P\times[a,b]\longrightarrow A

such that the equations below hold.

\displaystyle h(p,a)

\displaystyle=x_{a}(p),

\displaystyle h(p,b)

\displaystyle=x_{b}(p),

\displaystyle h(p,x\oplus y)

\displaystyle=m(h(p,x),h(p,y)).

The last equation says that
h
is a homomorphism in its second
argument. As in the non-parametric case, we use the operator
symbol
\oplus
to denote the midpoint operation of a parametrised interval object.
Easily, every parametrised interval object is an interval object.
Hence if both objects exist then they are isomorphic (via
midpoint homomorphisms).
Thus, a category with binary products may or may not have an interval
object, and if it does, the interval object may or may not have the
property of being parametrised.
The result below is analogous to Proposition
2.3
.

2.21 Proposition

If
\mathcal{C}
is cartesian closed then any interval object is parametrised.

The proof, which is routine given Proposition
2.15
, is omitted.

2.22 Proposition
(Multiplication)

If
I=[-1,1]
is a parametrised interval object
then there is a unique map
(x,y)\mapsto x\cdot y:I\times I\longrightarrow I
such that

\displaystyle x\cdot-1=-x,

(14)

\displaystyle x\cdot 1=x,

(15)

\displaystyle x\cdot(y\oplus z)=x\cdot y\oplus x\cdot z.

(16)

\displaystyle x\cdot 0=0,

(17)

\displaystyle x\cdot y=y\cdot x,

(18)

\displaystyle(x\cdot y)\cdot z=x\cdot(y\cdot z).

(19)

Proof.

Existence and uniqueness follow from the universal property by
considering the negation and identity functions as the parametric
points. All equations are proved using the same method. We show
the commutativity of multiplication, which is the most interesting.
By the uniqueness condition of the free property of an interval
object, it suffices to show that the defining equations are
satisfied when the arguments are swapped, i.e. that

\displaystyle-1\cdot y=-y,\qquad 1\cdot y=y,\qquad(x\oplus y)\cdot z=(x\cdot z)\oplus(y\cdot z).

For the first equation, we have that
-1\cdot-1=-(-1)
and
-1\cdot 1=-1
and
-1\cdot(z\oplus z^{\prime})=(-1\cdot z)\oplus(-1\cdot z^{\prime})
, all by the definition of multiplication. Hence the map
z\mapsto-1\cdot z:I\to I
is a homomorphism satisfying the
defining properties of
{-}:I\to I
. Thus indeed
-1\cdot z=-z
.
The second equation is established similarly. To show the third
equation, which says that multiplication is a left-homomorphism, we
show that the maps
(x,y,z)\mapsto(x\oplus y)\cdot z
and
(x,y,z)\mapsto(x\cdot z)\oplus(y\cdot z)
from
I\times I\times I
to

I
are both right-homomorphisms agreeing on
-1
and
1
, and thus
equal by the parametrised initiality of
I
. The map
(x,y,z)\mapsto(x\oplus y)\cdot z
is trivially a right-homomorphism,
because multiplication is. For
(x,y,z)\mapsto(x\cdot z)\oplus(y\cdot z)
, we have that

\displaystyle(x\cdot(z\oplus z^{\prime}))\oplus(y\cdot(z\oplus z^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((x\cdot z)\oplus(x\cdot z^{\prime}))\oplus((y\cdot z)\oplus(y\cdot z^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((x\cdot z)\oplus(y\cdot z))\oplus((x\cdot z^{\prime})\oplus(y\cdot z)),

where the first equation holds because
\cdot
is a right-homomorphism
and the second by transposition. For agreement on
-1
, we have that

\displaystyle(x\cdot-1)\oplus(y\cdot-1)={-x}\oplus{-y}=-(x\oplus y)=(x\oplus y)\cdot-1

where the first and last equations hold by definition of
multiplication and the second by definition of negation.
And agreement on
1
is similar.
∎

Note that the proofs of the above propositions do not make any use of
iterativity.

The above results are just some first steps in using
the universal property of a parametrised interval object
to define and prove equalities between functions on the interval.
For another example,
given any midpoint-convex body
X
and a parametrised interval
object
[0,1]
, we can define a binary convex combination function

c:[0,1]\times X\times X\to X
as the unique map which is a
homomorphism in its first argument and
m(0,x_{0},x_{1})=0
and

m(1,x_{0},x_{1})=x_{1}
. The intuition is that

m(\lambda,x_{0},x_{1})=(1-\lambda)x_{0}+\lambda x_{1}
.
This operation, in the case that
X
is itself the interval, plays
a significant role in defining the
primitive interval functions
in
[
ES01
]
,
and in an extension of Gödel’s system T with a primitive type for the interval of real numbers
[
ES14
]
.

We end this subsection with a brief summary of how
Proposition
2.16
relates to interval objects.
We first observe that any functor
F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
with both left and
right adjoint preserves interval objects. By
Proposition
2.16
(
2
), the functor

\overline{F}:\operatorname{CB}(\mathcal{C})\to\operatorname{CB}(D)
has a right adjoint.
Easily
\overline{F}
preserves
\mathbf{1}
, so, as it also
preserves coproducts,
it preserves interval objects.

Further, an immediate consequence of Proposition
2.16
(
1
)
is that if
\mathcal{C}
is
a full reflective subcategory of
\mathcal{D}
and if
\mathcal{D}
has an interval
object
I
, which is also an object of
\mathcal{C}
, then

I
is also an interval object in
\mathcal{C}
.

3
Consequences of iteration and cancellation

In this section, we establish a couple of technical consequences
of the iteration and cancellation properties.

The first result says that,
in an iterative midpoint object, nested applications
of the infinitary
M
can be reduced to a single
application over a sequence of terms,
each expressed solely in terms
of the binary
m
.
This property is conveniently formulated
using
(n+1)
-ary operations,
m_{n}
, derived
from the midpoint operation by

\displaystyle m_{0}(x)\>

\displaystyle=\>x,

\displaystyle m_{n}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})\>

\displaystyle=\>m(x_{0},m_{n-1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}))

\displaystyle\text{$n\geq 1$}.

Thus
m_{1}
is just
m
itself.

As in Section
2
, we state and prove the
proposition below using the convenient language
of set theory. However,
with
a little care,
both the statement and proof generalize to
apply to iterative midpoint objects in any
category with finite products and parametrised nno.

(Flattening)

In any iterative midpoint object,

\displaystyle M_{i}(M_{j}\,x_{ij})\>=\>M_{l}\>m(\,

\displaystyle m_{(l+1)}(x_{0\,(l+1)},\dots,x_{l\,(l+1)},x_{ll}),\,

(20)

\displaystyle m_{(l+1)}(x_{(l+1)\,0},\dots,x_{(l+1)\,l},x_{ll})\>).

(21)

Proof.

Define

\begin{split}z_{l}\>=\>m(\,&m(\,m_{(l+1)}(x_{0\,(l+1)},\dots,x_{l\,(l+1)},x_{ll}),\,m_{(l+1)}(x_{(l+1)\,0},\dots,x_{(l+1)\,l},x_{ll})),\\
&z_{l+1}\,),\end{split}

First, observe the straightforward:

\displaystyle\phantom{=\>m(\,m(\,}\,m_{l+1}(\,M_{i\geq(l+2)}\,x_{i0},\>\dots\>,\,M_{i\geq(l+2)}x_{il},\,M_{i\geq(l+1)}M_{j\geq(l+1)})\,)\,)

\displaystyle=\>m(\,m(\,m_{l+1}(\,x_{\,0\,(l+1)},\>\dots\>,x_{\,l\,(l+1)},\,x_{ll}\,),\,m_{l+1}(\,x_{\,(l+1)\,0},\>\dots\>,\,x_{\,(l+1)\,l},\,x_{ll})\,),

\displaystyle\phantom{=\>m(\,}\,z_{l+1}).

∎

We do not know if the equation established by the proposition
is more generally true for arbitrary supermidpoint objects.

The second result in this section illuminates the
power of the cancellation axiom for supermidpoint objects in the special case of the category of sets—cancellation is
equivalent to
an important and useful approximation property.

3.2 Proposition
(Approximation)

For any supermidpoint set
X
, the following are equivalent.

1.

X
is cancellative.

2.

X
satisfies the following
“approximation” property.

For all sequences
(x_{i})
,
(y_{i})
,
(z_{i})
,
(w_{i})

of points of
X
, if,
for all
n\geq 0
,

\displaystyle m_{n}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1},z_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{n}(y_{0},\dots,y_{n-1},w_{n})

then
M(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\dots)=M(y_{0},y_{1},y_{2},\dots)
.

Proof.

To prove (
2
) implies (
1
),
assume
M:X^{\omega}\to X
satisfies approximation.
To show that
(X,m)
is cancellative, assume
m(x,z)=m(y,z)
.
We prove by induction that, for all
n\geq 0
,

\displaystyle m_{n}(\overbrace{x,\dots,x}^{n\;\mathit{times}},z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{n}(\overbrace{y,\dots,y}^{n\;\mathit{times}},z)

(24)

The
n=0
and
n=1
cases
are
immediate. For
n\geq 2
, assume the equality holds for
lower
n
.
Write
w=m_{n-2}(x,\dots,x,z)
. By the induction hypothesis,

w=m_{n-2}(y,\dots,y,z)

and
m(x,w)=m_{n-1}(x,\dots,x,z)=m_{n-1}(y,\dots,y,z)=m(y,w)
. So:

\begin{array}[]{lcll}m_{n}(x,\dots,x,z)&=&m(x,m(x,m_{n-2}(x,\dots,x,z)))\\
&=&m(x,m(x,w))\\
&=&m(m(x,x),m(x,w))&\\
&=&m(m(x,x),m(y,w))&\text{induction hypothesis}\\
&=&m(m(x,y),m(x,w))&\\
&=&m(m(x,y),m(y,w))&\text{induction hypothesis}\\
&=&m(m(y,y),m(x,w))&\\
&=&m(m(y,y),m(y,w))&\text{induction hypothesis}\\
&=&m(y,m(y,w))&\\
&=&m(y,m(y,m_{n-2}(y,\dots,y,z)))\\
&=&m_{n}(y,\dots,y,z).\end{array}

Thus (
24
) holds. Hence, by approximation,

M(x,x,x,\dots)=M(y,y,y,\dots)
, i.e.
x=y
.
This proves cancellation.

For the converse implication, assume

(X,m)
is cancellative and hence iterative.
We first observe that the following
“one-sided approximation” property holds:
if, for all
n\geq 0
, it holds that

x=m_{n}(y_{0},\dots,y_{n-1},w_{n})

then
x=M(y_{0},y_{1},y_{2},\dots)
.
Indeed, if the premise is satisfied then, by
repeated applications of cancellation,

w_{0}=m(y_{0},w_{1})
,
w_{1}=m(y_{1},w_{2})
, etc. Hence

x=M(y_{0},y_{1},y_{2},\dots)
, by iterativity.

Next, we prove the following equality. For all
(x_{i})
,
(y_{i})

and
n\geq 0
:

\begin{split}M_{i}(m(x_{i},y_{i}))\>=\>m(\,&m_{n+1}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n},y_{n}),\\
&M(y_{0},\dots,y_{n-1},m(x_{n+1},y_{n+1}),m(x_{n+2},y_{n+2}),\dots)\,).\end{split}

(25)

The proof is by induction on
n
. When
n=0
, we have:

r.h.s.

\displaystyle=\>m(m(x_{0},y_{0}),M(m(x_{1},y_{1}),m(x_{2},y_{2}),\dots))

\displaystyle=\>M(m(x_{0},y_{0}),m(x_{1},y_{1}),m(x_{2},y_{2}),\dots).

For
n>0
, we have:

r.h.s.

\displaystyle\>=m(\,m(x_{0},m_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{n})),\,

\displaystyle\phantom{\>=m(\,}\,m(y_{0},M(y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1},m(x_{n+1},y_{n+1}),m(x_{n+2},y_{n+2}),\dots))\,)

\displaystyle=\>m(\,m(x_{0},y_{0}),\,

\displaystyle\phantom{\>=m(\,}\,m(\,m_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{n}),\,

\displaystyle\phantom{\>=m(\,m(\,}\,M(y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1},m(x_{n+1},y_{n+1}),m(x_{n+2},y_{n+2}),\dots)\,)\,

\displaystyle=\>m(\,m(x_{0},y_{0}),\,M(m(x_{1},y_{1}),m(x_{2},y_{2}),\dots)\,)

ind. hyp.

\displaystyle=\>M(m(x_{0},y_{0}),m(x_{1},y_{1}),m(x_{2},y_{2}),\dots).

Finally, to prove approximation, suppose that,
for all
n\geq 0
,

\displaystyle m_{n}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1},z_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{n}(y_{0},\dots,y_{n-1},w_{n}).

(26)

We show below that

m(M_{i}\,x_{i},\,M_{i}\,z_{i})\>=\>m(M_{i}\,y_{i},\,M_{i}\,z_{i}),

(27)

from which the desired equation,

M_{i}x_{i}=M_{i}y_{i}
, follows
by cancellation.

To verify (
27
), we have:

l.h.s.

\displaystyle=\>M_{i}(m(x_{0},z_{0}),m(x_{1},z_{1}),m(x_{2},z_{2}),\dots)

\displaystyle=\>m(\,m_{n+1}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n},z_{n}),\,

\displaystyle\phantom{=\>m(\,}\,M(z_{0},\dots,z_{n-1},m(x_{n+1},z_{n+1}),m(x_{n+2},z_{n+2}),\dots)\,)

by (
25
)

\displaystyle=\>m(\,m_{n+1}(y_{0},\dots,y_{n},w_{n}),\,

\displaystyle\phantom{=\>m(\,}\,M(z_{0},\dots,z_{n-1},m(x_{n+1},z_{n+1}),m(x_{n+2},z_{n+2}),\dots)\,)

by (
26
)

\displaystyle=\>M(\,m(y_{0},z_{0}),\dots,m(y_{n-1},z_{n-1}),m(y_{n},w_{n}),

\displaystyle\phantom{=\>M(\,}\,m(x_{n+1},z_{n+1}),m(x_{n+2},z_{n+2}),\dots)

by (
25
)

\displaystyle=\>m_{n}(m(y_{0},z_{0}),\dots,m(y_{n-1},z_{n-1}),w^{\prime}_{n}),

where
w^{\prime}_{n}=M(m(y_{n},w_{n}),m(x_{n+1},z_{n+1}),m(x_{n+2},z_{n+2}),\dots)
. But the above is
true for any
n\geq 0
. Hence, by one-sided approximation,
we can continue:

l.h.s.

\displaystyle=\>M_{i}(m(y_{0},z_{0}),m(y_{1},z_{1}),m(y_{2},z_{2}),\dots)

\displaystyle=\>m(M(y_{0},y_{1},y_{2},\dots),M_{i}(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2},\dots)),

as required.
∎

In contrast to all previous results, although
the statement of the above proposition can be formulated in any
category with finite products and parametrised
natural-numbers object, its proof does not
go through at this level of generality. In particular,
the “repeated applications of cancellation”, used to establish
one-sided approximation, cannot be performed in
an arbitrary such category.
Nevertheless, the proof above
can be directly formalized within the internal logic of a
locally-cartesian-closed category,
and hence in any elementary topos. Indeed
Proposition
3.2
will be fundamental to the
development in Section
6
, where the fact that it holds
in any topos is crucial.

4
Iterative midpoints in the category of sets

In this section we study iterative midpoint objects in the category
of sets, establishing a connection with so-called superconvexity,
and proving that the standard interval of real numbers is indeed
the interval object in the category.

4.1
Superconvex sets

Superconvexity, in the sense of Rodé
[
Rod80
]
(see
also König
[
Kön86
]
), is an algebraically formulated notion
of closure under countable convex combinations.

A
superconvex weight function
, over a set
X
,
is a pair
(I,\lambda)
where
I
is an arbitrary
index set, and
\lambda\colon I\to[0,1]
satisfies
\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}=1
(which implies
that its
support

\{i\in I\mid\lambda_{i}\neq 0\}

is countable).
A
convex weight function
is a superconvex weight function
with finite support. A
dyadic convex weight function

is a convex weight function in which each
\lambda_{i}
is a
dyadic rational. We shall simultaneously develop
notions of superconvex set, convex set and dyadic convex set.
To do this conveniently, we use
\operatorname{\mathsf{K}}
to stand for
any one of the terms: superconvex, convex or dyadic convex.

A
\operatorname{\mathsf{K}}
set
is given by a set
X
together
with a
\operatorname{\mathsf{K}}
combination
operation

\sum^{X}
that maps each triple

(I,\lambda,x)
, where
(I,\lambda)
is a

\operatorname{\mathsf{K}}
weight function and
x\colon I\to X
is a function,
to an element
\sum^{X}(I,\lambda,x)\in X
,
written
{\sum^{X}_{i\in I}}\,\lambda_{i}\cdot x_{i}
.
The operation
\sum^{X}
must satisfy:

\displaystyle{\sum_{i\in\mathbf{1}}}^{X}\,1\cdot x=x,

(28)

\displaystyle{\sum_{i\in I}}^{X}\,\lambda_{i}\cdot\left({\sum_{j\in J}}^{X}\,\mu_{ij}\cdot x_{j}\right)={\sum_{j\in J}}^{X}\,\left(\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}\mu_{ij}\right)\cdot x_{j}.

(29)

It is easily seen that,
for each of the three cases for
\operatorname{\mathsf{K}}
, in the right-hand side of
equation (
29
),
\sum^{X}
is indeed applied to
a
\operatorname{\mathsf{K}}
weight function.
In the case of convex combinations, it will
be convenient to write

\lambda_{1}\cdot x_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{k}\cdot x_{k}

for the value
\sum^{X}(\{1,\dots,k\},\lambda,x)
.

Lemma
4.1
below shows that the equations of

\operatorname{\mathsf{K}}
convexity, (
28
) and (
29
), are
complete
in a natural sense. In its formulation, the
indicator

of a
\operatorname{\mathsf{K}}
combination
(I,\lambda,x)
is the
function
\operatorname{w}(I,\lambda,x):X\to[0,1]

defined by

\operatorname{w}(I,\lambda,x)(y)\>=\>\sum_{i\in I}\,\lambda_{i}\delta_{yx_{i}}

where
\delta_{yx_{i}}
is the Kronecker delta.

4.1 Lemma

(Completeness)

For any two
\operatorname{\mathsf{K}}
combinations
(I,\lambda,x)
and

(J,\mu,y)
over a
\operatorname{\mathsf{K}}
set
X
, if

\operatorname{w}(I,\lambda,x)=\operatorname{w}(J,\mu,y)
then

\sum_{i\in I}\,\lambda_{i}\cdot x_{i}=\sum_{j\in J}\,\mu_{j}\cdot y_{j}
.

For a proof see
[
Kön86
, Lemma 1.1]
.
The reader is also referred to
[
Kön86
]
for a discussion
of examples and applications of superconvex sets, although
we shall also review a range of examples
in §
4.5
.

A function
f\colon X\to Y
, between superconvex sets, is
said to be
superaffine
if it preserves superconvex
combinations, i.e.

f\left({\sum_{i\in I}}^{X}\,\lambda_{i}\cdot x_{i}\right)\>=\>{\sum_{i\in I}}^{Y}\,\lambda_{i}\cdot f(x_{i}).

Similarly, a function between (dyadic) convex sets is said to be

(dyadic) affine
if it preserves (dyadic) convex combinations.

A
proper binary
\operatorname{\mathsf{K}}
combination
is one of the
form
\lambda\cdot x+\mu\cdot y
where the weights
\lambda,\mu
are taken from
the open interval
(0,1)
. A
\operatorname{\mathsf{K}}
set is said to be
cancellative
if,
whenever

\lambda\cdot x+\mu\cdot z=\lambda\cdot y+\mu\cdot z
for a proper
binary combination, then

x=y
.

4.2 Lemma

A
\operatorname{\mathsf{K}}
set
X
is cancellative if and only if,
for all
x,y,z\in X
, it holds that

\>\frac{1}{2}\cdot x+\frac{1}{2}\cdot z=\frac{1}{2}\cdot y+\frac{1}{2}\cdot z

implies
x=y

For a proof, see §3 of
[
Kön86
]
.

A superconvex set is said to be
iterative
if,
for every sequence of proper binary combinations
satisfying
x_{i}=\lambda_{i}\cdot y_{i}+\mu_{i}\cdot x_{i+1}
, for which
the weights
\mu_{i}
satisfy the condition
\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\prod_{i<n}\mu_{i}\right)=0
,
the equality

x_{0}\>=\>\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\,\lambda_{i}\!\left(\prod_{j<i}\mu_{j}\right)\cdot y_{i}

(30)

holds. In this definition, the limit condition is necessary for the right-hand side (
30
) to be a superconvex combination.
Any cancellative
superconvex set is automatically iterative, but not vice versa; a fact which follows from
the analogous statement for midpoint objects (Proposition
2.12
(
2
)) together with the lemma below.

4.3 Lemma

A superconvex set is iterative
if and only if, for all sequences
(x_{i})_{i}
and
(y_{i})_{i}
satisfying

x_{i}\>=\>\frac{1}{2}\cdot y_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot x_{i+1},

(31)

it holds
that
x_{0}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\,2^{-(i+1)}\cdot y_{i}
.

Proof.

For the non-trivial right-to-left implication, assume the right-hand side of the if-and-only-if holds and
consider any sequence of proper binary convex combinations
satisfying

x_{i}\>=\>\lambda_{i}\,\cdot\,y_{i}\,+\,\mu_{i}\,\cdot\,x_{i+1},

(32)

for which the condition
\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\prod_{i<n}\mu_{i}\right)=0
applies.
We need to prove equation (
30
).

Let
n_{0}\geq 0
, be the smallest number for which

\prod_{i\leq n_{0}}\mu_{i}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, which exists by the limit assumption.
Using (
32
) for
i=0,\dots,n_{0}
in combination, expand
x_{0}
as a convex combination involving
y_{0},\dots,y_{n_{0}}
and
x_{n_{0}+1}
:

\displaystyle x_{0}\penalty\

\displaystyle=\penalty\ \sum_{j\leq n_{0}}\left(\lambda_{j}\prod_{i<j}\mu_{i}\right)\cdot\,y_{j}+\left(\prod_{i\leq n_{0}}\mu_{i}\right)\cdot x_{n_{0}+1}

\displaystyle=\penalty\ \sum_{j<n_{0}}\left(\lambda_{j}\prod_{i<j}\mu_{i}\right)\cdot\,y_{j}\penalty\ +\penalty\ (\gamma_{1}+\delta_{1})\cdot\,y_{n_{0}}\penalty\ +\penalty\ \left(\prod_{i\leq n_{0}}\mu_{i}\right)\cdot x_{n_{0}+1}\,,

where
\delta_{1}:=\frac{1}{2}-\prod_{i\leq n_{0}}\mu_{i}
and
\gamma_{1}:=\left(\lambda_{j}\prod_{i<n_{0}}\mu_{i}\right)-\delta_{1}
,

\displaystyle=\penalty\ \frac{1}{2}\cdot w_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot z_{1}\,,

(33)

where

\displaystyle w_{0}\penalty\

\displaystyle:=\penalty\ \sum_{j<n_{0}}\left(2\lambda_{j}\prod_{i<j}\mu_{i}\right)\cdot\,y_{j}\penalty\ +\penalty\ 2\gamma_{1}\cdot y_{n_{0}}

and

\displaystyle z_{1}\penalty\

\displaystyle:=\penalty\ 2\delta_{1}\cdot y_{n_{0}}\penalty\ +\penalty\ \left(2\prod_{i\leq n_{0}}\mu_{i}\right)\cdot x_{n_{0}+1}\,.

Continuing in the same vein, for each
i\geq 1
in turn, define
n_{i}
to be the least number such that

\prod_{i\leq n_{1}}\mu_{i}\leq 2^{-i-1}
, and expand
z_{i}
as

z_{i}\penalty\ =\penalty\ \frac{1}{2}\cdot w_{i}\;+\;\frac{1}{2}\cdot z_{i+1},

where each
w_{i}
is given by a convex combination of
y_{n_{i-1}},\dots,y_{n_{i}}
.
With the inclusion of (
33
),
we obtain a system of equations that, by the main assumption, has a unique solution

x_{0}\penalty\ =\penalty\ \sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\,2^{-(i+1)}\cdot w_{i}.

(34)

By the derivation of
each
w_{i}
as a convex combination of
y_{n_{i-1}},\dots,y_{n_{i}}
, it holds that
the weight of
y_{i}
in the superconvex combination on the right-hand side of (
34
) is

\lambda_{i}\prod_{j<i}\mu_{i}
. So by the superconvex instance of completeness (Lemma
4.1
),
equation (
30
) indeed holds.
∎

One consequence of the lemma above is an analogue of
Proposition
2.12
(
2
) for superconvex sets.

4.4 Proposition

Every cancellative superconvex set is iterative.

Proof.

If
X
is a cancellative superconvex set, then

M(x\_0,x_{1},x_{2},\dots)\penalty\ :=\penalty\ \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{-(i+1)}\cdot x_{i}\,,

defines a cancellative supermidpoint structure on
X
. By Proposition
2.12
(
2
)
the corresponding midpoint structure is iterative.
Hence, by Lemma
4.3
, the superconvex structure is also iterative.
∎

The main theorem of this section states that the
iterative midpoint sets
are exactly the iterative superconvex sets, and the same holds for the cancellative case.
Moreover, the associated categories, of midpoint homomorphisms
and superaffine maps respectively,
are isomorphic. In particular the category of m-convex bodies
is isomorphic to the category of cancellative
superconvex sets.

4.5 Theorem

1.

If
X
is an iterative superconvex set then
(X,m)

is an iterative midpoint set, where

m(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot x+\frac{1}{2}\cdot y
.
Moreover, if
X
is cancellative then so is
(X,m)
.

2.

If
(X,m)
is an iterative midpoint
set then there exists
a unique superconvex structure
\sum
on
X
such that

\frac{1}{2}\cdot x+\frac{1}{2}\cdot y=m(x,y)
. This
superconvex structure is iterative.
Moreover, if
(X,m)
is cancellative then so is
X
.

3.

Let
(X,m)
and
(X^{\prime},m^{\prime})
be iterative midpoint
sets with their unique superconvex structures, then a function

f\colon X\to X^{\prime}
is a midpoint homomorphism
if and only if it is superaffine.

The proof of the theorem occupies
§§
4.2
–
4.4
, after which applications
of it are discussed in Section
4.5
.

4.2
Dyadic convex sets and midpoints

We first establish an analogue of Theorem
4.5
,
which expresses a coincidence between dyadic convex sets
and midpoint sets.

4.6 Proposition

1.

If
X
is a dyadic convex set then
(X,m)

is a midpoint set, where

m(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot x+\frac{1}{2}\cdot y
.

2.

If
(X,m)
is a midpoint set then there exists
a unique dyadic convex structure
\sum
on
X
such that

\frac{1}{2}\cdot x+\frac{1}{2}\cdot y=m(x,y)
.

3.

Let
(X,m)
and
(X^{\prime},m^{\prime})
be midpoint sets with
their unique dyadic convex structures, then a function

f\colon X\to X^{\prime}
is a midpoint homomorphism
if and only if it
is dyadic affine.

We give only an outline of the proof of this proposition, since the details are routine to fill in, and similar results appear in the literature
[
Sto49
,
SSY+18
]
. For future reference, notice that the proof outlined below is constructive.

Statement
1
is straightforward. One only needs to verify the
midpoint equations.
For statement
2
, we first
show how to represent any dyadic combination,

q_{1}\cdot x_{1}+\dots+q_{k}\cdot x_{k},

using the midpoint
operation
m
. Let
t\geq 0
be the smallest integer such that every

q_{l}
(where
1\leq l\leq k
) can be expressed as an integer
fraction with denominator
2^{l}
. Then the dyadic convex combination
can be equivalently expressed as

\sum_{d=1}^{2^{t}}\,2^{-t}\,\cdot\,y_{d}

with each
y_{d}\in\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
.
Such a combination is easily represented in terms of the binary midpoint operation
m
,
using an expression that is a full binary tree of height
t
.
Next, one proves, by induction on the height of trees, that
if two full term trees assign the same total weight to
each element
x_{n}
then the terms are
provably equal using the midpoint equations.
This fact suffices to
verify that equations (
28
) and (
29
)
hold between the interpretations of dyadic convex combinations.
Finally, statement
3
follows directly from the mutual
interpretations of dyadic convex combinations and midpoints.

Proposition
4.6
allows one to use the convenient notation
of dyadic convex combinations instead of manipulating cumbersome
midpoint expressions. We shall make frequent use of such notation in
our proof of Theorem
4.5
.

4.3
Term algebras

In the outline proof of Proposition
4.6
,
we made reference to term trees over the binary operation
m
.
To establish properties of iterative midpoints, we
again need to consider terms, but allowing also the infinitary
operation
M

For an index set

the free term algebra over a single binary operator
and a single infinitary operator. This

\mathcal{T}_{I}

\mathcal{T}_{I}

We write

for the smallest subset of

closed

Given a function

substitution function

\displaystyle[\sigma]i

\displaystyle=\sigma(i)

\displaystyle[\sigma](s,t)

\displaystyle=([\sigma]s,[\sigma]t)

\displaystyle[\sigma](t_{l})_{l}

\displaystyle=([\sigma]t_{l})_{l}.

If
X
is a supermidpoint set,
with operations
M
and

, then the interpretation function

\operatorname{val}\colon\mathcal{T}_{I}\times X^{I}\to X

is defined by

\displaystyle\operatorname{val}(i,x)

\displaystyle=x_{i}

\displaystyle\operatorname{val}((s,t),x)

\displaystyle=m(\operatorname{val}(s,x),\operatorname{val}(t,x))

\displaystyle\operatorname{val}((t_{l})_{l},x)

\displaystyle=M_{l}\,\operatorname{val}(t_{l},x).

4.7 Lemma

For any
t\in\mathcal{T}_{I}
,
\;\sigma\colon I\to\mathcal{T}_{J}

and

\operatorname{val}([\sigma]t,y)=\operatorname{val}(t,\,i\mapsto\operatorname{val}(\sigma(i),y)).

Proof.

A straightforward induction on
t
.
∎

Analogously to the earlier definition for
\operatorname{\mathsf{K}}

\displaystyle\operatorname{w}(i)(j)

\displaystyle\operatorname{w}((s,t))(j)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{w}(s)(j)+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{w}(t)(j)

\displaystyle\operatorname{w}((t_{l})_{l})(j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\,2^{-(l+1)}\operatorname{w}(t_{l})(j).

For any

\operatorname{w}([\sigma]t)(j)\>=\>\sum_{i\in I}\,\operatorname{w}(t)(i)\cdot\operatorname{w}(\sigma(i))(j).

A straightforward induction on
t
.

6
Interval objects in an elementary topos

Let
\mathcal{E}
be an elementary topos with natural numbers
object
\mathbf{N}
. Our references for topos theory
are
[
Joh77
,
MLM94
]
.
Our notation is standard, in particular we write
\mathcal{P}(X)
for
the powerobject of
X
.
We shall make substantial use of the (intuitionistic) internal logic of
\mathcal{E}
,
the Mitchell-Bénabou language, using it informally, but
taking care to distinuish between
ordinary (external) mathematical statements and
statements that are to
be interpreted internally in
\mathcal{E}
. We shall use the following (standard)
definition.

6.1 Definition
(Separated object)

An
object
X
of
\mathcal{E}
is said to be
(double-negation) separated

if, internally in
\mathcal{E}
,

for all
x,x^{\prime}\in X
,
\lnot\lnot(x=x^{\prime})
implies
x=x^{\prime}
.

It is easily seen and standard that any subobject of a separated object is
itself separated.

It is a well-known fact that different constructions
of the real numbers, which are equivalent to each other using
classical logic, give different notions of real number
when interpreted within the intuitionistic internal logic
of a topos. Two notions are
particularly prevalent, the

Dedekind
reals and the
Cauchy
(or
Cantor
) reals.
Both are defined using the object of rationals
\mathbf{Q}
and its
associated ordering. We give brief reviews of the definitions.
For more details see
[
Joh77
]
.

One way of defining the object of
Dedekind reals in
\mathcal{E}
is as the subobject

\mathbf{R}_{D}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Q})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Q})
of
pairs
(L,U)
of sets
of rationals that satisfy:
L
and
U
are disjoint
inhabited sets;
L
is a down-closed set, each element of which
has a strictly greater element in
L
;
U

is an upper-closed set, each element of which
has a strictly lower element in
U
; and
(L,U)
satisfies
the
locatedness
property that
x<y
in
\mathbf{Q}
implies
either
x\in L
or
y\in U
. We write
\mathbf{R}_{D}
for the object
of Dedekind reals, and use standard notation for the
usual arithmetic operations on it.
We also identify

\mathbf{Q}
explicitly as a subobject of
\mathbf{R}_{D}
(via the
embedding
q\mapsto(\{r\mid r<q\},\{s\mid s>q\})
). It is not hard to
show that
\mathbf{R}_{D}
is a separated object in
\mathcal{E}

[
Joh77
, Lemma 6.63.iii]
.

One direct way of defining the Cauchy reals is as a quotient
of the object of all Cauchy sequences of rationals (where the
notion of Cauchy sequence must be phrased in an appropriate
constructive way, see below).
For our purposes, it is more convenient
to adopt an alternative equivalent definition, identifying
the Cauchy reals as particular Dedekind reals. First, we
recall the relevant intuitionistic definition of a Cauchy sequence
of Dedekind reals, which requires an explicit modulus of convergence.
A sequence
\alpha_{(-)}\in{\mathbf{R}_{D}}^{\mathbf{N}}
of Dedekind reals
is
Cauchy
if

\exists m\in\mathbf{N}^{\mathbf{Q}^{+}}.\;\forall\epsilon\in\mathbf{Q}^{+}.\;\forall i,j\geq m(\epsilon),\;|\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}|\leq\epsilon\,,

where
\mathbf{Q}^{+}
is the object of strictly positive rationals.
We say that
x\in\mathbf{R}_{D}
is the
limit
of the Cauchy sequence
\alpha

if

\exists m\in\mathbf{N}^{\mathbf{Q}^{+}}.\;\forall\epsilon\in\mathbf{Q}^{+}.\;\forall i\geq m(\epsilon),\;|\alpha_{i}-x|\leq\epsilon.

Here it is not strictly necessary to require the
modulus function to exist, as the assumed modulus of
\alpha

can be used instead.
The Cauchy reals are defined explicitly by

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{x\in\mathbf{R}_{D}\mid\mbox{$\exists\alpha\in\mathbf{Q}^{\mathbf{N}}$ s.t.
$\alpha$ is a Cauchy sequence and $x=\mathit{\>\lim\>}\alpha$}\},

where we are viewing
\mathbf{Q}
as a subobject of
\mathbf{R}_{D}
as above.
By the remark after Definition
6.1
,
\mathbf{R}_{C}
is
a separated object.

We say that a subobject
X\subseteq\mathbf{R}_{D}

is
Cauchy complete
if every Cauchy sequence in
X^{\mathbf{N}}
has
a (necessarily unique) limit in
X
. It is not hard to show that

\mathbf{R}_{D}
is Cauchy complete. Obviously
\mathbf{Q}
is not Cauchy complete.

\mathbf{R}_{C}
partially rectifies the non-completeness of
\mathbf{Q}
by
adding all limits of Cauchy sequences of rationals.
If
\mathcal{E}
satisfies the choice principle,

\displaystyle(\forall n\in\mathbf{N}.\,\exists m\in\mathbf{N}.\>\phi(n,m))\text{\>implies\>}(\exists f\in\mathbf{N}^{\mathbf{N}}.\,\forall n\in\mathbf{N}.\>\phi(n,f(n)))

\displaystyle(\mathrm{AC_{\mathbf{N}\mathbf{N}}})

which holds in any boolean topos, then it follows that

\mathbf{R}_{C}
is itself Cauchy complete. However, in general,

\mathbf{R}_{C}
is
not
Cauchy complete, see
[
Lub07
]
.

The possible failure of Cauchy completeness for
\mathbf{R}_{C}
makes it
natural to introduce another
object of reals, namely the Cauchy completion of
\mathbf{Q}

within
\mathbf{R}_{D}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigcap\{X\subseteq\mathbf{R}_{D}\mid\mbox{$\mathbf{Q}\subseteq X$ and $X$ is Cauchy complete}\}\,.

For reasons explained below,
we shall refer to
\mathbf{R}_{E}
as the object of
Euclidean
reals.

By definition,
\mathbf{R}_{E}
is Cauchy complete. Also, by the
remark after Definition
6.1
,

\mathbf{R}_{E}
is separated.
In contrast to the Cauchy reals, we do not have a direct method
of constructing the Euclidean reals without first going via
the Dedekind reals.

So far, we have identified three objects of reals

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\mathbf{R}_{C}&\subseteq&\mathbf{R}_{E}&\subseteq&\mathbf{R}_{D}\,.\end{array}

In the case that
\mathcal{E}
satisfies
\mathrm{AC_{\mathbf{N}\mathbf{N}}}
, both
inclusions are equalities. The Grothendieck
topos
\mathit{Sh}(\mathbb{R})

(sheaves over Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}
) is a simple example
in which the second inclusion is strict.
An example showing that the first inclusion need not be an isomorphism
has been given by Lubarsky
[
Lub07
]
.

Each kind of real number object determines a corresponding
interval; for example:

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\mathbf{I}_{D}&=&\{x\in\mathbf{R}_{D}\mid-1\leq x\leq 1\}&&\\
\mathbf{I}_{E}&=&\{x\in\mathbf{R}_{E}\mid-1\leq x\leq 1\}&=&\mathbf{R}_{E}\,\cap\,\mathbf{I}_{D}\end{array}

Again, by the remark after Definition
6.1
,
each of these intervals is a separated object of
\mathcal{E}
.
Our motivation for introducing the Euclidean reals
is that, as we shall see, it
is the Euclidean interval
\mathbf{I}_{E}
that gives
an interval object in
\mathcal{E}
.
Thus closed Euclidean intervals in
\mathcal{E}
enjoy the same universal property as closed intervals with the
Euclidean topology in
\mathbf{Top}
. Moreover, this universal property is motivated by constructions from Euclidean geometry,
as discussed in Section
1
. It is for these reasons that we refer to

\mathbf{R}_{E}
as the Euclidean reals in
\mathcal{E}
.

For
\mathbf{I}_{E}
to be an interval object it must

a fortiori
carry a midpoint algebra structure.
That it does so is not immediately obvious.
Indeed, as part of the proof that

\mathbf{I}_{E}
is an interval object, we shall prove
that
\mathbf{I}_{E}
is closed under several important operations
on
\mathbf{I}_{D}
, including
\oplus
.
We next define the closure properties to be established.

We say that a subobject
X\subseteq\mathbf{I}_{D}
is an

(endpointed) subalgebra
of
\mathbf{I}_{D}
if
-1,1\in X
and
X

is closed under
\oplus:\mathbf{I}_{D}\times\mathbf{I}_{D}\to\mathbf{I}_{D}
.
We say that a subalgebra

X\subseteq\mathbf{I}_{D}
is

symmetric
if
X
is closed under

-:\mathbf{I}_{D}\to\mathbf{I}_{D}
.

Next we observe that there is a
unique morphism
\operatorname{I}:\mathbf{R}_{D}\to\mathbf{I}_{D}
in
\mathcal{E}
satisfying,
internally in
\mathcal{E}
,

\operatorname{I}(x)\>=\>\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if $1\leq x$}\\
x&\mbox{if $-1\leq x\leq 1$}\\
-1&\mbox{if $x\leq-1$}.\end{array}\right.

(53)

There is some subtlety here.
The above does not, in itself, constitute a definition
of a morphism as it is not necessarily true
in
\mathcal{E}
that

for all
x\in\mathbf{R}_{D}
,
1\leq x
or

-1\leq x\leq 1
or
-1\leq x
.

Instead, (
53
) should be read as a constraint
on
\operatorname{I}
. A morphism satisfying
the constraint is defined explicitly by

\operatorname{I}(x)=\operatorname{min}(1,\operatorname{max}(x,-1))
.
The uniqueness of the morphism
is a routine consequence
of
\mathbf{R}_{D}
being a separated object.
The following is easy to show.

6.2 Lemma

For a symmetric subalgebra
X\subseteq\mathbf{I}_{D}
,
the following are equivalent.

1.

For all
x\in X
,
\operatorname{I}(2x)\in X
.

2.

For all
x,y\in X
,
\operatorname{I}(x+y)\in X
.

3.

For all
x,y\in X
,
\operatorname{I}(x-y)\in X
.

We say that a symmetric subalgebra

X
of
\mathbf{I}_{D}
is

if any of the equivalent
conditions of Proposition

hold.

6.3 Theorem

1.

\mathbf{I}_{E}
is a magnifiable symmetric subalgebra of
\mathbf{I}_{D}
.

2.

(\mathbf{I}_{E},\oplus,-1,1)
is an interval object in
\mathcal{E}
.

Crucial to the proof of Theorem

is an
alternative description of the Euclidean interval, which
is better adapted to establishing the universal property
of an interval object. To motivate its definition,
we first observe that
(\mathbf{I}_{D},\oplus)
is an m-convex body.
This follows from Proposition
2.6
(interpreted
in
\mathcal{E}
)
on account of the morphism

\bigoplus:{\mathbf{I}_{D}}^{\mathbf{N}}\to\mathbf{I}_{D}
defined by:

\bigoplus_{i}x_{i}\>=\>\sum_{i\geq 0}2^{-(i+1)}x_{i}\,,

using the Cauchy completeness of
\mathbf{R}_{D}
.
The alternative description of the interval object
is as the smallest subalgebra of
(\mathbf{I}_{D},\oplus)

that is closed under
\bigoplus
and contains

and

.
Explicitly, define

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigcap\{X\subseteq\mathbf{I}_{D}\mid\text{$-1,1\in X$ and $\bigoplus_{i}x_{i}\in X$
for all $x_{(-)}\in X^{\mathbf{N}}$}\}.

Then
\mathbf{I}
is itself closed under
\bigoplus
, hence also
under binary
\oplus
,
because

. Also,
\mathbf{I}\subseteq\mathbf{I}_{E}

because, by the Cauchy completeness of
\mathbf{R}_{E}
, it holds
that
\mathbf{I}_{E}
is closed under
\bigoplus
.

Theorem

is an immediate consequence of
the proposition below.

6.4 Proposition

1.

\mathbf{I}
is a magnifiable symmetric subalgebra of
\mathbf{I}_{D}
.

2.

(\mathbf{I},\oplus,-1,1)
is an interval object in
\mathcal{E}
.

3.

\mathbf{I}=\mathbf{I}_{E}
.

We now embark on the proof of the above proposition.
The whole proof is structured around its crucial use of Pataraia’s
fixed-point theorem
[
Pat97
]
, which we now
recall.

Let
(X,\leq)
be a poset in
\mathcal{E}

(i.e.
\leq
is a subobject
of
X\times X
satisfying the usual axioms for a non-strict
partial order, expressed in the internal logic of
\mathcal{E}
). Internally in
\mathcal{E}
,
we say that a subobject
D\subseteq X

(i.e. an element
D\in\mathcal{P}X
) is

directed
if it is inhabited (i.e. there exists some
x\in D
)
and, for any
x,y\in D
there exists
z\in D

with
x\leq z\geq y
.
(Because we are working intuitionistically,
the condition of being inhabited is stronger than the condition
of being nonempty.)
We say that
X
is a
directed-complete
partial order (dcpo)

if, internally in
\mathcal{E}
, every directed subobject
D\subseteq X

has a least upper bound (lub) in
X
.
We say that an endofunction
f
on a poset
X
is

monotone
if
x\leq y
implies

f(x)\leq f(y)
and
inflationary
if
x\leq f(x)
.

6.5 Theorem
(Pataraia
[
Pat97
]
)

If
X
is an inhabited dcpo then, internally in
\mathcal{E}
,
every monotone inflationary function on
X

has a fixed point.

Proof.

Let
I
be the object of monotone inflationary endofunctions on
X

ordered pointwise. It is easily checked that
I
is a dcpo:
lubs can be constructed pointwise because the image of a directed set
(of functions) under a monotone function (application to
an argument) is directed, and the lub so constructed is indeed
inflationary. Moreover,
I
is itself directed: the identity is in
I
;
and, given
f,g\in I
,
it holds that
f\leq f\circ g\geq g
. Therefore
I
considered as
a subobject of itself has a lub, i.e. I has a maximum element,
t
.
Then, for any
f\in I
we have that
f\circ t=t
. Thus, given
any element
x\in X
, it holds that
t(x)
is a common fixed point for
all monotone inflationary functions on
X
.
∎

Pataraia’s result has many applications. For example,
the authors have previously applied it to
the construction of initial algebras of endofunctors

[
Sim99
, Theorem 5]
, and to a localic version
of the Hofmann–Mislove Theorem
[

]
.
We now proceed with our third application, a proof
of Proposition

6.1

\mathcal{E}
with

Internally in
\mathcal{E}
, we say that a subalgebra

is

from

to

such that

Consider the subobject

(henceforth just

7
Discussion, questions and open problems

Our notion of interval object is motivated by Lawvere’s concept of

natural numbers object (nno)
, see Definition
2.1
. The
notion of nno gives a way of identifying the correct natural numbers
structure in an arbitrary category. Our definitions perform the same
task for the interval. Such an enterprise has been attempted before
in many different ways. In categories with rich
internal
logics
, such as
elementary toposes
, various (non-equivalent)
logical definitions of the real line are available, see, for example,

[
Joh02
, §D4.7]
. The idea of using algebraic
structure instead was first investigated by
Higgs
[
Hig78
]
, who defined
magnitude modules

(with a nullary operation
0
, a unary operation
h
, and an

\omega
-ary operation
\sum
subject to suitable axioms) and
characterised the interval
[0,\infty]
in the category of sets as the
free magnitude module on one generator, with
h(x)=x/2
and sums as
ordinary sums. Other authors have proposed
coalgebraic

definitions; for example,
[
PP99
]
. Peter Freyd, in
particular, obtained a characterisation, valid in any elementary
topos, of a closed interval of reals as a final
coalgebra
[
Joh02
, Theorem D4.7.17]
. Although his
theorem succeeds in characterising intervals of Dedekind reals, which
are often considered the real numbers of choice in a topos, its very
formulation relies on strong properties of the internal logic of a
topos. In contrast, as we have emphasised throughout the paper, the
formulation of our universal property requires only a category with
finite products. Also, our approach has the distinctive property of
being based on direct geometric conceptions of the interval and real
line. Furthermore, our universal property provides a direct means for
defining the arithmetic operations and other functions between
intervals of real numbers, as described in
Section
2.5
. This line was pursued further
in the original conference version of the
paper
[
ES01
]
, where we proposed a notion
of
primitive interval function
, a sort of primitive recursive
function on the interval
[-1,1]
, based on our universal
property. In
[
ES14
]
, we developed this
further, in the context of an extension of Gödel’s system T with a
primitive interval type, showing that the resulting type theory provides an extensional characterisation of the
class of all functions on the interval that are definable in system T
using an intensional representation of reals (the signed digit
representation).

One can consider Higgs’ magnitude modules
[
Hig78
]
in a
topological setting, in which the unary and
\omega
-ary operations
are required to be continuous, and realising that the continuity
requirement on the countable-sum operation rules out the Euclidean
topology. The free topological magnitude algebra on one generator is
presumably
[0,\infty]
with the topology of lower semicontinuity, but
we have not checked the details. The failure of continuity with
respect to the Euclidean topology is repaired, in this paper, by the
use of the iterated midpoint operation, leading to the results of
Section
5
.

We remark that useful topologies, different from
the Euclidean topology, can be obtained in our setting by considering
free midpoint-convex bodies over more exotic generating spaces. For
example, the midpoint-convex body freely generated by the Sierpiński
space (with one isolated point and one limit point) is the unit
interval with the topology of lower semicontinuity (which is the same
as the Scott topology), and the midpoint-convex body freely generated
by the poset with one minimal and two maximal elements, with the Scott
topology, is the unit interval domain (closed subintervals of
the unit interval ordered by reverse inclusion) with the Scott
topology.

Section
4
of this paper characterises the interval in the
category of sets (under classical logic), not just as the free
midpoint-convex body on two generators, but more generally as the free
interative midpoint object on two generators. That is, the
cancellation property of m-convex bodies does not need to be
assumed. This is achieved via Theorem
4.5
, which
shows an equivalence between iterative midpoint sets and iterative
superconvex sets. This result implies that supermidpoint sets are
equivalent to superconvex sets if either iterativity or cancellativity
is assumed on both sides. It would be interesting to know whether, in
fact, supermidpoint sets are equivalent to superconvex sets in full
generality, without either of the two non-equational assumptions. This
can only be true if equation (
20
)
from Proposition
3.1
holds for arbitrary supermidpoint
objects. Independently of this, we conjecture that
if (
20
) is added as an additional equation to the
supermidpoint axioms (Definition
2.10
) then one obtains a complete
equational axiomatisation of superconvex sets in terms of the iterated midpoint operation alone. We remark that the paper
[
MR17
]
presents a finite equational axiomatisation of an algebraic theory containing the iterated midpoint operation, where the algebraic theory is the theory of continuous maps between infinite dimensional cubes
[0,1]^{\kappa}
, studied by Isbell
[
Isb82
]
, who was the first to realise the importance of the iterated midpoint operation in that context. The finite axiomatisation in
[
MR17
]
does not answer our conjecture, because it crucially relies on the presence of additional non-affine operations in the theory of
[
Isb82
]
.
