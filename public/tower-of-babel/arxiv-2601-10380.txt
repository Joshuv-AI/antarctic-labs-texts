Learning Hamiltonians in the Heisenberg limit with static single-qubit fields
Shrigyan Brahmachari, Shuchen Zhu, Iman Marvian, Yu Tong

Abstract
Learning the Hamiltonian governing a quantum system is a central task in quantum metrology, sensing, and device characterization. Existing Heisenberg-limited Hamiltonian learning protocols either require multi-qubit operations that are prone to noise, or single-qubit operations whose frequency or strength increases with the desired precision. These two requirements limit the applicability of Hamiltonian learning on near-term quantum platforms. We present a protocol that learns a quantum Hamiltonian with the optimal Heisenberg-limited scaling using only single-qubit control in the form of static fields with strengths that are independent of the target precision. Our protocol is robust against the state preparation and measurement (SPAM) error. By overcoming these limitations, our protocol provides new tools for device characterization and quantum sensing. We demonstrate that our method achieves the Heisenberg-limited scaling through rigorous mathematical proof and numerical experiments. We also prove an information-theoretic lower bound showing that a non-vanishing static field strength is necessary for achieving the Heisenberg limit unless one employs an extensive number of discrete control operations.



A broad range of problems in quantum metrology and sensing can be formulated as learning the properties of an unknown Hamiltonian
H
that governs the dynamics of a closed quantum system, through measurements of its time evolution

Degen et al. 2017
;
Giovannetti et al. 2011
;
Giovannetti et al. 2006
;
Caves 1981
;

;

For example, a qubit magnetometer

can be used to measure an unknown external magnetic field
\vec{B}
by probing the evolution generated by the Hamiltonian

H=-\mu\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\sigma}
, which describes a spin-
1/2
system with magnetic moment
\mu

\vec{B}

By monitoring the system over time, one can extract information about the unknown Hamiltonian
H

\vec{B}

To learn such an unknown Hamiltonian with precision
\epsilon
, one must probe the system for a time that scales at least as
\epsilon^{-1}

However, even for the practically important cases of single- and two-qubit Hamiltonians with multiple unknown parameters, the simple protocol consisting of state preparation, free evolution under
H

T=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
to reach error
\epsilon

Pang and Brun 2014
.
Achieving Heisenberg-limited scaling for such general Hamiltonians therefore requires some form of
quantum control
.
For instance, one may apply control operations to effectively reshape or simulate the Hamiltonian
Huang et al. 2023
, or introduce multi-qubit interactions that modify the Hamiltonian
Dutkiewicz et al. 2024
;
Bakshi et al. 2024
;
Haah et al. 2023
. However, these techniques come with their own challenges. For example, the Hamiltonian-reshaping technique in
Huang et al. 2023

\epsilon

\sqrt{\epsilon}

Dutkiewicz et al. 2024
;
Bakshi et al. 2024
;
Haah et al. 2023

In this Letter, we introduce a simple and practical Hamiltonian learning protocol that achieves the optimal Heisenberg-limited scaling, enabling the estimation of a quantum Hamiltonian with error
\epsilon
using a total evolution time
T=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
.
Crucially, unlike previous approaches (e.g., Ref.
Huang et al. 2023
), our protocol requires only single-qubit control in the form of static fields whose strengths are independent of the target precision. Specifically, static control fields of fixed strength
\nu>0
are applied along the
x
,
y
, and
z
directions, modifying the system Hamiltonian from
H
to
H-\nu H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}
(see Fig.
1
).

Furthermore, the protocol does not require any entangling gates, even when the Hamiltonian
H
itself is entangling. The qubits are initially prepared in a tensor product of Pauli eigenstates, evolve under the modified Hamiltonian
H-\nu H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}
, and are finally measured via single-qubit Pauli measurements. Finally, the protocol is robust against state-preparation and measurement (SPAM) errors: as long as these errors remain below a fixed threshold independent of
\epsilon
, the target precision can still be achieved. Theorem
1
characterizes the performance of the protocol.

Theorem 1
.

For an unknown Hamiltonian given in (

2.

Our second main result, which complements the above theorem, is a generalization of the so-called Heisenberg limit. It establishes a fundamental lower bound on the minimum total evolution time
T
required to achieve a target error
\epsilon
. More precisely, we derive a quantitative trade-off between the achievable precision
\epsilon
and the relevant physical and operational resources, namely the field strength
\nu
, the total evolution time
T
, and the number of
discrete control operations

L
, defined as the number of times the system’s evolution is interrupted in order to measure, prepare, or reset the qubits, or to modify the control Hamiltonian. Crucially, our bound fully accounts for the possibility of adaptive learning protocols. We note that related extensions of the Heisenberg limit have previously been established in settings without static fields but with discrete control gates
Dutkiewicz et al. 2024
;
Ma et al. 2024
.

Theorem 2

(Informal version of Theorem S6 in SM)
.

Suppose there exists a (possibly adaptive) protocol for learning an unknown single-qubit Hamiltonian

H=\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\sigma}
, with
\|\hat{n}\|=1
, to precision
\epsilon
using

L=o(1/\epsilon)
discrete control operations. Suppose the protocol is allowed to apply control Hamiltonians of the form
-\nu H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}
,
where
H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}
is an arbitrary single-qubit Hermitian operator with operator norm
at most
1
.
Then, for generic Hamiltonians
H
, any such protocol must use a total evolution time at least

T=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{4\nu\epsilon+3\epsilon^{2}}\right).

In the above we write
f=\Omega(g)
to mean
f(x)\geq cg(x)
for some constant
c>0
and sufficiently large
x
.
A proof is included in Section VII in SM.
In particular, this theorem shows that without an extensive number of discrete control operations, i.e., for
L\ll 1/\epsilon
, one can only achieve the Heisenberg-limited scaling with static field strength
\nu=\Omega(1)
, and
1/\nu
also lower bounds the prefactor appearing in the
1/\epsilon
scaling.
We need to exclude the situation where
\Omega(1/\epsilon)
discrete control operations are used because that alone would be sufficient for attaining the Heisenberg limit
Dutkiewicz et al. 2024
;
Bakshi et al. 2024
. This lower bound reveals a fundamental tradeoff between the total evolution time, the field strength, and discrete control operations needed to attain a given precision.

To prove this theorem, we consider the task of discriminating between two fixed single-qubit Hamiltonians corresponding to two known normalized vectors, e.g.,
\hat{z}
and
(\hat{z}+\epsilon\hat{x})/\sqrt{1+\epsilon^{2}}
, a problem previously studied in Ref.
Pang and Brun 2014
. If there exists a learning protocol that learns them with precision
\epsilon/2
then we will be able to distinguish these two Hamiltonians.
Our key observation, proven in Corollary S1 in SM, is that in the presence of the control Hamiltonian
\nu H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}
,
the distance between the unitary evolution operators generated by these two Hamiltonians grows like
\mathcal{O}((\nu\epsilon+\epsilon^{2})T+L\epsilon)
. We adopt the framework used in
Huang et al. 2023
;
Huang et al. 2022
to represent any adaptive experimental protocol as a tree, and analyze the distance between output distributions generated by these two Hamiltonians, thereby generating a lower bound through Le Cam’s two-point method
Yu 1997
.

The Setup
– In the rest of this Letter, we describe and analyze our learning protocol in further detail. We consider a system of
n
qubits evolving under an unknown Hamiltonian

H(\bm{\lambda})=\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}\setminus 0^{n}}\lambda_{\mathbf{a}}\sigma^{\mathbf{a}},

(1)

where
\sigma^{\mathbf{a}}=\sigma^{a_{1}}\otimes\sigma^{a_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma^{a_{n}}
. Here
\sigma^{0}
denotes the single-qubit identity, while
\sigma^{1,2,3}
denote the Pauli
X
,
Y
, and
Z
matrices, respectively (We will also sometimes use
\sigma^{x,y,z}_{k}
to denote the Pauli-X, Y, or Z operator acting on the
k
th qubit). Without loss of generality, we assume that the Hamiltonian is traceless, and to fix the normalization, we assume the unknown coefficients
\lambda_{\mathbf{a}}
satisfy
-1\leq\lambda_{\mathbf{a}}\leq 1

(Any Hamiltonian can be rescaled so that this condition holds).

Our goal is to obtain estimates
\hat{\bm{\lambda}}=(\hat{\lambda}_{\mathbf{a}})
such that with probability, at least,
1-\delta
,
its
\ell^{2}
-distance with actual

\bm{\lambda}
is bounded by
\epsilon
, i.e.,

\Pr[\|\hat{\bm{\lambda}}-\bm{\lambda}\|\leq\epsilon]\geq 1-\delta.

(2)

For the control Hamiltonian, we will apply sufficiently strong static single-qubit fields along the
\pm\hat{x}
,
\pm\hat{y}
, and
\pm\hat{z}
directions. More specifically, for
n\geq 2
, we choose the field strength on the
k
th qubit to be
\nu/2
and that on the remaining qubits to be
\nu
. As an example, Figure
1
shows a control Hamiltonian

\nu H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}=\nu\sigma^{x}_{1}-\nu\sigma^{x}_{2}+\nu\sigma^{z}_{3}+\frac{\nu}{2}\sigma^{z}_{4}-\nu\sigma^{y}_{5}+\nu\sigma^{z}_{6}

(3)

Any such Hamiltonian can be specified by a set of parameters
(k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})
, where
\mathbf{s}
is a bit-string specifying the signs (
0,1
for
+,-
respectively), and
\bm{\beta}\in\{1,2,3\}^{n}
specifies the directions. In the above example
\mathbf{s}=010010
,
\bm{\beta}=(1,1,3,3,2,3)
, and
k=4
. The system then evolves under the total Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{tot}}(\bm{\lambda},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})=H(\bm{\lambda})-\nu H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta}).

(4)

This choice of the control Hamiltonian gaurantees that the ground state
\left|\Psi_{0}\right\rangle
and the first excited state
\left|\Psi_{1}\right\rangle
of
H_{\mathrm{tot}}(\bm{\lambda},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})
are both nondegenerate and protected by energy gaps scaling with
\nu
(That is why we have chosen a different field strength of
\nu/2
on one of the qubits.). Moreover, they are close to the ground state
\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle
and first excited state
\left|\Phi_{1}\right\rangle
of
-\nu H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})
respectively. Also, our construction ensures that
\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle
,
\left|\Phi_{1}\right\rangle
, and their superposition
\left|\Phi_{+}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle+\left|\Phi_{1}\right\rangle)

are unentangled product state of single-qubit Pauli eigenstates, and are therefore easy to prepare.

Phase estimation experiment.
To learn the coefficients
\bm{\lambda}
, we perform a sequence of
phase-estimation experiments
to estimate the energy gap
E_{\Delta}(\bm{\lambda},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})
between the ground and first excited states of
H_{\mathrm{tot}}(\bm{\lambda},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})
. As explained below, this can be achieved despite the fact that
H
is unknown and the relevant eigenstates of
H_{\mathrm{tot}}
and their superpositions cannot be prepared.

The key idea is to prepare the qubits in the state
\left|\Phi_{+}\right\rangle
, i.e., the superposition of the ground and first excited states of
-H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}
. We then let the system evolve under the modified Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{tot}}=H-\nu H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}
for time
t
, and then measure one of two observables
O_{c}
and
O_{s}
, each of which is a single-qubit Pauli operator on qubit
k
. Specifically, they are chosen such that
O_{c}|\Phi_{0}\rangle=|\Phi_{1}\rangle
, and

O_{s}|\Phi_{0}\rangle=-i|\Phi_{1}\rangle
, which means they act as
\sigma^{x}
and
-\sigma^{y}
in the subspace spanned by
\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle
and
\left|\Phi_{1}\right\rangle
(Note that
\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle
and
\left|\Phi_{1}\right\rangle
are identical on all qubits, except qubit
k
).
As an example, in the setting described in Figure
1
, with
\beta_{k}=3
, we can simply choose
O_{c}=\sigma^{x}_{k}
and
O_{s}=-\sigma^{y}_{k}
, which are Pauli operators acting on the
k
th qubit.

Measuring the Pauli operator
O
on qubit
k
, we observe
(-1)^{b}:b=0,1
eigenvalue, where bit
b=0
with probability

\Pr[b=0]=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\braket{\Phi_{+}|e^{iH_{\mathrm{tot}}t}Oe^{-iH_{\mathrm{tot}}t}|\Phi_{+}}\right),

(5)

where
O
stands for
O_{c}
or
O_{s}
, depending on which we choose to measure. Each run yields a sample from this distribution. From this statistic, one can obtain estimates of the expectation values
\braket{O_{c}(t)}
and
\braket{O_{s}(t)}
as functions of time
t
, for the initial state
|\Phi_{+}\rangle
.

Next, applying perturbation theory, in SM we show that for sufficiently large
\nu
these expectation values are close to the expectation values obtained for the initial state
|\Psi_{+}\rangle=(|\Psi_{0}\rangle+|\Psi_{1}\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
, the equal superposition of the actual ground and first excited states of
H_{\mathrm{tot}}
. Hence, the dominant frequency in signals
\braket{O_{c}(t)}
and
\braket{O_{s}(t)}
is determined by the energy gap
E_{\Delta}
between these two eigenstates. Indeed, we show in SM that the error is bounded by

|\braket{O_{c}(t)}+i\braket{O_{s}(t)}-e^{iE_{\Delta}t}|=\mathcal{O}(\|H\|/\nu).

(6)

Crucially, as long as this error remains bounded by a finite constant, one can extract the dominant frequency
E_{\Delta}
with Heisenberg-limited precision using a robust frequency-estimation protocol
Li et al. 2024
;
Ma et al. 2024
;
Hu et al. 2025
;
Möbus et al. 2023
, closely related to the robust phase-estimation algorithm of Ref.
Kimmel et al. 2015
.
More precisely, to estimate
E_{\Delta}
to precision
\epsilon
, one only needs to estimate every
\braket{O_{c}(t)}
and
\braket{O_{s}(t)}
to a precision independent of
\epsilon
, namely within
1/\sqrt{8}
additive error. Indeed, we show that using
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{polylog}(\epsilon^{-1}\delta^{-1}))
experiments that run in time at most
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon)
, one obtains an
\epsilon
-accurate estimate of
E_{\Delta}
. The total evolution time needed for this series of experiments is
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1}\log(1/\delta))
.

Moreover, the errors in estimates for
\braket{O_{c}(t)}
and
\braket{O_{s}(t)}
are not required to be unbiased. Therefore, even if there is a systematic bias in our estimates created by the SPAM error, our protocol can still achieve arbitrarily high precision in the estimation for
E_{\Delta}
. In this sense our protocol is robust against SPAM error.

Learning protocol.

The next step is to learn the unknown Hamiltonian coefficients
\bm{\lambda}
from the collected information about the energy gaps
E_{\Delta}(\bm{\lambda},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})
. Note that, a priori, it is not clear whether
\bm{\lambda}
can be (i) uniquely determined from
E_{\Delta}(\bm{\lambda},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})
, and (ii) determined robustly, i.e., if the energy gaps are estimated with precision
\epsilon^{\prime}
, the recovery map
g
estimates the coefficients to precision at most
C\epsilon^{\prime}
, for some constant
C
independent of
\bm{\lambda}
. For instance, this condition fails if
g(x)=\sqrt{x}
, since achieving precision
\epsilon
in estimating
x\ll 1
results in a quadratically worse precision,
\sqrt{\epsilon}
. In the present context, such behavior would prevent achieving the Heisenberg limit.

We prove that these requirements are all satisfied by the least-square minimizer, defined below. Let
\mathbf{E}(\bm{\lambda},\nu)=(E_{\Delta}(\bm{\lambda},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta}))_{k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta}}
be the vector of all actual energy gaps corresponding to all experiments, and
\hat{\mathbf{E}}
be the corresponding estimates obtained through the above method. Define the loss function

L(\hat{\mathbf{E}},\bm{\mu},\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\|\hat{\mathbf{E}}-\mathbf{E}(\bm{\mu},\nu)\|^{2}\ ,

(7)

where
\bm{\mu}
is a free variable.
Then, we choose our estimate of the Hamiltonian coefficient as
\hat{\bm{\lambda}}=\mathrm{argmin}_{\bm{\mu}}L(\hat{\mathbf{E}},\bm{\mu},\nu)
.

Note that since the problem is nonlinear, in general, it may contain multiple local minima, making it difficult to uniquely recover the correct
\bm{\lambda}
. However, thanks to the choice of our control Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}
, in SM we are able to prove the following crucial properties in SM:

1.

When
\bm{\mu}
and
\hat{\mathbf{E}}
are within a constant distance of the true values
\bm{\lambda}
and
\mathbf{E}^{\star}=\mathbf{E}(\bm{\lambda},\nu)
, respectively,
the cost function
L(\hat{\mathbf{E}},\bm{\mu},\nu)
is locally strongly convex. In particular,
all eigenvalues of the Hessian matrix
\partial_{\bm{\mu}}^{2}L(\hat{\mathbf{E}},\bm{\mu},\nu)
are lower bounded by
2^{n-1}
(See Proposition S1 in SM).

2.

Using a modified version of the inverse function theorem proved in the SM, this strong convexity guarantees that, for
\hat{\mathbf{E}}
sufficiently close to
\mathbf{E}^{\star}
, there exists a unique
\mu
that minimizes the cost function, which we take as the estimate
\hat{\bm{\lambda}}
. That is, there exists a map
g
such that

\hat{\bm{\lambda}}=g(\hat{\mathbf{E}})=\mathrm{argmin}_{\bm{\mu}}L(\hat{\mathbf{E}},\bm{\mu},\nu).

(8)

This map can be computed using standard numerical optimization methods, such as Newton’s method.

3.

Our modified inverse function theorem provides a constant upper bound on
\|\partial_{\hat{\mathbf{E}}}g(\hat{\mathbf{E}})\|
in the neighborhood of
\mathbf{E}^{\star}
. This, in particular, implies that a perturbation of magnitude
\epsilon^{\prime}
on
\hat{\mathbf{E}}
translates to a perturbation at most
C\epsilon^{\prime}
on
\hat{\bm{\lambda}}
, for a fixed constant
C
.

In conclusion, these results imply that recovering the coefficients
\bm{\lambda}
to precision
\epsilon
only requires estimating
\mathbf{E}^{\star}
to precision
\epsilon/C
, thus preserving the Heisenberg-limited scaling. Since the strong convexity plays a key role in the effectiveness of our method, we provide some intuition. The Hessian matrix can be computed as

\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial\bm{\mu}^{2}}=\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}}{\partial\bm{\mu}}\right)^{\top}\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}}{\partial\bm{\mu}}\right)+(\mathbf{E}(\bm{\mu})-\hat{\mathbf{E}})^{\top}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{E}}{\partial\bm{\mu}^{2}}\ .

(9)

When
\bm{\mu}
is close to the actual value
\bm{\lambda}
and
\hat{\mathbf{E}}
close to
\mathbf{E}^{\star}
, both
\mathbf{E}(\bm{\mu})
and
\hat{\mathbf{E}}
will be close to
\mathbf{E}^{\star}
, making the second term on the right-hand side small. Furthermore, as we show in SM, the first term is

\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}}{\partial\bm{\mu}}\right)^{\top}\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}}{\partial\bm{\mu}}\right)=J_{0}+\mathcal{O}_{n}(1/\nu)\ ,

(10)

where
J_{0}
is the Hessian matrix in the
\nu\to\infty
limit for
\hat{\mathbf{E}}=\mathbf{E}(\bm{\mu})
. Therefore, for large enough
\nu
the smallest eigenvalue of the Hessian matrix
\partial_{\bm{\mu}}^{2}L
can be well-approximated by that of
J_{0}
. In Section III of SM, we explicitly compute the matrix
J_{0}
using the fact that in
\nu\to\infty
limit, the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian and coefficients
\bm{\lambda}
are related through a Walsh-Hadamard transform, also known as the Fourier transform over the Boolean group
(\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{\times n}
..

Error and time analysis.

In the SM, we analyze the error in the protocol and prove Theorem
1
. To estimate the coefficients
\bm{\lambda}
with precision
\epsilon
, we first obtain an estimate
\hat{\mathbf{E}}
of the eigenvalues via phase-estimation experiments with precision
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
. This requires a total evolution time
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon)
, achieving Heisenberg-limited scaling. The estimate
\hat{\bm{\lambda}}=g(\hat{\mathbf{E}})
is then obtained by solving the optimization problem defined by (
7
). Theorem S2 in the SM guarantees that this solution is close to the true value of
\bm{\lambda}
. The map
g(\hat{\mathbf{E}})
is computed using the iterative solver in Algorithm 1 of the SM, which requires an initial guess within a constant distance of the true parameters. Such an initial guess can be obtained using a suboptimal Hamiltonian learning protocol
Stilck França et al. 2024
that does not employ quantum control, and instead relies on state preparation, time evolution under the Hamiltonian
H
, and measurement. Since this preliminary step only requires constant precision, its non-Heisenberg-limited scaling does not affect the overall performance. The protocol is fully non-adaptive, and the control operations depend only on the number of qubits.

Discussions.
In this work, we introduce a Heisenberg-limited Hamiltonian learning protocol that overcomes practical limitations of existing approaches. Unlike prior methods that rely either on multi-qubit operations or on single-qubit control whose strength or frequency scales with the desired estimation precision, our protocol achieves optimal Heisenberg-limited scaling using only single-qubit control implemented through static fields of fixed, precision-independent strength. This makes the method well suited for near-term quantum devices with limited control capabilities. In terms of the analysis of the algorithm, we note that it goes beyond a rotating wave approximation (e.g., as described in
Burgarth et al. 2022
;
Burgarth et al. 2024
) because we cannot allow any approximation error to accumulate in time.

Our protocol applies to an arbitrary Hamiltonian on
n
qubits. For generic Hamiltonians, the presence of
4^{n}-1
independent coefficients implies an exponential runtime in
n
. In contrast, for Hamiltonians with only local interactions, the cost scales polynomially, or even polylogarithmically, with the system size
n

;
Sinha and Tong 2025
;
Rosati 2025
;
Zhao 2025
. It is then natural to ask whether one can generalize the ideas in this work to the setting of local Hamiltonians with a
\mathrm{poly}(n)
or
\mathrm{polylog}(n)
cost.

Besides Hamiltonian learning, the idea of extracting information by applying single-qubit static fields may be useful for related tasks such as learning a function of Hamiltonian parameters
Abbasgholinejad et al. 2025
;
Eldredge et al. 2018
;
Proctor et al. 2017
;
Proctor et al. 2018
;
Suzuki et al. 2020
;
Ehrenberg et al. 2023
;
Bringewatt et al. 2024
without learning the whole Hamiltonian. In this setting, ideas from Hamiltonian learning such as Hamiltonian reshaping has already proved useful
Abbasgholinejad et al. 2025
. One may also consider the setting in which only part of the quantum system is accessible, as studied in
Chen et al. 2025
, and static fields may provide a useful tool to decouple and to probe the system. One may also consider leveraging techniques from quantum error correction to mitigate decoherence in the quantum system
Zhou et al. 2017
.

i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma^{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(11)

For an
n
-qubit quantum system, we will write
\sigma^{a}_{j}
for
\sigma^{a}
acting only on the
j

Let
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n})\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}

\sigma^{\mathbf{a}}=\sigma^{a_{1}}\otimes\sigma^{a_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma^{a_{n}}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\sigma^{a_{j}}_{j}.

For Pauli eigenstates, we use

to denote the
(-1)^{s}

\sigma^{a}

\left|0,1\right\rangle=\left|+\right\rangle,\quad\left|1,1\right\rangle=\left|-\right\rangle,\quad\left|0,2\right\rangle=\left|+i\right\rangle,\quad\left|1,2\right\rangle=\left|-i\right\rangle,\quad\left|0,3\right\rangle=\left|0\right\rangle,\quad\left|1,3\right\rangle=\left|1\right\rangle.

For two strings
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}
, we say they are

, denoted by
\mathbf{a}\sim\mathbf{b}
, if for every

a_{j}=b_{j},\ a_{j}=0,\text{ or }b_{j}=0.

(12)

\mathbf{a}\sim\mathbf{b}
, then
\sigma^{\mathbf{a}}
and
\sigma^{\mathbf{b}}
commute in every component.
For any
a\in\{0,1,2,3\}
, we define

s(a)=\begin{cases}0\text{ if }a=0,\\
1\text{ if }a\neq 0.\end{cases}

(13)

For
\mathbf{a}\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}
, we also define

s(\mathbf{a})=(s(a_{1}),s(a_{2}),\cdots,s(a_{n})).

(14)

We write

|\mathbf{a}|=\sum_{j}s(a_{j})

(15)

to denote the number of non-zero elements in

.

We adopt the asymptotic notation:
f=\mathcal{O}(g)
means
|f|\leq Cg
for some absolute constant
C
;

f=\mathcal{O}_{n}(g)

I
Problem setup

We assume access to a quantum system evolving under the unknown Hamiltonian

H(\bm{\lambda})=\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}}\lambda_{\mathbf{a}}\sigma^{\mathbf{a}},

(16)

where
-1\leq\lambda_{\mathbf{a}}\leq 1
, and
\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{\mathbf{a}})_{\mathbf{a}\neq\mathbf{0}}
. We assume that the Hamiltonian is traceless so as to avoid the ambiguity coming from the global phase. Our goal is to obtain estimates
\hat{\lambda}_{\mathbf{a}}
for each
\mathbf{a}\neq\mathbf{0}
such that

\Pr[\|\hat{\bm{\lambda}}-\bm{\lambda}\|\leq\epsilon]\geq 1-\delta,

(17)

Here
\|\cdot\|
denotes the
\ell^{2}
-norm,
\epsilon
is the target precision and
\delta
is the failure probability we can tolerate.

We will probe the system by applying external fields in the form of single-qubit Hamiltonians. More precisely, add to the original Hamiltonian a control Hamiltonian
-\nu H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})

H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})=\sum_{j\neq k}(-1)^{s_{j}}\sigma^{\beta_{j}}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}(-1)^{s_{k}}\sigma^{\beta_{k}}_{k},

(18)

where
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\cdots,s_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{n})\in\{1,2,3\}^{n}
. The system then evolves under the Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{tot}}(\bm{\lambda},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})=H(\bm{\lambda})-\nu H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta}).

(19)

II
The phase estimation experiment

For simplicity we will temporarily omit the dependence on
\nu,\bm{\lambda},\bm{\beta},k,\mathbf{s}
since these are fixed within a single experiment.
We define the following states:

\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle=\bigotimes_{j}\left|s_{j},\beta_{j}\right\rangle,\quad\left|\Phi_{1}\right\rangle=\left(\bigotimes_{j<k}\left|s_{j},\beta_{j}\right\rangle\right)\otimes\left|1-s_{k},\beta_{k}\right\rangle\otimes\left(\bigotimes_{j>k}\left|s_{j},\beta_{j}\right\rangle\right).

(20)

One can readily verify that they are the ground state and the first excited state of
-H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}
respectively. In our phase estimation experiment, we will initialize the quantum system in the state

\left|\Phi_{+}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle+\left|\Phi_{1}\right\rangle).

(21)

Note that this state is still a tensor product of single-qubit Pauli eigenstates and is therefore easy to prepare. We then let the quantum system evolve under the unknown Hamiltonian
H
together with the external control
-\nu H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}
, which can be described by the time evolution operator
e^{-iH_{\mathrm{tot}}t}
. At the end of the experiment when measure one of the two single-qubit Pauli observables
O_{c}
and
O_{s}
. They are chosen so that

O_{c}\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle=\left|\Phi_{1}\right\rangle,\quad O_{c}\left|\Phi_{1}\right\rangle=\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle,\quad O_{s}\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle=-ii\left|\Phi_{1}\right\rangle,\quad O_{s}\left|\Phi_{1}\right\rangle=i\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle.

(22)

Such
O_{c}
and
O_{s}
are always available in the form of single-qubit Pauli operators.
As an example, when
\beta_{k}=3
, we can simply choose
O_{c}=\sigma^{1}_{k}
and
O_{s}=-\sigma^{2}_{k}
, i.e., Pauli-
X
and
Y
operators acting on the
k
th qubit. Therefore from each run of the experiment we sample a binary output
b
such that,

\Pr[b=0]=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\braket{\Phi_{+}|e^{iH_{\mathrm{tot}}t}O_{c/s}e^{-iH_{\mathrm{tot}}t}|\Phi_{+}}\right),

(23)

where the observable
O_{c/s}
in the middle is
O_{c}
or
O_{s}
depending on which we choose to measure.

II.1
Analysis of the phase estimation experiment

In this subsection we will discuss what results we expect from the phase estimation experiment described above. Our initial state is
\left|\Phi_{+}\right\rangle
, which is an equal superposition of
\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle
and
\left|\Phi_{1}\right\rangle
. Note that these two states are the ground state and the first excited state of
-H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}
respectively, whereas the system evolves under
H_{\mathrm{tot}}=H-\nu H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}
.
We denote the ground state and the first excited state of this Hamiltonian by
\left|\Psi_{0}\right\rangle
and
\left|\Psi_{1}\right\rangle
, with the corresponding eigenvalues
E_{0}
and
E_{1}
.
Because
\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle
and
\left|\Phi_{1}\right\rangle
are eigenstates of
-H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}
with energy gap
1
, we have the following lemma, which follows from applying Lemma
S4
to
(1/\nu)H_{\mathrm{tot}}=-H_{\mathrm{ctrl}}+H/\nu
:

Lemma S1
.

If
\nu\geq 3\|H\|
, with an appropriately chosen global phase for
\left|\Psi_{0}\right\rangle
and
\left|\Psi_{1}\right\rangle
, we have

\left\|\left|\Psi_{0}\right\rangle-\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle\right\|\leq 3\|H\|/\nu,\quad\left\|\left|\Psi_{1}\right\rangle-\left|\Phi_{1}\right\rangle\right\|\leq 3\|H\|/\nu.

This means that
\left|\Phi_{+}\right\rangle
is approximately a superposition of
\left|\Psi_{0}\right\rangle
and
\left|\Psi_{1}\right\rangle
. We will use this observation to approximately compute the output probabilities given in (
23
). It suffices to compute

\Big|\braket{\Phi_{+}|e^{iH_{\mathrm{tot}}t}O_{c/s}e^{-iH_{\mathrm{tot}}t}|\Phi_{+}}-\frac{1}{2}(\left\langle\Psi_{0}\right|e^{iE_{0}t}+\left\langle\Psi_{1}\right|e^{iE_{1}t})O_{c/s}(e^{-iE_{0}t}\left|\Psi_{0}\right\rangle+e^{-iE_{1}t}\left|\Psi_{1}\right\rangle)\Big|\leq 6\sqrt{2}\|H\|/\nu

(24)

where we have used Lemma
S1
and the triangle inequality.
Using (
22
) we have

\displaystyle\Big|\braket{\Phi_{+}|e^{iH_{\mathrm{tot}}t}O_{c}e^{-iH_{\mathrm{tot}}t}|\Phi_{+}}

\displaystyle-\cos((E_{1}-E_{0})t)\Big|\leq 6\sqrt{2}\|H\|/\nu,

(25)

\displaystyle\Big|\braket{\Phi_{+}|e^{iH_{\mathrm{tot}}t}O_{s}e^{-iH_{\mathrm{tot}}t}|\Phi_{+}}

\displaystyle-\sin((E_{1}-E_{0})t)\Big|\leq 6\sqrt{2}\|H\|/\nu.

Therefore, we define the energy gap

E_{\Delta}=E_{1}-E_{0},

(26)

then, when we are measuring
O_{c}
, the probability of obtaining
0
satisfies

\Big|\Pr[b=0]-\frac{1+\cos(E_{\Delta}t)}{2}\Big|\leq 6\sqrt{2}\|H\|/\nu,

(27)

and when we are measuring
O_{s}
the probability likewise satisfies

\Big|\Pr[b=0]-\frac{1+\sin(E_{\Delta}t)}{2}\Big|\leq 6\sqrt{2}\|H\|/\nu,

(28)

II.2
Robust frequency estimation

The robust frequency estimation algorithm
Li et al. 2024
;
Ma et al. 2024
;
Hu et al. 2025
;
Möbus et al. 2025
, which is a variant of the robust phase estimation algorithm
Kimmel et al. 2015
, allows us to estimate the energy gap
E_{\Delta}
to precision
\epsilon
with
1/\epsilon
total evolution time, as stated in the following theorem:

Theorem S1

(Theorem 4.1 in
Möbus et al. 2025
)
.

Let
\theta\in[-\Phi,\Phi]
.
Let
X(t)
and
Y(t)
be independent random variables satisfying

\displaystyle|X(t)-\cos(\theta t)|<1/\sqrt{8},\text{ with probability at least }2/3,

(29)

\displaystyle|Y(t)-\sin(\theta t)|<1/\sqrt{8},\text{ with probability at least }2/3.

Then with independent samples
X(t_{1}),X(t_{2}),\cdots,X(t_{\Gamma})
and
Y(t_{1}),Y(t_{2}),\cdots,Y(t_{\Gamma})
, with

{\Gamma}=\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}(\Phi/\epsilon)),\quad T=t_{1}+t_{2}+\cdots+t_{\Gamma}=\Theta(1/\epsilon),\quad\max_{j}t_{j}=\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon),

(30)

and
t_{j}\geq 0
, we can construct an estimator
\hat{\theta}
such that

\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[|\hat{\theta}-\theta|^{2}]}\leq\epsilon.

(31)

The algorithm works by solving a sequence of
decision problems, in each of which we start with an interval
[a,b]
containing
\theta
, and shrink it to a smaller interval containing
\theta
that is either
[a,\frac{1}{3}a+\frac{2}{3}b]
or
[\frac{2}{3}a+\frac{1}{3}b,b]
, thus refining our knowledge of the unknown parameter
\theta
. These decision problems can be correctly solved with evolution time proportional to
1/(b-a)
with high probability using a few rounds of majority voting. This results in
an algorithm that is robust against noise and achieves the
1/\epsilon
total evolution time (i.e.,
T
) scaling.

In our setting, let
\hat{B}
be the fraction of experiments yielding
b=0
with
O_{c}
or
O_{s}
. Then
2\hat{B}-1
serves as an estimator of either
\cos(E_{\Delta}t)
or
\sin(E_{\Delta}t)
with bias at most
12\sqrt{2}\|H\|/\nu
by (
27
) and (
28
). The variance is at most
1/N_{b}
when
N_{b}
independent samples are used to compute the rate
\hat{B}
. Consequently one can estimate
\cos(E_{\Delta}t)
or
\sin(E_{\Delta}t)
up to error
12\sqrt{2}\|H\|/\nu+\sqrt{3/N_{b}}
with probability at least
2/3
as guaranteed by Chebyshev’s inequality. By Theorem
S1
, it suffices to have

12\sqrt{2}\|H\|/\nu+\sqrt{3/N_{b}}\leq 1/\sqrt{8},

for which we can choose
\nu=96\|H\|
and
N_{b}=96
. Importantly, these choices are independent of the precision
\epsilon
that we want to achieve.

Besides the expected error bound, we also want an error bound that holds with large probability. This can be obtained by taking the median of
N_{m}
independent samples of
\hat{\theta}
(i.e., the estimate for
\theta
, or in our setting
E_{\Delta}
, obtained from one run of the robust frequency estimation algorithm), which through Hoeffding’s inequality ensures the median is within
3\epsilon
distance from the actual value
E_{\Delta}
with probability at least
1-e^{-cN_{m}}
for an absolute constant
c
. Therefore a
1-\delta
success probability can be guaranteed with
\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\delta))
overhead.

We regard

as a function of
\bm{\lambda},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta}
, and write it as
E_{\Delta}(\bm{\lambda},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})
.
Because
\bm{\lambda}
has been used to represent the actual coefficients of the unknown Hamiltonian, we introduce a free variable
\bm{\mu}
to replace

E_{\Delta}(\bm{\mu},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})

All energy gaps computed from
\bm{\mu},\nu
for all
k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta}
(which means all
n\times 3^{n}\times 2^{n}
combinations) are collected into an array

\mathbf{E}(\bm{\mu},\nu)=(E_{\Delta}(\bm{\mu},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta}))_{k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta}}.

(32)

6^{n}n
:

III.1
The Jacobian matrix

We will first study the Jacobian matrix of
\mathbf{E}(\bm{\mu},\nu)
as a function of
\bm{\mu}
. We will show that the Jacobian matrix

J(\bm{\mu},\nu)=\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}(\bm{\mu},\nu)}{\partial\bm{\mu}},

(33)

is well-conditioned. This requires us to first estimate the smallest singular value of
J(\bm{\mu},\nu)
.
J(\bm{\mu},\nu)
is of size
6^{n}\times(4^{n}-1)
, where the
-1
comes from the fact that the coefficient associated with
I
in
H
is set to be
0
(so that
H
is traceless).

Using the eigenvalue perturbation theory
Bhatia 1997
, we have

\frac{\partial E_{\Delta}(\bm{\mu},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})}{\partial\lambda_{\mathbf{a}}}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\braket{\Psi_{1}(\bm{\mu},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})|\sigma^{\mathbf{a}}|\Psi_{1}(\bm{\mu},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})}-\braket{\Psi_{0}(\bm{\mu},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})|\sigma^{\mathbf{a}}|\Psi_{0}(\bm{\mu},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})}\big),

(34)

where
\left|\Psi_{0}(\bm{\mu},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})\right\rangle
and
\left|\Psi_{1}(\bm{\mu},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})\right\rangle
are the ground state and the first excited state of
H_{\mathrm{tot}}(\bm{\mu},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})
respectively. Using Lemma
S1
again, we have

\frac{\partial E_{\Delta}(\bm{\mu},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})}{\partial\lambda_{\mathbf{a}}}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\braket{\Phi_{1}(k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})|\sigma^{\mathbf{a}}|\Phi_{1}(k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})}-\braket{\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})|\sigma^{\mathbf{a}}|\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})}\big)+\mathcal{O}(\|H\|/\nu).

(35)

Because both
\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle
and
\left|\Phi_{1}\right\rangle
are tensor products of single-qubit Pauli eigenstates, we can readily compute their Pauli expectation values: If
\mathbf{a}\sim\bm{\beta}
, then

\braket{\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})|\sigma^{\mathbf{a}}|\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})}=(-1)^{\mathbf{s}\cdot s(\mathbf{a})},\quad\braket{\Phi_{1}(k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})|\sigma^{\mathbf{a}}|\Phi_{1}(k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})}=(-1)^{s(a_{k})}(-1)^{\mathbf{s}\cdot s(\mathbf{a})}.

(36)

If
\mathbf{a}\not\sim\bm{\beta}
, then both expectation values are
0
. Here the symbol
\sim
is defined in (
12
) and the function
s(\cdot)
is defined in (
13
) and (
14
). From the above, we can see that

\frac{\partial E_{\Delta}(\bm{\mu},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})}{\partial\lambda_{\mathbf{a}}}=\begin{cases}-(-1)^{\mathbf{s}\cdot s(\mathbf{a})}+\mathcal{O}(\|H\|/\nu),&\text{ if }\mathbf{a}\sim\bm{\beta}\text{ and }s(a_{k})\neq 0,\\
0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(37)

Therefore we can compute the matrix
J(\bm{\mu},\nu)^{\top}J(\bm{\mu},\nu)
in the following way

\displaystyle(J(\bm{\mu},\nu)^{\top}J(\bm{\mu},\nu))_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta}}\frac{\partial E(\bm{\mu},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})}{\partial\lambda_{\mathbf{a}}}\frac{\partial E(\bm{\mu},\nu,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta})}{\partial\lambda_{\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}}

(38)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k:s(a_{k})=1\\
s(a_{k}^{\prime})=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\beta}:\bm{\beta}\sim\mathbf{a}\\
\bm{\beta}\sim\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mathbf{s}}(-1)^{\mathbf{s}\cdot(s(\mathbf{a})+s(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}))}+\mathcal{O}_{n}(1/\nu).

Because

\sum_{\mathbf{s}}(-1)^{\mathbf{s}\cdot(s(\mathbf{a})+s(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}))}=2^{n}\delta_{s(\mathbf{a}),s(\mathbf{a}^{\prime})},

(39)

we have

(J(\bm{\mu},\nu)^{\top}J(\bm{\mu},\nu))_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}=2^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k:s(a_{k})=1\\
s(a_{k}^{\prime})=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\beta}:\bm{\beta}\sim\mathbf{a}\\
\bm{\beta}\sim\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\delta_{s(\mathbf{a}),s(\mathbf{a}^{\prime})}+\mathcal{O}_{n}(1/\nu).

(40)

Note that
\bm{\beta}\sim\mathbf{a}
and
\bm{\beta}\sim\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
imply
\mathbf{a}\sim\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
. Given the additional requirement that
s(\mathbf{a})=s(\mathbf{a}^{\prime})
for the term to be non-zero, we can then see that it is non-zero only when
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
. Therefore

\displaystyle(J(\bm{\mu},\nu)^{\top}J(\bm{\mu},\nu))_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=2^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k:s(a_{k})=1\\
s(a_{k}^{\prime})=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\beta}:\bm{\beta}\sim\mathbf{a}\\
\bm{\beta}\sim\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\delta_{s(\mathbf{a}),s(\mathbf{a}^{\prime})}+\mathcal{O}_{n}(1/\nu)

(41)

\displaystyle=2^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k:s(a_{k})=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\beta}:\bm{\beta}\sim\mathbf{a}\end{subarray}}\delta_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}+\mathcal{O}_{n}(1/\nu)

\displaystyle=|\mathbf{a}|2^{n}3^{n-|\mathbf{a}|}\delta_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}+\mathcal{O}_{n}(1/\nu),

where
|\mathbf{a}|
is the number of non-zero elements of
\mathbf{a}
, which appears in the above equation due to the summation over
k
. The factor
3^{n-|\mathbf{a}|}
is the number of bases
\bm{\beta}
that are compatible with
\mathbf{a}
. From the above we can see that
J^{\top}J
is almost a diagonal matrix with eigenvalue at least
2^{n}
(
|\mathbf{a}|\geq 1
because we omit the coefficient corresponding to the identity). We therefore reach the following lemma:

Lemma S2
.

Let
J(\bm{\mu},\nu)
be the Jacobian matrix defined in (
33
). Then its smallest singular value is lower bounded by
2^{n/2}-\mathcal{O}_{n}(1/\nu)
.

The limit of
J(\bm{\mu},\nu)^{\top}J(\bm{\mu},\nu)
for
\nu\to\infty
will be useful later on. Therefore we define

J_{0}=\lim_{\nu\to\infty}J(\bm{\mu},\nu)^{\top}J(\bm{\mu},\nu)

(42)

From (
41
) we can see that

(J_{0})_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}=|\mathbf{a}|2^{n}3^{n-|\mathbf{a}|}\delta_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}.

(43)

III.2
The least-squares problem

We will perform phase estimation experiments to obtain estimates for energy gaps collected into an array
\hat{\mathbf{E}}=(\hat{E}_{\Delta,k,\mathbf{s},\bm{\beta}})
. With these estimates we will solve a least-squares problem to recover the Hamiltonian coefficients
\bm{\lambda}
.
The exact values of the energy gaps then form a vector
\mathbf{E}^{\star}=\mathbf{E}(\bm{\lambda},\nu)
.

A natural way to solve for
\bm{\lambda}
is to solve a least-squares problem by minimizing the loss function

L(\hat{\mathbf{E}},\bm{\mu},\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\|\hat{\mathbf{E}}-\mathbf{E}(\bm{\mu},\nu)\|^{2}.

(44)

The first-order optimality condition yields the equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\mu}}L(\hat{\mathbf{E}},\bm{\mu},\nu)=(\mathbf{E}(\bm{\mu},\nu)-\hat{\mathbf{E}})^{\top}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\mu}}\mathbf{E}(\bm{\mu},\nu)=0.

(45)

By solving this equation around the exact value
\bm{\lambda}
we will be able to obtain an estimate
g(\hat{\mathbf{E}})
for the coefficients
\bm{\mu}
. We will first show that in a neighborhood of
\mathbf{E}^{\star}
, the equation (
45
) leads to a unique
C^{\infty}
map
g
.

Lemma S3
.

There exists
\nu_{0}=\mathcal{O}_{n}(1)
and
r=\Omega_{n}(1)
such that when
\nu\geq\nu_{0}
, there exists a
C^{\infty}
map
g:B_{r}(\mathbf{E}^{\star})\to\mathbb{R}^{4^{n}-1}
such that

\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\mu}}L(\hat{\mathbf{E}},g(\hat{\mathbf{E}}),\nu)=0.

Moreover, its Jacobian matrix is

\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{E}}}g(\hat{\mathbf{E}})=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial\bm{\mu}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}}{\partial\bm{\mu}}.

Proof.

We will use Theorem
S3
which is a modified implicit map theorem, to show the existence and uniqueness of such a function
g
. In the context of the equation (
45
) we want to solve, the various objects in Theorem
S3
are:

x=\hat{\mathbf{E}},\quad y=\bm{\mu},\quad f(x,y)=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\bm{\mu}}(\hat{\mathbf{E}},\bm{\mu}).

In the theorem we focus on a neighborhood of
(a,b)
where
f(a,b)=0
. Here
a=\mathbf{E}^{*}
and
b=\bm{\lambda}
, and
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\bm{\mu}}(\mathbf{E}^{\star},\bm{\lambda})=0
because
\mathbf{E}^{\star}=\mathbf{E}(\bm{\lambda})
and (
45
).

A key requirement in Theorem
S3
is that the matrix

Eigenstate and eigenvalue perturbations

Lemma S4
.

Let
H_{0}
be any Hermitian matrix. let
\left|\Phi_{k}\right\rangle
be its
k
th lowest eigenstate corresponding to an eigenvalue
E_{k}
, which is separated from the rest of the spectrum by a gap
\Delta
. Assume a Hermitian matrix
V
satisfies
\|V\|\leq\Delta/3
, and we let
\left|\Psi_{k}\right\rangle
be the
k
th lowest eigenstate of
H_{0}+V
. Then there exists
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
such that

\left\|\left|\Psi_{k}\right\rangle-e^{i\theta}\left|\Phi_{k}\right\rangle\right\|\leq 3\|V\|/\Delta.

Proof.

This lemma is similar to Lemma 3.1 in
Bravyi et al. 2011
, but we provide a proof here for completeness.
We let
H(s)=H_{0}+sV
. Then
H(0)=H_{0}
and
H(1)=H_{0}+V
. By the stability fo eigenvalues which follows from Weyl’s inequality
Tao 2010
, the
k
th eigenvalue of
H(s)
is separated from the rest of the spectrum by a gap of at least
\Delta-2\|V\|\geq\Delta/3
for all
s\in[0,1]
. We can then uniquely define the
k
th eigenstate to be
\left|\Psi_{k}(s)\right\rangle
Then by the eigenstate perturbation theory
Bhatia 1997
, the phases of these states can be chosen in such a way that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\left|\Psi_{k}(s)\right\rangle=-(H(s)-E_{k}(s))^{+}V\left|\Psi_{k}(s)\right\rangle,

where
(H(s)-E_{k}(s))^{+}
denotes the pseudoinverse of
H(s)-E_{k}(s)
, which satisfies
\|(H(s)-E_{k}(s))^{+}\|\leq 3/\Delta
due to the presence of the energy gap. Therefore we have

\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\left|\Psi_{k}(s)\right\rangle\right\|\leq 3\|V\|/\Delta.

Since
\left|\Phi_{k}\right\rangle=e^{i\theta_{0}}\left|\Psi_{k}(0)\right\rangle
and
\left|\Psi_{k}\right\rangle=e^{i\theta_{1}}\left|\Psi_{k}(1)\right\rangle
, for some
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
, from the above we can see that

\left\|\left|\Psi_{k}\right\rangle-e^{i\theta}\left|\Phi_{k}\right\rangle\right\|=\left\|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\left|\Psi_{k}(s)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}s\right\|\leq 3\|V\|/\Delta,

with an appropriately chosen
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
.
∎

VII
The total evolution time lower bound

An algorithm that can learn a Hamiltonian as described in the original Theorem 1 can be used to solve a simpler learning task of discrimination between the following single-qubit Hamiltonians. Assume we are given either
Z
or
Z+\epsilon X
(note that in Theorem 2 in the main text we use a normalized version of
Z+\epsilon X
, but here we use the unnormalized version first and then extend the result to the normalized case). If there is a learning
algorithm with a total evolution time at most
T
that succeeds in the learning task with probability at least
q
,
then we can use the learning algorithm to successfully distinguish between
Z
and the normalized version of
Z+\epsilon X
with probability at least
q
. We derive a lower-bound on the total Hamiltonian evolution time needed for this simple discrimination task, which we find to be
\in\Omega(\frac{1}{4\nu\epsilon+3\epsilon^{2}})
in Theorem
S6
in the following text. This implies a lower-bound on
T
for the original learning task.

The proof is split into 4 subsections. In the first subsection, we find an upper-bound for the diamond distance between the action of the unitaries generated by the Hamiltonians
Z
and
Z+\epsilon X
. Next we use this to derive an upper bound for the total variation (TV) distance between the distribution over measurement outcomes under a single experiment. Then we upper bound the TV distance between distributions of measurement outcomes under many experiments which can be adaptively chosen based on previous outcomes. We leverage a learning tree representation to capture the adaptivity introduced. Finally, we utilize Le Cam’s two-point method to turn this upper bound into a lower bound for the total evolution time, as shown in in Theorem
S6
.

VII.1
Diamond distance

Lemma S7
.

We have the following bound for the distance in terms of the spectral norm between

\exp\big(-it(Z+\nu P+\epsilon X)\big)
and
\exp\big(-it(Z+\nu P)\big)
, where
\epsilon\geq 0
,
P
is a traceless Hermitian operator with eigenvalues
\pm 1
denoting the direction of the external magnetic field,
0\leq\nu\leq 1
denoting the strength of the magnetic field and
t\geq 0
denotes how long the Hamiltonian is applied:

\displaystyle\|\exp\big(-it(Z+\nu P+\epsilon X)\big)-\exp\big(-it(Z+\nu P)\big)\|\leq 2\epsilon(2\nu+\epsilon)t+(2\epsilon(2\nu+\epsilon)+\epsilon)\min(t,\frac{1}{1-\nu})

(69)

Proof.

We define
w(\epsilon)=\|Z+\nu P+\epsilon X\|
.

\displaystyle\|\exp\big(-it(Z+\nu P+\epsilon X)\big)-\exp\big(-it(Z+\nu P)\big)\|

\displaystyle=\Bigl\|(\cos(w(\epsilon)t)-\cos(w(0)t))\mathbb{I}-i\sin(w(\epsilon)t)\,\frac{Z+\nu P+\epsilon X}{w(\epsilon)}+i\sin(w(0)t)\,\frac{Z+\nu P}{w(0)}\Bigr\|

The last expression follows from the following identity for single-qubit operators:
\exp\!\big(-it\,\vec{a}\!\cdot\!\vec{\sigma}\big)=\cos(|\vec{a}|t)\mathbb{I}-i\sin(|\vec{a}|t)\,\frac{\vec{a}}{|\vec{a}|}\!\cdot\!\vec{\sigma}
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\|\exp\big(-it(Z+\nu P+\epsilon X)\big)-\exp\big(-it(Z+\nu P)\big)\|

\displaystyle\leq\bigl|\cos(w(\epsilon)t)-\cos(w(0)t)\bigr|+\Bigl\|\sin(w(\epsilon)t)\,\frac{Z+\nu P+\epsilon X}{w(\epsilon)}-\sin(w(0)t)\,\frac{Z+\nu P}{w(0)}\Bigr\|

\displaystyle\leq\bigl|\cos(w(\epsilon)t)-\cos(w(0)t)\bigr|+\Bigl\|\big(\sin(w(\epsilon)t)\frac{w(0)}{w(\epsilon)}-\sin(w(0)t)\big)\frac{Z+\nu P}{w(0)}\Bigr\|+\frac{\epsilon}{w(\epsilon)}\|\sin(w(\epsilon)t)X\|

\displaystyle=\bigl|\cos(w(\epsilon)t)-\cos(w(0)t)\bigr|+\Bigl|\sin(w(\epsilon)t)\frac{w(0)}{w(\epsilon)}-\sin(w(0)t)\Bigr|+\frac{\epsilon}{w(\epsilon)}|\sin(w(\epsilon)t)|

The last line follows because
\|Z+\nu P\|=w(0).
Then,

\displaystyle\|\exp\big(-it(Z+\nu P+\epsilon X)\big)-\exp\big(-it(Z+\nu P)\big)\|

(70)

\displaystyle\leq\bigl|\cos(w(\epsilon)t)-\cos(w(0)t)\bigr|+\Bigl|\sin(w(\epsilon)t)-\sin(w(0)t)\Bigr|

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\Bigl|\frac{w(0)}{w(\epsilon)}-1\Bigr|\cdot|\sin(w(\epsilon)t)|+\frac{\epsilon}{w(\epsilon)}|\sin(w(\epsilon)t)|

\displaystyle\leq 2|w(\epsilon)-w(0)|t+\Bigl(\frac{|w(\epsilon)-w(0)|}{w(\epsilon)}+\frac{\epsilon}{w(\epsilon)}\Bigr)|\sin(w(\epsilon)t)|

(71)

The last inequality follows from
|\sin(b)-\sin(a)|\leq|b-a|
and
|\cos(b)-\cos(a)|\leq|b-a|
.

When
\nu<1
, we argue that
w(\epsilon)\geq 1-\nu
using the triangle inequality.

\displaystyle w(\epsilon)=\|Z+\nu P+\epsilon X\|\geq\|Z+\epsilon X\|-\|\nu P\|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+\epsilon^{2}}-\nu\geq 1-\nu.

In Section
VII.5
, we will show that
|w(\epsilon)-w(0)|\leq\epsilon(2\nu+\epsilon)
when
\nu<1
. We insert this in Eq. (
71
).

\displaystyle\leq 2|w(\epsilon)-w(0)|t+(\frac{|w(\epsilon)-w(0)|}{w(\epsilon)}+\frac{\epsilon}{w(\epsilon)})|\sin(w(\epsilon)t)|

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon(2\nu+\epsilon)t+(\epsilon(2\nu+\epsilon)+\epsilon)\min(t,\frac{1}{w(\epsilon)})

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon(2\nu+\epsilon)t+\epsilon(2\nu+\epsilon+1)\min(t,\frac{1}{1-\nu}).

The second inequality follows because
|\sin(w(\epsilon)t)|\leq 1
, and

\frac{|\sin(w(\epsilon)t)|}{w(\epsilon)}\leq\frac{w(\epsilon)t}{w(\epsilon)}=t
.

∎

VII.2
TV upper bound for a single experiment

A single experiment is defined the following way. Given an unknown Hamiltonian
H
equal to either
Z
or
Z+\epsilon X

E
specified by the following parameters,

1.

an arbitrary
N
-qubit initial state
\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{N}}
with an integer
N\geq 1
,

2.

an
N
-qubit unitary of the following form,

U_{L+1}(\exp\big(-it_{L}(H+\nu_{L}P_{L})\big)\otimes I\bigr)U_{L}\cdots U_{3}\bigl(\exp\big(-it_{2}(H+\nu_{2}P_{2})\big)\otimes I\bigr)U_{2}(\exp\big(-it_{1}(H+\nu_{1}P_{1})\big)\otimes I\bigr)U_{1},

where
P_{i}
and
0\leq\nu_{i}\leq\nu
are arbitrary single-qubit traceless hermitian operators with eigenvalues
\pm 1
representing direction of the external magnetic field and the strength of the field respectively,

3.

an arbitrary POVM
\mathcal{F}=\{M_{i}\}_{i}
on the
N
-qubit system,

for some arbitrary integer
L
which represents the number of times the Hamiltonian is evolved, arbitrary evolution times
t_{1},\dots,t_{L}
and arbitrary
N
-qubit unitaries
U_{1},\dots,U_{L},U_{L+1}
. Here
I
is the identity unitary on the
N-1
qubits. Here
L+1
can be interpreted as the number of discrete control operations involved in a single experiment.

Theorem S4
.

Consider the task of deciding whether a given single-qubit Hamiltonian
H
is
Z
or
Z+\epsilon X

where the experimentalist can apply a magnetic field of arbitrary strength at most
\nu<1
along a direction of choice denoted by a single-qubit Hermitian
P
with with eigenvalues
\pm 1
. A single experiment where the Hamiltonian is evolved
L
times with a total evolution time
T
has a total variation distance bounded by

TV(p_{\epsilon},p_{0})\leq(2\nu\epsilon+\epsilon^{2})T+\frac{\epsilon(2\nu+\epsilon+1)}{2(1-\nu)}L.

Proof.

We define
\left|\psi_{\epsilon}\right\rangle=U_{L}U_{\epsilon}(t_{L})\cdots U_{3}U_{\epsilon}(t_{2})U_{2}U_{\epsilon}(t_{1})U_{1}\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle
, where
U_{\epsilon}(t_{l})=\exp\big(-it_{l}(H+\nu_{l}P_{l})\big)\otimes I,\forall 1\leq l\leq L
.
By triangle inequality and telescoping sum, we have the following upper bound on the trace distance,

\|\left|\psi_{\epsilon}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi_{\epsilon}\right|-\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi_{0}\right|\|_{1}\leq\biggl\|\prod_{i}U_{i}(\exp\bigl(-it_{i}(Z+\nu_{i}P_{i}+\epsilon X)\bigr)\otimes I)-\prod_{i}U_{i}(\exp\bigl(-it_{i}(Z+\nu_{i}P_{i})\bigr)\otimes I)\biggr\|.

(72)

We can now upper bound the total variation distance
for the classical probability distribution when we measure the final state using the ideal POVM measurement

\mathcal{F}=\{M_{i}\}_{i}
. By definition, we have
p_{\epsilon}(i)=\left\langle\psi_{\epsilon}\right|M_{i}\left|\psi_{\epsilon}\right\rangle
.

\displaystyle TV(p_{\epsilon},p_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}|\left\langle\psi_{\epsilon}\right|M_{i}\left|\psi_{\epsilon}\right\rangle-\left\langle\psi_{0}\right|M_{i}\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle|

(73)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\left|\psi_{\epsilon}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi_{\epsilon}\right|-\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi_{0}\right|\|_{1}

(74)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2\cdot\biggl\|\prod_{i}U_{i}(\exp\bigl(-it_{i}(Z+\nu_{i}P_{i}+\epsilon X)\bigr)\otimes I)-\prod_{i}U_{i}(\exp\bigl(-it_{i}(Z+\nu_{i}P_{i})\bigr)\otimes I)\biggr\|.

(75)

The last inequality follows because
\|V\left|a\right\rangle\left\langle a\right|V^{\dagger}-|W\left|a\right\rangle\left\langle a\right|W^{\dagger}\|\leq 2\|V-W\|
, where
\left|a\right\rangle
is an arbitrary pure state and
V,W
are arbitrary unitaries.
By triangle inequality and telescoping sum, we show that

\displaystyle\biggl\|\prod_{i}U_{i}(\exp\bigl(-it_{i}(Z+\nu_{i}P_{i}+\epsilon X)\bigr)\otimes I)-\prod_{i}U_{i}(\exp\bigl(-it_{i}(Z+\nu_{i}P_{i})\bigr)\otimes I)\biggr\|

(76)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{L}\|\exp\bigl(-it_{i}(Z+\nu_{i}P_{i}+\epsilon X)\bigr)-\exp\bigl(-it_{i}(Z+\nu_{i}P_{i})\bigr)\|

(77)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{L}2\epsilon(2\nu_{i}+\epsilon)t_{i}+\frac{\epsilon(2\nu_{i}+\epsilon+1)}{1-\nu_{i}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{L}2\epsilon(2\nu+\epsilon)t_{i}+\frac{\epsilon(2\nu+\epsilon+1)}{1-\nu}

(78)

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon(2\nu+\epsilon)T+\frac{\epsilon(2\nu+\epsilon+1)}{1-\nu}L.

(79)

So, we have

\displaystyle TV(p_{\epsilon},p_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}|\left\langle\psi_{\epsilon}\right|M_{i}\left|\psi_{\epsilon}\right\rangle-\left\langle\psi_{0}\right|M_{i}\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle|

(80)

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon(2\nu+\epsilon)\,T+\frac{\epsilon(2\nu+\epsilon+1)}{1-\nu}\,L.

(81)

Hence,

\mathrm{TV}(p_{\epsilon},p_{0})\leq 2\epsilon(2\nu+\epsilon)T+\frac{\epsilon(2\nu+\epsilon+1)}{1-\nu}L,

which is the total variation distance between the measurement outcome distribution over the
two Hamiltonians under a single experiment.
∎

TV upper bound for many experiments

We consider the rooted tree representation

described in

Huang et al. 2022

u

E_{u}

1.

an arbitrary
N_{u}

\left|\psi_{u,0}\right\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{N_{u}}}

\mathcal{F}_{u}=\{M_{u,i}\}_{i=1}^{Q_{u}}

3.

an
N_{u}

\displaystyle U_{u,L_{u}+1}\bigl(\exp(-it_{u,L_{u}}(H+\nu_{u,L_{u}}P_{u,L_{u}}))\otimes I\bigr)U_{u,L_{u}}\cdots U_{u,3}

(82)

\displaystyle\bigl(\exp(-it_{u,2}(H+\nu_{u,2}P_{u,2}))\otimes I\bigr)U_{u,2}\bigl(\exp(-it_{u,1}(H+\nu_{u,1}P_{u,1}))\otimes I\bigr)U_{u,1},

(83)

for some arbitrary integer

which represents the number of times the Hamiltonian is evolved, arbitrary evolution times

For each node

•

\mathcal{T}_{u}
is the subtree with root

•

p^{(u)}_{\epsilon/0}

or

by considering the probability of ending

under the unknown Hamiltonian

or
Z
. When we start at the root node, we call the distribution

•

t_{(u)}\triangleq t(E_{u})\geq 0
is the evolution time for the single experiment

.
L_{u}
is the number of times the Hamiltonian is applied in this experiment.

•

\mathcal{T}_{u}
to a leaf node of

t(\mathcal{T}_{u})=\max_{P:\,\text{path on }\mathcal{T}_{u}}\;\sum_{w\in P}t_{w}.

Similarly,

\mathcal{T}_{u}
to a leaf node of

Because the total evolution time of the learning algorithm is upper bounded by

The algorithm begins from the root of
\mathcal{T}
. We can establish the total variation upper bound stated in Theorem

. First we build on the tree formalism and state an useful lemma.

Lemma S8
.

Consider a rooted tree for an adaptive learning algorithm. We use it for distinguishing the Hamiltonians
Z+\nu P
and
Z+\nu P+\epsilon X
,
\epsilon\geq 0
. Every node
u
in this tree corresponds to a single pre-defined experiment. Then

\displaystyle TV(p_{\epsilon}^{(\mathcal{T})},p_{0}^{(\mathcal{T})})\leq\max_{P:\,\text{path on }\mathcal{T}}\sum_{u\in P}TV(p_{\epsilon}^{(u)},p_{0}^{(u)}).

(86)

Proof.

The following inequality was shown in
Huang et al. 2023
:

\displaystyle 1-TV(p_{\epsilon}^{(\mathcal{T}_{u})},p_{0}^{(\mathcal{T}_{u})})\geq\Bigl(1-TV(p_{\epsilon}^{(u)},p_{0}^{(u)})\Bigr)\min_{w\in\text{child}(u)}\Bigl(1-TV(p_{\epsilon}^{(\mathcal{T}_{w})},p_{0}^{(\mathcal{T}_{w})})\Bigr).

(87)

By simple induction this would imply

\displaystyle 1-TV(p_{\epsilon}^{(\mathcal{T})},p_{0}^{(\mathcal{T})})\geq\min_{P:\,\text{path on }\mathcal{T}}\prod_{u\in P}\Bigl(1-TV(p_{\epsilon}^{({u})},p_{0}^{({u})})\Bigr).

(88)

Now

\displaystyle\min_{P:\,\text{path on }\mathcal{T}}\prod_{u\in P}\big(1-TV(p_{\epsilon}^{({u})},p_{0}^{({u})})\big)\geq\min_{P:\,\text{path on }\mathcal{T}}\big(1-\sum_{u\in P}TV(p_{\epsilon}^{({u})},p_{0}^{({u})})\big).

(89)

Combining these and simplifying we have

\displaystyle TV(p_{\epsilon}^{(\mathcal{T})},p_{0}^{(\mathcal{T})})\leq\max_{P:\,\text{path on }\mathcal{T}}\sum_{u\in P}TV(p_{\epsilon}^{(u)},p_{0}^{(u)}).

(90)

∎

Theorem S5
.

Consider the task of deciding whether a given single-qubit Hamiltonian is
Z
or

,

Using Lemma

\displaystyle TV(p_{\epsilon}^{(\mathcal{T})},p_{0}^{(\mathcal{T})})\leq\max_{P:\,\text{path on }\mathcal{T}}\sum_{u\in P}TV(p_{\epsilon}^{(u)},p_{0}^{(u)})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{P:\,\text{path on }\mathcal{T}}\sum_{u}\epsilon(2\nu+\epsilon)t_{(u)}+\frac{\epsilon(2\nu+\epsilon+1)}{2(1-\nu)}L_{(u)}

VII.4
Proof of the lower bound

We finally have all the tools necessary to prove the following theorem, which was informally described in the beginning of this section.

Theorem S6
.

Consider the task of deciding whether a given single-qubit Hamiltonian is
Z
or
\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\sigma}
(
\hat{n}=(\epsilon,0,1)/\sqrt{1+\epsilon^{2}}
,
\vec{\sigma}=(X,Y,Z)
) where the experimentalist can apply a magnetic field of arbitrary strength at most
\nu<1
along a direction of choice denoted by a single-qubit Hermitian
P
with eigenvalues
\pm 1
, and a fixed budget of
L
applications of the Hamiltonian i.e,
L+1,L\in o(\frac{1}{\epsilon})
discrete control operations. Then accomplishing this task with some constant probability of success at least
q
requires evolving the Hamiltonian for a total expected time of at least
T\in\Omega(\frac{1}{4\nu\epsilon+3\epsilon^{2}})
.

We will use the TV distance upper-bound we derived in the last section. However, note that while we wish to bound the expected time, Eq. (
94
) addresses the worst-case total time. We remedy this by showing that if the algorithm described in the theorem exists, a bounded-time version should exist as well. We state and prove the following lemma:

Lemma S9
.

(bounded time algorithm) Assume there exists an adaptive Hamiltonian learning algorithm that learns the Hamiltonian with
\epsilon
-accuracy with probability
q
with expected Hamiltonian evolution time at most
T_{0}
and expected number of times the Hamiltonian is evolved at most
L_{0}
(and therefore
L_{0}+1
discrete control operations). Consider a modified algorithm that terminates when evolution time exceeds
kT_{0}
(
k>\frac{2}{q}
) or when the number of times the Hamiltonian is applied exceeds
kL_{0}
, and therefore it has a maximum evolution time of
kT_{0}
and maximum number of Hamiltonian evolutions
kL_{0}
(i.e, therefore
kL_{0}+1
discrete control operations). Then this algorithm succeeds to
\epsilon
-accuracy with probability at least
q^{\prime}=q-\frac{2}{k}
.

Proof.

Note that the probability of success of this bounded time algorithm is equal to the joint probability of following a path with total evolution time less than
kT_{0}
as well as succeeding. Since the expected total evolution time is at most
T_{0}
, the probability associated with paths with evolution time
T_{\text{path}}>kT_{0}
must be less than
\frac{1}{k}
by Markov’s inequality. A similar argument implies that the probability associated with paths with number of Hamiltonian evolutions
L_{\text{path}}>kL_{0}
must be less than
\frac{1}{k}
. Then the probability of successful paths in the modified algorithm must be at least
q-\frac{2}{k}
.
∎

With the above tools we are ready to prove Theorem
S6
.

Proof.

(for Theorem
S6
) Assume there is an algorithm that can distinguish the two probability
distributions
p_{\epsilon}^{(\mathcal{T})},p_{0}^{(\mathcal{T})}
with probability at least
q
with expected total evolution time at most
T_{0}
and expected number of Hamiltonian applications at most
L_{0}
. Then, according to Lemma
S9
, there exists a bounded time algorithm with worst-case total evolution time
kT_{0}
and
kL_{0}
worst-case number of Hamiltonian applications with probability of success at least
q^{\prime}=q-\frac{2}{k}
(
k>2/q
). Le Cam’s two-point method
Yu 1997
implies

\displaystyle TV(p_{\epsilon}^{(\mathcal{T})},p_{0}^{(\mathcal{T})})\geq 2q^{\prime}-1=2(q-\frac{2}{k})-1.

(95)

Note the decision tree
\mathcal{T}
corresponds to the modified bounded time algorithm. Now the upper-bound in Theorem
