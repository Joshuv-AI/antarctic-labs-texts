Position: General Alignment Has Hit a Ceiling; Edge Alignment Must Be Taken Seriously
Han Bao, Yue Huang, Xiaoda Wang, Zheyuan Zhang, Yujun Zhou, Carl Yang, Xiangliang Zhang, Yanfang Ye

Abstract
Large language models are being deployed in complex socio-technical systems, which exposes limits in current alignment practice. We take the position that the dominant paradigm of General Alignment , which compresses diverse human values into a single scalar reward, reaches a structural ceiling in settings with conflicting values, plural stakeholders, and irreducible uncertainty. These failures follow from the mathematics and incentives of scalarization and lead to structural value flattening, normative representation loss, and cognitive uncertainty blindness. We introduce Edge Alignment as a distinct approach in which systems preserve multi dimensional value structure, support plural and democratic representation, and incorporate epistemic mechanisms for interaction and clarification. To make this approach practical, we propose seven interdependent pillars organized into three phases. We identify key challenges in data collection, training objectives, and evaluation, outlining complementary technical and governance directions. Taken together, these measures reframe alignment as a lifecycle problem of dynamic normative governance rather than as a single instance optimization task.

Introduction

The rapid integration of large language models (LLMs) into high-stakes socio-technical systems has exposed a growing gap between current training practices and the complex normative demands of real-world applications
(
50
)
.
While researchers have made significant progress in creating generally safe models
(
62
;
36
;
102
)
, these achievements remain limited to some scenarios. Understanding these limitations requires examining the evolutionary path of alignment research.

Alignment has evolved through three progressively stronger levels of constraint. Level 0,
functional alignment
, focuses on syntactic correctness and basic instruction following through supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
(
62
)
. At this stage, models learn to generate coherent and well-formed outputs, but remain normatively neutral. A functionally aligned model can produce a helpful tutorial or a deceptive message with equal fluency, lacking any capacity to reason about intent or consequence. Level 1, which we refer to as
General Alignment
, represents the dominant paradigm today. Implemented through reinforcement learning from human feedback, this approach aims to instill broad principles of helpfulness, honesty, and harmlessness
(
5
)
. It rests on the scalar reward hypothesis, which assumes that diverse human values can be aggregated into a single numerical objective
(
74
)
. In unambiguous cases, this assumption is often sufficient.

However, the scalar approach encounters fundamental limitations at decision boundaries where values conflict. For example, when a cybersecurity expert requests code for analyzing software vulnerabilities, the model faces competing priorities. Helpfulness suggests providing the information, while safety concerns suggest refusal. Without the ability to weigh these competing values, scalar optimization often leads to inflexible responses, either rejecting legitimate professional requests or inappropriately complying to maximize perceived helpfulness
(
101
;
38
)
.

In this paper, we argue that
General Alignment has hit a ceiling, and Edge Alignment must be taken seriously.
Beyond basic functionality and general principles lies a new domain we call edge alignment, which addresses alignment under value conflicts, pluralistic perspectives, and uncertainty
(
78
)
.
Edge Alignment
is not defined by specific algorithms, but by a fundamental shift in alignment goals. Rather than optimizing for a single measure of goodness, aligned systems must navigate competing norms, explain their reasoning, and adapt as uncertainty resolves through interaction
(
82
)
.

This shift raises three fundamental questions that current alignment paradigms struggle to answer.
How can models represent and reason over inherently multi-dimensional value tensions without collapsing them into a single scalar objective

(
84
)
?
How can alignment remain legitimate when aggregating preferences risks erasing minority or context-specific perspectives

(
64
)
? And
how can models act responsibly in situations where the correct response is not a single answer, but a process of clarification, deliberation, or negotiation

(
98
)
?

To address these questions, we synthesize and organize seven interconnected approaches spanning multi-objective optimization
(
32
)
, hierarchical constraints
(
18
)
, pluralistic alignment
(
78
)
, and interactive negotiation
(
90
)
. By reframing alignment as a problem of dynamic normative governance rather than static optimization, this paper outlines foundational directions for building AI systems that remain aligned precisely when alignment is hardest.

2
The Evolution of Alignment

The trajectory of AI alignment research reflects a progressive escalation of constraint complexity, moving from superficial mimicry to normative governance. As illustrated in
Figure 1
, we categorize this evolution into three distinct levels. We argue that the current industry standard (Level 1) has reached a theoretical ceiling due to its reliance on reductive mathematical assumptions, necessitating a transition to the
Edge Alignment
paradigm (Level 2).

2.1
Level 0: Functional Alignment

Functional Alignment
refers to the alignment of model behavior with the syntactic and procedural expectations of human instructions, ensuring task adherence without normative interpretation of intent.

The foundational layer addresses the challenge of basic instruction following. Technically implemented via SFT, the objective is to maximize the likelihood of target tokens within a curated demonstration dataset:

\theta^{*}=\arg\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\sum_{t}\log P_{\theta}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})].

At this stage, the model masters the
form
of human interaction, including grammar, coherence, and format compliance. However, Level 0 is fundamentally
value-neutral
. A functionally aligned model lacks the normative compass to distinguish between benign and malicious intent, as it is equally proficient at drafting a helpful tutorial or a deceptive phishing email
(
12
;
62
)
.

2.2
Level 1: General Alignment

General Alignment

seeks to align model behavior with broadly shared human values by using preference learning to aggregate a singular consensus response.

To rectify the value blindness of Level 0,
General Alignment
introduces human preferences via RLHF or DPO
(
68
)
. This paradigm rests on the
Scalar Reward Hypothesis

(
74
)
, assuming that complex human values can be compressed into a single real-valued signal
R\in\mathbb{R}
. Most implementations derive this reward from pairwise comparisons
(
16
)
using the Bradley-Terry model
(
11
)
.
While effective for resolving low-conflict queries, this approach hits a structural ceiling in complex scenarios. Mathematically, the linear scalarization of disparate rewards exhibits an
Archimedean compensatory property
, where a high score in one dimension can numerically mask a critical violation in another. Sociologically, this leads to
Mode Collapse

(
13
)
, where the model gravitates toward a sanitized consensus that erases minority viewpoints
(
78
)
.

2.3
Level 2:
Edge Alignment

As LLMs transition from isolated chatbots to autonomous agents in high-stakes environments, they increasingly encounter the “edge”—decision boundaries where universal principles contradict each other, intents become ambiguous, or pluralistic values collide. Level 2 represents a fundamental shift from
static optimization
to
dynamic boundary arbitration
.

Edge Alignment

is the capacity to operate at decision boundaries where intents, constraints, and plural values conflict, by dynamically reasoning about trade-offs, quantifying epistemic uncertainty, and negotiating resolutions through interaction.

Unlike its predecessors,
Edge Alignment
operates across three interdependent dimensions that address the fundamental limits of scalar optimization:

•

Structural:
Restoring the multi-dimensional nature of human values to overcome
Value Flattening
(
§ 3.1
).

•

Normative:
Protecting representation and legitimacy to mitigate the
Loss of Representation
(
§ 3.2
).

•

Cognitive:
Enabling epistemic agency to resolve
Uncertainty Blindness
through interaction (
§ 3.3
).

In the following sections, we formalize the failures of the Level 1 paradigm and detail the seven pillars of
Edge Alignment
.

3
Why General Alignment Fails at the Edge

General Alignment
has achieved strong performance by optimizing for average-case behavior, most commonly through RLHF and expected-utility objectives
(
62
;
74
)
. However, this paradigm reaches both theoretical and practical limits in edge cases. Scalarization provably loses representational capacity in multi-objective settings and fails to capture non-compensatory value hierarchies
(
84
)
. At the same time, empirical and normative analyses show that preference aggregation obscures plural values and amplifies sociotechnical bias
(
78
;
88
)
. These limitations are structural rather than data-driven, arising from representational and epistemic assumptions that prevent models from recognizing conflict or expressing calibrated uncertainty
(
98
)
. These failure modes and their corresponding remedies are summarized in
Figure 2
.

3.1
Structural Limit: Value Flattening

General Alignment
formulates value reconciliation as a single-objective optimization problem. By collapsing vector-valued objectives into a scalar reward
R=\sum_{i}w_{i}r_{i}
, prevailing methods implicitly assume that objectives are linear and mutually compensatory
(
3
)
. We argue that this
scalar fallacy
introduces a structural ceiling, causing
dimensional collapse
and the loss of
hierarchical integrity
in high-stakes settings
(
32
)
.

Figure 2
:

Geometric Limits of Linear Scalarization.
Linear scalarization cannot recover policies located in non-convex regions of the Pareto frontier. Formally, scalar maximization is restricted to solutions on the convex hull of the achievable set
(
84
)
. For any optimal trade-off
\pi^{*}
in a concave segment, no weight vector
\mathbf{w}
yields
\pi^{*}
as a scalar optimum. As a result, models favor extreme endpoints over nuanced compromise policies
(
100
)
. Edge Alignment addresses this limitation through
Multi-Objective Alignment
, employing Pareto-aware methods (e.g., PAMA) that recover Pareto-stationary points without collapsing the value vector
(
32
)
.

Hierarchical Collapse and Lexicographic Priority.
Scalar rewards further fail to capture the categorical structure of human ethics. Treating safety and utility as fungible terms permits
hierarchical collapse
, where sufficiently high helpfulness offsets severe safety violations
(
38
)
. In contrast, many normative systems impose lexicographic priorities (e.g.,
\text{Safety}\succ_{\text{lex}}\text{Helpfulness}
), where safety functions as a hard constraint rather than a trade-off variable. Edge Alignment preserves this structure via
Priority & Hierarchical Alignment
, using constrained optimization frameworks such as Safe RLHF, which enforce risk costs
C\leq\beta
through Lagrangian primal–dual methods
(
18
)
.

Case Study: Security Code Generation Dilemma

Empirical studies show that approximately
40% of code generated by GitHub Copilot contains exploitable security vulnerabilities

(
63
)
. This is a structural failure: scalar reward models, which optimize a weighted sum of helpfulness and harmlessness, cannot represent the non-convex trade-offs between utility and safety. As linear scalarization cannot reach concave regions of the Pareto frontier, models oscillate between pathological extremes such as
over-compliance
and
contextual blindness
(e.g., generating SQL injections)
(
63
;
84
)
. See
§ A.1
.

Implication.
The ceiling of
General Alignment
is its mathematical inability to represent non fungible values.
Edge Alignment
provides the necessary transition to
Vector Valued Optimization
and
Hard Lexicographic Constraints
, ensuring that models can navigate complex trade offs while maintaining normative integrity.

3.2
Normative Limit: Loss of Representation

General Alignment
assumes heterogeneous human preferences
u\in U
can be aggregated into a universal reward model
R_{\text{avg}}

(
78
)
by minimizing expected population loss:

\theta^{*}=\arg\min_{\theta}\sum_{u\in U}\mathcal{L}(y_{\theta},y_{u}).

We argue that this induces a
representation limit
, where the pursuit of consensus systematically erases minority viewpoints
(
64
)
.

Statistical Erasure and Mode Collapse.
This failure follows directly from Jensen’s inequality,
f(\mathbb{E}[X])\leq\mathbb{E}[f(X)]
, which explains why aggregate loss minimization fails in multi-modal value spaces
(
53
)
. Optimization over an aggregated preference distribution drives models toward the geometric mean of human values
(
78
)
, producing
distributional erasure
—a form of mode collapse in normative space
(
13
;
96
)
. The resulting behavior reflects no actual user population and disproportionately marginalizes preferences located in distribution tails
(
45
)
.

This effect is amplified by political economy. Because large-scale alignment requires concentrated compute and institutional capacity, dominant actors disproportionately shape the value distributions being optimized. When deployed broadly, such models normalize these perspectives, reinforcing epistemic and power asymmetries
(
64
;
88
)
.

Edge Alignment
mitigates this failure through
Pluralistic Alignment
, including steerable, distributional, and Overton-style mechanisms that preserve multiple normative modes rather than collapsing them into a single optimum
(
1
)
.

Context and Democratic Legitimacy.
Beyond statistical bias, General Alignment exhibits
contextual rigidity
: static reward models treat contextual variation as noise, ignoring that acceptability depends on domain and deployment setting
(
64
)
. Edge Alignment incorporates contextual and personalized policies (e.g., MiCRo
(
72
)
) and grounds legitimacy through collective and democratic alignment frameworks such as Collective Constitutional AI
(
35
)
, enabling objectives to reflect plural public will via generative social choice
(
23
)
.

Case Study: ChatGPT’s Cultural Profile

Studies show that ChatGPT exhibits a
hybrid cultural profile
that matches no real human population, combining inconsistent traits such as partial collectivism with low power distance
(
95
)
. Optimizing toward statistical consensus induces
mode collapse
: across users, the model systematically favors individual autonomy, marginalizing collectivist norms in scenarios like family–career conflicts. This follows from Jensen’s inequality: minimizing expected loss over aggregated preferences drives outputs toward the distribution mean, erasing minority values in the tails
(
78
)
. See
§ A.2
.

Implication.
The ceiling of General Alignment is its erasure of plurality. Taking Edge Alignment seriously requires a transition to models that represent the full distribution of human values and adapt to the specific context of every interaction.

3.3
Cognitive Limit: Uncertainty Blindness

General Alignment operates under the assumption that alignment is a one shot prediction problem. In this framework, given a context
x
, the model predicts an optimal response
y
by approximating the distribution
P(y|x)\approx P(y|x,I_{true},S_{true})
, where
I_{true}
and
S_{true}
represent the user’s true intent and the state of the world
(
98
)
. We argue that this formulation reaches a
Cognitive Limit
in edge cases where the prompt
x
is underspecified or ambiguous.

Intent Hallucination and Epistemic Blind Spots.
When the entropy of the intent distribution
H(I|x)
is high, General Alignment models are incentivized by RLHF to be assertive, often leading to
intent jump
or hallucination
(
22
)
. This creates epistemic blind spots where the model operates with a level of confidence not supported by the available information
(
98
)
. Edge Alignment addresses this through
Uncertainty and Risk Sensitive Alignment
. By employing frameworks such as Kuhn’s Semantic Uncertainty
(
46
)
or GrACE
(
98
)
, models can quantify task clarity and familiarity, distinguishing between epistemic uncertainty (lack of knowledge) and intrinsic risk (environmental stochasticity)
(
94
)
.

Temporal Conflict Arbitration.
Beyond mere quantification, Edge Alignment shifts the paradigm from static completion to dynamic
Interactive and Negotiable Alignment
. This process involves
conflict arbitration
, defined as the temporal negotiation of a safe path through high risk or ambiguous requests
(
94
)
. Instead of guessing, the model utilizes proactive reasoning to identify potential risks and initiates clarification dialogues. This allows the system to discover emergent user intents through collaborative interaction, ensuring that the model does not just follow instructions but participates in the ethical arbitration of human intent
(
47
)
.

$100 billion collapse
in Alphabet’s market capitalization in a single day. This incident underscores the model’s
uncertainty blindness
: an inability to recognize knowledge boundaries or data gaps. Instead of expressing epistemic caution or seeking clarification, the model is incentivized to fabricate plausible-sounding but false narratives when faced with ambiguity

The ceiling of General Alignment is its assertive blindness. Edge Alignment necessitates a transition toward

and

, ensuring that models can navigate the inherent uncertainty of human communication through proactive negotiation and risk awareness.

The Seven Pillars of
Edge Alignment

Edge Alignment
represents a paradigm shift from static optimization to dynamic normative governance. To overcome the structural limits of General Alignment, we propose a framework organized into three phases (
Figure 3
) that constitute a
stack of capabilities

Mathematical Structure
provides the
capacity
to represent conflicting values; (2)
Normative Governance
populates the model with pluralistic
content
; and (3)
Dynamic Cognition
grants the model the
agency
to arbitrate conflicts at inference time. For detailed descriptions and definitions, please refer to
Appendix B
.

4.1
Phase I: Reconstructing Structural Capacity

Reject the reductionist assumption that conflicting values can be linearly compressed, restoring the vector nature of human values

(
57
;
89
)
.

1. Multi-Objective Alignment (MOA)

Instead of a scalar reward, MOA treats alignment as a vector optimization problem
\vec{R}(x,y)=[r_{1},\dots,r_{n}]^{\top}

(
69
;
93
)
. This allows models to navigate non-convex regions of the Pareto frontier that are mathematically inaccessible to linear scalarization
(
52
;
103
)
. Recent advancements like the
PAMA
framework reduce computational complexity to
O(n)
, providing guarantees of convergence to Pareto stationary points for large-scale models
(
32
)
. Implementation archetypes such as
MODPO
,
MetaAligner
, and

)
.

2. Priority & Hierarchical Alignment

This pillar enforces lexicographic optimization for high-risk domains where safety acts as a non-negotiable ”defeater” over utility
(
37
;
38
)
. To manage model self-evaluation noise, frameworks like
SPA
utilize uncertainty-weighted loss functions to prioritize safety-first parameter updates
(
38
)
. Structural defenses are further reinforced through
Instruction Hierarchies

(

)
and
Constitutional AI

(

)
. During deployment,

and

allow for inference-time steering of safety thresholds without retraining
(
75
;

)
.

5
Challenges and Lifecycle Implications

Edge Alignment
problems are systemic: choices at data collection shape training objectives; training objectives determine what evaluation can detect; evaluation and deployment practices together create the incentives that systems follow in production. Below we summarize the core failure modes, state the concrete challenge and the immediate implication for the lifecycle (
Figure 4
), and give concise mitigation directions.

5.1
Data Collection as Normative Design

Challenge:
Existing alignment datasets systematically underrepresent conflicted, arbitration-style scenarios. While BeaverTails
(
41
)
contains strong preference conflicts between helpfulness and harmlessness, with over 50% of instances exhibiting explicit tension, most standard corpora are dominated by weakly differentiated comparisons where one response marginally dominates across objectives
(
53
)
. For instance, HH-RLHF analysis shows that only about 25% of samples exhibit a noticeable quality gap (score gap
>1.0
), and fewer than 0.5% show large differences (score gap
>5.0
)
(
67
)
. Consequently, datasets emphasize near-tie preferences rather than genuine value trade-offs.

Annotation protocols further exacerbate this bias by collapsing disagreement into a single preferred label. Low-agreement cases are often majority-voted or filtered out, rather than preserved as signals of legitimate normative ambiguity
(
73
)
. The resulting training distribution overrepresents consensus while erasing pluralistic structure.

Implication:
Models trained on such data lack internal representations for conflict recognition and justification, leading to brittle refusals or inappropriate compliance at deployment.

Mitigation:
Alignment datasets should explicitly surface and preserve value conflict through conflict-augmented cases with labels and justificatory traces, supported by synthetic multi-agent generation and targeted human validation
(
82
;
27
)
. More broadly, pluralistic data pipelines should incorporate demographically diverse participation and deliberative platforms such as Polis to ensure procedural legitimacy
(
45
;
4
)
. In high-risk domains, dataset construction should be treated as a governance decision with provenance metadata aligned to risk tiering frameworks
(
88
)
.

5.2
Training Objectives and Representational Limits

Challenge:
Scalar reward compression flattens multi-dimensional normative space, restricting optimization to the convex hull of the Pareto frontier
(
84
)
. Linear scalarization cannot recover solutions in concave regions, requiring explicit reasoning over Pareto sets:

\mathcal{P}^{*}=\{\pi\in\Pi\mid\nexists\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi:\pi^{\prime}\succ\pi\}.

Empirical studies confirm that scalarization misses substantial portions of the frontier
(
84
;
85
)
. Moreover, classical multi-objective methods scale poorly: MGDA incurs
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}d)
cost, which becomes prohibitive for billion-parameter models
(
32
)
.

Recent extensions such as MODPO adapt DPO to multi-objective settings via linear scalarization, achieving efficiency gains over MORLHF
(
103
)
. However, these approaches remain fundamentally limited to convex trade-offs.

Implication:
Scalar objectives yield models that cannot represent multiple legitimate resolutions or respect hard constraints. Convexity limitations induce systematic mode collapse and exclude Pareto-optimal policies requiring non-convex balancing.

Mitigation:
Preserve multi-objective structure through modular or inference-time optimization. PAMA reformulates MORLHF as a convex problem with closed-form updates, reducing complexity to
\mathcal{O}(n)
and enabling efficient fine-tuning at scale
(
32
)
. For categorical constraints, lexicographic methods remain necessary
(
55
;
38
)
, with frontier maintenance offloaded to offline services.

5.3
Evaluation and Certification

Challenge:
Existing benchmarks and leaderboards emphasize single-turn preference and surface-level user satisfaction, systematically penalizing clarification and deliberation. Evidence from MT-Bench shows that most models suffer substantial performance degradation in multi-turn settings compared to single-turn scenarios, a gap not explained by underlying capability differences
(
48
)
. Moreover, RLHF optimization can degrade multi-turn conversational competence while improving immediate response quality
(
87
)
. As a result, current evaluation protocols reward confident one-shot answers while failing to surface uncertainty or deliberative reasoning.

Implication:
Evaluations that ignore interactional process select for models that appear helpful in isolation but fail to recognize uncertainty or arbitrate normative conflict. Edge alignment, defined as the ability to identify and navigate tensions at the boundaries of acceptable behavior, remains largely untested. Recent studies indicate that LLM performance under normative conflict follows a fragile scaling law, where collapse probability grows as
\lambda\propto\exp(m/\sigma^{2})
, with
\sigma^{2}
capturing effective variance from sampling temperature and normative perturbations
(
71
)
. Certification based solely on static benchmarks therefore misses interactive failure modes that emerge only through sustained dialogue.

Mitigation:
Develop process-aware benchmarks that explicitly credit conflict recognition and principled arbitration. Evaluation should adopt multi-component metrics such as

Q(\pi)\approx\mathbb{E}\big[\,\alpha C+\beta A+\gamma O\,\big],

where
C
measures conflict recognition,
A
arbitration quality, and
O
outcome appropriateness
(
56
)
. For high-risk deployments, certification should further require staged field testing and longitudinal monitoring. We operationalize this process-aware evaluation through domain-conditioned edge stress tests (
Appendix C
), where domain-specific constraint structures determine both what constitutes an edge and what aligned arbitration entails.

5.4
Governance and Community Participation

Challenge:
Current governance frames alignment as a developer-centric engineering task or a pre-deployment compliance step. Affected communities are largely treated as passive data sources rather than participants with authority over how systems are trained, evaluated, and deployed
(
88
)
. This exclusion increases risks of misrepresentation and leaves limited avenues for redress when harms occur.

Implication:
Such exclusionary governance creates legitimacy and accountability gaps. It disproportionately encodes the normative preferences of
WEIRD
populations
(
34
;
6
)
, imposing them on broader publics and reinforcing power and epistemic asymmetries. From a Foucauldian perspective
(
26
)
, technical design functions as a mechanism of power that legitimizes particular forms of knowledge. Harms arising from these asymmetries cannot be resolved through technical fixes alone, leading contested deployments to erode trust and provoke regulatory or civic backlash.

Mitigation:
Shift from consultation to enforceable participation
(
19
)
. Establish compensated community steering bodies with formal authority over evaluation priorities and deployment decisions
(
76
;
64
)
; grant substantive controls in high-risk contexts (e.g., staged approval or local oversight); require transparency artifacts and independent audits with community representation; and provide clear redress mechanisms, including public incident reporting and timely remediation. These participatory rights should be embedded in contracts or regulation to make governance an enforceable component of responsible operation
(
35
;
23
;
88
)
.

6
Alternative Views

This paper argues that failures at the edge are structural and require a shift from scalar optimization to lifecycle-spanning governance. This position is not uncontested. We address two credible alternative views.

Edge Alignment
as Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning.
One counterargument holds that Edge Alignment is simply a restatement of MORL
(
83
;
70
)
. From this perspective, vector-valued rewards, Pareto optimization, and preference-conditioned policies already provide sufficient machinery for resolving value conflicts.

We acknowledge substantial technical overlap, particularly with methods such as PAMA
(
32
)
. However, this view conflates
optimization techniques
with an
alignment framework
. MORL specifies how to optimize given objectives, but it assumes those objectives and trade-off rules are fixed and legitimate. Edge Alignment addresses a prior and orthogonal question: how objectives are justified, contested, revised, and operationalized across contexts when no single resolution is normatively privileged. As detailed in
§ 4
, multi-objective learning is a necessary
instrument
within Edge Alignment, but not its organizing principle, which spans data design, evaluation, interaction, and governance.

Alignment Should Remain External to Models.
A second critique argues that alignment should be enforced exclusively through external mechanisms such as policies, oversight, and post hoc filtering, rather than embedded within models
(
88
;
33
)
. On this view, models should remain technically neutral while humans retain full normative authority.

We share concerns about over-empowering models and emphasize the central role of sociotechnical governance in
§ 5
. However, purely external alignment is insufficient for edge cases that emerge dynamically during interaction. Without internal capabilities for conflict recognition and uncertainty expression, external oversight cannot be reliably triggered at the moment of need.
Edge Alignment
therefore does not replace human governance; it enables it by surfacing normative tensions, supporting deliberation, and creating interfaces for accountable human decision-making.

7
Conclusion

The future of AI does not lie in discovering a single universal rule.
Such a rule is untenable mathematically, socially, and epistemically.
Edge Alignment accepts this reality by design. By restructuring objective representations, restoring normative governance, and enabling dynamic cognition, it offers a principled path toward responsible artificial intelligence. This shift is not merely technical, but essential for embedding human legal, ethical, and political judgment into machine intelligence.

B.1
Phase I: Reconstructing Objective Structure Beyond Scalar Rewards

Addressing Dimensional Collapse and the Alignment Tax.

The first phase rejects the reductionist assumption that conflicting values can be linearly compressed. It restores the vector nature of human values and the hierarchical structure of ethical constraints.

1. Multi-Objective Alignment

The evolution of alignment strategies has transitioned from a reductionist view of human values toward the sophisticated management of multi-dimensional objectives. Traditionally, alignment relied on RLHF to minimize a single scalar reward, which implicitly assumed that complex normative judgments could be flattened into a one-dimensional signal
(
57
)
. This scalar approach frequently results in an alignment tax
(
62
)
, a phenomenon where maximizing safety leads to the degradation of a model’s general reasoning or helpfulness because the objectives are treated as competing elements in a single sum
(
89
)
. Multi-Objective Alignment addresses this limitation by treating alignment as a vector optimization problem where the reward function is defined as
\vec{R}(x,y)=[r_{1},\dots,r_{n}]^{\top}

(
69
;
93
)
.

Definition:

Multi-Objective Alignment
is the capability to optimize a policy against a vector of independent reward signals, explicitly navigating trade-offs to approximate the Pareto frontier rather than a single static solution.

The initial attempt to resolve these conflicts involved linear scalarization
(
52
)
, where multiple rewards are combined via a weighted sum
R_{total}=\sum w_{i}R_{i}
. However, this technique is mathematically limited because it cannot reach optimal points on non-convex regions of the trade-off surface regardless of the weight distribution
(
103
)
. Consequently, the field shifted toward identifying the Pareto set
\mathcal{P}^{*}
. In this framework, a policy
\pi^{*}
is considered optimal if no other policy
\pi^{\prime}
can improve any single objective without harming another. Formally, a policy
\pi^{*}
is Pareto optimal if:

\nexists\pi^{\prime}\text{ s.t. }\forall i,J_{i}(\pi^{\prime})\geq J_{i}(\pi^{*})\text{ and }\exists k,J_{k}(\pi^{\prime})>J_{k}(\pi^{*})

where
J_{i}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[r_{i}(x,y)]
. This formulation ensures that the model operates at its maximum potential efficiency across all dimensions.

A significant barrier to implementing this optimization in large-scale models was the computational cost
(
9
;
52
)
, which traditionally grew at a rate of
O(n^{2}d)
relative to the number of parameters
d
. This challenge was recently addressed by the Pareto Multi-Objective Alignment (PAMA) framework, which converts the optimization into a convex problem with a closed-form solution. This advancement reduces the computational complexity to
O(n)
and provides a mathematical guarantee of convergence to a Pareto stationary point
(
32
)
.

Further refinement of the Pareto frontier is achieved through specialized techniques such as Multi-Objective Direct Preference Optimization (MODPO), which employs nonlinear Chebyshev scalarization to ensure that critical objectives like safety never drop below a mandatory threshold
(
103
)
. For applications requiring real-time flexibility, the MetaAligner architecture serves as an external correction module that refines model outputs according to user-specified preferences at the time of inference without the need for retraining
(
92
)
. Finally, to prevent parameter-level interference between conflicting goals, the Hierarchical Mixture-of-Experts (HoE) framework decouples these objectives by assigning them to specialized modules, allowing safety and helpfulness to be optimized independently through a dynamic routing network
(
55
)
.

2. Priority & Hierarchical Alignment

The progression of alignment techniques extends into the domain of structural constraints where certain objectives are treated as non-negotiable requirements rather than variables for trade-off. In high-risk applications such as medical or legal services, safety and ethical boundaries function as primary constraints that must be satisfied before any secondary goals, such as helpfulness, are optimized. This paradigm shift utilizes lexicographic optimization to establish a strict hierarchy of values. Under this ordering, a solution
y
is preferred over

if it performs better on the highest-priority objective, regardless of its performance on lower-level tasks. Formally, given an ordered set of functions
\{f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{k}\}

y\succ_{lex}y^{\prime}
is established if there exists an index
j
such that
f_{j}(y)>f_{j}(y^{\prime})
and for all
i<j
, the condition
f_{i}(y)=f_{i}(y^{\prime})
is met

3

3

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexicographic_optimization
.

Definition:

Priority & Hierarchical Alignment
establishes hierarchical value ordering, ensuring critical constraints are satisfied before optimizing secondary objectives

38

To handle the sensitivity of these boundaries and potential noise in model self-evaluation, SPA introduces an uncertainty-weighted loss function:

\mathcal{L}_{SPA}=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}[w(u)\cdot\log\sigma(\beta\Delta r)]

where
\Delta r=r(x,y_{w})-r(x,y_{l})
represents the reward difference, and

adjusts gradient influence based on model confidence in priority ranking. This mechanism ensures that the safety-first principle remains the dominant direction for parameter updates during reinforcement learning

Beyond objective functions, the alignment process incorporates structural defenses through the Instruction Hierarchy. By utilizing context distillation and training on adversarial samples, models learn to internalize these privilege levels and ignore attempts at prompt injection or instruction overriding

For dynamic control during deployment, the alignment hierarchy can be enforced through inference-time interventions.
