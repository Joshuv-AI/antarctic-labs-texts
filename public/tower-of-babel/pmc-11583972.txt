A pH-Sensitive cRGD-PEG-siRNA Conjugated Compound Targeting Glioblastoma
Authors: Qing Su, Junxiao Chen, Ziyuan Liu, Yiqi Fan, Shuai He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Glioblastoma ranks among the most prevalent primary intracranial tumors, characterized by high mortality and poor prognosis. Chemotherapy remains a key treatment strategy for gliomas, though most current drugs suffer from limited efficacy and significant toxicity. This study focuses on a cRGD-siEGFR coupling compound synthesized in a previous stage. Prior research indicated that cRGD-siEGFR molecules exhibited certain targeting and antitumor properties but faced issues of inadequate targeting, low efficacy, and high renal toxicity. To enhance antitumor efficacy and mitigate side effects, a pH-responsive, long-circulating, and highly targeted siRNA delivery system, the cRGD-PEG-siEGFR conjugate, was developed. The targeting, antitumor effects, and biological distribution of cRGD-PEG-siEGFR were examined. The results demonstrated that cRGD-PEG-siEGFR was effectively taken up by αvβ3-positive U87MG cells, specifically silenced EGFR gene expression, and exhibited antitumor effects. In normal physiological conditions, it avoided uptake by normal cells, thereby reducing side effects. Furthermore, in vivo biodistribution experiments revealed that cRGD-PEG-siEGFR, compared to cRGD-siEGFR, significantly decreased renal accumulation and exhibited prolonged circulation. Consequently, cRGD-PEG-siRNA emerges as a promising drug candidate with attributes of long circulation, high targeting, pH responsiveness, and substantial antitumor efficacy.

Introduction

Glioblastoma is the most prevalent malignant tumor in the central nervous system, characterized by high invasiveness, frequent recurrence, and poor prognosis, with a 5-year overall survival rate among the lowest for human cancers.1 Standard treatments for glioblastoma include surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. However, due to its highly infiltrative and diffuse growth patterns, complete surgical resection is often unachievable, necessitating adjunctive radiotherapy and chemotherapy to improve patient survival and quality of life. The blood-brain barrier poses a significant obstacle, preventing oral or intravenous chemotherapy drugs from effectively reaching the brain, thereby diminishing their antitumor efficacy.2,3 This underscores the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies for glioblastoma. Before 2002, the median overall survival (mOS) for patients with GBM was less than one year postdiagnosis, with a 5-year survival rate under 3%. Recent advancements have extended the mOS to 20.9 months.4 Despite these improvements, gliomas remain highly malignant with poor prognoses, making them exceedingly challenging to treat. Current research into new therapeutic strategies primarily focuses on immunotherapy, targeted therapy, and gene therapy. Notable approaches include immune checkpoint inhibitors, CAR-T cell therapy, EGFR inhibitors, PDGF receptor inhibitors, and small interfering nucleic acid molecules (siRNA) drug treatments.5−7 SiRNA therapy, an emerging treatment modality, directly targets specific genes, silencing them to inhibit tumor growth.8 The clinical potential of siRNA drugs is considerable, highlighted by the breakthroughs in RNA interference drugs and mRNA vaccines in 2018, which signified advancements in RNA delivery systems. Nevertheless, the complex tumor microenvironment has impeded the FDA approval of antitumor RNA-targeting drugs, indicating that current delivery systems are insufficient for effective antitumor treatment.9 Therefore, developing new tumor-targeted delivery systems remains imperative. SiRNA, with its high gene-silencing activity at low doses, can be tailored to target cancer-related genes, inhibit tumor growth and metastasis, and minimize impacts on normal tissues. By designing efficient delivery systems that facilitate siRNA entry into brain tissue and tumor sites, the therapeutic potential of siRNA in treating brain tumors can be fully realized.

The epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is a transmembrane glycoprotein belonging to the tyrosine kinase superfamily of receptors. Research indicates that mutations or amplification of the EGFR gene often result in excessive activation of EGFR, contributing to uncontrolled proliferation and drug resistance in glioma cells.10,11 Consequently, EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors have become pivotal in developing tumor-related drugs.12 The cell adhesion molecule integrin αvβ3 plays a significant role in cancer progression and is overexpressed in glioblastoma, melanoma, breast, ovarian, and prostate cancer.13 The peptide cRGD exhibits a high affinity for integrin αvβ3.14 Our previous research has synthesized cRGD-siEGFR conjugates. Both in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that cRGD-siEGFR could silence EGFR mRNA expression and inhibit tumor growth. However, the siRNA molecules constructed initially exhibited poor biodistribution and renal accumulation, leading to renal toxicity and reduced drug efficacy.15,16 Thus, designing a new delivery method to address these issues is imperative.

Pegylation, the technique of conjugating polyethylene glycol (PEG) groups to molecules, is widely employed to enhance the pharmacokinetics of proteins and chemical drugs in vivo.17 Previous studies have shown that PEG conjugation to siRNA improves its pharmacokinetics and biodistribution, extends its circulation time, and reduces renal excretion. However, PEGylation can hinder the interaction between the carrier and target cells, preventing the siRNA delivery system from effectively performing receptor-mediated endocytosis in cancer cells, thereby diminishing its antitumor activity. This challenge is known as the PEG dilemma.18 Through acid-sensitive design, drug molecules can be targeted to the weakly acidic tumor microenvironment, enhancing drug molecule accumulation in tumor tissues and improving antitumor activity.19,20 Hence, designing an acid-sensitive molecule may offer a solution to the PEG dilemma.

Given the pivotal role of the EGFR gene in regulating glioma proliferation and the promising potential of siRNA in targeted therapy, our team initially designed a cRGD-siRNA molecule for glioma treatment. Although cRGD-siRNA showed certain targeting and antitumor activity, it had limitations such as insufficient targeting, poor efficacy, and high nephrotoxicity.15 This study aims to modify the previously obtained cRGD-siRNA to enhance its targeting and antitumor activity while reducing renal toxicity. Numerous reports exist on PEG and acid-sensitive modifications in delivery systems like nanoparticles and liposomes.21,22 However, there are no relevant reports on the direct modification of siRNA to prepare acid-sensitive cRGD-PEG-siRNA conjugates. This study synthesized acid-sensitive PEG using hydrazone bonds, integrated acid-sensitive PEG molecules into the siRNA delivery system, and constructed a pH-responsive drug delivery system cRGD-PEG-siRNA conjugate. We then evaluated its targeting, antitumor activity, and nephrotoxicity to determine its potential as a drug candidate.

Results and Discussion

Preparation of cRGD-PEG-siRNA and Its Serum Stability

The molecular structure of the cRGD-PEG-siRNA conjugate is illustrated in Figure 1A. To enhance stability and reduce immunogenicity, modifying the siRNA backbone with 2′-O-methyl (2′-OMe) was recommended.23 Therefore, double-stranded EGFR siRNA (siEGFR) was chosen as a representative siRNA drug, with its sense and antisense strands individually modified with 2′-OMe. In this study, two single-stranded siRNAs with sulfhydryl groups were synthesized and coupled with cRGD peptide and PEG2000 molecules through a thiol-maleimide reaction, resulting in two differently modified single-stranded siRNAs. After annealing at 95 °C, the final compound, cRGD-PEG-siRNA, was obtained. The purified siRNA conjugate was analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Results indicated that, compared to cRGD-siRNA, cRGD-PEG-siRNA particles displayed a delayed migration curve on the agarose gel. This delay is likely due to PEG increasing the compound’s hydrodynamic radius and reducing its electric charge density. The cRGD-siRNA bands were observed under acidic conditions, suggesting that the hydrazone bond between siRNA molecules and PEG can be cleaved in the weakly acidic cellular microenvironment (Figure 1B). Incubation of modified siRNA molecules in mouse serum revealed significant degradation of uncoupled 2′-OMe modified siRNA at 48 h. Conversely, under identical conditions, cRGD-siRNA and cRGD-PEG-siRNA conjugates with 2′-OMe modification did not exhibit significant degradation at 48 h but were completely degraded at 96 h (Figure 1C).

MALDI-TOF/MS Characterization of the Molecular Structure of cRGD-PEG-siRNA

In the previous chapter, the molecular weight distribution of cRGD-ssEGFR, PEG2000-asEGFR, and cRGD-PEG-siEGFR was confirmed through gel electrophoresis, though specific molecular weights had not yet been determined. In this chapter, MALDI-TOF-MS was utilized to ascertain these specific weights. The results indicated that cRGD-ssEGFR had a molecular weight of 8456.075 Da (Figure 1D). Similarly, PEG2000-asEGFR and cRGD-PEG-siEGFR were found to have molecular weights of 8854.519 and 16510.794 Da, respectively (Figure 1E,F).

Cell Uptake and Intracellular Distribution of cRGD-PEG-siRNA In Vitro

To examine the uptake and intracellular distribution of cRGD-PEG-siRNA molecules in U87MG cells, treatments with fluorescently labeled cRGD-PEG-siNC, cRGD-siNC, and siNC molecules (labeled with indole dicarbonyl anthocyanin-5 (Cy5)) were conducted, with siNC serving as a negative control lacking homology with human and mouse genes. Figure 2A shows that bare siNC-Cy5 exhibited the lowest cellular uptake, with a positive rate of 1.37% and fluorescence intensity of 2945. In contrast, cRGD-PEG-siRNA and cRGD-siRNA showed Cy5 positive rates of 88.1 and 57.0%, respectively, suggesting that the modification with cRGD peptide and further PEG modification significantly enhanced the cellular uptake of siNC molecules. Further analysis under different pH conditions revealed that weakly acidic environments increased cellular uptake of cRGD-PEG-siNC (88.1% at pH 6.5 versus 33.8% at pH 7.4). This enhanced uptake might be due to the acid-sensitive linkage between PEG and siNC degrading under acidic conditions, exposing amino group positive charges, thus promoting cellular uptake. Unlike the U87MG cells treated solely with cRGD-PEG-siNC, the Cy5 positive rate of U87MG cells decreased to 18.1% after pretreatment with cRGD peptide, confirming that siRNA delivery molecules were internalized via endocytosis mediated by cRGD peptide and integrin αvβ3 receptor binding. Additionally, U87MG cells treated with fluorescently labeled cRGD-PEG-siNC, cRGD-siNC, and siNC molecules were examined for subcellular localization after 12 h using confocal microscopy. The observations indicated that exposed siRNA molecules failed to enter U87MG cells, whereas cRGD-PEG-siRNA (at pH 6.5) and cRGD-siRNA were absorbed (Figure 2D). However, uptake of cRGD-PEG-siRNA was significantly reduced at pH 7.4 compared to pH 6.5. Pretreatment with cRGD peptide blocked cRGD-PEG-siRNA uptake, indicating that cRGD-PEG-siNC-Cy5 was internalized through cRGD peptide and integrin αvβ3 receptor-mediated endocytosis, consistent with flow cytometry results.

Toxicity of cRGD-PEG-siRNA to Renal Tubular Epithelial Cells

To evaluate the cytotoxicity of cRGD-siNC and cRGD-PEG-siNC on human renal tubular epithelial cell HK2 cells, the cells were cultured with varying concentrations of the two delivery systems for 48 h, followed by assessment of cell viability using the CCK-8 solution. As depicted in Figure 2E, there was no significant difference in cell viability between the 100 and 500 nM cRGD-siNC group and the control group (P > 0.05). However, treatment with 1000 nM cRGD-siNC significantly decreased cell viability to 90.26% (P < 0.05), while 1500 and 2000 nM concentrations reduced viability to 82.80 and 74.96%, respectively, indicating substantial toxicity at higher concentrations. Conversely, even at elevated concentrations, cRGD-PEG-siNC treatment did not significantly affect cell viability compared to the control group, except at 2000 nM where viability decreased to 90.03%. This demonstrates that PEG modification can mitigate the cytotoxicity of siRNA delivery systems on renal tubular epithelial cells. Comparative analysis further revealed that cRGD-PEG-siNC exhibited lower toxicity than cRGD-siNC at 1500 and 2000 nM concentrations (P < 0.05), highlighting the potential of polyethylene glycol in reducing cytotoxicity for biomedical applications.

The Inhibitory Effect of cRGD-PEG-siRNA on Cell Proliferation

The objective of this section was to evaluate the inhibitory effect of cRGD-PEG-siEGFR at various concentrations (200, 400, and 800 nM) on U87MG cell proliferation under different pH conditions (7.4 and 6.5). Unidirectional analysis of variance revealed significant differences in cell proliferation activity between groups under the two pH conditions. As shown in Figure 3A, blue fluorescence indicates all DAPI-stained nuclei, while red fluorescence marks proliferating cells. After 72 h of treatment, cells in the normal, cRGD-PEG-siNC/pH 7.4, and cRGD-PEG-siNC/pH 6.5 groups proliferated rapidly. In contrast, U87MG cell proliferation was significantly inhibited in the cRGD-PEG-siEGFR/pH 6.5 and cRGD-siEGFR groups, with minimal proliferation observed. Additionally, although cell proliferation in the cRGD-PEG-siEGFR/pH 7.4 group was significantly reduced compared to the control and siNC groups, the inhibition was not as pronounced as in the cRGD-PEG-siEGFR/pH 6.5 group (Figure 3B). Figure 3E illustrates that U87MG cell survival rates decreased with increasing molecular concentration under both pH conditions. At pH 7.4, cell survival rates were 87.79, 69.3, and 58.73% at 200, 400, and 800 nM concentrations, respectively. Similarly, at pH 6.5, survival rates were 69.97, 52.36, and 43.76% at 200, 400, and 800 nM concentrations, respectively. Furthermore, cell proliferation rates under acidic conditions (pH 6.5) were generally lower than under neutral conditions (pH 7.4). These findings suggest that PEG-modified siRNA molecules exhibit enhanced inhibitory effects on tumor cell proliferation in acidic environments.

In Vitro Proapoptotic Effect of cRGD-PEG-siRNA

To determine the apoptosis induced in U87MG cells by different concentrations of siRNA molecules, the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Kit was employed to stain apoptotic cells, which were then quantified using flow cytometry. In apoptotic cells, phosphatidylserine (PS) translocates from the inner to the outer cell membrane, where it binds specifically with Annexin V, serving as a sensitive apoptosis marker. After 72 h of siRNA treatment, cell apoptosis was observed (Figure 3C). Unidirectional analysis of variance revealed significant differences in apoptosis rates between groups at 72 h (Figure 3D). Three days post-treatment, the apoptosis rates for the normal, NC (pH 7.4), and NC (pH 6.5) groups were 14.10, 13.82, and 19.92%, respectively. Following treatment with 800 nM cRGD-PEG-siEGFR, apoptosis rates were 53.3% at pH 6.5 and 32.73% at pH 7.4. Similarly, 800 nM cRGD-siEGFR treatment resulted in an apoptosis rate of 51.27%. These results suggest that cRGD-PEG-siEGFR effectively promotes apoptosis in U87MG cells, with acid-sensitive PEG-modified siRNA molecules showing enhanced potential in inducing tumor cell apoptosis under weakly acidic conditions.

In Vitro Gene Knockout of cRGD-PEG siRNA

To transfect U87MG cells with cRGD-PEG-siEGFR, no transfection reagents were employed; instead, the culture medium pH was adjusted to 7.4 and 6.5, and the molecules were directly added to the cells. The gene silencing efficiency of this molecule was evaluated using RT-qPCR to measure EGFR mRNA expression levels. As shown in Figure 3F, at pH 7.4, the EGFR mRNA levels in the 400 and 800 nM cRGD-PEG-siEGFR groups were significantly reduced compared to the control and cRGD-PEG-siNC groups, with expression levels of 72 and 54%, respectively. At pH 6.5, the silencing efficiency was even higher, with EGFR mRNA levels at 72, 52, and 31% for concentrations of 200, 400, and 800 nM, respectively. These results indicate that cRGD-PEG-siEGFR effectively inhibits EGFR mRNA expression in human glioma U87MG cells, with acid-sensitive PEG-modified siRNA molecules demonstrating superior gene silencing under acidic conditions.

To verify these findings, total protein was extracted 48 h post-transfection for electrophoresis and incubation with EGFR antibodies, followed by Western blotting to detect EGFR protein expression. ImageJ software was used to scan and obtain the optical density values of EGFR and HSP90 protein bands, with the relative expression of EGFR proteins calculated by dividing the optical density of EGFR bands by that of HSP90 bands. The control and cRGD-PEG-siNC groups were used as references. As shown in Figure 3G,H, at pH 7.4, EGFR protein expression levels in the 400 and 800 nM cRGD-PEG-siEGFR groups were significantly reduced compared to the control and cRGD-PEG-siNC groups, with levels at 72 and 54%, respectively. At pH 6.5, the EGFR protein expression in U87MG cells treated with the same concentrations was even lower, with levels at 72, 52, and 31% for 200, 400, and 800 nM concentrations, respectively. These results further support the efficacy of cRGD-PEG-siEGFR in inhibiting EGFR protein expression in human glioma U87MG cells, highlighting the superior gene-silencing effects of acid-sensitive PEG-modified siRNA molecules under acidic conditions.

Targeting and In Vivo Distribution of cRGD-PEG siRNA in the Intracranial Region

This study constructed a U87MG luc cell line with stable expression of luciferase and established a nude mouse model of intracranial glioma. The in vivo distribution of siNC-Cy5, cRGD-siNC-Cy5, and cRGD-PEG-siNC-Cy5 (all labeled with Cy5) was studied using small animal imaging technology. Following tail vein injection of the drug (1 nmol/20 g), imaging results (Figure 4A) revealed that cRGD-PEG-siNC-Cy5 and cRGD-siNC-Cy5 molecules specifically targeted and accumulated in intracranial tumors, peaking at 48 h. In contrast, siNC-Cy5 molecules exhibited minimal tumor accumulation. Additionally, all three molecules showed kidney accumulation; however, cRGD-PEG-siNC-Cy5 demonstrated significantly reduced kidney accumulation, indicating unaffected tumor targeting. Twenty-4 h postdissection, tissue imaging results (Figure 4B) confirmed that cRGD-PEG-siNC-Cy5 and cRGD-siNC-Cy5 effectively targeted and accumulated in intracranial tumor tissue, whereas siNC-Cy5 was scarcely present in glioma and brain tissues. No Cy5-labeled cRGD-siNC-Cy5 was observed in the heart, spleen, and lungs. The consistency between in vitro tissue and organ imaging and in vivo animal imaging suggests that cRGD-siNC-Cy5 specifically targets intracranial tumor tissue and accumulates in glioma following intravenous injection.

Microscopic observations of tumor tissue from distribution experiments in nude mice with intracranial gliomas indicated that cRGD-PEG-siNC-Cy5 specifically targeted glioma tissue and was distributed in the stroma of brain stromal tumors via CD31-labeled blood vessels, without distribution in normal brain tissue. Furthermore, HE staining of brain tissue revealed nuclear enlargement, cytoplasmic reduction, deepened staining, morphological abnormalities, and disordered arrangement, confirming the successful establishment of an intracranial glioma model. These results demonstrate that cRGD-PEG-siNC-Cy5 molecules specifically accumulate in glioma tissue, penetrate the blood-brain and blood-tumor barriers, and enter the glioma interstitial space for tumor cell uptake.

Discussion

The clinical application of siRNA as a therapeutic drug encounters significant challenges, including low transmission efficiency, poor immunogenicity, and instability.13,24 To harness the therapeutic potential of siRNA molecules, appropriate delivery vectors or chemical modifications are necessary to enhance their biological activity. Extensively studied siRNA delivery systems include polymer nanoparticles, liposome nanoparticles, and siRNA coupling copolymers.9,25 Polymer and liposome nanoparticles are promising drug delivery strategies due to their excellent drug delivery capabilities, targeting, drug loading, and controllable release properties. However, their macromolecular properties, risk of drug leakage, synthesis complexity, and immunogenicity contribute to limitations such as low stability, short circulation time, organ toxicity, and production challenges in clinical applications.26−28 Our research team has focused on siRNA-coupled copolymers, which offer advantages like simpler molecular structure, straightforward preparation methods, strong targeting ability, and lower toxicity compared to polymer and liposome nanoparticles. Studies have demonstrated that the direct coupling of siRNA molecules with PEG can significantly alter their biological distribution, renal accumulation, serum clearance, and gene silencing efficiency, with effects dependent on the molecular weight of PEG.29,30 The cRGD-siEGFR molecule developed in the early stages of this project exhibited certain targeting and antitumor effects but faced challenges such as insufficient transmission efficiency and poor biological distribution.15,16 This study combined the passive targeting and enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect of PEG with the active targeting properties of cRGD to synthesize a novel pH-responsive, long-circulating, highly targeted siRNA drug delivery system, cRGD-PEG-siEGFR conjugate. Preliminary evaluations confirmed its tumor targeting, antitumor efficacy, and biological distribution.

In this study, nucleic acid chemical synthesis was performed to create two single-stranded, thiol-modified siRNA molecules (ssRNA-HS and asRNA-HS) targeting the EGFR sequence from previous research, which demonstrated a silencing efficiency of over 80%. Hydrazone bonds, known for their high selectivity, biocompatibility, and ease of synthesis, were utilized as acid-sensitive linkages in the drug delivery system. The acid-sensitive system formed by coupling hydrazone bonds with PEG exhibited excellent biocompatibility, reducing toxic side effects and immune reactions. mPEG-CHO (molecular weight 2000) served as the raw material, reacting with p-aminobenzoyl hydrazide under alkaline conditions to generate acid-sensitive hydrazone bonds (hdy). Acid-sensitive PEG molecules (mPEG-hdy-Mal) were synthesized by modifying maleimidopropionic acid (MPA) through carboxyl dehydration condensation of amino groups. Monovalent c(RGDfk) peptides were synthesized using the Fmoc peptide solid-phase synthesis method. After dehydrating and condensing c(RGDfk) with 8-amino-3,6-dioxooctanoic acid, the terminal amino group was dehydrated and condensed with the carboxyl group of MPA to obtain maleimide-modified c(RGDfk)-Ma. The Michael addition reaction of maleimide and thiol groups was utilized to couple cRGD peptides and acid-sensitive PEG with siRNA molecules. This reaction, characterized by mild conditions and high selectivity, did not require high temperatures, strong acids or bases, or catalysts, thereby preserving the structure of siRNA molecules while successfully linking PEG and cRGD peptides to the siRNA.31 The reversible and straightforward synthesis process of this reaction made it suitable for large-scale synthesis. Finally, the two single strands were annealed at high temperatures to produce cRGD-PEG-siRNA molecules. After purification by high-performance liquid chromatography, the product was characterized by high-resolution mass spectrometry and validated by agarose electrophoresis. The results confirmed the successful synthesis of cRGD-PEG-siRNA molecules, which demonstrated clear structure, good pH responsiveness, and stability.

Integrin αvβ3 receptors are highly expressed on U87 cells, enabling cRGD to actively target and deliver drug molecules by specifically binding to this receptor. PEG polymer nanoparticles and PEG liposome nanoparticles are commonly used nanocarriers that enhance drug stability, solubility, and targeted delivery. By conjugating PEG to nanoparticles, drug efficacy and safety can be improved, addressing several limitations of traditional drug delivery systems. PEG-conjugated nanoparticles, PEG-modified protein drugs, and PEG-modified antibody drugs have already been studied and applied clinically.32 Consequently, PEG was incorporated into our siRNA conjugate delivery system. Direct coupling of PEG to siRNA enhances stability, prolongs blood circulation time, and improves intracellular uptake efficiency while reducing immune responses and toxicity. Furthermore, the incorporation of hydrazone bonds in PEG allows for precise control over the siRNA release rate.

These features endow cRGD-PEG-siRNA molecules with multiple targeting capabilities, low toxicity, and pH responsiveness. To validate the tumor targeting and toxicity of cRGD-PEG-siEGFR molecules, it was observed that PEG in cRGD-PEG-siEGFR cleaves in the weakly acidic tumor microenvironment, facilitating effective uptake by U87 cells. Additionally, the “hidden effect” of PEG reduces the uptake of cRGD-PEG-siRNA by renal tubular epithelial cells.

In cytotoxicity studies of siRNA delivery systems on HK2 cells, significant differences in cell survival rates were observed between cRGD-siRNA and cRGD-PEG-siRNA at concentrations of 1500 and 2000 nM. This indicated that high concentrations of cRGD-siRNA are significantly toxic to HK2 cells, whereas PEG introduction mitigates this toxicity. Tumor targeting studies revealed that cRGD-PEG-siRNA molecules were not effectively taken up by U87 cells under normal pH conditions. However, due to the acid sensitivity of the hydrazone bond, PEG cleavage under weakly acidic conditions enabled effective uptake by U87 cells. Flow cytometry and confocal microscopy results were consistent, demonstrating that the combination of active targeting by cRGD peptides, passive targeting by PEG, and pH-responsive targeting by hydrazone bonds successfully achieved tumor targeting of siRNA delivery systems.

The 5′ phosphate group, 3′ terminal hydroxyl, and nucleoside base groups of siRNA molecules are essential for their gene-silencing function. To investigate whether the siRNA delivery system retains efficient gene silencing after various structural modifications, we examined the inhibitory effect of cRGD-PEG-siEGFR on tumor cell proliferation at the cellular level. Specifically, we analyzed the expression level of EGFR gene silencing by cRGD-PEG-siEGFR and its impact on the proliferation and apoptosis of U87 cells. Our findings demonstrated that cRGD-PEG-siEGFR has an inhibitory effect on tumor cell proliferation under both pH 7.4 and pH 6.5 conditions. Under these conditions, cRGD-PEG-siEGFR significantly inhibited the expression of EGFR mRNA and protein in U87 cells, reduced cell proliferation, and promoted apoptosis. However, the acid-sensitive PEG-modified siRNA delivery system exhibited superior antitumor effects under weakly acidic conditions. This improved efficacy at pH 6.5 is likely due to the enhanced uptake of cRGD-PEG-siEGFR molecules by U87 cells as the concentration of the siRNA delivery system increased. After PEG cleavage at pH 6.5, the siRNA delivery system could be more effectively absorbed by the cells, leading to a more pronounced inhibitory effect on tumor cell proliferation. Therefore, cRGD-PEG-siEGFR exhibits excellent potential in inhibiting tumor cell proliferation, particularly under weakly acidic conditions.

PEG modification can reduce adsorption and recognition between particles and blood components, as well as minimize interactions with immune system cells, thus extending the circulation time of nanoparticles. Additionally, the Enhanced Permeability and Retention (EPR) effect facilitates the accumulation and localized drug release of PEG-modified particles within the tumor through the tumor stroma and lymphatic-restricted areas.17,18 Generally, smaller molecular weight PEG polymers exhibit higher blood-brain barrier permeability, whereas larger molecular weight PEG polymers face greater challenges in crossing this barrier. Considering factors such as molecular weight and drug solubility, PEG molecules with a molecular weight of 2000 were chosen. However, it remains uncertain whether PEG2000 molecules, when coupled with cRGD peptides and siRNA molecules, can penetrate the blood-brain barrier and reach tumor tissue, and how their biological distribution compares to cRGD-siRNA molecules. Therefore, this study compared the biological distribution of naked siRNA, cRGD-siRNA, and cRGD-PEG-siRNA molecules in mice with intracranial glioma in situ using in vivo imaging technology. Naked siRNA, lacking targeted modification, could not effectively reach tumor tissue and instead accumulated for metabolism in the kidneys. In contrast, cRGD-PEG-siRNA retained tumor-targeting properties, and PEG modification reduced the accumulation of the siRNA delivery system in renal organs compared to cRGD-siRNA. The 24-h in vitro organ imaging results aligned with the in vivo imaging results in mice. These results suggested that the newly synthesized delivery system effectively reaches tumor tissue with relatively low kidney accumulation and improved biological distribution. Tissue immunofluorescence experiments demonstrated that cRGD-PEG-siRNA molecules could reach tumor tissue through CD31-labeled blood vessels, further confirming this conclusion.

The cRGD-PEG-siRNA synthesized in our study capitalized on the active targeting of cRGD peptides, the EPR effect of PEG, and the tumor microenvironment responsiveness of the hydrazone bond to achieve multiple targeting effects for siRNA molecules. Experimental results confirmed that PEG 2000 molecules effectively inhibit kidney cell recognition without affecting tumor cell uptake, thereby reducing renal accumulation of cRGD-siRNA. However, the PEG-modified siRNA delivery system was still taken up by cells under nonacidic conditions. Additionally, the acid-sensitive hydrazone bond used exhibited poor hydrolysis efficiency at pH 6.5. Consequently, a significant portion of the siRNA delivery system remained outside tumor cells, preventing effective PEG cleavage and potentially compromising the gene silencing efficiency of cRGD-PEG-siRNA.

Experimental findings indicated that cRGD-PEG-siRNA molecules exhibited superior cellular uptake, in vitro silencing effect, and in vivo targeting compared to cRGD-siRNA molecules. Hence, the synthesized cRGD-PEG-siRNA molecule demonstrated overall better performance than the cRGD-siRNA molecule. However, the in vivo pharmacodynamics verification of cRGD-PEG-siRNA remains to be conducted, which is a limitation of this study.

Current research on siRNA delivery systems primarily focuses on enhancing silencing efficiency. Nevertheless, many preclinical delivery systems face issues such as immunogenicity and insufficient targeting. By synthesizing pH-responsive cRGD-PEG-siRNA molecules, it is possible to improve the biodistribution of siRNA, reduce toxicity, and achieve multiple targeting effects. This approach provides new strategies and foundational methods for addressing these existing challenges.

Conclusions

This study successfully prepared a pH-responsive cRGD-PEG-siRNA conjugate. Compared to conventional cRGD-siRNA, PEG modification extended circulation time in vivo, enhanced tumor tissue distribution, and significantly reduced uptake by normal tissue cells and metabolic accumulation in the kidneys. The pH-responsive design also addressed the reduced cellular uptake caused by PEG, effectively ensuring the antitumor efficacy of cRGD-PEG-siRNA. Consequently, cRGD-PEG-siRNA shows promise as a new antitumor drug candidate.

Experimental Procedures

Materials

The U87MG cell line, a human malignant glioblastoma multiforme cell line (ATCC number: HTB-14), was utilized in this study. The U87MG-Luc cell line, developed in our laboratory, was created by transfecting a reporter gene encoding firefly luciferase. Additionally, the HK-2 cell line, a human renal cortical proximal convoluted tubule epithelial cell line (ATCC number: CRL-2190), was employed. Both cell lines were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, CA, USA) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2, following standard protocols.

The SYBR Green One-Step qRT-PCR Kits and Reverse Transcription Master Mix (with gDNA Remover) used in the study were obtained from EZBioscience (Suzhou, China). Opti-MEM, DMEM, and FBS were sourced from Gibco Corporation (Gaithersburg, MD, USA), while D-Luciferin was acquired from APExBIO (Houston, TX, USA). The EGFR antibody (ab52894) was sourced from Abcam (Cambridge, England), the anti-CD31 antibody (AF3628) from R&D Systems (MN, USA), and the HSP90 antibody (4875) from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). The Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Kit was obtained from Elabscience (Wuhan, China). Additionally, mPEG-hdy-Mal and cRGD peptides were synthesized in-house using services from Ruixibio (Xi’an, China).

siRNA Sequence Modifications and Verification

The siRNA sequences and modifications used in this research are as follows:

Human EGFR sense strand (Sense strand: 5′-(mC)(mA)(mA)AGUGUGUAAC-GGA(mA)(mU)(mA)dTdT-3′; Antisense strand: 5′-(mU)(mA)(mU)UCCGUUACA-CACU(mU)(mU)(mG) dTdT-3′).

Negative control siRNA (Sense strand: 5′-(mA)(mU)(mC)GAAUUCCUGCA-GCCC(mG)(mU)(mU)dTdT; Antisense strand: 5′-(mA)(mA)(mC)GGGCUGCAGG-AAUUC(mG)(mA)(mU) dTdT-3′).

In these sequences, “m″ denotes 2′-O-methyl sugar-modified RNA nucleosides. The siEGFR effectively silences Homo sapiens EGFR expression, serving as the representative siRNA in this study. The negative control siRNA (siNC) lacks homologous sequences in human and mouse transcriptomes. Additionally, a Cy5-labeled siNC (siNC-Cy5) was prepared. All siRNAs were synthesized by Guangzhou RiboBio.

Preparation of cRGD-PEG-siRNA, cRGD-siRNA Conjugate

To prepare cRGD-PEG-siRNA and cRGD-siRNA molecules, the cRGD polypeptide was covalently coupled to the 5′-end of the siRNA sense strand using thiol-maleimide chemistry as previously described. Similarly, PEG-hdy-Mal was covalently coupled to the 3′-end of the siRNA antisense strand. The purity of the siRNA was assessed using high-performance liquid chromatography. The chromatographic conditions were as follows: Shim-pack GIST C18 column (250 × 10 mm, 5 μm, Shimadzu Corporation); injection volume 50 μL; column temperature 40 °C; flow rate 2.5 mL/min; mobile phase A: 0.1% TFA acetonitrile, mobile phase B: 0.1% TFA in water; elution program: 0–30 min, 5%–50% A; 30–32 min, 50%–5% A; detection wavelength: 260 nm. To generate double-stranded siRNA, equal volumes of the sense and antisense strands were mixed in an annealing buffer, heat-denatured at 95 °C for 4 min, and then slowly cooled to room temperature.

Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (MALDI-TOF/MS)

Molecular weight detection was performed using MALDI-TOF/MS (UltrafleXtreme MALDI TOF/TOF, Bruker, Germany). The preparation involved mixing 1 μL of protein standard Ι with 1 μL of HPA matrix, and applying the sample onto a Ground Steel sample target. Similarly, 1 μL of the sample solution (dissolved in 0.1% TFA) was mixed with 1 μL of HPA matrix and applied onto a Ground Steel sample target. The target plate, once dried at room temperature, was placed in the mass spectrometer for detection. Data acquisition was managed using FlexControl software with parameters set to positive ion reflection mode (RP), a laser intensity of 30%, a molecular weight scanning range of 5000–20000 Da, and a mass deviation of 500 ppm.

Agarose Gel Electrophoresis

The pH responsiveness and serum stability of cRGD-PEG-siRNA were qualitatively analyzed using agarose gel electrophoresis. To assess pH responsiveness, cRGD-PEG-siRNA was incubated under various pH conditions for 2 h, mixed with a loading buffer, and subjected to electrophoresis in a 1.2% (w/v) agarose gel at 120 V for 20 min. Results were visualized with a UV projector. For serum stability analysis, 5 μL of 20 μM cRGD-PEG-siRNA, cRGD-siRNA, and siRNA were mixed with 5 μL of mouse serum and incubated at 37 °C for different time intervals (0, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h). Aliquots were collected at each time point, combined with a loading buffer, and loaded onto a 1.2% (w/v) agarose gel for electrophoresis at 120 V for 20 min. Results were visualized using a UV projector.

Cellular Uptake and Confocal Fluorescence Microscopy Assay

U87MG cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates 1 day before transfection and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Cells were transfected with Opti-MEM for 12 h under different pH conditions. To confirm the receptor binding effect of cRGD and αvβ3, U87MG cells were pretreated with cRGD polypeptide before transfection. For cellular uptake determination, cells were washed three times with PBS, digested with EDTA-free trypsin, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in 0.2 mL PBS. Fluorescence data were collected using a CytoFLEX flow analyzer (Beckman) and analyzed with Cell Quest software. For confocal fluorescence microscopy assays, transfected cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, stained with DAPI for 3 min, and observed using confocal microscopy (NIS-Elements 5.4, Nikon).

Toxicity of cRGD-PEG-siRNA to Renal Tubular Epithelial Cells

HK-2 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The cells were then treated with cRGD-PEG-siRNA and cRGD-siRNA at concentrations of 100, 200, 500, 1000, 1500, and 2000 nM. After 48 h of incubation, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a microplate reader to plot cell viability as a percentage of the blank control group.

Cell Proliferation Analysis by CCK-8 and EdU Assays

U87MG cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well 1 day before transfection and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. After 72 h of cell transfection under different pH conditions, CCK-8 solution was added to each well following the manufacturer’s protocol, and the plates were incubated for another 2 h before measuring absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Additionally, 72 h post-transfection, 10 μM EdU solution was added and incubated for another 2 h. EdU labeling and staining were performed according to the manufacturer’s standard protocol, and imaging was conducted using a Nikon inverted microscope.

Apoptosis Analysis

U87MG cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well 1 day before transfection and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Seventy-2 h after transfection, both adherent and suspension cells were harvested. The cells were stained according to the manufacturer’s instructions and analyzed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman).

Western Blot

The efficiency of siRNA conjugate-mediated transfection was evaluated by performing Western blot analysis to detect EGFR protein expression levels. The day before transfection, U87MG cells were seeded into six-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cells were transfected with cRGD-PEG-siEGFR under various pH conditions, with increasing concentrations, using cRGD-PEG-siNC as a negative control. Forty-8 h post-transfection, total protein was extracted from cell lysates for quantification. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg/lane) were separated by 7.5% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk at room temperature for 1 h, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with EGFR and HSP90 antibodies. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated for 1 h with HRP-conjugated mouse antirabbit IgG (Dr. BOSTER, Nanjing, China). HSP90 served as the loading control. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence kit (Millipore, MA, USA) and quantified with a chemiluminescence instrument (Cambridge, England) and ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MA, USA).

Animals

BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old, approximately 20g) were obtained from Zhuhai BesTest Bio-Tech Co., Ltd. (Zhuhai, China). All animals were housed in an SPF environment at the Animal Experimental Center of Zhujiang Hospital, Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China), under controlled conditions (22 °C ± 1 °C, relative humidity 50 ± 1%, 12-h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water. All experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Zhujiang Hospital of Southern Medical University (No. LAEC-2021–186) and conducted in accordance with animal ethics guidelines. The anesthesia method involved intraperitoneal injection of 25% tribromoethanol at a dose of 200 mg/kg, maintaining anesthesia for 1 h. Experimental animals were euthanized by carbon dioxide asphyxiation. The procedure included placing the animal in a carbon dioxide anesthesia box, gradually increasing the carbon dioxide concentration to 100% until the animal lost consciousness, and continuing ventilation for 2 min to ensure death.

Tumor Model Establishment

An intracranial orthotopic GBM model was established to study the biodistribution of cRGD-PEG-siRNA, following a previously described method. Briefly, U87MG-Luc cells were digested, resuspended in PBS, and kept on ice. After anesthetizing the nude mice and securing them on a small animal stereotaxic apparatus, an incision was made on the top of the head to expose the skull and bregma. A total of 5 μL of 1 × 105 U87MG-Luc cells was slowly injected 2 mm to the right and 3 mm posterior to the bregma, within a 5 min period. After disinfection, the mice were kept in an SPF environment and monitored daily for health and growth status. Tumor growth was tracked using an IVIS Lumina II (Caliper, USA).

In Vivo Distribution

Nude mice carrying orthotopic U87MG-Luc GBM were randomly divided into three groups, each receiving an intravenous injection of 1 nmol/20g cRGD-PEG-siRNC-Cy5, cRGD-siNC-Cy5, or siNC-Cy5. Biodistribution was monitored at 12, 24, 48, and 72 h postinjection using an IVIS spectral imaging system at the appropriate wavelength (Cy5: λex = 640 nm, λem = 680 nm). Tumors and major organs were excised and imaged 24 h after intravenous injection.

Immunofluorescence Analysis

Tissues from the in vivo distribution assessments were processed as previously described. Brain tissue was cryosectioned, immunofluorescently stained, and visualized under a confocal microscope to observe the distribution within the tissue.

Statistical Analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard error (X ± S). Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 22.0 software. Differences between groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance for two-way testing. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
