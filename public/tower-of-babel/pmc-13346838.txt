Learning patient-specific spatial biomarker dynamics via operator learning for Alzheimer’s disease progression
Authors: Jindong Wang, Yutong Mao, Xiao Liu, Wenrui Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a complex, multifactorial neurodegenerative disorder with substantial heterogeneity in progression and treatment response. Despite recent therapeutic advances, predictive models capable of accurately forecasting individualized future biomarker states remain limited. Here, we present a machine learning-based operator learning framework for personalized modeling of AD progression, integrating longitudinal multimodal imaging, biomarker, and clinical data. Unlike conventional models with prespecified dynamics, our approach directly learns patient-specific disease operators governing the spatiotemporal evolution of amyloid, tau, and neurodegeneration biomarkers. Using Laplacian eigenfunction bases, we construct geometry-aware neural operators capable of capturing complex brain dynamics. Embedded within a digital twin paradigm, the framework enables individualized predictions, simulation of therapeutic interventions, and in silico clinical trials. Applied to AD clinical data, our method achieves high prediction accuracy exceeding 90% across multiple biomarkers, substantially outperforming existing approaches. This work offers a scalable, interpretable platform for precision modeling and personalized therapeutic optimization in neurodegenerative diseases.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) remains one of the most pressing global health challenges, affecting over 50 million people worldwide. Despite recent FDA approvals of anti-amyloid therapies such as Aducanumab (2021) and Lecanemab (2023), their clinical benefits, particularly in slowing cognitive decline, remain limited and controversial1,2. With more than 140 therapeutics currently in clinical trials and significant investment of resources, the persistently high failure rate underscores fundamental gaps in our understanding of AD’s complex, multifactorial pathophysiology and the substantial heterogeneity in disease trajectories and treatment responses3–5.

The classical pathological hallmarks of AD, amyloid-β plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles, remain central to diagnosis and therapeutic targeting, yet their precise roles in driving cognitive decline are incompletely understood6,7. Mounting evidence characterizes AD as a heterogeneous disorder involving genetic, vascular, metabolic, and neuroinflammatory factors8,9. As a result, population-based models and one-size-fits-all treatment approaches often fail to capture patient-specific disease mechanisms and therapeutic responses, posing a major obstacle for precision medicine in AD.

Computational modeling presents a promising approach to addressing this challenge by simulating disease dynamics, elucidating causal interactions, and evaluating personalized therapeutic interventions in silico. Several computational models have been developed to characterize AD biomarker progression, including mechanistic ordinary differential equation (ODE) models10–12, statistical latent time models13,14, and disease progression scoring frameworks15,16. More recently, systems biology approaches have integrated omics and imaging data to infer causal relationships among biomarkers17,18. While these models have advanced our understanding, most rely on prespecified model forms and fixed assumptions, limiting their capacity to capture the diverse, nonlinear progression patterns observed in individual patients. A central open question remains: how can data itself reveal the governing dynamical models of disease progression?

To address this, growing attention has turned to the concept of digital twins—computational replicas of individual patients constructed from multimodal clinical, imaging, and biomarker data to simulate future biomarker states and predict therapeutic outcomes. However, a critical bottleneck persists: How to efficiently and interpretably learn personalized disease models directly from high-dimensional, multimodal patient data. This motivates the integration of machine learning with operator-based modeling, a methodology well-established in fields such as fluid dynamics and materials science, but largely unexplored in neurodegenerative disease modeling.

Recent studies have explored statistical, deep learning, and mechanistic approaches for modeling AD progression19. In parallel, advances in operator learning and scientific machine learning have demonstrated the ability to learn governing dynamics directly from data20,21, while geometric representations based on Laplacian eigenmodes have been shown to capture biologically meaningful spatial patterns in brain data22,23. More recently, deep learning-based progression models24–26, as well as optimization-based formulations27, have achieved promising performance in longitudinal prediction tasks.

In this study, we introduce a novel machine learning-based operator learning framework for modeling AD progression. Unlike conventional machine learning models that map finite-dimensional inputs to outputs, our approach learns mappings between function spaces, enabling direct approximation of the dynamical operators governing longitudinal biomarker and imaging trajectories without prespecifying model structures. Building upon this formulation, we integrate the digital twin paradigm to construct individualized computational models from multimodal patient data, enabling personalized trajectory forecasting, simulation of therapeutic interventions, and in silico experimentation. Technically, the proposed framework employs Laplacian eigenfunction bases to parameterize neural operators, yielding a geometry-aware and computationally efficient spectral representation that preserves spatial coherence across structured and unstructured brain domains. Applied to longitudinal imaging and biomarker data from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) cohort28, this formulation enables robust modeling of the spatiotemporal dynamics of amyloid, tau, neurodegeneration, and cognitive decline. Beyond predictive performance, the learned operators provide insight into disease progression through analysis of spatial interaction patterns and operator structure. Overall, this work establishes a scalable, interpretable, and personalized operator-learning framework for neurodegenerative disease modeling, with direct implications for precision medicine, virtual clinical trials, and individualized therapeutic optimization.

We report both Acc1 and Acc2 because they quantify complementary aspects of forecasting accuracy under heterogeneous standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) scales. Acc2 (relative ℓ2) measures global field fidelity and is standard in operator-learning/PDE surrogates, whereas Acc1 (mean relative ℓ1) is less dominated by high-intensity regions and better reflects distributed, region-wise agreement; together they provide a more robust evaluation of spatiotemporal propagation quality.

Learning on SUVR data from 3D brain networks

To evaluate the applicability of our framework on real neuroimaging data, we applied the LENO framework to longitudinal SUVR measurements of amyloid-β (A), tau (τ), and neurodegeneration (N) collected from 100 patients. Each patient contributed 3–7 longitudinal imaging time points, with visit schedules varying across individuals due to realistic clinical follow-up and data availability. Our proposed framework does not assume uniform temporal sampling; instead, it is trained using patient-specific longitudinal pairs with the actual time intervals explicitly incorporated, enabling robust modeling of disease progression under irregular sampling and missing visits.

Training and prediction accuracy

Table 2 reports both training and forward-prediction accuracies for A, τ, and N on patients used for operator learning and unseen patients evaluated through transfer learning. Across biomarkers, the consistently high Acc2 and Acc1 indicate that the learned operator captures a consistent evolution dynamics that generalizes beyond the observed time points and does not collapse under irregular longitudinal sampling. Notably, prediction accuracies remain close to training accuracies, suggesting reliable time propagation under the learned dynamics.

These results are enabled by two key design choices. First, the Laplacian eigenfunction representation provides a geometry-aware and patient-specific spectral coordinate system, which preserves spatial coherence of SUVR patterns on individualized brain networks and reduces sensitivity to irregular graph structures. Second, our operator evolution framework explicitly incorporates the actual time intervals between visits, allowing the model to learn from heterogeneous follow-up schedules and to forecast at later ages without requiring uniform sampling. Together, these components support accurate reconstruction and forecasting of biomarker trajectories on realistic, irregular brain connectomes.

In addition, we report prediction performance for the cognitive score C. The strong accuracy in forecasting C indicates that the learned mapping from evolving neurodegeneration patterns to downstream clinical readouts is consistent over time, providing a practical bridge from imaging-derived biomarker dynamics to patient-level functional outcomes.

Data sparsity analysis

Longitudinal data sparsity is inherent in clinical studies, as different patients are observed at varying numbers of follow-up visits. For example, for the Aβ biomarker, 48 patients have 3 longitudinal time points, 28 patients have 4 time points, 18 patients have 5 time points, and the remaining patients have more than 5 time points.

To evaluate robustness to longitudinal data sparsity, we conducted an additional analysis in which, for each subject, we restricted the training set to the first k available visits (k ∈ {2, 3, 4, 5}) and used the subsequent visit for forward prediction under the same forecasting protocol. We also report the performance using all available visits (Full) as a reference. Importantly, adaptation is achieved by freezing the operator and optimizing only a patient-specific temporal scaling factor, which calibrates the progression speed without changing the learned evolution law. This parameter-efficient design is meaningful both mechanistically and clinically: it suggests a shared propagation mechanism with heterogeneous individual rates, and it enables reliable personalization in realistic longitudinal settings where each patient has limited, irregular follow-up. As a result, the framework provides a practical route toward individualized forecasting and stratification—predicting future biomarker trajectories for new patients while preserving an interpretable, consistent disease dynamics model.

Discussion

In this work, we developed an innovative PDE learning framework that directly infers the spatiotemporal dynamics governing AD biomarkers from longitudinal neuroimaging data. Leveraging the LENO, our approach integrates mechanistic insights from brain network topology with data-driven nonlinear operator learning to model the propagation of amyloid-β (A), tau (τ), and neurodegeneration (N) across personalized anatomical connectivity graphs. This fusion of interpretable mathematical modeling and advanced machine learning represents a powerful advancement for characterizing the multi-scale, heterogeneous nature of AD progression.

Unlike existing deep learning models for AD progression, such as Leadnet24, STEADYNet25, and LCADNet26, which formulate disease modeling as a discrete-time sequence prediction problem, learning mappings between longitudinal measurements at predefined follow-up intervals. We instead model AD progression as a continuous-time dynamical process governed by a learned nonlinear operator. Future brain states are obtained via explicit time integration, enabling prediction at arbitrary temporal horizons and native handling of irregular longitudinal data. By representing geometry-valued brain states in the Laplacian eigenfunction basis, our framework, LENO, preserves spatial coherence in brain shape evolution while remaining computationally efficient.

A key strength of our framework is its physics-aligned and geometry-aware formulation, which provides both interpretability and practical personalization. LENO represents biomarker fields in a Laplacian eigenfunction basis defined intrinsically on each subject’s brain geometry or network topology, yielding a hierarchical decomposition into spatial modes with clear mathematical meaning. Rather than producing opaque predictions directly in the native spatial domain, the neural operator learns the temporal evolution of these mode coefficients, enabling mechanistic inspection in terms of mode-wise contributions and spatiotemporal interactions. Prior work has shown that Laplacian eigenmodes capture biologically meaningful multi-scale spatial patterns in brain data22, supporting the interpretability of this representation. At the same time, our framework learns a shared nonlinear evolution operator that captures cohort-level disease dynamics, and then performs parameter-efficient personalization for new subjects through a lightweight adaptation component (e.g., temporal scaling / linear recalibration), which adjusts how the shared evolution model is instantiated for an individual without modifying the learned disease dynamics. This design reflects clinical reality: while AD exhibits common pathological mechanisms, patients differ substantially in progression rate and trajectory30. Consequently, the proposed operator-learning framework supports robust forward prediction on irregular brain geometries and enables stable patient-specific adaptability under limited longitudinal observations, while maintaining consistency with the learned disease evolution process.

The clinical and scientific significance of this framework lies in its capacity to learn interpretable, biologically grounded PDE operators that describe disease dynamics in irregular brain geometries, enabling robust forward prediction and patient-specific adaptation through transfer learning. Traditional models of AD progression have relied on either simplified compartmental approaches or purely statistical predictions, often lacking spatial resolution or mechanistic interpretability. Our model addresses several limitations of traditional approaches by incorporating anatomical connectivity, which plays a key role in shaping biomarker diffusion and accumulation, thereby providing a more biologically grounded representation of neurodegenerative spread.

The stage-dependent connectivity patterns revealed by our model align closely with established theories of AD propagation, notably the network diffusion hypothesis proposed by Raj et al.31. Specifically, our inferred propagation pathways capture the well-characterized transition of tau pathology from localized regions in CN individuals to extensive limbic and neocortical involvement in AD, mirroring neuropathological Braak staging. This spatial progression is accompanied by increasingly complex coupling between amyloid, tau, and neurodegeneration, particularly within temporoparietal and insular cortices, consistent with findings from longitudinal multimodal imaging studies16,32. Our ability to reconstruct these detailed biomarker interdependencies and their evolving network topology from limited patient-specific SUVR data underscores the potential of data-driven PDE learning to capture multi-scale neurodegenerative processes in a personalized manner.

Moreover, the integration of cognitive decline dynamics as a downstream outcome of neurodegeneration bridges molecular pathology with clinical presentation. The ability to predict cognitive trajectories directly from imaging biomarkers highlights the translational relevance of the proposed framework, offering a quantitative link between molecular pathology and clinical manifestation. By linking network-level biomarker evolution to cognitive scores, our framework enables a more holistic understanding of disease progression that transcends static biomarkers alone.

Critically, the introduction of treatment effect terms within the PDE framework allows simulation of personalized therapeutic interventions, offering an in silico platform for exploring the differential impacts of anti-amyloid and anti-tau therapies on cognitive outcomes. Simulations within this framework suggest that anti-tau-targeted interventions may lead to more consistent cognitive improvements, which is broadly consistent with emerging clinical trends. This capability could accelerate the development and refinement of targeted treatments by predicting patient-specific responses based on their unique biomarker and network profiles, highlighting the utility of mechanistic models for guiding therapy optimization.

From a methodological perspective, the LENO framework’s reliance on Laplacian eigenfunctions as spatial basis functions facilitates efficient learning on irregular brain geometries and diverse network topologies. The demonstrated robustness to geometric variation and heterogeneity across patients, enabled by transfer learning through personalized temporal scaling, provides a scalable approach to modeling AD across populations. This adaptability is crucial for capturing the complex inter-individual variability that challenges conventional modeling techniques.

Despite the promising results, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, part of the evaluation relies on synthetic data, which are used as a simplifying assumption to examine model behavior and verify the feasibility of the proposed PDE learning framework under controlled conditions. While such data facilitate systematic analysis, they cannot fully capture the complexity and variability of real-world disease progression. For this reason, synthetic experiments are complemented by evaluations on realistic longitudinal neuroimaging data, which provide concrete validation and illustrative insight into disease dynamics.

Second, the cohort size in this study is relatively modest (N = 100), which is consistent with many longitudinal neuroimaging datasets but results in sparse and irregular temporal sampling. To mitigate this issue, our training strategy explicitly handles large temporal gaps by subdividing observed intervals into smaller integration steps during operator evolution. These intermediate steps do not correspond to additional imaging data but enable finer-grained time propagation, reduce temporal discretization error, and enhance the expressive capacity of the learned operator over long horizons. Supervision is applied only at the original observation times, ensuring that learning remains constrained by available data while benefiting from stable continuous-time modeling under irregular sampling and missing visits.

Future work will focus on validation using larger and more diverse cohorts, extension to fully real-world longitudinal datasets, and incorporation of additional modalities and clinical variables to further assess robustness and generalizability.

In conclusion, this work presents a versatile and interpretable PDE-based modeling framework that integrates clinical imaging data, network neuroscience, and nonlinear dynamics to study AD progression. Rather than offering a definitive clinical solution, the proposed approach represents a step toward personalized and mechanistically informed disease modeling. With further validation and extension, this framework has the potential to advance fundamental understanding of AD pathology and its network-driven spread, as well as to contribute to digital twin methodologies for analyzing future biomarker states and exploring therapeutic strategies in neurodegenerative disorders.

Remark 1

(Training strategy) Although the longitudinal data are irregularly sampled and patients do not share identical imaging time points, the neural operator is trained using each patient’s available follow-up schedule. Specifically, for each patient, the observed data at their individual time points are used to construct the training pairs. To handle large temporal gaps between consecutive observations, each observed interval is further divided into multiple smaller time steps. These intermediate steps do not correspond to additional imaging data; rather, they are introduced to enable finer-grained time stepping during operator evolution. This strategy reduces temporal discretization error and enhances the expressive capacity of the learned operator over longer time intervals. During training, the operator is evolved through these smaller steps, while supervision is applied only at the original observed time points. In this way, the learned operator remains constrained by the available longitudinal data while benefiting from more stable and accurate time propagation, enabling effective learning under irregular sampling and missing visits.

Remark 2

The learned operators reflect patterns present in the data under the imposed modeling assumptions and provide insights into disease progression dynamics. Unlike generic operator-learning architectures such as DeepONet20 or Fourier Neural Operators36, whose bases (e.g., trunk networks or Fourier modes) are not explicitly linked to the governing differential operator, LENO employs eigenfunctions derived from the underlying PDE. These eigenfunctions have been shown to capture biologically meaningful spatial patterns in brain data (e.g., ref. 22). In this formulation, the neural network coefficients correspond to functionals in the dual space of the eigenfunction basis, yielding a structured representation that combines physics-aligned spatial modes with data-driven evolution coefficients, thereby enhancing interpretability.

Transfer learning strategy

To accommodate inter-patient heterogeneity in disease progression rates, we also applied transfer learning by fixing the nonlinear neural operator and introducing a patient-specific disease progression score s = γt + β. The scaling parameter γ was optimized for each new patient, while most of the model structure remained unchanged.

Mechanistic connectivity analysis using the operator Jacobian

After training, the learned neural operators are used to predict future system dynamics across different stages of disease progression. To extract mechanistic insights, we compute the Jacobian of each neural operator at representative data points corresponding to clinical stages such as MCI and AD. This analysis reveals interpretable patterns of directional influence and quantifies effective interaction lengths between brain regions, shedding light on the underlying connectivity structure inferred from data.

Synthetic data simulation from 2D brain-shaped geometries

We consider unstructured, brain-shaped 2D geometries (referred to as slip 1 and slip 2) in Fig. 2. These geometries were designed to approximate realistic cortical boundaries while preserving heterogeneous spatial connectivity patterns.

The synthetic biomarker distributions were obtained by numerically solving a system of reaction-diffusion PDEs (Eq. 13) incorporating biologically inspired nonlinear interaction terms and no-flux boundary conditions. Reaction-diffusion PDEs have been widely applied to model neurodegenerative disease progression, as they capture both the local reaction dynamics—such as amyloid accumulation, tau propagation, and neuronal degeneration—and the spatial diffusion of pathology across connected brain regions. Specifically, the system of PDEs has the following form11:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{\begin{array}{ll}\frac{dA}{dt}-{\alpha }_{A}\Delta A & ={\lambda }_{A}A({K}_{A}-A),\\ \frac{d\tau }{dt}-{\alpha }_{\tau }\Delta \tau & ={\lambda }_{\tau A}A+{\lambda }_{\tau }\tau ({K}_{\tau }-\tau ),\\ \frac{dN}{dt}-{\alpha }_{N}\Delta N & ={\lambda }_{N\tau }\tau +{\lambda }_{N}N({K}_{N}-N),\\ \frac{dC}{dt} & ={\lambda }_{CN}\int \,N+{\lambda }_{C}C({K}_{C}-C),\end{array}\right.$$\end{document}dAdt-αAΔA=λAA(KA-A),dτdt-ατΔτ=λτAA+λττ(Kτ-τ),dNdt-αNΔN=λNττ+λNN(KN-N),dCdt=λCN∫N+λCC(KC-C),subject to no-flux boundary conditions. This modeling approach is consistent with the amyloid cascade hypothesis and prion-like trans-synaptic spreading mechanisms implicated in AD pathology19,31.

The initial conditions for each simulation were derived from PET imaging data of 20 patients to provide physiologically plausible baseline biomarker distributions.

SUVR data from 3D brain networks

The SUVR maps were spatially normalized and parcellated according to the Desikan-Killiany atlas, resulting in N = 68 cortical regions per patient. To incorporate individual-specific anatomical constraints, we constructed personalized brain networks using diffusion MRI tractography data for each patient. The anatomical connectivity was represented by an adjacency matrix W, where Wij denotes the fiber connection strength between brain regions i and j. From this, the graph Laplacian was computed as L = D−W, with D being the diagonal degree matrix defined by Dii = ∑jWij.

We then computed the first K = 48 Laplacian eigenfunctions for each patient-specific brain network. These eigenfunctions act as spatial basis functions in the LENO model, effectively encoding the underlying topological constraints of the brain’s network structure and supporting the learning of spatiotemporal biomarker dynamics. The number of eigenfunctions was selected based on a trade-off between accuracy and computational cost, and this choice yielded stable training and reliable predictions in both synthetic and patient data experiments.

We trained our model using the available longitudinal SUVR data for each patient, reserving the later time points for prediction. To quantify model performance, we computed relative accuracy metrics for both training and prediction phases.
