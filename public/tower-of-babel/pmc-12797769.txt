Introduction

With the increasing aging of the population and the prevalence of cancer risk factors, the incidence of cancer has been rising year by year and has become a major public health problem, which threatens global human health [1]. According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), there were approximately 19.96 million new cancer cases and about 9.74 million deaths worldwide in 2022 [2]. With the emergence and development of precision medicine, the treatment strategies for cancer include not only traditional standardized treatments like surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy, but also advanced technologies like targeted therapy, immunotherapy, gene therapy, photodynamic therapy and nanoparticles [3], which have significantly prolonged the survival period of cancer patients [4]. However, affected by the disease and treatment, cancer patients suffer various physical, psychological and social burdens, including physical discomforts such as pain [5], cancer-related fatigue [6], and body image disorder [7], as well as psychological and social distress such as anxiety and depression [8], disease stigmatization [9], and economic toxicity [10]. These burdens lead to low accessibility of social resources for patients with long-term cancer, showing loss of interest and social withdrawal, and weakening their willingness and ability to participate in society, which presents a state of social isolation characterized by actively reducing social activities and staying away from social groups [11, 12].

Social isolation for cancer patients refers to the lack of connection and interaction with others and society, or the feeling of being excluded and isolated by others and society due to unfulfilled social relationships, distancing themselves from social groups and presenting a state of self-isolation, which leads to a lack of goals, meaning and value in life [13]. Research shows that the incidence of social isolation for cancer patients is as high as 20.5% to 46.5% [14]. Social isolation not only aggravates the psychological burden of cancer patients and reduces their social well-being, but also increases the risk of tumor recurrence and progression, as well as the risk of death, thereby reducing the quality of life and survival rate of patients [13, 15]. Accurately assessing the social isolation problem of cancer patients and formulating effective intervention measures are of great significance for promoting cancer patients’ return to families and society and improving their quality of life [16].

At present, there are many types of assessment tools for social isolation of cancer patients, which vary in terms of measurement contents, scoring methods, etc. Moreover, there is a lack of systematic evaluation of their psychometric properties, which results in a lack of reference basis for medical staff when choosing assessment tools. Therefore, this study aims to follow the PRISMA-COSMIN for OMIs 2024 guideline [17, 18] to evaluate the methodological quality and psychometric properties of social isolation assessment tools for cancer patients, and screens out high-quality assessment tools, which provide reference for the assessment and empirical research of social isolation for cancer patients in the future.

Methods

This protocol will follow the checklist of PRISMA-P 2015 [19]. This systematic review will be conducted in accordance with PRISMA-COSMIN for OMIs 2024 guideline [17, 18]. It also has been registered at PROSPERO under the identifier CRD420251041263 [20].

Search strategy

The search strategy combines synonyms and MeSH terms with the ‘OR’ operator to search for studies related to the psychometric properties of social isolation assessment tools for cancer patients in databases such as CNKI, WANFANG, VIP, CBM, PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL and Embase. The search terms relate to ‘Neoplasm/Tumor/Cancer/Malignant Neoplasm’, ‘Social isolation/Social exclusion/Social alienation’, ‘Scale/tool/questionnaire/assessment’ and ‘reliab/valid/cross-cultural equivalence’. The initial search strategy for PubMed can be found in Supplementary material 1. Through the literature retrospective method, we will search for the references of review studies on social isolation assessment tools for cancer patients to ensure the comprehensiveness of the included literature.

Study selection

Two researchers who have completed the study of the COSMIN guidelines independently screen the references that are retrieved in accordance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria using the software Endnote V.20. They first screen initially through reading the titles and abstracts. And re-screen by reading the full text to determine the final studies that are included. We will resolve cases of disagreement by the research group discussing. The study selection procedure will be presented in the PRISMA flow chart in Fig. 1.

Study characteristics

Quality assessment

Two researchers independently evaluate the methodological quality of the included studies, the psychometric properties of the assessment tools and the level of evidence. In cases of disagreement, we will discuss the issues to be resolved by the research group.

Methodological quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies will be evaluated using the COSMIN risk of bias checklist version 3 which has 10 domains and 116 items covering PROM development, content validity, structural validity, internal consistency, cross-cultural validity/measurement invariance, reliability, measurement error, criterion validity, hypotheses testing for construct validity, responsiveness [21]. Each item will be assessed separately using a five-point rating system: “very good (V)”, “adequate (A)”, “doubtful (D)”, “inadequate (I)” and “not applicable (NA)”. The included studies adopt the “worst score counts” principle which refers to the lowest rating for any item as the rating for the study [21]. For example in Table 2.

Each result is rated based on the criteria for good measurement properties that are classified as either sufficient (+), insufficient (–), or indeterminate (?). Criteria for good measurement properties

AUC area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, CFA confirmatory factor analysis, CFI comparative fit index, CTT classical test theory, DIF differential item functioning, EFA exploratory factor analysis, ICC intraclass correlation coefficient, IRT item response theory, LoA limits of agreement, MIC minimal important change, PCA principal component analyses, RMSEA root mean square error of approximation, SEM standard error of measurement, SDC smallest detectable change, SRMR standardized root mean residuals, TLI Tucker-Lewis index

First, we evaluate each study for its psychometric properties. And then, we will synthesize the findings to provide an overall assessment of the quality of the PROMs. When the evaluations are consistent, results from different studies on the same psychometric property are qualitatively summarized or combined using meta-analysis, leading to a final rating of either sufficient (+) or insufficient (−) [17]. Meta-analysis will be conducted using RevMan 5.4.1 when quantitative data on psychometric properties are available. If significant heterogeneity is detected, we will conduct subgroup analyses to explore potential sources of inconsistency, such as study population or study methodology.

Grading the quality of evidence

The evidence rating of each measurement property for PROM will be conducted using the modified Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [23]. The quality of evidence grades starts from assuming “high” quality evidence to be downgraded based on four criteria: risk of bias (low quality of the studies), inconsistency (of the results of the studies), indirectness (evidence comes from different populations), and imprecision (wide confidence intervals or low sample sizes), which is classified into four levels: “high”, “moderate”, “low”, and “very low” [21]. The final recommendations are given in combination with psychometric properties and evidence levels.

Discussion

Although there are many types of assessment tools for social isolation of cancer patients at present, there is a lack of systematic evaluation of their psychometric properties. The findings from this review may identify problems with assessment tools, such as insufficient reliability and validity, and poor applicability, and help improve the quality of assessment results and provide a more scientific and accurate basis for relevant decisions.

This systematic review may assist medical staff in selecting appropriate social isolation assessment tools for cancer patients, which provide guidance for clinical psychological assessment of cancer patients, and enhance work efficiency. At the same time, the problems revealed by the systematic review will drive developers to improve and perfect the social isolation assessment tools for cancer patients, developing more advanced and applicable assessment tools and promoting the overall progress of the assessment field.

The existing limitations of our systematic review need to be discussed. Despite the search covering eight databases, only Chinese and English studies will be included, and we will also not include gray literature in the search strategy.
