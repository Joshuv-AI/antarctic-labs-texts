Targeted modulation of pennycress lipid droplet proteins impacts droplet morphology and seed oil content
Authors: Athanas Guzha, Barsanti Gautam, Damiano Marchiafava, Julius Ver Sagun, Tatiana Garcia, Brice A. Jarvis, Allison M. Barbaglia‐Hurlock, Christopher Johnston, Erich Grotewold, John C. Sedbrook, Ana Paula Alonso, Kent D. Chapman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYLipid droplets (LDs) are unusual organelles that have a phospholipid monolayer surface and a hydrophobic matrix. In oilseeds, this matrix is nearly always composed of triacylglycerols (TGs) for efficient storage of carbon and energy. Various proteins play a role in their assembly, stability and turnover, and even though the major structural oleosin proteins in seed LDs have been known for decades, the factors influencing LD formation and dynamics are still being uncovered mostly in the “model oilseed” Arabidopsis. Here we identified several key LD biogenesis proteins in the seeds of pennycress, a potential biofuel crop, that were correlated previously with seed oil content and characterized here for their participation in LD formation in transient expression assays and stable transgenics. One pennycress protein, the lipid droplet associated protein‐interacting protein (LDIP), was able to functionally complement the Arabidopsis ldip mutant, emphasizing the close conservation of lipid storage among these two Brassicas. Moreover, loss‐of‐function ldip mutants in pennycress exhibited increased seed oil content without compromising plant growth, raising the possibility that LDIP or other LD biogenesis factors may be suitable targets for improving yields in oilseed crops more broadly.

INTRODUCTION

The finite supply of petroleum‐derived products as well as the adverse environmental effects they pose coupled with the ever‐increasing global human population has spawned the need to develop more sustainable biofuel alternatives (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, 2022; Kong et al., 2019). Commonly, soybean (Glycine max L.) and canola (Brassica napus) have been grown as traditional oil crops used in diverse applications ranging from foodstuffs to biofuels (United States Department of Agriculture Foreign Agricultural Service, 2013; Volkova et al., 2016). However, the production of dedicated biofuel crops poses a threat to food security through reduced allocation of land and other resources for food crop production. One solution to this potential problem is the introduction of crop species able to complete their growth cycles within the “off season” of main food crops, consequently fitting in a traditional crop rotation without compromising the cultivation of important food crops.

Pennycress (Thlaspi arvense L.), a member of the Brassicaceae family, has emerged as a sustainable biofuel crop option because of its short winter growing cycle (Chopra et al., 2019; Sedbrook et al., 2014), which allows for it to be incorporated as an intermediate oilseed crop conferring cover crop benefits in a traditional corn‐soybean rotation. The ecological benefits that pennycress provides as a winter cover include conservation of soil, retention of nutrients in farm fields, suppression of weeds, and provision of a food source for pollinators (McGinn et al., 2019; Moore et al., 2020). Additionally, pennycress naturally produces copious amounts of seeds containing up to 36% oil and over 20% protein (Arias et al., 2023; Johnston et al., 2022; Sedbrook et al., 2014). Studies have shown the superiority of biofuels derived from pennycress seed oil, especially at low temperatures making it a suitable biofuel alternative for the aviation industry (Fan et al., 2013). In addition, combustion of pennycress‐derived biofuels produces 50% less greenhouse gas emissions compared to fossil fuels (Fan et al., 2013). The added ability to metabolically alter the fatty acid composition of pennycress oils means that there is greater potential of producing biofuels with minimum greenhouse gas emissions (Chopra et al., 2020; Esfahanian et al., 2021; Jarvis et al., 2021). Efforts to improve the agronomic traits of the crop are ongoing, and the fact that the pennycress diploid genome has almost 90% nucleotide sequences similar to Arabidopsis thaliana makes targeted and predictive genetic engineering approaches attractive (Dorn et al., 2015; Marks et al., 2021).

Pennycress, like most oilseeds, stores seed oil in the form of triacylglycerols (TGs) with a composition that includes the monounsaturated fatty acid, erucic acid (C22:1), comprising up to a half of the total seed fatty acids (Arias et al., 2023; Moser et al., 2009). Pennycress TGs are stored in cytoplasmic LDs present in the seeds, and these TGs serve as carbon and energy reserves that are mobilized during seed germination and early seedling development. LDs are unique organelles present in essentially all plant cells and consist of a hydrophobic core filled with neutral lipids encapsulated in a half‐unit phospholipid membrane embedded with different proteins (Guzha et al., 2023; Scholz et al., 2022). The proteins associated with LDs are responsible for formation, stability, and turnover of the LDs and these protein components differ across plant tissues (Guzha et al., 2023; Scholz et al., 2022). While the inventory of proteins associated with LDs is increasing in recent years with the development of more sensitive proteomics methods (Guzha et al., 2023; Kretzschmar et al., 2020), the precise function(s) of many of these proteins remains largely unexplored, especially outside of Arabidopsis. For instance, it has been shown that the overexpression of Arabidopsis SEIPIN1 involved in LD biogenesis results in the increased accumulation of TGs in Arabidopsis seeds (Cai et al., 2015). The overexpression of LDAPs in Arabidopsis leaves induced an increase in number of cytoplasmic LDs and leaf neutral lipid content (Gidda et al., 2013, 2016). However, despite recognizing the importance of these packaging proteins to compartmentalize storage lipids (Vanhercke et al., 2014), how these LD proteins might be exploited to improve oilseed crop yields has received limited attention.

To gain more insights into the proteins that modulate neutral lipid abundance, an unbiased transcriptome wide‐association study was performed previously using embryos from 22 pennycress accessions with varying oil yields at three developmental stages: 10 days post anthesis (DPA), 16 DPA, and mature embryos (Arias et al., 2023). Numerous gene candidates were identified that were differentially expressed, including some encoding LD associated proteins such as SEIPIN1, Lipid Droplet Associated Proteins (LDAP2 and LDAP3), and LDAP‐Interacting Protein (LDIP). We further investigated transcript abundances in embryos from a high oil (TAMN106) and a low oil pennycress accession (Ames 32872) at four developmental stages (10, 14, 17, and 20 DPA). Similar expression profiles were observed for the same development stages as before, and several additional genes of interest were identified that had not been evident in the study by Arias et al. (2023). Although the LD associated proteins are highly conserved with those identified in Arabidopsis, there is little information about how these proteins might participate in the accumulation of oils in emerging oilseed crops like pennycress. Consequently, we characterized these pennycress LD‐related proteins for their role in the compartmentation of storage lipids. As hypothesized, the ectopic expression of pennycress SEIPIN1, LDAP1, LDAP3, and LDIP in leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana resulted in increased numbers of cytoplasmic LDs and higher amounts of total neutral lipid content. Transient expression of translational fusions of eGFP with SEIPIN1, LDAP1, LDAP2, LDAP3, and LDIP confirmed a LD or LD‐ER localization. Because seed oil content in Arabidopsis was altered in ldip loss‐of‐function mutants (Coulon et al., 2020; Pyc et al., 2017), pennycress LDIP was selected for further study. CRISPR/Cas9 ldip knockouts were developed and pennycress ldip seeds and pollen revealed the presence of aberrant, large‐sized LDs like those observed previously in Arabidopsis ldip lines (Pyc et al., 2017, 2021). Pennycress LDIP restored wild‐type phenotypes when expressed in the Arabidopsis ldip background, highlighting the evolutionary conservation of components fundamental for LD formation. Importantly, disruption of ldip in pennycress increased the seed oil content in mature pennycress seeds by over 5% of the seed dry weight, with ldip knockout plants growth and yield characteristics indistinguishable from wild‐type plants. Taken together, we provide evidence that several key pennycress LD‐related proteins are functionally conserved relative to those in Arabidopsis, and LDIP, in particular, may represent a potential yield target in oilseed crops more broadly.

RESULTS

Pennycress lipid‐related gene expression during embryo development

Previous transcriptome‐wide associated studies of embryos from 22 pennycress accessions grown under the same greenhouse conditions led to the identification of gene candidates correlating with oil yield in mature pennycress seeds (Arias et al., 2023; Johnston et al., 2022). The analysis carried out by Arias et al. (2023) presented a comprehensive screening of 22 pennycress accessions at three embryo developmental stages (10 DPA, 16 DPA, and maturity). They reported 2134 transcripts correlated with fatty acid processes such as lipid synthesis, editing, degradation, export, transport, and storage. Most of the genes reported by Arias et al. (2023) were identified in our analysis here, using a high oil and low oil pennycress accession. However, we discovered additional genes that Arias et al. (2023) did not identify, likely due to our inclusion of more development stages (10 DPA, 14 DPA, 17 DPA, and 20 DPA) and our focus on two highly contrasting lines in terms of lipid content.

Our group also previously evaluated genome diversity of the aforementioned 22 accessions and improved genome annotations (García Navarrete et al., 2022). Briefly, phylogenetic tree reconstructions, principal component analysis, and discriminant analysis of principal components through Bayesian clustering were carried out with the same pennycress samples evaluated for Arias et al. (2023). Among the 22 pennycress samples, 60607 SNPs in coding regions were identified, out of which the low oil accession (Ames 32872), originally from Armenia, contributed 34755 unique SNPs, indicating high divergence from the other accessions. In contrast, in the high oil accession (TAMN106) from Minnesota‐USA, 259 SNPs were identified. This extensive differentiation of Ames 32872 was also reported using genotyping‐by‐sequencing (GBS) by Frels et al. (2019). Both studies suggested that divergence is likely due to pennycress origin in Eurasia and its subsequent migration from Europe to the American continent, starting in Canada and later spreading throughout the United States.

Here, selected lipid‐related gene expression was examined in embryos of high‐ and low‐oil accessions (TAMN106 and Ames 32872, respectively) at four (4) developmental timepoints to span the period of maximum seed oil accumulation (Figure 1). The heatmap in Figure 1(A) shows the expression of numerous genes that were correlated with oil content including HYDROSTEROID DEHYDROGENASE (HSD1‐AT5G50600), ALPHA/BETA HYDROLASEs (AT5G11650 and AT2G47630), STEROL METHYLTRANSFERASE (AT5G13710), RABA3 homolog (AT1G01200), LDAP3 (AT3G05500), ACYL‐COA‐BINDING PROTEIN 6 (ACBP6‐AT1G31812), RNA BINDING PROTEIN (AT5G06210), LDAP2 (AT2G47780), MONOACYLGLYCEROL LIPASE (AT2G39400), SEIPIN1 (AT5G16460), LYSOPHOSPHATIDYLETHANOLAMINE ACYLTRANSFERASE1 (LPEAT1‐AT1G80950), OLEOSIN (AT4G25140) and LDIP (AT5G16550). The expression of some storage lipid related genes including SEIPIN1, MONOACYLGLYCEROL LIPASE, and LPEAT1 increased during embryo development in both accessions. The expression of LDIP and OLEOSIN was similar in both the low and high oil accessions. LDAP3 expression was higher in the high oil line while LDAP2 was more highly expressed in the low oil line. Confocal laser scanning microscopy performed on pennycress embryos stained with BODIPY 493/503 (a neutral lipid specific fluorescent dye) at 10, 14, 17, and 20 DPA provided a visual readout of the marked accumulation of LDs as the embryo matures (green fluorescence, Figure 1B). The increase in the number of LDs in the embryos is accompanied by a change in the general LD morphology from the larger sized LDs at early developmental stages of the embryos (10–17 DPA) to the very small LDs in the embryos at maturity.

Expression of SEIPIN1, LDAP1, LDAP2, and LDAP3 in N. benthamiana leaves

Studies performed in Arabidopsis have shown that SEIPIN1, LDAP1, LDAP2, and LDAP3 (Brocard et al., 2017; Cai et al., 2015; Gidda et al., 2016) localize to the ER‐LD junction or to LDs themselves. To that end, we also expected the pennycress homologs of LDAP1, LDAP2, LDAP3, and SEIPIN1 to localize to LDs. (Note: Despite the absence of LDAP1 transcript in developing embryos, we included it in the study since the pennycress genome had indicated the presence of the three LDAP genes.) We, therefore, constructed eGFP in‐frame fusions to the N‐terminus of coding sequences of the selected genes. These sequences were co‐expressed in transient assays with the ER marker CFP‐HDEL (Szymanski et al., 2007) in N. benthamiana leaves using Agrobacterium infiltration followed by confocal laser‐scanning microscopy (CLSM). The LDs were stained with Nile red (a neutral‐lipid specific fluorescent dye with spectral characteristics that do not interfere with GFP) to enable evaluation of LD localization. The four GFP‐tagged fusion proteins localized to punctate structures that also stained with Nile red suggesting LD localization (Figure 2A–D). We noted that the ectopic expression of SEIPIN1 resulted in the reorganization of the ER from a web‐like reticulate structure to form discrete spherical CFP‐HDEL structures that also stained with Nile red suggesting ER‐LD localization (Figure 2A) as reported previously for Arabidopsis SEIPIN1 (Cai et al., 2015). Alignments of the polypeptide sequences for these proteins from Arabidopsis and pennycress as well as the models of the predicted protein structures using Alphafold2 (Jumper et al., 2021) shows high identity and similar 3D structures, consistent with a conserved subcellular (Figures S1 and S2).

Coding DNA sequences of the four pennycress LD associated proteins (SEIPIN1, LDAP1, LDAP2, and LDAP3) with an eGFP tag were cloned and expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves for localization.

(A–D) Representative confocal laser scanning microscopy (Z‐sections) of N. benthamiana leaves transformed with SEIPIN1 (A), LDAP1 (B), LDAP2 (C), and LDAP3 (D) shows that the proteins localize to LDs (arrows). The LDs were stained with Nile red, while HDEL‐CFP (cyan) was used as an ER marker. Blue is autofluorescence from the chloroplasts. Images are representative for at least ten images from nine leaves derived from three independent transformations for each protein. Bars = 10 μm.

We also tested if the pennycress homologs of SEIPIN1, LDAP1, LDAP2, and LDAP3 behaved in a similar manner in N. benthamiana leaves by modulating LD abundance and lipid content given their conserved amino acid sequences, predicted protein structures, and subcellular localizations. Arabidopsis LEAFY COTYLEDON2 (LEC2) regulates lipid accumulation factors in embryos (Stone et al., 2001) and induces the formation of LDs and increase storage lipid content in N. benthamiana leaves (Petrie et al., 2010). The DNA coding sequences of Pennycress SEIPIN1, LDAP1, LDAP2, and LDAP3 were cloned without a fluorescent protein tag and infiltrated alone or in combination with LEC2 into N. benthamiana leaves. LEC2 was also infiltrated alone as a positive control. Two negative controls also were included for comparison, that is, (1) a mock infiltration control with just infiltration media and (2) infiltration with cultures containing sequences for P19 [tomato bushy stunt virus protein to suppress post‐transcriptional gene silencing (Voinnet et al., 2003)], which was co‐infiltrated with in all ectopic expression experiments. The infiltrated leaves were observed using CLSM for changes in LD abundance and morphology at approximately four (4) days after infiltration. Ectopic expression of SEIPIN1 induced an increase in LDs (stained here with BODIPY493/503 and false‐colored green) compared to the negative controls, and co‐expression with LEC2 did not result in any additive effect on the abundance of LDs (Figure 3A). Ectopic expression of LDAP1 and LDAP3 resulted in the increased proliferation of LDs (Figure 3B,D). However, LDAP2 overexpression did not induce any significant proliferation of LDs (Figure 3C). Quantification of the LD abundance confirmed the trend where LDAP2 did not induce a significant increase in LD numbers while SEIPIN1, LDAP1, and LDAP3 induced an 83%, 150%, and 260% increase in LDs numbers, respectively compared to the mock infiltrated leaves (Figure 3E–H). RT‐PCR confirmed all DNAs were expressed even in the case of LDAP2, where there were no observable LD effects observed (Figure S3). Similarly, the quantification of the neutral lipid abundance (as total fatty acid in the neutral lipid fraction) in the infiltrated leaves indicated an increased accumulation of storage lipids in N. benthamiana leaves for all the candidate proteins except LDAP2 (Figure 3I–L) as expected by the observable proliferation of LDs. No additive effect was observed in N. benthamiana leaves co‐infiltrated with LEC2. To determine if the alteration in LD abundance in infiltrated leaves affected the lipid composition, a fatty acid composition analysis was performed on infiltrated N. benthamiana leaves. Generally, there was an increase in the abundance of the 18:2 and 18:3 FA species when LDAP2 and LDAP3 were ectopically expressed in N. benthamiana leaves (Figure S4). There were no significant changes observed in the total leaf protein content for the four candidates under investigation (Figure S5).

Pennycress LDIP localizes to LDs and its altered expression modulates LD morphology and seed oil content

Results from the ectopic expression of SEIPIN1, LDAP1, LDAP2, and LDAP3 in N. benthamiana leaves suggested a conserved functional role of LD associated proteins in pennycress and Arabidopsis. Combined with the finding that loss‐of‐function of Arabidopsis LDIP resulted in increased seed oil content (Pyc et al., 2017), we undertook an in‐depth analysis of pennycress LDIP. Amino acids sequence alignments performed on pennycress LDIP (TaLDIP) with A. thaliana LDIP (AtLDIP), Camelina sativa LDIP (CsLDIP), Jatropha curcas LDIP (JcLDIP), and B. napus LDIP (BnLDIP) revealed the protein is highly conserved in the Brassicaceae family members, with minor variation observed toward the N‐terminus when compared with JcLDIP (Figure S1E).

The TaLDIP protein was localized to LDs when produced transiently as an eGFP‐tagged protein in the leaves of N. benthamiana or when produced in stable, transgenic Arabidopsis lines, and this was consistent with previous reports for Arabidopsis LDIP (Coulon et al., 2020; Pyc et al., 2017, 2021). Pennycress eGFP‐LDIP localized to punctate structures that stained with Nile red (LDs) in N. benthamiana leaves. CFP‐HDEL was co‐infiltrated with TaLDIP as an ER marker (Figure 4A) and demonstrated proximity of LDs and ER. Similarly, TaLDIP‐overexpressing Arabidopsis lines showed an identical LD localization pattern in the epidermal cells of Arabidopsis leaves and in mature pollen grains (Figure 4B,C). Previous studies with Arabidopsis LDIP had revealed the presence of a hydrophobic region in the middle of the polypeptide sequence as well as an amphipathic α‐helix present near the N‐terminus that were reported to be important for targeting to LDs (Olarte et al., 2022; Pyc et al., 2017). Similar to Arabidopsis LDIP (Olarte et al., 2022; Pyc et al., 2017) computational analyses of the pennycress LDIP protein indicated the presence of a similar hydrophobic region in the middle of the polypeptide sequence (Figure 4D), a moderately hydrophobicity toward the N‐terminus, and the presence of an amphipathic α‐helix between amino acid residues 55 and 72 (Figure 4F,G) which is likely to be important for LD targeting of the pennycress LDIP protein.

Like the eGFP‐tagged protein, transient overexpression of untagged TaLDIP also resulted in an increase of BODIPY‐stained LDs in the leaves of N. benthamiana (Figure 5A) and quantification of the LDs indicated a two‐fold increase in LDs abundance compared to the negative controls (Figure 5B). The co‐expression of TaLDIP and LEC2 did not result in any noticeable additive accumulation of LDs in the infiltrated leaves. Similarly, the quantification of neutral lipids abundance using GC–MS revealed about a two‐fold increase in the TaLDIP‐infiltrated leaves when compared with mock‐transfected controls (Figure 5C). The neutral lipid fatty acid compositions in infiltrated N. benthamiana leaves were altered with the expression of TaLDIP resulting in the increased abundance of 16:0. Expression of LEC2 alone increased the abundance of the following fatty acid species: 16:0, 16:1, 18:0, 18:1, 18:2, and 18:3. Co‐expression of LEC2 and TaLDIP increased the abundance of 16:0 and 18:2 fatty acid species (Figure 5D) suggesting that both LDIP and LEC2 influence neutral lipid homeostasis with perhaps some nuanced differences.

To test the effect of LDIP loss‐of‐function in pennycress, TaLDIP‐targeted CRISPR/Cas9 mutant lines were generated in the Spring 32–10 background. Three mutant lines were identified and consisted of a 105 bp deletion line (ldip 105 bp), a 157 bp deletion (ldip 157 bp), and a guanine (G) and thymine (T) insertion (ldip GT) in the coding sequence of TaLDIP (Figure S6; Data S1). Analysis of BODIPY‐stained LDs in seeds of pennycress using CLSM showed the presence of large sized LDs especially in the embryonic axis tissues of ldip 157 bp and ldip GT mutant lines (Figure 6A,E). Similarly, BODIPY staining of LDs in pollen grains revealed the appearance of large sized aberrant LDs in the ldip 157 bp and ldip GT (Figure 6B,F). In contrast, the ldip 105 bp mutant line produced LDs similar to wild‐type Spring 32‐10 LDs in both seeds and pollen. LDs isolated from the mature seeds of the four genotypes were stained with BODIPY and viewed under CLSM revealing the presence of large sized LDs isolated from the ldip 157 bp and ldip GT mutant lines (Figure S7) similar in size to those observed in situ. Arabidopsis Col‐0 and ldip mutants were imaged for reference and showed consistent impacts on LD size (Figure 6C–F) with those reported elsewhere (Pyc et al., 2017, 2021).

The location of the 105‐nucleotide bases in‐frame deletion in the ldip 105 bp mutant likely resulted in the encoded protein missing 35 amino acids near the N‐terminus (blue box Figure 6G), upstream of the region predicted to form an amphipathic α‐helix (red box Figure 6G). In contrast, the ldip 157 bp mutant possesses a 157‐nucleotide bases deletion likely resulting in a frameshift and disruption of the predicted protein product (Figure S6; Data S1). The ldip GT mutant contains separate guanine and thymine nucleotide insertions (Figure S6; Data S1) within the LDIP coding sequence also likely resulting in a frameshift and disruption of the predicted protein product. Hence, it is likely that a mostly functional mutant polypeptide was produced in the ldip 105 bp mutant, yielding an LD phenotype unchanged from wild‐type. On the other hand, the two mutants with disrupted functional coding sequences showed striking, aberrant LD morphology in seeds and pollen.

We further probed the seed LD morphology of the four pennycress genotypes after initiation of germination in the dark. There were no visible differences in LD morphology in cotyledon cells at three (3) days post initiation of germination (3 DPI), while at seven (7) DPI, large‐sized LDs were visible, especially in the ldip 157 bp and ldip GT mutant lines (Figure 7A,B). The seed oil content was significantly higher in both the ldip 157 bp and ldip GT mutant lines (typically at least 5% higher) compared with the Spring 32‐10 background (and the ldip 105 bp line; Figure 7C,D). Relatively higher seed oil content was observed in the ldip 157 bp and ldip GT mutant lines, compared to wild‐type grown side by side over multiple generations in varying growth conditions.

The quantification of neutral lipids during post‐germinative growth showed higher amounts in the ldip 157 bp and ldip GT mutant lines at one (1), three (3), and seven (7) DPI compared to the Spring 32‐10 wild‐type. The ldip 105 bp mutant did not differ in neutral lipid content when compared to the Spring 32‐10 wild‐type at any of the time points. FA composition analysis of mature pennycress seeds did not reveal any major differences except in the 18:3 percentage which was more elevated in the ldip 157 bp line (Figure S8A). A similar analysis performed on germinated seeds/seedlings showed a greater abundance of the 16:0, 18:2, 18:3, 20:2, and 22:1 acyl moieties in the ldip 157 bp and ldip GT mutants at one (1), three (3), and seven (7) DPI compared to Spring 32‐10 (Figure S9) suggesting a delayed mobilization of seed fatty acids. Quantification of the total seed protein content in the mature pennycress seeds of Spring 32‐10 wild‐type compared to the ldip mutants did not reveal any significant differences (Figure S8B), indicating that the effects of LDIP disruption were restricted to storage lipids and did not extend to other seed storage constituents.

Disruption of LDIP increased seed weight but had minimal effects on other plant growth parameters

To determine if the disruption of LDIP in pennycress had an effect on plant growth parameters, the Spring 32‐10 wild‐type and the three ldip mutant lines (ldip 105 bp, ldip 157 bp, and ldip GT) were grown together in growth chambers at least three times. Data for the growth parameters were collected from a minimum of seven plants for each genotype and recorded. There were slight yet significant increases in the 1000 seed weight of the ldip 157 bp and ldip GT mutant lines (Figure S10A) in comparison to the Spring 32‐10 wild‐type, likely resulting from the measured increases in seed oil content in these mutants (Figure 7C). No significant differences were observed among any of the genotypes with respect to the number of seeds per plant, seed yield (weight) per plant, average plant height, germination rate, number of tillers and branches per plant, duration of flowering, and seed area/width/length (Figures S10B–E and S11A–C). With regards to overall plant morphology, no obvious differences were observed between the ldip mutants and the wild‐type (Figure S12).

To test if the aberrant LD morphology observed in the pollen of pennycress ldip 157 bp and ldip GT mutant lines (Figure 6B) resulted in a disruption in pollen germination and growth of the pollen tubes, pollinated wild‐type pistils were tested with pollen from the four genotypes. There were no noticeable differences in terms of the pollen tube lengths when observed and measured at 4 h post pollination (Figure S13).

TaLDIP reversed the LD phenotype of Arabidopsis ldip knockout mutants

Given the similar LD morphology phenotypes in Arabidopsis and pennycress loss‐of‐function mutants, we further tested the functional conservation of pennycress LDIP by its expression in the Arabidopsis ldip mutant background. We generated homozygous, single‐insertion lines of Arabidopsis ldip transformed with pennycress LDIP under control of the CaMV35S promoter. Because GFP‐tagged pennycress LDIP heterologously expressed in the Arabidopsis background had already indicated localization to LDs (Figure 4B,C), we hypothesized pennycress LDIP would functionally substitute for the loss of the Arabidopsis LDIP protein. Four independent Arabidopsis ldip lines transformed with pennycress LDIP (line #5, 21, 23 and 28) displayed a normal LD morphology in mature seeds, comparable to wild‐type Col‐0, a reversal of the supersized LDs in ldip mutants (Figure 8A). Pollen grains from these same lines also had a normal LD morphology, similar to wild‐type Col‐0 (Figure 8B), and opposite from that observed in the ldip mutant. The pennycress ldip GT mutant line was transformed with the same construct (pennycress LDIP under constitutive promoter CaMV35S‐pCaMV35S:TaLDIP) with the same result as in Arabidopsis, reversing the mutant phenotype in both pollen grains and seeds similar to the wild‐type Spring 32‐10 in pollen grains and seeds of two independently transformed lines (#21 and #41, Figures S14 and S15). Quantification of oil content in the seeds of complemented Arabidopsis ldip lines transformed with pennycress LDIP (line #5, 21, 23, and 28) revealed an oil content similar to wild‐type levels, a reversal of the higher seed oil content measured in the ldip mutant (Figure 8C).

DISCUSSION

In recent years, studies on pennycress for its potential use as a biofuel crop have intensified (Arias et al., 2023; Chopra et al., 2020; Cubins et al., 2019; Jarvis et al., 2021; Johnston et al., 2022; Sagun et al., 2023). Studies to genetically manipulate pennycress with the aim of altering seed oil composition have been aided by its close relation to Arabidopsis (Chopra et al., 2018; Dorn et al., 2015; Marks et al., 2021; McGinn et al., 2019). Similar to the genes encoding seed lipid metabolism, the occurrence of the genes involved in LD biogenesis predates angiosperms (de Vries & Ischebeck, 2020). However, there is limited functional information for this cellular machinery in oilseed crops outside of the Arabidopsis model. A more comprehensive investigation for the functional similarities is warranted since several studies have implicated LD‐associated proteins in seed storage lipid content (Alameldin et al., 2017; Kelly et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2013). In fact, recent work across many pennycress accessions indicates an association of seed oil content with transcript abundances of several gene products involved in LD biogenesis (Arias et al., 2023). Here, we demonstrate that although the seed oil composition of pennycress is very different from Arabidopsis, several proteins involved in LD biogenesis exhibit functional conservation between Arabidopsis and pennycress homologs. We used several approaches including heterologous expression, gene editing, and genetic complementation to characterize the gene candidates. Given the universal nature of lipid storage, it may not be that surprising that there is considerable structural and functional conservation among LD biogenesis components in related plant species. This is evidenced by our data that shows the localization of pennycress SEIPIN1, LDAP1, LDAP2, and LDAP3 to LDs and the ability of most of these proteins to modulate LD abundance and lipid content in N. benthamiana leaves. Of note, LDAP2 was highly expressed in the low‐oil pennycress accession and yet did not appear to induce LD proliferation upon ectopic expression in N. benthamiana leaves, despite its confirmed expression by RT‐PCR in these transient assays (Figure S3). It is possible that LDAP2 normally functions to restrict or reduce LD accumulation in pennycress seeds. Alternatively, it may be that to promote LD accumulation in a heterologous system, the TaLDAP2 requires other pennycress seed factors that are not present in N. benthamiana leaves. In Arabidopsis, LDAP proteins interact with LDIP and other LDAPs in yeast 2H assays, so additional proteins may be required for full LDAP2 functionality. Currently, there is no definitive information on the 3D structures of LDAPs, although predictive models can be suggested using algorithms (e.g., Figure S2) with varying degrees of confidence. Further comparisons among LDAPs from different plant species are warranted since there could be species‐specific functional differences among LDAPs that are not readily apparent from amino acid sequence comparisons. In any case, it is important to examine the extent of structural and functional conservation before extrapolation from a model system like Arabidopsis or N. benthamiana to an agriculturally‐relevant oilseed species like pennycress, where LD packaging proteins may represent targets for improving oil yield.

In addition, some nuances for the function of LDIP reported here, especially during seed development, suggest there may have been some overlooked features of LDIP function during seed maturation and oil accumulation. The observed shift in LD morphology during seed maturation could mark a fundamental difference between Arabidopsis and the oilseed crop pennycress, and we suggest that LDIP may participate in this process in pennycress seeds. The packaging of lipids into smaller sized LDs in pennycress seeds may reduce the surface‐to‐volume ratio, potentially facilitating faster LD degradation during post‐germinative growth and supporting accelerated seedling establishment. Although speculative, more rapid and consistent seedling establishment could have been a favorable trait selected during the domestication of pennycress, which may not apply to Arabidopsis. However, this concept will require further investigation in future studies.

LDIP from pennycress was localized to LDs both in transient assays in N. benthamiana as well as in stable transgenic Arabidopsis (Figure 4A–C). The loss of LDIP function in CRISPR/CAS9 pennycress lines resulted in both an alteration in LD morphology (Figure 6) and an increase in seed oil content measured by 1H NMR (Figure 8C) relative to the wild‐type background Spring 32‐10. On the surface, it is a bit puzzling that overexpression of LDIP in N. benthamiana leaves and the loss of function of LDIP in pennycress seeds both result in more oil accumulation. Part of the explanation may be due to tissue specific differences for LD dynamics in leaves and seeds. In leaves, LDs undergo diurnal cycles of formation and depletion (Gidda et al., 2016), while in developing seeds, LD turnover is minimal during the maturation process, and seed filling is the primary process. Here we presume that the excess LDIP produced in leaves “coats” the LD surface and perhaps promotes more binding of endogenous LDAPs (with which LDIP interacts; Pyc et al., 2017). Together, this likely increases the stability of LDs and limits access to lipase enzymes for LD turnover. On the other hand, in seeds LDIP was shown previously to cooperate with the SEIPIN complex for the initiation of LD formation in Arabidopsis (Pyc et al., 2021). Loss of LDIP, like the loss of SEIPIN (Taurino et al., 2018) uncouples TAG synthesis from its normal packaging into LDs. Perhaps without the restriction of a regulated packaging process, this allows for more oil to accumulate in seed cells, resulting in larger, misshapen LDs. It should be emphasized that the same mispackaging and increased seed oil content occur with the loss of SEIPINs (Taurino et al., 2018), the partners of LDIP in LD biogenesis. In pennycress, we propose that LDIP may play an additional role in a fission‐type process that reduces LD size during the course of seed development for more efficient mobilization after germination. Without LDIP in pennycress, perhaps this additional fission process—which may also slow or restrict the time for overall oil accumulation—does not occur on a normal time scale, resulting in larger LDs and increased oil content. While more work will be required to sort out the detailed mechanisms by which LDIP participates in seed oil accumulation, it is clear that LDIP influences both LD morphology and seed oil content in pennycress.

Because there were few notable differences in other growth or developmental characteristics in ldip knockouts, LDIP represents a likely target to enhance seed oil content in pennycress and other oilseeds. Interestingly, a 105 bp deletion resulted in a mutant allele that did not show phenotypic differences from wild‐type, indicating the N‐terminal 35 amino acids of the presumed translation product were dispensable for LDIP function. The targeting of proteins toward LDs is still not fully understood, but it is generally thought the hydrophobic regions and amphipathic α‐helices present on proteins are important for protein targeting to LDs (Prevost et al., 2018; Pyc et al., 2017, 2021). In the case of LDIP, the 35 amino acids at the N‐terminus then is presumed unlikely to be involved in LD targeting since disrupting its cellular location would almost certainly disrupt its function, but this remains to be determined experimentally. Moreover, the N‐terminus of the predicted J. curcas LDIP also had portions absent compared to LDIPs from other Brassicas (Figure S1E) consistent with these sequences at the N‐terminus possibly being dispensable for major functions. Little additional insights could be gleaned from structure predictions since the N‐terminus of LDIP in alpha‐fold structural models (Figure S2) was partially disordered with a partial α‐helix but with low confidence scores. It will be important to obtain accurate structural information for LDIP and these other LD proteins to better understand their function.

In considering the function of LDIP in the packaging of LDs in seeds, we noted a change in general LD size in seeds from early embryo stages to mature seeds, which was especially obvious for pennycress (Figure 9A; Figure S16). There was an overall shift in the size of the LDs from larger‐sized LDs in early development to smaller‐sized LDs at seed maturity, which are extremely small in normal pennycress seeds (Figures 1B and 9A; Figure S15). For Arabidopsis embryos, there did not seem to be a dramatic reduction in LD size from early development stage to maturity. However, in ldip mutants, the LDs in Arabidopsis embryos were much larger at both early development and at maturity. In pennycress ldip mutants, the LDs at early development were somewhat larger in size compared to those in wild‐type pennycress embryos, and they became much larger at maturity without LDIP. We hypothesize that pennycress LDIP may function in early embryo development in cooperation with SEIPIN, similar to Arabidopsis, and also function during maturation to reduce LD size—a feature that seems less active in Arabidopsis but still quantifiable (Figure S16). A proposed model for LD biogenesis suggests that pennycress LDIP is important for the formation of LDs through its interactions with the SEIPIN complex (Figure 9B), and this would be similar for both pennycress and Arabidopsis embryos. The interaction of LDIP with the SEIPIN complex at the ER was necessary for the budding of nascent LDs from the ER to the cytoplasm (Pyc et al., 2017, 2021). The LD monolayer somehow becomes embedded with LDIP and other surface proteins such as LDAPs and oleosins that help in the stability of the LDs. The normal presence of LDIP in the LD surface may help in the modification of LD size from larger sized LDs to smaller sized LDs, especially evident in pennycress, perhaps through a lipid transfer activity from larger LDs to smaller LDs. Or, it is possible that the LDs do not completely detach from the ER, and this facilitates transfer of TAGs to smaller LD through the combined action of LDIP and SEIPINs (Wang et al., 2016). Alternatively, there may be a fission activity that occurs to reduce LD size during seed maturation (Figure 9B), and in pennycress embryos, this process ultimately results in very small LDs. In fact, others showed that there is a dynamic change in LD size during seed development in Arabidopsis through both fusion and fission processes (Miquel et al., 2014; Siloto et al., 2006), and the loss of the major structural proteins such as the oleosins results in abnormally large LDs, similar to the loss of LDIP. The absence of LDIP on the LD surface (in LDIP knockouts) may compromise the stability of the LD monolayer resulting in the fusion of the LDs forming much larger LDs that were observed in the mutants. And/or, perhaps without LDIP protein, the LDs may grow larger in the absence of a functional LDIP‐SEIPIN interaction as TAG biosynthesis continues and the oil accumulates at the ER surface. We favor a model where, coincident with late stages of maturation, there is a fission process that occurs for LDs in oilseeds that allows for a reduction in LD size, packing oil into smaller cytoplasmic LDs, and this process would be dependent upon LDIP. Future studies into the mechanistic action of LDIP will be required to sort out these possible roles for LDIP in LD fission in seeds and other cell types.

In summary, our results demonstrate the conservation of several key LD biogenesis proteins in Arabidopsis and pennycress. The proteins SEIPIN1, LDAP1, LDAP3, and LDIP were able to induce the accumulation of LDs in non‐seed tissues such as N. benthamiana leaves. This induction of LD proliferation in leaves could provide an alternative energy‐dense biomass source for use in the generation of biofuels. Additionally, the generation of LDIP knockouts in pennycress resulted in an increase in seed oil content, suggesting LD proteins could be integral to future engineering strategies to increase the seed oil content of pennycress and other oilseed Brassicas.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

RNA‐seq data analysis

To identify core genes involved in pennycress seed oil accumulation, we used the previous RNAseq data reported by Johnston et al. (2022) for two contrasting pennycress accessions. TAMN106 (PC22) has a high percent of oil content (HO) at maturity, and Ames 32872 (PC2) has a low oil content (LO). Briefly, for the analysis, the data was evaluated with fastQC (Andrews, 2010), and clean data was mapped with HISAT v2.1.0 (Kim et al., 2015) to the pennycress reference genome (Genbank Acc.: GCA_000956625.1; Dorn et al., 2015). The resulting BAM format alignment files were used to calculate transcripts per million (TPM) with StringTie v2.1.1 (Pertea et al., 2016). Finally, we checked the gene expression and focused on genes highly contrasting in the four (4) stages of embryo development (10, 14, 17, 20 days post anthesis [DPA]) in the two lines evaluated. The genes selected were represented in the heatmap diagram (Figure 1) generated with mixOmics package (v. 6.26.0; Rohart et al., 2017) in R (v. 4.1; R Core Team, 2021).

Plant material, growth conditions, and transformations

Arabidopsis plants were grown in soil or on plates containing half‐strength Murashige and Skoog media (Murashige & Skoog, 1962) with hygromycin for selection in a growth chamber at 22°C with a 16‐h‐day/8‐h‐night cycle and 50 μE m−2 sec−1 light intensity. The WT and transgenic plants were grown together at the same time to harvest seed for further studies. All Arabidopsis experiments were conducted in the Col‐0 ecotype background while for pennycress, Spring 32‐10 (inbred 10 generations; McGinn et al., 2019; Nunn et al., 2022) and MN106 (Dorn et al., 2015) were used. Arabidopsis plants stably overexpressing LDIP and eGFP‐LDIP in Col‐0 and ldip background were generated using the floral dip method (Clough & Bent, 1998) with the Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 containing the binary plasmid pMDC32/LDIP or pMDC43/eGFP‐LDIP until single‐copy, homozygous lines overexpressing LDIP or eGFP‐LDIP Arabidopsis were obtained. PCR was used to confirm the presence or absence of transgene.

Nicotiana benthamiana plants used in transient expression experiments were grown on soil at 28°C under a 14/10‐h light/dark cycle in a growth chamber set at 50 μE m−2 sec−1 light intensity. Leaves of four (4)‐week old N. benthamiana plants were infiltrated with A. tumefaciens strain GV3101, carrying appropriate binary vectors, that is, SEIPIN1, LDAP1, LDAP2, LDAP3, and LDIP in pMDC32 and pMDC43. The Tomato bushy stunt virus (TBSV) gene P19 was also included in all infiltrations to suppress post transcriptional gene silencing (Petrie et al., 2010). Detailed procedures for A. tumefaciens growth, transformation, infiltration, and processing of N. benthamiana leaf material for microscopy are described in Cai et al. (2015).

Plasmid construction

All the reagents used were purchased from New England Biolabs (https://www.neb.com/), Thermo Fisher Scientific (https://www.thermofisher.com/) or Invitrogen (http://www.invitrogen.com/), and oligonucleotides were synthesized by Genwiz (http://www.genwiz.com/). Plasmids used for expression in plants were driven by the 35S Cauliflower Mosaic Virus promoter. The full‐length coding DNA sequences of SEIPIN1, LDAP1, LDAP2, LDAP3, and LDIP were amplified from pennycress embryo cDNA using primers listed in Table S1. The PCR products and the plant expression binary vector plasmids pMDC32 and pMDC43 (with N‐terminus GFP) were digested with restriction enzymes PacI and AscI. Cloning of the gene open reading frame into plasmid was performed using T4 DNA ligase before transformation into competent DH5α Escherichia coli cells. Positive transformants were identified through PCR and restriction digestion of the plasmid before sending for automated sequencing (Genewiz). These constructs were used for transient expression in N. benthamiana or for making stable expression lines in Arabidopsis and pennycress.

Multi guide vectors for pennycress ldip mutants

Multi guide constructs for LDIP were created using the pHEE401E plasmid and methods set forth in Fauser et al. (2014). These vectors contained the Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 endonuclease to induce double strand breaks, two (2) guide RNA (expression driven by U6‐26 and U6‐29 promoters) coding sequence with a restriction site for cloning the 19mer protospacer, and the hygromycin phosphotransferase (HPT) gene conferring hygromycin resistance in planta to select for transformants. Protospacer sequences were used as queries in Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) searches of the pennycress genome to confirm no identical sequences were present other than in the LDIP gene to avoid possible off‐target edits. Completed constructs were introduced into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 using a standard CaCl2 flash‐freeze/thaw transformation method (Holsters et al., 1978).

Pennycress agrobacterium‐mediated floral dip

Agrobacterium‐mediated pennycress plant transformations and selection for CRISPR/Cas9 edited mutant lines were carried out as described in McGinn et al. (2019). Surface‐sterilized T1‐generation seeds were plated onto 0.8% agar media containing one half‐strength Murashige and Skoog salts mix. Hygromycin resistance selection was carried out by including 40 μg mL−1 hygromycin B to select for transformants. Seedlings were germinated on plates in a Percival growth chamber set at 21°C, 16:8 (day:night) with a light intensity set at 50 μE m−2 sec−1 light intensity for 8–10 days until the hygromycin resistance present in transgenic seedlings was readily identifiable (the cotyledons of sensitive seedlings turned brown whereas resistant seedlings remained green and had continued root growth). Seedlings were transplanted in potted soil (typically four [4] plants per four‐inch square pot) and grown in environment‐controlled growth chambers cycling 16 h light/8 h dark (light was either 6500K fluorescent or a combination 4100K fluorescent/incandescent lighting, 175–250 μE m−2 sec−1 light intensity), at 21 or 22°C.

Selection of CRISPR/Cas9 edited lines

Leaf tissue was collected from T1 generation plants and genomic DNA extracted using a modified CTAB (cetyltrimethylammonium bromide) protocol (Doyle, 1987). The extracted DNA was used as a template in PCR reactions (primers in Table S1) to identify CRISPR/Cas9‐induced mutations at the protospacer‐targeted locations. PCR products were electrophoresed in agarose gels, then gel‐extracted and purified, followed by Sanger sequencing and sequence analysis using DNASTAR Lasergene software. Inheritance of the mutations was confirmed in subsequent generations, and homozygous lines were chosen which no longer harbored CRISPR/Cas9 transgenic DNA. Absence of the transgenic DNA was confirmed using the aforementioned seed germination assay to screen lines homozygous for hygromycin sensitivity, along with PCR analysis using primer pairs specific to portions of the Cas9 gene (Table S1).

Complementation assays

The plasmids containing the aforementioned constructs for stable expression were transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 using the CaCl2 flash‐freeze/thaw transformation method. The transformed Agrobacteria cultures were then used to transform ldip 105 bp and ldip GT homozygous mutants by floral dipping. Transformants were obtained for each of the rescue constructs dipped in both mutant backgrounds using hygromycin as a selectable marker.

Flowering times

Plants were observed and scored at a daily time for the opening of the first flower on their inflorescences. The same plants were also observed and scored daily at the same daily time for the final flower to open. These time points were relative to the time of transplant of seedlings into soil and to each other for calculating the total flower time window. Plants were grown in environment‐controlled growth chambers cycling 16 h light/8 h dark (light was either 6500K fluorescent or a combination 4100K fluorescent/incandescent lighting, 175–250 μE m−2 sec−1 light intensity), at 21–22°C.

Plant heights

Final plant heights were recorded after the plants had senesced, measuring the length of inflorescence from the base to the top of the main stem and straightening the stem against a measuring stick.

Seed analysis data

Quantifications of length, width, area, total seed weights per plant, and thousand‐seed weight of the different CRISPR mutants and wild‐type were performed using a MARViTECH MARViN 5.0 Seed Analyzer instrument along with an Ohaus STX223 balance. Quantification of oil in mature pennycress and Arabidopsis seeds was performed using a non‐destructive method (1H time‐domain nuclear magnetic resonance) with a Bruker Minispec mq20 (Bruker optics) as described in Horn et al. (2011).

Germination assay

Fifty (50) seeds were plated on freshly prepared one‐half strength MS 0.8% agar media and number of seeds that germinated was recorded on each day.

Microscopy

Confocal laser‐scanning microscopy (CLSM) images were acquired in situ in N. benthamiana leaves, Arabidopsis leaves, seeds and pollen, pennycress seeds and pollen and isolated pennycress seed LDs using a Zeiss LSM 710 confocal laser scanning microscope with Airyscan. Processing of acquired images was done using Zeiss Zen software (Black edition and Blue edition; v. 2011). Infiltrated leaves from four (4)‐weeks old N. benthamiana or Arabidopsis stable expression lines where collected using a hole punch and stained with either BODIPY 493/503 or Nile Red (Sigma‐Aldrich) neutral lipid stains at a concentration of 4 μg mL−1 in 50 mM PIPES buffer, as described in Cai et al. (2015) and Park et al. (2013). Whole Arabidopsis embryos where collected after soaking the seeds in water for 15 min and removing the seed coats by gently rubbing the seeds between a microscopy slide and a cover slip. Pennycress embryos were collected by soaking the seeds in water for 20 min and carefully removing the seed coats with dissection forceps. The embryonic axis was separated from the cotyledons before sectioning with a blade and staining as described for leaves. GFP (and BODIPY), Nile red and chloroplast autofluorescence were excited by a 488‐nm laserx and collected in a spectrum of 500–540, 560–620 and 640–720 nm, respectively. CFP was excited by a 405 laser and the emission signal was collected in a spectrum of 450–490 nm.

In vivo pennycress pollen tube assay

In vivo pollen tube growth assays were performed as described in Muller and Ischebeck (2018) with slight modifications. Pollen from the four genotypes under study was used to pollinate wild‐type pistils. At 4 h after pollination the pistils were cut and stained with aniline blue (Mori et al., 2006). Images were obtained under fluorescence microscope (Zeiss LSM710) and analyzed using ImageJ v.1.54D (Schneider et al., 2012).

LD isolation

Two‐hundred (200) mg of pennycress seeds from Spring 32‐10 and ldip mutants were used to obtain lipid droplet fractions and total cellular fractions. LD‐enriched fractions were obtained as described previously (Horn et al., 2021) and stained with BODIPY for confocal laser scanning microscopy.

Biomass extraction for quantification

Oil and protein were extracted from seedlings as described previously (Cocuron et al., 2014) with some modifications.

Oil extraction: Dried seeds or seedlings (between 2 and 10 mg DW) were ground in a 2 mL Sarstedt tube containing one 5 mm tungsten bead, 590 nmol of triheptadecanoin (C17:0) as an internal standard, and 1 mL of hexane/isopropanol (2:1). Tubes were then placed into a mixer mill and were shaken for 5 min at a frequency of 30 Hz. Samples were centrifuged at 17 000×g for 15 min at room temperature. The supernatant was carefully removed and pipetted into a 13 × 100 mm glass tube. To ensure a total extraction of oil, the steps above were repeated twice more. The pooled oil fractions were dried at 60°C under nitrogen and subjected to neutral lipid isolation.

Neutral lipids were separated from total lipid extract using Supelco Discovery® DSC‐Si SPE cartridges attached to Visiprep™ SPE Vacuum Manifold. The cartridges were initially washed with 3 mL of acetone once and then conditioned with 6 mL of hexane twice. The dried fatty acid extracts were resuspended in 200 μL of hexane and then loaded to the cartridge. Neutral lipids were eluted by adding 5 mL of hexane/diethyl ether (4:1) allowing to flow through the column at 1–2 drops per second to 13 × 100 mm glass collection tube. The eluted neutral lipids were dried at 60°C under nitrogen and stored at −20°C or were subject to methylation.

Fatty acids were methylated using methanolic/HCl. In brief, 150 μL of toluene and 0.5 mL of 3 N methanolic/HCl were added to the dried fatty acid extracts. The samples were transmethylated for 120 min at 80°C. The reaction was quenched using 250 μL of 5% (w/v) sodium bisulfate, and 1 mL of hexane was added to extract FAMEs. Samples were mixed and spun at 800×g for 5 min at room temperature. A 200 μL aliquot of supernatant was transferred into a 2 mL glass vial containing 800 μL of hexane, and FAMEs were ready for GC–MS analysis.

Proteins extraction: Proteins were extracted from the residual pellet after fatty acid extraction. Each tube received 0.5 mL of extraction buffer [20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 1% (w/v) SDS] previously warmed to 42°C. Tubes were then vortexed for 15 min at 42°C. Samples were centrifuged at 17 000×g for 10 min and the supernatant was transferred into a 2 mL tube. The process was repeated once more. The supernatants were combined and ready for quantification.

Quantification of biomass components

FAME and protein extracts were quantified according to previously published methods (Cocuron et al., 2014; Tsogtbaatar et al., 2015). Oil content was determined by GC–MS of FAMEs analyzed using Agilent 6890N GC‐system coupled to Agilent 5975B mass selective detector. FAME derivatives were separated using an Omegawax 250 capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 mm) at a constant flow rate of 1.4 mL min−1. Helium was used as the carrier gas. The GC conditions were as follows: initial temperature was set to 120°C and held for 0.5 min. The oven temperature was then raised to 245°C at 100°C min−1. The injection temperature was fixed at 225°C and the injection mode was set to split with a split ratio of 1:10. Mass spectra were acquired using electron impact ionization in positive ion mode. The ion source and the interface temperatures were set to 240 and 150°C, respectively. GC–MS data were acquired and processed using Enhanced ChemStation software. FAME derivatives were identified using the NIST library and neat FAME standards purchased from Sigma.

Proteins were quantified colorimetrically using the DC Protein Assay kit from Bio‐Rad in a Bio‐Rad SmartSpec Plus spectrophotometer at 750 nm. Bovine serum albumin (BSA) was chosen as the standard to determine the amount of proteins in pennycress embryos or tobacco leaves.
