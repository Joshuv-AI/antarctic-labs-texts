Further results on the free energy of the random field Ising chain in the case of centered disorder
Orphée Collin

Abstract
We study the expansion of the limiting free energy density of the random field Ising chain with centered IID disorder and homogeneous coupling parameter, when the latter goes to infinity. We extend the first order result of [ 4 ] to the general case of finite second moment. Furthermore, we identify the value of the unknown constant appearing in the main theorem of [ 2 ] , which is the first non-universal constant in the expansion of the limiting free energy density. AMS subject classifications (2010 MSC): 34D08, 60K37, 82B44, 60K35. Keywords: random field Ising chain, disordered systems, random matrix products This work is licensed under a Creative Commons Attribution — 4.0 International licence (CC-BY 4.0, https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/).

Introduction

1.1
Overview

Let
\mu
be a law on
\mathbb{R}
such that

\vartheta^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}\mu(\text{\rm d}x)\in(0,\infty)\,,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\mu(\text{\rm d}x)=0\,,

(1)

i.e.,
\mu
has a finite second moment, is centered and different from
\delta_{0}
, and its variance is denoted
\vartheta^{2}
.
We consider, under a probability measure denoted
\mathbb{P}
, an i.i.d. sequence
h=(h_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of real random variables following law
\mu
. We further consider a real positive parameter
J
.

The object of this article is to study, as
N
goes to infinity, the product
M_{1}\dots M_{N}
of the random matrices

M_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{h_{n}}&e^{-2J+h_{n}}\\
e^{-2J-h_{n}}&e^{-h_{n}}\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad n=1,2,\dots

(2)

Following Furstenberg’s theory (as will be detailed in Section
1.2
),
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely, the four coefficients of the matrix
M_{1}\dots M_{N}
all have the same exponential growth rate as
N
goes to infinity, this rate is deterministic, it is called the Lyapunov exponent of this random matrix product and we will denote it by
{\mathcal{F}}(J)
.

Our first main result (Corollary
6
) states that under assumption (
1
), as
J\to\infty
,

{\mathcal{F}}(J)\sim\frac{\vartheta^{2}}{2J}\,.

(3)

In
[
2
]
it was shown under some regularity assumption and under stronger integrability assumption on the law
\mu
that the Lyapunov exponent
{\mathcal{F}}(J)
has the following asymptotic expansion as
J\to\infty
:

{\mathcal{F}}(J)=\frac{\vartheta^{2}}{2J+\kappa}+R(J)\,,

(4)

where the decay of the rest
R(J)
depends on the integrability assumption on
\mu
: see Section
1.3
for a detailed statement.
The expression given in
[
2
]
for the constant
\kappa
is not explicit.
The second main contribution of the present work (Corollary
7
) is to provide a more explicit expression for
\kappa
. This will be done by establishing an expansion up to order
1/J^{2}
of
{\mathcal{F}}(J)
, as
J\to\infty
.
Our technique does not go beyond the order
1/J^{2}
, so it does not enlighten the remarkable simplicity of the expansion in (
4
).

Our approach relies on the physical origins of the problem. The random matrix product indeed expresses the partition function of the random field Ising chain (RFIC) and the Lyapunov exponent
{\mathcal{F}}(J)
can be identified as the limiting free energy density of the RFIC.

We will proceed in two steps. First we will estimate the limiting maximal energy density
\mathcal{M}(J)
of the RFIC (defined in Lemma
1
below). Then we will relate the limiting free energy density
{\mathcal{F}}(J)
with the limiting maximal energy density
\mathcal{M}(J)
: inspired by the description of the typical configurations given for example in
[
3
]
, we will estimate the partition function of the RFIC by considering the contributions given by those configurations which have an energy close to the maximum.

1.2
Definitions and basic facts

For a given realisation of the sequence
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{N})
, for every positive integer
N
, every positive real number
J
, and every pair
(g,d)\in\{+1,-1\}^{2}
, the Ising model on the chain
\{1,\dots,N\}
, with coupling constant
J
, external field
h
, and boundary conditions
(g,d)
is usually defined by the following Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle\{+1,-1\}^{N}

\displaystyle\to{\mathbb{R}}

(5)

\displaystyle\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{N})

\displaystyle\mapsto J\sum_{n=0}^{N}\sigma_{n}\sigma_{n+1}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}h_{n}\sigma_{n}

with the convention
\sigma_{0}=g,\sigma_{N+1}=d
.

We decide to subtract
(N+1)J
to the Hamiltonian so, using the relation
\sigma_{n}\sigma_{n+1}=1-2{\mathbf{1}}_{\sigma_{n}\neq\sigma_{n+1}}
, we rather consider the following Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H_{N,J,h}^{gd}:\qquad\qquad\{+1,-1\}^{N}

\displaystyle\to{\mathbb{R}}

(6)

\displaystyle\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{N})

\displaystyle\mapsto-2J\sum_{n=0}^{N}{\mathbf{1}}_{\sigma_{n}\neq\sigma_{n+1}}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}h_{n}\sigma_{n}

still with the convention
\sigma_{0}=g,\sigma_{N+1}=d
.

Each
configuration

\sigma\in\{+1,-1\}^{N}
of
spins

-1
or
+1
on the
system

\{1,\dots,N\}
is thus assigned a value
H_{N,J,h}^{gd}(\sigma)
, which we will also refer to as its
energy
.
The partition function associated with this Hamiltonian is:

Z_{N,J,h}^{gd}=\sum_{\sigma\in\{+1,-1\}^{N}}\exp\left(H_{N,J,h}^{gd}(\sigma)\right)\,.

(7)

A central object of study in statistical physics is the limiting free energy density associated with a Hamiltonian. Let us introduce it by the following lemma, as well as another quantity of interest for us: the limiting maximal energy density.

For a given realisation of the sequence
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{N})
, for every positive integer
N
, every positive real number
J
, and every pair
(g,d)\in\{+1,-1\}^{2}
, we consider the maximum of the Hamiltonian (or maximal energy):

\displaystyle M_{N,J,h}^{gd}:=\sup_{\sigma\in\{+1,-1\}^{N}}M_{N,J,h}^{gd}(\sigma).

(8)

For the lemma, we only require that
\mu
(sometimes refered to as the disorder law) admits a finite first moment.

Lemma 1
.

Assume that
\mu
has a finite first moment. For fixed
J\in(0,\infty)
and
(g,d)\in\{+1,-1\}^{2}
, for almost every realization of the disorder sequence
h
(i.e.,
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely), there is convergence as
N\to\infty
of the free energy density

\frac{1}{N}\log\left(Z_{N,J,h}^{gd}\right)

(9)

and of the maximal energy density

\frac{M_{N,J,h}^{gd}}{N}

(10)

towards deterministic quantities denoted respectively
{\mathcal{F}}(J)
and
\mathcal{M}(J)
, which depend on the law
\mu
, the coupling intensity
J
, but not on the boundary conditions
(g,d)
. The convergences also hold in
{\mathbb{L}}^{1}
.

The quantity
{\mathcal{F}}(J)
is called the limiting free energy density, we will sometimes call it simply the free energy.
We call the quantity
\mathcal{M}(J)
the limiting maximal energy density.

Proof.

We work with fixed
J\in(0,\infty)
and start by treating the case of boundary conditions
++
. In this proof, we use the abbreviations:
Z_{N,h}=Z_{N,J,h}^{++}
and
M_{N,h}=M_{N,J,h}^{++}
. We will use Kingman’s subadditive ergodic theorem. Indeed, the application

\displaystyle\Theta:{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}\,,

(11)

\displaystyle h=(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

\displaystyle\mapsto\Theta h=(h_{n+1})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\,,

and the law
\mu^{\otimes{\mathbb{N}}}
define an ergodic system and, for every positive integers
N
and
M
, using the lower bound

\displaystyle H_{N+M,J,h}^{++}

\displaystyle((\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{N+M}))

(12)

\displaystyle\geq H_{N,J,h}^{++}((\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{N}))-4J+H_{M,J,\Theta^{N}h}^{++}((\sigma_{N+1},\dots,\sigma_{N+M}))\,,

we have

Z_{N+M,h}\geq Z_{N,h}e^{-4J}Z_{M,\Theta^{N}h}\,,

(13)

and

M_{N+M,h}\geq M_{N,h}-4J+M_{M,\Theta^{N}h}\,.

(14)

Kingman’s subadditive ergodic theorem then gives the almost sure and
{\mathbb{L}}^{1}
convergence of

\frac{1}{N}\log\left(Z_{N,h}e^{-4J}\right)

(15)

and of

\frac{M_{N,h}-4J}{N}

(16)

towards deterministic quantities. This yields the lemma in the case of boundary conditions ++.

To treat other boundary conditions
(g,d)\in\{+1,-1\}^{2}
, observe that for every configuration
\sigma\in\{+1,-1\}^{N}

H_{N,J,h}^{++}(\sigma)-4J\leq H_{N,J,h}^{gd}(\sigma)\leq H_{N,J,h}^{++}(\sigma)+4J\,,

(17)

so that

e^{-4J}Z_{N,J,h}^{++}\leq Z_{N,J,h}^{gd}\leq e^{4J}Z_{N,J,h}^{++}\,,

(18)

and

M_{N,J,h}^{++}-4J\leq M_{N,J,h}^{gd}\leq M_{N,J,h}^{++}+4J\,.

(19)

∎

Let us now relate the partition function and the free energy of the RFIC with the random matrix product in (
2
). We introduce the matrices

Q_{J}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&e^{-2J}\\
e^{-2J}&1\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad T_{x}:=\begin{pmatrix}e^{x}&0\\
0&e^{-x}\end{pmatrix}\,,x\in{\mathbb{R}}

(20)

and view them as indexed by
\{+1,-1\}
. Then the partition function
Z_{N,J,h}^{gd}
is equal to the coefficient indexed by
(g,d)
of the matrix product:

Q_{J}T_{h_{1}}Q_{J}T_{h_{2}}Q_{J}\dots Q_{J}T_{h_{n}}Q_{J}\,.

(21)

Indeed, when computing the
(g,d)
-coefficient, one has to take into account the contributions to the matrix product of all the paths of length
2N+1
with values in
\{+1,-1\}
starting at
g
and ending at
d
. When going through a matrix of the type
T_{x}
, the path should actually not switch (otherwise it has weight 0), and gains a factor
e^{x}
or
e^{-x}
depending on its value. When going trough a matrix
Q_{J}
, the path can switch to opposite value and then it gets a factor
e^{-2J}
. This yields exactly
Z_{N,J,h}^{gd}
.

We deduce that
{\mathcal{F}}(J)
is, almost surely, the exponential growth rate of the coefficients of the random matrix product in (
21
).
Removing the left-most matrix
Q_{J}
there does not change that fact and this yields that
{\mathcal{F}}(J)
is also the almost sure exponential growth rate of the coefficients of
M_{1}\dots M_{N}
with

M_{n}:=T_{h_{n}}Q_{J}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{h_{n}}&e^{-2J+h_{n}}\\
e^{-2J-h_{n}}&e^{-h_{n}}\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad n\geq 1\,.

(22)

Hence,
{\mathcal{F}}(J)
is the so-called Lyapunov exponent of the product
M_{1}\dots M_{N}
.

Remark 2
.

For completeness, let us present the definition of the Lyapunov exponent in a general setup. If
(M_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a random sequence of
n\times n
matrices, we consider the following limit, if it exists:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\log|M_{1}\dots M_{N}|

(23)

where
|\cdot|
is any norm on
\mathcal{M}_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
; the limit being independent of the choice of the norm, due to the equivalence of norms in finite dimension. Choosing a submultiplicative norm, we have for every positive integers
N
and
M
,

\log|M_{1}\dots M_{N+M}|\leq\log|M_{1}\dots M_{N}|+\log|M_{N+1}\dots M_{N+M}|\,.

(24)

Hence, if the sequence
(M_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is i.i.d. and if
(\log|M_{1}|)_{+}
has a finite expectation, then Kingman’s subadditive theorem yields that the above limit almost surely exists, is deterministic, and that the convergence holds also in
{\mathbb{L}}^{1}
.

If furthermore all the coefficients of
M_{1}
are almost surely positive, then Furstenberg’s theory yields that all the coefficients of
M_{1}\dots M_{N}
have the same exponential growth rate, equal to the Lyapunov exponent.

1.3
Theorems

1.3.1
Results concerning the limiting maximal energy density

Theorem 3
.

Assume (
1
). Then, as
J\to\infty
,

\mathcal{M}(J)\sim\frac{\vartheta^{2}}{2J}\,.

(25)

Theorem 4
.

Assume (
1
) and that

(H-1)

there exists a positive integer
n_{0}
such that
h_{1}+\dots+h_{n_{0}}
has a density,

and either that

(H-2)

there exists
\xi>5
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|^{\xi}\mu(\text{\rm d}x)<\infty
,

or that

(H-2
′
)

there exists
c>0
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(c|x|)\mu(\text{\rm d}x)<\infty
.

Then there exists a positive constant
\widehat{\kappa}
such that, as
J\to\infty
,

\mathcal{M}(J)=\frac{\vartheta^{2}}{2J+\widehat{\kappa}}+\begin{cases}O\left(1/J^{\xi-4}\right)&\text{ under {\bf(H-2)}},\\
O\left(\exp\left(-\delta J\right)\right)&\text{ under {\bf(H-2${}^{\prime}$)}},\end{cases}\,

(26)

for a suitable choice of
\delta>0
.

We will provide a somewhat explicit expression for the value of the constant
\widehat{\kappa}
.
To give this expression, let us introduce
S=(S_{n})_{n\geq 0}
the random walk associated to
h=(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
:

S_{0}=0\,,\qquad S_{n}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}h_{j}\,,\qquad n=1,2,\dots

(27)

Further denote

\displaystyle\alpha_{\vartriangleleft}

\displaystyle:=\inf\{n\geq 1:S_{n}>0\}\,,\qquad H_{\vartriangleleft}:=S_{\alpha_{\vartriangleleft}}\,,

(28)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\vartriangleright}

\displaystyle:=\inf\{n\geq 1:S_{n}<0\}\,,\qquad H_{\vartriangleright}:=-S_{\alpha_{\vartriangleright}}\,.

The constant
\widehat{\kappa}
is given by

\widehat{\kappa}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[H_{\vartriangleleft}^{2}]}{{\mathbb{E}}[H_{\vartriangleleft}]}+\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[H_{\vartriangleright}^{2}]}{{\mathbb{E}}[H_{\vartriangleright}]}\right)\,.

(29)

1.3.2
Result concerning the free energy

Theorem 5
.

Assume (
1
). Then there exists a positive constant
\widetilde{\kappa}
such that, as
J\to\infty
,

{\mathcal{F}}(J)=\mathcal{M}(J)+\frac{\vartheta^{2}\widetilde{\kappa}}{(2J)^{2}}+o(1/J^{2}).

(30)

We will provide a somewhat explicit expression for the value of the constant
\widetilde{\kappa}
.
To give this expression, we first need to introduce a process
Z=(Z_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
indexed by
{\mathbb{Z}}
and with values in
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Let us first extend
(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
to
(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
, still an i.i.d. sequence with marginal law
\mu
, and extend the random walk
S=(S_{n})_{n\geq 0}
to the whole of
{\mathbb{Z}}
by setting:

S_{0}=0\,,\qquad S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}\text{ for }n\geq 1\,,\quad S_{n}=-\sum_{i=n+1}^{0}h_{i}\text{ for }n\leq-1\,.

(31)

In other terms,
S=(S_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is the walk satisfying
S_{0}=0
and
S_{n}-S_{n-1}=h_{n}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
The process
Z=(Z_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is then obtained by conditioning
S
to be non-negative: for a rigorous definition, see Section
2.3
, where we will also argue that
Z
(respectfully,
(-Z_{-n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
) locally describes the walk
S
around a
\Gamma
-minimum (respectfully, a
\Gamma
-maximum).
The constant
\widetilde{\kappa}
is given by the following expectation:

\widetilde{\kappa}={\mathbb{E}}\left[\log\left(\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{-2Z_{n}}\right)\right]\,.

(32)

Note that
\widetilde{\kappa}
is always positive, since
Z_{0}=0
.

1.4
Consequences and links with the existing literature

From Theorems
3
and
5
we derive the following corollary.

Corollary 6
.

Assume (
1
). Then, as
J\to\infty
,

{\mathcal{F}}(J)\sim\frac{\vartheta^{2}}{2J}\,.

(33)

This first order expansion of
{\mathcal{F}}(J)
was already proven in
[
4
]
under the slightly stronger hypothesis that
\mu
admits a moment of order
\xi
for some
\xi>\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}\approx 2.618
. The present paper thus extends this result to the most general case where it can be expected to hold: if
\mu
does not have a finite second moment, then the variance
\vartheta^{2}
is simply not defined.
On the other hand, in
[
4
]
, a control was given on the rest, depending on the value of
\xi
, but let us stress that this control was not very precise: it did not go down to the order
J^{-2}
.

Furstenberg’s theory for random matrix products provides an expression for the Lyapunov exponent
{\mathcal{F}}(J)
in terms of the invariant probability measure of some Markov chain on
{\mathbb{R}}
. In
[
2
]
, exploiting this expression, it was established that under the same assumptions as in our Theorem
4
, there exists
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
such that for
J\to\infty

{\mathcal{F}}(J)=\frac{\vartheta^{2}}{2J+\kappa}+\begin{cases}O\left(1/J^{\xi-4}\right)&\text{ under {\bf(H-2)}},\\
O\left(\exp\left(-\delta J\right)\right)&\text{ under {\bf(H-2${}^{\prime}$)}},\end{cases}\,

(34)

for a suitable choice of
\delta>0
.

The technique used in
[
2
]
to derive this result yields expressions for the constants appearing in the numerator and in the denominator of (
34
) which are not explicit (see formulas (3.13) and (3.17) in
[
2
]
). The fact that the constant in the numerator is equal to the disorder variance
\vartheta^{2}
derives from our Corollary
6
, but also already from
[
4
]
; and in fact in the appendix of
[
2
]
the authors managed to show that their complicated expression for this constant can be reduced to
\vartheta^{2}
.
On the contrary the value of the coefficient
\kappa
in the denominator remained implicit: its expression in
[
2
]
involves two quantities depending on the invariant measures of some Markov chains on
\mathbb{R}
. Grasping the value of
\kappa
is also beyond the reach of
[
4
]
, since, as already mentioned, the expansion proven there does not go down to the order
J^{-2}
.

From Theorems
4
and
5
in the present work we derive a more explicit expression for the constant
\kappa
in (
34
).

Corollary 7
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
4
, the constant
\kappa
appearing in (
34
) is given by

\kappa=\widehat{\kappa}-\widetilde{\kappa}

(35)

where
\widehat{\kappa}
is given in (
29
) and
\widetilde{\kappa}
in (
32
).

Let us stress that in the main theorem of
[
2
]
, i.e., in (
34
) above,
\kappa
is relevant only if
\mu
has a moment of order larger than 6.

Although we are unable to make explicit computations for any particular choice of the law
\mu
(see Remark
8
), this expression
\kappa=\widehat{\kappa}-\widetilde{\kappa}
seems to depend on fine details of the law
\mu
, so it does not seem to exhibit the same universality as the constant in the numerator of (
34
).

Remark 8
.

Unfortunately, we are unable to compute
\kappa
in any special case using our formulas. If
\mu
is the Laplace law, then we remark that
\widehat{\kappa}
can be computed since, using the loss of memory property of exponential variables,
H_{\vartriangleleft}
and
H_{\vartriangleright}
are distributed as exponential variables. The constant
\widetilde{\kappa}
is however more complicated to compute.

If
S
is the simple random walk, then
\widehat{\kappa}=1
, but we are unable to compute
\widetilde{\kappa}
; anyway, the simple random walk does not comply with hypothesis
(H-1)
of Theorem
4
.

Continuing our review of the literature, let us mention a special choice of the law
\mu
. In
[
2
]
it is shown that if
\mu
has density
\frac{1}{(2\cosh(x/2))^{2}}
, then
\kappa
is equal to zero.

We mention that in
[
5
]
Lyapunov exponents of more general products of i.i.d. random
2\times 2
-matrices have been studied. A first order development of the Lyapunov exponent was obtained, which coincides with ours. In particular, the paper allows for negative coefficients in the matrices, which is well beyond our reach since we rely on lower and upper bounds that exploit the positivity of the terms contributing to the partition function. We stress that these results however do not go far beyond order
1/J
and are established only for compactly supported disorder variables.

Finally, let us mention that beyond order
1/J^{2}
, the regularity assumptions on the disorder law are expected to play a role: in
[
7
]
, it is indeed claimed that when
S
is the (lazy) simple random walk, the next term in the expansion is of the form
\frac{\omega(J)}{J^{3}}
where
\omega
is a 1-periodic function.

1.5
Structure of the paper

In Section
2
we describe a configuration which maximises the Hamiltonian, with the help of the notion of
\Gamma
-extrema of the walk
S
associated to
h
. Examining the behaviour of these
\Gamma
-extrema for large
\Gamma
, we obtain a proof of Theorem
3
.

In Section
3
, using the point of view of the large energy configurations of the RFIC, we prove Theorem
5
.

Finally, in Section
4
, we prove Theorem
4
. This last proof is essentially independent of the rest of the paper, in particular it is not based on the description of the configurations with large energy; it is modelled on the proof of the main theorem in
[
2
]
.

2
Maximal energy configuration

2.1
Process of
\Gamma
-extrema associated to the random walk
S

We use the notations of
[
3
]
, which we recall now. Recall that
S=(S_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is the random walk associated to the i.i.d. sequence
h=(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
:

S_{0}=0\,,\qquad S_{n}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}h_{j}\,,\qquad n=1,2,\dots

(36)

Let us introduce for
0\leq m\leq n

S^{\uparrow}_{m,n}\,:=\,\max_{m\leq i\leq j\leq n}(S_{j}-S_{i}),\qquad S^{\downarrow}_{m,n}:=\max_{m\leq i\leq j\leq n}(S_{i}-S_{j})\,,

(37)

and for every
\Gamma>0
the
first time of
\Gamma
-decrease

\mathtt{t}_{1}(\Gamma)\,:=\,\inf\left\{n>0:S^{\downarrow}_{0,n}\geq\Gamma\right\}\,.

(38)

For almost all realizations of
(h_{n})
we have that
\limsup_{n}S_{n}=-\liminf_{n}S_{n}=\infty
: we assume that we work on such realizations,
so the infimum in (
38
) can be replaced by a minimum and

\mathtt{t}_{1}(\Gamma)<\infty
.
Next we introduce (with the notation
\llbracket j,k\rrbracket:=[j,k]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}
for the integers
j\leq k
)

\mathtt{u}_{1}(\Gamma)\,:=\,\min\left\{n\in\llbracket 0,\mathtt{t}_{1}(\Gamma)\rrbracket:S_{n}=\max_{i\in\llbracket 0,\mathtt{t}_{1}(\Gamma)\rrbracket}S_{i}\right\}\,,

(39)

so
\mathtt{u}_{1}(\Gamma)
is the first time that
S
reaches its maximum before having a decrease of at least
\Gamma
:
this is what we call
location of the first
\Gamma
-maximum
.
Note however that
S
may reach this maximum also at later times and before time
\mathtt{t}_{1}(\Gamma)
. This is a situation we describe as the occurrence of
multiple
\Gamma
-extrema
. In fact,
this happens with positive probability (at least for
\Gamma
large) if and only
if there exists a positive integer
n
and real numbers
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
(not necessarily disctinct) which are atoms of the law of
h_{1}
and such that
x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}=0
.
Therefore, we introduce also

\mathtt{u}^{+}_{1}(\Gamma)\,:=\,\max\left\{n\in\llbracket 0,\mathtt{t}_{1}(\Gamma)\rrbracket:\,S_{n}=\max_{i\in\llbracket 0,\mathtt{t}_{1}(\Gamma)\rrbracket}S_{i}\right\}\,.

(40)

Now we proceed by looking for the first time of
\Gamma
-increase after
\mathtt{t}_{1}(\Gamma)

\mathtt{t}_{2}(\Gamma)\,:=\,\min\left\{n>\mathtt{t}_{1}(\Gamma):\,S^{\uparrow}_{\mathtt{t}_{1}(\Gamma),n}\geq\Gamma\right\}\,,

(41)

and we set

Next, we explain how the sequence of

.

•

-stretches, are independent.

Proof.

We use fluctuation theory for the random walk

\rho_{0}:=0\,,\quad\text{ and, for }k\geq 0,\quad\rho_{k+1}:=\inf\{n>\rho_{k}:S_{n}\geq S_{\rho_{k}}\}\,,

(47)

which are called the weak ascending record times. They decompose the walk

in i.i.d. portions, called excursions:

\left(S_{\rho_{k}+n}-S_{\rho_{k}}\right)_{0\leq n\leq\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k}}\,,\qquad k=1,2,\dots

(48)

Then the first occurence of a drop of size at least
\Gamma
in

is determined by the first excursion with depth
\Gamma
or larger. Let us denote by
p_{\Gamma}
the probability for an excursion to have a depth not smaller than
\Gamma
. The random walk up to time

can be sampled as follows. Toss a coin:

•

with probability

, decide that the first excursion has depth not smaller than

and sample it conditionally on this fact; then repeat the procedure to sample the next excursions.

•

with probability

, decide that the first excursion has depth not smaller than
\Gamma
and sample it only partially: sample it up to the point where it reaches depth
\Gamma
, i.e., sample a realisation of the walk conditioned on hitting

before returning in
[0,\infty)
.

The end of this partial excursion is the time

, and its starting point is

.
In view of this sampling procedure, we get that the portions of walk from

to

and from

to

are independent.

Considering the excursions that preceed time

to

into two independent portions: from

to
u_{1}(\Gamma)
and from
u_{1}(\Gamma)
to
u_{1}^{+}(\Gamma)
.

After the stopping time

, we repeat the same arguments, alternating weak descending record times and weak ascending record times.
∎

Remark 11
.

Throughout Sections
2
and
3
, we will repeatedly use the law of large numbers (LLN), taking advantage of the i.i.d. structure provided by Fact
10
. We record here two elementary but useful observations.

First,
if
(X_{n})
is a sequence of non-negative random variables all stochastically dominated by a common integrable random variable, then almost surely

\frac{X_{n}}{n}\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

An illustrative application of this is the following: since
t_{2K}(\Gamma)
is the sum of the i.i.d. random variables
t_{2k}(\Gamma)-t_{2(k-1)}(\Gamma)
for
k=1,\dots,K
(denoting
t_{0}(\Gamma)=0
), the LLN yields the almost sure convergence

\frac{t_{2K}(\Gamma)}{K}\to\mathbb{E}[t_{2}(\Gamma)]\quad\text{as }K\to\infty,

but the same holds also for the ratios
\frac{u_{2K}(\Gamma)}{K}
,
\frac{t_{2K+1}(\Gamma)}{K}
and
\frac{u_{2K+1}(\Gamma)}{K}
.

Second,
the independence assumption in the LLN can sometimes be relaxed. Suppose
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a sequence of identically distributed integrable random variables such that the subsequences
(X_{2n})_{n\geq 0}
and
(X_{2n+1})_{n\geq 0}
are each independent. Then, applying the LLN separately to both subsequences yields the LLN for the full sequence.

We introduce the following (positive) variables:

H_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}=S_{u_{1}(\Gamma)}-S_{u_{2}(\Gamma)}\,,\qquad H_{\Gamma}^{\uparrow}=S_{u_{3}(\Gamma)}-S_{u_{2}(\Gamma)}\,,

(49)

L_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}=u_{2}(\Gamma)-u_{1}(\Gamma)\,,\qquad L_{\Gamma}^{\uparrow}=u_{3}(\Gamma)-u_{2}(\Gamma)\,,

(50)

which are distributed as the heights and lengths of generic descending and ascending
\Gamma
-stretches. We note that, due to Fact
10
,
u_{1}(\Gamma)
is distributed as
u_{3}(\Gamma)-t_{2}(\Gamma)
, so
L_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}+L_{\Gamma}^{\uparrow}
is distributed as
t_{2}(\Gamma)
.

Using the LLN, we derive the following formula for the limiting maximal energy density.

Corollary 12
.

For every
J>0
, we have, recalling that
\Gamma=2J
,

\mathcal{M}(J)=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[H_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}+H_{\Gamma}^{\uparrow}\right]-2\Gamma}{{\mathbb{E}}\left[L_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}+L_{\Gamma}^{\uparrow}\right]}\,.

(51)

Remark 13
.

In the case where the law
\mu
is symmetric, the couple
(H_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow},L_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow})
is distributed as
(H_{\Gamma}^{\uparrow},L_{\Gamma}^{\uparrow})
and the above formula takes the slightly simpler form:

\mathcal{M}(J)=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[H_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}\right]-\Gamma}{{\mathbb{E}}\left[L_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}\right]}\,.

(52)

If
\mu
is not symmetric, we cannot a priori claim that the couple
(H_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow},L_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow})
is distributed as
(H_{\Gamma}^{\uparrow},L_{\Gamma}^{\uparrow})
.
However, it still holds that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[H_{\Gamma}^{\uparrow}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[H_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}\right]
. Indeed, by the LLN
S_{n}/n
converges almost surely towards 0 as
n\to\infty
, in particular so does
S_{u_{2K}(\Gamma)}/u_{2K}(\Gamma)
; but using the LLN twice, this ratio also converges towards the ratio
\frac{E\left[H_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}-H_{\Gamma}^{\uparrow}\right]}{{\mathbb{E}}\left[L_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}+L_{\Gamma}^{\uparrow}\right]}
, whose numerator must therefore be zero.

Proof of Corollary
12
.

Let us take
K\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
N=N_{K}=t_{2K}(\Gamma)
. We use the configuration given in Fact
9
, which maximises the Hamiltonian. For this configuration, we have

M_{N_{K},J,h}^{++}=H_{N_{K},J,h}^{++}(\sigma)=-\Gamma\times 2K+S_{u_{1}}+\left(\sum_{1\leq k\leq 2K-1}|S_{u_{k+1}}-S_{u_{k}}|\right)+S_{t_{2K}}-S_{u_{2K}}\,.

(53)

The variables
|S_{u_{k+1}}-S_{u_{k}}|,k=1,3,\dots
being i.i.d., as well as the variables
|S_{u_{k+1}}-S_{u_{k}}|,k=2,4,\dots
, the LLN yields that, almost surely,

\frac{M_{N_{K},J,h}^{++}}{K}\underset{K\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}-2\Gamma+{\mathbb{E}}[H_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}+H_{\Gamma}^{\uparrow}]\,.

(54)

Furthermore, as already stressed in Remark
11
, almost surely,

\frac{N_{K}}{K}=\frac{t_{2K}(\Gamma)}{K}\underset{K\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}{\mathbb{E}}\left[t_{2}(\Gamma)\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[L_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}+L_{\Gamma}^{\uparrow}\right]\,.

(55)

Since, by Lemma

,
\mathcal{M}(J)
is the almost sure limit of
M_{N_{K},J,h}^{++}/N_{K}
, the corollary follows.
∎

2.2
Statistics of the
\Gamma
-stretches for large

and proof of Theorem

We now describe the statistics of the
\Gamma
-stretches when
\Gamma
is large. In
[

(see Proposition 1.3 there) it was shown using Donsker’s invariance principle that, under the Brownian scaling, the heights and lenghts of generic

-stretches converge in law towards the corresponding variables for a Brownian motion, which have been studied in
[

. Let us state this result.

Fact 14
.

Assume (
1
). Then, as
\Gamma\to\infty
, the couples

\left(\frac{H^{\downarrow}_{\Gamma}-\Gamma}{\Gamma},\frac{L_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}}{\Gamma^{2}/\vartheta^{2}}\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad\left(\frac{H^{\uparrow}_{\Gamma}-\Gamma}{\Gamma},\frac{L_{\Gamma}^{\uparrow}}{\Gamma^{2}/\vartheta^{2}}\right)

(56)

both converge in distribution towards the same law on
[0,\infty)^{2}
, which is explicitely described in
[
8
]
. This law is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
[0,\infty)^{2}
and its marginals both have expectation 1.

We deduce the following first order estimates.

Corollary 15
.

Assume (
1
). Then, as
\Gamma\to\infty
,

{\mathbb{E}}\left[H^{\downarrow}_{\Gamma}\right]\sim{\mathbb{E}}\left[H^{\uparrow}_{\Gamma}\right]\sim 2\Gamma\,,

(57)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[L^{\downarrow}_{\Gamma}\right]\sim{\mathbb{E}}\left[L^{\uparrow}_{\Gamma}\right]\sim\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{\vartheta^{2}}\,.

(58)

Proof.

To derive the corollary from Fact
14
, it is sufficient to prove that for large enough
\Gamma_{0}
, the families
\left(\frac{H^{\downarrow}_{\Gamma}}{\Gamma}\right)_{\Gamma>\Gamma_{0}}
,
\left(\frac{H^{\uparrow}_{\Gamma}}{\Gamma}\right)_{\Gamma>\Gamma_{0}}
,
\left(\frac{L_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}}{\Gamma^{2}}\right)_{\Gamma>\Gamma_{0}}
and
\left(\frac{L_{\Gamma}^{\uparrow}}{\Gamma^{2}}\right)_{\Gamma>\Gamma_{0}}
are uniformly integrable.

We first treat the case of the lengths. Since
L_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}+L_{\Gamma}^{\uparrow}
is distributed as
t_{2}(\Gamma)
, it is enough to show that the families
\left(\frac{t_{1}(\Gamma)}{\Gamma^{2}}\right)_{\Gamma>\Gamma_{0}}
and
\left(\frac{t_{2}(\Gamma)-t_{1}(\Gamma)}{\Gamma^{2}}\right)_{\Gamma>\Gamma_{0}}
are unifomly integrable for large enough
\Gamma
. Those two families can be treated similarly, we focus on the first one. Using Fact
14
, let us take
\Gamma_{0}
large enough so that, for some large
C
, for every
\Gamma>\Gamma_{0}
,

{\mathbb{P}}\left[t_{1}(\Gamma)>C\Gamma^{2}\right]\leq e^{-1}

(59)

Denoting
L=\left\lfloor C\Gamma^{2}\right\rfloor
, we bound the probability that there is no drop of size
\Gamma
in
[\![0,nL]\!]
by the probability that for all
k=0,\dots,n-1
there is no drop of size
\Gamma
in
[\![kL,(k+1)L]\!]
to derive that for
\Gamma>\Gamma_{0}
and every integer

\mathbb{P}\left[t_{1}(\Gamma)>n\left\lfloor C\Gamma^{2}\right\rfloor\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[t_{1}(\Gamma)>\left\lfloor C\Gamma^{2}\right\rfloor\right]^{n}\leq e^{-n}

(60)

Hence the family
\left(\frac{t_{1}(\Gamma)}{\Gamma^{2}}\right)_{\Gamma>\Gamma_{0}}
is uniformly integrable.

Concerning the heights, let us focus on the family
\left(H_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}\right)_{\Gamma>\Gamma_{0}}
.
We are going to show that, as

,

\sup_{\Gamma>\Gamma_{0}}{\mathbb{P}}[H_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}\geq x\Gamma]=O\left(\frac{\log(x)}{x^{2}}\right)

(61)

which readily implies uniform integrability of the family
\left(\frac{H^{\downarrow}_{\Gamma}}{\Gamma}\right)_{\Gamma>\Gamma_{0}}
.
To do so, we observe, by writing

{\mathbb{P}}[t_{2}(\Gamma)>2t]\leq{\mathbb{P}}[t_{1}(\Gamma)>t]+{\mathbb{P}}[t_{2}(\Gamma)-t_{1}(\Gamma)>t]\,,

(62)

that a statement similar to (
60
) holds with
t_{1}(\Gamma)
replaced by
t_{2}(\Gamma)
and we observe that if
H_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}\geq x\Gamma
, and
t_{2}(\Gamma)\leq\lfloor 3\log(x)\rfloor\lfloor C\Gamma^{2}\rfloor
, then

\sup_{0\leq n\leq\lfloor 3\log(x)\rfloor\lfloor C\Gamma^{2}\rfloor}|S_{n}|\geq\frac{x\Gamma}{2}\,.

(63)

The probability of the latter event can be controlled using Kolmogorov’s inequality.
So we write:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[H_{\Gamma}^{\downarrow}\geq x\Gamma\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left[\sup_{0\leq n\leq\lfloor 3\log(x)\rfloor\lfloor C\Gamma^{2}\rfloor}|S_{n}|\geq\frac{x\Gamma}{2}\right]+{\mathbb{P}}\left[t_{2}(\Gamma)>\lfloor 3\log(x)\rfloor\lfloor C\Gamma^{2}\rfloor\right]

(64)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lfloor 3\log(x)\rfloor\lfloor C\Gamma^{2}\rfloor\vartheta^{2}}{\left(\frac{x\Gamma}{2}\right)^{2}}+\exp(-\lfloor 3\log(x)\rfloor)

\displaystyle\leq 12C\vartheta^{2}\frac{\log(x)}{x^{2}}+\frac{e}{x^{3}}\,.

This yields (
61
) and the proof is complete.

Now, we define

To begin with, let us handle the statement in the lemma concerning the walk

We start by establishing the existence of a positive constant

As annouced we derive from (

Proof of Theorem

3.1
Heuristic

We give here a high-level overview of the main ideas behind the proof of Theorem
5
.

For the
lower bound
, we focus on configurations that deviate slightly from the maximal energy configuration described in Fact
9
. Specifically, we allow for
small displacements
of the domain walls in that configuration. These local modifications yield a quantifiable energy cost, which results in the additive correction term
\frac{\widetilde{\kappa}}{(2J)^{2}}
appearing in the lower bound of Theorem
5
.

For the
upper bound
, we base our analysis on the process of
(\Gamma-5\log\Gamma)
-extrema of the walk
S
. Let us denote by
\sigma^{(\Gamma-5\log\Gamma)}
the configuration prescribed (in the sense of Fact
9
) by these
(\Gamma-5\log\Gamma)
-extrema. The proof proceeds in several steps:

•

Step 1.
In the same spirit as in the proof of Fact
9
, we show that any configuration
\sigma
that introduces “too many” domain walls compared to
\sigma^{(\Gamma-5\log\Gamma)}
is suboptimal. Moreover, the gap between
\Gamma-5\log\Gamma
and
\Gamma
allows for a quantitative estimate of the energy penalty incurred by such excess walls. This justifies restricting our attention to configurations obtained from
\sigma^{(\Gamma-5\log\Gamma)}
by performing
small displacements
of domain walls, or by removing some of them.

•

Step 2.
We then quantify the energy cost of these small displacements. After accounting for this contribution, we can further restrict to configurations in which spin changes occur only at the positions of the
(\Gamma-5\log\Gamma)
-extrema.

•

Step 3.
We observe that any
(\Gamma-5\log\Gamma)
-stretch with height exceeding
\Gamma+5\log\Gamma
must contain a corresponding spin domain in any nearly-optimal configuration. Once again, we can provide a quantitative estimate for the energy gain from including such domains. This leaves only a small number of remaining possibilities — namely, the configuration remains free on the stretches whose height does not exceed
\Gamma+5\log\Gamma
, which form a small fraction of the total. For each such residual configuration, we bound its energy from above by the maximal energy.

Finally, we combine the estimates obtained in the previous steps, and invoke the LLN together with our controls from Fact
14
, Corollary
15
and Lemma
18
. In particular, the additive term
\frac{\widetilde{\kappa}}{(2J)^{2}}
in the upper bound arises from small displacements of domain walls around the
(\Gamma-5\log\Gamma)
-extrema, in the same way as in the lower bound.

3.2
Lower bound

In this section, for convenience we are going to drop the dependence in
\Gamma
in the notations, so we write

,

and
u_{k}^{+}
instead of

,

and

.
We work with some large integer

and
\tau_{k}^{+}
around

(104)

where we use the same notations as in Lemma

We finally use Corollary

.
As before, we consider large integer
K
and we are going to bound (this time from above) the partition function on the system of size
N=N_{K}=t_{2K}
, but we insist on the fact that now
t_{2K}
is defined with
\Gamma-c_{\Gamma}
instead of
\Gamma
.
For pratical purposes, we also set
u_{0}=0
and
u_{2K+1}^{+}=t_{2K}
.

First step.

By construction, inside an ascending (respectively, descending)
(\Gamma-c_{\Gamma})
-stretch, there is no drop (respectively, rise) larger than
\Gamma-c_{\Gamma}
.
Using this fact, we reduce the summation defining
Z_{N,J,h}^{++}
to those configurations satisfying the following conditions:

1.

For every
k=1,\dots,2K
there exists at most one wall located at a position between
u_{k-1}
and
u_{k+1}^{+}
(inclusive) having the same type as
u_{k}
(i.e., a wall from +1 to -1 for
k
odd, a wall from
-1
to
+1
for
k
even). If such a wall exists, we refer to it as the wall attached to
u_{k}
.

2.

For every
k=1,\dots,2K-1
, provided they exist, the wall attached to
u_{k}
is on the left of the wall attached to
u_{k+1}
.

To justify this restriction, we make the following observation. If
\sigma
is a configuration which does not follow those conditions, then it is always possible to remove a pair of walls in such a way that the energy increases by at least
2c_{\Gamma}
.
Indeed:

•

Assume that the first condition is violated, say for some odd
k
(so
u_{k}
is a
(\Gamma-c_{\Gamma})
-maximum); then the configuration
\sigma
possesses a - domain entirely contained in
\llbracket u_{k-1},u_{k}\rrbracket
or a + domain entirely contained in
\llbracket u_{k},u_{k+1}\rrbracket
; in the first case (respectively, the second case) this domain corresponds to a drop (respectively a rise) in
S
of size smaller than
\Gamma-c_{\Gamma}
and removing this domain increases the energy by at least
2\Gamma-2(\Gamma-c_{\Gamma})=2c_{\Gamma}
.

•

Assume that the first condition is satisfied but the second condition is violated, say for some odd
k
(so
\llbracket u_{k},u_{k+1}\rrbracket
is a descending
\Gamma
-stretch); then, similarly as before, the two walls correspond to a rise in
S
of size smaller than
\Gamma-c_{\Gamma}
and removing those two walls increases the energy by at least
2\Gamma-2(\Gamma-c_{\Gamma})=2c_{\Gamma}
.

So, starting from some configuration
\sigma
let us remove pairs of walls as described above until we obtain a configuration meeting the conditions and let us denote by
r
the number of steps needed, so the number of removed pairs of walls. There may be a number of choices to be made along the procedure, let us assume we fix a deterministic rule. We claim that there are at most
\binom{N}{2r}
configurations that lead in
r
steps to a given configuration satisfying the conditions.
Denoting by
C_{N,J,h}^{++}
the sum defining
Z_{N,J,h}^{++}
but restricted to configurations satisfying the conditions, it follows that:

\displaystyle Z_{N,J,h}^{++}\leq C_{N,J,h}^{++}\times\sum_{r\geq 0}\binom{N}{2r}\exp(-2rc_{\Gamma})=C_{N,J,h}^{++}\times(1+e^{-2c_{\Gamma}})^{N}.

(105)

Second step.

We are going to relate
C_{N,J,h}^{++}
to the partition function of an auxiliary RFIC.

Let us consider a configuration
\sigma
satisfying the conditions. To
\sigma
we associate the only configuration
\eta:\{2,\dots,2K\}\to\{-1,+1\}
such that, with respect to the boundary conditions
\eta_{1}=+1
and
\eta_{2K+1}=+1
, for each
k\in\{1,\dots,2K\}
,
\eta_{k}\neq\eta_{k+1}
if and only if there is a wall attached to
u_{k}
in configuration
\sigma
. In other terms, for each
k
,
\eta_{k}
reflects the value taken by the configuration
\sigma
on the
(\Gamma-c_{\Gamma})
-stretch from
u_{k-1}
to
u_{k}
, more precisely inbetween the (possible) walls attached to
u_{k-1}
and
u_{k}
.
Then, the energy of configuration
\sigma
can be rewritten as:

H_{N,J,h}^{++}(\sigma)=-\Gamma\sum_{k=1}^{2K}{\mathbf{1}}_{\eta_{k}\neq\eta_{k+1}}+S_{u_{1}}+\sum_{k=2}^{2K}H_{k}\eta_{k}+(S_{t_{2k}}-S_{u_{2K}})+\sum_{k}2(-1)^{k}(S_{n_{k}}-S_{u_{k}})\,,

(106)

where the sequence
H=(H_{k})_{k\geq 2}
is defined by
H_{k}=S_{u_{k}}-S_{u_{k-1}}
. Conversely, we observe that the configurations that are associated to a given
\eta
are given by the positions of the walls, so a collection of the following form: for each
k\in\{1,\dots,2K\}
such that
\eta_{k-1}\neq\eta_{k}
a value
n_{k}
between
u_{k-1}
and
u_{k+1}^{+}
(inclusive), with the further requirement that the sequence of
n_{k}
’s must be increasing.

The quantity
C_{N,J,h}^{++}
can thus be rewritten as:

\displaystyle C_{N,J,h}^{++}=\sum_{\eta\in\{+1,-1\}^{2K}}\Bigg(

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\Gamma\sum_{k=1}^{2K}{\mathbf{1}}_{\eta_{k}\neq\eta_{k+1}}+\sum_{k=2}^{2K}H_{k}\eta_{k}+(S_{u_{1}}+S_{t_{2k}}-S_{u_{2K}})\right)

(107)

\displaystyle\sum_{(n_{k})_{k}\text{ corresponding to }\eta}\exp\left(\sum_{k}2(-1)^{k}(S_{t_{k}}-S_{u_{k}})\right)\Bigg)

We now formally extend the sum over
(n_{k})_{k}
corresponding to
\eta
to a sum over all collections
(n_{k})_{k=1,\dots,2K}
such that
u_{k-1}\leq n_{k}\leq u_{k+1}^{+}
for each
k
to yield the following upper bound:

\displaystyle C_{N,J,h}^{++}\leq

\displaystyle Z_{1,2K,J,H}^{++}\exp(S_{u_{1}}+S_{t_{2k}}-S_{u_{2K}})

(108)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{k=1}^{2K}\left(\sum_{u_{k-1}\leq n_{k}\leq u_{k+1}^{+}}\exp\left(2(-1)^{k}(S_{n_{k}}-S_{u_{k}})\right)\right)\,,

where

Z_{1,2K,J,H}^{++}=\sum_{\eta:\{2,\dots,2K\}\to\{+1,-1\}^{2K}}\exp\left(-\Gamma\sum_{k=1}^{2K}{\mathbf{1}}_{\eta_{k}\neq\eta_{k+1}}+\sum_{k=2}^{2K}H_{k}\eta_{k}\right)

is the partition of function of the RFIC on the system
\{2,\dots,2K\}
, with external field
H=(H_{k})_{k\geq 2}
.

Third step.

We now focus on
Z_{1,2K,J,H}^{++}
, which is the partition function associated to an external field with values that alternate signs and are large:
|H_{k}|\geq\Gamma-c_{\Gamma}
by definition of
H_{k}
. Having in mind Fact
15
and Remark
16
, the
|H_{k}|
’s are in fact typically larger than, say,
\Gamma+c_{\Gamma}
and this auxiliary chain should tend to align with the external field. Let us make this more rigorous.

Let us denote by
I_{K}
the indices
k\in\{2,\dots,2K\}
such that
H_{k}\in[\Gamma-c_{\Gamma},\Gamma+c_{\Gamma}]
. Let us consider a configuration
\eta\in\{+1,-1\}^{\{2,\dots,2K\}}
. For every
k\in\{2,\dots,2K\}\setminus I_{K}
, if
\eta_{k}
is not aligned with
H_{k}
, flipping
\eta_{k}
induces an energy gain of at least
2c_{\Gamma}
.
Doing so we end with a configuration which is such that
\eta_{k}
is aligned with
H_{k}
for each
k\in\{2,\dots,2K\}\setminus I_{K}
. We observe that there are at most
2^{\#I_{K}}
such configurations; for which we simply bound the energy by the maximal energy

M_{1,2K,J,H}^{++}:=\sup_{\eta:\{2,\dots,2K\}\to\{+1,-1\}^{2K}}-\Gamma\sum_{k=1}^{2K}{\mathbf{1}}_{\eta_{k}\neq\eta_{k+1}}+\sum_{k=2}^{2K}H_{k}\eta_{k}\,.

(109)

It follows that:

\displaystyle Z_{2K,J,H}^{++}

\displaystyle\leq\exp(M_{1,2K,J,H}^{++})\times 2^{\#I_{K}}\times\sum_{i\geq 0}\binom{2K-1}{i}\exp(-2ic_{\Gamma})

(110)

\displaystyle=\exp(M_{1,2K,J,H}^{++})\times 2^{\#I_{K}}\times(1+e^{-2c_{\Gamma}})^{2K-1}\,.

Let us finally observe that
M_{1,2K,J,H}^{++}+S_{u_{1}}+S_{t_{2k}}-S_{u_{2K}}
can be intrepeted as the maximum defining
M_{N,J,h}^{++}
, restricted to a smaller set: the set of configurations having walls at positions of
(\Gamma-c_{\Gamma})
-extrema. Hence,
M_{1,2K,J,H}^{++}+S_{u_{1}}+S_{t_{2k}}-S_{u_{2K}}
is not larger than
M_{N,J,h}^{++}
(in fact, they are equal, due to Fact
9
).

Conclusion.

Combining the estimates obtained in the previous steps, taking the
\log
and dividing by
N=N_{K}=t_{2K}
(and using the fact that
2K-1\leq N
), we get:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\log\left(Z_{N,J,h}^{++}\right)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{M_{N,J,h}^{++}}{N}+2\log\left(1+e^{-2c_{\Gamma}}\right)

(111)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{2K}\log\left(\sum_{u_{k-1}\leq n_{k}\leq u_{k+1}^{+}}\exp\left(2(-1)^{k}(S_{t_{k}}-S_{u_{k}})\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\#I_{K}}{N}\log 2

Let us now take
K
to infinity. Then
N
goes to infinity and, by Lemma
1
,
\frac{1}{N}\log\left(Z_{N,J,h}^{++}\right)
converges almost surely towards
{\mathcal{F}}(J)
, while
\frac{M_{N,J,h}^{++}}{N}
converges almost surely towards
\mathcal{M}(J)
. Using the LLN, almost surely,

\frac{N}{2K}=\frac{t_{2K}}{2K}\underset{K\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[t_{2}(\Gamma-c_{\Gamma})]}{2}=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[L_{\Gamma-c_{\Gamma}}^{\downarrow}+L_{\Gamma-c_{\Gamma}}^{\uparrow}]}{2}\,,

(112)

while (using also Remarks
11
and
17
)

\frac{1}{2K}\sum_{k=1}^{2K}\log\left(\sum_{u_{k-1}\leq n_{k}\leq u_{k+1}^{+}}\exp\left(2(-1)^{k}(S_{n_{k}}-S_{u_{k}})\right)\right)

(113)

converges as
K\to\infty
towards

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log\left(\sum_{u_{-1}\leq n\leq u_{+1}^{+}}e^{-2S_{n}^{(\Gamma)}}\right)\right]

(114)

and

\frac{\#I_{K}}{2K}\underset{K\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\frac{{\mathbb{P}}[H_{\Gamma-c_{\Gamma}}^{\downarrow}\leq\Gamma+c_{\Gamma}]+{\mathbb{P}}[H_{\Gamma-c_{\Gamma}}^{\uparrow}\leq\Gamma+c_{\Gamma}]}{2}\,.

(115)

It follows that

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathcal{F}}(J)\leq\mathcal{M}(J)+2\log\left(1+e^{-2c_{\Gamma}}\right)+\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[L_{\Gamma-c_{\Gamma}}^{\downarrow}+L_{\Gamma-c_{\Gamma}}^{\uparrow}]}{2}\times

(116)

\displaystyle\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\log\left(\sum_{u_{-1}\leq n\leq u_{+1}^{+}}e^{-2S_{n}^{(\Gamma)}}\right)\right]+\frac{{\mathbb{P}}[H_{\Gamma-c_{\Gamma}}^{\downarrow}\leq\Gamma+c_{\Gamma}]+{\mathbb{P}}[H_{\Gamma-c_{\Gamma}}^{\uparrow}\leq\Gamma+c_{\Gamma}]}{2}\right)\,.

Now, as
\Gamma\to\infty
we have, due to Corollary
15
, that

\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[L_{\Gamma-c_{\Gamma}}^{\downarrow}+L_{\Gamma-c_{\Gamma}}^{\uparrow}]}{2}\sim\frac{(\Gamma-c_{\Gamma})^{2}}{\vartheta^{2}}\sim\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{\vartheta^{2}}\,,

(117)

and, due to Fact
14
, that

\frac{{\mathbb{P}}[H_{\Gamma-c_{\Gamma}}^{\downarrow}\leq\Gamma+c_{\Gamma}]+{\mathbb{P}}[H_{\Gamma-c_{\Gamma}}^{\uparrow}\leq\Gamma+c_{\Gamma}]}{2}\to 0\,.

(118)

For the term in (
114
), use Lemma
18
: this concludes the proof of the upper bound in Theorem
5
.

Proof of Theorem

In this section, we prove Theorem

To simplify expressions and avoid recurring factors of 2, we adopt the following notation. As in the previous sections, we work with the parameter

. Additionally, we define

\mathbf{z}_{n}:=2h_{n}\,,\qquad n=1,2,\dots

(119)

and

(120)

We also denote by
\zeta
the law of
\mathbf{z}_{1}=2h_{1}

\mu

\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{z}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

4.1
Integral formula for
\mathcal{M}(J)

Let
M_{0}^{+}=0
,
M_{0}^{-}=-2J
, and for
n\geq 1
,
M_{n}^{+}=M_{n,J,h}^{++}
and
M_{n}^{-}=M_{n,J,h}^{+-}
. By distinguishing according to the value of the spin at position
n
, we get the recurrence relation

\begin{cases}M^{+}_{n+1}&=\max(M_{n}^{+}+h_{n+1},M_{n}^{-}-2J-h_{n+1})\,,\\
M^{-}_{n+1}&=\max(M_{n}^{+}-2J+h_{n+1},M_{n}^{-}-h_{n+1})\,,\end{cases}

(121)

for every
n\geq 0
. Now, let us consider
X_{n}:=M_{n}^{+}-M_{n}^{-}
. We have
X_{0}=2J
and we derive from (
121

X_{n+1}=h_{\Gamma}(X_{n}+\mathbf{z}_{n+1})\,,

(122)

where we recall that
\Gamma=2J
and
\mathbf{z}_{n}=2h_{n}
and we set

h_{\Gamma}(x)=\begin{cases}-\Gamma&\text{ if }x\leq-\Gamma\,,\\
x&\text{ if }-\Gamma\leq x\leq\Gamma\,,\\
\Gamma&\text{ if }x\geq\Gamma\,.\end{cases}

(123)

Note that the process
X=(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a Markov chain; we denote its transition kernel by
\pi_{\Gamma}
. Since
\widehat{h}_{\Gamma}
sends
{\mathbb{R}}
to
[-\Gamma,\Gamma]
, we may consider that the state space of the chain is reduced to
[-\Gamma,\Gamma]
instead of
{\mathbb{R}}
.

This chain is positive recurrent (
\Gamma
and
-\Gamma
are accessible states) and a simple coupling argument shows that it has a unique invariant probability measure. Indeed, for
x,y\in[-\Gamma,\Gamma]
, let us couple two copies of the chain
X
, started respectfully at
x
and at
y
by driving them by the same i.i.d. sequence
(\mathbf{z}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
; let us assume without loss of generality that
x>y
, then the chains will stay ordered in this way, and they will meet at latest when the chain started from
x
hits
-\Gamma
, and will evolve together afterwards. Since, clearly, this will happen almost surely
(recall that
\zeta
is centered and non-trivial), the uniqueness of the invariant measure follows.
We denote by
\nu_{\Gamma}
the unique invariant probability measure of the chain
X
.

Moreover, rewriting the first equation in (
121

M^{+}_{n+1}=M_{n}^{+}+h_{n+1}+\max(0,-X_{n}-\Gamma-\mathbf{z}_{n+1})\,,

(124)

and since

, we have for all positive integers

M_{N}^{+}=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}h_{n+1}+\max(0,-X_{n}-\Gamma-\mathbf{z}_{n+1})\,.

(125)

Diving by
N
and using the LLN and the ergodic theorem yields the almost sure convergence

\frac{M_{N}^{++}}{N}\underset{N\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\max(0,-x-z-\Gamma)\nu_{\Gamma}(dx)\zeta(\text{\rm d}z)\,.

(126)

We have thus established the second convergence in Lemma
1
once again, this time with an explicit formula for the limiting maximal energy density, given by the right-hand side of (
126
). Let us denote for every probability measure
\nu
on
{\mathbb{R}}

\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}[\nu]=\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\max(0,-x-z-\Gamma)\nu(dx)\zeta(\text{\rm d}z)\,.

(127)

The limiting maximal energy density
\mathcal{M}(J)
is equal to
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}[\nu_{\Gamma}]
.

4.2
Looking from the edge: the reduced chain

If we sit on
-\Gamma
, that is if we make it our new origin, in the limit as

Y_{n+1}\,=\,\max(Y_{n}+\mathbf{z}_{n+1},0)\,.

(128)

Since one iteration of the chain sends

inside
[0,\infty)

[0,\infty)
. This chain is sometimes refered to in the literature as the Lindley process.

Theorem 20
.

Assume (
1
).
Then, the Markov chain
Y
has a unique nonzero invariant
measure
\nu
. This measure
\nu
satisfies that
{\nu([0,x])<\infty}
for every
x\geq 0
, and
\nu([0,\infty))=\infty
.
Moreover,
Y
is recurrent in the sense that if
O\subset[0,\infty)

\nu(O)>0
, then, given any
Y_{0}
,

\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}\in O\text{ i.o.})=1
.

Furthermore, additionally assuming hypothesis
(H-1)
of Theorem
4
and the following condition:

(T)

there exists
\xi>3
such that

\int(z^{+})^{\xi}\zeta(\text{\rm d}z)<\infty

the measure
\nu
satisfies that

\nu([0,x])\overset{x\to\infty}{=}c_{\nu}x+d_{\nu}+O(1/x^{\xi-3})\,.

(129)

for appropriately chosen constants

and

.

Finally, if
(T)
is replaced by

(T
′
)

there exists

then (

For nonnormalizable measures uniqueness is of course meant

\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(y)\nu(\text{\rm d}y)\,=\,\iint_{\mathbb{R}}g((y+z)_{+})\zeta(\text{\rm d}z)\,\nu(\text{\rm d}y)\ \ \text{ for every measurable }g\geq 0\,.

We stress that, since the invariant measures are infinite, what is relevant is the ratio

\frac{d_{\nu}}{c_{\nu}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[H_{1}^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[H_{1}]}\,,

(131)

where
H_{1}=T_{\alpha_{1}}

When applying
Theorem

Proof.

We observe that 0 is an accessible and recurrent state for the Markov chain

(\mathbf{z}_{n})_{n=1,2,\ldots}
is an IID sequence with law
\zeta
which, under (
1

(T_{n})
is the corresponding random walk starting at
0
:
T_{0}=0
and
T_{n}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{z}_{j}
for

. Of course this random walk is null recurrent. Let us now consider
Y
started from some point

. We introduce

\tau_{0}=\inf\{n\geq 1:y_{0}+T_{n}\leq 0\}\,.

(132)

We note that:

(133)

so that

is the return time to 0 of

.
By recurrence of

is almost surely finite, hence
Y
almost surely hits 0. It follows that
Y
admits a unique invariant measure
\nu
, up to a multiplicative constant, and that
\nu(\{0\})<\infty
.
The above arguments show that the expected time between two visits of

at 0 is infinite, hence
Y
is null recurrent and any nonzero invariant measure
\nu
is infinite. Furthermore, the loop representation of
\nu
yields for every

\nu([0,x])=\nu(\{0\})\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{\tau_{0}-1}{\mathbf{1}}_{Y_{n}\leq x}\right]

We argue that

(0,x]

\nu([0,x])

Let us now assume
(H-1)
and
(T)
(respectively,
(T

)

We introduce the strictly ascending ladder times of the random walk

\alpha_{0}:=0\,,\quad\text{and, for }k\geq 1,\quad\alpha_{k}:=\min\{n\geq\alpha_{k-1}:T_{n}>T_{\alpha_{k-1}}\}\,,

as well as the associated sequence of strictly ascending ladder heights

We note that, a.s., for every

We use the observation (

\displaystyle\nu([0,x])

\displaystyle=\nu(\{0\})\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{\tau_{0}-1}{\mathbf{1}}_{Y_{n}\leq x}\right]

(136)

\displaystyle=\nu(\{0\})\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[T_{n}\leq x,T_{1}>0,T_{2}>0,\dots,T_{n}>0]

\displaystyle=\nu(\{0\})\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[T_{n}\leq x,T_{n}>T_{n-1},\dots,T_{n}>T_{1},T_{n}>0]

Now, we can use the renewal lemma to control the sum

[

O(\exp(-\delta\,x)),\qquad&\mbox{under {\bf(T${}^{\prime}$)}}\,.\end{cases}

(137)

The proof is therefore complete.

(X_{0},X_{0}^{\prime})
):

X_{n+1}=h_{\Gamma}(X_{n}+\mathbf{z}_{n+1})\,,\qquad X_{n+1}^{\prime}=h_{\Gamma}(X_{n}^{\prime}+\mathbf{z}_{n+1})\,.

(139)

First, we note that, since
h_{\Gamma}
is 1-Lispschitz, we have that for every
n\geq 0
,
|X_{n}-X_{n}^{\prime}|\leq|X_{0}-X_{0}^{\prime}|
.
Secondly, we claim that, using the notation
t_{1}(\Gamma)
introduced in Section
1.2
for the first instant where a drop of
2\Gamma
or larger has appeared in the walk
T
, we have
X_{n}=X_{n}^{\prime}
for every
n\geq t_{1}(\Gamma)
. Indeed, at time
t_{1}(\Gamma)
both processes are at
\Gamma
and they remain together afterwards.
We thus derive the following bound:

d(\mu\pi_{\Gamma}^{n},\nu\pi_{\Gamma}^{n})\leq\mathbb{P}[t_{1}(\Gamma)>n]\times\mathbb{E}[|X_{0}-X_{0}^{\prime}|].

(140)

Optimizing over the couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
yields

d(\mu\pi_{\Gamma}^{n},\nu\pi_{\Gamma}^{n})\leq\mathbb{P}[t_{1}(\Gamma)>n]\times d(\mu,\nu).

(141)

Finally, we use the bound (
60
) established in the proof of Corollary
15
.
∎

Recall that
\nu_{\Gamma}
is the invariant measure of the Markov chain
X
. From Lemma

we derive the following corollary.

Corollary 22
.

There exists
\Gamma_{1}>0
and a constant

such that for every
\Gamma>\Gamma_{1}
and every measure

(142)

Proof.

Using the fact that

and Lemma

, we write:

\displaystyle d(\nu,\nu_{\Gamma})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\geq 0}d(\nu\pi_{\Gamma}^{n},\nu\pi_{\Gamma}^{n+1})

(143)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\geq 0}C\exp\left(-c\frac{n}{\Gamma^{2}}\right)\times d(\nu,\nu\pi_{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=C\times\frac{1}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{c}{\Gamma^{2}}\right)}\times d(\nu,\nu\pi_{\Gamma})

Finally, we use the fact that for large

Approximating the invariant probability

We are now going to patch together the (infinite!) invariant measure of the

\Gamma

Let us introduce some notations for the cumulative mass functions.
If

If

F_{X}(x):=\mathbb{P}[X\leq x]\,,\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}\,.

(145)

Let
F_{\vartriangleleft}(x):=c_{\nu}^{-1}\nu([0,x])
for

F_{\vartriangleleft}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x\to\infty}}{{=}}x+c_{\vartriangleleft}+\begin{cases}O\left(x^{-(\xi-3)}\right)&\text{ under {\bf(T)}}\,,\\
O\left(\exp(-\delta x)\right)&\text{ under {\bf(T${}^{\prime}$)}}\,,\end{cases}

(146)

.
Let

).
Using this, we define an approximate stationary distribution
\gamma_{\Gamma}
on

F_{\gamma_{\Gamma}}(x)\,:=\,\begin{cases}1-\left(F_{\vartriangleright}(\Gamma-x)/C_{\Gamma}\right)&\text{ if }x\geq 0\,,\\
F_{\vartriangleleft}(x+\Gamma)/C_{\Gamma}&\text{ if }x\leq 0\,.\end{cases}\ \ \ \text{ with }C_{\Gamma}:=F_{\vartriangleleft}(\Gamma)+F_{\vartriangleright}(\Gamma)\,.

(147)

We register also that for the risk function probability

G_{\gamma_{\Gamma}}(x)\,:=\,\begin{cases}F_{\vartriangleright}(\Gamma-x)/C_{\Gamma}&\text{ if }x\geq 0\,,\\
1-\left(F_{\vartriangleleft}(x+\Gamma)/C_{\Gamma}\right)&\text{ if }x\leq 0\,,\end{cases}

(148)

Set

\widehat{\kappa}_{1}:=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\max(0,-x-z)\nu_{\vartriangleleft}(x)\zeta(\text{\rm d}z)

(149)

\widehat{\kappa}_{2}\,:=\,\frac{c_{\vartriangleleft}+c_{\vartriangleright}}{2}\,.

(150)

Assume that both

(151)

(152)

and

\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}[\gamma_{\Gamma}]\,=\,\frac{2\widehat{\kappa}_{1}}{C_{\Gamma}}+\begin{cases}O(1/\Gamma^{\xi-2})&\text{ under {\bf(T)} for both $\pm\mathbf{z}$},\\

(154)

F_{X_{n+1}}(x)\,=\,\begin{cases}0&\text{if }x<-\Gamma\\

(156)

Proof of Proposition

.

) follows readily from (

) and its analogue for

Now for the proof of (

We refer to assumption “
(T)
for

” and to “

for

is assumed. Let us recall a useful identity concerning the Wasserstein-1 distance between two measures

and

d(\mu,\nu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|F_{\mu}(x)-F_{\nu}(x)\right|\text{\rm d}x=\|F_{\mu}-F_{\nu}\|_{1}\,,

with

\displaystyle d(\gamma_{\Gamma},\gamma_{\Gamma}\pi_{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=\left\|F_{\gamma_{\Gamma}}-F_{\gamma_{\Gamma}\pi_{\Gamma}}\right\|_{1}\,

\displaystyle=\,\left\|(F_{\gamma_{\Gamma}}-F_{\gamma_{\Gamma}\pi_{\Gamma}}){\mathbf{1}}_{(-\infty,0)}\right\|_{1}+\left\|(F_{\gamma_{\Gamma}}-F_{\gamma_{\Gamma}\pi_{\Gamma}}){\mathbf{1}}_{(0,\infty)}\right\|_{1}

where

(159)
