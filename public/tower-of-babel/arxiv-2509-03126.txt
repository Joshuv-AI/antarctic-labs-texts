Abstract
Coordinating the interactions between flexibility assets in multi-carrier integrated energy systems (MIES) can lead to an efficient integration of variable renewable energy resources, and a cost-efficient energy transition. However, the proliferation of flexibility assets and their participation in active demand response increases the complexity of coordinating these interactions. This paper introduces different approaches to model the coordination of flexibility scheduling in MIES. We propose a market auction-inspired model coupling approach to address the challenges of preserving the autonomy and privacy of flexibility providers, and the issue of scalability. We benchmark our approach against co-optimization and an iterative price-response method by conducting experiments with varying problem sizes and computing infrastructure. We show that our approach scales well and is suitable for modeling flexibility in large-scale energy systems in a more realistic way. From an optimality standpoint, the flexibility dispatch schedules and electricity prices are “near-optimal”. Our methodology is implemented as a new open-source software, which offers several practical applications. For example, flexibility providers and network operators can couple their models to simulate the interaction between their systems without disclosing confidential information; policy regulators can use it to investigate new market design and regulations to optimize the utilization of flexibility in MIES.

I
Introduction

Electrification of energy demand increases the interactions and interdependencies between electricity, (hydrogen) gas, and heat systems, resulting in multi-carrier integrated energy systems (MIES). While energy system integration offers new sources of flexibility, the inclusion of many different types of distributed energy resources (herein, referred to as flexibility assets) increases the complexity of the energy system
[
1
]
. This is because it becomes challenging to coordinate the interactions between demand-side flexibility assets (e.g., electric vehicles, electrolyzers, etc.)
[
2
]
. Unlike traditional flexibility providers such as conventional generators that are (partially) controlled by the system operator, demand-side flexibility assets are controlled by prosumers who have full autonomy over their systems. This implies that prosumers can participate in active demand response where they schedule their power consumption and/or generation predominantly based on price signals
[
3
]
. As shown in
[
4
]
, excluding the autonomy of prosumers in energy system models can result in lower operational profits, sending misleading investment signals to flexibility providers. Moreover, disregarding the autonomy of prosumers fails to capture the potential consequences of demand response. For example, uncoordinated response from a large pool of demand-side flexibility assets can aggravate congestion in the electricity grid if prosumers simultaneously ramp up consumption in response to low electricity prices. Therefore, energy system integration brings along a methodological challenge—the challenge of coordinating the interactions among an arbitrarily large number of different types of flexibility assets (owned and operated by prosumers with full autonomy) to optimally schedule flexibility.

I-A

Conventional modeling approach vs. model coupling

The conventional approach to model the coordination of flexibility scheduling in MIES is based on combined optimization (co-optimization). Co-optimization is a monolithic approach that models all subsystems using a standalone computational tool to coordinate their interactions by optimizing a global utility function, e.g., system costs. Li
et al.

[
5
]
used co-optimization to optimize flexibility scheduling in a MIES under uncertainty. In
[
6
]
, a co-optimization model is developed to quantify flexibility from electrifying heat demand. Many similar studies can be found in the literature, for example, see
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
. Although widely used, co-optimization has several drawbacks: (1) its top-down control strategy assumes that agents give up control over their assets and that local control (dispatch) decisions are made centrally. This assumption takes away the autonomy of prosumers, which, as we already argued, is a key characteristic that reflects the operational realities of flexibility providers. Therefore, while co-optimization could be a valid approach for modeling a single agent’s problem, it is, however, not suitable for studying flexibility in a MIES where many agents (with full autonomy) interact; (2) to centrally compute dispatch decisions, co-optimization requires complete information of all agents, including the techno-economic parameters and internal states of their assets. This raises a privacy issue as in reality, agents will not be willing to share such information due to confidentiality reasons; (3) co-optimization scales poorly with problem size (number of agents) since all dispatch decisions are computed centrally; (4) similar to all monolithic methods, co-optimization struggles to accurately represent all dimensions of energy system integration—the market interactions of agents and physical interactions of assets. This is because monolithic tools are generally developed with a particular dimension of interest in mind, making them either only economic or technical in nature
[
10
]
. So, in order to squeeze “everything” into a single modeling tool, they make simplifying assumptions that can result in misleading conclusions
[
11
]
.

These drawbacks of co-optimization not only limit its application for the analysis of flexibility, but also limit its capability to investigate market design and policy regulations that influence the behavior of flexibility providers. This is because the lack of autonomy in agents’ local decision-making makes it impossible for co-optimization to fully represent prosumer behavior since agents are modeled as passive market participants. Also, the impracticality of sharing confidential data due to privacy concerns makes it difficult for co-optimization to model the interactions between flexibility providers in a more realistic way. Therefore, from the perspective of regulators and policymakers who seek to achieve efficient utilization of flexibility resources, such models are inadequate to investigate the impact of heterogeneous policy instruments (e.g., electricity grid tariffs) on prosumer behavior, system-wide social welfare, and their spillover effects on other energy sectors
[
12
]
.

To address the limitations of co-optimization, we propose to use a model coupling-based approach. Model coupling combines different models developed in various computational tools by leveraging software techniques and algorithms to manage data exchange and time progression
[
13
]
. Our motivation for model coupling is twofold: autonomy and privacy. First, model coupling can preserve autonomy since it enables agents to locally optimize their dispatch decisions. This makes it possible to represent prosumer behavior and model their interactions in a more realistic way. Moreover, preserving agents’ autonomy makes it possible to simulate each agent (subsystem) in its native tool with dedicated solvers. From a computational point of view, this not only increases accuracy, but also increases scalability due to the potential for parallelization
[
11
]
. Second, since coupled models only need to share interface variables, model coupling offers a way to include confidential information in simulations without violating privacy. Another added benefit of model coupling is that it allows for the reuse of existing software, avoiding the need to make rudimentary assumptions to develop overarching, all-encompassing models
[
14
]
.

In the literature, the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) has been proposed as a possible model coupling strategy
[
15
]
. This model coupling approach, herein referred to as price-response model coupling, uses a distributed coordination architecture. Agents locally optimize their dispatch and interact to cooperatively optimize a global utility. The required information exchange here includes: (1) point-wise demand-supply timeseries computed locally by each agent as a response to prices broadcast by the coordinator agent; (2) the system imbalance computed as the difference between total demand and supply. Given that agents’ dispatch must satisfy the system power balance constraint that couples their decisions, prices and dispatch decisions are iteratively updated based on the imbalance until a consensus price is reached where the imbalance is below the specified threshold. Generally, provided an equilibrium exists, this iterative process converges to a solution (almost) equal to that of co-optimization
[
16
]
.

However, although optimal, this approach has several drawbacks. First, the autonomy of agents is somewhat partial since their dispatch decisions are penalized over iterations to align them towards cooperatively optimizing a global utility. Second, in its basic form, the ADMM algorithm is not fully privacy-preserving
[
17
]
. This is because it repeatedly shares the system imbalance over iterations—a signal that contains the aggregate dispatch of agents. This iterative process exposes the price-response method to potential privacy leakage since adversarial models can reconstruct agents’ subproblems through iterations
[
17
]
. Third, this approach often requires many iterations before converging, which is computationally expensive since decisions in each iteration are computed over the full simulation horizon. Hence, price-response scales poorly with complex systems that have long timeseries. Moreover, there is generally no guarantee of convergence for such iterative procedures if the problem involves integer decision variables
[
18
]
.

I-B

The proposed model coupling approach

To address the challenges of preserving the full autonomy and privacy of flexibility providers, and the issue of scalability, we propose a market auction-inspired model coupling approach that uses a decentralized coordination architecture instead. This approach is characterized by the following: (1) it allows for full autonomy in agents’ local dispatch decisions in a more realistic way. This is because it relaxes the assumption of a central agent optimizing dispatch decisions in a top-down control manner, or a coordinator agent penalizing agents’ local dispatch decisions to align them towards a global optimum. This, however, comes with a sacrifice in optimality compared to co-optimization and the price-response method; (2) the coordination mechanism simulates market auctions by matching agents’ bids through social welfare maximization, requiring that information is exchanged within the resolution and horizon of the market clearing. This approach promises significant gains in scalability since the full horizon is partitioned into smaller windows and simulated in a rolling manner; (3) it only requires agents to share their demand and supply bid functions that internalize their techno-economic and intertemporal constraints. How they construct these bids is their confidential trade strategy encapsulated in their respective models. Interestingly, exchanging bid functions rather than point-wise timeseries eliminates the need for iterations, which is usually prone to non-convergence.

. As shown, these approaches differ mainly in the coordination architecture, coordination mechanism, required information exchange, and autonomy in agents’ local decision-making.

I-C

Contribution

The main contributions of this paper with respect to the scientific literature include:

1.

We propose a market auction-inspired model coupling approach to model the coordination of flexibility scheduling in MIES. The software-coupled framework of our approach makes it possible to model different subsystems in their domain-specific software, and exploit parallelism to increase computational efficiency. Its information exchange requirements eliminate the need for iterations, which, together with parallelism, make our approach highly scalable. Finally, its interface design provides a way to include confidential information about flexibility into system-wide energy models without violating privacy.

2.

We benchmark our proposed model coupling approach against co-optimization and price-response methods by conducting several experimental runs with varying problem sizes and computing infrastructure. This benchmark provides insights into the trade-off that energy system modelers need to make between accuracy (preserving agents’ autonomy), optimality, and scalability.

3.

Our model coupling framework is openly available on GitLab and archived on Zenodo
[
19
]
as a new modeling software, which the energy system modeling community can freely use to explore an alternative approach to model the coordination of flexibility scheduling in MIES. The code for running our experiments is also made open-source
[
20
]
to ensure that the results presented in this paper are reproducible by third parties.

A practical application of our software is that it provides a modeling environment where prosumers, flexibility aggregators, and network operators can couple their models to simulate the interactions between their systems—to avoid myopic operational or investment decisions. For example, for electricity and heat network operators, coupling their models will provide insights into the cost of electrifying heat demand on their business case, and the impact of that on the electricity infrastructure. Policymakers and regulators can use it to assess the value of different flexibility assets in an integrated energy system setting, and investigate policy instruments that can lead to an efficient integration of different flexibility options.

Methodology

This section provides a description of the physical and economic interactions between flexibility assets and agents, and the problem formulations under the three coordination models. While any coordination objective could be used, we assume that the objective for all three models is to minimize costs or maximize revenues. Without loss of generality, the formulations presented here are based on the simplified MIES shown in Fig.

The multi-carrier integrated energy system

The MIES consists of heat, hydrogen, and transport systems coupled to the electricity system through P2X and X2P technologies. The electricity system has generators, batteries, and traditional loads that represent demand from passive consumers. Each physical system enclosed in a dashed rectangle represents an agent with full autonomy. Taking the heat subsystem as example, the heat prosumer has a heat load that can be satisfied through multiple assets. For example, when electricity prices are lowest, the heat prosumer could choose to dispatch electric heating assets (e.g., heat pumps), shifting demand from other carriers to electricity. The heat prosumer also has a heat storage to buffer heat production and increase flexibility by decoupling demand and supply in time.

II-A

II-B

Price-response model coupling

In this coordination model, the problem is formalized as a set of optimization problems, each representing the dispatch optimization of a generator or prosumer. A visualization of the interactions in this approach is illustrated in Fig
3
. As shown, it is an iterative process that starts with a coordinator agent broadcasting a price timeseries and system imbalance to agents. Given this information, agents run their local optimization to determine their optimal dispatch. Next, they report back their electric power generation or consumption timeseries to the coordinator agent, who then calculates the new system imbalance. If the stopping criteria is not below the predefined threshold, a new price timeseries is computed and broadcast, and the process repeats again. In what follows, we define the optimization problem of each agent type in iteration
k
.

II-B
1
Generator agents

Each generator agent maximizes its revenues from electricity generation within its technical limits by solving the following optimization problem.

\displaystyle\left.\begin{aligned} &{\text{max}}\sum_{t}\Bigl(\lambda^{k-1}_{t}-(\alpha_{i}\cdot g^{k}_{i,t}+\beta_{i})\Bigr)\cdot g^{k}_{i,t}\\
&\phantom{=}+\frac{\rho}{2}\cdot\Big\|g^{k}_{i,t}-\Bigl(g^{k-1}_{i,t}-I^{k-1}\Bigr)\Big\|_{2}\hfill\\
&\text{subject to:}\quad\quad\text{constraints}\quad\ref{gen_dispatch_limit_cons}\end{aligned}\right\}\quad\forall i\in{N}_{g}

(14)

The first term in the objective represents revenue maximization, while the second term is an augmented penalty term. The penalty serves to dampen oscillations by constraining abrupt changes in agents’ local dispatch decisions across iterations. This helps to drive the iterative process towards an equilibrium state by penalizing agents’ dispatch decisions based on the system imbalance; thereby driving agents to align their local decisions towards a global optimum. At equilibrium, the penalty term converges to zero.

II-C

Market-auction model coupling

In our proposed approach, agents interact through a decentralized market mechanism, where dispatch and prices are determined through auctions as illustrated in Fig.
4
. First, agents run their local optimization based on electricity price forecasts to determine their bids. These bids, which reflect their willingness to generate or consume electricity at various price levels, are then submitted to the market operator. Next, the market operator clears the market, and the resulting market-clearing prices and dispatch quantities are communicated back to agents. The problems of the market operator (central model) and agents (satellite models) are described as follows.

III
Experiment design and case study

III-A

Experiment design

To evaluate the viability of our proposed approach, we benchmark it against co-optimization and price-response by comparing the generator and prosumer dispatch schedules, system costs, electricity prices, and scaling performance of each method. This benchmark provides insights into the trade-off between accuracy (preserving agents’ autonomy), optimality, and scalability. For scalability, we compare how the runtime of each method scales by varying the following parameters.

III-A
1
Problem size

Problem size can be varied along two dimensions: time (simulation horizon) and space (number of agents). As a first experiment, we vary problem size while maintaining the same computing infrastructure. We increase problem size by increasing the simulation horizon from 1 day to a week (with hourly resolution) and the number of agents from 30 to 90. Note that while experiments with larger problem sizes could be performed, the selected problem sizes are sufficient to capture scaling trends along temporal and spatial dimensions, without exhausting the available computational resources. Yet, our work provides a baseline for future studies to evaluate the scalability of different energy system models.

III-A
2
Computing infrastructure

The different coordination models make use of different architectures, meaning they also differ in how much they can benefit from parallelism in the problem definition. Knowing how much a modeling approach can benefit from parallelism will help inform which trade-off the user can make. Naturally, there will also be a limit to how much benefit can be obtained from parallelism, beyond which it will not make sense to deploy the modeling approach on a system with more parallel CPU cores. To give an initial indication of this trade-off, we vary the computational resources used to run the benchmark between the authors’ modern laptop with 16 CPU cores, and a more capable 48-core and 128-core server machine—Snellius
[
23
]
, as a second experiment. This will indicate some actual possible speed-up from parallelism in the modeling approaches.

III-B

Case study and data source

To use real energy system data for our experiments, we ran PyPSA-Eur model
[
22
]
with Germany only for the year 2013. The collected data include asset capacities (generators, heat pumps, etc.), generator marginal costs and hourly availability profiles for renewables; hourly demand profiles for electricity, heat, hydrogen, and transport; hourly price profiles for energy carriers such as methane, hydrogen, and biomass. We increase the capacity of storage to reflect a system with much flexibility.

A typical node in a PyPSA-Eur model looks like the MIES in Fig
1
, and nodes are interconnected through electricity transmission lines to form a network. To mimic electricity market zonal pricing, we wrote Python scripts to flatten the original network, resulting in a copper plate model in which all prosumers and generators are connected to a single electric bus. A prosumer is assumed to own all assets (excluding generators) that feed into the bus to which it is attached. If there is more than one demand on a bus, the first is assumed to be the base demand (inflexible), while the others are assumed to be flexible. To vary the number of agents for our scaling experiments, we use PyPSA-Eur clustering functionality to increase the spatial granularity of the network to generate the required number of agents. The smallest network instance (with a spatial granularity of 1) results in about 30 agents, of which 16 are generators and 14 are prosumers. Increasing the spatial granularity by a step size of 1 increases the number of agents by roughly 10, and the proportion of distribution between agent types is constant; for every 10 agents added, about 6 are generators and 4 are prosumers.

We used Gurobi solver with default parameters for all experiments. For the price-response method, we set the primal and dual residual convergence tolerances to 0.1 and employed a dynamic penalty parameter update strategy to accelerate convergence
[
18
]
. For the market auction method, satellite models were assigned a look-ahead horizon of 24 hours, while the market clearing horizon was set to 1 hour.

IV
Results

IV-A

Accuracy vs. Optimality

This section shows the performance of the three coordination models in terms of optimality and shows the impact of preserving agents’ autonomy (accuracy) on the “ideal” optimum computed by co-optimization.

IV-A
1
Generator and battery storage dispatch

The dispatch of generators and battery storage is shown in Fig.
5
. As expected, the generation schedules from co-optimization and price-response are almost identical. The main difference with market auction is in the dispatch of gas, CHP, and oil generators, which are used to satisfy peak demand. In the market auction method, less power is produced by these three generators in earlier periods. However, there is a steep ramp-up in power from these generators thereafter. This difference can be explained through the battery storage dispatch in Fig.
5
(b). Due to its perfect foresight, full knowledge, and controllability of the MIES, co-optimization can anticipate high prices in the future, and therefore dispatch more production from CHP and gas at an earlier time to charge the battery. The battery is then used to supply peak demand later, thereby preventing the more expensive oil generator from being dispatched at all. The same trend can be observed for the price-response method.

Conversely, the battery schedule from market auction is completely different. This difference arises from the autonomy of battery operators, who optimize dispatch based on local objectives—revenue maximization. As described in algorithm
1
, a battery operator looks for the cheapest price in the forecast and places buy bids there so it can sell later at a higher price. Since the cheapest price in the forecast appears at a later time, the battery operator does not charge earlier, causing the earlier peak demand to be supplied by gas, CHP, and oil. Such strategic behaviors from flexibility providers, along with their interactions under imperfect knowledge, are features that cannot be fully represented in the other two methods. Therefore, both co-optimization and price-response are less accurate (with respect to modeling flexibility under imperfect competition) since both methods disregard features that are inherent in real-world settings. Our approach can fully capture these key features of flexibility that must be studied and understood—in order to avoid myopic operational and investment decisions for market parties, and for regulators to design efficient policy instruments that can lead to an efficient integration and utilization of flexibility resources in MIES.

This highlights the difference between the “ideal” system-wide optimum computed by co-optimization and the higher, yet more realistic, costs that arise from the full autonomy of self-interested agents who follow strategic objectives rather than system-wide optimum. For the same reason mentioned earlier, the spike is caused by the way battery storage is operated, triggering the dispatch of the oil generator. In any case, the price from co-optimization is not meant to be a strict metric of performance for evaluating our approach since it results from an ”ideal” world, whereas actual market outcomes are almost never optimal. The goal of our approach is not to optimize prices, but to provide a modeling environment to simulate the behavior of flexibility providers and their interactions, which can be influenced through policy regulations to optimize system-wide benefits.

Scalability

The scaling performance of the three coordination models is shown in Fig.
8
. Results per method are normalized according to the runtime of the smallest problem instance instead of using absolute values. This normalization allows a fair comparison of how each method scales since it eliminates any bias in favor of a particular approach that could arise due to, for example, implementation-specific optimizations.

Fig. 8
:
Scaling performance

From the heat map, two main conclusions can be drawn: (1) moving from the left to right or bottom to top cell within the same subplots—i.e., increasing problem size while keeping computational resources constant—the solution time for both co-optimization and price-response increase rapidly, as indicated by the sharp change in the color gradient. On the other hand, the solution time for market auction increases gradually, reflecting better scaling performance; (2) moving from left to right across cells and subplots—i.e., increasing both problem size and computational resources—it can be observed that the solution time for co-optimization remains almost unchanged. In contrast, both the market auction and price-response methods benefit significantly from parallelism, as their solution times decrease with more CPU cores.

Most importantly, the market auction approach scales more efficiently than price-response. This is because of two main reasons. First, the market auction approach is non-iterative since the interaction between the central and satellite models is done once. Also, because market auction partitions the simulation horizon into smaller windows and solves in a rolling-horizon manner, the problem is solved more efficiently. Conversely, price-response requires many iterations of solving agents’ problems before converging, as shown in Fig.
9
. This increases the solution time by a factor equal to the number of iterations. Second, the price-response approach only decomposes the problem along the spatial dimension (per agent). In contrast, market auction decomposes the problem along both spatial and temporal dimensions; this significantly reduces the solution time for the market auction approach.

However, as shown in the events profiling in Fig.
10
, the solution time for market auction is constrained by the runtime of the slowest satellite, despite the central model and other satellites executing quickly. This highlights the importance of optimizing the performance of the slowest satellite to achieve further improvements in scalability. The scalability of the market auction method can also be enhanced by using a longer market-clearing horizon (e.g., 24 hours) since a larger chunk of the horizon will be rolled over per interaction round. However, this will require the implementation of a more advanced bidding strategy. Such a bidding strategy will require the use of block bids
[
24
]
to handle intertemporal dependencies in a single round of bid submission. Our current implementation handles intertemporal constraints by updating the internal state of satellite models on a per-hour basis after the market has cleared. It is worth mentioning that our rationale is that the bidding strategy of agents is to be confidential. This implies it is the responsibility of agents to come up with their bids so that their privacy is preserved. Therefore, while it is out of scope to provide a “perfect” bidding algorithm, yet, our limited implementation serves as a proof-of-concept that our approach can be used to couple energy models at scale.

In sum, our results show that the market auction approach offers superior scalability compared to co-optimization and price-response, making it particularly well-suited for large-scale implementations. These findings suggest that market auction-based model coupling is a promising approach for modeling the coordination of demand-side flexibility assets, as it offers the benefits of preserving the full autonomy and privacy of flexibility providers, and is highly scalable.

V
Conclusion

This paper introduces and compares several approaches to model the coordination of flexibility scheduling in MIES. We propose a market auction-inspired model coupling approach to deal with the issue of autonomy and privacy of flexibility providers, since it allows agents to locally optimize their systems and interact without disclosing confidential information. We benchmark our proposed approach against co-optimization and price-response to provide insights into the trade-offs between accuracy, optimality, and scalability. Results show that preserving the autonomy and privacy of agents allows for a more realistic way of modeling flexibility since real-world features such as strategic behavior and imperfect knowledge can be captured. We empirically show that our approach offers superior scalability, making it particularly well-suited for large-scale implementations.

The proposed methodology is implemented as an open-source software, which is freely available for the energy modeling community to explore. A practical contribution of this software is that market parties, flexibility aggregators, and network operators can use it to simulate the interaction between their systems and avoid myopic operational decisions. Policy regulators can use it as a ”test bed” to investigate new market design questions and policy instruments to optimize the integration and utilization of flexibility resources in MIES.

This work can be extended in multiple directions. First, the bidding strategy of agents can be extended to use block bids to encapsulate intertemporal dependencies spanning multiple hours. This will make it possible for the central model to run with a horizon of arbitrary length. Finally, while copper plate models are used for all subsystems, these can be extended to include spatially resolved networks. These are current subjects of research and will be addressed in our future work.
