Anti-staphylococcal activity of soil-derived Penicillium citrinum extract
Authors: Yousrah Yasser M Dusuki, Michael G Shehat, Amal M Khalil, Gehan M Aboelela, Alaa AY Abouelfetouh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe increasing prevalence of methicillin resistant S. aureus (MRSA) led to higher morbidity, mortality, and treatment costs. The escalating antibiotic resistance levels and the stagnation in antibiotic discovery highlight the need for novel therapeutic alternatives. Soil fungi are a source of antimicrobial agents, given their capacity to produce diverse bioactive metabolites. Soil fungi from Alexandria, Egypt was investigated for extracts with anti-staphylococcal potential.MethodsSoil samples were collected and fungal isolates were obtained on potato dextrose agar (PDA). Isolates were screened for antibacterial activity using agar well diffusion technique against standard bacterial strains. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of most active fungal extract was determined against S. aureus, including MRSA. Checkerboard assay was performed to assess the combination with commonly used antibiotics and MTT assay was used to evaluate the extract’s cytotoxicity. Major active metabolites were characterized by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) and a growth curve analysis was subsequently conducted to evaluate the antibacterial/growth inhibition activity of the most abundant metabolite.ResultsTwenty-three fungal isolates were obtained, Penicillium citrinum extract showed the strongest anti-staphylococcal activity. Plackett–Burman analysis was used to optimize fermentation conditions to enhance anti-staphylococcal activity. The MIC of the butanolic extract ranged between 500 and 1000 µg/mL against commensal, clinical and standard S. aureus. P. citrinum extract combination with gentamicin was synergistic against all clinical MRSA isolates and its combination with ciprofloxacin was synergistic against 37.5% of the isolates. Cytotoxicity on the human skin fibroblast cell line confirmed the safety of the extract at the concentrations used in the synergistic combinations. GC-MS analysis revealed the most abundant bioactive metabolites to be dibutyl phthalate (10.58%), tridecane (7.58%) and hexadecane (7.42%). Growth curve analysis of a representative clinical MRSA isolate in the presence of dibutyl phthalate showed its growth inhibition properties but with no enhancement of the gentamicin efficacy highlighting the importance of the extract as a whole.ConclusionThis study highlights Penicillium citrinum as a promising source for safe and effective antimicrobial agents, particularly as an adjunct to antibiotics against resistant staphylococcal infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04990-0.Keywords: MRSA, Soil fungi, Plackett-Burman design, Antibacterial, GC-MS, Cytotoxicity

Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a critical global health issue. It is defined as the tolerance of microbes to the effect of antimicrobial agents towards which they were previously susceptible [1]. AMR occurs through a combination of natural evolutionary processes and human-driven factors, particularly the overuse and improper use of antimicrobial agents in both medical, agricultural and veterinary settings, giving rise to a One Health aspect of the problem [2]. Recent studies indicate that the widespread and sometimes unnecessary use of empirical antimicrobial therapy during the COVID-19 pandemic, especially in hospitalized patients, has likely played a significant role in the growing prevalence of resistant infections worldwide [3].

The current and estimated death toll and economic burdens of AMR are staggering. It is estimated that, without effective action, AMR can lead to up to 10 million deaths annually and can cost up to 100 trillion USD by 2050 [4, 5]. This is due to prolonged hospital stays, mounting healthcare expenses and lost productivity. Furthermore, the World Health Organization (WHO) has estimated that AMR could potentially push up to 24 million people into extreme poverty by 2030 [4]. Antibacterial resistance, in particular, was responsible for an estimated 4.95 million deaths in 2019, mainly in low- and middle-income countries. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is listed as a high-priority pathogen on the 2024 Bacterial Priority Pathogens List for which research on novel antibacterial agents is needed [6]. It poses significant risks in both healthcare settings and the community, given its high prevalence and the severe infections it causes [6].

While the number of antibacterial agents in the clinical pipeline has risen from 80 in 2021 to 97 in 2023, the demand for new groundbreaking treatments remains critical to address severe infections and replace those losing effectiveness due to overuse [6]. A promising venue is the exploration of fungal secondary metabolites, because of their potential to inhibit a wide range of pathogenic microorganisms, including antibiotic-resistant bacteria [7, 8].

Only a small number of the fungal species have been studied, and these unstudied species could yield novel bioactive compounds that may lead to groundbreaking therapeutic developments [9, 10]. Given the rich diversity of fungal metabolites, focusing on these compounds may advance the creation of novel antibacterial agents, including anti-MRSA ones [7].

The production of the fungal secondary metabolites is influenced by the composition of the fermentation medium, since the nutrients play a critical role in metabolite synthesis [8]. Moreover, the extraction techniques used can increase the yield of these metabolites, ensuring a more sustainable and reliable production process [11]. Refining these methods is crucial for maximizing the discovery and development of novel chemotherapeutic agents .

This study screened soil fungi from Alexandria, Egypt for the production of antibacterial secondary metabolites effective against MRSA. The study also employed single- and multi-factorial experiments to optimize the anti-staphylococcal potential of the fungal extract. Furthermore, the extract was tested in combination with known antibiotics to assess possible synergistic effects.

Methods

Soil sample collection and fungal isolation

Soil samples were collected in sterile Falcon tubes, at a depth of 5–20 cm, from various locations near the intersection of latitude 31°12’53.0"N and longitude 29°57’03.0"E in Alexandria, Egypt, in June 2021. The samples were stored at 4 °C until further use. Fungi were isolated using the serial dilution plating method on Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) medium (Himedia, India). The plates were incubated at 28 °C for seven days. Well-isolated colonies were sub-cultured onto fresh PDA plates to ensure purity then purified isolates were transferred to PDA slants for long-term storage [12, 13].

Preparation of fungal culture filtrates

Fungal isolates were cultured on PDA at pH 5.8 for 7 days at 28 °C. Two fungal mycelial discs (8 mm) were then transferred to 100 mL Potato Dextrose Broth (PDB) (Himedia, India) and incubated statically for 7 days at 30 °C [14]. After incubation, the fungal mycelium was removed by filtration, and the resulting filtrate was tested for its antimicrobial activity [15].

In vitro antibacterial assay screening

The antibacterial potential of the fungal filtrate was tested against S. aureus ATCC 25923, Streptococcus agalactiae CCM 6187 and Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212 standard isolates using agar well diffusion method [15]. Nutrient agar plates were inoculated with the bacterial suspension and wells (8 mm in diameter) were created. A 100 µL volume of fungal filtrate was added to each well. Sterile broth medium, without fungal inoculation, was used as a negative control. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, the antimicrobial activity was evaluated by measuring the inhibition zone diameter in millimeters (mm). An inhibition zone diameter greater than 12 mm was indicative of susceptibility of the tested bacterium [16].

Identification of fungal isolates

Morphological identification

Following the initial screening of fungal isolates for antimicrobial activity, only the isolate that demonstrated the highest inhibitory effect against S. aureus ATCC 25923 was selected for further analysis. The colony characteristics (color, shape, size, and hyphae) of the fungal isolate showing highest antimicrobial activity were observed macroscopically on Czapek’s Yeast Extract Agar (CYA) and PDA after 7 days at 25 °C [17]. For microscopical identification, a lactophenol cotton blue (LPCB) wet mount was prepared and examined under a Zeiss Axiostar plus trinocular microscope at the Assiut University Mycological Center (AUMC) [18].

Molecular identification

The fungus, showing the highest activity against Staphylococcus, was cultured on CYA plates and incubated at 25 °C for 7 days. A small portion of the fungal mycelium was scraped, suspended in 100 µl of distilled water, boiled at 100 °C for 15 min, and then stored at -70 °C. The samples were sent to SolGent Company (Daejeon, South Korea), for DNA extraction and sequencing.

Briefly, fungal DNA was extracted and purified using SolGent purification beads. The internal transcribed spacer (ITS) regions of the nuclear ribosomal DNA were amplified with universal primers ITS 1 (5’ - TCC GTA GGT GAA CCT GCG G − 3’) and ITS 4 (5’ - TCC TCC GCT TAT TGA TAT GC − 3’). PCR amplification was performed on an ABI 9700 thermal cycler. The reaction mixtures consisted of 2.5 µl of 10X EF-Taq buffer, 0.5 µl of 10 mM dNTP, 1.0 µl of 10 pmol/µL forward primer, 1.0 µl of 10 pmol/µL reverse primer, 0.25 µl of EF-Taq (2.5U), 1.0 µl of template DNA, and distilled water to a total volume of 25 µl. The PCR protocol started with an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 15 min. This was followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 20 s, annealing at 50 °C for 40 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min. A final extension step was carried out at 72 °C for 5 min. The PCR product was purified using the SolGent PCR Purification Kit-Ultra (SolGent, Daejeon, South Korea) and separated on gel electrophoresis and the PCR products were sequenced. The DNA sequences were examined using Basic Local Aligment tool (BLAST) of the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) to match its homology with congeneric species from the GenBank database. Phylogenetic analysis was performed using CLCBio software and MegAlign for sequence alignment [17].

Optimization of fermentation conditions

Optimization of the culture conditions of the fungal isolate showing highest activity against S. aureus was done using single factorial experiments and a multi-factorial experiment, to obtain highest antibacterial bioactive metabolites production.

Single factorial experiments

The effects of incubation period, pH, and potato concentration on antimicrobial activity were assessed. For incubation period optimization, fourteen flasks containing 100 mL of PDB at pH 5.8 were inoculated with 7 day old 8 mm mycelial discs and incubated for up to 14 days at 28 °C. Every 24 h, one flask was filtered using Whatman filter paper no. 1 to obtain the filtrate. For pH optimization, PDB was prepared at various pH levels. Four flasks, each set to pH values of 4.5, 5, 5.8 or 6.5 respectively were inoculated and incubated at 28 °C for 7 days. The influence of different potato concentrations on antibacterial activity was also tested. Three flasks each containing potato concentrations of 200, 300, and 400 g/L, were used. The filtrates obtained from each condition were evaluated for antibacterial activity against S. aureus ATCC 25923. The experiment was carried out in triplicates and average inhibition zone diameter was calculated [19].

Multi-factorial experiment: statistical optimization by Plackett-Burman design (PBD)

Following single-factor optimization of three key variables, Plackett-Burman design was applied to further investigate the influence of seven additional factors on antibacterial activity. This design of experiments (DOE) approach is particularly useful for efficiently identifying significant variables among many, using a limited number of experimental runs [20]. According to the Plackett-Burman structure, the minimum number of required experiments is n + 1, where n represents the number of variables studied [21]. In this case, seven factors, dextrose concentration, KNO₃, MgSO₄, FeSO₄, yeast extract, temperature, and inoculum size were evaluated across eight experimental runs, which is the smallest possible subset of combinations compared to the 128 trials required for a full 27 factorial design [22]. A combination with basal levels (0) of all variables was also included in the matrix, where each variable was represented at two levels, high (1) and low (-1) as shown in (Table 1), with potato dextrose broth (PDB) serving as the basal medium [23]. The experiments were done in triplicates and average inhibition zone diameters (mm) were reported.

Statistical analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel and main effect of each factor on antibacterial activity was calculated, enabling the identification of variables with significant influence while minimizing experimental complexity and resource use. The multiple correlation coefficient (R) and the determination coefficient (R²) were calculated to assess the fit of the regression model. The multiple correlation coefficient (R) describes the overall strength and direction of the linear relationship between the observed and predicted values of the response variable, while the determination coefficient (R2) provides a measure of how much variability of the observed response can be explained by the experimental factors and their interactions. The R2 value will always be a number between 0 and 1. The closer the R2 value to 1.00 the stronger the model is and the better it predicts response [20].

Regression analysis of the experimental data was performed using Microsoft Excel. The t- and p-values illustrate the significance of a given variable where a higher t-value and a lower p-value indicate a more significant effect of a given variable [20]. A verification experiment was conducted to confirm the results of the Plackett-Burman design. The antibacterial activity of the filtrate from the optimized medium was compared to the basal medium by measuring the inhibition zone diameter against S. aureus ATCC 25923 [24].

Selection of the solvent of choice for extracting antibacterial secondary metabolites from fungal filtrate

Four solvents (n-butanol, chloroform, ethyl acetate, and ethanol) were tested for extracting antibacterial secondary metabolites. The filtrate was mixed with each solvent in a 1:1 ratio in a separating funnel. After vigorous shaking, the mixture was kept at 4 °C for 1 h, and then crude extract was separated. This extraction was repeated three times, and the final extract was evaporated at 40 °C using a rotary vacuum evaporator to obtain a dry extract, which was stored at 4 °C for further analysis. The dry extracts were then dissolved in water before use [24]. The antibacterial activity of each extract was tested against S. aureus ATCC 25923 using the agar well diffusion method. The solvent yielding the highest antimicrobial activity was chosen.

Determination of the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) by broth microdilution

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the crude extract and commercial antibiotics (ciprofloxacin, fusidic acid, gentamicin), all of which were pharmaceutical grade and either purchased from the Egyptian market or gifted by a pharmaceutical company, was determined using broth microdilution against a total of 32 previously characterized S. aureus isolates, including 16 commensal S. aureus and 16 clinical MRSA strains, according to CLSI guidelines [25–27], with MDR detected in 31.5% of the commensal isolates and in 100% of the clinical MRSA isolates. Stock solutions of the extract (2 mg/mL), ciprofloxacin (512 µg/mL), fusidic acid (2048 µg/mL) and gentamicin (8192 µg/mL) were prepared. Two-fold serial dilutions were added in 90 µL volumes to the wells of a 96-well microtiter plate then 90 µL of fresh bacterial culture suspension (5 × 105 CFU/mL) were added to each well. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Positive and negative controls were included. After incubation, the optical density (OD) of the plates was read using the microplate reader at 630 nm. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration that inhibited visible microbial growth, this was confirmed by OD readings. The MIC50 and MIC90 were also calculated, representing the concentrations inhibiting 50% and 90% of the isolates, respectively [27, 28].

Assessing the combinations of the fungal crude extract with some antibiotics using the checkerboard assay

To explore potential synergistic effect, the antimicrobial interaction between the fungal crude extract and antibiotics (ciprofloxacin, fusidic acid, gentamicin) was assessed using the checkerboard microdilution method, as described by Bellio et al. (2021) [29]. The interaction was tested against S. aureus ATCC 6538 and the 16 clinical MRSA isolates, where the tested antibiotic was added to the first well in rows A–H and serially diluted from column 12 to 2, while the extract was added to the first well in column 12 and diluted from row A to G, creating different concentration combinations in each well, negative controls were included alongside the experiment. Following incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, results of the checkerboard combinations were interpreted using the fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI). FICI was calculated as follows:

FICI = FICA + FICB, where FICA = where A is the antibiotic while FICB = where B is the extract .

FICI results were interpreted as follows: FICI ≤ 0.5 indicated synergistic combinations, FICI > 0.5 to ≤ 4 indicated indifferent combinations and FICI > 4 indicated antagonistic combinations [30].

Cytotoxicity of the butanolic extract using MTT assay

The cellular toxicity of the fungal butanolic extract was determined by MTT (3-[(4, 5-dimethyl thiazol-2-yl)-2, 5-diphenyl] tetrazolium bromide) assay against Human skin fibroblasts (HSF) [31]. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Minimal Essential Medium (DMEM) (Biowest, France) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Biowest, France), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 1% streptomycin (Biowest, Canada). They were seeded at a density of 15 × 10³ cells/well in sterile 96-well flat-bottom tissue culture plates 24 h before treatment. After 24 h, serial dilutions of the extract (ranging from 2 mg/mL to 7.8125 µg/mL) were added to the wells. The cells were then incubated for an additional 24 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator (Binder, Germany). Following incubation, MTT (Biobasic, Canada) dissolved in PBS (Biowest, Canada) was added to each well at a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL, and the plates were incubated for 3 h in the dark at 37 °C. The MTT solution was then removed, and 100 µL of DMSO (Fischer Scientific, USA) was added to dissolve the formazan crystals. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). The percentage of cell viability at each concentration was calculated relative to the control and IC50 values for each compound were determined using GraphPad software.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis of P. citrinum butanolic extract

The fungal extract was analyzed using GC-MS to identify its metabolite profile [32]. The fungal extract was analyzed using a Thermo Scientific TRACE GC Ultra with a TG1MS column (30 m, 0.32 mm internal diameter, 0.25 μm film thickness). A 2 µL sample was injected in splitless mode, with helium as the carrier gas (1 mL/min). The column temperature was initially set at 80 °C and increased to 240 °C at 7 °C/min, then raised to 280 °C at 2 °C/min, holding for 5 min. The mass spectrometer was operated in electron ionization mode at 70 eV, with a mass range of 50–500 amu. The NIST 2.0 library was used to identify compounds based on their mass spectra and identified components were quantified by their relative peak areas [33].

Growth curve analysis

A growth curve kinetics assay was performed to evaluate the antibacterial activity of dibutyl phthalate (DBP), the most abundant compound detected in the fungal extract, alone and in combination with gentamicin against one representative clinical MRSA isolate (isolate 22). Four experimental conditions were prepared in sterile test tubes containing a final volume of 5 mL. Tween 80 was added to all tubes at a final concentration of 2% to improve compound dispersion. The tested conditions comprised a growth control containing sterile saline, gentamicin at 0.5X MIC, dibutyl phthalate at 25% (v/v), and a combination of gentamicin (0.5X MIC) with dibutyl phthalate (25%).

Each tube was inoculated with 50 µL of a fresh bacterial suspension adjusted so that the final bacterial density in the test mixture was approximately 10⁶ CFU/mL. The tubes were incubated at 37 °C, and bacterial viability was assessed after 1, 3, and 24 h. At each time point, samples were serially diluted ten-fold in sterile saline, and 20 µL from each dilution were plated in duplicate on nutrient agar plates. After incubation, colonies were counted and expressed as CFU/mL [34].

Results

Soil fungi isolation and in vitro antibacterial assay screening

The soil samples yielded 23 fungal isolates that were screened for their antibacterial activity against the standard S. aureus ATCC 25923 strain using agar well diffusion. Only fungal isolates Y3 and Y18 demonstrated antibacterial activity against S. aureus ATCC 25923 with inhibition zone diameters of 26 and 13 mm, respectively. These isolates have also shown activity against other gram-positive standard strains including Streptococcus agalactiae CCM 6187 and Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212 (Data not shown). Negative control well containing only sterile broth medium showed no inhibition beyond the diameter of the well (8 mm), confirming that the antimicrobial activity was attributable to the fungal metabolites and not the medium itself.

Identification of fungal isolates

Morphological identification

Twenty-three fungal isolates were initially obtained and evaluated for their antimicrobial activity (supplementary Table S1). Among them, the isolate exhibiting the most potent inhibitory effect was chosen for subsequent analyses. The isolate designated Y3, which demonstrated the highest activity against S. aureus ATCC 25923, was subjected to morphological characterization. Microscopic examination revealed conidiophores, metulae, phialides, and chains of conidia. Based on Y3’s macroscopic and microscopic features (Fig. 1A-C), it was identified as Penicillium citrinum.

Optimization of the fermentation conditions

Single factorial experiments

To optimize the production of antibacterial secondary metabolites, the incubation period, pH, and potato concentration were altered, one factor at a time. A minimum of 2 days was required for P. citrinum to exert its antibacterial activity, and the highest activity, evidenced by largest inhibition zone diameter average (27.2 mm) was observed after 7 days of incubation. This was followed by a slight decrease in inhibition zone diameter. The largest inhibition zone diameter was recorded at pH 5.8 and potato concentration equal to 300 g/L making 7 days of incubation, a pH of 5.8, and potato concentration of 300 g/L the optimal conditions to maximize the antibacterial activity of the fungus as shown in supplementary Figure S2.

Multi-factorial experiment (Placket-Burman design)

Placket-Burman design was used to evaluate the effect of seven different variables on the antimicrobial activity of P. citrinum. The maximum antibacterial activity (29 mm) was achieved in combination 8, while the minimum activity (20 mm) was observed in combination 5 (Table 2).

Five variables, KNO3, FeSO4, dextrose, yeast extract concentration and inoculum size, showed positive main effect which indicated that these variables resulted in a higher inhibition zone diameter when examined at high levels, while MgSO4 and temperature showed a negative main effect indicating that the result showed a higher inhibition zone diameter when these variables were examined at a low level (supplementary Figure S3). KNO3 and FeSO4 were the most significant variables with p-value 0.030292344 and confidence level of 96.97%. The predicted optimum conditions were the following: dextrose concentration (25 g/L), KNO3 (1 g/L), FeSO4 (0.01 g/L), yeast extract (0.5 g/L), temperature (28 °C), inoculum size (3 fungal discs) and no MgSO4.

The optimized conditions resulted in a significantly larger average inhibition zone diameter (31 mm) compared to the basal media, against S. aureus ATCC 25923, representing an increase by 1.29-fold compared to average inhibition zone diameter (24 mm) of the filtrate resulting from the basal conditions (24 mm). The present result confirmed the validity of the optimized conditions.

Selection of the solvent of choice for extracting antibacterial secondary metabolites from fungal filtrate

Ethanol, ethyl acetate, chloroform and n-butanol were used as solvents to extract the antibacterial secondary metabolites from the fungal filtrate. The n-butanolic extract produced the largest average inhibition zone (25.7 mm). Chloroform extract, ethyl acetate and ethanolic extract produced considerably smaller inhibition zones (10.7 mm, 10 mm and 8.7 mm, respectively) as shown in supplementary Figure S4. Therefore, n-butanol was selected as the solvent of choice for extraction.

Determination of the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) using broth microdilution

The MIC of the butanolic P. citrinum extract was evaluated against previously characterized 16 commensal S. aureus and 16 clinical multidrug-resistant MRSA isolates. MIC values ranged between 500 and 1000 µg/mL, where 37.5% and 43.8% of the commensal and clinical isolates, respectively were inhibited by 500 µg/mL. The remaining isolates had an MIC of 1000 µg/mL as shown in Fig. 2. Since there was no significant difference between the MIC values against clinical versus commensal isolates, the MIC of the tested antibiotics was determined against clinical isolates only.

Clinical isolates exhibited 93.8% resistance to ciprofloxacin, with MIC values ranging from 2 to 256 µg/mL, MIC50 = 64 µg/mL and MIC90 = 256 µg/mL. Resistance to fusidic acid was observed in 68.8% of the isolates, with MICs ranging from < 2–1024 µg/mL, MIC50 = 16 µg/mL and MIC90 = 512 µg/mL. Gentamicin resistance was second to ciprofloxacin at 87.5%, however it showed the widest MIC range < 8–4096 µg/mL with the MIC50= 128 µg/mL and MIC90= 4096 µg/mL. MIC50 and MIC90 of the butanolic extract against the clinical MRSA isolates were both equal to 1000 µg/mL (Table 4 and supplementary Table S2).

Assessing the combinations of the fungal crude extract with other antibiotics using checkerboard assay

P. citrinum butanolic extract was combined with ciprofloxacin, fusidic acid or gentamicin, in different concentrations, and the combinations were tested against 16 clinical multidrug-resistant MRSA isolates. P. citrinum butanolic extract/ciprofloxacin combination exhibited a synergistic effect against 6 isolates, where in the majority of the cases inhibition of growth was achieved at concentrations equal one fourth of the original MICs. P. citrinum butanolic extract/gentamicin combination exhibited synergy against all tested isolates, with 4- to 64-fold reduction in the MICs relative to the agents alone (Table 4).

Discussion

Given the escalating crisis of AMR, there is an urgent need for innovative approaches to tackle resistant infections [6]. One promising approach is the investigation of fungal metabolites as potential antimicrobial agents [7]. Penicillium species are recognized for their bioactive compounds that exhibit various biological properties. This study evaluated the bioactivity of Penicillium citrinum extract isolated from Alexandria, Egypt as an antibacterial agent against Staphylococcus aureus, including several MRSA isolates.

Optimizing culture conditions is crucial for enhancing the yield and antimicrobial potential of fungal strains. Optimization of P. citrinum antibacterial activity was done using single factorial experiments as well as statistical experimental Plackett-Burman design. Results showed that the optimal culture conditions were 7-day incubation period and a pH of 5.8, consistent with findings from previous studies [19]. Although there is limited research on how varying potato concentrations impacts bioactive compound production in P. citrinum, our findings revealed that a concentration of 300 g/L was optimal, indicating that this factor affects the antibacterial secondary metabolite production.

The application of the Plackett-Burman design in this study allowed for the identification of key variables that significantly influenced the production of antibacterial metabolites by Penicillium citrinum [21]. The Plackett-Burman design included dextrose, yeast extract, potassium nitrate, magnesium sulphate, ferrous sulphate, temperature and inoculum size, selected based on literature due to their reported influence of fungal secondary metabolite production [35–40].The results demonstrated higher concentrations of dextrose, KNO3, FeSO4, yeast extract, and inoculum size, together with the absence of magnesium sulfate and lower incubation temperatures were more favorable for antibacterial metabolite production. Some of these findings were in line with previous studies, which reported similar results [19, 38, 40]. There is paucity of data describing the optimization of the culture conditions for antibacterial secondary metabolite production by P. citrinum using the Plackett-Burman design. Our findings highlight the importance of balancing key factors such as nutrient concentrations and environmental conditions to enhance the production of antibacterial compounds. These insights offer a solid foundation for scaling up P. citrinum cultivation, with potential applications in antibacterial therapy development.

It is equally important to focus on the downstream processes involved in extracting the desired metabolites [41]. Various methods for extracting bioactive metabolites from fungal strains exist. Yet, the common technique uses equal volumes of the fungal culture filtrate and a solvent, allowing for the partitioning of bioactive compounds between the aqueous and organic phases [42–44]. As opposed to the numerous recent studies that primarily utilized ethyl acetate for the extraction of bioactive metabolites from P. citrinum [45–48], in the current study, the n-butanolic extract produced a higher antibacterial effect against S. aureus ATCC 25923, relative to ethanol, ethyl acetate and chloroform. Again, this highlights the importance of condition optimization, including the solvent used for extraction, as it can dramatically impact the extracted bioactive compounds.

The MIC of the butanolic P. citrinum extract ranged from 500 to 1000 µg/mL against previously characterized commensal S. aureus and clinical MRSA strains, with no significant difference. This was true despite the difference in multidrug resistance (MDR) status between the commensal strains (31.5%) and the clinical MRSA strains (100%). These results align with findings from Kaur et al., who reported a MIC of 500 µg/mL for P. citrinum against a clinical MRSA isolate [15].

This consistency in MIC values across both commensal S. aureus and clinical MRSA strains highlights the broad-spectrum potential of the extract. It suggests that the bioactive compounds in the butanolic extract may act on bacterial mechanisms shared by both strains, regardless of their varying resistance profiles, which is particularly promising as it indicates that the extract could be effective in combating MRSA infections, which are often more challenging to treat due to their MDR.

The potential synergy between the butanolic P. citrinum extract and gentamicin, ciprofloxacin or fusidic acid was assessed using the checkerboard assay. The results demonstrated a selective synergy, where complete synergy was observed with gentamicin across all MRSA isolates, 37.5% of the isolates showed synergy when the extract was combined with ciprofloxacin, and no synergy was seen with fusidic acid.

The selective synergy observed between the P. citrinum extract and certain antibiotics may be partly attributed to a specific mechanism such as the inhibition of certain efflux pumps, a mechanism that is commonly involved in multidrug resistance. In previous studies, plant extracts were shown to inhibit efflux pumps in S. aurues which are responsible for the extrusion of antibiotics like ciprofloxacin and gentamicin [49].These findings suggest that the P. citrinum extract might operate through a similar mechanism, enhancing the efficacy of antibiotics by preventing their expulsion from the bacterial cell. This further supports the potential of P. citrinum metabolites as adjuncts in combination therapies to combat MDR infections.

The IC50 value of the butanolic extract against human skin fibroblasts was 359 µg/mL. This value is relatively low, which is consistent with previous studies suggesting that P. citrinum extracts may possess anticancer properties [15, 47]. It is important to note that the concentration used in the successful synergistic antibiotic combinations were below this threshold. In these combinations, the extract’s concentration remained well under the 300 µg/mL mark, ensuring that the extract could exert its antimicrobial effects without causing significant harm to human cells. 100% cell viability was maintained in all successful combinations of the extract with gentamicin, where extract concentrations ranged from 125 µg/mL to 31.25 µg/mL. This indicates that, at concentrations associated with enhanced antibacterial effects, the extract did not exert any detectable cytotoxicity. These results support the extract’s compatibility and safety when used in combination with gentamicin at active doses, which indicates that the extract could be developed as a viable antimicrobial agent, particularly in synergistic combinations with antibiotics, where its concentrations would stay within a non-toxic range for human cells.

The GC analysis of the butanolic extract of P. citrinum revealed the presence of 25 distinct compounds. Some of these compounds have reported biological activities (supplementary Table S4), e.g. Tetradecane [50], Hentriacontane [51], 1-Eicosanol [52], Heneicosane [53] ,1,2-Benzenedicarboxylic acid and bis(2-methylpropyl) ester [54], which is also known as diisobutyl phthalate (DiBP) reported to have antimicrobial activity. Dibutyl phthalate (DBP), the most dominant compound in the butanolic extract, has been shown to possess antimicrobial activity against several microorganisms [54, 55]. In our study, the growth kinetics assay demonstrated that DBP alone produced an inhibitory effect on the growth of the selected clinical isolate. However, DBP did not enhance gentamicin activity, suggesting that the observed synergistic effect of the crude extract may not be solely due to this major compound. Instead, the synergy observed with the crude extract is likely the result of complementary interactions among multiple metabolites.

Although Penicillium citrinum has been previously investigated for its biological activity, the present work focuses on evaluating its antimicrobial activity against multiple clinical multidrug-resistant MRSA isolates, addressing a significant clinical challenge. The study also explored the potential synergistic effects of the crude extract with commonly used commercial antibiotics, providing valuable preliminary data and highlighting the extract’s antimicrobial potential. A limitation of the current study is that it focused on screening crude fungal extracts rather than purified active compounds. Synergy testing with purified metabolites is planned for the second phase of the project, which will allow a more precise determination of the contributions of individual metabolites to the observed antimicrobial and synergistic effects.

Conclusion

This study highlights the antibacterial potential of the crude extract of Penicillium citrinum. Fermentation optimization improved extract yield, and synergy testing revealed consistent enhancement of gentamicin activity and partial synergy with ciprofloxacin. Cytotoxicity assays confirmed the safety of the effective concentration of the crude extract, supporting its potential for therapeutic use. The observed synergistic effects are likely due to the combined action of multiple metabolites within the crude extract rather than a single compound. These findings suggest that P. citrinum extract could serve as a valuable source of antimicrobial agents. Further studies are required to purify the active secondary metabolites, elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying their antimicrobial activity, and assess their potential as adjunct therapeutic agents in antibiotic combinations against resistant infections. In vivo validation will also be essential to support future clinical applications.
