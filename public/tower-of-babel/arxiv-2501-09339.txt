Pretty-good simulation of all quantum measurements by projective measurements
Michał Kotowski, Michał Oszmaniec

Abstract
In quantum theory general measurements are described by so-called Positive Operator-Valued Measures (POVMs). We show that in d d -dimensional quantum systems an application of depolarizing noise with constant (independent of d d ) visibility parameter makes any POVM simulable by a randomized implementation of projective measurements that do not require any auxiliary systems to be realized. This result significantly limits the asymptotic advantage that POVMs can offer over projective measurements in various information-processing tasks, including state discrimination, shadow tomography or quantum metrology. We also apply our findings to questions originating from quantum foundations by asymptotically improving the range of visibilities for which noisy pure states of two qudits admit a local model for generalized measurements. As a byproduct, we give asymptotically tight (in terms of dimension) bounds on critical visibility for which all POVMs are jointly measurable. On the technical side we use recent advances in POVM simulation, the solution to the celebrated Kadison-Singer problem, and a method of approximate implementation of “nearly projective” POVMs by a convex combination of projective measurements, which we call dimension-deficient Naimark theorem. Finally, some of our intermediate results show (on information-theoretic grounds) the existence of circuit-knitting strategies allowing to simulate general 2 ​ N 2N qubit circuits by randomization of subcircuits operating on N + 1 N+1 qubit systems, with a constant (independent of N N ) probabilistic overhead.

While in the classical picture of the world physical objects have well-defined attributes that are merely revealed by performing a measurement, in quantum physics system’s characteristics can be viewed as emerging in the course of the measurement process itself. II
Preliminaries

We start by providing the necessary background and notation on POVMs, their simulability properties, and mathematics that will be used in the rest of the paper.

We will be concerned with generalized measurements on a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{d}
. A positive operator-valued measure (POVM) is a tuple
\mathbf{M}=\left(M_{1},M_{2},\ldots,M_{n}\right)
of non-negative (
M_{i}\geq 0
) operators (called effects) normalized to identity (
\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}=\mathbb{I}_{d}
). We will denote the set of all quantum measurements on a finite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
by
\mathrm{POVM}(\mathcal{H})
. When
\mathbf{M}
is performed on a quantum state
\rho
, it yields a random outcome
i
, distributed according to the probability distribution
\mathrm{p}(i|\rho,\mathbf{M})=\tr\left(\rho M_{i}\right)
(Born rule). A measurement
\mathbf{P}=\left(P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{n}\right)
is called projective if its effects satisfy
P_{i}P_{j}=\delta_{ij}P_{i}
. The set of projective measurements on
\mathcal{H}
will be denoted by
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{H})
. According to the postulates of quantum mechanics, a projective measurement can be associated to measurements of a quantum observable
O
(effects
P_{i}
are projections onto eigenspaces of
O
).

Remark 1
.

Throughout the paper we will be considering measurements with
finite
number of outcomes. However, our results naturally extend to the general POVMs on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with continuous output sets. This is because of the main result of
[
49
]
which shows that general continuous-outcome measurements on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
can be realized as randomization of measurements with at most
d^{2}
nonzero effects. We are not concerned with the state of the system after the measurement – incorporating this would require considering more complicated objects: so-called quantum instruments
[
50
]
.

A natural way to physically realize a generalized quantum measurement is to perform a suitable projective measurement on an extended space.

Theorem 1

(Naimark extension theorem
[
1
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}=(M_{1},\ldots,M_{n})
be an
n
-outcome POVM on
\mathcal{H}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{d}
with rank-one effects (i.e.
M_{i}=\alpha_{i}\left|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}\right|
). Then there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{ex}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n}
, which contains
\mathcal{H}
as a subspace, and a rank-one projective measurement
\mathbf{P}^{\mathbf{M}}=(\left|\phi_{1}\rangle\langle\phi_{1}\right|,\ldots,\left|\phi_{n}\rangle\langle\phi_{n}\right|)
on
\mathcal{H}_{ex}
, such that for all states
\rho
supported on
\mathcal{H}
and all outcomes
i
we have

\tr(\rho M_{i})=\tr(\rho\left|\phi_{i}\rangle\langle\phi_{i}\right|)\ .

(1)

The extended space is often realized as a tensor product with an ancilla space, i.e.
\mathcal{H}_{ex}=\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{a}
. In order to realize the embedding of
\mathcal{H}
into
\mathcal{H}_{ex}
, one typically fixes a state
\left|\psi_{0}\right>\in\mathcal{H}_{a}
and identifies the subspace
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\left|\psi\right>\left|\psi_{0}\right>,\left|\psi\right>\in\mathcal{H}\}
with
\mathcal{H}
. The drawback of the Naimark theorem is that the dimension of the extended space
\mathcal{H}_{ex}
(and thus also the dimension of the ancilla space
\mathcal{H}_{a}
) is proportional to the number of outcomes
n
of the target POVM
\mathbf{M}
.

It is however possible to reduce the dimension of the ancilla space by complementing quantum resources with suitably chosen classical processing of generalized measurements. There exist three natural classical operations that can be applied to POVMs: taking convex combinations, post-processing and postselection. A convex combination of two POVMs
\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N}
(with the same number of outcomes
n
) can be operationally interpreted as a POVM realized by applying, in a given experimental run, measurements
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{N}
with probabilities
p
and
1-p
respectively (where
p\in[0,1]
is the mixing parameter). The resulting POVM is denoted by
p\mathbf{M}+(1-p)\mathbf{N}
and its
i
-th effect is given by
\left[p\mathbf{M}+(1-p)\mathbf{N}\right]_{i}=pM_{i}+(1-p)N_{i}
. See
[
51
]
for a thorough exposition of the convex structure of quantum measurements. Classical post-processing, on the other hand, refers to application of probabilistic relabelling of the measurements outcomes
[
52
,
53
]
. For an
n
-outcome POVM
\mathbf{M}
, the application of post-processing results in another POVM
\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{M})
with
n^{\prime}
outcomes and effects
\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{M})_{i}=\sum_{j}q_{i|j}M_{j}
, where
q_{i|j}
is an
n^{\prime}\times n
array of conditional probabilities, i.e.,
q_{i|j}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i}q_{i|j}=1
for each
j
. A class of projectively simulable measurements, introduced in
[
2
]
and denoted by
\mathrm{S}\mathbb{P}(d)
, comprises measurements on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
that can be realized by randomization and post-processing of projective measurements on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, i.e., without the use of ancillary systems.

Lastly, postselection, i.e., the process of disregarding certain outcomes, can be used to implement otherwise inaccessible POVMs.
We say that a POVM
\mathbf{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n},L_{n+1})
simulates a POVM
\mathbf{M}=(M_{1},\ldots,M_{n})
with postselection probability
q
if
L_{i}=qM_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. When we implement
\mathbf{L}
, then, conditioned on getting the first
n
outcomes, we obtain samples from
\mathbf{M}
. Thus, we can simulate
\mathbf{M}
by implementing
\mathbf{L}
and postselecting on non-observing the outcome
n+1
.
The probability of successfully doing so is
q
, which means that a single sample of
\mathbf{M}
is obtained by implementing
\mathbf{L}
on average
1/q
number of times. The reader is referred to
[
27
]
for a more detailed discussion of simulation via postselection.

Throughout this work we will be extensively using depolarizing channel acting on quantum states and (by duality) on quantum measurements. For
t\in[0,1]
(known as the visibility parameter) and
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
we define
\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}(X)\coloneqq tX+(1-t)\frac{\tr(X)}{d}\mathbb{I}_{d}
, where
\mathbb{I}_{d}
is identity operator on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Action of depolarizing channel on a quantum measurement
\mathbf{M}=(M_{1},\ldots,M_{n})
is defined by setting
\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}(\mathbf{M})
to be a measurement whose effects are depolarized versions of the original measurement operators:
[\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}(\mathbf{M})]_{i}=\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}(M_{i})
. Note that due to self-duality of
\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}
(with respect to Hilbert-Schmidt inner product) and functional form of the Born rule we have
\mathrm{p}(i|\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}(\rho),\mathbf{M})=\mathrm{p}(i|\rho,\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}(\mathbf{M}))
, i.e. output statistics of a depolarized state
\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}(\rho)
measured by the ideal measurement
\mathbf{M}
are identical to measurement statistics of a noisy POVM
\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}(\mathbf{M})
on the ideal state
\rho
. Following
[
2
]
we will use white noise robustness to quantify the non-projective character of a POVM on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
:

t_{\mathrm{S}\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{M})=\max\left\{t\ |\ \mathrm{\Phi}_{t}(\mathbf{M})\in\mathrm{S}\mathbb{P}(d)\right\}\ .

(2)

In a given dimension
d
, the minimal value of this function quantifies the worst-case robustness of quantum measurements against projective simulability:

t_{\mathrm{S}\mathbb{P}}(d)=\min_{\mathbf{M}\in\mathrm{POVM}(\mathbb{C}^{d})}t_{\mathrm{S}\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{M}).

(3)

Prior to this work it was known that
[
2
]

t_{\mathrm{S}\mathbb{P}}(d)\geq\frac{1}{d}
. However, no matching
upper bounds
on this quantity were known.

Notation.
We will use
\|A\|
to denote the operator norm of a linear operator
A
, and
n
to denote
n
-element set
\{1,\ldots n\}
. Finally, for two positive-valued functions
f(x),g(x)
we will write
f=\Theta(g)
if there exist positive constants
c,C>0
such that
cf(x)\leq g(x)\leq Cf(x)

.

III
Main results and consequences

In this section we present our main findings regarding the simulation of general POVMs via simpler classes of measurements and classical resources. Our first finding confirms a conjecture from
[
28
]
which asserted that arbitrary POVMs can be simulated with constant success probability by measurements requiring only a single auxiliary qubit to be implemented.

Result 1

(Constant success probability of simulation via POVMs with a single auxiliary qubit)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a POVM on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Then there exists a probability distribution
\{p_{\beta}\}
and a collection of POVMs
\{\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)}\}
such that (i) for every
\beta
the POVM
\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)}
has at most
d+1
outcomes and can be implemented by a single projective measurement on
\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
(i.e. using only a single auxiliary qubit as an ancilla), followed by classical post-processing, (ii) the convex combination
\mathbf{L}=\sum_{\beta}p_{\beta}\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)}
simulates
\mathbf{M}
with constant postselection probability
q=1/8=0.125
, i.e.
\sum_{\beta}p_{\beta}\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)}=(q\mathbf{M},(1-q)\mathbb{I}_{d})
.

The proof of this result is provided in Section
V
. Therein, we also give a general version of this result (Theorem
5
), which shows that by allowing to use POVMs that employ
k
-dimensional ancillas, it is possible to simulate an arbitrary measurement with success probability
q\geq 1-\Theta(k^{-1/2})
.

Our second result concerns the scenario when we are not allowed to use any auxiliary qubits. In this case it is impossible to implement an arbitrary POVM on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with success probability greater than
1/d
(cf.
[
27
]
). For this reason we turn to simulation of noisy (depolarized) versions of quantum measurements.

Result 2

(Depolarizing noise with constant visibility makes arbitrary POVM simulable by projective measurements)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}=(M_{1},\ldots,M_{n})
be a POVM on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Then, for
c=0.02
we have
\mathrm{\mathrm{\Phi}}_{c}(\mathbf{M})\in\mathrm{S}\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
. In other words, for every POVM
\mathbf{M}
its noisy version with effects
\left[\mathrm{\Phi}_{c}(\mathbf{M})\right]_{i}=cM_{i}+(1-c)\frac{\tr(M_{i})}{d}\mathbb{I}_{d}
can be simulated as a convex combination of projective measurements that do not require any ancillas to be implemented.

The crucial feature of both Result
1
and Result
2
is that the parameters
q,c
are independent of the dimension of the Hilbert space. This is in stark contrast to a number of prior results regarding white noise robustness of entanglement
[
54
]
, nonlocality
[
55
]
, non-Gaussianity for fermionic systems
[
56
]
or incompatibility of projective measurements
[
57
]
.

Although simple to state, our findings give rise to a number of nontrivial consequences in quantum information and quantum computing, which we describe in what follows. We will focus on explaining the context and relevance of different applications, delegating more involved proofs of technical statements to Appendix
B
.

III.1
Limitation of usefulness of POVMs in state discrimination and other linear games

Quantum state discrimination is one of the primary
applications of POVMs. There exist many variants of this problem (see
[
7
]
for a recent review) but in what follows we will focus on its most basic incarnation, the so-called minimal-error state discrimination. In this scenario one is given a source of quantum states which generates a state
\rho_{i}
with a priori probability
p_{i}
(the source can be characterized by an ensemble of quantum states
\mathcal{E}=\{p_{i},\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
). The task is then to find the label
i
by performing a POVM
\mathbf{M}=(M_{1},\ldots,M_{n})
on an unknown quantum state generated by
\mathcal{E}
. In the problem of minimal-error state discrimination, one is interested in optimizing the average success probability
p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{M})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\tr(\rho_{i}M_{i})
. Besides foundational interests, minimal-error state discrimination appears naturally in different contexts, as many tasks can be phrased as a variant of this problem: quantum communication
[
58
]
, asymptotic quantum cloning
[
59
]
, finding hidden subgroup states
[
18
,
19
]
, or port-based teleportation
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
. The following proposition shows the limitation of the relative power of generalized measurements over projective measurements (as a function of dimension
d
):

Proposition 1

(No unbounded advantage of general POVMs over projective measurements in minimal-error state discrimination)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}=\{p_{i},\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a source of quantum states on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Let
p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{M})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\tr(\sigma_{i}M_{i})
be the success probability of discriminating states in
\mathcal{E}
via POVM
\mathbf{M}=(M_{1},\ldots,M_{n})
. Then, for every
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathbf{M}
, a projectively simulable POVM
\mathbf{N}=\mathrm{\Phi}_{c}(\mathbf{M})\in\mathrm{S}\mathbb{P}(d)
satisfies
c\,p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{M})\leq\ p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{N})
, where
c=0.02
. Consequently, we have

(1/c)\max_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{d})}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{P})\geq\max_{\mathbf{M}\in\mathrm{POVM}(\mathbb{C}^{d})}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{M})\ .

(4)

Proof.

We have

III.2
Limitations of POVMs in shadow tomography and related protocols

Another important application of our results concerns potential usefulness of POVMs in shadow tomography. Shadow tomography, introduced in
[
63
]
, is an important algorithmic primitive whose purpose is to estimate expectation values of multiple non-commuting observables on an (unknown) quantum state (see
[
31
]
for a recent review of the method and exposition in the broader context of quantum technologies). The basic premise of the technique is that by avoiding direct tomography of a quantum state, it is possible to simultaneously estimate many (even exponentially many) observables on an
n
-qubit state by performing only

measurement rounds. The most popular classical shadow protocols
[
63
,
64
,
65
]
involve randomized implementation of projective measurements realized by appending randomly-chosen unitary transformation (chosen from the ensemble relevant to the particular application). However, in full generality, generalized measurements
[
66

13
,
67
]
can be used to define classical shadows and in some cases offer an advantage over the standard protocols (see also
[
68
,
69
,
70
]
for the complementary perspective in which incompatibility theory was proposed to estimate multiple non-commuting quantities for qubit and fermionic systems). The following result shows that, for a broad class of classical shadow protocols, POVMs do not offer asymptotically unbounded (with the dimension of the Hilbert space) advantage compared to projectively simulable measurements.

Proposition 2

(Limitations of single-shot classical shadows based on generalized measurements)
.

Let

be an unbiased estimator of the expectation value of

\mathbb{E}\hat{e}_{O}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{e}_{O}(i)\tr(\rho M_{i})=\tr(\rho O)\ ,

(9)

for every state
\rho

It is possible to produce an analogous statement about the lack of advantage for general POVMs over measurements

Our next application concerns local hidden variable models for correlations originating from noisy pure states of two qudits, i.e states of the form

III.6
Incompatibility robustness of POVMs

Measurement incompatibility, one of the defining features of quantum theory, states that certain POVMs cannot be simultaneously measured. Incompatibility underpins many nonclassical aspects of quantum physics, such as uncertainty relations or nonlocality, and has applications in numerous quantum protocols and subroutines (see e.g
[
75
]
for a recent review).

For projective measurements
\mathbf{P},\mathbf{P}^{\prime}
incompatibility is equivalent to non-commuting of measurement operators
P_{i},P^{\prime}_{j}
. However, a collection of general POVMs
\{\mathbf{M}^{(x)}\}_{x\in X}
can be jointly measurable in the sense that there exists a parent POVM
\mathbf{G}=(G_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
that can simulate each
\mathbf{M}^{(x)}
, i.e. for every
x\in X
we have
\mathbf{M}^{(x)}=\mathcal{Q}^{(x)}(\mathbf{G})
, for some classical post-processing operations
\mathcal{Q}^{(x)}
. Generally speaking, every collection of POVMs becomes jointly measurable once sufficient amount of noise is added to the measurements in question, with the paradigmatic example being noisy Pauli measurements:
M^{X,\eta_{x}}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{I}\pm\eta_{x}X)
,
M^{Y,\eta_{Y}}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{I}\pm\eta_{Y}Y)
and
M^{Z,\eta_{Z}}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{I}\pm\eta_{Z}Z)
, that are jointly measurable if an only if
\eta^{2}_{X}+\eta^{2}_{Y}+\eta^{2}_{Y}\leq 1

[
76
]
. There has been a significant interest in recent years to quantify noise tolerance for incompatibility for pairs of measurements
[
77
]
, mutually unbiased bases
[
78
]
, or collections of measurements exhibiting symmetries
[
79
]
(including Majorana-fermion observables
[
70
]
).

In
[
29
,
30
]
it was proven that for
t\leq t^{PM}_{iso}
(where
t^{PM}_{iso}
is given by Eq. (
17
)) all measurements of the form
\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}(\mathbf{P})
, where
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
, are jointly measurable. Our Result
2
allows to straightforwardly generalize this result to noisy POVMs.

Proposition 5

(Improved compatibility region for noisy POVMs)
.

Let
c=0.02
be the constant appearing in Result
2
. Furthermore assume

t\leq c\ t^{PM}_{iso}\approx\frac{c\log(d)}{d}\ .

(19)

\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}(\mathbf{M})
are jointly-measurable, where
\mathbf{M}
ranges over all POVMs in
\mathbb{C}^{d}

Proof.

Clearly, noisy versions

of all projectively-simulable POVMs

are jointly measurable for

\mathrm{\Phi}_{c}(\mathbf{M})\in\mathrm{S}\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
for all

(\mathrm{\Phi}_{\tau}\circ\mathrm{\Phi}_{c})(\mathbf{M})=\mathrm{\Phi}_{c\tau}(\mathbf{M})

\tau\leq t^{PM}_{iso}

∎

The inequality Eq.(
19

d)
– this is because
[
30
]
proved that noisy projective measurements
\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}(\mathbf{P})
are incompatible for

. The occurrence of the same bound

for the critical visibility for this problem and local models for projective measurements for isotropic states is due to a one-to-one relation between incompatibility of noisy measurements on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and so-called steering of isotropic states

4

4

Steering is a form of quantum correlation in bipartite systems weaker than non-locality. It assumes a partial trust in measurements performed in one subsystem participating in the Bell scenario.

III.7
Discussion and open problems

In this work we gave a surprising structural result on the power of generalized measurements in quantum information theory. Namely, we have shown that measurements implementable with no ancillas or low dimensional-ancillas can offer similar performance to general POVMs in a variety of applications. We realized this by concrete simulation protocols that build on previous results on POVM simulability
[
28
]
, newly-introduced dimension-deficient Naimark extension theorem (cf. Theorem
6
) and the use of the solution to the celebrated Kadison-Singer conjecture (cf. Theorem
3
).

There is a number of interesting open problems that originate from our work. First, we expect that it is possible to extend our simulation techniques to other relevant objects: quantum channels, instruments and combs. Second, it would be interesting to explore whether the solution to the Kadison-Singer problem (Theorem
3
and its generalizations, see

IV
Overview of the proof of the main results

In this part we present the outline of the proof of our main findings – Results
1
and
2
. The proof relies on explicit simulation protocol and consists of several steps and simplifications – see Fig.
3
for a schematic presentation of the argument. Along the way we introduce additional Lemmas whose proofs are given in latter parts of the article. The starting point is a target POVM
\mathbf{M}\in\mathrm{POVM}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
about which we do not assume anything except having a finite number of outcomes (our results easily extend to general POVMs with continuous number of outcomes, cf. Remark
1
).

Step 1 – Special form of a POVM.
We first use post-processing to reduce the structure of the target POVM in such a manner that the POVM to-be simulated has rank-one effects of
nearly equal magnitude
. It is well known that every POVM can be obtained by coarse-graining of a POVM with rank
1
effects. However, the fact that magnitude of the effects of fine grained POVM can be made (nearly) uniform is to our knowledge new.

Lemma 1

(Nearly flat fine-graining of POVMs)
.

For every
\delta\in(0,1)
and for every
\mathbf{M}\in\mathrm{POVM}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
there exist
\epsilon_{\ast}>0
such that for all
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{\ast})
there exists a POVM
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}
and a stochastic map
\mathcal{Q}
such that
\mathbf{M}=\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{M}^{\prime})
and
M^{\prime}_{i}=\alpha_{i}\left|\psi_{i}\right>\left<\psi_{i}\right|
,
\frac{\max_{i}\alpha_{i}}{\min_{i}\alpha_{i}}\leq 1+\delta
, and furthermore
\max_{i}\alpha_{i}\leq\epsilon
.

The proof of the Lemma is given in Appendix
A
. In what follows the flatness parameter
\delta>0
will play a relatively minor role and the right intuition is to think that we can set
\delta=0
. However, in order to keep our analysis elementary (that is, without invoking functional theoretic details) we have decided to keep nonzero
\delta
, and to phrase all intermediate auxiliary results with this parameter being present. At the end of the argument we take the limit
\delta\to 0
.

Step 2 – Simulation with postselection via simpler POVMs.
We show that a POVM with “nearly flat” effects of rank 1 can be simulated with postselection by
nearly projective
quantum measurements.

Lemma 2

(Simulation with postselection via nearly projective measurements)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}=\left(\alpha_{1}\left|\psi_{1}\right>\left<\psi_{1}\right|,\ldots,\alpha_{n}\left|\psi_{n}\right>\left<\psi_{n}\right|\right)\in\mathrm{POVM}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
be such that
\frac{\max_{i}\alpha_{i}}{\min_{i}\alpha_{i}}\leq 1+\delta
, for
\delta\in(0,1]
. Then there exist POVMs
\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)}
with outcome space
[n]\cup\{\emptyset\}
and a probability distribution
\{p_{\beta}\}
such that

(i)

The convex combination
\mathbf{L}=\sum_{\beta}p_{\beta}\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)}
simulates
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}
with postselection

\sum_{\beta}p_{\beta}\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)}=\left(q\mathbf{M}^{\prime},(1-q)\mathbb{I}_{d}\right)\ ,

(20)

for
q\geq\frac{0.068}{1+\delta}
.

(ii)

Each POVM
\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)}
is nearly projective in the sense that

N^{(\beta)}_{i}=\begin{cases}A_{i}\left|\psi_{i}\right>\left<\psi_{i}\right|&\text{if}\ i\in S_{\beta}\\
\mathbb{I}-\sum_{j\in S_{\beta}}A_{j}\left|\psi_{j}\right>\left<\psi_{j}\right|&\text{if}\ i=\emptyset\\
0&\text{otherwise}\\
\end{cases},

(21)

where
A_{i}\geq\frac{0.47}{1+\delta}
and
S_{\beta}\subset[n]
satisfies
|S_{\beta}|\leq d/2
.

The condition
|S_{\beta}|\leq d/2
is necessary so that later on we will be able to employ Lemma
3
, stating that noisy versions of measurements
\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)}
appearing in Lemma
2
are simulable by projective measurements. The proof of Lemma
2
is given in Section
V
and follows from Theorem
4
given therein.

Theorem
4
is our central result and utilizes the solution to the Kadison-Singer problem from
[
38
]
, which guarantees that the probabilistic POVM protocol developed in
[
28
]
is capable (for a suitable choice of the partition
\mathcal{S}
of the set
[n]
of outcomes of a POVM) of simulating measurements with nearly flat effects with a convex combination of measurements with number of outcomes
|S_{\beta}|+1=\Theta(d)
, while maintaining success probability
q=\Theta(1)
. Crucially, the measurements appearing in the simulation protocol have mostly effects of rank one, which allows them to be realized by a single auxiliary qubit and projective measurements. This directly underpins Result
1
, whose extended version is given and proven in Theorem
5
in Section
V
.

Step 3 – PM-simulability of noisy nearly projective measurements.

It turns out that noisy versions of measurements
\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)}
that appeared in
Step 2
are quite easy to simulate by convex combinations of projective measurements.

Lemma 3

(Noisy nearly projective measurements are easy to simulate by projective measurements)
.

Let
l\leq d/2
and let
\mathbf{N}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{l+1})\in\mathrm{POVM}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
be a POVM of the form

N_{i}=\begin{cases}A_{i}\left|\psi_{i}\right>\left<\psi_{i}\right|&\text{if}\ i\in[l]\\
\mathbb{I}_{d}-\sum_{j=1}^{l}A_{j}\left|\psi_{j}\right>\left<\psi_{j}\right|&\text{if}\ i=l+1\\
\end{cases}\ .

(22)

Then
\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}(\mathbf{N})\in\mathrm{S}\mathbb{P}(d)
for

t=\min_{i\in[l]}\frac{|W^{\perp}|A_{i}}{|W|(1-A_{i})+|W^{\perp}|}\ ,

(23)

where
W\coloneqq\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\left|\psi_{i}\right>\ |\ i\in[l]\}
and
W^{\perp}
is its orthogonal complement.

This result relies on dimension-deficient Naimark dilation theorem (Theorem
6
in Section
VI
) and simple but tedious algebraic manipulations. For this reason the complete proof is given in Appendix
A.4
. By applying the result to POVMs
\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)}
from Lemma
2
we obtain that their noisy versions are projectively simulable for
t_{NP}\geq\frac{0.3}{1+\delta}
.

Step 4 – Incorporate post-processing to show that noisy versions of
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{M}
are PM-simulable.

Let
\mathbf{L}
be a POVM appearing in the formulation of Lemma
2
. By applying
\mathrm{\Phi}_{t_{NP}}
to both sides of Eq.(
20
) and utilizing Lemma
3
we get

\mathrm{\Phi}_{t_{NP}}(\mathbf{L})=\sum_{\beta}p_{\beta}\mathrm{\Phi}_{t_{NP}}(\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)})\in\mathrm{S}\mathbb{P}(d)\ .

(24)

It is now straightforward to note that for all
t\in[0,1]

\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}(\mathbf{L})
simulates
\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}(\mathbf{M}^{\prime})
with success probability
q
i.e.
\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}(\mathbf{L})=(q\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}(\mathbf{M}^{\prime}),(1-q)\mathbb{I}_{d})
. Therefore, by applying to
\mathrm{\Phi}_{t_{NP}}(\mathbf{L})
the post processing
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}=\{q^{\prime}_{i|j}\}
such that
q^{\prime}_{i|j}=\delta_{ij}
for
i,j\in[n]
and
q^{\prime}_{i|j}=\tr(M^{\prime}_{i})/d
, for
j=\emptyset
and
i\in[n]
, we get that

\mathrm{\Phi}_{q}\circ\mathrm{\Phi}_{t_{NP}}(\mathbf{M}^{\prime})=\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(\mathrm{\Phi}_{t_{NP}}(\mathbf{L}))=\sum_{\beta}p_{\beta}\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\left(\mathrm{\Phi}_{t_{NP}}\left(\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)}\right)\right)\ ,

(25)

from which it follows that for
c=q\cdot t_{NP}
the measurement
\mathrm{\Phi}_{c}(\mathbf{M}^{\prime})
is projectively simulable, since
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\left(\mathrm{\Phi}_{t_{NP}}\left(\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)}\right)\right)\in\mathrm{S}\mathbb{P}(d)
. Finally, we note that for every stochastic map
\mathcal{Q}
, any
t\in[0,1]
and any POVM
\mathbf{M}
we have
\mathcal{Q}(\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}(\mathbf{M}))=\mathrm{\Phi}_{t}\left(\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{M}))\right)
. Consequently, using
\mathbf{M}=\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{M}^{\prime})
(where
\mathcal{Q}
is the stochastic map appearing in Lemma
1
of
Step 1
), we get

\mathrm{\Phi}_{c}(\mathbf{M})=\mathcal{Q}\left(\mathrm{\Phi}_{c}(\mathbf{M})\right)=\sum_{\beta}p_{\beta}\mathcal{Q}\circ\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\left(\mathrm{\Phi}_{t_{NP}}\left(\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)}\right)\right),

(26)

which concludes the proof of Result
2
for
c=q\cdot t_{NP}\geq\frac{0.0204}{(1+\delta)^{2}}
, since POVMs
\mathcal{Q}\circ\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\left(\mathrm{\Phi}_{t_{NP}}\left(\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)}\right)\right)
are projectively simulable and
\delta
can be chosen small enough to ensure
c\geq 0.02
.

Remark 2
.

In the above reasoning there is room for flexibility regarding tradeoffs between different parameters. Specifically, one can decide to simulate the POVM
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}
via POVMs with fewer outcomes. This generally results in smaller success probability of simulation
q
and smaller lower bound on
A_{i}
. At the same time smaller group size
|S_{\beta}|
implies smaller dimension
|W|
for which Lemma
3
has to be applied, which can increase
t_{NP}
. In fact, the specific choice of the group size
|S_{\beta}|
(or more precisely, the implicit parameter
C
which controls it, cf. proof of Lemma
2
) was made so as to maximize the product
c=q\cdot t_{NP}
.

V
Probabilistic simulation by measurements requiring ancillas with limited dimension

In what follows we give the proof of Lemma
2
, which states that rank-one POVMs with nearly flat effects can be simulated by
nearly projective
measurements with constant (dimension independent) success probability. Note that nearly projective measurements can be realized with only a single auxiliary qubit. We also provide generalizations of this result for higher dimensions of the ancilla. We will make use of the POVM simulation technique introduced in
[
28
]
, which gives a recipe to probabilistically simulate any POVM with measurements having smaller number of outcomes.

Theorem 2

(Simulation protocol from
[
28
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}=(M_{1},\ldots,M_{n})
be an
n
-outcome POVM on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Let
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{\beta}\}_{\beta=1}^{r}
be a partition of
[n]
into disjoint subsets. For a fixed
\beta
we set
\lambda_{\beta}=\|\sum_{i\in{S_{\beta}}}M_{i}\|
and define a POVM
\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)}
by

N^{(\beta)}_{i}=\begin{cases}\lambda_{\beta}^{-1}M_{i}&\text{if}\ i\in S_{\beta}\\
\mathbb{I}-\lambda_{\beta}^{-1}\sum_{i\in S_{\beta}}M_{i}&\text{if}\ i=\emptyset\\
0&\text{otherwise}\\
\end{cases}\

(27)

Set
p_{\beta}=\lambda_{\beta}/(\sum_{\beta=1}^{r}\lambda_{\beta})
. Then the POVM
\mathbf{L}=\sum_{\beta=1}^{r}p_{\beta}\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)}
simulates the POVM
\mathbf{M}
with success probability

q(\mathbf{M},\mathcal{S})=\left(\sum_{\beta=1}^{r}\left\|\sum_{i\in{S_{\beta}}}M_{i}\right\|\right)^{-1}\ ,

(28)

i.e.
\mathbf{L}=(q(\mathbf{M},\mathcal{S})\mathbf{M},(1-q(\mathbf{M},\mathcal{S}))\mathbb{I}_{d})
.

The potential advantage of the above protocol lies in the fact that for rank-one POVMs
\mathbf{M}
the dimension of the Hilbert space needed to implement the Naimark dilation of each of the measurements
\mathbf{N}^{(\beta)}
is upper bounded by
|S_{\beta}|+d-1
. However, it is a priori difficult to guarantee large success probability
q(\mathbf{M},\mathcal{S})
while maintaining small sizes of subsets
S_{\beta}
. Furthermore, optimizing over partitions
\mathcal{S}
with bounded subset size
|S_{\beta}|
is a hard computational problem. The following Theorem
4
nevertheless ensures that for nearly flat rank-one POVMs there always exists a “good partition”.

The key result on which we build is the solution to the Kadison-Singer problem. Translated into the language of POVMs it reads as follows.

Theorem 3

(
[
38
, Corollary 1.5]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}=(M_{1},\ldots,M_{n})
be an
n
-outcome POVM on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, with each
M_{i}
having rank-one effects and satisfying
\|M_{i}\|\leq\epsilon
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. Then for any
r\geq 1
there exists a partition
\{S_{\beta}\}_{\beta=1}^{r}
of
[n]
into disjoint subsets such that for each
\beta=1,\ldots,r
we have

\left\|\sum_{i\in{S_{\beta}}}M_{i}\right\|\leq\frac{1}{r}\left(1+\sqrt{r\epsilon}\right)^{2}\ .

(29)

Let us remark that in general the
O(\sqrt{\epsilon})
dependence on
\epsilon
in the upper bound cannot be improved (see
[
89
, Example 7]
).

By employing the above we obtain the following result on simulation of nearly flat POVMs with rank-one effects.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathbf{M}=(M_{1},\ldots,M_{n})
be an
n
-outcome POVM on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, with
M_{i}=\alpha_{i}\left|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}\right|
,
\tilde{\epsilon}\leq\alpha_{i}\leq\epsilon
. Fix
r\geq 1
and let
C=r\epsilon
. Then there exists a partition
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{\beta}\}_{\beta=1}^{r}
of
[n]
into disjoint subsets such that

q(\mathbf{M},\mathcal{S})\geq\frac{1}{(1+\sqrt{C})^{2}}\ ,

(30)

where
q(\mathbf{M},\mathcal{S})
is given in Eq. (
28
) and describes success probability of the simulation protocol from Theorem
2
. Furthermore, for all
\beta=1,\ldots,r

|S_{\beta}|\leq d\frac{\epsilon}{\tilde{\epsilon}}(1+1/\sqrt{C})^{2}\ .

(31)

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{M}
is a POVM with rank-one effects and
\|M_{i}\|\leq\epsilon
, we can apply Theorem
3
to obtain for every
r\geq 1
that there exists a partition
\{S_{\beta}\}_{\beta=1}^{r}
of
[n]
into disjoint subsets such that for all
\beta=1,\ldots,r
we have

\left\|\sum_{i\in{S_{\beta}}}M_{i}\right\|\leq\frac{1}{r}\left(1+\sqrt{r\epsilon}\right)^{2},

(32)

from which the Eq. (
30
) follows after summing over
\beta
and utilizing (
28
).

In order to prove (
31
) we observe that (
32
) is equivalent to

\sum\limits_{i\in S_{\beta}}M_{i}\leq\frac{1}{r}\left(1+\sqrt{r\epsilon}\right)^{2}\mathbb{I}_{d}.

(33)

Upon taking trace of both sides we obtain

\sum\limits_{i\in S_{\beta}}\alpha_{i}\leq\epsilon\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{C}}\right)^{2}d\ .

(34)

Using
\alpha_{i}\geq\tilde{\epsilon}
, we finally obtain

\tilde{\epsilon}|S_{\beta}|\leq\epsilon\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{C}}\right)^{2}d\ ,

(35)

which concludes the proof.
∎

We remark that Theorem
4
does not provide an effective method for finding a partition
\mathcal{S}
for which inequality (

) holds. This is due to the nonconstructive nature of the result of Marcus, Spielman and Srivastava. We leave open the problem of gauging the complexity of finding a good partitions
\mathcal{S}
(i.e. partitions for which

is large while maintaining
|S_{\beta}|=\Theta(d)
). In Appendix

we show (see Theorem

) that random (and thus efficient to find) partitions

yield success probability that decays like

while maintaining
|S_{\beta}|=\Theta(d)
.

VI
Dimension-deficient Naimark theorem

In this section we formulate and prove dimension-deficient Naimark extension theorem. This result states that nearly projective measurements on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with small number of outcomes can be approximated (in a suitable sense) by projecively simulable measurements on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. In what follows for a subspace
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
we denote by
|X|
its dimension, by
\mathbb{P}_{X}
the orthogonal projector onto it, and by
X^{\perp}
its orthogonal complement in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

Theorem 6

(Dimension-deficient Naimark dilation)
.

Let
l\leq d/2
and let
\mathbf{N}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{l+1})
be a POVM on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with effects satisfying
N_{i}=A_{i}\left|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}\right|
, for
i\in[l]
. Let
W=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\{\left|\psi_{i}\right>\}\ ,\ i\in[l]\}
. Let
\mathbf{F}=(F_{1},\ldots,F_{l+1})\in\mathrm{POVM}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
be defined by

F_{i}=A_{i}\left|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}\right|+\frac{1-A_{i}}{|W^{\perp}|}\mathbb{P}_{W^{\perp}}\ ,

(39)

for
i\in[l]
. Then the POVM
\mathbf{F}
is projectively simulable, i.e.
\mathbf{F}\in\mathrm{S}\mathbb{P}(d)
.

Proof.

We first note that Eq. (
39
) uniquely defines a POVM
\mathbf{F}^{\mathbf{N}}
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
because, due to normalization of POVMs, we have
F_{l+1}=\mathbb{I}_{d}-\sum_{i=1}^{l}F_{i}
and consequently

F_{l+1}=\mathbb{P}_{W}-\sum_{i=1}^{l}A_{i}\left|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}\right|+\left(1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{l}(1-A_{i})}{|W^{\perp}|}\right)\mathbb{P}_{W^{\perp}}\ .

(40)

It is easy to see that
F_{l+1}\geq 0
. This is because (i)
\mathbb{P}_{W}-\sum_{i=1}^{l}A_{i}\left|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}\right|\geq 0
(since
\left|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}\right|
have their support on the subspace
W\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
) and (ii) due to the inequality
\sum_{i=1}^{l}(1-A_{i})\leq d/2\leq|W^{\perp}|
. We now proceed to show that
\mathbf{F}
is projectively simulable.

Consider
\mathbf{N}^{W}\in\mathrm{POVM}(W)
that is defined via
N^{W}_{i}=\mathbb{P}_{W}N_{i}\mathbb{P}_{W}
, for
i\in[l+1]
. We have
\sum_{i=1}^{l+1}\mathrm{rank}(N^{W}_{i})\leq l+|W|\leq d
. It is therefore possible to realize the Naimark extension of
\mathbf{N}^{W}
(cf. Theorem
1
) using the space
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Let
\mathbf{P}^{W}=(P^{W}_{1},\ldots,P^{W}_{l+1})
be a projective measurement realizing the Naimark extension of
\mathbf{N}^{W}
. Because
\mathrm{rank}(N^{W}_{i})=1
for
i\in[l]
we have
P^{W}_{i}=\left|\phi_{i}\rangle\langle\phi_{i}\right|
, where
\{\left|\phi_{i}\right>\}_{i=1}^{l}
is a collection of orthogonal vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Because
\mathbf{P}^{W}
is a Naimark extension of
\mathbf{N}^{W}
we have for
i\in[l]

\mathbb{P}_{W}\left|\phi_{i}\rangle\langle\phi_{i}\right|\mathbb{P}_{W}=A_{i}\left|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}\right|\ .

(41)

Furthermore, by using
\mathbb{P}_{W}+\mathbb{P}_{W^{\perp}}=\mathbb{I}_{d}
,
\tr(\left|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}\right|)=1
and Eq. (
41
) we get that for
i\in[l]

\tr(\left|\phi_{i}\rangle\langle\phi_{i}\right|\mathbb{P}_{W})=A_{i}\ ,\ \tr(\left|\phi_{i}\rangle\langle\phi_{i}\right|\mathbb{P}_{W^{\perp}})=1-A_{i}\ .

(42)

In order to get from the projective measurement
\mathbf{P}^{W}
to
\mathbf{F}
we show these POVMs can be connected by a suitably crafted unitary twirling

\mathbf{F}=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}U\mathbf{P}^{W}U^{\dagger}\ ,

(43)

where
\mathcal{E}
is the uniform measure on unitaries of the form
U=\mathrm{exp}(i\varphi)\mathbb{P}_{W}\oplus V
, where
\varphi\in[0,2\pi]
is a phase and
V\in U(W^{\perp})
is a unitary on
W^{\perp}
. The twirling in (
43
) is defined by applying averaging separately on every component of a POVM i.e.

\left[\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}U\mathbf{P}^{W}U^{\dagger}\right]_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}UP^{W}_{i}U^{\dagger}\ .

(44)

Using basic tricks from Haar measure integration it is easy to see that for
i\in[l]

\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}UP^{W}_{i}U^{\dagger}=A_{i}\psi_{i}+\frac{1-A_{i}}{|W^{\perp}|}\mathbb{P}_{W^{\perp}}=F_{i}\ .

(45)

Indeed, for every operator
B
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
we have (cf. Lemma
6
in Appendix
A
for the proof)

\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}UBU^{\dagger}=\mathbb{P}_{W}B\mathbb{P}_{W}+\frac{\tr(B\mathbb{P}_{W^{\perp}})}{|W^{\perp}|}\mathbb{P}_{W^{\perp}}\ .

(46)

By inserting
B=P^{W}_{i}=\left|\phi_{i}\rangle\langle\phi_{i}\right|
and using equations (
41
) and (
42
) we get (
45
). Finally, from the POVM normalization condition (unitary twirling is a unitary channel and therefore
\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}U\mathbb{I}_{d}U^{\dagger}=\mathbb{I}_{d}
) we get
\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}UP^{W}_{l+1}U^{\dagger}=\mathbb{I}_{d}-\sum_{i=1}^{l}F_{i}
.

After establishing Eq. (
43
) we note that this identity proves
\mathbf{F}\in\mathrm{S}\mathbb{P}(d)
. This is because unitary twirling can be interpreted as randomization (convex combination) over projective measurements
U\mathbf{P}^{W}U^{\dagger}
.

∎
