A network analysis of short video addiction and inattention symptoms among undergraduate nursing students
Authors: Yuxuan Zhang, Wei Zhao, Shanshan Ma, Yu Sheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundShort video addiction is prevalent among nursing students and frequently coexists with inattention, and their comorbidity may exert substantial impacts on academic performance and mental health.AimThis study aimed to (1) identify central symptoms within the short video addiction and inattention symptom network, (2) detect bridge symptoms connecting distinct symptom clusters, and (3) simulate interventions to explore potential intervention and prevention targets.MethodsA multi-center cross-sectional survey was conducted between August and December 2025, involving 456 undergraduate nursing students from four nursing colleges in China. The research employed several tools, including a general information questionnaire, the revised Chinese Internet Addiction Scale, and the Inattention subscale of the Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale. Network analysis was used to construct symptom networks and identify central and bridge symptoms, while the NodeIdentifyR algorithm was applied to simulate alleviating and aggravating interventions on each node to examine projected effects on overall network symptom activation.ResultsNetwork analysis revealed that “Compulsive Use and Withdrawal” emerged as the most central symptom in the network, while “Interpersonal and Health Problems” served as the primary bridge symptom. Alleviating interventions suggested that decreasing the symptom value of “Compulsive Use and Withdrawal” resulted in the greatest projected reduction in network symptom activation. Aggravating interventions indicated that elevating the symptom value of “Does not listen” had the most projected increase in network activation.ConclusionsThis study highlights the importance of attending to “Compulsive Use and Withdrawal” and “Interpersonal and Health Problems” in the comorbidity of short video addiction and inattention among nursing students, and reveals that “Does not listen” may be a prevention target based on simulation results. These findings deepen our understanding of the network of short video addiction and inattention symptoms among undergraduate nursing students. These findings may offer useful insights for nursing educators and researchers to further explore this comorbidity and its implications for students’ well-being and academic performance.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04731-8.

Introduction

The pervasive integration of digital technologies into daily life has fundamentally reshaped behavioral patterns and social interactions in contemporary society [1]. The accelerated proliferation of short video content as a nascent medium of social interaction is indicative of this transition. Short videos are online videos ranging in length from a few seconds to a few minutes [2]. According to the 56th Statistical Report on Internet Development in China, as of June 2025, the number of short video users in China reached 1.068 billion, accounting for 95.1% of the total internet users [3]. Given this widespread popularity, concerns regarding its excessive use and the potential for addiction have emerged among the general public. Short video addiction (SVA) refers to a chronic or cyclical compulsive state resulting from the repeated use of short video applications [4], and is regarded as a manifestation of internet addiction behavior on a specific platform [5]. College students constitute the primary demographic of short video consumers [6]. The prevalence of SVA among college students ranges from 21.63% to 31.99% [7]. Short video platforms’ personalized recommendation algorithms and instant feedback mechanisms make users prone to developing compulsive urges and impaired self-control [8]. These manifestations share characteristics with other behavioral addictions. As usage behavior is repeatedly reinforced, tolerance increases, and negative emotional experiences, such as anxiety and irritability, emerge when use is discontinued [9]. Long-term excessive use may impair real-life functioning, including reduced interpersonal interaction, declining academic performance, and disrupted sleep [9]. This behavioral addiction demonstrates strong comorbidity with inattention symptoms [10, 11]. Inattention (IA) is a common issue in daily life, study, and work, and it significantly impacts people’s efficiency and performance. Symptoms associated with IA include diminished productivity in academic and professional contexts, impaired memory, and emotional volatility [12]. The relationship between SVA and IA can be interpreted within the I-PACE model (Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution) [13]. The model posits that addictive behaviors develop through an interaction between individual susceptibility, emotional and cognitive responses, and executive control processes. Within this framework, IA can be viewed as a susceptibility factor associated with executive function deficits. Short video platforms continuously increase cognitive load through high-frequency, algorithm-driven stimuli and rapid content switching, thereby promoting externally driven attentional capture [14]. Prolonged exposure to such environments with instant feedback may further impair inhibitory control and sustained attention, increasing dependence on and compulsive use of short video platforms [15]. Also, both conditions have been linked to the onset of sleep disorders, anxiety, and depressive symptoms [16, 17]. The co-occurrence of SVA and IA frequently intensifies adverse effects, endangering students’ mental well-being and academic success.

As the future backbone of the healthcare workforce, undergraduate nursing students must simultaneously master extensive medical knowledge and meet clinical internship demands, making sustained attention essential not only for academic and professional competency development, but also for ensuring care quality and patient safety in future practice [18, 19]. Given the substantial academic pressure faced by nursing students, they often demonstrate an increased need for effective emotion regulation [20]. The advent of novel social media platforms, such as short videos, has led to a notable increase in students’ reliance on short videos for emotional support and stress relief. However, the compensatory internet use model suggests that although such online behaviors may provide temporary relief, they can easily lead to excessive use and, in some cases, addiction [21]. The detection rate among nursing students has been reported to reach 31.30% [22]. A growing body of research suggests a link between SVA and IA with impaired cognitive function, reduced student engagement in challenging courses, and diminished academic performance [23, 24]. In the context of clinical practice, this co-occurring condition can undermine the clinical reasoning and decision-making abilities required for nursing care. It may also hinder the development of professional identity and increase potential risks to patient safety [25]. However, conventional research has employed only correlation analyses or total score comparisons, treating SVA and IA symptoms as unitary constructs, thereby failing to capture the complex symptom-level interactions that sustain psychopathology [26, 27]. A clearer understanding of the symptom structure of SVA and IA is essential to identify key symptoms among nursing students and to clarify how these symptoms interact.

Network analysis provides an innovative approach to understanding the structural relationships among symptoms [28], conceptualizing behavioral and cognitive symptoms as an interconnected system, where symptoms are conditionally associated with one another, rather than treating them as isolated constructs. This method identifies central symptoms exerting significant influence across the entire network and reveals bridge symptoms that connect different symptom communities, revealing key patterns of symptom interconnection underlying symptom co-occurrence [29]. Network analysis has been employed to examine the interactions among various psychological factors, including self-efficacy, anxiety, and depression, among students [30, 31], helping to identify potential targets for intervention. Given the unique academic and clinical practice requirements of undergraduate nursing students, which distinguish them from the general undergraduate population, it is worthwhile to conduct a dedicated study applying this network analysis to explore the interaction between SVA and IA at the symptom level within this group. Nevertheless, previous network analyses have identified potential intervention targets primarily through centrality metrics, which are based on the presence and strength of edges. However, scholars have raised concerns about its reliability and validity [29]. Firstly, centrality indices may be biased when the network contains a latent structure [32]. Secondly, computation of centrality is based on homeostasis, rather than a dynamic process [33]. We are uncertain how the network as a whole will be affected if one single node is altered. Computer-simulated intervention, which employs computational methods to simulate theoretical perturbations in network systems, has emerged as a promising innovation to address the limitations above. Lunansky et al. [34] developed the NodeIdentifyR (NIRA) algorithm within this framework. NIRA simulates interventions by reducing or increasing the occurrence probability of specific nodes, thereby triggering a series of reactions within the network and generating a simulated network. Once the simulation is complete, potential prevention and intervention targets can be estimated based on the magnitude of network changes. The NIRA model has been applied in numerous empirical mental health studies [35, 36], supporting its applicability in the present context. These insights may provide preliminary hypotheses for future intervention research to support the physical and mental well-being and attention of undergraduate nursing students. Therefore, this study uses network analysis and computer-simulated interventions to construct a symptom network of SVA and IA among undergraduate nursing students. Methods

Participants

This study conducted a questionnaire survey at four universities offering nursing programs between August and December 2025, recruiting participants through convenience sampling. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) full-time undergraduate nursing students; (2) aged between 18 and 23; (3) proficiency in operating a smartphone. Exclusion criteria included: (1) self-reported history of severe mental illness or recent major life stressors; (2) completion time under 240 s; (3) discernible patterned responses, such as selecting the same option for all questions.

Procedure

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of the authors’ university (Ethics Approval Number: 2022 − 1529). The research was conducted using a questionnaire survey method. During the questionnaire development phase, all measurement tools employed in the study were integrated into the “Wenjuanxing” online survey platform.

To facilitate participant recruitment, researchers contacted faculty members or class representatives at target universities and disseminated recruitment information through WeChat class groups. Following the dissemination of the recruitment notice, eligible students had the option of voluntarily participating in the study. No random selection or targeted screening was conducted. The recruitment announcement explicitly provided the link to the questionnaire and the QR code. Upon scanning the QR code or clicking the link to access the questionnaire homepage, participants were first presented with an informed consent form. This document meticulously delineates the research objectives, the voluntary nature of participation, the principle of anonymity, and the commitment that research data will be used solely for scientific purposes. Participants will only be permitted to proceed to the formal questionnaire response stage once they have checked the box indicating their consent.

The participants completed the questionnaire independently. To ensure the integrity of the system, a protocol was implemented that restricted each device’s IP address to a single submission. This mechanism was designed to prevent the occurrence of duplicate responses, thus maintaining the accuracy and authenticity of the data collected. Furthermore, the research team meticulously excluded invalid samples with excessively brief completion times in subsequent stages to ensure the reliability of the research sample.

Measurements

General information questionnaire

General information was collected from participants, including gender, grade level and residence, as well as frequently used short video apps.

Short video addiction

SVA was assessed using the revised Chinese Internet Addiction Scale. The scale was developed by Yu Bai et al. [37]. The scale encompasses four dimensions: Compulsive Internet Access and Internet Addiction Withdrawal Reactions, as well as Tolerance Symptoms of Internet Addiction, Interpersonal and Health-Related Problems of Internet Addiction, and Time Management Problems. The scale comprises a total of 19 items. The scale employed in this study ranged from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 4 (“strongly agree”). A total score of ≥ 53 indicates addiction. As a subtype of internet addiction, SVA exhibits a high degree of similarity in its core addictive mechanisms with internet addiction, including compulsive usage tendencies and withdrawal symptoms [38]. Concurrently, prior studies have employed general internet addiction measures to assess specific online behaviors such as SVA and social media use, thereby substantiating their cross-context applicability [39, 40]. Furthermore, this study explicitly required participants to base their responses on their personal short video usage experiences during administration, thereby further ensuring the alignment between the scale’s content and its measurement objectives. Previous studies have shown good internal consistency of the CIAS in college student populations (Cronbach’s α = 0.970) [41]. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale was 0.934 in this study.

Given the adaptation of the instructions, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was performed to ensure the scale’s structural validity in the context of SVA. The model exhibited an acceptable fit to the data: The four-factor model exhibited an acceptable fit: χ2(146) = 336.8, P < 0.001, χ²/df = 2.307. The Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) were 0.920 and 0.900, respectively. The Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) was 0.060 (90% CI [0.053, 0.070]), and the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) was 0.050. Furthermore, the measurement model demonstrated strong convergent validity, with all standardized factor loadings > 0.50, Composite Reliability (CR) > 0.70, and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) > 0.50. These results support the structural applicability of the CIAS for assessing SVA in the present sample.

Inattention

The present study used the Inattention subscale of the Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS) to assess inattention levels in a sample of nursing students. The scale was developed by Kessler et al. [42]. It consists of two subscales, Inattention and Hyperactivity and Impulsivity, with each subscale comprising nine entries. All entries are evaluated on a 5-point scale from 0 to 4, with higher scores denoting more severe inattention. The ASRS Inattention subscale, which is widely utilized for dimensional assessment of attention problems in non-clinical college student populations, has demonstrated good internal consistency [43, 44]. This subscale encompasses the core manifestations of sustained attention difficulties in daily academic and social contexts. Moreover, the present study concentrates on sustained attention difficulties in nursing undergraduates in everyday contexts as opposed to the clinical diagnosis of ADHD. The Hyperactivity/Impulsivity subscale reflects a distinct behavioral control dimension, which is not central to the focus of the current study and was therefore not included. In this vein, the present study also selected only the Inattention subscale to measure the inattention level of nursing students. Cronbach’s α in the current study was 0.894.

Sample size

The sample size was determined based on established guidelines for network analysis. In order to achieve stable network estimates, it is recommended that each node include 10–20 participants [45]. Given that the network under study contains 13 nodes (4 SVA projects and 9 IA projects), a minimum of 130–260 participants must be recruited. Moreover, increasing the sample size has been shown to enhance network stability and parameter precision [45]. The final effective sample size reached 456, which exceeded the aforementioned requirement.

Data analysis

A comprehensive set of descriptive statistics was conducted to comprehensively characterize the sample. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables are reported as frequency and percentage. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the bivariate relationship between SVA and IA, thereby establishing a baseline association pattern prior to network analysis. All statistical operations were executed using R software version 4.5.1, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Network estimation

The present study adopted the methodology proposed by Epskamp et al. [46, 47], employing the “qgraph” package in R for network analysis. Each node in the network is associated with a particular symptom indicator. For detailed definitions and abbreviations of the nodes, refer to Additional file 1 Table S1. The representation of associations between symptoms is illustrated by edges in the graph. Given the heterogeneous measurement characteristics of the variables included in this study, a mixed correlation analysis strategy was employed, implemented using the “cor_auto” function from the “qgraph” package [46]. This function applies the predefined measurement-level rules, using polychoric correlations for ordinal variables and Pearson correlations for approximately continuous variables [48]. To assess symptom associations, researchers employed the Gaussian Graph Model (GGM) [49]. The extended Bayesian Information Criterion glasso (EBICglasso) algorithm was implemented with a tuning parameter set to 0.50. This tuning parameter aligns with the default values recommended by Epskamp et al. [45]. To verify the robustness of the results, a sensitivity analysis was conducted within the range of gamma = 0, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, and 1.0. Furthermore, the “goldbricker” function from the “networktools” package was used to assess potential redundancy among network nodes during this study. Network visualization was optimized using the “ggplot2” package [50].

Centrality and bridge centrality estimation

Expected Influence (EI) and Bridge Expected Influence (BEI) were calculated for each node using the R packages “qgraph” and “networktools” to identify central and bridge nodes [51, 52]. The EI metric is a measurement of the overall impact of each symptom within the network [53]. Higher EI values are indicative of stronger positive correlations between a node and others, exerting greater influence across the entire network [51]. Nodes with higher BEI values play a key role in linking distinct symptom clusters, potentially triggering or maintaining comorbid symptoms [51].

Network stability and accuracy

Network stability and accuracy were assessed using the “bootnet” package [46]. Non-parametric bootstrapping with 1,000 resamples was used to estimate 95% confidence intervals for edge weights and to test differences between nodes and edges. A narrower confidence interval is indicative of enhanced precision in network parameter estimation [54]. Centrality stability was evaluated via case-dropping bootstrap, with CS-coefficients > 0.25 indicating acceptable stability and > 0.5 indicating robust stability [46].

Computer-simulated intervention

To simulate interventions targeting specific symptoms, the raw scores were binarized (0 and ≥ 1) and fitted to an Ising model [34]. The parameters of the Ising model were estimated using a logistic regression model. The intercept term in the logistic regression is equivalent to the raw threshold parameter for each item. The threshold parameter is indicative of the probability of symptom activation within the network. The NIRA generated observations for 5,000 simulated subjects. By adjusting the activation probability of each node in the network under a given simulated perturbation, we observed the impact of changes in node activation probabilities on the total score of the entire network [34]. Two intervention scenarios were defined: alleviating intervention, where the threshold parameter of a given item was reduced by 2 standard deviations, and aggravating intervention, where it was increased by 2 standard deviations. Nodes yielding the greatest predicted decrease and increase in total score were identified as potential intervention and prevention targets, respectively [34]. It is imperative to acknowledge the underlying assumptions of this simulation. Specifically, it posits that the connectivity structure among symptoms remains constant when interventions are applied at a particular node. Furthermore, all predictions are derived from cross-sectional data. Consequently, the identified targets are derived from theoretical deductions rather than empirically validated clinical outcomes.

Results

Study sample

A total of 480 valid responses were collected. Among these, 24 were excluded due to completion times under 240 s, resulting in 456 valid responses (valid response rate: 95.00%). The sample predominantly comprised third-year students (n = 156) and females (n = 354, 77.60%). Approximately half of the participants (n = 242, 53.07%) were from urban areas. Douyin has emerged as the preeminent short video application(61.0%) among nursing students (Table S2, Additional file 1). The screening results indicated that 148 participants (32.46%) reported SVA (CIAS total score ≥ 53) (Table S2). The mean scores for SVA and IA were 47.816 (SD = 10.931) and 17.496 (SD = 6.936), respectively (Table S2). Correlations between SVA and IA symptoms were generally positive (Fig. 1), suggesting that these symptom domains are interrelated and supporting the use of network analysis to further examine their conditional associations.

The results of the network analysis are presented in Fig. 2. The network under consideration comprises 13 nodes. In consideration of the 78 possible edges, 53 of these exhibit non-zero weights, thereby yielding a network density of 0.679 (53/78). Bootstrap difference tests for edge weights (Figure S1) indicate that stronger associations primarily occur within the same symptom domain. Network centrality and bridge centrality

“Compulsive Use and Withdrawal” (SVA.1) emerged as the most central node (EI = 1.048; Fig. 3A; Table S3), reflecting the strongest aggregate connections with other symptoms in the network. “Interpersonal and Health Problems” (SVA.3) demonstrated the highest Bridge Expected Influence (BEI = 0.376; Fig. 3B; Table S4), suggesting it serves as the primary cross-community connector between SVA and IA symptom clusters and may represent a structurally important node linking the two communities. Bootstrap difference tests confirmed that the EI and BEI values of were each statistically distinguishable from those of most other nodes (Figures S2 and S3), supporting the robustness of these rankings.

Network stability and accuracy

Bootstrap results indicated relatively narrow 95% confidence intervals for edge weights, suggesting acceptable network accuracy (Figure S4). The CS coefficients for EI and BEI both exceeded 0.5 (Fig. 4), indicating acceptable stability of centrality estimates. Bootstrap difference tests revealed statistically significant differences among most edge weights and centrality indices (Fig. S1–S3), providing some support for the reliability of node rankings. Sensitivity analyses indicated that EI and BEI values remained largely consistent across varying parameter settings (γ = 0, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.0) (Figures S5–S6). Redundancy analysis identified a single redundant node pair, SVA.1 and SVA.2, which showed high similarity in their correlation patterns.

The results of the computer simulation of the intervention are displayed in Fig. 5. Regarding the aggravating intervention (Fig. 5A), simulated intensification of “Does not listen” (IA.3) resulted in an increase in the predicted network symptom activation sum score from 3.528 to 5.809, suggesting that IA.3 may be a potential risk node with the greatest projected impact on the deterioration of overall network activation. Discussion

This study provides the first network analysis investigating symptom level interactions between SVA and IA among undergraduate nursing students. Within the network of SVA and IA exhibited by undergraduate nursing students, “Compulsive Use and Withdrawal” (SVA.1) demonstrated the highest EI, suggesting that this symptom may occupy a relatively central position within the overall network structure and may be more closely associated with other symptoms in the network. Bootstrap difference tests further indicated that the EI of this node was significantly higher than that of most other nodes, supporting the robustness of its central role. This finding is consistent with previous studies [55]. For undergraduate nursing students, who often face intensive academic workloads, frequent examinations, and emotionally demanding clinical training, short video use may serve as a convenient strategy for temporary stress relief and emotional escape [56]. This pattern is consistent with cognitive-behavioral models of behavioral addiction, which suggest that repeated reliance on specific behaviors for mood regulation may gradually strengthen compulsive tendencies and withdrawal experiences [57]. At the same time, the high EI value for “Compulsive Use and Withdrawal” suggests that this symptom is associated with other SVA and IA-related symptoms. This may indicate that, among some nursing students, compulsive short video use and withdrawal experiences may be linked to various behavioral and cognitive manifestations, such as difficulty sustaining attention during study and clinical practice, reduced task completion efficiency, and distractibility in classroom and skills training settings [25]. The implementation of training programs aimed at strengthening self-control among nursing students may be helpful in addressing compulsive tendencies toward short video use [58]. In addition, future support programs may also consider including emotional regulation and stress management courses, which could encourage nursing students to adopt more adaptive coping strategies and potentially reduce withdrawal-related experiences associated with short video use [59, 60]. However, whether these interventions can influence the overall network structure underlying SVA and IA symptoms remains to be verified by future research.

The results of this study showed that “Interpersonal and Health Problems” (SVA.3) exhibited the highest BEI in the symptom network of SVA and IA, suggesting that this symptom may play a relatively important linking role between the SVA and IA symptom clusters. Bootstrap tests further confirmed the bridging role of this node, with results showing that its BEI value was significantly higher than that of the other nodes. This finding suggests that short video use may be associated with interpersonal functioning, sleep status, and physical health. From the perspective of Compensatory Internet Use theory, individuals experiencing reduced interpersonal support or increased social stress may be more likely to engage in short video use as a compensatory strategy for unmet emotional and social needs [21]. Previous studies have shown that interpersonal relationship problems are often observed alongside cognitive decline and reduced sustained attention [61]. In addition, the continuous content updates and instant feedback features of short video platforms may be associated with prolonged usage time, which is often accompanied by delayed sleep onset, reduced sleep duration, and poorer sleep quality [62]. Previous research has found a close association between sleep problems and difficulties in sustaining attention [63]. Given the unique nature of their future profession, nursing students are required to maintain adequate attentional resources in both theoretical and clinical learning contexts. Difficulties in sustaining attention may be related to poorer academic performance among nursing students and may increase the risk of inattentiveness during clinical practice, potentially posing challenges to nursing quality and patient safety [64]. Future research could further explore whether strengthening opportunities for nursing students to develop stable in-person peer support networks, promoting appropriate physical activity, and supporting healthy rest and sleep patterns are associated with reduced interpersonal challenges and health problems related to SVA [65]. These strategies may represent potentially relevant directions for supportive interventions, but their effectiveness requires further validation in longitudinal and intervention studies.

Based on the NIRA model, this study identified potential target symptoms within the SVA and IA symptom networks by examining how simulated alleviation or aggravation of individual symptoms influenced overall network activation. “Compulsive Use and Withdrawal” not only emerged as a central symptom but also demonstrated the largest simulated influence in alleviating interventions, resulting in the highest projected decrease in network activation. Previous studies have indicated that compulsive use or craving behaviors may play a significant role in the development and maintenance of addiction [66]. In the network structure of this study, “Compulsive Use and Withdrawal” also demonstrated a close association with IA symptoms. Such highly interconnected symptoms may influence other symptoms through network structures and play a significant role in the overall activation of symptoms. “Does not listen” (IA.3) demonstrated the largest simulated influence in aggravating interventions, resulting in the highest projected increase in network activation. The term “Does not listen” (IA.3) refers to difficulties maintaining attention during interpersonal interactions and effective processing of external information. This finding is of considerable significance for nursing students in both academic and clinical settings. For nursing students, attentive listening and comprehension constitute essential professional competencies across classroom lectures, procedural demonstrations, and clinical practice, particularly in relation to receiving instructions and communicating patient conditions [67]. In instances of comorbid SVA and IA, persistent “Does not listen” (IA.3) is associated with substandard academic and professional performance among nursing students [67], which may be associated with poorer educational performance and potential challenges to clinical safety. Nevertheless, the exacerbation of “Does not listen” (IA.3) may be difficult for others to detect; systematic screening tools may be needed to identify it early. Incorporating this symptom into the routine monitoring of undergraduate nursing students’ mental health warrants further investigation as a potential strategy to prevent negative outcomes. However, these findings are primarily based on statistical simulation analyses of network structures, so their validity and reliability require confirmation through subsequent longitudinal studies and intervention trials.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study warrant acknowledgment. First, the convenience sampling approach and the restriction of the sample to four Chinese nursing colleges may not be representative of the broader undergraduate nursing students. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationships between SVA and IA, as well as the dynamics underlying simulation intervention targets. Longitudinal and experimental designs are warranted to elucidate these associations. Third, inherent methodological constraints of network analysis merit consideration. The identified network structures and simulation-based intervention targets are derived from cross-sectional data and thus cannot capture temporal or within-person dynamics. The simulation-based intervention assumes network structure invariance during node-specific perturbations, and the link between threshold parameter manipulation and clinical techniques lacks empirical support. The identified intervention targets should therefore be treated as theoretical hypotheses pending validation through longitudinal studies and randomized controlled trials, rather than direct clinical recommendations. Fourth, reliance on self-reported data introduces potential recall and social desirability biases, emphasizing the necessity for future studies to employ multi-method assessments, incorporating clinical interviews and behavioral tracking. Fifth, the present study did not adjust for potential confounding variables, including sleep quality, depression, anxiety, and academic stress, which may influence both SVA and IA; future studies should incorporate these factors to enhance the robustness and interpretability of the network structure. Sixth, potential item redundancy within the CIAS could affect the stability and interpretability of node associations. Furthermore, employing a general rather than an SVA-specific measurement instrument may reduce measurement precision. The lack of formal measurement invariance testing across subgroups, combined with demographic imbalances in the sample, may also compromise the robustness of subgroup comparisons. Future research should utilize more refined measurement instruments, ensure balanced sampling, and further validate network stability across diverse populations. Additionally, the relatively high network density suggests that lower-weight edges should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

The present study found that “Compulsive Use and Withdrawal” is a central symptom in the SVA and IA symptom networks among undergraduate nursing students, with “Interpersonal and Health Problems” functioning as the primary bridging symptom. Computer-simulated interventions suggest that “Does not listen” may increase overall network activation, indicating it may be a potential target for prevention. These findings provide a preliminary, hypothesis-generating basis for nursing education researchers to further investigate symptom mechanisms underlying SVA and IA. However, the specific network structures identified in this study require further validation through longitudinal studies and experiments. In the context of nursing education, it is important to consider the co-occurrence of SVA and IA. This not only helps maintain students’ physical and mental well-being and academic performance but also contributes to cultivating higher-quality healthcare professionals who are capable of providing high-quality professional nursing care to patients.
