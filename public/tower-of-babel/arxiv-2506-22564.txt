Efficient Tensor Decomposition via Moment Matrix Extension
Bobby Shi, Julia Lindberg, Joe Kileel

Abstract
Motivated by a flurry of recent work on efficient tensor decomposition algorithms, we show that the celebrated moment matrix extension algorithm of Brachat, Comon, Mourrain, and Tsigaridas [ 11 ] for symmetric tensor canonical polyadic (CP) decomposition can be made efficient under the right conditions. We first show that the crucial property determining the complexity of the algorithm is the regularity of a target decomposition. This allows us to reduce the complexity of the vanilla algorithm, while also unifying results from previous works. We then show that for tensors in S d ​ ℂ n + 1 S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1} with d d even, low enough regularity can reduce finding a symmetric tensor decomposition to solving a system of linear equations. For order- 4 4 tensors we prove that generic tensors of rank up to r = 2 ​ n + 1 r=2n+1 can be decomposed efficiently via moment matrix extension, exceeding the rank threshold allowed by simultaneous diagonalization. We then formulate a conjecture that states for generic order- 4 4 tensors of rank r = O ⁡ ( n 2 ) r=O(n^{2}) the induced linear system is sufficient for efficient tensor decomposition, matching the asymptotics of existing algorithms and in fact improving the leading coefficient. Towards this conjecture we give computer assisted proofs that the statement holds for n = 2 , … , 17 n=2,\dots,17 . Next we demonstrate that classes of nonidentifiable tensors can be decomposed efficiently via the moment matrix extension algorithm, bypassing the usual need for uniqueness of decomposition. Of particular interest is the class of monomials, for which the extension algorithm is not only efficient but also improves on existing theory by explicitly parameterizing the space of decompositions. Code for implementations of the efficient algorithm for generic tensors and monomials are provided, along with several numerical examples.

Introduction

Tensors can be thought of as multidimensional arrays. Of particular interest in data science applications are symmetric tensors: tensors unchanged by permutation of indices
[

(1)

span the vector space of symmetric tensors, so such a decomposition always exists
[

]
. Such a decomposition is useful in various applications, including blind source separation
[

,
15
,
27
]
, independent component analysis
[
63
,
41
]
, psychometrics
[
18
]
, magnetic resonance imaging
[
7
]
, telecommunications
[
19
,
26
,
71
,
66
]
, signal processing
[
29
]
, latent variable estimation for Gaussian mixture models
[
34
,
62
,
40
]
, moment estimation
[
72
]
, and hidden Markov models
[
2
,
4
]
.

Though finding such a decomposition (with
r
minimal) is known to be NP-hard
[
37
]
, there has been a flurry of recent research that aims to develop
efficient
algorithms for specific classes of tensors
[
57
,
46
,
51
,
69
,
48
,
43
]
. In these contexts

n^{d}
.
These algorithms largely build on a simultaneous diagonalization procedure that realizes tensor components as common eigenvectors of some set of matrices.

1.1
Our contributions

In this paper we refine and improve the analysis of the algebraic algorithm of
[
11
]
.
At a high level, said algorithm reduces symmetric tensor decomposition over
\mathbb{C}
to a
moment matrix extension
problem. The extension procedure involves solving a system of polynomial equations in a set of
moment variables
. In Section
3
our first contribution is to show that the key measure of complexity in the extension algorithm is the
regularity

[
30
]
of a target decomposition. When the regularity of a target decomposition is sufficiently small relative to the order of the tensor, we demonstrate that the polynomial system can reduce to a linear system. Under additional conditions solving this linear system is sufficient and the task of symmetric tensor decomposition can be performed efficiently. Therefore, low regularity is the crucial property for our perspective on efficient tensor decomposition – more so than low rankness or uniqueness of the decomposition, in contrast to existing literature.

Low regularity of decompositions holds for almost all tensors (i.e., generically) of low enough rank.
On the other hand, showing that for such tensors the task of tensor decomposition reduces to solving a linear system of equations is more challenging.
In Section
4
we prove that order-
4
tensor decomposition generically reduces to solving a linear system for ranks up to
2n+1
, exceeding the ranks provably allowed by simultaneous diagonalization.
Further, we formulate a conjecture giving sufficient conditions under which symmetric tensor decomposition generically reduces to a linear system at higher ranks.
We provide evidence for the conjecture’s validity through a computer-assisted proof verifying its correctness up to
n=17
.
An affirmative answer to the conjecture would imply that the moment matrix extension method can decompose almost all order-
4
tensors of rank up to
r=O(n^{2})
efficiently. The precise leading coefficient in this bound, which we work out explicitly, improves existing work concerning different tensor decomposition algorithms. Moreover, the sufficiency of the linear system provides a new certificate for uniqueness of decomposition for specific tensors.

In addition, we show that classes of nonidentifiable tensors can also be decomposed efficiently by the extension algorithm. This is in contrast to previous work on efficient tensor decomposition, which requires that the underlying decomposition be unique.
In particular, we show that two classes of nonidentifiable tensors, tensors corresponding to three collinear points (Section
4.2.3
) and arbitrary monomials (Section
5
), can be decomposed efficiently.

We provide supplementary code with an implementation of the algorithm using only linear algebra (with no symbolic algebra packages needed) at:

https://github.com/rhshi/TensorDecomposition.jl

1.2
Related work

Symmetric tensor decomposition has been classically studied from the perspective of algebra and geometry as the problem of finding a Waring decomposition of a homogeneous polynomial
[
42
,
53
]
. Using tools from projective algebraic geometry, this approach has led to algebraic algorithms via apolarity theory by casting tensor decomposition as a root-finding problem
[
68
,
61
]
(e.g., Sylvester’s method, the catalecticant method, and more sophisticated eigenvector-based methods using Young flattenings
[
54
]
). Closely related to apolarity based methods are normal form algorithms
[
69
,
8
,
10
,
11
]
, which consist of more systematic procedures for solving relevant polynomial systems, as well as algorithms using generating polynomials
[
59
]
. The method of generating polynomials reduces tensor decomposition to solving a different, but related, polynomial system of degree two.

The mentioned decomposition algorithms are often not efficient, as they reduce tensor decomposition to solving nonlinear polynomial systems of equations where the number of variables (and possibly the degrees) increase with the number of tensor entries.
On the other hand, efficient tensor decomposition is known to be possible for certain classes of tensors.
This has been well-studied in the case of order-
3
and
4
tensors. Indeed for (asymmetric) order-
3
tensors of size
n\times n\times n
, generic tensors of rank
r\leq n
are efficiently decomposable via the classic simultaneous diagonalization algorithm
[
36
,
55
]
. For order-
4
tensors, the FOOBI algorithm for certain partially symmetric
n\times n\times n\times n
tensors
[
27
,
15
]
can efficiently decompose overcomplete tensors of rank
r>n
; see
[
43
]
for a recent treatment, including the symmetric setting.
Recently, more sophisticated algorithms have focused on improving rank bounds for efficient tensor decomposition of asymmetric tensors
[
51
,
47
,
46
,
69
]
. Related is the question of identifiability, in particular the search for specific criteria for tensor identifiability
[
52
,
21
,
56
,
28
,
22
,
20
,
6
]
.

Alternative approaches in the study of efficient tensor decomposition include iterative algorithms
[
49
]
such as alternating least squares
[
48
]
, methods based on power iterations
[
50
]
, methods based on approximations
[
65
]
, methods combining the catalecticant method with power iterations
[
45
]
, methods based on homotopy continuation
[
9
]
, as well as algorithms dependent on random models
[
35
,
57
,
39
,
38
,
13
]
.
These numerical methods rely on nonconvex optimization and often do not have global guarantees, although analysis of their nonconvex optimization landscapes has been achieved in some cases (e.g.,
[
44
]
).
There also exist numerical methods involving truncations/relaxations of the generalized moment problem
[
60
,
33
]
, but these require extra real and/or positivity constraints on the tensor.

Part of the present paper deals with efficient decomposition of nonidentifiable tensors. Nonidentifiability has historically been considered from the algebraic perspective
[
64
,
17
,
12
]
. A case-by-case analysis for tensors in low dimensions and with low rank is given in
[

]
.

1.3
Paper outline

Section
2
contains notation and basic definitions. We discuss dehomogenization – viewing a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
in
n+1
variables as an inhomogeneous polynomial of degree at most
d
in
n
variables – as useful for moment matrix extension. We also describe the simultaneous diagonalization procedure and connect its performance to the regularity of sets of points.

Section
3
describes the moment matrix extension algorithm. We refine the algorithm to connect its complexity explicitly to the regularities of target decompositions, generalizing previous improvements to the algorithm of
[
11
]
. We then begin our study of efficient decomposition; as examples we describe tensors with decompositions of extremely low regularity and binary tensors.

Section
4
contains most of the main results of the paper. We specify the conditions needed for the moment matrix extension algorithm to reduce to solving a linear system of equations. For
d=4
we provide counts of the number of linear equations and moment variables, and prove generic sufficiency of the linear system for
r\leq 2n+1
.
We then present a sharper conjecture for efficient decomposition up to
r=O(n^{2})
; computational evidence towards this conjecture is provided.
We also touch on nonidentifiable tensors in this setting.

Section
5
presents an algorithm for efficient decomposition of monomials via the moment matrix extension method.
The method
gives a new explicit parameterization for the space of decompositions of monomials.
As another main result of the paper, it is not only able to algorithmically decompose monomials but also lets us provide and reprove theory.

Throughout the paper we defer some proofs and examples to the
Appendix
; many of these proofs are largely calculations.

Preliminaries

We use bold lowercase
\mathbf{z}
to denote vectors and uppercase
\mathbf{Z}
to denote matrices. With some abuse of notation we also use
\mathbf{Z}
to denote a set of points
\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{s}\}
; just take
\mathbf{Z}
to be the matrix with rows the points.

We will always use
\varphi
to denote a symmetric tensor, with varying format. The notation
S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
denotes the
d
th symmetric power of the vector space
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
, i.e., the vector space of symmetric tensors of dimension
n+1
and order
d
. The dimension of
S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
is
\binom{n+d}{n}
. The following identifications are well known:

\displaystyle S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\textrm{symmetric tensors}\\
\textrm{of order $d$ in $n+1$ variables}\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\leftrightsquigarrow\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]_{d}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\textrm{homogeneous polynomials}\\
\textrm{of degree $d$ in $n+1$ variables}\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\leftrightsquigarrow\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq d}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\textrm{inhomogeneous polynomials of degree at most $d$ in $n$ variables}\end{array}\right\}.

To index
\varphi
we will use the third identification.
When a tensor
\varphi\in S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
is viewed as a multiway array, it is oftened indexed by a
d
-tuple
(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})
where
i_{k}\in\{0,\dots,n\}
.
We index
\varphi
by a monomial of degree at most
d
in
n
variables
x^{\alpha}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
, where
\alpha_{j}
counts the number of times

appears in the
d
-tuple (
0
is ignored). Equivalently, we index
\varphi
directly with the exponent vector
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
.

For an exponent vector
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0}
we call
|\alpha|:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}
the
size
of
\alpha
. This is the same as the
degree
of
x^{\alpha}
. For a finite index set
S\subset\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
we write
|S|
to be the cardinality of
S
,
S_{k}:=\{\alpha\in S\mid|\alpha|=k\}
, and
\operatorname{deg}(S):=\max_{\alpha\in S}|\alpha|
. We will also take
S
to be the set of corresponding monomials
x^{\alpha}
. For
\gamma
in a monomial set

we write
\operatorname{pos}(\gamma)
to denote the position of
\gamma
in
S

x^{\gamma}
in
S
).

Definition 2.1
.

For
\varphi\in S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
and
k\leq d
, the
k
th-
catalecticant matrix
of

\operatorname{Cat}_{k}(\varphi):=\left(\varphi_{\alpha+\beta}\right)_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha|\leq d-k\\
|\beta|\leq k\end{subarray}}\in\mathbb{C}^{\binom{n+d-k}{n}\times\binom{n+k}{n}}.

\operatorname{Cat}_{k}(\varphi)
can equivalently be viewed as a linear operator
S^{k}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\to S^{d-k}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
.

We note that catalecticant matrices are equivalent to the usual
k
-mode symmetric flattenings of a symmetric tensor.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{C}^{s\times(n+1)}
be the matrix with rows
\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
. For an index set

Vandermonde matrix

\mathbf{Z}_{S,k}:=\left(\mathbf{z}_{i,0}^{k-|\alpha|}\mathbf{z}_{i,1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots\mathbf{z}_{i,n}^{\alpha_{n}}\right)_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,\dots,s\\

When

consists of all monomials of degree at most

, then
\mathbf{Z}_{S,k}
is the usual

Vandermonde matrix of

Note that
\mathbf{Z}_{k}

Definition 2.3
.

The
Hilbert function
of a graded ideal

is the function

k\mapsto\operatorname{dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]_{k}/I_{k}),

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{n}
a basis for
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
and dual basis
\mathbf{u}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{n}

An element

We write

.

The Hilbert function

of

is
k\mapsto\operatorname{rank}\left(\operatorname{Cat}_{k}(\varphi)\right)

It is known that
\varphi^{\perp}_{k}=\operatorname{ker}\left(\operatorname{Cat}_{k}(\varphi)\right)

\operatorname{Cat}_{k}(\varphi)
is viewed as a linear operator

53

. Similarly, a

\operatorname{ker}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{k}\right)

∎

It is clear that for

,
h_{\varphi}(k)=0

h_{\varphi}(k)=h_{\varphi}(d-k)

. For a set of points

is nondecreasing in

, i.e., there exists

h_{\mathbf{Z}}(k)=|\mathbf{Z}|
and for all

[
42
]
. An important property of sets of points that we will use is the

,
30
]

Definition 2.6
.

For a set of points
\mathbf{Z}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{s}\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n+1}

\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\otimes k},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{s}^{\otimes k}
are linearly independent as elements of

.

Equivalently, the regularity of
\mathbf{Z}
is the smallest integer

such that
\mathbf{Z}_{k}
is rank
s
. By Lemma

, this is the integer

at which

stabilizes. This is also equivalent to the
Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity
of
\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]/I_{\mathbf{Z}}

[
30
]
.

Definition 2.7
.

A set of points
\mathbf{Z}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{s}\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n+1}

decomposes
a tensor
\varphi\in S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
if
\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\otimes d}
. The smallest

such that there exists

(or

) of
\varphi
. The points
\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{r}
give a

of
\varphi
.

If a set of points
\mathbf{Z}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{s}\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
decomposes

\operatorname{Cat}_{k}(\varphi)=\mathbf{Z}_{d-k}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{k}
for

.
Then for such

and

it holds that

by submultiplicativity of the matrix rank. Although the minimal rank decomposition for a given tensor

may not be unique, the rank of

is invariant under the

action that acts via
\mathbf{M}\cdot\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{s}(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{z}_{i})^{\otimes d}
. This action can be defined and performed independent of any given decomposition.

Definition 2.8
.

A tensor is

Dehomogenization

The method of indexing we have chosen is especially useful when we
dehomogenize
with respect to the
0
th index. In this case, the index
0
– corresponding to variable
x_{0}
– is special. If
\mathbf{Z}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{s}\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
is a set of points giving a size-

with the property that
\mathbf{z}_{i,0}\neq 0
for

, then we can without loss of generality assume that
\mathbf{z}_{i,0}=1
.
In this case, for appropriate
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{s}\in\mathbb{C}
, we can write
\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\otimes d}
.

At the level of points, for an index set
S\subset\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
by dehomogenizing we can drop the dependency on
k
to write any Vandermonde matrix. In this case the
S
-Vandermonde matrix of
\mathbf{Z}
is

\mathbf{Z}_{S}:=\left(\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\alpha}\right)_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,\dots,s\\
\alpha\in S\end{subarray}}\in\mathbb{C}^{s\times|S|},

where
\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\alpha}:=\mathbf{z}_{i,1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots\mathbf{z}_{i,n}^{\alpha_{n}}
as
\mathbf{z}_{i,0}=1
, and
\mathbf{Z}_{k}
is the usual
k^{\text{th}}
Vandermonde matrix of
\mathbf{Z}
. For
k^{\prime}\leq k
,
\mathbf{Z}_{k^{\prime}}
is a submatrix of
\mathbf{Z}_{k}
via restricting indices to size at most
k^{\prime}
.

At the level of tensors, a tensor
\varphi
can be viewed as an object not only in
S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
but also in a larger vector space of objects that restrict to
\varphi
at specific degrees. This is the same as viewing a inhomogeneous polynomial of degree

in
n

d
.

If
\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{s}
gives a size-
s
decomposition of
\varphi
with coefficients
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{s}
then a family of tensors can be produced via

\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\otimes d^{\prime}},\quad d^{\prime}\geq 0.

If
d^{\prime}=d
then this is obviously
\varphi
and for
d^{\prime}<d
these are certain slices of
\varphi
, i.e., fixing
d-d^{\prime}
indices of
\varphi
. More interesting is if
d^{\prime}>d
; in this case
\varphi
is a slice of these higher order tensors, i.e., the restriction of these higher order tensors to multiindices of size at most

is precisely
\varphi
.

Model of computation

It is known that tensor decomposition is NP-hard in the worst case
[
37
]
. In this work we are mainly focused on the possibility of
efficient
algorithms; we will be using linear algebraic operations as subroutines (on polynomially sized inputs in the size of the tensor), including eigendecompositions and solving linear systems. Following
[
46
]
and other work on efficient tensor decomposition we assume these operations can be performed in exact arithmetic.

2.1
Warm-up: simultaneous diagonalization

As a warm-up we describe the classic simultaneous diagonalization algorithm for symmetric tensor decomposition (also known as
Jennrich’s algorithm
), see e.g.,
[
55
]
.

Write
D:=\left\lfloor(d-1)/2\right\rfloor
. Let
\mathbf{Z}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{s}\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
be a size-
s
decomposition of
\varphi\in S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
, and suppose
\mathbf{z}_{i,0}=1
for all
i
. Thus, we write
\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\otimes d}
. Write
\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{s}\}
.

Assume that
D\geq\rho(\mathbf{Z})
. Let
\hat{\varphi}_{0},\dots,\hat{\varphi}_{n}
be the
n+1
slices of
\varphi
matricized as elements of
S^{D}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\otimes\left(S^{d-1-D}{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}\right)^{*}
. Explicitly we have

\hat{\varphi}_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i,j}\operatorname{vec}\left(\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\otimes D}\right)\operatorname{vec}\left(\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\otimes d-1-D}\right)^{\top}=\mathbf{Z}_{D}^{\top}\Lambda_{j}\mathbf{Z}_{d-1-D},

where
\Lambda_{j}:=\operatorname{diag}\{\lambda_{1}\mathbf{z}_{1,j},\dots,\lambda_{s}\mathbf{z}_{s,j}\}
. Then letting
\dagger
denote the pseudoinverse, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}_{j}\hat{\varphi}_{0}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Z}_{D}^{\top}\Lambda_{j}\mathbf{Z}_{d-1-D}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{D}^{\top}\Lambda_{0}\mathbf{Z}_{d-1-D}\right)^{\dagger}

(2)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Z}_{D}^{\top}\Lambda_{j}\mathbf{Z}_{d-1-D}\mathbf{Z}_{d-1-D}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{0}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{D}^{\top}\right)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Z}_{D}^{\top}\operatorname{diag}\{\mathbf{z}_{1,j},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{s,j}\}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{D}^{\top}\right)^{\dagger},

where the second line follows because
\mathbf{Z}_{D}
is full row-rank and as
d-1-D\geq D
,
\mathbf{Z}_{d-1-D}
is also full row-rank. Now this last line is an eigendecomposition, where
\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\otimes D},\mathbf{z}_{i,j}
form an eigenpair. In the case where the eigenvalues are simple the decomposition is readily obtained as eigenvectors. Otherwise, we can pick
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
randomly from some continuous distribution (e.g., Gaussian) and form the linear combination

\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\hat{\varphi}_{j}\hat{\varphi}_{0}^{\dagger},

for which
(\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\otimes D},\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\mathbf{z}_{i,j})
form an eigenpair (as the
\hat{\varphi}_{j}\hat{\varphi}_{0}^{\dagger}
commute). With probability one the eigenvalues are simple.

The implications of this procedure succeeding are threefold. First,
s
must equal the
\operatorname{rank}(\varphi)
from
D\geq\rho(\mathbf{Z})
. Second, the decomposition
\mathbf{Z}
must be unique. Though we described the procedure in terms of
\mathbf{Z}
it can be seen that each step left no room for another decomposition (as we assumed that
\mathbf{Z}
is a decomposition in the first place). This is in fact an algorithmic proof of Theorem 2.17 of
[
58
]
, which states that if
\mathbf{Z}
gives a decomposition for
\varphi
and
d\geq 2\rho(\mathbf{Z})+1
then
\mathbf{Z}
is a unique decomposition. Indeed,
D\geq\rho(\mathbf{Z})
and
d\geq 2D+1
. Third, as an algorithm it is efficient: each step can be performed via polynomially many linear algebra operations.

2.1.1
Meta-algorithm for tensor decomposition

The above can be simplified slightly by choosing a set
B,|B|=s
indexing columns of
\mathbf{Z}_{D}
such that
\mathbf{Z}_{B}
is an
s\times s
invertible submatrix of
\mathbf{Z}_{D}
. Taking now
\mathbf{H}_{j}=\mathbf{Z}_{B}^{\top}\Lambda_{j}\mathbf{Z}_{B}
the above procedure returns

\mathbf{H}_{j}\mathbf{H}_{0}^{-1}=\mathbf{Z}_{B}^{\top}\operatorname{diag}\{\mathbf{z}_{1,j},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{s,h}\}\mathbf{Z}_{B}^{-\top}.

As long as
B
contains
\{1,x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
we can obtain the tensor decomposition from the eigendecomposition. This is not a strong assumption, nor is the assumption that we can find such a
B
– we will elaborate on both in Section
3
.

A limitation to the simultaneous diagonalization algorithm is that
\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\otimes D},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{s}^{\otimes D}
have to be linearly independent. In particular, this forces
s\leq\binom{n+D}{n}=O\left(n^{\left\lfloor(d-1)\right\rfloor/2}\right)
– and even if
s
is small enough, it is not always the case that this linear independence condition is satisfied.

Conceptually this can be addressed by taking a slightly inverted perspective – the issue is not that
s
is too large but
d
is too small. Indeed, let
\mathbf{Z}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{s}\},\mathbf{z}_{i,0}=1
be a set of points (with corresponding scalars
\lambda_{i}
). If we are given
\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\otimes d}
with
2\rho(\mathbf{Z})+1>d
then we would not be able to apply the simultaneous diagonalization algorithm; however, if we somehow had access to the tensor
\varphi^{\uparrow}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\otimes 2\rho(\mathbf{Z})+1}
then we can. Of course, we do not have access to
\mathbf{Z}
nor
\varphi^{\uparrow}
– but we would like to solve for, in some principled way, this
extension

\varphi^{\uparrow}
, motivating a meta-algorithm for tensor decomposition.

Input:
\varphi\in S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
; desired decomposition size
s

Output:
A size-
s
decomposition of
\varphi

1:

Solve for an
extension

\varphi^{\uparrow}
such that
\varphi^{\uparrow}
has a unique size-

decomposition that also decomposes
\varphi

2:

Apply a

algorithm to recover the unique decomposition from
\varphi^{\uparrow}

Algorithm 1

Meta-algorithm for tensor decomposition

The central idea of
[
11
]
is to introduce an algorithm for the first step of the meta-algorithm and apply simultaneous diagonalization as the second step. Given the “right” extension
\varphi^{\uparrow}
, simultaneous diagonalization, and therefore decomposition, is efficient. However, we know that tensors are not always identifiable nor efficiently decomposable; thus, both properties must manifest in the extension step.

We also note that in Step
1
it often suffices to obtain a
partial
extension; we do not necessarily need a full degree
2\rho(\mathbf{Z})+1
extension but rather enough of a sufficiently high degree extension to apply the core algorithm (which will often be simultaneous diagonalization).

Remark 2.9
.

This meta-algorithm is implicit in the work on average case tensor decomposition
[
57
,
38
]
. The main idea is to argue that some
\varphi^{\uparrow}
can be well-approximated with high probability over the randomness of the underlying components efficiently, and then show that simultaneous diagonalization on this approximated
\varphi^{\uparrow}
outputs a decomposition close enough to the random decomposition. Though we are instead focused on efficient
exact
decomposition and do not assume a random model, our exposition unifies some ideas from different fields.

3
Moment matrix extension

We discuss the algorithm first described by
[
11
]
. The crucial contribution in their work was to introduce the idea of extending a tensor
\varphi
to a higher order tensor; this is Step
1
of Algorithm
1
. We make a novel observation connecting possible decompositions output by this algorithm and the Hilbert functions of the corresponding sets of points, allowing for a refinement of the algorithm.

3.1
Key constructions

Definition 3.1
.

Given a tensor
\varphi\in S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
the corresponding
\binom{n+d}{n}\times\binom{n+d+1}{n}

Hankel matrix

\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}
is:

\left(\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}\right)_{\alpha,\beta}=\begin{cases}\varphi_{\alpha+\beta},&|\alpha+\beta|\leq d,\\
y_{\alpha+\beta},&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Here
|\alpha|\leq d
,
|\beta|\leq d+1
, and the
y
are formal
moment variables
. When
\varphi
is clear from context we simply write
\mathcal{H}
. For index sets
B,B^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{H}_{B,B^{\prime}}
denotes the corresponding submatrix.

Note the similarities of this definition with Definition
2.1
. We can view the Hankel matrix as the catalecticant matrix of an order
2d+1
“extension” of
\varphi
; however, we only have access to entries indexed by multiindices of size at most
d
so the moment variables of larger size are left unknown.

Definition 3.2
.

A set of monomials
B
is
connected to
1
if for all
x^{\alpha}\in B
either
x^{\alpha}=1
or there exists
x^{\alpha^{\prime}}\in B,i\in[n]
such that
x^{\alpha}=x^{\alpha^{\prime}}x_{i}
.

Connected to
1
will be required for Theorem
3.7
below which justifies the extension algorithm.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
B
be a set of monomials. Define the system of
determinantal relations
with respect to
B
to be the polynomial system in the moment variables

\operatorname{det}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{i}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{j}\}}\right)-\operatorname{det}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{j}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{i}\}}\right)=0

for
x^{\alpha},x^{\beta}\in B,i\neq j
.

Let
Y
be the set of moment variables involved in these relations; then
Y
can be explicitly given by

Y=\{y_{x^{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}x_{i}}\mid|\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}|\geq d,x^{\alpha},x^{\alpha^{\prime}}\in B,1\leq i\leq n\}.

(3)

We will also denote elements of
Y
by just the indices
x^{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}x_{i}
. Note that
Y
indeed contains all the variables appearing in the determinantal relations as in each equation, the variable
x^{\alpha+\beta}x_{i}x_{j}
cancels and the rest of the variables are of the form
x^{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}x_{i},x^{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}x_{j}
for
\alpha^{\prime}\in B
such that
|\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}|\geq d
.

Definition 3.4
.

For a set of monomials
B
connected to
1
define the
n

shifted matrices

\mathcal{H}_{B,B_{i}}
, where

B_{i}=\{x^{\alpha}x_{i}\mid x^{\alpha}\in B\}.

If
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
is invertible define the
i
th
multiplication matrix
as

\mathbf{M}^{B}_{i}(Y):=\mathcal{H}_{B,B_{i}}\mathcal{H}_{B,B}^{-1}.

The entries of each multiplication matrix are polynomials in the moment variables
Y
. More commonly used in the literature are the
commuting relations

[
11
,
10
]
.

Definition 3.5
.

Define the system of
commuting relations
to be the polynomial system in the moment variables

\mathbf{M}_{i}^{B}(Y)\mathbf{M}_{j}^{B}(Y)-\mathbf{M}_{j}^{B}(Y)\mathbf{M}_{i}^{B}(Y)=0,\quad\forall i\neq j.

Sometimes
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
is not fully specified, i.e., there are moment variables appearing in some entries, and thus invertibility of
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
is not well-defined.
In particular, this occurs when
\operatorname{deg}(B)>d/2
. Using the identity with the adjugate:

\mathcal{H}_{B,B}^{-1}=\operatorname{det}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B,B}\right)^{-1}\operatorname{adj}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B,B}\right),

we can clear denominators and, as
\operatorname{adj}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B,B}\right)
is entry-wise polynomial in
Y
, the commuting relations are polynomials in
Y
.

The following lemma shows that the commuting and determinantal relations are equivalent; this is essentially Theorem 6.3 of
[
11
]
but we derive the result directly.

Lemma 3.6
.

The determinantal and commuting relations are equivalent (up to a global factor).

Proof.

First, the commuting relations are explicitly

\mathcal{H}_{B,B_{i}}\mathcal{H}_{B,B}^{-1}\mathcal{H}_{B,B_{j}}\mathcal{H}_{B,B}^{-1}-\mathcal{H}_{B,B_{j}}\mathcal{H}_{B,B}^{-1}\mathcal{H}_{B,B_{i}}\mathcal{H}_{B,B}^{-1}=0.

Up to multiplication by
\operatorname{det}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B,B}\right)
this is equivalent to

\mathcal{H}_{B,B_{i}}\operatorname{adj}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B,B}\right)\mathcal{H}_{B,B_{j}}-\mathcal{H}_{B,B_{j}}\operatorname{adj}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B,B}\right)\mathcal{H}_{B,B_{i}}=0.

We can index the matrix on the left-hand side by
(\alpha,\beta)
for
\alpha,\beta\in B
. The corresponding entry is

\mathcal{H}_{B,x^{\alpha}x_{i}}^{\top}\operatorname{adj}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B,B}\right)\mathcal{H}_{B,x^{\beta}x_{j}}-\mathcal{H}_{B,x^{\alpha}x_{j}}^{\top}\operatorname{adj}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B,B}\right)\mathcal{H}_{x^{\beta}x_{i}}.

(4)

It is straightforward to see that

\operatorname{det}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{i}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{j}\}}\right)=\mathcal{H}_{x^{\alpha+\beta}x_{i}x_{j}}\operatorname{det}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B,B}\right)-\mathcal{H}_{B,x^{\alpha}x_{i}}^{\top}\operatorname{adj}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B,B}\right)\mathcal{H}_{B,x^{\beta}x_{j}}

via the Laplace expansion of the determinant. Then setting equation
4
equal to zero is equivalent to

\operatorname{det}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{i}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{j}\}}\right)-\operatorname{det}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{j}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{i}\}}\right)=0.\qed

We observe that
Y
is also the set of moment variables appearing in the matrices
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B,B_{i}},i=1,\dots,n
. In other words, once a solution to the determinantal relations is found, substituting this solution into the matrices
\mathcal{H}_{B,B},\mathcal{H}_{B,B_{i}},i=1,\dots,n
fully specifies these matrices.

3.2
Algorithm for size-
s

Motivated by the discussion in Section

we seek to “extend”
\varphi
to a higher-order tensor. We have the pieces: the Hankel matrix

represents symbolically this unknown higher-order tensor, and the determinantal/commuting relations represent relations that the entries of this higher-order tensor must satisfy. Indeed, reconsidering the base algorithm the multiplication matrices
\mathbf{M}_{j}^{B}(Y)
are generalizations of the matrices
\hat{\varphi}_{j}\hat{\varphi}_{0}^{\dagger}

In this section we do not necessarily look for a minimal rank decomposition and we let

First we make the following weak assumption:

The size-

Theorem 3.7

Proof sketch.

We sketch an alternative proof compared to the proof in

Theorem

, is the dimension of such
V
.

Note that the number of essential variables is well-defined as the number of essential variables is invariant under the
\operatorname{GL}(n+1)
action. Moreover, making
\varphi
concise is an easy task via the following proposition.

Proposition 3.13

(
[
16
]
, Proposition 1)
.

Let
\varphi\in S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
. Then

N_{\text{ess}}(\varphi)=\operatorname{rank}\left(\operatorname{Cat}_{1}(\varphi)\right).

Moreover, a basis for the row space of
\operatorname{Cat}_{1}(\varphi)
serves as essential variables for
\varphi
.

From Proposition
3.13
we can now without loss of generality assume our tensor is concise. We formalize this as an assumption.

Assumption 2
.

The tensor
\varphi\in S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
is concise.

Under Assumption
2
we have the following two guarantees on the functions
h_{\mathbf{Z}}(k)
for any size-
s
decomposition
\mathbf{Z}
of
\varphi\in S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
and thus conditions for a valid
\mathcal{B}_{s}
.

1.

h_{\mathbf{Z}}(1)=n+1
, so that
|B_{1}|=n
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}_{s}
,

2.

h_{\mathbf{Z}}(k)=s
for
k\geq d
, so that
\operatorname{deg}(B)\leq d
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}_{s}
.

The first guarantee is due to submultiplicativity of the matrix rank. The second is due to the fact that for
\varphi
to admit a size-
s
decomposition,
\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\otimes d}
and the elements
\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\otimes d},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{s}^{\otimes d}
must be linearly independent. Then
\operatorname{rank}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{d}\right)=s
. We have thus recovered Theorem 3.9 of
[
10
]
. We now show we can do better in the following theorem; it is similar to Lemma 2.15 of
[
58
]
.

Theorem 3.14
.

Instate Assumption
1
. Suppose we include
B\in\mathcal{B}_{s}
such that
\operatorname{deg}(B)=D
. Then we can also impose
|B_{\leq k}|=\operatorname{rank}\left(\operatorname{Cat}(\varphi,k)\right)
for
k\leq d-D
.

Proof.

Suppose we can choose
B
such that
\operatorname{deg}(B)=D
. Let
\mathbf{Z}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{s}\}
be a decomposition output by Algorithm
2
corresponding to
B
. Then
\operatorname{deg}(B)=D
implies that
\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{Z}_{D})=s
.

Writing
\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{s}\}
, since
\operatorname{Cat}_{k}(\varphi)=\mathbf{Z}_{d-k}^{\top}\Lambda\mathbf{Z}_{k}
, if
k\leq d-D
then
d-k\geq D
, so that
\mathbf{Z}_{d-k}^{\top}
is full column rank, i.e., it is an injective linear map. Therefore, as
\Lambda
is also rank
s
, we must have
\operatorname{rank}\left(\operatorname{Cat}_{k}(\varphi)\right)=\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{Z}_{k})
. Then
|B_{\leq k}|=\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{Z}_{k})=\operatorname{rank}\left(\operatorname{Cat}_{k}(\varphi)\right)
.
∎

This is a “data-dependent” way of forming a valid
\mathcal{B}_{s}
and serves as a refinement to the vanilla algorithm of
[
11
]
. For example, we can determine the following:

1.

Suppose we desire a size-
s
decompositions, but
s>\max_{k}\operatorname{rank}\left(\operatorname{Cat}_{k}(\varphi)\right)
. Then by submultiplicativity of the matrix rank, any valid
B\in\mathcal{B}_{s}
must be such that
\operatorname{deg}(B)>d/2
.

2.

On the other hand, suppose we include
B\in\mathcal{B}_{s}
with
\operatorname{deg}(B)=D\leq d
. Then Theorem
3.14
leaves free the choice of size of
B_{k}
for
k>d-D
. In particular, the sizes of possible decompositions are not fully specified by
\operatorname{deg}(B)
.

Remark 3.15
.

Our discussion has an interesting connection to Question 2.14 of
[
58
]
, which asks if all minimal rank decompositions of a tensor
\varphi
have the same Hilbert functions. This was shown to be false in
[
5
]
. The counterexamples involve sets of points with regularity larger than
d/2
. In light of Theorem
3.14
this makes sense. From the perspective of Algorithm
2
, when we choose
B
with
\operatorname{deg}(B)>d/2
the matrix
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
is not fully specified by the tensor, and so the degree sequences of possible
B
specify the differing regularities of these decompositions.

3.3
Efficient decomposition

Algorithm
2
is not a priori efficient:
\mathcal{B}_{s}
can be of size exponential in the number of tensor entries (if
s
is large) and the relations can be polynomials of large degree in the moment variables
Y
.

However, if we can ensure that for a tensor
\varphi
and a desired decomposition size
s
, both a valid
\mathcal{B}_{s}
can be made to be a collection of size polynomial in the number of tensor entries (or even better, of constant size), and for each
B\in\mathcal{B}_{s}
the solution to the relations is fully specified by a (sequence of)
linear
system(s) of size polynomial in the number of tensor entries, then Algorithm
2
is efficient.

We now describe classes of tensors for which this is possible. Each of these classes satisfy certain conditions that make this Algorithm
2
efficient.
In Section
4
we use our discussion in Section
3.2.1
to detail a method for efficient decomposition for even order tensors with decompositions of low regularity – giving efficient decomposition for generic tensors of rank larger than allowed by simultaneous diagonalization – and expand on the theory for
d=4
. In Section
5
we demonstrate that if the given tensor to be decomposed is a monomial in a canonical format, the algorithm parametrizes the space of decompositions (monomials are nonidentifiable) to output
arbitrary decompositions
efficiently.

As a lead-in, we outline how efficient decomposition is possible for two simple classes of tensors: tensors decomposable via simultaneous diagonalization (to be expected from the discussion in Section
2.1
) and binary tensors.

3.3.1
Tensors decomposable via simultaneous diagonalization

As described in Section
2.1
, simultaneous diagonalization is a specific instance of the moment matrix extension algorithm where no polynomial system needs to be solved.

Condition 3.16
.

Let
D=\left\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\right\rfloor
.
\varphi
satisfies

\operatorname{rank}\left(\operatorname{Cat}_{D}(\varphi)\right)=\operatorname{rank}(\varphi).

This is a condition on the regularity of target decompositions: by submultiplicativity of the matrix rank, for any minimal rank decomposition
\mathbf{Z}
of
\varphi
,
D\geq\rho(\mathbf{Z})
. In particular, by Theorem
3.14
there exists a monomial set
B,|B|=\operatorname{rank}(\varphi)
connected to
1
with
\operatorname{deg}(B)\leq D
, and the corresponding Hankel matrix
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
is invertible and fully specified (i.e., no moment variables are present) as
d\geq 2D+1
.

Claim 3.17
.

The monomial set
B
described above can be found efficiently.

Therefore,
\mathcal{B}_{s}=\{B\}
is valid with respect to
\varphi
. This claim is a special case of a more general proposition we prove in Section
4
. As
d\geq 2D+1
the set of moment variables
Y
is empty, and under Condition
3.16
we assume existence of a decomposition of size
\operatorname{rank}(\varphi)
; thus the determinantal relations must be trivially satisfied and an eigendecomposition recovers the unique decomposition. This discussion implies the following result.

Proposition 3.18
.

Let
\varphi\in S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
satisfy Assumption
1
and Condition
3.16
. Then Algorithm
2
is efficient. In particular, generic tensors of rank at most
\binom{n+D}{n}
are efficiently decomposable via moment matrix extension.

3.3.2
Binary tensors

We now consider the class of binary tensors, i.e., tensors
\varphi\in S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Classically, Sylvester’s algorithm
[
68
]
can be used for decomposing binary tensors. We show that an application of Algorithm
2
similarly decomposes binary tensors, efficiently.

Recall that the determinantal relations require
1\leq i<j\leq n
. However, since
n=1
there are no relations. Moreover, a monomial set
B
connected to
1
is simply a set
B=\{1,x,\dots,x^{k}\}
for some integer
k
. Thus, a desired decomposition size
s
forces
\mathcal{B}_{s}=\{B\}
with
B=\{1,x,\dots,x^{s-1}\}
; it is valid with respect to
\varphi
.

This implies an efficient algorithm for size
s
decomposition of binary tensors satisfying Assumption
1
: simply take
B=\{1,x,\dots,x^{s-1}\}
and form the matrix
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
and shifted matrices
\mathcal{H}_{B,B_{i}}
. There are no moment variables in any of the matrices when
2s-1\leq d
, there are moment variables in the shifted matrices when
2s-1\geq d+1
, and
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
has moment variables when
2s-2\geq d+1
. When there are moment variables, because there are no determinantal relations, the practitioner can treat the moment variables as free parameters. The only constraint is that the choice of values for the moment variables must satisfy that
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
is invertible and the resulting multiplication matrices
\mathbf{M}_{i}^{B}
are nondefective.

In the case where
s<\operatorname{rank}(\varphi)
there cannot exist a choice of values for the moment variables that make the multiplication matrices nondefective (otherwise we would have a size
s<\operatorname{rank}(\varphi)
decomposition) by definition of the tensor rank. For larger decompositions, we have the following restriction on the existence of size
s
decompositions.

Proposition 3.19
.

Let
\varphi\in S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and write
r:=\operatorname{rank}(\varphi)
. Then there are no decompositions of
\varphi
of size
s
for
r+1\leq s\leq d-r+1
.

Proof.

If
r+1\leq s\leq d-r+1
then
s+r\leq d+1
. Take
|B|=s
, which must be
\{1,x,\dots,x^{s-1}\}
, and consider
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
. In particular, take
S=\{1,x,\dots,x^{r}\}
; the
(r+1)\times s
matrix
\mathcal{H}_{S,B}
is the first
r+1
rows of
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
. Since
s+r\leq d+1\implies r+s-1\leq d
,
\mathcal{H}_{S,B}
is fully specified with no moment variables. As the rank of
\varphi
is
r
this means that
\mathcal{H}_{S,B}
is rank at most
r
. Thus, even though
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
may contain moment variables, no choice of values for the moment variables can make
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
invertible.
∎

This result is likely known, but our proof demonstrates that a simple analysis of the extension algorithm can give theoretical results. We perform similar analysis in Section
5
. Our discussion implies the following result.

Proposition 3.20
.

Let
\varphi\in S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{2}
satisfy Assumption
1
. Then generic choices of values for the moment variables correspond to a decomposition of size
s
, if such a decomposition exists. In particular, parameterized decompositions of binary tensors can be found efficiently.

Remark 3.21
.

For the statement in Proposition

Even order tensors with decompositions of low regularity

Condition 4.1
.

\varphi

\operatorname{rank}\left(\operatorname{Cat}_{\left\lfloor d/2\right\rfloor}(\varphi)\right)=\operatorname{rank}(\varphi).

This implies that for any minimal rank decomposition

of

\rho(\mathbf{Z})\leq\left\lfloor d/2\right\rfloor

there exists a monomial set

|B|=\operatorname{rank}(\varphi)
,

2.

B

,

3.

\operatorname{deg}(B)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\right\rfloor

We first show that such a

Finding a valid monomial set

Under Condition

4.1
Determinantal equations

When

is odd, Condition

is equivalent to Condition

. On the other hand, for

we are outside the regime of the simultaneous diagonalization algorithm. In this case the set of moment variables

will be nonempty and there will be nontrivial determinantal relations.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
d
be even,
B
be such that
\operatorname{deg}(B)=d/2
and
B
chosen by Algorithm
3
. Then the expression

\operatorname{det}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{i}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{j}\}}\right)-\operatorname{det}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{j}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{i}\}}\right)

is

1.

the zero polynomial if
|\alpha+\beta|\leq d-2
or
\alpha=\beta
or

\left(x^{\alpha}x_{i}\in B\vee x^{\beta}x_{j}\in B\right)\wedge\left(x^{\alpha}x_{j}\in B\vee x^{\beta}x_{i}\in B\right);

2.

linear if
|\beta|=d/2-1
and
|\alpha|=d/2
, and
x^{\beta}x_{i}\notin B\vee x^{\beta}x_{j}\notin B
(or vice-versa for
\alpha
);

3.

quadratic otherwise.

Proof.

1.

If

\left(x^{\alpha}x_{i}\in B\vee x^{\beta}x_{j}\in B\right)\wedge\left(x^{\alpha}x_{j}\in B\vee x^{\beta}x_{i}\in B\right)

then there is a repeated row or column in both matrices, so by definition the determinant is zero. Next, suppose
|\alpha+\beta|\leq d-2
. Then
\operatorname{deg}(x^{\alpha+\beta}x_{i}x_{j})\leq d
. Consider the submatrix of
\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{i}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{j}\}}
given by
\mathcal{H}_{B_{\leq|\alpha|+1}\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{i}\},B_{\leq|\beta|+1}\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{j}\}}
. This matrix is fully specified, i.e., no moment variables present, and is a submatrix of
\left(\operatorname{Cat}_{d/2}(\varphi)\right)_{|\beta|+1}
. But we know Algorithm 2 guarantees
|B|_{\leq|\beta|+1}=\operatorname{rank}\left(\left(\operatorname{Cat}_{d/2}(\varphi)\right)_{|\beta|+1}\right)
, and so
\mathcal{H}_{B_{\leq|\alpha|+1}\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{i}\},B_{\leq|\beta|+1}\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{j}\}}
must have linearly dependent columns. Then

\operatorname{det}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{i}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{j}\}}\right)=0
. The same holds for
\operatorname{det}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{j}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{i}\}}\right)
.

2.

Without loss of generality suppose
|\beta|=d/2-1
and
x^{\beta}x_{j}\notin B
and
|\alpha|=d/2
, so that
x^{\alpha}x_{i}
indexes the last row and
x^{\beta}x_{j}
the last column of
\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{i}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{j}\}}
. The row corresponding to
x^{\alpha}x_{i}
will contain moment variables

, where

Lemma 4.16
.

Let

Proof.

We can proceed by an induction. The format
(n,r)

and the format

specifies

as
r+1\leq 2n+2<2(n+1)+1
. Similarly, we index the variables and equations for the former format by

, respectively, and likewise for the latter format.

We show that
Y^{(n,r)}
“embeds” in

in a suitable way, and similarly for
E^{(n,r)}
in

. Consider a variable in

, which is indexed by

for

. This is precisely

for
x^{\alpha},x^{\alpha^{\prime}}\in B^{(n+1,r+1)}_{2}=B^{(n,r)}_{2}

Consider an equation

\displaystyle Y^{(n+1,r+1)}

\displaystyle=Y^{(n,r)}\cup(Y^{(n+1,r+1)}\setminus Y^{(n,r)}),

\displaystyle E^{(n+1,r+1)}

\displaystyle=E^{(n,r)}\cup(E^{(n+1,r+1)}\setminus E^{(n,r)}).

.

4.2.3
Further application: three collinear points

One might ask if there is a non-generic rank
r
tensor
\varphi\in S^{4}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
where
(n,r)
is an efficient format but
\mathbf{A}
drops rank. In this subsection we show that this is indeed the case for a specific class of nonidentifiable tensors – decompositions with three collinear points. Although these tensors are non-generic, linear relations can still suffice for extension and decomposition.

Fix the format
(n,n+2)
. We know from Theorem
4.17
that this is an efficient format. From Lemma
4.3
the quadratic relations are of the form

\operatorname{det}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{i}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{j}\}}\right)-\operatorname{det}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{j}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{i}\}}\right)=0

for
\alpha,\beta\in B_{2},i\neq j
. Now if
\varphi
satisfies Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
and is of this format, then there are no quadratic moment relations:
B_{2}
is simply one element and so the quadratic relations are the constant zero polynomials. Tensors of this format are thus completely specified by linear equations – including when there are multiple decompositions.

Condition 4.21
.

\varphi\in S^{4}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
is such that
\operatorname{rank}(\varphi)=n+2
and there exists a decomposition
\mathbf{Z}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{r}\}
where
\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots\mathbf{z}_{r-1}
are generic and
\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2},\mathbf{z}_{r}
are collinear.

A set of such points can be written

\mathbf{Z}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{z}_{2}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{z}_{n+1}^{\top}\\
t\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\top}+(1-t)\mathbf{z}_{2}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}

where
t\in(0,1)
(because we dehomogenize) and
\mathbf{z}_{i,0}=1
for all
i
.

Proposition 4.22
.

Let
\mathbf{Z}
be a set of points satisfying Condition
4.21
and let

B=\{1,x_{1},\dots,x_{n},x_{1}^{2}\}.

Then for all
i,j,k,\ell\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the points
\mathbf{Z}
satisfies the relations

\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{B\setminus\{x_{1}^{2}\}\cup\{x_{i}x_{j}\}}\right)\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{B\setminus\{x_{1}^{2}\}\cup\{x_{k}x_{\ell}\}}\right)-\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{B\setminus\{x_{1}^{2}\}\cup\{x_{i}x_{k}\}}\right)\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{B\setminus\{x_{1}^{2}\}\cup\{x_{j}x_{\ell}\}}\right)=0.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle m_{x_{1}^{2}}^{x_{i}x_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{r}(-1)^{r+k}\mathbf{z}_{k,i}\mathbf{z}_{k,j}\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}^{\hat{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\{1,2,r\}}(-1)^{r+k}\mathbf{z}_{k,i}\mathbf{z}_{k,j}\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}^{\hat{k}}\right),

as
\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}^{\hat{k}}\right)=0
for
k\neq 1,2,r
. We can also see
\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}^{\hat{1}}\right)=t\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}^{\hat{r}}\right),\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}^{\hat{2}}\right)=-(1-t)\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}^{\hat{r}}\right)
,
so we can deduce

\displaystyle m_{x_{1}^{2}}^{x_{i}x_{j}}/\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}^{\hat{r}}\right)=-t(1-t)\left(\mathbf{z}_{1,i}-\mathbf{z}_{2,i}\right)\left(\mathbf{z}_{1,j}-\mathbf{z}_{2,j}\right),

\displaystyle m_{x_{1}^{2}}^{x_{k}x_{\ell}}/\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}^{\hat{r}}\right)=-t(1-t)\left(\mathbf{z}_{1,k}-\mathbf{z}_{2,k}\right)\left(\mathbf{z}_{1,\ell}-\mathbf{z}_{2,\ell}\right),

\displaystyle m_{x_{1}^{2}}^{x_{i}x_{k}}/\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}^{\hat{r}}\right)=-t(1-t)\left(\mathbf{z}_{1,i}-\mathbf{z}_{2,i}\right)\left(\mathbf{z}_{1,k}-\mathbf{z}_{2,k}\right),

\displaystyle m_{x_{1}^{2}}^{x_{j}x_{\ell}}/\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}^{\hat{r}}\right)=-t(1-t)\left(\mathbf{z}_{1,j}-\mathbf{z}_{2,j}\right)\left(\mathbf{z}_{1,\ell}-\mathbf{z}_{2,\ell}\right).

∎

This actually holds for
B=\{1,x_{1},\dots,x_{n},x_{i}x_{j}\}
for any
1\leq i,j\leq n
, and replacing
x_{1}^{2}
with
x_{i}x_{j}
in the statement and proof. Using this proposition we give Example
A.3
, in the appendix, of a tensor of format
(n,r)=(2,4)
such that
\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2},\mathbf{z}_{3}
are generic and
\mathbf{z}_{4}
lies on the line passing through
\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2}
. We explicitly demonstrate that though
(2,4)
is an efficient format, when there are three collinear points the matrix
\mathbf{A}
drops rank; we then show that this corresponds to a one-dimensional parameterized space of decompositions. This example recovers case (4b)(ii) of Theorem 3.7 of
[
58
]
via an algorithmic method.

We generalize this in the following theorem, also generalizing some cases from Theorem 4.2 of
[
58
]
to larger
n
.

Theorem 4.23
.

Under Condition
4.21
the tensor
\varphi
has a one-dimensional space of decompositions: the points
\mathbf{z}_{3},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{n+1}
and
s_{1}\mathbf{z}_{1}+(1-s_{1})\mathbf{z}_{2},s_{2}\mathbf{z}_{1}+(1-s_{2})\mathbf{z}_{2},s_{3}\mathbf{z}_{1}+(1-s_{3})\mathbf{z}_{2}
, where
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}
are the three distinct solutions to a cubic with coefficients parameterized by a single moment variable. Each decomposition can be found efficiently via the parameterization.

Proof.

With a change of variables we can always write
\mathbf{Z}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{n+2}\}
such that
\mathbf{z}_{1}=\mathbf{e}_{0}
,
\mathbf{z}_{k}=\mathbf{e}_{0}+\mathbf{e}_{k-1}
for
k=2,\dots,n+1
, and
\mathbf{z}_{n+2}=\mathbf{e}_{0}+(1-t)\mathbf{e}_{1}
. It can be easily seen that
\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{B}\right)=-t(1-t)
, and for all
x^{\alpha}\neq x_{1}^{2}
that
\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{B\setminus\{x_{1}^{2}\}\cup\{x^{\alpha}\}}\right)=0
. In particular, the column
y_{x_{1}^{5}}
of
\mathbf{A}
is zero. Each other column
y_{x_{1}^{4}x_{j}},j\geq 2
contains
\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{B}\right)
in exactly one entry – the equation
(x_{1}^{3},x_{1}x_{j})\in E_{1}
. We can treat
y_{x_{1}^{5}}
as a free parameter, and as there are no quadratic relations, the dimension of the space of decompositions is one dimensional.

From the same analysis as in Example
A.3
, a generic choice for
y_{x_{1}^{5}}
results in multiplication matrices that are nondefective, with eigenvectors
\mathbf{z}_{3},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{n+1}
fixed regardless of choice of parameter. The remaining three eigenvectors are of the form
s_{i}\mathbf{z}_{1}+(1-s_{i})\mathbf{z}_{2},i=1,2,3
where
s_{i}
are the three solutions to a cubic in the choice of
y_{x_{1}^{5}}
.
∎

Remark 4.24
.

For this specific format, with
r=n+2
and
\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{B}\right)\neq 0
for
B=\{1,x_{1},\dots,x_{n},x_{i}x_{j}\}
, three points collinear seems to be a necessary condition for
\mathbf{A}
to drop rank.
For example, if
\mathbf{z}_{n+2}
is a nontrivial linear combination of
\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2},\mathbf{z}_{3}
, so we have four points coplanar,
\mathbf{A}
is full column rank (verified by a specialization).

We conjecture that when
Condition
4.5
and Condition
4.21
are satisfied, the relations

\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{B\setminus\{x_{1}^{2}\}\cup\{x_{i}x_{j}\}}\right)\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{B\setminus\{x_{1}^{2}\}\cup\{x_{k}x_{\ell}\}}\right)-\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{B\setminus\{x_{1}^{2}\}\cup\{x_{i}x_{k}\}}\right)\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{B\setminus\{x_{1}^{2}\}\cup\{x_{j}x_{\ell}\}}\right)=0

generate the radical of the vanishing of the
|Y|\times|Y|
minors of
\mathbf{A}
.

Remark 4.25
.

When
r>n+2
there are nontrivial quadratic determinantal relations. However, similar behavior as above appears when
(n,r)
is still an efficient format,
\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{r-1}
are generic, and
\mathbf{z}_{r}
is collinear with
\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2}
. We observe experimentally that
\mathbf{A}
drops rank by one. Fixing one variable and writing all other variables as a linear expression in the fixed variable, we can substitute these expressions into the quadratic relations to produce a quadratic system in one variable, which is guaranteed to have a solution. However, instead of this quadratic system specifying a finite number of solutions for this fixed variable we observe that these quadratic relations become the constant zero – again indicating that linear equations suffice and the space of decompositions is one-dimensional. An example is included in our attached code. This merits further consideration and we leave it open for future work.

4.3
Higher order tensors

Our analysis is not restricted to order-
4
tensors. Indeed, under Condition
4.1
we still can obtain a significant number of linear equations. However, counting the number of equations and variables remains difficult unless we impose something similar to Condition
4.5
, which for larger
d
becomes unrealistic and too strong.

If we do choose to proceed with a similar condition, counting equations and variables becomes possible. We expect for
r=O(n^{d/2})
the matrix
\mathbf{A}
will be tall, and similar to our conjecture we also expect this matrix to be generically full column rank. This condition is one on the expected growth of the Hilbert function
h_{\varphi}
, i.e., ranks of catalecticant.

On the other hand, in our supplementary code we give an explicit example of an order-
6
tensor that does not achieve the expected growth of the Hilbert function yet decomposition still reduces to a linear system. This certainly motivates future study.

5
Monomials

In this section we discuss applying Algorithm
2
on symmetric tensors that are
monomials
. It holds that monomials given in a canonical form satisfy Assumption
1
, which allows us to determine algorithmically the rank of monomials and provide a parameterization of the space of decompositions efficiently.

Condition 5.1
.

\varphi
is a monomial, i.e.,
\varphi_{x_{0}^{d_{0}}\dots x_{n}^{d_{n}}}=1
and all other entries are zero, with
d_{0}\leq d_{1}\leq\dots\leq d_{n}
.
\varphi
is an order-
d:=\sum_{j=0}^{n}d_{j}
symmetric tensor.

The following is known:

Proposition 5.2

(
[
12
]
, Corollary 19)
.

For
\varphi
satisfying Condition
5.1
, if
\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{r}
gives a minimal rank decomposition of
\varphi
then
\mathbf{z}_{i,0}\neq 0
for all
i
.

Proposition
5.2
ensures that Assumption
1
holds for decompositions of monomials. Form the Hankel matrix
\mathcal{H}
corresponding to
\varphi
, dehomogenizing with respect to
x_{0}
; the first step is to find a monomial set
B
such that
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
is invertible. Consider

B:=\{x^{\alpha}\mid 0\leq\alpha_{i}\leq d_{i},1\leq i\leq n\}.

(13)

Then
|B|=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(d_{j}+1)
and

\mathcal{H}_{B,B}=\begin{bmatrix}0&&&&1\\
&&&1&\\
&&\iddots&&\\
&1&&&\\
1&&&&*\end{bmatrix},

that is, the antidiagonal of
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
is all ones and above it lies all zeros. To see this, write
\overline{d}:=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
. An antidiagonal element of
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
is precisely indexed by
\left(x^{\alpha},x^{\overline{d}-\alpha}\right)
and by Condition
5.1
,
\varphi_{x^{\alpha}x^{\overline{d}-\alpha}}=\varphi_{x^{\overline{d}}}=1
. Above this antidiagonal are indices of degree less than
d
, so must be zero. Below the antidiagonal there can be moment variables in some of the entries.

Using Proposition
5.2
and the above observation, we can prove a lower bound on the rank of
\varphi
.

Proposition 5.3
.

\operatorname{rank}(\varphi)\geq\prod_{j=0}^{n}(d_{j}+1)
.

Proof.

Proposition
5.2
and Theorem
3.7
show that all decompositions of
\varphi
can be realized via extensions using the Hankel matrix
\mathcal{H}
. Therefore, numerical ranks of submatrices of
\mathcal{H}
lower bound the rank of
\varphi
. Given

B=\{x^{\alpha}\mid 0\leq\alpha_{i}\leq d_{i},1\leq i\leq n\}

and realizing
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
is lower antidiagonal with
1
’s on the antidiagonal, we have
\operatorname{rank}(\varphi)\geq\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal{H}_{B,B})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(d_{j}+1)
regardless of what happens below the antidiagonal.
∎

Henceforth, fix the
B
given in Equation
13
. As a brief road map to our discussion on monomials, the next two subsections we describe the moment variables and determinantal equations and reveal a certain grading in the equations. We then show how certain moment variables act as free parameters, reducing solving the determinantal equations to
polynomial evaluation
, implying an efficient algorithm for decomposition of monomials.

5.1
Variables

We describe the variables present in the determinantal relations.

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=\{x^{\alpha}x^{\alpha^{\prime}}x_{i}\mid x^{\alpha},x^{\alpha^{\prime}}\in B,1\leq i\leq n\},

(14)

\displaystyle Y_{k}

\displaystyle:=\{x^{\gamma}\in Y\mid(k-1)(d_{0}+1)+1\leq|\gamma|-d\leq k(d_{0}+1)\}.

Y_{k}
stratifies
Y
. Indeed let
m
be the smallest integer such that
|\gamma|-d\leq m(d_{0}+1)
for all
x^{\gamma}\in Y
. Then
Y=\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}Y_{k}
. Furthermore, define

Y_{k}^{\ell}:=\{x^{\gamma}\in Y_{k}\mid\exists T\subseteq[n],|T|=\ell\text{ s.t. }\forall i\in T,\gamma_{i}>d_{i},\forall j\notin T,\gamma_{j}\leq d_{j}\}.

(15)

In other words,
Y_{k}^{\ell}
is the set of
x^{\gamma}\in Y_{k}
such that
\gamma_{i}>d_{i}
on exactly
\ell
coordinates
i
. A useful fact is that if
x^{\gamma}\in Y
then
\gamma_{i}
can be equal to
2d_{i}+1
on at most one coordinate
i
.

5.2
Equations

We now describe the determinantal relations. Recall that equations are of the form

\operatorname{det}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{i}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{j}\}}\right)-\operatorname{det}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{j}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{i}\}}\right)=0

for
x^{\alpha},x^{\beta}\in B,i\neq j
. We abuse some notation from the previous section and define

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{(x^{\alpha}x_{i},x^{\beta}x_{j})\mid x^{\alpha}\in B,x^{\beta}\in B,i\neq j,x^{\alpha}x_{i}\notin B\wedge x^{\beta}x_{j}\notin B,x^{\alpha}x_{j}\in B\vee x^{\beta}x_{i}\in B\},

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle:=\{((x^{\alpha}x_{i},x^{\beta}x_{j}),(x^{\alpha}x_{j},x^{\beta}x_{i}))\mid x^{\alpha}\in B,x^{\beta}\in B,i\neq j,x^{\alpha}x_{i},x^{\alpha}x_{j},x^{\beta}x_{i},x^{\beta}x_{j}\notin B\},

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle:=E_{1}\cup E_{2}.

As before, we consider
E_{1},E_{2}
and
E
as sets of equations defined by the given indices. However, in contrast with Section
4
, these equations are not necessarily linear.

For
\alpha,\beta,i,j
such that the corresponding equation is in
E_{2}
we need
x^{\alpha}\in B,x^{\beta}\in B,i\neq j,x^{\alpha}x_{i},x^{\alpha}x_{j},x^{\beta}x_{i},x^{\beta}x_{j}\notin B
. In particular, this means that
\alpha_{i}=d_{i},\alpha_{j}=d_{j},\beta_{i}=d_{i},\beta_{j}=d_{j}
and so,
x^{\alpha+\beta}x_{i}x_{j}\notin Y
. By contrapositive every
x^{\gamma}\in Y
can be realized as
x^{\gamma}=x^{\alpha+\beta}x_{i}x_{j}
with
(x^{\alpha}x_{i},x^{\beta}x_{j})\in E_{1}
. Henceforth it suffices to focus on
E_{1}
.

It will be useful to define the sets
S_{k}\subseteq B\times B
for
k\geq 0
:

S_{k}:=\{(x^{\gamma},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in B\times B\mid k(d_{0}+1)\leq|\gamma^{\prime}|-|\gamma|\leq(k+1)(d_{0}+1)-1\}.

(16)

Implicitly we are assuming
|\gamma^{\prime}|\geq|\gamma|
. We can show that
(x^{\gamma},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{k}\implies x^{\overline{d}-\gamma+\gamma^{\prime}}\in Y_{k}
:

\displaystyle|\overline{d}-\gamma+\gamma^{\prime}|

\displaystyle=|\gamma^{\prime}|+|\overline{d}-\gamma|

\displaystyle=|\gamma^{\prime}|+d-d_{0}-|\gamma|,

the second equality being justified since
x^{\gamma}
divides
x^{\overline{d}}
. Then

\displaystyle|\gamma^{\prime}|+d-d_{0}-|\gamma|

\displaystyle\leq d-d_{0}+(k+1)(d_{0}+1)-1

\displaystyle=d+k(d_{0}+1).

The lower bound in Equation
16
holds similarly. Technically we need to check that
x^{\overline{d}-\gamma+\gamma^{\prime}}\in Y
but this is clear.

The next two lemmas we present give recursive expressions for
\mathcal{G}:=\mathcal{H}_{B,B}^{-1}
and the determinantal equation
\operatorname{det}\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{i}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{j}\}}
. In particular, we show that these expressions possess a certain graded structure. The proofs are in Appendix
B
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
(x^{\gamma},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{k}
. Then

\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}}}=-y_{x^{\overline{d}-\gamma+\gamma^{\prime}}}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}\sum_{(x^{\delta},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{\ell}}\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\delta}}y_{x^{\overline{d}-\delta+\gamma^{\prime}}}.

In particular,
\operatorname{deg}\left(\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}}}\right)=k
and the only moment variable in
Y_{k}
is
y_{x^{\overline{d}-\gamma+\gamma^{\prime}}}
; all other moment variables present are in
\bigcup{\ell<k}Y_{\ell}
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
x^{\alpha+\beta}x_{i}x_{j}\in Y_{k}
, with
i\neq j
. Then
\operatorname{det}\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{i}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{j}\}}
equals

\displaystyle-y_{x^{\alpha+\beta}x_{i}x_{j}}+\sum_{d-|\alpha|\leq|\gamma|\leq|\beta|-d_{0}}y_{x^{\alpha+\gamma}x_{i}}y_{x^{\overline{d}-\gamma+\beta}x_{j}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\gamma|\geq d-|\alpha|,\\
|\gamma^{\prime}|\leq|\beta|-d_{0},\\
(x^{\gamma},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{\ell}\end{subarray}}y_{x^{\alpha+\gamma}x_{i}}\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}}}y_{x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}+\beta}x_{j}}.

In particular,
\operatorname{deg}\left(\operatorname{det}\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{i}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{j}\}}\right)=k
and the only moment variable in equation
Y_{k}
is
y_{x^{\alpha+\beta}x_{i}x_{j}}
; all other variables are in
\bigcup{\ell<k}Y_{\ell}
.

Importantly, note that there may be distinct equations corresponding to different
\alpha,\beta,i,j
and
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}
even though
x^{\alpha+\beta}x_{i}x_{j}=x^{\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}}x_{i^{\prime}}x_{j^{\prime}}
.

Example 5.6
.

Let
\varphi=x_{0}x_{1}x_{2}^{2}
. Then

B=\{1,x_{1},x_{2},x_{1}x_{2},x_{2}^{2},x_{1}x_{2}^{2}\}.

The variables are

Y=\{y_{x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{2}},y_{x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{3}},y_{x_{1}x_{2}^{4}},y_{x_{2}^{5}},y_{x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{3}},y_{x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{4}},y_{x_{1}x_{2}^{5}},y_{x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{4}},y_{x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{5}}\}

and
E_{1}

\displaystyle y_{x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{3}}=0,y_{x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{3}}=0,y_{x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{4}}=0,

\displaystyle y_{x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{4}}-y_{x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{2}}y_{x_{1}x_{2}^{4}}-y_{x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{3}}^{2}=0,

\displaystyle y_{x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{5}}-y_{x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{2}}y_{x_{2}^{5}}-y_{x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{3}}y_{x_{1}x_{2}^{4}}=0.

E_{2}
is empty. If we substitute we end up with the two equations

y_{x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{4}}=y_{x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{2}}y_{x_{1}x_{2}^{4}},\quad y_{x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{5}}=y_{x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{2}}y_{x_{2}^{5}}.

As a lead-in to the next section, we see that we can treat
y_{x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{2}},y_{x_{1}x_{2}^{4}},y_{x_{2}^{5}},y_{x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{5}}
as free parameters.

5.3
Parameters

Now that we have described the variables and equations in the determinantal equations, and have shown that these equations possess a graded structure. We draw attention to a particular subset of variables that we claim serve as “parameters for extension.”

Definition 5.7
.

Define the set

Y_{P}:=\bigcup_{k}Y_{k}^{1}

to be the parameters for extension.

The important characteristic of the set
Y_{P}
is that variables in this set do not appear as a linear term in any equation. To be precise, consider equations in
E_{1}
, we require
i\neq j
, and for
x^{\alpha}x_{i},x^{\beta}x_{j}\notin B
we need
\alpha_{i}=d_{i},\beta_{j}=d_{j}
. But then
x^{\gamma}=x^{\alpha+\beta}x_{i}x_{j}
is such that
\gamma_{i}\geq d_{i},\gamma_{j}\geq d_{j}
, so
x^{\gamma}\notin Y_{P}
. On the other hand, for
x^{\gamma}\in Y\setminus Y_{P}
we can always find
\alpha,\beta,i\neq j
such that
x^{\gamma}=x^{\alpha+\beta}x_{i}x_{j}
with corresponding equation in
E_{1}
.

Proposition 5.8
.

In the determinantal relations the value of a variable
x^{\gamma}\in Y_{k}\setminus Y_{k}^{1}
is determined by the values of
\cup_{\ell<k}Y_{\ell}^{1}
.

Proof.

This is a straightforward induction on
k
. We know from Lemma
5.5
that a variable
x^{\gamma}
with
\gamma\in Y_{k}
is determined by variables in
Y_{\ell},\ell<k
as it appears linearly in the corresponding equations (which may be distinct), whereas there are no other variables outside
\cup_{\ell=1}^{k-1}Y_{\ell}^{1}
except for
x^{\gamma}
in these equations. But a variable
x^{\delta}\in Y_{\ell}
with
\ell<k
is either in
Y_{\ell}^{1}
or is determined by
\cup_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{\ell-1}Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{1}
by induction. We simply need to prove the base case
Y_{1}\setminus Y_{1}^{1}
. From Equation
14
, if
x^{\gamma}\in Y_{1}
then
|\gamma|\leq d+d_{0}+1
. If there exists
\alpha,\beta,i\neq j

Remark 5.14
.

The discussion in this section crucially relies on
\varphi
being given in a canonical form. However, in a different basis the resulting tensor may not be easily decomposed. Determining if a tensor is a monomial is decidable and can be determined using Gröbner bases and symbolic algebraic methods, however, these methods are a priori not efficient. This begs the question: suppose an arbitrary tensor is given; is there an efficient method that can distinguish if the tensor is a monomial or not, and if it is a monomial, provide the change of basis to convert it to its canonical form? If so, then Condition
5.1
can be relaxed and our discussion applies to a monomial in any presentation.

Discussion and Future Work

We refined and performed theoretical analysis of the tensor decomposition algorithm of
[
11
]
, connecting its complexity to the regularity of a target decomposition. We showed that for even-order tensors with decompositions of low enough regularity, moment matrix extension and therefore tensor decomposition can reduce to solving a linear system. For order-
4
tensors we proved that this linear system suffices for decomposition of generic tensors of rank at most
2n+1
, implying that the extension algorithm is efficient and gives an effective criterion for identifiability of specific tensors. We formulated a conjecture that the linear system suffices for rank up to
O(n^{2})
, with improved leading coefficient compared to existing literature, and gave a computer-assisted proof verifying the correctness of the conjecture up to
n=17
. We also demonstrated that certain classes of nonidentifiable tensors, including monomials, can be decomposed efficiently. To our knowledge, handling nonidentifiability is a first in work on efficient tensor decomposition. In these cases, we algorithmically reproved existing theory as well.

There are various opportunities for future work. Of course, Conjecture
1
remains open. Relatedly, we could further understand the algebra of the vanishing of the maximal minors of the coefficient matrix
\mathbf{A}
and implications for identifiability.
More broadly, we could ask how far we can push the rank bounds for efficient decomposition of generic tensors; in terms of our meta-algorithm, this can include using both new core algorithms (e.g., new normal form methods) and new extension algorithms. One idea for the extension algorithms is to use equations arising from Young flattenings (rather than the Hankel matrix)
as defining equations.
One could use
[
51
]
, which develops a core algorithm based on these flattenings.

\mathbf{z}_{3}

Proof of Lemma
5.4
.

As
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
is symmetric lower antitriangular,
\mathcal{G}
is symmetric upper antitriangular. By definition, for
x^{\gamma},x^{\gamma^{\prime}}\in B
,
\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma}}^{\top}\mathcal{H}_{B,x^{\gamma^{\prime}}}=1
if
\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}
and
0
otherwise.

Explicitly, we have

\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma}}^{\top}\mathcal{H}_{B,x^{\gamma^{\prime}}}=\sum_{\delta}\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\delta}}\mathcal{H}_{x^{\overline{d}-\delta},x^{\gamma^{\prime}}}.

\mathcal{H}_{\overline{d}-\delta,\gamma^{\prime}}
is nonzero when either
\delta=\gamma^{\prime}
, so that the corresponding entry is
1
, or
(x^{\delta},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{k}
for
k\geq 1
, in which case the corresponding entry is
y_{x^{\overline{d}-\delta+\gamma^{\prime}}}
(note that it suffices to consider only those
\delta
such that
|\delta|\leq|\gamma^{\prime}|
; for
|\delta|>|\gamma|
the size of
\overline{d}-\delta+\gamma^{\prime}
is too small so the entry of
\mathcal{H}_{B,B}
is zero). Then

\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma}}^{\top}\mathcal{H}_{B,x^{\gamma^{\prime}}}=\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}}}+\sum_{(x^{\delta},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{k},k\geq 1}\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\delta}}\mathcal{H}_{x^{\overline{d}-\delta},x^{\gamma^{\prime}}}\\
=\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}}}+\sum_{(x^{\delta},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{k},k\geq 1}\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\delta}}y_{x^{\overline{d}-\delta+\gamma^{\prime}}}.

In particular, for
\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}

\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\gamma}}=1-\sum_{(x^{\delta},x^{\gamma})\in S_{k},k\geq 1}\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\delta}}y_{x^{\overline{d}-\delta+\gamma}}

and when
\gamma\neq\gamma^{\prime}

\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}}}=-\sum_{(x^{\delta},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{k},k\geq 1}\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\delta}}y_{x^{\overline{d}-\delta+\gamma^{\prime}}}.

First, note that for
|\gamma|>|\gamma^{\prime}|

\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}}}=0
; this can be seen easily from upper antitriangularity of
\mathcal{G}
. Assuming now
|\gamma|\leq|\gamma^{\prime}|
we compute
\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}}}
for
(x^{\gamma},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{k}
for increasing
S_{k}
. Let
k=0
; then
0\leq|\gamma^{\prime}|-|\gamma|\leq d_{0}
. But the summation is vacuous: suppose there exists
\delta
such that
(x^{\delta},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{k},k\geq 1
. For
|\gamma|>|\delta|
we know
\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\delta}}=0
, so we can also assume
|\gamma|\leq|\delta|
. But then we have
|\gamma^{\prime}|-|\delta|\geq d_{0}+1
and
-\gamma\geq-\delta
, so
|\gamma^{\prime}|-|\gamma|\geq d_{0}+1
, a contradiction. Therefore, for
(x^{\gamma},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{0}
,
\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}}}=1
is
\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}
and is
0
otherwise.

Now suppose
(x^{\gamma},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{k}
for some
k\geq 1
. In this case the entry is always

\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}}}=-\sum_{(x^{\delta},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{k},k\geq 1}\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\delta}}y_{x^{\overline{d}-\delta+\gamma^{\prime}}}.

In fact, we can always upper bound
k
in the summation. Suppose
(x^{\delta},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{\ell}
for some
\ell>k
. Then by the same argument as before, as
|\gamma|\leq|\delta|
we have

|\gamma^{\prime}|-|\gamma|\geq|\gamma^{\prime}|-|\delta|\geq\ell(d_{0}+1),

a contradiction. On the other hand, for
\ell\leq k
there will always exist some
(x^{\delta},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{\ell}
(e.g., for
\ell=k
just take
\delta=\gamma
). Thus, we can write

\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}}}=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{(x^{\delta},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{\ell}}\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\delta}}y_{x^{\overline{d}-\delta+\gamma^{\prime}}}.

Let us be more precise. We can see that if
(x^{\delta},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{\ell}
, that we immediately have the upper bound
|\delta|-|\gamma|\leq(k-\ell+1)(d_{0}+1)-1
, i.e., if
(x^{\delta},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{\ell}
and
(x^{\gamma},x^{\delta})\in S_{\ell^{\prime}}
, then
\ell^{\prime}\leq d-\ell
. In particular, if
(x^{\delta},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{k}
then
(x^{\gamma},\delta)\in S_{0}
; as we know that for
(\gamma,x^{\delta})\in S_{0}
,
\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\delta}}\neq 0
if and only if
\gamma=\delta
, we finally write

\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}}}=-y_{x^{\overline{d}-\gamma+\gamma^{\prime}}}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}\sum_{(x^{\delta},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{\ell}}\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\delta}}y_{x^{\overline{d}-\delta+\gamma^{\prime}}}.

It is easy to induct via this expression and see that the degree of
\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}}}
is
k
and the only moment variable in
Y_{k}
is
y_{x^{\overline{d}-\gamma+\gamma^{\prime}}}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.5
.

We can see that

\operatorname{det}\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{i}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{j}\}}=-y_{x^{\alpha+\beta}x_{i}x_{j}}+\sum_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}_{x^{\alpha}x_{i},x^{\gamma}}\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}}}\mathcal{H}_{x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}},x^{\beta}x_{j}}.

Then the expression simply comes from the fact that
\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\gamma}}=1
.

By induction we see that for fixed
\ell
,

\operatorname{deg}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\gamma|\geq d-|\alpha|,\\
|\gamma^{\prime}|\leq|\beta|-d_{0},\\
(x^{\gamma},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{\ell}\end{subarray}}y_{x^{\alpha+\gamma}x_{i}}\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}}}y_{x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}+\beta}x_{j}}\right)=\ell+2,

so that if
x^{\alpha+\beta}x_{i}x_{j}\in Y_{k}
then
\operatorname{deg}\left(\mathcal{H}_{B\cup\{x^{\alpha}x_{i}\},B\cup\{x^{\beta}x_{j}\}}\right)=k
with degree
\ell
part graded in the above manner. For some fixed
\ell
we calculate the sizes of the variables present. First, from
(x^{\gamma},x^{\gamma^{\prime}})\in S_{\ell}
we can see
|\gamma|\leq|\gamma^{\prime}|-\ell(d_{0}+1)
. Then

\displaystyle|\alpha|+|\gamma|+1

\displaystyle\leq|\alpha|+|\gamma^{\prime}|-\ell(d_{0}+1)+1

\displaystyle\leq|\alpha|+|\beta|+2-(\ell+1)(d_{0}+1)

\displaystyle\leq d+(k-(\ell+1))(d_{0}+1).

Thus, if
x^{\alpha+\gamma}x_{i}\in Y_{\ell^{\prime}}
then we must have
\ell^{\prime}\leq k-(\ell+1)
. The same idea works for
x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}+\beta}x_{j}
. We also now that the maximum size of any variable that appears in
\mathcal{G}_{x^{\gamma},x^{\overline{d}-\gamma^{\prime}}}
is in
Y_{\ell}
. Therefore, we have again that the only variable in
Y_{k}
in the equation is
x^{\alpha+\beta}x_{i}x_{j}
.
∎
