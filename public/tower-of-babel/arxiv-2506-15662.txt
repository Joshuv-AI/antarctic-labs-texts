CC-Learn : Cohort-based Consistency Learning
Xiao Ye Shaswat Shrivastava Zhaonan Li Jacob Dineen

Abstract
Large language models excel at many tasks but still struggle with consistent, robust reasoning. We introduce Cohort-based Consistency Learning ( CC-Learn ), a reinforcement learning framework that improves the reliability of LLM reasoning by training on cohorts of similar questions derived from shared programmatic abstractions. To enforce cohort-level consistency, we define a composite objective-combining cohort accuracy, a retrieval bonus for effective problem decomposition, and a rejection penalty for trivial or invalid lookups—that reinforcement learning can directly optimize, unlike supervised fine-tuning. Optimizing this reward guides the model to adopt uniform reasoning patterns across all cohort members. Experiments on challenging reasoning benchmarks (including ARC-Challenge and StrategyQA) show that CC-Learn boosts both accuracy and reasoning stability over pretrained and SFT baselines. These results demonstrate that cohort-level RL effectively enhances reasoning consistency in LLMs.

Large language models (LLMs) have made remarkable progress in complex reasoning tasks through strategies like prompting and step-by-step solution traces. Techniques such as
chain-of-thought
prompting
(
wei2022cot
)
enable models to decompose problems into intermediate steps, significantly improving performance on arithmetic, commonsense, and various reasoning challenges. Similarly, decoding strategies like self-consistency
(
wang2023selfconsistency
)
enhance accuracy by sampling multiple reasoning paths and selecting the most consistent answer across benchmarks. Despite these advances, LLMs frequently exhibit
inconsistency
: a model may correctly answer a question in one formulation but fail on a paraphrase or logically equivalent variant
(
yu2024reasonagainusingextractablesymbolic
;
zhou2024conceptualunbiasedreasoninglanguage
;
li2024deceptivesemanticshortcutsreasoning
)
. This behavior suggests brittle reasoning processes and undermines reliability in practical applications
(
mccoy2019right
;
geirhos2020shortcut
)
.

Figure
1

(
yang2018hotpotqa
)
. The original question asks:

“Are
Control Room
and
Meeting Resistance
both documentary films that involve Iraq?”

An LLM can follow a correct chain of thought for the original—but when presented with a similar variant,

“Are
Super Size Me
and
Amy
both documentary films that involve America?”

it answers incorrectly by relying on an association between “Amy” and “UK” rather than applying the same logical checks
(
sinha2021surface
;
zhao2021calibrate
)
. This gap between high single-question accuracy and poor generalization across variants highlights the need for training methods that enforce consistency.

To address this gap, we propose
CC-Learn
, which trains LLMs on
cohorts
of similar questions expressed as executable programs, and optimizes a
cohort-level
reward via reinforcement learning. First, each question is transformed into a masked template exposing its core reasoning structure (e.g.,
“Are
Film1
and
Film2
both
DocumentaryType
films that involve
Country
?”). From this template we automatically generate a cohort of factual variants: by requiring a single program to succeed on most or all variants during rl training, we eliminate cases where an incorrect reasoning path nonetheless produces the right answer by accident, directly enforcing true consistency. We then prompt the model to emit a compact program that issues only simple, atomic
retrieve
calls for each substep and uses a rejection filter to block any multi-step or invalid queries. This design uses the same simple
retrieve
calls for all cohort members, so the program runs unchanged on each variant; any change in output comes only from different facts, not from different reasoning steps. Finally, we apply Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
shao2024deepseekmath
)
to maximize a composite reward—balancing cohort-level accuracy, retrieval-efficiency bonuses, and penalties for rejected lookups—across the entire cohort. This pipeline compels the model to learn uniform, verifiable reasoning procedures rather than exploiting shortcuts on individual instances.

In experiments on five challenging reasoning benchmarks—ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge
(
clark2018arc
)
, StrategyQA
(
geva2021strategyqa
)
, HotpotQA, and CommonsenseQA
(

Ablations confirm the importance of similar-question training, and human evaluation shows a 47% win rate for our model’s reasoning paths, demonstrating that cohort-based RL with structured retrieval effectively enhances both accuracy and robustness across diverse reasoning tasks.

Related Work

Reasoning Consistency

Large language models often exhibit inconsistent reasoning when faced with paraphrased or logically equivalent inputs. For example, prompts with similar surface familiarity but different underlying complexity yield divergent performance
(
Li2024FamiCom
)
, and models may exploit spurious semantic cues rather than following intended chains of reasoning
(
li2024deceptivesemanticshortcutsreasoning
)
. To mitigate this, researchers have added training regularizers or auxiliary losses for paraphrase-invariance
(
elazar2021consistency
)
, leveraged knowledge graphs to generate paired questions for fine-tuning
(
Rajan2024KonTest
)
, and applied self-consistency decoding to vote out illogical paths
(
wang2023selfconsistency
)
. Despite these efforts—and evidence that larger models do not automatically improve in logical consistency
(
jang2022becel
)
—maintaining reasoning invariance across similar questions remains an open challenge. Our work enforces consistent, programmatic reasoning across groups of similar questions that share the same reasoning path.

Programmatic Abstractions for Reasoning

Programmatic or symbolic abstractions introduce a formal structure that can be executed and verified, improving transparency and reliability.
(
zhou2024conceptualunbiasedreasoninglanguage
;
zhou2025selfsupervisedanalogicallearningusing
)
translate questions into symbolic programs for verifiable reasoning, while
(
hong2024abstractionofthoughtmakeslanguagemodels
)
requires an Abstraction-of-Thought plan before refining a concrete solution. These approaches show that enforcing structured intermediate representations reduces reliance on brittle shortcuts and enhances problem-solving robustness.

Reinforcement Learning for Enhanced Reasoning.

Reinforcement learning (RL) objectives can substantially boost an LLM’s ability to solve multi-step problems by optimizing the reasoning path.
Verifiable intermediate rewards catch and correct logical mistakes, leading to more stable reasoning
(
xu2025dro
)
.
Composite reward functions that blend answer accuracy, factuality, and faithfulness yield more dependable outputs
(
wang2024f2rl
)
.
Potential-based shaping adds domain priors as soft constraints, speeding up training while keeping policies optimal
(
nguyen2020shaping
)
.
Together, these advances in RL-driven reasoning path optimization, verifiable reward design, and structured shapinginspire our cohort-level consistency framework.

Method

In this section, we introduce our framework for training language models to execute transparent reasoning through structured, executable programs. Our approach leverages a similar question generation strategy, rejection prompting, and a tailored RL scheme. This combination is designed to penalize shortcuts and reward consistent performance across similar questions, thereby compelling models to develop generalizable reasoning procedures rather than relying on superficial pattern-matching heuristics.

3.1
Program Generation

We represent each reasoning path as a small Python function of the form:

def answer(param1: Type1, …, paramK: TypeK) -> int:
# sequence of retrieve(...) calls and control flow

Only atomic
retrieve(question: str, type)
calls and control flows are allowed. The model is given four inputs:

•

Masked question
: the template preserving logical structure (e.g.,
“Are
Film1
and
Film2
both
DocumentaryType
films that involve
Country
?”).

•

Parameters
: the placeholder names (e.g.,
Film1
,
Country
).

•

Options
: the answer choices (e.g.,
[’No’,’Yes’]
).

•

Function header
: the exact
def answer(...) -> int
signature.

We guide the model’s output format with four few-shot examples—covering boolean checks, numeric comparisons, list-based loops, and dependent lookups—selected to span our cohort templates (see Appendix
A.5
). During generation, the policy model fills in the function body; at execution time, each
retrieve
call is issued to the retriever (with rejection filtering) to complete the lookup.

3.2
Data Generation and Preprocessing

To foster generalizable reasoning and provide a strong foundation for our models, we employ a high-quality data preparation pipeline.

Similar Questions Generation

Central to our approach is the construction of cohorts of similar questions that share same reasoning paths but differ in factual content. This process begins with a corpus of 5,000 original questions from five domains (1,000 questions per domain). As
Figure 1
shows, for each original question, we generate an abstraction by creating an abstraction template that preserves its core reasoning structure while parameterizing entities, allowing for the substitution of different facts(see Appendix
A.2
). Using LLaMA-3.3-70B-Instruct
(
grattafiori2024llama3herdmodels
)
, we then instantiate 5 similar questions for each abstraction(see Appendix
A.3
). These variants are solvable through the same reasoning path dictated by the abstraction template but feature different specific entities and details. To ensure the fidelity of our training data, answers to all generated similar questions are cross-validated using three state-of-the-art models: LLaMA-3.3-70B-Instruct,
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B
(
deepseekai2025deepseekr1incentivizingreasoningcapability
)
, and Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct
(
qwen2
;
qwen2.5
)
. This process is supplemented by human verification of both the validity of similar questions and the correctness of their labels, as detailed in
4.7
.

Program Corpus for Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT).

To establish a robust baseline, we create a high-quality corpus of 500 exemplar programs (100 per domain), generated using LLaMA-3.3-70B-Instruct prompted with few-shot examples. Each program in this corpus is rigorously verified to ensure a 0% rejection rate and 100% execution accuracy on its corresponding question.

3.3
Cohort-based Reinforcement Learning

We train the policy model using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) on cohorts of similar questions. Based on abstractions, the policy model generates an executable program, runs it on all variants (with a retriever handling simple lookups and rejecting complex queries), and receives a single, composite cohort-level reward. This reward drives accuracy across the cohort, efficient retrieval usage, and discourages invalid lookups, guiding the model toward consistent, verifiable reasoning.

Model Architecture

Our framework employs two complementary language models:
(a) Policy Model:
We utilize Qwen-2.5-7B-Coder-Instruct
(
qwen2
;
qwen2.5
;
hui2024qwen2
)
as our primary model. It is tasked with generating structured, executable programs in response to the abstracted questions. This model is responsible for formulating the reasoning path.
(b) Retriever Model:
Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct serves as our retriever model. It executes the simple retrieval calls generated within the programs by the policy model, specifically during the reward computation phase of GRPO. This architectural separation ensures that the policy model must formulate its reasoning strategy without direct access to factual information during program generation.

Rejection Prompts in Retrieval

To encourage the policy model to learn robust, generalizable reasoning and prevent the policy model from circumventing the intended reasoning process by issuing trivial or multi-step queries—behavior akin to the “deceptive shortcuts” observed in prior work
(
li2024deceptivesemanticshortcutsreasoning
)
—we equip the retriever model with a rejection-prompt filter. With a few-shot prompt (see Appendix
A.1
), the retriever only accepts straightforward, single-step factual questions (e.g., Is {Film1} a {DocumentaryType} film?) and replies with “idk” to any multi-step or invalid queries. Any rejected call will incur the penalty
R_{\mathrm{rej}}
, thereby incentivizing the policy model to offload only elementary lookups and to internalize the full reasoning chain within the generated program.

Interactive Training Pipeline.

The GRPO training loop proceeds interactively. First, the policy model proposes a program in response to an abstracted question. Second, this program is executed on both the original question and its five similar variants. Any information retrieval calls embedded in the program are handled by the retriever model. Finally, based on the execution outcomes across the entire question family, a scalar reward is computed. This reward considers accuracy, retrieval efficiency, and the rejection ratios.

Reward Components.

Our composite reward function
R
is designed to guide the model towards effective and generalizable reasoning. It is defined as
R=R_{\text{acc}}+R_{\text{ret}}+R_{\text{rej}}
, where the components are:

•

Accuracy Reward (
R_{\text{acc}}
):
This reward is calculated as
R_{\text{acc}}=0.2\cdot n_{\text{correct}}
, where
n_{\text{correct}}\in\{0,1,\dots,6\}
is the number of correctly answered questions within the cohort of similar questions (1 original + 5 similar). This yields
R_{\text{acc}}\in[0,1.2]
.

•

Retrieve-Function Usage Reward (
R_{\text{ret}}
):
This component encourages appropriate problem decomposition and is assigned based on the number of retrieval calls (
n_{\text{calls}}
) made by the program:

R_{\text{ret}}=\begin{cases}-0.6&\text{if }n_{\text{calls}}=0\\
\phantom{-}0&\text{if }n_{\text{calls}}=1\\
+0.6&\text{if }n_{\text{calls}}>1\end{cases}

This results in
R_{\text{ret}}\in\{-0.6,0,0.6\}
. The retrieve-function usage reward encourages problem decomposition and discourages trivial solutions with no retrieval calls.

•

Rejection Penalty (
R_{\text{rej}}
):
This penalty discourages ineffective retrieval calls and is given by
R_{\text{rej}}=-0.1\cdot n_{\text{rejected}}
, where
n_{\text{rejected}}\in\{0,1,\dots,6\}
is the number of questions in the group whose retrieve call is rejected by the retrieve model. This leads to
R_{\text{rej}}\in[-0.6,0]
. The Rejection Penalty penalizes attempts to re-ask the original question or formulate overly similar questions, forcing multi-step, valid reasoning.

The retrieve function reward is crucial for encouraging the model to break down complex problems into simpler, verifiable steps via appropriate information retrieval calls, rather than attempting to solve them monolithically or exploit loopholes (e.g., generating an empty program). The rejection mechanism explicitly blocks attempts to shortcut the reasoning process, such as re-asking the input question, thereby forcing genuine problem decomposition.

RL Variants

We explore two variants of our RL approach to assess different aspects of learning:

(a) Cohort Accuracy:
Accuracy-based rewards (
R_{\text{acc}}
) are granted only if the generated program successfully answers at least 4 out of the 6 questions in one group. This enforces a higher standard of generalizability.
(b) Normal Accuracy:
Accuracy rewards (
R_{\text{acc}}
) are granted for every successful program execution on a question within the group, regardless of performance on other questions in that group. This provides a more granular learning signal.

3.4
Cohort Execution Test

We evaluated each generated program on its cohort of six questions (the original plus five variants), and consistency measured the results under two criteria:

•

Strict Accuracy:
A program is considered correct only if it successfully answers at least
5
out of the 6 questions in the group.

•

Lenient Accuracy:
A program is considered correct if it successfully answers at least
4
out of the 6 questions in the group.

4
Experiments

In this section, we present comprehensive experimental details and results demonstrating the efficacy of the RL framework for training LLMs to perform transparent reasoning through structured, executable programs.

4.1
Baselines

We compare our Cohort-based Reinforcement Learning models against two baseline configurations to demonstrate the efficacy of our approach:
(a) Vanilla Model:
The off-the-shelf Qwen-2.5-Coder-Instruct checkpoint, used without any additional supervised fine-tuning or RL, serves as a strong pretrained LLM reference.

(b) Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):
The Qwen-2.5-Coder-Instruct model further trained on our curated corpus of 500 high-quality exemplar programs (see
subsection 3.2
) until the held-out evaluation loss stabilized—typically around epoch 1.1—representing conventional program-level supervision.

4.2
Hyperparameters for RL

We train the RL model using the VERL framework
(
Sheng_2025
)
with a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-6}
, a KL-divergence loss coefficient of 0.001, 5 rollouts per update, and a training batch size of 128. The temperature for the rollouts is 1.

4.3
Experimental Setup

Datasets and Test Set

We evaluate on five publicly available benchmarks: ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, CSQA, StrategyQA, and HotpotQA. Our test set comprises 2,500 questions in total (500 per dataset), each paired with five similar questions generated by our abstraction pipeline.

Evaluation Protocol

For each of the 2,500 test questions, we sample the policy model 11 times to assess self-consistency, execute each resulting program on its six-question cohort, and then compute both strict and lenient accuracy as defined in
subsection 3.4
. This protocol enables us to report not only standard accuracy but also the critical generalization-across-variants metrics.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a novel training framework that substantially improves the consistency and reliability of language model reasoning. By leveraging cohorts of similar questions that share the same reasoning paths but differ in surface details, our approach explicitly teaches models to develop generalizable reasoning strategies rather than superficial shortcuts or brittle heuristics.

Our key innovation lies in the combination of executable program-based reasoning with a tailored RL scheme that rewards consistent performance across question variants. This approach addresses a fundamental challenge in LLM reasoning: the tendency to answer nearly identical questions inconsistently, which undermines reliability in real-world settings.

Experimental results across five diverse reasoning datasets demonstrate that our method significantly outperforms both vanilla and supervised fine-tuning baselines, with particularly pronounced improvements on complex multi-step reasoning tasks. The robust performance under strict correctness criteria (requiring correct answers to at least 5 out of 6 question variants) confirms that our approach successfully instills generalizable reasoning capabilities. Human evaluation further validates our approach, showing a preference (47%) for the reasoning paths generated by our Cohort RL model over those from supervised fine-tuning.

6
Limitations

Model Scale

Due to computational constraints, we did not utilize different policy models for RL training. We plan to investigate the applicability of our approach to larger or alternative models in future work.

Hyperparameter Tuning

GRPO-based RL is resource-heavy and slow on our hardware, so we used one heuristic hyperparameter setup instead of exhaustive tuning. This may understate our method’s true performance—future work should systematically tune these parameters to improve both accuracy and efficiency.

The papers not including the checklist will be desk rejected.
The checklist should follow the references and follow the (optional) supplemental material. The checklist does NOT count towards the page

Please read the checklist guidelines carefully for information on how to answer these questions. For each question in the checklist:

,
[No]
, or
[N/A]
.

[N/A]

•

Please provide a short (1–2 sentence) justification right after your answer (even for NA).

The checklist answers are an integral part of your paper submission.
They are visible to the reviewers, area chairs, senior area chairs, and ethics reviewers. You will be asked to also include it (after eventual revisions) with the final version of your paper, and its final version will be published with the paper.

The reviewers of your paper will be asked to use the checklist as one of the factors in their evaluation. While "

Claims

Guidelines:

•

The claims made should match theoretical and experimental results, and reflect how much the results can be expected to generalize to other settings.

Limitations

Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors?

Answer:
[Yes]

Guidelines:

•

The answer NA means that the paper has no limitation while the answer No means that the paper has limitations, but those are not discussed in the paper.

•

The authors are encouraged to create a separate "Limitations" section in their paper.

•

The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to violations of these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings, model well-specification, asymptotic approximations only holding locally). The authors should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the implications would be.

•

The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated.

•

The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach. For example, a facial recognition algorithm may perform poorly when image resolution is low or images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be used reliably to provide closed captions for online lectures because it fails to handle technical jargon.

•

The authors should discuss the computational efficiency of the proposed algorithms and how they scale with dataset size.

•

If applicable, the authors should discuss possible limitations of their approach to address problems of privacy and fairness.

•

While the authors might fear that complete honesty about limitations might be used by reviewers as grounds for rejection, a worse outcome might be that reviewers discover limitations that aren’t acknowledged in the paper. The authors should use their best judgment and recognize that individual actions in favor of transparency play an important role in developing norms that preserve the integrity of the community. Reviewers will be specifically instructed to not penalize honesty concerning limitations.

Guidelines:

Depending on the contribution, reproducibility can be accomplished in various ways. For example, if the contribution is a novel architecture, describing the architecture fully might suffice, or if the contribution is a specific model and empirical evaluation, it may be necessary to either make it possible for others to replicate the model with the same dataset, or provide access to the model. In general. releasing code and data is often one good way to accomplish this, but reproducibility can also be provided via detailed instructions for how to replicate the results, access to a hosted model (e.g., in the case of a large language model), releasing of a model checkpoint, or other means that are appropriate to the research performed.

While NeurIPS does not require releasing code, the conference does require all submissions to provide some reasonable avenue for reproducibility, which may depend on the nature of the contribution. For example

(a)

If the contribution is primarily a new algorithm, the paper should make it clear how to reproduce that algorithm.

(b)

(c)

(d)

In the case of closed-source models, it may be that access to the model is limited in some way (e.g., to registered users), but it should be possible for other researchers to have some path to reproducing or verifying the results.
