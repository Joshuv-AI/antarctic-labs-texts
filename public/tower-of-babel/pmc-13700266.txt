Challenging diagnostic cases of peripheral ameloblastoma: a case series
Authors: Pouyan Aminishakib, Sahar Jahanzad, Ata Garajei, Faraz SeyedForootan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAmeloblastoma is a common neoplasm originating from the odontogenic epithelium and is known for its slow growth, local invasiveness, and benign nature. The 2022 World Health Organization classification divides ameloblastoma into five clinical types: conventional, unicystic, adenoid, metastasizing, and peripheral. Peripheral ameloblastoma (PA), a rare subtype comprising 1–10% of all cases, shares histological features with classic types. PA typically manifests as a painless, solid, exophytic lesion that is often mistaken for other gingival tumors.Case presentationThis report presents three cases of PA, illustrating diagnostic complexities and treatment outcomes. Patient 1 involved a 58-year-old Persian male initially misdiagnosed with squamous cell carcinoma, which was later confirmed as PA and successfully excised with no recurrence. Patient 2 was a 42-year-old Persian female initially diagnosed with basaloid squamous cell carcinoma, which was later reclassified as follicular-type PA and effectively excised. Patient 3 described a 59-year-old Persian male with a smoking history initially diagnosed with verrucous carcinoma and later reclassified as ameloblastic carcinoma following radical excision.ConclusionAccurate histopathological evaluation is crucial for preventing misdiagnosis and ensuring appropriate treatment, as PAs rarely progress to malignant forms. Conservative surgical excision is recommended for benign PAs, whereas more aggressive approaches are necessary for ameloblastic carcinoma.

Introduction

Ameloblastoma is the most common neoplasm originating from the odontogenic epithelium and arises from enamel organ-type tissue without progressing to enamel formation [1, 2 ]. These tumors are typically slow-growing, locally invasive, and intraosseous and follow a benign course [3]. According to the latest classification by the World Health Organization in 2022, ameloblastoma is categorized into five clinical types: conventional (solid/multicystic), unicystic, adenoid, metastasizing, and peripheral/extraosseous [4, 5 ].

Metastasizing ameloblastoma and ameloblastic carcinoma are rare malignant forms of ameloblastoma. Metastasizing ameloblastomas resemble benign ameloblastomas but can spread to other sites, such as the lung. Ameloblastic carcinoma has the histological features of ameloblastoma with cytological atypia, with or without metastases [6].

Peripheral ameloblastoma (PA), also known as extraosseous ameloblastoma, soft tissue ameloblastoma, or ameloblastoma of mucosal origin, is a rare benign odontogenic tumor that affects soft tissues. It accounts for 1–10% of all ameloblastomas [7–10]. The 5th WHO histological classification of tumors of the oral cavity includes extraosseous/peripheral ameloblastoma as one of the variants of ameloblastoma. This classification indicates that peripheral ameloblastomas originate from the same histological structures as the classic type. The tumor cells originate from the remaining Serres, the remnants of the reduced enamel epithelium, and the basal cells of the surface epithelium. Regarding the former hypothetical possibility, a definitive conclusion can be reached through specific cases of PAs that demonstrate complete separation from the covering squamous epithelium [8, 9 ].

Clinically, peripheral ameloblastoma (PA) is a painless, solid, exophytic, extraosseous, sessile lesion that gradually grows from the soft tissue in tooth-bearing areas. It commonly occurs in the gingiva, leading to its alternative name "gingival ameloblastoma". PAs can be mistaken for other lesions with similar appearances, such as pyogenic granulomas, peripheral ossifying fibromas, peripheral giant cell granulomas, and squamous papillomas [8, 11, 14 –]. Peripheral ameloblastic carcinoma demonstrates more aggressive clinical behavior, including rapid growth, cortical perforation, and peripheral extension [15].

Histologically, the most common histological form presents follicular or plexiform cells. The epithelium is always well defined, but a fibrous tissue capsule is not recognizable, so the tumor epithelium is often in close contact with the surrounding mucosa [14, 16 ].

Case presentation

Patient 1

A 58-year-old Persian male patient with a soft tissue mass in the right posterior maxillary region accompanied by bone erosion was referred to our institution. The patient had no significant past medical history, comorbidities, medications, or family history of relevant condition. Initially, a biopsy of the lesion was performed, and the pathologist diagnosed it as squamous cell carcinoma without considering imaging.

This biopsy sample was subsequently sent to another laboratory for consultation, and an experienced pathologist determined that the lesion was not related to SCC but was related to an odontogenic lesion.

A cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) study revealed a solid and expansile 14 × 16 mm lesion adjacent to tooth No. 2, elevating the sinus floor upward without causing any damage. The lesion showed expansion and erosion in the palatal cortex and destruction of the buccal cortex. Tooth No. 3 was not affected (Fig. 1). Microscopic examination was performed, and the sections revealed parakeratinized stratified squamous epithelium with papillary hyperplasia and a core of connective tissue. The elongated bulbous rete ridges were in close proximity to nests of various sizes, showing peripheral palisading and reverse polarity. Additionally, there were crowded epithelial islands with peripheral palisading, central bland stellate-like squamous nests, a tumor connection to the overlying epithelium, areas of loose suprabasal arrangement resembling the stellate reticulum, a scant fibrotic stroma, and foci of reverse polarization favoring peripheral/acanthomatous ameloblastoma (Fig. 2). An additional CT scan revealed an enhancing soft tissue mass with adjacent bone erosion in the right retromaxillary space. There was no evidence of cervical lymphadenopathy, right maxillary sinusitis, or abnormalities in the thorax.

Patient 2

A 42-year-old Persian female patient was referred to our institution with a soft tissue mass showing a verrucous surface on the lingual side of the mandibular right premolar region, along with bone erosion. The patient’s medical history was unremarkable, with no significant medications or family history of relevant conditions. An incisional biopsy was conducted, and the initial diagnosis by the pathologist was basaloid squamous cell carcinoma, due to the small, shallow biopsy. To confirm the diagnosis, the biopsy sample was sent to another laboratory for a second opinion. Patient 3

A 59-year-old Persian male patient was referred to our institution complaining of swelling in the right submandibular area. The patient had a history of smoking for 34 years. Imaging and intraoral examination revealed a lesion in the left neck and submandibular region. He had recently undergone incisional biopsy at another facility, and a diagnosis of verrucous carcinoma was made, due to clinical mimic and shallow biopsy depth. The lesion was radically excised via left mandibulectomy, both submandibular glands were resected, the left cervical nodes were dissected, and reconstructive surgery was performed via a flap from the pectoralis major muscle. The excised block (2 cm * 1.5 cm * 1.5 cm) was sent for histopathological examination, and the diagnosis was changed to malignant ameloblastoma at that time. This term was used inaccurately at first, as malignant ameloblastoma requires distant metastasis, which was absent, whereas the tumor exhibited cytological malignancy consistent with ameloblastic carcinoma. The patient was treated with radiotherapy with a total dose of 6000 rads in 30 fractions. No obvious enhanced mass lesions were observed on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) during the 10-month follow-up (Fig. 6).Fig. 6A postoperative magnetic resonance image of the patient showing no evidence of residual lesion. The patient returned for a face and neck CT scan three years later. Imaging revealed an abnormal heterogeneous area in the left lateral neck space, from the left suprahyoid area to the left supraclavicular area. A biopsy of the lesion was performed, and two pathologists provided different diagnoses: one for malignant ameloblastoma and the other for ameloblastic carcinoma. The paraffin blocks were sent to another pathologist for consultation, who made the diagnosis of ameloblastic carcinoma of the peripheral type on the basis of the histopathological features.

Histopathological examination revealed neoplastic tissue invading the adjacent lamina propria, originating from the overlying epithelium. The peripheral cells of the tumor islands exhibited prominent palisading and reverse polarity, whereas the centrally located cells showed cellular atypia and nuclear hyperchromatism. Discussion

Peripheral ameloblastoma is a rare, benign odontogenic tumor. Despite its benign nature, it can be mistaken for other gingival tumors, leading to potential misdiagnosis and inappropriate medical interventions. The differential diagnosis usually includes pyogenic granuloma, peripheral giant cell granuloma, peripheral odontogenic fibroma, peripheral ossifying fibroma, papilloma, epulis [16] and intraoral basal cell carcinoma (BCC) [14]. However, peripheral ameloblastoma may also be misdiagnosed as squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), as in our case, because both involve the invasion of epithelial cells from the overlying epithelium into the underlying connective tissue [17]. Histopathologic examinations showing epithelial islands with peripheral palisading and reverse polarity suggest a diagnosis of peripheral ameloblastoma. The diagnoses on this case series were based on Vickers–Gorlin morphological features only. However, in more challenging cases, immunohistochemical staining can be helpful in distinguishing ameloblastoma from other odontogenic lesions and SCC. Positive staining for cytokeratin 5, 14, and 19, calretinin, and amelogenin is observed in PAs [18].

Peripheral ameloblastoma is significantly uncommon compared to conventional intraosseous ameloblastomas. In a recent European multicenter study of 244 cases of ameloblastoma, only 8 cases (3%) were classified as peripheral [19]. Unlike intraosseous ameloblastomas, which are usually treated with a more aggressive approach due to their higher recurrence rates, peripheral lesions can often be effectively managed with conservative local excision.

Peripheral ameloblastomas rarely progress to a malignant form, transforming into ameloblastic carcinomas or metastasizing ameloblastomas. The 2005 World Health Organization classification categorizes ameloblastic carcinoma as primary (de novo), secondary, intraosseous (carcinoma ex ameloblastoma), or secondary, peripheral (carcinoma ex peripheral ameloblastoma). While most ameloblastic carcinomas are believed to originate de novo, they can also undergo malignant transformation from a benign ameloblastoma [20].

The third case may not be definitively classified as malignant transformation of a primary peripheral ameloblastoma. However, it could be interpreted as a primary ameloblastic carcinoma originating from the overlying epithelium.

In the 2022 WHO classification of odontogenic tumors, ameloblastic carcinoma is classified as a primary odontogenic carcinoma that resembles ameloblastoma rather than being its malignant counterpart [21]. Ameloblastic carcinoma shares histologic features with ameloblastoma but also presents cytologic characteristics of malignancy, such as cytologic atypia, nuclear pleomorphism, hyperchromatic nuclei, increased mitotic activity, and an elevated nuclear to cytoplasmic ratio [20].

Peripheral ameloblastic carcinoma is an extremely rare variant of this neoplasm, with only approximately 13 cases reported in the English literature (Table 1). The recommended treatment for peripheral ameloblastoma, a benign tumor, is conservative surgical excision with small yet sufficient margins [22]. However, for ameloblastic carcinoma, a rare malignant form, more aggressive measures should be pursued. The usual treatment includes wide, radical surgical excision of the tumor, with 2 to 3 cm of a safe margin [23]. One study suggested that a 1-cm margin is sufficient for early peripheral tumors [24]. Since these tumors are less aggressive than the intraosseous variant, the safety margin may be reduced with further studies [24].

In some cases, surgery may be followed by radiotherapy or chemotherapy. However, a study presented a case where adjunctive radiotherapy may have contributed to the progression of ameloblastic carcinoma transforming into anaplastic carcinoma [17].

Neck dissection may also be considered part of the treatment strategy if lymph nodes at levels II, III, and IV are larger than 1 cm or if submandibular lymph nodes are larger than 1.5 cm [25]. It is also recommended when an irregular shape or central necrosis is observed [25].

Conclusion

Peripheral ameloblastoma presents a challenge in the head and neck region because of its ability to be diagnosed and treated. This case series emphasizes the crucial role of pathologists in distinguishing between peripheral ameloblastoma and invasive squamous cell carcinoma to avoid unnecessary wide resection. This finding also underscores the importance of integrating clinical and radiographic information with histopathological findings for an accurate diagnosis. A limitation of this series is the short follow-up period for patient 1 (16 weeks). Given the potential for late recurrence in peripheral ameloblastoma, long-term surveillance, ideally for a minimum of 5 years, is mandatory even after complete excision with clear margins.
