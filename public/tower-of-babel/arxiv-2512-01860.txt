Biharmonic Equations Thanks: Corresponding Author . Dirk Pauly
Dirk Pauly, Alberto Valli

Abstract
. In this note we devise and analyse well-posed variational formulations and operator theoretical methods for boundary value problems associated to the biharmonic operator Δ 2 \Delta^{2} . Of particular interest are Neumann type and over-/underdetermined (maximal/minimal) boundary value problems.

Introduction

Throughout this paper – unless otherwise stated – let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
be a domain (connected and open set) with boundary
\Gamma:=\p\Omega

for which we assume no regularity at all.

Recently, in the new edition of the book of Valli
[
23

, the second author discussed

and presented proofs for the well-posedness of related variational formulations
under very strong regularity assumptions (
\mathsf{C}^{4}

1.1.
Historical Remarks

Let us consider a Neumann boundary value problems for the biharmonic operator,
that is

\Delta^{2}u=f\text{ in }\Omega\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Delta u=0,\quad n\cdot\na\Delta u=0\text{ on }\Gamma,

more closely.
Later, the corresponding proper Neumann biharmonic operator will be denoted by

\operatorname{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathtt{N}}=\operatorname{\mathcal{B}}_{\circ,\cdot}=\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}},

see Section
2.2.7
.
From the physical point of view this problem
is not the most interesting, as the model which describes the equilibrium position of an elastic thin plate,
unconstrained on the boundary, involves other second order and third order boundary operators,
in which the Poisson ratio also has a role (see Courant and Hilbert
[
5
, p. 250]

and more recently, e.g., Verchota
[
24
]
, Provenzano
[
21
]
;
the original physical model even dates back to Kirchhoff and Kelvin).

Instead of

\Delta^{2}=\Delta\Delta,

the bi-Laplacian can also be factorised by means of

\Delta^{2}=\divDiv\nana,

cf.
[
14
,
12
]
,
with different Neumann boundary conditions and hence different physical interpretations.
We shall come back later to this alternative,
cf. Section
1.3
and Section
2.3
.

However, despite these remarks, we think that the Neumann problem for the biharmonic operator
has a nice and simple mathematical structure, similar to that of other classical problems,
and we find it interesting from the mathematical point of view.
Moreover, it is the limiting case, for the Poisson ratio going to
1
of suitable physical models.

Let us first compare a bit the Neumann problems for
-\Delta
and
\Delta^{2}
.
As it is well-known, the Neumann boundary value problems
for the negative Laplacian
-\Delta

and what we call the bi-Laplacian/biharmonic operator
\Delta^{2}
,
i.e.,

\displaystyle-\Delta v

\displaystyle=g,

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}u

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle n\cdot\na v

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\Delta u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma,

\displaystyle n\cdot\na\Delta u

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma,

have similarities and differences.
In particular, for both of them the solution is not unique:
adding to
v
a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
resp. to
u
a harmonic function
h\in\mathbb{H}

gives another solution.
Moreover, for both of them the data have to satisfy a compatibility condition
(Fredholm alternative):
we have

g\bot\,\mathbb{R}\quad\text{for }-\Delta\qquad\text{and}\qquad f\bot\,\mathbb{H}\quad\text{for }\quad\;\Delta^{2}.

Note that in the smooth case we have

\displaystyle\langle g,1\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}\na v=-\int_{\Gamma}n\cdot\na v=0,

\displaystyle\langle f,h\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(\Delta^{2}u)h=\int_{\Gamma}(n\cdot\na\Delta u)h-\int_{\Gamma}\Delta u\,(n\cdot\na h)=0.

A first difference concerns the type of the boundary conditions:
the Neumann boundary condition for
-\Delta
satisfies the so-called complementing condition
of Agmon, Douglis, and Nirenberg
[
1
]
,
that in the present situation simply says that the polynomial

t
is not divisible by
t-i
.
This is not true for the Neumann boundary condition for
\Delta^{2}
:
the complementing condition would require that the polynomials
1+t^{2}
and
t+t^{3}

were linearly independent modulo
(t-i)^{2}
, and it is well-known that this is not the case.
For the ease of the reader, let us readily show this last statement: we have

\frac{1+t^{2}}{(t-i)^{2}}=1+\frac{2(1+it)}{(t-i)^{2}},\qquad\frac{t+t^{3}}{(t-i)^{2}}=t+2i+\frac{2(i-t)}{(t-i)^{2}},

and the second remainder
2(i-t)
is proportional to the first remainder
2(1+it)
by a factor
i
.
Note that the difference between the two Neumann problems
still shows up when considering the so-called Lopatinskiĭ–Šapiro condition
(see Wloka
[
25
, Examples 11.2 and 11.8]
);
in fact, it is known that the complementing condition and the Lopatinskiĭ–Šapiro
condition are equivalent (see, e.g., Negrón-Marrero and Montes-Pizarro
[
9
, Appendix A]
).

Another difference seems to be related to well-posedness: in fact,
the Neumann boundary value problem associated to
-\Delta
is well-posed in a suitable space
where uniqueness is recovered and for data which satisfy the necessary compatibility condition
(Fredholm alternative).
This well-known result is an easy consequence of the Poincaré inequality,
cf. Definition
2.6
,
and the Riesz representation theorem, or of more elaborate arguments
using linear functional analysis, cf. the discussion of the Neumann Laplacian
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}}
from Section
2.2.4
.
On the contrary, well-posedness for the Neumann boundary value problem associated to the
\Delta^{2}

operator seemed to be questionable so far
(see, e.g., what is explicitly reported in Verchota
[
24
, p. 217 and Sc. 21]
,
and in a more indirect way in Renardy and Rogers
[
22
, Sc. 9.4.2 and Example 9.30]
,
Gazzola, Grunau and Sweers
[
6
, Sc. 2.3]
,
Provenzano
[
21
, p. 1006]
).
In addition to this, it can be noted that Begehr
[
4
]
presents a long list of
twelve
boundary value problems
for the biharmonic operator that are either well-posed or solvable under suitable compatibility conditions,
and in that list the Neumann problem is not included.

Going a little bit more in depth, in Renardy and Rogers
[
22
]
,
Gazzola, Grunau and Sweers
[
6
]
, and Provenzano
[
21
]

the comments about the fact that the Neumann problem for the biharmonic operator
\Delta^{2}

is possibly not well-posed are related to the fact that the complementing condition is not satisfied
(in particular, this condition is assumed in the existence and uniqueness
Theorems 2.16 and 2.20 in
[
6
]
; there see also Remark 2.17).
This is apparently to be meaningful, as in Agmon, Douglis and Nirenberg
[
1
, Sc. 10]

it is explicitly proved that the complementing condition is necessary
for obtaining higher order a-priori estimates in Hölder and
\mathsf{L}^{p}
spaces
(in this respect, see also Lions and Magenes
[
8
, Chap. 2, Sc. 8.3 and Remark 9.8]
).

However, rather surprisingly,
it turns out that this condition is not necessary for well-posedness
in suitable Hilbert spaces, as the following example shows:
Consider the operator

\Delta^{2}+1.

Since the complementing condition only depends on the principal parts of the spatial and boundary operators,
we are in the same situation of the Neumann problem for the biharmonic operator;
therefore the complementing condition is also not satisfied.
However, the weak formulation of the problem (with homogeneous boundary data) reads:
For
f\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
find
u\in\mathsf{H}(\Delta,\Omega)
such that

(1)

\displaystyle\forall\,\varphi\in\mathsf{H}(\Delta,\Omega)\qquad\langle\Delta u,\Delta\varphi\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle u,\varphi\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle f,\varphi\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)},

which is uniquely solvable by Riesz’ representation theorem
with
|u|_{\mathsf{H}(\Delta,\Omega)}\leq|f|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}
.
Being now evident that the complementing condition is not necessary for well-posedness in a suitable Hilbert space,
in this paper we shall show that indeed, among others, the Neumann boundary value problem
for the biharmonic operator is well-posed.

1.2.
Approach by Bi-Laplacians

The approach in
[
23
, Section 5.6]
is based on the representation
of the biharmonic operator as bi-Laplacian
\Delta^{2}
with
Dirichlet/Neumann type and over-/underdetermined boundary conditions.

The story begins with the (full, not mixed) Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions for the negative Laplacian
-\Delta
.
These are the two reasonable boundary conditions for

-\Delta=-\operatorname{div}\na,

resulting in the apparently four “reasonable” (full) boundary conditions
for the biharmonic operator interpreted as bi-Laplacian.
More precisely:
For a possible solution
u
of

\Delta^{2}u=f\qquad\text{in }\Omega

we may impose a couple of the boundary conditions

(2)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma,

(
Dirichlet on
u
, i.e.,
u\in\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{1}(\Omega)
)

(3)

\displaystyle n\cdot\na u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma,

(
Neumann on
u
, i.e.,
\na u\in\mathring{\mathsf{H}}(\operatorname{div},\Omega)
)

(4)

\displaystyle\Delta u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma,

(
Dirichlet on
\Delta u
, i.e.,
\Delta u\in\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{1}(\Omega)
)

(5)

\displaystyle n\cdot\na\Delta u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma,

(
Neumann on
\Delta u
, i.e.,
\na\Delta u\in\mathring{\mathsf{H}}(\operatorname{div},\Omega)
)

as presented in the equations
[
23
, (5.24)-(5.27)]
.
Some of these six pairs have prominent names:

(6)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{Dirichlet:}&\quad\eqref{eq:bcD}\wedge\eqref{eq:bcN}&\text{Navier:}&\quad\eqref{eq:bcD}\wedge\eqref{eq:bcDD}\\
\text{Neumann:}&\quad\eqref{eq:bcDD}\wedge\eqref{eq:bcND}&\text{Riquier:}&\quad\eqref{eq:bcN}\wedge\eqref{eq:bcND}\end{aligned}

As we will see later, cf. Section
2.2.7
,
the latter four pairs
as well as
\eqref{eq:bcD}\wedge\eqref{eq:bcND}
,
then denoted by

\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}},\quad\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}},\quad\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}},\quad\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}},\quad\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}},

make
\Delta^{2}
well-posed,
while
\eqref{eq:bcN}\wedge\eqref{eq:bcDD}
,
then denoted by
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}}
, does not,
at least without further restrictions on the domain of definition.
The second author proposes variational formulations
for the Dirichlet, Neumann, Navier, and Riquier
biharmonic problems,
but notices that for
\eqref{eq:bcD}\wedge\eqref{eq:bcND}

and
\eqref{eq:bcN}\wedge\eqref{eq:bcDD}
no well-posed
variational formulations are at hand.

[
23
, Section 5.6.1]
is then specifically devoted to the Neumann problem
for the biharmonic equation
\eqref{eq:bcDD}\wedge\eqref{eq:bcND}
(
\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}
).
The natural Sobolev spaces for the variational method are then given by

\mathsf{H}(\Delta,\Omega)
from (
1
)
as well as
\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}(\Delta,\Omega)

and
\widetilde{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega)
.
For
f\in\widetilde{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega)

the variational formulation is then
to find
u\in\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}(\Delta,\Omega)
such that

\forall\,\varphi\in\mathsf{H}(\Delta,\Omega)\;\big(\text{or }\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}(\Delta,\Omega)\big)\qquad\langle\Delta u,\Delta\varphi\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle f,\varphi\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)},

cf. (
27
) in Section
2.2.6
.
It has been emphasised that the problem is well-posed if the
Poincaré type estimate

(7)

\displaystyle\exists\,c>0\quad\forall\,\varphi\in\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}(\Delta,\Omega)\qquad|\varphi|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq c|\Delta\varphi|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

holds, which follows if the embedding

(8)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}(\Delta,\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

is compact. The second author could show by regularity and Rellich’s selection theorem
that this is indeed true if
\Omega
is bounded and of class
\mathsf{C}^{4}
,
since then the embedding
\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}(\Delta,\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathsf{H}^{2}(\Omega)
is continuous,
more precisely,

(9)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}(\Delta,\Omega)=\Delta\big(\mathsf{H}^{4}(\Omega)\cap\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{2}(\Omega)\big)=\mathsf{H}^{2}(\Omega)\cap\mathbb{H}^{\bot}

with equivalent norms.
It has also been noted that
\mathsf{H}(\Delta,\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
is in general not compact
even for the unit ball and
N=2
.
The space
\Delta\big(\mathsf{H}^{4}(\Omega)\cap\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{2}(\Omega)\big)
(of high regularity)
has already been proposed by Provenzano
[
21
]

for studying an eigenvalue problem for the Neumann biharmonic operator.

A theory relying on
\mathsf{C}^{4}
-boundaries is unsatisfying.
In this contribution we shall present a solution theory
for quite a zoo of biharmonic problems – including the Neumann type –
which works fine with bounded Lipschitz domains
\Omega

or even beyond, cf. the notion of admissible domains in Definition
2.6
and Remark
2.7
,
which need neither to be bounded nor smooth (Lipschitz).

1.2.1.
Sketch of Basic Ideas and Concepts

We promote a more operator theoretical method
using basic functional analysis.
For simplicity, in this introduction, let us assume that
\Omega
is a bounded Lipschitz domain.

We consider the two gradients
(minimal and maximal)

\displaystyle\naga:\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{1}(\Omega)\subset\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega);

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle\mapsto\na\phi,

\displaystyle\naes:\mathsf{H}^{1}(\Omega)\subset\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega),

cf. Section
2.2.4
and
[
10
]

for more details and results on the de Rham complex.
Note that we have the Friedrichs’ and Poincaré’s estimates,
cf. Remark
2.9
and (
24
),

(10)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \exists\,c_{\mathsf{f}}>0\qquad\forall\,\varphi&\in\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{1}(\Omega)&|\varphi|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}&\leq c_{\mathsf{f}}|\na\varphi|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)},\\
\forall\,\varphi&\in\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{2}(\Omega)&|\varphi|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}&\leq c_{\mathsf{f}}^{2}|\Delta\varphi|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)},\\
\exists\,c_{\mathsf{p}}>0\qquad\forall\,\varphi&\in\widehat{\mathsf{H}}^{1}(\Omega)=\mathsf{H}^{1}(\Omega)\cap\mathbb{R}^{\bot}\qquad&|\varphi|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}&\leq c_{\mathsf{p}}|\na\varphi|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.\end{aligned}

With (
10
)

\naga
and
\naes
are well-defined, i.e.,
densely defined and closed linear operators,
with closed ranges,
and so are their Hilbert space adjoints
(maximal and minimal divergence)

\displaystyle-\dives=(\naga)^{*}:\mathsf{H}(\operatorname{div},\Omega)\subset\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega);

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle\mapsto\operatorname{div}\Phi,

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}_{\Gamma}=(\naes)^{*}:\mathring{\mathsf{H}}(\operatorname{div},\Omega)\subset\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega).

Then the Dirichlet and Neumann negative Laplacians

\displaystyle\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{D}}=\naga^{*}\naga=-\dives\naga:D(\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{D}})\subset\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega);

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle\mapsto-\operatorname{div}\na\phi,

\displaystyle\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{N}}=\naes^{*}\naes=-\divga\naes:D(\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{N}})\subset\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

with domains of definition

\displaystyle D(\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{D}})

\displaystyle=\big\{\varphi\in\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{1}(\Omega):\na\varphi\in\mathsf{H}(\operatorname{div},\Omega)\big\}=\big\{\varphi\in\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{1}(\Omega):\Delta\varphi\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\big\},

\displaystyle D(\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{N}})

\displaystyle=\big\{\varphi\in\mathsf{H}^{1}(\Omega):\na\varphi\in\mathring{\mathsf{H}}(\operatorname{div},\Omega)\big\},

are selfadjoint and non-negative
with kernels
N(\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{D}})=\{0\}
and
N(\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{N}})=\mathbb{R}
.
With the closed ranges we have the Fredholm alternatives

R(\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{D}})=N(\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{D}})^{\bot}=\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega),\qquad R(\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{N}})=N(\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{N}})^{\bot}=\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\cap\mathbb{R}^{\bot}=\widehat{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega).

Thus
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}}=\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{D}}

and
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}}=\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{N}}|_{\mathbb{R}^{\bot}}
are bijective
and the inverse operators

\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}}^{-1}:\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\to D(\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}}),\qquad\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}}^{-1}:\widehat{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega)\to D(\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{N}})\cap\mathbb{R}^{\bot}=D(\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}})

are bounded by (
10
).

We may also consider over- and underdetermined negative Laplacians
\mathring{\operatorname{L}}
and
\operatorname{L}
,
respectively, cf. Section
2.2.5
.
Now (
10
), cf. Lemma
2.8
and Lemma
2.1
,
yields that

\displaystyle\mathring{\operatorname{L}}:\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{2}(\Omega)\subset\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega);

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle\mapsto-\Delta\varphi,

\displaystyle\operatorname{L}:=\mathring{\operatorname{L}}^{*}:\mathsf{H}(\Delta,\Omega)\subset\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

are densely defined and closed with kernels

N(\mathring{\operatorname{L}})=\{0\}
and
N(\operatorname{L})=\mathbb{H}

and closed ranges

R(\mathring{\operatorname{L}})=N(\operatorname{L})^{\bot}=\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\cap\mathbb{H}^{\bot}=\widetilde{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega),\qquad R(\operatorname{L})=N(\mathring{\operatorname{L}})^{\bot}=\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega).

Therefore,
\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}=\mathring{\operatorname{L}}

and
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}=\operatorname{L}|_{\mathbb{H}^{\bot}}
are bijective
and the inverse operators

\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}{}^{-1}:\widetilde{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega)\to D(\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}),\qquad\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}:\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\to D(\operatorname{\mathcal{L}})=D(\operatorname{L})\cap\mathbb{H}^{\bot}=\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}(\Delta,\Omega)

are bounded by (
10
).

One could say that the dual (adjoint) pair

\mathring{\operatorname{L}}
and
\operatorname{L}
defines the minimal and the maximal

\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
-realisations of the negative Laplacian, in the sense

\mathring{\operatorname{L}}\subset\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{D}},\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{N}}\subset\operatorname{L}

with the two selfadjoint realisations
\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{D}}
and
\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{N}}
in-between.

Let
f\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
,

\widehat{f}\in\widehat{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega)
,
and

\widetilde{f}\in\widetilde{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega)
.
Then:

•

\mathring{u}=\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}{}^{-1}\widetilde{f}\in D(\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}})

is the unique solution of the overdetermined negative Laplace boundary value problem

\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}\mathring{u}=\widetilde{f}
.

•

u_{\mathtt{D}}=\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}}^{-1}f\in D(\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}})

is the unique solution of the Dirichlet negative Laplace boundary value problem

\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}}u_{\mathtt{D}}=f
.

•

u_{\mathtt{N}}=\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}}^{-1}\widehat{f}\in D(\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}})

is the unique solution of the Neumann negative Laplace boundary value problem

\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}}u_{\mathtt{N}}=\widehat{f}
.

•

u=\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}f\in D(\operatorname{\mathcal{L}})

is the unique solution of the underdetermined negative Laplace boundary value problem

\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}u=f
.

In classical terms we have

\displaystyle-\Delta\mathring{u}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{f},

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{\mathtt{D}}

\displaystyle=f,

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{\mathtt{N}}

\displaystyle=\widehat{f},

\displaystyle-\Delta u

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\mathring{u}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle u_{\mathtt{D}}

\displaystyle=0

In general,
\operatorname{A}
and
\operatorname{A}^{*}
are unbounded

\forall\,x\in D(\operatorname{A})\quad\forall\,y\in D(\operatorname{A}^{*})\qquad\langle\operatorname{A}x,y\rangle_{\mathsf{H}_{1}}=\langle x,\operatorname{A}^{*}y\rangle_{\mathsf{H}_{0}}.

Moreover, the projection theorem
yields the orthogonal decompositions (Helmholtz type decompositions)

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{0}=\overline{R(\operatorname{A}^{*})}\oplus_{\mathsf{H}_{0}}N(\operatorname{A}),\qquad\mathsf{H}_{1}=\overline{R(\operatorname{A})}\oplus_{\mathsf{H}_{1}}N(\operatorname{A}^{*}),

\operatorname{\mathcal{A}}:=\operatorname{A}|_{N(\operatorname{A})^{\bot}},\qquad\operatorname{\mathcal{A}}^{*}:=\operatorname{A}^{*}|_{N(\operatorname{A}^{*})^{\bot}},

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{A}}:D(\operatorname{\mathcal{A}})\subset N(\operatorname{A})^{\bot}

\displaystyle D(\operatorname{\mathcal{A}})

\displaystyle:=D(\operatorname{A})\cap N(\operatorname{A})^{\bot},

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{A}}^{*}:D(\operatorname{\mathcal{A}}^{*})\subset N(\operatorname{A}^{*})^{\bot}

\displaystyle\to\overline{R(\operatorname{A}^{*})}=N(\operatorname{A})^{\bot},

\displaystyle D(\operatorname{\mathcal{A}}^{*})

\displaystyle:=D(\operatorname{A}^{*})\cap N(\operatorname{A}^{*})^{\bot}.

(\operatorname{\mathcal{A}},\operatorname{\mathcal{A}}^{*})
are also densely defined and closed
forming another dual pair.
Moreover, by (
15

(16)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} D(\operatorname{A})&=D(\operatorname{\mathcal{A}})\oplus_{\mathsf{H}_{0}}N(\operatorname{A}),&D(\operatorname{A}^{*})&=D(\operatorname{\mathcal{A}}^{*})\oplus_{\mathsf{H}_{1}}N(\operatorname{A}^{*}),\\
R(\operatorname{A})&=R(\operatorname{\mathcal{A}}),&R(\operatorname{A}^{*})&=R(\operatorname{\mathcal{A}}^{*}).\end{aligned}

Here we have used the symbols

\overline{\,\cdot\,}
,

, and

(ii)

R(\operatorname{A})
is closed.

(ii

The projections in (

For

More precisely,

such that

.

Now let us consider the bounded linear operator

and its Banach space adjoint

Using the Riesz isometry

2.2.5.
Over- and Underdetermined Laplacians

Let
\Omega
be Friedrichs admissible.

Lemma 2.8
.

On
\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{2}(\Omega)
the norms

|\cdot|_{\mathsf{H}^{2}(\Omega)}
and
|\Delta\cdot|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

are equivalent. More precisely,

\forall\,\varphi\in\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{2}(\Omega)\qquad|\varphi|_{\mathsf{H}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq c_{\Delta}|\Delta\varphi|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)},

where
c_{\Delta}:=\sqrt{1+c_{\mathsf{f}}^{2}+c_{\mathsf{f}}^{4}}
.

Proof.

By (
24
)

\forall\,\varphi\in D(\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{D}})\qquad|\varphi|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+|\na\varphi|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+|\Delta\varphi|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq c_{\Delta}^{2}|\Delta\varphi|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

For
\varphi\in\mathring{\mathsf{C}}^{\infty}(\Omega)
we observe

\sum_{i,j}\langle\p_{i}\p_{j}\varphi,\p_{i}\p_{j}\varphi\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=\sum_{i,j}\langle\p_{i}^{2}\varphi,\p_{j}^{2}\varphi\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=|\Delta\varphi|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

which extends to
\varphi\in\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{2}(\Omega)
by continuity
and shows the stated estimate.
∎

Lemma
2.8
and Lemma
2.1
yield that
the over- and underdetermined Laplacians

\displaystyle\mathring{\operatorname{L}}:\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{2}(\Omega)\subset\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega);

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle\mapsto-\Delta\varphi,

\displaystyle\operatorname{L}:=\mathring{\operatorname{L}}^{*}:\mathsf{H}(\Delta,\Omega)\subset\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

are densely defined and closed with closed ranges
R(\mathring{\operatorname{L}})
and
R(\operatorname{L})
.
Moreover,

(25)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} N(\mathring{\operatorname{L}})&=\{0\},&R(\mathring{\operatorname{L}})&=N(\operatorname{L})^{\bot}=\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\cap\mathbb{H}^{\bot}=\widetilde{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega),\\
N(\operatorname{L})&=\mathbb{H},&R(\operatorname{L})&=N(\mathring{\operatorname{L}})^{\bot}=\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega).\end{aligned}

The reduced operators

\displaystyle\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}:\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{2}(\Omega)\subset\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to\widetilde{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}:\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}(\Delta,\Omega)\subset\widetilde{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}(\Delta,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{H}(\Delta,\Omega)\cap\mathbb{H}^{\bot}

are bijective with bounded inverse operators

\displaystyle\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}{}^{-1}:\widetilde{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to D(\mathring{\operatorname{L}})=\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{2}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}:\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to D(\operatorname{\mathcal{L}})=\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}(\Delta,\Omega),

\displaystyle|\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}{}^{-1}|_{\widetilde{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=|\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq c_{\mathsf{f}}^{2},

cf. (
24
).

Remark 2.9
.

In particular, we have for all

\varphi\in D(\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}})=\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{2}(\Omega)

and all
\varphi\in D(\operatorname{\mathcal{L}})=\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}(\Delta,\Omega)

|\varphi|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq c_{\mathsf{f}}^{2}|\Delta\varphi|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

Let
f\in\widetilde{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega)
.
Then
u:=\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}{}^{-1}f\in D(\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}})=\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{2}(\Omega)

is the unique solution of
the overdetermined negative Laplace boundary value problem
\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}u=f
, i.e.,

\displaystyle-\Delta u

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma,

\displaystyle n\cdot\na u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma.

Remark 2.10
.

Note that
u|_{\Gamma}=0
implies
n\times\na u|_{\Gamma}=0
.
Hence, together with
n\cdot\na u|_{\Gamma}=0
we see
\na u|_{\Gamma}=0
.
In other words, for
u\in\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{1}(\Omega)
it holds

u\in\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{2}(\Omega)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\na u\in\mathring{\mathsf{H}}(\operatorname{div},\Omega)\cap\mathsf{H}^{1}(\Omega).

Let
f\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
.
Then
u:=\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}f\in D(\operatorname{\mathcal{L}})=\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}(\Delta,\Omega)

is the unique solution of
the underdetermined negative Laplace boundary value problem
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}u=f
, i.e.,

\displaystyle-\Delta u

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\;\bot\;\mathbb{H}.

Remark 2.11
.

Note that for the different negative Laplacians we have

\mathring{\operatorname{L}}\subset\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{D}},\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{N}}\subset\operatorname{L}
, that is

\mathring{\operatorname{L}}\subset\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{D}}=-\operatorname{div}\nac\subset\operatorname{L},\qquad\mathring{\operatorname{L}}\subset\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{N}}=-\divc\na\subset\operatorname{L}.

In this sense,
\mathring{\operatorname{L}}
and
\operatorname{L}
are minimal and maximal

\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
-realisations of the negative Laplacian, respectively.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
\Omega
be bounded. Then

D(\operatorname{\mathcal{L}})=\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}(\Delta,\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

is compact.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
we have that the embedding

D(\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}})=D(\mathring{\operatorname{L}})=\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

is compact if and only if the embedding

D(\operatorname{\mathcal{L}})=\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}(\Delta,\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

is compact.
Hence the latter embedding is compact by Rellich’s selection theorem
for, e.g.,
\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{1}(\Omega)
.
∎

2.2.7.
A Zoo of Biharmonic Operators

Let
\Omega
be Poincaré admissible.
Note that
\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{H}
and hence

\widetilde{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega)\subset\widehat{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega).

Let us recall the different reduced negative Laplacians

\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}},\qquad\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}}=\operatorname{L}_{\mathtt{D}}=-\operatorname{div}\nac,\qquad\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}}=-\divc\na_{\widehat{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega)},\qquad\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}

with domains of definition

\displaystyle D(\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}})

\displaystyle=\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{2}(\Omega),

\displaystyle D(\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}})

\displaystyle=\big\{\varphi\in\mathring{\mathsf{H}}^{1}(\Omega):\Delta\varphi\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\big\},

\displaystyle D(\operatorname{\mathcal{L}})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}(\Delta,\Omega),

\displaystyle D(\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}})

\displaystyle=\big\{\varphi\in\widehat{\mathsf{H}}^{1}(\Omega):\na\varphi\in\mathring{\mathsf{H}}(\operatorname{div},\Omega)\big\}

and their respective bounded inverse operators

(28)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}{}^{-1}:\widetilde{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega)&\to D(\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}})\subset\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega),&\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}}^{-1}:\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)&\to D(\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}})\subset\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega),\\

In Section
2.2.6
we already discussed
the Dirichlet and Neumann biharmonic operators

\operatorname{\mathcal{B}}_{\cdot,\circ}:=\operatorname{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathtt{D}}=\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}=\operatorname{L}\mathring{\operatorname{L}}=\operatorname{B}_{\mathtt{D}},\qquad\operatorname{\mathcal{B}}_{\circ,\cdot}:=\operatorname{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathtt{N}}=\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}},

being selfadjoint, positive, bijective and boundedly invertible
with the well posed (well defined and uniquely solvable)
inverse operators
\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}{}^{-1}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}
and
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}{}^{-1}
.

Combining the four different Laplacians we obtain
a whole zoo of formally sixteen biharmonic operators.
Due to the restrictions of
\widehat{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega)
and
\widetilde{\mathsf{L}}^{2}(\Omega)

some combinations are – even formally – not possible
(without further restrictions), those are the five combinations

\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}{}^{-1}\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}{}^{-1},\qquad\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}{}^{-1}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}}^{-1},\qquad\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}{}^{-1}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}}^{-1},\qquad\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}}^{-1}\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}{}^{-1},\qquad\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}}^{-1}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}}^{-1},

corresponding to

\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}
,
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}}\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}
,
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}}\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}
,
\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}}
,
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}}
, respectively.
One has to consider different operators to realise even stronger or weaker boundary conditions.
We come back to this later.

We end up with only nine more well posed
biharmonic operators, namely

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{B}}_{\cdot,\cdot}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}},

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathtt{D},\cdot}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}},

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathtt{N},\cdot}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}},

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{B}}_{\cdot,\mathtt{N}}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}},

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathtt{N},\mathtt{N}}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}},

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{B}}_{\circ,\mathtt{D}}

\displaystyle:=\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}},

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{B}}_{\cdot,\mathtt{D}}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}},

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathtt{D},\mathtt{D}}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}},

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathtt{N},\mathtt{D}}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{N}}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{D}}.

All eleven biharmonic operators are
bijective and boundedly invertible, more precisely

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{B}}_{\cdot,\circ}^{-1}=\mathring{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}{}^{-1}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}:\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega),
