Systemic lupus erythematosus comorbid with chronic spontaneous urticaria: a multicentre retrospective study
Authors: Xuedan Zeng, Hao Xu, Qian Li, Shanshan Wei, Naiyu Lin, Li Yin, Junhao Zhu, Shan Yang, Yuanjing Tang, Guixin Liang, Weinan Lai, Wenjing Zhang, Kuan Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis retrospective study aims to characterise the epidemiological features of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) concomitant with chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU), examines the correlation between CSU occurrence and lupus disease activity, and identifies comorbidity patterns and risk factors associated with SLE-CSU.MethodsA total of 40 SLE patients with concomitant CSU and 160 age-matched and sex-matched SLE controls without CSU were included. The Mann-Whitney U-test was used to assess disease activity at SLE onset in both groups. A Wilcoxon signed-rank test was conducted to compare the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) scores before and after CSU onset in patients with SLE. χ2 tests were applied to evaluate differences in SLE activity grading, clinical manifestations, laboratory findings and treatments between patients with SLE with and without CSU. In addition, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify risk factors for CSU development in patients with SLE.ResultsWilcoxon signed-rank tests revealed that in the SLE-CSU group, the occurrence of CSU did not result in an increase of SLEDAI scores. Univariate analysis revealed significant differences between the SLE-CSU and SLE-only groups in the presence of cylindruria, elevated IgM, IgA, IgG, serositis, mucosal ulcers and anti-Pm-Scl antibodies (p<0.05). Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified cylindruria (OR: 6.152, CI 2.352 to 16.093, p＜0.001), elevated IgA (OR: 7.598, CI 1.194 to 48.368, p=0.032), elevated IgG (OR: 3.252, CI 1.331 to 7.946, p=0.010) and mucosal ulcers (OR: 3.838, CI 1.166 to 12.637, p=0.027) as independent risk factors for CSU occurrence in patients with SLE.ConclusionThe presence of CSU in patients with SLE does not necessarily indicate increased lupus activity. Rather, cylindruria, mucosal ulcers and elevated IgA and IgG levels were identified as independent risk factors for CSU in patients with SLE.Keywords: Systemic Lupus Erythematosus, Risk Factors, Autoantibodies

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a systemic autoimmune disease that can affect multiple organs and systems and is characterised by the production of various autoantibodies. Its immunological profile includes dysregulation of B and T cells, along with the generation of multiple autoantibodies targeting nuclear antigens, complement components and DNA.1 2 Among these, double-stranded DNA-specific IgG is the most prevalent.3 The global prevalence of SLE varies widely, ranging from 15.87 to 108.92 per 100 000 individuals.4

Chronic urticaria (CU) has a complex aetiology and pathogenesis. In 2017, the European Urticaria Guidelines classified CU into two categories: chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) and chronic inducible urticaria.5 CSU is characterised by recurrent episodes of hives and/or angioedema without identifiable triggers, persisting for at least 6 weeks. It is the most common form of CU, accounting for approximately 90% of cases.6 CU has been associated with various autoimmune diseases, including dermatomyositis, polymyositis, Sjögren’s syndrome and rheumatoid arthritis.7 As a key autoimmune disorder, the relationship between SLE and CSU is increasingly being recognised and studied.8 9

SLE is a systemic chronic inflammatory disease, whereas CSU is primarily considered an allergic condition affecting the skin and mucous membranes. Despite differences in their pathophysiology and clinical manifestations, it is not uncommon for patients with SLE to present with concomitant CSU in clinical practice. Some researchers suggest that CU may serve as an early symptom of SLE,10 while others propose that SLE may contribute to the development of CU and that CU itself could be a high-risk factor for SLE onset.11 However, studies investigating the coexistence of SLE and CSU remain limited. This raises important questions: Is CSU occurrence in patients with SLE associated with SLE disease activity? Are there distinct clinical and laboratory differences between patients with SLE with and without concomitant CSU? To address these questions, this study includes 40 patients with SLE with CSU and 160 age-matched and sex-matched SLE controls without CSU.

Methods

Study population

A retrospective search was conducted using the outpatient and inpatient record systems of Nanfang Hospital, Zhujiang Hospital, The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University and The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University. Patients diagnosed with SLE complicated by CU between January 2008 and November 2023 were identified based on predefined criteria. For each patient with SLE-CSU, a control was randomly selected from patients with SLE without urticaria in Nanfang Hospital, matched at a 1:4 ratio based on gender and age at SLE onset. Clinical and laboratory indicators were collected from these matched SLE control patients at the corresponding time points after their diagnosis.12 13

Exclusion criteria: patient records were individually reviewed to exclude cases of CU triggered by specific factors, such as cold-induced or cholinergic urticaria. Cases of CU attributed to medications, infections or other identifiable causes, as well as those diagnosed with urticarial vasculitis, were also excluded. In addition, patients with incomplete clinical data (eg, insufficient symptom descriptions or missing laboratory investigations) were excluded. Furthermore, cases with neurological or psychiatric impairments or those with compromised cardiac, hepatic or renal function due to conditions such as hepatic encephalopathy, nephrotic syndrome, IgA nephropathy, severe valvular heart disease or myocardial infarction were excluded.

Diagnostic criteria: the diagnosis of SLE in this study was based on the 1997 criteria established by the American College of Rheumatology. CSU was diagnosed in patients presenting with spontaneous hives and/or angioedema persisting for at least 6 weeks.

Data collection

This retrospective study involved a comprehensive review of patient medical records from both inpatient and outpatient settings. Data were collected on sex, age at SLE onset, initial symptoms of SLE, Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index 200014 15 at the time of SLE diagnosis, age at CSU onset, SLE disease activity before and after CSU occurrence, average duration of CSU episodes and systemic involvement. The latter included renal, musculoskeletal, neurological, gastrointestinal, respiratory, cardiovascular and haematological systems as well as alopecia and treatment details.

Laboratory investigations included assessments of complement levels and a broad panel of autoantibodies, including anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm, antinuclear, anti-Sm/RNP, anticardiolipin, anti-UI-RNP, anti-SSA, anti-SSB, antimitochondrial, anticentromere, antinucleolar, anti-SCL-70, anti-PM-Scl, anti-Ro52, anti-ribosomal, anti-Jo-1 and anti-PCNA antibodies. In addition, haematological parameters such as red blood cell count, haemoglobin level, platelet count, white blood cell count and immunoglobulin levels (IgM, IgA and IgG) were evaluated.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS V.26.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York) and SPSSAU. The normality of continuous variables was assessed prior to analysis. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean±SD (x¯±s) and compared between groups using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median (P25, P75), with between-group differences analysed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were reported as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Differences in disease activity before and after the onset of CSU in SLE patients were analysed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple hypothesis testing. Univariate and logistic regression analyses were performed to identify potential influencing factors. Variables with a significance level of p<0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis. p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

At the Nanfang Hospital, we collected a total of 212 patients diagnosed with SLE and urticaria (SLE-urticaria), accounting for 2.13% of the patient with SLE population and 0.37% of the urticaria patient population. Among the 212 patients with SLE-urticaria, 44 cases were SLE-CSU, accounting for 0.44% of the SLE population and with a male-to-female ratio of 1:7. However, only 29 cases of them had complete information successfully included in this study. Additionally, six cases of SLE-CSU were recorded at The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, three at The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University and two at the Zhujiang Hospital, totalling 40 cases of SLE-CSU included in the investigation (figure 1).

The study involved 40 cases of patients with SLE-CSU, randomly selected at a 1:4 ratio compared with patients with SLE with matched gender and age of SLE onset, leading to a male to female ratio of 1:7. Among 40 patients with SLE-CSU, the median age at CSU onset was 34 years (IQR 21–47), and the mean duration of CSU was 15.5 months. The incidence density of SLE comorbid with CSU is 5.6 cases per person-year. Furthermore, with an average follow-up duration of 52 months, seven individuals among the 40 cases developed CSU 2–8 years before their SLE diagnosis, while nine patients experienced CSU at the early stages of lupus onset (within 3 months). Baseline analyses were performed on both groups, revealing no significant discrepancies in terms of age of SLE onset, initial activity of SLE or treatment modalities. However, the SLE-CSU group exhibited a higher prevalence of mucosal ulcers in comparison to the SLE group (p=0.028) in terms of initial SLE symptoms (table 1).

To investigate whether there was an elevation in SLEDAI for patients with SLE-CSU on the occurrence of CSU, this study longitudinally assessed the SLEDAI for patients with SLE at the onset of CSU and before and after it. The median intervals (IQR) were 3 months (IQR 2–4) between the ‘Before’ and ‘Onset’ timepoints, 2.3 months (IQR 1–6) between ‘Onset’ and ‘After’, and 5 months (IQR 3–9) between ‘Before’ and ‘After’. By employing the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (table 2, figure 2), the research revealed no significant disparity in SLEDAI scores between the preonset and onset phases of CSU in SLE-CSU patients. Additionally, as illustrated in figure 2, over time, there was a reduction in SLEDAI values for SLE-CSU after the occurrence of CSU, indicating that the presence of urticaria in SLE patients did not escalate SLE activity.

Furthermore, in the univariate analysis, the SLE-CSU group and the SLE group exhibited statistical discrepancies concerning the presence of mucosal ulcers (27.5% vs 10.63%), serositis (10.0% vs 26.25%), cylindruria (42.50% vs 10.63%), elevated levels of IgM (7.50% vs 1.25%), IgA (17.50% vs 1.25%), IgG (62.50% vs 26.88%) and anti-Pm-Scl antibodies (10.00% vs 0.63%) with a significance level of p<0.05; while no notable differences were observed in other clinical and laboratory characteristics (table 3). In the multivariate analysis, cylindruria (OR: 6.152, CI 2.352 to 16.093, p<0.001), elevated IgA (OR: 7.598, CI 1.194 to 48.368, p: 0.032), elevated IgG (OR: 3.252, CI 1.331 to 7.946, p: 0.010), and mucosal ulcers (OR: 3.838, CI 1.166 to 12.637, P: 0.027) were identified as factors associated with the occurrence of CSU in patients with SLE, thus representing risk factors for SLE-CSU (table 4).

Discussion

SLE is an autoimmune disease, resulting from immune system dysregulation, leading to the formation and deposition of immune complexes in various tissues, ultimately causing tissue damage.11 Organ involvement often signifies disease severity and can be life-threatening. CSU, however, presents as recurrent wheals, angioedema16 and persistent itching lasting over 6 weeks without identifiable triggers.

In clinical practice, patients with SLE may present with CSU at any stage of the disease. Although the relationship between SLE and CSU appears to be closely linked, few studies have specifically examined this association. In this study, patients identified with both SLE and CSU account for 0.44% of the SLE population, with a male-to-female ratio of 1:7. In nearly half (40%) of the patients with CSU-SLE, onset of CSU is before or at the early stages of SLE, which suggests that CSU could potentially serve as an early indicator of SLE, aligning with the findings of Spadoni and Kutlu A.7 17

Using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for longitudinal analysis, we compared SLEDAI values before, during and after the onset of CSU in patients with SLE-CSU. The results indicated that the occurrence of CSU in SLE patients does not necessarily signify increased disease activity. Most patients who experienced CSU were in a mild or inactive stage of SLE, with no significant increase in SLE activity compared with before the onset of CSU. This finding contrasts with the results reported by Ferriani et al,8 who suggested that urticaria, a specific skin manifestation in lupus, primarily occurs during active phases of the disease and correlates with moderate to high disease activity. The discrepancy may stem from differences in study design and population. Ferriani et al focused on 10 Brazilian paediatric lupus patients in a cross-sectional study, whereas our study involved a larger cohort of Chinese adults, with four times the sample size, allowing for a longitudinal analysis of SLE activity before and after the occurrence of CSU. Notably, our study revealed that the SLEDAI score of post-CSU was lower than both preonset and onset of CSU. To exclude potential confounding from intensified immunosuppressive therapy during CSU episodes, we compared the dosages of corticosteroids, hydroxychloroquine and immunosuppressants across three phases (preonset, onset and postonset), observing no significant interphase differences (online supplemental table S1). The reason for the decline of SLEDAI scores among patients with SLE after CSU onset remains unclear and warrants further investigation in the future.

Factors with a significance level of p<0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis, which revealed that the presence of cylindruria, mucosal ulcers and elevated IgA and IgG levels were risk factors for the development of CSU in patients with SLE.

Regarding treatment, the majority of patients in this study received glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants and hydroxychloroquine. Some studies suggest that the onset of CU in patients with SLE may be triggered by lupus medications, particularly non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.5 18 19 Notably, none of the 40 cases in this study had used non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and prednisone dosages ranged from 10 to 50 mg/day, with no changes in the variety of medications. For the vast majority of cases, steroid doses remained unchanged compared with the preceding 3 months, thereby reducing the likelihood of medication-induced effects (online supplemental table S1).

The role of IgG antibodies in the pathogenesis of both SLE and CSU has garnered substantial attention from researchers. SLE is typically characterised by the production of autoantibodies targeting nuclear antigens, complement fragments and DNA, with the majority of autoantibodies being double-stranded DNA-specific IgGs. Studies indicate that approximately 40% of patients with CU also produce IgG autoantibodies, primarily directed against IgE and the high-affinity IgE receptor (FcεRI). In the cohort of 40 patients with SLE-CSU, 25 individuals exhibited elevated IgG levels, constituting 62.5% of the total, suggesting a high IgG profile in SLE-CSU. Kolkhir et al reported that patients with CU with elevated IgG tend to have a longer disease course and a poorer response to antihistamine therapy compared with those without elevated IgG.20 This finding implies that SLE-CSU patients with elevated IgG may require consideration of more advanced treatment strategies for CU in clinical practice.

A retrospective study is inherently subjected to information bias, including selection bias in the choice of study participants, the limited number of patients included and the absence of SLEDAI assessments or incomplete laboratory examinations in some cases. Unequal follow-up durations among cases and controls prevent it from employing a time-to-event analysis. These factors may affect the comprehensiveness and generalisability of the findings.

Despite its limitations, our study provides valuable insights into the clinical characteristics of patients with SLE with concomitant CU, offering useful information for clinical practice. We look forward to large-scale prospective studies to further explore the comorbidity of SLE and CU and to develop predictive models for assessing the risk of this comorbidity.

Conclusions

This study revealed that patients with both SLE and CSU accounted for 0.44% of SLE cases, with a male-to-female ratio of 1:7. The average duration of CSU in patients with SLE-CSU was 15.5 months. The occurrence of CSU in SLE patients does not necessarily indicate increased disease activity; rather, CSU may serve as an early manifestation of SLE. Notably, cylindruria, mucosal ulcers and elevated IgA and IgG were identified as independent risk factors for the development of CSU in patients with SLE. Further fundamental research is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of this comorbidity.
