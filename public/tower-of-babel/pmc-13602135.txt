Effect of Fenfluramine on convulsive seizures and rescue medication usage: A case series of adults within the Lennox-Gastaut syndrome spectrum
Authors: Lisa Chilson, Boubker Zaaimi, Shanika Samarasekera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAdults with Developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEE) within the Lennox-Gastaut Syndrome (LGS) spectrum suffer disabling convulsive seizures which may remain refractory and are associated with increased risk of hospitalisations and death. Fenfluramine (FFA) was licenced in the UK for seizures associated with LGS in March 2025.MethodsAdjunctive FFA was assessed in a series of 6 adults with a genetic DEE within the LGS spectrum treated for refractory convulsive seizures. The minimum period of observation was 9 months (range 9–32 months). 3 patients commenced FFA pre-licencing on compassionate grounds. 4 patients had a VNS, 5 patients were prescribed concomitant Cannabidiol. Reduction in convulsive seizures and reduction in use of rescue medications/ frequency of hospitalisations was also assessed. Caregiver responses were recorded using the Clinical Global Improvement of Change (CGI—I).ResultsAt last follow up, all 6 patients remained on FFA. No significant side effects were observed. There was a significant reduction in seizures at last follow up (p = 0.035), with a corresponding reduction in use of rescue medications and hospitalisations. On the CGI—I, 5 of the 6 patients were rated as much/ very much improved, the remaining patient was rated as minimally improved.ConclusionAdjunctive FFA was tolerated without significant side effects and associated with a significant reduction in convulsive seizures in this limited cohort. Corresponding reduction in use of rescue medications and hospitalisations has the potential to impact on both patient and caregiver quality of life.Keywords: epilepsy, fenfluramine, Lennox-gastaut spectrum

Introduction

Lennox Gastaut Syndrome (LGS) is a disabling and life limiting epilepsy syndrome arising in childhood and associated with refractory seizures which may persist into adulthood [1]. It is characterized by multiple seizure types, including generalized tonic clonic seizures (GTC) which can develop subsequent to other seizure types and are the common trigger for repeated hospitalisations. GTC are a key risk factor for injury and death –GTC are associated with a 10-fold increased risk of Sudden unexplained death in epilepsy (SUDEP) [2].

By the time they transition to adult care, young people with LGS have often tried multiple anti-seizure medications (ASMs) and where accessible, surgical strategies including corpus callosotomy and vagal nerve stimulation (VNS).

In 2018, trials of cannabidiol in LGS heralded a potential therapeutic strategy for refractory seizures in this population. The median number of ASMs tried before recruitment into GW Pharmaceuticals trials into cannabidiol for seizures associated with LGS: GWPCARE 3 and GWPCARE4 was 6–8 [3], [4]. In our study of 51 adults prescribed adjunctive cannabidiol with clobazam for LGS, 54% of whom had received prior VNS, 35 of 47 (74%) adults at last follow up (median 24 months) experienced significant (≥50%) GTC reduction [5]. In a recent multi-centre study of 102 patients with LGS including 71 adults treated with adjunctive cannabidiol and clobazam, 14 of 25 (56%) experienced significant GTC reduction at 12 months [6]. The number of patients who had received prior surgical strategies was not reported. It appears therefore, that a proportion of adults with LGS continue to experience uncontrolled GTC in spite of maximal therapy.

In 2020, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence NICE (UK) approved Fenfluramine (FFA) for the treatment of Dravet Syndrome. This followed the results from three placebo-controlled RCTs where FFA was efficacious in significantly reducing monthly convulsive seizures by between 54 and 65% [7]. Subsequent case series of FFA in children with genetically mediated epileptic encephalopathies within the LGS spectrum – CDKL5 [8] and SCN8A [9], showed promising results with up to 90% reduction in convulsive seizures. Following this, practical recommendations for the use of FFA in developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEE) within the LGS spectrum were issued by a panel of Spanish epileptologists in 2025 [10]. They reflected on its potential efficacy for both seizure reduction and improvement in non-seizure outcomes. NICE approved the use of FFA for LGS in March 2025.

LGS spectrum

Seizures in the context of LGS can change over the lifespan, tonic seizures (especially in sleep), a hallmark of the LGS phenotype in childhood, may not be the predominant seizure type in the adult patient. The typical childhood EEG findings of slow spike and wave and/or paroxysmal fast activity may also not be present [11]. Our cohort was therefore defined as LGS spectrum: Having an epilepsy syndrome comprising multiple seizure types arising in childhood associated with progressive cognitive and behavioural impairment and historical abnormalities on EEG (slow spike and wave and/or paroxysmal fast activity). This is an adaptation of the ILAE LGS criteria [12].

Objectives

To assess the effectiveness of adjunctive FFA in reducing convulsive seizures for adults with a genetically mediated DEE within the LGS spectrum.

To assess the impact of FFA on use of rescue medications and hospitalisations in this cohort.

Methods

The case records of adults initiated on FFA between May 2023 and May 2025 were retrospectively reviewed. 3 adults were treated with FFA on compassionate grounds via individual funding request pre-March 2025.

Baseline demographic data including genetic, clinical and EEG features of the LGS-Spectrum diagnosis, duration of epilepsy, number of previous and concomitant ASMs and presence of VNS were recorded. Frequency of GTC (including focal seizures evolving to GTC) was assessed using seizure diaries and caregiver records. Frequency of rescue medication use in the 12 months pre FFA was recorded as daily/weekly/monthly. Frequency of hospitalisations in the last 12 months of follow up post FFA was also recorded as daily/weekly/monthly/less frequently. Change in seizure intensity was assessed by caregiver reporting in tandem with reduction is use of rescue medications.

Change in overall function following FFA was assessed using the Clinical Global Impression of Improvement (CGI—I) scale [13]. Assessments were unblinded and made by 2 raters (LC and SS).

Cardiac monitoring

In keeping with NICE guidance, echocardiography was undertaken in all patients pre initiation of FFA 6 monthly for the first two years and yearly thereafter.

Statistics

Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare key parameters: change in number of convulsive seizures pre and post FFA and change in number of hospitalisations. Frequency and percentages were additionally used to assess change in seizure reduction (significant reduction was defined as ≥50%).

Ethics

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in accordance with trust guidelines. It was approved by the Trust's ethics committee and Clinical Audit Registration and Management System (CARMS)- registration 23813.

Results

Baseline demographics including GTC frequency

6 adult patients (4 female) were treated with FFA for a minimum of 12 months (range 12–32, mean 23 months). Age at initiation of FFA treatment was between 23 and 32 years. All patients had a genetically mediated DEE within the LGS Spectrum with associated severe (patients 4,5,6) or profound (patients 1.2.3) intellectual disability (ID) as defined by DSM 5 [14] and reflected by their need for constant daily supervision (Table 1). 3 patients had a history of tonic seizures in sleep, 4 patients had a history of paroxysmal fast activity on EEG. 1 of these patients also had slow spike and wave features on EEG.

5 patients experienced daily convulsive seizures. These 5 patients required rescue medication (buccal midazolam) for prolonged seizures at least weekly. In 3 of the 5 patients this resulted in at least monthly hospitalisation. Two patients experienced prolonged seizure clusters requiring hospitalisation at least once/ 2 months. For geographical reasons, patient 2 continued to be managed at home with frequent paramedic support and without regular hospitalisation.

The minimum number of ASMs tried prior to recruitment was 8 (range 8 to 16). The minimum number of concomitant ASMs was 2 (range 2–6). 4 patients had a VNS, in 1 patient this had been switched off due to lack of efficacy, 1 patient was weaning down. The remaining patients' VNS settings were unchanged during the monitoring period.

Efficacy for GTC and reduced hospitalisations

In the last 12 months of follow-up, FFA significantly reduced GTC frequency in all 6 individuals (p = 0.035), with a corresponding reduction in rescue medications in the 5 individuals using these regularly (p = 0.054)- see supplementary data. Patients 1, 4 and 5 experienced a ≥ 75% reduction in seizure-related hospital admissions (Fig. 1). Our patient with CDD experienced a reduction in seizure clusters resulting in a 50% reduction in rescue medication. 2 of the 3 patients experiencing ≥50% seizure reduction were prescribed concomitant cenobamate (Table 1).

CGI-I outcomes

5 of the 6 patients (83%) were recorded as being much improved/ very much improved. No patients were recorded as unchanged/ worse (Fig. 2).

CGI-I outcomes following initiation of FFA.

Reduction in concomitant ASMs

Following FFA initiation, cannabidiol (CBD) was reduced in 4 of the 6 patients. In patients 2 and 6 (see supplementary table 1) CBD was reduced from 10 mg/kg/day to 2.5 mg/kg/day over 12 months. In patient 1 CBD was reduced from 10 mg/kg/day to 7.5 mg/kg/day over 24 months and in patient 4 from 5 mg/kg/day to 2.5 mg/kg/day over 12 months. Seizure control remained stable in all patients.

Tolerability and adverse events

FFA was well tolerated and none of the patients discontinued treatment. No significant side effects (such as significant weight loss) were observed.

Cardiac function and safety reporting

No changes in cardiac function (including valvular gradients) were observed on serial echocardiography.

Discussion

This is the first real world case series of young adults with a diagnosis of DEE on the LGS Spectrum treated with adjunctive FFA for GTC control. This is a severe refractory population, with regular prolonged seizures necessitating intensive use of emergency care and a clear risk of SUDEP. FFA was well tolerated; our experience suggests that judicious reduction of other potentially sedating medications, including cannabidiol may have improved tolerability. Both cannabidiol and cenobamate can increase clobazam levels and thereby contribute to sedation.

The traditional indicator of ASM efficacy - ≥ 50% seizure reduction is much harder to measure in a population experiencing multiple daily seizures. Despite this a significant reduction in GTC was seen within this small cohort, with all patients experiencing ≥30% reduction in GTC. Real world markers of efficacy –significant reduction in use of rescue medications and hospitalisation were independently observed in at least 3 of the 6 individuals.

Quantifying change in overall function is challenging in a group of patients with such diverse disabilities and needs. The CGI-I results are encouraging and a likely reflection of seizure reduction, though without blinded and serial baseline assessments, it is not possible to interpret these further.

Limitations

This is a small case series from a single centre. We recognise that, whilst EEG features of LGS can change over the lifespan, these patients did not universally fulfil the typical LGS criteria and varied substantially in terms of underlying genetic aetiology. It is therefore more challenging to extrapolate these results across the wider population of patients with ‘typical’ LGS.

Practice may vary across centres; although the threshold for indication of improvement with FFA in the UK is ≥30% reduction in drop seizures, our cohort were prescribed FFA pre-licencing primarily to control GTC. Whilst drop seizures were present in some of our cohort, they were not the primary seizure type and so were not recorded as a key parameter of change. Given our limited cohort of 6 patients and multiple paired comparisons, the reported p values are best interpreted as descriptive, rather than confirmatory.

Concomitant use of cannabidiol and cenobamate

Interpretation of FFA efficacy is limited by concomitant use of ASMs including cannabidiol and cenobamate, both of which may independently reduce both focal to GTC and GTC. Two patients demonstrating >50% seizure reduction were co-prescribed cenobamate, raising the possibility of additive or synergistic effects. Concomitant reduction in Cannabidiol in 4 patients could have negatively confounded seizure outcome, though loss of seizure control was not clinically observed.

Conclusions

Adjunctive FFA was tolerated without significant cardiac side effects and associated with seizure reduction of at least 30% which was clinically meaningful in our case series of 6 adults with intellectual disability and severe refractory epilepsy on the LGS Spectrum. Addition of FFA enabled reduction in concomitant ASMs, reducing the cost burden and potentially contributing to a reduction in side effects. Use of FFA was associated with a reduction in healthcare utilisation in 4 of 6 patients, which whilst modest, is critical in this vulnerable population at risk of life-limiting co-morbidities such as hospital acquired infection.

This case series demonstrates the need for larger real-world studies of Fenfluramine in combination with other third generation ASMs, with a view to potentially stratifying combination therapies by seizure subtype and phenomenology. There is a clear need for tailoring existing treatments within this diverse and complex population.
