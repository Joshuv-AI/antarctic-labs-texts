Half-space decay for linear kinetic equations
Émeric Bouin, Stéphane Mischler, Clément Mouhot

Abstract
. We prove that solutions to linear kinetic equations in a half-space with absorbing boundary conditions decay for large times like t − 1 2 − d 4 t^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{d}{4}} in a weighted 𝖫 2 \mathsf{L}^{2} space and like t − 1 − d 2 t^{-1-\frac{d}{2}} in a weighted 𝖫 ∞ \mathsf{L}^{\infty} space, i.e. faster than in the whole space and in agreement with the decay of solutions to the heat equation in the half-space with Dirichlet conditions. The class of linear kinetic equations considered includes the linear relaxation equation, the kinetic Fokker-Planck equation and the Kolmogorov equation associated with the time-integrated spherical Brownian motion.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The question at hand

The optimal polynomial decay of dissipative semigroups in unbounded domains—with or without boundary—is an interesting theoretical question, and it is important for many applications. In terms of spectral theory, it corresponds to understanding the role of the continuous spectrum in the time asymptotics.

Diffusion semigroups are well-understood but less so are the
hypodissipative
semigroups, particularly in an unbounded domain with a boundary. The standard elementary method to understand the polynomial decay of diffusion semigroups in the whole space is through self-similar changes of variables: it reduces the problem to establishing a spectral gap on a new unknown. However, the scaling structure of hypodissipative semigroups is usually too complicated for such a method to have any chance.

The standard elementary method to understand the decay of diffusion semigroups in the simplest unbounded domain with boundary, the half-space, is to extend them to semigroups in the whole space by symmetrisation. Again, such extensions by symmetrisation do not seem available for hypodissipative semigroups of kinetic theory considered here, due to the more complicated interplay between spatial and kinetic variables.

1.2.
The setting

To study of the interplay between hypodissipativity and unbounded domains with boundaries, we consider the
linear kinetic equation
of the form

(1.1)

\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\mathcal{L}f\quad\hbox{on}\quad(0,\infty)\times\Omega_{x}\times\Omega_{v},

where the unknown
f
depends on the variables

t\in[0,+\infty),\quad x\in\Omega_{x}:={\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}\quad\text{and}\quad v\in\Omega_{v},

with the velocity domain

\Omega_{v}:=B(0,1)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\quad\text{or}\quad\Omega_{v}=\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\quad\text{or}\quad\Omega_{v}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Here,
d\geq 1
is the underlying space dimension and
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}
is the open half-space

{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}:=\left\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,:\,x_{1}>0\right\}.

The unknown
f\geq 0
is the density of particles and the linear operator
\mathcal{L}
acts only on the kinetic variable
v
. We denote
\Omega:=\Omega_{x}\times\Omega_{v}
. We complement the evolution equation with initial conditions
f(0,\cdot,\cdot)=f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}(\cdot,\cdot)
, and with the kinetic counterpart of the Dirichlet boundary conditions, i.e. the “
no-incoming particle conditions
”:

(1.2)

f(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)=0\quad\hbox{on}\quad(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega_{+},

where
\Omega_{+}
is the ingoing part of the boundary:

\partial\Omega_{+}:=\left(\{0\}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}\right)\times\Big(\Omega_{v}\cap\{v_{1}>0\}\Big).

In other words,
f(t,x,v)=0
whenever
x_{1}=0
and
v_{1}\geq 0
, which means there are no
incoming
particles from the boundary.

Regarding the collision operator
{\mathcal{L}}
at the r.h.s. of (
1.1
), we assume that it acts only on the velocity variable
v\in\Omega_{v}
and will consider the following three paradigmatic cases:

(1)

the
linear relaxation operator

\mathcal{L}f:=M\,\int_{\Omega_{v}}f(v_{*})\,{\mathrm{d}}v_{*}-f
,

(2)

the
Fokker-Planck operator

\mathcal{L}f:=\Delta_{v}f+\nabla_{v}\cdot(\bullet f)
(only with
\Omega_{v}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
),

(3)

the
Laplace-Beltrami operator

\mathcal{L}f:=\Delta^{\text{LB}}_{v}f
(only with
\Omega_{v}=\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
),

where in the first example, either
\Omega_{v}
is bounded and
M
is a probability measure such that

\int_{\Omega_{v}}vM(v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v=0\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\Omega_{v}}(v\otimes v)\,M(v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v=\text{Id},

or either
\Omega_{v}:={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
M
is the normalised gaussian
M=\mathfrak{M}:=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{-|\cdot|^{2}/2}
.
It is worth emphasizing that in both cases such a function
M
is a local equilibrium profile, namely
{\mathcal{L}}M=0
.
We also denote
M:=|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|^{-1}
the local equilibrium profile for the Laplace-Beltrami operator in
\Omega_{v}=\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

and
M:=\mathfrak{M}

the local equilibrium profile for the Fokker-Planck operator in
\Omega_{v}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

In order to state our main result, we have to introduce the functional spaces in which we will work in. For a weight function
\xi:\Omega\to(0,\infty)
and an exponent
p=1,2
, we denote

\mathsf{L}^{p}(\xi):=\mathsf{L}^{p}(\Omega;\xi\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v)
the Lebesgue space on
\Omega
endowed with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra and associated to the measure
\xi\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}x
.
On the other hand, for a weight function
\omega=\omega(v):\Omega_{v}\to(0,\infty)
and two exponents
p\in[1,\infty]
,
k\in\{-1,0,1\}
, we also define the weighted Lebesgue norm

(1.3)

\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{p}_{k,\omega}}:=\|f(\lfloor x_{1}\rceil+\lfloor v\rceil)^{k}\omega\|_{\mathsf{L}^{p}_{x,v}(\Omega)}

with the notation
\lfloor y\rceil:=(1+|y|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
for
y\in{\mathbb{R}}
or
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. We shall use the shorthand
\mathsf{L}^{p}_{\omega}=\mathsf{L}^{p}_{0,\omega}
. For further reference, we also define

(1.4)

{\mathscr{M}}_{0}:=\int_{\Omega}(x+v)f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}(x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}x,

for
f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\in\mathsf{L}^{1}_{1,1}(\Omega)
, in dimension
d=1
.
Next, depending of the collision operator, we will deal with different classes of weight functions.

(i) When
\Omega_{v}
is bounded, we do not need weight function and thus just assume
\omega\equiv 1
.

(ii) For the linear relaxation operator when
\Omega_{v}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we call admissible weight function any function
\omega:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that
\lfloor\cdot\rceil^{d}\lesssim\omega\lesssim\mathfrak{M}^{-1}
.

(iii)
Finally for the Fokker-Planck operator, we say that
\omega
is an admissible weight function if

\omega:=e^{r\lfloor\cdot\rceil^{s}},

with

s=1
,
r>1
, or
s\in(1,2)
,
r>0
, or
s=2
,
r\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
.

It is worth emphasising for further discussion, that in any case
\omega:=M^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is an admissible weight function.

Our main results are as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider an integrable non-negative function
f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}
. Then the associated global solution
f
to the linear kinetic equation (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) satisfies:

(i)

Integral decay in general dimension
d\geq 1
:

(1.5)

\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2}\lesssim\lfloor t\rceil^{-1-\frac{d}{2}}\left[\|f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(\lfloor x_{1}\rceil)}^{2}+\|f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2}\right].

(ii)

Pointwise decay in general dimension
d\geq 1
:

(1.6)

\left\|f\right\|_{\mathsf{L}_{-1,\omega}^{\infty}}\lesssim\lfloor t\rceil^{-1-\frac{d}{2}}\left[\|f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}_{1,\omega}}+\|f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{\infty}_{-1,\omega}}\right],

where
\omega
is any admissible weight function (as described above).

Theorem 1.2
.

Localisation of the mass in dimension
d=1
.

Assume now
{\mathscr{M}}_{0}>0
. For the solutions to the linear relaxation equation (related to linear relaxation operator (1)) or to the linear Fokker-Planck kinetic equation (related to linear the Fokker-Planck operator (2)), there are
S_{0}>0
,
0<a<b<+\infty
,
0<A<B<+\infty
and
x_{t}\in\left[a\sqrt{t},b\sqrt{t}\right]
so that

(1.7)

\forall\,t\geq S_{0},\ x\in\left[x_{t}-1,x_{t}+1\right],\ v\in\Omega_{v},\quad\frac{AM(v)^{1+0}}{t}\leq f(t,x,v)\leq\frac{BM(v)}{t},

where
a,b,A,B
depend on
{\mathscr{M}}_{0}
and on some weighted Lebesgue norms of
f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}
that will be specified during the proof.

Remarks 1.3
.

(1)

The pointwise decay estimate is stronger than the integral one since the unknown is not squared in this second estimate. Note that (
1.5
) immediately follows from (
1.6
) and the uniform in time
\mathsf{L}^{1}
moment estimate established in Subsections
3.6
–
3.7
. However, our proof is based on
first
establishing (
1.5
) (see Section
3
) and
then
deducing (
1.6
) (see Section
4
).

(2)

In the case when
\Omega_{x}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
without boundary condition, the decay rates of the squared
\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})
norm and of the
\mathsf{L}^{\infty}(M^{-1})
norm are only
t^{-\frac{d}{2}}
. See
[
7
, Theorem 1]
for the
\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})
decay, and the next section for a discussion of the pointwise decay. These rates are optimal in general.

(3)

These rates match those of the heat equation in the half-space, see the next section.

(4)

It is likely that the same result holds for more general equilibrium state
M
in the case of the linear relaxation operator, and in particular, we will discuss what are the properties really needed for
M
at the beginning of section
3.1
.
We however do not try to be the most exhaustive and we only focus on the paradigmatic case in the present work.

In Section
2
, we review known results on the heat equation as a warm-up. It allows us to introduce a key differential inequality we will then aim at obtaining in the kinetic case, and to recall the decay rates for the heat flow that are to be matched. In Section
3
, we prove the first integral decay estimate (
1.5
); the proof combines hypocoercivity estimates adapted to keep track of boundary terms, trace estimates, and moment estimates. In Section
4
we prove the second pointwise decay estimate (
1.6
); the proof uses the semigroup factorisation techniques developed in
[
21
,
31
]
in order to perform an approximate duality argument on part of the semigroup. We deduce a precise localization of the mass in Section
5
by using iterated Duhamel principles and Harnack inequalities.

In the follow-up paper
[
9
]
, we use these decay results on the initial boundary value problem to study the Bramson correction for kinetic reaction-transport equations.

2.
Reminder on the heat equation

2.1.
The whole space

Consider the heat equation

(2.1)

\partial_{t}\rho=\Delta_{x}\rho

on the unknown
\rho=\rho(t,x)
for
t>0
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, associated to an initial datum
\rho(0,\cdot)=\rho_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\in\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Using Kato’s inequality
\text{sign}(\rho)\Delta_{x}\rho\leq\Delta_{x}|\rho|
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\text{sign }\rho)\Delta_{x}\rho\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Delta_{x}|\rho|\,{\mathrm{d}}x=0,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}=-2\|\nabla_{x}\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2},

so that the
\mathsf{L}^{1}
and
\mathsf{L}^{2}
norms are non-increasing. Combined with Nash’s inequality
[
38
]

(2.3)

\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{1+\frac{2}{d}}\lesssim\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{\frac{2}{d}}\|\nabla_{x}\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})},

the previous
\mathsf{L}^{2}
estimate implies

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\|\rho(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}\lesssim-\|\rho(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{-\frac{4}{d}}\|\rho(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2+\frac{4}{d}}.

Using now
\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq\|\rho_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
due to the
\mathsf{L}^{1}
decay estimate, we get

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}\lesssim-\|\rho_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{-\frac{4}{d}}\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2+\frac{4}{d}}.

Recalling that
\bar{u}:=(\alpha^{-1}Kt)^{-\alpha}
is a solution to the ordinary differential equation
u^{\prime}=-Ku^{1+\alpha}
and using a standard comparison principle for differential equations, we deduce the decay

\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}\lesssim(1+t)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\|\rho_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}.

To obtain the pointwise decay, one simply observes that due to the symmetry of the semigroup, the last inequality implies by duality

\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}\lesssim t^{-\frac{d}{2}}\|\rho_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}

and by combining the last two inequalities we get

\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}\lesssim\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\left\|\rho\left(\tfrac{t}{2},\cdot\right)\right\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}\lesssim\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)^{-d}\left\|\rho_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\right\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}.

These integral and pointwise decay rates are optimal as can be easily seen from the formula giving the fundamental solution of (
2.1
).

2.2.
The half-space with Dirichlet conditions

We now consider the heat equation (
2.1
) on
\rho=\rho(t,x)
, in the domain
x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}
with the initial condition
\rho(0)=\rho_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\in\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})
and with the Dirichlet boundary conditions
\rho(t,x)=0
at the boundary
\partial{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,:\,x_{1}=0\}
. By linearity, we can assume without loss of generality that the initial data
\rho_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}
is nonnegative, which implies that the solution
\rho
is nonnegative for all times.

Then, the
\mathsf{L}^{1}
norm is non-increasing since

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})}=-\int_{\partial{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\partial_{x_{1}}\rho(t,x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}\leq 0,

where
x=(0,x^{\prime})
on the boundary. Here, the right hand side is non-positive because
\partial_{x_{1}}\rho\geq 0
on the boundary
\{x_{1}=0\}
since
\rho(t,x)\geq 0
and
\rho=0
at the boundary. The first moment is preserved and captures the location of the mass:

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\|x_{1}\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}(\Delta_{x}\rho)x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\partial_{x_{1}}\rho\,{\mathrm{d}}x=0,

by integration twice by parts.

The energy estimate (
2.2
) still holds, but the Nash inequality (
2.3
) can now be improved. Indeed, our density
\rho
defined on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}
can be extended to a function defined on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
by anti-symmetrisation:
\rho(x_{1},x^{\prime})=-\rho(-x_{1},x^{\prime})
for
x_{1}\leq 0
and
x^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
. It produces a function
\rho\in\mathsf{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
so that
\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}((1+|x_{1}|)\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}
is finite and

(2.4)

\forall\,x^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1},\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(x_{1},x^{\prime})\,{\mathrm{d}}x_{1}=0.

Therefore, its Fourier transform
\hat{\rho}
satisfies
\hat{\rho}(0,\xi^{\prime})=0
for all
\xi^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
and, given
R>0
,

\forall\,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,:\,|\xi_{1}|\leq R,\quad|\hat{\rho}(\xi)|\leq R\|\partial_{\xi_{1}}\hat{\rho}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq R\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho(x)|x_{1}|\,{\mathrm{d}}x.

We may thus estimate the
\mathsf{L}^{2}
norm of
\rho
as follows

\displaystyle\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}=\|\hat{\rho}\|^{2}_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}=\int_{|\xi_{1}|\leq R}|\hat{\rho}|^{2}+\int_{|\xi_{1}|>R}|\hat{\rho}|^{2}\lesssim R^{d+2}\|\rho|x_{1}|\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}+\frac{1}{R^{2}}\|\nabla_{x}\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2},

and by optimising
R
, we deduce the following improved Nash inequality

\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{1+\frac{2}{d+2}}\lesssim\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,|x_{1}|\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}^{\frac{2}{d+2}}\|\nabla_{x}\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}.

Because of the anti-symmetry of
\rho
, the same holds on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}
rather than
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
:

(2.5)

\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+})}^{1+\frac{2}{d+2}}\lesssim\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+},\,|x_{1}|\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}^{\frac{2}{d+2}}\|\nabla_{x}\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+})}.

We finally plug this improved Nash inequality into the energy estimate (
2.2
) to deduce

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})}^{2}\lesssim-\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d},\,x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}^{-\frac{4}{d+2}}\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})}^{2+\frac{4}{d+2}}.

Using the conservation
\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d},\,x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}=\|\rho_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d},\,x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}
, we obtain

(2.6)

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\|\rho(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})}^{2}\lesssim-\|\rho_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d},\,x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}^{-\frac{4}{d+2}}\|\rho(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})}^{2+\frac{4}{d+2}},

which implies, by the same previous standard comparison principles, the improved decay

(2.7)

\|\rho(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})}^{2}\lesssim(1+t)^{-1-\frac{d}{2}}\|\rho_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d},\,x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}^{2}.

To obtain the pointwise decay we use the same duality argument as in the case of the whole space, which yields

\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d},\,x_{1}^{-1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}\lesssim t^{-1-\frac{d}{2}}\|\rho_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d},\,x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}^{2}.

Again, the decay rates obtained both in
\mathsf{L}^{2}
and
\mathsf{L}^{\infty}
are optimal. This can be deduced from the formula on the fundamental solution; the latter being obtained from the fundamental solution in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
by the same anti-symmetrisation procedure we have used above. However, this simple anti-symmetrisation procedure does not seem applicable to the case of kinetic equations, and we develop another method in Section
3
.

2.3.
Localisation of the mass in dimension
1

We expect the solution to asymptotically resemble the eigenmode with lowest eigenvalue that appears in the orthogonal decomposition of the initial data. In the case of the half-space, the anti-symmetrisation procedure allows to construct a solution in the whole space which has no component along the first Gaussian eigenmode, so we expect the localisation of mass to resemble that of the second eigenmode
(t,x)\mapsto x\,t^{-\frac{3}{2}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{t}}
, with in particular

\forall\,t\geq 0,\qquad\int_{a\sqrt{t}}^{a\sqrt{t}}x\,t^{-\frac{3}{2}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{t}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}

and

\forall\,x\in\left[a\sqrt{t},b\sqrt{t}\right],\qquad\frac{\min_{z\in[a,b]}z\,e^{-z^{2}}}{t}\leq\frac{x}{t^{\frac{3}{2}}}\,e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{t}}\leq\frac{\max_{z\in[a,b]}z\,e^{-z^{2}}}{t}.

Although such a behaviour would follow from spectral arguments, let us prove these bounds directly by energy estimates. This will prepare us for the kinetic case, where we do not have fundamental solutions and explicit eigenbasis at our disposal. Standard interpolation yields (remember that in this subsection,
d=1
.),

(2.8)

\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}\lesssim\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}^{\frac{2}{3}}\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},x\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}^{\frac{1}{3}}.

Therefore, by using the conservation of the first moment
\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},x\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}
and the decay estimate (
2.7
), we deduce

(2.9)

\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}\lesssim\frac{\|\rho_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},x\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}}{t^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

We then compute the time evolution of the second moment (assuming it is initially finite):

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}x^{2}\rho(t,x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x=2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\rho(t,x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\lesssim\frac{1}{t^{\frac{1}{2}}}

from what we get, after time integration,

\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},x^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}\lesssim t^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality then implies

\displaystyle\|\rho_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|^{2}_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},x\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},x\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},x^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}\lesssim t^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})},

which proves the lower bound

(2.10)

\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}\gtrsim\frac{1}{t^{\frac{1}{2}}}\|\rho_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|^{2}_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},x\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}.

Together with the previous upper bound, this shows the time evolution

\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}\approx t^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\rho_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|^{2}_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}x\,{\mathrm{d}}x)},\quad\forall\,t>0.

To localise more precisely where this
\mathsf{L}^{1}
mass is located we can use the
\mathsf{L}^{2}
estimate (
2.7
) to prevent concentration near
x=0^{+}
, and the estimate on the first moment, which prevents loss of mass at infinity:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a\sqrt{t}}\rho(t,x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\lesssim\sqrt{a}t^{\frac{1}{4}}\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}\lesssim\sqrt{a}t^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\rho_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},x\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}

\displaystyle\int_{b\sqrt{t}}^{+\infty}\rho(t,x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\lesssim b^{-1}t^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\rho_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},x\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}.

Therefore by taking
a>0
small enough and
b>0
large enough, we deduce

\int_{a\sqrt{t}}^{b\sqrt{t}}\rho(t,x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\gtrsim t^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

This proves the integral lower bound, and the integral upper bound already followed from the
\mathsf{L}^{1}
estimate. To then obtain the pointwise localisation of the mass without using the fundamental solution, one could use the elliptic regularisation
\mathsf{L}^{1}\to\mathsf{L}^{\infty}
for the upper bound, and the (weak) Harnack inequality for the lower bound.
For proving that last estimate for kinetic equations, we will use hypoelliptic regularisation and Harnack inequalities when available, but for hypodissipative kinetic equation without hypoellipticity we will use a different approach based on the transport characteristics and the associated dispersion property.

3.
Integral decay for the kinetic problem

3.1.
Setting

We consider the linear kinetic equation (
1.1
) on
(0,\infty)\times\Omega
with absorbing boundary condition (
1.2
) on
(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega_{+}
, where
\Omega
,
\mathcal{L}
and
M
as described in the introduction.
The unknown
f
is assumed, without loss of generality, to be non-negative.

In this section, we prove the first integral decay estimate (
1.5
). As in
[
7
]
, the proof relies on a modification of the systematic technique developed in
[
17
]
, which is itself part of the broader series of works developing hypocoercivity estimates
[
24
,
23
,
37
,
41
,
22
,
5
]
.
The novelty is the use of the coercivity at the boundary to capture the (weak) decay, see also
[
22
,
4
,
5
]
for related arguments. Let us start making more explicit the fundamental features of the collision operator we will use in our analysis.
First, denoting by
{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}
the adjoint of
{\mathcal{L}}
in the flat space
L^{2}(\Omega_{v})
, we have

(3.1)

{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}1=0\quad\hbox{and}\quad\exists\,\phi\hbox{ such that }{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}\phi=-v_{1},

where
\phi(v):=v_{1}
in the cases (1) and (3), and where
\phi(v):=v_{1}/(d+1)
in the case (2). For further reference, we denote
\phi=c_{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}v
.
In the case (1), a direct computation shows indeed that
\phi(v):=v_{1}
satisfies
\mathcal{L}^{\sharp}\phi=\int v_{1}M\,{\mathrm{d}}v-v_{1}=-v_{1}
. In the case (3) and for the same
\phi
, we also straightforwardly compute

\mathcal{L}^{\sharp}\phi=(\Delta_{v}-v\cdot\nabla_{v})v_{1}=-v_{1}
.
In order to deal with the Laplace-Beltrami operator (with
d\geq 2
), we introduce the coordinate system
v=(\cos\theta,[\sin\theta]\,w)
with
w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
, so that

\displaystyle\Delta^{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}_{v}=(\sin\theta)^{2-d}\partial_{\theta}\left((\sin\theta)^{d-2}\partial_{\theta}\right)+\Delta^{\mathbb{S}^{d-2}}_{w}.

Observing that

\displaystyle(\sin\theta)^{2-d}\partial_{\theta}\left((\sin\theta)^{d-2}\partial_{\theta}\left(\frac{\cos(\theta)}{d-1}\right)\right)=-\cos(\theta),

we thus deduce that the function
\phi(v)=v_{1}/(d-1)
satisfies
\mathcal{L}^{\sharp}\phi=\mathcal{L}\phi=-v_{1}
.

Next, we assume there exists a measurable uniformly bounded probability measure
M:\Omega_{v}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
which is a local equilibrium profile, that is
{\mathcal{L}}M=0
, and such that

(3.2)

\lfloor\bullet\rceil^{k}M\in\mathsf{L}^{1}(\Omega_{v}),\ k>d+6,\,\,\,\int_{\Omega_{v}}vM\,{\mathrm{d}}v=0,\,\,\,\int_{\Omega_{v}}(v\otimes v)\,M\,{\mathrm{d}}v=\text{Id}.

Furthermore,
{\mathcal{L}}
is symmetric in
\mathsf{L}^{2}_{v}(M^{-1})
, it has a one-dimensional kernel
\hbox{Ker}\,{\mathcal{L}}=\hbox{Span}(M)
and it enjoys a
spectral gap inequality
: there exists
\lambda_{m}>0
such that ,

(3.3)

\left\langle\mathcal{L}f,f\right\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}_{v}(M^{-1})}\leq-\lambda_{m}\|f-\rho[f]M\|_{\mathsf{L}_{v}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2},

for any smooth enough function
f
on
\Omega_{v}
,
where we use here and in the sequel the notation

\rho[f]:=\int_{\Omega_{v}}f(v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v,\quad\iota[f]:=\int_{\Omega_{v}}vf(v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v.

The fact that our paradigmatic operators fufill this spectral gap property is very classical. We refer for instance to
[
15
,
29
]
for the linear relaxation operator,
to
[
1
,
2
]
for the Fokker-Planck operator and to
[
2
]
for the Laplace-Beltrami operator.
In the two last cases, this estimate is nothing but the Poincaré inequality.

3.2.
The hypocoercivity method

The essence of hypcoercivity techniques is the use of commutators to control what is “missed” by the coercivity of the operator. Here

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}v\cdot\nabla_{x}\left[f^{2}\right]M^{-1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v+2\left\langle\mathcal{L}f,f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(M^{-1})}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\int_{\Omega_{v}}|v_{1}|f(t,0,x^{\prime},v)^{2}M^{-1}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}+2\left\langle\mathcal{L}f,f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(M^{-1})}.

The first term on the right hand side is non-positive thanks to the boundary conditions, which provides some coercivity at the boundary. The second term on the right hand side satisfies for the operators
\mathcal{L}
we consider the spectral gap condition (
3.3
): therefore this energy estimate fails to provide the coercivity on the component
\rho[f]
of the unknown, which is “missed” by (
3.3
).
Using the
spectral gap inequality (
3.3
),
we thus deduce

(3.4)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\int_{\partial{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\int_{\Omega_{v}}|v_{1}|f(t,0,x^{\prime},v)^{2}M^{-1}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}

\displaystyle\qquad-2\lambda_{m}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\|f-\rho[f]M\|_{\mathsf{L}_{v}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x.

3.3.
Modifying the norm

We define a new norm, equivalent to the weighted norm
\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})
on
\Omega
as follows. Let
\mathbf{R}
be the operator defined by
u=\mathbf{R}\eta
, where
u
is the unique solution to the elliptic problem on the half space with Dirichlet conditions

(3.5)

u\in\mathsf{H}^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}),\quad(1-\Delta_{x})u=\eta\hbox{ in }{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}.

More precisely,
\mathbf{R}\eta\in\mathsf{H}^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})
satisfies

(3.6)

\int(\mathbf{R}\eta)\varphi+\int\nabla(\mathbf{R}\eta)\cdot\nabla\varphi=\int\eta\varphi,\quad\forall\,\varphi\in\mathsf{H}^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}).

Then, for some
\epsilon>0
small (to be chosen later), we define

(3.7)

|\hskip-1.13791pt|\hskip-1.13791pt|f|\hskip-1.13791pt|\hskip-1.13791pt|^{2}:=\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2}+\epsilon\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\right)\cdot\iota[f]\,{\mathrm{d}}x.

By standard elliptic techniques (see for instance
[
10
, Theorem 9.25]
for the second estimate), we have

(3.8)

\|\mathbf{R}\eta\|_{\mathsf{H}^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+})}\lesssim\|\eta\|_{\mathsf{H}^{-1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+})}\quad\text{ and }\quad\|\mathbf{R}\eta\|_{\mathsf{H}^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+})}+\|\nabla_{x}^{2}\mathbf{R}\eta\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})}\lesssim\|\eta\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+})},

where
\nabla_{x}^{2}
denotes the matrix of second-order derivatives, so that
\mathbf{R}:\mathsf{L}^{2}\to\mathsf{H}^{1}_{0}\cap\mathsf{H}^{2}
and
\mathbf{R}:\mathsf{H}^{-1}\to\mathsf{H}^{1}_{0}
.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, using either the boundedness of
\Omega_{v}
or the decay of
M
when
\Omega_{v}
is unbounded, we have

(3.9)

\|\rho[f]\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+})}^{2}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\|\iota[f]\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+})}^{2}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2},

so that, applying the above first ellipticity estimate,

\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\right)\cdot\iota[f]\,{\mathrm{d}}x\right|\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2}.

The norm
|\hskip-1.13791pt|\hskip-1.13791pt|\cdot|\hskip-1.13791pt|\hskip-1.13791pt|
is thus equivalent to the
\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})
norm for
\epsilon>0
small enough: we next choose
\epsilon>0
such that

(3.10)

\frac{1}{2}\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}\leq|\hskip-1.13791pt|\hskip-1.13791pt|f|\hskip-1.13791pt|\hskip-1.13791pt|\leq 2\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}.

3.4.
Time-evolution of the modified norm

We differentiate in time the modified norm along the flow and we use (
3.4
) in order to get

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}|\hskip-1.13791pt|\hskip-1.13791pt|f|\hskip-1.13791pt|\hskip-1.13791pt|^{2}=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2}+\epsilon\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\right)\cdot\iota[f]\,{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle\leq-\int_{\partial{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\int_{\Omega_{v}}|v_{1}|f(t,0,x^{\prime},v)^{2}M^{-1}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}-2\lambda_{m}\|f-\rho[f]M\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\epsilon\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[({\mathcal{L}}-v\cdot\nabla_{x})f]\right)\cdot\iota[f]\,{\mathrm{d}}x+\epsilon\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\right)\cdot\iota[({\mathcal{L}}-v\cdot\nabla_{x})f]\,{\mathrm{d}}x.

Denoting

(3.11)

f^{\bot}:=f-\rho[f]M,

we then calculate

\displaystyle\rho[(\mathcal{L}-v\cdot\nabla_{x})f]=-\nabla_{x}\cdot\iota[f^{\bot}],

\displaystyle\iota[(\mathcal{L}-v\cdot\nabla_{x})f]=\iota[\mathcal{L}f^{\bot}]-\nabla_{x}:\int_{\Omega_{v}}(v\otimes v)\,f(v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v,

where we have used the second and last properties in (
3.2
) to deduce

(3.12)

\iota[f]=\iota[f^{\bot}],\quad\mathcal{L}[f]=\mathcal{L}[f^{\bot}].\quad\color[rgb]{1,0,0}

As a consequence, we find

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[(\mathcal{L}-v\cdot\nabla_{x})f]\right)\cdot\iota[f]\,{\mathrm{d}}x=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\nabla_{x}\cdot\iota[f^{\bot}]\right)\cdot\iota[f^{\bot}]\,{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\mathbf{R}\nabla_{x}\cdot\iota[f^{\bot}]\right)\cdot\left(\nabla_{x}\cdot\iota[f^{\bot}]\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\left\|\mathbf{R}\nabla_{x}\cdot\iota[f^{\bot}]\right\|^{2}_{\mathsf{H}^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+})}

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\|\nabla_{x}\cdot\iota[f^{\bot}]\|_{\mathsf{H}^{-1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})}^{2}\lesssim\|\iota[f^{\bot}]\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})}^{2}\lesssim\|f^{\bot}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2},

where we have used (
3.6
) (with
\eta:=\nabla_{x}\cdot\iota[f^{\bot}]
and
\varphi:=\mathbf{R}\eta
) in the last equality and (
3.8
) in the first inequality.
In order to control the last term in the above estimate on the time evolution of the modified norm,
we write

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\right)\cdot\iota[(\mathcal{L}-v\cdot\nabla_{x})f]\,{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\right)\cdot\iota[\mathcal{L}f]\,{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\right)\cdot\left(\nabla_{x}:\int_{\Omega_{v}}(v\otimes v)\,f(v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x,

and we deduce

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\right)\cdot\iota[(\mathcal{L}-v\cdot\nabla_{x})f]\,{\mathrm{d}}x\lesssim\|\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})}\|f^{\bot}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\nabla^{2}_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\right):\left(\int_{\Omega_{v}}(v\otimes v)\,f(v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x+\int_{\partial{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\partial_{x_{1}}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\left(\int_{\Omega_{v}}v_{1}^{2}f\,{\mathrm{d}}v\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime},

where we have used the identity
\iota[\mathcal{L}f]=-c_{\mathcal{L}}\iota[f^{\bot}]
(coming from (
3.1
) and (
3.12
) together), the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the second estimate in (
3.9
) in order to estimate the first term and we have just performed one integration by parts for dealing with the second term.
We then decompose
f
thanks to (
3.11
) in the second term of the right hand side:

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\right)\cdot\iota[(\mathcal{L}-v\cdot\nabla_{x})f]\,{\mathrm{d}}x\lesssim\|\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})}\|f^{\bot}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\Delta_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\right)\rho[f]\,{\mathrm{d}}x+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\nabla^{2}_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\right):\left(\int_{\Omega_{v}}(v\otimes v)f^{\bot}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\int_{\partial{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\partial_{x_{1}}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\left(\int_{\Omega_{v}}v_{1}^{2}f\,{\mathrm{d}}v\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}.

To control the boundary term, we use the following trace and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities

\displaystyle\int_{\partial{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\partial_{x_{1}}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\right)^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}\lesssim\|\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\|^{2}_{\mathsf{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})},

\displaystyle\int_{\partial{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\int_{\Omega_{v}}v_{1}^{2}f\,{\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}\lesssim\int_{\partial{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\int_{\Omega_{v}}|v_{1}|f(t,0,x^{\prime},v)^{2}M^{-1}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime},

and we deduce overall

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\right)\cdot\iota[(\mathcal{L}-v\cdot\nabla_{x})f]\,{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\|_{\mathsf{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})}\left(\|f^{\bot}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2}+\int_{\partial{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\int_{\Omega_{v}}|v_{1}|f(t,0,x^{\prime},v)^{2}M^{-1}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\|\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\|_{\mathsf{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f^{\bot}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2}+\int_{\partial{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\int_{\Omega_{v}}|v_{1}|f(t,0,x^{\prime},v)^{2}M^{-1}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}-\|\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\|_{\mathsf{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})}^{2}.

Combining all terms, we deduce for
\epsilon
small enough

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}|\hskip-1.13791pt|\hskip-1.13791pt|f|\hskip-1.13791pt|\hskip-1.13791pt|^{2}\lesssim-\|f^{\bot}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2}-\int_{\partial\Omega}|v_{1}|f(t,0,x^{\prime},v)^{2}M^{-1}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}-\epsilon\|\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\|_{\mathsf{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})}^{2}.

Introducing the notations

(3.13)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Z}}:=|\hskip-1.13791pt|\hskip-1.13791pt|f|\hskip-1.13791pt|\hskip-1.13791pt|^{2},\quad{\mathcal{Y}}:=\|f^{\bot}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2}+\|\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho[f]\|_{\mathsf{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})}^{2}

and throwing away the boundary contribution,
we obtain in particular

(3.14)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}{\mathcal{Z}}\lesssim-{\mathcal{Y}}.

3.5.
Nash improved inequality

We now use a Nash inequality adapted to our setting in order to deduce from (
3.14
) a decay estimate on
\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2}
.
First notice that, given
u=\mathbf{R}\rho
, Kato’s inequality implies

-\Delta_{x}|u|+|u|\leq\text{sign}(u)\left(-\Delta_{x}u+u\right)=\text{sign}(u)\rho\leq|\rho|.

Moreover the boundary conditions imply

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}}x_{1}\Delta_{x}|u|\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}}x_{1}\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}|u|\,{\mathrm{d}}x=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}}\partial_{x_{1}}|u|\,{\mathrm{d}}x=0.

Therefore, we deduce

(3.15)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}}x_{1}|u|\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}}x_{1}\left(-\Delta_{x}|u|+|u|\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}}x_{1}|\rho|\,{\mathrm{d}}x.

Meanwhile, we have

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}}\rho^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left(u-\Delta_{x}u\right)^{2}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}}\left[u^{2}+(\Delta_{x}u)^{2}+2|\nabla_{x}u|^{2}\right]\,{\mathrm{d}}x.

Plugging the improved Nash inequality (
2.5
) on
u
in the previous line, we get

\displaystyle\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}_{x}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+})}^{2}\leq 2\|\nabla_{x}u\|_{\mathsf{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+})}^{2}+\|u\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+},x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}^{\frac{4}{d+4}}\|\nabla_{x}u\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+})}^{2-\frac{4}{d+4}}.

This last estimate and the Kato estimate (
3.15
) together imply

(3.16)

\displaystyle\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}_{x}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+})}^{2}\lesssim\|\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho\|_{\mathsf{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d})}^{2}+\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+},x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}^{\frac{4}{d+4}}\|\nabla_{x}\mathbf{R}\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}_{x}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+})}^{2-\frac{4}{d+4}},

for any
\rho\in\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+},x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x)\cap\mathsf{H}^{-1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+})
.
Recalling the notations (
3.13
), using the equivalence (
3.10
)
together with the notation and the elementary identities

\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2}=\|f^{\bot}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{2}+\|\rho[f]\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}_{x}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+})}^{2}

and

{\mathfrak{M}}:=\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}=\|\rho[f]\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x)},

the improved Nash type estimate (
3.16
) applied to
\rho:=\rho[f]
implies

(3.17)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Z}}\lesssim{\mathcal{Y}}+\mathfrak{M}^{\frac{4}{d+4}}{\mathcal{Y}}^{1-\frac{2}{d+4}}.

For
m\geq 0
, we define

\Phi_{m}(y):=y+m^{\frac{4}{d+4}}y^{\frac{d+2}{d+4}}\geq y,

and the inverse function
\Psi_{m}:=\Phi^{-1}_{m}
which is well defined because
y\mapsto\Phi_{m}(y)
is strictly increasing. In particular,
\Psi_{m}
is strictly increasing and
\Psi_{m}(z)\leq z
for any
z\geq 0
.
We observe that for
z\leq z_{0}
, we have
\Psi_{m}(z)\leq\Psi_{m}(z_{0})\leq z_{0}
, next

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=\Psi_{m}(z)+m^{\frac{4}{d+4}}\Psi_{m}(z)^{1-{\frac{2}{d+4}}}\leq\Psi_{m}(z_{0})^{\frac{2}{d+4}}\Psi_{m}(z)^{1-{\frac{2}{d+4}}}+m^{\frac{4}{d+4}}\Psi_{m}(z)^{1-{\frac{2}{d+4}}}

\displaystyle=(\Psi_{m}(z_{0})^{\frac{2}{d+4}}+m^{\frac{4}{d+4}})\Psi_{m}(z)^{\frac{d+2}{d+4}}

and thus

\displaystyle(z_{0}^{\frac{2}{d+4}}+m^{\frac{4}{d+4}})^{-\frac{d+4}{d+2}}z^{1+\frac{2}{d+2}}\leq\Psi_{m}(z).

Together with the elementary inequality

\displaystyle(z_{0}+m^{2})^{-\frac{2}{d+2}}\lesssim(z_{0}^{\frac{2}{d+4}}+m^{\frac{4}{d+4}})^{-\frac{d+4}{d+2}},

we have established

\displaystyle(z_{0}+m^{2})^{-\frac{2}{d+2}}z^{1+\frac{2}{d+2}}\lesssim\Psi_{m}(z).

From that estimate and (
3.17
), we obtain the functional inequality

(3.18)

\displaystyle({\mathcal{Z}}_{0}+\mathfrak{M}^{2})^{-\frac{2}{d+2}}{\mathcal{Z}}^{1+\frac{2}{d+2}}\lesssim{\mathcal{Y}},

conditionally to
{\mathcal{Z}}\leq{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}
. Coming back to the differential inequality (
3.14
), which in particular implies
{\mathcal{Z}}(t)\leq{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}
,
and using (
3.18
), we conclude with

(3.19)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}{\mathcal{Z}}\lesssim-({\mathcal{Z}}_{0}+\mathfrak{M}^{2})^{-\frac{2}{d+2}}{\mathcal{Z}}^{1+\frac{2}{d+2}}.

At that stage, it turns out clearly that one needs an estimate on
\mathfrak{M}
. It happens that the way we control on the moment
\mathfrak{M}
depends on the velocity space
\Omega_{v}
. The two last subsections are dedicated to that issue.

3.6.
The bounded velocities case

We first consider the case where the velocities are bounded, i.e.
\Omega_{v}=B(0,1)
or
\Omega_{v}=\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, which corresponds to the relaxation operator or the Laplace-Beltrami operator.
In that case, a simple uniform bound is available.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any solution
f
to the kinetic equation (
1.1
) associated to the relaxation operator in the velocity domain
\Omega_{v}=B(0,1)
or to the Laplace-Beltrami operator, there holds

\forall\,t\geq 0,\quad\|f(t,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(\Omega,\,x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}\lesssim\|f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(\Omega,(1+x_{1})\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}.

Proof of
Lemma

3.1
.

We use the function
\phi:\Omega_{v}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
defined in (
3.1
), so that here
\phi\in\mathsf{L}^{\infty}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\sharp}\left(\phi\right)(v)=-v_{1}
.

We compute

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}\left(x_{1}+\phi(v)+c_{0}\right)f(t,x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(x_{1}+\phi(v)+c_{0}\right)\left(\mathcal{L}-v\cdot\nabla_{x}\right)f(t,x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\phi(v)\mathcal{L}f\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}x-\int_{\Omega}\left(x_{1}+\phi(v)+c_{0}\right)\left(v\cdot\nabla_{x}f(t,x,v)\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\left(\phi(v)+c_{0}\right)v_{1}f(t,x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}\,{\mathrm{d}}v,

by using

\int_{\Omega}(x_{1}+c_{0})\mathcal{L}f=0\quad\int_{\Omega}\phi(v)\mathcal{L}f=-\int_{\Omega}v_{1}f,\quad\text{and }-\int_{\Omega}x_{1}v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\int_{\Omega}v_{1}f.

If
c_{0}:=\|\phi\|_{\infty}
, we have
\phi+c_{0}\geq 0
and since
f=0
for
v_{1}\geq 0
on the boundary, we get

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}\left(x_{1}+\phi(v)+c_{0}\right)f(t,x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v\leq 0,

so that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}x_{1}f(t,x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\left(x_{1}+\phi(v)+c_{0}\right)f(t,x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\left(x_{1}+\phi(v)+c_{0}\right)f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}(x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v\leq\int_{\Omega}\left(x_{1}+2c_{0}\right)f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}(x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

which concludes the proof of (
3.1
).
∎

We are now able to establish (
1.6
) in the bounded velocities case.
Combining (
3.19
) and
Lemma

3.1
, we indeed have

(3.20)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}{\mathcal{Z}}\lesssim-({\mathcal{Z}}_{0}+\|f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(\Omega,(1+x_{1})\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}^{2})^{-\frac{2}{d+2}}{\mathcal{Z}}^{1+\frac{2}{d+2}},

which is the same differential inequality (
2.6
) as for the heat equation. We then conclude to (
1.6
) with the same comparison argument.

3.7.
The unbounded velocities case

We consider
\mathcal{L}
the linear relaxation operator or the Fokker-Planck operator in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and we recall that in that cases, the equilibrium function
M
is the normalised Gaussian on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, so that
M(v)=\prod_{i}\mathfrak{M}_{1}(v_{i})
, with
\mathfrak{M}_{1}
the normalised Gaussian on
{\mathbb{R}}
. We start by establishing a time depending control of the first moment.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any nonnegative solution
f
to the kinetic equation (
1.1
) associated to the relaxation operator
or the Fokker-Planck operator, both in the velocity domain
\Omega_{v}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and for any nonnegative
C^{1}
function
u
on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
increasing fast enough so that
t\mapsto\mathfrak{M}_{1}(u(t))\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
,
there holds

(3.21)

\|f(t,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}\lesssim\|f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}+\|f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}+\int_{0}^{t}u^{\prime}(s)\|f(s)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(\Omega)}\,{\mathrm{d}}s,

for any
t\geq 0
, and in particular,

(3.22)

\|f(t,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}\lesssim\|f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}+\|f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}+u(t)\|f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(\Omega)},

for any
t\geq 0
.

Proof of
Lemma

3.2
.

Let us consider
f
and
u
as defined in the statement of the Lemma.
Using the cancellation

\int_{\Omega}x_{1}(-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f)+\int_{\Omega}(x_{1}+v_{1}+u)\mathcal{L}f=\int_{\Omega}v_{1}f+\int_{\Omega}f\mathcal{L}^{\sharp}v_{1}=0,

thanks to an integration by parts and (
3.1
), we may then calculate

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}\left(x_{1}+v_{1}+u\right)f(t,x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v=\int_{\partial\Omega}\left(v_{1}^{2}+uv_{1}\right)f(t,0,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v+u^{\prime}\|f(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(\Omega)},

where we have used the positivity of
u^{\prime}
and
f
.
Integrating in time, we deduce

(3.23)

\|f(t,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}\leq-\int_{\Omega}v_{1}f(t,x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v\\
+\int_{\Omega}(x_{1}+u(0)+|v_{1}|)f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}(x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v\\
+\int_{0}^{t}u^{\prime}(s)\|f(s)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(\Omega)}\,{\mathrm{d}}s+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left(v_{1}^{2}+u(t)v_{1}\right)f(t,0,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v,

where we have used the positivity of
uf
.
We estimate each term separately. First

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}v_{1}f(t,x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v=\int_{\Omega_{v}}v_{1}^{2}f(t,0,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v-\int_{\Omega}v_{1}f(t,x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v,

from which we deduce

\int_{\Omega}v_{1}f(t,x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v=\int_{\Omega}v_{1}f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}(x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{\Omega_{v}}v_{1}^{2}f(s,0,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v\right)e^{-(t-s)}\,{\mathrm{d}}s.

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies

\left(\int_{\Omega_{v}}v_{1}^{2}f(s,0,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{2}\leq\left(\int_{\Omega_{v}}|v_{1}|^{3}M(v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v\right)\left(\int_{\Omega_{v}}|v_{1}|f^{2}(s,0,v)\frac{\,{\mathrm{d}}v}{M}\right),

and since (

) implies that

\int_{\Omega}v_{1}f(t,x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v\lesssim\|f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(|v_{1}|\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}+\|f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})},

which bounds the first term in (

).

To control the last boundary term in (

), we use that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{v_{1}\leq-u(s)}|v_{1}|^{3}M(v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{v_{1}\leq-u(s)}|v_{1}|^{3}\mathfrak{M}_{1}(v_{1})\,{\mathrm{d}}v_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}s

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{M}_{1}(u(s))^{\frac{1}{2}}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\int_{v_{1}\leq 0}|v_{1}|^{3}\mathfrak{M}_{1}(v_{1})^{\frac{1}{2}}\,{\mathrm{d}}v_{1}<+\infty

holds because of the condition imposed on
u
, and we estimate

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left(v_{1}^{2}+u(t)v_{1}\right)f(t,0,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}s\\
\leq\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{v_{1}\leq-u(s)}|v_{1}|^{3}M(v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}s\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v_{1}|f(s,0,v)^{2}M^{-1}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}s\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
\lesssim\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v_{1}|f(s,0,v)^{2}M^{-1}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}s\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\|f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})},

by using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the previous inequality and (
3.4
) integrated in time as we did for estimating the first term.
We immediately conclude to (
3.21
) by gathering (
3.23
) with the two estimates we have established above and the second estimate in (
3.9
).
The last term in (
3.22
) can be immediately bounded by

\int_{0}^{t}u^{\prime}(s)\|f(s)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(\Omega)}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\leq u(t)\|f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(\Omega)},

where we have used that the
\mathsf{L}^{1}
norm is non-increasing and that

is non-negative and non-decreasing. We thus deduce (
3.22
) from (
3.21
) and that last inequality.
∎

We now prove the decay rate (
1.5
) in two steps thanks to a bootstrap argument.

Step 1.
We define
u(t):=\lfloor t\rceil^{{\varepsilon}}
,
{\varepsilon}>0
, and we observe that it satisfies the stated requirement in
Lemma

3.2
. From (
3.22
) in
Lemma

3.2
, we thus first deduce the control

\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+},\quad\|f(t,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v)}\lesssim\mathfrak{A}_{0}\lfloor t\rceil^{{\varepsilon}},

with

. Plugging that information in (

) and using a comparison argument for ordinary differential equations, we deduce

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Z}}(t)

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathfrak{A}_{0}^{2}\,\lfloor t\rceil^{-1-\frac{d}{2}+2{\varepsilon}},

with say

, which is a first, but not optimal, decay.

Step 2.
As a consequence of the interpolation inequality (in dimension

),

\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v)}^{\frac{1}{3}}\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+})}^{\frac{2}{3}}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v)}^{\frac{1}{3}}\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})}^{\frac{2}{3}},

we deduce from the two estimates yet established in the first step, the following decay on the mass

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}}\lesssim[\mathfrak{A}_{0}\lfloor t\rceil^{{\varepsilon}}]^{\frac{1}{3}}[\mathfrak{A}_{0}^{2}\lfloor t\rceil^{-1-\frac{d}{2}+2{\varepsilon}}]^{\frac{1}{3}}\lesssim\mathfrak{A}_{0}\lfloor t\rceil^{-\frac{1}{3}-\frac{d}{6}+{\varepsilon}},

by choosing

. Using now (
3.21
) in
Lemma

3.2
,
we obtain

(3.24)

\|f(t,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}\lesssim\mathfrak{A}_{0}+\mathfrak{A}_{0}\int_{0}^{t}s^{{\varepsilon}-1}\lfloor s\rceil^{-\frac{1}{3}-\frac{d}{6}+{\varepsilon}}\,{\mathrm{d}}s,\lesssim\mathfrak{A}_{0},

by choosing

. We may finally proceed exactly as in the bounded velocities case in Section
3.6
: plugging
that information in (

) and using the ODE comparison argument, we deduce

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Z}}(t)

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathfrak{A}_{0}^{2}\,\lfloor t\rceil^{-1-\frac{d}{2}},

what is nothing but the claimed rate (
1.5
).

.

It is worth observing that the same conclusion holds when the equilibrium profile satisfies

,

, which is the case when

is uniformly bounded and satisfies the moment condition in (

).

4.
Pointwise decay

In this section, we prove that the integral decay (
1.5
) implies the pointwise decay (
1.6
) by expanding upon the semigroup extension techniques introduced in
[
21
]
and developed further in
[
31
,
33
,
32
,
25
]
. The new idea as compared to these works is the use of an approximate duality argument based on the factorisation formula.

4.1.
The dual estimate

We start reformulating the estimate (
1.5
) in a semigroup language to obtain a dual estimate. We formally denote by
S(t)
the semigroup associated to the kinetic equation (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) (defined for instance on the “core” of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}
functions), and consider bounds on the operator norm
S(t)
in various functional spaces (in which the core is dense). For that purpose, we introduce the weight functions

\omega_{0}:=1,\quad\omega_{1}:=M^{-\frac{1}{2}},\quad\omega_{2}:=M^{-1}.

In particular,
\omega_{1}
is always an admissible weight function (whatever the collision operator
{\mathcal{L}}
is). Recalling (
1.3
), we rephrase (
1.5
) as follows

(4.1)

\|S(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}_{1,\omega_{0}}\cap\mathsf{L}^{2}_{0,\omega_{1}}\to\mathsf{L}^{2}_{0,\omega_{1}}}\lesssim\lfloor t\rceil^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{d}{4}}.

We denote by
S^{*}(t)
the adjoint semigroup associated to the
\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})
duality product. It is easy to see that
S^{*}
is the semigroup associated to the dual problem

(4.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}g-v\cdot\nabla_{x}g=\mathcal{L}g\quad\hbox{in}\quad(0,\infty)\times\Omega,\\[5.69054pt]
g=0\quad\hbox{in}\quad(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega_{-},\end{array}\right.

where
\partial\Omega_{-}:=(\{0\}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1})\times(\Omega_{v}\cap\{v_{1}<0\})
and where we have used that
v\cdot\nabla_{x}
is antisymmetric and
{\mathcal{L}}
is symmetric in
\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})
. Since (
4.17
) has the same structure as (
1.1
)-(
1.2
), the analysis of Section
3
applies and any solution
g
to (
4.17
) associated to the initial datum
g_{\rm in}
satisfies

\|g(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}_{0,\omega_{1}}}\lesssim\lfloor t\rceil^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{d}{4}}\left(\|g_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}_{1,\omega_{0}}}+\|g_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}_{0,\omega_{1}}}\right),

or equivalently

\|S^{*}(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}_{1,\omega_{0}}\cap\mathsf{L}^{2}_{0,\omega_{1}}\to\mathsf{L}^{2}_{0,\omega_{1}}}\lesssim\lfloor t\rceil^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{d}{4}}.

By
\mathsf{L}^{2}(M^{-1})
duality, we deduce

(4.3)

\|S(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}_{0,\omega_{1}}\to\mathsf{L}^{\infty}_{-1,\omega_{2}}+\mathsf{L}^{2}_{0,\omega_{1}}}\lesssim\lfloor t\rceil^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{d}{4}}

with the following norm on a sum
X+Y
of normed spaces:

\|\zeta\|_{X+Y}:=\inf\Big\{\|x\|_{X}+\|y\|_{Y}\ :\ \zeta=x+y,\ x\in X,\ y\in Y\Big\}.

4.2.
The extension argument

The core idea is to introduce a splitting

\mathcal{L}-v\cdot\nabla_{x}=\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B},

so that the semigroup
S_{\mathcal{B}}
associated with the generator
{\mathcal{B}}
and the boundary condition (
1.2
) decay fast as
t
goes to
\infty
, and the time-dependent operator
{\mathcal{A}}S_{\mathcal{B}}(t){\mathcal{A}}
has regularisation properties. The splitting will depend on each case and the precise form will be introduced in Sections
4.3
,
4.4
and
4.5
.

By iterating the Duhamel formulas

S=S_{\mathcal{B}}+(S_{\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{A})\star S=S_{\mathcal{B}}+S\star(\mathcal{A}S_{\mathcal{B}}),

where the symbol
\star
denotes the time convolution between two functions defined on the positive half real line, we get (see for instance
[
25
, (1.28)]
)

(4.4)

\forall\,n\geq 1,\quad S=\underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{2n-1}(S_{\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{A})^{\star j}\star S_{\mathcal{B}}}_{S_{1}}+\underbrace{(S_{\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{A})^{\star n}\star S\star(\mathcal{A}S_{\mathcal{B}})^{\star n}}_{S_{2}}.

Note that separately each term of this decomposition does not satisfy the semigroup property in general. We also stress the fact that the factor
S_{2}
has a symmetric number of prefactors
\mathcal{A}S_{\mathcal{B}}
and
S_{\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{A}
. The core decay and estimate of
S_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\mathcal{A}
is

Proposition 4.1
.

For any admissible weight function
\omega
, there are

and

such that

(4.5)

\displaystyle\|S_{\mathcal{B}}(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}_{1,\omega}\to\mathsf{L}^{1}_{1,\omega}}+\|S_{\mathcal{B}}(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{\infty}_{-1,\omega}\to\mathsf{L}^{\infty}_{-1,\omega}}\lesssim e^{-\mathfrak{a}t},

(4.6)

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{A}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}_{1,\omega}\to\mathsf{L}^{1}_{1,\omega}}+\|{\mathcal{A}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{\infty}_{-1,\omega}\to\mathsf{L}^{\infty}_{-1,\omega}}\lesssim 1,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\left\|({\mathcal{A}}S_{\mathcal{B}})^{\star n}\right\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}_{0,\omega}\to\mathsf{L}^{2}_{0,\omega_{1}}}+\left\|(S_{\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{A})^{\star n}\ \right\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}_{0,\omega_{1}}\to\mathsf{L}^{\infty}_{0,\omega}}\lesssim e^{-\mathfrak{a}t},

This proposition has to be shown case by case, which we will do in the forthcoming subsections. Let us prove
Theorem

1.1
-(
1.6
), taking
Proposition

4.1
for granted. The proof does not depend on the explicit structures of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
but only on the above estimates.

Proof of (
1.6
) assuming Proposition

We first write

\displaystyle S(t)

\displaystyle=S\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)S\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=S_{1}\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)S_{1}\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)+S_{1}\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)S_{2}\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)+S_{2}\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)S_{1}\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)+S_{2}\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)S_{2}\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)

which proves (

Step 1.
Combining (

4.3.
The linear relaxation equation

In this subsection, we focus on the case of the linear relaxation operator as defined by (1) in Section

. We then define

(4.16)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}f:=\rho[f]M,

and
S_{\mathcal{B}}
the semigroup associated to the evolution equation

(4.17)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}f=-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f-f\quad\hbox{in}\quad(0,\infty)\times\Omega,\\[5.69054pt]
f=0\quad\hbox{in}\quad(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega_{+}.\end{array}\right.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
p\in[1,+\infty]
,

and any admissible weight function
\omega:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to(0,\infty)
,
the semigroup
S_{\mathcal{B}}
satisfies

(4.18)

\forall\,t\geq 0,\quad\|S_{\mathcal{B}}(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{p}_{k,\omega}\to\mathsf{L}^{p}_{k,\omega}}\lesssim e^{-\kappa_{p,k}t},

with
\kappa_{p,-1}:=1-\tfrac{1}{p}
,
\kappa_{p,0}:=1
,
\kappa_{p,1}:=\tfrac{1}{p}
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.3
.

Let
f\geq 0
be a solution to (
4.17
) and
p\in(1,\infty)
. We compute

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}f^{p}\omega^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}f^{p-1}\left(-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f-f\right)\omega^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(vf^{p}\right)\omega^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v-\int_{\Omega}f^{p}\omega^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\int_{\partial\Omega}v_{1}f^{p}\omega^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}v-\int f^{p}\omega^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v\leq-\int f^{p}\omega^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v,

by integrating by parts and using the boundary condition, which implies (
4.18
) for
k=0
. When
k=1
, we first estimate

\frac{v\cdot\nabla_{x}\lfloor x\rceil^{p}}{p}=v\cdot x\lfloor x\rceil^{p-2}\leq\frac{1}{p}\lfloor v\rceil^{p}+\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\lfloor x\rceil^{p}

and we compute

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}\lfloor x\rceil^{p}f^{p}\omega^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v=-\int_{\Omega}\lfloor x\rceil^{kp}\frac{v\cdot\nabla_{x}\left(f^{p}\right)}{p}\omega^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v-\int_{\Omega}\lfloor x\rceil^{kp}f^{p}\omega^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{p}\int_{\partial\Omega}v_{1}\lfloor x\rceil^{p}f^{p}\omega^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}\,{\mathrm{d}}v+\int_{\Omega}\frac{v\cdot\nabla_{x}\left(\lfloor x\rceil^{p}\right)}{p}f^{p}\omega^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v-\int_{\Omega}\lfloor x\rceil^{p}f^{p}\omega^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}f^{p}\lfloor v\rceil^{p}\omega^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v-\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}\lfloor x\rceil^{p}f^{p}\omega^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v,

where we have used the boundary conditions. By the Gronwall lemma and the previous estimate in the case
k=0
with the weight
\lfloor v\rceil\omega
, we deduce

\int_{\Omega}\lfloor x\rceil^{p}f(t,x,v)^{p}\omega^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v\leq e^{-t}\int_{\Omega}\lfloor x\rceil^{p}f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}^{p}\omega^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v+e^{-t}\left(\frac{1-e^{(1-p)t}}{p-1}\right)\int_{\Omega}\lfloor v\rceil^{p}f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}^{p}\omega^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v.

Together with the estimate in the case
k=0
with the weight
\lfloor v\rceil\omega
, we conclude to (
4.18
) for
k=1
. Since this holds for any weight function
\omega
, we obtain the case

by duality.
∎

Proof of Lemma

For

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{A}}f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{p}_{1,\omega^{\prime}}}

4.4.
The kinetic Fokker-Planck equation

In this subsection, we assume that
{\mathcal{L}}
is the Fokker-Planck operator, as defined by (2) in Section
1.2
, and we prove Proposition
4.1
in that case.
The splitting is now

\mathcal{L}-v\cdot\nabla_{x}=\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B},\quad\mathcal{A}f:=\mathfrak{C}\chi_{R}f,

with
\mathfrak{C}
and
R
positive constants to be chosen later,
\chi_{R}:=\chi(\cdot/R)
, with
\chi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
so that
{\bf 1}_{B(0,1)}\leq\chi\leq{\bf 1}_{B(0,2)}
,
and the semigroup
S_{\mathcal{B}}
is associated to the kinetic equation

(4.24)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}f=-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\Delta_{v}f+\nabla_{v}\cdot(vf)-\mathfrak{C}\chi_{R}f,\quad\hbox{on}\quad(0,\infty)\times\Omega,\\[8.53581pt]
f=0\quad\hbox{on}\quad(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega_{+},\qquad f(0)=f_{0}\quad\hbox{in}\quad\Omega.\end{array}\right.

We first establish the decay estimate (
4.5
) in Proposition
4.1
.

Lemma 4.6
.

There exist positive
R
and
\mathfrak{C}
large enough such that any solution
f
to the modified kinetic Fokker-Planck equation
(
4.24
) satisfies
the decay

(4.25)

\|f(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{p}_{k,\omega}}\lesssim e^{-2t}\|f_{0}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{p}_{k,\omega}},

for any
p\in[1,\infty]
,
k\in\{-1,0,1\}
and admissible weight function
\omega
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.6
.

We only deal with the case
k=1
, since the cases
k=0
and
k=-1
can be tackled in a similar way. Let
f\geq 0
be a solution to (
4.24
) and
p\in(1,\infty)
.
We compute

\frac{1}{p}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{p}_{1,\omega}}^{p}=\int_{\Omega}(-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+{\mathcal{L}}f-\mathfrak{C}\chi_{R}f)\,f^{p-1}\,(\lfloor x_{1}\rceil\omega)^{p}dv\,{\mathrm{d}}x.

On the one hand, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f)\,f^{p-1}\,(\lfloor x_{1}\rceil\omega)^{p}dv\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}\frac{x_{1}v_{1}}{\lfloor x_{1}\rceil^{2}}(f\lfloor x_{1}\rceil\omega)^{p}dv\,{\mathrm{d}}x,

by performing an integration by parts and using the boundary condition.
On the other hand, from
[
31
, (3.18)]
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(({\mathcal{L}}-\mathfrak{C}\chi_{R})\,f)\,f^{p-1}\,\omega^{p}dv=-(p-1)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\nabla_{v}(f\omega)|^{2}\,(f\omega)^{p-2}\,{\mathrm{d}}v+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(f\omega)^{p}\,\varpi\,{\mathrm{d}}v,

with

(4.26)

\varpi=\varpi_{\omega,p}(v):=\left(1-\frac{2}{p^{\prime}}\right)\frac{\Delta_{v}\omega}{\omega}+\frac{2}{p^{\prime}}\frac{|\nabla_{v}\omega|^{2}}{\omega^{2}}+\frac{d}{p^{\prime}}-v\cdot\frac{\nabla_{v}\omega}{\omega}-\mathfrak{C}\,\chi_{R},

and
p^{\prime}:=\frac{p}{p-1}
stands for the conjugate exponent.
Furthermore, computing

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla_{v}\omega}{\omega}(v)=rsv\lfloor v\rceil^{s-2},\quad\frac{\Delta_{v}\omega}{\omega}(v)=rsd\lfloor x\rceil^{s-2}+rs(s-2)|v|^{2}\lfloor v\rceil^{s-4}+(rs)^{2}|v|^{2}\lfloor v\rceil^{2s-4},

we see that

\varpi=(rs)^{2}\lfloor\cdot\rceil^{2s-2}-(rs)\lfloor\cdot\rceil^{s}+{\mathcal{O}}(1)-\mathfrak{C}\chi_{R}.

Gathering the above pieces of information, we find

(4.27)

\frac{1}{p}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\|f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{p}_{1,\omega}}^{p}\leq-\frac{4(p-1)}{p^{2}}\||\nabla_{v}(f\lfloor x_{1}\rceil\omega)|^{\frac{p}{2}}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}}+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(f\lfloor x_{1}\rceil\omega)^{p}\,\widehat{\varpi}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}x,

with, by choosing
\mathfrak{C}
and
R>0
large enough,

\widehat{\varpi}=|v_{1}|+\varpi\leq-2-\nu\lfloor v\rceil^{s},

where
\nu:=\frac{r-1}{2}>0
if
s=1
,
\nu:=\frac{rs}{2}>0
if
s\in(1,2)
and
\nu:=r(1-2r)>0
if
s=2
. We get (
4.25
) when
p\in(1,\infty)
thanks to the Gronwall lemma. Finally, (
4.25
) holds when
p\in[1,\infty]
by a continuity argument as
p\to 1
and
p\to\infty
.
∎

We split the proof into five steps.

Step 1.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\left\{f^{2}\omega^{2}\left[\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{2}}\frac{1}{\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\lfloor v\rceil^{s}\right]+|\nabla_{v}(f\omega)|^{2}\right\}\varphi^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}t

Step 2.

We next claim that

with

Once more with (

p=1
, we deduce

\|f(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}_{0,\omega}}\lesssim\frac{1}{t^{\Theta/2}}\|f\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}_{0,\omega}}\lesssim\frac{e^{-t}}{t^{\Theta/2}}\|f_{0}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}_{0,\omega}},\quad\forall\,t>0.

This last estimate is half of the proof of (
4.28
). In order to prove the other half part, we may use the Nash duality trick already used in Section
4.1
, and we refer to

[
11
, Section 4.3.]
for details. We may also use an adaptation of the Moser iterative argument
[
35
,
39
]
, and we refer to

[
19
, Section 3.4.]
for details.

Step 5.

Because
{\mathcal{A}}:\mathsf{L}^{2}_{0,\omega}\to\mathsf{L}^{2}_{0,\omega^{\prime}}
for any admissible weight functions
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
, we may combine that information together with (
4.28
) and (
4.25
) for
k=0
,
p=1
and
p=\infty
and thus deduce (
4.7
) with
a_{\mathcal{B}}:=-\frac{1}{2}
and
n:=\lfloor\Theta\rfloor+2
thanks to
[
32
, Proposition 2.5]
.
∎

4.5.
The kinetic Laplace-Beltrami equation

In this section, we assume that
{\mathcal{L}}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator, as defined by (3) in Section
1.2
,
and prove Proposition
4.1
in that case. We recall that in this situation, no weight function is needed since the velocity domain is bounded.

We introduce the splitting

{\mathcal{A}}f:=\mathfrak{D}f,\qquad{\mathcal{B}}f:=\mathcal{L}f-\mathfrak{D}f-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f,

with
\mathfrak{D}
a positive constant to be chosen later and the semigroup
S_{\mathcal{B}}
associated to the kinetic equation

(4.33)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}f=-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\Delta_{v}^{\text{LB}}f-\mathfrak{D}f,\quad\hbox{on}\quad(0,\infty)\times\Omega,\\[8.53581pt]
f=0\quad\hbox{on}\quad(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega_{+},\qquad f(0)=f_{0}\quad\hbox{in}\quad\Omega.\end{array}\right.

We first establish the decay estimate (
4.5
) in Proposition
4.1
.

Lemma 4.9
.

There exists
\mathfrak{D}>0
such that any solution
f
to the modified kinetic Brownian motion equation
(
4.33
) satisfies
the decay

(4.34)

\|f(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{p}_{k}}\lesssim e^{-2t}\|f_{0}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{p}_{k}},

for any
p\in[1,\infty]
,
k\in\{-1,0,1\}
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.9
.

We compute,

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}f^{p}\lfloor x_{1}\rceil^{k}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}pf^{p-1}\left(-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\Delta_{v}^{\text{LB}}f-\mathfrak{D}f\right)\lfloor x_{1}\rceil^{k}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}f^{p}\left(v\cdot\nabla_{x}(\lfloor x_{1}\rceil^{k})-p\mathfrak{D}\lfloor x_{1}\rceil^{k}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}f^{p}\left(-p\mathfrak{D}+|k|\lfloor x_{1}\rceil^{-1}\right)\lfloor x_{1}\rceil^{k}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq\left(|k|-p\mathfrak{D}\right)\int_{\Omega}f^{p}\lfloor x_{1}\rceil^{k}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v,

where we have used the dissipativity of the Laplace-Beltrami operator. The Gronwall lemma allows to deduce

(4.35)

\|f(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{p}_{k}}\lesssim e^{-\left(\mathfrak{D}-\frac{|k|}{p}\right)t}\|f_{0}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{p}_{k}},

and a choice for
\mathfrak{D}
is then clear in order to conclude the proof.
∎

From there, following the same arguments as in the proof of lemma (
4.7
), we have existence of the associated semigroup
S_{\mathcal{B}}
. We do not reproduce the proof. To establish the gain of integrability (
4.7
), we follow the same steps as for the kinetic Fokker-Planck equation – however, since
\Omega_{v}
is a strict submanifold of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the end of the fourth step needs a special treatment.

Lemma 4.10
.

The semigroup
S_{\mathcal{B}}
satisfies the ultracontractivity property

(4.36)

\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\star}^{+},\qquad\|S_{\mathcal{B}}(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}\to\mathsf{L}^{\infty}}\lesssim\frac{e^{-t}}{t^{\Theta}},

for some
\Theta\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\star}^{+}
, and thus (
4.7
) holds for
n=n(\Theta)
large enough.

Sketch of the proof of Lemma
4.10
.

The proof is split into six steps.

Step 1.

Let
f\geq 0
be a solution to (
4.33
),
T>0
and
0\leq\varphi\in\mathcal{D}((0,T))
. Using
Z
given by

Z:=1-\frac{v_{1}}{2}\delta^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\delta(x):=\min(1,x_{1}),

we claim that

(4.37)

\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\left\{f^{2}\left[\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}}+1\right]+|\nabla_{v}^{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f|^{2}\right\}\varphi^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}t\lesssim\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\Omega}f^{2}\left[|\varphi^{\prime}|^{2}+\varphi^{2}\right]\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}t.

This estimate is straightforward by repeating the proof of
Lemma

4.6
with
p=2
and the fact that

-v\cdot\nabla_{x}Z=\frac{1}{4}{v_{1}^{2}}{x_{1}^{-{1}/{2}}}{\bf 1}_{x_{1}\in(0,1)}.

Step 2.

We make use of the following lemma, which proof will be given later,

Lemma 4.11
.

For any function
g:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
, there holds

\int_{\Omega}\frac{g^{2}}{\delta^{\beta}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v\lesssim\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{v}^{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}g|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v+\int_{\Omega}g^{2}\left(\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}}+1\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v,

with and explicit
\beta>0
.

to derive, as in the proof of
Lemma

4.8
, that

(4.38)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\Omega}f^{2}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\delta^{\beta}}+1\Bigr)\varphi^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}t+\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{v}f|^{2}\varphi^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\lesssim\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\Omega}f^{2}[|\varphi^{\prime}|^{2}+\varphi^{2}]\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}t.

Step 3.

We now claim that for
p:=6d/(3d-2)>2
and any
\alpha>p
, there holds

(4.39)

\left\|f\varphi\delta^{\frac{\alpha}{p}}\right\|_{\mathsf{L}^{p}({\mathcal{U}})}\lesssim\|(\varphi+|\varphi^{\prime}|)f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathcal{U}})},

with
{\mathcal{U}}:=(0,T)\times\Omega
. The proof follows the same ideas as for (
4.31
), given in
[
12
]
. It is an interior regularity result, leading to a penalisation close to the boundary. The only thing to comment on is how to obtain an interior regularity result for (
4.33
). This is a little bit specific since the velocity space is a strict submanifold of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

From
[
8
, Proposition 3.5]
, we have that the semigroup associated to the kinetic Laplace-Beltrami operator in the whole space satisfies the following regularisation estimate for
t
small enough,

\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha t\|\nabla_{v}f\|_{2}^{2}+2\beta t^{2}\left\langle\nabla_{v}f,\nabla_{x}f\right\rangle+\gamma t^{3}\|P_{v^{\perp}}\nabla_{x}f\|^{2}_{2}+\delta t^{4}\|\nabla_{x}f\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\|f_{0}\|_{2}^{2},

for explicit constants
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta>0
such that
\beta<\alpha\gamma
. From this, after localisation, one deduces that
S_{\mathcal{B}}(t)
is bounded from
\mathsf{L}^{2}
to
\mathsf{H}^{1}
for
t>0
. This offers by Sobolev embedding an
\mathsf{L}^{2}
to
\mathsf{L}^{p}
estimate for some
p>2
. Since
S_{\mathcal{B}}
has exponential decay in
\mathsf{L}^{p}
, see
Lemma

4.9
, the
\mathsf{L}^{p}
bound runs for all positive times
and (
4.39
) follows.

Step 4.

We next claim that

(4.40)

\left\|f\varphi\right\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathcal{U}})}\lesssim\|(\varphi+|\varphi^{\prime}|)^{2\tfrac{r-1}{r-2}}\varphi^{-\tfrac{r}{r-2}}f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathcal{U}})},

with
r>2
and
{\mathcal{U}}:=(0,T)\times\Omega
.
On the other hand, interpolating (
4.38
) and (
4.39
), there holds

\left\|f\varphi\right\|_{\mathsf{L}^{r}({\mathcal{U}})}\lesssim\|(\varphi+|\varphi^{\prime}|)f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathcal{U}})},

with

\frac{1}{r}:=\frac{1-\theta}{p}+\frac{\theta}{2},\quad\theta:=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta},

To conclude, use Holder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\|(\varphi+|\varphi^{\prime}|)f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathcal{U}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|f\varphi\right\|_{\mathsf{L}^{r}({\mathcal{U}})}^{\frac{r-1}{r}}\|(\varphi+|\varphi^{\prime}|)^{2\tfrac{r-1}{r-2}}\varphi^{-\tfrac{r}{r-2}}f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathcal{U}})}^{\frac{1}{r}}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\varphi+|\varphi^{\prime}|)f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathcal{U}})}^{\frac{r}{r-1}}\|(\varphi+|\varphi^{\prime}|)^{2\tfrac{r-1}{r-2}}\varphi^{-\tfrac{r}{r-2}}f\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathcal{U}})}^{\frac{r-2}{r-1}},

from which we finally deduce (
4.40
).

Step 5.

Combining (
4.40
) with
(
4.34
) for
k=0
and
p=1,2
, we obtain that

\forall\,t>0,\quad\|f(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}}\lesssim\frac{1}{t^{\frac{\Theta}{2}}}\|f_{0}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}},

with
\Theta:=\frac{r}{r-1}\left(\tfrac{1}{r}+1+\tfrac{2}{r-2}\right)
.
Once more with (
4.34
) for
k=0
and
p=1
, we deduce

\|f(t)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}}\lesssim\frac{1}{t^{\frac{\Theta}{2}}}\|f\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}}\lesssim\frac{e^{-t}}{t^{\frac{\Theta}{2}}}\|f_{0}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}},\quad\forall\,t>0.

This last estimate is half of the proof of (
4.28
). The other part (the
\mathsf{L}^{2}\to\mathsf{L}^{\infty}
) may be derived with the Nash duality trick already used in Section
4.1
.

Step 6

Because
{\mathcal{A}}:\mathsf{L}^{2}\to\mathsf{L}^{2}
is bounded, we may combine that information together with (
4.36
) and (
4.34
) for
k=0
,
p=1
and
p=\infty
and thus deduce (
4.7
) with
a_{\mathcal{B}}:=-\frac{1}{2}
and
n:=\lfloor\Theta\rfloor+2
thanks to
[
32
, Proposition 2.5]
.

∎

We end this section with the proof of the interpolation inequality and the
\mathsf{L}^{2}\to\mathsf{L}^{p}
estimate used above.

Proof of Lemma
4.11
.

We start writing

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{g^{2}}{\delta^{\beta}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{g^{2}}{\delta^{\beta}}\mathbf{1}_{\delta\geq\alpha}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v+\int_{\Omega}\frac{g^{2}}{\delta^{\beta}}\mathbf{1}_{|v_{1}|>\delta^{\eta}}\mathbf{1}_{\delta<\alpha}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v+\int_{\Omega}\frac{g^{2}}{\delta^{\beta}}\mathbf{1}_{|v_{1}|\leq\delta^{\eta}}\mathbf{1}_{\delta<\alpha}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha^{\beta}}\int_{\Omega}g^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v+\int_{\Omega}\frac{g^{2}}{\delta^{\beta}}\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{\delta^{2\eta}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v+\int_{\Omega}\frac{g^{2}}{\delta^{\beta}}\mathbf{1}_{|v_{1}|\leq\delta^{\eta}}\mathbf{1}_{\delta<\alpha}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v.

Observe that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}g^{2}\,\mathbf{1}_{|v_{1}|\leq\delta^{\eta}}\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}g^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{\frac{2}{p}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbf{1}_{|v_{1}|\leq\delta^{\eta}}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{1-\frac{2}{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}|g|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}v+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}|\nabla^{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}g|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbf{1}_{|v_{1}|\leq\delta^{\eta}}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{1-\frac{2}{p}},

using the Sobolev inequality on the sphere, with
p=\frac{2d}{d-2}
if
d>2
and
p=4
if
d=2
. Take
\alpha:=2^{-1/\eta}
, and estimate, for
x_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
,

\frac{1}{\delta^{\beta}}\mathbf{1}_{\delta<\alpha}\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbf{1}_{|v_{1}|\leq\delta^{\eta}}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\lesssim\delta^{\eta\left(1-\frac{2}{p}\right)-\beta}\mathbf{1}_{\delta<\alpha}\lesssim\alpha^{\eta\left(1-\frac{2}{p}\right)-\beta}.

Taking the parameters

\beta=\eta\left(1-\frac{2}{p}\right),\qquad\beta+2\eta=\frac{1}{2},

the conclusion is then reached.
∎

5.
Localisation of the mass in dimension one

In this paragraph, we focus on the special case
d=1
and we prove
Theorem

1.2
.
We only consider the two first collision operators, namely the relaxation operator and the Fokker-Planck operator, since the Laplace-Beltrami operator is not defined in one dimension.

5.1.
The linear relaxation equation

We fix
f_{\text{{\tiny{\rm in}}}}\in\mathsf{L}^{1}_{1,\omega}\cap\mathsf{L}^{\infty}_{-1,\omega}
for an admissible weight function
\omega
, and recalling (
1.4
), we assume
{\mathscr{M}}_{0}>0
as well as
d=1
.
We present now the proof of (
1.7
) in the case of the linear relaxation equation, that we split into two steps.

Step 1.
Denoting
\rho_{t}:=\rho[f(t,\cdot)]
,
j_{t}:=j[f(t,\cdot)]
and recording Lemma
3.1
(in the bounded velocities case) and (
3.24
) (in the unbounded velocities case), there exists a constant
C\in(0,\infty)
such that

(5.1)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}x\rho_{t}\leq C

for any
t\geq 0
,
as well as from (
1.6
) and (
4.15
), we have

(5.2)

\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{\infty}_{-1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}+\|\rho\|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}^{2}+\|j\|_{\mathsf{L}^{\infty}_{-1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}\leq{C\over t^{\frac{3}{2}}},

for any
t>0
.
Similarly as (
2.8
), standard interpolation yields

(5.3)

\|u\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathsf{L}^{\infty}_{-1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}^{\frac{1}{3}}\|u\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\,x\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}^{\frac{2}{3}},

from what we deduce

(5.4)

\|\rho_{t}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}\leq{C\over t^{\frac{1}{2}}},

which is the same rate as for the heat equation, see (
2.9
).
On the other hand, we define the weight function
\bar{\omega}:=x^{2}+2xv+4v^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}(|xv|+x^{2})
, and taking advantage of the fact that
{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}v=-v
,
{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}v^{2}\leq 1-v^{2}
, we compute

\displaystyle{d\over dt}\int_{\Omega}f\bar{\omega}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}fv\,\partial_{x}\bar{\omega}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v-\int_{\partial\Omega_{+}}fv^{3}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v+\int_{\Omega}f{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}\bar{\omega}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f(2xv+2v^{2})\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v+\int_{\Omega}f(2x{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}v+{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}v^{2})\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v-2\int_{\Omega}fv^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v\lesssim{1\over t^{\frac{1}{2}}},

where we have used that the boundary contribution is nonpositive. Denoting and integrating in time this differential inequality, we thus obtain

(5.5)

\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2}\rho_{t}\,{\mathrm{d}}x+\int_{0}^{\infty}x|j_{t}|\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq 2\int_{\Omega}f\bar{\omega}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\lesssim t^{\frac{1}{2}}.

In particular, together with (
5.2
) and (
5.3
), we get

(5.6)

\|j_{t}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}\leq{C\over t^{\frac{1}{6}}},\quad\forall\,t>0.

Finally, we observe that

\displaystyle{d\over dt}\int_{\Omega}(x+v)f\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v=\int_{\partial\Omega_{+}}fv^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v+\int_{\Omega}f[v\,\partial_{x}(x+v)+{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}(x+v)]\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v=\int_{\partial\Omega_{+}}fv^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}v\geq 0,

so that

\int_{0}^{\infty}x\,\rho_{t}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\geq{\mathscr{M}}_{0}-\int_{0}^{\infty}j_{t}\,{\mathrm{d}}x,

for any
t\geq 0
. Together with (
5.6
), we deduce

\int_{0}^{\infty}x\,\rho_{t}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\geq\frac{{\mathscr{M}}_{0}}{2},

for any
t\geq T_{0}
and some
T_{0}>0
large enough. Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the first estimate (
5.5
) similarly as in the proof of (
2.10
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathscr{M}}_{0}}{2}\leq\|\rho_{t}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}\|\rho_{t}\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},x^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x)}\lesssim t^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}

which proves

\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}\gtrsim\frac{{\mathscr{M}}_{0}}{t^{\frac{1}{2}}},

which is the same lower bound (
2.10
) that the one obtained for the heat equation. Again, that estimate can be localised in the following way.
Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the second estimate in (
5.2
), we have

\int_{0}^{a\sqrt{t}}\rho_{t}(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq a^{\frac{1}{2}}t^{\frac{1}{4}}\|\rho_{t}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}\leq a^{\frac{1}{2}}Ct^{-\frac{1}{2}},

and next using estimate (
5.1
), we have

\int_{b\sqrt{t}}^{\infty}\rho_{t}(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq b^{-1}t^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}x\rho_{t}(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq b^{-1}t^{-\frac{1}{2}}C.

Gathering (
5.4
) and the three last estimates for
a>0
small enough,
b>0
large enough, we have obtained

(5.7)

\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{t}}\leq\int_{a\sqrt{t}}^{b\sqrt{t}}\rho_{t}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq\frac{c_{1}}{\sqrt{t}},

for any
t\geq T_{0}
and some constructive constants
c_{i},a,b>0
.

As a consequence, there exists
I_{t}:=\left[x_{t}-\tfrac{1}{2},x_{t}+\tfrac{1}{2}\right]\subset[a\sqrt{t},b\sqrt{t}]
such that

(5.8)

\int_{I_{t}}\rho(t,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\gtrsim\frac{1}{t}.

Indeed, assuming the opposite, for any
{\varepsilon}>0
, we would have

\int_{a\sqrt{t}}^{b\sqrt{t}}\rho_{t}(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\leq\sum_{k\leq b\sqrt{t}}\int_{k}^{k+1}\rho_{t}(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\leq\frac{b{\varepsilon}\sqrt{t}}{t}\leq\frac{{\varepsilon}b}{\sqrt{t}},

and this is a contradiction to the second estimate in (
5.7
).

Step 2.

We will use the transport characteristics to transfer information from space integrals of
\rho
to pointwise quantities. First,
using characteristics, the solution
f
to the relaxation equation (
1.1
) satisfies

(5.9)

f(t+s,x,v)=e^{-s}f(t,x-vs,v)+M(v)\int_{0}^{s}e^{-\sigma}\rho_{f}(t+s-\sigma,x-\sigma v)\,{\mathrm{d}}s,

for any
t,s\geq 0
,
x\in(0,\infty)
and
v\in\Omega_{v}
.
We denote
{\mathcal{V}}:=[-V_{0},V_{0}]\subset\Omega_{v}
and
J_{t}:=\left[x_{t}-\tfrac{1}{2}-V_{0},x_{t}+\tfrac{1}{2}+V_{0}\right]
. Throwing away the last term in (
5.9
)
and integrating in both
x
and
v
variables, we have

\displaystyle\int_{J_{t}}\rho(t+s,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y

\displaystyle\geq\int_{J_{t}}\int_{\mathcal{V}}f(t+s,y,w)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\,{\mathrm{d}}w

\displaystyle\geq e^{-s}\int_{\mathcal{V}}\int_{J_{t}}f(t,y-ws,w)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\,{\mathrm{d}}w

\displaystyle\geq e^{-s}\int_{\mathcal{V}}\int_{I_{t}}f(t,z,w)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\,{\mathrm{d}}w\gtrsim\frac{1}{t},

for any
s\in[0,1]
, where we have used (
5.8
) in the last inequality and the fact that
J_{t}-sw\supset I_{t}
for any
s\in[0,1]
and
w\in[-V_{0},V_{0}]
.
Integrating in time, we deduce

\int_{0}^{\tfrac{1}{3}}\!\!\int_{J_{t}}\rho(t+s,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\,{\mathrm{d}}s\gtrsim\frac{1}{t}.

By a covering argument, there exists
\tilde{x}_{t}\in J_{t}
such that, defining

\tilde{J}_{t}:=\left[\tilde{x}_{t}-\tfrac{V_{0}}{6},\tilde{x}_{t}+\tfrac{V_{0}}{6}\right]=\tilde{x}_{t}+\tfrac{1}{6}{\mathcal{V}},

we have

\int_{0}^{1/3}\!\!\int_{\tilde{J}_{t}}\rho(t+s,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\,{\mathrm{d}}s\gtrsim\frac{1}{t}.

We observe that we also have
x+\tfrac{1}{3}{\mathcal{V}}\supset\tilde{J}_{t}
for any
x\in\tilde{J}_{t}
.
Thoughing away the first term in (
5.9
), integrating in the
v
-variable and taking advantage of the last estimate, we have

\displaystyle\rho(t+s,x)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathcal{V}}\!\int_{0}^{s}e^{-\sigma}\rho_{f}(t+s-\sigma,x-\sigma v)M(v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma

\displaystyle\gtrsim\int_{\tfrac{1}{3}}^{s}\!\int_{x+\tfrac{1}{3}{\mathcal{V}}}\rho_{f}(t+s-\sigma,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma\,{\mathrm{d}}y\gtrsim\int_{0}^{s-\tfrac{1}{3}}\!\!\!\int_{x+\tfrac{1}{3}{\mathcal{V}}}\rho_{f}(t+\sigma,y)d\sigma\,{\mathrm{d}}y\gtrsim\frac{1}{t},

for any
s\in\left[\tfrac{2}{3},1\right]
and
x\in\tilde{J}_{t}
.
Finally, thoughing away again the first term in (
5.9
) and taking advantage of the last estimate, we have

f(t+s,x,v)\geq\left(\int_{0}^{\tfrac{1}{12}}e^{-\sigma}\rho_{f}(t+s-\sigma,x-\sigma v)\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma\right)M(v)\gtrsim\frac{1}{t}

for any
x\in\tilde{x}_{t}+\frac{1}{12}{\mathcal{V}}
and
s\in[5/6,1]
. Thus for any
t\geq T_{0}+1
, there exists
\bar{x}_{t}\sim\sqrt{t}
such that

f(t+s,x,v)\gtrsim\frac{1}{t},\quad\forall\,s\in\left[0,\frac{1}{6}\right],\ \forall\,x\in\bar{x}_{t}+\frac{{\mathcal{V}}}{12},

which concludes the proof by a finite iteration argument.

5.2.
The kinetic Fokker-Planck equation

We fix again
