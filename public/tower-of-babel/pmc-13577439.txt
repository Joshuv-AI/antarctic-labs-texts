Utilization of the PK Papyrus Covered Stent for the Treatment of Direct Carotid Cavernous Fistulas: A Case Series
Authors: Lindsey M. Schwartz, Paras Gupta, Shourya Kumar, Jaime R. Guerrero, Marcus S. Wong, Andrew O. Schindler, Gavin W. Britz, Orlando M. Diaz, Alex N. Hoang, Kurt A. Yaeger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES:Carotid cavernous fistulas (CCFs) pose significant challenges in management. Traditional treatment modalities including endovascular embolization, surgical ligation, and even stereotactic radiosurgery, are often limited by their inability to effectively seal arteriovenous shunts while preserving perforating vessels. In recent years, covered stents have been investigated as a possible treatment option for various intracranial vascular pathologies, including CCFs, offering the potential to occlude sites of shunting or hemorrhage. Traditional covered stents were noted to be stiff and difficult to navigate in tortuous vessels, and complications such as endoleaks, vasospasm, restenosis, and thrombosis have also hindered their widespread adoption.METHODS:We present a retrospective, single-institution case series of 4 adult patients with direct CCFs treated with the PK Papyrus covered stent (Biotronik Inc.). By leveraging the unique properties of the PK Papyrus stent, we demonstrate its potential as a safe and effective therapeutic option for challenging CCF cases.RESULTS:Four patients with Barrow Type A CCFs were successfully treated with the PK Papyrus stent. Complete obliteration of the CCF was noted in all cases. Intraoperative complication included only 1 incidence of vasospasm of the distal internal carotid artery that resolved with intra-arterial verapamil. All 4 patients have completed long-term follow-up angiograms demonstrating persistent fistula obliteration without signs of endoleak, in-stent thrombosis, or stenosis.CONCLUSION:The Papyrus covered stent is a viable option in treating direct CCFs. Future multicenter studies are needed to investigate the long-term efficacy of these devices for treatment of CCFs.

Carotid-cavernous fistulas (CCFs) are abnormal vascular connections between the carotid artery system and the cavernous sinus.1 CCFs are classified as either direct, arising directly from the internal carotid artery (ICA), or indirect, with arterial supply from perforating branches of the ICA and/or supply from the external carotid artery.1,2 Direct CCFs most frequently arise after trauma or from a ruptured cavernous segment aneurysm.2 The etiology of indirect CCFs is less clear, but is believed to be secondary to dural venous sinus or cortical vein thrombosis.1,3 Diagnosis typically involves noninvasive Computed Tomography Angiography or Magnetic Resonance Angiography or catheter cerebral angiography.4 High-risk features, such as cortical venous drainage and cavernous sinus varices, are associated with increased intracranial pressure and heightened risk of hemorrhage.5

CCFs pose significant challenges in management, often requiring a multimodal approach because of their complex vascular nature. Traditional treatment options include endovascular embolization, surgical ligation, and stereotactic radiosurgery in low-flow cases. CCFs were once treated routinely with surgical ligation, however, given technical difficulty, complex anatomy within vital structures and overall success rate reportedly between 31% and 79%, it is now reserved for cases in which endovascular treatment has failed or is not possible.6 Stereotactic radiosurgery is primarily used for low-flow indirect CCFs.6 Indirect CCF are often treated by transvenous embolization of the cavernous sinus. Multiple endovascular strategies can be used including Onyx or n-BCA embolization, flow-diverting stents, or coil embolization of the fistula.6 Direct CCF may be best treated by finding the rupture point in the ICA and occluding it either by coil, stent, or other endovascular devices.

Intracranial stents have been used in the treatment of CCFs, but they are not without limitations. Flow diverting stents can cross important branching vessels off the ICA, but may not limit flow to the fistula to the extent necessary to cause thrombosis. Alternatively, covered stents can be used to completely block flow to the site of fistula, but can only be used in carotid artery segments without perforating branches. Complications such as endoleaks, vasospasm, restenosis, and thrombosis have hindered their widespread adoption. Furthermore, because these stents have been designed for coronary or peripheral use, the deployment technique can be challenging in the intracranial circulation, where the vasculature is often more tortuous. These stents have previously been used to treat aneurysms, CCFs, and ICA injuries.7 Occlusion rates are influenced by factors such as stent porosity, geometry, and design, all of which affect hemodynamic properties.8 Covered stents, in particular, have emerged as a promising alternative for intracranial vascular pathologies, offering the potential to address some of these limitations by providing an impermeable barrier across the fistula site.9 However, earlier covered stents, such as the Jostent, were often noted to be too stiff and difficult to navigate in tortuous vessels.10-13 In some cases, stent placement failed because of the rigidity of the stent and the tortuous anatomy of the ICA.14 Despite these challenges, covered stents have proven to be effective and safe for treating direct CCFs, with advantages such as high immediate and long-term complete occlusion rates, no coil herniation into the parent vessel, and preservation of the parent vessel.10,11 When used alone, they may also eliminate the need for cavernous sinus packing, thereby reducing the risk of cranial nerve injury.10,11

In this report, we present a series of 4 cases illustrating the use of the PK Papyrus (Biotronik, Inc.) covered stent in the treatment of CCFs. By leveraging the unique properties of the PK Papyrus stent, we demonstrate its potential as a safe and effective therapeutic option for challenging direct CCF cases. Our analysis contributes to the growing understanding of the role of covered stents in intracranial neurovascular interventions and underscores the importance of innovative approaches in direct CCF management. This case series has been reported in line with the Preferred Reporting of Case Series in Surgery guidelines.15

METHODS

In this case series, we retrospectively analyzed 4 patients with direct CCFs who were treated with covered stents at our institution between April 2023 and March 2024. We retrospectively reviewed the electronic medical record for patient characteristics, procedural data, and angiographic and clinical follow-up. Health Insurance Portability and Accountability act identifiers were removed and patient identity was not disclosed. This study discusses an off-label use of the PK Papyrus covered stent for treatment of CCF (Biotronik, Inc.). This retrospective case series did not require Institutional Review Board approval in accordance with institutional policy. Informed consent was obtained from patients for all procedures performed when clinically feasible. All imaging, clinical records, treatment, and follow-up data were obtained from the electronic medical records, and all data are included within the article, no statistical analyses were performed, and no artificial intelligence was used for this research. This case series has been reported in line with the Preferred Reporting of Case Series in Surgery Guideline.15

Procedural Information

All endovascular procedures were performed by a transfemoral approach under general anesthesia. Preoperatively, the patients were placed on dual antiplatelet therapy (Brilinta 90 mg, Aspirin 81 mg) or loaded with integrilin if emergent. A P2Y12 assay was obtained preoperatively to ensure response to antiplatelet therapy. Procedures were performed with standard biplane angiographic technique with the patient under systemic heparinization. A select catheter was advanced through a guide sheath and positioned within the relevant common carotid and a standard cervical arteriogram was performed. Next, the internal carotid was selected with a wire, and an intermediate catheter of the physician's choice, such as the NavienTM 72 catheter (Medtronic), was advanced into the distal cervical or petrous segment ICA. The minimum guiding catheter inner diameter for the PK Papyrus covered stent is 5F or 6F, depending on stent size. A 3-dimensional rotational angiogram was obtained to localize the precise ICA rupture point and fistulous connection with the cavernous sinus and, in conjunction with a dyna computed tomography, was used to select the appropriate stent size. Before advancement, the balloon-mounted Papyrus covered stent was prepared using standard aspiration and saline flushing techniques, either on the back table or in situ after positioning across the target segment, according to operator preference, with particular attention to removal of residual air from the balloon system. The balloon mounted Papyrus covered stent was then advanced over a 0.014-inch microwire, such as the Aristotle 14 (Scientia Vascular), and deployed across the fistulous connection. The balloon was inflated to either 7 or 8 atm depending on the stent size, in accordance with manufacturer guidelines. After stent placement, an angioplasty balloon was used to perform post stenting angioplasty. Repeat angiography was performed to ensure exclusion of the fistula without residual filling. Dyna computed tomography with injection of dilute contrast was performed to assess wall apposition of the stent in each case. A final cerebral angiogram was performed to confirm patency of the distal ICA without in-stent stenosis or distal emboli. On completion of the procedure, the patients were admitted to the intensive care unit overnight for close blood pressure and neurological monitoring.

Case 1

A 79-year-old woman with a medical history of hypothyroidism presented with a 1-year history of bilateral conjunctival injection, pulsatile tinnitus, and 4 months of worsening vision in her left eye. The patient was evaluated by a neuro-ophthalmologist and ultimately underwent Computed Tomography Angiography which was concerning for CCF. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was performed demonstrating a right sided direct CCF with bilateral cortical venous reflux secondary to a presumed ruptured cavernous aneurysm (Figure 1). The CCF was treated the following day with coil embolization of the aneurysm and the right cavernous sinus. Given persistent filling of the fistula, the decision was made to place a 5 × 15 mm Papyrus covered stent across the cavernous ICA. A second 5 × 20 mm covered stent was placed further distally, and complete occlusion of the fistula was noted on angiography without concern for distal emboli, spasm, or parent vessel occlusion. A 6-month follow-up DSA demonstrated persistent complete occlusion of the CCF without parent vessel thrombosis or in-stent stenosis. At last follow-up, the patient noted persistent improvement in left eye vision and conjunctival injection.

Case 2

A 40-year-old woman with a medical history of bilateral vertebral artery dissection presented with 1 month of progressive headaches, right eye conjunctival injection, and vision loss. Magnetic Resonance Angiography performed was concerning for CCF. DSA was performed demonstrating a right-sided direct CCF (Figure 2). The patient was taken for treatment shortly after. A 4.5 × 15 mm Papyrus covered stent was placed across the site of the fistula. Follow-up angiography demonstrated complete occlusion of the fistula without concern for distal emboli or parent vessel occlusion. Mild vasospasm of the distal cervical and petrous ICA was noted and 10 mg of intra-arterial verapamil was injected. Follow-up angiogram demonstrated improved vasospasm and no further complications were noted. A 6-month follow-up DSA demonstrated persistent complete occlusion of the CCF without parent vessel thrombosis, with mild in-stent stenosis. At last follow-up, the patient continued to note persistent improvement in right eye vision and headaches.

A microwire is demonstrated directly entering the fistulous connection C before balloon expansion of the Papyrus stent across the fistula D. Immediate angiographic occlusion was noted after stent placement along with spasm of the cervical and petrous internal carotid artery E and F. Final stent placement showed good apposition against the vessel wall without in-stent stenosis or thrombosis G and H.

Case 3

A 46-year-old woman with no significant medical history presented with a week of headaches, tinnitus, left eye ptosis, and ophthalmoplegia after suffering direct trauma to her eye. MRI performed was concerning for CCF. DSA was performed demonstrating a left-sided direct CCF (Figure 3). This was 80% occluded with a flow re-direction endoluminal device flow diverter and coils at the time of initial DSA. A 4-week follow-up DSA demonstrated persistent direct left CCF with signs of venous hypertension. Multiple attempts using a venous approach were not successful. The decision was made to place a 5 × 20 mm Papyrus covered stent at the fistula site. Angiography demonstrated complete occlusion of the fistula without concern for distal emboli, parent vessel occlusion, or vasospasm. A 6-month follow-up DSA demonstrated complete occlusion of the CCF without parent vessel thrombosis. At last follow-up, the patient's headaches have resolved and her left eye motility deficits have stabilized.

Digital subtraction angiography of patient 3 illustrates continued filling of a left-sided direct carotid cavernous fistula despite coil embolization with flow diversion A and B. Immediate angiographic occlusion was noted after stent placement C and D.

Case 4

A 40-year-old woman presented with intractable epistaxis during transsphenoidal operation for pituitary adenoma for Cushing's disease. Intraoperatively, there was noted to be brisk arterial bleeding concerning for carotid artery injury. The nasal sinuses were packed, and the patient was taken emergently to angiography which demonstrated an iatrogenic left sided direct CCF (Figure 4). The decision was made to place a 4.5 × 20 mm Papyrus covered stent across the site of the fistula. Angiography demonstrated complete occlusion of the fistula without concern for distal emboli, parent vessel occlusion, or vasospasm. Epistaxis resolved and in-hospital follow-up angiography at 9 days postoperation demonstrated complete occlusion of the CCF without parent vessel thrombosis. At last follow-up, the patient remained neurologically stable with persistent headaches, but no further epistaxis noted.

A Papyrus covered stent was placed across the cavernous internal carotid artery with immediate fistula obliteration C and D.

DISCUSSION

This case series of 4 patients with direct CCF demonstrates the technical feasibility, safety, and efficacy of the Papyrus covered stent in ICA pathology. The device is not Food and Drug Administration approved for treatment of CCF and was used off-label in this series, with all patients achieving durable fistula occlusion without short- or long-term complications noted.

Historically, covered stents used for intracranial pathologies have primarily been coronary stents such as the Jostent and Symbiot stents used in off-label fashion. These stents, particularly the Jostent, were often difficult to navigate through tortuous intracranial vessels because of their rigidity, sometimes causing vasospasm or straightening of the cavernous ICA.10-13 In some cases, stent placement failed because of the tortuous ICA anatomy.14 The risk of in-stent thrombosis posed a serious complication, further limiting their widespread use.16

Despite these limitations, covered stents have demonstrated effectiveness in treating direct CCFs, offering high rates of immediate and durable occlusion while preserving the parent vessel and avoiding coil herniation.10,11 When used alone, they may also eliminate the need for cavernous sinus packing, reducing the risk of cranial nerve injury.10,11 The largest case series of CCF occlusion with covered stents have been performed in China, Italy, and Colombia, using the Jostent.10-13 More recently, case series have described successful treatment of intracranial pseudoaneurysms and direct CCFs using the Willis covered stent, which was specifically designed for intracranial use and is approved by China's Food and Drug Administration.17,18

The Willis covered stent is a balloon expanded stent covered with polytetrafluoroethylene and designed for intracranial applications.19 Its structure incorporates staggered, single-linking points connecting adjacent segments rather than multiple welded connections in a direct line, improving longitudinal flexibility and facilitating navigation through tortuous vessels.19 The Willis stent's combined balloon-stent diameter of 1.27 mm is compatible with a 6F guiding catheter, making it better suited for challenging intracranial anatomy.19

The PK Papyrus covered coronary stent, originally designed and Food and Drug Administration- approved for treating coronary artery perforations (CAPs), incorporates similar design features that may benefit intracranial applications. The ultrathin strut (60 μm) stent is covered with an electrospun polyurethane matrix on its abluminal surface, improving navigation through vessels compared with older devices such as the Jostent.20 Successful delivery and perforation sealing rates were 97.7% and 92.1%, respectively in CAP studies, underscoring the efficacy and safety of this device.21,22

Although primarily used in treatment of CAPs, the PK Papyrus stent has recently been explored off-label for intracranial pathologies, including CCFs, with favorable outcomes.23-26 These studies include 2 case series and a case report, totaling 18 patients with CCFs successfully treated with the PK Papyrus stent, demonstrating high rates of complete occlusion without recurrence. In our institution, the stent was selected for its deliverability, immediate vessel coverage, and favorable safety profile, particularly in complex cases or after failure of conventional treatments. Specifically, the device was used after persistent fistula after coil embolization (Case 1), a complex fistula with progressive vision loss (Case 2), failure of previous coil embolization and flow diversion (Case 3), and iatrogenic carotid injury with intractable epistaxis (Case 4). During the study period, other direct CCFs were treated with standard approaches, including coil embolization and flow diversion, with the PK Papyrus stent reserved for cases where conventional strategies were unsuccessful or unlikely to succeed. Our work underscores existing literature demonstrating the safety and efficacy of the PK Papyrus stent in complex neurovascular pathologies, although larger prospective studies are needed.

In this series, the PK Papyrus stent achieved complete CCF occlusion without recurrence or need for re-treatment.23 Although all cases were successfully treated, Case 1 required 2 stents to completely obliterate the fistula. If complete occlusion is not achieved, potential reintervention strategies include restenting, alternative endovascular embolization techniques (coils or liquid embolization agents), flow diversion with adjunctive techniques, or combined surgical-endovascular approaches.

Repeat PK Papyrus stent placement after initial failure lacks supporting evidence.24 Management should be individualized based on the mechanism of failure and vascular anatomy. When incomplete occlusion occurs after covered stent placement, redilation of the existing stent is often attempted before additional stenting. In 1 series, redilation eliminated endoleak in 2 of 3 patients with incomplete occlusion after initial covered stent deployment.14 This approach addresses inadequate stent apposition without introducing additional hardware.

Adjunctive endovascular techniques are frequently used when covered stents alone prove insufficient. Embolization using coils and/or liquid embolic agents (such as Onyx) can achieve complete obliteration rates of 82%-94% for both direct and indirect CCFs.27-29 Transvenous embolization, flow diversion with adjunctive techniques, or surgical approaches may also offer better outcomes than simply repeating the same intervention.30,31 Flow diverting stents combined with coils or liquid embolic agents have been successfully used for direct CCFs, with recommendations to first reduce fistula flow through coiling before flow diversion.30 If endovascular routes are no longer favorable, combined surgical-endovascular approaches may be necessary. Given the absence of data supporting repeat PK Papyrus stenting for failed CCF occlusion, a more comprehensive endovascular strategy incorporating alternative techniques or routes should be considered before restenting.

The PK Papyrus covered stent offers a lowest crossing profile of 1.25 mm and, depending on the size stent required, is compatible with either a 5F or 6F guide catheter, which is comparable to the Willis stent's design (Table 2).24 Like the Willis stent, the PK Papyrus stent also consists of a covered single stent design rather than the traditional sandwich stent design used in products such as the Jostent.19,32

Limitations

Despite these advances, areas for improvement remain. Further refinement of stent design should focus on enhancing flexibility without compromising radial strength to further aid navigating difficult intracranial vasculature. Although early results with the PK Papyrus covered stent are encouraging there are only few case reports and case series. Larger, randomized multicenter studies directly comparing its performance to devices such as the Willis stent are needed to establish the optimal treatment approach for complex direct CCFs.

CONCLUSION

This study, along with the previous studies of intracranial use of the PK Papyrus covered stent, highlights the safety and efficacy of the stent in the context of intracranial pathology. We also directly compare design features of the PK Papyrus covered stent to the new Willis covered stent. As research continues to explore novel uses of covered stents in neurovascular interventions, the PK Papyrus stent offers an encouraging alternative, potentially reducing the complications that have limited the use of earlier devices.
