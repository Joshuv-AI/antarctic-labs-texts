Random-effects meta-analysis via generalized linear mixed models: A Bartlett-corrected approach for few studies
Keisuke Hanada, Tomoyuki Sugimoto

Abstract
Random-effects meta-analysis is widely used for synthesizing results across studies, but the implicit assumption of normality in study-specific aggregate data is often violated. Such violations can lead to biased estimates and misleading conclusions, especially in meta-analyses with small studies or rare events. A prominent example occurs with the log-odds ratio, which exhibits bias that depends on the within-study sample sizes. We first show that conventional methods assuming normality fail to eliminate such biases, even as the number of studies increases. To overcome this limitation, we introduce a generalized linear mixed-effects model that uses only aggregate data, accommodating a wide range of outcome types, including binomial, Poisson, gamma, and other members of the exponential family, without requiring individual participant data. To enable valid interval estimation when the number of studies is small, we further propose a simple Bartlett correction for the test statistics. The proposed method yields consistent point estimators without relying on the normality assumption and achieves accurate interval coverage across diverse outcome types. It is particularly applicable in clinical and epidemiological research where only summary data are available, making it a practical alternative to conventional approaches. Simulation studies and applications to three published meta-analyses with binary, Poisson, and gamma outcomes demonstrate that the method provides reliable inference and maintains nominal coverage, thereby supporting sound decision-making and guideline development when only aggregate data are available.



Keywords
Bartlett correction, few studies, generalized linear model, meta-analysis, random-effects model.

1
Introduction

The random-effects model, commonly used to account for between-study heterogeneity when synthesizing results across studies, is often implemented using the method proposed by
DerSimonian and Laird 1986
.
This approach is simple and easy to implement, but it implicitly relies on three within-study normality assumptions: (A1) the estimated effect in each study is unbiased; (A2) the within-study variance is known; and (A3) the estimated effect follows a normal distribution
(
Jackson and White 2018
)
.
A well-known example, where these assumptions are violated, is meta-analysis using the log-odds ratio as the outcome.
In particular, when rare events are involved, the log-odds ratio estimator exhibits bias that depends on the within-study sample sizes.
To address the limitations of meta-analysis under the normality assumption for biased effect measures, previous studies have proposed generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) that assume binomial distributions for event counts or their extensions
(
Stijnen et al. 2010
;
Bhaumik et al. 2012
;
Jackson et al. 2018
)
.
For even rarer events, Poisson regression models have also been applied
(
Böhning et al. 2015
)
.
These approaches avoid assumptions (A1)-(A3) by explicitly modeling the outcome distribution.
However, they are limited to specific outcome types, such as event counts.
The present study generalizes these methods to accommodate outcomes from any distribution within the exponential family.
This extension enables consistent and asymptotically normal inference without requiring large sample sizes within each study, relying instead on an increasing number of studies.
The advantage justifies including studies with small sample sizes in a meta-analysis.
Such situations often arise in rare disease research, early-phase clinical trials, and subgroup analyses, where the number of participants per study is inherently limited.

Another important issue in meta-analysis is that, when the number of studies is small, the variance of the average treatment effect is often underestimated.
The underestimation results in confidence intervals and prediction intervals with coverage probabilities falling below the nominal level
(
Li et al. 1994
;
Viechtbauer 2005
)
.
Empirical studies have shown that such undercoverage occurs in meta-analyses using odds ratios or risk ratios when the number of studies is small
(
Seide et al. 2019
)
, and similar degradation in performance has been observed in the GLMM.
Under the normality assumptions (A1)–(A3), several methods have been proposed to construct confidence and prediction intervals that achieve nominal levels
(
Michael et al. 2019
;
Nagashima et al. 2019
;
Hanada and Sugimoto 2023
)
.
Among these methods, the Bartlett correction for profile likelihood estimators is known to yield conservative intervals, regardless of the magnitude of heterogeneity
(
Noma 2011
)
.
However, when biased effect measures such as the log-odds ratio are used, the coverage probability can be further compromised due to the presence of bias.
The Bartlett correction improves the approximation accuracy of the profile likelihood ratio statistic from
O(K^{-1})
to
O(K^{-2})
, and is especially effective when the number of studies
K
is small
(
Barndorff-Nielsen and Hall 1988
)
.
However, the Bartlett correction requires third- and fourth-order derivatives of the profile likelihood, which are often difficult to compute in GLMMs due to parameter transformations via link functions.
To avoid the complex calculation of higher-order derivatives, bootstrap-based estimation of the Bartlett correction term has been proposed and applied to network meta-analysis
(
Rocke 1989
;
Noma et al. 2018
)
.
However, this approach involves repeated optimization of the profile likelihood across bootstrap samples, leading to a substantial computational burden.
The present study shows that the Bartlett correction term derived under the normality assumptions by
Noma 2011
can be effectively applied within the GLMM framework in many practical settings.
This simple Bartlett correction requires only a single optimization of the profile likelihood and yields interval estimates with nominal coverage, even when the number of studies is small.

The main contributions of the present study are as follows:
(i) to mathematically clarify the limitations that arise under the normality assumptions (A1)–(A3);
(ii) to establish that, when individual participant data follow a distribution in the exponential family, the corresponding aggregate data also follow a distribution in the exponential family, thereby allowing parameter estimation using only aggregate data;
(iii) to propose a profile likelihood approach with simplified Bartlett correction (PLSBC), which applies the Bartlett correction originally developed under the normal-normal model, and to demonstrate its validity both theoretically and through simulations.
The resulting confidence intervals achieve nominal coverage regardless of the number of studies or the magnitude of between-study heterogeneity.
The proposed method is implemented in the R package
metaGLMM
, which is publicly available at
https://github.com/keisuke-hanada/metaGLMM
.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
presents the methodology of meta-analysis under the normality assumptions (A1)–(A3) and shows that, violations of these assumptions can result in biased inference and lead to the rejection of incorrect null hypotheses.
Section
3
introduces a GLMM in which individual outcomes follow a distribution in the exponential family. Based on this framework, we propose a maximum likelihood estimation procedure using only aggregate data, along with a profile likelihood-based interval estimation method incorporating a simplified Bartlett correction.
Section
4
evaluates the performance of the proposed method through simulations under representative exponential family models and compares it with existing methods based on the normality assumptions.
Section
5
applies the proposed method to reanalyze three published meta-analyses involving binary, Poisson, and gamma outcomes.
Finally, Section
6
summarizes the findings and discusses directions for future research.

2
Meta-analysis under the normality assumption

Let
K
denote the number of studies included in the meta-analysis, and
n_{k}
be the number of subjects in study
k
. Suppose that heterogeneity across the
K
studies is captured by random effects
V_{1},\dots,V_{K}
, which are assumed to have a probability density function
f_{V}(\cdot;\tau^{2})
with zero mean and variance
\tau^{2}
, and
v_{1},\dots,v_{K}
are the realizations of
V_{1},\dots,V_{K}
.

2.1
Individual participant data under the normal assumption

Let
Y_{ki}
denote the outcome of subject
i

(=1,\ldots,n_{k})
in study
k

(=1,\ldots,K)
, and that
Y_{k1},\dots,Y_{kn_{k}}
are independently and identically distributed within the same study. For each subject
i
in study
k
, we assume that the outcome model

\displaystyle\mu_{k}

\displaystyle=E[Y_{ki}\mid V_{k}=v_{k}],\quad\theta_{k}=g(\mu_{k})={\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}+v_{k},

(1)

where the mean
\mu_{k}
of outcomes in study
k
is formulated by the conditional expectation given the study heterogeneity
v_{k}
, and
{\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}=(x_{k1},\dots,x_{kp})^{T}
is a
p
-dimensional covariate vector for study
k
. The function
g(\cdot)
is a link function for connecting the study mean
\mu_{k}
with the linear predictor
\theta_{k}
, which can be analyzed statistically via a regression model. The coefficient vector
{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})^{T}
in the linear predictor specifies the effect of the study-level covariates.
Although the canonical link function is often used due to its natural connection to the exponential family, any one-to-one link function is theoretically admissible.
For example, the identity link
g(\mu_{k})=\mu_{k}
is canonical for normal outcomes; the logit link
g(\mu_{k})=\mathrm{logit}(\mu_{k})
for binomial outcomes;
and the inverse link
g(\mu_{k})=1/\mu_{k}
is canonical for gamma outcomes, although the log link
g(\mu_{k})=\log\mu_{k}
is commonly used in practice for numerical stability.

In standard analyses using individual participant data, covariates are observed for each individual.
In contrast, meta-analysis typically uses only aggregate data for each study.
We assume that a common covariate vector
{\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}
is available for each study
k
.
Examples of such study-level covariates include the average age, gender ratio, and year of the study; specific cases are described in Section
5
.

In this setting, given the study-level covariates
{\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}
, individual-level observations
(y_{k1},\dots,y_{kn_{k}})
are independently and identically sampled from the outcome model (
1
).
At the meta-analysis stage, the available information typically includes the sample size
n_{k}
, the sample mean
\bar{y}_{k}=n_{k}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}y_{ki}
, the estimate
\hat{\theta}_{k}=g(\bar{y}_{k})
of
\theta_{k}
, the variance estimate
\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}
of
\hat{\theta}_{k}
, and the study-level covariates
{\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}
.
In meta-analysis assuming normality, the following three conditions are implicitly required
(
Jackson et al. 2018
)
:

(A1)

\hat{\theta}_{k}
is an unbiased estimator:
E[\hat{\theta}_{k}\mid V_{k}=v_{k}]=\theta_{k}={\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}+v_{k}
.

(A2)

The within-study variance
\sigma_{k}^{2}
is known.

(A3)

\hat{\theta}_{k}
is normally distributed given
V_{k}=v_{k}
:
\hat{\theta}_{k}\mid V_{k}=v_{k}\sim N(\theta_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2})
.

Although these assumptions rarely hold exactly, they are often considered reasonable due to the asymptotic normality of
\hat{\theta}_{k}

(
DerSimonian and Laird 1986
)
.
For example, when
\hat{\theta}_{k}
is the sample mean, i.e.,
\hat{\theta}_{k}=n_{k}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}y_{ki}
, normality assumptions (A1)–(A3) are satisfied if all
n_{1},\dots,n_{K}
are sufficiently large.
However, the assumption that all studies have sufficiently large sample sizes implicitly discourages the inclusion of those with small sample sizes in a meta-analysis.
Therefore, it is necessary to have a meta-analysis whose theoretical validity is ensured solely by an increasing number of studies or by an increasing total sample size.

2.2
Random-effects meta-analysis with the normal assumption

To simplify the discussion in this section, we consider the setting of univariate meta-analysis, where
p=1
,
{\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}=1
, and
{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}=\theta^{*}
.
Under assumptions (A1)–(A3), and assuming that the random effects are normally distributed as
V_{k}\sim N(0,\tau^{2})
, (
1
) can be written as the marginal model of estimator
\hat{\theta}_{k}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{k}

\displaystyle\sim N(\theta^{*},\sigma_{k}^{2}+\tau^{2}).

(2)

We refer to model (
2
) as the
normal-normal model
.
Under the normal-normal model, the average treatment effect
\theta^{*}
can be estimated by the weighted mean

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\tau^{2})\hat{\theta}_{k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\tau^{2})},

(3)

where
w_{k}(\tau^{2})=1/(\sigma_{k}^{2}+\tau^{2})
.
Under the normal-normal model, (
3
) is unbiased and the maximum likelihood estimator.
A Wald-type test statistic for testing the hypothesis
H_{0}:\theta=\theta_{0}
is given by

\displaystyle T_{K}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\tau^{2})(\hat{\theta}_{k}-\theta_{0})}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\tau^{2})}}\sim N\left((\theta^{*}-\theta_{0})\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\tau^{2})},1\right).

However, the estimator
\hat{\theta}_{k}
may be biased depending on how it is constructed.
A representative example is the log odds ratio
(
Bhaumik et al. 2012
)
.

Example 1

(Bias of the log odds ratio)
.

Suppose
Y_{j}\sim B(n_{j},p_{j})
with
\mathrm{logit}(p_{j})=\mu+j\theta
for
j=0,1
.
Then, the log odds ratio with continuity correction parameter
a\in[0,1]
is defined by

\displaystyle\log\mathrm{OR}_{a}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{Y_{1}+a}{n_{1}-Y_{1}+a}-\log\frac{Y_{0}+a}{n_{0}-Y_{0}+a},

and its expectation is given by

\displaystyle E[\log\mathrm{OR}_{a}]

\displaystyle=\theta+\left(\frac{\frac{1}{2}-a}{n_{1}}\right)(e^{\mu+\theta}-e^{-\mu-\theta})-\left(\frac{\frac{1}{2}-a}{n_{0}}\right)(e^{\mu}-e^{-\mu})+O(n^{-2}),

where
n=\min(n_{0},n_{1})
.
When
a=0
, the bias term
b_{k}
is given by

\displaystyle b_{k}=\frac{e^{\mu+\theta}-e^{-\mu-\theta}}{2n_{1}}-\frac{e^{\mu}-e^{-\mu}}{2n_{0}}+O(n^{-2}).

The result indicates that a nonlinear transformation of the outcome can induce bias in the expectation of the estimator.
Taking the presence of bias into account, we reformulate model (
2
) as

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{k}=\tilde{\theta}_{k}+b_{k},\quad\tilde{\theta}_{k}\sim N(\theta^{*},\sigma_{k}^{2}+\tau^{2}),\quad b_{k}=\frac{c_{k}}{n_{k}^{r}}=O(n^{-r}),~~~r>0,

(4)

where
c_{k}
is a constant, and
n_{k}=na_{k}
with
\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}=1
and
a_{k}>0
.
In the model (
4
), the bias
b_{k}
decreases as the within-study sample size
n_{k}
increases, but does not diminish with the number of studies
K
.
Thus, even if only one study has a small
n_{k}
, the overall bias in
\hat{\theta}
does not vanish and cannot be corrected by increasing the number of studies.

The following proposition highlights the necessity of assumption (A1) in meta-analysis under the normal-normal model.

Proposition 1
.

Assume model (
4
) holds for
\hat{\theta}_{k}
,
\tilde{\theta}_{k}
, and
b_{k}
. Then, the estimator
\hat{\theta}
defined in (
3
) satisfies

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}

\displaystyle=\theta^{*}+\frac{C_{K}}{n^{r}}+o_{p}(1)\quad\text{as }K\to\infty,

and for the true null hypothesis
H_{0}\!:\theta=\theta^{*}
, the test statistic satisfies

\displaystyle P\big(|T_{K}(\theta^{*})|>z_{a}\big)\to 1\quad\text{as }K\to\infty,

where
C_{K}=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\tau^{2})c_{k}/a_{k}^{-r}\right\}\left/\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\tau^{2})\right\}\right.
and
z_{a}
is the upper
100a\%
quantile of the standard normal distribution.

This proof is provided in Appendix
A.1
.
This result shows that when the study-specific summary statistics
\hat{\theta}_{k}
are biased (i.e., assumption (A1) is violated), such bias cannot be eliminated under the normal-normal model.
As a practical implication, when including studies with limited sample sizes or effect measures subject to substantial bias, conventional random-effects meta-analysis may not guarantee valid inference and may instead promote the rejection of a true null hypothesis.

3
Random-effects meta-analysis under the generalized linear mixed model

3.1
Expansion to exponential family

For outcomes involving nonlinear transformations, such as the log odds ratio, meta-analysis based on the normal-normal model may suffer from bias, as demonstrated in Proposition
1
.
To reduce the bias, we consider a model that is consistent with the assumed outcome distribution in each study.

When individual participant data are available, the likelihood function for study
k
is given by

\displaystyle L_{k}({\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},\tau^{2})=\int_{\Omega}\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{n_{k}}f_{Y}(y_{ki};{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},v_{k})\right\}f_{V}(v_{k};\tau^{2})\,dv_{k},

(5)

where
f_{Y}(\cdot)
denotes the density (or probability mass) function of the outcome
Y
, and
\Omega
is the support of the random effect
V_{k}
.
In general, we require access to the individual-level observations
y_{ki}
in estimation of
\beta
based on the likelihood (
5
).
In aggregate data meta-analysis, the individual-level observations are unavailable, and thus the estimator in equation (
3
) is commonly used.
As shown in Proposition
1
, however, this estimator cannot eliminate the bias in
\hat{\theta}_{k}
, even as the number of studies increases.

We consider the case in which the outcome follows a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM).
Assuming that the distribution of
Y_{ki}
belongs to the exponential family, its density function can be expressed as

\displaystyle f_{Y}(y_{ki};{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},v_{k})=\exp\left(\frac{y_{ki}({\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}+v_{k})-b({\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}+v_{k})}{a(\varphi_{k})}+c(y_{ki},\varphi_{k})\right),

(6)

where
\varphi_{k}
is the dispersion parameter,
a(\varphi_{k})
and
b({\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}+v_{k})
are known functions specific to the distribution, and
c(y_{ki},\varphi_{k})
is the normalizing function.

Although individual outcomes
y_{ki}
are typically not available in practice, the study-level mean
\bar{y}_{k}
is usually reported.
Since
\bar{y}_{k}
is a realization from an exponential family distribution conditional on
V_{k}=v_{k}
, and the density
f_{V}
of the random effect does not include
y_{ki}
and
x_{k}
, we have the following result:

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that
Y_{ki}
follows the exponential family distribution (
6
) and that
V_{k}
has the density
f_{V}(\cdot;\tau^{2})
. Then, given
({\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{1},\dots,{\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{K})
, the vector
(\bar{y}_{1},\dots,\bar{y}_{K})
is a sufficient statistic for the parameters
({\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},\tau^{2})
, and the likelihood function for study
k
can be written as

\displaystyle L_{k}({\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},\tau^{2};\bar{y}_{k},{\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k},\varphi_{k})

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n_{k}}e^{c(y_{ki},\varphi_{k})}\right)\int_{\Omega}\left\{\exp\left(\frac{n_{k}(\bar{y}_{k}({\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}+v_{k})-b({\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}+v_{k}))}{a(\varphi_{k})}\right)\right\}f_{V}(v_{k};\tau^{2})dv_{k}.

(7)

This proof is provided in Appendix
A.2
.
Lemma
1
implies that if
Y_{ki}
follows a distribution in the exponential family, then the parameters
({\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},\tau^{2})
can be estimated using only the study-level means
\bar{y}_{k}
, even when the individual-level observations
y_{ki}
are unavailable.
Under the normal-normal model, the likelihood function in equation (
7
) has a closed-form expression.
In contrast, when the individual-level outcomes follow an exponential family distribution, the likelihood involves an intractable integral that must be approximated numerically.
Common numerical integration techniques include the Laplace approximation, adaptive Gaussian-Hermite quadrature, and penalized quasi-likelihood
(
Ju et al. 2020
)
.
Among these techniques, Gaussian-Hermite quadrature and Laplace approximation allow fast computation.
However, maintaining accuracy of the integration over the entire domain
\Omega
for arbitrary values of
\tau^{2}
requires adaptively adjusting the integration region, which can be computationally expensive if performed separately for each study.

A simpler numerical approach is Monte Carlo integration. That is,

\displaystyle\log L_{k}({\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},\tau^{2};\bar{y}_{k},{\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k},\varphi_{k})

\displaystyle\propto\log\int_{\Omega}\exp\left(\frac{n_{k}\left(\bar{y}_{k}({\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}+v_{k})-b({\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}+v_{k})\right)}{a(\varphi_{k})}\right)f_{V}(v_{k};\tau^{2})\,dv_{k}

\displaystyle\approx\log\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\exp\left(\frac{n_{k}\left(\bar{y}_{k}({\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}+v^{(b)})-b({\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}+v^{(b)})\right)}{a(\varphi_{k})}\right),

(8)

where the integration over
v_{k}
is independent across studies.
A single set of samples
v^{(1)},\dots,v^{(B)}
can be independently drawn from
f_{V}(\cdot;\tau^{2})
and shared across all
K
studies for numerical integration.
Monte Carlo integration achieves a convergence rate of
O(B^{-1/2})
, but the repeated likelihood evaluations required for profile likelihood estimation result in a substantial computational burden.
To reduce the computational burden, we improve the convergence rate to
O(B^{-1}\log B)
by using quasi-Monte Carlo integration
(
Sobol and Asotsky 2003
)
.
In quasi-Monte Carlo methods, a low-discrepancy sequence
(u_{1},\dots,u_{B})\in[0,1]^{B}
is generated, and the samples of random effects are obtained as
v^{(b)}=F_{V}^{-1}(u_{b})
, where
F_{V}^{-1}
denotes the quantile function of
V
.

The parameters
({\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},\tau^{2})
can be estimated by MLE
(\hat{{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}},\hat{\tau}^{2})
based on the likelihood (
3.1
), where

\displaystyle(\hat{{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}},\hat{\tau}^{2})=\mathop{\rm arg~max}\limits_{({\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},\tau^{2})}\log L({\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},\tau^{2}),

(9)

and
L({\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},\tau^{2})=\prod_{k=1}^{K}L_{k}({\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},\tau^{2})
.
Although this optimization does not admit a closed-form solution, it can be computed numerically using only the study-level data
\{n_{k},\bar{y}_{k},x_{k},\varphi_{k}\}_{k=1,\dots,K}
, without requiring access to individual-level data.
Moreover, because the estimator (
9
) relies on the same likelihood as that based on the full individual participant data, the estimator inherits the consistency and asymptotic normality properties under standard regularity conditions
(
Hoadley 1971
;
Philippou and Roussas 1975
)
.
This approach retains the consistency and asymptotic normality properties of maximum likelihood estimation without requiring large sample sizes in individual studies. It therefore enables reliable inference even for studies with small sample sizes or effect measures involving nonlinear transformations, provided that the number of studies or the total sample size increases.

3.2
Profile likelihood interval estimation with simple Bartlett correction

When the number of studies is small, it is well known that the between-study variance tends to be underestimated.
In the normal-normal model, restricted maximum likelihood estimation is often used to mitigate this underestimation of
\tau^{2}
.
However, for interval estimation of
\beta
, the variance of
\hat{{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}}
also tends to be underestimated. As a result, the confidence intervals based on normal approximation become overly narrow.
A more reliable approach for constructing confidence intervals is the profile likelihood method.
Let
\beta_{\ell}
denote the parameter of interest, and define the vector
{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}(b_{\ell})=(\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{\ell-1},b_{\ell},\beta_{\ell+1},\dots,\beta_{p})
.
The profile likelihood ratio statistic is asymptotically chi-squared distributed as follows:

\displaystyle T(\beta_{\ell}^{0})

\displaystyle=-2\left\{\log L(\hat{{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}}(\beta_{\ell}^{0}),\tilde{\tau}^{2}(\beta_{\ell}^{0}))-\log L(\hat{{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}},\hat{\tau}^{2})\right\}\xrightarrow{d}\chi^{2}_{1}\quad(K\to\infty),

where
\chi^{2}_{1}
denotes the chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom,
\xrightarrow{d}
denotes convergence in distribution, and the constrained maximum likelihood (CML) estimates under the constraint
\beta_{\ell}=\beta_{\ell}^{0}
are obtained as

\displaystyle(\hat{{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}}(\beta_{\ell}^{0}),\tilde{\tau}^{2}(\beta_{\ell}^{0}))

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm arg~max}\limits_{(\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{\ell-1},\beta_{\ell+1},\dots,\beta_{p},\tau^{2})}\log L({\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}(\beta_{\ell}^{0}),\tau^{2}).

Using the asymptotic chi-squared approximation of the profile likelihood, the resulting confidence interval attains the nominal coverage level when the number of studies is sufficiently large.
However, in small-sample settings, such as when
K=5
, the nominal level may not be maintained.
In such cases, conservative interval estimation based on the Bartlett correction, which improves the approximation error of the chi-squared distribution for
T(\beta_{\ell}^{0})
from
O(K^{-1})
to
O(K^{-2})
, is useful
(
Noma 2011
)
.
Let
T^{*}(\beta_{\ell}^{0})
denote the Bartlett-corrected version of
T(\beta_{\ell}^{0})
, defined as

\displaystyle T^{*}(\beta_{\ell}^{0})=\frac{T(\beta_{\ell}^{0})}{1+2C^{*}(\beta_{\ell}^{0})}.

The correction term
C^{*}(\beta_{\ell}^{0})
is given by (equation (4) in
Lawley 1956
)

\displaystyle C^{*}(\beta_{\ell})=\frac{1}{2K}\left\{l_{2}^{-2}\left(\frac{1}{4}l_{4}-l_{31}+l_{22}\right)-l_{2}^{-3}\left(\frac{5}{12}l_{3}^{2}-2l_{3}l_{21}+2l_{21}^{2}\right)\right\},

where

\displaystyle l_{r}

\displaystyle=E\left[\frac{\partial^{r}l}{\partial\beta_{\ell}^{r}}\right],\quad l_{rs}=\frac{\partial^{s}l_{r}}{\partial\beta_{\ell}^{s}},\quad l(\beta_{\ell})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log L_{k}(\hat{{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}}(\beta_{\ell}),\tilde{\tau}^{2}),\quad r=1,2,3,4,\quad s=1,2.

Since computing
C^{*}(\beta_{\ell})
requires the third- and fourth-order derivatives of the log-likelihood function, it is difficult to evaluate analytically when
L({\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},\tau^{2})
involves an integral.
Therefore, we consider an approximate Bartlett correction using the correction term
C(\beta_{\ell}^{0})
derived under the normal-normal model
(
Noma 2011
)
:

\displaystyle C(\beta_{\ell}^{0})=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\sigma_{k}^{2}+\tilde{\tau}^{2})^{-3}}{\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\sigma_{k}^{2}+\tilde{\tau}^{2})^{-1}\right\}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\sigma_{k}^{2}+\tilde{\tau}^{2})^{-2}\right\}}.

Using this expression, we define an approximate Bartlett-corrected likelihood ratio statistic as

\displaystyle\tilde{T}(\beta_{\ell}^{0})=\frac{T(\beta_{\ell}^{0})}{1+2C(\beta_{\ell}^{0})}.

Since
C(\beta_{\ell}^{0})>0
, this correction yields more conservative interval estimates than those based directly on
T(\beta_{\ell}^{0})
, and improves the asymptotic behavior with respect to the number of studies.
However, this correction still relies on the asymptotic normality that results from increasing the within-study sample sizes.
Accordingly, for the number of studies
K
and the within-study sample sizes
n_{1},\dots,n_{K}
, the following result holds:

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that
Y_{ki}
follows the exponential family distribution (
6
) and
v_{1},\dots,v_{K}\sim N(0,\tau^{2})
. Then, for any
\beta_{\ell}=\beta_{\ell}^{0}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{T}(\beta_{\ell}^{0})

\displaystyle=T^{*}(\beta_{\ell}^{0})+O_{p}(n^{-1/2})=\chi_{1}^{2}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}+K^{-2}).

This proof is provided in Appendix
A.3
.
Therefore, the
100(1-\alpha)\%
confidence interval for
\beta_{\ell}
is defined as the region satisfying

\displaystyle\tilde{T}(\beta_{\ell})\leq q_{\chi^{2}_{1},\alpha},

where
q_{\chi^{2}_{1},a}
denotes the upper
100a\%
quantile of the chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom.

In the case of the normal-normal model,
C^{*}(\beta_{\ell}^{0})=C(\beta_{\ell}^{0})
holds, and hence
\tilde{T}(\beta_{\ell}^{0})=\chi^{2}_{1}+O(K^{-2})
.
In this setting, the chi-squared approximation improves as the number of studies increases, regardless of the within-study sample sizes.
Another advantage of using
C(\beta_{\ell}^{0})
is that it always satisfies
C(\beta_{\ell}^{0})>0
, which implies
\tilde{T}(\beta_{\ell}^{0})<T(\beta_{\ell}^{0})
.
Therefore, the confidence interval based on
\tilde{T}(\beta_{\ell}^{0})
is always wider than that based on
T(\beta_{\ell}^{0})
.
Moreover, since
C(\beta_{\ell}^{0})=O(K^{-1})
, we have
\tilde{T}(\beta_{\ell}^{0})\to T(\beta_{\ell}^{0})
in probability as
K\to\infty
.
These properties imply that the use of
\tilde{T}(\beta_{\ell}^{0})
yields confidence intervals that are consistently more conservative than those based on
T(\beta_{\ell}^{0})
, while maintaining the same asymptotic behavior as the number of studies increases.
This approach is thus useful both when the number of studies is small and when it is sufficiently large.
From a practical standpoint, this method mitigates the risk of overly narrow confidence intervals, particularly when meta-analyzing studies with limited sample sizes, and thus helps reduce the likelihood of overconfident conclusions in clinical decision-making.

4
Numerical simulation

4.1
Simulation setup

We evaluate the performance of meta-analysis methods under the normal-normal model and compare it with the proposed method under the GLMM framework.
The evaluation indices include bias, coverage probability, and the length of the 95% confidence interval.
The methods compared are as follows:
(i) the DerSimonian-Laird estimator under the normal-normal model (nDL);
(ii) the Bartlett-corrected conservative interval estimation proposed by
Noma 2011
(nPLBC);
(iii) the exact inference on the normal-normal model by
Michael et al. 2019
(nMI);
(iv) the likelihood ratio-based confidence interval under the GLMM (gPL); and
(v) the simplified Bartlett-corrected interval under the GLMM (gPLSBC).

Individual participant data are generated from three types of distributions for
f_{Y}
: binomial, Poisson, and gamma.
The data-generating assumes normally distributed random effects
v_{1},\dots,v_{K}\sim N(0,\tau^{2})
and is performed by

\displaystyle\theta_{k}=g(\mu_{k})=\theta_{0}+v_{k}.

The specifications of the link function
g(\mu_{k})
, the transformed parameter
\theta_{k}
, the dispersion parameter
a(\varphi_{k})
, and the cumulant function
b(\theta_{k})
for each model, along with additional simulation details, are provided in Appendix
B
.

We fix
\theta_{0}=-2
and first consider the scenario where the between-study variance is fixed at
\tau^{2}=1
and the number of studies varies as
K=5,8,10,30,50
.
Next, we fix
K=5
and vary the between-study variance as
\tau^{2}=0.5,1.0,2.0
.
The sample size
n_{k}
for each study is determined as the integer part of a random draw from the uniform distribution
U(15,150)
.
All simulation results are based on 10,000 replications.

4.2
Simulation results

The results for the scenario with fixed between-study variance
\tau^{2}=1
are presented in Figure
1
, while those for the scenario with a fixed number of studies
K=5
are presented in Figure
2
.

Regarding bias, the methods assuming the normal-normal model (nDL, nMI, and nPLBC) exhibited noticeable bias, particularly under the binomial and Poisson models, and mild bias under the gamma model.
As shown in Proposition
1
, this bias does not diminish as the number of studies increases, and becomes more pronounced as
\tau^{2}
increases.
In contrast, the GLMM-based methods (gPL and gPLSBC) showed slight bias when the number of studies was small, but the bias decreased as
K
increased and became negligible at
K=10
.

For the coverage probability of the 95% confidence intervals, nDL consistently fell below the nominal level under the binomial and Poisson models due to bias, and also failed to maintain nominal coverage under the gamma model when the between-study variance was large.
As
K
increased, the coverage further deteriorated, particularly in the binomial and Poisson settings.
Although nMI and nPLBC maintained nominal coverage when
K=5
, their coverage probability declined with increasing
K
.
Under the gamma model, nPLBC maintained nominal coverage regardless of
K
or
\tau^{2}
, but tended to be overly conservative when the number of studies was small.
The gPL method attained nominal coverage when
K
was sufficiently large, but underperformed when
K
was small.
In contrast, gPLSBC consistently maintained the nominal coverage level across all outcome models, values of
\tau^{2}
, and numbers of studies. This consistent performance indicates that gPLSBC is the most reliable method among those considered.

Regarding the length of the 95% confidence intervals, nDL produced the shortest intervals in most settings, followed by gPL.
Methods incorporating the Bartlett correction (nPLBC and gPLSBC) yielded longer intervals.
For the binomial and Poisson models, gPLSBC yielded slightly longer intervals than nPLBC, whereas for the gamma model, nPLBC yielded longer intervals than gPLSBC.

In summary, gPL and gPLSBC provided nearly unbiased estimation even in scenarios where nDL and nPLBC were biased.
In particular, gPLSBC achieved valid confidence interval estimation that maintained nominal coverage regardless of the outcome model, between-study variance, or number of studies.
gPLSBC mitigates overconfident inference even in the presence of small studies or non-normally distributed outcomes.

5
Re-analysis for three real data meta-analyses

This section presents three real-data meta-analyses in which the outcomes follow the binomial, Poisson, and gamma distributions, respectively.

5.1
Data analysis: binomial outcome

Chu et al. 2020
conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to investigate the optimal physical distance for preventing human-to-human transmission of SARS-CoV-2 and COVID-19.
Among the studies included in the meta-analysis, seven non-randomized studies examined the effect of maintaining a distance greater than one meter, and were used in the COVID-19 analysis.
As the focus is on the incidence of infection between groups, a GLMM assuming that the outcome for subject
i
in group
z
of study
k
follows a Bernoulli distribution,
Y_{kzi}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_{kz})
, is appropriate.

When the parameter of interest is the log odds ratio, the treatment effect in group
z
for study
k
can be defined as

\displaystyle\theta_{kz}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{p_{kz}}{1-p_{kz}}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}z+V_{kz}.

The random effect
V_{kz}
can be modeled in various ways, such as
V_{kz}=zV_{k}
or
V_{kz}=(2z-1)V_{k}/2

(
Stijnen et al. 2010
;
Bhaumik et al. 2012
)
. In this study, we assume that
V_{kz}
follows a normal distribution
N(0,\tau^{2})
, independently across all groups and studies, to account for heterogeneity at both the group and study levels.
The log-likelihood function for this model is given by

\displaystyle\log L({\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},\tau^{2})

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{z=0}^{1}\log\int_{\Omega}\exp\left\{n_{kz}\bar{y}_{kz}\theta_{kz}-n_{kz}\log(1+e^{\theta_{kz}})\right\}f_{V}(v_{kz};\tau^{2})\,dv_{kz}.

In the original study, the method of
DerSimonian and Laird 1986
was used.
We reanalyzed the data using the proposed GLMM-based methods.
Figure
3
presents a forest plot comparing the original results with those obtained from our reanalysis.
For each study, the log-odds ratio was computed using both the continuity-corrected estimator and the CMLE-Fisher estimator (i.e., conditional MLE under Fisher’s exact framework).

It is important to note that Wald-type confidence intervals with continuity correction often exhibit poor coverage when zero-event cells are present
(
Fagerland et al. 2015
)
.
For instance, in Study 2, the continuity-corrected point estimate and 95% confidence interval are
-4.905\ [-9.051,\ -0.76]
, whereas the point estimate and confidence interval based on the CMLE-Fisher method are
-\infty\ [-\infty,\ -0.58]
.

Because the DL, PLBC, and MI methods under the normal-normal model rely on continuity-corrected log-odds ratios and their associated variances, the resulting meta-analytic estimates are biased toward zero, and the confidence intervals become excessively narrow.
In contrast, the GLMM-based analysis yielded
\mathrm{PL}=-5.061\ [-14.291,\ -0.879]
and
\mathrm{PLSBC}=-5.061\ [-17.726,\ -0.004]
, which more appropriately reflect between-group differences while maintaining accurate interval coverage.
These findings highlight that, for binomial outcomes with zero-event cells, the GLMM-based PLSBC method can provide more accurate and conservative interval estimates than conventional normal-normal approaches, thereby avoiding overconfident conclusions in meta-analyses with few studies.

5.2
Data analysis: Poisson outcome

Takayasu arteritis (TAK) is a rare large-vessel vasculitis that disproportionately affects women.

Rutter et al. 2021
conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of eleven population- or center-based cohort studies published between 2009 and 2018 to estimate the global incidence of TAK.
A subgroup analysis by sex (Fig. 2 in
Rutter et al. 2021
) reported a markedly higher incidence in women (2.00 per million person-years; 95% CI: 1.29–3.11) than in men (0.28 per million person-years; 95% CI: 0.14–0.55), consistent with the well-established female predominance.
Because this sex difference is likely attributable to hormonal, genetic, and immunological mechanisms, sex-stratified reporting is standard in TAK epidemiology.
In our reanalysis, we included sex, publication year, and their interaction as covariates to evaluate whether temporal trends in incidence differ by sex across studies.

Assume that
t_{ki}
denotes the observed person-years for subject
i
in study
k
, and that the number of events
Y_{ki}
follows a Poisson distribution
Poisson(\mu_{k}t_{ki})
.
We define the incidence ratio (IR) as the effect measure
\theta_{ki}=\log\mu_{k}t_{ki}
.
The corresponding log-likelihood function is

\displaystyle\log L({\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},\tau^{2})

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\int_{\Omega}\exp\left\{t_{k}(\bar{y}_{k}\log\mu_{k}-\mu_{k})\right\}f_{V}(v_{k};\tau^{2})\,dv_{k},

where
t_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}t_{ki}
is the total person-years in study
k
and
\bar{y}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}y_{ki}/t_{k}
is the average incidence rate.
We use the following linear predictor for the log incidence:

\displaystyle\theta_{ki}

\displaystyle=\log\mu_{k}+\log t_{ki}=\beta_{0}\ (\mathrm{Intercept})+\beta_{1}\ (\mathrm{Sex})+\beta_{2}\ (\mathrm{Year})+\beta_{3}\ (\mathrm{Sex}\times\mathrm{Year})+V_{k}.

The regression lines for females are nearly identical between models, while a slight discrepancy is observed in the male regression lines.
Under the GLMM, the influence of sex on the incidence ratio appears smaller, with a reduction of approximately 0.2 in the corresponding coefficient.

No substantial difference was observed between the normal-normal model and the GLMM, suggesting that inference under the normal-normal model is adequate in this example.

Data analysis: gamma outcome

-5.72

Because ICU LoS is strictly positive and typically exhibits right-skewness, the normal-normal model may inadequately capture tail behavior and the mean-variance relationship.

We reanalyzed the ICU LoS data using a Gamma-distributed GLMM with a log link.
Let
Y_{ki}
denote the ICU LoS for subject
i
in study
k
, which is assumed to follow a gamma distribution
Gamma(\gamma_{1k},\gamma_{2k})
with shape parameter
\gamma_{1k}
and scale parameter
\gamma_{2k}
, independently across subjects.
The treatment effect for group
j
in study
k
is defined using the log link as

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{k}=\log\bar{y}_{k},\quad\sigma_{k}^{2}=\frac{s_{k}^{2}}{\bar{y}_{k}^{2}},

where
\bar{y}_{k}
is the sample mean and
s_{k}^{2}
is the sample variance.
The study-specific gamma parameters are estimated as

\gamma_{1k}=\hat{\theta}_{k}^{2}/\sigma_{k}^{2}
and

\gamma_{2k}=\sigma_{k}^{2}/\hat{\theta}_{k}
,
allowing for both skewness and heteroscedasticity.

The corresponding log-likelihood is given by

\displaystyle\log L({\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},\tau^{2})\propto\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\int_{\Omega}\exp\left\{\frac{n_{k}\bar{y}_{k}\theta_{k}+n_{k}\log(\theta_{k})}{\sigma_{k}}\right\}f_{V}(v_{k};\tau^{2})\,dv_{k}.

We included only Intercept and Treatment as covariates, using the following model:

\displaystyle\theta_{k}

\displaystyle=\log\mu_{k}=\beta_{0}\ (\mathrm{Intercept})+\beta_{1}\ (\mathrm{Treatment})+V_{k}.

6
Conclusion

This study proposed a method for random-effects meta-analysis under the framework of GLMM, in which study-specific treatment effects are modeled using distributions from the exponential family and between-study heterogeneity is captured via random effects.
The proposed method yields consistent estimators solely through an increasing number of studies and does not require the assumption, which is often implicit under the normality assumptions (A2) and (A3), that each study has a sufficiently large sample size.
This property preserves a key advantage of meta-analysis: aggregating multiple small studies can produce reliable evidence, even when individual studies lack statistical power.
Unlike conventional methods relying on the normal-normal model, which may fail to deliver valid inference under biased or non-normal outcomes (as demonstrated in Proposition
1
), the proposed approach enables valid inference by appropriately modeling the distribution of study-specific estimates.

The framework assumes that individual-level outcomes follow a distribution in the exponential family. This assumption allows researchers to specify flexible models as long as the outcome distribution belongs to this family.
While this study focused on commonly used distributions such as binomial, Poisson, and gamma, more complex outcome models are also compatible.
Examples include the negative binomial distribution for overdispersed counts
(
Anscombe 1949
;
Lindén and Mäntyniemi 2011
)
, the Conway-Maxwell-Poisson distribution for both overdispersion and underdispersion
(
Shmueli et al. 2005
;
Gupta et al. 2014
)
, and the Dirichlet distribution for compositional data
(
Hijazi and Jernigan 2009
;
Douma and Weedon 2019
)
.
Although meta-analytic applications involving such complex distributions remain rare, partly due to the limited availability of detailed data, the GLMM framework developed in this study provides a principled approach to extending meta-analysis beyond the standard normal assumptions.

Confidence intervals under the GLMM framework can be constructed using the profile likelihood method.
However, when the number of studies is small, the coverage probability may fall below the nominal level.
In such cases, the Bartlett correction serves as a useful remedy.
Because deriving the Bartlett correction term for arbitrary distributions in the exponential family and arbitrary link functions is analytically challenging,
we proposed a simplified Bartlett correction that reuses the correction term derived under the normal-normal model.
This approach consistently yields more conservative confidence intervals than the uncorrected profile likelihood method, although it may be overly conservative in theory when the number of studies is small.
As a more accurate alternative, the bootstrap-based Bartlett correction can be considered.
However, even when estimating a single regression coefficient, the bootstrap method imposes a substantial computational burden.
As a result, its application to meta-regression becomes difficult
(
Rocke 1989
;
Noma et al. 2018
)
.
In contrast, the simplified Bartlett correction is analytically tractable and therefore advantageous for models with unknown random effects.

A limitation of this study is that the proposed method is restricted to models within the exponential family.
For example, survival outcomes, which are frequently analyzed in meta-analyses, are typically modeled using the Cox proportional hazards model.
Since the proportional hazards model cannot be easily formulated within the exponential family, the proposed method cannot be directly applied to such settings.
In these cases, including survival data, researchers still need to rely on normal approximations, as in conventional approaches.
Developing methods that directly accommodate outcome distributions outside the exponential family remains an important direction for future research.

In summary, the proposed random-effects meta-analysis method under the GLMM provides theoretically justified inference, even in situations where bias arises under the normal-normal model or where studies with small sample sizes are included.
Because the simple Bartlett correction yields conservative interval estimates, the method can produce valid confidence intervals even when the number of studies is small.
This feature ensures robust estimation and hypothesis testing across a wide range of practical scenarios, regardless of the number of studies or the degree of between-study heterogeneity.
This implies that, even in clinical studies with limited sample sizes or non-normal outcomes, the method can provide reliable evidence without overconfidence. In turn, this supports sound decision-making and the development of evidence-based guidelines.

Proof.

Under the model (
4

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\tau^{2})\hat{\theta}_{k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\tau^{2})}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\tau^{2})\tilde{\theta}_{k}+b_{k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\tau^{2})}

, we have

\displaystyle|\tilde{T}(\beta_{\ell})-T^{*}(\beta_{\ell})|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{T(\beta_{\ell})}{1+2C(\beta_{\ell})}-\frac{T(\beta_{\ell})}{1+2C^{*}(\beta_{\ell})}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{T(\beta_{\ell})}{1+2C^{*}(\beta_{\ell})}\frac{1+2C^{*}(\beta_{\ell})}{1+2C(\beta_{\ell})}-\frac{T(\beta_{\ell})}{1+2C^{*}(\beta_{\ell})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{2T(\beta_{\ell})}{(1+2C^{*}(\beta_{\ell}))(1+2C(\beta_{\ell}))}\right|\left|C^{*}(\beta_{\ell})-C(\beta_{\ell})\right|

\displaystyle=M_{1}\left|C^{*}(\beta_{\ell})-C(\beta_{\ell})\right|,

where

\displaystyle M_{1}

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{2T(\beta_{\ell})}{(1+2C^{*}(\beta_{\ell}))(1+2C(\beta_{\ell}))}\right|\leq 2|T(\beta_{\ell})|.

From the theorem of
Wilks 1938
, we have
T(\beta_{\ell})=\chi_{1}^{2}+O_{p}(K^{-1/2})
. Then, we have
M_{1}\leq 2|T(\beta_{\ell})|=2\chi_{1}^{2}+O_{p}(K^{-1/2})=O_{p}(1)
.

We next show
\left|C^{*}(\beta_{\ell})-C(\beta_{\ell})\right|=O(n^{-1/2})
.
The term of Bartlett correction is given as (equation (4) in
Lawley 1956
)

\displaystyle C^{*}(\beta_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2K}\left\{l_{2}^{-2}\left(\frac{1}{4}l_{4}-l_{31}+l_{22}\right)-l_{2}^{-3}\left(\frac{5}{12}l_{3}^{2}-2l_{3}l_{21}+2l_{21}^{2}\right)\right\},

where

\displaystyle l_{r}

\displaystyle=E\left[\frac{\partial^{r}l}{\partial\beta_{\ell}^{r}}\right],~~~l_{rs}=\frac{\partial^{s}l_{r}}{\partial\beta_{\ell}^{s}},~~~r=1,2,3,4,~~s=1,2

and
l(\beta_{\ell})
is the profile likelihood function for
\beta_{\ell}
,

\displaystyle l(\beta_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log L_{k}(\hat{{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}}(\beta_{\ell}),\tilde{\tau}^{2}(\beta_{\ell})).

Because
Y
is a random variable belonging to the exponential family, we have
E[Y]<\infty
,
E[|Y|^{s}]<\infty,s=1,2,3
, and the distribution function of
Y
is smooth.
Then, we can apply Theorem 2 in
Bhattacharya et al. 1978
to
L_{k}
, and we have below uniform convergence as
n_{k}\to\infty
such that

\displaystyle\log L_{k}({\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},\tau^{2})

\displaystyle=\log\phi(g(\bar{y}_{k});{\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},\sigma_{k}^{2}+\tau^{2})+O(n_{k}^{-1/2}),

where
\phi(\cdot;{\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}},\sigma_{k}^{2}+\tau^{2})
is the probability density function of normal distribution with mean
{\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}
and variance
\sigma_{k}^{2}+\tau^{2}
.
We can estimate the normal approximated log profile likelihood function of
l(\beta_{\ell})
using

\displaystyle\hat{l}(\beta_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\phi(g(\bar{y}_{k});{\text{\boldmath$x$}}_{k}^{T}\tilde{{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}}(\beta_{\ell}),\sigma_{k}^{2}+\tilde{\tau}^{2}(\beta_{\ell})).

From the relationship between
\log L_{k}
and
\log\phi
, we have
l(\beta_{\ell})=\hat{l}(\beta_{\ell})+O(n^{-1/2})
.
Since
l(\beta_{\ell})
and
\hat{l}(\beta_{\ell})
are smooth, we have

\displaystyle l_{r}^{m}

\displaystyle=\hat{l}_{r}^{m}+O(n^{-1/2}),~~~l_{rs}^{m}=\hat{l}_{rs}^{m}+O(n^{-1/2}),~~~m=-3,-2,1,

from the chain rule and Leibniz rule.
Because
C^{*}
is the sum of
l_{r}^{m}
and
l_{rs}^{m}
,

\displaystyle C^{*}(\beta_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2K}\left\{\hat{l}_{2}^{-2}\left(\frac{1}{4}\hat{l}_{4}-\hat{l}_{31}+\hat{l}_{22}\right)-\hat{l}_{2}^{-3}\left(\frac{5}{12}\hat{l}_{3}^{2}-2\hat{l}_{3}\hat{l}_{21}+2\hat{l}_{21}^{2}\right)\right\}+O(n^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=C(\beta_{\ell})+O(n^{-1/2}).

Thus, for any
\beta_{\ell}
, there exist a constant
M_{2}>0
such that
\left|C^{*}(\beta_{\ell})-C(\beta_{\ell})\right|<M_{2}n^{-1/2}
.
Then, we have the desired inequality as

\displaystyle|\tilde{T}(\beta_{\ell})-T^{*}(\beta_{\ell})|

\displaystyle<M_{1}\left|C^{*}(\beta_{\ell})-C(\beta_{\ell})\right|

\displaystyle<O_{p}(1)M_{2}n^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

Because
T^{*}(\beta_{\ell})=\chi_{1}^{2}+O_{p}(K^{-2})
, we have
\tilde{T}(\beta_{\ell})=\chi_{1}^{2}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}+K^{-2})
.
∎

Appendix B
Simulation detail for each individual distribution

We present the parameter settings for the binomial, Poisson, and gamma distributions used in the simulation study.

B.1
Binomial distribution
Bin(n_{k},p_{k})

The parameters in the density function of
Y_{ki}
given in (
6
) are specified as follows:

\displaystyle\theta_{k}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{p_{k}}{1-p_{k}},\quad a(\varphi_{k})=1,\quad b(\theta_{k})=n_{k}\log(1+e^{\theta_{k}}),\quad c(y_{ki},\varphi_{k})=-\log\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n_{k}\\
y_{ki}\end{array}\right).

With this specification, the density function is given by

\displaystyle f_{Y}(y_{ki})

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{y_{ki}\theta_{k}-n_{k}\log(1+e^{\theta_{k}})+\log\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n_{k}\\
y_{ki}\end{array}\right)\right\}.

This corresponds to a model where the outcome follows a binomial distribution with the canonical link function.
Since the dispersion parameter
\varphi_{k}
does not appear in the distribution, no estimation of nuisance parameters is required.

B.2
Poisson distribution
Poisson(\mu_{k}t_{ki})

The parameters in the density function of
Y_{ki}
given in (
6
) are specified as follows:

\displaystyle\theta_{ki}

\displaystyle=\log\mu_{k}+\log t_{ki},\quad a(\varphi_{k})=1,\quad b(\theta_{ki})=e^{\theta_{ki}}=\mu_{k}t_{ki},\quad c(y_{ki},\varphi_{k})=-\log(y_{ki}!).

With this specification, the density function is given by

\displaystyle f_{Y}(y_{ki})

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{y_{ki}\theta_{ki}-e^{\theta_{ik}}-\log(y_{ki}!)\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{y_{ki}\log\mu_{k}-\mu_{k}t_{ki}+y_{ki}\log t_{ki}-\log(y_{ki}!)\right\}.

This corresponds to a model where the outcome follows a Poisson distribution with the canonical link function.
As in the binomial case, the dispersion parameter
\varphi_{k}
does not appear in the distribution, and thus no estimation of nuisance parameters is required.

B.3
Gamma distribution
Gamma(a,b)

Let
a>0
be the shape parameter and
b>0
the scale parameter. The distribution can be reparameterized as
a=1/\varphi_{k}
,
b=\varphi_{k}/\mu_{k}
.
The parameters in the density function of
Y_{ki}
given in (
6
) are specified as follows:

\displaystyle\theta_{k}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mu_{k}},\quad a(\varphi_{k})=\varphi_{k},\quad b(\theta_{k})=-\log(\theta_{k}),\quad c(y_{ki},\varphi_{k})=\left(\frac{1}{\varphi_{k}}-1\right)\log(y_{ki})-\frac{1}{\varphi_{k}}\log(\varphi_{k})-\log\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\varphi_{k}}\right).

With this specification, the density function is expressed as

\displaystyle f_{Y}(y_{ki})

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\frac{y_{ki}\theta_{k}+\log(\theta_{k})}{\varphi_{k}}+\left(\frac{1}{\varphi_{k}}-1\right)\log(y_{ki})-\frac{1}{\varphi_{k}}\log(\varphi_{k})-\log\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\varphi_{k}}\right)\right\}.

This corresponds to a model where the outcome follows a gamma distribution with a log link function.
While the binomial and Poisson models used the canonical link, the canonical link for the gamma distribution is
g(\mu_{k})=-1/\mu_{k}
, which requires
\mu_{k}>0
and is often numerically unstable.
Therefore, we adopted the log link function.

In the simulation, the dispersion parameter
\varphi_{k}
was set as

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}=\frac{1}{3}\left(1+4\frac{k-1}{K}\right),\quad k=1,\dots,K.

Since
\varphi_{k}
is unknown in real applications, we estimated it using the sample mean
\bar{y}_{k}
and variance
\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}
from each study as
\hat{\varphi}_{k}=\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}/\bar{y}_{k}^{2}
, and substituted the estimated value into
f_{Y}(\cdot)
.
