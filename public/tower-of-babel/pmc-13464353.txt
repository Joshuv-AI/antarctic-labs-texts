Identifying high-risk and actionable profiles for prevention in hypertensive patients with concurrent dyslipidemia: a population-based retrospective study
Authors: Yizhou Xu, Hansong Huang, Jiaqi Wang, Yue Wu, Jian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension and dyslipidemia are major global risk factors for cardiovascular diseases, with their prevalence increasing annually. In China, hypertension and dyslipidemia exhibit a “dual high” epidemic characteristic. To contribute to the reduction of their prevalence, this study analyzes factors related to dyslipidemia in hypertensive patients from a clinical perspective.MethodsThis study analyzed data from 5,018 hypertensive patients recruited from 110 hospitals across China. Participants were categorized into a dyslipidemia group and a non-dyslipidemia group based on diagnostic criteria. Variables selected based on clinical relevance and univariate associations were included in a multivariate logistic regression model to determine independent factors associated with the co-occurrence of hypertension and dyslipidemia.ResultsAnalysis of the dyslipidemia group and non-dyslipidemia group revealed that the dyslipidemia group exhibited significantly higher proportions (p < 0.05) in multiple disease conditions (stroke, angina pectoris, atrial fibrillation), medication use (beta-blockers, antiplatelet agents, etc.), and family histories of certain diseases (hypertension, coronary heart disease, diabetes) compared to the non-dyslipidemia group. Regression analysis further identified BMI, family history of hypertension, stroke, angina pectoris, atrial fibrillation, obstructive sleep apnea, and family history of coronary heart disease as significant correlates of hypertension combined with dyslipidemia.ConclusionsHypertension comorbid with dyslipidemia demonstrates significant correlations with body mass index (BMI) and familial predisposition (family histories of hypertension and coronary heart disease). Furthermore, individual medical histories and pharmacological regimens exhibit substantial associations with disease progression. These findings suggest potential targets for a three-tier prevention approach. With appropriate validation in prospective studies, prioritized screening and early intervention for high-risk individuals may contribute to halting or delaying disease progression.

Introduction

Hypertension and dyslipidemia are leading global risk factors for cardiovascular diseases, with their epidemiological characteristics exhibiting significant regional and population disparities. Over the past decade, studies have revealed a continuous rise in the prevalence of both conditions, affecting approximately 23.2% of the global adult population. China demonstrates a concurrent high prevalence pattern of hypertension and dyslipidemia (“dual high” epidemic characteristic), with an escalating disease burden [1]. In 2015, China had 244.5 million hypertension patients aged ≥ 18 years, and hypertension-related deaths accounted for over 25% of all-cause mortality nationwide [2]. By 2022, the age-standardized prevalence rate of adult hypertension surged to 27.6% (a 31% increase from 2015), while dyslipidemia prevalence exceeded 40%, accompanied by a significant rise in metabolic comorbidities [3, 4]. The frequent co-occurrence of hypertension and dyslipidemia creates mutual exacerbation effects that accelerate cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease progression [5]. The Chinese government has established the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (China CDC) to continuously monitor national data on chronic disease prevalence rates and other critical health indicators [6, 7], additionally, it formulates targeted policies at the national health policy level to curb the rising burden of chronic diseases [8]. To reduce dyslipidemia incidence among hypertensive populations, this study analyzes patient data on familial predisposition, baseline health metrics, and clinical history to identify shared characteristics of high-risk profiles. The findings aim to inform the development of a three-tier prevention framework, providing actionable insights for primary prevention (preventing disease onset) and secondary prevention (arresting disease progression).

Methods

Study subjects

This study analyzed data from 5,018 hypertensive patients recruited from 110 hospitals across China between 2015 and 2020. This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital.

The participants were required: (1)diagnosed hypertension, ༈2༉age ≥ 18 years. The participants were excluded based on the following criteria: (1)diagnosis of secondary hypertension, (2) pregnancy or lactation, (3) severe comorbidities with a life expectancy < 2 years, (4) newly diagnosed cardiovascular/cerebrovascular events (disease duration < 3 months), (5) age < 18 years.

Diagnostic criteria for hypertension [9]

Clinic blood pressure (OBPM): ≥140/90 mmHg, Home blood pressure (HBPM): ≥135/85 mmHg,24-hour ambulatory blood pressure (ABPM):24-hour average: ≥130/80 mmHg, Daytime average: ≥135/85 mmHg, Nighttime average: ≥120/70 mmHg.

Diagnostic criteria for dyslipidemia [10]

Dyslipidemia refers to abnormal levels of lipids (such as cholesterol and triglycerides) in the blood resulting from genetic, metabolic, or environmental factors. It is typically diagnosed when one or more of the following criteria are met: LDL-C: > 3.4 mmol/L, TG: > 1.7 mmol/L, TC: > 5.2 mmol/L, HDL-C: < 1.0 mmol/L.

Research objective

Investigation of Factors Associated with Hypertension Comorbid with Dyslipidemia.

Data collection types

Basic information: Age, BMI, Systolic blood pressure, Diastolic blood pressure, Heart rate, Sex, Alcohol consumption, Smoking.

Medication History: Diuretics, ACEI, ARB, Beta-blockers, Antiplatelet agents, Statins, Hypoglycemic agents,

Family history: Family histories of coronary heart disease, diabetes mellitus, malignant neoplasms, stroke, hypertension.

Medical History: Cerebral stroke, TIA, cerebrovascular malformation, Angina pectoris, Myocardial infarction(MI), Atrial fibrillation (AF), Heart failure (HF), Peripheral vascular disease (PVD), Diabetes mellitus (DM), Malignant neoplasms, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), Asthma, Renal disease, Hepatic disease, Peptic ulcer disease(PUD), Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG),

Statistical analysis

Participants were stratified into DL Group (dyslipidemia group, n = 434) and the Non-DL Group (non-dyslipidemia group, n = 4,584). Continuous data that did not conform to a normal distribution are expressed as medians; Categorical variables were analyzed as percentages (%). Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test, while continuous variables were assessed via independent t-tests, with Fisher’s exact test employed as a sensitivity analysis for small-sample comparisons. To identify independent factors associated with hypertension comorbid with dyslipidemia and to address potential confounding, we constructed multivariable logistic regression models. Variable selection was based on a combination of clinical relevance from the literature and univariate associations. To enhance the interpretability and explore heterogeneity of effects, we tested a priori specified interaction terms between key variables, such as BMI category (normal vs. overweight/obese) and family history of cardiovascular diseases, etc. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp.), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF) (Table 4).

Regression analysis of factors influencing the hypertension with dyslipidemia

A near-significant association was observed for family history of diabetes (p = 0.06), suggesting a potential influence that requires further validation. In contrast, no statistically significant effects were found for age, sex, DM, renal disease, gastrointestinal ulcer, or prior PCI. (Table 3)

Discussion

Cardiovascular diseases have emerged as the primary cause of global mortality, with their incidence showing a persistent upward trend [11]. As two of the most critical risk factors for cardiovascular diseases, the clinical significance of dyslipidemia and hypertension is well-established. Given the substantial prevalence of hypertension, reducing the incidence of hyperlipidemia in this population represents a critical public health priority. This retrospective study analyzed factors associated with hypertension combined with dyslipidemia, providing insights for disease prevention in hypertensive patients and informing future prospective research.

Our findings demonstrated that compared to the Non-DL Group, the DL Group exhibited significantly higher proportions of elevated BMI, comorbidities (stroke, angina pectoris, atrial fibrillation), medication use (beta-blockers, antiplatelet agents, statins), and family histories of hypertension, coronary heart disease, or diabetes (all p < 0.05). A study conducted in 2024 noted that obesity prevalence is markedly higher in dyslipidemia patients compared to the general population [12], aligning with our observation that elevated BMI strongly correlates with dyslipidemia. Current mechanistic studies highlight shared pathways between hypertension and dyslipidemia, including dysregulation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and increased endothelin-1 production [13, 14], pathways also implicated in cerebrovascular disease progression. These overlapping mechanisms likely explain the higher prevalence of stroke, angina, atrial fibrillation, and medication use in patients with hypertension and comorbid dyslipidemia. Additionally, family histories of related diseases further elevated the risk of hypertension with comorbid dyslipidemia, consistent with the evidence underscoring familial pre disposition as a key consideration for disease prevention and management [15, 16]. Although factors such as BMI, family history, and cardiovascular comorbidities are recognized risk factors for dyslipidemia in patients with hypertension, our study provides several practical advancements. First, it is based on data from 110 hospitals in China, offering contemporary, multicenter, real-world estimates. Second, it quantifies the independent associations of these factors, with relatively narrow confidence intervals, giving the findings statistical robustness. Third, it translates these known risk factors into actionable priorities within the three-tier prevention system—for example, screening for obstructive sleep apnea, identifying angina, and collecting specific family history. Thus, the study builds a bridge between epidemiological evidence and local implementation strategies.

Regression analysis confirmed BMI as a significant factor of hypertension with comorbid dyslipidemia. For high-BMI individuals without overt disease, regular blood pressure/lipid monitoring and weight management strategies (for example, DASH diet combined with 150-min/week moderate exercise) [17, 18] are recommended to mitigate risk. For diagnosed patients, weight reduction remains critical for disease control [19]. Within public health practice, it is necessary to elevate population-level awareness of BMI as a pivotal health metric, implement evidence-based weight management campaigns to enhance health literacy, and conduct community-tailored educational initiatives aimed at optimizing public understanding of sustainable weight control strategies.

Stroke, angina, AF, and OSA showed strong positive associations with hyper tension with comorbid dyslipidemia (all with odds ratios [OR] > 1). OSA exacerbates cardiovascular risks through sympathetic activation and blood pressure fluctuations [20]. OSA increases the risk for hypertension, and increases hedonic responses to high-fat stimuli by gut microbiome neo-colonization to elevating serum triglyceride levels in susceptible population [21]. Stroke, angina, and AF represent established complications of hypertension with comorbid dyslipidemia. These findings reinforce the necessity of primary, secondary and tertiary prevention strategies [22]. Particularly in patients with OSA. In contrast, age, sex, Diabetes mellitus, and renal disease showed no significant associations. While these variables may genuinely lack clinical relevance, potential false-negative results due to retrospective data limitations (sample size constraints or measurement bias) cannot be excluded [23].

The observed links between statins, beta-blockers, antiplatelet agents, and hypertension with comorbid dyslipidemia likely reflect disease severity rather than a causal relationship. For instance, statins’ use inherently indicates preexisting dyslipidemia rather than causality. Patients prescribed antiplatelet therapy are typically those with established cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases requiring secondary prevention to mitigate disease progression. This association can inform clinical protocols to enhance systematic dyslipidemia screening and strengthen preventive measures within high-risk patients. Associations between dyslipidemia and cardiovascular comorbidities or medication use likely reflect disease clustering and indication bias, however, beyond this, the findings cannot be used for causal inference, as retrospective designs preclude causality [24].

Our findings align with the 2025 AHA/ACC hypertension guideline [25]. First, the guideline newly emphasizes OSA as a condition requiring screening; our study quantifies OSA as a very strong correlate (OR = 14.42) in a large Chinese cohort. Second, the guideline highlights family history of early-onset hypertension/stroke; we found that family history of coronary heart disease (OR = 2.20) may be a stronger driver than family history of diabetes (p = 0.06) in non-diabetic hypertensive patients. Third, the guideline’s PREVENT risk calculator includes BMI as a core variable, consistent with our finding that elevated BMI independently correlates with dyslipidemia (OR = 1.08). These comparisons show how local real-world data can complement international guidelines.

Family histories of hypertension and coronary heart disease demonstrate a strong association with hypertension combined with dyslipidemia, underscoring gene-environment interactions. Despite limited public awareness in China regarding familial risk, substantial evidence supports its prognostic value [26, 27]. Integrating family histories into risk stratification protocols could enhance early intervention targeting high-risk population. Clinical protocols should prioritize enhanced routine screening for family history of chronic diseases during primary care encounters. For individuals who have identified family histories, it is recommended to implement genetic testing for dyslipidemia predisposition complemented by biennial lipid panels and periodic health evaluations. Tailored preventive measures (exercise plans and dietary regimens et al.) should be developed based on individuals’ differences to achieve the essential objectives of public health prevention and control. Based on the real‑world evidence from diabetic patients, our findings in a hypertensive population call for population‑specific risk stratification and lipid management [28].

As China is transitioning into an aging society [29], prioritizing a three-tier prevention system [30]—encompassing primary prevention to avert disease onset, secondary prevention to manage disease progression at early stages, and tertiary prevention to mitigate complications [31]—is critical from both public health and economic perspectives. This study found that the comorbidity is strongly associated with BMI, hypertension, and a family history of coronary heart disease, with narrow confidence intervals indicating high precision. We propose that by screening individuals with elevated BMI and those with a family history of hypertension or coronary heart disease, it is possible to implement prevention strategies before disease onset (primary prevention) and to intensify weight management after diagnosis (secondary and tertiary prevention), thereby control disease progression. This approach could better control disease progression, reduce the likelihood of adverse outcomes, and ultimately prolong the overall population’s healthy lifespan while alleviating the substantial economic burden on public health systems. This is the clinical significance of our findings.

Limitations

Firstly, the collection of the data spans from 2015 to 2020, which may affect the study’s conclusion. Secondly, this study is a retrospective observational study, the retrospective design precludes causal inference; all associations are correlational only. Thirdly, this study is limited by the lack of longitudinal lipid measurements, as well as the absence of data on medication dose, duration, and adherence, and by potential center-level heterogeneity across participating hospitals. The observed associations from this study require validation through prospective studies. Fourth, due to anonymization, hospital identifiers were unavailable, precluding multilevel modeling. However, sample size distribution and outcome rates were balanced across hospitals, suggesting limited center effects. Future validation is warranted.

Conclusion

Hypertension with comorbid dyslipidemia demonstrates significant association with patients’ BMI and family histories (including hypertension and coronary heart disease). These findings suggest potential targets for a three-tier prevention approach. With appropriate validation in prospective studies, prioritized screening and early intervention for high-risk individuals (those with elevated BMI, family histories of hypertension and coronary heart disease) may contribute to halting or delaying disease progression. However, given the observational design, causal inferences should be made with caution. These findings provide critical evidence for developing clinical prevention and management strategies, while also establishing a foundation for further prospective observational study.
