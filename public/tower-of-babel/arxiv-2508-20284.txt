Sourced Carrollian Fluids Dual to Black Hole Horizons Preprint: APS/123-QED
Sercan Hüsnügil, Luis Lehner

Abstract
The (degenerate) geometry of event horizons is linked to Carrollian fluids. We investigate the behavior of event horizons via a perturbative coupling to a massless scalar field, making connections to Carrollian hydrodynamics with a driving source, and discuss the fluid equilibration in tandem with the horizon’s relaxation to equilibrium. We observe that after the perturbation dies off, the Carrollian fluid energy and momentum densities approach equilibrium as the horizon asymptotically becomes non-expanding. We connect the equilibration of the Carrollian fluid dual to the black hole horizon through the expansion of its background geometry.



I
Introduction

Fully unraveling the behavior of black holes in dynamical settings is key to our understanding of gravity. They lie at the core of some of the most extreme (potentially) observable phenomena in our universe and challenge our understanding on many fronts. Of particular current interest is to understand their nonlinear behavior at a fundamental level, even when it is tractable via numerical simulations. That is, to extract robust, first-principles information beyond specific cases.

Nonlinear behavior of field theories is notoriously difficult to explore. Perturbative approaches in such regimes become unreliable or require high-order computations, which might render them impractical.
Exploiting possible analogies with hydrodynamics—a field that has traditionally had to embrace the nonlinear regime through multiple means—provides us with complementary techniques to explore them. The early works by Damour and Znajek showed how black holes under electromagnetic fields can behave as ordinary conductors
Damour 1978
;
Znajek 1978
and how the components of the Einstein equations, when projected on the black hole horizon, can be interpreted as the equation of energy conservation and Navier–Stokes equations of a hypothetical horizon fluid
Damour 1982

Thorne et al. 1986
;
Price and Thorne 1986
;
Macdonald and Suen 1985
;
Thorne and MacDonald 1982
. There, the fluid analogy was constructed through projections on a timelike surface (the
stretched horizon
) located outside the null event horizon. Such a timelike surface bypassed the singular nature of null surfaces and helped identify a fluid system with mechanical, electrical, and thermodynamic properties. The null nature of the event horizon obscured the structure of the resulting equations and was only recently interpreted as analogous to a special theory of fluids.

A closely related link between hydrodynamics and gravity was pursued later with important results. Motivated by AdS/CFT holography
Maldacena 1999
, a duality between the long-wavelength perturbations of
black holes in the bulk and relativistic hydrodynamics on the AdS boundary was derived
Policastro et al. 2002
;
Kovtun et al. 2003
;
Bhattacharyya et al. 2008
;
Hubeny et al. 2011
. Subsequent works connected phenomena with gravitational dynamics, first in asymptotically AdS
Carrasco et al. 2012
;
Adams et al. 2014
;
Green et al. 2014
;
Raamsdonk 2008
and later in asymptotically flat spacetimes
Yang et al. 2015
;
Iuliano et al. 2025
;
Ma et al. 2025
. Combined with the membrane paradigm, this duality has also been utilized for studying transport coefficients in boundary theory, drawing insights from gravity
Iqbal and Liu 2009

In a recent parallel development, a duality of the properties of null surfaces and the vanishing speed of light,
c\rightarrow 0
, limit of relativistic hydrodynamics, named
Carrollian fluids
was shown
Donnay and Marteau 2019

In both cases, the relativistic description, which puts temporal and spatial coordinates on equal footing, no longer holds: the Galilean limit results in an absolute time, whereas the Carrollian limit yields an absolute space
Duval et al. 2014a
. Remarkably, the resulting equations—upon a suitable identification—can be linked to those obtained by the projection of Einstein equations onto a null surface; that are, Raychaudhuri and Damour equations.

Most of the work cited above on the Carrollian literature has so far focused on the kinematics of the vanishing-speed-of-light limit of relativistic theories and of null surfaces, in particular, the null infinity. While the notion of Carrollian fluids has also been studied as the limiting case of relativistic hydrodynamics, the Carrollian fluid dual to gravity has often been studied in vacuum spacetimes without a particular focus on the dynamics. Recently, Redondo-Yuste and Lehner

took a dynamical point of view and studied the Carrollian fluid dual of a spherically symmetric black hole perturbed by gravitational waves. They interpreted their results to connect the properties of the fluid, such as its viscosity, to the metric perturbations by adapting the dictionary between Carrollian fluid parameters and the geometric properties of the horizon.

In this work, we study the Carrollian fluid/gravity duality in the non-vacuum case. We follow the stretched Carrollian geometry formalism laid out in
Freidel and Jai-akson 2022
;
Freidel and Jai-akson 2024

After establishing the general form of the equations, we focus on a massless scalar field in the bulk as an instructive example. We then concentrate on an ideal system with a spherically symmetric black hole coupled to an infalling massless scalar field and solve the coupled equations for fluid parameters perturbatively to demonstrate the effects of the existence of the matter fields. Linking our results on the evolution of the horizon with the behavior of the dual Carrollian fluid, we connect the relaxation of the former to the equilibration of the latter.

II.1
Vectors, Forms & Projectors

Here, we summarize the recipe for the stretched Carrollian structure presented in
Freidel and Jai-akson 2024
for completeness. We then restrict ourselves to a particular gauge choice in the subsequent sections.

In the following, we reserve the lowercase Latin indices from the first half of the alphabet to denote the indices of tensors defined on
\mathscr{M}
. We use the letters starting from
i
for the indices intrinsic to the coordinates on
\mathscr{H}
. Finally, we use capital Latin letters to denote the coordinates intrinsic to the sphere manifold
\mathcal{S}
, which is the spacelike base manifold of the null hypersurface
\mathscr{N}
. Unless otherwise specified, we work with geometric units in which

We denote the metric on
\mathscr{M}
by

and the covariant derivative compatible with it by
\nabla_{a}
. We also define on
\mathscr{M}
: a vector field

which is tangent to
\mathscr{H}
, a normal form
n_{a}
, and a null
rigging

k^{a}

These objects, alongside their metric duals, satisfy the following relations:

\displaystyle n_{a}k^{a}

\displaystyle=1\;,

\displaystyle n_{a}l^{a}=0\;,

\displaystyle k_{a}l^{a}

\displaystyle=1\;,

\displaystyle n_{a}n^{a}

\displaystyle=2\rho\;,

\displaystyle k_{a}k^{a}=0\;,

\displaystyle l_{a}l^{a}

\displaystyle=-2\rho.

We further define a rigging projector
\Pi_{a}^{\hskip 4.0ptb}
in
\mathscr{M}
as

\Pi_{a}^{\hskip 4.0ptb}\equiv\delta_{a}^{b}-n_{a}k^{b}\;,

(1)

which projects the tensors defined on
\mathscr{M}
onto
\mathscr{H}
. Through this projector, we can relate the tangent vector field
l^{a}
to the metric dual of the normal form,
n^{a}
, as
l^{a}=n^{b}\Pi_{b}^{\hskip 4.0pta}
. Therefore, we can decompose

in terms of
l^{a}
and
k^{a}
as

n^{a}=l^{a}+2\rho k^{a}.

(2)

This decomposition implies that the vectors
n^{a}
and
l^{a}
coincide on the null surface
\mathscr{N}
since
\lim_{\rho\rightarrow 0}n^{a}=l^{a}
.

The induced metric on
\mathscr{H}
is given by the projection of the bulk metric as

\displaystyle h_{ab}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{a}^{\hskip 4.0ptc}\,g_{cd}\,\Pi_{b}^{\hskip 4.0ptd}

(3)

\displaystyle=g_{ab}-n_{a}k_{b}-n_{b}k_{a}\,.

(4)

Restricting ourselves to the indices intrinsic to
\mathscr{H}
, we now have established the stretched Carrollian structure on
\mathscr{H}
with the data
\left(l^{i},k_{i},h_{ij},\rho\right)

Freidel and Jai-akson 2024
;
Freidel and Jai-akson 2022

The stretched Carrollian structure on the timelike hypersurface connects naturally to the Carrollian structure on the null hypersurface for the vanishing stretching
\rho

\mathscr{H}
as the following

\displaystyle h_{ij}=q_{ij}-2\rho k_{i}k_{j}\,.

(5)

Here,

is the null Carrollian metric that satisfies

. Notice that
\lim_{\rho\rightarrow 0}h_{ij}=q_{ij}
.

It is also useful to introduce the horizontal projector
q_{a}^{\hskip 4.0ptb}
which is defined as

q_{a}^{\hskip 4.0ptb}\equiv\Pi_{a}^{\hskip 4.0ptb}-k_{a}l^{b}.

(6)

This projector further projects the tensors on
\mathscr{M}
to the horizontal base space of the bundle,
\mathcal{S}
. Using the horizontal projector, we can also induce the null Carrollian metric directly from the metric in the bulk as

q_{ab}=q_{a}^{\hskip 4.0ptc}q_{b}^{\hskip 4.0ptd}g_{cd}\,.

(7)

The hypersurface
\mathscr{N}
has the structure of a fiber bundle with a 2-dimensional base

To complement the fluid interpretation of the Carrollian structure, the following first-derivative quantities are introduced

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{L}_{l}k_{i}\,,

(8)

\displaystyle\varpi_{ij}

\displaystyle=-q_{i}^{\hskip 4.0ptk}q_{j}^{\hskip 4.0ptl}\partial_{\left[\right.k}k_{\left.l\right]}\,,

(9)

where the square brackets denote the anti-symmetrization of indices. The first quantity,

Both the kinematics and the dynamics of the geometric structure introduced in this section can be conveniently studied by introducing two deviation tensors associated with the tangent vector
l^{a}
and the rigging vector
k^{a}

plays a crucial role in the dynamics of the dual Carollian fluid. We define the deviation tensors
\mathcal{S}
as the following:

\displaystyle B_{AB}

\displaystyle\equiv q_{A}^{\hskip 4.0pta}q_{B}^{\hskip 4.0ptb}\nabla_{b}l_{a}\,,

(10)

\displaystyle\bar{B}_{AB}

\displaystyle\equiv q_{A}^{\hskip 4.0pta}q_{B}^{\hskip 4.0ptb}\nabla_{b}k_{a}\,.

(11)

These deviation tensors can be decomposed into expansion (the trace), shear (the traceless symmetric part), and twist (the antisymmetric part), respectively, as follows:

\displaystyle B_{AB}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Theta\,q_{AB}+\sigma_{AB}+\omega_{AB}\,,

(12)

\displaystyle\bar{B}_{AB}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bar{\Theta}\,q_{AB}+\bar{\sigma}_{AB}+\bar{\omega}_{AB}\,.

(13)

Another useful information is the inaffinity,

\displaystyle l^{b}\nabla_{b}l^{a}=\kappa l^{a}\,.

(14)

Contracting with

\kappa=k_{a}l^{b}\nabla_{b}l^{a}

Finally, we define the Hájíček one-form

This quantity is interpreted as an “surface density of impulsion”

The deviation tensors with their components, surface gravity, and the Hájíček field are the essential pieces for the fluid description of the horizon. As we will discuss in the upcoming sections, the stress-energy tensor describing

On the timelike hypersurface

IV
Fluid Sources

We introduce matter fields in the bulk spacetime and examine the properties of the Carrollian fluid dual to the horizon. We start from the sourced equations of the relativistic fluid dual to
\mathscr{H}
and take the limit
\rho\rightarrow 0
to recover the Carrollian equations for the fluid on

In the presence of a non-vacuum stress-energy tensor

Schematically, we would like to relate the reaction of the Carrollian fluid to the stress-energy tensor on

\mathscr{N}
.
Thus, we can study the behavior of the Carrollian fluid dual to the horizon in a spacetime with non-vacuum sources via modeling the fluid equations subject to sources and sinks.

We continue by computing the corresponding source fields for an example matter field on
\mathscr{M}
. Consider a massless scalar field in
\mathscr{M}
described by the following stress-energy tensor:

(34)

Here, the scalar field is defined on

\displaystyle G_{ll}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathscr{N}}}{{=}}8\pi\left(\partial_{v}\Phi\right)^{2}

(36)

\displaystyle G_{lA}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathscr{N}}}{{=}}8\pi\partial_{v}\Phi{\partial}_{A}\Phi

Next, we will study the horizon equations with sources in a perturbative expansion on the scalar field amplitude and solve the system numerically.

V
Perturbative Treatment

Nonlinear effects have recently garnered renewed attention in black hole perturbation theory, with a focus on analyzing the ringdown phase of gravitational wave signals. Some of these efforts study the imprints of nonlinearities induced by the non-stationary nature of the black hole as it accretes energy and angular momentum (effectively resulting in changes to the black hole parameters) on the ringdown signal
Sberna et al. 2022
;
May et al. 2024
. Others make use of second-order black hole perturbation theory to study mode couplings and the generation of quadratic quasinormal modes during ringdown
Loutrel et al. 2021
;
Ripley et al. 2021
;
Ma and Yang 2024
;
Bucciotti et al. 2024
;
Khera et al. 2023
;
Khera et al. 2025
. A recurring theme is the presence of a transient phase whose complexity exceeds that of a simple decomposition into linear modes. This highlights the need for novel approaches to capture the dynamics of this stage and to elucidate the mechanisms governing the transition toward linear behavior on an
effectively static background (e.g.
Yang et al. 2015
;
Iuliano et al. 2025
;
Ma et al. 2025
).

Here, we focus on a black hole perturbed by a surrounding matter field and perturbatively solve for the back-reaction on the horizon geometry. In the process, we also connect with the question of driving Carrollian fluids and their equilibration.

Black holes interact with their surroundings.
The gravitational field of the matter in the bulk affects the dynamics of the horizon. Furthermore, the infalling matter to the black hole results in the expansion and shearing of the horizon.

V.2.2
Horizon area

We quantify the strength of the scalar field perturbations by tracking the change in the horizon area, which is also related to the mass of the black hole.

The surface area of the horizon can be calculated as

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\sqrt{q}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi\,.

(78)

where
q\equiv\det q_{AB}
and the integral is evaluated over the (deformed) 2-sphere
\mathcal{S}
.
Taking a derivative with respect to the null time, the surface area rate of change can be related to the null expansion.

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}A}{\mathrm{d}v}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\frac{\partial_{v}q}{2q}\sqrt{q}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi\,\,

(79)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\Theta\sqrt{q}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi\,.

(80)

Here, we used

\displaystyle\Theta\equiv\frac{1}{2}q^{AB}\partial_{v}q_{AB}=\frac{\partial_{v}q}{2q}\,.

(81)

In the perturbative solution, this becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}A}{\mathrm{d}v}=\epsilon^{2}(2M)^{2}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\vartheta\sin{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi\,.

(82)

On the other hand, the energy flux due to the scalar field passing through the horizon is given by
Poisson 2004

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}v}

\displaystyle=\int_{S}\mathcal{T}_{ab}l^{a}l^{b}\sqrt{q}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi

(83)

\displaystyle=\int_{S}\mathcal{T}_{vv}\sqrt{q}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi\,,

(84)

which, in the perturbative scheme, becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}v}=\epsilon^{2}(2M)^{2}\int_{S}\left(\partial_{v}\varphi\right)^{2}\sin{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi\,.

(85)

where
M
is the final black hole mass.

Since the perturbation parameter
\epsilon
is small, the change in the black hole parameters through the entire time evolution can be assumed quasistatic.
In this regime, one can relate these two expressions via the first law of black hole mechanics

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}E=\frac{\kappa}{8\pi}\mathrm{d}A+\Omega_{H}\mathrm{d}J\,.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{32\pi M}\frac{\mathrm{d}A}{\mathrm{d}v}+\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{4}\right)\,,

V.2.3

Another physical quantity that can be extracted from the simulations is the angular momentum carried by the scalar field. As the black hole relaxes to a Schwarzschild solution at late times, after the perturbations die off, one can think of the non-axisymmetric modes in the scalar field source as carrying angular momentum of the opposite sign to the initially spinning black hole.

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}J}{\mathrm{d}v}

\displaystyle=-\int_{S}\mathcal{T}_{ab}l^{a}\psi^{b}\sqrt{q}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi\,,

\displaystyle=-\int_{S}\mathcal{T}_{v\phi}\sqrt{q}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi\,.

(89)

\psi^{a}\equiv\partial_{\phi}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}J}{\mathrm{d}v}

\displaystyle=-\epsilon^{2}(2M)^{2}\int_{S}\left(\partial_{v}\varphi\partial_{\phi}\varphi\right)\sin{\theta}\mathrm{d}\theta\mathrm{d}\phi\,.

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{S}\mathcal{H}_{A}\psi^{A}\mathrm{d}\Omega\,.

VI
Conclusions

In this paper, we studied the dynamical properties of Carrollian fluids dual to a perturbed black hole event horizon. Within the program of Carrollian fluid/horizon duality, we worked out the dynamics of a black hole event horizon perturbed by matter fields in the bulk to investigate the behavior of the Carrollian fluid under sources. On the gravity side, equations describing the horizon dynamics coupled to a matter field are analytically derived. These horizon equations are then numerically solved for a toy model of a Schwarzschild black hole perturbed by a non-axisymmetric distribution of a massless real scalar field near its horizon. On the Carrollian fluid side, we interpret the relaxation of the horizon after the perturbation is turned off as a mechanism for the dual Carrollian fluid to equilibrate.

Fluid “particles” cannot move in space in the Carrollian limit. However, the analogous dynamics of the horizon demonstrate that the null expansion vanishes at
v\rightarrow\infty
. The Carrollian fluid/horizon dictionary identifies the fluid energy density with the null expansion of the horizon.
Therefore, the Carrollian fluid analogous to the event horizon equilibrates via modifying the geometry on which it lives.

We plan to extend our results to tackle the Carrollian fluid dual to a black hole horizon in a full nonlinear treatment. This could include a dynamical approach to
hydrodynamical horizons

Freidel and Jai-akson 2024
, which is briefly discussed at the end of Appendix
B.2
. Another natural extension of this work is to build on the discussions in
Redondo-Yuste and Lehner 2023
to study the nonlinear-to-linear transition and explore enstrophy definitions within Carrollian fluids and the counterpart in gravity, if such a concept exists for such fluids.

A formalism similar to this work can be used to investigate the Carrollian fluid dual to any other null surface at null infinity
Ciambelli 2025
or null surfaces in the bulk not corresponding to an event horizon. Notice that, in such a case, the surface will always expand (if outside the horizon) or always contract (if inside of it), and thereby the associated Carrollian energy will not equilibrate.
Finally, on the black hole perturbation front, the horizon equations we studied in this work can yield insights about the dynamics of the horizon,
and its relation to quasinormal mode behavior and the non-linear/linear transition (See, for example,
May et al. 2024
).

Appendix A
Derivatives & connections

The prescription
Freidel and Jai-akson 2024
we followed to construct a null hypersurface involved projecting quantities in the bulk onto different hypersurfaces, as discussed in the main text. Here, we summarize how the covariant derivatives and curvature connections are induced in such hypersurfaces.

The rigged derivative
D_{i}
is defined
Freidel and Jai-akson 2022
as the projection of the covariant derivative
\nabla_{a}
on
\mathscr{M}
onto
\mathscr{H}
. For a tensor with mixed indices on
\mathscr{H}
, the rigged derivative acts as the following

D_{i}X_{j}^{\hskip 4.0ptk}\equiv\Pi_{i}^{\hskip 4.0ptd}\Pi_{j}^{\hskip 4.0pte}\left(\nabla_{d}X_{e}^{\hskip 4.0ptf}\right)\Pi_{f}^{\hskip 4.0ptk}

(93)

A different notion of derivative is defined for tensors on
\mathcal{S}
through

\hat{\nabla}_{A}X_{B}^{\hskip 4.0ptC}\equiv q_{A}^{\hskip 4.0pti}q_{B}^{\hskip 4.0ptj}\left(D_{i}X_{j}^{\hskip 4.0ptk}\right)q_{k}^{\hskip 4.0ptC}

(94)

where it is useful to note
q_{A}^{\hskip 4.0pta}=\left(e_{A}\right)^{a}
. This derivative satisfies
\hat{\nabla}_{C}\,q_{AB}=0
and preserves Carrollian diffeomorphism; therefore, it is often referred to as the Levi-Civita–Carroll covariant derivative
Ciambelli et al. 2018b
. Related to this covariant derivative, a Levi-Civita–Carroll connection is introduced. The Levi-Civita–Carroll covariant derivative acts on a vector as

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{A}X^{B}=\hat{\partial}_{A}X^{B}+\hat{\gamma}^{B}_{AC}X^{C}\,,

(95)

where
\hat{\partial}_{A}=\partial_{A}+\frac{b_{A}}{\alpha}\partial_{t}
, and
\hat{\gamma}^{B}_{AC}
is the Levi-Civita–Carroll connection
Ciambelli et al. 2018b
.

The commutation of the Levi-Civita–Carroll covariant derivative gives rise to the Riemann–Carroll tensor, which is covariant under Carrollian diffeomorphisms
Ciambelli et al. 2018b
:

\displaystyle\left[\hat{\nabla}_{A},\hat{\nabla}_{B}\right]X^{C}=\hat{R}^{C}_{\hskip 4.0ptDAB}X^{D}+\omega_{AB}\frac{2}{\alpha}\partial_{t}X^{C}\,.

(96)

Here,
\hat{R}^{A}_{\hskip 4.0ptBCD}
is the Riemann–Carroll tensor and
\omega_{AB}
is the vorticity.
Contracting the Riemann–Carroll tensor, the Ricci–Carroll tensor,
\hat{R}_{AB}
, is obtained:

\displaystyle\hat{R}^{C}_{\hskip 4.0ptACB}=\hat{R}_{AB}\,.

(97)

The Ricci–Carroll tensor can be expressed in terms of the geometric quantities defined on the horizon as follows
Freidel and Jai-akson 2024
:

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{AB}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{R}q_{AB}+\sigma_{A}^{\hphantom{A}C}\,\omega_{CB}+\frac{1}{2}\Theta\omega_{AB}\,,

(98)

where
\hat{R}=q^{AB}\hat{R}_{AB}
is its trace.
Therefore, its symmetric, traceless, and antisymmetric parts are given simply by

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{\langle AB\rangle}=\sigma_{A}^{\hphantom{A}C}\omega_{CB}\,,\quad\hat{R}_{\left[AB\right]}=\frac{1}{2}\Theta\omega_{AB}\,.

(99)

Note that, within the gauge expressed in the main text, yielding the metric in Eq. (
II.2
), the following choices are made:
\alpha=1
and
b_{A}=0
. Therefore, the structures introduced in this appendix simplify accordingly. Specifically, the Riemann–Carroll and Ricci–Carroll tensors agree with the induced Riemann and Ricci tensors on the hypersurfaces.
See the Appendix sections of
Ciambelli et al. 2018b
and
Freidel and Jai-akson 2024
for more details on derivatives and connections summarized here.

Appendix B
Gauge choices on the stretched Carrollian formalism

Starting from the general metric on the bulk spacetime given in section 3 of
Freidel and Jai-akson 2024
, we imposed some conditions as discussed in the main text. Here, we summarize other possible choices and their implications for the horizon and Carrollian dynamics.

B.1
Rescaling the tangent vector
l^{a}

The null tangent vector on the horizon can be rescaled while preserving all the properties of the Carrollian geometry. While we set the scaling parameter
\alpha
to be
1
in the main text, here we consider how this parameter affects the dynamics of the fluid and its stress-energy tensor.

We define a new tangent vector, which is related to
l^{a}
as

\displaystyle\ell^{a}\equiv\alpha l^{a}\,.

(100)

We continue imposing the other coordinate choices in the main text.
Rescaling the tangent vector with a constant value does not alter the equations of motion. However, rescaling it with a function on the horizon, i.e.,
\alpha=\alpha\left(v,\theta,\phi\right)
, induces a non-zero Carrollian acceleration and modifies the Hájíček one-form as follows:

\displaystyle\varphi_{A}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial_{A}\alpha}{\alpha}

(101)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{A}

\displaystyle=q_{AB}\partial_{r}V^{B}+\frac{\partial_{A}\alpha}{\alpha}

(102)

These, in turn, modify the DNS equation (Eq. (
28
)) to result in the following:

\displaystyle G_{lA}

\displaystyle=\partial_{v}{\mathcal{H}}_{A}+{\Theta}\left(\mathcal{H}_{A}+\varphi_{A}\right)+

\displaystyle\quad\left({\nabla}_{B}+\varphi_{B}\right)\left[\sigma_{A}^{\hskip 4.0ptB}-\left(\kappa+\frac{\Theta}{2}\right)\delta^{B}_{A}\right]\,.

(103)

In the main text, setting
\alpha=1
resulted in a vanishing acceleration on the horizon. This made it possible to close the system of equations with the four projections of the Einstein equations discussed in the main text. If the acceleration is non-zero, more relations are needed to solve the projected Einstein equations self-consistently.

The non-affinity parameter,
\kappa
, is also changed by the scaling of the tangent vector. The new value of
\kappa
becomes

\displaystyle\kappa=\alpha\partial_{r}\rho+\partial_{v}\alpha\,.

(104)

From this, we see that we can choose the scaling function
\alpha
in a way to ensure that the tangent vector is affinely parametrized, hence the non-affinity becomes
0
. In the cases, as in ours, where the stretching is only a function of
r
and also in order
\mathcal{O}(r)
(so that
\alpha
is independent of
r
), we can integrate the equation
\kappa=0
to find the form of
\alpha
that casts
l^{a}
affine:

\displaystyle\alpha=e^{-(\partial_{r}\rho)v}\,.

(105)

For the perturbed Schwarzschild model we considered, this function becomes

\displaystyle\alpha=e^{-\frac{v}{4M}}.

(106)

This suggests that the null tangent
l^{a}
should scale differently as the time evolves to keep the parametrization affine.

B.2
Choices on
b_{A}
and
V_{A}

To construct the metric (Eq. (
II.2
)), we used
b_{A}=0
and
V_{A}\neq 0
. Instead, if we had imposed

, vorticity
\omega_{AB}
on the horizon alongside a nonzero Hájíček field. The Carrollian acceleration explicitly appears in the horizon equations under this coordinate choice. Moreover, the covariant derivative in the expressions has to be changed with the Carroll covariant derivative that preserves Carrollian diffeomorphisms
Ciambelli et al. 2018b
as summarized in Appendix
A
. The associated partial derivative acts on a scalar function
f\left(x^{i}\right)
on
\mathscr{H}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\partial}_{A}f=\partial_{A}f+\frac{b_{A}}{\alpha}\partial_{v}f\;.

(107)

The resulting horizon equations become much more complex as the Levi-Civita–Carroll covariant derivatives and the Ricci–Carroll tensors in this case do not agree with the induced covariant derivative and the Ricci tensor on the hypersurface.

If instead, we follow the conventions of Randers–Papapetrou gauge in
Donnay and Marteau 2019
;
Ciambelli et al. 2018b
;
Freidel and Jai-akson 2022
to have
b_{A}{\left(x^{i}\right)}\neq 0
and
V_{A}=0
, while still keeping
\alpha=1
, we make the following intricate observation regarding the dynamics of the set of projected Einstein field equation on the horizon. We find that both
\varphi_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{A}
depend on
b_{A}
on the horizon as

\displaystyle\varphi_{A}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{N}}}{{=}}\partial_{v}b_{A}

(108)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{A}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{N}}}{{=}}-\frac{1}{2}b_{A}

(109)

Hence, the extra term of
\varphi_{A}
appearing in the DNS (Eq. (
28
)) is not an independent function; rather, it satisfies
\varphi_{A}=-2\mathcal{H}_{A}
. This, however, changes the character of the time evolution of the Hájíček field. The exponential behavior of the field converges to 0 as the null time evolves to infinity. This behavior is the opposite of that of the null expansion, which diverges in the infinite future as the Raychaudhuri equation (Eq.
(
27
)) dictates. This well-known “problem” in the horizon evolution is explained by its teleological nature
Thorne et al. 1986
.

The argument of teleology is briefly stated as follows: The event horizon is defined as a global null surface that is the boundary between null rays that can reach null infinity and those that cannot. This notion of the event horizon only makes sense from a global perspective where one knows the entire time evolution of the spacetime. Because of this, the horizon surface can behave acausally by responding to the outside fields before the fields reach the horizon itself. Such a consideration resolves the divergence in the Raychaudhuri equation and imposes correctly the boundary condition that the null expansion should vanish as the horizon evolves towards the infinite future. By the extension of this argument, we require that all our quantities behave similarly to the null expansions, i.e., diverge in the infinite future. Because of the observation that the Hájíček field will converge in the infinite future, we were driven to impose the opposite condition where we set
b_{A}=0
but allow instead
V_{A}\neq 0
.

The teleological nature of the event horizon is also the reason why we implement a backward-in-time evolution to solve the horizon equations
Gómez et al. 2001
.

One could also impose a near-horizon peeling for the shift field by requiring that
b_{A}(x^{a})=r\beta_{A}(x^{i})
(while
V^{A}(x^{a})=0
). This radial dependence guarantees that the Carrollian acceleration and vorticity both vanish on the horizon. However, the Hájíček field also vanishes.

The general case as studied in
Freidel and Jai-akson 2024
sets both
b_{A}\neq 0
and
V_{A}\neq 0
. Then the Hájíček field near the horizon behaves as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{A}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{N}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{r}V_{A}-b_{A}\right)

(110)

This, however, introduces
b_{A}
as an extra degree of freedom in the horizon equations. Therefore, more constraints need to be satisfied to solve the system of equations self-consistently. A set of such constraints called
hydrodynamical horizon
is studied in
Freidel and Jai-akson 2024
. We intend to approach the resulting system with the numerical methods developed in this paper in future work.

Appendix D
Details of numerical implementation

Starting from the spin-weighted functions introduced in Sec.
V.1
, we implement a spectral method to solve the system of coupled differential equations we obtained.

First, we decompose every scalar, vector, and tensor quantity into time-dependent coefficients multiplied by spherical harmonics of spin weights 0, 1, and 2, respectively. Below, we explicitly show the decompositions of the variables appearing in order
\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon\right)
:

\displaystyle k(v,x^{A})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,m}k_{\ell m}\left(v\right)\,{}_{0}Y_{\ell m}\left(x^{A}\right)\,,

(120)

\displaystyle h(v,x^{A})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,m}h_{\ell m}\left(v\right)\,{}_{1}Y_{\ell m}\left(x^{A}\right)\,,

(121)

\displaystyle c(v,x^{A})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,m}c_{\ell m}\left(v\right)\,{}_{2}Y_{\ell m}\left(x^{A}\right)\,,

(122)

\displaystyle s(v,x^{A})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,m}s_{\ell m}\left(v\right)\,{}_{2}Y_{\ell m}\left(x^{A}\right)\,.

(123)

Similar decomposition is applied to the variables of
\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)
.

For the scalar field source, we follow the same expansion using spherical harmonics,

\displaystyle\varphi\left(v,x^{A}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,m}\varphi_{\ell m}\left(v\right)\,{}_{0}Y_{\ell m}\left(x^{A}\right)\,.

(124)

After decomposing each term in the equations, we multiply both sides of the equations by the suitable spin-weighted spherical harmonic and integrate over the unit sphere to utilize the orthogonality property

\displaystyle\int_{S^{1}}{}_{s}\bar{Y}_{\ell m}\,{}_{s}{Y}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}=\delta_{\ell l^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\,,

(125)

where the bar denotes the complex conjugate and
\delta_{nn^{\prime}}
denotes the Kronecker delta. However, for the nonlinear terms appearing in order
\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)
, we need to incorporate the following integrals:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{}_{s_{1}}\bar{Y}_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}\,{}_{s_{2}}{Y}_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}\,{}_{s_{3}}{Y}_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}={}_{\left(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\right)}\mathcal{I}^{\ell_{2}m_{2},\ell_{3}m_{3}}_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}\,.

(126)

In the case for which
-s_{1}+s_{2}+s_{3}=0
, these integrals can be computed via Wigner-
3j
symbols as in
Redondo-Yuste and Lehner 2023
:

\displaystyle{}_{\left(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\right)}\mathcal{I}^{\ell_{2}m_{2},\ell_{3}m_{3}}_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}=\sqrt{\frac{\left(2\ell_{1}+1\right)\left(2\ell_{2}+1\right)\left(2\ell_{3}+1\right)}{4\pi}}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
m_{1}&m_{2}&m_{3}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
s_{1}&-s_{2}&-s_{3}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(127)

Notably, for the expression we obtain, the condition
-s_{1}+s_{2}+s_{3}=0
is satisfied, and the numerical integration of the harmonics can be avoided using the
3j
-symbols in SymPy
Meurer et al. 2017
.

We further simplify the set of equations on the sphere by restricting ourselves to the case in which the effect of the bulk physics is incorporated only through the matter fields by imposing
\partial_{r}q_{AB}=\lambda=0
, that is
s=0
,
S=0
. Moreover, as effects of the matter field only appear in order
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
, we tentatively set all the remaining first-order quantities

,
h

k
to 0. Thus, we end up with the following system of equations describing the different modes of

quantities:

\displaystyle\partial_{v}\vartheta_{LM}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4M}\vartheta_{LM}-\sum_{\ell_{1},m_{1}}\sum_{\ell_{2},m_{2}}{}_{\left(0,0,0\right)}\mathcal{I}^{\ell_{1}m_{1},\ell_{2}m_{2}}_{LM}\partial_{v}\varphi_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}\partial_{v}\varphi_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}

(128)

\displaystyle\partial_{v}C_{LM}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2M}C_{LM}+4M\sqrt{(L+1)(L-1)}H_{LM}

\displaystyle\quad+2M\sum_{\ell_{1},m_{1}}\sum_{\ell_{2},m_{2}}{}_{(-2,1,1)}\mathcal{I}_{LM}^{\ell_{1}m_{1},\ell_{2}m_{2}}\sqrt{\ell_{1}(\ell_{1}+1)}\sqrt{\ell_{2}(\ell_{2}+1)}\varphi_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}\varphi_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}

(129)

\displaystyle\partial_{v}H_{LM}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{L(L+1)}K_{LM}+\frac{1}{8M^{2}}\sqrt{(L+2)(L-1)}\partial_{v}C_{LM}

D.1
Scalar field source profile

For each source mode
\varphi_{\ell m}

and a ringdown phase following the peak of the Gaussian.

\displaystyle\varphi_{\ell m}\left(v\leq v_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{\ell m}\exp{\left(-\frac{\left(v-v_{0}\right)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)}\cos{\left(f_{\ell m}\left(v-v_{0}\right)\right)}

\displaystyle\varphi_{\ell m}\left(v\geq v_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{\ell m}\exp{\left(-\tau_{\ell m}\left(v-v_{0}\right)\right)}\cos{\left(f_{\ell m}\left(v-v_{0}\right)\right)}

where
\mathcal{A}_{\ell m}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\ell m}
are amplitudes,

is the width of the Gaussian,
\omega_{\ell m}=f_{\ell m}+i\tau_{\ell m}
is the complex quasinormal mode (QNM) frequency and
v_{0}
is the starting time of ringdown.

In the perturbed Schwarzschild model we study, we use Schwarzschild QNM frequencies for scalar modes

;
Stein 2019
. Specifically, we used the source modes
(\ell,m)=(0,0)
,
(1,1)
,
(1,-1)
, and
(2,0)
as illustrated in Fig.
1
in the main text. For simplicity, we set

and

.

We chose the mode
(0,0)
have the strongest amplitude,
\mathcal{A}_{00}=1
, while we set
\mathcal{A}_{11}=0.5
, and
\mathcal{A}_{20}=0.25
. To make sure that the scalar field

is real-valued, we set
\varphi_{1-1}=-\varphi_{11}^{*}

The modes

break the axisymmetry in the perturbed spacetime. We keep these modes non-zero to observe the effects related to horizon angular momentum and a non-vanishing Hájíček field as discussed in the main text.

Appendix E
Geometric quantities in example spacetimes

Here, we explicitly write the geometric quantities defined in Sec.
II
for the Schwarzschild and slowly-rotating Kerr spacetimes. Our goal is to elucidate the consequences of our gauge choices in familiar spacetimes, as these are easier to interpret. Note that, in this appendix, we work with vacuum spacetimes; however, it is straightforward to extend the analysis to non-vacuum cases via a perturbation scheme as implemented in the main text.

E.1
Schwarzschild

The Schwarzschild metric in Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates with advanced null time is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\mathrm{d}v^{2}+2\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}r+r^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega^{2}_{\mathrm{(Sph)}}\,,

(132)

where
\mathrm{d}\Omega^{2}_{\mathrm{(Sph)}}\equiv\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}{\theta}\mathrm{d}\phi^{2}
.

From this metric, the following vectors and forms are identified to describe the horizon geometry as well as the rigging structure:

\displaystyle l^{a}\partial_{a}

\displaystyle=\partial_{v},

\displaystyle k^{a}\partial_{a}

\displaystyle=\partial_{r},

\displaystyle n_{a}\mathrm{d}x^{a}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}r\quad

\displaystyle\left(e_{A}\right)^{a}\partial_{a}

\displaystyle=\delta^{B}_{A}\partial_{B}.

The metric duals of these are computed using the metric and its inverse.

\displaystyle l_{a}\mathrm{d}x^{a}

\displaystyle=-\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\mathrm{d}v+\mathrm{d}r,

\displaystyle k_{a}\mathrm{d}x^{a}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}v,

\displaystyle n^{a}\partial_{a}

\displaystyle=\partial_{v}+\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\partial_{r},

\displaystyle\left(e_{A}\right)_{a}\mathrm{d}x^{a}

\displaystyle=q_{AB}\mathrm{d}x^{B}.

Comparing these with the Carrollian metric in Eq. (
II.2
), we further identify

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\,,

\displaystyle V_{A}=0\,,

\displaystyle q_{AB}=r^{2}\Omega^{(\mathrm{Sph})}_{AB}\,.

(133)

Finally, we employ the near-horizon expansion. The event horizon of the Schwarzschild black hole is the null surface at
r=2M
. We define a new radial coordinate
\tilde{r}\equiv r-2M
which vanishes on the horizon. We impose the near-horizon expansion by assuming
0\leq\tilde{r}<<2M
and expanding the expressions in orders of
\tilde{r}
. To compare with Eq.s (
16
) & (
17
), we expand the stretching and the metric induced on the base manifold up to
\mathcal{O}\left(\tilde{r}^{2}\right)
:

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{2M}{\tilde{r}+2M}\right)=\frac{\tilde{r}}{4M}+\mathcal{O}\left(\tilde{r}^{2}\right)

(134)

\displaystyle q_{AB}

\displaystyle=\left(\tilde{r}-2M\right)^{2}\Omega^{(\mathrm{Sph})}_{AB}

(135)

\displaystyle=4M^{2}\Omega^{(\mathrm{Sph})}_{AB}-4M\tilde{r}\Omega^{(\mathrm{Sph})}_{AB}+\mathcal{O}\left(\tilde{r}^{2}\right)

(136)

Then, the non-zero geometric quantities on the horizon are:

\displaystyle\kappa\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathscr{N}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{4M}\,,

\displaystyle\bar{\Theta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathscr{N}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{M}\,.

(137)

Note that the components of the deviation tensor
B_{AB}
, defined with respect to the horizon tangent vector
l^{a}
, the Hájíček field, and the shear (
\bar{\sigma}_{AB}
) and vorticity (
\bar{\omega}_{AB}
) computed from the rigging vector
k^{a}
, all identically vanish on the horizon for the unperturbed Schwarzschild spacetime.

The Schwarzschild metric above is the unperturbed metric studied in Sec.
V
.

E.2
Slowly-rotating Kerr

Another spacetime of interest here is the slow rotation limit of Kerr spacetime. We use this metric to gain insights about the Hájíček field and its relation to horizon angular momentum. If purely axisymmetric perturbations (
\varphi_{(\ell m)}
with
m=0
) were applied to the background Schwarzchild metric in the perturbative scheme in Sec.
V
, the perturbed metric would have been studied with the slowly-rotating Kerr spacetime.

The slow rotation limit of Kerr spacetime is obtained from the standard Boyer–Lindquist coordinates by setting
a<<1
, where
a\equiv J/M

Poisson 2009
. The line element in this limit becomes:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}_{\mathcal{M}}=-f\mathrm{d}t^{2}+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{f}\mathrm{d}r^{2}

\displaystyle+r^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega^{2}-\frac{4Ma}{r}\sin^{2}{\theta}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\phi

(138)

where
f=1-2M/r
.
This metric satisfies vacuum Einstein field equations up to
\mathcal{O}\left(a^{2}\right)
. Therefore, the slow-rotation approximation introduces errors only at order
\mathcal{O}\left(a^{2}\right)
. To work on this approximation systemically, we demand that all the equations are satisfied up to
\mathcal{O}\left(a^{2}\right)
.

To express the slowly-rotating Kerr geometry with a null time coordinate and in the form similar to that of the Carrollian metric in Eq. (
II.2
), we implement the following coordinate transformations:

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle\equiv t+r+2M\ln{\left(\frac{r}{2M}-1\right)}

(139)

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\mathrm{d}v=\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{f}\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle\equiv\phi+\frac{a}{r}+\frac{a}{2M}\ln{\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)}

(140)

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\mathrm{d}\psi=\mathrm{d}\phi+\frac{2Ma}{r^{3}f}\mathrm{d}r

These new coordinates are motivated in
Poisson 2009
as they are constant on the generators of null hypersurfaces defined by
v=\mathrm{constant}
. Thus, we obtained the following metric:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\mathrm{d}v^{2}+2\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}r+r^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega_{(\mathrm{Sph})}^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{4Ma}{r}\sin^{2}{\theta}\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}\psi

(141)

Here, we denote
\mathrm{d}\Omega^{2}_{\mathrm{(Sph)}}\equiv\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}{\theta}\mathrm{d}\psi^{2}
. Now, we can identify the following vectors and forms as before:

\displaystyle l^{a}\partial_{a}

\displaystyle=\partial_{v}+\frac{2Ma}{r^{3}}\partial_{\psi},

\displaystyle k^{a}\partial_{a}

\displaystyle=\partial_{r},

\displaystyle n_{a}\mathrm{d}x^{a}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}r\quad

\displaystyle\left(e_{A}\right)^{a}\partial_{a}

\displaystyle=\delta^{B}_{A}\partial_{B}.

The metric duals of these are given by

\displaystyle l_{a}\mathrm{d}x^{a}=-\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\mathrm{d}v+\mathrm{d}r,

\displaystyle k_{a}\mathrm{d}x^{a}=\mathrm{d}v,

\displaystyle n^{a}\partial_{a}=\partial_{v}+\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\partial_{r}+\frac{2Ma}{r^{3}}\partial_{\psi},

\displaystyle\left(e_{A}\right)_{a}\mathrm{d}x^{a}=-\frac{2Ma}{r}\sin^{2}{\theta}\mathrm{d}v+q_{AB}\mathrm{d}x^{B}.

Comparing these with the Carrollian metric in Eq. (
II.2
), we identify

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\,,

(142)

\displaystyle V^{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{2Ma}{r^{3}}\delta^{A}_{\psi}\,,

(143)

\displaystyle q_{AB}

\displaystyle=r^{2}\Omega^{(\mathrm{Sph})}_{AB}\,.

(144)

We further introduce a new radial coordinate
\tilde{r}
that vanishes on the horizon
r=M+\sqrt{M^{2}-a^{2}}=2M+\mathcal{O}\left(a^{2}\right)
. Note that the corrections to the location of the event horizon are of order
\mathcal{O}\left(a^{2}\right)
, hence are neglected in the slow rotation limit. As we did for the Schwarzschild case, we use this new radial coordinate to implement the near-horizon expansion, for which we
0\leq\tilde{r}<<2M
and expand the expressions in orders of
\tilde{r}
.

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{2M}{2M+\tilde{r}}\right)=\frac{\tilde{r}}{4M}+\mathcal{O}\left(\tilde{r}^{2}\right)

(145)

\displaystyle V^{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{2Ma}{\left(2M+\tilde{r}\right)^{3}}\delta^{{\psi}}_{A}\,,

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{4M^{2}}\delta^{{\psi}}_{A}-\frac{3a\tilde{r}}{8M^{2}}\delta^{{\psi}}_{A}+\mathcal{O}\left(\tilde{r}^{2}\right)\,

(146)

\displaystyle q_{AB}

\displaystyle=\left(2M+\tilde{r}\right)^{2}\Omega^{(\mathrm{Sph})}_{AB}

\displaystyle=4M^{2}\Omega^{(\mathrm{Sph})}_{AB}-4M\tilde{r}\Omega^{(\mathrm{Sph})}_{AB}+\mathcal{O}\left(\tilde{r}^{2}\right)\,

(147)

Then, the non-zero geometric quantities on the horizon are:

\kappa\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathscr{N}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{4M}\,,

(148)

\bar{\Theta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathscr{N}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{M}\,,

(149)

\mathcal{H}_{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathscr{N}}}{{=}}-\frac{3a}{4M}\sin^{2}\theta\delta^{A}_{\psi}\,.

(150)

All other geometric quantities introduced in Sec.
II
vanish on the horizon.

Finally, we compute the horizon angular momentum using the Hájíček field. As the spacetime is axisymmetric, there exists the axial Killing vector which can be expressed in these coordinates as
\partial_{{\psi}}
. Following Damour’s derivation
Damour 1982
, we compute the angular momentum of the horizon via

\displaystyle J_{\mathcal{N}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{8\pi}\int\mathcal{H}_{{\psi}}|_{\mathcal{N}}\,\mathrm{d}S_{\mathcal{N}}

(151)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{8\pi}\int\left(-\frac{3Ma}{4M^{2}}\sin^{2}{\theta}\right)\,4M^{2}\sin{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}{{\psi}}

(152)

\displaystyle=Ma\,.

(153)

This is the expected value of the event horizon angular momentum for the slowly-rotating Kerr metric.

The identifications from the slowly-rotating Kerr spacetime to the geometric quantities that are useful for the Carrollian fluid/horizon dynamics dictionary can be extended to the full Kerr solution at the cost of trading the simple results of this appendix for much more complex relations.
