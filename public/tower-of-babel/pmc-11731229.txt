Gold nanorod-based engineered nanogels for cascade-amplifying photothermo-enzymatic synergistic therapy
Authors: Ling Ding, Xiaoshan Wang, Qing Wu, Xia Wang, Qigang Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated anticancer modalities, which disturb the redox balance of cancer cells through multi-pathway simulations, hold great promise for effective cancer management. Among these, cooperative physical and biochemical activation strategies have attracted increasing attention because of their spatiotemporal controllability, low toxicity, and high therapeutic efficacy. Herein, we demonstrate a nanogel complex as a multilevel ROS-producing system by integrating chloroperoxidase (CPO) into gold nanorod (AuNR)-based nanogels (ANGs) for cascade-amplifying photothermal-enzymatic synergistic tumor therapy. Benefiting from photothermal-induced hyperthermia upon near-infrared (NIR) laser exposure, the exogenous ROS (including H2O2) were boosted by the AuNR nanogel owing to the intercellular stress response. This ultimately promoted the efficient enzyme-catalyzed reaction of loaded CPO combined with the rich endogenous H2O2 in tumor cells to significantly elevate intracellular ROS levels above the threshold for improved therapeutic outcomes. Both in vitro and in vivo studies have verified the cascade-amplifying ROS-mediated antitumor effects, providing feasible multimodal synergistic tactics for tumor treatment.

Introduction

Cancer is a major threat to human health and life owing to its diversity and complexity. Cancer-specific treatment tactics usually depend on the well-described hallmarks of cancer [1]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) have been regarded as the highlighted signaling molecules that affect cancer cells to generate various cancer hallmarks in all stages of tumor development [2]. ROS, which include superoxide radicals (O2·−), hydroxyl radicals (·OH), singlet oxygen (1O2), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), are highly reactive small molecules that can activate multiple biological oxidation process and play a vital role in physiological functions [3,4]. Commonly, normal cells tightly maintain a dynamic balance in ROS generation and elimination depending on its antioxidant defense systems [5]. Owing to the elevated ROS level and enhanced-reliance on the antioxidant system, cancer cells are more susceptible to excessive ROS compared to normal cells [6,7]. Overproduced ROS can interrupt the antioxidant defense and elevate the oxidative stress, ultimately inducing cell death via irreversible damage to DNA, proteins, and lipids [8]. Thus, selective elevation of ROS level above the redox threshold at localized tumor regions is an attractive and ingenious tumor therapeutic strategy to kill cancer cells while minimizing the undesired damage to healthy cells [9,10].

To date, with extensive efforts, various ROS-mediated approaches have been explored to disrupt the tumoral redox homeostasis for cancer treatment [[11], [12], [13]]. For instance, photodynamic and sonodynamic therapies, as the paradigm for generating exogenous ROS, have been well-designed to convert oxygen molecules (O2) to cytotoxic 1O2 for cancer therapy induced by physical laser or ultrasound stimulus to activate the photosensitizers or sonosensitizers [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. The emerging endogenous chemodynamic therapy employs inorganic nanoparticle (NP)-initiated Fenton or Fenton-like reactions to effectively catalyze the conversion of the endogenous H2O2 to highly cytotoxic OH˙ for tumor treatment [[21], [22], [23]]. More interestingly, inspired by the immune defense mechanism of neutrophils which involves the biocatalysis of myeloperoxidase and H2O2, enzymatic elevation of ROS in a sustained manner has been developed as an emerging therapeutic option, termed enzyme dynamic therapy (EDT) [[24], [25], [26]]. This therapy employs the enzymatic oxidation of endogenous ROS (H2O2 and O2·−) to highly reactive 1O2, which significantly disrupts the intracellular redox homeostasis and induces tumor cells apoptosis or necrosis. However, a single biocatalytic therapy modality for ROS-mediated strategies may not always achieve satisfactory therapeutic efficiency because of endogenous H2O2-dependent limitations and intricate redox homeostasis and adaptation in cancer cells. Thus, the integration of the proposed EDT with other treatment modalities to achieve a cascade-amplifying synergistic therapeutic effect is strongly desired.

Photothermal therapy (PTT), which adopts the photothermal agents to convert the near-infrared (NIR) light energy to hyperthermia for tumor ablation, is a promising tumor therapeutic modality [[27], [28], [29], [30], [31]]. PTT has gained considerable attention owing to its minimal invasiveness, specific spatiotemporal selectivity, and deep tissue penetrability [32]. According to the reported studies, hyperthermia not only induces cancer cell apoptosis but also promotes ROS generation (including H2O2 or O2·−) because of the intracellular stress response, which enables the synergistic PTT/EDT for cascade-amplifying production of ROS to overwhelm the intracellular antioxidant defenses [33,34]. Moreover, PTT is an oxygen-independent therapeutic modality, which shows advanced superiority in treating tumors with a hypoxic microenvironment compared to other physical stimulations that may be limited in hypoxic tumors [35]. Comprehensively, augmenting ROS levels by synergistically using exogenous PTT and endogenous EDT would be a feasible option for improving anticancer outcomes at the target site.

Herein, motivated by the photothermally activatable property of PTT and the highly efficient biocatalytic activity of EDT, we developed an enzyme-encapsulated hybrid nanogel system by integrating chloroperoxidase (CPO) into gold nanorod (AuNR)-based nanogels (ANGs) to achieve cascade-amplifying photothermal-enzymatic therapy against malignant tumors. The AuNRs exhibit a favorable photothermal conversion effect, endowing the spatiotemporally controlled production of exogenous ROS upon NIR laser irradiation, accompanied by the amplification of H2O2 [36,37]. CPO can provoke the biocatalytic conversion of the upregulated and endogenous H2O2 and halogen species into toxic 1O2 in tumor cells [38], further elevating the ROS level. This multistage ROS upregulating approach can synergistically increase intracellular ROS levels above the threshold value in cancer and promote cell apoptosis. Thus, this nanogel complex could provide a promising multimode strategy for site-specific cascade-amplifying oxidative stress in a synchronous exogenous and endogenous ROS-augment method to improve cancer therapeutic efficiency.

Materials and methods

All materials were used as received without further purification. NaBH4, AgNO3, and H2O2 were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Chloroauric acid (HAuCl4), cetyltrimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB; 1.3220 g/mL), N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC·HCl), N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), ascorbic acid, 2-morpholinoethanesulphonic acid (MES) solution, and Tris-HCl buffer were purchased from Baoman Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Sodium hyaluronate (molecular weight (MW) = 3000–10000) was purchased from Shandong Freda Bioeng Co., Ltd. (Jinan, China). Acid phosphatase (AP; MW = 10 kDa; enzyme commission (EC) 3.1.3.2) and CPO (≥3000 U/mL; EC 1.11.1.10) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China); N-(fluorenyl-methoxycarbonyl) tyrosine phosphate (Fmoc-Tyr(H2PO3)-OH), 1-hydroxybenzotriazole (HOBt), dithiothreitol (DTT), and cystamine dihydrochloride were purchased from GL Biochem (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Singlet oxygen sensor green (SOSG) and 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (Shanghai, China). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). All other reagents were used as received without further purification.

Preparation of AuNRs

AuNRs were prepared by the reported method [39]. Briefly, 0.25 mL of HAuCl4 (0.01 M), 0.6 mL of NaBH4 (ice-cold solution, 10 mM), and 7.5 mL of CTAB (0.1 M) were firstly mixed to form a 10 mL of homogeneous solution. After stirring for 1 min under the condition of 30 °C water bath, the solution changed the color from colorless to brown, and then kept at 30 °C for 2 h, the Au seed solution was thus synthesized. Afterward, the growth solution was prepared by blending of 100 mL of CTAB (0.1 M), 3 mL of HAuCl4 (0.025 M), 800 μL of AgNO3 (50 mM), 2 mL of H2SO4, and 800 μL of ascorbic acid (0.1 M) under 300 °C water bath, and the yellow solution was turned to colorless after stirring. At last, 0.24 mL of above Au seed solution was added into the prepared growth solution with stirring for 1 h, and then the mixed solution was maintained at 30 °C for 18 h. The solution was washed three times by ultrapure water to remove excess CTAB, and AuNRs was finally obtained by centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 15 min).

Synthesis of sulfhydryl-modified hyaluronic acid (HA-SH) and carboxyl-functionalized AuNRs (AuNRs-COOH)

HA-SH was synthesized according to the method reported previously. Typically, 5 g sodium hyaluronate (MW 3,000–10,000) was firstly dissolved in 250 mL of PBS solution (pH 7.0), and then EDC·HCl (3.71 g) and HOBt (5.07 g) were added and stirred for 2 h to activate the carboxyl group of HA. After that, 8.45 g of cysteamine dihydrochloride solution was further added into the solution dropwise to introduce sulfhydryl groups. After stirring for 24 h, the resulted solution was dialyzed against deionized water for 24 h to remove extra reactants (8,000–14,000 Da). Finally, 9.64 g of DTT was added into the dialyzed solution with continuous stirring for 24 h. The final solution was transferred into a dialysis bag (8,000–14,000 Da) and dialyzed against deionized water at for two days to remove the excess DTT. The obtained HA-SH was lyophilized and stored at −20 °C for further use.

The AuNRs-COOH was synthesized as follows: the prepared AuNRs was dispersed in 15 mL of MES solution (pH 6.7), and then the lyophilized HA-SH was added and stirred for 30 min at 30 °C. The AuNRs-COOH was finally acquired by washing with MES (1 M, pH 6.7) for three times and centrifugation (9,000 rpm, 15 min).

Preparation of ANGs and enzyme-loaded hybrid nanogels (ANCGs)

The as-prepared AuNRs-COOH was suspended in 15 mL of MES solution (1 M, pH 6.7), and then added 10 mg of EDC and 10 mg of NHS with stirring for 30 min to activate the carboxyl on the surface of AuNRs. Then, the activated AuNRs was washed three times with deionized water and redispersed in 2 mL of AP solution (1 mg/mL) via ultrasonication to anchor the AP on AuNRs. After stirring for another 2 h, the AP-attached AuNRs (AuNRs-AP) was obtained by centrifugation (9,000 rpm) and resuspended in 30 mL of ultrapure water. Subsequently, Fmoc-Tyr(H2PO3)-OH (30 mg) and Na2CO3 (6 mg) were added in the acquired AuNRs-AP solution and stirred for 12 h at 30 °C. Finally, the ANGs was collected from the mixture via centrifugation (9,000 rpm) and washed three times with deionized water to remove the residual Fmoc-Tyr(H2PO3)-OH.

To prepare ANCGs, the obtained ANGs was further dispersed in 2 mL of Tris-HCl buffer (1 M, pH 6.0) containing 0.2 mL of CPO (3,000 U/mL) and stirred for 24 h at room temperature. The enzyme CPO can be encased in the network of the formed peptide nanogel through electrostatic interaction. Thus, the ANCGs was obtained by centrifugation (9,000 rpm) and washed three times with deionized water. The supernatant was used to calculate the residual CPO concentration and determine the loading amount of CPO.

Loading amount and activity test of CPO

The loading amount of CPO in ANCGs was quantified by subtraction of the residual CPO from the initially added CPO and residual CPO concentration was detected via the ultraviolet (UV) absorbance at 403 nm. CPO activity was evaluated using a typical reaction, in which CPO catalyzes the oxidization of monochlorodimedon (MCD) to dichlorodimedon (DCD) in a PBS solution (pH 2.75) containing potassium chloride (KCl) and H2O2. In detail, 29 μL of free CPO (3,000 U/mL) or 140 μL of ANCGs (including 87 U CPO) was added into 0.1 M PBS solution (0.1 M, pH 2.75) containing 0.1 mM MCD, 20 mM KCl, and 2 mM H2O2 to form a total volume of 3 mL of mixture. Immediately, the UV absorbance changes at 278 nm was monitored over time at 25 °C. The reaction rate in the initial 30 s was employed to determine the CPO activity.

Characterization

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), scanning TEM (STEM), and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) images were recorded using a JEM 2100F electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) operated at 200 kV. The TEM size distribution of the AuNRs and ANGs was calculated using ImageJ. The hydrodynamic size and zeta potential studies of the NPs were performed on a Malvern instrument Zetasizer Nano system (Nano ZS90; Malvern, Worcestershire, UK). Thermalgravimetric (TG) analysis was carried out on a Netzsch Thermoanalyzer STA 409 instrument (Netzsch, Hanau, Germany) in a nitrogen environment with a heating rate of 10 °C/min from room temperature to 900 °C under Ar. The UV-visible (UV-vis) spectra were taken by UV-2700 (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectrum was obtained using a Bruker A300 spectrometer (Bruker Biospin, Billerica, MA, USA). The Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrum was detected a fluorophotometer (FLUOROLOG-3-11, Horiba, Kyoto, Japan).

Photothermal heating of the ANGs and ANCGs

A 500 μL of the as-synthesized ANGs or ANCGs solution with different concentrations (1.875, 3.75, 7.5, and 10 μg/mL) was injected into a quartz cell and then exposed to an 808 nm NIR laser (1.5 W/cm2; VLSM-808-B-004, Shanghai Baiantek Sensing Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) for 10 min. The time-dependent temperature increase of the solution was measured by a thermocouple with 10 s intervals. To achieve normalization, all the initial temperatures were 24 ± 1 °C. Three independent experiments were conducted and date are the means ± standard deviation (SD) (n = 3).

In vitro detection of 1O2 production

The in vitro 1O2 generation from ANCGs was firstly measured by EPR spectrum (JES FA200, Shanghai Aifei Electronic Technology Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China). The mixture of 200 μL of ANCGs (0.05 mM), 50 μL of NaCl (50 mM), and 50 μL of H2O2 (500 mM) with 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP; 1O2 capturer) was quickly transferred to a quartz capillary for EPR analysis, and the EPR spectrum was taken every 4 min.

Furthermore, fluorescence spectrum was also employed for 1O2 determination in vitro. Firstly, the mixture of 400 μL of NaCl (100 mM), 10 μL of H2O2 (10 mM), 40 μL of SOSG (0.1 mM), and 260 μL of deionized water were well combined in the cuvette. Then, 200 μL of ANCGs (0.05 mM) was injected. Immediately, the fluorescence spectrum was conducted at different times (excitation (Ex) = 488 nm).

Intracellular ROS detection

To evaluate the intracellular ROS generation, human hepatocarcinoma (SMMC-7721) cells were seeded on six-well plate at a density of 1.0 × 105 cells per well. After incubation for 24 h, the medium was replaced by a serum-free medium and further incubated for 6 h. Then, PBS, ANGs or ANCGs (with Au concentration of 10 μg/mL) was added and co-incubated with the cells for 6 h. Afterward, the medium was replaced by the serum-free medium containing 10 μM DCFH-DA, and incubated for another 1 h. As for the NIR (+) group, the cells were further irradiated with an 808 nm laser (0.5 W/cm2) for 10 min. Finally, the cells were washed with PBS, and the intracellular ROS signals were detected by confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) (Ex/emission (Em) = 488 nm/525 nm) (Carl Zeiss AG, Jena, Germany).

Flow cytometry analysis was further performed on normal mouse embryo fibroblasts (NIH-3T3) cells and SMMC-7721 cells to investigate the cellular ROS levels using DCFH-DA as the fluorescence probe. NIH-3T3 cells (1.0 × 105 cells/well) and SMMC-7721 cells (1.0 × 105 cells/well) were firstly seed on six-well plates. Then, the medium for SMMC-7721 cells was replaced by a serum-free medium, while the NIH-3T3 cells were normally cultured. After 6 h incubation, both NIH-3T3 cells and SMMC-7721 cells were treated with ANGs and ANCGs (with Au concentration of 10 μg/mL), and further incubated for 6 h. PBS was used as the control. DCFH-DA (10 μM) was then added into the cell culture, and the cells were incubated for another 20 min. As for NIR (+) group, the cells were further exposed to an 808 nm laser (0.5 W/cm2) for 10 min. Finally, the cells were trypsinized and collected for flow cytometry (BD Accuri C6; BD Accuri Cytometers Inc., Ann Arbor, MI, USA) analysis (Ex/Em = 488 nm/525 nm).

In vitro cell cytotoxicity

The cytotoxicity and safety of the NPs was evaluated by CCK-8 assays and live/dead staining. For the CCK-8 assays, the SMMC-7721 cells (5000 cells/well) or NIH-3T3 cells (5000 cells/well) were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Then, the medium for SMMC-7721 cells was replaced by a serum-free medium, while the NIH-3T3 cells were normally cultured. Meanwhile, ANGs and ANCGs was added to the medium at different concentrations (0, 1.575, 3.75, 7.5, and 10 μg/mL). As for NIR (+) group, the cells were further exposed to an 808 nm laser (0.5 W/cm2) for 10 min after 4 h co-incubation and followed by another 20 h incubation, while other groups were normally co-incubated for 24 h. Finally, each well was treated with CCK-8 for 2 h, and the optical density (OD) value at 450 nm was recorded by a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc., Winooski, VT, USA). For the live/dead staining measurement, SMMC-7721 cells were seeded on six-well plate at a density of 1.0 × 105 cells per well. After 24 h incubation, the cell culture was replaced by serum-free medium and further incubated for 6 h. Then, the cells were co-incubated with PBS, ANGs, or ANCGs (with Au concentration of 10 μg/mL) for 12 h. As for NIR (+) group, the cells were irradiated with an 808 nm laser (0.5 W/cm2) for 10 min. Then, the cells were stained with calcein-acetoxymethylester (calcein-AM) and propidium iodide (PI) and incubated for 30 min. Finally, the cells were washed with PBS and observed by CLSM.

Immunofluorescence assay

To evaluate the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) expression in tumor cells, SMMC-7721 cells after 6 h treatment on six-well plate at a density of 1.0 × 105 cells per well were washed three times, and then labeled with primary antibody Nrf2 (ab62352; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) at 1/500. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100. Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488) secondary antibody (1/1000, A11008; Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) was used as the secondary antibody. 1 μg/mL 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Beyotime Biotechnology) was used to stain the nuclei blue. PBS, instead of the primary antibody, was used as the negative control. The obtained cells were then used for CLSM (Olympus FV1000, Olympus America Inc., Center Valley, PA, USA) imaging after rinsing.

In vivo antitumor studies

All animal experiment were performed in accordance with the guidelines approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tongji University (Approval No.: TJAC00323106). The SMMC-7721 tumor-bearing mice model was established by injection of SMMC-7721 cells suspension (1.5 × 106 cells) into the right armpit of nude mice (6−8 weeks). When the tumor volumes reached to approximately 100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into six groups (four mice each group) for intratumoural and intravenous (i.v.) treatment, respectively.

Four groups for intratumoural injection: 1) PBS (50 μL, pH 7.4); 2) ANGs (50 μL, 5 mg/mL), NIR laser irradiation for 10 min; 3) ANCGs (50 μL, 5 mg/mL); and 4) ANCGs (50 μL, 5 mg/mL), NIR laser irradiation for 10 min. The therapeutic agent was injected at the days 0, 1, 2, 4, and 7, and NIR irradiation (808 nm, 0.5 W/cm2) was immediately implemented after each drug injection.

Two additional groups of SMMC-7721 tumor bearing mice were intravenously injected with PBS (100 μL, pH 7.4) and ANCGs (100 μL, 5 mg/mL), respectively. After injection for 24 h, the tumors of ANCGs group were irradiated by an 808 nm NIR laser (0.5 W/cm2) for 10 min. The therapeutic agent was injected at the days 0, 1, 2, 4, 7, 8, 12, 18, and 24.

The tumor sizes and body weight of all the mice were recorded before each injection of the therapeutic agent, and the tumor volumes were calculated by the equation: V = (length × width2)/2. Finally, all mice were sacrificed, and the major organs and tumor tissues were separated and sliced for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining analysis.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were indicated as mean ± SD. Means were compared using the student's t-test. ∗∗P < 0.01 and ∗∗∗P < 0.001 were considered as statistical significance.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of the ANCGs

A schematic of the construction route for enzyme-loaded hybrid nanogel systems and their synergistic photothermal-enzymatic therapy mechanisms is shown in Scheme 1. The obtained AuNRs-COOH were first activated by EDC/NHS, and then AP was attached to the surface of the AuNRs as the active seeds. Afterward, interfacial AP induced the dephosphorylation of the peptide precursor Fmoc-Tyr(H2PO3)-OH to form a supramolecular hydrogel coating around the AuNR cores, resulting in the generation of ANGs. Finally, ANCGs were fabricated by entrapment of CPO into the ANGs through electrostatic interactions between the nanogel network and CPO. In tumor microenvironment, the exogenous NIR activation can efficiently upregulate the endogenous ROS due to the stress response induced by the photothermal effect of the AuNRs cores in hybrid nanogels, and subsequent “deadly” 1O2 can be produced through the enzymatic bio-oxidation of CPO in the nanogels and elevated H2O2 in tumor regions. The in vitro photothermal properties of the prepared ANCGs were investigated. Briefly, the ANGs and ANCGs solution with different Au concentrations (1.875, 3.75, 7.5, and 10 μg/mL) were irradiated using an 808 nm NIR laser (1.5 W/cm2) for 10 min, and the temperature changes were simultaneously monitored in real time at 10 s intervals. The temperature change curves obtained are shown in Figs. 2E and 2F. The temperatures for both the ANGs and ANCGs solutions significantly increased with irradiation time and finally reached a plateau after 10 min of exposure. Particularly, the temperature changes increased with the increase in Au concentration (1.875–10 μg/mL) within 10-min irradiation, revealing a concentration-dependent photothermal property. As for ANCGs, the temperature change can be reached 8.17 °C after 10-min NIR exposure with an Au concentration of 10 μg/mL, which is close to that of ANGs (approximately 8.23 °C). The results demonstrate that the ANCG hybrid nanogels possess an excellent photothermal property and the immobilization of CPO has almost no impact on the photothermal effect at higher Au concentrations (10 μg/mL), suggesting the potential applications for further photothermal treatment in vivo.

In vitro1O2 generation

The efficacy of 1O2 generation through biocatalytic oxidation is a pivotal component of the proposed synergistic photothermal-enzymatic treatment. Therefore, the loading capacity and bioactivity of ANCGs were evaluated, as shown in Figs. 3A, 3B, and S1. The loading efficiency of CPO in the ANCGs was determined to be as high as 52.5% based on the UV-vis absorption peak at 398 nm. The corresponding activities of free CPO and CPO loaded in ANCGs were assessed using MCD as the substrate, and the kinetic conversion of MCD to DCD was monitored using UV-vis spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 3A, according to the comparison of the initial reaction rates between free CPO and ANCGs, the immobilized CPO in ANCGs maintained 81.7% of its initial activity relative to free CPO. Amplification of oxidative stress via intracellular ROS production by the photothermal-enzymatic synergy

According to the proposed photothermal-enzymatic synergy, NIR exposure can elevate endogenous ROS in tumor cells, including H2O2 which triggers the production of 1O2 through the enzymatic reaction of CPO to further amplify the intracellular ROS level for tumor therapy. To confirm this hypothesis, variations in cellular ROS levels induced by the photothermal-enzymatic synergy of ANCGs were investigated in SMMC-7721. After co-incubating the SMMC-7721 cells with PBS, ANGs, or ANCGs for 4 h and a subsequent 808 nm NIR laser irradiation (with or without laser irradiation) for 10 min, the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA was added to visualize the ROS level in tumor cells using CLSM. As shown in Fig. 4A, SMMC-7721 cells exhibited detectable green fluorescence after ANCG incubation compared with ANGs without NIR exposure, implying that the expected 1O2 generation inside the cells was caused by the enzymatic reaction. In addition, a distinct fluorescence enhancement was detected in cells incubated with ANGs after NIR irradiation (ANGs/NIR). Moreover, flow cytometry was performed to confirm the ROS yield in NIH-3T3 and SMMC-7721 cells treated with PBS, ANGs, and ANCGs with or without NIR irradiation. As illustrated in Figs. 4B and S2, the endogenous ROS level of normal 3T3 cells showed a slight increase even after ANCG/NIR treatment, demonstrating that there is insufficient ROS production in normal cells to surpass the redox threshold to cause cell death because of the strong antioxidant defense system. In vitro cytotoxicity evaluation

The cytotoxicity of ANGs and ANCGs in cancer cells SMMC-7721 and NIH-3T3 cells was investigated using CCK-8 assays to evaluate the efficacy and safety of the photothermal-enzymatic synergy approach. As shown in Fig. 4C, ANGs had an insignificant impact on the proliferation of SMMC-7721 cells in the presence of NIR irradiation, and ANCGs without irradiation also exhibited inferior cytotoxicity, indicating insufficient ROS production through the single-modal photothermal effect or biochemical oxidization in tumor cells. In contrast, when exposed to laser irradiation, ANCGs exhibited significant inhibition of cell proliferation in SMMC-7721 cells, and the cell viability was further decreased to 49% at 10 μg/mL, which is consistent with the proposed therapeutic process that NIR-induced ROS generation and thus CPO-activation for further amplifying the 1O2 production to kill cancer cells. Moreover, the safety and biocompatibility of the hybrid nanogels was studied by co-incubating with normal NIH-3T3 cells with or without NIR irradiation at concentrations varying from 1.875 to 10 μg/mL. As presented in Fig. 4D, no obvious suppression on NIH-3T3 cells survival was found for ANGs and ANCGs with or without laser irradiation, and all the cell viabilities were maintained at more than 90% even at maximal concentration of 10 μg/mL, revealing the innocuousness and safety of the nanotherapeutic agent to normal cells in tumor treatment.

In addition, live/dead assays were performed to investigate the cytotoxicity of the various treatments on SMMC-7721 cells. Confocal images were obtained after staining the cells with calcein-AM/PI, as shown in Fig. 4E. Compared to the control and ANG groups, a small number of dead cells (red fluorescence) were observed in the ANCG and ANGs/NIR groups, and the obvious green fluorescence of live cells remained. In comparison, a significant increase in red fluorescent cells was demonstrated when simultaneously treated with ANCGs and NIR irradiation, indicating the highest therapeutic effect of ANCGs plus laser irradiation. These results agree well with the CCK-8 assay that the single-modal photothermal effect and biochemical oxidization cause a subthreshold dose of toxicity to cancer cells, whereas photothermal-enzymatic synergy exhibits significantly enhanced tumor therapy potential based on multilevel ROS amplification.

The intracellular ROS accumulation induced immune response

Under low oxidative stress conditions, the Nrf2 pathway is activated to resist the harmful effects of hyperoxidation or hyperthermia, thereby promoting cell survival. However, when ROS levels further increase above a critical threshold, Nrf2 expression significantly increases and continues to accumulate in the nucleus to amplify oxidative stress, consequently leading to cell death [40]. As shown in Fig. S3. Nrf2 expression was induced in SMMC-7721 cells after H2O2 and ANCG treatment. Moreover, the fluorescence signals for both H2O2 and ANCG/NIR significantly increased with increasing concentration. This confirms that the Nrf2 signaling pathway is activated by hyperoxidation. Subsequently, variations in cellular Nrf2 expression induced by the photothermal-enzymatic synergy of ANCGs were investigated. As shown in Fig. 4F, the ANCG, ANGs/NIR, and ANCGs/NIR groups showed significantly enhanced Nrf2 expression and nuclear translocation compared to the PBS and ANGs groups, implying the expected ROS generation inside the cells caused by PTT and EDT. Furthermore, the ANCGs/NIR group showed a significantly stronger fluorescence signal than the ANCG and ANGs/NIR groups, which was attributed to the synergistic photothermal-enzymatic effect of the ANCGs. These results indicate that ANCG/NIR can effectively generate large amounts of ROS and induce the expression and nuclear transport of Nrf2, thereby potentially inducing cancer cell death.

In vivo cancer therapy and biosafety evaluation

Tumor-bearing mice with tumor volume of 80–100 mm3 were randomly divided into four groups: PBS, ANGs/NIR, ANCG, and ANCGs/NIR. For ANGs/NIR and ANCGs/NIR groups, each mouse was irradiated by an 808 nm NIR laser for 10 min (0.5 W/cm2) after intratumor injection of different therapeutic agents (Fig. 5A). As depicted in Fig. 5B, the tumors in the ANCGs/NIR group exhibited noticeable shrinkage, and the relative tumor volumes distinctly decreased to 1.0 after seven days of treatment, demonstrating that high therapeutic efficacy due to the photothermal-enzymatic synergy enhanced ROS generation in the tumor region. In contrast, the PBS, ANGs/NIR, and ANCG groups showed a rapid tumor development trend during the treatment period. Compared with the PBS group, the ANGs/NIR and ANCG groups also showed a certain suppressive effect on tumor growth, which can be ascribed to the individual effects of PTT and enzyme therapy. Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed an efficient photothermal-enzymatic synergetic system for multilevel ROS-upregulated tumor therapy, which was constructed using an AuNR-based hybrid nanogel and encapsulated CPO. Under irradiation by an 808 nm laser, intracellular ROS were generated by ANCGs owing to the photothermal effect, and the accumulated ROS (including H2O2) simultaneously mediated and reinforced continuous 1O2 production through enzymatic conversion, resulting in cascade-amplifying ROS levels that surpassed the cellular redox threshold and caused tumor cell death, while normal cells remained unaffected. This synergistic photothermal-enzymatic effect and biosafety were confirmed by both in vitro cytotoxicity tests and in vivo antitumor experiments. This study provides a promising exogenous/endogenous cascade-amplified ROS-producing strategy for the safe and effective cancer therapy by integrating spatiotemporally controlled physical activation and endogenous biochemical oxidation and offers an important impetus for developing synergistic ROS-mediated therapeutic options.
