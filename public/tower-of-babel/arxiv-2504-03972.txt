1.
Introduction

Let
n,k,N\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\in[1,\infty]
, and let us also fix an open bounded domain
\Omega\Subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Given a mapping
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\supseteq\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the derivatives (of first, second, and
k
-th order) will be denoted by

\left\{\ \ \ \begin{split}\mathrm{D}u=\big(\mathrm{D}_{i}u_{\alpha}\big)_{i\in\{1,...,n\}}^{\alpha\in\{1,...,N\}}&\ :\ \ \Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{Nn},\\
\mathrm{D}^{2}u=\left(\mathrm{D}^{2}_{ij}u_{\alpha}\right)_{i,j\in\{1,...,n\}}^{\alpha\in\{1,...,N\}}&\ :\ \ \Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{Nn^{2}}_{s},\\
\vdots\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ &\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \vdots\\
\mathrm{D}^{k}u=\left(\mathrm{D}^{k}_{i_{1}...i_{k}}u_{\alpha}\right)_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in\{1,...,n\}}^{\alpha\in\{1,...,N\}}&\ :\ \ \Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{Nn^{k}}_{s},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \end{split}\right.

and they are valued into their respective (symmetric) tensor spaces, defined as

\mathbb{R}^{Nn^{k}}_{s}:=\bigg\{{\bf X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\!\otimes\underbrace{\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}}_{k\text{ times}}\ :\ {\bf X}_{\alpha i_{1}\cdots i_{k}}={\bf X}_{\alpha\sigma(i_{1}\cdots i_{k})},\sigma\text{ permutation on }(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\bigg\}.

Finally,
{\bf X}\!:\!{\bf Y}
denotes the Euclidean (Frobenius) inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{Nn^{k}}_{s}
. For brevity, we introduce the following compact notation for the “
k
-th order jet” of
u
:

\mathrm{D}^{[k]}u\,:=\,\Big(\cdot,u,\mathrm{D}u,\mathrm{D}^{2}u,...,\mathrm{D}^{k}u\Big)\ :\ \ \Omega\,\longrightarrow\,\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{Nn}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{Nn^{k}}_{s}.

Given a continuous function

\mathrm{H}\ :\ \ \Omega\times\Big(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{Nn}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{N(n-1)^{k}}_{s}\Big)\times\mathbb{R}^{Nn^{k}}_{s}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},

we will symbolise the arguments of the supremand
\mathrm{H}
throughout this note as
\mathrm{H}(x,\mathrm{X},\textbf{X})
. Let us consider the functional

(1.0.1)

\ \ \ \ \ \mathrm{E}_{p}(u):=\big\|\mathrm{H}(\mathrm{D}^{[k]}u)\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)},\ \ \ u\in\mathrm{W}^{k,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N}).

Here
\mathrm{W}^{k,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N})
stands for the standard Sobolev space of
k
-times weakly differentiable maps
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\supseteq\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
, which lie, together with all their partial derivatives up to
k
-th order, in the space
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N})
. For technical simplicity in the estimates, we will use the rescaled
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-norms, when
1\leq p<\infty
:

\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)}:=\left(\,-\hskip-13.0pt\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|f|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{n}\!\right)^{\!1/p}.

Here
\mathcal{L}^{n}
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Our general functional space, measure theory and PDE notation is generally standard, or otherwise self-explanatory, as e.g. in
[
22
]
. Let us define the functional

(1.0.2)

\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \mathrm{C}_{\infty,p}(u)\,:=\,\frac{\mathrm{E}_{\infty}(u)}{\mathrm{E}_{p}(u)},\ \ \ \ u\in\mathrm{W}^{k,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N}),\ \mathrm{E}_{1}(u)\neq 0.

Since
\mathrm{E}_{1}(u)=0
if and only if
\mathrm{E}_{p}(u)=0
if and only if
\mathrm{H}(\mathrm{D}^{[k]}u)=0
a.e. on
\Omega
, (
1.0.2
) is well defined.

Herein we are interested in the
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
variational problem

(1.0.3)

\ \ \ \ \ \mathrm{C}_{\infty,p}(u_{\infty})\,=\,\inf\Big\{\mathrm{C}_{\infty,p}(u)\ :\ u\in\mathrm{W}^{k,\infty}_{\varphi}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N}),\ \mathrm{E}_{1}(u)\neq 0\Big\},

where
\varphi\in\mathrm{W}^{k,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N})
is a given mapping,
p\in[1,\infty)
is fixed, and
\mathrm{W}^{k,\infty}_{\varphi}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N})
symbolises the standard affine Sobolev space of zero-trace mappings

\mathrm{W}^{k,\infty}_{\varphi}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N})\,:=\,\varphi+\mathrm{W}^{k,\infty}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N}).

In other words, we are interested in finding a minimiser
u_{\infty}\in\mathrm{W}^{k,\infty}_{\varphi}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N})
which realises the infimum in (
1.0.3
). The primary motivation to study the problem (
1.0.3
) comes from applications. When
p\in\{1,2\}
in (
1.0.2
), the functionals
\mathrm{C}_{\infty,1}
and
\mathrm{C}_{\infty,2}
are known as the “Crest factor” and arises as the “peak–to–average ratio” problem in numerous applications, including eg. the modelling of nuclear reactors, engineering, signal and sound processing, se e.g.
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
24
,
26
,
28
]
. Further, if
\mathrm{H}(x,\cdot,\cdot)
is for all
x\in\Omega
positively homogeneous of some degree
K>0
, then (
1.0.3
) is equivalent to the following constrained
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
variational problem (with integral constraint):

(1.0.4)

\ \ \ \ \ \mathrm{E}_{\infty}(u_{\infty})\,=\,\inf\Big\{\mathrm{E}_{\infty}(u)\ :\ u\in\mathrm{W}^{k,\infty}_{\varphi}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N}),\ \mathrm{E}_{p}(u)=1\Big\}.

Interestingly, the variational problem (
1.0.3
) is ill-posed. On the one hand, the admissible class is not weakly* closed in
\mathrm{W}^{k,\infty}_{\varphi}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N})
in any reasonable manner. On the other hand, the functional
C_{\infty,p}
is not sequentially weakly* lower-semicontinuous. Therefore, a priori the infimum in (
1.0.3
) might not be attained by a mapping in the admissible class. This implies that standard variational approaches, including the Direct Method of the Calculus of Variations, cannot straightfowardly be applied to yield the existence of a minimiser. The problem (
1.0.3
) can be seen as a kind of generalised eigenvalue problem for supremal functionals in the context of the Calculus of Variations in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
, in the spirit of the works
[
11
,
12
,
18
]
. Here the Crest factor (
1.0.2
) plays the role of a generalised Rayleigh quotient, but unlike the classical Rayleigh quotient, technical complications noted earlier arise because the denominator involves derivatives of the same order as the numerator.

In this note we demonstrate how we can easily resolve (
1.0.3
) without involving neither standard variational methods, nor any generalised methods for functionals which are not weakly lower semicontinuous, typically involving Young measures, relaxations and lower semi-continuous envelopes. Instead, our approach is inspired by some recent progress on higher order supremal variational problems made in
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
. More specifically, we prove that set of minimisers of (
1.0.3
) can be characterised as the set of strong a.e. solutions
(u,\Lambda)\in\mathrm{W}^{k,\infty}_{\varphi}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N})\times[\Lambda_{*},\infty)
to the eigenvalue Dirichlet problem for the next fully nonlinear PDE of
k
-th order, above some “critical” eigenvalue
\Lambda_{*}\geq 0
:

(1.0.5)

\left\{\ \ \ \begin{array}[]{ll}\big|\mathrm{H}\big(\cdot,u,\mathrm{D}u,\ldots,\mathrm{D}^{k}u\big)\big|=\Lambda,&\text{ a.e.\ in }\Omega,\\
u=\varphi,&\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\\
\mathrm{D}u=\mathrm{D}\varphi,&\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\\
\ \ \ \ \vdots&\ \ \ \ \vdots\\
\mathrm{D}^{k-1}u=\mathrm{D}^{k-1}\varphi,&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Given our notation for jets, we can rewrite the
(k-1)
-th order Dirichlet problem (
1.0.5
) in the following abbreviated form as:

\left\{\ \ \ \begin{array}[]{ll}\big|\mathrm{H}\big(\mathrm{D}^{[k]}u\big)\big|=\Lambda,&\text{ a.e.\ in }\Omega,\\
\mathrm{D}^{[k-1]}u=\mathrm{D}^{[k-1]}\varphi,&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Our main result in this regard is Theorem
3
, which fully characterises the equivalence between these two classes of mappings. The idea of the proof is as follows: noting that Hölder’s inequality implies
\mathrm{C}_{\infty,p}(u)\geq 1
, we show that the infimum in fact is equal to one if and only if
\big|\mathrm{H}\big(\mathrm{D}^{[k]}u\big)\big|
is essentially constant on
\Omega
. Of course, the proof of equivalence still leaves open the question when (
1.0.5
) is actually solvable, and in fact solvability of this problem is a necessary condition in Theorem
3
. To this end, we present two different results in this regard. Both results rely essentially on the Dacorogna-Marcellini Baire Category method for implicit PDEs, for which the standard reference is
[
14
]
.

The first result regarding the existence of solutions for (
1.0.5
), Proposition
1
, is essentially a direct adaptation of results from
[
14
]
to our setting, providing sufficient conditions for the solvability of (
1.0.5
), which unsurprisingly include quasiconvexity and coercivity for
\mathrm{H}
with respect to the argument for the leading derivative term. The least possible eigenvalue
\Lambda_{*}
for solvability is determined by the value of the nonlinear operator applied to boundary condition
\varphi\in\mathrm{W}^{k,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N})
. However, for reasons relevant to approximation results involving quasiconvex functions, one needs additional convexity assumptions to have a completely general boundary condition as above. Otherwise, the problem is solvable for piecewise smooth boundary conditions (for details see
[
14
]
and subsequently).

Our second result regarding the existence of solutions for (
1.0.5
), Proposition
2
, is a less straightforward application of the Baire Category method, and applies to the case of equal dimensions
n=N
and for order
k=1
in (
1.0.5
). It relies on the solvability of the singular value problem, and interestingly does not require quasiconvexity of
\mathrm{H}
with respect to the leading order term, only some other structural assumptions, it does however apply only to piecewise smooth boundary conditions
\varphi
. One could perhaps remove the assumption that
n=N
by arguing as in the paper
[
13
]
, but it is not clear whether this method can yield general boundary conditions in this case too as in
[
13
]
. Anyhow, we refrain from delving into this task on this occasion, as the sufficient conditions provided by Proposition
1
are rather natural.

We conclude this introduction by placing this problem into a wider context. Supremal variational problems were first considered in the 1960s by the pioneer of this field, Aronsson (see
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
). For scalar first-order
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
variational problems (in which the supremand depends on the gradient of real-valued functions and perhaps lower-order terms), the theory is fairly well developed. Without any attempt to be exhaustive, we may refer to the following interesting relevant papers:
[
1
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
23
,
25
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
. The vectorial first-order case is considerably more challenging, and largely still under development. Our work herein is inspired by recent progress in the vectorial and higher order case
[
12
,
20
,
21
]
, in which relevant phenomena were exhibited.

Except for the intrinsic theoretical interest, one of the advantages of minimising supremal energies is that they provide a uniformly small pointwise energy, whereas minimising an integral energy may allow for large spikes of the maximum pointwise energy, even though the area under the graph could still be small. This approach could provide better models in applications where this difference is relevant. However, the development of the theory is extremely challenging, as suprema do not enjoy the measure-theoretic properties of integrals, and practically no standard method of integral functionals applies to supremal functionals. In particular, they are not Gateaux differentiable regardless of the regularity of the supremand
\mathrm{H}
, and global minimisers may not minimise on subdomains with respect to their own boundary conditions.

2.
Main results and proofs

We begin by establishing two existence results for strong solutions to the fully nonlinear eigenvalue problem (
1.0.5
). Let us recall the following concepts taken from
[
14
]
, for the convenience of the reader:

•

Let
\Omega\Subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded open set. A map

is called a piecewise-

map and we write

if there exists a union of countably many disjoint open set

such that
\mathcal{L}^{n}\big(\Omega\setminus(\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}))=0
, namely whose complement in

is a Lebesgue nullset, and

, namely

extends to a map which is
\mathrm{C}^{k}
up to the boundary of
\Omega_{i}
.

•

A continuous function
F:\mathbb{R}^{Nn^{k}}_{s}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called (Morrey) quasiconvex if

f(\textbf{X})\,\leq\,-\hskip-13.0pt\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{n}}f\big(\textbf{X}+\mathrm{D}^{k}\psi\big)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{n},

\text{ for all }\psi\in\mathrm{W}^{k,\infty}_{0}({\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{N})\text{ and all }\textbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn^{k}}_{s}.

]
on the Dacorogna-Marcellini Baire Category method for implicit PDE and differential inclusions.

Proposition 2
.

Fix
\Omega\Subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\varphi\in\mathrm{C}^{1}_{{\rm pw}}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Suppose that
\mathrm{H}:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies

\mathrm{H}(x,\mathrm{X},{\bf X})=h\big(x,\mathrm{X},{\bf X}^{\top}{\bf X}\big),

for some
h\in C\big(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}_{s}\big)
. Assume also that for any
(x,\mathrm{X})\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the function
h(x,X,\cdot)
is strictly increasing along the direction of the identity, namely
t\mapsto h\big(x,X,t\hskip 1.0pt\mathbb{I}_{n}\big)
is strictly increasing on
\mathbb{R}
. Further, we suppose that there exists
\alpha_{0}>0
such that

(2.0.6)

\underset{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\sup}h\big(\cdot,\cdot,\alpha_{0}\mathbb{I}_{n}\big)\,<\,\infty,

and also that the following uniform coercivity assumption is satisfied

(2.0.7)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\Big(\,\underset{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\inf}h\big(\cdot,\cdot,t\hskip 1.0pt\mathbb{I}_{n}\big)\Big)\,=\,\infty.

Then, the eigenvalue Dirichlet problem

(2.0.8)

{\begin{cases}\ \ \mathrm{H}(\cdot,u,\mathrm{D}u)=\Lambda,\ &\text{a.e.\ on }\Omega,\\
\ \ u=\varphi,\ &\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}}

has (infinitely many) strong solutions
(u,\Lambda)\in\mathrm{W}^{1,\infty}_{\varphi}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times(\Lambda_{*},\infty)
, where

(2.0.9)

\Lambda_{*}\,:=\,\max\left\{\underset{\Omega}{\mathrm{ess}\sup}\,h\Big(\cdot,\varphi,\|\mathrm{D}\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\mathbb{I}_{n}\Big)\,,\,\underset{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\sup}h\big(\cdot,\cdot,\alpha_{0}\mathbb{I}_{n}\big)\right\}.

In fact, for any fixed
\Lambda>\Lambda_{*}
, (
2.0.8
) has infinitely many solutions
u\in\mathrm{W}_{\varphi}^{k,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Finally, the result above remains true if
h\geq 0
, but we only require that
t\mapsto h\big(x,X,t\hskip 1.0pt\mathbb{I}_{n}\big)
be strictly increasing on
[0,\infty)
(alongside (
2.0.6
) and (
2.0.7
)), resulting in the exact same conclusions via the same proof, with the additional premise that
\Lambda_{*}\geq 0
.

Proof of Proposition
2
.
Step 1
.
We first obtain some estimates directly from our assumptions. Let
\Lambda_{*}
be as in (
2.0.9
), and fix
\Lambda>\Lambda_{*}
. It follows that

\Lambda\,>\,h\Big(x,\varphi(x),\|\mathrm{D}\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\mathbb{I}_{n}\Big),\ \ \text{ a.e. }x\in\Omega.

By the strict monotonicity assumption on
h
, we have

\begin{split}h\big(x,\varphi(x),(\cdot)\mathbb{I}_{n}\big)^{-1}(\Lambda)&>\,\lVert\mathrm{D}\varphi\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\\
&=\,\underset{\Omega}{\mathrm{ess}\sup}\,\big\{(\mathrm{D}\varphi^{\top}\mathrm{D}\varphi):\mathbb{I}_{n}\big\}\\
&\geq\,\underset{\Omega}{\mathrm{ess}\sup}\,\Big\{\max_{e\in\mathbb{R}^{n},|e|=1}(\mathrm{D}\varphi^{\top}\mathrm{D}\varphi):e\otimes e\Big\}\\
&=\,\underset{\Omega}{\mathrm{ess}\sup}\,\lambda_{n}(\mathrm{D}\varphi^{\top}\mathrm{D}\varphi),\end{split}

pointwise on
\Omega
, which implies

(2.0.10)

h\big(x,\varphi(x),(\cdot)\mathbb{I}_{n}\big)^{-1}(\Lambda)\,>\,\lambda_{n}(\mathrm{D}\varphi^{\top}\mathrm{D}\varphi),\ \ \text{ a.e.\ on $\Omega$.}

Further, again by (
2.0.9
), we have

\Lambda\,>\,h\big(x,X,\alpha_{0}\mathbb{I}_{n}\big),

for some
\alpha_{0}>0
and all
(x,X)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By the monotonicity assumption on
h
and the above inequality, we infer that

\begin{split}h\big(x,X,(\cdot)\mathbb{I}_{n}\big)^{-1}(\Lambda)\,>\,h\big(x,X,(\cdot)\mathbb{I}_{n}\big)^{-1}\Big(h\big(x,X,\alpha_{0}\mathbb{I}_{n}\big)\Big)\,=\,\alpha_{0},\end{split}

for all
(x,X)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which yields

(2.0.11)

\begin{split}\underset{(x,X)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\inf}h\big(x,X,(\cdot)\mathbb{I}_{n}\big)^{-1}(\Lambda)\,>\,\alpha_{0}.\end{split}

Further, by the uniform coercivity assumption (
2.0.7
) on
h
, there exists an
A\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\Lambda\,\leq\,\underset{(x,X)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\inf}h\big(x,X,A\hskip 1.0pt\mathbb{I}_{n}\big).

By the monotonicity of
h
, this estimate is equivalent to

(2.0.12)

\underset{(x,X)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\sup}h\big(x,X,(\cdot)\mathbb{I}_{n}\big)^{-1}(\Lambda)\,\leq\,A,\ \ \ A\in\mathbb{R}.

Step 2
.
We now demonstrate the solvability of (
2.0.8
) by constructing an auxiliary problem. Then, the estimates of
Step 1
will be the sufficient conditions for existence of solution to the auxiliary problem. To this aim, let us define the function
\alpha:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
by setting

(2.0.13)

\alpha(x,X)\,:=\,h\big(x,X,(\cdot)\mathbb{I}_{n}\big)^{-1}(\Lambda).

For any matrix
\mathrm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}_{s}
, let
\{\lambda_{1}(\mathrm{X})
, … ,
\lambda_{n}(\mathrm{X})\}
denote its eigenvalues in increasing order. Consider the following Dirichlet problem involving singular values of the gradient matrix

(2.0.14)

{\ \ \ \begin{cases}\ \ \lambda_{i}(\mathrm{D}u^{\top}\mathrm{D}u)\,=\,\alpha(\cdot,u),&\text{a.e. on }\Omega,\quad i=1,\ldots,n,\\
\ \ u=\varphi,\,&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}}

By
[
14
, Theorem 7.28, p. 199]
, the problem (
2.0.14
) has (infinitely many) strong solutions in
\mathrm{W}^{1,\infty}_{\varphi}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, as long as
\alpha
is continuous, bounded above, bounded below by a positive constant away from zero, and the boundary condition
\varphi\in\mathrm{C}^{1}_{{\rm pw}}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a “strict subsolution”, in the sense that

\lambda_{n}(\mathrm{D}\varphi^{\top}\mathrm{D}\varphi)\,<\,\alpha(\cdot,\varphi),\ \ \text{ a.e.\ on }\Omega.

By (
2.0.10
), (
2.0.11
), (
2.0.12
) and (
2.0.13
), it follows that all the necessary conditions for the solvability of the problem (
2.0.14
) are satisfied.

Step 3
.
We conclude by showing how the solvability of the auxiliary problem (
2.0.14
) implies the solvability of (
2.0.8
). For any fixed
u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,\infty}_{\varphi}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
solving (
2.0.14
) for a given
\Lambda>\Lambda_{*}
, by the spectral theorem we have that there exists a measurable map of orthogonal matrices
\mathcal{O}\in L^{\infty}\big(\Omega;\mathrm{O}(n,\mathbb{R})\big)
such that

(2.0.15)

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}u^{\top}\mathrm{D}u

\displaystyle=\,\mathcal{O}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}(\mathrm{D}u^{\top}\mathrm{D}u)&&\mathbb{O}\\
&\ddots&\\
\mathbb{O}&&\lambda_{n}(\mathrm{D}u^{\top}\mathrm{D}u)\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{O}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\,\alpha(\cdot,u)\hskip 1.0pt\mathcal{O}\hskip 1.0pt\mathbb{I}_{n}\hskip 1.0pt\mathcal{O}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\,\alpha(\cdot,u)\hskip 1.0pt\mathbb{I}_{n},

a.e. on
\Omega
. We may then compute

\begin{split}\mathrm{H}\big(x,\,u(x),\,\mathrm{D}u(x)\big)&=\,h\Big(x,\,u(x),\,\mathrm{D}u^{\top}\mathrm{D}u(x)\Big)\\
&=\,h\Big(x,\,u(x),\,\alpha\big(x,u(x)\big)\mathbb{I}_{n}\Big)\\
&=\,h\Big(x,\,u(x),\,(\cdot)\mathbb{I}_{n}\Big)\big(\alpha\big(x,u(x)\big)\big)\\
&=\,\Big[h\Big(x,\,u(x),\,(\cdot)\mathbb{I}_{n}\Big)\circ h\Big(x,\,u(x),\,(\cdot)\mathbb{I}_{n}\Big)^{-1}\Big](\Lambda)\\
&=\,\Lambda,\end{split}

a.e. on
\Omega
. The conclusion ensues. ∎

Remark 4
.

\Lambda\geq\Lambda_{*}

\begin{split}\Lambda=\underset{\Omega}{\mathrm{ess}\,\sup}\,\big|\mathrm{H}(\mathrm{D}^{k}u_{\infty})\big|&\geq-\hskip-13.0pt\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\big|\mathrm{H}(\mathrm{D}^{k}u_{\infty})\big|\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{n}\\
