Understanding Generational Differences in Muslim American Mental Health Help-Seeking
Authors: Leena Badran, Niveen Rizkalla, Steven P. Segal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Attitudes toward mental health help-seeking are shaped by a complex interplay of environmental, sociocultural, religious, and generational factors. Within Muslim communities, these attitudes are further informed by religious teachings, cultural norms, and intergenerational dynamics. This study explores the disparities in mental health-seeking behavior between first- and second-generation Muslim Americans. The sample consisted of 37 participants, including 16 first-generation immigrants (foreign-born) and 21 second-generation individuals born in the USA to at least one immigrant parent. The study used a combined deductive and inductive thematic approach to the analysis of semi-structured interviews. Interview content codes and themes were developed using a six-phase method for qualitative thematic analysis. Four major themes emerged encompassing distinct subthemes. First, acculturation effects including: (a) mental health awareness, normalization, and knowledge; and (b) the influence of acculturation on attitudes. Second, intentions including: (a) first-generation participants often sought private help-seeking; and (b) openness to public help-seeking and a health-conscious orientation. Third, perceived behavioral control. Fourth, pathways to seeking mental health care including: (a) preference for informal support systems; (b) incorporation of religious aspects; and (c) a preference for Muslim therapists. The first three themes highlighted notable intergenerational differences; the fourth theme revealed shared pathways to seeking care between the two groups. Culturally adapted practices to mental health service provision should integrate both traditional and non-traditional cultural awareness while addressing intergenerational gaps to enhance mental health support within these communities.

Scholarly work reveals that attitudes and service utilization for mental health vary across ethno-racial and religious communities. Studies found that minority groups, including Muslims in the USA, are less likely to seek help for mental health challenges despite coping with such challenges in high rates (Al-Krenawi & Graham, 2011; Amri & Bemak, 2013). This reluctance to use formal mental health services persists even though timely interventions can prevent the progression of psychological or psychiatric issues into chronic conditions (Christodoulou & Christodoulou, 2007).

Gaps between generations can occur regardless of the society or culture in which individuals reside. Social processes such as globalization, technology, mobility, historical periods, life expectancy, changes in family structure, and migration are some of the factors contributing to the disparities between generations (Al-Lawati, 2019). The intergenerational gap in attitudes toward seeking mental health help presents challenges in both understanding and addressing mental health needs within Muslim communities in the USA (Mudryk & Johnson, 2023). Although Muslim young generations are more open to integrating professional mental health services into their lives when required (Aromaa et al., 2010), older generations often retain traditional spiritual solutions and support within the family in a time of need, reflecting the broader societal stigma surrounding mental illness (Jimenez et al., 2012, 2022; Mudryk & Johnson, 2023). In general, it has been observed that the younger Muslim generation who were offspring of immigrants and natives of eastern cultures, embraced seeking mental health help accustomed to western countries/cultures where they were born and raised, while also preserving the eastern cultural values rooted in their parents’ heritage. This duality may lead to a cognitive dissonance between the two generational perspectives, causing conflicts and distress (Ahmed, 2019; Rasmi et al., 2015). This divergence can create barriers to effective communication and deprive support within families, which might ultimately affect the well-being of individuals and the community (Herzig, 2011).

Muslims in the USA

The Muslim American population is diverse and rapidly growing, with an estimated 3.45 million individuals of all ages, of which 2.15 million are adults. This population is characterized by a mix of backgrounds/nationalities among immigrants and their descendants, and is divided into three main groups: first-generation (58%), immigrants who were born in another country; second-generation (18%), citizens who were born in the USA with at least one immigrant parent; and a third group of approximately 24% who are US natives with both parents born in the USA (Pew Research Center, 2017).

The Muslim population in the USA faces a variety of social, cultural, and political challenges, leading to heightened stressors. Among these various stressors are cultural adjustment, immigration difficulties, language barriers, discrimination, and Islamophobia, which have been strongly linked to negative mental health impacts (Al Wekhian, 2015). Existing studies point to a general reluctance among Muslims to engage with mental health services due to multiple factors (W. Abu-Ras, 2007; Aloud & Rathur, 2009; Martin, 2014).

Muslims live within a strong family system embedded in a collectivistic society, where social influences from family, peers, and religious scholars and institutions significantly impact daily decision-making. In addition, Islam encourages collectivism as a way of life among Muslims (A. Ali et al., 2020). Muslim families often maintain collectivist values and norms, in perceiving an individual's crisis as part of a family concern that affects the entire system, including the broader community (Christodoulou & Christodoulou, 2007). Therefore, collectivism might promote a preference for seeking mental health support from informal sources within the family or community, which at times is compounded by a significant social stigma surrounding mental health conditions (Badran et al., 2025a, 2025b). Imams, as Muslim faith leaders, play a crucial role in supporting community mental health. Although Muslim Americans may be inclined to seek guidance from religious leaders because of cultural familiarity, mistrust of formal systems, or concerns about social stigma, engagement with Imams should not be viewed as mutually exclusive from professional mental health care. Recent literature highlights the potential for faith leaders, including Imams, to serve as effective bridges to mental health services by providing initial support, reducing stigma, and facilitating appropriate referrals to professional care (Alhomaizi et al., 2018; Ciftci et al., 2013; Humam et al., 2023).

Muslim Americans often integrate aspects of the biopsychosocial model when understanding mental illness. This model is based on the idea that mental illness results from the interplay between a person's biology, internal mental experiences, and social and experiential factors, including religion and spirituality, that shape these experiences. By contrast, the predominant western biomedical model primarily frames mental illness as a medical condition rooted in biological dysfunction and typically emphasizes diagnosis, pharmacological treatment, and clinical intervention (Roberts, 2023). Although they typically adhere to the predominant western biomedical model, many also perceive mental illness as God's will, a test or punishment, or even possession by spirits, perceiving it as an opportunity to strengthen their connection with God (Ali et al., 2022). Findings indicated that the biomedical beliefs and Islamic religiousness predicted more openness to mental health care. Such a finding offers a nuanced understanding of how biological aspects of mental health may align with Islamic spiritual values (Mudryk & Johnson, 2023).

Religion holds a significant value for Muslims, not only for its spiritual attributions, but also as a coping mechanism. The role of religion as a protective factor has been examined in various studies, yielding mixed results (W. Abu-Ras & Abu-Bader, 2009). However, there is empirical support for religiosity and religious coping serving as protective factors against psychological distress (Goforth et al., 2014; Ikizler & Szymanski, 2018; Rizkalla et al., Under review). Middle Eastern/North African Americans turn to religion for comfort, particularly if they attribute their distress to supernatural causes or if they have strong beliefs in religious fatalism. For these individuals, religious beliefs provide solace and a way to make sense of their struggles (Aloud & Rathur, 2009).

The interplay between the multiple identities Muslim Americans hold—religious, ethnic, national—and belonging can heighten their stress levels, especially among immigrants (Berry, 2005; Han & Pong, 2015; Kaur, 2022; Saechao et al., 2012). Immigrants encounter various degrees of acculturation stress based on factors such as reasons for immigration, adjustment to the western country/society, English proficiency, sociopolitical history, socioeconomic status, experiences of trauma, and racial or ethnic discrimination (Amri & Bemak, 2013). Herein, we try to understand how individuals of different generations have integrated these challenges and demands associated with seeking mental health service following the theory of planned behavior. This theory explains behavior by focusing on three main factors: attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. Attitudes refer to an individual's personal views and feelings about a behavior and whether they see it as positive or negative. Subjective norms describe how individuals perceive social pressure, including whether people important to them approve or disapprove of the behavior. Perceived behavioral control reflects how capable a person feels in performing the behavior, taking into account their own abilities as well as any external barriers they may face (Ajzen, 1991). This paper explores the intergenerational gaps in attitudes toward seeking mental health help within Muslim communities in California, considering the cultural, religious, and societal factors that contribute to these disparities. Understanding these gaps might assist in developing sensitive and culturally appropriate mental health interventions that cater to the needs of all generations in this unique minority group.

Methods

Participants and Procedure

Thirty-seven American Muslims who reside in California, USA participated in the study (10 men and 27 women). The participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 63 (M = 30, SD = 13), 78% described themselves as religious and 27% were students. Immigration classification was determined as follows: 16 participants were first-generation immigrants and 21 were second-generation immigrants. Participants from both groups had diverse ethnic backgrounds and originated from the following regions: North Africa, South Asia, Europe, Southeast Asia, Middle East, and West Africa (Table 1).

Procedure

The study received approval from the Institutional Review Board at the University of California, Berkeley (2022-12-15929). A convenience sampling method was used, with the researchers (first two authors) reaching out to Muslim community centers, educational institutions (schools and universities), and various Muslim professional groups for recruitment. Initially, the researchers sought assistance in the recruitment of participants from Imams of mosques, project coordinators, and other community leaders. In instances in which collaboration yielded low responsiveness, snowball sampling was implemented. Inclusion criteria comprised: being a Muslim residing in California, at least 18 years old, and proficient in English or Arabic. Participant recruitment concluded upon reaching data saturation (Saunders et al., 2018) indicating that further interviews were unlikely to yield new information.

Instrument and Data Collection

This qualitative study utilized semi-structured interviews for data gathering. Interviews lasted around 90 minutes and were by the first author in English or Arabic (based on the participant's preference) through the Zoom platform. Interviews were audio-recorded after gaining permission from participants who were only required to verbally consent to participate in the study. The co-authors are an interdisciplinary group of three researchers consisting of a Muslim Palestinian, a Christian Palestinian, and an American Jew, all of whom have studied the topic of mental illness among minorities. Guided by the theory of planned behavior (Ajzen, 1991), the interview guide was designed to explore participants’ mental health help-seeking intentions through three core constructs: attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. The guide consisted of ten open-ended questions aligned with these constructs to allow participants to describe their perspectives in their own words. The interview guide was reviewed by mental health experts serving Muslim and Arab communities and was refined based on their feedback.

Data Analysis

Using a combined deductive and inductive thematic approach within the areas of the theory of planned behavior, the data was systematically coded to extract themes based on participants’ responses. The codes and themes were developed using Braun and Clarke (2006) six-phase method for qualitative thematic analysis. The audio-recorded interviews were initially transcribed and translated by the first author. The transcripts were then analyzed by both the first and second authors, who were responsible for reviewing all the transcripts to create initial codes and organize the data to identify themes. Major themes were inductively identified from these codes. Following this, the first two authors reviewed and categorized the coded data into major themes and subthemes, ensuring that these were well-supported by the transcripts (Smith, 2003). Regular meetings among the authors facilitated the comparison and refinement of themes and subthemes, with any disagreements resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached. This collaborative process clarified and re-defined the themes and refined their organization and interrelationships. Given the research team's insider perspectives, reflexivity was practiced throughout the analytic process. The researchers engaged in ongoing self-reflection, discussed assumptions during team meetings, and used iterative coding and peer debriefing to reduce potential bias and maintain interpretations closely grounded in participants’ narratives. Hard and electronic copies of the transcripts were used during the analysis, which was conducted manually, instead of using specialized software. To maintain participant anonymity and confidentiality, pseudonyms were used. The third author supervised the study design, ethical and institutional procedures, manuscript preparation, and reviewed and edited the final version of the manuscript.

Results

Our analysis revealed four main themes with several subthemes. First, acculturation effect: (a) mental health awareness, normalization, and knowledge; (b) acculturation impact on attitudes. Second, intentions: the first-generation participants often sought mental help secretly, whereas the second-generation participants were inclined toward being more open and health-conscious oriented. Third, perceived behavioral control. Fourth, pathways to seeking mental health care: (a) preference for informal support systems, (b) incorporation of religious aspects in seeking help, and (c) preference for Muslim therapists.

We have organized the analysis of themes based on intergenerational differences and similarities.

Discussion

This qualitative study explored intergenerational gaps (disparities and similarities) in seeking mental health help among first- and second-generation Muslims in California. Participants were Muslim Americans who resided in California. Our results revealed four major themes: the effects of acculturation, intentions, perceived behavioral control and pathways to seeking mental health help. These themes highlight both differences and similarities between the two groups.

Differences

Differences in Mental Health Awareness, Normalization, and Knowledge

The second-generation group exhibited greater awareness, normalization, and acceptance of mental health challenges compared with the first-generation group. Second-generation participants were more comfortable discussing their mental health needs and seeking professional support, which was influenced by exposure at schools, higher education, workplaces, and media in California. Research shows that integrating a continuum component of mental health support directly into the school's system leads to improved social, emotional, behavioral, and academic outcomes (Hoover & Bostic, 2021). This environment helped normalize mental health care in their experiences, and fostering more favorable attitudes by emphasizing the benefits of seeking help, equating its importance to physical health.

Furthermore, the first generation continues to grapple with a cultural disjunction, feeling caught between their traditional cultural values—characterized by stigma and shame surrounding mental health—and the evolving perspectives of the dominant society. For first-generation participants, cultural beliefs about mental health care remained deeply ingrained, even for those who had studied or lived in the USA. This cultural tension made it difficult to fully embrace western approaches to mental health despite acknowledging their potential benefits. This struggle highlights the enduring impact of cultural heritage on attitudes toward mental health and the challenges faced in reconciling traditional beliefs with western approaches.

Understanding of mental health causality also differed between the two groups. Although many participants adopted a biopsychosocial perspective, some first-generation individuals associated mental health conditions with religious factors, such as a perceived weakness of faith. Some first-generation participants acknowledged personal resilience and emotional self-control as protective factors against distress but recognized the need for professional help in cases of chemical imbalances or challenges beyond their control. First-generation participants experienced acculturation stress, which shaped their beliefs and attitudes toward mental health. They were more influenced by social stigma, which affected their willingness to seek help. By contrast, the second-generation participants expressed more favorable attitudes, emphasizing the benefits of seeking professional mental health support. These findings were consistent with another study conducted in the UK, where younger generations had more favorable attitudes toward depression compared to older generations (McClelland et al., 2014).

Intentions

The two groups differed in their approach to seeking mental health help. The first generation tended to do so secretly, constrained by cultural norms and the stigma surrounding mental health. They expressed a desire to break free from these pressures, but often struggled to do so. By contrast, the second generation viewed seeking mental health support as one of many valid options, largely unaffected by cultural or societal stigma. For the second generation, mental health care was framed as a tool for personal growth and well-being, rather than a medicalized or stigmatized action.

Perceived Behavioral Control

Financial cost emerged as a significant barrier to accessing formal mental health services, particularly for second-generation participants who were mostly single and relied on parental support. High out-of-pocket fees may limit students’ ability to seek care independently, highlighting the need for affordable, accessible mental health options for young adults in this community. Conversely, first-generation participants, all of whom were married, described how their marital and family roles shaped their sense of control and support when considering mental health care.

Similarities

Despite the diverse disparities between the two generational groups, they have also presented several similarities.

Preference for Informal Support

Both groups prioritized informal support systems, such as family, friends, and their inner societal community, before considering or seeking external/professional help. They emphasized the role of parents and elders in decision-making related to mental health and considered the family dynamic central to coping with challenges. It is common among individuals from collectivist backgrounds to first seek help from family members or religious figures before considering formal mental health services. These cultural and religious norms emphasize collective well-being and the importance of maintaining community-based solutions over individual therapy (Abideen & Abbas, 2021). This sense of collectivism fosters emotional security, closeness, and strong connections with significant figures (Rizkalla & Rahav, 2016). Kapadia et al. (2017) found that family was the primary support system for Pakistani women, unlike White women. Among Muslims, help-seeking is often described as a contextual process, where social networks offer support as an alternative to formal services, which is perceived as less judgmental (Badran et al., 2025a). Hashemi et al. (2019) found that among Middle Eastern migrants in Australia, ethnic acculturation was directly associated with psychological well-being because it provided individuals with a sense of belonging, stability, and relatedness due to shared collectivist values within their own ethnic group.

Importance of Religious Guidance and Preference for Muslim Therapists

Participants across generations valued religious coping mechanisms, such as consulting with Imams, reading the Quran, and drawing on religious stories for encouragement and solace. Religious practices, such as prayer and consultation with scholars, are often seen as valuable steps in managing mental health conditions, reflecting a strong cultural and religious influence. Both generations expressed a preference for Muslim therapists, highlighting the importance of shared cultural and religious understanding in fostering effective therapeutic relationships and outcomes. The impact of religion on individuals who have experienced difficult life events is a recurring topic in the literature, with ongoing debates about whether its influence is positive or negative (Cetty et al., 2022). Many studies highlight that religious practices often serve as a source of comfort, relief, a protective factor, and a sense of personal security (Abu-Raiya et al., 2011; Florez et al., 2018). Religion offers a framework for interpreting distress, which can help individuals reframe challenging situations in a more positive light, fostering resilience (Humam et al., 2023). In cultures where religion holds a dominant role, such as among Muslims, religious organizations/institutions and community events are crucial support systems (Badran et al., 2025a). These institutions not only provide spiritual guidance to Muslims, but they also serve as social networks that offer emotional, practical, and psychological support to individuals facing mental health challenges (Ali et al., 2022). The communal aspects of religious practices can create a sense of belonging and connectedness, which are essential for coping with distress and promoting well-being (Badran et al., 2025a). This notion is reinforced by a study on Muslim community members in California who expressed the need for developing support groups within their mosque space (W. M. Abu-Ras, 2003).

It is essential to acknowledge the diversity within Muslim communities, considering sociocultural backgrounds, country of origin, educational levels, economic status, degrees of religiosity, and migration/displacement experiences. Attitudes toward seeking mental health support may also shift over time as communities grow more familiar with mental health concepts and the services available to them. To address these needs, it is vital to ensure the availability of affordable, accessible, and culturally sensitive health and mental health services tailored to this population (Farrag & Hammad, 2005). Given that many younger adult Muslims reside with their families until attending distant universities, colleges, or marrying, the role of the family remains central in designing and implementing interventions for individuals experiencing mental health challenges. Furthermore, the preference for therapists who share similar religious beliefs and values underscores the importance of culturally sensitive care. Participants emphasized the importance of having their values and beliefs understood and respected during therapy, advocating for approaches that prioritized non-medicated treatments unless a diagnosis necessitated medication.

Limitations and Implications

It is important to recognize that Muslim Americans are a diverse and heterogeneous population, which makes drawing meaningful comparisons across different subgroups very challenging. Thus, the findings of this study can only shed some light on the intergenerational disparities among Muslims who reside in California. In addition, factors such as the extent of religiosity and length of stay in the USA have been shown in other studies to influence the acculturation process. However, these variables were not included in the demographics of the current study, which might limit the ability to fully understand the nuances of acculturation within the studied population. Future studies may explore generational disparities across multiple US states.

Conclusion

The study highlights important implications for research, practice, and policy. Culturally adapted interventions that integrate traditional beliefs with contemporary practices are needed, along with research on generational differences in mental health attitudes and informal support systems. mental health professionals should provide culturally sensitive care and actively engage with the family and religious support systems, especially with immigrant population. Educational outreach programs and culturally appropriate campaigns are essential. Policy efforts should ensure access to culturally appropriate services, allocating funding that mitigate social stigma.
