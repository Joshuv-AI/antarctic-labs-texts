Introduction

Cystatins, members of the I25 superfamily, are protease inhibitors that play essential roles in regulating the activity of endogenous and, in some cases, exogenous cysteine proteases across a wide range of organisms. Structurally, members of the cystatin superfamily share a conserved fold consisting of a central α-helix wrapped around a five-stranded antiparallel β-sheet (Alvarez-Fernandez et al., 2005). Their inhibitory activity is mediated by three key regions that together form a wedge-shaped binding interface: the flexible N-terminal trunk and two hairpin loops (L1 and L2). These regions contain conserved sequence motifs, including an N-terminal Gly residue, the Gln-Xxx-Val-Xxx-Gly motif in the L1 loop, and either a Leu-Pro or Pro-Trp motif in the L2 loop. Together, these elements interact with the active site cleft of papain-like peptidases (clan CA, family C1) (Pemberton, 2006). Some cystatins also contain SND/SNS/TND motifs responsible for the inhibition of legumain (clan CD, family C13) (Alvarez-Fernandez et al., 1999; Ilgová et al., 2017). Variations in these structural features, particularly in parasite-derived or non-canonical cystatins, influence inhibitory specificity as well as additional functional properties (Gregory and Maizels, 2008).

The superfamily comprises three groups: type 1 cystatins (I25A; stefins), type 2 cystatins (I25B; true cystatins), and type 3 cystatins (I25C; multidomain cystatins) (Turk et al., 2008, 2019). Classical stefins lack signal peptides and disulfide bonds and are predominantly distributed intracellularly (cytosolic and nuclear). By contrast, type 2 cystatins contain an N-terminal signal peptide and disulfide bonds and are largely secreted or targeted to the endo-lysosomal/secretory pathway, modulating extracellular and lysosomal cysteine protease activity. Beyond their intracellular regulatory roles, parasite cystatins are key mediators of host-parasite interactions and often act as immunomodulators (Gregory and Maizels, 2008; Turk et al., 2019). Cystatins have additionally been explored as vaccine candidates, diagnostic markers, and therapeutic targets in parasitic diseases (Abdulla et al., 2007). They have also attracted interest as therapeutic modulators in cancer and inflammatory disease (Breznik et al., 2019; Smallwood et al., 2017). More recently, a distinct subtype, cystatin-like stefins, has been identified exclusively in two groups of parasites, myxozoans and foodborne flukes (Bartošová-Sojková et al., 2021; Buša et al., 2023). These molecules differ markedly from canonical stefins both phylogenetically and structurally, displaying highly divergent primary structures, the presence of a signal peptide, and the absence of the otherwise conserved C-terminal region (Bartošová-Sojková et al., 2021; Buša et al., 2023).

Myxozoa are obligate endoparasites within the Cnidaria that primarily infect fish and annelids. Despite their phylogenetic proximity to free-living cnidarians such as jellyfish, myxozoans exhibit extreme morphological simplification and genome reduction as an adaptation to parasitism (Chang et al., 2015). Their cystatin repertoire is limited to cystatin-like stefins (Bartošová-Sojková et al., 2021). Sphaerospora molnari, a myxozoan pathogen of common carp (Cyprinus carpio), causes gill and skin sphaerosporosis and severe hemolytic anemia (Korytář et al., 2020). During the presporogonic phase, motile SMBS rapidly proliferate in the host’s circulatory system, feeding on erythrocytes and later invading the liver (Korytář et al., 2019, 2020). Sporogony occurs intercellularly in the gills and skin, where mature spores develop and are released into the aquatic environment to infect an as-yet unidentified invertebrate host (Dobai and Bartosová-Sojková, 2024).

While metazoan cystatins are relatively well-studied (Gregory and Maizels, 2008; Turk et al., 2008, 2019), myxozoan stefins remain largely uncharacterized. With the exception of a recently published comprehensive phylogeny of myxozoans stefins (Bartošová-Sojková et al., 2021), data on their expression patterns and biochemical properties remain scarce, and structural as well as functional characterization is virtually absent (Alama-Bermejo et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2020). Characterizing these inhibitors in early-branching metazoans therefore offers a unique opportunity to uncover previously unknown biochemical and structural features of cystatin-like proteins and to infer ancestral principles of protease regulation that shaped their diversification in later lineages. In addition, elucidating their roles may open up avenues to limit myxozoan infections, which pose an emerging risk to both wild fish populations and farmed aquaculture systems (Alama-Bermejo et al., 2022).

Here, we selected Smolstatin1, a cystatin-like stefin and one of three putative cysteine protease inhibitors expressed by S. molnari, for detailed characterization. We define its evolutionary relationships and biochemical properties, resolve its structure, and assess its potential contribution to parasite survival and development during the intrapiscine life cycle.

Material and methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The experimental infection and handling of live animals in this study were carried out in accordance with the Animal Protection Law of the Czech Republic No. 246/1992 Coll., ethics approval No. 88/2019, and protocols approved by the responsible committee of the PAU, BC CAS. Animal handling further complied with the relevant European guidelines on animal welfare (Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes) and the recommendations of the Federation of Laboratory Animal Science Associations.

Sampling procedure

Common carp free of any exposure to S. molnari or vaccination/antigenic challenge were raised in the animal facility of the PAU, BC CAS, referred to as naïve fish in the manuscript from this point on. Starting with naïve fish, we sampled blood, gills, and liver, or experimentally infected fish with SMBS as part of our in vivo S. molnari model, previously described in Korytář et al. (2019). From infected carp, we isolated host-free SMBS from blood using DEAE-cellulose ion exchange chromatography, following the method of Born-Torrijos et al. (2022). As separating parasite cells from host liver cells is not feasible, liver tissue harboring presporogonic S. molnari liver stages was also collected.

Since our laboratory model system does not progress to parasite spore production (Holzer and Pimentel Santos, 2021), sporogonic stages mixed with host gill cells were obtained from naturally infected common carp maintained in a recirculating system of the Research Institute for Fisheries and Aquaculture (Szarvas, Hungary) and from fish reared in the Kardašova Řečice pond system (Velká Ochoz, Czech Republic).

All fish were anesthetized with MS-222 prior to any handling or bleeding, while tissue sampling was performed following spinal transection. Details on sample processing are below.

Data mining and in silico quantification of stefin expression

To investigate the cystatin gene repertoire during the intrapiscine development of S. molnari, we analyzed the parasite genome (GenBank SAMN45157437; Kosakyan et al., 2026). The search methods described in Bartošová-Sojková et al. (2021) were applied to identify I25 superfamily homologues. Stage-specific gene expression of the identified cystatin homologues (genome-based expression analysis) was then assessed by quantifying RNA-seq reads mapped to the S. molnari genome using gene models annotated from the genome (Wiśniewska et al., 2025) as well as in de novo and genome-guided transcriptomes (nrs. SRX23359784–SRX23359794; Wiśniewska et al., 2025). Expression values (TPM) for each identified homologue, were calculated using the Salmon software (Patro et al., 2017), and were used to generate a cross-sample normalized TMM gene expression matrix (TMM), using the Trinity package (Haas et al., 2013; Robinson and Oshlack, 2010). Average TMM values were calculated for each developmental stage using 5 biological replicates per stage (Table S1).

DNA and RNA extraction and PCR validation of target genes

Smolstatin sequences mined from presporogonic (SMBS, liver) and sporogonic (gill) stages were verified by PCR amplification from genomic DNA and cDNA. Previously published primers of Bartošová-Sojková et al. (2021) and newly designed primers targeting the 5′ and 3′ UTRs were used (Table S2). DEAE-cellulose purified SMBS (2 × 105 stages) and homogenized infected carp liver and gill tissue containing parasite stages were preserved in TNES-urea buffer or RNAprotect® Cell Reagent (Qiagen). Genomic DNA was extracted from TNES-preserved material by phenol-chloroform extraction protocol adjusted to myxozoan samples, following overnight proteinase K digestion (Serva) at 55 °C (Holzer et al., 2004). Total RNA was extracted from RNAprotect®-preserved samples using the NucleoSpin® RNA II kit (Macherey-Nagel). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the Transcriptor High-Fidelity cDNA Synthesis Kit (Roche). PCRs were performed using Wizzpure® HS-PCR FDmix (Wizbiosolutions), following the conditions given in Table S3. Amplicons were purified (Geneaid Gel/PCR DNA Fragments Extraction Kit) and DNA sequencing was performed via SEQme, s.r.o. (Czech Republic). The sequencing outputs were verified using tBLASTn searches against the NCBI database, and chromatogram quality was assessed in Geneious Prime v2019.0.4 (Kearse et al., 2012).

Phylogenetic analysis and structural predictions

Phylogenetic analysis utilized stefin homologues extracted from the dataset of Bartošová-Sojková et al. (2021), which included 214 cystatin superfamily protein sequences of choanoflagellates and metazoans, along with S. molnari stefins. Sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.017 with iterative G-INS-i option in Geneious Prime v2019.0.4, resulting in 130 positions. Maximum likelihood analysis was conducted in RAxML v7.0.3 (Stamatakis, 2014), using the LG substitution model, as determined by ModelFinder on the IQ-TREE webserver (Trifinopoulos et al., 2016), with 1000 bootstrap replicates for branch support. Due to its ancestral cystatin superfamily features (Kordiš and Turk, 2009), Giardia intestinalis cystatin (GenBank XP_001705664) served as an outgroup similarly as in previous studies (Bartošová-Sojková et al., 2021; Buša et al., 2023). Signal peptides were predicted in SignalP v5 webserver (Armenteros et al., 2019).

qPCR expression profiling

RNA was extracted from RNAprotect®-preserved blood, liver, and gill parasite stages. cDNA synthesis followed the procedures described above. The smolstatin1-specific primers for RT-qPCR were designed to span exon-intron boundaries (Table S2) and validated for functionality and specificity via conventional PCR prior to RT-qPCR (Table S3). RT-qPCR was carried out using the FastStart™ Universal SYBR® Green Master Mix (with ROX) on a LightCycler® 480 Real-Time PCR System (Roche), with reaction details and cycling conditions provided in Table S3. Relative smolstatin1 expression levels in each parasite stage were normalized to the parasite housekeeping genes ef-2 and gapdh (Kosakyan et al., 2019) and calculated using the Pfaffl method (Pfaffl, 2001). The relative abundance of the target smolstatin1 gene, normalized to the housekeeping genes, was scaled to the sample with the highest observed gene expression level. Data visualization, including confidence intervals and box plots, was performed using GraphPad Prism v10 (GraphPad Software Inc. San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical differences in relative smolstatin1 abundance across blood, liver, and sporogonic parasite stages were assessed via unpaired t tests in GraphPad Prism v10. Significance thresholds were set at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, and ns = not significant.

RNA-ISH

RNA extraction of SMBS samples and cDNA preparation were performed as described above. Riboprobe templates were generated via conventional PCR using smolstatin1-specific primers incorporating T7 promoter sequences (Table S2). The resulting PCR products were purified using the Gel/DNA PCR Extraction Kit (Geneaid Biotech Ltd) and used to synthesize DIG-labeled sense (control) and antisense RNA probes with the High Yield T7 Digoxigenin RNA labeling kit (Jena Bioscience) at 37 °C for 2 h. Potential DNA contamination was removed by DNase I treatment (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C for 15 min. The RNA-ISH assay followed a modified protocol based on Qadiri et al. (2019). Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded carp gill tissues of naïve fish or infected fish with sporogonic stages and whole-mount SMBS samples were all rehydrated through graded ethanol or methanol series, digested with proteinase K (Serva), and post-fixed in 4% formaldehyde. Pre-hybridization was performed at 65 °C for 1 h using a hybridization buffer. Hybridization was performed overnight at 65 °C with DIG-labeled probes diluted 1:1,000 in hybridization buffer, followed by post-hybridization washes at 65 °C and 37 °C. Slides were blocked in Blocking reagent (Roche) at 37 °C for 2 h. The DIG-labeled hybrids were detected with α-DIG-AP Fab fragments (Roche) at 37 °C for 2 h and visualized using NBT/BCIP (Roche). Counterstaining was performed with Nuclear Fast red or Bismarck brown dyes (Sigma-Aldrich). Finally, slides were mounted in glycerol or DPX mounting medium (Sigma-Aldrich). Imaging was conducted with an Olympus BX51 light microscope.

Recombinant Smolstatin1 and antibody production

Cloning and expression of recSmolstatin1 were performed using the Champion™ pET SUMO Expression System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the Smolstatin1 coding sequence (as Sphaerospora molnari stefin 2 gene in GenBank; GenBank MT127417; Bartošová-Sojková et al., 2021) was PCR-amplified from S. molnari cDNA as described above using forward primer 5′-TCA AAG TTA GTG GGG GGA TTT AGT G-3′ and reverse primer 5′-TTA ATG GGA GTG AGT CAA TTC AGA TGC-3′ (Table S2). The purified amplicon was cloned into the Champion™ pET SUMO vector and transformed into Escherichia coli TOP10 for amplification and sequencing verification. The confirmed construct, encoding 6 × His-SUMO-Smolstatin1, was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3)-Gold cells supplied with the kit for expression. Cultures were initiated from a 10 mL overnight inoculum and scaled up to 400 mL LB medium supplemented with 100 µg/mL ampicillin in 2.8-L baffled flasks. Cells were grown at 37 °C to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.8, harvested by centrifugation (3000 × g, 10 min), and resuspended in fresh LB medium containing 1 mM IPTG for induction. Cultures were incubated overnight at 37 °C to allow protein expression. Cells were then collected (3500 × g, 5 min) and resuspended in a resuspension buffer (50 mM phosphate, 300 mM NaCl, pH 8.0), followed by lysis by sonication on ice. Lysates were centrifuged (10,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), and the soluble fraction was applied to a HisTrap™ HP 5 mL Ni²⁺-affinity column (Cytiva) mounted on an ÄKTA FPLC system. Bound proteins were eluted with a resuspension buffer containing 0.5 M imidazole. Fractions were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and WB using an α-polyHistidine–peroxidase conjugate (Sigma-Aldrich). Positive fractions were pooled to obtain a final recSUMO-Smolstatin1 fusion protein concentration of approximately 0.5 mg/mL in a total volume of 20 mL, corresponding to an expression yield of 25 mg/L of bacterial culture. This sample was dialyzed against PBS with 10% glycerol at 4 °C for 3 h using 3.5 kDa MWCO tubing (SERVAPOR®, Serva), and concentrated with Amicon® Ultra centrifugal filters (3 kDa MWCO; Millipore, Merck). SUMO protease cleavage was performed overnight at 30 °C using the kit-supplied enzyme according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cleavage mixture was re-applied to the HisTrap™ column to remove the 6 × His-SUMO tag, and untagged recSmolstatin1 was collected in the flow-through. Fractions were verified by SDS-PAGE, pooled, dialyzed against 20 mM ethanolamine (pH 9.3, 3.5 kDa MWCO SERVAPOR® tubing), and further purified by ion-exchange chromatography on a GE Pharmacia Mono Q 5/50 column mounted on an ÄKTA FPLC system (0–1 M NaCl gradient). Fractions containing recSmolstatin1 were pooled and concentrated using 3 kDa MWCO Amicon® Ultra centrifugal filters (Millipore, Merck) to approximately 9 mg/mL, as determined by the Bradford assay. Protein identity was further confirmed by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry of SDS-PAGE-separated protein bands. Polyclonal affinity-purified antibodies against recSmolstatin1 were generated in rabbits (Davids Biotechnologie GmbH) and validated by SDS-PAGE and WB analysis against both recSmolstatin1 and SMBS parasite lysates. Aliquots of recSmolstatin1 and α-recSmolstatin1 sera were stored at −20 °C until further use.

SDS-PAGE and WB analysis

For SDS-PAGE, SMBS lysates (0.5–3 x 106 SMBS/well), naïve carp blood and gills (20 µg/well), or recSmolstatin1 (400 ng/well) were mixed with DTT and NuPAGE™ LDS Sample Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by heat denaturation. Electrophoresis was performed using 4–20% Mini-PROTEAN® TGX Stain-Free™ Precast Gels (Bio-Rad) in Tris/glycine running buffer, with PageRuler™ Prestained Protein Ladder (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as the molecular weight standard. For WB, SDS-PAGE-separated proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes using the Trans-Blot Turbo™ system (Bio-Rad) according to Perner et al. (2016). Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) non-fat dry milk in TBS containing 0.1% Tw (TBS-Tw), incubated overnight at 8 °C with the α-recSmolstatin1 primary antibody (1:1,000 in TBS-Tw), and subsequently incubated for 1 h at RT with either goat α-rabbit IgG-HRP conjugate (Sigma; 1:2,500 in TBS-Tw) or StarBright Blue 700 goat α-rabbit secondary antibody (Bio-Rad; 1:2,000 in TBS-Tw). Wash steps of 3 × 5 min in TBS-Tw were performed between each incubation, followed by a wash of 4 × 5 min in TBS-Tw. Detection was carried out using Clarity™ Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad) on a ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad). To verify protein loading, either a separate SDS-PAGE gel was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue, or TGX stain-free gel images were captured prior to transfer. Images were analyzed using LabImage protein analysis software (Bio-Rad).

Immunomicroscopy

For immunofluorescence visualization of endSmolstatin1 using confocal microscopy, 2 × 106 DEAE-purified SMBS, isolated from infected carp and suspended in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) during purification and short-term handling, were allowed to adhere to the SuperFrost Plus™ adhesion slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at RT. The parasites were not maintained in culture; RPMI-1640 was used only as a handling medium prior to slide preparation. Fresh carp gill and skin scrapes containing S. molnari spores and sporogonic plasmodia were resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium and adhered to slides using cytospin for 10 min at 1500 g. Blood smears and imprints of gill and liver tissues were prepared from infected and naïve common carp. All samples were fixed in paraformaldehyde-containing IC fixation buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at RT for 30 min. After attachment to the slide, the medium was removed during fixation and washing steps. Slides were washed in PBS, blocked with 1% BSA and 10% goat serum in PBS or in 0.3% PBS-Tw for 1 h, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with α-recSmolstatin1 antibody (1:200). After PBS/PBS-Tw washing, samples were fluorescently labeled with Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated goat α-rabbit antibodies (1:500) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and counterstained with DAPI (Sigma), for 10 min. Negative controls were performed with naïve carp blood or with primary antibodies omitted. Slides were mounted in Fluoromount (Sigma), imaged with an Olympus FluoView™ FV3000 confocal microscope, and analyzed using Fluoview (FV31S-SW, v2.6.1) software.

For immunogold labeling of endSmolstatin1 by TEM, we followed a modified protocol from Hartigan et al. (2016). Five x 106 SMBS resuspended in RPMI salt medium (similar to RPMI-1640 but containing only salt components and glucose; Born-Torrijos et al., 2022) were fixed by mixing the SMBS suspension 1:1 with fixative (8% glutaraldehyde/0.2% formaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer) for 1 h at RT. Carp gill filaments containing sporogonic stages were fixed in 4% glutaraldehyde/0.1% formaldehyde. Samples were embedded in 10% gelatin, cryoprotected in 2.3 M sucrose for 72 h at 4 °C, frozen in liquid nitrogen, ultrathin sectioned, blocked in 3% BSA with 0.05% Tw, and immunolabeled with α-recSmolstatin1 antibodies (1:25) for 30 min at RT. Negative controls omitted primary antibodies. Samples were incubated with protein A conjugated to 10 nm gold nanoparticles (CMC, Utrecht) diluted 1:50 in 0.5% BSA for 45 min at RT, washed, and postfixed. Sections were contrasted using a 9:1 mixture of 2% methylcellulose and 3% aqueous uranyl acetate and imaged using JEOL 1400. To visualize immunogold-labeled subcellular structures in detail and to investigate whether the signal is localized within these structures, electron tomography was used. Electron tomograms were acquired on a JEOL 2100F TEM equipped with a Gatan K2 direct electron detector and controlled by SerialEM (Mastronarde, 2005). Tilt series were recorded at ±65° range and 1° step, processed in Warp (Tegunov and Cramer, 2019), reconstructed using imod software (Mastronarde and Held, 2017), and contrast-enhanced using CLAHE (Zuiderveld, 1994). Image analysis was performed in ImageJ (Schneider et al., 2012).

For SEM, fixed SMBS were adhered to ITO glass slides (Sigma-Aldrich) and stained, dehydrated, carbon-coated, and observed with an Apreo SEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Labeling density of gold nanoparticles was statistically evaluated using ImageJ software across SMBS replicates from three individual fish (Table S5). RLI was calculated as the ratio of LDobs/LDexp, with chi-squared tests applied to assess preferential labeling significance, following Mayhew et al. (2002).

RNAi silencing

RNAi-mediated gene silencing of smolstatin1 was performed using a protocol of Kyslík et al. (2024). Briefly, high-yield cDNA templates were generated via standard PCR using gene-specific primers (Table S2), and 1 µg of the resulting 500 bp amplicon was used for in vitro transcription with the MEGAscript™ T7 Transcription Kit (Invitrogen). Because the length of smolstatin1 coding sequence is short and split by an intron, two dsRNA constructs targeting the 5′ and 3′ regions of the gene were designed. Each dsRNA fragment was initially tested separately, and, based on these preliminary assays, equimolar amounts of both fragments were subsequently combined for RNAi experiments. BLAST analyses against the carp genome and transcriptome confirmed the absence of significant homology to other genes, minimizing potential off-target effect. T7 promoter overhangs were incorporated into the primers flanking the qPCR amplicon regions (Table S2). A dsRNA targeting GFP was used as a non-target control, as described in Kyslík et al. (2024). RNAi assays targeting smolstatin1 were conducted in cultured S. molnari in three independent experiments over a 48-hour period at 20 °C and 5% CO2 following the previously published culturing and experimental workflow (Kyslík et al., 2024). For each condition, 200,000 SMBS were used. The culture medium contained RPMI 1640 medium, 20% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Biosera Europe), 1 mL of GlutaMAX™ (Themo Fisher Scientific), carp red blood cell lysate equaling 1:10 parasite cells: host cells (host cells lysed in water), and 0.008 mL of antibiotics/antimycotics (10,000 U/mL, Sigma-Aldrich. Parasites were harvested at 24 and 48 h post-RNAi. Knockdown efficiency and potential off-target effects were assessed by RT-qPCR, using primer sets listed in Table S2, as described above.

Live-cell imaging and phenotype evaluation

To evaluate the phenotype resulting from smolstatin1 silencing, SMBS motility was recorded by live-cell imaging using an Olympus IX70 inverted microscope equipped with a Canon 1300D camera. Cells cultured in 96-well plates (Kyslík et al., 2024) were recorded for 60 s at 30 frames per second using a 40 × objective, and videos were analyzed using Fiji/ImageJ (Schindelin et al., 2012). Motility was used as a proxy for overall parasite fitness, as it is an intrinsic feature of viable SMBS (Hartigan et al., 2016). For the motility analyzes, time-lapse recordings of individual parasites were acquired at 20 frames per second under each experimental condition. For each parasite and condition, two frames separated by a defined interval (frames between positions) were selected, and the positions of fixed reference points on SMBS were extracted as X–Y coordinates in Fiji/ImageJ (Schindelin et al., 2012). Displacement between the two point positions was calculated from the differences in X and Y coordinates, converted from pixels to micrometers using image calibration, and normalized to elapsed time based on number of frames between positions and the acquisition frame rate. Motility speed (µm/min) was quantified for ten parasites per condition, with three independent intervals analyzed per each parasite.

Changes in parasite morphology were assessed by measuring 2D surface area of SMBS from the same time-lapse recordings as used for motility speed measurements. The dynamics of SMBS is given by rapid emergence and retraction of membrane folds (Hartigan et al., 2016). SMBS outlines were manually traced in Fiji/ImageJ, and surface area was quantified using the "Measure" function. Area measurements were obtained for ten parasites per condition at four time points. Percentage changes in surface area over time were calculated as the relative difference between consecutive measurements for each parasite.

Statistical significance among experimental conditions was determined by two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test in GraphPad Prism v10. Significance thresholds were defined at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, and ns = not significant.

Fish immunization and challenge experiment

The antigenic potential of Smolstatin1 was evaluated by intraperitoneal injection of naïve common carp with Freund’s incomplete adjuvant mixed with recSmolstatin1 at a concentration of 10 µg/fish (n = 5) or 30 µg/fish (n = 5), or with PBS (n = 5) as a control group. Additional control groups were naïve fish (n = 5) and fish with intraperitoneal S. molnari experimental infection (n = 5; 150,000 SMBS/each fish). Circulating IgM antibody in naïve fish plasma as well as in plasma collected 28 days post-recSmolstatin1/PBS vaccination or during S. molnari experimental infection (Ganeva et al., 2020; Chan et al., 2024), was assessed by ELISA. Plate wells were coated with recSmolstatin1, blocked with 7% skimmed milk, and incubated with 1:100 diluted fish sera and α-recSmolstatin1 antibody as a positive control. Afterwards, wells were incubated with 1:2,500 diluted mouse α-carp IgM monoclonal secondary antibodies, produced from the hybridoma cells as described previously (Chan et al., 2024). Final incubation was performed with 1:5,000 diluted goat α-mouse IgG polyclonal tertiary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase. Tetramethylbenzidine substrate was used for substrate detection. To investigate the immunoprotective potential of recSmolstatin1 vaccination against S. molnari infection, parasitemia (parasite burden in the blood) was quantified in the recSmolstatin1-vaccinated and control fish that were intraperitoneally infected with SMBS (50,000 stages/fish). In total, 4 μl of full blood from each challenged fish were collected in the TNES buffer on 24, 28, 31, 35, and 38 DPI. The protective effect of immunization was determined by quantifying parasite numbers using qPCR, as described by Korytář et al. (2019). Statistical significance among experimental conditions was determined by one-way ANOVA with Šidak's multiple comparison test and by two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test in GraphPad Prism v10. Significance thresholds were defined as at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Inhibitory assays

Enzymatic inhibition assays were performed as described previously (Kotál et al., 2019). Inhibition of cysteine and serine proteases by recSmolstatin1 was assessed by measuring loss of fluorogenic substrate hydrolysis in the presence of increasing inhibitor concentrations. recSmolstatin1 was pre-incubated for 10 min with mammalian cysteine proteases (cathepsins B, C, H, K, L, S), Ixodes ricinus legumain (IrAE1; Sojka et al., 2007) and mammalian serine proteases (α-chymotrypsin, chymase, plasma kallikrein, elastase, factor Xa, factor XIa, plasmin, factor XIIa, matriptase, tissue plasminogen activator, and trypsin type III). Corresponding fluorogenic substrates were then added at the final concentrations listed in Table S4, and reactions were monitored at 37 °C using an Infinite® 200 PRO plate reader (Tecan) (Ex 365 nm/Em 465 nm). Hydrolysis rates were converted using AMC (Invitrogen) calibration standards. E64 (Sigma-Aldrich/Merck) and the legumain-specific inhibitor RR-11a (MedChemExpress) were used as positive controls for cathepsin L and legumain assays, respectively. For initial screening, activity in the presence of 1 µM recSmolstatin1 was normalized to uninhibited enzyme activity (100%). For inhibited enzymes, IC50 values were determined by nonlinear sigmoidal curve fitting. All assays were performed in triplicate, and data were analyzed using GraphPad v10. Statistical differences between uninhibited control and recSmolstatin1-treated serine protease activities were analyzed using unpaired t tests in GraphPad Prism v10. Significance thresholds were set at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Active-site labeling

Lysates were prepared as follows: livers from naïve C. carpio were harvested and kept in sterile PBS until processing. In parallel, 1.7 × 10^7 SMBS were isolated, pelleted (800 × g), and resuspended in 50 µL sterile PBS. Liver and SMBS samples were homogenized using Dounce homogenizers, sonicated on ice (50% amplitude; 5 cycles of 15 s on/30 s off), and clarified by centrifugation (16,000 × g, 15 min) to remove insoluble debris. Liver lysates were diluted to 2 µg/mL total protein, whereas SMBS lysates were used undiluted due to low protein concentration. Proteolytic activity was verified using Z-Leu-Arg-AMC (MedChemExpress) prior to labeling. To minimize nonspecific labeling, AEBSF was added to both lysates (final 1 µM). Where indicated, samples were pre-incubated with E64 (1 µM), CA074 (1 µM), or recSmolstatin1 (1, 2, or 4 µM) for 2 h at RT in McIlvaine buffer (pH 4.5) supplemented with 150 mM NaCl. Biotinylated DCG-04 (MedChemExpress) was then added to 1 µM and incubated for 1 h. Labeled samples were analyzed by SDS–PAGE and WB as described previously, using HRP–streptavidin to detect DCG-04.

Smolstatin1 crystallization and structural determination

The SUMO-produced recSmolstatin1 was concentrated to 18 mg/mL using an Amicon® Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Device with a 3 kDa MWCO membrane (Millipore, Merck). Initial crystallization screening was conducted with an OryxNano crystallization robot (Douglas Instruments) in MRC 2 Lens crystallization plates (SWISSCI LTD) using the sitting drop vapor diffusion method. Several commercial crystallization screening kits (Jena Bioscience, Molecular Dimensions, Hampton Research) were employed. Crystallization drops were prepared by mixing protein and crystallization solution in a 1:1 ratio and equilibrating them against 50 µl of crystallization solution at 4 °C. Crystals suitable for diffraction experiments were obtained from a condition containing 0.1 M calcium chloride dihydrate (pH 5.5), 20% w/v PEG 6000, and 10% v/v ethylene glycol. The crystals were flash-cooled in liquid nitrogen without any additional cryoprotection, and diffraction data were collected on beamline BL14.1 at the BESSY II electron-storage ring operated by the Helmholz-Zentrum Berlin (Mueller et al., 2015). Diffraction data processing was performed using XDS (Kabsch, 2010) with the XDSAPP graphical interface Sparta et al., 2016). The recSmolstatin1 crystal structure was solved by molecular replacement method in Phenix (Adams et al., 2010) using an AlphaFold 2-predicted model (Jumper et al., 2021) as the initial template. Structure refinement was carried out in Phenix and REFMAC5 (Murshudov et al., 2011) from the CCP4 suite (Winn et al., 2011) and manually rebuilt in Coot (Emsley et al., 2010). The quality of the final crystallographic model was validated using MolProbity (Chen et al., 2010). Data collection and refinement statistics are detailed in Table S6. Structural figures were prepared using PyMOL (Schrödinger, 2020), which was also used for structural superposition of Smolstatin1 and other stefins and calculations of the root-mean-square deviation of atomic positions (RMSD).

Secondary structure alignment of Smolstatin1 with other stefins was performed using Geneious Prime v 2019.0.4 (Kearse et al., 2012), with annotations of secondary structure elements derived from the DSSP-web server (Touw et al., 2015) assignments for relevant PDB entries. The prediction of residue conservation was performed using ConSurf 2016 (Ashkenazy et al., 2016). Residue accessibility, reflecting the degree of individual amino acid burial within the domain, was calculated using Naccess (Hubbard and Thornton, 1993) implemented in the Consurf online platform, incorporating six sequences structurally similar to Smolstatin1.

Results

Sphaerospora molnari expresses three highly divergent and evolutionarily unique cystatin-like stefins

Data mining of S. molnari NGS datasets revealed that the parasite possesses three cystatin-like stefin-encoding genes, designated herein as smolstatin1 (GenBank MT127417), smolstatin2 (GenBank MT127416) and smolstatin3 (GenBank PX046165). All three smolstatins were identified in the parasite genome assembly. smolstatin1 and smolstatin2 were also detected in both de novo and genome-guided transcriptomes in both the blood and sporogonic stage, while smolstatin3 was only found in the de novo transcriptome in the sporogonic stage (Fig. S1A–C). All three genes were PCR-verified (previously by Bartošová-Sojková et al., 2021; this study Fig. S1D), and their sequences matched the templates derived from NGS data.

All three smolstatin genes contain one or two introns with conserved GT–AG splice sites (Fig. S1A–C). These introns were consistently positioned between the residues 43–44 and 81–82 of the amino acid alignment (Fig. 1A). Smolstatins are highly divergent, sharing only 13.0–25.5% amino acid identity. Despite this divergence, the conserved motifs of the cystatin domain are preserved, including an N-terminal Gly residue, a Gln-Xxx-Val-Xxx-Gly motif, and a C-terminal Leu-Pro motif. However, the Leu-Pro motif is modified to Leu-Gly and Phe-Pro in Smolstatin1 and Smolstatin3, respectively. Notably, the C-terminal Tyr-Phe motif characteristic of canonical stefins is absent from all three Smolstatins. Interestingly, unlike Smolstatin2 and Smolstatin3, Smolstatin1 contains a predicted signal peptide, suggesting entry into the secretory pathway and potential secretion or subcellular localization (Fig. 1A).

In a predicted protein-based phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1B), Smolstatin1 and Smolstatin3 cluster together, whereas Smolstatin2 groups with a homolog from another myxozoan species. Smolstatins grouped with other myxozoan cystatin-like stefins in a monophyletic clade. This clade, along with homologues from foodborne flukes and canonical stefins of metazoans, formed a monophyletic stefin group (Fig. 1B). Genomic context is consistent with the phylogenetic grouping of Smolstatins: smolstatin1 and smolstatin3 are located on the same contig, separated by 1.7 Mb while smolstatin2 resides on a different contig (Fig. S2). The phylogenetic proximity and colocalization of smolstatin1 and smolstatin3 suggest that they likely arose from a duplication event, although the precise mechanism (tandem vs. segmental) cannot be resolved with the current data.

The smolstatin1 gene is expressed in intrapiscine developmental stages, predominantly during sporogony

In silico genome-based gene expression analysis revealed distinct, stage-specific patterns for the three smolstatin genes during intrapiscine parasite development (Fig. 1C–D). Both smolstatin1 and smolstatin2 were expressed in the blood and in sporogonic stages. Notably, smolstatin1 was predominantly expressed in the sporogonic stage, whereas smolstatin2 was upregulated primarily during parasite proliferation, particularly in the blood. In contrast, smolstatin3 expression was restricted exclusively to sporogenesis (Fig. 1D), a pattern confirmed by PCR amplification only from sporogonic-stage cDNA (Fig. S1D). Although smolstatin3 was detected in the genome-based and de novo transcriptomic datasets, it was absent from the genome-guided transcriptome, potentially reflecting stringent filtering thresholds or low expression levels (Table S1). In silico expression analysis of smolstatin genes in the liver stage was not assessed because low read retention in liver samples precluded reliable analysis (Wiśniewska et al., 2025).

Based on the literature review and our findings, we selected the first S. molnari stefin, Smolstatin1, for detailed functional characterization. The decision was based on several factors: i) smolstatin1 is consistently expressed across both intrapiscine parasite stages, with SMBS readily accessible year-round from our S. molnari-fish laboratory model to perform the experiments, ii) it contains a signal peptide, suggesting potential involvement in host-parasite interactions as a secreted molecule, and iii) it is of ancient origin with a unique sequence structure and phylogenetic positioning within the metazoan tree.

The ORF of the smolstatin1 gene is 434 bp long and contains two introns of 37 bp each (Fig. 1A; Fig. S1A). No gene copies were identified in the genome. Similarly, transcriptome assemblies revealed no isoforms. The CDS spans 360 bp and encodes a 120-amino acid pre-protein with a predicted 20-residue signal peptide. The Smolstatin1 protein contains a single cystatin domain.

The qPCR profiling of smolstatin1 gene expression across intrapiscine stages corroborated the in silico analysis (Fig. 1D–E): expression was markedly upregulated during the sporogonic phase in carp gills (mean ± SD = 35.1 ± 29.3), compared with parasite presporogony in blood (2.5 ± 3.7) and liver (1.5 ± 1.2) (Fig. 1C, E).

Smolstatin1 RNAi silencing impairs parasite motility and alters parasite morphology

We achieved RNAi-mediated knockdown of smolstatin1 in SMBS (Fig. 6). Efficient silencing was observed only when equimolar amounts of dsRNAs targeting both the 5′ and 3′ ends of the gene were combined (Fig. S6), resulting in a time-dependent transcript knockdown in both 24- and 48-hour post-treatment (Fig. 6A). In contrast, dsRNA targeting either region alone did not produce detectable knockdown (data not shown). Transient smolstatin1 knockdown led to a significant reduction in parasite motility at 48 h post-RNAi compared with negative and GFP dsRNA controls (Fig. 6B; Video S1–S3).

Smolstatin1 is antigenic but not immunoprotective

Because Smolstatin1 localizes to the SMBS surface, we next evaluated its potential as a vaccine antigen. Two groups of common carp were immunized with either 10 µg or 30 µg of recSmolstatin1. Both groups showed a significant increase in specific antibody levels measured by ELISA four weeks after immunization (Fig. 7A). Following parasite (SMBS) challenge, fish vaccinated with 30 µg of recSmolstatin1 exhibited a statistically non-significant tendency towards a delayed onset of parasitemia relative to non-vaccinated control, most notably on 24 and 28 DPI. Nevertheless, parasitemia levels reached comparable levels in all groups by 31 DPI. At 38 DPI, parasitemia decreased in both the PBS control and group immunized with 10 µg of recSmolstatin1, while it remained comparatively higher in the 30 µg group (Fig. 7B).

Smolstatin1 potently inhibits cathepsin L and also targets legumain

To evaluate the inhibitory potential of recSmolstatin1, we tested its activity against a panel of cysteine and serine proteases (Fig. 8A–C). The recSmolstatin1 was identified as a potent inhibitor of papain-like cathepsins (clan CA, family C1), showing a strong inhibitory effect towards cathepsin L and S endopeptidases. The recSmolstatin1 exhibited exceptionally strong inhibitory activity against human cathepsin L, with an IC₅₀ of 5.4 nM, surpassing the potency of the synthetic cysteine protease inhibitor E-64 (IC₅₀ = 27.2 nM) (Fig. 8B). In contrast, inhibition of cathepsin K and cathepsin B and H was markedly weaker, by several orders of magnitude, compared to cathepsins L and S. No inhibition was observed for the dipeptidyl peptidase cathepsin C, even at the highest recSmolstatin1 concentration tested (11.2 μM) (Fig. 8A, B). Strikingly, beyond its action on clan CA proteases, recSmolstatin1 also inhibited tick asparaginyl endopeptidase IrAE1 (legumain; clan CD, family C13) with an IC₅₀ of 376 nM (Fig. 8A, B). Although this value is higher than that of the specialized legumain inhibitor RR-11a (IC₅₀ = 13.5 nM), it clearly demonstrates that Smolstatin1 uniquely targets two mechanistically distinct protease families. In contrast, tested serine proteases were either only weakly affected or completely resistant to inhibition (Fig. 8C).

Smolstatin1 crystallizes as a domain-swapped dimer

The recSmolstatin1 crystallized as a domain-swapped dimer in the primitive space group P 1 21 1 and diffracted to the resolution of 1.99 Å. The atomic coordinates and experimental structure factors have been deposited in the PDB under the accession code 8AND. Crystallographic analysis revealed that Smolstatin1 adopts the typical cystatin fold, with two polypeptide chains (protomers) arranged as a 3D domain-swapped dimer with the solvent content of 50.57% (Table S6). Clear electron density for each protomer (chain A and chain B) enabled modeling of residues 5–94 and 4–95, respectively, out of the total 100 amino acid residues of the mature protein. Non-protein positive 2Fo – Fc electron density map corresponds to two ethane-1,2-diol molecules from the crystallization solution and 134 water molecules.

The Smolstatin1 dimer is formed by a swap of the subdomains of each protomer (Fig. 10A–C). Each protomer consists of N- and C-terminal disordered regions, one α-helix, four β-sheets, and four loops/coils, arranged sequentially as follows: N-terminal disordered region – β-sheet – coil– α-helix – loop – extended β-sheet containing the β-linker – loop – β-sheet – loop – β-sheet – C-terminal disordered region. Spatially, each protomer can be divided into two major parts. The first α-helical subdomain includes the N-terminal disordered region, a single residue β-sheet, a coil, a five-turn α-helix, a connecting loop, and the initial portion of a long β-sheet. The β-linker region connects this subdomain to a second β-type subdomain, which consists of the second half of the β-sheet, two additional β-sheets, an interconnecting loop, and the C-terminal disordered region. Following the subdomain swapping, the chimeric monomer-like domains are reconstituted in the Smolstatin1 dimer. The dimer features two "closed interfaces" and one "open interface" (Janowski et al., 2001). The "closed interface" occurs in each monomer-like domain between the α-helix and β-sheets. This interface is formed by interactions between the α-helix and part of the long β-sheet of the first protomer and two β-sheets and part of the long β-sheet of the second protomer. These interactions resemble a general cystatin fold typical of monomeric cystatin superfamily members. The "open interface" of Smolstatin1, a novel structural feature typical for dimeric stefins, is formed by interactions between the β-linker regions (βL-βL sheet) of each protomer. The combined interactions of both the "open" and "closed" interfaces result in the formation of a contiguous antiparallel β-sheet of the dimer formed by 21 residues of each protomer. This extended β-sheet is stabilized by 20 main chain hydrogen bonds formed between the two protomer chains. Although a cysteine residue is present at position 56 in the Smolstatin1 molecule (Fig. 10D), it does not form a disulfide bond with the cysteine of the second protomer (33 Å distance between Cys56 of each protomer).

Although the cystatin fold is preserved within the monomeric-like domains of the Smolstatin1 dimer, its spatial distribution of functional motifs differs from that of monomeric stefin proteins. In monomers, the interaction of cystatins with cysteine cathepsins is facilitated by the conserved N-terminal trunk and L1 and L2 hairpin loops, which in stefins contain the conserved Gly-Gly, Gln-Xxx-Val-Xxx-Gly, and Leu-Pro motifs. In the Smolstatin1 dimer, the N-terminal trunk and L2 loop are retained and include the conserved Gly5 in the N-terminal disordered region and Leu74-Gly75 residues in the final loop of the molecule. As seen in other stefin dimers, the L1 loop, typical of monomeric forms, is transformed into an extended β-sheet (β-linker) in the Smolstatin1 dimer (Fig. 10C–D). This β-linker, corresponding to the conserved Gln48-Ile49-Val50-Ser51-Gly52 motif, contributes to forming the extended β-sheet in each protomer (Fig. 10A–D). To evaluate the spatial accessibility of the conserved residues important for the interaction of the inhibitor with the cysteine proteases, the Consurf prediction with the crystal structure of the swapped dimer Smolstatin1 was performed. The analysis identified Gly5, Gln48, Val50, Ser51, and Leu74 as predicted functional residues, which are highly conserved and exposed in the 3D structure (Fig. S7), indicating that such residues are available for the interaction with other proteins. We also investigated the motifs that, in other cystatins, have been recognized as key for legumain inhibition (SND/SNS/TND). Inspection of the Smolstatin1 sequence revealed two asparagine residues in the mature protein. One of these is located within the sequence SLPNQP (residues 33–38), which forms a surface-exposed and highly conserved loop, whereas the second, located in GVNYC (residues 52–56), lies within an extended β-sheet and shows only moderate conservation (Fig. S7).

Our comparative analysis of Smolstatin1 and known PDB structures of domain-swapped stefin dimers identified the dimeric part of the human stefin B tetramer (PDB ID: 2OCT) as the most structurally similar molecule, with an RMSD of 8.991 Å based on the superposition of 967 Cα atoms (Fig. S8A). In contrast, the structure of the domain-swapped dimer of human stefin A (PDB ID: 1N9J), determined using the NMR, exhibited substantial structural differences from Smolstatin1, with an RMSD of 12.126 Å for the superposition of 972 Cα atoms (Fig. S8B).

Discussion

Protease inhibitors of the cystatin superfamily (I25) are key regulators of cysteine proteases and important immunomodulators across Metazoa (Gregory and Maizels, 2008). Although relatively well studied in animals, including various parasitic lineages, their roles in early-branching metazoans such as myxozoans remain largely unexplored. This knowledge gap is particularly intriguing given the basal position of myxozoans within the Metazoa and their obligate parasitic lifestyle, which may have shaped unique adaptations in their protease-inhibitor networks. Thus, functional and biochemical analysis of these cystatin-like stefins can reveal ancestral functions, diversification, and roles at the host–parasite interface.

In S. molnari, we identified a multigene group of cysteine protease inhibitors represented by three cystatin-like stefins – Smolstatin1, Smolstatin2, and Smolstatin3. Smolstatin1 and Smolstatin2, previously identified in SMBS by Bartošová-Sojková et al. (2021), were additionally detected in the sporogonic stage in this study, demonstrating their expression throughout the parasite’s intrapiscine development. The newly discovered gene smolstatin3 is specific to the parasite's sporogony, indicating potential functional divergence. All three inhibitors share sequence and structural features with cystatin-like stefins of other myxozoans (Alama-Bermejo et al., 2022; Bartošová-Sojková et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2020) and foodborne flukes (Buša et al., 2023; Cancela et al., 2017), the only taxa where this inhibitor type has ever been identified. As in those groups, Smolstatins completely lack the C-terminal Asp/Glu-Xxx-Leu-Xxx-Tyr/His-Phe motif typical for canonical stefins, supporting the view that cystatin-like stefins represent an independently diversified lineage that may partially compensate for the absence or limited repertoire of type 2 cystatins (Bartošová-Sojková et al., 2021; Buša et al., 2023).

Despite extensive descriptive work on S. molnari infection (Hartigan et al., 2016, 2020; Korytář et al., 2019, 2020; Kyslík et al., 2024), molecular determinants of host-parasite interactions remain undefined. Here, by integrating expression profiling, ultrastructural localization, biochemical assays, and gene silencing, we provide the first in-depth characterization of a myxozoan cystatin-like stefin, revealing its both conserved and highly specialized properties. Smolstatin1 preferentially inhibited cathepsin L-like endopeptidases (clan CA, family C1), showing strongest activity against human cathepsin L, whereas inhibition of cysteine exopeptidases and serine proteases was minimal or absent. Reduced inhibition of cathepsin B is consistent with structural constraints, including the occluding loop characteristic of this protease (Musil et al., 1991; Turk et al., 2019). Notably, recSmolstatin1 also inhibited the papain-unrelated clan CD asparaginyl endopeptidase/legumain (family C13 of cysteine proteases; Ixodes ricinus IrAE1; Sojka et al., 2007), whose vertebrate homologs play multifaceted roles in immunity (Solberg et al., 2022). Among parasite stefins, convincing experimental evidence for legumain inhibition is limited. The EnStef from Eudiplozoon nipponicum remains the clearest example and contains an atypical SNS motif associated with clan CD inhibition (Ilgová et al., 2017). By contrast, FhCyLS-2 from Fasciola hepatica was reported to inhibit clan CA but not clan CD proteases (Buša et al., 2023), whereas TK-cystatin, from Thelohanellus kitauei lacking the SNX motif was only characterized against papain-family enzymes (Zhang et al., 2020). Smolstatin1 lacks an obvious canonical legumain-binding motif, but two of its asparagine residues, located in the conserved surface-exposed loop SLPNQP and in the extended β-sheet GVNYC, could contribute to legumain recognition. We therefore propose that Smolstatin1 inhibits legumain through a non-canonical, low-affinity binding mode, consistent with its substantially weaker activity against IrAE1 than against cathepsin L.

We suggest Smolstatin1 regulates multiple cysteine proteases of both endogenous and host origin, modulating immunity, and minimizing host tissue damage similarly as shown in other cystatin-like stefins (Buša et al., 2023). In the S. molnari parasite–carp host system, there is a rich protease milieu available on both sides: common carp expresses a wide array of proteases across tissues (Kolder et al., 2016, Nayak et al., 2026) and in S. molnari, cysteine proteases account for nearly 40% of identified peptidase homologues (Hartigan et al., 2020; Wiśniewska et al., 2025), with several papain-like enzymes up-regulated during the gill stage (Wiśniewska et al., 2025). These likely contribute to hemoglobin degradation as the parasite feeds on host erythrocytes (Korytář et al., 2020). Smolstatin1, expressed in the stages within the fish blood and blood-rich gills, may help to regulate the activity of these proteases, similar to cysteine protease inhibitors in other blood-feeding parasites (Ilgová et al., 2017; Yamaji et al., 2009).

Moreover, Smolstatin1 has the potential to regulate endogenous cysteine proteases during critical stages of development. Parasites are especially vulnerable to proteolytic damage during transitions such as sporogony (Morris and Adams, 2008), requiring strict modulation of protease activities for parasite protection (Pimenta et al., 1997). Such protease regulation is often mediated by cystatins elsewhere (Lee et al., 2013; Turk et al., 2008, 2019). Similar to the expression pattern of a cystatin-like stefin in the related myxozoan Ceratonova shasta (Alama-Bermejo et al., 2022), Smolstatin1 is also upregulated during the parasite's sporogony. Our study further shows its localization to key structural elements in spores, including sporoplasm, shell valves, and capsulogenic cells, suggesting Smolstatin1’s roles in cellular remodeling.

Besides the detection of Smolstatin1 within the parasite, immunogold electron microscopy localized the protein on the SMBS surface. Surface localization is a novel finding for stefins, as these molecules typically reside in the cytosol of many cell types and tissues, although they also appear in lysosomes or extracellularly in body fluids (Lee et al., 2013; Turk et al., 2019). Smolstatin1 surface localization, together with its signal peptide (a hallmark of secreted cystatins; Turk et al., 2008) and cystatin-like stefins of other myxozoans (Bartošová-Sojková et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2020) and flukes (Buša et al., 2023; Cancela et al., 2017) supports an extracellular function at the host-parasite interface.

This is consistent with the DCG-04 activity-probe experiments demonstrating that recSmolstatin1 inhibits both endogenous as well as host cysteine cathepsins, when exogenous inhibition from host proteases in fish liver could help protecting the presporogenic feeding stage of S. molnari (Korytář et al., 2019). The absence of disulfide bonds, common in many type 2 cystatins and some cystatin-like stefins (Buša et al., 2023; Robinson and Bulleid, 2020; Turk et al., 2019), leaves open the question whether Smolstatin1 primarily acts as a surface-tethered inhibitor, as proposed for some other parasite stefins (Gregory and Maizels, 2008) or can also function as a secreted protein with immune-related roles.

Several secreted cystatins and cystatin-like stefins have immunomodulatory properties and are being explored as therapeutic agents for inflammatory diseases (Smallwood et al., 2017). Smolstatin1 may fulfil a similar role at the S. molnari–carp interface, potentially contributing to modulation of host responses during parasite establishment or persistence. However, although fish immunization with recSmolstatin1 induced robust ELISA-detectable antibody responses, it did not translate into a significant reduction in parasite burden and resulted only in a potential transient delay in the onset of parasitemia. These findings indicate, that recSmolstatin1 alone is insufficient to confer protective immunity in carp under the conditions tested. One possible explanation is that the role of Smolstatin1 may be restricted to a specific developmental window, limiting the efficacy of vaccine-induced responses against other parasite stages. The modest delay in infection kinetics nevertheless suggests that Smolstatin1 may contribute to early host–parasite interactions or stage-specific developmental processes, which warrants further investigation. Future studies should determine whether Smolstatin1 acts primarily as an immunomodulatory molecule and whether it could contribute more effectively to protection as part of a multicomponent vaccine rather than as a standalone antigen.

We applied a recently established RNAi-mediated knockdown workflow in myxozoans (Kyslík et al., 2024) to directly test the function of Smolstatin1 in SMBS. The need to combine both dsRNA 5’ and 3’ fragments most likely reflects a threshold effect in RNAi efficiency rather than a specific positional requirement. Since neither fragment alone produced measurable silencing, each individual dsRNA was likely insufficient to generate an effective pool of siRNAs or to achieve adequate cellular uptake and processing. In contrast, the combined treatment probably increased the abundance and diversity of effective siRNAs, thereby enabling productive silencing. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies showing that RNAi efficacy depends on dsRNA uptake and on parameters such as dsRNA size, dose, and notably, size, along with effective siRNA yield (Saleh et al., 2006; Miller et al., 2012). Because this RNAi system is transient and most robust within the first 48 h (Kyslík et al., 2024), the observed post-silencing phenotype is best interpreted as an immediate effect on parasite condition under short-term in vitro handling rather than as a long-term developmental outcome. In this context, the marked reduction in motility and the altered parasite morphology after smolstatin1 knockdown indicate compromised parasite fitness and integrity. This interpretation is biologically plausible because motility is a defining functional feature of viable SMBS, and perturbation of motility-linked machinery has previously been associated with clear phenotypic consequences in this parasite model (Hartigan et al., 2016; Kyslík et al., 2024). This phenotype may reflect disruption of protease-inhibitor balance required for cellular homeostasis, structural maintenance, and development, suggesting an essential role in parasite survival. In parasites, cysteine proteases are involved in essential processes such as host interaction, tissue invasion, nutrient acquisition, and developmental progression (Sajid and McKerrow, 2002), while their endogenous inhibitors are increasingly recognized as important regulators of these pathways rather than passive antagonists alone (Gregory and Maizels, 2008; Turk et al., 2019; Roy et al., 2022). Although direct functional data for cystatin-like inhibitors in myxozoans are still lacking, studies in other parasitic eukaryotes support the view that endogenous cysteine protease inhibitors can influence parasite motility, egress, and developmental success. In Plasmodium berghei, an inhibitor of cysteine proteases was shown to be critical for sporozoite motility and infectivity, and also essential for exo-erythrocytic development (Boysen and Matuschewski, 2013). These parallels do not demonstrate mechanistic equivalence in myxozoans, but they support the idea that disruption of endogenous protease control can produce broad defects in parasite performance. In our experiments, the upregulation of smolstatin2 following smolstatin1 silencing further suggests a compensatory regulation among related cystatin-like inhibitors. Such compensation is commonly observed following silencing of gene family members (Liu et al., 2024) and may reflect functional redundancy that stabilizes protease control under stress. However, because the smolstatin2 transcriptional response did not prevent the observed phenotype, the two paralogues are unlikely to be fully interchangeable and may instead show only partial functional redundancy. Taken together, our data suggest a role for Smolstatin1 in maintenance of parasite fitness, motility-associated physiology, and cellular integrity. At the same time, because viability was not directly quantified, these results should be interpreted cautiously as evidence of impaired parasite condition rather than proven loss of viability. Future work should determine whether Smolstatins directly regulate parasite-encoded cysteine proteases, whether S. molnari smolstatins differ in inhibitory specificity, and whether their disruption also affects infectivity or developmental progression in vivo.

Crystallographic analysis revealed that recSmolstatin1 forms a domain-swapped dimer composed of two identical monomers, an architecture unique among known cystatin-like stefins and distinct from the monomeric FhCyLS-2 of Fasciola hepatica (PDB: 6I1M), the only crystallized cystatin-like stefin to date (Buša et al., 2023). Dimerization is common in proteins with flexible conformations (Marianayagam et al., 2004) and has been documented in human stefins and type 2 cystatins (Janowski et al., 2001; Jerala and Zerovnik, 1999; Nandwani et al., 2019; Staniforth et al., 2001). It involves the conserved Gln-Xxx-Val-Xxx-Gly motif (Nandwani et al., 2019) and typically occurs at low pH (Ekiel and Abrahamson, 1996; Janowski et al., 2001; Staniforth et al., 2001) as well as high protein concentration (Ekiel and Abrahamson, 1996; Janowski et al., 2001; Staniforth et al., 2001). Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that the high protein concentration used during crystallization promoted or stabilized formation of the Smolstatin1 dimer. However, because recSmolstatin1 was present in both monomeric and dimeric forms during purification, the crystallographic dimer likely reflects an intrinsic structural propensity rather than a purely crystallization-induced artifact. While dimerization can disrupt the L1 loop and abolish inhibitory activity (Jerala and Zerovnik, 1999; Nandwani et al., 2019), some domain-swapped dimers remain functional (Taler-Verčič et al., 2014). In humans, dimerization can lead to amyloid formation (Janowski et al., 2001; Staniforth et al., 2001), but it may also serve non-pathological regulatory roles (Marianayagam et al., 2004). Specifically, cystatin dimerization and inactivation may regulate lysosomal proteases under acidic conditions (Ekiel and Abrahamson, 1996). The behavior of recSmolstatin1 during chromatographic analyses indicates the presence of a dynamic equilibrium between monomeric and dimeric states, or alternatively, monomer generation during the sample denaturation. The physiological relevance of these states remains to be clarified, and future crystallographic studies of recSmolstatin1 in complex with target proteases will be key to resolving their structural basis and inhibitory mechanisms of this protein.

Comparative analysis of recSmolstatin1′s 3D structure with human stefin A (PDB ID: 1N9J; Staniforth et al., 2001) and stefin B (PDB IDs: 2OCT; Kokalj et al., 2007) revealed a conserved overall fold but notable structural divergence, reflected by high RMSD values, largely due to primary sequence mismatches between canonical and cystatin-like stefins (Fig. 10D; Bartošová-Sojková et al., 2021; Buša et al., 2023). Structural differences of Smolstatin1 and human stefin may also arise from organismal origin, methodological approaches (X-ray vs. NMR), or the absence of features such as the β-sheet bend in stefin B, which enables tetramerization via domain-swapped dimers and loop interlinking. Notably, Smolstatin1 lacks the conserved Pro (Pro74) in the Leu-Pro motif critical for this process, and is replaced by Gly, consistent with the absence of tetramer formation (this study). Unlike well-characterized human stefins, structurally resolved cystatin-like stefins are scarce and only available as in silico monomer predictions in myxozoans (Alama-Bermejo et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2020). In fact, the monomeric fluke FhCyLS remains the only crystallized example to date (Buša et al., 2023).

Conclusion

This is the first comprehensive functional and structural characterization of a cystatin-like stefin in myxozoans, an early-branching animal lineage. Smolstatin1 shows a potent inhibitory activity, especially against cathepsin L-like proteases, of both host and parasite origin. Despite its ancient origin, it shares sequence and functional features with homologues in other parasitic taxa, however, it also shows a unique domain-swapped dimeric architecture. Its expression and localization patterns support key roles in protease regulation during parasite development. Ongoing investigations aim to define its physiological functions in greater detail, particularly regarding its involvement in host-parasite interactions, with implications for evolutionary biology, protein engineering, and protease modulator design in biotechnology and medicine.
