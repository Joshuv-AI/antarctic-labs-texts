Real-world quality of life and healthcare resource utilization in patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis – Circular definition of CVRM comorbidities undermines the validity of core subgroup comparisons
Authors: Yajie Zhuang, Ying Shen
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We read with interest the real-world study by Marquardt and colleagues on health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and healthcare resource utilization (HCRU) in patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH).1 We write to highlight a methodological issue in the core subgroup stratification that, in our view, substantially affects the interpretation of the study’s primary conclusions: the circular use of cardiometabolic risk factors that are required for MASH diagnosis as “additional CVRM comorbidities.” This distinction is not addressed in the paper’s discussion or limitations section, yet it has important implications for how clinicians and payers interpret disease burden in MASH.

The study appropriately bases its MASH diagnostic framework on the 2024 EASL–EASD–EASO Clinical Practice Guidelines, which explicitly require the presence of at least one cardiometabolic risk factor (including overweight/obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, or type 2 diabetes) to establish the diagnosis of MASLD/MASH.2 However, the authors then classify these same baseline diagnostic risk factors as “additional CVRM comorbidities” when defining their three analytical subgroups: early fibrosis without additional CVRM comorbidities (EFnoC), early fibrosis with additional CVRM comorbidities (EFwC), and advanced fibrosis with additional CVRM comorbidities (AFwC).

This approach creates an inescapable circular logic. By definition, the EFnoC group is restricted to patients possessing exactly one qualifying cardiometabolic risk factor (the minimum required for MASH diagnosis), whereas the EFwC and AFwC groups consist of patients with two or more of these same risk factors. Consequently, the primary distinction between the subgroups is not the presence or absence of unrelated “additional” comorbidities, but rather a gradient in baseline metabolic risk severity inherent to the diagnosis of MASH itself. Despite this, the authors attribute all observed differences in EQ-5D-5L scores, Work Productivity and Activity Impairment (WPAI) domains, and HCRU outcomes primarily to comorbidity status and fibrosis stage.

Compounding this definitional issue is the marked imbalance in subgroup sizes. The EFnoC group comprises only 170 patients in the physician-reported cohort, with just 48 patients providing self-reported HRQoL data. In contrast, the EFwC group includes 1,575 patients, and the AFwC group includes 478 patients. Such extreme imbalance, when combined with the circular subgroup definition, highlights the challenges of interpreting findings derived from highly unbalanced subgroups in MASH research.3

This methodological consideration has tangible consequences for clinical and scientific interpretation. The original paper’s core message positions fibrosis progression and “additional” CVRM comorbidities as largely independent drivers of humanistic and economic burden in MASH. However, the observed differences in HRQoL and HCRU may instead largely reflect varying degrees of baseline metabolic risk that are fundamental to the disease definition. Prior work has shown that careful consideration of cardiometabolic risk factors and comorbidities is critical when interpreting disease burden and clinical outcomes in MASLD/MASH.4

To address this issue and fully realize the value of this large, multicenter real-world dataset, we suggest several targeted and readily implementable revisions that require no new data collection. First, the authors could redefine “additional CVRM comorbidities” by excluding the cardiometabolic risk factors required for MASH diagnosis, restricting this category exclusively to confirmed clinical cardiovascular or renal events (for example, congestive heart failure, stroke, chronic kidney disease stages 3–5, or myocardial infarction). Second, they could re-run all primary between-group comparisons using the revised subgroup definitions while retaining their existing entropy-balancing framework for age and sex. These re-analyses should be accompanied by caution regarding the limited statistical power inherent to small subgroup analyses.5

Finally, the authors could update the discussion and conclusions sections to explicitly separate the effects attributable to the baseline MASH metabolic risk gradient from those of truly additional clinical comorbidities. Such clarification would enable readers, including clinicians and healthcare payers, to more accurately identify the true drivers of HRQoL impairment and HCRU in this important cohort. Additional sensitivity analyses restricted to patients meeting stricter comorbidity definitions would further strengthen the robustness of the findings.

This study by Marquardt and colleagues represents a valuable contribution, providing one of the largest real-world multicenter datasets on patient-reported outcomes and resource utilization in MASH across Canada and three major European countries. The suggested revisions are modest in scope yet hold the potential to meaningfully enhance the interpretability, clinical utility, and long-term impact of this rich dataset for the hepatology community. We thank the authors for their important work and look forward to their response.
