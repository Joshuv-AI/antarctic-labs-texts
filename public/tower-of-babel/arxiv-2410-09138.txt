Resolution of an Erdős’ problem on least common multiples
Stijn Cambie

Abstract
Erdős posed the question whether there exist infinitely many sets of consecutive numbers whose least common multiple ( lcm \lcm ) exceeds the lcm \lcm of another, larger set with greater consecutive numbers. In this paper, we answer this question affirmatively by proving that the ratio of the lcm \lcm ’s can be made arbitrarily large.

47
years ago

Introduction

In his
1979
paper (based on remarks from a conference in October 1977), Erdős
[
2
]
posed several questions and conjectures in number theory, which he considered as unconventional.
We focus on one such simple posed question, for which the answer seemed frustratingly elusive at that time.

The least common multiple (
\lcm
) of a set of numbers is the smallest positive integer divisible by each number in the set (and thus divides their product).
The
\lcm
of a set with larger values or greater cardinality is typically larger than the
\lcm
of a set with smaller values or cardinality. However, this is not necessarily true for arbitrary sets.
Therefore, Erdős considered the
\lcm
of consecutive numbers.
For sets of two consecutive natural numbers, their
\lcm
is larger if their values are larger, since
x<y\Rightarrow\lcm(x,x+1)=x(x+1)<y(y+1)=\lcm(y,y+1).

In contrast, for
k\geq 3
consecutive natural numbers, this is not true.
For example when
x
is even and
y>x
is odd and not much larger than
x
,
we may have that
\lcm(x,x+1,x+2)=x(x+1)(x+2)>\frac{y(y+1)(y+2)}{2}=\lcm(y,y+1,y+2).

The non-monotonicity can be extreme.
Let
f(n)=\lcm\{n,n+1,\ldots,n+6\}
. Then
f(1)\leq f(2)\leq\ldots\leq f(7)\leq f(8)>f(9),
and also
f(7)=2^{3}\cdot 3^{2}\cdot 5\cdot 7\cdot 11\cdot 13>f(10)=2^{4}\cdot 3\cdot 5\cdot 7\cdot 11\cdot 13
. This is a counterexample to behaviour that seemed plausible to Erdős.

When comparing the
\lcm
of
k
small consecutive integers with the
\lcm
of
\ell>k
larger consecutive integers, determining which is larger becomes harder.
For example, one might wonder which of the following two quantities is larger:

\lcm\{676,677,\ldots,773\}\mbox{ and }\lcm\{798,799,\ldots,903\}.

Some minimal examples, with a cardinality of
7
and smallest elements
37
and
48
, respectively, illustrating the unexpected inequality are:

\lcm\{53,54,55,56,57,58,59\}>\lcm\{63,64,65,66,67,68,69,70\}\mbox{ and }

\lcm\{37,38,39,40,41,42,43,44\}>\lcm\{48,49,50,51,52,53,54,55,56\}.

Erdős
[
2
]
posed the question whether there exist infinitely many cases in which the
\lcm
of the larger such set is smaller than the
\lcm
of the smaller set.
This question is also listed as problem
678
at
https://www.erdosproblems.com/678
.
We answer the question by giving an elementary proof for the following stronger result in
Section

2
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
C\geq 1
be a constant.
For every
k
sufficiently large, there exist integers
0<x<y
with
y>x+k
such that

\lcm\{x,x+1,\ldots,x+k-1\}>C\cdot\lcm\{y,y+1,\ldots,y+k\}.

We conjecture that an even stronger form of this result is true.

Conjecture 2
.

Let
C\geq 1
be an arbitrary constant.
Then there exist integers
k
and
0<x<y
with
y>x+k
such that

\lcm\{x,x+1,\ldots,x+k-1\}>\lcm\{y,y+1,\ldots,y+k+C-1\}.

We note that a proof for
Conjecture

2
along the same lines as the proof in
Section

2
would follow from density results for solutions from the Chinese remainder theorem (which even only need to hold for some values of
k
). This reduction is proven in Subsection
2.1
.

Question 3
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be fixed.
Then there exists
0<\delta<\varepsilon
, such that for all sufficiently large
k
the following is true.

Let the primes strictly between
\sqrt{k}
and
k
be written as
\sqrt{k}<p_{1}\leq\ldots\leq p_{r}<k
. Let
M=\prod_{j=1}^{r}p_{j}
.
For every
1\leq j\leq r
,
let
I_{j}=\left\{1,2,\ldots,\left\lceil p_{j}^{1-\delta}\right\rceil\right\}
.
Then among every
\left\lceil M^{\varepsilon}\right\rceil
consecutive integers,
there is at least one value
x
for which

x\bmod p_{j}
belongs to
I_{j}
for every
1\leq j\leq r
.

The state-of-the-art to solve this type of problem seems to be sieve-type bounds, but no contacted expert could tell it was possible to prove.
The possible non-trivial nature of this (or a related) desired density result can be guessed from studies on the distribution
[
3
]
and equidistribution
[
4
]
of the outcomes from the Chinese remainder theorem.

Proof of
Theorem

1

We start with two conventions. We will assume that
k
is taken sufficiently large such that all statements work, and will use
a\mod n
for the unique integer between
0
and
n-1
which has the same residue modulo
n
as
a.

Let
M=\lcm\{1,2,\ldots,k\}=\prod_{p\leq k}p^{\left\lfloor\log_{p}(k)\right\rfloor}
be the product of all maximal prime powers bounded by
k.
Using the notation
m=\prod_{p\leq\sqrt{k}}p^{\left\lfloor\log_{p}(k)\right\rfloor}
, we can also write
M=m\cdot\prod_{\sqrt{k}<p\leq k}p
.

Let
A
and
B
be the sets of vectors
\vec{a}=(a_{p}\mid\sqrt{k}<p\leq k)
and
\vec{b}=(b_{p}\mid\sqrt{k}<p\leq k)
, whose coordinates satisfy
1\leq a_{p}\leq p-(k\mod p)
and
p-(k\mod p)\leq b_{p}\leq p
for every prime
k\geq p>\sqrt{k}
respectively.

For every
\vec{a}\in A
and
\vec{b}\in B
, we consider the unique solutions
0\leq x_{\vec{a}},y_{\vec{b}}<M
satisfying the following systems of modular equations

x_{\vec{a}}\equiv\begin{cases}1\pmod{m}\\
a_{p}\pmod{p}\mbox{ for every }\sqrt{k}<p\leq k\end{cases}\mbox{ and }y_{\vec{b}}\equiv\begin{cases}0\pmod{m}\\
b_{p}\pmod{p}\mbox{ for every }\sqrt{k}<p\leq k.\end{cases}

The existence and uniqueness is known by the Chinese remainder Theorem.

We first prove the following claim, which says that among all linear combinations with sufficiently many choices for the coefficients, there are not too many consecutive numbers excluded.

Claim 4
.

Let
p_{1}<p_{2}<\ldots<p_{r}
be primes and
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{r}
be integers, such that the combinations
\sum_{i}c_{i}w_{i}
over all possible combinations with
0<c_{i}\leq p_{i}
lead to all residues modulo
P=p_{1}p_{2}\ldots p_{r}.
Let
B_{i}\subset[p_{i}]
be a set of size at least
(1-\varepsilon)p_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq r
.
If
\varepsilon(p_{1}+p_{2}+\ldots+p_{r})<n\leq p_{1},
among every
n
consecutive integers there is at least one which equals
\sum_{i}c_{i}w_{i}
modulo
P
where
c_{i}\in B_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq r
.

Proof.

By the condition, there is a unique solution to
\sum c_{i}w_{i}\equiv 1\pmod{P}
,
which implies for each of the
n
consecutive integers
z
,

z\equiv\sum(zc_{i})w_{i}\pmod{P}
.
Among the
n
numbers of the form
zc_{i}\mod p_{i},
at most
\varepsilon p_{i}
are not in the interval
B_{i}.

Thus at most
\varepsilon(p_{1}+p_{2}+\ldots+p_{r})<n
combinations are not of the desired form, from which the conclusion trivially follows.
∎

By the density of primes, see e.g.
[
1
]
, we know that for fixed tiny
\varepsilon>0
, there are many primes in the range
(k/2,(1+\varepsilon)k/2)
and
((1-\varepsilon)k,k)
.
We will use this to find suitable
x_{\vec{a}}
and
y_{\vec{b}}.

Choose two primes
p_{1},p_{2}\in(k/2,(1+\varepsilon)k/2).

Let
B_{1}=[2p_{1}-k,p_{1}]=[p_{1}-(k\mod{p_{1}}),p_{1}]
and
B_{2}=[2p_{2}-k,p_{2}]
.

Let
B^{\prime}=\{b_{p}=0\ \forall p\not\in\{p_{1},p_{2}\},b_{p_{1}}\in B_{1},b_{p_{2}}\in B_{2}\}.

Note that the vector
\left\{y_{\vec{b}}\mid\vec{b}\in B^{\prime}\right\}
differ by multiples of
\frac{M}{p_{1}}
and
\frac{M}{p_{2}}.

By
Claim

4
, for every
(\varepsilon(p_{1}+p_{2}))+1)\frac{M}{p_{1}p_{2}}<\frac{M}{k}
consecutive integers, at least one can be expressed as
y_{\vec{b}}
(for some
\vec{b}\in B^{\prime})
.
This implies that there is a solution
y:=y_{\vec{b}}
satisfying
\frac{M}{5C}(1+1/k)<y<\frac{M}{4C}-k
.

By
Claim

4
applied to
3
primes in
((1-\varepsilon)k,k)
, similarly as how
y
was derived, we can choose a value
x:=x_{\vec{a}}
such that
x<y<x+\frac{M}{5Ck}.

Note that
y-x\geq m-1>k
by the choice of their residues modulo
m.

Claim 5
.

\frac{y\cdot(y+1)\cdot(y+2)\cdot\ldots\cdot(y+k)}{\lcm\{y,y+1,\ldots,y+k\}}=M\cdot\frac{x\cdot(x+1)\cdot\ldots\cdot(x+k-1)}{\lcm\{x,x+1,\ldots,x+k-1\}}

Proof.

We will conclude that the left hand side equals the right hand side,
LHS=RHS,
by proving that for every prime
p
,
v_{p}(LHS)=v_{p}(RHS)
.

For any prime
p\leq\sqrt{k},
we note that in
k
or
k+1
consecutive numbers at most one element can be a multiple of
p^{\left\lfloor\log_{p}(k)\right\rfloor+1},
in which cases also the least common multiple of all these numbers will have the same
p
-adic value as this element.
Taking into account that
x\equiv y+1\equiv 1\pmod{m}
, this implies that

\displaystyle v_{p}(LHS)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=y}^{y+k}\min\left(v_{p}(i),\left\lfloor\log_{p}(k)\right\rfloor\right)-\left\lfloor\log_{p}(k)\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=y+1}^{y+k}\min\left(v_{p}(i),\left\lfloor\log_{p}(k)\right\rfloor\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=x}^{x+k-1}\min\left(v_{p}(i),\left\lfloor\log_{p}(k)\right\rfloor\right)

\displaystyle=v_{p}(RHS).

The last inequality holds due to
v_{p}(M)=\left\lfloor\log_{p}(k)\right\rfloor.

For every prime
\sqrt{k}<p\leq k
, both sides contain at most one multiple of
p^{2}
, and so it is sufficient to count the number of multiples of
p
in the numerators to conclude
v_{p}(LHS)=v_{p}(RHS).

The latter being true by the choice of
x\mod p=a_{p},y\mod p=b_{p}
.

Primes
p>k,
appear at most once among
k
or
k+1
consecutive numbers, and so these primes satisfy
v_{p}(LHS)=v_{p}(RHS)=0.

∎

Finishing the proof is now done by the following computation.

\displaystyle\frac{\lcm\{x,x+1,\ldots,x+k-1\}}{\lcm\{y,y+1,\ldots,y+k\}}

\displaystyle=M\cdot\frac{x\cdot(x+1)\cdot\ldots\cdot(x+k-1)}{y\cdot(y+1)\cdot(y+2)\cdot\ldots\cdot(y+k)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{M}{y+k}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle>4C\left(\frac{k}{k+1}\right)^{k}>C.

Question

3
implies
Conjecture

Here we assume
Question

3
has a positive answer, and will deduce that in that case also
Conjecture

2
is true.

Note that by the prime number theorem,
M=k^{\Theta(k/\log{k})}
.
The average size of values
x
satisfying the condition stated in
Question

3
among
M^{\varepsilon}
consecutive integers is at least
M^{\varepsilon-\delta}
, which is large for
k
large. One can e.g. assume that
(\varepsilon-\delta)k^{0.01}>\log{k}
. The latter indicating why
Question

3
should have a positive answer.

Proof of
Question

3

\Rightarrow

Conjecture

2
.

Take
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{3C+6}
and let
0<\delta<\varepsilon
be a constant which works for
Question

3
. Let
K
be a sufficiently large integer.
For every prime
\sqrt{K}<p\leq 2K
, there are at most

2p^{1-\delta}\left(\frac{K}{p}+1\right)\leq 6p^{-\delta}K\leq 6K^{1-\delta/2}
values
K\leq k<2K
for which
k\mod p>p-p^{1-\delta}
or
k\mod p<p^{1-\delta}.

By averaging, we know that there is a choice of
K\leq k<2K
for which a fraction no more than
7K^{-\delta/2}<\varepsilon
of the
r
primes
\sqrt{k}\leq p<k
satisfy
k\mod p>p-p^{1-\delta}
or
k\mod p<p^{1-\delta}.

Pick such a
k
, and let the
r
primes be
p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\ldots\leq p_{r}.

Let
x
and
y
be any solution for which

x\mod p_{j}
and
-y\mod p_{j}
both belong to
I_{j}
for every
1\leq j\leq r
.
So except from an
\varepsilon
fraction of the
r
primes these satisfy
x\mod p_{j}\leq p_{j}-(k\mod p_{j})
and
y\mod{p_{j}}\geq p_{j}-(k+C-1\mod p_{j}).

For those primes, we know that

v_{p}\left(\frac{y\cdot(y+1)\cdot(y+2)\cdot\ldots\cdot(y+k+C-1)}{\lcm\{y,y+1,\ldots,y+k+C-1\}}\right)\geq v_{p}\left(\frac{x\cdot(x+1)\cdot\ldots\cdot(x+k-1)}{\lcm\{x,x+1,\ldots,x+k-1\}}\right)+1.

Let
G
be the subset of the
r
primes satisfying the above.
Let
B
be the subset of primes for which it is not the case (but then it is still true with
+1
replaced by
-1
).
Note that
\prod_{p\in B}p\leq M^{2\varepsilon}
and
m=\prod_{p\leq\sqrt{k}}p^{\left\lfloor\log_{p}(k)\right\rfloor}<M^{\varepsilon}.

As such, we have
\frac{\prod_{p\in G}p}{m\prod_{p\in B}p}\geq M^{1-5\varepsilon}.

We conclude that (instead of
Claim

5
)

\frac{y\cdot(y+1)\cdot(y+2)\cdot\ldots\cdot(y+k+C-1)}{\lcm\{y,y+1,\ldots,y+k+C-1\}}>M^{1-5\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{x\cdot(x+1)\cdot\ldots\cdot(x+k-1)}{\lcm\{x,x+1,\ldots,x+k-1\}}.

Now by
Question

3
, we can choose
x,y
such that
M^{\varepsilon}<y-x<3M^{\varepsilon}
and
M^{2\varepsilon}<x<y+k+C<M^{3\varepsilon}
.

The proof is now done by the following computation.

\displaystyle\frac{\lcm\{x,x+1,\ldots,x+k-1\}}{\lcm\{y,y+1,\ldots,y+k+C-1\}}

\displaystyle>M^{1-5\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{x\cdot(x+1)\cdot\ldots\cdot(x+k-1)}{y\cdot(y+1)\cdot(y+2)\cdot\ldots\cdot(y+k+C-1)}

\displaystyle>\frac{M^{1-5\varepsilon}}{(y+k+C)^{C}}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^{k}>1.

∎
