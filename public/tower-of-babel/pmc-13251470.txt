Massive bilateral pulmonary hydatid disease in a seronegative child: Thoracoscopic management of an unusual presentation
Authors: Raed Al-Taher, Abdallah Alabadla, Salameh Al-Halaseh, Leen AlZghoul, Yara Abu Salah, Salsabil Fakih
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulmonary hydatid disease is uncommon in children and may present diagnostic difficulties, particularly when serology is negative. We report a 10-year-old boy from an endemic rural area who was incidentally found to have three giant pulmonary cysts involving both lungs. Despite negative Echinococcus serology, radiologic features were highly suggestive of hydatid disease. The patient underwent staged bilateral thoracoscopic endocystectomy with capitonnage following albendazole therapy. Histopathology confirmed the diagnosis. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, with complete radiological resolution and no recurrence at three-year follow-up. This case highlights the limitations of serology in isolated pulmonary hydatid disease and supports thoracoscopic lung-preserving surgery as an effective treatment in children.

Introduction

Cystic echinococcosis is a zoonotic parasitic disease caused by the larval stage of Echinococcus granulosus and remains endemic in Mediterranean countries, the Middle East, Africa, and parts of Asia. Although the liver is the most frequently affected organ, pulmonary involvement is proportionally more common in children due to increased lung elasticity and compliance [1,2].

Diagnosis relies primarily on imaging, while serological tests serve as an adjunct. However, serologic sensitivity is significantly reduced in isolated pulmonary disease, especially when cysts remain intact, resulting in false-negative results in up to 40% of cases [3].

We present a rare pediatric case of giant bilateral pulmonary hydatid cysts with negative serology, successfully managed via staged thoracoscopic surgery.

Case presentation

A 10-year-old male from rural Jordan, known to have neurofibromatosis type 1 and idiopathic intracranial hypertension, was admitted for a scheduled lumbar puncture. Six days prior to admission, he experienced fever, abdominal pain, back pain, vomiting, and night sweats without seeking medical attention.

On admission, vital signs were stable with an oxygen saturation of 97% on room air. Physical examination revealed decreased air entry over the right hemithorax, with no abdominal tenderness or organomegaly.

Laboratory investigations demonstrated mild anemia (Hb 11.8 g/dL), thrombocytosis, mild neutrophilia, elevated CRP (90 mg/L), and ESR (75 mm/h). Liver and renal function tests were within normal limits.

Chest radiography revealed multiple bilateral pulmonary opacities with rib notching, mediastinal shift, and tracheobronchial compression (Fig. 1B). Contrast-enhanced chest CT demonstrated three large, well-defined cystic lesions: two in the right upper lobe (largest measuring 10.7 × 9 cm) and one in the anterior segment of the left lower lobe. Associated bronchiectasis and mild bilateral pleural effusion were noted (Fig. 1A). Abdominal ultrasound showed no hepatic involvement. Despite negative serology, multidisciplinary consensus favored pulmonary hydatid disease based on imaging and epidemiological context. Albendazole therapy was initiated (10 mg/kg/day), and staged thoracoscopic intervention was planned.

The patient underwent right-sided thoracoscopic endocystectomy. Trocar placement was carefully planned to avoid inadvertent rupture of the cysts upon entry into the thoracic cavity (Fig. 2A). Following aspiration of the cystic contents using a Veress needle, no scolicidal agent was instilled. Both cysts were completely evacuated, and identified bronchopulmonary fistulae were managed with capitonnage.Fig. 2(A) Patient positioning on right lateral decubitus at the time of first operation, where the site of 1st trocar insertion was planned (arrow) to avoid rupturing the lesions. The postoperative course was notable for mild subcutaneous emphysema that resolved with chest tube suction over a five-day period. After a seven-day interval, during which the patient remained clinically stable and suitable for further surgical intervention, the contralateral cyst was treated using the same thoracoscopic technique (Figs. 2B and 3). Histopathology confirmed hydatid disease, revealing scolices, laminated membranes, and inflammatory infiltrates.

At three-month follow-up, the patient was asymptomatic, chest radiography showed complete resolution, and serologic tests remained negative. At three-year follow-up, the patient was asymptomatic with near-normal chest radiography (Fig. 1C).

Discussion

Pulmonary hydatid disease remains a diagnostic challenge, particularly in pediatric patients with atypical presentations or negative serology. Sensitivity of Echinococcus IgG assays is significantly lower in isolated pulmonary disease, especially when cysts remain intact, as antigen exposure to the immune system is minimal [3].

Children may harbor disproportionately large cysts due to lung compliance, often presenting with compressive symptoms rather than classic respiratory complaints. In our case, constitutional and abdominal symptoms preceded respiratory findings, further obscuring the diagnosis.

The patient's underlying neurofibromatosis type 1 was considered incidental and unrelated to the development or course of hydatid disease.

Surgical excision is the treatment of choice for pulmonary hydatid disease. Lung-preserving techniques are strongly recommended in children. Thoracoscopic approaches offer reduced postoperative pain, faster recovery, and adequate visualization, even for large cysts, as demonstrated in this case [[4], [5], [6]]. Scolicidal agents were deliberately avoided due to the risk of chemical pneumonitis in the setting of bronchial communication and the thoracoscopic approach, a concern previously highlighted in pediatric series [4]. A staged approach was chosen to minimize anesthesia-related risks and allow adequate lung re-expansion between procedures.

Adjunctive albendazole therapy remains essential to reduce recurrence risk and manage unseen residual disease [7].

Conclusion

Pulmonary hydatid disease should remain a key differential diagnosis for pediatric lung cysts in endemic regions, regardless of serologic results. Thoracoscopic surgery represents a safe, effective, and lung-conserving treatment option for giant pediatric pulmonary hydatid cysts.
