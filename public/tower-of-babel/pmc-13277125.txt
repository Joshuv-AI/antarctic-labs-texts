A social cognitive perspective on teacher technology self-efficacy, inclusion, and student aspirations
Authors: Huang Lei, Fang Na, Liu Lu, Hassan Jawad Soomro, Ghulam Hussain Khan Zaigham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study examines how digital inclusion at the school level shapes the relationship between teacher technology-integration self-efficacy and students’ aspirations for upward mobility. Drawing on social cognitive theory, we propose that confident teachers create inclusive classroom environments that foster student engagement and long-term educational aspirations, and that these effects are strengthened when schools provide systemic support for digital technology. Data were collected through a three-wave design involving 292 higher-secondary schools in Punjab, Pakistan. Structural equation modeling and moderated mediation analysis revealed that teacher technology self-efficacy predicted stronger perceptions of classroom inclusion, which in turn enhanced students’ intent to pursue upward social mobility. School-level digital inclusion amplified this indirect effect, highlighting the combined role of teacher capacity and institutional infrastructure. The findings demonstrate how investments in digital support and teacher development can contribute to equitable and future-oriented educational outcomes, offering policy and practice implications for technology-enhanced education in diverse contexts.

Introduction

The spread of technology into classrooms has not followed a predictable or smooth pattern. In some higher-secondary schools, digital tools are embedded into daily instructions. In others, the use of technology feels peripheral or even absent [1]. What drives this inconsistency? A growing body of work suggests that infrastructure alone does not explain the variation [2, 3]. Instead, much depends on how comfortable and confident teachers feel when using technology for instruction [4]. It is not simply about having devices in the room but about the teacher’s sense of control over them [5, 6]. Teachers who believe they can use technology successfully are more inclined to experiment, adapt, and integrate digital tools into their teaching practices [4, 6]. A growing body of research confirms that teacher self-efficacy is a critical predictor of technology integration, influencing both the frequency and quality of technology use in classroom instructions [6]. Empirical studies further show that teachers with stronger self-efficacy beliefs demonstrate higher levels of technological pedagogical engagement and are more likely to translate digital competencies into effective teaching practices [7]. For example, teachers with higher technology self-efficacy are more likely to design interactive lessons using digital tools, such as incorporating multimedia presentations, real-time quizzes, and collaborative platforms that actively engage students [7]. They are also more inclined to personalize instruction by adapting digital resources to meet diverse student needs, including differentiated assignments and adaptive learning systems [8]. In contrast, teachers with lower confidence often rely on more traditional, lecture-based approaches and may avoid using technology altogether. The way belief connects to action has puzzled educational researchers for decades [9], yet evidence increasingly shows that self-efficacy translates into observable classroom practices that shape how students participate and learn. It keeps surfing in different forms. Bandura [10], in social cognitive theory (SCT) discourse, spoke of behavior not as something isolated, but as something entangled in a back-and-forth exchange between what people think, what they do, and the environments they move through. Bringing that to the school setting, it feels reasonable to say that a teacher’s sense of “I can handle this technology” acts as a kind of internal push. Without that belief, the first step toward actually using digital tools might never happen. Or it might happen and then stall. Teachers who perceive themselves as capable of integrating technology are more likely to engage in innovative teaching practices, shaping both classroom behaviors and student outcomes [4]. Through modeling, reinforcement, and social persuasion, teachers transmit beliefs about learning potential and inclusion [11, 12], directly affecting how students perceive their educational environment.

Existing research illustrates how digital technologies can function as both enablers and barriers to inclusion. Navas-Bonilla et al. [8] show that while assistive technologies, mobile devices, and augmented reality offer pathways to personalized and accessible learning, the benefits depend significantly on institutional readiness and educator preparedness. This underscores SCT’s assertion that the environment’s facilitative conditions can either strengthen or constrain the enactment of personal efficacy beliefs. However, most studies, e.g. [3, 4, 13], have examined either teacher technology practices or digital equity independently; few have considered how teacher self-efficacy interacts with environmental factors, such as school digital inclusion, to shape student experiences of classroom inclusivity [14, 15].

The concept of an inclusive climate, particularly within higher-secondary education, has been explored for its links to school well-being and positive student-teacher relationships [11, 16, 17]. Yet the literature remains largely silent on how teacher technology-integration self-efficacy may influence students’ perceptions of inclusion through the use of digital tools. Additionally, while upward mobility intent - a student’s belief in their ability to improve socioeconomic status through education - has been tied to classroom culture and teacher expectations [18], empirical exploration connecting this with digital inclusion and teacher self-efficacy is noticeably lacking. In this study, upward mobility intent is conceptualized as a future-oriented cognitive and motivational construct that reflects students’ beliefs, expectations, and aspirations regarding their ability to achieve higher socioeconomic status through educational attainment. It captures how students interpret educational opportunities as a pathway for social advancement within their broader social context [19].

This study addresses these gaps by proposing and assessing a novel conceptual model grounded in SCT. We hypothesize that teacher technology-integration self-efficacy influences students’ upward mobility intent through its impact on students’ perceptions of classroom inclusion, with school-level digital inclusion moderating this relationship. This interaction captures the essence of SCT’s reciprocal determinism: teacher behaviors influenced by personal efficacy interact with the broader school environment (digital infrastructure and support) to shape student cognitive appraisals and motivational outcomes.

The anticipated contributions of this research are threefold. Theoretically, it extends SCT’s application in educational settings by embedding constructs of digital inclusion and student upward mobility aspirations, areas rarely integrated in prior models. Practically, the study offers valuable insights for school leaders and policymakers seeking to design interventions aimed at strengthening teacher capacity for technology use and enhancing inclusive teaching practices. Such strategies are essential to foster equitable educational environments that promote all students’ aspirations for academic and social advancement. Policy-wise, the findings reinforce the argument for comprehensive digital inclusion initiatives that simultaneously target teacher development and systemic school support.

As education systems worldwide continue to adapt to the demands of the digital era, understanding how personal agency, environmental resources, and student beliefs interact becomes increasingly critical. By anchoring this inquiry within SCT, this study endeavors to deliver empirically grounded insights into how technology integration and inclusion practices can be leveraged to empower both teachers and students within the complex ecosystem of higher-secondary education. Theoretical background and hypothesis development

Social cognitive theory

Social cognitive theory (SCT) emerged as a deliberate response to the limits of behaviorist thinking. Early models of learning focused heavily on observable responses to stimuli, overlooking how internal processes guide behavior [20]. Bandura [21] challenged this, proposing that behavior results not from single causes, but from a continuous interaction between personal beliefs, external factors, and behavioral choices, i.e., the dynamic he called reciprocal determinism. Within that framework, the idea of self-efficacy became central. It refers to people’s assessment of whether they can organize and perform the actions needed to achieve specific objectives, even when circumstances are difficult or unpredictable [22].

SCT offers useful lens for understanding teacher behavior in technology integration. Research consistently shows that when teachers feel confident in their ability to use digital technologies effectively, they are more likely to experiment with, adopt, and continue using these tools [7]. This aligns with SCT’s position that self-belief strongly influences behavior. However, self-efficacy alone does not determine outcomes. Environmental enablers such as institutional support and infrastructure shape whether intentions translate into action.

Our research model extends SCT by exploring not only teacher behavior but also how it indirectly shapes student experience. Specifically, the model proposes that teacher technology-integration self-efficacy promotes a classroom climate that students perceive as inclusive. Previous work has shown this factor to be strongly associated with student engagement and well-being [11]. This perception may, in turn, influence students’ own beliefs about their capacity to succeed, shaping their upward mobility intentions, i.e., understood as aspirations to improve their future socioeconomic status through education [18]. Finally, our framework considers digital inclusion at the school level as a contextual factor. Schools differ in the availability of technology resources and teacher support structures [8]. This variability may strengthen or weaken the influence of teacher self-efficacy, demonstrating SCT’s emphasis on the interplay between personal agency and environmental opportunity.

Teacher technology-integration self-efficacy and inclusion climate

Teachers’ confidence in their ability to use digital technologies for instruction does not exist in isolation, it shapes classroom environments in ways that students experience daily [23]. SCT suggests that individuals who believe strongly in their competence tend to approach tasks with more determination and persistence, even in situations that present uncertainty or limited resources [22]. In the education sector, this principal manifests through teacher self-efficacy. Educators who perceive themselves as capable of integrating technology are not only more likely to attempt it, but they are also more likely to create supportive learning contexts that encourage student engagement and collaboration [7].

This influence extends beyond the use of devices or applications. It affects how students perceive the atmosphere of the classroom itself. When teachers exhibit confidence in managing and applying technology, it often translates into smoother lesson delivery, clearer expectations, and more equitable participation opportunities [4]. Students respond to these cues. They are likely to experience the classroom as a more inclusive and responsive environment, one that values their presence and contributions. Previous research confirms that perceptions of inclusion are strongly shaped by the behaviors and expectations modeled by teachers [2, 11, 24]. The SCT interprets this as the environmental component of reciprocal determinism. Teacher behavior, specifically confidence with technology, interacts closely with student perceptions of classroom inclusion and may also influence future student behaviors such as engagement and participation [25]. This interplay reflects what Bandura [20] described as reciprocal determinism, where teacher actions and student responses continuously affect each other. It follows from this line of reasoning that teachers with greater technology-integration self-efficacy are likely to cultivate classroom environments that students perceive as more inclusive. Inclusion climate and upward mobility intent

Students’ perceptions of classroom inclusion may not only influence how they experience school but also shape how they imagine their future possibilities [26]. SCT proposes that environmental factors interact with personal beliefs to guide motivation and behavior [21]. In educational settings, when students perceive classrooms as inclusive, i.e., feeling supported, valued, and fairly treated, they are more likely to believe that success is attainable through their own efforts. That belief connects directly to self-efficacy, the perception of personal capability to achieve intended outcomes despite challenges [27].

Research supports this link. Schwab [11] found that students who view their school climate as inclusive report greater well-being and engagement. Engagement, in turn, has been associated with future-oriented aspirations, particularly for students navigating systems where barriers to upward mobility exist. Students who perceive themselves as active participants in supportive learning environments often internalize higher expectations for their own academic and life outcomes [18].

Following the logic of SCT, an inclusive classroom environment functions as a social structure that reinforces positive beliefs about personal competence. Students exposed to such environments are more likely to envision themselves as capable of achieving upward social mobility through education. Based on this theoretical and empirical foundation, we expect a positive relationship between students’ perception of classroom inclusion and their intent to improve their future socioeconomic standing through academic achievement.H2. Teacher technology-integration self-efficacy and upward mobility intent

Confidence is rarely a private state in teaching; it shapes how classrooms function and how students experience learning [13]. SCT describes human action as emerging from the constant interaction of personal beliefs, behaviors, and environmental influences [21]. Teacher technology-integration self-efficacy, defined as the belief in one’s capability to organize and execute technology-driven instruction, represents a personal factor that SCT identifies as essential for initiating behavior [22].

Research indicates that teachers who are confident in their ability to use technology are more likely to create supportive and inclusive learning environments [2]. These environments are not neutral. Students perceive classrooms where digital tools are used effectively and fairly as more inclusive spaces, where participation and respect for diversity are encouraged. Such perceptions matter, as they contribute to students’ broader beliefs about whether their educational experience can translate into personal advancement.

When viewed through the lens of SCT, this relationship can be conceptualized as a mediated pathway. Teachers’ technology-integration self-efficacy represents a personal belief that leads to behavioral change in classroom practice. These behaviors shape students’ perceptions of classroom inclusion, which in turn influence their beliefs about future advancement. Matamoros-Lima et al. [18] showed how such beliefs about mobility intentions develop when students feel empowered by their school experience. Moderating role of digital inclusion

SCT holds that personal beliefs and behaviors are shaped not only by individual agency but also by the broader environment in which actions take place [21]. Even when teachers feel confident about their capacity to integrate technology into their instruction, whether that intention is fully realized often depends on conditions external to the teacher. Bandura [22] described this as the influence of environmental enablers or constraints on behavioral enactment.

Recent work suggests that school-level factors can either reinforce or limit a teacher’s application of digital tools. Zheng et al. [7] found that teacher confidence positively predicts technology use, yet the presence of supportive infrastructure and institutional backing strongly affects the extent of that use. Environments that actively support technology integration, through both access and training, create circumstances where teachers can more easily translate personal belief into classroom practice. In the context of this study, school-level digital inclusion is proposed to moderate the relationship between teacher technology-integration self-efficacy and classroom inclusion. Where resources and support are strong, teachers with high self-efficacy are more likely to foster inclusive environments. In contrast, when digital inclusion is low, the positive effect of teacher self-efficacy on classroom inclusion is likely to weaken. This reflects SCT’s view that personal and environmental factors jointly shape outcomes. Based on this reasoning, the following hypothesis is proposed.H4. School-level digital inclusion positively moderates the relationship between teacher technology-integration self-efficacy and classroom inclusion, such that the relationship is stronger at higher levels of digital inclusion.

SCT argues that human behavior is influenced by a dynamic interplay between personal agency and environmental conditions [21]. In classrooms, a teacher’s belief in their ability to successfully integrate technology often sets the foundation for behavior, but that belief alone does not guarantee student outcomes. Bandura [22] proposed that external factors, including access to resources and environmental support, can either strengthen or limit the translation of intentions into results.

Evidence suggests that teacher technology-integration self-efficacy can enhance students’ perceptions of classroom inclusion, which in turn contributes to student beliefs about their capacity to pursue upward social mobility through education [11, 18]. However, the relationship between teacher beliefs and student perceptions does not operate in a vacuum. Zeng et al. [7] showed that institutional support plays a critical role in determining whether teacher confidence leads to actual changes in classroom practice. In the context of this study, it is proposed that digital inclusion as school-level support for technology use acts as a moderating factor in this mediated process. When schools provide strong infrastructure and encouragement for technology use, teachers with high self-efficacy are more likely to foster inclusive environments, and this strengthens the pathway toward student upward mobility intent. Where such support is weak, the influence of teacher self-efficacy on student outcomes may be reduced. This perspective aligns closely with SCT’s view that environmental opportunities interact with personal beliefs and behaviors to shape complex outcome chains.H5. Methods

Measures

To ensure measurement validity and maintain study rigor, this research used pre-validated scales. Teacher technology-integration self-efficacy was assessed using a six-item instrument first developed by Bandura [10] and later refined and validated by Masry-Herzalah and Dor-Haim [3]. A representative item from this scale states, “I always succeed in handling new technologies, including AI.” The perception of classroom inclusion was measured through two dimensions: teacher support and care, and emotional experience. This construct was captured by a fourteen-item scale developed by Schwab et al. [11]. One of the items asks students to reflect on whether “My teachers make sure that students, who face difficulty in learning a subject, receive enough support and guidance.” To assess students’ upward mobility intent, the study applied the upward social mobility beliefs 4-item scale designed by Matamoros-Limas et al. [18]. A sample item from this scale reads, “In this class, children often achieve a higher socioeconomic status than the household in which they grew up.” Finally, digital inclusion was operationalized using a six-item subscale focusing on school-level support for technology integration, drawn from Kim et al. [1]. An example item from this measure is, “Teachers are provided with resources for technology use.”

Data collection

The data was collected from 292 higher-secondary school in the province of Punjab, Pakistan. Schools were approached using a purposive sampling strategy, focusing on institutions that had implemented some level of digital infrastructure or technology-supported teaching practices. This approach ensured that respondents had relevant exposure to the constructs under investigation. To enhance representativeness, efforts were made to include schools from different geographic areas within Punjab, including both urban and semi-urban settings, and to capture diversity in teacher demographics such as gender and teaching experience. The final sample reflected a balanced composition, with 44% male and 56% female respondents. The reliance on the region was made due to multiple reasons. Punjab offers an unusually strong context for exploring how teacher self-efficacy with technology connects to student aspirations. In the last decade, provincial authorities introduced a variety of programs aimed at upgrading school infrastructure and teaching capacity. These initiatives included structured teacher training programs in ICT and digital pedagogy, often delivered through workshops and blended learning formats to enhance teachers’ instructional capacity and confidence in using technology [28]. In parallel, resource-based interventions focused on expanding access to digital infrastructure, such as the establishment of computer laboratories, provision of internet connectivity, and introduction of smart classroom technologies across public schools. More recent efforts have also emphasized integrating emerging technologies into the curriculum, including digital literacy and AI-related content, supported by hands-on training and project-based learning approaches [28]. These initiatives were expected to improve teaching quality, increase student engagement, and enhance equitable access to learning resources across schools. In addition, the broader cultural context of Pakistan, which is characterized by high-power distance and respect for teacher authority, may further shape how teacher behaviors and beliefs influence student perceptions and aspirations. In such settings, teachers often play a significant role in shaping classroom climate and students’ future-oriented beliefs, making teacher self-efficacy particularly influential in educational outcomes [19]. These contextual features make Punjab a relevant setting for exploring how teacher self-efficacy and institutional support interact to influence student outcomes.

Although these initiatives were implemented at different scales and levels, they reflected a consistent policy focus on modernizing education in the region. The education profile of Punjab itself made it suitable for this investigation. Recent national reporting placed the province at the highest literacy level in the country, reaching an estimated 66% [29], but still falls behind any developed country. With this mix of policy experimentation and relative educational advantage, Punjab provided the setting necessary to investigate whether school-level conditions influence how teachers translate confidence in technology use into broader classroom and student outcomes.

The data was collected in three phases, with a two-month interval between each phase. This time-lagged, multi-wave design was adopted primarily to reduce the risk of common method bias by temporally separating the measurement of key variables [30]. Such procedural remedies are recommended in behavioral research to minimize respondents’ ability to use previous answers to inform subsequent responses and to reduce consistency motifs and evaluation apprehension [30]. Similar multi-wave designs have been widely used in educational and organizational research to improve causal inference and reduce measurement bias. We approached schools through education department of the Punjab government. The first round of the questionnaire survey included items on demographics, teacher technology-integration self efficacy and digital inclusion were sent to 587 teachers of 9th to 12th grade. The first round of rendered 490 questionnaires. The second round, initiated after two months of the first round, questionnaires containing items on inclusion climate were sent to the students of the teacher responded in the first round. At the end of the second phase, 411 filled questionnaires were received. After two months of the second round, we initiated the third round where questionnaires containing information on students’ upward mobility intent. The third-round asked teachers to rate their perception of the students’ upward mobility intention based on their observation of the students who responded in the second round. The third round rendered 305 filled responses. After cleaning and removing incomplete questionnaires and the questionnaire not fulfilling dyads, the final response sample consisted of 292. The final response sample represented 44% as male and 56% females.

AMOS was selected for confirmatory factor analysis due to its strong capability in structural equation modeling and its widespread use in educational and behavioral research for assessing measurement validity and model fit. The PROCESS macro in SPSS was employed for hypothesis testing, particularly for mediation and moderated mediation analysis, as it provides robust bootstrapping procedures and is well-suited for testing conditional indirect effects. These tools are commonly applied in educational research and are appropriate for the present study given their focus on latent constructs and complex relationships.

Discussion

The findings of this study offer valuable insight into how teacher beliefs, classroom environment, and school-level support interact to influence students’ aspirations for future mobility. SCT provides a useful framework for interpreting these results. Bandura [21] posited that human behavior emerges from the interplay between personal factors, behavioral actions, and environmental conditions. Rather than reiterating the theory, the present findings allow a more nuanced understanding of how these elements operate in practice within higher-secondary school settings. Teacher technology-integration self-efficacy demonstrated a positive link to students’ perceptions of classroom inclusion, confirming that personal belief translates into observable teaching behaviors that shape students’ experience of their environment. This finding aligns with earlier work showing that teacher self-efficacy predicts willingness to experiment with technology and integrate digital tools into instruction [7]. However, our study extends this knowledge by demonstrating how this teacher behavior indirectly affects students’ beliefs about their own potential to improve future socioeconomic standing. Few studies have connected teacher confidence with student upward mobility beliefs. By documenting this mediated pathway, the present study builds upon the inclusion-climate research of Schwab et al., [11] while also addressing the call by Matamoros-Lima et al. [18] to investigate school-level factors that shape mobility beliefs.

The indirect relationship between teacher technology-integration self efficacy to student upward mobility intent, mediated through classroom inclusion, provides an important confirmation of SCT’s reciprocal determinism [34]. Teacher actions influenced student perceptions, which in turn appear to feed back into student motivation. The strong significance of this pathway suggests that inclusion climate may function as a critical bridge between teacher behavior and student beliefs about long-term opportunity. This extends prior research [4] that has tended to examine teacher efficacy and student outcomes as parallel constructs rather than part of a causal chain.

The moderating role of digital inclusion also produced noteworthy findings. As hypothesized, elevated levels of digital inclusion strengthened the relationship between teacher technology-integration self-efficacy and classroom inclusion. The finding echoes earlier work by Zeng et al. [7], who argued that teacher beliefs are often resilient despite variable external conditions. Still, our results provide an added dimension by quantitatively demonstrating the moderating effect of school-level infrastructure, something previous studies speculated but did not evaluate directly. The moderating effect of digital inclusion suggests that teacher self-efficacy does not operate uniformly across contexts. In settings where digital resources are limited, even confident teachers may face constraints in translating their capabilities into inclusive classroom practices. This indicates that individual capability alone may be insufficient without supportive institutional conditions, pointing to a boundary condition in the application of self-efficacy within resource-constrained environments.

The moderated mediation analysis adds a final layer of complexity. The strength of the indirect relationship between teacher self-efficacy and upward mobility intent increased under conditions of high digital inclusion. This finding reinforces SCT’s view that environmental facilitators amplify personal agencies but do not entirely substitute for it. This study documents this effect within a developing country context, where digital access and institutional support vary across schools.

While the hypothesized relationships were supported, the strength of some effects suggests important nuances. For instance, the relatively strong association between classroom inclusion and upward mobility intent indicates that students’ perceptions of their immediate learning environment may play a more significant role in shaping future-oriented beliefs than teacher characteristics alone. This implies that the influence of teacher self-efficacy may operate primarily through shaping classroom experiences rather than directly affecting student aspirations. Such a pattern highlights the importance of proximal classroom processes over more distal teacher attributes. An additional point worth noting is that the effect of digital inclusion appears to amplify, rather than substitute for teacher self-efficacy. This suggests that institutional support does not replace individual capability but instead enhances its impact. Such a finding provides a more balanced view of technology integration, where both human and structural factors remain necessary.

Theoretical contributions

Expanding on the discussions, the findings indicate that teacher technology-integration self-efficacy shapes students’ perceptions of classroom inclusion, which in turn influences their intentions to move upwards, while school-level digital inclusion conditionalizing this relationship. These findings highlight the interrelationship between personal beliefs, classroom conditions, and institutional support in influencing student performance. Based on these results, the research provides a number of theoretical contributions.

The results of this research support further the major SCT propositions in an educational setting. Although the earlier studies have been conducted to study teacher self-efficacy and student outcomes as two distinct areas, this study evaluates a mediated association, where inclusion climate connects teacher behavior and student upward mobility beliefs. This is in line with the reciprocal determinism of Bandura [21] since it demonstrates how student beliefs and environmental factors interplay to produce student outcomes. The findings indicate that beliefs of teachers are linked to the classroom environment, which also correlates with student aspirations reflecting the interdependent nature of processes outlined in SCT. It is interesting to mention that the significance of school-level support of technology implies the role of environmental enablers in the interpretation of how self-efficacy converts to classroom outcomes. In line with Bandura [22], the results show that school digital inclusion can enhance the connection between teacher self-efficacy and inclusion in classrooms. Such a combination of personal belief and institutional reinforcement has not been comprehensively studied in the context of SCT literature, especially within the education context.

Beyond SCT, the results can be used in a wider discussion of digital equity and student mobility. Although this research has been based on SCT, its results are also echoed by other theoretical approaches to educational technology. An example of this is the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) that stresses perceived usefulness and ease of use to be among the important factors that drive use of technology, which is consistent with the influence of teacher self-efficacy on the development of technology integration practices. In a similar way, the theory of Diffusion of Innovations emphasizes the role of personal preparedness and situational facilitation in the process of adopting new practices, which is similar to the interplay between teacher beliefs and school-wide digital inclusion presented in this paper.

Not many models placed inclusion climate as an aspect that links teacher behavior with long-term student aspirations. Investigating this mechanism, the study unites the results of the literature on technology adoption through the lens of teachers [7] and student engagement and equity outcomes [11]. It also provides an empirical contribution in that it goes to show empirical data in a developing country context where the disparities in infrastructure and institutional support are significant to investigate these relationships.

Practical implications

The practical usefulness of these insights lies in the hands of policymakers and school leaders. Allowing teachers the assurance and the organizational framework to incorporate digital tools can not only enhance the quality of instruction but also establish learning conditions that foster student interest and motivation.

Resource-only interventions, without simultaneous attempts to develop teacher self-efficacy, might not be sufficient to produce a sustained change. The policymakers must thus take a moderate standpoint that involves investing in digital infrastructure as well as ongoing professional development initiatives that would help in enhancing the confidence and pedagogical application of technology among teachers. In the developing world, it can be considered a priority to increase access to simple digital tools, secure internet connectivity, and provide scalable teacher training programs that can be applied in a variety of school environments. In more developed settings, where infrastructure may already be established, the focus can be on improving the quality of technology integration by using advanced training, personalized learning systems, and data-driven instructional practices. Also, policymakers can think of community and stakeholder engagement, including collaboration with local organizations or technology providers, to facilitate resource mobilization and sustainability of digital initiatives. These steps can be implemented to make policy a reality and make sure that digital inclusion initiatives result in significant changes in teaching and student achievement.

Limitations and future research

Some caution is warranted in generalizing these results. This research was limited to higher-secondary schools within one province, i.e., Punjab province of Pakistan, which might restrict the extrapolation of the results to other areas with varying educational, cultural, or technological backgrounds. Nonetheless, the chosen context offers a topical environment with constant digital change and inconsistency in the resources at the school level, which is why it is appropriate to investigate the suggested relationships. Future studies might consider applying this model to other areas and nations, both developed and developing, to determine the strength and the generalizability of the result.

The cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference. Future studies may be extended to other educational settings, investigate longitudinal outcomes, and examine other moderating factors like teacher training quality or community support systems. This kind of work may also help to explain how individual and institutional factors interact to influence the path of students in various learning settings.
