Postoperative clinical outcomes with and without short-term intravenous tranexamic acid after posterior lumbar interbody fusion: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Xianqun Ji, Xiaochen Wang, Liang Shi, Fangtao Tian
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The use of tranexamic acid (TXA) in posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) surgery is believed to be beneficial for reducing intraoperative blood loss, postoperative drainage volume (PDV), and length of hospital stay (LOS). However, whether continued administration of intravenous TXA within 24 hours after surgery is more beneficial to patients has not yet been studied. This study prospectively analyzed the perioperative outcomes of patients who received and did not receive intravenous TXA within 24 hours after PLIF (≥2 segments) surgery from January 2018 to December 2021. A total of 78 and 69 patients were included in the TXA (receive intravenous TXA intraoperatively and within 24 hours postoperatively) and non-TXA (only receive intravenous TXA intraoperatively) groups, respectively. No significant differences were observed in the intraoperative blood loss and operative time between the 2 groups. The postoperative drainage volume, postoperative drainage time, and length of hospital stay in the TXA group were significantly lower than those in the non-TXA group. The rates and volumes of postoperative blood and albumin transfusions were significantly lower in the TXA group than those in the non-TXA group. No significant differences were observed in the perioperative complication rates between the 2 groups. No increase in thrombosis-related complications was observed with postoperative TXA administration. Short-term TXA use after PLIF (≥2 segments) surgery is safe. In addition to intraoperative use of TXA, additionally administration of intravenous TXA within 24 hours postoperatively can improve the perioperative clinical outcomes of patients without increasing the risk of thrombotic events.

1. Introduction

Posterior lumbar fusion, including posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) and posterolateral fusion, is a widely used method for the surgical treatment of lumbar diseases, such as lumbar spondylolisthesis and lumbar spinal stenosis.[1,2] Although the PLIF method shows a higher fusion rate compared with that of posterolateral fusion,[3–5] the method, especially multi-segment fusion surgery, can still cause significant intraoperative bleeding and postoperative drainage. This is due to the invasive nature of PLIF surgery that involves the intraspinal venous plexus, leading to perioperative hypoproteinemia or anemia in patients.[6–8]

Several clinicians have adopted the use of antifibrinolytics, particularly tranexamic acid (TXA), in posterior lumbar fusion surgeries, and the TXA use has been demonstrated to help improve the perioperative clinical outcomes, such as reduction in intraoperative blood loss (IBL) and operative time (OT), transfusion rates, hidden blood loss, and length of hospital stay (LOS).[9–34] However, to date, studies comparing the differences in perioperative clinical outcomes with and without postoperative TXA administration are lacking. The impact of the postoperative TXA use on reducing postoperative drainage volume (PDV), LOS, and the rate of postoperative blood or albumin transfusions remains to be elucidated. In addition, whether postoperative TXA use increases the perioperative risk of thrombotic events has not yet been studied. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the differences in perioperative clinical outcomes between patients with and without intravenous TXA administration within 24 hours after PLIF surgery.

2. Methods

We prospectively collected and analyzed the clinical outcomes of patients who underwent PLIF (≥2 segments) for lumbar spinal stenosis or spondylolisthesis between January 2018 and December 2021. This series of patients is not randomly assigned. Patients were enrolled according to the time line. From January 2018 to March 2019, patients were assigned into the group who did not receive TXA 24 hours after surgery, while patients from April 2019 to December 2021 were assigned into group who receive TXA 24 hours after surgery. This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangyang No. 1 People’s Hospital. All patients signed an informed consent form prior to participation in this study. All patients received intravenous TXA treatment during surgery. The patients were divided into a postoperative TXA group (receiving intravenous TXA 24 hours postoperatively) and a non-TXA group (not receiving TXA postoperatively). Patients were excluded based on the following exclusion criteria: (1) underwent single lumbar decompression surgery; (2) undergoing posterolateral fusion or single-segment PLIF; (3) undergoing fusion surgery for spinal tumors or scoliosis; (4) underwent non-posterior fusion surgery, such as oblique lumbar or anterior lumbar interbody fusion; (5) with a preoperative abnormal coagulation function; and (6) with preoperative lower limb venous thrombosis.

2.1. Collection of basic information

Basic patient information, such as sex, age, body mass index, and surgical segments, was obtained from the hospital case registration system. All patients underwent a lower limb vascular ultrasound examination before surgery and at discharge.

2.2. Perioperative clinical outcomes

Clinical follow-up personnel collected the visual analog scale (VAS) scores of low back pain (LBP) and leg pain (LP) from all patients preoperatively, on the first and third postoperative days, and at discharge. Additionally, the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) was assessed preoperatively and at discharge.

2.3. Surgical methods

All patients underwent PLIF performed by 2 qualified surgeons (JX and TF). All patients were administered TXA during surgery. The patients received a loading dose of 20 mg/kg TXA over 15 minutes before surgery, followed by a maintenance infusion of TXA (2 mg/kg/h) until the surgery was completed (Fig. 1).

The protocol of administration of intravenous TXA of the 2 groups.

2.4. Postoperative treatment

All patients received intravenous antibiotics within 48 hours postoperatively. The drainage tube was removed after the drainage fluid volume reached <50 mL and became serous within 24 hours. In the TXA group, patients were administered 2 g of TXA (10 mg/kg/h, maintenance dose) within 24 hours postoperatively (Fig. 1). The OT, IBL, PDV, postoperative drainage time (PDT), postoperative blood transfusion (PBT), postoperative albumin transfusion (PAT), and LOS were recorded. IBL = (preoperative Hct − postoperative Hct)/preoperative Hct × weight × 7% × 1000 mL. The postoperative Hct values are the Hct values before the postoperative transfusion. The rates and volumes of PBTs and PATs were statistically evaluated in both groups. Hemoglobin < 90 g/L and albumin < 30 g/L postoperatively were indicators for PBTs and PATs, respectively.

2.5. Primary and second outcomes

The primary outcome of the 2 groups is ODI at discharge, and the second outcomes including LBP and LP VAS scores at discharge, TPBL, PDV, PDT, and LOS.

2.6. Perioperative complication assessment

The perioperative complication was assessed by TF (Chief surgeon), which including the complications during the patient’s hospitalization and readmission within 1 month after surgery. These complications including minor complications such as poor wound healing, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak, ganglionitis, postoperative fever, lower limb superficial vein thrombosis, etc and significant complications such as pulmonary infection, deep infection which need reoperation, nerve root injury, intraincision hematoma, etc.

2.7. Discharge

There is a set of standards for the discharge of patients. Firstly, the patient needs to remove the drainage tube. Secondly, the patient’s incision did not show poor healing or infection. Finally, the patient’s blood indicators, including blood routine, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and C-reactive protein, showed a decreasing trend after surgery.

2.8. Statistical analysis

We assumed a standard deviation of 20 for the change in ODI. This study would have 80% power to detect a between-group difference of 15 in the degree of improvement in ODI,[35] at a two-sided significance level of 0.05. The minimum sample size required for this study is 56 cases. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to evaluate the distribution of data. A t-test (mean values ± standard deviations) or Mann–Whitney U test (mean values, Min–Max) was used to examine the differences in continuous variables. The chi-squared test or Fisher exact test was used to examine categorical variables. P < .05 was considered statistically significant. SPSS software (version 26, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) was used for the statistical analyses.

3. Results

3.1. Demographics

The study initially included 298 patients. After excluding 105 patients who underwent single-segment lumbar fusion surgery, 2 patients with preoperative lower limb venous thrombosis, 3 patients who underwent posterior fusion surgery, 19 patients who underwent non-posterior surgery, and 22 patients with incomplete clinical outcomes, 147 patients (69 in the TXA group and 78 in the non-TXA group) were included in this study (Fig. 2). No significant differences were observed in sex, age, rate of diabetes, or fusion segment between the 2 groups. The body mass index of the TXA group was lower than that of the non-TXA group (Table 1).

3.4. Complications

Table 4 shows perioperative complications observed in the 2 groups. No significant differences were observed in the overall complication rates between the 2 groups. In the TXA group, a patient experienced postoperative CSF leakage due to a dural tear. The drainage tube was removed on the sixth day after the incision healed. Another patient had an incision infection during the perioperative period, and the incision ultimately healed after debridement. Two other cases of poor incision healing were observed; conservative treatment was used that eventually led to the healing of these incisions. In the non-TXA group, 2 cases of postoperative CSF leakage caused by dural tears were observed. On the seventh day after surgery, the drainage tube was removed and the incision healed satisfactorily. One case of severe LP caused by postoperative ganglionitis was relieved by nerve root block surgery. Three cases of poor wound healing were also observed. One patient underwent debridement surgery, and the other 2 patients were cured through conservative treatment. No venous thrombosis or symptomatic intraspinal hematomas were observed in either group.

4. Discussion

TXA is widely used in lumbar fusion surgery because it reduces IBL, OT, LOS, and even blood transfusion rates.[9–34] A systematic review and meta-analysis by Gong et al demonstrated that intravenous TXA during PLIF surgery is safe and can help reduce surgical blood loss; however, the authors concluded that it does not reduce transfusion rates.[10] Bai et al made similar conclusions.[10] However, Hui et al and Lin et al found that perioperative intravenous TXA administration in spinal surgery can reduce IBL as well as transfusion rates.[11,12] In addition, more than one study has found that intraoperative TXA use can reduce PDV and LOS.[13–18] However, no studies have reported whether using TXA or not using TXA within 24 hours after surgery will result in different perioperative clinical outcomes.

In this study, we found that the PDV, PDT, and LOS in the TXA group were significantly lower than those in the non-TXA group. The rates and volumes of PBTs and PATs were significantly lower in the TXA group than those in the non-TXA group. No significant differences were observed in perioperative complication rates between the 2 groups. This indicates that, in addition to using TXA during surgery, using a reasonable dose of TXA within 24 hours after lumbar fusion surgery is safe, as it does not increase the risk of thrombosis or other related complications. In addition, we found that TXA use significantly reduced PDV and PDT. Previous studies have focused on the influence of using TXA during surgery on IBL and postoperative drainage. The present study demonstrated that postoperative TXA use also helps reduce postoperative drainage. In addition, we found that using TXA within 24 hours after surgery could reduce blood and albumin transfusion rates. This could be related to a decrease in PDV (hidden blood loss), which helps slow the continuous loss of hemoglobin and albumin after surgery. In addition, patients who received TXA within 24 hours after surgery had their drainage tubes removed earlier than those who did not receive TXA after surgery, which could have led to an earlier discharge time. Ehresman et al found that TXA use during long-segment spinal surgery (≥4 segments) is highly cost-effective and using TXA in short-segment surgery does not increase costs.[31] Yuan et al also indicated that TXA use during surgery can significantly decrease total hemostasis costs and perioperative blood loss in patients undergoing spinal osteotomy.[32] In the present study, hemostasis costs may have been increased due to the additional TXA use after surgery; however, the TXA use significantly reduced postoperative drainage, transfusion rate, albumin rate (volume), and LOS, offsetting any increment in cost of using TXA after surgery.

The optimal dosage of TXA used in spinal surgery remains controversial. Rahmani et al summarized the intraoperative doses of TXA that are currently used in clinical practice.[36] At present, the following are the 4 dosing regimens: 10 mg/kg (loading dose) + 1 mg/kg/h (maintenance dose), 10 mg/kg (loading dose) + 2 mg/kg/h (maintenance dose), 15 mg/kg (loading dose), and 15 mg/kg (loading dose) + 1 mg/kg/h (maintenance dose). However, no study has found that the use of a single-dose regimen is more advantageous than other dose regimens. In the present study, a loading dose of 20 mg/kg TXA was administered over 15 minutes before surgery, followed by a maintenance infusion of TXA (2 mg/kg/h). In addition, 2 g TXA (10 mg/kg/h, loading dose) was administered within 24 hours postoperatively. This is considered a safe and acceptable daily dose of TXA.[36,37]

TXA is a synthetic antifibrinolytic drug that prevents fibrinolysis and protects against blood clots, thereby reducing blood loss from surgical wounds.[38] Compared with epsilon–aminocaproic acid and aprotinin, TXA is considered the most effective antifibrinolytic drug.[39] Although it may help reduce surgical blood loss, administering TXA to unsuitable patients or at inappropriate doses may increase the risk of thrombosis.[40–43] However, most studies have reported that TXA use before and during lumbar fusion surgery does not increase the risk of postoperative thrombosis. Surgeons should be cautious when identifying patients at a high risk of thrombosis before using TXA. Postoperative epidural hematoma is a complication that requires additional attention. Some researchers have raised concerns about increased epidural hematoma risk during TXA use. Therefore, they administered rivaroxaban in combination with TXA during surgery.[44] However, the present study did not find that the postoperative use of TXA increased the risk of epidural hematoma.

This study had a few limitations. First, the number of cases in this study was limited, and a larger sample size is needed in the future to confirm the safety and clinical efficacy of using TXA within 24 hours after surgery. Second, although we prospectively collected clinical data from 2 groups of patients. However, compared to randomized controlled trial, this prospective cohort study still has unavoidable bias. Besides, it should be pointed out that we planned to collect clinical information on postoperative days 1, 3, 5, and 7 initially. However, we found that some patients had already been discharged on the 5th and 7th day after surgery. Therefore, we only counted the clinical outcomes at postoperative 1st, 3rd, and at discharge. Additionally, this study lacked data on the long-term follow-up of patients. Therefore, this study could not determine the long-term impact of using additional TXA within 24 hours after surgery. Finally, the optimal postoperative dose of TXA remains uncertain. Therefore, further studies are required to identify and establish the most appropriate dose for postoperative TXA administration.

In conclusion, to our knowledge, this is the first study to compare the perioperative outcomes after lumbar fusion surgery with and without postoperative TXA administration. We found that the short-term TXA use after lumbar fusion surgery was safe. Compared to patients who did not receive TXA treatment after surgery, receiving TXA within 24 hours postoperatively improved the perioperative clinical outcomes of patients who underwent PLIF (≥2 segments) surgery without increasing the risk of thrombotic events.
