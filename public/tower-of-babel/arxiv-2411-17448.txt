1.
Introduction

Our main theorem is the following. Throughout the paper, we write
[X]=\{1,\dots,X\}
for positive integer
X
.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists
c_{0}>0
for which the following holds. Let
X\geqslant 10
and suppose that
A\subseteq[X]
has no solutions to
a_{1}-a_{2}=n^{2}
with
a_{1},a_{2}\in A
and
n\in\mathbf{N}
.

|A|\leqslant X\exp(-c_{0}\sqrt{\log X}).

1.1.
History

Denote by
s(X)
the size of the largest subset of
[X]
not containing two distinct elements differing by a square. Lovász conjectured that
s(X)=o(X)
. Independently, Furstenberg
[
6
, Theorem 1.2]
and Sárközy
[
19
]
established the conjecture. Furstenberg’s work was based on methods from ergodic theory and in particular relies on his eponymous correspondence principle (
[
6
]
is also where Furstenberg famously gave an alternate proof Szemerédi’s theorem).

Sárközy’s proof was based on Fourier analysis and therefore, unlike Furstenberg’s work, gave a rate on
s(X)
. In particular, Sárközy established that

s(X)\ll X(\log\log X)^{2/3}(\log X)^{-1/3}.

This improvement of
(\log X)^{-1/3+o(1)}
over the trivial bound was further enhanced to
(\log X)^{-\omega(X)}
for some function
\omega(X)\rightarrow\infty
by Pintz, Steiger, and Szemerédi
[
16
]
. In particular, Pintz, Steiger, and Szemerédi
[
16
]
proved that

s(X)\ll X(\log X)^{-\frac{1}{12}\log\log\log\log X}.

Bloom and Maynard
[
3
]
subsequently showed that one may take

s(X)\ll X(\log X)^{-c\log\log\log X}

for a suitable constant
c>0
, which was the best known bound before the first version
[
7
]
of this work, which obtained a bound of the shape

s(X)\ll X\exp(-(\log X)^{1/4-o(1)}).

Regarding lower bounds, a standard probabilistic deletion argument allows one to prove that
s(X)\gg X^{1/2}
. Erdős conjectured that
s(X)\ll X^{1/2}(\log X)^{O(1)}
but this was disproven by Sárközy
[
20
]
. However Sárközy
[
20
]
still conjectured that
s(X)\ll X^{1/2+o(1)}
. This was disproven by Ruzsa
[
18
]
who established that
s(X)\gg X^{1/2+\kappa}
with
\kappa:=\frac{\log 7}{2\log 65}\approx.233077
. This was refined in work of Lewko
[
15
]
, who showed that
\kappa=\frac{\log 12}{2\log 265}\approx.233412
is permissible. It remains a major open question to decide whether
s(X)\ll X^{1-c}
for some constant
c>0
, or not. (The authors of the present paper would speculate that the answer is yes.)

We remark that the greedy construction starting from
0
gives a set
\{0,2,5,7,10,12,15,17,20\}
which appears experimentally to have around
n^{0.7}
elements up to
n
. Analysing the behaviour of this sequence rigorously seems an interesting problem. This sequence is discussed in the Online Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences
https://oeis.org/A030193
as sequence A030193.

\{P(n):n\in\mathbf{Z}\}
with different polynomials

is intersective;

The overall approach to the proof of
Theorem

1.1
is the density increment strategy, as used in all previous papers giving an explicit bound for
s(X)
. The basic idea is to establish a dichotomy, stating that if
A\subset[X]
is a set of size
\alpha
then either it contains two elements differing by a square, or else we may pass to a long subprogression with square common difference on which the density of
A
is appreciably larger. Iteration of this argument (and careful control of the parameters) leads to bounds on
s(X)
.

1.2.
Arithmetic level
d
inequality

As with previous works, we make heavy use of the Fourier transform (more specifically, a form of the Hardy-Littlewood circle method) in order to obtain the necessary density increment. Our primary new input for
Theorem

1.1
is the following result, which potentially has other applications.
If
f:\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
is a function, we define the Fourier transform as

\widehat{f}(\theta):=\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}f(x)e(-\theta x).

Theorem 1.2
.

Set
C_{0}:=2^{13}
. Let
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
and let
X\geqslant 1
be parameters with
\alpha>2X^{-1/2}
. Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a set of pairwise coprime positive integers such that
\max_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}q\leqslant X^{1/32\log(1/\alpha)}
. Let
1\leqslant d\leqslant 2^{-7}\log(1/\alpha)
. Let
f:[X]\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
be a function with such that
|f(x)|\leqslant 1
for all
x
. Then either

(1.1)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq\mathcal{Q}\\
|S|=d\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a(\operatorname{mod}\,\prod_{q\in S}q)\\
q\in S\Rightarrow q\nmid a\end{subarray}}\Big|\widehat{f}\Big(\frac{a}{\prod_{q\in S}q}\Big)\Big|^{2}\leqslant\alpha^{2}X^{2}\Big(\frac{C_{0}\log(1/\alpha)}{d}\Big)^{d},

or else for some set
S\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
,
1\leqslant|S|\leqslant 2\log(1/\alpha)
, and for some
r\in\mathbf{Z}
,
the average of
|f(x)|
on the progression
P=\{x\in[X]:x\equiv r(\operatorname{mod}\,\prod_{q\in S}q)\}
is greater than
2^{|S|}\alpha
.

Remark
.

At the cost of changing
C_{0}
, one may replace the condition of finding a progression of density
2^{|S|}\alpha
with a progression of density
\lambda^{|S|}\alpha
for any fixed
\lambda\geqslant 1
.

The proof of
Theorem

1.2
makes very substantial use of the results and ideas in a paper of Keller, Lifshitz and Marcus
[
13
]
concerning

in product spaces. This is the culmination of body of work, of which we mention in particular the earlier work of Keevash-Lifshitz-Long-Minzer
[
12
]
. We remark in our context, a “local” function is one which strongly correlates with an arithmetic progression with common difference which is a product of “few” elements of
\mathcal{Q}
. A global function by contrast does not correlate strongly with such functions. We will introduce these techniques in the next section.

We remark that when applying
Theorem

1.2
to derive a density increment from arithmetic information such as a weighted average of Fourier coefficients
|\widehat{1_{A}}(a/q)|^{2}
being large, it has proven to be quantitatively advantageous to use many separate applications of
Theorem

1.2
with different sets
\mathcal{Q}
. In particular, in our density increment argument we divide into different types of
q
based on the structure of the prime factorization of
q
, and for each factorization type use a different

. Furthermore in our analysis it turns out to be advantageous to handle ‘small’ prime factors of

by breaking

1.4.
Notation

Most of our notation is very standard. We write
\mathbf{1}_{S}
for the characteristic function of the set
S
, defined by
\mathbf{1}_{S}(x)=1
of
x\in S
and
0
otherwise. If
S
is finite, and if
f:S\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
is a function, we write
\mathbb{E}_{S}f
or
\mathbb{E}_{x\in S}f(x)
for
|S|^{-1}\sum_{x\in S}f(x)
.

For
x\in\mathbf{R}
,
\|x\|_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}
denotes the distance to the nearest integer. As is standard, we let
e(\theta)=e^{2\pi i\theta}
.

We normalise the Fourier transform on finite abelian groups using the normalised counting measure on physical space and the counting measure in frequency space. That is, we define
\widehat{f}(\chi)=\mathbb{E}_{x\in G}f(x)\overline{\chi(x)}
for a character
\chi
on
G
, and the inversion formula reads
f(x)=\sum_{\chi\in\widehat{G}}\widehat{f}(\chi)\chi(x)
.

Similarly, if
G
is a finite abelian group and if
f:G\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
is a function then the
L^{p}
-norms are defined with respect to normalised counting measure, thus
\|f\|_{p}^{p}:=\mathbb{E}_{x\in G}|f(x)|^{p}
for
p\geqslant 1
.

If
\Sigma
is a finite set, we denote by
B(\Sigma)
the space of functions
f:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
.

We use standard asymptotic notation for functions on
\mathbf{N}
throughout. Given functions
f=f(x)
and
g=g(x)
, we write
f=O(g)
,
f\ll g
, or
g\gg f
to mean that there is a constant
C
such that
|f(x)|\leqslant Cg(x)
for sufficiently large
x
. Subscripts indicate dependence on parameters.

On many occasions we will see quantities such as
\exp(\log(1/\alpha)^{C})
. This means
\exp\big((\log(1/\alpha))^{C}\big)
(which we believe is the natural interpretation), but to avoid notational clutter we suppress the extra pair of brackets.

2.
Statement of hypercontractivity results

Our main ingredient from other work will be a result on so-called global hypercontractivity from
[
13
]
. We state that result, in the language we will use in this paper, here. The general setup will be that we have a set
\mathcal{Q}
of pairwise coprime positive integers, and we consider the product group

G_{\mathcal{Q}}:=\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbf{Z}/q\mathbf{Z}\cong\mathbf{Z}/\big(\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}q\big)\mathbf{Z}

with the natural isomorphism given by the Chinese remainder theorem. The groups
\mathbf{Z}/q\mathbf{Z}
will correspond to the sets
\Omega_{i}
in
[
13
]
.

We will consider several different linear operators from
B(G_{\mathcal{Q}})
to itself. For many of them it seems most natural in our context to define them on the Fourier side, though in
[
13
]
(where the sets
\Omega_{i}
need not have any group structure) the authors proceed combinatorially.

To define these operators, we fix some notation for the Fourier transform on
G_{\mathcal{Q}}
. We identify the dual of
\mathbf{Z}/q\mathbf{Z}
with
\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{Z}/\mathbf{Z}\subset\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
, with
\theta\in\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{Z}/\mathbf{Z}
corresponding to the character which, with a slight abuse of notation, we write
x\mapsto e(\theta x)
. We may then identify
\widehat{G}_{\mathcal{Q}}
with
\bigoplus_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}(\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{Z}/\mathbf{Z})\subset\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
. If
\xi=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\xi_{q}/q
then we define
\operatorname{Supp}(\xi):=\{q:\xi_{q}\neq 0(\operatorname{mod}\,q)\}
and
|\xi|=|\operatorname{Supp}(\xi)|
.

All of our operators will be Fourier multiplier operators of the form
\Phi e(\xi\cdot)=w(\xi)e(\xi\cdot)
for some
w(\xi)\in\mathbf{R}
. Specifically, for all
S\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
we define

•

the
averaging
operator
E_{S}
, with
w(\xi)=1
if
\xi_{q}=0
for all
q\in S
, and
w(\xi)=0
otherwise;

•

the
laplacian
operator
L_{S}
, with
w(\xi)=1
if
\xi_{q}\neq 0
for all
q\in S
, and
w(\xi)=0
otherwise.

Moreover, we define

•

for integer
d
, the Fourier level
d
operator
W_{d}
, with
w(\xi)=1
if
|\xi|=d
and
w(\xi)=0
otherwise;

•

for
\rho\in(0,1)
, the noise operator
T_{\rho}
, with
w(\xi)=\rho^{|\xi|}
.

Remarks
.

These definitions are consistent with those in
[
13
]
. The action of these operators on general functions is easily computed using linearity and the Fourier inversion formula; for instance

T_{\rho}f(x)=\sum_{\xi}\rho^{|\xi|}\widehat{f}(\xi)e(\xi x).

Note that
W_{0}f=\widehat{f}(0)=\mathbb{E}f
and that
W_{d}f=0
for
d<0
.

We record some basic properties of these needed later in the paper.

Lemma 2.1
.

We have

(2.1)

L_{S}=\sum_{T\subseteq S}(-1)^{|T|}E_{T},

and

(2.2)

E_{S}f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{y\equiv x(\mbox{\scriptsize{mod}}\,\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}\setminus S}q)}f(y).

Proof.

The statement
2.1
is immediate from the identity

\prod_{q\in S}\big(1-\mathbf{1}_{\xi_{q}=0}\big)=\sum_{T\subseteq S}(-1)^{T}\prod_{q\in T}\mathbf{1}_{\xi_{q}=0}.

To obtain
2.2
, we expand
f
on the Fourier side and use that

\mathbb{E}_{y\equiv x(\mbox{\scriptsize{mod}}\,\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}\setminus S}q)}e(\xi y)=e(\xi x)

if
\xi_{q}=0
for all
q\in S
, and
0
otherwise.

To state the key global hypercontractivity result (
Theorem

2.5
below) we need the notions of
global function
and
noise operator
. The notion of a function being global (as considered in
[
13
]
) is what the authors of that paper call a ‘derivative-based’ notion of globalness, which is somewhat different to the corresponding notions considered in
[
12
]
. These notions however are closely related; see
[
13
, Lemma 4.9]
.
To define the derivative operators we first need to define specialisation operators. Let
S\subset\mathcal{Q}
and let
x\in G_{S}
. Then we define the specialisation operator

\sigma_{S\rightarrow x}:B(G_{\mathcal{Q}})\rightarrow B(G_{\mathcal{Q}\setminus S})

via

(\sigma_{S\rightarrow x}f)(y)=f(x,y)

for
y\in G_{\mathcal{Q}\setminus S}
. (Here, of course, we have identified
G_{\mathcal{Q}}=G_{S}\times G_{\mathcal{Q}\setminus S}
in the obvious way.)

Now we come to the key definition of
derivatives
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
and
x\in G_{S}
. The we define the derivative operator
D_{S,x}:B(G_{\mathcal{Q}})\rightarrow B(G_{\mathcal{Q}\setminus S})
by
D_{S,x}=\sigma_{S\rightarrow x}L_{S}
. That is,
(D_{S,x}f)(y):=(L_{S}f)(x,y)
.

To keep notation concise, we will often write
D_{S}
for a derivative operator where the value of
x\in G_{S}
can be arbitrary but whose exact nature is unimportant.

Finally we define the derivative-globalness which will be used to state the primary technical input from
[
13
]
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a set of pairwise coprime integers. Let
r\geqslant 0
. Let
f\in B(G_{\mathcal{Q}})
. We say that

is
(r,\gamma)
-derivative-global if for all
S\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
and
x\in G_{S}
we have

\lVert D_{S,x_{S}}f\rVert_{2}\leqslant r^{|S|}\gamma.

Remarks
.

We make the following comments:

(1)

In
[
13
]
this notion is called
(r,\gamma)
-

-global, there also being notions of

We now state a key lemma concerning derivatives and Fourier weight operators. This is stated during the proof of

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
be a set of size
r
,
x\in G_{S}
and
d
be a nonnegative integer. Then we have
D_{S,x}W_{d}=W_{d-r}D_{S,x}
.

Proof.

Since all operators are linear, it suffices to check this when
f=e(\xi\cdot)
for some
\xi
. Write
\xi^{\prime}=(\xi_{q})_{q\in S}
and
\xi^{\prime\prime}=(\xi_{q})_{q\in\mathcal{Q}\setminus S}

G_{\mathcal{Q}}=G_{S}\times G_{\mathcal{Q}\setminus S}
as before, we have
e(\xi\cdot(x,y))=e(\xi^{\prime}x)e(\xi^{\prime\prime}y)
. Write
\chi_{\xi}=e(\xi\cdot)
and similarly for
\chi_{\xi^{\prime}},\chi_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}
. We have

(2.3)

D_{S,x}W_{d}\chi_{\xi}(y)=L_{S}W_{d}\chi_{\xi}(x,y)=\mathbf{1}_{S\subseteq\operatorname{Supp}(\xi)}\mathbf{1}_{|\xi|=d}\chi_{\xi}(x,y).

On the other hand note that

D_{S,x}\chi_{\xi}(y)=L_{S}\chi_{\xi}(x,y)=\mathbf{1}_{S\subseteq\operatorname{Supp}(\xi)}\chi_{\xi}(x,y)=\mathbf{1}_{S\subseteq\operatorname{Supp}(\xi)}\chi_{\xi^{\prime}}(x)\chi_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}(y),

so

(2.4)

W_{d-r}D_{S,x}\chi_{\xi}(y)=\mathbf{1}_{S\subseteq\operatorname{Supp}(\xi)}\chi_{\xi^{\prime}}(x)\mathbf{1}_{|\xi^{\prime\prime}|=d-r}\chi_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}(y)=\mathbf{1}_{S\subseteq\operatorname{Supp}(\xi)}\mathbf{1}_{|\xi^{\prime\prime}|=d-r}\chi_{\xi}(x,y).

Comparing
2.3
and

, the result now follows from the observation that
\mathbf{1}_{S\subseteq\operatorname{Supp}(\xi)}\mathbf{1}_{|\xi|=d}=\mathbf{1}_{S\subseteq\operatorname{Supp}(\xi)}\mathbf{1}_{|\xi^{\prime\prime}|=d-r}
.

3.
Liftings

The hypercontractive results stated in the previous section take place in the context of finite (and possibly large) cyclic groups
G_{\mathcal{Q}}
. To use them to prove results about the integers, we need to do some lifting.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
X\geqslant 1
be a parameter. Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a set of pairwise coprime positive integers. Denote by
\pi_{\mathcal{Q}}:\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow G_{\mathcal{Q}}
the natural projection map. Suppose that
P\subseteq[X]
is an arithmetic progression with common difference coprime to all
q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Then we define the lifting map
\Psi_{P,\mathcal{Q}}:B(P)\rightarrow B(G_{\mathcal{Q}})
by

(\Psi_{P,\mathcal{Q}}f)((t_{q})_{q\in\mathcal{Q}})=|P|^{-1}|G_{\mathcal{Q}}|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in P\\
x\equiv t_{q}(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)\\
q\in\mathcal{Q}\end{subarray}}f(x).

Remark
.

The normalization is chosen so that

(3.1)

\mathbb{E}_{G_{\mathcal{Q}}}(\Psi_{P,\mathcal{Q}}f)=\mathbb{E}_{P}f.

The following lemma details the relation between restrictions and lifts.

X\geqslant 1

is coprime to all
q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Let
S\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
be such that
(\max_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}q)^{|S|}\leqslant X^{1/4}
, and suppose that
T\subseteq S
. Let
a\in G_{S}
. Denote
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{Q}\setminus S
, and let
P^{\prime}:=\{x\in P:x\equiv a(\operatorname{mod}\,\prod_{q\in S\setminus T}q)\}
. Then there is
\eta

4.
Proof of the arithmetic level

In this section, we prove

1.2

[

. The main new idea is to alter the use of Hölder’s inequality in
[
13
, Lemma 5.1]
(which fails to account for the sparsity of

when embedded as a subset of

) via an appeal to

3.4

In the proof of
Theorem

1.2
we will use the following definition, which essentially encodes the failure of the second clause in
Theorem

1.2
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
P\subset\mathbf{Z}
be an arithmetic progression and let
\mathcal{Q}
be a set of pairwise coprime integers, all coprime to the common difference of
P
. Let
f:P\to\mathbf{C}
. We say that
f
is
(r,\alpha,d)
-integer-global with respect to
P,\mathcal{Q}
if, for every subprogression
P^{\prime}=\{x\in P:x\equiv a(\mbox{{mod}}\,\prod_{q\in S}q)\}
, where
S\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
is a set of cardinality at most
d
, the average of
|f(x)|
on
P^{\prime}
is at most
r^{|S|}\alpha
.

We note that this definition enjoys a hereditary property: if
f
is
(r,\alpha,d)
-integer-global with respect to
P,\mathcal{Q}
, then it is
(r,r^{|S|}\alpha,d-|S|)
-integer-global with respect to
P^{\prime},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
, for any subprogression
P^{\prime}\subseteq P
cut out by a congruence condition as above and with
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}=\mathcal{Q}\setminus S
.

We now write down a claim which is somewhat stronger than
Theorem

1.2
, so as to enable an inductive argument. One difference is that it involves a progression
P\subseteq[X]
, whilst
Theorem

1.2
corresponds to the case
P=[X]
. This is necessary because the proof proceeds inductively by passing to subprogressions. A more important difference is that the claim encodes (via the notion of globalness,
Definition

2.3
) certain bounds on derivatives which do not appear in
Theorem

1.2
. Again, this is to allow an inductive argument to work.

Define for non-negative integer
d
and real

the quantities

(4.1)

r_{d}(\alpha):=\Big(\frac{d}{\log(1/\alpha)}\Big)^{1/2}\text{ and }\gamma_{d}(\alpha):=\Big(\frac{C_{0}\log(1/\alpha)}{d}\Big)^{d/2}\alpha.

where
C_{0}=2^{13}
and with the convention that
\gamma_{0}(\alpha)=\alpha
.

Claim 4.2
.

Let
X\geqslant 1
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
be parameters with
\alpha>4X^{-1/2}
. Write
L:=\log(1/\alpha)
. Let
d\leqslant 2^{-7}L
be a nonnegative integer. Let
P\subseteq[X]
be an arithmetic progression of length
\geqslant X^{3/4+d/16L}
. Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a set of pairwise coprime positive integers with
\max_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}q\leqslant X^{1/32L}
, and with all

coprime to the common difference of
P
. Suppose that

is
(2,\alpha,2L)
-integer-global. Then the function

is
(r_{d}(\alpha),\gamma_{d}(\alpha))
-derivative-global in the sense of

2.3

Before proving the claim, we deduce

1.2

Proof of

1.2
.

We seek to apply the claim with

is larger, that is to say

(4.12)

\lVert W_{d}\widetilde{f}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leqslant 2^{1/m}\alpha^{(2m-1)/2m}\lVert W_{d}\widetilde{f}\rVert_{2m}.

Suppose from now on, as a hypothesis for contradiction, that
4.8
fails to hold. Then
W_{d}\widetilde{f}
is
(r_{d}(\alpha),\lVert W_{d}\widetilde{f}\rVert_{2})
derivative-global. Indeed, we have already shown the stronger condition

\|D_{S}W_{d}\widetilde{f}\|_{2}\leqslant r_{d}(\alpha)^{|S|}\gamma_{d}(\alpha)

when
|S|>0
, and for
S=\emptyset
the condition

\|D_{S}W_{d}\widetilde{f}\|_{2}\leqslant r_{d}(\alpha)^{|S|}\|W_{d}\widetilde{f}\|_{2}

is trivial.

With an application of
Theorem

2.5
in mind, we choose parameters as in the comments after the statement of that result. Thus, set

r:=r_{d}(\alpha),\quad m=\lceil r^{-2}\rceil=\lceil L/d\rceil,\quad p:=2m,\quad\rho:=\frac{1}{20}m^{-1/2}.

Set
\gamma=\|W_{d}\widetilde{f}\|_{2}
so that, as discussed above,
W_{d}\widetilde{f}
is
(r,\gamma)
-derivative-global.

Apply
Theorem

2.5
; the conclusion is that

(4.13)

\lVert W_{d}\widetilde{f}\rVert_{2m}=\rho^{-d}\lVert T_{\rho}W_{d}\widetilde{f}\rVert_{2m}\leqslant\rho^{-d}\lVert W_{d}\widetilde{f}\rVert_{2}.

Thus
4.12
,
4.13
give

(4.14)

\lVert W_{d}\widetilde{f}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leqslant 2^{2/m}\alpha^{(2m-1)/m}\rho^{-2d}\leqslant 4(400)^{d}\alpha^{2-1/m}m^{d}\leqslant 4(400e)^{d}\alpha^{2}m^{d}.

On the other hand, from the assumption that
4.8
fails and the definition
4.1
of
\gamma_{d}(\alpha)
we have

(4.15)

\|W_{d}\widetilde{f}\|_{2}^{2}\geqslant\gamma_{d}(\alpha)^{2}\geqslant(C_{0}(m-1))^{d}\alpha^{2}.

Since
C_{0}
is so large, the bounds
4.14
and
4.15
contradict one another.

Thus, we were wrong to suppose that
4.8
does not hold, and this completes the proof of all cases of
Claim

4.2
.
∎

5.
Weight functions and exponential sums

In this section we define a smooth weight function on the squares and prove some exponential sum estimates for it. The estimates are slightly bespoke to the application at hand, but of essentially standard type.

5.1.
A smooth weight on the squares

To control Fourier transforms, we will need a smooth function supported on squares. To construct this, we use a smooth bump function
w
with the following properties.

Lemma 5.1
.

There exists a smooth function
w:\mathbf{R}\to[0,1]
, supported on
[-1,1]
, such that
w(x)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
for
x\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
and satisfying the Fourier estimate
|\widehat{w}(t)|\ll e^{-\sqrt{|t|}}
for all
t\in\mathbf{R}
.

Proof.

The existence of such
w
is standard and we omit the proof. One may proceed either using explicit functions as in
[
4
, Lemma 9]
, or via infinite convolutions of normalized characteristic functions of intervals (one being
[-\frac{3}{4},\frac{3}{4}]
and the rest being
[-n^{-3/2},n^{-3/2}]
for
n=101,102,\dots
, say) as described in
[
11
, Chapter V, Lemma 2.7]
in connection with the Denjoy-Carleman Theorem.
∎

We will also need Weyl’s inequality, which we give in in the following form.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
X
be sufficiently large. Let
\theta\in\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
and suppose that
|\mathbb{E}_{x\in[X]}e(\theta x^{2})|\geqslant\delta X
, where
\delta\geqslant X^{-1/3}
. Then there is some
q\ll(\log X)^{2}\delta^{-2}
such that
\|\theta q\|_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}\ll(\log X)^{2}\delta^{-2}X^{-2}
.

Proof.

We recall the usual statement of Weyl’s inequality (in the case of a quadratic phase). If
a,q
are such that
(a,q)=1
and
|\theta-a/q|\leqslant 1/q^{2}
, then

(5.1)

\big|\mathbb{E}_{x\in[X]}e(\theta x^{2})\big|\ll(\log X)\big(q^{-1/2}+X^{-1/2}+q^{1/2}X^{-1}\big).

See, e.g.
[
5
, Lemma 3.1]
. In the quadratic case, the factor
X^{o(1)}
present in that reference can be replaced by
O(\log X)
, since the use of the divisor bound at
[
5
, Equation (3.3)]
can be replaced by a trivial bound. (This tighter bound is not at all essential for our argument.)

Now set
Q:=c(\delta/\log X)^{2}X^{2}
for some small
c>0
. By Dirichlet’s theorem, there is some
q
,
1\leqslant q\leqslant Q
, such that
|\theta-a/q|\leqslant 1/qQ\leqslant 1/q^{2}
. Applying
5.1
, for an appropriate
c
the term
O(q^{1/2}X^{-1}\log X)
is
\leqslant\delta X/4
, and the term
O(X^{-1/2}\log X)
satisfies the same bound by the assumption on
\delta
. It follows that
\delta X/2\ll(\log X)q^{-1/2}
, so
q
satisfies the required bound. Noting that
\|\theta q\|_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}\leqslant Q^{-1}
completes the proof.
∎

We now define our smoothly-weighted version of the squares.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
w
be as in
Lemma

5.1
. Define
g_{X,\Box}:\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
via

g_{X,\Box}(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{ if $|x|$ is not a square }\\
tw(t^{2}/X)&\text{ if }|x|=t^{2}\text{ with }t\in\mathbf{N}.\end{array}\right.

Here, and in what follows, write

\widehat{g_{X,\Box}}(\theta):=\sum_{x}g_{X,\Box}(x)e(-\theta x).

The estimates we need concern minor- and major- arc values of
\theta
respectively. First, the minor arc estimate.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
X
be a sufficiently large parameter. Suppose that
\delta\geqslant X^{-1/10}
and that
|\widehat{g_{X,\Box}}(\theta)|\geqslant\delta X
.
Then there exists a natural number
q\ll(\log X)^{2}\delta^{-4}
such that

\lVert q\theta\rVert_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}\ll(\log X)^{2}\delta^{-4}X^{-1}.

Proof.

In this proof
C
denotes a sufficiently large constant which may depend on the choice of function
w
in
Lemma

5.1
but is independent of all other parameters.

Writing in the definition of
g_{X,\Box}
, we see that the assumption is

2\Big|\operatorname{Re}\sum_{1\leqslant t\leqslant X^{1/2}}tw\Big(\frac{t^{2}}{X}\Big)e(-\theta t^{2})\Big|\geqslant\delta X.

Expanding
w
via Fourier inversion and applying the triangle inequality, we obtain

\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\big|\widehat{w}(\xi)\big|\Big|\sum_{1\leqslant t\leqslant X^{1/2}}2te\Big(\Big(-\theta+\frac{\xi}{X}\Big)t^{2}\Big)\Big|d\xi\geqslant\delta X.

By
Lemma

5.1
and the trivial bound of
2X
for the inner exponential sum, the contribution from
|\xi|\geqslant C/\delta
is at most
\delta X/2
(a much weaker estimate on
\widehat{w}
than the one in
Lemma

5.1
would suffice here). Since
\int|\widehat{w}(\xi)|d\xi\leqslant C
, it follows that there is some
\xi
with
|\xi|\leqslant C/\delta
such that

\Big|\sum_{1\leqslant t\leqslant X^{1/2}}2te\Big(\Big(-\theta+\frac{\xi}{X}\Big)t^{2}\Big)\Big|\geqslant\frac{\delta X}{2C}.

By partial summation this may be written

2X^{1/2}\Big|\int^{1}_{0}dy\sum_{yX^{1/2}\leqslant t\leqslant X^{1/2}}e\Big(\Big(-\theta+\frac{\xi}{X}\Big)t^{2}\Big)\Big|\geqslant\frac{\delta X}{2C},

so for some
y\in[0,1]
we have

\Big|\sum_{yX^{1/2}\leqslant t\leqslant X^{1/2}}e\Big(\Big(-\theta+\frac{\xi}{X}\Big)t^{2}\Big)\Big|\geqslant\frac{\delta X^{1/2}}{4C}.

Thus, by the triangle inequality, there is some
N\leqslant X^{1/2}
(we can take either
N=X^{1/2}
or
N=yX^{1/2}
) such that

(5.2)

\Big|\sum_{t\leqslant N}e\Big(\Big(-\theta+\frac{\xi}{X}\Big)t^{2}\Big)\Big|\geqslant\frac{\delta X^{1/2}}{8C}\geqslant\frac{\delta N}{8C}.

We now apply Weyl’s inequality,
Lemma

5.2
. The condition
\delta/8C\geqslant N^{-1/3}
required for this application follows from the assumption
\delta\geqslant X^{-1/10}
, the fact that
N\geqslant\delta X^{1/2}/8C
(which follows from the left-hand inequality in
5.2
) and the fact that
X
is sufficiently large. We conclude that there is some
q\ll(\log X)^{2}\delta^{-2}
such that

\Big\|q\Big(-\theta+\frac{\xi}{X}\Big)\Big\|_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}\ll(\log X)^{2}\delta^{-2}N^{-2}\ll(\log X)^{2}\delta^{-4}X^{-1}.

From the triangle inequality and the fact that
|\xi|\leqslant C/\delta
, this implies

\|q\theta\|_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}\ll(\log X)^{2}\delta^{-4}X^{-1},

which is the desired result.
∎

Now we turn to the major arc estimate.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
X
be a sufficiently large parameter. Let
q
be a positive integer with
q\leqslant X^{1/8}
and suppose that
a
is coprime to
q
. Suppose that
|\theta|\leqslant X^{-7/8}
. Then

\Big|\widehat{g_{X,\Box}}\Big(\frac{a}{q}+\theta\Big)\Big|\ll Xq^{-1/2}e^{-|\theta X|^{1/2}}+X^{3/4}.

Proof.

Let
Y\geqslant 1
be a real parameter. If
\psi:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
is continuous on
[0,1]
and differentiable on
(0,1)
and if
1\leqslant q\leqslant Y
then we have the bound

(5.3)

\Big|\frac{q}{Y}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant n\leqslant Y\\
n\equiv b(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)\end{subarray}}\psi\Big(\frac{n}{Y}\Big)-\int^{1}_{0}\psi(x)dx\Big|\ll\frac{q}{Y}\max(\|\psi\|_{\infty},\|\psi^{\prime}\|_{\infty}).

To see this, first note that by the mean value theorem and matching summands

\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant n\leqslant Y\\
n\equiv b(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)\end{subarray}}\psi\Big(\frac{n}{Y}\Big)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant n\leqslant Y\\
n\equiv b^{\prime}(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)\end{subarray}}\psi\Big(\frac{n}{Y}\Big)\Big|\ll\|\psi\|_{\infty}+\|\psi^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

for any
b,b^{\prime}
(where here the
\|\psi\|_{\infty}
term comes from
n<q
and
n>Y-q
, which may be present in one sum but not the other). Thus, by averaging it is enough to handle the case
q=1
, which is the standard comparison between sums and integrals.

Let
F:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
be continuous on
[0,1]
and differentiable on
(0,1)
. Applying
5.3
with
\psi(x)=2xF(x^{2})
and making a substitution in the integral gives

\Big|\frac{q}{Y}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant n\leqslant Y\\
n\equiv b(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)\end{subarray}}\frac{2n}{Y}F\Big(\frac{n^{2}}{Y^{2}}\Big)-\int^{1}_{0}F(x)dx\Big|\ll\frac{q}{Y}\max\Big(\|F\|_{\infty},\|F^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\Big).

Applying this with
F(x):=w(x)e(-\xi x)
yields

(5.4)

\Big|\frac{q}{Y}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant n\leqslant Y\\
n\equiv b(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)\end{subarray}}\frac{2n}{Y}w\Big(\frac{n^{2}}{Y^{2}}\Big)e\Big({-\frac{\xi n^{2}}{Y^{2}}}\Big)-\widehat{w}(\xi)\Big|\ll\frac{q|\xi|}{Y}\max\big(\|w\|_{\infty},\|w^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\big)\ll\frac{q|\xi|}{Y}.

As in the proof of
Lemma

5.4
,

\widehat{g_{X,\Box}}\Big(\frac{a}{q}+\theta\Big)=2\operatorname{Re}\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant X^{1/2}}nw\Big(\frac{n^{2}}{X}\Big)e\Big({-\Big(\frac{a}{q}+\theta\Big)n^{2}}\Big).

Setting
Y:=X^{1/2}
and
\xi:=\theta X
and splitting into residue classes modulo
q
, we obtain

\widehat{g_{X,\Box}}\Big(\frac{a}{q}+\theta\Big)=\operatorname{Re}\sum_{b(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)}e\Big({-\frac{ab^{2}}{q}}\Big)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant n\leqslant Y\\
n\equiv b(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)\end{subarray}}2nw\Big(\frac{n^{2}}{Y^{2}}\Big)e\Big({-\frac{\xi n^{2}}{Y^{2}}}\Big).

By
5.4
,

\widehat{g_{X,\Box}}\Big(\frac{a}{q}+\theta\Big)=\frac{X}{q}\operatorname{Re}\Big(\widehat{w}(\xi)\sum_{b(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)}e\Big({-\frac{ab^{2}}{q}}\Big)\Big)+O(X^{1/2}q|\xi|).

To conclude, we apply the standard Gauss sum estimate

\Big|\sum_{b(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)}e\Big({-\frac{ab^{2}}{q}}\Big)\Big|\ll\sqrt{q},

which may be found (for example) as
[
5
, Lemma 6.4]
and the estimate on
\widehat{w}(\xi)
from
Lemma

5.1
.
∎

6.
Density increment arguments

As explained in the introduction, we will use the density increment strategy common to previous works on the problem. Suppose that
A\subset[X]
is a set with density
\alpha
and no square difference. We will establish a variety of different types of density increment, which we summarise in the following proposition.

Constants denoted by
c,c_{0},C
are absolute.

Proposition 6.1
.

There are constants
c,C
with
0<c<\frac{1}{10}
and
C>10
such that the following holds. Let
X\geqslant c^{-C}
and suppose that
X^{-1/100}<\alpha\leqslant\frac{2}{3}
. Suppose that
A\subset[X]
is a set with density
\alpha
and no square difference. Then in all cases

(
\ast
)

there is a subprogression of
[X]
with square common difference and length
\geqslant\alpha^{C}X
on which the density of
A
is at least
\alpha+\alpha^{C}
.

Moreover, if
\alpha\leqslant c
then at least one of the following options holds:

(1)

\alpha\leqslant\exp(-c\sqrt{\log X})
;

(2)

for some
m\in\mathbf{N}
, there is a subprogression
P
with square common difference and length
\geqslant\alpha^{Cm}X
on which the density of
A
is at least
2^{m}\alpha
;

(3)

there is a subprogression
P
with square common difference and length
\geqslant\alpha^{C}X
on which the density of
A
is at least
(1+c)\alpha
.

Remarks
.

The subprogressions in (
\ast
), (2) and (3) are all proper (that is, not equal to
[X]
) since the density of
A
on them is strictly larger than
\alpha
in each case. We could easily combine (2) and (3) into one statement (simply by replacing the constant
2
in (2) by
1+c
) and in fact we will do this in the proof that
Proposition

6.1
implies
Theorem

1.1
below. We have left them separate in the statement of
Proposition

6.1
to emphasise that they come from slightly different sources, as will become apparent later.

The proof of this proposition will occupy most of the rest of the paper. We now show that it implies
Theorem

1.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
, assuming
Proposition

6.1
.

Recall that
s(X)
denotes the size of the largest subset of
[X]
containing no square difference.
For notational brevity, in the following proof it is convenient to write

(6.1)

F(X):=(\log X)^{1/2}

for
X\geqslant 1
.
Suppose that we have the statement of
Proposition

6.1
. We will prove, by strong induction on
X
, that for a suitable positive constant
c_{0}
(which will depend on
c,C
) and for all
X\geqslant 1
we have

(6.2)

s(X)\leqslant X\exp(-c_{0}F(X)),

which is the statement of
Theorem

1.1
. If
1\leqslant X\leqslant c^{-C}
, this statement is trivial if one takes
c_{0}
small enough, so suppose from now on that
X\geqslant c^{-C}
and that we have established
6.2
for all
X^{\prime}<X
.

In several places in what follows, we will need the bound

(6.3)

F(X)-F(e^{-h}X)\leqslant h(\log X)^{-1/2}

for
h\leqslant\log X
. To see this, note that
F(X)-F(e^{-h}X)=(\log X)^{1/2}-(\log X-h)^{1/2}
. For
0\leqslant h\leqslant t
we have

t^{1/2}-(t-h)^{1/2}=t^{1/2}\Big(1-\Big(1-\frac{h}{t}\Big)^{1/2}\Big)\leqslant ht^{-1/2},

where the last step follows from
(1-x)^{1/2}\geqslant 1-x
for
0\leqslant x\leqslant 1
. From this, the bound
6.3
follows.

Let
A\subset[X]
be a set of density
\alpha=s(X)/X
such that
A
contains no square difference.
The aim is to show that

(6.4)

\alpha\leqslant\exp(-c_{0}F(X)).

In the following discussion,
P
will always denote a subprogression of
[X]
with square common difference, length
X^{\prime}<X
and on which the density of
A
is
\alpha^{\prime}>\alpha
.

Suppose first that
\alpha>c
. Observe that
\alpha\leqslant 2/3
as any
A\subseteq[X]
with
|A|>2X/3
contains a pair of consecutive elements and hence a square difference. Then as
\alpha\in(c,2/3]
we may apply
Proposition

6.1
(
\ast
) to find
P
with
X^{\prime}\geqslant\alpha^{C}X\geqslant e^{-h}X
and
\alpha^{\prime}\geqslant\alpha+\alpha^{C}\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)\alpha
, where
h:=C\log(1/c)
and
\varepsilon:=1+c^{C-1}
. By the inductive hypothesis,

(1+\varepsilon)\alpha\leqslant\alpha^{\prime}\leqslant\exp(-c_{0}F(X^{\prime}))\leqslant\exp(-c_{0}F(e^{-h}X)).

We wish to deduce
6.4
from this, which is equivalent to showing

F(X)-F(e^{-h}X)\leqslant\frac{\log(1+\varepsilon)}{c_{0}}

By
6.3
this holds provided that
c_{0}
is chosen sufficiently small. Note here that the condition
h\leqslant\log X
required for the application of
6.3
is satisfied because of the assumption that
X\geqslant c^{-C}
.

Suppose from now on that
\alpha\leqslant c
. We may assume that
\alpha>X^{-1/100}
, since otherwise
6.4
follows immediately. Then one of options
Proposition

6.1
(1) – (3) holds. If (1) holds, we are done provided that
c_{0}\leqslant c
(which we may assume). It remains to treat the cases (2) and (3). We can combine these into one by asserting that there is
P
with length
X^{\prime}\geqslant\alpha^{Cm}X
on which the density of
A
is at least
(1+c)^{m}
. Set
h:=Cm\log(1/\alpha)
. Since the density of
A
on
P
cannot exceed
1
, we have
m\leqslant\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{\log(1+c)}\leqslant\frac{2}{c}\log(1/\alpha)
and so
h\leqslant\frac{2C}{c}\log(1/\alpha)^{2}
. If
c_{0}\leqslant(c/2C)^{1/2}
, this implies that
h\leqslant\log X
unless
\alpha\leqslant\exp(-c_{0}(\log X)^{1/2})
, that is to say unless
6.4
holds. We may therefore assume in what follows that
h\leqslant\log X
.

By the inductive hypothesis,

(1+c)^{m}\alpha\leqslant\exp(-c_{0}F(X^{\prime}))\leqslant\exp(-c_{0}F(e^{-h}X)),

with
h
as above. Thus (
6.4
) follows if we can show that

(6.5)

F(X)-F(e^{-h}X)\leqslant\frac{m\log(1+c)}{c_{0}}.

By
6.3
, it is enough to show

\log(1/\alpha)\cdot(\log X)^{-1/2}\leqslant\frac{\log(1+c)}{c_{0}C}.

If
c_{0}\leqslant\big(\frac{\log(1+c)}{C}\big)^{1/2}
then this holds unless
\alpha\leqslant\exp(-c_{0}(\log X)^{1/2})
. Either way,
6.4
holds.

∎

6.1.
Fourier coefficients and density increments

Let
A\subseteq[X]
be a nonempty set of density
\alpha
with balanced function
f_{A}:=\mathbf{1}_{A}-\alpha 1_{[X]}
. In this section we recall some results stating that large Fourier coefficients of
f_{A}
allow one to find a long progression on which the density of
A
is increased.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\eta\in(0,1)
. Let
q\geqslant 1
be an integer, and let
\xi\in\mathbf{R}
. Set
T:=\max(1,|\xi|X)
. Suppose that either

(6.6)

\Big|\widehat{f_{A}}\Big(\frac{a}{q}+\xi\Big)\Big|\geqslant\eta\alpha X

for some
a\in\mathbf{Z}
or that

(6.7)

\sum_{a(\mbox{\scriptsize{mod}}\,q)}\Big|\widehat{f_{A}}\Big(\frac{a}{q}+\xi\Big)\Big|^{2}\geqslant\eta\alpha^{2}X^{2}.

Then there is a progression
P\subseteq[X]
with common difference
q^{2}
and length
\gg q^{-2}T^{-1}\eta X
on which the density of
A
is at least
(1+\eta/20)\alpha
.

Proof.

If
q^{2}T>2^{-10}\eta X
then the result is trivial (just take
P
to be a progression of length
1
consisting of a point of
A
). Suppose henceforth that

(6.8)

q^{2}T\leqslant 2^{-10}\eta X.

We now handle the two conditions
6.6
and
6.7
in turn, beginning with the former. In this case the lemma states the type of density increment used by Sárközy in his original paper
[
19
]
. Foliating into progressions to modulus
q
,
6.6
implies that

(6.9)

\sum_{b\in\mathbf{Z}/q\mathbf{Z}}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in[X]\\
n\equiv b(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)\end{subarray}}e(-\xi n)f_{A}(n)\Big|\geqslant\eta\alpha X.

Consider any partitioning of
[X]
into intervals of the following type: for each
b
, partition
\{n\in[X]:n\equiv b(\operatorname{mod}\,q)\}
into
k_{b}
progressions
I_{j,b}
,
j=1,\dots,k_{b}
of common difference
q^{2}
and diameter
\leqslant\frac{1}{32}T^{-1}\eta X
. Applying the triangle inequality to
6.9
gives

(6.10)

\sum_{b\in\mathbf{Z}/q\mathbf{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{b}}\Big|\sum_{n\in I_{j,b}}e(-\xi n)f_{A}(n)\Big|\geqslant\eta\alpha X.

For each
j,b
let
n_{j,b}
be an arbitrary point of
I_{j,b}
. The error in replacing each
e(\xi n)
by
e(\xi n_{j,b})
is at most

\sup_{j,b}\sup_{n\in I_{j,b}}|e(-\xi n)-e(-\xi n_{j,b})|\sum_{b\in\mathbf{Z}/q\mathbf{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{b}}\sum_{n\in I_{j,b}}|f_{A}(n)|\leqslant 2\pi|\xi|(\frac{1}{32}T^{-1}\eta X)\sum_{n\in[X]}|f_{A}(n)|<\frac{1}{2}\eta\alpha X.

It follows from this and
6.10
that

\sum_{b\in\mathbf{Z}/q\mathbf{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{b}}\Big|\sum_{n\in I_{j,b}}f_{A}(n)\Big|\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\eta\alpha X.

Thus, since
f_{A}(n)
sums to
0
on
[X]
, we have

\sum_{b\in\mathbf{Z}/q\mathbf{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{b}}\Big(\sum_{n\in I_{j,b}}f_{A}(n)+\Big|\sum_{n\in I_{j,b}}f_{A}(n)\Big|\Big)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\eta\alpha X.

Therefore, by pigeonhole there is some
I_{j,b}
on which

\sum_{n\in I_{j,b}}f_{A}(n)+\Big|\sum_{n\in I_{j,b}}f_{A}(n)\Big|\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\eta\alpha|I_{j,b}|

and hence

(6.11)

\sum_{n\in I_{j,b}}f_{A}(n)\geqslant\frac{1}{4}\eta\alpha|I_{j,b}|.

Now we specify a partitioning of the above form. First, partition
[X]
into intervals of length between
\frac{1}{64}T^{-1}\eta X
and
\frac{1}{32}T^{-1}\eta X
; on each one, and for each
b^{\prime}\in\mathbf{Z}/q^{2}\mathbf{Z}
, the set of elements with
n\equiv b^{\prime}(\operatorname{mod}\,q^{2})
is a progression of diameter
\leqslant\frac{1}{32}T^{-1}\eta X
and length
\gg q^{-2}T^{-1}\eta X
. The possibility of this partitioning and the last assertion both follow from
6.8
. From
6.11
, it follows that the density of
A
on at least one of these progressions is at least
(1+\eta/4)\alpha
, and this completes the analysis of
6.6
.

We now turn to the analysis of
6.7
. We remark that an equivalent statement to this is implicit in
[
16
]
. One may check by expanding and orthogonality of characters that is equal to

(6.12)

\sum_{a(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)}\Big|\widehat{f_{A}}\Big(\frac{a}{q}+\xi\Big)\Big|^{2}=q\sum_{b\in\mathbf{Z}/q\mathbf{Z}}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in[X]\\
n\equiv b(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)\end{subarray}}e(-\xi n)f_{A}(n)\Big|^{2},

so from the assumption
6.7
we have

(6.13)

\sum_{b\in\mathbf{Z}/q\mathbf{Z}}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in[X]\\
n\equiv b(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)\end{subarray}}e(-\xi n)f_{A}(n)\Big|^{2}\geqslant q^{-1}\eta\alpha^{2}X^{2}.

Suppose there is
b
such that

(6.14)

\Big|\sum_{n\equiv b(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)}e(-\xi n)f_{A}(n)\Big|\geqslant 5q^{-1}\alpha X.

Since

\Big|\sum_{n\equiv b(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)}e(-\xi n)f_{A}(n)\Big|\leqslant q^{-1}\alpha X+1+\sum_{n\equiv b(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)}\mathbf{1}_{A}(n),

it follows that

\sum_{n\equiv b(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)}\mathbf{1}_{A}(n)\geqslant 3q^{-1}\alpha X,

and so

has density at least
2\alpha
on the progression
\{n\in[X]:n\equiv b(\operatorname{mod}\,q)\}
. By a trivial averaging,
A
also has density at least
2\alpha
on some progression
\{n\in[X]:n\equiv b^{\prime}(\operatorname{mod}\,q^{2})\}
, which certainly implies the result. Suppose then that
6.14
does not hold for any

. Then
6.13
implies that

\sum_{b\in\mathbf{Z}/q\mathbf{Z}}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in[X]\\
n\equiv b(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,q)\end{subarray}}e(-\xi n)f_{A}(n)\Big|\geqslant\frac{1}{5}\eta\alpha X.

This is exactly

(albeit with
\eta

) and we may conclude as in the analysis of

.
∎

6.2.
First steps towards the density increment

We now proceed towards the proof of
Proposition

6.1
, which we will complete in the next section.
In what follows,
C_{1}
denotes an absolute constant whose exact nature arises from the implicit constants in
Section

5
; the conditions it needs to satisfy will be specified during the course of the proof of

6.3
below. We imagine that this constant is fixed, once and for all.

The aim is then to show that if further constants

with

are taken then, if

and

are sufficiently large, all the statements in
Proposition

6.1
are true.

With this in mind, suppose in what follows that
A\subset[X]
is a set with no square difference and density
\alpha
. Assume throughout that
X\geqslant c^{-C}
and that
X^{-1/100}<\alpha\leqslant\frac{2}{3}
, as in the statement of
Proposition

6.1
. In particular, we may always assume that
X
is sufficiently large, and we will do so without further comment. For notational brevity, we write

We define the major arcs
\mathfrak{M}\subset\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
by

(6.15)

\mathfrak{M}:=\bigcup_{q\leqslant C_{1}\alpha^{-2}}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\Big[\frac{a}{q}-\tau,\frac{a}{q}+\tau\Big],\quad\mbox{where}\quad\tau:=C_{1}L^{2}X^{-1}.

Set
\mathfrak{m}:=(\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z})\setminus\mathfrak{M}
.
Note that
\mathfrak{M}
and
\tau
both depend on
\alpha
, but we do not indicate this explicitly

Lemma 6.3
.

We have
\sup_{\xi\in\mathfrak{m}}|\widehat{g_{X,\Box}}(\xi)|\leqslant 2^{-9}\alpha X
.

Proof.

It is convenient to introduce some auxilliary ‘wide’ major arcs
\tilde{\mathfrak{M}}\subset\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
by

\tilde{\mathfrak{M}}:=\bigcup_{q\ll(\log X)^{2}\alpha^{-4}}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\Big[\frac{a}{q}-\tilde{\tau},\frac{a}{q}+\tilde{\tau}\Big],\quad\mbox{where}\quad\tilde{\tau}:=C_{1}(\log X)^{4}\alpha^{-8}X^{-1}.

Suppose that
|\widehat{g_{X,\Box}}(\xi)|\geqslant 2^{-9}\alpha X
. By
Lemma

5.4
, there is
q\ll(\log X)^{2}\alpha^{-4}
such that
\|q\xi\|_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}\ll(\log X)^{2}\alpha^{-4}X^{-1}
. If
C_{1}
is chosen appropriately large, this means that
\xi\in\tilde{\mathfrak{M}}
.

To analyse
\xi\in\tilde{\mathfrak{M}}
further, we use
Lemma

5.5
. By the previous paragraph, there is some
a\in\mathbf{Z}
such that if we write
\theta=\xi-a/q
then
|\theta|\leqslant\tilde{\tau}
. Applying
Lemma

5.5
with this

, the required condition
|\theta|\leqslant X^{-7/8}
is satisfied by the definition of
\tilde{\tau}
and the assumption that
\alpha>X^{-1/100}
(and since
X
is sufficiently large in terms of
C_{1}
). We conclude that

\alpha X\ll Xq^{-1/2}e^{-|\theta X|^{1/2}}+X^{3/4}.

The
X^{3/4}
term can be ignored since
\alpha>X^{-1/100}
, so we obtain
\alpha\ll q^{-1/2}e^{-|\theta X|^{1/2}}
, from which we deduce that both

(if
C_{1}
is chosen appropriately) and that

, and thus

. Thus (again, if
C_{1}
is chosen appropriately)

∎

Remarks
.

At this point, all the conditions
C_{1}
needed to satisfy have been specified. Introducing the two types of major arcs (or an equivalent device) is a well-known technique in the circle method, used to deduce a ‘log-free’ variant of Weyl’s inequality from the usual one, which is essentially what has been accomplished here. See for instance
[
21
]
. In our setting it also has the advantage that we can exploit the very smooth weight
w
and hence take the major arcs
\mathfrak{M}
to be rather narrow.

Now we turn to the main analysis. Observe that, from the assumption that
A
has no square difference,

(6.16)

\sum_{x,y}\mathbf{1}_{A}(x)\mathbf{1}_{A}(y)g_{X,\Box}(x-y)=0.

We may assume that

(6.17)

\sum_{x,y}\mathbf{1}_{A}(x)\mathbf{1}_{[X]}(y)g_{X,\Box}(x-y)\geqslant 2^{-8}\alpha X^{2}.

To see this, note that
x-y
is supported on
\pm t^{2}
,
t^{2}\leqslant X
, by the definition of
g_{X,\Box}
. Thus if
6.17
fails, we have

\sum_{x}\sum_{1\leqslant t\leqslant X^{1/2}}\mathbf{1}_{A}(x)1_{[X]}(x-t^{2})tw(t^{2}/X)\leqslant 2^{-8}\alpha X^{2}

(or an essentially identical case with
+t^{2}
, which we omit). That is,

\sum_{1\leqslant t\leqslant X^{1/2}}tw(t^{2}/X)\big|A\cap[X-t^{2}]\big|\leqslant 2^{-8}\alpha X^{2}.

In particular, by the support properties of
w
(given in
Lemma

5.1
) it follows that

\sum_{X^{1/2}/4\leqslant t\leqslant X^{1/2}/2}t\big|A\cap[X-t^{2}]\big|\leqslant 2^{-7}\alpha X^{2},

whence there is some
t\in[\frac{1}{4}X^{1/2},\frac{1}{2}X^{1/2}]
such that
|A\cap[X-t^{2}]|\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\alpha X\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\alpha(X-t^{2})
, which implies (after a short calculation) that
|A\cap(X-t^{2},X]|\geqslant\frac{3}{2}\alpha t^{2}
. That is,
A
has density at least
3\alpha/2
on the interval
(X-t^{2},X]
, which has length at least
3X/4
. This is an enormous density increment for
A
, much better than both
Proposition

6.1
(
\ast
) and
Proposition

6.1
(3).

Therefore
6.17
does hold. Writing
f_{A}:=\mathbf{1}_{A}-\alpha\mathbf{1}_{[X]}
for the balanced function of
A
, it follows from
6.16
and
6.17
that we have

\Big|\sum_{x,y}\mathbf{1}_{A}(x)f_{A}(y)g_{X,\Box}(x-y)\Big|\geqslant 2^{-8}\alpha^{2}X^{2}.

Taking Fourier transforms, this implies that

(6.18)

\Big|\int_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{A}}(-\xi)\widehat{f_{A}}(\xi)\widehat{g_{X,\Box}}(\xi)d\xi\Big|\geqslant 2^{-8}\alpha^{2}X^{2}.

By Cauchy-Schwarz, Parseval and
Lemma

6.3
we have

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\mathfrak{m}}\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{A}}(-\xi)\widehat{f_{A}}(\xi)\widehat{g_{X,\Box}}(\xi)d\xi\Big|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{\xi\in\mathfrak{m}}|\widehat{g_{X,\Box}}(\xi)|\Big(\int_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}|\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{A}}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\int_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}|\widehat{f_{A}}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{-9}\alpha^{2}X^{2}/2,

so from
6.18
we have

(6.19)

\Big|\int_{\mathfrak{M}}\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{A}}(\xi)\widehat{f_{A}}(-\xi)\widehat{g_{X,\Box}}(\xi)d\xi\Big|\geqslant 2^{-9}\alpha^{2}X^{2}.

Write

(6.20)

\mathfrak{M}:=[-\tau,\tau]\cup\mathfrak{M}^{\prime},\quad\mbox{where}\quad\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}:=\bigcup_{2\leqslant q\leqslant C_{1}\alpha^{-2}}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\Big[\frac{a}{q}-\tau,\frac{a}{q}+\tau\Big].

Here

is as in the definition
6.15
of the major arcs.
From
6.19
we have either

(6.21)

\Big|\int^{\tau}_{-\tau}\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{A}}(\xi)\widehat{f_{A}}(-\xi)\widehat{g_{X,\Box}}(\xi)d\xi\Big|\geqslant 2^{-10}\alpha^{2}X^{2}

or

(6.22)

\Big|\int_{\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}}\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{A}}(\xi)\widehat{f_{A}}(-\xi)\widehat{g_{X,\Box}}(\xi)d\xi\Big|\geqslant 2^{-10}\alpha^{2}X^{2}.

We handle these two cases slightly differently.

Case 1:

6.21

\sup_{|\xi|\leqslant\tau}|\widehat{f_{A}}(\xi)|\int^{\tau}_{-\tau}|\widehat{g_{X,\Box}}(\xi)|d\xi\geqslant 2^{-10}\alpha X.

Now from

5.5
with
(a,q)=(0,1)
(noting from the definition

Therefore

To this conclusion, we apply

Case 2:

7.
Applying the level

Our task now is to obtain a density increment as in
Proposition

6.1
from

Proposition

6.1
(
\ast
) for all densities
\alpha\leqslant\frac{2}{3}
. The remaining statements of

concern the case where

is sufficiently small, so we are free to assume this, and consequently that

is sufficiently large, in all that follows.

We will accomplish this task in the next two sections. Our main input will be
Theorem

1.2
, and in this section we develop our main tool for applying it to

, which is

below.

The analysis involves a rather careful division of the set of

according to properties of their prime factorizations, and we begin in the next subsection by setting up appropriate nomenclature.

Remark
.

The reader may be interested to know that, although the final argument is not especially long, it was very far from obvious to us. Indeed, the set of exponents in our main theorem that we have at some point alighted upon during the development of this work is roughly
\{c,\frac{3}{14},\frac{1}{4}-o(1),\frac{3}{11},\frac{2}{7},\frac{3}{10},\frac{1}{3}-o(1),\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{5},\frac{1}{2}-o(1),\frac{1}{2}\}
. A detailed proof of the exponent
\frac{1}{4}-o(1)
may be found in the first arXiv version of the paper
[
7
]
.

7.1.
Basic setup and parameters

Suppose that
q\in\mathbf{N}
has prime factorization
q=\prod_{p}p^{v_{p}}
. Then
q=q_{*}q^{\prime}
where
q_{*}:=\prod_{p:v_{p}\geqslant 3}p^{v_{p}}
and
q^{\prime}:=\prod_{p:v_{p}\leqslant 2}p^{v_{p}}
. We call
q_{*}
and
q^{\prime}
the cube-full and cube-free parts of
q
respectively. Note that
(q_{*},q^{\prime})=1
.

Observe for later use that

(7.1)

\sum_{t\;{\textnormal{cube-full}}}t^{-1/2}\leqslant\prod_{p}\Big(1+\sum_{k\geqslant 3}\frac{1}{p^{k/2}}\Big)\ll 1;

the fact that this inequality holds is the reason for using the notion of cube-free, rather than the more familiar notion of square-free, as we will see in the analysis that follows.

Throughout the next two sections, let
W
be a sufficiently large absolute constant. How large it needs to be will be determined at the end of
Section

8.2
. Let
L=\log(1/\alpha)
as usual. Given
q\in\mathbf{N}
and its cube-free part
q^{\prime}
:

•

define
k(q^{\prime})
to be
1
plus the number of distinct prime factors of
q^{\prime}
which are
>WL
;

•

let
d(q^{\prime})
be the product of all prime factors of
q^{\prime}
which are
\leqslant WL
(with multiplicity, which is
1
or
2
for each prime occurring);

•

let
p_{2}(q^{\prime})<\cdots<p_{k}(q^{\prime})
be the prime factors of
q^{\prime}
which are
>WL
.

Write

(7.2)

\mathcal{D}:=(\log L)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{\geqslant 0}.

For
t\in\mathbf{N}
,
k\in\mathbf{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
and
\vec{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k})\in\mathcal{D}^{k}
, define

\mathscr{Q}_{t,k,\vec{\beta}}:=\{q\in\mathbf{N}:2\leqslant q\leqslant C_{1}\alpha^{-2},\;q_{*}=t,\;k(q^{\prime})=k,\;d(q^{\prime})\sim L^{\beta_{1}},\;p_{i}(q^{\prime})\sim L^{\beta_{i}}\;\mbox{for $i=2,\dots,k$}\}.

Here, and in what follows,
x\sim L^{\beta}
means that
L^{\beta}\leqslant x<eL^{\beta}=L^{\beta+(\log L)^{-1}}
. Note that, since the
\beta_{i}
range over
\mathcal{D}
, the sets
\mathscr{Q}_{t,k,\vec{\beta}}
form a partition of the set of all integer
q
with
2\leqslant q\leqslant C_{1}\alpha^{-2}
, that is to say the set of all
q
in
6.25
.
Observe that all but finitely many of the
\mathscr{Q}_{t,k,\vec{\beta}}
are empty. More precisely, we may suppose throughout that

(7.3)

t\leqslant C_{1}\alpha^{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad k\leqslant 2L/\log L.

The second bound here follows from the fact that
L
is sufficiently large and the observation that if
k(q^{\prime})=k
then
q
has
k
distinct prime factors and so is certainly at least the product of the first
k
primes, which is
e^{(1+o(1))k\log k}
by the prime number theorem.
Denoting

(7.4)

B_{k}:=\{\vec{\beta}\in\mathcal{D}^{k}:0\leqslant\beta_{1},\;1\leqslant\beta_{2}\leqslant\beta_{3}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\beta_{k},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mbox{$L^{\beta_{i}}\leqslant C_{1}\alpha^{-2}$}\}

we may assume that
\vec{\beta}\in B_{k}
since, again, if this is not so then the corresponding set
\mathscr{Q}_{t,k,\vec{\beta}}
is empty.

7.2.
An arithmetic energy bound

Let
A\subset[X]
be a set, as in the statement of
Lemma

6.2
. Let
\xi\in\mathbf{R}
be a real number with
|\xi|\leqslant\tau=C_{1}\alpha^{-2}
(as for example in
6.25
). Define

(7.5)

E_{t,k,\vec{\beta}}:=\sum_{q\in\mathscr{Q}_{t,k,\vec{\beta}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\Big|\widehat{1_{A}}\Big(\frac{a}{q}+\xi\Big)\Big|^{2}.

We suppress the dependence on
\xi
in the notation. The following estimate will be our main application of
Theorem

1.2
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Suppose that
1\leqslant r\leqslant k
. If
r\geqslant 2
, suppose additionally that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}\leqslant 4k
. Then either
A
enjoys one of the density increments in
Proposition

6.1
, or else

E_{t,k,\vec{\beta}}\ll\Big(\frac{32r}{W}\Big)^{r-1}\Big(\frac{C_{0}}{k-r}\Big)^{k-r}L^{(\beta_{2}+\cdots+\beta_{r})+k-2r+1}\alpha^{2}X^{2},

where the implied constant is absolute. Here, one should interpret
\big(\frac{C_{0}}{k-r}\big)^{k-r}
as equal to
1
in the case
r=k
.

Before turning to the proof, we establish some notation and basic results concerning restrictions of
A
to subprogressions. Let
Q\geqslant 1
be an integer and suppose that
b\in\{0,1\dots Q-1\}
. We define the set
A_{Q,b}
by

(7.6)

1_{A_{Q,b}}(x^{\prime}):=1_{A}(b+Qx^{\prime}).

Since
A
is supported on
[X]
,
A_{Q,b}
is supported on
0\leqslant x^{\prime}\leqslant\lfloor X/Q\rfloor
. We will use the following identity involving the Fourier coefficients of
A_{Q,b}
, which is essentially the same as
6.12
:

(7.7)

\sum_{\lambda=0}^{Q-1}\Big|\widehat{1_{A}}\Big(\frac{\lambda}{Q}+\theta\Big)\Big|^{2}=Q\sum_{b=0}^{Q-1}\Big|\widehat{1_{A_{Q,b}}}(Q\theta)\Big|^{2}.

To verify the identity, we start with the RHS. We have

\displaystyle\widehat{1_{A_{Q,b}}}(Q\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}}1_{A}(b+Qx^{\prime})e(-Q\theta x^{\prime})=\sum_{x}1_{A}(x)1_{x\equiv b(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,Q)}e\Big({-Q\theta\Big(\frac{x-b}{Q}\Big)}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}1_{A}(x)\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\lambda=0}^{Q-1}e\Big({-\frac{\lambda(x-b)}{Q}}\Big)e\big({-\theta(x-b)}\big)=\frac{1}{Q}e\big(\theta b\big)\sum_{\lambda=0}^{Q-1}\widehat{1_{A}}\Big(\frac{\lambda}{Q}+\theta\Big)e\Big(\frac{\lambda b}{Q}\Big).

Therefore

\displaystyle Q\sum_{b=0}^{Q-1}\Big|\widehat{1_{A_{Q,b}}}(Q\theta)\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\lambda=0}^{Q-1}\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}=0}^{Q-1}\widehat{1_{A}}\Big(\frac{\lambda}{Q}+\theta\Big)\overline{\widehat{1_{A}}\Big(\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{Q}+\theta\Big)}\sum_{b=0}^{Q-1}e\Big(\frac{(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})b}{Q}\Big),

8.1.
Discarding large parameters

Before turning to the main analysis, we first dispense with the case

Lemma 8.1

Suppose that

We will use

from

We isolate the following lemma from the proof.

Lemma 8.2
.

The number

of tuples

9.
Limitations of the density increment approach

We end with discussion of an example which limits the density increment method to bounds of quasi–polynomial shape.

Proposition 9.1
.

Let
X
be a large integer parameter and let
\eta>0
. Suppose that

\exp(-(\log X)^{3/4-\eta})\leqslant\alpha\leqslant\alpha_{0}(\eta),

where
\alpha_{0}(\eta)
is a sufficiently small absolute constant. Then there is a function
f:[X]\rightarrow[0,1]
with the following properties:

(1)

\mathbbm{E}_{x\in[X]}f(x)=\alpha
;

(2)

the sum of
f(x)f(y)
over all
x,y\in[X]
with
x-y
a square is at most
\frac{1}{100}\alpha^{2}X^{3/2}
;

(3)

the length
X^{\prime}
of the longest subprogression of
[X]
on which
f
has density
\geqslant 2\alpha
satisfies
X^{\prime}<X\exp(-\log(1/\alpha)^{1/3})
.

Remarks
.

By a random sampling argument one can show the existence of a set
A\subset[X]
such that the characteristic function
\mathbf{1}_{A}
has similar properties to the function
f
; we omit the detailed statement and proof.

We interpret this result as saying that the density increment method cannot give a bound on
s(X)
superior to
s(X)\ll X\exp(-c(\log X)^{3/4})
at least in anything like the form in the present paper (and in previous works). Let us make a couple of points to support this view.

First of all we make the comment that from the perspective of Fourier analysis, the statement that a set
A
of density
\alpha
has
\leqslant\frac{1}{100}\alpha^{2}X^{3/2}
square differences is just as good as the assumption that
A
contains no square differences at all: to be more specific, the analysis starting at
6.16
would work just as well with this assumption.

Our second point is that if one takes the statement ‘there is a subprogression of length
X^{\prime}=X\exp(-\log(1/\alpha)^{1/3})
on which the density of
A
is at least
2\alpha
’ and then uses this in a density increment argument (or equivalently an inductive argument along the lines of
Proposition

6.1
) one ends up proving a bound
s(X)<X\exp(-c(\log X)^{3/4})
(and no better). We leave the details of this check to the reader.

Proof.

To begin the construction, let
p
be a prime with
p\equiv 1(\operatorname{mod}\,4)
. For a parameter
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
to be specified shortly, and define
\psi_{p}:\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow[0,1]
by

\psi_{p}(x):=(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\big(1-\varepsilon\cos(2\pi sx/p)\big),

where
s
is a quadratic nonresidue modulo
p
. We note that

\widehat{\psi_{p}}(r)=\mathbbm{E}_{x\in\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z}}\psi_{p}(x)e\Big({-\frac{rx}{p}}\Big)=(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\Big(\mathbf{1}_{r=0}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathbf{1}_{r=s}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathbf{1}_{r=-s}\Big)

for all
r\in\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z}
.
Note also that from standard Gauss sum estimates (see for instance
[
9
, Equation (3.29)]
) we have

\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{\Box}}(s)=\frac{1}{2p}\Big(1+\left(\frac{s}{p}\right)\sqrt{p}\Big)=\frac{1-\sqrt{p}}{2p},

where
\Box
denotes the set of squares modulo
p
(including zero). We therefore compute that if

(9.1)

\varepsilon\geqslant 4p^{-1/4}

then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x,y\in\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z}}

\displaystyle\psi_{p}(x)\psi_{p}(y)\mathbf{1}_{\Box}(x-y)=\sum_{r}\big|\widehat{\psi_{p}}(r)\big|^{2}\widehat{1_{\Box}}(r)=\big|\widehat{\psi_{p}}(0)\big|^{2}\Big(\widehat{1_{\Box}}(0)+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\widehat{1_{\Box}}(s)\Big)

(9.2)

\displaystyle=\big(\mathbbm{E}\psi_{p}\big)^{2}\Big(\frac{p+1}{2p}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\Big(\frac{1-\sqrt{p}}{2p}\Big)\Big)\leqslant\big(\mathbbm{E}\psi_{p}\big)^{2}\frac{1}{2}\Big(1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8\sqrt{p}}\Big),

where we used
9.1
in the last step.
The next step is to take a product of such functions over a set of primes
p
. Let
C
be some large absolute constant to be specified later. Let
T\geqslant C^{4}
be a parameter such that if we set

(9.3)

\varepsilon:=C(\log T)^{1/2}T^{-1/4}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad M:=\lfloor T/4\log T\rfloor

then

\alpha\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)^{-M}\leqslant 2\alpha.

It is easy to see that (if
\alpha
is small enough) there will be such a parameter and that we will have

(9.4)

T^{3/4}(\log T)^{-1/2}\asymp\log(1/\alpha).

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of
M
primes in
[T,2T]
, all
1(\operatorname{mod}\,4)
. Such a set will exist if
\alpha
is sufficiently small (and hence
T
is sufficiently large). Let
N:=\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}}p
. Taking a product of the functions
\psi_{p}
(and applying the Chinese remainder theorem), we obtain a function
\psi_{N}:\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow[0,1]
such that, using
9.2
, we have

(9.5)

\mathbb{E}_{x,y\in\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z}}\psi_{N}(x)\psi_{N}(y)\mathbf{1}_{\Box}(x-y)\leqslant 2^{-M}(\mathbb{E}\psi_{N})^{2}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\Big(1-\frac{C^{2}\log T}{8(pT)^{1/2}}\Big)\leqslant 2^{-M}(\mathbb{E}\psi_{N})^{2}e^{-C^{2}/64}.

Here,
\Box
denotes the squares modulo
N
; note also that the condition
9.1
required for
9.2
to hold is satisfied. Note that

(9.6)

\mathbbm{E}\psi_{N}=(1+\varepsilon)^{-M}\in[\alpha,2\alpha].

Let us also note that
N\leqslant 4^{2T}
so, in view of
9.4
and the assumption that
\alpha>\exp(-(\log X)^{3/4-\eta})
, we have
N<X^{1/10}
provided that
\alpha
is sufficiently small.

Finally we come to the definition of the function
f
itself. We define

f(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}c\psi_{N}(x(\operatorname{mod}\,N))&x\leqslant N\lfloor X/N\rfloor\\
\alpha&N\lfloor X/N\rfloor<x\leqslant X.\end{array}\right.

where
c\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
is chosen so that
c\mathbbm{E}\psi_{N}=\alpha
(which is possible by
9.6
). By construction, we have
\mathbbm{E}_{[X]}f=\alpha
, which is property (1) in the proposition.

Turning to (2), we abuse notation by writing
\Box
for the set of squares in
\mathbf{N}
(retaining the earlier use of the same symbol for the squares in
\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z}
). We wish to bound the sum

S:=\sum_{x,y\in[X]}f(x)f(y)\mathbf{1}_{\Box}(x-y).

The contribution to
S
from
x
or
y
larger than
N\lfloor X/N\rfloor
is
\ll NX
(ignoring the constraint
x-y\in\Box
completely) which is negligible compared to
\alpha^{2}X^{3/2}
by the assumption on
\alpha
and the bound
N<X^{1/10}
. For the remaining portion of the sum, we have

(9.7)

\sum_{1\leqslant x,y\leqslant N\lfloor X/N\rfloor}f(x)f(y)\mathbf{1}_{\Box}(x-y)=c^{2}\sum_{a,b\in\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z}}\psi_{N}(a)\psi_{N}(b)\mathbf{1}_{\Box}(a-b)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant x,y\leqslant N\lfloor X/N\rfloor\\
x\equiv a(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,N)\\
y\equiv b(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,N)\end{subarray}}\mathbf{1}_{\Box}(x-y).

For fixed
y
, the inner sum over
1\leqslant x\leqslant N\lfloor X/N\rfloor
is bounded above by the number of squares in
[X]
which are congruent to
a-b(\operatorname{mod}\,N)
. Such squares must be
x^{2}
with
x\equiv t(\operatorname{mod}\,N)
, where
t
is one of the square roots of
a-b(\operatorname{mod}\,N)
, of which there are at most
2^{M}
. Thus for fixed
y
the inner sum over
x
in
9.7
is
\ll 2^{M}(X^{1/2}/N)
. For fixed
b
, there are
\ll X/N
choices of
y
. Summing over these, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant x,y\leqslant N\lfloor X/N\rfloor}f(x)f(y)\mathbf{1}_{\Box}(x-y)

\displaystyle\ll 2^{M}X^{3/2}N^{-2}\sum_{a,b\in\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z}}\psi_{N}(a)\psi_{N}(b)\mathbf{1}_{\Box}(a-b)

\displaystyle=2^{M}X^{3/2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z}}\psi_{N}(a)\psi_{N}(b)\mathbf{1}_{\Box}(a-b).

By
9.5
and
9.6
it follows that

\sum_{1\leqslant x,y\leqslant N\lfloor X/N\rfloor}f(x)f(y)\mathbf{1}_{\Box}(x-y)\ll\alpha^{2}X^{3/2}e^{-C^{2}/64}.

Taking
C
sufficiently large, we see that
S\leqslant\frac{1}{100}\alpha^{2}X^{3/2}
, which is what we wanted to prove.

Finally we turn to (3). We first consider the case that
P
is a maximal subprogression of
[X]
whose common difference is a product
p_{1}\cdots p_{m}
of distinct primes from
\mathcal{P}
.
By inspection of the construction one may see that the average of
\psi_{N}
over any congruence class modulo
p_{1}\cdots p_{m}
is at most
(1+\varepsilon)^{m-M}
. Thus, by the construction of
f
we see that the average of
f
over
P
is at most
(1+\varepsilon)^{m}\alpha
. Now let
P
be an arbitrary subprogression of
[X]
, of length
X^{\prime}
. It is contained in a maximal subprogression
\tilde{P}
of the above form, and unless
X^{\prime}
is already very small, the averages of
f
on
P
and on
\tilde{P}
are very close; we certainly have that the average of
f
on
P
is bounded above by
\frac{3}{2}(1+\varepsilon)^{m}\alpha
.

Thus we see that in order to double the average of
f
from
\alpha
to
2\alpha
, we must take
(1+\varepsilon)^{m}\geqslant\frac{4}{3}
, which means that

X^{\prime}\leqslant XT^{-m}\leqslant X\big(\frac{4}{3}\big)^{-\log T/\log(1+\varepsilon)}<X\exp(-T^{1/4})<X\exp(-\log(1/\alpha)^{1/3}),

where here we used the definition
9.3
of
\varepsilon
and
9.4
, and the fact that
T,1/\alpha
are sufficiently large.∎

We remark that a minor modification of this construction shows that the density increment argument cannot give better than quasipolynomial bounds for related (but seemingly easier) questions such as locating a non-zero square in
2A-2A
.

In this appendix we deduce
