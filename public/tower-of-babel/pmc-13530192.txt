Free electron topological bound state induced by a light beam with a twisted wavefront
Authors: Yiming Pan, Ruoyu Yin, Yongcheng Ding, Huaiqiang Wang, Daniel Podolsky, Bin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent advances in ultrafast electron emission, microscopy, and diffraction have demonstrated a remarkable ability to manipulate free electrons with quantum coherence using light beams. Here, we present a framework for exploring free electron topological charge in ultrafast electron-light interactions. We derive an explicit Jackiw-Rebbi solution for a low-energy free electron wavefunction subjected to a spatiotemporally twisted laser field, resulting in a flying topologically protected bound state with a topological charge of e/2. This flying bound state is dispersion-free due to its topological nature. We demonstrate the topological confinement and pair generation mechanism of topological charges in free space, expanding their domain beyond the topological states typically found in solids and photonics. This advancement enhances our understanding of emulating exotic quantum and topological effects with low-energy free electrons.Subject terms: Matter waves and particle beams, Ultrafast photonics

Introduction

Modern physics allows fractional charges. There are two primary approaches to explore such exotic existence. One method examines the topological structure of the massive Dirac equation, initially demonstrated by Jackiw-Rebbi solutions in quantum field theory1. The other delves into many-body interactions in condensed matter systems, exemplified by Laughlin’s wave function in the fractional Quantum Hall Effect (FQHE)2. Both realizations require specific materials such as polyacetylene3, semiconductors4, and artificial disclinations5–8. Traditionally, electrons were thought to carry only integer electric charges. However, in 1976, Jackiw and Rebbi challenged this conventional notion by proposing zero-energy bound states arising from the Dirac equation with a kink-profiled mass term. They argued that these zero-energy modes carry fractional charges, specifically e/21, due to their topological nature. This prediction was confirmed by Su, Schrieffer and Heeger’s discovery in Polyacetylene in 19793. Further evidence came from the FQHE9, where a bunch of composite quasiparticles exhibited fractional charges.

Recently, numerous proposals and experiments are actively exploring fractional charges as topological bound states in quantum materials10–12 and topological photonics with disclinations13,14. However, the detection of topological charges in materials and devices remains indirect, relying on methods such as local density of states, shot noise measurement, edge state current, and Aharonov-Bohm oscillations4,15–19. Direct utilization of these topological charges is still far from being feasible. These intriguing developments raise a compelling question: Is it possible to construct material-free topological states with free electrons, and if so, can we manipulate the flying bound state?

It is noteworthy that our proposal of a free electron bound state aligns well with the advancing frontier of ultrafast physics. Recent breakthroughs in ultrafast free electrons have revolutionized electron microscopy20, diffraction21,22, and even free electron radiation and lasing23,24. Electron beams have achieved extremely high temporal resolution on the femtosecond and attosecond scales25–27. However, a significant challenge remains for low-energy electrons: the nonrelativistic dispersion and propagation chirping of ultrashort electron pulses, even in a vacuum. For instance, slow electrons operating at an energy of 100 eV are not immune to dispersion. Understanding and controlling the dispersion effect is crucial for harnessing and characterizing ultrafast electron pulses in innovative experiments, as recently demonstrated by Refs. 12,28–30. Electron wavefunction engineering holds enormous potential, offering deeper insights into the quantum realm and paving the way for groundbreaking technological advances12,31–33. Considering the possibility of generating and manipulating free electron topological charges, our focus now shifts to recent developments in low-energy electron wavefunction and quantum light interaction. These advancements have unveiled fascinating dispersion effects, such as free electron Rabi oscillations and self-trapping in energy space34–38.

Here, we present a pioneering strategy that leverages the dispersion of wide spectral sidebands to construct a pulsed “two-level” electron wavefunction, involving only two photon-induced sidebands as a spinor (see Fig. 1b)34. This Dirac-type interaction arises from the coupling of a slow electron pulse with a monochromatic light field with a spatiotemporally twisted wavefront, mimicking the kink profile of the mass term in the celebrated Jackiw-Rebbi model1. The resulting light-induced kink yields a topological bound state of free electron with zero energy, with fractional charge of e/2. This flying topological bound state propagates in a dispersion-free manner, a striking feature for manipulating low-energy slow electron pulses using a light beam. Additionally, we propose a time-of-flight streaking experiment to demonstrate the topological confinement and pair generation of these light-induced topological charges.

To achieve and implement the Jackiw-Rebbi solution, we employ a laser beam with a phase profile modulated by a Spatial Light Modulator (SLM). As illustrated in Fig. 2, the femtosecond laser beam is split into two paths. If we apply a twisted beam, a solitary wave carrying a topological charge of e/2 (from the viewpoint of topological insulator41) “surfs” on the kink structure of the twisted wavefront. Also, instead of a step-function-type mass term, we choose a comoving kink profile as κ(ξ)=−∣κ∣tanh(ξ), and the profile of the solitary solution is proportional to sech(ξ), exhibiting light-induced non-spreading confinement (compared to Eq. 32 in Methods).

Specifically, the local domain wall carries a topological charge of ±e/2, with the sign determined by the phase profile difference between boundary values, θ+∞−θ−∞. We confine that in the single-particle picture the fractional charge is interpreted as the topological nature arising from the domain wall defined by the twisted phase front. The normalization of the electron density remains unity, while its wavefunction can scatter into both zero modes and bulk. Splitting electrons into topological charge is a pivotal concept in many-body framework, revolving around the pair generation and annihilation of topological charge, with each kink contributing an increment of e/2 to the overall charge, resulting in one electron. Notably, splitting an electron into two topological charges and splitting its single-particle wavefunction into two components is subtle but critical. At the single-particle level, our system cannot distinguish whether an electron is divided into two topological charges or two wavefunction components. The topological charges separated by a pair of kink-antikink are not independent physical entities but represent the fractional charges associated with the topological bound states at the domain walls. It is worth mentioning that in the celebrated SSH model, the two fractional charges originating from anomaly are located at the boundaries in an inversion-symmetric manner43. Note that the light-induced topological charge goes beyond a simple combination of “a kink and a fermion”. It arises from the interaction between electromagnetic fields and free electrons, akin to the topological soliton in polyacetylene when Bloch electrons resonantly interact with phonons. The topological charge, a flying bound state, combines aspects of bosonic and Dirac-type fermionic fields. To avoid complications in interpreting how light splits one electron into two fractional charges, we view these charges purely as topological bound states possessing half the degrees of freedom of a “normal” electric charge. This is similar to the fractional excitations that host a fraction of the total number of states demonstrated in topological photonic materials7,8. This interpretation clarifies the nature of the flying bound state as a novel light-induced topological phenomenon.

Discussion

Due to the topological nature of the free electron bound state, its confined temporal profile depends on the specific spatiotemporal configuration of the twisted light wavefront (Eq. 7 and Eq. 9). This light-induced localized temporal profile can be indirectly measured through a magnetic spectrometer and streaking experiments, such as time-of-flight detector. The dispersion-free propagation of the flying bound states, unlike that of slow electrons, allows for experimental calibration of signals indicating strong confinement (or dispersion) by switching the light field on (or off). However, the existence of the topological bound state depends on the twisted optical field, and exhibits significant dispersion once the modulation light is turned off. This topological bound state serves as a promising light-induced quantum probe, opening pathways for new investigations in light-matter interactions and material characterization with ultrafast electrons. The stability and dispersion immunity of this state offer advantages for applications requiring high temporal resolution, such as low-energy electron diffraction (LEED) or free-free electron transition systems. These aspects, while beyond the scope of the study, highlight the broader impact of our work.

Furthermore, this topological charge leverages a ‘clean quantumness’ to advance quantum simulations using low energy electron wavepackets, where the free electron would not suffer from the complex interaction environment with atomic nuclei, phonons and other excitations like bound electrons do. Free electrons, fundamental since the inception of quantum mechanics, exhibit manipulatable coherence and interact readily with electromagnetic waves. This allows fine-tuning of their dispersion and coupling, as demonstrated by the creation of flying bound states (Figs. 3 and 4). Echoing previous work44, our results show that manipulating quantum light interacting with electrons can profoundly shape the latter. Recent advances have enabled information transfer between light and electrons within a photon-electron-entangled system45. Our proposal, particularly constructing an effective Dirac equation and free electron topological bound state through a twisted light beam, signifies a novel method for quantum simulations46–50. This approach, using a femtosecond laser to control low energy electrons, could revolutionize our understanding and manipulation of exotic quantum systems.

In brief, we have constructed the Jackiw-Rebbi solution for low energy free electrons using a spatiotemporally-twisted laser field, discovering a topological fractional charge of e/2. This light-induced quantum state exhibits a topological nature. Our work elucidates the emergence of a flying topological bound state in vacuum rather than in material. Our free-electron construction has profound implications, extending the domain of exotic topological excitations beyond conventional material realms. We hope that such entities could serve as novel sources and applications for ultrafast electron diffraction, microscopy and spectroscopy.

Additionally, our work suggests a paradigm shift in studying the topological physics of free electrons, utilizing a material-free approach. By employing multiphoton processes, such as photon-induced near-field electron microscopy (PINEM)20, we are able to simulate the phenomena of bound electrons in a solid using free electrons on the PINEM synthetic lattice, such as Bloch oscillation38 and Rabi oscillation34–36. Hence, we open avenues to explore phenomena traditionally reserved for condensed matter physics within ultrafast free electron systems. This framework allows free electrons to emulate bound (or quasi-free) electrons in solids, trapped by laser fields rather than atomic potentials, achievable through all-optical control. Consequently, our construction holds promise for an emerging direction of “free-electron condensed matter physics”, complementing recent studies on free-electron synthetic dimensions35,36,38. Further exploration of space-charge interactions within multi-electron beams51,52 may reveal new possibilities for emulating fantastic physics such as strongly correlated systems.

Fractional charge and anomaly inflow using the Goldstone-Wilczek approach

To explain the fractional topological charge for free electrons in the absence of the concept of atomic limit or Wannier centers in the typical condensed matter system, here we first present the anomaly inflow mechanism in the context of quantum field theory or many-body systems, and then interpret the topological charge as the Jackiw-Rebbi solution arising from the 1D effective massive Dirac equation in the single-particle quantum mechanical picture. This anomaly inflow mechanism provides a framework to understand the concepts of topological charges and conservation laws in continuous systems without crystalline lattices. We note that the charge density (ρx,t) is localized around the domain wall and vanishes far from it. Remarkably, the total fractional charge Q depends only on the global phase difference (θ+∞−θ−∞) far away from the local kink and not on the local configuration of the mass term. This topological charge Q=e/2 is the fractional quantum number first proposed by Jackiw and Rebbi1. Moreover, the driven fractional charge can be tuned arbitrarily by adjusting the optical phase of both sides via an SLM device. In addition, when the phase profile θx,t of the twisted light is spatiotemporally variable, an anomalous conservation arises between fractional charge and anomalous current: ∂μjμ=∇∙J⃗+∂ρ/∂t=0. This phenomenon is referred to as anomaly inflow, or the Callan-Harvey mechanism53. Detailed discussions of this mechanism lie beyond the scope of the presented work. Notably, a recent implementation of anomaly inflow was reported in laser direct-written photonic lattices54. The physics of this anomalous current in our setup will be explored in future studies.

It is important to emphasize that interpreting fractional charges in a single-particle picture is challenging. However, one can directly obtain the fractional charge density via the fully quantum mechanical Jackiw-Rebbi solution. As Wilczek noted55, the essential distinction of fractional charge lies in its non-local excitation. In other words, while the topological charge emerges from the local twisted structure, exhibiting a local bound-state characteristic at the quantum-mechanical level, its existence is dictated by the non-local phase difference configuration of the twisted phase (Eq. 31). This interpretation, expressed in the Goldstone-Wilczek approach, is consistent with the Jackiw-Rebbi description of the free-electron topological bound state in a single-particle picture and aligns with experimental evidence of topological physics in systems such as topological photonics7.
