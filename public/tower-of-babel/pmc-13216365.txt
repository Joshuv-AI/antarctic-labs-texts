Transverse skeletal proportionality in the prognostic evaluation of facemask therapy for skeletal class III malocclusion: a retrospective cephalometric study
Authors: Song E Park, Mi Sun Kim, Hyeyeon Park, Hyo Won Ahn, Su-Jung Kim, Kyung-A Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to identify prognostic skeletal factors associated with long-term stability following early orthopedic treatment in growing patients with skeletal Class III malocclusion by integrating lateral (LAT) and posteroanterior (PA) cephalometric analyses.Materials and methodsForty-four growing patients with skeletal Class III malocclusion and maxillary retrusion treated with facemask therapy were retrospectively analyzed. Cephalometric records were obtained at pretreatment (T0), posttreatment (T1), and post-growth completion (T2). Patients were classified into favorable growth (FG) and unfavorable growth (UG) groups based on occlusal and skeletal outcomes at T2. Twenty lateral and fourteen posteroanterior cephalometric variables were assessed. Principal factor analysis with Varimax rotation was performed to derive latent skeletal components, which were subsequently evaluated using multiple linear regression to identify predictors of ANB at T2. A classification decision tree was constructed using T0 variables to classify long-term growth outcomes.ResultsFactor analysis yielded five principal variables (PV1–PV5), of which transverse maxillary structure (PV1), maxillomandibular transverse relationship (PV2), and vertical skeletal pattern (PV3) demonstrated significant associations with ANB at T2. A classification decision tree identified the transverse differential between mandibular and maxillary width (Mn. width – Mx. width) as the strongest initial discriminator of long-term prognosis, followed by AB–mandibular plane angle and Sum. The model achieved a classification accuracy of 93.2% in distinguishing favorable and unfavorable growth outcomes.ConclusionsTransverse skeletal proportionality plays a central role in determining the long-term stability of early orthopedic treatment in skeletal Class III malocclusion. Prognostic assessment may benefit from incorporating transverse, sagittal, and vertical skeletal dimensions including measurements from LAT and PA cephalograms, to improve individualized treatment planning and outcome prediction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40510-026-00623-4.Keywords: Class III malocclusion, Maxillary protraction, Maxilla growth, Cephalometry, Growth prediction

Background

The ultimate goal of early orthopedic treatment was to solidify individual patient care predictive, personalized, and preventive. Particularly in Class III treatment, the ability to precisely anticipate both a patient’s skeletal growth and the necessity for orthopedic intervention could have significant implication for the diagnosis and treatment of orthodontic and orthognathic surgical practices [1, 2].

Skeletal Class III malocclusion is characterized by mandibular protrusion, maxillary retrusion or a combination of both Longitudinal studies [3] in subjects with Class III malocclusions indicated that morphological differences between Class I and III at growth stages primarily resulted from growth disparities after the age of 6 [4]. Class III patients exhibited a slower antero-posterior (A-P) maxillary growth rate and a faster A-P mandibular growth rate, resulting in a shorter maxilla but longer mandible compared to adult Class I patients at age 20, and these differences worsen with growth [5]. For orthodontists, deciding whether to initiate treatment immediately, wait a little longer, or consider surgery until the completion of growth was challenging, as the growth spurt of Class III malocclusion tends to last longer than that of Class I and Class II adolescents [6].

Based on etiology and growth patterns, orthopedic maxillary protraction with or without rapid maxillary expansion had been considered a major treatment modality for skeletal Class III growing patients with maxillary deficiency. However, the effects of treatment and the response of the maxilla and mandible were diverse and limited in terms of timing, magnitude, rate, and duration of growth, making it difficult to estimate the prognosis and stability after treatment [7]. In the long-term stability, a critical factor was not only the maxillary response to forward traction but also the amount and direction of mandibular growth both during and after adolescence. Therefore, the decision making for treatment must be established after making an accurate diagnosis of present skeletal or functional problems and growth [8].

In 1966, Tweed [9] identified two different Class III malocclusions prognostic patterns. In 1969, Bjork [10] described seven structural signs to predict mandibular growth rotation. Since then, many studies have investigated prognostic factors for the growth of Class III malocclusion. In most studies, the subjects were divided into stable and unstable groups to predict stability variables, and various lateral cephalometric (LAT) predictors have been reported, such as the AB-mandibular plane angle [11], Wits appraisal [12], articular angle [13], N-perpendicular to point A [14], length of mandibular ramus [15], angulation of the cranial base, inclination of the mandibular plane to the cranial base, lower face height, gonial angle, the position of the condyle with reference to the cranial base [16], ramal length, and mandibular length.

While most studies had focused on LAT analysis, only a few analyzed prognostic factors of treatment success in Class III malocclusion on dental arches. In 1997, Franchi et al. [17] evaluated three linear transversal measurements including the maxillary and mandibular deciduous intermolar width, transverse discrepancy in 45 dental casts of patients with Class III malocclusion in deciduous dentition and found that a wider mandibular transverse width was a predictor of relapse. In 2017, Wendl et al. [18] examined the differences between successfully and unsuccessfully treated Class III malocclusion patients on dental casts and found that a narrower maxillary intermolar width could be an additional risk for relapse. These findings suggested that maxilla-mandibular morphology should also be analyzed in the transverse direction. However, most prognostic studies of early Class III treatment have relied primarily on lateral cephalometric predictors describing sagittal and vertical relationships, whereas transverse parameters derived from posteroanterior radiographs (PA) have been relatively underutilized in prognostic evaluation. Skeletal transverse maxillomandibular proportionality has rarely been systematically evaluated as a prognostic factor, which may limit the accuracy of pretreatment risk stratification for facemask therapy.

Morphological integration (MI) refers to a set of mechanisms that integrate elements within an anatomical system, quantify their associations, and measure covariation to infer developmental or functional relationships [19]. Because MI highlights that morphological changes in one structure are inherently linked to changes in others, it is essential to recognize their three-dimensional interdependence during evaluation. Consequently, when assessing the anteroposterior relationship between the maxilla and mandible and their growth patterns, the transverse and vertical relationships must also be considered, as these dimensions cannot be evaluated in isolation.

Regarding the treatment success of Class III malocclusion, variations in success criteria, patient characteristics, and the timing of outcome assessments across studies have produced a wide range of reported success rates, making it difficult to generalize these outcomes to all patients receiving orthopedic treatment. Previous studies [11–16, 20, 21] reported long-term success rates of 50.0%–93.3% for orthopedic treatment of skeletal Class III malocclusion. In addition to that, there had been limited exploration of PA analysis in transverse direction, as the accurate evaluation was challenged by the superimposition of three-dimensional (3D) structures onto a two-dimensional image [22].

From these perspectives, the aim of this study was to compare maxillomandibular morphology in the A-P, vertical, and transverse dimensions within distinct growth groups classified as favorable and unfavorable growth groups. The analysis was conducted using LAT and PA analysis. Furthermore, the study aimed to discern predictor variables characterized by high predictive power in determining successful outcomes of orthopedic treatments.

Materials and methods

Patients and datasets

From patients who received orthodontic evaluation between July 2007 and August 2023, forty-four individuals (mean initial age: 8.02 ± 1.47 years; 19 males, 25 females) were selected based on the presence of skeletal Class III malocclusion with maxillary retrusion and an anterior crossbite. These patients underwent orthopedic treatment during their growth period, and only those with follow-up records of at least five years after completion of orthopedic treatment were included in this retrospective study. Inclusion criteria encompassed the correction of Class III problems after the orthopedic treatment, initiation of orthopedic treatment at a skeletal maturity index of modified Cervical Vertebral Maturation (CVM) less than 3 [23], and availability of pretreatment (T0), completion of maxillary protraction (T1), and post-growth (T2) records, each with at least five years of follow-up after the T1. T1 referred to the end of maxillary protraction, and T2 signified growth completion, confirmed by fusion of the epiphysis and metaphysis of the distal radius on a hand-wrist radiograph, after CVM 4 or the end of height growth [24]. Exclusion criteria included centric relation-centric occlusion discrepancy, functional Class III, congenital missing teeth, systemic disease, craniofacial syndrome, and previous orthodontic treatment or orthognathic surgery. Patients at T1 after CVM 3 were also excluded. A total of 44 patients met the inclusion and exclusion criteria for this study.

Our samples included 132 LAT images at T0, T1, T2 and 44 PA images at T0, all captured using the CX-90SP (Asahi Roentgen, Kyoto, Japan). All radiographs were retrieved from a digital archiving system, anonymized, and exported for analysis. The images were captured using standardized natural head and neck postures at rest.

The active orthopedic phase was defined as the interval from T0 to T1. Facemask therapy was performed without rapid maxillary expansion. Extraoral elastic force was applied through a tooth-borne labiolingual appliance connected to the facemask, delivering approximately 400 g of force per side for 12–14 h per day, until correction of the anterior crossbite and improvement of the skeletal Class III relationship were achieved. No fixed appliances or Class III elastics were used during the T0–T1 orthopedic phase, allowing evaluation of the orthopedic effects of maxillary protraction alone. By the end of the orthopedic intervention (T1), all patients achieved correction of the skeletal Class III relationship and anterior crossbite, resulting in a positive overjet. Patients attended regular follow-up visits during the T1–T2 period without the use of any retention appliance.

To assess the stability of the orthopedic treatment, patients were stratified into two distinct groups: the favorable growth group (FG) and the unfavorable growth group (UG), with representative patients illustrated in Fig. 1. Based on the data at T2, patients in the UG (mean T0 age 8.00 ± 1.69 years; mean T1 age 9.70 ± 2.71 years; mean T2 age 16.54 ± 2.06 years; n = 24, 10 males and 14 females) demonstrated persistent skeletal Class III characteristics (ANB ≤ 0°), accompanied by a non-positive overjet. Conversely, patients in the FG (mean T0 age 8.05 ± 1.19 years; mean T1 age 10.05 ± 1.50 years; mean T2 age 16.80 ± 2.72 years; n = 20, 9 males and 11 females) exhibited a Class I skeletal relationship (ANB > 0°) accompanied by a positive overjet (Table 1).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R software version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Auckland, New Zealand) with a significance level of p < 0.05. To assess measurement reliability, ten participants were randomly selected, and all measurements were repeated twice by a single orthodontic specialist with a 4-week interval. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated as the primary measure of reliability. Control chart analysis using the Lower Control Limit (LCL) and Upper Control Limit (UCL) was additionally performed as a supplementary assessment to evaluate the consistency of repeated measurements.

To compare cephalometric measurements between the FG and UG groups, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied, and p-values were adjusted using the Holm method for multiple comparisons. Given the high inter-correlation among the 34 cephalometric variables obtained from LAT and PA radiographs at T0, principal factor analysis with Varimax rotation was conducted for exploratory dimension reduction. The suitability of the dataset for factor analysis was evaluated using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity to confirm that the correlation matrix was appropriate for factor analysis. Factors were extracted using a conservative criterion of eigenvalue ≥ 2.0, together with factor loading ≥ 0.50, communality ≥ 0.50, and cumulative explained variance ≥ 60%. In addition, the scree plot was examined to confirm the number of retained factors (Supplementary Figure S1). The scree plot revealed a distinct elbow point after the fifth factor, supporting the extraction of five principal factors (PV1–PV5), which collectively explained approximately 68% of the total variance. These extracted factors were subsequently entered into a multiple linear regression model to identify significant predictors influencing ANB at T2.

Finally, to classify whether each subject at the T0 stage would belong to the FG or UG group, a classification decision tree was constructed using the 34 cephalometric variables measured at T0. The tree was generated using the Gini impurity criterion for node splitting. Model complexity was controlled through cost-complexity pruning, and the final tree structure was selected based on cross-validated performance to reduce potential overfitting. The classification performance was evaluated using a confusion matrix, and model accuracy was calculated as (True Positive + True Negative) / (True Positive + True Negative + False Positive + False Negative. Additional bootstrap validation was conducted to assess potential overfitting and obtain optimism-corrected estimates of model performance. Model discrimination was additionally assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).

Results

Measurement reliability assessed by the ICC showed high reproducibility for all variables at T0, T1, and T2, with ICC values ranging from 0.87 to 1.00. Control chart analysis was additionally performed and confirmed the consistency of repeated measurements.

In terms of the cranial base, no significant differences were observed between the two groups. Regarding maxillary (Mx) and mandibular (Mn) measurements, no variables showed statistically significant differences after adjustment for multiple comparisons. For the anteroposterior relationships, APDI at T2 remained significantly higher in the UG group compared with the FG group after Holm correction (p < 0.001), indicating a stronger Class III skeletal tendency in the UG group at growth completion. However, the AB to mandibular plane angle at T2 remained significantly higher in the FG group after correction (p < 0.001). Among dental relationships, U1 to SN showed no significant difference between the groups. IMPA remained significantly higher in the FG group at both T1 and T2 after Holm correction (p < 0.001).

In addition to maxillary width, several transverse proportional indices remained significantly different between the two groups after Holm correction for multiple comparisons. Specifically, statistically significant differences were observed in nasal cavity width / facial width, Mx width / facial width, Mx aw – Mn aw, Mx aw / Mn aw, Mn width – Mx width, and Mx width / Mn width (all p < 0.001).

Predictor variables influencing ANB at T2

This study investigated the predictor variables influencing the stability of Class III orthopedic treatment, as indicated by the ANB at T2 (ANB_T2). Cephalometric measurements were utilized to examine the correlation between each variable and the predictor variables derived from factor analysis. Prior to factor extraction, the adequacy of the dataset was evaluated using the KMO measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Bartlett’s test was highly significant, confirming that the correlation matrix was not an identity matrix and supporting the application of exploratory factor analysis. The factor analysis identified five predictor variables (PV1–PV5), each representing distinct cephalometric dimensions (Table 6). PV1 consisted of variables associated with the nasal cavity width ratio and alveolar width, reflecting transverse maxillary structural characteristics. PV2 included variables related to maxillary width and the transverse ratio between the maxilla and mandible, indicating maxillomandibular transverse relationships. PV3 was composed of variables representing vertical skeletal relationships and mandibular height. PV4 comprised variables involving cranial base and anteroposterior angular measurements, including the saddle angle, SN–FH, SNA, and SNB. PV5 included variables related to mandibular size—both width and length—and the posterior cranial base. Based on the cumulative variance values, PV3 explained the largest proportion of variance, followed sequentially by PV1, PV2, PV5, and PV4.

Classification decision tree and the confusion matrix at T0 (Fig. 4)

Classification decision tree model for predicting long-term growth outcomes following facemask therapy in skeletal Class III malocclusion. The initial split was determined by transverse skeletal proportionality (Mn. width – Mx. width), followed by sagittal–vertical skeletal expression (AB–MP) and an integrated cephalometric index (Sum)

In the evaluation of the classification decision tree at T0, the primary classification variable was determined to be Mn width – Mx width. Specifically, if the Mn width – Mx width was less than 16.91, the subject was categorized as FG. Further classification could be made using subsequent variables, AB-MP and Sum. Conversely, if the Mn width – Mx width was 16.91 or higher and the AB-MP was less than 61.35, the subject was estimated to be UG. Additionally, for subjects where Mn width – Mx width was 16.91 or higher and AB-MP was 61.35 or higher, the classification could further depend on the Sum variable. If the Sum value was less than 395.98, the subject would be categorized as UG; otherwise, it would be classified as FG. In summary, the prediction criteria were Mn width – Mx width < 16.91, or Mn width – Mx width ≥ 16.91 & AB-MP ≥ 61.35 & Sum ≥ 395.98, and UG otherwise. The accuracy of this classification decision tree model was estimated to be approximately 93.2%. The model demonstrated a sensitivity of 90.0% for identifying favorable growth outcomes and a specificity of 95.8% for identifying unfavorable outcomes.

Supplementary modeling analyses

As a supplementary analysis to evaluate potential overfitting and assess model robustness, additional predictive modeling approaches were explored. A LASSO logistic regression model was applied using the baseline cephalometric variables measured at T0. The apparent model performance showed an AUC of 0.924 (95% CI, 0.837–1.000), whereas the bootstrap-corrected AUC was 0.871 (95% CI, 0.759–0.983), indicating a moderate level of optimism in model performance, based on 100 bootstrap resamples. The optimal penalty parameter was selected using 5-fold cross-validation (λ = 0.046). The variables retained in the LASSO model were primarily related to transverse skeletal proportionality, including nasal cavity width/facial width (β = 4.52) and Mx width/facial width (β = 4.48), supporting the findings from the factor analysis and classification decision tree models. For comparison, the classification decision tree model showed an apparent AUC of 1.000 (95% CI, 1.000–1.000), whereas the bootstrap-corrected AUC decreased to 0.900 (95% CI, 0.801–0.999), suggesting potential overfitting in this relatively small dataset.

Discussion

This study underscores the importance of incorporating both LAT and posteroanterior PA cephalometric analyses, extending beyond conventional lateral-only approaches, to more comprehensively evaluate the prognosis of skeletal Class III malocclusion. By integrating transverse, A-P, and vertical skeletal dimensions, the present investigation highlights the multifactorial nature of growth modification and orthopedic treatment outcomes in growing Class III patients. Although various historical classification patterns and structural signs for predicting mandibular growth have been proposed, comprehensive prognostic studies—particularly those incorporating both LAT and PA analyses—remain limited. PA cephalometry was employed as a pragmatic surrogate for evaluating transverse maxillomandibular proportionality in situations where three-dimensional imaging datasets were not routinely available. The comparison between favorable and unfavorable growth groups (FG and UG) provides insight into factors influencing long-term outcomes. Although baseline cephalometric characteristics were largely comparable, differences in transverse skeletal measurements suggest a role for initial morphology. However, the greater skeletal discrepancies observed at T2 indicate that post-treatment growth patterns are a major determinant of long-term prognosis.

Regarding A-P relationships, differences in APDI between the FG and UG suggested a greater Class III tendency in the UG at the initial stage. Hirschfeld and Moyers [25] highlighted the multifactorial nature of predicting the maxillomandibular A–P relationship, emphasizing that such prediction requires consideration of the interrelationships among craniofacial components, the timing of growth events, growth vectors, growth velocity, and the potential influence of orthodontic therapy on anticipated growth outcomes. The peak growth period for the cranial base and mandible in Koreans was documented to occur around 11–12 years of age for girls and 12–13 years of age for boys [26]. Suggesting that further late mandibular growth and differential growth of the maxillomandibular complex had been occurred in considerations of the remaining growth potential [27].

Significant intergroup differences were also observed in AB–MP, which emerged as a key decision criterion in the classification decision tree analysis, with higher values in the FG. The mandibular plane reflects vertical skeletal relationships, whereas the AB plane represents sagittal skeletal relationships; therefore, AB–MP can be interpreted as an integrated indicator of sagittal–vertical skeletal interaction. Son et al. [28]. identified AB–MP as a prognostic factor for skeletal Class III growth using stepwise discriminant analysis, and Franchi et al. [17]. emphasized the critical role of vertical skeletal relationships in determining long-term outcomes of early Class III treatment. Although previous studies reported associations between increased gonial angles and relapse risk [29, 30], no significant differences in the sum or gonial angle were observed between FG and UG in the present study. This discrepancy is likely attributable to the exclusion of hyperdivergent skeletal patterns. Given that facemask therapy in patients with increased vertical facial dimensions may further increase facial height and exert unfavorable effects [31, 32], the exclusion of hyperdivergent cases may have mitigated the influence of vertical divergence on growth outcomes. These findings reinforce that sagittal relationships should not be considered in isolation but rather interpreted as dimensions influenced by vertical skeletal characteristics and their reciprocal effects on growth direction and long-term orthopedic stability.

In transverse analysis, the FG exhibited significantly greater maxillary width than the UG, and proportional indices further highlighted intergroup differences. Rather than isolated linear deficiencies, these proportional discrepancies suggest that transverse balance between the maxilla and mandible plays an interrelated role alongside sagittal and vertical skeletal dimensions. Previous studies have demonstrated that skeletal Class III patients often present transverse discrepancies even in the absence of posterior crossbite, and CT- and cast-based evaluations consistently report narrower maxillary basal widths in Class III patients compared with Class I controls [33]. These findings support the concept that transverse maxillary deficiency represents a structural hallmark of skeletal Class III malocclusion and underscore the importance of considering transverse correction, such as maxillary expansion, when clinically indicated [34]. Early identification and management of transverse discrepancies during the appropriate stage of maxillary growth may therefore enhance orthopedic responsiveness and long-term stability [35, 36].

Building upon evidence that transverse imbalance is closely interrelated with sagittal and vertical skeletal characteristics [11–16], this study incorporated PA-derived transverse parameters to identify prognostic markers of long-term treatment stability. Factor analysis yielded exploratory cephalometric factors (PV1–PV5). PV1 (transverse maxillary structure), PV2 (maxillomandibular transverse relationship), and PV3 (vertical skeletal pattern) showed significant associations with ANB at T2 in the multivariate model, indicating that pretreatment transverse and vertical skeletal configurations meaningfully influence long-term orthopedic outcomes. Although the significance of PV3 differs from some previous reports due to the specific characteristics of the present sample, this finding suggests that mandibular plane inclination and vertical skeletal height contribute to posttreatment adaptation and relapse tendencies, highlighting the need for careful vertical control during growth modification. In contrast, PV4 (cranial base morphology) and PV5 (mandibular size) were not significant predictors, consistent with earlier evidence showing weak or inconsistent associations with long-term sagittal stability [37].

The classification decision tree analysis performed at T0 identified Mn width – Mx width as the initial and strongest discriminator for long-term growth prognosis, underscoring the relevance of transverse skeletal proportionality within our Class III cohort. Because the sample consisted of retrognathic maxillary cases treated with facemask therapy and excluded subjects with marked mandibular excess, the transverse differential between maxilla and mandible likely reflects a characteristic structural imbalance within this population, consistent with previous reports emphasizing the role of alveolar and basal width discrepancies in treatment stability and relapse risk in Class III malocclusion [18, 19, 38, 39].

The subsequent incorporation of AB–MP and the Sum variable as hierarchical nodes indicates that sagittal–vertical skeletal expression and multivariate morphological interactions further refine the differentiation between favorable and unfavorable posttreatment growth. The criteria of AB–MP > 61.35 and Sum > 395.98 successfully classified individuals into the FG, aligning with earlier findings that AB–MP reflects combined sagittal and vertical relationships, while the Sum index captures composite cephalometric characteristics beyond single-dimensional descriptors. Considering that smaller saddle angles have been associated with a stronger Class III tendency, the prognostic implications of these thresholds should be interpreted alongside the fact that severe mandibular hyperdivergent patterns were excluded at baseline. Growth-related mandibular rotation during the posttreatment period may be anticipated due to the tendency of the craniomaxillofacial structures to re-establish biomechanical equilibrium over time [40]. Several studies have reported that Class III patients treated with orthopedic modalities, including facemask therapy, tend to exhibit a counterclockwise mandibular rotational pattern during the retention and growth period, accompanied by subtle gonial angle reduction, which has been interpreted as a compensatory mechanism counteracting the clockwise mandibular rotation commonly observed during early treatment [41, 42]. It may be speculated that subjects within the UG particularly those presenting with relatively greater counterclockwise rotational tendencies demonstrated more pronounced anterior morphogenetic remodeling, including forward displacement of B-point, as part of this adaptive growth response, compared to more normodivergent skeletal pattern of FG group.

Collectively, the model-derived thresholds (Mn width – Mx width < 16.91, AB–MP > 61.35, and Sum > 395.98) correctly classified individuals into the FG with approximately 93.2% accuracy. These results highlight transverse maxillomandibular relationships and the sagittal–vertical configuration of the maxillomandibular complex supplemented by integrative morphologic patterns as principal determinants of long-term orthopedic prognosis in Class III malocclusion. However, given the relatively small dataset and the exploratory nature of the modeling framework, the proposed threshold values should be interpreted with caution. Supplementary modeling analyses using bootstrap validation and LASSO regression also suggested moderate optimism in model performance, indicating that these thresholds should be considered exploratory and require validation in larger independent cohorts.

Despite valuable insights, several limitations should be acknowledged in interpreting the findings of this study. The predictive modeling relied solely on T0 cephalometric variables, which did not account for early skeletal responses following orthopedic intervention—factors that may serve as important prognostic indicators during active growth modification. In addition, treatment outcomes were defined primarily using cephalometric parameters, which may not fully reflect the multidimensional aspects of treatment success. The classification decision tree model, while clinically interpretable, may also be prone to overfitting due to the limited sample size. This concern is supported by the difference between apparent and bootstrap-corrected model performance, suggesting that the derived threshold values might not be directly generalizable to other patient populations or treatment protocols. Furthermore, the inherent variability in growth direction among Class III patients and the multifactorial nature of craniofacial development imply that complex biological influences cannot be fully captured within linear regression or branching decision-tree frameworks alone. The absence of PA data at T1 and T2 restricted longitudinal evaluation of transverse adaptation and treatment-related changes, while the lack of cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) limited comprehensive three-dimensional assessment of basal skeletal morphology. Additionally, PA analysis in growing patients may introduce measurement uncertainty due to landmark distortion, variability in dental development, and anatomical changes during growth [43]. Lastly, constraints in sample size and challenges in securing long-term follow-up for untreated skeletal Class III individuals reduced the extent to which the results can be broadly generalized. Future investigations incorporating early treatment response at T1, multi-timepoint PA imaging, CBCT-based morphologic evaluations, and three-dimensional dental or occlusal datasets, together with larger longitudinal cohorts may provide more refined predictive models and deepen our understanding of skeletal adaptation, treatment response, and long-term stability in Class III malocclusion.

Conclusion

In conclusion this study demonstrates that incorporating transverse, anteroposterior, and vertical skeletal assessments derived from both lateral and posteroanterior cephalograms enhances the prediction of long-term orthopedic outcomes in skeletal Class III malocclusion. Transverse maxillomandibular relationships emerged as particularly influential, functioning alongside sagittal–vertical configurations and integrative morphologic patterns as key prognostic indicators. Although the applicability of the derived criteria is limited by sample size, imaging constraints, and the simplified nature of the predictive model, these findings underscore the clinical relevance of comprehensive three-dimensional skeletal evaluation in early diagnosis and treatment planning. Future investigations employing larger longitudinal cohorts and advanced imaging modalities are needed to refine predictive frameworks and to support more individualized management of growing Class III patients.
