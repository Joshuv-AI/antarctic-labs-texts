SwitchCodec: Adaptive Residual-Expert Sparse Quantization for High-Fidelity Neural Audio Coding
Xiangbo Wang, Wenbin Jiang, Jin Wang, Yubo You, Sheng Fang, Fei Wen

Abstract
Recent neural audio compression models often rely on residual vector quantization for high-fidelity coding, but using a fixed number of per-frame codebooks is suboptimal for the wide variability of audio content—especially for signals that are either very simple or highly complex. To address this limitation, we propose SwitchCodec, a neural audio codec based on Residual Experts Vector Quantization (REVQ). REVQ combines a shared quantizer with dynamically routed expert quantizers that are activated according to the input audio, decoupling bitrate from codebook capacity and improving compression efficiency. This design ensures full training and utilization of each quantizer. In addition, a variable-bitrate mechanism adjusts the number of active expert quantizers at inference, enabling multi-bitrate operation without retraining. Experiments demonstrate that SwitchCodec surpasses existing baselines on both objective metrics and subjective listening tests.

Neural audio codecs are grounded in the Vector Quantization Variational Autoencoder (VQ-VAE) paradigm

[
27

adopts Grouped RVQ (GRVQ) to expand codebook capacity. Recent work on learned vector quantization
[
30
]
has further advanced high-fidelity audio compression by optimizing quantization strategies. However, these state-of-the-art systems still suffer from limitations tied to fixed quantization structures: they use constant configurations, overspending bits on simple segments.

Recent efforts to overcome the rigidity of fixed quantization structures have explored sparse activation strategies. Mixture-of-Experts Vector Quantization (MoE-VQ)
[
25
,
21
]
, for instance, activates experts according to latent features but lacks a structured residual hierarchy, often causing training instability. Adaptive RVQ
[
4
]
varies the number of RVQ layers using heuristic energy thresholds, which limits generalization. Other adaptive neural audio codecs
[
24
]
investigate dynamic bitrate allocation, but typically require complex retraining or still forgo a structured residual hierarchy. These shortcomings motivate our design of selection–order decoupling and data-driven expert allocation for more stable and flexible quantization.

In this paper, we present SwitchCodec, a high-fidelity neural audio codec built on Residual Experts Vector Quantization (REVQ)—a dual-path quantization framework. A shared base quantizer first captures universal structure, providing a stable baseline representation. A pool of routed quantizers is then sparsely activated through a learned gating network, which selects the top-
k
experts per window using encoder features. The chosen experts are applied in a fixed index-based order, preserving an energy-descending residual hierarchy. This design decouples bitrate from codebook capacity: bitrate depends on the number of active experts rather than a fixed cascade depth, preventing overspending on simple segments while retaining detail on complex content. In addition, a lightweight variable-bitrate (VBR) mechanism adjusts the number of active experts at inference, allowing a single model to operate across 0.89 kbps to 8 kbps without retraining.

In summary, the main contributions of this work are:
1) Residual Experts Vector Quantization: a dual-path quantization framework that combines a shared base quantizer with sparsely activated expert quantizers, mitigating bit waste and capacity constraints of fixed RVQ cascades.
2) Lightweight variable-bitrate mechanism: a top-
k
expert selection strategy that dynamically adjusts the number of active experts, enabling multi-bitrate operation within a single model with negligible routing-mask overhead.

2.2
Residual Experts Vector Quantization

Standard Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) uses a fixed sequence of quantizers to process the latent representation. This approach is effective at high bitrates, but its performance degrades significantly at low bitrates, where its limited codebook struggles to represent diverse latent
Z
, leading to substantial quantization error. Figure
2
illustrates this problem. A fixed strategy, using only the first few quantizers, results in a reconstruction that poorly matches the original latent distribution. In contrast, an adaptive strategy that selects the best-suited quantizers produces a far more accurate reconstruction. Experimental validation demonstrates that our adaptive approach achieves a 17.6% improvement in reconstruction accuracy when utilizing three quantizers compared to the fixed strategy. In contrast, an adaptive strategy that selects the best-suited quantizers produces a far more accurate reconstruction. This clearly shows the need for a dynamic quantization method, which motivates our proposed Residual Experts Vector Quantization (REVQ).
Residual Experts Vector Quantization (REVQ) augments a shared quantizer with sparsely activated routed quantizers selected by a router. This reduces quantization error while preserving a residual hierarchy.
To assign routed quantizers for each audio segment, a gating network (router) is employed, following the setup of DeepSeek-V3
[
5
]
, with a bias-free learnable matrix
{U^{\top}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times N_{r}}
used to compute affinity scores.
Let
Z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times D}
denote the transposed output of the encoder; we compute affinity scores
S
and
mask_{i}
as follows:

S=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left({Z^{\prime}}\cdot{U^{\top}}\right),

(1)

mask_{i}=\begin{cases}1,&S_{i}\in\text{TopK}\left(\{S_{j}\mid 1\leq j\leq N_{r}\},\ k_{r}\right),\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(2)

where
N_{r}
denotes the number of routed quantizers,
T
the number of frames, and
D
the latent dimension.
\text{TopK}(S,k)
selects the top-
k
scores. The resulting
mask_{i}
selects routed quantizers in both encoder and decoder. This requires transmitting the identities of the
k_{r}
selected routed quantizers per window, introducing an overhead of
\left\lceil\log_{2}\binom{N_{r}}{k_{r}}\right\rceil
bits per window.
For a
W
-s window, this amortizes to
\left\lceil\log_{2}\binom{N_{r}}{k_{r}}\right\rceil/W
bps.
For example, with
N_{r}=7
routed quantizers (plus one shared) and
k_{r}=2
, a 2-s window adds approximately
\log_{2}\binom{7}{2}/2\approx 2.2
bps, which is below 0.1% at 2.67 kbps.

Notably, a pivotal design choice in our proposed REVQ resides in decoupling the selection of quantizers from their application order. While a subset of
k_{r}
routing quantizers is adaptively selected based on routing scores, their application abides by a stringent, predetermined sequence. Specifically, the chosen quantizers are applied not in conformity with their selection scores, but in a fixed sequence dictated by their original ascending indices.
For illustration, consider a scenario where quantizer 3 (with a higher affinity score) and quantizer 1 (with a lower affinity score) are both selected for a given input. Despite its lower score, quantizer 1 is invariably applied first to the initial residual; subsequently, quantizer 3 is employed to process the new residual generated by the preceding quantization. This stringent, index-based application order ensures that, within any selected group, lower-indexed quantizers consistently model higher-energy components first.

Consequently, REVQ inherits the principled, energy-descending hierarchy of traditional RVQ. This structured approach provides two key benefits: it enhances interpretability, as the routing mechanism learns to map high-energy latents to lower-indexed quantizers, and it improves training stability by assigning specialized, non-overlapping roles to each routing quantizer. This clear division of labor not only streamlines the model architecture but also reinforces robust learning.
Since the mask involved in the quantization process is non-differentiable, we apply the straight-through estimator
[
15
,
3
]
to estimate the gradient for backpropagation:

mask=S+\text{sg}(mask-S),

(3)

where sg denotes the stop-gradient operation.
Computationally, REVQ keeps the encoder/decoder complexity comparable to standard RVQ: the routing stage reduces to a single matrix–vector product per frame. The additional side information is negligible in practice (about 2.2 bps in our setting).

2.3
Variable Bitrate Support

We enable variable-bitrate operation by sampling the number of active routed quantizers per input (selecting top-
k
by affinity). This design leverages the content-aware nature of the gating network, which computes affinity scores from latent features such as spectral flatness and temporal entropy, ensuring that selected quantizers match the audio’s complexity. This provides direct control over bits per second while preserving the REVQ architecture: the encoder–decoder and codebooks remain unchanged across bitrates. The only auxiliary data is a routing mask that identifies active quantizers; with second-level windowing, this overhead amortizes to less than 0.1% of total bitrate (e.g., 2.2 bps for a 1 s window at 2.67 kbps).

At deployment, a single model serves a broad bitrate range of 0.89 kbps to 8 kbps by adjusting
k_{r}
during inference. This eliminates the memory and latency overhead of multi-model VBR schemes (e.g., EnCodec
[
7
]
trains separate models for 3 kbps, 6 kbps, and 12 kbps), because the network weights remain fixed regardless of the target bitrate. The routing mask transmitted per window encodes quantizer selection using combinatorial coding; for
k_{r}{=}2
with
N_{r}{=}7
routed quantizers, there are
\binom{7}{2}{=}21
combinations, which can be represented in 5 bits.

3.3.2
Subjective listening

Subjective quality was assessed using the MUSHRA paradigm in accordance with ITU-R BS.1534-3
[
14
]
. 20 experienced listeners with over 2 years of research or engineering experience in audio signal processing participated in the double-blind evalua. Data from listeners who scored the hidden reference below 90 or any test sample above the reference were excluded to ensure evaluation reliability. Each trial comprised 10 distinct stimuli (5 speech and 5 music) including a hidden reference, standard anchors, and the systems under test (SwitchCodec, DAC, EnCodec); the item order was randomized, listeners rated perceived quality on a 0–100 scale with guidance to use the full range, and seamless switching among stimuli was permitted prior to score submission. Averaged outcomes align with Table
1
: at 2.67 kbps and 5.33 kbps, SwitchCodec attains mean MUSHRA scores of 91.7 and 93.4 (near-transparent quality), whereas DAC yields 86.3 and 88.9 and EnCodec achieves 61.3 and 70.4 at comparable operating points, substantiating that routed sparse quantization confers consistent perceptual benefits across diverse content.

Conclusion

We introduced SwitchCodec, a high-fidelity neural audio codec with Residual Experts Vector Quantization (REVQ), combining a shared quantizer with sparsely routed experts in a selection–order–decoupled residual hierarchy. This design expands the embedding space with negligible side information and no added decoder cost, while a lightweight VBR mechanism enables one model to cover a broad bitrate range without retraining. Experiments on diverse datasets show SwitchCodec surpasses strong baselines at 2.67 kbps to 5.33 kbps in both objective and subjective quality. Scaling the expert pool sustains quality with fewer activations, demonstrating efficient codebook use. Routed sparse quantization thus decouples bitrate from representational capacity, offering a general approach for audio coding.
