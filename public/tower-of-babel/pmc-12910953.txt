Severe paediatric scrub typhus with complications: a case report and literature review
Authors: Xian Wu, Haiyang Zhang, Mei Wu, Kaiyu Zhou, Yi Liao, Fang Liu, Qian Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This case report documents a rare case of scrub typhus with multiple serious complications in a 8-year-old patient. Scrub typhus is usually more prevalent in adults, but serious complications in children are uncommon. This report examines a severe pediatric case involving septic shock, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). The patient initially presented with erythema of the umbilicus, which then progressed to characteristic crusting with high fever, hepatosplenomegaly, and enlarged lymph nodes. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) confirmed the presence of Scrub typhus in the patient’s blood sample. Notably, this is the first case of scrub typhus found in lung using mNGS, providing strong evidence for early detection. Treatment included a combination of antibiotics, particularly doxycycline and rifampicin, as well as supportive measures such as invasive mechanical ventilation, plasma exchange, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) and chemotherapy. With this comprehensive treatment approach, the patient’s condition gradually improved and he was eventually discharged with complete recovery. This case emphasizes the importance of timely and accurate diagnosis and multidisciplinary supportive care in the treatment of severe scrub typhus in children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06435-5.

Introduction

Scrub typhus is an acute febrile illness caused by Orientia tsutsugamushi, transmitted by the bite of larval chiggers, and is considered a zoonotic disease [1–4]. High temperatures and humidity provide optimal conditions for mite activity, placing individuals engaged in outdoor activities and agricultural work in rural areas at higher risk of infection [2]. Globally, the Asia-Pacific region is the primary endemic area for scrub typhus, commonly referred to as the “Tsutsugamushi Triangle.” This area spans over 8 million square kilometers [2, 5].

It is estimated that approximately one million new cases of scrub typhus occur annually worldwide, affecting over one billion people [2, 5]. Similarly, in eastern India, the incidence of scrub typhus among children was significant. Research indicated that between June and November 2017, 61 out of 94 suspected pediatric cases were confirmed as scrub typhus, with an average patient age of 6.1 years, and the highest incidence occurring in children under five years old [6]. In China, scrub typhus is prevalent in the southwestern, southeastern coastal, and eastern regions, with a seasonal peak during the summer months. The peak incidence months vary by region, likely due to climatic differences [2, 7]. Recently, the incidence of scrub typhus has risen significantly in China [2, 7]. A study in Guangzhou reported 4,821 cases of scrub typhus from 2006 to 2014, showing an increasing trend in annual incidence. Most cases occurred between May and October (86.8%), with higher incidence rates in rural areas compared to urban areas (61.6% vs. 33.9%). Children and the elderly were identified as high-risk groups [7].

The incubation period of scrub typhus in humans generally spans from 4 to 21 days. The disease typically presents with an acute onset of fever, often accompanied by nonspecific symptoms such as rash, conjunctival congestion, cough, headache, myalgia, abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting. Generalized superficial lymphadenopathy and hepatosplenomegaly are common findings. The most characteristic clinical feature is the presence of an eschar at the site of the chigger bite (Fig. 1A) [8–13]. If the infection is severe or diagnosis and treatment are delayed, scrub typhus can lead to multiple complications, including hepatitis, septic shock, ARDS, HLH, encephalitis, myocarditis, and acute kidney injury [1, 9, 10, 13–22].

We report a case of pediatric scrub typhus, which had severe complications of septic shock, HLH, and ARDS, aiming to raise awareness among pediatricians regarding the severe complications of scrub typhus in children. Early recognition and timely management of these life-threatening conditions in clinical practice are crucial. By reviewing relevant literature, we hope to provide more comprehensive diagnostic and therapeutic guidance to clinicians, ultimately reducing the incidence of severe complications and the associated high mortality rate in pediatric scrub typhus.

Case description

The patient, an 8-year-old and 10-month-old boy, resides in Xichang City, Sichuan Province. One month before illness onset, he had a history of outdoor activities, and the he child’s parents complained of having heard reports of scattered scrub typhus in the area during the same period (however, the exact number could not be provided because exact local case data is not publicly available). On July 7, 2024, the child was admitted to the Infectious Diseases Department with “umbilical redness and swelling for two weeks, fever with pain and swelling in the left inguinal region for four days.” Initially, the patient presented with redness and swelling of the umbilical region, which gradually developed into a pustule that eventually formed a scab (Fig. 1A). During this period, he experienced recurrent high fever, with peak temperatures reaching 41 °C, accompanied by cough and the production of thick white sputum, as well as pain and swelling in the left inguinal region. Routine pathogen tests (sputum culture, blood culture, and culture of umbilical secretions) showed no abnormal results, and a chest CT scan (performed three days before admission) revealed no abnormalities. After three days of empirical treatment with oxacillin and metronidazole, the patient continued to have recurrent high fever. Monitoring of CRP and PCT showed significant increases, reaching maximum levels of 104.7 mg/L and 8.76 ng/mL, respectively. The patient also developed respiratory distress, shock, and multiple organ dysfunction. After fluid resuscitation, a repeat chest CT revealed bilateral pulmonary inflammation with partial consolidation and interstitial changes, as well as small pleural effusions on both sides (Fig. 2A-C). On the third day of admission, the child was transferred to Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) for further treatment and management.

When admitted to the PICU, the patient’s physical examination showed significantly elevated heart and respiratory rates (HR 146 bpm, R 35 breaths/min). The patient’s SpO2 was 90% with oxygen via face mask, and blood pressure was 89/46 (60) mmHg, showing a progressive decline, with a minimum of 50/30 (37) mmHg. Scattered red maculopapular rashes were observed on the face, trunk, and limbs, which blanched upon pressure. The patient exhibited non-pitting edema of the extremities. Conjunctival hemorrhages were visible in both eyes, and multiple enlarged lymph nodes were palpable in the preauricular, postauricular, cervical, and bilateral inguinal regions, with the largest lymph node measuring approximately 2.5 × 1.0 cm. These nodes were of moderate consistency, mobile, and some were tender on palpation. The lips were cyanotic, breath sounds were coarse bilaterally, and fine crackles were heard. The abdomen was slightly distended, with a black eschar approximately 0.5 cm in diameter visible at the umbilicus (Fig. 1A). The surrounding skin was red, without exudation on pressure, but tender. The liver of the child was palpable 3 cm below the costal margin and 4 cm below the xiphoid process, with a blunt edge, but the spleen was not enlarged. Cardiovascular and neurological examinations were unremarkable. The extremities were cool, with a capillary refill time of 3 s.

The initial diagnosis included septic shock, severe pneumonia, respiratory failure, capillary leak syndrome, and coagulopathy. Upon transfer to the PICU, the patient was immediately treated with a combination of adrenaline and norepinephrine to maintain blood pressure and improve circulation. Empirical antibiotic therapy with meropenem and vancomycin was initiated to cover bacterial infections.

Following the aforementioned treatments, the patient’s shock was corrected, but progressive respiratory distress developed, with worsening oxygen saturation. Arterial blood gas analysis revealed a PaO2/FiO2 ratio of 112.7 mmHg. A follow-up bedside chest radiograph indicated diffuse bilateral pulmonary infiltrates, significantly worsening within 3 h of PICU admission (Fig. 2D). ARDS was suspected, leading to endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation with protective lung strategies, fluid restriction, and CRRT to reduce fluid overload.

Serial blood tests after admission showed pancytopenia (white blood cell count nadir 3.3 × 10^9/L, hemoglobin nadir 99 g/L, platelet count nadir 23 × 10^9/L). Liver function tests revealed elevated ALT (130 U/L), AST (288 U/L), and LDH (853 U/L) levels, with decreased albumin (26.3 g/L). Lipid profile showed hypertriglyceridemia (1.88 mmol/L). Coagulation tests indicated prolonged PT (16.7 s), APTT (46.1 s), and INR (1.58), along with elevated D-dimer (29.62 mg/L FEU) and reduced fibrinogen (103 mg/dL). Ferritin levels were markedly elevated (3985.60 ng/mL), NK cell activity was decreased (14.11%), and sCD25 was significantly elevated (10,050.00 U/mL). Multiple cytokines were notably elevated, including IL-1β, IL-2R, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10 and INF-γ. Bone marrow smears revealed an increased granulocyte-to-erythroid ratio and the presence of histiocytes phagocytizing blood cells (Fig. 1B), consistent with hemophagocytic syndrome (HPS) based on diagnostic criteria [23]. Starting on the 4th day of admission, dexamethasone and etoposide were administered according to the HLH-1994 protocol [24], along with plasma exchange to remove inflammatory cytokines and CRRT. The child`s significant signs and organ functions were continuously monitored.

The patient’s condition deteriorated rapidly, leading to the onset of septic shock, ARDS, and HLH within a short period, without the identification of common pathogens. The uncertainty of the causative pathogen posed significant challenges in treatment. Therefore, mNGS was performed on the patient’s blood and deep tracheal aspirate samples. Although we attempted bronchoscopy to obtain bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) for mNGS analysis to ensure the reliability of the data, the procedure was prematurely terminated due to pulmonary hemorrhage, preventing the collection of BALF. Consequently, a deep tracheal aspirate sample was collected using a nasotracheal aspiration technique. Both blood and sputum samples were sent to our hospital’s laboratory for nucleic acid extraction and mNGS analysis.

The mNGS results from both blood and sputum identified O. tsutsugamushi with high confidence, with 607 and 2903 unique sequences, respectively, and relative abundances of 86.84% and 65.9%, respectively (Fig. 3A and B). The details of mNGS methods was shown in supplementary material. The child was diagnosed with scrub typhus and was subsequently treated with doxycycline and rifampicin for O. tsutsugamushi infection.

During the course of the illness, further tests, including the Widal-Wright test, autoantibody screening, and tumor markers, were all negative. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis revealed no significant abnormalities. Screening for other pathogens (e.g., tuberculosis, multiple respiratory viruses, Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, and mumps virus) was also negative.

The patient experienced complications such as pulmonary hemorrhage, gastrointestinal bleeding, and neutropenia. To manage these complications, the treatment regimen included the administration of etamsylate and batroxobin to control bleeding, plasma and platelet transfusions to address coagulopathy, and granulocyte colony-stimulating factors to promote neutrophil recovery. Due to high-risk factors including PICU admission, broad-spectrum antibiotic use, chemotherapy, and the presence of invasive devices, voriconazole was initiated for antifungal prophylaxis, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole was prescribed to prevent Pneumocystis pneumonia. Additionally, the patient was treated with albumin to combat capillary leak syndrome, immunoglobulin to support the immune system, and further transfusions of plasma, prothrombin complex concentrate, and platelets to correct coagulation disorders.

Following these treatments, the patient’s body temperature gradually normalized, bleeding ceased, inflammatory markers returned to normal, and the complete blood count and organ functions improved. Chest imaging showed significant resolution of abnormalities (Fig. 2E), and the patient was eventually discharged in a stable condition. Discussion

The presence of eschar on the skin is a clinically significant diagnostic feature of scrub typhus. In the literature, the eschar in pediatric scrub typhus cases is most commonly located on the chest, axilla, anterior abdominal wall, and neck [25, 26].In the case we reported, the eschar was located at the umbilicus, a site that has not been previously documented in the literature.

Table 1 summarizes key characteristics of pediatric scrub typhus cases reported in the past three decades, highlighting the variability in clinical presentation and outcomes [1, 20, 27–32]. Unlike previous reports, our case uniquely demonstrates the first documented umbilical eschar in a pediatric patient, the co-occurrence of septic shock, ARDS, and HLH within a single case, and the novel use of mNGS to detect Orientia tsutsugamushi in sputum, which not only accelerated diagnosis but also validated the pathogen’s pulmonary involvement. These findings underscore the critical role of molecular diagnostics in resolving atypical presentations and guiding multidisciplinary interventions for severe scrub typhus.

O. tsutsugamushi is an obligate intracellular bacterium, meaning that only antibiotics capable of penetrating cells are effective against it. Current expert consensus recommends tetracyclines, macrolides, chloramphenicol, and rifampin for treatment [33]. Doxycycline is the first-line antibiotic, and azithromycin can be used as an alternative for patients who are allergic to or contraindicated for doxycycline. Chloramphenicol and rifampin are reserved as second-line treatments [33]. Studies have shown that children generally respond better to antibiotic therapy (doxycycline or azithromycin) compared to adults. Most pediatric patients experience clinical improvement within 1–2 days of treatment, especially those with meningitis or encephalitis. However, treatment response is notably delayed in children with complications such as shock, heart failure, disseminated intravascular coagulation, or HLH [6, 34]. Children tend to recover more quickly and experience fewer complications when treated promptly. However, delayed treatment can lead to rapid deterioration, resulting in severe complications like ARDS and septic shock [1, 8, 35]. In the case we reported, we initiated doxycycline treatment early. However, due to the patient’s multiple severe complications and the possibility of doxycycline resistance, the treatment response was suboptimal. After confirming the diagnosis of scrub typhus and ruling out active tuberculosis infection, we initiated treatment with rifampin. This was combined with supportive measures such as mechanical ventilation, plasmapheresis, CRRT, and chemotherapy, which resulted in a rapid improvement of the patient’s clinical symptoms and organ function, leading to a positive treatment outcome. In adult cases, scrub typhus often requires longer treatment durations and the use of multiple drugs, likely due to the presence of comorbidities. The prognosis depends on the severity of the disease and the effective management of complications [36, 37].

Due to the diverse and non-specific nature of clinical manifestations, the risk of misdiagnosis and missed diagnosis of scrub typhus is high, making early diagnosis and timely treatment crucial for reducing mortality. Rapid diagnostic methods such as PCR and serological tests are widely used, but they have limitations in terms of accuracy and reliability [1, 38]. mNGS is a high-throughput, rapid, and accurate DNA/RNA detection method with the potential to comprehensively identify multiple pathogens in a single test [1, 39]. Additionally, mNGS provides greater sensitivity in detecting specific pathogens compared to traditional methods, serving as a valuable supplement to conventional diagnostic approaches to improve the chances of early diagnosis [1, 40]. While pulmonary infections are rare in scrub typhus patients, this case shows that scrub typhus can lead to severe lung damage, even progressing to ARDS. This indicates that clinicians should be aware of the possibility of multi-system involvement when treating similar cases and should take proactive steps for early diagnosis and treatment. In this report, although the Weil-Felix test was negative, metagenomic sequencing of sputum enabled us to quickly and accurately identify O. tsutsugamushi. This marks the first reported detection of O. tsutsugamushi in sputum using this method. The application highlights the significant potential of mNGS in diagnosing complex infections, especially when traditional methods fail to provide a rapid diagnosis, thus offering crucial diagnostic support.

The primary mechanism by which scrub typhus leads to septic shock is linked to the systemic inflammatory response syndrome. When infected with O. tsutsugamushi, the body generates a significant amount of IL-6 and TNF-α, which cause damage to endothelial cells and increase vascular permeability. This leads to vasodilation, a decrease in blood pressure, and insufficient organ perfusion, ultimately resulting in circulatory failure and multiple organ dysfunction [15, 38]. Pulmonary complications associated with scrub typhus are usually mild cases of interstitial pneumonitis, while ARDS is a rare but serious complication characterized by rapid respiratory failure, extensive lung infiltration, and a high oxygen requirement that necessitates mechanical ventilation support [41, 42]. The development of ARDS involves a combination of endothelial infection, inflammatory damage, and immune responses, leading to lung vascular injury, widespread cellular infiltration, diffuse alveolar damage, pulmonary hemorrhage, and pulmonary edema [8, 22, 41, 42].

Preventing scrub typhus mainly involves avoiding mite bites. Individuals should take proper precautions, such as wearing long-sleeved clothes, boots, and hats to minimize skin exposure, and using insect repellents [2, 43]. Additionally, environmental control measures are crucial. These include clearing vegetation, controlling rodent populations, applying insecticides, and treating soil chemically to disrupt the breeding and transmission cycles of mites, thereby reducing the risk of infection [2, 43]. Currently, a vaccine for scrub typhus is still under development. Studies suggest that the immunity provided by antibodies against O. tsutsugamushi is relatively short-lived, with protective effects diminishing over time. Future research might focus on multivalent subunit vaccines that include adjuvants to stimulate both B-cell and T-cell memory responses [44].

Conclusion

In this case report, we present a severe instance of septic shock, ARDS, and HLH in a child caused by scrub typhus. This case underscores the potential for severe complications from scrub typhus in pediatric patients and highlights the need for clinicians to maintain a high level of vigilance when encountering children with non-specific symptoms. Early diagnosis and prompt identification and management of complications are crucial for reducing mortality associated with the disease. Additionally, it is essential for government and health authorities to collaborate in promoting educational initiatives on scrub typhus, raising public awareness about prevention and control measures, and encouraging personal protective practices and environmental management to reduce the incidence of this disease.
