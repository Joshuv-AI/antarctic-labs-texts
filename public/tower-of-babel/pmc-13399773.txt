Reverse Passing of Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction With Simultaneous Anterolateral Ligament Reconstruction Using Hamstring Autograft
Authors: Ezio Adriani, Simone Giusti, Marco Adriani, Edoardo De Fenu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnterior cruciate ligament reconstruction continues to evolve as surgical techniques aim to optimize graft placement, increase rotational control, and reduce graft failure in high‐risk populations. This technical note describes an inverse anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction combined with an anatomic anterolateral ligament reconstruction, incorporating a retrograde tibial half‐tunnel and outside‐in femoral drilling. The technique is compatible with hamstring, quadriceps tendon, and bone–patellar tendon–bone autografts. Tibial fixation is achieved using a suspensory button that can be directly visualized on the anteromedial cortex, while femoral fixation is performed with an interference screw. The remaining semitendinosus strand or the FiberTape reinforcement is routed beneath the iliotibial band and lateral collateral ligament and anchored at Gerdy's tubercle to anatomically recreate the anterolateral ligament. This procedure is indicated for young athletes, patients with high rotational demands, positive pivot‐shift or Jerk tests, primary cases with high rerupture risk, and selected revision scenarios. Advantages of the technique include enhanced anteroposterior and rotational stability, improved graft‐bone interface healing through circumferential tunnel contact, preservation of the iliotibial band, minimized morbidity of anterolateral ligament augmentation, and reliable intraoperative visualization of tibial fixation. Additionally, the outside‐in femoral approach offers versatility for revision surgery and physeal‐sparing application in skeletally immature patients.

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction is by far one of the most common orthopedic procedures carried out worldwide, with multiple national registries showing that every year, between 4.1 and 51.3 ACL reconstructions (ACLRs) are carried out per 100,000 adults. 1 The biomechanical outcomes of modern ACLR using the most common grafts, coupled with rigorous surgical technique and intensive rehabilitation have been proven to give exceptional durable outcomes which, according to certain studies, are superior to the native ACL. 2 The most frequently used grafts worldwide are bone–patellar tendon–bone (BPTB), hamstring tendon (HT), and quadriceps tendon (QT) autografts, with optimal graft choice still considered an extremely debatable subject. 3 With regard to surgical technique, no single one has been identified as the gold standard in the available literature. By far, the most commonly implemented techniques worldwide are transtibial—where the tibial tunnel is used as a guide to perform the femoral tunnel, transportal femoral drilling—to create a more horizontal femoral tunnel, over‐the‐top technique to preserve femoral bone stock, the outside‐in technique to achieve greater obliquity of the femoral tunnel without compromising the posterior wall, and lastly all‐inside ACL reconstruction with the creation of 2 half‐tunnels.

Despite numerous studies supporting these various ACLR techniques, the continuous improvement of surgical instrumentation and materials allows surgical techniques to evolve rapidly. With this surgical technique, we aim to present a standardized, replicable method for ACLR which has the potential of improving patient outcomes, especially in selected cases, and reduce complication risks associated with the previously listed available ACLR techniques.

This study describes our ACLR technique with combined anterolateral ligament (ALL) reconstruction or augmentation which allows for the implementation of the 3 most commonly used grafts worldwide: HT, QT, and BPTB.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

The ideal candidates for this technique are patients under the age of 18 with high rerupture risk, active patients with high functional requirements who present a + Jerk test, revision ACLR, and, more rarely, patients who are candidates for combined ACLR and unicompartmental knee arthroplasty.

Positioning

After sedation, the patient is positioned supine on the operating table according to the standard arthroscopic setup. A tourniquet is applied high on the thigh and inflated to 250 mmHg. A nonsterile lateral post is positioned over the tourniquet, and the foot is supported distally, allowing for the knee to flex at 90° and move freely within its range of motion. The arthroscopic cabinet is placed in front of the operating surgeon.

Graft Harvest and Preparation

The technique allows the operator to use all 3 common autografts: HT, BPTB, or the QT. The technique will be explained using HT; for reference, HTs are harvested using a standard technique with a tendon stripper, ensuring lengths of approximately 220 mm for the semitendinosus tendon and 160 mm for the gracilis tendon (Figure 2).

Patient in supine position. Medial aspect of the leg. Hamstring tendons are harvested using a standard technique. (A) Hamstring tendon stripper. (B) Gracilis tendon. (C) Semitendinosus tendon.

After harvesting the ends of the tendons, they are whipstitched with 2 nonabsorbable sutures of different colors (No. 2 Ethibond; Ethicon, No. 2 VICRYL; Ethicon). The 2 prepared tendons are then duplicated to create a quadrupled graft, which is looped through the ACL FiberTag TightRope II (Arthrex, Naples, FL). The completed graft should have a length of approximately 8 cm, ensuring 2 cm for each tunnel and at least 2 cm for the intra‐articular component. A tape (FiberTape, Arthrex, Naples, FL) is then applied to the proximal end of the graft (Figure 3).

(A) A tape (FiberTape, Arthrex, Naples, FL) is then applied to the proximal end of the graft. (B) The distal end of the graft is prepared asymmetrically.

ACL Reconstruction and Reinforcement of the ALL

A diagnostic knee arthroscopy is performed to identify and address meniscal and cartilaginous lesions. The residual ACL is removed using a shaver, and the footprint is identified. A small incision is made in the lateral femoral region, just posterior and proximal to the lateral femoral epicondyle (Figure 4).

View of the lateral aspect of the knee, in 90° flexion. (A) A small incision is made in the lateral femoral region, just posterior and proximal to the lateral femoral epicondyle.

Tibial Half‐Tunnel Preparation

The tibial guide is set at a 55° angle from the external medial tibial cortex to achieve proper positioning of the guidewire in the footprint of the native ACL. The same incision used for the harvesting of the hamstrings grafts is used to position the jig. Once correctly positioned, a tibial half‐tunnel is created using the FlipCutter III Drill (Arthrex, Naples, FL) with the same diameter as the ACL graft—this often varies between 20 and 25 mm (Figure 5).

Patient supine. Antero‐medial aspect of the leg. (A) A tibial jig is advanced through the hamstring‐harvesting incision using a tibial guide set at approximately 55° from the external medial cortex. (B) A tibial half‐tunnel is created using the FlipCutter III Drill (Arthrex, Naples, FL).

Femoral Tunnel Preparation

The lateral epicondyle of the femur is identified via palpation. A 3 cm longitudinal incision is carried out from the epicondyle proximally along the femoral shaft. The iliotibial (IT) band is identified directly under the incision line. The IT band is then incised longitudinally along its fibers for approximately 3 cm and retracted superiorly and inferiorly. Small perforating vessels are often encountered—these are coagulated (Figure 6). The electrosurgical knife is used to mark the entry point of the femoral tunnel on the femoral cortex.

Lateral aspect of the knee. (A) The electrosurgical knife is used to coagulate small perforating vessels and mark the entry point of the femoral tunnel on the femoral cortex.

Through the anteromedial portal under arthroscopic visualization, an outside‐in ACL femoral guide (Arthrex,Naples, FL) is positioned at the level of the femoral footprint. The guide sleeve is advanced onto the lateral cortex through the incision previously described for the correct placement of the femoral tunnel reamer. A guidewire is then placed using an outside‐in technique from the lateral femoral cortex (the point previously marked) to the femoral insertion of the ACL (Figure 7).

A full tunnel is drilled over the guidewire to match the dimensions of the ACL graft. The femoral tunnel is cleaned of bony remnants and soft tissue inside the tunnel using a shaver (Figure 8).

Graft Passage

A “shuttle” suture (No. 2 VICRYL; Ethicon) is threaded outside‐in through the tibial jig's 3.5 mm drill guide and, using an arthroscopic grasper, is carried through the femoral tunnel and outside the joint. The suspension mechanism sutures are then carried across the tunnels and joint using the shuttle system (Figure 9).

Lateral aspect of the knee. (A) The suspension mechanism sutures are then carried across the (B) femoral tunnel and joint using the shuttle system.

Graft Fixation

With the knee flexed at 90°, the graft is passed from the femoral tunnel to the tibial tunnel under arthroscopic view. Maintaining the knee flexed at 20°, the graft is secured at the tibial level using the ACL TightRope II Attachable Button System (Figure 10).

Antero‐medial aspect of the knee. (A) The graft is secured at the tibial using the ACL TightRope II Attachable Button System. (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament.)

Subsequently, the graft is fixed at the femoral level using a resorbable interference screw of the same width as the ACL graft. The screw is inserted at 90° to the femoral cortex and then fixed at 30° to 40° with the knee placed in posterior drawer stress (Figure 11).

Lateral aspect of the knee. (A) The distal end of the graft is maintained in tension. (B) The femoral interference screw is inserted at 90° to the femoral cortex and then fixed at 30° to 40° with the knee placed in posterior drawer stress.

Reinforcement of the ALL

The distal end of the asymmetrically prepared semitendinosus tendon is passed beneath the IT band and the LCL using a Kelly clamp or equivalent (Figures 12 and 13).

Lateral aspect of the knee. (A) A Kelly clamp is threaded through the incision overlying Gerdy's tubercle. (B) The Kelly clamp is used to grab the (C) distal end of the asymmetrically prepared semitendinosus tendon.

Lateral aspect of the knee. (A) The distal end of the asymmetrically prepared semitendisosus tendon is passed beneath the iliotibial band and the LCL using a Kelly clamp. (LCL, lateral collateral ligament.)

The graft is then anchored to Gerdy's tubercle (to ensure the highest bone density) using a Knotless FiberTak 2.6 mm (Arthrex, Naples, FL) (Figure 14).

Lateral aspect of the knee. (A) The distal end of the graft is anchored to Gerdy's tubercle using a (B) Knotless FiberTak 2.6 mm.

Careful suture of the lateral fascia is performed using a VICRYL 2.0 suture.

To conclude the reconstruction, a drainage system (12‐14 mm) is employed, positioned in the anterolateral arthroscopic portal under direct visualization.

The technique can also be performed using a QT autograft. For autograft harvesting of the QT, a minimally invasive approach is employed via a longitudinal skin incision approximately 3 to 4 cm in length, originating from the upper pole of the patella. The autograft, with a desired length of at least 8 to 9 cm, thickness of around 10 mm, and width of approximately 10 mm, is harvested using the QuadPro Tendon Harvester (Arthrex, Naples, FL). This harvest exclusively involves soft tissue without the bone plug. The proximal end of the tendon is prepared with a No. 2 Ethibond suture (Ethicon), while the distal end is prepared with the FiberTag TightRope Implant (Arthrex, Naples, FL).

The tape derived from the ACL graft, emerging from the femoral tunnel, is passed beneath the IT band and the LCL using an arthroscopic grasper. The tape is then anchored to Gerdy's tubercle using a Knotless FiberTak 2.6 mm (Arthrex, Naples, FL).

DISCUSSION

The risk of graft failure is substantially higher in selected patient groups, 4 highlighting the need for a more standardized technical approach to these high‐risk cases. One of the main advantages of this technique is an increase in anterolateral and rotational stability. The importance of providing additional anterolateral support has been subject of multiple debates, especially in recent years with the advent of new methodologies. The original lateral extra‐articular tenodesis was proposed by Arnold‐Coker 5 in 1979, and despite the technique being widely implemented worldwide, the risk of overconstraint of the lateral compartment remained a significant issue to consider when approaching this technique. 6 Our proposed technique allows for a more anatomical solution for ALL reinforcement as it enables us to recreate the native ALL positioning from the lateral epicondyle of the femur to the anterolateral aspect of the proximal tibia, on Gerdy's tubercle. The choice to anchor the graft on Gerdy's tubercle and not in the mid‐point between Gerdy's Tubercle and the fibular head is because, in our experience, Gerdy's tubercle has a greater bone density, allowing for better fixation of the anchor system. Moreover, when compared to the most common lateral extra‐articular procedures, our technique offers a less invasive option for comparable results. In fact, by using the same graft (in the hamstring variant) we previously used for the ACL reconstruction and the remainder of the tape (in the tape variant), there is no need to surgically violate the integrity of the IT band, hence avoiding the formation of a further donor‐site morbidity. The IT band in our technique is simply split along its fibers horizontally for 2 to 3 cm and sutured back together at the end of the procedure. Furthermore, in the majority of the other lateral extra‐articular procedures, the LCL is often compressed under the tenodesis, whereas our reinforcement is placed beneath both the IT band and the LCL, avoiding unnecessary stress on the structure.

A further advantage of this technique is the complete 360° contact between the graft and the bone interface, with no interposition of fixation devices between the graft and the bone. The complete contact between the 2 interfaces greatly reduces delay in tunnel healing.

One of the main advantages of this technique is the direct visualization of tibial fixation. In fact, one of the main concerns of suspension fixation mechanisms is the risk of the fixation device surpassing the cortical bone and impinging on the soft tissue overlying the bone. This can inevitably cause issues with tensioning during the reconstruction and long‐term loosening of the graft. In our technique, with the ACL TightRope II Attachable Button System, we are able to directly visualize the button on the tibial cortical bone, eliminating the risk of noncontact between the 2. The ACL graft in this technique is fixed on the femur with the knee placed in 30° to 40° flexion; however, numerous studies have shown that satisfactory tensioning can be obtained both at lower and higher degrees of flexion (0°‐90°). 7

A crucial aspect of this technique is the possibility of obtaining an anatomical reconstruction of the ACL. This is primarily obtained via the outside‐in technique for the femoral tunnel preparation which allows the operator greater freedom of choice for the tunnel positioning. Moreover, the entry point on the femur will also function as the origin for the lateral extra‐articular tenodesis—the surgeon will be able to position this on the lateral epicondyle of the femur, where the native ALL originates, once again obtaining an anatomical reconstruction. A further advantage of the outside‐in femoral tunnel preparation is the possibility to implement the technique in skeletally immature patients. In fact, by placing the tunnel parallel to the physeal plate, we are able to obtain a physeal‐sparing ACL reconstruction; this is particularly useful as young patients are among the most common candidates for lateral extra‐articular procedure.

As shown by multiple recent meta‐analyses 8 of combined ACLR and ALL reconstruction, the goal of this procedure is to reduce graft failure rates and residual knee laxity and improve return to sport rates among young athletic individuals.
