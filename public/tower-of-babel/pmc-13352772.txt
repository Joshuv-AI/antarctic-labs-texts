Large language models for deductive qualitative content analysis in dementia-focused embedded pragmatic clinical trials: A comparative methodological study
Authors: Jeffrey Turner, Spencer Phillips Hey, Zachary G Baker, Vincent Mor, Jennifer L Sullivan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThematic coding helps researchers characterize intervention implementation in embedded pragmatic clinical trials (ePCTs), particularly interventions for older adults with dementia and care partners. However, manual coding is time-consuming, requiring multiple researchers. Because implementation science relies on systematic identification of determinants, barriers, and facilitators, advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI), specifically large language models (LLMs), may automate this process meaningfully by accelerating implementation evaluations within ePCTs. We developed and tested an automated algorithm using Chat GPT-4o and Chat GPT-4o-mini to achieve human-level performance coding interview transcripts.MethodsWe created a Python-based system that uses LLMs to process and code semi-structured interview transcripts about implementation challenges in translating dementia interventions into healthcare systems. The system matches excerpts to an existing codebook. Multiple iterations, including expert review, were used to refine accuracy and efficiency.ResultsThe LLM consistently coded more excerpts than humans. In the third iteration (V3), the LLM captured 61.7% of human-coded excerpts, with matching rates reaching as high as 72.6% for individual transcripts. Matching was higher for descriptive codes, 63.7%, than interpretive codes, 57.7%. The LLM identified 206 correct coded excerpts that human coders missed. In the fourth iteration (V4), GPT-4o outperformed GPT-4o-mini: descriptive code matching reached 89% (e.g. “Site Characteristics”), compared to 69% for GPT-4o-mini with the R1+R2 85% threshold. GPT-4o showed a weak, but positive correlation (r = 0.230) between transcript word count and matching agreement, while 4o-mini showed a moderate, but negative correlation (r = −0.452). The LLM workflow yielded a 97% reduction in time and a 99% reduction in cost per transcript.ConclusionThis study compared an LLM-powered workflow with human coding for thematic analysis. The LLM aligned strongly with human coders. While error rates necessitate human oversight, time and cost reduction, and ability to identify missed excerpts make it a potentially reliable supplementary tool. Although ePCTs and implementation science share complementary goals, they differ in focus, this flexible approach enhances efficiency and scalability, with acceptable accuracy. It streamlines the qualitative research workflow from outlining to the analysis of implementation processes in real-world settings and may accelerate existing implementation approaches while minimizing implementation resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-026-00953-8.Keywords: Thematic coding, Artificial intelligence (AI), Qualitative analysis automation, Embedded pragmatic clinical trials (ePcts), Older persons with dementia

Contributions to the literature• Demonstrated that an automated, LLM-powered workflow can substantially reduce the time (97%) and cost (99%) of coding in-depth interview transcripts while expanding the scope of analysis in ePCTs• Identified key limitations of automated coding, including variability in LLM performance, the need for human oversight to refine and validate outputs, and trade-offs between exhaustive coding and maintaining relevance and precision.• Documented that LLMs more frequently assigned multiple codes to excerpts, found additional excerpts to relevant themes that human coders had not previously coded, while also generating irrelevant excerpts that require additional human review.

Background and Objective

Thematic coding is a core component of qualitative research, allowing researchers to methodically identify, analyze, and interpret meaning in text-based data [1], ]. Thematic coding of interviews with healthcare providers ensures a systematic approach to understanding healthcare research for older adults, where understanding real-world intervention implementation is critical. In gerontological implementation research, insights into understanding the barriers and facilitators to implementing complex interventions are essential to elucidate the nuances inherent in the process of healthcare improvement [2, 3]. These insights are crucial for refining and aligning implementation strategies with the realities and needs of those they aim to serve. For example, in the domain of AD/ADRD care, understanding the experiences of older adults is essential for the development, implementation, and sustainment of interventions that can improve their quality of life and health outcomes [4]. The lack of advancements in interventions that provide meaningful therapeutic benefits for Alzheimer’s Disease and Alzheimer’s Disease Related Dementias (AD/ADRD) [5, 6] has only worsened this growing crisis.

However, despite the clear value in identifying the lessons learned from ePCT implementation, the application of qualitative methods in this field may be hindered by the significant time and resources required to analyze text data. Typically, in qualitative research projects, an interview is recorded, transcribed, and cleaned before it is ready to be analyzed. It is common practice for a research team to have two or more members individually analyze a transcript before meeting together to reconcile their codes and reach consensus on interpreting data line-by-line. This manual coding process ensures higher reliability but increases the time required for analysis. It is also beneficial when testing a coding scheme since a research team will need to refine their codes. Some projects employ the practice of “double coding” until a level of agreement is reached on consecutively coded transcripts, while others maintain this structure until the end of the project [7–9]. While this approach strengthens analytic rigor, it can require several person-hours per transcript to complete the process of initial coding followed by discussion and reconciliation between coders.

With the advent and wide availability of large language model-(LLM) powered tools, there is an opportunity to dramatically reduce this workload utilizing Artificial Intelligence (AI). A growing body of research on thematic analysis has used LLMs to evaluate excerpts from in-depth interviews [10], free-text responses [11], feedback responses [12], responses to curiosity-driven questions [13], and criminal court opinions [14]. This work suggests that LLMs can be highly effective and efficient in rapidly automating and organizing qualitative data [11, 12, 15]. However, these studies have also shown that reliable use of LLMs still requires human oversight, as errors can arise from the LLM misunderstanding context or nuanced data [12, 15]. Xiao et al. [13], using expert-drafted codebooks, employed an LLM to conduct qualitative analysis and found fair to substantial agreement between the LLM and expert-coded results. Additionally, research has demonstrated that using multiple prompt iterations yields better results than a single prompt [10]. De Paoli [15–18] used an LLM for inductive, thematic analysis, reflecting on how it aligns with Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis framework and the model’s outputs. In comparing LLM-generated analyses of in-depth interviews with video game players and data science lecturers against prior analyses, the findings indicated that an LLM successfully identified most of the key themes from the previous research [17].

The use of AI is a controversial subject among researchers. While there is acceptance of AI for transcription purposes, its use for other components of the research process, particularly at research intensive universities, is less widely embraced [19]. While there are already dedicated applications that attempt to perform thematic coding and various other steps in the qualitative research process, the black-box or opaque nature of these tools, and LLMs in general, creates a fundamental tension with the scientific process (e.g., Delve, ATLAS.ti). This is not to say that existing tools are unreliable or unvalidated, but rather that the scientific community may be reluctant to adopt these tools until their properties and performance characteristics are better documented and understood.

While De Paoli and colleagues have made significant contributions to evaluating the use of an LLM through inductively thematically analyzing semi-structured in-depth interview transcripts, little research has evaluated their ability to deductively code such transcripts. Specifically, no studies, to our knowledge, have used this approach for the intersection of implementation science methods and gerontological research. To that end, we piloted and documented our steps in studying an automated, LLM-powered workflow to code 10 semi-structured in-depth interview transcripts, using OpenAI’s API for its GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini LLMs. These transcripts were drawn from a project funded by the IMbedded Pragmatic Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and AD-Related Dementias (AD/ADRD) Clinical Trials (IMPACT) Collaboratory, a cooperative agreement (U54) of the National Institute on Aging (NIA) aiming to build the nation’s capacity to conduct embedded pragmatic clinical trials (ePCTs) of interventions for persons living with dementia and their care partners [20]. In the following sections, we describe our approach, the final workflow, lessons learned, and provide recommendations for how this work could be most useful to implementation science qualitative research in the near term, as well as tools future researchers might use to maximize the quality of any LLM-assisted qualitative coding.

The objective of this project was to develop and test an automated algorithm with the goal of achieving human-level performance coding interview transcripts using GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini models. We evaluated LLM-coded excerpts for alignment with human-coded excerpts from interviews with respondent researchers who designed and tested ePCTs for persons living with dementia and their care partners. We assessed whether excerpts were categorized under the same theme, whether LLM-coded excerpts were correct but had not been coded by the humans, and misclassification. Collectively, we hope our guidance may inform future implementation science researchers using LLMs to conduct thematic analysis to better understand benefits and challenges and decide whether this is the best methodology for their work.

Research design and Methods

We first describe the initial, human-led methods to code the transcripts, followed by the automated algorithm development, and finally describe our evaluation process.

Human-led process

Interview process

To learn from the experiences of all funded 24 pilots and 6 demonstration projects, interviews were conducted 6 months and 12 months after the first participant was enrolled by an experienced and trained qualitative research team member via Zoom. JT was the primary interview facilitator and the interviews were typically 40–60 minutes, audio-recorded with the participant’s permission, and professionally transcribed. Interview notes were recorded following the interview. We used a purposive sampling approach, including the Principal Investigator of the pilot/demonstration project and anyone they deemed important to implementation, and invited them to the interview via email. All transcripts were reviewed to ensure accuracy, and any personally identifiable information (PII) was anonymized. There was no Health Information (HI) collected during the interviews. For the present investigation, we only used the 6-month transcripts. These interviews focused on how researchers who received funding to run ePCTs from the IMPACT Collaboratory implemented their ePCTs for people living with AD/ADRD and their care partners. We aimed to understand team roles, interested parties’ engagement, intervention adaptations, multi-level barriers and facilitators to implementation, and health care systems approach to change [21]. For more information on the interview guide, please see Supplementary Fig. 2.

Codebook

Two researchers drafted a codebook based on the interview guide. We used an a priori directed content analysis approach [22], beginning with categories that reflected anticipated challenges in embedding ePCTs into routine health care systems [21]. These categories were informed by our understanding of pragmatic trial conduct and the dynamics of engaging frontline staff in intervention delivery [23]. In addition, we consulted with the Implementation Core, an expert ensemble of researchers convened through the IMPACT Collaboratory, who explicitly focus on the implementation of dementia care intervention to review our codes and make recommendations for refinements. At the same time, we remained open to emergent themes beyond these initial categories. This codebook was adjusted iteratively to add and remove codes and refine code definitions. The codebook was retrospectively reviewed by JT to differentiate descriptive and interpretive codes, and consensus was done in collaboration with ZB. The descriptive codes aimed to categorize straightforward information requiring less interpretation. The interpretive codes involved a more nuanced understanding, as the text categorized there goes beyond surface-level observations to comprehend the context and underlying meaning (see Supplementary Fig. 1). We outlined the codes and definitions used in the project to ensure consistency during the analysis and to clarify the understanding of terms [24].

Coding

Ten de-identified and cleaned transcripts were double-coded by at least two of four coders. Four trained team members analyzed the interviews: two master’s-level public health researchers, one PhD in Social and Health Psychology, and one undergraduate. All had between one and seven years of qualitative research experience, with the two primary coders (JT & ZB) possessing seven and six years of experience, respectively. The coders initially coded independently and then met to discuss the codes. During this discussion, they compared codes, discussed coding differences, and achieved consensus on final codes. The double-coded transcripts were stored on a secure drive. All interview transcripts include a maximum of 2 participants. The number of words per transcript ranged from 3376 to 9262 words. Automated process

Data preparation

To create our baseline dataset of human-coded text, we used a Python script to process the human-coded files. Codes in these files were represented as highlighted and commented text, where the contents of the comment corresponded to the code number in the codebook. Our script extracted these comments and the associated text and then uploaded this as structured data to an Airtable database. See Supplementary Figure 3 for a sample of these data. The “Passage” is the text from the interview. “Code” is the code number from the codebook. “Name” is the unique record ID created by Airtable.

In pilot testing, we found that performance of the LLM was much improved when it was given shorter input. Therefore, we used a second Python script to extract all the raw text from the interview files and then split this text into separate.txt files whenever the interviewer initiated a new question. These.txt files were saved into a new folder, which would serve as the input text to be processed by our final LLM-driven coding script (see Supplementary Figure 4 for the complete script).

Prompt creation

To generate the prompts for the LLM, we created an Airtable database from our qualitative codebook. This table contained each code’s name, code number (all codes were assigned a number from 1 to 11), description, and examples of relevant passages with a justification for why that passage fit the code that reproduced the interpretive quality of human coding. We wrote a Python script that iterated through this table. For each code, it produced a prompt for the LLM. The structure of the prompt included (a) basic instructions about the task to be completed, (b) information about the specific code, (c) an example of the desired output, and (d) instructions for how to format the output. See Supplementary Figure 4 for the complete Python script with a representative prompt example.

For the first iteration (V1), the LLM was given the code name, code number, and definition, and produced coded excerpts on 1 transcript. The research team did a preliminary review of outputs and modified the LLM prompt to try and improve accuracy. The second iteration (V2), involved editing the prompt to include exemplar quotes under each theme and the specific intervention description for each transcript. Full automated coding was completed on two transcripts and once again a preliminary analysis was conducted to determine areas for improvement. For the third iteration (V3), the number of transcripts tested was expanded to five, and the research team provided the LLM with an explicit definition for “implementation” defined as ‘putting an intervention into effect’ since the LLM had applied “implementation” in a way that was excessively broad. The fourth iteration (V4) included 10 transcripts and involved two runs across two different LLMs. Since LLM outputs are non-deterministic, we hypothesized that additional relevant passages might be identified for each code through multiple passes. LLM coding environment and model computation

All runs of the LLM coding algorithm included in our final analysis took place between September 23, 2024 and December 19, 2024. These runs were executed on a PC, running Windows 10, with 32 GB of RAM (with 8 GB of VRAM) and an AMD Ryzen 7 3700 × 8-Core Processor, using Python 3.10. The LLM Python scripts included imports of the base64, requests, json, os, and pandas packages. In the API calls to OpenAI’s servers, default parameters were used (Temperature = 1.0, Top_p = 1.0, Context Window = 128,000 tokens, Frequency Penalty = 0.0, Presence Penalty = 0.0), except for max_tokens, which was set at 5000. The specific model snapshots were GPT-4o-2024–08-06 and GPT-4o-mini-2024–07-18.

LLM coding

In the next step, author SPH wrote a Python script (Supplementary Fig. 4) that would iterate through each text file in an interview transcript’s file folder, generate the LLM prompt for each code, and send this through OpenAI’s API, using the GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini models with max tokens (i.e., basic units of text such as words, subwords, or characters) set to 5000 [25]. GPT-4o aims form maximum performance and accuracy, while GPT-4o-mini is an efficient, affordable, and accessible model that sacrifices power for affordability [26]. The API response was converted to a Python dictionary format.

To mitigate the risk of the LLM generating false information (i.e., “hallucinating”) [27], we introduced a text matching step, that verified, for each passage, that the exact wording appeared in the original transcript. This step allowed us to identify any content that the model added or altered. The result of this “Exact” match was recorded in the Python dictionary as a binary Yes/No variable. Then the data was uploaded to an Airtable database, recording the relevant section of text, the corresponding code number, and the justification. See Supplementary Fig. 5 for example. JT then conducted a side by side comparison of the LLM-coded and human coded excerpts and verified that the excerpts identified as matches, did in fact, match. This script also tracked the total input and output tokens needed to process and complete the set of codes for a transcript. This information was recorded in order to calculate the cost of the automated analysis.

Analysis

Similar to Drápal et al., (2023) we stipulated that the human-led analysis was the benchmark for comparison; human coding was used as a comparator to the LLM coding and the denominator in our calculation of matching and error rates. Matching rates were determined by comparing the number of human coded excerpts that were also identified by the LLM for each transcript and each code (i.e., the human-coded excerpt served as the reference unit, and we assessed whether the LLM identified a corresponding excerpt under the same code.) We assessed matching at four thresholds: 100, 85, 70, and 65%, which were selected based on iterative empirical testing during workflow development. Across this process, we observed that lower thresholds included excerpts with only superficial lexical overlap that human coders would not recognize as substantively equivalent, whereas higher thresholds were overly sensitive to minor transcription variations, punctuation differences, or small paraphrases. The thresholds are as follows (1) 100%, which required that the entire text of a human coded excerpt appeared, word for word, within an LLM coded excerpt of the same code; (2) “85%” which evaluated the Levenshtein distance, a string metric measuring the distance between two sequences [28], between human coded excerpt and LLM coded excerpt and counted a match if the ratio was 0.85 or greater; (3) “70%” which evaluated the Levenshtein ratio and counted a match if the ratio was 0.70 or greater, and (4) 65%, which evaluated the Levenshtein ratio and counted a match if the ratio was 0.65 or greater. We acknowledge that these thresholds were not derived from a formal optimization procedure and should be understood as reasonable starting points rather than definitively validated cutoffs. Levenshtein distance was used in the present study as a measure of surface-level textual overlap between LLM-coded and human -coded excerpts, not as a measure of semantic or conceptual similarity. Accordingly, this metric may underestimate agreement when excerpts differ in wording but capture similar meanings.

Error rates were determined by comparing the number of incorrect LLM-coded excerpts to the total number of LLM-coded excerpts across transcripts and codes. Additionally, during our experimentation with V3, we conducted an exploratory comparison of LLM coded excerpts and human-coded excerpts. After the LLM produced and categorized excerpts into the different codes based on the text, two qualitative team members, JT & ZB, who were content experts, reviewed each LLM-coded excerpt that was not identified as at least a 65% match with the human-coded excerpt. Separately, JT & ZB reviewed each non-matching LLM-coded excerpt sentence by sentence to determine if the LLM coded-excerpt was correct. JT & ZB reviewed the LLM’s justification for coding under each theme and determined whether it matched the code’s definition. JT & ZB then met to reconcile the LLM’s coding, where each LLM-coded excerpt was recorded as correct or incorrect.

Results

Number of coded excerpts

The human coders across 10 transcripts coded on average 72 excerpts per transcript, with a range of 127. When comparing the number of coded excerpts produced by human coders and the LLM, the LLM consistently coded more excerpts across all iterations. As the number of transcripts increased from V1 (1 transcript) to V4 (10 transcripts), variability in LLM coded-excerpts also increased, with a range between the fewest and most excerpts coded by the LLM across 10 transcripts being as high as 142 in the second run (“R2”) of V4-4o-mini. Matching rates between LLM- and human-coded excerpts (V3)

In V3, the matching rate varied across all five transcripts with the lowest being Transcript 4, 39/92 (42.4%) and the highest being Transcript 1, 85/117 (72.6%). The LLM captured over 70% of human coded excerpts in transcript 1, 85/117 (72.6%) and transcript 2, 113/157 (72%), suggesting a stronger alignment with the human coding compared in these transcripts than the other three transcripts. In total, 327/530 (61.7%) of the human coded excerpts were captured by the LLM. For more information, see Supplementary Figure 6.

The LLM performed better at correctly categorizing descriptive codes 214/336 (63.7%) that were identified by the human coders. Comparatively, the LLM correctly categorized 112/194 (57.7%) of the interpretive coded excerpts. Site characteristics and challenges and concerns, descriptive codes, had the highest matching rates, with the LLM correctly categorizing 40/51 (78.4%) and 29/36 (80.6%), respectively, of the findings identified by the human coders. In contrast, there was a broader range of matching rates, with some high, such as organizational culture, 24/31 (77.4%), and others much lower, such as code dissemination and sustainment post-ePCT, 20/55 (36.4%), and mode of intervention delivery, 6/13 (42.6%). Interestingly, transcripts with fewer human-coded excerpts tended to have lower LLM agreement, although this observation is descriptive and further analysis is needed to account for other factors that may contribute to differences in performance. Threshold-level effects

Table 6 and Supplementary Figure 9 provides matching rates for both 4o and 4o-mini models across various test conditions (R1, R2, R1+R2) and matching thresholds (70, 85, and 100%). On average, 4o consistently outperformed 4o-mini in terms of matching rates across all conditions. The Pearson correlation values revealed that 4o demonstrated a stronger and more consistent relationship between word count and matching rates compared to 4o-mini. With regard to the correlation between total words per transcript with the R1+R2 85% threshold, we found that for 4o, there was a weak, but positive correlation (0.230), meaning that, as the word count increased, the percent matching agreement increased slightly. For 4o-mini, there was a moderate, but negative correlation (−0.452) between the total words per transcript and the R1+R2 85% threshold, indicating that as the word count increased, the % matching agreement tended to decrease. The data also showed that the performance of 4o and 4o-mini varied depending on the matching threshold. The 70 and 85% thresholds resulted in significantly better performance in both models, but particularly for 4o. Additionally, 4o achieved higher matching rates in the majority of the tests across different transcripts and codes, with the highest correlation values being observed with the 70 and 85% matching thresholds.

Transcript-level performance

When assessing individual transcripts, on average, 4o achieved higher matching rates across all test conditions than 4o-mini. Notably, in Transcript 1, using 4o, the R1+R2 of 4o with a 100% threshold had a matching rate of 84/117 (72%) compared to the 48/117 (41%) matching rate with 4o-mini. However, there were several instances where 4o and 4o-mini were similar or identical, for example, in Transcript 5, on both the R1+R2 70 and 85% thresholds, 4o and 4o-mini had an 37/46 (80%) matching rate. Under the 100% matching threshold, matching rates were consistently lower than with the 70% or 85% threshold, indicating that if the bounds around passages of text do not need to be 100%, then LLM performance improves.

The performance varied across different transcripts, for example Transcript 1 and 2 had notably higher matching rates across both 4o and 4o-mini compared to Transcript 9 and Transcript 10, which may reflect transcript characteristics or content complexity. Combining the R1+R2 scores typically yielded the highest matching rates, as expected when aggregating multiple runs. For more detailed information regarding matching rates between human and LLM coded excerpts across ten transcripts by GPT model, see Supplementary Figure 7.

Code-level performance

When comparing the performance of 4o and 4o-mini across the 10 transcripts by code, while there wasn’t a substantial difference between descriptive and interpretive codes, 4o consistently showed a higher matching rate than 4o-mini in nearly every category. For example, when comparing their performance in correctly categorizing site characteristics, 4o achieved a 48/54 (89%) matching rate at the 85% threshold on R1+R2, while 4o-mini had a 37/54 (69%) matching rate. 4o’s highest matching rate was with the descriptive code, site characteristics, where it had a 48/54 (89%) match on the R1+2 85% threshold. 4o’s lowest matching rate was 13/88 (15%) on dissemination and sustainment post-ePCT, an interpretive code, on R1 using the 100% match threshold. For 4o-mini, the highest matching rate was 41/54 (76%) under the descriptive code, challenges and concerns, at the R1+R2 70% threshold, as well as the interpretive code, policy and context, at 22/29 (76%) for both the R1+R2 85% and R1+R2 70% thresholds. The lowest matching rate was 12/155 (8%) in planning, preparation, and design, a descriptive code, on R1, with the 100% match. For more information, see Supplementary Figures 8 and 9.

Word-count observations

Supplementary Figure 10 shows the total word count for each transcript alongside the combined (and average) word counts for all the coded excerpts across the human and LLM runs. This provides more granular insight into the volume of data, the relationship between the length of the transcript and the number and length of coded passages, and the differences between the human-coded and LLM-coded results.

The data continues to show that there is generally greater variability in the human coding process compared to the LLM. For example, the fraction of coded words to total words is always greater than 1, across all four LLM runs, regardless of the model. Whereas the proportion of human coded words drops below 1 for 3 of the 10 transcripts, and never exceeds 2.

Cost

Using OpenAI’s GPT 4o LLM (as of September 24, 2024), the average cost to code an interview transcript was just under $1 USD. Using GPT 4o-mini, this cost is reduced to USD 0.04. On the other hand, we estimate that a minimum of 5 hours of highly skilled human coding effort was required to code each transcript. With a conservative estimate of paying a Research Assistant $20/hour, the human cost to code an interview transcript would be $100. Given these parameters, the LLM produces a 97% reduction in time and a 99% reduction in cost per transcript compared to traditional methods.

Discussion and implications

Summary of main findings

We used an automated, LLM-powered workflow to process and thematically code 10 semi-structured in-depth interview transcripts in a study focused on dementia care intervention implementation lessons learned in ePCTs and compared the LLM’s production to human coders’. By automating the coding of in-depth interview transcripts, there were significant reductions in time and cost while expanding the scope of analysis in implementation evaluation. However, our findings highlight: (1) variability in type of LLM performance; (2) the need for human oversight to refine and validate outputs; and (3) the trade-off between exhaustive coding and maintaining relevance and coding precision. These insights suggest that while an LLM can serve as a valuable supplemental coder, before the wholesale adoption of this technology, additional careful research on the conditions under which LLMs match human coders’ performance is required.

Context and implications

Our findings contribute to the growing body of literature exploring the role of LLMs in qualitative research [10–12, 14–18]. Similar to De Paoli [16–18] and [12] the LLM and human coding matched well. Our findings revealed that matching rates between human and LLM-coded excerpts reached as high as 93% for some codes, supporting potential use in accelerating the analytical process of implementation data. In addition, we found that GPT-4o consistently outperformed GPT-4o-mini. While the LLM consistently coded more excerpts than the human coders, their consistency diminished as transcripts increased, possibly due to differences in coding styles or interview facilitators between transcripts. Despite promising matching rates, for some transcripts the LLM had error rates as high as 56%, indicating a need for careful monitoring to ensure reliability for implementation evaluation. However, verifying the accuracy of the LLM’s coding could be significantly more burdensome than traditional human coding. Additionally, the LLM performed notably better with descriptive codes rather than interpretive codes. Future research should consider accounting for these variables when designing and evaluating an automated coding workflow and methodology.

Second, the need for human oversight to refine and validate LLM outputs remains necessary [11]. The need for careful review of LLM outputs reinforces the importance of human oversight to ensure that automated coding accurately captures barriers, facilitators, and context-specific implementation insights. Dismissing the importance of human oversight can lead researchers to inaccurate conclusions or misinformed decisions if not properly addressed. While LLMs can supplement humans in thematic analysis, human researchers must still iteratively review, refine, and reconcile discrepancies. However, if human review is required for verifying every LLM-coded excerpt (and it should), does this reduce the efficiency gains that automation provides? We think the answer may be a tentative yes (e.g., reducing from 2 coders to 1 can still result in substantial reductions of time and cost). But further research should explore best practices for balancing oversight with automation.

Third, the LLM assigned multiple codes on excerpts more frequently than the human coders; this finding has not been widely discussed in the literature, to our knowledge. This suggests that humans were more inclined to match passages to single codes, whereas the LLM would more freely match the passages to multiple codes if it determined them to be relevant. And, indeed, the high word count of LLM excerpts suggests that it frequently matched passages to multiple codes. Notably, the LLM consistently coded thousands of words more than the humans, perhaps suggesting that it was somewhat less discriminating in its coding. Please see Supplementary Figure 11 for more information. It is important to note that while this was beneficial, as the LLM correctly categorized an additional 206 excerpts to themes that the human coders had not previously coded (due to the LLM employing an exhaustive coding strategy with thematic relevance), this came with an increased volume of irrelevant excerpts. At the same time, this highlights the tension between using human comparators as a reference for comparison and the fact that valid LLM-coded excerpts not captured by human coders may reflect the inherent incompleteness or variability of human qualitative coding. This flexibility presents both opportunities and challenges for future researchers to consider. On one hand, this process allows for a broader exploration of themes that can potentially uncover previously overlooked insights. On the other hand, it may hinder the coding process by introducing excess, non-essential data that needs to be reviewed by human coders, a time-consuming process. Future research should explore: How can automated coding be refined and optimized to capture critical excerpts while minimizing irrelevant data?

Fourth, we observed significant reductions in time and cost when automating the coding of in-depth interview transcripts, aligning with previous research emphasizing the efficiency of LLMs [14]. The ability to process 10 transcripts in minutes, at a fraction of traditional costs, opens the door for larger and more diverse datasets. Although our study focused on coding methodology, improving the efficiency and scalability of qualitative analysis could have downstream effects on implementation evaluation, allowing for quicker identification of effective interventions and implementation strategies. Still, cost and time are just two criteria and, while important, should not overshadow wider considerations of the contributions of the above methodology.

Limitations

The manual process of code development and team-based review can deliver intellectual benefits for evaluating implementation processes, for the scientists themselves, and perhaps also for the quality of the final scientific output. These benefits may be altered by offloading this work to a fully automated system.

Evaluating LLM performance is challenging. We have chosen to compare the algorithm’s output to the human-coded and reconciled transcript, but there is no perfect objective standard by which we can judge that a human-coded transcript is “correct”. The practical purpose of the thematic coding exercise is to prepare a set of data for further analysis. Thus, the acceptability (or accuracy) of a set of thematic codes should, we believe, ultimately be measured by the quality and insights of this downstream analysis, but that analysis is not a simple function of the coded excerpts. High levels of agreement between human coding and an LLM’s coding does not necessarily mean better analysis, what matters is whether these coded excerpts contribute to meaningful insights for implementation science. Another limitation is that LLMs may have more difficulty with complex social phenomena as opposed to more concrete topics. While the present study incorporates elements of both, it will be important for future research to more deeply delve into complex social phenomena and evaluate the performance of LLMs in those domains.

The fact that we achieved as high as 70% agreement between the automated and human-codes may be imperfect. If, for example, the 30% that was not coded by the LLM is critical to a sound thematic analysis, then overall analysis would suffer, whereas if it is nonessential, then automation is practical. In addition, if 30–50% of the LLM-coded excerpts for a transcript or code, are actually incorrect, these are substantive inaccuracies. Ignoring these errors may undermine the reliability of the dataset and have serious consequences and lead to flawed analysis, ineffective intervention design, and misguided implementation and scaling. While quantitative metrics like those presented are important for understanding matching with human coders, subsequent investigations might find alternative ways to evaluate code quality and richness. In particular, an important limitation of the present study is that Levenshtein distance captures surface-level textual overlap rather than semantic or conceptual equivalence, string similarity metrics are imperfect proxies for qualitative coding agreement. As a result, the reported agreement rates may underestimate alignment when LLM-coded and human-coded excerpts differ in wording but convey similar meanings. Future work should therefore incorporate semantic similarity metrics or approaches to assess conceptual alignment more directly. Furthermore, not every run will yield the same level of agreement. While we were evaluating the usefulness of an LLM as a tool in the qualitative research workflow, our experiment did not aim for absolute reproducibility. Additional metrics might provide for a more well-rounded view of the relative strengths and weaknesses of coding with LLMs. Another limitation is that the codebook for the present investigation was considerably narrower than some coding schemes. An interesting future research question would be to explore how LLMs function in coding with considerably different coding schemes (e.g., less structured interviews that employ narrower codes and/or more numerous codes).

In further explorations of automating in this domain, we believe it will be essential for the scientific field to better clarify the standards for what should be considered good/bad or acceptable/unacceptable coding. Without clear standards, if quality of coding is all “in the eye of the beholder,” researchers might overly rely on automatic thematic coding workflows or dismiss them entirely, then it seems reasonable to dispense with the laborious and expensive human coding efforts altogether, and instead, simply execute automated workflows for as many runs as required until the downstream analyst is satisfied with their “raw materials”. An important limitation of the present study is that Levenshtein distance captures surface-level textual overlap rather than semantic or conceptual equivalence. As a result, the reported agreement rates may underestimate alignment when LLM- and human-selected excerpts differ in wording but convey similar meanings. Future work should therefore incorporate semantic similarity metrics or human adjudication-based approaches to assess conceptual alignment more directly.

We also acknowledge the limitation that LLMs are an incredibly fast-moving field of technology and development. New LLM-powered tools, including open-source models, are emerging in the market regularly. It is possible that our approach could be rendered obsolete by more powerful models or tools that have validated superior performance on this very task. That said, neither is a guarantee that such a transformation will come to pass. Thus, we believe the methods and learnings are sufficiently compelling, generalizable, and adaptable to other settings to be worthy of dissemination [29].

When we began this project in 2024, OpenAI’s GPT-4o represented the state-of-the-art in accessible LLMs, consistently outperforming available open-source alternatives [30–33]. For this reason, we selected GPT-4o to develop and test our approach; testing on an inferior model would not have allowed us to meaningfully evaluate the viability of the method. However, the rapid pace of LLM development means that open-source models, some rivaling or surpassing GPT-4o, are now available.

Conclusion

In summary, this work highlights the performance of an LLM-powered workflow in thematically coding in-depth interview transcripts. By automating the process, LLMs allow interested researchers the opportunity to handle larger datasets and capture a broader range of experiences from key informants, which is beneficial in studying the lives of older adults. We found that the use of LLMs can efficiently analyze large datasets, be beneficial in supplementing human coding, and provide additional invaluable insights. However, human oversight over LLM generated work remains crucial to ensuring accuracy and nuance, especially when employing implementation science concepts. If used correctly, a hybrid approach combining the best of human coders and LLMs, can enhance both the efficiency and analytical rigor of gerontological research, ultimately aiding in the development of interventions to improve the health and quality of life of older adults. As LLMs continue to improve their performance, relying solely on human coding in the future qualitative research might not be the most efficient use of time and resources.
