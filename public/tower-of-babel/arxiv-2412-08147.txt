How to Weight Multitask Finetuning? Fast Previews via Bayesian Model-Merging
Hugo Monzón Maldonado, Thomas Möllenhoff, Nico Daheim, Iryna Gurevych, Mohammad Emtiyaz Khan

Abstract
When finetuning multiple tasks altogether, it is important to carefully weigh them to get a good performance, but searching for good weights can be difficult and costly. Here, we propose to aid the search with fast previews to quickly get a rough idea of different reweighting options. We use model merging to create previews by simply reusing and averaging parameters of models trained on each task separately (no retraining required). To improve the quality of previews, we propose a Bayesian approach to design new merging strategies by using more flexible posteriors. We validate our findings on vision and natural-language transformers. Our work shows the benefits of model merging via Bayes to improve multitask finetuning.

1
Introduction

Multitask finetuning has recently gained popularity due to the success of large pretrained models, but a careful weighting of tasks is crucial to get good performances
(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2024
;
Chung et al. 2024
)
. Similarly to the traditional multi-task learning
(
Caruana 1997
;
Ruder 2017
)
, the weighting is useful in tackling data imbalance, task interference, negative
transfer, and also effects of variable task difficulty
(
Raffel et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2023
)
. When
left unresolved, these can lead to issues, for instance, regarding safety
(
Jan et al. 2024
)
. Weighting is also useful for
pre-finetuning
recently used for multi-lingual transfer and continual pretraining
(
Aghajanyan et al. 2021
;
Gemma Team 2024a
;
Martins et al. 2024
;
Fujii et al. 2024
)
.

Despite its importance, little has been done to address task weighting for multitask finetuning.
For large models, an exhaustive search over weights is out of the question, but even if we could try a few weighting configurations, which ones should we try? There is no guide for that. Weights are often chosen arbitrarily and sometimes heuristically but these are not sufficient; see, for example,
Liu et al. 2023
. The weighting methods used for deep learning and pretraining can be adapted to search for good weights

(
Ren et al. 2018
;
Chen et al. 2018
;
Raffel et al. 2020
;
Groenendijk et al. 2021
;
Du et al. 2022
;
Yan et al. 2022
;
Xie et al. 2023
;
Thakkar et al. 2023
)
, but
a quick guidance on reasonable search areas will still be useful to assist the search.

In this paper, we propose to aid the search with fast
previews
of performances, estimated
to obtain weights that improve accuracy of multitask finetuning.
We use model merging to create the previews where we train and store models on each task separately and reuse them later to create previews by simply averaging the model parameter for a wide-range of weights (
Fig.

1
).
Our main contribution is a Bayesian approach to design new merging strategies that yield better previews over a wider range of weights. This differs fundamentally from previous works which only focus on the best performing weights
(
Don-Yehiya et al. 2023
;
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Feng et al. 2024
;
Stoica et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024
)
.
Such strategies do not always yield good-quality previews; see
Fig.

2(a)
for an illustration.

We propose a Bayesian approach where more flexible posteriors yield better previews but with slightly higher costs (
Figs.

2(b)
and
2(c)
). For instance, we show that Task Arithmetic
(
Ilharco et al. 2023
)
for LLMs corresponds to isotropic Gaussian posteriors, while better (and slightly more costly) Hessian-based methods
(
Daheim et al. 2024
)
employ more flexible Gaussian forms. We generalize this result to generic exponential-family posteriors and present a recipe to derive new merging
strategies.
We validate our method on several benchmarks on vision and natural-language transformers. For example, we experiment with image classification using Vision Transformers (ViTs)
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2021
)
of 86M parameters and adding new languages to GEMMA with 2B parameters
(
Gemma Team 2024b
)
for machine translation.
Our results consistently show that
more flexible posteriors produce better previews and helps us choose weights to perform more accurate multitask finetuning.

Our work combines ideas from model merging and Bayesian learning to improve multitask finetuning.

2
Weighted Multitask Finetuning

Multitask finetuning aims to finetune on multiple tasks altogether. For example, given a Large Language Model (LLM) trained for English, we may want to finetune it on multiple languages
(
Muennighoff et al. 2023
)
, for instance, German, French, Chinese, Japanese, etc. Denoting the loss of each task by
\ell_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
for the model parameters
\bm{\theta}
, we want to finetune over a weighted loss

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\ell_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}),\text{ where }\alpha_{t}>0\text{ for $t=1,2,\ldots,T$}.

(1)

We denote the loss-weight vector by
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\alpha}$}$}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{T})
and the finetuned parameters obtained with the weighting by
\bm{\theta}_{\bm{\alpha}}
. We will generally assume that
\alpha_{t}
sum to 1 but this is not strictly required.
Such multitask finetuning has recently become important for LLM alignment and usability.
For example, it is used for improving instruction-following abilities
(
Chung et al. 2024
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
, different kinds of safety tuning
(
Gemma Team 2024a
)
, combining coding tasks
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
, and mixing coding and math skills into LLMs, which is useful even when they are designed for other tasks like machine translation
(
Martins et al. 2024
)
.

In practice, it is important to choose
\bm{\alpha}
carefully for reasons that are true for any multi-task learning problem
(
Caruana 1997
;
Ruder 2017
)
. For instance, one issue is due to data-imbalance: different tasks may contain different types of information and some of higher quality than others. There is also task interference, for example, a model that does math well, may not necessarily be the best at languages. Additionally, learning some tasks might hurt
the performances in the other tasks, and then there is variability in task difficulty: some tasks are harder to learn and we do not want those to impact the tasks that are relatively easier to learn.

The effects of these issues are often felt in practice. For example, adding too much safety data can make the model more conservative and reduce its usefulness
(
Bianchi et al. 2024
)
; too much instruction finetuning can undo safety alignment and open new vulnerabilities
(
Qi et al. 2024
;
Jan et al. 2024
)
.
Such problems can be avoided by careful task weighting.
Weighting is also useful during
pre-finetuning
where we try to balance multiple tasks differently during the last, say,
10\%
of pretraining
(
Aghajanyan et al. 2021
;
Gemma Team 2024a
;
Martins et al. 2024
;
Fujii et al. 2024
)
.

Despite its importance, not much work has been done to find good ways to set the weights. Decades of work exist for multitask learning but multitask finetuning is a relatively new area and is still under-explored. Similarly to multi-task learning, an exhaustive search over the whole
\bm{\alpha}
-space is not feasible when
T
is large.
With little guidance, arbitrary values are tried to get an idea, for example,
Fujii et al. 2024
try only two values of
\bm{\alpha}
for continual pretraining.
Sometimes heuristics are used and meta-learning approaches are also adopted, but the results are not always satisfactory, for example, see
Liu et al. 2023
who report such a result for LLMs trained for code generation.

Our goal in this paper is to provide a fast (and cheap) method to assist the search of good
\bm{\alpha}
values. Such a guiding tool is useful to restrict the search to a few values and also to warm-start the optimization process. For this, we need a fast but accurate approach to quickly estimate the performance of
\bm{\theta}_{\bm{\alpha}}
for a wide-range of
\bm{\alpha}
values. Model merging is a useful tool for this, but the choice of merging strategy matters to get a high quality preview.
We show that simple merging methods are not satisfactory because they can be quite inaccurate and may only yield good estimates for a small region in the
\bm{\alpha}
space (see
Fig.

2(a)
).
Our main technical contribution is to address this with a Bayesian approach to expand the region by designing a more accurate merging strategy. The quality is improved at the expense of cost but the approach still remains fast enough to be employed in practice. Existing methods on model merging in this space only focus on the best performing weights
(
Don-Yehiya et al. 2023
;
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Feng et al. 2024
;
Stoica et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024
)
. Our work
instead aims to design merging strategies that work for a wide range of
\bm{\alpha}
values.

3
Fast Previews via Bayesian Model-Merging

Our goal is to create fast previews
, that is, we want to estimate
\bm{\theta}_{\bm{\alpha}}
obtained by finetuning over
\sum_{t}\alpha_{t}\ell_{t}
for a wide-variety of
\bm{\alpha}
values. The previews are useful to choose the
\bm{\alpha}
that give the most accurate results for joint multitask finetuning over all tasks.
Our approach does this in three steps:

1.

Finetune
T
models (denoted by
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}
) each separately over their own task
\ell_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
.

2.

Use Bayesian learning to build surrogate
\ell_{t}\approx\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}
by using
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}
.

3.

Create previews by finetuning over
\sum_{t}\alpha_{t}\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}
for many
\bm{\alpha}
values.

In step 2, we design accurate
\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}
by using exponential-family posteriors. Such posterior always has a closed-form merging formula which enables step 3. We start by describing model merging.

3.1
Model Merging as a Weighted-Surrogate Minimization

Model merging uses simple parameter averaging but it can also be seen as minimization of a weighted sum of surrogates. For example, consider the simple averaging (SA)
(
Wortsman et al. 2022
)
,

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\text{SA}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}\,\,\,=\arg\min_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}}\,\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma\underbrace{\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\|^{2}}_{\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{0}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}})}+}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\underbrace{\left(\ell_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})+\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}\|^{2}\right)}_{=\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}})},

(2)

where the surrogate
\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}
is a quadratic
function
. The term
\ell_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})
is a constant and can be ignored. The
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
is a regularizer with
\gamma=1-\sum_{t}\alpha_{t}
, which disappears if
the

\alpha_{t}
sum to 1. The equality can be verified by setting the derivative to zero. Other merging techniques can also be interpreted this way. For example, Task Arithmetic (TA)
(
Ilharco et al. 2023
)
uses finetunes of an LLM with parameters
\bm{\theta}_{\text{LLM}}
and can be seen as the following weighted-surrogate minimization with a regularizer,

\begin{split}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\text{TA}}&=\bm{\theta}_{\text{LLM}}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{\text{LLM}})\,\,\,=\arg\min_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}}\,\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\bm{\theta}_{\text{LLM}}\|^{2}+}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}\|^{2}.\end{split}

(3)

Here, we removed the constant
\ell_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})
for clarity.
In general, many model merging methods can be interpreted as weighted-surrogate minimization, including
Wortsman et al. 2022
;
Matena & Raffel 2022
;
Jin et al. 2023
;
Ortiz-Jimenez et al. 2023
;
Daheim et al. 2024
.

The interpretation highlights a major source of error when estimating performance over a wide range of
\bm{\alpha}
values: the accuracy of the surrogates. The surrogates used above can be seen as a simplistic Taylor approximation where we assume
\nabla\ell_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})
to be zero (due to local optimality) and the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\ell_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})
is set to identity,

\begin{split}\ell_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})&\approx\ell_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})+\nabla\ell_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})^{\top}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})+\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})^{\top}\nabla^{2}\ell_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})\\
&\approx\ell_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})+\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}\|^{2}.\end{split}

(4)

The surrogates
\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}
are tight at only one point and their inaccuracy increases as we move away from it. Model merging can be seen as using
\sum_{t}\alpha_{t}\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}
(along with the regularizer
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
) as a proxy to estimate the results of finetuning the original
\sum_{t}\alpha_{t}\ell_{t}
. However, when using a wide range of
\bm{\alpha}
values, this inaccuracy can lead to poor estimates in some regions in the
\bm{\alpha}
space. Essentially, the errors in different

\bm{\theta}
-regions become relevant and ultimately lead to a poor estimate.

Ideally, we would like the surrogates to be designed such that they are not only locally accurate but also in a wider region.
We expect such surrogates to be globally accurate and beyond the point used in their definition
, but how can
we
design them? Is there a general recipe to do so? We will now propose a Bayesian approach to answer
these questions
.

3.2
A Bayesian Approach to Model Merging

The surrogate minimization approach can be seen as a special case of distributed Bayesian computation where there is a natural way to merge information distributed in different locations. We will first describe this approach and then connect it to surrogate minimization to design better surrogates.

Consider a multitask setup in a Bayesian model where there are
t
tasks each using a likelihood
p(\mbox{${\cal D}$}_{t}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
over data
\mbox{${\cal D}$}_{t}
and a common prior
p_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
. For this case, there is
a
closed-form expression to quickly get the weighted multitask posterior. We first compute
T
posteriors
p_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\propto p(\mbox{${\cal D}$}_{t}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})p_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
, separately over their own likelihood
s
. The weighted posterior can be obtained by reusing the posterior
p_{t}
by using the fact that the
likelihood can be written as the ratio of posterior and prior
p(\mbox{${\cal D}$}_{t}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=p_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})/p_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
. This is shown below,

p_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\alpha}$}$}}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\,\,\propto\,\,p_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(\mbox{${\cal D}$}_{t}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})^{\alpha_{t}}\,\,\propto\,\,p_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})^{\gamma}\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})^{\alpha_{t}}.

(5)

The
\gamma=1-\sum_{t}\alpha_{t}
is the same scalar used in front of the regularizer in
Eqs.

2
and
3
. Such
posterior merging
is a popular method in Bayesian literature, for example, see Bayesian committee machine
(
Tresp 2000
)
or Bayesian data fusion
(
Mutambara 1998
;
Durrant-Whyte & Stevens 2001
)
.

In fact, by choosing the Bayesian model appropriately, we can even exactly recover the solution for the weighted multitask problems. For example, suppose we want to recover the minimizer
\bm{\theta}_{\bm{\alpha}}
by minimizing the objective
\gamma\mathcal{R}_{0}+\sum_{t}\alpha_{t}\ell_{t}
, then we can choose

p(\mbox{${\cal D}$}_{t}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\propto\exp(-\ell_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})),\qquad p_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\propto\exp(-\mathcal{R}_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})).

These choices are valid within the generalized-Bayesian framework
(
Zhang 1999
;
Catoni 2007
;
Bissiri et al. 2016
)
.
With these, the minimizer
\bm{\theta}_{\bm{\alpha}}
is simply the maximum-a-posterior (MAP) solution of the merged posterior
p_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\alpha}$}$}}}
, that is,

\bm{\theta}_{\bm{\alpha}}=\,\,\arg\max_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}}\,\log p_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\alpha}$}$}}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\,\,=\,\,\arg\max_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma\underbrace{\log p_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})}_{=-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{0}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}})}+}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\,\alpha_{t}\underbrace{\log p_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})}_{=-\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}})}.

(6)

Here, we assumed that all single-task problems use the same prior but the same principle can be used to extend it to the case where one uses different priors.

Comparing this objective to
Eqs.

2
and
3
, we see that the Bayesian framework suggests to use the surrogate
\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=-\log p_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
and regularizer
\smash{\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=-\log p_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})}
. These surrogates are perfect
because
using them recovers the exact solution, but computing them
exactly
is also difficult. Our key idea is to use approximate Bayesian learning, specifically variational learning, to obtain posterior approximations
and use them as surrogates.

3.3
Variational Bayesian Learning to Build Exponential-Family Surrogates

To build surrogates
\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}
for each task, we propose to use variational learning which finds a posterior approximation
q_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
to the exact posterior
p_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
,

q_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\arg\min_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\,\,\mathbb{E}_{q}[\ell_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})]+\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[q(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\,\|\,p_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})].

(7)

We choose
\mathcal{Q}
to
be
the set of exponential-family approximations or their mixtures, for example, Gaussian distribution. For such posteriors, we have good optimizers that
perform well
at large scale
(
Khan & Rue 2023
)
. For instance, for Gaussians, the above problem can be optimized by using Adam-like optimizers
(
Shen et al. 2024
)
.
Even Adam can be seen as solving this problem
(
Khan et al. 2018
)
. We can just use such optimizers to compute the posterior
q_{t}
.

Motivated by
Eq.

6
, we propose to use
q_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
to build the surrogate
and estimate
\bm{\theta}_{\bm{\alpha}}
, as shown below:

\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=-\log q_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\quad\implies\quad\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\alpha}}=\arg\max_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}}\,\,-\gamma\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})}

(8)

As for the prior
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
, we will use a Gaussian prior
, although other choices are also possible
. These choices can recover existing model-merging strategies. For example, we recover
Eq.

2

up to a constant
by choosing
q_{t}
to be the Gaussian approximation
shown below:

q_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\,|\,\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{I}$}$})\quad\implies\quad\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}\|^{2}+\text{const.}

(9)

The posterior
q_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
is a Laplace approximation obtained as a special case of variational learning
(
Khan & Rue 2023
, Eq. 25)
.

The prior can be chosen to be isotropic Gaussian:
p_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\,|\,0,\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{I}$}$})
.
Similarly, Task
A
rithmetic can be derived just by simply changing the prior to
p_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\,|\,\bm{\theta}_{\text{LLM}},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{I}$}$})
.

Next, Hessian-Weighted merging methods are obtained by using a full-Gaussian posterior, again with the Laplace
approximation
q_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\,|\,\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t},\smash{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{t}^{-1}})
using Hessian
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{t}
. With this,
we get the
squared
Mahalanobis distance
\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})^{\top}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})
as the surrogate, which yields
a Hessian-Weighted merging
(detailed derivation is
included in
Section

A.1
)
,

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\text{Hess}}=\Big(\sum_{t}\alpha_{t}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{t}\Big)^{-1}\sum_{t}\alpha_{t}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{t}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}.

(10)

Here, we use
p_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\,|\,0,\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{I}$}$})
and
\sum_{t}\alpha_{t}=1-\gamma
.
Given the prior
p_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\,|\,\bm{\theta}_{\text{LLM}},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{0}^{-1})
, the posterior corresponding to the Laplace approximation takes the following form
q_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\,|\,\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t},(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{t}+\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{0})^{-1})
, which leads to the surrogates
\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})^{\top}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{t}+\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{0})(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})
, recovering
the method proposed by
Daheim et al. 2024
.
A derivation is included in
Section

A.1
.

In general, we can use any exponential-family posterior and employ them as
surrogates
. Such surrogates always have a closed-form solution. This is because of the form of the posterior,

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\propto e^{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}}_{t}^{\top}\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}})}}\quad\implies\quad\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{t}^{\top}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})+\text{const.}

where we denote sufficient statistics by
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
and natural parameter by
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{t}
. For example, for Gaussian, we have
\smash{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}^{\top})}
giving rise to
the
quadratic surrogates derived earlier.
The merged model
\smash{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\alpha}}}
obtained by solving
Eq.

8
always has a closed-form because it is equivalent to the MAP of an exponential family distribution.
This is shown in
Section

A.2
.
The surrogates not only take flexible forms, but
are also
globally accurate. This is because they are obtained by solving
Eq.

7
which is equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence to the exact posterior
p_{t}
. Minimizing the divergence ensures that the surrogates are accurate not only locally at
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}
but also globally in regions where
q_{t}
has high probability mass; see a discussion in
Opper & Archambeau 2009
.

3.4
Improved Merging via Mixtures of Exponential-Families

Here, we extend to mixture of exponential-family distributions which provide more expressive posteriors and therefore even more accurate surrogates.
For simplicity, we assume that
\sum_{t}\alpha_{t}=1
, so there is no regularizer.
While mode finding for mixtures is still tractable, it requires an iterative expectation-maximization (EM) procedure which should still be cheap if it converges within
a
few steps.
We assume that the
k
’th mixture component is an EF with natural parameter
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{tk}
. Each component is weighted by
\pi_{k}>0
and
\sum_{k}\pi_{k}=1
.
Then, the posterior and
surrogate
take the following form:

q_{t}\propto\sum_{k=1}^{K}\underbrace{\pi_{k}e^{{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}}_{tk}^{\top}\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}})}}}_{\propto{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{p}}_{tk}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}})}\quad\implies\quad\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=-\log\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}e^{{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}}_{tk}^{\top}\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}})}}.

(11)

Clearly, the surrogate is much more expressive than the quadratic surrogates used in model merging.

Despite the non-concavity of the objective, we can maximize it using an iterative Expectation-Maximization (EM) approach where each step has a closed-form solution. A detailed derivation is in
Section

A.3
.
As a special case, consider mixture-of-Gaussians (MoG) posterior where the updates take the following form similarly to
Eq.

10
:

\displaystyle\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}^{(i+1)}\leftarrow(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\alpha}$}$}}}^{(i)})^{-1}\sum_{t,k}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\hat{\pi}_{tk}^{(i)}}\alpha_{t}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{tk}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{tk},\text{ where }\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\alpha}$}$}}}^{(i)}=\sum_{t,k}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\hat{\pi}_{tk}^{(i)}}\alpha_{t}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{tk}.

(12)

The main difference is that each component is now weighted by
\smash{\hat{\pi}_{tk}^{(i)}\propto\pi_{k}\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}^{(i)}\,|\,\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{tk},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{tk}^{-1})}
, normalized over
k
.
This update generalizes the fixed-point algorithm of
Carreira-Perpiñán 2000
which was proposed to find the modes of Gaussian mixtures.

Our methods to generate previews follow the 3-step procedure described in
Section

. Below we list three versions where different algorithms are used to generate

1.

(

)

10

(

Equation

10
.

3.

. For each task, we train using multiple runs of IVON and use them to construct a mixture-of-Gaussians posterior. The cost is
K

Algorithm

1
.

We also show results for using full Hessians on small-scale experiments in

5
Conclusion

Multitask finetuning is a crucial ingredient in many neural network training recipes but good weightings between tasks are hard and expensive to find.
Here, we propose to aid the search for such weightings with previews obtained from model merging, where single task models can be reused for many weight combinations.
We show that model merging strategies can be derived using a Bayesian framework by defining suitable surrogate losses to the multitask objective for exponential-family-based distributions.
We use this to outline various preview and merging strategies, including a new mixture-based algorithm for improved model merging.
Along various experiments including image classification with Vision Transformers and machine translation with LLMs we show that model merging can effectively be used to preview multitask finetuning weightings.

Flexible model merging can improve the preview quality, but also increase the cost. For example, mixture posteriors sometimes need too many mixture component to get improvements. This is not ideal for large model where storing many models is not possible. We hope to address this limitation in the future.

Appendix A
Derivations

A.1
Derivation of Hessian-Weighted Merging

First, we consider the simple case and set
\sum_{t}\alpha_{t}=1
, which implies that
\gamma=0
.
For
q_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\,|\,\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t},\smash{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{t}^{-1}})
, the problem in
Eq.

8
can be written as

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}}\,\,-\gamma\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}}\,\,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})^{\top}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}}\,\,\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}^{\top}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{t}\right)\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}^{\top}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{t}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}\right)

Minimizing this, we get
Eq.

10
.

Next, we derive the Hessian-Weighted merging of
Daheim et al. 2024
. They assume single-task finetuning as the following problem:

\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}=\arg\min_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}}\;\ell_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})+\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\bm{\theta}_{\text{LLM}}\|^{2}_{\text{$\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{0}$}}

From a Bayesian perspective, this problem can be seen as Laplace approximation of
Equation

7
, where the prior is set as
p_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\,|\,\bm{\theta}_{\text{LLM}},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{0}^{-1})
. Then, the posterior takes a Gaussian form
q_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\,|\,\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t},(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{t}+\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{0})^{-1})
, leading to the following regularizer and surrogates:

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{0}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\bm{\theta}_{\text{LLM}}\|_{\text{$\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{0}$}}^{2},\quad\quad\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}\|_{\text{$\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{0}$}+\text{$\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{t}$}}^{2}.

For those, we recover a Hessian weighted extension of task arithmetic. For a similar derivation, see also
Daheim et al. 2024
. To see how the above surrogate and regularizer gives Hessian weighted task arithmetic, we set the gradient of the loss in
Eq.

8
to zero. This gives us the following stationarity condition:

\displaystyle\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{0}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\alpha}}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{\text{LLM}})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{0}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\alpha}}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{\text{LLM}})+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{0}+\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{t})(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\alpha}}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\bar{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\alpha}}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{0}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{\text{LLM}}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\left[(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{0}+\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{t})\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{0}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{\text{LLM}}\right]

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\alpha}}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{\text{LLM}}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\bar{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}}^{-1}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{0}+\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{t})\left(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{\text{LLM}}\right).

Above, we have abbreviated
\bar{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{0}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{t}
. This is exactly the Hessian weighted task arithmetic method of
Daheim et al. 2024
.

A.2
Closed-Form Expression for MAP of Exponential-Family Distribution

For simplicity, we will ignore the prior
p_{0}
, but the derivation can be straightforwardly extended to the case when it is included. We start by noting
that minimizing
\sum_{t}\alpha_{t}\smash{\widehat{\ell}}_{t}
is equivalent finding MAP of
\prod_{t}q_{t}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})^{\alpha_{t}}
. Let us denote it by
p_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\alpha}$}$}}}
. The mode of
p_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\alpha}$}$}}}
is available in closed-form because we can always rewrite the posterior in an exponential form that allows us to compute the max. This is shown below where we first
rewrite the posterior with a log-partition function
A(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
and an alternate sufficient statistic
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{t}$}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{\alpha})
, and then simply take the derivative to get a closed-form expression for
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\alpha}}
,

\displaystyle p_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\alpha}$}$}}}\propto\exp(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}^{\top}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{t}$}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{\alpha})-A(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}))\,\,\implies\,\,\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\alpha}}=\arg\max_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}}\log p_{\alpha}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\nabla A^{-1}\left(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{t}$}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{\alpha})\right).

(13)

The alternate form is essentially following the form of the conjugate prior (see
Bishop 1995
) written to match the form of the likelihood. For instance,
for Gaussian
p_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\alpha}$}$}}}=\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{\alpha},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{\alpha}^{-1})
obtained by, Hessian-Weighted merging in
Eq.

10
,

p_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\alpha}$}$}}}\propto\exp(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}^{\top}\underbrace{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{\alpha}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{\alpha}}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{t}$}$}}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}}_{\alpha})}-\underbrace{\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}^{\top}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{\alpha}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}_{A(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}})})\quad\implies\quad\underbrace{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}_{\bm{\alpha}})}_{\nabla A(\bm{\theta}_{\bm{\alpha}})}=\underbrace{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}_{\alpha}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{\alpha}}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{t}$}$}}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}}_{\alpha})}\quad\implies\quad\bm{\theta}_{\bm{\alpha}}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{\alpha}

Solutions of variational objectives always have this form
(
Khan & Rue 2023
, Sec. 5)
, while convexity of
A(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
ensures that the mode always exists and can be easily found without retraining on individual objectives. In summary, we can always get a closed-form expression as follows

1.

Compute
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{t}
of individual objectives.

2.

Aggregate them
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{\alpha}=\sum_{t}\alpha_{t}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{t}
.

3.

Compute
\bm{\theta}_{\bm{\alpha}}
using
Eq.

13
.

Below, we discuss another example of the Beta-Bernoulli model to model coin-flips
y_{t}\in\{0,1\}
with probability
\pi
,

p(y_{t}\,|\,\pi)\propto\pi^{y_{t}}(1-\pi)^{1-y_{t}},\quad p(\pi)\propto\pi^{a_{0}-1}(1-\pi)^{b_{0}-1}.

The unknown is then modeled as
\theta=\log(\pi/(1-\pi))
, to get the posterior which is also a Beta distribution with parameters

a_{t}=y_{t}+a_{0},\quad b_{t}=1-y_{t}+b_{0},\quad p(\theta\,|\,\mbox{${\cal D}$}_{t})\propto\pi^{a_{t}-1}(1-\pi)^{b_{t}-1}\propto\exp(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{t}^{\top}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\theta))

where
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\theta)=(\log\pi,\log(1-\pi))
and
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{t}=(a_{t}-1,b_{t}-1)
.
With this, the aggregate posterior
p_{\alpha}
is a Beta distribution natural parameter
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{\alpha}=(a_{\alpha}-1,b_{\alpha}-1)
where
a_{\alpha}
and
b_{\alpha}
are simply a weight average of
a_{t}
and
b_{t}
respectively. To get the maximum of
p_{\alpha}
, we write it in an exponential form,

p_{\alpha}(\theta)\propto\exp(\theta\underbrace{a_{\alpha}-1}_{t(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}}_{\alpha})}-\underbrace{(a_{\alpha}+b_{\alpha}-2)\log(1+e^{\theta})}_{A(\theta)})

We can then compute
\nabla A(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
, plug it in
Eq.

13
, and simplify to get the closed-form update:

\underbrace{\frac{a_{\alpha}+b_{\alpha}-2}{1+e^{-\hat{\theta}_{\alpha}}}}_{\nabla A(\hat{\theta}_{\alpha})}=\underbrace{a_{\alpha}-1}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{t}$}$}}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}}_{\alpha})}\quad\implies\quad\hat{\theta}_{\alpha}=\underbrace{\log\frac{a_{\alpha}-1}{b_{\alpha}-1}}_{\nabla A^{-1}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{t}$}$}}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}}_{\alpha}))}\quad\implies\quad\hat{\pi}_{\alpha}=\log\frac{a_{\alpha}-1}{a_{\alpha}+b_{\alpha}-2}.

A.3
Derivation of the EM algorithm for Mixture Posteriors

To do so, we use the EM algorithm by viewing the summation over
k
in
Eq.

11
as marginalization over a discrete variable
z_{k}\in\{1,2,\ldots,K\}
of the joint
p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$},z_{t}=k)=\tilde{p}_{tk}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
. Then, given parameters
\smash{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}^{(i)}}
at each iteration
i
, we maximize the EM lower bound. The posterior over
z_{k}
is

p(z_{t}=k\,|\,\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}^{(i)})=\hat{\pi}_{tk}^{(i)}=\frac{\tilde{p}_{tk}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}^{(i)})}{\sum_{k^{\prime}}\tilde{p}_{tk^{\prime}}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}^{(i)})}.

Using this, we can write the following lower bound,

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\log\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\tilde{p}_{tk}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})}{\hat{\pi}_{tk}^{(i)}}\hat{\pi}_{tk}^{(i)}\right)\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{t}\hat{\pi}_{tk}^{(i)}\log\tilde{p}_{tk}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})+\text{c}=\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{t}\hat{\pi}_{tk}^{(i)}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{tk}^{\top}}_{=\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\alpha}$}$}}}^{(i)}}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})+\text{c.}

The above corresponds to the log of an exponential family (denoted by
p_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\alpha}$}$}}}^{(i)}
) with natural parameter
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\alpha}$}$}}}^{(i)}
, which gives us the following iterative procedure:

\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}^{(i+1)}\leftarrow\arg\max_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\hat{\pi}_{tk}^{(i)}}\alpha_{t}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{tk}^{\top}\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}),

(14)

where we use the posterior
\smash{\hat{\pi}_{tk}^{(i)}}=p(z_{t}=k\,|\,\smash{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}^{(i)})\propto\tilde{p}_{tk}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}^{(i)})}
which is obtained by normalizing over
k
. The iterates
\smash{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}^{(i)}}
converge to a local maximum which provides a solution for
\smash{\widehat{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\alpha}$}$}}}}
. For
K=1
, the algorithm reduces to the exponential-family case.
