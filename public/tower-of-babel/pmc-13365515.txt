Numerical assessment of failure load and soil–footing contact stress distribution for concrete foundations on cohesionless soil
Authors: Zaid S Alajlan, Sabry Fayed, EL-Said A Bayoumi, Saad A Yehia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe behavior of isolated reinforced concrete (RC) footings is strongly influenced by the interaction between the footing and supporting soil, which governs settlement, load capacity, and contact stress distribution. Classical analytical solutions often overestimate edge stresses and fail to account for soil nonlinearity, progressive footing cracking, and stiffness degradation. Experimental studies have shown non-uniform, saddle-shaped stress distributions beneath rigid foundations, yet most research focuses on idealized footings, and conventional design methods typically assume uniform contact stresses, overlooking the evolution of footing stiffness. This study addresses these gaps by developing a validated nonlinear finite element (FE) model in ABAQUS to simulate square RC footings on dense sandy soil under concentric vertical loading. The model was validated against experimental results, showing 1% discrepancy in ultimate load and 3% in settlement. A parametric study investigated the effects of reinforcement ratio (0.26–3.0%), footing thickness (250–500 mm), and concrete compressive strength (20–60 MPa). Increasing reinforcement ratio raised central contact stress by 102%, ultimate load by 111%, and energy absorption by 312%. Increasing thickness to 500 mm improved central stress by 42%, ultimate load by 37%, and energy absorption by 74%, whereas reducing thickness to 250 mm caused reductions of 83%, 61%, and 90%, respectively. Enhancing concrete strength to 60 MPa increased central stress by 152%, ultimate load by 149%, and energy absorption by 367%. The findings highlight the critical role of footing stiffness, cracking, and soil–structure interaction, demonstrating the limitations of uniform stress assumptions and providing practical guidance for safe, efficient, and economical design of RC footings on granular soils.Keywords: Cohesionless soil, Footings, Contact stress distribution, Shallow foundations, Soil–structure interaction, Numerical analysisSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Among various foundation systems, isolated reinforced concrete (RC) footings are extensively used due to their simplicity and effectiveness in transferring loads from the superstructure to the soil. The performance of these shallow foundations is governed by the interaction between the footing and the supporting soil, which directly affects settlement behavior and bearing resistance1. Consequently, detailed investigation of contact stress distribution and deformation beneath isolated footings is essential for ensuring reliable and economical foundation design.

The theoretical investigation of shallow footing behavior began with the analytical solutions proposed by Boussinesq and was later refined by Borowicka, who idealized the footing–soil interaction as a rigid foundation resting on an elastic, homogeneous, semi-infinite medium. While these early formulations provided critical insight into the mechanics of stress transfer beneath foundations, they consistently predicted excessive stress concentrations at footing edges under concentric loading. Subsequent experimental studies demonstrated that such stress distributions were inconsistent with observed soil response, revealing inherent limitations in the classical elastic assumptions2,3. These shortcomings motivated extensive laboratory research programs focused on directly measuring contact pressure distributions beneath rigid footings supported by granular soils.

A large body of experimental research indicates that the contact stress beneath footings resting on sandy soils generally follows a parabolic (bell-shaped) distribution, with peak stresses concentrated near the footing center and gradually decreasing toward the edges4,5. This interpretation, however, was later questioned by subsequent experimental studies. Using a tactile sensing system, Samuel et al.6 conducted laboratory measurements of normal contact pressures beneath a rigid strip footing model. The results revealed a pronounced concentration of contact stress at the footing centerline, forming a non-uniform distribution pattern. This experimentally derived stress profile was consistent with established theoretical expectations, yet it contrasted sharply with the simplified uniform stress distribution frequently assumed in practical design applications. In particular, Murzenko7 reported a fundamentally different stress distribution characterized by a saddle-shaped profile, where stresses near the footing edges were significantly reduced or approached zero, underscoring the critical roles of soil nonlinearity and footing rigidity in governing stress redistribution.

Despite these advances, the majority of previous studies have focused on rigid or idealized footing systems, with limited consideration of the realistic response of RC footings. In practical conditions, RC footings experience progressive cracking under increasing load, resulting in stiffness degradation that directly affects contact stress redistribution and soil–structure interaction mechanisms. Nevertheless, prevailing design approaches typically assume uniform or simplified contact stress distributions and neglect the evolution of footing stiffness following cracking. In addition, soil response is often idealized as constant, disregarding the progressive mobilization of shear resistance as load levels increase.

Recent full-scale experimental studies by Fouda et al.8 on square footings resting on sandy soils reported pronounced discrepancies between measured contact stress distributions and those predicted by classical elastic solutions. Although finite element modeling was employed to capture the soil–footing interaction, the influence of stiffness degradation on stress redistribution and failure evolution was not comprehensively addressed. Complementary numerical investigations by Yamin9 further demonstrated that rigid circular footings founded on cohesionless soils develop highly non-uniform, saddle-shaped contact stress distributions at ultimate bearing capacity. These findings indicate that the commonly adopted assumption of uniform contact stress is more appropriate for flexible foundations rather than rigid footing systems. More recent work by Fouda et al.10 demonstrated that contact stress behavior beneath reinforced concrete footings cannot be attributed solely to soil characteristics but is strongly affected by cracking-induced reductions in footing stiffness. Using nonlinear finite element modeling, they showed that decreased footing stiffness results in lower edge-to-center stress ratios, while increasing the reinforcement ratio enhances the ultimate bearing capacity and alters the contact stress distribution. These results are inconsistent with traditional theoretical models and underscore the need for further experimental verification.

Although the interaction between RC footings and cohesionless soils has been extensively documented in the literature, a clear gap remains in the integrated understanding of how cracking-induced stiffness degradation of RC footings interacts with nonlinear soil response to govern contact stress evolution and failure mechanisms. In particular, existing studies have not systematically quantified the coupled effects of key structural parameters—such as reinforcement ratio, footing thickness, and concrete compressive strength—on both structural performance and soil stress redistribution within a unified framework. Accordingly, this study does not aim to introduce a new theoretical formulation or numerical technique. Instead, it presents a novel integrated investigation that distinguishes itself by explicitly incorporating the effects of progressive cracking and stiffness degradation of RC footings within a validated nonlinear finite element framework, combined with a comprehensive parametric analysis of governing design variables. Unlike previous studies that idealize footings as rigid or neglect post-cracking behavior, the present work provides a more realistic representation of soil–structure interaction by capturing the evolution of footing stiffness and its direct influence on contact stress redistribution. The primary objective of this research is to investigate the distribution of contact stresses beneath square RC footings resting on cohesionless soil and subjected to concentrated loading. A detailed parametric study is conducted to evaluate the effects of reinforcement ratio, footing thickness, and concrete compressive strength on load–settlement response, ultimate load capacity, and stress distribution patterns. The findings offer quantitative and mechanistic insights that enable more accurate prediction of contact stress behavior before and after cracking, improve estimation of failure load, and support the development of more reliable design approaches for shallow foundations.

Numerical model

Model geometry

The experimental results used in this study were adopted from the work of Fouda et al.8 and served as a benchmark for validating the developed finite element model. The authors of the present study did not conduct the experimental program; instead, the published data were utilized to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the numerical simulations. The referenced experimental program comprised square RC footings constructed on a dense sand bed and tested under both concentric and eccentric loading conditions. Vertical loads were applied using a hydraulic jack operating in load-control mode, with incremental increases of 5 kN. At each loading stage, the load was maintained for 1–2 min to allow for displacement measurements, crack observation, and visual inspection. Vertical displacements of both the footing and the surrounding soil were recorded using LVDTs, while contact stresses beneath the footing were measured using four embedded cells. For the purpose of numerical validation, a concentrically loaded square footing specimen was selected from the experimental study. The footing had plan dimensions of 1200 × 1200 mm and a thickness of 400 mm, as shown in Fig. 2. To ensure consistency and enable a direct comparison, the material properties, reinforcement configuration, and geometric details implemented in the numerical model were identical to those reported in the referenced experimental work.

Numerical modeling strategy

A comprehensive finite element model was developed in ABAQUS v.202111 to simulate the soil–structure interaction between the concrete footing and the supporting sand soil. The use of the ABAQUS/Explicit solver allowed for an accurate representation of concrete nonlinearity, particularly under tensile stresses associated with cracking and crack propagation. The numerical model was verified by comparing simulation outputs with experimental measurements8, revealing a high level of agreement in ultimate load capacity and settlement behavior. The robustness of the predicted results was closely related to modeling parameters such as mesh resolution, constitutive modeling of concrete and sand, definition of contact behavior at the footing–soil interface, and boundary condition configuration.

Geometry description

To accurately reproduce the experimental conditions, the developed FE model incorporated all essential structural components, namely the sand soil, RC footing, applied loading columns, and steel reinforcement. Material definitions were carefully assigned based on experimentally obtained properties to ensure realistic response prediction. The geometry of the concrete footing was created using the exact measured dimensions from laboratory testing. The soil mass and concrete footing were discretized using eight-node linear brick elements with reduced integration (C3D8R), which provide computational efficiency while maintaining acceptable accuracy. The columns transmitting the load were modeled as rigid bodies using four-node rigid surface elements (R3D4). Meanwhile, the reinforcing bars were simulated using two-node truss elements (T3D2) with three translational degrees of freedom per node (Fig. 3)12–15.

Element types of the tested specimens.

To achieve an optimal balance between numerical accuracy and computational efficiency, the general soil domain and less regions of the RC footing were discretized using a relatively coarse mesh with an element size of 100 mm to reduce computational demand. In contrast, a refined mesh was applied in the vicinity of the column–footing interface and the adjacent soil regions (Fig. 4), where stress concentrations and load transfer mechanisms are more pronounced. A mesh sensitivity analysis (Fig. 5) was performed using element sizes ranging from 100 mm to 20 mm within this critical zone. The results indicate that the variation in ultimate load between the 50 mm and 20 mm meshes is less than 2%, confirming that the 50 mm mesh provides a reliable level of accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency. In addition, the loading procedure was implemented through a displacement-controlled approach applied at a reference node coupled with the top steel plate, as depicted in Fig. 4. Boundary conditions included full restraint of the soil foundation base and suppression of lateral displacements along the soil domain edges to ensure realistic confinement. A dynamic explicit solver was adopted for the analysis due to its robustness in handling nonlinear material responses, especially concrete fracture processes and stiffness deterioration resulting from crack propagation16.

In the CDP framework, four essential parameters define the constitutive response: the dilation angle, eccentricity ε, the deviatoric yield surface factor and the compressive strength ratio . The dilation angle influences the plastic flow characteristics, whereas eccentricity governs the curvature of the plastic potential. The factor adjusts the yield surface shape in the deviatoric plane, and the strength ratio accounts for the increase in concrete strength under biaxial compression compared with uniaxial loading. For this study, values of 35, 0.1, 0.67, and 1.16 were adopted from the literature25,31–33. A viscosity coefficient of 0.001 was introduced to stabilize the numerical solution and limit mesh-related sensitivity.

Steel reinforcement

For the reinforcing steel bars, a bilinear stress–strain model recommended by Kachlakev34 was adopted. The model assumes an initial linear elastic response up to the yield point, after which the material exhibits plastic deformation with lower tangent stiffness until the ultimate strength is attained. This formulation effectively captures both the elastic behavior and the post-yield characteristics of steel reinforcement.

Soil modeling

To represent the behavior of the supporting soil layer, an elasto-plastic model based on the Mohr–Coulomb failure criterion was adopted35,36. In the current study, the failure mechanism was similarly assumed to depend primarily on the available shear strength of the soil, and the shear–normal stress interaction was effectively captured using an internal friction angle and an appropriate dilation angle. In the present study, an internal friction angle of 43.9° and a dilation angle of 13.6° were adopted to represent shear-induced volumetric changes in dense sand, while the soil cohesion was assumed to be zero to simulate cohesionless conditions, consistent with previous studies8,37. It should be noted that the linear Mohr–Coulomb model has inherent limitations for representing dense sand, particularly at high stress levels. Dense sand exhibits pressure-dependent stiffness, strain-softening, and dilatancy, which the linear model cannot fully capture. Consequently, the model may underestimate or overestimate settlements and local stress redistribution under extreme loading. Despite these limitations, the Mohr–Coulomb model was selected for its computational efficiency, simplicity, and ability to provide reasonable predictions of the elastic and early post-yield response of dense sand in the footing–soil system38,39.

Interaction of different components

To represent the interaction between reinforcing steel and the concrete footing, the embedded element technique was employed. This modeling strategy enables reinforcement bars to be embedded within the concrete domain while implicitly capturing bond behavior, including dowel action and potential slip effects through the tensile characteristics of concrete31–33,40. The reinforcement bars were idealized as truss elements embedded in solid concrete elements with a fractional exterior tolerance of 0.05. In addition, the concrete–soil interface was modeled using a surface-to-surface contact formulation. Normal interaction was assumed linear with unit stiffness, whereas tangential behavior was defined using a penalty friction model with a friction coefficient of 0.40 to simulate possible sliding between the footing and soil10. This simplified approach was adopted to ensure numerical stability and computational efficiency while adequately capturing the overall soil–structure interaction behavior. However, it is recognized that the interface friction is not strictly constant in reality and may evolve with increasing relative displacement due to mechanisms such as particle rearrangement, surface wear, and grain crushing under high stress levels.

Validation of the FE model

Overall performance

A comparison between the experimental results and numerical predictions, summarized in Table 1, demonstrates the ability of the developed finite element model to accurately capture the overall behavior of the footing. The discrepancy in ultimate load prediction is approximately 1%, while the maximum difference in average settlement does not exceed 3%. In addition, the predicted contact stress shows good agreement with the experimental measurement, with a deviation of about 4.8%. These small differences indicate the robustness and reliability of the adopted modeling approach. As depicted in Fig. 7, the experimental and numerical load–average settlement (P-δ) curves exhibit strong consistency during the elastic stage, confirming accurate initial stiffness prediction. Slight divergence becomes apparent as failure approaches, likely resulting from complex nonlinear interactions between soil deformation and concrete cracking. The overall consistency between numerical predictions and experimental findings confirms that the developed finite element model can effectively simulate the structural response of reinforced concrete footings under concentric loading, providing reliable insight into their load-bearing behavior and deformation characteristics.

Failure modes

A flexural failure mechanism governed by yielding of the bottom tensile reinforcement was consistently observed in both the experimental program and numerical analysis. The developed numerical model was capable of reproducing the key stages of failure progression recorded during testing. As illustrated in Fig. 8, the predicted and observed failure patterns show strong similarity. Nevertheless, slight differences in the plastic deformation range were noted, which can reasonably be attributed to the constitutive modeling approach adopted for concrete using damaged plasticity theory and the simplified bilinear stress–strain model for steel. However, it is important to recognize that the observed failure mode does not result solely from flexural yielding of the reinforcement but rather from a combined soil–structure interaction failure mechanism. As the applied vertical load increases, the underlying dense sand progressively mobilizes its shear resistance, with plastic zones developing beneath the footing edges and spreading inward. Concurrently, the RC footing experiences flexural cracking, stiffness degradation, and eventual yielding of the tensile reinforcement. The ultimate failure condition is attained when the footing’s flexural capacity is exhausted, and the soil’s bearing capacity is simultaneously or sequentially mobilized. In this sense, the overall system failure represents a coupled interaction between structural yielding and soil shear failure, rather than a purely flexural or purely bearing capacity failure. The numerical results further elucidate this combined failure interpretation. Figure 8 illustrates that at ultimate load, the footing exhibits significant flexural cracking concentrated in the central region, accompanied by wide cracks extending toward the edges. Simultaneously, the underlying soil reaches a state of plastic flow, characterized by the formation of a well-defined shear failure mechanism extending below the footing. This behavior is consistent with classical punching shear failure patterns observed in shallow foundations on dense sand, where the footing penetrates into the soil with limited surface heave. The interplay between footing stiffness degradation and progressive soil yielding governs the post-cracking response and ultimately dictates the failure load and deformation capacity. Given the strong correlation between experimental measurements and numerical predictions, the developed numerical models can be considered sufficiently reliable for extending the scope of the experimental investigation.

Effect of soil domain size

The influence of soil domain size on the numerical prediction of footing behavior was investigated through four finite element models. These include the reference specimen C1 with a soil domain of 2400 × 2400 × 70 mm, and three additional models, namely C1-B0.75, C1-B1.25, and C1-B1.5, with soil domain dimensions of 1800 × 1800 × 70 mm, 3000 × 3000 × 70 mm, and 3600 × 3600 × 70 mm, respectively. All specimens share identical footing geometry (1200 × 1200 × 400 mm), reinforcement ratio (0.26%), and material properties; therefore, any variation in response is solely attributed to the adopted soil domain size.

The results demonstrate that the smallest domain (C1-B0.75) yields the highest ultimate load of 993 kN, corresponding to an increase of approximately 6.9% relative to the control specimen (928.7 kN), while exhibiting a reduced ultimate deflection of 13.78 mm (− 4.97%). This behavior is attributed to artificial boundary confinement, commonly referred to as the box effect, whereby restricted lateral soil deformation results in an apparent increase in stiffness and bearing capacity, as shown Fig. 9. As the domain size increases, the ultimate load decreases to 914.8 kN (− 1.5%) for C1-B1.25 and further to approximately 910 kN (− 2.0%) for C1-B1.5. In parallel, the ultimate deflection increases slightly to 14.6 mm (+ 0.7%) and 14.7 mm (+ 1.3%), respectively. This trend reflects the progressive release of boundary constraints, allowing more realistic lateral soil deformation and stress redistribution beneath the footing. The convergence observed in both load and deformation responses with increasing domain size confirms that smaller domains lead to unconservative predictions of bearing capacity and artificially stiff behavior. In contrast, larger domains provide a more accurate representation of soil–structure interaction. Beyond a domain size of approximately 2400 × 2400 × 70 mm, further increases result in negligible changes in both ultimate load and deflection, indicating that this domain extent is sufficient to eliminate boundary-induced effects and ensure reliable numerical predictions.

Effect of reinforcement ratio (Group GI)

Group GI was designed to investigate the influence of reinforcement ratio on the contact stress distribution, ultimate load capacity, deformation response, and energy absorption behavior of square reinforced concrete (RC) footings resting on sandy soil (Table 2). Seven footings (C1, C38, C67, C85, C110, C158, and C300) were analyzed. All specimens had identical geometry (1200 × 1200 × 400 mm), concrete compressive strength (20 MPa), and steel yield stress (407 MPa). The only variable was the reinforcement ratio, which increased from 0.26% in the control footing (C1) to 3.00% in specimen C300.

Central contact stress at failure

A progressive increase in central contact stress at failure load was observed with increasing reinforcement ratio. Compared with the reference footing C1 (464.6 kPa), the central contact stress increased slightly for moderate reinforcement ratios (Fig. 10), reaching increases of about 3–12% for specimens C38 to C110, as shown in Table 3. However, a pronounced increase occurred for higher reinforcement levels, with increases of approximately 52% for C158 and 102% for C300. This trend is primarily attributed to the enhanced flexural stiffness provided by higher reinforcement ratios. Increased reinforcement delays tensile cracking, limits crack propagation, and reduces stiffness degradation in the concrete footing. Consequently, the footing behaves more rigidly, promoting greater stress concentration beneath the central region (Fig. 11) and improving stress transfer efficiency to the supporting sandy soil.

Ultimate load capacity

The ultimate load capacity increased substantially with increasing reinforcement ratio, rising from 928.7 kN for the control footing to 1962.5 kN for specimen C300, corresponding to an increase of approximately 111%. Moderate reinforcement increases produced gradual improvements, whereas higher reinforcement ratios led to a more significant enhancement in capacity (Fig. 12a). This behavior reflects the improved flexural resistance of the footing as reinforcement increases. Higher reinforcement ratios delay yielding of tensile steel, control crack development, and allow more effective mobilization of soil bearing resistance before structural failure occurs. Additionally, the improved stiffness reduces premature cracking, enabling a more uniform redistribution of stresses within the footing–soil system.

Effect of reinforcement ratio on performance of footings.

Deformation behavior

Ultimate deflection increased from 14.5 mm for the control specimen to 33.5 mm for specimen C300 (Fig. 12b). Although increased reinforcement generally enhances stiffness under service loads, at ultimate conditions it improves ductility by preventing brittle cracking and allowing stress redistribution. This enables the footing to sustain higher loads while undergoing larger overall deformations prior to failure. The observed increase in ultimate deflection therefore reflects improved structural toughness and interaction between reinforcement confinement effects and progressive soil deformation beneath the footing.

Energy absorption capacity

Table 3 presents the calculated absorbed energy (E) values for the tested specimens, providing insights into their toughness and energy-dissipation capacity under loading. The absorbed energy represents the total work performed on a footing up to failure and was determined by integrating the area under the load–deflection curve. Numerical integration was carried out using the trapezoidal rule, where E corresponds to the cumulative sum of incremental load–deflection products up to the ultimate load13,41,42 (Fig. 13), as described in Eq. 6. This parameter serves as a key indicator of a footing’s ability to resist cracking, sustain deformation, and maintain structural integrity under progressive loading43,44.

Absorbed energy assessment31.

A significant improvement in energy absorption capacity was observed as reinforcement ratio increased (Fig. 12c). The absorbed energy increased from approximately 9098 kN·mm for the control footing to about 37,486 kN·mm for specimen C300, representing an increase exceeding 300%. This marked improvement results from the combined effects of higher ultimate load capacity and greater deformation prior to failure. Increased reinforcement enhances both strength and ductility, leading to a larger area under the load–deflection curve. Such behavior is particularly advantageous for foundations subjected to dynamic, cyclic, or seismic loading, where energy dissipation capacity plays a critical role in structural resilience.

The results of Group GI demonstrate that reinforcement ratio is a key parameter influencing the structural performance and soil–structure interaction of RC footings. Increasing reinforcement significantly improves load capacity, central contact stress, ductility, and energy absorption capacity. However, the nonlinear improvement observed at higher reinforcement levels suggests that soil response, cracking behavior, and stiffness degradation interact in a complex manner. These findings highlight the importance of carefully optimizing reinforcement ratios in foundation design. While increased reinforcement enhances performance, excessive reinforcement may lead to diminishing returns relative to cost. Therefore, realistic modeling of cracking-induced stiffness reduction and soil–structure interaction is essential for achieving safe and economical footing design, particularly for footings resting on granular soils where contact stress redistribution plays a critical role.

Additionally, the observed non-uniform contact stress distribution also raises the important question of potential edge lift-off. In the present study, the reinforced concrete footings behave as flexible systems rather than ideal rigid bodies due to cracking-induced stiffness degradation captured by the concrete damage plasticity (CDP) model. This flexibility plays a key role in governing the soil–footing interaction and the resulting stress distribution. The numerical results indicate that, although the contact stress becomes increasingly non-uniform with increasing load, complete edge lift-off was not observed within the investigated parameter ranges. Instead, the footing maintains continuous or near-continuous contact with the supporting soil, with stresses redistributing from the edges toward the central region as stiffness increases. This behavior can be attributed to the relatively compliant response of the cracked footing, which promotes deformation compatibility with the soil, in addition to the confining effect of the surrounding soil domain that limits excessive separation at the edges. It is worth noting that edge lift-off is more likely to occur in idealized rigid footings or under eccentric loading conditions. In contrast, the centrally loaded flexible footings considered in this study exhibit stress redistribution without full detachment, thereby maintaining overall stability.

Effect of footing thickness (Group GII)

Group GII was established to investigate the influence of footing thickness on contact stress distribution, ultimate load capacity, deformation characteristics, and energy absorption of square RC footings resting on sandy soil. Six footings were analyzed, namely C1 (reference specimen), C1-t450, C1-t500, C1-t350, C1-t300, and C1-t250 (Table 2). All specimens had identical plan dimensions (1200 × 1200 mm), reinforcement ratio (0.26%), steel yield strength (407 MPa), and concrete compressive strength (20 MPa). The only varying parameter was the footing thickness, which ranged from 250 mm to 500 mm.

Central contact stress at failure

Footing thickness exhibited a pronounced influence on the central contact stress at failure. Increasing thickness from 400 mm (C1) to 450 mm and 500 mm resulted in increases of approximately 24% and 42%, respectively. Conversely, reducing thickness to 350 mm, 300 mm, and 250 mm led to significant decreases of about 33%, 69%, and 83%, respectively (Table 3). This behavior is mainly attributed to changes in flexural stiffness. Thicker footings possess higher bending rigidity, which limits tensile cracking and stiffness degradation, allowing the footing to behave closer to a rigid plate. This promotes higher stress concentration beneath the central region and more efficient load transfer to the supporting soil (Fig. 14). In contrast, thinner footings experience cracking earlier and increased flexural deformation, leading to stress redistribution away from the center and reduced bearing efficiency (Fig. 15).

Contact stress distribution at the centerline of the Group GII footings.

Ultimate load capacity

The ultimate load capacity increased substantially with increasing thickness. Relative to the control footing (928.7 kN), ultimate load increased by approximately 20% for C1-t450 and 37% for C1-t500. In contrast, decreasing thickness resulted in reductions of about 20%, 43%, and 61% for thicknesses of 350 mm, 300 mm, and 250 mm, respectively (Fig. 16a). This trend reflects the strong dependence of footing load capacity on flexural rigidity. Increased thickness enhances bending resistance and reduces tensile stress concentrations within the concrete, delaying cracking and reinforcement yielding. Additionally, stiffer footing mobilizes greater soil bearing resistance before structural failure occurs. Conversely, thinner footings exhibit premature cracking and flexural failure, limiting the mobilization of soil strength.

Effect of footing thickness on performance of footings.

Deformation behavior

Ultimate deflection increased with increasing thickness, reaching 18.0 mm and 20.5 mm for C1-t450 and C1-t500, respectively, compared with 14.5 mm for the control footing (Fig. 16b). Conversely, thinner footings showed markedly lower ultimate deflections, decreasing to 9.5 mm, 5.0 mm, and 3.5 mm for thicknesses of 350 mm, 300 mm, and 250 mm. Although thicker footings are stiffer under service conditions, their higher load capacity allows them to sustain greater overall deformation before failure (Table 3). Thinner footings, by contrast, experience premature cracking and reduced structural integrity, leading to earlier failure at lower deformation levels.

Energy absorption capacity

Footing thickness had a substantial effect on energy absorption capacity (Fig. 16c). Increasing thickness to 450 mm and 500 mm resulted in energy absorption increases of approximately 40% and 74%, respectively. In contrast, reductions in thickness led to dramatic decreases, with energy absorption declining by approximately 46%, 80%, and 90% for 350 mm, 300 mm, and 250 mm thicknesses, as shown in Table 3. This behavior is governed by the combined influence of load capacity and deformation at failure. Thicker footings sustain higher loads and larger deformations, resulting in a significantly greater area under the load–deflection curve. Thinner footings fail earlier with limited deformation, reducing their ability to dissipate energy.

The results of Group GII highlight footing thickness as a critical parameter controlling stiffness, contact stress distribution, ultimate load capacity, and energy absorption of RC footings on sandy soils. Increasing thickness significantly enhances structural performance by improving flexural rigidity, delaying cracking, and promoting more efficient stress transfer to the soil. Conversely, insufficient thickness leads to premature cracking, reduced load capacity, and diminished energy absorption. These findings emphasize the importance of properly selecting footing thickness in design, particularly for foundations subjected to high loads or demanding service conditions. Accurate consideration of stiffness degradation, cracking behavior, and soil–structure interaction is essential for realistic prediction of footing performance and for achieving safe, economical foundation designs suitable for granular soil conditions.

Although the observed increase in load-carrying capacity with greater footing thickness and reinforcement is consistent with established design principles in ACI 31845 and Eurocode 246, the present results provide deeper insight into the underlying behavior. Specifically, the numerical findings reveal that such increases significantly influence the redistribution of contact stresses beneath the footing, leading to a more uniform stress profile and reduced stress concentration at the center. This behavior is closely associated with enhanced structural stiffness, which in turn modifies the soil–structure interaction and improves settlement performance. Therefore, the results demonstrate that the role of thickness and reinforcement extends beyond simply increasing ultimate capacity, contributing to improved load transfer mechanisms and overall foundation efficiency.

Effect of concrete compressive strength (Group GIII)

Group GIII was designed to investigate the influence of concrete compressive strength on contact stress distribution, ultimate load capacity, deformation characteristics, and energy absorption of square reinforced concrete (RC) footings resting on sandy soil (Table 2). Five footings were analyzed: C1 (reference specimen), C1-C25, C1-C35, C1-C45, and C1-C60. All specimens had identical plan dimensions (1200 × 1200 mm), footing thickness (400 mm), reinforcement ratio (0.26%), and steel yield stress (407 MPa). The only variable was the concrete compressive strength, which ranged from 20 MPa for control footing to 60 MPa for the strongest specimen.

Central contact stress at failure

A significant increase in central contact stress at failure load was observed with increasing concrete compressive strength (Fig. 17). Compared with the control footing C1 (464.6 kPa), the central contact stress increased by approximately 25%, 65%, 101%, and 152% for concrete strengths of 25, 35, 45, and 60 MPa, respectively (Table 3). This trend is primarily attributed to the higher elastic modulus and improved cracking resistance associated with higher-strength concrete. Stronger concrete delays crack initiation and limits crack propagation, thereby reducing stiffness degradation in the footing. As a result, the footing behaves more rigidly, leading to greater stress concentration beneath the central region (Fig. 18) and improved efficiency in load transfer to the supporting sandy soil.

Ultimate load capacity

The ultimate load capacity increased markedly with increasing concrete compressive strength (Fig. 19a). Relative to the reference footing (928.7 kN), the ultimate load increased by approximately 20% for C1-C25, 63% for C1-C35, 100% for C1-C45, and nearly 149% for C1-C60 (Table 3). This behavior reflects the enhanced compressive strength and flexural resistance of higher-strength concrete. Increased concrete strength improves the footing’s ability to resist bending stresses and compressive stresses transmitted from the soil reaction. Additionally, stronger concrete reduces the likelihood of premature crushing or cracking in compression zones, allowing greater mobilization of soil bearing resistance before structural failure occurs.

Deformation behavior

Ultimate deflection increased progressively with increasing concrete compressive strength, rising from 14.5 mm for the control footing to 35.0 mm for specimen C1-C60 (Fig. 19b). Although high-strength concrete is typically associated with reduced material ductility, the observed increase in global deflection is governed by the footing–soil interaction. Higher-strength concrete delays crack and allow the footing to sustain higher loads prior to failure, which results in larger overall displacements at ultimate conditions. Thus, the increase in deformation reflects improved structural integrity and enhanced load-carrying capacity rather than increased material ductility.

Energy absorption capacity

Energy absorption capacity exhibited the most pronounced improvement among all measured parameters (Fig. 19c). Compared with control footing, energy absorption increased by approximately 45%, 128%, 214%, and 367% for concrete strengths of 25, 35, 45, and 60 MPa, respectively (Table 3). This substantial enhancement is attributed to the combined increase in ultimate load and deformation capacity, which enlarges the area under the load–deflection curve. Higher-strength concrete enables the footing to sustain greater loads while maintaining structural stability, thereby significantly improving the toughness of the footing–soil system.

The findings of Group GIII confirm that concrete compressive strength is a dominant parameter governing the structural performance and soil–structure interaction behavior of RC footings resting on sandy soils. Increasing concrete strength significantly enhances central contact stress, load-carrying capacity, deformation capacity at failure, and energy absorption characteristics. These results highlight the importance of incorporating realistic concrete stiffness degradation and strength characteristics in foundation design models. Reliance on simplified uniform contact stress assumptions may underestimate stress concentration and structural capacity, particularly for footings constructed with higher-strength concrete. Proper consideration of concrete strength effects can therefore lead to safer, more efficient, and economically optimized foundation designs.

As the structural stiffness increases, the agreement between analytical and numerical results improves significantly. A comparable trend is observed with increasing footing thickness, where the ratio decreases markedly from 8.86 (C1–t250) to approximately unity (1.04 for C1–t500). This behavior indicates that the assumptions underlying Terzaghi’s theory become more valid as the footing response approaches that of a rigid body. The influence of concrete compressive strength further supports this observation. Increasing the strength from C25 to C60 results in a substantial increase in FE-predicted contact stress (579.16–1170.76 kPa), while the corresponding ratio decreases from 1.18 to 0.59. This trend demonstrates that the analytical model becomes increasingly conservative for high-strength, stiffened systems. Overall, these findings indicate that Terzaghi-based formulations provide reasonable estimates only when the rigid footing assumption is approximately satisfied. The model tends to overestimate the bearing capacity for flexible systems, while becoming conservative for stiffer configurations. This highlights the limitations of traditional bearing capacity approaches and underscores the necessity of incorporating soil–structure interaction effects and stiffness-dependent behavior into advanced foundation design methodologies.

A validated nonlinear finite element model was employed to simulate soil–structure interaction. Based on the results, the following key conclusions are drawn.

The developed FE model showed excellent agreement with experimental data, with only about 1% difference in ultimate load and approximately 3% in settlement, confirming its suitability for studying RC footing behavior.

Limitations and future recommendations

This study provides valuable insights into the behavior of RC footings on cohesionless soil, yet certain limitations should be acknowledged. The analysis was intentionally restricted to dense sand to isolate the effects of footing-related parameters—namely reinforcement ratio, thickness, and concrete compressive strength—on contact stress distribution and failure behavior. Considering multiple soil types or densities could introduce additional variability, potentially masking the distinct influence of footing stiffness and cracking-induced degradation, which remain the primary focus. Consequently, the observed trends are largely governed by the interaction between footing stiffness and soil rigidity. A representative footing width of 1.2 m was employed. While scale effects—particularly the variation of the bearing capacity factor Nγ with footing width—may influence absolute bearing capacity, comparative trends remain robust, as all parametric analyses were performed with consistent footing dimensions.

Future research should explore soil variability by including different densities (e.g., loose or medium-dense sands) and types (e.g., clay or layered soils) to evaluate the generalizability of the findings. Investigating a wider range of footing sizes would clarify the role of scale bearing capacity, contact stress distribution, and failure mechanisms. Incorporating advanced constitutive soil models, such as critical-state or state-dependent plasticity, could better capture pressure-dependent stiffness, strain-softening, and dilatancy behavior. The response of RC footings under cyclic, dynamic, or seismic loading should also be assessed to quantify energy dissipation and performance beyond monotonic concentric loading. Finally, full-scale or large-scale experiments under varied soil conditions are recommended to validate and refine the numerical predictions.
