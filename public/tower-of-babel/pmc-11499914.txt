Amending clayey and sandy soils with nano - bio phosphorous for regulating tomato growth, biochemical, and physiological characteristics
Authors: Abolfazl Baroutkoob, Maryam Haghighi, Mohammad Ali Hajabbasi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Phosphorus is a critical nutrient that significantly enhances tomato production, so maintaining an adequate level of phosphorus plays an essential role in enhancing the growth of tomato by being present in the soil. This study assessed the impact of soil texture and phosphorus content on tomato plant properties using a factorial, complete, randomized design with four replications. Treatments included clayey and sandy soils with varying phosphorus sources: non-phosphorus (P0), calcium phosphate (CaP1 and CaP2), and nano-hydroxyapatite (PN1 and PN2), where 1 indicates a concentration of 0.12 g and 2 indicates a concentration of 0.23 g per 5-kilogram pot of fertilizer. Results indicated that treatments significantly influenced yield parameters such as average fruit weight, juice content, antioxidant activity, and fruit volume. In the clayey soil, CaP2 treatment had a superior effect on yield, average fruit weight, and shoot fresh weight. In comparison with sandy conditions, CaP2 produced a 50% increase in fruit number, 29% increase in average fruit weight, and 91% increase in fruit yield. The treatments then impacted the shoot fresh weight and root length, while the phosphorus concentration appeared to be more dependent on soil type than on phosphorus sources. Similar to the CaP1 and CaP2 treatments, the PN1 treatment in clay soil also resulted in the highest fresh and dry weights of tomato shoots when compared with the control group. Generally, the findings from this study suggest that the use of CaP2 can serve as a reliable method to improve the growth, yield, and fruit quality of tomatoes, especially in clayey soil environments. However, nano-based phosphorous sources need to be tested more to see if they can improve tomato performance in a range of soil conditions. Also, further research should look into the long-term effects of phosphorous interventions on soil health and sustainability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-76389-8.

Introduction

Tomatoes, with the scientific name Solanum lycopersicon L., are a member of the Solanaceae family. Furthermore, it is cultivated globally. Global tomato production reached 182 million tons in 2022, with output exceeding 20 trillion tons1. Although it is usually grown as an annual crop, in its natural habitat, it is a valuable perennial vegetable2. Tomatoes are known for their health benefits and are a natural source of antioxidants rich in lycopene, carotene, and anthocyanins3,4.

Phosphorus plays a crucial role in tomato growth and is an essential component of DNA and cell membranes5. Phosphorus deficiency can significantly harm crop productivity and, in severe cases, lead to plant death. Despite its importance, phosphorus from chemical fertilizers is often unavailable due to non-biological soil processes, such as surface absorption and leaching6. This indicates a significant gap in current agricultural practices regarding phosphorous management.

Recent studies indicate that phosphorus fertilization can substantially affect the enzymes involved in the mevalonate pathway, which is critical for the biosynthesis of terpenes key constituents of essential oils7. The availability of phosphorus appears to directly influence the production and accumulation of valuable secondary compounds8. Therefore, exploring innovative methods for the production and application of phosphorus fertilizer are essential for advancing sustainable agricultural practices.

Nano-fertilizers are a crucial part of modern agricultural techniques for increasing productivity9. According to Zahra et al.10, nanofertilizers can enter plant cells with greater efficiency, allowing for the regulation of nutrient release, waste reduction, and improved utilization of nutrients11. These fertilizers can enter plant cells more effectively, allowing for better regulation of nutrient release and waste reduction12. For instance, nano-hydroxyapatite improves nutrient absorption and promotes root elongation in tomato seedlings13. The increased availability of nutrients, which facilitate optimal growth and metabolic processes, ultimately boosts crop yield14.

However, nanoparticle-based fertilizers directly applied to soil have certain limitations. They may have a short shelf life and stability issues and can be affected by environmental factors such as pH, temperature, and radiation. This can impact their performance and effectiveness15. Nano bio-based fertilizers, on the other hand, are becoming more popular than traditional chemical fertilizers. Chemical fertilizers have negative impacts on the natural environment, such as toxicity and long-lasting residues in soil. Nano-bio fertilizers are created by combining engineered nanoparticles with biofertilizers15.

In addition to the source of phosphorus, the availability of phosphorus in soil is influenced by soil texture16. Sandy soils are looser and fragile, promoting root growth, whereas clayey soils are more compact and have a high capacity for phosphorus adsorption, which correlates positively with clay content17. As a result, clayey soils have greater phosphorus adsorption, whereas sandy soils tend to maintain higher concentrations of available phosphorus after phosphorus fertilization16. Sandy and medium-textured soils typically exhibit higher phosphorus deficiency and lower phosphate adsorption capacity than clayey soils18. This nutrient isn’t as easy to find in sandy soils because phosphorus doesn’t stick as well to sand particles and there is less clay and silt, which lowers both the adsorption capacity and specific surface area19.

The current study’s novelty lies in its approach to understanding the interactive effects of soil texture and sources of soil phosphorous fertilizer on tomato plant vigor and growth. The hypothesis was that applying nano-hydroxyapatite would improve phosphorus availability and uptake in tomato plants compared to traditional calcium phosphate fertilizers. The other approach was to show how soil texture (sandy and clayey) would interact with the mentioned treatments to improve some morphological, physiological, and biochemical characteristics, as well as the growth and yield of the tomato plant.

Materials and methods

Experimental area

The study was conducted in the soilless Isfahan University of Technology’s greenhouse (32°43′4.80″N and 51° 31′ 55.20″ E), with an average daily/nightly temperature of 25 ± 2/ 17 ± 2 °C. During this period, humidity levels were maintained at a consistent 70%, and a photosynthetic photon flux density of 270 µmol m² s⁻¹ was provided to ensure optimal growth conditions for the plants.

Treatments and experimental design

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate how soil texture and the sources of phosphorus affect the morphological and physiological characteristics of tomato plants. The experiment followed a factorial design in the form of a completely randomized design (CRD) with four replications. The treatments consisted of two types of soil texture (clay and sandy), as detailed in Table 1. We specifically selected clay soil from the Kohrang area based on the findings of Kelishadi et al.20 which confirmed its classification as clay. Our criteria for soil selection focused on distinguishing between clay and sandy soil types, as this differentiation is crucial for understanding soil behavior in relation to phosphorus availability. The sources of phosphorus treatments included non-phosphorus (P0), calcium phosphate at dosages equal to and twice the recommended soil test (0.12 and 0.23 g for each 5 kg pot) (CaP1 and CaP2), and nano-hydroxyapatite at dosages equal to and twice the recommended soil test (0.12 and 0.23 g for each 5 kg pot) (PN1 and PN2). The goal of using these various types of phosphorus was to see how well nano-hydroxyapatite from natural sources (bone) worked compared to regular fertilizers, especially calcium phosphate. This comparison allows us to assess the potential advantages of using more sustainable phosphorus sources in agricultural practices.

Plant growth conditions

Tomato seedlings (Lycopersicum esculentum var. Seminis 4129) were placed in a blend of cocopit and perlite (50:50 V/V). Soil samples were collected from two locations: Fami (33° 40′ N and 51° 25′ E), known for its sandy texture, and Kohrang (32° 49′ N and 50° 26′ E), which had a clay texture, subsequently dried with air and filtered through a 2 mm sieve to measure the physicochemical characteristics. We compiled all relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation for plant cultivation. When the seedlings reached a length of 15 cm with 3–4 leaves, they were transferred into pots containing sandy or clay soil (the pots have almost 1 L volume, with a height of 15 cm, a diameter of 25 cm, with a soil volume of 6000 cm3 filled with 5 kg soil). The seedlings were fed nutrient solutions composed of KNO3 (10 g), CuSO4 (0.04 g), Na2MoO4 (0.01 g), ZnSO4 (0.1 g), MnSO4 (0.26 g), MgSO4 (2.5 g), Fe EDTA (0.46 g), H3BO3 (0.06 g) in 20 L of water, 1–2 times per week. In the early stages of growth, plants received half the recommended amount of the nutritional formula. Manual watering of the seedlings was performed as needed. The fertilizer treatments were thoroughly mixed with the soil before the pot filling. Calcium phosphate was obtained from Merck, Germany, and nano-hydroxyapatite was obtained from cow bones from local markets. The various parameters were measured 3 months after the seedlings were planted.

Hydroxyapatite preparation

Hydroxyapatite powder was prepared locally from bio-sourced nano-phosphorus extracted from cow bones. The bones were washed, sliced, and boiled in water at 100 °C for 3 h, then dehydration in an oven at the same temperature for 24 h. After calcination at 750 °C for 3 h, the bones cooled naturally, resulting in a white powder free of carbon and organic materials. The nano-hydroxyapatite powder was then produced via milling and screening. Spectral analysis of hydroxyapatite obtained via calcination (Fig. S1 and Table S1 supplementary file) and X-Ray diffraction analysis of hydroxyapatite derived from bovine bone using the same method as that reported by Jalali et al. has not been published yet (Fig. S2 supplementary file). Additionally, images of samples prepared using a scanning electron microscope and taken at two different sizes (400 nm and 500 nm) are included in (Fig. S3 supplementary files).

Parameters measured

Yield and appearance of fruits

We harvested all fruits from a single plant to measure yield and appearance and then calculated the average. Four replicates were considered for each treatment. After harvesting, the plants were cleaned. A steel blade was used to cut the shoots from the roots, and a precise scale (0.01 balances) was used to weigh the fruits, roots, and stems as they remained fresh. The weights of the fruits, shoots, and roots were measured both fresh and dry after drying for two days at 70 °C to obtain a constant weight. A caliper measuring in millimeters was used to measure the fruit diameter, and a ruler measuring in centimeters was used to measure the fruit and root lengths. The fruit volume was calculated using the cubic centimeters of water displacement as the base. Following the experiment, the total number of fruits produced by each tomato was counted to determine the quantity of fruits produced by each plant. The weight and number of fruits collected from each plant were used to calculate fruit yield.

Firmness of the fruit

At harvest, the firmness of the pericarp was assessed using a penetrometer (model OSK-I-10,576,Ogawa Seiki Co. Ltd., Tokkyo, Japan), using, the approach outlined by Pathaveerat et al.21. The force applied to the penetration probe was measured for data analysis, and the findings were quantified in Newton units.

Color index

The fruits were collected when they reached 70% color break. The fruits were photographed using a Canon EOS 6D digital camera, ensuring that they were placed in the same position and illuminated with uniform lighting. Photoshop CS5 was then used to apply image processing and coding techniques to extract color components and other relevant information from the images. The L*, a*, and b* indices were measured in pixels22.

Relative water content

Fully developed mature leaves from the middle of the plant were carefully removed from the bottom of the leaf blade, with four replicates selected at random, and their fresh weight (FW) was promptly measured. The leaves were then soaked in pure water for 12 h at ambient temperature. The leaves were cut using a sheet of paper to eliminate any extra moisture, and their weight was documented as turgid weight (TW). The dry weight (DW) was determined by subjecting saturated leaves to an oven-drying process at 60 °C. Chlorophyll index

The chlorophyll content in mature middle leaves was measured using a non-destructive method with a Minolta SPAD (SPAD502 plus, Japan) leaf chlorophyll meter with four replicates selected randomly. The middle mature leaves were specifically selected for measurement, and the positions of the leaves were consistent across all treatments, ensuring comparability. Three readings were made from different leaves of each plant for the sum of nine measurements for each duplicate. The mean of these measurements was subsequently determined24.

Total carbohydrate content of fruits

The anthrone technique was used to measure all soluble carbohydrates. First, 1.0 g of fruit tissue was mixed with 5 mL of 95% ethanol and subsequently subjected to centrifugation at a speed of 3500 rpm for 10 min at a temperature of 10 °C. The resultant supernatant was transferred to a fresh tube, and this procedure was repeated three times, with 70% ethanol used in the second and third iterations. After completing the third step, 100 µl of the extract and 3 mL of anthrone solution containing 0.15 g of anthrone mixed with 100 mL of 70% sulfuric acid were used and heated at 95 °C for 15 min. A UV Spectrophotometer Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan (UV 160 A model) was employed to assess absorbance at 625 nm after cooling. This measurement was performed to quantify total soluble carbohydrates, with glucose used as the standard25,26.

Proline concentrations

The ninhydrin test, as described by Bates et al.27, was hired to evaluate proline levels. First, leaf samples (0.2 g) were mixed thoroughly at 4 °C with 10 milliliters of a solution containing 3% sulfosalicylic acid. The resultant solution was subsequently placed in an incubator and then centrifuged at 5000 revolutions per min for 20 min. The liquid portion was mixed with a solution consisting of 2 ml of ninhydrin (with a concentration of 2.5%), 2 ml of phosphoric acid (with a concentration of 60% volume/volume), and 1 mL of concentrated glacial acetic acid (with a concentration of 100%), and the amount of light absorbed was measured at a wavelength of 518 nm.

Total phenolic compounds

To evaluate total phenolics, a solution consisting of 1 mL of extract with a concentration ranging from 100 to 500 µg.mL− 1 was combined with 2.5 mL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent at a concentration of 10% (w/v). Following a 5-min delay, a solution containing 2.0 mL of Na2CO3 with a concentration of 7.5% and 1 mL of pure water was added to the mixture. The resultant blend was exposed to incubation at 50 °C for 10 min with periodic agitation. Subsequently, the specimen was chilled, and the absorbance was quantified using a spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 730 nm. A blank sample without extracts was used as a reference. Quantification of the results was defined as equivalent to the mg of gallic acid per g of fresh extract28.

Antioxidant activity

The levels of antioxidants in tomato leaves were evaluated according to the method outlined by Koleva et al.29 study from 2002. Initially, 200 µL of the sample was combined using 800-µL Tris-HCl buffer in a 7.4 test tube. Subsequently, 1 mL of DPPH solution was introduced, and the mixture was mixed for 10 s using a test tube shaker before being left at room temperature and away from light. After 30 min from the addition of the DPPH solution, the amount of light in the solution at a wavelength of 517 nm was measured using a spectrophotometer. The blank solution consisted of 800 µL of Tris-HCl buffer mixed with 1.2 mL ethanol. Upon introduction of the sample, the absorbance was immediately referred to as As and the absorbance in the presence of ethanol was referred to as Ac. Nutrient concentration

The fruit, root, and leaf tissues were cut into tiny sections and dehydrated in an oven at 60 °C for 48 h. The leaf samples were ground with a grinding machine, placed in small plastic bags, and stored in a dry location. For analysis, 0.5 g of each sample was placed inside a container and heated to 550 °C in a furnace for 6 h to obtain dry ash. The ashes were fully digested by adding drops of 2 N-HNO3 and then 1 N-HCl solution. The mixture was warmed at 70 °C for 20 min until a lightly colored solution was obtained. The remaining substance was purified and transferred to a 50 mL container using purified water and Whatman 4 filter paper. Nitrogen levels were analyzed using an automated Kjeldahl apparatus (V40, Iran) as described by Wang et al.31. Calcium content was determined using a flame photometer (Model PFP7) from Jenway, England, and phosphorus concentration was assessed using the ammonium molybdate vanadate technique as outlined by Karadağ et al.32.

Extraction of essential oils

The steam distillation method was used to extract essential oil using a Clevenger apparatus (MS-E104, South Korea). This equipment comprises three primary parts: a glass flask, an oil separator tube, and a cooling system. The dried samples were finely ground and placed into a 1-L flask, followed by the addition of approximately 1 L of distilled water. Subsequently, the device was operated for 5 h until an oily layer formed on the water surface, which was collected in a tube33. Gas chromatography, also known as GC, was employed to identify essential oil compounds. An MS-GC apparatus (Agilent Technologies, located in Palo Alto, California, United States) was used in the well-equipped research laboratory at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis

The terpenoid composition was examined using a gas chromatograph (Model No. 7890; Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The instrument was fitted with an HP-5 MS 30 m long slender tube measuring 0.25 mm in diameter and with a film thickness of 0.25 mm. Initially, the oven was preheated to 70 °C for 2 min before being raised to 200 °C and held at that temperature for 1 min. The temperatures of the injector and detector were kept constant at 210 °C and 220 °C, respectively. Helium served as the carrier gas at a pressure of 17.7 pounds per square inch at the inlet and a split ratio of 1:10. A connection was established between the gas chromatograph and an Agilent 5975 C mass spectrometer, which operated at a specific ionization voltage of 70 electrons in the detector. The temperatures of the ion generator and quadrupole mass were set to 230 °C and 150 °C, respectively. The mass spectrum analysis covered a range of 35 to 500 mass-to-charge ratio units. The GC MSD Chem-Station software was employed as the operational software. The retention indices were calculated using the Kovats index formula based on the retention times for n-alkanes ranging from C8 to C21 that were injected before analyzing the essential oil under identical conditions. The essential oil identification components were determined by comparing the retention indices (RIs) with literature values and mass spectra with those in the NIST 08 and Wiley275 libraries stored in the ChemStation data system. Adams34 describes the process of determining the composition percentage by analyzing the highest points of the overall terpenoid composition.

Statistical techniques

The study used a factorial experimental design with a CRD with 4 replications. The data collected from the trial were organized in Excel, assessed for normality, and log-transformed using the Shapiro-Wilk test when required. The Statistix 8 software was used to perform data analysis, which is based in Tallahassee, FL, the USA. The data were subjected to two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and the significance of the means was assessed using the least significant difference (LSD) test at a significance level of P < 0.05. PCA was conducted using Statgraphics Centurion in its sixteenth version. A heat map graph was generated using the website https://discover.nci.nih.gov/cimminer/oneMatrix.do.

Results

The effects of type of soil and different sources of phosphorus on growth characteristics and color index of fruit

ANOVA revealed that soil characteristics could significantly alter various fruit growth parameters, including fresh and dry weight, length, firmness, number of fruits, average weight, and fruit yield (Table S2 supplementary file). Furthermore, the use of phosphorous sources had a significant impact on most growth parameters. The interaction between soil and sources of phosphorus also had a statistically significant impact on fruit growth parameters (Table S3 supplementary file).

In clay soil, the fresh weight of fruit was greater than in sandy soil. In sandy soil, CaP2 treatment yielded the highest fresh weight of fruits. Conversely, in clay soil, all phosphorus sources significantly enhanced fruit fresh weight, with no notable differences among them, all achieving high levels (Fig. 1A). The clayey soil significantly increased the dry weight of tomatoes, with PN1 and CaP2 recording the highest values compared to other phosphorus sources (Fig. 1B). The length of the fruits increased in the clayey soil compared to the sandy soil, with the clayey soil showing the longest fruits at P0, PN1, and CaP2 compared to the other phosphorus treatments. However, the sandy soil did not exhibit a significant difference in fruit length among the different phosphorous treatments (Fig. 1C). CaP2 application produced the highest fruit volume in sandy soil, while CaP1 application produced the lowest fruit volume when compared to other phosphorous sources. In contrast, in clay soil, there were no significant differences in fruit volume among the various phosphorus sources, as all treatments resulted in similarly high fruit volumes (Fig. 1D). The highest number of fruits was observed following the application of CaP2 and clayey soil (Fig. 1E). The different forms of phosphorus did not significantly affect the average fruit weight in clayey and sandy soils; however, the clayey soil with CaP2 treatment yielded the highest average fruit weight (Fig. 1F). The maximum yield was observed in the clayey soil treated with CaP2, and it was significantly higher than that in the sandy soil (Fig. 1G). The sandy soil had the highest percentage of water content in the fruit, while the clayey texture soil with the application of PN1 showed the lowest percentage (Fig. 1H).

The effects of type of soil and different sources of phosphorus on some growth parameters of shoot and root of the tomato

The data analysis indicated that different soil textures could notably alter the fresh and dry weight of shoots, as well as the fresh weight and length of roots, but did not have a significant effect on the dry weight of roots (Table S4 supplementary file). Furthermore, the application of phosphorus sources and the interaction between soil and phosphorus sources had a significant influence on shoot and root parameters (Table S5 supplementary file).

The clayey soil exhibited the highest fresh weight of shoots compared to the sandy texture soil. Compared to the P0 treatment, the shoots with the most fresh weight had the most CaP1 phosphorous, followed by CaP2 and PN1 (Fig. 4A). Compared to sandy soil, the clayey soil texture significantly increased the dry weight of shoots, while the control treatment showed the lowest dry weight of shoots for both soil textures (Fig. 4B). Specifically, the CaP1 treatment in the sandy soil led to the highest fresh and dry weight of roots. CaP2 treatment improved the fresh and dry weight of roots in the clayey texture soil (Fig. 4C, D). After applying CaP2, we observed the longest root length in sandy soil, which increased by 15% compared to the control and by 30% compared to PN1 (Fig. 4E).

The effects of type of soil and different sources of phosphorus on some physiological parameters of the tomato

The combination of different soil textures and phosphorous levels significantly altered the amounts of RWC, fluorescence chlorophyll, proline, and EL. In addition, the sources of phosphorus treatment and the interaction between soil texture and phosphorus levels had a significant effect on all physiological parameters (Tables S6 and S7 supplementary file).

Among the different sources of phosphorus, the highest RWC was observed in soil treated with CaP2 for both textures of soil (Table 2). Chlorophyll fluorescence was higher in the clayey soil than in the sandy soil, and the highest chlorophyll fluorescence levels were observed in the CaP2 treatment compared with other phosphorus sources (Table 2). The chlorophyll index exhibited the highest value in the sandy soil when P0 was applied. In contrast, the application of PN1 treatment in the sandy soil led to a decrease in the chlorophyll index compared with the P0 control (Table 2). The highest carbohydrate content was observed following the application of CaP2 and PN1 in both soil textures (Table 2). The sandy soil treated with the PN1 phosphorus source exhibited the highest proline content, whereas the sandy soil treated with CaP2 had the lowest proline concentration (Table 2). The highest amount of phenol was observed in the clayey soil when CaP1 was applied, whereas the lowest amount was observed with P0 (Table 2). The antioxidant activity of sandy soil increased with the application of CaP2 (Table 2). The P0 and CaP2 treatments in sandy soil recorded the highest EL, while the other phosphorus sources showed low EL values without significant differences. The PN1 treatment yielded the highest EL in clay soil, with the other phosphorus sources displaying similarly low EL values without significant differences. (Table 2).

Discussions

The study collected initial data regarding the possible effects of CaP and PN on tomato development, physiological traits, and mineral composition. Phosphorus fertilizer amounts and the presence of other nutrients affect crop responses to phosphorus fertilization differently35. Furthermore, the intricate interactions between soil, climate, and agricultural management practices can affect the amount of phosphorus in plant tissues as well as the development and absorption of nutrients by these plants36.

The effects of soil type and different sources of phosphorus on some growth parameters and color index of tomato

The results of this study indicate that the application of CaP2 and NP1 significantly enhances fruit weight, length, and volume in clay soil compared to the control soil. Phosphorus is well known for its vital role in boosting fruit production in plants. A balanced supply of phosphorus promotes robust root and vegetative growth, leading to improved nutrient absorption and translocation to fruits, ultimately resulting in higher yields37. Furthermore, Abd-Elghany et al.38 demonstrated that the growth of Ocimum basilicum is positively influenced by nano calcium phosphate (NCaP). In our study, applying PN1 to clay soil resulted in a remarkable increase in fresh shoot weight (by 70%) and dry weight (by 34%) compared to the control. This growth enhancement may be attributed to the unique properties of highly reactive nanoparticles, which possess a greater reactive area density and specialized surface area. Additionally, hydroxyapatite nanoparticles are known to stimulate root growth, thereby improving nutrient and water absorption in plants39. Increased absorption can elevate carbohydrate and chlorophyll levels, further promoting plant growth40. In contrast, our study found that root lengths were greater in sandy soil than in clay soil. Andrén et al.41 noted that sandy soils support deeper root distribution due to better downward water seepage because they have lower field capacity than clay soils. This characteristic leads to higher nutrient concentrations in deeper soil layers42. However, the observed decrease in root dry weight in our study under nanofertilizer conditions might be linked to an increase in free radicals, which can oxidize cell membranes and inhibit plant growth. Moreover, the accumulation of nanofertilizers on root surfaces can clog root pores, reducing water absorption by the fine roots. These observations align with the findings of Lu et al.43. On the other hand, when comparing sandy and clay soils, treatment with PN1 in clay soil resulted in an impressive increase in shoot fresh weight 82%, shoot dry weight 33%, and fruit dry weight 37%. Sharma et al.42 suggested that higher mineral nutrient contents in clay soil enhance nutrient absorption, thereby improving plant growth.

The L* value of wheat grains increased with higher applications of phosphorus fertilizer, according to a study by Shahbazi et al.44, which led to a shift in grain surface color leaning toward a brighter and pale tone tint. These findings indicate that using fertilizers like phosphorus affects the chemical and physical characteristics of grains and other qualitative attributes, including surface color. Cucumber fruits treated with a pre-harvest foliar application of 20 ppm Zn nanoparticles had the greatest L*, a*, and b* values; control plants had the lowest values45. The application of PN1 increased the L*, a*, and b* indices, with the a* index increasing more than the other indices, according to the results of this study. The fruit turned red as the index increased.

The effects of soil type and different sources of phosphorus on some physiological parameters of tomato

The RWC of plants is a crucial indicator of their water status, which is significantly influenced by soil texture and phosphorous availability. Studies have shown that a decrease in leaf RWC correlates with reduced turgor potential, which hampers plant growth due to insufficient water availability for cellular processes46,47. Conversely, an increase in RWC suggests that CaP application enhances water uptake or reduces water loss, thereby improving leaf water potential48. This positive effect is attributed to phosphorus’s vital role in various plant functions, including enzyme regulation, nutrient transport, and carbohydrate metabolism. Furthermore, phosphorus is essential for the development of robust root systems49, which enhances the plant’s ability to access and absorb water, leading to improved RWC50. Our findings indicate that plants grown in clay soil exhibited higher leaf RWC than those grown in sandy soil, particularly under CaP treatment. This difference is likely due to the coarse texture of sandy soil, which has lower water retention capacity, resulting in reduced water availability for plants51.

Phosphorus content is important for plant stability because it supports green pigments in leaves. However, the intake of ideal phosphorus levels is necessary for enhancing metabolic features and the production of color molecules52. Apricot seedlings grown under ideal phosphorous conditions exhibit higher levels of total chlorophyll and greater plant growth53. Conversely, low levels of phosphorus cause lower levels of protein and chlorophyll54. Fv/Fm were used as noninvasive techniques to assess the operational status of photosynthetic machinery. According to El-Yazal et al.55, phosphorous application greatly increases Fv/Fm in bean plant leaves. Phosphorus plays a direct role in photosynthesis, particularly during ATP synthesis (ADP + Pi = ATP). Disruptions in phosphorous nutrition can impair this process, affecting light-dependent photosynthetic mechanisms such as electron transfer from PSII to PSI56. Our study revealed that chlorophyll fluorescence was higher in clay soil than in sandy soil, with the highest levels observed following CaP2 treatment. The enhanced chlorophyll fluorescence in clay soil can be attributed to its superior water retention capacity, which enhances phosphorous availability and uptake by plant roots. Improving the nutrient status facilitates optimal chlorophyll synthesis and efficient photosynthesis.

Because they scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS), soluble carbohydrates are essential for preserving and improving membrane stability57. Carbohydrates are used by plants for various functions, including energy storage, signaling, and stress adaptation; thus, their accumulation is associated with improved plant resistance58. According to the findings of our study, applying PN1 to the clayey soil increased the amount of carbohydrates by 16% over the control. Additionally, Amira et al.59 reported that Baobab plants treated with nano-hydroxyapatite spray exhibited significant increases in total carbohydrates and various developmental parameters, including fresh weight. Sajadinia et al.60 noted that nanohydroxyapatite synthesis technologies could help prevent phosphorous loss, making it more accessible for plant uptake. In contrast to loam and sandy loam soils, humus soils had the largest soluble sugar concentrations, as reported by Ji et al.61. The amounts of carbohydrates in the clayey and sandy soils did not differ considerably from one another, according to our research findings. It can be speculated that although clay soil retains more moisture and nutrients, which support overall plant health and growth, the actual synthesis and accumulation of carbohydrates can be affected by other physiological processes. For example, plants in clay soils may prioritize growth over carbohydrate storage under certain conditions, especially when the soil is not stressed.

Proline serves as a protective osmotic solute that helps maintain the structure of macromolecules and organelles, as noted by Xu et al.62. Proline is generally considered a nontoxic osmolyte because it dissolves more easily in water than many other amino acids63. Under stress conditions, it helps to scavenge ROS and preserve the integrity of plant cells. In bean plants, the application of calcium phosphate nanoparticles (CaP-NPs) resulted in proline increases of 12%, 30%, and 24% during the vegetative, blooming, and pod stages, respectively64. Furthermore, our study found that applying PN1 to sandy soil boosted proline concentrations by 12% compared with the control. Jahan et al.65. observed that soybeans grown in sandy loam soil had higher leaf proline content than those grown in clay loam soil. This difference may be attributed to the greater water retention capacity of clay loam soil, which affects the water stress conditions experienced by plants66.

Tomatoes, which contain high levels of sugars such as glucose and fructose, serve as precursors for phenolic compounds67. Research by Cozzolino et al. indicates that a balanced supply of essential mineral nutrients, including nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, is linked to increased sugar accumulation, which in turn boosts phenolic content68. Phosphorus plays a critical role in cellular energy metabolism, phospholipid formation, and nucleic acid synthesis, necessitating its substantial presence in the production of primary and secondary metabolites. Furthermore, Tavarini et al. found that phosphorous availability significantly enhances in vitro antioxidant activity, total phenol levels, and flavonoid levels in stevia plants. Attarzadeh et al.69 demonstrated that phosphorous fertilizers can influence the expression of key genes in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, thereby affecting the conversion of caffeic acid. The highest phenol concentrations were found in clay soil treated with PC1. The removal of organic matter led to a notable decrease in the sorption of phenolic acids onto soil particles, indicating that organic matter contributes significantly to this sorption process70. Therefore, it can be inferred that the greater organic matter content in clay soil compared to sandy soil in this study resulted in increased phenol concentrations.

The extracts of Solanum nigrum from clayey loam soil exhibit enhanced antioxidant activity. This variation in antioxidant capability suggests that environmental factors, including soil type, may influence the concentrations of bioactive compounds71. High levels of flavonoids and phenolics corresponded to the antioxidant efficacy of Solanum nigrum extracts, as assessed by the DPPH assay. These results support the hypothesis that flavonoids and phenolic acids are key contributors to antioxidant activity72. Furthermore, numerous studies have established a direct correlation between total phenol concentration in plants and their antioxidant capacity73. Our research indicated that the highest DPPH radical scavenging activity was associated with CaP1 and CaP2 treatment.

The effects of soil type and different forms of phosphorus on some mineral elements of tomato

In research conducted by Garcia-Caparros et al.,74 phosphorus application increased the levels of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium in the roots and shoots of bean plants compared to the control treatment. These results indicate a strong connection between improved vegetative growth and increased nutrient levels in the stems and underground parts of plants. Additionally, studies by Zahra et al.75 showed that the largest increases in root and shoot length occurred alongside improvements in the phosphorus content of the plants. According to Yusuf et al.76 elevated nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium levels enhance photosynthesis, which in turn promotes vegetative growth. Furthermore, the addition of phosphorus led to enhanced lateral root development and an increased root-to-stem ratio, thereby improving the plant’s ability to absorb water and essential nutrients77. These findings align with those of Ehiagiator et al.78 who suggested that increasing phosphorus application can raise nutrient levels in plants, leading to improved nutrient uptake. In our study, the highest phosphorus content in leaves and fruits was observed in clay soil, particularly following the application of CaP1. Moreover, clayey soil treated with CaP2 exhibited the highest calcium levels in fruits and roots. In our research, the phosphorus content was higher in clay soil than in sandy soil due to the textural properties of clay soil and its ability to retain water and nutrients. Mahmoud Soltani and Samadi79 also demonstrated a significant correlation between phosphorus content and various soil properties, such as acidity, clay percentage, organic matter, and calcium carbonate.

The effects soil type and different sources of phosphorus on the secondary metabolites of tomato

The research conducted by Aziz and Youssef80 highlighted that increased phosphorous application enhances the production of monoterpene hydrocarbons while simultaneously reducing the levels of sesquiterpene hydrocarbons. Specifically, the highest concentrations of α-pinene, limonene, and terpinolene were recorded with elevated phosphorus levels. Complementing these findings, Bleeker et al.81 observed significant increases in the release of β-phellandrene and terpinolene from both wild-type (Castlemart) and cultivated (Moneymaker) tomato plants, suggesting that these compounds might bolster plant defenses through their direct toxicity or repellent properties. In our study, the application of CaP2 and PN2 to clayey soil resulted in terpinolene compositions of 20.23% and 72.62%, respectively. The observed increases in terpinolene concentrations in clayey soil compared with sandy soil may be attributed to several factors. Clayey soils typically possess higher nutrient and moisture retention capacities, which enhance overall plant health and metabolic activity. This improved environment may promote the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites like terpenes. Furthermore, the increased surface area of clay particles may facilitate better nutrient absorption and retention, resulting in more effective plant growth and secondary metabolite production.

Conclusion

Fine textured (sandy) soils were more suitable for enhancing root length, easy realizing water up taking thus more water content in fruit, more proline and phenol content, and more antioxidant activity, whereas clayey soils favored traits related to fruit growth, yield, and nutritional elements. Among the phosphorous treatments, 0.23 g calcium phosphate for each 5 L pot application in clayey soil was the most effective treatment for increasing overall fruit yield. Although nano-hydroxyapatite (0.23 g for each 5 L pot) and calcium phosphate (0.23 g for each 5 L pot) in clayey soil increased terpinolene, which has antioxidant properties and enhances tomato biotic and abiotic tolerance, leading to increased growth and yield. Calcium phosphate (0.23 g for each 5 L pot) as a source of phosphorus had a more effective role in improving tomato growth traits affected the plant was effective in clay soil. In conclusion, in clay soil, we should use calcium phosphate as a phosphorus to improve the production quality and quantity. Soil quality and health is a vital and critical issue and concerns for human food security, so any new additives including nano materials of any kind entering to this medium should seriously be under consideration. Also, comparative studies between conventional and nano-phosphorus, is necessary for providing a more robust foundation for future research recommendations. However, the suitability of nano-based phosphorous sources for improving tomato performance across diverse soil and climate types and conditions requires specific further evaluations.
