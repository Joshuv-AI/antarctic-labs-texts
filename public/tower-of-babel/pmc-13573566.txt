Synergistic and Fungicide-Reducing Effects of Biological Agents with Chemical Fungicides against Ginseng Rusty Root Rot
Authors: Xue-Qing Li, Chen-Xi Xing, Ran Dong, Hao Tang, Ling-Xin Kong, Xiao-Lin Chen, Bao-Hui Lu, Zhong-Wei Zou, Minghai Song, Jie Gao, Yun Jiang, Chang-Qing Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ginseng rusty root rot caused by Ilyonectria robusta threatens ginseng production. This study screened synergistic combinations of biocontrol agents and chemical fungicides. Combined treatments inhibited mycelial growth, yield, and conidial production and germination. Among nine combinations, five achieved over 50% control efficacy; NJF 5:1 reached 81.48% field efficacy and reduced I. robusta in roots and soil. Biocontrol strain colonization increased versus singlestrain treatments. Ginseng defense enzyme genes and soil enzyme activities were enhanced. Three-stage soil microbiomics showed improved diversity, increased beneficial Granulicella and Pseudogymnoascus, and decreased Ilyonectria pathogen, which positively correlated with disease index, while beneficial genera correlated with soil enzyme activity. Seven lipopeptide biosynthesis genes were upregulated by the combination versus single strain. Metabolomics and transcriptomics confirmed that fludioxonil promoted the accumulation of antifungal p-hydroxybenzaldehyde and 4-methylphenol in NJ13. This study presents a novel synergistic interaction model reducing chemical usage and clarifies multiple synergistic effects against ginseng rusty root rot.

Introduction

Panax ginseng is a perennial herb that is rich in nutrients, has significant economic benefits, and is primarily cultivated artificially in the Changbai Mountain area in Northeast China. This UNESCO Biosphere Reserve constitutes a vital ecotone in Northeast Asia, harboring over 2600 plant species and serving as both a global biodiversity hotspot and critical watershed regulator. In recent years, the Chinese government has consistently rolled out policies to vigorously develop green agriculture, strengthen the reductions in pesticide application, improve efficiency technology, and facilitate the transition from traditional plant protection practices to green agricultural practices. Therefore, it is imperative to investigate and develop green production technologies for ginseng cultivation with the aim of reducing reliance on chemical inputs and achieving harmonious balance between sustainable agricultural production and ecological conservation.

Ginseng rusty root rot, also known as ginseng cancer, is a soil-borne disease that poses great threats to the yield and medicinal qualities of ginseng. Currently, the incidence of this disease in China typically ranges from 20% to 30%, with some seriously infected fields exceeding 70%. − Chemical control remains the primary strategy for managing ginseng rusty root rot owing to its operational immediacy and ability to rapidly suppress pathogens. However, due to the perennial growth of ginseng, the latent nature of soil-borne diseases, the short duration of chemical treatment, and the lack of effective chemical fungicides, current chemical fungicide control methods are largely ineffective. Furthermore, long-term heavy use of chemical fungicides can also lead to fungicide resistance and the presence of chemical residues in ginseng and soil and even cause severe damage to the ecological environment of Changbai Mountain. Moreover, biological control has emerged as an ecologically sustainable alternative that is environmentally friendly, maintaining the potential for plant resistance induction and persistent biocontrol. , However, several restrictions remain for the practical implementation of biological agents, including susceptibility to environmental fluctuations, inefficient host colonization, and delayed disease suppression, which collectively hinder their popularization and application. ,

The management of ginseng rusty root rot highlights the notable contrast between chemical and biological control strategies, each of which has its own advantages and limitations. This dichotomy emphasizes the need to scientifically develop integrated approaches that leverage the complementary strengths of each strategy, which is an area of growing interest in modern phytopathology. Recent studies have shown that combining biological and chemical interventions can significantly improve efficacy across various plant disease systems. Zhang et al. reported that combining Trichoderma asperellum with hymexazol was more effective in controlling cowpea wilt than its individual use was. Similarly, Wang et al. reported significant synergistic interactions between Bacillus subtilis and ningnanmycin in mitigating tomato bacterial wilt. Furthermore, the inhibitory effect of strobilurins in combination with B. subtilis H158 was greater than that of strobilurins alone in controlling rice sheath blight. Ayer et al. reported that the combination of B. subtilis and benzofluoropyridine allowed a reduction in chemical use for the treatment of apple black spot disease. In addition, Kim et al. suggested that the combination of B. amyloliquefaciens and chemical fungicides synergistically suppressed wheat blast disease. Our prior investigations validated this phenomenon, as mixing chemicals with biological agents improved the management of ginseng root rot caused by Fusarium solani and ginseng black spot caused by Alternaria panax. , Collectively, these studies show that the combined application of biological agents and chemical fungicides can effectively control disease and reduce chemical reliance, exhibiting considerable potential for application in sustainable agricultural practices. However, the mechanism underlying the synergistic effects of combination strategies remains unclear. This study aimed to assess the effectiveness of compound agents in controlling ginseng rusty root rot and to elucidate the underlying synergistic mechanisms involved. The synergistic interactions between biological agents and chemical fungicides were investigated through comparative multiomics analysis, focusing on (1) the colonization capacity of biocontrol bacteria, (2) the induction of resistance in ginseng, (3) enzyme activity and microecological dynamics in ginseng rhizosphere soil, and (4) the identification, functional validation, and influence of chemical agents on the active differentially abundant metabolites produced by the biocontrol bacteria. This study is expected to provide a scientific basis as a new approach for improving ginseng rusty root rot control and reducing the use of chemical fungicides.

Materials and Methods

Strains, Culture and Agents

The pathogen Ilyonectria robusta (strain CBLJ-3) was cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA) at 25 °C and stored in the Green Prevention and Control Laboratory of Ginseng Disease at Jilin Agricultural University. The ginseng used in this study was 3-year-old Damaya. The biological agents NJ13 (B. velezensis), FG14 (B. amyloliquefaciens) and NT35 (B. velezensis) were independently developed and stored in the laboratory. In our previous study, the biological activities of six biological agents in combination with nine chemical fungicides against I. robusta were determined, and a total of 82 compound combinations were found to have synergistic effects. The nine compounds with clear synergistic effects indoors were chosen for this experiment, and three biological agents and three chemical fungicides were used as single treatment controls (Table S1 and Table S2).

The media used in this study included PDA media (200 g of potato, 20 g of dextrose, 15 g of agar, and 1000 mL of distilled water), NA media (5 g of beef paste, 10 g of peptone, 5 g of NaCl, 10 g of sucrose, 1 g of yeast extract, 15 g of agar, and 1000 mL of distilled water, pH 7.2 to 7.4), LB media (10 g of tryptone, 5 g of yeast extract, 10 g of NaCl, 15 g of agar, 20 g of sucrose, and 1000 mL of distilled water), PD broth (200 g of potato, 20 g of glucose, and 1000 mL of distilled water), and WA media (15 g of agar and 1000 mL of distilled water).

Compatibility of Three Chemical Fungicides with Three Strains

Two plates of each strain (NJ13, FG14 and NT35) were inoculated into LB liquid media at 28 °C and incubated for 12 h at 160 rpm to obtain the seed culture (1 × 107 cfu/mL). Then, the 4% seed culture was inoculated into fermentation broth for incubation at 28 °C and 160 rpm for 72 h. Additionally, LB media plates containing carbendazim, difenoconazole or fludioxonil at concentrations of 0, 0.1, 10, 20, and 40 mg/L were prepared. Then, a 50 μL sample of the fermentation broth was serially diluted in sterile water, and the diluted samples were spread on the LB plates containing chemical fungicides for incubation at 48 h at 28 °C for colony enumeration. Each treatment consisted of three replicates.

Effects of the Compounds on the Growth of the Pathogen CBLJ-3

Inhibition of I. robusta Mycelia Growth

The inhibitory effects of the compounds were assessed by investigating I. robusta mycelial growth. The biological agents and chemical fungicides were mixed in specific proportions to prepare the compounds, which were then incorporated into PDA media to create fungicide-containing plates (Table S2). A control plate was prepared by adding an equivalent volume of sterile water to PDA media. Discs of I. robusta CBLJ-3 (8 mm in diameter) were inoculated at the center of each plate for incubation at 25 °C. Each treatment consisted of three replicates. The inhibition rate (%) was calculated as follows: 100 × (control diameter – treatment diameter)/(control diameter – 8).

Mycelia from the edge of I. robusta CBLJ-3 colonies grown on fungicide-containing plates were collected for scanning electron microscopy (SU5000, HITACHI, Japan) observation. The plates treated with sterile water served as controls. Additionally, the inhibitory effects of the compounds on the CBLJ-3 mycelial yield were evaluated using liquid culture, as described by Wang et al. Each treatment consisted of three replicates.

Inhibition of I. robusta Conidia Production and Germination

A hemocytometer was used to determine the inhibitory effects of the compounds on I. robusta conidia. A CBLJ-3 disc (8 mm) was placed in the center of fungicide-containing plates, with a plate containing sterile water (equal to the amount of fungicide) was used as a control. Each treatment consisted of three replicates. The plates were incubated at 25 °C for 7 d, after which the conidia were flushed with 5 mL of sterile water. The conidia yield was evaluated using a hemocytometer, and the inhibition rate (%) was calculated as follows: 100 × (control conidia production - treatment conidia production)/control conidia production. The rate of inhibition of CBLJ-3 conidial germination was calculated using the method described by Jiao et al.

Inhibition of I. robusta Growth on Ginseng Roots In Vitro

An in vitro experiment was performed with ginseng roots according to Li et al. Four wounds, each 3 mm deep, were generated on ginseng roots using a sterile pipet tip with a diameter of 2 mm, after which the ginseng wounds were wiped with cotton wool that had been dipped in 5 mL of compound solution. The experiment consisted of three treatments: preventive treatment (24 h of compound treatment followed by inoculation with I. robusta), therapeutic treatment (compound treatment 24 h after inoculation with I. robusta) and control treatment (application of an equal volume of sterile water). Each treatment consisted of three replicates.

Control Efficacy of Ginseng Rusty Root Rot in the Field and qPCR Detection of I. robusta

Field trials were performed where ginseng rusty root rot occurred frequently in 2023 at the Baixi base of Songjianghe town, Fusong County, Jilin Province, China. The ginseng cultivar used was Damaya (3 years of age). A total of 17 treatments were conducted, with each treatment consisting of three plots. Each plot contained 60 plants and covered an area of 2 m2. Different compounds, biological agents and chemical fungicides (listed in Table S3) were mixed with the soil at a concentration of 10 g/m2 before ginseng planting. Samples were taken at maturity, cleaned, photographed, and examined for root traits (length, weight, and diameter), and disease severity was assessed according to the disease classification standard. Then, the disease indices and control effects were calculated. The DNA of ginseng samples was extracted using the NuClean Plant Genomic DNA Kit (CWBIO, Jiangsu, China), and the DNA of soil samples was extracted using the DNeasyPowersoil Pro Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). The I. robusta detection system constructed in the laboratory was used for qPCR detection to measure the content of I. robusta in the roots and soil.

Colonization Ability of the Strains in Ginseng and Rhizosphere Soil

The double-resistant strains NT35-Rif160-Stre400, FG14-Rif120-Stre250 and NJ13-Rif320-Stre400 were cultured in the laboratory according to Li et al. Nine compound treatments, NJC 5:1, NJC 3:1, FGC 3:1, NTC 1:3, NJD 3:1, NJD 5:1, NJD 1:5, NJD 1:1, and NJF 5:1, were used in the test, and 3 single-strain treatments (NJ13, FG14, and NT35) were used as controls. The fermentation broth of the double-resistant strains (1 × 108 cfu/mL) was mixed with the appropriate proportion of chemical fungicides. Each treatment group consisted of 30 plants. Root irrigation was carried out, with 100 mL of fermentation broth added to each plant during the leaf spreading stage (June 15th). At 7, 14, 35, 49, and 63 days post innoculation (dpi), the ginseng roots, stems, leaves and soil were collected to recover the colonized microorganisms, as described by Santhanam et al. Each treatment consisted of three replicates.

Defense Enzyme-Encoding Gene Expression in Ginseng

In this experiment, six treatments were applied, NJC 5:1, NJF 5:1, carbendazim, fludioxonil, NJ13 and a water control (CK). The biocontrol bacteria fermentation broth (1 × 108 cfu/mL) and chemical fungicides were diluted with an appropriate amount of clean water, and 100 mL of solution was applied to the ginseng plants via root irrigation after leaf spreading (June 20). Each treatment group consisted of 30 plants. Samples were collected at 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, and 11 d after root irrigation, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus, reverse transcribed into cDNA using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Takara Biotechnology, Dalian, China), and subjected to qRT–PCR with an LC96 fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The changes in the relative expression levels of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), β-1,3 glucanase (β-1,3-GLU), Chitinase (CHI), superoxide dismutase (SOD) and peroxidase (POD) genes of P. ginseng were calculated by the 2–ΔΔCt method, β-actin was selected as the internal reference gene. Each gene was assessed using three biological and three technical replicates.

The specific primer sequences used for qRT–PCR are listed in Table S4. Each qRT–PCR (20 μL) included 1 μL of cDNA, 1 μL of upstream and downstream primers (10 μmol/L), 10 μL of 2× Fast SYBR Mix, and 7 μL of ddH2O. qRT–PCR was performed with predenaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, and annealing and extension at 60 °C for 30 s, for a total of 40 cycles.

Enzyme Activity in Ginseng Rhizosphere Soil

The soil samples used in the experiment were collected from field plots in Section . Ginseng rhizosphere soil was sampled during three growth stages: leaf spreading (June 15), flowering (July 15), and root expansion (August 15) for soil enzyme activity determination (urease, catalase, phosphatase, invertase). The experiment consisted of 12 treatments, each consisting of 30 g of soil, which was stored at 4 °C. The activities of urease, catalase, phosphatase, and invertase in the soil were determined according to methods reported in the literature, and the experiment was repeated three times.

Ginseng Rhizosphere Microbial Community

High-throughput sequencing was used to analyze the microbial community in ginseng rhizosphere soil, using the samples acquired from the field plots described in Section . The ginseng rhizosphere soils treated with different compounds were sampled at the leaf spreading, flowering and root expansion stages. Ginseng rhizosphere soil treated with single agents or water were used as controls. Each treatment consisted of four replicates. DNA extraction and sequencing of the soil samples were performed by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

The V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primers 341F (5′-CCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACCAGGGTATCTAAT-3′), , whereas the fungal ITS2 region was amplified using the primers ITS2F (5′-GCATCGATGAAGAACGCAGC-3′) and ITS2R (5′-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3′). Library preparation and sequencing were conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Statistical analyses, including α diversity and β diversity analyses, were performed to characterize the structure and composition of the microbial communities. Spearman correlation analysis was employed to evaluate the relationships among the disease index, soil enzymes and microbial community.

Lipopeptide Biosynthesis Gene Expression in the NJ13 and FG14 Strains

The effects of the compounds on the expression of genes related to lipopeptide biosynthesis in the NJ13 and FG14 strains were determined according to the methods of Wang et al. A total of 30 mL of fermentation broth from the NJ13 and FG14 strains was added to 20 mL of I. robusta supernatant and 2 mL of a mixture of chemical fungicide and strain seed culture, and the resulting mixture was cultured at 28 °C and 160 rpm. As a control, 30 mL of fermentation broth was added to 20 mL of I. robusta supernatant and 2 mL of strain seed culture. Samples were taken after 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 h of incubation.

The total RNA of the biocontrol bacterial strains was extracted using an RNApure Bacteria Kit (Kangwei Century Biotechnology, Jiangsu, China) and reverse transcribed using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Takara Biotechnology, Dalian, China). Real-time PCR detection and data analysis were performed as described above. The sequences of primers used are listed in Table S5.

Metabolomic and Transcriptomic Analyses of the Effect of Fludioxonil on the NJ13 Strain

Metabolomic Analysis

The fermentation broth and seed culture of the NJ13 strain were first prepared. Two treatments (Treatment 1:50 mL of fermentation broth +2 mL of NJ13 seed culture +0.4 g of fludioxonil; and Treatment 2:50 mL of fermentation broth +2 mL of NJ13 seed culture) were prepared for 48 h of incubation at 28 °C and 160 rpm, after which the bacterial suspension was collected, quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Each treatment included four replicates. Untargeted metabolomic analysis was conducted by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

One milliliter of each sample was freeze-dried and resuspended in prechilled 80% methanol by vortexing. Then, the samples were incubated on ice for 5 min and centrifuged at 15000 rpm and 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was diluted to a final concentration of 53% methanol with liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS)-grade water. The samples were subsequently transferred to a new Eppendorf tube and centrifuged at 15000 rpm and 4 °C for 15 min. Finally, the supernatant was analyzed by LC–MS/MS. The detected metabolites were annotated using the KEGG database, HMDB and LIPID MAPS database.

Transcriptomic Analysis

Two treatments (Treatment 1:50 mL of fermentation broth +2 mL of NJ13 seed culture +0.4 g of fludioxonil; and Treatment 2:50 mL of fermentation broth +2 mL of NJ13 seed mixture) were prepared for transcriptomic analysis, as described in Section . Sequencing, data quality control, assembly, gene annotation, and expression level statistical analysis were carried out by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Briefly, RNA integrity was assessed using a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). After library quality control, different libraries were pooled on the basis of the effective concentrations and target amount of data and subjected to sequencing on the Illumina X Plus platform. Analysis of the differentially expressed genes between the two groups was performed using the DESeq Two package in R (1.20.0). A corrected P value of <0.05 was set as the threshold for significant differential expression. GO enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes was performed with the GOseq package in R with gene length bias correction. KOBAS software was used to evaluate the statistical enrichment of the differentially expressed genes in KEGG pathways.

Integrated Analysis

A combined metabolomic and transcriptomic analysis was performed on the NJ13 strain treated with fludioxonil. All identified differentially expressed genes and differentially abundant metabolites were mapped to the KEGG pathway database to obtain shared pathway information. This approach aimed to identify the main biochemical and signal transduction pathways associated with the differentially abundant metabolites and differentially expressed genes.

Inhibitory Effects of the Metabolites on I. robusta

To determine the inhibitory effect of metabolite standards on I. robusta, the experiment was performed as described in Section . The concentrations of the metabolite standard solutions were 10 mg/L. The control sample contained an equal amount of acetone, and each treatment consisted of three replicates. The diameter of the colonies was measured, and the inhibition rate was calculated.

The metabolite standards that exhibited inhibitory effects were further tested at 1, 5, 10, 50, and 100 mg/L. The mycelial growth rate was used to obtain the inhibition rate, and three replicates were carried out for each concentration. Regression analysis of the mycelial growth inhibition rate as a function of the logarithm of the concentration of the agent was carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0. The toxicity regression equation in the form of y = bx + a, EC50 values and correlation coefficients were determined.

The inhibitory effects of two metabolite standards on I. robusta in ginseng roots was performed as described in Section at concentrations of 10 and 50 mg/L. The experiment consisted of preventive, therapeutic and control treatments, and each treatment consisted of three replicates.

Statistical Analysis

The normal distribution of the residuals and uniformity of the variances were confirmed prior to statistical analysis. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to assess the significance of the differences between means, and Tukey’s multiple range test was subsequently used for posthoc multiple comparisons. The average values of the treatments were compared at the 5% probability level. The data presented in all the plots are the means ± SDs from three replicates. Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 25.0.

Results

Compatibility of Three Chemical Fungicides with Strains NJ13, FG14, and NT35

The NJ13, FG14 and NT35 strains all grew in LB media supplemented with 0, 0.1, 10, 20, or 40 mg/L chemical fungicide. The viable counts of the NJ13 and NT35 strains did not differ at chemical fungicide concentrations ranging from 0–20 mg/L, and there was no significant change in the viable counts of the FG14 strain at fungicide concentrations no greater than 10 mg/L (Figure S1). These findings indicated that the biocontrol bacteria are compatible with these chemical fungicides in a certain concentration range.

Effects of the Compounds on the Growth of the Pathogenic Strain CBLJ-3

Inhibition of I. robusta Mycelial Growth

I. robusta mycelia on the plate treated with sterile water exhibited a smooth and elongated structure, which regularly extended outward for growth (Figure A and C). In contrast, the mycelia on the plates containing compounds became thicker and exhibited an obviously abnormal morphology, including twisting (Figure E), deformations (Figure F), breakage (Figure G and H) and apical expansion (Figure B and D); thus, mycelial growth was effectively inhibited. Moreover, all nine compounds exhibited greater than 73% inhibition of CBLJ-3 mycelia, with the greatest effect produced by NJC 3:1 treatment (87.39%), followed by NJF 5:1 and FGC 3:1, with inhibition rates of 85.87% and 77.21%, respectively. The compounds inhibited mycelial yield by 42.82% to 61.07%. Among them, NJF 5:1 treatment resulted in the greatest inhibition of mycelial dry weight (61.07%). Additionally, the NJD 5:1 (60.83%) and NJD 1:1 (57.19%) treatments demonstrated significant inhibitory activities compared with that of the control (Table S6).

SEM Images of I. robusta CBLJ-3 mycelia treated with sterile water (A, C), NJF 5:1 (B, D), NJC 3:1 (E, G), and FGC 3:1 (F, H). The bars represent the actual sizes.

Inhibition of I. robusta Conidia Production and Germination

Conidia production by CBLJ-3 treated with the compounds was significantly lower than that after treatment with sterile water (control). The inhibition rates of the nine compounds ranged from 53.52% to 69.94%, with NJC 5:1 exhibiting the highest value of 69.94%, followed by NJF 5:1 and NJC 3:1, with inhibition rates of 67.27% and 62.08%, respectively. The compounds inhibited conidia germination by 50.62% to 64.12%. NJF 5:1 treatment resulted in the highest inhibition rate (64.12%), followed by the NJC 5:1 (60.92%) and NJC 3:1 (58.63%) treatments (Table S7). These results indicated that the compound treatments significantly inhibited I. robusta conidial growth.

Control of Ginseng Rusty Root Rot In Vitro

After inoculation with I. robusta CBLJ-3, rust-colored spots appeared on the ginseng root epidermis (Figure S2). I. robusta infection gradually spread to the inside of the roots, and the spots gradually expanded over time. Compared with that of the sterile water control, the depth (lesion length) of CBLJ-3 infection inside the ginseng roots decreased to varying degrees upon compound treatment, ranging from 5.60 to 8.57 mm at 11 d posttreatment (Table S8). In particular, the preventive and therapeutic effects of the nine compound treatments were greater than those of the individual biological agent and fungicide treatments. The FGC 3:1 treatment resulted in the greatest inhibition with the smallest infestation depth (5.60 mm), which was significantly less than that after treatment with carbendazim (7.43 mm) and FG14 (7.87 mm). NJF 5:1 treatment (5.64 mm) also resulted in a significantly smaller infestation depth than did the fludioxonil (6.85 mm) and NJ13 (6.24 mm) treatments, suggesting an obvious synergistic effect.

Control of Ginseng Rusty Root Rot and qPCR Detection of I. robusta in the Field

The control effect of each agent on ginseng rusty root rot ranged from 29.63% to 81.48%, with a mean of 47.92% (Figure and Figure ). Among them, the NJC 5:1, NJC 3:1 and NJF 5:1 combinations reduced the amounts of carbendazim and fludioxonil needed by 75% and 83%, respectively, while the control effects were significantly better than those of treatment with carbendazim, fludioxonil and NJ13 alone. NJF 5:1 treatment produced the greatest control effect (81.48%), which was much greater than those of fludioxonil (40.74%) and NJ13 (33.33%). Both the NJC 5:1 and NJC 3:1 treatments resulted in a control effect of 59.26%, which was significantly greater than that of the carbendazim (51.85%) and NJ13 (33.33%) treatments.

In addition, the compounds had promoted ginseng growth, and the root growth indices (length, diameter and weight) clearly improved after compound addition. Ginseng root yield increased upon compound treatment in the range of 23.92%–142.46%, with NJF 5:1 application resulting in the highest yield.

The contents of I. robusta in the field ginseng root and rhizosphere soil samples were detected, and the standard curve of I. robusta content determined via qPCR was Y = −4.0363X + 24.546; R 2 = 0.99. The water treatment group had the highest content of I. robusta and the content in the soil (1597.29 fg/μL) was greater than that in the ginseng roots (608.87 fg/μL), followed by the commercial biocontrol agent WX, with values of 652.38 fg/μL and 145.53 fg/μL, respectively. However, the contents of I. robusta in the soil and ginseng roots treated with the compounds decreased significantly. The content in the soil ranged from 17.64 fg/μL to 275.51 fg/μL, with the I. robusta DNA concentration in the NJF 5:1 group being the lowest (17.64 fg/μL), which was 94.96% lower than that in the fludioxonil group and 96.27% lower than that in the NJ13 group. The I. robusta content in the soil in the NJC 3:1 treatment group (69.51 fg/μL) was 68.65% and 85.31% lower than that in the carbendazim and NJ13 treatment groups, respectively, whereas that in the NJD 3:1 treatment group (96.53 fg/μL) was 62.68% and 79.63% lower than that in the difenoconazole and NJ13 treatment groups, respectively. Furthermore, the I. robusta content in NJC 5:1 treatment group (153.95 fg/μL) was 30.56% and 67.43% lower than that in the carbendazim and NJ13 groups, respectively, and that in the FGC 3:1 group (198.48 fg/μL) was 10.71% and 25.93% lower than that in the carbendazim and FG14 groups, respectively (Figure S3).

Moreover, the I. robusta content in ginseng roots ranged from 14.45 fg/μL to 194.82 fg/μL. Among the treatments, NJF 5:1 presented the lowest concentration of 14.45 fg/μL. This level was substantially lower than that in both the fludioxonil (88.99% reduction) and NJ13 (90.88% reduction) treatment groups. NJD 3:1 treatment resulted in a concentration of 35.95 fg/μL, which was higher than that after difenoconazole treatment (27.19 fg/μL) but 77.27% lower than that after NJ13 treatment. Similarly, NJC 5:1 administration (36.18 fg/μL) led to a 63.37% and 77.17% lower content than carbendazim and NJ13 administration did, respectively. NJC 3:1 (45.51 fg/μL) resulted in a 53.92% reduction compared with carbendazim and a 71.26% reduction compared with NJ13. Finally, the concentration after FGC 3:1 treatment was 79.19 fg/μL, which was 23.66% and 61.24% lower than that after carbendazim and FG14 treatment, respectively (Figure S3). These results suggested that the compounds produced synergistic I. robusta inhibitory effects compared with the single agent treatments.

Spatiotemporal Changes in the Colonization of Biocontrol Strains in Different Ginseng Tissues and Rhizosphere Soils

The three biocontrol strains NJ13, NT35 and FG14 successfully colonized both in vivo and in the ginseng rhizosphere soil within 63 d, with the highest colonization occurring in the soil, followed by the roots, stems, and leaves (Figure ). Colonization in all of the ginseng tissues and soils was significantly greater after treatment with the nine compounds than that after treatment with the single agents. Colonization in each treatment group peaked at 7 d in the soil, ranging from 2 × 104 cfu/g to 7.23 × 104 cfu/g, with the greatest colonization in the NJD 5:1 group (7.23 × 104 cfu/g), followed by the NJD 1:1 group (6.43 × 104 cfu/g), which were 262% and 222% greater than that in the NJ13 group (2 × 104 cfu/g), respectively (Figure A).

Root colonization peaked at 35 d, with the number of lateral roots ranging from 1 × 103 cfu/g to 8.7 × 103 cfu/g. Colonization was highest after NJC 3:1 treatment (8.7 × 103 cfu/g), followed by NJD 3:1 treatment (8.13 × 103 cfu/g), which were 118% and 103% greater than that after NJ13 treatment (4 × 103 cfu/g), respectively (Figure B). Fibrous root colonization ranged from 3.75 × 103 cfu/g to 8.33 × 103 cfu/g, and the greatest degree of colonization was observed in the NJC 5:1 treatment group (8.33 × 103 cfu/g), followed by the NJF 5:1 treatment group (7.07 × 103 cfu/g), which were 67% and 41% greater than that in the NJ13 treatment group (5 × 103 cfu/g), respectively (Figure C). Colonization in the root bark ranged from 8.5 × 102 cfu/g to 8.77 × 103 cfu/g, and NJC 3:1 treatment resulted in the greatest degree of colonization (8.77 × 103 cfu/g) while colonization in the NJC 5:1 group was 8.73 × 103 cfu/g, which were 109% and 108% greater than that in the NJ13 group (4.2 × 103 cfu/g), respectively (Figure D). Colonization in the root cores ranged from 2 × 103 cfu/g to 8.43 × 103 cfu/g. NJC 3:1 treatment resulted in the greatest colonization (8.43 × 103 cfu/g), with colonization in the NJC 5:1 group 8.27 × 103 cfu/g, which were 322% and 314% greater than that in the NJ13 group (2 × 103 cfu/g), respectively (Figure E).

Stem colonization peaked at 49 d, ranging from 3.5 × 102 cfu/g to 6.13 × 103 cfu/g. NJD 5:1 treatment resulted in the greatest colonization (6.13 × 103 cfu/g), followed by NJC 3:1 treatment (4.87 × 103 cfu/g), which were 513% and 387% greater than that after NJ13 treatment (1 × 103 cfu/g), respectively (Figure F). Leaf colonization peaked at 63 d, ranging from 2 × 102 cfu/g to 4.27 × 103 cfu/g, with the greatest colonization in the NJF 5:1 treatment group (4.27 × 103 cfu/g), followed by the NJD 3:1 treatment group (3.37 × 103 cfu/g), which were 2035% and 1585% greater than that in the NJ13 treatment group (2 × 102 cfu/g), respectively (Figure G).

Overall, compound treatment increased colonization with the single agent treatments, indicating that the colonization ability of the biocontrol bacteria was stronger in the presence of chemical fungicides. Thus, combining biological agents with chemical fungicides was conducive to the colonization of biocontrol bacteria in ginseng and soil.

Expression of Defense Enzyme-Encoding Genes in Ginseng

The relative expression of five defense enzyme-encoding genes in ginseng roots increased after compound treatment, among which the expression of the β-1,3-GA gene peaked at 5 d and ranged from 1.05 to 7.65. NJF 5:1 treatment (7.65) increased β-1,3-GA gene expression by 49% compared to fludioxonil treatment (5.14) and 243% compared to NJ13 (2.23) treatment, while NJC 5:1 treatment (6.10) increased β-1,3-GA gene expression by 8% compared with carbendazim treatment (5.64) and 174% compared with NJ13 treatment (2.23). Similarly, PAL gene expression peaked at 5 d, ranging from 1.05 to 10.15. NJF 5:1 treatment (10.15) increased PAL gene expression by 83% compared with fludioxonil treatment (5.55) and 264% compared with NJ13 treatment (2.79), whereas NJC 5:1 treatment (7.49) increased PAL gene expression by 17% compared with carbendazim treatment (6.41) and 168% compared with NJ13 treatment (2.79). SOD gene expression peaked at 7 d, ranging from 1.05 to 13.86. NJF 5:1 treatment (13.86) increased SOD gene expression by 148% compared with fludioxonil treatment (5.59) and 310% compared with NJ13 treatment (3.38). Furthermore, NJC 5:1 treatment (11.17) increased SOD gene expression by 85% compared with carbendazim treatment (6.05) and 168% compared with NJ13 treatment (3.30). CAT gene expression peaked at 7 d, ranging from 1.05 to 10.93. NJF 5:1 treatment (10.93) increased CAT gene expression by 111% compared with fludioxonil treatment (5.19) and 281% compared with NJ13 treatment (2.87). Additionally, NJC 5:1 treatment (8.48) increased CAT gene expression by 45% compared with carbendazim treatment (5.85) and 195% compared with NJ13 treatment (2.87). CHI gene expression also peaked at 7 d, ranging from 1.05 to 11.53. NJF 5:1 treatment (11.53) increased expression CHI gene expression by 118% compared with fludioxonil treatment (5.30) and 181% compared with NJ13 treatment (4.11). Furthermore, NJC 5:1 treatment (9.87) increased CHI gene expression by 83% compared with carbendazim treatment (5.40) and 140% compared with NJ13 treatment (4.11) (Figure S4). The relative expression levels of these defense enzyme-encoding genes in the NJC 5:1 and NJF 5:1 treatment groups were significantly greater than those in the carbendazim, fludioxonil and NJ13 treatment groups, exhibiting clear synergistic effects.

Enzyme Activity in Ginseng Rhizosphere Soil

The activities of phosphatase and urease generally tended to increase but then decreased, while those of invertase and catalase tended to increase continuously (Figure S5). Soil enzyme activity in response to the five compounds was clearly greater than that in response to the individual agents during the three growth periods. Phosphatase activity peaked at the flowering stage and ranged from 161 μg/g to 258 μg/g. Phosphatase activity in the NJC 5:1 group (258 μg/g) was the highest, which was 26% greater than that in the carbendazim group (205 μg/g) and 24% greater than that in the NJ13 group (208 μg/g). NJF 5:1 treatment (240 μg/g) increased phosphatase activity by 21% compared with fludioxonil treatment (199 μg/g) and 15% compared with NJ13 treatment (208 μg/g). Urease activity was highest at the flowering stage and ranged from 330 μg/g to 580 μg/g, with the highest activity occurring in the NJF 5:1 group (580 μg/g), which was 57% greater than that in the fludioxonil group (370 μg/g) and 49% greater than that in the NJ13 group (390 μg/g). NJC 3:1 treatment (532 μg/g) increased urase activity by 40% compared with carbendazim treatment (380 μg/g) and 36% compared with NJ13 treatment (390 μg/g). Invertase activity peaked at 15 mg/g to 39.83 mg/g during the root expansion stage, with NJF 5:1 treatment (39.83 mg/g) resulting in the highest activity, which was 56% greater than that after fludioxonil treatment (25.54 mg/g) and 141% greater than that after NJ13 treatment (16.52 mg/g). NJC 3:1 treatment (37.67 mg/g) increased invertase activity by 38% compared with carbendazim treatment (27.21 mg/g) and 128% compared with NJ13 treatment (16.52 mg/g). Catalase activity was highest at the root expansion stage, ranging from 0.3 mL/g to 1.3 mL/g. NJF 5:1 treatment (1.3 mL/g) increased catalase activity by 225% compared with fludioxonil treatment (0.4 mL/g) and 63% compared with NJ13 treatment (0.8 mL/g). Moreover, NJC 5:1 treatment (1.1 mL/g) increased catalyze activity by 57% compared with carbendazim treatment (0.7 mL/g) and 38% compared with NJ13 treatment (0.8 mL/g).

Changes in the Ginseng Rhizosphere Microbial Community

Microbial Community Diversity

In general, the Chao1 index represents community richness, and the Shannon and Simpson indices represent community diversity and distribution uniformity. The Chao1 index of the bacterial community generally tended to increase but then decreased. The Simpson index decreased but then increased during the three growth periods (Figure S6 and Figure S7). The Chao1 and Simpson indices of the three compound groups were greater than those of the single agent and water control treatment groups at the leaf spreading stage. In contrast, the Chao1, Shannon and Simpson indices of the fungal community varied greatly during each growth period, with the Chao1 and Shannon indices showing a decreasing and then increasing trend and the Simpson index showing an overall increasing trend. The Chao1 index was lower than that in the single agent treatment groups at the leaf spreading stage and higher than that of the single agent treatment groups at the flowering stage. The Simpson index was greater than that of the single agent treatment groups at the leaf spreading and root expansion stages.

Composition of the Microbial Community

On the basis of the species annotation and abundance information from the individual samples at the genus level, the top 10 genera were chosen for cluster analysis to plot the relative abundances of microbial community species as bar charts (Figure ). The high-abundance bacterial genera during the three periods of ginseng growth were Candidatus_Udaeobacter, Rhodanobacter, Granulicella, Pedobacter, Flavobacterium, Escherichia-Shigella, Pseudomonas and HSB_OF53-F07. In particular, compared with the single agent treatments, the compounds increased the abundances of Candidatus_Udaeobacter, Granulicella, and Pseudomonas and decreased the abundances of Rhodanobacter and HSB_OF53-F07. Among them, Rhodanobacter is an acidophilic bacterium, and Granulicella is related to carbon degradation in plant litter. Flavobacterium and Pseudomonas promote inter-root growth, and Pedobacter promotes bioremediation, nitrogen transformation and recycling.

In the fungal community, the abundance of Pseudogymnoascus was greater in the compound treatment groups than in the single agent and the water control treatment groups at the leaf spreading stage, with the highest abundance in the FGC 3:1 group (55.40%), which was 12.92% and 9.87% more abundant than in the carbendazim and FG14 groups, respectively. The abundance of Ilyonectria was the highest (10.82%) in the water control treatment. In contrast, the abundances of Ilyonectria in response to the compound treatment groups (1.26%, 0.54%, 5.08%, and 2.21%) were significantly lower than those in the single agent and water control treatment groups at the leaf spreading stage. In addition, the abundance of Ilyonectria increased in all the treatment groups at the flowering stage compared with that at the leaf spreading stage, while the abundance of Pseudogymnoascus remained high. Furthermore, the abundance of Ilyonectria decreased at the root expansion stage, and its abundance was lower after compound treatment than after the single agent and water control treatments. Specifically, the FGC 3:1 group had the lowest Ilyonectria abundance (1.50%), which was lower than that in the carbendazim (3.82%) and FG14 (3.37%) single agent treatment groups. In addition, in the NJF 5:1 treatment group (3.41%), the abundance of Ilyonectria was significantly lower than that in the fludioxonil (7.40%) and NJ13 (8.89%) single agent treatment groups. Interestingly, the highest abundance of Pseudogymnoascus was in the FGC 3:1 group (40.08%), followed by the NJC 3:1 group (13.04%), both of which exceeded their respective single agent treatment levels (Figure ). Species of the genus Ilyonectria cause ginseng rusty root rot, whereas Pseudogymnoascus can produce cellulase and metabolites with Ilyonectria inhibitory activity. The decrease in the abundance of Ilyonectria and the increase in the abundance of Pseudogymnoascus in the inter-root soils treated with the compounds suggested that the use of the compounds could reduce the abundance of harmful pathogens in the soil while increasing the abundance of beneficial genera.

Correlation Analysis of the Microbial Community, Disease Index and Growth-Promoting Indicators

In terms of the bacterial community, the disease index was significantly positively correlated with the abundance of Rhodanobacter and positively correlated with the abundances of unidentified_Chitinophagaceae, HSB_OF53.F07, Flavobacterium, and Pedobacter (Figure S8A). In addition, there was a significant positive correlation between root weight and the abundances of Candidatus_Udaeobacter, Candidatus_Solibacter, Candidatus_Nitrosotalea and Pseudolabrys. Following compound treatment, the abundance of Candidatus_Udaeobacter increased, whereas the abundances of Rhodanobacter and HSB_OF53-F07 decreased. These findings suggest that these compounds can selectively modulate the bacterial community, reducing the abundance of genera positively correlated with the disease index and increasing the abundance of genera positively correlated with root weight. Consequently, this modulation likely contributes to reducing disease occurrence and promoting ginseng growth.

Among the top 10 fungal genera (Figure S8B), the abundance of Ilyonectria was positively correlated with the ginseng disease index and negatively correlated with ginseng root weight. In contrast, the abundance of Pseudogymnoascus was negatively correlated to the ginseng disease index and positively correlated with ginseng root weight. The use of these compounds resulted in a decrease in the abundance of Ilyonectria and an increase in the abundance of Pseudogymnoascus, indicating that compound treatment could reduce the abundance of pathogenic fungal genera and increase the abundance of beneficial fungal genera, which were positively correlated with ginseng root weight.

Correlation Analysis of the Microbial Community and Enzyme Activity

Spearman correlation analysis of the microbial communities associated with the activities of four soil enzymes, catalase, phosphatase, urease, and invertase, at the genus level (Figure S9A) revealed that, in the bacterial community, the abundance of Candidatus_Solibacter was positively correlated with urease and invertase activity. Moreover, the abundances of Granulicella, TM7a, and unidentified_Chloroplast were positively correlated with the activities of all four soil enzymes. Nevertheless, the HSB_OF53-F07 abundance was negatively correlated with the activities of the four soil enzymes. Among them, the abundance of Granulicella increased and the abundance of HSB_OF53-F07 decreased in compound-treated soil, which proved that the compounds could increase the abundance of beneficial bacterial genera related to soil enzyme activity in the soil, which promoted the increase in soil enzyme activity.

For the fungal community, the abundance of Ilyonectria was negatively associated with the activities of the four soil enzymes. Additionally, Pseudogymnoascus abundance was positively correlated the activities of invertase, urease and catalase, and the abundance of Fungi_gen_Incertae_sedis was positively correlated with catalase and phosphatase activity. The decrease in the abundance of Ilyonectria and the increase in the abundance of Pseudogymnoascus in the soil after treatment with the compounds were conducive to improving soil enzyme activity (Figure S9B).

Lipopeptide Biosynthesis Gene Expression in the NJ13 and FG14 Strains

Seven genes encoding enzymes involved in the biosynthesis of inhibitory lipopeptides were upregulated in the biocontrol strains after coculture with chemical fungicides. Their relative expression tended to increase and then decrease, with the itu D gene ranging from 1.18 to 12.11, bam B ranging from 1.22 to 11.14, fen D ranging from 1.12 to 9.26, fen A ranging from 1.12 to 9.96, srf AA ranging from 1.03 to 9.49, srf AB ranging from 1.15 to 10.41 and ynd J ranging from 1.06 to 7.26. From 12–60 h, compared with the single strain NJ13, NJF 5:1 treatment showed the most significant effect in promoting the synthesis of antifungal lipopeptides in biocontrol strains, with the related genes upregulated by 4% to 1112%, which was following closely by the NJD 3:1, NJC 3:1, and NJC 5:1 treatments. In these treatments, seven genes encoding enzymes involved in the biosynthesis of inhibitory lipopeptides were upregulated, ranging from 22% to 936% for NJD 3:1, 6% to 921% for NJC 3:1, and 28% to 870% for NJC 5:1. Additionally, compared with FG14 treatment, seven lipopeptide biosynthesis genes were upregulated by 25% to 853% after treatment with FGC 3:1. Interestingly, the expression of these genes almost peaked at 48 h after incubation, which indicated that the greatest production of these inhibitory substances may occur at this time (Figure ). In summary, these data proved that chemical fungicides could promote the expression of lipopeptide biosynthesis genes in biocontrol bacteria and increase the production of inhibitory substances, thus enhancing the ginseng rusty root rot control.

Metabolomic Analysis of the Effect of Fludioxonil on Strain NJ13

PCA and PLS-DA revealed obvious separation between the NJC 5:1 (NF) and NJ13 (N) treatments (Figure S10 and Figure S11). Fludioxonil contributed substantially to metabolism in the NJ13 strain, and the model was considered reliable and suitable for further analysis. The volcano plots allow visualization of the overall distribution of the differentially abundant metabolites (Figure S12A and B). Compared with those in the N treatment, 132 metabolites identified in positive ion mode increased and 36 metabolites decreased, while 46 metabolites identified in negative ion mode increased and 41 metabolites decreased.

Matchstick diagrams were constructed on the basis of the differentially abundant metabolites identified from each comparison group to more show indicate the increases and decreases in metabolite contents as well as substances with large fold changes in abundance. The metabolites identified in positive ion mode with significant increases included emamectin B1a, 7-(2-thienyl)-5-(trifluoromethyl) [1,2,4] triazolo [1,5-a] pyrimidine, 7α-hydroxytestosterone, l-methionine sulfone, ranitidine N-oxide, tylosin and others. The metabolites identified in negative ion mode with significant increases included 4-toluenesulfonic acid, 2-nitro-5-(phenylsulfonyl) phenol, P-toluenesulfonic acid, 6-(2-furyl)-2-hydroxy-4-(2-thienyl) nicotinonitrile, 3-phenyl-2-thioxo-1,2,3,4-tetrahydroquinazolin-4-one, AICA ribonucleotide and others (Figure S12C and D).

According to the results of KEGG enrichment, the metabolic pathways significantly enriched in the differentially abundant metabolites detected in positive ion mode included oxidative phosphorylation, purine metabolism, lysine degradation, aldosterone synthesis and secretion, thiamine metabolism, the FoxO signaling pathway, the AMPK signaling pathway, carbapenem biosynthesis, and C5-branched dibasic metabolism (Figure S12E). The metabolic pathways significantly enriched in the differentially abundant metabolites detected in negative ion mode included biosynthesis of antibiotics, vancomycin resistance, protein digestion and absorption, peptidoglycan biosynthesis, and lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis (Figure S12F).

Transcriptomic Analysis of the Effect of Fludioxonil on the NJ13 Strain

The results of the Pearson correlation analysis suggested that the two treatments were closely correlated and that the repeatability among replicate samples within groups was ideal (Figure S13A). The coexpression Venn diagram revealed 3673 genes common to the NJF and NJ13 treatments, and 206 and 18 genes were identified exclusively in the NJF and NJ13 treatments, respectively (Figure S13B).

In the NJF vs N comparison (Figure S14A), a total of 2275 differentially expressed genes were identified, including 1138 upregulated genes and 1137 downregulated genes. KEGG enrichment analysis (Figure S14B) revealed that these differentially expressed genes were enriched in the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites and microbial metabolism in diverse environments.

Combined Transcriptomic and Metabolomic Analysis of the NJ13 Strain Treated with Fludioxonil

Correlation analysis of the differentially expressed genes and metabolites in NJ13 cocultivated with fludioxonil was performed. Notably, the pathways related to metabolism exhibited the most significant enrichment, suggesting that fludioxonil had the most pronounced effect on metabolism in the NJ13 strain.

Discussion

Integrating biological agents with chemical fungicides establishes a synergistic plant protection paradigm. This approach leverages the complementary strengths of both strategies: it utilizes the rhizocompetence of biocontrol bacteria for sustained rhizosphere colonization, the induction of systemic resistance, and microbiome mediated soil conditioning, while exploiting the rapid and stable pathogen suppression characteristic of chemical fungicides. Consequently, their combination effectively improves control efficacy and reduces the reliance on chemical fungicides, representing a model of complementary and synergistic advantages. However, there are few related research reports and applications in this field. The chemical fungicides carbendazim and difenoconazole have been reported to be combined with the biofungicides T. harzianum and B. amyloliquefaciens to control rice seedling blight and tomato wilt, although fludioxonil combined with biological agents has rarely been reported. In this study, carbendazim, difenoconazole, and fludioxonil combined with three biofungicides had synergistic control effects on ginseng rusty root rot compared with the single agent treatments both in vitro and in the field, with the highest field efficacy of 81.48%. Moreover, the growth indices of ginseng were significantly improved by the use of these compounds, and the highest yield was 5.83 times greater than that of the individual agent treatments.

Chemical reduction has become an inevitable trend in sustainable development, and the exploration of efficient and environmentally friendly methods for controlling plant disease has become a hot topic. − The combination of tebuconazole and B. subtilis LS3 effectively managed quinoa black stem disease and reduced the amount of chemical agent used by 40%. Gu et al. found that the synergistic control of strawberry anthracnose by B. amyloliquefaciens and prochloraz was able to reduce the usage of prochloraz by 50%. Peng et al. reported that the combined use of B. subtilis B-001 and a chemical for the control of tomato green wilt reduced the chemical fungicide dosage by 30%. Compared with those in prior studies, the compounds screened in this study have more significant chemical fungicidal reduction and synergistic effects, which can reduce the dosages of carbendazim, difenoconazole and fludioxonil needed by to 75%–83% with control effects 1.00–2.44 times greater than that of single agent treatment, indicating favorable economic and ecological benefits. It is highly important to reduce chemical fungicide residues during ginseng production, which would guarantee the quality and safety of ginseng products and protect the ecological environment, especially in the Changbai Mountain area.

Moreover, after applying a variety of combinations of three chemical fungicides and three biological agents in this study, it was proven that the different ratios of different combinations of chemical fungicides and biological agents had obvious synergistic effects on ginseng rusty root rot, surpassing the performance of the individual agents. These findings indicated that their synergistic combination mode was not accidental but rather scientific and repeatable and has greater potential for popularization and application. The present study is the first to identify compounds with better control effects on ginseng rusty root rot, and the synergistic mechanism has been clarified from multiple perspectives, which provides a reference for research on the synergistic effects of biocontrol agents and chemicals.

Colonization ability is an important factor affecting the stability and durability of biocontrol agents in controlling soil-borne diseases. However, in practical applications, the common problems associated with biocontrol bacteria are their short duration of action and low degree of colonization. , Chen et al. reported that chemicals can help biocontrol bacteria overcome rejection by pathogens and other environmental microorganisms, promote their colonization in soil and plants, and thereby improve biocontrol. Niu et al. found that carbendazim can enhance the colonization and survival rate of Pseudomonas fluorescens in the soil and cotton roots. In this study, the degrees of colonization of the three biocontrol bacteria in nine compound combinations across six different ginseng tissues and rhizosphere soils were more than those in the single agent treatment groups throughout ginseng growth. The compounds increase the colonization abilities of the biocontrol strains, which is conducive to the regulation of soil microecology and a prolonged duration of action, which is one of the important synergistic mechanisms. −

Soil microbiology mainly encompasses two interdependent biotic components: soil enzymes and soil microorganisms. The results showed that, compound treatment increased the four soil enzymes activities in the rhizosphere soil of ginseng compared with the single agents (biological and chemical fungicides), which could improve the soil environment and increase the uptake and utilization of soil nutrients by ginseng. Soil microorganisms are essential components of the soil environment, and the composition of inter-root microbial communities is a major indicator of soil health. The introduction of biocontrol bacteria can regulate the soil environment and alter the population composition and diversity of soil microorganisms, thereby affecting the ecological function of the soil. It has been reported that biocontrol bacteria play key roles in the early stages of microecology, primarily by inducing changes in the soil microbial community. These changes are reflected in the recruitment of beneficial bacteria, reducing the abundance of harmful communities, and increasing microcosmic diversity, all of which contribute to a more balanced and healthy soil ecosystem. , In this study, compared with single agent treatment, compound treatment decreased the abundance of harmful genera and recruited more beneficial genera to the soil microbial community. Mortierella alpina exerts prominent biocontrol effects against P. notoginseng root rot mainly by regulating the rhizosphere microbial communities. The Bacillus-Serratia consortium can reprogram rhizosphere fungal communities and enhance α-diversity in P. notoginseng. Zhou et al. reported that soil microbes and enzymes were significantly associated with wheat yield. Therefore, the compound treatments control ginseng rusty root rot and promote ginseng growth by decreasing the contents of pathogenic fungi and increasing the contents of beneficial genera, which are positively correlated with ginseng root weight and soil enzyme activity.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that bacteriostatic lipopeptides are the primary antimicrobial compounds secreted by B. subtilis that play a pivotal role in plant disease control. − For example, Xu et al. reported that the gene encoding a lipopeptide synthetase in B. amyloliquefaciens SDTB009 was upregulated 2- to 4-fold in the presence of difenoconazole. Similarly, Madhurankhi et al. noted that fungicide synthesis can influence lipopeptide production. In the present study, the expression levels of seven genes involved in the synthesis of inhibitory lipopeptides in biocontrol strains were significantly increased in the presence of chemical agents, which aligns with previous findings. These data suggest that chemical compounds may increase the expression of antimicrobial biosynthesis genes in biocontrol strains, thereby promoting the production of antimicrobial metabolites. This upregulation likely contributes to the improved inhibitory capacity of biocontrol bacteria in laboratory settings and their enhanced efficacy in field applications. These also lay the groundwork for subsequent metabolomic investigations.

Previous research has utilized transcriptomic and metabolomic approaches to explore the role of flavonoids in wheat resistance to powdery mildew, demonstrating that exogenous flavonoids reduce infection severity. Additionally, the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway was activated in cotton infected with Verticillium dahliae, with five metabolites of caffeic acid, coniferyl alcohol, coniferin, scopoletin, and scopolin exhibiting significant inhibitory effects on V. dahliae growth in vitro, highlighting their role in cotton resistance to Verticillium wilt. In this study, metabolomic analysis of the NJ13 strain revealed several metabolites that were significantly upregulated upon its cocultivation with fludioxonil. Notably, p-hydroxybenzaldehyde and 4-methylphenol effectively inhibited I. robusta, confirming that this chemical agent promotes the production of inhibitory substances by biocontrol bacteria. These findings provide a mechanistic explanation for the synergistic effects observed when biocontrol agents are combined with chemical fungicides and offer a theoretical foundation for the potential development of p-hydroxybenzaldehyde and 4-methylphenol as biological control agents for ginseng rusty root rot.

In this study, transcriptomic analysis revealed that chemical agents significantly impacted the transcriptome of the NJ13 strain, with 2275 genes showing differential expression. These findings offer valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the synergistic use of biofungicides and chemical agents. Homologous recombination has long served as a pivotal tool in bacterial genetic research. By leveraging the principle of homologous recombination, a targeted gene fragment can be replaced with a synthetically designed homologous DNA fragment via a process termed gene knockout, which enables precise genetic deletion. Subsequent phenotypic characterization of the knockout strain allows for functional inference of the corresponding gene, elucidating its role in cellular processes or stress responses. , It is plan to conduct an investigation and functional validation of the core differentially expressed genes in strain NJ13. Identifying key genes regulating antibacterial activity in strain NJ13 through gene knockout technology allows for exploring their potential for biocontrol applications.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated the viability of the synergistic use of biological agents and chemical fungicides to control plant diseases, achieved by strategically integrating a variety of chemical fungicides with different types of biocontrol agents. This integrated approach not only significantly enhanced disease control efficacy but also markedly reduced the reliance on chemical fungicides. Moreover, this study revealed the synergistic mechanism of these compounds from multiple aspects, establishing a solid foundation for the development of the combined use of biofungicides and chemicals for controlling other crop diseases.
