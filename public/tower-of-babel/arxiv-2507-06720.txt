Green function and singularities in Stokes flow confined by cylindrical walls
Giuseppe Procopio

Abstract
In this article, the Green function for the Stokes flow in the interior, exterior, and annular regions bounded by cylindrical walls is derived as a function of the pole position and expressed invariantly both at the field and pole points. Specifically, the Green’s function is obtained using a cylindrical harmonic expansion of the Stokes flow within the bitensorial formulation introduced in Procopio and Giona 2023 . This formulation allows us to obtain higher-order singularities within the same domains, such as the confined Couplet and Stresslet , by simply differentiating the Green’s function at its pole. Moreover, the confined Sourcelet and its associated multipoles are derived from the Green’s function through a new method that enforces the reciprocal properties of the Stokes flow. The resulting singularities are then employed to address hydrodynamic problems involving active and passive colloids interacting with cylindrical walls, such as sedimenting particles in the annular cylindrical region and the attractive or repulsive hydrodynamic forces exerted by the cylindrical boundaries on a microswimmer.

Introduction

Stokes flows inside or outside cylindrical boundaries represent hydrodynamic systems of great interest in physical, chemical, and biological sciences and their technological applications.
The Poiseuille flow in a cylindrical capillary and the Taylor-Couette flow between two concentric rotating cylinders are among the most paradigmatic flows in hydrodynamics.
However, many hydrodynamic systems delimited by cylindrical boundaries involve more complex flows than simple Poiseuille and Taylor-Couette flows, especially whenever colloids are suspended in the fluid. This is the case of several classical equipment for treating colloidal suspensions

Edam et al. 2011

In Section

VIII
Conclusions

In a general perspective, the method applied here shows how, by employing the bitensorial formalism introduced in
Procopio and Giona 2023
, it is possible to obtain all the Stokes singularities in a confined domain once the Green’s function is known. More specifically, it is shown that all singularities can be derived either by differentiating the Green’s function at its pole (in the case of ”momentum” singularities such as the Couplet, Stresslet, and higher-order Stokes multipoles), or by using the reciprocal relations of the Green’s function (in the case of ”mass” singularities such as the Sourcelet, Source Dipole, and higher-order Source multipoles). Therefore, this represents an efficient method for evaluating the singularities associated with a given confinement in a unified manner.

The bitensorial representation — which takes into account the tensorial nature of the singular fields at both the field and pole points — paves the way for describing the hydrodynamics of many singularities in confined domains, and thus for investigating the mutual interactions between multiple colloids and, simultaneously, their interactions with the confining walls.
Moreover, as shown in
Procopio and Giona 2024b
, knowledge of all derivatives of the Green’s function can provide the full hydromechanics of colloids in confined flows, even in cases involving complex colloid shapes and characteristic sizes comparable to that of the confinement. Therefore, the results obtained in this article might represent the starting point for characterizing the complex hydrodynamic interactions that can arise in other systems of scientific and technological interest.

More specifically, the study of hydromechanics and colloid transport near cylindrical walls reveals peculiar characteristics that might differ considerably from those occurring in other well-studied geometries such as planar or spherical walls. The importance in applications, the widespread adoption and the simplicity of this geometry call for a thorough understanding of its microscale hydrodynamics, to which this work aims to contribute. In fact, Stokes singularities provide a valid model for describing the hydromechanics of colloids, and this article addresses examples of colloidal hydrodynamic interactions with cylindrical walls — including interactions with sedimenting particles and microswimmers — though only at a preliminary level, as a comprehensive analysis of these phenomena falls outside the scope of this study.

Appendix C
Useful geometric relations for the cylindrical coordinate system

The metric tensor of the cylindrical coordinate system at the point
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is obtained by using eqs. (
20
) and the expression
Synge and Schild 1978

g_{a\,b}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\dfrac{\partial\overline{x}_{i}}{\partial x^{a}}\,\dfrac{\partial\overline{x}_{i}}{\partial x^{b}}

(112)

From which

g_{1\,1}({\boldsymbol{x}})=1;\quad g_{2\,2}({\boldsymbol{x}})=(x^{1})^{2};\quad g_{3\,3}({\boldsymbol{x}})=1;\quad g_{a\,b}({\boldsymbol{x}})=0\quad\text{for}\quad a\neq b

(113)

the contravariant metric tensor
g^{a\,b}({\boldsymbol{x}})
, defined by the relation
g^{a\,c}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,g_{c\,b}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\delta^{a}_{b}
reads

g^{1\,1}({\boldsymbol{x}})=1;\quad g^{2\,2}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\dfrac{1}{(x^{1})^{2}};\quad g^{3\,3}({\boldsymbol{x}})=1;\quad g^{a\,b}({\boldsymbol{x}})=0\quad\text{for}\quad a\neq b

(114)

Similarly, at the point
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, the metric tensor of the cylindrical coordinate system is

g_{1\,1}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=1;\quad g_{2\,2}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=(\xi^{1})^{2};\quad g_{3\,3}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=1;\quad g_{\alpha\,\beta}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=0\quad\text{for}\quad\alpha\neq\beta

(115)

and

g^{1\,1}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=1;\quad g^{2\,2}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\dfrac{1}{(\xi^{1})^{2}};\quad g^{3\,3}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=1;\quad g^{\alpha\,\beta}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=0\quad\text{for}\quad\alpha\neq\beta

(116)

The
non vanishing entries of the
Christoffel symbols
\Gamma^{a}_{b\,c}({\boldsymbol{x}})
of second kind at the point
{\boldsymbol{x}}

are

\Gamma^{1}_{2\,2}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,=\,-x^{1};\quad\Gamma^{2}_{1\,2}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,=\,\Gamma^{2}_{2\,1}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,=\,\dfrac{1}{x^{1}}

(117)

and of the Christoffel symbols
\Gamma^{\alpha}_{\beta\,\gamma}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})

at the point
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}

\Gamma^{1}_{2\,2}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\,=\,-\xi^{1};\quad\Gamma^{2}_{1\,2}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\,=\,\Gamma^{2}_{2\,1}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\,=\,\dfrac{1}{\xi^{1}}

(118)

The transformation matrix
\dfrac{\partial\overline{x}_{i}}{\partial x^{a}}
,
that transforms the contravariant entries of a vector
{\boldsymbol{v}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
from the cylindrical coordinates into the Cartesian coordinates via

\overline{v}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\dfrac{\partial\overline{x}_{i}}{\partial x^{a}}\,v^{a}({\boldsymbol{x}})

(119)

admits entries

\begin{array}[]{l}\dfrac{\partial\overline{x}_{1}}{\partial x^{1}}=\cos{(x^{2})};\quad\dfrac{\partial\overline{x}_{1}}{\partial x^{2}}=-x^{1}\sin{(x^{2})};\quad\dfrac{\partial\overline{x}_{2}}{\partial x^{1}}=\sin{(x^{2})};\quad\dfrac{\partial\overline{x}_{2}}{\partial x^{2}}=x^{1}\cos{(x^{2})};\\[15.0pt]
\dfrac{\partial\overline{x}_{3}}{\partial x^{3}}=1;\quad\dfrac{\partial\overline{x}_{1}}{\partial x^{3}}=\dfrac{\partial\overline{x}_{3}}{\partial x^{1}}=\dfrac{\partial\overline{x}_{2}}{\partial x^{3}}=\dfrac{\partial\overline{x}_{3}}{\partial x^{2}}=0\end{array}

(120)

Its inverse matrix
\dfrac{\partial x^{a}}{\partial\overline{x}_{i}}
is defined componentwise

\begin{array}[]{l}\dfrac{\partial x^{1}}{\partial\overline{x}_{1}}=\cos{(x^{2})};\quad\dfrac{\partial x^{1}}{\partial\overline{x}_{2}}=\sin{(x^{2})};\quad\dfrac{\partial x^{2}}{\partial\overline{x}_{1}}=-\dfrac{\sin{(x^{2})}}{x^{1}};\quad\dfrac{\partial x^{2}}{\partial\overline{x}_{2}}=\dfrac{\cos{(x^{2})}}{x^{1}};\\[15.0pt]
\dfrac{\partial x^{3}}{\partial\overline{x}_{3}}=1;\quad\dfrac{\partial x^{1}}{\partial\overline{x}_{3}}=\dfrac{\partial x^{3}}{\partial\overline{x}_{1}}=\dfrac{\partial x^{2}}{\partial\overline{x}_{3}}=\dfrac{\partial x^{3}}{\partial\overline{x}_{2}}=0\end{array}

(121)

Similarly, at the point
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, the transformation matrix

\dfrac{\partial\overline{\xi}_{i}}{\partial\xi^{a}}

\begin{array}[]{l}\dfrac{\partial\overline{\xi}_{1}}{\partial\xi^{1}}=\cos{(\xi^{2})};\quad\dfrac{\partial\overline{\xi}_{1}}{\partial\xi^{2}}=-\xi^{1}\sin{(\xi^{2})};\quad\dfrac{\partial\overline{\xi}_{2}}{\partial\xi^{1}}=\sin{(\xi^{2})};\quad\dfrac{\partial\overline{\xi}_{2}}{\partial\xi^{2}}=\xi^{1}\cos{(\xi^{2})};\\[15.0pt]
\dfrac{\partial\overline{\xi}_{3}}{\partial\xi^{3}}=1;\quad\dfrac{\partial\overline{\xi}_{1}}{\partial\xi^{3}}=\dfrac{\partial\overline{\xi}_{3}}{\partial\xi^{1}}=\dfrac{\partial\overline{\xi}_{2}}{\partial\xi^{3}}=\dfrac{\partial\overline{\xi}_{3}}{\partial\xi^{2}}=0\end{array}

(122)

and the inverse matrix
\dfrac{\partial\xi^{a}}{\partial\overline{\xi}_{i}}

\begin{array}[]{l}\dfrac{\partial\xi^{1}}{\partial\overline{\xi}_{1}}=\cos{(\xi^{2})};\quad\dfrac{\partial\xi^{1}}{\partial\overline{\xi}_{2}}=\sin{(\xi^{2})};\quad\dfrac{\partial\xi^{2}}{\partial\overline{\xi}_{1}}=-\dfrac{\sin{(\xi^{2})}}{\xi^{1}};\quad\dfrac{\partial\xi^{2}}{\partial\overline{\xi}_{2}}=\dfrac{\cos{(\xi^{2})}}{\xi^{1}};\\[15.0pt]
\dfrac{\partial\xi^{1}}{\partial\overline{\xi}_{1}}=1;\quad\dfrac{\partial\xi^{1}}{\partial\overline{\xi}_{3}}=\dfrac{\partial\xi^{3}}{\partial\overline{\xi}_{1}}=\dfrac{\partial\xi^{2}}{\partial\overline{\xi}_{3}}=\dfrac{\partial\xi^{3}}{\partial\overline{\xi}_{2}}=0\end{array}

(123)
