Pre-existing hemagglutinin-specific antibody levels are associated with B-cell repertoire maturation and antibody breadth after influenza vaccination
Authors: Wei Wang, Yan Sun, Qing Liu, Yidan Xia, Jing Wang, Minhao Hu, Mengyao Kong, Jun He, Rongbao Gao, Yong Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Repeated influenza exposures generate complex antibody landscapes, yet how pre-existing antibodies are associated with subsequent vaccine responses remains unclear. We longitudinally tracked 21 adults stratified by pre-vaccination neutralizing titers to examine B-cell dynamics using flow cytometry, B cell receptor (BCR) sequencing, and monoclonal antibody characterization. After vaccination, participants with high baseline titers showed a longitudinal increase in the frequency of HA+CD27−IgD+ naive-phenotype B cells. BCRs recovered from hemagglutinin (HA)-binding (HA+) B cells in the high-titer group displayed lower somatic hypermutation and shorter CDR3s, whereas those recovered from the lower-titer groups showed higher mutation levels and more extensive class switching. Correspondingly, monoclonal antibodies from the high-titer group generally showed narrow reactivity, while those from lower-titer groups showed broader neutralizing activity against historical strains. Clonal tracing further suggested that mAbs from lower-titer groups were more often linked to pre-existing sequences. Together, these findings suggest that pre-existing antibody levels are associated with differences in B-cell repertoire maturation and antibody breadth after influenza vaccination.

Introduction

Seasonal influenza causes up to one billion infections and at least 290,000 deaths worldwide each year (Iuliano et al., 2018). Vaccination remains a key public health intervention, but its effectiveness has varied substantially over the past two decades, with protection rates ranging from less than 20%–60% in some years (U.S. CDC, 2025). Hemagglutinin (HA), a surface glycoprotein of the influenza virus, is the principal antigen that elicits protective antibody responses. Despite annual updates to influenza vaccines, rapid HA mutations frequently result in mismatches between vaccine strains and circulating strains, undermining vaccine efficacy (Tricco et al., 2013; Uyeki et al., 2022). Additional factors, including age (Frasca and Blomberg, 2020; Liu et al., 2021), sex (Engler et al., 2008; Furman et al., 2014), adjuvant use (Clark et al., 2009; Jackson et al., 2012), and prior infection history (Xie et al., 2017; Khurana et al., 2019), also influence vaccine effectiveness. Most individuals are initially exposed to influenza during childhood and may encounter antigenic variants every 5–10 years (Bodewes et al., 2011; Lessler et al., 2012). Over time, these repeated exposures generate pre-existing immunity through both memory B cells and antibodies produced by long-lived plasma cells, forming two major arms of immune memory (Akkaya et al., 2020; Koutsakos M. and Ellebedy, 2021).

Memory B cells mediate rapid recall responses by differentiating into antibody-secreting cells upon antigen re-exposure. Although most antibody-secreting cells are short-lived (3–5 days) (Wrammert et al., 2008), a subset develops into long-lived plasma cells that reside in the bone marrow and continuously produce antibodies with the potential to cross-react with antigenic variants (Koutsakos M. and Ellebedy, 2021). The “original antigenic sin (OAS)” hypothesis, first proposed in the 1960s, suggests that initial or early exposure to influenza biases subsequent immune responses toward conserved epitopes shared by primary and variant strains (Francis, 1960). However, immune responses after repeated exposure are not restricted to memory recall. In mice, re-immunization with antigenic variants induces de novo responses to novel epitopes, and secondary germinal centers have been reported to contain a substantial proportion of naive B cells (Mesin et al., 2020; Viant et al., 2020; Kuraoka et al., 2022; Tas et al., 2022; Schiepers et al., 2023). The participation of naive B cells in secondary responses may also be influenced by antigenic variation and pre-existing humoral immunity (Viant et al., 2020; Tas et al., 2022). In humans, influenza vaccination has also been shown to induce heterogeneous germinal-center responses involving B cells with different phenotypic and clonal features (Turner et al., 2020). These observations suggest that repeated antigen exposure may reshape the responding B-cell repertoire rather than simply reproduce the primary response.

Pre-existing antibodies may contribute directly to this process. Depending on their concentration, affinity, and epitope specificity, circulating antibodies can promote antigen deposition through immune-complex formation or limit access to previously targeted epitopes through epitope masking and steric hindrance. BCR affinity for antigen can influence the selection of B cells into memory or germinal-center compartments (Viant et al., 2020), while high concentrations of circulating antibodies can restrict the recruitment of cognate naive B cells during secondary responses (Tas et al., 2022). These antibody-mediated feedback mechanisms may therefore alter which B-cell clones engage vaccine antigens and subsequently undergo expansion, somatic hypermutation, class switching, and functional selection.

Human influenza immunity is particularly complex because most individuals experience repeated infections and vaccinations involving antigenically distinct H1N1, H3N2, and influenza B viruses across multiple seasons. As a result, baseline antibody levels may reflect heterogeneous exposure histories and may be associated with different B-cell response trajectories. However, it remains unclear whether pre-existing antibody levels are associated with longitudinal changes in HA+ B-cell populations, the maturation characteristics of the responding BCR repertoire, and the breadth of antibodies generated after seasonal influenza vaccination.

In this study, we longitudinally tracked 21 influenza vaccine recipients, stratified by pre-existing neutralizing antibody titers. By integrating flow cytometry and B cell receptor (BCR) profiling, we observed that high pre-existing antibody titers were associated with an increase in HA+CD27−IgD+ naive-phenotype B cells and with less mature BCR repertoire features. In contrast, the medium- and low-titer groups showed more mature BCR repertoire features, including higher SHM, greater class switching, and longer CDR3s. Their expressed mAbs also more often displayed broader binding and neutralization profiles. These findings suggest that pre-existing antibody levels are associated with B-cell repertoire maturation and antibody breadth after influenza vaccination. This association may offer a useful perspective for future vaccine design.

Results

Pre-existing antibody levels are associated with homologous antibody responses following influenza vaccination

We enrolled 21 healthy adults (Supplementary Table S1) and administered the 2021–2022 Northern Hemisphere seasonal quadrivalent inactivated influenza vaccine (QIV) containing A/Victoria/2570/2019 H1N1 (A/VC/19), A/Cambodia/e0826360/2020 H3N2 (A/CA/20), B/Washington/02/2019 B/Victoria (B/WA/19), and B/Phuket/3073/2013 B/Yamagata (B/PH/13). Serial blood samples were obtained on Days 0 (pre-vaccination), 14, and 28 post-vaccination. These samples were analyzed for HA-specific IgG levels by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and for neutralizing antibodies via pseudovirus neutralization assays.

Pre-existing HA-specific IgG titers varied substantially among participants. Post-vaccination, IgG titers against all four vaccine strains increased significantly (Fig. 1A), with similar kinetics observed for the homologous components (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Pseudovirus-based neutralization assays demonstrated significant increases in neutralizing antibodies against A/CA/20 and B/WA/19 post-vaccination (Fig. 1B). Contrary to some previous reports suggesting interference by pre-existing antibodies (Dugan et al., 2020; Ellebedy et al., 2020; Wild et al., 2021), we did not observe a clear correlation between pre-existing and post-vaccination titers for HA-specific IgG or neutralizing antibodies (Supplementary Fig. S1C and S1D). In contrast, pre-existing antibody levels were inversely associated with the fold-increase in HA-specific IgG levels (Fig. 1C) and neutralizing antibody titers after vaccination (Fig. 1D). Pre-existing HA-specific IgG levels were also significantly correlated with pre-existing neutralizing antibody titers for all vaccine components except for B/WA/19 (Fig. 1E).

To assess the cross-protective potential of the vaccine, we tested neutralization against two updated 2022–2023 QIV components: A/Darwin/9/2021 H3N2 (A/DA/21) and B/Austria/1359417/2021 (B/AU/21). Neither strain showed a significant increase in neutralization after vaccination (Supplementary Fig. S1E). Similarly, no cross-neutralization was detected against the avian influenza strains A/Anhui/1/2013 H7N9 (A/AH/13) or A/Anhui/1/2005 H5N1 (A/AH/05) (Supplementary Fig. S1F).

Taken together, pre-existing antibody levels were inversely associated with the magnitude of the homologous serologic response after vaccination, whereas cross-reactive neutralization against updated seasonal or avian strains remained limited under these conditions.

HA+CD27−IgD+ naive-phenotype B cells increased within the high-titer group after vaccination

While circulating antibodies are produced exclusively by antibody-secreting cells, they do not fully represent the antigen-specific B cell repertoire (Davis et al., 2019; Gaebler et al., 2021; Turner et al., 2021). To examine how pre-existing neutralizing antibodies are associated with B-cell responses, we stratified the 21 participants according to the sum of their Day 0 Log IC50 values against the four vaccine strains. Participants with values above the 75th percentile, between the 25th and 75th percentiles, and below the 25th percentile were assigned to the high-, medium-, and low-titer groups, respectively (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 2A). Because no established biological threshold was available in this setting, the 25th and 75th percentiles were chosen to compare participants at the lower and upper ends of the cohort distribution with those in the middle range. These cutoffs were used for exploratory within-cohort comparisons and were not intended to represent validated biological thresholds. Although the flow-cytometric probes combined four vaccine HAs, strain-resolved serology (ELISA and neutralization) showed component-specific boosting (Supplementary Fig. S1A and B). Four HA proteins from the 2021–2022 vaccine were biotinylated and mixed in equal amounts. HA+ B cells were identified as CD19+CD20+CD38−HA+ and were further classified into CD27−IgD+ naive-phenotype and CD27+IgD+/− memory-phenotype populations (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. S2). The term “naive-phenotype” refers only to the CD27−IgD+ surface phenotype and does not imply a confirmed antigen-naive origin. After vaccination, HA+ B cells increased in the high- and medium-titer groups (Supplementary Fig. S3, Fig. 2C), and the magnitude of this expansion was positively correlated with pre-existing neutralizing antibody titers (Supplementary Fig. S4A). The frequency of HA+CD27−IgD+ naive-phenotype B cells changed differently across the three baseline-titer groups (group-by-time interaction, F (4, 33) = 4.007, P = 0.0093). In the high-titer group, the frequency increased from Day 0 to Days 14 and 28, whereas no comparable increase was observed in the medium- or low-titer groups (Supplementary Fig. S3, Fig. 2D). Pre-existing antibody levels were also positively correlated with the fold change in this population on Days 14 and 28 (Supplementary Fig. S4B). During secondary immune activation, class-switched memory B cells (IgD−CD27+) rapidly differentiate into plasma cells and contribute to the early antibody response, whereas non-class-switched memory B cells (IgD+/ IgM+) may re-enter the germinal centers for further selection (Seifert and Küppers, 2016) and contribute to later memory responses (Pape et al., 2011). Class-switched memory B cells peaked transiently on Day 14 before declining, whereas non-class-switched memory B cells showed more sustained persistence in the high- and medium-titer groups (Supplementary Fig. S3, Fig. 2E). To further characterize HA+ B cells after vaccination, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on sorted HA+ B cells from 10 participants with different levels of pre-existing antibodies at Day 28 post-vaccination (Supplementary Table S1). HA+ B cells were grouped into four subsets based on gene expression (Fig. 3A, B). Naive-like clusters showed high expression of early activation-associated regulators such as BACH2 and IRF4, as well as the germinal center commitment factor BCL6 (Fig. 3C) (Ochiai et al., 2013; Willis et al., 2014; Recaldin and Fear, 2016). In contrast, memory B-cell clusters showed higher expression of activation-related regulators, including EBI3, DHRS9, TBX21, KLF10, ZBTB32, ZBTB20, TFEC, and KLF2 (Fig. 3C). Exhausted cells showed high XBP1 expression, whereas none of the subsets expressed the plasma cell differentiation marker BLIMP1 (Fig. 3C). Surface molecule analysis further distinguished naive-like subsets by relatively higher expression of IL21R, IL4R, and CCR7, consistent with enhanced responsiveness to IL-21/IL-4 signaling and lymphoid homing potential. In contrast, memory clusters showed increased expression of CD86 and CXCR3 (Fig. 3D). Pathway analysis indicated enrichment of proliferation- and antigen-recognition-related pathways in naive-like cells, whereas memory subsets were more enriched for antigen presentation and NF-κB-related survival pathways (Fig. 3E). HA-binding BCR sequences in the high-titer group show less mature repertoire features

To analyze vaccine-induced HA-binding BCR dynamics, we performed next-generation sequencing (NGS) on longitudinal samples from 10 donors and single-cell V(D)J sequencing on pooled HA+ B cells isolated from Day 28 PBMCs (Supplementary Fig. S5). Because the number of sorted HA+ B cells was limited, cells from all 10 donors were pooled to achieve sufficient loading for the 10 × platform. We recovered 10,776 paired BCR sequences from the pooled single-cell dataset, and 1638 heavy-chain sequences had at least one match in the donor-resolved NGS data based on identical amino acid sequences in the heavy-chain complementarity-determining region 3 (CDRH3), together with the same IGHV and IGHJ gene usage (Fig. 4A). Antigen-experienced B cells typically undergo clonal expansion, somatic hypermutation (SHM), class-switch recombination (CSR), and clonal selection, ultimately differentiating into long-lived plasma cells that produce high-affinity antibodies (Akkaya et al., 2020). B cells with identical heavy- and light-chain CDR3 sequences were grouped into the same clonotype, and clonotypes containing more than one cell were defined as expanded clonotypes. Using this approach, we identified 140, 1308, and 231 clonotypes in the high-, medium- and low-titer groups, respectively. The high-titer group showed limited clonal expansion (2 expanded clonotypes, compared with 124 in medium-titer and 12 in low-titer groups; Fig. 4B, Supplementary Fig. S6A). HA-binding BCRs assigned to the high-titer group also showed less mature repertoire features, including lower SHM levels, a higher proportion of unmutated sequences, and shorter heavy chain CDR3 regions (Fig. 4C–E, Supplementary Fig. S6B-D). In contrast, sequences assigned to the medium- and low-titer groups showed features more consistent with antigen-experienced repertoires, including higher SHM levels (Fig. 4C), increased class switching to IgG/IgA (Fig. 4D), and longer CDR3 lengths (Fig. 4E).

Certain variable-region germline genes have been reported in broadly reactive antibodies targeting conserved influenza epitopes (Ekiert et al., 2009; Sui et al., 2009; Joyce et al., 2016; Sangesland et al., 2019). Among them, IGHV1-69 has been frequently identified in stem-targeting broadly reactive antibodies (Avnir et al., 2014; Lang et al., 2017). In this analysis, the high-titer group showed lower usage of some V genes previously reported in cross-reactive influenza antibodies (Fig. 4F, Supplementary Fig. S6E). Although germline gene usage is not a direct feature of repertoire maturation, the lower SHM levels, higher proportion of unmutated sequences, reduced class switching, and shorter CDR3s collectively support a less mature BCR repertoire in the high-titer group. The lower usage of several V genes previously reported in broadly reactive influenza antibodies may additionally indicate reduced representation of such antibody features, although this observation should be interpreted cautiously.

Monoclonal antibodies with distinct binding and neutralization profiles were identified across pre-existing antibody levels

To characterize the binding and neutralizing properties of the identified antibodies, we analyzed recombinant monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) derived from single-cell V(D)J sequencing. A total of 37 mAbs were expressed, including 9 assigned to the high-titer group and 28 assigned to the medium- and low-titer groups (Supplementary Table S2). While 28 of the 37 mAbs bound the 2021–2022 vaccine HA (Fig. 5A), those assigned to the high-titer group tended to show weaker binding than those from the medium- and low-titer groups (e.g., strongest binder 2c4: EC50 = 1.88 × 10−3 μg/mL versus 17c2: EC50 = 1.50 × 10−4 μg/mL; Fig. 5A and B). To further assess the functional breadth of these antibodies, we tested neutralization against historical influenza strains that predate the 2021–2022 seasonal vaccine (Supplementary Table S3). In the high-titer group, only mAb 4c1 neutralized the historical H3N2 strains A/Wuhan/359/1995 and A/Fujian/411/2002, whereas several mAbs from the medium- and low-titer groups showed broader neutralization profiles. For instance, 5c2 neutralized B/Hong Kong/330/2001 (IC50 = 6.39 × 10−3 μg/mL), and 6c2 neutralized A/Wuhan/359/1995 (IC50 = 4.68 × 10−3 μg/mL), while mAb 10c1 neutralized multiple influenza B strains (Fig. 5D). Overall, 57% of mAbs from the medium- and low-titer groups neutralized at least one historical strain, compared to only 11% in the high-titer group (Fig. 5E). To examine whether representative mAbs from different baseline-titer groups competed with reference mAbs recognizing defined HA regions, we selected representative mAbs from each group, including both strain-specific and cross-reactive mAbs. For each HA-reference mAb pair, only mAbs showing sufficient binding to the corresponding HA antigen were tested. Among the representative mAbs from the high-titer group, most showed no competition with the reference mAbs, although 4c1 partially competed with CR9114. In the medium-titer group, 10c1 strongly competed with CR8071 for binding to both B/WA/19 and B/PH/13 HA. Partial competition with CR8071 was observed for 5c1, 5c2, and 6c9, while 10c3 partially competed with C05. mAbs 6c2 and 6c9 partially competed with CR9114, and 8c2 and 10c2 with FluA-20. In the low-titer group, 17c2 partially competed with CR9114, and 17c4 showed weak partial competition with CR9114 on A/WH/95 HA but not on A/VC/19 HA (Supplementary Fig. S7). These competition patterns suggest that several selected mAbs recognized HA regions overlapping with or proximal to those recognized by CR8071, C05, CR9114, or FluA-20.

To further investigate the temporal dynamics of mAb clonal lineages, we analyzed their presence at Days 0, 14, and 28 after vaccination. Among the 37 identified mAb clonal lineages, 11 were detectable before vaccination, and only one of these was assigned to the high-titer group (Fig. 6A). Five mAb clonal lineages from the high-titer group (2c3, 4c2, 4c3, 4c4, and 4c5) were detected only at Day 28 in the available samples, whereas most lineages from the medium- and low-titer groups were already detectable by Day 14 despite being absent at baseline (Fig. 6A). Phylogenetic trees were evaluated for all mAb lineages with sufficient longitudinally matched sequences to generate informative trees across the three baseline-titer groups (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. S8). Discussion

In this study, pre-existing neutralizing antibody levels were associated with several features of the HA+ B-cell response after influenza vaccination. Higher baseline titers were inversely associated with fold increases in HA-specific IgG and neutralizing antibody titers, a distinct longitudinal increase in HA+ CD27−IgD+ naive-phenotype B cells, and less mature BCR repertoire features. In contrast, BCR sequences and expressed mAbs from the medium- and low-titer groups more often showed higher SHM and a higher proportion of class-switched sequences, and broader binding or neutralizing activity. These findings indicate differences in BCR repertoire maturation and antibody breadth across baseline-antibody levels but do not establish whether the responding cells originated from naive or memory B-cell compartments. The flow-cytometric findings should also be interpreted in the context of the mixed-HA probe, whereas the strain-resolved serologic assays showed different levels of boosting across the four vaccine components (Supplementary Fig. S1A, B).

Several observations support this association. First, the longitudinal change in HA+CD27−IgD+ naive-phenotype B cells differed across the three baseline-titer groups. Although the frequency increased from Day 0 to Days 14 and 28 in the high-titer group, the frequencies at Day 14 did not differ significantly among the groups (Fig. 2D). This result reflects different longitudinal trajectories rather than a higher post-vaccination frequency in the high-titer group. The pooled single-cell transcriptomic data identified naive-like and memory-like transcriptional states among post-vaccination HA+ B cells, but the pooled design did not allow group-specific comparisons at the individual level (Fig. 3C–E). Second, HA-binding BCRs from the high-titer group showed lower SHM, a higher proportion of unmutated sequences, less class switching, and shorter CDR3s. In contrast, sequences from the medium- and low-titer groups more often showed features associated with antigen experience (Fig. 4C–E). The expressed mAbs from the medium- and low-titer groups also showed broader binding and neutralizing profiles (Fig. 5). Competition ELISA further showed that several mAbs from the medium- and low-titer groups partially or strongly competed with reference mAbs recognizing defined HA regions, whereas most tested mAbs from the high-titer group showed little or no competition (Supplementary Fig. S7). Third, clonal tracing identified different temporal patterns across groups, with more lineages linked to pre-existing sequences in the medium- and low-titer groups (Fig. 6). However, because lineage detection in peripheral blood is influenced by sampling depth, these data should be interpreted cautiously and do not on their own establish naive versus memory origin.

Some findings in the medium- and low-titer groups were consistent with OAS hypothesis, including broader neutralization of historical strains and more lineages linked to pre-existing sequences. By contrast, the high-titer group did not show the same pattern. Classical OAS focuses mainly on the strain encountered during early influenza exposure and the later bias toward that strain or shared epitopes. We instead examined the level of neutralizing antibodies present before vaccination. These levels were associated with different B-cell phenotypes, BCR repertoire features, and antibody breadth after vaccination. This suggests that the pre-existing antibody levels at the time of vaccination may also be relevant to how earlier influenza exposure affects later responses. Because the priming strains and complete exposure histories were unknown, we could not determine whether the observed patterns represented classical OAS.

A recent review summarized the key mechanisms by which antibodies can provide both positive and negative feedback on B-cell responses (Cyster and Wilson, 2024) and offers a useful framework for interpreting our findings. Antibodies may form immune complexes that promote antigen deposition in lymphoid follicles, but they may also reduce access to previously targeted epitopes through epitope masking or steric hindrance. The balance of these effects is likely to depend on antibody concentration, affinity, and epitope specificity. Such mechanisms could alter the opportunity for different B-cell clones to engage vaccine antigens and may contribute to the repertoire differences observed across the baseline-titer groups. Previous studies have shown that high concentrations of circulating antibodies can block recruitment of cognate naive B cells to germinal centers during secondary responses in HIV models (Tas et al., 2022). In our study, however, higher baseline titers were associated with an increase in the frequency of HA+CD27−IgD+ naive-phenotype B cells. This observation should not be interpreted as direct evidence that high antibody levels promoted naive B-cell recruitment, because the CD27−IgD+ phenotype does not establish antigen-naive origin. In addition, the mixed-HA probe used here captured a polyclonal population recognizing multiple HA proteins and epitopes, whereas studies using defined epitopes mainly assess competition between antibodies and cognate naive B cells recognizing the same epitopes. Antibodies masking previously targeted epitopes might restrict some B-cell clones while leaving other HA regions accessible to clones with different specificities. Antibody feedback may influence which B-cell clones can engage HA. Their subsequent selection and maturation also depend on cognate CD4+ T-cell help, particularly from T follicular helper cells. T follicular helper cells support germinal-center selection, class switching, and somatic hypermutation after influenza vaccination (Koutsakos et al., 2019). Differences in pre-existing or vaccine-induced T-cell help may therefore have contributed to the BCR maturation patterns observed across the baseline-titer groups. However, T-cell responses were not assessed in this study, and their contribution could not be determined.

Despite these insights, the study has several limitations. First, the overall sample size was small (n = 21), particularly in the high-titer group (n = 4), which limited statistical power, especially for subgroup comparisons, and restricted the generalizability of the findings. Although participants were not selected according to antibody titer, selection bias associated with recruitment of a small volunteer cohort cannot be excluded. The findings therefore require confirmation in larger cohorts. Complete histories of prior influenza exposure could not be reliably reconstructed. We therefore used Day 0 neutralizing antibody titers as a functional measure of pre-existing humoral immunity, but this measure could not capture the timing, frequency, or specific strains involved in previous exposures. In addition, no established biological threshold was available for stratification. The 25th- and 75th-percentile cutoffs were used only for exploratory comparisons within this cohort and should not be interpreted as validated biological or clinical thresholds. Second, the study did not capture the full immune response to influenza vaccination. We focused on HA and did not assess antibody responses to NA, M2e, NP, or other influenza proteins. These responses may vary with prior influenza exposure and may influence vaccine responses through mechanisms not captured by the present analyses. T-cell responses were also not measured, and the contribution of CD4+ T-cell help, particularly from T follicular helper cells, could not be assessed. Plasmablasts and plasma cells were not reliably captured because HA-probe identification required detectable surface BCR, and no evident CD38high antibody-secreting-cell population was observed within the HA+ B cells analyzed. The present findings should therefore be interpreted within the scope of HA-focused B-cell and antibody responses and do not represent the complete vaccine-induced immune response. Third, the assessment of HA binding and antigen specificity had several constraints. We did not include an irrelevant antigen produced in the same mammalian expression system or a cord-blood comparator. Fully orthogonal negative controls were not available. To reduce nonspecific binding, we used FMO-based thresholds, required dual-color concordance, and applied a sample-internal streptavidin Qdot 705 background correction. Nevertheless, very low-affinity cross-reactivity cannot be excluded. The mixed-HA probe precluded per-strain frequency estimates and may have led to overestimation of low-affinity cross-binding. HA binding by these monoclonal antibodies alone should not be interpreted as evidence of protection. In addition, CD27−IgD+ expression alone did not confirm that these cells were naive or newly recruited, and antigen-experienced cells may also have been included. Future work in independent cohorts using single-HA probes and additional orthogonal controls will be needed to refine specificity estimates and address these constraints. Fourth, the functional and clonal analyses were limited. Only two expanded clonotypes were identified in the high-titer group, which limited the number of clonally expanded mAbs available for functional characterization. The expressed mAbs represented an exploratory panel rather than a quantitative sample of the HA-binding BCR repertoire. The findings on antibody breadth should therefore be interpreted within the tested mAb panel. Competition ELISA was further restricted to a subset of mAbs, and no direct epitope-mapping experiments were conducted. Therefore, group-level differences in HA epitope targeting could not be determined. Lineages detected only after vaccination may have arisen from memory-derived clones that were not detected at baseline, rather than exclusively from newly recruited naive B cells (Lee et al., 2016, 2019). Finally, without tissue samples, we could not determine whether peripheral blood changes mirrored those in secondary lymphoid organs. Prior work suggests that only three of eight vaccinated individuals mount effective germinal center responses (Turner et al., 2020), and the extent to which our peripheral readouts reflect lymphoid-tissue dynamics remains uncertain.

Conclusions

In conclusion, pre-existing neutralizing antibody levels were associated with differences in HA+ B-cell repertoire maturation and antibody breadth after influenza vaccination. Although these exploratory findings require confirmation in larger independent cohorts and do not establish the origin of the responding B cells, they highlight pre-existing antibody levels as a potential factor associated with the quality of subsequent B-cell responses.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Twenty-one healthy adults aged 18–55 years were enrolled in this study. Participants were eligible if they had no known chronic medical conditions or immunodeficiency and were not receiving immunosuppressive therapy. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, a history of allergy to influenza vaccines, and a recent cold or other acute respiratory tract infection. Prior influenza infection or vaccination history and baseline antibody titers were not used as eligibility criteria. Baseline neutralizing antibody titers were unknown at enrollment, and participants were not selected according to antibody titer. The high-, medium-, and low-titer groups were defined after the Day 0 neutralization measurements. All participants received the 2021–2022 Northern Hemisphere seasonal quadrivalent inactivated influenza vaccine. Peripheral blood samples (20 mL) were collected at three time points: before vaccination (Day 0), and on Days 14 and 28 post-vaccination. Plasma and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were separated through density gradient centrifugation.

Vaccine

The 2021–2022 Northern Hemisphere seasonal quadrivalent inactivated influenza vaccine was obtained from Jiangsu GDK Biotechnology Co., Ltd. It contained the following components: A/Victoria/2570/2019 (H1N1) pdm09-like virus, A/Cambodia/e0826360/2020 (H3N2)-like virus, B/Washington/02/2019 (B/Victoria lineage)-like virus, and B/Phuket/3073/2013 (B/Yamagata lineage)-like virus.

Cell lines

Expi293F cells, kindly provided by Tengchuan Jin, were cultured in Union-293 medium (Union Biotech) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. 293T and MDCK cells, sourced from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures, were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) under the same conditions. Suspension MDCK cells were adapted to serum-free medium. A 293T-ST6Gal1 cell line was constructed in our lab by synthesizing a codon-optimized ST6Gal1 gene and cloning it into a pHBLV plasmid. Lentiviral packaging was performed using psPAX2 and VSVG plasmids to infect 293T cells.

Virus

Vaccine virus strains were obtained from the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Influenza A virus strains were expanded in suspension MDCK cells cultured at 3 × 106 cells/mL in 6 mL of serum-free medium. Cells were infected with 200 μL of virus (8 HA units) and 10 μg/mL TPCK-treated trypsin, and then incubated for 1 h at 35 °C with 5% CO2 on a shaker at 220 rpm. Then 4 mL of buffer B (Wuhu Interferon) was added to the culture, which was then incubated for an additional 72 h. Influenza B virus strains were expanded in adherent MDCK cells following WHO standard procedures. The viral supernatants were collected by centrifugation, the virus identity was confirmed by hemagglutination assay, and the supernatants were stored at −80 °C.

HA antigen production

HA sequences codon-optimized for mammalian expression were synthesized, with transmembrane and intracellular regions replaced by a GS linker, a leucine zipper trimerization domain, and a His tag. These were cloned into pTT5 expression vectors as described previously (Margine et al., 2013). Endotoxin-free plasmids were transfected into Expi293F cells using PEI. After 5 days, supernatants were collected, and HA proteins were purified by Ni-NTA affinity chromatography. Purified proteins were concentrated with 50 kDa MWCO Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters (Millipore) and buffer-exchanged with PBS three times. Biotinylated proteins were prepared for flow cytometry staining. The concentration of purified HA protein was adjusted to 1 mg/mL, and biotin (Biomatrik) was mixed with HA protein at a molar ratio of 20:1 and incubated at 4 °C for 18 h. Excess biotin was removed using 3.5 kDa MWCO dialysis membranes.

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting and flow cytometry

For antigen-binding B cell sorting, cryopreserved PBMCs were filtered using a 40 μm cell strainer and washed twice with FACS buffer (1 × PBS supplemented with 2% FBS and 1 mM EDTA). B cells were enriched from PBMCs using the Human CD19 Positive Selection Kit II (STEMCELL Technologies) according to the provided instructions. The enriched B cells were then resuspended in 100 μL of FACS buffer. Four biotinylated vaccine strain HAs were each mixed with either PE-streptavidin or APC-streptavidin (BD Biosciences) at a molar ratio of 4:1 (HA:streptavidin) and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min. After incubation, cells were washed twice and resuspended in 400 μL of FACS buffer. For viability staining, cells were incubated with 7-AAD in the dark for 5 min. HA+ B cells were sorted using a BD FACSAria III instrument, and sorted cells were collected into 30% FBS in PBS.

For analysis of B cell population, cryopreserved PBMCs were washed twice and resuspended in 100 μL of FACS buffer. Cells were stained with IgG-FITC (BD Biosciences, clone G18-145, 2 μL), IgM-Alexa Fluor 700 (BioLegend, clone MHM-88, 1 μL), CD19-PE-Texas Red (BD Biosciences, clone HIB19, 1 μL), CD38-PE-Cy5 (BioLegend, clone HIT2, 1 μL), CD20-PE-Cy7 (BioLegend, clone 2H7, 1 μL), CD45-APC-Cy7 (BioLegend, clone HI30, 1 μL), IgD-AmCyan (BD Biosciences, clone IA6-2, 1 μL), CD27-Qdot 605 (BD Biosciences, clone L128, 1 μL), CD71-Qdot 655 (BioLegend, clone CY1G4, 1 μL), CD3-Qdot 800 (BD Biosciences, clone SK7, 1 μL) as well as the previously described APC- and PE-labeled HA proteins. Staining was performed at 4 °C for 30 min, followed by washing with FACS buffer twice. DAPI was added to exclude non-viable cells. Data acquisition was carried out with a BD FACSAria III instrument, and analysis was performed using FlowJo v.10. To control for nonspecific streptavidin-mediated signals, we incorporated a streptavidin-Qdot705 (no-antigen) channel in every sample to quantify probe-only background. Positivity thresholds for each fluorescent channel were set using FMO controls acquired in parallel. HA+ events were stringently defined as dual-positive for both PE-HA and APC-HA above their respective FMO-derived gates, thereby requiring concordant binding across two independently labeled probe sets.

ELISA

96-well ELISA plates were coated with 100 ng per well of HA protein and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Plates were blocked overnight at 4 °C with 5% BSA in PBS and then washed three times with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBST). Heat-inactivated plasma (starting at 1:100 dilution) and purified monoclonal antibodies (starting at 100 μg/mL) were serially diluted 10-fold in PBS, and 100 μL of each sample was added to the wells. 100 μL of PBS was added to the negative control wells. After 2 h of incubation at 37 °C, plates were washed and incubated with 100 μL of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (1:250 dilution in PBS) for 1 h at 37 °C. Plates were washed, and 100 μL of TMB substrate was added. After a 15 min incubation at room temperature, the reaction was stopped with 50 μL of stop solution (sulfuric acid free), and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. The area under the curve (for plasma) and half-maximal binding concentrations (for monoclonal antibodies) were calculated using GraphPad Prism.

For competition ELISA, the reference mAbs were biotinylated using the same method described above for HA proteins, and free biotin was removed using 3.5 kDa MWCO dialysis membranes. Binding of each biotinylated reference mAb to the corresponding HA antigen was confirmed by ELISA before use. HA coating and blocking were performed as described above. After blocking, unlabeled competitor mAbs were added at 20 μg/mL and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Plates were washed and incubated with the corresponding biotinylated reference mAb for 2 h at 37 °C. The working concentration of each biotinylated reference mAb was determined by preliminary titration to yield an OD450 of approximately 1.0–1.5 in wells without competitor mAb. After washing, HRP-conjugated streptavidin diluted 1:2000 in PBS was added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Plates were then washed, and TMB substrate was added. The reaction was stopped, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm as described above. Wells without competitor mAb were used as no-competitor controls, and wells containing the corresponding unlabeled reference mAb were used as self-blocking controls. After background subtraction, competition value was calculated as [1 − (OD sample/OD no-competitor control)] × 100%. Competition values of <30%, 30%–70%, and >70% were defined as no competition, partial competition, and strong competition, respectively (Bangaru et al., 2019; Guthmiller et al., 2022).

Monoclonal antibody expression and purification

Heavy- and light-chain variable-region sequences codon-optimized for mammalian expression were synthesized and cloned into IgG1 heavy-chain and Igκ (or Igλ) light-chain expression vectors containing the constant regions. Expi293F cells were co-transfected with heavy- and light-chain expressing plasmids (1:1 ratio) and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 5 days. Supernatants were collected, and antibodies were purified using a Protein A agarose column on ÄKTA Pure (GE Healthcare). The samples were eluted with 0.1 M acetic acid (pH 3.0–4.5) and immediately neutralized with 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0). Antibodies were concentrated using 50 kDa MWCO Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters and buffer-exchanged with PBS three times.

Pseudovirus neutralization

The mammalian codon-optimized HA and NA sequences were cloned into pcDNA3.1(+). These plasmids were co-transfected into 293T cells together with the pNL4-3.Luc.R−E- and pCAGGS-TMPRSS2 plasmids. Pseudovirus-containing supernatant was harvested after 48 or 72 h (Wang et al., 2008). 293T-ST6Gal1 cells were seeded at 5 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plates and infected with pseudovirus. After 48 h, Britelite Plus reagent (PerkinElmer) was added, and relative luminescence units (RLU) were measured using a PerkinElmer Envision instrument.

For neutralization, antibodies or plasma were serially diluted and incubated with 100 TCID50 pseudovirus for 1 h before adding to 293T-ST6Gal1 cells (Ding et al., 2021). The positive control consisted of pseudovirus, while the negative control consisted of culture medium only. Inhibition rate = (RLUpositive control – RLUsample)/(RLUpositive control – RLUnegative control) × 100%. Nonlinear fitting and IC50 calculations were performed using GraphPad Prism.

Next-generation sequencing

Total RNA extraction and library preparation

Total RNA was extracted from PBMCs using Trizol. Reverse transcription was performed with 5′-RACE, followed by PCR amplification with immunoglobulin heavy-chain constant-region primers.

The PCR products were purified with DNA magnetic beads and processed using the End Prep Enzyme Mix to include 5′-terminal phosphorylation and 3′-terminal A-tailing. Sequencing adapters were ligated to both ends, and the DNA was purified using DNA Clean Beads before amplification with P5 and P7 primers. The amplified products were cleaned with beads, quality-checked using the Qsep100 (Bioptic), and quantified using a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiSeq platform using a 2 × 300 bp paired-end configuration, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Data processing

Raw image data were processed with Bcl2Fastq (v2.20.0.422) for base calling. Pass-filtered reads were further processed with Cutadapt (v1.9.1) to remove adapter sequences, low-quality bases (Q < 20) at the 5′ and 3′ ends, and reads shorter than 200 bp. FastQC (v0.11.5) was used to validate quality. Paired-end reads were merged using FLASH (v2.2.00), and sequences were aligned to the IMGT reference database to identify V(D)J genes and CDR regions. MiXCR (v3.0.13) was employed for two-step alignment and clone clustering. Invalid sequences were removed after the first BLAST search, including unmatched sequences and sequences containing stop codons. The sequences were searched against the database again using BLAST and clustered into clones based on similarity. The abundance and V(D)J genes of each clone were obtained. Clonal lineages were inferred across time points by clustering CDR3 nucleotide sequences (≥ 75% identity) with identical IGHV and IGHJ genes and CDRH3 length.

Phylogenetic tree construction

Phylogenetic trees were constructed based on full-length heavy chain variable regions, and nucleic acid sequences with the same V(D)J germline were selected across multiple time points, irrespective of allele. Sequence alignments were performed using MAFFT. Phylogenetic trees were subsequently built with IQ-TREE (maximum-likelihood method) using default parameters. Visualizations were created with ChiPlot online.

Single-cell V(D)J (scVDJ-seq) and single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq)

Sample preparation and GEM generation

Cell suspensions were analyzed for size, concentration, and viability using the TC20 Automated Cell Counter (Bio-Rad). Barcoded single-cell 5′ Gel Beads were mixed with cells, enzymes, and partitioning oil on a Chromium instrument to form Gel Beads-in-emulsion (GEMs). Cells were lysed to release oligonucleotides, which were then mixed with the cell lysate and a Master Mix containing RT reagents and poly(dT) primers. Following incubation of the GEMs, 10 × barcoded full-length cDNA was generated. First-strand cDNA was purified using silane magnetic beads and amplified by PCR. BCR-specific primers were used for nested amplification. The amplified products were quantified and qualified using a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the 10 × Genomics Chromium Single Cell V(D)J Reagent Kit with v1 chemistry and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform using a 2× 150 bp paired-end configuration.

Single-cell V(D)J data processing and analysis

Raw sequencing data were processed with Bcl2Fastq (v2.20.0.422). Pass-filtered data were obtained by preliminary quality analysis using Illumina’s built-in software. Cell Ranger’s pipelines (V7.1.0) were used to analyze pass-filtered data. Vdj_GRCh38_alts_ensembl-7.1.0 was used as the V(D)J Reference. Clonotypes were defined as cells sharing a common unmutated ancestor. Clonotype enrichment was calculated based on cell counts. Enclone was used for B cell clonotype grouping and germline sequence derivation. Heavy-chain sequences from the pooled single-cell V(D)J dataset were matched to the donor-resolved NGS data based on an identical CDRH3 amino acid sequence together with the same IGHV and IGHJ gene usage. Sequences without a donor match were excluded from individual- and group-level analyses.

Single-cell RNA-seq data processing and analysis

Raw sequencing reads were processed using Cutadapt (v1.9.1) to remove adapters, trim low-quality bases (quality < 20 or containing ‘N’ bases) at sequence termini, and exclude reads shorter than 50 bp after trimming. Filtered reads were aligned to the GRCh38-2020-A genome via 10 × Genomics Cell Ranger (v7.1.0) with default parameters, generating UMI-corrected feature-barcode matrices. Subsequent single-cell analysis was performed in Seurat (v4.3.0) with rigorous quality control: cells expressing fewer than 200 or more than 2500 genes, or exhibiting > 5% mitochondrial gene content were excluded, along with genes detected in fewer than 3 cells. After quality control, a total of 12,301 cells were obtained. The top 2000 highly variable genes were identified. The top 15 principal components were selected for subsequent cluster analysis, and these cells were categorized into 11 clusters. SingleR was used to perform B cell subpopulation annotations, including exhausted B cells, naive B cells, non-switched and switched memory B cells. Clusters of cells were visualized using UMAP.

Statistics

No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed because this was an exploratory study without a prespecified primary endpoint or expected effect size. Data are shown as mean ± SEM, median with interquartile range, or individual values, as indicated in the corresponding figure legends. All analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism version 9 unless otherwise noted in the figure legends. Sample sizes (n), number of replicates, and statistical significance tests are indicated in the figures or legends. Correlations were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient for data sampled from Gaussian distributions, and Spearman’s rank correlation for nonparametric data. Statistical significance was defined as ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.
