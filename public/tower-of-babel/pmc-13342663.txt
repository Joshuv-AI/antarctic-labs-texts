Intelligent optimization of micro-electrical discharge machining of aluminum nitride ceramics using nano abrasive zirconium oxide-enhanced biodegradable dielectric
Authors: Manikandan Ravi, Gopalakrishnan Thangavel, Manimegalai Raja, T G Sakthivel, Nagaraj Ashok, Abhijit Bhowmik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores the use of nano-enhanced Canola Oil Methyl Ester (COME) formulated with 0.5 wt% of zirconium oxide (ZrO2) nanoparticles as a biodegradable dielectric fluid in sustainable micro-electrical discharge machining (micro-EDM) of aluminum nitride (AlN) ceramics. The Box-Behnken design was employed to test the voltage (100–150 V), capacitance (10–20 nF) and electrode geometry (O-shaped, D-shaped) using Response Surface Methodology (RSM) and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) to optimize several objectives simultaneously. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) has identified dielectric type the most significant contributor to the variation in Material Removal Rate (MRR), Relative Wear Rate (RWR), and Surface Roughness (SR), with a contribution of 33.0%, 37.4% and 45.2% correspondingly (p < 0.0001). Optimal conditions at 125.4 V and 15.2 nF with O-COME (+) denoting an O-shaped tool electrode operating at positive polarity with COME dielectric achieved a predicted MRR of 26.89 mm³/min and RWR of 0.43 and SR of 4.87 μm, demonstrating significantly better material removal and wear behavior compared to the conventional electrode metalworking fluid (EMWF). The ANN model was found to be highly predictive (R2 = 0.96, mean squared error (MSE) = 0.002) with an average prediction error of less than 3.5%. Nonetheless, the experimental variability (CV = 19.9–53.6%) was considerable among conditions, which implies that better control of the processes and higher sample size should be implemented before complete implementation in industries. CFD confirmed the superior dielectric flow behavior of the COME variants, and thus, the important formation of conductive layers needed to machine non-conductive AlN. There is a slight reduction in surface quality compared to EMWF. However, nano-enhanced COME shows strong potential for sustainable precision manufacturing. This work improves precision machining processes and replaces petroleum-based dielectric fluids with biodegradable vegetable oil-based alternatives. Introduction

Electric Discharge Machining (EDM) has evolved as a critical unconventional manufacturing process, with extensive research focusing on fundamentals, powder-mixed variants, and optimization strategies. Nevertheless, standard dielectric fluids such as kerosene and deionized water, kerosene and deionized water, are efficient at machining, but are highly hazardous to the environment and human health because of their non-biodegradable property and release of volatile organic compounds1. With increasing regulatory and industrial pressure toward sustainable production, biodegradable substitutes to traditional dielectric fluids have become the subject of significant research. Micro-EDM is a non-contact machining process that has a very high level of precision and that removes material through controlled electrical discharges and is commonly applied in making sub-millimetre features in hard and brittle materials in the aerospace, electronics, and biomedical applications. ANN-based parameter optimization enhances process efficiency by enabling prediction of the process because it allows predicting the important parameters of the performance, including MRR, electrode wear, and the quality of the surface at different discharge conditions, eliminating the need to conduct extensive experiments by trial and error. Therefore, the micro-EDM technology combined with ANN-based optimization can be an avenue to sustainable and efficient precision manufacturing.

The implementation of COME as a biodegradable dielectric medium represents a significant advancement in sustainable EDM processes, particularly for machining AlN materials valued for their thermal conductivity and mechanical strength2,3. COME has been tested and proven to have several properties that are beneficial to EDM like high flash and fire points (136 °C and 350 °C, respectively), enhanced lubrication properties and thermal stability similar to those of conventional hydrocarbon-based fluids, at a significantly lower environmental harmfulness4. These properties render COME especially appropriate to machining AlN ceramics, which are thermally conductive (170–220 W/mK) and highly insulated electrically, so that they find numerous applications in power electronics and aerospace thermal management. The dielectric performance of COME is further improved by the addition of 0.5 wt% nano-ZrO₂, which improves the discharge stability in the inter-electrode gap and the formation of a conductive surface layer necessary to machine non-conductive AlN5,6. Besides, past investigations involving D-shaped and O-shaped WC-Co electrodes and tool electrode polarity (positive and negative) with respect to the COME dielectric have indicated that the geometry of electrode is a key factor in the control over dielectric flow behaviour, which directly influences the rate of material removal and electrode wear during comparable discharge energy operation7.

Farooq et al. (2024) conducted the experimental investigations on reducing micro-machining errors during electric discharge machining of titanium alloy using non-ionic liquids, demonstrating that non-ionic liquid-mixed dielectrics showed noticeable improvement in terms of achieving low machining errors as compared to the conventional dielectric system8. Their work revealed that cryogenically treated graphite electrode with pulse ON: OFF time of 50:50 µs and non-ionic liquid S-60 with 25 g/L concentration achieved minimum micro-machining errors with the highest desirability index of 98.60%. Yadav et al. conducted a parametric study on Ti6Al4V ELI using SiC powder-mixed EDM with four tool materials The results showed that the best balance between surface quality and machining efficiency was achieved using an aluminium electrode. The optimal parameters included a pulse current of 14 A, pulse on time of 75 µs, pulse off time of 75 µs, and negative polarity.

In another significant study on die repair and maintenance applications, Ishfaq et al. (2021) explored geometrical machining errors using three electrode materials (copper, graphite, and tungsten carbide), finding that copper electrode reduced axial errors by 80.9% and 47.6% compared to graphite and tungsten carbide respectively9. Ehsan et al. (2022) investigated wire electric discharge machining (WEDM) of M42 high-speed steel through controlled flushing characteristics, revealing that nozzle diameter exerted the most substantial influence on machining performance (76.85% contribution), while other parameters contributed 14.21–34.50% to MRR optimization10. Hannan et al. (2024) provided extensive sustainability analysis of EDM for SS310 alloy, examining trade-offs between copper and brass electrodes across machining performance, economic factors, and environmental impact11. While copper electrodes demonstrated superior MRR (2.67 mm³/min) and surface quality (3.36 μm Ra), brass electrodes achieved higher composite desirability (56.21%) when considering holistic sustainability frameworks, establishing new paradigms for precision machining in aerospace, biomedical, and automotive applications.

Abhishek et al. (2022) successfully developed micro holes in Al/SiC composites using RSM and Multi-objective Grasshopper Optimization Algorithm (MOGOA), achieving optimal conditions with pulse on-time showing maximum influence on MRR and tool wear rate (TWR) with F-values of 134.68 and 62.28 respectively12. The hybrid RSM-MOGOA approach identified optimal parameters yielding MRR of 0.028 gm/min and TWR of 0.173 gm/min with excellent reproducibility (± 4% error), while morphological investigations confirmed significant reduction in burr formation and machining irregularities under optimized conditions. This research establishes electric discharge machining as a viable alternative for precision manufacturing of composite materials, providing a foundation for multi-objective optimization in unconventional machining processes where traditional cutting methods prove inadequate for ceramic-reinforced materials.

Ali et al. (2024) demonstrated significant advancements in hybrid aluminum matrix composite machining through genetic algorithm-integrated neural network optimization, achieving notable improvements in MRR across different geometric profiles (plane, angular, and curve) while optimizing wire wear ratios through systematic parameter control including pulse voltage, current, wire feed rate, and drum speed13. The integration of artificial intelligence with EDM processes has been further demonstrated by Sana et al., (2024) who developed sustainability-targeted optimization frameworks using alumina-mixed deionized water as dielectric, achieving substantial improvements in MRR (64.82%), SR reduction (27.45%), and energy consumption optimization (46.60%) through multi-objective genetic algorithms, while simultaneously addressing environmental concerns by replacing conventional kerosene-based dielectrics14. Complementing these optimization studies, Asad et al. (2025) investigated micro-impression machining in Al6061 using alumina-mixed deionized water. The study provided important insights into electrode wear mechanisms and improvements in accuracy index using RSM. The optimal conditions were identified as IP: 5 A, SV: 2 V, ST: 3 µs, and CP: 1.5 g/100 ml. Under these conditions, the electrode wear rate improved by 50.85%, and the accuracy index increased by 2.67%15. These investigations demonstrate the evolution of EDM from conventional machining to intelligent and sustainable manufacturing processes. Powder-mixed EDM (PMEDM) has emerged as a promising approach that improves process stability and enhances surface quality. It also enables precise machining of difficult-to-machine materials through the controlled addition of conductive and non-conductive particles in dielectric fluids. In addition, advanced optimization techniques such as neural networks, genetic algorithms (GA), and RSM provide reliable frameworks for parameter selection and performance prediction across different material–dielectric combinations.

Sana et al. (2024) developed an artificial intelligence methodology to study the effects of cryogenic electrode treatment, nano-powder, and surfactant-mixed dielectrics on the EDM performance of Inconel 617. The study showed that AI-based approaches can effectively optimize electrode wear rate and overcut16. Significant improvements of 47.05% in electrode wear rate and 85.00% in overcut were achieved through parametric optimization. The work by Hurairah et al. (2024) focuses on improving dimensional accuracy in EDM of stainless steel 316. The study investigated three electrode materials: copper, brass, and aluminum. Graphene was used as a dielectric additive to reduce radial overcut. The results showed that copper electrodes provided the best performance, achieving 70.51% better dimensional accuracy compared to the highest overcut values. The study also used ANN modeling with R² values above 0.99. In addition, a genetic algorithm-based multi-objective optimization approach was applied, resulting in improvements of 40.21% and 34.37% for brass and aluminium electrodes, respectively17.

Building upon the established foundation of EDM research utilizing artificial intelligence frameworks for process optimization and sustainable dielectric formulations, this study addresses critical gaps in micro-EDM applications for precision manufacturing. The integration of 0.5wt% nano-zirconium oxide (ZrO₂) into COME builds on previous research indicating nano-additives’ potential to enhance dielectric properties and stabilize electrical discharge, while addressing the limited understanding of nano-enhanced biodegradable dielectrics in micro-EDM processes18. A significant research gap exists in comprehensively evaluating the combined effects of electrode geometry, nano-enhanced dielectric fluids, and process parameters on machining performance, particularly for non-conductive materials like AlN.

The combined use of RSM and ANN was chosen to effectively model the micro-EDM process involving nano-enhanced biodegradable dielectrics. RSM helps in understanding the influence of process parameters and their interactions in a structured way, while ANN improves prediction accuracy by capturing nonlinear relationships in the data. Therefore, using both methods together provides reliable modelling and better understanding of the machining process. RSM was used as a main design and analysis tool because it can build empirical relationships, quantify the interaction of parameters and give statistically interpretable models. Compared to Central Composite Design (CCD), the Box-Behnken Design (BBD) was chosen among the RSM designs due to several reasons of significant importance19. BBD avoids experimental runs at extreme combinations of factors. This is important for micro-EDM of non-conductive ceramics, as extreme discharge conditions can lead to unstable arcing, electrode damage, and cracking of the workpiece. BBD requires fewer experimental runs than CCD for the same number of factors. This improves experimental efficiency while still providing sufficient accuracy for quadratic modelling. Furthermore, BBD fits well into the limited experimental spaces, particularly in sensitive dielectric systems, nanoparticle suspensions where very high concentrations can lead to either agglomeration or instability. Equivalent arguments regarding the choice of statistically effective experimental designs in manufacturing procedures have been documented in the recent studies20. ANN model was added to complement RSM to represent relationships and complex interactions with high nonlinearity which cannot easily be represented with the help of the polynomial regression model. RSM provides interpretable mathematical models with statistical significance, while ANN delivers higher prediction accuracy and better generalization. The integration of statistical techniques with machine learning approaches has been extensively used in advanced manufacturing optimisation and offers improved reliability compared to standalone methods21,22.

Regarding the selection of optimization technique, Teaching–Learning-Based Optimization (TLBO) was chosen over other metaheuristic approaches such as Genetic Algorithm (GA), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), Ant Colony Optimization (ACO), and Fuzzy Logic for the following reasons. TLBO is a parameter-free algorithm, eliminating the need for algorithm-specific tuning (such as mutation rate in GA or inertia weight in PSO), which reduces computational complexity and improves reproducibility23. Additionally, TLBO has been shown to provide faster convergence and stable global optimum solutions in multi-parameter manufacturing optimization problems. Conversely, GA and PSO need sensitive parameter tuning and can be susceptible to premature convergence or local minima. Although the ideas of fuzzy logic are useful in dealing with uncertainty, the method demands rule set definitions by experts, which is subjective and restrictive in terms of scaling. More recent literature has also discussed these comparative advantages of hybrid optimization and machine learning in manufacturing24,25.

Electrical discharge machining (EDM) has seen significant improvements in recent years with the development of advanced dielectric fluids and intelligent modelling techniques; however, several important research gaps still remain. Recent studies have used alumina-mixed deionized water as a machining medium for Al6061, but these works mainly focus on conductive metallic systems15,26. Limited research is available on the use of biodegradable oil-based dielectrics for machining electrically non-conductive ceramic materials. Similarly, nano-graphene-based bio-dielectrics have been investigated for metallic workpieces, while non-conductive ceramics such as AlN, which require conductive surface modification for stable discharge initiation, have not been adequately explored9. In addition, existing sustainability studies predominantly focus on electrode material performance, with limited attention to the energy efficiency associated with dielectric selection. Therefore, there is a lack of integrated research combining electrode geometry, nano-enhanced biodegradable dielectric behaviour, and discharge energy efficiency within a unified RSM–ANN–CFD framework for micro-EDM of non-conductive ceramics11.

The current research is a systematic and integrated study that seals these gaps. It explores, first, the efficiency of nano-ZrO2-functionalized COME as a biodegradable dielectric fluid to micro-EDM AlN, thus expanding the use of sustainable dielectrics to non-conductive ceramic systems. Second, CFD is integrated with an experimental study to determine the features of dielectric flow and energy transfer mechanisms, which quantitatively give insight into discharge efficiency. Third, an ANN model is created to forecast machining performance by incorporating the effects of electrode geometry with nano-enhanced dielectric characteristics with high predictive accuracy (R2 = 0.96) and low error rates. Therefore, a sustainability-oriented performance assessment is conducted, demonstrating substantial improvement in machining efficiency compared to conventional dielectric fluids while maintaining environmental compatibility. Based on these considerations, this study has three main objectives. First, to evaluate the effectiveness of nano-ZrO₂-enhanced COME as a sustainable dielectric fluid in micro-EDM of AlN. Second, to optimize machining parameters to achieve higher material removal rate (MRR) and lower tool wear rate (TWR) using different electrode geometries. Third, to develop and validate an ANN-based predictive model supported by CFD simulations for efficient and reliable process optimization.

Although the results are encouraging, there are some shortcomings of the current research that must be mentioned. The range of process parameters, nano-ZrO2 concentration (0.5 wt%), and the choice of electrode geometries are restricted to this specific range and could change in different machining conditions or material conditions. Moreover, nano-dielectric long-term stability and scale-up viability in terms of industrial usage were not investigated in this study. However, this research study has a practical implication on shop-floor engineers who are working on micro-EDM of non-conductive ceramics like AlN. The optimized parameter space, tested ANN predictive model, and understanding of dielectric behavior may help to choose stable machining conditions, decrease the number of trial-and-error work, and enhance machining efficiency. The biodegradable nano-enhanced COME also offers a greener alternative version of traditional dielectric fluids and hence the proposed method can be used in sustainable manufacturing processes in precision electronics and high-technology ceramic machining industries. This work has a non-technical benefit as it facilitates sustainable manufacturing by improving precision machining of hard non-conductive ceramics in power electronics and clean energy uses. It shows how a biodegradable, non-toxic canola oil methyl ester dielectric is used to replace traditional petroleum-based EDM fluids, which decrease the amount of hazardous industrial waste. It also reduces the emission of volatile organic compounds and enhances safety since it eliminates exposure that comes with using kerosene-based dielectrics during conventional EDM operations. The suggested framework can be extrapolated to other advanced machining of ceramics other than AlN.

Materials and methods

Preparation of the metalworking fluid

COME was made when refined canola oil underwent transesterification reaction with methanol (CH3OH) and potassium hydroxide (KOH) as catalysts. During the procedure, the catalyst solution was prepared by dissolving 5 g of KOH in 250 ml of methanol and was further mixed with canola oil and stirred at the rate of 1500 rpm at 65 °C during 90 min. The glycerin by-product was drained to the lower phase, and left crude COME in the upper phase. The catalyst was washed with distilled water several times to remove residual catalyst, and the ester was again heated at 110O C in 20 min in order to get rid of residual moisture. The researcher measured the kinematic viscosity and thermal conductivity of the purified COME and compared the findings against EMWF (Table 1), to ensure dielectric suitability. Nano-ZrO2 (particle size 40 nm, purity 99.5 wt) was added to COME in the amount of 0.5 wt% (4.41 g/L) to act as a dielectric nano-additive to increase thermal conductivity of the workpiece, decrease surface tension, and promote the formation of stable conductive layers on the surface of AlN workpiece during micro-EDM.

Nano-ZrO₂ synthesis methods and preparation protocol

Sol–gel method is one of the few methods of synthesizing nanoparticles with a high level of control over particle size and crystallinity due to the control of pH and calcination, generally resulting in tetragonal ZrO2 nanoparticles of 10–20 nm. Large-scale production is predominantly done by the co-precipitation technique as it is simple and cost-effective and produces particles of sizes between 15 and 50 nm with tunable phases. Solution combustion offers a fast and energy-saving method of producing nanoparticles whereas hydrothermal synthesis offers high crystalline with narrow size distribution nanoparticles under controlled temperature and pressure. Microwave- and ultrasonic-assisted techniques also increase kinetics of nucleation and uniformity of particles. But it is a standard practice in unconventional machining equipment like powder-mixed EDM and micro-EDM to purchase commercially each nanoparticle of certified purity and size and subsequently to control its dispersion in the dielectric medium28. This method provides reproducibility and consistency between experiments. Nanoparticles (ZrO₂, Al₂O₃, TiO₂) are usually dispersed by ultrasonic homogenization to avoid agglomeration and create stable suspensions, and stability is checked by sedimentation, viscosity and zeta potential. The nano-ZrO2 particles (40 nm, ≥ 99.5% purity) in the current research were purchased as a certified sample and dissolved in COME at a concentration of 0.5 wt% through the use of ultrasonic homogenization (40 kHz, 30 min) to a nanofluid. Before conducting the experiments, the particle characteristics were verified using XRD and TEM. This approach follows standard practice in nano-powder mixed dielectric fluid (NPMDF) preparation for EDM, where emphasis is placed on proper dispersion and characterization rather than in-house nanoparticle synthesis.

COME variants and relationship to powder-mixed EDM

Three dielectric fluids were used in this study, two of which were COME-based fluids, and the remaining one was a conventional fluid. COME is non-modified Canola Oil Methyl Ester that is processed without any nano-additives. It was used as a reference to understand the behaviour of the base biodegradable dielectric. The second dielectric is nano abrasive-COME, which is made by incorporating 0.5 wt% ZrO2 nanoparticles (> 99.5% purity, − 40 nm). Ultrasonic homogenisation was used to prepare the suspension at a frequency of 40 kHz over a 30-minute period to make the suspension homogeneous. By enhancing inter-electrode conductivity and facilitating the development of conductive layers on non-conductive AlN, the addition of nanoparticles leads to a higher stability of discharges and to a better thermal behaviour. In all the big experimental conditions, this dielectric was used. The third dielectric is EMWF, a traditional hydrocarbon-based fluid that is employed in comparison. EMWF exhibits a low fire point, flash point and thermal conductivity than COME, which underscores the safety and environmental superiority of the biodegradable COME-based dielectric.

Conventional powder-mixed EDM (PMEDM): in traditional EDM, powders of a conductive or semi-conductive metal are placed into the dielectric to enhance discharge characteristics by increasing the discharge gap and enhancing the stability of the spark. The nano abrasive-COME used in this study operates on the same principle, but differs in two aspects by employing a biodegradable base fluid and being applied to the machining of a non-conductive workpiece (AlN). Here, nanoparticles help to create a conductive surface layer during preliminary discharges, which makes machining stable. Consequently, this process is sensitive to dielectric composition and polarity of the electrodes, which are taken into account in this work.

Experimental setup

All micro-EDM experiments were conducted on a Sarix SX-200 micro-EDM drilling machine, equipped with a servo-controlled z-axis positioning system, an RC-type discharge circuit module, and a recirculating dielectric fluid supply system. Figure 1 illustrates the experimental configuration, which comprises the z-axis input microcontroller, the RC discharge circuit module, and the dielectric fluid supply and filtration system. Figure 2 shows the complete experimental setup of the micro-EDM process used in this study, including the machine system and a close-up of the machining zone. The electrode’s z-axis input was calibrated to maintain a consistent voltage throughout the machining operation. In an EDM system, the z-axis input microcontroller and discharge circuit module are integral components that function together to regulate the machining process. While the former ensures precise electrode positioning, the latter governs the electrical discharges responsible for material removal. This synergy enables the EDM machine to operate with exceptional precision and efficiency, yielding the desired machining outcomes40. The discharge capacitance was instrumental in regulating the z-directional feed, a critical factor in maintaining the EDM process’s accuracy and precision. The system is designed to maintain the discharge separation within an optimal range, thereby ensuring consistent machining performance. The z-axis received a + 10 μm adjustment to maintain fixed discharges upon reaching the critical voltage, particularly when contact between the tool and workpiece was established to evaluate the effectiveness of nano abrasive-COME as a dielectric fluid. Mechanical agitation was employed to maintain the ZrO₂ nanoparticle suspension. Copper-coated ZrO₂ particles with a depth of 1 μm were deposited on the substrate via evaporation. These particles were further coated with copper, extending their diameter to 20 μm through electroplating in a 0.7 M copper sulfate solution at a voltage of 15 kV for 5 min. The copper coating enhances the conductivity of the ZrO₂ particles, which plays a crucial role in the EDM process by facilitating efficient energy transfer during machining. Spherical tungsten carbide (WC-Co) wires were used to fabricate the electrodes. These specific electrode shapes were chosen on the basis of their distinct flow characteristics and material removal efficiencies, which are explored in subsequent sections of this study. The difference between discharge in micro-EDM cannot be directly measured during machining since it is within the dielectric when the machine is under sparking. Thus, a nominal distance of 10 μm was created and checked in three ways. A high-resolution (0.1 μm resolution) optical encoder-controlled z-axis (resistance in electrical contacts, ± 0.5 μm) was used to step the tool to the reference position by moving the tool at a speed of 0.1 μm/s until electrical contact (resistance < 10 Ω) occurred, and then moved the tool back by 10 μm; the system The gap was also indirectly monitored with the mean inter-electrode voltage which was calibrated at 68 ± 5 V under standard conditions (150 V, 10 nF). Moreover, overcut (5000×) and profilometry measured post-process SEM measurements (ranged between 8.5 μm and 13.2 μm with an average of 10.4 ± 1.2 μm) were performed to ensure that the target discharge gap was constant.

AlN ceramics are electrically non-conductive (bulk resistivity is greater than 10¹² Ω·cm), and thus do not allow direct spark discharge initiation on the bare workpiece surface. A two-step process of workpiece preparation was used to allow micro-EDM. The initial phase involved placing the auxiliary copper electrode (diameter 20 μm) deposited through electroplating into light contact with the smooth AlN surface at low open-circuit voltage (50 V) and minimal capacitance (5 nF). The outer diameter of the WC-Co tool electrode was 100 μm in the case of the O-shaped and the D-shaped. Nominal drilling depth target was 300 μm in all experimental runs, which gave a hole aspect ratio of about 3:1. Considering the measured overcut of 10.4 ± 1.2 μm, the overall diameter of the drilled holes was between 108 μm and 114 μm across all experimental conditions. The holes drilled are thus cylindrical micro-bores, with nominal dimensions of about 112 μm diameter x 300 mm deep in the AlN workpiece. The low-energy discharges through the controlled of the auxiliary electrode deposited a thin conductive layer predominantly comprising of the pyrolytic carbon and ZrO2 particles of the nano abrasive COME dielectric on the AlN surface that lowered the surface electrical resistance sufficiently to sustain stable spark discharge initiation. During the second step, the conductive initiation layer was already formed (measured by following the discharge current waveform to reach a stable oscillation as shown in Sect. 3.2), the WC-Co tool electrode was then moved along the z-axis with the help of the servo-controlled positioning system to initiate actual micro-EDM drilling. The cavity depths formed during this conductive layer initiation stage, ranging from 50 μm to 350 μm depending on machining duration and applied voltage, constitute the pre-drilled pilot holes used in the CFD geometrical model described in Sect. 2.3. These initial cavity types are not mechanically drilled but they are products of the auxiliary electrode-aided micro-EDM initiation process. Each of the five CFD simulation cases simulates the dielectric fluid flow within these cavities at specified depths to evaluate how flow characteristics, and thus the efficiency of conductive layer formation, change as the hole depth increases during progressive micro-EDM drilling of AlN.

They examined four experimental configurations, denoted by a notation that coded the electrode shape, dielectric fluid and tool electrode polarity [Electrode Shape]-COME (Polarity). The prefix of shape is either O (O-shaped WC-Co electrode) or D (D-shaped WC-Co electrode), and COME denotes the nano abrasive-COME dielectric (COME + 0.5 wt% ZrO2). The polarity sign indicates the electrical connection of the tool electrode to the RC discharge circuit: positive (+) tool polarity, the tool is connected to the positive terminal, and the AlN workpiece to the negative terminal; negative (−) tool polarity, the tool is connected to the negative terminal, and the electrode wear decreases at the expense of lower MRR. These four are O-COME +, O-COME, D-COME and EMWF (conventional metalworking fluid base, tested in standard negative polarity). The concept of polarity is specifically important in AlN micro-EDM where the non-conductive workpiece must have an auxiliary conductive layer fed by an auxiliary electrode, and the direction of polarity dictates the effectiveness of the formation of the conductive layer, as explained in Sect. 3.1. The selection of 0.5 wt% nano zirconium oxide as an additive to COME was informed by its potential to enhance the thermal and electrical properties of the dielectric fluid, leading to improved machining performance. This specific concentration was selected after preliminary tests showed that it optimally balanced the viscosity and thermal expansion properties, which are crucial for achieving the desired MRR and SR.

Selection of input process parameters and nano-additive concentration

The parameters of input process, such as applied voltage, capacitance, electrode geometry and nano-ZrO2 concentration were chosen in two-step procedure that included preliminary screening experiments and critical literature review and the full numerical findings of all preliminary experiments are summarized in Supplementary File S1 (Tables S1-S3). This solution provided feasibility of the experiment and congruence to the practices of micro-EDM of non-conductive ceramics. Preliminary experiments were done with the nano-ZrO2 concentration of 0.25 wt%, 0.5 wt%, and 1.0 wt%. The intermittent discharges were due to the formation of unstable conductive layers on the AlN surface at 0.25 wt%. Nanoparticle agglomeration at 1.0 wt% enhanced dielectric viscosity, which prevented evacuation of the debris and led to unstable arcing. The middle concentration of 0.5wt% gave an ideal combination of amplified thermal conductivity, constant formation of conductive layers, and reasonable fluid flow characteristics. All the quantitative results of the ZrO2 concentration screening trials, such as measured MRR, RWR, SR, and kinematic viscosity, discharge stability, and quality of the conductive layer at all three concentrations are provided in the Table S1. The ZrO2 nanoparticles (particle size: 40 nm, purity: 99.5+) were dispersed in COME at a concentration of 0.5 wt% (corresponding to 4.41 g/L of COME density of 881 g/L) by means of ultrasonic homogenization at 40 kHz over 30 min. This finding is aligned with earlier investigations, which document an optimal nano-additive concentration of 0.3–0.7 wt% of bio-dielectric EDM systems4,27.

The preliminary experiments and the literature were used to determine the applied voltage range (100–150 V). Voltages less than 100 V were not high enough to cause stable discharge in AlN ceramics and voltages greater than 150 V caused uncontrolled arcing and heat damage. Thus, three levels (100, 125 and 150 V) were taken to study both quadratic and linear effects. All the numerical data of the voltage screening experiments, MRR, SR, and surface condition measurements at each of the five levels of voltages tested (75–175 V) are in Table S2. Ceramic micro-EDM systems have also been reported to have similar operating ranges15,29. The range of capacitance (10 20 nF) was chosen based on its direct effect on the discharge energy in the micro-EDM type systems based on RC circuit. Initial trial experiments showed that capacitance values below 10 nF did not generate enough spark energy for effective machining, whereas values above 20 nF caused larger craters, poorer surface finish, and higher electrode wear. Therefore, the selected range was considered appropriate and is also in line with earlier studies on biodegradable dielectric EDM3. The entire screened capacitance screening data (MRR, RWR, SR, electrode condition tests) at each of the five tested levels (5–25 nF) are recorded in Table S3. The electrode geometries, namely O-shaped and D-shaped, were chosen to study how dielectric flow affects the discharge process. The O-shaped electrode allows more uniform fluid circulation and even spark distribution, while the D-shaped electrode promotes directional flushing and different debris removal patterns. This difference becomes more important when using higher-viscosity biodegradable dielectrics like COME, and similar effects have been reported in fluid–tool interaction studies27.

Numerical simulation setup

The thermophysical properties of all three dielectric fluids investigated such as EMWF, COME, and nano abrasive-COME (COME + 0.5 wt% ZrO₂) used as inputs for the CFD simulations are presented in Table 3. Simulation of flow behavior of the three dielectric fluids of EMWF, COME, and nano abrasive-COME under micro-EDM operation conditions individual on ANSYS Fluent 2021 R1 was conducted to directly compare their discharge gap flow features and compare the results with experimental data. Table 4 shows the boundary conditions used with the simulations of all three dielectric fluids (EMWF, COME, and nano abrasive-COME) where only the fluid thermophysical properties vary among the simulation cases and all other boundary conditions are the same to provide a controlled comparative basis. Three-dimensional geometric models were developed to represent the actual experimental configuration. The AlN workpiece geometry included starter cavities of 100 μm diameter with depths varying from 50 μm to 350 μm, formed during the auxiliary electrode-assisted conductive layer initiation stage described in Sect. 2.2 and referred to as pilot holes in the CFD model. WC-Co tool electrodes with D-shaped and O-shaped cross-sections and an outer diameter of 100 μm were positioned at discharge gaps of 10 μm, 20 μm, and 30 μm from the cavity bottom. The computational domain was taken as a cylindrical region extending 500 μm in the radial direction and 400 μm in the axial direction from the machining zone, which is sufficient to capture the flow behavior while keeping the computation efficient.

ANN

The architecture of the proposed ANN designed to predict the machining parameters is shown in Fig. 2, respectively. It consists of an input layer with 5 neurons representing the main process parameters, two hidden layers with 10 and 8 neurons selected through trial and error, and an output layer with 3 neurons corresponding to MRR, RWR, and SR. The hidden layers employ the ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit) activation function to ensure non-linearity, while the output layer uses a linear activation function for precise regression outputs. The training algorithm employs backpropagation with gradient descent, using a learning rate of 0.01 over 500 epochs. The dataset comprises experimental data augmented with synthetically generated data via interpolation techniques to enhance model robustness. The model performance was evaluated using the MSE for accuracy and the correlation coefficient (R²) to validate the prediction reliability.

The overall dataset included 30 experimental data points of BBD design complemented with 60 synthetically obtained intermediate data points of cubic spline interpolation within the validated parameter space - to produce a total of 90 data points of all four dielectric-electrode configurations and of all three responses (MRR, RWR, SR). This augmentation was required to achieve adequate data density to train ANNs reliably because the experimental number of runs was low. Stratified random sampling was used to divide the 90-point dataset into three subsets (no overlaps) in order to represent all four dielectric configurations in proportional numbers in each subset: training set (70, n = 63 data points), validation set (15, n = 14 data points), and testing set (15, n = 13 data points). The minimisation of the error in prediction was done with the help of the training set by updating the backpropagation weights after 500 epochs. The validation set was monitored at the end of each epoch to detect overfitting. Training was stopped when the validation MSE began to increase and continued to rise for 10 consecutive epochs, satisfying the early stopping criterion. The testing set was completely independent of the training procedure and only used to test the generalisation ability of the model to unseen data giving unbiased estimate of prediction performance. The performance measures monitored in all the three subsets were MSE, RMSE, MAE, MAPE, and R2, which were indicated individually in each phase. The training was carried out with a batch of 32 and a total of 500 epochs to balance between the computational efficiency and gradient stability.

Results and discussion

Conductive layer formation

In the EDM process using COME dispersed with nano zirconium oxide (ZrO₂), the formation of a conductive layer on the workpiece surface is crucial for enhancing the machining performance, particularly with non-conductive materials like AlN. This layer plays a vital role in maintaining the continuity of the EDM process by enabling consistent discharge between the tool and the workpiece. The formation of a conductive layer on the workpiece surface during the EDM process significantly influences the machining by facilitating the spark discharge and ensuring stable material removal. This layer typically comprises carbon and metallic particles deposited from the electrode and the dielectric fluid as they break down during the discharge process. The conductive layer enhances the electrical conductivity of the surface, allowing for more efficient energy transfer and consistent spark generation. However, if the layer becomes too thick or uneven, it can lead to unstable machining conditions, such as short circuits or reduced precision. Therefore, managing the formation of this layer is crucial for maintaining the optimal EDM performance20.

During the EDM process, the interaction between the COME dielectric, ZrO₂ nanoparticles, and the AlN ceramic leads to the formation of a conductive layer on the workpiece surface. This layer mainly consists of carbon from the COME fluid and aluminium oxide from the workpiece. The auxiliary electrode (AE) helps in forming this layer by supporting uniform and continuous deposition during machining. This process ensures that the surface remains conductive throughout the machining process, enabling effective energy transfer and material removal. The EDS analysis shown in Fig. 3 presents the elemental composition of the AlN workpiece surface before and after micro-EDM at 150 V and 20 nF capacitance. The findings indicate that there are some serious compositional variations after machining. The content of aluminium (Al) and oxygen (O) decreased at 23.45% to 20.11%, respectively, and the content of aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃) compounds formed and deposited on the surface was obtained as a result of thermal oxidation of AlN in the course of electrical discharge. Simultaneously, the concentration of the zirconium (Zr) element dropped to 33.78% instead of 42.33%, and it is possible that ZrO2 nanoparticles of the nano abrasive-COME dielectric were partially redistributed or consumed during the discharge processes that led to the formation of the conductive layer. There was a slight decrease in nitrogen (N) level at 6.62% to 5.22%, which is evidence of partial disintegration of the AlN lattice in the discharge zone. Equally, the content of carbon (C) reduced at 7.49% to 4.93%, meaning, as the COME dielectric deposits pyrolytic carbon, it is oxidized in part during the conditions of the high temperatures of discharge. All these compositional modifications point to the development of a composite Al2O3 and ZrO2 conductive layer on the AlN surface that reduces the surface electrical resistance and helps to maintain a stable spark discharge in micro-EDM. The presence of this conductive layer is essential for maintaining the stability of the EDM process, particularly when machining challenging materials like AlN. The continuous flow of COME is required to sustain the formation of this layer, ensuring that the workpiece surface remains conductive and that the machining process can proceed efficiently. The optimized formation of the conductive layer not only enhances the MRR but also improves the SR, leading to better overall machining performance.

Effect of O-shaped and D-shaped electrodes on the discharge and machinability

In this analysis, the electrode polarity of the WC–Co tool refers to the electrical connection of the electrode within the RC discharge circuit. In the O-COME and D-COME configurations, the O-shaped and D-shaped electrodes act as the tool electrode, while the AlN serves as the workpiece. In these configurations, the tool electrode is connected to the negative terminal of the RC generator, and the AlN workpiece is connected to the positive terminal. Under this situation, the electrons coming off of the negatively biased tool electrode strike the workpiece surface, where they deposit energy selectively onto the workpiece and facilitate greater material removal, but at the expense of greater erosion of the electrode. In positive tool polarity, which is abbreviated as O-COME, the tool electrode is attached to the positive terminal and workpiece to a negative terminal. In this setup, the electrons will be concentrated towards the workpiece and positive ions in the plasma are focused on the tool surface. In the case of non-conductive AlN, positive polarity would increase the formation of conductive layers in the phase of the auxiliary electrode initiation because ion bombardment would help to deposit the ZrO2 and carbon particles of the nano abrasive-COME dielectric onto the AlN surface.

The cross-sectional geometry of the tool electrode has direct effect on spreading electrical charge over the inter-electrode gap, and the AlN workpiece surface in both polarities. The resulting electrode geometry is the O-shaped shape that possesses a symmetric annular cross-section that causes a radially homogeneous electric field throughout the whole perimeter of the electrode, leading to evenly spaced discharge events at the tool -workpiece interface27. In the O-COME positive polarity, the bombardment of positive ions on the workpiece produces uniform deposition of ZrO2 and carbon and a uniform layer of conductive material, whereas in the O-COME negative polarity electrons are emitted uniformly, resulting in the formation of uniform crater morphology and predictable surface finish. Conversely, the D-shaped electrode has an asymmetric cross-section that is flat-faced, and the electric field is concentrated at the edges of the high curvature. Consequently, the events of discharge take place at these regions more often, resulting in the preferential removal of material and directional biased crater patterns. This charge imbalance, where the open flow channel is created by the D-shaped geometry when the electrode is rotating at 600 rpm, is conducive to creating the directional dielectric flow and enables the formation of the conductive layers at increased depths of drilling as shown by the CFD streamline analysis. The overall polarity mechanism is not affected by the electrode shape. However, the spatial distribution of charge depends on the electrode geometry. The O-COME positive condition provides a higher material removal rate and produces a smoother surface finish. In contrast, the D-COME negative condition is more suitable for deep-hole drilling. This is mainly due to its ability to maintain effective dielectric flow at greater depths.

The establishment of the conductive layer with negative tool polarity was researched by observing the peak discharge current against drilling depth, presented in Fig. 9. The formation of conductive layers under the negative polarity electrode was observed to be not optimal under the O-shaped electrode with reduced dielectric availability at higher depths of more than 100 μm. Though energy deposition occurs mainly in the direction of the workpiece, limited dielectric flow through the confined annular gap prevents carbon and ZrO2 particle replenishment, which increases conductive layer resistance (RCL) and ultimately causes a process interruption at 170 μm drilling of the workpiece. Conversely, the negative polarity of the D-shaped electrode demonstrated better dielectric flow because of an open asymmetric structure, which was able to form a continuous conductive layer at deeper levels.

Current changing with the electrode position.

In positive polarity of the tool when using the O-shaped electrode, the discharge characteristics alter to greater ion contact with the workpiece surface. The tool would serve as the anode and, though the highest voltage is lesser because of the lowered conductive layer resistance, the frequency of discharge and rate of material removal are elevated considerably. This can be explained by the formation of a more stable conductive layer due to the deposition of ZrO₂ nanoparticles from the nano abrasive-COME dielectric. This reduces inter-electrode resistance and ignition delay. As a result, the number of effective discharge pulses increases. The O-COME setup with positive polarity recorded a micro-drilling depth of 350 μm with hole diameters of 150 μm and less with optimal performance in terms of conductive layer stability, discharge frequency, and material removal efficiency29.

Surface roughness (SR)

All the values of surface roughness (SR) in this section were taken on the inside of the micro-drilled holes in the AlN ceramic workpiece. Measurements were not done on the tool electrode surface or on the top surface of the workpiece. The inner wall was chosen because it is the real functional surface of the micro-hole and is the direct measure of machining quality in use in the micro-electronics and aerospace thermal management. The measurements of all surface roughness (SR) were performed at the inner wall of the micro-drilled holes in the AlN workpiece. The holes are too small (100 μm) to be measured with conventional stylus-type profilometers, which have probe dimensions that are too large to measure them. Thus, the measurements were performed with a micro-contact surface profilometer, which has a micro-bore stylus. The stylus was tipped at 0.5 μm and had a diameter of shank at 60 μm, making it able to get to holes with a diameter of 80 μm. A cut-off wavelength (0.08 mm) and an evaluation length (0.4 mm) were used to measure them with a low contact force of 0.3 mN. The stylus was then delicately inserted into the hole along the axial direction, by a precision micro-positioning stage during measurement. The probe was then dragged in the longitudinal direction along the inner wall to provide the surface profile. Three measurements were taken at the angular positions of 0, 120, and 2400 around the circumference of each hole. These three values of Ra were averaged to obtain the surface roughness of that specific experiment, as per ISO 4288. Table 8 below shows the analysis of variance of the surface roughness (SR) model. The model shows a good predictive power, as R2 = 0.938 and adjusted R2 = 0.915 implies that a large percentage of variation in the surface roughness is accounted. The R² value meets the commonly accepted criterion of R² > 0.90 for reliable prediction in RSM-based machining applications and is comparable to values reported in other EDM optimisation studies36. The small difference between R² and adjusted R² (0.023) indicates that the model is well fitted and not over-parameterised. The minimum required value of 4.0 is much lower than the Adequate Precision of 11.23, and this evidence is a strong signal-to-noise ratio and robust model discrimination throughout the design space, as it is in recent hybrid optimisation research studies in machining37. The model explains 68.9% of the total variation in surface roughness, which is lower than that observed for MRR and RWR. This result is expected, as surface roughness is influenced by several uncontrolled factors, including debris adhesion, recast layer formation, and localized thermal fluctuations, as reported in previous studies38[,39. The remaining unexplained variation ranging from 21.5% to 33.0%, suggests that additional process variables may be required to further improve the prediction of surface roughness. Nevertheless, the non-significant lack-of-fit (p > 0.05) indicates that the model is statistically adequate. Furthermore, all primary factors, including dielectric type, voltage, and capacitance, are highly significant (p < 0.001). Thus, the evolved SR model is applicable in the analysis and optimisation of surface quality in micro-EDM of AlN ceramics.

Voltage scheduling

Cracks frequently appear on the surface of the AlN due to the high discharge energy encountered when the instrument reaches the copper surface, particularly when using the assist electrode technique. The external discharge affects the surface area of the peripheral outflow, leading to potential damage and inconsistencies. A voltage scheduling technique was implemented to manage the potential difference across all modes, ranging from 100 V to a small voltage up to 150 V (VV mode). The draft gradients of the boreholes in relation to the different configurations and voltage patterns are summarized in Fig. 20. During the micro-EDM drilling process, the current remained stable at 50 V for a drilling depth of 50 μm. As the drilling depth increased, the voltage rapidly increased to 150 V at a depth of 170 μm and remained at 150 V until the completion of the process. This controlled voltage scheduling technique effectively managed the external response, ensuring a consistent and stable micro-EDM drilling operation43. The draft angle of the cross-section decreased significantly, from approximately 2.56° to 0.57°, indicating a more controlled and precise drilling process under the VV mode.

The voltage transmission with respect to the electrode position and the comparative results of the constant voltage (CV) and Variable Voltage (VV) modes are illustrated in Fig. 21. In the CV mode, all configurations, except the O-shaped electrode in the CV mode, resulted in draft angles of less than 10°, demonstrating a relatively consistent drilling performance. However, the VV mode exhibited distinct advantages in terms of the MRR and tool wear rate (TWR). The MRR increased while the RWR decreased significantly under the VV mode due to the lower discharge potential at the initial stage of drilling. Additionally, tool wear was minimized throughout the processing time in the VV mode, leading to reduced production time and enhanced overall efficiency. The RWR was reduced by more than 60%, maintaining a low discharging current throughout the discharge phase, which contributed to the overall improvement in the machining performance24.

The experimental results, as depicted in Fig. 22, provide a clear comparison of the MRR and RWR under different voltage modes—namely, Constant Voltage (CV) and Variable Voltage (VV) modes. The VV mode demonstrates a significant advantage over the CV mode in achieving higher MRR and lower RWR, which can be attributed to several critical factors explored during the study.

Voltage transmission with respect to electrode position under constant voltage (CV) and variable voltage (VV) modes.

The VV mode’s superior performance in terms of MRR is primarily due to its ability to adjust the voltage during the micro-EDM process dynamically. This adjustment ensures that the voltage is optimized at each stage of the machining process, allowing for more efficient material removal while minimizing excessive wear on the electrode. In contrast, the CV mode maintains a constant voltage throughout the process, which can lead to suboptimal conditions where the voltage is either too high, causing excessive wear, or too low, resulting in insufficient material removal. The lower RWR observed in the VV mode is also significant, as it suggests that the electrode experiences less degradation over time. It is crucial for extending the lifespan of the electrode and maintaining the precision of the machining process44. The ability of the VV mode to reduce the RWR while enhancing the MRR is a key finding, highlighting the importance of voltage control in optimizing the EDM process. The use of COME as a biodegradable dielectric fluid plays a pivotal role in the improved performance of the VV mode. COME’s high dielectric strength and thermal conductivity contribute to the efficient transfer of energy to the workpiece, which is essential for achieving high MRR. Moreover, COME’s biodegradable nature and its ability to form a conductive layer on the workpiece surface further enhance the EDM process by stabilizing the discharge and reducing the likelihood of unwanted side reactions that could degrade the electrode or the workpiece surface. The addition of 0.5 wt% nano zirconium oxide with COME positively influenced the machining process by improving the fluid’s viscosity and thermal expansion properties. This enhancement allows for better control over the discharge gap and ensures consistent dielectric fluid flow, which is crucial for maintaining stable machining conditions and achieving the desired surface finish25.

Figure 23 shows optical cross-sectional images of micro-drilled holes achieved with various electrode geometries and electrical modes, showing how the draft angle varies with the depth of the hole. Taper between the inlet and outlet diameters is observed as the difference between the outlet and inlet wall of the holes, as shown by the divergence. In the case of the D-shaped electrode being used in negative CV mode, the draft angle of the hole is relatively higher meaning that there is a sharp inlet- outflow taper. This is explained by the non-uniform discharge concentration on the curved sides of the D-shaped geometry that causes the localized removal of the material and uneven distribution of energy throughout the depth. The effect is further noticeable as machining advances, which causes greater sidewall divergence. Conversely, an O-shaped electrode with positive CV mode exhibits an apparently lower draft angle, and sidewalls that tend to be uniform with almost parallel sidewalls.

The symmetric geometry of the O-shaped electrode leaves the electric field more uniform in distribution and results in the consistency of discharge behavior and controlled material removal throughout the depth of the hole. This is made to give better dimensional accuracy and lesser taper. In the negative CV, the O-shaped electrode retains a higher uniformity than the D-shaped setup although slight taper increment is noticed as compared to positive polarity. This shows that stability of conductive layer formation and discharge distribution is affected by polarity, which also affects the draft angle45. With the negative VV mode, the taper is stiffer with respect to CV mode, indicating that the change of voltage brings an instability in discharge power, resulting in the less controllable material removal and higher deviation of the hole sides. The optical images validate that the draft angle is very sensitive to both the geometry of the electrode and the electrical mode. The O-shaped electrode in the positive CV mode is the one that offers the most even profile of holes with minimum taper and the D-shaped electrode (in negative conditions) offers larger draft angles because discharge is asymmetrically distributed and the edges are more strongly affected46.

Conclusion

This study presents an integrated framework combining nano abrasive-COME biodegradable dielectric, RSM-ANN optimisation, and CFD-based flow analysis for micro-EDM of non-conductive AlN ceramics. The effects of voltage, capacitance, and electrode-dielectric configuration were evaluated. The findings indicate that MRR, RWR and SR are the most affected by the dielectric type. The nano abrasive-COME dielectric is superior to conventional EMWF on both material removal and electrode wear because it has better conductivity and formation of conductive layers.

Dielectric type contributed 33.0% to MRR, 37.4% to RWR, and 45.2% to SR (p < 0.0001), making it the most significant factor.

Future scope

Future research should prioritize improved experimental control to reduce process variability, larger sample sizes for reliable statistical inference, comprehensive lifecycle assessment for quantitative sustainability evaluation, and extended parameter ranges to enhance model robustness. The methodology and findings provide a solid foundation for advancing sustainable precision manufacturing technologies, though industrial implementation requires careful consideration of application-specific performance requirements and economic feasibility. This work represents a significant step toward reconciling environmental responsibility with manufacturing excellence in the precision machining industry.
