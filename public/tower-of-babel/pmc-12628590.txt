Genome doubling as a dynamic driver of ovarian cancer evolution: insights from single-cell sequencing
Authors: Tianjiao Zhao, Tianshi Zhao, Dengyu Dong, Dengyue Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhole-genome doubling (WGD) is a macro-evolutionary event that is both prevalent and prognostically significant in human cancers, particularly in high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC). Historically, WGD has been viewed as a consequence of widespread genomic instability, but recent advancements in single-cell sequencing (sc-seq) have reframed its role as a central, dynamic driver of tumor evolution. This review summarizes cutting-edge findings, demonstrating how WGD acts as a catalyst for a distinct evolutionary trajectory characterized by the rapid accumulation of chromosomal losses and the selection of highly adaptable clones. A key finding is the resolution of a biological paradox: WGD-driven chromosomal instability, which should provoke an immune response, is instead correlated with a profoundly immunosuppressive phenotype via the repression of key innate immune pathways. Finally, this review discusses the clinical implications of these discoveries, highlighting WGD’s potential as a predictive biomarker and a source of unique therapeutic vulnerabilities, paving the way for targeted strategies in advanced HGSOC.Keywords: Whole-genome doubling, Tumor evolution, Chromosomal instability, Immunosuppression, High-grade serous ovarian carcinoma

Introduction

Genomic instability is a fundamental hallmark of cancer, driving the accumulation of the genetic and epigenetic alterations that enable malignant transformation and progression[1–3]. For decades, conventional bulk sequencing has provided a static, averaged view of this instability, identifying widespread chromosomal copy number alterations (CNAs) as a defining feature of many solid tumors [4], including the most common and aggressive form of ovarian cancer, high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC) [5–7]. Indeed, the seminal work by The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) consortium established that, beyond the near-ubiquitous TP53 mutation, HGSOC genomes are characterized more by widespread CNAs than by recurrent point mutations[5, 8]. This genomic landscape, laden with numerical and structural abnormalities, distinguishes HGSOC from other solid tumors and underpins its inherent heterogeneity and adaptive capacity [8–10].

Within this context, whole-genome doubling (WGD), the duplication of the entire chromosome complement, has been recognized as a macro-evolutionary event that occurs in approximately 30–40% of human cancers [11–13]. WGD is a strong negative prognostic factor, associated with increased risk of death, metastasis, and drug resistance, independent of other clinical factors [11, 14]. However, its precise role has remained a profound paradox: WGD is both a barrier to cell proliferation, imposing immense cellular stress, and a potent driver of evolution, offering a selective advantage [15–17]. The challenges of reconstructing the precise timeline of WGD events and their subsequent genomic consequences from bulk sequencing data have long hindered a deeper mechanistic understanding [18, 19].

The advent of single-cell sequencing technologies has revolutionized the ability to resolve this paradox [20, 21]. Preliminary findings from a recent preprint by Sallese et al. suggest that by profiling the genome of individual cells, it overcomes the limitations of bulk sequencing, which averages data from millions of cells and obscures critical subclonal heterogeneity and the chronological ordering of events[20, 22]. This technological leap has allowed for the reconstruction of tumor phylogenies and, for the first time, the unprecedented ability to place WGD events in time relative to other genomic alterations, providing a dynamic view of cancer evolution [23–25]. This review provides a comprehensive synthesis of these new insights, presenting WGD not as a passive consequence of genomic chaos, but as an active, dynamic force that sculpts the HGSOC genome, dictates its evolutionary path, and profoundly influences its interaction with the immune microenvironment.

Whole-genome doubling: a foundational event in cancer evolution

Prevalence and prognostic significance

WGD is a hallmark genomic abnormality in human cancers, with studies identifying it in nearly 30% of prospectively sequenced advanced cancer patients [26, 27]. Its prevalence is particularly high in HGSOC, where a significant majority of tumors, approximately 66%, have been categorized as WGD-high, meaning more than 80% of their cells have undergone at least one instance of genome doubling[27–29]. This macro-evolutionary event is a strong negative prognostic factor, predicting an increased risk of death across various cancer types [30]. Its association with poor clinical outcomes extends to increased rates of metastasis and drug resistance, solidifying its role as a key driver of advanced and difficult-to-treat malignancies [27]. Although WGD is generally associated with poor prognosis, its influence can be context-dependent [17]. Studies have shown that while it is a negative prognostic factor in colorectal, pancreatic, and breast cancers, it is paradoxically linked to improved survival outcomes in bladder urothelial carcinomas [17], highlighting that the effect of WGD is not universal and depends on the specific tumor type and its underlying genomic landscape (Fig. 1)[17, 31].

Cellular mechanisms of polyploidization

The process of WGD can be initiated by several distinct cellular mechanisms that uncouple normal cell cycle checkpoints and result in the duplication of the entire chromosome set [17, 32] One of the primary pathways is mitotic slippage, also known as endomitosis, where a cell enters mitosis but fails to undergo proper chromosome segregation or cytokinesis [33, 34]. This is often triggered by the spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC), which stalls mitotic progression due to improper attachment of chromosomes to the mitotic spindle [35]. Instead of undergoing apoptosis or correcting the error, the cell exits mitosis without division, resulting in a single tetraploid cell with a doubled chromosome and centrosome complement[17, 33, 36]. Another common mechanism is cytokinesis failure, where the cell completes nuclear division but the final division of the cytoplasm is defective, leading to the formation of a single binucleated cell with a doubled genome[17, 37–39]. WGD can also occur through endoreduplication, a process where cells undergo successive rounds of genome replication without an intervening mitosis, bypassing the entire cell division process[17, 32, 40]. Finally, less common but still relevant mechanisms, such as cell fusion, can also result in a tetraploid state by the unification of two cells [17, 41]. A critical factor enabling these events is the circumvention of checkpoint pathways that would normally halt the cell cycle or induce apoptosis in response to the cellular stress and DNA damage caused by abortive mitosis [32, 42, 43]. A common precursor is the loss of functional p53, a critical tumor suppressor, which normally enforces the G1 checkpoint and would otherwise prevent the proliferation of these unstable tetraploid cells (Fig. 2) [44, 45].

WGD as a response to pre-existing haploidy

The paradoxical nature of WGD—being both a source of instability and a feature of advanced tumors—can be illuminated by considering its potential selective advantages [17, 40]. One compelling evolutionary scenario is that WGD is not merely a random event but a specific response to a pre-existing genomic vulnerability [46]. Tumors with extensive loss of heterozygosity (LOH) have large haploid regions of their genome where recessive deleterious somatic alterations are exposed [47]. The unchecked accumulation of these harmful mutations could compromise cellular fitness and act as an evolutionary roadblock [48, 49]. WGD, by duplicating the entire chromosome complement, provides a mechanism to mitigate the negative effects of these deleterious alterations [32, 50]. This genome-wide duplication effectively restores a diploid-like state to the LOH regions, providing a protective effect and buffering against the “ratchet-like accumulation” of harmful mutations [51, 52]. The observation of a significant positive correlation between the amount of haploid LOH in non-WGD tumors and the frequency of WGD events across different tumor types supports this model [53]. This indicates that WGD is not just a random error but can be an evolutionarily selected strategy that offers a survival advantage to cell populations under selective pressure from haploidy, providing a powerful explanation for its prevalence and association with tumor progression.

The single-cell revolution: Deciphering the Temporal and Spatial dynamics of WGD

Limitations of bulk sequencing

For many years, the study of cancer genomics was predominantly reliant on bulk sequencing, which measures the average genomic or transcriptomic profile of a large population of cells from a single tumor biopsy [20, 54, 55]. While this approach has provided foundational insights into cancer biology, it suffers from a major limitation: it obscures the extraordinary cellular heterogeneity that is a defining characteristic of cancer [20, 56, 57]. The average profile of a bulk tumor sample can mask the presence of rare but clinically significant subpopulations of cells, such as those that have acquired drug resistance or are responsible for metastasis [58, 59]. Furthermore, bulk sequencing provides a static snapshot in time, making it impossible to infer the chronological sequence of evolutionary events, such as when specific mutations or large-scale genomic alterations like WGD occurred relative to one another [18, 19, 60]. This temporal ambiguity has long been a barrier to understanding the true evolutionary dynamics of cancer.

The power of single-cell sequencing

The advent of single-cell sequencing has ushered in a new era of cancer research by providing a high-resolution view of cell-to-cell variation at the genomic, transcriptomic, and epigenetic levels [61–63]. Technologies such as single-cell whole-genome sequencing (scWGS) and Direct Library Preparation (DLP) single-cell sequencing (sc-seq) enable the profiling of tens of thousands of individual cells, allowing for the detection of distinct cellular subpopulations that would be lost in bulk analysis [27, 64]. This unprecedented resolution allows for the reconstruction of a tumor’s evolutionary history by building phylogenetic trees based on the shared and distinct genomic alterations found in individual cells [27, 65]. A central and transformative application of sc-seq is its ability to resolve the temporal relationship between WGD and other forms of genomic instability [66, 67]. By analyzing the phylogeny of individual cancer cells, researchers can categorize chromosomal copy number alterations (CNAs) as occurring before or after a WGD event [27, 68]. The methodology for this temporal ordering relies on basic principles of somatic evolution. First, a phylogenetic tree is constructed from the shared and distinct CNAs across thousands of single cells [69]. The WGD event is identified as a branch in this tree where the baseline chromosome number for the entire clone abruptly shifts from diploid (~ 2n) to tetraploid (~ 4n). CNAs are then timed relative to this event: alterations present in a diploid state (e.g., a single copy loss, 2n→1n) that are shared by all cells within the WGD clone are classified as pre-WGD events, as they were subsequently doubled along with the rest of the genome (becoming 2n→4n, and the loss becoming 1n→2n). Conversely, alterations that are subclonal (present in only a subset of WGD cells) or result in odd-numbered copy states (e.g., a loss from 4n→3n) must be classified as post-WGD events, as they could only have occurred after the doubling [70–72]. This is not merely a technical improvement; it represents a paradigm shift from observing a correlation between WGD and instability to inferring a causal sequence [73]. This ability to place WGD events within the context of the evolutionary tree allows for a dynamic, rather than static, view of their role in tumor progression [27, 74].

Despite these profound advantages, single-cell sequencing is not without its challenges. The minute amount of DNA in a single cell necessitates a whole-genome amplification (WGA) step, which introduces technical artifacts, such as low genome coverage, allele dropout, and amplification bias [75, 76]. To overcome these hurdles, the field has advanced on multiple fronts. Methodologically, the development of improved WGA protocols and the adoption of direct library preparation (DLP+) methods have significantly enhanced the uniformity and quality of sequencing data at the source [77, 78]. To handle the inherent ‘noise’ in single-cell data, a key best practice is the use of sophisticated bioinformatics tools. These algorithms move beyond simple read-depth analysis and instead leverage the B-allele frequency of heterozygous SNPs, coupled with advanced segmentation and smoothing algorithms (e.g., SCOPE, S-CNA), to denoise the data and infer copy number states with much higher confidence [79, 80]. Looking forward, the integration of multi-modal and spatial technologies is poised to overcome the remaining limitations. Multi-omic approaches that profile the genome and transcriptome from the same single cell (e.g., 10x Multiome) provide a powerful means to functionally validate genomic findings, for instance by correlating a gene’s amplification with its increased expression [81, 82]. Furthermore, the rise of spatial omics addresses the critical limitation of tissue dissociation by placing genomically-defined clones back into their native architectural context. This enables the direct investigation of how WGD-positive cells interact with the tumor microenvironment, transforming our understanding of cancer evolution from a dissociated cell-intrinsic process to a spatially resolved ecosystem (Fig. 3; Table 1).

WGD as a dynamic force: shaping the HGSOC genome and clonal evolution

The high-resolution view afforded by single-cell sequencing has led to a fundamental revision of the proposed role of WGD in cancer evolution [87]. Based on the temporal ordering of genomic events, a new model is emerging where the event is no longer seen as a mere chaotic byproduct of genomic instability but rather as a pivotal event that precedes and is associated with a “catalyst for increased adaptability” that appears to sculpt the tumor genome [27, 71, 72, 88, 89]. This new paradigm is supported by several key observations from sc-seq phylogenies [88, 89]. A striking finding is the predominance of chromosomal losses over gains, particularly in the post-WGD evolutionary phase [27]. While this temporal ordering does not definitively prove causation, it strongly challenges the prior assumption that cancer genomes are primarily shaped by amplifications and gene dosage increases [90]. Instead, WGD seems to be followed by a powerful process of “contraction and pruning,” where cells shed unnecessary or deleterious genetic material, retaining only the combinations that confer a selective advantage [91]. This mechanism, where a massive duplication is followed by a burst of selective loss, effectively acts as a “genomic reset button.” [92] It allows the tumor to bypass small, incremental evolutionary steps and make a major phenotypic leap, explaining the correlation between WGD and aggressive tumor phenotypes [71].

Further analysis of clonal lineages has revealed a fitness-driven evolutionary scenario where clones that undergo WGD early in their evolutionary history—found on the “truncal” branch of the phylogenetic tree—are selectively advantaged [27, 93]. These early WGD clones harbor considerably more chromosomal alterations, including three times as many arm and whole chromosome losses, than their subclonal counterparts [27, 53]. This suggests that the initial doubling event sets the stage for rapid genomic remodeling and subsequent clonal expansion, fostering the expansion of highly adapted populations within the tumor microenvironment [27, 94]. This provides an elegant solution to the long-standing mystery of the pseudotriploid karyotypes frequently observed in HGSOC [9]. Rather than arising from a simple, incremental gain of chromosomes, these karyotypes are now understood to be the result of a single WGD event followed by the progressive and selective loss of chromosomes over time (Table 2) [27, 53, 95].

The immunological paradox: WGD-driven immune evasion

The profound genomic instability that follows a WGD event creates a significant biological paradox [96]. Chromosomal missegregation and the formation of supernumerary centrosomes lead to a high rate of micronuclei—small, extra nuclei containing fragments of DNA—which are a hallmark of chromosomal instability [33, 97]. These micronuclei can rupture, spilling DNA into the cytoplasm, which should, in theory, act as a powerful danger signal that activates the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) innate immune signaling pathway [27, 98]. This activation would typically trigger an inflammatory immune response to fight the cancer [27, 99].

However, single-cell studies have revealed a remarkable and counter-intuitive finding: WGD-high HGSOC tumors, despite their high level of chromosomal instability and frequent micronuclei formation, are adept at evading the immune system and are found within an immunosuppressive microenvironment [96, 100]. The resolution to this paradox lies in a mechanism of active immune suppression. WGD-high tumors have been found to develop ways to dampen the immune response, primarily by repressing the STING pathway itself [100]. This repression allows the cancer cells to bypass the negative selective pressure that their own genomic instability would otherwise impose, enabling them to survive and proliferate in a state of chronic cellular stress [27, 101]. This finding establishes a clear causal link: WGD not only fuels genomic instability but also actively shapes and selects for an immunosuppressive phenotype, transforming the tumor microenvironment to favor its continued progression [17]. This is in stark contrast to WGD-low tumors, which have lower levels of chromosomal instability (CIN) and intact STING signaling, and are thus more visible to the immune system and more likely to trigger an inflammatory response [29].

While the exact mechanisms of this immune evasion are an active area of investigation, several compelling hypotheses are emerging, supported by multi-omics data. One leading hypothesis is the direct epigenetic silencing of the cGAS-STING pathway components. For instance, promoter hypermethylation of the STING1 gene has been observed in various cancers and is associated with a dampened immune response, providing a plausible mechanism for the functional repression seen in WGD-high tumors [102–104]. A second, non-mutually exclusive mechanism involves the secretion of immunosuppressive factors. The chronic cellular stress and inflammatory signaling induced by chromosomal instability can lead to the upregulation of cytokines and chemokines, such as TGF-β or CCL2, which actively recruit suppressive immune cells like regulatory T cells (Tregs) and M2-polarized macrophages into the tumor microenvironment [105–107]. While current sc-seq studies robustly demonstrate the immunosuppressive cell state, future work integrating spatial transcriptomics and proteomics will be crucial to definitively link WGD-status to these specific molecular mechanisms within the tumor architecture.

Clinical implications and emerging therapeutic strategies

The insights gained from single-cell sequencing into the role of WGD have direct and significant clinical implications. The high prevalence of WGD in HGSOC and its clear association with poor clinical outcomes make it a valuable prognostic biomarker [10, 108]. Assessing WGD status could provide critical information for risk stratification and for guiding treatment intensity in a personalized manner [109]. Furthermore, the discovery of the distinct immunological profiles of WGD-high versus WGD-low tumors suggests that WGD status could serve as a predictive biomarker for response to specific immunotherapies [27]. Specifically, the immunosuppressive, or “cold,” tumor microenvironment fostered by WGD-high tumors is characteristic of tumors that respond poorly to immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapies), which rely on a pre-existing anti-tumor T-cell response [110, 111]. This provides a strong rationale for using WGD status as a stratification biomarker in ongoing and future immunotherapy trials, sparing patients unlikely to respond from potential toxicity and enriching for populations that might benefit from novel combinations, such as STING agonists paired with checkpoint blockade. Therefore, WGD status could potentially be used to identify patients unlikely to benefit from checkpoint blockade alone. More importantly, this suggests a rational therapeutic strategy: combining STING agonists, which could re-activate the innate immune pathway and convert the tumor from “cold” to “hot,” with immune checkpoint inhibitors to overcome this resistance in patients with WGD-high HGSOC [112, 113]. The relationship between WGD and response to PARP inhibitors (PARPi), a cornerstone of HGSOC treatment, is an area of intense investigation [114]. WGD often co-occurs with homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), suggesting potential sensitivity to PARPi. However, the profound genomic plasticity conferred by WGD may also provide a rapid route to acquiring PARPi resistance [115, 116]. When integrated with HRD scores, WGD status could provide a more nuanced prediction of both initial response and the likelihood of subsequent relapse. This improved prediction could help identify patients who may benefit from alternative maintenance strategies or combination therapies.

Emerging therapeutic strategies and developmental progress

The unique biology of WGD-positive tumors creates several exciting therapeutic opportunities, with some strategies now advancing toward clinical application.

Targeting Synthetic Lethality: KIF18A Inhibitors A highly promising strategy is to target the synthetic lethal dependencies of WGD + cells. As previously mentioned, the mitotic kinesin KIF18A is a prime target due to its essential role in managing the mitotic stress of tetraploid cells [71]. Significant preclinical progress has been made in this area, with several biotechnology companies developing potent and selective KIF18A inhibitors. These agents have demonstrated robust efficacy in chromosomally unstable and WGD-positive preclinical cancer models, and the first clinical trials testing KIF18A inhibitors in patients with advanced solid tumors are now underway (e.g., NCT05593135) [117]. This marks a critical step in validating this therapeutic concept and bringing a new class of targeted therapy to this high-risk patient population.

Unresolved questions and future directions

The single-cell revolution has shifted our view of WGD from a genomic state to a dynamic process, yet this new perspective illuminates several profound and unresolved biological questions [130].

A primary mystery is the paradox of cellular fitness immediately following a WGD event. While WGD is linked to long-term adaptability, newly-formed tetraploid cells must overcome severe mitotic stress, centrosome amplification, and a high risk of apoptosis or senescence [96, 131]. What specific molecular adaptations—beyond TP53 loss—are required for a cell to survive this initial crisis and establish a proliferating WGD-positive clone? Understanding this immediate survival bottleneck is critical to explaining why only some nascent tetraploid cells succeed [132, 133].

Furthermore, the starkly different prognostic implications of WGD across cancer types remain a fundamental controversy. Why is WGD a marker of aggressive disease and poor outcomes in HGSOC, breast, and pancreatic cancers, yet paradoxically associated with improved survival in bladder urothelial carcinoma [17, 50, 134, 135]? This suggests that the evolutionary impact of WGD is not universal but is instead highly context-dependent, likely dictated by the underlying genomic landscape, the specific tissue microenvironment, and co-occurring mutations that synergize with or antagonize the polyploid state.

Finally, while this review highlights the evolutionary path enabled by WGD, a key question is whether there are alternative, equally potent macro-evolutionary strategies in aggressive, WGD-negative HGSOC. Do these tumors rely on qualitatively different processes, such as chromothripsis or gradual chromosome accumulation, to achieve the aneuploidy and heterogeneity required for progression? Comparing the evolutionary dynamics and resulting therapeutic vulnerabilities of WGD-positive versus WGD-negative aggressive tumors represents a critical frontier. Answering these fundamental questions will require moving beyond genomic snapshots and leveraging next-generation methodologies. For instance, spatial transcriptomics can be used to directly investigate the microenvironmental interactions of WGD-positive clones [136, 137]. By overlaying inferred copy number status onto the spatial map of a tumor section, researchers can test hypotheses about immune evasion by quantifying the proximity and activation state of cytotoxic T cells to WGD-positive cancer cells, or by identifying suppressive cellular niches that co-localize with these aggressive subclones.

Furthermore, integrating single-cell genomics with other -omics layers is essential for uncovering novel functional consequences of a doubled genome. By simultaneously profiling the genome and transcriptome of the same single cell (e.g., using 10x Multiome), it becomes possible to directly test emerging hypotheses, such as a link between WGD and metabolic reprogramming [138]. This could reveal whether WGD-positive cells upregulate specific pathways, like glycolysis or glutamine metabolism, to cope with the immense bioenergetic stress of maintaining a tetraploid state. Identifying such metabolic addictions would not only deepen our biological understanding but could also unveil an entirely new class of therapeutic vulnerabilities in WGD-driven cancers.

Conclusions

This review has highlighted a fundamental paradigm shift in the understanding of whole-genome doubling in ovarian cancer. Moving beyond a view of WGD as a mere consequence of genomic chaos, single-cell sequencing has demonstrated that it is a central, dynamic driver that actively sculpts the tumor genome and dictates its evolutionary trajectory. The evidence presented indicates that WGD is a potent catalyst for rapid evolution, enabling a process of genomic pruning that selects for highly fit clones while simultaneously allowing cancer cells to evade the very immune responses that their own instability should provoke. These new insights into WGD’s dual role as both a genomic accelerator and a source of therapeutic vulnerabilities hold immense promise for the future of precision medicine. The continued application of single-cell technologies, coupled with integrated multi-omic approaches, is poised to unlock novel strategies for combating advanced HGSOC by targeting the very evolutionary forces that drive its progression.
