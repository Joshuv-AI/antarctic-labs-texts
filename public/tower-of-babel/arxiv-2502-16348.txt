Accretion and Ablation in Deformable Solids using an Eulerian Formulation: A Finite Deformation Numerical Method
S. Kiana Naghibzadeh, Anthony Rollett, Noel Walkington, Kaushik Dayal

Abstract
Surface growth, i.e., the addition or removal of mass from the boundary of a solid body, occurs in a wide range of processes, including the growth of biological tissues, solidification and melting, and additive manufacturing. To understand nonlinear phenomena such as failure and morphological instabilities in these systems, accurate numerical models are required to study the interaction between mass addition and stress in complex geometrical and physical settings. Despite recent progress in the formulation of models of surface growth of deformable solids, current numerical approaches require several simplifying assumptions. This work formulates a method that couples an Eulerian surface growth description to a phase-field approach. It further develops a finite element implementation to solve the model numerically using a fixed computational domain with a fixed discretization. This approach bypasses the challenges that arise in a Lagrangian approach, such as having to construct a four-dimensional reference configuration, remeshing, and/or changing the computational domain over the course of the numerical solution. It also enables the modeling of several settings — such as non-normal growth of biological tissues and stress-induced growth — which can be challenging for available methods. The numerical approach is demonstrated on a model problem that shows non-normal growth, wherein growth occurs by the motion of the surface in a direction that is not parallel to the normal of the surface, that can occur in hard biological tissues such as nails, horns, etc. Next, a thermomechanical model is formulated and used to investigate the kinetics of freezing and melting in ice under complex stress states, particularly to capture regelation which is a key process in frost heave and basal slip in glaciers.

Introduction

Surface growth is the addition (accretion) or removal (ablation) of mass from the boundary of a solid body.
Numerous natural and engineering processes involve interactions between surface growth and stress, and this interaction plays a major role in both the mechanisms of surface growth as well as the development of stresses within the growing deformable solid body.
Notable examples include the melting and refreezing of glacial ice, where thermomechanical stresses give rise to crack (crevasse) propagation
[
32
]
and basal pressure melting and regelation
[
31
]
;
additive manufacturing, where thermomechanical stresses introduce residual stress and distortion in printed objects
[
37
]
;
electrochemical deposition in batteries, where instabilities and dendrite growth can be significantly influenced by external pressure
[
87
]
;
stress-induced growth of biological tissues
[
6
]
; and cell motility
[
72
]
.

A continuum description of surface growth requires a time-evolving set of material particles, in which both the current and reference configurations of the growing body are evolving in time.
This feature presents significant challenges both in terms of formulating the problem within the framework of continuum mechanics and in devising numerical methods to solve it.

Prior Work on Formulation of Surface Growth.

Solid mechanics typically uses the Lagrangian description, i.e., material particles are labeled by, and the deformation is computed using, the locations of the material particles in the reference configuration.
This description is convenient when the set of material particles remains constant, which is not the case when dealing with surface growth phenomena.
The extension of Lagrangian approaches to problems of surface growth typically include a time-evolving reference configuration, to incorporate the history of particle attachment to the object
[
12
,
45
,
79
,
78
,
1
,
70
]
.
This introduces significant complexity but does allow for the use of standard methods to compute the deformation gradient
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}
that is required for the stress response.
In the limit of infinitesimal deformations, the reference configuration can be approximated with the current configuration, making the formulation of surface growth less challenging
[
63
,
52
]
, but this is obviously limited in applicability.

An alternate approach to surface growth is the use of Eulerian-based approaches
[
65
,
40
]
, that work directly with the current configuration and bypass the direct definition of the reference configuration, e.g.
[
27
,
28
]
.
However, this raises the challenge of computing the deformation gradient
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}
— that is required for the stress response — when we do not have an explicit description of the reference configuration.
In our previous work, our approach to this problem was composed of 2 key elements
[
65
,
66
]
.
First, we use an evolution equation for
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}
that is posed in the current configuration, following
[
58
]
.
Second, we introduced a new kinematic descriptor, the
elastic deformation
,
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{e}
, that accounts for the part of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}
that causes stress.
Using these elements together, we can completely eliminate
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}
from the formulation in favor of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{e}
, where the time-evolution of the latter is governed by a transport-type PDE.
The key advantages of using
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{e}
are: first, it is not constrained to satisfy the compatibility constraint, unlike
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}
; and, second, for the material particles that are being attached to the body during surface growth, the reference configuration — and consequently
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}
— are not well-defined quantities, whereas the stress — and consequently
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{e}
— are well-defined.

All of these elements provide an approach wherein we can compute the stress and deformation of a growing body without explicitly computing or using the complex reference configuration.
Unlike previously-developed Lagrangian approaches, this approach is not limited by model-specific assumptions required for extending the reference configuration; a detailed comparison of this method with some of the available Lagrangian methods is discussed in
[

Contributions of This Paper.

In this paper, we focus on developing a fully-Eulerian FE framework based on the formulation developed in
[
65
]
to numerically simulate surface growth for arbitrary growth velocity, geometry, and stress state of the added material (but neglecting the effect of inertia).
We exploit the advantage of an Eulerian formulation to solve the governing equations on a fixed computational domain with a fixed discretization.
This requires the use of a domain larger than the initial body to contain the growing deformable solid body in the computational domain for the entire time interval of interest.
Therefore, we use a phase function to distinguish the solid body from the rest of the computational domain, following ideas that have been used extensively in the numerical simulation of multiphase problems, e.g.
[
67
,
81
,
47
]
.

We apply the numerical method to study 2 examples.
First, we demonstrate model simulations of “non-normal growth”
[
74
,
75
]
, observed in several hard biological tissues such as horn and nail.
Here, growth occurs at a fixed surface and the generating cells that push the growing material outward are not aligned with the normal vector of the surface
[
75
]
.
Hence, the growth of a stress-free material in a non-normal direction is observed.
However, conventional interface-based methods use only the normal interface velocity and cannot directly simulate non-normal growth; we use the elastic deformation to model non-normal growth by applying a pre-stress to the accreted material that drives shear as the pre-stress relaxes.

Second, we develop a thermomechanical extension of the phase-field model to study stress-induced melting and refreezing (i.e., regelation) in ice, based on ideas from
[
8
,
69
,
47
,
36
,
3
,
4
,
11
]
.
The typical approach to studying this phenomenon is by using the Clausius-Clapeyron relation, which relates the melting temperature to the hydrostatic pressure in the solid.
However, the Clausius-Clapeyron relation is difficult to incorporate in a consistent thermomechanical setting, wherein the stress is obtained from solving the field equation of momentum balance and is hence typically not hydrostatic
[
73
,
76
]
.
The free energy approach does not require the explicit use of the Clausius-Clapeyron relation, but it is instead an outcome of the formulation of the free energy, the balance laws, and a kinetic law.
This approach is consequently applicable to realistic problems with spatially-varying non-hydrostatic stress fields.
The problem of regelation also involves both fluid and solid phases with transformations between the phases in both directions; the deformation of the fluid phase would, in general settings, be exceptionally challenging for a Lagrangian formulation.

Formulation of the Growth Problem

We model the process of surface growth through the introduction of sources of mass and linear momentum that are localized on the growing surface, following
[

To define the source terms, we specify the rate of addition of mass per unit area
\displaystyle M

Accretion is modeled by

2.C
Constitutive Response

To solve the balance laws in Section
2.A

We use the framework of hyperelasticity, wherein the Cauchy stress tensor
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\sigma}
only depends on the deformation gradient
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}

\displaystyle\hat{W}({\mathbfit F})

\mathbfit{\sigma}=\rho\frac{\partial\hat{W}}{\partial{\mathbfit F}}{\mathbfit F}^{T}

Since the primary kinematic descriptor in this model is
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{e}

\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{e}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbfit{\sigma}=\rho\frac{\partial W}{\partial{\mathbfit F}_{e}}{\mathbfit F}_{e}^{T}

In all the problems considered in this paper, we use a compressible neo-Hookean material with the following strain energy function:

Numerical Method

In this section, we present the formulation of the numerical method.
Specifically, given the equilibrium density, elastic deformation, and the spatial domain occupied by the body at some discrete timestep, we aim to update these quantities, under the action of growth and mechanics, to their values at the next timestep.

Our method is implemented in Firedrake
[
44
]
, an open-source FE library, and the link to the code is provided at the end of the paper.
We use a continuous Galerkin polynomial interpolation for the displacement, elastic deformation, density, and phase function.
Although only 2-d examples are considered in this paper, the approach is readily extendable to 3-d.

This section is organized as follows:

•

In Section
3.A
, we provide definitions of the key quantities.

•

In Section
3.B
, we introduce a phase indicator function
\displaystyle\phi
that implicitly tracks the shape of the growing solid body, and discuss its evolution and an associated regularization scheme.

•

In Section
3.C
, we discuss the incremental solution of the quasistatic hyperelastic problem that represents the balance of momentum.

•

In Section
3.D
, we discuss the evolution equations for mass (
15
)-(
16
) and elastic deformation (
19
)-(
20
).
The overall approach is to split the equations into source and transport contributions and solve them separately
[
68
]
.

•

In Section
3.E
, we discuss the time-stepping scheme that accounts for both growth and mechanics.

•

In Section
3.F
, we summarize the overall method that brings together the components above.

3.A
Preliminary Definitions

The numerical method aims to evolve from one discrete time-step
\displaystyle t=t^{n}
to the next time-step
\displaystyle t=t^{n+1}:=t^{n}+\Delta t
.
Specifically, we are given the shape of the body, the spatial density distribution
\displaystyle\rho
, and the spatial elastic deformation distribution at equilibrium
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{e}
, and we describe how to find these quantities at the next time-step.
To perform this time-stepping, we decompose the evolution such that we solve
separately
for growth and mechanical equilibrium.
That is, we consider the evolution of these quantities driven exclusively by growth and driven exclusively by mechanics (Fig.
3a
).
When we consider evolution driven by growth, we focus exclusively on the accretion and ablation of material particles without considering that the system is not in mechanical equilibrium; and when we focus on mechanics, we keep the set of material particles fixed and solve for mechanical equilibrium using the balance of momentum.

This leads to the following definitions:

•

We denote by
\displaystyle\Omega^{r}
the reference configuration; while we never construct the reference numerically, it is useful for various closed-form calculations.
We denote the location of material particles in the reference by
\displaystyle{\mathbfit X}
.

•

We use
\displaystyle{\mathbfit x}
to denote spatial locations.

•

We denote by
\displaystyle\Omega^{n}
and
\displaystyle\Omega^{n+1}
the spatial domains occupied by the solid body at equilibrium at times
\displaystyle t^{n}
and
\displaystyle t^{n+1}
.
The spatial locations of the material particle located at
\displaystyle{\mathbfit X}
are
\displaystyle{\mathbfit x}^{n}
and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit x}^{n+1}
respectively.

Note that
\displaystyle\Omega^{n+1}
and
\displaystyle\Omega^{n}
are the regions occupied by the equilibrium configurations of the body at different times and they include different sets of material particles due to growth, so they are not simply the mapping of the body under the deformation.

•

We denote by
\displaystyle\Omega^{n}_{g}
the non-equilibrium intermediate configuration that arises from
\displaystyle\Omega^{n}
after growth has occurred but before mechanical equilibrium occurs.
The spatial location of the material particle located at
\displaystyle{\mathbfit X}
is
\displaystyle{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}
.

•

Traction boundary conditions are applied over the subset of the boundary denoted by
\displaystyle\Gamma_{t}^{r}
and
\displaystyle\Gamma_{t_{g}}^{n}
for the domains
\displaystyle\Omega^{r}
and
\displaystyle\Omega_{g}^{n}
respectively.

•

We are focused here on a single timestep
\displaystyle\Delta t
and assume that the velocity
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}
is approximately constant over this timestep.
Hence, the incremental displacement
\displaystyle{\mathbfit u}:={\mathbfit x}^{n+1}-{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}
is related to the velocity by
\displaystyle{\mathbfit u}\approx{\mathbfit v}\Delta t
.

•

Since we use a discretization that is fixed in space as the solid body deforms, we use a computational domain — that we denote
\displaystyle R
— that contains the entire body as well as some part of the surrounding medium, such that the growing regions are always contained with
\displaystyle R
.

Given
\displaystyle\Omega^{n}
,
\displaystyle\rho^{n}({\mathbfit x}):=\rho({\mathbfit x},t=t^{n})
, and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{e}^{n}({\mathbfit x}):={\mathbfit F}_{e}({\mathbfit x},t=t^{n})
, we develop a numerical algorithm that provides
\displaystyle\Omega^{n+1}
,
\displaystyle\rho^{n+1}({\mathbfit x}):=\rho({\mathbfit x},t=t^{n+1})
, and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{e}^{n+1}({\mathbfit x}):={\mathbfit F}_{e}({\mathbfit x},t=t^{n+1})
.

We define extensions of various quantities from the growing solid body to the entire domain
\displaystyle R
, for reasons of numerical convenience.
The phase-field
\displaystyle\phi
enables us to distinguish between the physically-relevant portion that is within the solid body, and the physically-irrelevant extensions.
For instance, we use a highly compliant linear elastic solid outside the physical body to provide smooth extensions of the displacement and mechanical velocity, and we use
\displaystyle\phi
to appropriately construct the position-dependent elastic energy density.

3.B
Phase Function

We introduce a phase field
\displaystyle\phi
that implicitly tracks the shape of the body, which is required to be able to apply the appropriate boundary conditions.
It is defined on
\displaystyle R
such that
\displaystyle\phi({\mathbfit x})\approx 1
if
\displaystyle{\mathbfit x}
is within the solid body and
\displaystyle\phi({\mathbfit x})\approx-1
if
\displaystyle{\mathbfit x}
is outside it, i.e., at time
\displaystyle t=t^{n}
:

\phi({\mathbfit x})\approx\begin{cases}1&\text{if }{\mathbfit x}\in\Omega^{n}\\
-1&\text{if }{\mathbfit x}\in R\setminus\Omega^{n}\end{cases}

(21)

For numerical convenience,
\displaystyle\phi
is defined as a smooth function transitioning from
\displaystyle-1
to
\displaystyle 1
, where the level set
\displaystyle\phi=0
represents the boundary of the region occupied by the growing body.
Further, the outward unit normal of the solid body is
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbfit n}=-\frac{\nabla\phi}{|\nabla\phi|}
.

For the evolution of
\displaystyle\phi
, we use a phase-field formulation that allows for arbitrary kinetics, following
[
13
,
3
,
4
,
21
,
41
,
22
]
:

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+{\mathbfit v}_{\phi}\cdot\nabla\phi=0

(22)

which represents the transport of level sets of
\displaystyle\phi
with velocity
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}_{\phi}
, as discussed in
[
3
]
.

We highlight some important features of (
22
).
First, the only level set of physical significance is that corresponding to
\displaystyle\phi=0
, which represents the boundary of the solid body.
Second,
\displaystyle\nabla\phi
is parallel to
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbfit n}
, and hence only the normal component of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}_{\phi}
is of significance.
Hence, we define
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}_{\phi}
such that it satisfies
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}_{\phi}={\mathbfit V}_{b}
on the level set
\displaystyle\phi=0
, but are free to define any smooth extension of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}_{\phi}
over
\displaystyle R
.
We can then write (
16
) as
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}_{\phi}={\mathbfit v}+{\mathbfit v}_{g}
on the level set
\displaystyle\phi=0
.
For
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}_{g}
, we use a smooth extension of the growth velocity to
\displaystyle R
, with the specific choice of extension being chosen case-by-case for each problem.
For
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}
, which is the mechanical velocity, it is already defined over the solid body, and is extended over
\displaystyle R
as described in Section
3.C.3
.

We can write (
22
) as:

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+({\mathbfit v}_{g}+{\mathbfit v})\cdot\nabla\phi=0

(23)

As mentioned in
3.A
, at each timestep we solve
separately
for mechanical equilibrium and growth.
Solving for mechanical equilibrium provides
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}
, and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}_{g}
is specified through additional growth physics.
We use a split-step method to evolve (
23
) in time, i.e., we use each velocity individually to propagate a partial time step.
The specifics of solving this transport equation follow Section
3.D.1
.

3.B.1
Regularization of the Phase Function

The thickness of the regularized boundary of the solid body, i.e. the transition region where
\displaystyle\phi
transitions from
\displaystyle-1
to
\displaystyle+1
, is important for the numerical results.
If the thickness is smaller than the mesh size, it is not properly captured, while if it is very thick, the physical problem is not well represented.
Further, even if we start with
\displaystyle\phi
having a moderate thickness, the evolution equation (
22
) will often flatten or sharpen
\displaystyle\phi
over time
[
67
]
.
Finally, it has been observed that
\displaystyle\phi
can potentially lose smoothness and regularity over time with the appearance of growing artificial oscillations.

We use the following approach to ensure the smoothness of
\displaystyle\phi
over time during numerical calculations
[
24
]
.
Let
\displaystyle\phi
denote the numerical solution of (
23
) at a given time step.
We apply the following variational filter to suppress potential oscillations and also prevent the interface from overly widening or sharpening:

\mathrm{Reg}[\bar{\phi}]=\int_{R}\left(\sigma_{\phi}\left(\bar{\phi}-\phi\right)^{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\bar{\phi}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\left(\bar{\phi}^{2}-1\right)^{2}\right)

(24)

Minimizing this functional provides a map
\displaystyle\phi\mapsto\bar{\phi}
with the following features: (1) the first term controls the distance between
\displaystyle\bar{\phi}
close to the physically-governed
\displaystyle\phi
; (2) the second term controls the interface smoothness to avoid spurious numerical oscillations; and (3) the third term controls the interface thickness.

\displaystyle\sigma_{\phi}
and
\displaystyle\epsilon
are regularization parameters that weight the terms
[
15
]
.

The numerical values of the regularization parameters are problem-dependent, and are listed in the description of the problems that we solve in later sections.

3.C
Momentum Balance through Quasistatic Incremental Hyperelasticity

We describe here how to find the displacement

\displaystyle{\mathbfit u}({\mathbfit x})={\mathbfit x}^{n+1}-{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}

to satisfy mechanical equilibrium, keeping fixed the set of material particles, i.e., without growth.

3.C.1
Kinematics

We begin by noting the useful identity:

\begin{split}&{\mathbfit x}^{n+1}({\mathbfit X})={\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}({\mathbfit X})+{\mathbfit u}\left({\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}\left({\mathbfit X}\right)\right)\\
\implies&\frac{\partial{\mathbfit x}^{n+1}}{\partial{\mathbfit X}}=\frac{\partial{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}}{\partial{\mathbfit X}}+\frac{\partial{\mathbfit u}}{\partial{\mathbfit X}}=\frac{\partial{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}}{\partial{\mathbfit X}}+\frac{\partial{\mathbfit u}}{\partial{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}}\frac{\partial{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}}{\partial{\mathbfit X}}\\
\implies&{\mathbfit F}_{e}^{n+1}{\mathbfit F}_{relax}^{n+1}={\mathbfit F}_{e_{g}}^{n}{\mathbfit F}^{n}_{relax_{g}}+\left(\nabla_{{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}}{\mathbfit u}\right){\mathbfit F}_{e_{g}}^{n}{\mathbfit F}^{n}_{relax_{g}}=\left({\mathbfit I}+\nabla_{{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}}{\mathbfit u}\right){\mathbfit F}_{e_{g}}^{n}{\mathbfit F}^{n}_{relax_{g}}\end{split}

(25)

where
\displaystyle\nabla_{{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}}
is gradient with respect to
\displaystyle{{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}}
.
Using that
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{relax}
does not evolve for a given material particle, it can be cancelled out to give:

{\mathbfit F}_{e}^{n+1}=\left({\mathbfit I}+\nabla_{{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}}{\mathbfit u}\right){\mathbfit F}_{e_{g}}^{n}

(26)

3.C.2
Incremental Variational Principle

Given the definition of
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\sigma}
for a hyperelastic material (
13
), the solution of the balance of momentum (
17
) and (
18
) in a quasistatic setting is equivalent to the minimization of the energy functional:

I\left[{\mathbfit x}^{n+1}\right]=\int_{\Omega^{r}}\hat{\rho}_{r}({\mathbfit X})\left(W\left({\mathbfit F}_{e}\left({\mathbfit x}^{n+1}\right)\right)-{\mathbfit b}\cdot{\mathbfit x}^{n+1}\right)\ \mathrm{d}{\mathbfit X}-\int_{\Gamma_{t}^{r}}{\mathbfit t}_{b_{r}}\cdot{\mathbfit x}^{n+1}\ \mathrm{d}{\mathbfit X}

(27)

where
\displaystyle{\mathbfit b}
,
\displaystyle{\mathbfit t}_{b_{r}}
, and
\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{r}
are the body force per unit mass, the traction at the boundary, and the density in the reference configuration
\displaystyle\Omega^{r}
.

We transform this to the configuration
\displaystyle\Omega_{g}^{n}
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle I\left[{\mathbfit x}^{n+1}\right]=\int_{\Omega_{g}^{n}}\tilde{\rho}_{r}\left({\mathbfit x}_{g}^{n}\right)\left(W\left({\mathbfit F}_{e}\left({\mathbfit x}^{n+1}\right)\right)-{\mathbfit b}\cdot{\mathbfit x}^{n+1}\right)\det\left(\frac{\partial{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}}{\partial{\mathbfit X}}\right)^{-1}\ \mathrm{d}{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}-\int_{\Gamma_{t_{g}}^{n}}{\mathbfit t}_{b}\cdot{\mathbfit x}^{n+1}\ \mathrm{d}{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}

(28)

where
\displaystyle{\mathbfit t}_{b}
is the traction in the configuration
\displaystyle\Omega_{g}^{n}
and equal to
\displaystyle\mu_{J}^{-1}{\mathbfit t}_{b_{r}}
with the area stretch
\displaystyle\mu_{J}
between the configurations
\displaystyle\Omega^{r}
and
\displaystyle\Omega_{g}^{n}

[
42
]
, and
\displaystyle\rho_{r}=\hat{\rho}_{r}({\mathbfit X})=\tilde{\rho}_{r}\left({\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}\right)
is the referential density.

We rewrite the energy functional (
28
) with respect to
\displaystyle{\mathbfit u}
instead of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit x}^{n+1}
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle I[{\mathbfit u}]=\int_{\Omega_{g}^{n}}\rho^{n}_{g}({\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g})\left(W\left(\left({\mathbfit I}+\nabla_{{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}}{\mathbfit u}\right){\mathbfit F}_{e_{g}}^{n}\left({\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}\right)\right)-{\mathbfit b}\cdot\left({\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}+{\mathbfit u}\right)\right)\ \mathrm{d}{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}-\int_{\Gamma_{t_{g}}^{n}}{\mathbfit t}_{b}\cdot\left({\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}+{\mathbfit u}\right)\ \mathrm{d}{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}

(29)

where we have defined
\displaystyle\rho^{n}_{g}({\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}):=\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{r}\left({\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}\right)}{\det{\mathbfit F}_{g}^{n}\left({\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}\right)}
.

When we use small increments in time, we have that
\displaystyle{\mathbfit u}
is small, and approximate
\displaystyle\nabla_{{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}}
by
\displaystyle\nabla
, where
\displaystyle\nabla
is the spatial gradient.
Therefore, to find
\displaystyle{\mathbfit u}
, we can minimize the incremental energy functional:

\displaystyle\displaystyle I[{\mathbfit u}]=\int_{\Omega_{g}^{n}}\rho_{g}^{n}({\mathbfit x})\left(W\left(\left({\mathbfit I}+\nabla{\mathbfit u}\right){\mathbfit F}_{e_{g}}^{n}\left({\mathbfit x}\right)\right)-{\mathbfit b}\cdot\left({\mathbfit x}+{\mathbfit u}\right)\right)\ \mathrm{d}{\mathbfit x}-\int_{\Gamma_{t_{g}}^{n}}{\mathbfit t}_{b}\cdot\left({\mathbfit x}+{\mathbfit u}\right)\ \mathrm{d}{\mathbfit x}

(30)

3.C.3
Extension to the Entire Computational Domain

A simple, yet effective, approach to extend
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}
beyond the solid body to the entire computational domain
\displaystyle R
is to consider that a very soft isotropic linear elastic solid occupies the rest of the computational domain
\displaystyle R\backslash\Omega_{s}
, with elastic energy density function per unit mass given by:

W_{\text{linear}}\left(\nabla{\mathbfit u}\right)=\frac{\mu_{c}}{4}\left|\nabla{\mathbfit u}+\left(\nabla{\mathbfit u}\right)^{T}\right|^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{c}}{2}\trace\left(\nabla{\mathbfit u}\right)

(31)

where
\displaystyle\mu_{c}
and
\displaystyle\lambda_{c}
are the Lame parameters with numerical values smaller by a factor
\displaystyle\approx 10^{-3}
than the physical solid.
From our numerical examples, we find that these values are sufficiently small that any numerical artifacts are negligible.

We solve (
30
) over
\displaystyle R
instead of
\displaystyle\Omega_{g}^{n}
, and use an elastic energy density of the form:

W\left(\left({\mathbfit I}+\nabla{\mathbfit u}\right){\mathbfit F}_{e_{g}}^{n}\right)=H_{l}(\phi({\mathbfit x}))W_{s}\left(\left({\mathbfit I}+\nabla{\mathbfit u}\right){\mathbfit F}_{e_{g}}^{n}\right)+(1-H_{l}(\phi({\mathbfit x})))W_{\text{linear}}\left(\nabla{\mathbfit u}\right)

(32)

where
\displaystyle W_{s}
is the elastic energy of the solid, and
\displaystyle H_{l}(\phi)
is a function that transitions rapidly and smoothly from
\displaystyle 0
to
\displaystyle 1
when the argument changes sign, such that
\displaystyle H_{l}(\phi=1)=1
and
\displaystyle H_{l}(\phi=-1)=0
.
Throughout this paper, we use
\displaystyle H_{l}(\phi)=\frac{1+\tanh(l\phi)}{2}
where
\displaystyle l
is a modeling constant prescribing the width of the transition zone.

3.C.4
Boundary Conditions

For boundaries with prescribed displacement that is constant in time, we use a computational domain such that those boundaries of the solid body coincide with the boundaries of the computational domain, and we treat them as in standard FE approaches.
While we do not consider problems in which the prescribed boundary evolves, the diffuse Nitsche method provides a promising approach to impose the displacement on the boundary
[
64
]
.

For boundaries with prescribed traction
\displaystyle{\mathbfit t}_{b}
, we use a computational domain such that these boundaries lie within the domain.
We then smear out the traction vector over the width of the interface and replace it with a body force equal to
\displaystyle{\mathbfit t}_{b}{|\nabla H_{l}(\phi)|}
using the following approximation
[
64
]
:

\int_{\Gamma_{t_{g}}^{n}}{\mathbfit t}_{b}\cdot\left({\mathbfit u}+{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}\right)\ \mathrm{d}{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}=\int_{R}{\mathbfit t}_{b}\cdot\left({\mathbfit u}+{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}\right)\delta_{\Gamma_{t_{g}}^{n}}\ \mathrm{d}V\approx\int_{R}{\mathbfit t}_{b}\cdot\left({\mathbfit u}+{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}\right){|\nabla H_{l}(\phi)|}\ \mathrm{d}V

(33)

where
\displaystyle\delta_{\Gamma_{t_{g}}^{n}}
is a Dirac mass supported on the boundary
\displaystyle\Gamma_{t_{g}}^{n}
.

3.D
Evolution of Elastic Deformation and Density

The evolution equations for density (
15
) and elastic deformation (
20
) have the common structure:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+{\mathbfit v}\cdot\nabla f=\cal{F}

(34)

where
\displaystyle f
can be
\displaystyle\rho
or
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{e}
.
Using a finite-difference approximation to the time derivative, we can split the equations to separate the source and transport contributions, and then solve these separately
[
68
]
.

3.D.1
Transport Contribution

We first turn to the transport contribution that is discretized in time using an explicit difference to give:

\frac{f^{n+1}-f^{n}_{g}}{\Delta t}+{\mathbfit v}\cdot\nabla f^{n}_{g}=0

(35)

Let
\displaystyle\cal T
denote a discretization of
\displaystyle R
, and
\displaystyle\bar{\mathbfit x}_{i}
denote the spatial location of the grid point indexed by
\displaystyle i
in
\displaystyle\cal T
.
For a fixed discretization,
\displaystyle\bar{\mathbfit x}_{i}
remains constant in time.
We denote the nodal values
\displaystyle f^{n}_{g}(\bar{\mathbfit x}_{i})
by
\displaystyle f_{g_{i}}^{n}
and
\displaystyle f^{n+1}(\bar{\mathbfit x}_{i})
by
\displaystyle f_{i}^{n+1}
.
Here, we describe how to evaluate
\displaystyle\left\{f\right\}^{n+1}
given
\displaystyle\left\{f\right\}^{n}_{g}
and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}(\bar{\mathbfit x}_{i})
.

Recall that
\displaystyle{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}
and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit x}^{n+1}
are the spatial locations of the material particle
\displaystyle{\mathbfit X}
in the configurations
\displaystyle\Omega^{n}_{g}
and
\displaystyle\Omega^{n+1}
.
Hence, we have:

f^{n+1}\left({\mathbfit x}^{n+1}\right)=f^{n}_{g}\left({\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}\right)

(36)

Using
\displaystyle{\mathbfit x}^{n}_{g}\approx{\mathbfit x}^{n+1}-{\mathbfit v}\left({\mathbfit x}^{n+1}\right)\Delta t
in the argument of the left side in the equation above gives:

f^{n+1}\left({\mathbfit x}^{n+1}\right)=f^{n}_{g}\left({\mathbfit x}^{n+1}-{\mathbfit v}\left({\mathbfit x}^{n+1}\right)\Delta t\right)

(37)

Setting
\displaystyle{\mathbfit x}^{n+1}=\bar{\mathbfit x}_{i}
gives:

f_{i}^{n+1}=f^{n}_{g}\left(\bar{\mathbfit x}_{i}-{\mathbfit v}\left(\bar{\mathbfit x}_{i}\right)\Delta t\right)

(38)

Since
\displaystyle\bar{\mathbfit x}_{i}-{\mathbfit v}(\bar{\mathbfit x}_{i})\Delta t
does not generally lie on any mesh point, we use interpolation to evaluate
\displaystyle f^{n}_{g}\left(\bar{\mathbfit x}_{i}-{\mathbfit v}(\bar{\mathbfit x}_{i})\Delta t\right)
inside an element containing
\displaystyle\bar{\mathbfit x}_{i}-{\mathbfit v}(\bar{\mathbfit x}_{i})\Delta t
.
The case when
\displaystyle\bar{\mathbfit x}_{i}-{\mathbfit v}(\bar{\mathbfit x}_{i})\Delta t
is outside
\displaystyle R
is discussed in the examples below.

We note that an explicit approach does not require the solution of a system of nonlinear algebraic equations and is computationally efficient while maintaining stability and accuracy, as we see in the examples.

3.D.2
Source Contribution

Elastic Deformation.

We use implicit Euler time discretization to write:

\frac{{\mathbfit F}_{e}^{n+1}({\mathbfit x})-{\mathbfit F}_{e_{g}}^{n}({\mathbfit x})}{\Delta t}=\left(\nabla{\mathbfit v}\right){\mathbfit F}_{e}^{n+1}({\mathbfit x})\implies({\mathbfit I}-\nabla{\mathbfit u}){\mathbfit F}_{e}^{n+1}({\mathbfit x})={\mathbfit F}_{e_{g}}^{n}({\mathbfit x})

(39)

For small
\displaystyle\nabla{\mathbfit u}
, we can use the approximation
\displaystyle\left({\mathbfit I}-\nabla{\mathbfit u}\right)^{-1}\approx\left({\mathbfit I}+\nabla{\mathbfit u}\right)
to write:

{\mathbfit F}_{e}^{n+1}({\mathbfit x})=({\mathbfit I}+\nabla{\mathbfit u}){\mathbfit F}_{e_{g}}^{n}({\mathbfit x}).

(40)

Density.

We use implicit Euler time discretization to write:

\frac{\rho^{n+1}({\mathbfit x})-\rho_{g}^{n}\left({\mathbfit x}\right)}{\Delta t}=-\left(\divergence{\mathbfit v}\right)\rho^{n+1}\left({\mathbfit x}\right)\implies\left(1+\divergence{\mathbfit u}\right)\rho^{n+1}\left({\mathbfit x}\right)=\rho_{g}^{n}({\mathbfit x})

(41)

For small
\displaystyle\nabla{\mathbfit u}
, we can use the approximation
\displaystyle\left(1+\divergence{\mathbfit u}\right)\approx\det\left({\mathbfit I}+\nabla{\mathbfit u}\right)
to write:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rho^{n+1}({\mathbfit x})=\frac{\rho_{g}^{n}({\mathbfit x})}{\det\left({\mathbfit I}+\nabla{\mathbfit u}\right)}

(42)

We note that although we use an implicit approach, our linearization enables us to bypass solving a system of algebraic equations, making the implicit scheme computationally efficient.

3.D.3
Extension to the Entire Computational Domain

To obtain a smooth extension of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{e}
and
\displaystyle\rho
over
\displaystyle R
, we simply use that we have considered the entirety of
\displaystyle R
to be occupied by a solid body following Section
3.C.3
.
Consequently, all quantities associated with the physical solid body are also extended smoothly to the soft surrounding solid, using an interpolation of the form in (
32
).

3.E
Time-stepping with Growth and Mechanics

We discuss two different physical situations which must be distinguished in the numerical method.
With reference to Figure
3
, the top row shows growth occurring at a boundary at which the displacement is not prescribed, which we denote “Unconstrained Growth”, and the bottom row shows growth occurring at a boundary at which the displacement is prescribed, which we denote “Constrained Growth”.
In the notation of Section
2.D
, Constrained Growth corresponds to the case where there is nontrivial
\displaystyle\Gamma_{u}\cap\Gamma_{g}
.
Specifically, if growth occurs at portions of the boundary where the displacement is prescribed, we must take care to ensure that both the specified growth velocity conditions and the displacement boundary conditions are satisfied.
We first discuss the case of Unconstrained Growth.

(a)
Unconstrained Growth

(b)
Constrained Growth

(b) Constrained Growth: growth occurs at a boundary where the displacement is prescribed, thereby constraining the relation between the boundary velocity, growth velocity, and mechanical velocity; the figure shows the case that
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}_{g}
and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}
are coupled through
\displaystyle{\mathbfit V}_{b}=\mathbf{0}
.
During growth, we do not consider mechanics, and during mechanical relaxation, we do not consider growth.
In
\displaystyle\Omega_{g}^{n}
, the region that is not shaded consists of material from the previous time-step
\displaystyle t=t^{n}
, the region shaded orange is the accreted material with
\displaystyle\rho=\hat{\rho}_{a},{\mathbfit F}_{e}=\hat{\mathbfit F}_{e}^{*}
, and the region shaded grey is the ablated material.

3.E.1
Unconstrained Growth

We recall the decomposition in (
16
) that
\displaystyle{\mathbfit V}_{b}\cdot\hat{\mathbfit n}={\mathbfit v}_{g}\cdot\hat{\mathbfit n}+{\mathbfit v}\cdot\hat{\mathbfit n}
on
\displaystyle\partial\Omega_{s}
, i.e., the motion of the boundary is the superposition of the growth velocity
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}_{g}
and the material velocity
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}
.
The case of Unconstrained Growth is straightforward: when
\displaystyle{\mathbfit V}_{b}
is not prescribed, we can compute the shape of the body due to growth using the prescribed growth velocity
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}_{g}
, and then use mechanical equilibrium with this added material to find
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}
.

Within a single time-step, we perform the following calculations, referring to Figure

1.

We evolve
\displaystyle\phi

2.

3.

\displaystyle\phi
,
\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{e}

The evolution of
\displaystyle\phi
in this step provides the change in shape of the body due to mechanical relaxation.

3.E.2
Constrained Growth

In the case of Constrained Growth, both
\displaystyle{\mathbfit V}_{b}
and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}_{g}
are prescribed on some parts of the boundary.
Hence,
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}\cdot\hat{\mathbfit n}={\mathbfit V}_{b}\cdot\hat{\mathbfit n}-{\mathbfit v}_{g}\cdot\hat{\mathbfit n}
is constrained.
To deal with this constraint, we perform the following calculations in a single time-step (Fig.
3
(b)).

1.

We solve for mechanical equilibrium of the body without growth, using a displacement boundary condition that violates the prescribed displacement but “makes room” for the ablation and accretion of material.
That is, we use the boundary condition corresponding to
\displaystyle{\mathbfit V}_{b}-{\mathbfit v}_{g}
prescribed, rather than
\displaystyle{\mathbfit V}_{b}
prescribed, on the relevant part of the boundary when we solve for mechanical equilibrium.

2.

We use the mechanical velocity
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}
that is computed from mechanical relaxation in the previous step to transport
\displaystyle\phi
,
\displaystyle\rho
,
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{e}
.
The evolution of
\displaystyle\phi
in this step provides the change in shape of the body due to mechanical relaxation.

3.

We “fill in” the ablation and accretion of material using
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}_{g}
, which restores the required displacement boundary condition that
\displaystyle{\mathbfit V}_{b}
is prescribed.

4.

We solve for mechanical equilibrium of the body with the ablated and accreted material, with the displacement-prescribed boundary conditions, to account for the deformation due to the added material.
This provides the displacement of the body due to mechanical relaxation.

5.

We use the mechanical velocity
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}
that is computed from mechanical relaxation in the previous step to transport
\displaystyle\phi
,
\displaystyle\rho
,
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{e}
.
The evolution of
\displaystyle\phi
in this step provides the change in shape of the body due to mechanical relaxation.

In the growth of hard biological tissues such as horns and nails, growth occurs at a fixed surface, and the generating cells that push the growing material outward might not be aligned with the normal vector of the surface

5
Application to Regelation: Stress-driven Melting and Refreezing

Stress-induced melting occurs when a solid, at a constant temperature that is below the melting temperature, undergoes a phase transition into the liquid state due to the application of external load; once the load is removed, the liquid refreezes.
This phenomenon is called regelation and occurs when the solid state density is different from that of its liquid state.
A well-known illustration of this phenomenon is hexagonal ice — the most abundant ice on the earth — whose density is approximately
\displaystyle 10\%
lower than that of water, and which is often not too far below the melting temperature in environmental settings.
Understanding the dynamics of the stressed ice and water interface is important for various environmental mechanics issues
[
33
]
such as glaciology
[
85
]
and frost heave analysis in soil, permafrost, and infrastructure
[
53
]
, as well as cryobiology
[
82
]
.

Unlike the model problems considered in Section
4
, here we do not arbitrarily specify the growth velocity and properties of the accreted material.
Instead, we obtain it using thermodynamics, by formulating a thermomechanical free energy and applying the Coleman-Noll procedure to construct an evolution equation that is consistent with the laws of thermomechanics
[
42
]
3
3

3

We assume that the temperature does not evolve in time, and hence do not need to solve the energy equation explicitly beyond its role in the Coleman-Noll analysis.
.
Specifically, we obtain the growth velocity as a function of temperature
\displaystyle T
, elastic deformation
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{e}
, and the state of the system, by identifying the thermomechanical driving force for the melting/freezing process and then assuming a linear kinetic relation between the driving force and growth velocity
4
4

4

We highlight that the linear kinetic relation is sufficient but not necessary for consistency with the Coleman-Noll procedure.
.
We demonstrate the thermomechanical model and the numerical method by simulating a famous demonstration of regelation: the passage of a weighted wire through a block of ice without splitting it
[
19
]
.

5.A
Thermomechanical Model

We use
\displaystyle\phi=-1
and
\displaystyle\phi=+1
to denote the solid ice state and the liquid melt state respectively.
All derivatives, such as gradient and divergence operators, are with respect to spatial coordinates.

5.A.1
Equilibrium Free Energy

The Helmholtz free energy is assumed to be composed additively of thermal energy, elastic energy, and interfacial energy.

Thermal energy

Building on
[
69
]
, we use the following function for the thermal contribution to the free energy:

\hat{f}_{Th}(T,\phi)=c_{p}T\log\left({\frac{T_{m}^{0}}{T}}\right)+H_{l}(\phi)L\frac{T_{m}^{0}-T}{T_{m}^{0}}

(43)

where
\displaystyle L
,
\displaystyle c_{p}
, and
\displaystyle T_{m}^{0}
are, respectively, the latent heat, heat capacity, and stress-free melting temperature, and are all assumed to be constants; and
\displaystyle H_{l}(\phi)
is a smooth version of the sign function such that
\displaystyle H_{l}(1)=1
and
\displaystyle H_{l}(-1)=0
.

Elastic Energy

We use the compressible neo-Hookean form (
14
) to model the elastic energies of the solid
\displaystyle W_{\text{solid}}
and the melt
\displaystyle W_{\text{melt}}
, with the shear modulus of the melt being very small but not completely zero for numerical well-posedness.
Note that the neo-Hookean form in (
14
) is minimized when the argument is the identity tensor
\displaystyle{\mathbfit I}
.
To account for the change in density due to the phase transition in terms of the elastic deformation, we redefine the stress-free state appropriately, and write the elastic free energy density as:

\hat{f}_{El}({\mathbfit F}_{e},\phi)=(1-H_{l}(\phi))W_{\text{solid}}\left(\left(\frac{\rho^{\text{solid}}}{\rho^{\text{melt}}}\right)^{1/d}{\mathbfit F}_{e}\right)+H_{l}(\phi)W_{\text{melt}}\left({\mathbfit F}_{e}\right)

(44)

where
\displaystyle d
is the space dimension that we are working in.

To understand (
44
), we notice that when
\displaystyle\phi=+1
corresponding to the liquid state, the first term on the right vanishes, and the argument of
\displaystyle W_{\text{melt}}
evaluates to
\displaystyle{\mathbfit I}
when
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{e}={\mathbfit I}
.
That is, in the liquid state, the minimum energy state is when the deformation is the identity (or any rotation); further, the shear modulus is very small, so essentially any volume-preserving deformation is a state of minimum energy.
When
\displaystyle\phi=-1
, corresponding to the solid state, the second term on the right vanishes, and the argument of
\displaystyle W_{\text{solid}}
evaluates to
\displaystyle{\mathbfit I}
when
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{e}=\left(\frac{\rho^{\text{solid}}}{\rho^{\text{melt}}}\right)^{-1/d}{\mathbfit I}
.
Hence, an isotropic volume change is the state of minimum energy.

We highlight here that we could consider the process of phase transition as a change in the stress-free deformation
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{relax}
.
However, this would lead to a more complex evolution law for
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{e}
as well as require us to formulate the evolution of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{relax}
.
The strategy above has essentially enabled us to use a constant
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}_{relax}
and pose the phase transition process in terms of a change in the elastic energy.

Interfacial energy

As in Section
3.B.1
, to ensure the regularity of the interface for numerical purposes, we consider the following interfacial energy, consistent with the phase field model described in
[
69
]
:

\hat{f}_{In}(\nabla\phi,\phi)=\kappa_{1}|\nabla\phi|^{2}+\kappa_{2}(\phi^{2}-1)^{2}

(45)

where
\displaystyle\kappa_{1}
and
\displaystyle\kappa_{2}
are small positive modeling constants.

The Helmholtz free energy is then:

\begin{split}\hat{f}(T,{\mathbfit F}_{e},\nabla\phi,\phi)&=\hat{f}_{Th}(T,\phi)+\hat{f}_{El}({\mathbfit F}_{e},\phi)+\hat{f}_{In}(\nabla\phi,\phi)\\
&=c_{p}T\log\left({\frac{T_{m}^{0}}{T}}\right)+H_{l}(\phi)L\frac{T_{m}^{0}-T}{T_{m}^{0}}+(1-H_{l}(\phi))W_{\text{solid}}\left(\left(\frac{\rho^{\text{solid}}}{\rho^{\text{melt}}}\right)^{1/d}{\mathbfit F}_{e}\right)\\
&\qquad+H_{l}(\phi)W_{\text{melt}}\left({\mathbfit F}_{e}\right)+\kappa_{1}|\nabla\phi|^{2}+\kappa_{2}(\phi^{2}-1)^{2}\end{split}

(46)

5.A.2
Kinetics of Melting and Freezing

The evolution of
\displaystyle\phi
for a given material point due to phase transition occurs by the motion of the ice-water interface, and is modeled using:

\dot{\phi}+{\mathbfit v}_{g}\cdot\nabla\phi=0

(47)

where
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}_{g}
is the interface velocity.
Note that the material time derivative in Eulerian form is
\displaystyle\dot{\phi}:=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+{\mathbfit v}\cdot\nabla\phi
, and hence (
47
) is identical to (
23
).
Further, with the energy formulated as in (
46
), the right side of (
47
) being
\displaystyle 0
corresponds to suppressing the nucleation of phases; only existing interfaces are allowed to propagate
[
3
]
.

We now find a form for
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}_{g}
using the laws of thermomechanics.
Neglecting heat sources, the first and second laws of thermodynamics can be written as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rho\dot{e}=-\divergence{\mathbfit q}+\mathbfit{\sigma}:\nabla{\mathbfit v}+\divergence\left({\mathbfit t}_{\phi}\dot{\phi}\right)

(48)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rho\dot{s}\geq-\frac{\divergence{\mathbfit q}}{T}+\frac{{}{\mathbfit q}\cdot\nabla T}{T^{2}}

(49)

where
\displaystyle s
is entropy per unit mass;
\displaystyle{\mathbfit q}
is heat flux;
\displaystyle e
is internal energy per unit mass.
While most terms above are standard, e.g.
[
42
]
, we introduce a non-classical work contribution to account for the non-classical phase-field variable.
Specifically, we introduce
\displaystyle t_{\phi}={\mathbfit t}_{\phi}\cdot{\mathbfit n}
as the work conjugate of
\displaystyle\phi
at the boundary, which appears above as
\displaystyle\divergence\left({\mathbfit t}_{\phi}\dot{\phi}\right)
.

Introducing the free energy
\displaystyle f=e-Ts
and using
\displaystyle f=\hat{f}(T,{\mathbfit F}_{e},\phi,\nabla\phi)
, we follow the classical Coleman-Noll procedure
[
42
]
, with appropriate extensions for the presence of
\displaystyle\phi
and
\displaystyle\nabla\phi
, to arrive at the inequality:

\rho\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial T}+s\right)\dot{T}+\left(\rho\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi}-\divergence{\mathbfit t}_{\phi}\right)\dot{\phi}+\left(\rho\frac{\partial f}{\partial\nabla\phi}-{\mathbfit t}_{\phi}\right)\cdot\nabla\dot{\phi}+\left(\rho\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\mathbfit F}_{e}}-\rho\left(\nabla\phi\otimes\frac{\partial f}{\partial\nabla\phi}\right){\mathbfit F}_{e}^{-T}-\mathbfit{\sigma}{\mathbfit F}_{e}^{-T}\right):\dot{\mathbfit F}_{e}+\frac{{\mathbfit q}\cdot\nabla T}{T}\leq 0

(50)

We require each of the five terms in (
50
) to satisfy the inequality independently.
The fifth term gives the classical constraint on
\displaystyle{\mathbfit q}
, but we assume that
\displaystyle{\mathbfit q}\equiv\mathbf{0}
and that the spatial temperature field is constant in time.
Setting the first, third, and fourth terms to
\displaystyle 0
identically gives:

\displaystyle\displaystyle s

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial f}{\partial T}

(51)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbfit{\sigma}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\rho\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\mathbfit F}_{e}}{\mathbfit F}_{e}^{T}-\rho\left(\nabla\phi\otimes\frac{\partial f}{\partial\nabla\phi}\right)

(52)

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\mathbfit t}_{\phi}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\rho\frac{\partial f}{\partial\nabla\phi}

(53)

Using (
51
) and
\displaystyle f=e-Ts
, we obtain the relation
\displaystyle e=c_{p}T+H_{l}(\phi)L
which relates the specific heat and latent heat to the internal energy
[
69
]
; (
52
) is the standard hyperelastic definition of the Cauchy stress tensor (
13
) with an interfacial contribution
5
5

5

The interfacial energy contains spatial derivatives of
\displaystyle\phi
, which leads to a dependence on the deformation gradient and hence the appearance of interface terms in the expression for the stress.

; and (
53
) provides an expression for the work-conjugate to
\displaystyle\phi
.

Finally, we are left with the second term in (
50
):

\left(\rho\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi}-\divergence\left(\rho\frac{\partial f}{\partial\nabla\phi}\right)\right){\mathbfit v}_{g}\cdot\nabla\phi\geq 0

(54)

where we have substituted for
\displaystyle\dot{\phi}
from (
47
).
Consequently, we use the following choice for
\displaystyle{\mathbfit v}_{g}
, that is sufficient — but not necessary — for consistency with thermodynamics:

{\mathbfit v}_{g}=\kappa\frac{\nabla\phi}{|\nabla\phi|}\left(\rho\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi}-\divergence\left(\rho\frac{\partial f}{\partial\nabla\phi}\right)\right)

(55)

where
\displaystyle\kappa>0
is a constant.

Evaluating explicitly the variational derivative of
\displaystyle\phi
— also denoted as the driving force on
\displaystyle\phi
— from (
55
), by using the expression for
\displaystyle f
from (
46
), we have:

\begin{split}&\left(\rho\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi}-\divergence\left(\rho\frac{\partial f}{\partial\nabla\phi}\right)\right)\\
&\qquad=\rho L\frac{T_{m}^{0}-T}{T_{m}^{0}}H^{\prime}_{l}(\phi)+\rho H^{\prime}_{l}(\phi)\left(W_{\text{melt}}\left({\mathbfit F}_{e}\right)-W_{\text{solid}}\left(\left(\frac{\rho^{\text{solid}}}{\rho^{\text{melt}}}\right)^{1/d}{\mathbfit F}_{e}\right)\right)-2\kappa_{1}\divergence\left(\rho\nabla\phi\right)+4\rho\kappa_{2}\phi(\phi^{2}-1)\end{split}

(56)

We notice that the first 3 terms on the right are localized at the interface, and hence do not drive nucleation, while the last term is decoupled from the deformation and also does not drive nucleation
[
3
]
; the model therefore allows phase transitions exclusively by the propagation of existing interfaces.

is only non-zero around the interface, and hence this body force approximates a traction force applied at the interface.

We prescribe the displacement to be
\displaystyle\mathbf{0}
at the bottom face; prescribe the horizontal displacement to be
\displaystyle 0
and the vertical traction to be
\displaystyle 0
on the left and right faces; and prescribe the traction to be
\displaystyle\mathbf{0}
at the top face.
When performing the transport update (Section
3.D.1
), the situations in which we require information from outside
\displaystyle R
occur only at the top face and we use the value of the nearest grid point at the boundary.

We note some aspects of the numerical method specific to this problem:

•

There is no growth on any of the boundaries on which the displacement is prescribed, and hence the growth is unconstrained.
To smoothly extend the growth velocity over the computational domain
\displaystyle R
, we simply apply the expression in (
55
) over
\displaystyle R
since the quantities in that expression are defined everywhere in
\displaystyle R
.

•

From (
47
), (
55
), (
56
), we have that the evolution of
\displaystyle\phi
is governed by the equation:

\dot{\phi}+\kappa\left(\rho\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi}-\divergence\left(\rho\frac{\partial f}{\partial\nabla\phi}\right)\right)|\nabla\phi|=0

(58)

The driving force contains higher-order derivatives that cannot be handled by using a weak form as in standard FEM.
Consequently, we used a mixed FEM approach.
We first evaluate
\displaystyle M:=|\nabla\phi|
at the beginning of each time-step, and then use an implicit Euler time discretization scheme to solve:

\dot{\phi}+\kappa\left(\rho\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi}-\divergence\left(\rho\frac{\partial f}{\partial\nabla\phi}\right)\right)M=0

(59)

•

Since (
58
) includes higher derivatives that ensure the smoothness of the interface, we do not apply the filtering described in Section
3.B.1
.

•

We specify the density and elastic deformation of the accreted material — i.e., the refreezing ice — assuming that these are identical to the properties of melt immediately before accretion.

6
Concluding Remarks

We develop a numerical method to model surface growth using an Eulerian description, following the formulation developed in
[
65
,
66
]
.
This formulation allows the modeling of surface growth without explicitly referring to an unknown time-dependent reference configuration.
We use the phase-field method to enable the use of a fixed discretization within a fixed computational domain for solving the equations numerically.
In contrast to Lagrangian descriptions of surface growth, the method proposed in this paper is expected to provide several potential advantages for generality, efficiency, and robustness; however, we highlight that comparing the efficiency and robustness systematically in comparison to other methods is an important future goal.

First, the method in this paper does not impose restrictions on the form of the growth velocity and the stress state of the added material.
This enables the direct application of the phenomenon of non-normal growth, observed in biological tissues
[
75
]
,
Second, it potentially enables the study of the important problem of growth-induced instabilities due to non-uniform growth from a fixed surface, e.g.
[
10
,
9
,
23
]
.
Third, using a fixed discretization potentially provides a robust alternative to other available methods which require a moving mesh, re-meshing, and/or extending the computational domain as the computation proceeds.
Fourth, it enables the simulation of the transformation between fluid and solid phases, which is challenging for purely Lagrangian methods that are unsuited for modeling fluids, as well as for Eulerian/Lagrangian hybrid methods that cannot handle the transformation between fluid and solid.

We first applied the method to study non-normal growth observed in some hard biological tissues.
While non-normal growth is not possible to model with typical interface-based methods that use only the normal interfacial velocity, we are able to do this by prescribing the elastic deformation of the accreted material.
Specifically, if the accreted material is pre-stressed at the instant of accretion, it will lead to effective non-normal growth as the pre-stresses relaxes at mechanical equilibrium.
In our work, as a first step we considered only uniform growth at a flat boundary.
However, non-uniform growth generates residual stresses in the system that can lead to the development of instabilities at the top surface
[
9
,
84
,
10
]
, and are an important topic for further work.

We next developed a thermomechanical model for first-order phase transformations to describe the freezing and melting of ice under mechanical and thermal loads.
The model does not directly specify the Clausius-Clapeyron relation; instead, this relation emerges as an outcome of a thermomechanical free energy formulation that accounts for general stress states.
While we apply it to the stress-induced melting and refreezing of ice, the overall structure of the model is readily adaptable to various other settings, including ferroelectric
[
17
]
and ferromagnetic
[
18
]
phase transformations, where the extension of the Clausius-Clapeyron relation to general stress states is important
[
50
,
49
,
8
]
.
The problem of regelation also involves both fluid and solid phases with transformations in both directions; the deformation of the fluid phase would, in general settings, be exceptionally challenging for a Lagrangian formulation.

In addition to the setting of surface growth, the proposed numerical method can be used to study the mechanics of non-growing solid bodies undergoing extreme deformations where Lagrangian methods perform poorly, e.g.
[
14
]
.
Specifically, instead of using a moving mesh as in typical Lagrangian and Lagrangian/Eulerian approaches
[
16
]
, transporting the material properties on a fixed discretization following an Eulerian approach is expected to improve the numerical performance by avoiding mesh entanglements under large deformations.
It would also be of interest to apply the Eulerian approach to multiphysics problems, such as poromechanics
[
29
,
26
,
54
]
and fracture
[
25
,
30
,
5
,
43
,
86
]
.

Our approach to solving the transport equations that govern the evolution of the density, elastic deformation, and phase-field used a relatively cumbersome procedure that required the identification of the finite element that contains the transported material point.
While very easy to implement in FEniCS, it requires working directly with nodal values as well as special procedures to handle points that lie outside the computational domain.
An alternative for future work is the Discontinuous Galerkin FEM, that provides an elegant and promising approach that is potentially more efficient
[
48
]
.
Similarly, we use a standard FEM formulation that treats the displacement as the primary unknown to solve the incremental equations of hyperelasticity for mechanical equilibrium, but the high elastic contrasts and large deformations could potentially be more accurately and efficiently evaluated by sophisticated mixed approaches following the promising studies in
[
51
]
.
