INTS1 is required for maintaining accurate transcriptional integrity and behavior in zebrafish
Authors: Shir Confino, Yair Wexler, Lior Fishman, Yoav Gothilf, Michal Rabani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrecise regulation of RNA polymerase II (RNAPII) is fundamental to transcriptional fidelity in eukaryotes. The Integrator complex, a conserved multi-subunit assembly, safeguards transcriptional integrity through its interaction with RNAPII, mediating transcription initiation and termination by nascent RNA cleavage and 3′ end processing. INTS1, the largest subunit of the Integrator complex, is critical for its function, and its mutations are linked to human neurodevelopmental disorders, yet the underlying transcriptional effects remain poorly defined. Here, we characterize the global transcriptional effects of INTS1 deficiency in zebrafish. INTS1 loss leads to widespread gene expression changes, including genes linked to hyperactivity and pathways associated with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. We observe mutant-specific first intron retentions and transcript extensions. These findings underscore a central role for INTS1 in coordinating transcriptional regulation and highlight the essential function of the Integrator complex in maintaining transcriptome integrity. Together, they offer insights into how INTS1 disruption may contribute to behavioral and physiological abnormalities.

Introduction

Accurate control of RNA polymerase II (RNAPII) is essential for regulating transcription in metazoans. The Integrator complex plays a crucial role in ensuring transcriptional integrity through its interaction with RNAPII [1–5]. This evolutionarily conserved complex, composed of multiple subunits, mediates cleavage of nascent coding and non-coding RNAs and controls the pause-release machinery of transcription [6–11].

The transcriptional effects of Integrator complex dysfunction have been studied in several model organisms, including Drosophila melanogaster, the planarian Schmidtea mediterranea, and the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, by utilizing RNAi approaches and transcriptome analyses. These revealed a role for the Integrator complex in small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs) and piRNA 3′ processing [12–17]. Nevertheless, the Integrator complex shows variation in subunit composition across model organisms compared to vertebrates. Specifically, Drosophila lack the INTS6l subunit [18], while C. elegans [16] lack the INTS14 subunit. Such differences make it unclear to what extent insights from these systems can be generalized to vertebrates. Functionally, in vertebrate systems such as mouse models, Integrator was shown to regulate the differentiation of adipocytes, hematopoiesis, and cortical neuron migration [19–21], suggesting broad developmental functions. Studies in cultured cells have increasingly recognized its roles in 3′ end processing of coding and non-coding RNAs and termination of promoter-proximally paused RNAPII [3, 4, 11, 22], yet in vivo research assessing its effects on transcription in a vertebrate species is lacking. The core and largest subunit of the complex, INTS1, is highly conserved across vertebrates [3, 23]. In humans, mutations in INTS1 are associated with neurodevelopmental defects including intellectual disability with lack of verbal communication and disrupted sleep–wake cycles [24–27]. Targeted disruption of the Ints1 gene in mice leads to embryonic lethality during the early blastocyst stage [24, 28], limiting research on the role of INTS1 in vertebrates. Conversely, zebrafish deficient in INTS1 survive through the larval stage, develop all organs and systems, and exhibit behavioral changes [20, 22], making them a valuable model for investigating the functions of INTS1 and the Integrator complex in vertebrates and INTS1-related diseases. Studies of the Integrator complex in zebrafish have demonstrated a role for INTS6 in confining the dorsal organizer during embryogenesis [29] and confirmed the role of INTS1 in lens development [24]. We recently demonstrated the impact of INTS1 deficiency on sleep regulation and general activity, supposedly via changes in the locus coeruleus [27], a subcortical nucleus that plays a crucial role in regulating sleep and arousal [30, 31].

In the current study, we explore the global transcriptional consequences of INTS1 deficiency on coding mRNA expression in vertebrates, using our INTS1-deficient zebrafish model. We focus on 7 days post-fertilization (dpf) larvae, a developmental stage when all major internal organs are established, including a fully differentiated brain. We demonstrate that INTS1 deficiency leads to changes in gene expression levels, including genes associated with a hyperactive phenotype of the INTS1-deficient fish, and to mutant-specific first intron retention and extensions in 3′ and 5′ transcript ends. Thus, INTS1, and hence the Integrator complex, is globally crucial for proper transcription and mRNA processing, essentially affecting a plethora of physiological systems and behavior.

Materials and methods

Fish and embryo maintenance

Adult heterozygous fish for the Ints1 loss-of-function mutations were raised and maintained in system water at 28°C under a light–dark (LD) cycle. Breeding was achieved by placing males and females in a tank with a partition the evening before spawning, which was removed when the lights were turned on the following day. Eggs were collected and placed in Petri dishes, with 40 eggs per dish, containing embryo water with methylene blue (0.3 ppm) to prevent contamination. All husbandry and experimental procedures were approved by the Tel Aviv University Animal Care Committee (permit number TAU-LS-IL-2207–171-2) and conducted in accordance with the regulations of the National Council for Animal Experimentations.

Establishing a CRISPR-generated zebrafish line carrying INTS1 loss-of-function

The zebrafish line used in this study was generated using CRISPR/Cas9 technology that carries an 11-bp deletion mutation in the 7th exon of the INTS1 gene. This deletion causes a frameshift after 358 amino acids, resulting in the introduction of an early stop codon after 32 out-of-frame amino acids. This INTS1-deficient zebrafish line is registered at Zebrafish Model Organism Database (ZFIN) under the designation Ints1tlv13. For a comprehensive description of the generation of this line, please refer to Confino et al. [27]

Larval locomotor activity assays

For the detection of larval locomotor activity, the DanioVision observation chamber (Noldus Information Technology, Wageningen, Netherlands) was used. At 6-h post-fertilization (hpf), fertilized eggs were placed in a 48-well plate containing embryo water, then transferred to the DanioVision observation chamber under dark conditions and with water temperature of 28°C. At 120 hpf, larvae were exposed to a 2-h light pulse (980 lux), followed by 4.5 more days in darkness. The data presented begin 24 h after the light pulse and continue for an additional 3 days. Post-experiment DNA extraction was conducted for each larva, followed by genotype determination using PCR amplification and enzymatic digestion of the amplicon. The data collected with EthoVision 15.0 software (Noldus Information Technology, Wageningen, Netherlands) were analyzed to measure the distance moved by the larvae per time interval (cm/10 min). Calculations of total locomotor activity and phase were carried out as previously described [32–34].

Preparation of RNA samples

We recently observed significant disruptions in sleep–wake cycles in humans with INTS1 mutations and in INTS1-deficient zebrafish. Our findings indicated that INTS1 knockout zebrafish exhibit a delayed phase shift under various light conditions, suggesting a disturbance in circadian rhythm. This delayed activity phase was evident even under LD cycles, likely impacting gene expression due to alterations in circadian clock function. To address this, we minimized circadian synchronization prior to sampling by eliminating external cues. Upon spawning, fertilized eggs were distributed into Petri dishes with 40 eggs per dish and kept in a dark incubator at 28°C for the next 120 h. At 120 hpf, larvae were exposed to 2 h of light, after which heads were sampled and placed in 50 Trizol for storage at −80°C. DNA extraction was performed from each larva’s tail, and genotypes were determined using PCR amplification and enzymatic digestion of the amplicon. Following genomic determination, samples were pooled into groups of 13 individuals based on their genotype, resulting in a total of 8 final samples comprising 4 WT and 4 INTS1 knockout samples. Total RNA was extracted from samples using the RNeasy Mini Kit, Qiagen (Qiagen, catalog no. NC9677589), following the manufacturer’s instructions and including an on-column DNase treatment. An additional ethanol precipitation was performed for extra purification. All samples had an RNA integrity number score ranging from 9.3 to 9.5.

RNA sequencing

The samples were sequenced using Illumina NovaSeq SP—200 cycles. Libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA protocol. 2 × 100 bp paired reads were sequenced on a NovaSeq (SP 2500 Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) to generate 200 bp paired reads. Nine base pairs of unique molecular identifier tags were added before PCR amplification to reduce errors and quantitative bias introduced by the amplification. All samples passed quality control and pre-processing and were used for further analyses (Supplementary Fig. S5).

RNA sequencing data processing

Poly-A/T stretches and Illumina adapters were trimmed from the reads using cutadapt [35]; resulting reads shorter than 30 bp were discarded (Supplementary Fig. S5). Reads were mapped to the Danio rerio reference genome GRCz11 using STAR [36], supplied with gene annotations downloaded from Ensembl (with EndToEnd option and outFilterMismatchNoverLmax was set to 0.04). Reads with the same UMI were removed using the PICARD MarkDuplicate tool using the BARCODE_TAG parameter. Pipeline was run using snakemake [37].

Calculation of mature and pre-mRNA expression

Mapping

The following reference genomes and annotations were used for alignment: zebrafish genome GRCz11 (GenBank:GCA_000002035.4) and Ensembl annotations release 102 [38, 39]. RNA-Seq reads were aligned to the relevant reference genome using STAR with default parameters [36].

Expression

Annotation files were supplemented to include a single precursor mRNA for each gene. Precursors span the entire gene locus, from the earliest annotated isoform start point to the last annotated isoform end point. Isoform-specific FPKM values were calculated using Cufflinks [40]. Mature mRNA expression was calculated by summing the FPKM values of all mature mRNA isoforms. All transcripts included in the analysis were filtered using a cutoff of mature RNA with FPKM >1, determined after examining the distribution of normalized read counts. Differentially expressed genes were identified using a t-test with an FDR-corrected P-value <.05.

Analysis of maternal and zygotic contributions

Zebrafish RNA-seq dataset was taken from published metabolically labeled RNA-Seq data of zebrafish embryos [41]. Reads were aligned to the zebrafish genome (GRCz11) and Ensembl annotations release 102. Alignment was performed using STAR version 2.7.10a and version 2.7.11b with parameters --outSAMattributes MD NH. Reads were filtered using samtools view -F 512 (samtools version 1.19.2). All reads were then converted (T to C for read 2 and A to G for read 1) and realigned using STAR to converted genomes using the same parameters. Reads were filtered using samtools view -F 512, and only reads mapped to the appropriate strand based on the conversion (T to C or A to G) were kept. Reads were converted back to the original genome and recalculated NM and MD tags. Reads were further filtered to remove multimappers, and alignments were combined with preference to an alignment to the unconverted genome. Reads were filtered with a minimum MAPQ = 1. New RNA ratios were quantified using GRAND-SLAM (version 2.0.7b), with the parameters -minEstimateReads 10 000 -full -allGenes -D -trim5p 15. An estimated half-life was calculated using the slope of a linear regression model fitted to samples measured between 4 and 8 hpf.

RNA-seq datasets were taken from published studies in mice and human embryos [42–44]. Reads were aligned to the relevant reference genome using STAR with default parameters: mouse genome GRCm39 and Ensembl annotations release 108; human genome GRCh38 and Ensembl annotations release 110. FPKM values were calculated using Cufflinks and summing the FPKM values of all mRNA isoforms. FPKM values were log2 transformed, and values below −3 were floored to this value. Temporal FPKM values were normalized by iteratively selecting a set of control genes and normalizing all temporal samples by their mean expression.

Gene Ontology pathway analysis

We conducted Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis using the Gene Ontology Resource [45, 46], with a P-value threshold of <.05 after FDR correction.

Genomic feature analysis of differentially expressed genes

Genomic features, including gene length, transcript length, and intron number, were obtained from Ensembl BioMart using the zebrafish genome assembly GRCz11 (Ensembl Genome Browser 115). Statistical significance between groups was evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Expression analysis of 3′ and 5′ flanking regions

Annotation

The 3′ and 5′ regions were defined as 1000 bp downstream and 500 bp upstream, respectively, based on the zebrafish genome GRCz11 (GenBank: GCA_000002035.4) and Ensembl annotations (release 102) [38, 39].

Expression

FPKM values were calculated using Cufflinks [40]. For the 3′ and 5′ regions mentioned above, expression thresholds were determined by calculating the mean expression of each genotype across four samples. Regions were included if the mean FPKM of at least one of the groups exceeded 0.5, FDR-corrected t-test P-value was below .05, and the fold change in expression was >2. Correlation analysis was conducted using these significant regions and their corresponding mature RNA, defined as those with FPKM > 1.

Motif analysis

Splicing branch motif analysis was conducted using the “Biostrings” package in R [47], which facilitates efficient assessment of biological sequences. Input sequences, provided in FASTA format, included first introns from the whole genome, excluding significant retained introns, and the sequences from the significant retained introns of genes with mature FPKM >1. The identified sequences were subsequently analyzed for their frequency, distribution, and positional enrichment across the dataset. Statistical analysis was performed using mixed model logistic regression, with P-values and confidence intervals adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction. Motif enrichment within the retained introns was analyzed using the STREME algorithm [48]. The algorithm was applied to identify statistically significant overrepresented motifs within the intronic sequences, with a significance threshold of P < .05. The identified motifs were further analyzed using k-means clustering, based on a distance matrix calculated using Q-gram distance by a two-dimensional scaling [49–51].

3′ and 5′ UTR sequence enrichment analysis

3′ and 5′ UTR sequences were downloaded from Ensembl BioMart, and the “canonical” isoform was selected. Sequences shorter than 10 bp were filtered out. We associated a short sequence (k-mer) with a regulatory effect as previously described [52]. We tested all short sequences (k-mers) between 3 and 7 nucleotides long. To control for UTR length-dependent effects, we (i) removed the top and bottom 10% of lengths from the analysis and (ii) normalized values by their length dependence. For that, we used a linear model to connect each property with 3′ UTR length and subtracted the resulting model from measured values.

Single-cell RNA-seq data processing

Mapping the INTS1 expression across different cell types was performed using published single-cell data from the Daniocell project [53, 54]. The Daniocell2023 SeuratV4.rds file was downloaded, and a new threshold was established based on the median expression for each cell type at the sampled time point, excluding cells with no detectable expression. The data were then subjected to hierarchical clustering and presented as a heatmap.

Results

INTS1-deficiency leads to changes in transcript levels

Utilizing our recently published INTS1-deficient zebrafish model [27], we investigated transcriptional changes that are associated with INTS1 deficiency in zebrafish larvae. Polyadenylated RNA isolated from pools of the head region of wild-type (WT) and INTS1-deficient zebrafish larvae at 7 dpf was subjected to RNA-seq (quadruplicate for each genotype) and analyzed. At this stage all internal organs and the brain are already developed, all senses are functional, and the larvae are already exhibiting basic behaviors such as hunting and escape response [27, 55].

This analysis identified 18 277 genes that are expressed above the threshold (out of ∼30 000 annotated transcripts). Although most genes show an overall similar expression in INTS1-deficient and WT larvae (Fig. 1A), we identified 411 significantly upregulated genes (P < .05, ≥2-fold increase) and 88 significantly downregulated genes (P < .05, ≥2-fold decrease) in the mutants (Fig. 1B). Notably, downregulated genes tended to have shorter gene lengths and fewer introns compared to upregulated genes, whereas the mature transcript length did not differ significantly between the two groups (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. S1). GO analysis of significantly up- and downregulated mRNA (Supplementary Table S1) revealed reduced expression of genes related to sensory system development and to nervous system processes. Specifically, the expression of genes related to eye development, phototransduction processes, and sensory perception to light stimulus was reduced, which is consistent with the observations of cataracts in patients with biallelic sequence variants in INTS1 and thickness of the lenses in INTS1-deficient zebrafish [24]. Upregulated gene enrichment was not associated with any neuronal-specific structure or activity but rather with various metabolic processes.

To relate these changes with the expression of INTS1 across zebrafish tissues, we curated data from the recently published DanioCell project [53, 54]. We calculated the median expression of INTS1 for each cell type (excluding cells with no detectable expression) at each specific time point, followed by hierarchical clustering (Fig. 1D). This analysis shows high expression of INTS1 mRNA during the first 3–4 hpf in cells with blastomeres, periderm, and axial mesoderm signatures. This early expression declines over time, and after the first 24 h of embryonic development, the expression pattern shifts, with neurons and glial cells exhibiting the highest INTS1 expression. This aligns with the functional annotations of downregulated genes in INTS1-deficient larvae (Supplementary Table S1) and their phenotypes, as described below.

Since INTS1 transcripts are already present at blastomeres (Fig. 1D), we speculated that INTS1 transcripts are maternally inherited. We therefore examined INTS1 expression during the early hours of development by analyzing published metabolically labeled RNA-Seq data of zebrafish embryos [41]. Indeed, these data revealed that INTS1 is both maternally inherited and zygotically expressed in zebrafish embryos (Fig. 1E), with only a minimal change in maternally contributed mRNA levels between 1 and 8 hpf (20% reduction). Using a linear regression model, we estimate a half-life time of 6 h for the maternal INTS1 transcripts, suggesting that these transcripts remain stable in the embryo long after the onset of zygotic gene expression. These observations raise the possibility that maternally inherited INTS1 allows INTS1-deficient zebrafish embryos to complete the entire developmental process and hatch, compared, e.g., to lethality in INTS1-deficient mice [28]; while INTS1 is maternally inherited also in mouse embryos [42–44] (Supplementary Fig. S2), their slower developmental pace limits the rescue of later stages of development by maternal INTS1.

Differentially expressed genes may explain behavioral phenotypes of INTS1 deficiency

The above analysis was conducted on polyadenylated RNA isolated from the heads of 7 dpf zebrafish larvae immediately after their first exposure to light (for 2 h). The rationale for this protocol was that the phase of the circadian clock of both genotypes would be identical, avoiding potential artifacts due to circadian phase differences. To verify this assumption, progeny of heterozygous crosses were exposed to a single 2-h pulse of light at 120 hpf (5 days), followed by locomotor activity recording for three days under constant darkness, and subsequently genotyped. Under these conditions all three genotypes exhibited circadian rhythms of locomotor activity with a similar phase, indicating that the single light pulse was sufficient to entrain the circadian clock system in mutants and wildtype and that any difference in gene expression is not due to phase differences that do occur under LD cycle [27]. Nevertheless, the level of locomotor activity was significantly elevated in the mutants compared with WT and heterozygous larvae (Fig. 2A–C), strengthening our earlier observation that INTS1-deficient zebrafish are hyperactive [27].

The pronounced hyperactivity observed in INTS1-deficient zebrafish larvae prompted us to investigate whether pathways associated with ADHD have been affected by INTS1 deficiency. Zebrafish have proven to be an effective model for this purpose due to conserved neuronal circuits and well-established behavioral assays that parallel key aspects of ADHD [56, 57]. Several human genes associated with ADHD are involved in regulating central dopaminergic, serotonergic, and noradrenergic systems [56, 58]. Searching the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM®) database and reviewing the current utilization of zebrafish models in ADHD research [56, 57], we noted 22 genes associated with ADHD that were also represented in our dataset. Of the 22 ADHD genes, 4 were significantly downregulated in INTS1-deficient larvae (Fig. 2D), demonstrating over-representation of ADHD genes within downregulated transcripts (4/22 genes, hypergeometric P < 4 × 10−6). Classification of ADHD-related genes based on neurotransmitter-signaling pathways highlighted the serotonergic, noradrenergic, and dopaminergic systems, with the dopaminergic system being the most affected. This includes reduced expression of genes encoding for dopamine receptors (drd4a, drd4b) and the dopamine transporter (slc6a3) [56] (Fig. 2D). The expression of sypa, which encodes Synaptophysin, an integral synaptic vesicle protein that regulates the kinetics of endocytosis and exocytosis in central neurons, was also significantly reduced [59, 60]. On the other hand, the serotonin transporter slc6a4a [61] and the basic helix–loop–helix repressor her6 were both upregulated. The latter plays a critical role in regulating the neurogenic gradient in the zebrafish thalamus. Loss of her6 results in the premature generation of glutamatergic neurons, while its continued expression promotes the production of GABAergic neurons [62].

INTS1 deficiency significantly alters the relative abundance of other Integrator subunits

Previous in vivo studies of the Integrator complex have predominantly been conducted in non-vertebrate organisms that differ from vertebrates in their Integrator subunit composition and in some cases completely lack specific subunits present in the human complex [16]. The zebrafish Integrator complex exhibits nearly identical composition in terms of subunits, their protein domains, length, and structure compared to those of the human Integrator complex, with the exception of additional domains in zebrafish INTS9 and the recently identified INTS15 [63–65] (Fig. 2F). Interestingly, siRNA-mediated depletion of the INTS1 subunit in human HEK293T cells resulted in elevated expression of other Integrator subunits’ INTS9 and INTS11 [66], suggesting that the expression of certain Integrator subunits is regulated by a feedback loop [66].

Accordingly, we examined whether the expression of Integrator subunit-encoding genes is affected by INTS1 deficiency in zebrafish. We observed significant upregulation of two Integrator subunits, demonstrating over-representation of Integrator subunits within upregulated transcripts (2/16 subunits, hypergeometric P ≤ .05). The only transcript that was downregulated is that encoding INTS1, likely reflecting nonsense-mediated mRNA decay. Upregulated subunits are INTS6 and INTS6L (Fig. 2E), which differ from those upregulated following INTS1 siRNA-mediated knockdown in human cells. In Drosophila, overexpression of INTS6 limits Integrator function by titrating protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A), thereby modulating the efficiency of transcription termination events in a subset of coding genes [18].

INTS1 deficiency is associated with a higher frequency of first intron retention

By regulating the expression of snRNAs in C. elegans, S. mediterranea, Drosophila, and human HEK293T cells, the Integrator complex has been suggested to modulate the splicing machinery [1, 12, 13, 16, 67]. Recently, INTS1 depletion in HEK293T cells was also linked to increased premature transcription termination and release of incomplete pre-mRNAs with retained introns [11]. Therefore, INTS1 deficiency may be assumed to alter splicing patterns also in zebrafish. Toward examining this possibility, we initially distinguished between the levels of fully spliced mRNAs (mature) and their unspliced pre-mRNAs for all 18 277 expressed genes. Pre-mRNA levels in WT and mutants were tightly correlated (Fig. 3A; R2 = 0.9; P < 10−16), but some differences were identified (Fig. 3B). As with mRNA (Fig. 1A), we identified more genes with upregulated pre-mRNA levels in the mutants (360 genes; Fig. 3B) and fewer with downregulated pre-mRNAs (148 genes). These pre-mRNAs partially overlapped genes with upregulated (136 out of 411 genes, 33%, hypergeometric P < 10−134; Fig. 3C) and downregulated (33 out of 88 genes, 38%, hypergeometric P < 10−46; Fig. 3D) mature mRNAs. These results suggest that the effect of INTS1 deficiency on pre-mRNA levels stems from changes in their transcription. We also identified a strong positive correlation (R2 = 0.71; P < 10−114) between fold changes in pre-mRNA and mature mRNA levels (Fig. 3E). Thus, again, changes in pre-mRNA observed in INTS1-deficient mutants are associated with similar changes in the mRNA levels of the respective gene, further supporting transcriptional errors.

Nevertheless, the above analysis did not directly address disrupted splicing at specific locations within a gene. Therefore, we extend the analysis to investigate the retention of each individual intron within a gene independently of other introns. We calculated the coverage of each individual intron in a gene and compared between WT and INTS1-deficient larvae. This analysis revealed retention of the first intron of 294 genes in mutants, with minimal occurrence in subsequent introns (Fig. 3F and G, e.g. sin3b, and Fig. 3K). We did not identify a correlation between the fold change in expression of the first retained introns and their respective mRNAs (R2 = 0; Fig. 3H), suggesting that the observed first intron retentions are not directly linked to elevated mRNA expression and therefore more likely a specific splicing regulation deficiency.

Toward understanding the underlying mechanism for first intron retention in INTS1-deficiency, we analyzed the nucleotide sequences of the retained first introns. Retained introns had elevated GC content (Fig. 3I; P < 10−15), which has been previously demonstrated to influence splice-site recognition and to impact splicing through pre-mRNA secondary structures [68–70]. GC-rich introns in zebrafish were also shown to harbor higher G content in the 3′ splice acceptor region [68] as a defining feature of GC-rich introns, reflecting their distinct structural composition. Indeed, we observed a decreased adenine content within the 3′ splice acceptor region of the retained introns (Fig. 3J; P < .001, Supplementary Fig. S3). Finally, analysis of the retained intron sequences using STREME [48] identified several sequences specifically enriched in this group compared to first introns across the entire genome. These sequences were subsequently grouped into four distinct clusters based on their nucleotide composition [49–51] (Fig. 3L and Supplementary Fig. S3).

The effect of INTS1 deficiency on 5′ and 3′ transcript ends

The Integrator complex was shown capable of driving accurate transcription termination [3, 71] and regulating pause-release mechanisms during transcriptional initiation [2] at RNAPII-transcribed loci. Regulation by the Integrator occurs at both coding and noncoding loci [4] by recognizing specific features of paused elongation complexes [6]. This activity depends on dual enzymatic activities of the complex: an endonuclease that cleaves nascent RNA and a phosphatase that simultaneously dephosphorylates RNAPII to facilitate transcription termination [9, 71–73]. Although this was shown in several invertebrate in vivo models, including planaria, worms, and flies [12–16], similar studies in vertebrate models have not yet been performed. This activity can directly affect expression at both 3′ and 5′ ends of mRNA transcripts. At the 3′ end, Integrator was suggested to contribute to transcription termination as a backup or stress-induced mechanism [11, 74]. At the 5′ end, loss of Integrator may affect promoter-proximal termination and the transcription of enhancer RNAs (eRNAs) [75], which are short (∼200–400 nt) bidirectional non-coding RNAs transcribed from active enhancer and promoter regions, resulting in longer yet less abundant eRNAs. This prompted us to investigate both 3′ and 5′ end processing in the INTS1-deficient mutants.

To investigate these phenomena, we analyzed expression levels of the genomic regions immediately upstream (5′ end) or downstream (3′ end) of the annotated transcripts (500 and 1000 bp, respectively) in WT and INTS1-deficient mutants. We limited our analysis to end regions with mean FPKM >0.5 in either WT or mutant samples, resulting in 4781 genes with 5′ end and 5330 genes with 3′ end expression. Differential expression analysis has identified 66 genes with elevated and 11 genes with reduced 5′ end expression in the mutant (Fig. 4A, e.g. sult1st1, and Fig. 4C). In 3′ end analysis, we identified 94 genes with elevated and 39 genes with reduced 3′ end expression in the mutant (Fig. 4D, e.g. ssu72, and Fig. 4F). To determine if changes in 5′ and 3′ end regions are linked to changes in expression levels of the associated mRNA, we compared the fold change of their expression. We identified a correlation with mRNA changes for the 11 genes with reduced 5′ end expression (R2 = 0.52; P < .05, Fig. 4B) but not for 66 genes with elevated 5′ end expression (R2 = 0.01). On the other hand, in 3′ end analysis we revealed a correlation with respective mRNA changes for the 94 genes with increased 3′ end expression (Fig. 4E; R2 = 0.57; P < 10−4), but not for 39 genes with reduced 3′ end expression (R2 = 0.02). These results suggest that increased expression of 5′ end regions and reduced expression of 3′ end regions in mutants might occur independently of changes in their mRNA levels and could be associated with the Integrator complex’s effects on transcription termination at RNAPII-transcribed loci.

Finally, we compared the occurrence of short sequence motifs in 3′ and 5′ UTRs [52] with expression levels at 3′ and 5′ end regions. This analysis associated lower 3′ end regions expression in both mutants and WT with a canonical nuclear polyadenylation signal (AAUAAA, FDR-corrected Kolmogorov–Smirnoff P < 10−19; Fig. 4G). The polyadenylation signal likely allows a precise transcriptional termination of polyA + mRNAs independently of the Integrator complex. On the other hand, our analysis also associated higher-end region expression with C-rich sequences (e.g. CCCC, P < 10−8; Fig. 4H and I) in both 3′ and 5′ UTRs. The C-rich associated increase in end region expression was lower in the mutants compared to WT, suggesting a possible effect of INTS1 deficiency on this activity.

Discussion

The Integrator complex is a crucial multi-protein assembly involved in the regulation of RNAPII activity, particularly through its role in transcriptional initiation, pause-release, and 3′-end processing of nascent transcripts [1–3, 10, 15, 44, 52]. In vertebrates, the Integrator also contributes to the regulation of gene expression, spliceosome assembly, and RNA stability [1, 6, 9, 76]. Through these interconnected functions, it is vital for maintaining cellular homeostasis and influences a broad spectrum of biological processes, including development, cell differentiation, and the function of the immune and central nervous systems [21, 23, 26, 66, 77], as reflected in the phenotypic outcomes of pathogenic INTS1 variants in humans [24–27].

In this work, we assess the contribution of INTS1, and hence the Integrator complex, to gene expression and RNA processing in vivo. We take advantage of our recently published zebrafish models of INTS1-deficiency [27] to study the molecular consequences of the loss of INTS1 through a transcriptomic analysis of INTS1-deficient zebrafish larvae and their WT siblings. While previous functional studies of Integrator subunits in mammalian cell cultures have provided valuable insights into their roles [11, 14], further in vivo investigation of INTS1 function has been limited due to embryonic lethality of mouse models [24–28]. On the other hand, although INTS1-deficient zebrafish do not survive to adulthood and exhibit early lethality, we could analyze their transcriptome at a stage in which all major internal organs, including a fully formed brain, are present. At this stage, sensory systems are functional, and the larvae exhibit key behaviors such as prey capture and escape responses [27, 55], indicating a sufficiently mature neurophysiological state suitable for transcriptomic analysis in the context of a fully differentiated vertebrate species. Zebrafish single-cell and transcriptomic data mining [52–54, 78] confirmed the maternal inheritance of INTS1 mRNA and revealed a major shift in INST1 expression profile toward the nervous system in the developing vertebrates. Indeed, we were able to identify significant changes in the expression of genes involved in sensory and nervous system development and demonstrate that the loss of INTS1 significantly affects the expression of ADHD-related genes, which correlates with robust locomotor activity in INTS1-deficient larvae, even in the absence of light. This observation reinforces our previous findings of a hyperactivity phenotype and further supports the involvement of the dopaminergic and noradrenergic systems in the disrupted sleep-wake cycle phenotype associated with INTS1 dysfunction, as we previously reported based on spatial expression of dbh [27]. This also points to a limitation in this approach, which relies on bulk RNA sequencing from whole-brain tissue, which inherently masks spatial heterogeneity in gene expression. For instance, dbh—a marker of noradrenergic neurons—is expressed in both the locus coeruleus and the medulla oblongata, regions with anatomical and functional differences [27, 79, 80]. Thus, combining transcripts from these distinct regions obscured region-specific transcriptional alterations.

RNA-Seq also enabled us to study mRNA splicing, revealing the retention of first introns in INTS1-deficient larvae independently of the gene’s expression level. We identified several sequence-based characteristics of these retained introns: adenine-deficient 3′ end splicing branch, GC-rich intronic sequences, and several enriched sequence motifs [48], including a relatively high number of AU/AC-rich elements. Analysis of functional groups highlighted by GO annotation, including ADHD-related genes, 43 neurodevelopmental genes with zebrafish orthologs [81, 82], and genes involved in eye development and phototransduction, did not reveal any global association with first intron retention of changes at the 3′ and 5′ ends of those genes, further supporting independence of these phenomena from mRNA expression changes. The mechanistic relevance of these sequence features warrants further investigation. Several hypotheses could mechanistically link INTS1 deficieny with first intro retention. First, it might be linked to epigenetic modifications associated with Integrator enrichment near transcription start sites [4, 73]. Second, it may arise by spliceosome damage related to INTS1 dysfunction that affects specific splice site recognition mechanisms [68]. Expression analysis of 238 splicing-related protein-coding genes in zebrafish did not identify significant expression changes in the mutants (data not shown), suggesting that it is less likely that splicing-regulating proteins are involved. Instead, damaged snRNA processing may influence this phenotype, based on the established role of the Integrator complex in snRNA processing [12, 13, 16, 67]. However, the lack of snRNA measurement is a limitation in our current RNA-seq methodology. Indeed, we primarily concentrated on polyA + protein-coding mRNAs in our transcriptome analysis, while our capacity to examine changes in non-coding transcripts, such as snRNAs and in intermediates of mRNA splicing, was more limited.

Our intron retention analysis might also be affected by changes in gene expression levels (Supplemental Fig. S4). Future work using techniques specifically designed to study the transient transcriptome, such as TT-seq [22], GRO-seq [75], or metabolic labeling-based approaches [52, 83], in combination with total-RNA-seq will help to refine and expand our findings.

There are several noticeable similarities and distinctions between our work and studies performed in cultured mammalian cells. Structural studies indicate that INTS1 functions as the primary docking site for Integrator on the paused elongation complex [10, 84]. Acute INTS1 depletion in HEK293T cells leads to dissociation of the Integrator complex from RNAPII [11] and increased RNAPII premature termination and production of incomplete pre-mRNAs with retained introns. Our findings regarding retention of the first intron are consistent with these results. In contrast, depletion of INTS10 Integrator subunit in cultured cells leads to widespread transcriptional changes, affecting ∼10 000 genes [85], whereas our analysis in whole animals revealed only a few hundred altered genes upon INTS1 loss. Such differences may arise from methodological and analytical choices, system-specific factors, or the specific perturbation involved. As we show, maternally provided INTS1 mRNAs and the resulting protein may provide partial compensation to INTS1 depletion, thereby mitigating some transcriptional effects. Studies in cultured cells have also shown that depletion of the INTS11 subunit causes upregulation of short genes and downregulation of long genes, due to failure of RNAPII to reach the ends of long genes [11, 71]. By contrast, our results reveal the opposite trend: downregulated genes are generally shorter with fewer introns. This pattern may reflect an indirect effect triggered by INTS1 mutation or an inherent characteristic of zebrafish genome architecture. Evidence from cultured cells suggest that the Integrator complex may function as a transcriptional repressor through termination mechanisms [3, 72, 73, 86], but it may also facilitate transcriptional activation [2, 17, 87]. Our results imply that in zebrafish, the Integrator complex is primarily involved in transcriptional inhibition. However, further work distinguishing direct from indirect effects will be necessary to draw definitive conclusions.

Examining the expression of all 16 Integrator subunits revealed that INTS6 and INTS6L are upregulated in INTS1-deficient larvae. In Drosophila, where INTS6L is absent [18], INTS6 overexpression sequesters PP2A and reduces transcription termination efficiency in a subset of coding genes [18]. Thus, the behavioral and transcriptomic effects observed in INTS1 deficiency could be mediated, at least in part, by Ints6 and Ints6L upregulation, highlighting an important direction for future studies.

The primary and most well-documented role of the Integrator complex is in transcription termination of coding and non-coding transcripts [6, 12, 15, 67, 72], yet it is debated to what extent this activity also applies to canonical 3′ ends of coding mRNAs. Through analysis of transcripts’ 5′ and 3′ end regions, we suggest that increased expression upstream and reduced expression downstream of annotated transcripts in mutants might occur independently of changes in their mRNA levels and could be associated with interfering with the Integrator complex’s activity in transcription termination at RNAPII transcribed loci. Changes in downstream 3′ ends are associate with mRNA transcription termination, and changes in upstream 5′ ends with aberrant termination of eRNA transcription. Motif analysis in 3′ and 5′ UTRs linked higher 3′ and 5′ end expressions with C-rich sequences. This effect was lower in INTS1-deficient samples, suggesting a potential role for Integrator in regulating C-rich sequence-associated processing. Increased 3′ end expression was associated with parallel changes in mRNA expression and with lack of a canonical polyadenylation signal (AAUAAA), consistent with Integrator-independent transcriptional termination. Thus, it likely reflects a phenomenon contingent upon elevated mRNA expression levels in the absence of INTS1.

In summary, this study characterizes the consequences of INTS1 deficiency on gene expression and fidelity of splicing and transcript end processing in zebrafish and reinforces its essential role in nervous system development and function. Our results align with the emerging recognition of the Integrator complex as a key regulator of gene expression and its broader relevance in human disease and vertebrate transcriptional control. These findings lay the groundwork for future studies into the mechanistic roles of Integrator complex in sequence-specific transcript processing and its contribution to neurological disorders.
