Generalized Clapeyron’s theorem
Yury Grabovsky, Lev Truskinovsky

Abstract
Clapeyron’s Theorem in classical linear elasticity provides a way to explicitly express the energy stored in an equilibrium configuration in terms of the work of the forces applied on the boundary. We derive several new integral relations which can be viewed as nonlinear analogs of this classical result, reinterpreting them as rather general statements within Calculus of Variations. In the framework of nonlinear elasticity these relations reflect various partial symmetries of the material response, for instance, scale-invariance or scaling homogeneity. In particular, when the energy functional is scale-free, the obtained result can be interpreted as the Generalized Clapeyron’s Theorem (GCT). Remarkably, it combines rather naturally the work of physical and configurational forces. We present a series of illuminating case studies showing the variety of applications of various obtained relations in different seemingly unrelated problems of mechanics.

Introduction

A well known theorem of linear elasticity, first attributed to Clapeyron in
[
63
]

states that the elastic energy stored in a body loaded by (dead) forces is equal to half of the work
done by the loading device
[
67
,

More precisely, it states that

\int_{\Omega}W(\bm{x},e(\bm{u}))d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}(\bm{\sigma}\bm{n},\bm{u})dS(\bm{x}),

(1.1)

where
W(\bm{x},\bm{\varepsilon})=(1/2)(\mathsf{C}(\bm{x})\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\varepsilon}),

\mathsf{C}(\bm{x})
is the elasticity
tensor ,
\bm{u}(\bm{x})
is the displacement,
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{x})=\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u})
is the linear stress
tensor and

e(\bm{u})=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\bm{u}(\bm{x})+(\nabla\bm{u}(\bm{x}))^{T})

(1.2)

is the linear strain
tensor. While this theorem is obtained by simple integration by parts and is widely used in applications
[
11
,
71
,
49
,
74
]
it carries a certain flavor of mystery.

First, it has never
been published by the author himself
[
108
, p. 418]
. Second, it contains this
puzzling disappearance of the half of the work, which can be linked to the fact that
elastostatics is only a weak limit of elastodynamics (as the appropriately normalized magnitudes of the
characteristic velocities diverge)
[
31
]
. Finally, to the best of our knowledge, a relation (
1.1
) has been viewed as strictly limited to classical elasticity in the sense that an adequate analog does not exist in geometrically and physically nonlinear elasticity.

Here we close the apparent gap between linear and nonlinear elasticity perspectives on surface representation of the bulk energy and expose a deeper structure of the classical Clapeyron’s Theorem unifying it with some other similar results in mechanics and physics. This will not only allows us
to generalize Clapeyron’s Theorem beyond linear elasticity but will also open the possibility to view it as a general result in the broader Calculus of Variations.

We begin with the observation that the abstract problem which one can associate with the classical Clapeyron’s Theorem is to find conditions ensuring that the energy of an extremal configuration can be expressed through the data on the boundary.
Our solution of the problem links the generalization of Clapeyron’s Theorem with scale invariance of the elastic energy, which we show to be sufficient alone for obtaining a representation of the total energy
as a surface integral. Remarkably, while in the classical Clapeyron’s Theorem the implied boundary term contains the work of physical forces only, we show that in our Genralized Clapeyron’s Theorem (GCT), the energy of an extremal configuration can be expressed through what can be interpreted as the generalized work of both physical and configurational forces
[
15
,
43
,
85
,
65
,
103
,
72
]
. This shows that GCT represents an indirect generalization of the classical Clapeyron’s Theorem. A direct generalization of this classical result,
that does not involve any reference to configurational forces, relies on what we call
p
-homogeneity of the energy density. We show that if both scale invariance and
p
-homogeneity are operative at the same time, there is a corresponding conserved quantity that links the work of physical and configurational forces. Eliminating one or the other from GCT using this link, we can express the equilibrium energy through the work of either only physical or only configurational forces.

The proposed broader reading of the
Clapeyron’s Theorem allows one to advance in several directions simultaneously, from generalizing the classical Green’s formula in nonlinear elasto-statics
[
39
]
(see also
[
47
]
), to obtaining a far reaching
multidimensional generalization of the classical result of the Calculus of Variations expressing the minimal value of the energy through
the Weierstrass excess function
[

,
36
,

]
.

The crucial step in our analysis is the realization that behind the conventional Clapeyron’s Theorem is a formula first obtained by E. Noether

,
83
]

Noether considered the action of a continuous group of
symmetries of the variational integral and showed that when a symmetry parameter is varied, the
corresponding perturbation of the functional can be written as a sum of the Euler-Lagrange operator and a divergence
[

,
83
,
8

The underlying Noether’s formula has been used broadly to generate various integral identities in many different domains of science

Following some previous work, see for instance

We begin by using Noether’s formula to show that by means of an appropriate scaling of the dependent variable, classical Clapeyron’s Theorem can be linked to
degree 2 homogeneity of the quadratic energy density of linear elasticity.
Our GCT then emerges from the same type of analysis but now using a scaling reparametrization transformation, involving both dependent and independent variables, which does not leave the functional invariant but multiplies it by
a scalar factor.

As we have already mentioned, an intriguing feature of the GCT is that it naturally combines what is known in solid mechanics as the work performed by the Piola
and Eshelby stresses
[
27
,
97
,
42
,
84
,
102
,
44
]
. Moreover, we show that there exists a higher dimensional representation of GCT featuring a new tensor
combining
the Piola stress and the Eshelby stress. To obtain such a representation we first observe that for parametric problems energy density is a 1-homogeneous function of deformation gradient and therefore one can express the total energy along an extremal as a surface integral. Then we show that one can take a general non parametric problem and always map it into a higher dimensional parametric problem obtaining a desired boundary integral representation in the higher dimensional space. This higher dimensional representation allows one to give to the GCT a compact form involving the implied combination of the Piola and Eshelby stresses.

Since our work is motivated by applications which allow for surfaces of jump discontinuities of field gradients we account in our representation of GCT of inherent lack of smoothness of extremals
[
60
,
59
,
5
,
36
]
.
This issue becomes particularly important in our analysis of GCT applicable to nonlinear elastodynamics where the formation of singularities is unavoidable despite the presence of the overall variational structure
[
22
,
33
]
.

To highlight the utility of the obtained results, we present a series of illuminating case studies. In particular, we consider a generic problem of void formation in a nonlinear elastic body where the possibility of scaling reparametrization allows one to study infinitely small voids in a finite domain as finite voids in the infinite space and show that the use of GCT allows one to unify several previous partial results. We show further that GCT opens the way to addressing in a new way some important problems of material stability ranging from fracture to phase nucleation.
We also provide evidence that another important set of applications of the GCT is associated with the possibility to rule out the presence of strong local minimizers that are not global in nonlinear elasticity in the case of hard device loading.

Finally, we mention again that since partial invarinace of the type studied in this paper is a rather general property of variational problems, the obtained nonlinear versions of the classical Clapeyron’s Theorem applies to a broad class of variational problems not at all related to elasticity theory. For instance, our methods offer a unified approach to Rellich and Pohozaev type identities normally used in reaction diffusion framework. In this perspective, the obtained integral identities should be viewed as general results within Calculus of Variations reflecting particular structure of the corresponding energy functionals. Notwithstanding the immense breadth of possible application of GCT in different disciplines, we have chosen to focus our presentation on problems of solid mechanics while dedicating only brief comments to other applications.

The paper is organized as follows. Our Section
2
contains the background material including the introduction of inner and outer variations, the definitions of Piola and Eshelby stresses, the analysis of general transformation families for variational problems and the derivation of the generalized Noether’s formula, which we illustrate in a detailed study of multidimensional elasto-dynamics.

In Section
3
we specialize the generalized Noether’s formula to the case of partial symmetries with parametric Lagrangians as a prominant example. We then show how different types of partial symmetries allow one to use the general Noether’s formula to derive various invariant integrals, for instance, the nonlinear versions of

L

The actual GCT is derived only in Section
4
where we introduce the idea of scale invariance and derive the integral identity resulting in the corresponding generalized Noether’s formula. We then map an original non parametric
problem into a higher dimensional parametric one and take advantage of the ensuing scale invariance to obtain a hiher dimensional version of the GCT in the extended space, naturally combining the Piola and Eshelby stresses. In the same section we present versions of GCT applicable in the presence of constraints and in the case when extremals exhibit surfaces of jump discontinuities of field gradients.
A special place among what we call “partial symmetries” is occupied by
p
-homogeneity which we use, for instance, to simplify GCT in the case of linear elasticity where the energy density is a quadratic function of the strain tensor. The case study in this section is again the elastodynamics where now we focus on the derivation of a version of GCT that accounts for the emergence of shock waves.

Our Section
5

In particular we show how the use of GCT allows one to formulate a new necessary condition of metastability, establish the non-existence of strong local minima, and gain nontrivial insight into notoriously inaccessible quasi-convex envelopes of elastic energies. We then use GCT to obtain a new general representation of the energy release associated with the void formation in nonlinear elasticity theory. In particular, we provide a novel resolution of the well known “Griffith’s error”

Our conclusions are summarized in Section

Preliminaries

We begin by introducing a general variational problem of minimizing the energy functional

E[\bm{y}]=\int_{\Omega}W(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))d\bm{x},

(2.1)

where, using the language of elasticity theory, we interpret the
function
\bm{y}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
as a deformation field defined on the
reference (Lagrangian) configuration
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. With elastodynamics and other applications in mind, we deal here with a more
general framework where
m
and
n
are arbitrary, the energy density
W
is allowed to depend on both
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
, and most importantly, where
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is assumed to be only Lipschitz continuous, most often with only singularities of
\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x})
being smooth surfaces of jump discontinuity, as occurs in the presence of shocks and during martensitic phase transitions. Since there are many examples of Lipschitz extremals with more general singular sets, even for strictly convex energy densities
[
23
,
78
,
4
,
98
,
105
,
77
]
, in each case we will specify the extra regularity assumptions placed on
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
, which will otherwise always be assumed Lipschitz continuous.

The image
\Omega^{*}=\bm{y}(\Omega)
is called actual or Eulerian configuration.
In classical nonlinear elasticity it is assumed that

W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})=W(\bm{F})
does not depend explicitly on
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
and is objective, i.e.

W(\bm{R}\bm{F})=W(\bm{F})
for all
\bm{R}\in SO(3)
and all
\bm{F}
,
\det\bm{F}>0
. However, here we are not making these assumptions, as our conclusions hold for a far more general class of energy densities.

We will assume, in general, that
W
is of class
C^{1}
, if not globally, then
on the open set containing the range of
(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))
for any
configuration
\bm{y}
under consideration. In the context of three-dimensional nonlinear elasticity, for example, this means that the range of
\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x})
will be a closed subset of
\{\bm{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}:\det\bm{F}>0\}
, eliminating the difficulties typically arising when one considers Sobolev solutions, whose existence is guaranteed by
[
3
]
.
In some situations that we will identify explicitly, the regularity in the
\bm{x}
variable will not be assumed, as in the case of a heterogenous or composite media.

2.1
First variation of a graph

We begin with a brief derivation of the first variation of the energy.
Following Noether, we adopt a more geometric point of view and regard the energy density
W
as a function on the tangent bundle
T\Gamma
, where

\Gamma=\{(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x})):\bm{x}\in\Omega\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+n}

is the graph of
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
.
We will now examine the effect of the graph perturbation on the energy functional. Hence, we consider
a smooth family of Lipschitz maps
\bm{\Phi}_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{m+n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m+n}

\bm{\Phi}_{0}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=(\bm{x},\bm{y})

\delta E=\left.\frac{dE[T\Gamma_{\epsilon}]}{d\epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=0},

(2.2)

where

E[T\Gamma_{\epsilon}]=\int_{\Omega_{\epsilon}}W(\widetilde{\bm{x}},\bm{y}_{\epsilon}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}),\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}))d\widetilde{\bm{x}}.

(2.3)

To explain the notations in (
2.3
), we write

\bm{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=(\bm{X}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\epsilon),\bm{Y}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\epsilon)),

(2.4)

where
\bm{X}

\bm{Y}

According to (

\widetilde{\bm{y}}_{\epsilon}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})=\bm{Y}(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\epsilon)

\bm{y}_{\epsilon}(\bm{X}(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\epsilon))=\bm{Y}(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\epsilon),

(2.5)

and hence, we obtain (
2.3
),
where
\Omega_{\epsilon}
is the image of

\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon}(\bm{X}(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\epsilon))\nabla\bm{X}=\nabla\bm{Y}

must be taken into account.
The identity (

) can be then rewritten in the form similar to (

)

\delta E=-\int_{\Omega}\{(\nabla\cdot\bm{P})_{\rm reg}\cdot\delta\bm{y}+(\nabla\cdot\bm{P}^{*})_{\rm reg}\cdot\delta\bm{x}\}d\bm{x}\\
+\int_{\partial\Omega}\{\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\delta\bm{y}+\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\delta\bm{x}\}dS-\int_{\Sigma}\{[\![\bm{P}]\!]\bm{n}\cdot\delta\bm{y}+[\![\bm{P}^{*}]\!]\bm{n}\cdot\delta\bm{x}\}dS.

(2.16)

where

(\nabla\cdot\bm{P})_{\rm reg}
and
(\nabla\cdot\bm{P}^{*})_{\rm reg}
are
C^{1}
functions on
\Omega\setminus S
, suffering a jump discontinuity across
\Sigma
, defined by
\nabla\cdot\bm{P}(\bm{x})
and
\nabla\cdot\bm{P}^{*}(\bm{x})
,
\bm{x}\in\Omega\setminus\Sigma
.
If we follow the standard convention that the unit normal to a surface
\Sigma
always points from the “
-
” side of
\Sigma
to its “

” side, then

[\![\bm{P}]\!]=\bm{P}_{+}(\bm{x})-\bm{P}_{-}(\bm{x}),\quad\bm{x}\in\Sigma.

2.2
Extremal and stationary configurations

Our primary interest lies in problems of Calculus of Variations arising from elastostatics and elastodynamics. The latter is governed by the action principle, whereby

\delta E=0

(2.17)

for all outer variations
\delta\bm{y}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
, and
\delta\bm{x}=0
; the former—by the energy minimization procedure, one of whose consequences is (
2.17
) for all variations
\delta\bm{y}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
and
\delta\bm{x}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. In both cases condition (
2.17
) requires that

\mathfrak{E}_{W}(\bm{x})=0,

(2.18)

which serves in such theories as the main governing equation.

Definition 2.3
.

We say that the Lipschitz configuration
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is an extremal, if it satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation (
2.18
)
in the sense of distributions.

This definition allows for singularities of
\nabla\bm{y}
corresponding, for instance, to shock waves and phase boundaries
[
109
]
.
We recall that stationarity is fully compatible with jump discontinuities of this type as it places restrictions on such singularities but does not rule them out. This issue is discussed in more detail in Section
4.4
.

An important implication of Thorem
2.1
is that for any Lipschitz extremal, which is of class
W^{2,1}_{\rm loc}
outside of a closed nowhere dense singular set
S
,
\mathfrak{E}^{*}_{W}(\bm{x})
is a distribution supported on
S
. In the special case when
\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x})
suffers a jump discontinuity across a smooth surface
\Sigma\subset\Omega
, outside of which
\bm{y}
is of class
C^{2}
, the distribution
\mathfrak{E}^{*}_{W}(\bm{x})
has a particularly simple form. To derive it, we take an arbitrary point
\bm{x}_{0}\in\Sigma
, choose a direction of the normal
\bm{n}_{0}
to
\Sigma
at
\bm{x}_{0}
and a radius
r>0
, such that
\Sigma
splits
B(\bm{x}_{0},r)
into two disjoint subdomains
B^{+}(\bm{x}_{0},r)
and
B^{-}(\bm{x}_{0},r)
, with
\bm{n}_{0}
pointing from
B^{-}(\bm{x}_{0},r)
and into
B^{+}(\bm{x}_{0},r)
, by our convention. Then there is a unique choice of the smooth normal
\bm{n}(\bm{x})
to
\Sigma
at
\Sigma\cap B(x_{0},r)
, such that
\bm{n}(\bm{x}_{0})=\bm{n}_{0}
. Then for any test function
\bm{\phi}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B(\bm{x}_{0},r))
,

\langle\mathfrak{E}^{*}_{W}(\bm{x}),\bm{\phi}\rangle=\int_{B(\bm{x}_{0},r)}\{W_{\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{\phi}+\langle\bm{P}^{*},\nabla\bm{\phi}\rangle\}d\bm{x}=\\
\int_{B^{+}(\bm{x}_{0},r)}\{W_{\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{\phi}+\langle\bm{P}^{*},\nabla\bm{\phi}\rangle\}d\bm{x}+\int_{B^{-}(\bm{x}_{0},r)}\{W_{\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{\phi}+\langle\bm{P}^{*},\nabla\bm{\phi}\rangle\}d\bm{x}.

We can now integrate by parts on each of the domains
B^{\pm}(\bm{x}_{0},r)
, taking (
2.13
) and
\mathfrak{E}_{W}(\bm{x})=0
into account:

\langle\mathfrak{E}^{*}_{W}(\bm{x}),\bm{\phi}\rangle=-\int_{\Sigma}[\![\bm{P}^{*}]\!]\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\phi}\,dS.

(2.19)

The vector field
[\![\bm{P}]\!]^{*}\bm{n}
in (
2.19
) can be simplified further, taking into account again that
\mathfrak{E}_{W}(\bm{x})=0
, which implies that

[\![\bm{P}]\!]\bm{n}=\bm{0}.

(2.20)

In this case we can write

[\![\bm{P}^{*}]\!]\bm{n}=[\![W]\!]\bm{n}-[\![\bm{F}^{T}\bm{P}]\!]\bm{n}=[\![W]\!]\bm{n}-[\![\bm{F}]\!]^{T}\bm{P}\bm{n},

where we use the usual notation
\bm{F}(\bm{x})=\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x})
.
We now recall that on the smooth surface of jump discontinuity of
\nabla\bm{y}
the following Hadamard relations hold:

[\![\bm{F}]\!]=\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n},

(2.21)

where
\bm{n}(\bm{x})
is the unit normal on an
n-1
-dimensional surface
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\bm{a}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a
C^{1}
vector field on
\Sigma
. Then

[\![\bm{P}^{*}]\!]\bm{n}=([\![W]\!]-\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{a})\bm{n}=([\![W]\!]-\langle\bm{P}_{\pm},[\![\bm{F}]\!]\rangle)\bm{n}=p^{*}\bm{n}.

Hence,

\mathfrak{E}^{*}_{W}(\bm{x})=-p^{*}_{\Sigma}\bm{n}\delta_{\Sigma},

(2.22)

where
\delta_{\Sigma}
is a distribution defined by

\langle\delta_{\Sigma},\phi\rangle=\int_{\Sigma}\phi(\bm{x})dS,\quad\forall\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

In (
2.22
) we also introduced a continuous scalar field

p^{*}_{\Sigma}=[\![W]\!]-\langle\bm{P}_{\pm},[\![\bm{F}]\!]\rangle

(2.23)

on the smooth surface of jump discontinuity
\Sigma
. Here
\bm{P}_{\pm}
indicates that it does not matter whether one uses
\bm{P}_{+}
or
\bm{P}_{-}
in the formula for
p^{*}_{\Sigma}
, as long as one uses this formula for an extremal, so that (
2.20
) holds.

As we have already mentioned, in static problems we are usually interested in minima of energy functionals, subject to specified boundary conditions. Then, Lipschitz minimizers of (
2.1
) must satisfy
\delta E=0
for all variations

\delta\bm{x}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and
\delta\bm{y}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
. Hence, in addition to (
2.18
) the solutions of such problems must also satisfy

\mathfrak{E}^{*}_{W}(\bm{x})=0,

(2.24)

again understood in the sense of distributions.

Definition 2.4
.

We say that a Lipschitz configuration
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is
stationary
if
it is an extremal in the sense of Definition
2.3
, and additionally satisfies (
2.24
).

Note that in view of Theorem
2.1
if
\overline{\bm{y}}\in W^{2,1}_{\rm loc}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
is an extremal, then it is stationary
in the sense of Definition
2.4
. Indeed
\nabla\overline{\bm{y}}\bm{\phi}\in W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
and

\int_{\Omega}\{W_{\bm{y}}\cdot\bm{\psi}+\langle\bm{P},\nabla\bm{\psi}\rangle\}d\bm{x}=0,\quad\forall\bm{\psi}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m}).

By the density argument it follows that

\int_{\Omega}\{W_{\bm{y}}\cdot\bm{\psi}+\langle\bm{P},\nabla\bm{\psi}\rangle\}d\bm{x}=0,\quad\forall\bm{\psi}\in W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m}).

Taking
\bm{\psi}=\nabla\overline{\bm{y}}\bm{\phi}
we obtain

\langle(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}})^{T}\mathfrak{E}_{W}|\bm{\phi}\rangle=0\quad\forall\bm{\phi}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Theorem
2.1
then implies that (
2.24
) holds.

Note also that formula (
2.22
) implies that for
stationary extremals with a smooth surface of jump discontinuity
\Sigma
we must have

p^{*}_{\Sigma}=[\![W]\!]-\langle\bm{P}_{\pm},[\![\bm{F}]\!]\rangle=0.

(2.25)

In this sense
stationarity, is not a consequence
of equilibrium even for piecewise smooth
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
.

While we introduced in (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) two important tensorial fields,
\bm{P}
and
\bm{P}^{*}
, which appear for instance in (
2.10
), their physical meaning has not been explained.
Observe first that while Piola stress
\bm{P}
acts on the virtual displacements
\delta\bm{y}
of the points on the boundary of the domain in the actual (Eulerian) space, the Eshely stress
\bm{P}^{*}
acts on the virtual changes of the shape of the domain
\Omega
in the reference (Lagrangian) space. The former is usually interpreted as the action of “physical forces”, and the latter – as the action of “configurational forces”. Therefore, we can conclude that the two terms in the surface integral in (
2.10
) represent the work of both physical and configurational forces, see for instance,
[
44
]
.
More specifically, the implied two energy increments can be associated with physical stress, respectively configurational prestress. Here, while the meaning of the physical stress does not require any clarifications as it is related to the work of applied forces, we associate configurational prestress with the work that has been stored during the
creation
of the physical body. To elucidate the implied fundamental difference between
\bm{P}
and
\bm{P}^{*}
we present below to simple illustrative examples.

.

Consider a one-dimensional body, given in Lagrangian coordinates as an interval

Here we show how to construct the simplest

Specifically,
the goal is to obtain a nontrivial radial stationary extremal on the ball

E[\bm{u}]=\int_{B(0,R)}W(\bm{x},e(\bm{u}))d\bm{x},

\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\neq 0,\quad|\bm{x}|=R.

(2.32)

The minimizer solves the following traction boundary value problem:

\begin{cases}\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bm{u})+\Delta\bm{u}=2\nabla\cdot\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\bm{x}),&\bm{x}\in\Omega,\\
\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}=(e(\bm{u})-\bm{\varepsilon}_{0})\bm{n}=0,&\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

To obtain such a minimizer explicitly, when
\Omega=B(0,R)
—the ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, centered at 0 with radius R, and
\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\bm{x})
given by (

.

Since

\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\bm{x})

{\mathcal{H}}=L^{2}(\Omega;\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))

The incompatible component

can be found by solving the system

\begin{cases}{\rm Ink}[\bm{\sigma}_{0}]=-{\rm Ink}[\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}],&\bm{x}\in\Omega\\

\bm{\sigma}_{0}
, and therefore the energy

which allows us to conclude that our “physically unloaded” body was ultimately “loaded” by configurational forces.

3
Partial invariance

Throughout this section we assume that both
W
and the extremal are of class
C^{2}
.
The main significance of the developed formalism lies in its usefulness for converting special scaling and invariance properties of variational problems into useful
formulas
satisfied by stationary configurations or almost regular extremals (as in Theorem
2.7
(b)). The idea is to use particular deformations
\bm{\Phi}_{\epsilon}
of the
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
space under which, due to the special properties of the Lagrangian
W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})
, the functional
E[T\Gamma]
in (
2.3
) transforms
in a known way
.

In this section we collected several examples illustrating the idea that if
E[T\Gamma_{\epsilon}]
can be simplified or tranformed by means of specific properties of the Lagrangian, then
\delta E
, computed as (
2.2
) will express this “partial symmetry”, while equations (
2.47
) or (
2.46
) will represent the corresponding “partial conservation laws”. In all these cases we show how such partial invariance of a problem can be used to obtain useful information about the solutions.

3.1
Parametric problems

An important set of examples concerns parametric variational integrals

E[\Sigma]=\int_{D}W(\bm{z}(\bm{t}),\nabla\bm{z}(\bm{t}))d\bm{t}.

(3.1)

The goal is to find an optimal
n
-dimensional surface
\Sigma=\{\bm{z}(\bm{t}):\bm{t}\in D\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open, bounded and smooth. The solution is not supposed to depend on a particular choice of parametrization.

Definition 3.1
.

Suppose that
\bm{t}(\bm{s})
is any diffeomorphism between the
closures of the open, bounded and smooth domains
\widetilde{D}
and
D
and

\widetilde{\bm{z}}(\bm{s})=\bm{z}(\bm{t}(\bm{s}))
. The Lagrangian
W
is called

parametric
if

\int_{D}W(\bm{z}(\bm{t}),\nabla\bm{z}(\bm{t}))d\bm{t}=\int_{\widetilde{D}}W(\widetilde{\bm{z}}(\bm{s}),\nabla\widetilde{\bm{z}}(\bm{s}))d\bm{s}.

(3.2)

for any smooth function
\bm{z}(\bm{t})
.

The notion of the parametric Lagrangian can be reformulated as a
a variational symmetry.
Let
\bm{\theta}:D\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, be an arbitrary smooth function, and let

\bm{s}=\bm{t}+\epsilon\bm{\theta}(\bm{t})
. Then
\widetilde{\bm{z}}(\bm{s})
is a reparametrization of
\Sigma
for all sufficiently small in absolute value
\epsilon
, if

\bm{z}(\bm{t})=\widetilde{\bm{z}}(\bm{s})=\widetilde{\bm{z}}(\bm{t}+\epsilon\bm{\theta}(\bm{t})).

By definition of the parametric variartional integral equation (
3.2
) must hold. But then, formula (
2.9
) applied to the transformation

\bm{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(\bm{t},\bm{z})=(\bm{t}+\epsilon\bm{\theta}(\bm{t}),\bm{z}),\quad\delta\bm{t}=\bm{\theta},\quad\delta\bm{z}=0,

yields taking into account that
W
is independent of
\bm{t}
,

\int_{D}\langle\bm{P}^{*},\nabla\bm{\theta}\rangle d\bm{t}=0.

Since, both the domain
D
and the vector field
\bm{\theta}
were
arbitrary, we conclude that

\bm{P}^{*}(\bm{z},\bm{F})=0

(3.3)

identically. Equation (
3.3
) is then a universal relation to be
satisfied for any solution of a parametric problem.

It is now easy to see what it takes for the problem to be parametric. If we fix
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\bm{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, and differentiate the function

GL(n)\ni\bm{A}\mapsto\Phi(\bm{A})=\frac{W(\bm{z},\bm{F}\bm{A})}{\det\bm{A}}

we obtain

\nabla\Phi(\bm{A})=\frac{\bm{F}^{T}W_{\bm{F}}(\bm{z},\bm{F}\bm{A})-W(\bm{z},\bm{F}\bm{A})\bm{A}^{-T}}{\det\bm{A}}=\frac{\bm{A}^{-T}}{\det\bm{A}}\bm{P}^{*}(\bm{z},\bm{F}\bm{A})=0.

We can then conclude that if
W(\bm{z},\bm{F})
is a parametric Lagrangian, then it must have the property

W(\bm{z},\bm{F}\bm{A})=W(\bm{z},\bm{F})\det\bm{A}

(3.4)

for any
\bm{A}\in GL^{+}(n)
. Conversely, if (
3.4
) holds for
all
\bm{A}\in GL^{+}(n)

3.4
) in
\bm{A}

at
\bm{A}=\bm{I}_{n}
, we obtain
\bm{P}^{*}(\bm{z},\bm{F})=0
. Therefore, the
algebraic property (
3.4
) is equivalent to (
3.3
) and
gives a complete characterization of parametric Lagrangians. Note that a well known example of parametric invariance in the context of nonlinear elasticity is the invariance with respect to “material remodeling”, see for instance
[
26
]
.

To show that parametric problems are ubiquitous, consider again the transformations
\bm{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=(\bm{X}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\epsilon)
,
\bm{Y}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\epsilon))

)

\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}

\bm{x}=\bm{\eta}(\bm{t}),

(3.5)

Indeed, since all transformations (

Finally, we show that
for every stationary configuration
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
of the original functional
(
2.1
) and any smooth diffeomorphism
\bm{\eta}:D\to\Omega
the function

\bm{z}(\bm{t})=(\bm{\eta}(\bm{t}),\bm{y}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{t})))
is an equilibrium configuration of

{\mathcal{E}}[\bm{z};D]
. Intuitively, the statement is apparent, since any weak outer
variation of
\bm{z}=(\bm{x},\bm{y})
is a combination of an inner and outer variations
for
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
and it can also be verified by a direct calculation.
Indeed, the extended Euler-Lagrange equation

\nabla_{\bm{t}}\cdot{\mathcal{W}}_{\bm{{\mathcal{F}}}}(\bm{z},\bm{{\mathcal{F}}})={\mathcal{W}}_{\bm{z}}

can be written in terms of

\bm{y}(\bm{x})
as a system

\begin{cases}\nabla_{\bm{t}}\cdot\left[\bm{P}^{*}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{t}),\bm{y}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{t})),\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{y}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{t})))\mathrm{cof}\nabla_{\bm{t}}\bm{\eta}(\bm{t})\right]=W_{\bm{x}}\det\nabla_{\bm{t}}\bm{\eta}(\bm{t}),\\
\nabla_{\bm{t}}\cdot(\bm{P}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{t}),\bm{y}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{t})),\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{y}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{t})))\mathrm{cof}\nabla_{\bm{t}}\bm{\eta}(\bm{t}))=W_{\bm{y}}\det\nabla_{\bm{t}}\bm{\eta}(\bm{t}).\end{cases}

(3.9)

It is then clear that a smooth change of variables
\bm{x}=\bm{\eta}(\bm{t})
converts the

\mathfrak{E}^{*}(W)=0,\,\,\ \mathfrak{E}(W)=0.

3.2
Invariant integrals

Suppose that the energy density

W
is homogeneous, i.e., does not depend explicitly on
\bm{x}

given by

\bm{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=(\bm{x}+\epsilon\bm{a},\bm{y}).

(3.10)

Then according to (

-\nabla\cdot\bm{P}^{*}=\mathfrak{E}^{*}_{W}(\bm{x})=-\nabla\bm{y}^{T}\mathfrak{E}_{W}(\bm{x})=0,

\int_{\partial V}\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\,dS=0.

To show that such statement can be highly nontrivial, it sufficient to refer to the J-integral in fracture mechancis which in two space dimensions
\bm{n}dl=(dx_{2},-dx_{1})

J_{1}=\int_{C}\left\{Wdx_{2}-\bm{t}\cdot\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{y}}{\partial x_{1}}dl\right\},\qquad J_{2}=-\int_{C}\left\{Wdx_{1}+\bm{t}\cdot\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{y}}{\partial x_{2}}dl\right\}.

Here we use the notation
\bm{t}=\bm{P}\bm{n}
for traction forces
and
C

\Omega

\bm{J}=\int_{\partial U}\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\,dS,

where the subdomain

S

either loop homotopy invariant or fixed end-point homotopy invariant in

\Omega=\Omega_{0}\setminus S
. The most important example of a singular set

of this type is provided by the mechanical fracture or crack set

, usually represented by one or more smooth surfaces of displacement discontinuity with elastic
deformation
\bm{y}
remaining smooth (at least

) on

\Omega=\overline{\Omega}_{0}\setminus S

16

in that we permit the energy density to depend on both

\bm{y}

\bm{F}

Another example of this type comes naturally if we replace translational invariance by rotational invariance. In mechanics the corresponding homotopy-invariant quantity, called the L-integral in
[
12

make a distinction between a reference and a deformed configuration. To give a variational interpretation of the now classical L-integral, we will have to define isotropy in a way that is not quite
natural from the point of view of finite elasticity.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that the energy density
W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})
is isotropic if

W(\bm{R}\bm{x},\bm{R}\bm{y},\bm{R}\bm{F}\bm{R}^{T})=W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F}),\quad\forall\bm{R}\in SO(3).

(3.13)

It is easy to check that the transformation group (

3\times 3
antisymmetric matrix

2
2

2

The

\star\omega

\omega[v_{1},\ldots,v_{p}]=\star\omega[v_{p+1},\ldots,v_{n}]

of

\omega[v_{1},\ldots,v_{p}]=\omega_{i_{1}\ldots i_{p}}v_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots v_{p}^{i_{p}}

defined in our specific case of

It is clear that the left-hand side in

where

is a homotopy-invariant integral. The nonzero values of

containg one or more components of the crack set

We point out again that the homotopy

\bm{y}

\bm{F}

Yet another example of an invariant integral in linear elasticity emerges if we note that the corresponding energy density is a quadratic function of
the displacement gradient. The corresponding scaling invariance property can be also formulated as a
classical variational symmetry. Suppose for simplicity that
W=W(\bm{F})
is
independent of either
\bm{x}
or
\bm{y}

W(\lambda\bm{F})=\lambda^{p}W(\bm{F}),\quad\forall\lambda>0,\ \forall\bm{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},

(3.15)

for some

It is straightforward to verify that the corresponding transformation group

\int_{\partial V}\left\{\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}+\frac{p-n}{p}\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}\right\}dS=0.

(3.16)

In the special case

M_{2D}=\int_{\Sigma}\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\,dS,

If

M_{3D}=\int_{\Sigma}\left\{\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}\right\}dS,

which can be viewed as a generalization of the relation first obtained in

3.3
Case study: Pohozaev-type identities

In this section we show how to use Noether’s formula (
2.47
) to
generalize the so called Pohozaev’s identity which is often used in the proof of various uniqueness results for nonlinear PDEs
[
86
,
113
]
.
Consider a boundary value problem for a nonlinear PDE of the form

\begin{cases}\nabla\cdot W_{\bm{F}}(\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))+\Phi_{\bm{y}}(\bm{y}(\bm{x}))=0,&\bm{x}\in\Omega,\\
\bm{y}(\bm{x})=0,&\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(3.17)

where the potentials
\Phi
and
W
are of class
C^{1}
,
\Phi(0)=0
,

W(\bm{F})
is positively homogeneous of degree
p\geq 1
, and

W(\bm{u}\otimes\bm{v})\geq 0
for all
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
We also assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is star-shaped with
respect to the origin
3
3

3

We are free to choose the origin arbitrarily because
the PDE in (
3.17
) does not depend on
\bm{x}
explicitly.
. The
key observation is that any solution
\bm{y}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{m})
must be a stationary extremal of the functional

E[\bm{y}]=\int_{\Omega}\{W(\nabla\bm{y})-\Phi(\bm{y})\}d\bm{x}.

(3.18)

To obtain the generalized Pohozaev’s identities we apply
Noether’s formula (
2.47
) to the functional (
3.18
)
for the family of transformations

\widetilde{\bm{x}}=e^{\epsilon}\bm{x},\quad\widetilde{\bm{y}}=e^{A\epsilon}\bm{y},

(3.19)

where
A\in\mathbb{R}
is arbitrary. In this case,
\delta\bm{x}=\bm{x}
,

\delta\bm{y}=A\bm{y}
, and the Noether’s formula (
2.47
) becomes

\delta E=\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\,dS,

(3.20)

since
\delta\bm{y}(\bm{x})=A\bm{y}(\bm{x})=0
on
\partial\Omega
, in view of the boundary conditions. Here

\bm{P}^{*}(\bm{x})=W(\nabla\bm{y})\bm{I}_{n}-(\nabla\bm{y})^{T}W_{\bm{F}}(\nabla\bm{y}),\quad\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega,

since
\Phi(0)=0
. We also compute

\delta E=\int_{\Omega}\{n(W(\nabla\bm{y})-\Phi(\bm{y}))+(A-1)\langle\nabla\bm{y},W_{\bm{F}}(\nabla\bm{y})\rangle-A\Phi_{\bm{y}}(\bm{y})\cdot\bm{y}\}d\bm{x}.

Since,
A
is arbitrary, Noether’s formula (
3.20
) implies
the two generalized Pohozaev’s identities for
C^{2}
solutions of the
boundary value problem (
3.17
):

\int_{\Omega}\{\langle\nabla\bm{y},W_{\bm{F}}(\nabla\bm{y})\rangle-\Phi_{\bm{y}}(\bm{y})\cdot\bm{y}\}d\bm{x}=0

(3.21)

and

\int_{\Omega}\{n(W(\nabla\bm{y})-\Phi(\bm{y}))-\langle W_{\bm{F}}(\nabla\bm{y}),\nabla\bm{y}\rangle\}d\bm{x}=\\
\int_{\partial\Omega}\{(W(\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n})-\langle W_{\bm{F}}(\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n}),\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n}\rangle)(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})\}dS,

(3.22)

where we have used the boundary conditions in (
3.17
), and denoted

\bm{a}(\bm{x})=\partial\bm{y}/\partial\bm{n}\in C^{1}(\partial\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
. The positive

p
-homogeneity of
W(\bm{F})
allows us to simplify Pohozaev’s identities:

\int_{\Omega}\{pW(\nabla\bm{y})-\Phi_{\bm{y}}(\bm{y})\cdot\bm{y}\}d\bm{x}=0,

(3.23)

\int_{\Omega}\{(n-p)W(\nabla\bm{y})-n\Phi(\bm{y})\}d\bm{x}=(1-p)\int_{\partial\Omega}W(\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n})(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})dS.

(3.24)

We can use (
3.23
) to eliminate
\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\bm{y})
from
(
3.24
):

\int_{\Omega}\{(n-p)\Phi_{\bm{y}}(\bm{y})\cdot\bm{y}-np\Phi(\bm{y})\}d\bm{x}=p(1-p)\int_{\partial\Omega}W(\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n})(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})dS.

By assumption,
W(\bm{a}(\bm{x})\otimes\bm{n}(\bm{x}))\geq 0
for all
\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega
,
while on a star-shaped domain we have
\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\geq 0
for all

\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega
. Therefore,

\int_{\Omega}\{np\Phi(\bm{y}(\bm{x}))-(n-p)\Phi_{\bm{y}}(\bm{y}(\bm{x}))\cdot\bm{y}(\bm{x})\}d\bm{x}\geq 0.

(3.25)

If the potential
\Phi(\bm{y})
is such that the integrand in
(
3.25
) is negative, except at
\bm{y}=0
, then
\bm{y}=0
is the
only smooth solution of the boundary value problem (
3.17
). Hence, we have proved
the following theorem.

Theorem
3.3
.

Suppose that
W\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
is a positively

p
-homogeneous function,
p\geq 1
, that is nonnegative on the rank-one cone.
Let
\Phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
be such that
\Phi(0)=0
and

np\Phi(\bm{y})\leq(n-p)\Phi_{\bm{y}}(\bm{y})\cdot\bm{y}

(3.26)

for all
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Then
\bm{y}(\bm{x})=0
is the only

C^{2}
solution of the boundary value problem (
3.17
), provided
\{0\}
is the connected component of

Z=\{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:np\Phi(\bm{y})=(n-p)\Phi_{\bm{y}}(\bm{y})\cdot\bm{y}\}

that contains
\{0\}
.

We note that if
\Phi(\bm{y})
is positively
k
-homogeneous, and
\Phi(\bm{y})>0
for all
\bm{y}\not=0
, then
\Phi(\bm{y})
satisfies (
3.26
) whenever

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\leq\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}.

Indeed,

np\Phi(\bm{y})-(n-p)\Phi_{\bm{y}}(\bm{y})\cdot\bm{y}=\Phi(\bm{y})(np-(n-p)k)\leq 0.

Therefore, the conclusion of Theorem
3.3
applies when

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}<\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}.

Note that in the critical case of equality in (
3.26
),

Z=\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and we must have

(1-p)W\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{y}}{\partial\bm{n}}\otimes\bm{n}\right)(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})=0,\quad\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega.

If
p>1
, the domain
is strictly star-shaped and
W
is positive on all the rank-one matrices, then
we also have
\partial\bm{y}/\partial\bm{n}=0
on
\partial\Omega
. In may cases, as in the
original Pohozaev’s PDE

\Delta u+a^{2}u^{\frac{n+2}{n-2}}=0,

this is
sufficient to assert that
\bm{y}=0
is the only solution.

4
Generalized Clapeyron theorem

In the previous section we presented several examples of
variational problems with partial invariance and the correspondence
between partial symmetries and partial conservation laws is exemplified by the classical
Clapeyron’s Theorem of linear elasticity. Here we show that it is but one member of a broader class of formulas expressing the stored elastic energy of the body in stable equilibrium in terms of physical and configurational tractions on its boundary. In what follows we show how different integral relations of this kind emerge as we vary our assumptions regarding the partial symmetries of the Lagrangian.
We refer to a particularly important formula from this class associated with scale invarince of an associated variational problem as the Generalized Clapeyron Theorem (GCT).

Assume that
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
satisfies all assumptions of Theorem
2.7
,
and consider a special class of scaling transformations of the form

\widetilde{\bm{x}}=e^{\epsilon}\bm{x},\quad\widetilde{\bm{y}}=e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}.

(4.1)

In this case
\delta\bm{x}=\bm{x}
,
\delta\bm{y}=\bm{y}
, and
\widetilde{\bm{y}}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})=e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}(\bm{x})=e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}(e^{-\epsilon}\widetilde{\bm{x}})
. We compute

\delta E=\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}W(e^{\epsilon}\bm{x},e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))e^{n\epsilon}d\bm{x}\right|_{\epsilon=0}=nE[\bm{y}]+\int_{\Omega}\{W_{\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{x}+W_{\bm{y}}\cdot\bm{y}(\bm{x})\}d\bm{x}.

(4.2)

By our assumptions,
\mathfrak{E}_{W}=0
and
\mathfrak{E}^{*}_{W}=-p^{*}_{\Sigma}\bm{n}\delta_{\Sigma},
. Therefore, the generalized Noether’s formula (
2.46
) gives the following representation of the elastic energy

nE[\bm{y}]=-\int_{\Omega}\{W_{\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{x}+W_{\bm{y}}\cdot\bm{y}(\bm{x})\}d\bm{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\{\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}+\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\}dS-\int_{\Sigma}p^{*}_{\Sigma}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\,dS,

(4.3)

where
-W_{\bm{y}}
has the meaning of externally applied bulk forces, while
-W_{\bm{x}}
describes external configurational bulk forces. Equation (
4.3
) represents the most general Clapeyron-type relation which we use below to derive our main results.

4.1
Scale-free theories

Suppose further that the simultaneous change of scale
\bm{x}\mapsto\lambda\bm{x}
,

\bm{y}\mapsto\lambda\bm{y}
, with
\lambda>0
, does not affect
the energy density.
In other words, we assume that there exists a partial symmetry of the form

W(\lambda\bm{x},\lambda\bm{y},\bm{F})=W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F}),\quad\forall\lambda>0.

(4.4)

If
W
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, property (
4.4
)
simply means that
W=W(\bm{F})
and does not depend on
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}

explicitly. If
W
is not required to be continuous at
\bm{x}=0
or

\bm{y}=0
, then the class of functions satisfying (
4.4
) is
much broader. In this case assumption (
4.4
) implies that

W_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})\cdot\bm{x}+W_{\bm{y}}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})\cdot\bm{y}=0

identically. Hence, we have proved the following theorem

Theorem
4.1
.

Suppose that
\Sigma\subset\Omega
is a smooth surface of jump
discontinuity of a Lipschitz extremal
\bm{y}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Sigma)
of the functional
(
2.1
). If the energy density function satisfies
(
4.4
), then

nE[\bm{y}]=\int_{\partial\Omega}\{\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}+\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\}dS-\int_{\Sigma}p^{*}_{\Sigma}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\,dS.

(4.5)

If
\bm{y}
is also stationary, i.e.
p^{*}_{\Sigma}=0
, then

E[\bm{y}]=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}+\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\}dS(\bm{x}).

(4.6)

Note that (
4.6
) is a generalization to not necessarily smooth extremals and arbitrary values of
m
and
n
of the known results in elasticity theory (
m=n=3
) first obtained in
[
29
, Eq. (16)]
and independently in
[
39
, formula (2.10)]
(see also
[
57
, Eq. (2.5)]
and
[
47
, Eq. (38)]
).

We now turn to the interpretation of the obtained result. Observe first that due to the
presence of the configurational stress
\bm{P}^{*}
and the prefactor
1/n
, the relation (
4.6
)
cannot be regarded as a simple “energy = work” relation, even
though the first term seems to be very similar to the work of tractions

\bm{P}\bm{n}
over displacements
\bm{u}=\bm{y}-\bm{x}
on the boundary of the
domain, since we can rewrite (
4.6
) as

E[\bm{y}]=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{u}+\bm{P}_{\rm disp}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\}dS(\bm{x}),

where

\bm{P}_{\rm disp}^{*}=W\bm{I}_{n}-(\nabla\bm{u})^{T}\bm{P}.

Note also that (
4.6
) is clearly fundamentally different
from the classical Clapeyron’s theorem (
1.1
), where the additive term on the right-hand side with the Eshelby
(Hamilton, energy-momentum) tensor
\bm{P}^{*}
is absent and even in 3D space instead of
1/n
the multiple in front of the surface integral is
1/2
. As we are going to see in what follows the coefficient 1/2 emerges when we
impose an additional assumption that the energy is a quadratic
function of deformation gradient. The origin of the Eshelby stress in (
4.6
) is
more interesting. It reflects a subtle fact that an elastic body may
have a nonzero energy even if it is not loaded either internally by “physical”
bulk forces or externally by “physical” surface tractions.

We recall that in the previous
section we have already discussed a situation of this type in our Example
2.6
. Using Theorem
4.1
we can generalize it to shed more light on what the loading by configurational tractions
\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}
is all about.
Suppose that there exists a “transformation strain”
\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\widehat{\bm{x}})
, such that the energy density
W(\widehat{\bm{x}},\bm{\varepsilon})
satisfies, for any
\bm{\varepsilon}\in\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and any unit vector
\widehat{\bm{x}}
, an inequality

W(\widehat{\bm{x}},\bm{\varepsilon})\geq c|\bm{\varepsilon}-\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\widehat{\bm{x}})|^{p},

(4.7)

where
p>0
and
c>0
. Note that the energy density (
2.33
) with (
2.35
) in our Example
2.6
is of this type.

Now, if there exists a Lipschitz stationary equilibrium
\bm{u}_{0}
in
\Omega
satisfying both
\bm{P}\bm{n}=0
and
\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}=0
on
\partial\Omega
in the sense of traces, then, according to (
4.6
) we must have

n\int_{\Omega}W(\widehat{\bm{x}},e(\bm{u}_{0}))d\bm{x}=0.

But then

\int_{\Omega}|e(\bm{u}_{0})-\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\widehat{\bm{x}})|^{p}d\bm{x}\leq\displaystyle\frac{1}{c}\int_{\Omega}W(\widehat{\bm{x}},e(\bm{u}_{0}))d\bm{x}=0.

It would then mean that
\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\widehat{\bm{x}})=e(\bm{u}_{0})
is a compatible strain.

To make clear the connection between the incompatibility of
\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\widehat{\bm{x}})
and the configurational tractions on the boundary we now rephrase the problem. Suppose that
\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\widehat{\bm{x}})
is an incompatible strain field, and suppose that
\bm{u}_{0}
is the minimizer in

\mathcal{M}=\min_{\bm{u}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\int_{\Omega}W(\widehat{\bm{x}},e(\bm{u}))d\bm{x}.

(4.8)

Observe that due to (
4.7
), we can regard
\mathcal{M}>0
as a measure of incompatibility of
\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\widehat{\bm{x}})
. At the same time, the minimizer
\bm{u}_{0}
in (
4.8
) must be a stationary extremal, satisfying
\bm{P}\bm{n}=0
. But then, Theorem
4.1
gives

\mathcal{M}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\,dS,

which ultimately links the “applied” configurational tractions to the incompatibility of the “transformation strain”
\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\widehat{\bm{x}})
.

Since in our Example
2.6
we could obtain the solution of the energy minimization problem explicitly, we could also directly
compute the stored elastic energy which turned out to be
different from zero despite the absence of “physical” loading. Here we independently
compute the same energy by applying
Theorem
4.1
; it is obviously
applicable because the energy denisity (
2.33
) with
(
2.35
) satisfies (
4.4
). We obtain

E[\bm{u}]=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\,dS=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\,dS.

(4.9)

where we used the fact that in our example “physical” tractions were absent and therefore

\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}=W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})\bm{n}.
Furthermore, in our special case
formula (
4.9
) gives

E[\bm{u}]=\frac{R}{n}\int_{\partial B(0,R)}W(\bm{x},\nabla\bm{y})dS=\frac{R|\partial B(0,R)|}{2n}\left((\eta^{\prime}(R)-a)^{2}+(n-1)\frac{\eta(R)^{2}}{R^{2}}\right).

Using the explicit solution

\eta(r)=\frac{a}{n}\left(r+(n-1)r\ln\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)\right),

we can then obtain the expression for the energy

E[\bm{u}]=\frac{a^{2}(n-1)}{2n^{2}}|B(0,R)|,

which expectedly agrees with the rescaled energy (
2.43
).

We have therefore illustrated the fact that even if a body is unloaded externally
(in view of the absence of physical forces) it can be still loaded
externally by “applied” configurational forces. Such loading would then reveal itself
through a residual prestress generated by the acquired
incompatibility.
We reiterate that such an incompatibility should be viewed as being effectively embedded into the
body during the process of its creation, which can be in turn viewed as an outcome of the the work of
configurational forces
\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}
applied on the boundary of the
body. Specifically, in our Example
2.6
a nonzero configurational loading was represented by the configurational tractions

\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}=\frac{a^{2}(n-1)}{2n^{2}}\bm{n}\not=0.

We reiterate that the classical Clapeyron’s Theorem does not account for such “work of creation” since it cannot accommodate any transformation strain as it breaks the homogeneity of degree 2, required for classical Clapeyron to hold. Instead, our GCT remains suitable because of the scale-invariant nature of the transformation strain in our Example
2.6
.

We finish this section with a remark that will prove useful in what follows.

Remark 4.2
.

When
W
does not depend explicitly on
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
, the
stationary extremals satisfy

\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}\bm{n}\,dS(\bm{x})=\bm{0},\qquad\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\,dS(\bm{x})=\bm{0}.

These relations allow one to re-write the Clapeyron
Theorem
4.1
as

\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\bm{y})d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{(\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n},\bm{x}-\bm{a})+(\bm{P}\bm{n},\bm{y}(\bm{x})-\bm{b})\}dS(\bm{x}),

(4.10)

where
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are arbitrary constant
vectors.

4.2
Problems with constrants

We now show that the relation (
4.6
) can be easily generalized to cover the case of scale-free energy with scale-free pointwise constraints. A typical example is incompressible elasticity, where every deformation
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
must satisfy the incompressibility constraint

\det\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x})=1

(4.11)

for all
\bm{x}\in\Omega
. Specifically, one can prove the following theorem:

Theorem
4.3
.

Suppose that the deformation
\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x})
satisfies
i=1,2,3...k
pointwise constraints

C^{i}(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))=0,

(4.12)

where both functions
W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})
and
C^{i}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})
are scale-free in the sense of (
4.4
). Then, if
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is a
C^{1}
energy minimizer that satisfies the constraints, the following relation holds

\int_{\Omega}nW(\bm{x},\bm{y},\nabla\bm{y})d\bm{x}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\{(\bm{P}_{W}-\Lambda_{i}(\bm{x})\bm{P}_{C^{i}})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}+(\bm{P}^{*}_{W}-\Lambda_{i}(\bm{x})\bm{P}^{*}_{C^{i}})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\}dS,

(4.13)

where
\Lambda_{i}(\bm{x})
are the Lagrange multipliers corresponding to the constraints (
4.12
).

Proof.

From the general theory of constrained variational problems we know that the
C^{1}
energy minimizers
can be obtained as stationary extremals of the augmented Lagrangian

L(\bm{x},\hat{\bm{y}},\hat{\bm{F}})=W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})-\bm{\Lambda}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{C}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F}),

Here the functions
\bm{\Lambda}(\bm{x})
are Lagrange multipliers,
\hat{\bm{y}}=[\bm{y};\bm{\Lambda}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m+k}
,
\widehat{\bm{F}}=[\bm{F};\bm{G}]\in\mathbb{R}^{(m+k)\times n}
and
\bm{G}=\nabla\bm{\Lambda}
.
Note first that the augmented Lagrangian is affine in
\bm{\Lambda}
and does not depend explicitly on
\bm{G}
.

In the regular case, when
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is the energy minimizer satisfying the constraints, there are smooth Lagrange multipliers
\Lambda_{i}(\bm{x})
, such that
\hat{\bm{y}}=[\bm{y};\bm{\Lambda}]
solves the Euler-Lagrange equations
\mathfrak{E}_{L}(\bm{q})=0
for the augmented Lagrangian, and hence also the stationarity equations
\mathfrak{E}^{*}_{L}(\bm{q})=0
.

Note next that by assumption both the Lagrangian and the constraints are scale-free. Therefore we can again consider the action of the following relevant deformation

\bm{X}=e^{\epsilon}\bm{x},\quad\widehat{\bm{Y}}=[e^{\epsilon}\bm{y};\Lambda].

The deformed functional

E_{\epsilon}[\widehat{\bm{Y}}]=\int_{e^{\epsilon}\Omega}L(\bm{X},\widehat{\bm{Y}}_{\epsilon}(\bm{X}),\nabla\widehat{\bm{Y}}_{\epsilon}(\bm{X}))d\bm{X}

becomes, after the change of variables
\widetilde{\bm{x}}=e^{\epsilon}\bm{x}

E_{\epsilon}=\epsilon^{n\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}\{W(e^{\epsilon}\bm{x},e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))-\bm{\Lambda}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{C}(e^{\epsilon}\bm{x},e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))\}d\bm{x}.

and since both
W
and
\bm{C}
satisfy (
4.4
), we have

E_{\epsilon}=\epsilon^{n\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}\{W(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))-\bm{\Lambda}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{C}(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))\}d\bm{x}=\epsilon^{n\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}Wd\bm{x},

where we took into account that
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
satisfies the constraints
\bm{C}(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))=0
.
Therefore,

\delta E=n\int_{\Omega}W(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))d\bm{x},

and Noether identity (
2.47
) becomes (
4.13
),
since

\delta\bm{x}=\bm{x}
,
\delta\widehat{\bm{y}}=[\bm{y};0]
,

\bm{P}_{L}=[\bm{P}_{W}-\Lambda_{i}(\bm{x})\bm{P}_{C^{i}};0],\quad\bm{P}_{L}^{*}=\bm{P}^{*}_{W}-\Lambda_{i}(\bm{x})\bm{P}^{*}_{C^{i}}.

∎

In particular, when we deal with incompressible elasticity and the equilibrium configuration
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
has to
satisfy the constraint (
4.11
), the GCT reads

\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{(\bm{P}-p(\bm{x})\mathrm{cof}\bm{F})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}(\bm{x})+(\bm{P}^{*}+p(\bm{x})\bm{I}_{n})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\}dS(\bm{x}).

(4.14)

Here, following convention, we have denoted the Lagrange multiplier
\Lambda(\bm{x})
by
p(\bm{x})
to indicate its physical meaning as pressure, and took into account that

\bm{P}^{*}_{C}=(\det\bm{F}-1)\bm{I}_{n}-\bm{F}^{T}\mathrm{cof}(\bm{F})=-\bm{I}_{n}.

Note that when
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is an orientation preserving diffeomorphism, we can change variables
\bm{y}=\bm{y}(\bm{x})
in the first term on the right-hand side of (
4.14
) and obtain

\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\bm{y})d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega^{*}}(\bm{\sigma}-p(\bm{y})\bm{I}_{n})\bm{N}\cdot\bm{y}\,dS^{*}(\bm{y})+\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}(\bm{P}^{*}+p(\bm{x})\bm{I}_{n})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\,dS(\bm{x}),

(4.15)

where
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{y})
is the Cauchy stress tensor,
\bm{N}(\bm{y})
is the outward unit normal to
\partial\Omega^{*}=y(\partial\Omega)
, and
p(\bm{y})=p(\bm{x})
, with
\bm{y}=\bm{y}(\bm{x})
.

4.3
p
-homogeneous energies

Now assume that the Lagrangian
W
instead of scale invariance has the following property of

p
-homogeneity

W(\bm{x},\lambda\bm{y},\lambda\bm{F})=\lambda^{p}W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F}).

(4.16)

In this case the deformation

\widetilde{\bm{x}}=\bm{x},\qquad\widetilde{\bm{y}}=e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}

(4.17)

results in
\widetilde{\bm{y}}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})=e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}(\bm{x})=e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})
, so that

E[\widetilde{\bm{y}}](\epsilon)=\int_{\Omega}W(\widetilde{\bm{x}},e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}),e^{\epsilon}\nabla\bm{y}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}))d\widetilde{\bm{x}}=e^{p\epsilon}E[\bm{y}].

We have now
\delta\bm{x}=0
,
\delta\bm{y}=\bm{y}
which leads to a new Clapeyron-type relation satisfied by
equilibrium energy

\int_{\Omega}W(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}\,dS.

(4.18)

We stress that the relation (
4.18
) is an expression of the
p
-homogeneity of the energy
density that manifests itself through a rescaling of the displacements

\bm{y}
without the concommittant rescaling of the Lagrangian
coordinates. Therefore the validity of (
4.18
) does not require that
\bm{y}
be stationary, i.e. solving (
2.12
). Also, since
\delta\bm{x}=0
the relation
(
4.18
) remains valid even when the Lagrangian
W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})
as a function of
\bm{x}
is only
measurable. We note that while (
4.18
) can be interpreted as a direct generalization of the classical Clapeyron’s theorem because the latter is clearly a special case of the former, we preserve the name GCT for (
4.6
) as it marks a more radical generalization of this classical result linking together the work of physical and configurational forces.

Thus, below we show that a whole family of GCT type results emerge when scale invariance coexist with
p
-homogeneity. In particular we show that if the energy gets rescaled when we stretch the Lagrangian
coordinates, without the concommittant stretching of the displacements,

W(\lambda^{-1}\bm{x},\bm{y},\lambda\bm{F})=\lambda^{p}W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F}),

(4.19)

then the energy can be expressed as a surface integral involving only the
conjugate momenta
(\bm{P}^{*})^{T}\bm{x}
.

Indeed, in this case the transformation

\widetilde{\bm{x}}=e^{\epsilon}\bm{x},\qquad\widetilde{\bm{y}}=\bm{y}

(4.20)

results in
\widetilde{\bm{y}}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})=\bm{y}(\bm{x})=\bm{y}(e^{-\epsilon}\widetilde{\bm{x}})
, so that

E[\widetilde{\bm{y}}](\epsilon)=\int_{e^{\epsilon}\Omega}W(\widetilde{\bm{x}},\bm{y}(e^{-\epsilon}\widetilde{\bm{x}}),e^{-\epsilon}\nabla\bm{y}(e^{-\epsilon}\widetilde{\bm{x}}))d\widetilde{\bm{x}}=\\
e^{-p\epsilon}\int_{e^{\epsilon}\Omega}W(e^{-\epsilon}\widetilde{\bm{x}},\bm{y}(e^{-\epsilon}\widetilde{\bm{x}}),\nabla\bm{y}(e^{-\epsilon}\widetilde{\bm{x}}))d\widetilde{\bm{x}}=e^{(n-p)\epsilon}E[\bm{y}].

Now we have
\delta\bm{x}=\bm{x}
,
\delta\bm{y}=0
, and therefore

E[\bm{y}]=\int_{\Omega}W(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n-p}\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\,dS,

(4.21)

if
n\not=p
, and

\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\,dS=0,

(4.22)

if
n=p
.

More generally, it is clear that in the case when
W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})
is both scale-free and
p
-homogeneous, in
the sense of (
4.4
) and (
4.16
), respectively, then (
4.19
) holds as a consequence:

W(\lambda^{-1}\bm{x},\bm{y},\lambda\bm{F})=W(\lambda^{-1}\bm{x},\lambda\lambda^{-1}\bm{y},\lambda\bm{F})=\lambda^{p}W(\lambda^{-1}\bm{x},\lambda^{-1}\bm{y},\bm{F})=\lambda^{p}W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F}).

Thus both formulas (
4.18
) and (
4.21
) (or (
4.22
), if
n=p
) hold.
Combining (
4.18
) and (
4.21
), when
n\not=p
, we also obtain the relation between the two terms on the right-hand side of (
4.6
):

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}\,dS=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n-p}\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\,dS.

(4.23)

Note that in view of (
4.23
) equation (
4.6
) which does not
contain
p
can be obtained as a linear combination of (
4.18
) and (
4.21
).
We remark that formula (
4.23
) is valid for any subdomain of
\Omega
and can therefore be expressed as an apparently new concervation law

\nabla\cdot\left(p(\bm{P}^{*})^{T}\bm{x}-(n-p)\bm{P}^{T}\bm{y}\right)=0,

(4.24)

corresponding to the full variational symmetry unifying scale-invariance and
p
-homogeneity and described by the transformation group
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\mapsto(e^{p\epsilon}\bm{x},e^{(p-n)\epsilon}\bm{y})
,
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}
.

It is instructive to present explicit examples of Lagrangians that are both scale-free and
p
-homogeneous.
If
W
depends on all of its variables, it would have to satisfy

W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})=\left(\frac{|\bm{y}|}{|\bm{x}|}\right)^{p}W\left(\hat{\bm{x}},\hat{\bm{y}},\frac{|\bm{x}|}{|\bm{y}|}\bm{F}\right),\quad\hat{\bm{x}}=\frac{\bm{x}}{|\bm{x}|},\ \hat{\bm{y}}=\frac{\bm{y}}{|\bm{y}|}.

If
W
does not depend explicitly either on
\bm{x}
or on
\bm{y}
, then

W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})=W(\widehat{\bm{w}},\bm{F}),\qquad W(\widehat{\bm{w}},\lambda\bm{F})=\lambda^{p}W(\widehat{\bm{w}},\bm{F}),

for every
\lambda>0
, where
\bm{w}
is either
\bm{x}
or
\bm{y}
.

Let us now demonstrate that in some sense the
p
-homogeneous Clapeyron-type Theorem
(
4.18
) implies the GCT represented by (
4.6
). This becomes clear if we realize that at their source, the transformations (
2.4
)
treat the
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
-space

as a single entity. Therefore, to see the implied relation we need to go back to the description of the problem in the extended space introduced in Section

.

We recall that in this space the original Lagrangian

is replaced by the

graph
-Lagrangian

where
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}

, and

\bm{F}_{1}
is the upper
n\times n
submatrix of
\bm{{\mathcal{F}}}
.

Now, if

\bm{z}

{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{{\mathcal{F}}})

Using formula (

\bm{P}(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{y}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{t})))\mathrm{cof}(\nabla_{\bm{t}}\bm{\eta}(\bm{t}))\bm{\nu}(\bm{t})\cdot\bm{y}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{t}))\}dS(\bm{t}),

4.4
Case study: shock waves

Using the formula for the derivative of an integral over a moving

-\int_{S(t)}V^{\rm sh}[\![\bm{v}\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{F}\bm{x})]\!]dS.

(4.29)

We also have

\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\{\bm{v}\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{F}\bm{x})\}\right)_{\rm reg}=\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{v}}{\partial t}\right)_{\rm reg}\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{F}\bm{x})+\bm{v}\cdot(\bm{v}-(\nabla\bm{v})_{\rm reg}\bm{x})=\\

Integrating
(\nabla|\bm{v}|^{2})_{\rm reg}\cdot\bm{x}
by parts, we obtain

\int_{\Omega(t)}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\{\bm{v}\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{F}\bm{x})\}\right)_{\rm reg}d\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega(t)}\left\{\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{v}}{\partial t}\right)_{\rm reg}\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{F}\bm{x})+\frac{n+2}{2}|\bm{v}|^{2}\right\}d\bm{x}\\
-\int_{\partial\Omega(t)}\frac{1}{2}|\bm{v}|^{2}\bm{x}\cdot\bm{n}\,dS+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{S(t)}[\![|\bm{v}|^{2}]\!]\bm{x}\cdot\bm{n}^{\rm sh}dS.

Substituting this into (
4.29
) and back into (
4.28
),
we obtain the desired dynamical version of the Clapeyron Theorem

n\int_{\Omega(t)}Ud\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega(t)}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{v}}{\partial t}\right)_{\rm reg}\cdot(\bm{F}\bm{x}-\bm{y})d\bm{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega(t)}\{\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}+\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\}dS(\bm{x})\\
+\int_{S(t)}\{V^{\rm sh}[\![\bm{v}\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{F}\bm{x})]\!]-[\![\bm{v}]\!]\cdot\{{\{{\!\mkern-14.7mu}\mkern 14.7mu}{}\!\bm{v}\!\!{\}{\}\mkern 14.7mu}\mkern-14.7mu}{}(\bm{x}\cdot\bm{n}^{\rm sh})-p^{*}_{S(t)}\bm{x}\cdot\bm{n}^{\rm sh}\}dS(\bm{x}),

where we have passed from the space-time

to its spatial version via (
2.66
).
Next, using Hadamard’s kinematic compatibility relations
(
2.63
) on the shock in space-time we simplify the right-hand side of the relation above, by observing that

V^{\rm sh}[\![\bm{v}\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{F}\bm{x})]\!]-[\![\bm{v}]\!]\cdot\{{\{{\!\mkern-14.7mu}\mkern 14.7mu}{}\!\bm{v}\!\!{\}{\}\mkern 14.7mu}\mkern-14.7mu}{}(\bm{x}\cdot\bm{n}^{\rm sh})=V^{\rm sh}[\![\bm{v}]\!]\cdot(\bm{y}-\{{\{{\!\mkern-14.7mu}\mkern 14.7mu}{}\!\bm{F}\!\!{\}{\}\mkern 14.7mu}\mkern-14.7mu}{}\bm{x}),

and obtain our preliminary version of the dynamic Clapeyron Theorem

n\int_{\Omega(t)}Ud\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega(t)}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{v}}{\partial t}\right)_{\rm reg}\cdot(\bm{F}\bm{x}-\bm{y})d\bm{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega(t)}\{\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}+\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\}dS(\bm{x})\\
+\int_{S(t)}\{V^{\rm sh}[\![\bm{v}]\!]\cdot(\bm{y}-\{{\{{\!\mkern-14.7mu}\mkern 14.7mu}{}\!\bm{F}\!\!{\}{\}\mkern 14.7mu}\mkern-14.7mu}{}\bm{x})-p^{*}_{S(t)}\bm{x}\cdot\bm{n}^{\rm sh}\}dS(\bm{x}),

(4.30)

To associate physical meaning with different terms in the right-hand side of (

), we first introduce several notations. Thus, we observe that

\bm{b}_{i}=-\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{v}}{\partial t}\right)_{\rm reg}

is the regular component of the inertial (d’Alambert) body force
density. The Noether identity (
2.13
) assigns to

\bm{b}_{i}^{*}=\bm{F}^{T}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{v}}{\partial t}\right)_{\rm reg}=-\bm{F}^{T}\bm{b}_{i}

the meaning of the the regular component of the inertial configurational body force
density.

The distributional part of the inertial force density

-\bm{a}=-\partial\bm{v}/\partial t
is supported on the shock surface and can be also interperted as
inertial traction on the shock; it takes the form

\bm{t}_{i}=V^{\rm sh}[\![\bm{v}]\!].

Similar reasoning suggests that

\bm{t}_{i}^{*}=-V^{\rm sh}\{{\{{\!\mkern-14.7mu}\mkern 14.7mu}{}\!\bm{F}\!\!{\}{\}\mkern 14.7mu}\mkern-14.7mu}{}^{T}[\![\bm{v}]\!]=-\{{\{{\!\mkern-14.7mu}\mkern 14.7mu}{}\!\bm{F}\!\!{\}{\}\mkern 14.7mu}\mkern-14.7mu}{}^{T}\bm{t}_{i}

can be identified with the configurational inertial tractions on the
shock.

Using these notations, we can now present the dynamic GCT in the final form

\int_{\Omega(t)}Ud\bm{x}=\frac{\mathfrak{S}+\mathfrak{I}}{n},

(4.31)

where we separated in the right-hand side of (
4.31
) the “static” term
\mathfrak{S}
from the “dynamic”, or inertial term
\mathfrak{I}
. The former

\mathfrak{S}=\int_{\partial\Omega(t)}\{\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}+\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\}dS(\bm{x})-\int_{S(t)}p^{*}_{S(t)}\bm{x}\cdot\bm{n}^{\rm sh}dS(\bm{x}).

(4.32)

represents the work of the physical and configurational forces on the
boundary of the domain, minus the energy dissipated on the shock
surface (see formula (
4.5
) in the statics case).
The latter

\mathfrak{I}=\int_{\Omega(t)}\{\bm{b}_{i}\cdot\bm{y}+\bm{b}_{i}^{*}\cdot\bm{x}\}d\bm{x}+\int_{S(t)}\{\bm{t}_{i}\cdot\bm{y}+\bm{t}_{i}^{*}\cdot\bm{x}\}dS(\bm{x})

(4.33)

represents the work of the inertial physical and configurational
forces inside the domain, plus the work of the corresponding inertial
tractions acting on the shock surface.

5
Applications

In this section we show the utility of the Clapeyron Theorem in various problems of mechanics and physics rooted in the Calculus of Variations. Throughout this section we assume that
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is a Lipschitz stationary extremal in
\Omega
that is class
C^{2}
on a neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
, as in Remark
2.8
.

5.1
Necessary condition of metastability

The problem of metastability is central for nonlinear elasticity theory and, more generally, for vectorial variational problems with nonconvex energy density. In our special setting, it concerns the existence of strong local minimia that are not automatically global.
To analyze this problem we can take advantage of the fact that GCT gives a formula for the energy in terms of the boundary values of the solution and its gradients. The idea is to consider the difference of two such expressions and rearrange it advantageously.
As our subsequent analysis shows a helpful hint is to isolate in the boundary integral an expression involving a Weierstrass excess function

{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F},\bm{G})=W(\bm{G})-W(\bm{F})-(W_{\bm{F}}(\bm{F}),\bm{G}-\bm{F}),

(5.1)

and then compare the energies of two different configurations with the same Dirchlet data. The result of the implied rearrangement can be formulated in the form of a theorem which relies essentially on the GCT.

Theorem
5.1
.

Assume that the Lipschitz deformations
\bm{y}_{1}(\bm{x})
and
\bm{y}_{2}(\bm{x})
are
stationary, of class
C^{2}
near
\partial\Omega
and that

\bm{y}_{1}(\bm{x})=\bm{y}_{2}(\bm{x})=\bm{y}_{0}(\bm{x}),\quad\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega.

(5.2)

Then

\int_{\Omega}\{W(\bm{F}_{2})-W(\bm{F}_{1})\}d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F}_{1},\bm{F}_{2})(\bm{x},\bm{n})+(\bm{P}_{1}-\bm{P}_{2})\bm{n}\cdot(\bm{F}_{2}\bm{x}-\bm{y}_{0})\}dS(\bm{x}),

(5.3)

where

\bm{F}_{j}=\nabla\bm{y}_{j}(\bm{x}),\qquad\bm{P}_{j}=W_{\bm{F}}(\nabla\bm{y}_{j}(\bm{x})),\qquad j=1,2.

Proof.

Since the energy is scale-free, we can use the nonlinear Clapeyron Theorem (
4.6
) and write

\int_{\Omega}W(\bm{F}_{i})d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{\bm{P}_{i}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}_{0}+\bm{P}_{i}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\}dS,

Subtracting these two equations and expanding
\bm{P}^{*}_{j}
via (
2.8
) we obtain

\int_{\Omega}\{W(\bm{F}_{2})-W(\bm{F}_{1})\}d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F}_{1},\bm{F}_{2})(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})+(\bm{P}_{1}-\bm{P}_{2})\bm{n}\cdot(\bm{F}_{2}\bm{x}-\bm{y}_{0})+\\
\langle\bm{P}_{1},\bm{F}_{2}-\bm{F}_{1}\rangle(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})+(\bm{P}_{1}\bm{n},(\bm{F}_{1}-\bm{F}_{2})\bm{x})\}dS.

Since
\bm{y}_{1}
and
\bm{y}_{2}
are of class
C^{2}
near
\partial\Omega
and
\bm{y}_{1}-\bm{y}_{2}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, we conclude that there exists a
C^{1}
field
\bm{a}:\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, such that
\bm{F}_{1}-\bm{F}_{2}=\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n}
. Therefore,

(\bm{P}_{1}\bm{n},(\bm{F}_{1}-\bm{F}_{2})\bm{x})=(\bm{P}_{1}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{a})(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})=\langle\bm{P}_{1},\bm{F}_{1}-\bm{F}_{2}\rangle(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}),

and (
5.3
) follows.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

Switching
\bm{y}_{1}
and
\bm{y}_{2}
we also have

\int_{\Omega}\{W(\bm{F}_{2})-W(\bm{F}_{1})\}d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{-{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F}_{2},\bm{F}_{1})(\bm{x},\bm{n})+(\bm{P}_{1}-\bm{P}_{2})\bm{n}\cdot(\bm{F}_{1}\bm{x}-\bm{y}_{0})\}dS(\bm{x}).

(5.4)

Taking the average of (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) we obtain a more symmetric version of the energy increment formula

\int_{\Omega}\{W(\bm{F}_{2})-W(\bm{F}_{1})\}d\bm{x}=\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}({\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F}_{1},\bm{F}_{2})-{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F}_{2},\bm{F}_{1}))(\bm{x},\bm{n})+(\bm{P}_{1}-\bm{P}_{2},\{{\{{\!\mkern-14.7mu}\mkern 14.7mu}{}\!\bm{F}\!\!{\}{\}\mkern 14.7mu}\mkern-14.7mu}{}\bm{x}-\bm{y}_{0})\right\}dS(\bm{x}),

where
\{{\{{\!\mkern-14.7mu}\mkern 14.7mu}{}\!\bm{F}\!\!{\}{\}\mkern 14.7mu}\mkern-14.7mu}{}=(\bm{F}_{1}+\bm{F}_{2})/2
. Subtracting (
5.3

\int_{\partial\Omega}({\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F}_{1},\bm{F}_{2})+{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F}_{2},\bm{F}_{1}))(\bm{x},\bm{n})dS(\bm{x})=\int_{\partial\Omega}((\bm{P}_{2}-\bm{P}_{1})\bm{n},(\bm{F}_{2}-\bm{F}_{1})\bm{x})dS(\bm{x})

(5.5)

Observe further that if
\bm{y}_{1}
and
\bm{y}_{2}
, satisfying (

), are smooth near
\partial\Omega
, then
\bm{F}_{2}-\bm{F}_{1}=\bm{a}(\bm{x})\otimes\bm{n}(\bm{x})
for all

is an outward unit normal to

is rank-one convex, we have for all

{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F}_{1},\bm{F}_{2})=W(\bm{F}_{2})-W(\bm{F}_{1})-\langle\bm{P}_{1},\bm{F}_{2}-\bm{F}_{1}\rangle\geq 0.

In view of these observations and the fact that, if the origin is a star point in

\int_{\Omega}\{W(\bm{F}_{2})-W(\bm{F}_{1})\}d\bm{x}\geq\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{(\bm{P}_{1}-\bm{P}_{2})\bm{n}\cdot(\bm{F}_{2}\bm{x}-\bm{y}_{0})\}dS(\bm{x}).

(5.6)

Now, if
\bm{y}_{2}(\bm{x})

One can see that to allow for metastaility condition

.

5.2
Quasiconvex envelope

To formulate the problem considered in this section we would need first to recall several standard definitions. The most important one is the definition of the quasiconvex envelope of the energy density
W(\bm{F})
—the largest quasiconvex function that does not exceed
W(\bm{F})
. It is denoted
QW
and is often referred to as quasiconvexification of
W
. There is a formula for
QW

[
21
]

QW(\bm{F})|D|=\inf_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\By|_{\Md D}=\BF\Bx}{\By\in W^{1,\infty}(D;\bb{R}^{m})}}\int_{D}W(\nabla\bm{y})d\bm{x}.

(5.8)

We would also need the notion of rank-one convexity, i.e. convexity along all straight lines connecting two points that differ by a rank-one matrix. Similarly to the quasiconvex envelope of
W
we define the rank-one convex envelope
RW
to be the largest rank-one convex function that does not exceed
W
. It was shown in
[
76
]
that every quasiconvex function is rank-one convex and therefore,
RW(\bm{F})\geq QW(\bm{F})
. The question whether
QW(\bm{F})=RW(\bm{F})
has been answered in the negative in
[
104
]
, except in the case
m=2
. Nonetheless examples where
QW(\bm{F})\not=RW(\bm{F})
are extremely rare
[
35
]
.

While, in many cases the variational problem (
5.8
) has no solutions, it is known that the
quasiconvexification
QW
of
W
does not depend on the choice of the domain
D
in (
5.8
), and there are cases when one is be able to choose a particular domain
D
, where Lipschitz solutions do exist
[
38
]
. In those cases, the minimizers must be stationary extremals for which GCT is applicable.

In this context one can regard such minimizers as known and pose the question whether GCT provides information about the function
QW
in general, rather than about a specific solution of equilibrium equations. One representative result of this type, allowing one to transfer the knowledge about quasiconvexity from the boundary to the interior of the domain, is formulated below in the form of a theorem:

Theorem
5.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a star-shaped domain with Lipschitz boundary. Assume that

(BVP)

For a given
\bm{F}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
the function
\overline{\bm{y}}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
solves

\nabla\cdot\bm{P}(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}})=\bm{0},\quad\nabla\cdot\bm{P}^{*}(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}})=\bm{0},

(5.9)

in the sense of distributions, and satisfies
\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})=\bm{F}_{0}\bm{x}
for all
\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega
;

(REG)

\overline{\bm{y}}
is of class
C^{2}
near
\partial\Omega
;

(RCX)

W(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x}))=RW(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x}))
for a.e.
\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega
;

(Q=R)

RW(\bm{F}_{0})=QW(\bm{F}_{0})
.

Then

(i)

RW(\bm{F}_{0})=QW(\bm{F}_{0})=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}})d\bm{x}
, i.e.
\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})
is the global minimizer in (
5.8
).

(ii)

QW(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x}))=W(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x}))
for a.e.
\bm{x}\in\Omega
.

Proof.

Assumption (BVP) implies that the Clapeyron theorem is applicable to the stationary equilibrium
\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})
of (
5.8
) we obtain, by virture of (REG),

\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}})d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}(W(\bm{F}_{0}+\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n})-\bm{P}(\bm{F}_{0}+\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{a})(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})dS.

(5.10)

Next, we appeal to
[
37

.
We quote the part of the lemma needed for the proof.

Lemma
5.4
.

Let
V(\bm{F}_{0})
be a rank-one convex function such that
V(\bm{F}_{0})\leq W(\bm{F}_{0})
. Let

{\mathcal{A}}_{V}=\{\bm{F}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:W(\bm{F}_{0})=V(\bm{F}_{0})\}.

Then for every
\bm{F}_{*}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{V}
,
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\bm{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

V(\bm{F}_{*}+\bm{b}\otimes\bm{m})\geq W(\bm{F}_{*})+\bm{P}(\bm{F}_{*})\bm{m}\cdot\bm{b}.

(5.11)

We now apply Lemma
5.4
by choosing

,

\bm{F}_{*}=\nabla\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})=\bm{F}_{0}+\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n}
, and
\bm{b}\otimes\bm{m}=-\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n}
,
for each
\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega
. The

Finally, we use the assumption that

5.3
Probing the binodal

It is well-known
[
3
]
that if
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is a strong local minimizer of (
2.1
), then the function
\bm{F}\mapsto W(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\bm{F})
must be quasiconvex at
\bm{F}
, i.e. satisfy the inequality

\int_{D}\{W(\bm{F}+\nabla\bm{\phi}(\bm{x}))-W(\bm{F})\}d\bm{x}\geq 0\quad\forall\bm{\phi}\in W_{0}^{1,\infty}(D;\mathbb{R}^{m}),

(5.14)

where
D
is any domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus, the gradient of any metastable configuration must avoid the binodal region of the phase space

{\mathcal{B}}=\left\{\bm{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:\exists\bm{\phi}\in W_{0}^{1,\infty}(D;\mathbb{R}^{m}):\int_{D}\{W(\bm{F}+\nabla\bm{\phi}(\bm{x}))-W(\bm{F})\}d\bm{x}<0\right\}.

Its boundary
\partial{\mathcal{B}}
is called the
binodal
.

Since the function is quasiconvex, if and only if its binodal region is empty,
the binodal region can then be characterized by the inequality
[
50
]

{\mathcal{B}}=\{\bm{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:QW(\bm{F})<W(\bm{F})\}.

Note also that if
W(\bm{F}_{0})
is strictly quasiconvex at
\bm{F}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, then
\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})=\bm{F}_{0}\bm{x}
is the unique solution of (
5.8
)
[
107
]
. If
\bm{F}_{0}
lies on the binodal, we expect the emergence of nontrivial equilibria in unbounded domains, where the volume fraction of the region where
\nabla\overline{\bm{y}}

is a finite distance from
\bm{F}_{0}
to be zero, even though in finite domains we still expect that
\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})=\bm{F}_{0}\bm{x}
be the unique solution. To follow this path one can, for instance, try to find radial solutions of (
5.9
) in the entire space. As we show below, in case of the success, Clapeyron’s Theorem can deliver a formula for
QW(\bm{F})
for a range of
\bm{F}
.

Suppose
m=n
, and
W(\bm{F})
is objective and isotropic

W(\bm{F})=w(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}),

where
v_{j}
are
the sigular values of
\bm{F}
and the function
w
does not change after a permutation of its arguments.
We look for a radial solution
\bm{y}(\bm{x})=\eta(r)\widehat{\bm{x}}
of (
5.9
).
We first observe that

\nabla\bm{y}=\eta^{\prime}\widehat{\bm{x}}\otimes\widehat{\bm{x}}+\frac{\eta}{r}(\bm{I}_{n}-\widehat{\bm{x}}\otimes\widehat{\bm{x}}).

Hence,
\bm{F}=\nabla\bm{y}
has a singular value
|\eta^{\prime}(r)|
and
n-1
singular values
|\eta(r)/r|
.
We will assume that
\eta(r)\geq 0
and
\eta^{\prime}(r)\geq 0
. In that case we define two functions

w_{1}(r)=\displaystyle\frac{\partial w}{\partial v_{1}}(\eta^{\prime}(r),\eta(r)/r,\ldots,\eta(r)/r),\quad w_{2}(r)=\displaystyle\frac{\partial w}{\partial v_{2}}(\eta^{\prime}(r),\eta(r)/r,\ldots,\eta(r)/r).

The Piola stress tensor is
[
4
, p. 564]

\bm{P}=w_{1}\widehat{\bm{x}}\otimes\widehat{\bm{x}}+w_{2}(\bm{I}_{n}-\widehat{\bm{x}}\otimes\widehat{\bm{x}}).

Therefore,
\eta(r)
must solve the nonlinear second order ODE

w_{11}\eta^{\prime\prime}+(n-1)w_{12}\left(\frac{\eta}{r}\right)^{\prime}+\frac{n-1}{r}(w_{1}-w_{2})=0,

(5.15)

where

w_{11}(r)=\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}w}{\partial v_{1}^{2}}(\eta^{\prime}(r),\eta(r)/r,\ldots,\eta(r)/r),\quad w_{12}(r)=\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}w}{\partial v_{1}\partial v_{2}}(\eta^{\prime}(r),\eta(r)/r,\ldots,\eta(r)/r).

If
\eta^{\prime}(r)
suffers a jump discontinuity at

[\![w_{1}]\!](r_{0})=0,\quad[\![w]\!](r_{0})=w_{1}(r_{0})[\![\eta^{\prime}]\!].

Typically, we would look for a solution of the form

\bm{y}(\bm{x})=\begin{cases}f_{0}r\widehat{\bm{x}},&|\bm{x}|<1,\\
\eta(r)\widehat{\bm{x}},&|\bm{x}|>1\end{cases}

(5.16)

In this case we must have

w_{1}(\eta^{\prime}(1^{+}),f_{0},\ldots,f_{0})=w_{1}(f_{0},f_{0},\ldots,f_{0})

w(\eta^{\prime}(1^{+}),f_{0},\ldots,f_{0})=w(f_{0},f_{0},\ldots,f_{0})+w_{1}(f_{0},f_{0},\ldots,f_{0})(\eta^{\prime}(1^{+})-f_{0}).

These can be regarded as equations determining the values of
f_{0}
and
\eta^{\prime}(1^{+})
.
Ultimately, the radial solution
\eta(r)
will be fixed by

f_{\infty}=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{\eta(r)}{r}=\lim_{r\to\infty}\eta^{\prime}(r).

In Appendix
A.1
we prove that

\eta(r)=f_{\infty}r+\frac{A}{r^{n-1}}+O(r^{-n}),\text{ as }r\to\infty.

(5.17)

Next we show how Clapeyron’s Theorem can use the knowledge of quasiconvexity at infinity (the boundary of all-space), transfers it to the interior and finally delivers an explicit formula for
QW(f\bm{I}_{n})
for all
f\in[f_{0},f_{\infty}]
.

Theorem
5.5
.

Suppose that
\eta\in C^{2}([1,+\infty))
is such that

(i)

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})=\begin{cases}f_{0}\bm{x},&|\bm{x}|<1,\\
\eta(|\bm{x}|)\widehat{\bm{x}},&|\bm{x}|>1\end{cases}
solves (
5.9
) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the sense of distributions.

(ii)

\displaystyle f_{\infty}=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{\eta(r)}{r}=\lim_{r\to\infty}\eta^{\prime}(r)
is such that

W(f_{\infty}\bm{I}_{n})=QW(f_{\infty}\bm{I}_{n})
.

Then

(a)

W(f_{0}\bm{I}_{n})=QW(f_{0}\bm{I}_{n})
;

(b)

for all
r\geq 1

W\left(\eta^{\prime}(r)\widehat{\bm{x}}\otimes\widehat{\bm{x}}+\frac{\eta(r)}{r}(\bm{I}_{n}-\widehat{\bm{x}}\otimes\widehat{\bm{x}})\right)=QW\left(\eta^{\prime}(r)\widehat{\bm{x}}\otimes\widehat{\bm{x}}+\frac{\eta(r)}{r}(\bm{I}_{n}-\widehat{\bm{x}}\otimes\widehat{\bm{x}})\right).

(5.18)

(c)

for every
r\geq 1

QW\left(\frac{\eta(r)}{r}\bm{I}_{n}\right)=w_{1}(r)\frac{\eta(r)}{r}+w(r)-\eta^{\prime}(r)w_{1}(r).

(5.19)

Proof.

According to (
5.14
), quasiconvexity is defined in terms of a bounded domain. In
[
36
]
we have linked quasiconvexity to test functions in all-space.
According to
[
36
, Theorem 4.6]
the existence of the radial solution
\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})
of (
5.9
) as in assumption (i) implies that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\mathcal{E}}(f_{\infty}\bm{I}_{n},\nabla\overline{\bm{y}})d\bm{x}=0,

as

\nabla\overline{\bm{y}}-f_{\infty}\bm{I}_{n}\in{\mathcal{S}}=\{\bm{\phi}\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n}):\nabla\bm{\phi}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})\},

according to (
5.17
). Then,
by
[
36
, Theorem 3.10]
, the assumption (ii) implies

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\mathcal{E}}(f_{\infty}\bm{I}_{n},\nabla\bm{y})d\bm{x}\geq 0

for all
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
, such that
\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x})-f_{\infty}\bm{I}_{n}\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
In that case conclusions (a) and (b) follow from
[
36
, Theorem 4.3]
.

To prove part (c) we observe that radial solutions have the property that

\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})=f_{R}\bm{x}
for all
\bm{x}\in\partial B(0,R)
, where
f_{R}=\eta(R)/R
. Hence, by the already eastablished conclusion (b), Theorem
5.3
applies and says that

|B(0,R)|QW\left(\frac{\eta(R)}{R}\bm{I}_{n}\right)=\int_{B(0,R)}W(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}})d\bm{x}.

(5.20)

We complete the proof by the application of the Clapeyron theorem:

\bm{P}\bm{n}=w_{1}\widehat{\bm{x}},\quad\bm{P}^{*}=w\bm{I}_{n}-\eta^{\prime}w_{1}\widehat{\bm{x}}\otimes\widehat{\bm{x}}+w_{2}\frac{\eta}{r}(\bm{I}_{n}-\widehat{\bm{x}}\otimes\widehat{\bm{x}})

Thus, according to (
4.6
),

\int_{B(0,R)}W(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}})d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial B(0,R)}\left\{w_{1}\widehat{\bm{x}}\cdot\eta\widehat{\bm{x}}+(w-\eta^{\prime}w_{1})\widehat{\bm{x}}\cdot R\widehat{\bm{x}}\right\}dS.

We conclude that

QW\left(\frac{\eta(R)}{R}\bm{I}_{n}\right)=w_{1}(R)\frac{\eta(R)}{R}+w(R)-\eta^{\prime}(R)w_{1}(R).

The theorem is now proved.

∎

5.4
Formation of a void

In this section we show how GCT can be
used for solving specific problems coming from engineering
applications. Specifically, we consider here a classical problem of
small void nucleation in a preloaded body which plays important role
in a broad range of mechanical theories
[
73
,
66
,
14
,
6
,
75
,
80
,
110
,
32
,
61
,
2
,
19
,
48
,
64
,
79
,
91
,
12
]
. In this subsection we assume that both
W
and all extremals are of class
C^{2}
.

Our starting point is a classical hyperelastic body with nonlinear energy density
W(\bm{F})
which occupies a domain
\Omega
in its reference state and is loaded at the boundary by
tractions
\bm{t}(\bm{x})
. The corresponding energy functional is

E=\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\bm{y})d\bm{x}-\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{t}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{y}\,dS(\bm{x}),

(5.21)

Suppose that the applied
tractions are fully equilibrated and therefore

\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{t}\,dS=0,\quad\int_{\partial\Omega}\{\bm{t}\otimes\bm{x}-\bm{x}\otimes\bm{t}\}dS=0.

The resulting deformation

\bm{y}(\bm{x})
can be found by solving the system of equations

\begin{cases}\nabla\cdot W_{\bm{F}}(\nabla\bm{y})=0,&\bm{x}\in\Omega,\\
W_{\bm{F}}(\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))\bm{n}=\bm{t}(\bm{x}),&\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(5.22)

and we assume that the solution is of class
C^{2}
.

To describe the process of void formation, we introduce
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a bounded domain, such that
0\in\omega
.
Suppose that during the implied point ablation, at
0\in\Omega
a small stress-free void
\epsilon\omega
is
formed inside
\Omega
through the removal of mass. Suppose further that
\epsilon>0
is so small that
\epsilon\omega\subset\Omega
, and
\Omega\setminus\epsilon\omega
is connected.

The equilibrium deformation
\bm{y}_{\epsilon}
, resulting from the described mass removal, is assumed to be of
class
C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}\setminus\epsilon\omega)
. It must then solve the system of equations

\left\{\begin{array}[c]{cl}\nabla\cdot\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon})=\bm{0},&\bm{x}\in\Omega\setminus\epsilon\omega\\
\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon})\bm{n}=\bm{0},&\bm{x}\in\partial(\epsilon\omega)\\
\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon})\bm{n}=\bm{t}(\bm{x}).&|\bm{x}|\in\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.

(5.23)

The potential energy of the resulting configuration is (up to a constant)

E_{\epsilon}=\int_{\Omega\setminus\epsilon\omega}W(\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon})d\bm{x}-\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{t}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{y}_{\epsilon}dS(\bm{x}),

(5.24)

We can now show how GCT can be used to obtain an expression for the
energy release associated with the implied void formation and defined by the formula

\Delta E=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{E_{\epsilon}-E}{\epsilon^{n}}.

(5.25)

This apparently straightforward question has caused considerable
controversy in the past
[
20
,
54
,
73
,
55
,
96
,
101
,
25
,
68
,
53
]

and to the best of
our knowledge has never been so far resolved in the fully nonlinear
setting.

Note first that one can expect the solution
\bm{y}_{\epsilon}
of the equilibrium problem
(
5.23
) in the ablated medium to exhibit an effective boundary
layer near the cavity. Such boundary layer would be different from
classical boundary layers in singularly perturbed problems, where the exponential decay of the solutions within the
boundary layer sets the length scale. In the case of a small cavity, it is the size

\epsilon
of the defect that sets the length scale. In all other respects
the treatment of the solution with such boundary layer can be expected to follow the
standard procedure of matching the inner and outer solutions.

To shortcut the formal mathematical study and simplify the presentation, we make upfront the following assumptions which will be later at least partially justified by our explicit constructions.

(A1)

The boundary value problems (
5.22
) and (
5.23
) have unique, up
to an additive constant solutions
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
and
\bm{y}_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})
,
respectively.

(A2)

There exists
\bm{w}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{0\})
, such

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\bm{y}_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})-\bm{y}(\bm{x})}{\epsilon^{n}}=\bm{w}(\bm{x}),

(5.26)

in
C^{1}
on all compact subsets of
\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{0\}
. The
assumption (
5.26
) is motivated by the behavior of radial
solutions in the context of linear elasticity, see our
Appendix
A.2
for details. In particular, we also have

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})-\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x})}{\epsilon^{n}}=\nabla\bm{w}(\bm{x})

is uniform on compact subsets of
\Omega\setminus\{0\}
. The function

\bm{y}_{\rm out}(\bm{x})=\bm{y}(\bm{x})+\epsilon^{n}\bm{w}(\bm{x})
will be called the outer
solution with the understanding that the normalized
displacement increment
\bm{w}(\bm{x})
governs the “far field” induced by the formation of a void.

(A3)

The inner solution in the immediate vicinity of the cavity is

\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon}(\epsilon\bm{z})\to\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z}),\quad\bm{z}\not\in\omega

(5.27)

uniformly, on compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\omega
, where

\bm{y}_{\rm in}
is the unique solution of

\begin{cases}\nabla\cdot\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in})=\bm{0},&\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\omega\\
\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in})\bm{n}=\bm{0},&\bm{z}\in\partial\omega\\
\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})\to\nabla\bm{y}(0),\quad&|\bm{z}|\to\infty.\end{cases}

(5.28)

This assumption regarding the inner solution in (
5.27
) is again motivated by the behavior of radial
solutions of linearly elastic isotropic
exterior problem which in our case can be written in the form (see again our Appendix
A.2
for details):

\bm{u}_{\rm in}^{\rm lin}(\bm{z})=\frac{p\bm{z}}{n\kappa}+\frac{p\bm{z}}{2\mu(n-1)|\bm{z}|^{n}}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\bm{u}_{\epsilon}(\epsilon\bm{z})}{\epsilon},\quad|\bm{z}|>1.

(5.29)

Since the deformation field
\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})
characterizes the local field in the immediate vicinity of the void, it will be interpreted as the inner solution.

(A4)

There exists a constant
n\times m
matrix
\bm{S}
, such that

\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})=\nabla\bm{y}(0)\bm{z}+\frac{\bm{S}\bm{z}}{|\bm{z}|^{n}}+O(|\bm{z}|^{-n}),\text{ as }|\bm{z}|\to\infty,

where
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
solves (
5.22
).

(A5)

One can replace

\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm out}(\epsilon\bm{z})
with
\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})
, when

|\bm{z}|
is large, while
\epsilon|\bm{z}|
is small.

We can now formulate our main result in the form of a theorem:

Theorem
5.6
.

Assume that assumptions (A1)–(A5) hold. Then

\Delta E=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\omega}W(\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z}))(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{z})dS(\bm{z}).

(5.30)

Note that a related result was obtained in
[
91
]
and its relation with Theorem
5.6
is discussed in Appendix
A.6
.

Proof.

Observe first that if we apply GCT (
4.6
) to the solution

\bm{y}
of (
5.22
)
we obtain

E=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}+\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\}dS-\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{t}\cdot\bm{y}\,dS.

In view of the boundary conditions
\bm{P}\bm{n}=\bm{t}
we also know that

\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}=W(\nabla\bm{y})\bm{n}-(\nabla\bm{y})^{T}\bm{P}\bm{n}=W(\nabla\bm{y})\bm{n}-(\nabla\bm{y})^{T}\bm{t}.

Therefore

E=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{\bm{t}\cdot\bm{y}+W(\nabla\bm{y})(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})-\bm{t}\cdot\nabla\bm{y}\bm{x}\}dS-\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{t}\cdot\bm{y}\,dS.

(5.31)

Similarly, since the solution
\bm{y}_{\epsilon}
of (
5.23
) has the same boundary conditions as
\bm{y}
on
\partial\Omega
and
traction-free boundary condition
\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon})\bm{n}=0
on
\partial\omega
, application of the GCT (
4.6
) finally gives

E_{\epsilon}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{\bm{t}\cdot\bm{y}_{\epsilon}+W(\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon})(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})-\bm{t}\cdot\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon}\bm{x}\}dS\\
-\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial(\epsilon\omega)}W(\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon})(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})dS-\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{t}\cdot\bm{y}_{\epsilon}dS.

(5.32)

Subtracting (
5.31
) and (
5.32
), dividing by
\epsilon^{n}
and passing to the limit
using our assumptions (
5.26
) and (
5.27
), we compute

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Delta E&=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{\bm{t}\cdot\bm{w}+\langle\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}),\nabla_{\partial\Omega}\bm{w}\rangle(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})-\bm{t}\cdot\nabla_{\partial\Omega}\bm{w}\bm{x}\}dS\\
&-\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\omega}\{W(\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in})(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{z})dS(\bm{z})-\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{t}\cdot\bm{w}\,dS.\end{split}

(5.33)

This is our preliminary formula for the desired energy increment and now we show that this formula can be simplified and expressed
entirely in terms of the inner solution. Performing in (
5.33
) integration by parts in the terms containing
\nabla_{\partial\Omega}\bm{w}
and
using the formula

\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{f}\cdot\nabla_{\partial\Omega}\phi\,dS(\bm{x})=\int_{\partial\Omega}\{\phi(\bm{f}\cdot\bm{n})\nabla_{\partial\Omega}\cdot\bm{n}-\phi\nabla_{\partial\Omega}\cdot\bm{f}\}dS,

(5.34)

we obtain

\Delta E=J-\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\omega}W(\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in})(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{z})dS(\bm{z}),

(5.35)

where

J=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{(\nabla_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P})[\bm{x},\bm{n},\bm{w}]-(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})(\nabla_{\partial\Omega}\cdot\bm{P})\cdot\bm{w}\}dS,

(5.36)

and

(\nabla_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P})[\bm{x},\bm{n},\bm{w}]=(\nabla_{\partial\Omega}P_{i}^{\alpha}\cdot\bm{x})n_{\alpha}w^{i};

note that we assumed summation over repeated indices. To finalize the proof of the
theorem we need to show that
J=0
which is done in our Appendix
A.3
.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

We emphasize that Theorem
5.6
represents the energy release due to the formation of a traction-free void in terms of the negative of the work of configurational forces as the formula we have actually used is

\Delta E=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\omega}\bm{P}^{*}(\nabla\bm{y}_{\in})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{z}\,dS.

(5.37)

This illustrates the physical meaning of the second term in GCT (
4.6
), as the energy associated with creation (in this case destruction) of a body.

The derivation presented above is not the only way how GCT can be used in this problem and below we provide an alternative proof of Theorem
5.6
.
Here we take advantage of the known properties of the Weierstrass excess function

{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F},\bm{G})
, defined in (
5.1
).

Alternative proof of Theorem
5.6
.

We begin by rewriting
the work of the loading device in the problems (
5.21
) and (
5.24
) in the form of
volume integral, respectively, as

E=\int_{\Omega}\{W(\nabla\bm{y})-\langle\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}),\nabla\bm{y}\rangle\}d\bm{x},

and

E_{\epsilon}=\int_{\Omega\setminus(\epsilon\omega)}\{W(\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon})-\langle\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon}),\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon}\rangle\}d\bm{x}.

Rearranging the terms we can write

E_{\epsilon}-E=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}
, where

I_{1}=\int_{\Omega\setminus(\epsilon\omega)}{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F},\bm{F}_{\epsilon})d\bm{x}+\int_{\Omega\setminus(\epsilon\omega)}\langle\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y})-\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon}),\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon}-\nabla\bm{y}\rangle\}d\bm{x},

I_{2}=\int_{\Omega\setminus(\epsilon\omega)}\langle\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y})-\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon}),\nabla\bm{y}\rangle\}d\bm{x},

I_{3}=-\int_{\epsilon\omega}\{W(\nabla\bm{y})-\langle\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}),\nabla\bm{y}\rangle\}d\bm{x}.

Integration by parts in
I_{2}
, while taking the boundary conditions from
(
5.22
) and (
5.23
) into account, gives

I_{2}=-\int_{\partial(\epsilon\omega)}\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}\,dS(\bm{x})=-\int_{\epsilon\omega}\langle\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}),\nabla\bm{y}\rangle d\bm{x},

where a divergence theorem on
\epsilon\omega
was used in the second equality above.
Thus,

I_{2}+I_{3}=-\int_{\epsilon\omega}W(\nabla\bm{y})d\bm{x}.

Changing variables
\bm{z}=\epsilon\bm{x}
and passing to the limit, we obtain

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon^{-n}(I_{2}+I_{3})=-|\omega|(W(\bm{F}_{0}).

We also have

\epsilon^{-n}I_{1}=\int_{(\epsilon^{-1}\Omega\setminus\omega)}\{{\mathcal{E}}(\nabla\bm{y}(\epsilon\bm{z}),\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon}(\epsilon\bm{z}))+\langle\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}(\epsilon\bm{z}))-\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon}(\epsilon\bm{z})),\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon}(\epsilon\bm{z})-\nabla\bm{y}(\epsilon\bm{z})\rangle\}d\bm{z},

To pass to the limit, we observe that
\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})\to\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x})
, as
\epsilon\to 0
,
when
|\bm{z}|\to\infty
, such that
\bm{x}=\epsilon\bm{z}
is finite.

We note that the main reason for using the Weierstrass excess function
{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F},\bm{G})
in this calculation is the estimate

|{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F},\bm{G})|\leq C|\bm{F}-\bm{G}|^{2},

(5.38)

provided both
\bm{F}
and
\bm{G}
belong to a fixed compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, determining the value of the constant
C
in (
5.38
).

Note next that the second term in
I_{1}
clearly satisfies the same estimate, and that is what allows us to replace
\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon}(\epsilon\bm{z})
by
\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})
,
\nabla\bm{y}(\epsilon\bm{z})
by
\bm{F}_{0}=\nabla\bm{y}(0)
, and
\epsilon^{-1}\Omega
by
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the expression for
\epsilon^{-n}I_{1}
. Hence,

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon^{-n}I_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\omega}\{{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F}_{0},\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z}))+\langle\bm{P}_{0}-\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})),\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})-\bm{F}_{0}\rangle\}d\bm{z},

(5.39)

where
\bm{F}_{0}=\nabla\bm{y}(0)
, and
\bm{P}_{0}=\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}(0))
.

Since
\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}
is a smooth extremal of
\widehat{W}(\bm{F})={\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F}_{0},\bm{F})
, and because of the estimate (
5.38
), we can use GCT (
4.6
) in the first term in (
5.39
) and integrate by parts in the second one, while we remain in the
infinite
domain
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\omega
. We obtain

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\omega}{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F}_{0},\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z}))d\bm{z}=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\omega}\{(\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in})-\bm{P}_{0})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}_{\rm in}+{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F}_{0},\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in})(\bm{n},\bm{z})-\\
(\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in})-\bm{P}_{0})\bm{n}\cdot\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})\bm{z}\}dS=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\omega}\{\bm{P}_{0}\bm{n}\cdot(\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}\bm{z}-\bm{y}_{\rm in})+{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F}_{0},\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in})(\bm{n},\bm{z})\}dS,

where we took into account the boundary condition
\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in})\bm{n}=0
on
\partial\omega
.
Similarly,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\omega}\langle\bm{P}_{0}-\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})),\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})-\bm{F}_{0}\rangle d\bm{z}=-\int_{\partial\omega}\bm{P}_{0}\bm{n}\cdot(\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})-\bm{F}_{0}\bm{z})dS=\\
|\omega|\langle\bm{P}_{0},\bm{F}_{0}\rangle-\int_{\partial\omega}\bm{P}_{0}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})dS.

Expanding
{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F}_{0},\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in})
, we obtain

-\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\omega}{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F}_{0},\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z}))(\bm{n},\bm{z})dS=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\omega}W(\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z}))(\bm{n},\bm{z})d\bm{S}+\\
(W(\bm{F}_{0})-\langle\bm{P}_{0},\bm{F}_{0}\rangle)|\omega|+\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\omega}\langle\bm{P}_{0},\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}\rangle(\bm{n},\bm{z})dS

Thus,

\Delta E=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\omega}W(\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in})(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{z})dS(\bm{z})+J^{*},

where

J^{*}=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\omega}\left\{\bm{P}_{0}\bm{n}\cdot(\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}\bm{z}-\bm{y}_{\rm in})-\langle\bm{P}_{0},\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}\rangle\bm{z}\cdot\bm{n}+n\bm{P}_{0}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}_{\rm in}\right\}dS.

(5.40)

Remark 5.8
.

The obtained results allow us to elucidate a delicate point, which has led to a famous “Griffith’s error” in the analogous calculation conducted in the context of linear elasticity

53
]

\epsilon^{-n}I_{2}
above, we would have passed to the limit “directly” and replaced
\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon}(\epsilon\bm{z})
by
\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})
,
\nabla\bm{y}(\epsilon\bm{z})
by
\bm{F}_{0}=\nabla\bm{y}(0)
, and
\epsilon^{-1}\Omega
by
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then in the re-scaled expression
\epsilon^{-n}I_{2}

5.5
Case study: linear elasticity

As we have already mentioned, the defining commonality of all nonlinear versions of Clapeyron’s Theorem is that they express the elastic energy of a stable configuration in terms of boundary tractions, which implies both physical and configurational tractions. In this section we will juxtapose the classical Clapeyron’s Theorem (
1.1
) against the formulas (
4.6
), (
4.18
) and (
4.21
) which are all relevant, given that in classical linear elasticity the energy density function is both scale-invariant and 2-homogeneous. Indeed, consider the energy density

W(\bm{x},\bm{F})=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathsf{C}(\bm{x})\bm{F}_{\rm sym},\bm{F}_{\rm sym}\rangle,

(5.42)

where

\bm{F}_{\rm sym}=\frac{1}{2}(\bm{F}+\bm{F}^{T}),

and where the tensor of elastic moduli

\mathsf{C}(\bm{x})
takes values in the space of positive definite quadratic forms on
\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
—the space of
n\times n
symmetric real matrices, and can otherwise be an arbitrary bounded and measurable function of
\bm{x}
. Note first that according to (
5.42
) the function

W(\bm{x},\bm{F})
is
p
-homogenous in the sense of (
4.16
) with
p=2
. This means, in particular, that, given a solution of

\nabla\cdot(\mathsf{C}(\bm{x})e(\bm{u}))=0,

understood in the sense of distributions, we can apply formula (
4.18
)
and obtain the classical Clapeyron theorem (
1.1
)

E[\bm{u}]:=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathsf{C}(\bm{x})e(\bm{u}),e(\bm{u})\rangle d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{x})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{x})dS(\bm{x}),

(5.43)

Note next that when the body is homogeneous and the tensor of elastic moduli
\mathsf{C}
is constant, the energy density function (
5.42
) is both scale-free and 2-homogeneous in the sense of (
4.4
) and (
4.16
), with
p=2
, respectively. In this case one can also show that all equilibrium solutions are smooth and therefore, by the Noether formula (
2.13
), they are also stationary. Hence, in addition to (
4.18
) that has the form (
5.43
) in the linearly elastic context, formulas (
4.21
), (
4.23
), and (
4.6
) are also applicable. However, in contrast to (
5.43
), these formulas involve the skew-symmetric part
w(\bm{u})
of
\nabla\bm{u}
,

w(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\bm{u}-(\nabla\bm{u})^{T}),

representing the infinitesimal rotations. Indeed, formula (
4.21
) takes the form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}),e(\bm{u})\rangle d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n-2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle\bm{\sigma},e(\bm{u})\rangle\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}-\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}\cdot(e(\bm{u})+w(\bm{u}))\bm{x}\right\}dS(\bm{x}).

(5.44)

Formula (
4.23
) relating the right-hand sides of (
5.43
) and (
5.44
) can be now rewritten as an (apparently new) integral relation satisfied on the boundary by the antisymmetric part of the gradient of an equilibrium solution:

\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}\cdot w(\bm{u})\bm{x}\,dS=\int_{\partial\Omega}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle\bm{\sigma},e(\bm{u})\rangle(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})-\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}\cdot e(\bm{u})\bm{x}-\frac{n-2}{2}\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{u}\right\}dS.

(5.45)

In the special case of
n=2
formula (
5.45
) simplifies

\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}\cdot w(\bm{u})\bm{x}\,dS=\int_{\partial\Omega}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle\bm{\sigma},e(\bm{u})\rangle(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})-\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}\cdot e(\bm{u})\bm{x}\right\}dS.

Finally, formula (
4.6
), representing the linear version of GCT, takes the form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathsf{C}(\bm{x})e(\bm{u}),e(\bm{u})\rangle d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left\{\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}\cdot(\bm{u}-w(\bm{u})\bm{x})-\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}\cdot e(\bm{u})\bm{x}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle\bm{\sigma},e(\bm{u})\rangle(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})\right\}dS.

(5.46)

which is clearly different from the classical Clapeyron’s Theorem.

Note also that the conservation law (
4.24
) can be now written as a differential equation for
w(\bm{u})
in terms of
e(\bm{u})
:

\nabla\cdot(\bm{\sigma}w(\bm{u})\bm{x})=\nabla\cdot\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle\bm{\sigma},e(\bm{u})\rangle\bm{x}-\bm{\sigma}e(\bm{u})\bm{x}-\frac{n-2}{2}\bm{\sigma}\bm{u}\right).

Interestingly, we can also rewrite it as a differential equation for
\nabla\bm{u}

\nabla\cdot(\bm{\sigma}\nabla\bm{u}\bm{x})=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot(\langle\bm{\sigma},e(\bm{u})\rangle\bm{x}-(n-2)\bm{\sigma}\bm{u}),

(5.47)

which in two space dimension simplifies as a representation in terms
e(\bm{u})
only:

\nabla\cdot(\bm{\sigma}\nabla\bm{u}\bm{x})=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot(\langle\bm{\sigma},e(\bm{u})\rangle\bm{x}).

To the best of our knowledge, formulas (
5.44
)–(
5.47
) are new. It is likely that they did not appear in the literature on linear elasticity due to the rather striking appearance in them of the skew-symmetric part of the gradient
w(\bm{u})
. Nonetheless, formulas (
5.44
)– (
5.45
) may be useful in applications, as we show, for instance, in our Appendix
A.5
.

Despite their fundamentally variational origin, formulas (
5.44
)– (
5.45
) could have been be also discovered by direct computation. For instance, the direct differentiation in (
5.47
), taking into account the equilibrium equations
\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}=0
and
\bm{\sigma}^{T}=\bm{\sigma}
gives

\langle\bm{\sigma},\nabla\nabla\bm{u}\bm{x}\rangle=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\nabla\langle\bm{\sigma},e(\bm{u})\rangle\cdot\bm{x}.

This equation can be now verified directly using a version of Betti reciprocity which still relies essentially on the fact that the elastic tensor is constant

\langle\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{\sigma}}{\partial x^{i}},\bm{\varepsilon}\rangle=\langle\bm{\sigma},\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{\varepsilon}}{\partial x^{i}}\rangle.

If we turn to applications, i is instructive to first of all reconsider in the context of linear elasticity the problem of void formation which we have discussed in the general nonlinear setting in Section
5.4
.

Recall that our goal in this setting is to compute the difference between the potential energies

E=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}),e(\bm{u})\rangle d\bm{x}-\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{t}\cdot\bm{u}\,dS,

and

E_{\epsilon}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{\epsilon}}\langle\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}_{\epsilon}),e(\bm{u}_{\epsilon})\rangle d\bm{x}-\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{t}\cdot\bm{u}_{\epsilon}dS

of the undamaged and damaged bodies loaded by boundary tractions

\left\{\begin{array}[c]{cl}\nabla\cdot\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u})=\bm{0},&\bm{x}\in\Omega\\
\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u})\bm{n}=\bm{t}(\bm{x}),&\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.,\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[c]{cl}\nabla\cdot\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}_{\epsilon})=\bm{0},&\bm{x}\in\Omega\setminus\epsilon\omega\\
\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}_{\epsilon})\bm{n}=\bm{0},&\bm{x}\in\partial(\epsilon\omega)\\
\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}_{\epsilon})\bm{n}=\bm{t}(\bm{x}),&\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(5.48)

Specifically, we would like to compute

\Delta E=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{E_{\epsilon}-E}{\epsilon^{n}}.

(5.49)

Instead of adapting to the case of linear elasticity our general Theorem
5.6
which relies on GCT, we use here the classical Clapeyron’s Theorem (
5.43
) to prove an apparently different formula for
\Delta E
:

Theorem
5.9
.

\Delta E=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\omega}\bm{\sigma}(0)\bm{n}(\bm{z})\cdot\bm{u}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})dS(\bm{z}),

(5.50)

where
\bm{u}_{\rm in}
solves an exterior boundary value problem

\begin{cases}\nabla\cdot\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}_{\rm in})=\bm{0},&\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\omega\\
(\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}_{\rm in}))\bm{n}=\bm{0},&\bm{z}\in\partial\omega\\
e(\bm{u}_{\rm in})\to\bm{\varepsilon}_{0},&|\bm{z}|\to\infty,\end{cases}

(5.51)

where
\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}=e(\bm{u})(0)
, and where
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{x})=\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}(\bm{x}))
.

For equivalent results in different specialized settings, see
[
99
,
28
,
52
,
13
,
94
,
101
,
91
,
17
,
7
]
.

Proof.

We first note that by the classical Clapyeron theorem (
1.1
)

E=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}),e(\bm{u})\rangle d\bm{x},

(5.52)

and

E_{\epsilon}=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{\epsilon}}\langle\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}_{\epsilon}),e(\bm{u}_{\epsilon})\rangle d\bm{x},

(5.53)

where
\bm{u}_{\epsilon}
and
\bm{u}(\bm{x})
are the displacement vectors in the
damaged and the undamaged domains, respectively.

Let
\bm{w}_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})=\bm{u}_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})-\bm{u}(\bm{x})-\bm{a}_{\epsilon}
, where

\bm{a}_{\epsilon}=\bm{u}_{\epsilon}(\epsilon\bm{z}_{0})-\bm{u}(\epsilon\bm{z}_{0})
, and
\bm{z}_{0}\in\partial\omega

is an arbitrarily selected, and henceforth fixed, point.
Then, the vector field
\bm{w}_{\epsilon}
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[c]{cl}\nabla\cdot\mathsf{C}e(\bm{w}_{\epsilon})=\bm{0},&\bm{x}\in\Omega\setminus\epsilon\omega\\
\mathsf{C}e(\bm{w}_{\epsilon})\bm{n}=-\bm{\sigma}(\bm{x})\bm{n},&\bm{x}\in\partial(\epsilon\omega)\\
\mathsf{C}e(\bm{w}_{\epsilon})\bm{n}=\bm{0},&\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(5.54)

Observe that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{\epsilon}}\langle\mathsf{C}e(\bm{w}_{\epsilon}),e(\bm{w}_{\epsilon})\rangle d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{\epsilon}}\langle\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}_{\epsilon}),e(\bm{u}_{\epsilon})\rangle d\bm{x}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{\epsilon}}\langle\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}),e(\bm{u})\rangle d\bm{x}-\int_{\Omega_{\epsilon}}\langle\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}_{\epsilon}),e(\bm{u})\rangle d\bm{x}

Integrating the last term by parts, and using
\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}_{\epsilon})\bm{n}=0
on
\partial(\epsilon\omega)
, we obtain

\int_{\Omega_{\epsilon}}\langle\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}_{\epsilon}),e(\bm{u})\rangle d\bm{x}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{x})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{x})dS(\bm{x})=\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}),e(\bm{u})\rangle d\bm{x}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{\epsilon}}\langle\mathsf{C}e(\bm{w}_{\epsilon}),e(\bm{w}_{\epsilon})\rangle d\bm{x}=E-E_{\epsilon}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\epsilon\omega}\langle\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}),e(\bm{u})\rangle d\bm{x}.

Then

E_{\epsilon}-E=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{\epsilon}}\langle\mathsf{C}e(\bm{w}_{\epsilon}),e(\bm{w}_{\epsilon})\rangle d\bm{x}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\epsilon\omega}\langle\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}),e(\bm{u})\rangle d\bm{x}.

Applying Clapeyron’s theorem again, this time to
\bm{w}_{\epsilon}
, we get

E_{\epsilon}-E=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\omega_{\epsilon}}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{x})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{w}_{\epsilon}dS(\bm{x})-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\epsilon\omega}\langle\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}),e(\bm{u})\rangle d\bm{x},

where
\bm{n}
is the outer unit normal to
\partial\omega
.
Now let us change variables
\bm{x}=\epsilon\bm{z}
and obtain

E_{\epsilon}-E=-\frac{\epsilon^{n}}{2}\left(\int_{\partial\omega}\bm{\sigma}(\epsilon\bm{z})\bm{n}(\bm{z})\cdot\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{\epsilon}(\bm{z})dS(\bm{z})-\int_{\omega}\langle\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u})(\epsilon\bm{z}),e(\bm{u})(\epsilon\bm{z})\rangle d\bm{z}\right),

where
\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{\epsilon}(\bm{z})=\epsilon^{-1}\bm{w}_{\epsilon}(\epsilon\bm{z})
.
We observe that

\nabla\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{\epsilon}=\nabla\bm{u}_{\epsilon}(\epsilon\bm{z})-\nabla\bm{u}(\epsilon\bm{z})\to\nabla\bm{u}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})-\nabla\bm{u}(0)
, uniformly on compact subsets of

\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, while
\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{\epsilon}(\bm{z}_{0})=0
. It follows
that
\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{\epsilon}(\bm{z})\to\bm{u}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})-\nabla\bm{u}(0)\bm{z}-\bm{a}_{0}
,
where
\bm{a}_{0}=\bm{u}_{\rm in}(\bm{z}_{0})-\nabla\bm{u}(0)\bm{z}_{0}
.
Therefore,

\Delta E=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{E_{\epsilon}-E}{\epsilon^{n}}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\omega}\bm{\sigma}(0)\bm{n}(\bm{z})\cdot(\bm{u}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})-\nabla\bm{u}(0)\bm{z}-\bm{a}_{0})dS(\bm{z})-\frac{|\omega|}{2}\langle\bm{\sigma}(0),\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}\rangle.

Observing that

\int_{\partial\omega}\bm{\sigma}(0)\bm{n}(\bm{z})\cdot\bm{a}_{0}\,dS(\bm{z})=0,

and

\int_{\partial\omega}\bm{\sigma}(0)\bm{n}(\bm{z})\cdot\nabla\bm{u}(0)\bm{z}\,dS(\bm{z})=|\omega|\langle\bm{\sigma}(0),\nabla\bm{u}(0)\rangle=|\omega|\langle\bm{\sigma}(0),\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}\rangle,

we obtain

\Delta E=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{E_{\epsilon}-E}{\epsilon^{n}}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\omega}\bm{\sigma}(0)\bm{n}(\bm{z})\cdot\bm{u}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})\,dS(\bm{z}).

6
Conclusions

In this paper we revisited the classical Clapeyron’s Theorem of the linear elasticity theory
that expresses the energy of an equilibrium configuration in terms of the work of physical forces on the boundary. We derived various nonlinear analogs of this well known engineering result by revealing its link with the classical work of E. Noether on variational symmetries in nonlinear field theories. We showed that in view of this connection, the obtained family of formulas expressing “energy in terms of work” can be interpreted as a rather general statement within Calculus of Variations reflecting partial variational symmetries of the corresponding theories. Specifically, we showed that in the case of scale invariance combined with
p
-homogeneity one obtains not only a direct generalization of the Clapeyron’s theorem of linear elasticity containing the stress tensor but also its dual version expressed through the corresponding Eshelby tensor. In this respect an important mechanical aspect of our work is that it brings together physical and configurational forces and allows one to differentiate between the part of the elastic energy stored due to the action of applied loads and the “cold work type” part of the energy emerging due to elastic incompatibility. More generally, the proposed broader reading of the Clapeyron’s theorem allowed us to link it to various apparently unrelated classical results, including the Green’s formula in nonlinear elastostatics and the expression of the minimal value of the equilibrium energy through the Weierstrass excess function. Of particular interest are the applications of our generalized Clapeyron Theorem (GCT) to elastodynamics where it naturally accounts for the inevitable formation of shocks. We have also shown that our study provides new useful tools allowing one to advance in various applied variational problems where explicit solutions are not readily available. In particular, we showed that GCT can be potentially useful in generating stability limits for elastic solids undergoing fracture and phase transitions.

Observing that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}y^{j}}{\partial x^{\gamma}\partial x^{\beta}}x^{\beta}=\nabla(\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x})\bm{x}-\bm{y}(\bm{x})+\bm{y}(0))^{j}_{\gamma},

We note that in order to proceed further we need to apply the matching principle, whereby

\nabla\bm{y}_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})\approx\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x})+\epsilon^{n}\nabla\bm{w}(\bm{x})
, when

is not too small, must match

, when

is not too large.
Hence,

\bm{z}\in B(0,M)

\bm{w}(\epsilon\bm{z})\to\epsilon^{-n}(\epsilon\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})-\bm{y}(\epsilon\bm{z})+\bm{y}(0)).

where the free constant in

Making the replacement in (

\left.\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial y^{j}}{\partial x^{\beta}}(\epsilon\bm{z})z^{\beta}-\frac{y^{j}(\epsilon\bm{z})-y^{j}(0)}{\epsilon}\right)\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial y_{\rm in}^{i}}{\partial z^{\alpha}}(\bm{z})-\displaystyle\frac{\partial y^{i}}{\partial x^{\alpha}}(\epsilon\bm{z})\right)n_{\gamma}(\bm{z})\right]\,dS(\bm{z})=0,

We conclude that (
5.30

A.4
Completion of the alternative proof of the Theorem

To finalize the alternative proof of the Theorem

First we observe that we can write

We observe that we can replace
\bm{y}_{\rm in}(\bm{z})

It is clear that

A.6
Rice and Drucker’s analysis of void formation

In this section we revisit, for the sake of completeness, the calculation of the total energy release upon the removal of a subregion
\omega\subset\Omega
, due to Rice and Drucker
[
91
]
because their result is in some sense intermediate between the asymptotic formulas (
5.30
) and (
5.50
) and because their argument is conducted almost entirely in the context of nonlinear elasticity, while leading to a formula resembling (
5.50

Consider a body
\Omega
loaded by a combination of “fixed grips” (displacement boundary conditions) on
\partial\Omega_{D}
, tractions on
\partial\Omega_{N}
, and body forces in
\Omega
. The total energy functional is
4
4

4

In
[
91
]
Rice and Drucker were working in the context of geometrically linear, physically nonlinear elasticity. However, nothing in their analysis, reproduced below appealed to the assumption of geometric linearity.

E[\bm{y}]=\int_{\Omega}\{W(\bm{x},\nabla\bm{y})-\bm{b}\cdot\bm{y}\}d\bm{x}-\int_{\partial\Omega_{N}}\bm{y}\cdot\bm{t}\,dS,

where for notational convenience we used
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
instead of the displacements
\bm{u}(\bm{x})=\bm{y}(\bm{x})-\bm{x}
in the work of external body forces and boundary tractions.
We emphasize that the explicit dependence of the energy density

Let
\bm{y}_{0}
be the energy minimizer solving

\begin{cases}\nabla\cdot\bm{P}(\bm{x},\bm{F}_{0})=\bm{b},&\bm{x}\in\Omega,\\
\bm{y}_{0}(\bm{x})=\bm{g}(\bm{x}),&\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{D},\\
\bm{P}(\bm{x},\bm{F}_{0})\bm{n}=\bm{t},&\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{N},\end{cases}

(A.14)

where
\bm{g}(\bm{x})-\bm{x}
are the prescribed displacments of the Dirichlet part
\partial\Omega_{D}
of the boundary.
Let
\bm{y}_{d}
be the energy minimizer of

E_{d}[\bm{y}]=\int_{\Omega\setminus\omega}\{W(\bm{x},\nabla\bm{y})-\bm{b}\cdot\bm{y}\}d\bm{x}-\int_{\partial\Omega_{N}}\bm{y}\cdot\bm{t}\,dS,

where
\omega\subset\Omega
is the damaged region that has been

\bm{y}_{d}
solves the boundary value problem

\begin{cases}\nabla\cdot\bm{P}(\bm{x},\bm{F}_{d})=\bm{b},&\bm{x}\in\Omega\setminus\omega,\\
\bm{y}_{d}(\bm{x})=\bm{g}(\bm{x}),&\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{D},\\
\bm{P}(\bm{x},\bm{F}_{d})\bm{n}=\bm{t},&\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{N},\\
\bm{P}(\bm{x},\bm{F}_{d})\bm{n}=0,&\bm{x}\in\partial\omega.\end{cases}

(A.15)

Our goal is to find the formula for the energy increment

\Delta E=E[\bm{y}_{0}]-E_{d}[\bm{y}_{d}]

\partial\omega
.

We begin with the obvious expression

E[\bm{y}_{0}]-E[\bm{y}_{d}]=E_{\omega}+\int_{\Omega\setminus\omega}\{W(\bm{x},\bm{F}_{0})-W(\bm{x},\bm{F}_{d})-\bm{b}\cdot(\bm{y}_{0}-\bm{y}_{d})\}d\bm{x}-\int_{\partial\Omega_{N}}\bm{t}\cdot(\bm{y}_{0}-\bm{y}_{d})dS,

where we used the shorthand
\bm{F}_{0}=\nabla\bm{y}_{0}
and
\bm{F}_{d}=\nabla\bm{y}_{d}
, and where

E_{\omega}=\int_{\omega}\{W(\bm{x},\bm{F}_{0})-\bm{b}\cdot\bm{y}_{0}\}d\bm{x}.

In what follows we use the shorthand

\Delta^{\prime}E=E[\bm{y}_{0}]-E[\bm{y}_{d}]-E_{\omega}

in order not to write
E_{\omega}
in
every formula.
The first observation of Rice and Drucker is that for any smooth test field

vanishing on

\int_{\Omega\setminus\omega}\langle\bm{P}(\bm{x},\bm{F}_{d}),\nabla\bm{u}\rangle d\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega\setminus\omega}\bm{b}\cdot\bm{u}d\bm{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega_{N}}\bm{t}\cdot\bm{u}\,dS.

We then use formula (
A.16
) with

and obtain

\Delta^{\prime}E=\int_{\Omega\setminus\omega}\{W(\bm{x},\bm{F}_{0})-W(\bm{x},\bm{F}_{d})-\langle\bm{P}(\bm{x},\bm{F}_{d}),\bm{F}_{0}-\bm{F}_{d}\rangle\}d\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega\setminus\omega}{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F}_{d},\bm{F}_{0})d\bm{x},

-function is defined in (

Next, Rice and Drucker use the following construction.
Let
\bm{t}_{\omega}(\bm{x})=\bm{P}(\bm{x},\bm{F}_{0}(\bm{x}))\bm{n}_{\partial\omega}(\bm{x})
,

\bm{x}\in\partial\omega
, where

\partial\omega

A.7
Computation of the Griffith’s discrepancy term (
5.41
)

In order to compute the limit in (
5.41
) we need to use the far-field asymptotics (
A.8
), obtaining

(\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}_{\rm in})-\bm{P}_{0})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{F}_{0}\bm{z}=\frac{(\mathsf{C}\bm{S})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{F}_{0}\bm{z}}{|\bm{z}|^{n}}-n\frac{(\mathsf{C}(\bm{S}\bm{z}\otimes\bm{z}))\bm{n}\cdot\bm{F}_{0}\bm{z}}{|\bm{z}|^{n+2}}+O(|\bm{z}|^{-n}),

where
\mathsf{C}=W_{\bm{F}\bm{F}}(\bm{F}_{0})
, and
\bm{S}
is
the polarization tensor
[
1
]
that depends in the shape of
\omega
.
Using the fact that
|\bm{z}|=R
and
\bm{n}=\widehat{\bm{z}}=\bm{z}/|\bm{z}|
on
\partial B(0,R)
, we obtain

G=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\{n(\mathsf{C}(\bm{S}\widehat{\bm{z}}\otimes\widehat{\bm{z}}))\widehat{\bm{z}}\cdot\bm{F}_{0}\widehat{\bm{z}}-(\mathsf{C}\bm{S})\widehat{\bm{z}}\cdot\bm{F}_{0}\widehat{\bm{z}}\}\,dS(\widehat{\bm{z}}).

Thus, taking into account that

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}z^{i}z^{j}dS=\frac{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}{n}\delta^{ij},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}z^{i}z^{j}z^{k}z^{l}dS=\frac{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}{n(n+2)}\left(\delta^{ij}\delta^{kl}+\delta^{ik}\delta^{jl}+\delta^{il}\delta^{jk}\right),

we obtain

G=\frac{|B(0,1)|}{n+2}(C_{ijkl}(nS_{kj}F_{il}-2S_{kl}F_{ij})+nC_{ijkj}S_{kl}F_{il}),

(A.24)

where the summation over repeated indices is assumed, and where we used the relation
|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|=n|B(0,1)|
between the surface and the volume of the unit ball.

If
\mathsf{C}
is an isotropic elastic tensor with Lamé moduli
\lambda
and
\mu
, then

\mathsf{C}(\bm{S}\widehat{\bm{z}}\otimes\widehat{\bm{z}})\widehat{\bm{z}}\cdot\bm{F}_{0}\widehat{\bm{z}}=(\lambda+\mu)(\bm{S}\widehat{\bm{z}},\widehat{\bm{z}})(\bm{F}_{0}\widehat{\bm{z}},\widehat{\bm{z}})+\mu(\bm{S}\widehat{\bm{z}},\bm{F}_{0}\widehat{\bm{z}}),

(\mathsf{C}\bm{S})\widehat{\bm{z}}\cdot\bm{F}_{0}\widehat{\bm{z}}=\lambda(\bm{F}_{0}\widehat{\bm{z}}\cdot\widehat{\bm{z}})\mathrm{Tr}\,\bm{S}+2\mu\bm{S}\widehat{\bm{z}}\cdot\bm{F}_{0}\widehat{\bm{z}},

and thus,

G=\frac{|B(0,1)|}{n+2}((n\mu-2\lambda)\mathrm{Tr}\,\bm{F}_{0}\mathrm{Tr}\,\bm{S}+(2\lambda n+\mu(n^{2}+2n-4))\langle\bm{S},\bm{F}_{0}\rangle).

(A.25)

When
n=2
we have

G=\frac{\pi}{2}((\mu-\lambda)\mathrm{Tr}\,\bm{F}_{0}\mathrm{Tr}\,\bm{S}+2(\lambda+\mu)\langle\bm{S},\bm{F}_{0}\rangle),

When
n=3
we have

G=\frac{4\pi}{15}((3\mu-2\lambda)\mathrm{Tr}\,\bm{F}_{0}\mathrm{Tr}\,\bm{S}+(6\lambda+11\mu)\langle\bm{S},\bm{F}_{0}\rangle).

If
\bm{F}_{0}=\frac{p}{n\kappa}\bm{I}_{n}

G=\frac{2(n-1)p\mu|B(0,1)|}{n\kappa}\mathrm{Tr}\,\bm{S}=\begin{cases}\frac{\pi\mu p}{\kappa}\mathrm{Tr}\,\bm{S},&n=2,\\
\frac{16\pi\mu p}{9\kappa}\mathrm{Tr}\,\bm{S},&n=3.\end{cases}

The case of nucleating a small spherical cavity in a body occupied by isotropic linearly elastic material is exactly solvable. The solution of (
5.51

In this case all of our results in this section can also be verified by a direct calculation,

A.4
