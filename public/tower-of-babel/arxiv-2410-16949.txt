Rayleigh-Plateau Instability on an angled and eccentric fiber: An alternative approach Preprint: APS/123-QED
Dilip Kumar Maity 1, Christopher Wagstaff 2, Sandip Dighe 1, Tadd Truscott 1

Abstract
This research explores the modulation of Rayleigh-Plateau Instability (RPI) by adjusting the orientation angle and eccentricity of a wire within a nozzle. We demonstrate that both the angle and eccentricity significantly influence the RPI regimes. They both also influence characteristics, such as velocity, wavelength, and droplet volume. Notably, when wires are both angled and eccentric, the effect of angle prevails. Our approach includes an empirical scaling analysis, comparing gravity, surface tension, and viscosity forces on a single bead, yielding a unified empirical viscous force law, enhancing understanding of RP regime dynamics. This new framework enriches our understanding of the forces at play in RPI and provides practical insights into the manipulation of fluid dynamics in industrial applications.



Introduction

Wetted wires present a potential advantage compared to traditional gas-liquid contactors, such as spray columns, wetted-wall columns, and packed-bed columns, commonly employed for heat and mass exchange between liquids and gasses
Kreith and Boehm 1988
;
Sadeghpour et al. 2019
(e.g., distillation, absorption, fog harvesting

Nozaki et al. 1998
;
Wehinger et al. 2013
and decreasing gas-side pressure drop
Hattori et al. 1994
;
Grünig et al. 2010
;
Grünig et al. 2012
. Additional benefits include a reduction in dry area
Chinju et al. 2000
, velocity control
Kliakhandler et al. 2001
, and surface area control
Grünig et al. 2010
;
Grünig et al. 2012
;
Wagstaff et al. 2023
. A wetted wire
Quéré 1990
;
Kliakhandler et al. 2001
involves a fluid flowing down it, leading to the formation of a string of droplets on the wire due to the Rayleigh-Plateau instability (RPI)

;
Rayleigh 1892
. Typically, three phenomena can occur: i) an isolated regime where primary droplets are separated by secondary droplets, ii) a periodic Rayleigh-Plateau regime (RP) with a constant velocity and bead spacing, iii) an unstable Kapitza or convective regime characterized by random coalescence between primary beads
Kliakhandler et al. 2001
. Consequently, the manipulation of the Rayleigh-Plateau instability on a wire stands as a crucial aspect of current research.

Furthermore, this approach holds great utility where adjusting the primary parameters are constrained. For instance, if the flow rate, wire diameter, wetability, and wire geometry are fixed, one could still angle the wire to control the RPI based on our findings.

It is well accepted that the periodic RP regime shifts to the chaotic-convective regime (aka Kapitza regime) with increasing flow rates when all other parameters remain the same
Kliakhandler et al. 2001
. The phenomenon requires that the fluid be sufficiently viscous and the three main forces in competition are gravity, surface tension, and viscosity. Theoretical investigations
Kliakhandler et al. 2001
;
Craster and Matar 2006
;
Duprat et al. 2007
;
Smolka et al. 2008
;
Duprat et al. 2009
;
Ji et al. 2019
begin by estimating the undisturbed film thickness on the wire by neglecting the surface tension and assuming a velocity profile near the nozzle (i.e., pre-instability or Nusselt thickness). The undisturbed jet is assumed to be a cylinder because of analytical constraints, yet, in most experiments the jet is conical. Therefore, the theory often doesn’t capture the instability quantitatively. Thus, we argue that the force balance on a single droplet far below the pre-instability region could be more effective in explaining the phenomenon.

The current investigation involves a deliberate alteration of the RPI through two main adjustments: (i) manipulating the wire angle, and ii) varying the eccentricity.
Our findings indicate that by increasing the angle for a given flow rate the RP regime is first reduced and then shifted upward. Eccentricity simply reduces the RP regime. Moreover, alterations in angle and eccentricity lead to notable changes in droplet spacing (aka wavelength) and velocity within the RP regime. We also present a scaling analysis within the RP regime that juxtaposes the impacts of gravity, surface tension, and viscous forces. This analysis yields a cohesive empirical viscous-force relationship, which facilitates a comparative understanding of the three primary forces within the RP regimes.

II.4
Scaling law on the RPI on wire

In earlier investigations
Kliakhandler et al. 2001
;
Craster and Matar 2006
;
Duprat et al. 2007
;
Smolka et al. 2008
;
Duprat et al. 2009
;
Ji et al. 2019
, researchers established a transition boundary between RP and convective regimes. This was accomplished by delineating the Nusselt thickness by focusing solely on the interplay between viscous and gravity forces through the Navier-Stokes equations and solving for velocity to yield the thickness of the film on the wire. Notably, they accomplish a solution to the velocity profile by ignoring surface tension, which is later added to address the instability. Consequently, the theoretical model implies that an undisturbed jet (pre-instability jet) maintains a purely cylindrical shape, in contrast to the observed conical form in most experiments (Fig
3
f
). This discrepancy leads us to question the realism and practicality of these theoretical approaches. Consequently, we propose a novel perspective by considering the interplay of three forces: gravity, surface tension, and viscosity acting on a single bead to elucidate the transition phenomenon, rather than focusing on the jet near the nozzle as was done previously.

We adopt a single bead approach to elucidate the transition, where the bead comprises two segments: a slender cylindrical part and a substantial droplet section. Our hypothesis posits that the force from gravity equals the combined force from surface tension and viscosity on a bead within the RP regimes. When gravity surpasses the cumulative forces, a convective regime ensues. Calculating the force (
F_{g}
) due to gravity is straightforward:
F_{g}=\rho\forall g\cos{\theta}
, with
\rho
and
g
denoting liquid density and gravitational acceleration. Yet, determining the viscous force
(F_{\mu})
and surface tension force
(F_{\sigma})

is the non-dimensional area of the wire in contact with motion of the fluid,
\mu

III
Conclusion

In conclusion, our study has successfully demonstrated the significant impact of manipulating the Rayleigh-Plateau instability (RPI) by altering both the angle of the nozzle-wire system and the eccentricity of the wire within the nozzle. The investigation revealed a strong dependence of the RPI regimes on angle and eccentricity. In general, angling the wire decreases the range and increases the average of the range of volumetric flow rate. Eccentricity affects mainly vertical wires where the RP regime shrinks with increasing eccentricity. The effect of angle dominates over eccentricity when the wire is both angled and eccentric. Within the RP regime noteworthy changes occur in velocity, wavelength, and bead volume.

Furthermore, to provide a comprehensive understanding, we conducted an empirical scaling analysis, comparing the forces associated with gravity, surface tension, and viscosity on a single bead. The outcome led to the formulation of a unified empirical viscous force law, which serves as a valuable contribution to the understanding of the intricate dynamics within only the RP regime.

There are several major implications for industrial development of wetted-wire columns. First, making eccentric and angled wires is easier in fabrication. Second, increased eccentricity shifts into the convective regime and increased angle increases circulation for a fixed flow rate which increases heat and mass transfer
Zeng et al. 2018
. Third, within the RP regime heat and mass transfer are improved by increasing the number of beads and decreasing the bead velocity. Fourth, slightly increasing the angle allows a user to shift into or out of the convective regime. Fifth, angled wires are less sensitive to eccentricity than vertical wires.
