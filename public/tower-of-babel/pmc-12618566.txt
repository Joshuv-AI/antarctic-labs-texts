Pultrusion of glass fiber reinforced polypropylene bidirectional composites and their mechanical performance
Authors: Mohiuddin Emon, Kirill Minchenkov, Sergey Gusev, Chenggao Li, Guijun Xian, Alexander Safonov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the advantages of thermoplastic resin, pultrusion process has struggled to create bidirectional laminates, resulting in weaknesses in joint connections and transverse properties. To tackle this issue, the study focuses on developing a methodology for pultruding bidirectional laminates. Initially, two types of sheet thermoplastic prepreg of glass fiber and polypropylene were produced: one with transverse (90°) orientation of fibers and another with longitudinal (0°) orientation of fibers. These sheets are then integrated into a single pack where fibers are oriented both transversely and longitudinally, and fed into the pultrusion machine. The resulting strip profiles (75 × 3.5 mm) demonstrate threefold increase in pin-bearing strength and transverse properties, showing a considerable promise for applications that demand robust joint connections and dimensional stability. The addition of transversely reinforced plies makes it possible for the pultruded profile to meet the EN 13706-3 requirements to mechanical performance. The bidirectional thermoplastic profiles produced in this study demonstrated high mechanical properties and are suitable for use in composite structures with bolted joints.Keywords: Thermoplastic composite, Bidirectional composite, Thermoplastic pultrusion, Pin bearing strength, Mechanical propertiesSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Thermoplastics composites have several advantages over thermoset composites, including recyclability, lower environmental impact, weldability, reshapeability, and higher impact toughness1–7. Pultrusion is the most efficient method for producing composite profiles with constant cross-sections, making it ideal for applications in civil engineering, transportation, and marine construction8–13. Pultrusion makes it possible to produce thermoplastic composite profiles of virtually indefinite length, featuring higher production rates and lower energy consumption14. Thermoplastic pultrusion15–19 uses various pre-impregnated materials, such as towpregs20–22, commingled yarns3,23–28, tapes29–31, and sheets32,33, pulling through the guiding system into the heated die where polymer is melted and a pultruded profile takes the desired shape. The use of pre-impregnated materials in thermoplastic pultrusion improves mechanical properties of pultruded profiles and makes the impregnation stage of the process unnecessary. From a pultrusion process standpoint, sheet prepreg has the advantage of being cut into tapes/sheets of any width. This makes it possible to cut sheets of the same width as the die, which significantly reduces the number of pre-impregnated raw materials at the die inlet and simplifies the process control and the equipment preparation.

Many articles on thermoplastic pultrusion are devoted to unidirectional glass fiber reinforced polypropylene (GF/PP) composites20,29–31,34–38. This is due to the ease of processing polypropylene and the low cost of glass fibers. The paper30 presents GF/PP strip profile of 75 × 3.5 mm with fiber volume content of 50%, and longitudinal flexural modulus at least 37 GPa. A 30 × 20 × 3.5 mm tube and a 31.5 × 25.0 × 3.5 mm channel were applied in a PVC (polyvinylchloride) window structure as a reinforcement for improving the thermal performance34. The paper36 presents an investigation into the use GF/PP bars as reinforcement in concrete structures for marine applications. However, no studies have been conducted so far on pultrusion of bidirectional thermoplastic composites. Bidirectional composites, with fibers oriented in two directions, typically at 0° and 90°, offer several advantages over unidirectional composites due to their balanced mechanical properties in multiple directions, providing more balanced in-plane strength and stiffness characteristics of the profile39. The fibers oriented perpendicular to the primary load direction provide more effective distribution of loads around the bolt hole and better load support in the bearing area. This improves the joint’s strength and resistance to failure modes such as bolt hole elongation and cracking40, making this configuration favorable in terms of bolted joints41.

This study explores a novel development in the pultrusion process, making it possible to manufacture bidirectional thermoplastic composite pultrusions. Three batches of strip profiles 75 × 3.5 mm were pultruded with different fiber layups using prepreg sheets made of glass fiber and polypropylene. The mechanical properties of pultruded laminate specimens were evaluated through a series of tests, including tension, flexure, and pin bearing strength testing. A comparative analysis was conducted between thermoplastic bidirectional profiles and a range of alternative materials, including thermoplastic unidirectional laminates, as well as thermoset unidirectional and bidirectional profiles. The results demonstrated significant improvements, particularly in transverse properties and pin bearing strength, highlighting the advantages of pultruded bidirectional thermoplastic composite profiles.

Materials and methods

Source materials, pultrusion device and preparation technology

Sheet prepreg made of T984 unidirectional E-glass fiber rovings (Taishan Co., China) and PPR-MT75 polypropylene matrix (Sinopec, China) was used as raw material for thermoplastic pultrusion of strip profile with dimensions 75 × 3.5 mm. The sheets have the thickness of 0.3 mm and the width of 630 mm, and are supplied in rolls. Durability and mechanical properties of sheets can be found in the study32.

The roll was cut into strips 75 mm wide and 10,000 mm long, along the fiber direction. One strip was used as a single longitudinally reinforced layer with fibers oriented in the (0°) direction, which coincides with the pulling direction. To obtain a transversely reinforced layer (90°), another roll was cut across the fiber direction into strips with a width of 75 mm and a length of 630 mm (roll width). Transversely reinforced strips were then adhered onto the longitudinally reinforced strips by melting polypropylene with a hot air gun. The hot air gun was adjusted to a temperature of 350 °C and a high air flow rate. The nozzle was held at a distance of 30–50 mm at an angle of 45–60° to the surface, continuously moving it along the gluing line. The speed of movement was selected so that the heating time of the section was 3–7 s, sufficient for melting the matrix, but not for destruction. The visual sign of proper melting was the transformation of the matrix into a transparent and glossy layer. The appearance of smoke or darkening spots was excluded. Immediately after heating, the strips were rolled with a roller for compaction. Cooling occurred naturally to avoid internal stresses. The bonding quality was checked after cooling. When trying to peel off the corner, the destruction should have occurred not along the adhesive layer, but along the matrix itself. The success of the method was ensured by a short-term thermal pulse that melts only the surface layer. This process resulted in the formation of a two-layer prepreg sheet. The guiding frames were used to maintain the integrity of the prepreg sheets during pultrusion. The roll of GF/PP sheet, cut sheets and their distribution by guiding frames are shown on Fig. 1.

The heated die block for pultrusion of strip thermoplastic profile consists of a heated die (200 × 115 × 60 mm) and a cooling die (75 × 115 × 60 mm) made of steel, with chrome plated cavity surfaces. The cooling die has a rectangular cavity. The heated die cavity consists of the 10 mm long rectangular section and the 190 mm long tapered section. The die filling ratio (the ratio between the die exit area and the die entrance area) is 0.42, corresponding to the taper angle of 0.72°. Heating and cooling water channels are located in horizontal through holes. Thermocouples are inserted in the slots in the upper part of the die blocks. The inlet edges of the heated and cooling dies are rounded with a radius of 3 mm. A distance between the heated and cooling dies is 47 mm. The heated die temperature was set at 210 ± 10 °C. The cooling die temperature was maintained at 60 ± 10 °C. The pulling speed was set at 0.1 m/min.

Microscopy analysis

Microscopy analysis was conducted for all manufactured laminates using the Axio Scope A1 optical microscope. The specimens were cut perpendicular to the longitudinal direction and then cast in the epoxy resin (Axson Technologies Inc., USA) and polished using the Metprep 3/PH-3 polisher (Allied High Tech Products Inc., USA).

Surface roughness analysis

Surface roughness measurements were conducted using the Surtronic Duo profilometer (Taylor Hobson, UK) in accordance with ISO 4287 guidelines. The measurements were taken over the whole profiles without segmentation. Specifically, four measurements were taken on each side of the profiles at different locations to capture variations in surface characteristics. Measurements were taken both along the fiber direction (longitudinal surface roughness) and across the fiber direction (transverse surface roughness) to obtain comprehensive evaluation of surface characteristics.

Mechanical tests

A series of mechanical tests were performed on the pultruded profiles, including tensile tests per ISO 527-2, flexural tests per ASTM D790-02, and plain-pin bearing tests per ISO 12815:2013 (E) requirements. The mechanical tests were carried out using the Instron 5969 testing machine (Instron, USA). For strain measurements was used the Instron 2630-111 extensometer (Instron, USA) with the gauge length of 50 mm. To obtain each mechanical property, a minimum of 6 specimens were tested. Fiber volume fraction was measured according to ASTM D3171-15 procedure G (matrix burn-off in a muffle furnace).

Results and discussion

Manufactured materials

Three batches of pultruded thermoplastic profiles were manufactured. The profiles in the first batch named SP 9/2 consisted of nine layers of unidirectional fibers oriented in the longitudinal direction and two layers of unidirectional (UD) fibers oriented in the transverse direction (90°). The layup of the SP 9/2 profile is [0, 90, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 90, 0]. The profiles in second batch named SP 7/4 consisted of seven layers of UD fibers oriented in the longitudinal direction and four layers of UD fibers oriented in the transverse direction. The layup of the SP 7/4 profile is [0, 90, 0, 90, 0, 0, 0, 90, 0, 90, 0]. The profiles in the third batch named SP 5/6 consisted of five layers of UD fibers oriented in the longitudinal direction and six layers of UD fibers oriented in the transverse direction. The layup of SP 5/6 profile is [0, 90, 90, 0, 90, 0, 90, 0, 90, 90, 0]. The die block during pultrusion and manufactured profiles are shown in Fig. 3.

Pultrusion process: (a) the heated die block, (b) manufactured thermoplastic strip profiles, (c) surface defects of SP 5/6 profile.

The SP 9/2 and SP 7/4 profiles exhibit a smooth glossy surface, indicating the absence of defects during pultrusion. The SP 5/6 profile has a surface defect manifested as an accumulation of matrix and broken fibers. Additionally, several cavities can be observed at the surface, which can be attributed to the adhesion of polymer to the inner surfaces of the die.

Optical microscopy

An optical microscopic observation was conducted on three batches of profiles. Figure 4 shows the micrograph of the central section of the SP 5/6 profile. The profile features the rough surface with cavities up to 150 µm deep. The transverse (90°) and longitudinal layers (0°) are clearly separated from each other by a thin layer of polymer without fibers. In the region of the transversely reinforced layer (90°) the fibers assume an ellipse-shaped configuration, exhibiting considerable length variations (20 to 1700 μm). The back flow of the resin bends and shifts the fibers from their initial position, thereby inducing a change in fiber orientation toward the longitudinal direction (0°). Given that each longitudinally reinforced layer is located between two transversely layers, the longitudinally reinforced layers inside the profile are straight without significant deformations. Some pores can be observed between the fibers and within the matrix between the layers.

Optical micrograph of the SP 5/6 profile.

Figure 5 shows the optical micrograph of the SP 7/4 profile. In contrast to the SP 5/6 profile, the SP 7/4 profile exhibits a flat surface without defects, with unimpregnated fibers inside the transversely oriented layers. The fibers within the transversely reinforced layers are elliptical in shape, with the majority of fibers representing the long strips. Given that each transversely reinforced layer is located between two longitudinal layers, the transverse fibers are less likely to be displaced from their original position as a result of the backward flow of resin. The fibers within the longitudinally reinforced layers are assembled into bundles with polymer rich areas observed between the fiber bundles.

Optical micrograph of the SP 7/4 profile.

Figure 6 shows the micrograph of the SP 9/2 profile. The profile has a flat surface without defects as opposed to the SP 5/6 profile, with fewer pores in the matrix. In the right region of the profile, the vertical cracks are evident, similar to those observed in thermoset profiles42,43, formed due to thermal shrinkage of the material. No such cracks were observed previously in profiles with UD fibers made from the same prepreg30. The transverse reinforcement in the profile prevents the material displacements due to thermal shrinkage, leading to the accumulation of stress in UD layers and to the subsequent formation of cracks. The longitudinally reinforced layers inside the profile are significantly deformed.

Optical micrograph of the SP 9/2 profile.

The alternation of longitudinal and transverse layers in the SP 5/6 profile leads to an increase in compaction pressure inside the die. This leads to an increase in the friction force and adhesion of the polymer to the die surface, due to the elevated pressure within the heated die. As a result, the number of broken fibers increases, leading to the appearance of surface defects and cavities.

The mechanical properties are express in histograms on Fig. 9. The pultruded thermoplastic U-TP profile exhibits the longitudinal tensile strength of 840 MPa. The tensile strength of the SP 9/2 profile constitutes 649 MPa, representing a 23% reduction compared to the U-TP profile, a 21% increase compared to the SP 7/4 profile, and a 49% increase compared to the SP 5/6 profile. The longitudinal (axial) tensile modulus of the thermoplastic U-TP profile is 30 GPa, that of the SP 9/2 profile is 25 GPa, and that of the SP 7/4 profile is 22 GPa. The longitudinal tensile modulus of the SP 5/6 profile is 12 GPa, which is approximately 1.8 times less than that of the SP 7/4 profile. The longitudinal flexural strength is the highest for the SP 9/2 profile, constituting 505 MPa. The longitudinal flexural strength of the SP 7/4 profile and of the U-TP profile is 5% and 3% less, respectively, than that of the SP 9/2 profile. The flexural strength of the SP 5/6 profile is 37% less than that of the SP 9/2. The longitudinal flexural modulus of SP 9/2 and SP 7/4 profiles, exhibits slight variations and corresponds to an approximate value of 20 GPa. The lowest value of the axial flexural modulus constituting 15 GPa is observed for the SP 5/6 profile, which is approximately 25% lower than that of other thermoplastic profiles. The SP 9/2 profile meets the requirements of EN 13706-3 standard to the axial tensile modulus. However, the axial tensile moduli of the SP 7/4 and SP 5/6 profiles are 6% and 49%, respectively, less than the required minimum. All other longitudinal (axial) mechanical properties of bidirectional thermoplastic profiles meet the requirements specified in the EN 13706-3 standard.

Histograms with mechanical properties.

Mechanical properties of thermoplastic U-TP profile in transverse direction are found to be significantly inferior to those of bidirectional profiles. The tensile strength of the SP 7/4 profile in transverse direction is 313 MPa, which is 44% higher than that of the SP 5/6 profile and 54% higher than that of the SP 9/2. The highest tensile modulus in transverse direction corresponds to the SP 5/6 profile, constituting approximately 15 GPa. However, the SP 5/6 profile demonstrates the highest standard deviation of 4.5 GPa, which corresponds to a coefficient of variation of 30%. The tensile modulus of the SP 7/4 profile in transverse direction constitutes 13.2 GPa, which is 10% lower than that of the SP 5/6 profile and approximately twice as high as that of the SP 9/2 profile. The highest flexural strength in the transverse direction corresponds to the SP 7/4 profile, constituting 359 MPa. The transverse flexural strength of the SP 9/2 and SP 5/6 profiles is 21% and 10% lower, respectively, than that of the SP 7/4 profile. The flexural modulus of the SP 7/4 and SP 5/6 profiles in transverse direction exhibits slight variations, falling within the range of 11.4 to 12.0 GPa. The flexural modulus of the SP 9/2 profile is 9.6 GPa, which is 20% lower than that of the SP 7/4. The tensile modulus of the SP 9/2 profile in transverse direction is close to the requirements of the EN 13706-3, being 6% lower than the required value. All other mechanical properties of the SP 9/2 profile meet the standard requirements. In terms of transverse mechanical properties, SP 7/4 and SP 5/6 profiles are found to fully meet the requirements of the standard.

Theoretical calculations of the tensile modulus were carried out according to the rule of mixtures for a pultruded composite laminate10. The mechanical properties of each individual layer were assumed to be equal to the mechanical properties of the U-TP profile. As can be seen in Table 2, the experimental moduli of profiles are in good agreement with predicted ones. Predicted values for the SP 5/6 profile are higher than experimental ones by 19% and 13% for longitudinal and transverse directions, respectively. This is due to the fact that the calculation does not take into account the influence of the surface defects in the SP 5/6 profile. The difference in mechanical properties of profiles is related to the laying of layers of unidirectional fibers. With a decrease in the number of layers of unidirectional fibers in the longitudinal direction, a decrease in mechanical properties in the longitudinal direction is observed. And with an increase in the number of layers of unidirectional fibers in the transverse direction, an increase in mechanical properties in the longitudinal direction is observed.

Unidirectional thermoset profile U-TS has the highest mechanical properties in longitudinal direction, while its mechanical properties in transverse direction are inferior to those of thermoplastic U-TP profiles and do not meet the requirements of the EN 13706-3. Bidirectional thermoset profile B-TS are superior to bidirectional thermoplastic profiles in terms of tensile and flexural moduli in the longitudinal direction, but inferior in terms of strength. Bidirectional thermoplastic profiles are superior to bidirectional thermoset profile B-TS in all mechanical properties in the longitudinal direction except for the flexural modulus. The thermoset U-TS profile has a fiber volume fraction of 63%, while the thermoset bidirectional profile B-TS and thermoplastic profiles have fiber volume fraction of 46% and 37% respectively.

Table 2 also lists the results of the pin-bearing test conducted for four thermoplastic profiles (SP 9/2, SP 7/4, SP 5/6 and U-TP), compared with results of thermoset profiles (U-TS and B-TS). It is obvious that bidirectional thermoplastic profiles have superior performance in terms of bearing stress, while unidirectional profiles have significantly lower load-bearing capacity. The strength of the thermoplastic U-TP profile is 2.5 times less than that of the thermoset U-TS profile. The ductility of the thermoplastic matrix allows for the elongation of the thermoplastic U-TP profile of up to 1.2 mm, as opposed to 0.1 mm in the case of the thermoset unidirectional profile U-TS. The SP 7/4 profile has 11% higher strength relative to the SP 9/2 profile, while the SP 5/6 shows the 6% reduction in strength relative to SP 7/4. The SP 9/2 profile has 3 times higher pin-bearing strength than the thermoplastic U-TP profile.

Therefore, the bidirectional thermoplastic pultruded profiles produced in this study demonstrate high mechanical properties and are suitable for use in structures with bolted joints on par with thermoset profiles used in large-scale frames and pedestrian bridges10.

Conclusions

The aim of this study was to investigate the feasibility of producing transversely reinforced thermoplastic composite profiles 75 × 3.5 mm by pultrusion process. A sheet glass fiber / polypropylene prepreg was used to fabricate a profile with bidirectional reinforcement. The transverse reinforcement layer was bonded onto the longitudinal reinforcement layer during the pultrusion preparation stage. In total, three profiles consisting of 11 layers of prepreg with different lay-up were produced: the SP 9/2, SP 7/4, and SP 5/6 (number of longitudinal layers / number of transverse layers). The resulting profiles were then subjected to mechanical tests and microscopic observation. The results of the study can be summarized as follows:
