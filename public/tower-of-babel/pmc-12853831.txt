Identification and prioritization of high-frequency biomarkers and therapeutic targets in gastric cancer trials
Authors: Yuchentian Xu, Zhihao Wei, Ming Li, Wenwei Yang, Zhijun Chen, Run Shi, Weixiong Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastric cancer (GC) is the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. An investigation of clinical trials and therapeutic targets for GC was conducted.MethodsUsing the Trialtrove database, we anlyzed global and Chinese clinical trials on GC. Subsequently, we investigated oxaliplatin, S-1, apatinib, nivolumab, and sintilimab, alongside associated oncogenic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. The safety of these targets was evaluated through a joint analysis of the GTEx-RNA, HPA-RNA, and HPA-Proteins datasets. Finally, we assessed their specificity and clinical prospects using HPA pathology and CPTAC data.ResultsCurrently, global clinical trials on GC are primarily concentrated in China (> 50%) and the United States. Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most common cancer and the third leading cause of cancer death worldwide. The number and type of GC clinical trials have increased significantly as novel therapeutic strategies emerge and enter human trials. This reflects the medical community's enthusiasm for overcoming treatment bottlenecks and improving patient prognosis. However, due to its rapid progression, it isn't easy to systematically monitor the diversity, development dynamics, and clinical significance of various treatment regimens.

The evaluation of safety and efficacy for new medicines and therapeutic approaches is fundamentally reliant on clinical trials. To improve GC treatment efficacy, investigators are actively exploring the safety profiles and antitumor activities of new therapeutic strategies. Trastuzumab or pembrolizumab are the current primary treatments for GC [1]. While these first-line treatments have achieved high objective response rates (ORR) in GC (e.g., pembrolizumab was 22% in the KEYNOTE-012 trial [2], and trastuzumab was 43% in HER2-positive GC [3]), most patients still experience treatment-related adverse reactions (e.g., decreased cardiac function, immune pneumonia, and autoimmune inflammatory reactions) [4, 5] In combination therapy, pembrolizumab plus trastuzumab increased the ORR by 22.7% compared with trastuzumab alone [6]. Such studies highlight that primary drug resistance continues to be a principal challenge. Accordingly, many ongoing trials are investigating various combination therapies to address this problem. Moreover, a majority of patients tend to develop acquired resistance later in their treatment course, which severely limits subsequent therapeutic choices. Therefore, treatment often starts with monotherapy and ends with combination therapy [7].

GC patients show significant geographical heterogeneity, primarily due to underlying etiological differences, including Helicobacter pylori infection rates, dietary habits, lifestyles, underlying diseases (e.g., chronic atrophic gastritis), and genetic susceptibilit [8]. These factors affect tumor molecular subtypes and the characteristics of the immune microenvironment, ultimately leading to significant differences in treatment response. Therefore, clinical trial designs must fully incorporate regional stratification factors to provide clearer efficacy information, improve the feasibility and safety of treatment regimens, and provide precise guidance for subsequent trial designs.

Despite the large number of clinical trials and the continuous emergence of new therapies for GC, there remains a lack of a comprehensive, systematic analysis to depict its overall development landscape and effectively identify potential therapeutic targets. Most studies focus on individual drugs or a limited number of targets, lacking a broad comparison of numerous potential targets, especially in two critical dimensions that determine clinical success: safety (off-target toxicity risk) and tumor specificity (expression differences between cancerous and normal tissues). Therefore, this study aims to achieve the following two core objectives by integrating the Trialtrove clinical trial database, regular tissue expression profiles (GTEx, HPA), and tumor proteomics data (CPTAC): (1) comprehensively analyze the current status, trends, and challenges of global and Chinese gastric cancer clinical trials; (2) establish an evaluation framework integrating multi-omics data to systematically assess the safety and specificity of potential therapeutic targets, thereby providing data-driven decision support for the prioritization of new targets and the design of future clinical trials.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a two-phase analytical framework. First, a comprehensive description of gastric cancer clinical trials in the trialtrove database was conducted to reveal the development patterns, geographical distribution, phase transition rates, and evolution of treatment modalities in the field. This section provides a baseline overview of the field's current state. In addition, Hypothesis-Driven and Evaluative Analysis. To address the scientific question of “how to identify potential therapeutic targets from a vast amount of clinical trial data,” we established an evaluation process that integrates multi-omics data. This process includes two core analyses. Using GTEx and HPA data, we systematically predicted potential off-target toxicity risks by comparing target expression profiles across normal tissues. By integrating CPTAC and HPA pathology data, we identified targets specifically upregulated in GC by comparing protein expression levels between cancerous and normal tissues. Ultimately, we combined safety and specificity scores to prioritize potential targets, aiming to provide data-driven decision support for future target selection and drug development.

Gastric cancer clinical trial data

We retrieved 5,747 GC clinical trials from the Trialtrovedatabase (https://pharma.informa.com/) using “gastric cancer” or “GC” as the keywords. The search spanned from 1993 to April 2025. We analyzed this data using Microsoft Excel 2021 and Rstudio (version 4.4.1) to visualize the distribution of trials by country, phase, treatment modality, and target. Based on this analysis, we reviewed relevant literature to summarize the data and develop insights.

The search strategy of this study aims to to capture clinical research related to GC treatment broadly. Therefore, we used ‘gastric cancer’ or ‘GC’ as keywords. We noted that, based on the Trialtrove database classification and current clinical practice, many clinical trials simultaneously include patients with gastric adenocarcinoma, gastroesophageal junction cancer, and/or esophageal adenocarcinoma. This is primarily based on the following considerations: (1) anatomically, pathologically, and molecularly, GEJ cancer and some EAC exhibit high similarity and overlap with gastric cancer; (2) pivotal phase III clinical trials (such as CheckMate-649 and KEYNOTE-811) and treatment guidelines (such as the NCCN guidelines) often study and recommend treatment for these cancers as part of the same disease spectrum. Therefore, to comprehensively reflect the overall landscape and cutting-edge trends in gastric cancer treatment, particularly in the application of immunotherapy and targeted therapy, this study includes these trials in the analysis. We acknowledge that this may introduce heterogeneity, but we believe it better represents the real clinical research ecosystem.

Normal tissue expression data

Normal tissue GTEx-RNA, HPA-RNA, and HPA-Proteins data were gained from the HPA database. We uniformly classified tissues across all three datasets using the methodology described in “The Therapeutic Landscape for Cells Engineered with Chimeric Antigen Receptors” [9].

Cancer data

GC-related data were retrieved from the CPTAC database.

Data analysis

We used Microsoft Excel 2021 to clean and plot the GC clinical data from the Trialtrove database. In RStudio(version 4.4.1), we extracted GC-related drug target information from the GTEx-RNA, HPA-RNA, and HPA-Proteins data and integrated it with GC protein expression data from the CPTAC database. For each cell type in a tissue, HPA protein levels were categorized as “ND-L” (Not Detected to Low) or “M-H” (Medium to High). A tissue was designated as “variable” if it exhibited cell types with both ND-L and M-H expression. The overall expression for a tissue group was assigned based on the highest category present (M-H > variable > ND-L). For RNA expression, HPA RNA and GTEx RNA levels were defined as ND-L if expression was below log₂ (TPM + 1) of 4; otherwise, it was M-H. TPM stands for transcripts per million. RNA tissue expression levels were represented by the average of log₂ (TPM + 1). GTEx tissue names were converted to their corresponding HPA names. We then used Microsoft Excel 2021 to create expression distribution plots for targets in normal and tumor tissues, which were integrated into a single Figure in Adobe Illustrator 2023.

Classification of genes

Relying principally on the HPA pathology page, target genes were categorized as: “CD markers,” “Transporters,” “Membrane proteins only,” “Predicted secreted,” “G-protein coupled receptors, GPCRs,” “FDA approved,” and “Cancer-related.” The “Membrane-only” class identifies membrane-associated proteins without further functional description. “Protein class” pertains to proteins with unreliable antibody validation, designated as low confidence (white) by HPA.

Gastric cancer therapeutic target analysis

We screened for potential GC therapeutic targets by combining typical GC subtype expression patterns from current clinical trials with HPA pathological annotations. Sex-specific organs were considered separately to ensure that the identified targets were either universally applicable or specific to one sex.

Results

Landscape and evolutionary trends of global gastric cancer clinical trials

A total of 5,747 GC clinical trials were retrieved from the Trialtrove clinical trial database. China and the United States account for over 50% of the annotated trials (Fig. 1). Globally, most trials had no gender restrictions (Fig. 1A), primarily enrolling adults, with only a few recruiting children or both adults and children (Fig. 1B). The geographical distribution of trials was concentrated in Asia, the Americas, and Europe (Fig. 1C); Asia had the highest proportion (49%, 3,376 trials), with China leading in both trial count and targets (Fig. 1C, F).

Trial phase distribution showed that 85% were in Phase I-II, with only 5% in Phase IV (Fig. 1D). Since 2020, the number of Phase I and I/II trials has shown a decreasing trend (Fig. 1G). From 2000 to 2019, the total number of trials steadily increased, stabilizing after 2020 (a plateau period) (Fig. 1G). China and the US are their lead (Fig. 1F). The number of clinical trials in China has increased significantly each year since 2014. This trend is also related to a decrease in clinical trials initiated by other countries (Fig. 1H).

The concentration of GC clinical trials in China and the US aligns with the high incidence of GC in East Asia (including China) and parts of the Americas. China’s high disease burden underscores the need for more clinical trials. The US maintains a significant position due to its leading pharmaceutical R&D capabilities and financial investment. In recent years, China has accelerated its trial process through pharmaceutical innovation and clinical trial approval reforms (e.g., the “13th and 14th Five-Year Plans” listed biopharmaceuticals as a key industry, providing dedicated policies and funding, such as the “Major New Drug Innovation” project), leading to a surge in trial numbers. The mature system of the US FDA also ensures its long-term advantage.

Distribution of treatment modalities for gastric cancer

Regarding the distribution of GC clinical trials (Fig. 2A), the “Completed” phase accounts for the largest share, with 3,180 trials. However, only 155 of these reached Phase IV, with over 85% (2,729 trials) stopping at or before Phase II, indicating a low conversion rate. The “Ongoing” phase is subsequent (1,147 trials), primarily comprising Phase I (351), Phase I/II (202), and Phase II (397) trials, reflecting significant interest and a continuous influx of new treatments. The “Planned” phase includes only 395 trials, suggesting that despite rapid development, many proposed strategies still await implementation. The high number of terminated trials (765) is striking, revealing multiple obstacles in the innovation process, such as insufficient efficacy, drug resistance, significant side effects, or a failure to effectively target specific GC subtypes (e.g., chromosomal instability, microsatellite instability, EBV-positive, and genomically stable types) [10, 11] his warrants attention from patients, clinicians, and researchers.

In 2010, the TOGA trial first demonstrated a survival benefit of targeted therapy in HER2-positive GC (trastuzumab: median OS11.1 → 13.8 months) [12] Since then, targeted and immunotherapies have become pivotal in both monotherapy and combination treatments. A comparison of the top ten most frequently tested drugs from 1993 (Fig. 2C) with those from 2015 onwards (Fig. 2D) shows that among the ten drugs, only apatinib was a novel targeted therapy (Fig. 2C). In the last decade, however, immunotherapeutic drugs such as nivolumab, sintilimab, and pembrolizumab (Fig. 2D) have emerged, gradually replacing the chemotherapy-dominated model. This shift validates the clinical value of biomarker-guided personalized medicine (e.g., PD-L1 CPS score, HER2 status) and combination strategies (immuno + chemo/anti-angiogenesis). However, an analysis of drug testing frequency in GC clinical trials (Fig. 2B) shows that most trials were conducted only once. This observation points to considerable difficulties in the initial phases of many trials, signifying that significant advancement in this domain is still required.

An overview of clinical trials for gastric cancer: China

As a high-incidence country for GC, China has accounted for over 50% of global GC clinical trials since 2021 (Fig. 1H), warranting separate analysis. Most Chinese GC trials are in Phase I, Phase I/II, or Phase II, together comprising 70% (Fig. 3A). Phase IV trials number 189 (8.7%), indicating notable progress (Fig. 3A). Nearly half of the trials are in the “Planned” or “Open” stages (Fig. 3B), far exceeding global levels, reflecting sustained research activity.

Analysis of the past 15 years (Fig. 3E) shows Phase II trials have predominated since 2011, driven by the intersection of the domestic innovation ecosystem and clinical demand. Policy incentives and corporate strategies have made Phase II the most cost-effective development stage. At the same time, the urgent need for later-line therapies in advanced GC has prompted rapid Phase II evaluation of PD-1 inhibitors, anti-angiogenic agents, and other breakthrough treatments. Since 2021, the number of trials has remained relatively stable, possibly related to the COVID-19 pandemic or a bottleneck in GC therapeutic development.

Drug analysis shows that targeted and immunotherapeutic agents such as apatinib, sintilimab, camrelizumab, and nivolumab rank among the top ten (Fig. 3E). This pattern differs from global drug data (Fig. 2C) but closely aligns with domestic trends over the past decade (Fig. 2D), indicating China’s shift from a “follower” to an “innovation source” in drug development, supported by strong national backing for domestic innovation. The high cost-effectiveness of agents such as sintilimab, following their inclusion in national reimbursement, also contributes to their high ranking.

Analysis of gastric cancer treatment plans: oxaliplatin, S-1, apatinib, nivolumab, sintilimab

Oxaliplatin is a key component of standard chemotherapy for GC, commonly combined with S-1 or capecitabine (e.g., SOX, XELOX regimens), widely used in locally advanced and metastatic cases [12, 13]. Due to high GC incidence in East Asia, the top countries conducting oxaliplatin-related studies are China (373), the US (87), and Japan (66). As an oral fluoropyrimidine unique to East Asia, S-1 plays a major role in treatment, with most studies concentrated in China (236), Japan (236), and South Korea (46), often combined with oxaliplatin or nivolumab for perioperative and adjuvant therapy [14]. Research on S-1 is limited in Western countries, likely due to differences in metabolism, regulatory approvals, and guideline adoption. Apatinib, a China-developed anti-angiogenic targeted drug, was approved in 2014 for second-line treatment of advanced GC in China [15], with related studies almost exclusively conducted there (Fig. 4A), reflecting the high proportion of late-stage patients and strong domestic drug development suppor [16, 17].

Nivolumab, a fully human IgG4 anti–PD-1 monoclonal antibody, is central to immunotherapy for advanced GC, especially after standard chemotherapy failure. Global trial distribution (Fig. 4B) shows concentration in China, Japan, the United States, and Europe, reflecting its broad applicability across populations, subtypes, and treatment lines. Fourteen and seventeen trials have reached Phase III and IV, respectively, with a higher Phase III/IV proportion than the overall GC trial profile (Fig. 4B). Since 2019, Nivolumab trials have remained high, indicating rapid development, strong academic and industry engagement, and substantial resource and patient investment. In the ATTRACTION-2 study, Nivolumab significantly improved overall survival in Asian patients with advanced GC [18], while CheckMate-649 established Nivolumab plus chemotherapy as first-line treatment for PD-L1 CPS ≥ five patients, gaining global approval [14]. Its efficacy depends on the tumor immune microenvironment (e.g., PD-L1, TMB, MSI) [19, 20], making biomarker testing critical for patient selection.

Sintilimab, another PD-1 monoclonal antibody, restores T-cell antitumor function by blocking the PD-1 pathway [21, 22] and is essential for immune clearance in advanced GC [23]. Similar to Apatinib, Sintilimab trials are almost entirely conducted in China (Fig. 4C), with the United States ranking second (3 trials) after China (122). This pattern reflects its domestic origin, policy support, market demand, and strategic considerations. Clinically, Sintilimab development remains in the early stages, with most trials in Phase II (84 trials, > 70%). Since 2019, the number of trials has steadily increased (Fig. 4C), suggesting encouraging early results and driving further trial initiation. These studies remain in a “rapid exploration” phase. While Sintilimab is active in GC treatment in China, international expansion will require overcoming regulatory, data, and competitive challenges.

Distribution of gastric cancer clinical trials by cancer biomarkers and therapeutic targets

We analyzed the distribution of GC-related cancer biomarkers (Fig. 5A, B, D). Biomarkers with a frequency > 200 included ERBB2 (28.92%), glutamate pyruvate transaminase (GPT, 20.02%), albumin (11.20%), CYP3A4 (8.49%), EGFR, DPYD, CD8A, VEGFA, and CD4 (Fig. 5A, D). ERBB2 is the first and most widely used biomarker in GC-targeted therapy [16, 24]. HER2-targeted trials began with ToGA’s validation of trastuzumab, followed by the development of lapatinib, pertuzumab, and antibody–drug conjugates (e.g., T-DM1, trastuzumab deruxtecan), though efficacy is often limited by resistance or heterogeneity. GPT, a marker of liver function, is routinely monitored for hepatotoxicity during trials, as chemotherapy or targeted agents (e.g., oxaliplatin, apatinib) may cause ALT elevation. Low albumin levels are associated with significantly shorter survival in advanced GC and are frequently used as a prognostic indicator in clinical studies. CYP3A4 is a key enzyme in the metabolism of chemotherapy drugs; most GC-targeted agents are metabolized by CYP3A4, making co-administration with CYP3A4-modulating drugs inadvisable and necessitating periodic monitoring during targeted therapy. However, the overall frequency of biomarker use remains low (Fig. 5B).

From trial data, we also identified the top ten therapeutic targets: KDR, PD-1, HER2, EGFR, FLT4, TUBB1, RET, TOP1, TS, and KIT (Fig. 5C, E). PD-1, KDR, and HER2 constitute the current pillars of GC treatment (immunotherapy, anti-angiogenesis, and targeted therapy), while studies on resistance mechanisms to traditional chemotherapy targets (TS, TUBB1) are informing combination strategies. For example, PD-1 plus KDR/FLT4 blockade—via anti-angiogenic remodeling of the tumor microenvironment with apatinib—can enhance PD-1 inhibitor efficacy; in trial NCT03736863, the objective response rate (ORR) increased to 35%.

Expression of gastric cancer therapeutic targets in normal tissues

Since the majority of today’s therapeutic targets originate in normal cells and tissues, there is an inherent risk of toxicity. To investigate this, we first sourced all gastric cancer-associated targets from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/). We then analyzed target expression in normal tissues using GTEx RNA, HPA RNA, and HPA protein data to identify tissue groups potentially affected by target-directed drugs (Fig. 6A, B). These datasets are complementary. It is important to emphasize that our analysis of the expression profiles in normal tissues aims to preliminarily identify targets that may pose higher safety risks due to high expression in critical organs. This provides a prioritized list for subsequent, more detailed evaluations. This is not a final judgment on the safety of the drugs. AFP, BRCA2, IL2, INSRR, THPO, and TNF were consistently classified as “not detected or low expression” (ND-L) across all tissue categories, suggesting that they may have a low potential on-target toxicity risk, but this needs to be further validated in clinical studies. Immune-related targets such as PDCD1 and TIGIT also showed ND-L expression outside the immune system, supporting their tissue-specificity for immunotherapy.

Specific targets displayed sex-related expression differences. For example, ROS1 showed moderate–high (M-H) expression in female tissues, while TNFRSF18 was more highly expressed in male tissues and skin. This indicates possible sex bias in target expression, highlighting the need to consider sex as a stratification factor in personalized treatment to balance efficacy and safety. The HPA protein data revealed that most targets were expressed at M-H or variable levels in immune tissues or across at least 3 tissue types, precluding analysis of their restricted expression. CTLA4, PDCD1, TIGIT, and CYP2C8 were M-H or variable in only one tissue type, with ND-L in the others. ALK (neural tissue & skin), CD274 (immune tissue & lung), TNFRSF18 (female tissue & skin), ROS1 (male tissue & lung), and CLDN18 (stomach & lung) were M-H or variable in only two tissue types, ND-L elsewhere (Fig. 6A, B). These findings allow the prediction of potential adverse effects and the identification of optimal patient subgroups for specific GC-targeted therapies. For instance, ROS1-targeted therapy in female patients may pose a lower toxicity risk to female tissues; similarly, PGR or TNFRSF18 targeting in male patients may present a lower toxicity risk to male tissues, although TNFRSF18 may also affect skin cells. Such predictions can guide follow-up studies and evidence-based target selection to minimize or monitor off-tumor toxicity in GC therapy (Fig. 6A, B).

An inference of safety for gastric cancer targets from normal tissue expression profiles

For GC therapeutic targets, tissue specificity is generally limited (Fig. 6A, B), yet it is a key determinant of target safety. For example, TNFRSF18 (GITR), indicated for advanced melanoma and non–small cell lung cancer [25], shows detectable protein expression only in normal skin and female tissues. Similarly, FDA-approved targets CD274 (PD-L1) and CLDN18 display high tissue specificity: CD274 is detected only in lung and immune tissues, and CLDN18 only in gastric and lung tissues. Such restricted expression patterns may underlie the favorable efficacy and low toxicity of PD-1/PD-L1 and GITR inhibitors in clinical trials. Likewise, ALK, EPOR, KDR, and ROS1 show similar expression profiles, suggesting comparable safety advantages.

In contrast, some potential GC targets—including ADSL, ARID1A, ERBB3, PARP1, and PTEN—are expressed across diverse cell populations in most normal tissues (Fig. 6B), potentially increasing the risk of off-tumor toxicity during targeted therapy.

Expression of gastric cancer therapeutic targets in tumor tissues

To further identify targets suitable for GC therapy, we analyzed the HPA pathology database and CPTAC protein expression data (Fig. 6C). As expected, targets with medium-to-high (M–H) expression in normal gastric tissue—such as ADSL, ARID1A, ERBB3, PARP1, and PTEN—also exhibited M–H expression in GC samples. We identified eight proteins with not undetected -low (ND–L) expression in normal gastric tissue. However, we upregulated or differentially expressed in GC, including EGFR, FGFR2, KDR, CD40, ROS1, VEGFR, TROP, and CLTA4, indicating strong potential for therapeutic development. Notably, EGFR, FGFR2, KDR, ROS1, VEGFR, and CLTA4 are FDA-approved drug targets (Fig. 6D). Based on comparative screening, FGFR2, CLTA4, and TROP were emerged as promising candidates with favorable safety and specificity profiles. It is noteworthy that while these targets show promising molecular characteristics, their clinical development trajectories vary. FGFR2 is amplified and overexpressed in certain GC subtypes and is an important precision therapy target [26]; the FigureHT trial demonstrated preliminary efficacy of the anti-FGFR2b antibody bemarituzumab plus chemotherapy, with phase III trials (FORTITUDE-101/102) ongoing. CLTA4 inhibits T-cell proliferation and function by competitively binding CD80/CD86 during early T-cell activation, inducing immune tolerance. TROP-based therapy employs anti-TROP2 antibodies to bind tumor cell–surface TROP2, delivering conjugated to them cytotoxic agents into cancer cells via endocytosis [27].

Discussion

This study systematically analyzed 5,747 gastric cancer clinical trials, revealing trends in their geographical distribution, phase transitions, and treatment modalities. More importantly, we went beyond traditional descriptive analysis and, for the first time, constructed a target evaluation process based on multi-omics data. Through this process, we not only validated the rationale for approved targets such as PD-1 and HER2, but also identified high-potential candidate targets including FGFR2, CTLA4, and TROP that exhibit good safety and specificity. This discovery provides new insights and clear candidates for overcoming the common issues of off-target toxicity and drug resistance in current targeted therapies.

Our macro analysis of 5,747 global clinical trials reveals two prominent contradictions. First, despite the large number of trials and China's emergence as a global innovation engine, up to 85% of trials stagnate at early stages, with only a very small proportion successfully transitioning to confirmatory clinical (Phase IV) outcomes. This highlights the challenging path of clinical development for gastric cancer drugs. Secondly, although the treatment paradigm has shifted from chemotherapy-dominated approaches to a diverse exploration of targeted and immunotherapies, most drugs are tested at very low frequencies, exhibiting a “fragmented” innovation pattern. These phenomena point to a fundamental issue: many treatment strategies lack sufficient target validation when entering clinical trials, making it difficult to demonstrate broad efficacy or acceptable safety in the highly heterogeneous GC population. Therefore, it is imperative to establish a reliable target prioritization system during preclinical and early clinical research stages.

To address the aforementioned challenges, the core contribution of this study lies in developing a scalable target evaluation process. Unlike studies that focus solely on efficacy or a single dimension (such as genetic mutations), our framework places equal importance on target safety and tumor specificity. We utilize the GTEx and HPA normal tissue atlases to predict off-target toxicity risks at both the transcript and protein levels. Concurrently, we integrate CPTAC tumor proteomics data with HPA pathological slides to quantify the specific expression of targets within gastric cancer tissues. This cross-validation approach using multi-omics data significantly enhances the predictive accuracy and reliability of downstream target selection. We believe that this process is not only applicable to gastric cancer but also provides a valuable paradigm for target discovery in other solid tumors.

Applying this framework, we successfully identified FGFR2, CTLA4, and TROP2 as the most promising targets from among numerous candidates. These findings are grounded in solid scientific evidence and support. FGFR2b is an isoform of the FGFR2 gene produced through selective splicing, with 20% to 30% of GC patients exhibiting FGFR2b overexpression (IHC detection: any tumor cell staining at 2 +/3 +) [28]. Our data confirm its limited expression in normal tissues (good safety) and significant upregulation in specific gastric cancer populations (high specificity). Bemarituzumab, a first-in-class humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody targeting FGFR2b, exerts antitumor effects through dual mechanisms [29]. It highly selectively inhibits the binding of the FGF ligand to the FGFR2b receptor, blocking FGFR signaling; simultaneously, its Fc segment, glycoengineered, can induce enhanced antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), leading to antitumor effects [29]. In the FIGHT trial [30], bemarituzumab treatment led to the first-ever breakthrough in overall survival of over two years for advanced GC patients, providing strong external validation for precision therapy targeting the FGFR2b subtype. CTLA4, an immune checkpoint, plays a role in the early stages of the tumor immune cycle and has a complementary mechanism to PD-1. Our safety analysis shows its expression is highly concentrated in immune tissues, indicating a manageable toxicity profile. Although evidence for its solo use in GC is limited, its combination with PD-1 inhibitors has shown synergistic effects in various cancers, and our research adds theoretical support for its further exploration in gastric cancer [31]. TROP2, highly expressed in various epithelial cancers, is an ideal platform for antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs). Our analysis shows its specific upregulation in GC, while its low expression in normal gastric tissue suggests a wide therapeutic window. ADCs targeting TROP2 (such as SKB264) have shown promise in early trial [32], and our data support the rationale for their further development based on target characteristics.

However, the candidate targets identified in this study, such as FGFR2, CTLA4, and TROP2, indeed face challenges or are in relatively slow stages of clinical development. This, in fact, highlights the value of our study. Our analytical framework aims to conduct preliminary screening based on fundamental dimensions of safety and tumor specificity, while the success of clinical development often depends on other complex factors. The development of FGFR2 inhibitors is limited by the need for effective patient enrichment strategies (not all patients with FGFR2 overexpression respond) and the inherent toxicity of the drugs (such as hyperphosphatemia caused by non-selective FGFR inhibition) [33, 34]. The efficacy of CTLA4 monotherapy in GC is limited mainly due to its unique immune-activation mechanism, which can trigger severe immune-related adverse events, and primary resistance in “immune desert” tumors [35–37]. TROP2, as an ADC target, has great potential, but its development relies on the optimization of linker technology and payloads to balance efficacy and toxicity [38, 39].

Therefore, our findings do not imply that these targets should be abandoned, but rather indicate the future directions for their development: For FGFR2, more precise biomarkers (such as the FGFR2b isoform) and safer next-generation inhibitors are needed; for CTLA4, its future lies in rational combination with PD-1 inhibitors and dose optimization; for TROP2, the focus should be on the iteration of ADC technology. Our work demonstrates the potential of these targets based on their inherent characteristics, and the subsequent challenges require innovation in medicinal chemistry and clinical strategies to address.

CLDN18.2 is indeed one of the most notable breakthroughs in targeted therapy for gastric cancer. It is worth noting that the success of CLDN18.2 perfectly validates the target evaluation logic adopted in this study. CLDN18.2 is strictly confined to specific cell layers of the gastric mucosa in normal tissues while being widely expressed in GC [40–41]. This high degree of tumor specificity forms the molecular basis for its remarkable efficacy and controllable safety profile [40, 42]. Although CLDN18.2 was not prominently highlighted in our initial target list, possibly due to database retrieval strategies or the timing of the analysis, its characteristics align closely with our screening criteria. Including CLDN18.2 in our considerations not only does not weaken our conclusions but strongly reinforces our core argument: Target screening based on differential expression between normal and tumor tissues is an effective strategy for identifying therapeutic windows with high efficacy and low toxicity. The success of Zolbetuximab (anti-CLDN18.2 monoclonal antibody) provides an encouraging precedent for other candidate targets identified through principles similar to those used in our study [40, 42].

The clinical fate of the different therapeutic strategies revealed in this study is rooted in the fundamental interaction between target biology and tumor heterogeneity. The success of targeted therapies hinges on precisely targeting oncogenic driver pathways and utilizing accurate biomarkers. For example, the success of HER2-targeted therapy not only stems from its inhibition of the MAPK/PI3K signaling pathways but also from the “HER2-positive” selection criteria established by the ToGA trial, ensuring that the drug acts on tumors addicted to this pathway [43]. Similarly, the potential targets identified in this study, such as FGFR2, also exhibit characteristics of driver genes, with their efficacy relying on precise patient stratification [44]. Conversely, the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as nivolumab) is not universal; it is highly dependent on the tumor immune microenvironment defined by biomarkers such as PD-L1, TMB, and MSI [43]. For instance, MSI-H (“inflammatory”) tumors are sensitive to PD-1 inhibitors, whereas most MSS tumors exhibit resistance due to a lack of T-cell infiltration (“immune desert”), explaining the need for combination therapies with chemotherapy or anti-angiogenic drugs to reshape the microenvironment [43]. Moreover, tumor heterogeneity and clonal evolution leading to resistance are common challenges. Examples include PTEN loss/PIK3CA mutations in HER2 therapy and the intratumoral heterogeneity that ADC drugs must overcom [43]. These challenges indicate that the future success of the targets we identified (such as FGFR2, CTLA4, and TROP2) will depend on our ability to precisely define the optimal patient populations and deeply understand their resistance mechanisms.

In summary, this study goes beyond mere data description; it first diagnoses the core bottlenecks in the clinical research and development of gastric cancer, and then provides a universal solution—an analytical framework that integrates macro-level insights with micro-level target evaluation. Through this framework, we not only outline a comprehensive overview of the field but also deliver a data-validated, high-potential priority list of targets. Although retrospective data have inherent limitations, our research offers essential tools to overcome the randomness in target selection. Future efforts should focus on combining such bioinformatics discoveries with preclinical functional experiments and advancing biomarker-based precision clinical trial designs, ultimately translating these promising targets into tangible survival benefits for gastric cancer patients.

This study has some limitations. First, our analysis includes clinical trials for gastric adenocarcinoma, GEJ cancer, and EAC. While this inclusion criterion helps us obtain a broad perspective on the field of gastric cancer treatment and aligns with the design of many cutting-edge trials, we acknowledge that there are known biological and clinical differences between these cancer subtypes. Future research can build on this by conducting subgroup analyses of clinical trials to reveal differences in treatment strategies for tumors at different anatomical locations or with different molecular profiles. Nonetheless, the broad perspective and target evaluation framework provided by this study lay an important foundation for subsequent detailed research.

Additionally, concerning the dimension of target safety analysis, our approach primarily infers potential toxicity risks based on expression levels in normal tissues (ND-L/M-H), which is indeed a simplified model. The actual safety of a drug is a complex, multidimensional property that depends not only on target expression abundance but also on the critical physiological functions of the target in specific tissues, the distribution and clearance characteristics of the drug, and the breadth of the therapeutic window. While this method serves as an effective preliminary filter to quickly identify high-risk candidates due to high expression in essential organs from a vast pool of targets, it should by no means replace comprehensive preclinical and clinical safety evaluations.

Although the Trialtrove database has broader coverage than ClinicalTrials.gov, it may still not capture all GC-related clinical research. Given differences in data sources and recording methods, these datasets were not merged. The HPA database covers over 90% of protein-coding genes, classifying them by expression pattern and function. However, its IHC-based data can be platform-dependent and should be validated with other omics data. To improve target selection specificity, we incorporated CPTAC proteomic data to compare protein expression between GC tumors and matched normal tissues, cross-validating with HPA results. Despite improving rigor, the lack of standardized target evaluation methods and threshold definitions can lead to inconsistencies across databases. Moreover, most data are from Western populations. They may not fully reflect molecular features of East Asian patients—such as higher CLDN18.2 positivity and more frequent EBV-positive GC in the Asia–Pacific region. Future efforts should focus on building geographically representative, localized databases. With the rise of AI and machine learning, AI-based target discovery and prediction systems may help identify high-value, underexplored targets, thereby optimizing the discovery and development process for targeted therapies.

Conclusions

This study systematically analyzed GC-related clinical trials and subsequently evaluated the safety and specificity of markers for targeted therapy at the transcriptomic and proteomic levels, identifying FGFR2, CTLA4, and TROP as potential candidates. This study provides a valuable reference for evaluating drug target safety and specificity. Future drug design should focus on: (1) functional criticality of targets, selecting those that play key regulatory roles in tumorigenesis, progression, or resistance to inhibit tumor growth fundamentally; (2) high expression in cancer cells with low or absent expression in normal tissues to minimize treatment-related toxicity; (3) stable expression throughout the treatment course to ensure sustained therapeutic effect; and (4) cellular localization on the cancer cell surface to facilitate binding and delivery of antibody-based or large-molecule therapies. However, tumor heterogeneity and dynamic evolution pose significant challenges for target selection. Therefore, target screening should balance broad patient coverage with adaptability to tumor evolution. We hope this study’s findings and insights will inform future GC clinical research and precision therapy strategies.
