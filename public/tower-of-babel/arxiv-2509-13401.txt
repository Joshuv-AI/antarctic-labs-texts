Effective density matrix for vacua in asymptotically flat gravity
Temple He, Prahar Mitra, Kathryn M. Zurek

Abstract
We explicitly construct the density matrix associated to the vacuum state of a large spherically symmetric causal diamond of area A A in four-dimensional asymptotically flat gravity. We achieve this using the soft effective action, which characterizes the low-energy gravitational degrees of freedom that arise in the long-distance limit of the Einstein–Hilbert action and consists of both the soft graviton mode and the Goldstone mode arising from the spontaneous breaking of supertranslation symmetry. Integrating out the soft graviton mode, we obtain an effective action for purely the Goldstone mode, from which we extract the density matrix and therefore the modular Hamiltonian K ^ s \hat{K}_{s} associated to the vacuum state. As a corollary, we explicitly compute the mean and variance of K ^ s \hat{K}_{s} , finding ⟨ Δ ​ K ^ s 2 ⟩ = A / ϵ UV 2 \langle\Delta{\hat{K}}_{s}^{2}\rangle=A/\epsilon_{\text{\tiny UV}}^{2} , with ϵ UV \epsilon_{\text{\tiny UV}} being a length-scale UV cutoff on the celestial sphere.

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\langle}\Delta{\hat{K}}_{s}^{2}{\rangle}&=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{A}{{\epsilon}^{2}}-4\right),\end{split}

(28)

which is precisely of the form given in Eq. (
2
). Comparing with Eq. (
26
), we see that except for the term
\ln{\langle}{\mathcal{C}}|{\mathcal{C}}{\rangle}
,
{\langle}\Delta{\hat{K}}_{s}^{2}{\rangle}
is precisely equal to
{\langle}{\hat{K}}_{s}{\rangle}
. Similar results for the modular Hamiltonian of a causal diamond have previously been obtained in
[
5
,
43
,
44
]
.

Finally, let us now discuss the last term in Eq. (
26
), which may appear troubling since it is divergent. This divergence arises from the fact that the eigenstates of
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
are non-normalizable, which arises due to the fact that the conjugate of
{\hat{\mathcal{C}}}
, namely
{\hat{\mathcal{N}}}
, is non-compact. The same divergence arises in the thermal density matrix of a free particle on a line, and indeed, our density matrix in Eq. (
18
) is qualitatively similar to the free particle Hamiltonian. To be precise, we show in the Supplementary Materials that for
{\hat{H}}=\frac{{\hat{P}}^{2}}{2m}
with
{\hat{X}}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the mean of the modular Hamiltonian is (see Eq. (
S16
))

\begin{split}{\langle}{\hat{K}}_{\text{line}}({\beta}){\rangle}=\frac{1}{2}+\ln\left(\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi{\beta}}}{\langle}p|p{\rangle}\right).\end{split}

(29)

The divergence in Eq. (
29
) due to the logarithm of
{\langle}p|p{\rangle}
is precisely the same one in Eq. (
26
) and can be regulated in several ways. We explore two such ways in the Supplementary Materials. For instance, if we compactify
{\hat{X}}\sim{\hat{X}}+2\pi R
, then we show that for large
R
, we have

\begin{split}{\langle}{\hat{K}}_{\text{ring}}({\beta}){\rangle}~\xrightarrow{R\to\infty}~\frac{1}{2}+\ln\left(\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi{\beta}}}(2\pi R)\right),\end{split}

(30)

which regulates
{\langle}p|p{\rangle}
in Eq. (
29
) to
2\pi R
. Alternatively, the divergence can be regulated by turning on a small potential for the field. For example, we consider a simple harmonic oscillator described by the Hamiltonian
{\hat{H}}=\frac{{\hat{P}}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}m{\omega}^{2}{\hat{X}}^{2}
. In this case, we find that for small
{\omega}
,

\begin{split}{\langle}{\hat{K}}_{\text{\tiny SHO}}({\beta}){\rangle}~\xrightarrow{{\omega}\to 0}~1-\ln( \b\o),\end{split}

(31)

which regulates
{\langle}p|p{\rangle}
in Eq. (
29
) to
\sqrt{2\pi/(m{\omega})}
.

Indeed, the latter type of regulation is precisely what we expect to occur for the SEA as well once we allow the soft graviton and Goldstone modes to have small but nonzero energy. More precisely, the SEA in Eq. (
14
) describes the dynamics of soft modes
{\hat{\mathcal{C}}}
and
{\hat{\mathcal{N}}}
, which have exactly zero energy (
{\omega}=0
), while the holographic model that describes all of four-dimensional gravity should include modes of all energies
{\omega}>0
. At lowest derivative order, we expect the effective action of the mode at energy
{\omega}
will be precisely that of a harmonic oscillator. It follows the mean and variance of the modular Hamiltonian can be obtained (at least to lowest derivative order) by integrating
{\langle}{\hat{K}}_{\text{\tiny SHO}}({\beta}){\rangle}
over
{\omega}
with a frequency density
n({\omega})
that depends on the details of four-dimensional theory. As we show in the Supplementary Materials, if we assume a uniform distribution (which is expected at lowest derivative order), we obtain the equality

\displaystyle{\langle}{\hat{K}}_{\text{\tiny SHO}}({\beta}){\rangle}={\langle}\Delta{\hat{K}}^{2}_{\text{\tiny SHO}}({\beta}){\rangle},

(32)

a relation that has been obtained in a variety of setups
[
42
,
43
,
44
,
31
,
14
]
. Thus, to summarize, we view the logarithmic divergence in Eq. (
26
) as a symptom of the zero energy limit, which is the realm where the SEA is applicable. We leave an analysis on how to appropriately uplift the SEA to nonzero energies for future work.

Finally, we observe that the scaling of
{\langle}\Delta{\hat{K}}_{s}^{2}{\rangle}
as the area in Eq. (
28
) was also obtained using other arguments for flat spacetimes
[
42
,
43
,
44
,
31
,
14
]
. However, whereas all such previous arguments involved either thermodynamic arguments or were effectively a semiclassical analysis of perturbative gravity, Eq. (
28
) takes into account quantum effects coming from the IR divergent term in the SEA, demonstrating that the scaling survives loop corrections.

Conclusions.
In this letter, we have shown that by taking the soft effective action derived in
[
26
]
as our starting point, we are able to construct a density matrix
{\hat{\rho}}_{s}
in the soft Hilbert space of a large causal diamond that reproduces both the leading soft theorem as well as the IR factorization found in
S
-matrix elements. This density matrix captures the entanglement in the vacuum sector of gravitational theories in asymptotically flat spacetimes. By further computing the variance of the modular Hamiltonian, we are able to quantify the fluctuation of the entanglement and show that it obeys an area law. In fact, because we have constructed the density matrix, we can further compute other information-theoretic quantities that are of interest.

We would like to make a few comments regarding the density matrix that we have constructed. In four spacetime dimensions, the parameter
{\alpha}
is logarithmically divergent (as
\mu/\Lambda\to 0
) and captures precisely the IR divergences in the one-loop determinant of the graviton. Hence, if we formally take this limit inside the integrand in Eq. (
18
), we see that the density matrix becomes proportional to the identity. This suggests that the density matrix in the vacuum sector has a flat entanglement spectrum and is maximally mixed. This result was obtained in the context of de Sitter spacetime in
[
62
,
63
]
, and more generally in the context of maximally symmetric spacetimes in
[
3
]
.

On the other hand, if we are interested in phenomena that depends sensitively on the IR data, we do not want to take
{\alpha}\to\infty
. Instead,
{\alpha}
is formally a large, but finite, number. In this case, the density matrix is no longer maximally mixed. As was argued in
[
64
]
, again in the context of de Sitter spacetime, one does not need a flat entanglement spectrum to obtain Eq. (
28
). Rather, it holds for any value of
{\alpha}
.

Finally, we conclude by observing that Eq. (
21
) implies

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Delta{\hat{K}}_{s}^{2}&\equiv({\hat{K}}_{s}-{\langle}{\hat{K}}_{s}{\rangle})^{2}\\
&=\bigg(\frac{\pi}{{\alpha}{\kappa}^{2}}\oint_{z}{\hat{\mathcal{C}}}^{zz}{\hat{\mathcal{C}}}_{zz}-{\langle}S_{{\text{eff}}}[{\hat{\mathcal{C}}}]{\rangle}\bigg)^{2},\end{split}

(33)

which means that
\Delta{\hat{K}}_{s}^{2}
scales as the four-point function of the Goldstone mode
{\mathcal{C}}
. As
{\mathcal{C}}
is part of the physical data present in the shear
C_{zz}
, this suggests that modular fluctuations, which have the same scaling behavior as area fluctuations, involve the four-point function of length fluctuations in a perturbative quantum theory of gravity. It would be of great interest to try to connect such length fluctuations to actual observables within an experimental setup, and we hope to address this in future work.

I
The Path Integral Measure

The goal of this appendix is to construct the measure that satisfies Eq. (
20
), which we reproduce here for convenience:

\displaystyle\int[{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{C}}]e^{-S_{\text{eff}}[{\mathcal{C}}]}=1.

(S1)

First, we recall from Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
) that

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{\text{eff}}[{\mathcal{C}}]&=\frac{\pi}{{\alpha}}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\ell_{\max}}\sum_{m=1}^{2\ell+1}(\ell-1)\ell(\ell+1)(\ell+2){\mathcal{C}}_{\ell m}^{2},\qquad\ell_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{A}{{\epsilon}^{2}}}-1.\end{split}

(S2)

Now, the path integral measure in general can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int[{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{C}}]=\frac{1}{\text{vol}({\mathbb{R}}^{4})}\exp\left(-a_{1}-\frac{c}{6}\ln\frac{A}{{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)\prod_{\ell=0}^{\ell_{\max}}\prod_{m=1}^{2\ell+1}\frac{a_{2}}{(A/{\epsilon}^{2})^{\lambda}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\text{d}}{\mathcal{C}}_{\ell m},\end{split}

(S3)

where
a_{1},a_{2},c,{\lambda}
are all currently unspecified dimensionless parameters that we will determine to ensure the UV divergences are renormalized. This measure is manifestly invariant under supertranslations
/\mathbb{R}^{4}
and the
SU(2)
subgroup of
SL(2,\mathbb{C})
(it is an interesting question whether the form of this measure can be further fixed by imposing the full
SL(2,\mathbb{C})
). Notice that Eq. (
S3
) with Eq. (
12
) imply that the gauge-invariant Dirac delta function involving
{\mathcal{C}}
, which excludes the
\ell=0,1
gauge modes, is then in terms of spherical harmonic modes

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\delta}({\mathcal{C}}-{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime})&=\left[\frac{(A/{\epsilon}^{2})^{\lambda}}{a_{2}}\right]^{4}\exp\left(a_{1}+\frac{c}{6}\ln\frac{A}{{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)\prod_{\ell=2}^{\ell_{\max}}\prod_{m=1}^{2\ell+1}{\delta}\left(\frac{a_{2}}{(A/{\epsilon}^{2})^{\lambda}}({\mathcal{C}}_{\ell m}-{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}_{\ell m})\right).\end{split}

(S4)

The constants
a_{2}
and
{\lambda}
in Eq. (
S3
) renormalize
{\mathcal{C}}
, whereas
a_{1}
is associated to a counterterm action. Additionally, we divide by
\text{vol}({\mathbb{R}}^{4})
to account for the gauge symmetry given by Eq. (
6
).

As an aside, we note that
c
parametrizes the logarithmic divergence in the measure that cannot be canceled by local counterterms. Indeed,
c
is the central charge of the theory
[
65
]
. To see this, we note that we can think of the change in the radius
R
of the sphere as a Weyl transformation given by

\displaystyle{\delta}_{\omega}g_{ab}=2{\omega}g_{ab}\quad\implies\quad{\delta}_{\omega}R=\omega R.

(S5)

Noting that our soft effective action is conformally invariant, only the nonlocal term will contribute to the trace anomaly. Using Eq. (3.4.6) of
[
66
]
, we can compute the central charge
c_{T}
of the theory to be

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\delta}_{\omega}\bigg(-\frac{c}{6}\ln\frac{A}{{\epsilon}^{2}}\bigg)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{S^{2}}{\text{d}}^{2}x\,\sqrt{g}\omega{\langle}T^{a}{}_{a}{\rangle}=-\frac{c_{T}}{24\pi}\int_{S^{2}}{\text{d}}^{2}x\,\sqrt{g}\omega{\mathcal{R}}_{g}=-\frac{c_{T}}{3}\omega,\end{split}

(S6)

where
{\mathcal{R}}_{g}=2/R^{2}
is the Ricci scalar of the sphere, and we used the fact
{\langle}T^{a}{}_{a}{\rangle}=-c_{T}{\mathcal{R}}_{g}/12
. Using
A=4\pi R^{2}
and substituting in Eq. (
S5
), we get
c_{T}=c
, proving our claim that
c
is the central charge.

We now want to confirm that we can choose the parameters in Eq. (
S3
) such that Eq. (
S1
) holds. Substituting Eqs. (
S2
) and (
S3
) into Eq. (
S1
) and carrying out the resultant Gaussian integrals, we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\exp\left(-a_{1}-\frac{c}{6}\ln\frac{A}{{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)\prod_{\ell=0}^{\ell_{\max}}\left[\frac{a_{2}}{(A/{\epsilon}^{2})^{\lambda}}\right]^{2\ell+1}\prod_{\ell=2}^{\ell_{\max}}\left[\frac{{\alpha}}{(\ell-1)\ell(\ell+1)(\ell+2)}\right]^{\frac{2\ell+1}{2}}=1.\end{split}

(S7)

Taking the logarithm of both sides, we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}0=-a_{1}-\frac{c}{6}\ln\frac{A}{{\epsilon}^{2}}+(\ell_{\max}+1)^{2}\left[\ln a_{2}-{\lambda}\ln\frac{A}{{\epsilon}^{2}}\right]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\ell_{\max}}(2\ell+1)\ln\frac{{\alpha}}{(\ell-1)\ell(\ell+1)(\ell+2)}.\end{split}

(S8)

We evaluate the sum in the last term in
Mathematica
and simplify using the identity (for
\text{Re}\,x>-1
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{s}\zeta(s,x+1)\big|_{s=-1}&=\psi^{(-2)}(x)+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+x\ln x-\frac{x}{2}\log(2\pi e )+\frac{1}{12}-\ln{\mathcal{A}},\end{split}

(S9)

where
\zeta(s,x)
is the generalized Reimann zeta function,
\psi^{(\nu)}(x)
the polygamma function, and
{\mathcal{A}}\approx 1.2824
is the Glaisher-Kinkelin constant. This implies

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\ell_{\max}}(2\ell+1)\ln\frac{{\alpha}}{(\ell-1)\ell(\ell+1)(\ell+2)}&=\frac{1}{2}(\ell_{\max}-1)(\ell_{\max}+3)\ln{\alpha}-4\psi^{(-2)}(\ell_{\max})\\
&\qquad-\frac{1}{2}(2\ell_{\max}+1)\ln(\ell_{\max}+2)-\frac{3}{2}(2\ell_{\max}-1)\ln\ell_{\max}\\
&\qquad-2\ell_{\max}\big[\ell_{\max}+\ln(\ell_{\max}+1)-\ln(2 \pi e)\big]+\frac{1}{2}\ln(432).\end{split}

(S10)

Substituting Eq. (
S10

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&=\frac{A}{{\epsilon}^{2}}\left[-({\lambda}+1)\ln\frac{A}{{\epsilon}^{2}}+\ln( a_2 \sqrt{\a} e )\right]+\frac{8-c}{6}\ln\frac{A}{{\epsilon}^{2}}-a_{1}-2\ln{\alpha}-4\ln{\mathcal{A}}-2+\frac{1}{2}\ln 432+{\mathcal{O}}({\epsilon}^{2}/A).\end{split}

(S11)

It is then straightforward to check that this equality is satisfied given

\begin{split}{\lambda}=-1,\qquad a_{1}=-2+\frac{1}{2}\ln 432-4\ln{\mathcal{A}}-2\ln{\alpha},\qquad a_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\alpha}}e},\qquad c=8.\end{split}

(S12)

This completes our verification that a measure satisfying Eq. (
S1
) exists.

II
Thermal Partition Functions

We review the usual calculation of the thermal partition function for the free particle and the simple harmonic oscillator (SHO). For power counting reasons, we keep all factors of
\hbar
explicit.

II.1
Free Particle on a Line

The Hamiltonian of a free particle on a line with mass
m
is given by

\begin{split}{\hat{H}}_{\text{line}}=\frac{{\hat{P}}^{2}}{2m},\qquad[{\hat{X}},{\hat{P}}]=i\hbar,\qquad{\hat{X}}\in{\mathbb{R}}.\end{split}

(S13)

Momentum (and energy) eigenstates with eigenvalue
p\in{\mathbb{R}}
(
E_{p}=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}
) are denoted by
|p{\rangle}
. These are
{\delta}
-function normalized, in that
\innerproduct{p}{p'}=2\pi\hbar{\delta}(p-p^{\prime})
. The thermal partition function is given by

\begin{split}Z_{\text{line}}({\beta})={\text{Tr}}\big[e^{-{\beta}{\hat{H}}_{\text{line}}}\big]={\langle}p|p{\rangle}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{{\text{d}}p}{2\pi\hbar}e^{-\frac{{\beta}p^{2}}{2m}}=\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi{\beta}\hbar^{2}}}{\langle}p|p{\rangle}.\end{split}

(S14)

The mean and variance of any modular Hamiltonian
\hat{K}({\beta})
is given in terms of the partition function
Z({\beta})
by the standard thermodynamic identities

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\langle}{\hat{K}}({\beta}){\rangle}=(1-{\beta}\partial_{\beta})\ln Z({\beta}),\qquad{\langle}\Delta{\hat{K}}^{2}({\beta}){\rangle}={\beta}^{2}\partial_{\beta}^{2}\ln Z({\beta}).\end{split}

(S15)

Substituting Eq. (
S14
) into the above identities, we obtain

\begin{split}{\langle}{\hat{K}}_{\text{line}}({\beta}){\rangle}=\frac{1}{2}+\ln\bigg( \sqrt{ \frac{m}{2\pi\b\hbar^2}} \la p | p \ra\bigg),\qquad{\langle}\Delta{\hat{K}}_{\text{line}}^{2}({\beta}){\rangle}=\frac{1}{2}.\end{split}

(S16)

Since
|p{\rangle}
is non-normalizable, we see the mean has a logarithmic divergence.

II.2
Free Particle on a Ring

The logarithmic divergence in Eq. (
S16
) can be regulated by making
{\hat{X}}
compact. To see this, consider the Hamiltonian for a free particle on a ring of radius
R
with mass
m
, so that

\begin{split}{\hat{H}}_{\text{ring}}=\frac{{\hat{P}}^{2}}{2m},\qquad[{\hat{X}},{\hat{P}}]=i\hbar,\qquad{\hat{X}}\sim{\hat{X}}+2\pi R.\end{split}

(S17)

Momentum (and energy) eigenstates with eigenvalue
p_{n}=\frac{n\hbar}{R}
(and
E_{n}=\frac{n^{2}\hbar^{2}}{2mR^{2}}
), where
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, are denoted by
\ket{n}
. These are normalized as
{\langle}n|n^{\prime}{\rangle}={\delta}_{nn^{\prime}}
. The thermal partition function is now

\begin{split}Z_{\text{ring}}({\beta})={\text{Tr}}\big[e^{-{\beta}{\hat{H}}_{\text{ring}}}\big]=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{{\beta}\hbar^{2}}{2mr^{2}}n^{2}}=\vartheta_{3}\left(0,e^{-\frac{{\beta}\hbar^{2}}{2mR^{2}}}\right).\end{split}

(S18)

In this case, we find

\begin{split}{\langle}{\hat{K}}_{\text{ring}}({\beta}){\rangle}&=(1-{\beta}\partial_{\beta})\ln Z_{\text{ring}}({\beta})=-{\beta}\partial_{\beta}\ln\vartheta_{3}\Big(0,e^{-\frac{{\beta}\hbar^{2}}{2mR^{2}}}\Big)+\ln\vartheta_{3}\Big(0,e^{-\frac{{\beta}\hbar^{2}}{2mR^{2}}}\Big).\end{split}

(S19)

We see that the logarithmic divergence in Eq. (
S16
) is regulated by the Jacobi theta function. In the limit
R\to\infty
where the ring approximates the line, we recover the logarithmic divergence since

\begin{split}\ln\vartheta_{3}\Big(0,e^{-\frac{{\beta}\hbar^{2}}{2mR^{2}}}\Big)\quad\xrightarrow{R\to\infty}\quad\ln\left(\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi{\beta}\hbar^{2}}}(2\pi R)\right).\end{split}

(S20)

Comparing this to Eq. (
S16
), we see that putting the free particle on a ring regulates
{\langle}p|p{\rangle}
to
2\pi R
.

II.3
Simple Harmonic Oscillator

Another way to regulate the logarithmic divergence in Eq. (
S16
) is to add a potential term to the Hamiltonian. To see this, consider the simple harmonic oscillator (SHO) with mass
m
and frequency
\omega
is given by

\displaystyle{\hat{H}}_{{\text{\tiny SHO}}}=\frac{{\hat{P}}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}{\hat{X}}^{2},\qquad[{\hat{X}},{\hat{P}}]=i\hbar,\qquad{\hat{X}}\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(S21)

In this case, even though
{\hat{X}}
is non-compact, large eigenvalues of
{\hat{X}}
, namely when
|x|\gg\sqrt{\hbar/(m{\omega})}
, are highly suppressed due to the quadratic potential. This effectively makes
{\hat{X}}
compact, which then regulates the logarithmic divergence.

Using the fact the energy spectrum of the SHO is given by
E_{n}=(n+\frac{1}{2})\hbar\omega
where
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, the thermal partition function for the SHO is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}Z_{\text{\tiny SHO}}({\beta})&\equiv{\text{Tr}}\big[e^{-{\beta}{\hat{H}}_{\text{\tiny SHO}}}\big]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\langle}n|e^{-{\beta}{\hat{H}}}|n{\rangle}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{{\beta}\hbar\omega(n+\frac{1}{2})}=\frac{e^{\frac{{\beta}\hbar\omega}{2}}}{e^{{\beta}\hbar\omega}-1}.\end{split}

(S22)

Having computed the partition function
Z_{\text{\tiny SHO}}({\beta})
, we can evaluate the mean and variance of the modular Hamiltonian using

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\langle}{\hat{K}}_{\text{\tiny SHO}}({\beta}){\rangle}&=(1-{\beta}\partial_{\beta})\ln Z_{\text{\tiny SHO}}({\beta})=\frac{{\beta}\hbar{\omega}}{e^{{\beta}\hbar{\omega}}-1}-\ln( 1 - e^{- \b\hbar\o} ),\\
{\langle}\Delta{\hat{K}}_{\text{\tiny SHO}}^{2}({\beta}){\rangle}&={\beta}^{2}\partial_{\beta}^{2}\ln Z_{\text{\tiny SHO}}({\beta})=\frac{({\beta}\hbar{\omega})^{2}e^{{\beta}\hbar{\omega}}}{(e^{{\beta}\hbar{\omega}}-1)^{2}}.\end{split}

(S23)

Notice that in the main text, the mean and variance of
{\hat{K}}_{s}
we computed is analogous to us taking
\omega\to 0
in Eq. (
S23
), as our
{\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{N}}
modes are at exactly zero energy and behave as free scalar fields. In that case, we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lim_{\omega\to 0}{\langle}{\hat{K}}_{\text{\tiny SHO}}({\beta}){\rangle}&=1-\ln(\b\hbar\w),\qquad\lim_{\omega\to 0}{\langle}\Delta{\hat{K}}_{\text{\tiny SHO}}^{2}({\beta}){\rangle}=1.\end{split}

(S24)

This is precisely the analog of Eqs. (
26
) and (
28
).

Thus far, we evaluated the mean and variance of the modular Hamiltonian of a single SHO in a thermal background. A typical field theory consists of a continuum of SHOs. For a non-interacting continuum of SHOs with frequency density
n({\omega})
, the mean and variance of the modular Hamiltonian are given by

\begin{split}{\langle}{\hat{K}}_{\text{\tiny SHO}}({\beta}){\rangle}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\text{d}}\omega\,n(\omega)\bigg[\frac{{\beta}\hbar{\omega}}{e^{{\beta}\hbar{\omega}}-1}-\ln( 1 - e^{- \b\hbar\o} )\bigg],\qquad{\langle}\Delta{\hat{K}}_{\text{\tiny SHO}}^{2}({\beta}){\rangle}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\text{d}}\omega\,n(\omega)\frac{({\beta}\hbar{\omega})^{2}e^{{\beta}\hbar{\omega}}}{(e^{{\beta}\hbar{\omega}}-1)^{2}}.\end{split}

(S25)

This follows from the fact that for non-interacting systems, the mean and variance of the total modular Hamiltonian is the sum of the mean and variance of the modular Hamiltonian of each system, respectively. For a free massive field theory in
D
dimensions, we have
n({\omega})=n_{0}\hbar{\omega}(\hbar^{2}{\omega}^{2}-m^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}(D-3)}{\theta}(\hbar{\omega}-m)
, where
\theta
is the Heaviside step function. In particular, notice that for massless fields in two dimensions,
n({\omega})=n_{0}
is independent of
{\omega}
, and so Eq. (
S25
) reduces to

\begin{split}{\langle}{\hat{K}}_{\text{\tiny SHO}}({\beta}){\rangle}&={\langle}\Delta{\hat{K}}_{\text{\tiny SHO}}^{2}({\beta}){\rangle}=\frac{\pi^{2}n_{0}}{3{\beta}\hbar}.\end{split}

(S26)
