Impact of Short-term Rehabilitation on Chronic Stress-induced Brain Alterations and Associated Cognitive Deficits
Authors: Shilpa Borehalli Mayegowda, B. S. Shankaranarayana Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPersistent stress is frequently comorbid with major depressive disorder and anxiety, leading to significant psychological and behavioural impairments. However, the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying stress reversal remain poorly understood. Although several reversal strategies, including pharmacological antidepressants, brain stimulation therapies and nutritional interventions, have been explored, their outcomes remain inconsistent.PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of rehabilitation as a non-pharmacological intervention for reversing stress-induced behavioural and neurobiological alterations.MethodsChronic stress was induced in rats, followed by short-term rehabilitation through standard housing conditions without additional physical or behavioural enrichment. Behavioural assessments, including learning and memory tests, the open field test (OFT) and the elevated plus maze (EPM), were conducted to evaluate cognitive, exploratory and anxiety-like behaviours. Morphological changes in specific brain regions were examined and correlated with molecular markers, including neurotrophic factors and astrocytic markers.ResultsShort-term sedentary rehabilitation failed to reverse stress-induced behavioural abnormalities and brain region alterations. Chronically stressed rats exhibited significant learning and memory deficits, reduced exploratory behaviour in the OFT, and altered anxiety-like behaviour in the EPM. These behavioural impairments were accompanied by morphological changes and dysregulation of neurotrophic and astrocytic markers, indicating insufficient neuroplastic recovery under sedentary rehabilitation conditions.ConclusionThe findings highlight the limited efficacy of short-term, non-enriched rehabilitation in reversing chronic stress-induced neurobehavioural alterations. The study underscores the importance of lifestyle-based therapeutic approaches, including behavioural, physical and relaxation therapies, for effective stress reversal. Interventions such as social support, exercise, enriched environments, and task-based learning—analogous to cognitive behavioural therapy—may offer greater neuroprotective and restorative benefits. These findings provide translational relevance for developing social rehabilitation strategies in clinical conditions associated with overwhelming stress and stress-related cognitive deficits.

Introduction

Chronic stress is a significant health concern, often leading to a depressive-like phenotype, major depressive disorder (MDD), anxiety or post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).1–3 Exposure to stressors of short duration is generally considered beneficial and more adaptive for regulating hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis feedback mechanisms than chronic or prolonged exposure to stressors. 4 Hence, chronic stress results in a spectrum of impairments, many of which are not understood clearly so far. Numerous studies have shown that metabolic abnormalities have been identified throughout the reward system in mood disorders.5, 6 Repeated stress is associated with depressive symptoms like anhedonia and behavioural despair, which are evident from behavioural assessments like sucrose preference and forced swim tests (FST), respectively. At the cellular level, chronic stress impairs hippocampal long-term potentiation (LTP) and facilitates long-term depression. 7 In a clinical scenario, MDD is associated with a spectrum of cognitive alterations like lack of concentration or attention deficits, 8 impaired decision-making, errors in working memory and verbal and visuospatial memories.9, 10 Many studies have focused on reversal modalities like pharmacological antidepressants and other non-pharmacological approaches, including voluntary exercise, enriched environment, intracranial self-stimulation and others. However, very little validation has been done so far about recovery after a stress regimen involving physiological and psychological responses. Understanding what happens to a stressed brain left undisturbed or provided with a recovery phase becomes crucial. Few have addressed this and found polymorphic results depending on different areas of the brain that interest them in their investigations.

A report from García et al. 11 demonstrated that immobilisation stress was very intense and showed an increase in corticosterone (CORT) levels compared to tail nick and restraint stress. Consequently, the immobilised animals in this study exhibited several abnormalities, such as cognitive impairment, volumetric modification and decreased neurotrophic factor expression, which were followed by post-stress events. Recovery following stress exposure can be considered a form of rehabilitation aimed at restoring physiological and behavioural functions. The speed of recovery from stress often depends on the intensity of the stressor, with more severe stressors causing greater disruption of homeostasis. Adaptation to prolonged stress, reflected in the process of allostasis, is mediated by the responsiveness of the HPA axis. 12 Hence, the severity of the stressor plays a crucial role in aggravating the deficits rather than the duration of the stressor. The present study reveals how immobilisation stress can damage the body’s physiological functioning and cause various impairments associated with cognitive decline in animals exposed to prolonged stress paradigms. We aim to shed light on these lacunae. We are the first to explain the importance of the recovery time interval. The recovery period may be influenced by factors such as the intensity of the stressor, previous experiences, and the effects of repeated stress exposure and reinforcement. We postulated that failure in post-stress recovery after 14 days may be due to a shorter recovery period. Also, 10 consecutive days of the prolonged stress protocol are too extreme, leading to extensive damage of the neuroanatomical regions with behavioural manifestations observed in the current study. Since immobilisation stress is considered to be the most severe form of physical as well as psychological stress, it showed drastic changes. Nevertheless, we have not done the complete follow-up after 14 days of post-stress interval to better understand HPA response beyond the actual recovery period, which remained insufficient. In contrast, our earlier findings showed reversal from chronic stress with an enriched environment, 3 and here we found insignificant amelioration in the sedentary rehabilitation regime. This signifies the importance of lifestyle improvement strategies like physical exercise, yoga, meditation, social interaction and counselling to recover from chronic stress-induced deficits and associated abnormalities.

Methods

Adult Wistar male rats weighing 200–220 g were obtained from the Central Animal Research Facility (CARF), NIMHANS, Bengaluru. Animals were housed in polypropylene cages (32 × 24 × 16 cm) and had access to food and water ad libitum in their home cage. An average of three rats were housed socially in a cage and maintained on a 12:12 h lighting environment with temperature (25 °C ± 2 °C) and humidity (50%–55%) until experiment onset. These animals were experimentally grouped into three groups: control (Con), chronic immobilisation stress (CIS), and CIS animals left in a home cage for post-stress recovery (Rec). All experiments were carried out per the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Mammals in Neuroscience and Behavioural Research (The National Academies Press, Washington, USA, 2003). The institutional animal ethics committee approved experimental protocols.

Stress induction: CIS is a restraint stressor in which animal movement is completely restricted in rodent immobilisation bags. These bags are made of polythene with only a small opening near the nostril for breathing. Immobilisation stress was carried out by subjecting animals to rodent immobilisation bags (2 h/day, 10 am–12 noon) without food or water access for 10 consecutive day. The experimental design and post-stress recovery protocol are illustrated in Figure 1. 3

Spatial Learning and Memory in the Partially Baited Radial Arm Maze Task

Experimentation was divided into habituation, acquisition and retention. After completing the stress protocol, spatial learning and memory in the radial arm maze (RAM) were assessed. All groups of animals were trained in a partially baited RAM for 16 days after 2 days of acclimatisation. The learning criterion was fixed during the training period at 80% correct choices. After 16 days of acquisition, a 10-day gap was given, and retention was assessed.3, 9, 10

Assessment of Behavioural Depressive-like Behaviour

Sucrose preference test (SPT): On day 1, animals were kept in separate housing throughout the training period; each animal had access to two bottles in the home cage, one containing 1% w/v sucrose solution and the other with regular drinking water. The amount of sucrose water consumed was multiplied by the total amount of liquid consumed to determine the sucrose preference score.3, 10

Sucrose preference test (SPT): On day 1, animals were kept in separate housing throughout the training period; each animal had access to two bottles in the home cage, one containing 1% w/v sucrose solution and the other with regular drinking water. The amount of sucrose water consumed was multiplied by the total amount of liquid consumed to determine the sucrose preference score.3, 10

FST: On day 2, the animals were made to swim for 5 min as part of a test, and their behaviour was recorded. Offline analyses were conducted using the coded recorded data by an observer blind to the treatments. The immobility parameter, which is described by a rat floating vertically and only moving slightly to keep its head above the water’s surface, was measured.3–10

Assessment of Anxiety-like Behaviour by Elevated Plus Maze

Anxiety-like behaviour was assessed using the elevated plus maze (EPM) as described previously (Mayegowda et al.). Each rat was given 5 min to explore the closed and open arms. A camera mounted on the roof captured every trial on film, and in between sessions, 70% alcohol was used to clean the maze. Videos were scored based on how much time was spent in open and closed arms, open and full arm entries, vertical rearing, and head dips.9, 10

Evaluation of Locomotor and Exploratory Behaviour Using the Open Field Test

The open field test (OFT) was used to evaluate exploratory behaviour following the previously described protocol (Mayegowda et al.). Rats chosen at random from each group were positioned in a corner of the arena that faced the centre. The behaviour was recorded for 5 min and analysed using the tracking software Noldus Ethovision XT (Noldus, Wageningen, The Netherlands). Rats were tested just once, and the arena was cleaned with 70% ethanol following each testing session. The total distance travelled and the number of squares crossed were counted.3, 9, 10

Estimation of Biomarkers for Neuroplasticity, Astroglial and Angiogenic Markers (Brain-derived Neurotrophic Factor, Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein and Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor) by Western Blotting

Western blotting was carried out as previously reported by Mayegowda et al. and Shilpa et al. The micro-dissected brain regions (frontal cortex, hippocampus and amygadlar complex) were homogenised in ice-cold lysis solution containing protease inhibitors and then centrifuged to obtain the protein extract. Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated by sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), followed by blotting experiments. Following the transfer, the blots were incubated in a blocking solution. The primary antibodies against brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) (1:500), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) (1:500), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) (1:1,000) and anti-β-actin (1:2,000) were followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Immunoreactive bands were visualised by chemiluminescence or 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining and documented using a gel documentation system. Protein expression was quantified by densitometric analysis using ImageJ software, with β-actin serving as the internal loading control.3, 10

Volumetric Analysis of the Basolateral Amygdala, Hippocampus and Dentate Gyrus

Experimental animals were sacrificed under halothane anaesthesia, followed by transcardial perfusion with ice-cold saline and 10% (v/v) formaldehyde. Brains were post-fixed, and 40 µm coronal sections were obtained using a vibratome. Serial sections were stained with cresyl violet (Nissl stain), and the dentate gyrus (DG), hippocampus and basolateral amygdala (BLA) were identified according to the Paxinos and Watson rat brain atlas. Regional brain volumes were estimated using the unbiased stereological Cavalieri method with Stereo Investigator software, following the protocols described previously.3, 9, 10

Statistical Evaluation

GraphPad Prism 5 was used for the statistical analyses. In RAM, group differences over many days were evaluated using a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Bonferroni’s post hoc test. Significant group differences among the OFT, EPM, SPT, FST, neurotrophic factors and volume data were analysed using one-way ANOVA. Further group differences were then compared using Tukey’s post hoc test. A p value of .05 or less was considered statistically significant.

Results

Physiological changes: Immobilisation stress induction was confirmed by measuring variations in the body, adrenal and spleen weights. The body weight of animals was recorded at the beginning and completion of the stress procedure (0 and 11th day). At the end of the stress protocol, the adrenal gland and spleen weights were recorded in control, CIS and post-stressed rats. We observed a significant decrease in body weight in stressed animals compared to standard controls. Surprisingly, after 10 days of CIS, animals kept for rehabilitation in a standard cage showed a considerable increase in body weight (F3,48 = 202.1, p < .001; Figure 2A(a)). Also, stressed and recovery animals showed hypertrophy of the adrenal glands and spleen weights (F3,15 = 45.34 and F3,15 = 13.31; p < .001; Figure 2A(b and c)). Hence, these findings confirm the stress induction in the animals subjected to immobilisation for 2 h for 10 consecutive days.

Depressive-like Behavioural Phenotypes

SPT: Animals subjected to a post-stress rest period for 14 days were assessed for behavioural depression using the SPT. These animals showed less sucrose preference than control animals, confirming anhedonia, a depressive-like symptom (F3,37 = 23.39, p < .001; Figure 2B(a)). Short-term standard housing did not reverse these deficits.

Anxiety-like Behaviour in the Elevated Plus Maze

The results of the present study show that the percentage of time spent by post-stress rest period animals in the open arm and central arena (data not shown) was significantly decreased compared to controls. These animals spent considerably more time in the closed arms, exhibiting anxiety-like behaviour. The frequency of entries into the open and closed arms and the central area was recorded as zone transitions. Short-term rehabilitation of depressive-like animals had a greater number of closed arm transitions (F3,39 = 13.74, p < .001; Figure 3(a)) and displayed fewer open arm transitions, demonstrating elevated anxiety (F3,39 = 41.01, p < .001; Figure 3(b)). Total arm transitions (F3,39 = 22.24, p < .001; Figure 3(c)) of CIS animals were reduced, exhibiting decreased exploratory behaviour.

In the current study, we scored other ethological activities like defecation, vertical rearing and head dips for anxiolytic and exploratory behaviour, respectively. We observed that CIS and recovery animals had more egesta pellets than the control group, indicating enhanced anxiety-like behaviour (F3,39 = 6.709, p < .01; Figure 3(d)). The numbers of vertical rearings and head dips were reduced in rehabilitated rats; however, these changes were not significantly different from those observed in stressed rats (F3,39 = 25.35 and F3,39 = 38.31, p < .001; Figure 3(e and f), respectively), indicating increased anxiety and decreased exploratory plus locomotory behaviour in these animals. Stressed animals kept for a short rest phase showed behaviour analogous to chronically stressed rats. Hence, remedial therapy for a shorter period did not influence anxiety and exploratory behaviour.

Locomotory and Exploratory Behaviour in the Open Field Test

The OFT is generally used to measure anxiety-like behaviour and other etiological features such as exploration and locomotory activity. OFT is based on a conflict between an animal’s motivation to explore or to avoid a novel environment. Our results from the Ethovision track (Figure 4A) depict that post-stress rehabilitated animals had diminished total pathlength after keeping in a recovery period (F3,50 = 7.774, p < .01; Figure 4B(c)). These post-recovery depressive-like animals hardly spent time in the central arena, symbolising an anxiety feature (F3,50 = 7.810, p < .01; Figure 4B(a)). Fewer zone transitions (F3,50 = 16.08, p < .001; Figure 4B(b)) were seen in the recovery group from the peripheral arena to the centre, similar to stressed rats.

Short-term Recovery Period Perseveres Stress-induced Impairment in Acquisition of Spatial Learning in Partially Baited Radial Arm Maze Task

During the learning period, control animals attained more than 80% of correct choices by the last day of training, whereas CIS and rehabilitated animals exhibited poor learning; they failed to reach 80% correct choices even after 16 days of training (60.1%), and these groups showed a plateaued learning curve from the 4th block until the end of acquisition. CIS and post-CIS standard housing animals made more reference memory errors (RMEs). The number of RMEs started declining after the initial training sessions in normal animals, whereas the Rec group was not significantly different from the CIS group (Figure 5A). Any re-entries to baited arms were considered working memory errors (WMEs). The WMEs are of two types: working memory error correct (WMEC; re-entries to baited arms) and incorrect (WMEIC; re-entries to unbaited arms). Interestingly, we did not observe any changes in both WMEC and WMEIC between all three groups.

In the retention test, learning impairment persisted in depressive-like animals and similar results were also seen in animals housed in standard housing for 10 days after CIS. Both groups of animals made more errors in the retention test and had fewer correct choices. At the same time, normal animals could retain learnt information and make more accurate choices in the retention task. The reference memory impairment in rehabilitated animals persisted in the retention trials. However, the working memory was unaffected (data not shown). These results indicated that CIS-induced spatial learning and memory impairments could not be reversed or improved by a shorter recovery period.

Expression of Brain-derived Neurotrophic Factor, Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein and Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor Levels in Different Brain Areas

BDNF: We found that BDNF levels were decreased in the hippocampus of stressed (depressive-like) and rehabilitated animals. Although BDNF expression appeared slightly reduced in the frontal cortex of stressed animals, statistical analysis revealed no significance among all groups. Interestingly, in the amygdalar complex, the level of BDNF was upregulated more than fourfold in stressed rats and was the same in post-CIS standard housing. We observed that chronic stress has a contrasting effect on BDNF expression in the hippocampus and amygdala (Figure 6A).

VEGF: We also studied the expression of VEGF in different brain regions. We observed a decrease in the expression of VEGF in the hippocampus and frontal cortex of stressed animals. Contrastingly, VEGF was overexpressed (<4-fold) in the amygdalar complex of chronically stressed animals (Figure 6B). Hence, chronic stress resulted in differential expression of neurotrophic factors across various anatomical regions of the brain. However, this dimorphic expression of the vasculature marker was not normalised by short-term rehabilitation.

GFAP: The effect of astrogliosis on stress-induced disorders was indirectly measured by GFAP expression in different brain areas. We observed CIS-induced dichotomy in the astrocytic cytoskeletal protein expression pattern in different brain areas. GFAP was found to be significantly decreased in the hippocampus and the frontal cortex, followed by CIS, and was normalised by short-term rehabilitation. In contrast, overexpressed GFAP persisted even after rehabilitation in the amygdala complex (Figure 7).

Post-stress Period-induced Volumetric Changes in the Different Brain Areas

Animals subjected to chronic immobilisation stress and subsequently maintained under standard housing conditions for 14 days showed persistent alterations in brain structure and behaviour. CIS animals showed significantly reduced DG and total hippocampal volumes comparable to normal animals. Compared to DG and hippocampus, a contrasting hypertrophy of the BLA was observed, with an increase of about 37% and 25% in CIS and rehabilitants, respectively (Figure 8).

Discussion

Our current article is the comparative finding of the earlier manuscript. 3 Here, we aim to understand the rehabilitation regime, one of the most effective stress management techniques. Conversely, little research has been conducted concerning recovering from stress-induced deficits apart from knowing their effects. We attempted to draw a correlation for the concept of rehabilitation, commonly known as the recovery period in animal studies. We also tried to summarise a few ideas for undoing the harmfulness of stress and regaining the lost homeostasis during the immobilisation stress protocol. Chronically stressed animals subjected to shorter recovery intervals showed increased body weight without any change in the weight of the adrenal gland or spleen, 14 a typical symptom observed in Cushing’s syndrome due to hypersecretion of cortisol. 15 The rapid weight gain could be due to the hyperactivity of the HPA axis, causing the uncontrolled release of cortisol from adrenal glands to produce higher cortisol levels.15–17 It has also been shown that despite adaptation to stress, the weight of the adrenal glands was increased, which in turn may indicate the demand on the ability of the adrenals to secrete even higher amounts of CORT.18, 19 Repeated exposure to stress reduces liver glycogen and plasma insulin by elevating free glucose in the bloodstream.20, 21

Reduced glucose metabolism due to lowered insulin levels22, 23 and increased sympathetic activity contribute to a reduction in body weight in chronically stressed animals. A recent study shows elevated CORT restoration in the chronic unpredictable stress (CUS) animal model after 355 days of recovery. 24 In parallel, chronic stress reduces negative feedback regulation of cortisol secretion in animal models and non-human primates. 25 Stress may lead to the impairment of LTP, a cellular mechanism underlying learning and memory. 26 Heightened anxiety-like behaviour and increased secretion of CORT, indexed by hypertrophy of the adrenal gland, a hallmark feature of stress induction and later short-term rehabilitation, remained unsuccessful in amending. Hyper-CORT secretion resulted in impairment and caused substantial loss of mossy fibre terminal morphology by the reduction of synapses. However, these modifications were moderately reversed after rehabilitation from stress or CORT treatment. 27 A study exhibited an improvement in learning after 21 days of the post-immobilisation period, with remarkable structural reorganisation in hippocampal subfields correlating with commensurate progression in behaviour. In the present study, the Rec group was undisturbed in their home cage for 14 days after the stress treatment, which showed learning impairment. In contrast, stressed rats following recovery for only 10 days showed remodelling of CA3 dendrites, and spatial memory was undamaged in the restraint stress model.28, 29

A study done for restraint stress showed that impairment in avoidance learning was greater in males than in females. After 21 days of recovery, these animals could reverse appetitive learning in bar press for food reward and aversive learning tasks. Females were faster learners than males. 30 Also, cognitive disability in the Morris water maze was reversed entirely after post-stress rehabilitation. 31 Also, females are more susceptible to recovery than males. 32 Additionally, brain areas which are very much required for learning and memory, that is, DG and hippocampal volume, were found to be smaller in depressive-like animals. 33 Analogously, our investigation explains the significant reduction of DG volume and a contrasting increase in the BLA volume in depressive-like animals. These findings contrast with earlier structural studies, which revealed decreased spine density and dendritic atrophy of apical dendrites in the subiculum of depressed subjects. 34 The cognitive deficits observed may be attributed to several stress-induced variations, including structural changes in the DG and hippocampus, including reduced expression of GFAP and neurotrophic factors witnessed in the current study. 35 Another study showed increased glial-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) in the hippocampus, followed by 40 days of recovery. An increase in GDNF level suggests activation of astrocytes, which were initially damaged by CUS. 31 Likewise, depressed patients and animal models of depression also showed a decrease in the density of glial cells in cortical regions and reduced levels of GFAP in cortical and limbic structures in imaging studies.36, 37 In concurrence with the present findings, a few studies also suggest the role of astrocytic abnormalities in the development of depressive symptoms, with low GFAP levels in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (PFC), 38 orbitofrontal and anterior cingulate cortex 39 and hippocampus, precipitating depressive symptoms. 40

Accordingly, we hypothesise that reduced BDNF expression in the hippocampus and frontal cortex observed in these findings could be linked to depressive-like behaviour. In this regard, a study by Jha et al. 41 showed that mutant promoter IV, which drives the expression of BDNF, a major neuronal growth factor, may be implicated in the pathophysiology of MDD. It was seen in many studies that hippocampal volumes were affected due to significant atrophy in patients with multiple depressive episodes, 42 while others have reported no changes. 43 Reduction in BDNF expression during recovery time may be the main causative factor for impaired spatial learning and memory and may not be beneficial compared to physical exercise, which triggers plasticity.3, 44 Others have shown long-term recuperation for 21 days after stress normalised CORT levels and cognitive deficit betterment, but failed to restore BDNF levels. 45 A study by Hoffman et al. 46 showed CA3 apical dendritic retraction recovery several weeks after chronic stress, and malfunctioning of glutamatergic transmission may lead to diminished learning and memory, which has been seen in animal models of depression as well as in the human post-mortem brain of MDD. 47

When stressed animals were given an opportunity for a more extended recovery phase, they showed good amelioration in the Barnes maze task, with normalisation of AMPA receptor transmission in the hippocampus through GluA-1, 2 and 3 receptor subunits. 48 Downregulated CREB/pCREB expression in DG and PFC was not re-established in females even after 21 days of post-stress rest in a standard cage. Instead, social housing was beneficial in coping with chronic stress-induced changes. 49 Persistent lowering of hippocampal volumes and BDNF expression may relate to impaired performance in the RAM task. Results may demonstrate that the shorter rehabilitation regime given to CIS animals had no reversal role in volumetric alterations in the hippocampus and amygdalar region, alterations in trophic factor support and glial expression.

Studies have shown that increased BDNF levels 50 play an essential role in strengthening anxiety-like behaviours due to increased spinogenesis in the BLA.3, 10, 51, 52 Increasing the trophic factor and glial marker may enhance LTP and progressive plasticity in pyramidal neurons. Enhanced potentiation may be facilitated by increased BDNF levels in the amygdalar complex, leading to the formation of strong fear memories displayed as increased anxiety-like behaviour and reduced exploration, which could be due to spinogenesis in the BLA.3, 52, 53 The electrophysiological study showed enhanced LTP and NMDA receptor (NMDAR)-mediated synaptic response in principal neurons of the lateral nucleus following CIS.54, 55 Still, this will specifically target the relationship between the hippocampus and amygdala in learning, as this is considered to be a severe form of stress, exhibiting extensive hypertrophy and heightened anxiety after a post-stress period. 56

Chronic stress causes dendritic atrophy in pyramidal neurons of the hippocampus and PFC, and in contrast, it can elicit dendritic hypertrophy in the BLA.13, 57 A study by Vyas and Chattarji 58 reported that hippocampal atrophy was reversible after withdrawal of stress for 21 days; this kind of reversal was not observed in the present study (Rec). Interestingly, the existing report matches the earlier findings, showing sustained hypertrophy resulted in an unusual enhancement of anxiety-like behaviour after re-exposure to another anxiety test.13, 58 Several animal models have shown that chronic stress may precipitate MDD and/or anxiety-like behavioural responses by increasing excitatory tone on glutamatergic neurons. 59 Although studies demonstrate that amygdala hyper-responsiveness is the key underlying factor in psychiatric disorders. Still, no clinical investigation exists to explain the role of glutamatergic projection neurons or γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)ergic interneurons and glial cells seen in animal models. Our results showed increased levels of neurotrophic factors in the amygdala after chronic stress, which may contribute to the formation of fear memories via increasing structural plasticity in the amygdala. We suspect that the hypertrophy in the amygdala after the recovery period could be due to sustained expression of BDNF and VEGF nourishing the extensive growth in amygdalar regions, causing elevated anxiety with reduced exploratory drive.3, 58–61 Long-lasting glucocorticoids and their receptor anomalies, along with increased amygdalar volumes, are also seen in depressed and PTSD patients, and there might be a strong relationship between amygdalar structural plasticity and anxiety62, 63 and stress-induced amygdalar hypertrophy was correlated with enhanced anxiety-like behaviour as evidenced in many MDD and PTSD conditions.10, 35, 63 Additionally, we currently found a strong correlation between the volumes of the hippocampus and levels of BDNF in hippocampal-dependent spatial learning and memory. Reduction in the levels of BDNF was associated with hippocampal atrophy and hypertrophy in amygdala. 64 Chronic stress is highly susceptible to vascular damage, and recovery is extremely slow. 65 Hence, a short rehabilitation period may be insufficient to normalise VEGF expression observed in our study. Previous studies have shown that recovery of vascular-related changes is slower than other cellular changes, suggesting that VEGF-mediated neurovascular remodelling may require a longer recovery period with the involvement of the brain’s blood vessels and their supporting cells. This implies that long-term treatment interventions, such as behavioural improvement therapies, may be necessary to overcome stress insult.66, 67 All these might contribute to increased anxiety, reduced exploration and cognitive deficits in depressed animals. Collectively, all these alterations induced by chronic stress often dampen homeostasis and may precipitate several affective disorders with cognition.

Conclusion

Chronic stress is a significant health concern, often leading to anxiety, depression and cognitive impairments. While short-term stress can be adaptive, prolonged exposure disrupts the body’s homeostasis, impacting brain function and emotional regulation. Studies have highlighted metabolic abnormalities and structural changes in the brain due to chronic stress, particularly in the hippocampus. Recovery from stress varies based on intensity, duration and individual resilience. Our study emphasises the role of recovery periods and lifestyle interventions in mitigating stress-induced damage. Findings suggest that rehabilitation strategies like exercise, meditation and social interaction play a crucial role. However, further research is needed to explore long-term recovery mechanisms beyond brief post-stress intervals. Understanding stress recovery can guide effective therapeutic interventions for mental health.
