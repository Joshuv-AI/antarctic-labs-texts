NeoGuider: neoepitope prediction using advanced feature engineering
Authors: Xiaofei Zhao, Lei Wei, Zhen Xie, Xuegong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe design of neoepitope-based cancer immunotherapy requires predicting the immunogenicity of each patient-specific neoepitope candidate. We designed NeoGuider, a machine-learning model to address nonlinearity and class imbalance in such prediction. Central to NeoGuider is a supervised feature-transformation approach, which estimates odds using our custom kernel density estimation followed by centered isotonic regression. We implemented NeoGuider as a bioinformatics pipeline, which detects neoepitope candidates from sequencing data and prioritizes such candidates by predicted immunogenicity. Benchmarking on 7 cohorts, 113 patients, and 635 immunogenic candidates showed that NeoGuider outperformed existing methods in neoepitope prediction. NeoGuider is open-source at https://github.com/XuegongLab/neoguider.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13073-025-01592-9.Keywords: Cancer immunotherapy, Neoantigen, Neoepitope, Next-generation sequencing, Feature engineering, Nonlinearity, Class imbalance

Background

Cancer cells harbor genetic mutations that can generate aberrant proteins. The cleavage of these proteins generates aberrant peptides. Some of these peptides can be presented by major histocompatibility complexes (MHCs) onto the surface of cancer cells and then be recognized by the host immune system. Upon recognition, the immune system is likely to kill these cancer cells presenting the aberrant peptides. Aberrant proteins that generate the presented and recognized peptides are called neoantigens, and such generated peptides are known as neoepitopes. Neoepitopes can stimulate the host immune system to kill cancer cells, but this stimulation is often insufficient, resulting in cancer progression. Neoepitope-based immunotherapy can be designed to enhance this immune response. Examples of such immunotherapies include cancer vaccines and adoptive T cell therapy that use neoepitopes to induce the growth and proliferation of tumor-killing T cells in vivo and in vitro, respectively [1].

Neoepitopes are patient-specific, so a personalized design of neoepitope-based immunotherapy is needed for each patient. This personalized approach requires the computational prediction of neoepitopes from patient-specific sequencing data, typically including whole-exome sequencing (WES) data of tumor and normal tissues as well as RNA-seq data of tumor tissues. A typical neoepitope prediction pipeline detects patient-specific neoepitope candidates represented as peptide-MHC pairs from the sequencing data, extracts non-patient-specific features from these candidates, and uses these features to prioritize the detected candidates. Prioritization involves ranking immunogenic candidates (characterized by immunogenicity) ahead of the non-immunogenic candidates. Immunogenicity refers to the ability to provoke an immune response. Neoepitopes that are recognized by their host immune system are likely to be immunogenic.

Many immunogenicity-related features have been discovered and studied (e.g., [2–5]). For example, in 1994 and 2014, MHC-bound peptide (pMHC) binding affinity and stability were reported to be strongly related to immunogenicity, respectively [2, 3]. Such features are typically predicted or estimated by various computational tools. For example, netMHCpan [6] predicts pMHC binding affinity, and Kallisto [7] estimates transcript abundance (i.e., a correlate of the quantity of potentially immunogenic peptides).

Some studies have combined these features to prioritize neoepitope candidates (e.g., [4, 5, 8]). For example, in 2016, Rasmussen et al. studied the effect of using both pMHC binding affinity and stability on the prioritization [8]. In 2020, the Tumor Antigen Selection Alliance (TESLA) studied the performances of methods from 25 anonymous teams in detecting and prioritizing candidates from 14 features [4]. In 2023, Müller et al. studied combinations of many machine-learning techniques for prioritizing candidates from 31 features [5]. These studies showed limited performance in neoepitope prioritization of existing methods, underscoring the difficulty of this task.

By systematically analyzing the characteristics of immunogenicity-related features, we identified two key challenges that impede the prioritization of neoepitope candidates. The first challenge is nonlinearity: Correlations between immunogenicity and the related features cannot be adequately modeled by a generalized linear model (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). The second challenge is class imbalance: Even large discovery cohorts generate only a small number of positive training examples (approximately 100 immunogenic neoepitope candidates [5]), so a high-variance model overfits to these positive examples.

To address these challenges, we developed a novel piecewise-linear, monotonic, and hyperparameter-free feature transformation. This transformation utilizes our custom-designed adaptive kernel density estimation (KDE) [9] to capture nonlinear correlations with high variance, standard isotonic regression (IR) [10, 11] to reduce the overfitting caused by such high variance, and centered isotonic regression (centered IR, CIR) [12] to further mitigate the overfitting. Fundamentally, this transformation maps each raw feature value of a neoepitope candidate into immunogenicity log odds, which can then be inputted to conventional machine-learning classifiers (e.g., logistic regression, the generalized linear model for binary classification) to predict immunogenicity. Our feature transformation, when integrated with a generalized linear model, is also interpretable compared with techniques that are more black-box (e.g., random forest), offering an additional advantage in precision medicine [13, 14]. To the best of our knowledge, neither KDE nor IR has been used for neoepitope prediction in the past.

We utilized our feature transformation to develop NeoGuider—our neoepitope prediction method implemented as a bioinformatics pipeline. NeoGuider can detect neoepitope candidates from user-provided sequencing data, can extract immunogenicity-related features from such NeoGuider-detected or user-provided candidates, and can prioritize these candidates using such NeoGuider-extracted or user-provided features. NeoGuider demonstrates superior neoepitope prediction performance compared with the state-of-the-art methods on multiple test datasets corresponding to validation cohorts.

Methods

Default features used for prioritization

NeoGuider uses, by default, the set F of 5 immunogenicity-related features:

These 5 features respectively represent the binding strength, binding affinity (i.e., IC50, with high IC50 corresponding to low affinity), binding stability, quantity, and TCR-recognition potential of pMHCs, respectively. The TESLA study recommended the features ICfiftyBA, BindStab, NeoAbundance, and Agretop in 2020 [4]. After the completion of the study, Morten Nielsen’s research group, which had proposed the features ICfiftyBA and BindStab, proposed ScoreEL as a new key feature in 2020 [6]. These features are described in more details in the Supplementary Methods (Additional file 2). NeoGuider uses these 5 features by default, but can also use any user-provided features to prioritize neoepitope candidates.

Interestingly, we observed that the feature ScoreEL outperforms %RankEL, its normalized version, on the National Cancer Institute (NCI)-train discovery cohort. To rigorously confirm this counterintuitive observation, we performed a two-sided Wilcoxon test of the ranking scores computed by the formula proposed by Müller et al. [5], where each patient is a statistical sample. The test shows that ScoreEL outperforms %RankEL on this discovery cohort for ranking neoepitope candidates (p-value: 0.040). Hence, NeoGuider uses by default ScoreEL instead of %RankEL.

Feature selection influences the interpretability and performance of our model. Thus, we assessed the ability of a single feature to distinguish between positive and negative neoepitope candidates. The features that we selected should be both anomaly-free and informative to provide interpretability and performance, respectively.

Prioritization using feature transformation

The prioritization of neoepitope candidates can be abstracted as a binary-classification problem characterized by nonlinearity and class imbalance. In the context of this problem, odds and log odds refer to the ratio and logarithm of the ratio of the density of positive examples (immunogenic neoepitope candidates) to the density of negative examples (non-immunogenic neoepitope candidates), respectively. Each neoepitope candidate is characterized by the set F of features related to the probability of the candidate being immunogenic. Thus, we can design a machine-learning model to predict the probability , odds , and log odds of observing immunogenicity for the candidate.

The NeoGuider feature transformation first estimates the log odds based on the nonlinearly correlated features (Fig. 1). At the feature value of each training example, NeoGuider uses our custom-designed version of adaptive kernel density estimation (aKDE) to address nonlinearity. Odds refer to the ratio of the density of positive examples to the density of negative examples, so aKDE uses the same kernel to jointly estimate these two densities. The kernel bandwidth of aKDE is designed to dynamically adapt to the distance between consecutive positive examples in the domain of the function that represents the feature (i.e., examples are sorted by their feature values, and two positive examples are consecutive if the open interval spanned by their feature values does not contain any feature values from other positive examples). This novel design of kernel accounts for the highly variable density of positive examples associated with class imbalance. However, class imbalance still results in redundant applications of aKDE on negative examples. Hence, NeoGuider merges all negative examples that are between two consecutive positive examples, in the domain of the feature-representing function, into one representative example, with its weight equaling to the number of merged examples. Then, NeoGuider applies aKDE to only the representative example instead of the consecutive negative examples.

The aKDE-estimated log odds are computed from very few positive examples, so the estimation is affected by high variance (i.e., source of overfitting). To reduce this variance, NeoGuider iteratively refines the kernel bandwidth to avoid undersmoothing and then uses standard IR to ensure monotonicity. IR minimizes the mean squared error (MSE) of a predictor (which maps feature values to log odds in the context of neoepitope prediction) constrained to be monotonically either increasing or decreasing. We empirically observed that this monotonicity constraint is reasonable (Additional file 1: Fig. S1), supporting the use of standard IR.

The IR-estimated log odds can remain constant as the feature value varies, indicating that IR still overfits (Fig. 1). NeoGuider uses centered IR (CIR) [12] to mitigate this overfitting. CIR first averages the design point (i.e., feature) values characterized by identical response values (i.e., log odds estimated by standard IR) and then performs piecewise-linear interpolation on the averaged values [12].

Once different CIR-estimated log odds values are correspondingly generated from different features, the last step is to predict one immunogenicity probability by summarizing these individual estimates. In this step, NeoGuider uses logistic regression to predict such probabilities, which are then sorted in descending order to rank (i.e., prioritize) the candidates. Once trained, NeoGuider transforms features and predicts immunogenicity probabilities without further access to the training data. The prioritization of neoepitope candidates is described in detail in the Supplementary Methods (Additional file 2).

Some features, such as the pMHC binding affinities and strengths estimated by netMHCpan, are highly correlated. Our feature transformation, implemented in NeoGuider, transforms highly correlated features in similar ways (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). The NeoGuider-used logistic regression, which is L2 norm–regularized, is also unaffected by highly correlated features. Thus, this high correlation is not an issue for NeoGuider.

Our feature transformation is monotonic and therefore does not change the ranking of neoepitope candidates that are sorted (i.e., prioritized) by a single feature. Thus, the transformation does not change the single feature–based prioritization results.

Methods for feature importance analysis

We implemented in NeoGuider the functionality to perform single and ensemble feature-importance analyses, which estimate the importance of a given feature when ignoring and considering other features, respectively. Single feature-importance analysis estimates the expected increase or decrease in log odds after observing the value of a single feature by averaging the absolute values of prevalence-normalized log odds. Ensemble feature-importance analysis calculates the product of this average with the logistic regression coefficient of the corresponding transformed feature, so this product denotes the expected log odds contributed by the feature to the logistic regression. By default, NeoGuider uses the ensemble analysis to estimate feature importance.

Our feature transformation transforms raw feature values into log odds. Hence, the logistic regression coefficient of a transformed feature represents the importance of the feature-associated log odds, which is not raw feature value, relative to the log odds associated with other features. Thus, the logistic regression coefficients for transformed features do not have the typical meaning of such coefficients for raw features.

To visualize the relationships between different features, we generated a pair plot of the log odds that our feature transformation estimated for these features (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). The pair plot shows the distributions of positive and negative examples in the space spanned by each pair of features. In each subplot of the pair plot, the representative values of the corresponding feature are annotated on the top/right and bottom/left axes, displaying the raw and transformed feature values, respectively. Additional details on feature importance analysis are provided in the Supplementary Methods (Additional file 2).

Detection and prioritization implemented as a computational pipeline

We used the Snakemake workflow language to implement the NeoGuider pipeline, based on the workflow of vaccine design and the biological mechanism of immunogenicity (Fig. 1). The pipeline uses BWA MEM [15] and STAR [16] to align DNA-seq (e.g., WES) and RNA-seq reads to the GRCh37 human reference genome from the Trinity Cancer Transcriptome Analysis Toolkit (CTAT) [17], respectively. Next, the pipeline uses UVC to call (i.e., identify) tumor-specific small variants (i.e., somatic single-nucleotide variants and insertion-deletions) from paired tumor and normal DNA-seq data. Then, we use a customized Python script to translate, for each small variant, the REF and ALT genotypes characterized by the absence and presence of the small variant into wild-type and mutant peptides, respectively, because the mutant peptide can potentially be a neoepitope (Fig. 1). Concurrently, we use Kallisto [7] to estimate the abundance of each RNA transcript because this abundance is correlated with immunogenicity, and we use OptiType on the tumor RNA-seq data to perform human-leukocyte antigen (HLA) typing, determining the types of MHC molecules that are found in antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and tumor cells (Fig. 1).

Peptide generation, RNA-transcript abundance estimation, and HLA-typing collectively result in the generation of a neoepitope-candidate FASTA file containing combinations of peptide, MHC, and transcript-per-million. For each neoepitope candidate represented as a combination of peptide and MHC, NeoGuider uses netMHCpan [6] and netMHCstabpan [8] to predict the strength/affinity and stability of the binding between the peptide and MHC to form a pMHC, respectively, because pMHCs are presented by APCs and cancer cells. Finally, the pipeline extracts immunogenicity-related features from neoepitope candidates and uses such features to prioritize and classify these candidates. The implementation of the NeoGuider pipeline is described in detail in the Supplementary Methods (Additional file 2).

The pipeline can also run STAR-Fusion [18] and ASNEO [19] on tumor RNA-seq data to generate neoepitope candidates from fusion and splicing variants, respectively. However, validation of immunogenicity and report of clinical benefit for fusion and splicing variants are both lacking in the literature [20]. Therefore, while the generation of neoepitope candidates from fusion and splicing variants is included as an optional step in the pipeline, it is not described in detail.

Similarly, the pipeline can also run MixCR [21] on tumor RNA-seq data to detect T-cell receptor (TCR) clonotypes and ERGO-II [22] to predict, for each clonotype, the neoepitope candidates that can be recognized by the clonotype to induce an immunogenic attack by T cells. However, neoepitope-candidate prioritization with respect to each specific TCR clonotype has been observed to perform much worse than the prioritization with respect to the ensemble of all TCR clonotypes, presumably because TCR clonotypes are too diverse to be captured by a typical sequencing assay [23] and because the interaction between TCR and pMHC is hard to predict. These interactions are especially difficult to predict for peptides that are absent in databases cataloging pMHC-TCR interactions, and such peptides are from the majority of all neoepitopes [24, 25]. Thus, TCR-specific prioritization is incorporated as an optional step in the pipeline but is not described in detail.

Currently, the use of MHC class II (MHC-II) in the design of personalized cancer immunotherapy has not been explored yet [26]. Additionally, studies of MHC-II immunogenicity features have been limited [27]. Hence, MHC-II is not considered in the NeoGuider pipeline: Only MHC class I (MHC-I) is considered.

We specified versions for all third-party tools (netMHCpan, OptiType, etc.) used by our NeoGuider pipeline via Bioconda. We additionally provided a Docker image for full reproducibility.

Benchmarking NeoGuider against the states of the art using previously published protocols

To ensure consistent and fair comparisons, we benchmarked NeoGuider against state-of-the-art methods strictly following the evaluation protocols originally used by the authors of each method (Table 1 and Additional file 1: Fig. S3). The protocol consists of combinations of input types, tasks, discovery and validation cohorts corresponding to the training and test datasets (Table 2), hyperparameter-tuning strategies that are applicable to the method, feature sets, competing methods, and performance metrics. Because these reference protocols differ from method to method, we designed NeoGuider to support a wide range of input types so that all previously published protocols could, in principle, be reproduced. However, most existing methods are compatible with only a limited subset of input types (Table 1), and some (e.g., the 25 TESLA teams) have published results without releasing models or code [4]. Therefore, it is impossible to conduct exhaustive pairwise comparisons across all datasets and all prediction tools.

In designing our benchmark, we took particular care to avoid data leakage and confounding factors. Training and test datasets may share some neoepitope candidates; thus, we verified that immunogenic candidates found in a training dataset are absent in the corresponding test datasets (unless previous studies reported that these candidates come from different patients). Because HLA allotypes can affect neoepitope-prediction performance, we examined HLA distributions in all training and test cohorts. The allotypes were diverse in both sets (Additional file 1: Figs. S4 and S5). We further assessed whether prediction performance depends on specific HLA allotypes and found no such discernable dependency (Additional file 1: Fig. S6), consistent with previous observations [4].

The performance metrics listed in Table 1 cannot be used to evaluate tools that require human intervention to prioritize neoepitope candidates. Therefore, we did not evaluate this type of tools such as pVAC-seq [43], NeoANT-HILL [44], or NeoFox [45]. Such tools do not have the ability to prioritize neoepitope candidates, presumably because their design philosophy is to annotate each candidate with many features and to expect users to use these features along with their domain knowledge to manually prioritize the candidates. For example, users can manually remove all candidates with low predicted pMHC binding affinity, which corresponds to high half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50), because candidates with low binding affinity are less likely to be immunogenic.

In brief, for each task, we compared NeoGuider with the task-specific state-of-the-art methods. For the task of detecting and prioritizing neoepitope candidates (i.e., predicting neoepitopes from sequencing data), we compared NeoGuider with the TESLA [4] teams. For the task of prioritizing pre-detected candidates, we compared NeoGuider with MuPeXI [30], DeepHLApan [31], MixMHCpred [39], PRIME [39], netMHCpan [6], netMHCstabpan [8], and NeoRanking [5] built-in methods. For the task of improving the performance of machine-learning models in prioritizing pre-detected candidates, we compared NeoGuider with all scikit-learn feature transformations [32], which are commonly used in machine learning. More details about the design of our benchmark can be found in the Supplementary Methods (Additional file 2).

Results

Detection and prioritization of neoepitope candidates

We evaluated NeoGuider by using the data and scoring metrics that TESLA [4] used. TESLA published the raw sequencing data for five patients with IDs of 1, 2, 3, 12, and 16. For each patient, TESLA used top-20 immunogenic fraction (TTIF), fraction ranked (FR), and area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) as performance metrics to evaluate the 25 teams that participated in the TESLA study [4]. Thus, we also used these metrics with the original code published by TESLA [4, 46] to evaluate the performance of NeoGuider and TESLA teams on these five patients. Additionally, we defined the summary metric TFA-mean as the average of TTIF, FR, and AUPRC. Although TFA-mean provides a single score for each patient to reduce the burden of performance comparison, the importance of TTIF, FR, and AUPRC depends on real-world applications, so we recommended to look at each individual metric for these applications. More specifically, TTIF and FR respectively represent the immunogenic potential with 20 and 100 peptides, and AUPRC represents the overall sensitivity and specificity.

Figure 2 shows that NeoGuider performs favorably on the TESLA data compared with the 25 TESLA teams (i.e., anhinga, auklet, …, swallow). For each patient, the TFA-mean of NeoGuider is greater than that of every TESLA team, except that the team falcon is able to achieve a higher TFA-mean for the patient with ID = 16 (Fig. 2), and a possible cause for the underperformance of NeoGuider is a previously unnoticed variant-calling error. For this patient, we manually reviewed all putative variants that UVC and Mutect2 [47] called. Read alignments around the putative variants revealed the following: The immunogenic peptide (i.e., peptide that is part of an immunogenic neoepitope) YLNEAVFNFV can be generated only by a single nucleotide variant (SNV), and this SNV can be generated only by a variant-calling error from a double nucleotide variant that generates the peptide YLNEAMFNFV. Hence, YLNEAMFNFV should replace the immunogenic peptide YLNEAVFNFV, which was likely to be generated by this variant-calling error. After excluding this likely erroneous peptide, all the 33 remaining immunogenic peptides from the 5 TESLA patients were detected from variants called by UVC. Hence, the NeoGuider pipeline uses UVC by default to call somatic small variants (the SNV that generates the immunogenic peptide FLNCDIMLGV is missed by Mutect2 [47] but not missed by UVC).

Prioritization of pre-detected neoepitope candidates

The task of detecting neoepitope candidates requires sequencing data in FASTQ format as input. Unfortunately, such sequencing data and/or the code that has previously been used to analyze these data are often unavailable, but the intermediate results generated by these analyses are often publicly available. Thus, we evaluated NeoGuider on two independent datasets, in the form of such intermediate results represented as two tables, for the task of prioritizing neoepitope candidates. The first dataset has been used to evaluate MuPeXI 1.2 [30]. This dataset consists of 190 neoepitope candidates detected by MuPeXI 1.2 from three non–small-cell lung cancer patients, and eight of these 190 candidates are immunogenic [30, 40, 41]. The second dataset has been used to evaluate DeepHLApan [31]. This dataset consists of 64 immunogenic neoepitope candidates curated from the literature and 6,400 likely non-immunogenic such candidates obtained from random selection in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database [31, 42].

On the two aforementioned datasets, the candidates ranked by MuPeXI 1.2 and DeepHLApan were reranked by NeoGuider. On these two datasets, NeoGuider performs better than MuPeXI 1.2 and DeepHLApan in terms of AUC-ROC, area under the curve (AUC) of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) (Fig. 3). Thus, on the two independent datasets, NeoGuider also demonstrates strong performance in prioritizing neoepitope candidates.

Prioritization performance of each single feature

Next, we assessed the performance of using each single feature to rank neoepitope candidates on validation cohorts (i.e., test datasets). Our assessment shows that the performance of each single feature by itself is low (Fig. 4). Thus, accurate prioritization requires the use of multiple features.

The assessment also shows that the default pMHC features that we proposed for NeoGuider are reasonable (Fig. 4). First, we observed that the feature ScoreEL, a correlate of pMHC binding strength computed by netMHCpan 4.1, performs the best for the task of using only one single feature to rank neoepitope candidates. Second, we did not observe any anomalies in the features predicted by the netMHC software series: As expected from existing literature [2, 3, 6], ScoreEL (a correlate of pMHC binding strength) outperforms ICfiftyBA (a correlate of pMHC binding affinity), which in turn outperforms BindStab (a correlate of pMHC binding stability). These observations imply that it was reasonable to incorporate ScoreEL, as well as ICfiftyBA and BindStab, into the set of features that NeoGuider uses by default.

As noted earlier, ScoreEL, which represents the ability of a peptide to bind to an MHC, outperforms %RankEL (i.e., the percentile of ScoreEL among random peptides) on the NCI-train discovery cohort. We observed the same outperformance pattern on all three test datasets corresponding to validation cohorts (Fig. 4). This result seems unexpected because previous studies suggested that %RankEL should outperform ScoreEL [5, 34], similar to how %RankBA outperforms ScoreBA representing raw binding affinity [48]. However, we noticed that the two situations are not directly comparable. Raw affinity does not capture immunogenicity-related differences between MHC alleles [48], whereas ScoreEL does incorporate such allele-specific information [6]. When ScoreEL is transformed into %RankEL, this informative allele-specific variation is removed. Thus, this transformation is unnecessary and can even be detrimental. This possibly explains why ScoreEL consistently outperforms %RankEL in immunogenicity prediction.

The effect of feature transformation on prioritization

To show that our feature transformation can improve the performances of many machine-learning classifiers, we compared the effects of using this transformation and of using all scikit-learn [38] feature preprocessing techniques [32] on the performance of the commonly used machine-learning classifiers presented online at [33]. The classifiers affected by runtime error are omitted.

Indeed, our NeoGuider feature transformation improves the performances of many classifiers in prioritizing neoepitope candidates (Fig. 4). The improved multilayer perceptron and logistic regression outperform other combinations of feature preprocessing techniques, classifiers, and hyperparameter-tuning options—without requiring hyperparameter tuning. Thus, using NeoGuider with default-hyperparameter logistic regression achieves top performance while maintaining model simplicity.

The effect of feature transformation evaluated by NeoRanking using other features and hyperparameter tuning procedure

By default, NeoGuider applies our feature transformation to only the set F of features. To show that the improvement enabled by the transformation is feature-agnostic, we evaluated its performance on another published feature set. More specifically, we used the NeoRanking framework, developed by Müller et al. [5], to benchmark NeoGuider with the NeoRanking’s built-in methods, since Müller et al. showed that the built-in methods outperform many combinations of machine learning techniques. NeoRanking internally uses HyperOpt to optimize the hyperparameters of a classifier via cross validation, whereas NeoGuider is hyperparameter-free. Thus, to properly benchmark NeoGuider, we implemented the functionality to skip hyperparameter tuning in NeoRanking. The input to NeoRanking is a table in which rows and columns denote neoepitope candidates and 31 features extracted from each candidate, respectively.

NeoRanking internally uses the quantile transform-based feature-preprocessing technique (QT) [38] because Müller et al. [5] showed that QT performs the best. We evaluated the performances of using the features preprocessed by only QT and by QT-NeoGuider (i.e., QT followed by our feature transformation) in improving neoepitope-candidate prioritization. By replacing QT with the combination of QT and our feature transformation, we can evaluate the effect of incorporating our transformation.

Our feature transformation results in more immunogenic neoepitope candidates being ranked ahead of the non-immunogenic ones. Feature preprocessing by NeoGuider greatly improves the performance of default-hyperparameter logistic regression (i.e., LR) in candidate prioritization (Table 4). The improved performance is better than the performances of the hyperparameter-tuned classifiers that Müller et al. [5] incorporated into NeoRanking. The improvement is more notable for the TESLA and Human Integrated Tumor Immunology Discovery Engine (HiTIDE) cohorts, implying that NeoGuider is especially good at generalizing to completely independent cohorts.

The effect of feature transformation evaluated on the IMPROVE data and feature sets

To confirm that our feature transformation robustly improves the prioritization of neoepitope candidates, we evaluated the transformation using another independent dataset provided by Borch et al. [34]. We followed the evaluation protocol that Borch et al. previously used: We used out-of-fold ROC-AUC generated by fivefold cross validation as the performance metrics, and we used the folds pre-selected by Borch et al. such that candidates of the same patient are in the same fold. Moreover, we performed nested cross validation as follow: We used BayesSearchCV from scikit-optimize [36] to tune hyperparameters by maximizing inner fold ROC-AUC, and we used cross_val_predict from scikit-learn [38] to evaluate performance using outer fold ROC-AUC.

Our evaluation shows that our feature transformation improves the performance in prioritizing neoepitope candidates (Fig. 5). We evaluated many combinations of feature transformations, classifiers, and hyperparameter-tuning strategies. Our evaluation shows that the combination of NeoGuider feature transformation and default-hyperparameter logistic regression generally achieves the best performance, justifying the use of the transformation with default-hyperparameter logistic regression.

Results of feature importance analysis

The feature importance analysis provided by NeoGuider confirms previous findings. For example, the feature ScoreEL is more important than the feature ICfiftyBA for neoepitope-candidate prioritization (Additional File 1: Table S1), which is as expected since ScoreEL captures the entire biological process of pMHC presentation whereas ICfiftyBA is a single chemical property [6]. Additionally, NeoAbundance is not correlated with other features (Additional file 1: Fig. S2), and this lack of correlation has been observed in other studies [4].

Discussion

We developed NeoGuider, a neoepitope prediction method implemented as a bioinformatics pipeline. To ensure that its performance could be consistently and fairly compared with existing states of the art, we adopted the same evaluation protocols used in previous studies (Table 1). These evaluations demonstrate the superior performance of NeoGuider in detecting and prioritizing neoepitope candidates.

NeoGuider uses the combination of our hyperparameter-free feature transformation and the logistic regression configured with its default hyperparameters. The performance of NeoGuider can suffer due to its lack of hyperparameter options. Still, the hyperparameter-free NeoGuider outperforms all other commonly used combinations of machine-learning techniques although we performed hyperparameter tuning on these techniques (Figs. 4 and 5). Additionally, the hyperparameter-free property of NeoGuider results in its easiness to use and fast training.

Furthermore, our machine-learning model implemented in NeoGuider uses monotonic and piecewise linear functions to extend logistic regression. Thus, unlike other techniques such as random forest, our model can interpret immunogenicity probability as a product of odds extracted from features (Fig. 6). Such interpretability, which implies explainability but not vice versa, is highly desirable in precision medicine [13]. Although tools such as NetMHCpan 4.1 use complex (i.e., more black-box and less interpretable) internal models, their output features (e.g., ScoreEL and ICfiftyBA) represent well-established biochemical properties (e.g., binding strength and affinity). Clinicians can thus interpret these features without understanding the tools’ implementations, similar to using laboratory-measured values.

The feature transformation implemented in NeoGuider is not only interpretable but also broadly applicable. For example, it could enhance MHC-II epitope prediction by transforming relevant features into log odds. Similarly, for TCR-specific pMHC recognition prediction, our transformation method could integrate prediction scores from existing computational tools as input features, transform them, and feed them into an ensemble model to improve prediction performance. While current NeoGuider focuses on MHC-I due to data availability, our transformation is extensible to MHC-II/TCR features as more validation data emerge. Notably, we can generalize the task of predicting immunogenicity into the more abstract task of identifying positive examples from a given dataset using relevant features (Fig. 6). This generalization into binary classification highlights its potential utility across diverse problems. However, while our feature transformation shows promise for these applications, further benchmarking is necessary to validate its effectiveness.

Although our feature transformation is interpretable and broadly applicable, it requires the label-associated log odds to be monotonically either increasing or decreasing as a function of each feature. This monotonicity requirement is satisfied for the immunogenicity-related features used to predict neoepitopes, but it may not be satisfied for other features. Hence, the user should verify that this monotonicity requirement is met before using our feature transformation. To relax this requirement, we can use a convex function, instead of a monotonic function, to model the log odds as function of a feature. We implemented this convex function modeling in NeoGuider, although its performance on problems beyond neoepitope prediction remains unevaluated.

A bioinformatics tool should not only achieve high performance but also be able to take many types of data as its input. Our NeoGuider pipeline can take different types of data (e.g., FASTQ, FASTA, and tabular files representing sequencing data, neoepitope candidates, and tables, respectively) as its input, but many other tools cannot. Moreover, some tools are publicly unavailable: Only their performance results are available to us. Therefore, we cannot benchmark these tools together. Still, we can possibly compare these tools indirectly. Such indirect comparison may require another study.

In our study, the comparison is direct. However, the limited number of positive examples still causes challenges for discerning small difference in performance between different tools. In the future, new relevant data will still significantly contribute to the field of neoepitope prediction.

Although new data are highly desirable, a simple merging of data from different studies can result in some artifact. For example, in a pool of different studies, the number of a patient’s neoepitope candidates tested to be positive by an immunoassay remains relatively constant, whereas #Tested, the number of candidates tested by an immunoassay, varies by orders of magnitudes (Additional file 1: Fig. S7). Thus, the positive rate of a study decreases as #Tested increases, presumably because different studies employed different assay types and reporting schemes. For example, TESLA reported candidates tested positive by at least one multimer-staining assay as positives [4], whereas Müller et al. only reported candidates tested positive by both minigene and peptide immunospot assays as positives [5]. Thus, we may have to remove the batch effect associated with each study to analyze candidates in a pool of patients from different studies.

While immunogenicity remains the primary criterion for prioritizing neoepitope candidates, it is not the only one. We can consider additional factors to enhance the prioritization. For example, clonal mutations, which are present in all tumor cells of a patient, may elicit broader antitumor immunity through neoepitope generation compared to subclonal mutations, which are restricted to a subset of tumor cells. Similarly, neoepitopes with a lower likelihood of being lost through copy-number alterations may confer more durable therapeutic benefits. Thus, additional bioinformatics analyses can evaluate these factors to refine neoepitope prediction for real-world applications.

While accurate neoepitope prediction is crucial, it represents only one aspect of effective neoepitope-based therapy. Treatment efficacy also hinges on factors such as the adjuvant and the specific type of therapy used (e.g., mRNA vaccine, peptide vaccine, TCR-T cells) [49]. Consequently, future research should take a holistic approach to understanding how neoepitope prediction interacts with these factors to optimize patient outcomes.

The feature foreignness has been suggested as an immunogenicity-related feature for predicting neoepitopes [4, 50]. Thus, we also attempted to use the foreignness computed by the antigen.garnish R package [4, 50] as an additional feature to be included in the set F of features that NeoGuider uses by default. Unfortunately, unlike other features in F, the Fisher transform–based 95% confidence interval of the coefficient of Spearman correlation between foreignness and immunogenicity probability spans zero, and it remains zero even if more training data are used. Thus, foreignness is not correlated with, and does not help in predicting, immunogenicity. We also manually inspected the data to confirm this lack of correlation between foreignness and immunogenicity. Hence, we did not use foreignness as an additional feature, despite some evidence for its association with checkpoint blockade–based immunotherapy and survival in patients with pancreatic cancer [51]. Additional studies on the biological mechanism of immunogenicity may be required to explain the following paradox: Foreignness is associated with such immunotherapy and survival but not correlated with immunogenicity.

Conclusions

NeoGuider is a neoepitope prediction method implemented as an easy-to-use bioinformatics pipeline. NeoGuider is characterized by its superior performance in detecting and prioritizing neoepitope candidates. Thus, NeoGuider can improve the design of neoepitope-based cancer immunotherapy. Our feature transformation, implemented in NeoGuider, can improve neoepitope-candidate prioritization in many scenarios. This transformation may be applicable to other problems characterized by nonlinearity and class imbalance.
