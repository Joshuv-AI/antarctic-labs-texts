Beamforming Saturation in Two-Timescale RIS-assisted Communication
Masoud Sadeghian ⋆ , Angel Lozano ⋆ , and Gabor Fodor †♯

Abstract
This paper considers wireless communication assisted by a reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS), focusing on the two-timescale approach, in which the RIS phase shifts are optimized based on channel statistics to mitigate the overheads associated with channel estimation. It is shown that, while the power captured by the RIS scales linearly with the number of its elements, the two-timescale beamforming gain upon re-radiation towards the receiver saturates rapidly as the number of RIS elements increases, for a broad class of power angular spectra (PAS). The ultimate achievable gain is determined by the decay rate of the PAS in the angular domain, which directly influences how rapidly spatial correlations between RIS elements diminish. The implications of this saturation on the effectiveness of RIS-assisted communications are discussed.



I
Introduction

Reconfigurable intelligence surfaces (RISs)
enable creating smart radio environments in which the propagation is controlled by integrated electronic circuits and software
[
1
]
.
Since
RISs
can be built by predominantly passive elements, their deployment appears
as an energy and cost efficient complement to large-scale wireless infrastructures, particularly to overcome blockages
[
2
,
3
]
.

Relying on passive phase-shift elements that are devoid of signal processing capabilities implies that estimating both the transmitter-
RIS
and the
RIS
-receiver links becomes critical
[
4
,
5
]
. Indeed, channel estimation in
RIS
-assisted wireless networks is more challenging than in conventional
systems
[
5
]
.
An approach to circumvent these hurdles is to optimize the phase shifts based on only the channel statistics, rather than its realizations. Sometimes referred to as a two-timescale scheme
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
, this only requires that the receiver estimates—for coherent detection purposes—the
cascade channel connecting it with the transmitter via the RIS. Put differently, the RIS becomes transparent as far as channel estimation is concerned.
This
is attractive, since standardized sophisticated channel acquisition
procedures are supported in contemporary wireless networks.

In this paper, we consider the uplink of a
RIS
-aided cell in which the

base station (BS)
is equipped with multiple antennas, and ask the question of how the contribution of the RIS
depends on the number of elements it features in a two-timescale scenario.
This contribution depends on the amount of power captured by the RIS, and on its beamforming gain when re-radiating such power towards the receiver.
To address this question, an analytical expression is derived
for the
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
with optimum reception at the
BS
when the
RIS
phase shifts are configured based on the correlation among its elements.
These correlations are determined by the topology of the RIS and by the channel’s
power angular spectrum (PAS)
.

The main result of the paper is that, for a linear RIS and
a large class of PAS functions that includes those most commonly used to model multipath propagation, the two-timescale RIS beamforming gain saturates as the number of elements grows large.
The highest attainable gain depends on how rapidly the
PAS
decays angularly or, equivalently, on how rapidly the correlations decay over space.

II
System Model

A single-antenna
mobile station (MS)
communicates with an
N_{\text{b}}
-antenna
BS
, aided by a uniform linear
RIS
equipped with
N_{\text{r}}
elements.
Since empirical measurements indicate that the elevation angle plays a secondary role in most outdoor deployments
[
10
, Ch. 3]
, the
RIS is horizontally disposed and the
formulation involves only the azimuth.

II-A

Cascade Channel Model

The normalized channel between the
BS
and the
RIS
, deterministic and of unit rank, is
{\bm{H}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\text{b}}\times N_{\text{r}}}
given by

{\bm{H}}={\bm{a}}_{\text{b}}{\bm{a}}_{\text{r}}^{*},

(1)

where
{\bm{a}}_{\text{b}}
and
{\bm{a}}_{\text{r}}
are the respective steering vectors at
BS
and
RIS
. Precisely,
{\bm{a}}_{\text{b}}
is an arbitrary steering vector that depends on the topology of the BS and the angle of arrival thereon, while
{\bm{a}}_{\text{r}}
is a steering vector whose
m
th entry is

[{\bm{a}}_{\text{r}}]_{m}=\exp\!\left({\mathsf{j}}2\pi\frac{md\cos{\theta_\txr}}{\lambda_{\text{c}}}\right)\qquad\quad m=0,\ldots,N_{\text{r}}-1,

(2)

where
\theta_{\text{r}}
is the angle of departure relative to the linear RIS,
d
is the spacing between RIS elements, and
\lambda_{\text{c}}
is the carrier wavelength.

The normalized channel between the
MS
and the
RIS
, in turn, is
{\bm{h}}_{{\text{r}}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{0},{\bm{C}}_{\text{r}})
with covariance matrix
{\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\text{r}}\times N_{\text{r}}}
.
As it models the multipath portion of the channel,
{\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}
is nonsingular and, owing to
the linear and uniform structure of the RIS, it exhibits a Toeplitz structure; its
(k,\ell)
th entry depends only on
k-\ell=n
, being given by

c_{n}=\int_{0}^{\pi}\mathcal{P}(\theta)\,e^{{\mathsf{j}}2\pi\frac{nd\cos{\theta}}{\lambda_{\text{c}}}}\,{\mathrm{d}}\theta

(3)

for
n=0,\ldots,N_{\text{r}}-1
,
with
\mathcal{P}(\theta)
the
PAS
normalized so it can be interpreted as a probability density function
[
10
, Ch. 3]
supported on
[0,\pi]
, which corresponds to the half-space facing the
RIS
.
It follows from this PAS normalization that the entries of
{\bm{h}}_{{\text{r}}}
are of unit variance, and that
c_{0}=1
while
c_{-n}=c^{*}_{n}
.

With the configuration of the
RIS
specified by the phase-shift matrix
\bm{\Psi}=\text{diag}(\bm{\psi})
where
\bm{\psi}=[e^{-j\psi_{0}},\dots,e^{-j\psi_{N_{\text{r}}-1}}]^{\text{T}}
, the cascade channel at the
BS
from the
MS
is

{\bm{h}}={\bm{H}}\bm{\Psi}{\bm{h}}_{\text{r}}.

(4)

With a statistical optimization of
\bm{\Psi}
, the cascade channel satisfies
{\bm{h}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{0},{\bm{C}})
with

\displaystyle{\bm{C}}={\bm{H}}\bm{\Psi}{\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}\bm{\Psi}^{*}{\bm{H}}^{*},

(5)

which is singular on account of the rank-1 nature of
{\bm{H}}
, and therefore of
{\bm{h}}
.

While, as mentioned, the estimation of the channels to/from the RIS is utterly challenging, estimating the cascade channel
{\bm{h}}
at the BS is a standard procedure. In the case at hand, a single pilot symbol transmission suffices. By suitably repeating or power-boosting such pilot transmission, an arbitrarily precise estimate of
{\bm{h}}
can be procured
[
11
]
.
Accordingly, perfect knowledge of
{\bm{h}}
at the BS is considered in the sequel, yet the findings in the paper continue to apply in the face of channel estimation errors; they are merely obscured by the more involved formulation.

II-B

Uplink Data Signal Model

Upon uplink data transmission, the observation at the
BS
is

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}=\sqrt{\alpha}{\bm{h}}x+{\bm{n}},

(6)

where
\alpha
is the large-scale channel gain,

{\bm{n}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}}_{N_{\text{b}}})
is the additive white Gaussian noise, and
x
is a data symbol of power
P
.

III
Average SNR in Two-Timescale

RIS-Assisted Communication

With
{\bm{h}}
perfectly known by the BS, the optimum receiver thereon is a linear combiner
{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\text{b}}\times 1}
satisfying
[
10
]

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}\propto{\bm{h}}

(7)

and the ensuing SNR, for a given
{\bm{h}}
, equals

SNR

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\left|{\bm{w}}^{*}\sqrt{\alpha}{\bm{h}}x\right|^{2}|{\bm{h}}\big]}{\mathbb{E}\big[|{\bm{w}}^{*}{\bm{n}}|^{2}\big]}

(8)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha P}{\sigma^{2}}\|{\bm{h}}\|^{2}.

(9)

Since
{\bm{h}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{0},{\bm{C}})
with
{\bm{C}}
a rank-1 covariance matrix, the above SNR is exponentially distributed. Recalling (
1
) and (
5
), its average value is given by

\displaystyle\text{SNR}_{\text{avg}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha P}{\sigma^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}\big[\|{\bm{h}}\|^{2}\big]

(10)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha P}{\sigma^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}\big[\text{tr}({\bm{h}}{\bm{h}}^{*})\big]

(11)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha P}{\sigma^{2}}\,\tr(\bC)

(12)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha P}{\sigma^{2}}\,\tr(\bH\bPsi\bC_\txr\bPsi^* \bH^*)

(13)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha P}{\sigma^{2}}\,\tr(\bPsi\bC_\txr\bPsi^* \bH^* \bH)

(14)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha P}{\sigma^{2}}\,\tr(\bPsi\bC_\txr\bPsi^* \ba_\txr\ba_\txb^* \ba_\txb\ba_\txr^*)

(15)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha P}{\sigma^{2}}N_{\text{b}}\,\tr(\bPsi\bC_\txr\bPsi^* \ba_\txr\ba_\txr^*)

(16)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha P}{\sigma^{2}}N_{\text{b}}\,\tr(\ba_\txr^* \bPsi\bC_\txr\bPsi^* \ba_\txr)

(17)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha P}{\sigma^{2}}N_{\text{b}}\,{\bm{a}}_{\text{r}}^{*}\bm{\Psi}{\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}\bm{\Psi}^{*}{\bm{a}}_{\text{r}}

(18)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha P}{\sigma^{2}}N_{\text{b}}N_{\text{r}}\,{\bm{v}}^{*}\bm{\Psi}{\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}\bm{\Psi}^{*}{\bm{v}}

(19)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha P}{\sigma^{2}}N_{\text{b}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\underbrace{N_{\text{r}}\,\zeta}_{\text{RIS power gain}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

(20)

where in (
19
) and (
20
) we introduced, respectively,

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\text{r}}}}{\bm{a}}_{\text{r}}

(21)

and

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle={\bm{v}}^{*}\bm{\Psi}{\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}\bm{\Psi}^{*}{\bm{v}}

(22)

\displaystyle=\bm{\psi}\,\text{diag}({\bm{v}}^{*}){\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}\text{diag}({\bm{v}})\bm{\psi}^{*}.

(23)

Within the average SNR in (
20
), the multiplier
N_{\text{b}}
corresponds to the gain of the BS receive array, which leaves the product
N_{\text{r}}\zeta
as the power gain provided by the RIS. This gain is made up of two factors:

•

N_{\text{r}}
, which reflects the power captured by the RIS acting as a receiver and does not depend on the RIS phase shifts,

•

\zeta
, which represents the beamforming gain provided by the RIS acting as a transmitter, incorporating the effect of the RIS phase shifts. One can view
\zeta
as the average power gain relative to these phase shifts being random.

If the channel realizations to/from the RIS could be perfectly estimated, the RIS phase shifts could be instantaneously optimized yielding
\zeta=N_{\text{r}}
, for a RIS power gain
N_{\text{r}}\zeta
that would altogether be quadratic in the number of RIS elements
[
12
]
. In a two-timescale system, however, the beamforming gain
\zeta
behaves rather differently.

IV
Two-Timescale Beamforming Gain

The optimum phase shifts are those that maximize the beamforming gain,
\zeta
.
The optimization of the RIS phase shifts can thus be cast, recalling (
23
), as

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=\max_{\bm{\psi}:\,|\psi_{m}|=1\;\forall m}\bm{\psi}\,\text{diag}({\bm{v}}^{*}){\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}\text{diag}({\bm{v}})\,\bm{\psi}^{*}

(24)

whose objective function is quadratic, but whose unit-magnitude constraints are not convex.
This problem is addressed in
[
13
]
with an approximation guarantee, while
[
14
]
introduces a low-complexity alternative optimization approach.
Sidestepping numerical optimization procedures, in this section insights are gleaned analytically.

For
N_{\text{r}}=1
, trivially
\zeta=1
in a two-timescale system as it would be with instantaneously optimized phase shifts. For
N_{\text{r}}=2
, however, the latter would double to
\zeta=2
while the former amounts, after a bit of algebra, to

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=\max_{\psi_{0},\psi_{1}:\,|\psi_{0}|=1,|\psi_{1}|=1}1+\real\!\left\{c_{1}e^{-{\mathsf{j}}(\psi_{1}-\psi_{0})}e^{{\mathsf{j}}2\pi\frac{d\cos{\theta}}{\lambda_{\text{c}}}}\right\},

(25)

which, by setting
\psi_{1}-\psi_{0}=\arg(c_{1})+2\pi d\cos\theta_{\text{r}}/\lambda_{\text{c}}
, returns

\displaystyle\zeta=1+|c_{1}|.

(26)

As
{\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}
is nonsingular,
|c_{1}|<1
and thus the two-timescale beamforming gain satisfies
\zeta<2
, falling short of its instantaneously optimized counterpart.
In fact, the two-timescale beamforming gain is strictly sublinear in
N_{\text{r}}
for every
N_{\text{r}}
. This can be appreciated by relaxing the constraints in (
24
) to
\|\bm{\psi}\|=\sqrt{N_{\text{r}}}
, whereby

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\bm{\psi}:\,\|\bm{\psi}\|=\sqrt{N_{\text{r}}}}\bm{\psi}\,\text{diag}({\bm{v}}^{*}){\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}\text{diag}({\bm{v}})\,\bm{\psi}^{*}

(27)

\displaystyle=N_{\text{r}}\,\lambda_{\text{max}}\!\Big(\text{diag}({\bm{v}}^{*}){\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}\text{diag}({\bm{v}})\Big),

(28)

where
\lambda_{\text{max}}(\cdot)
denotes the largest eigenvalue of a matrix, and (
28
) is achieved by the maximum-eigenvalue eigenvector of
\text{diag}({\bm{v}}^{*}){\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}\text{diag}({\bm{v}})
.
Since
\sqrt{N_{\text{r}}}\,\text{diag}({\bm{v}}^{*})
is a unitary matrix, the above is tantamount to

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\text{max}}({\bm{C}}_{\text{r}})

(29)

\displaystyle<\text{tr}({\bm{C}}_{\text{r}})

(30)

\displaystyle=N_{\text{r}},

(31)

with (
30
) following from the nonsingular condition of
{\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}
.

If the RIS elements are uncorrelated, then
\lambda_{\text{max}}({\bm{C}}_{\text{r}})=1
and
the above corroborates the stronger result that, as expected, there is no beamforming gain to be had in a two-timescale system without RIS element correlation. In the sequel, therefore, only situations in which the RIS elements do exhibit correlation are considered.

Owing to the structure of
{\bm{v}}
, which descends from (
2
), the
(k,\ell)
th entry of
\text{diag}({\bm{v}}^{*}){\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}\text{diag}({\bm{v}})
equals

\displaystyle\left[\text{diag}({\bm{v}}^{*}){\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}\text{diag}({\bm{v}})\right]_{k,\ell}=c_{n}\;\frac{e^{{\mathsf{j}}2\pi nd\cos\theta_{\text{r}}/\lambda_{\text{c}}}}{N_{\text{r}}},

(32)

where, recall,
n=k-\ell
. Thus, the Toeplitz structure of
{\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}
is readily inherited by

\text{diag}({\bm{v}}^{*}){\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}\text{diag}({\bm{v}})
, whereby
its maximum eigenvalue is upper bounded by
\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}|c_{n}|

[
15
]
,
such that

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle\leq\!\!\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}|c_{n}|.

(33)

The beamforming gain is thus sure to be curbed if the right-hand side of (
33
) is finite, which corresponds to
{\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}
being a Wiener-class Toeplitz matrix.
This, in turn, is guaranteed if

|c_{n}|=o\!\left(\frac{1}{|n|}\right),

(34)

which, as seen in Sec.
V
, is satisfied by a number of functions that have been shown to be a satisfactory fit for the PAS.

Irrespective of whether (
34
) holds, a precise assertion of how the beamforming gain behaves can be made asymptotically in the number of
RIS
elements.
Indeed, for
N_{\text{r}}\to\infty
, numerical optimizations become unnecessary as
(
24
) admits a closed-form solution. Under mild conditions satisfied by any well-behaved PAS, for growing
N_{\text{r}}
the eigenvectors of
\text{diag}({\bm{v}}^{*}){\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}\text{diag}({\bm{v}})
converge, on account of the Toeplitz nature of this matrix, to Fourier vectors
of the form
[
15
]

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\text{r}}}}\left[1,e^{-{\mathsf{j}}2\pi m/N_{\text{r}}},\ldots,e^{-{\mathsf{j}}2\pi m(N_{\text{r}}-1)/N_{\text{r}}}\right]^{*}

(35)

for
m=0,...,N_{\text{r}}-1
.
As these vectors (scaled by
N_{\text{r}}
) comply with the constraints in (
24
), the solution to this original problem is sure to equal that of the relaxed problem in (
29
), meaning that the optimum beamforming gain satisfies

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle\approx\lambda_{\text{max}}({\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}),

(36)

which sharpens with growing
N_{\text{r}}
and becomes exact asymptotically.
The RIS phase shifts achieving this beamforming gain, in turn, converge to the Fourier eigenvector (scaled by
\sqrt{N_{\text{r}}}
) associated with the maximum eigenvalue of
{\bm{C}}_{\text{r}}
. As seen next, for many PAS functions of interest this eigenvalue is bounded from above.

V
Examples

Let us next entertain some of the functions most commonly considered to model the PAS.

•

Truncated Gaussian
.
A Gaussian function with mean
\mu_{\theta}
and angular spread
\sigma_{\theta}>0
, whose support is restricted to
\theta\in(0,\pi)
, has been shown to offer a good fit to the empirical PAS in elevated
BSs

[
16
]
. The function is

\mathcal{P}(\theta)=\frac{K_{\mathsf{G}}}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{\theta}}\exp\left(-\frac{(\theta-\mu_{\theta})^{2}}{2\sigma_{\theta}^{2}}\right),

(37)

where

K_{\mathsf{G}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}[1-2Q(\frac{\pi}{2\sigma_{\theta}})]},

(38)

is a normalization factor ensuring that the PAS integrates to one, and
Q(\cdot)
is the Gaussian Q-function.

For small
\sigma_{\theta}
, the integration in (
3
) yields
[
10
, Ch. 3]

|c_{n}|\approx\exp\!\left(-2\left[\frac{\pi dn\sin(\mu_\theta)\sigma_{\theta}}{\lambda_{\text{c}}}\right]^{2}\right),

(39)

whereby (
33
) specializes to
[
17
, Ch. 16.27]

\zeta\leq\vartheta\!\left(0,e^{-2\left[\frac{\pi d\sin(\mu_\theta)\sigma_{\theta}}{\lambda_{\text{c}}}\right]^{2}}\right),

(40)

where
\vartheta(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Jacobi theta function.
The decay of (
39
) is decidedly faster than
1/|n|
and the beamforming gain is therefore curbed.

•

Truncated Laplacian
.
Extensive measurements have shown a strong fit of the Laplacian
PAS
, with a properly tuned
\sigma_{\theta}
and support on
(0,\pi)
, to both indoor
[
18
]
and outdoor environments
[
19
]
. This function is
[
10
, Ch. 3]

\mathcal{P}(\theta)=\frac{K_{\mathsf{L}}}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{\theta}}\exp\left(-\left|\frac{\sqrt{2}(\theta-\mu_{\theta})}{\sigma_{\theta}}\right|\right),

(41)

with

K_{\mathsf{L}}=\frac{1}{1-\exp\!\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{\theta}}\right)}.

(42)

For small
\sigma_{\theta}
, it holds that
[
20
]

|c_{n}|\approx\frac{1}{1+2(\pi dn\sin(\mu_\theta)\sigma_{\theta}/\lambda_{\text{c}})^{2}}

(43)

and, from

\pi\cot(\pi z)=\frac{1}{z}+2z\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{z^{2}-n^{2}},

(44)

it follows that (
33
) specializes to

\zeta\leq\frac{\lambda_{\text{c}}}{\sqrt{2}d\sin(\mu_\theta)\sigma_{\theta}}\coth\!\left(\frac{\lambda_{\text{c}}}{\sqrt{2}d\sin(\mu_\theta)\sigma_{\theta}}\right).

(45)

In this case,
|c_{n}|=\mathcal{O}(1/|n|^{2})
, whereby the beamforming gain is again curbed.

•

Exponential correlation model
.
This simpler model is

|c_{n}|=\kappa^{|n|},

(46)

where
\kappa<1
is a parameter
[
21
]
.
Leveraging the properties of the geometric series, (
33
) specializes to

\zeta\leq\frac{1+|\kappa|}{1-|\kappa|},

(47)

which is always bounded.

VI
Conclusion

The two-timescale approach to RIS-assisted communication avoids the costly estimation of the channels to/from the RIS. Moreover, computationally attractive Fourier phase shifts can be applied in the case of a linear RIS. This overall convenience, however, comes at a price in performance. The power gain, quadratic in the number of RIS elements if
the RIS phase shifts could be instantaneously optimized, reverts to a scaling that is always sub-quadratic; for a broad class of channels, in fact, this scaling becomes merely linear beyond a relatively small number of elements.
This poorer scaling with the number of elements curtails the ability of the RIS to provide a decisive advantage relative to ambient propagation
[
22
,
23
]
, and renders the RIS less competitive against alternatives such as relays and network-controlled repeaters
[
24
]
. The generalization of these findings to planar RIS entails dealing with block-Toeplitz covariance matrices. The results are qualitatively similar, but a larger number of RIS elements is required for the power gain scaling to become merely linear.
