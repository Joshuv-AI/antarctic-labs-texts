Context Matters! Relaxing Goals with LLMs for Feasible 3D Scene Planning
Emanuele Musumeci, Michele Brienza, Francesco Argenziano, Vincenzo Suriani, Daniele Nardi, Domenico D. Bloisi

Abstract
Classical planning in AI and Robotics addresses complex tasks by shifting from imperative to declarative approaches (e.g., PDDL). However, these methods often fail in real scenarios due to limited robot perception and the need to ground perceptions to planning predicates. This often results in heavily hard-coded behaviors that struggle to adapt, even with scenarios where goals can be achieved through relaxed planning. Meanwhile, Large Language Models (LLMs) lead to planning systems that leverage commonsense reasoning but often at the cost of generating unfeasible and/or unsafe plans. To address these limitations, we present an approach integrating classical planning with LLMs, leveraging their ability to extract commonsense knowledge and ground actions. We propose a hierarchical formulation that enables robots to make unfeasible tasks tractable by defining functionally equivalent goals through gradual relaxation. This mechanism supports partial achievement of the intended objective, suited to the agent’s specific context . Our method demonstrates its ability to adapt and execute tasks effectively within environments modeled using 3D Scene Graphs through comprehensive qualitative and quantitative evaluations. We also show how this method succeeds in complex scenarios where other benchmark methods are more likely to fail. Code, dataset, and additional material are released to the community.

Planning with embodied agents involves creating a sequence of actions that an agent must execute to achieve a specific goal within its environment. This process becomes significantly more challenging moving from simulated to real-world settings. In real-world scenarios, actions must be accurately grounded in the environment, meaning there must be a precise mapping between high-level plans—often described in natural or formal languages—and the low-level executable actions of a robotic system.
Those tasks are often not directly groundable in the agent world representation, leading to unfeasible plans or unsolvable goals.
Recent advancements in AI have introduced Foundation Models (FMs), large-scale machine learning systems trained on extensive datasets. While initially developed for Natural Language Processing (NLP), FMs—particularly Large Language Models (LLMs)

In this work, we introduce a framework designed to overcome these challenges by integrating classical planning techniques with the capabilities of LLMs. Our goal is to simplify the planning process for embodied agents, through a framework that includes mechanisms to relax unfeasible tasks into functionally equivalent goals, allowing partial achievement of objectives suited to the agent’s context.

3D Scene Graph

At the same time, we retain robust classical planning methods to ensure reliability and correctness in executing any derived plan. This mechanism echoes human-like adaptability, dynamically modifying expected outcomes based on context while preserving high-level intentions and guaranteeing sound, executable solutions.

II-B

LLMs as Planners

The use of LLMs as planners for embodied agents has rapidly acquired growing interest from the scientific community. In the seminal work SayCan
[
18
]
, a robot can provide observations and actions for an LLM when grounding tasks in real-world scenarios, taking advantage of the vast semantic knowledge of the model. Following SayCan, several approaches started to emerge that tried to use LLMs as the planning component in many different use cases. In
[
7
,
19
]
, authors demonstrate that LLMs can operate effectively as zero-shot planners when appropriately prompted. In contrast,
[
19
,
20

highlight that fine-tuning LLMs in a few-shot setting enables them to outperform state-of-the-art Vision Language Navigation (VLN) models, even those trained on larger datasets, due to the LLMs’ intrinsic commonsense knowledge. The capabilities of LLMs can further expand when the input query adopts a more structured format, such as graph-based structures
[
16
]
— including 3D Scene Graphs. Integrating such structured data enhances performance across a variety of tasks by providing additional contextual or semantic information. Latest advancements in open-vocabulary systems, have further broadened the applications of LLMs for real-world planning
[
21
,
22
]

Planning can also include multi-cognitive levels, as in
[
23
]
, where the LLM can manage reasoning, planning, and motion generation. However, these approaches tend to struggle when dealing with long-horizon planning problems.

II-C

LLMs and Classical Planning

Combining LLMs with classical planners can solve planning problems in efficient ways to get optimal plans even with long-horizon planning problems, in contrast with pure LLM planning. For example, in
[
24
]
, they the robot’s action knowledge is dynamically augmented, and preconditions and effects of actions are modified, by leveraging task-oriented commonsense knowledge.
However, these approaches struggle in completely unseen environments, as they rely on a strict definition of both domain and problem, often leading to failures in such scenarios. To combine the strengths of natural language reasoning and classical planning,
[
25
]
proposes a method that translates a natural language description of a planning problem into a PDDL representation, allowing it to be solved using a PDDL planner. Since LLMs can still hallucinate and generate infeasible plans due to incomplete domain knowledge
[
26
]
introduces DELTA, an LLM-guided task planning framework. DELTA leverages 3D Scene Graphs as environmental representations within LLMs to quickly generate planning problem descriptions. To further improve planning efficiency, it breaks down long-term task goals into an autoregressive sequence of sub-goals using LLMs, enabling automated planners to solve complex tasks more effectively.

A key limitation of this class of approaches is the lack of a mechanism that dynamically adjusts the goal to real-world conditions. In fact, when a plan is generated, it often fails due to grounding into the real environment and no fallback procedure allows to recover and create a workable solution and adapt the task specifications. This limitation weakens the robustness of these approaches in real-world scenarios that often diverge from commonsense model assumptions.

We address the presented issues by borrowing concepts from a multi-tier framework of automatic planning
[
27
]
to reach adaptive behaviors for the agents. Such approaches have been demonstrated to be suitable for different domains
[
28
]
, leading to higher flexibility in the representation. In particular, we extend these representations, introducing two dimensions of flexibility, demonstrating to be able to enables embodied agents to make unfeasible tasks tractable by defining functionally equivalent goals through gradual relaxation. This mechanism supports partial achievement of the intended objective, suited to the agent’s specific context.

III
Methodology

This section provides a formal definition of a problem shifting and a problem relaxation operator -Subsection A-, leading to the definition of an implicit “
relaxation graph
”, a space of semantically related planning problems, organized according to a hierarchy defined in terms of the degree of complexity of the constraints inherent in the problem formulation. This problem space is navigated according to a heuristic, intrinsic in the emergent representations defined by commonsense knowledge-driven reasoning. Subsection B describes the architecture of a planning system capable of navigating the problem space, adapting the formulation of tasks that are otherwise unsolvable in the operating environment, by solving progressively relaxed but semantically related versions of the original goal. Subsection C describes the proposed implementation for this architecture.

III-A

Bi-Dimensional Task Adaptation

Situational Shifting (First dimension)

A given input environment
\mathcal{S}
(modeled as a 3DSG) is translated into a domain specification
\mathcal{M}_{\text{repr}}(S)=\Sigma
, aimed at satisfying a planning goal
G_{0}
, through a semantic representation
\mathcal{M}_{\text{repr}}
based on commonsense knowledge.
Let
\Sigma_{0}
be the original domain specification, represented by the tuple
\langle Obj,Pred,Act,InitState\rangle
, modeling the agent’s understanding of the operating environment, comprising the objects, predicates, initial state of the environment and the applicable agent actions, and let
G^{0}
be the initial assigned planning goal, we define the planning problem
\mathcal{P}=\langle\Sigma,G\rangle
.

We introduce a
situational shifting operator
:

\Sigma_{k}=\Gamma_{\text{shift}}(\Sigma_{k-1},G,\mathcal{S}_{\text{sem}},\mathcal{M}_{\text{repr}})=\Gamma^{k}_{\text{shift}}(\Sigma_{0},...)

(1)

which produces a new domain specification
\Sigma_{k}=\langle Obj_{k},Pred_{k},Act,InitState_{k}\rangle
, obtained by shifting the original domain
k
times. Assuming that the agent actions
Act
are fixed, the new shifted domain is obtained as an adaptation of the agent’s understanding of the operating environment to the current planning goal
G
. Each shifted domain
\Sigma_{k}
is at a certain
semantic distance

k
from the original
\Sigma_{0}
. Formally

\delta(\Sigma_{0},\Sigma_{k})=k,

(2)

where
\delta
is a distance function measuring the number of applications of the operator
\Gamma_{\text{shift}}
, required to reach the new shifted domain
\Sigma_{k}
from
\Sigma_{0}
, in the scene
S_{\text{sem}}
, to achieve the goal
G
, according to the semantic representation
\mathcal{M}_{\text{orig}}
.
By definition, a
shifted goal

G_{k}=\Gamma_{\text{goal}}(G_{k-1},\mathcal{S}_{\text{sem}},\mathcal{M}_{\text{repr}})=\Gamma^{k}_{\text{goal}}(G_{0},...)

(3)

is semantically equivalent (or as close as possible in semantic terms) but varied formulation of the goal in terms of domain objects required:

G_{k}\sim G_{0},\quad\forall k\in\{0,\dots,N\}.

The
shifted goal

G_{k}
is a variation of the original goal through the implicit representation
\mathcal{M}_{\text{repr}}
.

Goal Relaxation (Second dimension)

We introduce a comparison operator
\succ_{\text{rel}}
. Given two goals
G^{i}
and
G^{i-1}
,
G^{i}\succ_{\text{rel}}G^{i-1}
if the goal
G^{i}
is a semantically equivalent but
relaxed
(or less constrained, more general, more comprehensive, or more abstract) formulation of goal
G_{i-1}
. The
relaxation operator

G^{i}=\Delta_{\text{rel}}(G^{i-1},\mathcal{S}_{\text{sem}},\mathcal{M}_{\text{repr}})=\Delta^{i}_{\text{rel}}(G_{0},...).

(4)

yields a hierarchy of goals of increasing scope or abstraction, where each goal is a relaxed version of the previous one along the hierarchy.
Let
\,G^{0}
be the
initial goal
, the goal relaxation operator
\Delta_{\text{rel}}(\cdot)
induces a sequence of goals:

G^{m}\succ_{\text{rel}}\cdots\succ_{\text{rel}}G^{1}\succ_{\text{rel}}G^{0}

(5)

Combining the Two Dimensions

The application of a
relaxation

\Delta^{i}_{\text{rel}}
and a
shift

\Gamma^{k}_{\text{shift}}
to a given domain and goal pair
\langle\Sigma_{0},G_{0}\rangle
leads to a shifted and relaxed goal
G_{k}^{i}
and a shifted planning domain
\Sigma^{i}_{k}
, obtained by adapting the previous understanding of the operating environment through the representation
\mathcal{M}_{\text{repr}}
based on commonsense knowledge, to align it to a new relaxed planning problem.
We can now form an implicit two-dimensional grid of
planning problems
by combining each shifted domain
\Sigma_{k}
and relaxed goal
G^{i}
:

\mathcal{P}_{i,k}=\langle\Sigma^{i}_{k},G_{k}^{i}\rangle,\quad\forall k\in\{0,\dots,N\},\quad\forall i\in\{0,\dots,M\}.

(6)

Hence, each
\mathcal{P}_{i,k}
is a distinct planning problem having: (1) a domain specification
\Sigma^{i}_{k}
and (2) a goal specification
G^{i}_{k}
. A plan
\pi^{i}_{k}
must satisfy the goal
G^{i}_{k}

within
domain
\Sigma^{i}_{k}
.

Implicit relaxation graph

For each problem
\mathcal{P}_{k,i}
, we generate (1) an adapted planning
domain
specification (the set of objects, predicates and actions determined by
\Sigma^{i}_{k}
) and (2) a
shifted-relaxed
planning
problem

\mathcal{P}=\langle\Sigma^{i}_{k},G^{i}_{k}\rangle
. A planner is then invoked to compute a plan:

\pi_{i,k}\;=\;\mathrm{Plan}\bigl(\Sigma^{i}_{k},\,G^{i}_{k}\bigr).

(7)

These computed plans
\pi^{i}_{k}
are then executed or further analyzed by an implicit heuristic that steers the problem and domain adaptation in subsequent iterations.

\displaystyle\text{For the original goal }G_{0}:

(8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\langle\Sigma^{0}_{0},G^{0}_{0}\rangle}\;\;\rightarrow\;\;\mathbf{\langle\Sigma^{0}_{1},G^{0}_{1}\rangle}\;\;\rightarrow\;\;\cdots\;\;\rightarrow\;\;\langle\Sigma^{0}_{N},G^{0}_{N}\rangle,

\displaystyle\text{then for the next goal }G^{1}_{1}\succ_{\text{rel}}G^{0}_{1}:

\displaystyle\langle\Sigma^{1}_{0},G^{1}_{0}\rangle\;\;\rightarrow\;\;\mathbf{\langle\Sigma^{1}_{1},G^{1}_{1}\rangle}\;\;\rightarrow\;\;\mathbf{\langle\Sigma^{1}_{2},G^{1}_{2}\rangle}\;\;\rightarrow\;\;\cdots,

\displaystyle\vdots

\displaystyle\text{finally for }G^{m}_{N-1}\succ_{\text{rel}}\cdots\succ_{\text{rel}}G^{1}_{N-1}\succ_{\text{rel}}G^{0}_{N-1}:

\displaystyle\langle\Sigma^{m}_{0},G^{m}_{0}\rangle\rightarrow\ \cdots\rightarrow\ \mathbf{\langle\Sigma^{m}_{N-1},G^{m}_{N-1}\rangle}\rightarrow\ \mathbf{\langle\Sigma^{m}_{N},G^{m}_{N}\rangle}

In the first (horizontal) dimension of the implicit relaxation graph, the agent’s understanding of the environment is adjusted by modifying the planning domain and goal to a new domain, ensuring a solvable problem while preserving the original semantic intent. The second (vertical) dimension introduces a hierarchy of increasingly relaxed or generalized goals. The combination of these two dimensions yields a structured approach for handling semantic variability and hierarchical objective expansions in planning tasks, leveraging the intrinsic commonsense knowledge of a representation
\mathcal{M}_{\text{repr}}
, such as that offered by LLMs. In the proposed architecture, the exploration of this
relaxation graph
follows the order highlighted in bold in Eq.
8
, determined by a heuristic emergent from representation
\mathcal{M}_{\text{repr}}
itself, which guides the
shifting
and
relaxation
process. Problems are explored until a solvable instance is encountered, in which case an executable plan is returned. Moreover, this plan is already grounded in the environment being computed from a domain based on a 3DSG, which reflects the percepts of the robotic agent.

III-B

Architecture

In order to implement the
shift operators

\Gamma_{\text{shift}}
and
\Gamma_{\text{goal}}
, producing respectively a new shifted domain and goal, and the
relaxation operator

\Delta_{\text{rel}}
, which produces a relaxed goal, the proposed architecture (see Fig.
2
) exploits the emergent commonsense reasoning capabilities of LLMs, through the intrinsic representation
\mathcal{M}_{\text{repr}}
, following Alg.
1
.

Environment Representation

The agent’s operating environment is initially represented as a 3DSG
S
, acting as a structured representation of the agent’s perceptions. A distilled and semantically-enhanced graph
S_{\text{sem}}
is extrapolated, keeping only information about the location of objects in the scene, each tagged with a brief description.

Domain Generation

An initial symbolic model of the domain
\Sigma_{0}
, based on the agent’s skillset and the environment, can be provided a-priori. In case this is not possible, one can be generated from an enhanced scene graph
S_{\text{sem}}
, an initial goal
G_{0}
and a natural language description
\Sigma_{\text{desc}}
, through the
domain generation operator

\Gamma_{\text{dom}}
:

\Sigma_{0}=\Gamma_{\text{dom}}(G_{0},\mathcal{S}_{\text{sem}},\Sigma_{\text{desc}},\mathcal{M}_{\text{repr}})

(9)

This operator can be considered as the implementation of a sub-step of the
situational shifting operator

\Gamma^{k}
where the enhanced scene graph
S_{\text{sem}}
is provided as input instead of the initial domain, exploiting the implicit semantic representation
\mathcal{M}_{\text{repr}}
to map the representation
\mathcal{S}_{\text{sem}}
into the domain
\Sigma_{0}
.

Iterative Problem Refinement

The shifted planning problem
\mathcal{P}_{i,k}
is obtained through the
problem shifting operator
:

\mathcal{P}_{i,k}=\Gamma^{k}_{\text{prob}}(\Sigma_{0},G^{i}_{k},\mathcal{S}_{\text{sem}},\mathcal{M}_{\text{repr}})

(10)

Problem
\mathcal{P}_{i,k}
might not be immediately solvable, due to potential hallucinations introduced in the domain generation step or misalignments between any element of the tuple
\langle Obj,Pred,Act,InitState\rangle
and the ground information contained in
\mathcal{S}_{\text{sem}}
.
To maximize the semantic relevance of the domain to the current goal and its alignment to the environment, the
problem refinement operator

\Gamma_{\text{ref}}
uses common sense knowledge to iteratively correct the generated planning domain. A solution of the refined planning problem is therefore attempted with a symbolic planner to find a solution plan
\pi_{k,i}
. In case a solution is not found, any feedback obtained by the symbolic planning process is used in the subsequent
problem refinement
iteration, until a plan can be correctly generated. The problem refinement process can be considered a sub-step of the
situational shifting
operator
\Gamma^{k}
.
For example, after the application of
n
refinement steps to the domain
\Sigma^{i}_{k}
, the outcome is a problem refined
n
times:

\mathcal{P}^{n}_{i,k}=\Gamma_{\text{ref}}(G^{i}_{k},\Sigma^{i}_{k},\mathcal{S}_{\text{sem}},H_{fb},\mathcal{M}_{\text{repr}})

(11)

where the set
H_{fb}
contains natural language feedback obtained throughout the symbolic planning process. It should be noted that in this sub-step of the
situational shifting
operator, the goal
G_{k}
is not yet shifted. The planning process will conservatively attempt to modify the representation of the operating environment without modifying the goal, if possible, to avoid any unnecessary variation of the original task. We can decline the
domain shifting operator
, as the composition of a problem generation and refinement:

\Gamma^{k}_{\text{shift}}=(\Gamma^{k}_{\text{prob}}\circ\Gamma^{k}_{\text{ref}})

(12)

Plan Grounding Check

If a successful solution plan
\pi_{i,k}
has been computed, the correctness of the policy is evaluated by verifying the grounding of each action parameter, in an attempt to find a mapping between elements in domain
\Sigma^{i}_{k}
and the environment representation
S_{\text{sem}}
. If this process is successful, the plan is accepted, otherwise the situational shifting is complemented with a goal shifting and relaxation.

Goal Shifting and Relaxation

In case it is not possible to compute a groundable policy
\pi_{i,k}
, for the current planning problem
\mathcal{P}_{i,k}
, the
situational shifting
is completed by a
goal shifting
step, followed by a
goal relaxation

G^{i+1}_{k+1}=(\Gamma^{k}_{\text{goal}}\circ\Delta_{\text{rel}})(G^{i}_{k})
, which produces a shifted goal, further down in the relaxation hierarchy obtaining a shifted and relaxed planning problem
\mathcal{P}_{i+1,k+1}
.
The generation, refinement, grounding and relaxation process is then iteratively repeated, until a groundable plan is computed, as shown in Algorithm
1
.

Ultimately, it should be noted that in the current architecture, the
shifting operator
(the “lateral” movement in the grid of problems) is the composition of a domain generation sub-step, a problem shifting sub-step and a goal shifting sub-step:

\Gamma^{k}=(\Gamma^{k}_{\text{dom}}\circ\Gamma^{k}_{\text{shift}}\circ\Gamma^{k}_{\text{goal}})

(13)

III-C

Implementation

We represent the planning domain as a PDDL domain and problem pair. The
domain generation operator
consists of a variable number of sub-steps, at most two at the initial iteration, one for the generation of the PDDL domain and one for the PDDL problem.
In some experiments, a ground truth PDDL domain is provided, while in others, the domain is generated only in the first iteration to define the initial planning domain
\Sigma_{0}
.

The PDDL problem, however, is re-generated every time the goal is shifted or relaxed.
If a plan cannot be found, the solution undergoes validation by a symbolic validator and grounder, refining iteratively based on their feedback. The implementation, presented in Alg.
1
, comprises two nested loops: the outer loop produces increasingly relaxed goals and the corresponding symbolic planning problems and plans (if deemed feasible by the plan feasibility check function
Check
); the inner loop is aimed at iterative refining and validating the symbolic planning problem, using feedback about the syntax and coherence of the symbolic problem, from the PDDL validator (
Validate
). Once a solution plan is obtained, its correct grounding is verified in the 3DSG (
CheckGround
). If the grounding check fails, feedback on grounding issues (
ValidateGround
) is collected and used to correct the problem in subsequent iterations. Commonsense knowledge plays a key role in generating, refining, and shifting the problem, through properly engineered LLM prompts. By distilling and enhancing the provided environment representation
\mathcal{S}_{\text{sem}}
, removing useless data (such as spatial cues or 3D rendering data), and adding contextual information, the context length of LLM prompts is drastically reduced, with the desirable outcome of optimizing the results by minimizing hallucinations.
The
\Gamma^{k}_{\text{prob}}
operator uses one-shot prompting to steer the generation and correction of the planning domain. The domain generation operator
\Gamma_{\text{dom}}
is implemented by instructing the LLM to create a PDDL domain that satisfies the initial goal
G_{0}
within the distilled scene graph
S_{\text{sem}}
, with an example of an input and a desirable generated domain. Similarly, the problem generation step directs the LLM to generate a matching PDDL problem given a domain, goal, and scene graph. The
\Gamma_{\text{ref}}
problem refinement operator is implemented by prompting the LLM to properly react to the feedback from the validation,
H_{fb}
, by correcting the PDDL problem syntax, improving its logical coherence and its adherence to the operating environment. This is done by reasoning about errors before generating a corrected version. Like previous steps, input-output examples guide the process. To ensure compatibility, the LLM is instructed to adhere to a subset of the PDDL 1.2
[
29
]
formalism.

If a plan is not found,
the goal must be reformulated through shifting and relaxation.
Although plans are grounded in the environment representation, their correctness is verified by comparing the action arguments with the 3DSG objects, to detect LLM-induced hallucinations. Grounding feedback is then added to
H_{fb}
for the next iteration.
The goal shifting step
\Gamma_{\text{goal}}
, prompts the LLM to reformulate the goal using alternative objects in the scene. The goal relaxation step
\Delta_{\text{rel}}
, is implemented by instructing the LLM to reason about how to decompose the goal in single steps and to remove implicit restrictions, if needed, or retain the previous formulation is already feasible.

Experimental Results

This section introduces the dataset we release, the evaluation criteria, and the metrics adopted. Moreover, we provide a comparison with DELTA
[
26
]
, implementing the not-yet-released software, which is, to the best of our knowledge, the current state-of-the-art approach to combine planning and LLM reasoning, based on a 3DSG representation. We conclude this section by highlighting the limitations of our architecture.

IV-A

Dataset

Using LLMs for robotic task planning requires a comprehensive understanding of the operating environment. The widely used dataset by Armeni et al.
[
1
]
represents scenes in 3DSG form, enabling embodied agents to interact with real-world environments. However, the limited number of objects in these 3DSGs poses a challenge for LLM-based agents in generating effective plans for given tasks. To benchmark our architecture, we developed an extended dataset that builds on the original by incorporating additional objects into the scenes, following task families similar to those in DELTA. This expansion increases the environment’s complexity and scope, providing a more rigorous evaluation setting. We release the dataset as a resource for the scientific community to support comparisons across related approaches.
Our dataset is subdivided into several splits, each comprising a family of household tasks for which a PDDL domain is provided, as well as a natural language description of the goal and the domain structure. Each task in the split constitutes a variation around the primary theme of the main household task. A subset of the tasks is formulated to be impossible with the available objects, making it necessary to solve a relaxed version of the task, adapted to items available in the environment.
The dataset comprises 75 tasks across four scenes (Allensville, Kemblesville, Parole, Shelbiana) which are associated with five distinct domains, described below:

•

Dining Setup
: Tasks related to setting the table by placing tableware, with a varying type of meal or event.

•

House Cleaning
: The agent is required to collect rubbish and use a mop to clean the floor.

•

PC Assembly
: Problems related to gathering PC parts in a room to build a working PC.

•

Laundry
: Set of problems requiring to collect dirty clothes (if any), gather them in a washing machine, and operate them by properly using detergent or soap.

•

Office Setup
: The agent is required to move containers to another room, but it must first empty them, which is verifiable only through first-order logic with quantifiers.

IV-D

Discussion

The global averages shown in Table
I
demonstrate that our approach achieves better results on all tasks, both with and without domain generation. In half of our experiments, we explicitly check the correct grounding in the 3DSG, highlighting the ability of our model to compute pre-grounded plans.
While DELTA only considers a plan valid if it adheres to the generated PDDL problem (assuming that the 3DSG is faithfully reproduced by the PDDL domain generator), we perform an additional grounding check, to ensure plan actions reference existing domain objects in the 3DSG.
In case this check fails, we analyze the plan to provide feedback on grounding errors to the next generation iterations. This additional step is aimed at minimizing hallucinations introduced by the LLM throughout the generation process. Applying the same check on DELTA leads to failure in all experiments, due to a lack of control over hallucinations, conducting to the stratification of errors in the various generation steps.
A feedback loop to improve and refine the PDDL syntax and the overall logic of the domain formalization has proven essential to improve accuracy. This approach has been shown to correct errors that can occur in the initial phases, such as necessary object extraction, which is required to correctly achieve the goal. In our approach, the SR increased significantly as a result of this feedback loop.

In our experiments, we noticed that a single generation step is usually not enough to produce a coherent PDDL problem. For this reason, we iteratively refine the problem, using the textual feedback from the PDDL validation tools to provide instructions to the LLM on how to correct the issues with the planning domain formulation.
A frequent hallucination in the problem generation/refinement steps, appearing especially (but not exclusively) with the “Office Setup” domain, is the usage by the LLM of the “
connected
” predicate, to represent room connections. This aspect is not modeled in the ground-truth domains, as it is considered an unnecessary complication of the generated high-level plans. To prevent this hallucination we explicitly instructed the LLM to consider all rooms interconnected.
Our experiments show that goal relaxations improve the SR with respect to similar open-loop architectures.
According to the average relaxations and refinements metrics shown in Table
II
, the most complex tasks (in terms of planning time and expanded nodes) require more refinements and goal relaxations to complete successfully.

IV-E

Limitations and Future Directions

Our approach, like other LLM-based methods, can attempt to solve also impossible tasks, by relaxing the objective or finding a not adequate solution. Therefore, we performed an additional test by prompting the LLM to determine if the task could be completed using the available objects. The
SR w/ Possibility Check
in Table
I
is computed by considering automatically failed all those tasks that are deemed impossible by the LLM, highlighting a drop in SR. This indicates that our approach sometimes attempts to solve unfeasible tasks, highlighting its limitations in handling truly impossible problems.

Despite this, the results demonstrate how our approach achieves robust performance in generating domain and problem descriptions in PDDL from a textual representation of the scene, consistently producing reasonable and feasible outputs.
However, occasional hallucinations can lead to inaccuracies.
The proposed relaxation technique manages in effectively scaling the complexity of problems that are unsolvable by adapting the goal to the given environment.
Future work will further explore the properties of the currently implicit “
relaxation graph
”, to achieve more systematic and reliable approaches that maximize the goal feasibility, adapting it to the agent’s operating environment, while still recognizing the global impossibility of the assigned goal.

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we tackled the problem of planning for embodied agents in real-world environments, where high-dimensional action spaces and incomplete representations can hinder conventional planning approaches. By integrating the domain-independent flexibility of LLMs with the rigor of classical planning, we proposed a novel, two-dimensional mechanism for task adaptation and goal relaxation, preserving high-level objectives while refining tasks to match the agent’s operational constraints. Our hierarchical approach, leveraging contextual understanding and open-vocabulary reasoning, demonstrates that real-world planning problems can be tackled effectively.

Through qualitative and quantitative evaluations, the proposed approach achieves a robust Success Rate even under challenging conditions, outperforming state-of-the-art approaches for planning with 3D Scene Graphs. Additionally, we release our dataset to bring advantages to the research community.

These results confirm that the bi-directional feasibility formalism not only enhances plan execution in resource-constrained and uncertain settings but also provides a scalable pathway for bridging the gap between high-level instructions and low-level robotic actions. This framework holds promise for robotic applications, especially in expanding the applicability of FMs to a broader array of real-world tasks, since in all these applications,
context matters
.
