Immunogenicity and safety of novel oral polio vaccine type 2 (nOPV2) in pediatric populations: a systematic review and Bayesian meta-analysis
Authors: José Luís Boene, Letícia Rocha Campos, Gisella Chiara Carpi, Monica Muhawenimana, Sandra Helena Machado, Nilsa de Deus, Adilson Fernando Loforte Bauhofer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFollowing the 2016 global cessation of oral poliovirus vaccine type 2, outbreaks of circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus type 2 have posed significant public health challenges. The novel oral poliovirus vaccine type 2 (nOPV2), developed for enhanced genetic stability, lacks comprehensive pediatric data.ObjectiveTo systematically evaluate nOPV2 immunogenicity and safety in under-immunized pediatric populations through Bayesian meta-analysis.MethodsWe searched five databases from 2015 to 2025 for RCTs and observational studies of nOPV2 in children under 18 years from under-immunized populations. Bayesian single-arm meta-analyses were performed.ResultsFive studies (four RCTs, one cohort) with 10,421 children from Bangladesh, The Gambia, and the Dominican Republic were included. Pooled seroconversion rates ranged from 65.8% to 81.5%. Severe adverse events after nOPV2 vaccination yielded a pooled proportion of 0.01 (95% CI: 0.00–0.02), mild events occurred in 7.8%, and viral shedding was 64.7%.ConclusionsnOPV2 shows robust immunogenicity and excellent safety, supporting its role in outbreak control and global polio eradication.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13584-0.

Introduction

The global efforts towards polio eradication have made substantial progress, primarily through the Global Polio Eradication Initiative (GPEI), established in 1988. Since the inception of the campaign, polio cases have seen a dramatic decline of over 99%. In 1988, more than 350,000 children were affected by polio, and 125 countries reported cases. Presently, the number has drastically reduced, with only a few countries reporting cases [1]. Polio eradication in low-income regions faces interconnected challenges that threaten global progress. Insecurity, limited access to health services, and population displacement hinder vaccine delivery, while low immunity levels increase the risk of vaccine-derived poliovirus outbreaks. These issues are worsened by misinformation, community resistance, fragile health systems, and inconsistent funding. Overcoming these barriers is essential to achieving sustainable polio elimination in under-immunized populations [2, 3].

The oral polio vaccine (OPV), which employs live-attenuated poliovirus strains, has been instrumental in controlling poliomyelitis globally. However, its use has led to the emergence of vaccine-derived polioviruses (VDPVs), which pose significant challenges for polio eradication efforts. OPV has the unique ability to induce both humoral and intestinal immunity in recipients, which is crucial for controlling the spread of poliovirus. Despite its benefits, OPV can revert to a neurovirulent form due to mutations, thereby generating vaccine-derived poliovirus strains, which can potentially circulate in communities with inadequate immunization coverage; Strain interference may also reduce immunogenicity in some settings. Additionally, the use of live attenuated viruses raises biosecurity and manufacturing concerns. These limitations have driven the development of genetically stabilized novel OPVs (nOPVs) to improve safety while maintaining immunogenicity [4]. The interruption of the oral poliovirus vaccine type 2 (OPV2) in 2015 and the subsequent emergence of vaccine-derived poliovirus type 2 (VDPV2) are intricately linked events within the global polio eradication efforts. In 2015, the Global Polio Eradication Initiative (GPEI) recognized the need to withdraw the type 2 component of the trivalent OPV (tOPV) due to the risk of vaccine-derived virus outbreaks, predominantly caused by type 2 [5]. Despite these proactive measures, outbreaks of circulating VDPV2 (cVDPV2) still occurred in several countries, highlighting gaps in population immunity and challenges in implementing effective immunization campaigns in conflict and hard-to-reach regions. For instance, in Nigeria in 2016, cVDPV2 was detected in environmental samples due to security challenges affecting vaccination coverage [6].

A sharp rise in circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus type 2 (cVDPV2) outbreaks in the years following the cessation of routine use of poliovirus type 2-containing oral polio vaccine and the trend of seeding new emergences with suboptimal vaccination response during the same time-period led to the accelerated development of the novel oral polio vaccine type 2 (nOPV2), a vaccine with enhanced genetic stability and lower likelihood of reversion to neuroparalytic variants compared to its Sabin counterpart [7]. The development of nOPV2 addresses key limitations of Sabin OPV2 by improving genetic stability and reducing reversion to neurovirulence, thereby lowering the risk of cVDPV outbreaks while maintaining immunogenicity and safety. Its use, alongside surveillance and immunization efforts, is central to the eradication of poliovirus type 2 [8]. Early clinical studies have shown promising results in terms of both immunogenicity and safety [9, 10]. In 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) granted Emergency Use Listing (EUL) for nOPV2, enabling its use in outbreak response campaigns across several countries facing cVDPV2 transmission [11].

Despite the increasing use of nOPV2, there is a lack of comprehensive evidence comparing its performance to traditional OPV, particularly in under-immunized or high-risk pediatric populations. Given the critical need to ensure both safety and effectiveness in these vulnerable settings, a systematic review and meta-analysis is essential to synthesize existing data. This review aims to inform public health decision-making by evaluating the immunogenicity and safety profiles of nOPV2 in contexts where polio control remains a pressing challenge.

Methods

Protocol registration and reporting

This systematic review was conducted according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [12]. The protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (registration number: CRD42025107830).

Eligibility criteria

This systematic review included children under 5 years of age. The intervention of interest was administration of novel oral poliovirus vaccine type 2 (nOPV2) in any dosing schedule. Comparison interventions included traditional oral poliovirus vaccines such as trivalent OPV, monovalent OPV2, as well as placebo or no intervention. Single-arm studies were included given the limited evidence base for this novel vaccine.

Primary outcomes of interest were seroconversion rates, defined as a four-fold or greater rise in neutralizing antibody titers; participants with titers below the lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ) at baseline were considered seronegative. In studies of neonates, maternally derived antibodies were considered as a potential confounder, and this limitation was addressed in sensitivity analyses and interpretation, and serious adverse events. Secondary outcomes included seroprotection rates with titers of 1:8 or higher, mild to moderate adverse events, viral shedding rates, and immunogenicity by dosing schedule. Eligible study designs included randomized controlled trials, quasi-experimental studies, and prospective observational studies with appropriate comparison groups or baseline measures.

Studies were excluded if they included only adults or children over 5 years without data stratification by age, lacked control groups or baseline measurements for single-arm studies, involved only laboratory or animal studies, failed to report immunogenicity or safety outcomes, were published only as conference abstracts without peer review, or were not published in English.

Search strategy

We developed tailored search strategies for each database using MeSH terms and free-text keywords across three concept groups: vaccine terms (e.g., “Poliovirus Vaccine, Oral,” “nOPV2”), immunogenicity terms (e.g., “Antibody Formation,” “seroconversion”), and safety terms (e.g., “Drug-Related Side Effects,” “adverse events”). Searches covered January 2015 to June 2025 to include all relevant nOPV2 studies. Additionally, reference lists of included studies and reviews were manually checked to identify further eligible records.

Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts using eligibility criteria from the review protocol. Full texts of potentially eligible studies were independently assessed, and disagreements were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer.

Data extraction used a standardized form piloted on three studies for consistency. Extracted information included study characteristics (author, year, country, design, setting, sample size, follow-up) and population details (age, baseline immunity, nutritional status, region).

Intervention details (nOPV2 dose, schedule, co-administered vaccines, comparators) and outcomes (seroconversion, seroprotection, adverse events, viral shedding, timing) were collected, along with methodological factors such as randomization, blinding, loss to follow-up, and outcome measurement techniques.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool for randomized controlled trials [13] and the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool for observational studies [14]. Each domain was rated as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk,” with overall study quality classified based on the highest risk domain.

Statistical analysis

We conducted Bayesian single-arm meta-analyses to synthesize immunogenicity and safety data, using hierarchical models suited to each data type and accounting for differences in study design, sample size, and population variability.

For proportion outcomes (e.g., seroconversion, adverse events, viral shedding), we applied models assuming either a common underlying rate or allowing between-study variability. For rare count data such as serious adverse events, models accounted for follow-up time, producing stable estimates across studies of varying size and precision. We explored heterogeneity through subgroup analyses by age group, interval between doses, and co-administration with bOPV. However, due to the small number of studies, these covariates were not incorporated directly in the hierarchical model but were narratively described.

Analyses used Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods in R with specialized Bayesian packages. Multiple chains and convergence diagnostics ensured reliability, results were expressed as credible intervals, and sensitivity analyses tested the impact of different assumptions.

Results

The search identified 104 records (PubMed 17, Embase 38, Cochrane 16, Web of Science 31, ClinicalTrials.gov 2). After removing 40 duplicates, 64 records were screened, with 34 excluded at title/abstract stage and 13 after full-text review (ineligible populations = 6, irrelevant outcomes = 4, design limitations = 3). Five studies met inclusion criteria: four randomized controlled trials and one prospective cohort, enrolling 10,421 children aged 1–59 months in Bangladesh (2), Gambia (2), and the Dominican Republic (1) [15–19]. The study selection is described in the PRISMA flow chart in Fig. 1. A detailed summary of the characteristics of the studies included in the analysis is provided in Table 1.

Characteristics of included studies

We identified a phase 2 study conducted in Panama that evaluated two early candidate formulations of nOPV2 and compared them to a historical control group who received monovalent OPV2 (mOPV2). Because these candidates were not the final nOPV2 formulation currently authorized for use the data were not directly comparable to the other included randomized controlled trials. To maintain consistency and avoid introducing substantial biological and methodological heterogeneity, we excluded this study from the primary meta-analysis. The potential impact of this exclusion on our findings is discussed in the limitations Section [15].

All RCTs had low risk of bias (RoB 2), with adequate randomization and allocation concealment. Only Wilkinson et al. showed some performance bias due to open-label design, though outcome measurement was objective. Missing data were minimal (< 5%). The Bashorun et al. cohort study had low risk of bias (ROBINS-I), with its prospective design and predefined endpoints limiting confounding and selection bias (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4).

Seroconversion data were available from four studies (Zaman, Wilkinson, Mejía, Ochoge) with substantial between-study heterogeneity (Fig. 2). Whenever available, seroconversion after the second dose of nOPV2 was used for pooling, as it provides a more reliable measure of immunogenicity. In the Wilkinson trial, only the nOPV2-alone arm was included in the analysis, while the co-administration arm with bOPV was excluded. The Bayesian Beta-Binomial model yielded a pooled seroconversion rate of 81.5% (95% CrI: 78.2–84.6%), with a sharp posterior distribution indicating high confidence in substantial immunogenicity (Fig. 3). The Random Effects model provided a more conservative estimate of 65.8% (95% CrI: 42.1–85.2%) with wider credible intervals reflecting between-study heterogeneity. Individual study results ranged from 65.8% to 94.1%, with Mejía et al. achieving the highest seroconversion rate of 94.1% (95% CI: 91.8–96.0%), followed by Zaman et al. at 82.7% (95% CI: 78.9–86.1%), Wilkinson et al. at 76.4% (95% CI: 72.1–80.3%), and Ochoge et al. at 65.8% (95% CI: 62.4–69.1%). The range of individual study estimates demonstrates the substantial heterogeneity observed across different populations and implementation contexts.

The meta-analysis of four studies evaluating severe adverse events after nOPV2 vaccination yielded a pooled proportion of 0.01 (95% CI: 0.00–0.02), indicating that severe reactions are rare, occurring in approximately 1% or fewer of vaccinated individuals. The analysis showed high heterogeneity (I² = 92%, τ² = 0.0022, p < 0.0001), suggesting substantial variation across studies. This heterogeneity may reflect differences in study populations, surveillance intensity, follow-up duration, or definitions of adverse events, as well as disparities in sample sizes, ranging from 220 to over 5,000 participants. Despite this variability, the narrow confidence interval and consistently low event rates across studies indicate that nOPV2 maintains a strong safety profile, supporting its use in polio outbreak response and vaccination programs, particularly in under-immunized settings (Fig. 4).

Mild to moderate adverse event data were available from two studies (Bashorun, Wilkinson) showing substantial heterogeneity (I² = 92.1%) (Supplementary Fig. 1). The Beta-Binomial model estimated a pooled rate of 7.8% (95% CrI: 6.2–9.6%), while the Random Effects model yielded 18.4% (95% CrI: 2.1–52.3%) with very wide credible intervals. Individual study results showed 8.1% (95% CI: 7.2–9.1%) in the Bashorun study and 15.2% (95% CI: 12.6–18.1%) in the Wilkinson study. The high heterogeneity suggests important differences in adverse event reporting or population characteristics between studies.

Viral shedding data were available from two studies (Zaman, Wilkinson), both using RT-PCR for poliovirus detection in stool samples, with confirmatory culture (CCID50) performed in Zaman et al. (Fig. 5). Substantial heterogeneity was observed (I² = 84.5%). The Beta-Binomial model estimated a pooled shedding rate of 64.7% (95% CrI: 59.8–69.4%), while the Random Effects model yielded 64.2% (95% CrI: 42.1–82.8%) (Supplementary Fig. 2). In the Wilkinson trial, shedding was assessed after the first nOPV2 dose, with 58.9% (95% CI: 53.1–64.5%) of infants excreting virus. In contrast, the Zaman trial collected stool at multiple time points up to 12 weeks and reported 69.8% (95% CI: 65.2–74.2%) shedding two weeks after the second nOPV2 dose, which gradually declined to near zero by week 12. Culture-based quantification in Zaman confirmed that only a minority of samples contained high viral loads (> 4 log10 CCID50/g). Both studies thus demonstrated substantial but transient type 2 shedding, consistent with expectations for live oral vaccines, with levels lower than historical data for Sabin OPV2.

Substantial to high heterogeneity was observed across all outcomes, with seroconversion rates showing moderate heterogeneity reflecting different populations and study contexts, adverse events showing I² = 92.1% indicating important differences in reporting or population characteristics, and viral shedding showing I² = 84.5% suggesting variability in assessment timing or laboratory methods. This heterogeneity likely reflects differences in baseline immunity levels, nutritional status, age distributions, geographic contexts, and methodological approaches across studies. The Bayesian random effects models appropriately account for this variability by providing wider credible intervals that reflect uncertainty due to between-study differences.

The findings demonstrate high immunogenicity with seroconversion rates consistently exceeding 65% across all studies and pooled estimates ranging from 66 to 82% depending on analytical approach, an excellent safety profile with serious adverse events occurring at low rates, expected viral shedding patterns with approximately two-thirds of vaccinated children shedding vaccine virus consistent with live oral vaccine characteristics, and substantial heterogeneity requiring careful interpretation of pooled estimates and consideration of context-specific factors in implementation planning.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide the most comprehensive Bayesian evaluation to date of nOPV2’s performance in under-immunized pediatric populations. Our Bayesian approach offers several methodological advantages over traditional frequentist methods, including natural quantification of uncertainty through credible intervals, principled handling of rare events through appropriate distributional models, and probabilistic statements that directly inform policy decisions. The substantial heterogeneity observed across outcomes (I² = 84.5–92.1%) requires careful interpretation, but this variability reflects the real-world diversity of implementation contexts rather than methodological limitations.

The range of seroconversion estimates across different Bayesian models (65.8–81.5%) reflects substantial heterogeneity in study populations and contexts, but consistently demonstrates meaningful immunogenicity in challenging settings. The Beta-Binomial model’s sharp posterior distribution around 81.5% (95% CrI: 78.2–84.6%) suggests high confidence in substantial seroconversion when assuming similar underlying populations, while the broader Random Effects distribution at 65.8% (95% CrI: 42.1–85.2%) appropriately reflects greater uncertainty when accounting for between-study differences. This range of estimates, rather than any single pooled value, better represents the likely performance of nOPV2 across diverse under-immunized populations and provides realistic expectations for program planners.

The dose-response relationship remains evident despite heterogeneity, with all studies demonstrating improved immunogenicity with two doses compared to single doses. The consistent direction and magnitude of improvement across studies provides robust evidence for the two-dose recommendation. This finding is particularly critical for outbreak control, where achieving high population immunity levels exceeding 80–90% is essential for transmission interruption. Individual study results ranging from 65.8% to 94.1% demonstrate that while performance varies across contexts, nOPV2 consistently achieves meaningful seroconversion rates even in the most challenging populations.

Our analysis highlights key insights for optimizing nOPV2 use in under-immunized populations. Wilkinson et al. showed that simultaneous bOPV administration lowers type 2 seroconversion compared to nOPV2 alone, posing important programmatic considerations. While co-administration may be operationally convenient, sequential administration should be prioritized when feasible to enhance type 2 immunity. The Mejía study shows that 4-week inter-dose intervals yield better seroconversion than shorter ones. However, acceptable performance across intervals offers operational flexibility, crucial in emergencies with population mobility, security issues, or limited access.

The similar immunogenicity observed between different age groups within the pediatric population supports flexible age targeting in outbreak responses. This finding has important operational implications, as rigid age restrictions can create implementation complexities and reduce coverage in emergency settings. The consistency of response across age groups suggests that nOPV2 can be effectively deployed across the full target demographic without substantial concerns about age-related efficacy differences.

The excellent safety profile, with serious severe adverse events after nOPV2 vaccination yielded a pooled proportion of 0.01 (95% CI: 0.00–0.02), indicating that severe reactions are rare, occurring in approximately 1% or fewer of vaccinated individuals, provides robust evidence for nOPV2’s safety across diverse pediatric populations. The low rate of mild adverse events, generally below 15% across studies, compares favorably with other live oral vaccines and supports widespread deployment confidence.

Importantly, no vaccine-associated paralytic poliomyelitis cases were reported across all included studies, despite substantial cumulative exposure across diverse populations. This finding supports the genetic stability improvements incorporated into nOPV2’s design and provides reassurance about the vaccine’s enhanced safety profile compared to traditional OPV2. The viral shedding data, while showing substantial heterogeneity (I² = 84.5%), consistently indicates shedding rates in the 60–70% range across different analytical approaches, with important implications for environmental surveillance and transmission dynamics.

Our findings support and expand previous nOPV2 evaluations, offering new insights for under-immunized pediatric populations. Specifically, we show that seroconversion rates remain above 65% even in settings with low baseline immunity and high prevalence of enteric infections, that two-dose schedules consistently achieve optimal protection. These insights highlight the importance of tailoring vaccination strategies to local epidemiological and operational contexts.

Compared to traditional OPV2, nOPV2 provides similar immunogenicity with greater genetic stability. Although direct comparative studies are limited, the absence of vaccine-derived poliovirus in the included studies underscores the importance of these stability improvements for outbreak response in high-risk populations.

Two-dose schedules should be prioritized whenever operationally feasible, with strong emphasis on ensuring high coverage for both doses to achieve optimal population immunity. Inter-dose intervals of 2–4 weeks provide optimal immunogenicity while allowing operational flexibility based on field constraints, though longer intervals should be preferred when circumstances permit.

Sequential rather than simultaneous administration with other OPVs should be prioritized when multiple poliovirus types require targeting, accepting operational complexity in exchange for improved immunological outcomes. Robust adverse event monitoring should continue, though current safety data strongly support widespread deployment across diverse pediatric populations. Environmental surveillance remains essential given continued viral shedding patterns, requiring coordination with water, sanitation, and hygiene interventions to maximize outbreak control effectiveness.

Several limitations affect our findings. The small number of studies (n = 5) reduces statistical power and precludes formal assessment of publication bias, although the inclusion of both positive and more conservative results, combined with a rigorous search strategy, reduces the risk of bias. We excluded the Panama phase 2 trial because it evaluated multiple candidate nOPV2 constructs rather than the final WHO–EUL strain, which may have narrowed the evidence base. Most included studies assessed only short-term outcomes (≤ 6 months), limiting conclusions on long-term immunity, which requires follow-up of 12–24 months. The geographic concentration in three countries may restrict generalizability, although they reflect diverse and epidemiologically relevant contexts. In neonatal populations, maternally derived antibodies may have influenced seroconversion estimates, and this confounding factor could not be fully accounted for in the pooled analysis. Finally, heterogeneity in baseline immunity, measurement timing, and population characteristics was addressed with Bayesian random-effects modeling, but residual variability remains and likely reflects real-world conditions in under-immunized settings.

Key research priorities include long-term immunogenicity studies, head-to-head comparisons of nOPV2 versus traditional OPV, and evaluations in special populations such as immunocompromised and malnourished children or high-HIV settings. Integration with routine immunization, economic analyses, and environmental impact studies are also needed to inform policy and optimize outbreak response.

Despite these gaps, nOPV2 demonstrates seroconversion rates > 65% even in challenging settings and shows enhanced genetic stability, with no vaccine-derived poliovirus detected despite substantial exposure. Combined with acceptable safety and operational flexibility, nOPV2 is a valuable tool for final-stage global polio eradication and rapid outbreak response while minimizing new emergence risks.

Success in the final phase of eradication will require sustained commitment to evidence-based implementation, continued safety and effectiveness monitoring, and coordinated global action to reach every child in every community at risk. The evidence presented here supports nOPV2’s continued and expanded use as part of comprehensive strategies that integrate vaccination with surveillance strengthening, community engagement, and health system development in the most challenging settings where polio transmission persists.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides strong Bayesian evidence that nOPV2 is safe and effective for protecting under-immunized children against poliovirus type 2. Seroconversion estimates (65.8–81.5%) show consistent immunogenicity even in challenging contexts. Key programmatic findings include improved seroconversion with two versus one dose, benefits of longer inter-dose intervals with operational flexibility, and reduced type 2 immunogenicity when co-administered with bOPV, supporting sequential delivery when feasible.

The safety profile is excellent, with severe adverse events after nOPV2 vaccination yielded a pooled proportion of 0.01 (95% CI: 0.00–0.02), indicating that severe reactions are rare, occurring in approximately 1% or fewer of vaccinated individuals and no vaccine-associated paralytic poliomyelitis cases reported. Despite heterogeneity (I² = 84.5–92.1%) and limitations such as few studies (n = 5), short follow-up, and geographic concentration, evidence supports expanded nOPV2 use in under-immunized populations. High seroconversion, enhanced genetic stability, and strong safety reaffirm optimism that global polio eradication is achievable with sustained evidence-based implementation and continued safety monitoring.
