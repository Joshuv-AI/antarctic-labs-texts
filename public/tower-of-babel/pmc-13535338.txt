The Proctitis Paradox: Refractory Ulcerative Proctitis Associated With Development of Disease Complications in a Multicenter South Australian Cohort
Authors: Sreecanth S Raja, Samuel P Costello, Alexandra Sadler, Nicholas Wan, Bhuwan Tandon, Dharshan Sathananthan, Kate D Lynch, Chris K Rayner, Suzanne Edwards, Robert V Bryant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDespite limited extent, patients with ulcerative proctitis (UP) can suffer from debilitating symptoms, complications, and risk of disease extension. The aim was to provide a contemporary real‐world insight into the management and outcomes of UP.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted of UP patients attending three tertiary hospitals in Adelaide, South Australia, between 2015 and 2024. Demographic, treatment, and disease‐related data were captured. Treatment success was defined as clinical response at last follow up. The primary outcome was the development of disease‐related complications.ResultsOf 185 patients with UP, 60.5% were female, mean age at diagnosis was 41.1 years (±14.2) and median duration of follow up was 4.18 years (IQR 2.0–7.3 years). Most patients (n = 114, 62.3%) achieved treatment success with 5‐ASA therapy, but one third (n = 56, 30.3%) developed refractory UP. For those with refractory UP, treatment success was achieved in 45% of patients using systemic immunomodulators (n = 40) and in 59% using advanced therapies (n = 27). Topical tacrolimus was used in 10 patients, of whom three achieved treatment success. Overall, 34 patients (18.4%) developed either proximal disease extension (n = 22, 11.9%) or traditional UC related complications including hospitalization (n = 15, 8.1%) and colectomy (n = 1, 0.05%). The presence of refractory UP was associated with development of traditional UC related complications (p < 0.0001).ConclusionDespite a short segment of inflammation, UP remains a clinical conundrum as many patients develop refractory disease, disease extension, and complications. Further prospective studies are required to define optimal therapeutic strategies for this under‐recognized cohort.Keywords: biologic therapies, refractory proctitis, topical tacrolimus, ulcerative colitis, ulcerative proctitis

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the colon that typically manifests with rectal bleeding and fecal urgency. Patients are classified based on disease distribution as having either pancolitis, left‐sided colitis, or ulcerative proctitis (UP). UP is the anatomically limited subset of UC wherein inflammation is limited to the rectum [1].

In comparison with anatomically extensive UC, UP is associated with a lower risk of disease‐related complications such as hospitalization, colectomy, and colorectal cancer [2]. This has contributed to historical perceptions of UP as a benign and easily treatable condition [3]. However, individuals with proctitis can also suffer from debilitating symptoms of urgency and rectal bleeding akin to more extensive disease [4, 5]. Moreover, UP is a dynamic condition prone to proximal disease extension, with 53% of UP patients demonstrating extension to left‐sided or pancolitis over 25 years of follow‐up in a Danish longitudinal cohort study [6].

Topically administered 5‐aminosalicylates (5‐ASA) such as mesalazine are recommended as first line therapy in UP [7, 8]. Mesalazine suppositories are more efficacious in ameliorating UP disease activity than orally administered preparations [8, 9, 10]. Nevertheless, the addition of oral mesalazine and/or topical corticosteroids is recommended for patients who do not respond to topical mesalazine. While these treatments are effective, a subset of patients will have persistently active disease and are defined as having refractory ulcerative proctitis [11].

Treatment paradigms for refractory UP are not well established. International consensus guidelines typically recommend commencement of systemically acting immunosuppressive therapies, although prospective clinical evidence guiding their use is limited. Furthermore, there is also limited evidence to guide the choice of advanced therapies for refractory UP, partly because of the historical exclusion of this patient cohort from registration clinical trials [12]. The role of rectally administered therapies in the treatment of refractory proctitis is also of interest given the safety benefits of topical therapy.

This study aimed to address the lack of evidence to guide therapy by completing an updated real‐world study assessing treatment outcomes and disease course in a multi‐center cohort of patients with UP.

Methods

Study Setting

This study was conducted at three tertiary hospitals in South Australia: The Queen Elizabeth Hospital (QEH), Royal Adelaide Hospital (RAH) and Lyell McEwin Hospital (LMH).

Study Cohort

All patients with an initial diagnosis of UP between 2015 and 2024 who had been reviewed in the outpatient, endoscopy, or inpatient setting at participating tertiary healthcare centers were considered for inclusion.

Data Collection

De‐identified data were extracted from electronic medical records, medical notes and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools [13, 14]. UP was defined according to the Montreal classification as UC with inflammation limited to the rectum, which was confirmed using endoscopic and histological reports where available [1]. Individuals were excluded if there was a prior history of colonic surgery or proximal UC.

Data collated from electronic medical records included demographics, smoking status, time of initial UC diagnosis and follow up, disease severity at diagnosis as graded by Mayo endoscopic subscore (MES) and UC endoscopic index score (UCEIS), efficacy and safety of UC treatment modalities, and development of UC disease related complications. Treatment success with each therapeutic modality was defined as continuation of therapy with documented clinical remission and/or biomarker/endoscopic evidence of remission (defined by fecal calprotectin < 150, mayo endoscopic subscore of ≤ 1) at last follow up. Patients were deemed as having refractory proctitis if they had ongoing evidence of active disease despite treatment with oral and topical 5‐ASA and topical and/or oral corticosteroids [15, 16, 17]. Active disease was determined by clinical documentation of ongoing symptoms of persistent rectal bleeding ± urgency and/or objective evidence of inflammation (e.g., elevated fecal calprotectin, endoscopic activity) where available. Given the retrospective nature of the study and to account for the possibility of alternative sources of corticosteroid or 5‐ASA prescribing, escalation to advanced therapy or additional non‐5ASA topical therapy was also used as a pragmatic marker of treatment nonresponse and was considered sufficient to meet the definition of refractory proctitis.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the development of disease‐related complications, defined as either proximal disease extension or traditional UC complications (hospitalization due to flare/acute severe UC, colectomy or colorectal malignancy) during the follow‐up period. Disease extension represents a pivotal event in UP due to its prognostic implications, including heightened malignancy risk necessitating participation in colorectal cancer surveillance programs and elevated risk of disease flares [2].

Several secondary outcomes were evaluated, including: (i) the percentage of patients who developed refractory proctitis; (ii) the efficacy and safety of therapeutic modalities used in refractory proctitis; (iii) factors associated specifically with proximal disease extension; and (iv) factors associated with the development of traditional UC‐related complications.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics included baseline demographic and clinical data. Categorical data are presented as numbers and percentages. Continuous data are expressed as either mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) for normal and nonnormally distributed data respectively. Categorical variables were compared using chi‐squared and Fisher's exact test. Quantitative variables were compared with either unpaired t‐tests or Mann Whitney U tests.

Univariate binary logistic regression was conducted to examine the associations of proximal disease extension and UC‐related complications with demographic and clinical variables. Kaplan–Meier curve survival analysis was performed to demonstrate the proportion of patients with complication‐free survival over time following initial diagnosis. For those who did not develop complications, the observation was censored at the time of last follow up.

Ethical Approval

This study was approved by the Central Adelaide Health Network Research and Ethics Committee (Reference Number: 18871 & 18872) and Northern Adelaide Health Network (NALHN) HREC (Reference number: 16860). Waiver of informed consent was granted given retrospective, de‐identified data.

For those with available baseline endoscopic data (n = 87, 47%), mean endoscopic Mayo score was 1.86 (±0.66). Development of Complications

Thirty four patients (18.4%) reached the primary outcome of developing disease complications (Figure 1). Specifically, proximal disease extension to left‐sided colitis or pancolitis occurred in 22 patients (11.9%); 15 patients (8.1%) were hospitalized for treatment of acute UC flares; one patient (0.5%) underwent colectomy for medically refractory UP; and no patients were diagnosed with colorectal cancer during the follow‐up period.

Development of complications in multicenter UC Cohort (n = 185).

Refractory UP

Refractory proctitis occurred in 56 patients (30.3%). Of these, 21 developed disease complications. The median time from UC diagnosis to development of refractory proctitis was 36 months (IQR 17–71.5 months). Endoscopic confirmation of refractory proctitis was obtained in 40 patients (71.4%).

Factors Associated With Disease Complications

Factors associated with the development of proximal disease extension and UC disease‐related complications were assessed (Tables 2 and 3). The presence of refractory UP was strongly associated with the development of UC disease‐related complications (OR 11.45, 95% CI 3.09–42.49, p < 0.001) but not proximal disease extension specifically (OR 2.12, 95% CI 0.86–5.24, p 0.10). No other demographic or disease‐related factors were significantly associated with either outcome of interest. These findings remained consistent in sensitivity analyzes excluding patients who received FMT (Tables S1 and S2). Treatments for UP

5‐Aminosalicylate Therapy

Almost all patients were prescribed 5‐ASA therapy (n = 184, 99.4%). Stratifying 5‐ASA use by route of administration, 154 patients (83%) were treated with topical 5‐ASA, 148 (80%) were treated with oral 5‐ASA while 117 (63.2%) were treated with a combination of oral and topical 5‐ASA therapy. 5‐ASA therapy was successful in 114 patients (62.3%), in whom there was no subsequent need for escalation in UC medical therapy (Table 4).

Corticosteroid Therapy

Overall, 85 patients (56%) received treatment with either oral or topical corticosteroid therapy. Of these, 35% experienced treatment success and did not require escalation to maintenance immunosuppressive therapy. Topical steroids were used in 39 patients (21.1%) while 63 (34.1%) received oral corticosteroids. A combination of oral and topical corticosteroids was used in 15 patients (8.1%) (Table 4).

Thiopurine Therapy

Overall, 38 patients (20.5%) were commenced on thiopurine therapy. Of these, 36 patients received azathioprine while two received 6‐mercaptopurine (6‐MP). Thiopurines were utilized as a monotherapy in 35 patients (92.1%).

Treatment success was observed in 15 patients (45.4%) treated with azathioprine monotherapy and in both patients (100%) commenced on 6‐mercaptopurine.

ADRs were reported in 14 patients treated with azathioprine, of whom 11 required drug discontinuation. ADRs were categorized as mild–moderate in 12 patients. The serious ADRs included pancreatitis (n = 1) and serum sickness reaction (n = 1).

Methotrexate

Four patients received methotrexate treatment. Two patients utilized methotrexate in combination with TNF‐I therapy, while one was treated with methotrexate for concomitant inflammatory arthropathy. The remaining patient received methotrexate monotherapy primarily as a treatment for refractory UP but did not achieve treatment success.

Advanced Therapies

Overall, 27 patients (14.6%) received advanced therapies for the treatment of refractory UP. While 18 patients received only one advanced therapy, eight received multiple advanced therapies. Of these, six patients received two advanced therapies, and three patients received three.

Vedolizumab

Vedolizumab was used in 15 patients (8.1%), of whom 10 received vedolizumab as first‐line advanced therapy. Twelve patients received 8 weekly IV vedolizumab infusions, while three patients received dose‐escalated 4‐weekly IV infusions. Eight patients (53.3%) had sustained treatment success with vedolizumab at last follow‐up. In the remainder (46.7%), the median time to treatment failure was 22 months. One patient developed paraesthesia with vedolizumab necessitating discontinuation.

TNF‐Alpha Inhibitor Therapy

TNF‐alpha inhibitor (TNF‐I) therapy was used as the first‐line advanced therapy in 14 patients (7.6%). Of these, 12 commenced infliximab and two were treated with adalimumab. Five patients received TNF‐I advanced therapy in combination with an immunomodulator.

Treatment success was achieved in seven patients (58.3%) treated with infliximab as first‐line advanced therapy.

Three patients treated with infliximab experienced mild–moderate ADRs. One patient developed HSV infection, while two developed neurological symptoms of transient global amnesia and transient lower limb weakness due to a functional neurological disorder, respectively. Infliximab therapy was discontinued in both.

Janus Kinase Inhibitors (JAK‐I)

Four patients received Janus Kinase Inhibitor (JAK‐I) therapy; three received tofacitinib while one patient was commenced on upadacitinib. Each of these patients had failed a previous advanced therapy prior to the commencement of a JAK‐I. Treatment success was achieved in two patients (50%). ADRs were reported in two patients receiving JAK‐I. One patient developed HSV infection while another patient discontinued tofacitinib due to the development of abdominal and chest pain.

IL‐12/23 and IL‐23 Inhibitors

Six patients received ustekinumab and one guselkumab. Treatment success was achieved in five patients (83.3%) on ustekinumab and in the sole patient who received guselkumab.

Sphingosine‐1‐Phosphate(S‐1‐P) Receptor Modulators

One patient was successfully treated with etrasimod as first line advanced therapy following nonresponse to thiopurine therapy.

Topical Therapies for Refractory UP

Topical Tacrolimus

Rectally administered tacrolimus was used in 10 patients (5.4%). Clinical remission with topical tacrolimus therapy was achieved in three patients (30%), each of whom received topical tacrolimus as initial therapy for refractory UP.

Mild–moderate ADRs were reported in five (50%) patients and led to drug discontinuation in three (30%). These included anal itching and perianal pain, while one patient developed a metallic taste in the mouth.

Fecal Microbiota Transplantation

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) delivered by enema was used in 13 patients with active refractory UP as part of a pilot clinical trial conducted at TQEH between 2021 and 2022. Eight of these patients (61.5%) achieved clinical remission following induction FMT enemas.

Discussion

This study presents real‐world outcomes in a multicenter cohort of UP patients. A substantial proportion of our study cohort (30%) developed refractory UP and required commencement of systemic immunosuppression. Yet, there was limited uptake of topically administered therapies for refractory UP outside of clinical trial settings. Almost one in five patients developed either disease extension or a traditional UC‐related complication. These findings add to the body of evidence that challenges historical perceptions of UP as a benign entity.

Our findings add to emerging evidence that refractory UP is significantly associated with the development of UC‐related complications [18, 19, 20, 21]. Almost 10% of the cohort experienced UC‐related complications. Of these, most patients required hospitalization for intravenous corticosteroid treatment due to UC disease flares, while one patient underwent colectomy for refractory disease. Hospitalization in UC is associated with elevated healthcare costs, psychological morbidity and increased risk of requiring colectomy [22]. A meta‐analysis of six population‐based UC cohort studies reported pooled cumulative incidence rates at 1‐, 3‐, and 5 years of 10.4%, 17%, and 21.5%, respectively [23]. While overall hospitalization rates were lower in this UP cohort, those with refractory disease demonstrated significantly greater risk.

Proximal disease extension occurred in 11.9% of this study cohort over the follow‐up period. Disease extension in UP has prognostic implications, including heightened malignancy risk necessitating participation in colorectal cancer surveillance and elevated risk of disease flares [2]. A meta‐analysis of 30 studies reported that the overall pooled frequency of disease extension in UP was 17.8% at 10 years [24]. The lower rate of disease extension in our study is likely due to a relatively short duration of follow up.

The findings from this study support the need to delineate those with refractory disease, given prognostic divergence in this UP subgroup. While therapeutic response to 5‐aminosalicylate therapy and/or topical corticosteroids portended a favorable long‐term prognosis, the development of refractory UP was strongly associated with occurrence of UC related complications. A substantial proportion of our study cohort (30%) developed refractory proctitis and required commencement of systemic immunosuppression. No demographic or baseline clinical factors were associated with the future development of refractory disease. There is therefore a need to proactively assess treatment response to 5‐aminosalicylate and corticosteroid therapy in order rapidly to identify those individuals who would benefit from early therapeutic escalation. Evaluation of therapeutic response should incorporate clinical, biochemical, endoscopic and histological re‐assessments in keeping with contemporary treat to target guidelines for UC [25]. Transperineal ultrasound is also increasingly recognized as a valuable modality for assessing early treatment response in UP, particularly as it addresses the limitations of transabdominal ultrasound in evaluating rectal inflammation [26, 27].

Given the prevailing prescribing restrictions in Australia, many patients in our study were commenced on azathioprine as first line therapy for refractory UP. Use of thiopurine therapy was associated with systemic adverse effects necessitating discontinuation in 30% of patients. Advanced therapies were commenced in 14.3% of this study cohort, while almost 5% of patients were exposed to more than one advanced therapy. Given the delicate risk–benefit profile of patients with refractory disease of limited anatomical extent, nuanced systemic immunosuppressant selection may be beneficial in refractory UP. Therapies with favorable adverse effect profiles and gut‐selective immunosuppression such as vedolizumab or S1P inhibitors may hold a theoretical advantage. Vedolizumab was the most frequently used advanced therapy in our study cohort, with 53% (8/15) achieving treatment success. These findings are consistent with long term vedolizumab persistence data from the PANIC Study and suggest that treatment outcomes in UP are comparable to extensive UC [28]. Notably, only 6.7% (1/15) developed adverse effects to vedolizumab while 50% (2/4) and 21.4% had adverse effects with JAK‐I and TNF‐I, respectively. However, further prospective data are required to inform systemic therapy selection in refractory UP and this represents a key area for future research.

Topical therapies were under‐utilized in our study cohort. A recent systematic review identified several topical therapies that have been assessed in refractory proctitis including arsenic, tacrolimus and xilei san [29]. Among these, topically administered tacrolimus has the strongest supporting evidence, with randomized controlled trials demonstrating its superiority to placebo and noninferiority to beclomethasone enemas in the treatment of 5‐ASA resistant proctitis [30, 31]. Topical tacrolimus was used in 17.9% of those who developed refractory proctitis, typically as 1 mg enemas twice daily for a duration of 8 weeks. Treatment success was experienced in 60% (3/5) who used tacrolimus enemas as a first line therapy for refractory proctitis. Given its topical administration and favorable adverse effect profile, there may be a role for greater use of tacrolimus prior to the introduction of systemic immunosuppressant therapy. However, the lack of commercially available preparations, unclear role in maintaining remission, and lack of standardized dosing regimens are all barriers to its widespread adoption [29].

The current study has some limitations. Firstly, the relatively high rates of refractory proctitis observed may reflect referral bias, given that each of the study sites is a tertiary IBD center. Moreover, certain treatment approaches employed in this retrospective cohort (e.g., rectally administered tacrolimus and fecal microbiota transplant administered as part of a clinical trial) reflect local institutional experience and may not be universally available, further limiting the generalizability of study findings. Secondly, our study is subject to the inherent limitations of retrospective cohort studies including missing data, lack of validated patient reported outcome measure reporting, variable follow up duration and the absence of comparator groups. We are therefore unable to make strong conclusions regarding choice of therapy in the setting of refractory proctitis. Thirdly, our definition of treatment success did not require stringent endoscopic and/or histological confirmation. Endoscopic data was unavailable for a large proportion of patients because reports for procedures performed externally were not available. Treatment success may therefore be overestimated in the absence of documented objective endoscopic findings. Finally, the use of univariate regression analysis limits inference about independent associations but was necessary due to low primary outcome event rates and missing clinical data that could bias multivariable modeling. The strong association observed between refractory UP and risk of disease‐related complications must therefore be interpreted with caution, given the absence of covariate adjustment. This finding should be viewed as hypothesis‐generating rather than being interpreted as a causal relationship.

To summarize, this study demonstrates that disease‐related complications occur relatively frequently in a multicenter cohort of UP patients. Our study also highlights the importance of refractory UP as a driver of disease‐related complications. Treatment decisions in this UC subgroup should balance the risk of disease‐related complications and debilitating symptoms against the risks of exposing patients with limited disease distribution to systemically acting immunosuppressants. Collectively, our findings support recognizing refractory UP as a distinct and dynamic UC phenotype, underscoring the need for dedicated prospective research encompassing robust clinical trials and long‐term safety evaluations across different therapeutic classes.
