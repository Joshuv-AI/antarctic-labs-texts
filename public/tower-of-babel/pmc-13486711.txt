Evaluating orthokeratology lens fitting and the visual quality of artificial intelligence-fitted personalized lenses in myopic children with corneal asymmetry
Authors: Chenpei Zhao, Dongdong Jiang, Jiansen Yin, Weicong Lu, Yuyin Tian, Lin Leng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To evaluate the orthokeratology (ortho-K) lens fitting and visual quality of artificial intelligence (AI) -fitted personalized lenses in myopic children with corneal asymmetry.This retrospective study included 139 children with corneal asymmetry who underwent ortho-K fitting, grouped according to conventional vision shaping treatment (VST) lenses (n = 53), corneal refractive therapy (CRT) lenses (n = 47), and AI-fitted personalized Optoshare Good Positioning (GP) lenses (n = 39). Pentacam-acquired anterior corneal images were used to calculate aberrations (root mean square total, root mean square higher-order aberration, and Zernike aberrations) and MATLAB was used to quantify lens decentration. One-way analysis of variance, Pearson correlation, and multiple linear regression were employed for analysis. After 1 month, the VST group’s mean decentration (0.84 ± 0.35 mm) was significantly greater than that of the CRT (0.69 ± 0.22 mm, p = 0.023) and GP (0.60 ± 0.21 mm, p < 0.001) groups. All groups had increased aberrations; the VST group showed greater increases in coma Z3−1 than the GP group (p = 0.002) and in trefoil Z3−3 than both the CRT group (p = 0.014) and the GP group (p = 0.029). Lens decentration positively correlated with aberration increases; SE negatively correlated with aberration changes. Multiple regression showed that CRT lenses (β = −0.24, p = 0.008) and GP lenses (β = −0.38, p <0.001) had less decentration than VST lenses.Among children with corneal asymmetry, AI-based personalized GP lenses and CRT lenses exhibited better centration stability than VST lenses and can better control increase in higher-order aberrations.

Introduction

Myopia has emerged as a global public health crisis, characterized by a notable trend of younger onset and progression to high myopia1,2. Projections indicate that the global prevalence of myopia will reach 50% by 20503. Notably, myopia is not only a major risk factor for cataracts, glaucoma, retinal detachment, and myopic macular degeneration but also a leading cause of blindness4, significantly impairing quality of life and imposing substantial social burdens. Consequently, myopia prevention and control demand urgent action. Interventions for myopia control encompass optical, behavioral, and pharmacological approaches5,6, and optical interventions remain the most widely used approach to retard axial elongation.

Among optical interventions, orthokeratology (Ortho-K) has been widely clinically adopted as a safe, effective modality. Ortho-K lenses cause reversible remodeling of corneal epithelial cells through reverse geometry design rather than by altering the shape of the entire cornea. The remodeled cornea creates distinct focal planes within the eye, thereby inducing peripheral retinal myopic defocus, a mechanism believed to modulate ocular refractive development via retinal dopamine signaling and scleral matrix remodeling7. Clinically, ortho-K demonstrates an average 47%–64% reduction in annual axial elongation rate across randomized controlled trials8.

The ideal effect of wearing ortho-K lenses is to control myopia progression and improve uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA). In children with markedly asymmetric corneal topography, however, treatment zone displacement is common9. Previous study found that the mean decentration value of ortho-K lenses in patients wearing them was 0.75 mm, among which the proportion of patients with a decentration value exceeding 1.0 mm reached 19.4%10. A number of studies have confirmed that excessive lens decentration may lead to adverse consequences, although a mild degree of decentration may in some cases contribute to the myopia-control effect by increasing coma and spherical aberration, as suggested by previous studies11. The effect of lens decentration is therefore context-dependent and should be interpreted in relation to the clinical objective of orthokeratology fitting, particularly in the context of myopia management. Nevertheless, previous studies have also highlighted the potential adverse effects associated with excessive lens decentration. Li et al.’s research indicates that lens decentration can result in a significant increase in ocular higher-order aberrations (HOAs)12, which in turn cause problems such as decreased visual quality manifested as diplopia. Xue et al.’s study demonstrated that lens decentration can also reduce the safety of wearing ortho-K lenses13, which is also associated with an increased risk of ocular complications, including corneal epithelial staining. Toric and aspheric designs have shown promise in reducing lens decentration14, but both designs rely on axial symmetry, limiting their efficacy in correcting irregular corneal morphology.

To address excessive or unstable lens decentration and improve the success rate of ortho-K lens fitting in children with asymmetric corneas, various software approaches to lens fitting have emerged. The Optoshare Good Positioning (GP) lens is a brand that relies on artificial intelligence (AI) for personalized fitting. It features a four-zone, eight-axis free axial positioning design, which provides precise corneal topography matching. The present study aimed to evaluate the clinical performance of AI-assisted personalized GP lenses in patients with corneal asymmetry, with a focus on lens centration and visual quality outcomes.

Methods

Subjects

This retrospective study enrolled 139 subjects who underwent ortho-K lens fitting and completed regular follow-up examinations at Qingdao Eye Hospital, Shandong First Medical University, from July through December 2024. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Qingdao Eye Hospital, Shandong First Medical University in accordance with the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki. All minors or legal guardians provided informed consent after a full explanation of the procedures, precautions, potential risks, post-fitting care, and follow-up requirements of ortho-K lenses.

Follow-up visits

The subjects were instructed to wear their ortho-K lenses for 8–10 hours nightly after the lenses were dispensed. Appointments at baseline (i.e., prior to initiating ortho-K treatment) were followed up after 1 day, 1 week, and 1 month of wear.

The one-month visit was selected for analysis because it represents the early stabilization phase of orthokeratology treatment, during which corneal reshaping and lens centration typically reach a relatively stable state.

During each appointment, all subjects received a series of examinations, including ocular health assessment, UDVA tests, and corneal topography examinations. All examinations were performed between 8 a.m. and 10 a.m. and within a two-hour window after the ortho-K lenses were removed.

Lenses fitting

Three brands of ortho-K lenses were used:

1. Corneal refractive therapy (CRT) (Paragon Vision Sciences, USA) adopts a three-zone design, including the base curve (BC), reverse zone depth, and landing zone angle (LZA).

2. Euclid (Euclid Systems Corporation, USA) features a four-curve design, including the BC zone, reverse curve (RC) zone, alignment curve (AC) zone, and peripheral curve (PC) zone, and is a typical representative of vision shaping treatment (VST) lens design.

3. GP (Shenzhen New Industry Material of Ophthalmology co.,LTD, China) features a four-curve design, including BC zone, RC zone, AC zone, and PC zone. An automated framework was developed using fitting data and corneal topography from 3,524 eyes of 2,110 patients to derive eight-axis parameters, replacing conventional manual calculations and enabling intelligent, end-to-end workflows from parameter estimation to personalized lens design.

Optimal parameters were finalized through iterative trial fittings and adjustments. A conic curve model was adopted to reconstruct the three-dimensional morphology of the anterior corneal surface. A coordinate system was established with the corneal vertex as the origin and the optical axis as the Z-axis, where the corneal elevation was characterized by radial distance, vertex curvature, and eccentricity. Clinical keratometry reading K was converted to geometric curvature c and radius of curvature R using the standardized equivalent refractive index n = 1.3375. Given sampling points, K, and eccentricity e, the elevation was computed through parameter initialization, geometric derivation, intermediate variable calculation, and elevation solving. A baseline corneal model was first constructed with eight axial sectors.

Sample size determination

A Pearson correlation analysis with values of α = 0.05 (two-sided), power = 0.90, expected correlation coefficient r = 0.35, and standard deviation (SD) = 0.20 indicated the need for a minimum of 31 eyes per group. Accounting for potential retrospective data incompleteness, 36 eyes per group were targeted.

Inclusion criteria

Subjects were included who were aged 8–14 years, had a cycloplegic refraction spherical equivalent (SE) of −0.75 to −4.00 D, a best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of ≥20/20, had completed at least one follow-up visit ≥1 month after ortho-K lens fitting.

Corneal asymmetry was defined based on corneal topography measurements. Specifically, asymmetric cornea was identified as a nasal–temporal or superior–inferior curvature difference of ≥1.50 D at an 8-mm chord length, as derived from axial curvature maps. Exclusion criteria

Subjects were excluded who exhibited amblyopia, strabismus, or active ocular surface disease, had a history of ocular surgeries, traumatic injuries, or allergies to contact lens care products, or who did not comply with lens wear and care instructions.

Corneal aberrations

Three-dimensional images of the anterior segments of the participating children’s eyes were acquired using a rotating Scheimpflug tomographer (Pentacam, Oculus, Wetzlar, Germany). Raw corneal tomography data obtained from the Pentacam system were used directly without additional parameter standardization, as these measurements are clinically validated and routinely used in orthokeratology lens fitting. Only those with examination quality parameters marked as “OK” were selected. This quality criterion ensures adequate data coverage and excludes scans with incomplete acquisition or reliance on extrapolated values. The examination was conducted in a darkroom environment and performed by the same experienced technician. Zernike analysis was performed to calculate the root mean square (RMS) of total corneal aberrations, RMS of HOAs, and the values of horizontal trefoil aberration (Z33), vertical trefoil aberration (Z3−3), horizontal coma aberration (Z31), vertical coma aberration (Z3−1), and spherical aberration (Z40) within a 4-mm diameter centered at the corneal apex.

Lens decentration

The difference map was obtained by subtracting the tangential curvature map before wearing ortho-K lenses from the tangential curvature map after 1 month of wearing ortho-K lenses. The difference map was imported into MATLAB software to quantify the corneal optical treatment zone induced by the ortho-K lens. Eight points were plotted surrounding the central flattened area on which the power was 0.00 D on the difference map. These points were then processed through a least squares circle-fitting algorithm to compute the optimal circular approximation of the treatment zone boundary. The geometric center of the resultant circle was defined as the treatment zone centroid. Lens decentration was defined as the distance between the center of the treatment zone and the corneal vertex, measured in millimeters15.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS (Version 26). After 1 month of wearing the ortho-K lens, data from the patients’ right eyes were selected for analysis. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess data normality. All datasets met the criteria for a normal distribution (p >0.05), and the data were described as mean ± SD. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to compare differences between the VST, CRT, and GP groups, followed by post hoc multiple comparisons using Bonferroni correction. Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to evaluate the correlations between parameters. A multiple linear regression model was constructed to assess the effects of different lens designs on lens decentration. Statistical significance was set at p <0.05.

Changes in corneal aberration and amount of lens decentration

After 1 month of ortho-K lens wear, the mean decentration was 0.84 ± 0.35 mm in the VST group, 0.69 ± 0.22 mm in the CRT group, and 0.60 ± 0.21 mm in the GP group (p < 0.001). Post hoc Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons showed that the VST group exhibited significantly greater decentration than the CRT group (p = 0.023) or GP group (p < 0.001). Although the GP group exhibited lower decentration than the CRT group, the difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Changes in UDVA were also analyzed, and there was no statistical difference among the three groups (p > 0.05). And no significant correlation was observed between lens decentration and UDVA.

In all the groups, RMS total, RMS HOA, coma Z3−1, coma Z31, spherical Z40, trefoil Z3−3, and trefoil Z33 all increased compared to pre-wear baseline values. Among these, one-way ANOVA revealed significant changes in coma Z3−1 (p = 0.002) and trefoil Z3−3 (p = 0.006). For coma Z3−1, the VST group exhibited a significantly greater increase than both the CRT and GP groups, with the GP group showing the smallest increase. Post hoc Bonferroni tests identified a significant difference only between the VST and GP groups (p = 0.002). For coma Z3−3, the VST group’s increase was significantly greater than that of the CRT group (the smallest increase) and GP group. Discussion

As myopia emerges as a critical global public health challenge, the scientific community has increasingly focused on myopia control16. Ortho-K lenses, distinguished by their high efficacy, robust safety profile, and reversibility of refractive effects, have been established as the first-line intervention in international clinical guidelines for myopia control17. However, clinicians remain troubled by the major challenge of poor lens positioning after ortho-K lens fitting in children with irregular corneal topography. Li et al. report that after 12 months of ortho-K lens wear, the mean decentration was 0.70 mm in the VST group and 0.57 mm in the CRT group18. The decentration values observed in this study were higher than those reported in other studies, which may be attributed to the fact that selected subjects showed highly irregular corneal morphological characteristics18,19.

Ophthalmologists have explored various approaches to solve the problem of lens decentration, such as utilizing aspheric lens designs, transitioning from VST to CRT designs, and increasing peripheral toricity14,20,21. These approaches demonstrated certain positive effects in children with a corneal curvature difference of <1.50 D at the 8-mm chord of corneal topography. However, for corneas with a curvature difference of ≥ 1.50 D, the improvement in lens positioning still fails to meet expectations. Zhang et al.’s study found that some patients with moderate to high corneal astigmatism (1.50–3.50 D) still exhibited significant treatment zone decentration (>1 mm) after wearing toric-design lenses22. Similarly, Zhang et al. found that if the corneal height difference at an 8-mm chord length was greater than 41.06 μm, spherical and toric lenses yielded no significant difference in treatment zone decentration, thus indicating that toric designs are ineffective in mitigating lens positioning failure caused by severe corneal asymmetry20. This study found that patients with a curvature difference of ≥1.50 D at the 8-mm chord length exhibited lens decentration of varying degrees regardless of the design of ortho-K lenses. When compared with VST lenses, both CRT lenses and GP individualized custom lenses effectively improved lens decentration.

This study employed multiple linear regression analysis to investigate the effects on decentration of three lens designs. The results demonstrate that compared to VST lenses, CRT lenses and GP lenses showed significantly better centration, which we attribute to differences in their arc designs: CRT lenses feature a 3-arc design with a linear LZA landing zone that tangentially contacts the cornea, thus providing a larger contact area to stabilize centration and reduce the risk of lens entrapment23. In contrast, VST lenses use a curvilinear AC arc that fits the cornea, resulting in poorer tolerance of irregular corneal morphology23. Moreover, the toric design of CRT and VST lenses exhibits axial symmetry, leading to suboptimal positioning efficacy in myopic individuals with asymmetric corneal morphology. GP lenses features a unique 4-zone, 8-axis free axis design, which enables personalized fitting to the asymmetric morphology of a specific corneal meridian. This design ensures that the lens better conforms to the actual corneal shape, thereby enhancing centering stability. It should be noted that lens diameter is also an important factor influencing orthokeratology lens centration. In the present study, GP and Euclid lenses allowed flexible adjustment of diameter, whereas CRT lenses had a more limited range of options. However, Euclid lenses, despite having adjustable diameters, did not achieve comparable centration performance. This suggests that diameter flexibility alone does not account for the observed differences in clinical outcomes. Therefore, the results are more likely attributable to the combined effects of lens design, fitting strategy, and parameter optimization rather than a single parameter.

Overnight ortho-K lens wear induces a myopic defocus ring in the corneal mid-periphery, increasing corneal irregularity and correspondingly elevating HOAs. These changes gradually stabilize after 1 month of continuous wear24. Previous investigations have documented significant elevations in primary coma and primary spherical aberration (Z40) following the use of ortho-K lenses25. Consistent with these findings, this study observed that all measured third-order aberrations (i.e., coma, trefoil) and primary spherical aberration (Z40) at the 4-mm corneal zone showed increases. Correlation analysis across the cohort revealed significant positive associations between corneal eccentricity and postoperative changes in RMS total, RMS HOA, Z3−1, Z31, and Z33. The strongest correlations were observed for RMS HOA and horizontal coma, indicating a robust relationship between lens decentration and HOA increases.

Coma aberrations primarily characterize corneal asymmetry26. The marked increase in horizontal coma following ortho-K lens wear is likely due to lens decentration toward the nasal or temporal meridian, resulting in uneven horizontal corneal compression27. Chen et al. observed that temporal decentration accounted for 84.9% of all fitted ortho-K lenses28. Central corneal flattening and mid-peripheral steepening post-treatment contribute to the elevation in Z4029,30. Lens eccentricity induces asymmetric corneal epithelial remodeling, forming nonuniform pressure zones31 the trilobal pattern of peripheral compression may underlie the increase in primary trefoil. Notably, intergroup comparisons revealed statistically significant differences in Z3−1 and Z33 changes, with the GP and CRT groups showing smaller fluctuations than the VST group. This suggested that GP and CRT designs confered superior vertical centration improvements.

The present study found that initial refractive error also influenced changes in corneal aberrations after ortho-K use. In the entire cohort, postoperative variations in RMS total, RMS HOA, Z3−1, Z31, Z3−3, and Z33 exhibited significant negative correlations with initial refractive error, with RMS HOA and horizontal coma demonstrating the strongest associations. The higher the initial refractive error, the greater the change in the anterior corneal surface curvature induced by ortho-K lenses. This leads to uneven distribution of the tear film between the lens and the cornea, along with reduced stability32. All these changes contribute to an increase in the irregularity of the anterior corneal surface, resulting in a significantly larger variation amplitude of HOAs in patients with high myopia compared to those with mild to moderate myopia. As documented by Li et al., C-weighted defocus stabilizes after 1 month of continuous wear, with a positive linear relationship between initial myopia/corneal astigmatism and C-weighted defocus (β = −0.589, p <0.001), confirming initial refractive error as a pivotal determinant of postoperative corneal aberration dynamics18.

Previous studies have proposed a dual role for corneal HOAs following ortho-K lens wear: On the one hand, these aberrations, which are partly induced by treatment zone decentration, may act as optical signals to modulate choroidal thickness and scleral biomechanics, thereby contributing to retarding myopia progression.13 On the other hand, corneal HOAs reduce retinal image quality, leading to visual discomforts such as glare and ghosting33. This highlights the necessity of balancing myopia control efficacy with visual quality in clinical practice. This study found that after 1 month of lens wear, changes in UDVA were positively correlated with variations in RMS total, RMS HOA, RMS Z3−1, and RMS Z40. Chu et al. argue that lens decentration leads to increased glare or ghosting, exerting a negative impact on UDVA34. However, consistent with the findings of Laura Batres’ study 19, this research did not identify a correlation between the degree of decentration and UDVA.

However, this study has several limitations. Although corneal parameters stabilized after 1 month of continuous wear, the follow-up period of 1 month was relatively short, lacking long-term observational data. The sample size was small, and correlation studies with other visual quality metrics, such as contrast sensitivity, were absent. Further research is needed to validate the relationship between HOAs, subjective visual quality, and myopia control efficacy.
