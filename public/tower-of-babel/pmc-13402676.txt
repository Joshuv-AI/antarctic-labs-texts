LRP4 is an entry receptor for multiple encephalitic alphaviruses
Authors: Sicheng Tian, Bingting Ma, Hongyuan Guo, Zhenlu Chong, Theron C. Gilliland, Sean Hui, Guojie Wang, Yuyi Zhang, Jiayue Zhou, Alan Sariol, Xinran Sun, Yingxin Hu, Zhuohao He, Daved H. Fremont, William B. Klimstra, Michael S. Diamond, Ye Xiang, Rong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Encephalitic alphaviruses, including Eastern equine encephalitis virus (EEEV), cause severe neurological disease with high mortality rates, and thus are a public health threat. Although members of the low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) family, including VLDLR, LRP8 (ApoER2), and LDLR recently were identified as receptors for EEEV, residual infection in receptor-deficient cells suggests that additional entry factors exist. Using a CRISPR-based activation screen, we identified LDLR-related protein 4 (LRP4) as a candidate entry factor for EEEV and several related alphaviruses (Western equine encephalitis, Semliki Forest, and Sindbis viruses). LRP4 mediates viral attachment and internalization, and its ligand-binding domain binds directly to virions. Soluble LRP4 decoy proteins potently inhibit EEEV infection in primary mouse neuronal cells, male mice, and human brain organoids, suggesting possible therapeutic applications. Mammalian and avian LRP4 orthologs demonstrate conserved functions in promoting EEEV infection, supporting a possible role in its host range of infection and transmission. Our findings establish LRP4 as a shared entry receptor for multiple alphaviruses and expand our understanding of alphavirus tropism, pathogenesis, and countermeasure development.

Introduction

The arthropod-borne alphaviruses are a genus of enveloped, single-stranded, positive-sense RNA viruses that infect a range of vertebrate and invertebrate hosts. Based on their genetic relatedness and clinical manifestations, alphaviruses are classified into arthritogenic and encephalitic groups1. Arthritogenic alphaviruses typically cause acute or chronic musculoskeletal disease in humans2, whereas encephalitic alphaviruses induce severe neurological disease and lethality3,4. Canonical encephalitic alphaviruses include Venezuelan equine encephalitis (VEEV), Eastern equine encephalitis (EEEV), and Western equine encephalitis (WEEV) viruses. However, other alphaviruses, including Sindbis (SINV)5 and Semliki Forest (SFV) viruses6, can cause encephalitis in mammals. While EEEV causes sporadic outbreaks in North America, it has a high case-fatality rate in humans approaching 40%7,8. Indeed, in 2024, an EEEV outbreak in the eastern United States resulted in several fatalities9. Due to their potential transmission via aerosol routes, encephalitic alphaviruses are also considered possible bioterrorism agents10–13. Despite their public health threat, no approved vaccines or antiviral therapies are currently available for humans.

Host factors that mediate virion attachment to cell surfaces and/or entry into cells have pivotal roles in determining viral tropism and pathogenesis14. Previous studies have identified several factors facilitating EEEV entry. Heparan sulfate (HS) serves as a key attachment factor for infection of EEEV and its neuropathogenesis15–17, with lysine residues 71, 74, and 77 in the E2 protein of EEEV being critical for HS binding18. Additionally, the very-low-density lipoprotein receptor (VLDLR), apolipoprotein E receptor 2 (ApoER2 [also known as LRP8]), and low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) recently were identified as entry factors for EEEV19,20. The extracellular domain of VLDLR mediates EEEV entry through multiple binding modes, including interactions with the E1/E2 cleft and the E2 A domain21–23. Although LDLR enhances EEEV infection, its binding affinity to virions is lower, and soluble LDLR-Fc decoy proteins failed to efficiently inhibit infection in vitro or in vivo20. These findings highlight the complexity of EEEV entry, which likely involves multiple host factors. Importantly, deletion or blockade of known entry factors in specific cell types does not fully suppress EEEV infection, suggesting the existence of additional, unidentified host factors.

Here, we employed a gain-of-function CRISPR activation screen of endogenous membrane proteins and identified LRP4 as a host factor that promotes infection of several encephalitic alphaviruses, including EEEV, WEEV, SINV, and SFV, with particularly prominent effects on EEEV infectivity. The ligand-binding domain (LBD) of LRP4 directly binds EEEV virions, and soluble LRP4-Fc decoy proteins effectively inhibit infection in vitro and in vivo. Our work provides insight into the tissue tropism, cross-species transmission, and possible host range of several encephalitic alphaviruses.

Results

CRISPR activation screen identifies LRP4 as a host factor promoting EEEV infection

To identify host factors that promote EEEV infection, we first characterized the expression of common viral attachment factors on A549 lung adenocarcinoma cells (Supplementary Fig. 1A). We observed high levels of expression of HS, AXL, and TIM-1. The gene B3GAT3 encodes a key enzyme involved in the biosynthesis of HS, a main cellular attachment factor of EEEV15. The genes AXL and TIM-1 encode AXL receptor tyrosine kinase24 and T-cell immunoglobulin mucin domain-containing protein 1 (TIM-1)25, respectively; both of these proteins are phosphatidylserine (PtdSer) receptors that can mediate entry of enveloped viruses, including alphaviruses and flaviviruses26. To reduce background levels of EEEV infection in A549 cells, we generated a triple-knockout (KO) cell line (A549-BAT) lacking B3GAT3, AXL, and TIM-1 (Supplementary Fig. 1B, C). The infection by single-round EEEV virus-like replicon particles (EEEV VRPs), in which the structural proteins of EEEV package an SINV replicon, was markedly decreased in A549-BAT cells compared to parental wild-type A549 (Supplementary Fig. 1D–F).

We next designed a CRISPR activation screen using sgRNAs targeting the promoters of predicted human membrane proteins, with four independent sgRNAs per gene. Lentiviruses containing the sgRNA library were transduced into A549-BAT cells that expressed dCas9 and VP64. These cells were then inoculated with EEEV VRPs, and fluorescent protein reporter-positive cells were sorted for genomic DNA extraction and sequencing. Based on protein expression profiles, structural features, and known or reported cellular localization patterns, we selected 11 of the top 20 candidates for validation using CRISPR-mediated gene activation. Two independent sgRNAs per gene (Supplementary Data 2) were used. Among the candidates, sgRNAs targeting LRP4 and SIGLEC1 significantly increased EEEV infection (Fig. 1B). SIGLEC1 encodes sialic acid-binding Ig-like lectin 1, a reported attachment factor of enveloped viruses in antigen-presenting cells27–29. LRP4 encodes LDL receptor-related protein 4 (also known as MEGF7 or SOST2), a classical type-I membrane protein of the LDLR family30, and recently described entry receptor for yellow fever virus31, an orthoflavivirus. Notably, one of the sgRNAs targeting LRP4 enhanced the infection of EEEV VRPs by nearly 10-fold compared to the control group (Fig. 1B), which led us to prioritize this gene for further investigation.

To confirm the proviral function of LRP4 and rule out the possible off-target effects of CRISPR-based activation, we transduced a lentivirus encoding a C-terminal HA-tagged LRP4 cDNA into multiple cell lines, including human lung epithelial cells (A549-BAT and wild-type A549), erythroleukemic cells (K562), and glioma cells (U-87 MG) (Supplementary Fig. 2A–D). Infection of EEEV VRPs was significantly increased in these LRP4-expressing cell lines (Fig. 1C–F), with the most marked enhancement (nearly 30-fold) in A549-BAT cells (Fig. 1C). Reciprocally, CRISPR Cas9-mediated reduction of LRP4 expression in human neuroblastoma (SH-SY5Y) and hepatoma (Huh7) cells (Supplementary Fig. 2E, F) diminished infection of chimeric SINV-EEEV (Fig. 1G, H). A proviral role of LRP4 was further validated using an authentic EEEV strain (MADV, Brazil 1985)20 in LRP4-overexpressing K562 cells and LRP4-edited haploid myeloid leukemia (HAP1) cells deficient in HS expression31 (Fig. 1I, J).

We next assessed whether other LDL receptor family members—excluding large, high-molecular-weight proteins LRP1, LRP1B, and LRP2 that are not easily expressed—could promote EEEV infection. Ectopic expression in A549-BAT cells (Supplementary Fig. 2G) revealed that only known receptors VLDLR, LDLR, and LRP8 significantly enhanced viral infection (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Although LRP4 represents an additional proviral factor for EEEV in the LDL receptor family, editing of LRP4 in human cervical adenocarcinoma cells (HeLa), human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293T), and A549 cells did not reduce infection of EEEV VRPs (Supplementary Fig. 3B–D). As a control, individual knockout of B3GAT3, but not AXL or TIM-1, markedly reduced infection in A549 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3D, E). Editing of individual EEEV receptors (e.g., VLDLR, LDLR, and LRP8) in A549, but not in SH-SY5Y cells, also failed to attenuate infection (Supplementary Fig. 3D, F), likely due to receptor redundancy among known or undiscovered entry factors.

Since some receptors are used by multiple alphaviruses for cell entry (e.g., (MXRA832, VLDLR19,33, and LDLR20,34), we tested whether LRP4 expression could promote infection of other alphaviruses from different antigenic complexes (Supplementary Fig. 3G). Indeed, ectopic expression of LRP4 in A549-BAT cells enhanced infection of WEEV VRPs, SFV VRPs, and SINV, but not of VEEV VRPs, CHIKV VRPs, or authentic GETV (Fig. 1K). Of note, EEEV35, WEEV36, SFV6, and SINV5 can invade and infect the central nervous system of humans or other mammals, and LRP4 is highly expressed in the brain37. Together, these results suggest that LRP4 may promote infection of multiple encephalitic alphaviruses.

LRP4 enhances EEEV entry into host cells

To determine the cellular stage at which LRP4 facilitates EEEV infection, we performed virus binding and internalization assays (Fig. 2A). In A549-BAT cells, ectopic expression of LRP4 increased the binding of EEEV VRPs by 2-fold (Fig. 2B). After virus binding at 4 °C, the cells were switched to 37 °C for 1 h to allow internalization. Expression of LRP4 resulted in a 3-fold increase of internalized virus compared to the vector control (Fig. 2C). Similar results were observed in LRP4-expressing U-87 MG cells with EEEV VRPs (Fig. 2D, E). EEEV VRPs contain RNA encoding the nonstructural genes of SINV that enable replication. To assess whether LRP4 impacts SINV replication, we generated SINV replicon RNAs by in vitro transcription (Fig. 2F) and transfected them into vector- and LRP4-expressing cells. Similar percentages of reporter-positive cells were detected (Fig. 2G), and differences in geometric mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) were not observed (Fig. 2H). These experiments confirm that LRP4 does not influence SINV replication but likely enhances EEEV glycoprotein-mediated entry of cells. To further validate the role of LRP4 in EEEV entry, we tested vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) and murine leukemia retrovirus (MLV)-based pseudoviral particles bearing EEEV glycoproteins. As expected, LRP4 expression promoted the infection of pseudoviruses packaged with EEEV glycoproteins but not with VSV glycoprotein (Fig. 2I–L). Collectively, these results establish that LRP4 promotes EEEV infection at the entry stage.

The LBD of LRP4 promotes EEEV infection

Like other members of the LDLR family (e.g., VLDLR and LDLR), LRP4 contains an N-terminal LBD with eight LDLR class A (LA) repeats, followed by multiple epidermal growth factor (EGF)-like repeats and β-propeller motifs, an O-linked sugar domain, and transmembrane and intracellular domains (Fig. 3A). To identify the functional domains of LRP4 required for EEEV infection, we generated stable cell lines that express HA-tagged truncations lacking either the LBD or EGF-like domains and verified their expression (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). A truncated form of LRP4 lacking the EGF-like domains still promoted EEEV infection in A549 and A549-BAT cells, whereas LRP4 lacking the LBD did not promote EEEV infection (Fig. 3B, C). To map which LA repeats within the LBD are essential for EEEV infectivity, we deleted each LA repeat individually in the backbone of LRP4 lacking the EGF-like domains and first validated expression (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. 4E). Unexpectedly, all truncated proteins supported EEEV infection without substantive differences, suggesting functional redundancy among LA repeats (Fig. 3E), as reported previously for EEEV and VLDLR21. We next expressed individual LA repeats fused to the O-linked sugar domain, transmembrane region, and cytoplasmic tail and verified their expression (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. 4F). Remarkably, single LA1, LA2, LA4, or LA7 repeats alone enhanced EEEV infection to levels comparable to the full LBD (Fig. 3G). In contrast, only the complete LBD facilitated WEEV infection (Supplementary Fig. 4G), possibly due to the lower avidity of individual LA repeats for WEEV glycoproteins.

To confirm the importance of LBD and exclude the confounding effects from transmembrane or cytosolic domains of LRP4, we swapped the full LBD or individual LA repeats onto the membrane proximal extracellular domain of MXRA8, an unrelated alphavirus entry receptor32 (Fig. 3H and Supplementary Fig. 4H). Ectopic expression of LBD, LA2, and LA4 in the MXRA8 scaffold significantly promoted EEEV infection, followed by LA1 and LA7, whereas LA3 and LA5 had a less proviral effect (Fig. 3I). These results demonstrate that LRP4 LBD is critical for EEEV infection, with multiple LA repeats capable of mediating this function.

The LBD of LRP4 directly binds to EEEV virions

We next tested whether LRP4 LBD can directly interact with EEEV virions. An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) revealed that Fc-tagged LRP4 LBD bound to EEEV virus-like particles (VLPs), similarly to the positive control VLDLR-LBD-Fc, a known EEEV entry receptor19, but not to the negative control LDLRAD3-LA1-Fc, a VEEV-specific entry receptor38 (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. 5A). Reciprocally, VEEV VLPs bound to LDLRAD3-LA1-Fc but not to VLDLR-LBD-Fc or LRP4-LBD-Fc (Fig. 4B). Biolayer interferometry (BLI) assays confirmed that LRP4-LBD-Fc binds to EEEV VLPs, whereas VEEV VLPs exclusively interacted with LDLRAD3-LA1-Fc. To determine how individual LRP4 LA repeats contribute to virion binding, we expressed and purified Fc-tagged individual LA repeat domains (Supplementary Fig. 5B). ELISA showed that all LA repeat domains except LA8 could bind to EEEV VLPs, and BLI studies indicated that LA2, LA3, LA5, LA6, and LA7 had comparable binding (Fig. 4E, F). Among these repeat domains, we measured the apparent binding affinities of LA2 and LA7 for EEEV, which were 70.6 nM and 1.23 μM, respectively, compared to 149.3 nM for the full LBD (Fig. 4G–I).

We also assessed the binding of LRP4 LA repeats to EEEV glycoproteins displayed on the plasma membrane of cells (Fig. 4J). In HEK293T cells transfected with a plasmid expressing the EEEV glycoproteins, multiple LA repeat domains of LRP4 (LA2, LA6, LA7, and full LBD) showed strong binding, with LA2 being the most potent (Fig. 4K). In Vero CCL-81 cells infected with SINV-EEEV chimeric virus (Fig. 4L), the full LBD, LA2, and LA7 exhibited the strongest binding, followed by LA3 and LA4. Notably, LA1, despite minimally binding to EEEV in BLI and cell-based assays (Fig. 4F, K, L), still enhanced EEEV infection (Fig. 3G, I), possibly because of multivalent interactions between the VLPs and the protein displayed on the cell surface. Overall, these experiments demonstrate that multiple LA repeats of LRP4 can directly engage EEEV and support binding.

Given that LRP4 promotes infection by multiple encephalitic alphaviruses, decoy proteins were used to evaluate their blocking efficacy in primary mouse neurons and astrocytes. As expected, Western blotting showed that LRP4 was expressed in both primary cell types, with higher levels in neurons (Supplementary Fig. 6A). LBD-Fc, LA2-Fc, and LA7-Fc all efficiently inhibited SINV-EEEV infection in primary neurons and astrocytes (Fig. 5D–F and Supplementary Fig. 6B–D). Similar inhibition by LRP4 decoys was observed in human brain organoids that recapitulate the diverse cell types of the developing human brain, with reduced reporter expression and viral RNA levels detected (Fig. 5G, H).

We next evaluated the protective efficacy of LRP4 decoy proteins against authentic EEEV (FL93-939 strain) infection in vivo. LA2-Fc and LA7-Fc were used based on their potent blocking activity in vitro and superior bioavailability in vivo compared to the LBD-Fc, which could circulate less freely due to endogenous lipoprotein binding of the larger multi-domain proteins21,39. Co-administration of either LA2-Fc or LA7-Fc with EEEV improved survival rates and reduced clinical disease compared to the LDLRAD3-LA1-Fc negative control (Fig. 5I–L). LA7-Fc demonstrated complete protection, with all treated animals surviving the challenge and no symptoms occurring. These findings establish that specific LA repeats of LRP4 can block EEEV infection both in vitro and in vivo, highlighting their treatment potential.

Analysis of LRP4 mRNA expression compared to the three other known EEEV receptors revealed that both LRP4 and LRP8 are highly expressed in brain tissues (Supplementary Fig. 7A). Western blotting using validated antibodies confirmed that LDLR is abundant in mouse astrocytes, whereas LRP8 is present in both mouse neurons and astrocytes (Supplementary Fig. 7B). The overlapping tissue expression profiles of these receptors raise the possibility that part of the inhibitory activity observed with LRP4 decoy proteins may result from cross-blockade of other LDLR family members, assuming they bind to shared or spatially proximal sites on the viral glycoprotein.

Mammalian and avian orthologs of LRP4 promote EEEV infection

The broad host range of EEEV in enzootic and epizootic cycles35 prompted us to investigate the potential role of LRP4 in mediating cross-species transmission. We engineered chimeric LRP4 constructs containing the LBDs from representative mammals (Mus musculus, Equus caballus, Ovis aries, Capra hircus, Camelus ferus, Bos taurus, and Bubalus bubalis), birds (Poecile atricapillus, Haemorhous mexicanus, and Dromaius novaehollandiae), and mosquitoes (Aedes aegypti, Aedes albopictus, and Culex pipiens pallens), each fused to the O-linked sugar domain, transmembrane and intracellular domains of human LRP4, as in Fig. 3F and verified their expression in A549-BAT cells (Supplementary Fig. 8A–C). All tested mammalian LBDs, including those from Equus caballus, a key epizootic host for EEEV, enhanced EEEV infection (Fig. 5M). LBDs from three passerine avian species, known enzootic hosts of EEEV, also promoted infection, albeit with reduced efficiency compared to human LRP4 (Fig. 5N). In contrast, mosquito LRP4 homologs did not promote EEEV infection (Fig. 5O), possibly reflecting sequence divergence and evolutionary distance from vertebrate LRP4 (Supplementary Fig. 8D, E). To corroborate these findings, we expressed and purified Fc-tagged LRP4 LBDs from representative avian and mosquito species (Supplementary Fig. 8F). BLI analysis revealed that human and avian but not mosquito LRP4 LBDs bound avidly to EEEV VLPs (Fig. 5P).

Discussion

Alphaviruses employ multiple entry factors to mediate infection, tissue tropism, pathogenesis, and cross-species transmission. In this study, we conducted a CRISPR-based activation screen in A549-BAT cells deficient in several alphavirus attachment factors and identified SIGLEC1 and LRP4 as candidate proteins promoting EEEV infection. SIGLEC1, which is expressed primarily on macrophages and dendritic cells40, facilitates viral entry by binding to gangliosides on virion envelopes, such as HIV27, MLV28, and SARS-CoV-229. Although further study is warranted, SIGLEC1 might promote EEEV infection through a mechanism that does not require direct interaction with viral glycoproteins. In contrast, because the role of LRP4 in virus infection was unknown, we prioritized this protein for subsequent experiments.

LRP4 promoted infection of EEEV and other encephalitic alphaviruses, including WEEV, SFV and SINV. The LBD of LRP4 is the functional domain that directly binds to virions to mediate cell entry. Importantly, soluble LRP4 decoy proteins potently inhibited EEEV infection in human cell lines, primary mouse neuronal cells, mice and human brain organoids. Moreover, mammalian and avian LRP4 orthologs exhibited conserved proviral activity, implicating LRP4 as a shared entry receptor for encephalitic alphaviruses that may facilitate cross-species transmission among diverse hosts.

Like other LDLR family members (e.g., VLDLR for EEEV, SFV, and SINV19,33,41 and LDLR for EEEV, WEEV, SFV, and GETV20,34,42), LRP4 requires its LBD to promote viral entry. The eight LA repeats of LRP4 LBD exhibit functional redundancy: deletion of any single LA repeat did not impair EEEV infection, and expression of some individual domains (e.g., LA1, LA2, LA4, LA7) sufficiently restored infection to levels comparable to the full LBD. Although binding assays revealed variable avidity among LA repeat domains, functional blockade consistently identified LA2-Fc and LA7-Fc as the most potently inhibitory decoy molecules. Some of the discrepancies between the functional activity of different LA domains may arise from differences between experimental systems: the structural conformation of the EEEV glycoproteins displayed on the cell-surface may differ from those on VLPs used for ELISA and BLI, and VRPs containing viral genomes may better mimic authentic virion architecture than VLPs.

WEEV required the full LRP4 LBD for infection, unlike EEEV, highlighting divergent LA repeat usage among alphaviruses. This pattern has been reported previously with alphaviruses and VLDLR: EEEV preferentially engages LA1, LA2, LA3, LA5, and LA6 of VLDLR21–23; WEEV (McMillan strain) uses LA1, LA2, LA3, and LA5 of VLDLR43,44, and SFV uses LA345. LDLR also exhibits differential usage of LA repeats across different alphaviruses20. EEEV preferentially uses LA3 of LDLR for infection, whereas GETV uses LA4 or LA5 of LDLR42. While some alphaviruses (e.g., CHIKV-MXRA846, VEEV-LDLRAD347,48, and PCDH10-WEEV43,44,49,50) rely on high-affinity receptors, others like EEEV with VLDLR, adopt complex, multi-domain interactions through concurrent binding to the E1-E2 cleft and E2 domain A to enhance avidity21,22. Structural studies are needed to elucidate the precise binding mode of LRP4 LBD with EEEV glycoproteins.

EEEV is the most virulent encephalitic alphavirus35, with its high mortality rate in humans potentially linked to receptor usage and cell tropism. Beyond VLDLR, LRP8, and LDLR, all of which are expressed in the brain51–53, we confirmed abundant LRP4 expression in primary mouse cortical neurons and lower levels in astrocytes. In addition, LRP4 has functions elsewhere, including neuromuscular junction formation54,55, bone and limb development56,57, and muscle spindle formation and maintenance58. Notably, EEEV infection induces extracerebral disease manifestations in mice, such as abnormal bone formation, osteocyte loss, reduced osteoid formation, and cartilage hypertrophy4,59, which mirror some of the features observed in LRP4 KO models, suggesting that these manifestations might reflect LRP4-dependent viral tropism and cellular injury. Further tropism studies in LRP4 KO and conditional KO mice are needed to fully understand the contribution of LRP4 to the pathogenesis of EEEV.

Limitations of the study

We acknowledge several limitations in our study. (a) The structural basis of LRP4 LBD, particularly its multiple LA repeats, binding to EEEV virions in comparison to VLDLR or LDLR, remains to be elucidated; (b) LRP4-mediated viral tissue tropism and pathogenesis require validation in KO mouse models; (c) while we demonstrated protection via co-administration of LRP4-Fc decoy proteins with EEEV, post-exposure therapeutic activity was not assessed; and (d) several experiments utilized EEEV VRPs and SINV-EEEV chimeric viruses packaged with EEEV glycoproteins to minimize work with highly pathogenic strains.

In summary, we identified LRP4 as an entry receptor for EEEV and related encephalitic alphaviruses. The broad usage of LDLR family receptors by diverse alphaviruses underscores the complexity of viral infection and tropism in tissues, and whether there are attributes (e.g., conservation across phylogeny) that make these proteins attractive candidates as viral receptors. The usage of multiple receptors in the same family may enable broader species adaptation, which likely poses challenges for developing prevention and control strategies against these arboviruses.

Methods

Cells and viruses

A549 (ATCC #CCL-185), HEK293T (ATCC #CRL-3216), BHK-21 (ATCC #CCL-10), Huh7, HeLa (ATCC #CCL-2), HEK293T (ATCC #CRL-3216), Vero (ATCC #CCL-81), and U-87 MG (ATCC #HBT-14) were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Hyclone) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% L-glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 10 mM HEPES, 1× nonessential amino acids, and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin. K562 (ATCC #CCL-243) was propagated in RPMI-1640 (Gibco) with 10% FBS. SH-SY5Y (ATCC # CRL-2266) cells were propagated in DMEM/F12 (Gibco) with 10% FBS. HS-deficient (SLC35B2 and XYLT2 double knockout) HAP1 cells31 were maintained in Iscove’s modified Dulbecco medium (IMDM) supplemented with 10% FBS, GlutaMax, 10 mM HEPES, nonessential amino acids and Penicillin-Streptomycin. Expi293F cells were cultured in 293F Hi-exp culture medium (OPM). All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. Primary mouse neurons were cultured in Neurobasal medium (Gibco) supplemented with 1× B-27, 1% L-glutamine, and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin. Primary mouse astrocytes were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Hyclone) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% L-glutamine, 10 mM HEPES, 1× nonessential amino acids, and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin.

The following viruses were used: Sindbis virus (SINV) (Girdwood strain) expressing GFP (a gift from M. Heise, University of North Carolina), Getah virus (GETV) (GDHYLC23 strain) expressing mGreenLantern (mGreen)60, EEEV (MADV, Brazil 1985, obtained from K. Plante and S. Weaver, World Reference Center for Emerging Viruses and Arboviruses, The University of Texas Medical Branch)20, and chimeric SINV-EEEV (generated as previously described61,62 by replacing structural genes of SINV (Girdwood) with those of EEEV (FL93-939) with an mGreen reporter gene inserted between the capsid and E3 genes of EEEV). Viruses were propagated in BHK-21 and titrated by focus-forming assay.

Production of virus-like replicon particles

Virus-like replicon particles (VRPs) were packaged using two DNA-launched plasmids as previously described with modifications19 (Supplementary Fig. 1D). One plasmid was constructed based on CMV-driven SINV (Girdwood) infectious cDNA; the structural genes of SINV are replaced by the capsid genes of EEEV (FL-914697 strain), WEEV (93A79 strain), VEEV (TC-83), SFV (SFV-4 strain), or CHIKV (37997 strain), followed by a virus-specific capsid autoprotease cleavage peptide and an mGreen reporter63. The second plasmid expressed the glycoproteins (E3-E2-6K/TF-E1) of the different alphaviruses. These two plasmids were co-transfected into HEK293T to package replication-defective VRPs that can recapitulate the entry stage of authentic viruses under BSL-2 containment64. At 48 h post-transfection, supernatants were harvested, and VRP titers were analyzed by flow cytometry65.

Analysis and knockout of viral attachment factors

A549 cells were detached by TrypLE (Thermo #12563029), and surface expression of viral attachment factors was analyzed with antibodies against HS (10E4) (USBiological #H1890, 1 μg/mL), AXL (R&D system #MAB154-SP, 1 μg/mL), TYRO3 (R&D system #MAB859-SP, 1 μg/mL), TIM-1 (Biolegend #353902, 1 μg/mL), TIM-4 (Biolegend #354002, 1 μg/mL), MERTK (R&D system #MAB8912-SP, 1 μg/mL), and isotype controls including mouse IgM isotype (BD biosciences #553472, 1 μg/mL) and mouse IgG1 isotype (Biolegend #401401, 1 μg/mL). After washing, cells were stained with goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) conjugated with Alexa Fluor 647 (Thermo #A-21235, 2 μg/mL) or goat anti-mouse IgM (H + L) conjugated with Alexa Fluor 647 (Thermo #A-21238, 2 μg/mL). After washing, cells were analyzed using an Attune NxT flow cytometer. The Supplementary Fig. 1G shows the gating strategy of flow cytometry used in this study.

A549-BAT triple-knockout (B3GAT3, AXL, TIM-1) cells were generated by delivering CRISPR/Cas9-based RNP complexes using the Neon transfection system (Thermo). Oligo DNAs comprising 5′ T7 promoter, sgRNA, and 3′ overlap sequences were designed using the EnGen sgRNA template oligo-designer (https://sgrna.neb.com/#!/sgrna) and synthesized (GENEWIZ, Suzhou). sgRNA sequences are listed (Supplementary Data 2). sgRNAs were generated by in vitro transcription using the EnGen sgRNA synthesis kit (NEB #E3322V). sgRNA-Cas9 (RNP) complexes were electroporated into A549 cells. Clonal KO cell lines were established by limiting dilution and verified by Sanger sequencing and flow cytometry for surface expression.

CRISPR activation screen

The CRISPR activation sgRNA library targeting predicted 3849 human membrane proteins, with four independent sgRNAs per gene, was custom-constructed and packaged in HEK293T cells. After co-transfection with psPAX2 (Addgene #12260) and pMD2.G (Addgene #12259) at a ratio of 2:2:1 using Fugene HD transfection reagent, supernatants were harvested and stored at −80 °C. For the screen, A549-BAT cells expressing dCas9 and VP64 (A549-BAT-dCas9) were generated by lentiviral transduction of Lenti dCas-VP64_Blast (Addgene #61425). Cells were further transduced with the packaged lentivirus library at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of ~0.3. After puromycin selection, the cell library was inoculated with EEEV VRPs (MOI 10, 24 h). The cells were then harvested and sorted for the mGreen-positive population. Genomic DNA from sorted and uninfected cells was extracted for sgRNA amplification and next-generation sequencing using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. sgRNA sequences were trimmed using FASTX-Toolkit and cutadapt 1.8.1. sgRNA abundance and gene ranking were analyzed using MAGeCK software (Supplementary Data 1).

Gene validation by endogenous activation

Based on protein expression profiles, structural features, and cellular localization patterns, the top 11 out of 20 candidate genes were selected for validation using CRISPR-based endogenous gene activation. Two independent sgRNAs per gene (Supplementary Data 2) were designed and cloned into plasmid pXPR_502 (Addgene #96923). Lentiviruses were packaged with plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2 as described above. A549-BAT-dCas9 cells were transduced with lentiviruses expressing individual sgRNAs and selected with puromycin. The gene-activated cells were seeded for infection by EEEV VRPs (MOI 10, 8 h). The percentage of mGreen-positive cells was analyzed by flow cytometry.

Gene validation by overexpression and knockout

Human LRP4 cDNA (GenBank: XM_017017734.2) with a C-terminal HA tag was cloned into lentiviral plasmid pLV-EF1a-P2A-mCherry (derived from pLV-EF1a-IRES-Puro (Addgene #85132), where the IRES-Puro cassette was replaced by P2A-mCherry). A549, A549-BAT, K562, and U-87 MG cells were transduced with LRP4-expressing lentiviruses. The mCherry-positive cells were sorted and validated by Western blotting using anti-HA tag antibody (Sigma #05-904, 1 μg/mL).

LRP4-expressing cells were inoculated with EEEV VRPs (A549: MOI 1, 8 h; K562, MOI 10, 12 h; U-87 MG, MOI 1, 6 h). LRP4-expressing A549-BAT cells were inoculated with the following viruses or VRPs: EEEV VRPs (MOI 10, 8 h), VEEV VRPs (MOI 10, 12 h), WEEV VRPs (MOI 10, 12 h), SFV VRPs (MOI 10, 6 h), CHIKV VRPs (MOI 10, 12 h), SINV (MOI 1, 6 h), and GETV (MOI 10, 24 h). Cells were harvested, fixed with 2% PFA, and GFP- or mGreen-positive populations were analyzed by Attune NxT flow cytometer. For authentic EEEV infection, LRP4-expressing K562 cells were inoculated with EEEV (MADV, Brazil 1985) (MOI 1, 8 h). Cells were harvested, fixed with 2% PFA, and subjected to flow cytometry analysis of E1-positive cells.

The sgRNAs targeting human LRP4 were cloned into LentiCRISPR v2 (Addgene #52961) with puromycin selection markers. SH-SY5Y, Huh7, HeLa, and HEK293T cells were transduced with the packaged lentivirus to edit the LRP4 gene. Editing efficiency was confirmed by Western blotting using an anti-LRP4 antibody (Thermo #MA5-27675, 1 μg/mL). Similarly, sgRNAs targeting attachment factors and selected LDLR family members were cloned for use in A549 and SH-SY5Y cells. All sgRNA sequences are provided in Supplementary Data 2. For authentic EEEV infection, LRP4-edited HAP1 (SLC35B2/XYLT2 double knockout) cells were inoculated with EEEV (MADV, Brazil 1985) (MOI 1, 8 h), and E1-positive cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Virus binding and internalization assays

For virus binding assays, EEEV VRPs (MOI of 20) were incubated with vector- or LRP4-expressing A549-BAT or U-87 MG cells on ice for 45 min. After washing, cells were lysed with RL buffer (TIANGEN #DP-430) to extract total RNA according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the internalization assay, after binding and washing, cells were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Surface-bound virions were removed by treating cells with 400 μg/mL proteinase K (Thermo #AM2546) on ice for 45 min. After additional washing, cells were lysed for RNA extraction. The binding and internalization assays were also performed similarly in control- or LRP4-edited SH-SY5Y and Huh7 cells. The qRT-PCR was performed using TaqMan One Step PrimeScrip RT-PCR kit (Takara #RR064A) with GAPDH as an internal control. The primers and probes used were as follows: SINV-EEEV, forward primer 5′-AAGATCATTGACGCAGTCATC-3′, SINV-EEEV reverse primer 5′-GCTGTGGAGGTAACCGAATCT-3′, SINV-EEEV probe: 5′-FAM-CCACCTTACTTCTGCGGTGGATTCA-BHQ-1-3′; GAPDH forward primer 5′-GAAGGTGAAGGTCGGAGT-3′, GAPDH reverse primer 5′-GAAGATGGTGATGGGATTTC-3′, GAPDH probe 5′-FAM-CAAGCTTCCCGTTCTCAGCC-BHQ-1-3′.

Replicon assay

A SINV (Girdwood) replicon plasmid was constructed by replacing structural genes with mGreen reporter downstream of the 26S sub-genomic promoter (Fig. 2F). Linearized plasmid was transcribed using mMESSAGE mMACHINE SP6 kit (Thermo #AM1340). Replicon RNAs were Lithium Chloride (LiCl)- precipitated and stored at −80 °C. To assess the effect of LRP4 on SINV replication, we transfected replicon RNAs into vector- and LRP4-expressing A549-BAT cells using the Neon transfection system. The mGreen-positive cells and geometric MFI were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Pseudotyped virus infection

Murine leukemia virus-based pseudovirus particles (MLVpp) were packaged as described44. Briefly, pCAGGS expressing EEEV (FL93-393) glycoproteins (E3-E2-6K/TF-E1) were co-transfected into HEK293T cells with retrovector pMIG (kindly provided by J. Li, Fudan University) for which the gene of target was replaced by mGreen gene, and MLV Gag-Pol plasmid. Supernatants were harvested, and virus entry was assessed by transduction of MLVpp bearing EEEV glycoproteins in vector- and LRP4-expressing A549-BAT cells. Thirty-six hours later, cells were collected to measure the mGreen-positive cells by flow cytometry.

VSV-based pseudovirus particles (VSVpp) were packaged as previously described66. HEK293T cells transfected with EEEV glycoproteins were infected with single-cycle scVSVΔG-Nluc-GFP virus66 for 2 h. After washing, fresh medium was added in the presence of anti-VSV-G antibody (1 μg/mL). VSVpp were harvested to infect vector- and LRP4-expressing A549-BAT cells, and GFP-positive cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

LRP4 truncations and expression

Full-length LRP4 cDNA (GenBank: XM_017017734.2) and truncated LRP4 constructs (ΔLBD: residues Δ25–350; ΔEGF: residues Δ351–1649) were cloned into the pLV-EF1a-P2A-mCherry vector with a C-terminal HA tag. Based on the ΔEGF LRP4 construct, each LA repeat within the LBD was individually deleted: ΔLA1 (residues Δ26–67), ΔLA2 (residues Δ70–106), ΔLA3 (residues Δ109–144), ΔLA4 (residues Δ147–183), ΔLA5 (residues Δ190–226), ΔLA6 (residues Δ230–266), ΔLA7 (residues Δ169–305), and ΔLA8 (residues Δ311–350). To express single LA domains, LA1 (residues 26–67), LA2 (residues 70–106), LA3 (residues 109–144), LA4 (residues 147–183), LA5 (residues 190–226), LA6 (residues 230–266), LA7 (residues 169–305), or LA8 (residues 311–350) was fused to the O-linked sugar domain, transmembrane domain, and cytoplasmic domain with a C-terminal HA tag. To express the LRP4 LBD or LA repeats in the context of an MXRA8 scaffold, LBD (residues 25–350), LA1 (residues 26–67), LA2 (residues 70–106), LA3 (residues 109–144), LA4 (residues 147–183), LA5 (residues 190–226), LA6 (residues 230–266), LA7 (residues 169–305), or LA8 (residues 311–350) with intervening (GGGGS)×5 linker sequences were fused to the transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains of MXRA8 with a C-terminal HA tag. All constructs were cloned into the pLV-EF1a-P2A-mCherry vector. Lentiviruses were packaged as described above and transduced into A549 or A549-BAT cells. The mCherry-positive cells were sorted, and expression of LRP4 truncations was verified by Western blotting or flow cytometry using an anti-HA antibody (Sigma #05-904, 1 μg/mL). EEEV or WEEV VRPs were used to infect A549 (MOI 1, 6 h) or A549-BAT (MOI 10, 8 h) cells, and mGreen-positive cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Recombinant LRP4 protein expression and purification

Human LRP4 LBD (residues 25–350), LA1 (residues 26–67), LA2 (residues 70–106), LA3 (residues 109–144), LA4 (residues 147–183), LA5 (residues 190–226), LA6 (residues 230–266), LA7 (residues 169–205), or LA8 (residues 311–305) with an upstream human IL-2 signal peptide and a downstream human IgG1 Fc tag were cloned into a CMV-driven expression vector. Similarly, LRP4 LBD from three mosquito species (Aedes aegypti, XP_021694933.1; Aedes albopictus, XP_019559720.3; Culex pipiens pallens, XP_039440150.1) and two avian species (Poecile atricapillus, XP_058708178.1; Dromaius novaehollandiae, XP_064368752.1) were codon-optimized for humans and cloned for expression and purification. To enhance folding, constructs were co-transfected into Expi293F cells with a plasmid expressing the chaperone protein LRPAP1 (NM_002337.4). Supernatants were harvested and filtered by 0.45 μm PES membranes, and Fc fusion proteins were purified by protein A chromatography (Genescript #L00210) via a gravity flow column. Proteins were concentrated and buffer-exchanged into 1× TBS (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 8.0) or 1× HBS (25 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl) containing 2 mM CaCl2 using Amicon 10 kDa or 30 kDa centrifugal filters (Millipore). Protein purity was assessed by SDS-PAGE followed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining (Beyotime). Negative control protein LDLRAD3-LA1-Fc48 and positive control protein VLDLR-LBD-Fc23 proteins were generated and purified as previously described.

Production of virus-like particles (VLPs)

EEEV and VEEV VLPs were produced as previously described23,48. Briefly, codon-optimized genes encoding EEEV structural proteins (PE-6, GenBank: AAU95735.1) with a K67N mutation in capsid or VEEV structural proteins (TC-83, GenBank: AAB02517.1) with a K64N mutation in capsid were synthesized and cloned into pCMV vectors. VLPs were produced in Expi293F cells by transfection with structural protein expression plasmids. After 60 h, supernatants were harvested and concentrated through a 30% (w/v) sucrose cushion by centrifugation at 110,000 × g for 2.5 h at 4 °C. Pelleted VLPs were resuspended in 20 mM HEPES buffer with 150 mM NaCl (pH 8.0) and further purified by sucrose density gradient ultracentrifugation (20–60% w/v) at 140,000 × g for 15 h. Gradient fractions containing VLPs were collected and stored at −80 °C.

ELISA-based binding assay

ELISA microplates (Corning) were coated with 0.2 μg/mL EEEV (PE-6) or VEEV (TC-83) VLPs at 4 °C overnight and blocked with 5% FBS in PBS at 37 °C for 1 h. After washing, serial dilutions of Fc fusion proteins (LRP4-LBD-Fc, LRP4-LA1-Fc, LRP4-LA2-Fc, LRP4-LA3-Fc, LRP4-LA4-Fc, LRP4-LA5-Fc, LRP4-LA6-Fc, LRP4-LA7-Fc, LRP4-LA8-Fc, VLDLR-LBD-Fc, and LDLRAD3-LA1-Fc) were added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Plates were washed five times by PBS and incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-human IgG second antibody (Promega #W4031, 1:4000) for 1 h. The plates were washed again and incubated with 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine substrate (Thermo #34028). The reactions were stopped with 1 M H2SO4. OD450 nm absorbance was measured by a Bio-Rad microplate reader.

Cell surface display-based binding assay

HEK293T cells were transfected with plasmids expressing EEEV glycoproteins (E3-E2-6K/TF-E1). After 36 h, cells were detached using TrypLE and incubated with increasing concentrations of LRP4-LBD-Fc, LRP4-LA1-Fc, LRP4-LA2-Fc, LRP4-LA3-Fc, LRP4-LA4-Fc, LRP4-LA5-Fc, LRP4-LA6-Fc, LRP4-LA7-Fc, LRP4-LA8-Fc, or LDLRAD3-LA1-Fc at 4 °C for 30 min. Cells were washed and incubated with Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated goat anti-human IgG antibody (Thermo, #A-21445, 2 μg/mL) at 4 °C for 30 min. After washing, cells were resuspended for flow cytometry analysis. Alternatively, Vero CCL-81 cells were infected with SINV-EEEV (MOI 10, 16 h) and processed for Fc protein binding as described above. The percentage of LRP4-bound cells were quantified by flow cytometry.

Biolayer interferometry (BLI)

Binding kinetics was measured at 25 °C using an Octet R8 biosensor system (Sartorius) as previously described23. Briefly, LRP4-LBD-Fc, LRP4-LA1-Fc, LRP4-LA2-Fc, LRP4-LA3-Fc, LRP4-LA4-Fc, LRP4-LA5-Fc, LRP4-LA6-Fc, LRP4-LA7-Fc, LRP4-LA8-Fc, VLDLR-LBD-Fc, or LDLRAD3-LA1-Fc (25 μg/mL) were loaded onto ProA Biosensors (Sartorius #18-5010) after a 180 s baseline measurement in loading buffer. EEEV (PE-6) or VEEV (TC-83) VLPs (100 μg/mL) were captured for 300 s, followed by dissociation in loading buffer for 300 s. For the measurement of LRP4 LBDs from avian and mosquito species, 200 μg/mL of EEEV (PE-6) VLPs were used.

For binding affinity measurements, 25 μg/mL of biotinylated antibody EEEV-14323,67 was immobilized onto the SA Biosensors (Sartorius #18-5019) for 3 min, and then EEEV (PE-6) VLPs (50 μg/mL) were incubated with the biosensor for 30 min. Subsequently, the tips were washed with buffer (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, pH 8.0, 0.1% BSA) for 3 min to obtain a baseline reading, then the biosensors were dipped into wells containing the LRP4-LBD-Fc, LRP4-LA2-Fc, and LRP4-LA7-Fc at various different concentrations for 5 min (association), followed by dissociation in buffer (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, pH 8.0, 0.1% BSA) for 5 min. The apparent KD values were calculated using Octet Data Analysis software. Two independent experiments were performed for each sample.

Soluble protein blockade assay

Replication-competent SINV-EEEV chimeric virus (MOI 3) was pre-incubated with LRP4-LBD-Fc, LRP4-LA1-Fc, LRP4-LA2-Fc, LRP4-LA3-Fc, LRP4-LA4-Fc, LRP4-LA5-Fc, LRP4-LA6-Fc, LRP4-LA7-Fc, LRP4-LA8-Fc, or LDLRAD3-LA1-Fc at 37 °C for 1 h. Proteins were serially diluted (10-fold, starting at 10 μg/mL). Virus-protein mixtures were added to Vero CCL-81, HEK293T, and A549 cells for 12 h, and the percentage of GFP-positive cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Isolation of primary mouse neurons and astrocytes and infection assay

Primary neurons were prepared as described68,69. Briefly, primary cortical and hippocampal neurons from embryonic day 17 (E17) C57BL/6J mouse embryos were dissociated using papain, plated on poly-D-lysine-coated 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well, and maintained in Neurobasal medium with B-27 and GlutaMAX. After 7 days, LRP4-LBD-Fc, LRP4-LA2-Fc, LRP4-LA7-Fc, or LDLRAD3-LA1-Fc (30 or 100 μg/mL) were pre-incubated with SINV-EEEV (MOI 1) at 37 °C for 1 h before infecting the neurons. At 6 h post-infection, cells were fixed with 2% PFA, stained with DAPI, and analyzed by Operetta high-content imaging.

Primary cortical astrocytes from neonatal C57BL/6J mice were prepared as described70. Briefly, cerebral cortex tissues were digested using trypsin, plated in T75 flasks, and purified by differential shaking to remove microglia and oligodendrocyte precursor cells. Astrocytes were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well and cultured for another 7 days. LRP4-LBD-Fc, LRP4-LA2-Fc, LRP4-LA7-Fc, or LDLRAD3-LA1-Fc (3 or 10 μg/mL) were pre-incubated with SINV-EEEV (MOI 3) at 37 °C for 1 h before infecting the astrocytes. At 24 h post-infection, cells were fixed, stained with DAPI, and imaged as above.

The expression of LRP4, LDLR, or LRP8 in primary mouse neurons and astrocytes was also determined by Western blotting using the following antibodies: anti-LRP4 (Thermo #MA5-27675, 1 μg/mL), anti-LDLR (Proteintech #10785-1-AP, 0.5 μg/mL), and anti-LRP8 (Thermo #MA5-53503, 0.5 μg/mL).

Human cerebral organoid differentiation and infection assay

Human embryonic stem cells H9 were differentiated into cerebral organoids using the STEMdiff Cerebral Organoid Kit (STEMCELL #08570) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, H9 cells were detached by cell dissociation reagent and re-suspended in embryoid body culture medium. Cells were plated onto an ultra-low adherent 96-well plate at a density of 9000/well. Embryoid body-forming medium was supplied at days 2 and 4 post-seeding. At day 7, differentiated embryoid bodies were embedded with Matrigel and transferred to an ultra-low adherent 6-well plate and continued to culture using embryoid body expansion medium for another 3 days. At day 10, embryoid body maturation medium was changed, and human cerebral organoids (hCOs) were cultured for another 10 days. At day 20 post-seeding, mature hCOs were used for blocking assays. Thirty micrograms per milliliter of LRP4-LBD-Fc, LRP4-LA2-Fc, LRP4-LA7-Fc, or LDLRAD3-LA1-Fc were pre-incubated with SINV-EEEV (10,000 PFU/organoid) at 37 °C for 1 h before infecting the hCOs. Fluorescence images were captured at 12 h intervals. At 24 h post-infection, hCOs were lysed, and total RNAs were extracted (TIAGEN #DP419). Viral RNA was quantified by qRT-PCR using TaqMan One Step PrimeScript RT-PCR Kit (Takara #RR064A). In vitro transcribed SINV replicon RNAs were serially diluted as templates to generate a standard curve. The following primers and probes were used: forward, 5′-AAGATCATTGACGCAGTCATC-3′; reverse, 5′-GCTGTGGAGGTAACCGAATCT-3′; probe, 5′-FAM- CCACCTTACTTCTGCGGTGGATTCA-BHQ-1-3′.

Mouse studies

Six-week-old male CD-1 mice were housed under controlled environment conditions (23 ± 2 °C, 30–70% relative humidity, 15 air changes per hour). Animal procedures performed at the University of Pittsburgh were carried out with approval of their Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee following the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Virus inoculations were performed under anesthesia induced by ketamine hydrochloride and xylazine. 103 PFU of EEEV (FL93-939) was pre-incubated with 100 μg of LA2-Fc, LA7-Fc, or control LDLRAD3-LA1-Fc at room temperature for 30 min. Mice were inoculated subcutaneously in both footpads with the virus-protein mixture. After 24 h, mice were administered 200 µg of each protein via intraperitoneal injection. Clinical signs of disease, weight loss, and survival were monitored daily. Authentic virus infection and mouse studies were performed under BSL-3 containment.

Expression of LRP4 orthologs and LDLR family members

The LBD sequences of LRP4 from different species including Mus musculus (NP_766256.3), Equus caballus (XM_023653983.2), Ovis aries (XP_027835505.2), Capra hircus (XP_017914617.1), Camelus ferus (XP_032344456.1), Bos taurus (NP_001071311.1), Bubalus bubalis (XP_025121887.3), Poecile atricapillus (XP_058708178.1), Haemorhous mexicanus (XP_059705364.1), Dromaius novaehollandiae (XP_064368752.1), Aedes aegypti (XP_021694933.1), Aedes albopictus (XP_019559720.3), and Culex pipiens pallens (XP_039440150.1) were codon-optimized for humans, synthesized, and fused to the O-linked sugar domain, transmembrane domain, and intracellular domains of human LRP4. All chimeric LRP4 orthologs with a C-terminal HA tag were cloned into the pLV-EF1a-P2A-mCherry plasmid. Lentiviruses were packaged and transduced into A549-BAT cells, and protein expression was verified by flow cytometry. Cells expressing individual LRP4 orthologs were seeded and inoculated with EEEV VRPs (MOI 10, 8 h). The percentage of mGreen-positive cells was analyzed by flow cytometry. The alignment and phylogenetic tree of LBD amino acid sequences from different LRP4 orthologues were analyzed using Mega 11.0 software.

The LDLR family members, including LDLRAD1 (NM_001010978.4), LDLR (NM_000527.5), VLDLR (NM_003383.5), LRP3 (NM_002333.4), LRP5 (NM_002335.4), LRP6 (NM_002336.3), LRP8 (NM_004631.5), LRP9 (NM_003105.6), LRP12 (NM_013437.5), LRP10 (NM_014045.5), LRP11 (NM_032832.6), LDLRAD2 (NM_001013693.3), LDLRAD3 (NM_174902.4), LDLRAD4 (NM_001378100.1), and LDLRAP1 (NM_015627.3) with a C-terminal HA tag, were cloned into pLV-EF1a-P2A-mCherry plasmid. Lentiviruses were packaged and transduced into A549-BAT cells, and protein expression was verified by Western blotting using an anti-HA tag antibody (Sigma #05-904, 1 μg/mL). Cells expressing individual LDLR family members were inoculated with EEEV VRPs (MOI 10, 8 h). The percentage of mGreen-positive cells was analyzed by flow cytometry.

Phylogenetic analysis of alphavirus

The following alphavirus glycoprotein sequences were used for phylogenetic analysis: CHIKV (QKY67868.1), GETV (WNT93885.1), ONNV (KX771232.1), MAYV (QDL88200.1), UNAV (YP_009665989.1), SFV (NP_463458.1), RRV (AYI50356.1), VEEV (AAC19322.1), EEEV (AAT96380.1), MADV (AXV43855.1), WEEV (AIC81896.1), SINV (AAA86136.1), AURV (NP_632024.1), WHAV (AEJ36239.1), HJV (YP_002802300.1), FMV (AAO33321.1), BCV (AEJ36227.1), BBKV (AVN98166.1), and OCKV (P27285.1). All sequences were obtained from NCBI. Mega 11.0 was used to perform sequence alignment, phylogenetic analysis, and visualization.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was assigned when P values were <0.05 using Prism Version 10 (GraphPad). Data analysis was performed using an unpaired two-sided t-test, one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s t-test, or two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparisons correction. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method with the log-rank test and the Holm–Šídák multiple comparisons correction.
