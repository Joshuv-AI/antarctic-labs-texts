Improved Accuracy for Private Continual Cardinality Estimation in Fully Dynamic Streams via Matrix Factorization
Joel Daniel Andersson, Palak Jain, Satchit Sivakumar

Abstract
We study differentially-private statistics in the fully dynamic continual observation model, where many updates can arrive at each time step and updates to a stream can involve both insertions and deletions of an item. Earlier work (e.g., Jain et al., NeurIPS 2023 for counting distinct elements; Raskhodnikova & Steiner, PODS 2025 for triangle counting with edge updates) reduced the respective cardinality estimation problem to continual counting on the difference stream associated with the true function values on the input stream. In such reductions, a change in the original stream can cause many changes in the difference stream, this poses a challenge for applying private continual counting algorithms to obtain optimal error bounds. We improve the accuracy of several such reductions by studying the associated ℓ p \ell_{p} -sensitivity vectors of the resulting difference streams and isolating their properties. We demonstrate that our framework gives improved bounds for counting distinct elements, estimating degree histograms, and estimating triangle counts (under a slightly relaxed privacy model), thus offering a general approach to private continual cardinality estimation in streaming settings. Our improved accuracy stems from tight analysis of known factorization mechanisms for the counting matrix in this setting; the key technical challenge is arguing that one can use state-of-the-art factorizations for sensitivity vector sets with the properties we isolate. In particular, we show that for approximate DP, the celebrated square-root factorization of the counting matrix (Henzinger et al., SODA 2023; Fichtenberger et al., ICML 2023) can be employed to give concrete improvements in accuracy over past work based on the binary tree mechanism. We also give a tight analysis of b b -ary tree mechanisms with subtraction under these sensitivity patterns, including a polytime routine for computing the ℓ p \ell_{p} sensitivity, yielding time and space efficient algorithms for both pure and approximate DP. Empirically and analytically, we demonstrate that our improved error bounds offer a substantial improvement in accuracy for cardinality estimation problems over a large range of parameters.

Introduction

Privacy is a central challenge for systems that process sensitive data, especially in settings where data evolves continuously and must be monitored or used over time.
The continual observation model of differential privacy
[
13
,
23
]
provides rigorous guarantees for individual privacy in exactly such settings. In this work, we revisit the problem of differentially private cardinality estimation under continual observation in the challenging
fully dynamic
setting where updates can involve insertions or deletions.

Our main contribution is to show that new analyses of factorization mechanisms can yield concrete accuracy improvements for several problems in the continual observation model of differential privacy.
To enable this, we (1) define and analyze a new structured class of sensitivity vectors that arise in reductions from cardinality estimation problems like counting distinct elements (
\mathsf{CountDistinct}
), releasing degree histograms (
\mathsf{DegreeCount}
), and releasing triangle counts (
\mathsf{TriangleCount}
) to continual counting;
(2) prove error bounds for factorization mechanisms based on Toeplitz factorizations for continual counting with respect to this new class of sensitivity vectors; and
(3) show that even tree-aggregation-based factorization mechanisms admit improved error guarantees when adapted to sensitivity vectors with the structure we identify.

Inability to Directly Use Improved Mechanisms

For some applications (such as in differentially private machine learning), the continual counting algorithms are run on data streams where for a neighboring input stream, the input differs by a bounded amount at exactly one (or a small constant number of) time steps.
In this case, the improved mechanisms for continual counting can be leveraged directly to obtain better utility.

In cardinality estimation problems such as counting distinct elements or releasing the degree list of a graph, the reduction to continual counting is more complicated. A frequent strategy for such problems is to construct the
difference stream

\mathit{d}_{\,f}[t]=f(\vec{\mathbf{x}}[0:t])-f(\vec{\mathbf{x}}[0:t-1])

Their analysis is also tailored to

This motivates the main question of this paper.

Can we improve the accuracy of differentially private cardinality estimation under continual observation by replacing the binary tree mechanism with other continual counting mechanisms?

We answer this question affirmatively, giving significant improvements in accuracy for several cardinality estimation problems.

Technical Overview

3.1
Overview of Framework

In order to prove the results in the previous section, we start by characterizing properties of sensitivity vectors for our problems of interest. We will then prove general results for problems whose sensitivity vectors satisfy these properties, which will give a unified framework from which we obtain results for all the cardinality estimation problems.

For a function
f
, let
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
and
\vec{\mathbf{y}}
be two neighboring streams and let
\mathit{d}_{\,f}
be a function that takes a stream
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
and produces the corresponding difference stream
\mathit{d}_{\,f}(\vec{\mathbf{x}})[t]=f(\vec{\mathbf{x}}[0:t])-f(\vec{\mathbf{x}}[0:t-1])
. Since we are interested in applying continual counting mechanisms to the difference streams, a key object in our study is the sensitivity vector
\vec{\Delta}_{f}=\mathit{d}_{\,f}(\vec{\mathbf{x}})-\mathit{d}_{\,f}(\vec{\mathbf{y}})
. Note that if the function
f
is vector valued, so are
\mathit{d}_{\,f}[t]
and
\vec{\Delta}_{f}[t]
.

Sensitivity Vector Sets for Cardinality Estimation Problems (

Our first main contribution is characterizing properties of sensitivity vectors that we can leverage to analyze the continual counting mechanisms of interest.

\displaystyle\mathsf{MaxSE}(L,R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})

\displaystyle\mathsf{MeanSE}(L,R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})

\displaystyle\mathsf{MaxSE}(L,R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{\pi\log(e)}-o(1)\right)\sqrt{kD\log(DT/k)\log T}\,,

\displaystyle\mathsf{MeanSE}(L,R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{\pi\log(e)}-o(1)\right)\sqrt{kD\log(DT/k)\log T}\,.

3.2
Techniques Used in Proofs

Reduction from
S_{D,k}
Streams to
S_{1,k}
Streams.
A key ingredient used throughout our proofs is a reduction from reasoning about a stream in
S_{D,k}
to reasoning about streams from
S_{1,k}
. We show the following in
Section

7
:

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
1\leq k\leq D\leq T
,
1\leq m\leq T\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
R
be any
m\times T
matrix.
Then,

D\cdot\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,\lfloor k/D\rfloor})\,\leq\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})\leq\max_{k_{1},\dots,k_{D}:\sum_{i=1}^{D}k_{i}=k}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,k_{i}})

The lower bound in this result is straightforward and follows from simple facts about the scaling of norms. The upper bound is more intricate and involves breaking up a sensitivity stream
\vec{\Delta}
from
S_{D,k}
into up to
D
different sensitivity streams
\vec{\Delta}^{(i)}
from
S_{1,k_{i}}
, where
\sum_{i=1}^{D}k_{i}\leq k
. Each stream corresponds to a distinct value that a prefix sum of
\vec{\Delta}
can take, and the streams are carefully created such that
\sum_{i=1}^{D}\vec{\Delta}^{(i)}=\vec{\Delta}
. Combining structural properties of this decomposition with basic norm inequalities yields the upper bound. Details appear in
Section

7
.

This reduction is crucial throughout our analysis because streams in
S_{1,k}
enjoy a much more rigid structure than those in
S_{D,k}
: they are
alternating
, meaning their non-zero entries have magnitude
1
and alternate in sign.

Techniques for Toeplitz Factorization Mechanisms.

We overview our techniques for Toeplitz factorizations here (full details can be found in
Section

8
.)

Overview of Upper Bound.
We give two proofs of Theorem
3.1
of different flavors. One proof is combinatorial and the other is more algebraic. Via the known link between sensitivity
\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})
and factorization error, applying this to the square-root factorization yields the error upper bounds of
Theorem

3.2
.

Let
c(i,j)=(R^{\top}R)[i,j]
. The starting point of both proofs is analyzing the quadratic form
\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}
. As a precursor, we show that monotonicity of lower diagonal values of
R
induces useful monotonicity properties of
c(\cdot,\cdot)
that we use in both proofs.

The combinatorial proof proceeds by first considering the alternating case
S_{1,k}
. We decompose the quadratic form into (i) a “squared term” of the form
c(i,i)\vec{\Delta}[i]^{2}
and (ii) a “cross term” involving
c(i,j)\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]
for
i\neq j
. The key step is to show that the cross term is non-positive. Using the alternating structure, whenever
\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]=1
for
i<j
, we can match this pair with another
i<j^{\prime}
where
\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j^{\prime}]=-1
and
j^{\prime}
lies closer to
i
. Monotonicity properties of
c(\cdot,\cdot)
then let us show that each matched pair contributes a non-positive amount to the cross term. This helps us prove Theorem
3.1
for
D=1
, which we then extend to all
D
using the reduction from
S_{D,k}
streams to
S_{1,k}
streams in Theorem
3.4
. Details appear in
Section

8.2
.

The second proof is linear algebraic and reasons about
S_{D,k}
directly; we find it more elegant, but much less intuitive. A key quantity that we work with is
\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}c(i,j)\vec{\Delta}[j]
. Writing
SUM_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{j}\vec{\Delta}[i]
, we write
\vec{\Delta}[j]
as a difference of adjacent prefix sums
SUM_{j+1}-SUM_{j}
, and then change indices of the sum-
\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}c(i,j)(SUM_{j+1}-SUM_{j})=\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}[c(i,j)-c(i,j+1)]\cdot SUM_{j}
, a transformation commonly called
Abel summation
. It turns out that the latter quantity is easier to reason about via the monotonicity of
c(\cdot,\cdot)
, and we leverage this carefully to prove the theorem. The details can be found in
Section

8.2
.

Overview of Lower Bound.

For the lower bound on the sensitivity
\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})
of the square-root factorization, we use the probabilistic method. We first treat
S_{1,k}
streams: draw sensitivity vector
\vec{\Delta}
uniformly at random from
S_{1,k}
(conditioned on
\|\vec{\Delta}\|_{1}=k
) and analyze
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\sqrt{A}\cdot\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}\right]
. We split the expectation into a squared term involving
\mathbb{E}[\vec{\Delta}[i]^{2}]
and a cross term involving
\mathbb{E}[\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]]
for
i\neq j
. The former is easy to reason about because each coordinate of
\vec{\Delta}
is equally likely to be non-zero.
Bounding the cross term ends up being the key challenge of the proof, which boils down to analyzing the probability that
\vec{\Delta}[i]\neq\vec{\Delta}[j]
, conditioned on them being non-zero.
This corresponds to the number of non-zero values falling between
i
and
j
being even, which is exactly the parity of a hypergeometric random variable. We hence prove a result characterizing this probability (which may be of independent interest) and use it to get a handle on the cross term. Controlling the absolute value of the cross term ends up requiring intricate analytical manipulation; we refer readers to
Section

8.3
for the details. This gives the results for
S_{1,k}
streams; extending to
S_{D,k}
streams again uses Theorem
3.4
.

Techniques for Tree-Aggregation-Based Factorization Mechanisms.

For tree aggregation, we work directly with the combinatorial structure of the tree and again begin with
S_{1,k}
streams, exploiting their alternating property. We reduce the sensitivity analysis (for
\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})
) to a new combinatorial problem we call
parity counting on trees
: given a complete
b
-ary tree with
T
leaves and
k
balls placed on leaves, how many internal nodes have an odd number of balls in their subtree? Due to the alternating structure of
S_{1,k}
streams, maximizing this quantity yields an exact characterization of the sensitivity
\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})
of the tree-aggregation matrix.

We use this characterization to derive upper and lower bounds that are tight up to lower order terms and to design a dynamic programming algorithm that computes the
\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})
exactly in polynomial time. Our combinatorial view also motivates a sparsified tree factorization tailored to our setting: since standard tree factorizations use only a
(1-1/b)
fraction of nodes, an equivalent fraction of nodes with an odd number of balls in their subtree can be ignored when computing the sensitivity. Combining these insights with the reduction to
S_{1,k}
streams yields Theorem
3.3
and related results; see
Section

9
.

3.3
Challenges in Analyzing Factorization Mechanisms

For continual counting on
S_{D,k}
-streams, the case when
k=D=1
has been studied extensively in prior work. Here, the square-root factorization achieves near-optimal bounds
[
30
,
38
]
. One might therefore wonder whether those techniques extend naturally to
k>1
(even when
D=1
). In this section, we explain why our different analytical approaches are necessary.

The error of any factorization mechanism depends on the value

\max_{\vec{\Delta}}\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}
,
where
R
is the right factor, giving a natural optimization problem.
When
k=1
, this optimization problem has a simple solution;
\max_{\vec{\Delta}}\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}
is exactly equal to
\|R\|_{1\to 2}
, the largest
\ell_{2}
norm over columns of
R
, a value that can be computed efficiently by iterating over the columns. Additionally, for structured factorizations like the square root mechanism,
R
is a lower-triangular Toeplitz matrix with monotonically decreasing diagonals. This means that the worst-case sensitivity vector is always
[1,0,\dots,0]
, allowing for direct computation of the sensitivity
\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,1})
. A natural first attempt to handle larger
k
might be to reduce to
k=1
by appealing to the operator norm of
R
. This would however give a suboptimal linear dependence on
k
(as opposed to the square root dependence we achieve).

Computational Challenges for Higher
k
.
To make progress for larger
k
, we first restricted attention to the class of alternating sensitivity vectors—those with integral entries of magnitude at most
1
whose non-zero values alternate in sign, motivated by the structure arising from our cardinality estimation problems.
However, even in this simplified setting, directly computing
\max_{\vec{\Delta}}\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}
is computationally prohibitive: A naive brute-force enumeration would have to iterate over
\Theta\bigl(T^{\,\min(k,\,n-k)}\bigr)
candidate sensitivity vectors, which is infeasible as
k
gets larger. In general, a convex quadratic
maximization
problem over a discrete set can be NP-hard. Thus, unlike the
k=1
setting, we cannot hope to solve the optimization problem by enumeration.

Identifying the Hardest Sensitivity Vector via Structure of Matrix.
One may instead hope to identify the ‘hardest’ sensitivity vector directly, as is possible for the square root factorization when
k=1
. Indeed, experiments on the square-root matrix for various values of
k
exhibit an encouraging heuristic pattern: the nonzero entries of the optimal alternating
k
-sparse vector tend to be somewhat evenly spaced. (See
Figure

2
for
T=30
and
k\in[1,10]
.)

Figure 2
:
Heatmap showing the alternating
k
-sparse sensitivity vectors
\vec{\Delta}
which maximize
\|\sqrt{A}\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}
for each
k\in[1,10]
and
n=30
. The yellow squares represent
1
, the purple squares represent
-1
and the teal squares represent
0
.

Unfortunately, there does not appear to be a clean closed-form description of these vectors. The alternating constraint introduces subtle interactions: note that to optimize
\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}
, we are interested in the norm of the linear combination of
k
columns (where some columns are added and others are subtracted). Inserting a
-1
at a particular position in
\vec{\Delta}
can increase the absolute value of some coordinates of
R\vec{\Delta}
(e.g., by making a previously small negative entry more negative), while simultaneously decreasing others (e.g., by cancelling positive contributions). How these effects trade off depends opaquely on the values of the matrix
R
.
Lacking a usable closed-form characterization, we ultimately abandoned the direct optimization approach from prior work and instead developed the combinatorial and algebraic upper-bound techniques and the probabilistic lower bound techniques described earlier.

Related Work

Differential Privacy under Continual Observation.

As described in the introduction, the seminal works of Dwork et al.
[
23
]
and Chan et al.
[
13
]
defined the continual observation model of differential privacy where inputs are received as a stream and the goal is to track a statistic over time while maintaining privacy. The version of the model they considered involves a single element arriving per time step and a single output being released per time step. We consider a more general model where many elements can arrive/updates can occur per time step, with a single output still released over each time step. This more general model has been studied in papers on private continual observation for graph problems
[
57
,
29
,
45
]
.

Continual Counting and the Binary Tree Mechanism.

Continual counting (or summation of bits) is a fundamental primitive used in many data analysis tasks. The papers defining the continual observation model (
[
13
]
and
[
23
]
) also gave the binary tree mechanism, a mechanism for continual counting with error scaling polylogarithmically in the time horizon
T
.

Mechanisms for continual counting are the main building block used to give mechanisms for many tasks in the continual observation literature, for example, continual observation of histograms
[
12
,
44
,
37
]
, state-of-the-art mechanisms for differentially private machine learning
[
46
,
20
,
16
,
15
,
58
,
54
]
, various frequency statistics on a stream
[
9
,
31
,
43
,
27
,
18
]
, continual observation of various graph statistics
[
57
,
29
,
45
,
56
,
26
]
, and continual observation for clustering problems
[
28
,
50
]
. Hence, recent works that we will discuss next attempt to give mechanisms for continual counting that improve upon the constants of the binary tree mechanism (though they achieve the same asymptotic error). Most of these mechanisms are factorization mechanisms that exploit clever factorizations to achieve improved error bounds for private continual counting. Note that the neighboring notion studied in all these works is that adjacent streams differ in a single bit at one time-step.

Factorization Mechanisms for Continual Counting.

Inspired by applications to cardinality estimation, we identify new neighboring relations under which we study private continual counting. Our work is effectively a study of the factorization mechanism
[
51
]
applied to the lower-triangular matrix of all-ones,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
under these new neighboring notions.
There is an extensive literature studying optimal matrix factorizations for private continual counting under the regular neighboring notion
[
20
,
3
,
30
,
38
,
21
,
4
,
40
,
34
,
5
,
42
]
. We now describe these in more detail.

Tree-based factorizations
[
23
,
13
,
3
,
2
]
were the first class of factorizations to achieve
O(\log T)
error, and do so efficiently with
O(\log T)
space and
O(T)
time.
Their key structure is sparsity, which allows for not only efficient computation, but also for a low
\ell_{2}
and
\ell_{1}
sensitivity, making them also state-of-the-art for the separate problem of
\varepsilon
-differentially private continual counting.
Techniques of
“denoising”
tree-based mechanisms have also been proposed, where the left factor is made denser for a constant factor improvement at the cost of efficiency
[
41
,
33
,
46
]
.

More general dense factorizations have subsequently been proposed
[
20
,
30
,
38
,
40
,
35
]
, and give improved leading constants of the error, often at a cost in efficiency.
We especially highlight the
square-root factorization

L=R=\sqrt{A}
, where
\sqrt{A}
is Toeplitz and lower-triangular with entries
\sqrt{A}[i,j]=\binom{2(i-j)}{i-j}/4^{i-j}
for
i\geq j
.
It attains an error of
\frac{\ln(T)}{\pi\sqrt{2\rho}}\pm O(1)
, which is optimal up to the additive constant for both of our error notions
[
30
,
38
,
52
]
.
Subsequent work showed how to approximate this error up to the additive constant with a lower-triangular Toeplitz factorization in
O(\log^{2}T)
space and
O(T\log^{2}T)
time using rational-function approximation
[
21
]
.

Factorization Mechanisms under ‘Non-Standard’ Neighboring Relations.

We finally note that our work is not the first to study factorization mechanisms for releasing prefixes under continual observation for a
“non-standard”
neighboring relation.
Motivated by allowing a user to contribute multiple data points in a machine learning pipeline
[
46
]
, the notion of
b
-min separated
k
-repeated participation
was introduced
[
16
,
15
]
.
Here any training example can appear at most
k
times, each example separated by at least
b
time steps.
For the factorization mechanism design problem, this translates to a sensitivity vector set
\mathcal{S}_{(k,b)}\subseteq[-1,1]^{T}
where vectors are
k
-sparse and with non-zero entries having a spacing of at least
b
.
As our
k
-sparse alternating sensitivity vector set (
S_{1,k}
) makes up a strict subset of
\mathcal{S}_{(k,1)}
, any upper bound on the error for a factorization mechanism under
\mathcal{S}_{(k,1)}
-participation extends to our setup.
Crucially, as there is no analogous structure on
\mathcal{S}_{(k,1)}
that enforces bounded prefix sums, these upper bounds are asymptotically worse than what we show.
In particular, factorizations based on a banded (inverse) square-root factorization
[

,

that are asymptotically optimal for this problem achieve a bound of

[

.
In contrast, we achieve a logarithmic dependence on

in our setting.

Comparison to Directly Related Work.

The most directly related work is that of
[
43
]
who study counting distinct elements in the fully dynamic streaming model and introduce the maximum flippancy parameter. Their setting differs slightly from ours
(neighboring streams in their notion are at most distance-2 away according to ours),
but their algorithm extends to our model, and we compare
the resulting bounds against ours.
As shown in Figure
1
, our error improves over theirs across the full range of the maximum flippancy parameter. An alternative parameterization based on total flippancy was proposed by Henzinger et al.
[
36
]
; while we focus on maximum flippancy—typically smaller for realistic streams—our improvements apply in that setting as well.

For
\mathsf{TriangleCount}
and
\mathsf{DegreeCount}
, Raskhodnikova and Steiner
[
56
]
give strong polynomial (in
T
) lower bounds under item-level DP for expected
\ell_{\infty}
error. Their model assumes only a single update per time step, and so while their lower bounds continue to apply in our setting, their upper bounds that are based on periodic recomputation do not apply. To the best of our knowledge, the previous best upper bounds for these problems come from naively running a batch DP algorithm at each time step and applying composition theorems. We introduce new parameters that are significantly smaller than the time horizon in realistic streams and obtain substantially improved error guarantees in terms of these parameters. Although our accuracy guarantees are stated in terms of
\mathsf{MaxSE}
and
\mathsf{MeanSE}
, they also yield improvements for
\ell_{\infty}
error via standard Gaussian tail bounds (see, for example, Section 2.4 of
[
21
]
), providing a way around the lower bounds in
[
56
]
for realistic streams.

We also note that there is additional incomparable work on releasing
\mathsf{TriangleCount}
and

\mathsf{DegreeCount}

[
57
,
29
,
45
]
).
These works focus on the insertion-only setting, which is less demanding than the fully dynamic case we study, though some of this work (e.g.,
[
45
]
) addresses the more challenging node-privacy model, whereas we focus on edge privacy. Moreover,
[
45
]
gives a
\mathsf{TriangleCount}
mechanism that is private for all insertion-only graph sequences, while our mechanism for
\mathsf{TriangleCount}
on fully dynamic streams is private only for a restricted class of sequences.

Finally, we emphasize that our focus differs from all of the above: we aim to obtain strong asymptotic guarantees and to optimize constants, whereas prior work targets asymptotics alone.

Preliminaries

Throughout the paper, we let
[i,j]=\{i,i+1,\dots,j-1,j\}
and use the shorthand
[n]=[0,n-1]
.
We zero-index vectors, meaning for
\vec{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\vec{\mathbf{x}}[i]
is the
i
th
entry for
i\in[n]
.
We will also find it convenient to use the shorthand
\vec{\mathbf{x}}[i:j]
, where
0\leq i\leq j\leq n-1
for the vector in
\mathbb{R}^{i-j+1}
with entries
(\vec{\mathbf{x}}[i:j])[k]=\vec{\mathbf{x}}[i+k]
.
Additionally we let
\vec{\mathbf{x}}[:k]=\vec{\mathbf{x}}[0:k]
and
\vec{\mathbf{x}}[k:]=\vec{\mathbf{x}}[k:n-1]
.
Analogously for indexing a matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, we use
B[i,j]
for
(i,j)\in[m]\times[n]
.
We use
\log_{b}(x)
for the logarithm of
x
in base
b
,
\ln(x)=\log_{e}(x)
for the natural logarithm and
\log(x)=\log_{2}(x)
.

Our main results are stated for zero-concentrated differential privacy, but we also discuss pure differential privacy.
We note that all notions of differential privacy quantifies the indistinguishability of outputs of a randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Y}
when run on sensitive
neighboring inputs
.
Throughout the paper we use the notation
x\sim x^{\prime}
for two such inputs, which are implicitly assumed be in
\mathcal{X}^{n}
from context.

We give all relevant privacy definitions below.

Definition 5.1

(
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-Differential Privacy
[
22
,
24
]
)
.

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Y}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private if for all
S\subseteq\mathrm{Range}(\mathcal{M})
and all neighboring inputs
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
, we have that

\Pr[\mathcal{M}(x)\in S]\leq\exp(\varepsilon)\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{M}(x^{\prime})\in S]+\delta\,.

We refer to
(\varepsilon,0)
-differential privacy as
\varepsilon
-differential privacy, or
pure
differential privacy.

Definition 5.2

(
\rho
-Zero-Concentrated Differential Privacy
[
10
]
)
.

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Y}
satisfies
\rho
-zCDP if for all neighboring inputs
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
, we have that

\forall\alpha>1:D_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}(x)\|\mathcal{M}(x^{\prime}))\leq\rho\alpha\,,

where
D_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}(x)\|\mathcal{M}(x^{\prime}))
is the
\alpha
-Rényi divergence between
\mathcal{M}(x)
and
\mathcal{M}(x^{\prime})
.

We note that
\rho
-zCDP also implies
(\rho+2\sqrt{\rho\ln(1/\delta)},\delta)
-DP for every
\delta>0

[
10
]
.

Definition 5.3

(
\ell_{p}
Sensitivity)
.

Let
f:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a function on sensitive inputs with a neighboring relation
“
\sim
”
.
Then we define the (global)
\ell_{p}
sensitivity of
f
as

\mathsf{sens}_{p}(f)=\max_{x\sim x^{\prime}}\|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})\|_{p}\,,

where we sometimes use
\mathsf{sens}(f)=\mathsf{sens}_{2}(f)
as a shorthand.

Definition 5.4

(Gaussian Mechanism
[
10
]
)
.

Let
f:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a function on sensitive inputs and define the (randomized) algorithm
\mathcal{M}(x)=f(x)+\vec{\mathbf{z}}
where
\vec{\mathbf{z}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.5\cdot\mathsf{sens}_{2}(f)^{2}/\rho)^{n}
is zero-mean,
n
-dimensional i.i.d. Gaussian noise.
Then
\mathcal{M}
satisfies
\rho
-zCDP.

Definition 5.5

(Laplace Mechanism
[
22
]
)
.

Let
f:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a function on sensitive inputs and define the randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}(x)=f(x)+\vec{\mathbf{z}}
where
\vec{\mathbf{z}}\sim\mathrm{Lap}(0,\mathsf{sens}_{1}(f)/\varepsilon)^{n}
is zero-mean,
n
-dimensional i.i.d. Laplace noise.
Then
\mathcal{M}
satisfies
\varepsilon
-DP.

5.1
Factorization Mechanism

The
factorization mechanism
(sometimes also referred to as
matrix mechanism

[
51
]
) is central to our contributions.
Given a query matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
and a set of sensitive inputs
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{T}
with neighboring relation
“
\sim
”
, it offers an approach for privately releasing
A\vec{\mathbf{x}}
for any
\vec{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{D}
.
For any factorization of
A
into matrices
L\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times m},R\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times T}
such that
A=LR
, it induces a mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{LR}(\vec{\mathbf{x}})
.
Given the
\ell_{2}
sensitivity of
R

\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}})\in\mathcal{D}^{2}\\
\vec{\mathbf{x}}\sim\vec{\mathbf{y}}\end{subarray}}\|R\vec{\mathbf{x}}-R\vec{\mathbf{y}}\|_{2}\,,\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}})\in\mathcal{D}^{2}\\
\vec{\mathbf{x}}\sim\vec{\mathbf{y}}\end{subarray}}\|R(\vec{\mathbf{x}}-\vec{\mathbf{y}})\|_{2}\,,

the mechanism outputs

\mathcal{M}_{LR}(\vec{\mathbf{x}})=A\vec{\mathbf{x}}+L\vec{\mathbf{z}}\qquad\text{where}\qquad\vec{\mathbf{z}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.5\cdot\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R)^{2}/\rho)^{m}

for a
\rho
-zCDP guarantee.
The intuition behind the mechanism is that
R\vec{\mathbf{x}}
is released with the Gaussian mechanism as
R\vec{\mathbf{x}}+\vec{\mathbf{z}}
, and then post-processed by
L
from the left into
\mathcal{M}_{LR}(\vec{\mathbf{x}})=L(R\vec{\mathbf{x}}+\vec{\mathbf{z}})
.

As our main contributions is to study factorization mechanisms under non-standard neighboring relations, we make the this dependence more explicit in our notation.
We define the
sensitivity vector set

\mathcal{S}
and express the associated
\ell_{p}
sensitivity of
R
,
\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S})
, as

\mathcal{S}=\{\vec{\mathbf{x}}-\vec{\mathbf{y}}:\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{D}\ \text{and}\ \vec{\mathbf{x}}\sim\vec{\mathbf{y}}\}\,,\quad\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S})=\max_{\vec{\Delta}\in\mathcal{S}}\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{p}=\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R)\,.

Throughout the paper we will leverage two common error metrics applied to the factorization mechanism:

\displaystyle\mathsf{MaxSE}(L,R,\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\max_{t\in[T]}\mathbb{E}\left[(A\vec{\mathbf{x}}-\mathcal{M}_{LR}(\vec{\mathbf{x}}))_{t}^{2}\right]}=\frac{\|L\|_{2\to\infty}\cdot\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S})}{\sqrt{2\rho}}

\displaystyle\mathsf{MeanSE}(L,R,\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}(A\vec{\mathbf{x}}-\mathcal{M}_{LR}(\vec{\mathbf{x}}))_{t}^{2}\right]}=\frac{\|L\|_{F}\cdot\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S})}{\sqrt{2T\rho}}\,.

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the
Frobenius
norm, and
\|\cdot\|_{2\to\infty}
is the largest
\ell_{2}
norm over all rows of the matrix.
We will later also work with the
\|\cdot\|_{1\to 2}
norm, which equals the largest
\ell_{2}
norm taken over all columns of the matrix.
The two error metrics correspond to the
root maximum (expected) squared error
and the
root mean (expected) squared error
taken over all outputs.
As the factorization mechanism is exact on expectation, these errors are independent of the input and can be thought of as the root of the maximum and average variance over all outputs.

We also remark that the same framework can be used to derive a mechanism satisfying
\varepsilon
-DP.
The only change is that the
R\vec{\mathbf{x}}
is now released with the Laplace mechanism which scales with the
\ell_{1}
sensitivity.
The corresponding error metrics can be derived from replacing
\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S})/\sqrt{2\rho}
by
\sqrt{2}\mathsf{sens}_{1}(R,\mathcal{S})/\varepsilon
.
All results and statements throughout the paper are for
\rho
-zCDP unless explicitly stated otherwise.

Continual Observation

Whenever the query matrix
A
is lower-triangular, it is possible to study the problem of privately releasing
A\vec{\mathbf{x}}

under continual observation
, also referred to as
(weighted) continual counting

[
23
,
13
]
.
The same neighboring relation from before can be considered, but now
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
is streamed one entry at a time with updated objective:

\text{On receiving}\ \vec{\mathbf{x}}[t]\ \text{at time}\ t,\,\text{immediately output}\ (A\vec{\mathbf{x}})[t]\,.

The corresponding factorization mechanism for the problem extends naturally:

\text{On receiving}\ \vec{\mathbf{x}}[t],\,\text{immediately output}\ \mathcal{M}_{LR}(\vec{\mathbf{x}})[t]=(A\vec{\mathbf{x}})[t]+(L\vec{\mathbf{z}})[t]\,.

It satisfies
\rho
-zCDP under continual observation, even for a stronger notion of
adaptive
DP
[
20
]
.
Here an input
\vec{\mathbf{x}}[t]
is allowed to depend on past private outputs
\mathcal{M}(\vec{\mathbf{x}})[t^{\prime}]
for
t^{\prime}<t
.
In particular, there are no structural constraints on either
L
or
R
for this stronger notion to hold.
Ultimately, our goal will be to reduce cardinality estimation problems to this setting.

Computational Model

The time and space usage of the factorization mechanism are also of interest.
As
A\vec{\mathbf{x}}
is efficient to compute, the resource footprint is determined by the hardness of outputting
L\vec{\mathbf{z}}
.
In line with past work on continual counting
[
3
,
21
,
4
]
, we restrict our attention to
linear
algorithms for outputting
L\vec{\mathbf{z}}
.
Space is measured by the number of real values that have to be stored, and time by the number of additions and multiplications.

5.2
Problems of Interest

In this section, we introduce the cardinality estimation problems we study in this paper, and the associated neighboring relations of interest. We note that the two error metrics with respect to a function
f
that we consider are the
root maximum (expected) squared error

\mathsf{MaxSE}(\mathcal{M},T)=\sqrt{\max_{t\in[T]}\mathbb{E}\left[(f(\vec{\mathbf{x}}[0:t])-\mathcal{M}(\vec{\mathbf{x}}[0:t]))^{2}\right]},

and the
root mean (expected) squared error

\mathsf{MeanSE}(\mathcal{M},T)=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}(f(\vec{\mathbf{x}}[0:t])-\mathcal{M}(\vec{\mathbf{x}}[0:t]))^{2}\right]}.

For
\mathsf{DegreeCount}
, which has vector outputs, we will consider a natural variant of these error metrics, discussed in the section on graph functions below.

Counting Distinct Elements under Continual Observation:

We study the distinct element problem for turnstile (fully dynamic) streams. In this problem, elements come from a universe
\mathcal{U}
. A stream
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
for this problem corresponds to a sequence of updates (each update is a set of insertions or deletions of elements, or a no op), that is for all
t\in[T]
,
\vec{\mathbf{x}}[t]\in\{\{\{+,-\}\times\mathcal{U}\}\cup\bot\}^{*}
. We will use
\vec{\mathbf{x}}[t][i]
to represent the
i^{th}
update at time step
t
. Informally, we will use
+u
to represent the insertion of an element
u
and
-u
to represent the deletion of an element
u
. Now, we are ready to formally define the count distinct problem.
\mathsf{CountDistinct}
is a function that takes as input a stream
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
(a sequence of
T
updates) and outputs a stream of
T
non-negative integers. For
t\in[T]
,

\mathsf{CountDistinct}(\vec{\mathbf{x}})[t]=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbbm{1}(\text{$u$ has been inserted more often than deleted in $\vec{\mathbf{x}}[0:t]$})\,.

Note that prior work studying this problem under continual observation
[
43
,
18
]
considered the situation where you only have one update per time step. We consider the more general model where you can have arbitrarily many updates per time step since it corresponds more directly to practical systems.

We will consider the following neighboring relation in our work. Privacy is defined with respect to this neighboring relation.

Definition 5.6

(Item Neighbors for
\mathsf{CountDistinct}
)
.

We say that two streams
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
and
\vec{\mathbf{y}}
for
\mathsf{CountDistinct}
are neighboring if there exists at most one element
u\in\mathcal{U}
such that replacing all updates corresponding to
u
(i.e.
-u
or
+u
) with
\bot
in one of the streams results in the other.

This is in (our opinion) a simpler definition than what has been used in prior work
[
43
]
. In that definition, neighboring corresponds to replacing a
subset
of updates corresponding to
u
with
\bot
. Semantically, we believe our neighboring notion captures the risks associated with revealing information about an element
u
just as well as the neighboring relation studied in prior work. Additionally, we note that being neighboring in otheir notion corresponds to being
2
-neighbors in our neighboring notion, and so our results (with this extra factor of
2
) apply to their neighboring relation as well.

Releasing Graph Functions under Continual Observation:

We study the degree histogram problem and triangle count problems for fully dynamic graph streams under edge differential privacy.

A stream
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
for this problem can be thought of as a sequence of graphs
G_{1},\dots,G_{T}
all on the same
n
nodes. Each element in the stream will correspond to a set of edge updates (corresponding to the insertion or deletion of an edge
e=(a,b)
between two nodes
a
and
b
in the graph, or a
\bot
operation indicating a no-op) that represent how the graph at time step
t
is generated from the graph at time step
t-1
. We will use
\vec{\mathbf{x}}[t][i]
to represent the
i^{th}
update at time step
t
. We will assume that
G_{-1}
is the empty graph. Informally, we will use
+(a,b)
to represent the insertion of an edge
(a,b)
and
-(a,b)
to represent the deletion of an edge
(a,b)
. We will also abuse notation to interchangeably refer to the stream as a sequence of edge updates or as a sequence of graphs depending on convenience.

Now, we are ready to formally define the degree estimation problem.
\mathsf{DegreeCount}
is a function that takes as input a stream
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
(a sequence of
T
graphs
G_{0},\dots,G_{T-1}
) and outputs a stream of
T
non-negative integer vectors each of size
n
. For
t\in[T]
,

\mathsf{DegreeCount}(\vec{\mathbf{x}})[t][i]=\sum_{a\in[n]}\mathbbm{1}[\text{edge }(i,a)\in G_{t}]\,.

Next, we will define the triangle count problem. A triangle in a graph corresponds to the presence of a set of edges
\{(a,b),(b,c),(a,c)\}
for three nodes
a,b,c
in the graph.
\mathsf{TriangleCount}
is a function that takes as input a stream
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
(a sequence of
T
graphs
G_{0},\dots,G_{T-1}
) and outputs a stream of
T
non-negative integers. For
t\in[T]
,

\mathsf{TriangleCount}(\vec{\mathbf{x}})[t]=\sum_{a,b,c\in[n]}\mathbbm{1}[a,b,c\text{ form a triangle in $G_{t}$}]\,.

Next, we are ready to define the neighboring relation of interest, with respect to which privacy is defined.

Definition 5.7

(Edge Neighbors for Graph Problems)
.

We say two streams
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
and
\vec{\mathbf{y}}
for
\mathsf{TriangleCount}
and
\mathsf{DegreeCount}
are edge neighboring if there exists at most one edge
(a,b)
such that replacing all updates corresponding to
(a,b)
(i.e.
-(a,b)
or
+(a,b)
) with
\bot
in one of the streams results in the other.

Note that prior work studying this problem under continual observation
[
56
]
considered the situation where only one edge is updated per time step. We consider the case where an arbitary number of edges can be updated per time step.

Note that we will be interested in the case where some parameter (for example, degree) of all graphs in the stream are bounded by a value
k
. We can define
k
-restricted neighbors as graph sequences that satisfy the above edge-neighboring requirement and additionally the restriction that all graphs in both graph sequences have maximum parameter bounded by
k
.
k
-restricted edge privacy can be defined with such a neighboring relation.

Finally, we note that since
\mathsf{DegreeCount}
is vector valued, our error notions are ill-defined for it. We will overload the error metrics as follows: we will define the root mean squared error for this problem as the max over coordinates of the root mean squared error over the outputs restricted to that coordinate, and the root max squared error similarly.

6
Sensitivity Vector Sets for Fully Dynamic Streams

In this section, we formally describe our sensitivity vector sets of interest, and explain how they relate to cardinality estimation problems. In specific, we will explain how error bounds for continual counting for our defined sensitivity vector sets correspond to error bounds for
\mathsf{CountDistinct}
,
\mathsf{DegreeCount}
and
\mathsf{TriangleCount}
.

Common to the problems we consider will be that algorithms for them involve running continual counting mechanisms on difference streams. Formally, for a counting function
\mathsf{PropCount}
, define difference streams
\mathit{d}_{\,\mathsf{PropCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}})[t]=\mathsf{PropCount}(\vec{\mathbf{x}})[t]-\mathsf{PropCount}(\vec{\mathbf{x}})[t-1]
.

Firstly, in
Section

6.1
we will characterize properties of sensitivity vectors for the difference streams for
\mathsf{CountDistinct},\mathsf{DegreeCount}
, and
\mathsf{TriangleCount}
. Then, in
Section

6.2
, we will consider how bounding the ‘contribution’ of elements in these streams adds additional structure to the sensitivity vector sets that we can leverage.

6.1
Properties of Sensitivity Vectors for Cardinality Problems

Consider neighboring streams
\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}}
defined over a universe
\mathcal{U}
associated with a counting problem
\mathsf{PropCount}
. The sensitivity vector associated with these neighboring streams is the subtraction of the corresponding difference streams:

\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{PropCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}})=\mathit{d}_{\,\mathsf{PropCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}})-\mathit{d}_{\,\mathsf{PropCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{y}})\,.

The sensitivity vector set for
\mathsf{PropCount}
is the collection of all such vectors over neighboring stream pairs:

\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{PropCount}}=\{\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{PropCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}})\;|\;\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{U}^{T}\text{ s.t. $\vec{\mathbf{x}}$ \& $\vec{\mathbf{y}}$ are neighbours}\}\,.

We first focus on sensitivity vectors with values in
\{-1,0,1\}^{T}
, and define a vector as
alternating
if its non-zero entries alternate in sign. For example, the vector
(1,0,0,-1,0,1)
is alternating. In
Theorems

6.2
and
6.3
, we prove that the sensitivity vector sets for
\mathsf{CountDistinct}
and
\mathsf{DegreeCount}
are alternating.

We generalize this notion by defining the set
\mathcal{S}_{D}
which contain all vectors with
D
-bounded interval sums,

\mathcal{S}_{D}=\bigg\{\vec{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{T}\;\bigg|\;\,\max_{i\leq j\in[T]}\Big|\sum_{t=i}^{j}\vec{s}[t]\Big|\leq D\bigg\}\,.

For all cardinality estimation problems considered in this work (
\mathsf{CountDistinct},\mathsf{DegreeCount}
, and
\mathsf{TriangleCount}
), we prove that their sensitivity vector sets are contained
\mathcal{S}_{D}
for appropriately chosen
D
. In fact, when
D=1
, the set
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is precisely the set of alternating vectors as proven in
6.1
below.

Claim 6.1
.

\vec{\Delta}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{T}\text{ is alternating }\;\iff\;\vec{\Delta}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}

Proof.

Observe that if the sensitivity vector is alternating, then any interval sum is at most
1
, since adjacent
1
s and
-1
s in the interval will cancel each other out, leaving at most one excess uncanceled value. On the other hand, if the sensitivity vector is not alternating, then there exists two non-zero values of the same sign with zeros between them and the interval sum corresponding to the indices of these non-zero values will have absolute value larger than
1
. Hence, this sensitivity vector will not be in
\mathcal{S}_{1}
.
∎

6.2
Sensitivity Vector Sets under Bounded Contribution

In this section, we characterize the effects of bounding the change in contribution of specific elements or items to the sensitivity vector sets.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{D}
be the set of vectors with the additional guarantee of
k
-bounded
\ell_{1}
-norms:

\mathcal{S}_{D,k}=\big\{\vec{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{T}\;\big|\;\,\vec{s}\in S_{D},\;\|\vec{s}\|_{1}\leq k\big\}\,.

We will prove that the notion of bounded maximum flippancy for counting distinct elements introduced in
[
43
]
corresponds to restricting the sensitivity vector set to a subset of
\mathcal{S}_{1,k}
where
k
is a bound on maximum flippancy. For degree histograms, and triangle counting, we will suggest new notions that capture bounded contribution that we term
maximum degree contribution
, and
maximum triangle contribution
respectively, and argue that it has a similar effect of restricting the sensitivity vector set to a subset of
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
where
k
is a bound on the respective parameter.

Bounded Contribution Changes for
\mathsf{CountDistinct}
via Maximum Flippancy:

Observe that for arbitrary neighboring streams
\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}}
for
\mathsf{CountDistinct}
, the
\ell_{1}
norm of the sensitivity vector could be as large as
T
(
\vec{\mathbf{x}}=\{+u,-u,+u,-u,\dots,+u,-u\},\vec{\mathbf{y}}=\{\bot,\bot,\dots,\bot\}
). However, such a stream
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
seems unrealistic. For this purpose, prior work
[
43
]
introduced the concept of maximum flippancy to capture what practical streams looked like, and to prove that for such streams, one could achieve better error bounds.

We start by recalling the definition of maximum flippancy from
[
43
]
. The flippancy
\mathsf{flip}(u)
of an item
u\in\mathcal{U}
in a stream
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
for
\mathsf{CountDistinct}
is defined as the number of time steps the item
u
goes from present to absent or absent to present in the stream
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
. This corresponds to the number of time steps there is a change in the contribution of the item to the
\mathsf{CountDistinct}
value for
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
. The maximum flippancy is defined as
\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathsf{flip}(u)
.

Theorem 6.8
.

Let
\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}}
be neighboring streams for
\mathsf{CountDistinct}
with maximum flippancy at most
k
. Then,
\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{CountDistinct}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}})\in\mathcal{S}_{1,k}
.

Proof.

Consider a stream
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
with maximum flippancy bounded by
k
, and consider a neighboring stream
\vec{\mathbf{y}}
with maximum flippancy bounded by
k
as well. Without loss of generality, assume
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
has updates corresponding to item
u\in\mathcal{U}
and
\vec{\mathbf{y}}
does not.

Consider the restricted stream
\vec{\mathbf{x}}|_{u}
restricted to item
u
(see
Equation

1
for a reminder of the definition). Observe that
\mathit{d}_{\,\mathsf{CountDistinct}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}}|_{u})
has at most
k
non-zero entries. On the other hand
\mathit{d}_{\,\mathsf{CountDistinct}}(\vec{\mathbf{y}}|_{u})
is
0^{T}
. Hence, the sensitivity vector
\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{CountDistinct}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}})
is
k
-sparse.

Since every value in
\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{CountDistinct}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}})
is in
\{1,0,-1\}
, this further implies that the
\ell_{1}
norm of
\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{CountDistinct}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}})
is bounded by
k
.
This combined with
Theorem

6.2
(
\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{CountDistinct}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}})\in\mathcal{S}_{1}

\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{CountDistinct}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}})\in\mathcal{S}_{1,k}

∎

Bounded Contribution Changes for
\mathsf{DegreeCount}
via Maximum Degree Contribution:

In this paper, we give a new definition of the degree contribution of an edge
(a,b)
in a graph sequence over
n
nodes. Note that with no further assumptions, the sensitivity vector restricted to the indices corresponding to node
a
or node
b
could have
\ell_{1}
norm as large as
T
. To see this consider
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
consisting of alternating insertions and deletions of the edge
(a,b)
, and a neighboring stream
\vec{\mathbf{y}}
being
\bot^{T}
. We define the maximum degree contribution of a stream to avoid such pathological cases.

Definition 6.9

(Maximum Degree Contribution)
.

The degree contribution of an edge
(a,b)
in a stream
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
is defined as the number of times it is updated throughout the stream
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
. The maximum degree contribution of a stream is the maximum degree contribution over its edges.

We next argue that a bound on the maximum degree contribution of a stream gives further structure to the sensitivity vectors.

Theorem 6.10
.

Let
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
and
\vec{\mathbf{y}}
be neighboring streams for
\mathsf{DegreeCount}
with maximum degree contribution bounded by
k
. Then, there are two nodes
a,b
such that:

1.

\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{DegreeCount}}[\cdot][a]\in\mathcal{S}_{1,k}
,
\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{DegreeCount}}[\cdot][b]\in\mathcal{S}_{1,k}
.

2.

For all nodes
c\neq a,b
, we have that
\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{DegreeCount}}[\cdot][c]=0^{T}
.

Proof.

WLOG let
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
contain updates corresponding to edge
(a,b)
and
\vec{\mathbf{y}}
contain no updates corresponding to edge
(a,b)
.
Item 2 follows directly from
Theorem

6.3
.
For Item 1, for nodes
a
and
b
, we have by Item 1 of
Theorem

6.3

\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{DegreeCount}}[\cdot][a]\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{DegreeCount}}[\cdot][b]\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
.

Finally, observe that if
\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{DegreeCount}}[t][a]=\mathit{d}_{\,\mathsf{DegreeCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}})[t][a]-\mathit{d}_{\,\mathsf{DegreeCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{y}})[t][a]\neq 0

\vec{\mathbf{x}}
and no corresponding update in

\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{DegreeCount}}[\cdot][a]\in\mathcal{S}_{1,k}

Bounded Contribution Changes for
\mathsf{TriangleCount}
via Maximum Triangle Contribution:

In this paper, we give a new definition of the triangle contribution of an edge
(a,b)
in a graph sequence over
n
nodes with maximum degree
D
. We start by motivating the definition. Firstly, note that with no further assumptions, there are neighboring graph sequences
\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}}
such that
\|\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{TriangleCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}})\|_{1}=DT
.
9
9

9

Consider for example that
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
corresponds to adding an edge
(a,b)
at the first time step and then adding all other edges
(a,\dot{)},(b,\dot{)}
at even time steps and deleting all edges of the form
(a,\dot{)},(b,\dot{)}
(other than edge
(a,b)
) at odd time steps that are not the first time step.
\vec{\mathbf{y}}
is identical to
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
other than the fact that at the first time step its update is
\bot
.

However, we would expect that for realistic streams
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
, we would not observe such an extreme case. We define maximum triangle contribution to capture this.

First attempt at a definition:
Similarly to flippancy, we could attempt to define triangle contribution of an edge as the number of time steps that there is a change in the number of triangles including this edge
(a,b)
. One subtlety in such a definition is that such a change in number of triangles could occur even for updates that
do not involve the edge
(a,b)
. Once triangle contribution is defined, maximum triangle contribution could be defined analogously to maximum flippancy by taking a maximum over edges.

Consider two neighboring streams
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
and
\vec{\mathbf{y}}
for
\mathsf{TriangleCount}
, such that
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
contains updates involving edge
(a,b)
and
\vec{\mathbf{y}}
does not. Observe that by
6.5
, we have that
\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{TriangleCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}})=\mathit{d}_{\,\mathsf{TriangleCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}}|_{(a,b)})
.
Now, since the maximum degree of the graph is
D
, observe that at most
D
triangles involving edge
(a,b)
could be affected at a time step where there is a change in the number of triangles including
(a,b)
. Additionally, observe by definition that
\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{TriangleCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}})
is
k
-sparse. Hence, if the maximum triangle contribution is bounded by
k
, we have that
\|\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{TriangleCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}})\|_{1}\leq kD
.

Our definition:
In the above argument, when we bound the
\ell_{1}
norm, we have to always consider the worst case where
D
triangles are affected each time there is a change in the number of triangles involving edge
(a,b)
. To avoid considering this worst case, we come up with a different definition that directly bounds the
\ell_{1}
norm.

Definition 6.11

(Maximum Triangle Contribution)
.

At any time step
t
, consider the change in number of triangles including an edge
(a,b)
, i.e.
\mathit{d}_{\,\mathsf{TriangleCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}}|_{(a,b)})[t]
. Then, the triangle contribution of edge
(a,b)
is defined as the sum of the absolute values of these changes over all time steps i.e.
\sum_{t\in[T]}|\mathit{d}_{\,\mathsf{TriangleCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}}|_{(a,b)})[t]|
. The maximum triangle contribution is defined as the maximum over edges of the triangle contributions of the edges.

Now, we can directly bound the
\ell_{1}
norm of the sensitivity vector.

Theorem 6.12
.

Consider neighboring graph streams
\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}}
with degree bound
D
and maximum triangle contribution bounded by
k
. The sensitivity stream
\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{TriangleCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}})\in\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
.

Proof.

Consider two neighboring streams
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
and
\vec{\mathbf{y}}
for
\mathsf{TriangleCount}
, such that
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
contains updates involving edge
(a,b)
and
\vec{\mathbf{y}}
does not.
Using
Theorem

6.4
, we have that
\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{TriangleCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}})\in S_{D}
.

Additionally, note that by
6.5
, we have that
\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{TriangleCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}})=\mathit{d}_{\,\mathsf{TriangleCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}}|_{(a,b)})
. This implies that

\|\vec{\Delta}_{\mathsf{TriangleCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}})\|_{1}=\|\mathit{d}_{\,\mathsf{TriangleCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}}|_{(a,b)})\|_{1}.

By
Definition

6.11
for maximum triangle contribution, we have
\|\mathit{d}_{\,\mathsf{TriangleCount}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}}|_{(a,b)})\|_{1}\leq k
. This completes the proof.
∎

be a factorization mechanism for continual counting using factorization matrices
L,R
for the sensitivity vector set
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
. Let
\mathcal{A}
reference
Algorithm

1
. Let streams
\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\mathbf{y}}
for
\mathsf{TriangleCount}
be
(D,k)
-neighboring if every graph in both has maximum triangle contribution bounded by

.
We have that:

•

\mathcal{A}

•

For streams for

\mathsf{MeanSE}(\mathcal{A},T)=\mathsf{MeanSE}(L,R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})\,.

A drawback of the above corollary is that privacy is only with respect to

-neighboring graph streams. Unlike the previous cases, it is unclear how to extend this algorithm to give an algorithm that is private for all graphs. The key difficulty is that ignoring updates to an edge after the maximum triangle contribution for that edge reaches its bound could have a cascading effect on other edges, since it will affect the triangle contributions of those edges too. This could result in neighboring graphs becoming vastly different after a truncation operation. This is the key question studied in the work of

We once again emphasize that existing bounds for

7
A Reduction from
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
to
\mathcal{S}_{1,k}

To recap, let
\mathcal{S}_{D}
be the set of vectors
D
-bounded partial sums,

\mathcal{S}_{D}=\bigg\{\vec{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{T}\;\bigg|\;\,\max_{i\leq j\in[T]}\Big|\sum_{t=i}^{j}\vec{s}[t]\Big|\leq D\bigg\}\,,

and let
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{D}
be the set of vectors with the additional guarantee of
k
-bounded
\ell_{1}
-norms,

\mathcal{S}_{D,k}=\big\{\vec{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{T}\;\big|\;\,\vec{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{D},\;\|\vec{s}\|_{1}\leq k\big\}\,.

We have shown how mechanism design for cardinality estimation problems, parameterized in
D
and
k
, can be reduced to the design of continual counting mechanisms, but for streams with sensitivity vector set
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
.
In particular, our focus is on
factorization mechanisms
in this space, for which the sensitivity analysis now is non-trivial.
Bluntly, for reasonable choices of matrix
R\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times T}
:

\textit{how do we compute }\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})=\max_{\vec{\Delta}\in\mathcal{S}_{D,k}}\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}\,\textit{?}

We note that, for standard continual counting of bits where two streams are neighboring if they differ at a single position, the corresponding sensitivity vector set is
\mathcal{S}_{1,1}
.
Here the answer is simple:

\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,1})=\|R\|_{1\to 2}\,,

i.e., the
\ell_{2}
sensitivity equals the largest
\ell_{2}
norm of any column in
R
.

To answer the question for other values of
k
and
D
, we will show a reduction.
In this section, we show that it often suffices to study
\mathcal{S}_{1,k}
streams, that is,
k
-sparse streams from
\{0,\pm 1\}^{T}
with alternating non-zero entries.
We can tightly estimate the sensitivity of
R
for
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
streams, by studying the simpler and more structured
\mathcal{S}_{1,k}
streams.
We begin by showing a lower bound in this direction.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
1\leq k\leq T
and
D,p\geq 1
where
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{T}
and
R\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times T}
for
m\geq 1
.
Then

\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})\geq D\cdot\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,\lfloor k/D\rfloor})\,.

Proof.

Consider
\vec{\Delta}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{1,\lfloor k/D\rfloor}
attaining the maximum norm, i.e.,

\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,\lfloor k/D\rfloor})=\max_{\vec{\Delta}\in\mathcal{S}_{1,\lfloor k/D\rfloor}}\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{p}=\|R\vec{\Delta}^{*}\|_{p}\,.

Noting that
\vec{\Delta}^{\prime}=D\vec{\Delta}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{D,D\lfloor k/D\rfloor}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
, we can deduce

\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})\geq\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,D\lfloor k/D\rfloor})\geq\|R\vec{\Delta}^{\prime}\|_{p}=D\|R\vec{\Delta}^{*}\|_{p}=D\cdot\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,\lfloor k/D\rfloor})\,,

finishing the proof.
∎

We will next show that a similar bound holds in the other direction.
The following lemma will be crucial.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
T\geq k\geq 1
and
D\geq 1
where
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{T}
.
Then for any
\vec{\Delta}\in\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
,

\displaystyle\qquad\exists k_{1},\dots,k_{d}\geq 0:\quad\sum_{d=1}^{D}k_{d}=k\qquad\text{such that}\qquad\vec{\Delta}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\vec{\Delta}^{(d)}\qquad\text{where}\qquad\vec{\Delta}^{(d)}\in\mathcal{S}_{1,k_{d}}\,.

Proof.

The decomposition can be identified in multiple ways, e.g., via a greedy
“stack coloring”
argument.
We will argue via the procedure given in
Algorithm

2
for a fixed
\vec{\Delta}\in\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
.

Algorithm 2

Procedure for decomposing
\vec{\Delta}
into
\vec{\Delta}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{\Delta}^{(D)}

1:

Input:
Vector
\vec{\Delta}\in\mathcal{S}_{D,k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{T}
.

2:

Output:
Vectors
\vec{\Delta}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{\Delta}^{(D)}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
.

3:

Initialize
\forall d\in[-D,D]:\vec{\Delta}^{(d)}\leftarrow\vec{0}
and
p\leftarrow 0
.

4:

for

t\leftarrow 0
to
T-1

do

5:

for

i\leftarrow 1
to
\lvert\vec{\Delta}[t]\rvert

do

6:

if

\mathrm{sign}(\vec{\Delta}[t])>0

then

7:

Set
p\leftarrow p+1

8:

Set
\vec{\Delta}^{(p)}[t]=1

9:

else

10:

Set
\vec{\Delta}^{(p)}[t]=-1

11:

Set
p\leftarrow p-1

12:

Re-label the set
\{\vec{\Delta}^{(d)}:d\in[-D,D]\}
such that
\vec{\Delta}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{\Delta}^{(D)}
includes all non-zero vectors.

13:

return

\vec{\Delta}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{\Delta}^{(D)}
.

By the definition of
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
, any interval sum on
\vec{\Delta}
is in the range
[-D,D]

and
the prefix takes on at most
D+1
distinct values.
To see the second claim, note that if the prefix were to take on more distinct values, then there would necessarily have to exist an interval sum taking on a value outside of
[-D,D]
.
Immediately after the double for-loop has been executed and before Line 10 has been executed, we make a few remarks about the variables:

1.

There are at most
D
distinct vectors in
\{\vec{\Delta}^{(d)}:d\in[-D,D]\}
which are non-zero.

2.

All
\vec{\Delta}^{(d)}\in\{0,\pm 1\}^{T}
are alternating vectors.

3.

\sum_{d\in[-D,D]}\vec{\Delta}^{(d)}=\vec{\Delta}
.

The first remark is immediate from there being at most
D+1
distinct prefix values combined with the order in which
p
and
\vec{\Delta}^{(p)}
is updated on line 4-5 vs. line 8-9.
E.g., if the prefix takes on values on the range
[a,b]
, then only
\{\vec{\Delta}^{(d)}:d\in[a,b+1]\}
.
The second remark is immediate: if
\vec{\Delta}^{(d)}
has a
+1
inserted at time
t
, then necessarily the next element inserted at time
t^{\prime}>t
must be a
-1
since the prefix must decrease in value to reach
d
again.
The third remark is also immediate: on reading
\vec{\Delta}[t]
, the same (signed) unit weight is spread equally over
\lvert\vec{\Delta}[t]\rvert
vectors.

After the re-labeling on line 10, It follows that
Algorithm

2
will return
D
alternating vectors
\vec{\Delta}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{\Delta}^{(D)}
from
\mathcal{S}_{1,k}
.
We can refine the statement to say that each
\vec{\Delta}^{(d)}\in\mathcal{S}_{1,k_{d}}
where
k_{d}=\|\vec{\Delta}^{(d)}\|_{1}
, from which we necessarily can claim that
\sum_{d\in[D]}k_{d}=k
, finalizing the proof.
∎

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
1\leq k\leq T
and
D,p\geq 1
where
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{T}
and
R\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times T}
for
m\geq 1
.
Then

\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})\leq\max_{k_{1},\dots,k_{D}\geq 0:\sum_{d=1}^{D}k_{i}=k}\sum_{d=1}^{D}\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,k_{d}})\leq D\cdot U(\lceil k/D\rceil)\,\,.

where the last bound holds for any concave and nondecreasing function
U:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,k})\leq U(k)
.

Proof.

We start by bounding the target sensitivity.

\displaystyle\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})

\displaystyle=\max_{\vec{\Delta}\in\mathcal{S}_{D,k}}\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{p}=\|R\vec{\Delta}^{*}\|_{p}\leq\sum_{d=1}^{D}\|R\vec{\Delta}^{(d)}\|_{p}\leq\sum_{d=1}^{D}\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,k_{d}})\,.

The first inequality uses
Lemma

7.2
applied to
\vec{\Delta}^{*}
decomposing it into a sum over
\{\vec{\Delta}^{(d)}\in\mathcal{S}_{1,k_{d}}:d\in[D]\}
, and then uses the triangle inequality.
The second inequality upper bounds each summand by the sensitivity.
To get the first upper bound in the lemma, we maximize the upper bound over all choices of integer
k_{1},\dots k_{d}\geq 0
such that
\sum_{d\in[D]}k_{d}=k
.
To finish the proof, we use
U
:

\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})\leq\max_{k_{1},\dots,k_{D}\geq 0:\sum_{d=1}^{D}k_{d}=k}\sum_{d=1}^{D}\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,k_{d}})\leq\max_{k_{1},\dots,k_{D}\geq 0:\sum_{d=1}^{D}k_{d}=k}\sum_{i=1}^{D}U(k_{d})

By
U
being concave and non-decreasing, we get that a near-even split maximizes the expression, i.e.,

\sum_{d=1}^{D}U(k_{d})\leq(D-r)U(q)+rU(q+1)

where
q=\lfloor k/D\rfloor
and
r=k-Dq
.
The intuition for why this is true, is that decrementing a larger argument to increment a smaller argument can never decrease the value of the sum.
Formally, for integers
0\leq a<b

U(a+1)+U(b-1)-(U(a)+U(b))=\underbrace{U(a+1)-U(a)}_{\Delta U(a)}-\underbrace{U(b)-U(b-1)}_{\Delta U(b-1)}\geq 0

where the inequality holds due to forward difference
\Delta U(\cdot)
being nonincreasing (concavity of
U
).
To finish the proof, we have that

\displaystyle(D-r)U(q)+rU(q+1)\leq D\cdot U(q+1)\,.

by
U
being non-decreasing.
Moreover,
q+1=\lceil k/D\rceil
, unless
r=0
, but in this case
q=\lfloor k/D\rfloor=\lceil k/D\rceil
and

\displaystyle(D-r)U(q)+rU(q+1)=DU(q)=DU(\lceil k/D\rceil)\,.

Hence,

\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{k,D})\leq D\cdot U(\lceil k/D\rceil)\,.\qed

It is natural to ask if
\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,k})=f(k)
itself is a concave function in
k
, and so if the lemma could be simplified.
Unfortunately, there are matrices
R
for which this is not the case: e.g.,

R=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times 3}\,,\qquad\text{where}\qquad f(1)=1\,,\quad f(2)=1\,,\quad f(3)=2^{1/p}\,,

which implies that
\frac{f(1)+f(3)}{2}=\frac{1+2^{1/p}}{2}\geq 1=f(2)
, disproving concavity of
f
.
Nevertheless, for all factorizations and right factors
R
that we consider, such concave, and tight, upper bounds exist.

We finish this section by summarizing
Lemma

7.1
and
7.3
in a single theorem.

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
1\leq k\leq T
and
D,p\geq 1
where
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{T}
and
R\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times T}
for
m\geq 1
.
Then

D\cdot\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,\lfloor k/D\rfloor})\leq\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})\leq\max_{k_{1},\dots,k_{D}\geq 0:\sum_{d=1}^{D}k_{i}=k}\sum_{d=1}^{D}\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,k_{d}})\leq D\cdot U(\lceil k/D\rceil)\,\,.

where the last bound holds for any concave and nondecreasing function
U:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying that
\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,k})\leq U(k)
.

In this section, we will prove error bounds for continual counting on

As before, let

In essence, our sensitivity analysis allows us to slot-in existing leading-constant-optimal factorizations for standard continual counting on

Firstly, in

Next, in

8.1
Sensitivity as Quadratic Form and Matrix Properties

Let
R\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
be a lower-triangular matrix Toeplitz matrix with diagonals
r_{0},\dots,r_{T-1}
that are monotonically non-increasing and non-negative.
Note that in such a matrix, we have that
R[i,j]=r_{i-j}
for
i\geq j
.
We will proceed by analyzing
\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}
, since this corresponds to a quadratic form that is easier to reason about algebraically.
By definition, we have that

\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}=\vec{\Delta}^{T}R^{T}R\vec{\Delta}\,.

We first analyze properties of the matrix
R^{T}R
and then reason about the above quadratic form to derive our bounds on the sensitivity
\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})
.
Let
c(i,j)\equiv R^{T}R[i,j]
.
We establish some key monotonicity properties of
c(i,j)
that will prove useful in our later analysis.

Claim 8.3
.

Let
c(i,j)
be as defined above. Then,

1.

Symmetry.
For all
0\leq i,j\leq T-1
, we have that
c(i,j)=c(j,i).

2.

Monotonicity along row (beyond diagonal entry).
Let
0\leq i\leq j\leq T-1
. For
0\leq m\leq T-j-1
, we have that
c(i,j)\geq c(i,j+m)
.

3.

Monotonicity of diagonal entries.
Let
0\leq m\leq j\leq T-max(i,j)-1
. We have that
c(i,j)\geq c(i+m,j+m)
.

Proof.

First, we write a closed form expression for
c(i,j)
:

\displaystyle c(i,j)

\displaystyle=(R^{T}R)[i,j]=\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}{R^{T}}[i,k]R[k,j]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=\max(i,j)}^{T-1}{R}[k,i]R[k,j]=\sum_{k=\max(i,j)}^{n-1}r_{k-i}r_{k-j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-\max(i,j)-1}r_{\ell}r_{\ell+|i-j|}\,,

(3)

where the second equality follows from the definition of matrix multiplication, the third equality follows because
R
is lower-triangular and by the definition of transpose, the fourth equality follows because
R
is Toeplitz and hence
R[i,j]=r_{i-j}
, and the final equality is substituting
\ell=k-\max(i,j)
and rewriting the sum in terms of
\ell
.

We now prove each item of the claim.

1.

Symmetry.

c(i,j)=c(j,i)
: this follows because
(R^{T}R)^{T}=R^{T}R
, by properties of the transpose operation.

2.

Monotonicity along row (beyond diagonal entry).
Let
0\leq i\leq j\leq T-1
. For
0\leq m\leq T-j-1
, consider
c(i,j)-c(i,j+m)
. By
Equation

3
, we have that

\displaystyle c(i,j)-c(i,j+m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{T-j-1}r_{\ell}r_{\ell+j-i}-\sum_{\ell=0}^{T-j-m-1}r_{\ell}r_{\ell+j+m-i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{T-j-m-1}r_{\ell}[r_{\ell+j-i}-r_{\ell+j+m-i}]+\sum_{\ell=T-j-m}^{T-j-1}r_{\ell}r_{\ell+j-i}

\displaystyle\geq 0\,,

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
r_{s}\geq r_{t}
for
s\leq t
for our matrix of interest, and by the fact that all
r
values are non-negative.

3.

Monotonicity of diagonal entries.
Let
0\leq i\leq j\leq T-1
. For
0\leq m\leq T-\max(i,j)-1
, consider
c(i,j)-c(i+m,j+m)
. By
Equation

3
, we have that

\displaystyle c(i,j)-c(i+m,j+m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{T-j-1}r_{\ell}r_{\ell+j-i}-\sum_{\ell=0}^{T-j-m-1}r_{\ell}r_{\ell+j+m-i-m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=T-j-m}^{T-j-1}r_{\ell}r_{\ell+j-i}\geq 0\,,

where the last inequality follows since every
r
value is non-negative.

∎

We are now ready to analyze the quadratic form of interest.

\displaystyle\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}\vec{\Delta}^{T}R^{T}R\vec{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[T]}(R^{T}R)[i,j]\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]=\sum_{i,j\in[T]}c(i,j)\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]\,.

(4)

We now analyze this quantity for the sensitivity vector sets that are of interest (those described in
Section

6
).

8.2
Upper Bounding the Sensitivity

The following theorem is the key result for this section, bounding the
\ell_{2}
sensitivity for
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
-streams.

Theorem 8.4
.

Let
R\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
be a lower-triangular Toeplitz matrix with non-increasing, non-negative diagonal values.
Fix
D,k\geq 1
.
Then,

\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})\leq\sqrt{Dk}\cdot\|R\|_{1\to 2}\,.

We will give two separate proofs for the theorem.
The first proof is combinatorial, relying on a matching argument.
It is arguably more intuitive than our second, shorter and more direct proof.

Proof of
Theorem

8.4
via matching argument.

First, consider the simpler case of
D=1
.
Consider any
\vec{\Delta}\in{\mathcal{S}_{1,k}}
. From Equation (
4
),

\displaystyle\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i,j\in[T]}c(i,j)\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}c(i,i)\vec{\Delta}[i]^{2}+2\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq T-1}c(i,j)\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}c(i,i)+2\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq T-1}c(i,j)\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]\,.

(5)

where the second equality follows because of Item 1 of
8.3
(
c(i,j)=c(j,i)
), and the second inequality follows because
\vec{\Delta}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{T}
, because
\vec{\Delta}
is
k
-sparse, and because of Item 3 in
8.3
(
c(i,j)\geq c(i+m,j+m)
for
0\leq m\leq T-\max(i,j)-1
).

We will argue that the second term in the above sum is negative via a combinatorial argument, and thereby bound the result by the first term.

Consider the following matching operation
M
that matches ordered pairs: for every
i<j
such that
\vec{\Delta}[i]\neq\vec{\Delta}[j]
and
\vec{\Delta}i,\vec{\Delta}[j]\neq 0
, match
(i,j)
with
(i,j^{\prime})
where
j^{\prime}
is the smallest value larger than
j
, such that
\vec{\Delta}[i]=\vec{\Delta}[j^{\prime}]
. If such a
j^{\prime}
does not exist, leave
(i,j)
unmatched.

Let the set of matches including
(i,.)
be
M_{i}
. Let the set of unmatched ordered pairs
(i,.)
be
{N_{i}}
.

We observe two properties of this matching
M
:

•

All pairs
(i,j)\in{N_{i}}
satisfy
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
.
We first argue that every
(i,j)
with
\vec{\Delta}[i],\vec{\Delta}[j]\neq 0
and
\vec{\Delta}[i]=\vec{\Delta}[j]
is matched. To see this, consider such an
i
and
j
. Since
\vec{\Delta}
is alternating, there exists an
i<j_{1}<j
such that
\vec{\Delta}[j_{1}]\neq 0
,
\vec{\Delta}[i]\neq\vec{\Delta}[j_{1}]
, and there exists no non-zero entry between
\vec{\Delta}[j_{1}]
and
\vec{\Delta}[j]
. Note that this implies
(i,j)
and
(i,j_{1})
are matched together. This implies that all pairs
(i,j)\in{N_{i}}
satisfy
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
.

•

Contribution of matched pairs is non-positive.
We also argue that in every match
\{(i,j_{1}),(i,j_{2})\}\in M_{i}
(where
\vec{\Delta}[j_{1}]\neq\vec{\Delta}[i]
and
\vec{\Delta}[j]=\vec{\Delta}[i]
) we have that the total contribution of the ordered pairs in this match to the sum of interest is non-positive, that is
c(i,j_{1})\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j_{1}]+c(i,j_{2})\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j_{2}]\leq 0
. This follows since
i<j_{1}<j_{2}
by the way the matching is done, which implies by Item 2 of
8.3
that
c(i,j_{2})\leq c(i,j_{1})
).

Then, analyzing the second term in the right hand side of
Equation

5
, and reducing this to reasoning about each index
i
separately, we get that

\displaystyle 2\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq T-1}c(i,j)\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]

\displaystyle=2\sum_{0\leq i\leq T-1}\left(\sum_{(i,a)\in{N_{i}}}c(i,a)\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[a]+\sum_{\{(i,s),(i,t)\}\in M_{i}}c(i,s)\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[s]+c(i,t)\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[t]\right)

\displaystyle\leq 0

where the last inequality follows from the two properties observed about the matching and from the fact that
c(.,.)
is always non-negative. Hence, in total, from
Equation

5
, we get that

\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}c(i,i)}\leq\sqrt{kc(0,0)}=\sqrt{k}\cdot\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,1}),

where the last inequality follows from the monotonicity of the diagonal entries (Item 3 of
8.3
).
In particular we have shown

\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,k})\leq\sqrt{k}\cdot\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,1})\,.

To complete the proof, we now consider
D>1
.
Using
Lemma

7.3
at the first step, we have that

\displaystyle\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\max_{k_{1},\dots,k_{D}:\sum_{i=1}^{D}k_{i}=k}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,k_{i}})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{k_{1},\dots,k_{D}:\sum_{i=1}^{D}k_{i}=k}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{D}\sqrt{k_{i}}\|R\|_{1\to 2}\right)^{2}\leq Dk\cdot\|R\|_{1\to 2}^{2}

where the second step used our result for
D=1
, and the last step used Cauchy-Schwarz.
Taking a square-root finishes the proof.
∎

In addition to this intuitive combinatorial proof, we also give a proof that is more analytical.
While we do not leverage it in this paper, the proof has the additional benefit that it also holds if
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
is defined over
\mathbb{R}^{T}
, rather than
\mathbb{Z}^{T}
.

Proof of
Theorem

8.4
via Abel summation.

Consider arbitrary
\vec{\Delta}\in\mathcal{S}_{D,k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{T}
.
We can write

\displaystyle\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i,j\in[T]}c(i,j)\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]=\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\vec{\Delta}[i]\underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}c(i,j)\vec{\Delta}[j]}_{Q_{i}}\,,

where we continue working with the inner sum.
Define the infinite sequences
(f_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}},(g_{t})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
with bounded support:

f_{t}=\begin{cases}c(i,t)\quad&\text{for}\ 0\leq t\leq T-1\,,\\
0\quad&\text{otherwise}\,,\end{cases}\quad g_{t}=\begin{cases}\vec{\Delta}[t]\quad&\text{for}\ 0\leq t\leq T-1\,,\\
0\quad&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

Defining
G_{m}=\sum_{t=-\infty}^{m}g_{t}
, we write

\displaystyle Q_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}c(i,j)\vec{\Delta}[j]=\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{t}(G_{t}-G_{t-1})=\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{t}G_{t}-\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{t}G_{t-1}=\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}(f_{t}-f_{t+1})G_{t}\,,

where the last step changes the indexing in the second sum and then merges the sums.
This transformation is sometimes called
Abel summation
.
By the monotonicity, non-negativity and symmetry of
c(i,j)
from
8.3
, we have that
c(i,j)-c(i,j+1)\geq 0
for
j\geq i
, and
c(i,j)-c(i,j+1)\leq 0
for
j<i
.
We can thus write

\displaystyle Q_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq i}\left[c(i,j)-c(i,j+1)\right]G_{j}-\sum_{j<i}\left[c(i,j+1)-c(i,j)\right]G_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}(G_{j})\sum_{j\geq i}\left[c(i,j)-c(i,j+1)\right]-\inf_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}(G_{j})\sum_{j<i}\left[c(i,j+1)-c(i,j)\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\sup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}(G_{j})-\inf_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}(G_{j})\right)c(i,i)\,,

where the inequality uses that
\inf_{k}(b_{k})\sum_{k}a_{k}\leq\sum_{k}a_{k}b_{k}\leq\sup_{k}(b_{k})\sum_{k}a_{k}
for a nonnegative sequence
(a_{k})_{k}
, and the final equality that the sums telescope.
By an analogous argument (swap the role of the sums), we can also derive a lower bound:

\displaystyle Q_{i}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\inf_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}(G_{j})-\sup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}(G_{j})\right)c(i,i)\,.

Combining the two bounds, we get

\displaystyle\lvert Q_{i}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}(G_{j})-\inf_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}(G_{j})\right)c(i,i)\leq D\cdot c(i,i)\,,

where the inequality uses that
G_{j}
is a prefix on
\vec{\Delta}
, and so the difference
\sup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}(G_{j})-\inf_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}(G_{j})
is a (signed)
interval sum on
\vec{\Delta}
, and so bounded by
D
from the definition of
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
.

We thus have (taking absolute value of the summand)

\displaystyle\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\vec{\Delta}[i]Q_{i}\leq D\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\lvert\vec{\Delta}[i]\rvert c(i,i)\,.

Using that
c(i,i)\leq c(0,0)=\|R\|_{1\to 2}^{2}
and that
\|\vec{\Delta}\|_{1}\leq k
by definition:

\displaystyle\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}\leq D\cdot\|R\|_{1\to 2}^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\lvert\vec{\Delta}[i]\rvert\leq Dk\cdot\|R\|_{1\to 2}^{2}\,.

Invoking the final bound for the sensitivity

\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})=\max_{\vec{\Delta}\in\mathcal{S}_{D,k}}\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{Dk}\cdot\|R\|_{1\to 2}

finishes the proof.
∎

8.3
A Lower Bound on the Square-Root Sensitivity

In the case of the square-root factorization,
Theorem

8.4
allows us to give a bound of

\mathsf{sens}_{2}(\sqrt{A},\mathcal{S}_{D,k})\leq\sqrt{Dk}\|\sqrt{A}\|_{1\to 2}=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}+o(1)\right)\sqrt{Dk\ln(T)}

for the
\ell_{2}
sensitivity.
It is natural to ask if our bound is tight for general values of
D
and
k
.
We show that, at least for sufficiently small values of
k/D
, it is.

Theorem 8.6
.

For
1\leq D\leq k
,
T
sufficiently large, and
k/D=O(T^{1/3})
, we have that

\mathsf{sens}_{2}(\sqrt{A},\mathcal{S}_{D,k})\geq\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}-o(1)\right)\sqrt{D^{2}\lfloor k/D\rfloor\ln\left(\frac{T}{\lfloor k/D\rfloor}\right)}\,,

where
o(1)
tends to zero as
T\to\infty
.
If additionally
k/D=\omega(1)
, then the following simpler bound holds

\mathsf{sens}_{2}(\sqrt{A},\mathcal{S}_{D,k})\geq\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}-o(1)\right)\sqrt{Dk\ln(DT/k)}\,.

Essentially, our upper bound on the sensitivity has the correct asymptotics for
k/D=O(T^{1/3})
.
Moreover, if
k/D
is subpolynomial and either (1)
D=1
, or (2)
k/D
is superconstant, then additionally the leading constant in the upper bound is tight.

The theorem is proved using the probabilistic method: for a
\vec{\Delta}
drawn uniformly at random from
\mathcal{S}_{1,k}
(conditioned on
\|\vec{\Delta}\|_{1}=k
), we show that
\mathbb{E}[\|\sqrt{A}\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}]\leq\mathsf{sens}_{2}(\sqrt{A},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})^{2}
has to be large.
Using
Lemma

7.1
, we are then able to extend this result to
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
.

We need the following two lemmas to prove
Theorem

8.6
.
The first lemma is a bound on
c(i,j)
for the square-root whenever
i\neq j
.
The techniques used for proving it are standard, with similar statements having appeared in past work studying the square-root factorization, see e.g.,
[
30
,
38
,
47
,
21
,
48
]
.
The second lemma bounds the expected parity of a hypergeometric random variable.
Such a result is likely folklore, but we could find no reference for it.

Lemma 8.7
.

Let
R=\sqrt{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
be the square-root matrix, and define
c(i,j)\equiv(R^{T}R)[i,j]
for
i,j\in[T]
.
The following bounds hold for
i\neq j
:

\displaystyle c(i,j)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\ln\left(1+\frac{T}{\lvert i-j\rvert}\right)+\frac{3}{2}\,.

Lemma 8.8
.

Let
N,K,n\geq 1
and
X\sim\mathrm{Hypergeom}(N,K,n)
.
That is,
X
describes the number of successful draws out of
n
, when sampling without replacement from a population of size
N
, out of which
K
elements are successes.
Then

\lvert\mathbb{E}\left[(-1)^{X}\right]\rvert\leq\exp(-2nK/N)+n(n-1)/N\,.

Proof of
Lemma

8.7
.

Recall that the square-root matrix has entries
\sqrt{A}[i,j]=r_{i-j}
where
r_{t}=\binom{2t}{t}/4^{t}
, and in particular satisfies the bounds

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(t+1)}}\leq r_{t}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi t}}

for all
t\geq 1
(
Theorem

8.5
).

Without loss of generality, let
j<i
.
Define
d=i-j>0
, and let
N=T-1-\max(i,j)=T-1-i

\displaystyle c(i,j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{N}r_{t}r_{t+d}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi d}}+\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t(t+d)}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi d}}+\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{d+1}}+\frac{1}{\pi}\int^{N}_{1}\frac{\mathrm{dz}}{\sqrt{z(z+d)}}

We continue working on the integral:

\int^{N}_{1}\frac{\mathrm{dz}}{\sqrt{z(z+d)}}=\left[2\cdot\mathrm{asinh}\left(\sqrt{\frac{z}{d}}\right)\right]_{z=1}^{z=N}\leq 2\cdot\mathrm{asinh}\left(\sqrt{N/d}\right)\,,

where
\mathrm{asinh}(x)\equiv\ln(x+\sqrt{1+x^{2}})
.
Given
x\geq 0
, we have
\sqrt{1+x^{2}}\leq 1+x
, and so
\mathrm{asinh}(x)\leq\ln(1+2x)
.
Further, note that
1+2\sqrt{x}\leq 3\sqrt{x}
if
x>0
, and so also
\ln(1+2\sqrt{x})\leq\ln(3)+\frac{1}{2}\ln(x)\leq\ln(3)+\frac{1}{2}\ln(1+x)
.
Similarly,
1+2\sqrt{x}\leq 3
for
0<x<1
, and so
\ln(1+2\sqrt{x})\leq\ln(3)\leq\ln(3)+\frac{1}{2}\ln(1+x)
.
We can thus write

\mathrm{asinh}\left(\sqrt{N/d}\right)\leq\ln(1+2\sqrt{N/d})\leq\ln(3)+\frac{1}{2}\ln(1+N/d)\,.

Putting everything together

\displaystyle c(i,j)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi d}}+\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{d+1}}+\frac{2}{\pi}\left(\ln(3)+\frac{1}{2}\ln(1+N/d)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi d}}+\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{d+1}}+\frac{2\ln(3)}{\pi}+\frac{1}{\pi}\ln(1+N/d)

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}+\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{2}}+\frac{2\ln(3)}{\pi}}_{\approx 1.489}+\frac{1}{\pi}\ln(1+N/d)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}+\frac{\ln\left(1+\frac{N}{d}\right)}{\pi}\,.

Since
c(i,j)=c(j,i)
, the final lemma statement follows from setting
d=\lvert i-j\rvert
and upper bounding
N=T-\max(i,j)-1\leq T
.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

8.8
.

We will upper bound the expression by approximating it with a binomial distribution.
Define
\hat{X}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)
for
p=K/N
.
We have that

\mathbb{E}\left[(-1)^{\hat{X}}\right]=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}(-1)^{\ell}\binom{n}{\ell}p^{\ell}(1-p)^{n-\ell}=(1-2p)^{n}\leq\exp(-2pn)

where the second equality follows from the binomial expansion of
(1-p+(-p))^{n}
.
Note that
X
describes sampling
without
replacement, and
\hat{X}

with
replacement.
Consider the process where we instead draw
n
samples with replacement from
N
.
The probability of avoiding a collision (sampling an element more than once) is:

\Pr[\text{\#collisions}=0]=\frac{N}{N}\cdot\frac{N-1}{N}\cdot\ldots\cdot\frac{N-n+1}{N}=\prod_{\ell=0}^{n-1}(1-\ell/N)

and so

\Pr[\text{\#collisions}\geq 1]=1-\prod_{\ell=0}^{n-1}(1-\ell/N)\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\frac{\ell}{N}=\frac{n(n-1)}{2N}\,,

where the inequality is a union bound.
We now use a coupling trick.
Let
\hat{X}
be the outcome of sampling these
n
samples with replacement from
N
.
If there is no collision among the
n
samples, then we define
X=\hat{X}
.
Otherwise, if there is a collision, we set
X
according to the outcome of sampling
n
fresh elements without replacement.
Clearly
X
and
\hat{X}
follow the correct hypergeometric and binomial distributions respectively, but now
\Pr[X\neq\hat{X}]\leq\Pr[\text{\#collisions}\geq 1]\leq\frac{n(n-1)}{2N}
.

To finish the proof, we establish that the expectations are close:

\displaystyle\big\lvert\mathbb{E}\big[(-1)^{X}\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[(-1)^{\hat{X}}\big]\big\rvert

\displaystyle=\big\lvert\mathbb{E}\big[(-1)^{X}-(-1)^{\hat{X}}\big]\big\rvert\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\big\lvert(-1)^{X}-(-1)^{\hat{X}}\big\rvert\big]

\displaystyle=2\cdot\Pr[X+\hat{X}\ \text{is odd}]\leq 2\cdot\Pr[X\neq\hat{X}]

\displaystyle\leq n(n-1)/N\,.

The final statement follows from invoking the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle\big\lvert\mathbb{E}\big[(-1)^{X}\big]\big\rvert\leq\lvert\mathbb{E}\big[(-1)^{\hat{X}}\big]\rvert+n(n-1)/N\leq\exp(-2nK/N)+n(n-1)/N\,.\qquad\qed

We proceed to prove
Theorem

8.6
.

Proof of
Theorem

8.6
.

Let
R=\sqrt{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
.
Consider the case where
D=1
and
k\geq 3
; we will return to the other cases towards the end of the proof.
We will argue via the probabilistic method.
Consider
\vec{\Delta}
drawn uniformly at random from
\mathcal{S}_{1,k}
, conditioned on
\|\vec{\Delta}\|_{1}=k
.
Call this distribution
\mathcal{U}
.
If we can show a lower bound on the expectation of
\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}
, then that implies a lower bound on the (squared)
\ell_{2}
sensitivity, i.e.,

\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,k})\geq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\vec{\Delta}\sim\mathcal{U}}\left[\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}\right]}\,.

We suppress the subscript of the expectation from now on; all expectations are taken over
\mathcal{U}
.
We have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{(i,j)\in[T]^{2}}c(i,j)\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]\right]=\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}c(i,i)\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\Delta}[i]^{2}\right]+2\sum_{j<i}c(i,j)\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]\right]\,,

by linearity of expectation.
We begin with the first term.
Noting that
\mathbb{E}[\vec{\Delta}[i]^{2}]=k/T
, we can directly bound it using the bound on
c(i,i)
from
Theorem

8.5
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}c(i,i)\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\Delta}[i]^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{k}{T}\left(T+\sum_{i=1}^{T-1}\frac{\ln(T-i)}{\pi}\right)\geq\frac{k}{T}\left(\frac{(\pi-1)T}{\pi}+\frac{T\ln(T)}{\pi}\right)=\frac{k(\ln(T)+\pi-1)}{\pi}\,,

where the last inequality uses Stirling’s approximation applied to
\ln(T!)\geq T\ln(T)-T
.

For the second term, we first reason about
\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]\right]
.
Let
X\sim\mathrm{Hypergeom}(N,K,n)
denote a random variable drawn from a hypergeometric distribution, where we are drawing
n=k-2
samples from a population of
N=T-2
, out of which
K=i-j-1
are labeled successes.
We claim that

\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]\Big|\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]\neq 0\right]\Pr\left[\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]\neq 0\right]=-\mathbb{E}\left[(-1)^{X}\right]\cdot\frac{k(k-1)}{T(T-1)}\,.

To see the last step, note that the probability of both
\vec{\Delta}[i]
and
\vec{\Delta}[j]
being non-zero equals the second factor, and that conditioning on this event, the sign of their product is decided by the parity of the number of non-zero elements in
\vec{\Delta}
between
i
and
j
, i.e., the parity of
X
.

Invoking
Lemma

8.8
, we arrive at the following bound.

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]\right]\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k(k-1)}{T(T-1)}\left(\exp\left(-\frac{2(k-2)(i-j-1)}{T-2}\right)+\frac{(k-2)(k-3)}{T-2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k^{2}}{T^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{2(k-2)(i-j-1)}{T-2}\right)+\frac{k^{4}}{T^{3}}

We can now start working with the off-diagonal term.

\displaystyle\left\lvert 2\sum_{j<i}c(i,j)\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]\right]\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{j<i}c(i,j)\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]\right]\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\frac{2k^{2}}{T^{2}}\sum_{j<i}\exp\left(-\frac{2(k-2)(i-j-1)}{T-2}\right)c(i,j)}_{S_{exp}}+\underbrace{\frac{2k^{4}}{T^{3}}\sum_{j<i}c(i,j)}_{S_{poly}}

We will first argue that
S_{poly}=o(k\log k)
.
Note that

\sum_{j<i}c(i,j)\leq\sum_{i,j\in[T]}c(i,j)=\|\sqrt{A}\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}=O(T^{2})\,.

The last step requires some computation, but follows from
(\sqrt{A}\mathbf{1})[\ell]=\sum_{t=0}^{\ell}r_{t}=O(\sqrt{\ell})
.
Thus
S_{poly}=O(k^{4}/T)=O(k)
from
k=O(T^{1/3})
.

We proceed with
S_{exp}
.
We will use that
c(i,j)\leq\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{\pi}\ln(1+\frac{T}{\lvert i-j\rvert})
from
Lemma

8.7
.

\displaystyle S_{exp}

\displaystyle=\frac{2k^{2}}{\pi T^{2}}\sum_{j<i}\exp\left(-\frac{2(k-2)(i-j-1)}{T-2}\right)\left(\frac{3\pi}{2}+\ln\left(1+\frac{T}{i-j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2k^{2}}{\pi T^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{T-2}\sum_{d=1}^{T-1-j}\exp\left(-\frac{2(k-2)(d-1)}{T-2}\right)\left(\frac{3\pi}{2}+\ln\left(1+\frac{T}{d}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2k^{2}}{\pi T^{2}}\sum_{d=1}^{T-1}(T-d)\exp\left(-\frac{2(k-2)(d-1)}{T-2}\right)\left(\frac{3\pi}{2}+\ln\left(1+\frac{T}{d}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2k^{2}}{\pi T}\sum_{d=1}^{T-1}\exp\left(-\frac{2(k-2)(d-1)}{T-2}\right)\left(\frac{3\pi}{2}+\ln\left(1+\frac{T}{d}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{3k^{2}}{T}\underbrace{\sum_{d=1}^{T-1}e^{-\alpha(d-1)}}_{Q_{1}}+\frac{2k^{2}}{\pi T}\underbrace{\sum_{d=1}^{T-1}e^{-\alpha(d-1)}\ln\left(1+\frac{T}{d}\right)}_{Q_{2}}\,,

where
\alpha=\frac{2(k-2)}{T-2}
.
We first bound
Q_{1}
:

\displaystyle Q_{1}=\sum_{d=1}^{T-1}e^{-\alpha(d-1)}\leq\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha d}=\frac{1}{1-e^{-\alpha}}\leq 1+\frac{1}{\alpha}=\Theta(T/k)\,.

where the second inequality uses that
1-e^{-x}\geq x/(1+x)
for any
x\geq 0
.
For
Q_{2}
, we have to do more work.
Let
f(x)=e^{-\alpha x}\ln\left(1+\frac{T}{x+1}\right)
, which is positive for
x\geq 0
.
Computing its derivative,

f^{\prime}(x)=-e^{-\alpha x}\left[\alpha\ln\left(1+\frac{T}{x+1}\right)+\frac{T}{(x+1)^{2}+T(x+1)}\right]\,,

we can verify that it is negative on
[0,\infty)
, and so
f
is strictly decreasing on this range.
We can thus bound
Q_{2}
as

\displaystyle Q_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{T-2}f(d)\leq\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}f(d)\leq f(0)+\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\,\mathrm{dx}=\ln(1+T)+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha x}\ln\left(1+\frac{T}{x+1}\right)\,\mathrm{dx}\,.

We continue working with the integral:

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha x}\ln\left(1+\frac{T}{x+1}\right)\,\mathrm{dx}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha x}\ln(1+T/x)\,\mathrm{dx}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-y}\ln(1+K/y)\,\mathrm{dy}\,,

where the last step makes the substitution
y=\alpha x
and sets
K=\alpha T=\Theta(k)
.
Re-writing the integrand, we have that

\ln(1+K/y)=\ln K-\ln y+\ln(1+y/K)\,.

Thus,

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-y}\ln(1+K/y)\,\mathrm{dy}=\ln K\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-y}\,\mathrm{dy}}_{=1}-\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-y}\ln y\,\mathrm{dy}}_{=-\gamma}+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-y}\ln(1+y/K)\,\mathrm{dy}\,,

where
\gamma\approx 0.5772
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant.
For the last integral, we use the bound
\ln(1+u)\leq u
for
u\geq 0
:

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-y}\ln(1+y/K)\,\mathrm{dy}\leq\frac{1}{K}\int_{0}^{\infty}ye^{-y}\,\mathrm{dy}=\frac{1}{K}\,.

We can now bound
Q_{2}
:

\displaystyle Q_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\ln(1+T)+\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-y}\ln(1+K/y)\,\mathrm{dy}\leq\ln(1+T)+\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\ln K+\gamma+\frac{1}{K}\right)\,.

As
\frac{2k}{T-2}\leq\alpha=\frac{2(k-2)}{T-2}\leq\frac{2k}{T}
and so
2(k-2)\leq K=\alpha T\leq 2k
, we simplify the bound further:

\displaystyle Q_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T-2}{2k}\bigg(\ln(2k)+\underbrace{\frac{2k\ln T}{T-2}+\gamma+\frac{1}{2(k-2)}}_{O(1)}\bigg)=\frac{T\ln k}{2k}+O(T/k)

where
k\ln(T)/T=O(1)
as
k=o(T)
.
We proceed to bound
S_{exp}
:

\displaystyle S_{exp}\leq\frac{k^{2}}{\pi T}(3\pi Q_{1}+2Q_{2})\leq\frac{k^{2}}{\pi T}\left(\frac{T\ln k}{k}+O(T/k)\right)=\frac{k\ln k}{\pi}+O(k)\,.

We are now ready to put all the pieces together.

\displaystyle\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,k})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\|R\vec{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}c(i,i)\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\Delta}[i]^{2}\right]+2\sum_{j<i}c(i,j)\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{k\ln(T)}{\pi}+O(k)-\left\lvert 2\sum_{j<i}c(i,j)\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]\right]\right\rvert

\displaystyle\geq\frac{k\ln(T)}{\pi}+O(k)-S_{exp}-S_{poly}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{k\ln(T)}{\pi}-\frac{k\ln(k)}{\pi}-O(k)

\displaystyle=(1-o(1))\cdot\frac{k\ln(T/k)}{\pi}\,.

To get the final statement, take the square-root.

\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,k})\geq\sqrt{(1-o(1))\cdot\frac{k\ln(T/k)}{\pi}}=(1-o(1))\cdot\sqrt{\frac{k\ln(T/k)}{\pi}}\,.

Lastly, we address the more general settings of
k
and
D
.
Note that for
D=1,k=1
, the problem is trivial since
\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,S_{1,1})=\|R\|_{1\to 2}
.
For
D=1,k=2
, a little more work is needed.
The off-diagonal term can now be computed directly

\displaystyle 2\sum_{j<i}c(i,j)\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\Delta}[i]\vec{\Delta}[j]\right]=-\frac{2\cdot 2\cdot 1}{T(T-1)}\sum_{j<i}c(i,j)=O(1)

where the final sum is
O(T^{2})
by the same argument as before, and this term is of lower order compared to the diagonal contribution of
k\ln(T)/\pi
.
For
1<D\leq k
, we invoke
Lemma

7.1
:

\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})\geq D\cdot\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{1,\lfloor k/D\rfloor})\geq\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}-o(1)\right)\cdot\sqrt{D^{2}\lfloor k/D\rfloor\ln\left(T/\lfloor k/D\rfloor\right)}

which allows us to reduce to the previous setting where
D=1
.
If additionally
k/D=\omega(1)
, then
\lfloor k/D\rfloor=(1-o(1))k/D
, and the expression can be simplified one step further.

\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})\geq\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}-o(1)\right)\cdot\sqrt{Dk\ln(DT/k)}\,.\qed

Proof of
Corollary

8.2
.

The corollary follows by multiplying the sensitivity
\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})
by either
\|\sqrt{A}\|_{2\to\infty}
or
\|\sqrt{A}\|_{F}/\sqrt{T}
, for
\mathsf{MaxSE}
and
\mathsf{MeanSE}
respectively, and then dividing by
\sqrt{2\rho}
.
Since
\|\sqrt{A}\|_{2\to\infty}
and
\|\sqrt{A}\|_{F}/\sqrt{T}
are
(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}+o(1))\sqrt{\ln(T)}
(See (2), (3) and (4) of
Theorem

8.5
), the corollary statement is immediate.
∎

9
Tree-based Factorizations

The first mechanism to achieve non-trivial error bounds for private continual counting was the
binary tree mechanism

[
23
,
13
]
.
Throughout this section we will consider the natural extension to
b
-ary tree mechanisms, which has been studied in prior work
[
55
,
17
,
11
,
2
]
.
While our focus is on
\rho
-zCDP, as in the case of standard continual counting on
\mathcal{S}_{1,1}
-streams, tree-based factorizations also give state-of-the-art error for pure differential privacy.
Towards the end of this section, we give error bounds for both privacy variants.

In
Section

9.1
we introduce tree-based factorizations studied in the context of regular
\mathcal{S}_{1,1}
-counting.
This includes stating the necessary (known) bounds on the left factor, which we also prove for the sake of completeness.
Next in
Section

9.2
, we investigate the sensitivity computation for
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
-streams.
We show how, in the context of trees, the sensitivity computation with respect to the right factor for
\mathcal{S}_{1,k}
reduces to a parity counting problem, which can then be extended to
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
using
Theorem

7.4
.
Finally,
Section

9.3
states tight error bounds for tree-based factorizations on general
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
-streams.

9.1
An Introduction to
b
-ary Tree Mechanisms

We begin with a short summary in the case where the number of steps
T=b^{h}
for some positive integer
h
.
Throughout we will use the notation

\mathcal{T}_{b,h}=\bigcup_{\ell=0}^{h}\mathcal{T}^{\ell}_{b,h}\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{T}_{b,h}^{\ell}=\{[j\cdot b^{\ell},(j+1)\cdot b^{\ell}]:\ 0\leq j\leq b^{h-\ell}-1\}\,,

for the
complete
b
-ary tree of height
h
with
m=(b^{h+1}-1)/(b-1)
nodes.
The
b
-ary tree mechanism can be expressed as a factorization mechanism for matrices
L_{b}\in\{0,1\}^{T\times m},R_{b}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times T}
.
Defining the bijective node-to-index mapping
i:\mathcal{T}_{b,h}\to[m]
, the factorization
L_{b}R_{b}=A
satisfies

1.

For each node
v\in\mathcal{T}_{b,h}:(R\vec{\mathbf{x}})_{i(v)}=\sum_{j\in v}\vec{\mathbf{x}}[j]
.

2.

For each time step
t\in[T]
, define
\mathcal{Q}_{b}(t)
as the (unique) minimal set of nodes
\mathcal{Q}_{b}(t)\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{b,h}
satisfying

\bigcup_{v\in\mathcal{Q}_{b}(t)}v=[0,t]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\forall v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}_{b}(t):v\cap v^{\prime}=\emptyset\quad\text{or}\quad v=v^{\prime}\,.

Then for each
v\in\mathcal{Q}_{b}(t):L_{b}[t,i(v)]=1
.

Intuitively, the
b
-ary tree mechanism releases a noisy version of the nodes
R_{b}\vec{\mathbf{x}}
, and decodes the tree into a prefix by adding together a minimal number of nodes per step.
As
\lvert\mathcal{Q}_{b}(t)\rvert
describes the number of nodes added together at time
t
, it follows that
\|L_{b}\|_{2\to\infty}
and
\|L_{b}\|_{F}
can be computed from the maximum and average value of
\lvert\mathcal{Q}_{b}(t)\rvert
respectively.
We sketch a proof of the following known lemma for completeness (see e.g.,
[
13
,
2
]
).

Lemma 9.1
.

For
T=b^{h}
,
\|L_{b}\|_{2\to\infty}=\sqrt{(b-1)h}
and
\|L_{b}\|_{F}/\sqrt{T}=\sqrt{(b-1)h/2+b^{-h}}
.

We will use the following observation about
\mathcal{Q}_{b}(t)
, see e.g.,
[
13
,
3
]
.

Claim 9.2
.

For
b\geq 2
, let
\vec{\mathbf{w}}(i)\in[b]^{h}
be the encoding of an integer
i\leq b^{h}-1
as an
h
-digit number in base
b
.
Then
\lvert\mathcal{Q}_{b}(t)\rvert=\|\vec{\mathbf{w}}(t+1)\|_{1}
.

Proof sketch of
Lemma

9.1
.

Note that
\|L_{b}\|_{2\to\infty}^{2}\hskip-1.0pt=\hskip-1.0pt\max_{t\in[T]}\lvert\mathcal{Q}_{b}(t)\rvert
and
\|L_{b}\|_{F}^{2}\hskip-1.0pt=\hskip-1.0pt\sum_{t\in[n]}\lvert\mathcal{Q}_{b}(t)\rvert
, and so the computation reduces to arguing about the maximum and average size of
\mathcal{Q}_{b}(t)
.
Both will be argued through
9.2
.
The result for
\|L_{b}\|_{2\to\infty}
follows by noting that the encoding
\vec{\mathbf{w}}(b^{h}-1)
has
h
digits with value
b-1
.
For
\|L_{b}\|_{F}
, note that
\vec{\mathbf{w}}(t+1)
cycles through all
h
-digit numbers in the
T
steps, except for
\vec{\mathbf{w}}(0)
, plus the
(h+1)
-digit number
\vec{\mathbf{w}}(T)
which is zero except for a most significant digit of 1.
As the average
h
digit number has an
\ell_{1}
weight of
h(b-1)/2
, we get
\sum_{t\in[T]}\lvert\mathcal{Q}_{b}(t)\rvert=hT(b-1)/2+1
.
Dividing by
T
and taking the root finishes the proof.
∎

Combining this result with the fact that
\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,1})=\sqrt{h+1}
allows for recovering the standard
O(\log T)
bounds on
\mathsf{MeanSE}(L_{b},R_{b})
and
\mathsf{MaxSE}(L_{b},R_{b})
for regular continual counting.
By tightly analyzing
\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R_{b},\mathcal{S}_{D,k})
, our goal will be to extend these error bounds to
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
-streams.

9.1.1
Constant Factor Improvement via Subtraction.

However, before doing that, we note that on fixing the right factor
R_{b}
, the choice of left factor
L_{b}
is not unique.
Recent work
[
2
]
suggested a different choice of left factor that improves leading constants at no cost in time or memory efficiency—they remain
O(T)
and
O(\log T)
for constant
b
.
While they studied pure differential privacy, the same idea extends to
\rho
-zCDP.
We summarize their construction next.

Let the branching factor
b
be odd.
On each level, their construction either adds
\leq(b-1)/2
left-most children or
subtracts

\leq(b-1)/2
right-most children to form a prefix.
The root of the tree can only be added, and the middle child of each parent is never used.
This uniquely identifies the nodes used at each step.
Denoting the (unique) set of nodes used for the prefix at time
t
by the union of the nodes added and subtracted,
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{b}(t)=\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{b}^{+}(t)\cup\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{b}^{-}(t)
, we have that

(A\vec{\mathbf{x}})[t]=\sum_{i=0}^{t}\vec{\mathbf{x}}[i]=\sum_{v\in\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{b}^{+}(t)}\sum_{i\in v}\vec{\mathbf{x}}[i]-\sum_{v\in\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{b}^{-}(t)}\sum_{i\in v}\vec{\mathbf{x}}[i]\,.

Each row of
\hat{L}_{b}
is thus defined by
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{b}(t)
where
\hat{L}_{b}[t,i(v)]=1
if
v\in\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{b}^{+}(t)
, equals
-1
if
v\in\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{b}^{-}(t)
and is otherwise zero.
The following lemma is adapted from
[
2
]
.

Lemma 9.3
.

Let
b\geq 3
be odd and
T=b^{h}
for
h\geq 1
.
Then

\|\hat{L}_{b}\|_{2\to\infty}=\sqrt{\frac{(b-1)h+2}{2}}\,,\qquad\frac{\|\hat{L}_{b}\|_{F}}{\sqrt{T}}=\frac{\sqrt{b(1-1/b^{2})h+2(1+b^{-h})}}{2}\,.

For proving it, we will use the following claim from
[
2
]
, relating the number of nodes used per step to the
\ell_{1}
weight of
offset
encodings in
b
-ary.

Claim 9.4
.

For odd
b\geq 3
, let
\hat{\vec{\mathbf{w}}}(i)\in[-(b-1)/2,(b-1)/2]^{h}
be the encoding of an integer
i\leq(b^{h}-1)/2
as an
h
-digit number in base
b
with offset digits.
Then
\lvert\hat{\mathcal{Q}_{b}}(t)\rvert=\|\hat{\vec{\mathbf{w}}}(t+1)\|_{1}
.

Proof of
Lemma

9.3
.

As before, the arguments reduces to analyzing

\max_{t\in[T]}\lvert\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{b}(t)\rvert\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}\lvert\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{b}(t)\rvert\,.

For the maximum number of nodes, it is equal to
1+h(b-1)/2
, and takes place when the root is included and every level below subtracts
(b-1)/2
nodes at
t=T-2
.
The average number of nodes requires a more elaborate argument, but follows almost directly from the proof of Lemma 14 in
[
2
]
.
By
9.4
, the number of nodes is again related to the
\ell_{1}
weight of encodings of
1,\dots,T
, but now they are encoded as
h+1
-digit numbers with digits in
[-(b-1)/2,(b-1)/2]
.
Over all
T
steps, the least significant
h
digits will cycle through every expressible number in
h
digits
exactly
once.
The average
\ell_{1}
weight of a single digit is
\frac{b(1-1/b^{2})}{4}
, so these lower digits contribute
\frac{b(1-1/b^{2})h}{4}
on average over
T
steps.
Taking into account that the root of the tree is only used for the last
(b^{h}+1)/2
steps, we arrive at a final average number of nodes

\frac{b(1-1/b^{2})h}{4}+\frac{(b^{h}+1)/2}{b^{h}}=\frac{b(1-1/b^{2})h+2(1+b^{-h})}{4}\,,

finishing the proof.
∎

For our final error bounds, we will also want to consider the case where the numbers of steps
T
is not an exact integer power of
b
.
Here we consider
h=\lceil\log_{b}T\rceil
, and define
\hat{L}_{b}
as only the first
T
rows of the corresponding matrix for
T^{\prime}=b^{h}
steps.
The existence of bounds in this case is implicit from
[
2
]
, but they are not formally proved in that work.
We do so next.

Corollary 9.5
.

Let
b\geq 3
be odd,
T\geq b
, and
h=\lceil\log_{b}(T)\rceil
such that
b^{h-1}<T\leq b^{h}
.
Then

\|\hat{L}_{b}\|_{2\to\infty}\leq\sqrt{\frac{(b-1)h+2}{2}}\,,\qquad\frac{\|\hat{L}_{b}\|_{F}}{\sqrt{T}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{b(1-1/b^{2})h+2(1+b^{2}+b^{-h})}}{2}\,.

If additionally
T\geq b^{2b}
we also have the lower bounds

\|\hat{L}_{b}\|_{2\to\infty}\geq\sqrt{\frac{(b-1)(h-1)+2}{2}}\,,\qquad\frac{\|\hat{L}_{b}\|_{F}}{\sqrt{T}}\geq\frac{\sqrt{b(1-1/b^{2})(h-1)+2(1-b^{2}+b^{-h})}}{2}\,.

Proof.

We begin with the maximum number of nodes.
It is easy to see that we can lower and upper bound this number by the number of nodes used at time
t^{\prime}=b^{h-1}-2<T-1
and
t=b^{h}-2
respectively.
Each corresponding to adding the root and subtracting
(b-1)/2
nodes per level for a tree of height
h-1
and
h
.
Thus

1+\frac{(h-1)(b-1)}{2}\leq\max_{t\in[T]}\lvert\hat{Q}_{b}(t)\rvert\leq 1+\frac{h(b-1)}{2}\,,

and the final bound follows from taking a square-root.

For the mean error, we will argue about how much the contribution at each level can deviate from its average.
As already stated, the lowest
h
digits contribute
b(1-1/b^{2})/4
nodes per step on average over
b^{h}
steps.
Note that each of these
h
digits appear cyclically, where the
\ell
th
least significant digit appears with period
b^{\ell+1}
.
By choosing
T
adversarially, we could end up with one incomplete period contributing more or less than the average.
It follows that the average number of nodes contributed by this digit can shift the average by at most
0.5(b-1)b^{\ell+1}/T<0.5(b-1)b^{\ell-h+2}
.
The total shift in average
\ell_{1}
weight over the lowest
h
digits is therefore
<\sum_{\ell=0}^{h-1}0.5(b-1)b^{\ell-h+2}=b^{2}/2
.

For the upper bound, note that the root of the tree is only used for the last
(b^{h}+1)/2
steps in the complete tree, and so its average contribution for the full tree already serves as an upper bound.
Thus:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}\hat{Q}_{b}(t)\leq\frac{b^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{b^{h}}\sum_{t\in[b^{h}]}\hat{Q}_{b}(t)=\frac{b(1-1/b^{2})h+2(1+b^{2}+b^{-h})}{4}\,.

For the lower bound, we take the exact average at time
t=b^{h-1}
, and again use that the total shift in average
\ell_{1}
weight at time
T
is at most
b^{2}/2
, i.e.,

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}\hat{Q}_{b}(t)\geq-\frac{b^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{b^{h-1}}\sum_{t\in[b^{h}]}\hat{Q}_{b}(t)=\frac{b(1-1/b^{2})(h-1)+2(1-b^{2}+b^{-(h-1)})}{4}\,.

The right-hand side can be checked to be positive for
h\geq 1+2b
.
This is satisfied when
T>b^{h-1}\geq b^{2b}
.
Taking square-roots on each of the bounds gives the statement.
∎

Corollary

9.5
essentially says that the scaling in
Lemma

9.3
holds when you relax to arbitrary
T
.
For sufficiently large
T
and constant
b
, both expressions are
\Theta(\sqrt{\log(T)})
with explicit, tight leading constants.

9.2
Sensitivity via Parity Counting

Having stated bounds for the left factor matrix, we shift our focus to the challenge unique to our setting: computing the sensitivity
\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R_{b},\mathcal{S}_{D,k})
.
Given the combinatorial structure of trees, it will prove tractable to study the problem for
\mathcal{S}_{1,k}
, where the sensitivity vectors are in
\{0,\pm 1\}^{T}
, alternating and
k
-sparse.
After giving tight bounds in this case, we extend them to
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
via
Theorem

7.4
.
Previous work
[
43
, Lemma B.1]
established a bound of

\mathsf{sens}_{2}(R_{2},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})\leq\sqrt{k(1+\log T)}

for the binary tree and its
\ell_{2}
sensitivity (after translating their neighboring relation to our setup).
We will tighten this bound asymptotically to
\Theta(\sqrt{k\log_{b}(T/k)})
for a
b
-ary tree.

We take a combinatorial view, reducing the problem to parity counting in the tree.
For now, assume that
T=b^{h}
for a positive integer
h
so that we are working with a complete tree
\mathcal{T}_{b,h}
.
Call the subset
\mathcal{B}\subseteq[T]
a configuration of
balls
.
For any node
v\in\mathcal{T}_{b,h}
, we consider its subtree a
bin
, and define
o_{v}(\mathcal{B})=\lvert\mathcal{B}\cap v\rvert\pmod{2}
as the indicator variable for if node
v
has an odd number of balls in its bin.
Define

F_{b}(h,k)=\max_{\mathcal{B}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{T}:\,\lvert\mathcal{B}\rvert=k}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{T}_{b,h}}o_{v}(\mathcal{B})\,,

that is,
F_{b}(h,k)
, is the maximum number of odd nodes in
\mathcal{T}_{b,h}
taken over all assignments of
k
balls.
The following lemma is central to our analysis.

Lemma 9.6
.

For
p\geq 1
,
\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})=\sqrt[p]{\max_{k^{\prime}\leq k}F_{b}(h,k^{\prime})}
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})=\max_{\vec{\Delta}\in\mathcal{S}_{1,k}}\|R_{b}\vec{\Delta}\|_{p}
.
For
\vec{\Delta}\in\mathcal{S}_{1,k}
, observe that
R_{b}\vec{\Delta}\in\{0,\pm 1\}^{m}
, due to each node in the tree being an interval sum on
\vec{\Delta}
, and
\vec{\Delta}
having non-zero entries of alternating sign.
Setting
\mathcal{B}
equal to the non-zero indices of
\vec{\Delta}
we therefore have that

\|R_{b}\vec{\Delta}\|_{p}^{p}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{T}_{b,h}}\lvert(R_{b}\vec{\Delta})[i(v)]\rvert^{p}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{T}_{b,h}}o_{v}(\mathcal{B})\,,

and so
\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})^{p}=\max_{\mathcal{B}\in\{0,1\}^{T}:\lvert\mathcal{B}\rvert\leq k}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{T}_{b,h}}o_{v}(\mathcal{B})=\max_{k^{\prime}\leq k}F_{b}(h,k^{\prime})
.
∎

In essence, parity counting exactly describes the sensitivity of the tree.
We next show how
F(h,k)
can computed using dynamic programming.

Lemma 9.7

(A dynamic program)
.

Let
h\geq 1
and
k\leq b^{h}
.
Then

F_{b}(h,k)=\mathbbm{1}_{[k\,\mathrm{odd}]}+\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+\dots+k_{b}=k\\
\forall i:0\leq k_{i}\leq b^{h-1}\end{subarray}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{b}F_{b}(h-1,k_{i})\right\}\,,\quad\mathrm{where}\quad F_{b}(0,k)=k\,.

Moreover,
F_{b}(h,k)
can be computed as a dynamic program with table size
O(k)
and in time
O(hk^{b-1})
.

Proof sketch.

The correctness of the recurrence follows immediately from the problem description.
To maximize the number of odd nodes, we can enumerate over all possible assignments of balls to the root’s children and recurse, and any leaf can contribute at most 1 to the final count.
The recurrence can be solved as a dynamic program
“bottom-up”
, and would require
O(k)
memory per level which can be re-used across levels.
For the time-complexity, observe that the maximization is enumerating over all integer partitions of
k
into
b
(non-negative) integer parts.
We can upper bound the number of such integer partitions by the number of weak compositions of
k
into
b
non-negative parts:
\binom{k+b-1}{b-1}=O(k^{b-1})
(for constant
b
).
This overcounts the number of integer partitions by at most a constant factor
b!\,
.
Performing this maximization over all levels gives the stated
O(hk^{b-1})
time complexity.
∎

We remark that estimating the sensitivity of a tree via dynamic programming is a natural idea, and that similar ideas have been explored in the related (but distinct) context of multi-participation
[
46
]
.
While the time complexity is polynomial in
k
for constant
b
, it quickly becomes infeasible once larger
b
are considered.
Motivated by this, we tightly bound
F_{b}(h,k)
next.

Lemma 9.8
.

Let
b\geq 2,h\geq 1,1\leq k\leq b^{h}
and
\alpha_{k}=\lceil\log_{b}k\rceil
. Then

k(h-\alpha_{k}+1)\leq F_{b}(h,k)\leq k(h-\alpha_{k}+1)+\frac{b^{\alpha_{k}}-1}{b-1}\,,

with the upper bound monotonically increasing in
k
.

Proof.

By definition, we have that level
\ell^{*}=h-\alpha_{k}
is the highest level in the tree with
k
or more nodes.
This follows from
b^{h-(\ell^{*}-1)}=b^{\alpha_{k}-1}<k\leq b^{\alpha_{k}}=b^{h-\ell^{*}}
.
For the upper bound, observe that level
\ell
in the tree can have at most
\min(k,b^{h-\ell})
odd nodes, thus

\displaystyle F_{b}(h,k)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{h}\min(k,b^{h-\ell})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\ell^{*}}k+\sum_{\ell=\ell^{*}+1}^{h}b^{h-\ell}=k(h-\alpha_{k}+1)+\frac{b^{\alpha_{k}}-1}{b-1}\,,

where the monotonicity in
k
is immediate from the expression after the first inequality.
For the lower bound, we assign balls to leaves by adding at most one ball to each subtree with root on level
\ell^{*}
, earning a contribution of
k
from every level up to
\ell^{*}
:

F_{b}(h,k)\geq k(\ell^{*}+1)=k(h-\alpha_{k}+1)\,,

and we are done.
∎

Corollary 9.9
.

Let
b\geq 2,h\geq 1,1\leq k\leq b^{h}
and
\alpha_{k}=\lceil\log_{b}k\rceil
.
Then

\max\left(k(h-\alpha_{k}+1),b^{\alpha_{k}-1}(h-\alpha_{k}+2)\right)\leq\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})^{p}\leq k(h-\alpha_{k}+1)+\frac{b^{\alpha_{k}}-1}{b-1}\,.

Proof.

By
Lemma

9.6
, we have that
\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})^{p}=\max_{k^{\prime}\leq k}F_{b}(h,k^{\prime})
.
Invoking the upper bound in
Lemma

9.8
, we get

\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})^{p}\leq\max_{k^{\prime}\leq k}\left\{k^{\prime}(h-\alpha_{k^{\prime}}+1)+\frac{b^{\alpha_{k^{\prime}}}-1}{b-1}\right\}=k(h-\alpha_{k}+1)+\frac{b^{\alpha_{k}}-1}{b-1}\,,

where the last step follows from the monotonicity in
k^{\prime}
.
Analogously for the lower bound, we get

\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})^{p}\geq\max_{k^{\prime}\leq k}k^{\prime}(h-\alpha_{k^{\prime}}+1)=\max_{k^{\prime}\leq k}L(k^{\prime})

but in this case
L(k^{\prime})
is not monotonically increasing in
k^{\prime}
.
Nevertheless, it is monotonically increasing on each of the intervals
(b^{j-1},b^{j}]
for integer
j\geq 1
, where it attains the maximum
M_{j}=b^{j}(h-j+1)
.
It is easy to check that
M_{j}
is non-decreasing in
j
, e.g.,

\frac{M_{j+1}}{M_{j}}=b\cdot\frac{h-j}{h-j+1}\geq 2\cdot\frac{1}{2}\geq 1\,.

It follows that
L(k^{\prime})
is maximized either at
k^{\prime}=k
, or at
k^{\prime}=b^{\alpha_{k}-1}
, proving the statement.
∎

When
k=o(T)
the second term in the upper bound is of lower order, in which case we have shown that

\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R_{b}\mathcal{S}_{1,k})^{p}=\left(1+o(1)\right)\cdot k(h-\lceil\log_{b}k\rceil+1)\,,

which asymptotically behaves like
\Theta(k\log(T/k))
.

9.2.1
A Sensitivity Reduction Trick.

We note that it is possible to improve lower order terms further at no additional cost.
The key observation is that most tree-based factorizations in fact only use (roughly) a
1-1/b
fraction of the nodes in the tree, so the same fraction of the odd node counts in
F_{b}(h,k)
can be ignored in the sensitivity computation.
For example, the standard
b
-ary tree mechanism never uses the right-most child of any parent; the subtraction variant never uses the middle-child.
Alternatively phrased, a
1-1/b
fraction of columns in
\hat{L}_{b}
(and
L_{b}
) are identically zero, and so we can zero-out the corresponding rows in
R_{b}
, producing
\hat{R}_{b}
, to get a new factorization
\hat{L}_{b}\hat{R}_{b}=A
with lower sensitivity.
This optimization is unique to our setting, and does not yield any improvement for standard
\mathcal{S}_{1,1}
-continual counting.

In what follows we derive tight bounds for
\hat{R}_{b}
.
The analysis is near-identical to the preceding case of the full tree, with
Corollary

9.12
being the analog of
Corollary

9.9
.
Let
\hat{F}(h,k)
be the corresponding odd node count where only
b-1
out of
b
children can contribute.
For completeness, we give a matching dynamic program for
\hat{F}(h,k)
.

Lemma 9.10

(A second dynamic program)
.

Let
h\geq 1
,
k\leq b^{h}
and
w\in\{0,1\}
.
Define
F_{b}(h,k,w)
via

\displaystyle F_{b}(h,k,w)

\displaystyle=w\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{[k\,\mathrm{odd}]}+\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+\dots+k_{b}=k\\
\forall i:0\leq k_{i}\leq b^{h-1}\end{subarray}}\left\{F_{b}(k_{1},h-1,0)+\sum_{i=2}^{b}F_{b}(k_{i},h-1,1)\right\}\,,

\displaystyle\quad F_{b}(k,0,w)=w\cdot k\,.

Then for real
p\geq 1
,
\mathsf{sens}_{p}(\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})=\sqrt[p]{\max_{k^{\prime}\leq k}\hat{F}_{b}(h,k^{\prime})}=\sqrt[p]{\max_{k^{\prime}\leq k}F_{b}(k,h,1)}
.
Moreover,
F_{b}(k,h,1)
can be computed as a dynamic program with a table of size
O(k)
and in time
O(hk^{b-1})
.

Proof sketch.

Observe that the recurrence is similar to the one from
Lemma

9.7
, except that now there is an additional variable
w
.
The variable is set to 1 whenever the corresponding node contributes to the total odd node count, and is otherwise set to 0.
Exactly
b-1
out of the
b
children of any node are assigned
w=1
, and the
w\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{[k\,\mathrm{odd}]}
will only contribute for any node with
w=1
.
That
\mathsf{sens}_{p}(\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})^{p}=\max_{k^{\prime}\leq k}\hat{F}_{b}(h,k^{\prime})
follows from an analogous argument to
Lemma

9.6
:
\hat{R}_{b}\vec{\Delta}
can still only take on values in
\{0,\pm 1\}
, so maximizing the odd node count for the reduced tree implies maximizing the norm.

For the dynamic program implementation, note that the number of weakly (ordered) compositions of
k
into
b
parts is still an upper bound on the number of configurations to optimize over for a fix
k,b
.
Moreover, the introduction of the variable
w
blows up the table size by at most a factor 2.
It follows that the space and time usage is asymptotically no different from that of
Lemma

9.7
.
∎

Before formally bounding
\hat{F}(h,k)
, we first empirically showcase the improved sensitivity in
Figure

3
.

\ell_{2}
sensitivity.

:

\ell_{2}

and

.

and

.

directly plots the value of
F_{b}(h,k)
and
\hat{F}_{b}(h,k)
, and

R_{b}
and
\hat{R}_{b}
respectively.
The top green line in

is using the upper bound from
[

, Lemma B.1]
, translated to our setting.

Our treatment offers substantial an substantial improvement over the bound from
[
43
]
, but the benefit from using a
“reduced”
factorization is subtle.
Moreover, the reduction is expected to be greater for smaller values of
b
, as then the fraction of nodes in the tree (rows) that can be discarded from
R_{b}
(roughly
1/b
), is greater.
It turns out that this improvement, even for a binary tree, is of lower order unless
k=\Omega(T)
.
We show this next.

Lemma 9.11
.

Let
h\geq 1
,
b\geq 2,1\leq k\leq b^{h}
and
\alpha_{k}=\lceil\log_{b}k\rceil
, then

k(h-\alpha_{k}+1-1/b)\leq\hat{F}_{b}(h,k)\leq k(h-\alpha_{k}+1)+b^{\alpha_{k}-1}\,,

with the the upper bound monotonically increasing in
k
.

Proof.

For
k=1
, note that
\hat{F}_{b}(h,1)=h+1
satisfies both bounds.
The rest of the proof focuses on
k\geq 2
.
By our choice of
\alpha_{k}
, as in
Lemma

9.8
, we again have that level
\ell^{*}=h-\alpha_{k}
is the highest level in the tree with
k
or more nodes.
The restriction on
k
enforces that
\ell^{*}<h
.
For the upper bound, level
\ell<h
in the tree can have at most
\min(k,(b-1)b^{h-\ell-1})
odd nodes, thus

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{b}(k,h)

\displaystyle\leq 1\hskip-1.0pt+\hskip-1.0pt\sum_{\ell=0}^{h-1}\min(k,(b-1)b^{h-\ell-1})=1\hskip-1.0pt+\hskip-1.0pt\sum_{\ell=0}^{\ell^{*}}k+(b-1)\sum_{\ell=\ell^{*}+1}^{h-1}b^{h-\ell-1}=k(h-\alpha_{k}+1)\hskip-1.0pt+\hskip-1.0ptb^{\alpha_{k}-1}\,.

The monotonicity of the upper bound follows from its form after the first inequality.
For the lower bound, analogously to
Lemma

9.8
, we assign
k
balls to each subtree rooted on level
\ell^{*}
, and we choose each leaf such that we get a contribution of
k
from every level
<\ell^{*}
.
On level
\ell^{*}
, however, we get a contribution of
\min(k,(b-1)b^{\alpha_{k}-1})
since there might be fewer than
k
contributing nodes.
Thus,

\hat{F}_{b}(h,k)\geq k(h-\alpha_{k})+\min(k,(b-1)b^{\alpha_{k}-1})\geq k(h-\alpha_{k}+1-1/b)\,,

where the last step uses that
(b-1)b^{\alpha_{k}-1}\geq(1-1/b)k
.
∎

Corollary 9.12
.

Let
b\geq 3,h\geq 1,1\leq k\leq b^{h}
and
\alpha_{k}=\lceil\log_{b}k\rceil
.
Then

\max\left(k(h-\alpha_{k}+1-1/b),b^{\alpha_{k}-1}(h-\alpha_{k}+2-1/b)\right)\leq\mathsf{sens}_{p}(\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})^{p}\leq k(h-\alpha_{k}+1)+b^{\alpha_{k}-1}\,.

Proof.

From
Lemma

9.10
, we have that
\mathsf{sens}_{p}(\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})^{p}=\max_{k^{\prime}\leq k}\hat{F}_{b}(h,k^{\prime})
.
Invoking the upper bound in
Lemma

9.11
, we have that

\mathsf{sens}_{p}(\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})^{p}\leq\max_{k^{\prime}\leq k}\left\{k^{\prime}(h-\alpha_{k^{\prime}}+1)+b^{\alpha_{k^{\prime}}-1}\right\}=k(h-\alpha_{k}+1)+\frac{b^{\alpha_{k}}-1}{b-1}\,,

which follows from the maximization objective being monotonic in
k^{\prime}
.
Analogously for the lower bound, we get

\mathsf{sens}_{p}(R_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})^{p}\geq\max_{k^{\prime}\leq k}k^{\prime}(h-\alpha_{k^{\prime}}+1-1/b)=\max_{k^{\prime}\leq k}L(k^{\prime})

but in this case
L(k^{\prime})
is not monotonically increasing in
k^{\prime}
.
Nevertheless, it is monotonically increasing on each of the intervals
(b^{j-1},b^{j}]
for integer
1\leq j\leq h
, where it attains the maximum
M_{j}=b^{j}(h-j+1)
.
We directly verify that
M_{j}
is non-decreasing in
j
,

\frac{M_{j+1}}{M_{j}}=b\cdot\frac{h-j-1/b}{h-j+1-1/b}\geq b\cdot\frac{1-1/b}{2-1/b}\geq 3\cdot\frac{1-1/3}{2-1/3}=6/5\,,

where we have used that
b\geq 3
.
It follows that
L(k^{\prime})
is maximized either at
k^{\prime}=k
, or at
k^{\prime}=b^{\alpha_{k}-1}
, proving the statement.
∎

Note that the corollary does not support
b=2
(the upper bound does, however).

9.2.2
Bounds for General
T
.

Before proceeding, we also give bounds for the sensitivity in the case where
T
is not an integer power of
b
.
We let
h=\lceil\log_{b}T\rceil
, and drop the last
b^{h}-T
rows of
\hat{L}_{b}
such that
\hat{L}_{b}\hat{R}_{b}=A=\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
.
Note that this may lead to additional zero-columns, in
\hat{L}
which allows for zeroing-out more entries in
\hat{R}_{b}
—we will not leverage this here, but note that it can yield an additional (lower order) reduction for certain values of
k
and
T
.

Corollary 9.13
.

Let
3\leq b\leq T
,
1\leq k\leq T
,
h=\lceil\log_{b}T\rceil
and
\tilde{k}=\min(k,b^{h-1})
.
Then

\big[\tilde{k}(\lceil\log_{b}T\rceil-\lceil\log_{b}\tilde{k}\rceil-1/b)\big]^{1/p}\leq\mathsf{sens}_{p}(\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})\leq\big[k(\lceil\log_{b}(T/k)\rceil+2)\big]^{1/p}\,.

If additionally
k\leq T/b
, then we get the following simplified lower bound

\mathsf{sens}_{p}(\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})\geq\left[k(\lfloor\log_{b}(T/k)\rfloor-1/b)\right]^{1/p}\,.

Proof.

For the upper bound, note that the sensitivity cannot decrease if we consider the full tree over
b^{h}\geq T
leaves, and so we can immediately invoke the upper bound in
Corollary

9.5
.
Thus

\displaystyle\mathsf{sens}_{p}(\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})^{p}

\displaystyle\leq k(\lceil\log_{b}T\rceil-\lceil\log_{b}k\rceil+1)+b^{\lceil\log_{b}k\rceil-1}\leq k(\lceil\log_{b}(T/k)\rceil+2)

where the last inequality uses that
\lceil a\rceil-\lceil b\rceil\leq\lceil a-b\rceil
and
\lceil a\rceil\leq a+1
for arbitrary
a,b\geq 0
.
For the lower bound, we consider the full tree over
b^{h-1}<T
leaves and let its associated (reduced) matrix be
\hat{R}_{b}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times b^{h-1}}
for some
m\geq 1
.
Define
\tilde{k}=\min(k,b^{h-1})
.
Maximizing the norm over a smaller tree and over a restricted range of
k
necessarily yields a smaller norm, and so

\displaystyle\mathsf{sens}_{p}(\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})^{p}

\displaystyle\geq\mathsf{sens}_{p}(\hat{R}_{b}^{\prime},\mathcal{S}_{1,\hat{k}})\geq\tilde{k}(\lceil\log_{b}T\rceil-\lceil\log_{b}\tilde{k}\rceil-1/b)

where we invoke the lower bound in
Corollary

9.5
and chose the first entry in the maximization.
This lower bound is also strictly positive.
For the more restricted case where
k\leq T/b
, we get that
\tilde{k}=k
and our lower bound can be simplified further

\displaystyle\mathsf{sens}_{p}(\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})^{p}\geq k(\lceil\log_{b}T\rceil-\lceil\log_{b}k\rceil-1/b)\geq k(\lfloor\log_{b}(T/k)\rfloor-1/b)

where we have used
\lceil a\rceil-\lceil b\rceil\geq\lfloor a-b\rfloor
.
For this case we have
\log_{b}(T/k)\geq 1
, and so the lower bound is guaranteed to be a positive quantity.
Taking the
p
-th root over all (positive) bounds give the final statement.
∎

As long as
k\leq T/b
(a practical regime), we have shown
\mathsf{sens}_{p}(\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})^{p}=\Theta(k\log(T/k))
.
We will later see that for
k=\Omega(T)
, there is a better choice of factorization.

9.2.3
Extending to
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
.

We are now ready to give our final result for the
\ell_{p}
sensitivity of tree-based factorization over
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
.
For convenience, we only state it for the case where
k\leq T/b
and the lower bound has a (relatively) clean form.
We also assume
D\leq k
, since otherwise
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
collapses to the set of vectors with only bounded
\ell_{1}
norm.

Theorem 9.14
.

Let
3\leq b\leq T
and
1\leq D\leq k\leq T/b

Then

D\left[\left\lfloor\frac{k}{D}\right\rfloor\left(\left\lfloor\log_{b}\left(\frac{DT}{k}\right)\right\rfloor-\frac{1}{b}\right)\right]^{1/p}\leq\mathsf{sens}_{p}(\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{D,k})\leq D\left[\left\lceil\frac{k}{D}\right\rceil\left(\left\lceil\log_{b}\left(\frac{DT}{k}\right)\right\rceil+2\right)\right]^{1/p}\,.

Proof.

For the upper bound, we begin by arguing that the upper bound

\mathsf{sens}_{p}(\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,k})\leq U(k)=[k(\lceil\log_{b}(T/k)+2)]^{1/p}

in
Corollary

9.13
is a concave, non-decreasing function in
k
on
[1,T]
.
As
g(x)=x^{1/p}
for
p\geq 1
is concave and non-decreasing, it suffices to argue for
f(x)=x(\lceil\log_{b}(x/k)\rceil+2)
being concave to show concaveness for
U=g\circ f
.
We show this via the non-increasing forward-difference
\Delta f(x)=f(x+1)-f(x)
:

\displaystyle\Delta f(x)

\displaystyle\leq(x+1)\left(\left\lceil\log_{b}\left(T/x\right)\right\rceil+2\right)-x\left(\left\lceil\log_{b}\left(T/x\right)\right\rceil\ +2\right)\leq\lceil\log_{b}T/x\rceil+2\,,

\displaystyle\Delta f(x)

\displaystyle\geq(x+1)\left(\left\lceil\log_{b}\left(\frac{T}{x+1}\right)\right\rceil+2\right)-x\left(\left\lceil\log_{b}\left(\frac{T}{x+1}\right)\right\rceil+2\right)=\left\lceil\log_{b}\left(\frac{T}{x+1}\right)\right\rceil+2\,,

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}f(x)

\displaystyle=\Delta f(x+1)-\Delta f(x)\leq\left\lceil\log_{b}\left(\frac{T}{x+1}\right)\right\rceil+2-\left(\left\lceil\log_{b}\left(\frac{T}{x+1}\right)\right\rceil+2\right)=0\,.

We can thus invoke
Lemma

7.3
, yielding

\displaystyle\mathsf{sens}_{p}(\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{D,k})

\displaystyle\leq D\cdot U(\lceil k/D\rceil)\leq D\left[\left\lceil\frac{k}{D}\right\rceil\left(\left\lceil\log_{b}\left(\frac{T}{\lceil k/D\rceil}\right)\right\rceil+2\right)\right]^{1/p}\,,

where the theorem statement is the result after applying
T/\lceil k/D\rceil\leq DT/k
.
For the lower bound, we immediately apply
Lemma

7.1
, yielding

\displaystyle\mathsf{sens}_{p}(\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{D,k})

\displaystyle\geq D\cdot\mathsf{sens}_{p}(\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{1,\lfloor k/D\rfloor})\geq D\left[\left\lfloor\frac{k}{D}\right\rfloor\left(\left\lfloor\log_{b}\left(\frac{T}{\lfloor k/D\rfloor}\right)\right\rfloor-1/b\right)\right]^{1/p}\,,

where similarly the theorem statement comes from
T/\lfloor k/D\rfloor\geq DT/k
.
∎

A few remarks can be made about
Theorem

9.14
.
First, note that the bounds are
tight
for the
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
sensitivities:

\displaystyle\mathsf{sens}_{1}(\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{D,k})

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(k\log\left(DT/k\right)\right)\,,

\displaystyle\mathsf{sens}_{2}(\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{D,k})

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(\sqrt{Dk\log\left(DT/k\right)}\right)\,.

They also strictly generalize
Corollary

9.13
, in the sense that they recover the bounds for
D=1
.

9.3
Error Bounds

Having finished our treatment of sensitivity, we are finally ready to derive our error guarantees.
We give bounds for both
\rho
-zCDP and
\varepsilon
-DP.
We settle for stating bounds for the case of
b
-ary trees with subtraction as it improves leading constants over using only addition.
Note that the conditions on
k/D
can be removed at the cost of a messier statement.

Theorem 9.15

(Trees for
\rho
-zCDP)
.

Let
b\geq 3
be odd,
T\geq b^{2b}
,
1\leq D\leq k\leq T/b
, and
\rho>0
.
Additionally assume
k/D=o(T)
and
k/D=\omega(1)
.
Then the
\rho
-zCDP
b
-ary tree mechanism with subtraction for counting on
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
-streams producing
T
outputs achieves the following asymptotic error bounds:

\displaystyle\mathsf{MaxSE}(\hat{L}_{b},\hat{R}_{b},\mathcal{S}_{D,k})

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(\sqrt{Dk\log(DT/k)\log(T)/\rho}\right)\,,

10
A Naive Factorization

Given that tree-based factorization can yield improved bounds for large
k
, a natural question is if tree-based factorization is the correct construction in this regime.
We show that the answer is subtle: for large enough
k
, a naive factorization
A=I\times A
outperforms it.

Lemma 10.1
.

Let
1\leq D\leq k\leq T
,
p\geq 1
and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
be the lower-triangular matrix of all-ones.
Then

\mathsf{sens}_{p}(A,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})=D\cdot T^{1/p}\,.

Proof.

Note that for any
\vec{\Delta}\in\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
, we have that
A\vec{\Delta}\in[-D,D]^{T}
by definition.
As the vector
\vec{\Delta}^{\prime}
with
\vec{\Delta}^{\prime}[0]=D
and zero in every other entry always exists in
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
, we immediately get
\mathsf{sens}_{p}(A,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})=\|A\vec{\Delta}^{\prime}\|_{p}=DT^{1/p}
.
∎

Theorem 10.2
.

For
1\leq D\leq k\leq T
, and
\rho>0
, consider the
\rho
-zCDP factorization mechanism for counting on
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
-streams with
L=I
and
R=A
.
It achieves error

\mathsf{MaxSE}(L,R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})=\mathsf{MeanSE}(L,R,\mathcal{S}_{D,k})=\frac{D\sqrt{T}}{\sqrt{2\rho}}\,.

Analogously, given
\varepsilon>0
, the
\varepsilon
-DP factorization mechanism (releasing
R\vec{\mathbf{x}}
with the Laplace mechanism), attains the following error:

\mathsf{MaxSE}(L,R,\mathcal{S}_{k,D})=\mathsf{MeanSE}(L,R,\mathcal{S}_{k,D})=\frac{\sqrt{2}DT}{\varepsilon}\,,

O(T)
and
O(1)

Proof.

We trivially have that
\|I\|_{2\to\infty}=\|I\|_{F}/\sqrt{T}=1
, so the error statement reduces to invoking
Lemma

10.1
for the
\ell_{2}
and
\ell_{1}
sensitivity.
For the statement on time and space usage: the mechanism adds fresh, independent noise at each step, which can be done in constant time per step, and does not require storing noise across time steps.
∎

Focusing on the
\rho

An error of

11
Analytical Comparison of Bounds

Here, we analytically compare the bounds in our paper and discuss the results. We focus on the results with respect to the sensitivity vector set
\mathcal{S}_{1,k}
(corresponding to the results for
\mathsf{DegreeCount}
and
\mathsf{CountDistinct}
).

In this analytical comparison, we will use the dominating term in each of the upper bounds corresponding to the square root factorization and the tree-based mechanism we describe in this paper, and the exact bound for the naive mechanism that adds Gaussian noise at each time step and pays for composition. As in the introduction, we will consider
\rho=1/2
for our comparison (since the dependence on
\rho
is identical in all our bounds, it does not affect our comparison).

The corresponding bounds are
c_{1}\sqrt{k}\log T
(for the square root factorization mechanism), where
c_{1}
is a constant that can be derived from the upper bound in
Corollary

8.1
,
c_{2}\sqrt{k\log(T/k)\log T}
(for tree-based factorization mechanisms) where
c_{2}
can be derived from the bounds in
Theorem

9.15
for the optimal choice of
b
, and
\sqrt{T}
which is the exact bound for the naive mechanism (see
Theorem

10.2
). Note that
c_{2}>c_{1}
.

Comparing these bounds, we can make the following conclusions. Note that the square-root mechanism dominates over the naive bound when
k\leq\frac{T}{c_{1}^{2}\log^{2}T}
. Similarly, the upper bound for the square root factorization dominates over the upper bound for the tree-based mechanism when
k\leq T^{1-(c_{1}/c_{2})^{2}}
and otherwise the upper bound for the tree-based mechanism dominates. Finally, observe that the upper bound for the tree-based mechanism dominates over the bound for the naive mechanism as long as
k\log(T/k)\leq\frac{T}{c_{2}^{2}\log T}
. Let the value of
k
at which equality is achieved be
k^{*}
.

Note that these bounds imply that for
k\in[1,T^{1-(c_{1}/c_{2})^{2}}]
, the upper bound based on the square root factorization dominates, for
k\in[T^{1-(c_{1}/c_{2})^{2}},k^{*}]
(where
k^{*}>\frac{T}{\log^{2}T}
), we have that the upper bound based on the tree-based mechanism dominates, and for
k\in[k^{*},T]
, the bound based on the naive composition mechanism dominates. We expect
k
to be much smaller than
T
in most practical applications, and hence our bounds give improvements for most practically significant values of
k
. We leave an exact characterization of the optimal bounds for different
k
to future work.

12
Conclusion

Our work gives improved error bounds for a range of cardinality estimation problems. We do so by recasting private cardinality estimation on fully dynamic streams as private linear queries over their difference streams and studying the associated sensitivity vector sets in detail. There are many possible directions for future work.

Triangle Counting with Privacy for All Graphs.

As already mentioned, the privacy afforded by reducing triangle counting to continual counting on the difference stream is conditioned on the graph stream having degree bounded by
D
and maximum triangle contribution bounded by
k_{\mathrm{tri}}
. Coming up with an algorithm that guarantees privacy for all graphs, but continues to give accuracy matching our approach for streams satisfying the parameter constraints, is a natural open question.

Other Linear Queries.

While this paper exclusively deals with continual counting applied to difference streams, alternative linear queries on these streams might be interesting to explore. For example, instead of computing the exact number of distinct elements, one might be interested in just knowing whether the number of distinct elements
recently has been increasing or decreasing
.
Such a statistic could naturally be derived from postprocessing the running count, but it might be more efficient to directly adapt the query matrix to the statistic in mind. Studying such query matrices via our framework is a natural direction for future work.

Space Efficiency.

There is evidence that the square-root factorization we employ does not admit any sublinear space algorithm
[
5
]
.
Recent work
[
21
]
introduced a Toeplitz factorization approximating its error up to an additive constant in space
O(\log^{2}T)
, but their construction does not enforce a monotonicity condition on the diagonals of the right factor.
Designing a space-efficient Toeplitz factorization with such a monotonicity constraint and near-optimal error for standard continual counting remains an open question, and would give error bounds for continual counting on
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
-streams via the results in
Section

8
.

Lower Bounds.
Finally we raise the question of establishing general lower bounds for continual counting on
\mathcal{S}_{D,k}
-streams, and for factorization mechanisms in particular.
We already know that for
\mathcal{S}_{1,1}
, the square-root factorization is leading constant-optimal for our error metrics
[
52
,
30
,
38
]
, but it is clear that it is not optimal for
\mathcal{S}_{1,T}
(see
Theorem

10.2
).
As such, different factorizations are expected to be optimal for different parameter regimes.
