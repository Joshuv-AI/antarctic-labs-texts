Clinical associations during the year before Parkinson’s disease diagnosis in Taiwan: a nationwide claims-based case-control study
Authors: Kai-Jung Lin, Kai-Lieh Lin, Chia-Wei Liou, Chia-Liang Tsai, Yung-Yee Chang, Min-Yu Lan, Ying-Fa Chen, Chien-Ning Hsu, Tsu-Kung Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCharacterizing clinical associations before Parkinson’s disease (PD) diagnosis may improve understanding of the diagnostic window in real-world care. Using nationwide claims data from Taiwan, clinical patterns and medication exposures recorded before incident PD were examined, and secondary neurological outcomes and all-cause mortality were evaluated during follow-up.MethodsA retrospective, population-based case-control study was conducted using Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database (2000–2016). Incident PD cases were matched 1:4 to controls using a two-stage approach: frequency matching on birth year and sex, followed by propensity score matching for healthcare utilization and selected comorbidities. Associations between prespecified comorbidities, medication exposures, and incident PD were estimated using multivariable logistic regression, while Kaplan–Meier methods and Cox proportional hazards models assessed secondary neurological outcomes and all-cause mortality.ResultsA total of 2124 patients with PD and 8496 matched controls were included. Within the one-year pre-diagnostic window, incident PD was significantly associated with head injury (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 2.16), ischemic stroke (aOR 2.90), hepatitis C virus infection (aOR 1.40), hospitalization for bacterial infection (aOR 1.43), chronic exposure to propranolol (aOR 1.72), and proton pump inhibitors (aOR 1.24), whereas hyperlipidemia showed an inverse association (aOR 0.67). During follow-up, PD patients had higher adjusted hazards for secondary neurological outcomes (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 2.24; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.94–2.58) and all-cause mortality (aHR 1.72; 95% CI 1.57–1.89) than controls.ConclusionsThese findings highlight priority variables for understanding the complex clinical landscape preceding a formal PD diagnosis.Keywords: Parkinson’s disease, gut-brain axis, pharmacoepidemiology, real-world evidence, clinical association

Background

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a chronic, progressive neurodegenerative disorder that poses a growing challenge to global healthcare systems. In Taiwan, incidence has increased alongside population aging, with prior estimates ranging from 28.8 to 35.3 per 100,000 between 2005 and 2011 [1]. Neuropathological hallmarks include selective loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta and accumulation of Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites [2]. Although age is the most consistent risk factor [3], PD etiology is multifactorial, involving genetic susceptibility, environmental exposures, and systemic metabolic and inflammatory stressors.

Clinically, PD is characterized by motor manifestations, including tremor, bradykinesia, rigidity, and gait impairment, together with non-motor symptoms such as autonomic dysfunction, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and cognitive decline. Despite therapeutic advances, effective disease-modifying treatment remains limited [4]. Understanding the clinical associations and medication exposures preceding PD diagnosis may therefore help define the diagnostic window in real-world care. This is especially relevant because PD remains a clinical diagnosis based on longitudinal history and neurological examination, and diagnostic certainty may be limited early in the disease course [5,6].

The study variables were selected a priori based on prior PD literature on prodromal features, associated comorbidities, and potential risk factors [7,8]. Because longitudinal evidence in Asian populations remains comparatively sparse, this study aimed to examine these factors in the year preceding incident PD diagnosis and assessed subsequent risks of a composite neurological outcome and all-cause mortality versus propensity score-matched controls.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective, population-based case-control study utilized Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Database (NHIRD). The study dataset was derived from a nationally representative sampling dataset within the NHIRD [9,10]. This dataset contains the healthcare records of 2 million individuals, selected from the entire insured population through stratified random sampling based on age, sex, and region. Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee approval was obtained from Chang Gung Medical Foundation, Taipei, Taiwan (IRB number: 201800330B0). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Written informed consent was waived due to the use of anonymized secondary claims data.

Case identification

Adults aged 18 years or older with a new diagnosis of PD between January 1, 2000, and December 31, 2016, were identified (Figure 1). PD was defined using the International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) code 332.0. Diagnostic specificity was enhanced by requiring either ≥1 inpatient discharge diagnosis or ≥2 outpatient diagnoses made by neurology specialists at separate visits within 1 year (Supplementary Table S1). The index date was defined as the first qualifying PD diagnosis. To reduce reverse causation, individuals were excluded if dementia, other CNS and related neurological diagnoses, psychotic disorders, or the use of anti-parkinsonian medications (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical [ATC] code N04) had occurred within 1 year prior to the index date.

Flowchart of study cohort selection from the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) of Taiwan (2000–2016). The diagram illustrates the identification of incident Parkinson’s disease (PD) cases, specific exclusion criteria (e.g. prior dementia or anti-Parkinson medication use), and the 1:4 propensity score matching process used to select the non-PD control group.

Control selection

A two-step matching strategy was applied (Figure 1). First, controls without PD were frequency-matched to cases (patients with incident PD) by birth year and sex to improve comparability in basic demographic characteristics [11,12]. Identical exclusion criteria were applied to controls. Each control was assigned the index date of the matched case. Second, 1:4 greedy propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using the SAS GMATCH macro [13]. Propensity score matching was then employed to balance both groups based on age, sex, healthcare utilization (outpatient visits and hospitalizations), and baseline cardiometabolic status (including diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia). Notably, the specific clinical associations under investigation were excluded from the matching algorithm to preserve their evaluative integrity.

Clinical association assessment

All comorbidities and medication exposures were assessed within the 365 days preceding the index date. The specific comorbidities evaluated included hepatitis B virus (HBV) and C virus (HCV) infections, tuberculosis, bacterial infections requiring hospitalization, cirrhosis, cerebrovascular diseases, epilepsy, head injury/traumatic brain injury (TBI), ischemic heart disease, asthma/COPD, diabetes mellitus (DM), and hyperlipidemia (Supplementary Table S1). Additionally, the baseline comorbidity burden was quantified using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) (Supplementary Table S2) [14].

Medication exposures were defined as dispensing for ≥90 cumulative days within 365 days prior to index date: selective beta-2-adrenoreceptor agonists, non-selective beta-blockers (propranolol), proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), glucose-lowering therapies (metformin-based and sulfonylurea-based), and lipid-lowering agents (Supplementary Table S1). For Helicobacter pylori (HP) eradication therapy, exposure was defined as dispensing 7–14 days; three- or four combined regimens within 365 days prior to the index date [15].

Outcomes

Follow-up was defined as the period from the index date until the first occurrence of the composite neurological outcome, death, withdrawal from the National Health Insurance program, or December 31, 2016 (Supplementary Figure S1). The secondary neurological composite outcome included dementia, psychotic disorders, and various other CNS and related neurological diagnoses such as cerebral degenerations, other extrapyramidal disease and abnormal movement disorders, spinocerebellar disease, multiple sclerosis, hemiplegia and hemiparesis, epilepsy, and migraine (Supplementary Table S1). All-cause mortality was ascertained using the National Register of Death database.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons used Chi-square test for categorical data and the t-test for continuous data. Multivariable logistic regression was employed to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for incident PD. To mitigate multicollinearity, conceptually overlapping variables were not entered simultaneously into the same model [16]. Because analyses were conducted in the propensity score-matched cohort, aORs and 95% CIs reflect associations within the matched analytic sample. Cumulative event rates for secondary follow-up outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and Cox proportional hazards models were used to determine adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) for the secondary neurological composite outcome and all-cause mortality. The proportional hazards assumption was verified through visual assessment of plots. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Characteristics of the study cohort

The final cohort included 2124 incident PD patients and 8496 propensity score-matched controls. Mean age at index date was 72.86 ± 11.76 years, and 55.6% were male. As shown in Table 1, the PD cohort had a higher comorbidity burden than controls (CCI score: 1.33 ± 1.64 vs. 1.06 ± 1.58, p < 0.0001). Medication exposures in the year prior to index date were also more frequent among PD patients.

Factors associated with incident PD

Multivariable logistic regression identified factors associated with incident PD among variables recorded during the 365-day pre-index window (Table 2). Increased odds were observed for hospitalization due to bacterial infection (aOR 1.43; 95% CI 1.19–1.73) and HCV infection (aOR 1.40; 95% CI 1.02–1.91). Increased odds were also observed for ischemic heart disease (aOR 1.20; 95% CI 1.07–1.35), ischemic stroke (aOR 2.90; 95% CI 2.47–3.41), other non-hemorrhagic strokes (aOR 2.09; 95% CI 1.74–2.54), TBI (aOR 1.34; 95% CI 1.04–1.72), and head injury (aOR 2.16; 95% CI 1.22–3.81). Chronic medication exposure was associated with increased PD odds for propranolol (aOR 1.72; 95% CI 1.40–2.12) and PPI (aOR 1.24; 95% CI 1.08–1.43). Hyperlipidemia (with or without treatment) was inversely associated with PD (aOR 0.67; 95% CI 0.52–0.88). The principal associations observed in the year before incident PD diagnosis are summarized in Figure 2.

Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrated higher cumulative risks of dementia, psychotic disorders, and other CNS and related neurological diagnoses among PD patients (log-rank test, p < 0.05) (Supplementary Figure S2; and Supplementary Table S3). Discussion

In this nationwide case-control study using Taiwan’s NHIRD, we evaluated clinical associations between incident PD and selected comorbidities and medication exposures recorded during the year before diagnosis. We identified positive associations for severe infections (hospitalization for bacterial infection and HCV infection), cerebrovascular and cardiovascular diseases, traumatic brain injury, and selected medication exposures, including propranolol and PPIs, while hyperlipidemia was inversely associated. Because the exposure assessment was restricted to the 365 days immediately preceding the index diagnosis and no additional lag period was applied, these findings are more informative for characterizing the clinical context surrounding diagnosis within the diagnostic window than for drawing conclusions about long-term etiologic risk.

Infection, inflammation, and the gut-brain axis

The dual-hit hypothesis proposes that PD pathology may originate in the enteric nervous system and propagate centrally via the vagus nerve. The observed association between antecedent severe bacterial infections and incident PD is consistent with a potential role of systemic inflammatory signaling. Severe infections may trigger peripheral inflammation, increase blood–brain barrier permeability, and promote microglial activation [17]. However, claims-based analyses cannot distinguish causality from markers of vulnerability (e.g. frailty) or increased healthcare contact during prodromal PD.

Prior literature has suggested links between Helicobacter pylori (HP) and PD [18–20], yet a significant association with peptic ulcer disease was not observed in this study. In contrast, PPIs exposure was associated with increased odds of PD. This finding should be interpreted with caution due to potential protopathic bias: gastrointestinal dysfunction frequently occurs during the prodromal phase of PD and may prompt acid-suppressive therapy. While long-term acid suppression is hypothesized to alter gastric pH and gut microbial composition, any mechanistic relevance to PD remains uncertain [21]. Consequently, these findings regarding PPIs should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing.

The significant association with HCV infection aligns with regional epidemiological patterns characterizing the higher endemicity of viral hepatitis in Taiwan relative to Western populations [22,23]. Pathophysiological mechanisms involving insulin resistance and cytokine-mediated neuroinflammation suggest a potential role for hepatotropic viruses in neurodegenerative pathways within this region [24]. Nevertheless, based on the current level of evidence, recommending systematic screening for prodromal PD symptoms among HCV-infected patients would be premature [23].

The metabolic paradox: hyperlipidemia and potential neuroprotection

Hyperlipidemia was inversely associated with incident PD, aligns with Western reports of a ‘lipid paradox’, which is consistent across different ethnic and dietary backgrounds [25]. Proposed mechanisms include links between cholesterol metabolism and mitochondrial antioxidant systems (e.g. coenzyme Q10 via the mevalonate pathway) [26] and the role of cholesterol in neuronal membrane integrity and lipid rafts that influence synaptic function and α-synuclein aggregation [27].

Nevertheless, residual confounding and reverse causation remain plausible. Statin exposure patterns and metabolic changes during prodromal PD (including weight loss and declining lipid levels) may all contribute to the observed association.

Vascular pathology and traumatic head injury

Notably, the high magnitude of association with ischemic stroke (aOR 2.90) suggests that vascular pathology may play a more prominent role in the clinical recognition and diagnostic window of PD in Asian populations than previously characterized in Western cohorts [28]. This observation is consistent with hypothesis that vascular insults may lower the threshold for clinical expression of parkinsonian syndromes [28]. Traumatic/head injury was significantly associated with incident PD in the present study. This finding is consistent with prior literature identifying TBI as a potential risk factor for PD [29]. Mechanisms such as neuroinflammation, axonal injury, and accelerated neurodegeneration have been implicated [29]. However, because exposure assessment was limited to the 1-year pre-index window and no additional lag analysis was performed, reverse causation cannot be excluded. Evolving parkinsonism, gait instability, or other injury-prone prodromal features may have increased the likelihood of injury prior to the formal PD diagnosis.

The case of propranolol

Propranolol exposure was associated with higher odds of incident PD. The observed temporal association is directionally consistent with parts of the prior experimental and epidemiologic studies have suggested that β2-adrenoreceptor signaling may influence α-synuclein biology [7,30]. However, propranolol is also commonly prescribed for tremor and anxiety, which may represent prodromal manifestations and introduce confounding by indication. Future studies specifically designed to address this source of bias are therefore warranted.

Diabetes mellitus and risk heterogeneity

Although type 2 diabetes mellitus has been implicated as a PD risk factor in some studies [31], a significant independent association was not observed. This may reflect balancing of cardiometabolic factors through matching and propensity score methods or heterogeneity by ethnicity, disease duration, and comorbidity patterns across studies [32].

Secondary follow-up outcomes

The higher risks of composite neurological outcomes and all-cause mortality provide supportive clinical coherence for the claims-based identification of incident PD. These secondary analyses were intended to show that individuals identified through the pre-diagnostic claims algorithm subsequently followed a clinically plausible neurodegenerative trajectory. The dementia burden observed during follow-up (incidence rate ratio, 1.715) also provides an Asian population–based reference for comparison with Western cohorts [6]. Because PD progression is clinically heterogeneous, differences in vascular comorbidity profiles may influence downstream neurological trajectories across populations. The high prevalence of cerebrovascular disease in our cohort may therefore be relevant when interpreting the post-diagnostic neurological burden.

Clinical and public health implications

This nationwide study characterizes clinical clusters observed preceding PD diagnosis, delineating the real-world context of routine care rather than establishing definitive causality. The observed associations—including infections, head injury, cerebrovascular disease, and selected medication exposures—likely reflect a complex interplay of prodromal manifestations, symptomatic treatment, comorbidity burden, and healthcare contact within the 1-year diagnosis window. These findings provide a pragmatic basis for hypothesis generation, highlighting priority variables for future studies using longer exposure windows, external comparator populations, and formal lag analyses to distinguish proximate correlates from robust etiologic factors. Notably, the inverse association for hyperlipidemia should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and does not warrant modification of current lipid-lowering practices.

Strengths and limitations

The present study has several important strengths. Its nationwide, population-based design provides evidence from an Asian healthcare system, where large-scale longitudinal PD data are comparatively limited, and enhances generalizability within Taiwan while reducing referral bias. A restrictive claims-based algorithm was used to improve PD case ascertainment, and the two-stage matching strategy helped reduce baseline imbalance in demographic characteristics, healthcare utilization, and selected cardiometabolic factors. In addition, the prespecified profiling of comorbidities and medication exposures allowed us to capture a broad range of infectious, vascular, traumatic, metabolic, and pharmacoepidemiologic signals relevant to the period immediately preceding PD diagnosis. Taken together, these features strengthen the study’s value for describing claims-based associations around the diagnostic window in a large real-world population.

Several limitations should nevertheless be considered when interpreting the findings. First, although the case algorithm was designed with input from PD specialists to approximate specialist-confirmed clinical diagnosis as closely as possible within the constraints of NHIRD data, PD remains inherently a clinical diagnosis that often requires longitudinal assessment, exclusion of mimicking conditions, and evaluation of treatment response [5,6,8]. Therefore, the claims-based case definition used in this study should be interpreted as an operational ascertainment approach rather than a definitive clinical diagnosis, and misclassification of both typical PD and atypical or secondary parkinsonism remains possible. Second, exposures and covariates were assessed within the year before the index date. This 1-year prediagnostic window should be interpreted as a proximal diagnostic-window assessment, not as evidence that exposures preceded PD pathogenesis by a sufficient etiologic interval. Because no longer lag analysis was performed, some observed associations—particularly for recent injuries and medication exposures—may partly reflect reverse causation, protopathic treatment, or diagnostic work-up close to the time of diagnosis rather than temporally robust causal risk factors. Third, important confounders were unavailable, including smoking, caffeine intake, pesticide exposure, family history, disease severity, and broader medication classes such as other beta-blockers.

Conclusions

This population-based study identifies a distinct pattern of clinical associations in the year prior to PD diagnosis in Taiwan. Severe infections, HCV, traumatic head injury, cerebrovascular disease, and exposure to propranolol or PPIs were significantly more frequent, while hyperlipidemia showed an inverse association. These signals highlight the clinical complexity of the pre-diagnostic period. Future research should prioritize longer look-back periods, formal lag analyses, and cross-population comparisons to clarify temporal sequences, mechanisms, and potential relevance to early recognition.
