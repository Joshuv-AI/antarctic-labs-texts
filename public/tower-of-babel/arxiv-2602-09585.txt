Introduction

The study of higher order line graphs forms a rich and classical area of graph theory with deep connections to structure theory, forbidden subgraph characterization, and extremal graph properties. For a simple connected graph
G
, its
line graph

L(G)
is defined as the graph whose vertices correspond to the edges of
G
, with two vertices adjacent in
L(G)
if and only if their corresponding edges in
G
share a common endpoint. The
r
-th order line graph of
G
, denoted as
L^{r}(G)
, is defined inductively as
L^{0}(G)=G
and
L^{r}(G)=L(L^{r-1}(G))
for
r\geqslant 1
.

Higher order line graphs have been investigated from multiple viewpoints—combinatorial, algorithmic, and structural. Early foundational work by Hemminger and Beineke
[
6
]
characterized all line graphs in terms of forbidden induced subgraphs, leading to the celebrated list of nine minimal forbidden configurations for the class of line graphs
[
1
]
. Subsequent studies refined these results and examined their implications for higher order line graph constructions and their structural invariants.

It is clear from the definition of line graphs and higher order line graphs, that it is possible that for a graph
G
on
n
vertices, the
r
-th order line graph
L^{r}(G)
has as many as
2^{\mathcal{O}(k^{2})}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
vertices (for example the complete graph on
n
vertices). Thus, it is interesting to see the evolution of efficiently checkable graph properties in the infinite sequence of graphs
\{G,L(G),L^{2}(G),\ldots,L^{i}(G),\ldots\}
created by iterated applications of the line graph operation on a graph
G
. In this paper, we are particularly interested in two polynomial-time checkable graph properties: (i) existence of an Euler path in a graph, (ii) calculating the maximum degree of a graph. We wish to analyze the two questions with respect to the number
n
of vertices in the initial graph
G
.

1.1
Previous Results

Beineke
[
1
]
proved that a graph
G
is a line graph if and only if it contains none of nine specific graphs as induced subgraphs, which includes the
claw
as one of the forbidden induced subgraphs. The
claw
(
K_{1,3}
) is the graph of four vertices, say
a,b,c,d
, and three edges
ab,ac,ad
. Šoltés
[
10
]
later reduced this forbidden list to seven graphs, providing a more succinct formulation of the same characterization. Hemminger and Beineke
[
6
]
showed that for any subgraph
H
of
G
, the corresponding
L(H)
is an induced subgraph of
L(G)
, which provides an inductive mechanism for understanding the structure of higher order line graphs. The recent work by Sanghi et. al
[
9
]
extends these ideas to higher order iterations, providing a forbidden subgraph-based framework for identifying when a graph
G
can be expressed as
L^{k}(H)
for some
H
. They introduce the concept of a
pure induced line subgraph
, a subgraph
G^{\prime}
of a line graph
G=L(H)
satisfying
G^{\prime}=L(H^{\prime})
for an induced subgraph
H^{\prime}
of
H
. This characterization was further generalized:
G=L^{n}(H)
,
n\geqslant 2
for some
H
, if
L^{-i}(G)=L^{n-i}(H)
is claw-free (i.e. does not contain a claw as an induced subgraph) for all
i
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant n-1
and
G
avoids a prescribed finite family of forbidden induced subgraphs.

An Euler circuit of a graph is a walk in the graph starting and ending at the same vertex, and traversing every edge of the graph exactly once. A graph is said to be Eulerian if it has an Euler circuit. Prisner
[
8
]
studied whether higher order line graphs are Eulerian. The work analyzed the values of
k
such that
L^{k}(G)
is Eulerian. He established that:

Proposition 1

(Prisner
[
8
]
)
.

A connected graph
G
has some iterated line graph
L^{t}(G)
that is Eulerian if and only if
G
is a path, or all its vertices have degrees of the same parity, or
G
is bipartite with every edge joining vertices of opposite parity.

This work also showed that if such a
t
exists, then
L^{r}(G)
is also Eulerian, for all
r\geqslant t
.

A Hamiltonian cycle of a graph
G
is a simple cycle containing all vertices of
G
. A graph is said to be Hamiltonian if it has a Hamiltonian cycle. It is known that higher order line graphs eventually become Hamiltonian
[
4
]
.

Proposition 2
.

For a connected graph
G
of
n
vertices which is not isomorphic to a path,
L^{k}(G)
is Hamiltonian for all
k\geqslant n-3
.

Parallel to these structural developments, Hartke and Higgins
[
5
]
analyzed the asymptotic behavior of
\Delta(L^{k}(G))
(where
\Delta(H)
denotes the maximum degree of the graph
H
), defining the
Maximum Degree Growth Property (MDGP)
:

Definition 3

(Hartke and Higgins
[
5
]
)
.

A graph
G
is said to satisfy Maximum Degree Growth Property (MDGP) if
\Delta(L(G))=2\Delta(G)-2
.

They show that for every connected graph
G
which is not a path, there exists a finite integer
K
(the
MDGP Index
) such that MDGP holds for all
L^{k}(G)
with
k\geqslant K
, and provided the recurrence

\Delta(L^{k}(G))=(\Delta(L^{K}(G))-2)\,2^{k-K}+2
(see Definition
27
).
This result implies that the maximum degree of iterated line graphs stabilizes into a predictable exponential growth regime after finitely many iterations.

1.2
Our Contributions

In this paper, we first extend the results of Prisner
[
8
]
on Euler circuits to Euler paths. An Euler path is a walk that starts and ends at two distinct vertices of the given graph, and traverses each edge exactly once. The tools required to characterise the existence of an Euler path in a higher order line graph
L^{k}(G),k\geqslant 1
are very different from those used in
[
8
]
. We show that for a graph
G
with
n
vertices and
m
edges the largest
k

where
L^{k}(G)
has an Euler path satisfies
k=\mathcal{O}(nm)
. We also design an
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}m)
-time algorithm to
output all
k
such that
L^{k}(G)
has an Euler path. Our results rely on the analysis of local relations such as the characterisation of the initial graph
G
when one of
L(G)
or
L^{2}(G)
does not have any Euler path. Note that our results are in contrast to those derived in
[
8
]
, as we show that eventually higher order line graphs will not have Euler paths.

The second question we address in this paper extends the work of Hartke and Higgins
[
5
]
. We study the growth of maximum degree of
L^{k}(G)
,
k\geqslant 0
, for a graph
G
. It is easy to calculate
\Delta(L^{k}(G))
when
G
is a path, cycle or a claw. Any other connected graph is called a
prolific graph

[
2
]
and we denote the set of all prolific graphs by
\mathcal{G}
. We show that for any prolific graph
G
, there exists a constant rational number
\mathsf{dgc}(G)
and an integer

such that for all
k\geqslant k_{0}
,
\Delta(L^{k}(G))=\mathsf{dgc}(G)\cdot 2^{k-4}+2
. We show that
\{\mathsf{dgc}(G)\mid G\in\mathcal{G}\}
has first, second, third, fourth, and fifth minimums, namely,
c_{1}=3
,
c_{2}=4
,
c_{3}=5.5
,
c_{4}=6
and
c_{5}=7
; the third minimum stands out surprisingly from the other four. Moreover, for
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
, we provide a complete characterization of
\mathcal{G}_{i}=\{\mathsf{dgc}(G)=c_{i}\mid G\in\mathcal{G}\}
. Apart from this, we show that the set
\{\mathsf{dgc}(G)\mid G\in\mathcal{G},7<\mathsf{dgc}(G)<8\}
is countably infinite. This landscape is depicted in Figure

.

3
Eulerian Path on Higher Order Line Graphs

For a connected graph
G
, recall that an Euler path (also known as an Eulerian path or an Eulerian trail) is a walk that starts and ends at distinct vertices, and traverses every edge of the graph exactly once. Note that we do not consider Euler circuits as Euler paths. A well known necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of Euler paths is stated below
[
7
]
.

Proposition 7
.

A connected graph has an Euler path if and only if exactly two of its vertices have an odd degree.

Deciding whether
L^{k}(G)
has an Euler path can be naively done by checking the number of odd degree vertices of
L^{k}(G)
. However, such an algorithm would take time exponential in
k
and size of
G
to terminate. In this section, we look into various structural properties of
L^{k}(G)
, aiding us to solve this problem more efficiently. We consider an exhaustive casework which analyzes all possible local structures of the graph
G
and deduce the values of
k
for which
L^{k}(G)
has an Euler path. This not only provides us with an exact classification of graphs where certain higher order line graphs admit Euler paths, but also results in a polynomial time algorithm deciding whether
L^{k}(G)
has an Euler path. In fact, our algorithm is stronger: For an input graph
G
with
n
vertices and
m
edges, in
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}m)
time, the algorithm outputs all
k
such that
L^{k}(G)
has an Euler path. Notice that the higher order line graphs eventually stop admitting Euler paths; such a result has an opposing flavour to Proposition
2
.

3.1
Definitions and tools

We start by laying down some definitions and observations relevant to the study of the existence of Euler paths in higher order line graphs.

Definition 8
.

For a graph
G
and a set

, let

. Let

We introduce the notion of an edge being

We use the following notation that associates edges of a graph to vertices in its line graph.

Definition 10

For a vertex

The next three results are about relations between critical edges of

In a graph

4
Maximum Degree Growth on Higher Order Line Graphs

In this section, we study the growth of the maximum degree in
L^{k}(G)
,
k\geqslant 0
, for a graph
G

is a path, a cycle or a claw, the maximum degree becomes of

stays

,
1
or
2
for

. Hence we restrict our focus to other simple connected graphs, i.e. graphs
G
that satisfy
G\in\mathcal{G}=\{G\mid G\text{ is a simple connected graph, }\Delta(G)\geqslant 3\text{ and }|E(G)|\geqslant 4\}
;
\mathcal{G}
is called the set of

[
2
]
.

We start by showing that the works of Hartke and Higgins
[
5
]
imply that for any prolific graph
G
, there exists a constant rational number
\mathsf{dgc}(G)
and an integer
k_{0}
such that for all
k\geqslant k_{0}
,
\Delta(L^{k}(G))=\mathsf{dgc}(G)\cdot 2^{k-4}+2
. We show that
\{\mathsf{dgc}(G)\mid G\in\mathcal{G}\}
has first, second, third, fourth, and fifth minimums, namely,
c_{1}=3
,
c_{2}=4
,
c_{3}=5.5
,
c_{4}=6
and
c_{5}=7
; the third minimum stands out surprisingly from the other four. We use structural properties of subgraphs and their corresponding images under line graphs as a tool to compare the parameter
\mathsf{dgc}
of two distinct graphs. Moreover, for
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
, we provide a complete characterisation of
\mathcal{G}_{i}=\{\mathsf{dgc}(G)=c_{i}\mid G\in\mathcal{G}\}
. Apart from this, we show that the set
\{\mathsf{dgc}(G)\mid G\in\mathcal{G},7<\mathsf{dgc}(G)<8\}
is countably infinite by providing a family of graphs attaining distinct values of
\mathsf{dgc}(G)
in this range.

4.1

We start by reiterating the definition of the

[

]
. Their work proves that for every prolific graph,

Definition 27

(Maximum Degree Growth Property Index (
\mathrm{MDGPI}
))

The

of a graph

, is the smallest integer

\Delta(L^{k}(G))=2\cdot\Delta(L^{k-1}(G))-2

If

\Delta(L^{k}(G))=(\Delta(L^{K}(G))-2)\cdot 2^{k-K}+2

Proof.

We proceed via induction. This holds trivially for

. Now, we assume this to hold true for some

. Therefore,
\Delta(L^{k}(G))=(\Delta(L^{K}(G))-2)\cdot 2^{k-K}+2
. From Definition

\displaystyle\Delta(L^{k+1}(G))

\displaystyle=2\cdot\Delta(L^{k}(G))-2=2\cdot((\Delta(L^{K}(G))-2)\cdot 2^{k-K}+2)-2

\displaystyle=(\Delta(L^{K}(G))-2)\cdot 2^{(k+1)-K}+2

This completes the inductive proof.
∎

Definition 29

For a graph

in

Their work deduce further propoerties of

If for a graph

.

Recall that Hartke and Higgins

Proposition 33
.

For every connected graph

c^{\prime}
, such that for all integers
k\geqslant\mathrm{MDGPI}(G)+1
, we have
\Delta(L^{k}(G))=c^{\prime}\cdot 2^{k-4}+2
. We call this constant

the degree growth constant, and denote it by
\mathsf{dgc}(G)
.

With the definition of
\mathsf{dgc}(G)
laid out, we restrict our focus to analyzing the exact values that
\mathsf{dgc}(G)
can attain for
G\in\mathcal{G}
. We start by defining two binary relations among graphs based on the maximum degrees of their higher order line graphs.

Definition 34
.

Consider two graphs
G_{1},G_{2}\in\mathcal{G}
. We say
G_{1}\leqslant_{k}G_{2}
for some non-negative integer
k
, if
\Delta(L^{r}(G_{1}))\leqslant\Delta(L^{r}(G_{2}))
for all
r\geqslant k
.

This binary relation is related to the degree growth constant as follows.

Observation 35
.

If for two graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, we have
G_{1}\leqslant_{k}G_{2}
for some non-negative integer
k
, then
\mathsf{dgc}(G_{1})\leqslant\mathsf{dgc}(G_{2})
.

Definition 36
.

Consider two graphs
G_{1},G_{2}\in\mathcal{G}
. Given two non-negative integers
a,b
with
a\leqslant b
, we say
G_{1}\leqslant_{a,b}G_{2}
if
L^{a}(G_{1})\subseteq L^{b}(G_{2})
, i.e.
L^{a}(G_{1})
is a subgraph of
L^{b}(G_{2})
.

The binary relations
\leqslant_{x,y}
and
\leqslant_{x}
can be shown to be related as follows.

Lemma 37
.

If
G_{1}\leqslant_{x,y}G_{2}
, then
G_{1}\leqslant_{x}G_{2}
.

Proof.

Since
G_{1}\leqslant_{x,y}G_{2}
, we have that
L^{x}(G_{1})\subseteq L^{y}(G_{2})
and
x\geqslant y
. Now, by Proposition
5
, we have
L^{x+i}(G_{1})\subseteq L^{y+i}(G_{2})
for all
i\geqslant 0
. Let
k\geqslant x
, then
L^{k}(G_{1})\subseteq L^{y+k-x}(G_{2})
. Note that
k+y-x\leqslant k
. We now show that
\Delta(L(G))\geqslant\Delta(G)
whenever
G
is not a star graph. Consider a
\Delta
-vertex
v\in V(G)
. Note that all neighbours of
v
cannot have degrees equal to
1
as otherwise
G
will be a star graph. Therefore,
\exists u\in N_{G}(v)
such that
d_{G}(u)\geqslant 2
. Let the vertex corresponding to edge

in
L(G)
be
v^{\prime}
. Then, by Proposition
4
,
\Delta(L(G))\geqslant d_{G}(v^{\prime})=d_{G}(v)+d_{G}(u)-2\geqslant\Delta(G)
.

Now, higher order line graphs cannot be isomorphic to star graphs as they are claw-free. Thus, we have that
\Delta(L^{k}(G_{1}))\leqslant\Delta(L^{y+k-x}(G_{2}))\leqslant\Delta(L^{k}(G_{2}))
, which in other words means that
G_{1}\leqslant_{x}G_{2}
.
∎

From Observation
35
and Lemma
37
, we have the following.

Observation 38
.

If for two graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, we have
G_{1}\leqslant_{x,y}G_{2}
for some non-negative integers
a\leqslant b
, then
\mathsf{dgc}(G_{1})\leqslant\mathsf{dgc}(G_{2})
.

With the definitions and the relevant preliminary results ready we establish a few more results which would finally help us in analyzing the set
\{\mathsf{dgc}(G)\mid G\in\mathcal{G}\}
.

5
Open Questions

This paper examines two structural properties of iterated line graphs: (i) existence of Euler paths and (ii) growth of maximum degree. In future, we hope to extend the understanding of iterated higher order line graphs with respect to other graph properties that are efficiently checkable, e.g.,
3
-connectedness, etc. Note that we characterize the precise graphs attaining the first four minimum values of the degree growth constant, but it remains open to obtain the precise graphs attaining the fifth minimum of
7
. An efficient algorithm to compute the
\mathsf{dgc}(G)
of an input prolific graph
G
remains an open problem. Another open algorithmic question would be to design efficient algorithms (or provide lower bounds) to compute the maximum degree of an iterated higher order line graph for some input graph
G
.
