Evaluating the efficacy of reprocessing contaminated single use devices for re-use in intensive care unit in Ethiopia
Authors: Yamirot Merga Duffa, Zerihun Shimelis Kasa, Kibrewossen Kiflu Aklilu, Feyissa Regassa Senbato, Zelalem Tazu Bonger, Eyasu Tigabu Seyoum, Eden Dagnachew Zeleke, Leulseged Takele Chekol, Zelalem Mekuria, Samuel Muluye Welelaw, Joan-Miquel Balada-Llasat, Shu-Hua Wang, Tadesse Eguale
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSingle use devices (SUDs) are designed for single use. However, in low-income countries, they are often reprocessed and reused. Because these devices are intended for single use, no standardized protocol for reprocessing these devices currently exists. We developed a protocol for reprocessing SUDs in low-income settings and assessed the efficacy of this protocol in eliminating bacterial burden from a subset of used respiratory SUDs.MethodsReprocessing of these devices was conducted in accordance with the National IPC reference manual with minor modifications. The surfaces of used SUDs were swabbed for bacterial culture before and after reprocessing. Serial dilutions of the swab samples were inoculated on plate count agar media and incubated. Bacterial burden was determined by colony forming unit (CFU) counts. Bacterial isolates were identified and characterized using standard microbiology techniques, antimicrobial susceptibility testing, and modified carbapenem inactivation method in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines.ResultsPrior to reprocessing, twenty-seven (77.1%) of 35 devices tested were culture-positive for one or more bacterial species. The average bacterial count before reprocessing ranged from 1.98 × 104 to 1.92 × 105 CFU/cm2. Nineteen (54.3%) tested devices were positive for coagulase-negative Staphylococcus, and 19 (54.3%) for Gram-negative organisms: Acinetobacter spp. (n = 7; 36.8%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (n = 6; 31.6%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (n = 3; 15.8%), Escherichia coli (n = 2; 10.5%), and K. oxytoca (n = 1; 5.3%). Ten (52%) of the Gram-negative organisms were multidrug-resistant. Following reprocessing, no bacterial growth was demonstrated on any of the devices.ConclusionThe reprocessing protocol employed in this study demonstrated absence of culturable bacteria from used SUDs. However, since the assessment was limited to surface swabbing of a small number of devices collected from a single facility and targeted only selected bacterial pathogens, the presence of other potential pathogens could not be completely ruled out. There is a need to assess the impact of the reprocessing procedure on the structural integrity and functionality of these devices. If SUDs are being reused, the efficacy of the decontamination method should be ascertained to mitigate the risk of transmission of pathogens between patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13756-025-01683-7.Keywords: Reprocessing, Decontamination, Single use devices, Low-income countries

Introduction

Medical devices, such as oxygen face masks, nasal cannulas, ventilator circuits, and suction tubes, are manufactured as single use devices (SUDs). When feasible, it is not recommended to reuse medical devices intended for single use [1, 2]. However, due to resource limitations in low-income countries, these devices may be routinely reprocessed and reused. In Ethiopia, these limitations include lack of affordability and availability for routine purchasing. Though contrary to manufacturer recommendations and best practice, these devices are often reprocessed in hospitals in Ethiopia to continue providing essential patient care [3].

SUDs that are reprocessed and reused should be undamaged, functional, cost-effective, and suitable for cleaning and decontamination [1, 2]. Previous published reports estimate that reprocessing SUDs can save up to 90% of costs, particularly when it is done at the healthcare facility [4]. It is recommended that a regulatory mechanism be in place to assess the quality of reprocessing. Some high-income countries have developed their own guidelines, regulations and quality assurance mechanisms for reprocessing and reuse of SUDs. An acceptable measure for the current standard of reprocessing SUDs is based on the level of reduction of microbial bioburden [5, 6].

In Ethiopia, the National Infection Prevention and Control Reference Manual outlines a five-step procedure for high-level disinfection of reusable semi-critical medical devices: point-of care cleaning, cleaning, rinsing, disinfection, and rinsing (Table 1) [7]. The National Infection Prevention and Control Reference Manual does not address the procedure for reprocessing SUDs, including respiratory devices, even though these items are frequently reused in the Ethiopian healthcare setting.

Prior to this study, we noted that water residue inside reprocessed nasal cannulas and ventilator circuits failed to dry given the length of tubing, even after hanging these items for more than 8 h. This residual moisture can create an opportunity for microbial growth, especially when items are stored in a closed bin before drying is complete. To address this problem, we conducted a preliminary study by including an additional, final step in the reprocessing procedure of rinsing with 96% ethanol. We observed that rinsing with 96% ethanol after the final water rinse led to elimination of water residue from nasal cannulas within a few minutes and from ventilator circuits within 3–4 h of rinse. Due to these findings, we added a final step of rinsing with 96% ethanol to our procedure.

In Ethiopia, despite wide reuse of single-use respiratory devices, there has been no literature published to our knowledge that assessed the effectiveness of disinfection post reprocessing. The added value of moisture elimination and rapid drying with an ethanol rinse was also not reported for the respiratory tubing although it was recommended for drying endoscopes after reprocessing [8]. The aim of this study was therefore to assess the effectiveness of reprocessing procedures for respiratory SUDs followed in a public hospital in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.

Methods

Study setting

This study was conducted at the intensive care unit (ICU) of Zewditu Memorial Hospital (ZMH). ZMH is a secondary level referral hospital in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia and has a ten-bed ICU for adult and pediatric patients.

Experimental protocol

Swab sample collection

A total of 35 used SUDs (9 oxygen face masks, 8 nasal cannula, 9 respiratory suction tubes, and 9 ventilator circuits) were randomly selected from a batch of dirty devices stored at the “dirty” designated area. Fairly equal number of items were selected from each equipment type, but swabbed items were selected randomly from each category. Information such as date of sample collection and type of devices were recorded. Swab samples were taken from devices and cultured three times: Before reprocessing, after reprocessing, and after rinsing with 96% ethanol.

Before reprocessing: A 5 cm by 5 cm internal surface at the sites that come in-contact with the patient were swabbed aseptically using sterile cotton swab moistened with sterile normal saline (Fig. 2A and B). For nasal cannula, the proximal part was incised, and internal part was swabbed. The swab was then immersed in a test tube containing 10 ml of sterile normal saline for culture.

Before reprocessing: A 5 cm by 5 cm internal surface at the sites that come in-contact with the patient were swabbed aseptically using sterile cotton swab moistened with sterile normal saline (Fig. 2A and B). For nasal cannula, the proximal part was incised, and internal part was swabbed. The swab was then immersed in a test tube containing 10 ml of sterile normal saline for culture.

Culture and standard plate counting

In the laboratory, each test tube containing swab samples was vortexed for 30 s for thorough mixing and the swab was removed from the test tube by gently pressing against the wall of the tube. One ml of the vortexed solution was then used to make a 10-fold serial dilution of 10− 1, 10− 2, 10− 3 and 10− 4. Subsequently, 10 µl of each of the dilutions was inoculated on standard plate count agar media using spread plate technique. The plates were then incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. The number of colony forming units (CFUs) were counted and recorded for each serial dilution. Bacterial load per cm2 was then calculated for each tested sample [9].

Pathogen identification, antimicrobial susceptibility testing, and carbapenemase enzyme detection

To establish pathogen species isolated, representative distinct colonies were subcultured on MacConkey, blood, and chocolate agar plates. MacConkey plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, whereas blood and chocolate agar plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h under 5% CO2. Pure colonies from each plate were further identified using standard biochemical tests such as lactose fermentation, indole, citrate, urease and motility. Gram-positive colonies were tested using catalase test to differentiate Staphylococcus from Streptococcus species. Then coagulase test and mannitol salt agar media were used to identify Staphylococcus aureus from coagulase-negative Staphylococcus (CoNS) species.

All isolated Gram-negative organisms were tested for their susceptibility to commonly prescribed antimicrobials using Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion assay. The following antimicrobial disks, with disk potencies were used: ampicillin (Amp 30 µg), amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (Amc: 20/10µg), ceftriaxone (Cro:30 µg), ceftazidime (Caz: 30 µg), cefepime (Fep:30ug), piperacillin/tazobactam (Tzp: 100/10ug), ciprofloxacin (Cip: 5 µg), gentamicin (Gm:10 µg), amikacin (An: 30 µg), sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim (Sxt: 23.75/1.25 µg), ertapenem (Etp:10ug) meropenem (Mem: 10ug). The diameter of the zone of inhibition of each antimicrobial disk was measured using caliper to the nearest millimeter and susceptibility profile was recorded as per Clinical and Laboratory Standard Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Isolates resistant to ertapenem and/or meropenem were also tested for production of carbapenemases using modified carbapenem inactivation method (mCIM) [10]. Enterobacterales positive for carbapenemase production were also tested for types of carbapenemase enzymes involved using real time PCR technique targeting four major carbapenemase genes KPC, NDM, VIM and OXA-48 like enzymes per the CLSI guideline at the Ethiopian Public Health Institute, National Microbiology Reference Laboratory [11].

Results

Bacterial load before and after reprocessing

A total of 35 swab samples from the 35 devices (9 oxygen face masks, 8 nasal cannula, 9 respiratory suction tubes, and 9 ventilator circuits) were cultured before reprocessing, after disinfection and boiled water rinse, and after rinsing with 96% ethanol. Only swabs taken from devices before reprocessing yielded bacterial growth. Of these preprocessed devices, 77.1% (27/35) were positive for 35 bacterial species: 19 CoNS, six Klebsiella pneumoniae, four Acinetobacter spp., three Pseudomonas aeruginosa, two Escherichia coli, and one Klebsiella oxytoca.

The bacterial load per cm2 showed wide variability among same and different device types. Among culture positive devices, both the lowest and the highest bacterial burden were from ventilator circuits (Range 3.9 × 103 to 9.4 × 105 counts per cm2). The mean load of bacterial contamination among the tested devices was highest for ventilator circuits, followed by suction tube and oxygen face mask whereas nasal prong carried lowest mean bacterial load (Fig. 3). None of the medical devices investigated in the current study were positive for bacterial growth post reprocessing: either after emersion in chlorine and rinsing with distilled water or after rinsing with 96% ethanol.

Seven of the nine tested oxygen face masks (78%) were positive: two P. aeruginosa, two Acinetobacter species, one K. oxytoca, and three CoNS. One of the face masks was positive for both Acinetobacter species and CoNS. Bacterial growth was recorded only in two of the eight nasal cannulas (25%) tested in which E. coli and CoNS were detected. All the suction tubes cultures grew one or more organisms. Three of the nine (33.3%) suction tubes were positive for K. pneumoniae and CoNS. Antimicrobial susceptibility profile of identified bacterial pathogens

Five (83.3%) of the K. pneumoniae isolated from different devices were resistant to amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, and cefepime, whereas one K. pneumoniae isolate was susceptible to all tested antimicrobials. Among the Klebsiella species identified, one K. pneumoniae isolated from an unprocessed oxygen face mask was resistant to all antimicrobials tested including ertapenem and meropenem; testing positive for carbapenemase production by mCIM. Real-time PCR assay conducted to identify type of enzymes associated with carbapenem resistance revealed both NDM and OXA-48 like enzymes. All 7 of the Acinetobacter species were resistant to sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim. Similarly, a high rate of resistance to the majority of antimicrobials tested was recorded among the two E. coli isolates (Table 3).

Discussion

There is a global increase in the burden of healthcare-associated infections caused by MDR pathogens, limiting therapeutic efficacy of available antimicrobials [12]. Contaminated devices are potential sources of hospital-acquired infections [13]. Inadequately reprocessed medical devices have been associated with outbreaks of healthcare-associated infections. An outbreak of Bacillus cereus respiratory tract infections affecting ventilated preterm neonates was reported from Birmingham, United Kingdom in 1999 [3]. An outbreak of pulmonary P. aeruginosa and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia infections related to contaminated bronchoscope suction valves was also reported from France [14]. Formation of biofilms on suction channels of flexible endoscopes has also been shown to contribute to increased risk of transmission if not properly decontaminated [15].

The current study assessed the efficacy of the reprocessing procedure in eliminating bacterial growth from a subset of semi-critical SUDs. Adequate reprocessing is expected to result in a 6-log reduction of CFU. A high log reduction value in CFU between before and after reprocessing corresponds with removal of high bioburden [16]. In the current study, no residual bacterial bioburden was detected after reprocessing in any of the devices tested. However, the sampling approach in this study was limited to collecting swab samples from accessible surfaces of SUDs and did not include deep biofilm scraping. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, as they may not fully represent the microbial load within deeper biofilm layers. Furthermore, this study did not evaluate the effectiveness of reprocessing protocol in eliminating fungal and viral pathogens that may potentially contaminate the SUDs from patients who had previously used them.

A high bacterial load was detected on previously used SUDs prior to reprocessing, particularly on ventilator circuits and suction tubes, with counts reaching up to 9.4 × 10⁵ CFU. The observed variability in bacterial burden across different device types may be influenced by the duration of device use, patient acuity, differences between pediatric and adult patient populations, and the surrounding microbial ecology. In addition, design features such as the folds and ridges present in ventilator circuits may facilitate bacterial adherence, contributing to higher bacterial loads observed on these devices [17].

Klebsiella, Acinetobacter, Pseudomonas species and CoNS were the predominant bacteria identified. These organisms are reported to be the common causes of infection in hospitals in North Ethiopia and detecting them from unprocessed SUDs is not surprising [18]. These organisms were also the predominantly isolated pathogens from clinical specimens among adult ICU patients during this period (data not shown). They are among the major pathogens responsible for healthcare-associated infections, and they are categorized under WHO critical and high priority pathogens list [19]. The majority of these isolates were resistant to most of the antimicrobials tested. Additionally, these bacterial species are known to cause hospital-acquired infections in immunocompromised patients [13, 18, 20]. One of the K. pneumoniae isolated from a suction tube was resistant to all of the 11 antimicrobials tested. The burden of these bacteria in Ethiopian hospitals underscores the need for effective reprocessing of medical devices.

If not properly reprocessed, there is risk of infection with subsequent complications, particularly in ICUs where patients are immunocompromised [13, 21]. While no CoNS were isolated from the devices post-reprocessing, 19/35 (54%) of devices tested had CoNS growth before decontamination. CoNS are part of the normal skin flora and can reside on the skin and hands of the healthcare workers, patients, and care providers. However, their elimination is critical, as CoNS are common causes of infections among immunocompromised individuals and those with invasive devices [22].

The presence of moisture in reprocessed medical devices such as endoscope channels has been shown to be associated with increased microbial contamination [23]. We noted the time required for residual distilled water to evaporate from interior surfaces of respiratory tubing after reprocessing was prolonged, thus increasing the risk of bacterial growth. The length of time required for devices to completely dry is further affected by the degree of ventilation and level of humidity in the reprocessing room. Complete drying of reprocessed devices is mandatory to avoid creation of a conducive environment for recontamination of decontaminated devices. Given this limitation, we added a final rinsing step with 96% ethanol to decrease the time required for drying. Time to drying was shortened in our protocols by use of ethanol; no bacterial growth was detected both prior to and after rinsing with alcohol. Final alcohol rinse and forced air drying of reprocessed semi-critical devices has been reported to reduce bacterial contamination by removing wet environment [8]. Although there is no evidence of pathogen transmission associated with the final alcohol rinse of reprocessed devices, repeated exposure to alcohol may affect the structural integrity of the devices, potentially impacting their functionality. Prolonged and repeated exposure to alcohol was reported to cause swelling and hardening of rubber and certain plastic tubings [8]. Therefore, further studies are warranted to assess the impact of alcohol application on SUDs and the number of times alcohol can be safely applied to SUDs without compromising their structural integrity and functional performance of the devices.

Eventhough no bacterial growth were detected from reprocessed SUDs, the collection of internal surface swabs from the devices only at the point of contact with the patient is unlikely to detect bacterial biofilms in the tubes. Biofilm formation on endoscopic suction channels has been associated with interference of cleaning and disinfection during the reprocessing [24]. We can postulate that biofilm formation can occur on these devices. If not properly decontaminated, use of these devices could pose a risk when used by subsequent patients.

Conclusion

The current study revealed a high burden of MDR Gram-negative bacterial species on used SUDs. In resource-limited settings where reprocessing and reuse of SUDs may occur, it is essential to validate decontamination methods to reduce the risk of pathogen transmission. Our findings demonstrated that with the applied reprocessing protocol, no bacterial growth was detected on the sampled SUDs. However, the fact that we used only surface swabbing, only few SUDs from single facility and targeting only a few bacterial pathogens, cannot rule out other pathogens and potential biofilm forming organisms inside the tubing. Further research and validation of protocols including patient infection outcomes are necessary to assess the safety and feasibility of reusing reprocessed SUDs, particularly in settings where adherence to best practices is constrained by resource limitations.
