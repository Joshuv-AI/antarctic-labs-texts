Integrated transcriptomics and metabolomics reveal suppression of the PPAR α/γ pathway linking purine metabolism disorder to high-protein diet-induced hyperuricemia in goslings
Authors: Xifeng Li, Yufei Xie, Mengyao Zhang, Tao Zou, Chonghua Zhong, Zahid Iqbal Rajput, Shicheng Bi, Liting Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hyperuricemia (HUA) and gout in goslings are increasingly recognized in intensive production systems, yet liver involvement in HUA remains poorly understood. This study examines how a high-protein diet influences gosling HUA through liver transcriptional and metabolic alterations. In Exp. Ⅰ, a total of 80 one-d-old male goslings (93.13 ± 0.96 g) were randomly assigned to four dietary treatments containing 18% (control group), 21% (P21 group), 24% (P24 group), and 27% (P27 group) crude protein (CP), with four replicates of five birds per treatment, for an 18-d experimental period to establish a HUA model. Goslings fed the 24% CP diet exhibited joint swelling, sparse plumage, and white feces, accompanied by reduced average daily gain (ADG; P = 0.011) and average daily feed intake (ADFI; P < 0.001). Serum uric acid (P = 0.005), xanthine oxidase (XOD; P = 0.009), xanthine dehydrogenase (XDH; P = 0.018), aspartate aminotransferase (AST; P < 0.001), urea (P < 0.001), interleukin-1β (IL-1β; P = 0.010), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α; P < 0.001) levels were significantly elevated in the P24 group, consistent with HUA-associated metabolic and inflammatory alterations. In Exp. Ⅱ, a total of 40 one-d-old goslings (93.88 + 0.92 g) were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 20 per group) and fed an 18% CP basal diet (CON group) or a 24% CP diet (HUA group) for 18 d. The HUA group showed increased liver index (P = 0.001), hydropic and partial lipid degeneration, hepatocyte swelling, and marked mitochondrial swelling observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Integrated transcriptomics and metabolomics of the liver revealed that decreased levels of 9-hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid (9-HODE) and 8(S)-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid [8(S)-HETE] were associated with suppression of the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α/γ (PPARα/γ) signaling pathway. Meanwhile, the upregulation of ACSL6 and ACSBG2 was linked to a 2.61-fold increase in adenosine monophosphate (AMP) accumulation. Elevated AMPD3 and NT5C1B expression enhanced AMP-to-inosine conversion (1.69-fold), and coordinated up-regulation of NT5C1B, PNP, and XDH further accelerated UA formation in purine metabolism, collectively contributing to HUA and associated inflammatory responses. Although graded increases in dietary CP induced proportional shifts in digestible amino acid concentrations, these results reveal a strong correlation between high-protein diets and HUA, as well as subsequent liver and renal injuries in goslings.

Introduction

Hyperuricemia (HUA) in poultry is a metabolic disorder that can be induced by excessive dietary crude protein (CP) intake as well as other environmental or pathological factors. Although early-stage HUA in birds is often subclinical, progression to gout can severely impair growth performance, resulting in not only a significant decline in poultry meat, egg, and feather production, but also adversely affect survival, flock uniformity, and slaughter yield. Unlike most mammals, birds lack functional urate oxidase (UOX), rendering uric acid (UA) the terminal product of purine catabolism. Consequently, birds maintain relatively high circulating UA levels compared with most mammals (Hong et al., 2020). Therefore, investigation of avian HUA is important for optimizing nutritional management in poultry and for improving understanding of UA-associated metabolic dysregulation.

Although UA accumulation has traditionally been attributed to impaired renal excretion, systemic UA levels are fundamentally driven by hepatic purine production rather than solely by excretory capacity. Serum metabolomic profiling in gout-affected geese suggests that hepatic metabolic disturbance contributes to the development of HUA and gout, whereas most previous studies have primarily emphasized renal urate excretion and deposition, with comparatively limited attention to hepatic metabolic regulation (Xi et al., 2020). In birds, the liver serves as the principal site of UA production, where xanthine oxidoreductase (XOR) exists in two interconvertible forms: xanthine dehydrogenase (XDH) and xanthine oxidase (XOD). These enzymes catalyze the terminal steps of purine degradation leading to UA formation; XDH preferentially utilizes nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide+ (NAD+), whereas XOD uses O2 as an electron acceptor (Hille and Nishino, 1995). As the primary organ responsible for amino acid metabolism and purine biosynthesis, the liver directly responds to dietary protein fluctuations, making it a key regulatory site in diet-induced HUA. High-protein diets provide excessive purines and purine precursors (e.g., glycine and glutamine), thereby increasing substrate availability for hepatic purine catabolism and UA production. In addition, amino acids derived from dietary protein may indirectly contribute to purine nucleotide synthesis, further promoting UA accumulation (Ichida et al., 2012; Yip et al., 2020). Given that UA overproduction precedes crystal deposition and renal pathology, elucidating hepatic regulatory mechanisms is essential for understanding the early pathogenesis of HUA. At physiological concentrations, UA functions as an important antioxidant in birds. Excessive UA has been reported to function as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP), promoting oxidative stress and inflammasome activation (Cantor et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2015). High-protein feeding has been shown to upregulate hepatic XDH expression in goslings (the full names of the abbreviations of all genes can be found in Table S1). Moreover, the activity of XOD and concentrations of XDH have been associated with pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), potentially mediated by oxidative stress during early-stage gout (Li, 2019; Pfeffer et al., 1994). In mammals, HUA has been associated with disturbances in amino acid, glucose, and lipid metabolism, which further underscore the multifaceted nature of hepatic metabolic dysregulation in this disorder (Rama Rao et al., 2001). However, corresponding mechanistic evidence in poultry remains limited.

Therefore, the objective of the present study was to establish a high-protein diet-induced HUA model in goslings and to investigate hepatic molecular and metabolic alterations associated with elevated UA. To achieve this, integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses were performed to characterize gene expression changes and metabolic perturbations in the liver. Collectively, this study provides mechanistic insights into gene-metabolite interactions underlying UA dysregulation and offers an experimental framework for future mechanistic and nutritional intervention studies aimed at preventing HUA and gout in poultry.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All experimental procedures applied in this study were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Review Committee of the Southwest University (approval number: IACUC-20240123-01, date: 2024-02-26), Chongqing, China. All procedures involving the handling, management, and healthcare of live birds was performed following the regulations of laboratory animals used for scientific purposes.

Animal Exp. Ⅰ

A total of 80 one-d-old goslings (initial body weight: 93.13 ± 0.96 g) were randomly allocated into four groups: a control group (control) and three graded CP groups designated as P21, P24, and P27, with four replicates per group and five goslings per replicate, with daily feed intake recorded on a replicate basis. The control group was fed a basal diet with a CP content of 18%, with CP content for the P21, P24, and P27 groups being 21%, 24%, and 27%, respectively. The experimental diets were based on corn-soybean meal raw materials, and the nutrient levels of the control group were designed according to the recommendations of the NRC (1994). The ingredient compositions of each protein group were adjusted to achieve the desired CP levels with minimal changes to other nutrient compositions. Metabolizable energy (ME), calcium and available phosphorus were maintained at comparable levels across diets. All goslings were housed in separate cages with ad libitum access to feed and water. Clinical signs were monitored daily. Growth performance

During Exp. Ⅰ , 8 goslings were randomly selected from each group and were weighed on an empty stomach before the start and end of the experiment, and the total amounts of feed and the feed remaining the next day in the feeding tray were recorded for each cage, each day. After the experiment, the average daily feed intake (ADFI), average daily gain (ADG), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated referencing previously published study (Xie et al., 2024) and using the following formulas:

ADG (g/d) = [Final weight (g) – Initial weight (g)]/Number of days (d);

ADFI (g/d) = Total feed intake (g)/Number of days (d);

FCR = Feed intake (g)/[Final weight of goslings (g) − Initial weight of goslings (g)].

Serum biochemical indicators

Blood samples were aseptically collected from the wing veins of goslings, allowed to clot at 37 °C for 1 h, and then stored at 4 °C for 12 h. Serum was obtained by centrifugation at 1500 × g for 10 min and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Serum concentrations of UA (C012-2-1), urea (C013-2-1), albumin (ALB; A028-2-1), total protein (TP; A045-1-1), creatinine (Cr; C013-2-1), and serum activities of alanine aminotransferase (ALT; C009-2-1) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST; C010-2-1), alkaline phosphatase (ALP; A059-3-1), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH; A020-2-1), and XOD (A002-1-1) were measured using biochemical kits (Nanjing Jiancehng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Serum concentrations of XDH (BPE62008), IL-6 (BPE60041), IL-1β (BPE60090), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α; BPE60157), and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1; BPE60260) were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Shanghai Lengton Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). All serum biochemical indicators were determined using commercial assay kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured using an Infinite M200 Pro multimode microplate reader (Infinite M200 Pro, Tecan Austria GmbH, Grödig, Salzburg, Austria).

Animal Exp.Ⅱ

Based on the results of Exp. Ⅰ, the CP level (24%) of the treatment group was used to construct the HUA model for liver and kidney pathological examination and transcriptional and metabolomics analyses. A total of 40 one-d-old goslings (initial body weight: 93.88 ± 0.92 g) were randomly assigned to two groups (four replicates per group and five goslings per replicate) and fed an 18% CP basal diet (CON group) or a 24% CP diet (HUA group). Breeding standards and procedures refer to Exp. Ⅰ. After 18 d, eight goslings were randomly selected from each group to be euthanized, and the liver and kidney were completely excised and weighed. Liver tissue was collected for transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses, and samples from both organs were subjected to histopathological examination.

Organ index of liver and kidney

In Exp. Ⅱ, liver and kidney tissues were collected intact and weighed after goslings were euthanized to calculate the organ index:

Organ index (mg/g) = Fresh mass of organs (mg)/Ante-mortem fasting mass (g).

Light microscopy of liver and kidney

Liver and kidney tissues from the same anatomical regions were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde, thoroughly washed in water, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin. The tissues were sectioned into 5-μm slices, three sections from each sample were cut, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and examined under an optical microscope (Model Axio Scope A1, Carl Zeiss (Shanghai) Management Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), at least five randomly selected fields from each tissue section were examined for histopathological evaluation.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of liver and kidney

The liver and kidney tissues were pre-fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (G916055, Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), and then re-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide (1722571, Beijing Zhongjingkeyi Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Subsequently, the tissues were dehydrated with acetone step by step and embedded with Epon-812 pure embedding agent (1260804, SPI Supplies, Structure Probe, Inc., West Chester, PA, USA). Ultra-thin sections (60–90 nm) were cut and stained with uranyl acetate (1722581, Beijing Zhongjingkeyi Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and lead citrate (1722582, Beijing Zhongjingkeyi Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) successively. The images of the copper mesh were collected and the specific lesions were observed using transmission electron microscope (JEM-1400FLASH, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Liver transcriptomics

Total RNA was isolated from liver tissues using TRIzol reagent (Cat. No. 15596018, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA). The concentration and integrity of RNA were determined with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). RNA samples meeting quality criteria were submitted to Personalbio (Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) for cDNA library preparation and sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered using fastp (v0.22.0) to remove adaptor sequences, poly(A) tails and low-quality reads. Clean reads were then aligned to the Taihu goose reference genome (GCF_040182565.1 T2T assembly) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). Gene-level read counts were summarized using HTSeq (v0.11.2). Gene expression levels were normalized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). The FPKM values were log2-transformed after adding a pseudocount of 1. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the prcomp function in R (v4.2.0). Heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap (v1.0.12) R package, and M-A plots were generated using ggplot2 (v3.4.0). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between groups were identified using DESeq2 (v1.38.0) based on raw count data. Genes with |log2 fold change| > 1 and a P-value < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. The full names of the abbreviations of all genes can be found in Table S1. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using topGO (v2.50.0), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using clusterProfiler (v4.6.0). Enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways with P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Liver metabolomics

Following metabolite extraction from liver tissues of the CON and HUA groups, untargeted metabolomics profiling was performed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS). Raw data were processed using the XCMS package (v3.16.1) in R (v4.2.0) for peak detection, alignment, and normalization. Metabolites were identified matching against an in-house MS2 spectral library, mzCloud online database, LIPID MAPS, HMDB, MoNA, and NIST 2020 MS/MS spectral library. Principal component analysis and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were conducted using the ropls package (v1.28.2) in R. Differential metabolites (DMs) were screened based on variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1 and P-value < 0.05. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, and false discovery rate (FDR) values were calculated. Hierarchical clustering was visualized using the pheatmap package (v1.0.12). Pathway enrichment analysis based on KEGG was conducted using a hypergeometric test, and pathways with P-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses

Enzyme-gene relationships were established based on KEGG pathway annotations. Differential metabolites were first mapped to KEGG compound IDs (C numbers), and their associated enzymatic reactions were retrieved based on KEGG reaction annotations and corresponding enzyme commission (EC) numbers. The corresponding EC numbers were then linked to gene orthologs (KO IDs) annotated in the transcriptomic dataset. Differentially expressed genes were functionally annotated to KEGG pathways using KO assignments, enabling the construction of metabolite-enzyme-gene triplets through annotated enzymatic reactions within defined KEGG metabolic pathways. Only metabolite-gene pairs connected through annotated enzymatic reactions within the same KEGG metabolic pathway were retained for subsequent integration analysis. Expression differences across experimental groups were visualized by integrating DMs and DEGs. Pathway convergence was determined through intersection analyses of KEGG-enriched terms from both omics datasets. Pearson correlation coefficients between DEGs and DMs in CON and HUA groups were calculated, with metabolite-gene pairs filtered using thresholds of |r| > 0.8 and P-value < 0.05. Expression trends of filtered pairs were analyzed via nine-quadrant plots. Correlation networks were constructed using the igraph package in R, visually identifying strongly associated metabolite-gene pairs (P < 0.05).

Reverse transcription quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from liver using the Trizol method, and the purity and concentration of total RNA was determined using Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA), with total RNA with an optical density (OD) value between 1.8 and 2.0 used for subsequent experiments. Reverse transcription was performed according to the instructions of the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Perfect Real Time) (RR047A, Takara Biomedical Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) for cDNA synthesis. β-Actin was used as the internal reference gene. NCBI Primer BLAST was used to design the primers for RT-qPCR, with the primer sequences shown in Table 2. The full names of the abbreviations of all genes can be found in Table S1. The primers were synthesized by Sangon Bioengineering (Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The RT-qPCR reaction system was prepared according to the instructions of the TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNaseH Plus) kit (RR420A, Takara Biomedical Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), consisting of 10 μL made up from: TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RnaseH Plus) 5 μL, PCR forward primer 0.4 μL, PCR reverse primer 0.4 μL, template DNA 1 μL, Rox reference dye 0.2 μL, ddH2O 3 μL. The reaction conditions were: predenaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s; annealing at 56 °C for 30 s; extension at 72 °C for 30 s; 40 cycles. Gene expression levels were determined by RT-qPCR using an StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (4376600, Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Foster City, CA, USA). Statistical analysis

The data are expressed as means and standard error of the mean (SEM), analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For the gradient test of Exp. Ⅰ, growth performance and serum biochemical indicators were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. For Exp. Ⅱ, organ indexes and RT-qPCR data were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was determined at P < 0.05. The statistical model used for one-way ANOVA was as follows:Yij=μ+Ti+εij,where Yij is the dependent variable; μ is the population mean; Ti is the fixed effect of dietary CP level, i = 18%, 21%, 24%, or 27%; and εij is the random error. Orthogonal polynomial contrasts were applied to evaluate the linear and quadratic effects of protein levels.

Results

Clinical manifestations

During Exp. Ⅰ, the goslings in control group were all in normal condition. On the d 7, the goslings in the P24 and P27 groups showed listlessness, and increased appetite, with some exhibiting ataxia. On the d 14, the goslings in the P21 group showed depression and clustering, while those in the P24 and P21 groups appeared depressed, emaciated, with sparse feathers (Fig. 1E), slightly swollen joints (Fig. 1G), and reluctance to walk, with some goslings excreting white thin feces (Fig. 1F), and some dying suddenly. Discussion

High-protein feeding in the present study induced a stable HUA model in goslings, characterized by growth retardation, hyperuricemia, hepatic and renal injury, and systemic inflammation. These results indicate that dietary protein overload disrupts hepatic purine homeostasis and triggers downstream metabolic and inflammatory cascades. Notably, the observed growth retardation suggests that excessive protein intake exceeded the metabolic processing capacity of the immature liver, thereby increasing nitrogen load and metabolic demand (Selle et al., 2021). Serum biochemical analysis revealed marked HUA in the P24 group, together with elevated AST, ALP, and LDH activities and increased urea concentrations, indicating combined hepatic injury and increased nitrogen turnover (Dissa et al., 2023). Consistently, serum levels of XOD, XDH, and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and MCP-1) were significantly elevated, indicating that enhanced purine degradation was coupled with inflammatory activation. The elevation of XOD concentration and enhancement of XDH activity were correlated with accelerated of UA biosynthesis rate, indicating enhanced purine degradation flux (Bortolotti et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2020; Yong et al., 2022). Uric acid functions as a DAMP capable of activating innate immune signaling, thereby linking metabolic overload to inflammatory amplification (Braga et al., 2017; Kim et al., 2015). These findings suggest that the coordinated up-regulation of purine-degrading enzymes and inflammatory mediators may represent a mechanistic link between hepatic UA overproduction and subsequent liver injury (Crişan et al., 2016; Gu et al., 2025). The observation that the P21 group exhibited reduced feed intake and early inflammatory activation without exceeding the hyperuricemia threshold suggests that metabolic disturbance may begin prior to overt UA accumulation. Based on phenotypic, biochemical, enzymatic, and inflammatory evidences, the P24 group was confirmed as a stable HUA model for subsequent mechanistic analyses. Histopathological examination of hepatic and renal tissues in the HUA group revealed that excessive accumulation of UA was associated with hepatic endothelial injury, mitochondrial oxidative stress, and renal tubular degeneration, suggesting a vicious cycle of metabolic dysregulation and impaired urate clearance (Zhang et al., 2015).

Transcriptomic profiling identified 969 DEGs, reflecting extensive reprogramming of hepatic metabolic and inflammatory networks in response to protein overload. Gene ontology enrichment analysis revealed predominant activation of inflammatory and stress-responsive processes, suggesting that hepatic UA elevation is accompanied by coordinated immune activation rather than isolated metabolic disturbance (Chang et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2017). Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes analysis further highlighted the PPAR signaling pathway as a central regulatory axis, with multiple PPAR-related genes significantly down-regulated in HUA-affected livers, thereby influencing hepatic metabolic and inflammatory homeostasis (Gu et al., 2025; Poiley et al., 2016). Metabolomic analysis further supported these observations. A total of 467 DMs were primarily enriched in amino acid, purine, and glucose metabolism pathways, supporting enhanced nitrogen flux and urate biosynthesis. Excessive protein intake likely increased hepatic amino acid catabolism and ATP turnover, imposing energetic stress on the immature liver and favoring AMP accumulation (Chen et al., 2025). The relatively stable digestible lysine supply argued against a classical essential amino acid imbalance, whereas the concurrent increases in serum urea, hepatic AMP, and purine-degrading enzyme activity suggested an increased nitrogen load and accelerated hepatic ATP turnover.

Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed that the PPAR signaling pathway represented the most significantly enriched pathway for both DEGs and DMs in liver of HUA goslings. This enrichment was reflected by two pivotal DMs, 9-hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid (9-HODE) and 8(S)-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid [8(S)-HETE], endogenous ligands of PPARγ and PPARα, respectively, which were significantly down-regulated, consistent with transcriptional suppression of PPARα/γ signaling (Evans et al., 2024; Yu et al., 1995). A recent study similarly demonstrated that significant suppression of PPARα/γ signaling pathways in hyperuricemic rats (Liu et al., 2024). Overall, these findings suggest a close association between the PPAR signaling pathway and UA metabolism. In the present study, the up-regulation of ACSBG2 and ACSL6 promoted long-chain fatty acyl-CoA synthesis, a process that consumes ATP and generates AMP. This shift in energy utilization was accompanied by a 2.61-fold increase in hepatic AMP levels, positioning AMP as a metabolic hub linking enhanced ATP turnover to purine degradation (Ellis et al., 2010). Moreover, AMP serves as a central node in purine metabolism. Elevated AMPD3 expression in HUA goslings further accelerated the conversion of AMP to inosine monophosphate (IMP), reinforcing purine flux toward downstream degradation, while up-regulated NT5C1B, PNP, and XDH facilitated sequential conversion of IMP to inosine, hypoxanthine, and ultimately UA (Chen et al., 2023). Together, these transcriptional and metabolic alterations indicate accelerated purine degradation flux, and appear to reflect early hepatic metabolic reprogramming under high-protein feeding that precedes overt UA accumulation (Gugliucci, 2025; Jacobs et al., 2025; Liang et al., 2024). Collectively, these findings support an energy–purine axis in which high-protein feeding imposes dual metabolic pressure: increased purine substrate availability and enhanced hepatic ATP turnover. The consequent AMP accumulation and accelerated purine flux appear to represent early hepatic metabolic reprogramming. Crude protein was increased primarily by adjusting corn gluten meal inclusion, and ME, Ca and AP were maintained at comparable levels. Digestible lysine also remained relatively constant (0.94%-0.96%), suggesting that major energy imbalance or lysine limitation was unlikely to be the principal factor driving the observed phenotype. Increasing CP was accompanied by proportional elevations in digestible sulfur-containing amino acids and arginine relative to lysine; however, these shifts appear to reflect inherent changes associated with higher protein supply rather than indicating a specific essential amino acid deficiency pattern.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study established a dietary-induced HUA model in goslings using a 24% CP diet. The high-protein diet impaired growth performance, increased serum UA and the activities of purine-degrading enzymes, and induced hepatic and renal inflammatory injury. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses further revealed that suppression of the PPARα/γ pathway, together with up-regulation of ACSL6 and ACSBG2, was associated with enhanced hepatic ATP turnover, AMP accumulation, and activation of downstream purine degradation pathways, ultimately promoting UA production. These findings suggest that elevated dietary protein supply imposes metabolic pressure on the immature liver, characterized by increased nitrogen load and enhanced ATP turnover, thereby accelerating purine flux and predisposing goslings to hyperuricemia and organ injury. Although graded increases in CP were accompanied by proportional changes in digestible amino acid supply inherent to higher protein inclusion, the present study provides evidence for an energy–purine axis underlying dietary protein-associated HUA and an experimental framework for nutritional strategies for preventing liver dysfunction in poultry.
