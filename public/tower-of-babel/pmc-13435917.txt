Implementation of an international continuing health professions education programme in sports medicine: a process evaluation
Authors: Faten Smiley, H Paul Dijkstra, Abdulaziz Farooq, Evert Verhagen, Femke van Nassau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe and evaluate the implementation and outcomes of a targeted health professions education (HPE) programme. Aspetar Orthopaedic Sports Medicine Hospital’s accredited education programme targets a global health professions audience—those working at the hospital, those employed to provide services at all Qatar’s sports clubs and federations and those from the region and globally.DesignA descriptive process evaluation using routinely collected survey data from accredited continuing professional development (CPD) activities in Aspetar between 2017 and 2023. The evaluation was structured by the RE-AIM framework which evaluates a program across the five dimensions: Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance.ResultsDuring the study period, 420 accredited CPD activities were completed and attended by 50 772 professionals. A total of 13 557 feedback survey responses were collected during the first accreditation cycle of the HPE programme (2017–2023). The HPE programme demonstrated a steady increase in reach over time, received positive effectiveness ratings and demonstrated a steady increase in attendance rate highlighting successful implementation.ConclusionApplying the RE-AIM framework highlighted key strengths in the reach, effectiveness and implementation of the Aspetar HPE programme. Overall reach was satisfactory; effectiveness was evident through perceived improvement in clinical performance and implementation through continuous learning over time. Although not all framework dimensions were applicable to our study, our findings could inform efforts to further improve the HPE programme, serve as a model for similar education programmes elsewhere and bolster healthcare services and patient outcomes in sports medicine.

Introduction

Health professionals in all disciplines, including sports medicine and science, must cope with much new and relevant scientific knowledge to stay updated. One way they keep up to date with the tsunami of new knowledge and skills is by engaging in health professions education (HPE) which includes accredited continuing professional development (CPD) and continuing medical education (CME) activities such as grand rounds, conferences and workshops.1 An effective HPE programme—wherever it is planned, implemented and delivered—should ideally keep health professionals up to date and address practice gaps that are clinically relevant and important to patients. In the USA, the Accreditation Council for CME (ACCME) reported a steady growth in accredited education over 10 years; the number of educational activities and participants has increased yearly since 2010.2 CME has proven effective in areas like cancer detection; one study demonstrated that an educational intervention led to improvements in early cancer detection and preventive services, including an increase in mammography rates3 and drug prescription.4 5 However, the lack of a standardised CME programme undermines the effectiveness and impact of CME.6 Effective CME is based on the identified needs of the target audience, consequently increasing health professionals’ knowledge, changing their clinical behaviour and ultimately improving patient outcomes.7 In many countries, hospitals are still the main providers of CME.8

Sports medicine is a relatively young and multidisciplinary field and includes several health and science disciplines (eg, physiotherapy, surgery, cardiology, dentistry, pharmacy, podiatry and the physician specialty of sport and exercise medicine). Strong evidence advocates the value of sports medicine in preventing chronic conditions such as musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, pulmonary diseases as well as type 2 diabetes mellitus.9 10 Global efforts to increase populations’ physical activity11 further spotlight the value of sports medicine. There is a lack of sports medicine training in medical school core curricula and training during medical residency.12 Therefore, to keep updated and further improve knowledge and skills of health professionals working in sports medicine, high quality accredited continuing education programmes are in high demand.13 In fact, in many countries accredited continuing education is now mandated for re-licensing of health professionals.

The Aspetar HPE programme was developed to enhance HPE in Qatar. Aspetar is a specialised orthopaedic and sports medicine hospital established in 2007 in Qatar. All health professionals working in Qatar’s National Sports Medicine Programme are employed by the hospital. They are involved in providing sports medicine services to sports clubs and federations across the country. As such, there was a need for an accredited HPE programme and, after being recognised as an accredited CPD provider by the Qatar Ministry of Public Health, the hospital launched its accredited HPE programme in June 2016.

With the completion of the first accreditation cycle (2017–2023), an evaluation of its impact and effectiveness is necessary to inform future improvements. This study describes and evaluates the reach, effectiveness and implementation of a specialised orthopaedic and sports medicine hospital’s HPE programme.

Methods

Study design

We performed a descriptive process evaluation, using routinely collected survey data from accredited CPD activities which took place in the hospital between 2017 and 2023. Following the first three elements of Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework, we applied education outcome measures to evaluate the reach, effectiveness and implementation of the HPE programme.14 We did not measure adoption and maintenance as they were not relevant to the aim of the study.

Programme description

Theoretical underpinning of the programme

Continuous improvement, professional development, relevance, reflection and self-assessment are the educational principles underpinning any good education programme—including the HPE programme in Qatar.15 Health professionals are mandated to participate in accredited CPD activities that continuously improve their knowledge and performance, address their identified needs and are relevant to their scope of practice. In addition to attending the CPD activities, they must reflect on the impact of the specific education activity on their practice and self-assess their practice gaps and learning needs.

Development of programme

The Qatar Ministry of Public Health’s Department of Healthcare Professions-Accreditation Section (DHP-AS) launched the CPD Accreditation programme on 7 March 2016.16 The programme requires ‘All healthcare practitioners in part-time or full-time practice in the State of Qatar to complete the annual and CPD cycle specific requirements to maintain their registration and renewal of licensure’.17 During the 2-year CPD cycle, health professionals must complete and document a minimum of 40 credits for accredited live (synchronous) in-person or online group learning activities and an additional 40 credits for asynchronous learning (publishing or reading peer reviewed papers, etc); thus a minimum of 80 credits over a 2-year CPD cycle. Attendance for in-person activities was verified using sign-in sheets for smaller activities and QR code scanning for larger events, whereas attendance for virtual activities was verified through automated attendance reports. Following each activity, the education coordinator submitted attendance records and a memorandum signed by the Chair of the Scientific Planning Committee. Following verification of the submitted documentation, the CPD Unit released participants’ certificates and corresponding CPD credits.

Qatar’s CPD Accreditation programme is designed to be equivalent to the CME accreditation programmes of the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada (RCPSC) and the ACCME.18 19 As such, any DHP CPD activity accredited by a DHP-accredited CPD provider or DHP-AS is recognised as equivalent to RCPSC and ACCME-accredited activities.

Content of the programme

During the first 6 years of the accredited HPE programme, a wide range of activities including conferences, courses, journal clubs, lecture series, symposia, webinars and workshops were delivered. Topics included a range of disciplines related to sports medicine and injury/illness prevention and management, exercise rehabilitation, sports performance and nutrition, psychological aspects of sports and healthcare, shared decision-making in sport, medical education and research (including academic writing and planning good research) and healthcare leadership and management with an emphasis on practical workshops and advanced medical treatments. Across activities, 46% involved male faculty, 25% female faculty and for 29% gender distribution of faculty was not recorded for larger CPD events.

Clinical departments assigned education coordinators and staff interested in designing and delivering CPD activities to be trained in key elements of HPE (including planning, accreditation, delivery and feedback; see online supplemental material).

Theoretical framework for evaluating the programme

The RE-AIM framework was developed 20 years ago to address the translation of evidence to practice20; its applications have since evolved to include evaluating continuing HPE programmes, which reflects the growing recognition of continuing education as a critical component of improving healthcare practice.21

Reach

Reach is defined as the number and representativeness of participants in a programme.14 To determine the reach of the HPE programme, we assessed (1) number of participants earning education credit by profession, (2) number of credits earned, (3) credits awarded for each activity session and (4) credits awarded for each activity overall.

Effectiveness

Effectiveness is defined as the impact of an intervention on important outcomes. The effectiveness of the HPE programme was assessed through participant ratings of learning objectives, relevance to practice and perceived barriers to applying knowledge. This data was collected from the end-of-activity surveys in which participants evaluated whether the overall activity and session-specific learning objectives were met. In addition, participants assess the extent to which they believe the CPD activity will improve their performance as healthcare professionals in terms of medical knowledge, patient management strategies, effectiveness with patients and interprofessional teamwork on a 5-point Likert scale.

Implementation

Implementation was defined as the quality of programme delivery. This was assessed through participant feedback on activity delivery, participant satisfaction, compliance and accreditation.

CPD evaluation survey

Per DHP-AS CPD Activity Accreditation Standards, participants must be allowed to evaluate the overall CPD activity and individual sessions requiring a minimum 50% response rate.22 At the end of each activity, participants receive a survey to evaluate the activity across several parameters. They assess whether the overall and session-specific learning objectives were met (on a 5-point Likert scale), whether the content is balanced and free of bias (on a 5-point Likert scale) and the potential impact of the activity on clinical practice (on a 5-point Likert scale). Moreover, the survey plays a role in planning future CPD activities: participants are requested to suggest topics based on their educational needs, perceived practice gaps and ways to improve the activity.

Data collection

We used routine collected data, originally designed for quality improvement purposes. After each offering of a CPD activity, participants were asked to complete the survey online. An email was sent to them with the survey link followed by at least two reminders. Responses are anonymous and it would take approximately 10–15 min to complete the survey.

Data analysis

All data from the evaluation survey was analysed quantitatively. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. In order to study the reach aspect of RE-AIM, a trend analysis was done to see the progress of number of participants earning CPD credits each year from 2017 to 2018 according to profession and a total of number of credits assigned to participants each year. The effectiveness was evaluated by assessing if the CPD activities met the overall learning objectives, assisted in removing barriers to practice and helped to increase perceived improvement in clinical performance.

Patient and public involvment

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this study as it focused on HPE.

In total, 13 555 survey responses were collected from 420 accredited CPD activity offerings during the first accreditation cycle (2017–2023).

Implementation

The attendance rate of CPD activities has steadily increased over 6 years (figure 4A). The per cent of participants indicating areas of improvement of the HEP programme decreased over time and eventually stabilised (figure 4B). The most selected areas of improvement were: more patient case discussions (47%), more practical sessions (38%) and more case-based presentations (37%) (figure 4C). Participant satisfaction with session-specific elements of the CPD activities was predominantly positive; 95% agreed the overall session was beneficial, 95% agreed the presenter was knowledgeable and 98% agreed the session was interactive (figure 4D).

Discussion

Good implementation is crucial for successful interventions, however, in implementation science, evaluation remains challenging.23 This paper describes the reach, effectiveness, implementation and evolution of an accredited HPE programme in sports medicine in Qatar.

The HPE programme significantly grew and improved since its inception in 2016. Specifically, and based on attendance and number of CPD credits assigned, the programme’s reach expanded. Measuring effectiveness, the content of the CPD activities aligned well with their learning objectives. In addition, participants on average reported perceived improvements in their clinical performance based on CPD activities. While a small percentage of participants indicated perceived barriers to changing clinical practice in response to attending the CPD activities, these barriers decreased over the years. As for the programme’s implementation, the attendance rate of CPD activities increased over the years. The percentage of participants indicating what could improve the programme decreased and plateaued.

The attendance rate of the CPD activities showed a steady increase over the study period with a notable surge in 2020, likely due to the introduction of online activities in March 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic. The reduction in perceived barriers over time suggests that repeated exposure to CPD activities may facilitate long-term knowledge retention and integration into clinical practice. This could be due to repeated exposure to CPD activities over time, which likely increased familiarity and facilitated the integration of acquired knowledge into clinical practice. Most barriers did not significantly change over time, possibly because the survey was anonymous and completed by different participants over the years. However, it is interesting to note the fluctuations in perceived patient compliance issues and the constant lack of opportunity for patients; the lack of patients likely refers specifically to a shortage of ‘registered athlete patients’, compared with ‘private patients’, thereby limiting the application of sport-specific knowledge. This finding is consistent with previous research, which identified patient characteristics as a major barrier to implementing CME knowledge.24 Future evaluation should continue to explore these trends in future qualitative research. Recognising and addressing barriers to education leads to more effective continuing education programmes and could ultimately improve clinical performance (and patient outcomes).25

A scoping review by Goodall categorised the broad impacts of CPD which included improvement in knowledge and practice change.26 Our study findings demonstrated an overall agreement among participants regarding self-reported perceived improvements in clinical practice in terms of medical knowledge, patient management strategies, effectiveness in clinical skills, communication and identification of different patient values and needs. Over time, these perceived improvements became more evident, aligning with previous studies which show that CPD interventions can improve professional practice. Specifically, those more tailored to the specific needs of health professionals and their clinical settings tend to have a stronger impact on practice.5 This reflects the mission of Aspetar’s HPE programme. Focused on improving clinical practice, our results support the importance of implementing HPE programmes tailor-made for the specific context, including patient profiles and clinical needs.

Highlighted by participants, the top three areas to improve the HPE programme were more practical sessions, more case-based presentations and more patient case discussions. While workshops were the most common activity format, participants strongly indicated that they should incorporate more case-based presentations and patient case discussions. This finding aligns with previous studies, which indicate that team-based and case-based learning methods were more effective learning methods.27

Our study has several strengths. Data were collected over several years and across multiple professions, thus providing a comprehensive evaluation of a multidisciplinary HPE programme in sports medicine. Not only did our participants include multiple professions in sports medicine, but they also practised in different settings—from hospital-based clinics, operating theatres and sports dentistry clinics to field-based clinics at Qatar’s clubs and federations and sporting events like the FIFA World Cup. Furthermore, our data reflects the views of international health professions participants, bolstered by online or hybrid live delivery of CPD activities. Consequently, the evaluation results encompass a wide range of data, offering valuable insights into the programme’s impact over time.

Limitations include the use of retrospective data, originally collected for quality improvement purposes. The fact that participants are required to complete the evaluation survey before receiving the CPD credits may influence the responses and affect the objectivity of the results. In addition, the study outcomes relied on self-reported survey data. As a result, the reported improvements in medical knowledge, clinical skills, patient management and communication should be interpreted with caution, as self-reported measures are susceptible to bias and may overstate the programme’s actual effectiveness. Furthermore, the findings of this study are, to a large extent and despite reaching participants from all over the world, specific to the HPE programme at Aspetar. This limits the generalisability of our results to other settings and contexts. The RE-AIM framework was selected to guide the evaluation, as it is commonly used in sports medicine research.28 29 Given the objectives of this study and the available data, the analysis focused on the reach, adoption and implementation elements of the framework. The effectiveness and maintenance elements were not evaluated, as they were beyond the scope of the current study. Yet, the RE-AIM framework provided guidance for the data collection and analyses.

The current evaluation process follows an iterative feedback model in which data is collected after each CPD activity offering; this leads to large data sets, which can be difficult to manage effectively. Although the Likert scale is a common method to collect survey responses,30 its use is subjective and prone to bias, hence we aim to enhance the programme by addressing these data limitations and refining the evaluation methods used. A key area for improvement involves identifying the best practices for evaluation, particularly in how they benefit participants. We are planning qualitative research to provide an in-depth exploration of participants’ perspectives on a HPE programme and its perceived impact on clinical practice.

Conclusion

This paper describes and evaluates the reach, effectiveness and implementation of a health professions sports medicine education programme—planned, accredited and delivered in Qatar but aimed at a global target audience. Applying the RE-AIM framework, the reach, effectiveness and implementation of the HPE programme highlighted several positive outcomes. These findings suggest that structured, multidisciplinary and accredited HPE programmes could serve as a model for similar institutions worldwide. Alignment of the education programme with the clinicians’ learning needs supports the development of their knowledge and skills, which may bolster healthcare services and ultimately patient outcomes in sports medicine through athlete-centred care.
