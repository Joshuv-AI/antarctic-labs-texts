Equipercentile Equating of Scores From Common Patient‐Reported Outcome Measures of Role, Social, and Emotional Functioning in Patients With Cancer
Authors: M Rothmund‐Grenier, J M Pilz, L Schlosser, J I Arraras, M Groenvold, B Holzner, M Van Leeuwen, M A Petersen, H Schmidt, T Young, M Rose, D Cella, J M Giesinger, the EORTC Quality of Life Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPatient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) are fundamental in evaluating cancer treatments within clinical research and practice. The existence of various PROMs that assess similar health domains presents challenges for comparing or aggregating study results and data. Score linking is a well‐established method for addressing this issue by converting scores from the metric of one PROM to another.AimsTo provide equipercentile equating of psychosocial scales from commonly used PROMs in cancer patients to facilitate data pooling and comparisons.MethodsCancer patients with diverse diagnoses from five European countries completed the EORTC CAT Core, EORTC QLQ‐C30, FACT‐G, SF‐36, and HADS Emotional, Role, and Social functioning (EF; RF; SF) scales. For scales with Spearman correlations exceeding 0.70, scores were linked using equipercentile equating as provided by the R package “equate”. To evaluate linking precision, Standardized Mean Absolute Errors (SMAEs) were calculated.ResultsA total of 945 cancer patients were included in the analysis (mean age 58.4 years; 54.7% male). Bidirectional conversion tables were established for three pairs of SF/RF scales and 10 pairs of EF scales. Overall, linking was possible for most scales from the EORTC Measures, SF‐36, and HADS, whereas the FACT‐G allowed linking only for its Emotional Well‐Being (EWB) scale.ConclusionsThe findings suggest greater linking ability for EF scales compared to RF and SF scales, which seem to be more heterogeneous in terms of content. The established crosswalks support the combined evaluation of data from different PROMs.Keywords: emotional functioning, EORTC CAT, equipercentile equating, FACT‐G, HADS, patient‐reported outcome measures, QLQ‐C30, role functioning, SF‐36, social functioning

Background

Patient‐reported outcomes (PROs) provide essential information about patients' perspectives on the burden of cancer and its treatment, making them a vital component of clinical outcome assessment, that is, of “how the patient feels, functions, or survives” [1]. In the context of clinical trials, PRO measures (PROMs) facilitate the assessment of treatment benefits and tolerability and commonly evaluate health‐related quality of life (HRQOL), conceptualised in a multidimensional framework that includes psychosocial domains such as role function (RF), social function (SF), and emotional function (EF) [2].

While psychosocial domains are included in the most frequently used PROMs in oncology, variations exist in the specific constructs they measure. The EORTC QLQ‐C30 [3], the FACT‐G [4], and the SF‐36 [5] assess the broad construct of EF or well‐being. In contrast, other measures offer specific assessments of psychosocial domains such as the HADS [6] assessing anxiety and depression, and the PROMIS measurement system, offering additionally a specific measure for anger [7].

Regarding social health, there is considerable variation in measurement approaches, which results in differences in the content of specific measures [8]. Commonly assessed social domains include RF and SF, evaluated separately, for instance, in the EORTC measures and the SF‐36. Here, RF emphasises the ability to complete tasks and meet demands [8], while SF pertains to social interactions and relationships [9]. In the PROMIS measurement system, for example, the concept of participating in social roles and activities offers a combined perspective on the SF and RF domains, as previously shown [8]. Although each measure may be beneficial in different contexts, the variety of instruments complicates the comparison of data across various studies, such as for meta‐analyses or data pooling. To enhance the comparability of scores from different scales, linking methods have been introduced that facilitate score conversion between emotional and social health scales [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17] from diverse PROMs (please note that in this manuscript, we use the terms 'equating' and 'linking' interchangeably).

The analysis presented in this article is part of an ongoing project by the EORTC Quality of Life Group that explores linking the EORTC CAT Core to other commonly used PROMs in cancer research based on equipercentile equating. Previously, the linking algorithms for PROMs related to physical function have been published [18].

In this article, we complement a previous qualitative content analysis [8, 19] of the EF, SF, and RF measures included in this study and evaluate linking algorithms for (A) the RF and SF domains of the EORTC CAT Core, EORTC QLQ‐C30, SF‐36, and FACT‐G, and (B) the EF domains of these four PROMs and the HADS.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Sample

The present study used a single‐group design. This means that one sample of patients was recruited, in which each patient was asked to complete all comparator measures of interest. This allows to compare predicted scores based on established crosswalks with observed scores on the target measure for each patient, to evaluate the linking precision.

This study is based on the same patient sample as our previous analysis of equipercentile equating of physical functioning scales [18]. We recruited a sample of cancer patients with mixed diagnoses from five centres across Europe: Austria (Medical University of Innsbruck), Germany (Medical Faculty of Martin Luther University Halle‐Wittenberg), the Netherlands (Netherlands Cancer Institute), Spain (University Hospital of Navarra), and the United Kingdom (Mount Vernon Cancer Centre). To achieve a heterogeneous sample, any cancer patient was eligible for the study, regardless of treatment status, if aged 18 years or older, had provided written informed consent, had sufficient command of the country's primary language, and demonstrated no cognitive impairment that would interfere with questionnaire completion. Patients were invited to participate in the study during a hospital visit or via email. The PROMs were administered electronically in both settings using the software CHES [20]. Data were included in the present analysis if patients completed at least two of the evaluated EF scales or at least two of the assessed RF or SF scales.

This study was approved by the Medical University of Innsbruck's ethics committee (AN20140368) and was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice and the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The PROMs administered to the patients included scales for EF, SF, and RF from the EORTC QLQ‐C30, the EORTC CAT Core, the FACT‐G, and the SF‐36. Additionally, we included the widely used HADS questionnaire, which assesses anxiety and depression. Detailed information on the included PROMs is provided in Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

To investigate the association between the scales considered for linking, we calculated Spearman rank correlation coefficients and examined the corresponding scatter plots. In previous studies, correlations exceeding thresholds of 0.70–0.75 were deemed indicative of the appropriateness of linking PROM scales [12, 24].

The R package “equate” [25] was used to establish equipercentile equating between the comparator measures. This approach aligns scores of different measures based on their percentile rank. Thus, it predicts scores on the target measures based on the assumption that a score on measure A corresponds to the score on measure B with the same percentile rank. To filter out random sampling and measurement noise, we smoothed the score distribution using polynomial log‐linear modelling. Next, we ran simulations (parametric bootstrapping) on this smoothed pattern to calculate standard errors, that is standard deviations over simulations for each score point. Lower standard errors indicate higher precision at the respective score point.

To evaluate the precision of the linking procedure, we calculated the mean absolute error (MAE), defined as the mean of the absolute differences between the predicted and the observed score, and the standardised MAE (SMAE), defined as the MAE divided by the standard deviation (SD) of the target measure in the total sample. Smaller MAEs and SMAEs indicate better precision. This was done in the total sample and separately for patient groups defined by sex, age (above or below 70 years), and treatment status (on or off treatment) to allow for a comparison of linking precision across patient groups.

Based on the described equating procedure, we produced linking tables to convert scores between the included measures. However, no conversion tables are provided between two scales of the same questionnaire (e.g., SF‐36 RP and SF‐36 RE), which are usually not assessed independently. Since the EORTC QLQ‐C30 items are part of the EORTC CAT Core item bank, their scores can be inverted directly via the underlying item response theory (IRT) model [9], and therefore, no conversion tables are provided between these two measures.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Data from 945 patients were available for analysis. The mean age was 58.4 years (SD = 13.4; range: 18–90 years), with 54.7% of patients being male. The most common diagnoses were breast cancer (10.8%), thyroid cancer (10.2%), and lymphoma (8.8%). At the time of assessment, 73.0% of patients were receiving anti‐cancer treatment. Descriptive Statistics and Correlations for PROMs Measuring Role and Social Function

The EORTC CAT Core RF T‐scores ranged from 15.0 to 59.0, with a mean score of 43.7 (SD = 11.5). The EORTC CAT Core SF T‐score ranged from 16.0 to 58.0, with a mean score of 43.6 (SD = 10.4). Example of Applying the Score Conversion Tables

The following example demonstrates the use of the linking algorithms. Suppose patient X completes the EORTC CAT Core EF domain and receives a score of 45 points on the T‐score metric of this measure. Based on the linking table provided in this study (refer to Supporting Information S2: Table 4 for this example), we can predict that patient X would score 68.8 on the 0‐100 metric of the SF‐36 MH scale, with a standard error of 1.4 points. Equally, we can assume that another patient Y scoring 69 on the SF‐36 MH scale, has a comparable level of EF as patient X.

Discussion

Our findings suggest that linking EF scores across most pairwise combinations of the EORTC measures, SF‐36, FACT‐G, and HADS is achievable. However, the linking possibilities for RF and SF are limited to the EORTC measures and the SF‐36 and exhibit partly higher SMAE values than those observed for the EF scales. These results indicate that, overall, linking between EF scales is more feasible than linking between SF and RF scales.

Our quantitative findings regarding the strength of the association between scales correspond with our previous qualitative work on the content assessed by the included PROMs. For EF, the items of the EORTC CAT Core, EORTC QLQ‐C30, FACT‐G, HADS, and SF‐36 primarily focus on anxiety and depression [19], two concepts known to be strongly correlated [26], which clearly overlap in terms of diagnostic criteria [27], and are both key components of the broader concept of general distress. The latter may be considered conceptually equivalent to EF.

For SF and RF, the content addressed by the measures is more heterogeneous [8]. The FACT‐G, for instance, does not focus on actual SF but evaluates environmental factors, such as social support. The EORTC measures and the SF‐36 assess similar concepts and share the distinction between RF and SF, with the SF‐36 providing additional detail by distinguishing RF as impacted by physical or emotional burdens. The EORTC CAT Core RF scores can be linked only to the SF‐36 RP scale, consistent with previous findings that, within the EORTC measurement system, RF is closely associated with physical function [28]. The SF scores from the EORTC CAT Core can be linked to the respective SF‐36 scales, given their conceptual similarity [8].

In a previous analysis, we presented linking algorithms for the physical function domain of the EORTC CAT Core, the EORTC QLQ‐C30, the SF‐36, and PROMIS. We generally observed higher bivariate correlations and higher linking precision between the physical function scales than between the psychosocial scales in the current analysis, where SMAEs were slightly above the previously used threshold of 0.5 [18]. This possibly reflects higher reliability and the narrower content of measures assessing physical functioning, which mainly includes items related to mobility and self‐care [29]. While it is difficult to compare them directly, we hypothesize that the psychosocial scales in this study are conceptually broader, that is cover more diverse aspects of a concept in a single domain [8, 19, 29]. The broader content of a domain may enhance content validity but could also explain the lower reliability of such scales [30], leading to lower bivariate correlations. In the RF and SF domains, there is also greater diversity in the concepts assessed by commonly used PROMs in oncology. This is evident not only in the FACT‐G, which focuses on social support, but also in the PROMIS measurement system, which, for example, combines RF and SF into a single domain.

Given the usefulness of linking algorithms for comparing study results, data pooling, and meta‐analyses, the literature is increasingly reporting linking algorithms for commonly used PROMs in oncology. Previously, linking algorithms have been established for several psychosocial PROMIS measures, including a study by Choi et al., which developed linking algorithms for the depression measures BDI‐II, CES‐D, and PHQ‐9 to the PROMIS depression scale using an IRT model and equipercentile equating [12]. Furthermore, Hardouin et al. [31] established linking procedures based on an IRT model for the PROMIS‐29 measure and the SF‐36, including linking the SF‐36 MH scale to the PROMIS Anxiety and Depression scales and the SF‐36 SF scale to PROMIS Social Participation. Additionally, relying on an IRT model, Huang et al. [32] established linking for the HADS, GAD, and PHQ‐9.

In our analysis, we relied on equipercentile equating as the preferred method due to its specific strengths, as discussed previously [18]. In short, equipercentile equating depends solely on scale scores (rather than on item‐level data, as in IRT models) and does not make specific assumptions about the score distribution, making this linking more flexible for application in clinical research. Furthermore, it can be easily implemented without relying on advanced statistical models.

Our study provides linking algorithms that enable bidirectional conversion of scores between two metrics, offering significant flexibility for comparing or combining scores. Another common approach to linking scores involves creating a “common metric” for different PROMs. This can be the established metric of a specific PROM to which the other measures are converted [12], which is similar to our approach but links in only one direction. Alternatively, a new metric can be generated from all included PRO scales [33, 34]. While common metrics may reduce complexity, they also present disadvantages. For instance, creating common metrics alters the scoring algorithm of at least one instrument, particularly if IRT‐based scoring is introduced for PROMs that use a sum score, or if the item parameters of an existing IRT model are modified to fit the common metric model. Additionally, when pooling datasets, it may be most appropriate to convert scores to the metric of the PROM that represents the largest portion of the data, in order to rely on as much original data as possible. Therefore, it is valuable to have linking algorithms for two PROMs that function bidirectionally. We want to point out that in meta‐analyses, using our conversion tables might require score interpolation, as the tables only show scores a patient can achieve on a specific PROM, while average scores from patient groups may fall between those values. Additionally, the use of these conversion tables is limited by the measurement range of the PROMs being compared.

We would like to note that the future availability of multiple linking algorithms for a specific pair of PRO scales is beneficial, as it will allow, for example, sensitivity analyses when aggregating trial data by evaluating the robustness of score conversion.

Study Limitations

A limitation of our study is that our assessment of how well linking algorithms perform across different patient groups in terms of SMAE included only groups defined by sex, age, and treatment status. While previous literature suggests that linking algorithms for depression measures demonstrate invariance across patient groups [16], further empirical validation is necessary. Generally, linking the RF and SF domains resulted in larger standard errors, making it less precise than the EF domain and the previously published physical function domain [18].

Clinical Implications

The variety of PROMs available to measure psychosocial domains in cancer research and practice allows selection of the most appropriate measure for a specific setting and purpose; however, it also poses challenges when comparing results across different clinical studies. The linking algorithms established in this analysis provide an easy‐to‐use method for converting scores obtained from one PROM to the metric of another, enabling, for example, the comparative evaluation of a larger body of literature related to a specific intervention. Furthermore, our analysis offers quantitative information on the similarity of psychosocial concepts measured by the different PROMs, thereby adding further evidence to our previous qualitative analysis of the conceptual overlap of such measures [8, 18].

Conclusions

Our study is the first to present linking algorithms that make scores from the psychosocial scales of commonly used PROMs in oncology comparable. Equipercentile equating offers an easily applicable linking approach that does not require item‐level data and preserves the original scoring algorithm of the PROMs. This will facilitate the combination and comparison of PRO data from clinical studies or real‐world settings that were collected using different PROMs to assess EF, RF, or SF.
