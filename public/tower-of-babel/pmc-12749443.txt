Association of the non-HDL cholesterol to HDL cholesterol ratio with possible sarcopenic obesity in Chinese adults
Authors: Wenbin Guo, Yuqing Lin, Min Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sarcopenic obesity (SO) is a major public health concern linked to lipid metabolism disorders. This study investigated the association between the non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHHR) and possible SO prevalence in China using data from the 2015 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Among 3,320 participants(21.02% men; median age 66.00 years [interquartile range, IQR 62.00–71.00]), multivariate logistic regression revealed that individuals in the highest NHHR tertile had a 66% increased risk of possible SO compared to the lowest tertile. Restricted cubic spline analysis identified a significant nonlinear relationship (p < 0.05). A two-piecewise regression model showed that for NHHR < 3.262, the adjusted odds ratio (OR) for possible SO was 2.27 (95% CI, 1.48–3.45; p < 0.001), whereas for NHHR ≥ 3.262, the OR was 0.97 (95% CI, 0.78–1.21; p = 0.78). This finding suggests that NHHR could serve as a novel and convenient biomarker for possible SO risk stratification; maintaining an NHHR below 3.262 may be a valuable target for preventing possible SO in the middle-aged and elderly Chinese population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29858-7.

Introduction

Sarcopenic obesity (SO) is defined as the coexistence of obesity and reduced muscle mass/function. It is diagnosed by combining the criteria for sarcopenia, based on appendicular skeletal muscle (ASM), with the criteria for obesity, defined by body mass index (BMI)1. SO is associated with diminished quality of life, disability, and increased mortality risk, and is also linked to a higher risk of obesity-related and age-related diseases2. Current studies suggest that approximately 11% of the global elderly population suffers from SO, with this prevalence increasing sharply beyond the age of 70 years3. Notably, A study demonstrated that 18% of older adults with obesity met the diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia as defined by the European Working Group on sarcopenia in older people4. SO is characterized by a state of systemic energy burden, in which the interplay between sites of fat storage and sites of force generation is thought to contribute to the initiation and progression of the condition. However, the underlying cellular and molecular mechanisms are complex and not yet fully elucidated. Current research points to a central role for several interconnected biological pathways. Key among these are chronic low-grade inflammation and insulin resistance, which create a catabolic environment detrimental to muscle and anabolic for fat. This is compounded by age-related factors such as reduced muscle satellite cell activity, hormonal imbalances (e.g., decreased anabolic hormones), and mitochondrial dysfunction. These underlying mechanisms, often influenced by lifestyle and genetic factors, collectively contribute to the onset and progression of SO5. To date, SO remains largely untreatable, with no targeted pharmacological therapies available.

Individuals with obesity frequently exhibit dyslipidemia, characterized by elevated triglycerides, reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), increased non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (Non-HDL-C), and the presence of small dense low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (sdLDL-C) particles6. The Non-HDL-C to HDL-C ratio (NHHR) is an emerging lipid marker with significant potential in predicting the risk of various diseases, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cardiovascular-renal-metabolic syndrome, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and depressive symptoms7–11. Substantial evidence indicates NHHR surpasses individual lipid components in predicting cardiovascular outcomes12. In a cohort of 7,504 adults with obesity, NHHR exhibited a significant U-shaped association with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, suggesting its monitoring could help mitigate risk in this population13. The accumulation of toxic fatty acid intermediates within muscle cells, driven by excess lipids, disrupts insulin signaling and triggers a detrimental cycle. This insulin resistance impairs glucose uptake, effectively starving the muscle of energy, which compromises its function and can lead to degradation. In turn, this metabolic failure fosters a vicious cycle of further fat storage within the muscle, accelerating the progression of metabolic disorders14,15. These findings suggest that NHHR may serve as a valuable marker for predicting diseases associated with metabolic dysfunction. Recent cross-sectional studies in Chinese and American adults have linked NHHR to sarcopenia. Sun CB et al.16 used a comprehensive definition of sarcopenia (including muscle mass and strength or physical performance) but was limited to participants aged 50 and older, questioning the generalizability of their findings to younger populations. In contrast, Yang X et al.17 defined sarcopenia based solely on muscle mass, omitting the critical component of muscle function. Furthermore, their results may be compromised by an imbalanced sample size and significant age disparities between the sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic groups. However, to our knowledge, no studies have yet reported on the relationship between NHHR and the increased risk of SO in the Chinese population. Establishing NHHR as a novel marker is crucial for elucidating pathogenic mechanisms, predicting, and treating SO.

This study aims to comprehensively investigate the association between NHHR and the prevalence of possible SO using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). We analyzed possible SO prevalence based on the 2015 wave of data, which included measurements of NHHR and other relevant covariates, and subsequently performed a stratified analysis. The findings may contribute to a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms linking lipid metabolic dysfunction and body composition, facilitate the early identification of high-risk individuals and support the development of tailored intervention strategies.

Methods

Data and sample sources

CHARLS is a nationally representative cohort of Chinese adults aged 45 years and older at baseline. It employs a multistage stratified sampling design to collect biennial data on demographics, health status, healthcare utilization, physical measurements, and blood samples. Data on physical performance, including handgrip strength and gait speed, were collected by CHARLS interviewers who underwent standardized training and followed strict, uniform protocols for equipment and procedures, as detailed in the CHARLS 2015 Biomarker Questionnaire. A more detailed CHARLS survey design has been published in a previous literature18. This cross-sectional study received approval from Peking University’s Biomedical Ethics Committee, with all participants providing written informed consent. From the 2015 cohort, we excluded 17,791 individuals due to: (1) missing sarcopenia/obesity status; (2) missing data of NHHR; and (3) incomplete demographic or blood test records. The final cross-sectional analysis comprised 3,320 individuals. The detailed flowchart of the study is presented in Fig. 1. Assessment of sarcopenia obesity

Sarcopenia was defined using the recommended diagnostic algorithm of the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia(AWGS) 201919, requiring low appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) plus low handgrip strength and/or low gait speed. “possible sarcopenia” is defined as either low muscle strength or low physical performance only. We identified 2 dichotomous variables, namely sarcopenia and possible sarcopenia. ASM was estimated using a validated equation for Chinese populations: ASM = 0.193 × Weight(kg) + 0.107 × Height(cm) − 4.157 × sex (males = 1, females = 2) − 0.037 × age − 2.63120. The appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI) was calculated as ASMI (kg/m²) = ASM/Height (m)², with low muscle mass defined by sex-specific 20th percentile cutoffs (ASMI < 7.0 kg/m² in males; <5.34 kg/m² in females)21. The measurements of handgrip strength and gait speed were based on a previous literature22. Handgrip strength and gait speed measurements followe standardized protocols, applying AWGS 2019 criteria: low handgrip strength as < 28 kg (men) or < 18 kg (women), low gait speed as < 1.0 m/s19. Obesity is defined by WHO Asian criteria (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m², BMI = weight [kg]/height [m]²)23. Possible SO was defined as the coexistence of possible sarcopenia, which was identified by either low handgrip strength (< 28 kg for men; <18 kg for women) or slow gait speed (< 1.0 m/s), and obesity (BMI ≥ 25.0 kg/m²). Based on the presence of possible sarcopenia and obesity, participants were classified into four body composition phenotypes: (1) possible sarcopenia: met criteria for possible sarcopenia but not obesity; (2) obesity: met criteria for obesity but not possible sarcopenia; (3) possible SO: met criteria for possible sarcopenia and obesity; and (4) non-possible sarcopenia and non-obesity (control group): did not meet definition for obesity or possible sarcopenia.

Definition of NHHR

NHHR was calculated as the ratio of non-HDL-C (mg/dl) to HDL-C (mg/dl), where non-HDL-C was determined by subtracting HDL-C (mg/dl) from total cholesterol (TC, mg/dl)24.

Potential covariates

Covariates comprised age, sex, height, weight, marital status, education, smoke status, drinking status, BMI, ASMI, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, heart disease, chronic lung disease, cancer, stroke, and blood biomarkers. The covariates were defined in line with a previous study22. The operational definitions for all covariates are detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

Statistical analysis

In this cross-sectional study, a complete-case analysis was performed, excluding participants with any missing data in covariates, outcome variables, or the lipid measures required for NHHR calculation. No data imputation was used. Continuous variables were summarized using mean and standard deviation or median (interquartile range, IQR), while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed data, differences among groups were compared using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons if the overall ANOVA was significant. For non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test with Bonferroni correction for pairwise comparisons if the overall test was significant. Pearson χ² or Fisher’s exact tests were employed for categorical data. Logistic regression models evaluated NHHR and SO associations across three adjustments: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (age, sex, education, marital status, smoke, drinking), and Model 3 (additional adjustment for hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, heart diseases, chronic lung diseases, stroke, cancer, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, white blood cell, mean corpuscular volume, platelets, C-reactive protein, triglycerides, serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, uric acid, cystatin C). Variance inflation factors (VIFs) assessed multicollinearity (> 2.0 excluded). This process resulted in the exclusion of several variables, including BMI, ASMI, and the comprehensive lipid profile (Cholesterol, High Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol, Low Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol, Triglycerides), due to their high collinearity with other anthropometric and body composition measures(Supplemental Table S3). Additionally, we assessed for nonlinear associations between NHHR and SO using restricted cubic spline regression with three knots. To investigate potential threshold effects, a two-piece logistic regression model was used. The optimal inflection point for NHHR was determined by an iterative grid search process, selecting the value that minimized the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Subgroup analyses were stratified by age (< 60/≥60 years), sex, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, cancer, heart disease, and stroke status. All analyses used R 4.2.2, with p < 0.05 defining significance. Statistical analyses were conducted using the following packages: broom (1.0.8), data.Table (1.17.6), dplyr (1.1.4), forestplot (3.1.7), ggrcs (0.4.3), haven (2.5.5), purrr (1.0.4), rcssci (0.4.0), rms (8.0–0), scitb (0.2.1), segmented (2.1–4.1), stringr (1.5.1), tidyr (1.3.1).

Results

According to the AWGS 2019 criteria, the prevalence of SO in our cohort was very low (0.029%, n = 1). This limited number of cases precluded a meaningful statistical analysis of the association between SO and NHHR. Therefore, to investigate this relationship, we used “possible SO” as the outcome variable for our primary analysis.

Characteristics of study population

The baseline characteristics of this study participants are presented in Tables 1 and 2. A total of 3,320 individuals were included in the final analysis, with an average age of 66.00 years (IQR: 62.00–71.00), including 78.98% female. Based on body composition phenotypes, participants were categorized into four groups: possible sarcopenia group (n = 955, 28.76%), obesity group (n = 629, 18.95%), possible SO group (n = 583, 17.56%), and control group (n = 1153, 34.73%). We compared demographic characteristics, clinical characteristics, and blood biomarkers for participants across these groups. Most demographic characteristics, clinical characteristics, and blood biomarkers differed significantly among the groups (all p < 0.05). However, no statistically significant differences were observed in the distribution of smoke, cancer, and blood urea nitrogen among the groups.

Association between NHHR and possible SO

The results indicated a robust association between higher NHHR and an elevated likelihood of possible SO. This association was significant in the unadjusted model (Model 1) [OR (95%CI): 1.41 (1.27–1.56), p < 0.001] and the minimally adjusted model (Model 2) [OR (95%CI): 1.39 (1.25–1.54), p < 0.001], which controlled for age, sex, education level, marital status, smoke, and drinking. Importantly, the association remained stable in the fully adjusted model (Model 3) [OR (95%CI): 1.24 (1.10–1.38), p < 0.001]. Furthermore, after transforming NHHR from a continuous to a categorical variable (tertiles), participants in the highest NHHR tertile exhibited a 66% higher risk of possible SO compared to the lowest tertile [OR (95CI%): 1.66 (1.25–2.20), p < 0.001]. Similarly, those in the middle tertile showed a 51% increased risk [OR (95CI%): 1.51 (1.17–1.96), p < 0.001] (Table 3).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis revealed a varied association between NHHR and possible SO across subgroups (Fig. 3). Significant associations were observed across age, sex, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and heart diseases (all p < 0.05). Specifically, NHHR remained associated with possible SO in those both with (OR = 1.54, 95% CI 1.15–2.07, P < 0.01) and without dyslipidemia (OR = 1.37, 95% CI 1.23–1.54, P < 0.001), though dyslipidemia didn’t significantly modify this (P for interaction = 0.39). In contrast, the association between NHHR and possible SO was only significant in those participants without diabetes, cancer, or stroke (p < 0.05). Moreover, for age, sex, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, cancer, heart diseases, and stroke, no significant interactions were observed (P for interaction > 0.05), suggesting the positive association between NHHR and possible SO was not significantly modified by these conditions.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the relationship between NHHR levels and the likelihood of possible SO in the Chinese population.

After adjusting for potential confounding factors, a positive relationship between NHHR and possible SO risk was observed. Each unit increase in NHHR was associated with a 24% increase in possible SO risk (OR, 1.24; 95% CI, 1.11–1.39). Further dose–response analysis confirmed a nonlinear association between NHHR and possible SO. Our findings identified a significant threshold at an NHHR of 3.262 within this relationship. Specifically, NHHR was positively associated with the incidence of possible SO when levels were below 3.262. In patients without diabetes, NHHR was significantly associated with possible SO, whereas in patients with diabetes, this association was attenuated, suggesting that other metabolic factors may play a more dominant role. This indicates that diabetes may mask or alter the effect of NHHR on possible SO through other mechanisms, such as insulin resistance and inflammation25. A similar pattern was observed for cancer and stroke: the NHHR-possible SO link was significant in patients without these conditions but not in those affected, again indicating that other pathophysiological factors may be more influential. Our findings provide new insights into how NHHR may positively influence possible SO in middle-aged and older Chinese population, highlighting the potential value of NHHR as a simple indicator for identifying individuals at higher risk for possible SO.

The low prevalence of SO observed in this study may be largely attributed to two key factors. Firstly, we strictly adhered to the “AND” logic diagnostic criteria of AWGS 2019, which requires the concurrent presence of low muscle mass and impaired muscle function, thereby inherently limiting the number of confirmed cases. Secondly, the limitations of the CHARLS database cannot be overlooked; its formula-based estimation of muscle mass may have systematically underestimated the true prevalence of low muscle mass, while the use of BMI to define obesity could have overlooked individuals with abnormally elevated body fat percentage. Therefore, our findings should be considered a conservative estimate, highlighting the urgent need for future research to incorporate more precise body composition measurement techniques.

The pathogenesis of SO involves multifactorial mechanisms driven by dynamic interplays among skeletal muscle atrophy, adipose tissue dysfunction, and endocrine alterations5,26. Dyslipidemia critically contributes to its progression. Lipid metabolic dysfunction leads to excess fat infiltrating skeletal muscle, causing intramuscular fat accumulation. This increases intramyocellular lipid content and ectopic storage, resulting in myosteatosis, insulin resistance, and dysfunction27. Meanwhile, These lipids induce mitochondrial dysfunction by impairing organelle biogenesis and quality control28. Mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by reduced β-oxidation and increased reactive oxygen species, creates lipotoxicity and insulin resistance29, while also increasing pro-inflammatory myokines that induce muscle dysfunction via autocrine or paracrine mechanisms. Through endocrine pathways, these myokines exacerbate adipose tissue inflammation and contribute to systemic inflammation30, establishing a vicious cycle that perpetuates SO. Similarly, our results indicated that the association between NHHR and possible SO was significant regardless of dyslipidemia status. In patients with dyslipidemia, the increase in possible SO risk associated with NHHR appeared more pronounced, although this interaction was not statistically significant.

As an accessible and inexpensive lipid marker, NHHR could serve as a practical biomarker for possible SO screening in primary care, especially in resource-limited settings. Its use provides a cost-effective means for early identification of at-risk individuals. Consistent with previous studies, our findings indicated that individuals with higher NHHR levels were more correlated with possible SO. This association remained significant even after adjusting for various demographic, lifestyle, and clinical variables, suggesting that the association between NHHR and possible SO is not fully explained by the measured confounders. Notably, a previous cross-sectional study of 8,601 adults (average age of 39.94 ± 11.45 years) from the United States reported that, after full adjustment, each one-unit rise in NHHR was linked to a 76% increased likelihood of SO. The study also identified a non-linear relationship with a breakpoint at 3.36, where NHHR significantly raised risk below this threshold but had no significant effect above it31. Our results are consistent with these findings, as we also identified a non-linear relationship between NHHR and possible SO with a similar inflection point at 3.262. Specifically, NHHR was significantly associated with a higher risk of possible SO below this threshold, but this association was not significant above it. The NHHR inflection point for predicting possible SO risk in our Chinese cohort (3.262) was slightly lower than the 3.36 cutoff reported by Liu et al.31 in an American population. This minor discrepancy may stem from several factors: fundamental differences in racial and demographic characteristics, varying diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia and obesity, and differences in adjusted covariates or comorbidity prevalence. Despite this, the close proximity of these values, derived from independent populations, strengthens the robustness of NHHR as a valuable biomarker and suggests a threshold near 3.2–3.4 may be broadly applicable for identifying high-risk individuals. The relationship between NHHR and sarcopenia has also been previously explored. For instance, an analysis of 9,012 adults from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2011–2018 revealed a significant non-linear association between NHHR and low muscle mass, suggesting its potential as a predictive marker32. Another cross-sectional study found that sarcopenic adults in the US had significantly higher NHHR levels than non-sarcopenic adults, an association independent of sex, income, education, smoking, and race17. However, a contrasting study in the Chinese population reported a non-linear relationship between NHHR and sarcopenia risk, with an adjusted OR of 0.65 for NHHR < 4.4, but no significant association for NHHR ≥ 4.416. In our study, a lower NHHR (< 3.262) was a risk indicator for possible SO (OR = 2.27), whereas in the study by Sun CB et al.16, a lower NHHR (< 4.4) was protective against sarcopenia (OR = 0.65). This apparent discrepancy, despite both studies utilizing the CHARLS database, may be primarily explained by their different endpoints: our study focused on possible SO, whereas the prior study investigated sarcopenia. This distinction is critical, as SO is pathophysiologically distinct from sarcopenia alone. In sarcopenia, a higher NHHR may indicate nutritional reserve, explaining its protective association. Conversely, in SO, it reflects atherogenic dyslipidemia linked to insulin resistance and inflammation. Thus, NHHR’s role is context-dependent: a nutritional marker in sarcopenia, but a metabolic risk marker in SO. Methodological variations also likely contributed. The studies analyzed different sub-cohorts based on distinct inclusion/exclusion criteria, leading to different baseline characteristics. Furthermore, the covariates adjusted for in the models differed, which can alter the observed association. This divergence highlights the need to consider body composition phenotypes holistically and acknowledge the impact of methodological choices. Collectively, these findings suggest a significant association between NHHR and possible SO, highlighting the importance of further investigating the role of NHHR in the pathophysiology of possible SO. The possible reasons why higher NHHR values are linked to a higher risk of possible SO remain unclear. While research directly linking NHHR to SO remains limited, the association among high-fat diets, dyslipidemia, and SO is well-established. Compared to sarcopenia or obesity alone, SO exhibits significantly greater dyslipidemia and insulin resistance33. A study involving 4,500 participants aged 50 and above from western China found that individuals with SO had elevated cholesterol, LDL, and total protein levels, while vitamin D levels were decreased34. Another study of 2,315 participants from minority groups in Guangxi, China, revealed that elderly women with SO had a significantly higher prevalence of dyslipidemia35. Furthermore, a review and meta-analysis of biomarkers for sarcopenia and SO in adults aged 45 and above found that sarcopenia was associated with low triglyceride levels and a stable HDL/LDL ratio, whereas SO was characterized by high triglycerides and cholesterol-related dyslipidemia, suggesting distinct underlying metabolic mechanisms36. These studies further support the notion that NHHR may serve as a biomarker for possible SO, providing a theoretical basis for our findings.

The onset of SO is driven by a complex interplay of multiple factors. While dyslipidemia is a known contributor, other critical elements such as sex, white blood cell, serum creatinine, uric acid, cystatin C, and its related comorbidities (e.g., hypertension) also play significant roles. In the prospective South Korea’s SO Study, which enrolled healthy volunteers aged 20–80, the prevalence of SO varied considerably. Reported rates ranged from 0.8% to 22.3% for women and from 1.3% to 15.4% for men37. A study found that the prevalence of SO was higher in women across all age groups, with age being a significant determinant, particularly after the age of 5038. Consistent with previous studies, we also found that being female was associated with a higher risk of possible SO. The White Blood Cell count reflects the core pathophysiology of SO: chronic low-grade inflammation. Excess adipose tissue secretes pro-inflammatory factors, triggering systemic inflammation, which may both stimulate the bone marrow, leading to an elevated WBC count, and directly activate muscle catabolism pathways. Serum creatinine and cystatin C have been reported in the identification of SO. In a study of 79 elderly patients (aged 65–99 years, 33 female), the serum creatinine/cystatin C ratio demonstrated superior accuracy to the sarcopenia index in identifying sarcopenia. The results showed that 40.5% of the participants were diagnosed with sarcopenia and 8.9% with SO39. In a study of 2001 participants aged ≥ 60 years, lipid metabolism indicators were significantly and negatively correlated with sarcopenia (P < 0.05). Furthermore, uric acid was found to be a partial mediator of this relationship, accounting for 8.91% to 20.66% of the effect40. A prospective observational study of 261 maintenance hemodialysis patients, followed until 2020, assessed the differences between nonobese sarcopenia and SO in terms of nutritional status, quality of life, and clinical outcomes. It was found that SO patients, compared to their nonobese sarcopenic counterparts, had higher albumin, creatinine, uric acid, and leptin levels, a larger phase angle, better nutritional scores, and lower adiponectin levels41.

Compared to sarcopenia without obesity, individuals with SO exhibited lower physical function, a higher risk of metabolic syndrome, and were 3.7 times more likely to have two or more comorbidities4,42. Consequently, in our study, we also observed that the association between NHHR and possible SO remained consistent across various subgroups stratified by age, sex, hypertension, heart disease and dyslipidemia. This highlights the potential importance of managing dyslipidemia to prevent SO in these populations. SO is not uncommon among elderly patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus, and its prevalence varies considerably depending on the specific surrogate markers used for excess body fat43. Diabetes-specific pathological mechanisms, such as muscle stiffness caused by advanced glycation end-product cross-linking and muscle fiber atrophy induced by neuropathy, can drive sarcopenia through pathways independent of lipid deposition44. Furthermore, the complex interplay among insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and certain medications may also affect muscle and adipose tissue in a manner not fully captured by the NHHR ratio. While the association appears stronger in the non-diabetic group, the lack of a significant interaction means we cannot conclude that diabetes truly modifies this relationship. Recent reports indicate that SO may affect up to one-third of cancer patients, a finding that strongly supports the need for its routine evaluation in oncological clinical practice, regardless of body mass index45. In a retrospective observational study of 277 patients with head and neck cancer, the prevalence of malnutrition (24.9%), sarcopenia (52.3%), and myosteatosis (82.3%) was found to be significant46. A cross-sectional study analyzed the association with sarcopenic obesity in 760 inpatients undergoing rehabilitation after an acute stroke. The results showed that 16 men (3.9%), 18 women (5.1%), and a total of 34 patients (4.5%) were diagnosed with sarcopenic obesity47. Cancer and stroke diminish the predictive value of NHHR for possible SO by introducing more powerful, non-lipid pathological factors. In healthy individuals, the dyslipidemia represented by NHHR is a key driver of SO. However, in the context of cancer or stroke, intense systemic inflammation and hypercatabolism, driven by cancer cachexia, neurological damage, and reduced mobility, become the dominant forces of muscle loss. These potent, non-lipid catabolic pathways mask the metabolic risk indicated by NHHR, weakening its association with SO and nullifying its predictive power. In essence, the primary cause of muscle atrophy shifts from metabolic dysfunction to the disease itself, rendering the NHHR metric ineffective. In conclusion, our study found a positive correlation between NHHR and possible SO, implying complex interactions between lipid metabolism dysfunction, insulin resistance, and inflammation.

When interpreting the predictive value of NHHR for possible SO, it is crucial to consider the bias introduced by unmeasured confounding factors, as they are intricately linked to the core biological pathways. Nutritional status and physical activity, as the cornerstones of body composition regulation, not only directly determine the balance between muscle and fat but also profoundly influence the lipid components of NHHR. Omitting these factors would lead to a significant overestimation of the association’s strength48. Furthermore, medication use, such as with glucocorticoids or statins, adds another layer of complex confounding, as these drugs can directly induce a SO phenotype or alter lipid levels, while also acting as proxy variables for disease risk49. Therefore, the association observed in this study is more accurately a reflection of a composite physiological state captured by NHHR, one that is intertwined with the “shadows” of these unmeasured factors. To truly elucidate the independent contribution of NHHR, future research must employ more refined designs, such as objectively quantifying diet and physical activity and meticulously recording medication history, to thoroughly disentangle these confounding effects in statistical models.

This study’s primary strengths are its large and representative sample. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. We adopted a broader definition of sarcopenia, which also included individuals with “possible sarcopenia” in the analysis. This result is based on a non-standard definition of sarcopenia and should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, our study is limited by a significant sex imbalance(78.98% female), a characteristic inherited from our source population in the CHARLS database. This skewness significantly limits the generalizability of our findings, especially for the male population, as the pathophysiology of possible SO and its relationship with markers like NHHR can be influenced by sex-specific factors. Therefore, our results should be interpreted with caution for men, and future research with more sex-balanced cohorts is warranted to validate these associations across both sexes. In addition, Its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, necessitating longitudinal validation. Residual confounding may persist despite comprehensive adjustment for covariates. The observed negative correlation above the NHHR threshold of 3.262 must be considered preliminary and speculative. This conclusion is derived from a restricted data sample, which may not be sufficient to establish a statistically robust or generalizable relationship. Consequently, the validity of this trend beyond the current dataset remains uncertain. The use of NHHR as a single indicator may also not fully capture the complexity of possible SO development. In addition, the extensive exclusion of participants from the original cohort may introduce selection bias, which should be considered when interpreting the results. Finally, the findings are specific to Chinese middle-aged and older adults, which limits their generalizability to other groups.

Conclusion

Our study reveals an independent association between elevated NHHR and an increased likelihood of possible SO in a middle-aged and older Chinese cohort, suggesting NHHR may serve as a readily accessible marker for identifying high-risk individuals. However, it is crucial to emphasize that NHHR is intended to complement, not replace, the direct measurements of muscle and fat mass required for a definitive SO diagnosis. Given the cross-sectional design of our study, large-scale prospective research is essential to validate NHHR’s predictive value and establish causality. Furthermore, the generalizability of these findings to other ethnicities or younger populations remains uncertain and warrants further investigation.
