Effects of 10-week modified badminton curriculum on physical fitness and sustained attention in elementary school children
Authors: Yang Wang, Qiyi Wang, Xiaoxiao Dong, Jia Yang, Kelong Cai, Dandan Chen, Aiguo Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
School Physical Education (PE) serves as an effective platform for promoting physical activity among elementary school students, playing a crucial role in enhancing their physical fitness and sustained attention through organized and disciplined PE curricula. However, traditional PE often emphasizes single skill training and physical exercises, which presents certain limitations in fostering both physical fitness and sustained attention. To address these limitations, this study aims to design a modified badminton curriculum tailored to the unique characteristics of the sport. An intervention experiment was conducted with 106 fifth-grade students over a 10-week period. The experimental group participated in the modified badminton curriculum, while the control group followed the traditional badminton curriculum. Both groups received the intervention three times per week, with each session lasting 40 minutes. Among them, the experimental group consisted of 53 participants, and the control group also included 53 participants. As a result, the final analytic sample comprised 86 participants, including 45 in the experimental group (mean age = 11.24 years, SD = 0.44) and 41 in the control group (mean age = 11.32 years, SD =0.47). Results from repeated measures analysis of variance indicated that the experimental group exhibited greater improvements in speed fitness, endurance, and Reaction Time Variability (RTV), outperforming the control group (p < 0.05). No significant differences were found in flexibility, coordination, strength, No-go accuracy, Go average reaction time, or response accuracy. Therefore, future PE should adopt scientifically grounded designs and diverse arrangements to effectively stimulate students’ participation interest and psychological motivation, thereby promoting the coordinated development of physical fitness and sustained attention.

Introduction

In recent years, the advancement of urbanization in China has been accompanied by a decline in students’ overall health1. Modern students increasingly engage in sedentary behavior and excessive screen time, particularly through short videos, which is associated with reduced physical fitness and weakened sustained attention2–4. Physical fitness remains a key factor for children’s physical and mental health and overall development. While, physical fitness is a key factor in children’s physical and mental health as well as their overall development1,5, playing a crucial role in their well-being6,7. Furthermore, the physical fitness of children and adolescents often persists into adulthood8. Therefore, developing good physical fitness during childhood lays a solid foundation for health in both adolescence and adulthood9–11. In parallel with physical development, cognitive abilities—particularly attention—are also essential to children’s growth. Sustained attention is fundamental to perception and learning12, playing a vital role in information processing and decision-making. It determines children’s ability to comprehend and integrate information13, thereby influencing how they absorb new knowledge, solve problems, and handle complex cognitive tasks. Consequently, both physical fitness and sustained attention are critical factors affecting children’s growth and academic performance14,15.

Previous studies have demonstrated that physical activity is one of the most effective interventions for enhancing both physical fitness and sustained attention in children due to its comprehensive benefits16–20, and long-term engagement yields more pronounced effects21. However, various sports, their types, training intensities, and organizational formats may differentially affect outcomes, leading to varied research findings9,19,20,22–27.

Among various sports, badminton requires agility, coordination, and strategic thinking28,29, and it is distinguished by its high demands on cognitive involvement30,31. Players must stay focused, anticipate their opponent’s actions, and make rapid decisions, which not only enhances physical fitness but also promotes the development of sustained attention30,31. Compared to other sports, badminton also offers several advantages that make it particularly suitable for Physical Education (PE). The sport requires minimal equipment and space, making it feasible in typical school environments29. Additionally, badminton involves limited physical contact, reducing the risk of injury. These characteristics make badminton a practical and engaging choice for enhancing students’ physical development within the constraints of school-based programs. Therefore, implementing badminton programs in school settings is considered an ideal approach to improving primary school students’ physical fitness and sustained attention.

As an effective means of engaging children in physical activity, PE classes play a crucial role in improving students’ physical fitness and sustained attention through structured and disciplined instruction32–38. In China, physical education is guided by national curriculum standards aimed at promoting both physical health and cognitive development among students. This policy context supports the adoption of innovative programs like the modified badminton curriculum used in this study. While traditional PE are valuable for building foundational skills, their focus on isolated drills and structured training can sometimes result in a lack of variety, potentially reducing students’ interest and active participation39. As a result, these traditional PE approaches have limited effectiveness in enhancing students’ physical fitness and sustained attention34–38. To address these challenges, this study is grounded in the National Physical Education and Health Curriculum Standards of China (2022 Edition). The specific policy document can be found on the official government website of China: (W020220420582362336303.pdf). The modified badminton curriculum was developed based on the theoretical framework of the Multipath Model of Exercise to Enhance the Brain and Mind in Children and Adolescents40. However, little is known about how a school-based badminton curriculum simultaneously influences both physical fitness and sustained attention in primary school students. In an effort to identify a more effective PE approach for enhancing elementary school students’ physical fitness and sustained attention. We hypothesize that a 10-week modified badminton curriculum will yield significantly greater improvements in physical fitness and sustained attention than traditional badminton curriculum.

Methods

Trial design

This study aimed to design a 10-week modified badminton curriculum that would exert more beneficial effects on elementary school students’ physical fitness and sustained attention compared to the traditional badminton curriculum. A quasi-experimental, 2 (Group: Experimental, control) × 2 (Testing time points: Baseline, post-test) mixed design was employed, with group as the between-subjects factor and testing time point as the within-subjects factor. Two intact classes were included, with one assigned to the experimental group and the other to the control group. Given the inclusion of only two clusters, between-class variability could not be reliably estimated, and the results should be interpreted as preliminary and exploratory.

Sample size was initially estimated at the individual level using G*Power, indicating that 34 participants (17 per group) would provide 80% power to detect a medium effect (f = 0.25). However, due to practical constraints requiring cluster sampling, the calculation did not account for the design effect, and thus serves as a theoretical reference rather than a definitive basis for inference.

Participants were blinded to group allocation (single-blind), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Stage 1 registered protocol, with no major deviations.

Participants

Two fifth-grade classes were randomly selected from among 16 classes at a public elementary school in Jiangsu Province, China, yielding a total of 106 students aged 11 to 12 years. Administrative support was provided by the school, and participation was voluntary. After two classes were selected, they were randomly assigned to the experimental group (n=53) and the control group (n=53). The participants’ basic demographic information was obtained from the student records provided by the primary schools they attended.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Han ethnicity; (2) fifth-grade students within the designated age range; (3) no severe chronic diseases or motor impairments preventing participation in badminton activities; and (4) right-handedness. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) students with physical disabilities or medical conditions preventing physical activity; (2) students with neurological or psychiatric disorders such as epilepsy or Tourette syndrome; (3) students with significant hearing or vision impairments; (4) students with marked learning disabilities or attention deficits hindering their ability to complete the curriculum; (5) students who have a certain foundation in badminton; and (6) students exhibiting aggressive, disruptive, or unsafe behaviors affecting classroom order and peer safety. These criteria were established to ensure participant safety and maintain the internal validity of the study. Han ethnicity was selected to reduce potential variability associated with genetic and cultural factors. Right-handed participants were included to control for possible lateralization effects on motor and attentional performance. Students with psychiatric disorders were excluded due to safety considerations during physical activity and to avoid potential confounding effects on attention measures. All inclusion and exclusion information was obtained through parent-reported health history forms, school medical records, and teacher observations.

Participant attrition included: (1) students who missed more than one week of classes (n = 8); (2) students who failed to complete the experimental tests (n = 9); and (3) students who prior badminton experience (n = 3). In total, 8 students from the experimental group and 12 from the control group were excluded. To minimize the impact of attrition on the study results, data from these students were excluded from the final analysis. As a result, the final sample consisted of 45 students in the experimental group and 41 students in the control group. Experimental procedure

The study was conducted between during the spring semester (from March 4 to June 15, 2024). The detailed timeline of the experimental procedure is shown in Figure 2.Fig. 2Timeline of the study intervention and assessments.

Timeline of the study intervention and assessments.

The first two weeks of the study served as the baseline assessment phase, which lasted approximately 10 days within a two-week period, during which participants’ initial physical fitness and sustained attention were evaluated. After the baseline assessments, the experimental group participated in the modified badminton curriculum, whereas the control group attended the traditional badminton curriculum. The intervention was implemented from the 3rd to the 12th week of the experimental period. This project was implemented as part of the regular PE. Both groups were taught by the same instructor and teaching assistant (one instructor and one assistant for both groups), both of whom held relevant teaching qualifications, to ensure consistency in instruction. To ensure the consistency of implementation, the 13th week was reserved specifically to make up for any missed sessions caused by holidays, school events, or unforeseen circumstances.

The post-intervention testing was conducted after the completion of all curriculum sessions and was completed during Weeks 14 to 15 of the experimental period, also lasting approximately 10 days within a two-week period. All procedures and personnel were consistent with those used in the baseline assessments, and all testing was conducted by 12 experienced professionals with relevant expertise.

Participants were instructed to refrain from vigorous exercise for 24 hours prior to the baseline and post-intervention assessments. In addition, the testers were blind to the participants’ group assignments. Prior to testing, participants were provided with an explanation of the procedures, ensuring no suggestive statements were made to influence their results. All test results were recorded exactly as observed to ensure objectivity and accuracy.

The badminton curriculum

The modified badminton curriculum comprised 30 sessions, each lasting 40 minutes, conducted three times per week over a 10-week period. This duration was selected based on prior studies indicating that 8 to 12 weeks is an optimal window for observing measurable improvements in both physical fitness and cognitive outcomes in school-aged children41. Additionally, the 10-week period aligned with the school’s academic calendar, ensuring continuity of instruction and minimizing scheduling conflicts. The curriculum covered fundamental skills, progressing from basic grip techniques to more advanced skills, including serving, receiving, clears, lifts, and net shots. The theoretical framework of the curriculum is based on Multipath Model of Exercise to Enhance the Brain and Mind in Children and Adolescents (see Figure 3)40. Physical fitness assessments

The assessment of physical fitness was conducted using the China National Students’ Physical Fitness Standard (CNSPFS)5. In the following order: (1) 50 m, (2) Sit-and-Reach, (3) 1-Minute Jump Rope, (4) 1-Minute Sit-Ups, (5) 50 m × 8 Shuttle Run. The Sit-and-Reach test was performed twice consecutively, with the best result recorded, while all other tests were administered only once. A 5-minute rest interval was provided between each test, during which participants remained seated or standing quietly to optimize recovery.

50 m: This test evaluates speed. In this test, children are instructed to run as fast as possible along a flat and clear surface in a straight line, covering a distance of 50 meters. Each child performs the test once (as a single maximal sprint), and the time is recorded at the finish line using a handheld electronic stopwatch, accurate to 0.1 seconds.

Sit-and-Reach: This test evaluates flexibility. In a seated position, with both knees fully extended and feet securely placed on a vertical support, children are asked to reach forward as far as possible along a measurement line. Each child performs the test twice, and the furthest distance (accurate to 0.1 cm) from the two trials is recorded.

1-Minute Jump Rope: This test evaluates coordination. Children are instructed to jump with both feet simultaneously. After determining the appropriate rope size and length for each child, they are asked to skip rope continuously for one minute, and the total number of jumps is recorded.

1-Minute Sit-Ups: This test evaluates abdominal muscle strength. Children are asked to perform as many sit-ups as possible within the one-minute testing time. The test protocol requires children to lie on their backs with their knees bent, feet flat on the mat (held by the tester), and hands placed behind the head with fingers interlocked. During the test, children are asked to raise their torso until their elbows touch their thighs, then return to the starting position by placing their shoulder blades back on the mat. Children’ form and technique were carefully monitored by trained testers to ensure that only correctly performed full sit-ups were counted. The tester counts and records the number of sit-ups completed.

50 m × 8 Shuttle Run: This test evaluates endurance. Children are required to run back and forth 8 times between two poles located 50 meters apart along a straight line. Children are instructed to run as fast as possible, turning at the poles in a counterclockwise direction at the end of the course, then running back to the starting line. Each child performs the test once, and their time is recorded using a handheld electronic stopwatch, accurate to the second.

Sustained attention assessments

Sustained attention was assessed using the Sustained Attention to Response Task (SART)45, testing sustained attention using go/No-go paradigm. Previous studies have demonstrated the reliability and validity of the SART in children aged 9–12 years, showing it effectively measures sustained attention in pediatric populations46.

The task consisted of 225 digit stimuli, with each digit from “1” to “9” appearing 25 times45. Participants were instructed to press the space bar as quickly as possible whenever a digit other than “3” appeared at the center of the screen, and to withhold any response when the digit “3” was presented. The task was performed using the participants’ dominant hand. Each trial involved the presentation of a digit stimulus for 250 ms, immediately followed by a masking stimulus lasting 900 ms. Participants could respond at any point during the digit or masking intervals. Prior to the task, the experimenters emphasized the importance of balancing speed and accuracy—participants were instructed not to prioritize one at the expense of the other. All stimuli were presented in a pre-arranged pseudorandom sequence. The detailed timeline of the experimental procedure is presented in Figure 5.Fig. 5Sustained attention test.

Each participant completed the task individually in the school’s computer room, using Lenovo desktop computers equipped with 19-inch monitors and standard keyboards. The task was programmed and executed using PSYCHOPY (version 2023.x). The software ensured standardized stimulus presentation and response recording. All assessments were supervised by trained research staff.

Statistical analyses

Prior to the main analyses, normality and homogeneity of variance were tested. The Shapiro–Wilk test showed that the data were normally distributed (p > 0.05), and Levene’s test confirmed the homogeneity of variances across groups (p > 0.05), supporting the use of parametric tests.

Independent sample t-tests and chi-square tests were used to compare demographic data (gender, age, height, weight, BMI), physical fitness (50 m, Sit-and-Reach, 1-Minute Sit-Ups, 1-Minute Jump Rope, 50 m × 8 Shuttle Run), and sustained attention (No-go accuracy, Go Average Reaction Time, RTV and Response Accuracy) between the experimental and control groups. Homogeneity tests were performed to assess baseline differences in demographic characteristics, physical fitness, and sustained attention between the two groups. Repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to investigate the effects of the enhanced badminton curriculum on physical fitness indicators and sustained attention in elementary school students. If a significant time × group interaction was found, further simple effect analysis was conducted. Chi-square tests, independent sample t-tests, and repeated measures ANOVA were conducted using SPSS 27.0 software.

Given that only two clusters (one per group) were included, the analyses were conducted with caution, and the findings are considered exploratory rather than definitive. All statistical results were presented as means with standard deviations (M ± SD). Effect sizes for ANOVA were calculated using partial η2, with conventional interpretations as follows: η2 = 0.01 indicates a small effect, η2 = 0.06 a medium effect, and η2 = 0.14 a large effect. These classifications were used to interpret the magnitude of group differences in all physical fitness and attention outcomes. A statistical significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted to indicate meaningful differences. If significant interactions were found, further post hoc tests were conducted along with Bonferroni corrections.

Discussion

This study aimed to design a 10-week modified badminton curriculum that would exert a more beneficial effects on elementary school students’ physical fitness and sustained attention. The main findings indicate that the 10-week modified badminton curriculum had a significantly greater effects on students’ speed and endurance qualities, as well as their RTV, compared to the traditional badminton curriculum.

Effects of the modified badminton curriculum on physical fitness

First, regarding speed-related fitness, a significant interaction effect was observed between time and group on the 50 m performance, showed a moderate effect size, indicating that the experimental group showed greater improvements in students’ speed development. This improvement is likely attributed to the specially designed components of the modified curriculum, which included reaction speed drills, footwork coordination exercises, and explosive-start games. Through a variety of situational games—such as “evasion tag” and “quick-change catching”—students’ rapid motor response was effectively stimulated, enabling them to complete starting, acceleration, and sprinting movements more efficiently in a short time, thereby enhancing overall speed performance. Furthermore, the modified curriculum placed an emphasis on rapid recognition and response to complex information. This combination of cognitive and motor training appeared to facilitate neural transmission and may have contributed to improvements in students’ agility and efficiency during sprinting51, potentially enhancing their short-distance running performance.

Second, a significant interaction effect was also found for the 50 m × 8 Shuttle Run, showed a small to moderate effect size, suggesting that the experimental group experienced greater improvements in endurance fitness. This enhancement can be attributed not only to physical conditioning itself but also to the multi-tasking stimuli embedded in the curriculum, such as frequent offensive-defensive transitions52. For instance, the instructional design incorporated simulated match-like scenarios, including multi-shuttle rallies and circuit training that alternated between physical conditioning and tactical drills, which indirectly improved aerobic endurance. In addition, competitive games and team-based cooperative tasks within the curriculum enhanced student engagement while simultaneously increasing exercise volume and training density without students being consciously aware of it43,44. This game-based, collaborative instructional atmosphere helped reduce perceived fatigue and allowed students to improve endurance in an enjoyable learning environment29,43,44,52.

Third, although the curriculum included components targeting flexibility, strength, and coordination, no significant differences were observed between the two groups for these indicators. This may be due to several factors. On one hand, the badminton curriculum placed more emphasis on speed-endurance and reaction-related training, while the frequency and intensity of flexibility and core strength training were relatively lower. On the other hand, improvements in these physical qualities typically require long-term, systematic training. Flexibility development depends on continuous stretching exercises, and core strength gains require high-intensity, repetitive muscle training. Given the 10-week intervention period, the training duration may have been insufficient to produce measurable effects. Improvements in abilities such as flexibility, coordination, and strength typically require longer-term, systematic training to produce measurable effects53,54. In contrast, speed and endurance can often be enhanced over shorter training periods through high-intensity and sport-specific exercises55. This differential responsiveness is attributed to the varying physiological and neuromuscular adaptations underlying these fitness components. Improvements in children’s physical fitness are substantially modulated by growth and biological maturation, which exhibit considerable inter-individual variability. Rapid periods of growth can transiently impair neuromuscular coordination and alter the way children respond to training, producing heterogeneous adaptations that may obscure statistically significant differences at the group level56.

Finally, while the current physical fitness tests are generally representative, they also have certain limitations. The 50 m primarily reflects straight-line acceleration and short-distance sprint capacity, offering limited coverage of agility-based, multidirectional speed. The 1-Minute Jump Rope, commonly used to assess coordination, emphasizes lower-limb quickness and rhythm coordination. Moreover, performance in Jump Rope is highly influenced by prior training experience, potentially masking true differences in coordination between individuals. The 1-Minute Sit-Ups, commonly used to assess core strength, primarily evaluate the abdominal and do not represent overall body strength. Sit-Ups, often employed to assess core strength, focus on a narrow muscle group and cannot represent overall strength across the upper and lower body. Although the 50 m × 8 Shuttle Run is intended to assess endurance, the results are also influenced by speed, turning agility, and rhythm control. Therefore, the current evaluation methods may not fully capture the physical fitness improvements brought about by the curriculum. In addition, potential moderating effects of covariates such as gender, height, weight, and baseline physical fitness should be considered to achieve more precise instructional interventions. Future studies may also adopt longer-term follow-up designs or stage-wise assessments to better evaluate training outcomes.

In summary, the findings of this study provide preliminary evidence that the modified badminton curriculum has a positive effect on improving primary school students’ speed and endurance. Future research should further refine methodological approaches, extend intervention periods, optimize testing indicators, and account for sample heterogeneity to comprehensively assess the long-term effects of PE on students’ physical development.

Effects of the modified badminton curriculum on sustained attention

Firstly, the interaction effect for RTV reached a significant level, showing a small to moderate effect size. RTV reflects the degree of fluctuation in an individual’s response time and is a key indicator of sustained attention49,50. Compared with the control group, the experimental group exhibited a significant reduction in RTV. A reduction in RTV indicates a developing tendency toward more stable attention, indicating a certain degree of enhancement in their ability to maintain attention during dynamic tasks48. This improvement may be attributed to the various cognitive tasks embedded in the modified curriculum. For example, the “ball-strike memory” activity required students to continuously hit the shuttle while receiving and recalling random numeric instructions, and the “dynamic target tracking” task involved adjusting hitting strategies based on changes in court lighting. These elements strengthened students’ ability to integrate external information while performing motor actions, helping them stay focused on core task goals in distracting environments, thereby improving sustained attention. These findings are consistent with previous research20,57.

In addition, the gamified scenarios in the curriculum stimulated students’ motivation to participate, allowing attentional resources to be systematically exercised through active engagement. Improvements in physical fitness may enhance the stability of neural connections58, thereby indirectly improving neural response efficiency and reducing interference from irrelevant motor activity59. This process potentially frees up more cognitive resources for information processing20,30,60.

However, no significant interaction effects were observed for either No-go Accuracy or Go Average Reaction Time. This may be related to the differing emphases of the training content between the two groups. While the modified curriculum integrated physical and cognitive elements—such as directional decision-making during rapid striking—it primarily focused on visuomotor response speed and fast decision-making skills29. In contrast, Inhibitory control typically relies on the function of the prefrontal cortex61, and its effective improvement often requires targeted training tasks with clear conflict-resolution demands. For example, tasks like “Red Light, Green Light” are effective in training individuals to suppress impulsive responses, but such components were relatively limited in the current program. Moreover, the 10-week intervention may have only led to short-term behavioral adaptations and may not have been sufficient to induce structural neural changes62. The development of core attentional functions may require longer and more focused training63. As such, improvements in this ability may have been similar between groups, resulting in no statistically significant differences.

Similarly, no significant difference was observed between the two groups in Response Accuracy. This outcome may be influenced by the nature of the testing task. Although the study employed a standardized sustained attention task, the cognitive challenges embedded in the curriculum emphasized real-time decision-making rather than static response accuracy. Future research could incorporate more process-oriented indicators, such as the frequency or trajectory of attentional shifts, and integrate neurophysiological measures such as EEG or eye-tracking to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the effects of PE on sustained attention.

In conclusion, this study found that the modified badminton curriculum effectively reduced RTV, suggesting a positive effect on enhancing students’ sustained attention. However, the current intervention did not yield significant improvements in No-go Accuracy, Go Average Reaction Time, or Response Accuracy. These outcomes may be influenced by the type of training tasks, the duration of intervention, and individual differences among students. Future studies should consider extending the intervention period, incorporating targeted inhibitory control tasks, and combining behavioral data with physiological indicators (e.g., heart rate variability) to further explore the underlying mechanisms through which PE may improve attention-related capabilities in primary school students.

Conclusion

Findings suggest that the modified badminton curriculum was associated with larger within-class improvements in primary school students’ speed, endurance, and RTV compared with the traditional version. These results are preliminary and should be interpreted cautiously, but they provide promising indications that school sports curricula extending beyond conventional physical and skill-based training to include cognitive elements may help foster students’ overall competencies. Further research with more rigorous designs is needed to substantiate these observations.

Limitations

The limitations of this study include: (1) The research sample was drawn from a single public elementary school in Jiangsu Province, China, and included only Han, right-handed students. This restricts external validity and may not be representative of all elementary school students. Recruiting participants from multiple schools, regions, and more diverse populations in future studies would enhance representativeness and generalizability. (2) Only two clusters (one class per group) were included in this study. This prevents accurate estimation of between-cluster variance and may lead to underestimation of standard errors in individual-level analyses, potentially inflating statistical significance. As such, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary exploratory results rather than definitive evidence of intervention effects. (3) The same instructors taught both experimental and control groups, raising the possibility of contamination or inadvertent influence across groups. (4) Analyses were conducted per protocol rather than using an intention-to-treat approach, which may overestimate the intervention effect by excluding participants with incomplete data. (5) The lack of midpoint assessments prevents capturing the trajectory of change throughout the intervention, limiting understanding of when improvements occurred. Additionally, physical fitness was assessed using standardized tests, which may not fully capture all dimensions of performance relevant to badminton or general physical development. (6) The study did not conduct analyses by gender, despite known physiological differences that could influence responses to physical activity. (7) The absence of a third group without any structured physical activity limits the ability to isolate the unique effects of badminton from those attributable to general exercise or school activities. (8) The pre- and post-test design may be influenced by uncontrolled factors such as seasonal changes, natural growth and development, or other environmental influences.
