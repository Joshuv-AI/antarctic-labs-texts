In vitro assessment of cytotoxic, genotoxic, and antimicrobial properties of pediatric restorative dental materials
Authors: Ceren Kaplan, Jülide Secerli, Merve Şavluk, Özlem Martı Akgün, Merve Güdül Bacanli, Nilgün Ünal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to comparatively evaluate the cytotoxic and genotoxic effects of three glass ionomer-based restorative materials and one alkasite material on murine fibroblast cells (L929). Additionally, their antibacterial activities against two prominent oral pathogens were investigated.MethodsThe tested materials included ChemFil Rock (Dentsply Sirona, Germany), EQUIA Forte HT Fil (GC, Japan), Ruby Liner (Rubydent, Turkey), and the alkasite material Cention Forte (Ivoclar Vivadent, Switzerland). Cytotoxicity and genotoxicity were assessed using the L929 fibroblast cell line through MTT and comet assays. Antibacterial properties against Streptococcus mutans and Enterococcus faecalis were determined using the agar diffusion method. Data were analyzed with one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s HSD post hoc test (GraphPad Prism v5.03). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.0001 for cytotoxicity and p < 0.05 for genotoxicity.ResultsAll tested materials exhibited cytotoxic and genotoxic effects on L929 cells, with no statistically significant differences among the groups. None of the materials demonstrated antibacterial activity, as indicated by the absence of inhibition zones against both S. mutans and E. faecalis.ConclusionsThe evaluated glass ionomer-based and alkasite restorative materials induced significant cytotoxic and genotoxic responses in vitro but showed no antibacterial efficacy. These findings underline the necessity for further in vivo investigations to determine their clinical safety and long-term biocompatibility.Keywords: Biocompatibility, Cytotoxicity, Genotoxicity, In vitro study, Pediatric dentistry, Restorative materials

Introduction

Tooth decay is one of the most common chronic diseases affecting children. This condition can lead to functional and aesthetic issues. Various restorative materials have been developed to address these issues. Significant advances have been made in this field in recent years. These advances allow the durability of amalgam to be combined with the biocompatibility of glass ionomer cements (GICs). Additionally, composite resins’ aesthetic properties have been incorporated into these materials, resulting in a new generation of restorative materials becoming available [1–3].

In paediatric dentistry GICs are frequently preferred because they bond chemically to dental tissue, release fluoride and are highly biocompatible. However, their low fracture resistance and aesthetic limitations mean there is a need to to improve their clinical performance [4, 5]. To address this, bioactive components such as glass particles, metal ions, antibacterial agents and nanoparticles have been incorporated into GIC formulations [6]. Bioactive components are defined as substances capable of interacting with surrounding tissues or microorganisms, thereby exerting antimicrobial effects, promoting mineralisation, or modulating cellular responses. The incorporation of such components is intended to enhance the physical, mechanical, and antimicrobial properties of the materials [7]. These materials are widely used in paediatric dental clinics. Furthermore, as their composition is constantly evolving, a better understanding of their biological behaviour is of clinical importance [7, 8].

Alkasites are a novel class of restorative materials that have been developed recently as a result of advancements in material science. These are ion-releasing, resin-based substances that release hydroxide ions (OH⁻) to produce an alkalizing effect. Cention N, the initial course material, was presented in 2016 [9]. Cention Forte, a capsule-based variant, came after it. Alkasite materials have better wear resistance and greater mechanical strength than GICs. Additional flexibility is offered by their capacity to polymerize chemically or by light-curing. Additionally, they are a promising choice in pediatric restorative dentistry due to their fluoride release and potential for use as bulk-fill substitutes in deep cavities [9, 10].

In vitro methods are essential for evaluating the cellular and microbial effects of restorative materials. These approaches allow for the standardized assessment of cytotoxic and antibacterial properties that are difficult to observe directly under clinical conditions [11]. L929 fibroblast cells are widely used in in vitro cytotoxicity testing due to their connective tissue origin and high sensitivity to toxic agents. This cell line is considered a standard model for the comparative assessment of the toxicological effects of dental materials [11–13]. Understanding the biological profiles of newly developed materials is crucial, particularly as their chemical compositions continue to evolve [14].

Numerous studies have investigated the toxicological and antibacterial properties of various restorative materials [15, 16]. However, only a limited number of studies have compared the toxicologic, genotoxicologic and microbiological properties of contemporary dental materials used in paediatric dentistry under the same experimental conditions [17, 18]. Therefore, more comprehensive data are required on the cellular and microbial effects of these newly developed materials used in pediatric patients.

Comprehensive comparative studies on the toxicological and antibacterial effects of alkasite materials are still scarce in the literature [19, 20]. Restorative materials with different chemical compositions may elicit distinct biological responses at the cellular and microbial levels due to variations in ion release, pH changes, and bonding mechanisms [17]. This study aimed to compare the in vitro cytotoxic, genotoxic, and antibacterial properties of three ionomeric materials and one alkasite material currently used in paediatric dentistry.

Materials and methods

Sample Preparation

ChemFil Rock, EQUIA Forte HT Fil, Ruby Liner, and Cention Forte are contemporary restorative materials indicated particularly for primary teeth and young permanent teeth. These materials exhibit distinct differences in composition and setting mechanisms [10, 20]. Glass ionomer cements (ChemFil Rock, EQUIA Forte HT Fil, Ruby Liner) are widely preferred in pediatric dentistry due to their fluoride release, chemical bonding potential, and ease of use in restorative procedures for children [18, 19]. In contrast, Cention Forte is a new-generation alkasite-based restorative material that stands out with its enhanced alkaline reactivity, high compressive strength, and potential to serve as an alternative to glass ionomers [9]. The selection of these four materials aimed to contribute to the literature by enabling an in vitro comparison of different restorative material classes in terms of their cytotoxic, genotoxic, and antibacterial properties. Preparation methods of test samples

In accordance with the surface area-to-volume requirements specified in ISO 10993–12, all disc specimens were prepared with a diameter of 5 mm and a thickness of 2 mm [21, 22]. Specimen preparation was performed under aseptic conditions using sterilised moulds. For the cytotoxicity assessment, 12 specimens were prepared for each material and divided into two subgroups of six to evaluate 24-hour and 72-hour incubation periods. For the genotoxicity assessment, six specimens were prepared per material, incubated for 72 h, and subsequently analysed using the alkaline comet assay. For the antibacterial evaluation, ten discs were prepared from each material and tested using the agar diffusion method.

Cell culture

The L929 murine fibroblast cell line was selected as the cellular model in this study because it is widely used in biological evaluation procedures, including those recommended by ISO 10993–5. Its ease of handling, stable growth pattern, and low biological variability make it a reliable and reproducible model for comparing the cytotoxic effects of different restorative materials.

Cryopreserved L929 cells stored at − 80 °C were thawed in a 37 °C water bath, gently washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and centrifuged at 800 rpm for 5 min. After removing the supernatant, the cell pellet was resuspended in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 1% L-glutamine. The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ until they reached approximately 80% confluence. At this stage, the cells were detached using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA, counted with a hemocytometer, and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well. The plates were then incubated for 24 h under standard culture conditions to allow the cells to attach and stabilise before cytotoxicity testing.

Cytotoxicity assay (MTT Test)

The cytotoxic effects of the restorative materials were assessed using the MTT assay. L929 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well and allowed to attach for 24 h under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂, 95% humidity). After incubation, the culture medium was replaced with the extract solutions of the respective materials. Untreated cells maintained solely in DMEM served as the negative control. Cytotoxicity was evaluated following 24- and 72-hour exposure periods. At the end of each exposure period, the extracts were removed and 100 µL of MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL) was added to each well, followed by a 3-hour incubation. The MTT solution was then aspirated, and the resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 µL of DMSO. The plates were gently shaken for 10 min, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (ELISA reader, BioTek, USA). Cell viability was calculated by normalizing the absorbance of each experimental group to the negative control. IC₅₀ values were determined using nonlinear regression analysis in GraphPad Prism 5.03 (GraphPad Software, USA). All experiments were performed in three independent replicates.

Genototoxicty test (COMET Assay)

The alkaline Comet assay was used to evaluate the genotoxic effects of the restorative materials. L929 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well and exposed to the material extracts for 72 h. At the end of the exposure period, the cells were kept on ice, and 75 µL of cell suspension was mixed with 1% low-melting agarose (pre-melted at 37 °C ± 0.5 °C). The mixture was immediately spread onto slides pre-coated with 1% normal-melting agarose, covered with coverslips, and allowed to solidify at + 4 °C for 2–10 min. After gel solidification, coverslips were removed and the slides were incubated in cold lysis buffer at + 4 °C for at least 1 h. The slides were then rinsed with distilled water, placed in an electrophoresis chamber with the agarose surface facing upward, and immersed in cold alkaline electrophoresis buffer. The slides were equilibrated for 20 min in the dark, after which electrophoresis was performed for 20 min at 25 V and 300 mA. Following electrophoresis, the slides were washed sequentially with distilled water and PBS, fixed in 50%, 75%, and 95% ethanol for 5 min each, and air-dried at room temperature for 24 h. For DNA visualization, 40 µL of ethidium bromide was added to each slide and coverslips were placed. A total of 100 cells per slide were analyzed under a fluorescence microscope, and DNA damage was quantified using the tail intensity parameter in the Comet Assay IV Analysis System (Perceptive Instruments, UK). All experiments were performed in three independent runs [23].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism® 5.03 (GraphPad Software, USA). Since each data point represented the average measurement obtained from multiple disks, the results were expressed as mean ± standard error (SE). Differences between the groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA, and multiple comparisons were conducted with Tukey’s HSD post hoc test. The significance level was set at p < 0.0001 for cytotoxicity assessments and p < 0.05 for genotoxicity evaluations.

Antibacterial activity assessment

Bacterial suspensions

The agar diffusion method was employed to evaluate the antimicrobial activity of the materials. Streptococcus mutans (ATCC 25175) and Enterococcus faecalis (ATCC 29212) were selected as test organisms due to their well-established roles in oral biofilm ecology. S. mutans is a dominant acidogenic and aciduric species that plays a major role in biofilm maturation. E. faecalis, on the other hand, is characterised by its resistance to adverse environmental conditions and its ability to penetrate dentinal tubules. Lyophilised strains were cultured in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) medium and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The bacterial suspensions were then standardised to a turbidity of 0.5 McFarland (≈ 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) in accordance with the recommendations of the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST).

Agar diffusion assay

The antimicrobial activity of the materials was evaluated using the agar diffusion method. Mueller–Hinton Fastidious (MH-F) agar supplemented with defibrinated horse blood was used for S. mutans, whereas BHI agar was used for E. faecalis. Standardised bacterial inocula were spread evenly on the agar surfaces using sterile swabs, and test discs were placed approximately 2.5 cm apart. Ciprofloxacin discs served as positive controls. For each material, ten discs were tested, and all assays were performed in triplicate. Plates inoculated with S. mutans were incubated at 35 ± 1 °C in 5% CO₂ for 18–24 h, while plates inoculated with E. faecalis were incubated aerobically under the same conditions. Following incubation, inhibition zones were measured in millimetres.

Results

MTT test assessment

The cytotoxic effects of various restorative materials on L929 fibroblast cells were examined using the MTT assay, with evaluations conducted at 24 and 72 h. Cell viability was calculated based on the absorbance measurements of each material group.

The 24-hour MTT assay results demonstrated a significant decrease in cell viability in all experimental groups compared to the control group. Viability rates ranged from 25.35% to 32.84%. The ChemFil Rock group exhibited the highest cell viability, while the Ruby Liner group showed the lowest. The cell viability values of all materials at 24 h were statistically significantly lower than those of the control group (p < 0.0001) (Table 3).

Discussion

In vitro models that examine cytotoxicity, genotoxicity, and antibacterial activity are frequently used in the researchs to evaluate the effects of restorative materials on cells and bacteria [24, 25]. These models provide the opportunity to examine, in a laboratory setting, how components released from the materials affect cell metabolism, DNA integrity, and bacterial growth [11, 26]. The MTT assay is commonly used to evaluate cell metabolic activity, the alkaline comet assay to assess DNA damage, and the agar diffusion method to evaluate material-derived antibacterial activity [17, 27]. This study aims to investigate the cytotoxic, genotoxic, and antibacterial effects of four different restorative materials in vitro and to present comparative results in terms of these three biological parameters.

Various in vitro methods and different cell lines are employed for the assessment of the cytotoxicity of restorative materials [11, 12]. The L929 fibroblast cell line and the MTT assay are the most commonly used standard models for these methods [21, 22]. Golkar et al. [27]. examined glass ionomer cements in L929 cells over a period of 24–72 h and found that materials with different compositions did not significantly affect cell viability over time [27]. Köymen et al. [13]. reported that dual-cure bulk-fill materials evaluated using the WST-1 assay reduced cell viability and induced morphological alterations at 24 h [13]. Similarly, Aydın et al. [28]. observed a trend toward decreased viability in L929 cells after 24 and 72 h of exposure to composite materials. However, this reduction was not statistically significant for all materials [28]. However, Oneț et al. [29]. reported no significant change in the viability of gingival mesenchymal stromal cells when assessed using the MTT assay. Although the authors noted that alterations in certain functional parameters could occur [29]. Evidence in the literature indicates that cellular responses to restorative materials may vary depending on several factors, including the cell line used, the material’s composition, the experimental method, and the exposure duration [13, 27, 29, 30]. In the present study, four restorative materials were tested in L929 fibroblasts at 24 and 72-hour intervals. Although a reduction in cell viability was observed at both time points, this decrease was not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Several analytical approaches are available to assess the genotoxic potential of restorative materials under in vitro conditions [31]. The comet assay is a widely used method that can detect DNA strand breaks and alkali-labile sites at the single-cell level, making it suitable for assessing early genetic responses [15, 31]. A range of genotoxicity assays has been applied to different cell lines and restorative material types in previous studies [16, 25, 32]. Tauböck et al. [32]. evaluated bulk-fill composites using both the comet and micronucleus assays and reported no significant differences in DNA damage between the experimental groups in either method [32]. Ribeiro et al. [33]. examined three glass ionomer cements in chinese ovary hamster (CHO) cells and found that a significant increase in DNA damage occurred only at high concentrations [33]. Bandarra et al. [34]. analysed different luting cements in 3T3 fibroblasts using the micronucleus test and reported that a detectable increase in DNA damage was observed only in specific material groups [34]. Angelieri et al. [35]. evaluated different orthodontic glass ionomer cements in CHO cells using the comet assay. They found that some formulations produced a significant increase in DNA fragmentation compared with the control group [35]. De Angelis et al. [16]. examined composite resins in gingival fibroblasts. In their study, a significant genotoxic effect was observed only in specific material group [16]. In the present study, the comet assay was used to evaluate the genotoxic potential of four restorative materials in L929 fibroblasts. Although the mean tail intensity values of the material groups were numerically higher than the control, these differences were not statistically significant. In line with similar studies in the literature, these findings suggest that restorative materials generally pose a limited tendency to induce genetic damage under short-term in vitro methods [15, 35]. Nevertheless, the extent of genotoxic response may vary depending on factors such as the material’s chemical composition, the selected cell line, and the test method.

Evaluating the antibacterial properties of restorative materials is essential for understanding their potential impact on microbial activity [25, 36]. Among the various in vitro methods used for this purpose, the agar diffusion test is frequently employed. This method assesses antibacterial activity by measuring the material’s ability to diffuse through the agar medium and inhibit the growth of adjacent microorganisms [37, 38].

There are several studies in the literature that have investigated the antibacterial activity of restorative materials. Khere et al. [37]. assessed the antibacterial effects of three different glass ionomer cements against S.mutans using the agar diffusion test and measured the diameters of the resulting inhibition zones [37]. Boondireke et al. [36]. employed the direct contact test (DCT) to evaluate the time-dependent antibacterial activity of various restorative materials against S. Mutans [36]. In another study, Aref et al. [38]. modified an alkasite-based restorative material with nano titanium dioxide (TiO₂) nanoparticles and investigated the antibacterial efficacy of the modified formulations against S. mutans, Staphylococcus aureus, and Escherichia coli [38].

Khere et al. [37]. reported that all tested materials demonstrated antibacterial activity in their study evaluating the inhibitory effects of three different glass ionomer cements on S. mutans using the agar diffusion method [37]. Similarly, Alsamolly et al. [39]. compared the antibacterial efficacy of an alkasite-based material and various glass ionomer-based restorative materials, finding that the alkasite group produced the largest inhibition zones and exhibited the highest antibacterial activity [39]. In another study, Shoaeb et al. [40]. observed that the glass ionomer cement modified with an antibacterial agent formed significant inhibition zones against St. mutans and Lactobacillus acidophilus, whereas the conventional glass ionomer cement did not produce any inhibition zone, indicating no detectable antibacterial effect under the conditions of the agar diffusion test [40]. In our study, the antibacterial effects of various restorative materials on S.mutans and E. faecalis were evaluated using the agar diffusion method. However, no inhibition zones were observed around the tested materials. The results obtained are similar to some studies in the literature, while differing from others [39, 40]. These differences may be related to the methods used, the types of microorganisms, or the compositional differences of the materials.

This study aimed to compare the cytotoxic, genotoxic, and antibacterial effects of four restorative materials currently used in pediatric dentistry under in vitro conditions. According to the findings, all tested materials exhibited similar cytotoxic and genotoxic potentials when compared to each other; however, these effects were found to be higher than those observed in the negative control group. Additionally, no inhibition zones were observed in the antibacterial assessment conducted using the agar diffusion method.

This study has certain limitations. Firstly, it was conducted exclusively under in vitro conditions, which may not fully reflect the complex biological responses observed in living organisms. The antibacterial activity was evaluated using only the agar diffusion test, a method that relies on the ability of a material to diffuse into the agar medium; thus, a lack of diffusion does not necessarily indicate the absence of antibacterial properties. Furthermore, the concentrations used in cytotoxicity and genotoxicity testing represent direct contact conditions, which may exceed the levels of exposure encountered in clinical practice.

Further studies involving different testing methods, cell types, and microbial models are necessary to better understand the biocompatibility and antimicrobial potential of these materials.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, all tested restorative materials exhibited cytotoxic and genotoxic effects on L929 fibroblast cells, with no statistically significant differences observed among them. Additionally, no antibacterial activity was detected against Streptococcus mutans and Enterococcus faecalis using the agar diffusion method, as none of the materials produced measurable inhibition zones. These findings suggest that while the materials may pose biological risks under experimental conditions, further investigations involving different methodologies, microbial strains, and in vivo models are essential to comprehensively assess their biocompatibility and antibacterial potential in clinical scenarios. Although all tested materials demonstrated cytotoxic and genotoxic effects in vitro, further studies are needed to evaluate the clinical relevance of these results under in vivo conditions.
