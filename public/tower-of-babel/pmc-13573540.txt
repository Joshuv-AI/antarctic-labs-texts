Re-emerging Marburg virus disease in Africa: spillover ecology, geographic expansion, and surveillance vulnerabilities
Authors: Markos Tibbo, Robert Barigye, Gobena Ameni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMarburg virus disease (MVD) is re-emerging across Africa as a high-consequence zoonosis shaped by expanding ecological suitability, repeated spillover, and uneven surveillance capacity. This review synthesizes current evidence on the ecological, epidemiological, and operational determinants of contemporary Marburg virus (MARV) emergence. We conceptualize MVD as an ecological-emergence system produced by interactions among reservoir-host biology, environmental change, human exposure, health-system readiness, and mobility, rather than as a series of isolated outbreaks. Recent detections in multiple African regions indicate wider enzootic circulation than previously recognized and support repeated, reservoir-associated introductions from distributed ecological foci. Spillover risk is heightened where mining, land-use change, agricultural encroachment, settlement growth, climate-sensitive habitat disruption, and population movement increase contact with Egyptian rousette bats (Rousettus aegyptiacus) and contaminated roost environments. Following primary spillover, diagnostic delays, fragmented surveillance, limited laboratory decentralization, healthcare-associated transmission, and mobility-linked exposure can enable outbreak amplification and delayed recognition. Serological findings further suggest possible “shadow epidemiology,” with unrecognized or mild MARV infections occurring outside confirmed outbreak chains. Critical preparedness gaps persist in ecological risk mapping, longitudinal reservoir surveillance, decentralized molecular diagnostics, genomic sequencing, data integration, and cross-border early warning. Future preparedness should move beyond reactive containment toward integrated One Health approach combining predictive ecological surveillance, rapid community-level detection, real-time genomics, infection prevention, risk communication, and regional coordination to identify spillover early and prevent human transmission.

Introduction

The emergence of zoonotic pathogens is accelerating as ecological, climatic and socioeconomic pressures intensify at the human–animal–environment interface. Land-use change, biodiversity loss, extractive industry expansion, climate variability, urbanization, and population mobility are altering the conditions under which pathogens spillover from reservoir hosts into human populations [1–4]. Within this changing landscape, Marburg virus disease (MVD) has re-emerged as a high-consequence filoviral zoonosis with increasing geographical and epidemiological significance across Africa [5].

Marburg virus (MARV), a member of the family Filoviridae, causes severe viral haemorrhagic fever, with historically reported case fatality ratios ranging from 24% to 90% and often exceeding 50% in recognized outbreaks [5,6]. Since its identification in 1967 during laboratory-associated outbreaks in Germany and Yugoslavia linked to African green monkeys imported from Uganda, MVD was long viewed as a rare disease largely confined to Central and East Africa [7,8].

Since 2021, first recognized MVD outbreaks have been reported in Guinea, Ghana, Equatorial Guinea, Tanzania, Rwanda, and Ethiopia [9–14]. These detections, when interpreted alongside genomic evidence of independent introductions and ecological evidence of widespread reservoir suitability, indicate that MARV risk is not confined to historically recognized outbreak zones. Contemporary emergence is therefore better explained by repeated reservoir-associated spillovers occurring across distributed ecological foci than by sustained regional human transmission alone [15–17]. This interpretation remains cautious: the reporting of outbreaks in additional countries does not, by itself, demonstrate a transmission pattern, but it provides epidemiological signals that require integration with reservoir ecology, phylogenetics, environmental suitability, and surveillance capacity.

The Egyptian rousette bat (Rousettus aegyptiacus) is a confirmed natural reservoir host for MARV. This species occupies caves, mines, tunnels, and rocky crevices across wide African ecological zones [17–19]. MARV maintenance in R. aegyptiacus is supported by asymptomatic infection, oral shedding, genetic diversity within bat-associated viral populations, and seasonal pulses of infection, particularly among juvenile bats [20–22]. Human spillover is thought to occur mainly through direct or indirect exposure to contaminated cave environments, bat excreta, saliva, partially eaten fruits, or other reservoir-associated ecological interfaces [3,23,24,38].

Environmental disruption can intensify these exposure pathways. Mining expansion, deforestation, agricultural encroachment, infrastructure development, and settlement growth increase human entry into bat habitats and contaminated roost environments. Climate variability may also alter bat movement, food availability, colony aggregation, reproductive timing, and seasonal contact patterns [1,4,25,26]. These interacting pressures support an ecological convergence model in which reservoir biology, human environmental intrusion, and climate-sensitive ecological change jointly increase the probability of spillover.

MVD also carries a strong transboundary dimension. The wide distribution of R. aegyptiacus, together with pastoral mobility, labour migration, mining economies, informal trade, displacement, and porous borders, creates epidemiological connectivity across African ecological corridors [27]. Ethiopia’s first recognized MVD outbreak in 2025 demonstrates how ecological exposure, delayed diagnosis, healthcare-associated amplification, fragmented surveillance, and population movement can converge, allowing a localized spillover event to acquire regional public health significance [14,28].

Despite growing recognition of MVD as an emerging threat, major scientific and operational gaps remain. Spillover frequency, infection heterogeneity, asymptomatic exposure, environmental persistence, transmission parameters, and the role of mild or subclinical infection remain poorly defined [27,29]. Genomic surveillance, ecological monitoring, predictive modelling, decentralized diagnostics, and cross-border early warning systems also remain insufficiently developed across many at-risk regions [15,30,31]. In this review we synthesize the ecological, epidemiological, and surveillance dynamics shaping contemporary MVD emergence in Africa and frame the disease as a mechanistically complex zoonosis shaped by ecological disruption, hidden spillover, cross-border ecological connectivity, and surveillance vulnerability.

Rationale for the review focus

This review uses a structured narrative and mechanistic synthesis rather than a formal meta-analysis. The available evidence is heterogeneous, including outbreak reports, ecological modelling, serological surveys, genomic analyses, surveillance assessments, and institutional reports. We therefore organized the evidence around recurrent mechanisms observed across MVD outbreaks: reservoir-associated exposure, ecological disturbance, human mobility, diagnostic delay, healthcare-associated amplification, genomic uncertainty, and surveillance asymmetry. Where evidence was available, these drivers were compared across outbreaks, including Angola, Uganda, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Equatorial Guinea, Tanzania, Rwanda, and Ethiopia. This approach does not claim to provide a formal predictive framework; rather, it offers an evidence-based interpretive model for understanding how ecological and health-system factors interact to shape contemporary MVD emergence in Africa.

Changing epidemiology and geographic expansion of MVD

Recent MVD outbreaks have changed the interpretation of MVD emergence in Africa. Since 2021, first recognized outbreaks have been reported in Guinea, Ghana, Equatorial Guinea, Tanzania, Rwanda, and Ethiopia, demonstrating wider recognized occurrence across West, Central, East, and Horn of Africa settings [9–14]. These events should not be interpreted from geography alone. Their significance lies in their convergence with genomic evidence of independent introductions, ecological evidence of reservoir suitability across wider areas, and persistent surveillance gaps in high-risk ecological interfaces [15–17]. Together, these findings support the hypothesis that contemporary MVD emergence reflects repeated reservoir-associated spillovers from distributed ecological foci rather than a single expanding human transmission chain.

Phylogenetic and genomic investigations indicate that many recent outbreaks likely resulted from independent spillover events rather than sustained regional human transmission chains [15–17]. This distinction is epidemiologically important because it suggests that contemporary emergence is driven primarily by repeated reservoir-associated introductions in multiple ecological settings. These patterns are consistent with the broad distribution of the Egyptian rousette bat (R. aegyptiacus), the confirmed natural reservoir host of MARV, across large areas of sub-Saharan Africa [17–19].

The changing epidemiology of MVD also reflects the intensifying ecological and anthropogenic pressures at the wildlife–human interface. Mining expansion, cave entry, deforestation, agricultural encroachment, settlement expansion, and infrastructure development increasingly bring human populations into contact with environments occupied by reservoir bat colonies [23–25]. In several outbreaks, exposure linked to caves, mines or other bat-associated environments has remained a recurring epidemiological feature, underscoring the role environmental contact interfaces in shaping spillover dynamics [23,24,38].

Large and recent outbreaks also show how diagnostic delay, healthcare-associated transmission, and mobility-linked exposure can amplify localized spillover into major public health events. The 2004–2005 Angola outbreak demonstrated the capacity of MARV to cause large, high-fatality outbreaks when recognition and infection prevention are delayed [32,33]. More recent outbreaks in Tanzania, Rwanda, and Ethiopia similarly highlight the operational importance of early detection, rapid laboratory confirmation, infection prevention and control, contact tracing, and coordinated risk communication [12–14,34].

Serological findings from several African regions suggest that human exposure to MARV or related filoviruses may occur outside recognized outbreak events. Studies in miners, cave-associated populations, and communities near bat habitats have detected filovirus-reactive antibodies among individuals without documented MVD, supporting the possibility of under-recognized exposure or limited spillover events that do not progress to recognized outbreaks [19,35–37]. Contemporary geographic expansion may therefore reflect both genuine ecological emergence and improved detection of previously unrecognized transmission systems.

Together, these developments indicate that MVD should be understood as a geographically diffuse and ecologically dynamic zoonosis shaped by environmental disruption, cross-border ecological connectivity, and surveillance heterogeneity. The expanding distribution of recognized outbreaks across Africa underscores the need to shift from reactive outbreak containment to proactive ecological surveillance capable of identifying spillover risk before widespread human amplification occurs. Spillover ecology and reservoir dynamics

The contemporary emergence of MVD is best interpreted through an ecological convergence framework, in which distributed enzootic systems, human-modified landscapes, and exposure behaviours create conditions for MARV spillover. Current evidence strongly supports the Egyptian rousette bat (R. aegyptiacus) as the principal natural reservoir host of MARV; however, reservoir presence alone does not determine emergence. Spillover occurs when reservoir ecology, environmental disruption, human activity, and surveillance limitations intersect at specific human-bat interfaces [17,18,20–22]. Human infection is initiated through exposure to reservoir-associated environments, particularly cave and mine systems, contaminated substrates, bat excreta, saliva, and other materials linked to infected bat colonies [3,23,25].

R. aegyptiacus occupies caves, mines, tunnels, rocky crevices, and other roosting environments across large areas in sub-Saharan Africa [17–19]. Infected bats are generally asymptomatic, a feature that supports viral persistence within colony systems. Experimental and field studies have demonstrated oral shedding, bat-to-bat transmission, genetic diversity within bat-associated MARV populations, and seasonal pulses of infection, particularly among juvenile bats after birthing periods [20–22,35]. These dynamics indicate that spillover risk is temporally heterogeneous and may increase during periods of intensified viral circulation within reservoir colonies.

Human infection is thought to occur through direct or indirect exposure to infectious bat excreta, saliva, contaminated roost environments, partially eaten fruits, or other reservoir-associated materials [3,23,38]. These pathways become more consequential where human activity repeatedly overlaps with bat habitats. Mining, cave entry, fruit harvesting, hunting, settlement expansion, and agricultural encroachment can increase the frequency of contact between people and contaminated ecological substrates. Human-modified landscapes play an increasingly important role in MARV emergence. Land-use change, agricultural expansion, infrastructure development, settlement growth, forest fragmentation, and extractive activities can alter bat foraging behaviour, roost accessibility, and spatial overlap among bats, humans, livestock, and food systems [2,3,25,26]. These pressures do not act as isolated risk factors. They reshape ecological boundaries and create repeated contact opportunities in landscapes where reservoir hosts, susceptible human populations and amplifying social systems converge.

Climate variability may further influence spillover interfaces by altering fruiting phenology, water availability, bat movement, colony aggregation, and human livelihood strategies [1,4,20–22]. Drought, rainfall anomalies, and vegetation changes can influence reservoir ecology and human behaviour, including pastoral movement, food sourcing and land use. Direct climate–MARV causal pathways remain incompletely defined; however, climate-sensitive ecological perturbations may plausibly shift the timing, location, and intensity of human–bat contact, particularly in agro-pastoral, forest-edge, and mining-associated settings.

MARV has no confirmed domestic animal amplification host, and livestock are not known to maintain endemic transmission. Nevertheless, livestock and other domestic animals may serve as ecological sentinels, because they share water points, grazing areas, fruiting trees, and peri-domestic environments with wildlife reservoirs. Integrating veterinary observations with human, wildlife, and environmental surveillance could therefore strengthen early warning, even in the absence of domestic animal maintenance cycles [39–41].

Overall, MVD spillover should be understood as a multi-step ecological process rather than a singular accidental exposure. Distributed enzootic systems maintain MARV across broad reservoir landscapes, while human-modified environments create interfaces where exposure becomes more likely. The probability of emergence increases when seasonal reservoir shedding, environmental contamination, occupational or livelihood exposure, population mobility, and delayed detection occur together. The ecological convergence framing is essential for designing predictive surveillance and upstream One Health interventions capable of identifying spillover risk before sustained human amplification occurs. Regional ecological connectivity and mobility-linked surveillance risk

The contemporary emergence of MVD cannot be adequately interpreted through national outbreak data alone. MVD increasingly reflects cross-border epidemiological connectivity shaped by shared ecological corridors, mobile populations, and uneven surveillance capacity. The confirmed reservoir host, the Egyptian rousette bat (R. aegyptiacus), is distributed across broad sub-Saharan ecological zones, while human movement systems link remote spillover interfaces to border districts, urban centres, and neighbouring countries [17–19].

Regional ecological corridors are central to this transboundary risk. Bat roosting and foraging systems, cave networks, forest–savannah mosaics, mining zones, water points, and pastoral landscapes often extend across political borders. Ecological suitability studies suggest that MARV-compatible environments occur across large areas of Africa, including regions with little or no documented outbreak history [16,17]. Detection of MVD in one country may therefore signal a wider enzootic system shared across neighbouring ecosystems. This interpretation is particularly relevant in the Central Africa, the Great Lakes region, West African forest–savannah interfaces and the Horn of Africa.

Human mobility increases the epidemiological significance of these shared ecological systems. Pastoral movement, informal trade, labour migration, mining-related mobility, displacement, and transport corridors can connect high-risk ecological interfaces with larger regional networks. In pastoral and agro-pastoral settings, seasonal movement of people and livestock across borders may increase contact with shared water points, fruiting trees, grazing areas, and wildlife habitats. These mobility systems do not necessarily generate sustained MARV transmission; however, they can expand exposure networks before an outbreak is detected and contained [3,42].

Surveillance asymmetry remains a major weakness in the transboundary landscape. Neighbouring countries and border districts often differ substantially in diagnostic capacity, laboratory access, reporting systems, genomic sequencing infrastructure, and outbreak response readiness [6,30,43]. One jurisdiction may detect a suspected cluster rapidly, while another may miss early spillovers because healthcare access is limited, febrile illness is common, or confirmatory testing requires long-distance sample transport. Early case detection is further complicated by non-specific clinical presentation of MVD, in which fever, fatigue, and gastrointestinal symptoms commonly precede the later, less consistent hemorrhagic manifestations [6,30,43]. These clinical and system-level constraints create asynchronous detection across shared ecological zones, allowing localized spillovers to remain unrecognized until clinical amplification becomes evident. Ethiopia’s first recognized MVD outbreak demonstrates this vulnerability. The outbreak occurred in a region connected to Kenya and South Sudan through pastoral mobility, informal commerce, and social networks. Although sustained regional transmission after delayed detection was not confirmed, domestic movement from the outbreak area and alerts in neighbouring areas showed how quickly a localized event can acquire broader epidemiological relevance [28,44]. Similar patterns may occur wherever spillover interfaces overlap with mobile populations and uneven surveillance systems.

MVD transmission requires close contact with infected body fluids and is less readily transmissible than airborne respiratory viruses. International dissemination remains possible, as shown by imported MARV cases detected in Europe and the United States after exposure to African endemic settings [45,46]. The greater contemporary concern, however, is recurrent regional emergence across connected African ecosystems rather than sustained global spread.

Future MVD preparedness should therefore organize surveillance around ecological and mobility networks rather than administrative borders alone. Cross-border alert systems, harmonized case definitions, shared laboratory referral pathways, regional genomic sequencing, and interoperable human–animal–environment surveillance platforms are essential for early spillover detection. In this context, MVD is not only a regional zoonotic threat but also a regional ecological surveillance problem shaped by cross-border connectivity and uneven detection capacity.

Outbreak amplification and surveillance vulnerability: lessons from Angola and Ethiopia

The 2004–2005 Angola outbreak provides the clearest example of how delayed recognition, health-system vulnerability, healthcare-associated transmission, and geographic spread can transform MARV spillover into a major public health emergency. The outbreak began before laboratory confirmation, was initially concentrated in Uíge Province, later involved multiple provinces, and became the largest recorded MVD outbreak, with very high fatality [32,33]. Angola therefore illustrates the full amplification pathway more clearly than recent smaller outbreaks: an initially undetected introduction followed by diagnostic delay, inadequate early infection prevention and control, healthcare and household transmission, and delayed coordinated response [32,33,43].

Ethiopia’s first recognized MVD outbreak should be interpreted more cautiously. It is best understood as a recent first-recognition and surveillance-vulnerability example, rather than as the primary model of outbreak amplification. Available reports indicate that the outbreak occurred in a setting where human activity, population movement, and potential bat-associated environments may have overlapped [14]. WHO reported 19 total cases, including 14 confirmed and five probable cases, and the end of the outbreak on 26 January 2026 [28]. The precise spillover pathway was not confirmed. However, absence of documented reservoir exposure does not exclude local spillover. Rousettus aegyptiacus, the recognized reservoir host of MARV, occurs across broad African ecological zones, and compatible human–bat interfaces may exist in affected or neighbouring areas [17–19]. The Ethiopian outbreak therefore justifies systematic ecological investigation of caves, mines, rocky roosts, peri-domestic fruiting trees, water points, and other human–bat interfaces, while avoiding any implication that reservoir exposure was demonstrated [17–19,23,24,38].

Diagnostic delay and diagnostic capacity should be treated as related but distinct concepts. In this review, diagnostic delay refers to the interval between symptom onset or early cluster recognition and laboratory confirmation or response activation.

Diagnostic capacity refers to the availability, accessibility, and performance of the systems required for confirmation and response, including specimen referral, molecular testing, biosafety, trained personnel, reagents, reporting systems, and decentralized testing platforms [30,43]. In Angola, delayed recognition contributed to large-scale amplification [32,33]. In recent outbreaks, including Tanzania, Rwanda, and Ethiopia, diagnostic capacity and surveillance readiness influenced the speed of confirmation, case isolation, contact tracing, infection prevention and control, and risk communication [12–15,28,34,43].

Mobility also requires cautious interpretation. Movement from an affected area can widen exposure networks, complicate contact tracing, and trigger regional alerts, but it does not by itself confirm epidemiological connectivity or cross-border transmission [27,42]. In Ethiopia, reported domestic movement and alerts in neighbouring areas demonstrated the operational importance of preparedness in border-linked and mobile populations, but sustained regional transmission was not confirmed [14,28]. The outbreak should therefore be used to illustrate surveillance vulnerability in mobile populations, not as evidence of regional dissemination.

Overall, the African MVD experience supports a convergence model of outbreak amplification, but this model is not specific to Ethiopia. Any MVD outbreak with secondary cases reflects some degree of post-spillover amplification. The strength of evidence varies by outbreak. Angola demonstrates the most complete amplification sequence, whereas Ethiopia provides a recent example of first recognition, diagnostic latency, ecological uncertainty, and regional preparedness concern [14,28,32,33].

Hidden transmission and the “shadow epidemiology” of MARV

A major unresolved issue in MVD epidemiology is whether recognized outbreaks represent only the clinically visible fraction of broader MARV exposure. This possibility can be described as shadow epidemiology: a layer of low-frequency, weakly detected, or clinically unrecognized human exposure that occurs beneath formally confirmed outbreaks. If present, this phenomenon would alter current assumptions about spillover frequency, disease burden, and the geographic extent of MARV risk.

Serological studies provide the strongest evidence for this possibility. Investigations among miners, cave workers, forest-edge communities, and populations living near bat roosts have detected antibodies reactive to MARV or related filoviruses in individuals without documented histories of clinically recognized MVD [19,35–37]. Although reported seroprevalence is generally low, these findings suggest that human exposure may occur more frequently than outbreak records indicate. This does not imply sustained silent human-to-human transmission. Rather, it suggests that primary spillovers or limited exposure events may go undetected unless they progress to severe disease clusters or recognized outbreaks.

The interpretation of serological evidence requires caution. Antibody detection may reflect previous mild infection, abortive infection, cross-reactivity with related filoviruses, or exposure that did not result in onward transmission. Nevertheless, repeated serological signals across high-risk populations support the view that confirmed outbreaks capture only a subset of MARV–human interactions. This distinction matters because outbreak-based surveillance may underestimate both frequency of zoonotic exposure and the ecological breadth of risk.

The role of asymptomatic or pauci-symptomatic MARV infection remains uncertain. Classical MVD is a severe disease with high case fatality ratios in recognized outbreaks [5,6]. Serological evidence raises the possibility that clinical outcomes after MARV exposure may be more heterogeneous than traditionally assumed. Exposure dose, route of infection, host immune status, and viral factors may influence disease expression. At present, however, there is no convincing evidence that asymptomatic individuals play a major role in MARV transmission. Most documented transmission occurs through close contact with symptomatic patients or infectious body fluids [29,43]. Subclinical exposure should therefore be interpreted as a potential contributor to underestimation of spillover frequency and disease burden, not as evidence of substantial silent transmission (Figure 5). The surveillance implications are substantial. Systems that rely primarily on severe febrile illness, hemorrhagic manifestations, or clusters of unexplained deaths will miss isolated infections, mild diseases, and exposure events that do not amplify. This limitation is especially important in regions where malaria, typhoid fever, dengue, and other febrile illnesses are common and access to laboratory testing is limited [6,30]. Without targeted sero-epidemiological studies, ecological monitoring, and improved diagnostic access in high-risk populations, MARV exposure will continue to be inferred mainly from recognized outbreaks rather than measured directly. The shadow epidemiology concept shifts MVD from a purely outbreak-centered disease model to an ecological exposure model. Future studies should combine longitudinal serology, reservoir surveillance, genomic sequencing, and community-level febrile illness surveillance to determine how often MARV crosses into human populations, which exposure pathways lead to clinical disease, and why only some spillover events become recognized outbreaks.

Surveillance gaps, genomic deficits and mechanistic uncertainties

Future preparedness for MVD will depend on shifting from outbreak-triggered detection to integrated ecological intelligence. Current surveillance systems often identify MARV only after clinical amplification has begun. A more effective system should detect the ecological, epidemiological, and health-system conditions that precede spillover and early transmission. This requires linking human health surveillance, wildlife ecology, environmental change, mobility data, diagnostics, and genomics within a continuous early warning architecture rather than maintaining them as separate sectoral reporting streams [30,43].

Decentralized diagnostics and genomic preparedness are immediate operational priorities. In many at-risk regions, MVD confirmation still depends on distant reference laboratories, delaying sample referral, laboratory diagnosis, case isolation, and response activation. Regional viral haemorrhagic fever laboratories, mobile molecular platforms, validated rapid diagnostic tools, and reliable sample transport systems would shorten this interval and reduce the risk of healthcare-associated amplification [30,34,43]. Diagnostic decentralization should be embedded in routine febrile illness surveillance, particularly in ecological interface zones where malaria, typhoid fever, dengue and other endemic infections can obscure early recognition of MVD [6]. Genomic surveillance remains uneven and largely outbreak-driven, limiting phylogeographic resolution, source attribution, and understanding of MARV diversity across distributed enzootic systems. The increasing use of real-time sequencing during recent outbreaks has shown its value for distinguishing independent spillovers from linked transmission chains and for reconstructing outbreak dynamics [15,16,33]. Future systems should integrate sequencing of human cases with reservoir and environmental sampling. Such integration would strengthen detection of independent spillovers, mobility-linked spread, lineage diversity, and regional viral connectivity.

Predictive ecological surveillance represents an important upstream frontier. Longitudinal monitoring of R. aegyptiacus colonies, especially in high-risk cave, mining, forest-edge, and peri-domestic interfaces, could identify seasonal changes in viral shedding and periods of increased spillover probability [18,20–22]. African applications can build on two practical models: longitudinal bat-colony monitoring that links juvenile recruitment, seasonal shedding and vegetation or rainfall proxies to periods of increased exposure risk; and outbreak-linked genomic surveillance, as demonstrated during the Rwanda outbreak and the Tanzania response, where sequencing and rapid laboratory confirmation helped distinguish introductions, define transmission chains and guide response operations [15,34]. These data should be combined with land-use change, mining activity, vegetation indices, rainfall anomalies, drought indicators, and human mobility patterns to generate dynamic risk maps. Such models will not predict every spillover event, but they can identify ecological windows and geographic corridors where enhanced diagnostics, community engagement, and preparedness should be pre-positioned.

Regional early warning and cross-border coordination systems are essential because MARV risk does not follow administrative borders. Shared ecological corridors, pastoral mobility, mining labour movements, informal trade, and displacement can connect high-risk interfaces across countries [3,42]. Harmonized case definitions, cross-border alert thresholds, joint investigation protocols, interoperable laboratory referral systems, and regional sequencing hubs would reduce surveillance asymmetry between neighbouring jurisdictions. Border areas and mobile populations should be treated as priority surveillance zones rather than peripheral settings. Recent outbreaks have demonstrated increasing regional zoonotic risk, with the potential for cross-border dissemination facilitated by mobility networks and regions with uneven surveillance capacity [47]. However, the epidemic potential remains constrained by relatively low transmissibility, suggesting that outbreak amplification is primarily driven by delayed detection and health system vulnerabilities rather than intrinsic viral spread efficiency.

Several mechanistic uncertainties continue to limit prediction and response. Key gaps include the frequency of primary spillover, the contribution of mild or subclinical infection, environmental persistence of MARV, transmission heterogeneity, serial interval estimates, and ecological drivers of seasonal shedding [27,29,35]. These questions cannot be resolved through outbreak data alone. Prospective studies should combine serology, ecological sampling, genomic sequencing, clinical surveillance, and mathematical modelling in populations living or working near reservoir-associated environments. Operational One Health targets for upstream spillover prevention

An integrated One Health surveillance framework for MVD should operate as an ecological intelligence system that links upstream reservoir signals with downstream clinical readiness. Ecological surveillance can identify periods and places of increased spillover risk; decentralized diagnostics can confirm early human cases; genomic sequencing can clarify viral origin and transmission pathways; and regional cross-border One Health systems can coordinate responses across connected ecological corridors.

Operational targets should therefore be positioned across the full spillover–outbreak continuum, from reservoir maintenance and spillover exposure to early infection, human-to-human transmission, outbreak amplification, and recovery. Without this shift, MVD will continue to be detected mainly after the onset of preventable amplification. Priority actions include reducing high-risk human–bat contact, strengthening early clinical recognition, improving infection prevention and control, ensuring rapid diagnosis and case isolation, and sustaining post-outbreak learning and preparedness. Conclusion

MVD is increasingly recognized as a geographically diffuse filoviral zoonosis shaped by reservoir ecology, ecological disturbance, repeated spillover, and uneven surveillance capacity. Recent outbreaks across West, Central, East, and Horn of Africa settings indicate wider recognized risk than historical outbreak records suggested, although interpretation must integrate outbreak data with genomic, ecological, and surveillance evidence. Angola remains the clearest example of how delayed recognition, healthcare-associated transmission and weak early response can amplify MARV spillover into a major public health emergency, while Ethiopia illustrates the surveillance challenges associated with first recognition in an ecologically compatible and mobile regional setting.

Future preparedness should move beyond reactive containment after laboratory confirmation. Priority should be given to ecological risk mapping, longitudinal surveillance of Rousettus aegyptiacus, decentralized molecular diagnostics, real-time genomic sequencing, infection prevention readiness, and cross-border early warning systems. Framing MVD as an ecological-emergence and surveillance challenge provides a more balanced basis for detecting spillover early, limiting human amplification, and reducing the impact of future outbreaks.
