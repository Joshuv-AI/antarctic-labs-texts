Central nervous system infection associated with Human herpesvirus 7 presenting with predominant persecutory delusions as initial psychiatric manifestations after allogeneic stem cell transplantation: a rare case report with diagnostic and therapeutic implications
Authors: Yifan Yao, Ziying Li, Lili Luo, Xuan Lu, Huafang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHuman Herpesvirus 7 (HHV-7)-associated central nervous system (CNS) infection is an extremely rare complication following allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT), with no prior reports of initial presentation dominated by psychiatric symptoms.Case presentationWe report a unique case of a 14-year-old female with high-risk acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) who developed acute persecutory delusions and auditory hallucinations as the sole initial manifestations 54 days post-allo-HSCT. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed multifocal lesions in the right frontal lobe and bilateral parieto-occipital regions. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) confirmed the presence of HHV-7, establishing the diagnosis of HHV-7-associated CNS infection. The patient achieved complete clinical and radiological remission following a comprehensive treatment regimen combining antiviral therapy, glucocorticoids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and antipsychotic medication. ConclusionsThis is the first documented case of HHV-7-associated CNS infection post-allo-HSCT presenting with persecutory delusions as the initial symptom, expanding the clinical spectrum of HHV-7-related CNS complications in immunocompromised hosts. Our findings emphasize the importance of considering atypical viral encephalitis in the differential diagnosis of acute psychiatric symptoms post-allo-HSCT and highlight the value of early neuroimaging and CSF mNGS for timely diagnosis and targeted intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13040-z.

Introduction

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) is a curative therapeutic modality for various hematological malignancies, but recipients face a high risk of opportunistic infections due to prolonged immunosuppression induced by conditioning regimens and graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis and treatment [1]. Human Herpesvirus 7 (HHV-7), a betaherpesvirus that primarily infects CD4+ T cells, is a rare but potentially severe pathogen associated with CNS complications in immunocompromised individuals [2]. Previous reports of HHV-7-related central nervous system (CNS) infections have been predominantly documented in non-allo-HSCT populations, mainly describing manifestations such as brainstem meningitis, optic neuritis, encephalitis, and myelitis—conditions that are often accompanied by severe neurological deficits and carry a poor prognosis [3–5]. In contrast, HHV-7-associated CNS infections in allo-HSCT recipients are extremely rare and lack systematic documentation in the literature. We herein present a unique case of HHV-7-associated CNS infection after allo-HSCT, in which the patient presented with acute psychiatric symptoms as the sole initial manifestation, in the absence of focal neurological signs. This case aims to: [1] expand the recognized clinical manifestations of HHV-7-associated CNS infections; [2] raise awareness among clinicians about this atypical presentation to avoid misdiagnosis; and [3] provide insights into the diagnostic approach and therapeutic management of such complex cases.

Case presentation

Clinical background

A 14-year-old female was diagnosed with high-risk acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) in September 2024, characterized by hyperleukocytosis and positivity for KRAS, PHF6, and PTPN11 mutations. She initiated induction therapy with the IVCP regimen (idarubicin, vincristine, cyclophosphamide, prednisone) on September 12, 2024, and achieved complete remission (CR) after the first cycle. Subsequent consolidation therapy included the VDLP regimen (vincristine, daunorubicin, L-asparaginase, prednisone), blinatumomab, and two doses of prophylactic intrathecal chemotherapy. Serial evaluations post-chemotherapy confirmed sustained CR with negative minimal residual disease (MRD) by flow cytometry and no evidence of CNS involvement by leukemic cells.

On April 11, 2025, the patient underwent a conditioning regimen consisting of etoposide (5 mg/kg every 12 h for 2 days), fludarabine (30 mg/m2/day for 3 days), busulfan (3.2 mg/kg/day for 3 days), and cyclophosphamide (60 mg/kg for 1 day). The patient received our center’s standard GVHD prophylaxis regimen, which was as follows: antilymphocyte globulin (ALG) was administered at 20 mg/kg on day − 4, and 30 mg/kg on days − 3 and − 2. Tacrolimus was given at 0.05 mg/kg twice daily from day − 1. Basiliximab was administered at 25 mg intravenously on days 0 and + 4. Methotrexate was given at 20 mg on day + 1, and 15 mg on days + 3, +6, and + 11. Additionally, mycophenolate mofetil was initiated at 0.5 g orally twice daily from day + 7. On April 23, 2025, she received allo-HSCT using peripheral blood stem cells from her father, a 6/10 HLA-matched donor.

Post-transplant, the patient developed acute hepatic GVHD on day + 20 (May 13, 2025), which was successfully managed with methylprednisolone (40 mg once daily) and ruxolitinib (5 mg twice daily), along with hepatoprotective and supportive care. She was discharged in stable condition. Bone marrow examination on day + 28 confirmed complete donor chimerism.

Acute psychiatric symptom onset and diagnostic evaluations

On day + 54 post-transplant (June 16, 2025), the patient abruptly developed acute psychiatric symptoms, including persistent persecutory delusions (believing family members and medical staff intended to harm her) and auditory hallucinations (hearing threatening voices). Neurological examination was unremarkable, with no focal deficits, meningeal signs, or altered consciousness.

Laboratory investigations on admission revealed: leukocyte count 15.28 × 109/L, hemoglobin 121 g/L, platelets 240 × 109/L, and neutrophils 13.8 × 109/L. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and bilirubin levels were normal; peripheral blood smear showed no fragmented red blood cells, and C5b-9 levels were not significantly elevated. Therapeutic drug monitoring showed serum tacrolimus and posaconazole concentrations within the therapeutic range. The patient’s height was 156 cm with a weight of 43.7 kg, yielding a body mass index (BMI) of 18.0 kg/m².

Brain MRI (June 16, 2025) demonstrated focal swelling and abnormal signals (long T1 and long T2-FLAIR) in the right frontal gyrus and bilateral parieto-occipital regions (Fig. 1a, b). Due to psychomotor agitation, a lumbar puncture was performed under sedation on June 19, 2025. The opening pressure was 120 mmH₂O. CSF analysis showed clear and colorless fluid with a total cell count of 1 × 10⁶/L and a white blood cell count of 1 × 10⁶/L (100% polynuclear cells, 0% mononuclear cells). Protein was negative by qualitative testing, with a quantitative level of 0.19 g/L. CSF glucose was mildly elevated at 4.92 mmol/L. Other CSF parameters—including chloride, AST, LDH, CK, and ADA—were all within normal limits. Autoimmune encephalitis antibody testing on CSF was negative. Flow cytometry found no abnormal immature cells, and mutation testing for KRAS, PHF6, and PTPN11 was also negative. On June 21, 2025 (day + 59 post-transplant), CSF metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) using the MetaCAP™ probe capture-based method detected HHV-7 sequences, with 1,096 reads, 69.10% relative abundance, and 99% confidence (Table 1). No other bacteria, fungi, viruses, parasites, resistance genes, or virulence genes were identified (Supplementary Table 1). A negative control processed in parallel showed no pathogen sequences, excluding background contamination.

Based on these findings, we systematically evaluated potential etiologies. Brain MRI showed focal inflammatory lesions, pointing to CNS involvement and guiding further testing. CNS leukemia relapse was ruled out by negative CSF flow cytometry and mutation testing. Transplant-Associated Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TA-TMA) was excluded by normal LDH, bilirubin, peripheral smear, and C5b-9 levels. Drug-induced neurotoxicity was considered unlikely given therapeutic tacrolimus and posaconazole levels and the lack of symptomatic improvement after these drugs were withdrawn or switched. Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome (PRES) was excluded by the absence of visual disturbances, headache, hypertension, and typical symmetric white matter edema on MRI. Steroid-induced psychosis was improbable as symptoms emerged during a stable low-dose steroid taper. Autoimmune encephalitis was ruled out by negative CSF autoantibody testing. With other causes excluded and imaging findings consistent, HHV-7 detection by CSF mNGS supported the diagnosis of HHV-7-associated CNS infection.

Treatment and outcome

A multidisciplinary team (MDT) consisting of hematologists, neurologists, and psychiatrists was promptly convened to formulate a comprehensive treatment plan. The patient received:

Antiviral therapy: Ganciclovir was chosen empirically. On day + 95 post-transplant (July 27, 2025), follow-up brain MRI showed near-complete resolution of the previously identified lesions in the right frontal lobe and bilateral parieto-occipital regions (Fig. 1c, d; see ESM_1–4 for dynamic pre- and post-treatment MRI videos). The patient’s psychiatric symptoms resolved completely, with restored logical thinking, coherent communication, and no recurrence of persecutory delusions or auditory hallucinations. She was discharged in stable condition.

On day + 181 post-transplant (October 21, 2025), bone marrow examination confirmed sustained complete donor chimerism, MRD negativity, and no recurrence of leukemia or psychiatric symptoms.

Discussion

Pathophysiological basis: HHV-7 neurotropism and symptom localization

HHV-7 is a betaherpesvirus that establishes latency in CD4+ T cells and salivary glands after primary infection [6]. In allo-HSCT recipients, the cumulative immunosuppression from conditioning regimens, GVHD prophylaxis, and treatment of GVHD may predispose patients to reactivation of latent viruses such as HHV-7. While HHV-7’s neurotropism is poorly understood, previous studies have suggested its ability to infect neural cells, leading to focal inflammation and tissue damage [7–9].

In our patient, brain MRI lesions were localized to the right frontal lobe and bilateral parieto-occipital regions—neuroanatomical sites that directly correlate with her psychiatric symptoms. The prefrontal cortex (a key component of the frontal lobe) is critical for executive function, reality monitoring, and emotional regulation; dysfunction in this region is well-documented to cause delusional thinking, including persecutory delusions. The parieto-occipital association cortex, meanwhile, plays a central role in perceptual integration and auditory processing; its impairment has been linked to auditory hallucinations in neuropsychiatric disorders [10, 11]. This suggests a unique tropism of HHV-7 for the prefrontal-parieto-occipital network in our patient, resulting in a purely psychiatric presentation—distinct from the brainstem or spinal cord involvement seen in previous HHV-7 CNS infection cases (Table S2).

Diagnostic approach

This case illustrates the value of systematically excluding common etiologies when encountering atypical psychiatric presentations post-transplant. As described in the case presentation, CNS leukemia relapse, TA-TMA, drug neurotoxicity, PRES, steroid-induced psychosis, and autoimmune encephalitis were all ruled out through comprehensive evaluation. The detection of HHV-7 by CSF mNGS, along with compatible neuroimaging and the exclusion of other causes, contributed to the diagnosis. This stepwise approach—excluding common causes before considering rare pathogens—may be helpful in the diagnostic workup of patients with atypical presentations.

Therapeutic insights

The successful outcome of this case highlights the value of a multidisciplinary, multimodal treatment approach:

Antiviral therapy selection: Currently, no specific antiviral agent is approved for HHV-7 infection. Ganciclovir, acyclovir, and foscarnet have all shown efficacy in published case reports, but there is no established preference among them. Given the patient’s normal blood counts and the concurrent use of multiple nephrotoxic medications—including immunosuppressants and antifungals—ganciclovir was empirically chosen as first-line therapy due to its favorable safety profile and lower nephrotoxicity risk [7–9, 12, 13]. The treatment duration was dynamically adjusted according to the patient’s symptomatic and radiographic recovery, ultimately resulting in a 4-week course.

Immunomodulation: Glucocorticoids were used to reduce CNS inflammation, while IVIG was added to enhance antiviral immunity—particularly critical given the patient’s post-transplant immunosuppression.

Early antipsychotic intervention: Olanzapine was initiated promptly to control psychiatric symptoms, which not only alleviated patient distress but also reduced psychomotor agitation, facilitating diagnostic procedures (e.g., lumbar puncture) and improving treatment adherence.

MDT collaboration: The integration of hematology, neurology, psychiatry, and infectious disease expertise ensured that all aspects of the patient’s condition—transplant-related immunosuppression, viral infection, CNS inflammation, and psychiatric symptoms—were addressed simultaneously.

Balancing GVHD prevention and infection risk

In our center, the doses of ALG and cyclophosphamide in the conditioning regimen were reduced compared to standard protocols, while basiliximab was added on days 0 and + 4 to effectively prevent GVHD [14, 15]. Despite these dose adjustments, the use of ALG and anti-CD25 antibody prior to transplantation still led to profound T-cell depletion, and the subsequent administration of corticosteroids and ruxolitinib for acute GVHD further exacerbated immunosuppression. This inevitably created an environment conducive to the reactivation of opportunistic pathogens such as HHV-7. Therefore, the selection and evaluation of GVHD prophylaxis strategies warrant further investigation, and how to balance effective GVHD prevention with the risk of opportunistic infections remains an important direction for future research.

Notably, previous HHV-7-associated CNS infection cases in allo-HSCT recipients had poor outcomes, likely due to delayed diagnosis and limited treatment of underlying inflammation or psychiatric complications. Our case demonstrates that a proactive, comprehensive approach can lead to complete remission.

Limitations

This case report has several limitations: [1] confirmatory polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for HHV-7 was not performed on CSF or peripheral blood samples; therefore, we could not assess viral load dynamics or correlate systemic and CNS infection; [2] detailed immunological parameters (e.g., CD4+ T cell count, lymphocyte subsets, NK cells, immunoglobulins) were not assessed at symptom onset; [3] long-term neurocognitive outcomes require further follow-up. Despite these limitations, the unique clinical presentation and successful treatment provide valuable insights for clinical practice.

Conclusion

This is the first reported case of HHV-7-associated CNS infection post-allo-HSCT presenting with persecutory delusions and auditory hallucinations as the initial manifestations. Our findings expand the clinical spectrum of HHV-7-associated CNS complications in immunocompromised hosts and emphasize the need to consider atypical viral encephalitis in the differential diagnosis of acute psychiatric symptoms post-allo-HSCT. Early neuroimaging, CSF mNGS, and a multidisciplinary treatment approach are critical for timely diagnosis and improved outcomes.
