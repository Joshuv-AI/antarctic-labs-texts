Surface morphology modulation in chlorine-doped diisopropylammonium bromide film via Cl-induced domain refinement
Authors: Jinghan Gao, Xuanyu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDiisopropylammonium bromide (DIPAB) has great potential in the field of flexible electronics. However, its room temperature ferroelectricity needs thermal treatment above the irreversible phase-transition temperature (T1), which limits its application. Therefore, chlorine-doped DIPAB (DIPAB-C, C6H16NBr1−xClx) was prepared by spin-coating in this study. XPS confirmed successful Cl incorporation into DIPAB. DSC revealed that Cl doping significantly reduces the T1 by up to 41°C, expanding the ferroelectric phase stability window. This doping-induced phase evolution was further confirmed by temperature-dependent XRD analysis. PFM was employed to confirm the ferroelectricity of the DIPAB-C film. XRD and EDS systematically indicated intensified lattice distortion and elemental distribution with increasing doping concentration. Polarized light microscopy (PLM) observations showed Cl doping reduces domain width from 100 μm to 3 μm, substantially enhancing domain wall density. This domain-dominated surface engineering provides a strategy for concurrent manipulation of microstructures and thermal stability in molecular ferroelectrics.

Introduction

Ferroelectrics have been widely used in non-volatile memory, sensors, and energy converters.1,2,3,4,5 Ferroelectrics are mainly divided into two categories which are classical inorganic ferroelectrics and molecular ferroelectrics. Inorganic ferroelectric materials, such as perovskite Pb(Zr,Ti)O3 (PZT), dominate commercial applications currently due to their excellent piezoelectric and ferroelectric properties.6 However, with the rapid development of flexible electronic devices, traditional inorganic ferroelectric materials have shown limitations in terms of flexible adaptability and functional diversity. Consequently, molecular ferroelectrics that combine structural designability and interface compatibility, have regained significant research interest.7 Molecular ferroelectrics, formed by self-assembly of organic or organic-inorganic hybrid molecular building blocks, exhibit switchable spontaneous polarization under applied electric fields.8,9,10,11 Among these molecular ferroelectrics, diisopropylammonium bromide (DIPAB) has attracted much attention due to its easy-processing, light-weight, and flexibility.12,13 DIPAB exhibits a high spontaneous polarization (Pr ∼23 μC/cm2) and a high Curie temperature (Tc ∼153°C) which is even higher than that of the BaTiO3 (Tc ∼120°C). These properties make DIPAB thin films promising candidates for ferroelectric devices.14

The practical applications of ferroelectric devices not only require materials to have excellent intrinsic electrical properties, but also need to maintain stable performance over a wide temperature range.4 For DIPAB, it exhibits two crystal phases at room temperature: the ferroelectric needle-like α-phase (monoclinic system, space group P21) and the non-ferroelectric block-like γ-phase (orthorhombic system, space group P212121). Although choosing methanol as the solvent can directly yield the room temperature ferroelectric α-phase, the needle-like morphology it forms poses difficulties for further processing.9 An alternative solution is to use bulk γ-phase crystals. This phase needs to be heat-treated at a temperature higher than its irreversible phase transition temperature (T1∼148°C) to transform into the room temperature stable ferroelectric α-phase. However, a critical challenge arises because the Curie temperature (Tc∼153°C) of DIPAB is extremely close to T1 (∼148°C), with only about 5°C.9 This narrow window would pose a significant challenge for practical device fabrication and operation. Therefore, it is of great significance to study the temperature-induced phase transition of DIPAB and attempt to explore ways to lower the T1 temperature.

Chemical doping represents a powerful strategy to tailor the phase stability and physical properties of ferroelectrics.15,16,17,18,19,20 In this work, chlorine (Cl) is selected as the dopant to partially substitute bromine (Br) in DIPAB. As members of the same halogen group, Cl and Br share similar ionic radii and bonding characteristics, facilitating substitutional doping at the halide sites while minimizing disruption to the structural integrity. This study systematically investigates chlorine-doped DIPAB (DIPAB-C) thin films. A series of Cl-doped DIPAB films were designed and prepared by spin-coating, and the effects of Cl doping on the phase transition behavior, lattice structure, and domain morphology were characterized and analyzed.

In order to characterize the effect of doping on the phase transition temperature, the DIPAB-C single crystals used as the precursor were systematically analyzed by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). For DIPAB, two key transition temperatures govern its processability and ferroelectricity. The irreversible γ to α transition temperature (T1) is the minimum temperature required to convert the non-ferroelectric γ-phase into the ferroelectric α-phase, which is stable at room temperature. The Curie temperature (Tc) is defined by the reversible α to β transition, at which the material undergoes a ferroelectric-to-paraelectric phase transition and loses its ferroelectricity upon heating.

Figure 2 shows the DSC test results of DIPAB-C powder samples with different doping amounts, in which the area with pink background corresponds to the heating process, while the area with green background corresponds to the cooling process. It is reported that there are three distinct phases for DIPAB including α-phase (P21), γ-phase (P212121), and β-phase (P21/m). At room temperature, DIPAB can stabilize in α-phase and γ-phase, with the former exhibiting ferroelectricity. This material exhibits reversible and irreversible phase transitions induced by temperature. The reversible transition occurs when the α-phase DIPAB is heated to approximately 153°C, undergoing a structural transformation from the ferroelectric α-phase with P21 space group to the centrosymmetric P21/m space group which is β-phase.19,20 This symmetry change eliminates macroscopic polarity, establishing 153°C as the Curie temperature (Tc). Irreversible phase transition means that with the increase of temperature, γ-phase will change into α-phase with ferroelectricity at 148°C. Based on this, heat treatment above this phase transition temperature is an important means to obtain a large number of DIPAB single crystals with ferroelectricity.9 As shown in Figure 2, the heating DSC curve exhibits two exothermic peaks. The peak at lower temperature corresponds to the irreversible γ to α transition (T1-heating), while the sharper peak at higher temperature corresponds to the reversible α to β transition (T2-heating ≈ Tc). During the cooling process, only one endothermic peak appears at the T2-cooling temperature, corresponding to the reversible phase transition from the β-phase to the α-phase.Figure 2Thermal behavior of DIPAB-C single crystalsDSC curves of DIPAB-C single crystals during heating (pink region) and cooling (green region).

Composition-dependent phase transition temperatures in DIPAB-C

In order to further investigate the influence of doping on the phase transition behavior of DIPAB-C, a representative composition with x = 0.6 was selected for temperature-dependent X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. The temperature-dependent structural evolution of the doped crystal (x = 0.6) upon heating is shown in Figure 3. The measured data was displayed as the black curves. The blue and red curves represent the simulated patterns based on the non-ferroelectric phase (space group P212121, CCDC 922546) and the ferroelectric phase (space group P21, CCDC 770675), respectively.9 At 25°C, the XRD pattern matches the simulated γ-phase (blue line), confirming that the as-prepared material adopts the non-ferroelectric γ-phase at room temperature. Upon heating to 120°C, which is above its irreversible phase transition temperature (118°C), the pattern transforms and aligns with the simulated α-phase (red line), confirming the γ to α phase transition induced by thermal treatment. Upon heating to 120°C, which exceeds its phase transition temperature (T1 ∼118°C), the diffraction features change distinctly and align well with the red fitting curve corresponding to the ferroelectric phase. This confirms an irreversible structural transition from the non-ferroelectric to the ferroelectric phase. These results collectively indicate that Cl doping induces this irreversible ferroelectric phase transition in DIPAB-C upon heating, further elucidating the role of doping in modulating the stability of the ferroelectric phase.Figure 3Temperature-dependent X-ray diffraction analysis of DIPAB-C with x = 0.6(A) X-ray diffraction patterns measured at 120°C. Doping-induced surface morphology in DIPAB-C films

To identify the crystal phase and predominant growth orientation of the as-prepared DIPAB-C thin films, XRD characterization was performed on samples with all doping ratios (x = 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, and 0.8). Figure 4 illustrates the structure evolution with doping concentration for the entire series of DIPAB-C thin films. The indexing of diffraction peaks was achieved by comparing with CCDC 770675.9 The XRD patterns of all films clearly show the (001), (002), and (003) diffraction peaks within the 10°–50° range, with no other significant diffraction signals observed. This indicates that all films successfully crystallized and exhibited a highly preferred orientation along the c-axis. Only a very weak diffraction response of (201) and (012) is observed in the patterns, which is most likely attributable to the minimal contribution from a few non-(001) oriented grains, further confirming the excellent crystallinity and high orientational uniformity of the films overall.Figure 4X-ray diffraction patterns of DIPAB-C thin films with varied doping ratios(A) XRD patterns from 10° to 50°.(B) Enlarged XRD patterns from 12.5° to 14° showing the peak shift.

Figure 4B shows the enlarged diffraction peaks of different films between 12.5° and 14°, and it clearly shows the evolution law of crystal plane diffraction peaks of DIPAB-C films under different x values. It can be observed that with the increase of doping concentration x value, the diffraction position of (001) peak changes significantly. The peak position continues to move toward a higher diffraction angle. This phenomenon directly reflects the change of the crystal structure of the film and is the direct evidence of the change of lattice parameters caused by chlorine doping. According to Bragg equation23(Equation 1)nλ=2dsinθWhere λ is the wavelength, d is the interplanar spacing, and θ is the diffraction angle. The diffraction angle θ is inversely proportional to d. When d decreases, sinθ must increase in order to satisfy the equal sign of the equation. The (001) diffraction peak observed in the experiment moves toward the high θ value, which clearly indicates the decrease of interplanar spacing of (001). In this study, chlorine doping partially replaces Br− ion in DIPAB lattice by Cl−. Although Cl− and Br− are both halogen ions, there is still a difference in ion radius. The ion radius of Cl− is nearly 181 pm, while the ion radius of Br− is 196 pm. Therefore, when Cl− is added to occupy the lattice sites of Br−, local lattice contraction will be caused.

In order to observe the surface morphology of as-prepared films with different doping content, SEM was employed for these samples. At the same time, the element distribution measurement with EDS can obtain the information of composition, thereby elucidating the corresponding changes during the crystal structure transformation. Figure 5 shows the relevant results of SEM and EDS. Figures 5A–5E represent the surface morphology of films with different doping amounts x (x = 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, and 0.8) respectively. In each image, the colored part on the left is the EDS element distribution map, while the gray-scale photo on the right is the SEM image. When the doping amount of chlorine is 0, it can be found from Figure 5A that there is a certain scale of area with flat and smooth surface, accompanied by a strip structure with clear edges. However, as the Cl doping amount (x) gradually rises, it is distinctly noticeable that more small bulges or depressions began to appear in the originally smooth area, and the overall roughness showed an upward trend. In sharp contrast to Figure 5A, when the value of x reaches 0.8, it is obvious from Figure 5E that the flatness of this area has a sharp downward trend. In addition, the original flat and smooth state in Figure 5A no longer exists, and a structure with coarsened particles and blurred boundary appears in Figure 5E. This phenomenon may be due to the lattice distortion caused by doping. In addition, the presence of this distortion influences the crystal structure, resulting in the difficulty for the microcrystals to achieve regular stacking on the substrate surface. The left parts of Figures 5A–5E present colored EDS element mapping which display the spatial distribution characteristics of each element in the film visually. The correspondence between elements and colors is specified in the legend at the bottom left of the images, with light blue denoting Cl and purple representing Br. The composite color patterns observed are generated by the superimposition of individual elemental distributions. As illustrated in the elemental mapping, the Cl element exhibits an overall uniform distribution throughout the surface of the thin film. Upon close examination of the image with the same doping content, subtle chromatic deviations at defect sites can be observed from the overall color. This may be caused by segregation during the doping process. As is well known, during the doping process, dopants tend to accumulate at high-energy locations, such as grain boundaries, surfaces, or other defects, forming segregation. By comparing the elemental distribution maps of samples with different nominal doping amounts, it can be seen that as the nominal Cl doping amount (x) increases, the depth of the light-blue region gradually deepens, indicating that the detected amount of Cl element in the actual samples shows an increasing trend.

In order to convert the aforementioned visual observations into quantitative data, the atomic percentages of each element were determined via energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis. In addition, the actual Cl doping level (y) was calculated accordingly. For thin-film samples with a nominal composition of C6H16NBr1−xClx, the actual measured doping ratio of Cl is y, with detailed results presented in Figure 5F. The actual doping level y exhibits a linear increasing trend with the nominal doping amount x as shown in Figure 5F. The phenomenon that the actual doping amount is lower than the nominal value is often reported in the preparation of materials. The reason can be attributed to two aspects. On the one hand, although the radius difference between Cl− and Br− can realize lattice substitution, the incorporation of Cl− will cause local lattice distortion and reduce the stability of the system. On the other hand, the heat treatment process in the preparation process may lead to partial loss of Cl volatilization, resulting in the reduction of the actual retention. These factors can result in the actual doping amount being lower than the nominal doping amount. However, the linear correlation between y and x shows that the doping system has good process controllability, and the actual Cl content can be controlled by adjusting the nominal doping amount, which lays the foundation for the subsequent material performance optimization.

Doping-mediated domain engineering in DIPAB-C films

To verify the ferroelectricity of DIPAB-C films directly at the microscopic scale, domain writing via piezoelectric force microscopy (PFM) were conducted on a representative composition x = 0.6. As shown in Figure 6, by applying a ±30 V DC bias to a local area of the surface, controllable switching of the ferroelectric polarization can be achieved. The PFM phase image clearly shows a nearly 180° reversal of the phase signal in the poled region, while the amplitude signal exhibits a pronounced decrease in the same area, consistent with the reduced piezoelectric response at ferroelectric domain walls. The written domain pattern remains stable after the bias is removed, confirming its non-volatile nature. This result provides direct evidence that the DIPAB-C films possess well-defined ferroelectricity at room temperature.Figure 6Piezoresponse force microscopy of the DIPAB-C with x = 0.6(A) Phase image, scale bars: 1 μm.(B) Amplitude image, scale bars: 1 μm.

Discussion

The correlation between structure and performance of materials is considered as the effective means of functional material design. The optimization of performance through microstructure regulation has been widely verified by a large number of researches.24,25 Among them, surface topography engineering has an important impact on the optical and electrical properties of thin film materials. It has been used as a key means for the modification of films and it has achieved remarkable results. For example, photonic crystals can induce special optical responses by constructing long-range ordered surfaces which is similar to single crystals.26 In ferroelectric system, domain structure, including domain width and domain wall density, has been reported to be related to its performance.27,28 This microstructural design of domain engineering can be induced by the coordination of multiple external fields or by doping.29,30 In classical inorganic ferroelectric materials, it has been found that the design of domain configuration can enhance the properties of ferroelectrics. Theoretical calculations and experiments show that the dielectric constant of the stationary domain wall in PZT is 6–78 times that of the intrinsic domain body phase.27 Researchers have found that optimizing the domain structure of BTO crystal can reduce the domain size and increase the domain wall density.28,31 This phenomenon implies that doping may cause changes in the surface microstructure. Therefore, it is of great significance to explore the effect of chlorine doping (x) on DIPAB-C films under polarized light.

Polarized light microscopy (PLM), as a non-contact characterization technique, enables effective observation of domain structures by leveraging the birefringent properties of materials, without requiring external mechanical stress or electric fields. Its operational principle relies on detecting polarization-dependent optical contrast arising from domain-specific birefringence. In ferroelectric materials, different domains usually have different optical anisotropy due to their different polarization directions. Therefore, the existence and distribution of ferroelectric domains can be observed by PLM. It is crucial to note that PLM contrast originates from differences in the orientation of the optical indicatrix within the material. Therefore, PLM is primarily sensitive to ferroelastic non-180° domains, where changes in spontaneous polarization are accompanied by a lattice shear, leading to a reorientation of the crystallographic axes and thus a change in birefringence. In contrast, 180° ferroelectric domains, where the polarization vectors are antiparallel, possess identical crystallographic orientation and optical indicatrix. Consequently, they exhibit no birefringence contrast and remain invisible under standard PLM observation. PLM can avoid the interference of weak external electric field and force, therefore, it has been extensively employed for domain characterization across diverse ferroelectric systems including conventional inorganic ceramics and molecular ferroelectrics. In the observation of PLM, the sample was placed between the cross polarization field formed by polarizer (P) and analyzer (A). Specifically, when the domain direction of ferroelectric material is parallel or perpendicular to the vibration direction of polarized light, the light will not birefringence, and the domain will show a dark state. When the direction of the domain is at a certain angle with the direction of the polarized light, the light will birefringence and the domain will appear bright. When the stage is rotated, the periodic changes of light and dark areas can be observed. During full-stage rotation in PLM characterization of DIPAB-C films, exactly four discrete extinction events were observed per 360° revolution, corresponding to sample orientations of 0°, 90°, 180°, and 270°. This result aligns with the phenomena in other studies on DIPAB.31,32,33

Figure 7 is a diagram of the domain structure observed by a PLM. Figures 7A–7E represents the films doped with Cl at x = 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, and 0.8, respectively. Domain structure characterization across all compositions reveals well-defined stripe-like domain patterns. The observed striped domain is consonant with domain morphologies which were reported in both PZT and molecular ferroelectric systems. Close observation of the stripe-like domains reveals a pronounced doping-dependent variation in domain width, exhibiting systematic evolution across compositions. As it is shown in Figure 7A, the undoped films display coarse domains (∼100 μm width). This kind of large-sized domain has also been reported in the Pb-based ferroelectric system.34 With increasing Cl-doping concentration, domain widths become narrow notably. This trend is exemplified by representative domains within the yellow circle in Figure 7C, where domain widths are approximately 25 μm. As the Cl doping concentration increases, dense domains are observed in Figure 7E, where the width within the yellow dashed circle decreases to approximately 3 μm. This trend is summarized and shown in Figure 7F. As demonstrated in Figure 7F, domain width undergoes nonlinear reduction versus doping concentration. An initial steep decline transitions is observed at lower doping levels. As the doping level increased, the decreasing trend of the domain width slowed down. To elucidate this composition-driven surface engineering mechanism, the physical origin of domain wall formation must be addressed. In ferroelectrics, small regions with uniform spontaneous polarization are termed domains. Adjacent domains are classified as 180° or non-180° domains based on polarization orientation differences. For 180° domains, antiparallel polarization vectors share identical optic axes, which makes them invisible under PLM without external stimuli. Therefore, non-180° domains are observed in this study. Such domains exhibit both ferroelasticity and ferroelectricity. From the perspective of total energy Etotal, the following formula can be expanded as35:(Equation 2)Etotal=Eelastic+Ewall

Eelastic refers to elastic energy, which decreases monotonically with the increase of domain wall density because domain wall refinement can effectively release lattice strain. Ewall represents domain wall energy, which increases linearly with the increase of the number of domains. The domain configuration evolves toward minimizing the total free energy. According to Kittel model, the equilibrium domain width is determined by the competition between the elastic energy (Eelastic) and the domain wall energy (Ewall).36 From the XRD results which was discussed previously, it can be found that as the Cl doping amount increases, the lattice distortion becomes more and more severe. The intensification of lattice distortion significantly increases the elastic energy. According to the Kittel scaling relation, a significant increase in Eelastic provides a strong driving force for the reduction of domain width, which is fully consistent with the domain refinement observed by PLM.

When dopant ions induce amplified atomic displacements, non-coordinated variations in bond lengths expand local strain (ε). According to Hooke’s law, accumulated stress scales linearly with strain, while the stored elastic energy density follows a quadratic growth law (Eelastic∝ ε2).37 Therefore, a small increase in the degree of distortion can trigger a significant increase in elastic energy, providing the driving force for the refinement of domains and the formation of domain walls. It is worth noting that with the increase of the number of domain walls, Ewall will also increase in a positive proportion. The nonlinear saturation trend of domain width reduction at higher doping levels in Figure 7F can be understood as the result of the system reaching an energy minimum, balancing the drastically increased Eelastic against the concomitant rise in Ewall. Although the domain size is small when the doping amount is high, the decreasing trend of domain width is slowed down. In this way, the domain wall density and distribution were reconstructed by adjusting the composition, and the domain structure was controlled. The mesoscopic structure can be controlled by using this surface topography engineering, which provides a platform for exploring the mechanism of improving the thermal stability of DIPAB films, and may help to achieve the regulation of performance.

In this study, the thin films of DIPAB-C were successfully prepared and systematically studied. The results demonstrate that Cl doping effectively shifts the irreversible phase transition temperature (T1) toward lower temperatures accompanied by a slight increase in Tc. XRD analysis reveals that the diffraction peaks shift toward higher angles with increasing doping concentration, indicating lattice distortion induced by Cl incorporation. Quantitative surface composition analysis further demonstrates that the actual Cl doping concentration exhibits a linear increase proportional to the nominal doping level. Further investigations on the domain structures of the doped films reveal that with increasing Cl-doping concentration, the domain size decreases substantially while the domain wall density increases correspondingly. This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the strain energy modulation arising from doping-induced lattice distortion, which finally alters the domain configurations. The doping-induced surface engineering strategy proposed in this work provides an effective way to control the domain structure and properties of ferroelectric materials. Precise control of Cl-doping concentration enables refinement of domain size, enhancement of domain wall density, and coordinated tuning of T1. These findings are of great significance for optimizing the application performance of ferroelectric materials in memory, sensors and other fields.

Limitations of the study

Within this work, there are still limitations that deserve to be pointed out. Firstly, the actual doping level of Cl in the thin film is lower than the nominal doping amount. Although there is a linear relationship between them, the influence of concentration deviation still needs to be considered. In addition, other doping ions are not attempted in this work. Therefore, the modification effects of different potential dopants are not discussed in this work, which limits the understanding of the general regularity of doping. Moreover, the film thickness was estimated from cross-sectional optical micrographs, providing a value on the order of tens of micrometers. This method has inherent precision limitations that constitute a methodological constraint in this study. This study also did not investigate the effect of environmental humidity on material properties such as ferroelectricity and domain stability.

Method details

Preparation of DIPAB-C film

The C6H15N (DIPA), HBr, and CH3OH were purchased from Macklin Biological Reagent Co., Ltd. HCl was purchased from Tianjin Xinqiao Chemical Trading Co., Ltd. All aqueous solutions were prepared using deionized (DI) water.

In order to further explore the effect of doping on the performance of DIPAB, a series of chlorine doped diisopropylammonium bromide compounds (C6H16NBr1−xClx, DIPAB-C) were designed and synthesized. Spin coating method was used to prepare DIPAB-C films in this work. The process encompasses two critical stages which are precursor solution synthesis and thin film preparation.

The precursor solution for spin-coating needs to be prepared in advance before film preparation. The specific preparation process of the precursor solution is as follows. Firstly, C6H16NBr1−xClx single crystals with different doping concentrations should be grown by adjusting the chlorine doping ratio (x = 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8). These DIPAB-C single crystals were grown by slow evaporation method. Diisopropylamine (DIPA) and mixed hydrohalic acids were dissolved in methanol at strictly stoichiometric ratios. The acid mixture comprised HBr and HCl in a molar ratio of (1-x): x, while the total acid-to-DIPA ratio was maintained at 1:1 mol/mol. Slow evaporation at room temperature for one week yielded DIPAB-C single crystals. These crystals were heated to obtain bulk room temperature ferroelectric phase crystal through irreversible phase transition. Subsequently, these synthesized DIPAB-C crystals were ground into powder, dissolved in methanol, and preheated in a water bath in order to obtain a spin-coating solution.

Prior to thin film preparation, SiO2 substrates were sequentially ultrasonicated in acetone and ethanol for 10 min per solvent, followed by comprehensive rinsing with ultrapure water and drying under a stream of nitrogen gas. Afterward, DIPAB-C thin films were prepared by spin coating method. A KW-4A spin coater was used for thin film deposition, and the acceleration parameter was set to 9 s from 0 r/min to 2000 r/min. When the target rotation speed was reached, the spin-coating solution was dropped onto the substrate surface, and then it was kept rotating at a high speed of 2000 r/min for 30 s to form a uniform thin film. The thin film samples obtained by spin coating were heat-treated at a high temperature for 10 min and then cooled to obtain the thin film.

Characterization

Surface chemical composition of the as-grown DIPAB-C single crystal was characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, K-Alpha, Thermo Scientific, USA). The thermal properties of as-prepared DIPAB-C single crystal were characterized by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, Q2000, TA Instruments, USA). The temperature-dependent phase structure of DIPAB-C powders was characterized by X-ray powder diffraction (XRD, MiniFlex600-C, Rigaku Corporation, Japan). The phase structure of the as-prepared films was determined by X-ray powder diffraction (XRD, UItima IV, Rigaku Corporation, Japan). The obtained XRD patterns were processed with Kα2 stripping to eliminate peak asymmetry for accurate phase identification. Morphology of the DIPAB-C film was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, MIRA, Tescan, Czech Republic), while the chemical composition was analyzed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, Xplore 30, Oxford Instruments, UK). The PFM experiments were carried out to characterize the ferroelectric domains using a commercial microscope (PFM, Jupiter XR, Asylum Research, USA). The observation of the domain structure on the surface of the thin-film sample was carried out with the help of a polarizing light microscope (PLM, LK200P, Lytek Optics, China).
