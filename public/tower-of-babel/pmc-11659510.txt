Penitentiaries: Bringing microbiological literacy to the fringes of society
Authors: Víctor de Lorenzo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis report highlights a science outreach effort for prisons launched by the Spanish National Research Council (CSIC) in collaboration with the NGO Solidarios para el Desarrollo. The Microbiology‐focused part of the initiative aims at educating inmates on some basic facts, in order to raise awareness about microorganisms and their impact on daily life. The outline of the talks, inspired by the International Initiative for Microbial Literacy, aims to encourage this collective to move from passive listeners into active participants, helping them understand that Earth is a microbial planet, and that their bodies harbour vast microbiomes that affect their health and social interactions. The talks introduce Microbiology using simple metaphors and emphasize the role of beneficial microorganisms. By explaining the power of microscopes, inmates are shown the hidden microbial world that surrounds them, sparking interest and curiosity. The talks also cover microbial biotechnology, using examples such as bioplastics, anti‐cavity bacteria, and skin microorganisms designed for acne prevention. Overall, this outreach initiative seeks to provide inmates with valuable scientific knowledge, fostering curiosity and critical thinking. Despite the challenges of delivering such content in a prison setting, the initiative demonstrates that even marginalized groups can benefit from microbiological literacy, helping them to both endure their terms and eventually reintegrate into society.

INTRODUCTION

Despite significant variations in the numbers and conditions, nearly all countries confine a portion of their population under a judicial mandate in response to actions deemed harmful to society. Regardless of the offence committed, the length of the sentence, or its fairness, prisons ultimately hold a group of individuals who are involuntarily deprived of freedom and often disconnected from, or indifferent to, scientific pursuits—let alone generally unaware of the presence and role of microorganisms in daily life. While penitentiary practices differ widely, many countries increasingly embrace the idea that prison should serve not just as punishment, but as an opportunity for rehabilitation and return to society. The goal is to assist those incarcerated to reintegrate as informed, capable citizens. For instance, the Spanish Constitution asserts in Article 25.2 that ‘… Prison sentences and security measures shall be aimed at re‐education and social reintegration…In all cases, he or she shall have the right to…access to culture and the comprehensive development of his or her personality…’. Although this mandate is not always fully realized, the ultimate aim is not simply punitive, but to return individuals to the human endeavour in a better state than when they entered. To support this, the Spanish penitentiary system has implemented numerous programs—often in collaboration with non‐governmental organizations (NGO)—offering education at all levels, from primary school to university, for inmates who wish to participate. These programs provide numerous incentives to encourage engagement during their sentences. Even in the case of very long terms, reality is that life continues inside the prisons, microorganisms keep on interacting with both inmates and prison officers and some basic information on their interplay with them can both ease the severity of such situations and open perspectives of becoming more aware of one's surroundings during and after the time in jail. In this context, the Spanish National Research Council (CSIC) initiated years ago a partnership with the local NGO Solidarios para el Desarrollo (Solidaries for Development; https://www.solidarios.org.es), for launching an ambitious science outreach effort for inmates in the Madrid region and other territories. This initiative called on scientists from the CSIC, many of whom responded with considerable interest (Figure 1).

The prison population in Madrid in July 2024 totalled 7371 individuals (6625 males and 746 females) across nine facilities. This collective is classified into different degrees of confinement, from Level 1 (most restrictive) to Level 3 (least restrictive). The talks are primarily targeted to Level 2 inmates, who stand to benefit the most from educational and divulgation initiatives aimed at preparing them for their after‐term life, as well as remand prisoners who are in custody while waiting for a definitive sentence. For a group of Spanish microbiologists inspired by the International Initiative for Microbial Literacy (Timmis et al., 2019, 2024; https://imili.org/) the CSIC/Solidarios collaboration presented an opportunity to enhance the microbiological knowledge of one of the most uninformed yet directly affected groups when it comes to the role of microorganisms in daily life. However, disseminating microbiological knowledge within prisons comes with its own set of significant challenges.

BEYOND MERE DIVULGATION: ENGAGING UNINFORMED HEARERS

Microbiology has the unique advantage, compared to other fields of science, of being relatively easy to connect to our everyday lives, even for those without any prior familiarity with the topic. This makes the contents of a talk on the issue more inherently accessible and understandable. After all, nearly everyone has experienced an infected wound or earache, or has eaten cheese and drank beer. Yet, in order to effectively share scientific information with an alien audience—especially one that may be entirely unfamiliar with the subject—it is not enough to simply aim for basic clarity. One must engage listeners in a way that transforms them from passive spectators into active participants. While a one‐way presentation may briefly entertain and capture attention, it leaves little lasting impact if the audience feels too disconnected from the topic. In our case, the ultimate goal is to lead them from being mere spectators, to participants in the conversation, and eventually to agents of action. But how to make that happen?

Experience from talks in various prisons shows that the most effective way to communicate our relationship with microorganisms to inmates (and other groups of scientifically lay persons), begins with inviting a reflection on what the much (ab)used term biodiversity (that everyone has heard or read in the media) really is. The way we perceive and measure biodiversity changes dramatically depending on whether we have access to a microscope. With even the most basic tool like the one invented by van Leeuwenhoek (van Zuylen, 1981) we start to distinguish life forms that are entirely foreign to our everyday, macroscopic experience. As the resolution and zooming‐in increases, we begin to observe microscopic animals for example, bedbugs, followed by protozoa, fungi, yeasts, and ultimately bacteria. What immediately captures the attention of a prison audience is that, with the help of a simple tool—a microscope—one discovers that behind our familiar settings an entire invisible world exists that shapes our lives around the clock. The talk typically progresses by presenting data on the percentage of microbial biomass (i.e. live agency) on the globe (Bar‐On et al., 2018) leading to the conclusion that Earth is fundamentally a microbial sphere and we humans are but a recent, transient accident in planetary history. From there it follows that Microorganisms are ubiquitous and omnipresent, including within our own bodies.

THE TIPPING POINT: THE MICROBIOME WITHIN US

That microbes are all over the place sets the stage for the part of the presentation that systematically captures the most attention and elicits more awe (Figure 2): the explanation that our own bodies host a vast microbiome, abundant in mass (Sender et al., 2016), and rich in diversity (Gomaa, 2020; Pflughoeft & Versalovic, 2012). And that the bacteria we live with plays a critical role not only in our health, but also in influencing virtually all our daily activities through emission and reception of signals that affect our mood and social interactions. Moreover, the idea that each of us is haloed by a distinct microbial cloud (Meadow et al., 2015), that bacteria are transmitted through physical contact (kisses, handshakes, hugs; Mela & Whitworth, 2014; Kort et al., 2014) and that they have a significant impact not only in our health but also in our personality, humour and sociality through the gut‐brain axis (Appleton, 2018; Mayer et al., 2015) often makes a strong impression on the audience. Some inmates were interested at this point on whether being under dysbiosis or being colonized by a mood‐influencing microbiome (Capuco et al., 2020) could be considered attenuating circumstances for lessening the culpability of the offender in a trial. While this issue has not received any formal attention and could be considered by some an scenario of Twinkie defence (Solnick & Hemenway, 2012) the inquiry shows how individuals can quickly grasp the multi‐tiered connection of everyone's microbiome with own specific circumstances.

Sample of images shown in the slides of divulgative talks to prison inmates that conspicuously elicit curiosity and interest. For this collective, who has generally only heard about microorganisms as pathogens to avoid, it is interesting to observe the reactions provoked by the speaker's narrative about bacteria as intimate inhabitants of every imaginable location, including our bodies. In particular, the audience reacts with surprise to the data on how the microbiome is able to influence not only our health but also our mood, even our interactions with others. Inmates express also puzzlement when told that the World is a microbial planet and that microorganisms were here before we arrived and will continue to be here when we become extinct.

MICROBIAL BIOTECHNOLOGY: DOMESTICATING BACTERIA FOR HUMAN BENEFIT

If we increase the magnification of live items further with adequate instruments, we encounter DNA—the blueprint of life—which, with modern techniques, can be modified to create something entirely new. The presentation to the inmates therefore concludes with an introduction to microbial biotechnology and the potential of genetic engineering (or redoing that DNA blueprint) to improve our lives and help the planet. The grand finale of the story is an overview of how many microorganisms have been domesticated for human use throughout history, and how, in recent decades, it has become possible to engineer them rationally by means modifying DNA to enhance their biotechnological value. It is remarkable that the choice of simple, adequate metaphors about DNA as a tape bearing a code that can be creatively amended (Temmerman, 2001), makes understanding of the basics of modern biotechnology quite easy to grasp even for such an unfamiliar audience. To illustrate this, it is helpful to discuss some biotechnological achievements with microorganisms that often surprise those unacquainted with the field. Apart of the customary narrative on for example, fermentations and bioplastics (Luengo et al., 2003), expression of camel antibodies (Zafra et al., 2011) or mouse metallothioneins (Valls et al., 2000) in soil bacteria for bioremediation purposes, or jellyfish fluorescent proteins for detection of anti‐personal mines (Garmendia et al., 2008) are examples that use to cause long lasting impressions. Other jaw‐dropping instances included faecal transplants (Kaakoush, 2020) as well as the production of molecules by certain oral bacteria that inhibit the development of cavities (López‐Santacruz et al., 2021), their potential transmission through kissing and their future exploitation in tooth paste (Ferrer et al., 2020; Zaura & Twetman, 2019). The discussion of the skin microbiome and the ongoing attempts to modify it for preventing acne and other dermatological problems (Paetzold et al., 2019) also provides an opportunity to revisit hygiene habits, sparking considerable interest.

THE CHALLENGE OF COMMUNICATING MICROBIOLOGY BASICS IN A SHORT SESSION

Experience shows that the optimal extension of the whole activity should be 1 h and a half to 2 h. Within this time frame, more than 1 h is dedicated to the explanation proper which is often interrupted by welcome questions by the inmates on the spot. And the rest goes into discussion on a variety of topics connected to the talk itself or related issues. In a microbiology‐oriented talk it is unavoidable to have many questions on infections, antibiotics resistances, allergies and vaccines. Throughout the talk, it is crucial to emphasize that the majority of microorganisms are beneficial. However, as in any family, there are a few black sheep—pathogenic bacteria—which tend to garner more attention, overshadowing their truly beneficial counterparts. Yet, the main agenda of these divulgation talks is to highlight the valuable roles of microorganisms and to increase the daily awareness of our interplay with them at every point of time and space. This perspective is complemented by subsequent talks by other microbiologists in the same divulgation program on bacterial and viral pathogens. It is truly extraordinary that even a brief exploration of the microbial world can ignite such curiosity in this audience, perhaps because it reveals that the topic is far more intertwined with our daily lives than most people realize. Though the information that can be provided about the microbial world is necessarily brief due to time constraints, it consistently sparks a remarkable level of interest, curiosity, and excitement among the prison audience—often more so than we have seen in outreach efforts with other target collectives. The questions and discussions that follow are remarkable, marked by sharp insights and enthusiastic, uninhibited inquiry. In our experience, this activity not only provides immediate enlightenment for the participants but also fosters a deeper desire to learn more about Microbiology and Science in general. It is deeply gratifying to see that Microbiology, as a field, is accessible to almost anyone—even those living on the fringes of society, such as the prison population. Moreover, it serves as a gateway to other areas of curiosity and plays a vital role in helping to create informed, engaged individuals, regardless of their circumstances.

CONCLUSION

Despite the limited scope of the activities described above, the CSIC/Solidarios efforts to promote scientific literacy among groups traditionally seen as challenging provide tangible proof that basic awareness of Microbiology can be just as essential to daily life as knowing how to read, drive a car, or operate household appliances. This kind of knowledge is not merely a curiosity or a luxury, but something that we should be aware of throughout our lives, in all kinds of situations, including difficult ones. The data from the outreach initiative for jails described above highlights that microbiological literacy is most effective when tailored to specific audiences, rather than delivered in a generic, top‐down, one‐size‐fits‐all approach. It also exposes the importance of reaching out collectives at the margins of society and of fostering engagement that goes beyond mere flamboyant divulgation shows. Paraphrasing the Spanish philosopher Ortega y Gasset's motto ‘…I am I and my circumstances…’ (Carpintero, 2006) the reality is ‘…We are we and our microbiomes…’ regardless of whether we are free citizens or inmates behind bars.
