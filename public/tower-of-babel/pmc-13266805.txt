Standardization of Chinese speech tasks for heart failure voice analysis: a cross-sectional diagnostic classification study
Authors: Hanyue Zhang, Changxin Li, Hangyu Li, Rong Sheng, Si Sun, Qian Chen, Yunfei Meng, Yifan Yao, Liwei Liu, Ming Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVoice analysis, a non-invasive and cost-effective approach, shows potential for improving heart failure (HF) management. Previous studies mainly focused on non-Chinese voice features, and speech tasks tailored to Chinese HF patients remain lacking. This study aimed to design and optimize speech tasks for Chinese HF patients and establish a foundation for a Chinese HF voice database.MethodsA total of 203 participants (101 HF patients and 102 healthy controls) were recruited from The Affiliated Taizhou People’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. Based on Chinese phonetics, 12 speech tasks were developed, including vowels, sentences, numbers, and the paragraph. Voice recordings were obtained for all participants. Binary logistic regression was used to analyze factors associated with task completion. Differences in voice features between groups were assessed using independent t-tests (P<0.05), and classification performance was evaluated using a fully connected neural network (FNN) based on the ComParE 2016 and eGeMAPSv02 feature sets.ResultsAmong all participants, 105 (51.7%) completed all tasks (Group A), whereas 98 (48.3%) failed due to reading (75.5%) or execution problems (67.3%). Regression analysis revealed that age [middle-aged, odds ratio (OR) =3.716; young, OR =27.28], education (college or higher, OR =6.919), and task difficulty (easy, OR =14.185) significantly influenced completion rates (P<0.05). After removing six low-completion tasks (tasks 2, 3, 6, 8, 10, and 12), an optimized six-task set (Group B) increased the completion rate to 92.1%, particularly for participants with lower education (6.9% to 35.5%) and older age (15.3% to 50.7%). The ComParE 2016 feature set extracted 1,617 (task “i”) to 3,039 (task “mm”) features, while the eGeMAPSv02 feature set extracted 19 (task “i”) to 53 (task “mm”) features. Tasks “pg”, “mm”, and “1” demonstrated greater discriminative capacity (F-values are 67.5, 54.4, and 49.4, respectively) and superior classification performance (F1 scores are 0.817, 0.813, and 0.774, respectively). FNN models based on ComParE 2016 achieved higher performance than those based on eGeMAPSv02 (P<0.05), but there was no significant difference in the FNN models based on two datasets after demographic adjustment (P=0.16).ConclusionsThis study developed standardized Chinese speech tasks for HF patients, identified factors influencing task completion, and demonstrated the feasibility of voice-based classification using both ComParE 2016 and eGeMAPSv02 feature sets. These findings support the development of large-scale voice databases and artificial intelligence models for early detection and management of HF in Chinese populations.

Highlight box

Key findings

• A 12-task Chinese speech protocol for heart failure (HF) was developed and optimized to 6 tasks, improving task completion from 51.7% to 92.1%.

• Age, education, and task difficulty were key factors influencing task completion.

• The optimized tasks, particularly “pg”, “mm”, and “1”, showed the better discriminative performance.

• The ComParE 2016 feature set demonstrated higher classification performance than eGeMAPSv02, although this difference was attenuated after demographic adjustment.

What is known and what is new?

• Speech biomarkers for HF have been reported, but task design has not been established for Chinese populations.

• We propose a standardized Chinese speech-task framework and identify the tasks with the best feasibility and discriminative utility.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Voice-based HF monitoring can enhance telemedicine and home-based management. Future work should include multi-dialect populations and build multimodal predictive models.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a severe cardiovascular disease affecting millions of individuals worldwide. In China, mortality among patients with HF remains high, particularly in those with reduced ejection fraction (1), imposing a substantial burden on the healthcare system (2). Effective strategies for the prevention, monitoring, and treatment of HF are urgently needed.

Current HF monitoring relies primarily on body weight measurement, physical examination for edema, detection of natriuretic peptides, cardiac imaging, and functional assessments (3). Recent advances in hemodynamic or pulmonary artery pressure monitoring, such as CardioMEMS (4,5), ReDS (6), and MultiSENSE (7), have enabled earlier detection of HF decompensation. However, these approaches are often expensive, invasive, and thus limited to patients with severe HF. Consequently, most patients with HF still lack objective, convenient, and non-invasive tools for early detection and continuous monitoring.

Non-invasive voice analysis has emerged as a promising approach for HF detection and monitoring (8-10). HF-related fluid accumulation in the lungs and laryngeal tissues may alter phonation, even at early stages, suggesting that subtle voice changes could serve as early indicators of HF progression (11-14). Despite this potential, traditional voice feature extraction often relies on subjective assessment, limiting clinical applicability. Recent advances in machine learning and voice processing technologies offer new opportunities to overcome these limitations (15,16).

Voice-based assessment offers several advantages in HF management. First, it may enable early detection of decompensation, for example, pause ratio has been identified as a potential marker of acute HF deterioration (17). Second, voice data, like creak percentage, fundamental frequency (F0), and cepstral peak prominence (CPP) standard deviation (SD), can be collected remotely, supporting continuous monitoring through telemedicine (11). Third, voice recording is entirely non-invasive and imposes minimal physical or psychological burden on patients.

Previous studies have investigated HF-related voice features using different speech tasks, including sustained vowels (e.g., /a/) (11,12), words reading (13,17), sentences (11,12,16,18), number counting (17), and standardized passages such as the “rainbow passage” (11,12). These studies involved speakers of non-Chinese languages, including English, Finnish, and German, and some may be measured in multilingual speech tasks. Given the substantial semantic and linguistic differences between Chinese and these languages, these tasks may not be directly applicable to Chinese HF patients. Therefore, it is imperative to develop speech tasks tailored to the linguistic characteristics of Chinese.

Machine learning approaches have been applied to classify HF using speech features. For example, Kiran Reddy et al. (13) extracted mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCC) and glottal features and trained four classifiers, including support vector machine, extra tree, AdaBoost, and feed-forward neural network (FFNN), to distinguish HF patients from healthy controls. MFCC-based models demonstrated higher accuracy, with FFNN achieving the best performance. In China, research on speech-based disease monitoring has focused on neurological and laryngeal disorders, using tasks such as sustained vowels (e.g., /a/, /i/, /u/), repeated syllables (e.g., /pakala/), and situational dialogues (19-22). However, voice features in Chinese HF patients have not been systematically investigated.

This study aims to develop a set of speech tasks specifically for Chinese HF patients and to establish a standardized Chinese speech database to support HF detection and management in both decompensated and stable phases. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0150/rc).

Methods

Study design and participants

There are currently no studies reporting effect sizes of speech features specifically in Chinese HF patients. This study adopted a conservative estimate, setting the effect size of the between-group difference at a medium level (Cohen’s d=0.5) (23). Using G*Power 3.1 software, with a two-tailed test, a significance level of 𝛼=0.05, and power 1−𝛽 =0.80, the sample size per group was calculated to be 64. Considering a 20% dropout rate and the need for subsequent subgroup analyses, this study planned to enroll 100 participants in the HF group and 100 participants in the healthy control group.

Participants were recruited from The Affiliated Taizhou People’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University between June 2023 and December 2024. Of the 244 consecutively enrolled participants, 27 withdrew immediately, and 14 were excluded due to contraindications to voice recording. All participants provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This single-center cross-sectional diagnostic classification study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Taizhou People’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (No. KY2023-073-01).

The inclusion criteria for HF patients were the following: (I) age 18–80 years; (II) a clinical diagnosis of HF according to the 2024 Chinese Guidelines (3), including typical symptoms or signs, elevated natriuretic peptides, and objective evidence of cardiac dysfunction confirmed by echocardiography: left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) <50% or LVEF ≥50% with N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) ≥125 pg/mL or B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) ≥35 pg/mL; (3) All patients had at least one episode of decompensated HF in the previous 6 months that resulted in a hospital admission. Healthy participants were mainly recruited from hospital staff and patients’ relatives, aged 18 to 80 years, with no history of chronic diseases (e.g., HF) and no regular use of medications. Admission for HF patients was decided by a senior physician blinded to the recording analysis.

Participants were excluded if they had significantly impaired glomerular filtration rate (<25 mL/min), current dialysis treatment, congenital heart disease, mechanical heart valves, sepsis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, respiratory infection, or a history of diagnosed vocal cord pathology or dysfunction.

Speech tasks

The Chinese phonetic system is composed of vowels (such as a, o, e, i, u, ü, etc.) and consonants, which are further divided into voiced consonants (such as r, m, n, l, j, w) and voiceless consonants (such as b, d, g, s, x, z, p, t, k, c, q) (22). Chinese exhibits a unique prosodic and rhythmic structure, and the prosodic hierarchy of Chinese can be simplified into three levels, including tone, tone-group, and intonational unit, to describe pitch variations caused by the superimposition of intonation on lexical tones, as well as duration changes caused by rhythm layered on tonal patterns (24).

Therefore, we designed 12 speech tasks (Group A) in four aspects: vowel phonation, sentence reading, number counting, and paragraph reading.

In speech tasks, tasks 7 and 8 were designed to capture the features of voiced sounds, like m, n, and l, while tasks 9 and 10 were designed to capture the features of voiceless sounds, like s, d, p, g, and t. Participants were asked to count from 1 to 100 and read the paragraph in Chinese, ensuring the inclusion of both voiced and voiceless sounds. Vowels (a, o, e, i, u, ü) were represented across all tasks.

Voice recording

The voice was recorded using a Xiaomi Pad 5 Pro 12.4 in a quiet room (ambient noise <50 dBA), handheld by the research staff, at a sampling rate of 22,050 Hz. Microphone-to-mouth distance was maintained at approximately 20 cm. Participants were instructed by research staff to perform speech tasks naturally in a natural manner. All voice recordings were labelled with collection date, demographic information (ID, sex, age), and HF status. For HF patients, voice recordings and clinical data were collected by the research staff, like New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification and echocardiography (Figure S1).

After the voice recording, all participants were asked to answer a feedback questionnaire, which included task completion status, task difficulty (easy, moderate, or difficult), and problems (Figure S2). Those who failed to complete speech tasks were asked to provide reasons for non-completion.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0. Normally distributed continuous variables were described as mean ± SD and analyzed with independent t-tests. Non-normally distributed data were described as median (P25, P75) and analyzed using non-parametric tests. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, and comparisons were performed using the chi-square (χ2) test. Binary logistic regression was conducted to analyze factors associated with speech task performance (P<0.05).

Feature extraction

As illustrated in Figure 1, this study utilized openSMILE 2.5.0 (25) for voice feature extraction, using eGeMAPSv02 and ComParE 2016 feature sets. eGeMAPSv02 (26), an 88-dimensional minimal set, is designed to capture physiological changes. ComParE 2016 (27), a larger feature set, comprises 6,373 features. To investigate differences in voice features between the two groups, independent t-tests were conducted. Values of P<0.05 were considered statistically significant. Feature selection was performed on the training dataset.

Model development and validation

A fully connected neural network (FNN) was constructed for binary classification (HF vs. healthy control) (Figure 1). Seventy percent of the data was used for training and the remaining 30% for testing. This procedure was repeated for each task and for both feature sets. To prevent data leakage and ensure robust performance estimation, model training was conducted using five-fold cross-validation. In each cross-validation fold during training, the data were partitioned into a training set (80%) and a test set (20%). Feature selection was embedded within the training process to avoid information leakage from the test set. Specifically, within each training fold, a pipeline was set up to select 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, and 70-dimensional features. Features were ranked by their discriminative power using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) comparing HF patients and healthy controls. Importantly, this feature selection procedure was performed independently within each training fold, ensuring that the model’s input features were determined solely based on training data without exposure to the test set. The selected features were then used to train the FNN, with the remaining 20% of data (the test fold) reserved for evaluation. This process was repeated five times, with performance metrics averaged across folds.

While the FNN operates as a “black-box” model, we employed ANOVA-based feature ranking (F-value) as an interpretable approach to quantify the discriminative power of individual features. This method provides a transparent, model-agnostic assessment of feature-level differences between groups, complementing the FNN classification results. Features consistently ranked highly across cross-validation folds were considered robust discriminators of HF status.

To validate whether the classification model captured HF-specific voice features rather than demographic differences (e.g., age, sex, education level), a sensitivity analysis was conducted by incorporating these variables as additional covariates into the FNN model. If the model’s performance varied significantly, it indicated that factors such as age, sex, and education may introduce collinearity or noise into the selected features, affecting the model’s ability to identify HF-specific features, and the model could not fully eliminate the influence of covariate factors. Conversely, stable performance would support the interpretation that the model captured HF-specific features.

To compare the classification performance of the ComParE 2016 and eGeMAPSv02 feature sets and the performance differences before and after controlling for demographic variables, paired t-tests were conducted using the F1 scores obtained from each speech task. For each task, the F1 scores were averaged across the five cross-validation folds to obtain a single paired observation per feature set. The difference in performance between the two feature sets was calculated for each task. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Performance metrics

Model performance was evaluated using multiple metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1 score. We defined the basic classification statistics as follows: true positive (TP), false positive (FP), true negative (TN), and false negative (FN). In this study, a “positive” event indicates HF, while a “negative” event indicates a control. The classification results were presented by a confusion matrix. These metrics and overall accuracy were calculated as follows:

❖ Precision = TP/(TP + FP);

❖ Precision = TP/(TP + FP);

❖ Sensitivity = TP/(TP + FN);

❖ Sensitivity = TP/(TP + FN);

❖ Specificity = TN/(TN + FP);

❖ Specificity = TN/(TN + FP);

❖ Accuracy = (TN + TP)/(TP + TN + FP + FN);

❖ Accuracy = (TN + TP)/(TP + TN + FP + FN);

❖ F1 score = 2 × Precision × Sensitivity/(Precision + Sensitivity).

❖ F1 score = 2 × Precision × Sensitivity/(Precision + Sensitivity).

Results

Participant characteristics

In this study, 244 participants were enrolled non-randomly and consecutively, of whom 27 withdrew immediately, and 14 were excluded because of contraindications to voice recording. Ultimately, 203 participants were included in the study, with 101 in the HF group and 102 in the healthy control group.

Baseline characteristics showed significant differences between the groups in age, sex, and education (P<0.001). In the HF group, 82 (81.2%) were elderly, 66 (65.3%) were male, and 55 (54.5%) had an educational level of primary school or lower. Among the 101 HF patients, 35 (34.65%) were classified as HF with reduced ejection fraction, 23 (22.77%) as HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction, and 43 (42.57%) as HF with preserved ejection fraction. Regarding symptom severity, 28 (27.72%) were in NYHA class II, 48 (47.52%) in class III, and 25 (24.75%) in class IV. In the control group, 31 (30.4%) were elderly, 63 (61.8%) were female, and 43 (42.2%) had a college degree or higher. Most HF patients were on regular medication, and hypertension was the most common comorbidity (Table 1).

Speech task

In total, 105 (51.7%) completed all tasks. Specifically, 66 participants (65.3%) did not complete the speech tasks in the HF group, compared with 32 participants (31.4%) in the control group. Among these 98 participants (48.3%), 74 (75.5%) failed due to reading problems such as illiteracy, visual, and hearing impairments; 66 (67.3%) failed due to execution difficulties, including excessive task load or physical weakness, and 13 (13.1%) failed due to comprehension problems. In detail, 29 (29.6%) failed to complete task 12, 25 (25.5%) task 10, 18 (18.4%) task 8, 12 (12.2%) task 11, 10 (10.2%) task 6, 8 (8.2%) task 3, 7 (7.1%) task 2, 5 (5.1%) task 9, 4 (4.1%) task 7, 3 (3.1%) task 5, 2 (2.0%) task 4, and task 1 was completed by all participants. All participants completed the questionnaire, and 88 (43%) rated the tasks as easy, 79 (39%) as moderate, and 36 (18%) as difficult.

Voice feature analysis

The speech files were cleaned by excluding data with background noise that overwhelmed the human voice and those with excessive missing speech tasks, resulting in 597 speech data entries from 101 HF patients and 547 speech data entries from 102 healthy subjects. Voice feature differences between groups across the six tasks were analyzed using the ComParE 2016 and eGeMAPSv02 feature sets for extraction, and independent t-tests identified features with statistically significant differences (Table 4). For the ComParE 2016 feature set, the number of extracted features ranged from 1,617 (task “i”) to 3,039 (task “mm”), while the eGeMAPSv02 feature set yielded 19 (task “i”) to 53 (task “mm”) features. Among the speech tasks, task “mm” yielded the highest number of features (3,092), while task “i” extracted the fewest (1,636). Due to the higher dimension of ComParE 2016, compared with eGeMAPSv02, ComParE 2016 extracted more voice features in each task. However, the number of selected features extracted by eGeMAPSv02 accounted for a higher proportion of its total feature set in most tasks.

Notably, for eGeMAPSv02, F0, MFCCs, and loudness features in each task showed significant divergence between groups, suggesting their potential relevance to HF-related vocal impairment, while the distribution of differential features across speech tasks in ComParE 2016 is relatively dispersed. The FNN model was employed to assess classification performance, with results shown in Tables 5,6. The FNN model based on the ComParE 2016 feature set outperformed that based on eGeMAPSv02 without demographic adjustment (P<0.05). For ComParE 2016, the highest performance was achieved for task “mm”, with an F1 score of 0.813 and a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.747–0.878, task “pg” (F1: 0.777, 95% CI: 0.648–0.905), and task “1” (F1: 0.774, 95% CI: 0.700–0.847). In contrast, eGeMAPSv02 had moderate performance, with the highest performance observed for task “1” (F1: 0.733, 95% CI: 0.620–0.845), task “mm” (F1: 0.716, 95% CI: 0.608–0.824), and task “pg” (F1: 0.708, 95% CI: 0.536–0.880). The inclusion of age, sex, and education as covariates had task-specific effects. For ComParE 2016, performance improved slightly for “pg” and “a” but decreased for other tasks. For eGeMAPSv02, however, controlling for demographic variables improved performance for most tasks, suggesting that the eGeMAPSv02 feature set benefits more from demographic adjustment due to its lower inherent acoustic information. There was no significant difference between the FNN models based on two datasets after demographic adjustment (P=0.16).

Discussion

In this study, we developed a set of speech tasks suitable for Chinese HF patients and identified three factors independently affecting task completion, including age, education, and task difficulty. For the Group B speech tasks, differences in voice features between the HF and control groups were analyzed. Voice features (e.g., F0, MFCCs, and loudness) demonstrated the ability to discriminate HF patients from healthy subjects. Tasks “pg”, “mm”, and “1” showed greater discriminative capacity compared to other tasks. The performance of two feature sets in distinguishing HF patients from healthy controls was compared using an FNN model. The ComParE 2016 feature set demonstrated superior classification performance compared with eGeMAPSv02 (P<0.05), while the difference was not significant after controlling for demographic variables (P=0.16). Tasks “mm” and “pg” consistently performed better than other tasks.

This study designed speech tasks incorporating vowels, sentences, and numbers, tailored to the prosodic and rhythmic characteristics of Chinese. The task structure is similar to that used in Murton’s study (11,12), which includes sustained vowels, standard voice assessment sentences, and spontaneous speech. However, speech materials such as the Rainbow Passage and CAPE-V are not suitable for the Chinese linguistic context. The tasks in this study are better aligned with Chinese pronunciation habits and comprehensively capture voice features like MFCCs associated with HF. The study by Amir et al. (16) designed speech tasks in different languages based on the patients’ native languages, including Hebrew, Arabic, and Russian, collecting a total of 1,484 recordings from 40 patients. This suggests that using the native language of participants facilitates speech data collection. Voice features analysis reveals significant variation in the number of features extracted across tasks. Vowel tasks, including tasks “a”, “i”, and “u”, yielded fewer features, likely due to their relatively simple phonatory structure with limited pitch variation and minimal respiratory–phonatory coupling. In contrast, task “mm” yielded the highest number of features, which may be related to its voiced articulation and increased involvement of continuous phonation and airflow modulation.

Task completion rates were significantly lower among participants with low education levels (primary school or lower) and older adults (>60 years). However, after optimization of the speech tasks, completion rates increased from 13.3% to 38.5% for the low-education group and from 29.5% to 55.1% for the elderly. In the studies by Amir et al. (16,18), patients (mean age was 75±12 years) achieved high speech task completion, with an average of about 40 recordings per patient. Schöbi et al. (17) enabled 104 older HF patients to perform five speech tasks, including numbers and words. This suggests that speech task design should account for population heterogeneity, particularly for older adults and patients with low education levels, by prioritizing simple and intuitive tasks.

In this study, the non-completion rate for speech tasks was significantly lower in the healthy control group compared to the HF group (31.4% vs. 65.3%). We performed binary logistic regression analysis to analyze the independent effects of age, HF, difficulty, and education level. The results demonstrated that after controlling for these factors, HF status was no longer a significant predictor of task completion (P=0.848), whereas age, education, and difficulty level remained significant independent predictors. Therefore, the observed difference in completion rates between the two groups may be largely attributed to demographic disparities rather than HF status. Given the high proportion of older participants and individuals with low education levels in the HF group, future studies should consider a prospective cohort design with frequency matching by age and education level to further minimize selection bias.

The ComParE 2016 feature set extracted a higher number of voice features [1,617–3,039] compared to eGeMAPSv02 [19–53], which may be due to its high-dimensional design and broad acoustic coverage, like spectral, prosodic, and articulatory. Notably, MFCC features within the ComParE 2016 have been shown to outperform glottal features in distinguishing HF-related speech characteristics, as reported by Kiran Reddy et al. (13), further validating that dynamic speech features can reflect pathophysiological changes in HF and serve as biomarkers for HF screening. Although the absolute number of discriminative features extracted by ComParE 2016 was higher than that of eGeMAPSv02, the latter had a higher proportion of its total features. eGeMAPSv02 is a compact, physiology-oriented feature set with features more directly aligned with clinically relevant acoustic changes, whereas ComParE 2016 is a high-dimensional, comprehensive feature set that provides broader coverage but may include redundant or less disease-specific information, thereby diluting the relative density of discriminative features.

The heatmap further underscores the importance of feature selection: energy-related features (e.g., loudness), F0, and MFCCs were among the most discriminative, aligning with prior studies linking HF to reduced vocal stability and breath support (9,11,12). Studies by Murton et al. (12) and Amir et al. (16) involved multiple speech collections from hospitalized HF patients, showing changes in speech measures, CPP, and F0 features before and after treatment. Tasks “pg”, “mm”, and “1” exhibit greater discriminative capacity compared to other tasks. This may be due to these sentences containing more variations in intonation and rhythm, involving respiratory-phonatory synergistic control. Furthermore, pulmonary congestion and vocal cord mucosal edema in HF patients substantially diminish glottal closure efficiency. These pathophysiological changes are more likely to be captured by speech tasks with richer phonatory and prosodic demands, resulting in enhanced sensitivity to HF-related alterations.

The FNN model was used for binary classification. Without controlling for demographic variables, the F1 score of the ComParE 2016 feature set ranged from 0.607 to 0.885, which was significantly higher than 0.596 to 0.831 for eGeMAPSv02 (P<0.05). In tasks “mm”, “pg”, and “1”, the F1 scores of ComParE 2016 reached 0.813, 0.777, and 0.774, respectively. After controlling for age, sex, and education level, the classification performance of the FNN model based on the ComParE 2016 feature set declined in most tasks, probably due to the information redundancy between the high-dimensional feature set and demographic variables. As a high-dimensional feature set, the acoustic features (such as F0, MFCC, etc.) extracted from ComParE 2016 may implicitly encode demographic characteristics such as age and sex related vocal features. Controlling for these variables may lead to multicollinearity issues in the model, especially in cases with a limited sample size, exacerbating the risk of overfitting and weakening the model’s generalization ability. In contrast, the eGeMAPSv02 feature set has a lower dimension and captures fewer demographic-related voice features. Therefore, the model performance slightly improved after introducing covariates, but its absolute classification performance in most tasks is still inferior to that of ComParE 2016. There was no significant difference in adjusted performance between the two (P=0.16), indicating a trade-off between the dimension of the feature set and the explanatory power of the model. Demographic covariates should be handled carefully when constructing an HF speech classification model to avoid overadjustment that may obscure disease-specific acoustic signatures.

Several limitations of the study should be noted. First, the sample was from a single center in Taizhou and showed a relatively homogeneous geographical and cultural background. Participants were predominantly from a single dialectal region, which may limit the generalizability of the speech task design and feature findings to broader Chinese populations with diverse dialects. Future multicenter studies incorporating dialectal diversity are needed to validate the robustness of these tasks. Second, the voice was recorded at an ambient noise level below 50 dBA, however, real-world environments are inherently more variable, and it is difficult to fully eliminate the influence of individual speech habits and emotional states. Therefore, the performance of these speech tasks in real-world telemedicine settings remains to be validated. Future studies should evaluate the performance of these speech tasks in home-based settings using consumer-grade devices and investigate the robustness of acoustic features under varying noise conditions. Finally, this study conducted a single speech collection, without recording changes in speech during hospitalization. Dynamic speech biomarkers associated with HF progression or treatment response remain to be elucidated.

In the future, we plan to conduct longitudinal data collection (28), including speech recordings from hospitalized HF patients before and after treatment, as well as post-discharge follow-up. In terms of voice feature applications, future studies may explore integrating both ComParE 2016 and eGeMAPSv02 feature sets to leverage complementary information, expand to multicenter and multidialect populations, and dynamic speech data could be collected in home settings using wearable devices. The diagnostic value of voice features combined with clinical indicators such as NT-proBNP and LVEF should be evaluated.

Conclusions

This study developed and optimized a standardized set of six speech tasks tailored to Chinese HF patients and revealed factors affecting the completion of these tasks. The feasibility of the feature sets of ComParE 2016 and eGeMAPSv02 in distinguishing HF patients from healthy controls in China was verified by the FNN model. The findings lay the groundwork for the establishment of large-scale speech databases and the development of artificial intelligence models for the early monitoring and management of Chinese HF patients.
