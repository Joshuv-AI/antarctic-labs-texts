Protocol for high-sensitivity detection of microRNAs in adult Drosophila tissues using hybridization chain reaction
Authors: Sneh Harsh, Amelie A Raz, Erika A Bach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMicroRNAs (miRNAs) are post-transcriptional regulators of gene expression and cell-fate determination. Here, we present a protocol for detecting miRNAs in Drosophila gonadal tissues using an optimized hybridization chain reaction (HCR)-based approach. We describe steps for using modular probes and antibody-free signal amplification to visualize miRNAs while preserving tissue morphology and spatial resolution. This approach could be adapted for simultaneous detection of multiple miRNAs.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Harsh et al.1Subject areas: Cell Biology, Developmental biology, Microscopy, Model Organisms, Stem Cells

Before you begin

This protocol describes a high-sensitivity HCR-based fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) method for detecting miRNAs in adult Drosophila tissues.1 Because miRNAs are short (∼20 nucleotides) and often expressed at low levels, careful probe design and tissue handling are essential for achieving specific and robust signals.

For any HCR detection, probe design is a critical parameter. HCR detection relies on DNA probes carrying initiator sequences that trigger binding of fluorescently-labeled hairpin amplifiers at the target RNA. Standard methods to detect longer mRNAs by HCR typically use long initiator sequences; however, the short length of miRNAs limits probe size and increase the risk of nonspecific binding. To address this, this protocol uses miRNA probes containing a single HCR initiator sequence, which improves specificity while maintaining efficient signal amplification.

Each probe consists of a 20-nucleotide sequence complementary to the target miRNA, downstream to a single initiator sequence connected by short linker bases. This design is based on previously established multiplex HCR systems and is compatible with standard orthogonal hairpin amplifiers.2

This protocol has been optimized for the adult Drosophila testis and ovary but can be adapted to other tissues and developmental stages. While the current workflow focuses on miRNA detection alone, it should be possible to incorporate co-detection of additional RNA targets and/or protein markers with appropriate optimization.

The protocol below outlines the experimental workflow (Figure 1). Briefly, following dissection, gonadal tissues are fixed, permeabilized, and hybridized with miRNA-specific probes. This is followed by signal detection through the addition of metastable, fluorescent HCR hairpins, which amplify the signal at probe-bound miRNA targets. After post-amplification washes, samples are mounted on glass slides and imaged using confocal microscopy. The acquired images are subsequently analyzed to quantify the spatial and temporal distribution of miRNAs across samples.

Experimental workflow for HCR-FISH detection of miRNAs in adult Drosophila gonadal tissues

Adult gonads are first dissected under RNase-free conditions and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 20 min at 20–25°C with gentle rotation. Fixed tissues are then incubated with miRNA-specific HCR probes for approximately 16–24 h at 37°C, allowing hybridization of the probe to the target miRNA. In the example shown, probes were designed against miR-263a. Following probe hybridization, fluorescent HCR hairpins (H1 and H2) are prepared by heating at 95°C for 90 sec, followed by cooling to 20–25°C for 30 min in the dark to allow proper hairpin folding. Hairpins are subsequently added to the samples to initiate HCR amplification, resulting in tethered fluorescent amplification polymers at probe-bound miRNA targets. Amplification is performed for approximately 16 h at 20–25°C in the dark. After amplification, samples are subjected to sequential washes in 5X SSCT to remove unbound hairpins and reduce background fluorescence. Tissues are then mounted in Vectashield containing DAPI and imaged using confocal microscopy with identical imaging settings across samples. Acquired images are subsequently used for quantitative analysis of HCR-FISH signal intensity across genotypes or experimental conditions.

Data generated using this protocol reveal expression of miR-263a (FlyBase: bereft (bft)) in Drosophila adult testes as compared to ovaries. These results demonstrate that miR-263a exhibits distinct expression patterns in the gonads: in wild-type (WT) testes, the signal is robustly enriched across multiple cell types, particularly somatic cells, whereas in WT ovaries, the signal is specifically enriched in polar cells, a specialized somatic cell population within the follicle cell epithelium. In addition to qualitative visualization, HCR-based detection allows quantitative comparison of miRNA signal intensity across samples. Because miRNA regulation plays a central role in molecular, cellular, and developmental biology,3 this protocol can be applied to investigate diverse biological tissues. The method can be readily adapted to detect other miRNAs by modifying the probe sequence used for HCR and have the potential to be extended to analyze multiple miRNA expression patterns in a single tissue in vivo.

Innovation

Previous studies investigating miRNA expression in Drosophila primarily relied on Northern blotting, qRT-PCR, small RNA sequencing, and Locked Nucleic Acid (LNA)-based in situ hybridization.4,5,6,7,8 While these approaches established developmental and tissue-specific miRNA expression patterns, the former three lack spatial and cellular resolution, while the latter (conventional LNA-based FISH) lacks the enzyme-free signal amplification provided by HCR. HCR generates localized fluorescent polymers directly at the target site thus improving signal intensity and signal-to-background ratio, making it well suited for sensitive fluorescence imaging and quantitative analysis of miRNAs. Here, we present an optimized HCR-FISH workflow for sensitive, spatially resolved detection of miRNAs in intact adult Drosophila gonads. Adapting from the single molecule FISH protocol of Raz et al. (2023),9 which introduced extended detergent-mediated permeabilization and pre-hybridization blocking for mRNA detection, we further optimized the method for miRNA detection by (1) incorporating gentle probe hybridization using a nutator within a 37°C incubator, (2) increasing HCR hairpin concentration, and (3) extending post-amplification washing to improve probe distribution, fluorescence signal generation, and signal-to-background ratio. To address the small size of miRNAs (∼20–22 nt), we employ a customizable single-probe design in which a miRNA-complementary sequence is linked via a short spacer to a single HCR initiator of comparable length, minimizing nonspecific interactions while enabling efficient signal amplification from a single probe-binding event. Together, these innovations provide a robust, sensitive, and reproducible workflow for high-resolution visualization and quantitative spatial analysis of endogenous miRNAs at cellular resolution in intact adult Drosophila tissues.

Design and order HCR probes for the miRNAs of interest

Timing: About 1 month before protocol

For each miRNA of interest, a miRNA-specific probe carrying an HCR initiator sequence must be designed. The sequence of the target miRNA can be obtained from databases such as FlyBase10 or miRBase.11 The probe is designed as an antisense sequence complementary to the miRNA, allowing specific hybridization to the target molecule. In HCR-based detection, miRNA-specific probes hybridize to the target miRNA(s), and each bound probe initiates a chain reaction of fluorescently labeled hairpin amplifiers, enabling visualization of their spatial expression patterns.

Previous methods for miRNA detection in Drosophila tissues primarily relied on Digoxigenin-labeled, LNA-based in situ hybridization. Although effective for tissue-specific visualization, these methods require slide-mounted tissue preparations, antibody- and enzyme-mediated signal detection, and are less suitable for whole-mount imaging and quantitative fluorescence analysis of intact tissues. In contrast, HCR-FISH provides enzyme- and antibody-free signal amplification through the self-assembly of fluorescent hairpins directly at the hybridized probe.

This protocol builds upon the HCR workflow developed by Raz et al.9 for whole-mount mRNA detection in adult Drosophila testes. Their protocol introduced extended detergent-mediated permeabilization and pre-hybridization blocking with salmon sperm DNA to improve probe penetration and reduce nonspecific probe binding in thick adult tissues. We further optimized this workflow for robust and reproducible detection of miRNAs in intact adult Drosophila gonads by performing probe hybridization with gentle continuous agitation on a nutator within a 37°C incubator, increasing HCR hairpin concentration, and extending post-amplification washes to improve fluorescence signal intensity, signal-to-background ratio, and reproducibility. Because miRNAs are only ∼20–22 nt long, the protocol employs a customizable single-probe design in which a miRNA-complementary sequence is linked via a short spacer to a single HCR initiator. Together, these optimizations provide a robust, sensitive, and reproducible workflow for high-resolution visualization and quantitative spatial analysis of endogenous miRNAs in intact adult Drosophila tissues.

Design of HCR probes for miRNA detection

Schematic representation of the probe architecture used for HCR-based detection of miRNAs. Each probe consists of an HCR initiator sequence (orange) positioned at the 5′ end, followed by a short spacer region (dark blue) and a target-specific antisense sequence complementary to miRNA (green). The antisense region hybridizes to the target miRNA (red), enabling specific recognition of the small RNA molecule. The initiator sequence serves as the trigger for HCR amplification by binding fluorescent hairpin amplifiers, which polymerize at the probe-bound target to generate a detectable signal. An example probe designed for miR-263a detection is shown, where the antisense region complements the miR-263a sequence. This modular probe design enables efficient signal amplification while maintaining specificity for short miRNA targets.

Drosophila adult gonad dissection and fixation

Timing: 30–40 min depending on the number of genotypes, probes and the number of flies dissected.

1.Before dissection, clean all surfaces, including the glass plate and dissection tools, with 75% ethanol and let them dry.

Before dissection, clean all surfaces, including the glass plate and dissection tools, with 75% ethanol and let them dry.

2.Spray the tools and glass plate with RNase AWAY to remove any possible RNases.

Spray the tools and glass plate with RNase AWAY to remove any possible RNases.

Note: Before dissecting a different genotype or experimental condition (e.g., control and knockdown samples), clean the dissection tools and glass plate with 75% ethanol, allow them to dry, and treat them with RNase AWAY before continuing. This minimizes RNase contamination and reduces the risk of cross-contamination between samples.

3.Anesthetize male flies using CO2 and place them on a fly pad.

Anesthetize male flies using CO2 and place them on a fly pad.

4.Using a fine paintbrush, select and transfer 15–20 males of the appropriate genotype per probe into a drop of ice-cold 1X PBS on the glass plate.

Using a fine paintbrush, select and transfer 15–20 males of the appropriate genotype per probe into a drop of ice-cold 1X PBS on the glass plate.

Note: Prepare about 15–20 males per probe (i.e., 30–40 adult testes); if many probes are to be used, dissect the testes together until the transfer into fixative. Ideally, one Drosophila culture bottle (6 oz polypropylene) or one vial (28.5 × 95 mm polypropylene) containing a high number of mature (dark) pupae should be cleared of adult flies one day prior to dissection. By the following day, the newly eclosed males will be of the appropriate age for the experiment.

Position the fly so that the dorsal side is up and the ventral side rests against the surface of the glass plate.

Following the SSCT wash (step 21), pre-hybridize the samples in pre-warmed probe hybridization buffer supplemented with salmon sperm DNA for 30 min at 37°C.

(A) The adult Drosophila testis contains two stem cell populations: germline stem cells (GSCs), which ultimately give rise to spermatids, and somatic cyst stem cells (CySCs), which give rise to somatic support cells required for germline differentiation. Male GSCs and CySCs are supported by the testis niche, a rosette-like cluster of terminally differentiated somatic cells, located at the apical tip of the testis. CySCs divide to produce squamous, quiescent daughter cells called cyst cells, two of which encapsulate each differentiating germ cells. GSCs divide to produce spermatogonial cells that undergo meiosis to give rise to spermatids. The fusome, a germline-specific organelle, serves as a useful marker of germ cell differentiation, appearing spherical in GSCs and becoming elongated or branched in differentiating germ cells.

(B) Schematic representation of the Drosophila ovary showing an ovariole and its developing egg chambers. The anterior region of the ovariole is the germarium, which serves as the stem cell compartment. At the anterior tip of the germarium, female GSCs reside in close association with niche cells and are characterized by the presence of spherical fusomes. As germ cells divide but undergo incomplete cytokinesis, the fusome elongates and spans the interconnected cells. The germarium also contains somatic follicle stem cells (FSCs) and supporting somatic cells (escort cells). FSCs divide to produce follicle cells (FCs), which differentiate into epithelial follicle cells that surround the germline cyst, stalk cells that separate adjacent egg chambers, and polar cells located at each pole of the egg chamber, which regulate the orientation of the oocyte and follicle cell behavior.

Expected outcomes

To assess the sensitivity, robustness, and practical utility of this protocol, we performed HCR-FISH on adult Drosophila gonads. Adult Drosophila gonads provide an ideal system to assess cell type-specific and spatially resolved gene expression due to their highly organized and well-characterized architecture. In the testis, stem cells are positioned at the apical tip surrounding a defined niche, with differentiating progeny arranged in a predictable apical-to-basal progression, enabling precise mapping of expression patterns across developmental stages (Figure 6A).17 Similarly, the ovary contains a well-defined niche that supports the stem cells. Differentiating cells have distinct and morphologically physical and molecular features that allow for unequivocal identification of specific cell types, such as germline cysts surrounded by epithelial somatic follicle cells (Figure 6B).18 This spatial organization makes Drosophila gonads particularly well-suited for evaluating in situ detection methods.

To validate the sensitivity and specificity of this protocol, we examined the expression of miR-263a (also known as bereft). miR-263a is part of a miRNA complex implicated in regulating cell fate programs and preventing activation of the somatic female differentiation program in the adult testis.1 Adult gonadal tissues were processed for miRNA fluorescent in situ hybridization as described in the protocol above, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI to visualize tissue architecture.

In a control testis that express tj-Gal4 but no other transgene (labeled tj>+), the miR-263a probe was observed throughout the apical tip (Figure 6C). miR-263a null mutants are lethal prior to adult stages, precluding our ability to use them to determine which cells in the adult testis express miR-263a. Instead, we somatically inactivated miR-263a using a specific miR-Sponge (SP).16 miRNA sponges are widely used to selectively inhibit mature, functional miRNAs by acting as competitive decoys that sequester miRNA-containing silencing complexes.19,20 Because the HCR probe sequence is complementary to the miRNA sequence, it has the potential to hybridize to both the primary miRNA transcript and the mature miRNA. Thus, the sponge provides additional control to assess the contribution of the mature miRNA to the observed HCR signal. We proved that miR-263a expression was predominantly somatic because when we somatically inhibited this miR using a miR-263aSP driven by tj-Gal4 (genotype tj>miR-263aSP), the probe was observed at much lower levels than the controls (Figure 6D). The marked reduction in fluorescence upon miR-263aSP expression suggests that a sizeable proportion of miR-263a detected in the testis corresponds to the mature miRNA, consistent with the sponge selectively sequestering this miRNA population. Quantification revealed a 0.6-fold reduction in signal intensity in somatic cells upon miR-263a depletion (Figure 6F). Together, these findings further support the specificity of the HCR probe and indicate that the mature miRNA contributes substantially to the observed HCR signal.

In contrast, the control ovary (tj>+) displayed overall lower miR-263a expression in follicle and germ cells compared to testes (0.4-fold reduction; Figures 6E and 6F). However, a distinct enrichment of miR-263a was observed in polar cells (Figure 6E), with approximately 5.4-fold higher signal in polar cells relative to surrounding ovarian cell types (Figure 6G). Polar cells are specialized somatic follicle cells located at the anterior and posterior poles of egg chambers, where they function as key signaling centers that pattern surrounding follicle cells and establish the anteroposterior axis of the oocyte.21 This selective enrichment suggests a potential role for miR-263a in polar cell function.

Because reliable tools for visualizing miRNAs in vivo remain limited, independent validation of spatial miRNA expression patterns can be challenging. Consistent with our observations of miR-263a expression in the testis, FlyBase annotations report robust expression of miR-263a in adult testes and adult ovaries. However, sequencing results do not provide cell-type specific resolution. Using HCR-FISH, we were able to demonstrate that the ovarian signal is specifically enriched in follicle polar cells, revealing a level of spatial specificity not captured by bulk sequencing.

Together, these results demonstrate that the HCR-FISH protocol provides a sensitive and reliable approach for detecting miRNAs in intact tissues, enabling the visualization of cell-type-specific and sexually dimorphic expression patterns in adult Drosophila gonads.

Limitations

This HCR-FISH protocol provides a sensitive and robust approach for detecting miRNAs in adult Drosophila tissues. However, the current version has not been validated in other tissues or organisms, and adaptation to different tissue types may require additional optimization, particularly for those with reduced permeability. Furthermore, this protocol is currently optimized for miRNA detection alone; integration with protein or other RNA co-detection strategies would require further modification of experimental conditions.

Troubleshooting

We recommend consulting the comprehensive Molecular Instruments HCR™ Gold RNA-FISH/IF User Guide available at the Molecular Instruments website for general HCR optimization. Because miRNAs are only ∼20–22 nt in length, probe design is constrained by the target sequence, and different miRNA targets may require empirical optimization of probe concentration, hybridization conditions, amplification time, and imaging parameters to maximize the signal-to-background ratio. We optimized this protocol for intact adult Drosophila gonads by incorporating an extended 2-hour Triton X-100 permeabilization step and a salmon sperm DNA pre-hybridization blocking step to improve probe penetration and reduce nonspecific background. If high background persists, increase the duration or number of post-hybridization and post-amplification washes while preserving tissue integrity.

Problem 1

Weak or no miRNA signal.

Potential solution

If the signal is weak or no signal is detected, the probe concentration should be increased two-to three-fold. Increasing the probe concentration may be useful, specifically when dealing with shorter probe sequences. Weak signal may also result from modifications to the fixation or hybridization conditions. This protocol has been optimized using 4% PFA fixation, without proteinase K treatment, and probe hybridization at 37°C. Previous studies have shown that inclusion of proteinase K treatment and/or hybridization at temperatures above 40°C can partially reverse formaldehyde crosslinks, allowing small miRNAs to diffuse out of the tissue during hybridization and wash steps, thereby reducing detection sensitivity.22 Therefore, users who modify the protocol by incorporating proteinase K digestion or increasing the hybridization temperature above 40°C should be aware of this potential loss of miRNAs and may consider using 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC) fixation to improve miRNA retention.22

Problem 2

Inconsistent signal between experimental samples.

Potential solution

Batch-to-batch variation in reagents or differences in sample processing and imaging conditions might result in inconsistent signal between experimental samples. All samples should be processed in parallel using the same master mix to ensure uniform reagent exposure. Additionally, imaging should be performed on the same day using identical confocal settings (e.g., laser power, gain, and exposure). During analysis, signal intensity should be normalized across samples to enable reliable comparison.

Problem 3

Difficulty in distinguishing true signal from background.

Potential solution

To address this, it is essential to include a negative control, such as a miRNA null mutant or specific inhibition of the miR using a sponge and specific Gal4 driver in each experiment. This allows accurate assessment of probe specificity and establishes background signal levels. Signal intensity should then be interpreted and normalized relative to this control to ensure reliable quantification of miRNA expression.

Problem 4

High background fluorescence in negative controls.

Potential solution

If high background is observed, increase the duration or number of post-hybridization washes in probe wash buffer and/or post-amplification washes in 5X SSCT to improve removal of nonspecifically bound probes and hairpins. In addition, ensure adequate pre-hybridization blocking with salmon sperm DNA, use freshly prepared wash buffers, and, if necessary, empirically optimize the probe concentration to improve the signal-to-background ratio.
