Application of three-dimensional visualization reconstruction and digital virtual diagnosis and treatment technology in robot-assisted laparoscopic partial nephrectomy for complex renal tumors: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Cunyao Li, Rongkang Li, Junfeng Jing, Wei Qi, Xiaoliang Yang, Yanbin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the value of three-dimensional visual reconstruction technology and virtual diagnosis and treatment technology in robot-assisted laparoscopic partial nephrectomy (RAPN) for complex renal tumors (R.E.N.A.L. score ≥ 7).MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of 60 patients scheduled to undergo RAPN for complex renal tumors at Hefei Second People’s Hospital between January 2021 and January 2025 and confirmed by postoperative pathological examination. The patients were divided into two groups: the 3D group (n = 30), which underwent three-dimensional visual reconstruction and virtual diagnosis and treatment technology for preoperative planning, and the 2D imaging group (n = 30), which underwent preoperative planning using enhanced CT and enhanced MRI. The following parameters were compared between the two groups: preoperative data (gender, age, body mass index, tumor diameter, R.E.N.A.L. score, laterality, and tumor location); intraoperative and postoperative indicators (total operation time, warm ischemia time, intraoperative blood loss, hemoglobin change, postoperative hospital stay, duration of drain retention, total cost, and blood transfusion rate); renal function change indicators (changes in serum creatinine and glomerular filtration rate preoperatively and at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively); and surgical complications (bleeding, subcutaneous emphysema, urinary leakage, intestinal injury).ResultsThere were no significant differences in the baseline characteristics (including age, gender, BMI, tumor laterality, tumor diameter, and R.E.N.A.L. score) between the two groups(P>0.05). Regarding surgical parameters, the 3D reconstruction group showed superior outcomes compared to the 2D imaging group, with statistically significant differences observed in the following metrics༈P<0.05༉: operative time (135.70 ± 18.41 min vs. 142.37 ± 14.25 min, P = 0.025), warm ischemia time (24.67 ± 5.48 min vs. 28.07 ± 5.92 min, P = 0.013), intraoperative blood loss (85.03 ± 25.23 mL vs. 99.67 ± 30.03 mL, P = 0.025), and hemoglobin change (6.97 ± 2.48 g/L vs. 8.67 ± 3.26 g/L, P = 0.022). The 3D reconstruction group showed favorable, though not statistically significant༈P>0.05༉, trends in the following parameters compared to the 2D imaging group: tumor resection time (11.27 ± 2.77 min vs. 12.80 ± 3.73 min, P = 0.059), postoperative hospital stay (7.70 ± 1.91 days vs. 8.57 ± 2.03 days, P = 0.063), time to drain removal (5.83 ± 1.15 days vs. 6.23 ± 1.17 days, P = 0.159), change in serum creatinine levels (11.20 ± 4.28 µmol/L vs. 12.53 ± 4.22 µmol/L), and change in glomerular filtration rate (14.33 ± 3.13 mL/min vs. 16.07 ± 3.77 mL/min). Regarding postoperative complications, no statistically significant difference was found between the two groups༈P>0.05༉. In the 3D group, complications included 2 cases of bleeding and 1 case of subcutaneous emphysema, with no instances of urinary leakage or intestinal injury. The 2D group had 1 case of urinary leakage, 2 cases of bleeding, 1 case of subcutaneous emphysema, and 2 cases of intestinal injury. In the 3D group, one patient with postoperative urinary fistula was classified as Clavien-Dindo grade IIIa, managed by ureteral stent placement under local anesthesia. Two patients with postoperative hemorrhage in the 3D group and two in the 2D group were classified as Clavien-Dindo grade IIIa, treated with arterial embolization in the interventional department. One patient in the 3D group and one in the 2D group were classified as Clavien-Dindo grade I, managed by clinical observation. One patient with intestinal injury in the 2D group was classified as Clavien-Dindo grade II, treated with antibiotic therapy for infection control. In the long-term renal function change indicators of the two groups of patients, data collection was limited to the 1st, 3rd, and 6th months post-surgery due to the follow-up period constraints. The changes in serum creatinine levels at 1 month postoperatively in the 3D reconstruction group (12.67 ± 4.40 µmol/L vs. 13.07 ± 3.82 µmol/L, P = 0.543), the changes in glomerular filtration rate at 1 month postoperatively (13.20 ± 3.07 ml/min vs. 14.70 ± 3.48 ml/min, P = 0.079), the changes in serum creatinine levels at 3 months postoperatively (7.60 ± 3.58 µmol/L vs. 8.50 ± 4.81 µmol/L, P = 0.467), the changes in glomerular filtration rate at 3 months postoperatively (7.00 ± 2.32 ml/min vs. 8.60 ± 3.28 ml/min, P = 0.053), the changes in serum creatinine levels at 6 months postoperatively (8.13 ± 1.70 µmol/L vs. 9.13 ± 2.71 µmol/L, P = 0.155), and the changes in glomerular filtration rate at 6 months postoperatively (7.17 ± 2.68 ml/min vs. 7.83 ± 2.57 ml/min, P = 0.482) showed no statistically significant differences༈P>0.05༉.ConclusionThe application of three-dimensional visual reconstruction and digital virtual diagnosis and treatment technology in RAPN for complex renal tumors can reduce operative time and warm ischemia time, decrease intraoperative blood loss, and shows certain value in enhancing surgical safety. Furthermore, the use of 3D visualization technology improves surgical efficiency without increasing the rate of postoperative complications, representing a safe and effective methodology.Keywords: Three-Dimensional Visualization Reconstruction, RAPN, Complex Renal Tumors, Digital Virtual Diagnosis, R.E.N.A.L score

Introduction

Renal tumors are common neoplasms of the urinary system, most of which are malignant and occur more frequently in males. Renal cell carcinoma accounts for approximately 2%–3% of all adult malignant tumors [1]. The incidence of renal tumors is second only to prostate and bladder tumors, and has shown an increasing trend in recent years [2]. It is the most lethal malignancy in the urinary system. Currently, Partial Nephrectomy (PN) is the primary treatment for localized renal tumors, with efficacy comparable to Radical Nephrectomy (RN) [3]. It avoids the significant surgical trauma and the postoperative state of a “solitary kidney” associated with the latter, establishing it as the gold standard for treating localized renal cancer [4]. With the iteration of minimally invasive techniques, robot-assisted partial nephrectomy (RAPN) has gradually surpassed traditional laparoscopic partial nephrectomy to become the core choice in domestic clinical practice, owing to its distinct advantages such as precise operation with three-dimensional visualization, optimized intraoperative blood loss, and faster postoperative recovery. Numerous studies have demonstrated that robot-assisted partial nephrectomy is a safe, minimally invasive, and rapid-recovery surgical approach [5, 6]. Complex renal tumors refer to localized tumors with clinical and biological characteristics such as a Renal score of ≥ 7, anatomical or functional solitary kidney, and absence of local/distant metastasis [7]. For complex tumors with a renal score ≥ 7, completely endophytic tumors in special locations, and large hilar tumors, the surgical difficulty of RAPN increases due to challenging localization and complex surrounding anatomical structures. How to achieve precise tumor excision, ensure tumor control, and simultaneously maximize renal function preservation while minimizing long-term complications has been a persistent consideration for urologists. In recent years, with the rapid development of digital imaging technology, the three-dimensional reconstruction technology of computed tomography (CT) images has become relatively mature in its application in orthopedics and neurosurgery [8–11]. Compared to the traditional two-dimensional imaging used for preoperative mental mapping, three-dimensional visualization technology, as an emerging technique, has been gradually applied in the diagnosis and treatment of various urological diseases, such as radical prostatectomy and the planning of puncture paths for complex renal calculi, enhancing the accuracy and safety of surgeries [12, 13]. However, in the field of renal tumor surgery, existing research is limited and mostly based on single-source CT imaging [14, 15]. This study retrospectively analyzes the role of three-dimensional visualization models constructed from CT and MRI imaging data in preoperative planning and intraoperative navigation for patients with complex renal tumors scheduled for RAPN surgery, aiming to explore the clinical application value of three-dimensional visualization and digital virtual diagnosis and treatment technologies in RAPN for complex renal tumors.

Materials and methods

Patient data

A retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of 69 patients with complex renal tumors who underwent RAPN treatment at Hefei Second People’s Hospital from January 2021 to January 2025, with postoperative pathological confirmation. After inclusion and exclusion screening, 60 cases were selected for analysis, with the inclusion and exclusion criteria as shown in Fig. 1. The patients were divided into a three-dimensional reconstruction group (undergoing three-dimensional visualization reconstruction and virtual diagnosis and treatment technology) and a two-dimensional imaging group (preoperative planning with enhanced CT and enhanced MRI), with the specific diagnosis and treatment process as shown in Fig. 2. The 3D reconstruction group consisted of 30 individuals, including 17 males and 13 females, with an average age of (57.60 ± 7.43) years and an average body mass index (BMI) of (24.13 ± 1.57) kg/m². The 2D imaging group also consisted of 30 individuals, including 17 males and 13 females, with an average BMI of (23.90 ± 1.63) kg/m². Preoperative data compared between the two groups included age, BMI, tumor diameter, Renal score, and tumor side. Perioperative indicators included total operative time, warm ischemia time, intraoperative blood loss, changes in hemoglobin levels before and after surgery, tumor resection time, postoperative hospital stay, drainage tube removal time, and changes in serum creatinine and glomerular filtration rate before and after surgery. The calculation method for intraoperative blood loss is: weight of blood-soaked gauze minus weight of dry gauze plus volume of blood in the suction bottle. The comparison of postoperative complications includes: hemorrhage, subcutaneous emphysema, urinary fistula, and intestinal injury. Long-term renal function indicators include: changes in serum creatinine and glomerular filtration rate at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively. All surgeries were performed by the same senior qualified surgeon using RAPN. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hefei Second People’s Hospital (No. 2024-research-085).

The inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

① Diagnosis of renal tumor confirmed, Renal score ≥ 7 points, clear surgical indications, and consent to undergo RAPN after doctor-patient communication; ② Tumor confined to one kidney only, with no lesions in the contralateral kidney; ③ Imaging studies confirm T1 stage renal tumor; ④ Patients informed about the surgical treatment plan and signed the consent form. Exclusion criteria: ① Presence of severe cardiovascular disease, poor pulmonary function, or other conditions significantly increasing surgical risk; ② Severe bleeding tendency or coagulation dysfunction; ③ Contraindications to MRI or CT examination; ④ Allergy to contrast agents; ⑤ Inability to cooperate and complete the study; ⑥ Multiple renal tumors; ⑦ Previous surgical history on the affected kidney. The follow-up period was one year post-surgery, including telephone follow-ups and outpatient follow-ups.

Reconstruction method

All 60 patients underwent imaging examinations using a Philips multislice spiral CT scanner with intravenous contrast administration via the antecubital vein, acquiring 1-mm thin-slice images through four-phase scanning (non-contrast, arterial, venous, and excretory phases). Additionally, bilateral renal and adrenal non-contrast MRI scans were performed using a Siemens next-generation whole-body MRI system with 2-mm slice thickness (in Fig. 3). The acquired T1-weighted and T2-weighted axial, coronal, and sagittal images were archived in DICOM format for subsequent analysis.

The original imaging data comprised contrast-enhanced CT scans (Pictures A1-A3) and contrast-enhanced MRI sequences (Pictures B1-B3)

The imaging data from all 30 patients in the 3D navigation group were imported into the MR medical imaging system for comprehensive quantitative holographic reconstruction. Image modeling was performed using both computer-automated threshold segmentation and manual segmentation techniques. Following data import, the reconstructed datasets were transferred to a 3D reconstruction workstation (Extended Brilliance Workspace V4.5, Philips Medical Systems, Cleveland, Ohio), where two experienced radiologists performed three-dimensional adrenal reconstruction employing volume rendering, maximum intensity projection (MIP), and surface shaded display (SSD) techniques. The arbitrary cutting function was utilized to obtain stereoscopic images of the adrenal glands and surrounding vasculature. Through fusion processing of CT and MRI datasets, a complete digital three-dimensional visualization model of the kidney and adrenal gland was constructed. The model permitted multi-angle adjustment to display all relevant anatomical structures, including the adrenal glands with tumor lesions, kidneys, collecting system, and renal arteries and veins(in Fig. 4).

Preoperative planning and intraoperative navigation

Within the software platform, surgeons could stereoscopically examine the target surgical area and utilize measurement tools to quantify adrenal dimensional parameters, tumor volume, infiltration range, and spatial relationships with renal vasculature. During preoperative planning, surgeons integrated the reconstructed imaging with patient medical records in the advanced laparoscopic system to formulate surgical strategies and identify critical anatomical structures requiring special attention. For intraoperative navigation, the surgical team used the latest US version of Microsoft HoloLens 2 augmented reality glasses, performing real-time rendering and adaptive model loading adjustments within the system. The setup enabled dynamic visualization with submillimeter accuracy and instantaneous holographic overlay updates during surgical maneuvers. (in Fig. 5)

Surgical technique

The 3D reconstruction group performed preoperative 3D digital reconstruction and holographic imaging construction, which was imported into Hololens2 to form a “picture-in-picture” real-time guidance for RAPN. During the procedure, the 3D visualization model workstation is connected to the da Vinci Surgical Robot System via the video input and output port connecting cables. The holographic images are projected and fused in real-time into the surgical field images. The fused images are synchronously displayed on the surgeon’s eyepiece of the da Vinci Robotic Surgical System and the 3D visualization model workstation, thereby enabling intraoperative navigation. The 2D imaging group collected preoperative 2D enhanced imaging data to understand the adjacency relationships between the kidney, tumor lesions, and their surrounding structures. The surgical procedure is as follows: Select a general anesthesia plan and approach through the abdominal cavity. After satisfactory anesthesia, place the patient in a healthy oblique lateral position, perform routine disinfection and draping. Take the lateral edge of the rectus abdominis on the affected side, two finger-widths above the umbilicus as the 2nd camera port, two finger-widths below the costal margin as the 1 st arm Maryland grasper, and two finger-widths lateral to the anterior superior iliac spine as the 3rd arm electrocautery scissors. The intersection perpendicular to the 2nd and 3rd arms is the 12 mm auxiliary port. Connect the Da Vinci robotic equipment, attach each port to the robotic arms, adjust the machine, insert the instruments, and debug the operation console. The machinery operates smoothly. Open the lateral peritoneum along the paracolic gutter, free the colon and liver, turn over the peritoneum, explore the affected kidney, and observe the tumor occupying the space. Dissect downward along the lower pole of the kidney to locate the ureter and gonadal vein, then dissect upward to open the renal sinus fat and locate the renal vein. Identify one renal artery each at the superior and inferior borders of the renal vein. Fully mobilize the kidney along the renal capsule, carefully separate the adherent fat, and adequately expose the tumor. Clamp the renal artery with a bulldog clamp, and enucleate the tumor bluntly and sharply along a 0.5 cm margin. Achieve complete hemostasis and close the defect with two layers of 3 − 0 barbed sutures. Remove the bulldog clamp to restore renal blood supply, ensure no bleeding from the wound, place one abdominal drainage tube, remove the renal mass, and close the incision. The 3D reconstruction team preoperatively constructs a three-dimensional visual model to guide puncture positioning. During the surgery, the anatomical structures of the actual organs are dynamically superimposed with the virtual 3D model built using holographic imaging technology. The lead surgeon accurately locates and clearly reveals the anatomical boundaries of the renal parenchyma and the tumor based on this integrated navigation system. Through the assistance of the digital model, the course of the renal vessels and the distribution of the tumor’s nourishing vessels are identified, minimizing the risk of vascular injury during the separation process. The tumor is meticulously dissected along its capsule plane to ensure the complete exposure of the lesion surface. After the tumor resection is completed, the holographic imaging field assists in renal suturing, while the 2D imaging team uses the preoperatively collected 2D imaging data for planning and intraoperative operations.

Observation metrics

Compare the perioperative indicators, complication rates, and long-term renal function indicators between the two groups of patients. The perioperative-related indicators include total operative time, warm ischemia time, intraoperative blood loss, the decrease in hemoglobin (Hb) from pre-operation to post-operation day 1, the change in serum creatinine (Scr) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (GFR) from pre-operation to post-operation day 1, postoperative hospital stay, and drainage tube removal time. The long-term renal function indicators include the changes in serum creatinine (Scr) and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) from pre-operation to 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months post-operation. Complications include intestinal injury, subcutaneous emphysema, urinary fistula, and hemorrhage.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R Studio (Version 1.4.1106), and the normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Measurement data conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as (x̄ ± s), and comparisons between groups were conducted using independent sample t-tests. For measurement data not conforming to a normal distribution, the comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Comparisons of count data were conducted using the X2 test, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Comparison of Surgical Parameters

Discusion

Compared to laparoscopic radical nephrectomy (LRN), laparoscopic partial nephrectomy (LPN) shows no significant difference in postoperative oncological survival outcomes [16, 17], with equivalent surgical efficacy. Additionally, LPN allows for the preservation of nephrons, reducing the incidence of postoperative chronic renal insufficiency, and has become the preferred treatment for localized renal cell carcinoma. However, for more complex renal tumors, surgeons need to maximize the preservation of the diseased kidney tissue while ensuring no residual tumor tissue remains during the operation. Additionally, they must complete complex procedures such as tumor resection, repair of the collecting system, hemostasis of the wound, and suturing within a limited warm ischemia time. These requirements place higher demands on the surgeon’s operative skills, and the use of LPN increases the surgical difficulty [18]. With the increasing complexity of renal tumors, prolonged warm ischemia time, increased blood loss, and collecting system injury leading to postoperative urinary fistula complications will also correspondingly increase [19].This undoubtedly poses a significant challenge to partial nephrectomy for complex renal tumors. Qiu Min et al. [20] analyzed the case data of 186 patients with medium to highly complex renal tumors who underwent LPN surgery. The results showed that as the RENAL score increased, the operative time and warm ischemia time also increased correspondingly, while the proportion of “trifecta” outcomes gradually decreased. Therefore, how to reduce the operative time, warm ischemia time, and surgical complications of partial nephrectomy for complex renal tumors, and improve surgical safety, has become one of the clinical issues urgently needing resolution in urology. The robot-assisted laparoscopic system offers superior flexibility, precision, three-dimensional high-definition visualization, and surgical instruments with over 360°of free rotation compared to LPN, significantly overcoming the technical limitations of LPN. In recent years, it has been widely applied in urological surgeries, not only reducing the complexity of operations but also enhancing surgical safety [21–23]. TachibanaH et al. conducted a study on 253 patients with renal tumors who underwent either RAPN or LPN. After adjusting the clinical data using propensity score matching (PSM), they found that RAPN was superior to LPN in terms of operative time, warm ischemia time, postoperative renal volume preservation, and residual renal function [24].

In recent years, three-dimensional visualization reconstruction has gradually gained widespread application in surgical procedures. This technique reconstructs traditional MRI or CT two-dimensional imaging data into three-dimensional visualization models. By differentially marking different tissues with colors and displaying them with varying transparency, it allows for the observation of tissue and tumor anatomical structures. Additionally, it enables rotation at different angles along various axes, accurately identifying the tumor’s location, surrounding vascular structures, tumor penetration depth, and its relationship with the collecting system [25], demonstrating significant advantages compared to traditional methods.Therefore, three-dimensional visualization technology plays a significant guiding role in surgical procedures.For minimally invasive PN surgery for complex renal tumors, preoperative planning is essential for the selection of surgical approach, identification and management of arteries, localization and excision of the tumor, and suturing of the wound. The branches of the renal segmental arteries are complex, and any inadvertent failure to block the arteries may lead to massive intraoperative bleeding, increase the difficulty of the surgery, and exacerbate the damage to renal function caused by warm ischemia. Traditional CT or MRI two-dimensional imaging is processed and analyzed based on single-phase data, which cannot perform image cutting and fusion, resulting in poor performance in coloration and perspective observation. It fails to accurately display the relative spatial position of tumor lesions and cannot intuitively show the state of blood vessels surrounding the lesions. The surgeon can only rely on personal experience to mentally reconstruct the two-dimensional images into a three-dimensional state, which is prone to discrepancies with the intraoperative field of view and may even lead to the omission of lesions. For complex renal tumors in special locations, such as completely endophytic tumors, hilar tumors, and tumors on the medial margin of the kidney, a more detailed understanding of the tumor’s protrusion direction, the distance between the tumor and the cortex, and the anatomical characteristics around the tumor is required. Relying solely on black-and-white two-dimensional imaging, the recognition of tumor features is not high, making it difficult to avoid potential risks. Three-dimensional visualization technology, by importing two-dimensional CT or MRI image data into a reconstruction system and combining it with the underlying algorithms of artificial intelligence, generates a 3D model. This model achieves a perfect alignment between the virtual image and the real surgical field through a virtual reality headset and is displayed in the visualization device. Bertolo R et al. [26]evaluated the use of three-dimensional reconstruction technology in assisting the preoperative planning of highly complex renal tumors, finding that both experienced experts, urologists, and residents altered their perspectives on surgical approaches after utilizing three-dimensional reconstruction technology. In this study, there was no significant difference in the general data between the two groups of patients. The operation time in the three-dimensional reconstruction group was shorter than that in the two-dimensional imaging group (135.70 ± 18.41 min VS 142.37 ± 14.25 min, P = 0.025), indicating that the use of three-dimensional visualization technology for preoperative planning was more detailed, allowing for rehearsal of potential issues during surgery, making the procedure smoother. However, the operation time was not significantly reduced, which may be related to the surgeon’s proficiency in using three-dimensional visualization technology, or to errors in the matching of intraoperative navigation with the real-time surgical field, requiring continuous adjustments. It may also be related to the current limited sample size, which has led to relatively limited results. Improvements will be made in subsequent studies. The warm ischemia time (24.67 ± 5.48 min vs. 28.07 ± 5.92 min, P = 0.013), intraoperative blood loss (85.03 ± 25.23 min vs. 99.67 ± 30.03 min, P = 0.025), and hemoglobin change (6.97 ± 2.48 g/L vs. 8.67 ± 3.26 g/L, P = 0.022) were all superior in the three-dimensional reconstruction group compared to the two-dimensional imaging group. This demonstrates the potential value of three-dimensional visualization technology in improving surgical safety, reducing blood loss, and minimizing renal function damage in the treatment of complex renal tumors. Our team believes that compared to traditional CT and MRI, the advantages of three-dimensional visualization technology are mainly reflected in the following aspects: ① Tumor anatomical location positioning: For tumors located purely on the ventral or dorsal side, traditional imaging examinations are relatively easy to judge. However, for tumors on the medial or lateral edge of the kidney, or tumors entirely at the upper or lower pole, it is necessary to decide whether to choose a retroperitoneal or transperitoneal surgical approach based on the direction of tumor protrusion. This can better expose the tumor, shorten the time required for intraoperative tumor exploration, and simplify the surgical procedure. For completely endophytic tumors, the surgical approach can be selected based on the direction of the nearest position to the renal cortex as determined by the three-dimensional image. ②Localization of renal vascular structures: Variations in the renal pedicle vessels are common, with accessory renal arteries and renal arteries being the most frequent types of variation [27]. During transabdominal robotic partial nephrectomy, the renal artery is typically identified from the inferior aspect of the kidney. If there is positional variation in the artery, it can significantly impact the surgical procedure, including the number of arteries and veins and abnormal courses, which can affect the success of the surgery. Three-dimensional reconstructed images can clearly display the course of the renal artery and the presence of branch vessels, aiding in the determination of whether branch vessel occlusion is feasible. More importantly, these images can simultaneously display the relative positions of arteries and veins, facilitating the identification of the artery during transabdominal surgery. Three-dimensional reconstruction can display tertiary blood vessels. For tumors around the renal hilum, even if the tumor is not large but closely adheres to the blood vessels and the renal pelvis collecting system, the three-dimensional image can clearly show the tumor and the surrounding blood vessels. Preoperative planning and avoiding potential bleeding risks can ensure surgical safety. ③ Assist in determining the shape of the tumor: Many kidney tumors have irregular shapes, with local protrusions presenting a lobulated appearance. There is a risk of tumor incision and potential spread during tumor resection/enucleation. Three-dimensional reconstruction provides a stereoscopic display of the tumor’s shape, allowing rotation viewing on the computer terminal along different axes, which facilitates understanding of the tumor’s external form and reduces the risk of cutting into the tumor. ④Indication of multiple tumors: Clinically, most preoperative imaging reviews are based on developed films, which can only display certain layers, potentially leading to incomplete visualization of some small tumors. Three-dimensional reconstruction, synthesized from CT/MRI electronic information, determines the reconstruction clarity based on the scanning layer thickness, thereby minimizing the likelihood of missing tumors. For junior surgeons, preoperative 3D reconstruction visualization can help establish an intuitive anatomical image, facilitate preoperative planning, and shorten the learning curve [28]. 3D visualization technology may also assist in preoperative doctor-patient communication, and facilitate remote surgical guidance.

The prolonged postoperative hospital stay in this study was attributed to our institution’s specific clinical pathway and more conservative discharge criteria for patients with complex renal tumors. Ensuring stable renal function and the complete absence of complications such as delayed bleeding or urinary leakage was our top priority before discharge. Some patients also opted to remain hospitalized until suture removal, further extending their stay. Additionally, within China’s healthcare context, patients themselves tend to prefer longer postoperative hospitalization for observation when conditions permit.

Qualitative feedback from the surgeons. They reported that the 3D models provided superior anatomical understanding, increased confidence during vascular dissection and tumor resection, and proved particularly valuable for preoperative planning and intraoperative navigation in these complex cases. By integrating the holographic imaging field of view with the real-time surgical field, surgeons achieved greater operational proficiency, making this a technology worthy of widespread application.

Limitation

Although this study provides some insights into the efficacy of three-dimensional visualization and digital virtual diagnosis and treatment technologies applied in RAPN, several limitations should also be acknowledged. Firstly, the retrospective design of the study introduces inherent biases and limitations, such as selection bias and incomplete data collection, which may affect the validity and generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the sample size of the study cohort, particularly the relatively small number of cases included, may limit the statistical power and precision of the results. Furthermore, the study was conducted at a single center, which may restrict the external validity of the findings in other clinical settings or populations with different characteristics. Moreover, the small sample size did not allow for the control of confounding factors through propensity score matching. These limitations underscore the need for further prospective studies with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up periods, and multicenter designs to provide more robust evidence regarding the relative efficacy and safety of three-dimensional visualization technology in treating complex renal tumors. Finally, although the findings of this study showed statistically significant reductions in operative time and warm ischemia time, the reductions were not substantial, possibly due to the operator’s unfamiliarity with intraoperative navigation using three-dimensional visualization technology and calibration errors between the model and the actual surgical field. The study did not demonstrate meaningful benefits in reducing surgical complications or preserving renal function, which is inconsistent with the conclusions of other multicenter, large-sample prospective studies. Subsequent research will continue to expand the sample size to obtain more substantial evidence and more comprehensive research outcomes.

Conclusion

In summary, our study provides some insights into the safety and efficacy of applying three-dimensional visualization technology in RAPN for the treatment of complex renal tumors, despite the inherent limitations of a retrospective study design and a small sample size. Our findings indicate that three-dimensional visualization reconstruction and digital virtual diagnosis and treatment technology in RAPN for complex renal tumors can reduce operative time and warm ischemia time, minimize intraoperative bleeding, and hold certain value in enhancing surgical safety. The use of three-dimensional visualization technology improves surgical efficiency without increasing surgical complications, representing a safe and effective approach.
