Long-Term Outcomes of Tafamidis Treatment for Transthyretin Amyloid Cardiomyopathy in Two Patients
Authors: Naoto Kuyama, Yasuhiro Izumiya, Seiji Takashio, Akihisa Tabira, Shinsuke Hanatani, Hiroki Usuku, Seitaro Oda, Yasushi Matsuzawa, Eiichiro Yamamoto, Mitsuharu Ueda, Kenichi Tsujita
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTafamidis is the only currently available disease-modifying agent for transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM); however, reports on its long-term efficacy, safety, and longitudinal cardiac parameter outcomes are lacking. Herein, we present 2 cases of wild-type ATTR-CM receiving tafamidis for 9 years, in which serial long-term follow-up data were obtained. In both cases, tafamidis treatment was continued without any adverse effects, and no hospitalization due to heart failure occurred. Notably, longitudinal observation revealed sustained inhibition of deterioration in cardiac biomarkers, left ventricular function, and hypertrophy during approximately a decade of tafamidis treatment. This case series suggests that tafamidis has the potential to sustainably delay progression of ATTR-CM.Key Words: long-term follow-up, tafamidis, transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy

Transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) is a life-threatening infiltrative cardiomyopathy characterized by progressive transthyretin (TTR) amyloid deposition in the myocardium that results in refractory heart failure, arrhythmia, and conduction disturbances.1 Patients lacking a TTR gene mutation are diagnosed with wild-type ATTR-CM. Tafamidis, a kinetic stabilizer of TTR tetramers, was approved for the treatment of ATTR-CM in Japan in March 2019, and it remains the only available disease-modifying agent for ATTR-CM. The effect of tafamidis on improving the prognosis of patients with ATTR-CM was established in the ATTR-ACT (Transthyretin Amyloidosis Cardiomyopathy Clinical Trial),2 and numerous studies have validated its efficacy.3, 4, 5

The ATTR-ACT2 and its subanalyses6,7 have reported serial parameter changes mainly focusing on the level of N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), conventional echocardiographic data, functional capacity, and quality of life during the 30 months after the initiation of tafamidis. Furthermore, an extension study of the ATTR-ACT8 reported the efficacy and safety of tafamidis for up to 58 months (median). Reports are lacking, however, on its long-term effects and associated longitudinal changes in ATTR-CM–related parameters, including cardiac biomarkers and traditional and speckle-tracking echocardiography.

Herein, we present 2 cases of wild-type ATTR-CM in patients who were assigned to the tafamidis 80 mg group in the ATTR-ACT in 2015.2 The patients continued receiving tafamidis for a period of 9 years, including after the approval of tafamidis in 2019, which enabled successful acquisition of serial long-term follow-up data.

Case Presentation

Informed consent was obtained from both patients.

Patient 1

An 82-year-old man with a history of lumbar spondylolisthesis presented at a nearby hospital with chest discomfort at rest or during exercise. Electrocardiography (Figure 1A) revealed the left anterior branch block and poor R-wave progression in the precordial leads. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) (Figure 1B) revealed diffuse left ventricular hypertrophy, with an interventricular septum thickness at diastole of 16.3 mm, and a mildly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (47.8%). Left ventricular global longitudinal strain (GLS) was impaired (−8.84%) with an apical sparing pattern (Figure 1C). Laboratory examinations revealed elevated levels of high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-cTnT) (0.034 ng/mL; normal range <0.014 ng/mL) and BNP (106 pg/mL; normal range <18.4 pg/mL). Using the conversion formula described in a previous report,9 the BNP level was converted to an NT-proBNP level of 216.7 pg/mL. According to the UK staging system,10 the patient was classified as stage I. Given the suspicion of cardiac amyloidosis, 99mtechnetium pyrophosphate scintigraphy (Figure 1D) was performed, which revealed cardiac uptake with a Perugini grade of 3 at 3 hours after radiotracer administration. Monoclonal proteins were not detected.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging could not be performed because of the patient’s history of metal bolt implantation for lumbar spondylolisthesis. Results of an endomyocardial biopsy showed amyloid deposition in the myocardium, which was confirmed to be TTR amyloid by immunohistochemical staining. No TTR mutations were found during genetic testing. The patient was therefore diagnosed with wild-type ATTR-CM.

The patient agreed to participate in the ATTR-ACT2 and was assigned to the tafamidis 80 mg group. Subsequently, 80 mg of tafamidis meglumine was administered when the patient was 83 years of age. The patient’s treatment was continued after the agent was approved in March 2019 in Japan, resulting in the successful acquisition of follow-up data for a duration of 9 years.

The patient underwent permanent pacemaker implantation due to the development of sick sinus syndrome and atrioventricular nodal ablation for refractory tachycardia attributable to atrial flutter 5 years after initiation of tafamidis. However, he did not require hospitalization due to heart failure during the 9 years following tafamidis initiation, despite the administration of no or low-volume diuretics.

The serial parameter changes after tafamidis initiation are summarized in Table 1 and Figure 2. Over time, a gradual decline in the left atrial peak longitudinal strain, right ventricular GLS, and dilatation of the left atrium were observed. Furthermore, LVEF decreased, especially after pacemaker implantation, probably due to an increase in ventricular pacing. Although left ventricular systolic function gradually declined, the pacemaker device has not been upgraded to cardiac resynchronization therapy, as there has been no worsening of heart failure symptoms or need for heart failure hospitalization. In contrast, a significant deterioration in hs-cTnT and BNP levels, left ventricular GLS, and advancement in left ventricular hypertrophy were prevented by the long-term continuation of tafamidis treatment. TTE performed 9 years after tafamidis initiation (Figures 1E and 1F) showed no worsening of left ventricular hypertrophy, systolic dysfunction, or impairment in left ventricular GLS compared with baseline before tafamidis treatment.

Patient 2

An 80-year-old man visited a nearby hospital with orthopnea. Although the patient had a history of bronchial asthma requiring inhalation treatment, an increase in the BNP level (299.2 pg/mL) and pleural effusion on chest radiography led to a diagnosis of heart failure, and the patient was referred to our hospital.

On admission, the patient’s electrocardiogram (Figure 3A) showed low voltage in the limb leads, left-axis deviation, and poor R-wave progression in the precordial leads. The hs-cTnT level increased to 0.068 ng/mL. Based on the conversion formula for the BNP level,9 the NT-proBNP level was estimated at 626.8 pg/mL. According to the UK staging system,10 the patient was classified as stage I. TTE findings (Figure 3B) revealed diffuse left ventricular hypertrophy (interventricular septum thickness at diastole 14.5 mm), a slightly reduced LVEF (53.8%), and impaired left ventricular GLS (−11.8%) assessed by speckle-tracking echocardiography (Figure 3C). Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging with cine sequence imaging revealed diffuse left ventricular hypertrophy (Video 1). T1 mapping showed an elevated native T1 value of 1,600 milliseconds (normal reference value for native T1 in the saturation recovery sequence using a 3.0-T scanner: 1,400 ± 50 milliseconds) (Figure 4A). The myocardial extracellular volume was significantly increased to 58%. Late gadolinium enhancement imaging revealed diffuse endocardial involvement and dark blood pools (Figures 4B and 4C). In addition, 99mtechnetium pyrophosphate scintigraphy (Figure 3D) revealed cardiac uptake with Perugini grade 3 at 3 hours after radiotracer administration. No monoclonal protein expression was observed. Amyloid deposition in the myocardium was confirmed by endomyocardial biopsy, and subsequent immunohistochemical staining revealed that TTR was the precursor protein of amyloid fibrils. Given the absence of TTR mutations in genetic testing, the patient was definitively diagnosed with wild-type ATTR-CM.

Similar to Patient 1, tafamidis meglumine 80 mg was administered when Patient 2 was 80 years of age, after participation in the ATTR-ACT,2 and he was clinically followed up. Although the patient required hospitalization for the treatment of bronchial asthma, he did not require hospitalization due to heart failure during the 9 years after tafamidis initiation. Longitudinal parameter changes following tafamidis administration are shown in Table 2 and Figure 5. Nine years after tafamidis administration, gradual deterioration in renal function, biventricular GLS, and left and right atrial peak longitudinal strain was observed. Notably, there was no significant worsening of BNP or hs-cTnT levels, left ventricular systolic function, or hypertrophy. TTE findings 9 years after tafamidis administration (Figure 3E) showed no significant deterioration in left ventricular systolic function. However, there was a mild increase in left ventricular wall thickness and left atrial volume, whereas left ventricular GLS (Figure 3F) declined to −7.95% compared with pre-tafamidis levels.

Discussion

Tafamidis, the first and only currently approved agent for the treatment of ATTR-CM, has been on the market since 2019. Given the effect of TTR tetramer stabilization and subsequent attenuation in progressive TTR amyloid deposition in the heart, this agent has been shown to improve the prognosis of patients with ATTR-CM, as found in the ATTR-ACT.2 Although an extension study8 reported the continuous efficacy and acceptable safety of tafamidis for up to 58 months (median), evidence is scarce on its long-term efficacy and safety. Herein, we presented 2 cases of wild-type ATTR-CM receiving tafamidis for 9 years. The continuous efficacy of long-term tafamidis use in preventing cardiac adverse events, including hospitalization due to heart failure and cardiac death, progressive left ventricular hypertrophy, and deterioration in cardiac biomarkers, suggests that tafamidis may sustainably repress the progression of ATTR-CM over the long term despite the progressive nature of the disease.

The ATTR-ACT2 and a few other studies8,11 have reported an attenuated increase in the NT-proBNP level, a widely applicable biomarker reflective of ATTR-CM, in the tafamidis-treated group compared with findings in the nontreated group. However, evidence is lacking on the longitudinal change in the hs-cTnT level, another prognostic and specific biomarker suggestive of cardiac damage due to ATTR-CM, during long-term tafamidis treatment. We previously reported a correlation between the level of hs-cTnT, but not that of BNP, and the cardiac amyloid load,12 indicating that the hs-cTnT level is more reflective of ATTR-CM disease progression than NT-proBNP or BNP levels. This case series shows the sustainable suppression of elevated hs-cTnT levels by long-term tafamidis administration, suggesting that the agent may attenuate progressive amyloid deposition in the heart, consequently alleviating amyloid-driven cardiac damage, and delaying disease progression.

The longitudinal changes in conventional and speckle-tracking echocardiographic data during long-term tafamidis treatment remain unclear. As observed in both of the current cases, gradual deterioration may occur in speckle-tracking echocardiographic parameters, although conventional echocardiographic findings, such as left ventricular systolic dysfunction and hypertrophy, may not deteriorate during long-term tafamidis treatment. As reported for cancer therapy–related cardiac dysfunction,13 deterioration in speckle-tracking echocardiographic parameters may be more suggestive of the potential disease progression of ATTR-CM before the impairment of LVEF and advancement in left ventricular hypertrophy.

Conclusions

Tafamidis treatment was continued for 9 years in both of the cases presented here of wild-type ATTR-CM. Long-term tafamidis treatment is safe and helps prevent disease progression in patients with wild-type ATTR-CM.
