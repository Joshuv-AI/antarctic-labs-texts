7 Tesla MRI with Quantitative Susceptibility Mapping: qualitative grading and quantitative focal measurements of the substantia nigra as a potential biomarker for Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Runa G Unsgård, Kjersti E Stige, Anne L Kristoffersen, Pål E Goa, Charalampos Tzoulis, Erik M Berntsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeParkinson’s disease (PD) is characterized by dopaminergic neuronal loss in the substantia nigra (SN). Imaging biomarkers sensitive to nigral pathology are limited. This study evaluated susceptibility changes in the SN using quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) and susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) at 7 Tesla MRI, and explored associations with clinical variables.MethodsIn this exploratory cross-sectional study, 76 healthy controls (HC) and 26 PD patients underwent 7 Tesla QSM; SWI was available in 53 HC and all PD patients. Grading of dorsal (SN) was performed on QSM and SWI using a four-point scale. Using Sectra IDS7, focal susceptibility values were measured in QSM images in anterior and dorsal SN ROIs. Correlation analyses with clinical variables were performed.ResultsIntra-rater reliability for repeated focal SN measurements ranged from moderate to excellent (ICC range 0.68–0.95). Focal dorsal SN susceptibility was significantly higher in PD patients compared with HC (p < 0.001). The area under the ROC curve for dorsal SN was 0.90 ( 95% CI 0.80–0.99). A two-step QSM approach combining visual grading and focal susceptibility measurement showed sensitivity of 88.5% and specificity of 89.5%. No clinical correlations remained significant after FDR correction.ConclusionIn this exploratory 7 Tesla MRI study, the focal dorsal SN susceptibility was significantly higher in PD patients. A combined QSM approach using visual grading followed by focal susceptibility measurement may provide added value in the imaging assessment of PD. These exploratory findings require validation in larger, preferably multicentre cohorts, with all readers blinded to disease status.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00234-026-04133-2.Keywords: 7 Tesla, QSM, Qualitative, Quantitative, Parkinson, Swallow-tail

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the most common neurodegenerative movement disorder and the fastest-growing cause of neurological disability, according to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2015 [1]. PD causes severe disability due to progressive motor and non-motor symptoms [2], and there are currently no disease-modifying therapies. Severe loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra (SN) pars compacta in the midbrain causes the typical motor symptoms [3], and both the extent and anatomical localization of neuronal loss play a role in the severity and evolution of motor symptoms [4, 5].

A major diagnostic challenge is the lack of clinical biomarkers with sufficient sensitivity and specificity for PD. Diagnosis remains primarily clinical, supported by functional imaging to confirm nigrostriatal dopaminergic degeneration. DaTSCAN scintigraphy and 18F-DOPA PET are the most reliable imaging modalities to demonstrate this process [6, 7] although they do not distinguish between the different neurodegenerative parkinsonian disorders. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has so far mainly served to exclude alternative diagnoses.

In 2013, Blazejewska et al. reported that the nigrosome-1 could be directly visualized on 7 Tesla T2* weighted imaging as an ovoid hyperintense area, corresponding to a neuromelanin-rich area on immunohistochemistry staining in postmortem examinations. This area was absent in PD patients on T2*-weighted images [8]. The following year, Schwarz et al. demonstrated that this ovoid area could be detected at 3 Tesla using susceptibility weighted imaging (SWI) and described it as “the swallow-tail appearance of the healthy nigrosome”, commonly referred to as “the swallow-tail sign” (STS) [9]. They found a diagnostic accuracy of 96% when using the STS to differentiate between healthy controls (HC) and PD patients. The loss of the STS has subsequently been suggested to reflect increased iron in and around nigrosome-1 [10–14]. In recent years, several studies have found non-equivalence or mismatch between the hyperintense area of the “swallow tail” on SWI and the histologically verified nigrosome-1 [15–17]. Nevertheless, there is broad agreement that the central hyperintense area of the swallow tail on SWI is absent or partially absent in patients with PD and other neurodegenerative parkinsonisms [18, 19]. However, it has been shown that the interpretation of the STS can be challenging due to normal variation in SN morphology [14, 20, 21], and that diagnostic accuracy of STS loss in neurodegenerative parkinsonisms varies considerably (47.8–98.5% according to a recent systematic review by Tseriotis et al.) [21]. Loss of the STS has also been reported in other neurological conditions [21–23].

Quantitative Susceptibility Mapping (QSM) is a promising MRI sequence capable of measuring susceptibility in different brain regions. On QSM images, the image intensities are reversed compared with SWI, meaning that the dorsal SN area described as affected in PD patients is hypointense on QSM imaging. QSM susceptibility values have been shown to be directly proportional to iron content in grey matter in histological validation studies [24, 25]. However, differences in acquisition protocols and post-processing methods have produced inconsistencies in measurements in previous studies [26]. Furthermore, iron deposition also occurs as a natural part of ageing, affecting the susceptibility values [27, 28]. Therefore, it is important to establish age- and sex-adjusted normal values before using QSM as an imaging biomarker for disease.

7 Tesla MRI provides a high signal-to-noise ratio and allows more detailed imaging of brain structures. It also provides improved susceptibility-weighted images and improved mapping of iron-containing structures and pathological iron deposits in the brain [11, 29].

The primary aim of this study was to qualitatively evaluate the dorsal SN with an adaptation of an established grading system using both QSM and SWI, and to quantitatively investigate focal susceptibility values adjusted for age and sex in the anterior and dorsal parts of the SN in PD patients and HC using QSM at 7 Tesla MRI. A secondary aim was to correlate quantitative and qualitatively findings with clinical features.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore 7 Tesla QSM dorsal SN grading combined with manual focal dorsal SN measurement. This is also the first study to use exclusively manual focal region-of-interest (ROI) measurements to measure this specific area in the dorsal SN.

Methods

Subjects and clinical variables

This was an exploratory cross-sectional study of PD patients and HC enrolled in the STRAT-PARK study arm at St. Olavs Hospital, Trondheim University Hospital, Norway [30]. The HC from STRAT-PARK were primarily spouses or close relatives without neurodegenerative disorders. To widen the age range, additional HC were recruited from another ongoing 7 Tesla MRI study, with the goal of 10 persons within each decade from 20 to 80 years. For these HC, self-reported CNS-disease or diseases affecting brain iron concentration were used as exclusion criteria, including neurodegenerative and neuroinflammatory diseases, genetic conditions and systemic disorders. No genetic testing was performed. Radiological signs of small vessel disease were defined as white matter changes of Fazekas grade ≥ 2 if < 75 years of age, and ≥ 3 if ≥ 75 years of age, the presence of central microhemorrhages or lacunar infarcts, and any findings suggestive of cerebral amyloid angiopathy according to the Boston criteria version 2.0 [31]. Patients met the following inclusion criteria at enrolment: Clinically established or probable PD according to the Movement Disorder Society (MDS) diagnostic criteria [32], positive DaTscan findings confirming nigrostriatal degeneration, Hoehn and Yahr stage ≤ 4, and age 20–100 years.

Clinical assessments of STRAT-PARK participants were recorded at baseline (May 2021–November 2024). In patients, the following variables were analyzed for potential correlations with dorsal SN QSM measurements: (1) age at baseline; (2) disease duration, defined as duration of motor symptoms; (3) age at onset of motor symptoms; (4) age at diagnosis; (5) MDS-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) Part III [33]; (6) tremor-dominant (TD) score (calculated from items in MDS-UPDRS Part II and Part III as described by Stebbins et al. [34]); (7) postural instability and gait difficulty (PIGD) score (also calculated from items in MDS-UPDRS Part II and Part III [34]); (8) levodopa-equivalent daily dose (LEDD) [35, 36]. Lateralized tremor and bradykinesia/rigidity scores were calculated from MDS-UPDRS Part III: (9) tremor score for the right side of the body (tremor score right); (10) tremor score left; 11) bradykinesia/rigidity score right; 12) bradykinesia/rigidity score left. The clinically dominant side was defined by summing tremor and bradykinesia/rigidity scores separately for each side of the body.

MRI protocol and QSM post-processing

Imaging was performed at baseline using a Siemens 7 Tesla Magnetom Terra system with a 1Tx32Rx head coil (Nova Medical, Inc.), both CE-labelled and FDA-approved for clinical use. The protocol included 3D T1 MP2RAGE T1 imaging, axial T2, QSM and SWI. For QSM, 3D multi-echo GRE images were acquired with TR = 31 ms, TE = 2.54/7.22/14.44/23.23 ms, flip angle = 12°, field of view (FoV) 171 × 228 × 168 mm, 0.75 × 0.75 × 0.75 mm voxel size, and acquisition time 8 min 44 s. Coil combination of phased-array channels was performed using the ASPIRE algorithm [37]. QSM processing was performed using the Python implementation [38] of the total generalized variation (TGV) method [39]. The TGV method was chosen because it provides improved delineation of small structures [39]. In brief, FSL BET [40] was used for brain extraction on magnitude images before TGV-QSM was applied to the phase images from the last three echoes individually, and the median of the resulting three single-echo QSM-images was used as the final QSM-image. Single-echo QSM was chosen because it is more robust to field inhomogeneities [41]. The resulting images containing QSM values in parts per billion (ppb) were then transferred to SECTRA IDS7, where region of interest (ROI) measurements were made.

Axial SWI was acquired using a 2D gradient-echo sequence TR = 21 ms, TE = 14 ms, flip angle = 10°, FoV = 179 × 220 mm, 0.122 × 0.122 mm pixel spacing (interpolated), acquisition matrix = 728 × 896, 1.5 mm slice thickness, oriented along the anterior commisure–posterior commissure line, with acquisition time 7 min 29 s.

Qualitative grading and quantitative measurements

Both visual grading and quantitative measurements were performed on QSM images of HC and PD patients. Quantitative measurements were made in both the anterior and dorsal SN. Figure 1 shows the workflow for grading and measuring the dorsal SN as a two-step process. The radiologist who performed the primary measurements and analyses was not blinded to disease status, reflecting the exploratory design of the study (Figs. 2 and 3).

Dorsal SN grading

The middle part of the dorsal SN appears hypointense on QSM and hyperintense on SWI. The dorsal SN was visually graded from 1 to 4 separately on each side on both QSM and SWI images by a single radiologist (RGU) using a scoring system adapted from Shams et al. (2017) [18], as follows: Grade 1: definitely normal, indicates a relatively large dorsal hypo-/hyperintensity; Grade 2: probably normal, indicates an obvious dorsal hypo-/hyperintensity, but not in the expected shape or slice; Grade 3: probably abnormal, indicates present, but clearly reduced area of hypo-/hyperintensity; Grade 4: definitely abnormal, indicates no detectable dorsal hypo-/hyperintensity. For examples of grading see Fig. 2. Additional examples of grade 1 and grade 3 and 4 can be found in Fig. 4a and b respectively. When evaluating the QSM/SWI images, emphasis was put on evaluating the presence of the hypo-/hyperintense area in the dorsal SN examining several slices, rather than identifying one slice with the typical “swallow-tail appearance”. This approach was chosen to reduce uncertainty related to morphological variation of the SN.

Susceptibility measurements

Measurements were performed in Sectra IDS7 version 26.2. On the QSM images, circular ROIs were placed bilaterally in the anterior SN (aSN; diameter ≥ 3 mm) and in the hypointense region of the dorsal SN (dSN; diameter ≥ 1.5 mm) as shown in Fig. 2.

Measurements were obtained on a slice where the dorsal hypointensity was clearly visible, typically within the three most caudal slices in which the SN is visible, at the level of the middle to caudal part of nucleus ruber. In the selected slice, the hypointensity, if present, is situated dorsally to the middle part of nucleus ruber (Fig. 3). To avoid confusing the dorsal hypointensity with vessel artifacts, only hypointensities with hyperintense tissue on both sides of the hypointensity was measured. For some patients, it was necessary to reconstruct the images to confirm the finding. The aSN ROI was placed in the same slice as dSN, where possible in a homogenous signal, and anterior to nucleus ruber.

The ROI measurements yielded the mean susceptibility of the voxels included in each ROI expressed in parts per billion (ppb), hereafter referred to as the unreferenced susceptibility value. The measurements of all PD patients and HC were made by one radiologist (RGU) at two separate time points at least three months apart. Participants with no remaining measurable dorsal hypointensity using a 1.5 mm circular ROI or grade 4 on QSM/SWI grading were not measured, because the exact location of the dorsal hypointensity could not be determined.

To correct for a potential phase offset, we added an internal reference measurement. The reference measurement was subtracted from the unreferenced measurement to obtain a referenced susceptibility value. Automatically segmented cerebral white matter was used as a reference region, in line with previous literature and ISMRM recommendations for QSM [24, 26, 42].

Statistical analysis

This was an exploratory, hypothesis-generating study based on the available eligible cohort. No formal sample size or power calculation was performed before data collection.

Analyses of susceptibility measurements were performed in STATA version 18 (StataCorp LLC), while clinical correlation analyses were performed in R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Reproducibility of repeated ROI measurements in HCs and PD patients was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC(1,1), absolute agreement) (< 0.5 poor, 0.5–0.75 moderate, 0.75–0.90 good, > 0.90 excellent) [43, 44]. Agreement between QSM and SWI grading was assessed with Cohen’s kappa (κ) (< 0.00 no agreement, 0.21–0.40 fair, 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 substantial, 0.81–1.00 almost perfect) [45]. Group comparisons of susceptibility values were performed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with age and sex as covariates. Because of the relatively large number of young HC, the ANCOVA group comparisons were repeated after restricting HC to the age range of the PD population.

Subanalyses of measurements in individuals with evidence of small-vessel disease (SVD) were performed using Welch’s t-test of healthy volunteers restricted to age over 70. A subset of the QSM images was graded and subsequently measured by a blinded second reader, a neuroradiologist with 11 years of experience (EBM). Inter-reader agreement for dorsal SN QSM grading was assessed using weighted Cohen’s kappa. Inter-reader reliability for focal QSM measurements was assessed using a two-way mixed-effects model for absolute agreement of single measurements, ICC(3,1) [43].

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate performance in distinguishing PD patients with measurable dSN hypointensity from HC [46]. Youden’s index [47] was used to identify the optimal cutoff value in this experimentaly study, Euclidean distance was also assessed due to low sample size.

Associations between susceptibility values and clinical measures were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. Permutation tests (n = 5000) were performed to obtain p-values, and bootstrap resampling (R = 2000) was used to derive confidence intervals (CIs). Permutation p-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. No formal threshold for correlation strength was applied because of considerable uncertainty of the estimates.

Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare clinical variables in PD patients with and without measurable dSN hypointensity.

Results

Participants

Seventy-six HC (36 female, 40 male, aged 21–80 years) underwent QSM. SWI was available for 53 HC; the remaining 23 did not undergo SWI due to protocol changes. Five HC had incidental findings of small-vessel disease, including white matter changes, lacunar infarcts and/or central microhemorrhages. One HC had findings suggestive of cerebral amyloid angiopathy, i.e. superficial siderosis and lobar microhemorrhages, but was subcategorised as incidental finding of SVD in this context. All volunteers with findings of small vessel disease were aged ≥ 75 years. Measurements from these volunteers were retained in the study because there were no significant susceptibility differences in the sub-analyses (t-test all referenced measurements p > 0.074, all unreferenced p ≥ 0.073).

There were 26 PD patients included in this study (12 female, 14 male, aged 51–77 years), all imaged with both QSM and SWI. Summary statistics for this group are presented in Table 1. Age distribution and sex of HC and PD patients are presented in the Supplementary information (Table S1).

Quantitative susceptibility measurements

Internal reference measurements

Mean susceptibility value of automatically segmented cerebral white matter ranged from − 4.13 to 0.70 ppb across HC. The white matter showed a slightly negative and significant association with age (β = -0.015 ppb per year, p = 0.033), after adjustment for sex and PD status (yes/no).

Susceptibility of the aSN

All HC and PD patients were included in ANCOVA analyses of the aSN susceptibility with age as a continuous covariate and sex as a categorical covariate (Table 2). Analyses were performed separately for the left and right aSN, using the mean susceptibility value from the respective aSN ROI (aSNL and aSNR), and for a combined measure using the highest mean susceptibility value from either side (aSNhighest).

In the ANCOVA of aSNhighest, we found a slightly higher adjusted mean susceptibility value in PD patients than in the HC group, but the difference did not reach statistical significance for either unreferenced (p = 0.140) or referenced (p = 0.163) adjusted mean susceptibility (Table 2). Age was a significant covariate with a mean increase of 0.5 ppb per year (unreferenced p = 0.008, referenced p = 0.007). Sex was not a significant covariate.

In the ANCOVA of aSNL and aSNR separately, a similar pattern was observed, with nonsignificantly higher mean susceptibility value of PD patients (Table 2). Age was a significant covariate in the aSNL (unreferenced p = 0.008, referenced p = 0.006). For aSNR, age did not reach statistical significance for the unreferenced measurement (p = 0.053) but was significant for the referenced measurement (p = 0.044).

Susceptibility of the dSN

All HC and PD patients with a measurable dorsal SN remnant on at least one side (n = 12) were included in ANCOVAs of dSN suscpetibility with age and sex as covariates (Table 2). Analyses were performed separately for each side, using the mean susceptibility value from the respective dSN ROI, and for the combined measure, using the highest mean susceptibility value from either side (dSNhighest).

In the ANCOVA of dSNhighest, there was a significantly higher mean susceptibility value in PD patients compared to HC for both the unreferenced and referenced mean susceptibility. Adjusted mean susceptibility in PD patients compared to HC was 58.7 versus 26.9 ppb for unreferenced measurements and 60.1 versus 29.0 ppb for referenced measurement (p < 0.001 for both) (Table 2). Age and sex were not significant covariates.

In ANCOVAs of the left dSN (dSNL) and the right dSN (dSNR) a similar pattern was seen, with both the unreferenced and referenced adjusted mean value for dSNL and dSNR in PD patients being significantly higher (p < 0.001; Table 2).

When ANCOVA was repeated with HC restricted to participants aged 51–80 years, the results were similar to those of the full analysis, except that age was no longer a significant covariate (Table S2). No significant group difference was observed for referenced anterior SN susceptibility (p = 0.252–0.388). As in the full analysis, referenced dorsal SN susceptibility remained significantly higher in PD patients than in HC across all dorsal SN measures (all p < 0.001).

In two PD patients with bilateral grade 3, the radiologist was unable to place a focal ROI because the remaining dorsal hypointensity was too small or blurring from adjacent structures. In one of these patients, it was also unclear whether the visible area represented the dorsal SN hypointensity or an artifact.

ROC analysis

ROC analysis was performed for both unreferenced and referenced measures to assess the properties of dSNhighest to differentiate PD patients with measurable dorsal hypointensity from HC. The AUC of the ROC was 0.90 for both unreferenced and referenced measurements (95% CI 0.80–0.99), Fig. 5. The optimal cutoff value to distinguish PD patients with measurable dorsal hypointensity from HC in this exploratory material was 47.1 ppb for the unreferenced measurement with a Youden’s index of 0.73 (sensitivity 83%, specificity 89%). For the referenced measurements the optimal cutoff was 49.1 ppb with a Youden’s index of 0.73 (sensitivity 83%, specificity 89%). Becuase n was < 20 in the PD group, Euclidean distance was also assessed. This yielded similar results with an optimal cutoff value of 48.3 ppb (sensitivity 92%, specificity 90%) for unreferenced and 50.3 ppb (sensitivity 92%, specificity 90%) for referenced measurements.

The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.90 for both unreferenced and referenced measurements in this exploratory study

To investigate the potential diagnostic performance of the two-step QSM assessment, we classified the PD patients as disease positive or negative by: (1) classifying all participants with visual grade 4 or no measurable dSN hypointensity as disease positive; and (2) using a cutoff value of 49.1 ppb to classify the remaining patients. This yielded a sensitivity of 75.0% (95% CI 56.6–88.5%) and a specificity 97.1% (95% CI 90.1–99.7%). The positive predictive value was 92.3% (95% CI 74.9–99.1%), and the negative predictive value was 89.5% (95% CI 80.3–95.3%).

Intra-rater reliability

In HC the reliability of repeated aSN measurements was good on the left side and excellent on the right side, with ICC values of 0.82 (95% CI 0.75–0.89) and 0.91 (95% CI 0.87–0.95), respectively. In HC the reliability of repeated dSN measurements was good on both sides, with an ICC value of 0.78 (95% CI 0.68–0.87) on the left side and 0.77 (95% CI 0.68–0.86) on the right side (Bland-Altman plots of HC repeated QSM measurements are shown in Fig. S1).

In PD patients the reliability of repeated aSN measurements was moderate to good, with ICC values of 0.68 (95% CI 0.48–0.89) for aSNR, 0.76 (95% CI 0.60–0.92) for aSNL, and 0.74 (95% CI 0.56–0.91) for aSNhighest. In PD patients the reliability of repeated dSN measurements showed moderate to good point estimates, with ICC values of 0.80 (95% CI 0.47–1.00) for dSNR, 0.87 (95% CI 0.71–1.00) for dSNL, and 0.73 (95% CI 0.46–1.00) for dSNhighest.

Inter-rater agreement and correlation

A data subset consisting of 13 PD patients and 13 age- and sex-matched HCs was graded and subsequently measured on QSM by the blinded second reader.

Inter-reader agreement for dorsal SN grading on QSM was almost perfect, with an agreement of 92.3% for the left dorsal SN (κ = 0.85, SE = 0.17, p < 0.001) and 94.9% for the right dorsal SN (κ = 0.90, SE = 0.18, p < 0.001).

Inter-rater reliability of focal anterior SN measurements was generally good with ICCs of 0.87 for aSNhighest (95% CI 0.74–0.94) and 0.89 for aSNL ( 95% CI 0.78–0.95). Agreement for aSNR was moderate (ICC = 0.72, 95% CI 0.48–0.87) (all p < 0.001, all n = 26).

Inter-rater reliability for focal dorsal SN measurements was also generally good based on point estimates, with ICCs of 0.88 for dSNhighest (95% CI 0.69–0.96; n = 14), 0.85 for dSNL (95% CI 0.58–0.95; n = 13), and 0.81 for dSNR (95% CI 0.46–0.94; n = 11).

Using the two-step method of grading and measuring the dorsal SN on QSM imaging, 25 of 26 individuals were correctly categorized as PD patient and HC by the second blinded reader. The HC incorrectly categorized as PD patient had bilaterally abnormal SN anatomy.

Clinical correlation

Dorsal SN grading on QSM and clinical variables for PD patients

Among the PD patients with dSN asymmetry based on QSM grading, i.e. grade 3 on one side and grade 4 on the other, we found that 8 of 10 (80%) PD patients had corresponding clinical asymmetry (Table S3). No significant correlations were observed between dSN grading and clinical variables.

Susceptibility measurements and clinical variables

Clinical correlation analyses were performed for dSNhighest (n = 12), dSNL (n = 8) and dSNR (n = 5). The results should be interpreted in light of the limited statistical power due to small sample sizes.

Spearman’s rank correlation between dSNhighest and clinical variables 1–8 showed the highest correlation coefficients for MDS-UPDRS Part III (variable 5) followed by the TD score (variable 6) and disease duration (variable 2) (ρ ~ 0.55–0.62 for referenced and unreferenced measurements; Table S4). However, confidence intervals were generally wide, with lower limits approaching zero, indicating uncertainty in the magnitude of the associations.

Correlation analyses between dSNL and clinical variables 1–8 and contralateral clinical variables (variables 9 and 11) showed the strongest association with tremor score on the right side of the body (variable 9, ρ = 0.79 and 95% CI 0.00–1.00 for both referenced and unreferenced measurements; Table S5).

Correlation analyses between dSNR and clinical variables 1–8 and contralateral clinical variables (variables 10 and 12) showed the strongest association with TD score (variable 6, ρ = 0.82 and 95% CI 0.00–1.00 for both referenced and unreferenced measurements; Table S6). However, since the CI included zero there is considerable uncertainty regarding the magnitude of the association. There were other variables with correlation coefficient of similar magnitude as TD score, but with broad CIs that included zero.

Some correlations showed nominally significant p-values, but none survived multiple testing correction.

The subgroup of patients with measurable dorsal hypointensity on QSM (n = 12) had numerically lower symptom scores compared to the group without measurable dorsal hypointensity (n = 14). However, the differences were not statistically significant (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) (Table S8).

Discussion

This exploratory study found significantly higher focal dorsal SN susceptibility in the PD patients compared to HC. Although there remains uncertainty regarding what the area represents biologically [15–17], our findings suggest that focal dSN QSM measurements may provide added value in the imaging assessment of PD patients.

Anatomical considerations

The anatomical interpretation of the swallow-tail sign remains debated. Brammerloh et al. [15] reported that the radiological swallow-tail sign and histologically defined nigrosome-1 only partially overlap. In the healthy population, this hypointense area in the dorsal SN is closer to white matter in QSM signal intensity. Although the biological basis of the dorsal SN hypointensity requires further research, the findings in this article supports substantial previous research showing that this hypointense area in dorsal SN on QSM is affected in PD patients. The 1.5 mm dSN ROI used in this study is near the lower limit of what can be reliably measured with a 0.75 mm isotropic voxel size, and partial volume effects may occur. The 1.5 mm ROI size was the largest ROI that could be consistently placed in the patient population, while remaining in the hypointense area.

The anatomy of the SN is also complicated by venous structures, especially in older individuals [21, 48]. QSM should in theory remove the venous blooming effect seen in SWI. The lower dSN grade on QSM than on SWI in five patients, which may indicate better visibility of the hypointense area in the dorsal SN, may reflect from reduced venous blooming. However, visual inspection of Figs. 2 and 4 did not provide evidence of reduced blooming in QSM. On the contrary, QSM values around large veins are reduced and visually the blooming effect visually appears stronger in QSM than in SWI. This could be caused by the lower spatial resolution of the QSM protocol compared with SWI. Therefore, in the present study, QSM and SWI were considered complementary, rather than QSM being a solution to the anatomical ambiguity in the dorsal SN.

Grading and measurements of the dorsal SN

The intra-rater reliability of the QSM measurements of both the aSN and dSN in HC suggested consistent focal ROI selection, whereas the intra-rater reliability of dSN in patients showed wide confidence intervals. The inter-rater agreement for both grading and measurements were high, suggesting that the approach is reproducible across observers. Further studies with larger preferably multi-centre cohorts are necessary to evaluate the consistency and reliability of the method for grading and focal ROI measurements in dSN.

The combination of no significant age-related changes in the dorsal SN and significantly higher susceptibility values in the dorsal SN in PD patients and a high AUC, suggests that focal QSM measurements may capture disease-related changes and potentially be a useful additional diagnostic tool. However, because of the limited sample size in our study, these estimates should be considered exploratory. The cutoff values in this study are therefore to be considered as suggestive values that require external validation.

The findings of the two-step QSM method showed high specificity and positive predictive value, suggesting that the method may be useful for supporting a PD diagnosis when positive, whereas the moderate sensitivity indicates that some disease-positive cases may not be detected. In future studies, for patients with residual dorsal hypointensity that is visible but too narrow to measure, a semi-automated segmentation could help determine whether these residual areas show increased susceptibility, to clarify whether the dorsal SN can be classified as grade 4 when the residual area is too narrow for manual ROI placement.

Comparison with the literature

Our findings align with histopathological evidence that dopaminergic neuronal loss and iron deposition are most pronounced in the dorsal SN, in the region previously associated with or named nigrosome-1 [15, 17, 49–51]. Increased susceptibility in this region is consistent with other QSM studies reporting increased iron accumulation in PD [52, 53].

Previous studies have shown that the swallow-tail appearance is largely age-independent [14, 54, 55]. In our study, the anterior SN showed an age-related susceptibility increase, consistent with studies showing increasing total SN iron with age [56–58].

Reference region

Selection of an internal reference remains a challenge in QSM. Automatically segmented cerebral white matter was used in our study because of its lower variability and its alignment with recent QSM consensus recommendations for patients with neurodegenerative disease [42].

We found slightly decreasing susceptibility values in the reference region with increasing age, but independent of PD status. When the ANCOVA was restricted to controls age-matched to the patient population, the association between age and white matter susceptibility was no longer significant. This suggests that automatically segmented white matter can serve as a useful reference region for QSM analysis in age-matched study populations. Age-related susceptibility changes should be considered when establishing future reference ranges. Other disease-related white matter changes could compromise accurate referencing, particularly in older individuals. There was no evidence of significant difference in measurements for individuals with evidence of SVD in our material, but the subgroup was small (n = 5). Further standardization of image acquisition and analysis will be essential for clinical translation. The results did not differ substantially between unreferenced and referenced measurements in this study, adding to the question of whether the use of reference region is always warranted in QSM.

Clinical correlations with QSM measurements and dorsal SN grading

Although relatively high correlation coefficients were observed (e.g. for MDS-UPDRS Part III), confidence intervals were generally wide, and no associations remained statistically significant after correction for multiple testing. This likely reflects limited power due to small sample size. Nevertheless, recent studies have reported associations between susceptibility in SN and motor symptom severity [58, 59]. This should be investigated further in larger cohorts.

Dorsal SN asymmetry corresponded with the clinically dominant side, similar to findings from DAT-imaging [60, 61] and in contrast to a previous report that found no association between the STS and clinical asymmetry [62].

The subgroup of patients with measurable dSN hypointensity had numerically lower symptom scores, including lower LEDD and MDS-UPDRS Part III scores. However, the differences did not reach statistical significance, likely reflecting limited statistical power.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore 7 Tesla QSM dorsal SN grading combined with manual focal dorsal SN suscptibility measurement.

The use of 7 Tesla MRI provides a higher signal-to-noise ratio and increased susceptibility contrast resulting in a more detailed mapping of iron related susceptibility changes. If validated as a biomarker, focal ROI QSM measurements could potentially save time in clinical practice compared to segmentation-based approaches.

One limitation of the study is that only the second reader, who evaluated a subset of the data, was blinded. However, the inter-rater agreement was almost perfect for QSM grading and inter-rater reliability was good for focal QSM measurements. Nevertheless, further independent external validation is needed.

Another limitation of this study is the relatively small PD cohort, and in particular the small number of patients with measurable dorsal SN hypointensity. As this was an exploratory imaging study based on the available cohort, no formal sample size or power calculation was performed. Although diagnostic performance metrics are reported, these should be interpreted strictly as exploratory estimates. The study was not designed or powered as a diagnostic validation study, and no independent validation cohort was available. Therefore, the ROC analyses and cutoff values should be regarded as preliminary estimates of discriminative potential rather than clinically validated diagnostic thresholds. The clinical correlation analyses were also limited by the small sample size, particularly for side-specific dorsal SN measurements. Although some nominal associations were observed, these did not remain significant after FDR correction. These findings should therefore be interpreted as explorative and hypothesis-generating.

Regarding clinical assessment, patients were examined in the ON state, which may underestimate motor severity and thereby mask correlations with imaging markers [58, 63]. It is possible that stronger associations would have been observed if assessments had been performed in the OFF state [58].

Future perspectives

Although 7 Tesla MRI provides improved contrast and resolution for small structures, including deep grey matter structures, it is not easily available for clinical use worldwide. Previous work indicates that optimized 3 Tesla QSM protocols can achieve image quality comparable to shorter 7 Tesla acquisitions. However, QSM values depend on acquisition parameters, and absolute susceptibility estimates may differ between 3 Tesla and 7 Tesla unless protocols are harmonized [64, 65]. Therefore, the present 7 Tesla findings should be interpreted as suggestive of susceptibility changes in the dSN that require dedicated 3 Tesla validation in a larger cohort before implementation as a clinical biomarker.

Recent data have shown good discriminatory performance when QSM is combined with other imaging metrics [59]. A limitation of the present study is the absence of direct comparison with other imaging biomarkers, including neuromelanin MRI, DaT-SPECT, diffusion imaging, and established structural markers used in atypical parkinsonism. However, these techniques are not interchangeable, as they investigate different biological or diagnostic features. DaT-SPECT reflects presynaptic dopaminergic terminal integrity and is useful for confirming a degenerative dopaminergic syndrome, mainly used for differentiating parkinsonism from essential tremor, vascular parkinsonism, pharmacological and psychogenic parkinsonism. Neuromelanin MRI is more directly related to the loss of neuromelanin-containing neurons in the substantia nigra [66, 67]. QSM is sensitive to susceptibility changes and may capture broader patterns of neurodegenerative disease and multiple pathologies [26, 68]. Therefore, neuromelanin MRI and QSM may both be useful and provide complementary information in the diagnostic assessment of patients with neurodegenerative conditions.

Future multimodal studies are needed to determine whether combining QSM with neuromelanin MRI, DaT-SPECT, and other MRI biomarkers improves diagnostic accuracy, and disease stratification in Parkinson’s disease and atypical parkinsonism.

Conclusion

In this exploratory 7 Tesla MRI study, the dorsal substantia nigra hypointensity on QSM could be consistently identified and measured in healthy controls. No significant age-related change in dorsal SN susceptibility was observed. In PD patients with a measurable dorsal SN hypointensity, focal dorsal SN susceptibility was significantly higher than in HC, independent of age and sex. A combined approach using visual grading of dSN on QSM followed by focal dSN QSM measurements may be useful, but the findings require independent validation with larger PD cohorts.

The findings in this exploratory study require validation in larger, preferably multicentre cohorts, with all readers blinded to disease status. Future studies should also investigate the approach in atypical parkinsonisms.
