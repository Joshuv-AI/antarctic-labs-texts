Ambient PM2.5 Induced Pulmonary Vascular Remodeling via MSC-Mediated Cellular Senescence in Pulmonary Arterial Endothelial Cells
Authors: Qingping Liu, Yizhe Yang, Yujia Xie, Mengqi Zou, Mengruo Wang, Jingyuan Wang, Lili Shi, Yaxian Pang, Lei Bao, Yujie Niu, Rong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ambient fine particulate matter (PM2.5) was recognized as one of the most pressing environmental challenges confronting public health. There was a strong association between PM2.5 and pulmonary vascular diseases, the primary feature of which was pulmonary vascular remodeling. However, the underlying mechanisms by which PM2.5 induces pulmonary vascular remodeling have not been clarified. To address this gap, we established an environmental PM2.5-exposed mouse model and an in vitro cell model. Bioinformatics-based approaches were employed to identify potential regulators associated with pulmonary vascular remodeling, followed by an investigation into their potential roles in regulating pulmonary arterial function. Our results showed that PM2.5 exposure induced elevated pulmonary artery pressure and increased pulmonary artery wall thickness in mice. PM2.5 exposure induced cellular senescence in mouse pulmonary arterial endothelial cells (MPAECs) in vivo and in vitro, and PM2.5 treatment promoted mitochondrial dysfunction in MPAECs. Next, we used bioinformatics methods and experimental validation to identify MSC as potential regulator for mitochondrial dysfunction. And overexpression of MSC alleviated mitochondrial dysfunction-associated senescence (MiDAS) induced by PM2.5. In the mechanism, as a transcription factor, MSC regulated its downstream target gene NDUFB3, thereby facilitating PM2.5-induced MIDAS. This study offered novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying PM2.5-induced pulmonary vascular diseases and presented strategies for the precise intervention of pulmonary vascular injury triggered by atmospheric environmental factors.

Introduction

Air pollution was considered to be one of the greatest environmental challenges facing public health. And air pollution had risen from fifth to fourth among the world’s leading causes of death in 2019. Among them, particulate matter less than 2.5 μm in diameter (PM2.5) was known as one of the most hazardous air pollutants. , As a key air pollutant, it poses significant risks to public health worldwide. In 2021, the recommended dosage for PM2.5 was tightened by the World Health Organization (WHO). 90% of the global population breathed PM2.5 that exceeded the WHO’s guideline limits, indicating that PM2.5 exposure has become an extremely prevalent public health issue worldwide, with nearly all individuals facing potential health risks posed by polluted air. And accumulating epidemiological evidence has shown that the high morbidity and mortality of respiratory diseases might be caused by PM2.5. ,

Pulmonary vascular remodeling is commonly observed in a variety of chronic lung diseases, such as pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). − It is characterized by structural and functional changes in the pulmonary vasculature, thereby affecting the normal functioning of the pulmonary circulation. Current therapeutic strategies were limited to the administration of targeted agents for alleviating clinical symptoms and minimizing further damage to the pulmonary vasculature and alveoli. , An unmet clinical need is the development of novel targeted therapies to prevent and treat associated pulmonary vascular injury. Previous evidence has shown that PM2.5 was associated with increased pulmonary vascular resistance. And our previous study has demonstrated that PM2.5 could increase pulmonary arterial pressure and lead to the PAH phenotype in mice. However, the specific molecular mechanisms underlying PM2.5-induced pulmonary vascular remodeling remained unclear.

Pulmonary arterial endothelial cells (PAECs) are the first barrier of the vasculature and play roles in accomplishing the synthesis and secretion, exchanging biomolecules as well as maintaining normal vascular activity. It has been found that the proliferation and migration of pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells could be triggered by dysfunction and injury of PAECs by activating or destroying certain intracellular factors, which in turn led to vascular stenosis and obstruction. Dysfunction of PAECs was considered to be an early event of pulmonary vascular remodeling. Multiple characteristic features are observed in vascular endothelial dysfunction, namely compromised endothelium-dependent vasodilation, increased oxidative stress, prolonged chronic inflammation, elevated vascular hyperpermeability and cellular senescence. Prior studies have shown that pulmonary diseases were associated with premature vascular aging and senescence of vascular cells. At the same time, vascular remodeling, degeneration and hardening could be promoted by senescent endothelial cells. , Several studies have shown that skin, nervous system and heart muscle cell aging could be caused by PM2.5 exposure. , However, the effects of PM2.5 exposure on the senescence of PAECs and then triggering pulmonary vascular remodeling have not been thoroughly investigated.

Cellular senescence is conceptualized as the irreversible impairment of a cell’s capacity to undergo division. There are two distinct categories of cellular senescence: physiological senescence, which occurs with the depletion of lifespan, and stress-induced premature senescence (SIPS). SIPS was triggered by external stimuli, which prematurely activated the intracellular aging cascade. The mechanisms of cellular senescence include mitochondrial dysfunction, oncogene activation, genotoxic stress and telomere shortening. Study has confirmed that mitochondrial dysfunction could cause a different type of cellular senescence and named it mitochondrial dysfunction-associated senescence (MiDAS). And it also showed that MiDAS was not caused by DNA damage or oxidative stress. The character of dysfunctional mitochondria is related to high expression of NADH. Simultaneous increase in cytoplasmic NADH and decrease in cytoplasmic NAD+/NADH ratio are prerequisites for senescence. And it had been reported that PM2.5 exposure induced a decrease in NAD+/NADH ratio and ATP production in embryonic heart tissue of zebrafish. Previous studies have found that sustaining activation of AMPK, which was an energy-sensing protein kinase and could be activated by a diverse array of metabolic stressors, inducing accelerated P53-dependent cellular senescence. Therefore, we hypothesized that PAECs dysfunction caused by PM2.5 exposure was induced by MiDAS which might be regulated by NAD+/NADH ratio decreases and AMPK activation.

Within the study, we established a PM2.5-exposed mouse model and a mouse pulmonary artery endothelial cells (MPAECs) model to investigate the effect of PM2.5 on pulmonary vascular remodeling and its mechanism. This study found that PM2.5 could lead to senescence of pulmonary artery endothelial cells, which is caused by mitochondrial dysfunction. By bioinformatics, the potential regulator of mitochondrial dysfunction had been found. This study contributes to an understanding of the ways in which PM2.5 jeopardizes vascular health in the lungs and helps to provide therapeutic targets for the early stages of pulmonary vascular remodeling.

Materials and Methods

Animals and Treatment

The procedure for establishing the animal model was elaborated in our previous literature. Briefly, six-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into three groups: filtered air (FA), unfiltered air (UA), and concentrated air (CA). Mice in the FA group were exposed to air in chambers equipped with high-efficiency particulate-air (HEPA) filters. The UA group was exposed to ambient air in chambers without HEPA filters. For the CA group, mice were exposed to PM2.5-concentrated air using a PM2.5 concentration enrichment system (Beijing Huironghe Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The exposure protocol was 6 h/day and 7 days/week for either 8 weeks or 16 weeks at the same fixed time daily. A detailed protocol is in the Supporting Information. Prior to the study, this protocol was granted approval by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Hebei Medical University (IACUC-Hebmu-2022030).

Assessment of Echocardiography

As an alternative marker for pulmonary artery pressure, detection of right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) is provided in detail in the Supporting Information. Cardiac functional parameters were measured for 3 consecutive cardiac cycles, and their average was calculated to serve as the final parameter.

Histological Analysis and Immunofluorescence Labeling

After euthanasia, the mouse lung tissues were isolated. Thickness and area of the pulmonary vessel wall were measured through ImageJ software. Five pulmonary vessels from each lung tissue slice were taken to measure their wall thickness and wall area. Six mice per group were used to analysis.

For immunofluorescence staining, lung tissues were dewaxed, rehydrated, and antigen repair. After sections were closed with bovine serum albumin, they were incubated with the following antibodies. Cell nucleus was stained with DAPI (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Images were observed by a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Instruments Co. Ltd., Japan).

Cell Culture and PM2.5 Treatment

Mouse pulmonary artery endothelial cells (MPAECs) were obtained from Qiansi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The detailed procedure for PM2.5 extraction is provided in the Supporting Information. Prior to treatment, PM2.5 was sonicated with 100 Hz ultrasound for 30 min and subsequently diluted with DMEM medium to reach the final concentration.

Cell Viability Assay

The detailed protocol is provided in the Supporting Information.

Senescence-Associated β-Galactosidase (SA-β-gal) Assay

Cell senescence of MPAECs was evaluated by using a SA-β-gal staining kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, the details of which are provided in the Supporting Information.

Western Blot and Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qPCR)

Detailed procedures are provided in the Supporting Information. And the antibodies and primers in the present study are summarized in Tables S1, S2.

JC-1 Assay

The mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was measured by a JC-1 assay (Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Arbor, Michigan, USA). MPAECs were incubated in a mixture of culture medium and JC-1 working solution in a 1:1 ratio in the dark for 30 min at 37 °C. Then, the cells were washed twice to remove free JC-1. Images were acquired by a fluorescence microscope (IX73P1F, Olympus Optical Co., Ltd., Japan) and quantitatively analyzed with ImageJ software.

Determination of ATP Content

The detailed procedure is provided in the Supporting Information.

Bioinformatics Analysis

Differential expression analyses were performed on the GSE15197 and GSE130391 data sets retrieved from the GEO database, respectively, with the cutoff criteria set at |log2FC| ≥ 1.5 and a false discovery rate (FDR) value (P value adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method) < 0.05. After intersecting two sets of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), overlapping genes were obtained. Further screening was conducted based on the abundance of genes in lung tissue from the Human Protein Atla Web site (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). The candidate factor was subjected to predict downstream target genes by using the Gene Transcription Regulation Web site (http://gtrd.biouml.org). The predicted targets were then intersected with mitochondrion-related genes retrieved from the MitoCarta database. Gene Ontology enrichment analysis of the overlapping genes was performed via the WebGestalt tool with hypergeometric tests employed for statistical validation, and pathways with a FDR <0.05 were defined as significantly enriched to characterize the candidate factor’s potential biological functions.

Cell Transfection

To verify the function of the potential regulator, MPAECs were transfected with MSC overexpression (OE-MSC) plasmids tagged with FLAG and negative control (NC) plasmids (Genepharma, Shanghai, China), respectively. Cells were transiently transfected using Advanced Transfection Reagent (Zeta Life, Atlanta, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 24 h of transfection, cells were harvested, and the overexpression efficiency of MSC was detected by Western blot and qPCR.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) Assay

The sonicated nuclear lysates and immunoprecipitates were purified by a ChIP assay kit (absin, Shanghai, China) according to standard protocols. The required antibodies were as follows: Flag-specific antibodies (Abclonal, Wuhan, China) and IgG (BersinBio, Guangzhou, China).

Statistical Analysis and Visualization

All results were expressed as mean ± SD. Independent sample t test was used between two groups, one-way ANOVA and two-way ANOVA were used between multiple groups, and the LSD test was used when appropriate. The P value <0.05 was considered statistically different. Statistical analysis, graphing and visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 and biorender Web site (EI29CWP7VG).

Results

PM2.5 Caused Pulmonary Vascular Remodeling in Mice

Over the PM2.5 exposure period, the 24-h average PM2.5 concentrations were as follows: for the FA, UA, and CA groups, they were 0, 12.42, and 118.56 μg/m3 at 8 weeks, and 0, 11.26, and 98.80 μg/m3 at 16 weeks, respectively. In parallel, the 24-h average atmospheric PM2.5 concentration was 74.81 μg/m3 over 8 weeks and 66.10 μg/m3 over 16 weeks in Shijiazhuang, China. Concentrations of SO2, NO2, CO, and O3 in every chamber were consistent with those in the outdoor air. For PM2.5 component, the chemical components in organic extract and water-soluble fractions of PM2.5 was consisted of various constituents with toxicity, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), alkyl-PAHs, polychlorinated dibenzo-p-dioxins (PCDD) and polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), as well as the trace elements and metal species (Li, Cr, Mn, Ni, Zn, As, Cd, Pb, SO42–, NO3– and so on).

Echocardiography was performed at the end of 8 and 16 weeks’ PM2.5 exposure. In Figure A and C, after 16 weeks’ exposure, RVSP in the CA group was significantly increased to 40.07 ± 2.21 mmHg from 36.92 ± 2.17 mmHg in the FA group (P < 0.05). As shown in Figure B, H&E staining showed thickening of the alveolar wall and infiltration of inflammatory cells. Further quantitative analysis about thickness of the pulmonary vascular wall, the results showed that after 8 weeks’ exposure, vessel wall thickness (WT) was significantly thicker in the CA group, increasing by 40.4% relative to the FA group (P < 0.05), and WT and the vessel wall area (WA) increased dose-dependently. After 16 weeks of exposure, WT was significantly thicker in the CA group, increasing by 62.8% and 35.0% relative to the FA and UA groups, respectively (P < 0.05), and WA was significantly larger in the CA group, increasing by 34.7% and 23.0% relative to the FA and UA groups, respectively (P < 0.05), and WT and WA increased dose-dependently, suggested remodeling of the pulmonary vasculature (Figure D).

PM2.5 Exposure Caused Senescence of Pulmonary Artery Endothelial Cells In Vivo and In Vitro

We used a double-labeling immunofluorescence assay to assess whether PM2.5 exposure induced senescence of pulmonary artery endothelial cells in mice. As shown, after 16 weeks’ PM2.5 exposure, the colocalization of P53 with Gja5 was significantly increased in the CA group relative to the FA group (P < 0.05) (Figure A, Figure S1), and the results of the animal experiment suggested that PM2.5 exposure promoted senescence of pulmonary artery endothelial cells in mice. To further verify whether PM2.5 exposure induced senescence of MPAECs in vitro, MPAECs were treated with 0, 12.5, 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL PM2.5 for 24 h, respectively. Cell survival status was significantly reduced dose-dependently, with 100%, 98.3%, 91.9%, 79.2% and 72.7%, respectively (P < 0.05) (Figure S2). Therefore, the final concentrations of PM2.5 exposure used in the following experiments were 0, 12.5, 25, and 50 μg/mL, respectively. SA-β-gal staining was used for the detection of the cell senescence phenotype after the PM2.5 treatment. Experimental results demonstrated a significant, dose-dependent increase in the number of SA-β-positive cells following PM2.5 treatment. Relative to the 0 μg/mL group, SA-β positive cells in the 12.5, 25, and 50 μg/mL groups were elevated by 2.77-fold, 4.43-fold and 5.40-fold, respectively (P < 0.05) (Figure B). To determine the expression levels of cellular senescence-related proteins after PM2.5 treatment, we used Western blot. Relative protein levels of P53 and P21 were dose-dependently increased in PM2.5-treated MPAECs. Relative to the 0 μg/mL group, the P53 protein expression levels in the 25 and 50 μg/mL groups were elevated 9.0-fold and 13.8-fold (P < 0.05). Relative to the 0 μg/mL group, the P21 protein expression levels in the 25 and 50 μg/mL groups were elevated 2.6-fold and 4.2-fold (P < 0.05) (Figure C). And statistically elevated mRNA levels of P53 and P21 were observed in MPAECs after PM2.5 treatment (P < 0.05) (Figure D). The aforementioned results implied that PM2.5 could cause senescence of the pulmonary artery endothelial cells in mice.

PM2.5 Contributed to Mitochondrial Dysfunction in MPAECs

Mitochondria are the primary site of energy production within the cell, which makes mitochondria key to maintaining cellular function and viability. To investigate whether PM2.5 caused mitochondrial dysfunction in MPAECs, mitochondrial function was assessed by measuring MMP and ATP levels after PM2.5 treatment. Relative to the control group, the MMP in the 12.5, 25, and 50 μg/mL PM2.5 groups were reduced by 20.1%, 44.6% and 56.2% (P < 0.05) (Figure A). The ATP content in the 50 μg/mL PM2.5 group was reduced by 41.6%, 37.9% and 29.0% relative to the 0, 12.5, and 25 μg/mL groups (P < 0.05) (Figure B). Meanwhile, the level of AMPK, a key protein involved in energy balance regulation, was determined. Relative to the 0 μg/mL group, the AMPK protein levels in the 25 and 50 μg/mL groups were elevated 7.2-fold and 15.2-fold (P < 0.05) (Figure C). And mRNA level of AMPK was significantly increased dose-dependently (P < 0.05) (Figure S3). The above results confirmed that PM2.5 could induce MiDAS in the MPAECs.

MSC Could Be the Potential Regulator in Pulmonary Vascular Remodeling via Difference Analysis and Correlation Analysis

To screen for potential regulators mediating pulmonary vascular remodeling, we screened potential core genes from online databases using bioinformatics analysis. The flowchart of the MSC screening process was shown in Figure D. Five overlapping genes (MSC, IFI44L, NAIP, ZNF595 and GJC1) were obtained by taking the intersection of data sets GSE15197 and GSE130391. According to the data sets of HPA Web site, MSC with high abundance in lung tissue were selected (Figure S4). And relative to the normal group, the level of MSC was significantly reduced in patients with PAH (Figure E). Human-mice homology comparisons were further performed (Figure F). In the present study, the protein and mRNA levels of MSC were significantly decreased after PM2.5 treatment of MPAECs (Figure G1, G2, and H), suggesting that MSC may play a vital role in PM2.5-induced cellular senescence in MPAECs.

OE-MSC Could Ameliorate Senescence Phenotype of MPAECs Induced by PM2.5

To explore whether MSC is a potential regulator in the senescence of MPAECs induced by PM2.5, we examined the hallmark indicators of cellular senescence after MSC overexpression. The efficiency of overexpression MSC is shown in Figure S5. And the number of positive cells for β-galactosidase staining in the NC group was significantly increased by 6.6-fold after PM2.5 treatment (P < 0.05), whereas the number of positive cells for β-galactosidase staining in the OE-MSC group was elevated by 2.1-fold after PM2.5 treatment. The increase of β-galactosidase staining positive cells in the OE-MSC group after PM2.5 treatment was alleviated relative to with the PM2.5-treated NC group (P < 0.05) (Figure A). As shown in Figure B, the contents of cellular senescence proteins P53 and P21 in the NC group were significantly increased (P < 0.05), whereas there were no significant changes in the contents of P53 and P21 in the OE-MSC group. Relative to the NC group, the elevated levels of P53 and P21 proteins in PM2.5-treated OE-MSC group were alleviated (P < 0.05). And the trend of mRNA of P53 and P21 was consistent with protein expression (P < 0.05) (Figure C). Above data expounded that MSC reversed the elevated level of cellular senescence in MPAECs induced by PM2.5.

MSC-NDUFB3 Axis Resulted in Mitochondrial Dysfunction in MPAECs after PM2.5

For further investigation of whether MSC was critical for mitochondrial dysfunction in MPAECs after PM2.5 exposure, mitochondrial function was assessed after MSC overexpression. In Figure A, MMP was significantly reduced by 71.7% after PM2.5 treatment in the NC group (P < 0.05), whereas MMP was relatively reduced by 7.1% after PM2.5 treatment in the OE-MSC group, and the reduction of MMP in OE-MSC group after PM2.5 treatment was alleviated relative to with the NC group (P < 0.05). In Figure B, the trend of ATP change was similar to the results of JC-1 (P < 0.05). Relative protein and mRNA level of AMPK in OE-MSC following by PM2.5 treatment were both significantly decreased (P < 0.05) (Figure C, D). The data illustrated that MSC reversed PM2.5-induced mitochondrial dysfunction.

To clarify the molecular mechanism by which MSC regulate mitochondrial dysfunction, we used CHIP-seq data provided by GTRD Web site to screen downstream targets of MSC. Among the 2990 downstream targets of MSC, 183 genes were categorized as mitochondria-associated genes by the MitoCarta3.0 database (Figure E). GO analysis of 183 overlapping genes showed that the mitochondrial respiratory chain complex was enrichment, including LYRM2, NDUFA6, NDUFAF6, NDUFB11, NDUFAF4, ACAD9, NDUFAB1, NDUFB3, NDUFAF3 and NDUFC1 (Figure F). Correlation analysis was performed between the MSC and downstream targets (Figure G). The mRNA level of NDUFB3 was significantly increased (P < 0.05), which was inconsistent with the trend of MSC mRNA level (Figure H). Then, the mRNA level of NDUFB3 was detected after MSC overexpression. NDUFB3 was relatively decreased in OE-MSC group after PM2.5 treatment relative to the NC group (P < 0.05) (Figure I). ChIP assay results demonstrated that MSC could directly bind to the NDUFB3 promoter (Figure J). Collectively, these data certified that NDUFB3 was a direct target of MSC, and the MSC-NDUFB3 axis further exerted a crucial role in regulating MiDAS.

Discussion

The present study focused on the observation that chronic PM2.5 exposure induced pulmonary vascular remodeling by triggering MiDAS in PAECs. Notably, we demonstrated that PM2.5 exposure downregulated MSC expression in PAECs, and MSC negatively regulated NDUFB3, which, in turn, caused mitochondrial energy imbalance. This imbalance drives cellular senescence, thereby contributing to pulmonary vascular remodeling. These novel findings provided new insights into the mechanism underlying vascular remodeling induced by MiDAS.

According to Global Air Quality Report 2023 (https://www.iqair.cn/), the air quality status around the world in 2023 was introduced. The PM2.5 air quality data of 124 countries, regions and territories still exceed the guidance value of the WHO annual average standard (5 μg/m3). During the exposure period of this study, the 24-h average atmospheric PM2.5 concentration in the Shijiazhuang outdoor environment was 74.81 μg/m3 over 8 weeks and 66.10 μg/m3 over 16 weeks, which was nearly 15 times higher than the WHO annual average standard. Building upon our prior investigations, cumulative pulmonary deposition burdens in mouse lungs were quantified via the Multiple Path Particle Dosimetry (MPPD) model, yielding values of 15.51 μg and 130.13 μg per mouse in the 11.26 μg/m3 and 98.80 μg/m3 exposure groups, respectively, following 16 weeks of continuous exposure. For the in vitro experiments, we established a dose-equivalence framework between cellular and animal exposures: assuming all particles suspended in the culture media reach the cell surface (6 cm cell culture dishes, 2 mL medium), 25 μg/mL PM2.5 solution was equivalent to 145.44 μg PM2.5 deposition in mice lung in the light of the 3.53 μg/cm2 alveolar surface area of mice based on the 82.2 cm2 of alveolar surface area in adult mice lungs, which was basically consistent with the deposition in mice. Consequently, the present study employed matched exposure doses for both in vitro cellular assays and in vivo experiments using CA mice, ensuring translational consistency between the experimental models. Previous studies have demonstrated that water-soluble extracts contained inorganic ions, metals and partial organic compounds, which covered broad spectrum of substances. , Moreover, the water-soluble extraction method was well-established and has been widely adopted for treating cells in published literature. − In view of the situation described above, water-soluble extracts of PM2.5 were employed for subsequent cell experiments. Although organic extracts were primarily derived from organic compounds adsorbed onto PM2.5, the exclusive use of water-soluble extracts may underestimate the toxic risks associated with exposure to whole PM2.5 particles that contain organic components. Based on the findings of this study, we will investigate the toxic effects of whole PM2.5 particles in subsequent work to more comprehensively reflect the complex biological effects of real-world PM2.5 exposure.

PM2.5 was a complex mixture of air pollution that poses a direct health risk, primarily through inhalation, which made the lungs the primary target organ for direct exposure. Because its particle diameter was small enough to be inhaled deep into the lungs and entered the bloodstream, leading to a range of health problems such as systemic inflammation, vasoconstriction and thrombosis, all of which could lead to the development of vascular disease. Previous study suggested that PM2.5 exposure for 3 weeks caused lung inflammation and vascular muscularization, and PM2.5 exposure for 2 and 4 months could cause thickening of the pulmonary vascular wall and smaller lumens. , And it has shown that pulmonary vascular remodeling was represented by an increase in the percentage of vessel wall thickness to outer diameter and the percentage of vessel wall area to total vessel area. , In our study, PM2.5 exposure for 8 and 16 weeks resulted in increased thickness and area of the pulmonary vessel wall in mice. Furthermore, study have showed that the degree of pulmonary vascular remodeling correlated with pulmonary artery pressure. It has been shown that RVSP measurement was the key to the detection and quantification of elevated pulmonary artery pressure. Previous study indicated that 2 months and 4 months of continuous whole-body exposure to PM2.5 in rats induced elevated RVSP. And studies have found that exposure to silica and diesel engine exhaust could cause an increase in RVSP. , Consistent with the above results, in this study, PM2.5 exposure for 8 and 16 weeks resulted in increased RVSP in mice. The above results indicated that PM2.5 exposure induced the development of pulmonary vascular remodeling in mice.

Pulmonary vascular remodeling included structural changes in the intima, midmembrane, and adventitia of the vessel wall. Because endothelial cells are the connection between blood and tissue, they were particularly susceptible to certain factors in blood flow. Therefore, exposure to specific cytokines or stimulation may lead to endothelial dysfunction and subsequent vascular disease. Study has shown that there was a significant correlation between short-term or long-term PM2.5 exposure and endothelial dysfunction. Furthermore, it has suggested that the initial cause of pulmonary vascular remodeling was the dysfunction of PAECs. And among them, endothelial cell senescence was one of the main manifestations of endothelial dysfunction. Studies have found that endothelial cell senescence could promote the inflammatory environment and enhance oxidative stress, thereby exacerbating vascular remodeling. , It has shown that pulmonary artery endothelial cell senescence regulated pulmonary vascular remodeling. ,, And previous study has shown that long-term PM2.5 exposure could accelerate human umbilical vein endothelial cells senescence. Studies have found that PM2.5 exposure could increase the levels of senescence-associated proteins P53 and P21 in human umbilical vein endothelial cells and accelerate vascular aging. In addition, it was also found that PM2.5 exposure induced lung inflammation and fibrosis by increasing the levels of P53 and P21 proteins in type II alveolar epithelial cells through SIRT3 gene depletion. , In this study, the results of animal immunofluorescence showed that the colocalization of P53 and the arterial endothelial cell marker Gja5 increased after 16 weeks of PM2.5 exposure. And it has been shown that the expression levels of the cellular senescence proteins P53 and P21 and the number of positive cells for SA-β-gal staining in MPAECs increased dose-dependently after PM2.5 exposure. The results showed that PM2.5 exposure could cause pulmonary vascular remodeling, which might be caused by the senescence of PAECs.

The senescent state of endothelial cells was caused by a number of factors, notably DNA damage, telomere wear and tear, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and epigenetic alterations. In our study, GO enrichment analysis showed that ATP-related biological processes were enriched. Since ATP production was mainly in mitochondria, we focused on cellular senescence induced by mitochondria-related pathways. There was evidence suggested that mitochondrial damage caused age-related degeneration of muscle tissue and neurons in animal model of mitochondrial protein gene knockout. It has shown that inhibition of mitochondrial function was associated with pro-proliferative activation, which enhanced the development of vascular remodeling and elevated pulmonary arterial pressure; it has also found that mitochondrial dysfunction and mtDNA damage in ECs induced increased pulmonary arterial pressure, and found that metabolic reprogramming and kinetic disturbances of the mitochondria played a role in pulmonary arterial pressure elevation and pulmonary vascular remodeling. In this study, we observed that the ATP content and mitochondrial membrane potential decreased in PM2.5-treated MPAECs. Our results suggested that PM2.5 exposure could cause mitochondrial damage in MPAECs. It is well-known that mitochondria are the core of cellular metabolism and energy production. Studies have shown that AMPK was the initial signaling step that linked energy-deficient acute metabolic reactions to increased glucose transport by sensing the energy status of the cell. , Moreover, it has shown that the decrease of ATP level and the consequent activation of AMPK could activate P53, which in turn led to cell cycle arrest. In addition, another study confirmed that MiDAS was caused by a decreased NAD+/NADH ratio, which led to AMPK and P53 activation. In this study, the results demonstrated that the expression levels of AMPK and P53 proteins were elevated, suggesting that PM2.5 exposure could induce MiDAS in MPAECs. Our results highlighted that PM2.5 exposure-induced cellular senescence might be caused by the mitochondrial-related pathway of MPAECs.

The potential regulator MSC functioned as a transcriptional repressor, belonging to the basic helix-loop family of transcription factors. It binds to specific DNA sequences to suppress transcriptional activity, thereby exerting a critical role in the negative regulation of gene transcription. It has been found that MSC–/– mice spontaneously developed lung inflammation with age. And studies have found that knockdown of the long noncoding RNA musculin antisense RNA 1 (lncRNA MSC-AS1) could inhibit cell proliferation and block the cell cycle. , In the present study, analysis of the GEO database revealed lower MSC expression in PAH patient samples (characterized by the same pathognomonic features as pulmonary vascular remodeling) compared to normal samples. Additionally, both mRNA and protein levels of MSC were found to be reduced in MPAECs following PM2.5 exposure. Therefore, we suggested that pulmonary vascular remodeling induced by PM2.5 exposure was associated with decreased MSC. In addition, we confirmed that the biological implication of MSC-mediated modulation of mitochondrial dysfunction could be expanded to PM2.5-induced pulmonary vascular remodeling. Specifically, we found that the PM2.5 exposure-induced elevation of cellular senescence and mitochondrial dysfunction in MPAECs was mitigated after MSC overexpression, suggesting that MSC overexpression might alleviate PM2.5-induced pulmonary vascular remodeling by reducing MiDAS in MPAECs. As a downstream target of MSC, NDUFB3 is NADH dehydrogenase (ubiquinone) 1 beta subcomplex 3, serving as a critical assembly factor of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I. Previous studies demonstrated that NDUFB3 were associated with ATP contents and activation of inflammasomes. Meanwhile, NDUFB3 overexpression has been confirmed to induce cell cycle arrest. Therefore, our results indicated that PM2.5 exposure induced pulmonary vascular remodeling via the following mechanism: PM2.5 exposure downregulated MSC expression, which in turn elevated the NDUFB3 level, activated the downstream AMPK/P53/P21 signaling pathway, and ultimately induced senescence of PAECs, thereby triggering pulmonary vascular remodeling.

Conclusion

This study suggested that PM2.5 exposure induced pulmonary vascular remodeling in mice, which might be partly due to mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to senescence of pulmonary artery endothelial cells. MSC was identified as the potential regulator by a combination of bioinformatics prediction and cellular experimental validation. In mechanism, MSC was directly interacted with NDUFB3, thereby causing mitochondrial dysfunction and promoting cellular senescence of PAECs. This study provided a new research perspective on the effects of PM2.5 on pulmonary vascular disease, at least in some patients, thus, identifying its potential therapeutic targets.
