Delayed progressive fibrotic lung disease associated with bilateral silicone breast implant rupture: A case report
Authors: Moath Bani Salem, Hamza Alzghoul, Ali Ataya, Kristianna Fredenburg, Adam Austin, Cesar Trillo-Alvarez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSilicone breast implants, although widely used in the United States, have been associated with a broad spectrum of pulmonary complications following implant rupture or silicone migration. These complications include pathologies such as embolic phenomena, organizing pneumonia, and, less commonly, interstitial lung disease. Usually, these complications occur years after the implant rupture, reflecting the indolent and delayed nature of the disease. In this case, we present a 77-year-old woman with a history of bilateral silicone breast implants at age 35 presented to the emergency department with progressive shortness of breath. Two years prior to her current presentation, a high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) showed early bilateral interstitial lung disease with minimal clinical symptoms and concurrently described ruptured bilateral silicone implants. A repeat CT scan during this admission showed an increase in the progression of the disease with diffuse ground-glass opacities, reticulation, architectural distortion, and traction bronchiectasis. Extensive infectious, autoimmune, and exposure evaluation were unrevealing, and silicone-induced lung disease was felt to be the most likely culprit. Pathology examination of lung biopsy showed organizing fibrin along with foamy histiocytes with lipid laden features. Additionally, lipid-like droplet suggestive of silicone was identified in vacuolized lung tissue. Despite aggressive treatment, patient developed progressive hypoxic respiratory failure and eventually transitioned to hospice care and died shortly afterward. This case shows the potentially fatal and delayed manifestations of silicone-related pulmonary disease presenting as progressive ILD following breast implant rupture. Awareness of this association is essential for early recognition and management in patients with unexplained ILD and prior silicone exposure.Keywords: Silicone, Interstitial lung disease, Fibrosis, Implant rupture, Foreign body reaction

Introduction

Silicone breast implants are widely used for cosmetic purposes, and their side-effect profile is generally considered safe [1]. Trauma or degeneration of the silicone capsule can lead to silicone particle migration and eventual adverse effects. Prevalence of silicone leakage from implants has been reported in the Netherlands Cohort to be as high as 98% [2]. Rare lung complications can occur, most commonly presenting as acute pneumonitis or embolic phenomena. Chronic interstitial lung disease is uncommon and may present years to decades after silicone implantation (mean 20 years) [3]. Symptoms include progressive dyspnea, cough, and fatigue. Diagnosis depends on imaging features showing diffuse ground glass opacities and nodular pattern bilaterally. Histopathology usually reveals non necrotizing epithelioid granuloma, foreign body giant cells surrounding silicone particles, and fat staining [3].

However, literature evidence demonstrates that these radiologic and histopathologic features are not always present. Imaging findings may be nonspecific and can include reticulation, architectural distortion, traction bronchiectasis, or fibrotic changes, while histopathology may show foamy histiocytes, airspace organization, or interstitial fibrosis without well-formed granulomas or identifiable giant-cell reactions [3,4]. Main treatment modality is removal of the implants and systemic corticosteroids therapy [5]. Here, we present a case of progressive interstitial lung disease associated with ruptured silicone breast implants.

Case report

A 77-year-old woman with a past medical history of former tobacco use with a 40-pack-year history, hypothyroidism, obesity, and bilateral silicone breast implants at approximately 37 years of age, who presented to our hospital with slowly progressive shortness of breath and generalized weakness over the preceding 6 months. She had no known family history of autoimmune disease, interstitial lung disease, or other heritable pulmonary disorders.

The patient first had evidence of breast silicone implant damage around the age of 69, when she presented with chest discomfort, and subsequent MRI demonstrated bilateral extra and intracapsular silicone implant rupture, more pronounced on the left than the right side (Fig. 3). The patient was counseled regarding the need for implant removal; however, the procedure was not pursued due to insurance-related limitations.

The patient’s first imaging showed no acute processes on X-ray (Fig. 1A). However, CT scan showed evidence of interstitial lung disease (ILD) was at the age of 75 (Fig. 2A). At that time, she had a negative ILD panel except for a positive antinuclear antibody (ANA). She denied vaping, occupational or environmental inhalational exposures, bird exposure, or gastroesophageal reflux symptoms.

(B) Follow-up non-contrast CT demonstrates interval progression with diffuse reticulation, ground-glass opacities, architectural distortion, and traction bronchiectasis, more pronounced on the right. Also, ruptured bilateral breast prostheses with extension of prosthetic material into the bilateral axillary regions are noted.

Upon presenting to the emergency department, she was found to be in respiratory distress with a respiratory rate of 26 breaths per minute. Laboratory evaluation at that time showed no leukocytosis, procalcitonin of 0.17, and CRP of 46. Respiratory virus panels and cultures were negative for microbial growth. Chest X-ray obtained demonstrated bilateral opacities concerning for inflammatory processes (Fig. 1B). Repeat computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest showed chronic ILD with interval progression, including diffuse reticulation, ground-glass opacities, architectural distortion, and traction bronchiectasis, compared with prior imaging performed at age 75 (Fig. 2B).

The patient was initially treated with antibiotics and systemic corticosteroids and admitted for further evaluation. Despite initial therapy, her respiratory status deteriorated, requiring escalating oxygen supplementation, and she was eventually transferred to the intensive care unit for acute hypoxemic respiratory failure requiring endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation. Bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage was performed and was negative for infectious etiologies, with a cellular differential showing neutrophils 43%, lymphocytes 23%, and eosinophils 2%. A repeat autoimmune evaluation revealed a positive ANA which was not clinically significant as extensive connective tissue disease serologies were negative.

The patient was extubated following treatment with high-dose corticosteroids, with only modest improvement in her respiratory status. Given ongoing diagnostic uncertainty and progressive disease, a transbronchial lung biopsy was performed and demonstrated airspace organization with organizing fibrin and vacuolization, and numerous foamy histiocytes with lipid laden features (Fig. 4A and B). Further evaluation of the pathology slides showed a lipid-like droplet suggestive of silicone within vacuolized lung tissue (Fig. 4C). The clinicoradiographic and histopathologic findings support the diagnosis of silicone-induced ILD.

(B) High power view of foamy histiocytes with lipid-laden features (black arrows surround a cluster of histiocytes). (C) High power view of lipid like droplet suggestive of silicone (black arrow) within vacuolized lung tissue.

Unfortunately, she suffered progression of her respiratory failure despite immunosuppression with high dose glucocorticoid therapy. Given her poor prognosis, goals-of-care were discussed, and she was discharged to a long-term acute care facility with hospice services. She died approximately 2 weeks after discharge.

Discussion

This case describes an elderly woman with a history of ruptured breast silicone implants who started having pulmonary symptoms of progressive dyspnea along with ongoing worsening interstitial lung disease years after implants rupture leading to worsening respiratory failure and death. Although the association between silicone implants and interstitial lung diseases is rarely observed, the limited reports in literature describe either stabilization or partial improvement after corticosteroid therapy and/or implant removal [5]. However, our patient had progressive fibrotic lung disease despite treatment, raising concern that persistent exposure from retained ruptured implants may have contributed to ongoing disease progression.

Additionally, despite the incidence being low, limited evidence shows that this statistic is likely under recognition rather than true low incidence. Acutely, silicone induced lung diseases include silicone embolism syndrome, typically occurring within hours to days following silicone injections [6]. In contrast, chronic interstitial lung disease can develop years after the exposure; 1 case series showed chronic silicone pneumonitis presenting at a median of 22 years after first implant placement. Furthermore, individual cases document persistent pulmonary lesions over 15 years after implantation [7]. These delayed presentations are uncommon in the literature and usually manifest as diffuse ground glass opacities and granulomatous pneumonitis [8]. Compared with these reports, our case is unique because the patient had documented bilateral extracapsular implant rupture, delayed development of progressive ILD, pathologic findings supportive of silicone-related lung injury, and ultimately a fatal clinical course. This highlights that chronic silicone-induced lung disease may not always follow an indolent or stable course.

The pathophysiology of silicone-induced lung disease is thought to involve migration of silicone particles through hematogenous or lymphatic pathways following implant rupture or silicone injections exposure, ranging from acute embolic phenomena to chronic interstitial lung disease. In patients with retained ruptured implants, continued silicone exposure may theoretically sustain local and systemic inflammatory responses, although the exact mechanism and risk factors for progression remain poorly understood [9].

Typical Imaging findings in chronic silicone-induced lung disease include diffuse ground glass opacities and bilateral nodular patterns. However, variation exists with some reports showing interstitial infiltrates, architectural distortion, and lymphadenopathy [10]. This is important to recognize as the absence of typical features and unawareness of other variations might lead to under-recognition of the disease [6,10]. Our patient’s imaging showed progressive ground-glass opacities with reticulation, architectural distortion, and traction bronchiectasis, suggesting evolution toward fibrotic lung disease rather than isolated inflammatory pneumonitis. This radiographic progression may help explain the limited response to corticosteroids and the poor clinical outcome.

Histopathologic findings are variable and may show vacuolated spaces consistent with silicone material, foamy macrophages, and features of organizing pneumonitis, with or without a foreign-body giant-cell reaction [4]. Other reported findings include interstitial fibrosis, and fat droplets staining [3,4]. The pathology in our case is consistent with these previously described findings, including vacuolization, lipid-laden foamy histiocytes, airspace organization, and a lipid-like droplet suggestive of silicone (Fig. 4A-C). Given the rarity of tissue-confirmed silicone-induced ILD, these findings add educational value by correlating the patient’s radiographic progression with histopathologic evidence of silicone-related lung injury.

In our patient, the diagnosis of silicone-induced lung disease is supported by the combination of bilateral extracapsular implant rupture and progressive increase in ground-glass opacities and fibrosis on CT imaging (Fig. 2). Surgical pathology showed features that are also consistent with this pathology, including vacuolization, numerous foamy histiocytes, airspace organization, and a droplet suggestive of silicone (Fig. 4). This clinicoradiographic and histopathologic correlation strengthens the diagnosis, particularly after exclusion of more common causes of progressive ILD.

Alternative etiologies were considered. Infectious etiologies were ruled out through an extensive workup, including negative cultures and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing performed on both blood and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) specimens. Connective tissue disease–associated ILD was less likely given only an isolated positive ANA result with negative Anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein and absent characteristic symptoms such as joints, rashes, or GI symptoms. Aspiration related lipoid pneumonia was also considered but was unlikely in the absence of dysphagia, gastroesophageal reflux symptoms, or known triggering factors.

Management of silicone induced lung disease is not standardized. Systemic corticosteroids have been used with variable success and were only partially helpful in our patient. Removal of the offending source may stop or decrease disease progression and is considered the main part of the treatment [5]. However, in our case, implant removal was discussed during earlier stages of disease but was not pursued due to insurance limitations.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of recognition of imaging and pathology variations associated with delayed, progressive silicone-induced pulmonary diseases following dual implant rupture. Clinicians and radiologists should maintain a high index of suspicion in patients with unexplained interstitial lung disease and a history of silicone implants rupture.
