Abstract
. Scenario-based training has been widely adopted in many public service sectors. Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown promise in simulating diverse personas to create these training scenarios. However, little is known about how LLMs can be developed to simulate victims for scenario-based training purposes. In this paper, we introduce VicSim (victim simulator), a novel model that addresses three key dimensions of user simulation: informational faithfulness, emotional dynamics, and language style (e.g., grammar usage). We pioneer the integration of scenario-based victim modeling with GAN-based training workflow and key-information-based prompting, aiming to enhance the realism of simulated victims. Our adversarial training approach teaches the discriminator to recognize grammar and emotional cues as reliable indicators of synthetic content. According to evaluations by human raters, the VicSim model outperforms GPT-4 in terms of human-likeness .

1.
Introduction

Scenario-based training has been widely used in many professional domains to enable trainees to acquire complex skills
(
Peeters et al. 2014
;
Salas et al. 2006
)
that are often unable to be learned through traditional lecturing or textbooks. Such training involves asking participants to practice skills by performing tasks designed with learning objectives contextualized in simulated scenarios.
However, scenario-based training usually requires extensive human resources and efforts from educators in designing and running high-quality scenarios
(
Peeters et al. 2014
)
. More specifically for public service providers who need to communicate with the general public, such as dispatchers and text-based safety incident reporting
(
Ming et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2023
)
, scenario-based training often involves simulated interactions with service users, typically role-played by training facilitators, to prepare providers for a wide range of real-world scenarios.

Computer-supported training has been widely adopted in many public sectors
(
Reuter et al. 2009b
;
Kolmanič et al. 2013
;
Reuter et al. 2009a
)
.
With recent advances in NLP research, natural language generation methods (e.g., LLMs) have been increasingly used to enrich training scenarios
(
Lin et al. 2023
;
Demasi et al. 2020
)
.
In the domain of AI/ML, user simulation aims to extract valuable insights and patterns from user behaviors in different in-context scenarios
(
Balog and Zhai 2023
;
Tan and Jiang 2023
)
.
This process is instrumental in adapting systems to cater to the unique requirements of specific user groups.

This aspect of user modeling is particularly critical but challenging in the context of safety incident reporting, given victims tend to demonstrate negative emotion fluctuations while experiencing safety-related incidents and exhibit varied reporting behavior
(
Feldman 2021
;
Tracy and Tracy 1998
;
Liu et al. 2023
)
.
In this context, user modeling can help enhance the customization and adaptation of training systems and improve dispatchers’ preparation for handling real-life incident reports with both accuracy and empathy.
However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no LLM-based simulator system for training dispatchers who handle incident reports.

In this paper, we focus on simulating "text-based incident" reporting. We introduced several enhancements to the scenario-based user simulation in the safety incident reporting domain. Building upon Llama 2-7B-based chat model
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
, we have developed a suite of improvements aimed at elevating both the informational and emotional faithfulness of simulated users.
Our methodology involves a multifaceted approach to guide dialogue generation to enhance the authenticity of user responses. With these improvements, our objective is to develop a dialogue experience that is more detailed and engaging, fostering deeper interactions within scenario-based applications. Specifically, by leveraging these enhancements, we seek to simulate a more human-like user based on constructed safety reporting incidents. This enables the chatbot to serve as a valuable tool for dispatcher training, allowing dispatcher trainees to engage in conversations with a simulated user that closely mimics real-world scenarios.
Our contribution includes the new model and empirical understanding of the effectiveness of the model in the potential scenario-based training context.

Specifically, we address the following research questions:

•

RQ1
:
How does scenario-based LLM user simulation differ from humans in terms of chat style and informational faithfulness in safety incident reporting?

•

RQ2
:
In what ways can we improve the emotional dynamics of the victim simulation model?

•

RQ3
:
In what ways can we better simulate human-like victims in incident reporting interactions in terms of chat style?

This research has practical implications that the contributed model
VicSim
can be applied to enrich scenario-based training for dispatchers in diverse settings to acquire and improve crucial skills and learn from diverse simulated scenarios.

2.
Related Works

2.1.
User Simulation

User simulation plays a crucial role in the understanding of user behaviors, facilitating the customization and adaptation of response systems tailored to a group of prototype users. It enables us to gain deeper insights into user behavior, allowing for the refinement of system responses. Additionally, user simulation aids in training system staff to effectively respond to potential users across various scenarios.

Through user simulation techniques, researchers have gained a deeper understanding of user behaviors, leading to customized intelligent assistance and significantly enhanced user experiences. For instance, when individuals search for an online course through customer service, user simulation techniques analyze their search history, predict their current learning interests, and provide personalized recommendations tailored to their preferences and learning goals. These simulations take into account factors such as past course selections, browsing patterns, engagement levels, course selection from users with similar query histories, and feedback to deliver relevant and engaging course suggestions. By leveraging user simulation, platforms can better cater to the diverse needs and interests of learners, ultimately improving the overall online learning experience. The impact of user simulation extends across various domains such as e-commerce
(
Berger and Kompan 2019
;
Vullam et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2022
;
Deldjoo et al. 2024
)
, social networks
(
Gatti et al. 2014
;
Birdsey et al. 2015
;
Li and Abhari 2017
)
, search engine
(
Salle et al. 2021
)
, task-oriented dialogues
(
Li et al. 2016
;
Gür et al. 2018
;
Terragni et al. 2023
)
, education
(
De la Croix and Skelton 2013
)
, etc. making it a highly active and influential research field.

User simulation typically involves mining and learning from user data, which includes user profiles, user-generated content, and user interactions with content and other users. User profiles are often characterized by attributed interests or personas. User-generated content encompasses diverse text data sources such as conversation histories, reviews, blogs, etc. which can be analyzed using natural language processing techniques. User interactions include actions as rating and sharing, which represent edges between nodes, e.g., users, in text-attributed graphs. Graph mining techniques are accordingly employed to analyze such interactions.
(
Beutel 2016
;
Wang et al. 2019
)
Consequently, user simulation leverages a combination of text-based and graph-based approaches, focusing on mimicking real humans from both text and graph data.

The advent of transformers has revolutionized user simulation research, particularly with pretrained language models operating under the pretrain-finetune paradigm. Pretrained language models leverage self-supervised learning on large unlabeled corpora and fine-tuning on specific tasks with a smaller labeled dataset. These models have showcased remarkable abilities in reasoning, generalization, and knowledge comprehension, surpassing human performance in various tasks. Their emergence has sparked a new era in User Simulation research, prompting a reevaluation of user-generated content mining strategies.

In the context of victims reporting incidents, the delivery of emotional support by dispatchers is crucial, especially considering the emotional distress individuals may experience when reporting traumatic or safety-related incidents
(

Simulating users’ emotions in various scenarios is thus vital for dispatcher training, ensuring they can effectively respond to callers’ emotional needs while gathering necessary information for incident handling. While existing research has primarily focused on the functional aspects of text-based reporting systems, there is a gap in understanding how dispatchers should navigate and address users’ emotional states within such systems to optimize service delivery. This highlights the need for further empirical research and training initiatives aimed at enhancing dispatcher capabilities in emotional support in the safety incident reporting domain.

Recent research on LLMs has demonstrated their potential in simulating users and personas

Jiang et al. 2023

Salewski et al. 2024

. Yet, little research has been done on how LLMs could be leveraged for simulating victims sending incident reports, such that the simulated messages can be used for scenario-based training.

To encourage human-like generations, we adapted an adversarial training approach by retraining the Llama-2-based user as a generator in a generative adversarial network (GAN)
(
Goodfellow et al. 2014
)
, as shown in Figure
1
. This involves leveraging a Flan-T5-based classifier
(
Longpre et al. 2023
)
as the discriminator and the Llama-2-based user as the generator.

To improve the text representation of the Flan-T5-based encoder, we instruction-tuned Flan-T5 with emotional prompts and prompts designed to simulate grammar errors. These prompts were used as instructions to align with the emotional and grammar error labels with the dataset described in section
3.3
. By incorporating emotional prompts, we aimed to imbue the encoder with a deeper understanding of nuanced emotional expressions within the text. Additionally, by simulating grammar errors, we sought to train an encoder that can recognize and potentially correct grammatical inconsistencies, thereby improving overall text coherence and quality. Through this meticulous tuning process, we aimed to refine the Flan-T5-based encoder to better capture relevant representations of text data.

Specifically, given a context prompt
p
,
G(p)
represents the output of the generator network, a.k.a. the simulated user. It is expected to produce synthetic data samples, i.e., fake sentences. In discriminator loss
L^{D}_{\text{GAN}}
, the first component, represents the expected value of the negative logarithm of the discriminator’s output when given real data samples
x
. This term encourages the discriminator to assign high probabilities to real data samples. While the second component,

\mathbb{E}[\log(1-D(G(p)))]
, represents the expected value of the negative logarithm of the discriminator’s output when given generated data samples

G(p)
. This term encourages the discriminator to assign low probabilities to utterances generated by LLM. The generator aims to minimize this term by generating samples that the discriminator is more likely to classify as real.

(1)

L_{\text{GAN}}^{G}=-\mathbb{E}[\log D(G(p))]

(2)

L_{\text{GAN}}^{D}=-\mathbb{E}[\log D(x)]-\mathbb{E}[\log(1-D(G(p)))]

3.2.
Dataset

3.2.1.
Data Collection

The dataset used in this study comprises chat logs collected through a text-based incident reporting system, which has been adopted by over 200 higher education institutions
(
Ming et al. 2021
)
. The system facilitates communication between members of an organization and safety teams responsible for risk management
(
Ming et al. 2021
)
. Community members can submit information or "tips" via their mobile app or web portal. Safety organization dispatchers, in turn, can respond to these tips using the dashboard. Tips may include text, photographs, video, or audio recordings and can be categorized into different "tip types," selected by local safety departments for each organization. We eliminated "Test" entries and retained only relevant categories. Specifically, we preserved nine well-populated, detail-rich event types, such as SuspiciousActivity, DrugsAlcohol, and NoiseDisturbance, etc. We retained chat logs committed between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2019, and retained chats with more than two utterances (or one conversation turn), as we focused on investigating chats involving at least one turn of information-collecting conversation. We then evaluated users’ utterances in different conversation stages, from turn 2 to turn 22, as illustrated in Figure
4
.

3.2.2.
Data Ethics

The study was carried out under a strict non-disclosure agreement (NDA), analyzing anonymized data from consenting users and organizations for research on system improvements. The data was thoroughly anonymized, removing or masking all personal and sensitive information, including identities, geolocations, and organizational details, with anonymous IDs and generic tags (e.g., [LOCATION], [NAME]). The masked information was then synthesized and filled in using Llama 2-70b. This approach significantly reduced de-anonymization risks.
Additionally, as a further privacy measure, data from a random selection of the institutions was excluded from the dataset prior to researchers’ access.

Evaluation

We evaluated
VicSim
from three key areas: user emotion understanding, grammar style fidelity, and context faithfulness. Additionally, we complement these three automatic evaluations with human judgment to gain deeper insights into the subjective aspects of response quality. Human evaluation allows us to assess nuances such as fluency, coherence, and overall naturalness, which are essential for generating engaging and contextually relevant conversations.

Users’ utterances at different conversation stages on the evaluation set

3.3.1.
Assessment of Informational faithfulness

In the assessment of context faithfulness, we employed NER modules within Stanford CoreNLP
(
Manning et al. 2014
)
as a proxy to extract keywords from the scenarios generated by the Llama-2 70B. Subsequently, as depicted in Figure
5
we calculated the overlap between these extracted keywords and those present in a user’s utterances. A successful recall is identified when a user mentioned a keyword that appeared in groundtruth keywords in the scenario summary. This approach allowed us to gauge the fidelity of the generated scenarios in capturing relevant contextual information, as indicated by the presence of matching keywords in user interactions. As shown in the example demonstrated in Figure
5
, the utterance generated by LLM successfully retrieved the ground truth keywords ’Cortright’ and ’teacher’ in the outlined scenario.

3.3.2.
Assessment of User Emotions

We fine-tuned a RoBERTa-based classifier on the GoEmotions dataset, which classifies emotions into 28 fine-grained emotions. We follow the positive / negative / neutral emotion classification as described in the GoEmotions dataset.
We evaluate the emotional faithfulness of the user simulation model based on the emotional dynamics within the generated responses. Given a scenario with ground truth conversations, we compare the classified emotion categories between each model-generated response with the ground truth.

3.3.3.
Assessment of Grammar styles

Humans make grammar mistakes, while language models don’t. To gauge this difference, We fine-tuned the RoBERTa-based model on a grammar classification error dataset
(
Kaggle 2021
)
along 2,000 grammatically correct sentences from C4_200M
(
Stahlberg and Kumar 2021
)
. The dataset encompasses 37 categories, which include 36 types of common grammar errors as well as a category for sentences with no errors. Through fine-tuning, we constructed a three-layer linear classification head on top of the RoBERTa output. This proxy evaluation model achieved an accuracy of 94.25% on the partitioned validation set.

3.3.4.
Human Evaluation

To understand the extent to which our model can simulate the responses of real victims, we created a survey and asked Amazon Mechanical Turk annotators to rate key aspects of the outputs generated by our model in terms of human-likeness.
For each rater, we randomly sampled one incident and presented the dialogue history to the rater.
We then present the last user response generated by our model, human, and GPT-4 to the user respectively, and collect rater’s ratings regarding four dimensions including coherency, consistency, level of detail, human likeness.
We designed a rating survey using 5-point Likert scale items for each dimension.
The responses for each incident are presented in a random order.

Participants also shared their perception of why discerning human responses was challenging.
Two major difficulties were identified: First, they were unfamiliar with the linguistic patterns exhibited by individuals in real life during urgent or emotionally charged situations;
Second, while they anticipated that the verbose or detailed nature of responses typically generated by LLMs might serve as a reliable indicator, they were surprised to find that human responses could also be similarly detailed.
Moreover, those participants who were successful in differentiating between human and LLM-generated responses often relied on subtle grammatical cues or specific uses of punctuation.

In this section, we present our findings in the emotional dynamics generated by
VicSim
from the following perspectives. First, we presented the different emotional trajectories over the reporting stages between human chats and
VicSim
-generated dialogue. We found that
VicSim
expressed more emotions than humans in general, and then we shared three observations that may explain the differences. For example, we found an association between emotional expressions and the chat length, LLM were more emotional when repeatedly describing situations, and LLM expressed emotions when they hallucinated. 4.2.1.
Different Emotional Expressions between
VicSim
and Humans

First, regarding emotional changes at different stages of reporting dialogue, with our RoBERTa-based emotion classifier, our analysis reveals a notable pattern in user emotions during safety incident reporting dialogues: users tend to express negative feelings at the beginning of a dialogue, typically when the conversation has progressed to around the 40% mark of the conversation. GPT-4, however, does not exhibit similar patterns. This finding underscores the significance of emotional support provision at the initial stages of user interaction, suggesting that users often require immediate emotional assistance or reassurance when initiating a dialogue regarding safety incidents. Furthermore, our aligned LLM, mimicking scenario-based user behavior, demonstrates a similar trend, indicating the model’s ability to capture and replicate key emotional dynamics observed in real user interactions. This alignment highlights the potential of utilizing AI-driven dialogue systems to effectively respond to users’ emotional needs in safety incident reporting scenarios, particularly by prioritizing emotional support delivery during the early stages of interaction. By emulating users’ emotional trajectories, these AI models can better serve as training systems to train human dispatchers on how to respond in scenarios that involve evolving emotions. 5.
Discussion

5.1.
VicSim
’s Effectiveness and Potential

The results of the
VicSim
model with the discriminators show that the LLM-based simulator is guided by the T5-based discriminator in the adversarial training flow. Different from previous work
(
Yu et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Donahue and Rumshisky 2018
;
Hu et al. 2023
)
, we highlighted on grammar errors through distilling T5 before adversarial training. Through this training process, the discriminator achieves a notable accuracy in distinguishing between human and
VicSim
responses. Particularly, when faced with human sentences containing punctuation errors, the discriminator displays a significantly higher accuracy in identifying
VicSim
’s sentences compared to other error types such as "Preposition Usage" or "Spelling Mistakes".

Punctuation errors are prevalent in human responses, likely due to instances where sentences in the dataset lack proper ending punctuation. For example, sentences like "They have stopped, no need to stop by now" or "Yes, it is" are common. The discriminator learns from this pattern and recognizes sentences with punctuation errors as more likely to be human-generated.
This leads to a notable observation: in adversarial training, there’s a tendency for
VicSim
to generate punctuation errors deliberately to deceive the discriminator. But at the same time, generate could not notice minority errors such as "Preposition Usage" or "Spelling Mistakes" even though discriminators can detect them with high accuracy. This observation is similar to the findings discussed in the literature of GAN-based text generation
(
De Rosa and Papa 2021
;
Bayer et al. 2022
)
.

It is evident from the results in Section
4.3
, that the discriminator relies heavily on grammar cues. When presented with human utterances devoid of any mistakes, the discriminator struggles to distinguish between
VicSim
and human responses, showing a significantly lower accuracy compared to the average accuracy achieved.

5.2.
Factors Contributing to LLMs’ Emotional Expressions

Previous research suggests that real humans often display more emotional cues when they lie, as seen in studies like
(
Warren et al. 2009
;
Gray 2011
)
.
Shaw and Lyons 2017
found that individuals who pay attention to these emotional cues tend to be more successful at detecting deception.
We found that, in Section
4.2.2
, by measuring the precision and recall of the keyword overlap, humans deliver more accurate information, while VicSim and GPT-4 sometimes make things up. Language models deliver information, from time to time, that does not belong to the in-context scenario. Furthermore, we find an interesting fact that LLMs address emotional expression more when they hallucinate.
As it is pointed out in empirical studies
(
Warren et al. 2009
;
Gray 2011
;
Shaw and Lyons 2017
)
, emotional cues provide a robust indication of human deceptions. We draw a similar observation in VicSim’s generation, when repeatedly generating sentences on the case scenario, human users are from different views, providing fine-grained details in response to dispatchers’ questions, while LLMs express distress, especially when they hallucinate.

5.3.
Practical Implications for Scenario-Based Training with Linguistic Fidelity

There are various factors that service providers need to consider when providing text-based services.

Zhou and Chang 2024
provided insights into how informational and emotional support lead to different outcomes when provided during conversational services. Our study provided further findings to support the discovery of additional factors that service providers should consider when designing user simulations for training.
We classified the factors into two categories: informational and emotional factors.
Simulating human emotion dynamics is of major significance when conducting user simulation
(
Feine et al. 2019
;
Becker et al. 2004
)
. From the results in
4.2
, we showed that LLMs fine-tuned using domain-specific data have the potential to achieve similar emotional dynamics to humans than off-the-shelf larger models such as GPT-4.
We also identified temporal emotional patterns that can be better simulated using the VicSim model, thus delivering a convincing simulation for training with high emotional faithfulness given a specified scenario.
Informational factors, on the other hand, were also found to be multi-faceted.
LLMs are known to have the issue of hallucination, and maintaining robust information faithfulness is crucial for believable user simulation
(
Sekulić et al. 2024
;
Tonmoy et al. 2024
)
. Through augmenting LLM fine-tuning with keywords using information extraction, we were able to improve the model’s information faithfulness as shown in
4.1
. The importance of factual consistency throughout the dialogue in terms of the simulation of human likeness was also reflected during the human evaluation.
Interestingly, we also identified language style as an informational factor in user simulation, and proposed a novel adversarial training method to improve the similarity between the grammar usage styles between LLMs and humans. In practice, this method can also be used to increase the linguistic fidelity of LLMs in other aspects beyond grammar style, such as formality and verbosity.

6.
Limitations

Our analysis uncovered novel insights into how LLMs can used for victim simulation in dispatcher training and potential methods of improvement. However, this study still has several limitations. Our method and analysis focused on text-based interactions, which tend to reflect less urgent incidents in comparison to those reported through voice-based emergency calls. In practice, victims’ reporting behavior and language cues might be different during voice-based communication.
Future research should explore diverse communication settings to enable multi-modal interaction (e.g., voice-enabled victim simulator) for a more immersive training environment.
Meanwhile, our evaluation of VicSim was not conducted under actual training scenarios with dispatchers and lacked empirical understanding of the training effectiveness of the system. Further studies involving trainees and domain experts should be conducted to provide a deeper understanding and collect user feedback.
Further research should explore how these simulation models can be integrated into real-world training curricula and assess their impact on service quality and practitioner preparedness.

7.
Conclusion

In this study, we provide valuable insights into how LLMs can be leveraged to simulate human users for scenario-based training in safety incident reporting domains. We focus mainly on emotional trajectory, grammar style, and information faithfulness in LLM-based agents.
By enhancing the models with techniques like adversarial training and key information prompting, we were able to generate more realistic and human-like victim responses compared to off-the-shelf models. While the adversarial training workflow is effective in inducing human-like behavior, specifically grammar style, experiments show more fine-grained refinement is needed to better align the emotional changes with those of human beings. Moving forward, further research should explore how these simulation models can be integrated into real-world training curricula and assess their impact on service quality and practitioner preparedness.
Our findings pave the way for more effective and immersive training experiences that better prepare service providers to handle complex, emotionally charged situations.
