Sustained disease remission after a limited duration of bispecific antibody therapy in patients with multiple myeloma
Authors: Gala Vega, Daniel Morillo, Christine Riedhammer, Febe Smits, Aishee Bag, Kelly Julian, Natalia Tovar Gomis, Carmen Montes Gaisan, Maria Aranzazu Bermudez, Mansi R Shah, Ghulam Rehman Mohyuddin, Binod Dhakal, Leo Rasche, Carolina Schinke, Anita D'Souza, Aniko Szabo, Niels W C J van de Donk, Rajshekhar Chakraborty, Meera Mohan
Published: 2026

Bispecific antibodies have shown unprecedented efficacy in relapsed refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 While bispecific antibody (bsAb) therapy is currently administered indefinitely until disease progression or intolerance, there is a subset of patients with RRMM who have prolonged remission, off treatment after a finite duration of bsAb therapy. 5 We previously reported sustained remission after a limited duration of bsAb therapy in 23 patients, with an 18‐month progression free survival (PFS) rate of 70%. 5 Here, we report sustained remission, humoral immune reconstitution, and infection risk off treatment in a larger cohort of patients treated across multiple centers with a finite duration of bsAb exposure and extended follow‐up.

Patients included in this analysis received bsAb, either as standard of care or in a clinical trial, and discontinued therapy for reasons other than disease progression or death and had at least 3 months of ongoing remission following treatment discontinuation. While the requirement for a minimum of 3 months of sustained remission off‐therapy was chosen somewhat arbitrarily, it was grounded in the pharmacokinetic properties of bsAb. BsAb have a prolonged serum half‐life and extended activity well beyond the time of the last administered dose. For example, teclistamab has a mean elimination half‐life of 27.2 days after the 13th weekly dose of 1.5 mg/kg. 6 A patient who relapses within 3 months of the last dose may therefore be experiencing disease escape in the context of resistance/refractoriness to bsAb rather than true off‐therapy relapse. Relapse‐free survival (RFS), relapse and non‐relapse mortality, humoral immune reconstitution, cumulative risk of ≥Grade 3 infection, and intravenous immunoglobulin replacement (IVIG) use were analyzed (see Supporting Information S1: Supplement for detailed methods).

Among the 860 patients treated across seven institutions, 81 patients who received bispecific or multispecific antibodies (hereafter referred to as bsAb) targeting either B‐cell maturation antigen (BCMA) and/or G protein‐coupled receptor Class C Group 5 Member D (GPRC5D) were included in this analysis. The baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. The median age was 70 (range 26–86) years (Supporting Information S2: Table 1). The median number of lines of therapy was 4 (range 1–9). In total, 48% of patients had high‐risk cytogenetics, and 7.7% had extramedullary disease (EMD).

The median duration of bsAb therapy was 7 months (range 1–40), with 32% of patients remaining on treatment for more than a year before discontinuation. 65.4% (n = 53) of patients were treated on a clinical trial. Patients receiving treatment on a clinical trial were treated longer prior to discontinuation (10 vs. 4 months) and had higher rates of ≥complete remission (CR) at treatment discontinuation (73% vs. 64%). The majority of patients received a BCMA‐targeted bsAb. All patients responded to therapy, with the majority (76%) achieving a CR at treatment discontinuation. Infectious complications were the most common reason for treatment discontinuation (48%, n = 39), followed by skin and nail adverse effects (12%, n = 10). The median duration of follow‐up after discontinuing bsAb therapy was 19 months (range 4–61). The 36‐month RFS was 62% (95% CI, 48%, 80%) with an estimated median RFS of 43 months (Figure 1). Notably, 7 patients in this study experienced sustained remission of at least 3 years after treatment discontinuation, with the longest remission approaching 5 years.

Relapse‐free survival of patients treated with a limited‐duration bispecific antibody therapy. The 12‐month relapse‐free survival was 81% (95% CI 72%, 90%), while the 24‐ and 36‐month relapse‐free survival rates were 71% (95% CI 61%, 83%) and 60% (95% CI 46%, 77%), respectively. The estimated median relapse‐free survival was 43 (95% CI 31, NR) months. bsAb, bispecific antibody.

On multivariate analysis, the presence of EMD (hazard ratio [HR] = 7.42; 95% CI 2.29, 24; P ≤ 0.001), and a higher number of prior lines of therapy (HR = 8.17; 95% CI 2.02, 33; P ≤ 0.003) were associated with an inferior RFS (Supporting Information S3: Table 2).

Patients who received ≥6 prior lines of therapy had a 36‐month RFS of 41% (estimated median RFS of 29 months), compared to 67% for those treated with 1–3 prior lines and 85% for those with 4–5 prior lines of therapy. The presence of EMD was associated with a 36‐month RFS of 17% and a median RFS of 10 months compared to 71% and a median of 43 months in patients without EMD. The 36‐month non‐relapse and relapse mortality rates were 7.7% (95% CI, 2%–19%) and 30% (95% CI, 17%–45%), respectively.

At the time of disease progression, two patients were retreated with the same bsAb. One patient received elranatamab but discontinued treatment after 1.5 months due to a Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) infection. After a 9‐month therapy pause, treatment was reinitiated at the time of disease relapse, resulting in a CR. The second patient also received elranatamab for 5.5 months before discontinuing due to an infection and had sustained remission off treatment that lasted for 7.5 months. However, reinitiating treatment was ineffective in the face of imminent disease progression.

A total of 74 ≥Grade 3 infection events occurred in 28 patients between bsAb discontinuation and the end of follow‐up for each patient. There were three Grade 5 infections. The 36‐month cumulative incidence of first, ≥Grade 3 infections was 41%. Infection rates gradually decreased and stabilized after approximately 6 months, suggesting a time‐dependent reduction in infection risk (Figure 2). The rates of recurrent infection averaged 0.07 (standard deviation 0.15) per month during <180 days of stopping therapy and decreased to 0.02 (0.07) as time off treatment increased. These rates dropped to zero after 900 days following treatment discontinuation.

A total of 404 IVIG were administered to 51 patients between the day of stopping bsAb (Day 0) and the end of follow‐up; the remaining 24 patients did not receive IVIG. In total, 67.4% of patients remained hypogammaglobulinemic even after 180 days off bsAb. The IgG levels increased by 14 mg/dL each month after stopping therapy, and fewer patients remained hypogammaglobulinemic over time. Serum IgG levels normalized in nearly 90% of patients after about 900 days off treatment. A similar trend of decreasing IVIG replacement was observed with longer time off treatment, with 13% of patients using IVIG in the 180‐day window beyond 900 days compared to 62.7% in the first 180 days. Not surprisingly, patients who received IVIG had lower IgG than those who did not receive IVIG within the first 90 days after treatment discontinuation. However, this difference was not statistically significant, suggesting immunue reconstitution occurred over time irrespective of IVIG necessity.

In summary, we demonstrate that a substantial proportion of patients can maintain durable responses after discontinuation of bsAb therapy, with a gradual improvement in infection risk and immune reconstitution over time. In this study, the 36‐month RFS off treatment was 62% with an estimated median of 43 months. The predictors of relapse after treatment discontinuation included the presence of EMD and an increased number of prior therapy lines (≥6).

The risk of ≥Grade 3 infections persisted for the first 6 months after stopping treatment but decreased with increasing time since treatment. Importantly, we show here a slow but steady improvement in serum IgG levels, with 14 mg/dL increases each month off‐therapy, along with a significant reduction in IVIG supplementation over nearly 2.5 years after stopping bsAb. This highlights the profound and prolonged immunodeficiency observed with depletion of plasma cells caused by bsAb therapy, persisting beyond the treatment period. 7 From a clinical perspective, these findings underscore the need for continued infection surveillance and prolonged IVIG supplementation beyond cessation of bsAb therapy.

Currently approved bsAbs, including teclistamab, elranatamab, linvoseltamab, and talquetamab, were all developed and tested as continuous therapy until progression or intolerance. Apart from financial implications, continuous bsAb exposure may have several disadvantages, including T‐cell exhaustion, leading to reduced efficacy and increased infection risk, 8 , 9 , 10 reduced efficacy of subsequent T‐cell engagers, and potentially impaired CAR‐T‐cell production if apheresis is performed immediately after bsAb, 11 as well as the possibility of inducing BCMA mutations or deletions due to continuous therapeutic pressure. 12 , 13

Sustained treatment‐free remission after a fixed duration of bsAb was first reported with AMG 420 (a BCMA‐targeting BiTE), where one patient discontinued therapy for polyneuropathy after just 2 weeks, and had a sustained CR at 8 months after treatment cessation. 14 Additionally, prolonged remissions have been demonstrated following a fixed duration of therapy (approximately 12 months) with cevostamab, a bispecific antibody targeting FcRH5, especially in patients who achieve a CR or better at the end of therapy. 15 A preliminary report of a Phase 2 single‐arm study of standard‐of‐care teclistamab reported a 6‐month failure‐free survival of 77%, with 6 of the 43 heavily pretreated myeloma patients experiencing disease progression. 16

It is plausible that bsAb‐mediated tumor cell lysis is not merely a terminal event but initiates epitope spreading, a powerful immunological phenomenon crucial for establishing sustained anti‐tumor immunity and preventing relapse. 17 , 18 , 19 Additionally, traditional pharmacokinetic analyses may be insufficient, necessitating consideration of additional response determinants such as soluble BCMA levels, the effector‐to‐target ratio, and T‐cell exhaustion. 20 , 21 , 22

Our study is limited by a heterogeneous patient population enrolled in early‐phase studies with varying drug doses; nevertheless, a significant proportion of patients achieved sustained remission off‐therapy. Further, we used an arbitrary inclusion criterion requiring patients to have at least 3 months of sustained remission without additional MM therapy after discontinuing bsAb therapy, which may have introduced selection bias by favoring patients with more favorable disease characteristics. Given the retrospective nature of the analysis, we may have selected patients in deep remission, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to this subset. A comparison of outcomes among patients on a continuous‐dosing schedule could not be conducted due to the lack of a control arm and insufficient follow‐up for meaningful analysis.

This study, together with ongoing prospective clinical trials such as LimiTEC, supports the feasibility of limited‐duration bsAb therapy. 16 These findings strongly advocate for prospective, ideally randomized, trials evaluating fixed‐duration bsAb therapy, ideally enrolling patients at the outset of earlier‐line treatment and using response‐adapted approaches, such as MRD status, to determine when to stop treatment.
