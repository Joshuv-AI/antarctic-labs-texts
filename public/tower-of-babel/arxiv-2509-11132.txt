Xiaoyu Zhang, Weipeng Jiang, Juan Zhai, Shiqing Ma, Qingshuang Bao, Chenhao Lin, Chao Shen, Tianlin Li, Yang Liu

Abstract
. Large language models (LLMs) are now an integral part of software development workflows and are reshaping the whole process. Traditional technology stack selection, i.e., the process of choosing the most suitable development tools and dependencies (e.g., third-party libraries), has not caught up. Most of the existing selection methods focus solely on the inherent attributes of the technology, overlooking whether the LLM can effectively leverage the chosen technology. For example, when generating code snippets using popular libraries like Selenium (one of the most widely used test automation tools with over 33k GitHub stars), existing LLMs frequently generate low-quality code snippets (e.g., using deprecated APIs and methods, or containing syntax errors). As such, teams using LLM assistants risk choosing technologies that cannot be used effectively by LLMs, yielding high debugging effort and mounting technical debt. We foresee a practical question in the LLM era, is a technology ready for AI-assisted development ? In this paper, we first propose the concept, AI coding proficiency , the degree to which LLMs can utilize a given technology to generate high-quality code snippets. To measure this, we design a systematic evaluation pipeline. Using this pipeline, we conduct the first comprehensive empirical study examining AI proficiency across 170 third-party libraries and 61 task scenarios, evaluating six widely used LLMs. Our findings reveal that libraries with similar functionalities can exhibit up to 84% differences in the quality score of LLM-generated code, while different models also exhibit quality gaps among their generation results using the same library. These gaps translate into real engineering costs and can steer developer choices toward a narrow set of libraries with high AI coding proficiency, threatening technological diversity in the ecosystem. We call on the community to integrate AI proficiency assessments into technology selection frameworks and develop mitigation strategies, preserving competitive balance in AI-driven development.



1.
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated impressive code generation and completion capabilities.
They have achieved code completion performance (e.g., in Python) that closes or even surpasses that of human developers
(
Hou and Ji 2025
;
OpenAI 2025
)
.
These models have been integrated into the daily workflows of developers worldwide, with recent reports indicating that 51% of professional developers use AI tools in their daily practice
(
Overflow 2025
)
.
This gives rise to new development styles such as ‘Vibe Coding’ that heavily rely on LLM-based software development, enabling small teams to efficiently deliver high-quality products
(
Hou and Ji 2025
;
Li et al. 2025
;
Masad 2025
)
.
As one of the most popular developer tools, the LLM is reshaping both the software ecosystem and development processes.

In the software development lifecycle (SDLC), developers always need to select appropriate programming languages, frameworks, and third-party libraries based on target requirements before engaging in actual coding, which is known as
technology stack selection

(
Stratoflow 2023
)
.
For example, when building a web application, developers usually decide to use one of the mainstream backend frameworks such as Spring Boot, Django, or Express.js, rather than building from scratch to boost productivity.
Technology stack selection has a decisive impact on development efficiency, maintainability, and the overall quality of the final software system
(
Birrell et al. 1988
;
Stratoflow 2023
)
.
From a corporate perspective, technology stack selection represents a critical strategic decision with far-reaching business implications.
Real-world cases have demonstrated that a good technology choice can effectively reduce development costs by up to 78%
(
Software 2025
)
, while an inappropriate technology stack can lead to the failure of enterprise projects and economic losses
(
Neville 2025
)
.
Recognizing the importance of an appropriate technique stack, existing software engineering (SE) research has proposed a variety of evaluation frameworks and guidelines to support the technology selection
(
Kazman et al. 2000
;
Bass et al. 2021
;
Kazman et al. 2001
;
International Organization for Standardization 2011
;
Golden et al. 1989
)
.
These approaches systematically evaluate multiple intrinsic quality attributes, such as maintainability, performance, and reliability, with quantitative metrics and qualitative assessments to rank candidate technologies, thereby facilitating the decision-making for optimal technology adoption that aligns with specific project requirements.

However, the advent of LLMs necessitates a fundamental rethinking of technology selection, as solutions that are friendly to human developers may not be equally effective for the workflows involving LLMs.
Taking the requirements description as an example, human users prefer to use readable natural language instructions, while scripted queries are easier to accurately parse by LLMs, thereby improving the efficiency and effectiveness of LLM-driven workflows
(
Beurer-Kellner et al. 2023
)
.
In software development, this mismatch between humans and models is becoming increasingly prominent.
Existing report has shown that when generating code snippets using libraries popular for human developers, LLM could generate low-quality code snippets that call deprecated APIs or methods and may even contain syntax errors
(
TESTINGMIND 2025
)
.
Therefore, there is an urgent need to investigate this emerging challenge and rethink the evaluation framework for technology stack selection in the LLM era.
While existing research has focused on evaluating the code generation performance of LLMs
(
Jiang et al. 2024
;
Peng et al. 2025
;
Tang et al. 2024
)
, to our knowledge, no work has systematically examined the performance of LLMs across different technologies, that is, whether the models can effectively generate high-quality code snippets using specific technologies.

To fill this gap, we firstly introduce a new perspective for technology stack selection in AI-assisted development in this paper, namely
AI coding proficiency
(hereafter ‘AI proficiency’), which refers to a property of a technology (e.g., a third-party library) that quantifies the degree of the given LLM to accurately understand and correctly invoke its APIs, thereby producing high-quality code snippets (e.g., maintainable).
We observe that even with similar functionality and popularity, technologies (in our study, third-party libraries) can exhibit substantial differences in AI proficiency, leading to large gaps in the quality of the code snippets generated by the same LLM.
For example, although
Shapely
and
GDAL
libraries have similar functionalities and popularity (i.e., Github stars), the code snippets generated by GPT-4o using
Shapely
typically have lower quality than those using
GDAL
(up to 20.21% on repeated runs of the same prompt), and some code snippets even exhibit basic syntax errors (
Fig. 2
).
The implications of AI proficiency are twofold.
At the engineering level, selecting a technology with low AI coding proficiency in development increases debugging effort and introduces additional economic and time costs, offsetting the productivity improvement brought by LLMs.
At the ecosystem levels, the gaps in AI proficiency can trigger a model-driven ‘Matthew effect’
(
Merton 1968
)
, where the technologies with higher AI proficiency are more likely to be adopted and receive more community attention and resources.
This dynamic can further suppress technological diversity of the community and even raise potential security risks and legal consequences
(
FTC 1914
;
Crandall and Hazlett 2023
)
.

To support a comprehensive study of AI coding proficiency, we develop a comprehensive evaluation methodology.
Based on traditional code quality literature and characteristics of LLM-generated code (i.e., code fragmentation and low context dependency), we design a multi-dimensional evaluation framework encompassing functional suitability, performance, maintainability, readability, and reliability.
We also construct an automated pipeline to build a comprehensive dataset containing 170 third-party libraries across 61 scenarios, where the overall AI coding proficiency is measured by the average score across all five dimensions.
Considering the code generation capability of LLMs
(
Twist et al. 2025
;
Qing et al. 2025
)
, this study focuses on Python code generation, and the technology involved is third-party libraries that can be used by Python code snippets.
Based on this framework and dataset, we conduct the study on six LLMs (i.e., GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Qwen3-Coder-Plus, and DeepSeek-R1), aiming to answer the following research questions (RQs).

\bullet

RQ1 (Proficiency Gap)
: How is the AI proficiency of different libraries on different LLMs?

We evaluate the AI proficiency scores of 170 libraries on six LLMs and compare ❶ the AI coding proficiency differences between competing libraries with similar functionality and ❷ the proficiency differences of the same library on different LLMs.
The findings show that the difference in AI coding proficiency between competing libraries and models is common, and over 10% of competing library pairs exhibit significant differences across different models.
In addition, the ‘winner’ of competing libraries usually varies across different models.
We call on developers to incorporate AI proficiency into the technology selection framework and evaluate candidate libraries and LLMs simultaneously to obtain an effective combination to improve development efficiency and quality.

\bullet

RQ2 (Failure Pattern)
: What typical failure patterns do LLMs exhibit when generating low-quality code snippets?

To gain a deeper understanding of the manifestations of low AI coding proficiency, we conduct an analysis of the code snippets marked with ‘low quality’ (using the interquartile range method) in RQ1.
We identify eight typical failure patterns, where ‘Incorrect Functionality’ and ‘Missing Edge Handling’ are the most common failure patterns, accounting for over 40% of all low-quality cases, directly endangering the safety and reliability of software development.

\bullet

RQ3 (Enhancement Strategy Exploration)
: Can existing prompting techniques improve the AI proficiency of libraries?

From the perspective of model users, we evaluate three prompt engineering techniques.
Experiment results show that the prompting methods can improve the overall code quality and AI coding proficiency scores and reduce the AI proficiency gaps between libraries, which provides a feasible solution for mitigating potential risks caused by the proficiency differences.

The contributions of this paper are shown as follows.

•

We define AI coding proficiency of technologies, providing a novel perspective for technology stack selection in the LLM era.

•

We construct a comprehensive evaluation methodology, covering a code quality assessment framework with five dimensions and a comprehensive dataset covering 170 libraries, providing a solid foundation for future AI code proficiency research.

•

We conduct the first empirical study to evaluate the AI coding proficiency of third-party libraries. Based on the six key findings from our study, we call on developers to consider the AI coding proficiency of different technologies on different models in technology selection, thereby achieving high-quality code snippets and efficient development.

2.
Background & Related Work

2.1.
LLM Code Generation

Large language models (LLMs), trained on large-scale code corpora, present new opportunities for software engineering (SE).
They have demonstrated strong potential in a number of challenging tasks, such as automatic patch generation
(
Ribeiro 2023
)
, code summarization
(
Chai and Li 2022
)
, and repository-level code generation
(
Zhang et al. [n. d.]
)
.
At present, the industry and academia have released a large number of LLMs, which are accelerating the production of developers around the world
(
Li et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2023a
)
.
To measure the code generation performance of these LLMs, researchers have proposed a series of benchmarks
(
Chen et al. 2021
;
Austin et al. 2021
)
and use metrics like pass@k to evaluate whether the generated functions can pass all unit tests.
Recent work has further constructed benchmarks to evaluate the performance efficiency of the generated code snippets
(
Huang et al. 2024
)
.
However, real-world software development requires additional quality considerations.
For example, LLM-generated code should be maintainable and reliable for collaborative development environments.
In this paper, we extend the perspective to a more comprehensive evaluation framework that assesses code quality across five dimensions, thereby describing how effectively an LLM can use a given technology in code generation.

2.2.
Technology Stack Selection

Fig. 1
.
Evolution of the Technology Stack Selection.

Technology stack selection has long been treated as a multi-criteria decision problem that balances factors such as functional suitability, cost, etc.
Architecture evaluation methods, such as the architecture tradeoff analysis method (ATAM), reason about trade-offs and risks across quality attributes (e.g., performance) to guide architectural choices
(
Kazman et al. 2000
;
Bass et al. 2021
;
Kazman et al. 2001
)
.
Multi-criteria decision-making methods (e.g., analytic hierarchy process) turn qualitative preferences into quantitative rankings of candidate technologies
(
Golden et al. 1989
;
Tzeng and Huang 2011
)
.
Standards-based quality models provide a vocabulary and structure for assessing maintainability, reliability, and other product qualities used for selection and governance
(
International Organization for Standardization 2011
)
.
Together, these methods support structured comparisons among competing technologies.

However, in the LLM era, software development has shifted from direct developer involvement to a collaborative effort between LLMs and developers (
Fig. 1
).
While traditional approaches comprehensively evaluate the intrinsic attributes of technologies, in the era of AI-assisted development, they overlook an important dimension, namely, whether the LLM can proficiently use the target technology to produce high-quality outputs.
To address this blind spot, we introduce a new complementary perspective for technology stack selection, namely
AI coding proficiency
, which evaluates whether a technology can be effectively used by LLMs to produce high-quality code snippets
(
Xia et al. 2019
;
Rojpaisarnkit et al. 2024
)
.
Our study aims to study and analyze AI coding proficiency across different technologies on different LLMs, establishing its significance as a critical new dimension for the technology selection framework in the LLM era.

2.3.
Code Quality Evaluation

Code quality is a multi-faceted concept that needs to be evaluated from multiple dimensions.
Based on the prior work, we review the evaluation methods of commonly used dimensions as follows
(
International Organization for Standardization 2011
)
.

Functional Suitability
represents the degree to which a system provides functions that fulfill the intended purpose and needs when used under specified conditions.
The related research on this dimension mainly evaluates the correctness of the generated code snippets.
For functional correctness, a common metric is pass@k, which quantifies the proportion of generated samples that successfully pass a predefined unit test suite
(
Chen et al. 2021
;
Austin et al. 2021
)
.
However, this approach requires the manual construction of multiple unit tests (often dozens of unit tests) for each task, and the evaluation results heavily depend on the quality and coverage of these tests, thereby limiting its scalability in large-scale automated evaluation scenarios.
Recently, with the advancement of LLMs’ reasoning and comprehension capabilities, researchers have begun to widely use the LLM as a judge to evaluate and score code snippets statically
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
;
Crupi et al. 2025
;
Xu et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Tong and Zhang 2024
)
.
By prompting LLMs with detailed rules or structured checklists, these methods enable the systematic assessment of multiple attributes of code snippets, including functional correctness, complexity, and maintainability.

Performance Efficiency
relates to the resources the code snippets consume during execution.
In traditional SE, it is typically evaluated through a range of profiling techniques, including load testing of complete software systems
(
Barna et al. 2011
;
Draheim et al. 2006
)
and algorithmic complexity analysis
(
Cormen et al. 2022
;
McGeoch 2012
)
.
However, these system-level or theoretical methods are often unsuitable for the automated evaluation of the short and isolated snippets generated by LLMs.
Researchers have evaluated the efficiency of code snippets by profiling the average execution time and peak memory usage
(
Huang et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Coignion et al. 2024
)
.
Recent work further explores the use of LLMs to analyze and estimate the time and space complexity of code snippets, thereby providing efficiency scores
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
;
Chambon et al. 2025
)
.

Maintainability
is the degree of effectiveness and efficiency with which a product can be modified.
This attribute is crucial for controlling software debugging and maintenance costs.
Traditional assessment combines code metrics with historical analysis (e.g., code churn) and inter-module dependency metrics
(
Hassan 2009
;
Nagappan and Ball 2005
;
Briand et al. 2002
)
.
For LLM code snippets that lack historical and inter-module context, the evaluation focuses on intrinsic code properties, including the Halstead volume, cyclomatic complexity, and maintainability index
(
Oman and Hagemeister 1992
;
McCabe 1976
;
Halstead 1977
;
Jin et al. 2023
)
.
These classic metrics analyze the linearly independent paths and vocabulary in the source code, thereby calculating and evaluating the complexity and maintainability of the code snippets.
Recently, researchers have also explored using LLMs to analyze and evaluate the maintainability of code snippets based on predefined checklists
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
.

Usability
measures the ease with which users can understand and apply the code to achieve their goals.
Traditional evaluation is mainly human-centric, relying on methods like developer surveys, cognitive complexity studies, and think-aloud protocols to assess a library’s or system’s learning curve and ease of use
(
Grossman et al. 2009
;
Piccioni et al. 2013
)
.
However, these methods are not scalable for automatically evaluating numerous code snippets.
Prior studies establish that readability is a key factor in a developer’s ability to understand and use code snippets
(
Letouzey and Ilkiewicz 2012
;
Rauf et al. 2019
)
.
Accordingly, existing research proposes code readability scores derived from linters or models to quantitatively evaluate the readability of generated code snippets
(
Posnett et al. 2011
)
.

Reliability
is the degree to which a system performs specified functions under specified conditions for a specified period.
Traditional methods involve fault injection testing, stress testing, and calculating metrics like mean time between failures
(
Natella et al. 2016
;
Cotroneo et al. 2020
)
.
These methods require a long-running, stateful application and are thus unsuitable for evaluating stateless code snippets generated by LLMs.
Recent studies have explored using a powerful LLM as a judge to score the reliability of code snippets based on a predefined set of rules, such as evaluating whether the code snippet implements necessary handling for edge cases
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
.

Based on the existing methods, in this study, we implement a series of metrics to assess the quality of code snippets generated by LLMs using different third-party libraries, thereby evaluating whether a library can be effectively used in the AI-assisted development.
More details are in
§4.2
.

3.
Motivation

Fig. 2
.
A Motivation Case on GPT-4o

3.1.
AI Coding Proficiency of Technology

LLMs, such as GPT-5 and Claude Sonnet 4, have demonstrated strong reasoning and comprehension capabilities that can help humans complete a wide range of tasks, such as software development and information retrieval.
As LLMs grow in influence across industry and daily life, people begin to pay attention to whether organizations and even the whole society are ‘ready’ for the AI-powered production and lives
(
Jöhnk et al. 2021
;
Tehrani et al. 2024
;
air 2024
)
.
Inspired by this concept, in this paper, we introduce the
AI coding proficiency of technology
as a new perspective for technology selection to answer the question ‘is a technology ready for AI-assisted development’.
Specifically, it evaluates whether the LLM can effectively utilize the given technology to produce high-quality code snippets.
This measurement provides a parallel, complementary dimension to traditional technology selection frameworks and quality models that focus on intrinsic attributes of one technology.

Definition 3.1 (AI coding proficiency of technology).

The AI coding proficiency of a technology refers to the degree to which a technology (e.g., a third-party library or service) can be effectively and skillfully utilized by a given AI model (e.g., LLMs) in software development processes.
Specifically, for technologies with high AI coding proficiency, an LLM can proficiently leverage their APIs and functions with minimal human intervention to produce high-quality code solutions that satisfy both functional requirements and critical non-functional attributes (e.g., maintainability and performance efficiency).
Higher AI coding proficiency translates to reduced integration and maintenance costs, lower technical debt, and more reliable outcomes in AI-assisted development workflows.

Note that in this study, we assess the AI proficiency of a technology by measuring the quality of code snippets generated by LLMs when they are asked to use the given technology in code generation.
A higher code quality score indicates a higher AI coding proficiency.

Without such guidance, selecting a library with low AI proficiency directly erodes the productivity gains of AI-assisted development.
It pulls developers away from an efficient generative workflow into a tedious fix-and-correct loop, driving up debugging, refactoring, and integration costs.
More seriously, it incurs technical debt and may even introduce potential security risks to development.
From a broader perspective, AI proficiency also has profound implications for the software community and ecosystem.
Developers naturally prefer to adopt higher-quality code snippets.
As a result, low-proficiency libraries will gradually be marginalized in the competitive landscape of the LLM era.
This AI-driven ‘Matthew effect’ could accelerate a winner-takes-all market dynamic, threatening healthy competition in the digital market and reducing the robustness and diversity of the entire software ecosystem.

To systematically study the AI proficiency of different technologies and explore its implications for software development and the ecosystem, we conduct an empirical study on six widely used LLMs, namely GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Qwen3-Coder-Plus, and DeepSeek-R1.
We construct a comprehensive dataset covering 61 task scenarios and 170 third-party libraries to evaluate the AI proficiency of different libraries.
From the experiments, we distill six key findings, aiming to provide new insights for software development and technology stack selection research in the LLM era.

4.
Evaluation Methodology

In this section, we introduce the evaluation methodology, including the dataset construction, experimental metric design, and implementation details.

4.1.
Dataset Construction

To explore and study the AI proficiency of different third-party libraries on various task scenarios, we design a pipeline to construct a comprehensive dataset through the following two stages,
scenario and library collection
and
prompt generation
.

\bullet

Scenario and Library Collection.

This stage begins with a collection of diverse software development task scenarios inspired by previous studies
(
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
.
We then collect competing third-party libraries related to each scenario.
Note that the ‘competing libraries’ here are defined as multiple libraries within the same scenario whose core functionalities highly overlap, enabling them to achieve equivalent goals under the same task description.
Our study focuses on Python code generation of LLMs, and all collected third-party libraries should be able to be used in Python (e.g., via Python adapters or bindings).

The analysis of collected libraries shows that most of the libraries are typically only applicable to specific tasks within a scenario, making it difficult for them to be considered as ‘competing libraries’.
Take the ‘Machine Learning’ scenario as an example.
The collected libraries contain
scikit-learn
,
TensorFlow
, and
PyTorch
.

Scikit-learn
is primarily used for traditional machine learning tasks (e.g., logistic regression, random forests), whereas
TensorFlow
and
PyTorch
are more commonly used for deep learning tasks (e.g., neural network training and inference).
Therefore,
scikit-learn
is not a direct competitor to
TensorFlow
and
PyTorch
.
In contrast, the functional overlap between
TensorFlow
and
PyTorch
is high, making them fit for the ‘competing libraries’.

To resolve this issue, we invite two experts with professional backgrounds in SE to further refine and restructure the scenarios based on the functional boundaries of the libraries, and to filter for ‘competing libraries’ within each refined scenario.
For any disagreements between the two experts on the reconstruction results, a third expert is invited to facilitate a discussion to reach a consensus.
Any refined scenario with fewer than two competing libraries will be discarded.
Finally, we collect 170 libraries across 61 refined scenarios.

\bullet

Prompt Generation.

We build an automated prompt generation pipeline to generate prompts for third-party libraries.
Based on prior work
(
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
, our prompt template is designed as follows:

Write a Python script that uses ‘
(
LIBRARY
)
‘ to complete the following coding task: ‘
(
DESCRIPTION
)
‘.

The
(
LIBRARY
)
indicates the name of the collected third-party library, and
(
DESCRIPTION
)
is a specific task description aligned with the scenario.
For example, in the ‘Data Visualization’ scenario,
(
DESCRIPTION
)
could be ‘
Create a line chart that plots the monthly sales data, displaying both the sales amount and the months on the appropriate axes
’.
To efficiently obtain task descriptions, the pipeline queries an LLM (i.e., GPT-4o) to generate five candidate descriptions for each scenario.
It then queries the model again to validate these candidates, ensuring that the given task can be accomplished by any competing library within this scenario.
For candidates that fail validation, the pipeline triggers regeneration until the retry budget is exhausted (up to three retries).
In total, this stage produces 850 prompts based on the collected third-party libraries.

4.2.
Evaluation Metrics

Based on the evaluation methods in
§2.3
, this study uses the following metrics to score the code snippets generated by LLMs on five commonly used quality dimensions.
All scores are normalized to the range of
[0,100]
, and a higher value indicates better quality on that dimension.

\bullet

Functional Suitability Score.
Following the prior work
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
;
Crupi et al. 2025
)
, we use an LLM to review the code snippets and score their functional correctness in conjunction with the task descriptions.

\bullet

Performance Score.
We use an LLM to estimate the time complexity and space complexity of the code snippets
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
and take their mean as the performance score, which reflects the performance of LLM-generated code snippets.

\bullet

Maintainability Score.
We calculate the widely used Maintainability Index (MI) as the maintainability score
(
Oman and Hagemeister 1992
)
.
This index is calculated by the Halstead volume, cyclomatic complexity, and the number of code lines.
A higher score indicates lower maintenance difficulty.

\bullet

Usability Score.
We adopt a readability evaluation model from prior work to score the readability of each snippet as the Usability Score
(
Posnett et al. 2011
)
.
This model uses features such as Halstead volume and the number of non-empty lines to reflect how easy the code is to read and use.

\bullet

Reliability Score.
Following prior work
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
, we use the LLM to assess the reliability of the given code snippets (e.g., whether exceptions and edge cases are handled appropriately).

Following the prior work
(
White et al. 2025
)
, we compute the arithmetic mean of the five dimension scores as the overall code quality score
S
, providing a more comprehensive view of the overall quality of the given code snippets.
We define the AI proficiency score
\mathcal{P}
of the library
l
as the average overall quality scores of code snippets generated by the model
m
across all prompts involving
l
in the scenario
s
, as shown follows:

\mathcal{P}=\frac{1}{\lvert P_{l,s}\rvert}\sum_{p\in P_{l,s}}S^{m}_{p},

where
P_{l,s}
indicate the set of prompts in a given scenario
s
that ask the LLM to generate code snippets with
l
, and
S^{m}_{p}
indicates the overal quality score of the code snippet produced by model
m
for prompt
p
.

5.
Experimental Results & Analysis

In this section, we report and analyze the experimental results to answer the following research questions (RQs):

5.3.
RQ2: Failure Pattern

To gain a deeper understanding of the manifestations of low AI proficiency and its profound impact on software development, we analyze the code snippets with low quality scores and construct a failure pattern taxonomy based on this analysis.
Specifically, we first rank all code snippets from small to large based on their code quality scores and then use the interquartile range method (threshold is
Q_{1}-1.5*IQR
)
(
Tukey et al. 1977
)
to identify the samples with the low quality scores among all 25,500 code snippets, resulting in a total of 814 low-scoring code snippets.
We then invite two SE experts to annotate the failure patterns in these code snippets.
Each expert has at least five years of Python development experience.
Before annotation, both experts receive unified training to familiarize themselves with the scoring methods for each code quality dimension in the
§4.2
.
During the annotation process, annotators can access the source code, the quality scores for each dimension, and the supporting reasons for the relevant quality scores from the LLM evaluator.
After the annotation, a third expert facilitates a discussion to solve any disagreements, ultimately reaching a consensus on the annotation results and developing a stable failure pattern taxonomy.
Note that a single code snippet can exhibit multiple patterns simultaneously, for example, the code snippet in the
Fig. 2
(b) contains both syntax errors and unused imports.
Therefore, we apply a multi-label annotation in this study.

\bullet

Taxonomy of Failure Patterns.

Based on the annotation results,
Table 2
summarizes eight categories of failure patterns (P1-P8), and we introduce them as follows:

P1: Syntax Error.

This category of pattern occurs when a generated code snippet is not executable due to syntax errors or incomplete snippets (e.g., mismatched indentation and truncated statements).
In
§3.2
(b), an extra ‘])’ appears on Line 34, causing the code snippet to fail syntax checking.
Such errors force developers to engage in iterations of locating, fixing, and rewriting, which not only prolongs debugging and integration processes but can also increase overall delivery costs and schedule risks.
In addition, frequent naive errors may force developers to abandon the use of specific models or libraries, potentially affecting the digital market and software ecology.

P2: Incorrect Library Usage.

This failure pattern occurs when a snippet contains the incorrect usage of a target library or its APIs, for example, invoking deprecated or non-existent functions, selecting APIs ill-suited to the input characteristics, or failing to call the target library as required.
Compared with syntax errors, such a misuse is more insidious.
It may lead to logical errors that are difficult to identify, posing security and reliability risks to software development.

P3: Incorrect Functionality.

In this pattern, the code snippet generated by the LLM may be executable, but it implements incorrect algorithms and fails to meet the core task requirements.
For instance, when asked to build a convolutional neural network in PyTorch, the model (e.g., Qwen3-Coder-Plus) may return a classifier with only fully connected layers and no convolutional layers, which is undoubtedly not enough for the task.
This pattern is the most common across six LLMs, reflecting the limitations of LLMs in performing complex tasks relevant to production scenarios to a certain extent.
Frequent functionality issues could force the developers to repeatedly rework and verify, erode trust in automated coding assistance, and increase testing and review overhead across the pipeline.

P4: Missing Edge Handling.

The code snippet omits checks or handlers for edge cases and inputs (e.g., empty inputs and extreme values), making it less reliable and likely to fail under certain conditions.
Such failures are often expensive to troubleshoot and difficult to expose in tests, posing a threat to the security and reliability of software development.

P5: Inefficient Implementation.

This pattern is related to the performance efficiency dimension.
It occurs when the code snippet contains inefficient implementations such as redundant and frequent I/O, nested loops, and exception checking.
This directly leads to higher latency and resource consumption, which degrades user experience and software scalability, accrues as performance debt, making later optimization riskier and more expensive.

P6: Missing Comments.

The code snippet provides insufficient documentation for complex functions, classes, or key steps.
Missing or minimal comments will increase cognitive load and misuse risk and slow code review, harming the long-term usability of the code snippet.

P7: Import Issues.

This failure pattern includes the situations where code snippets use APIs from specific libraries but do not import them, or import modules that are never used.
These issues can cause execution failures, pollute the environment, and cause dependency bloat, complicating software deployment and leading to potential issues in subsequent version control and dependency.

P8: Others.

This category includes various code quality problems, e.g., excessive demo inputs, which depress the scores in dimensions like maintainability.
Such issues can hinder human developers from understanding the code snippets generated by LLMs, slow down their learning speed, thereby reducing project maintainability and increasing development costs.

5.4.
RQ3: Prompting Techniques

To explore the impact of prompting techniques on AI proficiency of libraries, especially their effect on code quality gaps between competing libraries, we implement and evaluate three general prompting methods in this section.
❶
CoT
is the zero-shot variant of Chain-of-Thought prompting, which includes the phrase ‘Let’s think step by step’ in the system prompt
(
Kojima et al. 2022
)
.
It can encourage the model to think gradually and generate structured and detailed answers.
Prior work has demonstrated that CoT can improve performance on reasoning and generation tasks
(
Wei et al. 2022
;
Kojima et al. 2022
)
.
❷
Few-shot prompting
provides examples in the prompt and guides the model to generate high-quality answers.
Following the prior work
(
Xu et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2023b
;
PromptHub 2025
)
, for each prompt with a target library and a specific task, we provide a dedicated example containing a different task and a corresponding code snippet using the target library.
This example code snippet achieves an overall quality score of over 80.
❸
Regeneration
simulates the user workflow of ‘regenerating when dissatisfied’.
For each prompt, the model independently produces three candidate responses.
We then select the one with the highest overall quality score as the final output.

We evaluate the above three methods on six LLMs.
Due to resource constraints, we randomly select 20 pairs of prompts for each model.
Each pair contains two competing libraries under the same task.
10 pairs exhibit a significant quality gap between their original generated code snippets without the prompting methods, whereas the other 10 pairs do not (Cohen’s effect size).
Following the setup in
§5.1
, we repeatedly query each prompt five times on each model.

Table 4
shows the experimental results across six LLMs, where ‘Score’ represents the average overall code quality scores across LLMs, ‘Gap’ indicate the absolute difference in overall code quality scores between competing library pairs, and ‘Cons.’ represents the proportion of pairs whose leading relationship of code quality scores between two libraries remains unchanged after using different prompting methods.
‘Origin’ represents the results without using any prompting method.
‘Regen.’ is the abbreviation of the regeneration method.

\bullet

Analysis of Code Quality Score.

As shown in
Table 4
, prompt techniques can significantly improve the quality of code snippets generated by LLMs.
Specifically, ‘Few-shot’ and ‘Regeneration’ significantly improve the overall quality score (Wilcoxon signed-rank test,
p<0.05
), with the largest improvement reaching 5.38% (‘Few-shot’ on GPT-4o).
In addition, both methods significantly improve the scores on other quality dimensions such as functional suitability and Reliability.
In contrast, ‘CoT’ only provides slight gains on several models, and the improvement is small (the improved score is less than 1).
This may be because the advantage of ‘CoT’ primarily lies in ‘explicit reasoning’, while this study focuses more on quality dimensions such as the correctness and maintainability of implementation.
In such a situation, additional thought chains and reasoning may not improve the code quality but could lead to more complex implementations, increasing complexity and compromising maintainability scores, ultimately resulting in little improvement.
Developers in the LLM era should choose appropriate prompting techniques based on the actual requirements of the coding task to reach high AI proficiency and improve development productivity.

Finding 5:
‘Few-shot’ and ‘Regeneration’ significantly improve overall code quality on multiple LLMs, leading to better AI proficiency, while ‘CoT’ is less effective in improving code quality.

\bullet

Analysis of Competing Libraries.

We calculate the average code quality differences (absolute gaps) for competing library pairs, as shown in
Table 4
.
We can observe that both ‘CoT’ and ‘Few-shot’ methods significantly reduce the quality gap between libraries (
p<0.05
).
The ‘Few-shot’ method, in particular, reduces the average gap by up to 44.93% on Qwen3-Coder-Plus.
This provides a viable path to mitigate the potential ‘winner-takes-all’ problem (Matthew effect) caused by differences in AI proficiency.
It also suggests that in the LLM era, when selecting a technology stack, prompting techniques can be used as an auxiliary means to regulate the AI proficiency of target libraries.
Note that the impact of prompting techniques is conditional.
For competing libraries with significant code quality gaps without prompting, all three prompting methods are effective in narrowing the gap.
However, for pairs with a narrow gap, applying prompting methods can actually lead to a ‘lead reversal’ (where the score of library A is better initially, but library B achieves a higher score after prompting) and even widen the gap.
This phenomenon is most pronounced for the ‘Few-shot’ method.
The reason may be that the performance of few-shot prompts is highly dependent on the content and coverage of the selected examples.
When examples better align with a library’s API conventions or usage patterns, they can greatly improve the quality of code generated by LLMs using that library, thereby changing the relative advantages of two libraries.

Finding 6:
Prompt techniques such as ‘Few-shot’ and ‘CoT’ can narrow the AI proficiency gap between competing libraries.
However, for pairs with narrow gaps, prompting methods may lead to a shift in the leading relationship between the scores of libraries, or even widen the gap.

6.
Discussion

\bullet

AI Proficiency and Popularity.

Intuitively, the AI proficiency of one library should be related to its popularity.
More popular libraries have higher visibility in open-source communities, leading to more examples and documentation.
Therefore, they are more likely to be included in training corpora, enabling LLMs to more fully learn and effectively use the library.
To examine the correlation between AI proficiency and popularity, we collect the number of GitHub stars as the popularity signal for each library.
For each pair of competing libraries from
§5.2
, we use exact two-sided binomial tests to assess whether AI proficiency rankings align with popularity rankings. The concordance rate is 0.574 (95% confidence interval
[0.503,0.643]
), with significant results (
p<0.05
) but very small effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.15), indicating a statistically significant yet weak positive correlation between library popularity and AI proficiency.
Such observation further supports the viewpoint that popular libraries good for human users may not be suitable for LLM-driven workflows, further emphasizing the importance of incorporating AI proficiency into technology selection frameworks.
Furthermore, we observe that among 80 competing library pairs, the library with more GitHub stars actually receives lower AI proficiency scores.
For example, the data management and tracking library
DVC
has 2.12 times more GitHub stars than
Tensorboard
, but its AI proficiency score on GPT-4o is 6.46% lower than that of
Tensorboard
.
This divergence may stem from a variety of factors, including differences in the quality of use cases across different libraries in the open-source community, data filtering strategies used during LLM pre-training, and API iterations caused by rapid software updates.
In summary, technology selection solely from the developer’s perspective is insufficient in the context of AI-assisted development.
Incorporating AI proficiency as a new evaluation dimension helps teams assess whether LLMs can effectively use a given technology, avoiding debugging overhead and technical debt.

\bullet

Implications.

In the LLM era, software development processes are undergoing a transformation, shifting from a traditional process dominated solely by developers to a collaboration process between developers and LLMs. Our study has three practical implications.

❶
For Developers: Rethinking Technology Selection.

Our findings show that technologies that are easy for human developers to use may not be equally effective for LLMs and fail to produce high-quality code snippets.
This discrepancy reveals the limitations of existing technology selection methods, which focus solely on the intrinsic attributes of technologies (e.g., performance), ignoring whether LLMs can effectively leverage these technologies in code generation.
As the AI-assisted development becomes increasingly common, ignoring this discrepancy can lead to increased debugging costs and even the accumulation of unforeseen technical debt.
We urge developers to incorporate the new dimension of ‘AI coding proficiency’ into technology selection, thereby selecting appropriate technologies in the LLM era.

❷
For Software Community: Beware of Ecosystem Imbalance.

As LLMs are commonly used in development, the AI-proficient libraries may rapidly gain more users and resources, creating a ‘winner-takes-all’ situation.
This not only inhibits technological diversity but also potentially leads to legal consequences.
We recommend that software communities and maintainers acknowledge the ecological risks posed by disparities in AI proficiency and design mitigation mechanisms, for example, publishing high-quality few-shot examples for different libraries to help users achieve better LLM code generation results and narrow the AI proficiency gap with leading libraries.

❸
For Software Security: Beware of Emerging Risk Entrances.

While AI-assisted development improves efficiency, it also introduces new security risks.
On the one hand, the code generated by LLMs may lack critical logic (e.g., exception handling), allowing undetected vulnerabilities to infiltrate the system.
On the other hand, excessive reliance on a small number of libraries with high proficiency can amplify the potential harm of supply chain attacks (e.g., dependency injection or poisoning)
(
Pearce et al. 2022
;
Decan et al. 2018
)
.
Therefore, we call on incorporating AI coding proficiency into the governance and compliance assessment of AI systems, and the audit and control of LLM-generated content should be strengthened to ensure software security and supply chain resilience.

\bullet

Future Directions.

Future research can be carried out in the following directions:

❶
Comprehensive benchmark for AI proficiency.

Although this paper defines AI proficiency and reveals the proficiency gaps between competing libraries on six popular LLMs, our study is limited to the Python programming language and third-party libraries.
Future research could develop a larger-scale, cross-platform, and cross-language (e.g., C++) benchmark encompassing more technology types, including platforms and systems.
It should also incorporate complex tasks requiring the collaboration of multiple technologies to better approximate real production environments.
Furthermore, the benchmark could incorporate additional quality dimensions (e.g., cross-platform portability) to better assist developers in AI-assisted technology selection.

❷
New technology selection framework.

In addition to AI proficiency, the AI-assisted development raises other new dimensions of technology stack selection that need to be explored, for example, AI efficiency (how long and how many tokens it takes for the model to generate code snippets that meet requirements) and agent proficiency (whether a tool is ready for efficient invocation from the agentic AI).
Future research could extend existing methodologies (e.g., ATAM) to develop a comprehensive technology stack selection framework tailored to the LLM era.
This framework would integrate new dimensions, including AI proficiency, with traditional attributes (e.g., performance), offering a multidimensional approach with practical value for efficient software development.

❸
Enhancement strategy.

Another valuable future direction is to design methods to improve the AI proficiency of target libraries on a given model.
There are two potential approaches.
On the model side, model developers can augment training data and construct comparative fine-tuning to mitigate the AI proficiency differences between competing libraries.
On the library side, library maintainers can provide high-quality use cases and documentation to the open-source community and improve the interfaces and corresponding annotations to make the library-related data easier to learn and use for LLMs.
The coordinated implementation of these two approaches has the potential to narrow the AI proficiency gap between libraries, mitigate risks posed to the software ecosystem, and improve developer experience and delivery quality.

7.
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a new perspective for technology stack selection in the LLM era,
AI coding proficiency
, which evaluates whether LLMs can correctly utilize the given technology in code generation, thereby producing high-quality code snippets.
We conduct the first empirical study on the AI proficiency of third-party libraries across six LLMs.
Our findings reveal that competing libraries with similar functionality can exhibit significant gaps in AI proficiency on the same model, and the performance of different models using the same library can also vary.
Low AI proficiency not only undermines the efficiency and effectiveness of AI-assisted development, resulting in additional time and financial costs, but can also drive users toward high-proficiency libraries and exacerbate market concentration, ultimately harming technological diversity and security of the software ecosystem.
We call on the community to take heed of and incorporate AI proficiency into the technology selection framework and take practical improvement measures to ensure development efficiency and delivery quality, and maintain an open and diverse software ecosystem.
