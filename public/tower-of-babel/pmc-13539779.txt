Effects of erector spinae plane block on postoperative pain in patients undergoing implant-based breast reconstruction for breast cancer: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Dandan Ling, Shiyou Wei, Qianping He, Yulin Li, Qianyun Xu, Wei Jiao, Jun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImplant-based breast reconstruction after mastectomy causes acute pain.ObjectiveTo determine whether a single-shot T5 erector spinae plane block (ESPB) reduces postoperative pain.DesignSingle-center, RCT with allocation concealment; blinded assessors and statisticians.SettingTertiary cancer center in China.Patients100 adults scheduled for radical mastectomy with implant reconstruction were randomized (1:1); follow-up complete.InterventionBefore induction, ESPB was given under ultrasound guidance at T5 with 30 mL of 0.375% ropivacaine plus dexmedetomidine 1 μg/kg; controls received no block. Standardized general anesthesia and postoperative PCA for both groups.Main outcome measuresResting NRS at 6 h (MCID=1). Secondary outcomes were opioid consumption, quality of recovery, and PONV.ResultsESPB did not significantly reduce resting pain at 6 h at the median (τ =0.50; adjusted difference −0.9; p = 0.08). At the upper tail, pain intensity was lower (τ = 0.75; −1.8; p <0.01). Repeated measures provided additional time-point information, improving estimation precision and test sensitivity. ESPB get lower pain scores at 6, 12, and 24 hours (all p <0.01). But, the 95% CI includes the MCID, the clinical benefit remains uncertain. Opioid use decreased at 24 h (−13.5 mg; p <0.01) and 48 h (−6.6 mg; p <0.01). Quality of recovery improved at 24 h (difference 5 points; p <0.01), but not later. No differences were observed in intraoperative hemodynamics or PONV.ConclusionsSingle-shot T5 ESPB with perineural dexmedetomidine may reduce postoperative pain and opioid requirements and improve early recovery. Further large trials are warranted. Clinical relevance remains to be confirmed.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT06143020.

Introduction

Breast cancer has surpassed lung cancer to become the most common malignancy [1]. Surgery is the preferred treatment for the vast majority of patients with early- to mid-stage breast cancer. With increasing public awareness of cancer prevention and the widespread adoption of screening, the rate of early diagnosis has risen, the patient population is trending younger, and expectations regarding the aesthetic appearance and contour of the breast after curative surgery have increased [2]. The goals of surgical management of breast cancer have evolved from simple radical tumor extirpation, through repair of local defects and restoration of breast contour, to breast reconstruction that balances aesthetics and function [3,4].

Implant-based reconstruction is one of the primary approaches to breast reconstruction. The implant is often placed in the subpectoral plane to ensure adequate soft-tissue coverage. The silicone prostheses used have no direct association with human disease, are noncarcinogenic, and do not increase the risk of developing breast cancer [5].

Studies on patients undergoing breast cancer surgery have shown that about one-quarter suffer from moderate to severe postoperative pain [6]. Acute postoperative pain not only prolongs the patient’s stay in the recovery room but also impairs respiratory and immune function. In addition, it increases the risk of complications such as myocardial infarction in high-risk patients, leading to longer hospital stays [7,8]. In nearly half of these patients, acute postoperative pain is also a major contributing factor to the development of chronic persistent postoperative pain [6].

In implant-based breast reconstruction following radical mastectomy, prostheses or tissue expanders are often placed in the subpectoral plane, altering the normal anatomy of the pectoralis major and increasing muscular tension. Clinically, postoperative pain in these patients is often more severe than that observed after modified radical mastectomy [9,10]. Therefore, effective postoperative analgesia is crucial for patients undergoing breast cancer surgery [11]. In recent years, it has been widely recognized that, compared with traditional analgesic methods, regional blockade techniques significantly improve postoperative pain control in patients undergoing breast cancer surgery.

In 2016, Forero et al. [12] first introduced the Erector Spinae Plane Block (ESPB), a novel fascial plane block technique that can be applied for postoperative analgesia in thoracic and lumbar surgeries. It offers several advantages, including simplicity, safety, reliable analgesic efficacy, and a low incidence of complications, which have attracted widespread attention. In this technique, local anesthetic is injected into the middle layer of the thoracolumbar fascia—specifically, between the deep surface of the erector spinae muscle and the transverse processes. The local anesthetic then spreads along the path of least resistance within this potential fascial plane into the thoracic paravertebral space, thereby blocking the dorsal rami, ventral rami, and rami communicantes of the spinal nerves to achieve regional analgesia [13].

Although the application of the ESPB technique in breast cancer surgery has been reported, its use in implant-based breast reconstruction—where postoperative pain is typically more intense—has been rarely reported. This study hypothesized that ESPB could provide superior postoperative analgesia.

Methods

This trial was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (Approval No. 2109243-6, October 25, 2021; Chair: Zhen Chen) and was registered on November 19, 2023 (Trial ID: NCT06143020; Principal Investigator: Jun Zhang). Recruitment of the first participant began on November 20, 2023. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was conducted in accordance with the relevant principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This report was prepared in accordance with the CONSORT statement [14].

Routine anesthesia monitoring was applied throughout the trial. Both groups received flurbiprofen axetil (50 mg) and granisetron (3 mg) 30 min before the end of surgery.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were: patients scheduled for radical mastectomy and implant-based breast reconstruction surgery; American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–II; and age between 18 and 70 years.

The exclusion criteria included: coagulation disorders, known allergy to study drugs, obesity (BMI > 35 kg/m2), infection at the injection site, chronic opioid consumption (opioid use exceeding 2 weeks), inability to use a PCA device, or refusal to participate.

Randomization and blinding

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio into the ESPB group or the control group using block randomization without stratification. Group allocation was concealed in sealed, opaque envelopes, which were opened by the anesthesiologist immediately before anesthesia induction. Outcome assessors and statisticians were blinded to group assignments and treatment protocols.

Regional anesthesia protocol

Upon entering the operating room, patients remained awake while an experienced anesthesiologist performed the nerve block. Local anesthetic solutions were prepared by a designated anesthetic nurse. In the ESPB group, dexmedetomidine (100 μg/mL; Hengrui, China) was mixed at a dosage of 1 μg/kg with 0.375% ropivacaine (AstraZeneca, China) to make a 30-mL solution. No nerve block was performed in the control group.

At the end of surgery, both groups received an intravenous PCA pump containing sufentanil (0.75 μg/mL) with no background infusion, a locking interval of 10 min, and a bolus dose of 2 mL. Rescue analgesia with flurbiprofen axetil (50 mg, intravenous injection) was administered as needed.

ESPB procedure

The T5 level was selected as the block level in this study based on the spread property of single-shot ESPB, which can cover 2–6 vertebral levels [15], and the fact that T4–T5 is the clinically recommended level for ESPB in breast surgery [16]. All patients received an injection of 30 mL 0.375% ropivacaine; this dose has been confirmed to achieve complete sensory blockade from T1 to T6 in 92% of patients [17], and thus can reliably cover the T2–T6 analgesic range required for this surgical procedure.

With the patient in the lateral decubitus position, the operator stood on the dorsal side. Using an in-plane technique, a high-frequency linear ultrasound probe (6–13 MHz, Sonosite M-Turbo, USA) was placed approximately 3–5 cm lateral to the T5 spinous process. The probe was rotated to identify the erector spinae muscle and locate the transverse process. The needle was inserted in a caudal-to-cranial direction until the plane between the deep surface of the erector spinae muscle and the transverse process was reached. After confirming the plane with a hydrodissection test, 30 mL of the mixed local anesthetic solution was injected, resulting in the spread of a spindle-shaped hypoechoic area in the fascial plane. The block was verified by the anesthetic quality control physician on duty. The presence of a spindle-shaped liquid hypoechoic zone between the deep surface of the erector spinae muscle and the transverse process at the T5 level was regarded as a successful block; therefore, no further delineation of the spread range was required.

General anesthesia and perioperative management

Subsequently, general anesthesia was induced using intravenous propofol with a target effect-site concentration of 2–3 μg/mL, combined with sufentanil (0.3 μg/kg), midazolam (0.05 mg/kg), and rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg). After muscle relaxation was achieved, tracheal intubation was performed. Mechanical ventilation was set with a tidal volume of 6 ml/kg and a respiratory rate of 10–12 breaths per minute, maintaining an end-tidal carbon dioxide pressure of 35–40 mmHg. Anesthesia was maintained with propofol and remifentanil.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the resting pain Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) score at 6 h postoperatively. Pain was assessed using an 11-point NRS ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst imaginable pain). A difference of 1 point on the NRS was considered clinically significant [18].

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes included resting and movement-evoked pain scores at 1, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h postoperatively. Movement state was defined as the patient performing the maximal possible range of motion of the ipsilateral upper limb. Intraoperative cumulative opioid consumption was converted to intravenous sufentanil equivalents. Postoperative cumulative opioid consumption was converted to intravenous morphine milligram equivalents (MME) and evaluated at 24 and 48 h after surgery.

The conversion equivalence was defined as: 1 μg sufentanil = 10 μg remifentanil = 0.5 mg morphine. A between-group mean difference of 10 mg MME was considered clinically meaningful.

The quality of recovery at 24, 48, and 72 h postoperatively was assessed using the 15-item Quality of Recovery scale (QoR-15). The QoR-15 is a validated patient-reported outcome measure for evaluating early postoperative recovery across five dimensions: emotional state, physical comfort, psychological support, physical independence, and pain. Each item is rated from 0 to 10, yielding a total score from 0 to 150, with higher scores indicating better recovery. The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the QoR-15 is 6 points.

Other outcomes

Adverse intraoperative hemodynamic events were recorded, including the number of patients experiencing hypotension or hypertension, defined as systolic blood pressure < 85 mmHg and > 160 mmHg, respectively. The incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) was also documented as the number of patients who experienced nausea or vomiting after surgery.

Sample size calculation

The primary outcome of this study was the resting pain NRS score at 6 h postoperatively. In a preliminary experiment involving 15 patients in the control group, the mean resting NRS score was 3.7 ± 1.47. It was expected that the ESPB would reduce the pain score by 1 point. Using PASS 15 software (NCSS, USA) and the Two-Sample T-Tests Assuming Equal Variance method, with parameters set at α = 0.05, power = 90%, and a dropout rate of 5%, the required sample size was calculated to be 50 patients per group, for a total of 100 participants.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.3). Continuous variables were tested for normality with the Shapiro–Wilk test and are presented as mean ± SD for normally distributed data or median (Q1, Q3) for non‑normal data; categorical variables are presented as n (%). Multivariable models adjusted for prosthesis position, prosthesis style, axillary procedure, synthetic mesh use, ASA class, duration of surgery, age, BMI and number of drainage tubes. The primary outcome (rest NRS at 6 h) was analyzed by quantile regression (τ = 0.50) as the main analysis, with τ = 0.25 and τ = 0.75 as sensitivity analyses; standard errors and 95% CIs were estimated by paired bootstrap (R = 2000). Repeated‑measures outcomes (postoperative NRS and QoR‑15) were analyzed using linear mixed‑effects models with fixed effects for Group, Time and Group × Time and a random intercept for subject. The Group × Time interaction was tested by Type‑III Wald F (omnibus); between‑group comparisons at each time point are reported as adjusted marginal mean differences with Holm correction for multiple comparisons. Postoperative cumulative opioid consumption (MME) at 24 h and 48 h was compared using multivariable linear regression (ANCOVA framework) with OLS standard errors as the primary analysis and HC3 heteroscedasticity‑consistent standard errors for sensitivity. PONV was analyzed with Poisson regression using cluster‑robust (sandwich) standard errors and a log link, reporting adjusted risk ratios (aRR). All tests were two‑sided and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

All patients completed the treatment and were included in the final analysis. The baseline characteristics of the two groups were comparable (Table 1). This pattern suggests that ESPB may provide greater analgesic benefit among participants with higher pain intensity rather than shifting the overall median at 6 h.

Discussion

This RCT investigated the analgesic efficacy of a single-shot ultrasound-guided T5-level ESPB combined with perineural dexmedetomidine in patients undergoing implant-based breast reconstruction after mastectomy during the perioperative period. After adjusting for surgery-related confounding factors and analyzing repeated-measurement outcomes using a linear mixed-effects model, our findings showed that ESPB did not significantly reduce the median resting NRS pain score at 6 h in the primary quantile regression analysis (τ = 0.50). However, sensitivity analysis demonstrated statistical significance at τ = 0.75, suggesting that the analgesic effect may be more evident in participants with higher pain intensity. Consistent with this distributional pattern, the repeated-measures analysis indicated a significant Group × Time interaction and demonstrated significantly lower pain scores at 6, 12, and 24 h, along with reduced opioid consumption at 24 h. Overall, these results support a possible short-term analgesic benefit of ESPB-dexmedetomidine, while the clinical importance at a single prespecified time point remains uncertain.

Our findings are consistent with prior clinical trials evaluating the application of ESPB in breast surgery, and provide important supplementary evidence for implant-based breast reconstruction—a surgical population characterized by higher postoperative pain burden. Lin et al. [20] conducted a RCT and reported that ESPB not only reduced the incidence of chronic pain at 3 months following breast cancer surgery, but also significantly mitigated acute postoperative pain. Consistent with the temporal pattern of their acute-phase findings, the present study confirms the short-term possible analgesic benefit of the ESPB–dexmedetomidine protocol, and further reveals through quantile regression that the ESPB–dexmedetomidine protocol demonstrates more pronounced analgesic efficacy in patients with higher pain severity, a finding that may guide clinical individualized application. Raft et al. [21] reported in a multicenter RCT that for major breast oncological procedures, the analgesic efficacy of ESPB was comparable to paravertebral block, with a lower risk of surgery-related complications. Our findings align with their conclusions regarding the value of ESPB in acute pain management following breast cancer surgery, and further delineate the analgesic time window of single-shot ESPB–dexmedetomidine in implant-based reconstruction—specifically, the possible benefit is concentrated within 24 h postoperatively, a finding that may inform clinical decisions regarding blockade timing and intervention selection. Park et al. [22] previously demonstrated that ESPB reduced both postoperative pain scores at 24 h and opioid consumption in patients undergoing immediate tissue expander breast reconstruction. By contrast, the present study employed a more rigorous statistical approach—a linear mixed-effects model optimized for repeated-measurement data with adjustment for reconstruction-related confounding factors. The confidence interval of the adjusted effect size included both clinically meaningful and clinically trivial effects; therefore, a clinically important benefit of the ESPB-dexmedetomidine protocol cannot be excluded but remains uncertain. These findings permit more rigorous conclusions regarding ESPB–dexmedetomidine protocol’s perioperative role in this surgical population.

It should be noted that the intervention evaluated in this trial combined local anesthetic block with dexmedetomidine; therefore, the observed effects should be interpreted as attributable to this combined protocol rather than ESPB alone.

The mechanisms underlying ESPB remain controversial. Some evidence suggests that its efficacy is related to local anesthetic spread into the paravertebral space, whereas others argue that it primarily functions as a fascial plane block [23]. Anatomical studies by Gadsden et al. demonstrated [24] that ventral local anesthetic spread following ESPB is inconsistent. A subsequent randomized three-dimensional reconstruction imaging study corroborated this finding [25], indicating substantial inter-individual variability in the rate of local anesthetic spread from ESPB to the paravertebral space. Nevertheless, numerous clinical trials have demonstrated that ESPB provides safe and effective analgesia following breast surgery [16,23]. Our study similarly confirms that the possible analgesic benefit of ESPB–dexmedetomidine protocol is primarily concentrated within 24 h postoperatively, after which it gradually diminishes. The potential mechanisms underlying this time-limited benefit may include: (1) local anesthetic spread along the fascial plane, partially blocking the anterior and lateral cutaneous branches of the intercostal nerves, thereby effectively reducing incision-related resting pain and chest wall retraction-related movement pain [12]; (2) dexmedetomidine as an adjuvant exerts synergistic analgesic effects by prolonging blockade duration through peripheral mechanisms and producing additional central analgesic effects, while simultaneously reducing total opioid exposure to minimize opioid-induced hyperalgesia [26]; and (3) attenuating intraoperative nociceptive input signals, thereby reducing the incidence of central sensitization and mitigating the overall acute postoperative pain burden [27,28].

Although QoR-15 scores were higher in the ESPB group at 24 h postoperatively, the CI encompassed both clinically meaningful and clinically trivial effects; therefore, a clinically important benefit cannot be excluded but remains uncertain, and length of stay did not differ between groups. These findings suggest that the possible analgesic benefit of ESPB–dexmedetomidine protocol may not effectively translate into improved postoperative recovery outcomes. By contrast, Ayyala et al. reported that ESPB shortened length of stay in patients undergoing autologous breast reconstruction [29]. In the present study, the ESPB group demonstrated a 0.5-day earlier discharge compared to the control group; however, this modest difference is limited by the current sample size, and no meaningful conclusions can be drawn.

No significant difference in the incidence of PONV was observed between groups during the follow-up period. While prior evidence suggests that reduced opioid consumption correlates with lower PONV incidence [30,31], the between-group differences in both intraoperative and postoperative opioid consumption in our trial did not attain clinical significance. Furthermore, the prophylactic administration of antiemetic agents in both groups likely contributed to the attenuation of any potential between-group differences in PONV outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was powered to detect a 1-point difference in the primary outcome and was therefore insufficient to yield confidence intervals narrow enough to exclude the MCID for several secondary outcomes; interpreting results against the MCID, rather than by statistical significance alone, is more rigorous but leaves clinical importance uncertain. Second, the control group did not undergo sham blockade. Although outcome assessors and statisticians were blinded to group allocation, true patient blinding could not be achieved, which may introduce expectation bias—a major limitation inherent to pain studies with subjective outcomes. Third, sensory blockade distribution was not formally assessed following block completion; therefore, the functional extent of the nerve block was not objectively confirmed, which limited our ability to further investigate the anatomical basis underlying the heterogeneity of ESPB–dexmedetomidine protocol’s analgesic effects. Fourth, this trial evaluated only a single block regimen (30 mL of 0.375% ropivacaine with 1 μg/kg dexmedetomidine injected at the T5 level); therefore, the results cannot be extrapolated to other injection levels, different local anesthetic doses or concentrations, or regimens without dexmedetomidine.

Conclusion

In patients undergoing implant-based breast reconstruction after mastectomy, a single-shot T5-level ESPB combined with perineural dexmedetomidine demonstrates a possible early analgesic benefit in reducing postoperative pain scores, decreasing opioid consumption, and improving quality of recovery. Further validation with large-scale studies is warranted.
