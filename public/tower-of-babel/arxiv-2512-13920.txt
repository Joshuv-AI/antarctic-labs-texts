DAMA : A Unified Accelerated Approach for Decentralized Nonconvex Minimax Optimization—Part I: Algorithm Development and Results
Haoyuan Cai, Sulaiman A. Alghunaim, Ali H. Sayed

Abstract
In this work and its accompanying Part II [ 7 ] , we develop an accelerated algorithmic framework, DAMA ( D ecentralized A ccelerated M inimax A pproach), for nonconvex Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) minimax optimization over decentralized multi-agent networks. Our approach seamlessly integrates online and offline stochastic minimax algorithms with various decentralized learning strategies, yielding a versatile and unified framework that offers greater flexibility than existing methods. Our unification is threefold: i) We propose a unified decentralized learning strategy for minimax optimization over multi-agent networks that subsumes existing bias-correction methods (e.g., gradient tracking) and introduces new variants that achieve tighter network-dependent bounds, ii) we introduce a new probabilistic gradient estimator, termed GRACE ( GR adient AC celeration E stimator), which unifies the implementation of several momentum-based methods (e.g., STORM) and loopless variance-reduction techniques (e.g., PAGE, Loopless SARAH) for constructing accelerated gradients within DAMA . Moreover, GRACE is broadly applicable to a wide range of stochastic optimization problems, making it of independent interest, and iii) through a unified analytical framework, rather than case-by-case arguments, we establish a general performance bound for the proposed approach, achieving new state-of-the-art results with the best-known sample complexity in this setting. To the best of our knowledge, DAMA is the first framework to achieve a multi-level unification of decentralized learning strategies and accelerated gradient techniques, thereby addressing a key gap in the literature. In Part I of this work, we focus on the algorithm development of DAMA and the presentation of the main results. In its accompanying Part II [ 7 ] , we develop the theoretical analysis for DAMA to substantiate the main results. Moreover, we validate the effectiveness of DAMA and its variants across different network topologies using both synthetic and real-world datasets.

Introduction

Minimax optimization underpins a wide range of applications, including generative adversarial networks (GANs)
[
16
]
, reinforcement learning
[
25
]
, domain adaptation
[
1
]
, and distributionally robust optimization
[
46
]
.
At the same time, the ever-increasing scale of modern artificial intelligence (AI) tasks necessitates distributing data and computation across many agents (e.g., GPU clusters), highlighting the need for efficient decentralized minimax learning algorithms with provable convergence guarantees.
Motivated by this fact, we aim to solve nonconvex-nonconcave minimax optimization problems by utilizing the collective power of
K\geq 1
cooperating agents, which are connected by a network topology.
In particular, we consider the following distributed stochastic minimax problem:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}J(x,y)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}J_{k}(x,y),

(1a)

\displaystyle\text{where }J_{k}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\zeta}_{k}}[Q_{k}(x,y;\bm{\zeta}_{k})]&\textbf{(Online)}\\
\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{s=1}^{N_{k}}Q_{k}(x,y;\bm{\zeta}_{k}(s))&\textbf{(Offline)}\end{cases},

(1b)

where each agent
k
seeks an optimal solution through local interactions with its immediate neighbors under a decentralized network topology. Here, the global risk (cost) function
J(x,y)
is defined as the average of
K
local objectives
J_{k}(x,y)
, each privately owned by an agent
k
. We assume that
J(x,y)
is smooth and nonconvex in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}

but

possibly
nonconcave in
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
while satisfying the
Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL) condition
(see
Assumption 1
or reference
[
23
]
).
The focus of this work is on two important learning scenarios, where each agent
k
can draw independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples:
\bm{\zeta}_{k}
in the online streaming setting or
\bm{\zeta}_{k}(s)
in the offline finite-sum (or empirical) setting, with
\bm{\zeta}_{k}(s)
denoting the
s
-th local sample of agent
k
. These samples are used to evaluate the local instantaneous loss
Q_{k}(x,y;\bm{\zeta}_{k})
or
Q_{k}(x,y;\bm{\zeta}_{k}(s))
. Unlike prior works, we address
both
decentralized
online
stochastic and decentralized
offline
finite-sum scenarios. The former models online streaming regimes, while the latter fits many practical deep learning tasks.

Recent years have witnessed considerable progress in the algorithmic and theoretical development of minimax optimization
[

,
61
,
57
,
22
,
33
]
.
Given that general minimax problems are notoriously difficult
[
12
,
18
]
, prior works have primarily focused on a subclass of (
1a
)—(
1b
),
which impose structural assumptions on the risk function.
In particular, nonconvex–(strongly) concave minimax optimization has received significant attention

[
28
,
33
,
27
,
61
]
, since its nonconvex formulation is well-suited for nonlinear models such as neural networks that are essential for complex learning tasks.
In this work, however, we adopt a nonconvex–PL formulation that relaxes the strong concavity requirement on the
y
-variable, with the PL condition serving as a more suitable assumption for over-parameterized neural networks
[
29
]
.
Hence, our assumptions can more accurately represent important scenarios, such as cases where

and

parameterize different neural networks and one is over-parameterized.
Under a nonconvex-PL formulation, vanilla gradient descent–ascent (GDA) with a shared step size for both optimization variables tends to diverge since a uniform step size produces unbalanced dynamics under the asymmetric nonconvex–PL structure of
J(x,y)
; see the diverging example in
[
56
]
.
To address this issue, prior works adopted a two-time-scale step-size policy to calibrate the learning dynamics, where the step-size ratio is strictly constrained by polynomial orders of the condition number
\kappa\triangleq L_{f}/\nu
, where
L_{f}
is the smoothness constant of the cost and
\nu
is the strong concavity or PL constant.
A further challenge in stochastic nonconvex-PL minimax optimization is that the loss gradients of both variables are often computed from the same sample, resulting in correlated noise that breaks the martingale difference property.
This limitation may render the use of large batch sizes necessary in the stochastic GDA
[
28
,
8
]
, unless gradient noise is controlled through noise-reduction techniques
[
5
,
50
,
9
,
43
]
or alternative sampling schemes
[
57
,
8
]
.
Notably, several acceleration techniques have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing gradient noise while enhancing convergence speed
[
33
,
5
,
19
,
20
,
63
,
9
,
14
]
.
However,

the majority of existing acceleration techniques are often implemented and analyzed independently,

leaving the field without a unified gradient estimation strategy. This limitation motivates us to ask:

Q1: Can we design a unified gradient estimator that enables fast and efficient learning for stochastic minimax optimization?

We answer this question by proposing a new probabilistic gradient estimator, which unifies several key acceleration techniques, see
subsection II-B
for a detailed discussion.
We further extend this strategy to the multi-agent setting, which brings additional challenges. In particular, sparse communication topologies and data heterogeneity across the network complicate algorithmic design and can markedly impair learning performance.
To mitigate the data heterogeneity issue, bias-correction strategies such as gradient tracking (GT) can be employed
[
53
,
37
]
.
Actually,

existing minimax works rely heavily on the GT strategy to combat the imbalanced data challenge

[
50
,
19
,
15
,
14
,
63
,
6
]
. Nevertheless, this GT scheme is not guaranteed to achieve the state-of-the-art performance under a sparsely connected network
[
3
]
.
Alternative strategies, including Exact Diffusion (ED)
[
59
,
60
]
and EXTRA
[
45
]
, have been shown to achieve better rates compared with GT for minimization problems
[
3
,
42
]
. Yet these alternative methods remain underexplored in the minimax setting, and it is uncertain whether their advantages carry over to the minimax optimization domain. This limitation prompts our second question:

Q2:
Can we develop a generic approach that integrates the unified probabilistic gradient estimator with bias-correction decentralized learning strategies (e.g., ED and EXTRA) to achieve enhanced robustness against sparse communication networks and heterogeneous local data?

To this end, we present
DAMA
—a novel
D
ecentralized
A
ccelerated
M
inimax
A
pproach that seamlessly integrates our unified gradient estimator with mainstream decentralized learning strategies into a single, versatile framework.
In this way, the widely adopted GT method in
[

,

,
15
,
14
,
63
,
6
]

can be viewed as a special instance of

[

,
50
,
14
,
30
]
only established sample complexities that depend on the network spectral gap

in a weaker manner than the best-known results in the minimization setting

52

3

, where

denotes the mixing rate of the network (e.g., 0 for fully connected network and approaches 1 for sparse networks). Since the network spectral gap is extrinsic to the problem itself, this gap in performance bound suggests that sharper results should be attainable. These insights motivate our final question:

Q3: Is it possible to develop a unified analysis of DAMA with improved convergence guarantees?

We answer this question in the affirmative by establishing rigorous convergence guarantees for the proposed framework. Overcoming these challenges is far from trivial, as they do not combine in a simple additive manner; instead, our analysis requires delicate, nontrivial techniques. In particular, we develop a transformed recursion and carry out the analysis in the transformed domain rather than directly relying on the original one (see

We address Q1 and Q2 in Part I of this work and lay the groundwork for a unified theoretical analysis that will answer Q3 in Part II

II
Algorithm development

In this section, we present the essential algorithmic components for the unified approach. The content is organized into two parts: 1) in subsection
II-A
, we present a unified view of decentralized learning strategies by casting decentralized minimax optimization as a constrained two-level primal-dual optimization problem, which is then addressed through an iterative primal–dual approach, and 2) secondly, in subsection
II-B
, we revisit several prominent variance-reducing techniques in stochastic optimization and introduce a new probabilistic gradient estimator to unify them.

II-A

Unified decentralized learning framework

Our argument builds on prior works in minimization
[
3
,
42
,
2
]
, which provide the foundation for extending decentralized learning design to the minimax setting.
For convenience, we introduce the following network-augmented block vector quantities:

\displaystyle\mx=\mbox{\rm col}\{x_{1},\dots,x_{K}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{1}},\quad\my=\mbox{\rm col}\{y_{1},\dots,y_{K}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{2}}.

(2)

Our goal is to solve (
1a
) in a decentralized manner with

K
cooperating agents, where each agent has access only to its local data and communicates
only with its neighbors over
a partially connected network. A fundamental requirement in many multi-agent systems is that all participating agents reach a consensus. That being said, we can rewrite (
1a
) as the following equivalent constrained minimax problem

\displaystyle\min_{\mx\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{1}}}\max_{\my\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{2}}}\mathcal{J}(\mx,\my),\ {\rm s.t.}\begin{cases}x_{1}=\cdots=x_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\\
y_{1}=\cdots=y_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\end{cases},

(3)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\mx,\my)\triangleq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}J_{k}(x_{k},y_{k}).

(4)

The same problem can also be written in the form of a constrained two-player game
[
4
]
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\min_{\mx\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{1}}}\mathcal{J}(\mx,\my)\\
\displaystyle\min_{\my\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{2}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}-}\mathcal{J}(\mx,\my)\end{cases}\quad\operatorname{s.t.}\begin{cases}x_{1}=\cdots=x_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\\
y_{1}=\cdots=y_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\end{cases}.

(5)

Problem (
5
) seeks a stationary pair
(x^{\star},y^{\star})
while respecting the consensus constraint.
To unify several decentralized strategies for solving (
5
),
we start by introducing auxiliary block matrices

\mathcal{B}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{1}\times Kd_{1}},\mathcal{B}_{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{2}\times Kd_{2}},\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{1}\times Kd_{1}},\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{2}\times Kd_{2}}
that satisfy the equivalence conditions

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&x_{1}=\cdots=x_{K}=\bar{x}\Longleftrightarrow\mathcal{B}_{x}\mx=0\Longleftrightarrow\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{x}\mx=0\\
&y_{1}=\cdots=y_{K}=\bar{y}\Longleftrightarrow\mathcal{B}_{y}\my=0\Longleftrightarrow\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{y}\my=0\\
&\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{x}\text{ and }\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{y}\text{ are positive semi-definite}\end{cases}.

(6)

In other words, these matrices are chosen such that the nullspaces of
{\cal B}_{x},{\cal B}_{y},\overline{\cal C}_{x},\overline{\cal C}_{y}
are given by
\mathrm{null}(\mathcal{B}_{x})=\mathrm{span}(\mathds{1}_{K}\otimes\bar{x}),\mathrm{null}(\overline{\cal C}_{x})=\mathrm{span}(\mathds{1}_{K}\otimes\bar{x})
,

\mathrm{null}(\mathcal{B}_{y})=\mathrm{span}(\mathds{1}_{K}\otimes\bar{y})
, and
\mathrm{null}(\overline{\cal C}_{y})=\mathrm{span}(\mathds{1}_{K}\otimes\bar{y})
.
We will explain further ahead how these matrices are chosen; for now, we view them as degrees of freedom that are available to the designer. Different choices for these quantities will lead to diffferent algorithms.
Using (
6
),
we can rewrite
(
5
)
in the form of a constrained problem with linear constraints:
(see
[
42
]
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\min_{\mx\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{1}}}\mathcal{J}(\mx,\my)\\
\displaystyle\min_{\my\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{2}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}-}\mathcal{J}(\mx,\my)\end{cases}\quad\operatorname{s.t.}\begin{cases}\mB_{x}\mx=0\\
\mB_{y}\my=0\end{cases}.

(7)

We will now solve (
7
) by relying on the augmented Lagrangian approach,
which motivates us to introduce the following two-level primal-dual form:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\min_{\mx\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{1}}}\max_{\md_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{1}}}\Big\{\mathcal{J}(\mx,\my)+\frac{1}{\mu_{x}}\md^{\top}_{x}\mB_{x}\mx+\frac{1}{2\mu_{x}}\|\mx\|^{2}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{x}}\Big\},\\
\\
\displaystyle\min_{\my\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{2}}}\max_{\md_{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{2}}}\Big\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}-}\mathcal{J}(\mx,\my)+\frac{1}{\mu_{y}}\md^{\top}_{y}\mB_{y}\my+\frac{1}{2\mu_{y}}\|\my\|^{2}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{y}}\Big\},\end{cases}

(8)

where
\mu_{x}>0,\mu_{y}>0
are constants and
the network-augmented vectors

\md_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{1}}
and
\md_{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{2}}

are dual variables associated with constraints
\mB_{x}\mx=0
and
\mB_{y}\my=0
, respectively.
Furthermore, the rightmost quadratic terms impose penalties when the local models deviate from one another.
To solve (
8
), we will adopt an iterative primal-dual decentralized approach as follows. We introduce two other free parameters in the form of block symmetric doubly-stochastic matrices
\mA_{x}
and
\mA_{y}
satisfying
\mA_{x}\mx=\mx
and
\mA_{y}\my=\my
. These matrices will define the combination policies over the graph structure and will control information sharing among the agents, as will become evident soon. Using the parameters
\{{\cal A}_{x},{\cal A}_{y},{\cal B}_{x},{\cal B}_{y},{\cal C}_{x},{\cal C}_{y}\}
, we write down the following primal-dual iterations over
i\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\mx_{i+1}=\mathcal{A}_{x}\Big[\mx_{i}-\mu_{x}\nabla_{x}\mathcal{J}(\mx_{i},\my_{i})-\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{x}\mx_{i}\Big]-\mathcal{B}_{x}\md_{x,i},

(9a)

\displaystyle\md_{x,i+1}=\md_{x,i}+\mathcal{B}_{x}\mx_{i+1},

(9b)

\displaystyle\my_{i+1}=\mA_{y}\Big[\my_{i}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}+}}\mu_{y}\nabla_{y}\mathcal{J}(\mx_{i},\my_{i})-\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{y}\my_{i}\Big]-\mathcal{B}_{y}\md_{y,i},

(9c)

\displaystyle\md_{y,i+1}=\md_{y,i}+\mathcal{B}_{y}\my_{i+1}.

(9d)

These recursions are obtained from (
8
) by writing down gradient descent steps over the primal variables and gradient ascent steps over the dual variables.

Setting
\mathcal{C}_{x}=\mathrm{I}_{Kd_{1}}-\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{x},\mathcal{C}_{y}=\mathrm{I}_{Kd_{2}}-\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{y}
,
we simplify recursions
(

)—(
9d
)
to

\displaystyle\mx_{i+1}=\mA_{x}(\mathcal{C}_{x}\mx_{i}-\mu_{x}\nabla_{x}\mathcal{J}(\mx_{i},\my_{i}))-\mB_{x}\md_{x,i},

(10a)

\displaystyle(\textbf{DAMA})\

\displaystyle\my_{i+1}=\mA_{y}(\mathcal{C}_{y}\my_{i}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}+}}\mu_{y}\nabla_{y}\mathcal{J}(\mx_{i},\my_{i}))-\mB_{y}\md_{y,i},

(10b)

\displaystyle\md_{x,i+1}=\md_{x,i}+\mB_{x}\mx_{i+1},

(10c)

\displaystyle\md_{y,i+1}=\md_{y,i}+\mB_{y}\my_{i+1}.

(10d)

In (

y

II-B

Unified probabilistic gradient estimator

In this subsection, we devise efficient strategies for constructing stochastic gradient approximators
\bm{g}^{x}_{k,i},\bm{g}^{y}_{k,i}
. Our approach builds on two well studied families of algorithms: variance reduction techniques
[
38
,
24
,
26
,
63
]
and momentum methods
[
11
,
48
,
5
,
42
,
20
,
9
]
, both of which have demonstrated strong empirical performance in machine learning applications.
Momentum strategies are typically more memory efficient since they avoid large batch gradient computations. In contrast, variance reduction techniques periodically require high quality gradient estimates, often obtained through large batches, which can reduce their practicality for large data sizes. Nevertheless, in the finite sum setting, variance reduction methods can achieve better sample complexity than accelerated momentum when the sample size is below a threshold determined by the accuracy

\varepsilon
. Hence, we will consider both strategies in order to address different scenarios more effectively.
In what follows, we will demonstrate how the centralized minimax momentum strategy
[
43
]
and various accelerated momentum methods
[
11
,
48
,
5
,
42
,
20
,
9
]
can be jointly represented within a single form.
At the same time, a rich body of variance reduction techniques have been developed in the literature
[
38
,
33
,
63
,
24
,
26
,
55
]
, including classical double loop methods such as SARAH
[
38
]
and SVRG-type
[
41
,
33
,
55
]
, as well as loopless variants such as Loopless SARAH
[
24
]
and PAGE
[
26
]
. The loopless variance reduction approach employs a gradient estimator that probabilistically alternates between large batch and mini-batch computations with a prescribed distribution. In this work, we primarily focus on loopless variance reduction techniques, as their simple design naturally suits online learning by allowing the switching probability to adapt to the noise level.
Before we revisit several efficient gradient estimators, we introduce the following
notation for compactness:

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{k,i}

\displaystyle\triangleq{\rm col}\{\bm{x}_{k,i},\bm{y}_{k,i}\},

(21)

\displaystyle q_{x}(\bm{z}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})

\displaystyle\triangleq\nabla_{x}Q_{k}(\bm{x}_{k,i-1},\bm{y}_{k,i-1};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})-\nabla_{x}Q_{k}(\bm{x}_{k,i},\bm{y}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i}),

(22)

\displaystyle{\rm h}_{x}(\bm{z}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})

\displaystyle\triangleq\Big[\nabla^{2}_{x}Q_{k}(\bm{x}_{k,i},\bm{y}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i}),\nabla^{2}_{xy}Q_{k}(\bm{x}_{k,i},\bm{y}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})\Big]\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{k,i-1}-\bm{x}_{k,i}\\
\bm{y}_{k,i-1}-\bm{y}_{k,i}\end{bmatrix},

(23)

where
\nabla^{2}_{x}(\cdot)

and
\nabla^{2}_{xy}(\cdot)
are the Hessian and Jacobian operators, respectively,
\bm{z}_{k,i}
is a concatenated vector,
q_{x}(\bm{z}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})
represents the difference of loss gradient around

\bm{z}_{k,i}
and its neighboring point, and

h_{x}(\bm{z}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})
is the partial second-order information around the same point.

\bullet

Accelerated momentum

We focus on the updating rule for the momentum vector
\bm{g}^{x}_{k,i}
associated with the
x
-variable of agent
k
. The form for the

y
-variable follows analogously.
One class of momentum methods for updating
\bm{g}^{x}_{k,i}
in the minimax context can be constructed recursively as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{g}^{x}_{k,i}

\displaystyle=(1-\beta_{x})\Big[\bm{g}^{x}_{k,i-1}-\gamma_{1}q_{x}(\bm{z}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})\Big]+\beta_{x}\nabla_{x}Q_{k}(\bm{z}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i}),

(24)

where
\gamma_{1}\in\{0,1\}
is an integer;
\bm{\xi}_{k,i}=\bm{\zeta}_{k}
in the online setting, and
\bm{\xi}_{k,i}=\bm{\zeta}_{k}(s)
in the offline setting for some
s\in[N_{k}]
;
\beta_{x}\in[0,1]
is a smoothing factor. Note that
by setting
\beta_{x}=1
,
we recover the stochastic gradient method studied in
[
28
]
and by setting
\gamma_{1}=0

and
\beta_{x}\in(0,1)
, we recover the heavy-ball momentum method
[
43
]
.
Furthermore, by setting
\gamma_{1}=1

and
\beta_{x}\in(0,1)
,
we get the accelerated momentum algorithm STORM, which was introduced in
[
11
]
and later investigated in the minimax context by several works
[
50
,
19
,
20
,
49
]
.
STORM resembles heavy-ball momentum augmented with an additional correction term, namely,
q_{x}(\bm{z}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})
.
The additional term refines the past momentum by diminishing the influence of stale directions and reorienting it toward the latest direction with a new sample
\bm{\xi}_{k,i}
, which effectively reduces gradient variance over iterations.
Using a first-order Taylor expansion of the gradient function
\nabla_{x}Q_{k}(\bm{x},\bm{y};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})
at
(\bm{x}_{k,i},\bm{y}_{k,i})
yields

\displaystyle q_{x}(\bm{z}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})={\rm h}_{x}(\bm{z}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})+\mathcal{O}(\|\bm{z}_{k,i-1}-\bm{z}_{k,i}\|^{2}),

(25)

where
the increment of the weight can be approximated by
\mathcal{O}(\mu^{2}_{x}+\mu^{2}_{y})
under a small step size regime. That being said, the correction term

q_{x}(\bm{z}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})

can be approximated by
{\rm h}_{x}(\bm{z}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})
.
Replacing
q_{x}(\bm{z}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})

in (
24
)
by
{\rm h}_{x}(\bm{z}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})
, we arrive at another line of approach known as the Hessian-corrected momentum scheme, studied in the works
[
48
,
5
,
6
]
.
This scheme achieves a convergence rate on par with STORM under the alternative assumption that the cost function is second-order Lipschitz.
In the small step-size regime, we
assume
that both correction terms yield a similar effect, as the higher-order terms in (
25
) can be neglected.
Putting these schemes together, we obtain the following generic form:

\displaystyle\bm{g}^{x}_{k,i}

\displaystyle=(1-\beta_{x})\Big[\bm{g}^{x}_{k,i-1}-\gamma_{1}q_{x}(\bm{z}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})-\gamma_{2}h_{x}(\bm{z}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})\Big]+\beta_{x}\nabla_{x}Q_{k}(\bm{z}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i}),

(26)

where
\gamma_{2}\in\{0,1\}
.
By tuning
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\{0,1\}
, we can activate either of the momentum methods.

\bullet

Loopless variance reduction technique

Unlike momentum schemes, which rely on exponential averaging of past stochastic gradients, variance-reduction methods reduce noise by intermittently computing high-quality gradients with large batches.
Large-batch gradients are typically computed periodically in an outer loop as in the classical works
[
41
,
38
]
. Recently, loopless variance-reduction methods have gained attention
[
24
,
26
]
, as they eliminate the need for an explicit double-loop structure by probabilistically switching between two gradient estimators.
To implement the strategy,
we start by
drawing
a random Bernoulli variable
\bm{\pi}_{k,i}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)
at communication round
i
with a shared random seed across agents, where
p\in[0,1]
is the probability of the event
\bm{\pi}_{k,i}=1
, and then
perform:

\displaystyle\bm{g}^{x}_{k,i}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}_{k,i}|}\sum_{\bm{\xi}_{k,i}\in\mathcal{E}_{k,i}}\nabla_{x}Q_{k}(\bm{z}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})&\quad(\text{if }\bm{\pi}_{k,i}=1)\\
\displaystyle\bm{g}^{x}_{k,i-1}-\frac{1}{b}\sum_{\bm{\xi}_{k,i}\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{k,i}}q_{x}(\bm{z}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})&\quad(\text{if }\bm{\pi}_{k,i}=0).\end{cases}

(27)

Here,
\mathcal{E}_{k,i}
denotes a large-batch set of i.i.d. samples
and
|\mathcal{E}_{k,i}|=B
in the online setting, or a full batch with
|\mathcal{E}_{k,i}|=N_{k}
in the finite-sum setting.
Moreover,

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{k,i}
is a mini-batch set of i.i.d. samples with size
b
.
By setting
b=\mathcal{O}(1)
, construction
(
27
) reduces to the
Loopless SARAH
[
24
]
, which
probabilistically
alternates between the large-batch (or full-batch) gradient and SARAH.
By setting larger mini-batch size
b\leq\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{|\mathcal{E}_{k,i}|})
,
we achieve
PAGE
[
26
,
9
]
, which probabilistically switches between a large-batch (or full-batch) gradient and a minibatch variant of SARAH.
Furthermore, in the finite-sum setting, if we set the Bernoulli variable
p=1

Theoretical results

In this section, we report a general performance bound for

(see Theorem
1
) and refined bounds for specific strategies (see Table

For clarity, we focus on the presentation of the main results in Part I, while the technical details and derivations are provided in Part II
[
7
]
.
Since our approach unifies a broad range of learning methods, the analysis becomes considerably more intricate. To address this challenge, we reformulate the recursions by introducing a useful variable transformation that exploits a fundamental factorization of the transition matrix.
We then perform the theoretical analysis in the transformed domain. Our analyses rely on standard assumptions widely adopted in many minimax works
[
57
,
50
,
20
,
9
,
6
,
8
,

. We state these assumptions next.

III-A

Assumptions

We will seek an approximate stationary pair
(\bm{x}_{c,i},\bm{y}_{c,i})
such that the gradient norms evaluated at this point are bounded by a tolerance
\varepsilon
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{x}J(\bm{x}_{c,i},\bm{y}_{c,i})\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2},\quad\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{y}J(\bm{x}_{c,i},\bm{y}_{c,i})\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2},

(34)

where
(\bm{x}_{c,i},\bm{y}_{c,i})
is taken as the network centroid defined by

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{c,i}\triangleq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{x}_{k,i},\quad\bm{y}_{c,i}\triangleq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{y}_{k,i}.

(35)

Such a stationary pair provides a first-order approximation to the Nash equilibrium.
For ease of comparison, we express the network centroid in block form as

\displaystyle\mX_{c,i}=\mathds{1}_{K}\otimes\bm{x}_{c,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{1}},\quad\mY_{c,i}=\mathds{1}_{K}\otimes\bm{y}_{c,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{2}}.

(36)

To examine the convergence and performance guarantees of the
DAMA
strategy, we introduce the following assumptions.

Assumption 1

(
Cost function
)
.

The global cost function
J(x,y)

is nonconvex in
x

and nonconcave in
y
but satisfies a

\nu
-PL condition for any fixed
x
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{y}J(x,y)\|^{2}\geq 2\nu\Big(\max_{y}J(x,y)-J(x,y)\Big),

(37)

where
\nu>0
is a positive constant.
Furthermore, the maximum envelope function
P(x)=\max_{y}J(x,y)
is lower bounded, i.e.,

\displaystyle P^{\star}\triangleq\inf_{x}P(x)>-\infty.

(38)

We assume the local loss gradients are smooth in the following sense.

Assumption 2

(
Expected smoothness
)
.

The gradients of the local loss function
Q_{k}(x,y;\bm{\xi})
are assumed to be
L_{f}
-Lipschitz (or
L_{f}
-smooth) in expectation, i.e., for any
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}},y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
, there exists a constant
L_{f}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{x}Q_{k}(x_{1},y_{1};\bm{\xi})-\nabla_{x}Q_{k}(x_{2},y_{2};\bm{\xi})\|^{2}\leq L_{f}^{2}\big(\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}+\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|^{2}\big),

(39)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{y}Q_{k}(x_{1},y_{1};\bm{\xi})-\nabla_{y}Q_{k}(x_{2},y_{2};\bm{\xi})\|^{2}\leq L_{f}^{2}\big(\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}+\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|^{2}\big).

(40)

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, we can establish a Danskin-type Lemma
[
39
]
which implies that the maximum envelope function
P(x)
is
L\triangleq(L_{f}+\frac{\kappa L_{f}}{2})
-smooth and

\nabla P(x)=\nabla_{x}J(x,y^{o}(x))
, where
y^{o}(x)=\arg\max_{y}J(x,y)
. These are essential properties underlying our analysis.
Since we consider stochastic learning, we impose the following standard assumptions on the stochastic loss gradients.

Assumption 3

(
Gradient noise process
)
.

The local stochastic loss gradients
\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(\bm{x}_{k,i},\bm{y}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})
, where
w\in\{x,y\}
, are assumed to be conditionally unbiased with bounded variance for all
k,i
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(\bm{x}_{k,i},\bm{y}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})\mid\bm{\mathcal{F}}_{i}]=\nabla_{w}J_{k}(\bm{x}_{k,i},\bm{y}_{k,i}),

(41a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(\bm{x}_{k,i},\bm{y}_{k,i};\bm{\xi}_{k,i})-\nabla_{w}J_{k}(\bm{x}_{k,i},\bm{y}_{k,i})\|^{2}\mid\bm{\mathcal{F}}_{i}]\leq\sigma^{2}.

(41b)

where
\bm{\mathcal{F}}_{i}=\sigma({\bm{x}_{k,j},\bm{y}_{k,j}\mid k=1,\ldots,K,j\leq i})
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the state vectors of all agents up to iteration
i
, and
\sigma^{2}\geq 0
is a constant. In addition, the random samples
{\bm{\xi}_{k,i}}
are assumed to be spatially and temporally independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.).

Since we focus on decentralized learning, we impose the following assumption on the combination matrices.

Assumption 4

(
Combination matrices
)
.

Consider a combination matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
that is symmetric, doubly stochastic, and primitive (this latter condition is guaranteed if
W
corresponds to a connected graph and at least one diagonal entry is nonzero). Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{x}\triangleq W\otimes I_{d_{1}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{1}\times Kd_{1}},\ \mathcal{W}_{y}\triangleq W\otimes I_{d_{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{2}\times Kd_{2}}.

(42)

The matrices
\mA_{x},\mB_{x}^{2},\mathcal{C}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{1}\times Kd_{1}}
and
\mA_{y},\mB_{y}^{2},\mathcal{C}_{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{2}\times Kd_{2}}
are assumed to be polynomial functions of
\mathcal{W}_{x}
and
\mathcal{W}_{y}
, respectively, such that

(i)

\mA_{x},\mA_{y},\mathcal{C}_{x},\mathcal{C}_{y}
are symmetric and doubly stochastic;

(ii)

\mathrm{null}(\mB_{x})=\mathrm{span}\{\mathds{1}_{K}\otimes\bar{x}\},\mathrm{null}(\mB_{y})=\mathrm{span}\{\mathds{1}_{K}\otimes\bar{y}\}
for some nonzero vectors
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}},\bar{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}

The above assumption is also made in
[
3

2

42

and is mild, since, as shown in Table
II

is symmetric, doubly stochastic, and primitive, according to the Perron-Frobenius theorem

\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=2}^{K}

\displaystyle\lambda\triangleq\max_{i=2,\ldots,K}|\lambda_{i}|<1.

Conclusions

In Part I of this work, we proposed a unified framework for decentralized minimax optimization. Within this framework, we introduced a unified gradient estimator
GRACE
to accelerate. The methodology proposed herein subsumes several existing schemes as special cases, and suggests several other new schemes including methods with superior performance.

7
]

\bullet

The semi-adapt-then-combine GT (semi-ATC-GT) employs a hybrid learning strategy. Specifically, it combines a consensus learning step for local models and the adapt-then-combine diffusion step for updating the tracking variable.
When a semi-ATC-GT strategy is applied to solve a minimax problem, it admits the following form:

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}_{y}(\mG_{y,i}+\mM_{x,i+1}-\mM_{x,i}),

(84d)

\mG_{x,0}=\mM_{x,0},\mG_{y,0}=\mM_{y,0}

\mX_{0}=\mathcal{W}_{x}\mX_{0}(\bm{x}_{1,0}=\cdots=\bm{x}_{K,0}),\mY_{0}=\mathcal{W}_{y}\mY_{0}(\bm{y}_{1,0}=\cdots=\bm{y}_{K,0})

(

)–
(

we can transform the above recursions into

\displaystyle\mX_{i+2}

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{W}_{x}\mX_{i+1}-\mathcal{W}^{2}_{x}\mX_{i}-\mu_{x}\mathcal{W}_{x}(\mM_{x,i+1}-\mM_{x,i}),

(85a)

\displaystyle\mY_{i+2}

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{W}_{y}\mY_{i+1}-\mathcal{W}^{2}_{y}\mY_{i}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}+}}\mu_{y}\mathcal{W}_{y}(\mM_{y,i+1}-\mM_{y,i}),
