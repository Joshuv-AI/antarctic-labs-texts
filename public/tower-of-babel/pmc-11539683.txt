Identifying low-density, ancestry-informative SNP markers through whole genome resequencing in Indian, Chinese, and wild yak
Authors: Munish Gangwar, Sheikh Firdous Ahmad, Abdul Basit Ali, Amit Kumar, Amod Kumar, Gyanendra Kumar Gaur, Triveni Dutt
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe current investigation was undertaken to elucidate the population-stratifying and ancestry-informative markers in Indian, Chinese, and wild yak populations using whole genome resequencing (WGS) analysis while employing various selection strategies (Delta, Pairwise Wright’s Fixation Index-FST, and Informativeness of Assignment) and marker densities (5–25 thousand). The study used WGS data on 105 individuals from three separate yak cohorts i.e., Indian yak (n = 29), Chinese yak (n = 61), and wild yak (n = 15). Variant calling in the GATK program with strict quality control resulted in 1,002,970 high-quality and independent (LD-pruned) SNP markers across the yak autosomes. Analysis was undertaken in toolbox for ranking and evaluation of SNPs (TRES) program wherein three different criteria i.e., Delta, Pairwise Wright’s Fixation Index-FST, and Informativeness of Assignment were employed to identify population-stratifying and ancestry-informative markers across various datasets. The top-ranked 5,000 (5K), 10,000 (10K), 15,000 (15K), 20,000 (20K), and 25,000 (25K) SNPs were identified from each dataset while their composition and performance was assessed using different criteria. The average genomic breed clustering of Indian, Chinese, and wild yak cohorts with full density dataset (105 individuals with 1,002,970 markers) was 81.74%, 80.02%, and 83.62%, respectively. Informativeness of Assignment criterion with 10K density emerged as the best combination for three yak cohorts with 86.94%, 96.46%, and 98.20% clustering for Indian, Chinese, and wild yak, respectively. There was an average increase of 7.56%, 22.72%, and 30.35% in genomic breed clustering scores of Indian, Chinese, and wild yak cohorts over the estimates of the original dataset. The selected markers showed overlap multiple protein-coding genes within a 10 kb window including ADGRB3, ANK1, CACNG7, CALN1, CHCHD2, CREBBP, GLI3, KHDRBS2, and OSBPL10. This is the first report ever on elucidating low-density SNP marker sets with population-stratifying and ancestry-informative properties in three yak groups using WGS data. Introduction

Yak (Bos grunniens or Peophagus grunniens) are herbivores that are raised in habitats with an altitude of 3000–5000 m above the mean sea level [1]. These are the primary livestock species that graze on the Asian highlands, particularly the Himalayan mountains [2]. Asian highlands, the largest mountain region on earth, cover the Qinghai-Tibetan plateau and other Himalayan regions (including Hindu-Kush, Karakoram, and Pamir ranges). Its ecosystem provides livelihood opportunities with food security to rural masses in over 10 nations across the globe [1]. These regions present harsh environment with fluctuating temperatures and precipitation, hypobaric conditions with low oxygen content, and high ultraviolet radiations coupled with extreme winds and severe snowstorms. Pasture availability in these regions thus fluctuates, with poor-quality vegetation support to animals, especially during the winter season [3]. The global population of domestic yak is around 16 million along with 15–20 thousand wild yak [2, 4]. Over 95% of yak are raised in China; but the yak populations are also reared in Nepal, Bhutan, India, Pakistan, Mongolia, Russia, Afghanistan, Tajikistan, and Kyrgyzstan. India is home to around 58 thousand as per the 19th census report of 2019, recording a decrease of 24.08% from the previous livestock census of 2012 [5].

Chinese yak thrive in the high, cold mountainous regions of China (mainly its Qinghai-Tibet plateau), grazing on alpine grasslands in summer and shrubs under deep snow during harsh winters [6]. They are found in valleys, within northern and eastern parts of Tibet, parts of Sichuan and Yunnan (e.g., Jiulang), besides plateau grasslands with sub-zero temperatures (e.g., Maiwa and Luqu). On the other hand, the Indian yak mainly inhabit the north and north-eastern regions, specifically the arid, dry terrains of Himachal Pradesh and Ladakh, as well as the wet, cold areas of Arunachal Pradesh and Sikkim. Accordingly, the Indian yak populations are classed into four groups i.e., Arunachali, Sikkimi, Himachali, and Ladakhi [7]. The Monpa pastoralist community (Brokpa tribe) in Tawang and West Kameng districts has a long-standing tradition of raising the Arunachali yak, the only registered native yak breed of India [8]. Yak, the medium-sized bovines, possess a dense layer of hair and display a calm temperament, often sporting a black coat. In cold environments, the black coat color serves as an adaptation to help conserve body heat [9]. The yak populations play multifaceted roles in these societies serving important economic and cultural purposes. Beyond providing essential resources like milk, fur, meat, and other non-food products, yak contribute significantly to the livelihood of local communities [10, 11].

Genomic selection, based on the principle of linkage disequilibrium (LD) between genome-wide markers and phenotype (economic trait) of interest, has revolutionized the field of animal breeding [12, 13]. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) have emerged as markers of choice based on their distribution pattern and amenability to detailed bioinformatics analysis [14]. Using genomic selection, the genetic merit of animals can be predicted with high accuracy [15, 16]. It can thus help improve the rate of genetic gain in livestock populations along with the benefit of selection at an early age. Various sequencing and BeadChip array technologies/ platforms are now available and have been widely applied for the generation of genome-wide data on SNP markers in different farm animal species. The genome-wide SNP data generated using these platforms have been useful to make genomic predictions and gain deeper insights into the structural and functional aspects of the livestock genomes.

Usage of high-density data on a large animal cohort is a prerequisite to accurate prediction of genetic merit and application of genomic selection in livestock. The likelihood of an SNP in linkage disequilibrium with the phenotype (economic trait) of interest improves with increasing density of markers on a genome-wide scale [17]. However, the generation of higher number of SNP markers is economically intensive [18], which is one of the reasons for lower penetration of genomic selection in livestock, especially in developing countries [19]. Financial constraints limit the cohort size that is included in the genomic selection programs across developing nations with limited resources. Size of the reference population is directly linked to the accuracy of genomic selection programs [13].

The imputation process is of high significance in the application of genomic selection in livestock populations across the globe [20]. Low-density SNP panels have been developed for various livestock species, especially under resource-constrained conditions. Unlike many livestock species, no SNP BeadChip arrays are available for yak populations globally. Hence, low-density panels comprising population-stratifying and ancestry-informative markers can be immensely useful for making various predictions and breeding decisions in the global yak populations. However, efficient and strategic marker selection is an utmost need for designing low-density panels that may, in turn, be used to make reliable predictions for breed improvement, while incurring lower costs. Keeping in view the above points, the present study was undertaken to elucidate the population-stratifying and ancestry-informative markers in Indian, Chinese, and wild yak populations using whole genome resequencing data while employing various selection strategies and marker densities.

Individually, the performance of different marker sets with respect to genomic breed clustering varied for dataset A across different densities (Table 2). Using the Delta method [21], the highest genomic breed clustering (87.35%) for the Indian yak population in dataset A was observed with a 5K marker set; the scores decreased marginally with increasing density within this criterion for the Indian yak cohort. On the other hand, the Chinese yak population depicted the highest average genomic breed clustering (91.78%) with a 25K SNP set using the Delta method. The 5K SNP dataset showed the highest score of 98.09% for wild yak under this criterion.

Using Pairwise Wright’s Fixation Index-FST method [21], the Indian cohort reached the highest genomic breed clustering score (88.30%) with a 25K SNP dataset. Conversely, the Chinese population had the highest average genomic breed clustering score (84.97%) with a 5K marker set only, although this score was lower than the clustering levels scored with other criteria. The wild yak population showed the highest genomic breed clustering score (99.65%) with a 5K SNP dataset.

Under the Informativeness of Assignment method [21], the Chinese yak cohort revealed the highest genomic breed clustering score with a 20K SNP dataset achieving an average score of 96.92% (Fig. 1). The highest average genomic breed clustering score (87.98%) for Indian yak was revealed for the 5K SNP dataset, while the wild yak population showed the highest average score (98.89%) at the same density under this criterion. The average genomic breed clustering for the Indian yak cohort was comparatively similar across all methods and densities with performance ranging between 84.08% (Delta criterion; 10K density) and 88.30% (Pairwise Wright’s Fixation Index-FST criterion; 25K density). The wild yak population showed consistently high average genomic clustering scores across all criteria and densities with above 96% average clustering scores. Overall, Pairwise Wright’s Fixation Index-FST method showed better performance for the wild yak population, particularly with some densities showing near-absolute (~ 99%) genomic clustering (Table 2).

Any criterion that produces optimal results for all three cohorts with a minimal number of markers would be preferred. Therefore, Informativeness of Assignment criterion with 10K density emerged as the best combination for three yak cohorts with 86.94%, 96.46%, and 98.20% clustering for Indian, Chinese, and wild yak, respectively (Fig. 2b). There was an average increase of 7.56%, 22.72%, and 30.35% in genomic breed clustering scores of Indian, Chinese, and wild yak cohorts over the estimates of the original dataset (105 individuals*1,002,970 markers). The PCA plot also stratified the three yak cohorts distinctly using 10K marker set elucidated based on Informativeness of Assignment criterion (Fig. 2c). The individual-wise genomic clustering scores from the original and 10K datasets with the Informativeness of Assignment criterion have been depicted in supplementary file. The results were indicative of the ancestry-informative power of these low-density SNP panels in the studied yak populations.

Population-stratifying markers for Indian-Chinese and Indian-wild yak datasets

Using the whole set of 1,002,970 markers in dataset B (refer to Table 4), the Indian and Chinese yak cohorts showed an average genomic breed clustering of 81.94% and 96.74%, respectively (Fig. 3a). For dataset B, the low-density panels developed based on Informativeness of Assignment criterion performed better for Indian yak. The average genomic breed clustering was optimal (91.19–99.60%) throughout different criteria and densities for the Chinese yak cohort (Table 3). The results revealed efficient differentiation of Indian (99.23% clustering) and Chinese (91.37% clustering) yak using the minimum number of SNP markers developed using Informativeness of Assignment criterion with 5K density (Fig. 3b). There was an average increase of 23.94% in clustering of Indian yak individuals on using 5K dataset employing Informativeness of Assignment criterion over the original dataset (dataset B) with 1,002,970 SNP markers.

On analysis of full density dataset with 1,002,970 markers in set C, the Indian and wild yak cohorts showed an average genomic breed clustering of 87.79% and 93.49%, respectively (Fig. 3c). The average genomic clustering of Indian and wild yak cohorts was above 90% across all methods and densities except Informativeness of Assignment criterion with 25K density. Identification of common markers across different methods

Venn diagrams were used in the present study to visualize the common SNPs identified through different criteria i.e., Delta, Pairwise Wright’s Fixation Index-FST, and Informativeness of Assignment. Discussion

The acceptance of available genotyping technologies in farm animal species depends on many aspects, of which cost forms the prime deciding factor. Any factor that promises help with regard to reducing the cost of genotyping animal cohorts warrants a genuine investigation [22]. Furthermore, cost-effective approaches are necessary to genotype large animal populations concurrently, so that the prediction accuracies are imporved [23]. The availability of low-density marker panels with subsequent imputation will be useful to ensure the uptake of genotyping technologies for an increased number of animal populations, especially within developing countries [24]. Studies using WGS or double digest restriction-site associated DNA (ddRAD) sequencing approaches have been employed in Indian and Chinese yak [25–28], however, no genotyping SNP arrays are available for use in yak populations at the global level. Besides, no study has ever reported the elucidation and evaluation of population-stratifying and ancestry-informative SNP markers/ panels on a genome-wide scale in Indian or Chinese yak populations. This is the first report ever on the elucidation and evaluation of low-density SNP marker sets with population-stratifying and ancestry-informative properties vis-à-vis Indian, Chinese, and wild yak populations by employing various criteria across different densities.

Earlier, many studies have reported the elucidation and evaluation of low-density SNP panels in multiple farm animal species using variety of marker selection strategies. These methods vary from statistical approaches such as PCA, Wright’s FST, Delta, Informativeness, and genome-wide association study (GWAS), to machine learning algorithms including random forest, boruta, ANN, SVM, naïve bayes, and KNN [22, 29–38]. These studies have been carried out in multiple farm animal species including cattle, sheep, pigs, and chicken and have reported varied estimates of prediction accuracies. However, the matrix size in terms of number of SNPs*sample size (i.e., number of individuals) varied across these studies. The accuracy of predictions is multifactorial in nature and dependent on sample size, number of markers originally generated, extent of linkage disequilibrium among markers, population structure among studied populations and other technical aspects of the tools and strategies used for marker selection [33, 38]. Additionally, most of these studies have been undertaken on BeadChip array data; WGS data on naive populations poses an additional challenge. Zhao et al. [29] used whole-genome sequencing and SNP array data from 13 cattle breeds to compare statistical (Delta, FST, and Informativeness) methods and five machine learning algorithms for delineating breed-informative SNPs, achieving over 95% accuracy across various reference population sizes and SNP counts. The features of SNP arrays differ from WGS data in terms of linkage disequilibrium, minor allele frequency, genomic coverage and other parameters [39]. The performance (accuracy) and interplay of different features of low-density panels should thus be inferred accordingly.

In this study, three distinct methods i.e., Delta, Pairwise Wright’s Fixation Index-FST, and Informativeness of Assignment were used to select population-stratifying and ancestry-informative markers from the original dataset with 1,002,970 SNPs for low-density panels of five different sizes ranging from 5000 to 25,000 markers. The genomic breed clustering on WGS data containing these markers was also assessed for five datasets with different SNP densities i.e., 5K, 10K, 15K, 20K, and 25K. Based on genomic breed clustering estimates using three criteria, an opposite trend of genomic clustering was revealed with increasing density for Indian and Chinese cohorts using Delta and Pairwise Wright’s Fixation Index-FST methods. The Informativeness of Assignment criterion resulted in better genomic clustering scores at lower densities (5K). Overall, the Informativeness of Assignment criterion provided the most consistent and highest average genomic breed clustering scores for the Chinese population (range: 94.79–96.92% clustering) across different low-density SNP datasets. However, the results need to be inferred keeping in view various aspects that include the size (No. of markers) of low-density SNP panels and the overall performance for all three yak cohorts together. The low-density SNP panels should cover substantial variation, being capable of differentiating various yak cohorts and produce reproducible results. Inferences should also keep in mind the original clustering pattern of the full-density dataset with 1,002,970 markers.

In the present study, the elucidation of low-density panels was also evaluated separately for Indian-Chinese (dataset B) and Indian-wild (dataset C) yak combinations using similar criteria and densities as used for dataset A. The Delta and Pairwise Wright’s Fixation Index-FST criteria were equally efficient with nearly similar performance across different marker densities. Based on the results, the 5K dataset developed using the Informativeness of Assignment criterion can be recommended for efficient differentiation of Indian (99.23% clustering) and Chinese (91.37% clustering) yak using the minimum number of SNP markers (Fig. 3b). The findings highlighted the effectiveness of the Informativeness of Assignment method in achieving optimal genomic clustering in both populations.

The Informativeness of Assignment method showed the highest genomic clustering scores for the wild yak population with the 5K dataset (99.07%), similar to that of Pairwise Wright’s Fixation Index-FST method at the same density. The higher genomic clustering levels for wild yak cohort is attributable to various factors including its distinct genetic isolation from other two cohorts, relation of the selected markers with population structure, and underlying principles of twin methods (Pairwise Wright’s Fixation Index-FST and Informativeness of Assignment). Both these methods share underlying principle of genetic differentiation and were able to provide sufficient information on ancestry and accurately capture the population structure of the wild yak cohort. The Informativeness for Assignment criterion measures the ability of genetic markers to distinguish between populations (focusing on informativeness), while Pairwise Wright’s Fixation Index-FST quantifies the level of genetic differentiation. It also pointed towards the correlation of informativeness and genetic differentiation power of these markers, leading to similar accuracies [40]. However, the relation is complex and not straight-forward. Overall, the Pairwise Wright’s Fixation Index-FST method with the 5K SNP dataset provided the best clustering results for both the Indian (93.25%) and wild (99.06%) yak populations taken together which can be recommended for effective genomic clustering in twin yak populations (Indian-wild yak combination).

The presence of substantial proportion of common markers among different methods indicated a strong agreement in identifying key genomic regions that harboured population-stratifying and ancestry-informative markers across the genome of these populations [41]. These methods work on complementary genetic principles addressing different aspects of population differentiation (substructure), variance explained and assignment power of SNPs [21]. As the SNP density increased to 10K, the overlap among the methods showed a slight decrease. For instance, in the 10K dataset, a total of 5469 (54.69%) SNPs were common among the three methods (Fig. 4b), and a similar trend was shown by other datasets (Fig. 4c-e). The decreased overlap among the methods (Delta, Pairwise Wright’s Fixation Index-FST, and Informativeness of Assignment) at higher SNP densities likely reflects the intricate nature of genetic variation. As reported earlier by Ding et al. [41], only a minimal subset of ancestry informative markers can be sufficient to accurately predict the ancestry of an animal cohort with minimal bias. While all methods effectively identified population-stratifying SNPs at lower densities, they captured unique (additional) aspects of genetic variation (beyond those associated with population stratification) at higher densities. The intricate relationship between SNP density, genetic variation, and the performance of different marker selection methods was evident from the results. Another noteworthy trend was the higher proportion of markers common between the Delta and Informativeness of Assignment as compared to other combinations (Delta-Pairwise Wright’s Fixation Index-FST or Pairwise Wright’s Fixation Index-FST-Informativeness of Assignment) as depicted in Fig. 4a-e. Based on results from the present study, the Venn diagrams revealed significant overlap among different methods of SNP selection, indicating their robustness in marker selection. This can well be correlated with the performance in terms of average genomic breed clustering of these methods and SNP densities.

Keeping in view the best performance of the 10K dataset developed using the Informativeness of Assignment criterion, the distribution of markers along with the relation to the original number of markers on each dataset have been presented in Table 1. The distribution pattern of selected markers in low density panels highlighted the efficiency and precision of the SNP selection methods in selecting the most relevant and near-evenly spaced markers for genomic clustering.

Many of these genes that harboured the SNPs selected in best-performing low-density panel have been reported to be involved in various adaptation mechanisms, metabolic processes, and physiological pathways in yak [4, 42–48]. ADGRB3, located on BosGru_10, is a potential candidate gene that has been reported to contribute towards the high-altitude adaptation in yak [4]. It offers valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying altitude adaptation in animals [49]. On the other hand, the expression of the ANK1 gene (mapped on BosGru_28) has been linked to meat quality and tenderness, as evidenced by multiple reports [42, 43]. The CACNG7 gene, located on BosGru_20, has been associated with oxytocin signalling pathway, particularly in relation to yak physiology [44]. Hardie et al. [45] reported the CALN1 gene (located on BosGru_26) to play a significant role in the regulation of dry matter intake in animals. The expression of CREBBP gene (mapped on BosGru_26) has been reported in Sertoli cells and undifferentiated spermatogonia of yak. It helps regulate the stimulation of the follicle-stimulating hormone-mediated transferrin promoter in the testis, which directly impacts their growth during gonadal development [46]. Similarly, the expression of GLI3 (mapped on BosGru_4) gene has been linked to different phases of yak spermatogenesis [47]. Another gene i.e., OSBPL10 (mapped on BosGru_22) has been reported to be involved in the signalling and transport of lipids [48]. Overall, the SNPs selected via this method, that showed the highest genomic clustering for all three populations, were present in gene-rich regions and can be further evaluated for understanding the molecular basis of the adaptation of yak to unique high-altitude environments.

Conclusion

In the present study, different marker selection methods were implemented across five different densities in Indian, Chinese, and wild yak cohorts. These criteria work on contemporary principles of differences in allele frequency of SNPs on a genome-wide scale across populations under comparison. The low-density panels elucidated in the present study showed better population-stratifying and ancestry-informative potential for three yak cohorts with nearly even autosomal distribution. The 10K panel developed using the Informativeness of assignment criterion emerged as the best-fit, showing optimal genomic breed clustering for all three cohorts. On considering only Indian and Chinese yak cohorts, the 5K dataset developed using Informativeness of assignment showed efficient differentiation for Indian and Chinese yak cohorts. The Informativeness of assignment criterion with 5K and 10K markers may be recommended for developing low-density SNP panels for three yak cohorts. The results may be evaluated on unrelated datasets and other yak populations for their performance vis-à-vis population stratification and ancestry-informativeness.

Materials and methods

Populations and whole genome resequencing data

This study utilizes the concept of data reuse in agricultural/livestock genomics research to provide solutions to various research questions. An effort was undertaken to improve upon the existing whole genome resequencing (WGS) data obtained (in raw form) from Indian yak, Chinese yak, and wild yak populations (described in twin publications) [7, 50]. The current study provides a novel viewpoint by (re)analyzing several aspects (both analytical and non-analytical) that have not been previously studied/ documented. The current investigation involved a sample size of 105 individuals from three separate yak populations: Indian yak (n = 29), Chinese yak (n = 61), and wild yak (n = 15). It was hypothesized that these populations are related along their evolutionary history. In a study conducted by Qiu et al. [50], 84 unrelated yak had been sampled with Chinese (n = 69) or wild (n = 15) inheritance. Their study generated paired-end libraries of 500 bp reads using Hiseq2000 platform. The samples for study [50] were collected from the primary geographic breeding areas of Chinese yak. The authors [50] collected wild yak samples, as acquired from the bodies of deceased animals in the central Kokohili region, which is known to be home to around 15,000 yak, on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau [50, 51]. On the other hand, Kumar et al. [7] had generated WGS data on 30 Indian yak from three different populations: Himachali, Ladakhi, and Arunachali. The IlluminaR NovaSeq 6000 platform was utilized with the S4 flowcell to generate 150 bp long paired-end reads. Consistent base calling, and sequencing were ensured using standardized methods for every sample in both experiments.

Alignment, post-alignment processing and variant calling

As part of original data analysis in the present study, the raw data were processed via standard procedures of alignment and variant calling using various techniques and algorithms as detailed below. The unprocessed raw sequencing data (obtained from various repositories as discussed above) were initially processed for quality assessment using FastQC v0.12.0 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/; first accessed on 23 March 2024), while the removal of adapters and low-quality reads was performed using TrimGalore v0.6.5 [52, 53]. The quality of trimmed reads was re-evaluated using FastQC. After filtering and indexing the genome, the trimmed reads were aligned to the most recent BosGru3.1 domestic yak reference genome assembly (GCA_005887515.3). The alignment was performed using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner v0.7.17 (BWA) with default settings [54]. Subsequently, sorting and indexing of BAM files was done using SAMtools v1.17 [55]. The SM read tags were then added to the mapped reads. Afterwards, the PCR duplicates in BAM files were identified and eliminated using the ‘MarkDuplicates’ feature of Picard tools v2.25.1 (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/; first accessed on 23 March 2024). The variant calling was performed using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) program v4.1.8.0 (https://software.broadinstitute.org/gatk; first accessed on 23 March 2024).

The HaplotypeCaller function of the GATK was used to reconstruct the haplotypes using probabilistic modelling individually on 105 yak samples and detect the genetic variants (SNP and indels) from WGS data. Subsequently, the CombineVCF GATK tool was used to merge multiple VCF files into a single file for subsequent analysis. The GenotypeGVCFs tool was used to convert genome VCF (GVCF) files back into a standard VCF file that contained the genotyping information for specific samples. The SelectVariants tool was utilized to extract a subset of SNP variants from a VCF file.

Quality control (QC) of genome-wide SNP data

Initially, the genetic variants were filtered using the VariantFiltration tool in GATK. The QC via GATK applied the thresholds based on expression and mapping quality to eliminate variants with low confidence, mapping errors, or strand bias (if any). The different thresholds used for variant filtering included quality depth (QD) score of less than 2, a Phred-scaled genotype quality (QUAL) score of less than 30, a mapping quality (MQ) score of less than 40, a Fisher strand (FS) bias score of more than 60, a strand odds ratio (SOR) score of more than 3, a mapping quality rank sum test (MQRankSum) score of less than − 12.5, and a Read Position Rank Sum Test (ReadPosRankSum) score of less than − 8.

Post removal of indels, the VCF dataset was processed for QC in PLINK v1.9 [56]. The SNP markers without defined coordinates on yak genome, as well as those present on sex chromosomes or mitochondria, were removed. In addition, the present study utilized the standard quality control thresholds for various parameters such as the call rate of SNPs, genotyping rate of individuals, minor allele frequency, and Mendelian errors. Post-filtering, SNP markers that provided redundant information due to their close physical proximity on the respective chromosomes were removed. It was done by pruning out the markers in linkage disequilibrium (LD) with an r2 estimate greater than 0.2. A window size of 50 SNPs was used to group markers with a physical distance of 5 base pairs for LD pruning. The filtered dataset, consisting of independent (LD-pruned) and non-redundant markers, was utilized for subsequent analysis.

Ultimately, the original dataset was divided into three subsets i.e., A: whole sample set with 105 individuals from three populations (Indian, Chinese, and wild yak) and 1,002,970 SNPs; B: SNP data on 90 individuals from Indian and Chinese yak and 1,002,970 SNPs; and C: SNP data on 44 individuals from Indian and wild yak and 1,002,970 SNPs (Table 4). It was done to assess the composition and performance (vis-à-vis genomic breed clustering and other evaluations) of low-density marker sets on whole datasets and subsets separately.

Elucidation of population-stratifying and ancestry-informative markers

The elucidation of population-stratifying and ancestry-informative markers was undertaken in a Java-based program i.e., toolbox for ranking and evaluation of SNPs (TRES) [21]. Three criteria, i.e., Delta, Pairwise Wright’s Fixation Index-FST, and Informativeness of Assignment, were employed to identify population-stratifying and ancestry-informative markers across various datasets. These methods available within the TRES toolkit exploit various aspects of genetic variation, population structure (substructure), and genetic differentiation existing within the marker datasets and the populations being compared. The top-ranked 5,000 (5K), 10,000 (10K), 15,000 (15K), 20,000 (20K), and 25,000 (25K) SNPs were identified from each dataset while their composition and performance was assessed using different criteria.

The Delta statistic was calculated based on absolute differences in allele frequencies of each SNP marker within populations under comparison at each locus on a genome-wide scale. The markers were ranked based on absolute differences with the top-most marker (in order) showing the largest absolute difference in allele frequency between assessed populations. Commonality, distribution, and performance of selected marker sets

Marker sets from TRES program were assessed for overlap (density-wise) based on Venn diagrams using the list of top-ranked SNPs scored across different criteria. Venn diagrams were created using the Venny v2.1.0 program [57]. The distribution of selected markers across different criteria and densities was assessed and plotted across the yak autosomes. The genomic breed clustering and population-stratifying power of the original three datasets (A, B, and C), as well as different low-density marker sets (5K, 10K, 15K, 20K, and 25K across criteria), were assessed. For this purpose, the genotyping information on specific markers was extracted from original datasets in the PLINK v1.9 program. The binary files were processed for genomic breed clustering in the ADMIXTURE v1.3.0 program [58] using a K values of 2–6 in an iterative fashion until the convergence. Similarly, datasets B and C were processed with K value of 2. The output files from the ADMIXTURE program were assessed for average genomic clustering of each yak cohort (Indian, Chinese, and wild, for A, B, and C datasets) and plotted in the R-programming environment using a customized script. Furthermore, the best-performing marker set was processed for principal component analysis (PCA) using the Genome-wide Complex Trait Analysis (GCTA) program v1.94.1 [59]. Initially, the genomic relationship matrix (GRM) was deduced from binary PLINK files for the best-performing marker set. Subsequently, the eigenvectors and eigenvalues were elucidated and plotted in the form of PCA in R-programming environment. The best-performing marker set was also evaluated for gene enrichment by assessing the overlap within a small 10 kb window (5 kb each in upstream and downstream directions) in ENSEMBL Biomart (genome browser 112) using chromosomal coordinates of selected markers.
