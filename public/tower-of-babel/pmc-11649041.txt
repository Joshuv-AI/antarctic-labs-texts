Vasoactive Intestinal Polypeptide Secreting MS Neuroblastoma
Authors: Josy Thomas, Nitin James Peters, Aravind Sekar, Deepak Bansal, Jitender Singh, Ravi P. Kanojia, J. K. Mahajan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe present a rare case of a 17-month-old child diagnosed with MS-stage neuroblastoma and associated chronic diarrhea due to elevated vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) levels. The unusual occurrence of a VIP-secreting tumor in a patient with MS neuroblastoma is a rare combination, not previously reported in the literature. The patient underwent exploratory laparotomy and excision of the tumor, leading to a significant decline in VIP levels and resolution of symptoms.

INTRODUCTION

Neuroblastic tumors originate from the sympathetic nervous system. The sympathetic ganglia are primarily composed of adrenergic neurons that secrete catecholamines. However, a subset of neurons within the ganglia also engages in limited cholinergic transmission and secretes vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP). A small group of neuroblastomas is associated with high VIP levels and referred to as VIPoma, suggesting that neuroblastoma may also originate from the cholinergic component of the ganglia.[1] Unlike adrenergic neuroblastomas, this subset presents with chronic diarrhea, weight loss, and metabolic disorders, among other symptoms.

Evidence shows that there have been fewer than 100 cases of pediatric VIP-secreting tumors, most of which originate from adrenal pheochromocytomas or mixed pheochromocytoma–ganglioneuroma tumors.[2]

Here, we report the case of a 17-month-old child who was diagnosed with MS-stage neuroblastoma in early infancy and presented with chronic diarrhea and elevated VIP levels. To the best of our knowledge, VIPoma has not previously been reported in 4S neuroblastoma.

CASE REPORT

A 17-month-old girl, weighing 5.6 kg, presented with a history of spontaneous onset of skin nodules over the back, abdomen, and thighs since she was 3 months of age. At that time, she was evaluated and found to have a right suprarenal mass with liver metastasis. Fine needle aspiration cytology from the skin nodule revealed a malignant, round, blue cell tumor and she was categorized into MS low-risk neuroblastoma and was followed up and closely monitored. The right suprarenal mass and skin nodules remained static in size.

At the age of 11 months, she started having large volume, nonblood stained, and nonmucoid, watery diarrhea. She was noted to have hyponatremia and hypokalemia and was admitted and resuscitated. On further evaluation, raised VIP levels of 1093 pg/mL were detected (normal <70 pg/mL).

She was readmitted at 17 months of age with intractable diarrhea and on general physical examination then was found to have cachexia with a weight of 5.6 kg (Z-score for age: −4.6), height of 60 cm (Z-score for age: −6.9), and head circumference of 40 cm (Z-score for age: −4.4).

The patient had multiple subcutaneous skin nodules which were firm, nontender, and mobile over the back of the abdomen, buttocks, thigh, and left groin. The size of these lesions was variable being subcentimetric over the thigh and the largest over the back – 3 cm × 2 cm. She also developed new right axillary and left cervical lymph nodes. There was a palpable, nontender lump on the abdominal examination [Figure 1a and b].

Ultrasonography revealed a 6.5 cm × 7 cm × 7.2 cm right suprarenal mass which was static in size over the past 6 months. A fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) scan confirmed the presence of the right supra renal mass, and there was also FDG avid uptake in the right axilla, left cervical region, and over the skin nodules over the back and lower limbs. There was no skeletal metastasis, and bone marrow biopsy was negative for infiltration.

During her evaluation, she developed features of partial intestinal obstruction, which did not resolve with expectant management. She was taken up for an emergency exploratory laparotomy.

Intraoperatively, a small bowel loop was adhered to the right supra renal mass which measured 7 cm × 5 cm × 4 cm. The lesion was found to be abutting the inferior vena cava. The loop was dissected off, and the mass was excised in toto along with adjoining retrocaval and perinephric tissue. Histopathology from the same revealed a nodular variant of ganglioneuroblastoma with a favorable INPC histology, low MKI expression, and metastasis to the lymph nodes in the perinephric tissue.

Her postoperative recovery was uneventful, and her diarrhea gradually resolved. There was a significant decline in her VIP level 1 month after surgery to 61 pg/mL.

There was a minimal reduction in the size of the skin nodules which later required excision of the same, the biopsy of which was reported as metastatic neuroblastoma.

A PET computed tomography scan, repeated 6 months postexcision of the neuroblastoma, revealed somatostatin receptor-expressing cervical lymph nodes, cutaneous and muscular deposits (left gluteal region), and no residual lesion in the right suprarenal region. She is free of watery diarrhea on an 18-month follow-up.

DISCUSSION

Verner–Morrison syndrome, also colloquially known as VIPoma, originates from the non-β islet cells of the pancreas in 90% of the cases.[3] They produce VIP, a 28-aminoacid neuropeptide. High plasma levels of VIP can cause massive secretory diarrhea, severe dehydration, flushing, and weight loss associated with metabolic disturbances such as hypokalemia, achlorhydria, hyperglycemia, hypercalcemia, and metabolic acidosis (WDHA syndrome). VIPomas occur in the amine precursor uptake and decarboxylation cells in the gastroenteropancreatic endocrine system and adrenal or extra-adrenal neurogenic sites.[1]

Since the first reported case of VIP-secreting tumor in the 1970s, there have been <100 reported cases of VIP-secreting tumor in the pediatric population, as reported in the literature.[4] A review of literature conducted by Bourdeaut et al. in 2009 reported <80 cases of VIP-secreting neuroblastomas in the previous three decades when compared to the 2400 cases that had been registered with a diagnosis of neuroblastoma, hence safely assuming that <1% of all neuroblastomas display clinical evidence of VIP secretion.[2]

Children with neuroblastomas have an overall survival rate of 30%–40%, whereas children with VIP-secreting ganglioneuroblastoma have a significantly better survival rate of 90%.[4]

The current treatment of neuroblastomas is determined by the risk category which is divided into low, intermediate, and high risk based on the characteristics at the time of diagnosis: stage of the disease, patient age, histological features of the tumor, the presence or absence of amplification of the N-myc proto-oncogene, and quantitative deoxyribonucleic acid content of the tumor (DNA index or ploidy).[5]

Our patient was diagnosed with neuroblastoma MS (skin lesions) and was monitored with regular follow-up. She developed persistent watery diarrhea which on further evaluation was found to have elevated VIP levels which led to suspecting a VIP-secreting tumor. After the excision of the tumor, her symptoms and VIP levels resolved. A similar conclusion was reported by Yeh et al. in their review of the literature,[4] suggesting surgical excision of the primary tumor may be essential.

CONCLUSION

Children with ganglioneuroblastoma, ganglioneuroma, neurofibroma, and pheochromocytoma in the adrenal area with chronic, watery diarrhea should undergo evaluation with VIP levels. MS neuroblastomas rarely have a VIP-secreting element. Curative surgery in resectable tumors with high VIP levels significantly aids in bringing down VIP levels and associated symptoms.
