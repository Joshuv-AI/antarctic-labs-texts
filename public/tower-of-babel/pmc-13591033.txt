The changing paradigm of spine motion preservation: A bibliometric analysis of dynamic posterior stabilization
Authors: Sahin Karalar, Mustafa Abdullah Ozdemir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Instrumented posterolateral or transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion has long been accepted as the gold standard surgical procedure for a broad spectrum of degenerative spinal pathologies, including symptomatic spinal stenosis, degenerative disk disease, and low-grade spondylolisthesis. Although these traditional rigid stabilization methods provide excellent radiological arthrodesis rates owing to titanium alloy rods and pedicle screws, they significantly disrupt the physiological biomechanics of the lumbar spine. By completely eliminating mobility at the treated segment, rigid structures impose non-physiological stress concentration on the unfused cephalad and caudal motion segments, leading to compensatory hypermobility. Consequently, adjacent segment disease (ASD), characterized by accelerated disk degeneration, facet arthropathy, and secondary instability, has emerged as one of the most substantial long-term clinical challenges, increasing the need for revision surgery and directly undermining patient satisfaction [1, 2].

To overcome these biomechanical drawbacks of rigid arthrodesis, the non-rigid, dynamic posterior spinal stabilization paradigm based on motion preservation has been proposed. The primary objective of these flexible systems, particularly pedicle screw-based dynamic rods (such as the Dynesys system) and semi-rigid configurations, is to share the mechanical load with the anterior column, restrict non-physiological pathways of motion, and simultaneously maintain a degree of segmental flexibility to protect vulnerable adjacent levels. Over the past quarter-century, a massive body of literature, encompassing biomechanical finite element analyses, prospective clinical cohorts, and long-term retrospective registries, has accumulated globally to evaluate the clinical efficacy, survivorship, and complication profiles of these dynamic implants [3, 4].

However, while clinical and bioengineering data regarding dynamic posterior stabilization expand rapidly, the overall academic trajectory, shifting research frontiers, and structural organization of this scientific domain still present a highly fragmented appearance. Individual clinical studies and institutional series frequently report conflicting results regarding device failures, screw loosening, and the long-term protection of the adjacent segment. To date, no comprehensive, quantitative analysis has been conducted to objectively map the global intellectual structure, historical evolutionary trends, and collaboration networks defining this specific field of spine surgery.

Bibliometric analysis and computational science mapping methods offer a powerful mathematical approach to fill this gap in the literature. By systematically processing large-scale bibliographic metadata, bibliometrics makes it possible to decode the chronological development of concepts, determine the most productive core journals, uncover pioneering institutional research clusters, and reveal emerging hotspots that will guide future clinical practices.

Accordingly, the present study aims to subject the global literature on dynamic posterior spinal stabilization to a comprehensive bibliometric and science mapping analysis, covering the period from its inception in 1999 to June 2026. By tracking author productivity, country collaborations, and the structural transformations of keywords, this research intends to provide spine surgeons, clinical researchers, and bioengineers with an objective reference guide detailing the history of the field, the current scientific consensus, and where next-generation motion preservation technologies are heading.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Database Search Strategy and Metadata Extraction

Our investigation utilized the National Library of Medicine (PubMed/MEDLINE) database to map the global academic footprint of dynamic posterior spinal stabilization. We executed the primary data extraction on June 07, 2026. It is important to note that the inclusion of the year 2026 represents a partial annual volume. PubMed was purposefully selected due to its stringent indexing protocols, highly curated Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), and unparalleled prestige within the clinical spine surgery community. Since our manuscript was conducted through a literature review, no application was made to the ethics committee. Ethical principles and values were adhered to throughout our manuscript.

Rather than relying on overly broad search parameters that inevitably demand extensive manual pruning, we designed a high-precision Boolean search string deployed directly within the database. This targeted bibliographic strategy combined specific MeSH terms with focused free-text keywords: (“Posterior Spinal Stabilization: [Mesh] OR “dynamic stabilization” OR “dynamic posterior stabilization” OR “flexible rod” OR “semi-rigid stabilization” OR “motion preservation spine”) AND (“lumbar spine” OR “pedicle screws”). To capture the complete evolutionary trajectory of these surgical innovations, the temporal window was opened at their clinical inception in 1999 and extended through June 2026. The search was explicitly restricted to English-language and peer-reviewed documents.

This initial database search yielded a total of 375 documents. Automation tools and database filters initially removed 15 records (e.g., non-English language, non-peer-reviewed). The remaining 360 records underwent rigorous title and abstract screening, leading to the exclusion of 18 documents that were off-topic or involved irrelevant populations. Subsequently, the full texts of the remaining 342 articles were comprehensively assessed for eligibility. Ten articles were further excluded due to irrelevant interventions (n=6) or missing essential bibliometric metadata (n=4). This multi-step filtration protocol resulted in a final, highly targeted dataset of 332 high-quality peer-reviewed documents perfectly aligned with our predefined clinical and biomechanical inclusion parameters. The study was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, and a flow diagram detailing this identification and selection process is provided (Fig. 1). Furthermore, unlike conventional reviews that restrict analysis to a singular clinical study design, this bibliometric mapping integrated clinical trials, retrospective cohort series, and bioengineering simulations to construct a multidimensional blueprint of the field. Bibliometric Analysis and Computational Science Mapping

After validating the dataset, we extracted the complete bibliographic metadata of the 332 curated documents from PubMed in raw text format and subsequently parsed them into R Studio (v4.3.2). Duplicate records were automatically identified and removed during this initial data parsing phase. We handled all downstream quantitative processing, social network architecture tracking, and multi-layered thematic visualizations through the open-source Bibliometrix package. Our computational pipeline specifically isolated dynamic trends in annual scientific production, identified core productive journals, and mapped out top-performing authors alongside their institutional affiliations. Furthermore, we constructed strategic thematic maps and chronological trend topic timelines derived from keyword co-occurrence frequencies. This network mapping approach allowed us to objectively decode historical conceptual shifts and delineate the current boundaries of motion-preservation research.

Chronological Trajectory and Annual Scientific Production Trends

The global literature shows that interest in dynamic posterior stabilization has expanded at a steady annual compounding growth rate of 5.27%. This trajectory underscores a long-term, continuous commitment within the spinal surgery community to explore non-rigid stabilization alternatives over the last quarter-century. However, a closer look at the actual timeline indicates that this academic evolution was far from a simple, linear climb. Instead, global publication volumes split into distinct historical chapters, marked by two very clear productivity surges (Fig. 2).

Annual scientific production and chronological growth trajectory. Dynamic timeline showcasing the annual volume of peer-reviewed publications from 1999 through June 2026. The trajectory highlights the early baseline inception phase, followed by two definitive research surges peaking sharply in 2010 and 2016, illustrating the field’s transition into a mature scientific plateau.

The first decade of this timeline, spanning from 1999 to 2008, represents an initial introduction and baseline testing phase. During these early years, annual volume remained modest but stable while spine surgeons focused on gathering real-world clinical data regarding first-generation flexible instrumentation architectures. This groundwork eventually sparked a massive wave of wider academic interest, leading directly to a sharp publication peak in 2010. This specific surge reflects a collective global push to scrutinize the medium-term safety profiles, precise clinical indications, and hardware survivorship of early pedicle screw-based flexible configurations.

Following a brief post-peak consolidation, the literature entered a second, even more pronounced acceleration that reached its highest point in 2016. This secondary spike signals a major conceptual evolution across the entire discipline. The academic conversation moved decisively past basic hardware safety trials, shifting instead toward comprehensive bioengineering validations, computational load-sharing optimizations, and direct comparative trials against traditional rigid fusion techniques. From 2017 through June 2026, scientific output stabilized into a highly mature, high-volume plateau. This sustained steady state demonstrates that dynamic stabilization has successfully outgrown its status as a debated, novel alternative to become a deeply integrated, specialized sub-discipline within modern spine surgery (Fig. 3).

The prominent footprint of these bioengineering journals confirms that dynamic stabilization research follows a uniquely cross-disciplinary path. The field relies just as heavily on mechanical stress modeling and finite element simulations as it does on traditional clinical registries and patient cohorts.

Chronological timeline tracking uncovers a profound, multi-decade paradigm shift across the literature (Fig. 8). The early era of dynamic stabilization research focused heavily on basic hardware safety parameters, initial clinical cohort validations, and general complication tracking. During this baseline phase, keywords like “Dynesys,” “pedicle screw loosening,” “device failure,” and “short-term outcomes” dominated the academic landscape. These terms reflect a community primarily concerned with the immediate structural integrity and safety profiles of non-rigid implants.

DISCUSSION

The chronological trajectory of dynamic posterior stabilization literature over the last 27 years challenges the traditional assumption of linear scientific progress. Instead, our bibliometric analysis captures a distinct bimodal distribution in publication volume, revealing two major historical surges in 2010 and 2016. These peaks are not statistical anomalies; rather, they directly correspond to critical clinical trial completions, hardware safety transitions, and fundamental shifts in spinal surgical philosophy. The initial baseline phase spanning from 1999 to 2008 represents the early clinical introduction of pedicle-screw-based non-rigid instrumentation, dominated almost exclusively by early European data on the Dynesys system [5]. During this decade, the scientific community focused heavily on technical feasibility and short-term safety profiling. However, as these early patient cohorts transitioned past the 5-year post-operative mark, a critical clinical threshold was reached, triggering the massive publication surge observed in 2010.

This initial peak represents a collective global push to address unexpected intermediate-term challenges that began appearing in registries worldwide. Spine surgeons and bioengineers utilized this period to scrutinize specific clinical issues, most notably pedicle screw loosening at the rigid-flexible interface, late implant failures, and the true incidence of hardware-related complications [6]. The dense scientific output of 2010 effectively established the definitive baseline indications and patient selection criteria for first-generation dynamic constructs, moving the field past uncritical enthusiasm and into a phase of rigorous safety validation. Following a temporary post-peak stabilization, the secondary and more pronounced scientific surge in 2016 highlights an entirely different conceptual frontier. By 2016, the spinal surgery paradigm had fundamentally evolved from isolating hardware safety to analyzing holistic spinopelvic parameters and long-term adjacent segment degeneration (ASD) [7]. Caster-like or rigid fusion setups were increasingly criticized for accelerating mechanical breakdown at adjacent levels, driving the collective consensus toward semi-rigid configurations and hybrid constructs that combine rigid fusion with dynamic topping-off systems [8].

The 2016 peak marks the historical moment where dynamic posterior stabilization ceased to be viewed merely as a speculative alternative to fusion. Instead, it became deeply integrated with advanced bioengineering modeling. The focus shifted away from descriptive clinical case series toward multi-center comparative cohorts, rigorous patient-reported outcome measures like the Oswestry Disability Index, and precise computational validations [9]. The steady high-volume plateau maintained from 2017 through mid-2026 confirms that the discipline has reached full scientific maturity, securely positioned as a specialized, permanent arm of modern reconstructive spinal surgery. A compelling finding within this evolutionary timeline is the significant quantitative and qualitative contribution of the pioneering Turkish surgical school in shaping the global dynamic stabilization landscape. Led by prominent figures such as Ozer AF, Oktenoglu T, and Sasani M, this research node has maintained a notable impact in total publication volume, establishing a prominent output over the motion-preservation literature. The academic impact of this group extends far beyond local citation indexes, serving as foundational bedrock for contemporary non-rigid clinical protocols.

The historical impact of the Turkish school is deeply rooted in their early establishment of meticulously tracked, long-term prospective clinical registries. While many international centers abandoned flexible instrumentation during the turbulent post-2010 consolidation phase due to mixed short-term outcomes, the Turkish pioneers persisted. They provided the international community with critical, high-volume longitudinal data that proved the multi-decade safety, implant survivorship, and low revision rates of pedicle-based dynamic systems when applied with strict inclusion criteria [10]. Furthermore, our geographical network analysis highlights that this productivity was successfully projected worldwide through a robust, structurally dense transatlantic collaboration axis. This long-standing intellectual pipeline directly connected Turkish clinical hubs with elite North American spine centers. This collaborative framework was instrumental in validating the clinical efficacy of dynamic stabilization across diverse patient populations, standardizing patient-reported outcome metrics globally, and providing the western orthopedic and neurosurgical communities with a blueprint for motion-preservation surgery.

While the foundational Turkish-American axis securely anchors the clinical registry and patient cohort data of the literature, our strategic mapping captures a major contemporary shift driven by East Asian academic centers. Over the past decade, a high-density research surge coming out of China, Taiwan, and South Korea has significantly accelerated the field’s scientific volume [11, 12]. Crucially, this regional expansion introduces a distinct methodological transformation that marks the modern era of spine research. In contrast to the clinically oriented registries of the Mediterranean and North American pioneers, contemporary East Asian investigators focus intensely on advanced computer simulations, mathematical structural tracking, and bioengineering modeling. This surge has integrated sophisticated FEA directly into the heart of the dynamic stabilization discourse. East Asian research groups have successfully utilized multi-layered computational spine models to map out precise load-sharing configurations, predict the exact mechanical behavior of semi-rigid rods under multi-axial stress, and evaluate the biomechanical performance of hybrid constructs [11, 12]. The clinical translation of these computational findings is critical; modern FEA models directly assist practicing spine surgeons by predicting stress distributions and allowing for the customization of screw-rod interfaces to individual patient alignment before surgery.

This thematic diversification has effectively bridged the historical gap between pure benchtop bioengineering innovation and real-world clinical spine surgery. By providing predictive, mathematical data on stress distributions at the bone-screw interface and across adjacent segments, these modern computational studies have provided a solid mechanical rationale for the long-term clinical successes originally reported by the Turkish surgical cohorts. Consequently, contemporary literature has evolved into a highly balanced, interdisciplinary domain where surgical execution is continuously optimized by computational engineering insight. In sharp contrast to the sustained growth of pedicle-based dynamic systems and computational modeling, our strategic thematic clustering successfully identifies concepts that have drifted into declining quadrants. Most notably, complex posterior element reconstructions, specifically total facet arthroplasty, have experienced a steep drop in publication density, keyword centrality, and overall academic interest over the last decade [13].

This systematic decline reflects a broader, profound clinical consensus shift away from highly invasive, technically demanding motion-preserving hardware. Total facet arthroplasty devices were originally designed to completely replace degenerated posterior structures while maintaining segmental motion. However, long-term clinical tracking revealed prohibitive complication profiles, including high rates of device migration, early component loosening, persistent posterior column pain, and technically catastrophic revision scenarios [14]. The clear marginalization of facet arthroplasty keywords within our strategic mapping documents a collective retreat by the scientific community. The literature demonstrates that researchers and device manufacturers have largely abandoned extensive posterior arch replacements, redirecting the scientific focus back toward pedicle-based dynamic configurations and semi-rigid rod architectures. These systems achieve the desired goals of segment stabilization, load-sharing optimization, and ASD protection without destroying the underlying posterior spinal anatomy, ensuring a safer and more reproducible surgical profile.

Finally, this study has certain methodological limitations that must be acknowledged. The bibliometric analysis was conducted exclusively using the PubMed/MEDLINE database. While PubMed is widely regarded as a rigorous and definitive database for clinical spine surgery literature, the exclusion of other major databases such as Scopus or Web of Science may have resulted in the omission of broader multidisciplinary citation networks and certain bioengineering journals. Future mapping analyses incorporating multiple databases could provide an even more expansive view of the global literature.

Conclusion

This study establishes a comprehensive science mapping blueprint of the dynamic posterior spinal stabilization literature from 1999 to June 2026. The global landscape has successfully transitioned from basic hardware safety reporting to a sophisticated, engineering-driven computational discipline centered on finite element modeling and sagittal balance customization.

Driven by the prominent collaborative research axes connecting Turkish pioneers and East Asian cohorts to North American spine centers, pedicle screw-based dynamic and semi-rigid posterior instrumentation remains a highly active research field. Moving forward, integrating advanced computational simulations with long-term prospective patient registries will dictate the development of next-generation motion-preserving spinal implants.
