The Intrinsic Riemannian Proximal Gradient Method for Nonconvex Optimization
Ronny Bergmann, Hajg Jasa, Paula John, Max Pfeffer

Abstract
. We consider the proximal gradient method on Riemannian manifolds for functions that are possibly not geodesically convex. Starting from the forward-backward-splitting, we define an intrinsic variant of the proximal gradient method that uses proximal maps defined on the manifold and therefore does not require or work in the embedding. We investigate its convergence properties and illustrate its numerical performance, particularly for nonconvex or nonembedded problems that are hence out of reach for other methods.

1.
Introduction

Nonsmooth optimization on Riemannian manifolds has recently gained considerable interest.
Starting with inaugural algorithms like the subgradient method
[
23
]
and the proximal point algorithm
[
24
]
, introducing more properties of either the objective or the manifold has led to the book
[
6
]
.
For methods involving a bundle or approaches that involve a bundle of subgradients, see
[
27
,
12
]
.
Especially the proximal bundle method from
[
27
]

is one of the first methods to investigate the non-convex case.

A prominent approach to nonsmooth optimization are splitting methods, where the objective function
can be split. For a Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
, we consider the problem

(1)

\argmin_{p\in\mathcal{M}}f(p),\qquad\text{where}\quad f(p)=g(p)+h(p).

Some, like the cyclic proximal point algorithm (CPPA)
[
5
]
involve an arbitrary number of summands, while others also work for various splittings, e. g., convex-concave-splittings or difference of convex (DC) problems
[
37
,
4
,
11
]
.

In the case with two functions, as in
Equation

1
, the goal is to exploit properties of
g
and
h
that allow for an efficient minimization. For example when both have “cheap” proximal maps, the Douglas-Rachford algorithm
[
15
]
can be used also on Hadamard manifolds.
Another prominent algorithm is the so-called Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM)
[
25
]
, for which variants that work on manifolds were, e. g., considered in
[
31
,
33
]
.
Finally, together with an investigation of Fenchel duality,
[
36
,
35
]
allows for the so-called Chambolle-Pock algorithm
[
13
]
to be defined on manifolds, also involving proximal maps.

A prominent algorithm that is based on splitting is the proximal-gradient method, see
[
7
, Chapter 10]
for a comprehensive overview in the Euclidean case.
In the Euclidean case,
g
is assumed to be (
L_{g}
-)smooth and
h
to be convex and potentially nonsmooth.
If
h
has a proximal map that is fast to evaluate, maybe even in closed form, the algorithm is efficient, and a convergence rate can be derived.

To the best of our knowledge, the authors in
[
18
]
first introduced a partially convex
proximal-gradient version for the Stiefel manifold, phrasing the nonsmooth part as a constrained problem in the embedding, and requiring the nonsmooth function to be the restriction of a convex function in the embedding.
This was generalized to general Riemannian manifolds in
[
29
]
.
In this work, the authors also phrase the nonsmooth subproblem as a problem in the tangent space at the current iterate, but forgo convexity assumptions in their general analysis.
The same authors generalize their framework to an inexact version in
[
30
]
and still keep their convergence analysis from the previous paper.
The linear convergence of
[
18
]
and
[
29
]
under strong convexity has been studied in
[
19
]
.

The authors in
[
42
]
rephrase the nonsmooth subproblem as a trust-region problem.
All these works are based on a “generalized notion of the proximal map”
[
29
]
,
and solving the corresponding subproblem either in the embedding or the tangent space of the current iterate.
Most of these works assume
g
and
h
fulfil some convexity property, either along geodesics or along curves generated by retractions when using a retraction-based generalization of the proximal subproblem.
In the work of
[
29
]
, a “retraction smoothness” of
g
is required, but
h
can be nonsmooth and nonconvex for their convergence investigations.
However, their algorithm employs the embedding in a way that
h
is required to have certain properties thereon in order to solve their approximation to the proximal map.
In
[
22
]
the authors do employ a proximal map, but restrict their investigation to Hadamard manifolds and employing the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz property on manifolds.
In a related work,
[
34
]
consider gradient schemes that combine approximate solutions to proximal maps defined on the manifold, leading to a globally accelerated gradient descent algorithm on Hadamard manifolds.

In this work, we introduce a formulation of the Riemannian proximal gradient method that actually employs proximal maps for the nonsmooth step.
This relates the new proximal gradient method directly back to both the PPA
[
24
]
as well as gradient descent.
This new algorithm can still be generalized to a corresponding retraction-based proximal map,
which, similarly to
[
29
]
, can be understood as an approximate variant.
This new formulation allows to investigate several properties from
[
7
, Chapter 10]
on manifolds, for the case where neither the smooth part
g
nor the nonsmooth part
h
are convex.
The aim is to derive a convergence theory that is in accordance with
[
29
]
also for our intrinsic variant, that is, it is also based on intrinsic properties that
g
and
h
have as functions defined on the manifold.
A major difference to
[
29
]
is that all these investigations
will be done intrinsically.
This especially allows using Riemannian proximal maps defined intrinsically on the manifold, such as the one introduced in, e. g.,
[
24
]
, instead of Euclidean-flavored approximations thereof.
Furthermore, this approach can directly be applied also to manifolds that are
not embedded, like for example quotient manifolds. We show two main results:

•

In
Theorem

4.9
, we show sublinear convergence to
\varepsilon
-stationary points, with rate
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{k})
.

•

In
Theorem

4.10
, we show that any accumulation point
p^{\ast}
of the sequence of iterates is a stationary point of
f
, i. e.,
0_{p^{\ast}}\in\partial f(p^{\ast})
.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
After introducing the necessary notation and some preliminary properties in
Section

2
, we derive the new formulation of the nonconvex proximal gradient method in
Section

3
.
In
Section

4
, we discuss convergence properties of our proposed algorithm.
We introduce a generalization of the algorithm that uses retractions and employs a retraction based distance and proximal map in
Section

5
.

Section

6
illustrates the performance of the algorithm and its convergence, and
Section

7
concludes the paper.

Preliminaries

In this section, we recall some notions from Riemannian geometry.
For more details, the reader may wish to consult
[
20
,
16
]
.
Let
\mathcal{M}
be a smooth manifold with Riemannian metric
(\cdot\,,\,\cdot)
and let
D
be its Levi-Civita connection.
Given
p,q\in\mathcal{M}
, we denote by
C_{pq}
the set of all piecewise smooth curves
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to\mathcal{M}
from
p=\gamma(0)
to
q=\gamma(1)
.
The
arc length
of
\gamma\in C_{pq}
,
L\colon C_{pq}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is defined as

L(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}\lVert\dot{\gamma}\rVert_{\gamma(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{p}
is the norm induced by the Riemannian metric in the tangent space
\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}
at the point
p
.
The inner product given by the Riemannian metric at
p
is
(\cdot\,,\,\cdot)_{p}\colon\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
.
The explicit reference to the base point will be omitted whenever the base point is clear from context.
The sectional curvature at
p\in\mathcal{M}
is denoted by
K_{p}(X_{p},Y_{p})
for tangent vectors
X_{p},Y_{p}\in\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}
.

The Riemannian distance between two points,
d\colon\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{+\infty\}
, is given by

d(p,q)=\inf_{\gamma\in C_{pq}}L(\gamma).

A
geodesic arc
from
p\in\mathcal{M}
to
q\in\mathcal{M}
is a smooth curve
\gamma\in C_{pq}
that is parallel along itself, i. e.,
\textup{D}_{\dot{\gamma}(t)}\dot{\gamma}(t)=0
for all
t\in(0,1)
.
A finite-length geodesic arc is
minimal
if its arc length coincides with the Riemannian distance between its extreme points.
Given two points
p,q\in\mathcal{M}
, we denote with
{\gamma}_{p,q}
a minimal geodesic arc that connects
p={\gamma}_{p,q}(0)
to
q={\gamma}_{p,q}(1)
.
Given a point
p\in\mathcal{M}
and a tangent vector
X_{p}\in\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}
, a geodesic can also be specified
as the curve
{\gamma}_{p,X_{p}}
such that
{\gamma}_{p,X_{p}}(0)=p
and
{\gamma}_{p,X_{p}}^{\prime}(0)=X_{p}
.
The
exponential map
at
p\in\mathcal{M}
maps a tangent vector
X_{p}\in\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}
to the endpoint of the corresponding geodesic,
\expOp_{p}(X_{p})={\gamma}_{p,X_{p}}(1)
.
If it exists, the inverse map is called the
logarithmic map

\logOp_{p}\colon\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}
.
We call a set
uniquely geodesic
if every two points in that space are connected by one and only one geodesic in that set.
In this case the logarithmic map exists everywhere.
A set
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
is said to be
(uniquely) geodesically convex
if for any
p,q\in\mathcal{U}
, there exists a (unique) minimal geodesic arc
{\gamma}_{p,q}
which lies entirely in
\mathcal{U}
.
Observe that if a manifold
\mathcal{M}
has positive sectional curvature, the set
\mathcal{U}
may not be allowed to have arbitrarily large diameter.
For example, if
{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}}>0
, for
\mathcal{U}
to be uniquely geodesically convex, its diameter must be bounded by
\diam(\mathcal{U})<\pi/\sqrt{{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}}}
.
See, e. g.,
[
38
, Proposition 4.1]
or
[
17
, Proposition II.1.4]
.

We denote the
parallel transport
from
p
to
q
along
{\gamma}_{p,q}
with respect to the connection
D
by
\textup{P}_{q\leftarrow p}\colon\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{T}_{q}\mathcal{M}
.
This is defined by

\textup{P}_{q\leftarrow p}Y_{p}=X_{q}

for

Y_{p}\in\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M},

where
X
is the unique smooth vector field along
{\gamma}_{p,q}
which satisfies

X_{p}=Y_{p}\text{ and }\textup{D}_{\dot{\gamma}}{X}=0.

Notice that
\textup{P}_{q\leftarrow p}
depends on the choice of
{\gamma}_{p,q}
.

Given a differentiable function
f\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
, we denote the
Riemannian gradient
of
f
at
p\in\mathcal{M}
by
\grad f(p)\in\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}
.
See
[
16
, Definition 8.57]
for more details.
Given a uniquely geodesic set
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
and a real number
L_{f}>0
, the function
f
is said to be
L_{f}
-smooth
over
\mathcal{U}
if, for any
p,q\in\mathcal{U}
,

\mathopen{}\left\lVert\grad f(p)-\textup{P}_{p\leftarrow q}\grad f(q)\mathclose{}\right\rVert\leq L_{f}d(p,q);

see, e. g.,
[
41
, Definition 4]
, cf.
[
16
, Definition 10.44]
.
Furthermore, it can be shown that, if
f
is
L_{f}
-smooth over
\mathcal{U}
, then

f(q)\leq f(p)+(\grad f(p)\,,\,\logOp_{p}(q))+\frac{L_{f}}{2}d^{2}(p,q),

for any
p,q\in\mathcal{U}
; cf.
[
16
, Proposition 10.53]
.
Given a real number
L\geq 0
, a function
f
is
L
-Lipschitz if, for any
p,q\in\mathcal{M}
,

\lvert f(p)-f(q)\rvert\leq Ld(p,q).

Furthermore,
f
is
locally
Lipschitz if, for every
p\in\mathcal{M}
, there exists a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
p
such that
f\rvert_{\mathcal{U}}
is Lipschitz.
Let
f\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
be a locally Lipschitz function, and let
\hat{f}\coloneq f\circ\expOp\colon\mathcal{T}{}\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Then, the generalized Clarke directional derivative of
f
in the direction
X_{p}\in\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}
at
p\in\mathcal{M}
is defined as

(2)

f^{\circ}(p,X_{p})\coloneq\hat{f}^{\circ}(0_{p},X_{p})\coloneq\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y_{p}\to 0_{p}\\
t\downarrow 0\end{subarray}}\frac{f\circ\expOp_{p}(Y_{p}+tX_{p})-f\circ\expOp_{p}(0_{p})}{t}.

The generalized Clarke subdifferential of
f
at
p\in\mathcal{M}
is then defined as

(3)

\partial f(p)\coloneq\left\{X_{p}\in\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}\,\middle|\,f^{\circ}(p,Y_{p})\geq(X_{p}\,,\,Y_{p})\ \text{for all }Y_{p}\in\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}\right\}.

Let
f
be a locally Lipschitz function on
\mathcal{M}
and
p^{\ast}\in\mathcal{M}
a local minimizer of
f
, then

0_{p^{\ast}}\in\partial f(p^{\ast}),

see, e. g.,
[
40
, Proposition 3.3]
.
If now
f=g+h
with
g
of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}
and
h
locally Lipschitz, then

f^{\circ}(p,X_{p})=\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y_{p}\to 0_{p}\\
t\downarrow 0\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{t}\bigl(g\circ\expOp_{p}(Y_{p}+tX_{p})-g\circ\expOp_{p}(0_{p})\\
+h\circ\expOp_{p}(Y_{p}+tX_{p})-h\circ\expOp_{p}(0_{p})\bigr),

and since
\limsup(a_{n}+b_{n})\leq\limsup a_{n}+\limsup b_{n}
, we get

(4)

f^{\circ}(p,X_{p})=(g+h)^{\circ}(p,X_{p})\leq g^{\prime}(p,X_{p})+h^{\circ}(p,X_{p}),

where
g^{\prime}(p,X_{p})=(\grad g(p)\,,\,X_{p})
.
Since
h^{\circ}(p,X_{p})=((h+g)-g)^{\circ}(p,X_{p})
, applying
Equation

4
, we get

(5)

h^{\circ}(p,X_{p})+g^{\prime}(p,X_{p})\leq(h+g)^{\circ}(p,X_{p}).

Equation

4
and
Equation

5
now imply

(6)

(g+h)^{\circ}(p,X_{p})=g^{\prime}(p,X_{p})+h^{\circ}(p,X_{p}).

This and the definition of the Clarke subdifferential now give

(7)

\partial(g+h)(p)=\grad g(p)+\partial h(p)\quad\text{for all }p\in\mathcal{M}.

This is a non-Hadamard version of
[
10
, Lemma 3.1]
.
Therefore,
0\in\partial(g+h)(p)=\grad g(p)+\partial h(p)
is equivalent to the stationarity condition

(8)

-\grad g(p)\in\partial h(p).

Given a function
f
and a real number
\lambda>0
, we introduce the proximal map as

\proxOp_{\lambda f}(p)\coloneq\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{M}}f(q)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}d^{2}(p,q),

if the minimizer exists.
Note that, as opposed to other works, the minimum is taken
on the manifold
.

In the analysis of the proximal gradient method, we will also need the following curvature-dependent quantities.
Given real numbers
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}
, which will typically be lower and upper bounds to the sectional curvature of
\mathcal{M}
, and
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the following quantities, see also
[
32
, Definition 3.8]
:

(9)

\displaystyle\zeta_{1,\kappa_{1}}(s)

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\kappa_{1}\geq 0,\\
\sqrt{-\kappa_{1}}\,s\coth(\sqrt{-\kappa_{1}}\,s)\quad&\text{if }\kappa_{1}<0,\end{cases}

(10)

\displaystyle\zeta_{2,\kappa_{2}}(s)

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\kappa_{2}\leq 0,\\
\sqrt{\kappa_{2}}\,s\cot(\sqrt{\kappa_{2}}\,s)\quad&\text{if }\kappa_{2}>0,\end{cases}

(11)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}}(s)

\displaystyle\coloneq\max\bigl\{\zeta_{1,\kappa_{1}}(s),\;\lvert\zeta_{2,\kappa_{2}}(s)\rvert\bigr\}.

(12)

π

κ

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{cases}\infty&\text{if }\kappa\leq 0,\\
\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\kappa}}&\text{if }\kappa>0.\end{cases}

3.
The Riemannian Proximal Gradient Method

To derive
the proximal gradient method, the function
g
is replaced by a proximal regularization of its linearization; see
[
8
, Sec. 2.2]
for the derivation in the Euclidean case.
For
\lambda>0
and a point
p
we compute a new candidate as

\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{M}}g(p)+(\grad g(p)\,,\,\logOp_{p}q)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}d^{2}(p,q)+h(q).

We introduce
r(p)\coloneq\frac{1}{2\lambda}d^{2}(p,q)
and Taylor expand this function (cf.
[
16
, Sec. 4.8]
):

\begin{split}r(\expOp_{p}(X_{p}))&=r(p)+(\grad r(p)\,,\,X_{p})+\mathcal{O}(\lVert X_{p}\rVert^{2}),\\
&=r(p)+-\frac{1}{\lambda}(\logOp_{p}q\,,\,X_{p})+\mathcal{O}(\lVert X_{p}\rVert^{2}),\end{split}

where
X_{p}\in\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}
.
For the case
X_{p}=-\lambda\grad g(p)
this reads

\frac{1}{2\lambda}d^{2}(\expOp_{p}(-\lambda\grad g(p)),q)=\frac{1}{2\lambda}d^{2}(p,q)+(\logOp_{p}q\,,\,\grad g(p))+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2}),

using this as a second quadratic approximation we obtain the new candidate as

\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{M}}g(p)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}d^{2}(\expOp_{p}(-\lambda\grad g(p)),q)+h(q).

Since the first term
g(p)
does not change the minimizer, the resulting expression is equal to the proximal map

\proxOp_{\lambda h}\bigl(\expOp_{p}(-\lambda\grad g(p))\bigr)=\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{1}{2\lambda}d^{2}(\expOp_{p}(-\lambda\grad g(p)),q)+h(q).

In this article, we consider the general case where the function
h
or the Riemannian distance are not necessarily geodesically convex.
Then, the proximal map
\proxOp_{\lambda h}
might not be single-valued, and the iteration might not be well-defined.
In this case, inspired by
[
29
, Equation (3.1)]
, we only demand finding a stationary point
p^{\ast}
of the function

(13)

H(q)\coloneq h(q)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}d^{2}\left(q,\expOp_{p}\bigl(-\lambda\grad g(p)\bigr)\right),

s. t.
H(p^{\ast})\leq H(p)
.
We can thus state the nonconvex Riemannian Proximal Gradient method (NCRPG) in
Algorithm

1
.

1:

g
,
\grad g
,

,
\proxOp_{\lambda h}
, a sequence
\lambda^{(k)}
, an initial point
p^{(0)}\in\mathcal{M}
.

2:

while
convergence criterion is not fulfilled
do

3:

Find a stationary point
p^{(k+1)}
of
H
, s.t.
H(p^{(k+1)})\leq H(p^{(k)})
.

4:

Set
k\coloneq k+1

5:

end

while

4.
Convergence

In this section, we analyze the convergence behavior of the nonconvex Riemannian proximal gradient (NCRPG) method from
Algorithm

1
.
We will assume the curvature of the manifold to be bounded, and no convexity assumptions are made for the functions
g
and
h
.

The following assumptions are made in this section:

Assumption 4.1
.

(1)

There exist two numbers
{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}},{\hyperlink{def:k-max}{\kappa_{\mathrm{u}}}}\in\mathbb{R}

{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}}\leq K_{p}(X_{p},X_{p})\leq{\hyperlink{def:k-max}{\kappa_{\mathrm{u}}}}
for all
p\in\mathcal{M}
and all unit vectors
X_{p}\in\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}
.

(2)

For every starting point
p^{(0)}\in\mathcal{M}
the level set

{\hyperlink{def:level-set}{\mathcal{L}_{p^{(0)}}}}\coloneq\left\{p\in\mathcal{M}\,\middle|\,f(p)\leq f(p^{(0)})\right\}

is compact in
\mathcal{M}
.

(3)

g\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable and
L

g

-smooth on
L

p

(
0
)

.

(4)

h\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
is locally Lipschitz continuous and lower bounded.

(5)

In each step we find a stationary point
p^{(k+1)}\in\mathcal{M}
of

H(q)\coloneq h(q)+\frac{1}{2\lambda^{(k)}}d^{2}\Bigl(q,\expOp_{p^{(k)}}\bigl(-\lambda^{(k)}\grad g(p^{(k)})\bigr)\Bigr),

s. t.
H(p^{(k+1)})\leq H(p^{(k)})
.

Remark 4.2
.

Note that since our level set is compact and our objective function

is continuous, it is lower bounded by

.
Furthermore, since

and

are continuous we obtain bounds

such that

{\hyperlink{def:h-lower-bound}{\alpha_{1}}}\leq h(q)\leq{\hyperlink{def:h-upper-bound}{\alpha_{2}}}\text{ for all }q\in{\hyperlink{def:level-set}{\mathcal{L}_{p^{(0)}}}},\text{ and }\lVert\grad g(q)\rVert\leq{\hyperlink{def:gradg-upper-bound}{\alpha_{g}}}\text{ for all }q\in{\hyperlink{def:level-set}{\mathcal{L}_{p^{(0)}}}}.

Let

and

.
We introduce the iteration map
{\mathrm{T}}^{{g},\,{h}}_{{\lambda}}\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
that to each point

assigns a stationary point
{\hyperlink{def:iteration-map}{\mathrm{T}}}^{{g},\,{h}}_{{\lambda}}{\mathopen{}\left(p\mathclose{}\right)}\in\mathcal{M}
of the function

defined in

.
Note that, if the proximal map of

{\hyperlink{def:iteration-map}{\mathrm{T}}}^{{g},\,{h}}_{{\lambda}}{\mathopen{}\left(p\mathclose{}\right)}=\proxOp_{\lambda h}\Bigl(\expOp_{p}\bigl(-\lambda\grad g(p)\bigr)\Bigr)\in\mathcal{M}.

Furthermore, we introduce the gradient map
{\mathrm{G}}^{{g},\,{h}}_{{\lambda}}\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{T}{}\mathcal{M}
defined by

{\hyperlink{def:gradient-map}{\mathrm{G}}}^{{g},\,{h}}_{{\lambda}}{\mathopen{}\left(p\mathclose{}\right)}\coloneq-\frac{1}{\lambda}\logOp_{p}\bigl({\hyperlink{def:iteration-map}{\mathrm{T}}}^{{g},\,{h}}_{{\lambda}}{\mathopen{}\left(p\mathclose{}\right)}\bigr)\in\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}.

Whenever

4.1.
Sufficient decrease

This first step towards a convergence result is to show a sufficient decrease of the cost function. For this, we need the following technical lemma, that provides a curvature-dependent upper bound on the distance between the points
p
and
{\hyperlink{def:iteration-map}{\mathrm{T}}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)}
and ensures the strict positivity of
\zeta_{2,{\hyperlink{def:k-max}{\kappa_{\mathrm{u}}}}}
within the level set
L

p

(
0
)

.
Notably, in case
{\hyperlink{def:k-max}{\kappa_{\mathrm{u}}}}>0
, this lemma is crucial as it ensures that the gradient step
{\hyperlink{def:gradient-step}{z(p)}}=\expOp_{p}(-\lambda\grad g(p))
remains within a bounded distance from
p
and from
{\hyperlink{def:iteration-map}{\mathrm{T}}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)}
that is small enough to ensure that the logarithmic map, and hence also the gradient mapping
{\hyperlink{def:gradient-map}{\mathrm{G}}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)}
, is defined whenever it is needed.

Lemma 4.3
.

5.
A Riemannian proximal gradient method using retractions

Often, we do not have access to a closed form of the exponential map

\expOp_{p}{X}
or its inverse, the logarithmic map. In other cases, computing these maps is simply too costly. For first order convergence results, it is often enough to consider first order approximations of the exponential map, so called
retractions
, and their inverses. In
[
29
]
, the authors have shown convergence of their algorithm also for such retractions. This alters the proximal problem on the tangent space but it does not directly affect the algorithm itself. In this section, we will discuss the use of retractions in the NCRPG method and conclude that convergence theory remains largely an open problem.

A retraction on a Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
is a first-order approximation of the exponential map

\mathrm{R}\colon\mathcal{T}{}\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M},(p,X_{p})\mapsto\mathrm{R}_{p}(X_{p}),

such that each curve
c(t)=\mathrm{R}_{p}(tX_{p})
satisfies
c(0)=p
and
c^{\prime}(0)=X_{p}
.
Similarly, the inverse retraction
\mathrm{R}^{-1}_{p}(q)
is a first order approximation of the logarithmic map
\logOp_{p}q
.
Note that analogously to the logarithmic map, both the retraction and the inverse retraction might only be locally defined around
p
.

We can use these approximations to consider both an approximation of the Riemannian distance
d
and the proximal map
\proxOp_{\lambda f}
.
We denote

d^{\mathrm{R}}(p,q)\coloneq\lVert\mathrm{R}^{-1}_{p}(q)\rVert_{p}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\proxOp_{\lambda f}^{\mathrm{R}}{(p)}\coloneq\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{1}{2\lambda}d^{\mathrm{R}}(q,p)^{2}+f(q),

for a given retraction
\mathrm{R}
, respectively. We note that the retraction distance
d^{\mathrm{R}}
is not symmetric but both approximations
d^{\mathrm{R}}(p,q)\approx d(p,q)\approx d^{\mathrm{R}}(q,p)
are equally valid. We have chosen the latter in the retraction-based proximal map, as it leads to nicer expressions.

We can then replace the minimization of
Equation

13
by a retraction-based proximal map.

(31)

\displaystyle\proxOp_{\lambda h}^{\mathrm{R}}{\bigl(\mathrm{R}_{p}(-\lambda\grad g(p))\bigr)}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{1}{2\lambda}\Bigl(d^{\mathrm{R}}\bigl(q,\mathrm{R}_{p}(-\lambda\grad g(p))\bigr)\Bigr)^{2}+h(q)

(32)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{1}{2\lambda}\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathrm{R}^{-1}_{q}\Bigl(\mathrm{R}_{p}\bigl(-\lambda\grad g(p)\bigr)\Bigr)\mathclose{}\right\rVert^{2}+h(q).

Or, as above, by finding a stationary point
p^{\ast}
of the objective function

(33)

H^{\mathrm{R}}(q)\coloneq h(q)+\frac{1}{2\lambda^{(k)}}\left(d^{\mathrm{R}}\Bigl(q,\mathrm{R}_{p^{(k)}}\left(-\lambda^{(k)}\grad g(p^{(k)})\right)\Bigr)\right)^{2},

which fulfills

The main problem with obtaining a convergence theory of this method - at least using the considerations above - is that

6.1.
Sparse PCA

The first example is Sparse PCA. Here, we consider the following optimization problem:

(36)

\min_{X\in\text{OB}(n,r)}f(X)=\frac{1}{2}\|X^{\top}A^{\top}AX-D^{2}\|^{2}+\mu\lVert X\rVert_{1},

where

is a given data matrix,
D
contains the
r

on the diagonal,
\mu\geq 0
is a weighting parameter, and
\text{OB}(n,r)=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is the

of matrices with normalized columns. Hence, the goal to diagonalize

as much as possible using normalized components that are forced to be sparse by the
\ell_{1}
-penalty. We note that there are alternative formulations of the Sparse PCA problem on the Stiefel manifold that we do not consider here.

Soft Thresholding on the Sphere

The main advantage of our algorithm in this case is that the prox-problem can be efficiently solved using a fixed-point iteration, as we will show in the following. Since the Oblique manifold is a product of spheres, the prox-problem decomposes and it is sufficient to solve it on the sphere. Thus, we aim to solve

(37)

\argmin_{y\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\phi(y)\coloneq\frac{1}{2}d(x,y)^{2}+\xi\lVert y\rVert_{1},

where
x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
,
\xi\geq 0
, and
d(x,y)=\arccos(\langle x,y\rangle)
is the Riemannian distance.

We will now show that the minimum
\bar{y}=\argmin_{y\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\phi(y)
is attained at
\bar{y}=p_{x}(\bar{t})
and
\bar{t}
is a fixed point
\bar{t}=\sigma(p_{x}(\bar{t}))
where

(38)

\sigma(y)\coloneq\xi\frac{\sqrt{1-\langle x,{y}\rangle^{2}}}{\arccos\left(\langle x,y\rangle\right)},\qquad\text{and}\qquad p_{x}(t)\coloneq\frac{\proxOp_{t\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1}}^{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}(x)}{\lVert\proxOp_{t\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1}}^{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}(x)\rVert_{2}}

is the normalized Euclidean proximal operator. Then,

\bar{y}
can be computed in linear time using Banach’s fixed point theorem.

Notice that, since

\displaystyle=-\frac{\arccos\left(\langle x,{\bar{y}}\rangle\right)}{\|\projOp_{\mathcal{T}_{\bar{y}}\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}(x)\|_{2}}\projOp_{\mathcal{T}_{\bar{y}}\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}(x)+\xi\projOp_{\mathcal{T}_{\bar{y}}\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}(z),

With the projection onto the tangent space at

.
In summary, we obtain

x_{i}-\sigma(\bar{y})z_{i}=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\mathopen{}\left\lvert x_{i}\mathclose{}\right\rvert\leq\sigma(\bar{y}),\\
x_{i}-\sigma(\bar{y})\sgn(x_{i})&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

i. e.,
x-\sigma(\bar{y})z=\proxOp_{\sigma(\bar{y})\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1}}^{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}(x)
.
Now, since
\lVert\bar{y}\rVert_{2}=1
and
\langle{x-\sigma(\bar{y})z},{\bar{y}}\rangle>0
, we have

(42)

\bar{y}=\frac{x-\sigma(\bar{y})z}{\lVert x-\sigma(\bar{y})z\rVert_{2}}=p_{x}(\sigma(\bar{y})).

In particular this implies that
\bar{t}=\sigma(\bar{y})
is a fixed point of
\sigma(p_{x}(t))
.
∎

With the above theorem, we can conclude that we are looking for a fixed point of
\sigma(p_{x}(t))
. We will now show that this fixed point can be computed using Banach’s fixed-point theorem.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and

(43)

0<\xi<\min\left\{\frac{\pi}{8\sqrt{\lVert x\rVert_{0}}}\left(\sqrt{16\sqrt{\lVert x\rVert_{0}}\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}+\pi^{2}}-\pi\right),\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}\right\}.

Then
\sigma(p_{x}(t))\colon(0,\xi]\to(0,\xi]
has a unique fixed point
\bar{t}
, and for every
t_{0}\in(0,\xi]
the sequence
t_{k+1}\coloneq\sigma(p_{x}(t_{k}))
converges linearly to
\bar{t}
.

Proof.

Notice that, by our choice of
\xi
,
p_{x}(t)
is well-defined for all
t\in(0,\xi]
and
\sigma(p_{x}(t))\in(0,\xi]
.
We now show that
p_{x}(t)
is a contraction mapping.
Define
\psi(s)\coloneq\xi\frac{\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}{\arccos(s)}
for
s\in[0,1]
.
We then have

\sup_{s\in[0,1)}\psi^{\prime}(s)=\sup_{s\in[0,1)}\xi\frac{1-\frac{x\arccos(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}}{\arccos(x)^{2}}=\frac{4\xi}{\pi^{2}}.

Thus, for any
t_{1},t_{2}\in(0,\xi]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\left\lvert\sigma(p_{x}(t_{1}))-\sigma(p_{x}(t_{2}))\mathclose{}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\lvert\psi(\langle{p_{x}(t_{1})},{x}\rangle)-\psi(\langle{p_{x}(t_{2})},{x}\rangle)\mathclose{}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\xi}{\pi^{2}}\mathopen{}\left\lvert\langle{p_{x}(t_{1})},{x}\rangle-\langle{p_{x}(t_{2})},{x}\rangle\mathclose{}\right\rvert.

Since
p_{x}(t),x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, we have

\langle{p_{x}(t)},{x}\rangle=1-\frac{1}{2}\lVert p_{x}(t)-x\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Hence, for any
t_{1},t_{2}\in(0,\xi]
, we rewrite

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\left\lvert\langle{p_{x}(t_{1})},{x}\rangle-\langle{p_{x}(t_{2})},{x}\rangle\mathclose{}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\lvert\frac{1}{2}\lVert p_{x}(t_{1})-x\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\lVert p_{x}(t_{2})-x\rVert_{2}^{2}\mathclose{}\right\rvert.

W.l.o.g.,
\frac{1}{2}\lVert p_{x}(t_{1})-x\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\lVert p_{x}(t_{2})-x\rVert_{2}^{2}
.
We now use that
p_{x}(t_{1})
is a solution of

\argmin_{y\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\quad\frac{1}{2}\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}^{2}+t_{1}\lVert y\rVert_{1}.

A proof can be found in
[
30
, D.1]
.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\left\lvert\langle{p_{x}(t_{1})},{x}\rangle-\langle{p_{x}(t_{2})},{x}\rangle\mathclose{}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\lVert p_{x}(t_{1})-x\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\lVert p_{x}(t_{2})-x\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq t_{1}\left(\lVert p_{x}(t_{2})\rVert_{1}-\lVert p_{x}(t_{1})\rVert_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq t_{1}\lVert p_{x}(t_{2})-p_{x}(t_{1})\rVert_{2},

where we used the Lipschitz continuity of the
\ell_{1}
-norm with constant
1
for the last step.

Since
\lVert\proxOp_{t\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1}}^{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}(x)\rVert_{2}\geq\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}-\xi
for
t\leq\xi
, we get

\displaystyle\lVert p_{x}(t_{2})-p_{x}(t_{1})\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}-\xi}\lVert\proxOp_{t_{1}\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1}}^{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}(x)-\proxOp_{t_{2}\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1}}^{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}(x)\rVert

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}-\xi}\lVert\proxOp_{t_{1}\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1}}^{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}(x)-\proxOp_{t_{1}\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1}}^{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}(x+\bar{x}(t_{1}-t_{2}))\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}-\xi}\lVert x-(x+\bar{x}(t_{1}-t_{2}))\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\lVert x\rVert_{0}}}{\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}-\xi}(t_{1}-t_{2}),

where
\bar{x}
is defined as
\bar{x}_{i}\coloneq\sgn(x_{i})
and the second to last step uses the non-expansivity of the proximal operator of the
\ell_{1}
-norm, see, e. g.,
[
7
, Thm. 6.42]
.
All in all we get

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\left\lvert\sigma(p_{x}(t_{1}))-\sigma(p_{x}(t_{2}))\mathclose{}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\xi t_{1}\sqrt{\lVert x\rVert_{0}}}{(\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}-\xi)\pi^{2}}(t_{1}-t_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\xi^{2}\sqrt{\lVert x\rVert_{0}}}{(\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}-\xi)\pi^{2}}(t_{1}-t_{2}).

The upper bound on
\xi
in
Equation

43
ensures
\frac{4\xi^{2}\sqrt{\lVert x\rVert_{0}}}{(\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}-\xi)\pi^{2}}<1
.
Applying the Banach fixed-point theorem yields the stated result.
∎

Lemma

6.2
implies that, if additionally

(44)

\xi<\frac{\pi}{8\sqrt{\lVert x\rVert_{0}}}\left(\sqrt{16\sqrt{\lVert x\rVert_{0}}\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}+\pi^{2}}-\pi\right)

holds, the solution of
Equation

37
is unique and can be computed in linear time.
Since our method solves the proximal mapping with the parameter
\xi=\lambda^{(k)}\mu
, this bound is never exceeded in practice.

We tested the NCRPG method using a data matrix

, where

Experiments on the Real Grassmannian

The next experiment on the real Grassmannian is particular because here, we do not assume that the manifold is embedded.
While
[
29
]
also do not need embeddedness in their theory, they at least require that the total space of a quotient manifold is embedded (see
[
29
, Assumption 3.6]
), which, in the case of the Grassmannian, would be the Stiefel manifold.
Since they do not provide details on how their method would be implemented for the Grassmann manifold, we cannot compare to the RPG method in this case.

We aim to solve the optimization problem in
Equation

1
with

(46)

g(p)=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}d^{2}(p,q_{j}),\quad h(p)=\tau d(p,\bar{q}),

where
\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{N}\}\subset\mathcal{M}
is a set of
N=1000
randomly generated data points,
\bar{q}\in\mathcal{M}
is another fixed point,
\tau\coloneq\frac{1}{2}
is a weighting parameter, and
\mathcal{M}=\mathrm{Gr}(n,r)
is the Grassmann manifold of
r
-dimensional subspaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The function
g
represents a generalization of the sum of squared distances to data points, whose Riemannian gradient is
\grad g(p)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\logOp_{p}q_{j}
.
The function
h
acts as a regularization term encouraging proximity to the reference point
\bar{q}
, and its proximal mapping is given by

\proxOp_{\lambda h}(p)={\gamma}(\min\{\lambda,d(p,\bar{q})\};p\,,\,\bar{q}),

where
{\gamma}(\cdot;p\,,\,\bar{q})
is the unit-speed geodesic that starts from
p
in the direction of
\bar{q}
.
See, e. g.,
[
39
, Proposition 1]
and, e. g.,
[
9
]
for computational aspects and properties of the Grassmann manifold.

Implementation Details

The data points were generated by first choosing an anchor point
q_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
uniformly at random.
Then, we generated
N+2
normally distributed random tangent vectors to
q_{0}
with a standard deviation of
\sigma=1
, and mapped to
\mathcal{M}
using
\expOp_{q_{0}}
.
Each tangent vector was first scaled down to be of length strictly less than
\frac{{\hyperlink{def:generalized-sine-positive-zeros}{\pi_{\hyperlink{def:k-max}{\kappa_{\mathrm{u}}}}}}}{2}
.
This ensures that the data points thus generated are contained in a uniquely geodesically convex set
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{M}
.
Finally, we selected the first
N
points as the data points
\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{N}\}
for the function
g
, and the last two points as the reference point
\bar{q}
for
h
, and the initial point
p_{0}
of the optimization respectively.
Since
\mathcal{U}
is a uniquely geodesically convex set containing all data points
q_{j}
, the function
g
is
\zeta_{1,{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}}}(D)
-smooth over
\mathcal{U}
, where
D=\diam(\mathcal{U})
, see, e. g.,
[
3
, Lemma 2]
.
Hence, because
{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}}=0
on the Grassmannian, we can use the constant
{\hyperlink{def:gradg-lipschitz-constant}{L_{g}}}=\zeta_{1,{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}}}(D)=1
.

6.3.
Row-sparse Low-rank Matrix recovery

A common problem in signal recovery applications such as hyperspectral imaging
[
26
]
or blind deconvolution
[
21
]
is the task of recovering a signal
X\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
from a set of
m
observations that is simultaneously of low rank and admits a row-sparse structure.

Row-sparsity can be achieved by adding a
\ell_{1,2}
penalty to the cost function.
Instead of minimizing the nuclear norm, as is often done, we will assume that the rank
r
of the solution is known. We get an optimization problem stated over the manifold of rank
r
matrices
\mathcal{M}_{r}

[
21
]
:

(47)

\argmin_{X\in\mathcal{M}_{r}}\lVert\mathbb{A}(X)-y\rVert_{2}^{2}+\mu\lVert X\rVert_{1,2},

where
\mathbb{A}\colon\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a linear measurement operator that is often assumed to fulfill the
restricted isometry property (RIP)
.
The goal is to recover the (low-rank and row-sparse) signal
X
from as few measurements
y
as possible.

A convenient way to express a fixed rank matrix
X\in\mathcal{M}_{r}
is in the form of its compact SVD, i. e.,
X=U\Sigma V^{\top}
, where
U^{\top}U=V^{\top}V=I_{r}
and
\Sigma
is the diagonal matrix containing the singular values of
X
.
Using this notation, the tangent space at
X
can be expressed as
[
16
, (7.47)]

(48)

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}_{X}\mathcal{M}_{r}=\{UMV^{\top}+U_{p}V^{\top}+UV_{p}^{\top}\,|\,&M\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},U_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times r},V_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times r}\\
&\text{s.t. }U^{\top}U_{p}=0,V^{\top}V_{p}=0\}.\end{split}

The problem of low-rank and row-sparse matrix recovery does not fulfill all assumptions in
4.1
: The sectional curvature is bounded only locally and the level sets are not compact since their intersection with the manifold is not closed.
However, locally, this should not be a problem, which is why we will test only local convergence.

Furthermore, to our knowledge, there is no closed form for geodesics on the fixed-rank manifold. Consequently, the exponential and logarithmic maps cannot be used for optimization over
\mathcal{M}_{r}
. In our experiment, we will therefore use retractions (see
Section

5
) instead. As explained there, the convergence of a retraction based NCRPG remains an open problem. A detailed analysis of different retraction and inverse retraction methods, including their computation time and error compared to the exponential map, can be found in
[
1
]
. In the experiment, we therefore use
Algorithm

3
.

We will thus use the orthographic retraction which, for a given tangent vector
H=UMV^{\top}+U_{p}V^{\top}+UV_{p}^{\top}
, is given as

(49)

\mathrm{R}_{X}(H)=\left(U(\Sigma+M)+U_{p}\right)\left(\Sigma+M\right)^{-1}\left(\left(\Sigma+M\right)V^{\top}+V_{p}^{\top}\right).

For
Y\in\mathcal{M}_{r}
, the corresponding inverse orthographic retraction is simply:

(50)

\mathrm{R}^{-1}_{X}(Y)=\projOp_{\mathcal{T}_{X}\mathcal{M}_{r}}(Y-X),

with the projection onto the tangent space at
X
being given by

(51)

\projOp_{\mathcal{T}_{X}\mathcal{M}_{r}}(Z)=U\left(U^{\top}ZV\right)V^{\top}+\left(I_{M}-UU^{\top}\right)ZVV^{\top}+UU^{\top}Z\left(I_{N}-VV^{\top}\right).

We derive a closed-form solution for the proximal mapping
Equation

31
of
h=\mu\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1,2}
for
\lambda>0
at
X
using the retraction distance:

\displaystyle\proxOp_{\lambda\mu\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1,2}}^{\mathrm{R}}{\bigl(\mathrm{R}_{X}(-\lambda\grad g(X))\bigr)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\argmin_{Y\in\mathcal{M}_{r}}\frac{1}{2\lambda}\Bigl\|\projOp_{\mathcal{T}_{Y}\mathcal{M}_{r}}\bigl(\mathrm{R}_{p}(-\lambda\grad g(p))-Y\bigr)\Bigr\|_{q}^{2}+\mu\lVert Y\rVert_{1,2}.

For
Z\in\mathcal{M}_{r}
, let
D^{\lambda\mu}[Z]\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
be the row-wise soft-thresholding operator with

D^{\lambda\mu}[Z]_{ij}=\begin{cases}\frac{\lVert Z[i,:]\rVert_{2}-\lambda\mu}{\lVert Z[i,:]\rVert_{2}},&\qquad\text{if}\ i=j\text{ and }\lVert Z[i,:]\rVert_{2}>\lambda\mu,\\
0,&\qquad\text{else}.\end{cases}

From
[
21
]
we know that
D^{\lambda\mu}[Z]
solves

\proxOp_{\lambda\mu\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1,2}}^{\mathrm{R}}{\bigl(Z\bigr)}=\underset{Y\in\mathcal{M}_{r}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\ \frac{1}{2\lambda}\lVert Y-Z\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\mu\lVert Y\rVert_{1,2}.

Therefore, it holds that

0\in\mu\partial\lVert D^{\lambda\mu}[Z]Z\rVert_{1,2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}(D^{\lambda\mu}[Z]Z-Z).

Since
Z=U\Sigma V^{\top}
and
D^{\lambda\mu}[Z]Z=D^{\lambda\mu}[Z]U\Sigma V^{\top}
lie in the tangent space of
D^{\lambda\mu}[Z]Z
(because they have the same row-space
V
) we obtain

0\in\mu\partial\bigl\|D^{\lambda\mu}[Z]Z\bigr\|_{1,2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}P_{T_{D^{\lambda\mu}[Z]Z}\mathcal{M}_{r}}\bigl(D^{\lambda\mu}[Z]Z-Z\bigr),

and this implies
\mathrm{prox}_{\lambda\mu\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1,2}}(Z)=D^{\lambda\mu}[Z]Z
.
Hence, we get the closed form

\proxOp_{\lambda\mu\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1,2}}^{\mathrm{R}}{\bigl(\mathrm{R}_{X}(-\lambda\grad g(X))\bigr)}=D^{\lambda\mu}[\mathrm{R}_{X}(-\lambda\grad g(X))]\mathrm{R}_{X}(-\lambda\grad g(X)),

i. e., the prox step is simply a row-wise soft thresholding of the gradient step.

Implementation Details

We tested the local convergence behavior of NCRPG versus the Riemannian Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (RADMM)
[
33
]
for the problem described in
Section

6.3
. This algorithm replaces the first prox-step (of the smooth part
g
) in the Euclidean ADMM
[
7
]
with a gradient step on the manifold, whereas the second prox-step (of the nonsmooth part
h
) remains in the embedding space. As above, we used our closed form of the prox-step for
h
.

The measurement operator
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times MN}
was taken as a matrix with normally distributed entries in
\mathcal{N}(0,1/\sqrt{m})
, such that
\mathbb{A}(X)=\mathbb{A}\mathrm{vec}(X)=y
.
We note that this choice fulfills the RIP condition with very high probability.

To show local convergence of our method, we first generated a random ground truth matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
of rank
r
with
s
nonzero rows, which we perturb with a random matrix
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/\sqrt{m})
and truncate the rank.
The stepsize for NCRPG and RADMM with fixed stepsize was set to
\lambda=\eta=0.25
.
Backtracking was done with an initial stepsize of
s=0.5
.
The RADMM was implemented in
Julia
and the parameters
\rho=0.1,\beta=10^{-7}
were obtained by a grid search.
We stopped NCRPG when the norm of the gradient mapping with respect to the orthographic retraction fell below
10^{-7}
and the RADMM analogously when
\frac{1}{\eta}d^{\mathrm{R}}(X^{(k)},X^{(k-1)})<10^{-7}
.

Discussion

Despite the fact that the problem does not fulfill all assumptions, we observe (local) convergence in all cases, see
Table

3
.
The backtracking procedure is slower than NCRPG with constant stepsize, because more costly evaluations of the objective are required.
All algorithms find the row-sparsity pattern of the ground truth, but the RADMM is less confident: the average norm of those rows is higher.

We note that the number of measurements
m
is chosen such that the problem is underdetermined if either of the constraints (low rank, row-sparse) is considered alone.
Our results show that we recover a unique solution, indicating that, at least locally, the problem is well-posed.

7.
Conclusion & Outlook

In this article, we introduced a nonconvex Riemannian proximal gradient method that works intrinsically on a Riemannian manifold.
We established state-of-the-art convergence results with only mild assumptions.
Convergence theory for an analogous method that uses retractions as an approximation of the exponential map remains an open problem.
In the experiments, we have demonstrated the efficiency of our method in many nonconvex and intrinsic cases that have so far been out of reach for nonsmooth Riemannian optimization.

In an upcoming work
[
14
]
, we will consider the same method under stronger convexity assumptions, both on the functions
g
and
h
, as well as on the manifolds, considering in particular Hadamard manifolds.
We will show that under these stronger assumptions, we can obtain improved convergence rates and even show convergence to stationary points.
