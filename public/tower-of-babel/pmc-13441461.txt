Optimizing lifetime productivity in high-producing dairy cows: challenges in the management of age at first calving, double ovulation, and postpartum uterine health
Authors: Hiromi KUSAKA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The dairy industry has seen remarkable increases in milk yield per cow; however, these gains are often offset by declining fertility and a reduced lifespan. This review discusses strategies to enhance lifetime productivity, focusing on three key research areas: 1) Age at first calving (AFC): Retrospective analysis indicates that reducing AFC to < 22.5 months enhances lifetime daily milk yield without adverse effects through the third lactation, provided that the heifer reaches a body weight of ≥ 600 kg before first calving. 2) Double Ovulation: Sequential ultrasonic observation of the ovaries characterizes the pathophysiology of spontaneous double ovulation in high-producing cows during the early postpartum period. Multiparous cows exhibit a higher incidence of double ovulation that leads to twin pregnancies, which is associated with a negative energy balance. This metabolic state can be monitored using milk components such as lactose as a potential metabolic indicator of follicular dynamics. 3) Postpartum uterine health: Diagnostic strategies for endometritis have been optimized. Integrating vaginal discharge scoring and ultrasonography ensures a reliable assessment. This multimodal approach significantly improves the sensitivity of predicting reproductive performance compared to single-method assessments. Improving the lifetime productivity of modern dairy cattle requires precise management across various life stages. Automation and labor-saving technologies, particularly in routine husbandry, reproductive management, and veterinary practices, will become increasingly important for maintaining sustainable dairy operations.

Introduction

Over the past 70 years, the dairy industry has undergone significant structural shifts. In the USA, milk yield per cow has increased by nearly fivefold from 1950 to 2024, and currently, 30% of cows belong to operations with over 1,000 head [1]. This trend has been mirrored in Japan. The average milk yield per cow and the average herd size reached 9,753 kg and 110.3 head, respectively, by 2024 [2]. This productivity growth is driven by genetic and nutritional advancements as well as intensive husbandry practices.

Genetic selection for milk productivity substantially improved average daily gain (ADG) during the rearing period. Historically, Holstein heifers were selectively bred for an ADG of 0.5–0.6 kg/day; however, current strategies aim for 0.8–0.9 kg/day or higher to accelerate maturity [3, 4]. This allows heifers to reach pubertal weight earlier, reducing their age at first calving from 24 to 26 months [5]. The return on investment is typically not realized until the end of the first lactation period [6]; therefore, this shift offers substantial economic benefits.

However, the emphasis on productivity has created a complex trade-off in reproductive efficiency. Between 1960 and 2000, milk productivity increased rapidly, but reproductive performance declined sharply. For example, first-service conception rates in New York dairy cattle decreased from 65% in 1951 to 40% in 1996 [7], and this trend has been observed worldwide [8]. In Japan, the average days open increased from 124 in 1981 to 157 in 2011 [9], reflecting a similar decline in fertility. A gradual recovery has been observed since the early 2000s [10], driven by genomic selection for reproductive traits and the widespread adoption of reproductive technologies such as Ovsynch [11].

Despite these improvements, optimizing the productivity lifespan (first calving to culling) remains a primary concern, as it typically ranges from 2.5–4 years [12]. This turnover is remarkably higher than that in 1939, when annual culling rates were 21–28% [13], whereas modern rates can exceed 60% [14]. In Japan, the average parity at culling is declining. Notably, the proportion of cows culled after their first calving increased from 14.7% in 2012 to 16.5% in 2023 [2, 9].

The high rate of herd replacement is primarily due to involuntary culling, with reproductive failure being the leading cause [12, 15]. Recent data confirm this trend; reproductive issues account for 17.7% of disposals in the eastern U.S. [16], whereas in Canada, they account for 13.7%, surpassing mastitis (7.7%) and low production (7.1%) as the main causes [17]. Similarly, in Japan, reproductive disorders account for 16–17% of all culling cases, including conditions such as ovarian cysts, retained placenta, endometritis, and reduced fertility associated with twin births [2]. Therefore, suboptimal fertility remains the most challenging factor affecting the longevity of cows.

Dairy cattle reproduction faces multifaceted challenges during the periparturient period owing to profound homeotropic changes [18]. These shifts, such as transient insulin resistance and prioritized redirection of nutrients toward the mammary glands, frequently induce severe negative energy balance (NEB) and periparturient disorders, such as ketosis. Critically, metabolic stress disrupts the endocrine axis, which is the primary contributor to delayed ovarian cycle activity [19]. Physiological strains suppress postpartum immune function and increase susceptibility to uterine diseases [20]. The resulting inflammatory mediators and bacterial endotoxins interfere with the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis and directly inhibit follicular development, further hindering the resumption of the ovarian cycle [21]. Collectively, these metabolic, endocrine, and immunological disruptions create a substantial physiological bottleneck that compromises overall reproductive efficiency.

To address these complex issues and establish a sustainable production model, it is essential to optimize management practices throughout the life cycle of cows, from rearing to late lactation. In this minireview, I provide a comprehensive discussion on improving lifetime productivity by focusing on the following three key research areas:

(1) Impact of age at first calving on longevity and productivity

Although reducing the AFC shortens the non-productive rearing period, its long-term effects on reproductive performance and survival remain unclear. The performance throughout the third lactation period was evaluated to determine the influence of AFC on lifetime profitability, thereby identifying critical management points for balancing early maturity with sustained reproductive performance.

(2) Pathophysiology and risk factors of double ovulation in high-producing cows

Elevating milk yield has increased the incidence of ovarian disturbances, particularly double ovulation. This leads to twin births, heightening the risk of postpartum uterine diseases, culling rates, and milk loss. High-yielding dairy cows in a low-energy state were identified as the primary risk group through characterization of early postpartum spontaneous double ovulation.

(3) Optimizing diagnostic strategies for postpartum endometritis

Inconsistent diagnostic criteria for postpartum endometritis hinder effective fertility management. By comparing the diagnostic results obtained by the Metricheck device, ultrasonography, and the cytobrush technique, optimized diagnostic timings and refined protocols have been proposed to improve accuracy and clinical reliability.

Impact of age at first calving on longevity and productivity

The AFC varies globally, averaging 26.0 months in the USA and reaching up to 31.0 months in Kenya [22]. In Japan, although the common goal is 24.0 months, approximately 24% of Holstein heifers calve at 27.0 months or older [23].

To reduce the AFC without compromising performance, heifers must have adequate skeletal maturity and body weight (BW). However, excessively rapid weight gain before puberty can lead to fat deposition in the mammary glands, which may impair mammary development and consequently reduce milk yield [24,25,26]. While several studies reported lower milk yields in heifers calving before 23.0 months [24,25,26], a study found no adverse effects as long as an ADG of 0.80–0.85 kg/day was maintained [5]. In addition to the negative effect on milk production, a younger calving age can also affect subsequent reproductive performance. Fetal–maternal disproportion, often resulting from insufficient skeletal maturity, is a major cause of dystocia, particularly when the AFC is < 24 months [27]. However, some studies have indicated that calving at approximately 22.0 months does not increase parturition problems if adequate growth is achieved before calving [26, 28].

Although reducing AFC has considerable potential, its impact on milk productivity, calving, and reproductive performance is confounded by multiple factors. Particularly, AFC and BW are highly correlated; therefore, these effects were difficult to separate. Previous prospective studies have evaluated the AFC under controlled growth conditions [24, 25, 29]; however, large retrospective datasets often lack BW information [30,31,32,33,34]. Moreover, only a few studies have compared animal performance within the same herd environment [25, 29, 35].

To address these gaps, it was investigated that the association between AFC and BW at first calving with milk productivity, incidence of calving problems, and reproductive performance during the first lactation period [36]. This retrospective study used the calving records of 203 Holstein heifers from 1999 to 2012 from the Hokkaido Agricultural Research Centre of the National Agriculture and Food Research Organization (NARO, Sapporo, Japan). In this experimental herd, the milk yield increased significantly annually [37], which is consistent with the long-term genetic improvement trends reported in Japanese Holsteins. The analysis revealed that AFC and BW at first calving were not significantly associated with 305-day milk yield during the first lactation. Importantly, the incidence of difficult calving and the prevalence of stillbirth were significantly associated with BW at first calving but not with AFC [36]. Heifers with a low BW (< 625 kg at first calving) exhibited a significantly higher incidence of calving difficulty and stillbirth than those with a moderate BW (626–654 kg). These findings suggest that insufficient body development rather than low AFC is the primary risk factor for adverse calving outcomes. Although a BW of 550–625 kg at first calving is considered acceptable for modern Holstein heifers [38], stricter management is required when aiming for earlier AFC. Current and previous findings suggest that achieving a minimum weight of 600 kg before first calving is essential for mitigating potential risks [29, 36].

AFC has also been associated with longevity and lifetime productivity, in addition to its effects on first-lactation performance and calving outcomes. One study compared milk production and reproductive performance between heifers calving at 22.2 and 24.6 months of age, with accelerated (0.89 kg/day) and conventional (0.78 kg/day) growth rates during the rearing period, respectively [39]. In the study, calving as early as 22.2 months did not adversely affect milk yield or increase the incidence of reproductive disorders from the first to the seventh lactation period. In accordance with these findings, recent retrospective studies on Holstein-type herds suggest that relatively younger cows tend to exhibit improved survival and higher lifetime productivity [5, 28, 31, 40].

A secondary retrospective analysis utilizing the same calving records as in a previous study [36] also demonstrated that an AFC of less than 22.5 months was optimal for maximizing lifetime productivity (Fig. 1). Heifers in the early AFC group exhibited a superior survival rate at the third calving compared to those at later calving, indicating a clear longevity advantage [41]. Crucially, among cows that reached their third lactation, reproductive and milk performance remained consistent regardless of AFC, indicating that early calving cows maintained high productivity without physiological compromise. Moreover, a significant portion of late-calving cows (16.7–25.0%) were culled owing to low productivity or hoof diseases, but not in the < 22.5-month age group [41]. These issues are often triggered by overconditioning and excessive body weight at first calving [42, 43]. Consequently, by combining the highest survival rate with competitive individual performance, heifers calving before 22.5 months achieved the greatest cumulative lifetime productivity through the third lactation period.

Although reducing the AFC to 22 months yields significant benefits for lifetime efficiency and longevity [5, 36], precise herd management is required. Achieving a BW of at least 600 kg by this age requires rigorous nutritional monitoring to promote skeletal growth, while preventing excessive adiposity. This strategy is highly effective in herds with optimal care, but may pose substantial risks, such as increased dystocia or metabolic disorders, in management systems with suboptimal growth monitoring or transition support. In addition to strict growth management, strategic reproductive interventions are essential to minimize the risk of dystocia at first calving. This can be achieved by optimizing the calf birth weight using sexed semen, which increases the proportion of smaller female calves [44]. Furthermore, particularly in Japan, the application of beef cattle semen or embryo transfer using Japanese Black cattle is a common and effective strategy for producing calves that are typically smaller than purebred Holsteins [45].

Lowering the AFC is key to reducing rearing costs. In the experimental herd, achieving an AFC as early as 22.5 months increased profitability. Using a daily rearing cost of 386 JPY (approximately 3.95 USD based on the average exchange rate) from Japanese commercial data in 2013 [46], the 4.2-month earlier conception in the young AFC group resulted in savings of approximately 48,000 JPY (492 USD) compared to that in the latest group (Fig. 1). This figure surged recently due to rising feed and energy prices [47], making the economic impact of shortening the AFC even more significant. However, the current estimation is conservative, as it does not fully account for all pre-calving expenses. Because factors such as disease and nutrient intake influence BW, AFC, and subsequent performance [48], larger-scale studies integrating these multifactorial costs are required to clarify the long-term impact of AFC on farm profitability.

Pathophysiology and risk factors of double ovulation in high-producing cows

Twin pregnancies provide limited advantages, including increased calf production and reduced gestation length. However, these gains are overshadowed by a high incidence of health and reproductive complications. These include reproductive disorders (e.g., retained placenta, dystocia, and metritis) [49,50,51,52], metabolic disorders (e.g., displaced abomasum and ketosis) [49, 50, 53], an increased risk of culling [54, 55], and reduced milk yield [56]. Consequently, twin pregnancies result in substantial economic losses ranging between 59 and 161 USD per case, as estimated in various studies [57].

The twinning rate in the US Holstein cattle increased steadily from 1.4% to 4.8% between the 1980s and the early 2000s [58, 59]. Epidemiological studies have suggested that this trend was influenced by parity, hormonal treatment, and environmental factors, along with genetic selection for greater milk production [57,58,59].

Twinning in dairy cattle is largely attributable to double ovulation, and accumulating evidence indicates that most twin births are dizygotic rather than monozygotic [60, 61]. Previous studies based on manual corpus luteum counts reported double ovulation rates ranging from 2.9% to 14.3% [62, 63], whereas more recent investigations in high-producing cows have documented substantially higher rates (13.3–22.4%) [64,65,66,67]. Several studies have reported a higher incidence of double ovulation in high-producing cows [66, 68], whereas others have found no association between double ovulation and milk yield [67]. Additionally, most previous studies have focused on double ovulation after timed artificial insemination or from the end of the voluntary waiting period until pregnancy [64, 66, 67]. Consequently, the incidence of spontaneous double ovulation during the early postpartum period and its relationship with milk production have not yet been fully characterized.

To address this knowledge gap, postpartum ovarian activity was examined in 43 lactating Holstein cows (26 primiparous and 17 multiparous) that calved between 1999 and 2001 at NARO [69]. As previously proposed [70], increased milk production driven by genetic selection since the 1980s may have contributed to the rising frequency of double ovulation. One potential mechanism is that high milk production requires increased feed intake, which stimulates hepatic steroid metabolism and lowers circulating concentrations of progesterone and estradiol-17β [71]. This reduction in steroid levels weakens negative feedback to the hypothalamus and pituitary; consequently, the selection of a single dominant follicle is impaired, leading to the development of multiple dominant follicles and an increased incidence of double ovulation (Fig. 3Fig. 3.Alterations in steroid metabolism and milk component traits in multiparous cows exhibiting double ovulation.).

In contrast, in primiparous cows, the occurrence of double ovulation may not depend solely on milk production but could also be associated with the accumulation of genetic factors. In support of this hypothesis, selective breeding for increased double ovulation rates in beef cattle was conducted to establish herds with elevated twinning rates [72]. Analysis of primiparous cows in the aforementioned study [69] revealed that daily and peak milk yields did not differ between single and double ovulators among these cows, although some reports have described a higher incidence of double ovulation in high-yielding primiparous cows [64, 66]. Overall, double ovulation did not significantly affect reproductive traits regardless of parity, although the mean interval to double ovulation in the third sequence was prolonged (P < 0.05; Fig. 2). However, two of ten multiparous cows that had double ovulation subsequently conceived of twin fetuses, suggesting that early postpartum double ovulation may increase the risk of twin pregnancy.

The investigation further identified that double ovulators exhibit distinct physiological characteristics, mainly lower milk lactose concentrations during early lactation [69]. Multiparous cows that experienced double ovulation maintained significantly lower lactose levels throughout the first three months postpartum than cows that experienced a single ovulation (4.4–4.5% vs. 4.6–4.7%, P < 0.05). In cows, milk lactose is primarily synthesized from plasma glucose; therefore, its concentration serves as a reliable proxy for maternal energy status (Fig. 3). This metabolic indicator is particularly important because high-producing cows commonly experience NEB after parturition. Such a state is frequently associated with atypical ovarian activity, including anovulatory waves and double ovulation [73, 74]. The sustained suppression of lactose levels in double ovulators likely reflects an altered metabolic state characterized by reduced glucose availability during the early postpartum period. Consequently, milk lactose concentration holds potential as a non-invasive predictor of ovulation number and warrants further investigation.

Hormonal synchronization protocols such as Double-Ovsynch have been proposed to prevent twin calving in high-producing Holstein cows [75]. Increasing progesterone concentrations during the growth of synchronized pre-ovulatory follicular waves markedly reduce double ovulation rates [75, 76]. However, these approaches are costly, and twin pregnancies can still occur even with such programs. Early pregnancy diagnosis allows for three options: no intervention, pregnancy termination, or manual embryo reduction. A recent economic analysis demonstrated that attempting manual embryo reduction reduced economic losses associated with twin pregnancies by 23–45 USD per case [57]. More recently, alternative approaches have been proposed, including the direct administration of prostaglandins to one of the two corpora lutea to induce luteolysis [77] and puncture of the subordinate follicle by aspirating its follicular fluid at the time of artificial insemination [78]. Further research is needed to assess the efficacy and economic implications of these strategies.

Optimizing diagnostic strategies for postpartum endometritis

Postpartum endometritis poses a major economic burden, with 240–884 USD per case and annual losses reaching 40,000 USD in a 1,000-head herd, with a 10% incidence according to simulation models based on several previous reports [79]. The reported prevalence varies among countries, at 19.2% in Germany [80] and 6.8–7.6% in Japan [81], while studies in other countries have reported a range of 5% to 68% [82,83,84]. A meta-analysis suggested that these variations stem from inconsistencies in disease definitions, diagnostic methods, examination timing, and actual differences across study populations [85]. Such methodological disparities complicate direct comparisons among studies and prevent the establishment of standardized diagnostic criteria. To ensure accurate clinical assessment, these challenges highlight the need to refine time-dependent diagnostic protocols that account for physiological uterine recovery [85].

Endometritis is defined as endometrial inflammation. Histopathological biopsy is considered the diagnostic gold standard [86]. However, as biopsies are often impractical, diagnosis typically relies on clinical uterine findings associated with subfertility. Clinical endometritis is generally identified by the presence of purulent material in the uterus or vaginal discharge ≥ 3 weeks postpartum [87]. In contrast, subclinical endometritis lacks visible pus and is diagnosed by endometrial cytology to identify increased neutrophil proportions [88]. To enhance precision, LeBlanc et al. (2002) proposed the terms “purulent vaginal discharge” and “cytological endometritis” as alternatives, emphasizing a diagnosis based on specific clinical or cytological findings [87]. These conceptual and terminological revisions demonstrate that postpartum endometritis remains an evolving disease.

The clinical utility of these methods is well-documented; however, the optimal diagnostic timing remains poorly defined. This knowledge gap persists because most studies use only one or two examinations at broadly defined intervals [85]. Physiologically, the bovine reproductive tract faces microbial invasion during calving, which triggers normal neutrophil influx during the first postpartum week as part of the uterine defense response. While the intrauterine environment typically clears within 5–7 weeks as it repairs [94], the diagnostic criteria remain highly time-dependent. However, the specific influence of screening timing on diagnostic outcomes has not been adequately examined, necessitating a more detailed temporal investigation.

To clarify the impact of screening timing, postpartum uterine status was investigated weekly from 2 to 7 weeks after calving using three diagnostic methods: UT, MT, and CYT. The investigation included 28 lactating Holstein cows (7 primiparous and 21 multiparous) at the Livestock Research Institute of the Aomori Prefectural Industrial Technology Research Center [95]. The decline in positivity rates differed significantly among the methods, peaking at 3, 4, and 5 weeks for MT, UT, and CYT, respectively (Fig. 4 (b)). As each method targeted different aspects of the uterine involution process, the test agreement remained low between weeks 2 and 6. The consistency among the three methods improved for the first time in week 7 (Fig. 4 (c)).

As indicated in previous studies [82, 85, 96], CYT often detects neutrophil infiltration, even when no pus is visible on UT or vaginal examination [95]. In contrast, UT can identify intrauterine purulent material in the absence of vaginal discharge. Specifically, 43% of cows diagnosed via UT at 3 weeks postpartum did not exhibit vaginal pus [95]. These discrepancies are critical for clinical interpretation. The purulent discharge detected by MT may originate from vaginitis or cervicitis rather than from the uterus [97]. As suggested by Sheldon (2020), such inconsistencies likely stem from diagnostic subjectivity, pathological heterogeneity, and differences in the sensitivity and specificity of each tool [98]. Consequently, relying on a single tool during the early postpartum period is inherently challenging (Fig. 5Fig. 5.Diagnostic strategy for postpartum endometritis during the early postpartum period in dairy cows.). To address these limitations, reexamination and combined use of multiple diagnostic methods are essential when the uterine health status is uncertain [96, 99].

The clinical advantage of this combined approach is evidenced by its superior ability to predict subsequent reproductive performance. For instance, combining vaginoscopy and ultrasonography at 4–6 weeks postpartum improved the sensitivity for predicting pregnancy at 150 days to 16.4%, compared with only 7.1% for vaginoscopy and 10.0% for ultrasonography when used alone [82]. Similarly, the integrated use of MT-based vaginal mucus evaluation and ultrasonographic assessment of intrauterine contents at 3–5 weeks postpartum is a superior predictor of future fertility compared with any single-method approach [99].

The findings of a secondary analysis, involving 67 lactating Holstein cows (25 primiparous and 42 multiparous), further reinforce this concept. The analysis demonstrated that cows identified as positive using both MT and UT at 4 weeks postpartum exhibited a significantly more pronounced reduction in pregnancy likelihood than cows identified as positive using either MT or UT alone [100]. These observations strongly support the implementation of a multimodal diagnostic strategy that provides a more reliable assessment of uterine health. By integrating multiple diagnostic tools during the early postpartum period, clinicians can achieve more accurate prognoses and implement more effective management interventions in modern dairy systems (Fig. 5).

Despite advances in diagnostic tools, the precise identification of the uterine environment that impairs fertility remains clinically challenging. Therefore, effective management strategies should integrate diagnosis with strategies targeting the physiological and environmental drivers of infection. While genetic selection increasingly prioritizes health, the heritability of endometritis is less understood than that of metritis (0.08–0.26) [101, 102]; thus, practical control relies primarily on managing environmental factors. Critical risks include uterine trauma (retained placenta, dystocia, and twinning) and metabolic diseases (hypocalcemia and ketosis), with additional contributions from parity and calving season [84, 103,104,105]. Although the direct impact of housing hygiene is difficult to quantify [104, 106], maintaining high sanitary standards remains essential for overall disease control. Ultimately, establishing a systematic monitoring strategy that integrates diagnostic and environmental insights is essential for improving reproductive outcomes.

Conclusions

The global dairy industry is facing a critical turning point in balancing productivity and long-term sustainability. As highlighted in this review, improving the lifetime productivity of Holstein cows requires an integrated management strategy that views life as a continuous process.

Achieving “trouble-free” calving is a fundamental bottleneck for longevity, especially at first calving. It is well recognized that primiparous cows possess a reproductive advantage due to lower milk yields, which result in reduced metabolic strain and more stable ovarian activity. However, this benefit is easily lost if first calving is unsuccessful. Calving before physical maturity increases the risk of dystocia and is directly associated with postpartum uterine diseases such as retained placenta and endometritis. As cows become multiparous, management strategies must shift toward addressing the risks associated with high milk production. In high-yielding cows, increased steroid hormone clearance increases the risk of double ovulation and subsequent twin pregnancy. Because twin calving causes the aforementioned uterine diseases that drive involuntary culling, precise monitoring and hormonal interventions are essential for sustaining productivity beyond the first lactation.

To achieve optimal lifetime productivity, this review proposes the following benchmarks: a target AFC of 22.0–22.5 months to maximize lifetime productivity, a BW of at least 600 kg at first calving to mitigate obstetric risks, and the use of multimodal uterine diagnostics alongside strict control of double ovulation after the first parturition. However, current research findings remain fragmented, and the precise impact of AFC on the resumption of postpartum ovarian and uterine functions remains unclear. Further studies are needed to establish more sophisticated management strategies for sustainable dairy productivity.

Perspectives

Well-managed herds can minimize the negative impacts on fertility and culling, even though accelerated AFC increases the dystocia risk, as shown in the results obtained from experimental herds [36, 41]. Consequently, the management level of a herd is a decisive factor in maintaining reproductive performance when adopting intensive strategies. This review discusses the use of reproductive technologies such as sexed semen to accelerate AFC. It can offer an opportunity to increase lifetime productivity; however, ironically, its practical application may also make it easier for farmers to decide whether to replace cattle in their herds earlier. This change in decision-making might be associated with the inadvertent lowering of the culling threshold [12] and rising replacement rates after the first calving observed in Japan [2, 9]. Ultimately, the quality of herd management is the most important factor in making these technologies work and ensuring that cows remain healthy and productive.

Furthermore, regardless of how clearly critical herd management points are identified, implementing such management is becoming increasingly difficult owing to severe labor shortages, particularly in Japan [11]. To ensure industry sustainability, the integration of automation is now essential, not only for routine husbandry, but also for specialized reproductive management and veterinary practice. Although many farms utilize sensors for basic health monitoring, diagnosing postpartum reproductive disorders largely depends on manual expertise. The evolution of advanced robotics capable of mimicking sophisticated human tasks offers a promising frontier for automating these complex diagnostic processes. Therefore, further studies are needed to enhance efficiency and reduce the technical workload in reproductive medicine.

Beyond these technical challenges, conventional livestock production systems face mounting pressures, including environmental concerns, such as methane emissions and the loss of arable land due to climate change. As “lab-grown” milk and meat are increasingly proposed as sustainable alternatives to address these issues, their emergence may lead some in society to view animal-based production more critically. Under these circumstances, it is becoming increasingly important to prioritize animal ethics and welfare to justify and continue the use of living animals for food production. Future dairy systems must not only focus on efficiency but also strike a careful balance between high productivity and excellent animal care.
