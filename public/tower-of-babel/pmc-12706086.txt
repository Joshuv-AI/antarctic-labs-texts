Damping properties of bamboo and glass fiber reinforced epoxy hybrid composites with edge cracks for vibration damping applications
Authors: Solomon Alemneh Adimass
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Natural fiber thermoplastic composites have gained interest for anti-vibration applications because of their higher specific strength, viscoelastic properties, and better damping ratios. Although some research has looked into the damping ratio of composites, the damping properties of bamboo/glass fiber reinforced epoxy hybrid composites (BGHCs) are still unstudied. This paper examines how fiber weight fraction and crack depth affect the damping ratios of fiber-reinforced hybrid composites made of bamboo and glass with an epoxy matrix. The hybrid composites include bamboo fiber weight fractions of 10%, 20%, and 30%, and glass fiber weight fractions of 10%, 20%, and 30%, all with a consistent 60 wt% epoxy resin. Test specimens with an orientation angle of [00 900 00]s and lamina processing were made using a hand layup-assisted vacuum bagging method and tested according to ASTM E756 standards. The tests involved preparing specimens with edge crack depths of 3 mm, 7 mm, and 11 mm, each with a fixed crack length of 75 mm from the edge. Damping ratios were measured using the half-bandwidth method from the frequency response function graph, recorded with an accelerometer connected to a DAQ NI-6009 during testing. Results showed that the damping ratio decreases as crack depth and glass fiber content increase, while it rises with higher bamboo-glass fiber content. This study offers valuable insights into the damping behavior of BGHC composites with edge cracks, aiding in the design and optimization of components requiring vibration damping and cyclic load resistance.

Introduction

Composite materials have garnered significant attention across various engineering disciplines due to their excellent mechanical properties and versatility. Recently, researchers have explored the potential of natural fibers, such as flex and bamboo, as reinforcements in composite materials to promote sustainability and improve performance. These traits make bamboo a promising yet challenging candidate for vibration-damping composites. Additionally, the incorporation of synthetic fibers like E-glass fibers has been extensively studied to enhance the mechanical properties of composites1,2. A key feature of composite materials is their ability to absorb energy and dampen vibrations. Damping plays a vital role in reducing vibrations, noise, and preventing structural failures in many applications, including automotive, civil engineering, and aerospace3. Therefore, understanding and investigating the damping behavior of composite materials is increasingly important to optimize their performance and expand their uses4,5. Recent research by Senthilrajan et al.6 on short jute-fiber/polyester composites showed that increasing fiber content up to 25 wt% improved flexural strength and damping, while longer fibers enhanced acoustic absorption. This study confirmed that natural fibers significantly aid energy dissipation through their viscoelastic behavior, aligning with current research on bamboo/glass hybrid composites. Combining bamboo and glass fibers provides a unique chance to leverage the benefits of both natural and synthetic materials. This matches recent vibration analyses indicating that stacking sequence, fiber ratio, and treatment methods greatly influence the damping response of natural-fiber-reinforced composites7. Hybrid jute-glass fiber metal laminates have shown that stacking sequence strongly affects natural frequency and damping, with jute layers improving energy dissipation while glass layers increase stiffness8. Similarly, recent studies on banana and sisal fiber-reinforced polyester composites found that flexural stiffness and natural frequency increased with fiber loading up to approximately 50 wt %, with a 1% NaOH treatment further improving interfacial bonding and stiffness. However, damping was maximized near 35 wt %, suggesting a trade-off between stiffness and energy dissipation9. Bamboo is known for its exceptional damping capacity due to its viscoelastic properties, effectively absorbing and dissipating energy. In addition, bamboo fiber composites describe its structure, extraction methods, and high specific strength, confirming its suitability as reinforcement even though vibration studies are limited10. Meanwhile, glass fibers enhance the stiffness and strength of the composite, boosting overall mechanical performance11–13. Likewise, Sambandamoorthy et al.14 reported that the sound absorption of jute fibers increased with fiber length, reaching a maximum at 20 mm because longer fibers contain more lumens and Nano fibrils that facilitate energy dissipation. They also observed that surface treatments altered lumen structure and impacted damping performance. This demonstrates how fiber morphology and treatment significantly influence vibration and noise reduction, supporting the current study on bamboo/glass hybrid composites. Fazilay et al.12 examined the damping and mechanical properties of four compression-molded composites: polyester matrix, hybrid 1 (6% E-glass and 6% nettle by weight), and hybrid 2 (12% E-glass and 6% nettle by weight). Results indicated that the mechanical properties and damping ratio remained stable even when glass fibers were replaced with nettle fibers. Santhosh et al.15 carried out an experimental analysis of the dynamic and static mechanical properties of Bamboo Fiber-Reinforced Polyester Composites with varying fiber content. Their findings showed that improving the bonding and micro-coring between bamboo fibers and the matrix could enhance the static and dynamic performance of the coatings. Jena et al.16 investigated how cenospheres as fillers affected the damping ratios of natural fiber composites. The composite consisted of bamboo fibers as reinforcement and cenospheres as filler particles within an epoxy matrix. The results revealed that the seven-layer configuration exhibited the highest damping ratio and natural frequency. Bhattacharjee and Nanda17 studied the impact of different design factors on the damping ratio of GFRE (glass fiber reinforced epoxy) composites, analyzing fiber loading, number of fiber layers, and fiber orientation. They found that increasing the fiber volume fraction led to a higher damping ratio. Similarly, Lawal et al.18 conducted an experimental study on the mechanical behavior of hybrid epoxy matrix composites reinforced with glass and bamboo fibers. Their goal was to develop environmentally friendly composites using bio-based fillers. The results showed that combining glass and bamboo fibers improved the mechanical properties of the composites, although excessive bamboo content caused a noticeable reduction in wear resistance. Dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) was used to study the viscoelastic behavior of the composites, while vibration tests like the logarithmic decrement method and impact hammer testing evaluated damping properties19,20. Wang21 performed a parametric study on the vibration characteristics and thermal post-buckling of hybrid coated composites that experienced matrix cracking and contained layers reinforced with carbon nanotubes, resting on an elastic foundation. The study examined factors such as matrix cracks, the distribution of carbon nanotubes, nanotube volume fractions, and the foundation’s stiffness on vibration and post-buckling behavior. Gupta et al.22 analyzed how various parameters crack location, material length scale, gradient index, crack length, boundary conditions, and tapering affected the fundamental frequency of a plate with variable thickness. They concluded that, similar to uniform-thickness plates, cracks influence vibration behavior, but adjusting the plate’s thickness can mitigate these effects. They suggested that optimizing vibration performance involves using adjustable thickness plates rather than uniform ones, reducing the impact of cracks. Lei et al.23 studied three symmetric distributions of single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) through lamina thickness, modeling edge cracks as slit cracks that span the entire ply and are oriented transversely to the laminate plane. Their goal was to better understand the nonlinear vibration responses of this type of hybrid laminated structure. Kim et al.24 examined the free vibration of laminated composite beams with constant cross-sections and varying fiber angles, subject to different boundary conditions and containing cracks. They used the Jacobi-Ritz method and the first-order shear deformation theory (FSDT) to analyze how cracks and fiber orientation influence vibration behavior. Moradi et al.25 investigated the effects of cracks on plate vibrations before and after buckling, modeling cracks as massless rotational springs. They aimed to derive nonlinear differential equations for plates with open edge cracks and analyze their static and dynamic responses. While previous studies have explored the damping behavior of various fiber-reinforced composites under different conditions, real-world applications often involve defects like edge cracks that can significantly influence vibration and damping responses. In this context, including edge cracks is particularly relevant because they simulate actual damage scenarios in structural components. These cracks modify stiffness, stress distribution, and energy dissipation, thereby affecting damping performance. By analyzing damping ratios at different crack depths, this work seeks to deepen understanding of how such defects impact the dynamic behavior and reliability of bamboo/glass fiber-reinforced epoxy hybrid composites.

This research aims to experimentally study the damping ratios of cracked bamboo/glass fiber reinforced epoxy matrix hybrid plate composites. By incorporating cracks of constant lengths and orientations into the composite plate specimens with different edge crack depths, the study seeks to assess the impact of cracks on the composite’s damping characteristics. Understanding how cracks affect the damping behavior of the composite will provide valuable insights for designing more resilient and reliable composite structures in engineering applications. In particular, damping studies that consider the influence of crack depth are essential for components exposed to cyclic loading and vibration during service. Such conditions are common in automotive suspension systems, aerospace panels, and civil engineering structures, where small cracks can significantly alter dynamic performance. Therefore, analyzing the damping behavior with varying crack depths helps predict the service life and optimize the vibration control of these composite components in real-world applications. By exploring the potential of bamboo/glass fiber-reinforced hybrid composite plates as efficient damping materials, this research aims to bridge the gap between sustainable materials and advanced engineering applications. The study aligns with the growing demand for eco-friendly and high-performance materials, promoting sustainable engineering practices and fostering innovation in the field of composite materials. This research experimentally examines the damping ratios of bamboo/glass fiber-reinforced hybrid composite plates with an epoxy matrix. The experimental study involves fabricating composite specimens with different proportions and weight ratios of bamboo and glass fibers embedded in a constant weight ratio of epoxy matrix. The specimens are then subjected to comprehensive testing to evaluate their damping characteristics. The experimental results contribute to a deeper understanding of the dynamic response of hybrid composite materials and their ability to mitigate vibrations. Moreover, the findings will help optimize the configuration and design of hybrid composite materials for specific engineering applications that require effective vibration damping.

Experimental investigation details

Materials

The fibers used to produce hybrid composite plates include bamboo fiber, glass fiber, and epoxy resin. The bamboo fibers were collected from West Gojam Sekela, Ethiopia, and extracted from bamboo culms through chemical and mechanical stripping. During fiber extraction, the nodes and diaphragms were removed. The fibers were then treated with a 1% NaOH solution. The glass fibers were purchased from the Dejen Aviation Defense College Industry. Epoxy was used as the matrix for manufacturing the composite plates. Epoxy 501 and its hardener were obtained from the Kadisco Color Factory in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.

Composite plate Preparation

The prepared bamboo and glass fibers are mixed systematically with a 60% epoxy matrix for various volume fractions, such as 20%, 30%, and 10% for bamboo fibers, and 10%, 10%, and 30% for glass fibers. The mold was made from sheet metal with dimensions of 300 × 300 × 3 mm³. The hybrid composite plates consisted of 6 layers, and the fiber arrangement in the composite laminate was [bamboo-glass-bamboo-glass-bamboo-glass], as shown in Fig. 1.

The stacking sequence [B-G-B-G-B-G] and fiber orientation [00 900 00]s was chosen to create a quasi-symmetric, cross-ply laminate that provides balanced in-plane stiffness, reduces residual stresses, and improves vibration damping. The alternating layers of bamboo and glass fibers combine bamboo’s high damping ability with the stiffness and strength of glass fibers26. Then, the hybrid composite, [00 900 00]s bamboo/Glass fibers reinforced epoxy (BGFRE) hybrid composite plates, were manufactured by vacuum bagging hand layup assisted method shown in Fig. 2. The [00 900 00]s fiber orientation was chosen to achieve a balanced and symmetric laminate that improves stiffness and enables efficient load transfer between bamboo and glass fibers. Cross ply laminates are well known to enhance energy dissipation in both longitudinal and transverse directions compared to unidirectional or ± 45° configurations. Moreover, stacking sequence and fiber orientation significantly affect damping behavior and crack propagation. These effects support why the [0° 90° 0°]ₛ layout contributes to better dynamic stability and vibration damping under cyclic loading27,28. To manufactured hybrid composite plates, the mold surface was cleaned using the cleaning agent, wax. A wax was applied on the mold surface for easy elimination of hybrid composite plates. The layers (unidirectional mats) were cut to the required size and shape, then placed on the prepared mold surface area. Table 1 demonstrates the details of the sizes of bamboo, glass fibers and epoxy resin used for manufacture. The peel strand was placed over the fibers and followed by the spreading medium. The entire layup was then bagged and placed under a vacuum. The resin used in this manufactured process was an epoxy 501 mixed with a hardener (2:1 ratio). The hybrid composite laminates were fabricated using a hand layup-assisted vacuum bagging process to ensure proper fiber matrix adhesion and void minimization. After stacking the fiber layers and applying epoxy resin, a peel ply, breather, and vacuum bag film were placed sequentially over the layup. Damping ratio evaluation experimental setup and procedure

Vibration tests of hybrid composite plates made from bamboo/glass fibers were conducted, considering the plates as cantilever plates, as shown in Fig. 4. An ACC-103 accelerometer was used during the vibration test and was connected to the computer via a DAQ USB NI-6009, with LABVIEW 2018 software installed for data analysis. A DAQ was employed to convert the analog data into digital form to determine the damping ratio based on amplitude versus frequency responses. The NI-6009 DAQ served as the central unit, controlling multiple input/output signals, gathering information, and performing analysis through a LabVIEW block diagram. The accelerometer was attached at the free end of the cantilever plates (dimensions: 200 mm × 30 mm × 3 mm) using wax, as shown in Fig. 5.

Damping ratio calculation by using half power bandwidth method

The damping responses of the hybrid composite plates were determined using the half-power bandwidth method, applied to the first natural frequency mode of the specimen. The damping ratios were measured by employing the half-bandwidth method as shown in Fig. 6, and they can be calculated using Eq. 9.

Results and discussion

Mechanical properties of BGHC plates

Table 2 summarizes the mechanical properties of the BGHC plates. These values were obtained using classical lamination theory and rule-of-mixtures relations based on the constituent material properties.

Longitudinal stiffness and density: BGHC-3 showed the highest longitudinal modulus E11 = 17.180 GPa, and this increase reflected its higher density. The larger amount of glass fiber in this configuration added mass and strengthened the laminate along the fiber direction.

Shear modulus G12: The in-plane shear modulus G12 also changed with the fiber proportions. BGHC-2 achieved a slightly higher G12 = 2.411 GPa, showing that the greater bamboo content helped the laminate carry shear loads more effectively.

Poisson’s ratio V12: BGHC-2 showed the highest Poisson’s ratio V12​ = 0.345, which indicated that it contracted more laterally under longitudinal loading and displayed stronger coupling between longitudinal and transverse deformation.

Transverse modulus E22: When the transverse modulus E22 was compared​ across the three cases, BGHC-2 showed the most balanced stiffness between the longitudinal and transverse directions, which made it the closest to an isotropic response among the three composites.

Comparison of BGHC with individual bamboo and glass fiber composites

Generally, higher volume fractions of natural fibers lead to higher damping ratios due to their viscoelastic nature. It can be inferred that, besides fiber volume fraction, boundary thickness and boundary stiffness also significantly influence the damping mechanism. Overall, the highest damping ratios were recorded at the highest natural fiber volume fractions, and the highest natural frequencies were observed with the greatest volume fractions of synthetic fibers in the BGHC plates. This behaviour aligns with observations from Coir-polypropylene vibration studies show the same trend more fiber raises natural frequency and lowers damping-supporting the stiffness damping trade-off seen in natural fiber composites36. These results suggested that synthetic fibers make the hybrid composite plates stiffer, but the damping properties are reduced. Conversely, natural fibers enhanced the damping performance of the hybrid plates. Therefore, hybrid composites containing natural fibers are characterized by high damping capacity.

Similarly, Senthilrajan et al.6 recently demonstrated that aloe vera/jute woven laminates achieved the best natural frequencies and acoustic performance in a J-J-A-J stacking configuration, confirming that the laminate architecture strongly influences the vibration and sound absorption efficiency of natural fiber composites. This confirms that weave pattern and fiber orientation critically affect the dynamic behavior of natural-fiber composites.
