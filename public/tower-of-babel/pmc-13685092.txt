Cost-effectiveness of prehospital high-sensitivity troponin i testing versus standard emergency department care in patients with suspected acute coronary syndrome in France
Authors: Franck Maunoury, Raphaël Couvreur, Pierre Hausfater, Jérémy Guénézan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrehospital point-of-care high-sensitivity cardiac troponin I (hs-cTnI) may support earlier triage of adults with suspected acute coronary syndrome (ACS). We assessed its short-term economic consequences compared with standard emergency department (ED)-based care in France.MethodsWe developed a decision-analytic, continuous-time semi-Markov model over a 24-hour horizon using individual patient data from the prospective, multicentre, double-blinded cohort study (VALIDATE) conducted across four French hospitals (July 2021-May 2024; n = 737). The reference analysis adopted a restricted prehospital-and-ED perspective covering measured medical and paramedical resources from first emergency medical service contact through ED disposition (states 1–3). A complementary database audit searched for patients recorded with less than 24 h of follow-up in an extended “prehospital, emergency department and hospital” setting. None were identified; inpatient, intensive care unit (ICU), coronary angiography, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) costs were therefore excluded from the reference-case totals. The initial hs-cTnI H0 sample was obtained before hospital arrival, whereas subsequent H1 or H3 samples, when required, were intended to be collected after arrival. Costs were reported in 2025 euros. Sampling uncertainty was assessed using 1,000 non-parametric bootstrap replications and scenario analyses examined clinically relevant subgroups (> 3 h since symptom onset; availability of a prehospital HEART score).ResultsAmong 737 participants (mean age 65.4 years; 58.8% men), 77 (10.4%) had non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. ED attendance was lower with hs-cTnI than with standard care (90.9% [95% CI 88.6–92.8] vs. 96.7% [95% CI 95.2–97.8]; absolute difference − 5.8% points [95% CI − 9.2 to − 2.4]; P < 0.001). Among ED attendees, mean ED length of stay was 1.63 h versus 2.76 h (P < 0.001). Mean prehospital-and-ED cost per patient was €185.24 with hs-cTnI and €268.55 with standard care, a saving of €83.31 ([95% CI 70.81–96.60]; P < 0.001).ConclusionsWithin the restricted 24-hour prehospital-and-ED perspective supported by the available patient-level data, a strategy beginning with prehospital hs-cTnI testing was associated with fewer ED attendances, shorter ED stays and lower costs. Longer-term evaluations are planned because these findings do not establish system-wide for inpatient, wider organisational, patient and societal costs.Trial registrationThe VALIDATE study is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04950244).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13561-026-00832-x.

Background

Chest pain is a common reason for emergency medical service (EMS) assessment, but only a minority of patients are ultimately diagnosed with acute coronary syndrome. In a large ambulance chest-pain cohort, 9.6% of patients had acute coronary syndrome [1]; in VALIDATE, 10.4% had non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. Most patients nevertheless require transport, ED assessment and observation before a safe decision can be made. This places pressure on ambulances and crowded EDs.

Cardiac troponin is central to the diagnosis of myocardial infarction when the electrocardiogram does not show ST-segment elevation. Earlier prehospital point-of-care tests used conventional troponin assays. They produced rapid results, but their sensitivity was not sufficient to safely rule out myocardial infarction soon after symptom onset [2]. They were therefore mainly used alongside clinical risk scores rather than as stand-alone tests. Recent prospective studies are evaluating high-sensitivity point-of-care troponin within prehospital and primary-care decision rules [3, 4].

High-sensitivity point-of-care assays may allow useful information to become available earlier [5]. Current ESC guidance promotes accelerated diagnostic pathways based on short serial sampling intervals, particularly 0/1-hour and 0/2-hour algorithms, to rapidly rule out or rule in NSTEMI [6–8]. In routine practice, however, implementation can be difficult when central-laboratory testing is affected by specimen transport, laboratory turnaround times, ED crowding and organisational constraints. Consequently, 0/3-hour algorithms remain in use in many central-laboratory settings and have been externally evaluated [9]. A single early hs-cTnI value is not interpreted in isolation. It must be considered with symptoms, the electrocardiogram, time since symptom onset and repeat sampling when required, particularly in early presenters [6, 10]. In VALIDATE, the rule-out threshold of hs-cTnI < 2.2 ng/mL identified 6.5% of the cohort and had a negative predictive value of 1.00 (95% CI 0.93-1.00). The rule-in threshold of > 50.2 ng/mL identified 6.4% and had a positive predictive value of 0.64 (95% CI 0.48–0.77). A prehospital HEART score of 3 or lower was examined only as an additional low-risk subgroup rule, not as a requirement for every patient.

The possible economic benefit comes from changing where and when decisions are made. Earlier classification into rule-out, observation or rule-in pathways could reduce avoidable ED use for selected low-risk patients and speed access to hospital care for higher-risk patients. The added testing cost and any changes in the use of prehospital or hospital resources must therefore be weighed against potential savings later in the acute-care pathway.

The objective of this study was to estimate the short-term costs and ED resource consequences of a prehospital point-of-care hs-cTnI strategy compared with standard ED-based care for adults assessed by EMS for suspected ACS in France, and to quantify uncertainty around incremental costs and ED attendances.

Methods

This economic evaluation was reported in line with the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS 2022) [11].

Study design and perspective

We compared a pathway beginning with prehospital hs-cTnI testing with standard care. The reference analysis adopted a restricted prehospital-and-ED perspective covering measured medical and paramedical resources from first EMS contact through ED disposition (model states 1–3). This perspective is broader than an ED-only perspective because it includes measured prehospital resources, but it is narrower than the collective perspective recommended for a French reference-case evaluation [12]. Candidate downstream hospital costs, including inpatient care, ICU admission, coronary angiography, PCI and CABG, were initially considered because these interventions may be immediate consequences of the emergency physician’s diagnostic and triage decision. To determine whether they could be incorporated consistently within the 24-hour horizon, we performed a complementary audit of the VALIDATE database on 26 June 2026. No participant was recorded with less than 24 h of follow-up in the extended prehospital-ED-hospital setting. We therefore retained the prehospital-and-ED cost boundary and excluded these hospital-phase costs from the reference analysis. All costs are reported in 2025 euros.

Serial sampling was not intended to take place entirely in the prehospital setting. The initial hs-cTnI measurement was obtained at first medical contact before hospital arrival, whereas the subsequent H1 or H3 sample, when required, was intended to be collected after hospital arrival. The serial algorithm was therefore not expected to materially prolong EMS attendance. Medical and paramedical resources were included in both the out-of-hospital and ED phases where they were measured. Some wider organisational prehospital costs, such as implementation, training or changes in fleet availability, were not captured.

Data sources and study population

We used individual patient‑level data from the VALIDATE prospective, multicentre, double‑blinded cohort study conducted in four French university hospitals within physician-led emergency medical systems. Adults were enrolled in the prehospital setting after EMS assessment for symptoms prompting an ACS evaluation, most commonly chest pain. The point-of-care hs-cTnI result was available in approximately 8 min. Treating clinicians were blinded to this result, so usual-care decisions were not altered during the diagnostic study. The final diagnosis of non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction was adjudicated at hospital discharge using serial laboratory troponin measurements, clinical data and electrocardiographic findings. After exclusions, 737 participants were available for the economic analysis. Model structure

We fitted a 24-hour non-homogeneous continuous-time semi-Markov model to the observed patient pathways using the R package “msm” [13–16]. The model records when patients move between six states: (1) prehospital care, (2) ED stay, (3) ED discharge, (4) inpatient stay, (5) hospital discharge and (6) death. Transition rates were estimated from the study data and were allowed to change over time. The reference cost analysis used states 1–3 only because the complementary database audit found no participant with less than 24 h of recorded follow-up in the extended “prehospital-ED-hospital” setting.

Patients presenting within the first 2–3 h after symptom onset require particular caution because troponin release may still be incomplete [6, 10]. Current ESC guidance favours accelerated 0/1-hour or 0/2-hour diagnostic algorithms [6–8]. In routine central-laboratory practice, specimen transport, turnaround time, ED crowding and organisational constraints may prevent these short intervals from being achieved; 0/3-hour pathways therefore remain in use and have been externally evaluated [9]. In VALIDATE, the H0 sample was collected before hospital arrival and the later H1 or H3 sample, when required, was intended to be collected after hospital arrival. The H3 measurement was a protocol-defined delayed sample for patients in whom an initial result might be too early to interpret reliably; it was not proposed as a replacement for the ESC accelerated algorithms.

Three analyses were prespecified: the overall cohort; patients presenting more than 3 h after chest-pain onset, using a rule-out threshold of hs-cTnI < 4.7 ng/mL and a rule-in threshold > 50.2 ng/mL; and patients with a prehospital HEART score available, using a score of 3 or lower to identify a low-risk subgroup.

Strategies compared

In the hs‑cTnI strategy, the initial sample was obtained at first medical contact before hospital arrival (H0). When serial testing was required, the subsequent H1 or H3 sample was intended to be collected after hospital arrival rather than during prolonged ambulance attendance. A rule-out decision was not based on one result alone: the troponin value was interpreted with the clinical assessment, electrocardiogram, symptom timing and repeat testing when indicated. Patients with persistent symptoms, concerning electrocardiographic findings or other high-risk features remained eligible for standard ED and hospital assessment.

In standard care, patients followed the usual EMS and ED pathway, with central-laboratory high-sensitivity troponin and further observation, imaging or admission as clinically indicated. Figure 2 summarises the prehospital pathways for both compared strategies.

In the prehospital hs-cTnI strategy, a POC hs-cTnI assay was obtained at initial assessment (H0) and repeated at H1 or H3 according to the clinical protocol. Decision thresholds were evaluated for rule-out and rule-in pathways. In SOC, patients were managed according to standard ED-based algorithms using laboratory high-sensitivity troponin (typically hs-cTnT) with standard laboratory processing times, with downstream testing and observation as clinically indicated.

Costs and resource use

The reference states 1–3 cost analysis included prehospital assessment, electrocardiography, troponin testing, ED attendance, ED length of stay, routine laboratory investigations and ED disposition. Hospital micro-costing covered the medical and paramedical resources used in the out-of-hospital and ED phases where these were measured. Unit costs were obtained from hospital micro-costing and French reimbursement schedules, including Biol’AM [17]. Routine haematology and biochemistry tests were costed as one bundled panel, not as separate NABM tariffs. Inpatient care, ICU admission, coronary angiography, PCI and CABG were considered as potential immediate downstream consequences of the initial emergency diagnostic strategy. However, the complementary audit identified no patient with less than 24 h of recorded follow-up in the extended prehospital-ED-hospital setting; these costs were therefore not applied in the reference analysis. Candidate unit costs are documented in Supplementary Table S1 for transparency.

ED costs contained a fixed attendance component and a time-dependent component. The supplementary costing worksheet also reports a derived value labelled ‘cost of added ED length of stay due to SOC’. This is an explanatory decomposition: €118.85 equals the 1.15-hour difference in mean ED stay multiplied by the cardiology ED hourly cost (€103.35). It was not added again on top of the state-based total, so no double counting occurred. Table 2 summarises the inputs that contributed directly to the reference prehospital-and-ED analysis. Outcomes

The primary economic outcome was mean cost per patient over 24 h. The main utilisation outcome was ED attendance avoided. We also report the saving per ED attendance avoided. Secondary outcomes included ED length of stay and the distribution of patients across model states. Because the analysis did not estimate quality-adjusted life-years, the saving per ED attendance avoided should be read as a pathway-efficiency measure rather than a conventional welfare-based incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER).

Sensitivity and uncertainty analyses

Confidence intervals for ED attendance proportions used Wilson score intervals [18], and confidence intervals for the absolute difference used Newcombe’s method [19]. Sampling uncertainty was assessed with 1,000 non-parametric bootstrap replications. Bias-corrected and accelerated intervals were used for skewed cost outcomes [20]. We report the cost-effectiveness plane, the probability of dominance and the probability of cost-effectiveness across willingness-to-pay values per ED attendance avoided.

Structural uncertainty was explored through the 3-hour throughput analysis and the clinically defined subgroups. No separate additional EMS waiting-time cost was assigned for serial sampling because the H1 or H3 sample was intended to be collected after hospital arrival. The complementary data-boundary audit tested whether hospital-phase costs could be incorporated within the same 24-hour observation window; because no eligible extended-setting record was identified, the reference analysis remained restricted to prehospital and ED care. Remaining uncertainty concerning unmeasured organisational prehospital costs and downstream healthcare use was addressed in the limitations.

Ethics and governance

This study used fully anonymised individual-level data from the VALIDATE database maintained by the University Hospital of Poitiers. Under French data protection regulations and guidance from the French Data Protection Authority (CNIL; authorisation/committee no. 2014 − 190), additional ethical approval was not required for analyses based exclusively on anonymised secondary data. Nevertheless, the study protocol was reviewed by the institutional ethics committee of the University Hospital of Poitiers reviewed the study protocol and waived the requirement for informed consent.

Results

Study population and pathway outcomes

The analysis included 737 participants; mean age was 65.4 years, 58.8% were men and 77 (10.4%) had NSTEMI. ED attendance was lower with hs-cTnI than with SOC: 670/737 (90.9%; 95% CI 88.6–92.8) versus 713/737 (96.7%; 95% CI 95.2–97.8). The absolute difference was − 5.8% points (95% CI -9.2 to -2.4; P < 0.001).

Among patients who attended the ED, mean length of stay was 1.63 h (95% CI 1.51–1.76) with hs-cTnI and 2.76 h (95% CI 2.57–2.97) with SOC (P < 0.001).

Costs

Across prehospital care, ED stay and ED disposition, mean cost per patient was €185.24 (95% CI €177.10-€196.70) with hs-cTnI and €268.55 (95% CI €259.64-€278.63) with SOC. The mean saving was €83.31 per patient (95% CI €70.81-€96.60; P < 0.001), equivalent to 31.0% of the SOC cost. Prehospital costs were slightly higher with hs-cTnI because of the added test, but this was more than offset by fewer ED attendances and shorter ED stays. The complementary database audit identified no participant with less than 24 h of recorded follow-up in the extended prehospital-ED-hospital setting (0/737); inpatient, ICU and procedural costs were therefore not included in these reference-case totals.

The pathway saved approximately €1,428 for each ED attendance avoided. Because the H1 or H3 sample was intended to be collected after hospital arrival, the serial testing protocol was not expected to add substantial ambulance waiting time. Nevertheless, the analysis may not capture every organisational prehospital cost associated with implementation. Threshold and subgroup analyses

The hs-cTnI strategy was less costly in each prespecified analysis. The largest relative saving was observed in patients presenting more than 3 h after symptom onset. In that group, the higher rule-out threshold (< 4.7 ng/mL) identified more low-risk patients after enough time had elapsed for troponin to become informative. The HEART-score subgroup produced savings similar to the overall cohort. These subgroup results should be interpreted as exploratory evidence of pathway heterogeneity rather than separate confirmatory findings.

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis

Bootstrap results were close to the base case. Mean cost per patient was €270.50 (95% CI €259.93-€281.11) under SOC and €187.09 (95% CI €178.89-€196.24) under hs-cTnI, a saving of €83.40 (95% CI €69.06-€97.82; P < 0.001). The hs-cTnI strategy had lower costs and fewer ED attendances in all 1,000 bootstrap replications. This probability is conditional on the model structure and the restricted prehospital-and-ED cost boundary; it does not cover unmeasured organisational EMS costs or inpatient and longer-term health-system costs. Figure 3 shows cost-effectiveness plane for the bootstrap analysis. The vertical banding of bootstrap estimates reflects the discrete binary effectiveness outcome. Because incremental ED attendances avoided were calculated from integer attendance counts divided by the sample size, multiple bootstrap replications produced identical incremental-effect estimates while retaining different incremental costs.

Discussion

Prehospital hs-cTnI testing was associated with fewer ED attendances, shorter ED stays and lower 24-hour prehospital-and-ED costs. The extra cost of the test was small compared with the reduction in ED use. In the reference analysis, the mean saving was €83 per patient.

This result has an important boundary. It is not a claim that hs-cTnI saves money for the whole health system. The reference estimate covers measured medical and paramedical resources from the first prehospital assessment through ED disposition. The first sample was taken before hospital arrival, but subsequent serial samples were intended to be collected in hospital, so the protocol was not expected to shift a prolonged waiting cost to EMS. Downstream procedures such as coronary angiography, PCI, CABG and ICU admission may occur after the emergency physician’s diagnostic and triage decision and may represent immediate consequences of that decision. However, a complementary audit performed on 26 June 2026 identified no participant with less than 24 h of recorded follow-up in the extended prehospital-ED-hospital setting. Including these hospital costs would therefore have required incomplete or selectively observed data. The co-authors consequently retained the prehospital-and-ED perspective and excluded hospital-phase costs from the reference analysis.

The clinical goal is safer and earlier triage, not simply avoiding the ED. A low troponin value alone is not enough to rule out ACS. The result must be considered with symptoms, the electrocardiogram, the time since symptoms began and repeat testing when needed. Patients with continuing pain, abnormal findings or other high-risk features would still be transported and assessed in hospital. The HEART score was examined as an additional low-risk rule in a subgroup, rather than being applied to every patient.

The findings are consistent with studies showing that high-sensitivity troponin pathways can shorten the time needed to make decisions in acute care [7–9, 21–23]. An ED-based economic evaluation of a rapid high-sensitivity troponin algorithm also reported potential cost reductions [24]. The present analysis adds French patient-level data to the emerging evidence on prehospital testing [1, 25, 26]. The main economic mechanism is straightforward: earlier information moves part of the diagnostic process upstream, so some patients spend less time in the ED or do not attend it. Whether that translates into real system value depends on local EMS organisation, ED crowding and the availability of safe follow-up outside hospital.

The multi-state model was useful because it followed the timing of each patient’s pathway instead of assigning everyone to a fixed average route. The bootstrap analysis preserved the link between time in each state and cost. However, the finding of 100% dominance should not be read as absence of uncertainty. It reflects sampling uncertainty within the chosen model and restricted prehospital-and-ED cost boundary; it does not include all uncertainty about implementation behaviour, unmeasured organisational costs, inpatient care or longer-term outcomes.

Several limitations remain. First, the perspective is deliberately restricted to measured prehospital and ED resources. The complementary audit confirmed that no participant had less than 24 h of recorded follow-up in an extended prehospital-ED-hospital setting, so inpatient, ICU, coronary angiography, PCI and CABG costs could not be incorporated consistently in the reference analysis. The results may therefore reflect cost displacement beyond the ED rather than net savings for the whole health system. Some organisational prehospital costs may also not have been captured. Second, the 24-hour horizon does not capture repeat attendance, later cardiovascular events, mortality or quality of life. The study therefore cannot estimate cost per quality-adjusted life-year or lifetime cost-effectiveness. Third, clinicians were blinded to the point-of-care result. The model estimates what might happen under a protocolised pathway, rather than measuring how clinicians and patients behave after implementation. Fourth, the results may not transfer directly to systems with different ambulance staffing, ED capacity, laboratory turnaround times or community follow-up.

A pragmatic implementation study is now needed. It should measure EMS resource use, staff and vehicle time, the location and timing of serial samples, transport decisions, safety, immediate inpatient consequences, downstream care, patient-reported outcomes and total healthcare-system costs. Those data would show whether the short-term prehospital-and-ED savings identified here translate into broader health-system value.

Conclusions

Within a restricted 24-hour prehospital-and-ED perspective, a strategy beginning with pre-hospital hs-cTnI testing was associated with fewer ED attendances, shorter ED stays and lower costs than standard care. The complementary data audit did not support extending the reference cost boundary to inpatient care because no participant had less than 24 h of recorded follow-up in the prehospital-ED-hospital setting. The findings are promising but do not establish system-wide or lifetime cost-effectiveness. Broader-perspective implementation studies are required before system-wide adoption is considered.
