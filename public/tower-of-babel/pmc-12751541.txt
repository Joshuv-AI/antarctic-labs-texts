Using microwave ablation in combination with curettage and reconstruction with a vascularized iliac bone graft guided by a three-dimensional printed model for the treatment of a grade III talar giant cell tumor: a case report

Background

Talar giant cell tumors (GCTs) are extremely rare, primarily affecting younger adults and often resulting in significant functional impairment of the foot [1]. Currently, surgical management is the predominant treatment strategy for GCTs. When surgery is not feasible, alternative treatments such as radiation therapy or denosumab may be considered [2]. The anatomical complexity and insufficient blood supply of the talus have led some surgeons to advocate for resection rather than endosteal curettage as the preferred procedure for managing large, expansile tumors [3]. Theoretically, specific Campanacci grade III GCTs in the talus that are detected early, contain some remaining bone stock, and exhibit no evidence of talar collapse may be treated via a downgrading strategy (to grade II). Comparing to standard intralesional management for grade I and II GCTs, this approach involves more aggressive curettage with adjuvant therapy adjacent to articular cartilage in order to maximize local control of recurrence instead of sticking to retain 1–2 cm subchondral bone.

In recent years, microwave ablation has been identified as a promising modality for the treatment of GCTs of periarticular bone. The advantages of microwave ablation include the preservation of native joint with excellent function in the long term, a low rate of local recurrence, and the ability to achieve a large ablation volume in short procedural time [4, 5]. Several cavity fillers, including cement, allografts, and autografts, have been proposed for residual cavities after tumor removal in Grade I or II GCTs of the talus [2, 6, 7]. However, none have proven to be completely satisfactory for addressing the large cavities associated with Grade III GCTs of the talus. In such cases, the preservation of the ankle function is facilitated by fillers capable of providing sustained mechanical support and promoting rapid revascularization of the residual subchondral bone and articular cartilage under ischemic conditions during early rehabilitation.

The value of a vascularized iliac bone graft (VIBG) to revascularize talar necrosis [8] and augment the cavity following the removal of aneurysmal bone cysts [9] or chondroblastoma [10] in the talus has been demonstrated. A larger segment is harvested and then tailored to the needed size with additional operating time. However, this process is usually performed based on a paper template, and technical difficulty occurs when both the three-dimensional fitting for a large cavity and structural support to the articular surface in the talus are needed. Additionally, improper arrangement of afferent vessels to the graft increases the risk of vascular compression. Several pioneering reports have demonstrated the advantages of using three-dimensional printed (3DP) models of relevant anatomy to assist in the execution of vascularized bone grafts, especially for extremity and mandibular reconstructions in complex clinical scenarios [11–15]. In this report, we present a case of a Grade III GCT of the right talus involving the head, neck and medial body. This case was successfully treated using a downgrading strategy incorporating microwave ablation and curettage, followed by reconstruction with a VIBG, highlighting the utility of a 3DP model.

Case presentation

A 23-year-old male presented with progressive right ankle pain for one year. The symptoms were aggravated with weight bearing. Physical examination revealed tenderness on the medial right ankle and restricted movements of the ankle joint, particularly in inversion-eversion of the subtalar joint. Initial radiographs of the right ankle revealed an osteolytic lesion in the talus, but it was ambiguous. Computed tomography (CT) scans and magnetic resonance images (MRIs) at our institution better delineated the lesion; the lesion involved the head, neck and medial body of the talus, extended into the subchondral bone close to four articular surfaces and invaded the medial soft tissue. No collapse of the articular surface was present (Fig. 1A-D). The chest CT and laboratory test results were normal. CT-guided core needle biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of Campanacci grade III GCT. Considering the patient’s young age and preference for preserving a mobile ankle, we planned a treatment-downgrading strategy focused on joint preservation. This approach consisted of microwave ablation, curettage, and reconstruction via a VIBG with the aid of a 3DP model.

Bilateral talus CT data in Digital Imaging and Communication in Medicine format were imported into Mimics 21.0 software (Materialise, Inc., Belgium) for three-dimensional reconstruction of the bone. A reconstructive surgeon with a computer technician performed a virtual removal of the tumor tissue using a computer. Using these data, a rapid prototyping model of the residual cavity was manufactured with a 3DP system (UnionTech Inc., China). To facilitate surgical planning, a vertically positioned handlebar was integrated on the medial side of the model, marking the intended entry point for the vascular pedicle. The 3DP model was then sterilized for intraoperative use (Fig. 2).

Schematic representation of the manufacturing workflow demonstrating the steps and time costs used for 3D printing from CT acquistion to model sterilization

The lesion was exposed via a medial malleolar osteotomy. Ablation procedure was carried out utilizing a microwave ablation system (MWA system, 2450 MHz, Qinghai Research Institute of Microwave Electric, Nanjing, China), with the insertion of microwave antennas measuring 2.0 mm in diameter and 142 mm in length with the maximum available power of 80 W into the lesion site. During ablation, the microwave antennas was repositioned to ensure overlapping ablation zones and placed at lesion peripheries to achieve a broader ablation margin, with 3–5 min for each site. The temperature of the articular surface and surrounding tissue was monitored, with the target temperature ranging from 60 to 80 °C and the articular surface temperature of below 43 °C. After ablation, the tumor tissue was removed through a large cortical window created on the medial surface (Fig. 3A). The 3DP model was subsequently inserted to assess its congruency within the cavity. The results from the intraoperative frozen section analysis were consistent with the GCT diagnosis (Fig. 3B). After dissection of the afferent vessels and exposure of the iliac crest and body, the 3DP model was used as a guide for determining the most feasible osteotomy site. A quadrilateral tricortical graft approximately forty millimeters long and twenty-five millimeters wide, with a thickness of sixteen millimeters at the iliac crest and eight millimeters at the ilium body, was harvested with a vascular pedicle measuring 5 cm in length. The vascularity of the bone graft was confirmed from the osteotomized side prior to the ligation of the deep circumflex iliac artery and vein (Video supplement 1). The graft was subsequently shaped with a high-speed burr using the boundaries of the 3DP model to be inserted in a press-fit manner in the sagittal and coronal spans of the cavity to attain maximum mechanical support for the residual subchondral bone (Fig. 3C-F). After cancellous bone chips were packed into the lateral wall of the cavity in the talar body to compensate for the insufficient axial span, the graft was inserted with its superior cortex facing the dome and its inner table facing the medial wall of the cavity. Small gaps between the cavity walls and the graft were filled with cancellous bone. The graft was finally fixed via a K-wire. The medial malleolus was reduced, and fixed with one screw and one K-wire. The afferent vessel was brought to the medial aspect of the ankle. Then, the medial branch of the posterior tibial artery was exposed through an extended incision. After measuring the requirement of vascular length at the recipient site, the medial branch was cut. Anastomosis was performed end-to-end to the medial branch of the posterior tibial artery and accompanying vein (Fig. 4A-D). Passive mobilization was carried out to ensure there is no impingement of vascular pedicle after microsurgical anastomosis.

Postoperatively, arterial flow in the vascular pedicle was monitored frequently using a portable Doppler. On postoperative day 5, an incisional infection developed likely due to hemorrhage and inadequate drainage. This complication was managed successfully with drainage procedure, removal of the K-wires, and a two-week course of intravenous antibiotics. Subsequently, active and passive mobilizations were encouraged; however, weight-bearing of the affected extremity was restricted until CT scans confirmed bone union at two months postoperatively (Fig. 5A-C). By six months, the patient progressively advanced to full weight-bearing, as CT images demonstrated clear evidence of graft integration. Two years after surgery, the small gaps between the graft and the recipient bed had nearly disappeared, enabling uniform load transfer across the dome. The sclerotic appearance of the subchondral bone gradually returned to normal (Fig. 5D-F). X-rays confirmed maintenance of the tibiotalar joint space without evidence of recurrence, although a slight reduction in the medial ankle joint space was noted (Fig. 6A-B). The patient returned to work and attained a normal gait. He was able to perform his daily activities without any discomfort. The American Foot and Ankle Society hindfoot score improved from 54 preoperatively to 76, with 15° of ankle dorsiflexion, 45° of plantar flexion under the weightbearing conditions (Fig. 6C), and 0° of inversion and eversion.

Discussion

This case report presents a rare patient with a grade III GCT of the talus successfully treated using microwave ablation combined with aggressive tumor curettage and reconstruction via a VIBG with the aid of a 3DP model. We suggested that the selection criteria for applying this approach was defined as follows: young and active patient who wanted a mobile joint, about 50% bone stock in the talar body was remained, small extraosseous mass limited to one plane, exhibited no invasion of the joint and no evidence of talar collapse. Exclusion criteria included poor soft-tissue envelope, with a large soft tissue mass, pathological fracture, vascular comorbidities, or the presence of distant metastases. Although some structural bone might remain, reconstruction of a large juxta-articular cavity in the talus following removal of a Campanacci grade III GCT is highly challenging. Aggressive curettage often tends to disturb the normal articulating effect and compromise the talus’s fragile blood supply [16]. Currently, various materials are recommended for filling cavities after tumor curettage in Grade I or II GCTs on the basis of the surgeon’s preference. Cement augmentation offers instant stability for large cavities and allows early rehabilitation. However, subchondral placement of cement may induce degeneration of the articular cartilage [17]. Alternatively, packing cancellous bone into the cavity provides the potential for permanent reconstruction once incorporation occurs. Yet, maintaining mechanical stability is difficult due to bone graft resorption. Recent reports have highlighted the importance of mechanical support for juxta-articulating surfaces in weightbearing regions, advocating the use of strut bone [18] or hard-type β-tricalcium phosphate grafts [19]. Nonetheless, revascularization of these grafts occurs very slowly, and their ultimate incorporation remains uncertain. Hassenpflug et al. reported successful reconstructions of lateral talus defect caused by aneurysmal bone cysts and posterior talus defect caused by chondroblastoma with VIBG [9]. Similarly, Wagener et al. described three cases of talar defects resulting from chondroblastomas that were reconstructed with vascularized bone grafts (one using a VIBG and two using medial femoral condyle grafts) [10]. Inspired by the partial reconstructions achieved in these studies, we opted for a vascularized bone graft for our young, active patient, whose Grade III GCT was located in the head, neck and medial body of the talus, adjacent to four articulating surfaces. Among the available donor sites, the key rationale for selecting a VIBG was its superior biomechanical support, provided that reliable vascularization and press-fit bone contact could be ensured.

Three-dimensional printing has significantly advanced the medicine field since its introduction in the 1980s. This technology enables the precise fabrication of anatomical models from medical imaging data using computer-controlled deposition of successive material layers, thereby aiding in surgical planning for complex procedures [20]. Houdek et al. and Schmidt et al. reported that 3DP models could serve as valuable adjuncts in the design and execution of vascularized bone flap transfers for scaphoid reconstruction [11, 12]. Taylor and Iorio demonstrated that 3DP models facilitated precise creation of anatomic defects and rapid bone contouring in various vascularized bone flaps, including the medial femoral trochlea, medial femoral condyle, and fibula osteocutaneous flaps [13]. Matias et al. used a 3DP model to harvest a chimeric iliac crest flap in a patient undergoing maxillectomy for maxillary osteomyelitis [14]. Ettinger et al. employed a 3DP model to guide the assembly of scapular components in a chimeric scapular free flap during hemimandibular reconstruction [15]. To our knowledge, the treatment of Grade III GCTs in the talus via a downgrading strategy, combined with a 3DP model-guided VIBG reconstruction, has not been previously reported. Based on the patient’s CT data, the operation could be previewed preoperatively to accurately determine the tumor location and a real-size 3DP model of the talar defect was manufactured. Subsequently, the 3DP model provided clear visualization of the complex geometry of the residual cavity. Additionally, a VIBG could be more efficiently elevated and quickly shaped on a side table to approximately 1 mm larger than the model dimensions, facilitating a precise press-fit insertion. Therefore, we considered K-wire fixation sufficient for stability in the axial span. Additional fixation with a small fragment plate could have provided better stability. Since this operation took almost five hours, the method appeared to be slightly cumbersome compared with those involving nonvascularized bone grafts. Therefore, reducing the operation duration would be beneficial to both patients and surgeons. The attachment of a bar handle to the model facilitated its placement and simultaneously oriented the entry point of the musculoperiosteal branch on the graft. This vascular pedicle orientation helped prevent impingement against the medial malleolus or the deltoid ligament and allowed direct proximity of the flap vessels, as confirmed during the dissection of a short vascular pedicle of the medial branch of the posterior tibial vessels.

Unavoidable interruption of the intraosseous blood supply occurs during tumor invasion and surgical procedures through a medial approach [13]. Consequently, devitalization of the residual subchondral bone, which functions as an important load-sharing and support mechanism, is common. Postoperative CT images taken at an early stage demonstrated increased density of the subchondral bone, a pathognomonic feature of osteonecrosis [13]. Based on the radiographic findings, rapid bone incorporation without resorption of the cortex in the graft and revascularization of the necrotic region provided indirect evidence for the persisting vascularity of the VIBG. The two-year follow-up findings highlighted that mobile ankle function with a score of 76 was preserved in this young and active patient. Our suggested reasons for this suboptimal outcome were as follows. Firstly, to achieve better exposure of the tumor, most of the medial wall and partial articular cartilage of the talus was removed. Secondly, the precise scope of microwave ablation was difficult to control. Despite the continuous application of cool saline to the articular cartilage surface and adjacent normal tissues during the ablation, thermal injury to the cartilage surface might not have been entirely avoided. Thirdly, postoperative infection posed potential risk for cartilage damage [21], potentially leading to osteoarthritis. Encouragingly, no evidence of pain or stiffness of the ankle joint was observed at the two-year follow-up. The combination of VIBG reconstruction and early mobilization might have mitigated these risks. Although the classical method of arthrodesis or arthroplasty might also be an alternative management. The method of arthrodesis was accompanied by considerable function impairment and particularly indicated among the elderly and inactive, who would not need a mobile ankle. The method of arthroplasty had advantages in early-stage application, especially in function. However, prosthesis-related complications might cause future trouble [4, 5]. The greatest uncertainty concerns the long-term outcome, especially regarding the slight reduction in the medial joint space noted on postoperative radiographs. Should osteoarthritis develop at a later stage, the reestablished viable and stable bone bed would still permit salvage procedures, such as conversion to arthroplasty or arthrodesis. Furthermore, a second intralesional curettage and bone grafting would be indicated in the event of local recurrence, and denosumab therapy remains a potential treatment option [22].

It should be acknowledged that this case report has a number of limitations. The local recurrence and the long-term effect of ankle motion remain to be determined. While preoperative surgical planning and the use of intraoperative 3DP model guidance contributed to better theater efficiency and enhanced precision of reconstruction, the 3D printing workflow could be considered both expensive and time consuming. Additionally, VIBG harvest was technically challenging and time consuming. Obviously, larger series with longer follow up or comparative study with standard curettage combined with cement/allograft are needed for increased confidence.

Conclusion

We present a patient with a Campanacci grade III GCT in the talus who was successfully treated via a downgrading strategy. Following microwave ablation and aggressive tumor removal, the reconstruction of the large cavity with a VIBG, guided by a 3DP model, enabled the preservation of the ankle function. However, a longer follow-up is needed.
