The Brazilian Alagille syndrome study: New insights from a multicenter national cohort

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo elucidate the natural history of liver disease and identify the predictors of native liver survival (NLS) in a Brazilian cohort of children with Alagille syndrome (ALGS).MethodsMulticenter retrospective cohort study of children with ALGS. Descriptive statistics summarized clinical data. Overall survival was estimated using Kaplan–Meier curves. NLS was analyzed with competing risk models. Associations between laboratory parameters and NLS were tested using Pearson′s chi‐square.ResultsFrom 14 Brazilian reference centers, 120 children (52.5% male) were included, with mean age at diagnosis of 17.8 months and a median follow‐up time of 88.5 months. Most presented characteristic facies (89.9%), jaundice (88.2%), pruritus (87.9%), and neonatal cholestasis (79.2%). Thirty percent underwent liver transplantation (LT) and 6.7% died. Jaundice, xanthomas, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, and presence of excoriations were significantly associated with the need for LT or death. Direct bilirubin (DB) > 6 mg/dL, cholesterol >300 mg/dL, increased aspartate aminotransferase (AST) > 5 times the upper limit of normal (ULN), and AST‐to‐platelet ratio index (APRI) > 1 were associated with a significantly increased risk of LT or death, with hazard ratios of 18.2 (p < 0.001, 95% confidence interval [95% CI] = 5.1–64.5), 3.4 (p = 0.016, 95% CI = 1.3–9.2), 3.9 (p = 0.018, 95% CI = 1.2–12.4), and 10.2 (p = 0.013, 95% CI = 1.3–82.2), respectively.ConclusionsThe main clinical manifestations included characteristic facies, jaundice, and pruritus. Higher rates of LT or death were associated with jaundice, xanthomas, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly and presence of excoriations. Higher rates of NLS were associated with DB levels below 6 mg/dL, cholesterol levels below 300 mg/dL, AST levels <5x ULN, and an APRI < 1.

INTRODUCTION

First described by Daniel Alagille in 1969, Alagille syndrome (ALGS) is a complex developmental disorder with an estimated prevalence of 1 in 30,000–50,000 live births, 1 characterized by involvement of multiple organ systems and seven main features: intrahepatic cholestasis due to bile duct paucity, characteristic facies, cardiac defects, skeletal abnormalities, ocular abnormalities, and vascular and renal anomalies. 2 , 3 ALGS is mostly caused by mutations in the JAG1 gene (jagged canonical Notch ligand 1; located on chromosome 20p12), accounting for approximately 94% of cases. The remaining cases are attributed to mutations in the NOTCH2 gene (notch receptor 2; located on chromosome 1p12) or other unidentified genetic factors. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Mutations in JAG1 and NOTCH2 are typically heterozygous and approximately 50%–70% of ALGS cases represent de novo mutations, while the remainder are inherited. 7 , 8

The liver phenotype in ALGS is characterized by a paucity of intrahepatic bile ducts, resulting from impaired differentiation of hepatoblasts into cholangiocytes due to defective Notch signaling, with higher levels promoting cholangiocyte fate. Reduced Notch signaling disrupts the development and maintenance of bile ducts, leading to cholestasis and progressive liver damage. 9 The liver phenotype is present in more than 95% of patients with genetically confirmed ALGS, 1 with paucity of interlobular bile ducts manifesting clinically as cholestasis, jaundice, pruritus, xanthomas, and fat‐soluble vitamin deficiencies. End‐stage liver disease requiring transplantation eventually develops in approximately 30%–40% of patients during childhood. 10 Early onset of jaundice, high serum bilirubin and bile acid levels, and the presence of xanthomas are risk factors for poor hepatic outcomes. 10 Despite advances in understanding the genetic and molecular basis of ALGS, the clinical heterogeneity and variability in disease progression remain poorly characterized, especially in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) like Brazil. Additionally, identifying predictors of native liver survival (NLS) is clinically important because these markers support timely referral to pediatric liver transplant centers and help guide clinical decision‐making in settings where delayed access to transplantation may adversely affect outcomes.

The objective of this study was to describe the epidemiological characteristics, clinical manifestations and the natural history of liver disease in children with ALGS in Brazil, and to identify the predictors of survival with a native liver.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the ethics committee at each participating center and a waiver of informed consent was granted by each institutional review committee, if applicable. Conselho Nacional de Saúde/Ministério da Saúde (National Health Council/Ministry of Health) 466/2012, Certificado de Apresentação para Apreciação Ética (Certificate of Presentation for Ethical Review): 65567722.4.1001.0144, Number: 5.907.006.

Study overview

This is a multicenter retrospective study that included children diagnosed with ALGS, mainly based on established diagnostic criteria, 2 from 14 pediatric hepatology centers across different regions of Brazil. Genetic testing for JAG1 and NOTCH2 (cholestasis panel, by next‐generation sequencing, including genes implicated in ALGS, progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis, cystic fibrosis, and alpha‐1 antitrypsin deficiency) was performed in cases with diagnostic uncertainty or when clinically indicated, according to the availability at each center.

The study used standardized inclusion criteria and data collection procedures across all centers. From January 2023 to May 2024, data were extracted from medical records by trained personnel using a standardized REDCap form, with double data entry and periodic quality checks. 11 The following variables were evaluated: clinical characteristics; hematological, biochemical, and imaging tests; liver histology; and genetic tests. Clinical features such as hepatomegaly and splenomegaly were assessed according to local clinical practice, based on physical examination and/or imaging (primarily abdominal ultrasound), as documented in the medical records. For laboratory predictors, thresholds were considered reached only when exceeded on at least two separate measurements and outside episodes of sepsis or other acute clinical deterioration, to reduce the impact of transient abnormalities.

Statistical analysis

Qualitative data were described using frequencies/percentages and quantitative data summarized with descriptive statistics (mean, median, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, and interquartile range). Overall survival from diagnosis was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. NLS was assessed and associated with epidemiological, clinical features and laboratory alterations using Pearson′s chi‐square test calculated in exact form, when necessary, and Cox proportional hazards regression. Analyses were conducted on the subset of patients with available data for each variable. Overall patient survival rates were determined using the Kaplan–Meier method, and between‐group comparisons were made with the log‐rank test (Mantel‐Cox). A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and the analysis was performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 23.

RESULTS

One hundred and twenty children from various regions of Brazil were included, with 52.5% (n = 63) being male. The cohort included patients from different geographic regions of Brazil, reflecting a diverse population and health care settings. The median age at diagnosis and current age were 6.5 and 106.5 months, respectively. The median follow‐up time was 88.5 months (Table 1).

Clinical features

The most common clinical feature observed was characteristic facies (labeled before the diagnosis) present in 89.9% (n = 107/119) of the study population. This was followed by jaundice 88.2% (n = 105/119), pruritus 87.9% (n = 102/116), neonatal cholestasis 79.2% (n = 95/120), and excoriations 65% (n = 76/117). Xanthomas were observed in 33.6% (n = 39/116). Other findings included hepatomegaly 58.8% (n = 70/119), splenomegaly 49.2% (n = 58/118), pale stools 46.6% (n = 55/118), and renal anomalies 21.2% (n = 25/118). It is important to note that the manifestations were more frequently related to cholestatic disease (Table 1).

Genetic findings

Genetic testing for JAG1 and NOTCH2 mutations (cholestasis panel) was available for 41 of 120 patients (34.2%). Among those tested, JAG1 variants were identified in 38 patients (92.7%), while NOTCH2 variants were found in three patients (7.3%).

NLS and competing events (LT or death)

The cumulative incidence of NLS in the presence of competing events (LT or death) was 63.3% (n = 76/120). Thirty percent underwent LT (n = 36/120), with a median age of 40 months (range 8–151 months). The mortality rate was 6.7% (n = 8/120); all deaths occurred in the pre‐LT period, with a median age of 61.5 months (range 10–220 months) at the time of the event (Table 1). Deaths were attributed to sepsis, complications of renal failure, liver failure, and respiratory failure due to viral infection.

Predictors of survival with a native liver

Regarding predictors of survival with a native liver, from a clinical perspective, jaundice, xanthomas, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, and presence of excoriations were significantly associated with the need for LT or death. Among those with severe xanthomas (n = 9), 88.9% either underwent LT or died. These five clinical characteristics were significantly correlated with the need for LT or death (p < 0.01) (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This study provides valuable insights into the clinical characteristics, prognosis, and risk factors associated with ALGS in a Brazilian cohort. A total of 120 children with confirmed ALGS were analyzed.

The most common clinical manifestations observed were characteristic facial features (89.1%), jaundice (87.5%), and pruritus (85.0%), reinforcing the well‐documented phenotype of the disease. When comparing the data from this study with those from the GALA study, 10 regarding facial characteristics, we observed almost identical results between the two studies. Additionally, we found similar results for other features, such as pruritus, neonatal cholestasis, xanthomas, and the presence of any renal anomaly.

The mortality rate was also similar to that reported in the GALA study. However, the biggest difference was in NLS. The cumulative incidence of NLS was 63.3%, and 30% of the patients underwent LT at a median age of 40 months. Notably, the NLS rate was higher in the Brazilian study; however, it′s important to mention that the GALA study included patients up to adulthood, with 40% surviving with their native liver at 18 years, also higher than the 24% rate recently reported by the Childhood Liver Disease Research Network (ChiLDReN), and therefore may reflect a more severely affected group of patients in tertiary referral centers. 12 Importantly, this is one of the first large‐scale studies of ALGS conducted in a middle‐income country. Differences in health care infrastructure, access to genetic testing, and timing of LT may influence disease trajectory and survival when compared to cohorts from high‐income countries. This underscores the progressive nature of hepatic impairment in ALGS, necessitating early identification of at‐risk patients.

Recent studies have identified risk factors for poor hepatic outcomes, including early onset of jaundice, high serum bilirubin and bile acid levels, and the presence of xanthomas. 10 Regarding laboratory predictors, elevated DB levels (>6 mg/dL) were significantly correlated with poorer hepatic outcomes, increasing the likelihood of LT or death by 18.2 times. Similarly, cholesterol levels exceeding 300 mg/dL and AST levels above five times the ULN were associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes. These findings suggest that biochemical markers could play a crucial role in the clinical decision‐making process, aiding in early intervention strategies.

A shorter average time to death or LT among patients with DB levels above 6 mg/dL highlights the consequences of profound cholestasis and reveals the unmet need to develop targeted therapies for ALGS‐related cholestasis in the youngest children. Since not all children with cholestasis and ALGS inevitably require LT, it demonstrates variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance, resulting in a wide spectrum of clinical presentations, emphasizing that there is a strong need for early predictors of NLS. These findings are supported by previous research, which has highlighted early onset jaundice, elevated serum bilirubin, bile acid levels, and the presence of xanthomas as indicators of poor hepatic outcomes. 2 The association of elevated DB levels with decreased NLS further confirms the prognostic value of this biomarker, consistent with earlier studies demonstrating its correlation with disease progression. 10 , 12

Notably, the study emphasizes the impact of cholestatic disease as the predominant clinical manifestation. This aligns with previous findings that jaundice, and xanthomas are key indicators of disease severity. The need for LT or the risk of mortality was significantly associated with these clinical features, with 88.9% of patients with severe xanthomas undergoing LT or dying from the disease. As the study was conducted in tertiary referral centers, there is a potential selection bias toward patients with more severe hepatic manifestations or complex multi‐organ involvement.

Although the high prevalence of pruritus and xanthomas, as this was a retrospective study, it was not feasible to assess the severity and evolution from medical records, reflecting the variability with which these two clinical features were documented, and highlighting the need for standardized methods to assess and record these on a clinical basis. Additionally, data on the severity and evolution of extrahepatic features, such as cardiac and vascular anomalies, were limited by the retrospective design and variability in record keeping across centers. It is also important to note that the patients included in this study do not represent the total number of ALGS cases in Brazil, nor even the full ALGS population followed at each participating center, as inclusion depended on data availability and completeness of records. Future prospective studies, including the planned Brazilian ALGS registry, will be crucial to validate these findings, monitor long‐term outcomes, and inform national guidelines.

Importantly, this study coincides with a milestone in the management of ALGS in Brazil: the approval of maralixibat and odevixibat by the Brazilian Health Regulatory Agency (ANVISA) in 2025 for the treatment of cholestatic pruritus in this population. The approval of maralixibat was supported by the phase 2 ICONIC trial, which demonstrated sustained improvements in pruritus and reductions in serum bile acids over 48 weeks. 13 Similarly, the efficacy of odevixibat was confirmed in the phase 3 ASSERT trial, showing significant reductions in pruritus intensity and serum bile acid levels in patients with ALGS. 14 However, despite regulatory approval, these therapies are not yet routinely available within the Brazilian public health system (Sistema Único de Saúde—SUS), and only recently have the first patients in Brazil started receiving ileal bile acid transporter (IBAT) inhibitors. We believe our real‐world data will be particularly relevant for informing clinical decision‐making on patient selection for IBAT inhibitor therapy in Brazil and other similar healthcare settings. These findings reinforce the need for updated national clinical guidelines and integration of emerging therapies, as suggested by recent expert consensus on ALGS management. 2

CONCLUSIONS

In this Brazilian cohort of Allagile Syndrome, the main clinical manifestations included characteristic facies, jaundice, and pruritus. The NLS rate was 63.3%. Higher rates of LT or death were associated with jaundice, xanthomas, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, and presence of excoriations. Higher rates of NLS were associated with DB levels below 6 mg/dL, cholesterol levels below 300 mg/dL, AST levels less than five times the ULN, and an APRI score of less than 1.

Overall, the study reinforces the importance of close monitoring and timely management of children with ALGS, particularly considering the clinical and laboratory predictors identified. Continued research and the development of clinical guidelines are essential to optimize treatment protocols and improve long‐term outcomes for children with ALGS. Future research directions should focus on therapies to control pruritus, improve quality of life, and slow the progression of liver disease, with the ultimate goal of avoiding LT.
