VisualQuest: A Diverse Image Dataset for Evaluating Visual Recognition in LLMs
Kelaiti Xiao 1,2 , Liang Yang 1 , PAERHATI Tulajiang 1,2 , Hongfei Lin 1

Abstract
This paper introduces VisualQuest, a novel image dataset designed to assess the ability of large language models (LLMs) to interpret non-traditional, stylized imagery. Unlike conventional photographic benchmarks, VisualQuest challenges models with images that incorporate abstract, symbolic, and metaphorical elements, requiring the integration of domain-specific knowledge and advanced reasoning. The dataset was meticulously curated through multiple stages of filtering, annotation, and standardization to ensure high quality and diversity. Our evaluations using several state-of-the-art multimodal LLMs reveal significant performance variations that underscore the importance of both factual background knowledge and inferential capabilities in visual recognition tasks. VisualQuest thus provides a robust and comprehensive benchmark for advancing research in multimodal reasoning and model architecture design.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable success in processing complex textual data, and their capabilities have recently extended into multimodal tasks that integrate visual and textual inputs
[
1
]
. Applications such as automated captioning of everyday scenes and the analysis of medical imagery have showcased these models’ enhanced capacity to interpret visual information when combined with robust learning paradigms
[
2
]
. Nevertheless, most existing multimodal benchmarks predominantly focus on direct image captioning or coarse-grained classification tasks using real-world datasets like ImageNet
[
3
]
. While valuable, these approaches do not adequately assess the ability of LLMs to leverage domain-specific background knowledge in more sophisticated recognition scenarios.

To bridge this gap, we introduce “VisualQuest,” a dataset specifically designed to evaluate the performance of LLMs on images that deviate from conventional photographic representations. Unlike traditional datasets, VisualQuest centers on visuals infused with stylized, symbolic, and at times surrealistic elements. Popular imagery—such as caricatures, minimalistic designs, emoji-based symbols, and creative illustrations—presents unique recognition challenges that extend beyond simple object detection. Instead, successful interpretation demands familiarity with cultural, literary, or domain-specific references and the ability to decode abstract visual cues
[
4
]
.

VisualQuest comprises 3,529 images organized into four thematic categories: (1) Public Figures, (2) Popular Media, (3) Linguistic Expressions, and (4) Literature Works. Each category features artistic renditions—including memoji, caricature, symbolism, and visual puns—that intensify the complexity of the recognition task. For instance, cartoonish portrayals of renowned authors necessitate an understanding of literary figures, while minimalistic movie posters require comprehension of key cinematic elements. Moreover, when images incorporate symbolic idioms, proverbs, or slang, the recognition task relies on linking abstract visual clues with the appropriate cultural or linguistic concepts.

In summary, VisualQuest offers a systematic benchmark that transcends standard object recognition by integrating domain-specific knowledge with abstract visual reasoning. By challenging LLMs to interpret non-traditional imagery, our work aims to contribute a valuable foundation for future advancements in multimodal reasoning and model architecture design.

Dataset Details

This section describes the construction of the
VisualQuest
dataset, detailing the data collection process, filtering and curation pipelines, presentation method labeling, as well as annotation steps for questions and standardized labels. The final dataset consists of 3,529 images across four main categories and highlights multiple presentation styles specifically chosen to challenge conventional image-recognition capabilities in LLMs.

3.1
Data Collection

We recruited 10 student collectors, each instructed to gather 500 images from various online resources. The images were required to fulfill the following criteria:

•

Belong to one of four targeted categories:
Public Figures, Popular Media, Linguistic Expressions
, and
Literature Works
.

•

Use
non-realistic
or stylized presentation methods—examples included
caricature
,
illustration
,
emoji combination
, or
visual pun
(among others).

•

Include sufficient cultural, literary, or symbolic elements that deviate from straightforward photographic representations.

Throughout this stage, example images were provided to each collector to illustrate how stylized or symbolic depictions might appear. This example set helped ensure that the collected images reflected the dataset’s emphasis on non-traditional, symbolic cues.

3.2
Data Curation and Annotation

We began with an initial pool of 5,000 images sourced from decentralized collection efforts. To produce a concise and high-quality dataset, we applied several filtering and annotation steps described below, ultimately arriving at 3,529 images.

Duplicate Removal
:All repeated images were eliminated to preserve a strictly unique collection. This process both reduced the dataset size and helped avoid skewed representation of identical instances.

Unrecognizable Removal
: Ten English-major reviewers each attempted to identify the main entity or concept depicted in every image. Any image that failed to yield agreement from at least two reviewers was excluded, thereby ensuring that only interpretable visuals remained.

Presentation Method
: Images were annotated with a suitable presentation style, initially chosen from seven canonical categories (e.g., “Memoji,” “Caricature,” “Minimalism”), plus an “Other” option for styles not covered. After consolidation, three new categories (“Cartoon,” “Iconography,” and “Surrealism”) were added. Each image’s final method label was determined by majority vote, with ties settled by a designated arbiter.

Removal of Extra Characters
: Many images featured superimposed text (e.g., a celebrity’s name or a work’s title) that could compromise objective recognition. Non-essential text was removed to maintain a fair evaluation. However, any textual element deemed integral to the image’s symbolism or pun was retained.

Question Annotation
: Each image was assigned a concise query, beginning either with “Which” (for more straightforward recognition) or “Guess” (for puzzle-like images requiring deeper inference). This structure guides LLMs to combine recognition, domain knowledge, and reasoning.

Label Standardization
: Finally, we harmonized label usage, presentation method naming, and case conventions. In instances where an image could be recognized by multiple valid labels (e.g., character name or literary source), identifying any correct answer was considered sufficient. This standardization step helped maintain consistency across the entire dataset.

3.3
Dataset Structure

The final dataset comprises a total of 3,529 images distributed across four main categories. In particular, the dataset includes 1,024 images of Public Figures (e.g., Authors, Actors/Actresses, Scientists, Singers, Directors); 668 images related to Popular Media (e.g., Films, Television Shows, Anime, Mobile Applications, Songs, Video Games); 954 images representing Linguistic Expressions (e.g., Idioms, Proverbs, Words, Phrases, Slang); and 883 images corresponding to Literature Works (e.g., Novels, Fairy Tales, Epics, Folklores, Heroes, Characters, Deities). This balanced distribution ensures a diverse and comprehensive benchmark for assessing the capabilities of LLMs in recognizing both conventional and non-traditional visual cues.

Each image is stored in a dictionary-like structure with the following information:

1.

Standard Label:
Unambiguous entity or concept, standardized for consistent usage.

2.

General Category:
One of the four main categories.

3.

Question:
The query posed to the model (beginning with “Which” or “Guess”).

4.

Presentation Method:
One of the ten defined methods (e.g., Caricature, Minimalism, Visual Pun).

5.

Baseline Results:
Binary performance indicators from six baseline LLMs.

6.

Recognition Count:
Number of the baseline LLMs (out of six) that correctly recognized the image.

By assembling a large and stylistically diverse set of images, each annotated with multiple dimensions (label, presentation method, etc.), the
VisualQuest
dataset aims to reveal how well multimodal language models can link symbolic, cultural, and literary cues to perform accurate recognition. This multi-stage curation ensures a benchmark that is both challenging and richly informative for evaluating the evolving capabilities of LLMs in recognizing non-traditional imagery.

Overview of Multimodal LLMs

•

•

•

QVQ-72B-Preview
is an open-sourced experimental model developed by the Qwen team, focused on elevating visual reasoning capabilities alongside robust language performance.

By comparing their performance on stylized, symbolic, and non-traditional images, we aim to identify key areas where each approach excels and where further research may drive the next wave of advancements in multimodal tasks.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced VisualQuest—a novel and diverse image dataset designed to probe the limits of large language models (LLMs) in interpreting non-traditional, stylized imagery. Unlike conventional photographic benchmarks, VisualQuest challenges models with abstract, symbolic, and metaphorical visual cues that necessitate a combination of domain-specific background knowledge and advanced inferential reasoning. The dataset, curated through rigorous multi-stage filtering and annotation, comprises 3,529 images organized across four thematic categories and multiple presentation styles, thereby establishing a versatile benchmark for assessing multimodal recognition capabilities.

Our extensive experiments with state-of-the-art multimodal LLMs demonstrated that while models such as Gemini and GPT exhibit robust performance—particularly in scenarios grounded in well-known public figures and visually abstract constructs—there remains a significant performance gap among models when dealing with highly stylized images. These discrepancies underscore both the current limitations in the integration of factual knowledge and the need for enhanced reasoning strategies within multimodal architectures.

Overall, the findings presented highlight the dual importance of factual background knowledge and sophisticated reasoning skills in overcoming the challenges posed by abstract visual content. VisualQuest not only bridges the gap between traditional object recognition and abstract visual reasoning but also offers a critical benchmark poised to drive further advancements in the design and training of multimodal LLMs.
