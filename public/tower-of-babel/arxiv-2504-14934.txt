On negative eigenvalues of 1D Schrödinger operators with δ ′ \delta^{\prime} -like potentials
Yuriy Golovaty, Rostyslav Hryniv

Abstract
. We study the asymptotic behavior of the discrete spectrum of one-dimensional Schrödinger operators with δ ′ \delta^{\prime} -like potentials, which are used to construct exactly solvable models for localized dipoles in quantum mechanics. Although these operators converge in the norm resolvent topology to a limiting operator that is bounded from below, we prove that they can possess a finite but arbitrarily large number of discrete eigenvalues that diverge to negative infinity as the regularization parameter tends to zero. This phenomenon illustrates a spectral instability of Schrödinger operators with these singular potentials.

1.
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to establish the existence and describe the asymptotic behavior of negative eigenvalues of one-dimensional Schrödinger operators that serve as regularizations of formal Hamiltonians involving
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
potentials. These questions arise in the construction and analysis of exactly solvable models in quantum mechanics, a topic that continues to draw considerable attention in the literature (see
[
1
,
2
]
, as well as comprehensive reference lists therein, covering works up to the early 2000s).

Some point interactions (i.e., pseudopotentials supported on discrete sets) naturally lead to well-defined exactly solvable models; others, however, exhibit essential ambiguities in defining the corresponding Hamiltonians. A notable example of this contrast is provided by the
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
potentials. In the case of the
\delta
potential, the differential equation
-y^{\prime\prime}+\alpha\delta(x)y=\lambda y
is well-posed in the space of distributions
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
and has a two-dimensional solution space. In contrast, the equation
-y^{\prime\prime}+\alpha\delta^{\prime}(x)y=\lambda y
is ill-posed in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
and admits only the trivial solution when
\alpha\neq 0
. Moreover, while every reasonable regularization of Hamiltonians involving the
\delta
potential yields the same exactly solvable model, the
\delta^{\prime}
potential is sensitive to the regularization procedure, and different approximations may lead to different point interactions. As a result, the choice of the exactly solvable model for
\delta^{\prime}
potentials is not determined by mathematical considerations alone but must reflect the specifics of the particular physical experiment—a feature that only enriches the study of exactly solvable models.

In this paper, we demonstrate a further distinction between the
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
potentials, this time concerning the spectral properties of their regularized Hamiltonians. Natural approximations of both the
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
potentials by regular potentials yield operator families that converge in the norm resolvent topology to semi-bounded limits. However, we show that in contrast to
\delta
-like perturbations,
\delta^{\prime}
-like perturbations lead to operator families that are not uniformly bounded from below as the regularization parameter tends to zero. As a consequence, such regularized Hamiltonians can possess a finite (but arbitrarily large) number of low-lying eigenvalues that diverge to negative infinity. We explicitly determine the number of these eigenvalues and describe their asymptotic behavior in the singular limit.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
gives a brief overview of studies on exactly solvable models for Hamiltonians with
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
potentials. In Section
3
, we derive conditions under which Schrödinger operators with
\delta^{\prime}
-like potentials possess low-lying eigenvalues that diverge to negative infinity as the regularization parameter tends to zero.
Section
4
introduces methods for estimating the number of such eigenvalues and shows, in particular, that the emergence of a discrete spectrum is closely related to zero-energy resonances for the corresponding Schrödinger operators. In Section
5
we investigate how the non-trivial interaction of
\delta
-like and
\delta^{\prime}
-like perturbations leads to the emergence of a negative eigenvalue with a finite limit as the perturbation parameter tends to zero. Finally, Section
6
contains the proofs of Theorems
3
and
4
on the asymptotic behavior of eigenvalues.

2.
Short review of exactly solvable models for
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
potentials

In this section, we review existing approaches to constructing exactly solvable quantum mechanical models for one-dimensional Hamiltonians with pseudo-potentials involving the Dirac
\delta
-function and its derivative
\delta^{\prime}
.

The simplest case is the formal (pseudo-)Hamiltonian

-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\alpha\delta(x),\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.1)

Any reasonable method of associating a self-adjoint Hamiltonian to (
2.1
) – such as form-sum, generalized sum method, approximation by regular potentials – yields the same operator
H
, acting as
Hy=-y^{\prime\prime}
on the domain

\mathop{\rm dom}H=\big\{y\in W^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus{0})\colon y(+0)=y(-0),\;\;y^{\prime}(+0)-y^{\prime}(-0)=\alpha y(0)\big\}.

In other words, the distributional potential
\alpha\delta(x)
in the one-dimensional Schrödinger operator results in the point interaction imposing the interface condition

\begin{pmatrix}y(+0)\\
y^{\prime}(+0)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\alpha&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}y(-0)\\
y^{\prime}(-0)\end{pmatrix}.

(2.2)

Moreover, this model serves as a good approximation in the norm resolvent sense of the Schrödinger operators with integrable potentials of special form. Specifically, given a real-valued function
U
of compact support such that

\alpha=\int_{\mathbb{R}}U(x)\,dx,

the scaled potentials
U_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-1}U(\varepsilon^{-1}x)
converge in the space of distributions
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
to the distribution
\alpha\delta(x)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, and the corresponding operators

-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\varepsilon^{-1}U(\varepsilon^{-1}x)

converge to
H
in the norm resolvent sense
[
1
, Theorem I.3.2.3]
, i.e. their resolvents converge in operator norm to the resolvent of
H
. Similar convergence results hold even in the presence of background potentials
W
, i.e., for operators of the form

-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+W(x)+\alpha\delta(x).

Figure 1.
An example of
\delta^{\prime}
-like potentials.

One should not expect that every pseudopotential gives rise to a unique point interaction. Certain pseudopotentials are highly sensitive to the way they are approximated, and the
\delta^{\prime}
potential is one of them. In physics, the symbol
\delta^{\prime}
is often used to describe a strongly localized dipole-type potential, such as a high narrow barrier followed by a deep well (see Fig.
1
). Let
V
be an integrable function with compact support and a finite first moment; then the sequence
\varepsilon^{-2}V(\varepsilon^{-1}x)
converges in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, if and only if
\int_{\mathbb{R}}V(x)\,dx=0
, and in that case

\varepsilon^{-2}V(\varepsilon^{-1}x)\to\beta\delta^{\prime}(x),

(2.3)

where
\beta=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}xV(x)\,dx
. For this reason, we refer to such families of scaled potentials as
\delta^{\prime}
-
like
.

The question of how to correctly define the formal Hamiltonian

-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\beta\delta^{\prime}(x)

(2.4)

has a long and intricate history. As mentioned above, difficulties arise already at the level of interpreting the differential expression in (
2.4
), since the equation
-y^{\prime\prime}+\beta\delta^{\prime}(x)y=\lambda y
admits only the trivial solution in the space of distributions
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. Indeed, the product
\delta^{\prime}(x)\phi(x)
is well defined in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
only if
\phi
is continuously differentiable, and in that case it is equal to the distribution
\phi(0)\delta^{\prime}(x)-\phi^{\prime}(0)\delta(x)
. However, any nontrivial solution
y
of the above equation would have to be discontinuous at the origin, since its second derivative
y^{\prime\prime}=-\beta\delta^{\prime}(x)y+\lambda y
would necessarily include a
\delta^{\prime}
term. In this case, the product
\delta^{\prime}(x)y(x)
is not defined in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, making the equation invalid.

Moreover, the operator in (
2.4
) cannot be rigorously defined using standard approaches like the form-sum or generalized sum methods, or as a relatively bounded perturbation of the free Hamiltonian. For this reason, it is natural to approach this problem via regularization: one considers families of Schrödinger operators of the form

H_{\varepsilon}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\varepsilon^{-2}\,V(\varepsilon^{-1}x),\qquad\mathop{\rm dom}H_{\varepsilon}=W^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{R}),

with
\delta^{\prime}
-like potentials
\varepsilon^{-2}\,V(\varepsilon^{-1}x)
as a starting point for studying physical phenomena associated with zero-range dipoles. The construction of exactly solvable models for such dipole interactions is thus reduced to analyzing the limits of
H_{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

It has been shown that the operator family
H_{\varepsilon}
indeed converges in the norm resolvent sense as
\varepsilon\to 0
. In a seminal paper, Šeba
[
3
]
argued that the limiting operator is the free Hamiltonian
D_{0}
decoupled at the origin by the Dirichlet condition, namely

D_{0}y=-y^{\prime\prime},\qquad\mathop{\rm dom}D_{0}=\big\{y\in W^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus{0})\colon y(0)=0\big\}.

(2.5)

According to this result, no meaningful definition of a Schrödinger operator with a
\delta^{\prime}
-potential would be possible, since the limit
D_{0}
is completely impenetrable to a quantum particle and is independent of the specific form of the function
V
. However, this conclusion contradicts the findings of Zolotaryuk
et al.

[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
, who analyzed transmission probabilities through piecewise constant
\delta^{\prime}
-like potentials and observed examples of quantum tunnelling. These results prompted the revision of
[
3
]
; it was later rigorously proved in
[
8
]
that the operator
D_{0}
is the norm resolvent limit of
H_{\varepsilon}
only in the so-called
non-resonant
case, while in the
resonant
case, the situation is different.

We begin by recalling the relevant definitions. The operator
-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+V
is said to have a
zero-energy resonance
if the equation
v^{\prime\prime}=Vv
admits a non-trivial solution
v
that is bounded on the entire real line. Such a solution is called a
half-bound state
, and the potential
V
is then referred to as
resonant
. Every half-bound state
v
has finite, nonzero limits
v_{\pm}
at
\pm\infty
, and the ratio

\theta=\frac{v_{+}}{v_{-}}

is uniquely determined by
V
.
As proved in
[
8
]
(see also
[
9
,
10
]
), if the potential
V
is resonant, then the family
H_{\varepsilon}
converges in the norm resolvent sense as
\varepsilon\to 0
to the self-adjoint operator

H(\theta)=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}},\;\;\mathop{\rm dom}H(\theta)=\big\{y\in W^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus{0}):y(+0)=\theta y(-0),\;\theta y^{\prime}(+0)=y^{\prime}(-0)\}.

Regardless of whether the family
\varepsilon^{-2}V(\varepsilon^{-1}x)
converges in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
or not, the Schrödinger operators
H_{\varepsilon}
converge in the norm resolvent sense to either
H(\theta)
or
D_{0}
depending on whether
V
is resonant or non-resonant. Moreover, there is no functional dependence between the constant
\beta
appearing in the distributional limit (
2.3
) and the interface parameter
\theta
in the point interaction

\begin{pmatrix}y(+0)\\
y^{\prime}(+0)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\theta&0\\
0&\theta^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}y(-0)\\
y^{\prime}(-0)\end{pmatrix}

(2.6)

corresponding to
H(\theta)
.
Two different resonant, zero-mean potentials
V
may produce the same
\beta
but different values of
\theta
, and conversely, the same
\theta
may arise for different
\beta
. It is worth noting that Kurasov
[
11
,
12
]
was the first to establish a connection between the
\delta^{\prime}
-potential and the point interactions described by (
2.6
).

The approximations of pseudo-Hamiltonians
-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\alpha\delta(x)+\beta\delta^{\prime}(x)
by the Hamiltonians

-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\varepsilon^{-1}U(\varepsilon^{-1}x)+\varepsilon^{-2}\,V(\varepsilon^{-1}x)

with every regular functions
U
and
V
also converge in the norm resolvent topology
[
13
,
14
]
. If
V
is non-resonant, the operators converge to
D_{0}
. However, if
V
is resonant with a half-bound state
v
, then the limiting operator is associated with point interaction producing the interface conditions

\begin{pmatrix}y(+0)\\
y^{\prime}(+0)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\theta&0\\
\eta&\theta^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}y(-0)\\
y^{\prime}(-0)\end{pmatrix},

(2.7)

where

\theta=\frac{v_{+}}{v_{-}},\qquad\eta=\frac{1}{v_{-}v_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Uv^{2}\,dx.

(2.8)

A comprehensive study of exactly solvable models with point interactions (
2.7
) has been done by Gadella, Nieto
et al

[
15
,
16
,
17
]
. Besides the approximation of pseudopotentials by regular potentials, there are other methods to construct exactly solvable models: e.g., the method of self-adjoint extensions has been used by Nizhnik
[
18
,
19
]
, and the distributional approach has been proposed by Lunardi, Manzoni
et al
[
20
,
21
]
.

We note that the point interactions characterized by the interface conditions

\begin{pmatrix}y(+0)\\
y^{\prime}(+0)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\beta\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}y(-0)\\
y^{\prime}(-0)\end{pmatrix},

commonly referred to as
\delta^{\prime}
-
interactions
, are also sometimes interpreted as models of the formal
\delta^{\prime}
potential.
Exner, Neidhardt, and Zagrebnov
[
22
]
proposed a refined potential approximation of such interactions using a family of three
\delta
-like potentials centered at the points
\pm a
and
0
, with the separation distance
a
tending to zero in a carefully coordinated way with the coupling constants.
Further contributions in this direction include the works of Cheon and Shigehara
[
23
]
, Zolotaryuk
[
24
]
, and Albeverio, Fassari, and Rinaldi
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
; see also the recent publication
[
28
]
.
Although the potential families in
[
22
]
do not converge to
\delta^{\prime}
in the sense of distributions, the term “
\delta^{\prime}
-interactions” can be partially justified by interpreting
\delta^{\prime}
as a finite-rank perturbation; see
[
29
,
30
]
for further discussion.

Let
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
be the dual pairing between the Sobolev spaces
W_{2}^{-s}(\mathbb{R})
and
W_{2}^{s}(\mathbb{R})
. Since
\delta(x)y(x)=y(0)\delta(x)=\langle\delta,y\rangle\delta(x)
, the formal operator (
2.1
) can be written as

-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\alpha\langle\delta,\,\cdot\,\rangle\delta(x).

(2.9)

This shows that the
\delta
-potential can be interpreted as a rank-one perturbation of the free Hamiltonian, and the standard theory of regular finite-rank perturbations yields the same exactly solvable model as in (
2.2
). In the physical literature, the
\delta^{\prime}
-interaction is typically associated with rank-one perturbation of the free Hamiltonian as in (
2.9
) but with
\delta^{\prime}
in place of
\delta

[
1
, Ch. 1.4]
:

-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\beta\langle\delta^{\prime},\,\cdot\,\rangle\delta^{\prime}(x).

(2.10)

The more general results of
[
31
,
32
]
imply that there exist regular potentials
\phi_{\varepsilon},\psi_{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
converging to
\delta^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
such that the rank-one perturbations of the free Hamiltonian,

-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\beta\langle\phi_{\varepsilon},\ \cdot\ \rangle\psi_{\varepsilon}(x),

converge to (
2.10
) in the strong resolvent topology as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

However, the model (
2.10
) is not directly related to the formal expression (
2.4
) with a
\delta^{\prime}
-potential. Indeed, if the product
\delta^{\prime}(x)y(x)
is well defined in the distributional sense, then

\delta^{\prime}(x)y(x)=y(0)\delta^{\prime}(x)-y^{\prime}(0)\delta(x).

Using this identity, the formal expression (
2.4
) can be interpreted as a rank-two perturbation of the free Schrödinger operator:

-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\beta\langle\delta,\,\cdot\,\rangle\delta^{\prime}(x)+\beta\langle\delta^{\prime},\,\cdot\,\rangle\delta(x).

In
[
33
,
34
]
, the norm resolvent convergence of the regular Hamiltonians

-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+(g_{\varepsilon},\cdot)\,f_{\varepsilon}+(f_{\varepsilon},\cdot)\,g_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{-1}U\left(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\right).

was studied. Here,
f_{\varepsilon}
and
g_{\varepsilon}
are sequences of real-valued functions in
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
f_{\varepsilon}\to\delta^{\prime}
and
g_{\varepsilon}\to\delta
in the distributional sense, and
(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the inner product in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Under suitable assumptions on
f_{\varepsilon}
,
g_{\varepsilon}
, and the potential
U
, such operators were shown to approximate the two-parameter family of point interactions defined by the interface conditions

\begin{pmatrix}y(+0)\\
y^{\prime}(+0)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mu&\varkappa\\
0&\mu^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}y(-0)\\
y^{\prime}(-0)\end{pmatrix}.

Although there is no established theory of distributions on metric graphs, the notions of
\delta
-like and
\delta^{\prime}
-like potentials can be naturally extended to this setting. The construction of exactly solvable models on quantum graphs, as well as the approximation of singular vertex couplings—including mixed
\alpha\delta^{\prime}+\beta\delta
interactions—has been explored in
[
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
.

The above results illustrate the richness of approaches to modeling point interactions and exactly solvable models in quantum mechanics. While
\delta
-potentials admit a canonical interpretation, the situation becomes especially delicate when the formal
\delta^{\prime}
-potential is involved, as different approximations may lead to different exactly solvable models. The choice of the appropriate limit operator is, therefore, not unique and must be guided by the physical or mathematical context of the problem.

3.
Existence of low-lying eigenvalues for
\delta^{\prime}
-like potentials

Let us consider the family of operators

\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+W(x)+\varepsilon^{-1}U(\varepsilon^{-1}x)+\varepsilon^{-2}\,V(\varepsilon^{-1}x),\qquad\mathop{\rm dom}\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}=W^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{R}),

(3.1)

where
U
,
V
and
W
are compactly supported
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
-potentials. This restriction on the potentials avoids unnecessary technical complications; however, the results remain valid for a significantly broader class of potentials (cf.
[
10
]
for an example of how this constraint can be relaxed). We are interested in the emergence of negative eigenvalues in Hamiltonians due to
\delta
-like and
\delta^{\prime}
-like perturbations. Accordingly, we assume that
W\geq 0
, so that the unperturbed operator
H_{0}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+W
is non-negative and has a purely continuous spectrum.

As follows from the result of
[
13
]
, the operators
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
converge in the norm resolvent sense as
\varepsilon\to 0
. If the potential
V
is resonant, i.e., possesses a half-bound state
v
(see Section
2
), then
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
converge to the operator

\mathcal{H}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+W,\quad\mathop{\rm dom}\mathcal{H}=\big\{\phi\in W^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\colon\\
\phi(+0)=\theta\phi(-0),\quad\phi^{\prime}(+0)=\theta^{-1}\phi^{\prime}(-0)+\eta\phi(-0)\big\},

(3.2)

where
\theta
and
\eta
are given by (
2.8
).
In the non-resonant case, the family converges to the operator
D_{0}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+W
subject to the Dirichlet boundary condition at the origin as in (
2.5
). Both
\mathcal{H}
and
D_{0}
can be interpreted as perturbations of the operator
H_{0}
by point interactions at the origin.

If the potential
V
is zero, then the family
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded from below as
\varepsilon\to 0
. This follows from the fact that the
\delta
-like perturbation is form-bounded relative
H_{0}
, with relative bound
a<1
, so that there is a
b>0
such that

\forall\,\phi\in W^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{R}):\qquad\varepsilon^{-1}\big(U(\varepsilon^{-1}\cdot)\phi,\phi\big)\leq a(H_{0}\phi,\phi)+b(\phi,\phi).

In this case, as we show below, the operators
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
may have at most one eigenvalue, and this eigenvalue converges to a finite limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

In contrast, when
V
is not identically zero, although the limiting operators
\mathcal{H}
and
D_{0}
are semibounded from below, the family
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
is generally not uniformly bounded from below. Consequently, the family
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
may exhibit eigenvalues that diverge to
-\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0
; we refer to such eigenvalues as
low-lying eigenvalues
.

The following result characterizes precisely when such eigenvalues may occur.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
be the family of operators defined by (
3.1
). Then the operators
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
admit low-lying eigenvalues as
\varepsilon\to 0
if and only if the potential
V
is not identically zero and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}V\,dx\leq 0.

Moreover, the number of such eigenvalues is finite.

Proof.

Assume that the potential
V
is not identically zero and that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}V\,dx\leq 0
. By
[
39
, Th.XIII.110]
, the operator
H=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+V
has then at least one negative eigenvalue
-\omega^{2}
, and let
u
be a corresponding normalized eigenfunction. Denote by
W_{\varepsilon}(x)=W(x)+\varepsilon^{-1}U(\varepsilon^{-1}x)+\varepsilon^{-2}\,V(\varepsilon^{-1}x)
the perturbed potential in (
3.1
) and introduce the quadratic form

a_{\varepsilon}[\phi]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(|\phi^{\prime}(x)|^{2}+W_{\varepsilon}(x)|\phi(x)|^{2}\bigr)\,dx.

Then the scaled function
u_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-1/2}u\left(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)
belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and has norm one. A direct computation shows that

\varepsilon^{2}a_{\varepsilon}[u_{\varepsilon}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(|u^{\prime}(t)|^{2}+V(t)|u(t)|^{2}\bigr)\,dt+\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}}U\left(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)|u_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{2}\,dx\\
+\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}W(x)|u_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{2}\,dx=-\omega^{2}(1+O(\varepsilon)),

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Therefore, for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, one has
a_{\varepsilon}[u_{\varepsilon}]\leq-\frac{1}{2}\omega^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}
, and we conclude from the minimax principle that there exists an eigenvalue
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
of
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
such that
\lambda_{\varepsilon}\leq-\frac{1}{2}\omega^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}
.

Assume now that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}V\,dx>0
. Then for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, the integral

\int_{\mathbb{R}}W_{\varepsilon}(x)\,dx=\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}V(x)\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}U(x)\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}W(x)\,dx

is positive, and again by
[
39
, Th. XIII.110]
the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
has no negative eigenvalues.

Let
N_{\varepsilon}
be the number of negative eigenvalues of
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
. It is known
[
40
, Th.5.3]
that the inequality

N_{\varepsilon}\leq 1+\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|\>|W^{-}_{\varepsilon}(x)|\,dx

holds, where
f^{-}=\min\{f,0\}
is the negative part of a function
f
.
In view of the assumption
W\geq 0
, the negative part
W^{-}_{\varepsilon}
comes only from the
V
and
U
terms, and we estimate the integral above as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|\>|W^{-}_{\varepsilon}(x)|\,dx

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|\>|V^{-}\left(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)+\varepsilon U^{-}\left(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}|t|\>|V^{-}(t)|\,dt+\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}}|t|\>|U^{-}(t)|\,dt.

The right-hand side remains bounded uniformly in small
\varepsilon
, and thus
N_{\varepsilon}
is bounded as
\varepsilon\to 0
, which completes the proof.
∎

Relatively (form-) bounded symmetric perturbations preserve semi-boundedness of the perturbed operator; see
[
43
, Th. IV.4.11, Th. VI.1.38]
. However, even if a family of self-adjoint operators
A_{\varepsilon}
converges in the norm resolvent sense as
\varepsilon\to 0
to a self-adjoint operator
A
that is bounded from below, the family
A_{\varepsilon}
may fail to be uniformly bounded from below. Even if each operator
A_{\varepsilon}
is individually semi-bounded, its lower bound may diverge to
-\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. A classical example due to Rellich
[
43
, Ex. IV.4.14]
gives such an operator family
A_{\varepsilon}
with a single eigenvalue tending to
-\infty
. The family of operators
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
with
\delta^{\prime}
-like perturbations provides a much stronger illustration of this effect. While
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
converges in the norm resolvent topology to a self-adjoint operator that is bounded from below, the number of eigenvalues that diverge to
-\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0
can be arbitrary but finite. In the next section, we describe the procedure for counting these low-lying eigenvalues.

4.
Counting the number of low-lying eigenvalues

Let us consider the Schrödinger operators

T_{\alpha}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\alpha V(x),\qquad\mathop{\rm dom}T_{\alpha}=W^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{R}),

(4.1)

with real coupling constant
\alpha
. We denote by
\mathcal{R}(V)
the set of all values of
\alpha
for which the potential
\alpha V
is resonant. For each non-zero function
V\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with compact support, the set
\mathcal{R}(V)
is a countable subset of
\mathbb{R}
with accumulation points at

[
44
]
.

We now recall the following definition
[
42
]
. Let

and

be self-adjoint operators, with

relatively
A
-compact. Suppose that
(a,b)
is a spectral gap of

and that

of the perturbed operator

in the gap

for all

, and if

as

, then

is called a

. Klaus
[
42
]
established a connection between resonant potentials and such coupling constant thresholds. Both phenomena are closely related to the emergence of negative eigenvalues in Schrödinger operators.

Suppose that a Schrödinger operator
-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\mathcal{V}
has a zero-energy resonance with a corresponding half-bound state
v
. According to
[
42
, Th. 3.2]
, if
\mathcal{U}
is a real-valued potential such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{U}v^{2}\,dx<0,

then the perturbed operator
H_{\varkappa}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\mathcal{V}+\varkappa\mathcal{U}
,

, has a coupling constant threshold at
\varkappa=0
and possesses a unique threshold eigenvalue
\lambda_{\varkappa}
obeying the asymptotics
\lambda_{\varkappa}=-a^{2}\varkappa^{2}+O(\varkappa^{3})
, as
\varkappa\to 0
, where the coefficient

is given by

a=\frac{1}{v_{-}^{2}+v_{+}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{U}v^{2}\,dx.

(4.2)

By reversing the direction of
\varkappa

\varkappa
increases from zero, the operator
H_{\varkappa}
acquires a negative eigenvalue that detaches from the bottom of the continuous spectrum.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that the support of the potential

is contained in the interval

. We now consider the spectral

with spectral parameter

[

\begin{gathered}-\frac{d^{2}u}{dx^{2}}+(V(x)+\omega^{2})u=0,\qquad x\in(-1,1),\\
\frac{du}{dx}(-1)-\omega u(-1)=0,\quad\frac{du}{dx}(1)+\omega u(1)=0.\end{gathered}

(4.3)

A complex number

is called an eigenvalue of the Regge problem if there exists a nontrivial solution

of (

), in which case

is a corresponding eigenfunction.

Theorem 2
.

The number of low-lying eigenvalues of
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
is equal to each of the following:

(i)

the number of negative eigenvalues of the operator
T_{1}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+V(x)
;

(ii)

the number of points in the set

belonging to the interval

(iii)

the number of positive eigenvalues
\omega
of the Regge problem (

Proof.

(i)
Consider the family of Schrödinger operators

\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+V(x)+\varepsilon U(x)+\varepsilon^{2}W(\varepsilon x)

. This family is uniformly bounded from below and converges to the operator

in the norm resolvent sense as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Suppose that

has
n

n

is unitarily equivalent to

has exactly
n

(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(i)
The operator
T_{0}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}
has no eigenvalues.
Suppose that the set

is nonempty, and let

be its smallest element.
We write

\int_{\mathbb{R}}Vv_{1}^{2}\,dx=-\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{v^{\prime}_{1}}^{2}\,dx<0,

and the operator

and

As the parameter

, and at each such crossing the operator

acquires a new simple eigenvalue. Since no negative eigenvalue can get absorbed by the continuous spectrum as

]

.

(iii)
\Leftrightarrow
(i)

Suppose

is an eigenvalue of the operator

, with the corresponding eigenfunction

\psi(x)=\begin{cases}u(-1)\,e^{\omega(x+1)},&\text{if }x<-1,\\
\hfil u(x),&\text{if }|x|\leq 1,\\
u(1)\,e^{-\omega(x-1)},&\text{if }x>1.\end{cases}

(4.4)

Conversely, if

is an eigenfunction of

corresponding to eigenvalue

∎

Theorem
2
is of practical importance because solving the Regge problem on a finite interval or computing the resonance set

is typically much easier than directly counting the eigenvalues of a Schrödinger operator on the real line. Another useful observation is that by replacing

The following theorem describes the two-term asymptotic expansion of the low-lying eigenvalues, which are constructed and justified in Section

Theorem 3
.

Assume that the Schrödinger operator
T_{1}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+V
has
n
eigenvalues
-\omega_{1}^{2}<-\omega_{2}^{2}<\dots<-\omega_{n}^{2}<0
with eigenfunctions

. Then the operator family
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
has

with asymptotics

\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{k}=-\varepsilon^{-2}\left(\omega_{k}+\varepsilon\,\dfrac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}U|v_{k}|^{2}\,dx}{2\omega_{k}\|v_{k}\|^{2}}\right)^{2}+O(1),\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to+0.

(4.5)

The corresponding eigenfunctions

converge to zero in the weak topology.

We mention that one of the reasons why low-lying eigenvalues do not obstruct the norm resolvent convergence of

is that the corresponding eigenfunctions converge weakly to zero in

.

5.
Negative eigenvalues generated by
\delta
-like potentials

As shown in the previous section, the emergence of low-lying eigenvalues is caused by a
\delta^{\prime}
-like perturbation, and the number of these eigenvalues is determined by the profile
V
of the approximating
\delta^{\prime}
-like potential. However, negative eigenvalues may also arise from
\delta
-like perturbations, whether or not a
\delta^{\prime}
-like component is present in the operators
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
. In such cases, at most one negative eigenvalue may appear, and it always has a finite limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

The Schrödinger operator

S_{\alpha}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+W(x)+\alpha\delta(x)

with a
\delta
-potential of intensity
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
acts by
S_{\alpha}y=-y^{\prime\prime}+Wy
on its natural domain

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm dom}S_{\alpha}=\big\{y\in W^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus{0})\colon y(+0)=y(-0),\;\;y^{\prime}(+0)-y^{\prime}(-0)=\alpha y(0)\big\}.

So defined
S_{\alpha}
is self-adjoint and has an absolutely continuous spectrum filling the positive half-line
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, while its negative spectrum consists of at most one eigenvalue. We recall that the unperturbed operator
S_{0}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+W(x)
is non-negative.

Lemma 1
.

Assume
W\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is a nonnegative function of compact support. Then there exists
\alpha_{0}\in(-\infty,0)
such that, for all
\alpha<\alpha_{0}
, the operator
S_{\alpha}
has exactly one negative eigenvalue.

Proof.

If
W=0
, then the operator
S_{\alpha}
is non-negative for
\alpha\geq 0
, while for
\alpha<0
, it has a unique eigenvalue
\lambda=-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}
with the normalized eigenfunction

\psi_{\alpha}(x)=\sqrt{\tfrac{|\alpha|}{2}}\,e^{\frac{\alpha|x|}{2}},

see
[
1
, Th.3.1.4]
. For a generic
W
, we take an
\alpha<0
and find that

(S_{\alpha}\psi_{\alpha},\psi_{\alpha})=(-\psi^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha},\psi_{\alpha})+(W\psi_{\alpha},\psi_{\alpha})=-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}+\frac{|\alpha|}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}W(x)e^{\alpha|x|}\,dx.

Since
\int_{\mathbb{R}}W(x)e^{\alpha|x|}\,dx\to 0
as
\alpha\to-\infty
by the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, we conclude that the value

(S_{\alpha}\psi_{\alpha},\psi_{\alpha})=\frac{|\alpha|}{4}\Bigl(\alpha+2\int_{\mathbb{R}}W(x)e^{\alpha|x|}\,dx\Bigr)

becomes negative for negative
\alpha
of large enough absolute value. As a result, for such
\alpha
, the operator
S_{\alpha}
has a negative eigenvalue. This eigenvalue is unique, because
S_{\alpha}
is a rank-one perturbation of nonnegative operator
S_{0}
.
∎

Lemma
1
remains valid for positive potentials
W
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}W(x)e^{\alpha|x|}\,dx<\infty,\quad\text{for all }\alpha<0;

for example, for potentials with polynomial growth at infinity. Also, the above arguments suggest an explicit way to construct
\alpha_{0}
. The function

f(\alpha)=\alpha+2\int_{\mathbb{R}}W(x)e^{\alpha|x|}\,dx

(5.1)

is monotonically increasing in
\alpha\in(-\infty,0]
,
f(0)>0
and
f
becomes negative as
\alpha\to-\infty
.
Thus
f
has a unique non-positive zero
\alpha_{0}
. Since
(S_{\alpha}\psi_{\alpha},\psi_{\alpha})=\frac{|\alpha|}{4}f(\alpha)
, we conclude that the operator
S_{\alpha}
has a unique negative eigenvalue for all
\alpha<\alpha_{0}
.

Example 1
.

Consider the family of operators

S_{\alpha}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+b^{2}(1+\sin x)+\alpha\delta(x)

Since
\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+\sin x)e^{\alpha|x|}\,dx=-\frac{2}{\alpha}
for
\alpha<0
, the zero of

in (
5.1
) satisfies
\alpha^{2}=4b^{2}
. Hence
S_{\alpha}
has a unique negative eigenvalue for all
\alpha<-2|b|
.

Example 2
.

Let
S_{\alpha}
be the harmonic oscillator perturbed by the
\delta
potential:

S_{\alpha}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+kx^{2}+\alpha\delta(x),\quad k>0.

In this case, the zero of
f
is a negative root of
\alpha^{4}=8k
, since

\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}e^{\alpha|x|}\,dx=-\frac{4}{\alpha^{3}},\quad\alpha<0.

Therefore, the operator
S_{\alpha}
has a unique negative eigenvalue for all
\alpha<-2^{3/4}k^{1/4}
.

When
V=0
, the operators

\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+W(x)+\varepsilon^{-1}U(\varepsilon^{-1}x),

(5.2)

are uniformly bounded from below and converge in the norm resolvent sense to
S_{\alpha}
with

\alpha=\int_{\mathbb{R}}U\,dx
. This convergence, in particular, implies the convergence of negative eigenvalues; our next objective is to obtain a more precise asymptotic formula (proved in Section
6

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that
W
and
U
are
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
-functions of compact support, and that
W
is nonnegative. If
\int_{\mathbb{R}}U\,dx<\alpha_{0}
, where the threshold value
\alpha_{0}
is the root of (

\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
of (
5.2
)
has a unique negative eigenvalue
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
satisfying the asymptotics

\lambda_{\varepsilon}=\lambda+\varepsilon\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\psi(0)^{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}U(t)|t-\tau|U(\tau)\,d\tau\,dt\\
+\psi(0)\big(\psi^{\prime}(-0)+\psi^{\prime}(+0)\big)\int_{\mathbb{R}}tU(t)\,dt\bigg)+O(\varepsilon^{2}),\quad\varepsilon\to 0.

(5.3)

Here,
\psi
is a real-valued,
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
-normalized eigenfunction of
S_{\alpha}
, with
\alpha=\int_{\mathbb{R}}U\,dx
, corresponds to the unique negative eigenvalue

.

Moreover, the normalized eigenfunctions
\psi_{\varepsilon}
of

can be chosen in such a way that

in

.

If the potential

has asymptotics

\lambda_{\varepsilon}=\lambda+\frac{1}{2}\,\varepsilon\psi(0)^{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}U(t)|t-\tau|U(\tau)\,d\tau\,dt+O(\varepsilon^{2}),

since the eigenfunction

If

and

\lambda_{\varepsilon}=-\frac{1}{4}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}U(t)\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\>\varepsilon\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}U(t)|t-\tau|U(\tau)\,d\tau\,dt\right)^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{2})

and coinsides with the Abarbanel-Callan-Goldberger formula up to the factor

. The formula arises when studying the weakly coupled Hamiltonians

Now suppose that the potential

is nonzero. If

of the

may have—in addition to low-lying eigenvalues—an extra eigenvalue that has a finite limit as

given by (

), with constants

and

and

Lemma 2
.

Let
W
be a non-negative function in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
of compact support.
If
\eta\theta<0
and the condition

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\big(W(-x)+\theta^{2}W(x)\big)\,dx<\frac{|\eta\theta|}{2}

(5.4)

holds, then the operator
\mathcal{H}
defined by (
3.2
) has a unique negative eigenvalue.

Proof.

Assume first that
W=0
. Integration by parts, on account of the interface conditions, yields

(\mathcal{H}y,y)=\|y^{\prime}\|^{2}+\theta\eta|y(-0)|^{2},

thus for
\theta\eta\geq 0
the operator
\mathcal{H}
is non-negative. In contrast, if
\theta\eta<0
, then
\mathcal{H}
has a unique eigenvalue

\lambda=-\frac{\eta^{2}\theta^{2}}{(\theta^{2}+1)^{2}}

(5.5)

with the normalized eigenfunction

\Psi(x)=\tfrac{\sqrt{2|\eta\theta|}}{\theta^{2}+1}\cdot\begin{cases}\phantom{A}e^{-\frac{\eta\theta}{\theta^{2}+1}x}&\text{for }x<0,\\
\theta e^{\frac{\eta\theta}{\theta^{2}+1}x}&\text{for }x>0,\end{cases}

as can be verified by straightforward calculations.

Now consider the case of and arbitrary
W\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, and let the constants
\theta
and
\eta
from (
2.8
) satisfy
\theta\eta<0
. Using the function
\Psi
defined above, we find that

(\mathcal{H}\Psi,\Psi)=(W\Psi,\Psi)-\frac{\eta^{2}\theta^{2}}{(\theta^{2}+1)^{2}}.

Therefore,
\mathcal{H}
has a negative eigenvalue if

(W\Psi,\Psi)<\frac{\eta^{2}\theta^{2}}{(\theta^{2}+1)^{2}}.

This inequality is equivalent to

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\big(W(-x)+\theta^{2}W(x)\big)\,e^{-\frac{2|\eta\theta|}{\theta^{2}+1}x}\,dx<\frac{|\eta\theta|}{2},

which is guaranteed under condition (
5.4
).
∎

Theorem 5
.

Assume that
V
is resonant with a half-bound state

, and that the potentials

and

satisfy the conditions

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\big(v_{-}^{2}W(-x)+v_{+}^{2}W(x)\big)\,dx<-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Uv^{2}\,dx.

Then, for

has asymptotics

(5.7)

Proof.

Inequality (

6.
Asymptotic expansions of eigenvalues

In this section, we derive asymptotic formulas (
4.5
) and (
5.3
) by constructing and justifying formal asymptotic expansions of the eigenvalues. For the sake of definiteness, we assume that the supports of
U
and
V
are contained in
(-1,1)
.

6.1.
Formal asymptotics

We start with asymptotics (
5.3
). The equation

-\frac{d^{2}y_{\varepsilon}}{dx^{2}}+\left(W(x)+\varepsilon^{-1}\,U(\varepsilon^{-1}x)\right)y_{\varepsilon}=\lambda_{\varepsilon}y_{\varepsilon}

on
\mathbb{R}\setminus(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
reads

-\frac{d^{2}y_{\varepsilon}}{dx^{2}}+W(x)y_{\varepsilon}=\lambda_{\varepsilon}y_{\varepsilon},

(6.1)

while after rescaling
(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
to
(-1,1)
and introducing
w_{\varepsilon}(t)=y_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon t)
, one gets

-\frac{d^{2}w_{\varepsilon}}{dt^{2}}+\varepsilon^{2}W(\varepsilon t)w_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon U(t)w_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{2}\lambda_{\varepsilon}w_{\varepsilon}

(6.2)

on
(-1,1)
. Also, the components
y_{\varepsilon}
and
w_{\varepsilon}
must satisfy the matching conditions

w_{\varepsilon}(\pm 1)=y_{\varepsilon}(\pm\varepsilon),\qquad w_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\pm 1)=\varepsilon y_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\pm\varepsilon).

We look for approximations of eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the form

\displaystyle\lambda_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\sim\lambda_{0}+\varepsilon\lambda_{1},

(6.3)

\displaystyle y_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle\sim Y_{\varepsilon}(x)=\begin{cases}y_{0}(x)+\varepsilon y_{1}(x),&\text{if }|x|>\varepsilon,\\
w_{0}(\varepsilon^{-1}x)+\varepsilon w_{1}(\varepsilon^{-1}x)+\varepsilon^{2}w_{2}(\varepsilon^{-1}x),&\text{if }|x|<\varepsilon,\end{cases}

(6.4)

where
y_{0}\neq 0
. Substituting the approximations into (
6.1
) and (
6.2
), we find that
y_{0}
and
y_{1}
satisfy the equations

-\frac{d^{2}y_{0}}{dx^{2}}+W(x)y_{0}=\lambda_{0}y_{0},\qquad-\frac{d^{2}y_{1}}{dx^{2}}+W(x)y_{1}=\lambda_{0}y_{1}+\lambda_{1}y_{0}

on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, while the fast-variable components
w_{0}
,
w_{1}
, and
w_{2}
are solutions to the boundary value problems

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}w_{0}}{dt^{2}}=0,\qquad\frac{dw_{0}}{dt}(-1)=0,\quad\frac{dw_{0}}{dt}(1)=0;

(6.5)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}w_{1}}{dt^{2}}=U(t)w_{0},\qquad\frac{dw_{1}}{dt}(-1)=y_{0}^{\prime}(-0),\quad\frac{dw_{1}}{dt}(1)=y_{0}^{\prime}(+0);

(6.6)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{d^{2}w_{2}}{dt^{2}}&=U(t)w_{1}+(W(0)-\lambda_{0})w_{0},\\
&\frac{dw_{2}}{dt}(-1)=y_{1}^{\prime}(-0)-y_{0}^{\prime\prime}(-0),\quad\frac{dw_{2}}{dt}(1)=y_{1}^{\prime}(+0)+y_{0}^{\prime\prime}(+0).\end{aligned}

(6.7)

Furthermore, the equalities

\displaystyle w_{0}(-1)=y_{0}(-0),\quad w_{0}(1)=y_{0}(+0),

(6.8)

\displaystyle w_{1}(-1)=y_{1}(-0)-y_{0}^{\prime}(-0),\quad w_{1}(1)=y_{1}(+0)+y_{0}^{\prime}(+0)

(6.9)

hold. In view of (

) and (

),

is a constant function and therefore

.
Set

. Problem (

\frac{dw_{1}}{dt}(1)-\frac{dw_{1}}{dt}(-1)=y_{0}(0)\int_{-1}^{1}U(\tau)\,d\tau,

which yields the second interface condition for

y_{0}^{\prime}(+0)-y_{0}^{\prime}(-0)=\alpha y_{0}(0),\qquad\alpha=\int_{\mathbb{R}}U(\tau)\,d\tau.

(6.10)

Therefore, the leading terms

and

of (

), (

\begin{gathered}-\frac{d^{2}y_{0}}{dx^{2}}+W(x)y_{0}=\lambda_{0}y_{0}\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},\\

Since

must be a non-trivial solution, we conclude that

is an eigenvalue of

and

Moreover, problem (

Integrating twice the equation for

which on account of

Set
\alpha_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tau U(\tau)\,d\tau
. Then
y_{1}(+0)-y_{1}(-0)=-\alpha_{1}\psi(0)
, by (
6.10
).

To get the second interface relation for
y_{1}
, we integrate the equation for
w_{2}
and find that

w_{2}^{\prime}(1)-w_{2}^{\prime}(-1)=\int_{-1}^{1}U(t)w_{1}(t)\,dt+2(W(0)-\lambda_{0})\psi(0).

We assume that
W
is continuous in a vicinity of
x=0
and this implies that

\psi^{\prime\prime}(+0)=\psi^{\prime\prime}(-0)=(W(0)-\lambda_{0})\psi(0)

Combining this with the boundary conditions in (
6.7
) and (
6.11
), we obtain

y_{1}^{\prime}(+0)-y_{1}^{\prime}(-0)=\alpha y_{1}(-0)+\alpha_{1}\psi^{\prime}(-0)+\gamma\psi(0),

where

\gamma=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{-\infty}^{t}U(t)(t-\tau)U(\tau)\,d\tau\,dt.

So, we get the boundary value problem for
y_{1}
:

\displaystyle-\frac{d^{2}y_{1}}{dx^{2}}+W(x)y_{1}=\lambda_{0}y_{1}+\lambda_{1}\psi\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},

(6.12)

\displaystyle y_{1}(+0)-y_{1}(-0)=-\alpha_{1}\psi(0),

(6.13)

\displaystyle y_{1}^{\prime}(+0)-y_{1}^{\prime}(-0)-\alpha y_{1}(-0)=\alpha_{1}\psi^{\prime}(-0)+\gamma\psi(0).

(6.14)

Observe that the solution
y_{1}
, if exists, is determined up to adding a multiple of
\psi
; therefore, by the Fredholm alternative, the above non-homogeneous problem is solvable only when some extra conditions are met. To derive them, we multiply equation (
6.12
) by the eigenfunction
\psi
and then integrate by parts twice to get

\psi(0)\big(y_{1}^{\prime}(+0)-y_{1}^{\prime}(-0)-\alpha y_{1}(-0)\big)-\psi^{\prime}(+0)\big(y_{1}(+0)-y_{1}(-0)\big)=\lambda_{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi^{2}(x)\,dx.

Relations (
6.13
) and (
6.14
) result in the expression

\lambda_{1}=\gamma\psi^{2}(0)+\alpha_{1}\psi(0)(\psi^{\prime}(-0)+\psi^{\prime}(+0))

for the second term in asymptotics (
5.3
).

We seek an approximation of the low-lying eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenfunctions of the form

\displaystyle\sqrt{-\lambda_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\sim\omega_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-1}(\omega+\varepsilon\varkappa),

(6.15)

\displaystyle y_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle\sim Y_{\varepsilon}(x)=\begin{cases}e^{\frac{(\omega+\varepsilon\varkappa)x}{\varepsilon}},&\text{if }x<-\varepsilon,\\
u(\varepsilon^{-1}x)+\varepsilon w(\varepsilon^{-1}x),&\text{if }|x|<\varepsilon,\\
(a+b\varepsilon)e^{-\frac{(\omega+\varepsilon\varkappa)x}{\varepsilon}},&\text{if }x>\varepsilon,\end{cases}

(6.16)

where
\omega>0
. On the region
\mathbb{R}\setminus(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
, the function
Y_{\varepsilon}
satisfies equation (
6.1
) up to terms with a small norm in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. For instance, only the term
W(x)e^{\frac{(\omega+\varepsilon\varkappa)x}{\varepsilon}}
remains if
x<-\varepsilon
, and its norm in
L^{2}(-\infty,0)
is of order
O(\varepsilon^{1/2})
. By substituting
Y_{\varepsilon}
into (
6.2
) and matching the terms at
x=\pm\varepsilon
, we obtain

\begin{gathered}-\frac{d^{2}u}{dt^{2}}+(V(t)+\omega^{2})u=0,\quad-\frac{d^{2}w}{dt^{2}}+(V(t)+\omega^{2})w=-(2\omega\varkappa+U(t))u,\\
u(-1)=e^{-\omega},\quad\frac{du}{dt}(-1)=\omega e^{-\omega},\quad u(1)=ae^{-\omega},\quad\frac{du}{dt}(1)=-a\omega e^{-\omega},\\
\begin{aligned} w(-1)=&-\varkappa e^{-\omega},\quad\frac{dw}{dt}(-1)=\varkappa(1-\omega)e^{-\omega},\\
&w(1)=(b-a\varkappa)e^{-\omega},\quad\frac{dw}{dt}(1)=(a\varkappa(\omega-1)-b\omega)e^{-\omega}.\end{aligned}\end{gathered}

(6.17)

The relations for
u
can be written as

\displaystyle-\frac{d^{2}u}{dt^{2}}+(V(t)+\omega^{2})u=0,\qquad t\in(-1,1),

(6.18)

\displaystyle\frac{du}{dt}(-1)-\omega u(-1)=0,\quad\frac{du}{dt}(1)+\omega u(1)=0,

(6.19)

which is the Regge problem (
4.3
). We assume that
\omega
is an eigenvalue of the problem with real-valued eigenfunction
u
. Recall that
-\omega^{2}
is an eigenvalue of the operator
T_{1}
of (
4.1
) with the eigenfunction
\psi
given by (
4.4
).
We also have
a=u(1)/u(-1)
.

From (
6.17
) we similarly obtain the problem for
w
:

\displaystyle-\frac{d^{2}w}{dt^{2}}+(V(t)+\omega^{2})w=-(2\omega\varkappa+U(t))u,\qquad t\in(-1,1),

(6.20)

\displaystyle\frac{dw}{dt}(-1)-\omega w(-1)=\varkappa u(-1),\quad\frac{dw}{dt}(1)+\omega w(1)=-\varkappa u(1).

(6.21)

The problem is generally unsolvable because
\omega
is an eigenvalue of the homogeneous problem (

), (

Solvability condition of (

\varkappa=-\frac{\int_{-1}^{1}Uu^{2}\,dt}{u^{2}(-1)+u^{2}(1)+2\omega\|u\|^{2}}.

When

With

Observe that the right hand side of the latter expression is independent of the chosen partial solution

6.2.
Justification of asymptotics

We now justify the asymptotic representations for
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
and
y_{\varepsilon}
by constructing a so-called
quasimode
for the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
.

Let
A
be a self-adjoint operator in a Hilbert space
L
. A pair
(\mu,\phi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathop{\rm dom}A
is called a
quasimode
of
A
with accuracy
\epsilon
if
\|\phi\|_{L}=1
and
\|(A-\mu I)\phi\|_{L}\leq\epsilon
.

Lemma 3

(
[
46
, p.139]
)
.

Assume
(\mu,\phi)
is a quasimode of
A
with accuracy
\epsilon>0
and that the spectrum of
A
in the interval

[\mu-\epsilon,\mu+\epsilon]
is discrete. Then there exists an eigenvalue
\lambda
of
A
such that
|\lambda-\mu|\leq\epsilon
.

Moreover, if the interval
[\mu-\Delta,\mu+\Delta]
contains precisely one simple eigenvalue
\lambda
with normalized eigenvector
u
, then

\|\phi-e^{ia}u\|\leq 2\epsilon\Delta^{-1}

(6.22)

for some real number
a
.

A quasimode of
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
can be constructed based on the approximation
Y_{\varepsilon}
. Note, however, that the function
Y_{\varepsilon}
defined by (
6.4
) is not smooth enough to belong to the domain
\mathop{\rm dom}\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
, as it has jump discontinuities at the points
x=\pm\varepsilon
. Nevertheless, all these jumps are small due to the construction; namely,

\big|[Y_{\varepsilon}]_{-\varepsilon}\big|+\big|[Y_{\varepsilon}]_{\varepsilon}\big|+\big|[Y_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}]_{-\varepsilon}\big|+\big|[Y_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}]_{\varepsilon}\big|\leq c\varepsilon^{2},

(6.23)

where
[\,\cdot\,]_{x}
denotes the jump of a function at the point
x
.

Suppose the functions
\zeta
and
\eta
are smooth outside the origin, have compact supports contained in
[0,\infty)
, and
\zeta(+0)=1
,
\zeta^{\prime}(+0)=0
,
\eta(+0)=0
,
\eta^{\prime}(+0)=1
. We introduce the function

r_{\varepsilon}(x)=[Y_{\varepsilon}]_{-\varepsilon}\,\zeta(-x-\varepsilon)-[Y_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}]_{-\varepsilon}\,\eta(-x-\varepsilon)-[Y_{\varepsilon}]_{\varepsilon}\,\zeta(x-\varepsilon)-[Y_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}]_{\varepsilon}\,\eta(x-\varepsilon),

which has the jumps at
\pm\varepsilon
that are negative of those of
Y_{\varepsilon}
. Therefore, the function
\hat{y}_{\varepsilon}=Y_{\varepsilon}+r_{\varepsilon}

is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
along with its derivative and consequently belongs to
W_{2}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Moreover,
\|\hat{y}_{\varepsilon}\|=1+O(\varepsilon)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, because the main term
y_{0}=\psi
is normalized in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. The corrector function
r_{\varepsilon}
is small, because
r_{\varepsilon}
is identically zero on
(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
, and (
6.23
) makes it obvious that

\max_{|x|\geq\varepsilon}\big|r_{\varepsilon}^{(k)}(x)\big|\leq c\varepsilon^{2},\qquad k=0,1,2.

A straightforward computation shows that a pair
\big(\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{1}\varepsilon,\hat{y}_{\varepsilon}\big)
is a quasimode of
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
with accuracy of order
O(\varepsilon^{2})
, i.e.,
\|\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}\hat{y}_{\varepsilon}-(\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{1}\varepsilon)\hat{y}_{\varepsilon}\|\leq c\varepsilon^{2}\|\hat{y}_{\varepsilon}\|
. Hence,

|\lambda_{\varepsilon}-(\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{1}\varepsilon)|\leq C\varepsilon^{2},

where
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
. Since
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
is a simple eigenvalue, the corresponding eigenfunction
y_{\varepsilon}
can be chosen so that
y_{\varepsilon}\to y_{0}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, by (
6.22
).
Theorem
4
is proved.

The proof of Theorem
3
is similar, with one key difference. The approximation given by (
6.15
) and (
6.16
) is not sufficient to construct a quasimode of
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
with sufficiently small accuracy. It is therefore necessary to refine the approximation as follows:

\displaystyle\sqrt{-\lambda_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\sim\omega_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-1}(\omega+\varepsilon\varkappa_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}\varkappa_{2}+\varepsilon^{3}\varkappa_{3}),

\displaystyle y_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle\sim Y_{\varepsilon}(x)=\begin{cases}e^{\omega_{\varepsilon}x},&\text{if }x<-\varepsilon,\\
u\left(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)+\varepsilon u_{1}\left(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)+\varepsilon^{2}u_{2}\left(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)+\varepsilon^{3}u_{3}\left(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\right),&\text{if }|x|<\varepsilon,\\
(a+a_{1}\varepsilon+a_{2}\varepsilon^{2}+a_{3}\varepsilon^{3})e^{-\omega_{\varepsilon}x},&\text{if }x>\varepsilon,\end{cases}

after which the construction proceeds as in the case of Theorem
4
.
