Letting Tutor Personas “Speak Up” for LLMs: Learning Steering Vectors from Dialogue via Preference Optimization
Jaewook Lee, Alexander Scarlatos, Simon Woodhead, Andrew Lan

Abstract
With the emergence of large language models (LLMs) as a powerful class of generative artificial intelligence (AI), their use in tutoring has become increasingly prominent. Prior works on LLM-based tutoring typically learn a single tutor policy and do not capture the diversity of tutoring styles. In real-world tutor–student interactions, pedagogical intent is realized through adaptive instructional strategies, with tutors varying the level of scaffolding, instructional directiveness, feedback, and affective support in response to learners’ needs. These differences can all impact dialogue dynamics and student engagement. In this paper, we explore how tutor personas embedded in human tutor-student dialogues can be used to guide LLM behavior without relying on explicitly prompted instructions. We modify Bidirectional Preference Optimization (BiPO) to learn a steering vector, an activation-space direction that steers model responses towards certain tutor personas. We find that this steering vector captures tutor-specific variation across dialogue contexts, improving semantic alignment with ground-truth tutor utterances and increasing preference-based evaluations, while largely preserving lexical similarity. Analysis of the learned directional coefficients further reveals interpretable structure across tutors, corresponding to consistent differences in tutoring behavior. These results demonstrate that activation steering offers an effective and interpretable way for controlling tutor-specific variation in LLMs using signals derived directly from human dialogue data. Keywords Large Language Models ⋅ \cdot Math Education ⋅ \cdot Activation Steering ⋅ \cdot Tutor-Student Dialogues.

Introduction

With the emergence of large language models (LLMs) as a powerful class of generative artificial intelligence (AI), their use in educational tutoring has become increasingly prominent, spanning both general-purpose systems

Many approaches simply prompt LLMs with dialogue policies and guardrails to elicit best-practice pedagogy, including reducing solution leakage, encouraging scaffolding, and enforcing Socratic-style questioning

These approaches optimize proxies of pedagogical quality, such as improving feedback correctness and alignment with tutoring goals
[
26
]
, directly optimizing for pedagogical alignment signals derived from preference data over tutoring behaviors
[

]
, or leveraging pedagogical rubrics and LLM-based simulated students in-the-loop to encourage greater learning outcomes

.

1.1
Contributions

In this paper, we explore how inherent tutor personas embedded in human tutor-student dialogues can be used to guide LLM behavior, rather than relying on explicitly prompted instructions.
We use preference optimization to learn a steering vector and a scalar directional coefficient that capture and control the persona direction in the model’s representation space. Using these learned parameters, we then steer the LLM in a tutor-specific manner.
We find that our approach enables consistent recovery of tutor-specific steering across dialogue contexts, shifting the model away from population-mean behavior and toward the behavior exhibited by individual tutors. We further show that the strength of this effect can be controlled at inference time through a global scaling factor, allowing for smooth adjustment of the intervention. Finally, analysis of the learned directional coefficients reveals an interpretable structure over tutors that corresponds to variation in tutoring behavior, supporting activation steering as an effective way for capturing and controlling tutor-specific variation.

2
Related Work

2.1
Tutoring Strategies

Effective tutoring relies on a set of practical strategies rather than a single teaching approach, with tutors adjusting what they do in response to the learner’s needs. One core strategy is scaffolding, in which tutors provide support that helps learners solve problems they could not complete on their own, and then gradually reduce that support as learners become more capable
[
33
]
. Tutors also vary how directly they teach, moving between clear explanation or demonstration and guided practice that helps learners take on more responsibility over time
[
24
]
. In tutoring conversations, questioning strategies are used to prompt learners to explain their thinking while keeping the interaction focused on the learning goal
[
7
]
. During early stages of learning, tutors often use worked examples to show how problems can be solved and to make new skills easier to grasp
[
30
]
. As learners practice, tutors provide formative, error-focused feedback to identify misunderstandings and support improvement
[
28
]
. Finally, effective tutoring includes affective strategies such as encouragement, building rapport, and managing frustration in order to maintain engagement and persistence
[
5
]
.

2.2
Embedding Personas in LLMs

Research on embedding personas into LLMs generally follows two approaches: text-based conditioning and data-driven learning. In the text-based approach, personas are explicitly specified using structured descriptors such as the Five-Factor Model (Big Five)
[
16
]
, with prior work demonstrating that prompting an LLM with predefined Big Five trait profiles can induce consistent personality-aligned behaviors, which are validated by administering standardized personality questionnaires to the model
[
27
]
. In the context of intelligent tutoring systems, related work adopts explicit persona specifications to construct personality-aware student simulations that combine cognitive attributes and personality traits, enabling systematic analysis of how different learner personas elicit and evaluate adaptive tutoring strategies
[
14
]
.

In contrast, data-driven learning induces personas from examples rather than explicit textual descriptions, and is commonly implemented either through SFT or by manipulating representation-level features
[
3
,
15
]
. In this paper, we focus on activation steering using steering vectors, which estimate a direction from contrastive behaviors and inject that direction into a model’s hidden states to bias generation toward a target persona. A steering vector can be computed from differences in internal activations produced by paired inputs or outputs that reflect desired versus undesired traits, and then applied at selected layers and token positions during the forward pass to produce consistent behavioral shifts
[
13
,
23
,
31
,
32
]
. Our work builds on and modifies BiPO, which itself builds on Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) by combining preference-based learning to train a steering vector with activation steering to achieve finer-grained control over model behavior
[
1
,
21
]
. Like DPO, BiPO relies on relative preferences between model outputs rather than absolute likelihood targets, yielding a contrastive learning signal that enables bidirectional steering between preferred and non-preferred behaviors.

3
Methodology

In this section, we detail our approach to learning a steering vector from tutor-student dialogues. We first introduce the notation used throughout the paper:

\mathcal{D}\triangleq\bigcup_{i=1}^{I}\{(q_{j},d^{i}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{J_{i}},\quad d^{i}_{j}\triangleq(s_{j,0},\,t^{i}_{j,1},\,s_{j,1},\,\ldots,\,t^{i}_{j,K_{j}},\,s_{j,K_{j}}).

\mathcal{D}
denotes a human tutor-student dialogue dataset, where
I
is the total number of tutors and
i\in\{1,\ldots,I\}
indexes a specific tutor. For each tutor
i
,
J_{i}
denotes the number of dialogues conducted by tutor
i
, and
j\in\{1,\ldots,J_{i}\}
indexes an individual dialogue. Each dialogue
d^{i}_{j}
is associated with a math question
q_{j}
.
Each dialogue
d^{i}_{j}
consists of an alternating sequence of student turns
s_{j,k}
and tutor turns
t^{i}_{j,k}
.
k\in\{1,\ldots,K_{j}\}
indexes (student, tutor) turn pairs in the dialogue, where
K_{j}
is the total number of such turn pairs. The initial student turn
s_{j,0}
is included when the student initiates the dialogue. We refer to the textual content of each turn as an
utterance
.

A steering vector encodes a direction in an LLM’s activation space, and when added to the activations induces changes in the LLM’s behavior. Learning a tutor-specific vector requires making explicit what the model is being steered away from and toward. In our setting, we define this direction relative to an “average” tutor: for a given dialogue context, the steering vector should shift the model from a population-mean tutor utterance toward the utterance produced by a particular tutor. Accordingly, we construct pairs consisting of a population-mean utterance and the corresponding “ground-truth” actual tutor utterance for each dialogue turn. We then learn a steering vector from these pairs that captures tutor-specific stylistic variation.

3.1
Constructing Pairs

We use SFT to fine-tune an LLM to serve as a proxy for population-mean behavior, reducing response diversity and encouraging convergence toward a generic average-tutor behavior
[
11
]
; the resulting model is used to generate population-mean utterances.
Let
\pi_{\theta}
denote an LLM parameterized by
\theta
; we train
\pi_{\theta}
via SFT by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of tutor utterances in
\mathcal{D}
conditioned on the associated question
q_{j}
and the preceding dialogue context
(s_{j,\leq k},\,t^{i}_{j,<k})
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta)\;=\;\frac{1}{I}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\frac{1}{J_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{J_{i}}\frac{1}{K_{j}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{j}}-\log\pi_{\theta}\!\left(t^{i}_{j,k}\;\middle|\;s_{j,\leq k},\,t^{i}_{j,<k},\,q_{j}\right).

3.2
Learning a Steering Vector

We now detail what a steering vector is and how we learn it using the pairs.
We introduce a steering vector
v
, shared across tutors, which we use to modify the model’s internal weights during inference by adding it to the latent activations
A_{L}(\cdot)
at layer
L
. We assign each tutor a positive direction coefficient
\delta_{i}
that scales a shared steering direction
v
. We constrain these coefficients to to have unit mean across tutors to eliminate a scale ambiguity, since the intervention depends only on the product
\delta_{i}v
and could otherwise be left unchanged by scaling
\delta_{i}
up while scaling
v
down (or vice versa) without affecting the loss. To parameterize
\delta_{i}
while enforcing positivity, we introduce a tutor-specific learnable scalar
u_{i}
and define
\delta_{i}=\exp(u_{i})\big/\big(\frac{1}{I}\sum_{m=1}^{I}\exp(u_{m})\big)
. We jointly optimize each
u_{i}
and the shared steering direction
v
in training. We initialize
u_{i}=0
for all tutors (
\delta_{i}=1
), and initialize
v
from a standard Gaussian scaled by
0.5
to ensure a sufficiently strong initial steering magnitude. We note that while we learn the steering vector through preference optimization inspired by BiPO
[
1
]
, we differ in that, rather than using bidirectional steering, we learn a directional coefficient that scales a shared steering direction:

\min_{v,u}\mathbb{E}_{i,j,k}\!\!\left[-\!\log\!\sigma\!\!\left(\!\!\beta\log\!\!\frac{\pi_{\theta}\!\Big(t^{i}_{j,k}\,\big|\,A_{L}(\cdot)+\delta_{i}v\Big)}{\pi_{\theta}\!\Big(t^{i}_{j,k}\,\big|\,A_{L}(\cdot)\Big)}\!-\!\beta\log\!\!\frac{\pi_{\theta}\!\Big(\bar{t}_{j,k}\,\big|\,A_{L}(\cdot)+\delta_{i}v\Big)}{\pi_{\theta}\!\Big(\bar{t}_{j,k}\,\big|\,A_{L}(\cdot)\Big)}\!\!\right)\!\!\right].

For tutor
i
, dialogue
j
, turn
k
, our objective includes two relative preference terms. The first log term encourages the steered LLM to have higher likelihood in generating actual tutor utterance
t^{i}_{j,k}
compared to the unsteered SFT model. The right term does the exact opposite, encouraging the steered model to have lower likelihood in generating the population-average tutor utterance. The logistic loss encourages learning a shared steering direction
v
and tutor-specific coefficients
\delta_{i}
such that applying
\delta_{i}v
consistently favors tutor-specific outputs across tutors, dialogues, and turns. The hyperparameter
\beta
encourages similarity to the base model’s behavior without the steering vector applied. We optimize this objective by iterating for
T
steps until the loss converges.

At test time, we introduce a global scaling factor
\alpha
and apply activation steering as
A_{L}(\cdot)\leftarrow A_{L}(\cdot)+\alpha\,\delta_{i}v
. While the training objective identifies a shared steering direction
v
and relative tutor-specific strengths
\delta_{i}
with respect to the unsteered SFT model, a separate scaling factor is necessary to control the overall strength of the intervention during inference.

4
Experimental Settings

4.1
Dataset

We use the
Question-Anchored-Tutoring-Dialogues-2k
dataset
[
34
]
, containing dialogues from web-based conversations between expert tutors and real students in an online math learning platform. The conversations are initiated by tutors or students requesting assistance while working through a lesson.
We split the dataset into training, validation, and test sets at the dialogue level using an approximately 80/10/10 split for each of the 21 unique tutors. On average, each tutor has 74.19 (
\sigma=75.47
) dialogues in the training set, 8.90 (
\sigma=9.45
) dialogues in the validation set, and 10.10 (
\sigma=9.42
) dialogues in the test set. The average number of turns per dialogue is 11.75 (
\sigma=1.10
) in training, 12.06 (
\sigma=2.43
) in validation, and 12.25 (
\sigma=1.73
) in test.

4.2
Evaluation Setup and Metrics

We evaluate how closely the steered tutor utterances match the corresponding ground-truth on a per-tutor basis, along two main axes:
lexical similarity
and
semantic similarity
. For each tutor
i
, we compare steered outputs
t_{j}^{\delta_{i}}
to ground-truth utterances
t_{j}
across turns
j
. For lexical similarity, we measure surface-form overlap using
ROUGE-L

[
12
]
and
BLEU

[
19
]
. For semantic similarity, we assess semantic alignment using cosine similarity in embedding space (
CS
) and a pairwise preference-based win rate (
Win Rate
). We define

\mathrm{CS}(\delta_{i})=\mathbb{E}_{j}\!\left[\cos\!\big(\phi(t_{j}^{\delta_{i}}),\phi(t_{j})\big)\right]
,
where
\phi(\cdot)
denotes a sentence embedding function from a pretrained
SentenceBERT
encoder
[
22
]
.
For Win Rate, we use LLM-as-a-judge with
Prometheus-Eval
, given a dialogue and a ground-truth turn and chooses which of the steered or unsteered utterances more closely aligns with the tutoring style and language used
[
10
]
. We report the win rate over unsteered SFT utterances, where
0.5
indicates the two perform equally. We do not report ties because they are rare. All metrics are computed per tutor and then averaged across tutors.

4.3
Model Parameters

We use
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
as the base model for SFT, which we adapt using LoRA
[
8
]
with a learning rate of
5\times 10^{-5}
over
3
epochs, setting the LoRA rank to
r=32
and the scaling factor to
\alpha=64
.
For learning
v
and
u_{i}
, we use the validation set, applying each
u_{i}
to the dialogues corresponding to tutor
i
and selecting hyperparameters by tracking validation loss; we set
\beta=1.0
and learning rate
=0.01
, and apply activation steering at the final transformer layer (layer 32).
Text generation uses a temperature of
1.0
and top-
p
sampling with
p=0.95
, yielding a final validation loss of
0.543
at
T=17
. The learned
u_{i}
have a mean of
0.053
and a standard deviation of
0.106
. The activation steering mechanism follows the persona vector approach
[
3
]
, where the learned steering vector is added to the layer activations at all token positions. All training and evaluation are conducted on a single NVIDIA A40 GPU with 48 GB of VRAM.

Experimental Results

5.1
Quantitative Results

We quantitatively evaluate activation steering for tutoring along two dimensions: (1) how well it recovers tutor-specific behavior at different stages of a dialogue, and (2) how this behavior varies with the strength of the steering intervention. Specifically, we first compare steered tutor utterances
\hat{t}_{j}
with population-mean utterances
\bar{t}_{j}
, using ground-truth tutor utterances
t_{j}
as reference, in different stages of the dialogue (Table
1
). We then analyze the effect of varying the global scaling factor
\alpha
on both stylistic alignment and lexical similarity (Table
2
).

We conduct a stage-wise analysis because different parts of a dialogue serve different purposes. Early and late turns are largely greetings, encouragement, and closing remarks, which tend to follow common conventions and thus provide limited signal about a tutor’s instructional style. In contrast, the middle portion of the dialogue contains the core problem-solving interaction, where tutors explain concepts, provide hints, and respond to student errors. These turns are where tutor-specific strategies and language use are most evident. For this reason, we define the
mid stage
as the 10–90% interval of turns within each dialogue and focus our analysis on this region.

6
Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper, we explored how implicit signals in human tutor-student dialogues can be used to guide LLMs towards different tutoring styles. We proposed an activation steering method that learns a shared steering direction and tutor-specific coefficients from real tutor-student dialogues, relative to a population-mean reference, enabling personalized, controlled tutor-specific behavior. We quantitatively show that the learned steering direction and strengths lead to improved replication of individual tutor behavior.
We also qualitatively show that it encodes changing tutoring styles from scaffolded, rapport-building through efficient, task-focused guidance to minimal, task-completion-oriented instruction.

There are several directions for future work. First, while our learning objective isolates tutor-specific behavior through preferences, it does not explicitly encode pedagogical constraints or educational principles. Incorporating pedagogical rubrics, learning-theoretic objectives, or student outcome-oriented rewards may improve alignment with ground-truth tutoring behavior. Second, this work focuses only on steering tutor behavior. An important extension is to learn complementary steering vectors for student models, enabling simulated tutor–student interactions guided by latent student personas. Doing so may enable us to study how interaction dynamics and persona compatibility between tutors and students impact learning outcomes. Finally, the approach can be extended to learn multiple steering directions rather than a single one by extracting a low-rank subspace via dimensionality reduction, enabling finer-grained and potentially disentangled control over distinct pedagogical attributes.
