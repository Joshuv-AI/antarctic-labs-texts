Theory–practice discrepancy in cardiopulmonary resuscitation performance: a multi-centre evaluation of emergency healthcare providers in China
Authors: Hai Wang, Jun-jie Liang, Zheng-liang Zhang, Hong-hong Pei, Long-fei Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the widespread implementation of standardised cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) training programmes, the actual competency of emergency healthcare providers in China has not been established. This study evaluated the alignment between theoretical knowledge and practical CPR skills among frontline clinicians.MethodsIn total, 586 physicians and nurses of a hospital emergency department were evaluated. Theoretical knowledge was assessed via questionnaire, while practical skills were evaluated by two certified basic life support (BLS) instructors using standardised checklists and manikin-based simulation with audiovisual feedback. Key outcomes included adherence to guidelines and quantitative CPR quality metrics.ResultsParticipants were predominantly bachelor’s degree holders (480/586; 81.91%), nurses (379/586; 64.68%) and working in tertiary hospitals (436/586; 74.40%). Furthermore, 507 (86.52%) underwent CPR training within 6 months. In the theoretical assessments, over 88% correctly identified key steps, such as the compression landmark, compression rate and compression depth. However, practical evaluation by BLS instructors revealed lower proficiency in correct pulse location (383/586; 65.36%), use of a firm surface (240/586; 40.96%), recommended compression depth (250/586; 42.66%) and compression rate (350/586; 57.17%). The mean operation score awarded by BLS instructors was 24.89 ± 2.71. Audiovisual feedback revealed a mean compression accuracy of 17%, compression depth compliance of 34%, rate compliance of 55% and complete recoil of 77%. Background

CPR is a critical, life-saving intervention in emergency care [1–3]. Standardised CPR training has been widely implemented, enabling most healthcare professionals to master theoretical knowledge [4, 5]. However, evaluations of practical CPR skills among these professionals remain limited, leaving unaddressed the gap between theoretical understanding and actual clinical performance among medical staff. Although healthcare providers routinely receive education on CPR guidelines, previous training has traditionally placed greater emphasis on theoretical mastery than on practical skill development [6]. Consequently, critical components of effective CPR, such as achieving adequate compression depth, compression rate and complete chest recoil, are often inadequately executed in practice, thereby failing to meet recognised international standards [6]. This disconnect underscores the urgent need for systematic assessments of both theoretical knowledge and practical proficiency in CPR among healthcare professionals, ensuring that theoretical understanding translates into high-quality resuscitation practice. High-quality CPR, characterised by appropriate compression rate, depth, full chest recoil and minimal interruptions, is a critical determinant of patient survival and neurological outcomes following cardiac arrest [7]. In China, hospital-based resuscitation training has been extensively promoted [8]; thus, the extent of the theory–practice gap and its impact on CPR quality among frontline clinicians remains inadequately characterised. Discrepancies between knowledge must be assessed, and skill execution and specific causes of suboptimal CPR quality must be identified to refine targeted educational strategies and optimise clinical outcomes [9]. This study addressed these gaps by evaluating both theoretical knowledge and practical CPR performance among emergency physicians and nurses from a representative cohort of Chinese general hospitals. The findings would provide evidence for enhancing resuscitation training curricula and developing evidence-based interventions to increase the quality of CPR delivery in the clinical setting.

Methods

Study design

This multi-centre observational cross-sectional study was conducted based on data collected from quality control assessments of CPR conducted in 34 general hospitals in Shaanxi Province between December 2023 and March 2024.

Objective

To evaluate the theoretical knowledge and practical competence in CPR of emergency healthcare providers to identify training needs.

Inclusion criteria

Certified physicians and registered nurses with valid professional qualifications who were full-time employees under hospital labour contracts.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Physicians and nurses in standardised emergency department training, advanced studies or internships; (2) Individuals with a confirmed diagnosis of psychiatric disorders; (3) Refusal to participate; and (4) Withdrawal of participation during the study.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. The need for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective study design. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Baseline data collection

The variables included age, sex, height, weight, highest education, professional title, hospital level, profession, years of work experience, time since last CPR training, average number of CPR cases per year and time since last CPR practice.

Assessment of theoretical knowledge

Theoretical knowledge of CPR was evaluated using an online structured questionnaire based on the 2023 AHA guidelines [10], covering the following domains: Time to assess pulse and respiration, identification of compression landmark, use of compression feedback devices, application of physiological monitoring, performance of cerebral resuscitation, awareness of recommended compression rate and depth, compression-to-ventilation ratio, return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) assessment intervals, defibrillation mode for ventricular fibrillation and selection of biphasic defibrillator energy.

Assessment of practical skills

CPR skills were evaluated using a manikin equipped with audio-visual feedback and capable of real-time recording of compression accuracy, depth compliance rate, rate compliance rate and complete recoil rate, all of which were subsequently analysed.

Assessment scenario

The scenario simulated an unconscious adult man experiencing a sudden collapse in an out-of-hospital setting requiring CPR.

Examination process

The order of assessment was determined by random draw. Participants entered the closed testing area in sequence and performed CPR according to standard emergency procedures, consisting of initial patient assessment followed by continuous chest compressions for 2 min. All candidates waited outside the examination room prior to the assessment to ensure fairness and rigour.

Comprehensive practical assessment

Practical performance was graded using a CPR evaluation checklist, including judgement of cardiac arrest, chest compressions and respiratory support, with a total possible score of 30. Participants were scored independently based on the checklist by two research team members, both certified BLS instructors, and the mean score was calculated. The ckecklist was a well-established, standardized tool widely used in clinical training and assessment for Basic Life Support (BLS) and Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) certification courses. It was derived from the international resuscitation guidelines published by the American Heart Association (AHA) [11].(Supplementary Table 1).

Manikin-based CPR quality metrics

The following performance metrics were evaluated: compression rate (times/min), compliance with recommended compression rate (%), compression depth (cm), compliance with recommended compression depth (%), complete recoil rate (%) and overall compression accuracy rate (%).

Definition of key CPR quality metrics

Compression position accuracy: Proportion of compressions correctly delivered to the lower half of the sternum, measured via pressure sensors or infrared detection and calculated using the following formula: (number of correct compressions/total compressions) × 100%.

Compression position accuracy: Proportion of compressions correctly delivered to the lower half of the sternum, measured via pressure sensors or infrared detection and calculated using the following formula: (number of correct compressions/total compressions) × 100%.

Compression rate accuracy: Proportion of time spent within the recommended rate of 100–120 compressions per minute, measured using interval timing sensors and calculated as follows: (duration at correct rate/total compression duration) × 100%.

Compression rate accuracy: Proportion of time spent within the recommended rate of 100–120 compressions per minute, measured using interval timing sensors and calculated as follows: (duration at correct rate/total compression duration) × 100%.

Compression depth accuracy: Proportion of compressions within the recommended adult depth of 5–6 cm, recorded via displacement or pressure sensors and calculated as follows: (number at correct depth/total compressions) × 100%.

Compression depth accuracy: Proportion of compressions within the recommended adult depth of 5–6 cm, recorded via displacement or pressure sensors and calculated as follows: (number at correct depth/total compressions) × 100%.

Complete recoil accuracy: Proportion of compressions achieving full chest recoil, detected by sensors monitoring return to baseline and calculated using the following formula: (number with full recoil/total compressions) × 100%

Complete recoil accuracy: Proportion of compressions achieving full chest recoil, detected by sensors monitoring return to baseline and calculated using the following formula: (number with full recoil/total compressions) × 100%

Overall compression accuracy: Proportion of compressions simultaneously meeting criteria for correct position, rate, depth and recoil and calculated as follows: (number meeting all criteria/total compressions) × 100%.

Overall compression accuracy: Proportion of compressions simultaneously meeting criteria for correct position, rate, depth and recoil and calculated as follows: (number meeting all criteria/total compressions) × 100%.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 with two-sided tests. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (n) and percentage (%), while quantitative variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median and interquartile range (P25, P75), as appropriate. The comparison between theoretical knowledge and practical skills for key CPR indicators was performed using Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were calculated to quantify the association. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study population (N = 586) had a mean age of 33.20 ± 6.49 years, with 229 men (39.08%) and 357 women (60.92%). Height distribution revealed 310 participants (52.90%) were ≤ 165 cm, 213 (36.35%) were 166–175 cm and 63 (10.75%) were > 175 cm. Weight categorisation revealed 97 individuals (16.55%) weighing < 50 kg, 385 (65.70%) weighing 50–75 kg and 104 (17.75%) weighing > 75 kg. Regarding educational attainment, 80 were associate degree holders (13.65%), 480 were bachelor’s degree holders (81.91%), and 26 were master’s degree holders (4.44%). Regarding career status, 338 were junior level (57.68%), 215 were intermediate level (36.69%), and 33 were senior level (5.63%). Institutional characteristics revealed 150 participants (25.60%) were from secondary hospitals, while 436 (74.40%) worked in tertiary hospitals, 379 of whom were nursing staff (64.68%) and 207 were medical staff (35.32%). Regarding work experience, 191 participants (32.59%) had < 5 years of experience, 151 (25.77%) had 5–10 years of experience, and 244 (41.64%) had > 10 years of experience. CPR training history showed that 507 participants (86.52%) had training within ≤ 6 months. Analysis of annual CPR participation revealed that 223 (38.05%) have handled < 10 cases, while 363 (61.95%) have handled ≥ 10 cases. Furthermore, 368 participants (62.80%) had performed CPR within < 1 month, 141 (24.06%) within 1–3 months and 77 (13.14%) > 3 months (Table 1).

Questionnaire assessment of theoretical knowledge acquisition in CPR

Assessment of theoretical knowledge acquisition in CPR among participants revealed that the majority demonstrated proficiency across key variables. Most respondents (91.47%) could assess pulse and respiration within the recommended 5–10 s, while 88.23% accurately identified the mid-lower sternum as the correct chest compression landmark. The use of compression feedback devices and physiological monitoring during CPR was reported by 47.61% and 57.00% of respondents, respectively. Nearly half of the participants (49.49%) performed cerebral resuscitation practices. Encouragingly, adherence to the recommended compression rate (100–120 min⁻¹) and depth (5–6 cm) was exhibited by 96.76% and 97.95% of respondents, respectively. Furthermore, 98.98% selected the correct compression-to-ventilation ratio of 30:2, and 96.59% performed ROSC assessment every five cycles, adhering to current guidelines. In scenarios of ventricular fibrillation, 92.15% selected asynchronous defibrillation mode, while 88.40% correctly selected 200 J as the initial biphasic defibrillator energy. Collectively, these findings indicate a high level of adherence to evidence-based CPR protocols among participants, although the scope for further improvement was noted in the use of monitoring technologies and defibrillation practices.(Table 2; Fig. 1).

Assessment of cardiopulmonary resuscitation operation by two qualified BSL instructors

The assessment focused on critical components of CPR and revealed varying proficiency across different operations. Notably, adherence rates were observed in areas such as assessing the safety of the surrounding environment (91.98%) and evaluating patient consciousness (88.74%). The ability to determine pulse timing was high (92.32%); however, the ability to determine the pulse location was lower (65.36%), indicating a potential area for improvement.

Chest compression techniques presented a dichotomy in performance. The location for compressions was effectively identified (93.52%), with consistent placement on the lower half of the sternum. However, the skill in assessing for a firm surface for CPR delivery (40.96%) and correct positioning of the patient (54.10%) were notably lower. The technique of interlocking fingers for compressions achieved a high rate of compliance (92.83%), whereas ensuring the fingers remained lifted off the chest wall was moderately observed (72.87%). Furthermore, a significant proportion of respondents applied compression force with proper body mechanics (80.20%). However, only 42.66% maintained a compression depth of 5–6 cm, while 57.17% of the participants met the ideal compression rate of 100–120 compressions per minute.

Reassessment following five cycles of CPR demonstrated moderate compliance (71.67%), with the location reassessment rate at 74.06% and timing reassessment rate at 89.08%. Establishing a recovery position was performed effectively by 76.62% of participants, while the application of advanced resuscitation after ROSC was notably high at 94.20%. In terms of respiratory support, clearing oral secretions had a compliance rate of 76.79%. The correct execution of the head-tilt, chin-lift method was somewhat low (58.02%), although mouth-to-mouth ventilation was performed accurately by 88.74% of respondents. Lastly, techniques such as pinching the nostrils while delivering breaths had a commendable compliance rate of 89.25%, with 442 participants (75.43%) correctly releasing the pinch after delivering a breath.

The overall assessment yielded a mean score of 24.89 ± 2.71, reflecting a generally high competency level in CPR execution amongst the participants, with specific areas identified for further training and improvement to enhance performance. (Table 3; Fig. 2)

CPR quality metrics and scoring as evaluated with audiovisual feedback device

CPR quality metrics were analysed using an audiovisual feedback device, and areas of proficiency and those requiring improvement were identified. The mean compression accuracy rate was relatively low at 17% (SD 22%), with a median of 5% (interquartile range [IQR]: 0%–27%). The mean chest compression rate was 116.90 ± 13.90/min, with a median of 116.00 (IQR: 108.00–124.00/min). Compliance for recommended compression rates averaged 55% (SD 40%), with a median of 65% (IQR: 9%–95%). The average compression depth achieved was 5.34 ± 1.01 cm (median: 5.30 cm; IQR: 4.70–6.00 cm), while compliance with the recommended compression depth was 34% (SD 27%), with a median of 30% (IQR: 10%–56%). Notably, the rate of complete chest recoil was relatively high, averaging 77% (SD 34%) with a median of 99% (IQR: 55%–100%). These results indicate that while adequate compression rates and high rates of complete chest recoil were achieved, the precision of compression accuracy and adherence to optimal compression depth require further refinement to ensure high-quality CPR performance (Table 4 and Fig. 3).

Discussion

This multi-centre cross-sectional study revealed a profound and clinically significant disconnect between theoretical knowledge and practical CPR proficiency among Chinese emergency healthcare professionals. Despite exhibiting exceptional theoretical comprehension, with correct response rates exceeding 96% across all core indicators, their practical performance was markedly lower in most critical domains. The most pronounced deficits were observed in achieving adequate chest compression depth (42.66% practical success vs. 97.95% theoretical knowledge) and maintaining the correct compression rate (57.17% vs. 96.76%). These findings underscored a critical “knowing-doing” gap that could threaten the effectiveness of resuscitation efforts.

Our findings aligned with previous studies indicating that traditional CPR training in China and othercountries, has historically placed greater emphasis on theoretical mastery than on the development and maintenance of hands-on resuscitation skills [12, 13]. Although over 90% of participants could accurately answer key knowledge questions, significant proportions failed to achieve the recommended compression depth (42.66%), appropriate frequency (57.17%) and overall accuracy (17% by manikin feedback), indicating a knowledge–skills gap with potential clinical consequences. These deficits are particularly concerning given the established relationship between CPR quality and patient outcomes in cardiac arrest [14, 15]. The relatively high compliance for chest recoil but moderate operation scores further underscore that some skills are more readily retained than others, which may indicate relative emphasis during training or differences in psychomotor challenge.

Several factors may account for the overall suboptimal practical performance. First, although knowledge-based assessments are straightforward to administer and grade, they may inadequately reflect hands-on competence and knowledge retention under realistic conditions [16]. Second, infrequent or inadequately supervised hands-on demonstrations, lack of immediate feedback and insufficient use of high-fidelity simulation may contribute to skill decay and reinforce superficial competence [17]. Furthermore, the low utilisation of feedback devices and physiological monitoring observed in this study implies missed opportunities for reinforcing proper technique during clinical care and ongoing professional development [18].

The current results highlight the urgent need to recalibrate CPR training in China towards a more balanced, competency- and simulation-based approach, along with continuous assessment and reinforcement of psychomotor skills. Regular, structured skills refreshers employing manikin-based simulation and real-time audiovisual feedback, not only during certification but also as a component of continuing medical education, can effectively improve CPR quality in different settings [19, 20]. Furthermore, instructor-led debriefings, scenario-based assessments and integration of actual clinical data into quality improvement initiatives should be considered to attain and sustain improvements in practice [21, 22].

The identification of body weight as a significant factor of practical performance for strength-dependent skills introduced a novel and important physiological dimension to the discussion. Participants in the weight group (50–60 kg) significantly outperformed the other counterparts in achieving correct compression depth (48.95% vs. 39.65%), compression rate (60.53% vs. 29.04%), and complete recoil (72.11% vs. 58.59%). This suggested that factors beyond knowledge, such as physical strength, leverage, and fatigue resistance, were critical determinants of real-world CPR quality [23, 24]. This finding potentially explained the persistent difficulties in achieving compression depth, as it implied that standard training might not have adequately prepared individuals of all body types to generate the required force consistently. The absence of a difference in the compression-to-ventilation ratio, a non-strength-dependent skill, reinforced this interpretation.

The strengths of this study include its multi-centre design, large sample size and use of dual assessment modes (instructor evaluation and objective manikin feedback). Its limitations include its focus on a single province, which may limit generalisability to other regions or healthcare settings, and its cross-sectional design, which precludes assessment of long-term skill retention and impact on patient outcomes. Furthermore, our study only investigated the role of body weight, leaving the influence of other baseline characteristics unexamined—an essential area for future research to develop comprehensive remedial interventions.

In conclusion, Chinese emergency healthcare professionals possess strong theoretical knowledge of CPR but present substantial deficiencies in the execution of practical skills, especially in chest compression quality. Addressing these gaps will require systematic, simulation-enhanced and feedback-driven training strategies to ensure that advancements in theoretical knowledge translate into improved real-world resuscitation outcomes.
