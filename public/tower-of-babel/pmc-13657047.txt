Development of bioinformatics competency framework to address labor market demands in Thailand
Authors: Pathacha Suksakit, Dumnoensun Pruksakorn, Kamonnut Singkhamanan, Surasak Sangkhathat, Yotsawat Pomyen, Tanaporn Uengwetwanit, Saowalak Kalapanulak, Treenut Saithong, Weerayuth Kittichotirat, Teeraphan Laomettachit, Kanthida Kusonmano, Raphatphorn Navakanitworakul, Arinthip Thamchaipenet, Kanphavarith Pongthanatbutsadeewon, Sivamoke Dissook, Patcharawadee Thongkumkoon, Supapon Cheevadhanarak, Jutarop Phetcharaburanin, Peerut Chienwichai, Nuttinee Teerakulkittipong, Piroon Jenjaroenpun, Teerasak E-kobon, Wanna Chetruengchai, Monnat Pongpanich, Apiwat Sangphukieo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBioinformatics plays a pivotal role in advancing healthcare, agriculture, and biotechnology. However, in Thailand, expectations regarding workforce demand and the core competencies of bioinformatics graduates remain insufficiently defined. Survey findings reveal that the demand for bioinformatics graduates is predominantly concentrated in the public sector, with comparatively limited demand from the private sector. Furthermore, there are differences between the public and private sectors in terms of the skill sets expected of bioinformaticians. To address these gaps, the Bioinformatics Association in Thailand, supported by Hub of Talents for Thailand Bioinformatics Research Network, National Research Council of Thailand, has developed a national competency framework for bioinformatics. This framework is intended to standardize graduate development and align educational outcomes with labor market needs. It encompasses four core domains: knowledge, skills, ethics, and character. This framework is informed by the Ministry of Higher Education, Science, Research and Innovation, together with expert consultations and public hearings involving key stakeholders. The proposed framework provides a structured foundation for cultivating a workforce equipped with both theoretical expertise and practical capabilities. Its implementation is expected to strengthen the national bioinformatics ecosystem and enhance the global competitiveness of Thai bioinformatics graduates.Keywords: bioinformatics, competency framework, genomics, precision medicine

Introduction

Bioinformatics is a highly interdisciplinary field that integrates biological sciences with computational, mathematical, and statistical approaches to store, manage, analyze, and interpret biological data [1]. It plays a critical role across multiple sectors, including healthcare, agriculture, and the biotechnology industry (Fig. 1). In Thailand, Bioinformatics emerged in the early 2000s, driven by national government initiatives to strengthen life science research. The National Center for Genetic Engineering and Biotechnology (BIOTEC) established a dedicated bioinformatics research program and hosted the first International Conference on Bioinformatics in 2002. A major milestone followed in 2004, when King Mongkut’s University of Technology Thonburi (KMUTT) launched Thailand’s first Master’s degree program in bioinformatics, with student scholarships supported by BIOTEC [2]. Since then, many universities have established Master’s and doctoral programs in bioinformatics, reflecting the growing demand for expertise in the field. More recently, the national bioinformatics ecosystem has been further strengthened by the Genomics Thailand Initiative, launched in 2019, which has accelerated large-scale genomics research and data-driven precision medicine. Despite this expansion in education and research infrastructure, the number of bioinformatics researchers in Thailand remains limited relative to the increasing demand. The field is nevertheless supported by a growing professional community, exemplified by the establishment of the Bioinformatics Academy Association of Thailand (BAT) in 2023 and the annual Thailand Bioinformatics Research Network (TBRN) Symposium, which foster collaboration, training, and knowledge exchange among researchers nationwide.

The role of bioinformaticians across various industries.

International reports further highlight an urgent and growing demand for professionals with expertise in bioinformatics and data science [1, 3]. This trend aligns with Thailand’s strategic direction to enhance its global competitiveness. The Asia–Pacific region represents the fastest-growing market for bioinformatics, with a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of ~14%–18%, particularly in countries such as China, Singapore, India, and Thailand [4, 5]. This growth is driven in part by national policies promoting genome-based research and precision medicine. In Thailand, the bioinformatics market is projected to grow at a CAGR of up to 40.36% between 2024 and 2032, reaching an estimated market value of USD 160.46 million by 2032 [6]. Nevertheless, several challenges remain. These include a shortage of skilled bioinformatics professionals, the complexity of managing and analyzing large-scale biological data, and the need to develop standardized frameworks and regulatory policies for the use of genetic data.

Thailand’s shortage of skilled bioinformatics professionals is reflected in its efforts to strengthen precision medicine capabilities through the Genomics Thailand project. This initiative seeks to establish a national reference database of genetic variation that will serve as a foundational resource for advancing precision medicine and genomic research in the country. A key challenge in implementing this initiative is the shortage of bioinformatics experts, who are essential for managing, analyzing, and interpreting large-scale and complex genomic data [7]. Previous assessments estimated that Thailand had only ~20 bioinformaticians with expertise in handling large-scale sequencing data and projected a need for an additional 500 professionals to meet future demand [8]. Although these estimates were reported in 2019 and the workforce has likely grown since then, the expansion of national genomics initiatives and precision medicine programs continues to outpace the availability of skilled bioinformatics personnel. Therefore, to respond to the rapidly growing domestic labor market demands, it is imperative to develop appropriate curricula, competency frameworks, and supporting mechanisms to accelerate the development of a skilled bioinformatics workforce capable of driving precision medicine and genomic research in Thailand.

Survey findings from industry, research institutions, and academic organizations indicate that expectations and required skill sets for bioinformaticians remain diverse. However, a clearly defined competency framework to guide workforce development in this field is still lacking. According to a report by Grand View Research, bioinformaticians are expected to possess a range of core competencies, including programming, machine learning (ML), statistics, data processing, and data visualization [9].

To address this gap, the BAT, supported by a national expert network, developed a bioinformatics competency framework through a comprehensive assessment process involving stakeholder engagement. This process included the analysis of job roles, responsibilities, and required competencies at different levels. The resulting framework can be applied to education, workforce development, and recruitment, with the aim of strengthening Thailand’s human capital to effectively meet the demands of the global biotechnology industry.

Methods

Survey design

This study comprised two phases: a nationwide workforce demand survey and the development of a national bioinformatics competency framework, both conducted by the Hub of Talents for Thailand Bioinformatics Research Network and the BAT. The survey instrument was developed systematically: we first reviewed the program learning outcomes of all bioinformatics-related graduate programs in Thailand, which informed the competency domains and questionnaire items. To ensure clarity and content validity, the instrument was iteratively reviewed across institutions and piloted before full administration. The primary aim of the survey was to characterize the demand for bioinformaticians across different sectors. The survey was provided in Supplementary data 1 in both original Thai version and translated English version.

Sampling and scope

To ensure that respondents were relevant to the study aim, we restricted participation to institutions meeting predefined eligibility criteria.

Private sector sampling

Private companies were selected from the top 1%–5% by revenue of all 992 508 registered companies. The selected companies were categorized into five industry groups relevant to bioinformatics: (i) agriculture, forestry, and fisheries; (ii) manufacturing; (iii) wastewater management; (iv) scientific and technical services; and (v) healthcare and social services.

Public sector sampling

Public sector organizations were selected from government agencies, state enterprises, and universities. A total of 14 163 government units at the division level or higher were considered, with selections made from eight relevant ministries expected to be relevant to bioinformatics-related activities. Universities were selected from a total of 454 institutions (including all campuses), focusing on those with relevant faculties, institutes, or research areas, such as medicine, science, agriculture, and industrial technology.

Ranking-based scoring method

To derive conclusions from the survey responses, a ranking-based scoring method was applied. Each variable was evaluated using different levels of importance depending on the section of the questionnaire. For ‘recruitment criteria for bioinformatics positions’ (Fig. 2), a 4-point importance scale was used, where 4 indicated the highest importance and 1 indicated the lowest importance. For ‘skill expectations’ (Fig. 3), a 5-point importance scale was applied, where 5 indicated the highest importance and 1 indicated the lowest importance. In both cases, variables that were not evaluated by respondents were excluded from the calculation, indicating no consideration of importance. All variables were ranked based on the total aggregated importance scores from all respondents.

Controlling for institutional size bias

We recognized that workforce-demand estimates could be skewed toward larger institutions, which generally report greater absolute demand. To address this, we collected information on institution size and incorporated it into the analysis, allowing us to examine the demand relative to institution size.

Framework development and validation

The competency framework was developed through focus group discussions and subsequently underwent independent public consultation on 16 May 2025 at King Mongkut’s University of Technology Thonburi, with feedback systematically incorporated into the final version.

Results

Survey result

A total of 206 organizations responded to the survey, including 156 from the public sector and 50 from the private sector. The findings revealed that over 34% of public sector organizations (53 out of 156 respondents) reported employing personnel in bioinformatics-related positions, even though >92% of these organizations were not directly engaged in bioinformatics-related activities. These findings indicate a rising demand for bioinformatics professionals in the public sector.

Analysis by organizational size showed that most organizations employing bioinformatics personnel were large enterprises (assets >200 million THB), accounting for 53% (28/53) of organizations with such positions. Medium-sized organizations (assets 20–200 million THB) accounted for 38% (20/53), while only 1% were small organizations (assets <20 million THB). These findings suggest that bioinformatics employment is more prevalent in larger or higher-value organizations, which may be more likely to recognize the importance and benefits of such roles than smaller organizations.

In the private sector, only 14% (7/50) of respondent organizations reported employing personnel in bioinformatics-related positions. Among these, 71% (5/7) were large enterprises. Although none of these companies were bioinformatics startups, their engagement in or interest in bioinformatics-related activities likely contributed to hiring bioinformatics professionals. These findings suggest that bioinformatics roles are not yet widely recognized as a core strategic function within Thailand’s private sector. This may be partly due to the lack of clear short-term business outcomes from bioinformatics applications. In addition, small- and medium-sized enterprises may perceive such investments as requiring substantial resources in terms of both personnel and infrastructure. Furthermore, Thailand currently lacks clear success cases demonstrating the ability of bioinformatics to drive commercially viable innovations, products, or services.

Recruitment practices represent a critical process that influences organizational performance. Differences in recruitment approaches between the public and private sectors reflect distinct institutional priorities and expectations toward candidates. As shown in Fig. 2, public sector organizations primarily select candidates based on formal educational qualifications (32%), specifically requiring degrees in bioinformatics. This reflects a standardized human resource system in the public sector, which emphasizes centrally defined criteria, such as position classifications and qualification requirements established by the Office of the Civil Service Commission, rather than practical competency-based assessments. This trend aligns with national human capital strategies seen in regions like Malaysia, where formal degree programs are the cornerstone for developing a standardized bioinformatics workforce [10]. In contrast, private-sector organizations place greater emphasis on competency, practical skills, and demonstrated potential, primarily assessed through examinations and interviews (33%). Notably, candidates are not necessarily required to have a formal background in bioinformatics, as hiring decisions are largely based on demonstrated competencies assessed through organizational selection processes. This shift toward competency-based hiring is exemplified by professional standards such as the UK’s Level 7 Degree Apprenticeships, which allow individuals to qualify for professional bioinformatics roles through demonstrated practical work rather than traditional academic qualifications [11]. Moreover, this approach is further exemplified by platforms like BurkinaBioinfo in West Africa, which employs a performance-based selection model, identifying and promoting the ‘best-performing participants’ from intensive training workshops to fill specialist roles [12].

Bioinformaticians are expected not only to possess strong foundations in biological sciences but also to demonstrate proficiency in advanced data analysis tools, large-scale data processing, and the development of specialized algorithms or software. In addition, effective communication of scientific findings and the ability to collaborate across disciplines are essential attributes of successful bioinformaticians.

However, expectations regarding these competencies differ markedly between the public and private sectors, with important implications for the design of higher education curricula to better align with labor market demands (Fig. 3). Survey results indicate that public sector organizations prioritize programming skills as the most important competency, including proficiency in languages such as Python, R, Java, and Unix-based systems, reflecting the need to support large-scale research data analysis. This is followed by foundational biological knowledge and an understanding of omics technologies. In contrast, the private sector places the highest priority on knowledge of omics technologies, enabling the translation of scientific knowledge into product and service development. Biological knowledge and informatics skills (e.g. database management) are considered of comparable importance as secondary competencies.

The survey findings indicate that both public and private sector organizations in Thailand have a demand for at least 50 additional bioinformatics positions. The differences in recruitment approaches and skill expectations between the two sectors present a key challenge in designing higher education curricula that can effectively address the needs of both sectors in a balanced manner. In response to this challenge, this study proposes a bioinformatics competency framework aligned with labor market demands.

Implications and recommendations

The findings of this study highlight notable differences in recruitment priorities for bioinformatics personnel between public- and private-sector organizations in Thailand. While both sectors consider multiple criteria in their hiring decisions, the relative emphasis placed on each criterion differs. Public-sector organizations tend to prioritize formal educational qualifications, whereas private-sector organizations place greater importance on competency, practical skills, and demonstrated potential, alongside work experience. These differences suggest that recruitment practices are shaped by distinct institutional structures and operational needs, which, in turn, influence how candidate suitability is defined and assessed in each sector. Understanding these contrasting recruitment priorities is essential for aligning workforce development strategies and designing bioinformatics training programs that are responsive to both public and private sector demands.

Similar trends in competency-based workforce development have been observed internationally. In Malaysia, bioinformatics workforce development has been strongly supported through structured academic programs designed to standardize graduate competencies and address the growing needs of the biotechnology and biomedical sectors [10]. Likewise, the UK has adopted competency-oriented initiatives, such as Level 7 Degree Apprenticeships, which integrate academic learning with workplace-based training and emphasize demonstrated professional competencies alongside formal qualifications [11]. Across the broader Asia-Pacific region, APBioNet has long recognized the need to bridge bioinformatics capability gaps through education, training, and policy engagement [13], with countries such as Singapore developing robust bioinformatics ecosystems anchored by dedicated research institutes and government-supported degree programs [14]. Together, these examples illustrate a broader international shift toward competency-based workforce development, in which higher education and professional training are increasingly aligned to prepare graduates with the knowledge, skills, and competencies required by the evolving bioinformatics workforce.

The design of bioinformatics curricula must balance foundational scientific knowledge, practical competencies, and professional attributes required across diverse sectors, including pharmaceuticals, precision medicine, modern agriculture, and biotechnology. Although 12 graduate-level bioinformatics programs are currently offered by leading Thai universities, their curriculum structures and learning outcomes vary considerably as they have been developed independently to address different institutional priorities and areas of expertise. This diversity provides flexibility but also presents challenges in establishing consistent educational standards and graduate competencies at the national level. These challenges are further compounded by the rapid evolution of bioinformatics, which requires curricula to be continuously updated to incorporate emerging topics such as machine learning, pipeline automation, genomic medicine, health data analytics, artificial intelligence (AI)–driven biotechnology, and digital genetic diagnostics [1, 15]. At the same time, the growing demand from industry for graduates with these specialized competencies highlights the need for stronger alignment between higher education and workforce requirements. Furthermore, Thailand’s bioinformatics ecosystem is still in its early stages of development and continues to face challenges in workforce capacity, curriculum harmonization, and sustainable collaboration between academia, healthcare institutions, industry, and government. Addressing these issues is therefore essential for strengthening the national bioinformatics workforce and supporting Thailand’s long-term competitiveness in the bioeconomy.

To address these challenges, the Hub of Talents for Thailand Bioinformatics Research Network, in collaboration with the BAT, developed a bioinformatics competency framework to serve as a foundational guide for curriculum development, workforce training, and capacity building. The framework aims to ensure that graduates possess the competencies required by the labor market while remaining competitive in the international workforce. In addition to expert input, the proposed framework was informed by multiple evidence sources, including curriculum analysis, stakeholder consultation, and a nationwide workforce survey, providing a context-specific reference for curriculum development and workforce planning in Thailand.

The initiative began with a comprehensive review of 12 graduate-level bioinformatics programs (master’s and doctoral levels) offered by leading universities in Thailand (Table 1), particularly those with strengths in science, engineering, and medicine. Based on this review, representatives from the national bioinformatics research network identified and categorized the expected learning outcomes into three domains—knowledge, skills, and ethics and personal attributes—consistent with the national higher education standards established by the Higher Education Standards Committee [16]. These domains subsequently served as the foundation for stakeholder engagement activities, including focus group discussions with representatives from academia and industry across the healthcare, agriculture, and food sectors.

The recommendations are proposed for key stakeholders in bioinformatics workforce development in Thailand. Universities and curriculum developers should restructure bioinformatics programs to explicitly address the four competency domains proposed in this framework. Government and policymakers should establish a national mechanism to periodically review and update the competency framework in response to evolving labor market demands. The private sector is encouraged to engage with universities through curriculum advisory boards to better align graduate competencies with workplace expectations. Organizations seeking to establish such collaborations may also connect through the BAT, which serves as a national platform for networking and partnership between academia, industry, and government in the bioinformatics ecosystem.

To contextualize the proposed framework within an international standard, Table 2 presents a comparison between the Thai bioinformatics competency framework and the International Society for Computational Biology (ISCB) Competency Framework version 3.0 [11]. The two frameworks share a broadly aligned structure encompassing bioscience knowledge, technical and data science skills, and professional conduct. Notably, the Thai framework explicitly defines Ethics and Character as standalone domains, reflecting alignment with Thailand’s national higher education standards [16]. In addition, the Thai framework incorporates competencies specific to AI/ML bias identification and data privacy protection that are not explicitly defined in ISCB v3. Conversely, ISCB v3 includes dedicated competencies for FAIR data management (E3) and continuing professional development (M3), which may be considered for incorporation in future revisions of the Thai framework.

Conclusion

The development of a bioinformatics competency framework plays a critical role in bridging the gap between Thailand’s education system and labor market demands. The effective integration of this framework into teaching and learning practices will enable universities with existing bioinformatics programs to enhance and modernize their curricula while also providing a guideline for institutions seeking to establish new programs. Ultimately, this will support the development of a skilled bioinformatics workforce capable of driving the country’s bio-based economy and competing effectively on the global stage.

The proposed framework aligns with international standards, as demonstrated by its broad concordance with the ISCB Competency Framework version 3.0 [11], while also reflecting Thailand’s national higher education standards and the specific demands of its labor market. Notably, the explicit inclusion of Ethics and Character as standalone domains, along with competencies addressing AI/ML bias and data privacy, represents a context-specific contribution that extends beyond existing international frameworks.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The survey response rate from the private sector was relatively low, which may limit the representativeness of private sector demand data. Participation of the survey depended substantially on individual interest in the survey topic, which could not be estimated in advance. Consequently, a low response rate was anticipated even though we restricted our target group to individuals most closely related to bioinformatics. Therefore, we acknowledge that the survey result should be interpreted as indicative rather than fully representative of the wider bioinformatics community in Thailand. We regard this pilot as an opportunity to refine our survey approach, and the findings will directly inform a subsequent survey with a better-characterized target group, improved recruitment channels, and follow-up strategies designed to increase participation. In addition, the framework was developed based on the current state of bioinformatics education and labor market needs, and will require periodic revision to remain responsive to the rapid evolution of the field, particularly in areas such as large language models, spatial omics, and AI-driven drug discovery.

To maximize its impact, coordinated efforts among academia, industry, and policymakers are essential to ensure the sustained implementation and continuous refinement of the framework in response to evolving technological and workforce needs. Future work should focus on empirical evaluation of the framework’s implementation across universities, as well as the development of assessment tools to measure graduate competency outcomes against the defined standards.
