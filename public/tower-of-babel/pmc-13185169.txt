Effect of belt electrode-skeletal muscle electrical stimulation on fracture healing in a rat femoral fracture model
Authors: Yuta Tsubouchi, Takashi Kataoka, Ryota Takase, Takefumi Otsu, Ryoji Hamanaka, Masashi Kataoka, Nobuhiro Kaku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBelt electrode–skeletal muscle electrical stimulation (B-SES) has been reported to influence bone remodeling and muscle function. However, its potential role in fracture healing remains incompletely understood. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of B-SES on fracture repair in a rat femoral fracture model.MethodsTwelve male Sprague–Dawley rats with surgically induced femoral fractures were randomly assigned to either a B-SES group or a control group (n = 6 per group). B-SES was initiated immediately after fracture induction and applied for 20 min per day, 5 days per week, for 4 weeks. At 4 weeks post-fracture, femora, tibiae, periosteum, and vastus medialis muscle were harvested for radiographic assessment, micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), histological analysis, biomechanical testing, and reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) analysis.ResultsRadiographic evaluation demonstrated improved cortical bone continuity and greater callus formation in the B-SES group. Micro-CT analysis revealed significantly increased callus bone volume, trabecular bone thickness, and trabecular bone number in fractured femora, as well as improved trabecular bone microarchitecture in non-fractured tibiae. Biomechanical testing showed significantly higher yield load and toughness in the B-SES group. Histological analysis confirmed increased callus area in B-SES–treated rats. At 4 weeks post-fracture, RT-PCR analysis of the periosteum showed no significant between-group differences in runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (Rankl), or secreted protein acidic and cysteine rich (Sparc) expression, whereas Sclerostin (Sost) expression was significantly higher in the B-SES group. In skeletal muscle, Myostatin (Mstn) expression was significantly reduced in the B-SES group, while insulin-like growth factor 1 (Igf1) expression did not differ significantly between groups.ConclusionB-SES was associated with improved fracture healing parameters, enhanced bone microarchitecture, and increased mechanical strength in this preclinical model. Reduced Mstn mRNA expression suggests a potential contribution of muscle–bone interactions; however, given the single time-point and transcription-level analyses, these findings should be considered preliminary. Further studies are required to clarify the underlying mechanisms and translational relevance of B-SES in fracture management.

Background

Fractures are the most prevalent musculoskeletal injuries and can severely impair functional independence, leading to diminished activities of daily living (ADL), reduced social participation, loss of productivity, and increased healthcare burdens. The incidence of osteoporotic fragility fractures is rising, particularly in aging populations, underscoring the urgent need for effective preventive and therapeutic strategies.

Standard fracture management focuses on mechanical stabilization—via internal or external fixation—and functional restoration through early rehabilitation. However, complications such as delayed union, nonunion, and failure of osteosynthesis continue to present significant clinical challenges. These complications not only compromise patients’ quality of life (QoL) but often necessitate prolonged treatment and rehabilitation.

Pharmacological agents have been investigated for their bone healing properties. For example, parathyroid hormone (PTH), anti-sclerostin antibodies, and bisphosphonates (BPs) have demonstrated efficacy in promoting bone formation and inhibiting resorption. Our prior research has shown that combined PTH and BP therapy significantly enhances fracture healing and callus formation [1]. Additionally, we reported that preoperative administration of abaloparatide—a PTH-related peptide—followed by postoperative BP therapy augmented bone regeneration in a rat femoral osteotomy model [2]. A combination of anti-sclerostin antibodies and active vitamin D analogs also produced similar osteogenic effects [3]. Despite their effectiveness, these pharmacological interventions are constrained by adverse effects and limitations on administration duration imposed by the Japanese healthcare system. In addition to pharmacological and mechanical interventions, experimental evidence suggests that peripheral neurotransmitters may also modulate fracture healing; for example, peripheral dopamine administration has been shown to influence bone repair processes in a rat fracture model [4].

Among non-pharmacological treatments, low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) is a widely used physical therapy shown to facilitate fracture healing [5]. However, LIPUS primarily promotes osteogenesis and lacks efficacy in preventing disuse atrophy or maintaining muscle function—critical components of comprehensive fracture recovery.

To address this limitation, belt electrode-skeletal muscle electrical stimulation (B-SES) was developed as an innovative approach to stimulate large muscle groups, such as the quadriceps, through full-length electrode belts (Homer Ion, Tokyo, Japan) [6]. Studies have demonstrated that quadriceps contractions at 50–100 Hz are optimal for inducing skeletal muscle responses in rats [7, 8]. B-SES elicits synchronous muscle contractions, thereby increasing the mechanical load on adjacent bones. This mechanical stress is known to stimulate bone formation, as seen in models using treadmill exercise [9–11].

Because high-intensity exercise is often unfeasible for elderly individuals at risk for fragility fractures, B-SES-induced contractions may serve as a practical alternative. Indeed, animal studies involving immobilization-induced bone atrophy have shown that B-SES mitigates bone loss and preserves trabecular architecture [12]. However, the specific effects of B-SES on fracture healing—including its influence on osteogenesis, pseudogenesis, bone remodeling, and trabecular versus cortical microstructure—have not yet been fully elucidated.

Given that both structural repair and early restoration of motor function are essential for optimal fracture recovery, we hypothesized that B-SES could accelerate bone healing via three synergistic mechanisms: (1) direct electrical stimulation of the fracture site, (2) mechanical stress generated by muscle contractions, and (3) secretion of myokines associated with muscle hypertrophy.

Accordingly, this study investigated the effects of B-SES on fracture healing in a rat femur fracture model, focusing on callus volume and bone microarchitecture during different stages of healing.

Methods

Surgical technique used to construct the femur fracture model

A total of 12 male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (8 weeks old; CLEA Japan, Inc., Tokyo, Japan), each weighing approximately 300 g, were used in this study. Ethical approval was obtained from the Oita University Animal Research Committee (Approval No. 222402).

The sample size was determined based on previous similar animal studies and ethical considerations to minimize animal use; therefore, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed.

Twelve rats were used to establish a femoral fracture model. The surgical procedure followed protocols described in previous studies [1–3]. Anesthesia was induced via intraperitoneal injection of a mixture containing 0.15-mg/kg medetomidine, 2-mg/kg midazolam, and 2.5-mg/kg butorphanol.

The right hind limb was prepared under sterile conditions. With the rat in a lateral position, the right femur was accessed via a posterolateral approach. A transverse osteotomy was performed at the midshaft, and the fracture fragments were realigned. Intramedullary fixation was achieved using a 1.5-mm stainless steel wire, trimmed at the intercondylar groove to preserve knee joint mobility. The wire was applied circumferentially around the fracture site for stabilization. Fascia and skin were closed using 3–0 nylon sutures.

Postoperatively, analgesics (subcutaneous buprenorphine and paracetamol) were administered immediately and on subsequent days. Rats were housed individually, given food and water ad libitum, and monitored daily.

At 4 weeks post-surgery, the animals were deeply anesthetized for euthanasia using the same anesthetic protocol as that used for fracture model induction, consisting of an intraperitoneal injection of medetomidine, midazolam, and butorphanol (total volume: 4.0 mL), in accordance with our previous studies [1–3]. While under deep anesthesia, animals were humanely euthanized, and the right femur, right tibia, and a portion of the right vastus medialis muscle were harvested. The stainless steel wire was removed from the femur before further analyses.

Experimental design

No predefined inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied in this animal study. All rats that underwent successful fracture surgery and survived until the planned endpoint were included in the final analysis, and no animals were excluded. After fracture induction, animals were randomly allocated to the control or B-SES group using simple randomization (n = 6 per group).

The B-SES group received B-SES treatment for four weeks beginning on postoperative day 1. At four weeks post-fracture, all rats were euthanized, and the fractured femur along with the ipsilateral non-fractured tibia were harvested for radiography, micro-computed tomography, histological analysis, and biomechanical testing. In addition, peri-fracture periosteum and a portion of the vastus medialis were collected for subsequent Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) analysis (Fig. 1A).

B-SES protocol

Belt electrode-skeletal muscle stimulation was applied to the right hind limb using a small animal stimulator (Homer Ion, Tokyo, Japan). Anesthesia was administered using the same protocol as during surgery, with the control group receiving equivalent anesthesia to eliminate confounding effects.

Belt electrodes were positioned around the proximal thigh and distal lower leg to deliver stimulation via the B-SES device (Fig. 1B). The protocol consisted of 50-Hz frequency, 4.7-mA intensity, 2 s on/2 s off duty cycle, and 20 min/session. Stimulation was performed once daily, 5 days per week, for 4 weeks [8]. This stimulation intensity corresponds to 60% of the maximal voluntary contraction (MVC), a level considered most effective for preventing muscle atrophy.

Soft X-ray analysis

Right femoral and tibial specimens were harvested and stripped of soft tissue. Radiographs were obtained using a Softex CSM-2 X-ray system (Softex, Tokyo, Japan) with HS Fuji Softex film (Fuji Film, Tokyo, Japan) at 30 kV, 15 mA, for 20 s, and a source-to-object distance of 45 cm.

Fracture union was evaluated using both anteroposterior (A-P) and lateral views. Visual assessment of callus bridging scored up to four points (1 point per cortical side). The Radiographic Union Scale for Tibial Fractures (RUST) was also applied, with scores ranging from 4 (nonunion) to 12 (complete union) [13].

Radiographic evaluations were independently performed by two experienced assessors who were blinded to group allocation (one orthopedic surgeon and one physical therapist). Inter-observer agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. In cases of disagreement, final scores were determined by consensus discussion.

High-resolution micro-CT analysis

Bone micro-CT imaging was performed following established guidelines [14]. Micro-CT scanning was performed using the SkyScan 1276 system (Bruker, Kontich, Belgium) at a voxel size of 20 μm, 100 kV, and 100 mA. Scans were conducted over 180° with a 105-ms exposure. Reconstruction used a cone-beam algorithm, and analysis was performed with CT Analyzer software.

The femoral Region of interest (ROI) was defined as a ± 4.2-mm segment centered at the fracture line. For the tibia, trabecular bone was analyzed 0.8–3.8 mm distal to the growth plate, and cortical bone 3.0–3.8 mm distal. Measured parameters included tissue volume (TV), bone volume (BV), bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular bone thickness (Tb.Th), number (Tb.N), separation (Tb.Sp), cortical bone area (Cr.Ar), and thickness (Cr.Th).

Histological analysis

Five femurs per group were randomly selected for histological analysis (n = 5 per group). Samples were fixed in 70% ethanol (24 h), then 99.5% ethanol (24 h), followed by 1-day immersions in acetone, 99.5% ethanol, and 2-propanol. On Day 6, bones were embedded in glycolmethacrylate and polymerized for 10 days.

Section (3 μm) were prepared using a Leica RM2255 microtome and stained with toluidine blue. Histomorphometric analysis was conducted using the Histometry RT CAMERA system (System Supply, Tokyo, Japan), assessing TV, callus volume (CV), callus area (CA), cartilage tissue volume (Ca.V), and area (Ca.A) within 0.43 mm proximal and distal to the fracture line.

Biomechanical analysis

Tibial bone strength was assessed via three-point bending tests using a universal testing system (Instron 5865; Instron, Kanagawa, Japan). Each tibia was centered anterior-side-up on a support, and loaded at 1 mm/s until fracture. Mechanical properties measured included yield load, yield stress, stiffness, Young’s modulus, and toughness.

RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from the periosteum and quadriceps muscle using TRIzol® Reagent. cDNA was synthesized with PrimeScript RT Master Mix, and RT-PCR was performed using TB Green Fast qPCR Mix. Expression levels were normalized to Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh; NM_017008).

Periosteal target genes included runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2; NM_053470), receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (Rankl; NM_057149), secreted protein acidic and cysteine rich (Sparc; NM_012656), and sclerostin (Sost; NM_030584).

Muscle gene targets included insulin-like growth factor 1 (Igf1; NM_178866) and myostatin (Mstn; NM_019151) (Table 1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.3. Comparisons between the control and B-SES groups were conducted using unpaired t-tests. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Structural, biomechanical, and molecular analyses were performed using six biological replicates per group (n = 6), whereas histological analyses were conducted using five biological replicates per group (n = 5).

The primary outcome was micro-CT–derived callus BV/TV. Other micro-CT parameters, biomechanical outcomes, and gene expression analyses were considered secondary or exploratory outcomes. Because this study was exploratory in nature and evaluated multiple outcome domains, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied; therefore, p values for secondary outcomes should be interpreted cautiously.

No a priori sample size calculation was performed; however, a post hoc power analysis (G*Power 3.1; two-tailed t-test, α = 0.05) was conducted for the primary outcome using the observed effect size (Cohen’s d = 2.39). With n = 6 animals per group, the achieved power (1 − β) was 0.96.

Discussion

In this study, we applied B-SES for 20 min per day, 5 days per week, over a 4-week period using a rat femoral fracture model. Radiographic evaluation, micro-CT, and histological analyses demonstrated enhanced fracture healing, increased callus volume, and improved callus microarchitecture in the B-SES group. Biomechanical testing further indicated improvements in yield load and toughness. At 4 weeks post-fracture, reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction analysis of the periosteum revealed no significant differences between groups in the expression of Runx2, Rankl, or Sparc, whereas Sost expression was significantly upregulated in the B-SES group. In skeletal muscle, Mstn expression was significantly reduced in the B-SES group, while Igf1 expression showed no significant difference between groups. In addition, in the non-fractured tibia, B-SES was associated with increased trabecular bone volume and improved trabecular microarchitecture, consistent with the structural changes observed at the fracture site. Collectively, these findings suggest that B-SES may exert coordinated musculoskeletal effects in this preclinical model, warranting further investigation into its biological and translational implications.

Current fracture management strategies predominantly involve pharmacological interventions and ultrasound therapy. B-SES offers a novel approach that promotes physiological bone formation through the dual mechanisms of skeletal muscle activation and direct bone stimulation.

Micro-CT analysis and histological evaluations revealed an early and significant increase in callus bone volume and improvements in bone microarchitecture in the B-SES group. The enhanced callus and cartilage formation observed histologically suggests that B-SES may promote endochondral ossification at the fracture site. Previous in vivo studies have demonstrated that electrical stimulation promotes mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) proliferation and differentiation, enhances cytokine production (e.g., BMPs, TGF-β), and activates calcium-calmodulin signaling pathways—each of which contributes to osteogenesis [15, 16]. A meta-analysis of randomized, sham-controlled trials further reported that electrical stimulation reduces pain and the risk of bone fusion failure [17]. However, whether B-SES can deliver sufficient electrical energy to directly stimulate bone tissue remains uncertain and warrants further investigation.

Skeletal muscle contractions induced by B-SES may generate sustained and intermittent mechanical loading on bone tissue. Bone is highly responsive to mechanical stimuli, which are known to promote remodeling and new bone formation [18]. In the present study, the non-fractured tibia in the B-SES group exhibited increased trabecular number and thickness, which was accompanied by significant improvements in mechanical strength parameters, including yield load and toughness. Previous studies have demonstrated that other mechanical stimulation modalities, such as therapeutic ultrasound and shockwave therapy, enhance callus formation, mineralization, and bone stiffness in a frequency- and dose-dependent manner [19–22]. Collectively, these findings support our initial hypothesis that B-SES–mediated mechanical loading may contribute to bone repair–related processes and improvements in bone strength.

Unexpectedly, Sost gene transcription was significantly elevated in the B-SES group at 4 weeks post-fracture. Sclerostin is a well-established negative regulator of bone formation and a key mediator of mechanotransduction in bone tissue [23–25]. Mechanical loading is generally reported to suppress Sost expression, thereby activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling and promoting osteoblast activity [25]. In our previous rat femoral fracture model, administration of an anti-sclerostin antibody accelerated fracture healing, further supporting the inhibitory role of sclerostin in bone repair [3]. From this perspective, the observed increase in Sost expression appears inconsistent with the classical model of load-induced osteogenesis.

Several factors may explain this discrepancy. Skeletal responses to mechanical stimulation depend on loading modality. Impact-based loading, characterized by repetitive ground reaction forces, has been associated with suppression of sclerostin and activation of osteogenic pathways [26, 27]. In contrast, B-SES–induced muscle contractions may more closely resemble resistance-type loading with limited impact forces. In a study comparing impact and resistance exercise, acute impact loading significantly increased circulating sclerostin, whereas resistance exercise did not induce significant changes [28]. These findings suggest that sclerostin responses vary according to loading pattern and that B-SES may elicit regulatory effects distinct from impact-based stimuli. Responses to mechanical loading also depend on temporal dynamics. Acute loading may transiently elevate circulating sclerostin levels, whereas repeated or sustained loading has been associated with reductions in circulating sclerostin over time [28, 29]. Because B-SES was applied repeatedly until the day before sacrifice and gene expression was assessed only at a single late-stage time point (4 weeks post-fracture), it remains unclear whether the observed increase in Sost transcription reflects an acute response to recent stimulation, a longer-term adaptive process, or phase-specific regulation during fracture healing. Beyond loading modality and timing, the intrinsic temporal and spatial regulation of sclerostin during fracture healing should be considered. Gene expression was evaluated only at 4 weeks post-fracture, corresponding to a relatively late stage of healing in rodents [30]. In human fragility fractures, early downregulation of Sost expression has been reported within several days after injury, followed by upregulation of genes involved in bone formation and resorption, suggesting that transient suppression of sclerostin during the inflammatory phase may contribute to the initiation of bone remodeling [23, 24]. Stress fracture models further demonstrate spatially heterogeneous regulation, including localized reductions in sclerostin expression adjacent to microdamage and dynamic changes in surrounding regions associated with new bone formation [25]. Collectively, these findings indicate that sclerostin functions in a context-dependent and site-specific manner rather than as a simple on–off regulator during bone repair [23, 25]. Importantly, circulating sclerostin concentrations do not necessarily parallel local transcriptional activity within bone tissue. In the present study, only periosteal mRNA expression was assessed at a single time point, and protein levels, spatial distribution, downstream signaling activity, and functional validation experiments were not performed. Therefore, causal relationships between altered Sost transcription and fracture healing outcomes cannot be established. The increased Sost expression observed at 4 weeks may reflect late-stage remodeling processes, acute regulatory responses, or other context-dependent mechanisms rather than necessarily indicating direct inhibition of osteogenesis. Accordingly, these molecular findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive mechanistic evidence.

Consistent with this context-dependent interpretation, reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction analysis of the periosteum surrounding the fracture site revealed no significant differences between the B-SES and control groups in the expression of Runx2, Rankl, or Sparc. Efficient mesenchymal cell differentiation and appropriate regulation of osteoclastogenesis are essential for balanced bone regeneration [31]. Because gene expression was evaluated only at 4 weeks post-fracture [30], dynamic transcriptional changes that may have occurred during earlier inflammatory or reparative phases could not be captured. Thus, the absence of significant differences does not exclude the possibility of earlier transient molecular effects induced by B-SES.

Beyond direct osteocyte-mediated responses to mechanical loading, accumulating evidence supports the concept of muscle–bone crosstalk mediated by muscle-derived factors, or myokines [32, 33]. In this context, the present study demonstrated a significant reduction in Mstn mRNA expression in skeletal muscle following B-SES intervention. Myostatin is a well-established negative regulator of muscle mass and has been implicated in adverse skeletal effects, including inhibition of osteoblast differentiation and promotion of osteoclast activity [34]. Previous studies have shown that myostatin knockout mice exhibit reduced osteoclast activity under hindlimb unloading [34], and that genetic or pharmacological suppression of Mstn enhances muscle–bone signaling and is associated with increased bone mass [35]. In addition, electrical muscle stimulation has been reported to suppress myostatin expression and attenuate musculoskeletal deterioration in disuse models [36]. Taken together, these findings raise the possibility that reduced Mstn expression may contribute to the structural improvements observed in the B-SES group through modulation of muscle–bone interactions. However, in the present study, only transcriptional changes were evaluated at a single time point, and protein expression, secretion dynamics, downstream signaling activity, and functional validation experiments were not performed. Therefore, causal relationships between decreased Mstn expression and enhanced fracture healing cannot be established. The observed reduction in Mstn should be interpreted as an exploratory finding that may reflect one component of a broader regulatory network rather than definitive evidence of a primary mechanistic pathway. Longitudinal analyses and protein-level validation will be necessary to clarify the mechanistic contribution of muscle–bone crosstalk to B-SES–mediated fracture repair.

This study has several methodological limitations. First, a single set of B-SES stimulation parameters (intensity, frequency, pulse width, and duration) was used, and the optimal conditions for promoting bone healing remain undefined. Second, the follow-up period was limited to 4 weeks post-fracture, corresponding to a relatively late stage of healing in rodents. Earlier dynamic changes during the inflammatory and reparative phases may not have been captured, and long-term effects on bone remodeling, cortical architecture, and mechanical competence were not assessed. Extended longitudinal studies are required to determine the temporal profile and durability of the observed effects. Third, molecular analyses were performed at a single time point and were limited to mRNA expression. Protein expression, spatial distribution, downstream signaling activity, and functional validation experiments were not conducted. Therefore, causal relationships between altered gene expression—particularly changes in Sost and Mstn—and enhanced fracture healing cannot be established. These molecular findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of a primary mechanistic pathway. Fourth, the increased expression of sclerostin in the B-SES group contrasts with reports describing suppression of sclerostin under mechanical loading. In the absence of protein-level validation and temporal analyses, the biological significance of this finding remains uncertain. Fifth, the control group did not receive sham electrical stimulation. Although animals in both groups underwent comparable handling and anesthesia procedures, the absence of a sham control precludes complete exclusion of potential confounding effects related to device application or procedural stress. Sixth, the study was conducted in a small-sample preclinical rat model (n = 6 per group). Although a post hoc power analysis indicated adequate statistical power for the primary outcome (BV/TV), secondary and exploratory outcomes may have been underpowered. In addition, multiple secondary outcomes were assessed without formal adjustment for multiple comparisons, which may increase the risk of type I error and affect the interpretability of secondary findings. Accordingly, statistically significant results among secondary analyses should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, while structural and biomechanical improvements were observed, the findings remain preliminary. Extrapolation to clinical fracture management should therefore be undertaken with caution and requires validation in larger, longitudinal, and translational studies.

Future studies should aim to optimize B-SES parameters, evaluate long-term healing outcomes, and incorporate protein-level and longitudinal analyses to clarify the temporal dynamics of fracture repair. Further preclinical validation will be necessary to confirm these findings and elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms. At present, the results should be considered preliminary.

Conclusion

This study provides preclinical evidence that B-SES is associated with improved fracture healing outcomes, enhanced bone microarchitecture in non-fractured bone, and increased mechanical strength in a rat femoral fracture model. These findings suggest that B-SES may represent a potential non-invasive adjunctive strategy to support musculoskeletal recovery following fracture.

However, given the preclinical design, small sample size, limited follow-up period, and the absence of protein-level and longitudinal mechanistic validation, these results should be interpreted as preliminary. Further studies are required to optimize stimulation parameters, assess longer-term outcomes, and elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms before clinical translation can be considered.
