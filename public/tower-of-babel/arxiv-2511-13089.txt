A new minor-closed class of transversal matroids
Gerry Toft

Abstract
. We provide a characterisation of when a single-element contraction of a transversal matroid is itself transversal. Using this characterisation, we define a new class of transversal matroids closed under minors, which we call path-circular matroids. Path-circular matroids generalise both of the well-known classes of bicircular matroids and multi-path matroids.



1.
Introduction

Along with representable and graphic matroids, transversal matroids are one of the most common examples of naturally-arising matroids. With this in mind, it is perhaps surprising that many aspects of the class of transversal matroids remain mysterious. Unlike representable and graphic matroids, transversal matroids are not closed under minors, and it is not known when the contraction of a transversal matroid is itself transversal.

A recent breakthrough concerning minors of transversal matroids was made by Bastida
[
1
]
, who found a polynomial-time algorithm to determine whether a single-element contraction of a transversal matroid
M
is also transversal, and to construct a presentation if so. We consider the same problem from a structural, rather than algorithmic, point of view. The aim is to provide a tool which can be used in proofs concerning minors of transversal matroids. The first main results of this paper are Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. Theorem
1.1
is a characterisation for when a single-element contraction of a transversal matroid is transversal, and, if it is, Theorem
1.2
describes how to find a presentation. These theorems will require the following definitions.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
M
be a transversal matroid with presentation
\mathcal{A}
such that
|\mathcal{A}|=r(M)
, and let
e\in E(M)
. Let
G
be a minimal
(e,\mathcal{A})
-presenting graph. Then
M/e
is transversal if and only if
G
is a tree.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
M
be a transversal matroid, and let
e\in E(M)
. Let
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{r})
be a presentation of
M
, with
r=r(M)
, such that
e\in A_{i}
for all
i\leq k
, and
e\notin A_{j}
for all
j>k
. Let
G
be a minimal
(e,\mathcal{A})
-presenting graph which is a tree, and let
\phi
be an
(e,\mathcal{A})
-presenting map of
G
. Suppose the edge set of
G
is

E(G)=\{\{u_{1},v_{1}\},\{u_{2},v_{2}\},\ldots,\{u_{k-1},v_{k-1}\}\}\,.

Then

(A_{\phi(u_{1})}\cup A_{\phi(v_{1})}-e,A_{\phi(u_{2})}\cup A_{\phi(v_{2})}-e,\ldots,A_{\phi(u_{k-1})}\cup A_{\phi(v_{k-1})}-e,A_{k+1},A_{k+2},\ldots,A_{r})

is a presentation of
M/e
.

This research was motivated by the problem of finding the maximal minor-closed class of transversal matroids. The largest known minor-closed classes of transversal matroids are
bicircular matroids
and
multi-path matroids
. Bicircular matroids were introduced by Simões-Pereira
[
12
]
as a matroid whose elements are the edges of a graph, and whose circuits are minimal, connected subgraphs containing at least two cycles. Matthews
[
10
]
showed that a bicircular matroid can also be viewed as a transversal matroid that has a presentation in which every element is contained in at most two sets. To construct such a presentation
\mathcal{A}
, add a set to
\mathcal{A}
, for each vertex of the graph, consisting of the edges incident to that vertex. Multi-path matroids, introduced by Bonin and Giménez
[
3
]
, are transversal matroids with a cyclic ordering
\sigma
of their elements, and a presentation
\mathcal{B}
such that each set of
\mathcal{B}
is a cyclic interval of
\sigma
, and no set of
\mathcal{B}
contains another.

Theorem
1.1
implies that a necessary condition for a transversal matroid
M[\mathcal{A}]
to be a member of the maximal minor-closed class of transversal matroids is that, for all
e\in E(M[\mathcal{A}])
, each minimal
(e,\mathcal{A})
-presenting graph is a tree. We define a class of transversal matroids which satisfy a stronger condition, that these graphs are paths, and show that this class is closed under minors.

Let
G
be a simple graph. A
path-circular collection
\mathcal{P}
of
G
is a collection of (not necessarily distinct) paths of
G
such that, if
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{k}
are the vertices (in order) of a path in
\mathcal{P}
and
i\in\{2,3,\ldots,k-1\}
, then

(i)

the degree of
u_{i}
in
G
is
2
, and

(ii)

every path of
\mathcal{P}
which contains
u_{i}
also contains at least one of
u_{1}
and
u_{k}
.

Note that a path-circular collection may contain “null paths” consisting of zero vertices. These will correspond to loops in the resulting matroid.

Suppose
V(G)=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{r}\}
. For all
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,r\}
, let
N(v_{i})=\{p\in\mathcal{P}:v_{i}\text{ is a vertex of }p\}
. Denote by
M(\mathcal{P})
the transversal matroid on groundset
\mathcal{P}
with presentation
(N(v_{1}),N(v_{2}),\ldots,N(v_{r}))
. We call such a matroid a
path-circular matroid
.

The definition of path-circular matroids is a generalisation of the definition of bicircular matroids: the elements of a bicircular matroid correspond to edges of a graph, while the elements of a path-circular matroid correspond to paths. Thus, a bicircular matroid is a path-circular matroid in which every path of
\mathcal{P}
contains at most one edge. Note that, in this case, the conditions (i) and (ii) of a path-circular collection are satisfied trivially. Path-circular matroids also generalise multi-path matroids: a multipath matroid is a path-circular matroid in which the graph
G
is a cycle. This is illustrated in Figure
2
. In the image of a path-circular matroid in Figure

\mathcal{P}
are unbroken lines, with loops representing paths consisting of a single vertex, and the edges of the graph
G
are the non-loop edges implied by the paths.

Preliminaries and Basic Results

Our notation and terminology follows
[
11
]
unless specified otherwise. We will use
[n]
to represent the set
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.

Let
G
be a graph, and
U
a set of vertices of
G
. The
subgraph of
G
induced by
U
is the graph obtained from
G
by deleting every vertex not in
U
. We shall denote a path of
G
by an ordered list of vertices which the path passes through. If
p=v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}
is a path of
G
, then we say the vertices
v_{1}
and

are the

of

.

2.1.
Transversal Matroids

Let
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{r})
be a collection of (not necessarily distinct) subsets of a set
E
. A
partial transversal
of
\mathcal{A}
is a subset
\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m}\}
of
E
such that the elements
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m}
are distinct, and there exist distinct elements
k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{m}\in[r]
with
x_{i}\in A_{k_{i}}
for all
i\in[m]
. A
transversal
of
\mathcal{A}
is a partial transversal
X
such that
|X|=r
.

Edmonds and Fulkerson
[
5
]
showed that there is a matroid on ground set
E
whose independent sets are the partial transversals of
\mathcal{A}
. This matroid is called a
transversal matroid
, and denoted
M[\mathcal{A}]
. The collection of sets
\mathcal{A}
is called a
presentation
of
M[\mathcal{A}]
.

The following is a well-known theorem due to Hall
[
6
]
concerning transversals.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{r})
. Then
\mathcal{A}
has a transversal if and only if, for all
J\subseteq[r]
,

\left|\bigcup_{j\in J}A_{j}\right|\geq|J|\,.

Every deletion of a transversal matroid is transversal. This can be seen from the following (straightforward) lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
M
be a transversal matroid with presentation
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{r})
, and let
X\subseteq E(M)
. Then

(A_{1}\cap X,A_{2}\cap X,\ldots,A_{r}\cap X)

is a presentation of
M|X
.

Although Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
are stated in terms of contractions of transversal matroids, we shall prove these theorems by considering deletions of the duals of transversal matroids. We shall often refer to the duals of transversal matroids as
co-transversal
matroids. The following three lemmas, concerning the rank and independent sets of a co-transversal matroid, will be useful. For the first of these, see
[
7
, Lemma 4.1]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
M
be the dual of a transversal matroid with presentation
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{r})
such that
r=r^{*}(M)
. Let
X\subseteq E(M)
. Then

r(X)=\min_{Y\supseteq X}\left(|Y|-\left|\{i\in[r]:A_{i}\subseteq Y\}\right|\right)\,.

The next lemma, which will be used extensively in the proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, is an immediate consequence of Lemma
2.3
in the case where
X
is a flat. It can also be seen as the dual of
[
4
, Corollary 2.5]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
M
be a the dual of a transversal matroid with presentation
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{r})
such that
r=r^{*}(M)
. Let
F
be a flat of
M
. Then
r(F)=|F|-\left|\{i\in[r]:A_{i}\subseteq F\}\right|
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
M
be the dual of a transversal matroid with presentation
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{r})
such that
r=r^{*}(M)
. Let
X\subseteq E(M)
. Then
X
is independent if and only if, for all
J\subseteq[r]
,

\left|X\cap\left(\bigcup_{j\in J}A_{j}\right)\right|\leq\left|\bigcup_{j\in J}A_{j}\right|-\left|J\right|\,.

Proof.

A set
X
is independent in
M
if and only if
E-X
is spanning in
M^{*}
. Since
r=r^{*}(M)
, this means that there is a transversal of
\mathcal{A}
contained in
E-X
, or, equivalently, the collection

(A_{1}\cap(E-X),A_{2}\cap(E-X),\ldots,A_{r}\cap(E-X))

has a transversal. By Lemma
2.1
, this is true if and only if, for all
J\subseteq[r]
,

\left|(E-X)\cap\left(\bigcup_{j\in J}A_{j}\right)\right|\geq|J|\,.

This inequality is equivalent to

\left|\bigcup_{j\in J}A_{j}\right|-\left|X\cap\left(\bigcup_{j\in J}A_{j}\right)\right|\geq|J|\,,

which can be rearranged to give the desired result.
∎

2.2.
Cyclic Flats

A
cyclic flat
of a matroid is a flat which is a union of circuits. The set of cyclic flats of a matroid
M
is denoted
\mathcal{Z}(M)
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
M
be the dual of a transversal matroid with presentation
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{r})
. Let
F
be a cyclic flat of
M
. Then there exists
J\subseteq[r]
such that
F=\bigcup_{j\in J}A_{j}
.

Proof.

Let
e\in F
. Since
F
is cyclic, there is a circuit
C_{e}
of
M
such that
e\in C_{e}
and
C_{e}\subseteq F
. By Lemma
2.5
, there exists
J_{e}\subseteq[r]
such that

(1)

\left|C_{e}\cap\left(\bigcup_{j\in J_{e}}A_{j}\right)\right|>\left|\bigcup_{j\in J_{e}}A_{j}\right|-|J_{e}|\,.

Let
X_{e}=\bigcup_{j\in J_{e}}A_{j}
. The above inequality is still true if
C_{e}
is replaced with
C_{e}\cap X_{e}
, so
C_{e}\cap X_{e}
is dependent. Therefore, since
C_{e}
is a circuit, we have that
C_{e}=C_{e}\cap X_{e}
, so
C_{e}\subseteq X_{e}
.

Next, we show that
X_{e}\subseteq\cl(C_{e})
. Let
x\in X_{e}-C_{e}
. Since
C_{e}-e
is independent, Lemma
2.5
implies that

|(C_{e}-e)\cap X_{e}|\leq|X_{e}|-|J_{e}|\,,

but, by (
1
),

|C_{e}\cap X_{e}|>|X_{e}|-|J_{e}|\,.

Thus,

|(C_{e}-e)\cap X_{e}|=|X_{e}|-|J_{e}|\,,

and so

|((C_{e}-e)\cup\{x\})\cap X_{e}|=|X_{e}|-|J_{e}|+1\,.

Therefore, by Lemma
2.5
,
(C_{e}-e)\cup\{x\}
is dependent, which implies that
x\in\cl(C_{e})
. Hence,
X_{e}\subseteq\cl(C_{e})
.

Now consider
J=\bigcup_{e\in F}J_{e}
. Let
X=\bigcup_{j\in J}A_{j}
. To complete the proof, we show that
F=X
. For all
e\in F
, we have that
e\in C_{e}\subseteq X_{e}\subseteq X
, so
F\subseteq X
. Conversely,

X=\bigcup_{e\in F}X_{e}\subseteq\bigcup_{e\in F}\cl(C_{e})\subseteq\cl(F)=F\,.

Therefore,
F=X
, as desired.
∎

2.3.
Presentations

A transversal matroid can have many different presentations. The next lemma will be useful to move between different presentations.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
M
be a transversal matroid with presentation
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{r})
. Let
i\in[r]
, and let
B_{i}\subseteq E(M)
such that
\cl^{*}(B_{i})=\cl^{*}(A_{i})
. Furthermore, suppose that if
B\subseteq E(M)
with
B_{i}\subseteq B
and
A_{i}\not\subseteq B
, then
r^{*}_{M}(B)<|B|-|\mathcal{A}(B)|
. Then,

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{i-1},B_{i},A_{i+1},A_{i+2},\ldots,A_{r})

is a presentation of
M
.

Proof.

For a set
X\subseteq E(M)
, define
\mathcal{A}(X)=\{j\in[r]:A_{j}\subseteq X\}
. Similarly, let
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(X)
be
\mathcal{A}(X)\cup\{i\}
if
B_{i}\subseteq X
, and
\mathcal{A}(X)-i
otherwise.

Now, let
N=M[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}]
. We show that
N=M
by showing that
r^{*}_{N}(X)=r^{*}_{M}(X)
for all
X\subseteq E(M)
. First, we show that
|X|-|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(X)|\geq r^{*}_{M}(X)
. If
X
contains neither
A_{i}
nor
B_{i}
, or both
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
, then, by Lemma
2.3
,

\displaystyle|X|-|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(X)|

\displaystyle=|X|-|\mathcal{A}(X)|

\displaystyle\geq r^{*}_{M}(X)\,.

If
A_{i}\subseteq X
and
B_{i}\not\subseteq X
, then

\displaystyle|X|-|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(X)|

\displaystyle=|X|-|\mathcal{A}(X)|+1

\displaystyle>r^{*}_{M}(X)\,.

Finally, if
A_{i}\not\subseteq X
and
B_{i}\subseteq X
, then
r^{*}_{M}(X)<|X|-|\mathcal{A}(X)|
. Thus,

\displaystyle|X|-|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(X)|

\displaystyle=|X|-|\mathcal{A}(X)|-1

\displaystyle\geq r^{*}_{M}(X)\,.

Now,
\cl^{*}(B_{i})=\cl^{*}(A_{i})
, which means that
\cl^{*}_{M}(X)
either contains neither
A_{i}
nor
B_{i}
, or both
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
. Thus, Lemma
2.4
implies that

\displaystyle r^{*}_{M}(X)

\displaystyle=|\cl^{*}_{M}(X)|-|\mathcal{A}(\cl^{*}_{M}(X))|

\displaystyle=|\cl^{*}_{M}(X)|-|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\cl^{*}_{M}(X))|\,.

Therefore, for all
Y\supseteq X
, we have that
|Y|-|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(Y)|\geq r_{M}^{*}(X)
, with equality if
Y=\cl^{*}_{M}(X)
. Hence, by Lemma
2.3
,

\displaystyle r_{N}^{*}(X)

\displaystyle=\min_{Y\supseteq X}\left(|Y|-|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(Y)|\right)

\displaystyle=r^{*}_{M}(X)\,.

∎

A
maximal presentation
of a transversal matroid
M
is a presentation in which each set is a cyclic flat of
M^{*}
. Every transversal matroid has a unique maximal presentation
[
2
]
. The next lemma shows how to obtain this maximal presentation. It can be seen from repeated application of Lemma
2.7
, taking
B_{i}=\cl^{*}(A_{i})
, or from
[
2
, Lemma 3]
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
M
be a transversal matroid with presentation
(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{r})
such that
r=r(M)
. Then the maximal presentation of
M
is
(\cl^{*}(A_{1}),\cl^{*}(A_{2}),\ldots,\cl^{*}(A_{r}))
.

Observe that Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
require that the rank of the transversal matroid is equal to the number of sets in its presentation. This does not limit their usefulness — every transversal matroid has such a presentation. Furthermore, if a transversal matroid
M
has a presentation which does not have
r(M)
sets, then
M
necessarily contains a co-loop
[
4
, Lemma 2.3]
.

2.4.
Mason’s
\alpha
-Criterion

The basis of the proofs of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
is a necessary and sufficient condition for a matroid to be co-transversal due to Mason
[
9
]
. For a matroid
M
, this condition works by determining, if
M
were to be co-transversal, the number of times each set would have to appear in a presentation of its dual.

More precisely, consider a co-transversal matroid
M
whose dual has maximal presentation
\mathcal{A}
. Each set of
\mathcal{A}
is a cyclic flat
F
of
M
. By Lemma
2.4
, the number of sets of
\mathcal{A}
contained within
F
is equal to
|F|-r(F)
. Hence, if
F
properly contains
k
sets of
\mathcal{A}
, then the number of times
F
itself appears in
\mathcal{A}
is
|F|-r(F)-k
. This motivates the recursive definition of the
\alpha
-function for a matroid
M
and a subset
X\subseteq E(M)
given below:

\alpha_{M}(X)=|X|-r(X)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{Z}(M)\\
F\subset X\end{subarray}}\alpha_{M}(F)

Using the ideas discussed above, we can use
\alpha_{M}
to construct the maximal presentation of a transversal matroid, as in the next lemma.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
M
be the dual of a transversal matroid with maximal presentation
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{r})
. If
F\in\mathcal{Z}(M)
, then there are exactly
\alpha_{M}(F)
integers
i\in[r]
such that
A_{i}=F
.

Lemma
2.9
immediately implies that, if
M
is co-transversal, then
\alpha_{M}(F)\geq 0
for all
F\in\mathcal{Z}(M)
. In fact,
\alpha_{M}(X)\geq 0
for all
X\subseteq E(M)
, and co-transversal matroids are precisely the matroids for which this is true.

Lemma 2.10
.

A matroid
M
is co-transversal if and only if
\alpha_{M}\left(X\right)\geq 0
for all
X\subseteq E(M)
.

Proofs of Lemmas
2.9
and
2.10
can be found in
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
, or
[
4
]
.

3.
Single-Element Contractions

In this section, we prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. Throughout, let
M
be the dual of a transversal matroid with presentation
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{r})
such that
r=r(M)
. Order the sets of
\mathcal{A}
such that there exists
k\in[r]
with
e\in A_{i}
for all
i\leq k
and
e\notin A_{j}
for all
j>k
. Let
e\in E(M)
be the element of
M
which we are trying to delete. For ease of reading, we define the following notation for a set
X\subseteq E(M)
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(X)

\displaystyle=\{i\in[r]:A_{i}\subseteq X\}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{e}(X)

\displaystyle=\{i\in\mathcal{A}(X):e\in A_{i}\}\,.

Let
G
be a minimal
(e,\mathcal{A})
-presenting graph on vertex set
\mathcal{A}_{e}(E(M))
such that the identity map is an
(e,\mathcal{A})
-presenting map of
G
. If
U\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{e}(E(M))
, we shall use
G[U]
to denote the subgraph of
G
induced by
U
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For all cyclic flats
F\in\mathcal{Z}(M)
, the graph
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(F)]
is connected.

Proof.

We show that, for all distinct
i,j\in\mathcal{A}_{e}(F)
, there is a path between
i
and
j
in
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(F)]
. By the definition of an
(e,\mathcal{A})
-presenting graph, the graph
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(\cl_{M}(A_{i}\cup A_{j}))]
is connected. Furthermore, since
F
is a flat, we have that
\cl_{M}(A_{i}\cup A_{j})\subseteq F
, so

G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(\cl_{M}(A_{i}\cup A_{j}))]
is a subgraph of
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(F)]
. Therefore, there is a path between
i
and
j
in
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(F)]
, completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
X\subseteq E(M\backslash e)
. Then

r_{M\backslash e}(X)\leq|X|-|\mathcal{A}(X)|-\max\{|\mathcal{A}_{e}(X\cup\{e\})|-1,0\}\,,

with equality if
X
is a cyclic flat of
M\backslash e
.

Proof.

First, suppose that
|\mathcal{A}_{e}(X\cup\{e\})|=0
. By Lemma
2.3
,

\displaystyle r_{M\backslash e}(X)

\displaystyle=r_{M}(X)

\displaystyle\leq|X|-|\mathcal{A}(X)|\,,

with equality if
X
is a cyclic flat of
M
. We want to show that equality holds if
X
is a cyclic flat of
M\backslash e
. There is no
A_{i}\subseteq X\cup\{e\}
with
e\in A_{i}
, so, by Lemma
2.6
,
X\cup\{e\}
is not a cyclic flat of
M
. Therefore, if
X
is a cyclic flat of
M\backslash e
, then
X
is a cyclic flat of
M
, and we have the desired result.

Now suppose
|\mathcal{A}_{e}(X\cup\{e\})|\geq 1
. This means that
e\in\cl_{M}(X)
. Also, note that if
X
is a cyclic flat of
M\backslash e
, then
X\cup\{e\}
is a cyclic flat of
M
. Applying Lemma
2.3
again, we get

\displaystyle r_{M\backslash e}(X)

\displaystyle=r_{M}(X)

\displaystyle=r_{M}(X\cup\{e\})

\displaystyle\leq|X\cup\{e\}|-|\mathcal{A}(X\cup\{e\})|

\displaystyle=|X\cup\{e\}|-|\mathcal{A}(X)|-|\mathcal{A}_{e}(X\cup\{e\})|

\displaystyle=|X|-|\mathcal{A}(X)|-(|\mathcal{A}_{e}(X\cup\{e\})|-1)\,,

with equality if
X\cup\{e\}
is a cyclic flat of
M
, and thus if
X
is a cyclic flat of
M\backslash e
. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}
is a maximal presentation of
M^{*}
. Let
F
be a cyclic flat of
M\backslash e
such that the graph
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})]
does not contain a cycle.

(i)

If
|\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})|\leq 1
, then
\alpha_{M\backslash e}(F)=|\{i\in\mathcal{A}(F):A_{i}=F\}|
.

(ii)

If
|\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})|>1
, then
\alpha_{M\backslash e}(F)
is equal to the number of edges
\{i,j\}
of
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})]
such that
\cl_{M\backslash e}(A_{i}\cup A_{j}-e)=F
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2
, if
|\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})|\leq 1
, then

\displaystyle\alpha_{M\backslash e}(F)

\displaystyle=|F|-r_{M\backslash e}(F)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}(M)\\
F^{\prime}\subset F\end{subarray}}\alpha_{M}(F^{\prime})

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{A}(F)|-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}(M)\\
F^{\prime}\subset F\end{subarray}}\alpha_{M}(F^{\prime})\,,

and if
|\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})|>1
, then

\alpha_{M\backslash e}(F)=|\mathcal{A}(F)|+|\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})|-1-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}(M)\\
F^{\prime}\subset F\end{subarray}}\alpha_{M}(F^{\prime})\,.

The proof is by induction on the number of cyclic flats properly contained within
F
. First, suppose there are no such cyclic flats. If
|\mathcal{A}(F\cup\{e\})|\leq 1
, then
\alpha_{M\backslash e}(F)=|\mathcal{A}(F)|
. For all
i\in\mathcal{A}(F)
, we have that
A_{i}
is a cyclic flat, since
\mathcal{A}
is maximal. Therefore,
A_{i}
is not properly contained within
F
, so
A_{i}=F
. Hence, (i) holds.

Otherwise,
|\mathcal{A}(F\cup\{e\})|>1
, so
\alpha_{M\backslash e}(F)=|\mathcal{A}(F)|+|\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})|-1
. Let
i\in\mathcal{A}(F)
. We know that
A_{i}\not\subseteq F
, so
A_{i}=F
. But now
e\in\cl_{M}(A_{i})
, which contradicts the maximality of
\mathcal{A}
. Thus,
|\mathcal{A}(F)|=0
. By Lemma
3.1
, the graph
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})]
is a tree. Thus, the number of edges in
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})]
is

|\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})|-1=\alpha_{M\backslash e}(F)\,.

Furthermore, for all
i,j\in\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})
, we have that
\cl_{M\backslash e}(A_{i}\cup A_{j}-e)=F
, since
F
does not properly contain any cyclic flats. Thus, (ii) holds.

Now, suppose that the result is true for all cyclic flats
F^{\prime}\subset F
. Therefore,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}(M)\\
F^{\prime}\subset F\end{subarray}}\alpha_{M\backslash e}(F^{\prime})=|\{i\in\mathcal{A}(F):A_{i}\subset F\}|+k\,,

where
k
is the number of edges
\{i,j\}
of
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})]
such that
\cl_{M\backslash e}(A_{i}\cup A_{j}-e)\subset F
.

If
|\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})|\leq 1
, then
k=0
. Thus,

\displaystyle\alpha_{M\backslash e}(F)

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{A}(F)|-|\{i\in\mathcal{A}(F):A_{i}\subset F\}|

\displaystyle=|\{i\in\mathcal{A}(F):A_{i}=F\}|\,,

and (i) holds. Finally, suppose
|\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})|>1
. As before, there is no
i\in\mathcal{A}(F)
such that
A_{i}=F
, since then
e\in\cl_{M}(A_{i})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\alpha_{M\backslash e}(F)

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{A}(F)|+|\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})|-1-|\{i\in\mathcal{A}(F):A_{i}\subset F\}|-k

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})|-1-k\,.

Since
|\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})|-1
is the number of edges in
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})]
, this implies that (ii) holds, and completes the proof.
∎

The next three lemmas are concerned with showing that Theorem
1.1
is true in the case where
\mathcal{A}
is maximal.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}
is a maximal presentation of
M^{*}
. If
G
is a tree with edge set

E(G)=\{\{i_{1},j_{1}\},\{i_{2},j_{2}\},\ldots,\{i_{k-1},j_{k-1}\}\}

then

(\cl_{M\backslash e}(A_{i_{1}}\cup A_{j_{1}}-e),\cl_{M\backslash e}(A_{i_{2}}\cup A_{j_{2}}-e),\ldots,\cl_{M\backslash e}(A_{i_{k-1}}\cup A_{j_{k-1}}-e),A_{k+1},A_{k+2},\ldots,A_{r})

is a presentation of
(M\backslash e)^{*}
.

Proof.

To show that
M\backslash e
is co-transversal, we need to show that
\alpha_{M\backslash e}(X)\geq 0
for all
X\subseteq E(M)
. By Lemma
3.3
, this is true if
X
is a cyclic flat. So assume
X
is not a cyclic flat. If
|\mathcal{A}(F\cup\{e\})|\leq 1
, then Lemma
3.3
implies that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{Z}(M)\\
F\subset X\end{subarray}}\alpha_{M\backslash e}(F)=|\mathcal{A}(X)|\,.

Now, by Lemma
2.3
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{M\backslash e}(X)

\displaystyle=|X|-r_{M\backslash e}(X)-|\mathcal{A}(X)|

\displaystyle=|X|-r_{M}(X)-|\mathcal{A}(X)|

\displaystyle\geq|X|-(|X|-|\mathcal{A}(X)|)-|\mathcal{A}(X)|

\displaystyle=0\,.

Otherwise,
|\mathcal{A}(F\cup\{e\})|>1
. Since
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(X\cup\{e\})]
does not contain a cycle, it has at most
|\mathcal{A}_{e}(X\cup\{e\})|-1
edges. Thus, applying Lemma
3.3
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{Z}(M)\\
F\subset X\end{subarray}}\alpha_{M\backslash e}(F)

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{A}(X)|+|\mathcal{A}_{e}(X\cup\{e\})|-1

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{A}(X\cup\{e\})|-1\,,

Therefore, using the fact that
e\in\cl_{M}(X)
and Lemma
2.3
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{M\backslash e}(X)

\displaystyle\geq|X|-r_{M\backslash e}(X)-|\mathcal{A}(X\cup\{e\})|+1

\displaystyle=|X|-r_{M}(X\cup\{e\})-|\mathcal{A}(X\cup\{e\})|+1

\displaystyle\geq|X|-(|X\cup\{e\}|-|\mathcal{A}(X\cup\{e\})|)-|\mathcal{A}(X\cup\{e\})|+1

\displaystyle=0\,.

Thus,
\alpha_{M\backslash e}(X)\geq 0
, so
M\backslash e
is co-transversal, and Lemma
2.9
implies that
(M\backslash e)^{*}
has the desired presentation.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}
is a maximal presentation of
M^{*}
. If
G
has a cycle, then
M\backslash e
is not co-transversal.

Proof.

Let
F
be a cyclic flat of
M\backslash e
such that
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(F)]
contains a cycle
C
. Furthermore, suppose there is no cyclic flat
F^{\prime}\subset F
of
M\backslash e
such that
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(F^{\prime})]
contains a cycle. First, we show there is no edge
\{i,j\}
of
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(F)]
such that
\cl_{M\backslash e}(A_{i}\cup A_{j}-e)=F
. Suppose such an edge
\{i,j\}
exists, and let
G^{\prime}
be the graph obtained by deleting
\{i,j\}
from
G
. We shall show that
G^{\prime}
is an
(e,\mathcal{A})
-presenting graph. Take distinct
i_{0},j_{0}\in\mathcal{A}_{e}(E(M))
, and let
X=\cl_{M}(A_{i_{0}}\cup A_{j_{0}})
. If either
A_{i}\not\subseteq X
or
A_{j}\not\subseteq X
, then
G^{\prime}[\mathcal{A}_{e}(X)]=G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(X)]
, and is connected. On the other hand, if
A_{i}\subseteq X
and
A_{j}\subseteq X
, then
F=\cl_{M\backslash e}(A_{i}\cup A_{j}-e)\subseteq X
. In particular, every vertex of the cycle
C
is contained in
G^{\prime}[\mathcal{A}_{e}(X)]
. Therefore, there is still a path between
i
and
j
in
G^{\prime}[\mathcal{A}_{e}(X)]
, so this graph is connected. Thus,
G^{\prime}
is
(e,\mathcal{A})
-presenting. But this means that
G
is not a minimal
(e,\mathcal{A})
-presenting graph, a contradiction. Thus, no such edge
\{i,j\}
exists.

In particular, this means that, for every edge
\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}
of
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})]
, we have that
\cl_{M\backslash e}(A_{i^{\prime}}\cup A_{j^{\prime}}-e)\subset F
. Furthermore, there is no
i\in\mathcal{A}(F)
such that
A_{i}=F
, as then
e\in\cl_{M}(A_{i})
. So, by Lemma
3.3
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}(M)\\
F^{\prime}\subset F\end{subarray}}\alpha_{M\backslash e}(F^{\prime})=|\mathcal{A}(F)|+k\,,

where
k
is the number of edges of
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})]
.

Therefore, by Lemma
3.2
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{M\backslash e}(F)

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{A}(F)|+|\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})|-1-|\mathcal{A}(F)|-k

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})|-1-k\,.

G[\mathcal{A}(F\cup\{e\})]
contains a cycle and, by Lemma
3.1
, is connected. Thus,
k\geq|\mathcal{A}_{e}(F\cup\{e\})|
, and so
\alpha_{M\backslash e}(F)<0
. Hence,
M\backslash e
is not co-transversal.
∎

For the remainder of this section, we show that Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
follow from Lemmas
3.4
and
3.5
in the case where
\mathcal{A}
is not a maximal presentation.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
i\in[r]
, and let

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{i-1},\cl_{M}(A_{i}),A_{i+1},A_{i+2},\ldots,A_{r})\,.

Let
G^{\prime}
be a graph defined as follows.

(i)

If
e\notin\cl_{M}(A_{i})-A_{i}
, then
G^{\prime}=G
.

(ii)

If
e\in\cl_{M}(A_{i})-A_{i}
, then
G^{\prime}
is constructed from
G
by adding a vertex
i
, and adding an edge from
i
to exactly one
j\in\mathcal{A}_{e}(\cl_{M}(A_{i}))
.

Then
G^{\prime}
is a minimal
(e,\mathcal{A}^{\prime})
-presenting graph.

Proof.

First, suppose that
e\notin\cl_{M}(A_{i})-A_{i}
, so
G^{\prime}=G
. This means that
\mathcal{A}_{e}(E(M))=\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{e}(E(M))
. Furthermore, for all distinct
i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{e}(E(M))
, we have that

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{e}(\cl_{M}(A_{i^{\prime}}\cup A_{j^{\prime}}))=\mathcal{A}_{e}(\cl_{M}(A_{i^{\prime}}\cup A_{j^{\prime}}))\,.

Thus, a graph is
(e,\mathcal{A}^{\prime})
-presenting if and only if it is
(e,\mathcal{A})
-presenting, so
G^{\prime}
is a minimal
(e,\mathcal{A}^{\prime})
-presenting graph.

Otherwise, suppose
e\in\cl_{M}(A_{i})-A_{i}
, and that
G^{\prime}
is constructed as described. Observe that
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{e}(E(M))=\mathcal{A}_{e}(E(M))\cup\{i\}
, so
G^{\prime}
has the correct vertex set. Additionally, since
e\in\cl_{M}(A_{i})
, Lemma
2.6
implies that there does exist
j\in\mathcal{A}_{e}(\cl_{M}(A_{i}))
, as required by the construction of
G^{\prime}
.

We show that
G^{\prime}
is
(e,\mathcal{A}^{\prime})
-presenting. Take distinct
i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{e}(E(M))
, and let
X=\cl_{M}(A_{i^{\prime}}\cup A_{j^{\prime}})
. If
\cl_{M}(A_{i})\not\subseteq X
, then
G^{\prime}[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{e}(X)]=G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(X)]
, so
G^{\prime}[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{e}(X)]
is also connected. Otherwise,
\cl_{M}(A_{i})\subseteq X
. Since
j\in\mathcal{A}_{e}(\cl_{M}(A_{i}))
, we have that
j
is a vertex of both
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(X)]
and
G^{\prime}[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{e}(X)]
. Thus,
G^{\prime}[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{e}(X)]
can be constructed from
G[\mathcal{A}_{e}(X)]
by adding the vertex
i
and the edge
\{i,j\}
. Hence,
G^{\prime}[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{e}(X)]
is connected, so
G^{\prime}
is
(e,\mathcal{A}^{\prime})
-presenting.

Now we show that
G^{\prime}
is a minimal
(e,\mathcal{A}^{\prime})
-presenting graph.
Let
H^{\prime}
be a graph obtained from
G^{\prime}
by deleting an edge, say
\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}
. If
\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}=\{i,j\}
, then the vertex
i
has no incident edges in
H^{\prime}
, so this graph is disconnected. Hence, by Lemma
3.1
,
H^{\prime}
is not
(e,\mathcal{A}^{\prime})
-presenting. Otherwise,
\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}
is also an edge of
G
, so let
H
be the graph obtained from
G
by deleting
\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}
.
H
is not
(e,\mathcal{A})
-presenting, so there exist distinct
i^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{e}(E(M))
such that
H[\mathcal{A}_{e}(\cl_{M}(A_{i^{\prime\prime}}\cup A_{j^{\prime\prime}}))]
is disconnected. Clearly, adding a new vertex
i
and one edge to
i
cannot connect this graph, so
H^{\prime}[\mathcal{A}_{e}(\cl_{M}(A_{i^{\prime\prime}}\cup A_{j^{\prime\prime}}))]
is also disconnected. Thus,
H^{\prime}
is not
(e,\mathcal{A})
-presenting, which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Suppose
G
is a tree with edge set

\{\{i_{1},j_{1}\},\{i_{2},j_{2}\},\ldots,\{i_{k-1},j_{k-1}\}\}\,.

Define
B_{1},B_{2},\ldots,B_{r-1}
by

(i)

B_{m}=A_{i_{m}}\cup A_{j_{m}}-e
if
1\leq m\leq k-1
, and

(ii)

B_{m}=A_{m+1}
if
k\leq m\leq r-1
.

Choose
J\subseteq[r-1]
. Suppose
M\backslash e
has presentation
\mathcal{C}=(C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{r-1})
, where
C_{m}=B_{m}
if
m\notin J
, and
C_{m}=\cl_{M\backslash e}(B_{m})
if
m\in J
. Let
j\in J
. Then

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=(C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{j-1},B_{j},C_{j+1},C_{j+2},\ldots,C_{r-1})

is a presentation of
M\backslash e
.

Proof.

Let
X\supseteq B_{j}
such that
C_{j}\not\subseteq X
. We show that
r_{M\backslash e}(X)<|X|-|\mathcal{C}(X)|
, and the result follows by Lemma
2.7
. Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{-}(X)

\displaystyle=\{i\in\mathcal{C}(X):i<k\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{+}(X)

\displaystyle=\{i\in\mathcal{C}(X):i\geq k\}\,.

Let
\ell\in\mathcal{C}(X)
. If
\ell\geq k
, then

B_{\ell}=A_{\ell+1}\subseteq C_{\ell}\subseteq X\,,

so
|\mathcal{C}^{+}(X)|\leq|\mathcal{A}(X)|
. Otherwise,

, and

B_{\ell}=A_{i_{\ell}}\cup A_{j_{\ell}}-e\subseteq C_{\ell}\subseteq X\,.

In particular, every element of
\mathcal{C}^{-}(X)
correponds to an edge of

. Since

is a tree,
|\mathcal{C}^{-}(X)|\leq\max\{|\mathcal{A}_{e}(X\cup\{e\})|-1,0\}
.

We have that

and

. Therefore, if

, then
|\mathcal{C}^{+}(X)|<|\mathcal{A}(X)|
, and if

, then
|\mathcal{C}^{-}(X)|<\max\{|\mathcal{A}_{e}(X\cup\{e\})|-1,0\}
. By Lemma
3.2
,

\displaystyle r_{M\backslash e}(X)

\displaystyle\leq|X|-|\mathcal{A}(X)|-\max\{\mathcal{A}_{e}(X\cup\{e\})-1,0\}

\displaystyle<|X|-|\mathcal{C}^{+}(X)|-|\mathcal{C}^{-}(X)|

\displaystyle=|X|-|\mathcal{C}(X)|\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorems
1.1
and

.

\mathcal{A}^{+}
be a maximal presentation of

. Repeatedly apply Lemma

to construct a minimal

To prove Theorem

4.
Path-Circular Matroids

In this section, we prove that path-circular matroids are closed under minors. Throughout this section, let
G
be a simple graph, and let
\mathcal{P}
be a path-circular collection of
G
. Let
p=u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{k}
be a path of
\mathcal{P}
, and suppose the vertex set of
G
is

V(G)=\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{k},v_{k+1},v_{k+2},\ldots,v_{r}\}\,.

By the definition of a path-circular matroid,

\mathcal{A}=(N(u_{1}),N(u_{2}),\ldots,N(u_{k}),N(v_{k+1}),N(v_{k+2}),\ldots,N(v_{r}))

is a presentation of
M(\mathcal{P})
.

We will show that
M(\mathcal{P})/p
is path-circular using Theorem
1.2
. However, applying Theorem
1.2
requires that
|\mathcal{A}|=r(M(\mathcal{P}))
, which may not be true if
M(\mathcal{P})
contains a coloop. However, we can freely add a coloop to a path-circular matroid by adding a new vertex to the graph, and adding a new path which contains only that vertex. Hence, if
M(\mathcal{P})
contains co-loops, we can delete the coloops, determine
M(\mathcal{P})/p
, then add the coloops back in. Thus, we may assume that
M(\mathcal{P})
does not contain any coloops, and so
|\mathcal{A}|=r(M(\mathcal{P}))
.

Now, we describe how to construct a graph
G^{\prime}
and set of paths
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
of
G^{\prime}
such that
M(\mathcal{P})/p=M(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
. First, construct a new graph from
G
as follows. For all
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,k-1\}
, subdivide the edge
\{u_{i},u_{i+1}\}
by a new vertex
u_{i,i+1}
, and, if
i>1
, add an edge to
u_{i-1,i}
. Add edges between
u_{1,2}
and every other vertex adjacent to
u_{1}
, and add edges between
u_{k-1,k}
and every other vertex adjacent to
u_{k}
. Finally, delete the vertices
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{k}
. Let
G^{\prime}
be the resulting graph.

Next, we construct paths
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
of
G^{\prime}
from the paths in
\mathcal{P}-p
. Consider each path
p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}-p
. If
p^{\prime}
contains
u_{1}

Lemma 4.1
.

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is a path-circular collection of
G^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
p^{\prime}=u_{1}^{\prime},u_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,u^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}
be a path of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
, and let
p^{\prime\prime}
be the corresponding path of
\mathcal{P}-p
. Let
i\in\{2,3,\ldots,k^{\prime}-1\}
. We need to show that

(i)

the degree of
u^{\prime}_{i}
in
G^{\prime}
is
2
, and

(ii)

every path of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
which contains
u^{\prime}_{i}
also contains either
u^{\prime}_{1}
or
u^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}
.

If
u^{\prime}_{i}\in\{v_{k+1},v_{k+2},\ldots,v_{r}\}
, then
u^{\prime}_{i}
is also a vertex of
p^{\prime\prime}
. Therefore,
u^{\prime}_{i}
has degree
2
in
G
, so also degree
2
in
G^{\prime}
. Otherwise
u^{\prime}_{i}=u_{j,j+1}
for some
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,k-1\}
. If
j\neq 1
and
j\neq k-1
, then
u_{j,j+1}
has degree
2
in
G^{\prime}
. Without loss of generality, suppose
j=1
. Since
i\notin\{1,k^{\prime}\}
, one of
u^{\prime}_{i-1}
and
u^{\prime}_{i+1}
is
u_{2,3}
and the other is a vertex
v\in\{v_{k+1},v_{k+2},\ldots,v_{r}\}
. This implies that the path
P^{\prime\prime}
has a sub-path
v,u_{1},u_{2}
. Therefore,
u_{1}
has degree
2
in
G
, so
u_{1,2}
has degree
2
in
G^{\prime}
. This establishes (i).

Now, suppose
q\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
contains
u_{i}^{\prime}
. Let
q^{\prime}
be the corresponding path of
\mathcal{P}-p
. If
u^{\prime}_{i}
is an element of
\{v_{k+1},v_{k+2},\ldots,v_{r}\}
, then
u_{i}^{\prime}
is a vertex of both
q^{\prime}
and
p^{\prime\prime}
. Otherwise,
u_{i}^{\prime}=u_{j,j+1}
for some
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,k-1\}
. This implies that
q^{\prime}
contains at least one of
u_{j}
and
u_{j+1}
. Since
u_{j,j+1}
is not an end vertex of
p^{\prime}
, we also have that both
u_{j}
and
u_{j+1}
are vertices of
p^{\prime\prime}
. Therefore, either
u_{j}
or
u_{j+1}
is a vertex of both
q^{\prime}
and
p^{\prime\prime}
. In either case, the paths
q^{\prime}
and
p^{\prime\prime}
contain a vertex in common, which means that
q^{\prime}
also contains an end vertex of
p^{\prime\prime}
. Let this vertex be
u^{\prime\prime}_{1}
. If
u^{\prime\prime}_{1}\in\{v_{k+1},v_{k+2},\ldots,v_{r}\}
, then
u^{\prime\prime}_{1}
is also an end vertex of
p^{\prime}
and a vertex of
q
, and we have the desired result. Otherwise,
u^{\prime\prime}_{1}=u_{j}
, for some
j\in[k]
. This implies that either
u_{j-1,j}
or
u_{j,j+1}
is an end vertex of
p^{\prime}
. Furthermore, since
q^{\prime}
contains
u_{j}
, the path
q
contains
u_{j-1,j}
(unless
j=1
) and
u_{j,j+1}
(unless
j=k
). Thus,
q
contains an end vertex of
p^{\prime}
, completing the proof.

Lemma 4.2
.

The path
p
is a minimal
(p,\mathcal{A})
-presenting graph.

Proof.

Let
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\in[k]
such that
i_{1}<i_{2}<i_{3}
. We show that
N(u_{i_{2}})\subseteq N(u_{i_{1}})\cup N(u_{i_{3}})
. Let
p^{\prime}\in N(u_{i_{2}})
. Since
\mathcal{P}
is a path-circular collection, the path
p^{\prime}
either contains
u_{1}
or
u_{k}
. In the former case,
p^{\prime}
contains the vertices
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{i}
, since the degree of each of these vertices is
2
in
G
. In particular,
p^{\prime}
contains the vertex
u_{i_{1}}
, so
p^{\prime}\in N(u_{i_{1}})
. Symmetrically, in the latter case,
p^{\prime}\in N(u_{i_{3}})
. Therefore,
p^{\prime}\in N(u_{i_{1}})\cup N(u_{j_{3}})
, so
N(u_{i_{2}})\subseteq N(u_{i_{1}})\cup N(u_{i_{3}})
.

Now, consider
i,j\in[k]
with
i<j
. Let
i^{\prime}
be the minimum element of
[k]
such that

N(u_{i^{\prime}})\subseteq\cl^{*}_{M(\mathcal{P})}(N(u_{i})\cup N(u_{j}))\,,

and let
j^{\prime}
be the maximum element of
[k]
such that

N(u_{j^{\prime}})\subseteq\cl^{*}_{M(\mathcal{P})}(N(u_{i})\cup N(u_{j}))\,.

We have already shown that, for all
x\in\{i^{\prime},i^{\prime}+1,\ldots,j^{\prime}\}
,

N(u_{x})\subseteq N(u_{i^{\prime}})\cup N(u_{j^{\prime}})\subseteq\cl^{*}_{M(\mathcal{P})}(N(u_{i})\cup N(u_{j}))\,.

Therefore, the subgraph of
P
induced by the vertices
\{u\in V(p):N(u)\subseteq\cl^{*}_{M(\mathcal{P})}(N(u_{i})\cup N(u_{j}))\}
is the path
u_{i^{\prime}},u_{i^{\prime}+1},\ldots,u_{j^{\prime}}
. This is connected, so
p
is a
(p,\mathcal{A})
-presenting graph. Furthermore,
p
is a tree, so
p
is a minimal
(p,\mathcal{A})
-presenting graph by Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

M(\mathcal{P})/p=M(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Let
v
be a vertex of
G^{\prime}
. Suppose
v=u_{i,i+1}
for some
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,k-1\}
. Then each path of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
which contains
u_{i,i+1}
corresponds to a path of
\mathcal{P}-p
which contains either
u_{i}
or
u_{i+1}
. Thus, the paths of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
which contain
v
are the paths which correspond to
N(u_{i})\cup N(u_{i+1})-p
. Otherwise, each path of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
which contains
v
corresponds to a path of
\mathcal{P}-p
which also contains
v
. Therefore, the paths of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
which contain
v
are the paths which correspond to
N_{G}(v)-p=N_{G}(v)
. Hence,

(N(u_{1})\cup N(u_{2})-P,N(u_{2})\cup N(u_{3})-P,\ldots,N(u_{k-1})\cup N(u_{k})-P,N(v_{k+1}),N(v_{k+2}),\ldots,N(v_{r}))

is a presentation of
M(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
. By Lemma
4.2
and Theorem
1.2
, this is also a presentation of
M(\mathcal{P})/p
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

By Lemma
2.2
,
M(\mathcal{P})\backslash p=M(\mathcal{P}-p)
, and, by Lemma

,
M(\mathcal{P})/p=M(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
.
