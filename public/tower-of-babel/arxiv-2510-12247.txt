Faster State Preparation with Randomization
Yue Wang, Xiao-Ming Zhang, Xiao Yuan, Qi Zhao

Abstract
Quantum state preparation remains a dominant cost in many quantum algorithms. We introduce a randomized protocol that fundamentally improves the accuracy-cost trade-off for states with hierarchical amplitude structures, where amplitudes decay exponentially or by a power law with exponent greater than one. Rather than commonly employed deterministically truncating small amplitudes, we prepare an ensemble of simple circuits: each retains all large amplitudes while amplifying a single small one. We rigorously prove that the randomized ensemble achieves a quadratic improvement in trace-distance error over the deterministic truncation method. This quadratic scaling halves the number of encoded amplitudes for exponential decay and yields polynomial reductions for power-law decay, which directly translates to a reduction in circuit depth. Demonstrations on molecular wavefunctions (LiH), many-body ground states (transverse-field Ising), and machine-learning parameters (ResNet) validate the approach across diverse applications. The method broadens the class of states preparable on near-term and fault-tolerant quantum devices.

Formally, the truncation method works as follows: we partition the coefficients into two subsets based on a cutoff threshold

A=\{i:\alpha_{i}\geq t\}\qquad\quad B=\{i:\alpha_{i}<t\}.

Therefore, set

If we discard set

\ket{\psi_A}=\sum_{i\in A}\alpha_{i}\ket{i}

\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{j\in B}|\alpha_{j}|^{2}}\right)

This error bound directly follows from the trace distance between pure states:
\|\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}-\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}\|_{1}=2\sqrt{1-|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^{2}}

The specific threshold for partitioning can vary depending on the task, provided the set

includes higher-order excitations.

We first derive a state-level mixing lemma that quantifies the error for mixtures of pure states (proof in

Lemma 1
.

Let
\ket{\psi}
be a target pure quantum state, and let
\{\ket{\psi_m}\}_{m}

\{p_{m}\}_{m}

\left\|\sum_{m}p_{m}\ket{\psi_m}-\ket{\psi}\right\|\leq b,

for some

\rho:=\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi},
and
\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}:=\sum_{m}p_{m}\ket{\psi_m}\bra{\psi_m}.

Then, the trace distance between the mixed and target states satisfies

\|\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}-\rho\|_{1}\leq a^{2}+2b.

(6)

Here

\ket{\psi}

with real amplitudes
\alpha_{j}

(7)

be the normalized state, where

\Gamma:=\|\psi_{m}\|=\sqrt{1-\epsilon^{2}+S^{2}}
.
Then

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle:=\max_{m\in B}\left\|\ket{\tilde\psi_m}-\ket{\psi}\right\|\leq(c+1)\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{2}),

\displaystyle b

Resource Savings.—
We now translate the quadratic error suppression into resource savings. Let the amplitudes be ordered by decreasing magnitude,
|\alpha|_{(1)}\geq|\alpha|_{(2)}\geq\cdots\geq|\alpha|_{(N)}
. Given a cutoff
t
, deterministic truncation yields error
\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon\right)
, whereas the randomized mixture attains
\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)
. Let
K
denote the number of kept amplitudes (i.e.,
K=|A|
) and let
\epsilon(K)
be the tail
\ell_{2}
norm after keeping the top-
K
terms. The required
K
for a given target accuracy follows by inverting
\epsilon(K)
under two decay models: (i) exponential decay:
\epsilon(K)=\Theta(r_{\text{exp}}^{K})
, where
r_{\text{exp}}<1
is the decay rate. To achieve error
\tau
, truncation requires
K_{\det}(\tau)=\Theta\big(\log(1/\tau)/\log(1/r_{\Exp})\big)
, while randomized requires
K_{\text{rand}}(\tau)=\Theta\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\log(1/\tau)/\log(1/r_{\Exp})\big)
, and (ii) power-law decay:
\epsilon(K)=\Theta\big(K^{\tfrac{1-2r_{\textup{pow}}}{2}}\big)
, where
r_{\textup{pow}}>1
is the decay exponent. Thus
K_{\det}(\tau)=\Theta\big(\tau^{-\tfrac{2}{2r_{\textup{pow}}-1}}\big)
and
K_{\text{rand}}(\tau)=\Theta\big(\tau^{-\tfrac{1}{2r_{\textup{pow}}-1}}\big)
. Consequently, the extra amplitudes a deterministic scheme must include beyond the randomized cutoff to match the same target error are

\frac{K_{\det}(\tau)}{K_{\text{rand}}(\tau)}=\begin{cases}2,&\text{geometric decay},\\
\Theta\big(\tau^{-\tfrac{1}{2r_{\text{pow}}-1}}\big),&\text{power-law decay}.\end{cases}

Since implementation cost is at least proportional to the number of prepared nonzero amplitudes, these
K
-scalings translate directly to resource savings. Hence the randomized protocol yields a constant-factor halving of prepared amplitudes under exponential decay, and a polynomial reduction by
\tau^{-\tfrac{1}{2r_{\text{pow}}-1}}
under power-law decay, directly translating to commensurate circuit-cost savings.

Quantum Chemistry States.—
The randomized state-preparation approach is particularly beneficial for quantum chemistry applications
Lanyon et al. 2010
;
McArdle et al. 2020
;
Romero et al. 2018
;
Helgaker et al. 2013
, notably in configuration interaction (CI) methods. In CI, the many-electron wavefunction is represented as a linear combination of Slater determinants, inherently exhibiting a hierarchical structure. Typically, the largest amplitudes correspond to the reference determinant (often the HF solution) and low-lying excitations, whereas amplitudes for higher-order excitations diminish rapidly due to large energy denominators.

This hierarchy aligns directly with our randomized method: set
A
comprises the large coefficients from the reference determinant and significant low-order excitations, whereas higher-order excitations fall into set
B
. The energy-based suppression of high-order excitations ensures that
\sum_{j\in B}|\alpha_{j}|^{2}
is small and decaying.

Preparing the full configuration interaction (FCI) state is computationally prohibitive due to the exponential growth in determinants with electron and orbital counts. Consequently, practical workflows employ truncated expansions (e.g., singles, doubles, or limited higher excitations). Such truncations integrate naturally with our randomized preparation, reducing quantum resources by avoiding explicit reconstruction of the entire FCI state in a single instance.

The many-electron wavefunction is expanded in a basis of Slater determinants,

\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm{CI}}}=\sum_{I}c_{I}\ket{\Phi_{I}},

where each
\ket{\Phi_{I}}
is a Slater determinant and
c_{I}
is its CI coefficient. The reference determinant is
\ket{\Phi_0}
. Excited determinants arise by promoting one or more electrons into unoccupied orbitals. In the supplementary material we show that higher-order determinants have suppressed coefficients, e.g.,
|c_{I}^{(1)}|\leq\|\hat{V}\|/\Delta E_{I0}
, where
\Delta E_{I0}=E_{I}^{(0)}-E_{0}^{(0)}
and
\hat{V}
is a perturbing operator.

We validate our framework on three problems: the ground state of LiH, the ground state of the transverse-field Ising model (TFIM), and a quantum state encoding parameters of a trained machine-learning model. These represent both physically structured states and high-dimensional data-driven states, demonstrating broad applicability.

For LiH, we place lithium at the origin and hydrogen
1.6
Å

Starting from atomic orbital integrals, we perform a restricted HF calculation to obtain canonical molecular orbitals, then construct the FCI Hamiltonian. Diagonalization on a classical computer produces the ground-state wavefunction,

where
\{\ket{\Phi_I}\}
are Slater determinants (configurations) labeled by occupied orbitals, and

We map the wavefunction to 12 qubits via Jordan–Wigner encoding and form the ensemble density matrix

Discussion.—
We have presented a randomized state-preparation approach that reduces circuit complexity while maintaining high trace norm error. The method leverages hierarchical amplitude structures common in practice, such as chemistry, condensed-matter systems, and machine learning. Our analysis establishes error bounds proportional to the square of the discarded tail weight, yielding a quadratic improvement over direct truncation. Numerical simulations corroborate these gains in both accuracy and resource utilization.

The core intuition behind our randomized method exploits the amplitude hierarchy inherent in realistic quantum states. Typically, the complexity of state preparation is directly tied to the number of nonzero amplitudes required for state description. However, many amplitudes are negligible in magnitude yet numerous; each contributes little on its own, but together they exert a meaningful collective influence. Randomized approach avoid preparing all tiny amplitudes in one run thus reduce the resource needed. Unlike traditional deterministic methods, which uniformly address all coefficients, our approach prioritizes amplitude subsets based on significance, achieving a practical balance between trace norm error and circuit complexity.

Moreover, amplifying small coefficients can reduce the quantum resources needed for state preparation because we can employ less accurate gate synthesis. In the fault-tolerant regime, a rotation by a very small angle must be decomposed into a sequence of Clifford and
T
gates
Mooney et al. 2021
;
Ross and Selinger 2016
, where
T
gates are widely regarded as an expensive resource
Gidney 2024
;
Itogawa et al. 2024
. Recent advances, such as magic-state cultivation
Gidney et al. 2024
, suggest that
T
gates might eventually be implemented with overhead comparable to CNOT gates; nevertheless, such techniques are not yet fully mature, and
T
gates remain more resource-intensive than simpler Clifford operations. Consequently, by amplifying the small-magnitude terms, one effectively raises the minimum amplitude in the resulting density matrix. This, in turn, reduces the number of
T
gates required for small-angle rotations, thereby offering a more resource-efficient pathway to quantum state preparation in fully fault-tolerant architectures. Such an idea has also been employed to reduce
T
-gate counts in Trotter-based Hamiltonian simulation
Granet and Dreyer 2024
.

Previous applications of randomization to state-synthesis error
Akibue et al. 2024
;
Akibue et al. 2022
differ from our work in scope and results, and thus barely overlap with our randomized state-preparation framework. Those works consider a semidefinite programming optimization problem to quadratically improve the worst-case approximation error by identifying a set of allowed quantum states that are synthesis-error-free in the fault-tolerant setting. Since gate-synthesis error commonly scales as
{\mathcal{\tilde{O}}}(1/\epsilon)
, this approach halves the compilation length. We, in contrast, consider the cost of encoding a classical vector description in a quantum state. Our cost savings depend on the decay pattern of the amplitudes: with exponential decay, we also halve the cost, but with power-law decay, we achieve a polynomial speedup in cost, greatly outperforming their result. Moreover, since their work and ours target separate sources of error in the state-preparation problem, the results can be combined to achieve greater cost savings.

Our framework is well suited to practical implementation. The states in the ensemble
\ket{\tilde{\psi}_m}
are very similar, differing only in which sign on the state from
B
and in the corresponding normalization factor. Thus, the state-preparation procedures for the states in the ensemble are highly similar, favoring practical implementation of the randomized scheme. In summary, our approach reduces error for truncated state preparation, making it promising for near-term and fault-tolerant devices. The rigorous guarantees ensure that prepared states closely approximate targets with controllable error, enabling more efficient implementations of algorithms that rely on complex state preparation.

Appendix A
State-Level Mixing Lemma

We first propose a state-mixing lemmas.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\ket{\psi}
be a target pure quantum state, and let
\{\ket{\psi_m}\}_{m}
be a family of pure states with associated probabilities
\{p_{m}\}_{m}
satisfying:

\|\ket{\psi_m}-\ket{\psi}\|\leq a\quad\text{for all }m,\qquad\left\|\sum_{m}p_{m}\ket{\psi_m}-\ket{\psi}\right\|\leq b,

(9)

for some constants
a,b>0
. Define the target and mixed density matrices as

\rho:=\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi},\qquad\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}:=\sum_{m}p_{m}\ket{\psi_m}\bra{\psi_m}.

(10)

Then, the trace norm between the mixed and target states satisfies

\|\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}-\rho\|_{1}\leq a^{2}+2b.

(11)

Proof.

Define the deviation vectors
\delta_{m}:=\ket{\psi_m}-\ket{\psi}
, which satisfy
\|\delta_{m}\|\leq a
. Let their average deviation be

\Delta:=\sum_{m}p_{m}\delta_{m}=\sum_{m}p_{m}(\ket{\psi_m}-\ket{\psi}),

(12)

which satisfies
\|\Delta\|\leq b
by assumption (
9
).

Expanding each projector:

\ket{\psi_m}\bra{\psi_m}=(\ket{\psi}+\delta_{m})(\bra{\psi}+\delta_{m}^{\dagger})=\rho+\ket{\psi}\bra{\delta_m}+\ket{\delta_m}\bra{\psi}+\ket{\delta_m}\bra{\delta_m}.

(13)

Taking the weighted sum and subtracting
\rho
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}-\rho

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}p_{m}\left(\ket{\psi}\bra{\delta_m}+\ket{\delta_m}\bra{\psi}+\ket{\delta_m}\bra{\delta_m}\right)

\displaystyle=\ket{\psi}\bra{\Delta}+\Delta\bra{\psi}+\sum_{m}p_{m}\ket{\delta_m}\bra{\delta_m}.

(14)

We now bound the trace norm of each term in Eq. (
14
).

Define the operator

X:=\ket{\psi}\bra{\Delta}+\Delta\bra{\psi}.

(15)

This is a Hermitian operator of rank at most 2. By the triangle inequality and the identity
\|\ket{u}\bra{v}\|_{1}=\|u\|\|v\|
, we get

\|X\|_{1}\leq\|\ket{\psi}\bra{\Delta}\|_{1}+\|\Delta\bra{\psi}\|_{1}=2\|\psi\|\cdot\|\Delta\|=2\|\Delta\|\leq 2b.

(16)

Each term
\ket{\delta_m}\bra{\delta_m}
has trace norm
\|\delta_{m}\|^{2}\leq a^{2}
. Thus,

\left\|\sum_{m}p_{m}\ket{\delta_m}\bra{\delta_m}\right\|_{1}=\sum_{m}p_{m}\|\delta_{m}\|^{2}\leq a^{2}\sum_{m}p_{m}=a^{2}.

(17)

Combining Eqs. (
16
) and (
17
) in Eq. (
14
), we obtain the desired bound:

\|\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}-\rho\|_{1}\leq\|X\|_{1}+\left\|\sum_{m}p_{m}\ket{\delta_m}\bra{\delta_m}\right\|_{1}\leq 2b+a^{2}.

(18)

This proves the lemma.
∎

This lemma provides a bound on how well a convex mixture of approximating pure states reproduces the original pure state at the level of density operators. Importantly, the bound depends only on the worst-case deviation
a
and the average deviation
b
. The probabilities
p_{m}
may be arbitrarily small—the proof depends only on their normalization
\sum_{m}p_{m}=1
. This lemma is particularly useful when designing randomized state preparation protocols where each sampled state is close to the target, but their average is even closer.

Definition 1

An

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\{i:|\alpha_{i}|\geq t\},

(Random mixing error bound)
.

Let

For every

and the unnormalized state

(22)

(23)

and consequently

\;\|\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}-\rho\|_{1}\leq a^{2}+2b=O(\epsilon^{2})

Proof.

Using
\ket{\psi_m}=\ket{\psi_A}+\alpha_{m}/p_{m}\ket{m}

\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi_A}+\alpha_{m}\ket{m}+\sum_{k\neq m}\alpha_{k}\ket{k}

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_m'}-\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{-1}\ket{\psi_m}-\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\left(\Gamma^{-1}-1\right)\ket{\psi_A}+\left(\tfrac{\alpha_{m}}{p_{m}\Gamma}-\alpha_{m}\right)\ket{m}-\sum_{k\neq m}\alpha_{k}\ket{k}.

(24)

By (

We first verify that the condition does not hold in general. Let

S=\|\bm{\alpha}\|_{1}\;\leq\;\sqrt{K}\,\|\bm{\alpha}\|_{2}\;=\;\sqrt{K}\,\epsilon.

Thus a

Below we explain when (

denote the decreasing rearrangement of

S=\sum_{j\in B}|\alpha_{j}|=\sum_{k\geq 1}|\alpha|_{(k)},\qquad\epsilon^{2}=\sum_{j\in B}\alpha_{j}^{2}=\sum_{k\geq 1}|\alpha|_{(k)}^{2}.

(29)

We now connect the explicit decay models to the
\ell_{1}
-smallness

(30)

Appendix C
Configuration Interaction and Implementation

Configuration interaction (CI) is a many-body method used to capture electron correlation beyond the mean-field Hartree–Fock (HF) approximation. In CI, the exact wavefunction
\ket{\Psi}
of a molecule is expressed as a linear combination of Slater determinants:

\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm{CI}}}=\sum_{I}c_{I}\ket{\Phi_I},

(34)

where
\ket{\Phi_I}
represents a determinant formed by occupying a subset of spin orbitals.

Each Slater determinant
\ket{\Phi_I}
corresponds to a configuration of electrons distributed over spin orbitals. In the full configuration interaction (FCI) method, all possible electron configurations within the basis set are included, yielding an exact solution within that basis. However, the number of such determinants scales exponentially with system size, making FCI feasible only for small molecules.

For illustration, we simulate LiH with 4 electrons in 12 spin orbitals (from 6 spatial orbitals using the STO-3G basis). The HF reference state
\ket{\Phi_0}
occupies the lowest-energy molecular orbitals. All singly and multiply excited determinants
\ket{\Phi_I}
are constructed by promoting electrons into virtual orbitals.

The molecular Hamiltonian is expressed in second quantization as

\hat{H}=\sum_{p,q}h_{pq}a_{p}^{\dagger}a_{q}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p,q,r,s}\langle pq|rs\rangle a_{p}^{\dagger}a_{q}^{\dagger}a_{s}a_{r},

(35)

where
h_{pq}
and
\langle pq|rs\rangle
are one- and two-electron integrals.

Matrix elements
H_{IJ}=\bra{\Phi_I}\hat{H}\ket{\Phi_J}
define the CI Hamiltonian. Diagonalizing it yields coefficients
{c_{I}}
and the energy eigenvalues.

To prepare
\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm{CI}}}
on a quantum computer, we encode each determinant
\ket{\Phi_I}
as a computational basis state
\ket{x_I}
. The full wavefunction is then

\ket{\Psi_{{CI}}}=\sum_{I=1}^{\dim}c_{I}\ket{x_I}.

(36)

We define a threshold
t
and split coefficients:

A={I:|c_{I}|\geq t},\qquad B={I:|c_{I}|<t}.

(37)

Partition
B=\bigcup_{m}B_{m}
and define

\ket{\psi_A}=\sum_{I\in A}c_{I}\ket{x_I},\qquad\ket{\psi_m}=\sum_{I\in B_{m}}c_{I}\ket{x_I}.

(38)

Prepare the normalized ensemble

\ket{\tilde{\psi}_m}=\Gamma_{m}\left(\ket{\psi_A}+\frac{1}{p_{m}}\ket{\psi_m}\right)

(39)

and form the mixture:

\rho=\sum_{m}p_{m}\ket{\tilde{\psi}_m}\bra{\tilde{\psi}_m}.

(40)

This randomized method preserves fidelity while reducing state preparation complexity.

For the coefficient hierarchy of the CI coefficients, we can show the higher-order Slater determinants have suppressed coefficients via Rayleigh–Schrödinger perturbation theory. Suppose the molecular Hamiltonian can be written as

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{V},

where
\hat{H}_{0}
is is the Fock operator, whose ground‐state eigenfunction
\ket{\Phi_0}
is the HF determinant, and whose excited eigenfunctions
\ket{\Phi_I}
correspond to orbital excitations.
\hat{V}
is the difference between the full molecular Hamiltonian and
\hat{H}_{0}
. Practically,
\hat{V}
typically includes the parts of the electron-electron repulsion that go beyond the mean‐field approximation in
\hat{H}_{0}
.

Denote by
E_{0}^{(0)}
the unperturbed ground-state energy of
\hat{H}_{0}
, and let
E_{I}^{(0)}
be the energy of an excited configuration
\ket{\Phi_I}
under
\hat{H}_{0}
. The exact eigenstate
\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm{CI}}}
can be expanded as:

\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm{CI}}}=c_{0}\ket{\Phi_0}+\sum_{I\neq 0}c_{I}\ket{\Phi_I}.

In first-order perturbation theory, one has

c_{I}^{(1)}=-\frac{\langle\Phi_{I}|\hat{V}|\Phi_{0}\rangle}{E_{I}^{(0)}-E_{0}^{(0)}}=-\frac{V_{I0}}{\Delta E_{I0}},

where
\Delta E_{I0}=E_{I}^{(0)}-E_{0}^{(0)}
is the unperturbed energy gap. Furthermore, if
\|\hat{V}\|
is the operator norm of the perturbation, then

|V_{I0}|=\bigl|\langle\Phi_{I}|\hat{V}|\Phi_{0}\rangle\bigr|\leq\|\hat{V}\|.

Hence, any coefficient
c_{I}^{(1)}
for an excited determinant satisfies

|c_{I}^{(1)}|\leq\frac{\|\hat{V}\|}{\Delta E_{I0}}.

Coefficient distribution of the CI state for LiH molecule.

Even in higher-order perturbation theory or in full CI solutions, large denominator gaps
\Delta E_{I0}
strongly suppress amplitudes for high-lying excited determinants. We can see through Fig.
2
that the majority of coefficients have small magnitudes, while there are in total 69 coefficients in the FCI state of LiH molecule, only 2 of them are greater than 0.1 and only 9 of them are greater than 0.01.

In what follows, we let
n
be the number of spin orbitals, and we index Slater determinants by
I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
to indicate which orbitals are occupied. We denote
c_{I}
as the CI coefficient of determinant
\ket{\Phi_I}
, and
\gamma=\sum_{I\in B}\lvert c_{I}\rvert^{2}
. we have

\gamma\leq C\biggl(\frac{\|\hat{V}\|}{\Delta_{\rm min}}\biggr)^{2},

(41)

where
\Delta_{\min}=\min_{I\neq 0}\bigl(E_{I}^{(0)}-E_{0}^{(0)}\bigr)>0
and
C
absorbs constants from second-order (or higher) perturbation expansions. The factor
C
encapsulates the higher-order terms of Rayleigh–Schrödinger perturbation expansions. More precisely second-order (and higher) corrections introduce additional sums over intermediate excitations, each contributing denominators of the form
\Delta E_{I0}\pm\Delta E_{J0}
in the denominator and factors like
\langle\Phi_{I}|\hat{V}|\Phi_{J}\rangle
in the numerator. Bounding these systematically leads to combinatorial and system-dependent prefactors, which are collectively denoted by
C
.
