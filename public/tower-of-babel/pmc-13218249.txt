Medical dressing inspired by dandelion “smashed for external application” therapy for diabetic wound care
Authors: Shenyu Tan, Xi Luo, Shujia Yao, Xiaoyou Yang, Yalan Zhou, Fangwen Jiao, Hongzhi Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Developing bioactive dressings to remodel pathological microenvironments is critical for diabetic foot ulcer management. Drawing inspiration from the traditional "smashed for external application” of Taraxacum mongolicum Hand.-Mazz., a biomimetic film (TH-EVLPs@CNF−) is engineered by integrating extracellular vesicle-like particles (TH-EVLPs) from fresh dandelion juice with carboxylated cellulose nanofibrils (TH-CNF-) derived from plant residues. The TH-CNF- matrix exhibits exceptional biocompatibility and multifunctionality: enhancing cell proliferation, dynamically reprogramming macrophage polarization from pro-inflammatory M1 to reparative M2 phenotypes, and promoting angiogenesis via VEGF signaling. Leveraging botanical homology, the anti-inflammatory TH-EVLPs are highly loaded in the CNF− films (109 particles/cm2) with on-demand release, synergistically accelerating diabetic wound closure by 96% within 14 days (vs 56% in untreated controls) through collagen reorganization and vascular regeneration. This work reveals the concept of integration of medicinal and adjuvant components embodied in the traditional application of dandelion, and meanwhile provides insights for the integrated forming process of medical dressings based on homologous bioactive substances.

Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) are among the most common and recognized complications of diabetes [1]. Recent statistics indicate that approximately 18.6 million individuals worldwide are affected by DFUs annually, with a five-year mortality rate of 30% and over 70% for those requiring above-foot amputations, becoming a serious public health problem [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. Chronic hyperglycemia in diabetic patients leads to neuropathy and peripheral arterial disease, particularly in the lower limbs [6]. Once developing a wound, recurrent infections, persistent inflammation, and deep tissue necrosis typically ensue, ultimately contributing to the progression of DFUs [6]. At present, clinical management of DFUs primarily involves debridement and off-loading, but the therapeutic efficacy of these approaches remains limited [7,8]. Medical dressings, commonly employed for wound care, serve to absorb exudates, prevent inflammation and further injury, and promote tissue regeneration and epithelial migration [[9], [10], [11]]. To date, there is no specialized wound dressing designed for the treatment of DFU lesions. The currently available dressings are mainly divided into conventional basic dressings (e.g., gauze) and modern moist wound dressings (e.g., chitosan dressings, alginate dressings) [12]. However, conventional dressings tend to destroy growth factors or adhere to newly formed granulation tissue during dressing changes, resulting in secondary wound injury [13]. Silver-containing and moisturizing dressings have limited efficacy in promoting ulcer healing, while hydrophilic dressings are associated with a relatively high cost (a median price of RMB 66.3 per piece, equivalent to approximately USD 9.54) [14]. In general, the existing dressings are still far from meeting the clinical needs of patients in terms of skin compatibility, therapeutic efficacy and safety, ideal wound dressings are always in urgent clinical demand.

Accumulating evidence has established that the progression of DFUs is closely associated with macrophage polarization. M1 macrophages, the pro-inflammatory phenotype, play a crucial role in defending against exogenous pathogens and removing necrotic tissue during the initial stages of wound formation [15]. In contrast, M2 macrophages, the anti-inflammatory phenotype, facilitate tissue repair and wound healing [16,17]. Typically, macrophages transition from the M1 to the M2 phenotype during the proliferative phase of wound healing [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. However, in diabetic patients, chronic hyperglycemia leads to elevated levels of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), reactive oxygen species (ROS), and reactive nitrogen species (RNS), which hinder the typical polarization of macrophages [[21], [22], [23]]. Additionally, diabetic individuals often experience vasculopathy and impaired angiogenesis, further contributing to sustained inflammation and delayed wound healing. Consequently, regulating macrophage polarization and enhancing blood supply to the wound site represent promising therapeutic strategies for the treatment of DFUs [[24], [25], [26]].

Dandelion (Taraxacum mongolicum Hand.-Mazz., TH) is a traditional herbal medicine, with its fresh juice commonly used in clinical practice for the external treatment of various types of carbuncles. Previous studies have shown that fresh TH juice contains a substantial quantity of extracellular vesicle-like particles (TH-EVLPs), which exhibit extremely strong anti-inflammatory activity by inhibiting the NLRP3 inflammasome, NF-κB and MAPK pathways [27]. However, these TH-EVLPs do not directly promote cell proliferation or migration. Moreover, the development of customized dressings is essential to address the challenges associated with the loading, wound retention, and controlled release of TH-EVLPs during wound treatment.

Inspired by the usage of “smashed for external application” of fresh TH, plant fibers can be simply physically or chemically modified to produce different plant nanocellulose with exceptional mechanical properties, biodegradability, biocompatibility, breathability and hygroscopicity. Nanocellulose is typically classified into two major categories based on the preparation techniques: cellulose nanocrystal (CNC) and cellulose nanofibril (CNF) [28]. The unique three-dimensional structure and bonding capacity of nanocellulose enable the encapsulation, retention and controlled release of various therapeutic agents, positioning it as highly promising candidate for natural wound dressing. In this study, a biomimetic external film, TH-EVLPs@CNF−, was prepared by incorporating TH-EVLPs from TH juice and modified nanocellulose derived from the TH dregs (Fig. 1). The physicochemical properties of various TH-derived nanocellulose and their suitability for diabetic chronic wounds were investigated. The assembly and release mechanisms of TH-EVLPs were also elucidated. Furthermore, the ability to induce macrophage polarization and promote angiogenesis of TH-derived negatively charged CNF (TH-CNF-) was demonstrated, which accelerated wound healing in diabetic mouse wound model in synergy with TH-EVLPs. In conclusion, this study provides scientific evidence for the traditional external use of fresh crushed TH. It also proposes a biomimetic strategy to design wound dressings with synergistic multicomponent and multiconfiguration effects.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of biomimetic construction of cellulose-based films containing dandelion self-derived EVLPs (TH-EVLPs@CNF−) inspired by the traditional use of fresh herbs for diabetic wound care.

Materials and methods

Materials

Fresh TH was purchased from Xuzhou, Jiangsu, and identified by Professor Yang Hu from the Department of Chinese Medicine Resources, Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine. Sodium hydroxide was provided from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd, sodium bromide was obtained from Energy Chemical, 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine oxide were provided from Maya Reagent, hydrochloric acid, sodium hypochlorite, concentrated sulfuric acid were provided from Sinopharm, 2,3-epoxypropyltrimethylammonium chloride were provided from Beijing Innochem Technology, RPMI 1640 medium and fetal bovine serum FBS were provided from Gibco. All cytokine Precoated ELISA Kits were purchased from Shanghai Dakewei Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Ultrapure water (>18 MΩ) was used throughout this study, and other reagents are the highest purity grade reagents commercially available. L929 and HUVECs cells were both obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). iBMDM cells were provided by Prof. Feng Shao from National Institute of Biological Sciences (Beijing, China). All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (No. 202311A004) and were carried out in accordance with the Guidelines of the Animal Care and Use Committee of the establishment.

Extraction of TH-ELVPs and TH cellulose

Fresh TH was washed with ultrapure water for 2-3 times to remove surface dirt, and squeezed using an Omega juicer CNC82R-C (67 rpm). The obtained juice was sequentially centrifuged at 4000 g for 60 min and 10,000 g for 60 min to remove large particles and fibres. The supernatant was passed through 100 kDa and 3 kDa ultrafiltration membranes (Millipore, USA) in turn, and TH-EVLPs present in the final trapped fluid was collected. [29]. Particle diameter and concentration of TH-EVLPs were measured by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) (Nanosight NS300, Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK). Briefly, TH-EVLPs were diluted to 108-109 particles mL−1 in PBS, and 3 videos of 60 s were acquired.

TH residue was washed with a gentle stirring at 80 °C for 2 h and filtered out to remove excess impurities such as pectin and lignin [30]. Then, 30 g washed residue was further purified by alkali treatment and bleaching with 500 mL NaOH solution (4 wt%), 40 mL acetic anhydride, 500 mL NaClO solution (12 wt%) and 500 mL water. TH cellulose can be decolorized in 2 h at 90 °C, then washed with ultrapure water. Finally, the TH cellulose was suspended in deionized water and nanofibrillated by high-pressure homogenization (10,000 rpm, 15 min) to generate a 1 wt% TH-CNF aqueous slurry, which was then freeze-dried.

Preparation of different types of TH nanocellulose variants

Anionic cellulose (TH-CNF-), cationic cellulose (TH-CNF+) and cellulose nanocrystals (TH-CNC) were prepared by using the method described in literature. Briefly, 2,2,6,6-Tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl (TEMPO) (50 mg, 0.32 mmol) and NaBr (500 mg, 4.86 mmol) were fully dissolved in 50 mL ultrapure water and then slowly added to a 50 mL 1 wt% TH-CNF suspension under stirring at room temperature. Simultaneously, 15 mL of 12 wt% NaClO solution was added for the oxidation reaction, with 0.5 M NaOH solution used to maintain the system pH around 10. Once the pH ceased to decline, 1 mL anhydrous ethanol was added to end the reaction, and the pH was adjusted to 7 using 0.5 M HCl. Finally, the obtained TH-CNF- was washed thoroughly with ultrapure water more than five times [31,32]. About TH-CNF+, 5 g TH-CNF was added into 225 mL isopropanol solution and stirred at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, 2.5 mL 10 M NaOH solution was further added and stirred for 30 min before adding 17.8 mL EPTMAC. Ultrapure water was added to make the total solvent volume 250 mL. The mixture was stirred at 60 °C for 3 h, neutralized with glacial acetic acid, and then washed with anhydrous ethanol, filtered, and freeze-dried to obtain TH-CNF+ [33,34]. TH-CNC was prepared using the 64% sulfuric acid hydrolysis method [35]. Bleached TH cellulose was added into 64% H2SO4 aqueous solution under continuous mechanical stirring. The above suspensions were subjected to acid hydrolysis at 45 °C for 45 min, and the hydrolysis process was finished by dilution with ten-fold deionized water, followed by centrifugation (7500 rpm, 20 min). Repeat the dilution/centrifugation until the final pH of the filtrate was about 7. The TH-CNC suspension was subsequently diluted to 1 wt% using deionized water and sonicated for 15 min.

Microscopic morphology and zeta potential of nanocellulose

10 μL of 0.01 wt% nanocellulose suspension was prepared on a copper grid and observed with a Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEOL JEM-2100Plus, Japan) to analyze the size and morphology of different nanocellulose samples. The dimensions of the nanocellulose in the TEM images were measured with Image J1.52i software. The zeta potential of 1 wt% nanocellulose suspension was measured using a dynamic light scattering instrument (DLS, Zetasizer Nano-ZS90, Malvern, UK), at 25 °C with a scattering angle of 90°. The structural changes of nanocellulose were detected by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific K-Alpha spectrometer, USA).

Preparation and characterization of nanocellulose films

Nanocellulose films were prepared from 1 wt% nanocellulose suspension, which was cast on polystyrene petri dishes (ø 10 cm) and dried in a drying oven at 60 °C overnight. The surface functional groups of nanocellulose films were analyzed using a Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS20, USA). Surface and cross-sectional microstructures of nanocellulose films were examined under scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (ZEISS Sigma 300, Germany). Surface roughness was tested by atomic force microscopy (AFM) (Bruker Dimension Icon, Germany). The water contact angle (WCA) of nanocellulose films was measured by using a contact angle/surface tension measuring instrument (Dataphysics OCA20, Germany). The tensile mechanical properties of nanocellulose films were tested by using an electronic universal testing machine (CMT6103, MTS Industrial Systems) under constant temperature and pressure conditions.

Adhesion assay

6 mm full-thickness skin wounds were constructed on the backs of mice. In the Dye group, 20 μL dye was applied directly to the wound. In the nanocellulose film groups, a film was placed on the wound followed by 20 μL dye application. The mice were then inverted, and the wounds were rinsed with 200 μL PBS. The retention of the nanocellulose films on the wound surface after rinsing was used to evaluate their adhesion.

Porosity testing

The porosities of TH nanocellulose films were measured using the liquid penetration method, also known as the liquid displacement method [36]. Anhydrous ethanol was added into a vacuumed pycnometer and the initial weight G1(g) was accurately measured using an electronic balance. The nanocellulose, weighing G0 (g), was then placed into the empty pycnometer, vacuumized, and filled with anhydrous ethanol to ensure the ethanol completely filled the films’ pores. The pycnometer was weighed again, recording the weight as G2 (g). After removing the fully ethanol-saturated film, the remaining ethanol and the pycnometer were weighed together, recording the weight as G3 (g). The P (%) of the film was then calculated using the following formula: P (%) = (G2-G3-G0)/(G1-G3)×100%.

Hygroscopicity testing

The weight of the 1 × 1 cm2 dried nanocellulose films was measured before (G1) and after (G2) soaking in distilled water overnight. The water absorption per gram of the film G (g) was calculated using the following formula: G = (G2-G1)/G2.

Cell adhesion and proliferation

Nanocellulose suspensions were coated on 6-well or 96-well polystyrene petri dishes by covering the bottom completely with 5 wt% nanocellulose suspension, and dried at 60 °C overnight. Coatings were soaked in 75% ethanol and then air-dried under UV irradiation before the experiment. The planar wells were prewet with PM1640, followed by seeding L929, HUVECs, and iBMDM cells at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well, and the cell adhesion after 12 h and proliferation after 24, 48, and 72 h were detected using a CCK-8 reagent. Additionally, live/dead cell staining and inverted fluorescence microscope in 488/594 nm channels were used to further detect the cell adhesion and proliferation.

Macrophage polarization and anti-inflammatory ability of CNF coatings

The iBMDM cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and cultured overnight. For M1 or M2-type controls, 1 μg mL−1 LPS or 40 ng mL−1 IL-4/IL-13 was used to induce the cells for 24 h. In the test group, cells were inoculated into 6-well plates pre-coated with TH-CNF+ or TH-CNF-. After culturing for 24 h, the cells were stimulated with or without 1 μg mL−1 LPS for another 24 h. Subsequently, cell RNA and culture supernatant were collected for RT-PCR and ELISA assays to detect the relative mRNA and protein expressions of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, Arg-1, Ym-1, IL-10, TGF-β and VEGF.

Transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from control and TH-CNF--treated macrophages, and RNA quality was strictly evaluated by NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis. Qualified RNA samples were used for cDNA library construction and paired-end transcriptome sequencing (150 bp) on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. Raw sequencing reads were filtered to obtain high-quality clean reads. Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome (GRCm39) using Hisat2 software, and transcript assembly was performed with StringTie. Gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) using featureCounts. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2, with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined as |log2FoldChange| > 1 and adjusted p-value <0.05. Functional annotation of DEGs was performed via Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses using clusterProfiler.

Angiogenesis assay

Tube formation assay was performed according to a published protocol [37]. Firstly, 200 μL Matrix-Gel (Mogengel Biotech, China) was coated onto each well of a 24-well plate. Then, the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Next, 1 × 105 starved HUVECs were seeded into each well of the plate, and the medium was replaced with RPMI 1640 supplemented with TH-CNF- extract or TH-EVLPs (109 particles·mL−1) for 8 h. Live/dead cell staining was performed, and images were taken with an inverted fluorescence microscope at 488/594 nm. Total branch points and tube lengths were quantitatively analyzed using Image J1.52i software.

HUVECs were seeded in 96-well and 12-well plates and cultured overnight. TH-CNF- suspension, prepared at the same concentration as the plate pretreatment group, was added to both plates and incubated for 24, 48 or 72 h. After incubation, cell viability in 96-well plates was measured via MTT assay; cell pellets from 12-well plates were harvested for oxidative stress and apoptosis analysis. Intracellular ROS levels were detected by flow cytometry using the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), and cell apoptosis was analyzed via flow cytometry with an Annexin V-FITC/Propidium Iodide (PI) double staining kit. Meanwhile, cell culture supernatants were harvested to determine the release of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) using a commercial LDH cytotoxicity detection kit.

Cell scratch experiment

L929 cells were seeded onto 6-well plates coated with TH-CNF+ and TH-CNF- at a density of 1 × 106 cells/well and cultured to 80-90% confluence. The tip of a 20 μL pipette was used to scratch the cell monolayer, which was then washed with PBS [21]. Live/dead cell staining was performed, and scratch wound areas were observed and photographed by an inverted fluorescence microscope at 488/594 nm at 0 h (A0) and 24 h (A24). The scratch healing was quantified using the following equation: scratch healing (%) = (A0-A24)/A0×100%.

Three-dimensional cell scanning

iBMDM cells (1 × 105 cells/well) were seeded into TH-CNF+ or TH-CNF- coated confocal dishes. After culturing for 24 h at 37 °C, live/dead cell staining was performed and laser scanning confocal microscope (TCS SP8, Leica, Germany) was used in Z-stack mode to collect the images and videos. Subsequently, three-dimensional image reconstruction was carried out using Leica's LAS X analysis software.

Fluorescence spectroscopy

Quantitative analysis of FITC-labeled TH-EVLPs captured or released by the films were performed using a multimode microplate reader (EnVision, PerkinElmer, USA) [32]. FITC-labeled TH-EVLPs were diluted in different solution media (deionized water, 10 mM pH 7.4 P BS, or 150 mM sodium chloride solution) respectively to a final concentration of 5 × 1010 particles·mL−1. Subsequently, a 0.25 cm2 film was immersed in the solution for 12 h. The loading capacity of FITC-labeled TH-EVLPs was determined by calculating the fluorescence intensity difference between pre-immersion and post-film removal measurements. The FITC-labeled TH-EVLPs-loaded films were immersed in 10 mM PBS solutions (pH 4.0-9.0) and incubated under isothermal oscillation (32 °C, 50 rpm). Fluorescence intensity in the buffer was measured at predetermined intervals (0, 1, 2, 6, 12, and 48 h), and cumulative release profiles were plotted based on 48-h release data.

Release kinetics

Release data were fitted with four commonly used dynamic models, including zero-order elimination kinetics (Mt/M∞ = k), first-order elimination kinetics (Mt/M∞ = 1-ekt), Higuchi equation (Mt/M∞ = kt1/2), and Ritger-Peppas equation (Mt/M∞ = ktn) [38,39]. In the above formulas, Mt is the cumulative release amount at time t; M∞ is the cumulative release amount at equilibrium; k is the release rate constant; the release exponent n is a characteristic parameter representing the release mechanism; t is the release time.

Construction and treatment of diabetic wound models

Full-thickness skin wounds (6 mm) were constructed on the backs of db/db mice using a skin needle biopsy punch [40,41]. Then, they were randomly divided into five groups (n = 4). Saline, TH-EVLPs solution, TH-CNF+ film, TH-CNF- film, and TH-EVLPs@CNF− film were applied to the wound site every three days. After administration, the wounds were covered with sterile dressings. On the 3rd, 7th, and 14th day, the wounds were photographed and Image J1.52i software was used to analyze the wound area. The wound healing rate (%) was quantified using the following equation: (A0-At)/A0×100%, where At is the wound area at a certain day, and A0 represents the wound area on Day 0.

Histopathological analysis

The regenerated skin samples were excised and collected on the 7th and 14th days. Briefly, 8 μm sections were prepared for H&E and Masson trichrome staining. Meanwhile, skin wound specimens collected on the 14th day postsurgery for immunofluorescence staining of F4/80, CD86, CD206, IL-6, Arg-1 and CD31, thereby evaluating macrophage polarization and wound angiogenesis. All primary antibodies used in this study were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK), with dilution ratios set as follows: F4/80 (1:100), CD86 (1:100), CD206 (1:200), IL-6 (1:50), Arg-1 (1:100), and CD31 (1:100). All slices were photo-captured and analyzed by microscope (DMi8, Leica, Germany).

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as the mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.3.0 software. An unpaired Student's t-test (two-tailed) was applied for statistical analyses between two groups, and one-way ANOVA for multiple groups.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of TH-derived nanocellulose

In this study, four different TH-derived nanocellulose variants were prepared through different processing routes (Fig. 2A). As illustrated in Fig. 2B, TH-CNF was obtained by only mechanical treatment of TH cellulose, yielding an interconnected fibrillar network with diameters of 5-10 nm and lengths spanning 400-1000 nm. Subsequent cationic modification of TH-CNF with hydroxypropyltrimethylammonium (HPTMA) generated TH-CNF+, which retained comparable morphological characteristics to the unmodified counterpart but exhibited increased fibrillar aggregation. In contrast, TEMPO-mediated oxidation of TH-CNF produced TH-CNF-, characterized by shortened fiber lengths (100-400 nm) and a more uniform, well-defined network architecture. Acid hydrolysis of TH cellulose yielded TH-CNC, displaying characteristic rod-like morphology with dimensions of 5-10 nm in diameter and 20-60 nm in length. Surface charge analysis via zeta potential measurements provided further validation of successful synthesis: TH-CNF (−11.33 ± 1.01 mV), TH-CNF+ (+7.66 ± 0.52 mV), TH-CNF- (−18.53 ± 0.81 mV), and TH-CNC (−43.47 ± 2.20 mV) (Fig. S1). To elucidate the structural characteristics of four TH-derived nanocellulose variants, FTIR analysis was systematically conducted (Fig. S2). Spectral analysis identified characteristic cellulose absorption bands common to all samples, including: the broad hydroxyl stretching vibration at 3285 cm−1 (O-H), symmetric C-H stretching at 2902 cm−1 (CH2), pyranose ring C-O-C vibrations at 1000 cm−1, CH2 scissoring deformation at 1410 cm−1, and β-glycosidic linkage confirmation at 896 cm−1 [42,43]. Notably, TH-CNF+ and TH-CNF- exhibited distinct spectral modifications compared to untreated cellulose. The characteristic hemicellulose C=O stretching vibration at 1730 cm−1 demonstrated marked attenuation, consistent with alkaline hydrolysis-mediated hemicellulose degradation. TH-CNF+ displayed enhanced absorption intensity at 1200 cm−1 corresponding to C-N stretching vibrations, while TH-CNF- showed pronounced carboxylate group signatures at 1608 cm−1 (-COONa asymmetric stretching), confirming successful carboxymethylation. In contrast, TH-CNC spectra revealed intensified sulfonic acid group vibrations with characteristic S-O (1060 cm−1) and S=O (1110 cm−1) stretching modes. These features indicate C6 position sulfonation through sulfuric acid-mediated esterification during hydrolysis. The collective spectroscopic evidence conclusively confirmed the successful synthesis of four distinct nanocellulose derivatives through chemical modifications. Similarly, XPS further confirmed the aforementioned structural changes (Fig. 2C and S3). The C 1s XPS spectrum of TH-CNF- was deconvoluted into three characteristic peaks: C-C (284.8 eV), C-O (286.5 eV), and O-C=O (288.1 eV). The presence of the O-C=O component is a direct result of the introduction of carboxyl (-COOH) groups onto the cellulose chains during TEMPO-mediated oxidation. This finding is further supported by the O 1s spectrum, which displays peaks corresponding to C=O (530.6 eV) and C-O/OH (532.2 eV) (Fig. 2C). The successful formation of the cationic structure in TH-CNF+ was confirmed by the characteristic signals of quaternary ammonium nitrogen (N 1s at 402.1 eV) and chloride ions (Cl 2p), a weak S 2p signal observed at approximately 168 eV for TH-CNC is attributed to the sulfonate groups (-OSO3-) introduced during its preparation via sulfuric acid hydrolysis (Fig. S3).

Next, the film-forming capabilities of the four TH-derived nanocellulose variants were systematically evaluated. The dried films of TH-CNF, TH-CNF+, TH-CNF- and TH-CNC were shown in Fig. 2D. Cross-sectional and surface morphological analyses via SEM revealed distinct structural variations among the samples. Unmodified TH-CNF films exhibited a rough surface topography and loosely organized lamellar architecture in cross-sectional views (Fig. 2D). Chemical functionalization significantly altered the structural organization: TH-CNF+ films displayed a consolidated surface morphology with reduced roughness, accompanied by densely packed nanolayers interspersed with nano-scale interlayer voids. In contrast, TH-CNF- films showed increased surface porosity and a disordered lamellar arrangement in cross-sectional profiles. TH-CNC films demonstrated superior structural integrity, featuring a smooth surface and a homogeneous, compact layered structure with minimal interlayer spacing. Quantitative porosity analysis (Fig. S4) corroborated the SEM observations, yielding the following hierarchy: TH-CNF > TH-CNF- > TH-CNC > TH-CNF+. In general, higher porosity predicts greater drug loading capacity [44].

The interfacial nanotopographical profile, particularly surface roughness, serves as a critical determinant for cellular adhesion efficacy and subsequent proliferation. Natural extracellular matrices (ECM) exhibit optimized biological functionality through their characteristic micro-nanoscale hierarchical roughness [45,46]. Excessive surface roughness (submicron to micron scale) inhibits cell growth, while insufficient surface roughness (below the nanometer scale) affects cell adhesion. AFM images showed that the average surface roughness of different nanocellulose films ranged from 30 to 150 nm (Fig. 2E and S5), indicating suitable for cell adhesion and growth. The adhesive ability of nanocellulose has been verified in a mouse wound model (Fig. S6).

Next, water absorption capacity and WCA were measured. Fig. 3A and B showed that in the modified nanocelluloses, only TH-CNF+ had a decrease in water absorption capacity, while TH-CNF- has a 4-fold increase. In addition, the WCA values of all nanocellulose films were less than 90° (Fig. 3C), indicating good surface wettability. The TH-CNF+ film exhibited the highest WCA (69.7°), contrasting with TH-CNF- ‘s minimal value (20°). This phenomenon can be attributed to functional group variations. TH-CNF-‘s surface, enriched with hydrophilic -COO- and -OH groups, facilitates hydrogen bonding with water molecules [47], thereby enhancing hydration and reducing contact angles. Conversely, TH-CNF+ incorporates hydrophobic methyl (-CH3) groups alongside hydroxyl moieties. The non-polar -CH3 groups impede hydrogen bond formation, increasing surface hydrophobicity and elevating WCA values. The mechanical properties of nanocellulose films were systematically characterized through tensile testing (Fig. 3D–F). TH-CNF films demonstrated superior elastic modulus (118.58 ± 5.01 MPa) but minimal elongation at break (5.52 ± 0.06%), indicative of a rigid and brittle material profile. This mechanical behavior aligns with the characteristic interplay between modulus (stiffness) and elongation (toughness) in semicrystalline biopolymers [48], where TH-CNF's densely cross-linked hydrogen bonding network and highly ordered fiber architecture account for its exceptional stiffness. Chemical modifications significantly altered mechanical performance. Acid/alkali-treated derivatives exhibited reduced elastic moduli: TH-CNF+ (78.90 ± 0.05 MPa), TH-CNF- (102.15 ± 9.61 MPa), and TH-CNC (80.33 ± 6.62 MPa) (Fig. 3E). This reduction primarily stems from (1) cellulose chain scission during hydrolysis, decreasing molecular weight, and (2) partial dehydroxylation disrupting inter-fibrillar hydrogen bonding [49,50]. Conversely, modified films displayed enhanced ductility, with elongation at break increasing to 7.61 ± 0.38% (TH-CNF+), 7.76 ± 0.23% (TH-CNF-), and 10.75 ± 0.24% (TH-CNC) (Fig. 3F). The inverse correlation between stiffness and ductility confirms that chemical processing modulates nanocellulose films' mechanical behavior by strategically balancing crystalline domains and amorphous regions.

Effects of TH-derived nanocellulose films on cell adhesion and colonization

Cellular adhesion, a critical prerequisite for proliferation and differentiation in adherent mammalian cells, serves as a key indicator of biomaterial biocompatibility and colonization potential [51]. To assess this property, we functionalized 96-well plates with four nanocellulose coatings and evaluated their interactions with iBMDM, L929, and HUVECs. CCK-8 assays revealed attenuated cellular adhesion on TH-CNF and TH-CNC coatings relative to untreated surfaces (Fig. 4A–C). In contrast, TH-CNF+ and TH-CNF- films demonstrated superior adhesion performance, with TH-CNF+ exhibiting enhanced efficiency for iBMDM and L929 adhesion (80% vs. 70% for TH-CNF-), likely mediated by cationic surface charge facilitating electrostatic interactions with anionic cell membranes [52]. Quantitative viability assessment via live/dead staining corroborated these findings (Fig. 4D). Notably, TH-CNF+ and TH-CNF- coatings displayed enhanced resistance to enzymatic detachment, retaining >95% adherent cells after 10-min trypsinization versus complete suspension in standard cultureware (Fig. 4E). Confocal imaging revealed extensive cellular infiltration and adhesion within the nanocellulose matrix (Fig. 4F), suggesting the 3D porous architecture provides biomimetic topological cues for cell anchoring [29,53]. Effects of TH-derived nanocellulose films on cell proliferation and angiogenesis

The proliferation dynamics of iBMDM, L929, and HUVECs on nanocellulose films revealed material-dependent biological responses. While TH-CNF and TH-CNC coatings suppressed proliferation in both iBMDM (Fig. 5A–C) and L929 cells (Fig. 5D–F), TH-CNF- and TH-CNF+ films exhibited pro-proliferative effects, with TH-CNF- demonstrating the strongest enhancement. Strikingly, iBMDM cultured on TH-CNF- coatings adopted an elongated spindle morphology under fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 5C), a hallmark of M2 macrophage polarization. This morphological shift correlates with upregulated expression of M2 markers (arginase-1, CD206, YM-1) as established in prior mechanotransduction studies [54]. Angiogenesis is a dynamic biological process encompassing endothelial proliferation, differentiation and structural remodeling. Moderate cellular stress [55,56], physiological apoptosis [57] and metabolic reprogramming [58,59] are inherent physiological characteristics during endothelial tube formation. Thus, the observed proliferation changes cannot be clearly distinguished between TH-CNF--derived cytotoxicity and physiological angiogenic responses. To clarify the intrinsic biological effects of TH-CNF- on HUVECs, we established an additional exposure model. Briefly, HUVECs were pre-seeded on standard culture plates. After adherence, TH-CNF- suspension was added at the same dosage as the original film group. This setup eliminated the physical interference of TH-CNF- as a culture substrate, enabling a more accurate evaluation of the material's intrinsic cytocompatibility. By examining cell viability, apoptosis, oxidative stress and LDH release in this supplementary model, no obvious cytotoxicity or cellular damage was detected in TH-CNF--treated HUVECs (Figs. S7–S9), verifying its good biocompatibility. Collectively, the suppressed cell proliferation in the direct-contact film system was not caused by material-derived adverse effects. Instead, it largely resulted from the functional transition of endothelial cells from proliferation to differentiation and structural remodeling during angiogenesis [57].

To further investigate the mechanism by which TH-CNF- promoted tube formation of HUVECs, the cells were co-incubated with TH-CNF- extract. The results revealed no significant difference in capillary branch points or total length between the TH-CNF- extract and the control (Fig. 5K–M). This suggests that the chemical composition of TH-CNF- film is not responsible for the promotion of tube formation; instead, as mentioned above (Fig. 4F), the three-dimensional spatial structure and interfacial properties of TH-CNF- likely play a critical role [29,60]. Additionally, an independent experiment confirmed that TH-EVLPs similarly lacked direct pro-angiogenic activity in this assay (Fig. 5K–M), despite their established anti-inflammatory efficacy against Staphylococcus aureus exotoxin-mediated pathologies [29]. This functional dichotomy highlights physical microenvironmental regulation distinct from chemical signaling as the principal mechanism underlying TH-CNF--induced vascular network assembly, advancing our understanding of topography-driven angiogenesis in diabetic wound repair.

Effects of TH-derived nanocellulose films on macrophage polarization and cell migration

Given the superior cell-adhesive and proliferative properties of TH-CNF+ and TH-CNF- films, we investigated their immunomodulatory effects on iBMDM polarization. The results demonstrated that both films significantly attenuated lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced M1 polarization markers, with TH-CNF- showing pronounced suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) (Fig. 6A and B). Conversely, the expression levels of M2-type macrophage-associated cytokines (Arg-1, Ym-1, IL-10, TGF-β, and VEGF) were significantly increased in the TH-CNF- group (Fig. 6C and D). Notably, TH-CNF+ exhibited no significant immunomodulatory activity relative to controls (Fig. 6B and D). These results confirm that there is no definite correlation between cell adhesion and activation. It has been reported that HPTMA can deactivate macrophage activation [34], which is consistent with our M2 polarization results. The low secretion level of pro-inflammatory cytokines is in line with cellular morphology in Fig. 5C, where macrophages remained nearly spherical with limited spreading, indicating weak cell-material interaction in the TH-CNF+ group. Hence, the inhibitory effect of TH-CNF+ on LPS-induced M1 cytokine production could be partially attributed to surface adsorption [34]. In comparison, TH-CNF- exerted prominent inhibitory effects on M1-related cytokines and effectively promoted M2 cytokine secretion. Macrophage polarization is a dynamic, reversible process influenced by material properties (e.g., surface charge, morphology) and culture conditions [[62], [63], [64]]. To uncover the mechanism by which TH-CNF- regulates macrophage polarization, transcriptomic analysis was further conducted. The Venn diagram (Fig. S10A) showed 4650 co-expressed genes between the Control and TH-CNF- groups, with 612 unique genes in the TH-CNF- group. The volcano plot (Fig. S10B) illustrates the overall distribution of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) based on fold change and statistical significance. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (Fig. 6E) indicated that DEGs were enriched in multiple biological processes, such as regulation of GTPase activity, miRNA metabolic process, and cellular response to DNA damage, as well as diverse cellular components and molecular functions. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis (Fig. 6F) further identified multiple signaling pathways associated with inflammation and macrophage regulation, including NOD, Toll-like receptor (TLR), TNF, NF-κB, MAPK, IL-17, JAK-STAT and Notch pathways. Among them, inflammation-related cascades such as NOD, TLR, TNF, NF-κB and MAPK were the central cascades modulated by TH-CNF-. These results suggest that TH-CNF- remodels the transcriptomic profile and alleviates excessive inflammatory responses by regulating multiple pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, thereby facilitating macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype.

To further assess the impact of TH-CNF+ and TH-CNF- film on cell migration, a standardized scratch assay was performed using L929 fibroblasts. As shown in Fig. 6G and H compared to the control group, TH-CNF- film significantly accelerated the healing of L929 cell scratches at 24 h (about 20% higher), whereas TH-CNF+ film showed no significant effect.

In summary, among the four nanocellulose films, TH-CNF and TH-CNC are not conducive for cell adhesion and proliferation, making them unsuitable for wound dressings. In contrast, both TH-CNF+ and TH-CNF- can promote cell proliferation and adhesion. Moreover, TH-CNF- film can enhance cell migration, angiogenesis and induce macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype, which are beneficial for diabetic wound healing. Based on these findings, we proceeded with the preparation and characterization of TH-EVLPs-loaded formulations using TH-CNF+, TH-CNF-, and TH-CNF± film (a mixture of TH-CNF+ and TH-CNF- at a ratio of 1:1).

Preparation and characterization of TH-EVLPs loaded nanocellulose films

To mimic the usage of “smashed for external application” of fresh TH, TH-EVLPs-loaded nanocellulose films (TH-EVLPs@CNF+, TH-EVLPs@CNF−, and TH-EVLPs@CNF±) were fabricated via adsorption-lyophilization method (Fig. 7A and B). SEM images revealed heterogeneous TH-EVLPs distribution: clustered aggregates on TH-CNF+ and TH-CNF- films contrasted with uniform dispersion on the CNF± matrix (Fig. 7C). Quantification via fluorescence spectroscopy demonstrated loading efficiencies of 11.48 × 109, 7.58 × 109, and 11.32 × 109 particles/cm2, respectively (Fig. 7D) [32]. The loading differences may be attributed to the charge interaction between the material surface and the negatively charged TH-EVLPs. To verify this hypothesis, TH-EVLPs were immersed in 150 mM NaCl solution to shield the electrostatic interactions. As shown in Fig. 7E, the TH-EVLPs loading efficiency of TH-CNF- and TH-CNF± was improved to different degrees, in which TH-CNF- reaches the highest loading capacity of 14.99 × 109 particles/cm2. These results confirm that electrostatic forces may be involved in the interaction between TH-EVLPs and nanocellulose materials. In addition, hygroscopicity assessment showed the ability of the three dried films to load TH-EVLPs was significantly improved compared to the films soaked in water for 12 h (Fig. 7F), in which the increment of TH-CNF- greater than that of TH-CNF+ may be attributed to the better water absorption caused by the more loose structure of the former. Normal skin is naturally acidic with pH value ranging from 4 to 6, while chronic wounds exhibit an elevated pH around 7.4 that is conducive to bacterial infection (pH 7.5–8.9) [65,66] This pH gradient under physiological/pathological conditions guides the test scenarios for the release of TH-EVLPs loaded nanocellulose films. As a representative, the release curve of TH-EVLPs@CNF− was monitored at pH values ranging from 4 to 9. The typical pH-dependent release behavior was shown in Fig. 7G. Specifically, at pH = 4 and 5, nearly 100% of TH-EVLPs was released within 6 h. In contrast, at near-neutral or alkaline pH condition, the release was significantly slower, with approximately 80% of TH-EVLPs released within 48 h. To elucidate the release mechanism of TH-EVLPs@CNF−, we applied kinetic models to fit the release data. The results indicated that the release of TH-EVLPs@CNF− in various pH conditions followed the Ritger-Peppas kinetic model (R2 > 0.99), with a Fickian diffusion release mechanism (n < 0.45) (Tables S1–S4). The release rate constant (k) demonstrated that TH-EVLPs were released significantly faster under acidic conditions than in neutral and alkaline conditions. This aligned with the release curve, indicating that TH-EVLPs can be released in a stable and sustained manner on diabetic chronic wounds. To further validate this mechanism, we investigated the swelling ratio, morphology, and mass of TH-CNF- films before and after immersion in solutions of varying pH. No significant differences in these properties were observed under conditions mimicking normal or infected wounds (Fig. S11), suggesting that pronounced degradation is not a dominant process in either environment. While limited hydrolysis of cellulose glycosidic bonds may occur under acidic conditions and contribute to an accelerated release of TH-EVLPs, the available data suggest that such subtle structural alterations are more likely to enhance diffusion rather than fundamentally alter the diffusion-dominated release mechanism.

In vivo wound healing of TH-EVLPs loaded nanocellulose films

The therapeutic efficacy of TH-EVLPs loaded nanocellulose films was systematically evaluated in a diabetic db/db murine model of full-thickness cutaneous wounds (Fig. 8A). As shown in Fig. 8B and C, all treatment groups exhibited significantly improved wound healing rates compared to the control group at day 14. Notably, TH-EVLPs@CNF− film group demonstrated the best healing effect (96.18 ± 1.51%), outperforming the TH-CNF+ film group (72.01 ± 1.64%), the TH-CNF- film group (84.34 ± 2.61%), and the TH-EVLPs group (80.59 ± 1.97%). Notably, control and TH-CNF+ groups exhibited persistent erythema and edematous morphology at endpoint, indicative of unresolved inflammation (Fig. 8B). Diabetic wounds are characterized by impaired macrophage polarization, with a predominant presence of M1-type macrophages, which produce elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby exacerbating inflammation [67,68]. Moreover, the reduced polarization of macrophages to the M2 phenotype leads to a deficiency in anti-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors, hindering tissue regeneration and prolonging the wound healing process [69,70]. As Fig. 8D and E, TH-EVLPs and the three films (TH-CNF+, TH-CNF-, and TH-EVLPs@CNF−) resulted in varying degrees of inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokine mRNA and protein expression compared to the control group, with TH-EVLPs@CNF− demonstrating the most effective therapeutic outcome. Furthermore, TH-EVLPs@CNF− treatment significantly enhanced the expression of M2-associated cytokines and growth factors, such as Arg-1, IL-10, TGF-β, and VEGF, in the wound tissue (Fig. 8F and G). These findings correlated with the improved wound healing rates observed in diabetic mice treated with TH-EVLPs@CNF−.

Numerous studies have shown that macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype can enhance angiogenesis, promote collagen fiber formation, and ultimately facilitate tissue repair and remodeling [71,72]. To investigate this process, immunofluorescence staining and imaging analysis were employed to assess the expression of macrophage markers (F4/80-CD86 for M1 and F4/80-CD206 for M2), the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6, the anti-inflammatory cytokine Arg-1, and the vascular endothelial marker CD31 in wound tissue. As shown in Fig. 9B–F, after 14 days of treatment with TH-EVLPs@CNF−, there was a significant reduction in CD86 and IL-6 levels, while CD206 and Arg-1 expression were notably increased compared to the control group. This indicated that TH-EVLPs@CNF− can effectively promote the macrophage polarization from M1-type to M2-type, with TH-EVLPs playing a key role. Moreover, the expression of CD31 was significantly elevated in the wound tissue of the TH-EVLPs@CNF− treated group, suggesting enhanced angiogenesis (Fig. 9B and G).

Accumulating evidence has proved that the NLRP3 inflammasome acted as a key regulator of macrophage phenotypic switching. Inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome mitigates inflammatory signaling, suppresses M1 macrophage activation, and thereby facilitates the transition toward the M2 phenotype [73]. TH-EVLPs could suppress the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway to exert anti-inflammatory effects [27], and may act synergistically with TH-CNF- to promote M2 macrophage transition, thereby improving the immune microenvironment in DFUs. Moreover, while TH-EVLPs showed limited direct effects on angiogenesis in vitro, they produced favorable outcomes in in vivo assays and acted synergistically with TH-CNF-. This beneficial effect may be ascribed to modulation of the inflammatory microenvironment. Collectively, the three-dimensional architecture of TH-CNF- and the anti-inflammatory properties of TH-EVLPs function through independent yet complementary mechanisms, collectively promoting key biological processes underlying wound repair.

Conclusion

The traditional practice of "smashed for external application” in herbal medicine, while empirically validated for carbuncle and wound management, has lacked mechanistic elucidation, particularly regarding the functional role of cellulose, a structural cornerstone of plant-based therapeutics. This study bridges traditional wisdom with nanomaterial innovation by integrating TH-EVLPs into engineered nanocellulose scaffolds, replicating the natural dispersion observed in fresh plant matrices. Among the nanocellulose variants, anionic TH-CNF- emerged as the optimal platform, synergizing high porosity, tunable hygroscopicity, and pH-responsive release kinetics to enhance diabetic wound healing. Its nanofibrillar architecture promoted critical regenerative processes, including fibroblast migration, endothelial tubulogenesis, and immunomodulation via M2 macrophage polarization. In vivo validation revealed the superior therapeutic synergy of TH-EVLPs@CNF−, achieving nearly 100% wound closure in diabetic mice through tripartite mechanisms: (1) sustained anti-inflammatory action via pH-dependent TH-EVLPs release, (2) microenvironment remodeling, and (3) angiogenic activation. This performance surpassed standalone TH-EVLPs or nanocellulose treatments, underscoring the criticality of combinatorial design.

While this study establishes the potent wound-healing activity of dandelion-derived components, it does not preclude the possibility that similar effects could be achieved with materials from other sources. The bioactivity is likely linked to the specific composition or structure of dandelion. Future comparative studies with EVLPs from other medicinal plants or with synthetic biomimetic scaffolds will be crucial to delineate unique versus shared therapeutic mechanisms. Such work will further clarify the position of plant-derived materials within the broader landscape of regenerative nanomaterials.
