Complement-secreting CAFs are associated with better prognosis in pancreatic cancer: single-cell multiomics
Authors: Kai Chen, Yongsu Ma, Liling Huang, Pengfei Wu, Heng-Chung Kung, Bohan Yang, Jixin Zhang, Robert A Anders, Jacquelyn W Zimmerman, Qingfeng Zhu, Xiaodong Tian, Jin He, Yinmo Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccumulating evidence has demonstrated that distinct tumour-promoting and tumour-restraining cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) subtypes coexist in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma.ObjectiveTo develop targeted CAF therapeutic strategies by reprogramming tumour-promoting CAF subtypes.DesignWe leveraged multiomics technologies to systematically identify and characterise CAF subtypes transcriptionally, epigenetically and spatially and correlate them with clinicopathological features.ResultsWe found that complement-secreting CAFs (csCAFs), initially identified by our group and inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs) share significant overlap in their transcriptional profiles and chromatin accessibility. iCAFs specifically express transcription factors from the heme and oxidative homeostasis pathway and the activator protein 1 family, which are both involved in cellular response to oxidative stress. Notably, the composition of csCAFs among all CAFs declined during pancreatic carcinogenesis, while trajectory analysis showed that csCAFs could potentially differentiate into iCAFs. Spatially resolved analysis indicated that tumour regions with a higher csCAF composition were associated with lower levels of TGF-β ligands, fewer M2 tumour-associated macrophages and increased levels of lipid mediators. Additionally, we identified a spatially defined CXCL12-CXCR4 ligand–receptor interaction between csCAFs and T cells, but in distinct patterns between different metastatic organs. Patients with a higher composition of csCAFs have significantly longer overall survival and recurrence-free survival through multiplex immunohistochemistry and bulk RNA-seq deconvolution.ConclusionOur study demonstrates that csCAFs may represent an early-stage iCAF subtype and suggests a promising strategy for reprogramming iCAFs into csCAFs.

Introduction

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) constitute a major component of the dense pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) stroma1 and can promote tumour progression, metastasis and therapeutic resistance by secreting cytokines to support tumour growth,2 promoting angiogenesis,2 3 remodelling the extracellular matrix (ECM)4 and mediating an immunosuppressive microenvironment.5 However, previous preclinical6 7 and clinical studies8 targeting CAFs yielded unexpected results, suggesting the coexistence of tumour-promoting and tumour-restraining CAF subtypes and underscoring the heterogeneity across this cell population in PDAC. Previously identified CAF subtypes were mostly tumour-promoting, while tumour-restraining CAF subtypes have not been well identified. Studies since have suggested that normalising CAF functionality by reprogramming tumour-promoting CAFs, such as with vitamin D analogue and all-trans retinoic acid, can induce stromal remodelling and suppression of tumour growth, presenting a promising strategy beyond depleting CAFs.7 8 As such, a deeper understanding of the full spectrum of CAF heterogeneity and plasticity is required to guide development of future CAF-directed therapies.9 While several CAF subtypes have been identified in PDAC, the relationship between these subtypes, many overlapping in their functionality, their spatial distribution and clinical significance has yet to be fully investigated.51012 Furthermore, a recent study identified sub-tumour microenvironments (subTMEs) characterised by distinct CAF differentiation states, immune phenotypes and spatially defined cell–cell interaction.13 However, the underlying mechanism driving this intratumour CAF heterogeneity and relationship between CAF subtypes and immune cells within the subTMEs remains largely unexplored.

Here, we applied single-cell multiomics technologies to delineate the CAF landscape, characterise CAF subtypes at both transcriptional and epigenetic levels and explore their developmental trajectory. Due to the inherent limitation of dissociated single-cell approaches in capturing spatial context, spatially resolved analysis was performed to reveal prominent CAF intratumour heterogeneity and crosstalk with other subTME components. Moreover, we retrospectively collected resected human PDAC specimens and performed multiplex immunohistochemical staining (mIHC) and deconvoluted bulk RNA-seq data sets to correlate CAF subtypes with clinical outcomes (figure 1A).

Results

Single-cell RNA-seq delineates CAF landscape in human pancreatic cancer: an update

To further understand the CAF heterogeneity in PDAC, we conducted single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on nine resected PDAC tumours and six normal pancreatic tissue specimens (online supplemental table S1; online supplemental note 1). We isolated fibroblasts and performed unsupervised clustering analysis to further group fibroblasts into nine subclusters (figure 1B–D; online supplemental figure S1A–D; online supplemental table S2; online supplemental note 1). Canonical CAF markers, including LUM, DCN, FN1 and COL1A1, were expressed in all subclusters except subcluster 6 (figure 1E). We assessed the expression levels of well-established CAF subtypes markers14 (figure 1E; online supplemental table S2). Subcluster 3, which showed enriched expression of IL6, LIF, CXCL2 and HAS1, was identified as inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs). Subclusters 1, 2 and 8, which highly expressed ACTA2 (αSMA), COL11A1, MMP11 and POSTN, were identified as myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs). Subcluster 6 highly expressed markers of antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs) (CD74, HLA-DRA and HLA-DRB) as well as Schwann cell markers (GAP43 and S100B), but did not express typical CAF markers (LUM and DCN). This suggests that subcluster six represented Schwann cells that were merged with the fibroblast population, rather than apCAFs. Subcluster 4 exhibited high expression of ribosomal genes, indicating a deteriorated cellular state. Interestingly, subclusters 0, 5 and 7 were located near subcluster 3 (iCAFs) in the uMAP plot but did not express iCAF markers. In accordance with our previous study,15 subcluster 0, 5 and 7 demonstrated enriched expression of complement genes (C7, C3 and CFD) as well as GPX3, OGN, PTGDS, APOE and CXCL12, identifying them as complement-secreting CAFs (csCAFs). A close examination of transcriptional profiles revealed that csCAFs and iCAFs had largely overlapping gene signatures (figure 1E; online supplemental table S2).

PDAC exhibited a significantly lower composition of csCAFs among all CAFs compared with normal pancreas, while myCAFs were almost absent in the normal pancreas but dominated in PDAC (figure 1F; online supplemental figure S1E). In PDAC, csCAF markers (eg, CXCL12) were downregulated, whereas myCAF markers (eg, HOPX) were upregulated compared with normal pancreas (online supplemental figure S1F). To further characterise the identified CAF subtypes, we performed gene set annotation and enrichment analysis (figure 1G). We found that ‘complement and coagulation cascades’ was enriched in csCAFs, while ‘response to oxidative stress’ was enriched in iCAFs. Both ‘vasculature development’ and ‘collagen formation’ were enriched in csCAFs, iCAFs and myCAFs, indicating that ECM secretion and angiogenesis regulation are fundamental roles of CAFs. Notably, myCAFs were comparatively more enriched in ‘collagen formation’, confirming their key role in tumour fibrosis. Single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering analysis was conducted to infer key transcription factors (TFs) across CAF subtypes (figure 1H). We found that the activities of TFs from the activator protein 1 (AP-1) family (FOSL1, JUND, FOSB, ATF3, FOS, JUN and JUNB) and MAF family (MAFG and MAFF) were upregulated in iCAFs. Both AP-1 and MAF family TFs are involved in the cellular response to oxidative stress.16 In addition, we performed weighted gene coexpression network analysis to identify representative gene modules for each CAF subtype and validate their established transcriptional profiles (online supplemental figure S1G–K; online supplemental note 2). Taken together, csCAFs and myCAFs predominate in the normal pancreas and PDAC, respectively. csCAFs and iCAFs share common transcriptional features. iCAFs and myCAFs are distinctly characterised by the oxidative stress response and ECM organisation.

csCAFs can potentially differentiate into iCAFs

Given the substantial overlap in transcriptional profiles between csCAFs and iCAFs, we hypothesised that csCAFs may differentiate into iCAFs during PDAC progression. To explore this, we performed pseudotime (Monocle 3) and RNA velocity analyses to infer the developmental trajectory of CAF subtypes. We included the annotated pericyte cluster in this analysis, given that pericytes have been demonstrated as a possible cellular origin for CAFs.1 Pseudotime analysis showed a clear differentiation trajectory from the starting points ADH1B_csCAF_0 and CST2_csCAF_5 to the endpoint CXCL2_iCAF_3 (figure 1I). RNA velocity analysis showed a strong velocity pattern originating from the left half of ADH1B_csCAF_0 and CST2_csCAF_5 towards CXCL2_iCAF_3 (figure 1J; online supplemental figure S2A–C), further supporting the potential conversion of csCAFs into iCAFs. Neither pseudotime nor RNA velocity analyses revealed a differentiation link between pericytes and myCAFs.

External validation of csCAFs using integrated human and mouse scRNA-seq data sets

To further confirm that csCAFs and iCAFs represent distinct CAF subtypes, we integrated 13 human scRNA-seq data sets comprised 203 specimens from published available databases141730 (online supplemental table S1; online supplemental note 3). Similarly, fibroblasts were grouped into three major CAF subtypes (csCAFs, iCAFs and myCAFs), consistent with our previous findings (online supplemental figure S3A–E; online supplemental table S3). Interestingly, the same clustering pattern was observed, with csCAFs and iCAFs sharing overlapping transcriptional profiles (online supplemental table S3). Both csCAFs and iCAFs expressed C3, C7, CFD, GPX3, OGN, PTGDS, APOE and CXCL12, whereas iCAFs specifically expressed IL6, LIF, CXCL2 and HAS1 (online supplemental figure S3F). Notably, csCAFs exhibited a higher expression level of CXCL12 and ENG (CD105) compared with iCAFs (onlinesupplemental tables S2 S3). myCAFs specifically highly expressed ACTA2 (αSMA), COL11A1, MMP11 and POSTN (online supplemental figure S3F). apCAFs were not identified, and no Schwann cells (expressing S100B and GAP43) were mixed in this integrated human data set. We compared the composition of CAF subtypes across different sample types. We found that csCAFs were predominant in normal pancreas and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumour (pNET), while myCAFs were more prevalent in primary PDAC tumour. A significantly lower composition of csCAFs was observed in primary PDAC tumour in comparison with both normal pancreas and pNET (online supplemental figure S3G). Additionally, metastatic PDAC tumour (17/20 liver metastases) had significantly higher composition of csCAFs and lower composition of myCAFs than primary PDAC tumour (online supplemental figure S3G), suggesting distinct CAF landscapes between the PDAC primary tumour and metastatic sites.

We also retrieved four mouse PDAC scRNA-seq data sets with 60 samples10 13 31 32 from published available databases (online supplemental table S1). Subclustering of fibroblasts (expressing Lum, Dcn, Fn1 and Col1a1) resulted in four major CAF subtypes, including csCAFs, iCAFs, myCAFs and apCAFs (online supplemental figure S4A–E). csCAFs and iCAFs exhibited considerable overlap in their transcriptional profiles (online supplemental table S3), with both expressing C3, C4b, Dpt, Ogn, Ly6C, Col14a1 and Cxcl12 (online supplemental figure S4F). However, iCAFs also specifically expressed Il6, Has1, Cxcl1 and Cxcl2. myCAFs showed enriched expression of Acta2 (αSMA), Thbs2, Col12a1 and Sdc1. apCAFs were characterised by expression of Cd74, H2-Ab1 and Slpi, but did not express Schwann cell markers (Gap43 and S100b), confirming their identities.10 Altogether, csCAFs were identified in both human and mouse PDAC. However, there exist differences in CAF heterogeneity and their gene expression profile (eg, Ly6C expression was exclusive to mouse csCAFs and iCAFs) (online supplemental table S3), underscoring the importance of considering species-specific differences in translational comparisons.

Paired single-nuclei RNA-seq and ATAC-seq reveal the dysregulated transcription factor network in iCAFs

To gain a deeper understanding of CAF subtypes at the epigenetic level, we employed the 10× Genomics single-nuclei Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression assay (snMultiome-seq) for simultaneous measurement of chromatin accessibility and gene expression in individual nuclei. We performed snMultiome-seq on eight resected PDAC tumours and two normal pancreatic tissue specimens (online supplemental table S1; online supplemental note 4). The weighted nearest neighbour (WNN) method was used to integrate multimodal data to perform more accurate clustering and classification of CAFs (figure 2A; online supplemental figure S5A,B). A total of 40 clusters were identified and annotated into 10 common cell populations according to the gene expression patterns of each cluster (figure 2B). Fibroblasts were isolated for unsupervised clustering analysis using WNN, and the human CAF landscape was recapitulated (figure 2C,D). Consistent with our previous scRNA-seq analysis, both ‘BuildClusterTree’ analysis and Euclidean distance quantification revealed that csCAFs and iCAFs resided on closely related phylogenetic branches and exhibited a markedly shorter distance compared with other subtypes (data not shown), highlighting that csCAFs and iCAFs also share similar epigenetic profiles (figure 2C). We again observed a higher composition of myCAFs and a lower composition of csCAFs in PDAC compared with normal pancreas (figure 2E; online supplemental figure S5C). Importantly, increased chromatin accessibility was found near the transcription start sites (TSSs) of markers (eg, C7, IL6, HAS1 and COL11A1) specific to the corresponding CAF subtypes, suggesting that chromatin accessibility can be used as an additional feature for annotating cell populations (online supplemental figure S5D–G). Pseudotime analysis of Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin (ATAC) data confirmed that csCAFs may potentially convert into iCAFs (online supplemental figure S5H).

Subsequently, we performed motif analysis by finding over-represented motifs in a set of differentially accessible peaks (DAPs). Compared with csCAFs and myCAFs, iCAFs showed significant enrichment in motifs related to heme and oxidative homeostasis (BACH1 and NFE2L2) and AP-1 pathways (FOSL2, FOS, FOS::JUND, FOSL1::JUN, JUN, JUND, JUNB, FOSL2::JUN, FOSL1::JUND, FOS::JUNB and FOSL2::JUND) (figure 2F; online supplemental table S4). We also observed increased chromatin accessibility near the TSSs of heme and oxidative homeostasis pathway components (eg, BACH1, NFE2L2 and HMOX1) in iCAFs (figure 2G,H; online supplemental figure S6A). The heme and oxidative homeostasis pathway plays a critical role in regulating oxidative stress, iron metabolism and inflammatory responses to maintain cellular homeostasis.33 In addition, we calculated pathway scores using the ‘AddModuleScore’ function (online supplemental table S5) and found that csCAFs had the lowest glycolysis score, iCAFs had the highest hypoxia score and myCAFs had the highest oxidative phosphorylation score (figure 2I–K). These findings emphasise the prominent role of hypoxia and oxidative stress in iCAFs.

Heme and oxidative homeostasis and AP-1 pathways are activated in iCAFs

We next examined the gene expression levels of key components from heme and oxidative homeostasis and AP-1 pathways across CAF subtypes. In both human scRNA-seq and snMultiome-seq data sets, we found that iCAFs exhibited high expression of components from heme and oxidative homeostasis pathway (including BACH1, SOD2, NFE2L2, HIF1A, HMOX1, MAFB and MAFF) as well as AP-1 pathway (including FOS, FOSB, FOSL1, FOSL2, JUN, JUNB and JUND). In contrast, csCAFs expressed low levels of SOD2, NFE2L2, FOSL1 and FOSL2, but high levels of FOS, FOSB, JUN, JUNB and JUND. These genes were mostly absent in myCAFs (figure 3A–C,G–I). Similarly, in mouse scRNA-seq data sets, iCAFs consistently showed high expression of components from both the oxidative homeostasis pathway (including Bach1, Sod2, Nfe2l2, Hif1a, Hmox1 and Maff) and the AP-1 pathway (including Fos, Fosb, Fosl, Jun, Junb and Jund) (figure 3D,J). Furthermore, TF footprinting analysis was applied to investigate whether the above-mentioned TFs can bind to their corresponding motifs across CAF subtypes. The genomic regions corresponding to the motifs of BACH1, NFE2L2, FOS, FOSL1, FOSL2, JUN, JUNB and JUND displayed valleys in Tn5 insertion enrichment, with cumulative Tn5 insertion around these motifs being higher in iCAFs compared with csCAFs and myCAFs (figure 3E,F,K–P). This suggests that these TFs bind to their respective motifs, resulting in increased chromatin accessibility in the surrounding regions in iCAFs. In summary, iCAFs upregulated the expression levels of TFs from heme and oxidative homeostasis and AP-1 pathways, which exhibited higher motif activities in iCAFs, thereby regulating downstream genes involved in cellular homeostasis in response to oxidative stress.

Next, we examined the expression levels of components from IL-1, TGF-β and Hippo-YAP pathways across CAF subtypes (details provided in online supplemental note 5). In line with previous studies,14 iCAFs specifically upregulated the expression of IL-1 pathway genes, including IL1R1, IL6, LIF and CXCL8 (online supplemental figure S6B–D). Within iCAFs, increased chromatin accessibility was found near the TSS of CXCL8 in iCAFs (online supplemental figure S6E), which has been previously shown to promote an immunosuppressive TME and reprogramme tumour cell phenotypes.34 These differences in the IL-1 pathway between csCAFs and iCAFs further support their classification as distinct CAF states. Simultaneously, we found that csCAFs showed enriched expression of BMPR2, LRRC32, LTBP1 and LTBP2, while iCAFs expressed high expression levels of BMP2, TGFBR3, SMAD7, SNAI1 and SNAI2. myCAFs showed enriched expressions of TGFBR1, BMPR2, LRRC32, SMAD2, SMAD4, ZEB1 and TWIST1 (online supplemental figure S6F–H). The chromatin accessibility near the TSS of BMP2 in iCAFs was more significant (online supplemental figure S6I), indicating possible crosstalk between CAF subtypes via the TGF-β superfamily. Both SMAD2 (R-SMAD) and SMAD4 (Co-SMAD) were highly expressed in myCAFs, as opposed to SMAD7 (I-SMAD), which was highly expressed in iCAFs, suggesting that the TGF-β pathway was activated in myCAFs but inhibited in iCAFs. We found that myCAFs have significantly higher expression levels of RUNX2, TEAD1, TEAD3 and YAP1 compared with csCAFs and iCAFs (online supplemental figure S6J–L). YAP1 is the core transcriptional coactivator, which binds to TEAD family members to regulate the Hippo-YAP pathway.35 The TF footprinting analysis indicated an increased motif activity of RUNX2 in myCAFs (online supplemental figure S6M). These results suggest that the Hippo-YAP pathway is activated in myCAFs and may be a promising target.

In addition to the above supervised analysis, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and DAPs between csCAFs and iCAFs in an unsupervised manner. Although csCAFs and iCAFs share many common genes and peaks, iCAFs exhibited several significantly increased peaks, such as Chr22-35589022-35589967 and Chr10-75770473-75771879 (figure 3Q; online supplemental figure S6N–O; online supplemental table S4). myCAFs showed several increased peaks, such as Chr21-45049702-45051348 and Chr12-94983572-94984631 (figure 3Q). The similar gene expression and chromatin accessibility patterns between csCAFs and iCAFs suggest that csCAFs may represent an early developmental stage of iCAFs. However, the roles of CAF subtype-specific chromatin accessible peaks remain to be explored.

Inflammatory pericytes share common features with iCAFs

Interestingly, we found that iCAF makers (eg, IL6, LIF and CXCL2) could divide pericytes into two different subclusters, prompting further subclustering of pericytes to investigate the heterogeneity of this cell population in PDAC (online supplemental figure S2B). In our scRNA-seq data set, we identified two pericyte subclusters that both expressed RGS5, NDUFA4L2, ADIRF and MYH11, verifying their pericyte identities (online supplemental figure S7A). Subcluster 1 specifically expressed IL6, LIF, CXCL2 and CCL2, therefore identified as inflammatory pericytes (iPCs) (online supplemental figure S7B). We next compared iPCs and the other pericyte subcluster (PCs) and found that iPCs showed significant upregulation of AP-1 family members, such as JUNB, FOSL2, JUD and MAFF (online supplemental figure S7C), prompting us to further examine the expression levels of components from the heme and oxidative homeostasis pathway in iPCs. Similar to iCAFs, iPCs also exhibited enriched expression of BACH1, SOD2, NFE2L2, MAFB, MAFF and HIF1A (online supplemental figure S7D). Excitingly, iPCs were also identified in the integrated human scRNA-seq data set (online supplemental figure S7E–F), and pericyte subcluster two in the integrated mouse scRNA-seq data set may correspond to iPCs given their enrichment in Il6 and Ccl2 (online supplemental figure S7G–H). Therefore, we speculate that the formation of iCAFs may not be an isolated phenomenon during PDAC progression. There could be a driving factor converting tissue resident cell populations into inflammatory subsets, characterised by expression of inflammatory factors and activation of pathways related to oxidative stress.

SpaTial Enhanced REsolution Omics sequencing reveals intratumour CAF heterogeneity

To gain insights into the intratumour CAF heterogeneity and the spatially defined crosstalk between CAF subtypes and other cells, we applied SpaTial Enhanced REsolution Omics sequencing (Stereo-seq) on fresh frozen normal pancreas tissue and pancreatic cancer tumours, including three PDAC and one pancreatic adenosquamous carcinoma (PASC) specimens (online supplemental table S1). We spatially mapped the locations of cell types, identified in the previous scRNA-seq analysis (figure 4A,B; online supplemental figure S8A; online supplemental note 6). Spots (bin50) annotated as csCAFs, iCAFs and myCAFs specifically expressed their respective markers (figure 4C; online supplemental figure S8B,C). Next, we used H&E-stained sections to guide selection of fields of view (FOVs) for Stereo-seq on consecutive tissue sections (online supplemental figure S8D). These FOVs (800 μm × 800 µm) divided the adjacent normal pancreas and pancreatic cancer tissues into different regions, facilitating the intratumour heterogeneity analysis. In the adjacent normal pancreas section (Stereo_N001), the FOVs corresponding to acinar tissue were labelled as NP_1, while those corresponding to intraepithelial neoplasia were labelled as NP_2. We determined the ‘classical’ and ‘basal-like’ scores for spots annotated as ductal cells using the ‘AddModuleScore’ function to infer tumour phenotypes (online supplemental table S5). We observed that both ‘classical’ and ‘basal-like’ tumour cells coexisted within the same FOV, with some tumour cells being identified as neither phenotype (figure 4D), suggesting that classifying patients as either ‘classical’ or ‘basal-like’ based on bulk RNA-seq data overlooks the significant intratumour heterogeneity.

A close examination of the distribution of CAF subtypes revealed that myCAFs were adjacent to ductal cells, whereas csCAFs and iCAFs were positioned farther away, in line with the general understanding of CAF subtypes (figure 4D). We compared CAF subtypes composition across FOVs in different samples (figure 4E,F). There was a trend towards higher myCAF composition among all CAFs in PDAC compared with NP_1 and NP_2 (p=0.0522; p=0.0815). PASC had significantly lower csCAFs and higher myCAFs composition than both NP_1 and NP_2 (figure 4F). Notably, the composition of CAF subtypes varied significantly across FOVs in PDAC, underscoring intratumour CAF heterogeneity (figure 4E,F).

Higher csCAFs associate with lower M2 TAM and TGF-β ligands within subTMEs

We also compared selected immune cell types and pathways across different samples (details provided in online supplemental note 7). Notably, PASC exhibited significantly lower scores for M1 tumour-associated macrophage (TAM), M2 TAM, effector cell traffic, costimulatory molecules and exhausted T cell compared with PDAC, with a trend towards lower effector T cell scores (p=0.0726) (online supplemental figure S8E–L). In addition, significantly lower scores for lipid mediators, oxidative phosphorylation, FAK pathway and Hippo-YAP pathway in PASC than PDAC were observed (online supplemental figure S8M–R). A board-certified pathologist determined that the FOVs in PASC were squamous tumour area by the IHC staining of P40 (online supplemental figure S9A–C). Taken together, PASC (squamous tumour area) is mainly composed of tumour cells and myCAFs, with low infiltration of TAMs and T cells.

Given the significant intratumour CAF heterogeneity in PDAC, we next aimed to correlate CAF subtypes with other subTME features. We categorised the FOVs in Stereo_T002 (n=16) into two groups based on the median composition of CAF subtypes. The FOVs with higher csCAFs composition had lower M2 TAM scores (p=0.0563) and higher M1 TAM/M2 TAM ratio (p=0.0830), as well as significantly higher lipid mediators scores than those with lower csCAFs composition. However, myCAFs showed an opposite trend (figure 4H–O; online supplemental figure S10A–D). Intriguingly, FOVs with higher TGF-β ligands scores exhibited significantly higher myCAF composition and lower csCAF and iCAF compositions (figure 4P–R), supporting the notion that TGF-β levels vary across different subTMEs, with high TGF-β levels promoting and sustaining the formation of myCAFs. Simultaneously, we evaluated the relationship between tumour phenotypes and other TME features (online supplemental figure S11A–G; online supplemental note 7). The results indicated that subTMEs shaped by ‘basal-like’ and ‘classical’ tumour subtypes are characterised by opposing features, and subTMEs associated with the ‘basal-like’ subtype exhibited less immune cell infiltration (lower scores for myeloid cell traffic, M1 TAM, M2 TAM, effector T cell, exhausted T cell and costimulatory molecules), reflecting a ‘cold’ feature. This is in line with previous studies that suggest ‘basal-like’ tumours are more aggressive and have a poorer prognosis.36

Spatially defined CXCL12-CXCR4 interaction between csCAFs and T cells

The CXCL12-CXCR4 ligand–receptor pair, which plays a crucial role in lymphocyte chemotaxis and tumour metastasis,37 was identified in interactions between csCAFs/iCAFs and B cells, macro/mono, CD4 T cells and CD8 T cells (online supplemental figure S12A–D; online supplemental note 8). Notably, the CXCL12-CXCR4 interaction between csCAFs and CD8 T cells was identified only in PDAC (figure 5A). Visual inspection confirmed the spatial proximity of CXCL12 and CXCR4 expression (figure 5B). Subsequently, we analysed CXCR4+T cell populations in primary PDAC tumours as well as liver and lung metastases using mIHC (online supplemental table S6). Compared with primary PDAC tumours, both paired liver and lung metastases exhibited a significant increase in CXCR4+GZMB+ CD8 T and CXCR4+TIM3+ CD8 T, while CXCR4+Tregs were only increased in the paired liver metastasis (figure 5C–E). No significant differences were observed in CD8+T, CXCR4+CD137+ CD8 T and CXCR4+LAG3+ CD8 T between primary PDAC tumours and paired metastases (online supplemental figure S12E–G). This suggested that blocking the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis might have a more pronounced effect in the metastatic lesions due to the enrichment of immunosuppressive CXCR4+ T cells.

We next performed Xenium analysis to further explore the spatial distribution of CAF subtypes and crosstalk with immune cells. We conducted unsupervised analysis and cell type annotation for two low-grade (LG) pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN) and three high-grade (HG) PanIN specimens (figure 5F; online supplemental figure S12H,I; online supplemental table S1). Like normal pancreas, csCAFs predominantly existed in both LG and HG PanIN tissues, whereas myCAFs were less abundant in both. Additionally, HG PanIN showed significantly lower composition of csCAFs and a trend toward higher composition of iCAFs (p=0.0609) (figure 5G,H).

Consistent with our scRNA-seq analysis, CXCL12 was primarily expressed in csCAFs and iCAFs, with csCAFs exhibiting higher expression level of CXCL12 in the Xenium data sets (figure 5I; online supplemental figure S12J–L). We verified the spatially defined CXCL12-CXCR4 interaction between csCAFs/iCAFs and B cells, CD4 T cells and CD8 T cells in PDAC (Xenium_T001) with csCAFs showing stronger interactions compared with iCAFs (figure 5J). In contrast, no CXCL12-CXCR4 interaction between myCAFs and other cells was identified. We visualised the spatial expression of CXCL12 and CXCR4 and found csCAFs with high CXCL12 expression and CD4 T/CD8 T cells with high CXCR4 expression are spatially in contact with each other (figure 5I). We further investigated the cell composition around CAF subtypes. Cells located within 50 µm of csCAFs and iCAFs primarily included macro/mono, pericyte/endothelial and plasma cells, while cells within 50 µm of myCAFs were mainly epithelial cells. The most abundant cells surrounding iCAFs were macro/mono (figure 5K–M). compared with myCAFs, iCAFs were located closer to csCAFs, supporting a stronger relationship between csCAFs and iCAFs (figure 5N). myCAFs were closer to epithelial cells than both csCAFs and iCAFs, whereas csCAFs and iCAFs were closer to CD8 T cells than myCAFs (online supplemental figure S12M,N). These findings suggest that myCAFs might be more likely to communicate with tumour cells, while csCAFs and iCAFs tend to interact with immune cells. Moreover, we identified and visualised the distribution of csCAFs, iCAFs and myCAFs in situ in other PDAC tissue (Xenium_T002), but csCAF was the only CAF subtype identified in normal pancreas tissue (Xenium_N001) (online supplemental figure S13A–F). Not surprisingly, T cells with high CXCR4 expression were spatially in contact with csCAFs in the Xenium_N001 (online supplemental figure S13G).

csCAFs are correlated with better prognosis in PDAC

To explore the correlation between CAF subtypes and clinicopathological characteristics, we performed mIHC on resected human primary and metastatic PDAC tumours (online supplemental table S1; online supplemental note 9). A mIHC panel for CAF subtypes was developed according to the markers identified by the scRNA-seq analysis (online supplemental table S6). Visualisation via mIHC revealed that csCAFs, iCAFs and myCAFs exhibit clear spatial localisation within PDAC tissue (figure 6A). Furthermore, mIHC results confirmed the presence of CAFs expressing FAP, αSMA, C7, C3, OGN and APOE, reinforcing the existence of csCAFs and iCAFs. csCAFs did not express IL6, while iCAFs showed high expression of IL6. myCAFs highly expressed αSMA but did not express IL6, C7, C3 or APOE (figure 6A), verifying our scRNA-seq results. We also performed mIHC on mouse specimens and preliminarily identified CAF subtypes (online supplemental figure S14A,B). Additionally, flow cytometry of orthotopic KPC tumours showed CD45-EpCAM-PDPN+ CAFs could be divided into three populations by the expression of Ly6C and MHCII (online supplemental figure S15A), replicating previous findings.10 Intriguingly, Ly6C+MHCII CAF population, previously identified as iCAFs, could be further divided into two subsets based on the expression of IL6 (ie, csCAFs: MHCII-Ly6C+IL6-; iCAFs: MHCII-Ly6C+IL6+), consistent with our mouse scRNA-seq analysis.

First, we compared CAF subtypes across various clinical stages. In both Chemonaive and CTX cohorts, there was a significantly higher composition of csCAFs among all CAFs in patients with T1 stage (≤ 2 cm) compared with T2 and T3 stages (>2 cm) (figure 6B; online supplemental figure S15B,C). Next, we investigated the impacts of chemotherapy on CAF subtypes. Notably, patients in the CTX cohort had significantly lower density of myCAFs among all cells than those in the Chemonaive cohort (figure 6C; online supplemental figure S15D). We did not observe any significant differences in CAF subtypes composition or density between two different neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimens (online supplemental figure S15E). compared with patients with poor or moderate responses to FFX, those with marked responses exhibited a significantly higher composition of csCAFs and lower composition of myCAFs (online supplemental figure S16A).

Additionally, we compared CAF subtypes between patients with early recurrence (ER, ≤1 year) and late recurrence (LR, >1 year). In the Chemonaive cohort, patients with LR had higher csCAFs composition (p=0.0835) and significantly higher iCAFs composition, as well as significantly lower composition and density of myCAFs, compared with patients with ER (figure 6D–F; online supplemental figure S16B). Interestingly, in the CTX cohort, patients with LR showed an opposite trend with significantly lower iCAFs composition and higher composition and density of myCAFs (figure 6E,F; online supplemental figure S16B). This suggests that myCAFs can either serve as a risk or a protective factor for PDAC recurrence depending on whether patients received neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Investigation of the differences in CAF subtypes between paired primary PDAC tumours and metastatic tumours showed that liver metastasis had significantly higher composition and density of csCAFs, as well as significantly lower composition of myCAFs than the paired primary tumour. In contrast, lung metastasis exhibited significantly higher composition of iCAFs than the paired primary tumour (figure 6G–I; online supplemental figure S16C), further supporting the distinct CAF landscape across organ-specific metastases.

Finally, we correlated CAF subtypes with prognosis of patients with PDAC. In the Chemonaive cohort, patients with overall survival (OS) >2 years had higher csCAF composition (p=0.0606) and significantly lower composition and density of myCAFs compared with patients with OS <2 years. Nevertheless, no significant differences between the two groups were found in the CTX cohort (figure 6J,K; online supplemental figure S17A–D). Furthermore, Kaplan-Meier curves indicated that patients with a higher csCAFs composition had significantly longer OS and recurrence-free survival (RFS) than those with a lower csCAFs composition (figure 6L–O). Patients with higher myCAF composition showed a significantly shorter RFS in the Chemonaive cohort (online supplemental figure S17E–L). However, in the Chemonaive cohort, higher density of myCAFs and TANs predicted significantly shorter OS and RFS (online supplemental figure S18A–P). We next correlated csCAFs with TAMs and TANs and found that patients with higher csCAFs composition had a significantly lower M2 TAM (figure 6P,Q), in line with our Stereo-seq results (figure 4J). Taken together, a higher csCAFs composition was related to better patient outcomes, while a higher composition and density of myCAFs were linked to poorer prognosis. We next validated the clinical significance of csCAFs by applying deconvolution to bulk RNA-seq data sets (figure 7A–I; online supplemental figure S19A–G; online supplemental table S7; details provided in online supplemental note 10).

Discussion

In this study, multiomics technologies were applied to identify and characterise three CAF subtypes (csCAFs, iCAFs and myCAFs) in human PDAC, and four CAF subtypes (csCAFs, iCAFs, myCAFs and apCAFs) in mouse PDAC (figure 8). By comparing their transcriptional profiles, iCAFs, myCAFs and apCAFs identified in this study showed a high degree of consistency with their corresponding CAF subtypes reported in previous studies.10 csCAFs, initially identified by our lab, were characterised by the expression of multiple complement genes and enrichment of the ‘complement and coagulation cascades’ pathway.15 Through comparative analysis of marker genes, we found that csCAFs may partially correspond to the ENG+ (CD105+) iCAF_cluster3 subtype recently described by Cumming et al (data not shown).38 Normal pancreas was exclusively enriched in csCAF, with myCAFs absent. There was a declining trend in csCAFs from LG PanIN to high PanIN, and from small tumours (<2 cm) to large tumours (>2 cm). Therefore, we believe that csCAFs may represent tissue resident fibroblasts or perivascular fibroblasts that persist in PDAC.39

CAF subtypes were conserved between human and mouse PDAC, with csCAFs, iCAFs and myCAFs being consistently identified in both species. Notably, despite highly similar marker genes expressed in the corresponding CAF subtypes in human and mouse PDAC, some differences were observed. For example, mouse csCAFs and iCAFs expressed Ly6C, whereas humans lack the Ly6C gene. Conversely, human csCAFs and iCAFs expressed C7, which was not detected in their mouse counterparts. Furthermore, it is challenging to identify apCAFs in human PDAC.5 40 Consistent with previous studies,40 we were unable to identify apCAFs in human PDAC.

This study demonstrated that iCAFs highly expressed TFs from the heme and oxidative homeostasis pathway and AP-1 family and exhibited high activity of corresponding motifs, which are involved in cellular response to oxidative stress. During tumour progression, heme catabolism acts as a double-edged sword.41 Heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), the rate-limiting enzyme in heme degradation, plays a key role in adaptive responses to cellular stress and anti-inflammatory effects. Additionally, the heme-binding TF BACH1 (BTB and CNC homology 1) critically regulates heme homeostasis, oxidative stress, senescence, angiogenesis and immune cell differentiation.42 Both HO-1 and BACH1 have been closely linked to tumour prognosis and are recognised as promising therapeutic targets.43 However, their specific roles and therapeutic potential in reprogramming CAF subtypes remain to be elucidated. HIF-1 and AP-1 family are involved in mediating cellular homeostatic responses under hypoxic conditions.44 A deeper understanding of how the HIF-1 and AP-1 family regulate CAF heterogeneity may contribute to the development of novel strategies targeting specific CAF subtypes. Moreover, key components within these pathways were also activated in iPCs, suggesting that targeting these pathways could simultaneously modulate the functions of multiple cell types within the TME. We also validated that IL-1 and TGF-β pathways were primarily activated in iCAFs and myCAFs, respectively.10 45 Biffi et al found IL-1 could induce LIF expression and downstream JAK/STAT activation to generate iCAFs, while TGF-β could antagonise this process and promote differentiation into myCAFs,45 supporting the plasticity across CAF subtypes. In this study, we speculated that csCAFs may represent an early-stage iCAF subtype that can differentiate into iCAFs during PDAC progression, with the following supporting evidence: (1) The transcriptional profiles and epigenetic features of csCAFs and iCAFs showed considerable overlap; (2) Both the pseudotime analysis and RNA velocity analysis inferred the developmental trajectory from csCAFs to iCAFs; (3) Spatially resolved analysis revealed the close proximity of csCAFs and iCAFs, with interacting more closely with immune cells in the stroma; (4) During pancreatic carcinogenesis, csCAFs exhibited a declining trend, while iCAFs showed an increasing trend. However, alternative explanations for the relationship between csCAFs and iCAFs—including a shared progenitor origin—remain to be explored. Accumulating studies have suggested that reprogramming tumour-promoting CAF subtypes into tumour-restraining CAF subtypes presents a promising strategy to inhibit tumour growth.7 46 Here, we interpret the association between csCAF-rich tumours and improved survival as reflecting a less activated, less reactive and consequently tumour-restraining stromal state. Therefore, we hypothesise that antagonising the transition of csCAFs into iCAFs may be a promising strategy.

Recently, the intratumour CAF heterogeneity and their crosstalk with other cells within the subTMEs have attracted increasing attention.47 We found that CAF subtypes composition varied across different subTMEs through Stereo-seq analysis. SubTMEs with high expression of TGF-β ligands were enriched in myCAFs and that subTMEs with higher csCAFs composition had fewer M2 TAM and higher levels of lipid mediators. These findings suggested that TGF-β ligands promoted myCAF formation, and that csCAFs correlated with an immune-supportive TME, further supporting that PDAC subTMEs were driven by spatially defined crosstalk between distinct CAF subtypes and immune phenotypes.48 Previous understanding of the PDAC TME has been limited to primary tumours, with little known about the TME of liver and lung metastases. In our study, we found that, compared with the primary PDAC, liver metastasis exhibited more csCAFs and fewer myCAFs, while lung metastasis showed enrichment of iCAFs. Spatially resolved analysis identified the CXCL12-CXCR4 ligand–receptor pair between csCAFs and T cells. Moreover, we found that CXCR4+Treg and CXCR4+TIM3+ CD8 T were enriched in liver metastasis. As such, we speculate that csCAFs might further attract tumour cells and immune cells to liver metastasis by secreting CXCL12.37 Lignitto et al49 found human metastatic lung cancer displayed high levels of HO-1 (HMOX1) and BACH1, key regulators of the heme and oxidative homeostasis pathway, which were associated with poor survival. We found HO-1 and BACH1 were significantly upregulated in iCAFs. Therefore, enrichment of iCAFs in the lung metastasis may contribute to the elevated levels of HO-1 and BACH1. The differences in CAF subtypes between liver and lung metastasis highlight the potential of different therapeutic strategies targeting the stroma. The limitations of this study are discussed in the online supplemental discussion.

Overall, we systematically delineated the CAF landscape in pancreatic cancer using multiomics analysis. We revealed that csCAFs may represent an early-stage iCAF subtype, possess the potential to differentiate into iCAFs and are associated with a better prognosis.
