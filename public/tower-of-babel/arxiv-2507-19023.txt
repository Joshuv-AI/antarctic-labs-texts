Modulus of continuity for solutions of non-local heat equations
Ben Andrews, Sophie Chen

Abstract
. We extend the method of modulus of continuity for solutions of parabolic equations—as used, for instance, to prove the Fundamental Gap Conjecture—to solutions of non-local heat equations on ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} and in dimension one with a non-local Neumann boundary condition. Specifically, we show that if a solution of a non-local heat equation has an initial modulus of continuity satisfying simple criteria, then this modulus of continuity is preserved at all subsequent times. In the process of trying to generalise our result in one dimension, we found a counterexample suggesting that a non-local analogue of the Payne-Weinberger inequality would depend on more than the diameter of a bounded (convex) domain.

1.
Introduction and main results

For the remainder of this chapter, let
L
denote the non-local linear operator

(1.1)

\displaystyle Lu(x):=\int_{\Omega}\rho(z-x)(u(z)-y(x))dz,\qquad x\in\Omega,

where the function
u
belongs to a suitable function space,
\Omega
is an open set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the kernel
\rho
is a non-negative, non-increasing, and rotationally symmetric function that depends only on the distance between two points and rapidly decays to zero at infinity. For concreteness, one can think of the singular kernel that appears in the non-local fractional Laplacian. We are interested in showing that if a solution
u(x,t)
of the corresponding non-local heat equation

(1.2)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} u_{t}(x,t)&=Lu(u,t),&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,\infty)\\
u_{0}(x),&=u(x,0)&x\in\Omega\end{aligned}\right.

has an initial modulus of continuity with certain basic properties, then this modulus of continuity is preserved at all subsequent times. The three cases of interest are the following:

1.

\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
with no boundary condition;

2.

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded convex domain, giving rise to the ‘regional’ heat equation; and

3.

\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
with no boundary condition, but we add a non-linearity to the non-local operator.

These choices were made on the basis of their potential future applications to (conjecture and) prove, for instance, a non-local version of the Payne-Weinberger inequality. In the first case, we prove a modulus of continuity theorem for solutions of the non-local heat equation on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(Section
2
). It turns out, however, that for our choice of non-local Neumann boundary condition, there exist counterexamples to the second eigenvalue having a sharp lower bound that only depends on the diameter of the domain. This seems to imply that solutions of the regional heat equation in the second case only have the modulus of continuity property described above in one-dimension (Section
3
). Finally, the third non-linear case was included as a natural extension of the techniques employed in the proof of the first case (Section
4
).

In all three cases, the method of proof by contradiction employed follows a structure extensively used to prove similar propositions for solutions of classical heat equations; see
[
2
,
1
,
3
,
4
]
. Recall that a non-negative real-valued function
\omega:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is a modulus of continuity for a function
f:\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
if
\omega
is continuous at zero and vanishes there, and satisfies

(1.3)

|f(y)-f(x)|\leq 2\omega\left(\frac{|y-x|}{2}\right)

for all points
x,y
in
\Omega
. Suppose
u
is a solution of the non-local heat equation (
1.2
) and
\varphi:[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is a real-valued function satisfying simple criteria.
We want to demonstrate that if
\varphi(\cdot,0)
is an initial modulus of continuity for
u(\cdot,0)
on
\Omega
, then
\varphi(\cdot,t)
is a modulus of continuity for
u(\cdot,t)
at all subsequent times. More precisely, for any two points
x
and
y
in
\Omega
and a time
t\geq 0
, we wish to show that

|u(y,t)-u(x,t)|\leq 2\varphi\left(\frac{|y-x|}{2},t\right).

By defining an auxiliary function
Z_{\epsilon}:\Omega\times\Omega\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
given by

Z_{\epsilon}(x,y,t)=u(y,t)-u(x,t)-2\varphi\left(\frac{|y-x|}{2},t\right)-\epsilon e^{t}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}),

it suffices to show that
Z_{\epsilon}<0
for each
\epsilon>0
. The cases
t=0
,
x=y
, or when at least one of
x
or
y
is sufficiently large in magnitude will follow directly from the properties of
\varphi
that we specify. Most of the work therefore centres around showing the inequality holds when
x
and
y
are distinct and suitably small, and
t>0
. This is enabled by using a maximum principle.

We now turn to the results of this chapter, starting with a modulus of continuity theorem for solutions of a non-local heat equation on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2.
Non-local heat equation on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

Let
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a solution of the non-local heat equation

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} u_{t}(x,t)&=Lu(x,t),&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(0,\infty)\\
u_{0}(x),&=u(x,0),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{aligned}\right.

that is sufficiently smooth. The aim of this section is to prove a modulus of continuity theorem for solutions of equation (
2.1
):

Theorem 2.2

(Non-local modulus of continuity on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
)
.

Suppose
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies the non-local heat equation (
2.1
) and
\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq 1
. Let
\varphi:[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a function with the following properties:

(a.)

\varphi^{\prime}>0
and
\lim\limits_{r\to 0^{+}}\varphi(r,t)=\varphi(0,t)=0
for each
t\geq 0
;

(b.)

\varphi(\cdot,0)
is a modulus of continuity for
u(\cdot,0)
; and

(c.)

\varphi
has an odd extension

(2.3)

\tilde{\varphi}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\varphi(r),&r\geq 0\\
-\varphi(-r),&r<0\end{array}\right.

satisfying

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\pm\infty}\tilde{\varphi}(r)=\pm 1

and the one-dimensional non-local heat equation

(2.4)

\tilde{\varphi}_{t}(r,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{\rho}(w)(\tilde{\varphi}(r+w,t)-\tilde{\varphi}(r,t))dw,r\in\mathbb{R},t>0,

where
\tilde{\rho}(w)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\rho(w,p)dp
.

Then
\varphi(\cdot,t)
is a modulus of continuity for
u(\cdot,t)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,\infty)
.

Proof.

Fix two points
x
and
y
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a time
t\geq 0
. We want to show that

(2.5)

|u(y,t)-u(x,y)|\leq 2\varphi\left(\frac{|y-x|}{2},t\right).

By defining an auxiliary function
Z_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
given by

(2.6)

Z_{\epsilon}(x,y,t)=u(y,t)-u(x,t)-2\varphi\left(\frac{|y-x|}{2},t\right)-\epsilon e^{t}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}),

it suffices to show that
Z_{\epsilon}<0
for each
\epsilon>0
. The cases
t=0
,
x=y
, or when at least one of
x
or
y
is sufficiently large in magnitude follow directly from the properties of
\varphi
or
u
. We thus focus on the case where
x
and
y
are distinct and suitably small, and
t>0
.

To generate a contradiction, suppose that for some
\epsilon_{0}>0
,
Z_{\epsilon_{0}}
is positive at some point. It follows that there exists a first time
t_{0}>0
and a pair of sufficiently small and distinct points
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

such that
Z_{\epsilon_{0}}(x_{0},y_{0},t_{0})=0
, and
Z_{\epsilon_{0}}(x_{0},y_{0},t)<Z_{\epsilon_{0}}(x_{0},y_{0},t_{0})
for
t<t_{0}
. We refer to this as a maximum principle.
Thus, the time derivative of
Z_{\epsilon_{0}}
at the maximum point
(x_{0},y_{0},t_{0})
satisfies the inequality

(2.7)

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{\partial Z_{\epsilon_{0}}}{\partial t}\Big|_{(x_{0},y_{0},t_{0})}

\displaystyle=u_{t}(y,t)-u_{t}(x,t)-2\varphi_{t}\left(\frac{|x_{-}y|}{2},t\right)

\displaystyle-\epsilon_{0}e^{t}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})\Big|_{(x_{0},y_{0},t_{0})}.

For simplicity, write
\epsilon=\epsilon_{0}
,
x=x_{0},y=y_{0},s=|x-y|/2
,
Z=Z_{\epsilon}
, and suppress
t
-dependence in
u
and
\varphi
. Substituting equation (
2.1
) into inequality (
2.7
) gives

(2.8)

\displaystyle 0\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(z-y)(u(z)-u(y))dz-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(z-x)(u(z)-u(x))dz

\displaystyle-2\varphi_{t}(s)-\epsilon e^{t}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}).

We wish to generate a contradiction to inequality (
2.8
). Simply rearranging the integrands and applying
the maximum principle to the result does not work as all terms cancel except
-2\varphi_{t}
. To avoid this problem, our strategy is to introduce
\varphi_{t}
into the integrals via condition (c), which will enable us to cancel it out.

First, we make a change of variables
z=r+x
(or
z=r+y
), and choose coordinates so that the first standard unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by
e_{1}=\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}
. Then for any vector
r
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
relative to a reference point
x
, its reflection in the hyperplane through
x
and perpendicular to the chord
\overline{xy}
joining
x
and
y
is given by

(2.9)

\displaystyle Rr=r-2\langle r,e_{1}\rangle e_{1},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes dot product. Since the kernel
\rho
only depends on the distance between two points, it follows that
\rho(r)=\rho(|r|)=\rho(|Rr|)=\rho(Rr)
, so we can rewrite inequality (
2.8
) using the relationship

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(r)u(y+r)dr=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(r)u(y+Rr)dr

to give

\displaystyle 0\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(r)(u(y+Rr)-u(x+r))dr-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(r)(u(y)-u(x))dr

(2.10)

\displaystyle-2\varphi_{t}(s)-\epsilon e^{t}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}).

Next, observe that given a vector
r
such that
z=x+r
, there exists a vector
r^{\prime}
joining
x
to the reflection of
z
in the hyperplane perpendicular to the mid-point of the chord
\overline{xy}
so that

(2.11)

\displaystyle x+r

\displaystyle=y+Rr^{\prime}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad y+Rr=x+r^{\prime}.

See Figure
1
.

Figure 1.

Moreover, we can partition
\mathbb{R}^{n}
into three mutually disjoint sets

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle:=\left\{r\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle r,e_{1}\rangle=\frac{|x-y|}{2}\right\}

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle:=\left\{r\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle r,e_{1}\rangle>\frac{|x-y|}{2}\right\}

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle:=\left\{r\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle r,e_{1}\rangle<\frac{|x-y|}{2}\right\}.

Using the equations of (
2.11
) in the first integral of inequality (
2
), evaluated over the integration region
S
, implies that
r=r^{\prime}
; thus,

\displaystyle\int_{S}\rho(r)(u(y+Rr)-u(x+r))dr

\displaystyle=\int_{S}\rho(r)(u(x+r^{\prime})-u(x+r))dr

\displaystyle=\int_{S}\rho(r)(u(x+r)-u(x+r))dr

\displaystyle=0.

Otherwise,
r\neq r^{\prime}
, and the vectors belong to different half-planes
R
and
L
. To see this, suppose that
r
belongs to
L
. Using equations (
2.11
) and (
2.9
), and writing
r_{1}=\langle r,e_{1}\rangle
and
r^{\prime}_{1}=\langle r^{\prime}_{1},e_{1}\rangle
, we have

\displaystyle r^{\prime}=-(y-x)+r+2r^{\prime}_{1}e_{1},

which implies

\displaystyle r^{\prime}_{1}=-|y-x|+r_{1}+2r^{\prime}_{1},

or

\displaystyle r^{\prime}_{1}=|y-x|-r_{1}>\frac{|x-y|}{2},

since
r_{1}<\frac{|x-y|}{2}
. Hence,
r^{\prime}
belongs to
R
. (Of course, we can interchange the roles of
r
and
r^{\prime}
, and
R
and
L
, in the above argument.) Thus, inequality (
2
) becomes

\displaystyle 0\leq

\displaystyle\int_{L}\rho(r)(u(y+Rr)-u(x+r))dr+\int_{R}\rho(r)(u(y+Rr)-u(x+r))dr

(2.12)

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(r)(u(y)-u(x))dr-\varphi_{t}-\epsilon e^{t}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}).

Now given a vector
r
in
R
, define a map
g:r^{\prime}\mapsto r
, where
r^{\prime}
is in
L
, so that using equations (
2.11
), we can change variables
r=y-x+Rr^{\prime}
and re-write the integrand in the second integral of inequality (
2
) as

\displaystyle\rho(r)(u(y+Rr)-u(x+r))=\rho(g(r^{\prime}))(u(x+r^{\prime})-u(y+Rr^{\prime})).

This allows us to combine the first two integrals over a common integration region
L
giving

\displaystyle 0\leq

\displaystyle\int_{L}(\rho(r)-\rho(g(r)))(u(y+Rr)-u(x+r))dr-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(r)(u(y)-u(x))dr

\displaystyle-\varphi_{t}-\epsilon e^{t}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}).

Observe that since
r\in L
,
g(r)\in R
, and
\rho
is a non-negative, non-increasing, rotationally symmetric kernel,
\rho(r)\geq\rho(g(r))
, so
\rho(r)-\rho(g(r))\geq 0
. Hence, we may apply the maximum principle
to both integrals yielding

\displaystyle 0\leq

\displaystyle\int_{L}(\rho(r)-\rho(g(r)))\left(2\varphi\left(\frac{|y-x|}{2}-r_{1}\right)+\epsilon e^{t}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})\right)dr

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(r)\left(2\varphi\left(\frac{|x-y|}{2}\right)+\epsilon e^{t}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})\right)dr-\varphi_{t}-\epsilon e^{t}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}).

Finally, to introduce
\varphi_{t}
into the calculation, we need to split up the first
integral into an integral over
L
and an integral over
R
. We thus perform another change of variables
r=y-x+Rr^{\prime}
, again using (
2.11
), and calculate

\displaystyle\frac{|x-y|}{2}-r_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{|y-x|}{2}-\langle r,e_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{|y-x|}{2}-\langle y-x+Rr^{\prime},e_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{|y-x|}{2}+r_{1}^{\prime},

to finally arrive at

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\int_{L}\rho(r)\left(2\varphi\left(\frac{|y-x|}{2}-r_{1}\right)+\epsilon e^{t}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})\right)dr

\displaystyle-\int_{R}\rho(r)\left(2\varphi\left(-\left(\frac{|y-x|}{2}-r_{1}\right)\right)+\epsilon e^{t}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})\right)dr

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(r)\left(2\varphi\left(\frac{|y-x|}{2}\right)+\epsilon e^{t}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})\right)dr-\varphi_{t}-\epsilon e^{t}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(r)\left(\tilde{\varphi}\left(\frac{|y-x|}{2}-r_{1}\right)-\tilde{\varphi}\left(\frac{|y-x|}{2}\right)\right)dr-\varphi_{t}-\epsilon e^{t}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})

\displaystyle=2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{\rho}(\xi)\left(\tilde{\varphi}\left(\frac{|y-x|}{2}-\xi\right)-\tilde{\varphi}\left(\frac{|y-x|}{2}\right)\right)d\xi-\varphi_{t}-\epsilon e^{t}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})

\displaystyle=2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{\rho}(\xi)\left(\tilde{\varphi}\left(\frac{|y-x|}{2}+\xi\right)-\tilde{\varphi}\left(\frac{|y-x|}{2}\right)\right)d\xi-\varphi_{t}-\epsilon e^{t}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\varphi}_{t}-\tilde{\varphi}_{t}-\epsilon e^{t}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})

\displaystyle<0,

a contradiction. We used equation (
2.3
) to give the second inequality; the definition of
\tilde{\rho}
and a change of variables to produce the first and second equalities, respectively; and finally the non-local condition (
2.4
) for
\tilde{\varphi}
to yield the contradiction.

Hence,
Z_{\epsilon}<0
for each
\epsilon>0
, as desired.

3.
Regional heat equation

Restrict the non-local heat equation (
2
) to a bounded convex set
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, so yielding the ‘regional’ heat equation:

(3.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} u_{t}(x,t)&=Lu(x,t),&x\in\Omega,t>0\\
u(x,0)&=u_{0}(x),&x\in\Omega,t=0.\end{aligned}\right.

We show that while a modulus of continuity theorem holds for solutions of equation (

) on a compact interval in
\mathbb{R}
, this estimate does not generalise to higher dimensions.

3.1.
One-dimensional case

Let
I
denote the compact line interval
[-D/2,D/2]

Theorem 3.2

(Non-local modulus of continuity on a compact interval)
.

Suppose that
u:I\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies the one-dimensional non-local heat equation

(3.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} u_{t}(x,t)&=Lu(x,t),&x\in I,t>0\\

and
\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}<1
. Let
\varphi:[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a function with the following properties:

(a.)

\varphi^{\prime}>0
and
\lim\limits_{r\to 0^{+}}\varphi(r,t)=\varphi(0,t)=0
for each
t\geq 0
;

(b.)

\varphi(\cdot,0)
is a modulus of continuity for
u(\cdot,0)
; and

(c.)

\varphi
has an odd extension

(3.4)

\tilde{\varphi}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\varphi(r),&r\geq 0\\

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\pm\infty}\tilde{\varphi}(r)=\pm 1

(1.) Fix any two points

\epsilon>0

If

. We construct a proof by contradiction to handle the non-trivial case

3.2.
General case

Theorem
3.2
does not appear to generalise to bounded convex domains in higher dimensions. If it were to hold, then by adapting the proof of the classical Payne-Weinberger inequality using a modulus of continuity argument (reviewed below), we would be forced to conclude that the first non-trivial eigenvalue of the regional fractional Laplacian on a bounded convex domain
is optimally bounded below by a quantity that depends only on a geometric invariant (under rigid transformations) of the corresponding one-dimensional problem on a compact line interval, i.e., the length of the interval. On the other hand, it is possible to construct a counterexample to show the first nontrivial eigenvalue cannot only depend on the diameter of the domain.

We first consider the counterexample, followed by showing that this would appear to lead to a contradiction if the generalisation of the modulus of continuity theorem were also to hold.

3.2.1.
Counterexample

It well known that the regional fractional Laplacian has a discrete increasing spectrum of non-negative eigenvalues with corresponding eigenfunctions that form an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Since the first eigenvalue is simple, taking value zero with eigenfunction
1
, the first non-trivial eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}
can be defined using a variational formulation by

(3.14)

\displaystyle 0<\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in H^{s}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}\\
\langle u,1\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}u=0\end{subarray}}\bigg\{\frac{\mathcal{E}_{reg}[u,u]}{\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}\bigg\},

where
u
is an admissible function and
\mathcal{E}_{reg}[\cdot,\cdot]
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{reg}[u,u]=\frac{c_{n}}{2}\iint\limits_{\Omega\times\Omega}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy,x\in\Omega.

Let
\Omega=[0,L]\times[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
, where
0<L<<\epsilon
, and
u(x,y)=y
. We compute

(3.15)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u(x,y)dxdy=\int_{0}^{L}\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}ydydx=0.

Moreover, letting

, and observing that

(3.16)

\displaystyle(y_{1}-y_{2})^{2}\leq((x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}+(y_{1}-y_{2})^{2})^{1+s}=|p-q|^{2+2s}

for all

in

, we additionally have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{reg}[u,u]=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{(u(p)-u(q))^{2}}{|p-q|^{2+2s}}dpdq

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{(y_{1}-y_{2})^{2}}{|p-q|^{2+2s}}dpdq

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}1dpdq

(3.17)

\displaystyle=4L^{2}\epsilon^{2}.

Thus
\int_{\Omega}u=0
and

, so

as an admissible function.

Now, noting that

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}u(x,y)^{2}dxdy

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}y^{2}dydx

(3.18)

\displaystyle=\frac{2L\epsilon^{3}}{3},

and

using inequality (
3.2.1
), we see that for any fixed

,

\displaystyle 0<\lambda_{2}\leq\frac{\mathcal{E}_{reg}(u,u)}{\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}\leq\frac{CL^{2}\epsilon^{2}}{L\epsilon^{3}}=\frac{CL}{\epsilon}\to 0^{+},L\to 0^{+},

where

is a constant that depends on

(since

that only depends on the diameter of

(3.20)

\displaystyle 0<B(\epsilon)\leq\lambda_{2}\leq 0.

This suggests that, in general, the optimal lower bound for
\lambda_{2}
cannot just depend on the diameter of the domain.

-\varphi(-r),&r<0\end{array}\right.

satisfying

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\pm\infty}\tilde{\varphi}(r)=\pm 1

and the one-dimensional non-local heat equation

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}_{t}(r,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{\rho}(w)(\tilde{\varphi}(r+w,t)-\tilde{\varphi}(r,t))dw

(4.4)

\displaystyle=:\tilde{L}\tilde{\varphi}(r,t),

where
\tilde{\rho}(w)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\rho(w,p)dp
.

Then
\varphi(\cdot,t)
is a modulus of continuity for
u(\cdot,t)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,\infty)
.

Proof.

As before, we want to show that

\displaystyle|u(y,t)-u(x,t)|\leq 2\varphi\left(\frac{|x-y|}{2},t\right),

for any pair of points
(x,y)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
and time
t\geq 0
. To this end, we define an auxiliary function
Z_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\displaystyle Z_{\epsilon}(x,y,t)=u(y,t)-u(x,t)-2\varphi\left(\frac{|x-y|}{2},t\right)-\epsilon e^{ct}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}),

where
c
is a positive constant, and our aim is to show that
Z_{\epsilon}<0
for each
\epsilon>0
. The properties of
\varphi
guarantee that this inequality is true for
x=y
or
t=0
. It is also true when at least one of
x
or
y
is sufficiently large in magnitude. Thus, we only need to check it holds when
x
and
y
are sufficiently small and distinct, and
t>0
.

To produce a contradiction, assume that for some
\epsilon_{0}
there exists a first time
t_{0}>0
and distinct points
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
such that
Z_{\epsilon_{0}}(x_{0},y_{0},t_{0})=0
.
By the maximum principle from the proof of Theorem
2.2
,
this point is maximal for all
t\leq t_{0}
. It follows that at the maximum point, the time derivative of
Z_{\epsilon_{0}}
is non-negative. Using the fact that
u
satisfies equation (
4.1
) and
\varphi
satisfies the non-local condition (
(c.)
), we have that

\displaystyle 0\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\partial Z}{\partial t}\Big|_{(x_{0},y_{0},t_{0})}=u_{t}(y,t)-u_{t}(x,t)-2\varphi_{t}-\epsilon e^{ct}\Big|_{(x_{0},y_{0},t_{0})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Lu(y,t)+q(u(y,t),|Du|)-Lu(x,t)-q(u(x,t),|Du|)-2\tilde{L}\tilde{\varphi}-

\displaystyle 2q(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})-c\epsilon e^{ct}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})\Big|_{(x_{0},y_{0},t_{0})}.

For simplicity, let
\epsilon=\epsilon_{0},x=x_{0},y=y_{0},t=t_{0}
, and
Z=Z_{\epsilon}
, and suppress dependence on
t
in
u
and
\varphi
. The integral operator terms
Lu,Lu
, and
\tilde{L}\tilde{\varphi}
cancel using exactly the same argument as that employed in the proof of Theorem
2.2
. Thus, we assume this is done and conclude that

(4.5)

\displaystyle 0\leq q(u(y),|Du(y)|)-q(u(x),|Du(x)|)-2q(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})-c\epsilon e^{ct}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}).

Moreover, at the maximum point, the spatial partial derivatives of
Z
vanish. That is,
for each
i=1,...,n,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial Z}{\partial x_{i}}=-u_{i}(x)+\varphi^{\prime}\frac{y_{i}-x_{i}}{|y-x|}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial Z}{\partial y_{i}}=u_{i}(y)-\varphi^{\prime}\frac{y_{i}-x_{i}}{|y-x|},

so

\displaystyle u_{i}(x)=\varphi^{\prime}\frac{y_{i}-x_{i}}{|x-y|}=u_{i}(y),

which implies that

\displaystyle|Du|=\Big|\varphi^{\prime}\frac{y-x}{|x-y|}\Big|=\varphi^{\prime}

since
\varphi^{\prime}>0
by assumption. Thus, inequality (
4.5
) simplifies to

\displaystyle 0\leq q(u(y),\varphi^{\prime})-q(u(x),\varphi^{\prime})-2q(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})-c\epsilon e^{ct}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}).

Finally, at the maximum point, the maximum principle implies that

\displaystyle\frac{u(y)-u(x)}{2}\leq\frac{2\varphi(s)+\epsilon e^{ct}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})}{2},

where
s:=\frac{|x-y|}{2}
. Since
q
is odd, and concave and increasing for nonnegative values of the first argument, we can calculate

(4.6)

\displaystyle q(u(y),\varphi^{\prime})-q(u(x),\varphi^{\prime})\leq 2q\left(\frac{u(y)-u(x)}{2},\varphi^{\prime}\right)\leq 2q\left(\varphi+\frac{\epsilon e^{ct}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})}{2},\varphi^{\prime}\right).

The concavity of
q
in the first variable implies that it is bounded above by its first Taylor approximation: writing
q(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})=q(p,w)
and recalling that
\Big|\frac{\partial q(p,w)}{\partial p}\Big|<c
, the last term in inequality (
4.6
) satisfies

\displaystyle 2q\left(p+\frac{\epsilon e^{ct}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})}{2},w\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(q(p,w)+\frac{\epsilon e^{ct}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})}{2}\frac{\partial q(p,w)}{\partial p}\right)

\displaystyle<2q(p,w)+c\epsilon e^{ct}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}).

So

\displaystyle q(u(y),\varphi^{\prime})-q(u(x),\varphi^{\prime})-2q(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})<c\epsilon e^{ct}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}),

which means that

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{\partial Z}{\partial t}<c\epsilon e^{ct}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})-c\epsilon e^{ct}(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})=0,

a contradiction.

Thus,
Z_{\epsilon}<0
for every
\epsilon>0
, as claimed.
∎
