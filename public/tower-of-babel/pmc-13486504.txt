Hybrid fuzzy AHP–WASPAS optimization of turning parameters for electronic waste-derived aluminum alloys: advancing sustainable machining in line with sustainable development goals and circular manufacturing
Authors: S. P. Sundar Singh Sivam, V. G. Umasekar, Stalin Kesavan, P. Sathishkumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents a sustainable machining optimization framework for waste-derived Al–Cu–Mg alloys reinforced with zirconium dioxide (ZrO₂). A hybrid Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process–Weighted Aggregated Sum Product Assessment framework was applied to optimize turning parameters, including cutting speed of 11–42 m/min, feed rate of 0.05–0.15 mm/rev, and depth of cut of 0.5–1.0 mm. Multiple machining responses were evaluated, including surface roughness, material removal rate, tool wear, power consumption, dimensional accuracy, specific cutting energy, and chip volume. Three die-cast alloys were fabricated from recovered metallic waste streams: Sample A with 91% Al, 4.5% Cu, 1.8% Mg, and 2% ZrO₂; Sample B with 87.3% Al, 4.9% Cu, 1.8% Mg, and 6% ZrO₂; and Sample C with 82.5% Al, 5.5% Cu, 2% Mg, and 10% ZrO₂. The experimental trials were planned using a D-optimal Response Surface Methodology design, and the machining alternatives were ranked using the hybrid Fuzzy AHP–WASPAS model. The final ranking identified Alternative 8, corresponding to Sample B with 6 wt.% ZrO₂, as the overall optimum condition because it provided the best compromise among surface quality, productivity, tool wear, energy demand, dimensional accuracy, and material utilization. Compared with the unoptimized baseline condition, the optimum condition improved surface finish by 25%, increased material removal rate by 30%, and reduced tool wear by 15%. The superior performance of Sample B is attributed to balanced ZrO₂-induced grain refinement, improved reinforcement dispersion, reduced agglomeration, and stable tool–workpiece interaction. Although Run 3 showed the best energy-specific response, it was not the overall optimum because the final decision was based on simultaneous multi-response optimization. The proposed framework demonstrates a practical route for converting waste-derived aluminum alloy streams into value-added machinable composites while supporting energy-efficient and circular manufacturing aligned with SDG 9 and SDG 12.

Introduction

Sustainable manufacturing requires materials and machining routes that reduce waste generation, energy consumption, and dependence on primary raw materials while maintaining engineering performance. Aluminum alloys are widely used because of their low density, corrosion resistance, castability, and favorable strength-to-weight ratio. At the same time, electronic waste and industrial aluminum scrap offer secondary metallic streams that can be recovered and converted into value-added aluminum-based alloys. For such recycled alloys to become viable engineering materials, their composition, reinforcement content, microstructure, impurity level, casting quality, and machinability must be controlled. In this context, Al–Cu–Mg alloys reinforced with zirconium dioxide (ZrO₂) provide a promising route because ceramic reinforcement can improve hardness, wear resistance, grain refinement, and dimensional stability during machining.

The role of oxide particles in modifying material behavior has been widely recognized. The work in1 showed that nanoparticle concentration, dispersion, and oxide interaction strongly influence functional material performance, establishing the importance of controlling reinforcement distribution in particle-containing systems. This understanding was extended in2, where oxide carriers were shown to modify surface functionality and process behavior in Cu–ZnO-based systems, indicating that oxide phases can affect both material structure and response. A related investigation in3 demonstrated that doped and undoped zinc–zirconium oxides improve functional behavior, showing that zirconium-containing oxides are active modifiers rather than passive secondary phases. The comparative oxide analysis in4 involving HfO₂, ZrO₂, and SiO₂ further emphasized that oxide phases influence interfacial and electrostatic characteristics, which is relevant to reinforcement–matrix interaction in aluminum composites. Building from this, ZrO₂-containing Cu–ZnO systems supported on Mg–Al layered structures in5 showed improved process performance, while Zr–Al and Zr–Ti supported systems in6 confirmed that zirconium-based phases affect material stability. The doping study in7 further showed that Cu and Al can alter ZrO₂-based material characteristics, supporting the use of ZrO₂ within Al–Cu-based alloy systems.

The connection between ZrO₂ and aluminum-based materials becomes clearer in studies focused on Al–Cu and Al–Cu–Mg systems. ZrO₂ nanomaterials improved wettability and interfacial characteristics in Al–Cu-based filler metals in8, which is important because wettability directly influences particle bonding and load transfer in reinforced alloys. The role of Cu/Zn/Al/Zr precursor phases in9 further showed that zirconium-containing phases affect final material behavior, while the surface-intermediate mechanisms examined in10 provided insight into interfacial reactions that can influence bonding quality. In aluminum alloy systems, microstructural tailoring of Al–Zn–Mg–Cu alloys using Si and ZrO₂ improved mechanical performance in11, showing that ZrO₂ can contribute to alloy strengthening. This was supported by12, where ZrO₂-containing Cu/ZnO/Al₂O₃ systems demonstrated the importance of zirconium phases in multi-component materials. More directly, nano-ZrO₂-reinforced Al–Cu–Mg alloys in13 showed improved strength and stability, providing a strong basis for selecting Al–Cu–Mg–ZrO₂ alloys in the present work. The investigation of Cu/ZnO/ZrO₂/Al₂O₃ compositions in14 further confirmed that ZrO₂ changes material response in complex systems. Al–Cu–TiO₂–ZrO₂ composites in15 showed improved microstructure, hardness, and wear resistance, confirming that ZrO₂-containing aluminum composites can offer better mechanical behavior. The effect of Zr/Al ratio in16 showed that reinforcement proportion must be carefully controlled, while the hardness evaluation in17 confirmed that microstructural features directly affect mechanical response. Zr-based surface modification improved adhesion and corrosion resistance in Al–Cu galvanic couples in18, which further supports the importance of zirconium-assisted interfacial control. Mg–Al-based precursor systems in19 added understanding of multi-component oxide chemistry, and additive friction stir deposition of metal matrix composites in20 showed that reinforcement distribution is essential for stable composite performance. Together, [1–20] indicate that ZrO₂ can improve grain refinement, interface quality, hardness, and wear resistance, but excessive or poorly distributed reinforcement may cause agglomeration, porosity, and reduced ductility.

Overall, prior work shows that ZrO₂ can improve interfacial stability, grain refinement, hardness, strength, and wear resistance in aluminum-based composites; machining optimization can improve surface quality, material removal rate, tool wear, power consumption, dimensional accuracy, specific cutting energy, and chip volume; and recycling-based manufacturing can support circular-economy goals. Despite these advances, limited work has integrated e-waste-derived Al–Cu–Mg alloy fabrication, ZrO₂ reinforcement, microstructural characterization, turning-response evaluation, and hybrid RSM–Fuzzy AHP–WASPAS optimization within a single experimental framework. The present study addresses this gap by fabricating recycled Al–Cu–Mg–ZrO₂ alloys with different ZrO₂ contents and optimizing their turning performance using D-optimal RSM and Fuzzy AHP–WASPAS.

Despite significant advances in alloy design, machining optimization, and sustainable manufacturing, research integrating waste-derived Al–Cu–ZrO₂ alloys with multi-criteria machining optimization remains limited. Existing studies primarily focus on mechanical property improvement or process-specific optimization, often overlooking sustainability aspects such as energy consumption, tool wear reduction, and the use of industrial waste as raw material. The role of zirconium dioxide as a reinforcement in aluminum–copper–magnesium alloys is also underexplored, particularly regarding its influence on machinability, microstructural stability, and environmental performance. Additionally, few studies adopt a comprehensive methodology that combines experimental design approaches, such as Response Surface Methodology (RSM), with advanced multi-criteria decision-making tools like Fuzzy AHP and WASPAS to optimize multiple machining objectives—including surface quality, material removal rate (MRR), tool wear, and energy efficiency—within the framework of sustainable manufacturing.

The present study addresses these gaps by systematically analyzing die-cast Al–Cu–Mg alloys fabricated from industrial waste with varying zirconium dioxide content. A hybrid Fuzzy AHP-WASPAS optimization framework is applied to establish process parameters that achieve balanced mechanical and machining performance while improving resource efficiency and energy utilization. The results demonstrate enhanced surface finish, increased MRR, reduced tool wear, and improved process stability. Academically, this study contributes to circular economy-based alloy machining knowledge, and industrially, it provides a practical, resource-conscious framework aligned with SDGs 9 and 12, promoting sustainable, cost-efficient, and energy-aware manufacturing practices.

Materials and methods

Sample fabrication by E-waste and measurement techniques

The elemental and oxide composition of household electronic waste (E-waste) was identified in accordance with standard characterization practices (ASTM E1479, ISO 11420) in Fig. 1. Aluminum (Al) is predominantly recovered from structural and casing components such as laptop or PC housings, mobile phone frames, heat sinks, and power supply casings, typically through shredding followed by physical separation and re-melting into secondary aluminum alloys. Copper (Cu), mainly present in conductive wiring and coils within power cables, PCB tracks, connectors, transformers, motors, and charging cables, is isolated through mechanical separation and subsequently refined via conventional smelting or electrolytic methods. Magnesium (Mg), commonly used in lightweight alloy casings for mobile phones, cameras, and laptop bodies, is separated along with aluminum during alloy recycling streams. Zirconium (Zr), found in ceramic coatings, insulators, and high-end capacitors, is usually recovered in the form of zirconium oxide (ZrO₂) during specialized material processing. Oxygen, present primarily as oxides in ceramics such as alumina (Al₂O₃), silica (SiO₂), zirconia (ZrO₂), and magnesia (MgO), is obtained in its oxide form during metallurgical recovery. This systematic approach ensures compliance with international standards for E-waste characterization and recovery.Fig. 1E-waste materials, components, and forms of recovery.

E-waste materials, components, and forms of recovery.

To quantify the efficiency of the e-waste recovery and recycling route, a mass-balance analysis was carried out for each alloy composition. For every sample, the initial metallic e-waste charge was fixed at 1.25 kg. After melting, refining, slag/dross removal, argon-protected casting, and solidification, the final usable cast alloy mass was measured. The recovery yield was calculated as the ratio of final usable cast alloy mass to the initial metallic e-waste charge. Melt loss was determined from the difference between the initial charge and the final usable cast alloy mass. This analysis provides quantitative evidence for recovery efficiency, material loss during melting, and circular material-loop closure in the recycled Al–Cu–Mg–ZrO₂ alloy system.

The e-waste recovery and alloy fabrication process was carried out through collection, manual dismantling, cleaning, shredding, physical separation, melting, refining, composition verification, and casting, using household/industrial electronic-waste components such as laptop/PC housings, mobile-phone frames, heat sinks, power-supply casings, wires, connectors, transformer coils, PCB tracks, lightweight alloy casings, and ceramic insulating parts as raw materials; plastics, rubber, glass, batteries, solder-rich residues, coatings, and visibly contaminated non-metallic fractions were removed before metal recovery, and the cleaned fractions were shredded and separated into Al-rich, Cu-rich, Mg-containing, and ZrO₂/ceramic-rich streams using manual sorting, magnetic separation for ferrous contaminants, and density/component-based separation for non-ferrous fractions. For each fabricated sample, the total charge weight was fixed at 1.25 kg. Sample A contained 1.1375 kg Al, 0.05625 kg Cu, 0.0225 kg Mg, and 0.025 kg ZrO₂, corresponding to 91% Al, 4.5% Cu, 1.8% Mg, and 2% ZrO₂, with the remaining 0.00875 kg attributed to trace impurities or minor phases. Sample B contained 1.09125 kg Al, 0.06125 kg Cu, 0.0225 kg Mg, and 0.075 kg ZrO₂, corresponding to 87.3% Al, 4.9% Cu, 1.8% Mg, and 6% ZrO₂. Sample C contained 1.03125 kg Al, 0.06875 kg Cu, 0.025 kg Mg, and 0.125 kg ZrO₂, corresponding to 82.5% Al, 5.5% Cu, 2% Mg, and 10% ZrO₂. The Al-rich recovered fraction was used as the base matrix, while Cu- and Mg-containing fractions were added according to the required alloy composition, and ZrO₂ was incorporated as the ceramic reinforcement phase. The prepared charge was melted in a stir-casting furnace at 850 °C under continuous argon shielding to reduce oxidation and gas absorption; after complete melting, slag and dross were removed from the melt surface, and the molten alloy was stirred at 700 rpm to improve chemical homogeneity. Preheated ZrO₂ particles were gradually introduced into the vortex region of the molten alloy to improve reinforcement distribution and reduce particle agglomeration. The refined molten composite was poured into a preheated die using the bottom-pouring method and allowed to solidify under natural convection. Impurity control was performed during preprocessing, melting, and post-casting verification by removing non-metallic fractions, avoiding solder-rich residues, maintaining argon protection, skimming slag/dross, and verifying the final alloy composition using Optical Emission Spectroscopy in accordance with ISO 17054. The recovery yield was evaluated from the ratio of usable cast alloy mass to the processed metallic e-waste charge mass, while melt loss was determined from the difference between the initial 1.25 kg charge and the final cast mass after slag and dross removal; this mass-balance approach supports the reproducibility of the e-waste recovery route and strengthens the circular-manufacturing objective of the study. The sustainability performance of the developed hybrid composite was evaluated through recovery yield, melt loss, and circularity metrics. The total batch weight used during stir casting was 1.2 kg, whereas the final usable casting weight after solidification and slag removal was approximately 1.145 kg. The recovery yield of the developed composite was calculated as 95.42%, while the melt loss was limited to 4.58%, indicating efficient material utilization during fabrication. Furthermore, the incorporation of fish bone ash and sea shell powder contributed approximately 5% waste-derived reinforcement content to the overall composite composition, demonstrating the potential of the developed material system toward circular manufacturing and sustainable resource utilization.

Selection of responses

In turning operations, the selection of responses is crucial to evaluating machining performance. Surface Roughness (µm) represents the quality of the machined surface, influencing product durability and functionality. MRR (cm3/min) indicates productivity by measuring the volume of material removed per unit time. Tool Wear (µm) directly affects tool life and machining cost, making it a critical parameter for sustainability. Power Consumption (W) determines the energy efficiency of the process, which is essential for cost-effective and eco-friendly manufacturing. Accuracy (%) ensures the dimensional precision of the machined component, impacting assembly and operational reliability. Specific Cutting Energy (J/cm3) measures the energy required to remove a unit volume of material, reflecting machining efficiency. Chip Volume (cm3) provides insights into chip formation characteristics, which affect tool performance and material utilization.

Experimental response standards

Each response in turning operations has specific experimental standards to ensure accurate evaluation. Surface Roughness is typically measured using a profilometer following ISO 4287 or ASME B46.1 standards. Material Removal Rate is calculated based on feed rate, depth of cut, and cutting speed, adhering to machining productivity benchmarks. Tool Wear is assessed using optical or scanning electron microscopy as per ISO 8688-1 for tool life evaluation. Power Consumption is measured using wattmeters or energy meters in compliance with ISO 14955 for energy-efficient machining. Accuracy is determined using coordinate measuring machines (CMMs) against geometric tolerances defined in ISO 230-1. Specific Cutting Energy is derived from force measurements using dynamometers and standardized formulas under ISO 3002-4. Chip Volume is analyzed through high-speed imaging or volumetric calculations following chip formation principles in machining standards.

The Al–Cu–Mg–ZrO₂ composites were fabricated using the stir casting route, as shown in Fig. 2a. The casting setup consisted of an argon gas cylinder, mechanical stirrer, powder heater, and squeeze casting unit. The furnace was heated to 850 °C, and the Al–Cu–Mg alloy charge was gradually introduced into the crucible. Argon gas was supplied continuously during melting to minimize oxidation and gas absorption. After complete melting, the molten alloy was mechanically stirred at 700 rpm to improve melt homogeneity. The ZrO₂ reinforcement particles were preheated before addition to reduce thermal mismatch and improve wettability with the molten matrix. Execution of the hybrid optimization

The hybrid optimization framework integrated Response Surface Methodology (RSM), Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (Fuzzy AHP), and Fuzzy Weighted Aggregated Sum Product Assessment (Fuzzy WASPAS), as shown in Fig. 3a–c, to optimize the turning performance of recycled Al–Cu–Mg–ZrO₂ alloys under sustainable machining conditions. A randomized D-optimal RSM design was adopted to evaluate the effects of cutting speed, feed rate, depth of cut, and alloy composition on surface roughness, material removal rate (MRR), tool wear, power consumption, dimensional accuracy, specific cutting energy, and chip volume. The machining parameters were selected as cutting speed at 11, 26.5, and 42 m/min, feed rate at 0.05, 0.10, and 0.15 mm/rev, and depth of cut at 0.5 and 1.0 mm. The cutting-speed range of 11–42 m/min was deliberately chosen as a low-to-moderate speed domain because the work materials were recycled Al–Cu–Mg alloys reinforced with hard ZrO₂ particles. Compared with unreinforced aluminum alloys, ceramic-reinforced recycled alloys are more susceptible to abrasive tool wear, cutting-edge degradation, reinforcement pull-out, and unstable chip formation at high cutting speeds. Therefore, the selected speed range was intended to limit excessive flank wear, control thermal effects at the tool–workpiece interface, maintain stable chip formation, and generate measurable variation in the machining responses. The three cutting-speed levels of 11, 26.5, and 42 m/min represented the lower, intermediate, and upper stable cutting conditions used in the D-optimal design. All process and response units were standardized throughout the manuscript: cutting speed was expressed in m/min, feed rate in mm/rev, depth of cut in mm, surface roughness and tool wear in µm, MRR in cm3/min, chip volume in cm3, power consumption in W, dimensional accuracy in %, and specific cutting energy in J/cm3. A reduced quadratic model was fitted for each response, and model adequacy was evaluated using analysis of variance (ANOVA), model-fit statistics, lack-of-fit testing, and residual diagnostics. Model significance was assessed using F-values and p-values, with p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance at the 95% confidence level. Lack-of-fit was evaluated independently, where an insignificant lack-of-fit indicated that the residual error was not significantly greater than the pure experimental error. Adjusted R2 and predicted R2 values were compared to assess model robustness and predictive capability, while adequate precision values greater than 4 confirmed sufficient signal strength for navigating the design space. Residual diagnostics, including normal probability plots, residuals versus predicted values, and predicted versus actual plots, were examined to verify normality of error, constant variance, absence of heteroscedasticity, and lack of systematic model bias. The normal probability plots showed approximately linear residual trends, the residual-versus-predicted plots showed random scatter without funnel-shaped patterns, and the predicted-versus-actual plots indicated close agreement between experimental and model-predicted values. These diagnostics confirmed that the reduced quadratic models were suitable for describing the relationship between machining parameters and response variables. Repeated machining conditions in the D-optimal matrix were retained as replicate or confirmation runs to estimate pure experimental error, support lack-of-fit evaluation, and assess response repeatability, which is important for recycled ZrO₂-reinforced aluminum alloys because local variation in reinforcement dispersion, particle–matrix interaction, tool–workpiece contact, and chip formation can affect machining responses. After statistical validation, the RSM response outputs were transferred to the Fuzzy AHP–WASPAS decision-making stage. Fuzzy AHP was used to determine the relative importance of machining responses, while Fuzzy WASPAS was applied to rank the experimental alternatives and identify the optimum alloy composition and turning condition. Surface roughness, tool wear, power consumption, and specific cutting energy were treated as non-beneficial criteria because lower values represent improved surface quality, longer tool life, reduced energy demand, and better machining efficiency. In contrast, MRR, dimensional accuracy, and chip volume were treated as beneficial criteria because higher values indicate improved productivity, dimensional reliability, and effective material removal. Expert judgments were converted into triangular fuzzy numbers using the linguistic scale, and a fuzzy pairwise comparison matrix was constructed to represent the relative importance of the responses. The fuzzy geometric mean method was used to aggregate expert judgments and calculate fuzzy criterion weights, followed by defuzzification and normalization to obtain final crisp weights. In the WASPAS stage, beneficial criteria were normalized by dividing each response value by the maximum value of the corresponding criterion, whereas non-beneficial criteria were normalized by dividing the minimum value of the corresponding criterion by each response value. The Weighted Sum Model score was calculated from the weighted normalized matrix, while the Weighted Product Model score was obtained by raising each normalized value to its corresponding criterion weight and multiplying the resulting terms. The final WASPAS score was computed using an aggregation coefficient of λ = 0.5, assigning equal importance to the additive and multiplicative components. Alternatives were ranked in descending order of final WASPAS score, and sensitivity analysis was performed to examine ranking stability. The results identified Sample B, containing 87.3% Al, 4.9% Cu, 1.8% Mg, and 6% ZrO₂, as the best-performing composition because it provided the most balanced compromise among surface finish, MRR, tool wear, power consumption, dimensional accuracy, specific cutting energy, and chip volume. The integrated RSM–Fuzzy AHP–WASPAS framework therefore provides a statistically validated and decision-based route for selecting machining conditions for recycled ceramic-reinforced aluminum alloys, supporting resource-efficient manufacturing and the objectives of SDG 9 and SDG 12.Fig. 3(a) Experimental design, (b) Fuzzy AHP, (c) Fuzzy WASPAS.

Results and discussion

Chemical composition

The chemical composition of the samples was determined using Optical Emission Spectroscopy (OES) in accordance with ISO 17054. The results revealed that Sample A contained 91% Al, 4.5% Cu, 1.8% Mg, and 2% ZrO₂; Sample B consisted of 87.3% Al, 4.9% Cu, 1.8% Mg, and 6% ZrO₂; while Sample C comprised 82.5% Al, 5.5% Cu, 2% Mg, and 10% ZrO₂.

Microstructure

The three aluminium alloys (Samples A, B, and C) exhibit distinct microstructural features and mechanical properties that highlight the influence of their varying compositions, particularly the inclusion of zirconium dioxide (ZrO₂) as a reinforcement. Sample A (91% Al, 4.5% Cu, 1.8% Mg, 2% ZrO₂) exhibits a microstructure dominated by coarse grains with dendritic structures. The high aluminum content contributes to a relatively soft matrix, while the limited addition of copper and magnesium provides only moderate strengthening through solid-solution strengthening and precipitation hardening. However, the absence of significant secondary reinforcements (such as ZrO₂) limits the extent of grain refinement, resulting in relatively larger grains. This larger grain size is associated with reduced hardness and tensile strength, as the material’s resistance to plastic deformation is lower. However, the increase in ductility is evident, as larger grains typically promote enhanced plastic flow during deformation. This balance between strength and ductility makes Sample A suitable for applications where flexibility is more critical than mechanical strength. Figure 4a illustrates this coarse-grained microstructure, highlighting the reduced hardness and strength of Sample A compared to the other samples. While this sample’s properties favor ductility, its mechanical performance in terms of wear resistance is weaker than the other two samples. Sample B (87.3% Al, 4.9% Cu, 1.8% Mg, 6% ZrO₂) exhibits a more refined microstructure with smaller, more uniform grains, which is attributed to the increased presence of 6% ZrO₂. Zirconium dioxide acts as an effective grain refiner, limiting grain growth during solidification. This grain refinement not only enhances the strength of the matrix but also improves the toughness of the material by hindering grain boundary sliding under mechanical stress. As a result, Sample B demonstrates a better balance between strength and toughness, with improved performance in both machinability and wear resistance. The increased ZrO₂ content in Sample B strengthens the matrix and reduces material deformation under machining stresses, offering improved machinability compared to Sample A, as evidenced by the lower tool wear observed in experiments.Fig. 4Microstructure, (a) Sample A, (b) Sample B, (c) Sample C.

In Fig. 4b, the microstructure shows more uniform and finer grains, confirming the contribution of ZrO₂ as a strengthening phase that enhances the material’s machinability and resistance to wear. This improved balance between strength and ductility makes Sample B the optimal candidate for applications requiring both mechanical robustness and stable machining performance. Sample C (82.5% Al, 5.5% Cu, 2% Mg, 10% ZrO₂) exhibits the most refined microstructure among the three samples. The higher content of 10% ZrO₂ significantly enhances the grain refinement process, resulting in a denser and stronger matrix. The ZrO₂ reinforcement improves the mechanical properties by creating finer grains, which effectively reduce dislocation movement and promote precipitation hardening. The increased copper and magnesium content also contribute to solid solution strengthening, leading to higher hardness and tensile strength. However, the addition of ZrO₂, while beneficial for strengthening, also leads to a slight reduction in ductility due to the brittle nature of the ceramic phase. Figure 4c demonstrates the most refined grain structure in Sample C, contributing to superior strength and wear resistance. This microstructure, with its finely distributed ZrO₂ reinforcement, is more resistant to plastic deformation and wear, making Sample C the most mechanically robust material of the three. As a result, Sample C performs best in strength and wear resistance, but with a slight trade-off in ductility compared to Sample A. Despite this, its superior machinability makes it ideal for applications where high strength and wear resistance are paramount. The presence of zirconium dioxide (ZrO₂) in all samples plays a pivotal role in enhancing machinability and material properties. As the ZrO₂ content increases, the grain refinement becomes more pronounced, leading to improved mechanical strength and wear resistance. This trend is particularly evident in Sample C, which demonstrates the best overall performance in terms of strength and machinability due to the 10% ZrO₂ content.

Moreover, the use of waste-derived alloys like these, which incorporate recycled materials such as e-waste, aligns with sustainable manufacturing practices by reducing the need for primary raw materials and promoting a circular economy. The incorporation of ZrO₂ not only improves the strength and wear resistance of the alloys but also enhances the overall sustainability of the material by improving its performance in machining processes. This reduces the environmental impact of machining and extends the life cycle of the materials used. The comparative study of these samples emphasizes the role of ZrO₂ reinforcement in optimizing both material properties and machining performance, demonstrating that moderate amounts of ZrO₂ (around 6%) provide the best balance between strength, ductility, and machinability. While higher ZrO₂ content (such as in Sample C) provides the greatest strength and wear resistance, it also reduces ductility, which may limit its applications in scenarios requiring high plasticity.

Figure 5 presents the reinforcement distribution characteristics of the recycled Al–Cu–Mg–ZrO₂ alloys. This distribution confirms successful particle incorporation, but the limited particle population reduces the strengthening contribution of ZrO₂ within the aluminium matrix. Figure 5b shows Sample B, which exhibits the most uniform reinforcement distribution among the three samples. The ZrO₂ particles are distributed more homogeneously throughout the matrix, with fewer clustering regions compared with Sample C. This improved dispersion enhances matrix strengthening, restricts localized plastic deformation, and supports stable tool–workpiece interaction during machining. The uniform reinforcement distribution observed in Fig. 5b provides microstructural evidence for the superior balanced performance of Sample B reported in the machining optimization results.Fig. 5Reinforcement Distribution Analysis, (a) Sample A, (b) Sample B, (c) Sample C.

Figure 5c shows Sample C, where several agglomeration zones are clearly visible. These clusters indicate that increasing the ZrO₂ content to a higher level reduces dispersion stability during stir casting. Such agglomerated regions act as local stress-concentration sites and may promote particle pull-out, abrasive tool wear, unstable chip formation, and deterioration of machined surface quality. Therefore, although Sample C contains the highest ZrO₂ content, the non-uniform particle distribution limits its overall machining stability. Overall, Fig. 5a–c confirms that reinforcement distribution is more critical than reinforcement content alone. Sample B demonstrates the optimum distribution condition, where sufficient ZrO₂ is present to improve strengthening without causing severe agglomeration. This explains why the 6% ZrO₂ composition provides the best balance between mechanical response, machinability, tool wear resistance, and sustainable machining performance.

Mechanical property

The mechanical properties of the three aluminum alloys (Samples A, B, and C) reveal a clear trend of increasing strength with higher zirconium dioxide (ZrO₂) content, accompanied by a decrease in ductility. Figure 7a illustrates that Sample A, with the lowest ZrO₂ content (2%), demonstrates moderate strength (~ 350 MPa) and high ductility (~ 16–18% strain). This balance between strength and plasticity is attributed to the precipitation hardening effect from copper (Cu) in the Al₂Cu phase and solid-solution strengthening from magnesium (Mg), allowing the material to undergo significant deformation without fracture. In Fig. 7b, Sample B, which contains 6% ZrO₂, exhibits higher strength (~ 400 MPa) but slightly reduced ductility (~ 14–15%). The addition of ZrO₂ enhances load transfer efficiency and hindrance of dislocation motion, acting as a ceramic reinforcement that refines the grain structure, leading to improved strength but a decrease in plasticity. Figure 7c shows that Sample C, with the highest ZrO₂ content (10%), exhibits the highest strength (~ 450 MPa) but the lowest ductility (~ 12–13%). The increased ZrO₂ content leads to stronger particle–matrix interfaces and grain refinement, significantly enhancing strength. However, the presence of a higher volume of ZrO₂ introduces brittleness, as the ceramic particles tend to cluster, leading to stress concentration and reduced ability to undergo plastic deformation. This trend highlights the trade-off between strength and ductility: as the ZrO₂ content increases, strength improves due to dispersion hardening and grain refinement, but ductility decreases because of the brittle nature of the ceramic phase. The highest strength is observed in Sample C, but at the cost of significant reduction in ductility, while Sample A, with the least ZrO₂, shows better ductility but lower mechanical strength.Fig. 7Mechanical property, (a) Sample A, (b) Sample B, (c) Sample C.

DSC

In Fig. 11b (Sample B), the peak shifts to 0.365 J/g, indicating increased energy storage. This higher peak suggests that the presence of 6% ZrO₂ enhances the material’s interfacial energy storage, with the ZrO₂ particles acting as nucleation sites for precipitation. This results in a higher energy release, as ZrO₂ contributes to stronger precipitation hardening and better overall material strengthening. However, Fig. 11c (Sample C) shows a lower peak of approximately 0.198 J/g, significantly lower than Sample B, indicating a reduced energy storage capacity. The excess ZrO₂ content in Sample C restricts dislocation movement and limits the material’s ability to store additional energy, likely due to the brittle nature of the ceramic phase. The lower peak suggests that excessive ceramic reinforcement hampers the alloy’s plasticity, limiting further energy absorption. The stored energy trend increases from Sample A → B but decreases in Sample C, highlighting that moderate ZrO₂ reinforcement (as in Sample B) improves precipitation and interfacial strengthening, thereby enhancing energy absorption. In contrast, excessive ZrO₂ (as in Sample C) reduces energy storage due to increased brittleness, emphasizing the trade-off between improved strength and reduced plasticity with higher ceramic content. The FTIR, UV–Vis, and DSC results were interpreted in relation to the machining performance of the Al–Cu–Mg–ZrO₂ alloys. The FTIR spectra showed Al–O and Zr–O related absorption features, confirming the presence of oxide bonding and ceramic–metal interaction in the alloy system. This bonding behavior is relevant to machining because improved reinforcement–matrix interaction enhances particle stability during cutting, reduces particle pull-out, and contributes to better surface integrity. Sample B showed stronger Zr–O interaction than Sample A, indicating improved reinforcement incorporation, while avoiding the excessive ceramic concentration observed in Sample C. This supports the lower tool wear and improved surface finish obtained for Sample B. The UV–Vis response also increased with ZrO₂ content, indicating stronger interaction of the alloy surface with incident radiation due to the presence of ceramic oxide phases and increased scattering centers. Although UV–Vis analysis is not a direct machinability test, it provides supporting evidence of surface modification caused by ZrO₂ incorporation, which influences surface response, hardness, and tool–workpiece interaction during machining. Sample C showed the highest absorbance because of higher ZrO₂ content, but excessive ceramic reinforcement increased brittleness and particle clustering, which contributed to higher tool abrasion. DSC analysis provided a stronger link with machining behavior by showing differences in stored energy and thermal response among the samples. Sample B exhibited higher stored energy than Sample A, indicating improved precipitation behavior and interfacial strengthening, which supported stable chip formation and improved cutting performance. In contrast, Sample C showed reduced stored energy, suggesting restricted plastic deformation due to excessive ZrO₂ reinforcement and increased ceramic-induced brittleness. This reduced deformation capacity promotes unstable cutting, increased tool–particle abrasion, and higher wear. Therefore, the combined FTIR, UV–Vis, and DSC results support the machining trends observed experimentally: moderate ZrO₂ reinforcement in Sample B improves bonding, thermal stability, and deformation resistance, whereas excessive ZrO₂ in Sample C increases brittleness, particle clustering, and abrasive tool wear.

Multi-criteria decision validation

Fuzzy AHP consistency ratio check

Fuzzy AHP Consistency Ratio Check ensures that the weight assignments for machining parameters are consistent. This involves verifying the pairwise comparison matrix, where a consistency ratio (CR) of 0.1, lower typically indicates good consistency in the assigned weights, ensuring reliable decision-making in the analysis.

Sensitivity analysis

The Fuzzy WASPAS Sensitivity Analysis evaluates how changes in the weights of criteria affect the final ranking of parameter settings. It tests five scenarios: increasing the original weights by 10%, 15%, and 20%, and decreasing them by 10% and 15%. The goal is to assess how sensitive the rankings are to adjustments in the criteria’s importance. If the rankings remain stable across these scenarios, the model is considered reliable and robust. However, significant changes in rankings indicate that the decision might be too sensitive to weight changes, potentially affecting the model’s consistency and validity.

Life cycle assessment (LCA)

The process involves calculating the environmental impact of machining conditions by combining power consumption and specific cutting energy, which reflect both operational energy demand and material utilization efficiency. In the results, Run 3 (Best) showed a lower LCA impact (192) compared to Run 12 (Worse) with 221, indicating reduced energy-related emissions and lower resource burden. Scientifically, this difference is due to optimized cutting speed and feed in Run 3, which allow effective material removal with minimal excess energy use, thereby reducing indirect CO₂ emissions. The conclusion is that Run 3 is environmentally more sustainable, as it consumes less energy per unit volume of material removed, aligning with eco-efficient manufacturing practices.

Energy efficiency analysis

The process evaluates the inverse of power consumption, where lower power demand indicates higher efficiency and supports SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production). The results showed that Run 12 had a slightly higher energy efficiency index (0.007692) compared to Run 3 (0.006250), because the lower power consumption of 130 W at reduced cutting conditions requires less electrical input. Scientifically, this occurs because machining at moderate speeds reduces energy spikes, though it may sacrifice material removal efficiency. The conclusion is that Run 12 is marginally more energy efficient in terms of pure power demand, but the gain is offset by higher wear and lower material removal efficiency.

Wear debris analysis

The process assesses tool degradation by measuring tool wear, since excessive wear generates debris that may contaminate the machined surface or increase the need for tool replacement. The results showed that Run 3 recorded lower tool wear (8 µm) compared to Run 12 (11 µm). Scientifically, this is explained by the higher cutting speed in Run 3, which promotes stable chip formation and reduces frictional rubbing, while Run 12’s slower cutting speed increases adhesive wear and micro-fracture at the tool edge. The conclusion is that Run 3 is superior in sustainability from a wear-debris perspective, as it minimizes tool degradation and reduces the risk of contamination and waste generation.

Sustainability assessment of machining conditions

A sustainability assessment was performed by comparing the best machining condition (Run 3: 42 m/min cutting speed, 0.15 mm/rev feed, 0.5 mm depth of cut, Sample A: Al–Cu–Mg with 2% ZrO₂) with the worst condition (Run 12: 26.5 m/min cutting speed, 0.1 mm/rev feed, 0.5 mm depth of cut, Sample C: Al–Cu–Mg with 10% ZrO₂). Despite Run 3 consuming a higher instantaneous power of 160 W compared to 130 W in Run 12, its higher material removal rate (0.08 cm3/min vs. 0.05 cm3/min) translated into lower specific energy demand in Table 18. The normalized values were ≈ 2000 J/cm3 (0.00055 kWh/cm3) for Run 3 and ≈ 2600 J/cm3 (0.00072 kWh/cm3) for Run 12, corresponding to ~ 23% higher energy efficiency in Run 3. This improved efficiency reduces the carbon footprint per unit volume, with Run 3 exhibiting ~ 20–25% lower CO₂ emissions when grid emission factors are considered. From a resource utilization perspective, Run 3 also demonstrated superior surface finish (Ra 2.5 µm) and lower tool wear (8 µm) compared to Run 12 (Ra 3.1 µm and 11 µm wear). This not only extends tool life but also reduces the frequency of coolant flushing, lubricant demand, and material waste from rework or scrap. Conclusion

This study developed and optimized waste-derived Al–Cu–Mg–ZrO₂ aluminum alloys for sustainable turning using an integrated D-optimal RSM–Fuzzy AHP–WASPAS framework. Three recycled alloy compositions containing 2 wt.%, 6 wt.%, and 10 wt.% ZrO₂ were fabricated and evaluated under different cutting speeds, feed rates, and depths of cut. The machining responses included surface roughness, material removal rate, tool wear, power consumption, dimensional accuracy, specific cutting energy, and chip volume. The final Fuzzy AHP–WASPAS ranking identified Alternative 8, corresponding to Sample B with 87.3% Al, 4.9% Cu, 1.8% Mg, and 6 wt.% ZrO₂, as the overall optimum machining alternative. This result confirms that 6 wt.% ZrO₂ provides the best balance between surface quality, material removal productivity, tool-life behavior, energy demand, and dimensional accuracy. The improved performance of Sample B is mainly attributed to moderate ZrO₂-induced grain refinement, uniform reinforcement distribution, reduced particle clustering, lower porosity tendency, and stable Al–Cu–Mg matrix strengthening. In contrast, Sample A contained insufficient reinforcement for strong grain-boundary control, while Sample C showed excessive ZrO₂ addition, which promoted agglomeration, brittleness, and abrasive tool interaction. Compared with the unoptimized baseline condition, the optimum Sample B condition improved surface finish by 25%, increased material removal rate by 30%, and reduced tool wear by 15%. These same improvement values are used consistently in the abstract, results discussion, and conclusion to avoid conflicting interpretations. Confirmation testing showed close agreement between predicted and experimental response values, supporting the reliability of the RSM–Fuzzy AHP–WASPAS optimization model. The sustainability analysis showed that Run 3 provided the best energy-specific and CO₂-related response among the compared machining runs. However, Run 3 was not selected as the overall optimum because it represents an energy-focused result only. The overall optimum remains Alternative 8/Sample B because the Fuzzy AHP–WASPAS ranking considered all machining responses simultaneously. Therefore, the proposed optimization route supports circular manufacturing by converting recovered aluminum-based waste streams into machinable Al–Cu–Mg–ZrO₂ composites while improving machining efficiency, reducing tool degradation, and supporting resource-efficient production aligned with SDG 9 and SDG 12.
