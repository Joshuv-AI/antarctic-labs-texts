The State of Anesthesia and Critical Care Services in Somalia: Challenges and Opportunities
Authors: Ibrahim Abdullahi Mohamed, Bashiru Garba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnesthesia and critical care are indispensable to safe surgery, obstetric care, trauma management, and emergency response, yet they remain underdeveloped in Somalia. This commentary provides a policy-oriented narrative synthesis of available peer-reviewed literature and institutional reports describing workforce capacity, service readiness, and critical care infrastructure. Evidence consistently indicates a very low density of physician anesthesiologists (approximately 0.2 per 100,000), limited availability of functional intensive care unit (ICU) beds, and gaps in essential resources such as monitoring, oxygen, medications, and reliable electricity. In many settings, care relies on non-physician anesthesia providers with variable access to structured training, supervision, and regulation, particularly outside major cities. These system constraints contribute to avoidable perioperative and critical illness risk pathways, including delayed access to emergency interventions, hypoxemia, medication errors, and limited rescue capacity for complications. Despite these challenges, feasible opportunities exist. Priorities include strengthening governance and standards for safe anesthesia practice, expanding training and supervised task-sharing models, improving oxygen and power reliability, and implementing low-cost safety systems such as checklists and standard protocols. Coordinated national planning aligned with global benchmarks and universal health coverage agendas is essential to guide investment and accountability.

Introduction

Since the collapse of Somalia’s central government in 1991, the health system has faced chronic underinvestment, insecurity, and fragmentation.1 Emergency and surgical care—including anesthesia and critical care—were severely constrained for many years.1 As stability gradually returns in parts of the country, unmet needs remain substantial, driven by trauma, obstetric emergencies, neonatal conditions, and infectious diseases.2,3 Maternal mortality remains among the highest globally, underscoring the need for safe obstetric anesthesia, blood transfusion capacity, and effective emergency response.1,4

Global health policy frameworks increasingly recognize surgery, anesthesia, and critical care as foundational to universal health coverage (UHC) and health system resilience.4,5 However, Somalia’s perioperative and critical care capacity has been described through heterogeneous evidence sources spanning different years and scopes, including national facility assessments, single-hospital studies, and institutional reports.3,6 Clear acknowledgment of these sources and their timeframes is therefore essential for transparent interpretation.3,6

Aim and Contribution

This commentary synthesizes available evidence on anesthesia and critical care services in Somalia, frames deficits through a health-system and risk-management lens, and proposes feasible priorities for government, academic institutions, donors, and implementing partners.4,5 The intended audience includes policymakers, health system leaders, global health partners, and academic readers interested in fragile and conflict-affected settings.1,4

Methods: Evidence Identification and Narrative Synthesis

This study is a policy commentary and narrative synthesis, not a primary empirical study.4,7 To improve transparency, we identified and synthesized evidence from peer-reviewed publications describing anesthesia, emergency, surgical, and critical care capacity in Somalia.2,3,6,8 And institutional and multilateral sources (eg, WHO materials and professional society resources) relevant to anesthesia safety and health system strengthening.1,5,9–11

We prioritized sources that reported Somalia-specific measures of workforce, service readiness, ICU availability, oxygen/electricity reliability, emergency unit capacity, and patient safety processes.3,6,8 Evidence spans approximately 2014–2025; where quantitative claims are presented, the year and scope of the cited source are specified to reduce temporal ambiguity.3,6 We did not conduct new statistical analyses; descriptive measures (percentages, ratios, densities) are reported as stated in the original sources3,6,8 (Table 1). To enhance interpretability and relevance for a global risk management audience, we structured the narrative using the WHO Health System Building Blocks framework (service delivery; health workforce; information systems; access to essential medicines/technologies; financing; leadership/governance) and mapped key deficits to plausible clinical risk pathways.5,10

Current Landscape of Anesthesia and Critical Care Services in Somalia

Service Delivery

Facility evidence indicates limited availability of designated emergency services and critical care readiness in many hospitals.7 A nationwide assessment using the WHO Hospital Emergency Unit assessment tool (published 2025) reported that only a minority of hospitals had designated emergency departments, and very few operated continuously 24/7.7 Many facilities lack consistent surgical guidelines and reliable perioperative safety systems.3,6 In practice, hospitals may use operating theaters as ad hoc high-acuity areas during emergencies when no ICU bed is available.7,13

Health Workforce

A national workforce assessment (published 2024) reported approximately 22 physician anesthesiologists in Somalia, corresponding to about 0.2 per 100,000 population—substantially below internationally cited workforce benchmarks.8,9 Workforce concentration in Mogadishu and a small number of urban centers contributes to geographic inequity.8 Non-physician anesthesia providers (NPAPs)—including nurse anesthetists and other cadres—provide a large share of anesthesia care, particularly outside major cities.3,7 However, training pathways, credentialing, supervision, and regulation are inconsistent, increasing variability in competency and safety margins.2,3,10

Access to Essential Medicines, Oxygen, Equipment, and Technologies

Oxygen and electricity reliability remain recurring constraints.7 Across facility assessments, a substantial proportion of hospitals report inconsistent oxygen access and intermittent power, undermining safe anesthesia, postoperative monitoring, and the feasibility of mechanical ventilation.7 Availability of functional monitoring (including pulse oximetry), ventilatory support devices, and critical care equipment is variable and frequently limited.7 Medication availability may be affected by procurement and supply chain disruptions, contributing to stockouts of essential anesthetic and critical care drugs.7,10

Health Information Systems

Routine reporting systems for perioperative and critical care outcomes are limited, and standardized national indicators for anesthesia service readiness and perioperative mortality are not consistently available in the public domain.1,5 Fragmented data and inconsistent documentation reduce situational awareness for policymakers and hospital leaders and constrain the ability to target risk-reduction investments.5

Financing

Out-of-pocket payment remains a barrier to timely access to emergency and surgical care, particularly for rural and low-income patients.1,4 When cost delays presentation or referral, risk increases for advanced disease, obstetric complications, and trauma.4 Financing constraints also limit maintenance of equipment, oxygen supply chains, and the sustainability of training programs.1,7,11

Leadership and Governance

Regulatory oversight for anesthesia practice, minimum safety standards, and credentialing for NPAP cadres is limited.1,2,7 Governance fragmentation can also complicate coordinated procurement, national planning, and the integration of anesthesia and critical care into essential health benefit packages.1 Stronger alignment with international standards for safe anesthesia practice and national policy instruments would support accountability and safer expansion.4,10

Risk Pathways and System-Level Implications

From a risk management perspective, deficits in anesthesia and critical care capacity translate into predictable clinical hazards.5,10 Inconsistent oxygen supply and limited monitoring increase the likelihood of undetected desaturation during anesthesia and inadequate postoperative rescue.7,10 Stockouts and variable access to essential drugs create conditions for substitution errors, delayed care, and inconsistent adherence to protocols.7,10 Heavy reliance on NPAPs without standardized training, supervision, and credentialing increases variability in competency, particularly in pediatric, obstetric, and high-acuity cases.2,8,10,12 Unreliable electricity and limited biomedical maintenance increase downtime of concentrators, monitors, and ventilators, amplifying perioperative and ICU mortality risk pathways.7 Limited emergency unit capacity, weak transport, and delayed presentation contribute to late-stage disease and reduced survival for trauma and obstetric emergencies.1,4,7 Collectively, these pathways underscore why strengthening anesthesia and critical care should be treated as a foundational patient safety and health-system resilience priority rather than solely a specialty agenda (Figure 1).4,5Figure 1Framework linking WHO health-system gaps to anesthesia/critical care risk pathways and prioritized actions across short-, medium-, and long-term horizons in Somalia (narrative synthesis).Abbreviations: ED, emergency department; HDU, high-dependency unit; ICU, intensive care unit; NPAP, non-physician anesthesia provider; NSOAP, national surgical, obstetric, and anesthesia plan; UHC, universal health coverage; WHO, World Health Organization.

Opportunities and Recommendations

Short-Term Priorities (0–18 Months)

Immediate safety and risk reduction: Immediate risk reduction should focus on implementing minimum anesthesia safety standards and checklists by scaling the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist and basic monitoring norms where feasible, supported by local audit-and-feedback cycles.6,10 Concurrently, stabilizing oxygen and power reliability is essential, including functional oxygen systems (concentrators/cylinders) with maintenance plans and backup power solutions for operating rooms and high-acuity areas.7 Standardizing essential medication and equipment lists through defined minimum perioperative and emergency care bundles, alongside strengthened procurement planning, may reduce stockouts and unsafe substitutions.10 Targeted in-service training—using short courses and simulation-supported refreshers—should prioritize airway management, obstetric emergencies, pediatric resuscitation, and essential critical care competencies.11

Medium-Term Priorities (18 Months–3 Years)

Structured workforce development and supervised task-sharing: Medium-term progress requires formalizing NPAP training and credentialing through competency-based curricula, certification, and clearly defined scopes of practice linked to supervision and referral criteria.2,10 Establishing supervision and mentorship systems—such as hub-and-spoke models connecting regional hospitals to senior anesthesia leadership, including tele-mentoring where feasible—can help improve quality and consistency of care.1,11 In parallel, expanding basic high-dependency and ICU functionality in regional referral hospitals should begin with a small number of beds supported by reliable oxygen and power, essential monitoring, and appropriate nursing ratios.7,10

Long-Term Priorities (3–7 Years)

National planning, specialty pathways, and sustainable governance: Long-term sustainability depends on developing accredited physician anesthesia and critical care training pathways, including residency and fellowship capacity through partnerships with universities, professional societies, and diaspora networks.9,11 Integrating anesthesia and critical care into national planning—through NSOAP-aligned strategies embedded within UHC benefit packages with measurable targets—can strengthen coordination and accountability.4 Finally, strengthening regulation and quality systems through minimum facility standards, routine indicators, and continuous quality improvement structures is necessary to support safe expansion across regions.5,10

Implementation Barriers and Feasibility Considerations

Political instability, constrained fiscal space, workforce migration, and supply chain fragility can undermine sustainability.1 Implementation should therefore emphasize (i) phased targets, (ii) maintenance and biomedical engineering capacity, (iii) retention incentives and career progression, and (iv) partnership coordination to reduce duplication and improve accountability.1,5

Limitations

This commentary relies on secondary sources that vary by year, scope, and representativeness, including national assessments, facility surveys, and single-hospital studies. Consequently, some findings should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive national estimates. Data gaps in standardized national indicators for perioperative outcomes, ICU mortality, and anesthesia service readiness limit causal inference and trend analysis. Future work would benefit from harmonized national measurement, routine reporting, and periodic facility readiness audits to enable more precise prioritization and evaluation.

Conclusion

Somalia’s anesthesia and critical care services remain constrained by workforce shortages, limited emergency/ICU readiness, and gaps in oxygen, power, monitoring, and governance. For policymakers and partners, immediate risk-reduction priorities include implementing safety checklists and minimum standards, strengthening oxygen and power reliability, and supporting targeted training. Medium-term gains require formalized credentialing and supervised task-sharing models, alongside incremental development of high-acuity care capacity in referral hospitals. Long-term sustainability depends on national planning aligned with UHC agendas and the development of accredited training and regulatory systems. Addressing these priorities can reduce preventable perioperative and critical illness harm and strengthen broader health system resilience in Somalia.
