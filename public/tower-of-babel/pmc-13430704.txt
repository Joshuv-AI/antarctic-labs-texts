Relationships between perceived social support, self-efficacy, coping styles and resilience in older patients with Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Zihan Lin, Yuwen Fan, Mengqian Wu, Fang Wang, Kaijun Han, Yan Wang, Yingying Zhang, Lu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo explore the structural relationships among perceived social support, self-efficacy, coping styles, and resilience in older patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD).MethodsThis study is a cross-sectional study. A total of 220 older patients with PD were recruited from hospitals in China using convenience sampling. Patients completed a standardized questionnaire. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was employed to test the hypothesized relationships.ResultsThe resilience of older patients with PD was at a moderately low level. SEM results indicated that resilience was positively associated with perceived social support, confrontation coping, and self-efficacy, and negatively correlated with acceptance-resignation coping. Confrontation coping and self-efficacy partially mediated the relationship between perceived social support and resilience. A significant serial mediation effect (confrontation coping→self-efficacy) was also observed.ConclusionsConfrontation coping and self-efficacy are key mechanisms linking perceived social support to resilience. Interventions targeting confrontation coping strategies and enhancing self-efficacy may improve resilience among older patients with PD.Keywords: Older patients, Parkinson’s disease, Resilience, Social support, Self-efficacy, Coping styles

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder worldwide and is projected to become the fastest-growing neurological condition globally [1]. China ranks first among the ten countries with the highest number of PD cases [2]. The disease is clinically characterized by motor symptoms such as resting tremor and muscle rigidity, as well as non-motor symptoms including cognitive impairment, anxiety, and depression [3].

As a chronic and progressive disease, PD imposes a considerable psychological burden on patients, both at diagnosis and throughout disease adaptation. Previous studies have reported that approximately 38% of patients with PD experience depression [4], while the prevalence of anxiety can reach up to 30% [5]. These psychological disturbances not only diminish the quality of life but may also exacerbate motor symptoms [6]. Given that PD predominantly affects older individuals [7], this issue is particularly concerning, as older individuals already exhibit notable rates of anxiety (3.2%-14.2%) and depression (14.8%-23.6%) [8, 9]. Therefore, understanding the factors that influence resilience in older patients with Parkinson’s disease is of great importance.

With the development of positive psychology, resilience has emerged as a key construct for understanding psychological adaptation in chronic illness. Resilience refers to an individual’s ability to protect or recover themselves when facing adversity, trauma, illness, or other unfavorable conditions [10]. In patients with PD, resilience has been shown to vary across populations [11, 12]. It is negatively associated with psychological distress [13]. Moreover, the level of psychological resilience in patients with Parkinson’s disease is positively correlated with physical condition and quality of life [14]. Lower levels of resilience may increase psychological distress and be linked to poorer health outcomes [15, 16].

The Stress and Coping Theory proposes that stress-related factors, such as social support, coping strategies, and cognitive appraisal, can lead to various physiological, psychological, and behavioral changes, thereby affecting an individual’s health and daily life [17]. In the context of PD, both the disease itself and treatment-related adverse effects, such as motor fluctuations and dyskinesias associated with levodopa therapy [18], or impulse control disorders induced by dopamine agonists [19], serve as significant sources of stress.

Perceived social support refers to an individual’s perception and evaluation of social support, including expectations regarding the availability of such support [20], and plays a critical role in buffering stress. Due to the need to manage the complex symptoms of Parkinson’s disease, patients with PD typically require more sustained and comprehensive support [21]. Higher levels of perceived social support have been associated with greater resilience [11].

Self-efficacy, defined as an individual’s belief in their ability to organize and execute actions required to manage challenges [22], is another important psychological resource [23]. Evidence suggests that self-efficacy is positively associated with resilience [24]and may function as a key internal mechanism.

Coping style refers to the cognitive and behavioral strategies an individual adopts to deal with stressful situations and maintain psychological equilibrium in stressful situations. Confrontation coping reflects an active, problem-focused approach, whereas acceptance-resignation coping represents a passive, disengaged response. Previous studies have demonstrated that confrontation coping is positively associated with resilience, whereas acceptance-resignation coping is negatively associated with resilience [11].

Although prior research has identified bivariate relationships among perceived social support, self-efficacy, coping strategies, and resilience, the structural mechanisms underlying these associations remain unclear. In particular, few studies have examined these variables simultaneously within a unified theoretical model in older patients with PD.

Therefore, based on Stress and Coping Theory, this study aimed to examine the structural relationships among perceived social support, self-efficacy, coping styles, and resilience in older patients with PD. The following hypotheses were proposed: H1: Perceived social support is positively associated with resilience. H2: Coping styles (confrontation and acceptance-resignation) mediate the relationship between perceived social support and resilience. H3: Self-efficacy mediates the relationship between perceived social support and resilience. H4: Confrontation coping and self-efficacy sequentially mediate the relationship between perceived social support and resilience.

Methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional study of older Patients with PD.

Study population

From November 2025 to January 2026, older adults were recruited from two tertiary hospitals in Nanjing, China, using convenience sampling. Inclusion criteria included: (1) Meeting the 2016 Chinese diagnostic criteria for Parkinson’s disease [25]; (2) Age ≥ 60 years; (3) Clear consciousness, stable vital signs, normal speech and hearing, able to communicate effectively, and able to understand the meaning of the questionnaire. Exclusion criteria included: (1) Severe organ dysfunction, respiratory failure, or concurrent malignant tumors; (2) History of serious mental illness; (3) Presence of dementia or cognitive impairment (identified by neurologists based on clinical assessments documented in medical records during routine clinical care, or as determined through review of medical records); (4) Participation in other clinical trials during the same period.

According to the sample size requirements for structural equation modeling, the sample size should be 5–10 times the number of observed variables. This study included 12 observed variables, yielding a required sample size of 60–120 participants. According to the model fit requirements for structural equation modeling (SEM) [26], the sample size should be ≥ 200. A total of 233 questionnaires were distributed in this study, and 220 valid questionnaires were returned, yielding an effective response rate of 94.42%. This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Drum Tower Hospital (approval number:2025-0909-02). The principles of anonymity and voluntariness were followed, and the participants signed informed consent forms before participating in the study; no financial compensation was provided.

Material

Demographic information questionnaire

Including demographic information and disease-related information (disease duration and number of hospitalizations).

Multidimensional scale of perceived social support

The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) was developed by Zimet et al. [20]. It was translated and adapted into Chinese named Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS) by Jiang. It is used to assess an individual’s level of perceived social support. The scale comprises three dimensions: family support (4 items), friend support (4 items), and significant other support (4 items), with a total of 12 items. Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” and scored 1–7. The total score is the sum of the three dimensions, ranging from 12 to 84, with higher scores indicating higher perceived social support. In this study, the scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s ɑ of 0.912.

General self-efficacy scale

The General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) was developed by Schwarzer et al. [27]. It was translated and adapted into Chinese by Wang et al. The Chinese version of the scale consists of 10 items, rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from “completely incorrect” to “completely correct” and scored 1–4. The total score ranges from 10 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy. In this study, the scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s ɑ of 0.877.

Medical coping modes questionnaire

The Medical Coping Modes Questionnaire (MCMQ) was developed by Feifel et al. [28]. Moreover, translated and adapted into Chinese by Shen and Jiang. It is primarily used to assess patients’ attitudes and strategies for disease-related life events. The questionnaire includes three dimensions—confrontation, avoidance, and acceptance-resignation—comprising a total of 20 items, each rated ona 4-point Likert scale. The scale is widely used in studies of various chronic diseases in China. In this study, the Cronbach’s ɑ values for the three dimensions were 0.875, 0.874, and 0.873, respectively.

Connor-Davison’s resilience scale

The Connor-Davison’s Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) was developed by Connor and Davidson [29] in 2003. In 2007, it was translated and adapted into Chinese by Yu et al. The scale includes three dimensions with a total of 25 items: tenacity (13 items), strength (8 items), and optimism (4 items). It uses a 5-point Likert scale, with “never” scored as 0 and “always” scored as 4, for a total score of 100. Higher scores indicate better resilience. In this study, the scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s ɑ of 0.929.

Data collection

Before the survey, the researchers provided a standardized briefing to the patients with PD, explaining the purpose, significance, procedures, and precautions of the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. Any patient questions were addressed immediately. The completed questionnaires were collected on the spot and checked by the investigators for completeness and accuracy, with necessary clarifications or corrections made through communication with the patients. Disease-related information was verified and recorded using the hospital information system.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 25.0 and AMOS 26.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the general characteristics of patients with Parkinson’s disease. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages (%). Quantitative data that were normally or approximately normally distributed were expressed as mean ± standard deviation () and compared between groups using independent-samples t tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Quantitative data that did not meet normality assumptions were presented as medians and interquartile ranges and compared using rank-sum tests. Harman’s single-factor test was used to assess common method bias. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to explore the relationships among resilience, perceived social support, confrontational coping, acceptance-resignation coping, and general self-efficacy. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to identify factors influencing resilience. The significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Common method bias

This study used self-report scales, which may be subject to common method bias. To enhance internal validity and rigor, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted. The results showed 14 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the variance explained by the first factor was 26.60%, which is below the 40% threshold. This indicates that there is no serious common method variance in the data for this study [30].

Mediating effect analysis

Based on the hypothesized model, perceived social support was set as the exogenous latent variable, resilience as the outcome variable, confrontation coping, acceptance-resignation coping, and general self-efficacy as mediating latent variables. A structural equation model was constructed to examine the factors influencing resilience in patients with Parkinson’s disease. The specific results are shown in Fig. 1. The fit indices of the modified model were generally acceptable: χ2/df = 1.144, RMSEA = 0.026, GFI = 0.933, AGFI = 0.911, NFI = 0.913, TLI = 0.986, IFI = 0.988. All hypothesized paths were supported.

Discussion

The results showed that the mean resilience score for older patients with Parkinson’s disease was 47.84 ± 14.64, indicating a moderate-to-low level of resilience. Similar to the findings reported by Cai et al. [12]. However, some studies have found that patients with Parkinson’s disease may exhibit moderate-to-high levels of resilience [11]. These findings suggest variability in psychological resilience across different populations or settings. In our sample, older patients with Parkinson’s disease still exhibit certain deficiencies in psychological adaptation when facing multiple stressors, such as potential psychological stress associated with recurrent hospitalizations.

This study found that perceived social support directly influences resilience. This finding is consistent with the results reported by Karami et al. [32]. This finding has also been confirmed among hospitalized older adults [33]. According to the main effect model of social support [34]. Social networks provide individuals with overall positive experiences and relatively stable social rewards. Regardless of whether an individual is under stress, social resources can exert positive effects [35]. When external resources make patients feel understood, cared for, and not “alone” in managing their disease, their resilience may be enhanced.

Perceived social support can indirectly influence resilience through confrontation coping and acceptance-resignation coping. This finding is consistent with previous research [36]. Cultural background may offer an important explanation for this mediating effect. Within collectivist East Asian cultural contexts, Health-related decisions are often guided by family members or trusted healthcare professionals rather than made as autonomous individual choices [37, 38]. Especially given that patients with Parkinson’s disease are predominantly older adults, their health-related decision-making relies more heavily on support from family members and friends. When patients perceive greater external social support, they tend to have greater confidence in treatment, adopt confrontation coping strategies to manage the disease, and consequently demonstrate higher levels of resilience. Moreover, in collectivist cultures, maintaining harmonious, close social relationships is highly valued [39]. When patients feel unsupported or misunderstood during the course of treatment, they are more likely to adopt acceptance-resignation coping to disease management, which in turn leads to a decline in resilience.

This study found that perceived social support can influence resilience by affecting self-efficacy. This finding is consistent with the results reported by Wang et al. [40]. Bandura defined self-efficacy as an individual’s confidence in their ability to successfully complete specific tasks or bring about positive changes [22]. Therefore, self-efficacy is critically important in patient care, as it involves changes in health behaviors, psychological, and emotional well-being [41]. In managing long-term chronic diseases, external support networks alone are not sufficient to fully enhance an individual’s resilience; it is necessary to transform this support into the patient’s own psychological resources and sense of efficacy. Supportive family members, friends, and healthcare providers who provide information on self-care and symptom management and encourage patients to adapt to their condition can greatly strengthen patients’ confidence in their ability to cope with treatment and disease management [42].

This study found that perceived social support can improve patients’ resilience by enhancing self-efficacy through confrontation coping. This finding is inconsistent with previous research [43]. Previous studies have generally emphasized the leading role of self-efficacy in coping with disease-related stress, whereby higher self-efficacy promotes more active coping strategies. In contrast, the present study suggests an alternative pathway in older patients with Parkinson’s disease, in which coping strategies precede self-efficacy. Differences in patient age may partly explain this inconsistency with previous studies. In the context of Parkinson’s disease, older patients often face major challenges in controlling their condition, which may, to some extent, weaken their initial confidence in disease control. Under such circumstances, actively engaging in coping behaviors for disease management, especially with support from social resources and reinforcement of positive feedback, can improve patients’ psychological experiences and gradually enhance their self-efficacy. This process may help explain the pathway observed in this study: confrontation coping precedes self-efficacy, forming a “behavior–belief” reinforcement cycle.

This finding suggests a novel, actionable intervention pathway for promoting psychological resilience among older patients with Parkinson’s disease. A nurse-led, multi-component care approach can be integrated into routine care, focusing on enhancing social support, promoting confrontation coping, and strengthening self-efficacy. In practice, nurses can deliver brief (10–15 min) structured sessions during outpatient visits or hospitalization, including: (1) actively involving caregivers in disease-related education and caregiving guidance to enhance patients’ perceived support in daily life; (2) providing practical guidance on problem-solving and symptom management to encourage active coping; (3) Setting small, achievable weekly goals, along with regular follow-up and feedback (via telephone or digital platforms), to strengthen patients’ self-efficacy. In addition, Hospital-based digital platforms can further extend support by providing continuous medical guidance, informational assistance, and emotional support [44], thereby promoting sustained confrontation coping and resilience. Overall, these strategies represent a practical and scalable approach to improving resilience in older patients with PD. When needed, patients with greater psychosocial needs can be referred to mental health services through existing clinical pathways. This approach is simple to implement, requires minimal additional resources, and can be delivered by trained nurses across different healthcare settings.

We acknowledge that this study has some limitations. Firstly, it is a cross-sectional study, so causal inferences cannot be established. Secondly, patients were recruited only from hospitals in Nanjing, which limits the generalizability of the sample. Furthermore, this study did not include detailed clinical information such as symptom severity, medication dosage, or patients’ daily functioning, which may also influence resilience and related psychological variables.

Conclusions

The findings of this study indicate that confrontation coping and self-efficacy play multiple mediating roles in the relationship between perceived social support and resilience among older patients with Parkinson’s disease. Healthcare professionals should recognize the impact of positive psychological resources, such as coping strategies and self-efficacy, on patients’ resilience and its underlying mechanisms. Based on the stress-coping model, targeted intervention programs should be developed to strengthen patients’ positive psychological resources, thereby enhancing resilience and improving quality of life.
