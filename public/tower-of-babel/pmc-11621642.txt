Photodynamic Effects of Topical Photosensitizer, Photodithazine Using Micro-LED for Acne Bacteria Induced Inflammation
Authors: Su Min Lee, Soo-Hyun Kim, Zun Kim, Jee-Bum Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhotodynamic therapy (PDT) using topical photosensitizers has been widely used worldwide as a therapeutic modality for acne. However, there are no published reports on photodithazine PDT for acne treatment.ObjectiveWe investigated the effectiveness of PDT with photodithazine and micro-LED treatment for acne bacteria-induced inflammation.MethodsWe established an acne bacteria-induced inflammation model by injecting of Cutibacterium acnes into the backs of HR-1 mice. The mouse models were divided into seven groups for control and comparison. Topical photosensitizer (photodithazine) was administered to the mice, and then their acne lesions were exposed to a micro–light-emitting diode (micro-LED) source. The effectiveness of the treatment on acne lesions was evaluated through clinical findings and measurements of acne inflammation biomarkers using semi-quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction, and Western blot. Additionally, hematoxylin and eosin staining and immunohistochemical staining were employed to evaluate the changes in inflammatory cells and biomarkers in skin tissues.ResultsCompared with the control groups treated with either LED or photosensitizer alone, the acne lesions were significantly reduced in severity and number after PDT. The mRNA and protein levels of biomarkers (interleukin [IL]-1α, IL-1β, tumor necrosis factor-α, Toll-like receptor 2, matrix metalloproteinase-2, and IL-8) exhibited variable decreases in the PDT group relative to the others. Moreover, there was a decline in inflammatory cells and biomarkers in skin specimens after PDT.ConclusionThis in vivo study demonstrated that PDT using photodithazine and micro-LED technology is effective against inflammation induced by acne bacteria.

INTRODUCTION

Acne is a common skin disease, imposing considerable dermatological challenges and psychological burdens on patients worldwide. It can occur at any age, from infancy to old age, appearing in various forms and often exhibiting resistance to treatment1. Various mechanisms contribute to acne pathogenesis, including increased sebum production, follicular hyperkeratinization, colonization of Cutibacterium acnes, inflammation, local or systemic hormonal changes, heredity, and immunological factors23. For treating acne, a range of systemic and topical therapies, along with various procedures, are used, with the choice of treatment method depending on the stage of acne (acute inflammatory lesions vs. late scars). In particular, photodynamic therapy (PDT) can be used to treat inflammatory acne vulgaris, especially when the use of oral medications, such as isotretinoin, is contraindicated4.

PDT is a technique wherein a photosensitizer absorbs light of a specific wavelength and reacts with oxygen to release reactive oxygen species (ROS), causing cell death. Photosensitizers are widely used in the treatment of acne and other conditions, given their preferential accumulation in cancer cells and inflammatory cells5. Topical photosensitizers, such as 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) and methyl-aminolevulinate (MAL), which are porphyrin based, are widely used as photosensitizers for treating acne lesions, particularly inflammatory acne lesions3.

Chlorin, a second-generation photosensitizer, is developed via the modification of the phorphyrin pyrrole ring and is characterized by its shorter photosensitization times and absorption at longer wavelengths. Photodithazine, a water-soluble chlorin e6 derivative, is widely applied in the treatment of inflammatory conditions, such as candidiasis and malignant tumors678.

However, to our knowledge, there are no published reports of in vivo and in vitro experiments investigating acne treatment using photodithazine, which is a short-acting photosensitizer. We were interested in determining whether patch-type micro–light-emitting diodes (micro-LEDs) can be as effective as conventional light sources for PDT administration. Therefore, after establishing an acne animal model, we aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of PDT using novel photosensitizing treatments, specifically a photodithazine gel and micro-LEDs, by observing changes in the expression of acne inflammation markers.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

LED source

The light source used for the PDT was supplied by the Korea Photonics Technology Institute in the form of a micro-LED, exhibiting an output of 650 nm, 10 V, 40 mA (CC mode), and 2 mW/cm2 (Fig. 1A).

(A) Micro LED device. (B) Inducing acne inflammation in animal models. After c.acnes intradermal injection, lesions appeared on day 7 and peaked on day 14 and disappeared on day 21. (C) The overall experimental design. After stabilization of the experimental animals, the experimental group was injected with C. acnes, and 7 days after the injection, FDT gel application and LED irradiation were performed according to the classification of the experimental groups. LED irradiation was conducted for a total of 10 sessions for 10 minutes or 15 minutes each time. (D) Fluorescence expression after FDT gel application showed maximum expression after 15 minutes by immunohistofluorescence.

Animals

Experiments were performed on HR-1 (Hairless mouse, CrlOri: SKH1-Hr) females aged 6 weeks. The mice were housed in an air-conditioned room maintained at 22°C ±1°C and 50%±5% humidity, with a 12-hour light–dark cycle. After a 1-week stabilization period, the mice were randomly divided, with each group consisting of six animals.

Animal model for acne inflammation

The experimental group was divided into 7 groups. The normal control group was set as the 1st group, the 2nd group was the acne inflammation induced group that induced only acne inflammation, and the 3rd group was applied FDT gel 15 minutes to the group that induced acne inflammation. In the 4th group, 650 nm LEDs were applied for 10 minutes to the group that induced acne inflammation (Phototherapy [PT] 10 minutes). In the 5th group, 650 nm LEDs were applied for 15 minutes to the group that induced acne inflammation (PT 15 minutes). In the 6th group, acne inflammation was induced, FDT gel was applied for 15 minutes, 650 nm LEDs were irradiated for 10 minutes (Photo dynamic therapy 10 minutes). In the 7th group, acne inflammation was induced and FDT gel was applied for 15 minutes, and 650 nm LEDs were irradiated for 15 minutes (Photo dynamic therapy 15 minutes). To establish the animal model for acne inflammation, 100 µl of freeze-dried C. acnes (strain ATCC 6919, 1×109 CFU/µl) was injected intradermally into HR-1 to induce lesions. Previous experiments demonstrated that lesions began appearing on the fourth day after intradermal injection, peaking on the 14th day and subsiding until disappearance after the 21st day (Fig. 1B). Based on this information, the animal experiment schedule was set to start 7 days after the acne bacteria inoculation. Semi-quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (sqRT-PCR), western blot, H&E stain, and IHCS stain were performed on samples collected from the back of HR-1 mouse, an acne inflammatory lesion. Each experimental group was subdivided into 3 animals and an experiment of 2 sets (n=3) was performed. The overall experimental design is described in Fig. 1C.

PDT

Photodithazine (Fotoditazin®, Veta-Grand, Russia, FDT gel) was used as a photosensitizer for PDT. Photodithazine was provided by the ANCELL Bio Co., Ltd. PDT & PDIT Lab. The distribution of fluorescence expression induced by FDT gel was confirmed using confocal laser scanner microscopy. The observations indicated a variation in fluorescence expression depending on the skin application time of the FDT gel, with optimal fluorescence in tissues observed after 15 minutes of application and weakening after 30 minutes. Consequently, the FDT gel application time was set at 15 minutes (Fig. 1D). The micro-LED rays were shone on the back skin of the HR-1 mice in the form of a patch. Depending on the experimental group, irradiation for PT or PDT was conducted for either 10 minutes or 15 minutes at a time, with a total of 10 sessions at 1-day intervals for 2 weeks.

sqRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, Fremont, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. From 500 ng of total RNA, cDNA was synthesized using the Omniscript RT kit (Qiagen). For the sqRT-PCR assay, PCR-premixture kits (ELPIS, Daejeon, Korea) were used. The sequences of the specific primers for interleukin (IL)-1α, IL-1β, IL-8, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, Toll-like receptor (TLR)2, matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-2, and β-actin are listed in Table 1. The PCR products were analyzed using a 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis, stained with Cyber Safe DNA gel stain buffer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and visualized under a luminescent image analyzer (LAS 3000, Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan). Band intensities were relatively quantified using densitometry analysis and normalized to the β-actin values. All data are representative of four experiments.

Western blot analysis

Tissue extracts were prepared by sonication in RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.25% deoxycholic acid, 1% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0, and protease inhibitor cocktail). After centrifugation (12,000 × g, 15 minutes at 4°C), the protein concentration of the supernatant was measured by using a BCA protein assay kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA). The extract was separated in 6%–10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (0.45 mm, Millipore, MA, USA). After incubation with blocking solution (5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 hour at room temperature, the membranes were incubated with the relevant respective primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The primary antibodies used were anti-IL-1α (1:200, ab7632, Abcam, Cambridgeshire, UK), anti-IL-1β (1:1,000, sc-52012, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc., CA, USA), anti-IL-8 (1:200, NBP2-33819, Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO, USA), anti-TLR2 (1:200, ab24192, Abcam) and mouse monoclonal β-actin (1:5,000, ab-6276, Abcam). After incubation with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5,000, sc-2004, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) or anti-mouse IgG (1:5,000, sc-2005, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), immune complexes were visualized using the ECL kit (Millipore). The protein bands were visualized by luminescence (LAS 3000). Densitometric analyses were performed using the Multi Gauge V3.0 software (Fujifilm). Expression levels were normalized to the endogenous control β-actin. All data are representative of four experiments.

Hematoxylin and eosin and immunohistochemical staining

Six tissue samples from each mouse were obtained via punch biopsies and were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Fresh frozen tissues were embedded in Tissue-Tek O.C.T. Compound (Sakura Finetechnical Co., Tokyo, Japan) in cryomolds with liquid nitrogen. Cryosections (5 µm) were prepared using a Leica CM1850 cryostat (Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany) and mounted on slides (Fisherbrand, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). The sections were air-dried for 30 minutes, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (Lonza, Walkersville, MD, USA), mounted in DAPI (4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA).

Skin tissue samples were embedded in paraffin, cut into 5-µm slices, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or used for immunohistochemical staining with the R.T.U. VECTASTAIN® Universal Quick kit (Vector Laboratories). Tissue samples were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and pre-treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 minutes, followed by incubation in the blocking reagent for 10 minutes. Antigen retrieval was performed at 95°C in a water bath in Dako Target Retrieval Solution, pH 9, for 20 minutes, followed by incubation at room temperature for 30 minutes. Then, the sections were incubated with the primary antibodies under the following conditions: anti-IL-1α (1:200, ab7632, Abcam), anti-TNF-α (1:200, sc-52746, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti-NLRP3 (1:200, NBP1-77080, Novus Biologicals), anti-Caspase1 (1:200, ab1872, Abcam) and anti-SREBP1 (1:200, sc-8984, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) at room temperature or 4°C overnight. As the chromogen, 3-amino-9-ethylcarbazole was used, and then tissues were counterstained with Meyer’s hematoxylin. As a negative control for immunostaining, phosphate-buffered saline was used in place of the primary antibodies, and the secondary antibody was incubated under the same conditions as described above. Images were visualized using confocal microscopy with a laser scanning microscope (LSM 800 with Airyscan; Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) and analyzed using the ZEN 2 browser imaging software (ZEN 2.6 [Blue Edition]; Carl Zeiss).

Statistical analysis

All values are expressed as the means ± standard deviations. Statistical analyses consisted of one-way ANOVA (analysis of variance) with a post hoc Scheffe test, conducted using SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), when multiple comparisons were made. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

Official permission to handle mice for the experiments was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Chonnam National University Medical School (IRB No. CNUHIACUC-21023).

RESULTS

Visual assessment

24 hours after each LED irradiation, the skin surface condition of each experimental group was photographed for observations of changes in acne lesions. Compared with the other experimental groups, the groups that underwent PDT exhibited a noticeable decrease in acne lesions. Specifically, the lesional size reduction was greatest in group 7, where PDT was performed for 15 minutes (Fig. 2).

Visual observation of changes in acne inflammatory lesions in animal models. Inflammatory lesions were visually smaller in the PDT group that received FDT gel and LED irradiation. In particular, the lesions shrunk after irradiation ×5 and ×10 but not after a single session of irradiation; the most substantial lesion shrinkage was observed in the group that underwent LED irradiation for 15 minutes.

Quantitative analysis of acne inflammatory biomarkers (sqRT-PCR, Western blot)

Semi-quantitative RT-PCR analysis indicated that the mRNA levels of IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-8, TNF-α, TLR2, and MMP-2 decreased after 10 and 15 minutes of PDT, with the most substantial decreases noted after 15 minutes of PDT treatment (Fig. 3A and B, Table 2).

The reduction of inflammatory markers was large in the PDT group in which FDT gel and LED irradiation were applied together; in particular, the greatest change was in the group in which LED irradiation was applied for 15 minutes. Histological analysis using H&E staining

Histological analysis (with H&E staining) of the skin tissue biopsies from acne lesions of mice with induced acne and inflammation showed significantly increased dermal infiltration of inflammatory cells—such as lymphocytes, neutrophils, and macrophage—compared with the normal mice. Inflammatory cell proliferation decreased after FDT gel treatment, PT, and PDT on acne lesions, with the most pronounced decreases after 15 minutes of treatment (Fig. 4).

Comparison of inflammatory biomarker expression in skin tissue. Significantly increased inflammatory cell infiltration in acne lesions compared to the normal group and inflammatory cells were reduced again by FDT gel treatment, PT for 15 minutes, and PDT for 15 minutes on acne lesions (H&E staining). Using immunohistochemical staining, the expression levels of NLRP3, caspase1, SREBP1, IL-1α, and TNF-α in acne lesions were significantly increased compared with the levels in the control group; however, with FDT gel treatment, there were lesion reductions by PT of 15 minutes and PDT with 15 minutes of irradiation. In particular, it was confirmed that the expression of inflammatory factors in tissues was most reduced by PDT for 15 minutes (200×).

IL: interleukin, TNF: tumor necrosis factor.

Immunohistochemical staining

Immunohistochemical staining analysis showed that the expression of NLRP3, caspase 1, SREBP1, IL-1α, and TNF-α was significantly higher in acne lesions and inflamed tissue than in tissues from the normal group. However, the expression levels of these factors were reduced by FDT gel treatment, PT for 15 minutes, and PDT for 15 minutes. The 15-minute PDT yielded the most substantial reduction in inflammatory biomarkers expression (Fig. 4).

DISCUSSION

PDT is administered based on the principle that ROS produced in cells that have absorbed a photosensitizer cause cell death. This results from the interaction between three elements: a photosensitizer, light of an appropriate wavelength, and oxygen5. The photosensitizer is preferentially accumulated (relative to its accumulation in normal cells) in inflammatory cells or cancer cells, and the energy generated by absorbing light of a specific wavelength is converted into ROS. ROS exhibit therapeutic effects both directly (through cell necrosis and apoptosis) and indirectly (through the regulation of inflammatory mediators)9.

Initially, systemic photosensitizers were used for PDT, which was also used to treat dermatological diseases. However, the use of oral porphyrin raised concerns about the high risk of systemic side effects, such as porphyria, generalized photosensitivity, hepatotoxicity, nausea, and vomiting. Consequently, the available oral photosensitizer dosage was limited. Topical sensitizers were, therefore, developed to achieve localized effects and mitigate these side effects101112.

C. acnes, well known as a causative agent in acne vulgaris, characteristically produces coproporphyrin III, an endogenous porphyrin, making PDT a suitable acne treatment. Additionally, 5-ALA and MAL, which are mainly used as photosensitizers in dermatology, are well absorbed into the pilosebaceous unit, enabling selective treatment for inflammation of the pilosebaceous unit in acne vulgaris3.

In practice, treatment using ALA or MAL has resulted in decreased numbers of inflammatory acne lesions, a lower abundance of C. acnes, decreased sebum excretion, and decreased bacterial porphyrin fluorescence. Temporary side effects, such as pain, erythema, transient hyperpigmentation, superficial exfoliation, crushing, and urticaria-like rash, have been associated with ALA, but these have been shown to improve over time13141516.

Photodithazine, used in this study, is a second-generation photosensitizing agent and is the bis-N-methylglucamine salt of chlorin e6. Compared with first-generation photosensitizers, it has a shorter photosensitization period, a longer activation wavelength range of 630 to 660 nm, higher yields of singlet oxygen, lower toxicity, and is expelled from the body within 48 to 72 hours1718.

PDT using topical photodithazine exhibited efficacy comparable to that of nystatin topical treatment for oral candidiasis in mice17. For patients with dental stomatitis, the application of topical photodithazine for 20 minutes, followed by LED exposure at 660 nm, 50 J/cm2, has been shown to have therapeutic benefits exceeding those observed in a nystatin control group19.

In vitro studies on the antimicrobial effects on multispecies biofilm have also demonstrated the utility of PDT with LED technology and photodithazine2021. PDT with photodithazine has also exhibited antimicrobial activity against MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus)7.

In vitro and in vivo tests have shown PDT using photodithazine to cause immunogenic cancer cell death in murine glioma GL261 and fibrosarcoma MCA205 cells18. Even in association with systemic photodithazine administration, PDT has been associated with complete responses in 53.7% of cases and partial regression in 46.3% of cases of various malignancies, such as skin, oral, laryngeal, bronchial, esophageal, gastric, and vulvar cancers8.

Given the effectiveness of PDT with photodithazine against candida, MSSA, MRSA, biofilm, and cancer cells, this study hypothesized similar therapeutic impacts on acne bacteria through a comparable mechanism utilizing FDT gel.

FDT gel is effective in treating acne-induced inflammation in animal models only during the 15-minute incubation period. On average, existing photosensitizers—ALA and MAL—have been shown to take more than 1 hour of pre-incubation time for acne treatment131516. However, the shorter application time of photodithazine, compared with other photosensitizers, is meaningful in that it minimizes patient discomfort and reduces the duration of outpatient treatment. Given the observed therapeutic effect after 20 minutes of application in the treatment of denture stomatitis, a significant difference in clinical application (to acne patients) is not anticipated19. Further evaluation of the incubation period, associated with therapeutic effects when applied to clinical practice, is warranted.

In this study, changes in the expression levels of various acne-related inflammatory markers were measured in relation to the mechanism underlying acne development. Changes in IL-1α (an inflammatory cytokine that affects the hyperkeratinization of hair follicle keratin), as well as in IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-8 (which are inflammatory triggers), were measured. The expression change of TLR2, a innate immune mediator for acne bacteria, and the final activated inflammatory enzyme (MMP-2) were also measured. Compared with the control group, there was a general decline in these markers after PDT treatment, with the most significant decrease observed in the group treated with 15 minutes of PDT.

Microscopic examination of animal skin biopsies confirmed that inflammatory lesions of the epidermis and dermis were induced after acne bacteria inoculation, and subsequent treatment ameliorated this inflammation. The decrease in inflammatory lesions was the greatest after 15 minutes of PDT.

Changes in the expression levels of NLRP3, caspase, and SREBP1, as well as inflammatory markers IL-1α and TNF-α, were measured through immunochemical staining. NLRP3, an inflammasome component, is well known to be involved in the development of acne inflammation22. Caspase has been reported to be activated in relation to the action of neutrophils by acne bacteria23. SREBP1 is associated with lipogenesis and sebum production, which are among the mechanisms of acne development24. Relative to the expression levels in the normal control group, the expression of the relevant inflammatory markers increased after C. acnes injection, and the rate of decrease was relatively high after 15 minutes of PDT treatment.

The experimental group subjected to 15 minutes of PDT exhibited the highest relative reduction in phenotypic lesion severity, which mirrored the patterns of the molecular and histological biomarkers. Furthermore, patch-type micro-LEDs were used in this study to achieve the effect of PDT treatment. These can be applied directly to local lesions in the form of patches, minimizing side effects while maximizing the preservation of normal skin.

The limitation of this study is that a single experienced experimenter was administered with quantitative acne bacteria in the same area, but it is difficult to ascertain whether the same level of acne inflammation occurred in all experimental animals.

This in vivo experimental study demonstrated the changes of biomarkers associated with inflammation, immunological responses, sebum production, and follicular hyperkeratosis, which are the mechanisms underlying acne development before and after PDT for acne bacteria induced inflammation in mice model. Therefore, it is meaningful that PDT using micro-LED technology and photodithazine was effective not only for visually improving inflammatory lesions caused by acne but also at the molecular and histological levels. In order to actually use photodithazine as a photosensitizer in treating acne, more research regarding the residual time on the skin and the wash-out time after the photosensitizer application is also needed to address the need for safety assurance and sun protection after PDT treatment in real-world clinical practice.
