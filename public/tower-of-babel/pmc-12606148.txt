Evaluation of complete diesel replacement using propanol and biodiesel blends with carbon nanotube additives
Authors: Yahya Çelebi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As environmental concerns intensify globally and regulations on fossil fuel consumption become increasingly stringent, the development of clean, sustainable energy sources for diesel engines has become an urgent priority. With this aim, this research explored complete diesel replacement with biodiesel-propanol and three carbon nanotube types. These carbon nanotubes (SWCNT, DWCNT, and MWCNT) were added to biodiesel-propanol blends (90% biodiesel, 10% propanol) at 100 ppm concentration. Engine tests were performed at 1500 rpm and various operation loads. Combustion analysis revealed that SWCNT and MWCNT blends increased cylinder pressure by 0.2% and 0.7%, respectively, while DWCNT reduced it by 11.9% compared to diesel. Heat release rates improved by 13.1%, 11.2%, and 13.0% for SWCNT, DWCNT, and MWCNT blends, respectively. Performance metrics showed increased brake specific fuel consumption for all carbon nanotube blends compared to conventional diesel, with average increases of 9.2% for SWCNT, 12.7% for DWCNT, and 13.2% for MWCNT. Despite higher fuel consumption, brake thermal efficiency improved with B90 + SWCNT, achieving the highest gain at 6.3%, followed by DWCNT (3.1%) and MWCNT (1.5%). Emissions testing demonstrated significant benefits, with nitrogen oxides emissions decreasing by 8.4% (MWCNT), 7.6% (DWCNT), and 7.3% (SWCNT). Hydrocarbon emissions improved by up to 40.2% with carbon nanotube inclusion, particularly at higher loads. MWCNT was the most effective for reducing carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide emissions. These findings suggest MWCNT-enhanced blends could replace conventional diesel with lower emissions despite slightly higher fuel consumption.

Introduction

The global energy landscape is experiencing unprecedented transformation, driven by rapid population growth, infrastructure development, and technological advancement1. According to the U.S. Energy Information Agency, global energy consumption has dramatically increased from 280 quadrillion Btu in 1980 to approximately 619 quadrillion Btu in 20232. Projections indicate a further 37% surge in global energy demand by 2050, with China and India expected to account for 15% of this growth3.

Diesel engines have become integral to modern society, distinguished by their robust construction, minimal maintenance requirements, operational simplicity, superior brake thermal efficiency (BTE), and lower brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC)4. However, these advantages are accompanied by significant both environmental and health challenges. The emissions from diesel engines encompass both regulated pollutants, including particulate matter, carbon monoxide (CO), hydrocarbons (HC), carbon dioxide (CO2), and nitrogen oxides (NOx)5,6 and unregulated emissions such as acetaldehyde, propylene, formaldehyde, ethene, 1,3-butadiene, ethyne, and BTX (benzene, toluene, and xylene)7,8.

The impact of these emissions has escalated to critical levels globally. These emissions have been associated with multiple serious health conditions, including respiratory infections, heart disease, bronchitis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease9,10. The World Health Organization reported that atmospheric contamination led to 4.2 million early fatalities globally throughout 201911. The environmental impacts pose significant concerns through multiple phenomena including climate change, atmospheric warming, acid precipitation, ozone layer deterioration, and photochemical smog formation12. Fossil fuel combustion has significantly increased CO2 and other greenhouse gas emissions, leading to varied temperature rises worldwide13,14. September 2023 was recorded as the hottest month on record with temperatures surpassing the 1991–2020 average by more than 2.5 °C15.

With increasingly strict exhaust emission standards worldwide, conventional fuels are likely to be replaced with alternative energy sources. The use of renewable energy in compression ignition engines is expected to grow gradually. Research identifies biodiesel and alcohol as the two predominant biofuel alternatives compatible with diesel engine applications16,17. Alcohols have shown promise as substitutes in compression ignition engines, with their application projected to increase. Research into alcohols demonstrates practical significance and promising prospects18. Various additives, including alcohols, antioxidants, cetane number enhancers, and metal oxides, are being introduced with biodiesel and diesel blends to boost performance and diminish exhaust pollutants19. Fuel attributes affecting operational parameters in compression ignition engines are presented in Fig. 120.Fig. 1Fuel attributes affecting operational parameters in compression ignition engines.

Fuel attributes affecting operational parameters in compression ignition engines.

The concept of using biofuels in diesel engines dates back to 1900, when Rudolf Diesel first showcased his engine at the World Fair in Paris21. While biodiesel usage presents certain drawbacks, such as higher BSFC profiles and NOx emissions, it offers notable advantages, including reduced CO emissions, absence of aromatic content, and lower particulate matter emissions22. The effectiveness of alcohols as compression ignition engine fuels is primarily determined by their carbon number, which directly influences mixture stability and combustion behavior23.

An effective approach for significantly reducing exhaust emissions involves the application of numerous techniques such as exhaust gas after-treatment and additives like nanomaterials. Researchers explore nano-additives for biodiesel, including cerium oxide (CeO2), alumina (Al2O3), titanium oxide (TiO2), zinc oxide (ZnO), zinc dioxide (ZnO2), copper oxide (CuO), and iron oxide (Fe2O3)24,25. Additionally, carbon nanotubes (CNTs) as nano-additives in diesel–biodiesel mixtures have gained significant attention for their potential to boost engine performance and lessen harmful pollutants. Solmaz et al. (2021) studied multi-walled carbon nanotube (MWCNT) effects on diesel–biodiesel mixtures, revealing that increasing MWCNT from 25 to 100 ppm significantly dropped emissions while slightly increasing BTE and decreasing BSFC by up to 8.7%26. Sulochana et al. (2024) demonstrated that B20 (80% diesel with 20% biodiesel) with 50 ppm of MWCNT exhibited a 25% improvement in BTE and a 17% decrease in BSFC in comparison with B20, while NOx emissions were diminished by up to 36% in comparison with pure diesel and 43% in comparison to B2027. Similarly, Çılğın (2024) performed experiments with single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNT) and double-walled carbon nanotube (DWCNT) demonstrated significant improvements in combustion parameters and decreased CO and smoke emissions28. Gundoshmian et al. (2021) reported that adding carboxylated multi-walled carbon nanotube to 5% biodiesel and 95% diesel fuel enhanced power by 13.0% and 16.5% in comparison to pure diesel, while reducing CO emissions by 10.26% and HC emissions by 16.33%29. Najafi and Shadidi (2024) demonstrated comprehensive research that carbon nanotubes enhanced brake power, torque, and BTE by up to 4.8, 3.2% and 5.2%, respectively while lowering BSFC up to 6.9%. In contrast, HC, CO2, and NOx emissions were obtained higher up to 5.2%, 8.7% and 6.8%, respectively for carbon nanotube including blends30.

Currently, many countries have been integrating biofuels like ethanol and biodiesel into transportation systems, driving researchers to seek more efficient alternatives. Extensive research suggests that adding CNTs to fuel can boost combustion behavior and operational performance while reducing exhaust pollutants. Additionally, research also demonstrates that blending biodiesel with alcohol creates an effective alternative fuel combination capable of replacing conventional diesel. However, existing literature reveals a gap in research on the fuel performance of a diesel engine powered with the blends of biodiesel, propanol, with the addition of SWCNT, DWCNT and MWCNT. Although the promising properties of CNTs and biodiesel-propanol blends have both been studied separately, no research has combined them for full diesel replacement. This study explores their impact on the engine performance, combustion behavior, and emission patterns of a single-cylinder compression ignition engine. SWCNT, DWCNT, and MWCNT were added to biodiesel-propanol mixture (90% biodiesel with 10% propanol) at concentrations of 100 ppm to evaluate the fuel performance of these test fuels for complete diesel replacement. Although existing global policies establish biofuel targets between 5.5% and 20% through 2040, this study examines complete diesel substitution to assess potential future deployment scenarios. This research seeks to offer insights into the viability of expanded biodiesel utilization, going beyond present policy requirements.

Materials and methodology

Fuel preparation and characterization

Ultra-low sulphur diesel (ULSD) served as the comparison fuel for this experimental study, with all subsequent fuel blends systematically evaluated against both ULSD and pure biodiesel (B100). The biodiesel was sourced from a commercial company and produced from soya oil. To enhance the physical and chemical properties of propanol and biodiesel blend, carbon nanotubes were incorporated. Based on their carbon atom layer count, CNTs are commercially available in three types, including SWCNT, DWCNT, and MWCNT. The experimental fuels consisted of biodiesel and propanol in precise volumetric ratios of 90:10 (named B90), with each blend enhanced with carbon nanotubes at a concentration of 100 ppm per liter of fuel mixture to optimize performance characteristics. The preparation process employed ultrasonic mixing performed at 24 kHz for 30 mins, ensuring uniform dispersion of nanotubes throughout the fuel blend, while temperature was carefully maintained at 25 ± 2 °C during the mixing process. Exhaust emissions were measured using a Capalec CAP 3200 gas analyzer. The measuring device was calibrated before measurements with its sensor positioned at the exhaust pipe exit 1.5 m from the exhaust manifold. In-cylinder pressure (CP) measurements were obtained using an Optrand D33294-Q pressure transducer with 1.35 mV/PSI sensitivity. Specialized combustion analysis software recorded CP, rate of pressure rise (RoPR), cumulative heat release, mean gas temperature (MGT) and heat release rate (HRR) relative to crankshaft position. Before initiating trials, extensive apparatus checks were executed, encompassing assessment of motor lubricant level, lubricant contamination conditions, and ventilation filter conditions. All measurement equipment underwent calibration and validation for precision. The engine was run until achieving stable exhaust heat parameters to ensure dependable and uniform data collection. Additionally, every test was conducted in triplicate, with average figures employed to generate visual representations of combustion, performance and emission data.

The uncertainty evaluation considers individual parameters which are then combined using established statistical methods to determine the total system uncertainty. To minimize potential errors, the total uncertainty calculations incorporated contributions from all measuring instruments utilized in the experimental setup43. Result and discussion

Physicochemical characterization

GC–MS analysis

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis reveals the relative percentages of fatty acids in soya oil and biodiesel in Fig. 9, demonstrating significant compositional changes during the transesterification process. Linoleic acid, a polyunsaturated fatty acid, underwent substantial reduction from 51.5% in soya oil to 23.2% in biodiesel, indicating its effective conversion into methyl esters. Conversely, oleic acid, a monounsaturated fatty acid, increased dramatically from 25.9% in soya oil to 50.8% in biodiesel, establishing its dominance in the biodiesel composition. This pronounced shift reflects the preferential conversion of polyunsaturated fatty acids transesterification. Saturated fatty acids, including palmitic acid and stearic acid, exhibited moderate increases in biodiesel, with palmitic acid rising from 10.9% to 14.0% and stearic acid from 4.5% to 5.3%. These changes indicate their relative stability during the transesterification process. Minor fatty acids, such as gamma-linolenic acid and cis-11-eicosenoic acid, showed slight decreases in biodiesel, reflecting their partial conversion during the reaction. The resulting fatty acid profile of biodiesel demonstrated a higher proportion of monounsaturated and saturated fatty acids compared to soya oil. This compositional shift contributes to enhanced oxidative stability and improved fuel properties compared to soya oil.Fig. 9GC–MS analysis of soya oil and its derived biodiesel.

GC–MS analysis of soya oil and its derived biodiesel.

FTIR analysis

Figure 10 displays the Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis results for the produced soya vegetable oil biodiesel. The FTIR spectra highlighted the chemical transformation from soya oil to biodiesel during transesterification. Biodiesel exhibits a sharp carbonyl (C = O) stretching peak at 1742.7 cm⁻1, indicative of fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs), compared to soya oil’s triglyceride carbonyl peak at 1741.1 cm⁻1, with the slight shift and increased intensity confirming ester formation. Prominent C-O stretching bands in biodiesel at 1158.9 cm⁻1 and 1227.1 cm⁻1, stronger than in soya oil, signify the presence of methoxy groups (-OCH3) from methanol incorporation. The absence of hydroxyl (O–H) signals in the 3000–3500 cm⁻1 region in biodiesel verifies glycerol removal, a key transesterification byproduct, while soya oil may show weak glycerol-related signals. Methylene (C-H) stretching peaks at 2922 cm⁻1 and 2853 cm⁻1 remain consistent in both, reflecting preserved fatty acid chains. The biodiesel spectrum lacks triglyceride-specific C–O–C stretching (~ 1100 cm⁻1), further confirming FAME formation, and aligns with ASTM D6751 standards, indicating enhanced oxidative stability and fuel properties. These spectral changes robustly validate the successful conversion of soya oil to biodiesel.Fig. 10Comparative FT-IR spectra of soya oil and its derived biodiesel.

Comparative FT-IR spectra of soya oil and its derived biodiesel.

NMR analysis

Figure 11 shows the comparative hydrogen-1 Nuclear Magnetic Resonance spectroscopy (1H-NMR) spectrum of soya oil and its derived biodiesel. In the 1H-NMR spectrum of biodiesel, the most prominent feature was the appearance of a singlet at approximately 3.6 ppm, which corresponded to the protons of the methoxy group (-OCH3) in FAMEs. This signal was a key indicator of the transesterification process, as it was absent from the soya oil. Additionally, the α-methylene protons (-CH2-) of biodiesel, resonating at 2.2 to 2.4 ppm in the 1H-NMR spectrum, shift slightly from soya oil due to transesterification is justified by changes in their chemical environment. Transesterification converts triglycerides of soya oil to fatty acid methyl esters of biodiesel, replacing the glycerol backbone with a methoxy group (-OCH3). This alters the electron density and steric environment around α-methylene protons, causing subtle resonance shifts. Studies by Deviren and Çılgın41, and Tamrat et al.1 also confirms this behavior. The aliphatic chain protons, which included the methylene (-CH2-) and terminal methyl (-CH3) groups, appeared in the ranges of 1.2–1.6 ppm and 0.8–1.0 ppm, respectively, and were common to both biodiesel and soya oil. However, in soya oil, the glycerol backbone protons resonated around 4.1–4.3 ppm, a feature that disappeared in biodiesel, confirming the conversion of triglycerides to methyl esters.Fig. 11Comparative 1H-NMR spectrum of soya oil and its derived biodiesel.

Comparative 1H-NMR spectrum of soya oil and its derived biodiesel.

Figure 12 illustrates the comparative Carbon-13 Nuclear Magnetic Resonance spectroscopy (13C-NMR) spectrum of soya oil and its derived biodiesel. In the13C-NMR spectrum, biodiesel 13C-NMR exhibits a distinct methoxy carbon peak at 51–53 ppm, absent in soya oil, due to methanol’s reaction with triglycerides forming FAMEs, confirming successful conversion. Glycerol backbone carbons (62–68 ppm) in soya oil disappear in biodiesel, as transesterification removes glycerol, reducing viscosity and enhancing combustion. While the combustion curves of the test fuels varied according to the load conditions, the CP curves remained relatively consistent with each other. Results of exhaust emissions.

CO2 emissions were observed to increase with rising load for all test fuels. This rise is associated with improved combustion efficiency. Additionally, as the load increases, the quantity of fuel burned also increases, leading to higher CO2 emissions50. Compared to ULSD, CO2 emissions increased by approximately 4.1% and 2.0% for B90 + SWCNT and B90 + DWCNT, respectively, while B100 showed a 2.0% decrease, and B90 + MWCNT remained unchanged on average. The higher CO2 emissions in CNT-enhanced blends are due to the higher oxygen availability in propanol. Moreover, the increased CO2 emissions in CNT fuels can be connected to their greater combustion efficiency, resulting in more complete combustion. Another contributing factor is the lower heating value of biodiesel and propanol, which produces less brake engine power than that of ULSD at the same load. However, biodiesel blends produced lower CO2 emissions compared to ULSD. The higher CO2 concentrations for SWCNT and DWCNT are thought to result from their catalytic effects, which enhance combustion efficiency and increase CO2 emissions.

HC emissions occur when excessively rich fuel–air mixtures enter the cylinder and burn incompletely. The average HC emission reductions for B100, B90 + SWCNT, B90 + DWCNT and B90 + MWCNT were observed to be 51.2%, 2.4%, 40.2% and 28.3%, respectively, compared to ULSD. These reductions can primarily be connected to improved combustion, enabled by the oxygen content in biodiesel and propanol. Furthermore, the inclusion of CNTs contributed to lower HC emissions due to their high surface area-to-volume ratio and strong catalytic properties, which enhanced the ignition and air–fuel mixing characteristics. This improvement prevented the carbon activation temperature from being reached, enabling complete combustion and thereby reducing HC emissions49. These findings are further supported by the BTE results.

The most significant advantage of the CNT-enhanced biodiesel blends was evident in NOₓ emissions. The average decrease in NOₓ emissions with the usage of B100, B90 + SWCNT, B90 + DWCNT and B90 + MWCNT were 6.5%, 7.3%, 7.6% and 8.4%, respectively, when compared to ULSD fuel. Several factors explain these reductions. First, the high latent heat of vaporization of propanol in B90 blends creates a cooling effect in the combustion chamber, lowering peak combustion temperatures, which are a primary driver of thermal NOₓ formation. This cooling effect is particularly pronounced due to the oxygen content of propanol, which enhances combustion efficiency but moderates temperature spikes, as evidenced by the lower mean gas temperature. Second, the catalytic properties of CNTs, with their high surface area-to-volume ratios, improve fuel–air mixing and promote more uniform combustion, reducing localized hot spots that favor NOₓ formation. The superior performance of B90 + MWCNT likely stems from the MWCNT structure, which offers enhanced thermal conductivity and better dispersion in the fuel matrix, further optimizing combustion and minimizing temperature gradients. Additionally, the oxygen-rich composition of biodiesel and propanol in B90 blends facilitates more complete combustion, reducing the availability of HC that can contribute to prompt NOₓ formation. However, the study revealed that NOₓ emissions increased at higher engine loads for all fuels, likely due to higher fuel injection rates and elevated CP, which shorten ignition delay and increase thermal NOₓ formation. The presence of CNTs mitigates this rise by enhancing combustion efficiency, particularly for B90 + MWCNT, which consistently outperformed other blends.

Conclusions

In this paper, a serios study was conducted to evaluate diesel engine performance using propanol-biodiesel fuel mixtures enhanced with two types of carbon nanotubes. Testing incorporated single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNT), double-walled carbon nanotube (DWCNT) and multi-walled carbon nanotube (MWCNT) to assess their impact on engine ignition, combustion processes, emission outputs, and operational performance parameters. Tests showed that SWCNT and MWCNT blends slightly increased cylinder pressure by 0.2 percent and 0.7 percent, respectively, while DWCNT blends reduced it by 11.9 percent compared to ultra-low sulfur diesel (ULSD). Heat release rate improved by up to 13.1 percent for all CNT blends. Brake specific fuel consumption increased by 9.2 percent for SWCNT, 12.7 percent for DWCNT, and 13.2 percent for MWCNT blends, but brake thermal efficiency improved, with B90 + SWCNT achieving the highest gain at 6.3 percent, followed by DWCNT at 3.1 percent and MWCNT at 1.5 percent. Emissions improved significantly, with nitrogen oxides reduced by up to 8.4 percent (MWCNT), hydrocarbons by up to 40.2 percent (DWCNT), and carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide by up to 12.5 percent (MWCNT).

However, testing was limited to a single-cylinder, steady-state engine configuration. Comprehensive research must be conducted on commercial engines before these fuels are ready for practical applications. Long-term durability studies and cold-start behavior evaluation remain to be investigated. Additional studies are required for multi-cylinder validation, transient load testing, and long-term CNT stability in fuel systems. The overall experimental results demonstrate that both SWCNT and MWCNT are promising additives for alternative fuel blends in diesel engines.
