The concurrence of osteochondromatosis and bone infarction in a patient with anterior cruciate ligament rupture: a rare case report
Authors: Tai Duc Tran, Duc Hoang Tran, Vuong Ngoc Vu, Toan Nguyen Thanh, Tri Quang Le
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Synovial osteochondromatosis and bone infarction are two uncommon conditions that typically present independently and are frequently misdiagnosed due to their nonspecific symptoms. This case highlights an exceptionally rare clinical scenario, underscoring the importance of meticulous imaging evaluation and integrated surgical management.Presentation of case:A 38-year-old male diver with a history of heavy smoking presented with knee instability, pain, and swelling following trauma. Examination revealed anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture with concomitant meniscal tears. Imaging demonstrated multiple intra-articular calcified loose bodies, synovial proliferation consistent with synovial osteochondromatosis, and bone infarctions of the distal femur and proximal tibia. Arthroscopic management included loose body removal, synovectomy, meniscal repair, tibial drilling for infarct revascularization, and all-inside ACL reconstruction. Histopathology confirmed chronic synovitis and osteochondromatosis. At the 12-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated significant functional improvement, as assessed by clinical outcome scores.Discussion:The patient’s occupation and smoking habit may have contributed to vascular compromise, repetitive microtrauma, and synovial metaplasia. Arthroscopy offers a minimally invasive approach, enabling the simultaneous treatment of all lesions. Persistent mild pain was likely related to underlying bone infarction.Conclusion:To the best of our knowledge, this is an extremely rare case involving the concurrence of synovial osteochondromatosis, bone infarction, and ACL rupture in a single patient. Arthroscopic management was feasible and was associated with satisfactory short-term clinical and functional improvement in this patient.

Introduction

Synovial osteochondromatosis is an uncommon benign condition characterized by the formation of cartilaginous bodies resulting from the metaplastic transformation of the synovial membrane[1]. Males between the ages of 20 and 50 are more susceptible, with an incidence of approximately 1 per 100 000[1,2]. It usually presents with chronic joint pain and swelling, stiffness, crepitus, giving way, catching, or locking[3]. Radiographs usually demonstrate multifocal, spheroidal, articular or peri-articular calcific densities, whereas magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may reveal T2-hyperintense chondroid lesions in 5–30% of cases without radiographic abnormalities[4,5].

The term “bone infarction” refers to the death of bone and marrow tissue caused by insufficient blood supply (ischemia) without infection, and it typically occurs in the metaphysis or diaphysis of a long bone[6] and also occurs in axial bones[7]. Bone infarction affects patients of all ages and is associated with both traumatic and non-traumatic risk factors, such as corticosteroid use, Caisson disease, and cigarette smoking[8–11]. Bone infarction is often asymptomatic or presents with nonspecific symptoms; therefore, imaging findings are essential for diagnosis[6,7,11].

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture is a common injury occurring during sports and exercise, and is frequently associated with meniscal tears[12,13]. ACL reconstruction using the all-inside technique is one of the most common surgical methods that minimizes postoperative bleeding, soft tissue damage, and especially bone loss, as well as postoperative pain[14–16].

This case report presents a comprehensive clinical dossier, including the diagnosis and treatment of a patient with an ACL rupture who presented with two uncommon diseases occurring simultaneously, reported in accordance with the SCARE 2025 checklist[17].

Presentation of case

A 38-year-old male diver with 20 years of occupational diving experience and a 20-pack-year smoking history was admitted to our hospital with complaints of instability and pain in his right knee during ambulation. He sustained a fall while fishing and reported hearing a “pop” in his knee, followed by immediate pain and swelling. Symptoms partially improved after 2 weeks of self-medication with pain relievers and anti-inflammatory drugs. He also reported experiencing dull, vague pain in his right knee that occurred intermittently before his injury. However, he could not recall the exact time of onset. He denied a history of corticosteroid use and alcohol abuse. Routine hematological investigations were within normal limits. Autoimmune serological testing was not performed because there were no clinical features suggestive of an autoimmune disorder.

Clinical examination revealed right knee instability, with positive Lachman, anterior drawer, pivot-shift, McMurray, and Apley compression tests. The knee range of motion (ROM) was full. Plain radiographs and computed tomography (CT) demonstrated well-defined intramedullary geographic lesions in the distal femur and proximal tibia, characterized by central medullary lucency on radiographs (corresponding to low attenuation on CT) surrounded by serpiginous sclerotic margins – findings consistent with chronic bone infarction (Fig. 1A,B). Multiple calcified intra-articular nodules were also identified. The MRI revealed a complete rupture of the ACL, tears in both the medial and lateral menisci, and geographic intramedullary lesions involving the distal femur and proximal tibia with relatively well-defined margins and heterogeneous signal intensity on T2-weighted images, further suggestive of bone infarction (Fig. 1C). No evidence of subchondral collapse was identified. Bone scintigraphy was performed to evaluate the possibility of multifocal skeletal involvement and to support the diagnosis of bone infarction and showed increased radiotracer uptake in the right knee and shoulder (Fig. 1D). Step 2: Knee debridement and subtotal synovectomy involving the anterior compartment, suprapatellar pouch, and medial and lateral gutters were performed to remove the synovial osteochondromatosis lesions. Biopsy specimens were collected. The loose bodies were removed using a hemostat prior to the repair of the menisci (Fig. 2E).

Step 3: Several tunnels, each with a diameter of 1.5 mm and a depth of 10–15 mm, were created in the infarcted distal femur and proximal tibia to achieve core decompression.

Step 4: ACL reconstruction and meniscal repair were performed using an all-inside technique with a 9-mm hamstring autograft (Fig. 3). Femoral and tibial sockets were prepared with lengths of 20–25 mm. Cortical button fixation (TightRope) was used for both ends. After drilling femoral and tibial tunnels, bone tissue was collected for histopathological examination. Histopathological examination revealed multiple well-circumscribed nodules of hyaline cartilage within fibroconnective synovial tissue. The nodules contained clustered chondrocytes embedded in abundant chondroid matrix with focal calcification and areas of endochondral ossification. Mild cytologic atypia without significant mitotic activity was observed. These findings confirmed the diagnosis of synovial osteochondromatosis (Fig. 2F). After the stability of the knee was confirmed, the portals were closed. A hinged knee immobilizer was applied for 4 weeks to protect the reconstructed ACL. The patient was also instructed to follow a rehabilitation program with delayed weight-bearing, compared with other ACL reconstruction cases.

Overall, the patient’s symptoms have improved significantly, with normal knee ROM. Discussion

Pathogenesis and risk factors

Given the patient’s 20-year occupational history as a professional diver, prolonged mechanical loading and repetitive joint stress may have promoted chronic synovial inflammation. Chronic synovial inflammation has been implicated in synovial cell proliferation and chondroid metaplasia, contributing to the development of secondary synovial osteochondromatosis[18]. Furthermore, prolonged exposure to hyperbaric conditions may promote intravascular occlusion and impaired bone perfusion, both recognized mechanisms underlying bone infarction. Heavy cigarette smoking, the only established risk factor identified in our patient, may also have contributed to the development of bone infarction[19].

Imaging findings

A whole-body bone scintigraphy was indicated because a systemic or multifocal ischemic bone process was suspected beyond the area assessed on knee MRI and CT. This whole-body screening successfully identified an asymptomatic concurrent lesion in the right shoulder. In this clinical context, the increased radiotracer uptake demonstrated the active revascularization and bone remodeling phase of osteonecrosis/bone infarction, rather than an acute infection or stress fracture.

Treatment and outcome

Synovial osteochondromatosis is treated surgically by loose-body removal with or without synovectomy. Arthroscopy is often indicated for accessible lesions, whereas open surgery is required for lesions that are widespread or difficult to remove arthroscopically[1]. The significance of synovectomy remains controversial, with some studies suggesting no benefit or recommending it only for recurrent cases[20–22], whereas others advocate synovectomy to prevent recurrence[1,3,23].

Arthroscopic surgery was considered an appropriate treatment for this patient because it enabled simultaneous removal of loose bodies, synovial debridement, and ACL reconstruction through a minimally invasive technique. Retrograde drilling of both the distal femur and proximal tibia was performed based on the principles of core decompression, which is commonly used for femoral head osteonecrosis[24]. Although this patient was diagnosed with bone infarction, both conditions share a similar ischemic pathophysiological mechanism. By drilling retrograde tunnels into the infarcted lesions, we aimed to reduce elevated intraosseous pressure, potentially alleviating deep bone pain and creating a biological channel that stimulates neo-angiogenesis (creeping substitution) and bone remodeling.

A delayed weight-bearing rehabilitation program was prescribed to allow sufficient time for the tunnels (created during step 4) to heal and to minimize postoperative pain. The patient reported only mild residual pain during strenuous activities, prolonged walking, or standing. Although the patient’s knee joint has been diagnosed with mild osteoarthritis, bone infarction may also have contributed to the patient’s symptoms.

For the asymptomatic shoulder lesion, follow-up every 6–12 months, or an earlier re-evaluation if abnormal symptoms develop, was recommended, along with oral vitamin C and mineral supplementation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Follow-up was limited because of the long travel distance to our institution, preventing supervised rehabilitation and verification of the recommended home-based program. Continued exposure to intense physical demands and a high-pressure working environment may have affected recovery.

Despite favorable clinical and radiographic outcomes, subtle interval changes in the bone infarction lesions could not be fully assessed due to the absence of a 12-month MRI follow-up.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is an extremely rare case involving concurrent synovial osteochondromatosis, bone infarction, and ACL rupture in a single patient. Arthroscopic management was feasible and associated with satisfactory short-term clinical and functional improvement in this patient.
