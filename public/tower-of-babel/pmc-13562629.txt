The precisely regulated keystone taxa facilitate microbial mineralization of soil organic phosphorus via niche partitioning
Authors: Wenhui Yan, Chengdong Huang, Dandan Li, Jiayin Feng, Gu Feng, Hang Ma, Jan Jansa, John Klironomos, Minghao Zhuang, Fusuo Zhang, Lin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRhizosphere keystone taxa critically drive microbial community stability and soil biogeochemical cycles. However, the manipulation of such taxa remains a challenge. This study simulated plant-mediated modulation of keystone taxa via simplified synthetic root exudates to track their compositional shifts. The combination of luteolin, myristic acid, and glucose enhanced rhizosphere phosphatase activity, significantly enriched Domibacillus indicus D99, and converted it into a keystone taxon. This precise regulation was driven by transcriptional upregulation of C metabolism and an unusual fatty acid assimilation pathway. Additionally, metabolites produced by D. indicus D99 (such as bergapten and lactate) were preferentially utilized by phosphate-mineralizing bacteria, Bacillus sp. C67 and Domibacillus sp. C94. These partner bacteria exhibited less substrate overlap and pronounced resource partitioning, forming an efficient synergistic relationship with D. indicus D99 that amplified rhizosphere phosphatase activity and plant growth. This study highlights opportunities to utilize the ecological roles of keystone taxa in manipulating the microbiome.Subject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Microbiology

Introduction

The rhizosphere microbes play important roles in soil nutrient cycling and plant fitness, which greatly depend on the complex microbial interactions that range from competitive to cooperative1,2. In the rhizosphere, microbes usually form cooperative relationships, i.e., one microbe benefits the others through metabolite secretion. This kind of relationship has been studied for its ability to accelerate the mineralization of soil organic nutrients3,4. Microbial species have different abilities to establish cooperative relationships with other taxa5,6. Normally, species that form more cooperative relationships can exert a strong driving effect on nutrient mineralization7. Due to the high rhizosphere microbial diversity and intricate interaction networks8, we still poorly understand which specific taxon plays a central role in the microbial community, which constrains our potential to manipulate them for improving soil nutrient cycling and plant fitness.

The concept of keystone taxa, firstly proposed by Paine in 1969, was developed to elucidate complex interactions within marine shoreline communities9,10. In microbial communities, keystone taxa refer to highly connected taxonomic units that exert substantial influence on both the structure and function of the microbiome11. Recent advances in computational prediction, synthetic microbial communities, and laboratory manipulation have accelerated the identification of these taxa. Keystone taxa not only drive microbial community assembly12,13 and maintain community stability14–16, but also are closely linked to soil nutrient cycling and plant fitness17,18. Most nutrients in natural ecosystems, such as N and P, exist primarily in organic forms and are therefore unavailable for direct uptake by plant roots19,20. Keystone taxa can upregulate P-mineralizing genes to enhance organic P hydrolysis and improve maize yields21,22. However, keystone taxa are not stable members; they change with alterations in nutritional environments and exert their functions only under specific conditions23,24. In the rhizosphere, the changes in keystone taxa are usually associated with plant root exudates, such as monosaccharides, luteolin, and fatty acids25–28, and such shifts can influence soil nutrient mineralization and plant fitness17,29,30. This indicates that root exudates strongly select for distinct keystone taxa, enabling them to perform different functions under varying contexts. However, such studies rely on bioinformatic analyses without strain isolation and lack experimental validation of keystone taxa’s response advantages31. Therefore, we still lack clarity on why certain microbes are selectively recruited to become keystone taxa in the community.

The changes in keystone taxa within the rhizosphere microbiome usually induce corresponding changes in the abundance of keystone-associated microbial members. The keystone taxa may employ multiple strategies to influence these partners, either through direct cooperation via metabolite cross-feeding32,33 or through secondary metabolite-mediated antagonism that indirectly benefits the partners34,35. For instance, the keystone taxon Sphingomonas sanxanigenens secretes flavonoids to stimulate Funneliformis mosseae spore germination36. Additionally, the keystone taxon Streptomyces secretes albaflavenone to inhibit Pseudomonas, thereby indirectly enhancing cooperation between arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi and phosphate-mineralizing bacteria (PMB)34. However, these one-to-one interactions have limitations in explaining the relationships between keystone taxa and their multiple partners, as multiple partners can simultaneously form cooperative relationships with a single keystone taxon37. In microbial communities, the concept of niche is often employed to explain the existence of the highly biodiverse microbes38,39. The reduced niche overlap minimizes interspecific competition, enabling more species to coexist within a community40. Some microbes benefit from others by altering nutritional niches via their metabolites41,42. However, whether the maintenance of such one-to-many cooperative relationships by keystone taxa is associated with changes in nutritional niches remains poorly investigated.

Moreover, verifying such one-to-many interactions between keystone taxa and multiple partners in complex soil environments remains challenging due to the high diversity and environmental heterogeneity. Synthetic community (SynCom) systems provide a simplified and controllable approach to experimentally test whether one strain can structure microbial communities and maintain cooperative interactions with multiple partners43,44. Therefore, constructing a SynCom based on keystone taxa and their associated partners can help verify whether such one-to-many cooperative relationships exist and whether these interactions can further influence community functions such as nutrient mineralization.

The objective of the present study was to investigate the ecological advantages of keystone taxa in the rhizosphere microbiome and the mechanisms by which they drive soil phosphorus (P) mineralization. Therefore, we selected maize (Zea mays L.) as a model system to investigate P uptake across three distinct soil types (mollisol, fluvo-aquic, and brown soils). This system was chosen because maize is a major global staple crop, and P remains one of the least efficiently utilized fertilizer nutrients45. We simulated plant-mediated modulation of keystone taxa via simplified synthetic root exudates to validate the following hypotheses: (1) simplified synthetic root exudates would selectively enrich specific keystone taxa due to their superior resource utilization; (2) these modulated keystone taxa could reshape the bacterial community structure; and (3) the keystone taxa would further drive the functional performance of multiple partners via niche partitioning and enhance soil P mineralization.

Methods

First, we tracked shifts in keystone taxa through simulated plant-mediated modulation of keystone taxa via simplified synthetic root exudates—these exudates contain both C sources and signaling molecules (Fig. 1a). Subsequently, we screened and obtained the corresponding amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) in the rhizosphere microbiome using isolation and culture methods. These culturable ASVs were used to quantitatively determine phosphatase activity, and the plant growth-promoting functions and ecological functions of the keystone taxa were assessed (Fig. 1b). Through whole-genome sequencing and transcriptomic analysis, we explored the advantages of culturable keystone taxa in responding to simplified synthetic root exudates, and these advantages were verified via in vivo experiments (Fig. 1c). Finally, network analysis, in vitro experiments, metabolomic analysis, and synthetic microbial community (SynCom) experiments were used to verify the mechanisms by which keystone taxa maintain cooperative relationships with their partners (Fig. 1d).

Pot experiment with simplified synthetic root exudates

Maize (Zea mays L., cv. Zhengdan 958) was used as the experimental plant. Seeds were surface-sterilized with 10% (v/v) H₂O₂, germinated, and sown into pots containing 2 kg of soil (soil: sterilized sand = 4:1, w:w). Three soil types were used: mollisol (high fertility), fluvo-aquic and brown soils (moderate fertility). Soils were thoroughly mixed with simplified synthetic root exudate powders prior to sowing, and nutrients were added as described in Supplementary Note 1.

Root exudates naturally contain both C sources for microbial growth (e.g., sugars, organic acids)46–48 and signaling molecules (e.g., flavonoids, aromatic acids) that regulate microbial proliferation25,49,50. To mimic these functions, we designed the simplified synthetic exudates containing myristic acid (a slow-turnover C source)51,52, luteolin (a signaling compound), glucose and fructose (labile C sources to rapidly stimulate bacterial activity)51–53. These individual substances have been proven in previous studies to promote soil organic P mineralization54–56. The treatments were set-up as follows: Ctrl: no addition of compounds; M: myristic acid; ML: myristic acid + luteolin; MLG: myristic acid + luteolin + glucose; MLGF: myristic acid + luteolin + glucose + fructose. Each compound was added at 500 μM kg⁻¹ soil, a concentration previously shown effective54. This corresponded to 36–90 mg C kg⁻¹ soil, close to the estimated daily C released by maize root exudates (15–110 mg C day⁻¹ kg⁻¹ soil)57–60. Five treatments were set up in three soil types, totaling 15 treatments. Each treatment had four replicates. Plants were harvested 8 weeks after sowing. Shoots, rhizosphere soil, and bulk soil were collected for analysis of dry weight, P concentration, soil acid phosphatase (ACP) and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activities4, and Olsen inorganic P61 (Supplementary Note 2).

DNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing

Rhizosphere soil stored at –80°C was used for DNA extraction with the FastDNA SPIN Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentrations were quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, NC2000). The V4–V5 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified with primers 515 F and 907 R under PCR conditions described previously4. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 250 bp; Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Sequencing data were processed using QIIME2 (v2019.4)62. Reads shorter than 374 bp, those with quality scores ≤ 20, those containing ambiguous bases, or those with primer/barcode mismatches were removed. High-quality reads were denoised and clustered into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) using DADA263, with chimera removal. Taxonomic assignments were made with the SILVA138 database64. An average of 84,111 sequences per sample was obtained, and samples were rarefied to 56,720 reads. The 16S rRNA gene sequences are available in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession number PRJNA1294539.

Isolation and identification of culturable bacteria

Maize rhizosphere soil samples (stored at 4°C) from the pot experiment were used for bacterial isolation by dilution spread plating33. One gram of soil was suspended in 9 mL of sterile water, and the suspension was shaken at 200 rpm for 30 min, serially diluted (10⁻¹–10⁻⁶), and 100 μL of the diluted suspension was plated on four types of media: tryptic soy agar (TSA), yeast extract mannitol medium (YEM), Gause’s synthetic agar, and CASO agar (see Supplementary Table 1). Plates were incubated at 30°C for 3 days. Colonies were repeatedly streaked for purity.

A total of 341 isolates were obtained. The V4–V5 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified with primers 515F/907R and sequenced (Sanger sequencing; General BioL Co., Ltd., Chuzhou, China; Supplementary Note 3). Sequences were aligned to ASVs ( > 0.01% relative abundance) using UPARSE (100% similarity, score ≥330, e-value ≤ 1 × 10⁻¹⁷⁰)65. In total, 34 ASVs were matched, accounting for 10.8% of community relative abundance (Supplementary Table 2). Phosphatase activities of isolates were quantified as described previously34 (Supplementary Note 4).

Field microcosm incubation experiment

A total of 34 ASVs were obtained through isolation and cultivation. Among these, ASV_22207 was identified as a keystone taxon, and the corresponding isolate was identified as Domibacillus indicus D99. To evaluate whether this strain plays a role in shaping soil microbial community structure, a microcosm experiment was established with four treatments: Ctrl (no addition), Domi (D. indicus D99 inoculum, OD₆₀₀ = 1, 10 mL), MLG (500 μM kg⁻¹ MLG), and Domi+MLG (D. indicus D99 + 500 μM kg⁻¹ MLG). Field microcosms were constructed using PVC boxes perforated with 2 mm holes and lined with 30 μm nylon mesh to allow nutrient exchange while preventing root penetration (Fig. 1b). Each box contained 200 g of fresh soil with no additional amendments unless specified in the treatment. The boxes were buried at a depth of 15 cm between maize rows in two long-term experimental plots and retrieved after 2 months. Phosphatase activity, DNA extraction, and sequencing were performed as described above. Each treatment included four replicates. D. indicus D99-ASV matches were verified using UPARSE65.

Growth assays with MLG components

To evaluate the direct effects of MLG on the growth of the keystone taxon D. indicus D99, the strain was revived from –80 °C stocks, cultured on LB agar, and then grown in LB broth at 30 °C for 8 h. After centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended in sterile water to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.5 for use as the inoculum. Growth was assayed in M9 minimal medium66 supplemented with 2 mM MgSO₄, 0.1 mM CaCl₂, and 10 μM FeSO₄67. Four media were prepared: glucose (1%); myristic acid (500 μM); glucose + myristic acid; glucose + myristic acid + luteolin. Media (200 μL) were dispensed into 100-well plates, inoculated with 1% (v/v) inoculum, and incubated at 30 °C for 48 h. OD₆₀₀ was recorded every 30 min with a Bioscreen C MBR (Oy Growth Curves Ab Ltd., Finland). Each treatment was replicated four times. Additionally, to verify the growth-promoting potential of MLG across other community members, the same growth assays were performed on the remaining nine strains selected for the SynCom (SynCom; see Synthetic bacterial community and pot experiment in Methods and Supplementary Table 3 for selection criteria). Each treatment was performed with four biological replicates.

Whole-genome sequencing of D. indicus D99

D. indicus D99 was cultured in LB broth (30 °C, 200 rpm, 12 h). DNA was extracted using the FastDNA kit (MP Biomedicals) and quantified with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, NC2000). Whole-genome sequencing was performed on PacBio and Illumina MiSeq platforms (Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai). Functional annotation was performed using BLAST against the GO & KEGG databases. Genome sequences were deposited in NCBI SRA (PRJNA1294539).

Transcriptomic analysis of D. indicus D99

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol (Invitrogen) and assessed with a NanoDrop and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. The rRNA was removed using the Zymo-Seq RiboFree kit. Libraries were prepared via strand-specific cDNA synthesis, adaptor ligation, and size selection (400–500 bp), and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina; Shanghai Personal Biotechnology).

Raw reads were quality-filtered with fastp (v0.22.0), mapped to the reference genome using Bowtie2 (v2.4.1), and counted with HTSeq (v0.9.1). Expression levels were normalized as FPKM. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.38.3) with thresholds of |log₂FC | > 1 and P < 0.05.

Growth rescue assays with fatty acid synthesis inhibitors

D. indicus D99 was cultured in M9 medium with glucose (M9_G) or glucose + myristic acid (M9_GM). Growth inhibition assays used fatty acid synthase II (FASII) inhibitors MAC1377268 and triclosan69–71, dissolved in DMSO and diluted to final concentrations of 0.25–64 µg mL⁻¹ and 0.0025–3.2 µg mL⁻¹, respectively. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was defined as the lowest concentration preventing OD₆₀₀ increase after 18 h.

Rescue assays were conducted by pre-culturing cells in M9_G or M9_GM, then exposing them to inhibitors at MIC and above. OD₆₀₀ was measured every 2 h for 8 h.

Metabolite-mediated interactions

D. indicus D99 was cultured in glucose-M9 medium for 48 h, centrifuged, and the supernatant filtered (0.45 µm) to obtain cell-free supernatant. SynCom isolates were grown in this supernatant or fresh glucose-M9, and growth (OD₆₀₀, 48 h) was monitored with Bioscreen C. ALP activity was measured at 12 h as described previously. Attraction assays were conducted on solid media (Supplementary Note 5). For metabolomics, supernatants incubated with Bacillus sp. C67, Domibacillus sp. C94, Fictibacillus sp. A103, and Bacillus sp. D107 was analyzed using untargeted metabolomics (Supplementary Note 6).

Synthetic bacterial community (SynCom) and pot experiment

From the 34 culturable ASVs, 10 were selected for a SynCom based on relative abundance (>0.1%) and presence in at least two soil types (Supplementary Table 3), including the keystone taxon D. indicus D99, its associated partner PMB, and several non-partner strains identified from the network analysis. These collectively represented 6.86% of the community. Each strain was cultured in LB broth (30 °C, 200 rpm) for 2 days. The bacterial cells were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 5 min, washed twice with sterile water, and resuspended in sterile water to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.5. Equal volumes were combined to create the SynCom inoculum. Maize seeds and soils were prepared as described above, and plants were grown under SynCom inoculation. Plants and rhizosphere soils were harvested after 1 month.

Statistical analyses

Microbial taxa enriched under MLG were identified using LEfSe (LDA score > 3, FDR-adjusted P < 0.05). Taxonomic differences between treatments were tested using Welch’s t-tests (STAMP). Co-occurrence networks were constructed from ASVs ( > 0.01% relative abundance) using Spearman’s correlations (ρ > 0.8, BH-adjusted P < 0.05) and visualized with Cytoscape. Pearson correlations between ASVs and ALP activity were assessed with the Hmisc R package. Growth curves were modelled using ggplot2 (GAM). Transcriptome data were analyzed using DESeq2 (v1.38.3) with the following filtering criteria: |log₂FoldChange | > 1 and a significant P-value < 0.05.

Results

Simplified synthetic root exudates increased plant growth and soil phosphatase activity

We first conducted a pot experiment in the greenhouse using three soil types to test the effects of simplified synthetic root exudates on alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and rhizosphere microbiome composition (Fig. 1a). Compared to the control (Ctrl, no addition of compounds), both M (myristic acid) and ML (myristic acid + luteolin) significantly increased ALP activity in mollisol soil only (P < 0.05; Fig. 2a). In contrast, the MLG treatment (myristic acid, luteolin, and glucose) significantly enhanced maize shoot biomass, shoot P content, and ALP activity in fluvo-aquic and brown soils (P < 0.05; Fig. 2a; Supplementary Fig. 1). The MLGF treatment (myristic acid, luteolin, glucose, and fructose) also increased ALP activity in mollisol and fluvo-aquic soils (P < 0.05; Fig. 2a). Simplified synthetic root exudates increased maize shoot biomass and shoot P content, with efficacy differing among soil types (Supplementary Fig. 1). Overall, simplified synthetic root exudates significantly increased plant growth, shoot P uptake, and soil ALP activity, with the strongest effects observed under the MLG treatment in moderately fertile soils. These results suggest that enhanced P mineralization and plant P uptake may be linked to shifts in the rhizosphere bacterial community.

Simplified synthetic root exudates altered rhizosphere bacterial community composition

To determine whether the observed changes in plant growth and P mineralization were associated with shifts in the rhizosphere microbiome, the bacterial community composition was examined by Illumina MiSeq sequencing of the V4-V5 region of the 16S rRNA gene. None of the treatments significantly affected the bacterial alpha diversity compared to Ctrl (P > 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 2). At the phylum level, Proteobacteria, Bacteroidota, Actinobacteriota, and Firmicutes dominated in the rhizosphere. In fluvo-aquic soil, Firmicutes increased significantly under MLG and MLGF (8.8% in Ctrl vs. 26.6% and 25.9%, respectively; Fig. 2b), while Proteobacteria decreased significantly (51.8% in Ctrl vs. 40.45% and 41.97%; P < 0.05).

Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) revealed that the synthetic exudates caused greater community shifts in fluvo-aquic and brown soils than in mollisol soil (ANOSIM: mollisol R = 0.003, P = 0.463; fluvo-aquic R = 0.568, P = 0.001; brown R = 0.174, P = 0.043). In mollisol soil, none of the treatments differed significantly from Ctrl, whereas MLG (P = 0.028) and MLGF (P = 0.029) significantly altered communities in fluvo-aquic soil, and M (P = 0.041) altered communities in brown soil (Fig. 2c). Among all treatments, MLG consistently showed the strongest separation from Ctrl (Fig. 2c; Supplementary Fig. 3). Changes in bacterial community composition correlated significantly with maize P uptake in fluvo-aquic (R² = 0.34, P < 0.001) and brown soils (R² = 0.12, P = 0.005), but no significant correlation was observed in mollisol soil (P = 0.906, Fig. 1d).

Together, these results demonstrated that synthetic exudates, particularly MLG, significantly altered rhizosphere bacterial community composition in moderately fertile soils, and these community shifts were significantly associated with plant P uptake. This suggests that specific microbial taxa enriched under MLG may play key roles in organic P mineralization.

Keystone taxon ASV_22207 associated with organic P mineralization enriched under MLG treatment

Building on the correlation between community shifts and P uptake, network and enrichment analyses were employed to identify the keystone taxa in rhizosphere. At the genus level, three taxa were significantly enriched in MLG: Domibacillus, Herpetosiphon, and Microbacterium (LDA score > 3, Kruskal–Wallis test, FDR-adjusted P < 0.05; Fig. 3a). Of these, Domibacillus had the highest LDA score (Fig. 3b). In fluvo-aquic and brown soils, MLG significantly enriched Domibacillus and Herpetosiphon, while Microbacterium was enriched in mollisol soil (Fig. 3b). The relative abundance of Domibacillus increased markedly, from 0.37% in Ctrl to 6.81% in fluvo-aquic soil and from 0.18% to 1.64% in brown soil (P < 0.05; Fig. 3c).

To assess whether ASV-level enrichment matched genus-level patterns, we examined amplicon sequence variants (ASVs >0.01% relative abundance). Four ASVs were consistently enriched in fluvo-aquic and brown soils under MLG: ASV_22207 (Domibacillus), ASV_27167 (Domibacillus), ASV_28547 (Herpetosiphon), and ASV_42228 (f__Comamonadaceae). Among these, ASV_22207 showed the strongest increase (0.32% in Ctrl to 4.68% in fluvo-aquic; 0.14% to 1.43% in brown soil). Both ASV_22207 and ASV_27167 were significantly positively correlated with ALP activity (Fig. 3d, e). Beyond the MLG treatment, no comparable shared enriched ASVs correlated with ALP activity (Supplementary Fig. 4). Therefore, we identified Domibacillus as pivotal in enhancing organic P mineralization, prompting us to conduct a more detailed examination of this taxon.

Co-occurrence network analysis further validated the position of ASV_22207 within the community. The average network degrees of the Ctrl and MLG groups were 32.62 and 36.21, respectively. The number of edges, positive links, clustering coefficients, and network density were also higher in MLG compared with Ctrl, indicating closer microbial associations in the MLG treatment. We also calculated the closeness centrality and degree of each node, and taxa ranking in the top 5% were considered keystone taxa. This result highlighted the keystone role of ASV_22207. This result revealed a shift in keystone taxa, particularly the ASV belonging to the genus Domibacillus. The degree of ASV_22207 significantly increased following MLG treatment, rising from 48 in Ctrl to 58 in MLG. Specifically, ASV_22207 was not a keystone taxon in Ctrl soils but emerged as one of seven keystone ASVs under MLG (Fig. 3f), displaying seven positive correlations with other P-mineralizing ASVs and more than most other keystone nodes (Supplementary Fig. 5). Thus, MLG-driven enrichment of ASV_22207 was tightly associated with soil organic P mineralization and enhanced network connectivity, confirming its role as a keystone taxon in the rhizosphere. While bioinformatic evidence highlighted ASV_22207 as a keystone taxon, its functional validation required the use of cultured isolates.

Domibacillus indicus D99 had moderate P mineralization activity and could drive the change of bacterial community structures

To verify the identity of ASV_22207 and experimentally test its role in P mineralization and community assembly, we isolated rhizosphere bacterial strains and conducted inoculation experiments. We isolated 341 rhizosphere bacterial isolates from the first pot experiment (Fig. 1b; Supplementary Fig. 6). Of these, 34 isolates matched ASVs at 100% 16S rRNA gene similarity, representing 10.38% of the rhizosphere community (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Table 1). The isolate Domibacillus indicus D99 showed 100% identity with ASV_22207 (Supplementary Fig. 7). We quantified the ALP activity of these culturable bacteria, and D. indicus D99 exhibited only moderate phosphatase activity. Another greenhouse experiment was conducted to demonstrate that D. indicus D99 bioinoculant could increase maize biomass and rhizosphere ALP activity (Fig. 4b). Since ASV_22207 did not enrich in mollisol soil, we would only focus on the role of D. indicus D99 in the two medium-fertility soils in the subsequent research. In sterile fluvo-aquic and brown soils, we found that inoculation with D. indicus D99 alone did not significantly affect maize shoot biomass, even with MLG addition. However, inoculation with D. indicus D99 increased rhizosphere ALP activity to varying degrees (57.9% in fluvo-aquic; 32.9% in brown soil; Fig. 4b). These results indicated that D. indicus D99 itself did not have a strong phosphorus-mineralizing function, and the promotion of plant growth was limited when only D. indicus D99 was present.

Therefore, we further confirmed the role of D. indicus D99 in the bacterial community by using a field microcosm (Fig. 1b). Among the 4 treatments we set up, inoculating D. indicus D99 alone significantly changed the structure of the bacterial community. The application of MLG in the field also led to changes in the bacterial community, but when D. indicus D99 was additionally added, this alteration to the community structure was intensified. Such alterations in bacterial community structure were primarily driven by phyla including Acidobacteriota, Proteobacteria, Gemmatimonadota, and Methylomirabilota (Fig. 4d). Compared with the control, the co-occurrence network showed that D. indicus D99 significantly enhanced network complexity and the number of links in the network, whereas soil ALP activity showed no significantly increase in the soil. (P < 0.05; Fig. 4e; Supplementary Figs. 8, 9). Together, D. indicus D99 significantly altered the structure of the bacterial community, and its relative abundance was also enriched by MLG in the field experiments.

Given that D. indicus D99 alone exhibited limited P-mineralizing capacity but significantly reshaped the bacterial community, we hypothesized that it serves as a keystone metabolic hub. To test this, we tracked how it responds to MLG and supports functional partner strains, followed by validation of this cooperative network using a synthetic community.

MLG improved the growth of D. indicus D99 by stimulating its fatty acid synthesis pathway

Since D. indicus D99 was enriched under MLG and reshaped community structure, we next investigated the mechanism by which MLG promoted its growth (Fig. 1c). The whole-genome sequencing revealed two circular chromosomes (3.58 Mbp and 1.36 Mbp), with the highest similarity to D. indicus GCF 023715895.1 (ANI 95.71%; Supplementary Fig. 10). Functional annotation of genes on chromosomes 1 and 2 in the D. indicus D99 genome revealed pathways associated with fatty acid metabolism. Further analysis identified the capacity of D. indicus D99 to utilize two C sources (glucose and fatty acids) within MLG and related metabolic pathways. This suggested that D. indicus D99 may efficiently metabolize the dual-C-source simplified synthetic root exudates. Growth assays revealed that D. indicus D99 could not utilize myristic acid as a sole carbon source; however, when both glucose and myristic acid were supplied, its growth was significantly enhanced compared to growth with glucose alone. Addition of luteolin further increased final biomass despite a slower growth rate (Fig. 5a).

Comparative transcriptomics showed 1340 differentially expressed genes between glucose-only and MLG conditions (P < 0.05), with 645 upregulated and 695 downregulated (Supplementary Fig. 11). Genes in central C metabolism (pentose phosphate pathway, TCA cycle) and fatty acid synthesis (FabD, FabHA, FabH2, FabG, FabF, FabI) were significantly upregulated, while β-oxidation genes were unchanged (Fig. 5b). Genes for flagellar assembly were upregulated, while sporulation genes were downregulated (Fig. 5c).

These results suggested that MLG promoted a metabolically active state in D. indicus D99 by stimulating fatty acid biosynthesis rather than fatty acid catabolism. We used FASII inhibitors to suppress its endogenous fatty acid synthesis pathway. MAC1377268 and triclosan69–71 were FASII inhibitors that specifically targeted the initial condensation and fatty acid elongation steps, respectively in microbial fatty acid synthesis. After determining the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of FASII inhibitors, when D. indicus D99 was exposed to concentrations above the MIC, its growth was significantly inhibited. However, this growth inhibition caused by FASII inhibitors was rescued by the supplementation of myristic acid (Fig. 5e). Thus, myristic acid may be assimilated into membrane lipids, supporting growth under MLG conditions. Given that this growth-promoting effect of MLG is exclusive to D. indicus D99, we then investigated whether the enriched keystone taxon could facilitate other functional bacteria through metabolic interactions.

D. indicus D99 facilitated the partner PMBs through metabolite cross-feeding and niche partitioning

Given the observed interaction between the keystone taxon D. indicus D99 and other PMBs (Fig. 3f), we hypothesized that D. indicus D99 facilitated PMBs growth through metabolite cross-feeding. We selected 9 candidate isolates from our culturable microbial collection to investigate the ecological role of D. indicus D99 (Figs. 1d; 6a). Correlation analysis in first pot experiment identified Bacillus sp. C67 and Domibacillus sp. C94 as positively associated with D. indicus D99 (P < 0.05; Fig. 6b). Growth assays confirmed that both strains grew significantly better in D. indicus D99 spent medium than in fresh medium, with higher ALP activity, whereas other bacterial taxa remained unresponsive (Fig. 6b; Supplementary Fig. 12). On solid M9 medium, D. indicus D99 exhibited strong attraction to Bacillus sp. C67 and Domibacillus sp. C94 (Fig. 6c, d). Furthermore, no significant attraction was observed toward other bacterial strains (Fig. 6d).

These findings indicate a keystone-driven cooperative network in which MLG indirectly influences PMB through the keystone taxon.

Enrichment of D. indicus D99 promoted P mineralization through the partnership with two PMBs in the SynCom

To test our overall hypothesis that simplified synthetic root exudates (MLG) enriched keystone taxa, which in turn reshaped community structure and facilitated partner strains through niche partitioning to enhance community-level P mineralization, we constructed a SynCom consisting of D. indicus D99 and nine representative rhizosphere bacterial strains (Fig. 6a). All selected strains had relative abundances greater than 0.1%, consistent with the criteria used in the network analysis, and together accounted for 6.86% of the total community relative abundance. The SynCom was deliberately composed to include the keystone taxon D. indicus D99, its cooperative partner PMBs (Bacillus sp. C67 and Domibacillus sp. C94), as well as several non-partner strains. Notably, none of the other nine isolates (excluding D. indicus D99) exhibited enhanced growth under MLG supplementation, even though some of these strains were able to metabolize myristic acid when cultured individually (Supplementary Fig. 14).

In sterile fluvo-aquic soil, SynCom inoculation with MLG significantly increased shoot biomass and ALP activity, while only ALP activity was enhanced in brown soil (P < 0.05; Fig. 8a, b). MLG also altered SynCom composition in fluvo-aquic soil (Fig. 8c), strongly enriching D. indicus D99 (from 1.19% to 19.71%, P < 0.05), though this effect was not significant in brown soil (Fig. 8c). Correlation analysis between the 9 isolates and D. indicus D99, validated against initial pot experiments, revealed a consistent and stable partnership with two PMBs: Bacillus sp. C67 and Domibacillus sp. C94 (Fig. 8d). These results demonstrate that MLG specifically fosters a stable cooperative partnership between D. indicus D99 and two PMBs, leading to enhanced P mineralization.

Discussion

Manipulating soil microbial communities to enhance nutrient cycling and plant growth is a key strategy in sustainable agriculture72. Keystone taxa, due to their disproportionately large influence on microbial community structure and ecosystem function, represent promising targets for such manipulation37,73,74. In the rhizosphere, how specific microbes become keystone taxa and maintain cooperative relationships with other members to facilitate the nutrient mineralization remains unclear. Therefore, we simulated plant-mediated modulation of keystone taxa via simplified synthetic root exudates to investigate keystone formation mechanisms and reveal how they drive the function of PMBs.

In our study, we observed that the simplified synthetic root exudate MLG triggered a shift in keystone taxa in the rhizosphere, particularly exerting a stable regulatory effect on the keystone taxon D. indicus D99. Transcriptomic analysis indicated that this enrichment was linked to the upregulation of C metabolism and fatty acid biosynthesis pathways. Furthermore, metabolites derived from D. indicus were preferentially utilized by two PMBs with minimal resource competition, which established an efficient feeding network that enhanced rhizosphere ALP activity in the SynCom experiment (Fig. 9).

Conceptual Model Diagram.

The simplified synthetic root exudate mixture MLG, containing myristic acid, luteolin, and glucose, precisely increased the relative abundance of the keystone taxon Domibacillus indicus D99. The increase in D. indicus D99 by MLG was primarily achieved by promoting intracellular C metabolism and fatty acid synthesis. As the relative abundance of Domibacillus indicus D99 rose, it could secrete a substantial amount of phosphatase and generate many metabolites. Other phosphate-mineralizing bacteria, such as Bacillus sp. C67 and Domibacillus sp. C94, benefited stably from D99 due to lower overlap of consumption and a greater degree of resource partitioning. Consequently, the microbial community exhibited higher phosphatase activity, which mineralized soil organic phosphorus (P) and enhanced plant P uptake.

Root exudates are not merely nutrient sources, but ecological drivers that shape microbial community assembly through resource selection and niche differentiation. Root exudates drive rhizosphere microbial assembly and function in different ways. Low molecular weight organic substances (LMWOS) in exudates fuel microbial growth46–48, and signaling molecules (e.g., flavonoids, aromatic acids) control specific bacterial proliferation25,49,50. Numerous studies have explored using these components individually to modulate soil bacterial community56,75–77. In this study, we designed simplified synthetic root exudates to mimic two functions: providing C sources and signaling compounds to create ecological differentiation among microbial taxa. We included fatty acids as a slow-utilization C source and supplemented sugars as a labile C source to rapidly stimulate soil bacterial activity51–53. Notably, the addition of labile sugars was essential, as treatments lacking them (M and ML) failed to alter bacterial community structure or enhance phosphorus-mineralizing functions. In contrast, supplementation with labile sugars (MLG and MLGF) triggered significant shifts in bacterial community structure (e.g., enriching Firmicutes) and function, leading to markedly improved plant P uptake and alkaline phosphatase activity (Fig. 2). This aligns with the natural predominance of sugars like glucose (accounting for 40–50%) in root exudates78–80 and underscores that labile C sources are pivotal initiators for microbial restructuring. It is worth noting that sugars alone may not be sufficient to induce significant changes in bacterial community structure55,81,82, highlighting the importance of a more complex exudate composition. Our findings reveal the interactive effects of sugars and fatty acids in shaping the rhizosphere bacterial community and its functional output.

Natural plant root exudates are highly complex, comprising hundreds to thousands of primary and secondary metabolites (e.g., organic acids, amino acids, and phenolics), each with distinct functional roles25,49,50. While our simplified MLG mixture successfully deconvoluted specific cooperative mechanisms, it inevitably introduces an ecological abstraction. This approach may overlook the intricate inhibitory and competitive dynamics that occur when a broader array of C sources is available to a diverse microbial pool. Furthermore, the metabolic preferences of soil microbes for sugars and organic acids also considerably influence overall microbial activities and soil nutrient mineralization82,83. Therefore, bridging the gap between this mechanistic model and actual agricultural applicability will require future studies to evaluate these keystone-driven networks under more ecologically realistic conditions, utilizing genuine root exudates from multiple plant species or highly complex synthetic mixtures.

Following the observation of these community shifts, we focused on the key nodes driving these changes. In microbial co-occurrence networks, simulated synthetic root exudate treatments induced significant shifts in keystone taxa (Fig. 3). Among these synthetic root exudate treatments, MLG exerted the most significant effects on rhizobacterial community structure and ALP activity (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Fig. 5), so we focused on the network and keystone taxa changes induced by this treatment. We observed that the role of ASV_22207 (genus Domibacillus) and ASV_24087 (genus Microbacterium) shifted from a non-keystone role in the Ctrl to a keystone role under MLG treatment (Fig. 3f). Such shifts in keystone taxa are common in microbial communities, and significant role transitions of keystone taxa occur across different environmental conditions. For instance, Sulfurimonas and Thioalkalispira are neutral non-keystone taxa in situ, but they become keystone taxa under nitrate and benzo [a] pyrene-amended conditions84. Although keystone taxa are defined by their high connectivity and disproportionate influence on community structure rather than abundance11, their abundance dynamics can be functionally significant. The community importance of keystone taxa can be reflected by both presence-to-trait relationships and abundance-to-trait relationships9. The abundance of ASV_22207 and ASV_24087 as new keystone taxa increased significantly in MLG treatment and showed significant correlation with soil ALP activity (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Fig. 5). This finding supports that changes in keystone taxon abundance make important and positive contributions to the functional driving of microbial communities.85

Having identified the keystone taxa through bioinformatic networks, it was imperative to isolate the corresponding strain to experimentally validate its function or ecological role. We isolated rhizosphere bacteria and obtained 34 ASVs, including one keystone taxon D. indicus D99 matching ASV_22207 (Fig. 4; Supplementary Fig. 6). The other keystone taxa, such as ASV 24087, may not be successfully recovered via these standard isolation procedures. Subsequent in vitro and pot experiments demonstrated that D. indicus D99 itself possessed only a limited capacity for organic P mineralization and did not significantly promote plant growth (Fig. 4). This indicates that its ecological importance was not due to strong functional capacity, but rather due to its influence on microbial community structure and interactions. This observation prompted us to investigate which ecological or metabolic traits allowed this bacterium to become a keystone taxon.

Field microcosm incubation experiments demonstrated that the addition of D. indicus D99 exerted a significant impact on bacterial community structure and significantly enhanced bacterial community network complexity (Fig. 4c–f). Similarly, Paenibacillus amylolyticus as the keystone taxon, exhibits weak root colonization ability but significantly influences the spatial organization and functions of biofilms formed by other root-associated bacterial species16. Thus, the keystone taxa like D. indicus D99 may exert their primary influence not through direct functional superiority, but by establishing mutualistic interactions with other strains to generate emergent properties and potential functions of the community.

In natural systems, root exudates exist in a concentration decay continuum. Their influence is strongest at the root surface, often confined to a narrow zone of 1-3 mm, and diminishes sharply as they diffuse into the bulk soil86. The diffusion fluxes and consumption rates of different exudate components vary significantly, resulting in heterogeneous spatial ranges of microbial activity87,88. For example, in our MLG mixture, glucose is highly polar and rapidly mineralized, whereas myristic acid and luteolin are nonpolar with much lower mobility in soil. Because our microcosm experiment applied these synthetic exudates uniformly, it may overlook how the keystone status of D. indicus D99 is maintained as these compounds dilute or degrade over time and distance. Consequently, its effective ‘zone’ of promoting indirect phosphorus mineralization may be constrained by the rapid consumption of glucose or the low mobility of myristic acid. Future research employing techniques such as 13C-isotope imaging could spatially and temporally track how bacteria regulated by these exudates dynamically maintain their keystone roles and promote community-level phosphorus mineralization in a realistic soil continuum.

The significant increase in the relative abundance of D. indicus D99 appeared to be driven by the synergistic effect of multiple components in the synthetic root exudate. Given that soil bacterial growth is often C limited, we initially hypothesized that fatty acids in the synthetic root exudate might serve as C source but with slower turnover rates51,89,90. However, growth assays with isolated bacterial strains revealed that only D. indicus D99 exhibited enhanced growth in the MLG medium, despite being unable to utilize the provided fatty acids directly as a sole C source (Fig.5a; Supplementary Fig. 14). There are three ways fatty acids can be utilized by microbes91: (1) the resynthesis pathway, where molecules are completely degraded to acetyl-CoA units and then new fatty acids are rebuilt from the C2 units, which was not supported by our results for D. indicus D99; (2) partial stepwise degradation of C2 units, allowing part of the molecule to be modified and incorporated into new fatty acids; (3) untransformed utilization, such as the direct assimilation of palmitate and glycerol dialkyl glycerol tetraethers92. We therefore propose that D. indicus D99 may employ the latter two fatty acid assimilation pathways, saving energy by bypassing de novo fatty acid synthesis. Bacteria can develop phenotypic resistance to FASII inhibitors when exogenous fatty acids are available to circumvent FASII-mediated inhibition68,69.

We observed that myristic acid attenuated the growth inhibition of D. indicus D99 by the FASII-targeted inhibitors MAC1377268 and triclosan69–71. Concurrently, transcriptomic data revealed a significant upregulation of fatty acid biosynthesis genes in D. indicus D99 under MLG treatment, while the expression of β-oxidation genes remained unchanged (Fig. 5b–e). Furthermore, several key genes such as Aas (in Gram-negative bacteria) and FakA/B (in Gram-positive bacteria) are involved in this process93. And we found genes in the FakA family in the D. indicus D99 genome, which play an important role in exogenous fatty acid activation. It is important to note that D. indicus is a relatively recently characterized species94, and our understanding of its functions remains limited despite whole-genome sequencing of D. indicus D99. Therefore, to conclusively verify the proposed fatty acid assimilation pathways and quantify the energy savings, additional approaches such as position-specific isotope labeling and gene knockout are required to provide more direct evidence.

These results suggest that the enrichment of D. indicus D99 was driven by a unique metabolic advantage under the synthetic exudate conditions. This implies that keystone taxa may emerge not necessarily because they possess the strongest enzymatic functions, but because they occupy unique ecological niches that position them as central coordinators within microbial networks. However, because our experiment verified the response of D. indicus D99 in a simplified in vitro environment, it cannot definitively confirm whether D. indicus D99 forms a physically distinct spatial niche on the root surface. In natural systems, specific sites of root exudation create highly localized micro-niches. For instance, the localized leakage of amino acids frequently occurs at sites prior to Casparian strip formation, lateral root emergence zones, or regions with endodermal damage. These localized exudation sites generate strong spatial chemotactic signals that drive niche partitioning and bacterial aggregation95. Therefore, verifying whether the MLG mixture facilitates true spatial niche partitioning for D. indicus D99 in the rhizosphere will require future investigations employing techniques such as spatial metatranscriptomics.

Co-occurrence network analysis and in vitro experiments revealed that D. indicus D99 formed cooperative relationships with multiple PMB partners, rather than functioning as a strong mineralizer itself. Therefore, we sought clues from co-occurrence network analysis and used isolated bacterial strains to further verify which PMB partners interact synergistically with D. indicus D99. We demonstrated that D. indicus D99 exerted a strong promotional effect on Bacillus sp. C67 and Domibacillus sp. C94, which was coupled with a significant increase in their ALP activity (Fig. 6). Conversely, we also observed inhibitory effects of Bacillus sp. A113 and Streptomyces sp. C92 on the growth of D. indicus D99 (Fig. 6d). This was not surprising as high connectivity is a core characteristic of keystone taxa, which includes some negative correlations16,36. While this study focused on the promotional effects exerted unidirectionally by the keystone taxon on its partners, the influence exerted in the opposite direction—from the partners to the keystone taxon—is equally noteworthy96.

D. indicus D99 maintains synergistic relationships with both Bacillus sp. C67 and Domibacillus sp. C94 through niche partitioning. Previous studies on interactions between keystone taxa and partners have primarily focused on one-to-one relationships97. For instance, Sulfurovum significantly increases the biomass of the benzo[a] pyrene-degrading bacterium US6-1 and ultimately enhances benzo[a] pyrene degradation efficiency84. However, multiple partners can form cooperative relationships with a single keystone taxon simultaneously37, as this aligns with the definition of keystone taxa. We hypothesized that keystone-associated partners engage in metabolic cross-feeding with keystone taxa1. Moreover, certain microbes that achieve abundance dominance create niche opportunities for other microbes via metabolites, thereby driving community assembly41. Compared with low-cooperativity ecologically neutral bacteria (Fictibacillus sp. A103 and Bacillus sp. D107), keystone-associated partners not only have lower overlap in types of the metabolites consumed from D. indicus D99, but also utilize more metabolites with higher relative abundance, such as bergapten and lactate (Fig. 7). Low overlap and high centrality mean these bacteria utilize resources from D. indicus D99 more efficiently and possess stronger niche competitiveness40,98. However, this test only compared two neutral bacteria, and the metabolic resource characteristics of more neutral bacteria need to be compared with those of keystone-associated taxa. Beyond the perspective of nutritional niches, we need to integrate complementary approaches such as metabolic interaction models40, transcriptomics, and spatial metatranscriptomics99 to further decipher the maintenance mechanism of one-to-many relationships between keystone taxa and partners in rhizosphere microbial ecology. SynCom experiments were used to verify the relationships between D. indicus D99, Bacillus sp. C67, and Domibacillus sp. C94.

While our SynCom experiment confirmed that MLG application enhanced plant growth and ALP activity and stabilized the synergistic relationship between D. indicus D99 and its PMB partners in the rhizosphere (Fig. 8), the agronomic translatability of this synthetic exudate-mediated interaction is highly context-dependent. In high-fertility soils (e.g., mollisols) with elevated background levels of P and organic matter, the metabolic advantage conferred by the MLG mixture appears to be overridden, as the resident microbial communities are less reliant on cooperative nutrient-acquisition strategies. This nutrient-dependent efficacy is a well-documented ecological phenomenon; for example, arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi provide critical benefits in nutrient-poor soils but exhibit severely limited colonization and functional relevance in heavily fertilized, nutrient-rich systems100,101. Consistent with this, the stimulatory effect of our MLG mixture was lost in the nutrient-rich mollisol (Fig. 1). Therefore, translating this reductionist mechanistic model to real-world agricultural contexts requires careful consideration of nutrient inputs. Microbiome engineering via synthetic exudates is likely most feasible and effective in low-input or nutrient-limited agroecosystems. Future research should evaluate the stability of these keystone-mediated dynamics across a gradient of fertilization regimes.

Overall, we demonstrated that certain microbial members in the rhizosphere shifted to keystone taxa through their responsive advantage to simplified synthetic root exudates. These keystone taxa maintain cooperative relationships with two PMB partners via metabolite-mediated niche partitioning, thereby enhancing the P mineralization potential of the rhizosphere microbiome. This finding has significant implications for microbiome engineering: by identifying keystone taxa linked to specific functions, simplified synthetic root exudates can be used to modulate the abundance of these keystone taxa and promote rhizosphere microbiome functionality. This provides a nature-based solution for functional microbiome engineering in sustainable agriculture. Future studies should investigate the formation mechanisms and inherent characteristics of rhizosphere keystone taxa, and attempt to evaluate the impacts of keystone taxa manipulation on soil fertility and crop productivity across diverse plant species and soil types.
