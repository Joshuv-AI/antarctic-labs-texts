Analysis of factors associated with severity categories of fall-related injuries among inpatients at a tertiary grade A hospital in Zhejiang province from 2015 to 2025: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Xinyun Wang, Lili Sun, Lijuan Quan, Minglu Fan, Zhaodi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesUnderstanding the factors associated with the severity categories of fall-related injuries and intervening on modifiable determinants can mitigate the risk of serious fall injuries, thereby enhancing patient safety, reducing length of hospital stay and alleviating the economic burden imposed by these injuries. This study aims to identify factors associated with the severity categories of fall-related injuries and to explore differences in the clinical characteristics of patients who sustain such injuries.DesignA retrospective observational study.SettingA tertiary care hospital in Zhejiang Province, China.ParticipantsThis study included 1030 inpatient fall incidents that occurred between January 2015 and March 2025, drawn from the nursing adverse event reporting system.Outcome measuresBased on the fall-related injury outcomes recorded in the nursing adverse event reporting system. Descriptive statistics were employed to analyse the current status of fall-related injuries. Univariable and multivariable analyses were conducted to explore factors associated with the severity categories of fall-related injuries.ResultsThe incidence of fall-related injuries in this study was 60.6% and the incidence of serious fall injuries was 12.1%. Logistic regression analysis identified factors associated with the severity categories of fall-related injuries. The analysis revealed that patients aged ≥65 years (minor injuries OR=1.82; serious injuries OR=1.76), women (OR=1.59; 1.90), tripping falls (OR=3.94; 4.48), impact to the knee (OR=4.00) or trunk (OR=4.28; 5.34) and ambulatory status prior to the fall (OR=1.71; 1.97) were all associated with more severe fall injuries.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the need to strengthen fall and fall-related injury prevention efforts among inpatients, particularly among patients aged ≥65 years and women. Clinical professionals should prioritise protecting high-risk populations through fall prevention and control to reduce the risk of fall-related injuries.Keywords: GERIATRIC MEDICINE, Health & safety, Hospitalization, Nursing Care

Introduction

Falls represent one of the most common hospital-acquired adverse events, and the injuries they cause remain a persistent global threat to patient safety.1 The annual incidence of falls in hospitals ranges from 3 to 14 per 1000 patient days,2 with injury rates varying between 30% and 73.68%, and 2% to 44% sustain severe harm such as fractures or even death.3 4 Inpatients experience falls 36.3% more often than their community-dwelling counterparts—a disparity driven by acute illness, clinical complexity, polypharmacy and diminished functional reserve.5 6 These incidents frequently lead to serious consequences, including fractures, lacerations and haemorrhagic complications.7 Beyond compromising clinical outcomes, fall-related injuries also erode public trust in healthcare systems8 and trigger lasting psychological sequelae among patients (such as fear, anxiety and depression).9 More critically, survey data indicate that a single inpatient fall injury adds approximately US$14 000–US$32 000 in direct medical costs; when extended lengths of stay (typically 6–12 additional days) and regulatory penalties are factored in, the total economic burden escalates markedly.3 10

Fall injury is a critical determinant of long-term prognosis and future quality of life among inpatients.11 12 A substantial body of evidence demonstrates that interventions targeting known risk factors can effectively reduce the incidence of falls.13 Given that inpatients may face considerable burdens from serious fall injuries, including suffering, fear, functional decline and high medical costs,14 exploring contributing factors and developing targeted interventions to mitigate the occurrence of such injuries has become a priority in hospital prevention efforts. Within this context, stratified, individualised and multifactorial interventions represent the current best practice for preventing fall-related injuries in hospitals.2 Since the severity categories of fall-related injuries differ markedly in terms of incidence, clinical manifestations, treatment modalities, prognosis and intervention strategies, stratification by severity level is highly significant.15 16 The introduction of such stratification broadens the evaluative dimensions of patient fall outcomes and provides a valuable reference for risk prediction, tiered intervention and the allocation of nursing resources.

However, most studies have treated fall injury as a binary outcome, examining risk factors for whether it occurs. Although a limited number of studies have taken fall injury severity into account, most have focused on identifying risk factors for serious injuries while paying insufficient attention to outcomes with no injury or minor injuries.3 17 In reality, minor injuries can also threaten patients’ physical and mental well-being, elevate the risk of recurrent falls and occur far more frequently than serious fall injuries.18

Therefore, this retrospective study will systematically analyse inpatient fall incidents recorded over 10 years at a single hospital and categorise the severity of fall-related injuries into three levels: no injury, minor injuries and serious injuries. The primary objectives are to identify factors associated with the severity categories of fall-related injuries, to examine differences in the clinical characteristics of patients who sustain fall-related injuries, and to provide a reference for optimising fall-prevention strategies for inpatients and for further refining their content design.

Methods

Study design and setting

A retrospective observational study was designed to collect fall incidents reported to the nursing adverse event reporting system (AERS) of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (a comprehensive teaching hospital with 5000 beds) and its affiliated hospitals between January 2015 and March 2025.

Study participants

The study population comprised inpatients who experienced an accidental fall at the selected hospitals between January 2015 and March 2025, defined as per the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision codes for falls (W00–W19), and aged 18 years or older. Furthermore, we excluded the following patients: (1) those with incomplete or unavailable information and (2) non-inpatients (eg, outpatient or emergency department cases). Data collection

Data in the fall incident reporting system were verified and assessed by the nurses immediately after each fall event, based on interviews with patients, family members or caregivers, who served as the first eyewitnesses. Then they entered it into the electronic AERS in accordance with standardised documentation requirements. Based on relevant literature and clinical records, the following information was extracted from the electronic medical record system:

Patient information: age, sex, education level, past medical history, history of falls, with accompaniment. The history of falls refers to whether the patient has experienced any falls within the 6 months prior to the current fall incident during the current hospital stay. Accompaniment refers to whether a family member or caregiver was present at the time of the fall.

Outcomes of fall

According to the National Database of Nursing Quality Indicators of the American Nurses Association and previous studies, the severity of fall-related injuries was categorised into three levels: no injury, minor injuries and serious injuries.4 19 No injury indicates no signs or symptoms of injury; minor injuries include bruises, abrasions and pain that require basic treatment; and serious injuries manifest as muscle or joint injuries, fractures, nerve or visceral damage, and necessitate medical interventions such as surgery or casting.

Data analysis

IBM SPSS V.29.0 was used for data entry and statistical analysis. Continuous variables are expressed as mean±SD, while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. For missing data, mean imputation was used for the nurse-to-patient ratio at the time of the fall (missing <5%) and mode imputation for the risk assessment score (missing <5%). To describe the basic characteristics and distribution of fall-related injury events, one-way analysis of variance or the χ² test was used to compare factors associated with severity categories of fall injuries, with pairwise comparisons between groups adjusted using the Bonferroni method to control for type I error. A p<0.0167 (0.05/3) was considered statistically significant. Multinomial logistic regression analysis was performed to explore factors associated with severity categories of fall-related injuries. Before conducting the multinomial logistic regression analysis, multicollinearity diagnostics were performed for all independent variables. In all analyses, a two-tailed p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Characteristics of severity categories of fall-related injuries

During the study period, a total of 1058 adverse events involving accidental falls were recorded at the selected hospitals. A total of 1030 cases met the inclusion and exclusion criteria for this study (figure 1), among which 25.8% of patients sustained serious injuries. The mean age of the fallers was 63.3 (16.3) years, and 612 (59.4%) were men. The majority of fall-related injuries occurred in patients with no prior history of falls (90.1%) and without a companion at the time of the event (73.1%). Falls most frequently occurred between 00:00 and 07:59 (46.9%), with the bedside (42.4%) and restroom (27.2%) being the most common locations. Medical wards accounted for the highest proportion of falls (51.7%). All patients were assessed using the Johns Hopkins Fall Risk Assessment Tool, with 90.0% classified as being at moderate to high fall risk. Regarding event characteristics, collapse was the predominant type of fall (46.2%) and the hip was the most common point of impact (32.0%). Before the fall, the majority of patients were ambulatory (53.0%), and the most frequently reported activity at the time of the fall was toileting (29.6%) (see table 1).

Univariate analysis and intergroup comparison of severity categories of fall-related injuries

The results of this study showed that statistically significant differences were observed among the three severity categories of fall-related injuries in terms of age, sex, impact location, type of fall, prefall status and staff years of service (p<0.05; detailed p values are shown in table 1). Pairwise comparisons using the Bonferroni correction indicated the following statistically significant differences: between the no injury group and the serious injury group regarding sex, the age subgroup of >84 years, being bedridden or ambulatory prior to the fall, and impact on the hip or torso; between the no injury group and the minor injury group regarding impact on the hip, torso or knee; and between the minor injury group and the serious injury group regarding the age subgroup of >84 years, impact on the knee, and the primary nurse’s educational attainment of associate degree or below (all corrected p<0.05). No statistically significant differences were found for the remaining variables in pairwise comparisons (corrected p>0.05), as shown in table 1.

None versus minor injuries

The analysis revealed that, relative to no injury, minor injuries were significantly associated with women (OR=1.59, p=0.007), age 50–64 years (OR=1.82, p=0.017), a primary nurse with more than 9 years of service (OR=0.40, p=0.011), tripping as the type of fall (OR=3.94, p=0.006), impact on the torso (OR=4.28, p<0.001) or knee (OR=4.00, p<0.001), and being ambulatory before the fall (OR=1.71, p=0.004). Notably, having a more experienced primary nurse was associated with approximately a 60% reduction in the likelihood of minor injuries, whereas tripping and impacting the torso or knee increased this risk by approximately fourfold.

None versus serious injuries

Using no injury as the reference category, serious injuries were significantly associated with women (OR=1.90, p<0.001), age over 65 years (65–74 years: OR=1.76, p=0.047; 75–84 years: OR=2.26, p=0.011; >84 years: OR=4.34, p<0.001), absence of a companion at the time of the fall (OR=1.77, p=0.027), tripping (OR=4.48, p=0.004) and falling from bed (OR=1.64, p=0.023) as the types of fall, impact on the torso (OR=5.34, p<0.001) and being ambulatory before the fall (OR=1.97, p=0.001). Furthermore, women, patients aged ≥65 years, torso impact and ambulatory status all demonstrated stronger correlations with serious injuries than with minor injuries, indicating that these factors are more likely to elevate the risk from no injury to severe outcomes.

Discussion

In healthcare settings, reducing in-hospital falls and fall injuries is crucial for enhancing patient safety, lowering healthcare costs, and improving overall quality of care. Compared with the study by Burns et al,15 which was based on 85 reports of injurious fall incidents and focused exclusively on serious injuries, the present study draws on 1030 fall events recorded in a Chinese hospital between 2015 and 2025, focusing on three categories of fall-related injuries (no injury, minor injuries and serious injuries), and is one of the few large-sample studies spanning such an extended time period. Furthermore, this study complements and enriches previous research by identifying sex, age, type of fall, impact location and prefall status as factors associated with the severity classification of fall-related injuries. Notably, both studies underscore that refined severity classification is critical for fall management and that tiered, individualised interventions should be developed based on the severity categories of fall-related injuries in clinical practice.

Among the 1030 inpatients, 624 (60.6%) sustained some degree of fall-related injuries. This finding aligns with that of Xu et al,4 who reported a similarly high injury rate of 63.3% using a comparable definition of fall-related injury. Such consistency underscores that fall injuries remain highly prevalent among inpatients. While Chen et al20 and Mion et al21 documented lower injury rates (10.1% and 29%, respectively), this discrepancy may be attributable to the fact that the present study sample was drawn from a large general hospital, where patients tend to present with more severe and complex conditions compared with those in community or primary care settings, thereby rendering them more susceptible to injury following a fall. Furthermore, a meta-analysis by Cameron et al revealed that risk reporting of fall-related adverse events in hospitals is generally characterised by inadequate documentation and low quality.22 The data in this study were collected via a voluntary reporting system, and, given its 10-year retrospective design, a portion of the data still relied on paper records and manual transcription, which may have introduced similar limitations. Notably, the rate of serious fall injuries in this study was 25.8%, considerably higher than the 18.3% observed by Lyu et al.23 However, consistent with the findings reported by Xu et al,4 a plausible explanation lies in the relatively large sample sizes employed in both the present study and Xu et al.'s study, which help mitigate random error. In contrast, the serious injuries rate in the smaller sample study by by Lyu et al. may be more susceptible to random variation.24 Collectively, these findings underscore the critical need for targeted interventions to improve inpatient fall outcomes.

The findings of this study indicate that age over 65 years and being a woman among inpatients are significantly associated with a higher likelihood of sustaining more severe fall injuries. A substantial body of research has established age as a critical risk factor for fall-related injuries.25 26 The elevated risk of falls and fractures among older patients aged over 65 years remains a challenging global health issue. As China’s population ages rapidly and life expectancy continues to rise, age-related physiological changes persistently compound the risk of falls among older adults.27 These changes encompass diminished central processing capacity, leading to prolonged reaction times; reduced physical activity owing to chronic diseases; declining muscle function; and increased medication use.28 Compared with men, women are more likely to experience severe fall injuries (OR=1.90). This observation aligns with the findings reported by Peng et al29 and Ghosh et al.30 Moreover, it may be attributable to intrinsic risk factors specific to women, including declining oestrogen levels after menopause, bone deterioration, reduced bone mass and greater engagement in household chores.31 32 Nevertheless, evidence also exists indicating that men sustain more severe injuries and higher mortality from falls.33 This pattern may be explained by greater male pride, a higher propensity for risk-taking, a tendency to overestimate their physical capabilities and a lower likelihood of seeking assistance.34 Consequently, healthcare providers should incorporate age- and sex-heterogeneity into the framework for fall prevention and management. This entails not only strengthening bone mineral density screening, anti-osteoporosis pharmacotherapy and exercise/nutritional interventions for postmenopausal women and the oldest old but also prioritising cognitive function and health literacy as key targets of intervention for men.

Consistent with the findings of Lu et al,35 this study found that ambulatory patients were more likely to sustain severe fall injuries. It must be acknowledged that current evidence regarding the relationship between activity level and fall risk remains inconsistent. Some studies indicate that higher activity levels may mitigate fall-related injuries.36 In contrast, others have found no significant association22 or even suggest that increased activity may exacerbate the risk of fall-related injuries.37 Among inpatients, this relationship becomes further complicated by the presence of additional risk factors such as surgery and medication use. Nevertheless, research by Del Din et al38 demonstrated that maintaining ambulatory activity levels may reduce the risk of fall-related injuries without compromising mobility. Although promoting patient mobility may paradoxically increase fall rates, leading to injury and diminished quality of life, ambulation remains an indispensable component of nursing care due to its critical role in functional recovery and rehabilitation.

Notably, only 15.7% of patients in this study were accompanied at the time of the fall. Zhou et al39 suggested that unaccompanied patients had a 4.86-fold higher odds of falling compared with those under continuous supervision. Ambulatory inpatients tend to overestimate their own capabilities while underestimating fall risk, often engaging in unassisted activities or toileting without supervision, thereby precipitating falls. This suggests that the risk of fall-related injuries is influenced not only by physiological function but also by cognitive domains such as attentional allocation, executive function and judgement.40 Age-related cognitive decline may further exacerbate this vulnerability.41 Therefore, healthcare professionals should not only encourage physical training but also reinforce cognitive education regarding fall prevention. Patients should be advised to avoid unassisted ambulation and to adhere to supervised, progressive exercise regimens to mitigate both the incidence of falls and the severity of potential injuries.

Furthermore, our results indicate that the point of impact at the time of a fall is significantly associated with the severity of injuries sustained. Specifically, knee impacts tend to result in minor injuries, whereas torso impacts are more likely to lead to severe injuries. This aligns with previous research demonstrating that severe injuries, such as fractures, are closely associated with both the impact force and the body’s landing position during a fall.42 Kleiven and Sahandifar found that when a patient’s torso is near an upright position, the force exerted on the proximal femoral head reaches its peak. Under this condition, the risk of femoral injury from a lateral fall is highest, making the patient most likely to fall and sustain severe injuries.43 Notably, the position of the knees during a fall determines the impact force at the hip. Evidence suggests that knee contact can partially absorb and dissipate impact energy, thereby lowering the risk of hip injury; conversely, if the knees do not make contact with the ground, the hips are subjected to greater impact forces, increasing the likelihood of severe injuries.44 This appears to contradict the conclusions of the present study. This discrepancy may stem from the fact that only the patient’s final posture was recorded, without detailed information about the entire fall sequence. Therefore, it is recommended that clinicians better understand the complete process of a fall. Patients should be appropriately educated on how to respond to sudden falls to minimise injury—for instance, by using their arms to absorb impact or by relaxing their bodies to achieve a slower, more controlled landing, thereby reducing the impact force.45

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to categorise fall-related injuries into three distinct severity levels, namely no injury, minor injuries and serious injuries, and to explore their associated factors. Second, our findings further underscore the need for healthcare professionals to place greater emphasis on screening patients at high risk of falls and to devote adequate attention and intervention to those who remain persistently at elevated fall risk and are more susceptible to sustaining fall-related injuries. Nevertheless, this study is subject to several limitations. First, our investigation was confined to a single-centre sample from China, which may affect the robustness and generalisability of the results. Second, this study is subject to the inherent limitations of retrospective designs, including potential selection bias across groups and the inability to establish causality from observed associations. Residual confounding from unmeasured variables may also exist46; therefore, future research employing a multicentre, prospective cohort design is recommended. Third, due to incomplete electronic medical records and patient privacy constraints, this study did not incorporate important variables such as biochemical markers,33 psychological conditions47 and polypharmacy,48 nor did it include data on fall-related costs. Additionally, as noted by Fischer et al49 and Krauss et al50 in their large-scale hospital fall studies, the data for this investigation were derived from the nursing AERS. Given that information was entered post-fall and lacked follow-up, issues such as underreporting of events, misclassification of fall-related injuries and reporting bias were unavoidable.21

Conclusions

This retrospective study analysed the severity categories of fall-related injuries and associated factors among 1030 in-hospital fall incidents. The findings indicate that fall-related injuries among inpatients remain a persistent challenge for hospitals in China. The present study identified age, sex, ambulatory status and impact location during the fall as factors associated with severity categories of fall-related injuries. These findings hold considerable significance for identifying inpatients at heightened risk of sustaining fall-related injuries and provide an evidence-based foundation for developing targeted interventions. It is recommended that fall prevention and control efforts serve as a cornerstone, with particular emphasis on preventing serious fall injuries. Evidence-based fall prevention strategies should be implemented, including reducing or eliminating environmental risk factors that may lead to severe injuries, installing protective guardrails, conducting regular safety education sessions, encouraging the use of protective equipment and enhancing guidance on safe mobility. Furthermore, prospective, multicentre, large-sample cohort studies that incorporate more comprehensive environmental and behavioural factors are warranted to refine the in-hospital fall early warning system further.
