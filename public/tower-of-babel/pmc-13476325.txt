Biotransformation-guided production of quinalizarin as an antibacterial agent against Acinetobacter baumannii
Authors: Marwa E. Hasan, Ghada Attia, Engy Elekhnawy, Moataz A. Shaldam, Abdel-Rahim S. Ibrahim, Kamilia A. Abo El-Seoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe search for antibacterial agents remains a global priority, particularly against multidrug-resistant pathogens. In this study, microbial transformation of 1,8-dihydroxyanthraquinone (chrysazin) was performed to obtain derivatives with enhanced antibacterial activity. Biotransformation using Absidia corymbifera AUMC 7104 and Beauveria bassiana AUMC 5133 yielded two metabolites: 1,2,5,8-tetrahydroxyanthraquinone (1, quinalizarin) and 1,8-dihydroxy-3-methyl-anthraquinone (2, chrysophanol), respectively. Their structures were elucidated by HRESIMS, 1D/2D NMR analyses. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of quinalizarin production through microbial biotransformation. The antibacterial activities of both derivatives (1 and 2), along with their parent compound, were evaluated against multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii through in vitro assays. Compound 1 exhibited the least MIC values of 512–1024 µg/mL against A. baumannii isolates and was selected for further in vivo and in silico studies. In the murine infection model, the compound 1-treated group (group IV) exhibited a significant reduction (p < 0.05) in bacterial burden compared to the positive control group (group II). Immunohistochemical analysis of kidney, liver, and spleen tissues revealed modulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, indicating anti-inflammatory properties alongside antibacterial activity. Molecular docking suggests a potential multi-target mechanism for compound 1, with favorable interactions with five critical bacterial proteins: MetRS, MurB, LptG/F, DXR, and MacB efflux transporter. These findings highlight quinalizarin as a potential lead compound for further antibacterial development.Key points• Fungal biotransformation of chrysazin yielded the antibacterial agent quinalizarin.• Quinalizarin revealed in vitro and in vivo actions against Acinetobacter baumannii.• Docking studies suggested favorable interactions with multiple bacterial targets.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13960-5.

Introduction

Plants produce an immense variety of structurally diverse natural compounds, many of which serve as potential leads for therapeutic development (Rami et al. 2021; Nasim et al. 2022). Among plant secondary metabolites, anthraquinones (AQs) constitute a substantial class of natural compounds found in bacteria, fungi, and higher plants (Dulo et al. 2021). AQs were reported to have diverse pharmacological activities, including purgative (Wang et al. 2021), anti-inflammatory (Hu et al. 2021), antiviral (Joshi et al. 2020), antibacterial, antifungal (Raghuveer et al. 2023), antiparasitic (Friedman et al. 2020), antitumor (Woradulayapinij et al. 2022), antioxidant (Dawood et al. 2021), and immunomodulatory activities (Gadge 2021). Consequently, they attracted considerable interest (Shakour and Farag 2022); however, their pharmaceutical applications were hindered because of their insolubility (Qun et al. 2023).

Chrysazin (1,8-dihydroxyanthraquinone) is an example of polyketide-derived dihydroxyanthraquinone (Kwan and Schulz 2011). It is an anthraquinone derivative that was extracted from the roots of Rheum palmatum L. (often known as Chinese rhubarb), belonging to the family Polygonaceae (Gattuso and Kamm 1994) as well as some marine fungus (Greco et al. 2021). It has long been used clinically as a stimulant laxative in palliative care settings, although its clinical use has declined due to safety concerns, as it possesses documented carcinogenic properties (Gattuso and Kamm 1994).

Chemical synthesis of regio-defined, polyhydroxylated AQs is often challenging, requiring harsh conditions, multistep protection–deprotection sequences, and generating environmentally problematic waste, motivating the search for cleaner and more selective routes (Li et al. 2026).

Biotransformations are enzymatic reactions catalyzed by microorganisms or plant enzymes, providing a mild, selective, and environmentally friendly strategy for structural diversification of AQs (Yousuf et al. 2019b, 2019a; Ahmed and Binti Amran 2022; Lax 2023; Qin and Dong 2023). Microbial systems perform regio- and chemoselective modifications, such as hydroxylation, glycosylation, and methylation, which are difficult to achieve chemically (Pereira dos Santos et al. 2020; Poudel et al. 2022).

Hydroxylation is a crucial mechanism for making hydrophobic substances more soluble. The number and location of hydroxyl groups strongly influence color, redox properties, and biological activity, making anthraquinone derivatives a prime focus of drug development research (Poudel et al. 2022).

Regarding its antimicrobial potential, chrysazin and its biosynthesized glycoside derivatives have been evaluated against various human pathogenic bacteria (Poudel et al. 2022). Parent chrysazin showed only weak direct antibacterial effects. However, rhamnosylated chrysazin derivatives, generated in recombinant Escherichia coli, showed a markedly enhanced antimicrobial potency against methicillin-resistant and methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus, while the parent compound remains inactive at comparable concentrations (Poudel et al. 2022). In comparison to chrysazin alone, these rhamnosides also exhibited an enhanced antiproliferative activity against several human cancer cell lines, suggesting that appropriate glycosylation can intensify its cytotoxic and potential anticancer properties (Ma et al. 2014).

Acinetobacter baumannii is a non-motile, Gram-negative bacterium commonly found in hospital settings, where it causes serious infections primarily affecting patients who are critically ill or have weakened immune systems (Dubey et al. 2025). This pathogen produces several virulence factors that contribute to its ability to cause disease and enable it to survive in hostile environments, avoid immune detection, and invade host tissues. Additionally, A. baumannii possesses diverse strategies for resisting antibiotics, including breaking down antimicrobial drugs through enzymes, limiting drug entry into the cell, active efflux of antimicrobial drugs, and altering the targets that antibiotics normally attack (Qasemi et al. 2022). These resistance capabilities have led to widespread problems with multidrug-resistant isolates globally, highlighting the crucial necessity for alternate antibacterials. The evaluation of fungal biotransformation products of chrysazin against A. baumannii offers a promising approach to identify new compounds with enhanced antibacterial potential against such a problematic pathogen.

Several essential bacterial proteins involved in cell wall biosynthesis, protein synthesis, and drug efflux have been identified as promising therapeutic targets. Among these, 1-deoxy-D-xylulose-5-phosphate reductoisomerase (DXR) plays a crucial role in the non-mevalonate pathway of isoprenoid biosynthesis, which is absent in humans, making it an attractive antimicrobial target (Deng et al. 2011). In addition, MacB, a key component of the MacAB-TolC efflux system, contributes significantly to antibiotic resistance by actively exporting drugs outside the bacterial cell (Greene et al. 2018). Also, UDP-N-acetylenolpyruvylglucosamine reductase (MurB) is involved in peptidoglycan biosynthesis, an essential process for maintaining cell wall integrity, while methionyl-tRNA synthetase (MetRS) is critical for protein translation and bacterial growth (Sanapalli et al. 2026). Additionally, the lipopolysaccharide transport (LptF/G) complex is part of the lipopolysaccharide transport system, essential for outer membrane biogenesis and permeability, further contributing to intrinsic resistance (Sperandeo et al. 2017). Targeting these proteins simultaneously may provide a strategic approach to combating A. baumannii infections by disrupting multiple essential pathways and resistance mechanisms.

In this work, we aimed to establish a sustainable biocatalytic route for the biotransformation of chrysazin using Absidia corymbifera AUMC 7104 and Beauveria bassiana AUMC 5133. After 14 days of fermentation, two compounds: 1,2,5,8-tetrahydroxyanthraquinone (1, quinalizarin) and 1,8-dihydroxy-3-methyl-anthraquinone (2, chrysophanol) were obtained (Fig. 1A). To date, quinalizarin has not yet been isolated from microbial biotransformation. Materials and methods

Chemicals

Chrysazin (≥98% purity) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Company, Inc., USA. The purity of substrates was confirmed by TLC, 1H, and 13C NMR. All the reagents and solvents used for separation and purification were of analytical grade. The utilized microorganisms are mentioned in the supplementary file.

Chrysazin biotransformation by A. corymbifera AUMC 7104

Among the 30 isolates evaluated, only Absidia corymbifera AUMC 7104 and Beauveria bassiana AUMC 5133 produced detectable biotransformation products, whereas no observable conversion was detected for the remaining isolates (Table S1). Biotransformation of chrysazin using A. corymbifera (AUMC 7104) produced one metabolite. After dissolving the ethyl acetate residue (260 mg) in 15 mL of methanol, it was adsorbed on 260 mg silica and dried. The adsorbed sample was positioned onto a glass column (15 g, 25 × 1 cm) packed with silica using the wet technique. Then, 100% methylene chloride was used for gradient elution of the column, then ethyl acetate was added in increments of 5% to 100% to progressively increase the polarity by 5% up to 100%. The polarity increased with methanol stepwisely in 2% increment up to 10% methanol. Collected fractions (15 mL each) were analyzed on precoated TLC plates using solvent systems S1 and S2. Similar fractions were collected together and evaporated until they were completely dry. Fractions (21–33) were combined and evaporated to give a red residue (37 mg). Additional purification was accomplished via isocratic elution using 100% methanol HPLC as a mobile phase on Sephadex LH-20 column (20 g, 30 × 2 cm). Twenty-nine subfractions (3 mL each) were inspected on TLC plates. Subfractions (11–18) were pooled together to give pure compound 1 as reddish-brown (dark red) amorphous powder (16 mg, 6.15% yield, Rf 0.17 S1and 0.25 S2) (Figure S1a).

Chrysazin biotransformation by B. bassiana AUMC 5133

Biotransformation of chrysazin using B. bassiana AUMC 5133 afforded one metabolite. Methylene chloride residue (355 mg) was dissolved in 20 mL methylene chloride:methanol mixture (1:1), adsorbed on 355 mg silica, and dried. The adsorbed sample was loaded onto a glass column (31 g, 30 × 1 cm) packed with silica using the wet method. Using the gradient elution method, the polarity was gradually increased from 100% methylene chloride by adding ethyl acetate in 5% increments up to 100% and then methanol in 2% increments up to 15%. Collected fractions (80, 15 mL each) were collected. Similar fractions (45–62) were pooled together and further purified by isocratic elution on Sephadex LH-20 column (20 g, 30 × 2 cm), using 100% methanol HPLC. Subfractions (14–25) were pooled together to give pure compound 2 as a yellow to orange amorphous powder (22 mg, 6.19% yield, Rf 0.4 S1) (Figure S1b).

In vitro antibacterial activity

The antibacterial potential of the chemical compounds was evaluated employing agar well diffusion assay, as formerly explained (Alshawwa et al. 2023). Each plate contained negative (dimethyl sulfoxide) and positive (ciprofloxacin) controls. Then, we have evaluated the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of the tested chemical compounds via broth microdilution in 96-well microtitration plates in accordance with the early documented procedures (Alshawwa et al. 2023).

In vivo antibacterial assay

Forty adult male Swiss albino mice, weighing 22 to 26 g, were obtained from Cairo University’s animal house. The mice were given filtered water with a standard pellet at room temperature. The research ethics committee of the Faculty of Pharmacy, Tanta University, Egypt, authorized the experimental procedures (TP/RE/12/25 p-007). The acute toxicity test is shown in the supplementary file.

In vivo experimental model

The mice were randomly divided into four groups as follows (ten mice each):Group I (normal or negative control): They received saline via intraperitoneal (IP) injections once daily.Group II (A. baumannii or positive control): They were injected with 0.1 mL of A. baumannii suspension (107 CFU/mL) once via intravenous (IV) injection in their tail vein. After 2 weeks, the animals were anaesthetized and euthanized. The bacterial burden was determined in the kidneys, liver, and spleen by counting the number of colony-forming units per gram of tissue (CFU/g).

Histological evaluation and immunohistochemical exploration

Samples of kidneys, liver, and spleen were put in 10% formalin, then, after dehydration, they were placed in paraffin wax, spliced into thin sections, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (Negm et al. 2022; Abdelkader et al. 2022; Saad et al. 2024). Photos were captured for the stained sections. Six-micrometer tissue sections from the kidney, liver, and spleen were subjected to immunohistochemical staining; they were first dewaxed, rehydrated by a diminishing alcohol series, and treated with 10% hydrogen peroxide in methanol for 10 min. Following this, the sections were microwaved for 10 min in 0.01 M sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0), allowed to cool at room temperature, and then repeatedly washed with phosphate buffered saline for 5 min. After washing, antigens were recovered by autoclaving in citrate buffer for 11 min. Next, slices were incubated with primary antibodies for a whole night at 4 ℃. Then, the tissues were treated for 30 min at room temperature with 3, 3diaminobenzidine and polyclonal antibodies of interleukin one beta (IL-1β), interleukin six (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). The tissue sections were mounted for visibility, washed in xylene, and subtly counterstained with hematoxylin. Slides were examined under a light microscope. Using image analysis tools (Image J, 1.46a, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA), morphometric analysis was carried out. The mean area percentage of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α protein expression for each of the experimental groups was evaluated in ten non-overlapping fields within each region. Polyclonal antibodies of the were purchased from Invitrogen and implemented in the immunohistochemical investigation of the tested tissues (Attallah et al. 2022; Alqahtani et al. 2022; Al-kuraishy et al. 2022).

Docking studies

Five bacterial proteins were selected for the docking studies: MetRS, MurB, LptG/F, DXR, and MacB efflux transporter. The crystal structures of DXR (4ZN6), MacB (5UDF), MurB (6CAU), and MetRS (5URB) were obtained from the protein data bank, whereas the structures of LptF/G were Swiss-modeled using PDB code 8FRM (Pahil et al. 2024) as a template. Molecular docking investigations on compound 1 were conducted using AutoDock Vina (Trott and Olson 2010). The conformer with the highest energy preference was exported as a (*.pdb) file after ligand structures were created using Marvin Sketch V22.2. After removing water molecules, hydrogen was added, and gastier charges were applied using AutoDock procedures. Table S2 shows the centers and sizes of the grid boxes that make up the active site for each receptor. BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualize was used to create the 2D schematic presentation and the 3D visualization.

Statistics

GraphPad Prism software version 8 (USA) was utilized. The statistical difference among groups was revealed using ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison post hoc test. The level of significance was established at p < 0.05. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Identification of isolated compounds

Compound 1: Negative HRMS analysis revealed that the molecular formula of 1 was C14H8O6 (Figure S2), with a deprotonated molecular ion peak at m/z 271.02247 [M-H]− (calculated for C14H7O6). This is more than chrysazin by 32 Da, suggesting the introduction of two additional hydroxyl groups. When compared to chrysazin (Al Kazman and Prieto 2021; Poudel et al. 2022), the absence of proton signals at C-2 and C-5 in the 1H-NMR spectrum (Table 1, Figure S3) was noticed, suggesting hydroxyl substitution at these positions. The 1H and 13C NMR spectra of chrysazin are provided in Supplementary Information (Figures S24-S26). Only four protons resonated at δH 7.04, 7.23, 7.39, and 7.60, which were assigned to H-3, H-7, H-6, and H-4, respectively. The 13C-NMR data and DEPTQ 135 NMR spectrum (Table 1, Figure S4, S5) showed 14 distinct carbon signals, in which C-2 and C-5 signals were significantly deshielded by 29.8 ppm and 37.8 ppm, respectively, due to the produced hydroxyl groups at these positions. This assignment was confirmed by full investigation of the HSQC, HMBC, and COSY spectra of 1 (Figures S6-S11). In HMBC spectra, H-4 shows correlations with carbon at δC 153.8 (C-2). Moreover, H-7 displays correlations with carbon at δC 157.0 (C-5), confirming the proposed assignment of the additional hydroxyl groups (Fig. 1B). Hence, compound 1 was identified as 1,2,5,8-tetrahydroxyanthraquinone (quinalizarin). To our knowledge, this is the first report describing its isolation and full characterization using this biotransformation system.Compound 2: HRMS analysis of compound 2 exhibited a deprotonated molecular ion peak [M-H]− at m/z 253.05298 (calculated for C15H9O4), which is more than chrysazin by 14 Da suggesting methylation (Figure S12). When compared to chrysazin (Al Kazman and Prieto 2021; Poudel et al. 2022), the absence of proton signal at C-3 in 1H-NMR spectrum (Table 1, Figure S13, S14), along with the appearance of a sharp singlet signal at δH 2.40, supporting methyl substitution at this position. The 13C-NMR and DEPTQ 135 NMR spectra of 2 (Table 1, Figure S15, S16, S17) showed fifteen distinct carbon signals, which were closely related to those of chrysazin, except for a downfield shift of C-3 by 12.3 ppm and a signal appeared at δC 22.3 ppm, indicative of methyl group introduction at this position.This assignment was confirmed by full investigation of the HSQC, HMBC and COSY spectra of 2 (Figures S18-S22), where the HMBC analysis revealed that the methyl group correlates with C-2 and C-4. Similarly, H-2 and H-4 protons showed correlation to the methyl group, confirming its attachment at C-3 (Fig. 1B). All findings were consistent with previously reported data for chrysophanol (Dong et al. 2016; Mari et al. 2019). To date, this compound has not been reported to be isolated using this microbial biotransformation system.

Moreover, H-7 displays correlations with carbon at δC 157.0 (C-5), confirming the proposed assignment of the additional hydroxyl groups (Fig. 1B). Hence, compound 1 was identified as 1,2,5,8-tetrahydroxyanthraquinone (quinalizarin). To our knowledge, this is the first report describing its isolation and full characterization using this biotransformation system.

The five tested proteins MetRS, MurB, LptG/F, DXR, and MacB efflux transporter represent key components of essential bacterial pathways, including protein biosynthesis, cell wall assembly, isoprenoid metabolism, and multidrug efflux. These systems are widely recognized as promising targets for antimicrobial drug development against multidrug-resistant A. baumannii.

Molecular docking analysis revealed that compound 1 establishes a network of stable interactions within the active sites of several targets, particularly DXR (4ZN6 (Ball et al. 2021), MacB (5UDF (Kaplan et al. 2018), and MetRS (5URB (Tillery et al. 2020) as seen in Figure 7.Fig. 7Molecular docking analysis of compound 1, showing 3D and 2D interaction maps within A DXR (4ZN6), B MacB (5UDF), and C MetRS (5URB) active sites

Within the DXR active site, the compound forms key π–alkyl interactions with residues, such as Ala44 and His45, in addition to conventional hydrogen bonds, contributing to strong binding affinity. In the MacB transporter, the binding profile is dominated by hydrogen bonding interactions with Arg239 and Glu137, residues crucial for substrate recognition and ATP-dependent transport. Similarly, in MetRS, a dense network of π–alkyl and van der Waals interactions involving residues such as Tyr25 and Asn14 stabilizes the ligand–protein complex. These interaction patterns suggest that compound 1 can effectively occupy functional domains in enzymes involved in protein synthesis and drug efflux, thereby potentially disrupting bacterial homeostasis at multiple levels.

The interaction of compound 1 with MurB (PBD ID: 6CAU) (Sonkar et al. 2017) and the modeled LptG/F complex (based on template PDB ID: 8FRM), further highlights its multi-target potential (Fig. 8). In the MurB enzyme, which plays a central role in peptidoglycan biosynthesis, the docking pose is stabilized by a significant π–cation interaction with the catalytic magnesium ion (Mg2⁺, MG502) as well as an anionic interaction with the ANP cofactor. These contacts likely contribute to an inhibitory mechanism by “locking” the enzyme in an inactive conformation. In contrast, binding within the LptG/F transmembrane transporter is characterized by a predominantly hydrophobic environment. The ligand is deeply embedded within a pocket formed by hydrophobic residues such as Leu309, Ile, and Phe310, and is further stabilized by persistent π–alkyl interactions. Overall, the favorable docking scores obtained across all five targets (Table S4), encompassing both membrane transporters and cytosolic metabolic enzymes, indicate that compound 1 exhibits specific binding behavior with the tested targets. By simultaneously targeting diverse and essential biological pathways, including isoprenoid biosynthesis (DXR), protein translation (MetRS), cell wall synthesis (MurB), LPS transport (LptG/F), and drug efflux (MacB), compound 1 may function as a potent multi-target inhibitor. Such polypharmacological activity is particularly advantageous in combating multidrug-resistant A. baumannii, as it reduces the likelihood of resistance development and enhances antibacterial efficacy.

Discussion

The biotransformation of chrysazin by A. corymbifera and B. bassiana likely involves enzymatic systems commonly associated with fungal xenobiotic metabolism and secondary metabolite biosynthesis. The conversion of chrysazin to quinalizarin entails regioselective hydroxylation at C-2 and C-5, a reaction that may be catalyzed by cytochrome P450 monooxygenases or other oxidative enzymes capable of introducing hydroxyl functionalities into aromatic substrates. Fungal cytochrome P450 enzymes are recognized for their broad substrate specificity and their pivotal role in catalyzing oxidative modifications that enhance structural diversity and biological activity. In contrast, the formation of chrysophanol from chrysazin involves the introduction of a methyl substituent, a transformation that may be mediated by S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM)-dependent methyltransferases, which are widely involved in fungal secondary metabolic pathways. Although the enzymes responsible for these transformations were not characterized in the present study, the observed hydroxylation and methylation reactions are consistent with established fungal biocatalytic processes reported for anthraquinone and other aromatic metabolites. Further enzymological and genomic investigations are warranted to identify the specific catalytic systems involved and to elucidate the molecular basis of chrysazin biotransformation.

Owing to the spreading resistance among bacterial isolates, especially A. baumannii, it is necessary to reveal novel replacements for the conventionally employed antibacterials (Abdel Bar et al. 2022). A. baumannii is a pathogenic bacterium that can elicit various drug-resistant infections in humans via different resistance mechanisms, like β-lactamase production, efflux pump overexpression, and outer membrane protein alterations (Ahmed et al. 2025). In this context, fungal biotransformation emerges as a promising strategy for producing structurally modified products with potentiated biological and pharmacological activity (Qun et al. 2023).

Herein, we explored the antibacterial activity of chrysazin and its biotransformation products, compounds 1 and 2, against A. baumannii clinical isolates. Compound 1 possessed the most potent antibacterial action with an MIC range of 512–1024 µg/mL. Although the observed MIC values are relatively higher than those of conventional antibiotics, they remain noteworthy for a naturally derived anthraquinone metabolite obtained through microbial transformation. The results suggest that hydroxylation substantially enhances antibacterial activity relative to the parent compound and may provide a promising scaffold for further structural optimization and development of pharmaceutical formualtions. Hydroxy-anthraquinone derivatives revealed antibacterial and antifungal activities (Daly et al. 2015; Ng et al. 2015; Raghuveer et al. 2023), which supports our findings. The superior antibacterial action of compound 1 relative to the chrysazin parent compound could be attributed not only to the increased hydrogen-bonding capacity conferred by the additional hydroxyl groups but also to alterations in physicochemical properties that influence target recognition and cellular interactions. Hydroxyl substitution can modify electron distribution across the anthraquinone scaffold, potentially affecting redox behavior and interactions with bacterial enzymes. Furthermore, the presence of four hydroxyl groups may facilitate multiple stabilizing interactions within protein active sites, thereby improving binding affinity and inhibitory potential. The docking results support this hypothesis, as compound 1 established multiple hydrogen-bonding interactions with key amino acid residues across several target proteins. Moreover, the hydroxy- anthraquinone derivatives elucidated other pharmacological potentials, such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer capabilities (Uzor et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2016; Xia et al. 2019).

When tested in vivo, compound 1 retained its antimicrobial activity despite the complexity of biological systems. When compared to the positive control group, compound 1-treated mice had a statistically substantial reduction (p < 0.05) in bacterial load, confirming its therapeutic efficacy and indicating sufficient absorption and distribution into tissues. The agreement between the in vitro antibacterial activity, the reduction of bacterial burden in infected mice, and the favorable docking scores suggests that the biological activity of compound 1 is not limited to isolated biochemical interactions but may translate into meaningful therapeutic effects in vivo. This finding is especially noteworthy because numerous compounds that exhibit strong antibacterial properties in laboratory settings fail to achieve competitive effectiveness in living tissues, often due to poor pharmacokinetic properties, breakdown by metabolic processes, or excessive binding to proteins (Hedaya et al. 2017).

Infections caused by bacteria can cause damage and inflammatory responses across various organs, including the kidneys, liver, and spleen. This tissue injury may result from cytokine release triggered by bacterial endotoxins (Cao et al. 2023; Batiha et al. 2022). The histological investigations in this study revealed a renovation of the normal histological structures of the kidney, liver, and spleen in the compound 1-treated group (group IV). The compound 1-treated group (group IV) revealed a decreased positive brown tubular staining in the IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α immunostained kidney, liver, and spleen tissues which confirms its antibacterial and anti-inflammatory actions.

Docking investigations were accomplished by testing the binding affinity of compound 1 to five vital proteins that are necessary for A. baumannii survival. They included MetRS, which is necessary for protein translation, MurB, which is necessary for the synthesis of peptidoglycan cell walls, LptG/F, which is in charge of outer membrane assembly, MacB, which is an efflux transporter, and DXR, a key enzyme responsible for isoprenoid biosynthesis in the MEP pathway (Tillery et al. 2020). The molecular docking research of compound 1 against five important A. baumannii targets showed binding affinities that indicate possible interactions pointing to a potential multi-targeted mode of action. Yet, these results should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, as docking alone cannot establish a definitive multi-target mechanism of action. Furthermore, the use of homology models and large grid boxes introduces additional uncertainty in the predicted binding modes. Future experimental validation will be required to confirm these interactions.

A limitation of the current study is that the specific fungal enzymes responsible for chrysazin transformation were not identified. In addition, the elucidated antibacterial mechanism was based primarily on computational predictions. As this was designed as discovery-stage research, comprehensive microbiological assessments, including MBC determination, time-kill kinetics, serum activity, and synergy testing with standard antibiotics, were not performed. These assessments represent logical next investigative phases required to fully evaluate the therapeutic potential of compound 1. Further studies involving enzyme characterization, target validation, pharmacokinetic profiling, and toxicity assessment are required to fully evaluate the therapeutic potential of compound 1.

Another limitation of the present study is the reliance on comprehensive spectroscopic characterization (1H NMR, 13C NMR, 2D NMR, HRMS) for purity determination without complementary HPLC or GC chromatographic analysis. While the 1H NMR spectrum showed no extraneous signals indicative of significant impurities, spectroscopic methods do not provide quantitative purity values that chromatographic techniques deliver. However, chromatographic purity verification (HPLC/GC) will be incorporated in our future compound development studies to establish quantitative analytical standards.

Although compound 1 showed better antibacterial action than its parent compound and the other biotransformed compound (2), its MIC values (512–1024 µg/mL) remain much higher than the conventional antibiotics. Therefore, compound 1 should be considered a potential lead compound that requires performing synergetic studies to evaluate its effect with conventional antibiotics, such as carbapenems and colistin, against A. baumannii.

Conclusion

This study highlights fungal biotransformation as a sustainable and selective approach for generating structurally diverse anthraquinone derivatives. The ability of microbial systems to perform regioselective modifications underscores their value as versatile biocatalytic tools for natural product diversification and the discovery of biologically relevant molecules. Among the obtained metabolites, quinalizarin (compound 1) emerged as a potential lead candidate, exhibiting dual antibacterial and anti-inflammatory potential against A. baumannii infection. Its capacity to reduce bacterial burden, preserve tissue integrity, and modulate inflammatory responses supports its potential for further pharmaceutical use in synergetic combinations with various antibiotics. Future studies focused on elucidating the underlying enzymatic mechanisms, expanding the scope of microbial transformations, and conducting advanced preclinical investigations may further enhance the utility of this approach and facilitate the development of alternative therapeutic agents.
