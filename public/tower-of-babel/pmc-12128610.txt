A place-based spatial analysis of racial inequities in overdose in St. Louis County Missouri, United States
Authors: Phillip L. Marotta, Benjamin CB Leach, William D. Hutson, Joel M. Caplan, Brenna Lohmann, Charlin Hughes, Devin Banks, Stephen Roll, Yung Chun, Jason Jabbari, Rachel Ancona, Kristen Mueller, Ben Cooper, Theresa Anasti, Nathaniel Dell, Rachel Winograd, Robert Heimer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Objective:The objective of this study was to identify place features associated with increased risk of drug-involved fatalities and generate a composite score measuring risk based on the combined effects of features of the built environment.Methods:We conducted a geospatial analysis of overdose data from 2022 to 2023 provided by the St. Louis County Medical Examiner’s Office to test whether drug-involved deaths were more likely to occur near 54 different place features using Risk Terrain Modeling (RTM). RTM was used to identify features of the built environment that create settings of heightened overdose risk. Risk was estimated using Relative Risk Values (RRVs) and a composite score measuring Relative Risk Scores (RRS) across the county was produced for drugs, opioids, and stimulants, as well as by Black and White decedents.Results:In the model including all drugs, deaths were more likely to occur in close proximity to hotels/motels (RRV=39.65, SE=0.34, t-value=10.81 p<.001), foreclosures (RRV=4.42, SE=0.12, t-value = 12.80, p<.001), police departments (RRV=3.13, SE=0.24, t-score=4.86, p<.001), and restaurants (RRV=2.33, SE=0.12, t-value=7.16, p<.001). For Black decedents, deaths were more likely to occur near foreclosures (RRV=9.01, SE=0.18, t-value =11.92, p<.001), and places of worship (RRV= 2.51, SE=0.18, t-value = 11.92, p<.001). For White decedents, deaths were more likely to occur in close proximity to hotels/motels (RRV=38.97, SE=0.39, t-value=9.30, p<.001) foreclosures (RRV=2.57, SE=0.16, t-value =5.84, p<.001), restaurants (RRV=2.52, SE=0.17, t-value=5.33, p<.001) and, auto painting/repair shops (RRV=0.04, SE=0.18, t-value =3.39, p<.001).Conclusion:These findings suggest that places of worship, the hospitality industry, and housing authorities may be physical features of the environment that reflect social conditions that are conducive to overdose. The scaling up of harm reduction strategies could be enhanced by targeting places where features are co-located.

Introduction

Drug-involved deaths in the United States amounted to a total of 109,691 fatalities between April 2022 and April 2023. (Spencer et al., 2024) Deaths involving opioids and stimulants have risen fastest among Black, Indigenous, and other minority communities. (Choi et al., 2022; Cobert et al., 2020; Friedman & Hansen, 2022; Friedman & Hansen, 2022; Friedman, Beletsky, et al., 2022, Friedman, Montero, et al., 2022; Jones et al., 2020; Kariisa et al., 2022; Larochelle et al., 2021; Li et al., 2019; Liao et al., 2022; Scott et al., 2007; Srinivasan et al., 2024) The aftereffects of the COVID-19 pandemic (Friedman & Hansen, 2022; Friedman, Beletsky, et al., 2022, Friedman, Montero, et al., 2022; Jones et al., 2020; Larochelle et al., 2021; Scott et al., 2007) worsened pre-existing racial inequities in access to naloxone and medications for the treatment of opioid use disorders (MOUD). (Kariisa et al., 2022; Li et al., 2019; Liao et al., 2022) Inequities are deepened by heroin being replaced with synthetic opioids, most especially fentanyl, over the last 10 years. (Barboza et al., 2022; Choi et al., 2022; Cobert et al., 2020; Ghose et al., 2022; Friedman, Beletsky, et al., 2022, Friedman, Montero, et al., 2022; Srinivasan et al., 2024; Volkow, 2020) Fentanyl has become heavily adulterated with xylazine, a trend originating in the Middle Atlantic states and radiating nationwide. (Ciccarone, 2021; D’Orsogna et al., 2023; Dayton et al., 2020; Jones et al., 2023; Lopez & Dong, 2020; Rhodes et al., 1999; Rhodes et al., 2012; Rhodes et al., 2006; Slavova et al., 2020) Intersecting with these changes, stimulants have been involved in an increasing proportion of drug-involved deaths as an evolving fourth wave of the U.S overdose crisis. (Kariisa et al., 2021; Rhodes, 2002; Volkow, 2021) Drug-involved deaths are heavily concentrated geographically in areas disproportionately impacted by poverty, poor health care and marginalization from social determinants of health. (Alexander et al., 2022; Ayub et al., 2023; Ball et al., 2022; Barboza et al., 2022; Cano et al., 2024; Choi et al., 2022; Cobert et al., 2020; Gupta et al., 2023; Hoots et al., 2020; Kariisa et al., 2022; Li et al., 2019; Liao et al., 2022; O’Donnell et al., 2020; Quijano et al., 2023; Srinivasan et al., 2024) Actionable spatial intelligence into the dynamics of increasing drug-involved deaths in local communities can inform the deployment of emergency responders, harm reductionists, health care providers, law enforcement, community health workers and neighborhood residents to address rising overdose deaths. (Palis et al., 2022)

Spatial methods for identifying place-based determinants of drug-involved deaths

Risk Terrain modeling (RTM) is a novel place-based statistical analytic approach that examines how features of the physical environment co-locate to aggravate risk of a particular outcome. (DE Banks et al., 2023; Dworkis et al., 2017; Marotta et al., 2019; Stewart et al., 2017) RTM was originally developed to diagnose features of the built environment that create unique geospatial conditions for the occurrence of aggravated assault, larceny, auto thefts and homicide. (Stopka et al., 2019; Visconti et al., 2015) Specific areas within a given study space are identified as having below average, average, or above average risk relative to other areas, conditional on the presence of certain place features. (Dworkis et al., 2017; Forati et al., 2021; Forati et al., 2023) The co-location of multiple place features may reflect social conditions that shape risk of overdose. (Lopez et al., 2022; Marchment & Gill, 2021) Alcohol outlets, locations of drug paraphernalia sales, distance away from nearest treatment centers, and local drug market disruptions are associated with heightened risk of drug-involved deaths. (Berryessa & Caplan, 2020; Piza et al., 2018; Caplan & Kennedy, 2016a; Caplan & Kennedy, 2016b; Kennedy et al., 2011) New analytic strategies and practical approaches are needed to contain the overdose crisis that focus on how features of the social environment co-locate to create places that are most vulnerable to drug-involved deaths. (Drawve et al., 2016; Abell-Hart et al., 2022; Caplan & Kennedy, 2016c)

Liu and colleagues used RTM and found drug involved deaths were more likely to occur in close proximity to seven place features in Miami-Dade County, Florida, USA that included parks, bus stops, restaurants, grocery stores, elementary/secondary schools, barber shop/beauty salons, and child/day care services. (Nesoff et al., 2021) Chichester and colleagues found drug-involved deaths were more likely to occur in close proximity to places with six place features in Jefferson County, Alabama, USA. These place features included bus stops, and public schools, inpatient treatment, transitional living facilities, express loan establishments, and liquor vendors. (Magee et al., 2022) Using hotspots analysis Samuels and colleagues found concentrations of overdose had greater proportions of police stations, religious organizations, health centers and crowded housing. (Pustz et al., 2022) Another common approach involves aggregating indices measuring variations in disadvantage and related measures (e.g., rates of naloxone administration), social determinants of health (e.g., hospitals, schools, policing, etc.) as predictors of aggregate rates of overdose. (Becker et al., 2022; Hochstetler & Peters, 2023; Ray et al., 2023; Tempalski et al., 2022; Visconti et al., 2015; Yankey et al., 2021)

Prior studies within the context of economic conditions have suggested that foreclosures as well as evictions may be associated with drug-involved deaths. (Chichester et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2023; Samuels et al., 2024) During COVID-19, the federal government issued a moratorium on evictions and foreclosures involving Federal Housing Authority and Veterans Authority issued mortgage loans. The moratorium did not apply to commercially issued mortgage home loans. Studies are yet to examine whether spatial proximity to locations of residential foreclosures is associated with greater risk of overdose. Foreclosures as well as property vacancies may create conditions conducive to higher numbers of drug-involved deaths.

Overdose racial inequities in St. Louis, MO

Widening racial disparities in overdose rates are evidenced by significant clustering in neighborhoods with greater proportions of Black and other minority residents. (Rowe et al., 2016) Racial disparities are aggravated by the emergence of fentanyl as predominant opioid in the drug supply. (Rowe et al., 2016) Racial disparities in rates of overdose continue to rise in suburbs. (Rudolph et al., 2019) Such patterns of disparities exist in Missouri, where rates of drug-involved deaths more than doubled among Black people, from 52.6 in 2017 to 119.2 per 100, 000 in 2022. In contrast, drug-involved deaths among White men also increased but at a much smaller proportion: 26.91 per 100,000 in 2017 to 38.51 per 100,000 in 2022, (Piza et al., 2023) leading to a growth in disparities from just under 2:1 to >3:1. Over the past decade, drug-involved deaths in St. Louis have increasingly concentrated in neighborhoods with greater proportions of Black residents as fentanyl replaced heroin and the polysubstance-involved fourth wave of the overdose crisis emerged. (Rowe et al., 2016) Concentrations of foreclosures may also be experienced disproportionately in neighborhoods that are disproportionately Black and impoverished. (Liu et al., 2023)

In St. Louis County, the suburban metro surrounding the city of St. Louis, there were a total of 394 drug-involved deaths in 2022 representing 31.9 deaths per 100,000 people. (Piza et al., 2023) New dynamics of the overdose epidemic continue to unfold in St. Louis and nationally. While outside of a central city, St. Louis County represents a growing phenomenon—increased overdose risk with limited services and resources particularly in Black and other minority communities. Despite increased overdose risk, harm reduction services and health care providers are limited in their ability to optimize the deployment of existing resources. (Behar et al., 2016) Research is needed to optimize the deployment of social and healthcare resources to local neighborhoods. Doing so requires the development of new analytic strategies and practical approaches to containing the overdose crisis that focus on how features of the social environment co-locate to create places that are most vulnerable to overdose. (Drawve et al., 2016; Abell-Hart et al., 2022; Caplan & Kennedy, 2016c) Place-based approaches—which can be used to identify neighborhoods with the environmental conditions that create heightened vulnerability to overdoses—remain understudied. As a result, practitioners and health care providers are limited in their ability to optimize the deployment of harm reduction and overdose prevention resources.

St. Louis, like many other cities in the United States, is still experiencing the effects of redlining. (Brown & Wehby, 2019; Simon & Masters, 2024; Venkataramani & Tsai, 2020) When coupled with more contemporary socioeconomic factors redlining has led to greater residential racial isolation in St. Louis. Redlining involved laws and practices that racially segregated the St. Louis region through urban planning, districting, exclusion from mortgages and lending. (Venkataramani & Tsai, 2020) Redlining combined with more recent disinvestment, foreclosures and vacant housing has created the economic climate for the growth of deaths of despair such as drug-involved deaths in St. Louis. Geographic racial residential division in St. Louis County occurred along the area of Delmar Boulevard. “The Delmar Divide” is representative of many other parts of the United States in which a major road divides wealth and social determinants of health underscoring the presence of a geographic health disparity. Prior research by Banks et al., 2023 found rates of overdose are much higher among Black compared to White residents and are clustered north of the Delmar Divide. (Rowe et al., 2016) More research is needed that identifies features of the built environment within high risk places for drug-involved deaths to inform future strategies of improving equitable distribution of overdose prevention and treatment services. (Becker et al., 2022; Hochstetler & Peters, 2023; Tempalski et al., 2022; Yankey et al., 2021)

The present study

To address these gaps, we identified environmental correlates of drug-involved deaths, using Risk Terrain Modeling (RTM) using data from 2022 to 2023 from St. Louis County Missouri. The place features of interest were selected based on review of existing RTM literature with overdose and violence as outcomes. Place features were also identified by consulting with local community members of the St. Louis Public Safety Collaborative. (Becker et al., 2022; Hochstetler & Peters, 2023; Tempalski et al., 2022; Yankey et al., 2021)

Methods

Procedures

An IRB exemption was provided by the Washington University in St. Louis - Human Research Protection Office for these data (Wash U HRPO: 202307160). Data analysis occurred in close consultation and meetings with the St. Louis County Prosecutor’s Office and the St. Louis County Department of Health Office of the Medical Examiner.

Medical examiner data

The outcome of interest for this study, drug-involved deaths, was obtained through a request to the Office of the Medical Examiner for St. Louis County for cases where drug use was identified during the postmortem examination and toxicology results as a contributing cause of death. The period covered by the RTM was 1/1/2022–12/31/2022 (the most recent full year available at the time of the study) and a subsequent 6-month period from 1/1/2023–6/31/2023 to generate estimates of predictive accuracy of the models. The term “drug-involved death” is used instead of overdose to more accurately describe the role of drugs as a possible contributing and not necessarily the main cause of death. Moreover, deaths were coded in the toxicology report based on substances contributing to cause of death included stimulants, opioids, other drugs and alcohol. Sociodemographic characteristics consisted of: race, age, whether the decedent was pronounced at the scene, and the police department that was present (none, county, municipal). Variables were coded by the study team.

Place features

Table 1 presents 54 place features selected for empirical testing through a research partnership with SIMSI,1 a provider of public safety analytics, across the study space of St. Louis County. Place features were selected based on review of RTM articles with drug-involved deaths (Liu et al., 2024 and Samuels et al., 2024; Chichester et al., 2020) and violence (i.e. Caplan, 2015). St. Louis County Open Gov website provided foreclosure filings for the full year of 2020. The foreclosure dataset included the date of sale of homes sold back to private lenders of single and multi-family homes from the St. Louis County Open Gov website. (DE Banks et al., 2023) Foreclosure data from the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic were selected for our analyses of 2022 overdose data in line with previous research describing the delayed effect that conditions related to unstable housing can have on communities. Locations of residential properties sold as part of foreclosure proceedings were included in the analysis of place features. Data was provided by the St. Louis County GIS service center and included addresses that had a foreclosure filing in 2020 from a commercial residential loan lender or investment holding.2 Boundary files were obtained in the form of 2020 TIGER/Line Shapefiles from the United States Census Bureau. (Lichter et al., 2021) The study area included St. Louis County consisting of 93 municipalities.

Racial composition

Data on racial composition were obtained from the St. Louis County GIS Service Center who disseminated a shapefile with the County Council Districts and their respective numbers of Black residents, and the total population collected during the 2020 Census. Data were aggregated to the County District level and subsequently disseminated in a shapefile. A variable was created measuring racial composition by dividing the total number of Black residents by the total population. There are a total of 7 council districts in St. Louis County each with a population from 140,948 to 144,783 people covering 93 municipalities. Racial composition across the county was used as a basemap to add additional context to understanding the geographic distribution of high-risk places for the occurrence of drug-involved deaths. Results from the RTM visualized using racial composition as a background to illustrate the extent to which high risk areas may intersect with places that have greater racial composition of black residents.

Statistical analyses

Chi-square tests were used to examine differences by race in the types of substances contributing to the cause of death, whether police arrived on scene, and if the person was pronounced on scene or at the hospital. (Drug Overdose Dashboard - Fatal Overdoses, 2023; Montaque, Christenson, & Spector, 2022) The RTMDx software designed by Simsi was used to carry out the RTM analyses. Elastic net penalized Poisson regression models were used to test for environmental risk factors of overdose using cross-validation with Schwarz’s Bayesian Criterion (SBC) to mitigate spurious associations by favoring more parsimonious models. (Bureau UC, 2021; Diamond et al., 2020; Dworkis et al., 2017; Pandis, 2016) Analysis was performed using Generalized Additive Models for Location and Scale (GAMLSS) in R. The model with the lowest value was selected. (Smith et al., 2019) R script was generated and subsequently exported to be provided for other investigators interested in replicating the present study’s findings (available by request). (Prener, 2021) Three risk terrain models were run – for all cases, any case involving an opioid, and any case involving a stimulant – to identify the place features associated risk of drug-involved deaths. A second set of two models were ran for Black and White decedents.

Parameterization of risk terrain models

Inputs were set in the RTM to calibrate the model. Spatial proximity was set to 500 feet with a maximum of 3 blocks (1500 feet) to reflect larger suburban blocks in St. Louis County. For more details on RTM and selection of parameter settings refer to Caplan and Kennedy, (2016).

Relative risk values (RRV)

The RTM regression approach produced relative risk values (RRV) for each individual risk factor to describe the strength of associations between each place feature and drug-involved deaths relative to all the other risk factors. Specifically, individual risk values are weights of each individual risk factor relative to each other. The RRVs for each individual risk factor allows for comparisons between the risk factors within each of the models (see Liu et al., 2023 and Chichester et al., 2021). Relative risk values were provided in the tables for each of the place features.

Relative risk scores (RRS)

Each place feature’s risk map layer was combined to produce a composite relative risk score (RRS), a combined and weighted measure of spatial influence of all features at microspatial places (cells) throughout St. Louis County. (DE Banks et al., 2023; Diamond et al., 2020; Dworkis et al., 2017; McHugh, 2013; Pandis, 2016; Simsi, Inc, 2023; Smith et al., 2019) RTM was used to create shapefiles of continuous surface of 250 ft × 250 ft raster cells in which each cell represented approximately half a standard urban block creating a fishnet grid of conceptually meaningful sized units of analysis. Relative Risk Scores (RRSs) were calculated for each raster cell and visualized to identify areas that were at greater risk of overdose throughout St. Louis County. (Diamond et al., 2020; Dworkis et al., 2017; Jones-Eversley et al., 2018; Smith et al., 2019) RRSs were visualized to identify places of heightened risk throughout the region. The RRSs were saved in a shapefile visualized using QGIS capable of customizing map visualizations. Spatial locations greater than or equal to 1 % of the top RRS scores were considered high priority and 2 standard deviations (SD) above the mean score were considered exceptionally high-risk places. The RRS provides a composite measure of the extent to which individual risk factors are co-located to produce risk in a given place.

Maps were generated for deaths involving all drugs, opioid-involved, stimulant-involved deaths as well as by Black, and White race. RTM assumes the entire study space has some measurable amount of risk. A shapefile of racial composition at the District Council level was used to visually aid the interpretation of the RRS. Racial composition was used descriptively to show how the RRSs were distributed across neighborhoods.

Predictive accuracy index (PAI)

The Predictive Accuracy Index (PAI) provides an indication of the quality of model specification as well as the usefulness of the model in predicting future drug-involved deaths. PAI was calculated for all five sets of models. Results from the PAI sensitivity analysis are included in each of the tables for their respective analyses. (Lopez et al., 2022; Marchment & Gill, 2021) Values found in the PAI were comparable to prior literature. (DE Banks et al., 2023)

Results

Descriptive statistics and bivariate tests of differences by race among overdose decedents in 2022 in St. Louis county

A total of 329 drug-involved deaths that occurred in the county in 2022 were included in the final sample; 37 were excluded because of inaccuracies in recording the coordinates needed for geocoding (Table 2). Out of the 329 drug-involved deaths that were included in the study, 39.8 % were amongst Black decedents (n = 131) and 57.5 % (n = 189) were among White decedents. A little more than two-thirds of the decedents were men (68.4 % n = 225). The average age for all decedents was 43.3 years but Black decedents were significantly older (M = 46.0, SD=13.5) than White decedents (M = 42.1 SD=13.8) (chi-sq=6.9 p=.009). Black decedents were also more likely to be male (75.6 %, n = 99 vs. 62.4 %, n = 118, chi-sq=6.1 p=.013), had cocaine (58.8 %, n = 77 vs. 14.3 %, n = 27 chi-sq=69.8 p < .001), and synthetic opioids (80.2 %, n = 105 vs. 69.8 %, n = 132, chi-sq=4.3 p=.039) detected in their toxicology reports, and fewer had police arrive on scene (21.4 %, n = 28 vs. 12.2 %, n = 23, chi-sq=6.8, p=.034). Conversely, more White decedents had methamphetamines (38.1 %, n = 72 vs. 19.1 %, n = 25 chi-sq=13.2 p<.001), benzodiazepines (5.8 %, n = 11 vs 0.8 %, n = 1 chi-sq=5.5, p=.019) detected. Black and White decedents did not significantly differ regarding the day of week of the drug-involved death. The mean proportion of Black residents across the county districts was 26 % with a range of 3–75 %.

Place features associated with high-risk settings for drug-involved deaths by drug type

Table 3 presents Relative Risk Values (RRV), spatial proximity, and tests of significance from the RTM models by type of substance. In the model including all drugs, deaths were more likely to occur in close proximity to hotels (RRV=39.65, SE=0.34, t-value=10.81 p < .001), foreclosures (RRV=4.41, SE=0.12, t-value = 2.80, p < .001), police departments (RRV=3.13, SE=0.24, t-value=4.86, p < .001), and restaurants (RRV=2.33, SE=0.12, t-value=7.15, p < .001).

In the model of opioid-involved deaths, deaths were more likely to occur in close proximity to hotels/motels (RRV=50.89, SE=0.36, t-value=79.16, p-value<0.001) foreclosures (RRV=4.49, SE=0.14, t-value=10.71, p<.001), barber shops (RRV=2.92, SE=0.23, t-value 4.64, p < .001), auto painting/repair shops (RRV=1.97 SE=0.15, t-value=4.50, p<.001), and places of worship (RRV=1.85, SE=0.14, t-value=4.38, p < .001).

In the model of stimulant-involved deaths, deaths were more likely to occur in close proximity to residential foreclosures (RRV= 4.90, SE=0.16, t-value=0.89, p < .001), pharmacies (RRV=4.23, SE=0.34, t-value=4.31, p < .001), police departments (RRV=3.54, SE=0.29, t-value=4.45, p < .001), hotels/motels (RRV=2.87, SE=0.26, t-value=4.04, p<.001), convenience stores (RRV=2.17, SE=0.18, t-value=4.28, p < .001), and places of worship (RRV=1.89, SD=0.16, t-value=3.98, p < .001).

Place features associated with high risk settings for any drug-involved deaths by race of decedent

Table 4 presents relative risk values, standard errors (SE), t-values and p-values for Black and White decedents. For Black decedents, deaths were more likely to occur in close proximity to foreclosures (RRV=9.01, SE=0.18, t-value=11.92, p < .001), and places of worship (RRV=2.51, SE=0.18, t-value=11.92, p < .001). For White decedents, deaths were more likely to occur in close proximity to hotels/motels (RRV=38.97, SE=0.39, t-value=9.30, p < .001), foreclosures (RRV=2.57, SE=0.16, t-value=5.84, p < .001), restaurants (RRV=2.52, SE=0.17, t-value=5.33, p < .001), and auto painting/repair shops (RRV=2.04, t-value=5.33, p < .001) (Table 5).

Spatial mapping of relative risk scores calculated in the models

Figs. 1 and 2 present a visualization of relative risk scores calculated in the Risk Terrain Models showing vulnerable places to drug-involved deaths in the study area. The maps visualizing “high priority places” are defined as being in the highest 1 % of relative risk scores and are in pink. The orange areas highlight riskier places where overdose is ≥ 2 standard deviations (SD) from the mean. Maps were generated for overall, opioid, stimulant, Black and White decedents. Fig. 2 includes maps of the overlap between high priority/exceptionally high-risk settings for Black and White decedents. The overlap between high-risk places can illuminate similarities between Black and White decedents as well as important differences in the environmental settings that are conducive to drug-involved deaths in St. Louis County.

For all drug-involved deaths, the mean RRS across St. Louis County was 2.08 with an SD of 4.11. The range of RRSs was 1–408.69 with 11,306 high-risk places accounting for 4.81 % of the study space with RRS values greater than 2 SD from the mean. For opioid-involved deaths, the mean RSS was 2.00 with an SD of 5.17. Throughout the study space there were 3389 places with RRS that were greater than 2 SD from the mean accounting for 1.44 % of the study space. For stimulant-involved deaths, the mean RSS was 2.18 with a SD of 3.95. There were 5447 places across the grid that were greater than 2 SD from the mean with a range of scores from 1 to 175.76. For Black decedents, the mean RSS was 2.45 with a standard deviation of 4.36 and a range of 1–22.64. There were 8794 places that were >2 SD above the mean accounting for 3.74 % of the study space. For White decedents, the mean RSS was 2.02 with a standard deviation of 4.42 and a range of 1–513.55. There was a total of 4653 places or 1.98 % of the study space that were identified as at the greatest risk of drug-involved deaths with RRS that were greater than were > 2 SD from the mean. There was a total of 2399 places identified as either high priority (>1 % of top RRS) or exceptionally risky (>2SD from the mean RRS) that overlapped between Black and White decedents.

Racial composition and visualization of relative risk scores

Deaths involving any drug, opioids and stimulants were disproportionately clustered in council districts with between 45 and 75 % Black residents. The riskiest settings for drug-involved deaths for Black residents occurred in the two Council Districts where most of the residents were Black.

Discussion

The overarching objective of this study was to assess the role of several empirically-determined place features in creating social settings that are vulnerable to incidents of drug-involved deaths. In St. Louis County, drug involved deaths were most likely to occur in close proximity to hotels/motels, foreclosures, places of worship, barber shops, police departments, convenience stores, restaurants, and auto-painting/repair shops. Hotels near foreclosed properties that were also located near restaurants, for instance, were higher risk compared to all other hotels in the study area. Our study considered underlying racial composition in the context of St. Louis by visualizing spatial data of high-risk settings for overdose over a basemap of the percent of residents by council district who were Black compared to all other residents. Findings from this study generate implications beyond the context of St. Louis County that are relevant for overdose prevention nationally in the United States and globally. Although patterns and concentration of people is important, our study found important places that can be prioritized to increase the equitable distribution of overdose prevention resources in communities that have the greatest concentration of places that are vulnerable to the occurrence of drug-involved deaths.

Implications for public health practice and policy

Hotels/Motels

The place feature encompassing hotels and motels was the most robust risk factor associated with drug-involved deaths. There are several potential explanations from prior research for these findings. Motels are contexts within which risk of intravenous drug use and other risky drug use is high. (Giménez-Santana et al., 2018) Hotels/motels may function as a reprieve from challenges associated with inadequate housing and poor access to safe environments, ultimately giving rise to the cooccurrence of drug use and transactional activities, such as sex work where people may lodge and use drugs in between partners. (Piza et al., 2018; Kennedy & Dugato, 2018; Kennedy et al., 2011) A potential next step could be to work with corporate motel chains that are optimally situated to pilot and institute companywide policies around overdose prevention, harm reduction and safety.

Overdose prevention supplies have been distributed previously at hotels and motels particularly during COVID-19 (SFAF, 2020). (Kennedy et al., 2011) Naloxone distribution strategies are increasingly being marketed to the hospitality industry in recognition of this important access point. These interventions are yet to be evaluated. RTM provides a novel strategy of examining the impact of deploying naloxone vending machines at hotels on reductions in overdose rates. At the policy level implementation of corporate-wide policies around stocking naloxone could potentially make a sizable impact on the persistence of overdose deaths in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas. Future research is needed that focuses on how overdose prevention strategies could be disseminated across these varied industries. More research is needed into why hotels/motels may not be as salient a risk factor for Black decedents.

Places of worship

Our study found deaths were more likely to occur in close proximity to places of worship for opioid involved, stimulant-involved, and Black decedents. One explanation for these findings is Black urban and suburban churches have traditionally offered spaces for people who are experiencing a convergence of factors, such as houselessness and mental illness, that elevate their risk of adverse drug-related outcomes. (Caplan et al., 2011; Enhancing Guest Safety, 2024; Iversen et al., 2021; Kennedy et al., 2016; Mwangi et al., 2019; Pandey & Seelman, 2022; Rezaei et al., 2020) Moreover, people who use drugs may also perceive parking lots and public areas and lawns near places of worship as safe and accepting settings––both structurally as well as spiritually––that are sheltered from community violence and crime. Places of worship are often open only a few days a week and intermittently which could also create open spaces whereby people may feel comfortable consuming substances.

Prior research has found that churches are feasible, acceptable, cost effective and effective settings in which to deliver HIV prevention services. (Brown et al., 2003; Caplan et al., 2011; Fletcher et al., 2023; Kennedy et al., 2016; Rezaei et al., 2020; Vigliotti et al., 2020) Our study supports the promising findings of Jordan et al. (2020) which centers racial equity in a pilot study on substance use disorder treatment conducted in Black churches. Future efforts must (1) be directed at community-engaged data practices with clergy members that allows for input and direction into allocation of resources to the prevention of overdose and (2) focus on acceptability, cultural tailoring of interventions through mobile harm reduction and community engagement strategies with places of worship in St. Louis County as well as promoting safer-use alternatives to injection (Brown, Alick, Heaston, Monestime, & Powe, 2022).

Foreclosures

Drug-involved deaths were more likely to occur in close proximity to foreclosures that occurred immediately preceding the pandemic as well as sales that occurred during the pandemic (that were finalized before the pandemic started). Although the moratorium stopped new foreclosure determinations, houses that were foreclosed on already were sold during the initial months of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Explanations for these results can be found in prior research that suggests local and regional economic disadvantage, specifically through foreclosures are associated with increased risk of opioid related overdose deaths. (Berkley-Patton et al., 2013; Berkley-Patton et al., 2010; Brewer & Williams, 2019; Stewart et al., 2016) Higher concentrations of foreclosed properties may increase the likelihood of abandoned areas, resulting in higher crime risks, including drug-related crimes. More research is needed that incorporates foreclosure and vacancy status in the distribution of place features to provide more nuanced perspectives on risk factors of overdose.

Restaurants, convenience stores and barber shops

In addition to the hotel/motel industry, our findings that drug-involved deaths were more likely to occur in close proximity to restaurants and convenience stores is consistent with Liu et al., 2023′s study on overdose in South Florida. Our study found overdose was more likely to occur near barber shops. One possible speculation from these findings is that a combination of barber shops being cultural centers of activity and having areas to use drugs that increase risk of drug-involved deaths. In fact, previous research has described the promise of leveraging barber-shops to aid in the dissemination of health information (Berkley-Patton et al., 2012). These are cultural centers of activity and social interactions within local communities in St. Louis and offer direct opportunities for engagement around overdose prevention efforts. Future research is needed that focuses on dissemination of health information in barber shops, restaurants, and other locations where people may congregate.

Implications for advancing racial equity research, advocacy and policy practice

There are several implications for advancing racial equity research, advocacy and policy practice that arise from the findings presented in this research study. The drug involved deaths were more likely to occur in places with features that were different for Black compared to White decedents. For Black decedents, overdose deaths were more likely to occur in close proximity to residential foreclosures and places of worship. For White decedents, drug-involved deaths were more likely to occur in close proximity to foreclosures but additionally, hotels/motels, restaurants, and auto repair shops. Although deaths were more likely to occur in areas close to foreclosures for Black and White decedents, foreclosures are disproportionately concentrated in North St. Louis. North St. Louis is a region where economic challenges have disproportionately impacted Black people and other racial and ethnic minorities. Spatial mapping of the RRS illustrate how concentrated high risk places for overdose is particularly in the North St. Louis region of the county. Our findings reinforce existing research by Banks and colleagues that overdose among Black decedents was more heavily concentrated spatially in fewer neighborhoods compared to White decedents.

High-risk areas for drug-involved deaths for White decedents were found in both the northern and southern regions of the county. When compared, we found overlap between the places where White and Black individuals overdosed. These findings are underscored in the map that illustrates the physical places where risky settings overlapped for both Black and White decedents.

In the RTM models we found differences in the place features between Black vs white residents suggesting that although the geographic locations may be similar, the place features associated with overdose have important differences.

Limitations

Several limitations are worth noting. The first is this study is cross-sectional and did not examine how patterns of risk for overdose shift and are dynamic over time thus precluding any casual inference. We did not account for the time of day of overdose and discovery of the body because the times provided by the medical examiner’s office was representative of the time the person was found, rather than the exact time of fatal overdose. Although the deaths were more likely to occur in close proximity to “hotels/motels”, our data did not allow for further distinction between hotels and motels. Given the high-risk profile of those places, further investigation into nuances within the hospitality industry will help refine public health interventions.

The geographic data and overdose data are from different years. It is possible that some businesses closed during the time between the overdose data and the place feature data. Another limitation of this study that foreclosure data are from 2020, the most recent year that data was available and encompassed a year that included the federal moratorium on foreclosures due to COVID-19 through 2022. Our measure of foreclosures did not differentiate the properties that were vacant, which could determine the likelihood of someone using substances inside the property.

RTM focuses on places and does not incorporate characteristics of the underlying population such as areas where Black and White populations are concentrated. All the results were overlayed onto a basemap of the proportion of the population that was Black in 2020 to illustrate descriptively the underlying racial composition of the area.

This study did not incorporate into the RTMs the extent to which risk of overdose can be attributed to the characteristics of the underlying population but rather only incorporated factors of the built environment. Places are often ignored in research and overlooked in studies that focus exclusively on aggregate measures health and sociological outcomes. We did not look at levels of poverty of people who are living in the neighborhoods. Future research is needed that looks at the interaction between sociodemographic factors of people in neighborhoods (age, race, poverty) and place features (places of worship, restaurants).

Finally, there are some limitations introduced by relying in Medical Examiner Data which can include some variation in the ways medical examiners determine whether an overdose took place (Brown & Wehby, 2019; Jordan et al., 2021; Karimli, 2022; Megerian et al., 2024) compared to the way other medical toxicologists may classify cause of death. (Karimli, 2022) In St. Louis County, the term drug-involved death is used to reflect that deaths are determined by whether the toxicology results reveal any drugs or alcohol contributing to the cause of death. A significant limitation of this, is it is not possible to determine whether it was use of drugs that led to the persons’ death or if the drugs were onboard but not a causal factor in the death. Research has found that medical examiners may be more accurate in death reporting compared to coroners who often lack adequate training and resources (Brown & Wehby, 2019; Libman et al., 2012).

Conclusions

Places of worship, the hospitality industry, and housing authorities may be physical features of the environment that reflect social conditions that are conducive to overdose. Future hypothesis testing research must be employed that replicates this research in numerous metropolitan contexts testing hypotheses about anticipated place features. Through identifying where the riskiest settings are located and place features that are most associated with overdose, interventions can be strategized to engage with the community more effectively including through more upstream approaches to preventing overdose. Spatial strategies that focus on place features disproportionately impacting Black decedents may help advance goals of racial equity in the distribution of critical harm reduction, HIV prevention, and substance use treatment resources. The place features identified in this study associated with overdose underscore the importance of working closely with community leaders and partners, policy makers and residents to mobilize the social, economic, and religious organizations that are needed to be at the table to cultivate healing and recovery from prior spatial patterns of overdose. The findings that are presented in this analysis can be a starting point for greater investment in neighborhoods in St. Louis County to reduce overdose by city, state, local and private parties and doners. If these place-based approaches are deployed in metropolitan and suburban areas that exhibit high degrees of racial inequities in overdose, they may work to reduce racial inequities in overdose in St. Louis and permit a more equitable distribution of existing local resources.
