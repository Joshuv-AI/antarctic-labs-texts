A case of solid cervical ectopic thymus in a child: a case description and literature review
Authors: Zifeng Shi, Chengpin Tao
Published: 2026

Introduction

Ectopic thymus refers to a relatively rare congenital developmental anomaly in which thymic tissue is located outside its normal anatomic descent pathway from the angle of the mandible to the anterior mediastinum because of abnormal migration of the thymic primordium during embryogenesis (1). From an embryological perspective, the thymus originates from the ventral endoderm of the third pharyngeal pouch at approximately the sixth week of gestation. During the following weeks, it migrates caudally along with the primitive cardiovascular system and ultimately settles in the anterior mediastinum. Any arrest during this process or deviation from the normal migratory route may result in ectopic thymic tissue. Although neck masses are common in pediatric practice, cervical ectopic thymus (CET) is extremely uncommon. According to the literature, it accounts for less than 1% of cystic and solid cervical space-occupying lesions in children (2).

Clinically, CET usually presents as a painless, slowly enlarging neck mass and is more often unilateral, with a predominance on the left side. According to its histological morphology, CET is mainly classified into two types: cystic lesions (thymic cysts) and solid lesions (solid CET). The former is relatively more common and is usually caused by incomplete regression of the thymopharyngeal duct, whereas solid ectopic thymus is exceedingly rare and often lacks typical clinical symptoms and specific physical signs (3). Because pediatricians and radiologists are often unfamiliar with this condition, solid CET is frequently misdiagnosed during the initial evaluation as lymphangioma, lymphadenitis, lymphoma, neurogenic tumors such as neuroblastoma, dermoid cyst, or thyroid disease (4). These difficulties in differential diagnosis may lead to a series of unnecessary and even invasive examinations. In recent years, with the increasing use of high-frequency ultrasonography, including the characteristic “starry sky” appearance, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), accurate preoperative diagnosis of CET has become possible, and the clinical management strategy has gradually shifted from routine surgical excision to conservative observation (5,6). Based on this background, we report a pathologically confirmed case of solid ectopic thymus on the left side of the neck in an infant, and, in conjunction with the recent literature, discuss its imaging features, differential diagnosis, and management strategies, with the aim of improving diagnostic accuracy, optimizing clinical decision-making, and minimizing misdiagnosis and overtreatment.

Clinical data

Patient information

The patient was a 3-month-and-3-day-old boy who was admitted to Anhui Provincial Children’s Hospital because of an incidentally discovered left cervical mass that had been present for more than half a month. He was born at term by spontaneous vaginal delivery, with an unremarkable perinatal history and normal general condition. There was no family history of hereditary disease, and both parents were healthy. The patient had no fever, cough, weight loss, dysphagia, dyspnea, or other compressive symptoms. On physical examination, a visible swelling was noted in the upper left side of the neck. The overlying skin was normal in color and temperature, with no local heat. Palpation revealed a soft mass measuring approximately 3 cm × 3 cm × 2 cm, with an indistinct boundary from the surrounding tissues, no obvious tenderness, and relatively good mobility. Cervical motion was not restricted.

All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee(s) and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardian of the patient for publication of this article and accompanying images. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

Imaging findings

Unenhanced and contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the neck showed a solid soft-tissue mass in the left cervical region with homogeneous density, without calcification, cystic change, or necrosis. Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated homogeneous enhancement. The lesion was poorly demarcated from the left sternocleidomastoid muscle. Based on CT findings, myofibroma of the left neck was initially suspected (Figure 1A,1B).

(C) Ultrasound revealed a solid hypoechoic mass with the “starry sky” appearance. (D) Fat-suppressed T2WI showed signal intensity identical to the mediastinal thymus (arrow). Color Doppler ultrasonography of the neck demonstrated a solid hypoechoic mass measuring 2.9 cm × 2.7 cm × 2.2 cm in the left submandibular region. The lesion had a clear margin and regular shape, with heterogeneous internal echogenicity and scattered punctate hyperechoic foci. Color Doppler flow imaging revealed internal vascular signals. The preliminary ultrasonographic impression was enlargement of the submandibular gland (Figure 1C).

MRI of the neck further demonstrated that the mass was located within the left parapharyngeal space, causing medial displacement and compression of the adjacent tissues, with an indistinct boundary from the carotid sheath. The lesion was isointense on both T1-weighted and T2-weighted images. On fat-suppressed T2-weighted imaging, the signal intensity of the lesion was identical to that of the normal thymus in the mediastinum (Figure 1D). Diffusion-weighted imaging showed high signal intensity. Contrast-enhanced fat-suppressed T1-weighted imaging demonstrated mild homogeneous enhancement (Figure 1E). Several enlarged lymph nodes were also seen around the lesion. The preliminary MRI diagnosis included myofibroma or lymphoma.

Treatment and pathological findings

Because the nature of the mass remained uncertain on initial CT and ultrasound (which raised suspicion of myofibroma or submandibular gland enlargement) and the patient’s family expressed significant anxiety and requested a definitive diagnosis, surgical treatment was pursued. Complete excision of the left cervical mass was performed under general anesthesia. Intraoperatively, the mass was found posterior to the left submandibular gland and anterior to the carotid sheath. It appeared multilobulated, soft in texture, and encapsulated. The lesion was completely dissected and removed.

Gross pathological examination revealed a gray-red, lobulated soft-tissue specimen. Histopathological examination (hematoxylin-eosin staining, ×100) showed well-preserved thymic architecture composed of cortical and medullary areas, dense lymphocytes, epithelial reticular cells, and typical Hassall corpuscles in the medulla. To further document the diagnosis, the immunohistochemical findings have been described in detail. Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) showed nuclear positivity in numerous immature cortical thymocytes. CD3 highlighted the T-cell population, including both cortical thymocytes and more mature medullary T lymphocytes. Pan-cytokeratin (AE1/AE3) demonstrated a delicate reticular epithelial meshwork and stained epithelial components of Hassall corpuscles. This combined histological and immunohistochemical profile supported the diagnosis of ectopic thymic tissue rather than lymphoma, myofibroma, or another soft-tissue tumor. Representative hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) photomicrograph was available for Figure 1F; however, representative immunohistochemistry (IHC) photomicrographs could not be retrieved from the archived pathology materials and therefore could not be included in the figure. The final pathological diagnosis was solid ectopic thymus located posterior to the left submandibular gland.

Postoperative follow-up

A retrospective review of the imaging studies confirmed the presence of a normal thymus in the anterior superior mediastinum, with normal size, morphology, and density. Postoperative peripheral blood T-cell subset analysis showed no abnormalities, and no evidence of immunodeficiency was detected. Specifically, the results showed: CD3+ T-cells 68% (Ref: 55–75%), CD4+ T-cells 42% (Ref: 33–50%), CD8+ T-cells 22% (Ref: 18–30%), and a CD4/CD8 ratio of 1.9 (Ref: 1.2–2.2), all within normal limits for his age. Up to the latest follow-up, the child had shown normal growth and development, with no evidence of local recurrence.

Discussion

Embryological pathogenesis and anatomical distribution

The embryologic development of the thymus involves a complex migratory process characterized by a well-defined spatial and temporal sequence. During the sixth to eighth weeks of gestation, the thymic epithelial primordium develops predominantly from the ventral endoderm of the third pharyngeal pouch, in close association with the inferior parathyroid primordium. The paired thymic primordia subsequently elongate, detach from the pharyngeal wall, and descend caudally and medially through the neck. During descent, the thymic primordia remain transiently connected to the pharyngeal wall through the thymopharyngeal ducts. Subsequently, the thymic primordium migrates along a cervicomediastinal pathway, passing in close relation to the thyroid gland, carotid sheath, and major cervical vessels before reaching the anterosuperior mediastinum, where bilateral fusion occurs at approximately the eighth week of gestation (7).

Current developmental studies indicate that thymus organogenesis is not governed by endodermal migration alone, but also depends on reciprocal epithelial–mesenchymal interactions, neural crest-derived mesenchyme, and transcriptional programs that regulate thymic epithelial differentiation and maturation (8,9). This characteristic inferomedial migration from the third pharyngeal pouch to the anterior mediastinum has also been emphasized by Krishnan et al. (10).

Human thymus development also involves coordinated interactions among pharyngeal endoderm, thymic epithelial progenitors, mesenchymal stromal cells, and hematopoietic precursors (11,12). The dorsal component of the third pouch gives rise to the inferior parathyroid glands, whereas the fourth pharyngeal pouch is principally associated with the superior parathyroid glands and the ultimobranchial body (13). Although experimental and historical embryological studies have proposed possible contributions from other pharyngeal structures, direct evidence supporting a substantial contribution of the second or fourth pharyngeal pouch to normal human thymus development remains limited. Therefore, the third pharyngeal pouch should still be regarded as the principal source of the human thymic epithelial primordium. Recent human single-cell and spatial studies have further shown that thymic organogenesis depends on tightly regulated epithelial differentiation, stromal organization, and intercellular signaling networks that establish the functional thymic microenvironment during fetal development (14).

Disruptions during this descent may result in ectopic cervical thymus (ECT). Specifically, persistence of the thymopharyngeal duct, sequestration of thymic tissue along the migration pathway, incomplete descent of the thymic primordium, abnormal separation of the cervical and mediastinal components, or persistence of a cervical thymic extension may all lead to ectopic thymic tissue in the neck (15). According to the underlying developmental mechanism, ECT can be divided into two subtypes: sequestration, in which the cervical thymic tissue is completely separated from the mediastinal thymus, and arrested descent, in which thymic tissue remains along the normal descent pathway and a normal mediastinal thymus may still be present (16). Andreozzi et al. reported that the most common location of ECT lies along the line extending from the angle of the mandible to the manubrium, corresponding to the normal embryologic descent pathway of the thymus. Notably, approximately 70–80% of cases occur on the left side of the neck (4). The reason for this left-sided predominance remains uncertain, although asymmetry in thymic descent and involution of the thymopharyngeal ducts has been proposed. Because this explanation has not been conclusively demonstrated, it should be regarded as a developmental hypothesis rather than an established mechanism. A retrospective study by Purcell et al., involving 100 pediatric cases of ECT, further confirmed this left-sided predominance (17).

In the present case, the solid lesion was located posterior to the left submandibular gland and anterior to the carotid sheath, precisely along the typical embryologic descent pathway of the thymus. This anatomical distribution is therefore highly consistent with the embryologic mechanisms described above. Moreover, the coexistence of a normally located mediastinal thymus and a separate cervical thymic mass is compatible with sequestration or persistence of thymic tissue along the embryological descent pathway, although the precise developmental mechanism cannot be established with certainty (18). Recognition of this characteristic cervicomediastinal distribution may help distinguish ECT from lymphadenopathy, lymphoma, salivary-gland lesions, neurogenic tumors, and other pediatric cervical masses.

In-depth analysis of imaging features

Accurate preoperative imaging diagnosis is crucial to avoid unnecessary surgery. Each imaging modality has its own strengths and limitations in the diagnosis of ECT, and an optimal noninvasive diagnostic performance can only be achieved through their combined and complementary use. Ultrasonography is the first-line imaging modality for evaluating pediatric neck masses. The characteristic ultrasonographic appearance of solid ECT has been described as the “starry sky sign”, referring to scattered punctate or short linear hyperechoic foci within a hypoechoic lymphoid stroma, corresponding histologically to interlobular septa, vessels, and Hassall corpuscles of the thymus (19). Krishnan et al. further refined this internal echotexture as the “embroidery yarn sign”, suggesting that its specificity may be sufficient to establish the diagnosis of ECT directly on ultrasound, thereby avoiding subsequent costly cross-sectional imaging and invasive biopsy (10). Because the normal mediastinal thymus is usually not included in routine ultrasonographic examination, many radiologists are unfamiliar with the characteristic sonographic features of thymic tissue, which is an important cause of misdiagnosis. In this case, the lesion was initially misdiagnosed as a submandibular gland abnormality, highlighting the need for sonographers to remain alert to ectopic cervical thymic tissue and to compare the lesion with the normal mediastinal thymus whenever possible.

On CT, ECT usually appears as a well-defined, homogeneous soft-tissue mass with attenuation similar to that of muscle. CT can clearly demonstrate the anatomical relationship between the lesion and surrounding major vessels, such as the common carotid artery and internal jugular vein, and therefore has some value in preoperative planning. However, CT is inferior to MRI in soft-tissue contrast resolution, and children are also exposed to ionizing radiation; therefore, it is not considered the preferred diagnostic modality. On CT, solid ECT typically manifests as a well-defined, homogeneous soft-tissue mass with attenuation characteristics similar to those of the normal mediastinal thymus (20).

MRI is widely regarded as the gold standard for the diagnosis of solid ECT. Its characteristic features on MRI include signal intensity identical to that of the normal thymus across all sequences: it is isointense on T1-weighted imaging or slightly hyperintense relative to muscle, demonstrates homogeneous high signal intensity on T2-weighted or short tau inversion recovery (STIR) images, and shows uniform signal suppression on fat-suppressed sequences (21). In a systematic review, Chang et al. emphasized that extending the field of view to include the thorax and comparing the signal characteristics of the cervical mass with those of the mediastinal thymus can significantly improve the accuracy of noninvasive diagnosis (3). It should be noted, however, that some cases of ECT may show restricted diffusion on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), unlike the normal mediastinal thymus. This finding may lead to over-suspicion of malignancy, such as rhabdomyosarcoma, and consequently trigger unnecessary invasive biopsy (10). In the present case, retrospective review revealed that the lesion demonstrated signal intensity completely identical to that of the mediastinal thymus on fat-suppressed T2-weighted imaging. Had this feature been fully appreciated preoperatively and the scan range been extended for direct comparison, surgical intervention might have been avoided.

Management strategy and ethical considerations: surgery or observation?

The current international consensus on the management of solid ECT has gradually shifted toward a more conservative approach. Schloegel and Gottschall were the first to propose a systematic algorithm for the conservative management of ECT, recommending clinical surveillance as the preferred strategy for asymptomatic patients with typical imaging findings. This recommendation is based on the expectation that thymic tissue undergoes physiological involution with age and on the lack of convincing evidence suggesting that ECT carries a higher risk of malignant transformation than orthotopic mediastinal thymus (22). This perspective was further supported by the systematic review by Chang et al., in which 98% of pediatric patients with ECT managed conservatively remained free of complications (3).

The rationale for observation is grounded in the unique biological role of the thymus. Before puberty, the thymus is the most immunologically active lymphoid organ and plays an essential role in T-cell maturation and the establishment of central immune tolerance. Thereafter, it gradually involutes with age. Purcell et al. provided objective evidence for this physiological process in a cohort of children (mean age 4.1 years), demonstrating that the volume of ectopic thymic tissue decreased significantly over a mean follow-up period of 3.2 years, from 4.3 to 2.7 cm3 (P=0.008) (17). Moreover, there is currently no sufficient evidence to indicate that ECT is associated with a greater malignant potential than normal mediastinal thymic tissue (22).

Surgical excision should therefore be reserved for selected cases. Accepted indications for surgery include: (I) the presence of compressive symptoms, such as respiratory distress, stridor, or dysphagia; (II) atypical imaging features that preclude exclusion of malignancy; (III) rapid lesion enlargement; and (IV) concomitant thymoma or other thymic-origin neoplasms (4,22). Andreozzi et al. further emphasized that the decision to operate should be made cautiously, with full consideration of potential surgical complications, including injury to the innominate artery, recurrent laryngeal nerve damage, and trauma to adjacent superior mediastinal structures (4).

A critical safety prerequisite before any surgical intervention is confirmation of a normally located mediastinal thymus. Thoracic imaging, including ultrasound or MRI, should be routinely performed before resection of any cervical mass suspected to represent ECT. This step is not merely technical, but also of substantial ethical importance. The systematic review by Cavalcanti et al. demonstrated that early thymectomy, whether total or partial, is associated with long-term reductions in multiple T-cell subsets and a significant decline in T-cell receptor (TCR) diversity (23). More importantly, a large cohort study published by Kooshesh et al. in The New England Journal of Medicine in 2023 showed that adults undergoing thymectomy had significantly higher 5-year all-cause mortality (8.1% vs. 2.8%; relative risk, 2.9) and cancer incidence (7.4% vs. 3.7%; relative risk, 2.0) than matched controls, along with markedly reduced production of new CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells (24). These findings, although derived from adult populations, underscore the far-reaching immunological consequences of thymic loss and further strengthen the rationale for conservative management in children with ECT. In addition, several pediatric cases have been reported in which excision of the only functioning cervical thymus resulted in severe T-cell immunodeficiency, with devastating clinical consequences (10).

In the present case, the patient underwent surgical excision because of a preoperative misdiagnosis. Fortunately, preoperative imaging had already confirmed the presence of a normal mediastinal thymus, and postoperative immunological monitoring revealed no abnormalities. Nevertheless, this case highlights several important clinical lessons. First, ECT should be routinely included in the differential diagnosis of asymptomatic solid neck masses in children. Second, during ultrasonographic evaluation, the mediastinal thymus should be actively examined for morphological comparison. Finally, when imaging findings are characteristic of ECT, conservative follow-up should be prioritized to avoid unnecessary surgery and potential impairment of thymus-dependent immune function.

Conclusions

Although solid CET in children is rare, it has highly characteristic imaging features. Radiologists should be familiar with the key diagnostic clue that the lesion shows signal intensity or echogenicity consistent with that of the mediastinal thymus. For painless solid neck masses in infants and young children, MRI or ultrasonographic examination extending below the sternal notch should be routinely performed. When the diagnosis is confirmed and no compressive symptoms are present, follow-up observation is a more scientific and reasonable management strategy. This approach not only helps preserve immune function in children, but also reflects the integration of precision medicine with humane care.
