A Workforce‐Informed Competency Framework Defines 30 Core Abilities of Veterinary Diagnostic Imaging Specialists
Authors: Julie A Noyes, Jennifer Brisson, Gareth J Buckley, Laura Hammond, Timothy Manzi, Rachel E Pollard, Jaime Sage, Kenneth R Waller, Patrick Welch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVeterinary diagnostic imaging residency training lacks a workforce‐informed competency framework defining the core abilities required for specialty‐level practice. This study developed and content‐validated VIEW (Veterinary Imaging Education and Workforce), a competency framework for diagnostic imaging specialists. A modified Delphi design spanned three phases from January 2025 through February 2026. Phase 1 used a seven‐member focus group comprising six board‐certified diagnostic imaging specialists and one board‐certified emergency and critical care specialist to generate initial competencies through structured discussion and anonymous rating rounds. Competencies meeting an a priori consensus threshold (≥0.70) advanced to Phase 2, involving a survey of ACVR and ECVDI board‐certified specialists and diagnostic imaging residents. Survey respondents rated the importance and clarity of competencies and provided feedback. Phase 3 involved a structured focus group review of integrated survey findings to finalize the framework. Phase 1 identified 58 competencies, of which 52 advanced to Phase 2. Eighty respondents completed the Phase 2 survey, with 36 of 52 competencies (69.2%) meeting the consensus threshold. Qualitative analysis identified 356 competency‐specific comments, 20 additional competency suggestions, and 24 general comments. Survey feedback refined the framework through consolidation, restructuring, and one addition, resulting in 30 core competencies organized into six domains with 14 illustrative subcompetencies. VIEW competencies were mapped to the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education and the American Association of Veterinary Medical Colleges (AAVMC) Competency‐Based Veterinary Education (CBVE) frameworks, demonstrating structural alignment with established health professions competency frameworks. VIEW provides the first workforce‐informed competency framework for veterinary diagnostic imaging, supporting competency‐based curriculum design and assessment.Keywords: competency‐based medical education, expert consensus, modified Delphi methodology, veterinary graduate education, workforce‐informed framework

Veterinary Imaging Education and Workforce

Introduction

Diagnostic imaging residency programs in veterinary radiology operate within a training structure defined by the American College of Veterinary Radiology (ACVR), with expectations articulated through minimum case‐volume requirements (e.g., interpreting 4000 radiographic studies, 300 computed tomography [CT] studies, and 200 magnetic resonance imaging [MRI] studies) and procedural benchmarks over a minimum 36‐month training period [1]. These requirements provide clarity regarding what residents are expected to perform during training, but they do not explicitly define the underlying abilities required to perform those activities effectively across diverse clinical contexts. A comprehensive review of the published literature identified no empirically developed, externally validated competency framework defining the core abilities required for specialty‐level veterinary diagnostic imaging practice.

The absence of an explicit competency framework presents several challenges for residency programs. Task‐based expectations alone may fail to capture how trainees integrate knowledge, technical skills, clinical reasoning, communication, and professional judgment in real‐world practice [2, 3, 4]. Without clearly defined abilities as competency‐based curricular outcomes, programs may rely on surrogate indicators of readiness, such as time in training or procedural volume, rather than direct evidence of resident capability [3]. This reliance on volume‐based indicators assumes that exposure to a sufficient number of cases will translate into competence, yet does not directly measure whether residents have developed the clinical reasoning, diagnostic decision‐making, and communication abilities necessary for independent specialty practice [4]. Consequently, two residents completing identical case volumes may demonstrate markedly different levels of integrated clinical competence. This can limit alignment among educational goals, assessment strategies, and curricular content and may contribute to variability in how specialty competence is conceptualized and evaluated across training programs [5].

Competency‐based education (CBE) emerged across the health professions in response to limitations of time‐based and task‐centered training models, shifting the emphasis from exposure and completion to the development and assessment of observable abilities that integrate knowledge, skills, and professional behaviors [6]. Within residency education, competency frameworks serve as outcome‐oriented structures that define what residents should be able to do, how they should apply their expertise across clinical situations, and how their performance can be meaningfully assessed [6, 7].

When designed for residency training, competency frameworks offer several critical benefits. They define observable abilities as curricular outcomes, enabling programs to align assessment strategies with evidence that residents can demonstrate those abilities in practice [6]. Because competencies represent integrated performance rather than isolated tasks, assessment approaches must enable residents to demonstrate observable evidence of their abilities in authentic clinical contexts [8]. These defined outcomes guide the development of curriculum content and learning experiences needed to support residents in acquiring those abilities [6].

The goal of this study was to develop and content‐validate a diagnostic imaging competency framework that defines the core abilities required for veterinary specialty‐level practice. To achieve this goal, we employed a mixed‐methods, consensus development design that combined iterative expert focus group processes with survey validation. The resulting framework, termed VIEW (Veterinary Imaging Education and Workforce), defines integrated abilities encompassing knowledge, skills, and professional behaviors that extend beyond task execution to support effective performance across clinical scenarios.

VIEW serves multiple interconnected functions within residency training. As a publicly accessible, profession‐wide reference, VIEW defines curricular outcomes and enables programs to align assessment strategies with observable evidence of resident ability in authentic clinical contexts. By externalizing expectations that are often implicit in clinical supervision, VIEW offers a shared language to support mentorship, feedback, and progression decisions within existing residency training models. By articulating specialty practice in terms of integrated abilities rather than task completion alone, VIEW provides a foundation for residency programs to design assessment strategies that go beyond technical proficiency. These strategies ensure that residents acquire cross‐task abilities, such as clinical reasoning, diagnostic decision‐making, and professional communication, that are required for independent specialty‐level practice in veterinary diagnostic imaging.

Methods

Study Design

This Institutional Review Board (IRB)‐approved (Protocol #20251870) mixed‐methods consensus development study developed and content‐validated VIEW, a competency framework defining core abilities required for specialty‐level diagnostic imaging practice. The study employed a modified Delphi design to generate, refine, and validate competencies. Modified Delphi methodology combines structured group discussion with iterative anonymous surveys to achieve expert consensus. This approach retains core Delphi principles such as iteration, controlled feedback, and predefined consensus thresholds, while allowing participants to clarify concepts and establish shared understanding prior to rating [9, 10, 11]. This design is widely used across healthcare professions for developing competency frameworks [9, 12, 13].

Participants were recruited using purposive sampling, which intentionally selects individuals with relevant expertise [14, 15]. Inclusion criteria required extensive knowledge of the diagnostic imaging specialist role through active engagement in clinical or educational settings. The focus group comprised six board‐certified diagnostic imaging specialists and one board‐certified emergency and critical care specialist. Among the radiologists, two were in private practice, two were from university veterinary colleges, and two had worked in both settings. One of the radiologists also served as a residency program director. The emergency and critical care specialist was experienced in developing residency curricula and in Delphi, consensus, and competency‐development methodologies that supported the study's design. As a veterinary school faculty member, they trained residents, including diagnostic imaging residents, and worked closely with diagnostic imaging specialists. This strategy ensured the framework reflected informed professional perspectives grounded in current practice. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained prior to involvement.

The study was conducted over a 14‐month period (January 2025 to February 2026) and comprised three sequential phases. Phase 1 used a modified Delphi focus group process to generate an initial list of competencies representing the essential abilities of diagnostic imaging specialists. Phase 2 involved external validation of these competencies through a survey of diagnostic imaging workforce professionals, thereby providing broader confirmation of the consensus achieved in Phase 1. Phase 3 involved revising and finalizing the competency list based on quantitative importance ratings and qualitative feedback obtained during the Phase 2 validation survey.

A comprehensive literature review was conducted to determine whether a validated competency framework for veterinary diagnostic imaging specialty practice had been previously published. PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar were searched from 2000 to 2025 with no language restrictions. Search terms included “competency framework” OR “competency‐based education” combined with “veterinary” OR “veterinary medicine” combined with “diagnostic imaging” OR “radiology” OR “veterinary radiology” combined with “residency” OR “specialty training” OR “postgraduate training.” Titles and abstracts of retrieved records were screened for relevance, and full‐text review was conducted for records describing the development or validation of a competency framework applicable to veterinary diagnostic imaging specialty practice.

The search identified competency frameworks for entry‐level veterinary graduates, including the American Association of Veterinary Medical Colleges (AAVMC) Competency‐Based Veterinary Education (CBVE) 2.0 Model [16] and the VetPro framework [17], as well as Day One competency frameworks for veterinary subdisciplines including clinical pathology [18], pharmacology [19], and health informatics [20]. A competency framework for veterinary emergency practice has been previously published [21]. An ACVR/European College of Veterinary Diagnostic Imaging (ECVDI) consensus statement addressing competency domains specific to radiology reporting was identified but does not constitute a comprehensive competency framework for the specialty [22]. No published, content‐validated competency framework defining the full scope of core abilities required for specialty‐level veterinary diagnostic imaging practice was identified, confirming the gap this study was designed to address.

Phase 1: Focus Group (Initial Competency Development)

Phase 1 employed a modified Delphi process with two iterative rounds to develop initial competencies. Each round included a structured virtual focus group meeting followed by an anonymous survey, allowing participants to reconsider their judgments in light of group feedback [9, 10, 11]. Over an 8‐week period, the focus group participated in 1‐ to 2‐h virtual meetings (Microsoft Teams, Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA).

One week prior to the first meeting, participants received a list of 29 example competencies created by the project lead (author J.B.) to illustrate an appropriate competency structure and breadth, and to calibrate participants’ conceptualization of the competencies, consistent with established Delphi methods for developing competency frameworks [10, 11, 23]. The focus group was instructed that these items were illustrative of structure and breadth only and were not prescriptive or required for inclusion. The initial competencies were informed by frameworks from human radiology, veterinary medicine, and other health professions, adapted to reflect the diagnostic imaging specialist role. Orientation materials included CBE research literature [6, 7] and the AAVMC CBVE 2.0 Model [16]. These materials established a shared understanding of terminology and structure, ensuring discussion focused on content generation rather than conceptual clarification. The orientation introduced CBE principles that emphasize demonstrating specific, observable abilities rather than time‐based training [6, 7]. Competencies were defined as broad, observable abilities integrating knowledge, skills, and professional behavior [6]. Domains were defined as overarching categories grouping related competencies [24].

A moderator, external to the focus group, facilitated discussions to maintain alignment with the study objectives and prevent conceptual drift. The moderator was an educational psychologist and veterinarian (author J.N.) with formal training in Delphi methodology, experience conducting consensus development studies, and expertise in CBE. The moderator intervened only to answer questions and clarify key distinctions between two construct types. Competencies were defined as observable and transferable abilities applicable across species and practice contexts, whereas context‐specific skills were defined as abilities dependent on species, settings, or organizational structures [6].

Round 1

During the first round, participants ideated the knowledge, skills, and behaviors considered essential for diagnostic imaging specialists, emphasizing broad, transferable competencies rather than narrow, context‐specific tasks. Competencies generated during the discussion were recorded and organized into an initial draft list, articulated using action verbs aligned with Bloom's Taxonomy [25] to ensure observable, measurable behaviors.

Within 1 week after the meeting, participants completed an anonymous survey administered via Smartsheet (Smartsheet Inc, Bellevue, WA), in which they rated the importance of each competency for inclusion in the framework using a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = Not Important to 5 = Very Important). Participants could also provide open‐text comments. A priori consensus was defined as an agreement threshold of 0.70, calculated as the proportion of participants rating a competency as Important (score of 4) or Very Important (score of 5). Agreement scores at or above this threshold indicated consensus regarding importance for inclusion in the framework, consistent with established Delphi methodology [9]. Because agreement values moved in increments of approximately 0.14 (with the 0.70 threshold corresponding to agreement by at least five of seven members), a single member's rating could shift a competency across the threshold. Individual Round 1 dispositions were therefore treated as provisional, and all below‐threshold competencies were re‐examined in Round 2 rather than eliminated outright.

Round 2

The Round 2 discussion focused on the competencies that did not meet the 0.70 consensus threshold in Round 1. The competencies that met the threshold in Round 1 were confirmed for inclusion, as consensus had been achieved and no further rating was required. For the below‐threshold competencies, the focus group conducted a detailed review to determine whether low agreement reflected technical issues, such as unclear wording, redundancy, or fragmented concepts, rather than genuine disagreement about importance. This iterative approach prevented premature elimination of potentially important competencies due to ambiguous phrasing or misinterpretation, ensuring that only competencies with substantive concerns were excluded before proceeding to comprehensive external workforce validation. The focus group identified opportunities to consolidate redundant competencies, revised ambiguous wording for clarity, and flagged fragmented concepts for potential removal. Following this discussion, focus group members completed a Round 2 survey indicating disposition (retain as written, retain with revision, or exclude) for each of the below‐threshold competencies. Agreement was calculated as the proportion selecting either retention option. The focus group then reviewed Round 2 survey results alongside discussion outcomes to make final disposition decisions.

Phase 2: Workforce Survey (Competency Refinement and Content Validation)

Phase 2 validated the content of the 52 competencies advancing from Phase 1 through a structured survey assessing perceived importance and clarity across a broader professional population. Participants were recruited through purposive sampling using two specialty‐focused channels: a professional listserv and an annual specialty conference. Eligible respondents were veterinarians with direct knowledge of and extensive experience with the diagnostic imaging specialist role through close professional collaboration or diagnostic imaging training. This included board‐certified diagnostic imaging specialists (diplomates of the ACVR and ECVDI), diagnostic imaging residents, and other veterinarians who work closely with diagnostic imaging specialists in clinical or educational settings. All participants accessed the same survey instrument, and inclusion criteria were reiterated at the beginning of the survey to confirm eligibility.

Respondents provided demographic information, including professional role, years of experience, species worked with, and practice environment. For each competency, respondents first rated importance on a 5‐point Likert scale, then indicated whether the wording was clear (Yes/No). Respondents indicating unclear wording were prompted to provide suggested revisions. Optional open‐ended fields allowed additional qualitative feedback on phrasing, scope, or perceived overlap. Respondents could suggest additional competencies they believed were missing.

The same 0.70 consensus threshold applied in Phase 1 was used, with consensus calculated as the proportion rating a competency as Important (4) or Very Important (5). Competencies meeting this threshold were considered validated; those below were flagged for Phase 3 review. Following survey development and recruitment coordination, the survey opened June 12, 2025, for listserv participants and remained open through November 3, 2025, to capture participants reached through the 2025 Annual Scientific Conference.

Respondents provided open‐text qualitative feedback through three channels: (1) competency‐specific comment fields appearing when respondents identified unclear wording, allowing for suggested revisions and additional feedback, (2) a field for suggesting additional competencies, and (3) a general comments field. These responses captured feedback on clarity of wording, perceived redundancy, and potential gaps. Quantitative ratings and qualitative feedback were systematically reviewed and presented to the Phase 1 focus group during Phase 3 for final refinement.

Phase 3: Finalizing the Competency Framework

Phase 3 finalized the framework by integrating quantitative and qualitative data from Phase 2. Survey results, including importance ratings and open‐text feedback related to wording, scope, and perceived overlap, were analyzed and compiled by the research team over 2 weeks, then reviewed by the original focus group during a structured virtual meeting.

The focus group evaluated each competency using five decision criteria: (1) retain as written, (2) retain with minor or substantive revision for clarity or scope, (3) restructure as an illustrative subcompetency when content represented supporting detail rather than a distinct core ability, (4) remove due to redundancy when content was adequately addressed by other competencies, or (5) exclude when the competency did not represent a core, transferable ability required for diagnostic imaging specialty practice. Decisions were reached through facilitated group discussion using the same principles applied in Phase 1. Competencies were not removed or excluded solely for failing to meet the Phase 2 consensus threshold; rather, the focus group reviewed quantitative ratings and qualitative feedback to determine whether below‐threshold competencies reflected genuine disagreement about importance or wording issues that could be resolved through revision. This approach ensured final decisions were informed by both statistical consensus and expert judgment.

Discussion centered on resolving discrepancies between importance ratings and qualitative comments and clarifying conceptual boundaries between competencies. The focus group also identified and addressed potential gaps by proposing new competencies where essential abilities were absent from the draft framework, and confirmed that retained competencies reflected broad, cross‐task abilities rather than context‐specific skills. The project lead documented decisions and applied agreed‐upon revisions to produce a refined competency list, which the focus group reviewed to confirm accurate implementation.

The refined competency list with proposed dispositions was redistributed to the focus group for a final anonymous confirmation survey via an Excel workbook (Microsoft Corporation). The workbook contained (1) a summary of Phase 2 quantitative ratings and qualitative feedback with documented rationale for proposed decisions and (2) the revised competency list for final confirmation. Focus group members indicated whether they agreed with the proposed disposition for each competency based on the five decision criteria described above using a dichotomous response (Yes/No). Phase 3 served to confirm final decisions about the framework (removals, exclusions, and restructuring) rather than to screen candidate competencies for a subsequent round. Because these were final determinations rather than provisional ones, a more stringent threshold (≥86%, 6 of 7) was applied to ensure robust agreement before finalization. Following confirmation, competencies were reviewed to ensure alignment with CBE principles. Minor wording revisions removed language describing internal cognitive states (e.g., “knowledge of”) and developmental descriptors (e.g., “proficiently”) more appropriately defined within milestone frameworks [6, 26]. These technical adjustments did not alter competency content or scope.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative survey data were analyzed descriptively using Microsoft Excel 2021 (Microsoft Corporation). Respondent demographic characteristics from Phase 2 were summarized descriptively using frequencies and proportions. A proportion‐based consensus approach was applied with an a priori threshold of ≥0.70 across all phases [9, 12]. For importance ratings (Phase 1 Round 1 and Phase 2), the proportion of respondents rating each competency as Important or Very Important (scores of 4 or 5 on a 5‐point Likert scale) was calculated. For Phase 1 Round 2 disposition votes, agreement was calculated as the proportion selecting either retention option. For Phase 2 clarity ratings, the proportion of Yes responses to the dichotomous clarity question was calculated to identify competencies requiring wording revision prior to Phase 3 review. For Phase 3 confirmation votes, the proportion of affirmative responses was calculated for each proposed disposition, with ≥86% agreement (at least 6 of 7 focus group members) required for confirmation. To assess whether there was evidence that the extended survey window introduced temporal differences among participants, respondent‐level mean importance ratings were compared across calendar months using the Kruskal–Wallis test. The stability of the competency‐level consensus pattern over time was evaluated using Spearman rank correlations between the earliest month's agreement proportions and those of each subsequent month. Two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. These analyses were performed in Python using the SciPy library.

Open‐text qualitative data from Phase 1 surveys and from three Phase 2 feedback channels (competency‐specific comments, additional competency suggestions, and general comments) were analyzed using descriptive content analysis [27]. A single coder organized and grouped the comments into emergent categories; the full research team then independently reviewed the coded data and met to confirm the categories and resolve discrepancies through discussion until consensus was reached. No formal interrater reliability statistic was computed for the qualitative coding, consistent with this descriptive approach. Responses were reviewed iteratively, categorized based on conceptual similarity, and summarized using frequency counts and proportions to characterize response patterns descriptively. No inferential statistics were applied to the qualitative data.

Delphi Round 1 discussion produced 58 potential competencies, articulated using action verbs aligned with Bloom's Taxonomy [25] (e.g., “Implements and adapts a range of diagnostic techniques and interventions to address complex clinical scenarios”). Following the discussion, all seven focus group members completed an anonymous survey rating the importance of each competency. Of the 58 competencies evaluated, 35 met the 0.70 consensus threshold for inclusion, while 23 did not and were flagged for re‐evaluation in Round 2.

Round 2 discussion and survey resulted in the removal of six competencies and the retention of 17, producing a final draft of 52 competencies that advanced to Phase 2 workforce validation.

Importance Ratings

Quantitative analysis indicated that 36 of 52 competencies (69.2%) met or exceeded the 0.70 consensus threshold for importance, while 16 competencies (30.8%) fell below the threshold and were flagged for Phase 3 review. Agreement values for all competencies are presented in Table 3. Most respondents (76 of 80; 95%) completed the survey before September, well in advance of the October professional conference, with only four (5%) responding during the conference period. Respondent‐level importance ratings did not differ significantly across months (Kruskal–Wallis H = 5.18, df = 3, p = 0.16), and the competency‐level consensus pattern was strongly and significantly correlated between the earliest month and each subsequent month (Spearman's ρ = 0.76, 0.81, and 0.69 for July, August, and October, respectively; all p < 0.001). These results indicate no evidence of a history effect and are consistent with a homogeneous sample across the collection window, though the small number of later‐window respondents limits the power to detect subtle temporal differences.

Suggestions for Additional Competencies

Twenty respondents (25.0%) suggested additional competencies. Analysis identified four patterns. Most suggestions (50.0%) described granular skills at the subcompetency or milestone level, while 30.0% described content covered by existing competencies. Three suggestions (15.0%) described content specific to species, settings, or practice contexts rather than universal specialist abilities, and one (5.0%) suggested a competency addressing economic considerations in diagnostic planning. Notably, only one of the 20 suggestions (5.0%) represented a genuine gap not already captured by the existing framework, supporting the completeness of the validated competency set.

General Comments

Twenty‐four respondents (30.0%) provided general comments. Suggestions for greater granularity, such as specific tasks or numerical thresholds, were provided by 29.2% of respondents. Additional comments addressed framework structure (12.5%), scope appropriateness (20.8%), and survey design (12.5%). One‐quarter (25.0%) provided positive feedback regarding the framework development effort.

Clarity

Competency numbers in this paragraph correspond to the external survey numbering used in Tables 3 and 5. Respondents rated each competency for clarity, resulting in 4160 total ratings (52 competencies × 80 respondents). Overall, 91.4% of ratings (n = 3802) indicated the competency was clearly written; 356 comments suggesting clarification were provided across all competencies. Addition of New Competency

Based on convergent qualitative feedback from multiple Phase 2 respondents identifying a gap in the integration of welfare and economic considerations into diagnostic planning, the focus group proposed adding one new competency, initially worded “Integrates financial literacy into clinical decision‐making and recommendations” and later refined as too narrow for that gap.

Final Confirmation

The proposed dispositions were distributed to focus group members via an anonymous confirmation survey. Quantitative results indicated that all proposed dispositions achieved the required ≥86% agreement threshold (at least 6 of 7 members), confirming consensus on the framework structure.

Post‐Confirmation Review and Revision

Despite quantitative consensus, qualitative comments submitted during the confirmation survey identified several areas of persisting concern related to redundancy, scope, and structural phrasing consistency. The project lead (author J.B.) reviewed these comments and implemented targeted revisions. These revisions addressed editorial and structural refinements, including removal of redundant competencies, scope clarification, and phrasing consistency across modality‐specific competencies rather than substantive disagreements about competency content or professional expectations and therefore did not alter the outcomes established through the consensus process. Changes to disposition counts and competency wording resulting from these revisions are documented in Table 5.

The following revisions were implemented based on confirmation survey feedback. First, the newly proposed competency “Integrates financial literacy into clinical decision‐making and recommendations” was revised to better reflect the focus group's intent. The focus group determined that the initial “financial literacy” wording was too narrow and not a core diagnostic imaging competency, as it implied general knowledge of prices and practice management. In discussion, members instead identified a distinct and relevant dimension: using awareness of the costs of procedures and treatments to help clients select a clinically appropriate and financially feasible diagnostic pathway. The wording was therefore redirected from knowing prices toward incorporating welfare and economic considerations into diagnostic planning, consistent with the spectrum‐of‐care concept in veterinary medicine and with the welfare‐and‐resource gap identified in the Phase 2 feedback (Table 4). The competency was revised to: “Incorporates animal welfare and economic considerations into diagnostic planning.”

Second, the competency “Analyzes and applies common and advanced veterinary imaging techniques to diagnose diverse clinical conditions effectively” was revised to “Synthesizes and applies radiography principles and techniques to effectively diagnose a range of clinical conditions” to specify the modality scope and align wording with other modality‐specific competencies in the framework.

Third, two competencies were removed. The competency “Applies comprehensive understanding of the physics, principles, and practice of radiography in veterinary imaging” was removed as it did not represent an observable, transferable ability independent of the retained competency “Assesses and applies theoretical and practical physics principles to optimize medical imaging techniques and protocols.” The competency “Analyzes diagnostic imaging findings accurately and communicates results effectively with appropriate professional lexicon” was removed as its content was fully addressed by the retained competency “Produces clear, meaningful, and detailed radiology reports using appropriate professional lexicon when communicating findings to veterinary healthcare teams.”

Fourth, “Produces clear, meaningful, and detailed radiology reports that effectively communicate findings to veterinary healthcare teams” was further edited to “Produces clear, meaningful, and detailed radiology reports using appropriate professional lexicon when communicating findings to veterinary healthcare teams.”

Finally, the six competencies referring to individual imaging modalities were revised to adopt consistent parallel language (Synthesizes and applies [modality‐specific] principles and techniques to effectively diagnose a range of clinical conditions), ensuring structural uniformity across modality‐specific competencies in the final framework.

These post‐confirmation revisions resulted in the following updated disposition counts: retained as written (n = 9), retained with revision (n = 21, including the newly added competency), restructured as illustrative subcompetency (n = 9), removed due to redundancy (n = 10), and excluded (n = 4). These dispositions total 53, reflecting the 52 validated competencies plus the one newly added competency. The nine competencies retained as written and the 21 retained with revision together constitute the 30 core competencies of the final framework, while the remaining 23 (nine restructured as illustrative subcompetencies, 10 removed for redundancy, and four excluded) account for the difference. Final Framework

Following the post‐confirmation editorial review, seven competencies underwent additional minor wording revisions to ensure alignment with CBE principles [6]. Six competencies were revised to remove language describing internal cognitive states that are not directly observable or measurable, including “knowledge of,” “understanding of,” and “comprehensive knowledge” (e.g., “Applies comprehensive knowledge of normal anatomy to diagnose and treat veterinary patients” was revised to “Interprets diagnostic images using detailed anatomical knowledge to differentiate normal structures from pathological findings,” and “Utilizes knowledge of radiation biology” was revised to “Applies radiation biology”). One competency was revised to remove the modifier “proficiently,” as developmental descriptors indicating progression toward competency attainment are appropriately defined within milestone frameworks rather than within competency statements themselves [6, 26]. These revisions constituted minor technical adjustments to phrasing and did not alter the substantive content, scope, or professional expectations established through the three‐phase validation process. Discussion

The purpose of this study was to develop and content‐validate diagnostic imaging competencies that define the core abilities required for specialty‐level practice. Using a three‐phase modified Delphi approach that combines expert consensus with workforce validation, this study developed VIEW, a diagnostic imaging competency framework comprising 30 core competencies and six domains. By articulating diagnostic imaging competencies as integrated, observable abilities rather than variable modality‐specific tasks or exposure‐based requirements, VIEW addresses longstanding variability in training expectations and provides a shared foundation for competency‐based educational approaches.

Diagnostic imaging residency training, like specialty training across the health professions, is necessarily delivered within diverse clinical environments that vary in available imaging modalities, patient populations, referral patterns, and institutional resources. This contextual variability is an inherent and appropriate feature of specialty education, reflecting differences in practice settings rather than deficiencies in training quality. Existing standards, including case‐volume requirements and modality‐exposure expectations, provide important structure for residency programs and define the scope of clinical experience [1]. However, many of the abilities that distinguish readiness for independent specialty‐level diagnostic imaging practice, such as clinical judgment, diagnostic reasoning, communication, and professional decision‐making, are often developed and evaluated implicitly within these experiences [2, 3]. VIEW provides a mechanism to make such expectations explicit by articulating the core abilities that diagnostic imaging specialists should demonstrate, while preserving program autonomy in how those abilities are taught, practiced, and assessed within individual programs. By focusing on outcomes rather than prescribing educational pathways, the framework supports alignment around what residents must ultimately be able to do, without constraining the range of training models that characterize diagnostic imaging residency education.

VIEW (Table 6) provides a structured representation of the professional abilities required in diagnostic imaging, articulated to support curriculum development, assessment design, and professional development planning. Importantly, VIEW moves beyond task enumeration to describe the integrated knowledge, skills, and behaviors required to perform diagnostic imaging activities at the specialist level. By defining competencies as integrated abilities rather than isolated tasks, the framework enables translation of professional expectations into assessable outcomes and aligns with contemporary approaches to CBE across the health professions [6, 7]. By establishing consensus on both the content and structure of diagnostic imaging competencies, this study addresses a critical gap between conceptual competency statements and their practical implementation within training and assessment systems.

Interpretation of Competency Scope

A recurring theme in qualitative feedback was that competencies were “too broad” or “too vague,” with suggestions to specify particular modalities, techniques, species, or clinical contexts. This feedback aligns with patterns observed in other competency framework development studies [23, 28] and may reflect differences in familiarity with hierarchical CBE models rather than disagreement with framework content [6, 7]. Importantly, while 36 of 52 competencies (69.2%) met the importance threshold, a substantial minority (30.8%) did not, and several of these were retained or restructured rather than discarded; final inclusion was therefore driven by expert judgment integrating quantitative ratings and qualitative feedback rather than by survey consensus alone. Further supporting the framework's comprehensiveness, only one of the 20 additional‐competency suggestions (5.0%) reflected a genuine gap not already captured by VIEW, indicating that the validated set encompassed the core abilities identified by the broader workforce.

In VIEW, detail is not absent; it is architecturally structured to support practical implementation. Competencies establish the broad abilities expected of all diagnostic imaging specialists. Subcompetencies provide illustrative examples of how a competency might be demonstrated, and programs can develop additional subcompetencies tailored to their specific training contexts, available modalities, and patient populations [16]. For example, a program emphasizing equine imaging might define subcompetencies related to standing radiography and ultrasound‐guided procedures, while a program with a focus on advanced CT and MRI capabilities might emphasize cross‐sectional interpretation. This hierarchical structure ensures that the granular detail practitioners seek (specific modalities, techniques, species, and procedural expectations) is captured at the level where it can be meaningfully adapted to each program's unique training environment, while the competency framework itself provides a shared foundation for defining what all residents must ultimately be able to do upon completion of the program. To further operationalize this model for diagnostic imaging residency training, additional competency‐based components could be developed to support resident assessment. Milestones would define the expected developmental progression within each competency, providing a structured basis for feedback across the program, while entrustable professional activities (EPAs) would translate competencies into discrete, observable workplace activities, assessed along an entrustment scale that reflects the degree of supervision a resident requires [29].

Alignment With Health Professions Education Frameworks

VIEW's alignment with two foundational competency frameworks was evaluated: the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) core competencies (used in human medical specialty education) and the AAVMC CBVE Competency Framework (used in veterinary medical education) [16]. These alignments demonstrate VIEW's structural consistency with established educational models and facilitate integration across health professions education contexts.

The ACGME defines six core competencies that describe physician abilities shared across medical specialties: Patient Care, Medical Knowledge, Practice‐Based Learning and Improvement, Interpersonal and Communication Skills, Professionalism, and Systems‐Based Practice [30, 31]. These broad competencies provide the high‐level framework required for accreditation across all residency programs. Individual specialties then develop discipline‐specific competencies that operationalize how these domains manifest in their clinical context, effectively functioning as subcompetencies within the broader ACGME structure. For example, a diagnostic imaging residency program demonstrates how its curriculum addresses “Patient Care” through specialty‐specific competencies related to image interpretation, procedural skills, and diagnostic reasoning, while an internal medicine program addresses the same domain through competencies related to patient management and clinical decision‐making. This hierarchical approach allows accreditation standards to remain consistent across specialties while preserving the flexibility for each discipline to define what competent performance looks like in specialty practice. Table 7 demonstrates how VIEW competencies align with ACGME core competency domains, illustrating structural consistency between veterinary diagnostic imaging and established medical education frameworks. Notably, the largest cluster of VIEW competencies falls under Patient Care (n = 12), reflecting the procedural and interpretive demands that define diagnostic imaging specialty practice, while the distribution across the remaining five domains confirms that VIEW addresses the full breadth of professional competence expected of a specialist, not only technical proficiency. The size of this Patient Care cluster also reflects a structural choice; seven of the 12 competencies mapped to Patient Care are specific to modality or delivery (the six modality competencies plus telemedicine/teleradiology), a consequence of treating each imaging modality as a separate competency, so the cluster size reflects this organizational decision in addition to the specialty's procedural and interpretive emphasis.

Prior to Phase 1, the focus group reviewed the AAVMC CBVE 2.0 Model [16]. The VIEW framework can be conceptualized as a specialty‐level framework that maps onto the AAVMC CBVE Competency Framework, which establishes foundational “Day One” competencies for veterinary graduates [16]. Where CBVE defines the abilities expected of entry‐level veterinary graduates, VIEW defines the abilities of diagnostic imaging specialists who have achieved advanced expertise through focused residency training. In this sense, VIEW competencies represent the progression of foundational Day One competencies to the advanced level expected after specialty training. VIEW does not represent a parallel or competing framework, but rather operationalizes, at the specialty level, competencies that correspond to CBVE's domains, within the context of diagnostic imaging practice.

The mapping revealed that VIEW fully maps to six of the nine CBVE domains and partially maps to a seventh (Domain 8: Financial and Practice Management), addressing its workplace safety and adverse event dimensions while reflecting that financial management and regulatory compliance represent broad veterinary practice competencies that were not identified as core specialist abilities through the Delphi process.

There were two unrepresented domains, Animal Population Care and Management (Domain 3) and Public Health (Domain 4). We interpreted their absence as reflecting the framework's targeted scope rather than a gap in its design. We acknowledge, however, that population‐level and zoonotic considerations can arise in certain diagnostic imaging contexts, such as zoological, wildlife, and production‐animal imaging, although these were not identified as core, transferable specialist competencies through the Delphi process.

Notably, VIEW integrates communication and collaboration within a single domain rather than treating them as separate constructs, reflecting an emergent finding of the Delphi process. Diagnostic imaging specialists identified these abilities as deeply interdependent in practice, enacted primarily through the production of diagnostic reports, clinical consultation, and multidisciplinary care coordination. The graduate performs preventive, diagnostic, medical, and surgical procedures for the health, wellness, and treatment of animals, appropriate to the context and life stage.

10. Delivers diagnostic imaging services through telemedicine and teleradiology platforms while maintaining quality and communication standards.

11. Synthesizes and applies radiography principles and techniques to effectively diagnose a range of clinical conditions.

12. Synthesizes and applies ultrasound principles and techniques to effectively diagnose a range of clinical conditions.

13. Synthesizes and applies CT principles and techniques to effectively diagnose a range of clinical conditions.

14. Synthesizes and applies MRI principles and techniques to effectively diagnose a range of clinical conditions.

15. Synthesizes and applies fluoroscopy principles and techniques to effectively diagnose a range of clinical conditions.

16. Synthesizes and applies nuclear medicine principles and techniques to effectively diagnose a range of clinical conditions.

Domain 3: Animal Population Care and Management

The graduate designs and implements programs in herd/flock health, disease prevention, and disease control to improve the health, welfare, and productivity of animal populations.

Not represented: The VIEW framework was derived through a modified Delphi study engaging diagnostic imaging specialists and informed by workforce needs, evidence‐based best practices, and the specific competency demands of residency‐trained specialists. Herd and flock health, population‐level disease management, and biosecurity programming fall outside the clinical scope of diagnostic imaging specialty practice and were not identified as core abilities in any phase of the Delphi process. The absence of this domain reflects the precision of the VIEW framework rather than a limitation—a specialty framework that included population medicine competencies without empirical support from the workforce would lack content validity.

Domain 4: Public Health

The graduate responds to issues at the interface of animals, humans, and the environment, utilizing a global perspective and sensitivity to local cultures.

Not represented: The VIEW framework was derived through a modified Delphi study grounded in the actual competency demands of diagnostic imaging specialists. Public health responsibilities, including zoonotic disease surveillance, environmental health, and antimicrobial stewardship, fall outside the defined scope of diagnostic imaging specialty practice and were not identified as core abilities by participants across any phase of the Delphi process. This is consistent with the targeted nature of specialty competency frameworks, which are designed to define the advanced, discipline‐specific abilities that distinguish specialists from generalist graduates rather than to reproduce the full breadth of the foundational CBVE framework.

Domain 5: Communication

The graduate communicates effectively with diverse clients, colleagues, other healthcare professionals and the public to promote animal, human, and environmental health and well‐being.

17. Collaborates and communicates with multidisciplinary teams to optimize patient care and management.

18. Produces clear, meaningful, and detailed radiology reports using appropriate professional lexicon when communicating findings to veterinary healthcare teams.

19. Effectively advises clinicians on the necessity and appropriateness of diagnostics or procedures, distinguishing between essential tasks and optional ones.

Domain 6: Collaboration

The graduate collaborates with diverse colleagues, clients, and other stakeholders and demonstrates skills as a leader and interprofessional team member to improve outcomes and reduce error.

17. Collaborates and communicates with multidisciplinary teams to optimize patient care and management.

VIEW combines communication and collaboration within a single domain (Domain 3: Communication and Collaboration), reflecting the deeply integrated nature of these abilities in diagnostic imaging specialty practice, where interprofessional collaboration is enacted primarily through communication acts: consulting with clinicians, producing diagnostic reports, advising on imaging appropriateness, and coordinating multidisciplinary care. This integration was an emergent finding of the Delphi process and is consistent with the workforce‐informed design of VIEW. C17, which addresses direct collaboration with multidisciplinary teams, is the primary competency in this domain; however, C18 and C19 also carry significant collaborative dimensions through their emphasis on communication that directly shapes clinical decision‐making and team‐based care. The apparent sparseness of this domain relative to CBVE Domain 6 reflects a deliberate structural choice rather than a gap in collaborative competency coverage.

Domain 7: Professionalism and Professional Identity

The graduate demonstrates behaviors expected of the veterinarian, including ethical reasoning, reflective practice, self‐regulation, professional development, and personal well‐being.

20. Demonstrates and maintains high ethical standards in imaging activities, patient care, and professional interactions.

21. Demonstrates an ongoing commitment to professional growth and continuous pursuit of knowledge of best practices through current literature review, journal clubs, continuing education, research, and new technologies.

22. Balances efficiency with accuracy and quality assurance, ensuring diagnostic excellence.

23. Supports an inclusive work environment that prioritizes mental health, team wellness, and resilience.

Domain 8: Financial and Practice Management

The graduate utilizes business acumen to manage professional and personal decisions, complies with legal and regulatory requirements, and ensures safety of the workplace.

Partially represented: VIEW addresses the workplace safety and adverse event management dimensions of this domain (CBVE 8.3), which align directly with the radiation safety, imaging safety protocols, and patient safety obligations of diagnostic imaging specialists. The financial management (CBVE 8.1) and regulatory compliance (CBVE 8.2) dimensions of this domain reflect broad veterinary practice competencies that are not specific to specialty training and were not identified by the Delphi panel as core abilities of diagnostic imaging specialists.

24. Engages in continuous improvement initiatives to refine radiology services while minimizing patient and team stress.

25. Ensures that all appropriate safety measures are in place to protect the health and well‐being of veterinary healthcare team members and patients during imaging activities.

26. Applies radiation biology to enhance the effectiveness and safety of imaging activities.

27. Takes ownership and responds effectively to adverse events during veterinary imaging procedures.

Domain 9: Scholarship

The graduate demonstrates the systematic identification, evaluation, integration, and adaptation of evidence and experience to formulate questions and solutions, and educate others.

28. Applies findings from relevant, high‐quality research to clinical decision‐making.

29. Assesses the design, methodology, and outcomes of research studies to determine validity, reliability, and applicability to practice.

30. Educates peers, trainees, and team members in clinical skills, critical thinking, and best practices in imaging.

This nested structure with broad accreditation domains at the top, specialty competencies in the middle, and program‐specific subcompetencies, milestones, and EPAs at the implementation level mirrors competency framework models across health professions education [6, 7]. The structure preserves vertical integration from veterinary school through residency training and into specialty practice, ensuring that specialty‐level expectations build upon foundational competencies while allowing for the increased complexity, autonomy, and integrative reasoning required in advanced practice. The convergence of VIEW's structure with two independently developed frameworks, one from human medical education and one from veterinary education, provides convergent structural support for VIEW's content, suggesting that the competency domains identified through the Delphi process reflect genuine and transferable patterns of specialist professional practice rather than idiosyncratic choices of the study team. This alignment supports curriculum coherence across educational stages, facilitates cross‐disciplinary communication about CBE, and supports potential future harmonization of assessment approaches across health professions.

Implications for Training and Practice

VIEW's impact extends beyond residency training, transforming how the diagnostic imaging profession conceptualizes competence. The framework provides residency programs with explicit outcome expectations for curriculum design and assessment, replacing reliance on surrogate indicators like case volume with direct evidence of integrated abilities. Professional organizations can anchor accreditation standards and certification requirements in workforce‐informed competencies rather than program‐specific conventions. Employers gain standardized language to define position expectations, evaluate performance, and structure continuing professional development. Critically, VIEW achieves this alignment without constraining educational approaches. Programs retain autonomy in how they develop, teach, and assess competencies within their unique clinical contexts.

This shared foundation facilitates clearer communication regarding scope of practice, reduces variability in the preparedness of residents completing training, and enables targeted professional development through evidence‐based assessment strategies [6, 7]. While this study did not evaluate implementation outcomes, VIEW lays the groundwork for future research on how competency‐based approaches affect training effectiveness, assessment quality, and clinical performance across diverse practice settings.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. The modified Delphi approach remains dependent on subjective expert judgments. The Phase 1 expert focus group was appropriately sized for Delphi methodology, with findings validated through a broader workforce survey (n = 80). Purposive sampling ensured relevant expertise but limits statistical representativeness. Phase 2 participation was voluntary and may reflect perspectives of individuals interested in CBE. In addition, although providing an initial list of example competencies is an established component of competency framework development [10, 11, 23], it may also introduce some anchoring on early ideation. We considered this an accepted methodological trade‐off, as an illustrative initial list orients participants to the intended structure and breadth and grounds discussion in existing evidence. Any residual anchoring was mitigated by presenting the list as illustrative rather than prescriptive, encouraging open competency generation, and re‐examining below‐threshold competencies in Round 2. Notably, the focus group expanded the list from 29 to 58 competencies in Round 1, indicating it informed rather than constrained their ideation.

Qualitative comments were initially organized and coded by a single coder, which may introduce individual interpretive bias, although the full research team subsequently reviewed the coded data and confirmed categories by consensus to mitigate this risk.

Validation reflects consensus‐based content validity rather than psychometric validation, and consensus thresholds represent methodological conventions. VIEW's generalizability to subspecialty areas or emerging modalities was not evaluated. This study focused on framework development and validation but did not evaluate implementation or educational impact.

These limitations are inherent to Delphi methodology, which remains best methodological practice and the most widely adopted approach for developing consensus‐driven competency frameworks across health professions [9, 10, 11, 23].

Future Directions

Future research should examine how VIEW can be used to develop competency‐based residency training programs, including the development of additional subcompetencies mapped to competencies and the development of milestones and EPAs to enhance resident assessment and promote clinical outcomes. Studies examining the relationship between competency attainment and clinical performance indicators would provide predictive validity and support the framework's use in high‐stakes decisions about trainee progression and certification. A psychometric validation study is a logical next step, using VIEW's content‐validated competencies as the foundation for mapping to EPAs and evaluating entrustment‐based assessment. The methodology employed in this study may also serve as a model for developing competency frameworks in other veterinary specialties that lack workforce‐informed outcome expectations.

Conclusion

This study developed VIEW, a workforce‐informed, competency‐based framework comprising 30 core competencies that define essential abilities required for competent specialty practice in veterinary diagnostic imaging. By establishing a shared foundation for curriculum design and assessment while preserving flexibility in educational delivery, VIEW addresses variability across programs without constraining program‐specific approaches. This work advances CBE in veterinary medicine and provides a foundation for future efforts to strengthen diagnostic imaging training, assessment, and professional standardization.
