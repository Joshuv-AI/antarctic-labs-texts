An algebraic theory of Łojasiewicz exponents
Tài Huy Hà

Abstract
. We develop a unified algebraic and valuative theory of Łojasiewicz exponents for pairs of graded families and filtrations of ideals. Within this framework, local Łojasiewicz exponents, gradient exponents, and exponents at infinity are all realized as asymptotic containment thresholds between filtrations, governed by integral closure. This reformulation shows that Łojasiewicz exponents are fundamentally valuative optimization problems. The central structural contribution of the paper is a finite–max principle. Under verifiable algebraic hypotheses, the a priori infinite valuative supremum bounding the Łojasiewicz exponent reduces to a finite maximum, and computes the Łojasiewicz exponent precisely. We identify two complementary mechanisms leading to this phenomenon: finite testing arising from normalized blowups and Noetherian Rees algebras, and attainment via compactness of normalized valuation spaces under linear boundedness assumptions. This finite–max framework yields strong structural consequences. We prove rigidity results showing that common extremal valuations force equality of Łojasiewicz ratios, and we establish stratification and stability phenomena for Łojasiewicz exponents in families, including fractional linearity and wall-chamber behavior along natural one–parameter deformations. The theory recovers and explains classical results in toric and Newton–polyhedral settings, particularly, for Newton nondegenerate case, where the Łojasiewicz exponent is computed by finitely many toric divisorial valuations corresponding to facet data. Finally, we illustrate why the hypotheses underlying the finite–max principle are essential, delineating the precise scope of the theory.

Part I
Motivations, Goals and Statement of Main Results

We start the paper by describing motivation, conceptual background, and an overview of the main results.

1.
Introduction

The Łojasiewicz exponent is a fundamental invariant measuring the rate at which one
analytic object dominates another near a singular locus. More precisely, given analytic function germs

f_{1},\dots,f_{s},\;g_{1},\dots,g_{t}\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^{d},0},

the classical Łojasiewicz inequality
[
39
]
asserts that there exist constants
C>0
,
\theta>0
,
and a neighborhood
U
of the origin such that

\max_{1\leq j\leq t}|g_{j}(x)|^{\theta}\;\leq\;C\max_{1\leq i\leq s}|f_{i}(x)|\quad\text{for all }x\in U.

The infimum of such exponents
\theta
is called the (analytic) Łojasiewicz exponent of

(f_{1},\dots,f_{s})
with respect to
(g_{1},\dots,g_{t})
.

Introduced originally by Łojasiewicz in the study of real and complex analytic sets,
this exponent captures subtle asymptotic information about singularities: it quantifies
relative vanishing rates, governs gradient inequalities for isolated critical points,
controls stability and equisingularity phenomena, and plays a central role in the study
of global polynomial mappings and optimization problems. Several important variants
arise naturally, including the
gradient Łojasiewicz exponent
of hypersurfaces with isolated singularities (cf.
[
8
,
13
,
20
,
37
]
)
and the
Łojasiewicz exponent at infinity
for polynomial
mappings (cf.
[
14
,
23
,
53
,
54
]
). Despite their close conceptual relationship, these invariants have traditionally
been studied using markedly different techniques, ranging from analytic arc methods and
curve selection to Newton polyhedra and projective compactifications.

A key unifying insight already appears in the classical work of
Lejeune-Jalabert and Teissier
[
36
]
. They showed that analytic
Łojasiewicz inequalities are equivalent to integral–closure containments between powers
of the corresponding ideals. Particularly, if

\mathfrak{a}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{s})
and
\mathfrak{b}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{t})
, then inequalities of the
form above hold if and only if there exist integers
p,q
such that

\mathfrak{b}^{q}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{p}}.

This result places the analytic Łojasiewicz exponent squarely within the framework of
ideal containment problems and reveals its intrinsic valuative nature; see also
[
7
,
52
]
. This viewpoint has been generalized by Bivià-Ausina
[
1
]
, Bivià-Ausina–Encinas
[
2
,
3
]
and Bivià-Ausina–Fukui
[
4
]
in their studies of Łojasiewicz exponent of ideals via integral closures and mixed multiplicites.

Independently, ideal containment problems have played a central role in commutative
algebra and algebraic geometry; notably, the uniform symbolic–ordinary containment theorems of
Ein–Lazarsfeld–Smith
[
15
]
and Hochster–Huneke
[
27
]
,
together with generalization by Ma–Schwede
[
40
]
. Motivated by the
fact that such containments are often not sharp, Harbourne and Huneke

[
25
]
proposed improved containment conjectures, leading to a large body of work during the last fifteen years; particularly, the introduction of numerical invariants measuring failure of containment, namely, the
resurgence
, introduced by Bocci–Harbourne
[
6
]
and further developed by Guardo–Harbourne–Van Tuyl
[
19
]
. Morerecently, these ideas have been extended to
graded families and filtrations of ideals
, allowing symbolic powers, integral closures, and other asymptotic constructions
to be treated in a unified way; see
[
21
]
.

The purpose of this paper is to bring these two threads together.
We develop a unified algebraic and valuative theory of Łojasiewicz
exponents for pairs of graded filtrations of ideals, in which local,
gradient, and at–infinity exponents all arise as
asymptotic
containment thresholds
. This reformulation places classical
Łojasiewicz inequalities into a single containment framework and
reveals that Łojasiewicz exponents are governed by valuative
optimization problems.
In general, Łojasiewicz exponent is bounded by a supremum of asymptotic valuative
ratios associated to the underlying filtrations. While such variational
descriptions are a priori infinite, a central theme of this paper is
that, under verifiable algebraic or geometric hypotheses, this
supremum is in fact a maximum attained by finitely many
valuations and computes the Łojasiewicz exponent. Identifying when this finite–max phenomenon occurs and
exploiting structural consequences, including rigidity, stability, stratification and wall-chamber behaviors, is the main objective of this paper.

2.
Goals and statement of main results

This paper develops a unified algebraic and valuative theory of Łojasiewicz exponents. It has two overarching aims.
The first aim is
conceptual
:
to introduce a general notion of algebraic Łojasiewicz exponent attached to a pair of graded filtrations of ideals and show that all classical analytic Łojasiewicz exponents (local, gradient, and at infinity) arise uniformly as instances of this invariant. From this perspective, Łojasiewicz exponents are interpreted as
asymptotic containment thresholds
between filtrations, placing them within the framework of
integral closure
and
ideal containment
theory.
The second aim is
structural
:
to show that valuations are the computational mechanism governing these thresholds, and
to isolate verifiable hypotheses under which the resulting valuative optimization problems
reduce to finite maxima over explicit candidate sets.
This
finite–max
philosophy underlies the rigidity, stability, and polyhedral phenomena
developed later in the paper.

2.1.
From analytic inequalities to algebraic thresholds

After fixing analytic conventions in Section
3
, we introduce in
Section
4
the algebraic Łojasiewicz exponent

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
attached to a pair of filtrations

{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
and
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
.
By definition,
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
records the smallest asymptotic slope at which the
containments

\mathfrak{b}_{qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}\quad\text{hold for }t\gg 1.

The key point is that, for families of ordinary powers of two ideals, this algebraic invariant exactly recovers the analytic one (
Theorems

4.5
and
4.9
).
Consequently, local, gradient, and at–infinity Łojasiewicz exponents all arise as
instances of
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
, distinguished only by the choice of filtrations, by the class of valuations allowed to appear, and possibly by inverting the invariant.

2.2.
Valuative formulas and the finite–max principle

A central feature of the algebraic Łojasiewicz exponent

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
is that it admits a valuative description (
Section

5
).
Using the valuative criterion for integral closure, we show that

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
is bounded below by a supremum of ratios

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\;\geq\;\sup_{v}\frac{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})},

where
v
ranges over valuations centered at
\mathfrak{m}

(
Theorem

5.6
).

A fundamental question is whether this supremum is in fact a maximum,
and whether it computes the Łojasiewicz exponent.
We isolate two independent mechanisms that force such a finite–max phenomenon.
The first is a
finite testing principle
: under suitable
Noetherian hypotheses, containment of integral closures can be tested
on a fixed finite set of divisorial valuations arising from a
normalized Rees algebra or a fixed birational model
(
Theorems

5.8
and
5.9
).
The second mechanism relies on compactness properties of normalized
valuation spaces together with uniform convergence of the functions

v\mapsto v(\mathfrak{a}_{p})/p
and
v\mapsto v(\mathfrak{b}_{q})/q
, which ensures attainment
of the supremum even in the absence of finite generation
(
Theorem

5.14
). We also illustrate that considering only divisorial valuations may not be enough to realize the supremum in general (
Theorem

5.16
).

2.3.
Rigidity, stability, and stratification

When the finite–max principle applies, the valuative formula for
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
provides an effective description of the invariant, and the extremal valuation(s) attaining the maximum become geometrically meaningful data.
A further contribution of the paper is to show that these extremal valuations govern the
behavior of Łojasiewicz exponents in families.

We prove that common extremal valuations force rigidity of Łojasiewicz ratios and that,
under finite–max hypotheses, parameter spaces admit a natural stratification according to
the valuation computing the exponent
(
Theorems

8.1
and
9.9
).
Along one–parameter families, this yields fractional linearity of
1/\mathcal{L}
and shows that
affineness forces persistence of the extremal valuation
(
Theorem

9.14
,

Corollary

9.16
).
These results provide a unified algebraic explanation for stability and wall–chamber behavior
observed in analytic and polyhedral settings.

2.4.
Polyhedral models and explicit computations

We describe explicit formulas and finite computation methods in combinatorial situations. For monomial and toric ideals, the exponent is determined by polyhedral data and toric valuations (
Section

10
).
In these situations, integral–closure containments are encoded by linear inequalities,
toric divisorial valuations correspond to linear functionals, and the valuative optimization
problem reduces to a finite maximization over facet data of Newton polyhedra
(
Theorems

10.18
and
10.11
).

We also discuss in more details how Łojasiewicz exponent at infinity behaves in our algebraic framework. We prove valuative and Rees–algebraic formulas at infinity (
Theorem

11.3
), and obtain finite computation and Newton polyhedral formulas in the nondegenerate case (
Theorems

11.10
and
11.7
).

2.5.
Uniformity and necessity

The final part of the paper illustrates why the hypotheses used to obtain finite testing and
finite–max formulas are essential (
Theorems

12.1
and
12.3
).
Outside situations admitting verifiable finite testing, the set of valuations computing

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
need not admit any a priori finite reduction, delineating the precise
scope of the theory developed here (
Theorem

12.4
).

Part II
Łojasiewicz Exponents and the Algebraic Framework

In this part, we develop the algebraic framework underlying Łojasiewicz exponents.
We begin by recalling analytic Łojasiewicz exponents and their standard variants, and
then generalize these invariants to a purely algebraic notion attached to graded families and
filtrations of ideals. This perspective allows Łojasiewicz exponents to be considered in a more general context, interpreted as asymptotic containment thresholds, and sets the stage for the valuative analysis carried out in the subsequent parts of the paper.

3.
Analytic Łojasiewicz exponents

3.1.
Background

In this section we briefly fix the analytic setting and conventions for
Łojasiewicz exponents that will be used throughout the paper. Our goal is not to
survey the analytic theory, but to record a formulation that is compatible with the
algebraic containment framework developed later.

Given two systems of analytic function germs near a point, Łojasiewicz-type
inequalities compare their relative rates of vanishing. Several equivalent formulations
appear in the literature, differing mainly by normalization. We adopt a convention that
aligns the analytic exponent with integral–closure containments and the algebraic
Łojasiewicz exponent defined in Section
4
.

3.2.
Analytic definition

Let

\mathfrak{a}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}),\quad\mathfrak{b}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{t})\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^{d},0},

where
0\in f_{i}^{-1}(0)\cap g_{j}^{-1}(0)
for all
i,j
. Define

\|f(x)\|:=\max_{1\leq i\leq s}|f_{i}(x)|,\quad\|g(x)\|:=\max_{1\leq j\leq t}|g_{j}(x)|.

Definition 3.1

(Analytic Łojasiewicz exponent)
.

The analytic Łojasiewicz exponent of
\mathfrak{a}
with respect to
\mathfrak{b}
is

\mathcal{L}^{an}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a}):=\inf\left\{\theta>0:\exists\,U\ni 0,\ \exists\,C>0\ \text{such that}\ C\|f(x)\|\geq\|g(x)\|^{\theta}\ \forall x\in U\right\}.

Remark 3.2

(Convention)
.

The inequality is written with “
\geq
”, so that larger values of

\mathcal{L}^{an}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})
correspond to stronger domination of (the vanishing of)
\mathfrak{a}
by
\mathfrak{b}
near the
origin. Other formulations in the literature may also differ by inversion of the exponent.

3.3.
The gradient Łojasiewicz exponent

Let
f:(\mathbb{C}^{d},0)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
be a holomorphic function with an isolated critical
point at the origin, and let
J(f)
denote its Jacobian ideal. The

gradient
Łojasiewicz exponent of
f
at 0, usually denoted by
L(f,0)
, is the optimal (infimum) value
\eta>0
such that

\|\nabla f(x)\|\geq C\|x\|^{\eta}

for
x
near
0
. In light of Definition
3.1
, the gradient Łojasiewicz exponent of
f
at 0 is given by
L(f,0)=\mathcal{L}^{an}_{\mathfrak{m}}(J(f)).

3.4.
The Łojasiewicz exponent at infinity

In addition to local exponents, one also studies Łojasiewicz inequalities at
infinity for polynomial mappings

F:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{m},

typically of the form

\|x\|^{\alpha}\leq C\|F(x)\|\quad\text{for }\|x\|\gg 1,

or variants involving
\mathrm{dist}(x,F^{-1}(0))
. The
optimal
(i.e., supremum) exponent
\alpha
in such
an inequality is called the
Łojasiewicz exponent of
F
at infinity
, and denoted by
L_{\infty}(F)
.

Although inequalities at infinity are global on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, their meaning
is local after projective compactification: the condition
\|x\|\to\infty
corresponds to approaching
the divisor at infinity, and the growth of
F
is encoded by the relative vanishing orders of the
source and target defining functions along the boundary.

4.
Algebraic Łojasiewicz exponents of filtrations

In this section we introduce the algebraic Łojasiewicz exponent
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
attached to a pair of filtrations
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
and
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
of ideals in a Noetherian ring, viewed as the asymptotic containment threshold for
\mathfrak{b}_{qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}
for
t\gg 1
(
Definition

4.3
). We show that this invariant recovers the classical analytic exponent in the local analytic setting (
Theorem

4.5
). Finally, we package Łojasiewicz exponents at infinity into the same algebraic framework by working with the canonical growth filtrations induced along the boundary divisor of a compactification, leading to an equivalence for the local and global “at infinity” invariants in
Theorem

4.9
.

4.1.
Graded families and Łojasiewicz exponent

The algebraic definition naturally involves graded families and filtrations of
ideals, encompassing powers, integral closures, symbolic powers, and growth
filtrations arising in geometric contexts. Throughout this section,
R
denotes a Noetherian ring.

Definition 4.1

(Graded family and filtration)
.

A
graded family of ideals

{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
in
R
is a collection of proper ideals
satisfying

\mathfrak{a}_{p}\mathfrak{a}_{p^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}_{p+p^{\prime}}\quad\text{for all }p,p^{\prime}\geq 1.

A graded family
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
is called a
filtration
if

\mathfrak{a}_{p+1}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}_{p}\quad\text{for all }p\geq 1.

Remark 4.2
.

All graded families appearing naturally in this paper — including powers of ideals,
integral closures of powers, symbolic powers — are filtrations. By
ideals
, we always mean proper (non-unit) ideals.

The Łojasiewicz exponent records the smallest asymptotic slope at which one
filtration is eventually contained in another.

Definition 4.3

(Algebraic Łojasiewicz exponent for filtrations)
.

Let
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
and
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
be filtrations
of ideals in
R
.
The
algebraic Łojasiewicz exponent of
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}
with respect to
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}
is

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\;:=\;\inf\left\{\frac{q}{p}\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\ :\ \mathfrak{b}_{qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}\text{ for }t\gg 1\right\}\ \in\ \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\},

with the convention that
\inf\emptyset=\infty
. For ideals
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\subseteq R
we write
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})
for
\mathcal{L}_{\{\mathfrak{b}^{q}\}_{q\geq 1}}\bigl(\{\mathfrak{a}^{p}\}_{p\geq 1}\bigr)
.

4.2.
Algebraic and analytic Łojasiewicz exponents

We now compare the algebraic Łojasiewicz exponent with its analytic
counterpart in the local analytic setting.
As before, assume
R=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^{d},0}
,
\mathfrak{a}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{s}),\mathfrak{b}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{t})

where
0\in f_{i}^{-1}(0)\cap g_{j}^{-1}(0)
for any
i
and
j
, and

\|f(x)\|:=\max_{i}|f_{i}(x)|,\quad\|g(x)\|:=\max_{j}|g_{j}(x)|.

The comparison rests on the following analytic criterion for integral closure stated by Lejeune-Jalabert–Teissier
[
36
]
.

Lemma 4.4

(Lejeune-Jalabert–Teissier)
.

Let
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathfrak{b}^{q}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{p}}
.

(2)

There exist a neighborhood
U
of
0
and a constant
C>0
such that

\|g(x)\|^{q}\leq C\|f(x)\|^{p}\quad\text{for all }x\in U.

Proof.

This is the Lejeune-Jalabert–Teissier analytic criterion for integral closure; see
[
36
, Théorème 7.2]
.
For the reader’s convenience and to fix normalization conventions, we include a self-contained argument in the appendix.
∎

Our first result shows that, for two ideals
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
, the analytic and algebraic Łojasiewicz exponents agree.

Theorem 4.5
.

We have

\mathcal{L}^{an}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})=\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a}).

Proof.

Let
\theta>0
be admissible for
\mathcal{L}^{an}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})
(see
Definition

3.1
).
Then, there exist a neighborhood
U
of
0
and a constant
C>0
such that

\|g(x)\|^{\theta}\leq C\|f(x)\|\quad\text{for all }x\in U.

Shrinking
U
if necessary, we may assume
\|g(x)\|\leq 1
on
U
.
Fix
p\in\mathbb{N}
and set
q=\lceil p\theta\rceil
.
Raising the above inequality to the
p
-th power yields

\|g(x)\|^{p\theta}\leq C^{p}\|f(x)\|^{p}\quad(x\in U).

Since
q\geq p\theta
and
\|g(x)\|\leq 1
, we have
\|g(x)\|^{q}\leq\|g(x)\|^{p\theta}
, and so

\|g(x)\|^{q}\leq C^{p}\|f(x)\|^{p}\quad(x\in U).

By
Lemma

4.4
, this implies
\mathfrak{b}^{q}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{p}}.

It follows that, for any
t\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\mathfrak{b}^{qt}\subseteq\left(\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{p}}\right)^{t}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{pt}}.

Thus,
\frac{q}{p}
is admissible for
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})
. Therefore,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})\leq\frac{q}{p}<\theta+\frac{1}{p}.

By replacing
\frac{q}{p}
with
\frac{qt}{pt}
, which is also admissible for
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})
, and letting
t\rightarrow\infty
(so
pt\rightarrow\infty
), we conclude that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})\leq\theta
. Taking the infimum over admissible
\theta
gives

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})\leq\mathcal{L}^{an}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a}).

Conversely, let
\frac{q}{p}\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
be admissible for
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})
. Then, for
t\gg 1
, we have
\mathfrak{b}^{qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{pt}}.

By
Lemma

4.4
, there exist a neighborhood
U
of
0
and a constant
C>0
such that

\|g(x)\|^{qt}\leq C\|f(x)\|^{pt}\quad\text{for all }x\in U.

Taking
(pt)
-th roots gives

\|g(x)\|^{q/p}\leq C^{1/(pt)}\|f(x)\|\quad(x\in U).

Hence,
\theta=\frac{q}{p}
is admissible for
\mathcal{L}^{an}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})
, and so

\mathcal{L}^{an}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})\leq\frac{q}{p}
.
Taking the infimum over admissible
\frac{q}{p}
yields

\mathcal{L}^{an}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a}).

This completes the proof.
∎

Observe, in particular, that when
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
are
\mathfrak{m}
–primary, both the analytic and algebraic Łojasiewicz exponents are finite and governed by integral-closure containments.

Example 4.6
.

Let
R=\mathbb{C}[[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]]
,
\mathfrak{m}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and consider

\mathfrak{a}=(x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}},\dots,x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}})\text{ and }\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{m}.

It can be seen that the inequality

\|(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\|^{\theta}\leq C\max(|x_{1}|^{\alpha_{1}},\dots,|x_{n}|^{\alpha_{n}})

fails for
\theta<\max\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}
along the coordinate axis, and holds for
\theta=\max\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}
. Thus, by definition,

\mathcal{L}^{an}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})=\max\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}.

On the other hand, one can check on pure powers of the
x_{i}
’s that

\mathfrak{m}^{qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{pt}}\text{ for }t\gg 1\Longleftrightarrow\frac{q}{p}\geq\max\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}.

That is,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})=\max\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}=\mathcal{L}^{an}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a}).

4.3.
Exponents at infinity in the algebraic framework

We shall explore an algebraic formulation of Łojasiewicz exponents at infinity, understand precisely how the analytic condition
\|x\|\gg 1
is encoded by divisors at infinity on a suitable compactification, and show that the resulting analytic invariants agree with the algebraic containment invariants used in our
valuative framework.

Let
F=(f_{1},\dots,f_{m}):\mathbb{A}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathbb{A}^{m}_{\mathbb{C}}
be a polynomial mapping.
We fix the standard projective compactifications

\mathbb{A}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}
and
\mathbb{A}^{m}_{\mathbb{C}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{m}_{\mathbb{C}}
,
with hyperplanes at infinity

H_{X}:=\{Z_{0}=0\}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}\text{ and }H_{Y}:=\{W_{0}=0\}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{m}_{\mathbb{C}},

where
Z_{0},\dots,Z_{n}
and
W_{0},\dots,W_{m}
denote the homogeneous coordinates of
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}
and
\mathbb{P}^{m}_{\mathbb{C}}
, respectively.
Let
\Gamma_{F}\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}\times\mathbb{A}^{m}_{\mathbb{C}}
be the graph of
F
and let

\overline{\Gamma}_{F}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}\times\mathbb{P}^{m}_{\mathbb{C}}
be the normalization of the
Zariski closure of
\Gamma_{F}
.
Let

\pi:\overline{\Gamma}_{F}\to\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}\text{ and }\rho:\overline{\Gamma}_{F}\to\mathbb{P}^{m}_{\mathbb{C}}

be the natural projections and set

D_{X}:=\pi^{-1}(H_{X})\text{ and }D_{Y}:=\rho^{-1}(H_{Y}).

Denote by
\mathscr{I}_{X}:=\mathcal{I}_{D_{X}}
and
\mathscr{I}_{Y}:=\mathcal{I}_{D_{Y}}
the corresponding
ideal sheaves of
D_{X}
and
D_{Y}
on
\overline{\Gamma}_{F}
.
Approaching infinity in the source along the graph of
F
corresponds precisely to
approaching
D_{X}
inside
\overline{\Gamma}_{F}
.

What does
\|x\|\gg 1
mean near a boundary point.

Fix
\xi\in D_{X}
.
Choose indices
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,m\}
such that

\pi(\xi)\in\{Z_{i}\neq 0\}
and
\rho(\xi)\in\{W_{\ell}\neq 0\}
.
Define holomorphic functions near
\xi
by

u_{X}:=\Big(\frac{Z_{0}}{Z_{i}}\Big)\circ\pi\text{ and }u_{Y}:=\Big(\frac{W_{0}}{W_{\ell}}\Big)\circ\rho.

Then,
u_{X}
is a local defining equation of
D_{X}
at
\xi
.
If
\xi\in D_{Y}
and
\ell\geq 1
, then
u_{Y}
is a local defining equation of
D_{Y}
at
\xi
.
If
\xi\notin D_{Y}
, then we may take
\ell=0
and then
u_{Y}\equiv 1
near
\xi
.

Lemma 4.7
.

With notation as above, there exist an analytic neighborhood
U
of
\xi
in

\overline{\Gamma}_{F}
and constants
C_{X}\geq 1
and
C_{Y}\geq 1
such that
for all
(x,F(x))\in U\cap\Gamma_{F}
one has

(4.1)

\frac{1}{|u_{X}|}\leq\|x\|\leq\frac{C_{X}}{|u_{X}|}.

Moreover, if
\xi\in D_{X}\cap D_{Y}
and
\ell\geq 1
, then for all
(x,F(x))\in U\cap\Gamma_{F}
one has

(4.2)

\frac{1}{|u_{Y}|}\leq\|F(x)\|\leq\frac{C_{Y}}{|u_{Y}|}.

If
\xi\in D_{X}\setminus D_{Y}
, then
u_{Y}
is a unit near
\xi
and
\|F(x)\|
is bounded on

U\cap\Gamma_{F}
.
In particular, for
\xi\in D_{X}\cap D_{Y}
and
\theta\geq 0
, the inequality

\|x\|^{\theta}\leq C\|F(x)\|

holds on
(U\cap\Gamma_{F})\setminus D_{X}
if and only if

(4.3)

|u_{Y}|\leq C^{\prime}|u_{X}|^{\theta}

holds on
(U\cap\Gamma_{F})\setminus D_{X}
.

Proof.

Work in the affine chart
\{Z_{i}\neq 0\}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}
with coordinates

z_{0}:=Z_{0}/Z_{i}
and
z_{j}:=Z_{j}/Z_{i}
for
j\neq i
.
On
\mathbb{A}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}=\{Z_{0}\neq 0\}
we have
x_{j}=Z_{j}/Z_{0}=z_{j}/z_{0}
.
Thus, on
U\cap\Gamma_{F}
we have
u_{X}=z_{0}\circ\pi
and

|x_{j}|=\frac{|z_{j}\circ\pi|}{|u_{X}|}.

Shrinking
U
if necessary, we may assume
U
is relatively compact.
Since each
z_{j}\circ\pi
is holomorphic on
U
, it is bounded on
U
.
Hence, there exists
M_{X}\geq 1
such that
|z_{j}\circ\pi|\leq M_{X}
on
U
for all
j
.
This gives
\|x\|\leq M_{X}/|u_{X}|
, which is the upper bound in (
4.1
).
For the lower bound, note that
x_{i}=Z_{i}/Z_{0}=1/(Z_{0}/Z_{i})=1/z_{0}
, so on
U\cap\Gamma_{F}
we have

\|x\|\geq|x_{i}|=1/|u_{X}|
.

For the target side, the argument is analogous in the affine chart
\{W_{\ell}\neq 0\}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{m}_{\mathbb{C}}
.
If
\ell\geq 1
and
\xi\in D_{Y}
, then
u_{Y}=(W_{0}/W_{\ell})\circ\rho
vanishes at
\xi
and

F_{j}=W_{j}/W_{0}=(W_{j}/W_{\ell})/(W_{0}/W_{\ell})
, so bounding the affine coordinates yields (
4.2
).
If
\xi\in D_{X}\setminus D_{Y}
, we may take
\ell=0
, so
u_{Y}\equiv 1
near
\xi
and
\|F(x)\|
is bounded.
Finally, when
\xi\in D_{X}\cap D_{Y}
, combining (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) shows that

\|x\|^{\theta}\leq C\|F(x)\|
holds on
(U\cap\Gamma_{F})\setminus D_{X}
if and only if

|u_{Y}|\leq C^{\prime}|u_{X}|^{\theta}
holds there.
∎

Local and global analytic exponents at infinity.

Lemma

4.7
allows us to recast the definition of Łojasiewicz exponent at infinity in terms of divisors at infinity.

Definition 4.8
.

(1)

Fix
\xi\in D_{X}
and let
u_{X}
and
u_{Y}
be as above. The
local analytic Łojasiewicz exponent at infinity of
F
along
\xi
is

\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F):=\sup\left\{\theta\geq 0\ \middle|\ \exists\,C>0\text{ such that }|u_{Y}|\leq C|u_{X}|^{\theta}\text{ on }(U_{\xi}\cap\Gamma_{F})\setminus D_{X}\right\},

where
U_{\xi}
is a sufficiently small analytic neighborhood of
\xi
in
\overline{\Gamma}_{F}
. (Note that, if
\xi\in D_{X}\setminus D_{Y}
, then
u_{Y}
is a unit near
\xi
and therefore

\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)=0
.)

(2)

The
global analytic Łojasiewicz exponent at infinity
of
F
is

\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty}(F):=\inf_{\xi\in D_{X}}\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F).

We work with the convention
\inf\varnothing=\infty
,
1/\infty=0
and
1/0=\infty
. The next result shows that Łojasiewicz exponent at infinity can also be understood in our algebraic framework.

Theorem 4.9
.

For every
\xi\in D_{X}
, consider the graded families of ideals in
\mathcal{O}_{\overline{\Gamma}_{F},\xi}
given by

\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet,\xi}:=\{\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}^{p}\}_{p\geq 1}\text{ and }\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet,\xi}:=\{\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}^{q}\}_{q\geq 1}.

Then,

\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet,\xi}}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet,\xi})}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}_{\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}}(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi})}.

Equivalently,

\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)=\frac{1}{\inf\left\{\frac{q}{p}\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\ \middle|\ \mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}^{\,qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}^{\,pt}}\text{ for all }t\gg 1\right\}}.

Proof.

If
\xi\in D_{X}\setminus D_{Y}
, then
\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}=\mathcal{O}_{\overline{\Gamma}_{F},\xi}
, so the admissible
set in Definition
4.3
for
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet,\xi}}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet,\xi})
is empty and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet,\xi}}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet,\xi})=\infty
, i.e.,
1/\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet,\xi}}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet,\xi})=0
.
On the other hand,
u_{Y}
is a unit near
\xi
, so

\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)=0
by Definition
4.8
.
Thus, the equality holds in this case.

Assume now
\xi\in D_{X}\cap D_{Y}
.
Let
\theta=p/q\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
.
By Lemma
4.7
, the condition

\theta\leq\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)
is equivalent to the existence of

C>0
such that

|u_{Y}|\leq C|u_{X}|^{p/q}\text{ on }(U_{\xi}\cap\Gamma_{F})\setminus D_{X}.

Equivalently,
|u_{Y}|^{q}\leq C^{q}|u_{X}|^{p}
holds in a punctured neighborhood of
\xi
.
By the Lejeune-Jalabert–Teissier criterion,
Lemma

4.4
, this is equivalent to the containment

(u_{Y})^{q}\subseteq\overline{(u_{X})^{p}}\text{ in }\mathcal{O}_{\overline{\Gamma}_{F},\xi}.

Since
D_{X}
and
D_{Y}
are Cartier divisors, we have

\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}=(u_{X})
and
\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}=(u_{Y})
.
Thus, the above containment is equivalent to

\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}^{\,q}\subseteq\overline{\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}^{\,p}}\text{ in }\mathcal{O}_{\overline{\Gamma}_{F},\xi}.

Raising to
t
-th powers yields

\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}^{\,qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}^{\,pt}}\text{ for all }t\geq 1.

Therefore,

\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)}=\inf\left\{\frac{q}{p}\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\ \middle|\ \mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}^{\,qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}^{\,pt}}\text{ for all }t\gg 1\right\}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet,\xi}}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet,\xi}).\qed

Part III
Valuations as the Computational Engine

In this part, we describe the Łojasiewicz exponent in valuative terms and
identify conditions under which the lower bound supremum over valuations is
attained, reduces to a finite maximum, and computes the Łojasiewicz exponent.

5.
Valuative formulas and the finite–max principle

In this section we establish the valuative framework that serves as the computational engine of the paper. For a pair of filtrations
({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}},{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
on a Noetherian local domain, we first express
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
in terms of the asymptotic valuative data
v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
and
v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
and obtain the fundamental bound

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\ \geq\ \sup_{v}\frac{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})},

where
v
ranges over valuations centered at
\mathfrak{m}
(
Theorem

5.6
). The main structural contribution is to isolate verifiable hypotheses under which this supremum both equals
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
and is attained by a valuation, so the optimization problem reduces to a finite maximum: first via finite divisorial testing sets arising from Noetherian/finitely generated Rees algebras (
Theorems

5.8
and
5.9
), and second via compactness of normalized valuation spaces together with uniform convergence of the functions
v\mapsto v(\mathfrak{a}_{p})/p
and
v\mapsto v(\mathfrak{b}_{q})/q
(
Theorems

5.14
,
5.16
and
5.17
).

5.1.
Valuations and asymptotic values of filtrations

We recall basic notions on valuations and the asymptotic value of a
graded family with respect to a valuation. For a geometric perspective on spaces of valuations and their role in controlling
singularity invariants – especially in dimension two – we refer to Favre–Jonsson
[
16
]
monograph.

Throughout this section,
(R,\mathfrak{m})
is a Noetherian local domain.
A
(semi)valuation
on
R
is a map

v:R\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

satisfying
v(xy)=v(x)+v(y)
and
v(x+y)\geq\min\{v(x),v(y)\}
, extended by
v(0)=\infty
.
When discussing valuations on
R
, we always assume
v
is
centered at
\mathfrak{m}
, i.e.,
v(x)>0
for all
x\in\mathfrak{m}
.
For an ideal
I\subseteq R
we write

v(I):=\min\{v(x):x\in I\}.

For a graded family
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
, set

v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}):=\lim_{p\to\infty}\frac{v(\mathfrak{a}_{p})}{p}=\inf_{p\geq 1}\frac{v(\mathfrak{a}_{p})}{p}.

Remark 5.1

(Subadditivity and existence of
v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
)
.

Let
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
be a graded family and let
v
be a valuation centered at
\mathfrak{m}
.
Then, the sequence
\{v(\mathfrak{a}_{p})\}_{p\geq 1}
is subadditive:

v(\mathfrak{a}_{p+p^{\prime}})\leq v(\mathfrak{a}_{p})+v(\mathfrak{a}_{p^{\prime}})\quad\forall p,p^{\prime}\geq 1.

Thus, by Fekete’s lemma, the limit
v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\lim_{p\to\infty}v(\mathfrak{a}_{p})/p
exists and equals

\inf_{p\geq 1}v(\mathfrak{a}_{p})/p
.

5.2.
Valuative criterion for integral closure

We recall classical valuative criteria for integral closure, which we will repeatedly used in the paper.

Lemma 5.2

(Classical valuative criterion for integral closure)
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a Noetherian local domain,
I\subseteq R
an ideal, and
x\in R
.
Then,
x\in\overline{I}
if and only if

v(x)\geq v(I)

for every valuation
v
of
\Frac(R)
centered at
\mathfrak{m}
. Moreover, it suffices to test the inequalities on the finitely many Rees valuations of
I
, equivalently on the divisorial valuations associated to prime divisors on the normalized blowup of
I
.

Proof.

This result is classical and goes back to Rees
[
45
]
and Lipman
[
38
]
; see also Huneke–Swanson
[
30
, Chapters 6 and 10]
for a modern account.
∎

Remark 5.3

(Analytic arc criterion)
.

When
R=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^{d},0}
, the same condition can be tested on analytic arcs
via the curve criterion for integral closure; see, for instance
[
36
]
.

On a fixed birational model, the classical valuative criterion gives rise to the following reduction statement.

Lemma 5.4

(Integral–closure reduction on a fixed model)
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be an excellent Noetherian local domain. Let
\pi:Y\to\Spec(R)
be a proper birational morphism such that
Y
is normal. Let
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\subseteq R
be ideals, and assume that
\mathfrak{a}\mathcal{O}_{Y}
and
\mathfrak{b}\mathcal{O}_{Y}
are invertible. Write

\mathfrak{a}\mathcal{O}_{Y}=\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-A),\quad\mathfrak{b}\mathcal{O}_{Y}=\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-B),

with
A,B
effective Cartier divisors supported on the exceptional locus.

Then, for all
p,q\geq 1
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathfrak{b}^{q}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{p}}
in
R
;

(2)

\pi^{*}(\mathfrak{b}^{q})\subseteq\overline{\pi^{*}(\mathfrak{a}^{p})}
in
\mathcal{O}_{Y}
;

(3)

q\,B\geq p\,A
as divisors on
Y
;

(4)

\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathfrak{b}^{q})\geq\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathfrak{a}^{p})
for every prime divisor
E
on
Y
.

In particular, integral–closure containments are completely determined by
the finitely many divisorial valuations
\{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}\}
arising from the
exceptional divisors of
\pi
.

Proof.

Observe that
\mathfrak{a}^{p}\mathcal{O}_{Y}=\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-pA)
and
\mathfrak{b}^{q}\mathcal{O}_{Y}=\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-qB)
are invertible, and so, integrally closed on
Y
. Furthermore, since
Y
is normal,
\pi
factors through the normalized blowup of
\mathfrak{a}
. Thus, we have the following standard equality (cf.
[
30
, Chapters 10 and 19]
):

\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{p}}=\pi_{*}(\mathfrak{a}^{p}\mathcal{O}_{Y}).

Particularly, if
\mathfrak{b}^{q}\mathcal{O}_{Y}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^{p}\mathcal{O}_{Y}
, then pushing forward gives

\mathfrak{b}^{q}\subseteq\pi_{*}(\mathfrak{a}^{p}\mathcal{O}_{Y})=\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{p}}.

On the other hand, if
\mathfrak{b}^{q}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{p}}=\pi_{*}(\mathfrak{a}^{p}\mathcal{O}_{Y})
then
\mathfrak{b}^{q}\mathcal{O}_{Y}\subseteq(\pi_{*}(\mathfrak{a}^{p}\mathcal{O}_{Y}))\mathcal{O}_{Y}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^{p}\mathcal{O}_{Y}
, where the latter containment follows from the natural evaluation map
\pi^{*}\pi_{*}(\mathfrak{a}^{p}\mathcal{O}_{Y})\rightarrow\mathfrak{a}^{p}\mathcal{O}_{Y}
. Hence, we have the equivalence (1)
\Longleftrightarrow
(2).

The equivalences with (3) and (4) follow from the fact that
\mathfrak{a}^{p}\mathcal{O}_{Y}
and
\mathfrak{b}^{q}\mathcal{O}_{Y}
are invertible sheaves, and so
\mathfrak{b}^{q}\mathcal{O}_{Y}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^{p}\mathcal{O}_{Y}\Longleftrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-qB)\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-pA)
, which is equivalent to
qB\geq pA\Longleftrightarrow q\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathfrak{b})\geq p\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathfrak{a})
for all prime divisors
E\subseteq Y
.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

In the Newton nondegenerate setting, the toric modification associated to the
Newton fan of
f
provides such a morphism
\pi
, and the divisors
E
correspond
to compact facets of the Newton polyhedron (cf.
[
11
,
33
,
42
]
).

5.3.
Valuative formula for
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})

We derive a general valuative lower bound for the algebraic Łojasiewicz
exponent in terms of ratios of asymptotic valuation values. This is the basic computational engine that allows us to explore the finiteness mechanisms later.

Theorem 5.6

(Valuative lower bound)
.

Assume that
(R,\mathfrak{m})
is a Noetherian local domain. Let
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
and

{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
be graded families of ideals in
R
.
Then

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\ \geq\ \sup_{v}\ \frac{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})},

where the supremum is taken over all valuations
v
centered at
\mathfrak{m}
with
v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})>0
.

Proof.

Set
M=\sup_{v}{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}/{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}.

Fix a rational number
\frac{q}{p}\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
which is admissible in the definition of

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
, i.e. such that

\mathfrak{b}_{qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}\quad\text{for all integers }t\gg 1.

Let
v
be a valuation of
\Frac(R)
centered at
\mathfrak{m}
with
v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})>0
.
By the valuative criterion for integral closure (
Lemma

5.2
), the containment

\mathfrak{b}_{qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}
implies
v(\mathfrak{b}_{qt})\geq v(\mathfrak{a}_{pt})
for all
t\gg 1
. Thus,

(5.1)

\displaystyle\frac{v(\mathfrak{b}_{qt})}{t}\ \geq\ \frac{v(\mathfrak{a}_{pt})}{t}\quad\text{for all }t\gg 1.

By
Remark

5.1
, the limits
v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{v(\mathfrak{a}_{r})}{r}
and
v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{v(\mathfrak{b}_{r})}{r}

exist. In particular, we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{v(\mathfrak{a}_{pt})}{pt}=v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\text{ and }\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{v(\mathfrak{b}_{qt})}{qt}=v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}).

This, together with (
5.1
), implies that
q\,v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})\ \geq\ p\,v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}).

Since
v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})>0
, we conclude that

\frac{q}{p}\ \geq\ \frac{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}.

As this holds for every valuation
v
centered at
\mathfrak{m}
, it follows that
\frac{q}{p}\geq M.

Taking the infimum over all admissible
\frac{q}{p}
in the definition of

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
yields the desired inequality
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\geq M
.
∎

Example 5.7
.

Let
R=\mathbb{C}[[x,y]],\mathfrak{m}=(x,y)
, and consider

\mathfrak{a}=(x^{\alpha},y^{\beta})\text{ and }\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{m}.

For a monomial valuation
v_{u}
, corresponding to
u=(u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\geq 0}
, with
v_{u}(x)=u_{1}
and
v_{u}(y)=u_{2}
, we get

\frac{v_{u}(\mathfrak{a})}{v_{u}(\mathfrak{b})}=\frac{\min\{\alpha\cdot u_{1},\beta\cdot u_{2}\}}{\min\{u_{1},u_{2}\}}.

Taking the maximum value over
u
gives
\max\{\alpha,\beta\}
. Thus,
Theorem

5.6
gives

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})\geq\max\{\alpha,\beta\}.

On the other hand, by
Example

4.6
, we know that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})=\max\{\alpha,\beta\}.
This example illustrates that the bound given in
Theorem

5.6
is sharp.

Theorem

5.6
shows that
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
is governed by a valuative
optimization problem. In general, however, the inquality may be strict, the resulting supremum need not be a maximum, and the relevant candidate valuations need not be controlled uniformly without additional
finiteness hypotheses. The next subsections isolate two mechanisms that force the supremum
to be attained and to reduce to a
finite
maximum.

5.4.
From supremum to maximum: finite testing and Noetherian Rees algebra

We identify the first mechanism that forces the valuative supremum to define the Łojasiewicz exponent and to be a finite maximum.

Theorem 5.8

(Finite testing set
\Rightarrow
finite maximum formula)
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a local domain and let

{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
and
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
be graded families of ideals in
R
.
Fix a finite set of (divisorial) valuations
V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{N}\}
of
\Frac(R)
centered at
\mathfrak{m}
.
Assume:

(H1)

For all
p,q\geq 1
one has the equivalence

(5.2)

\mathfrak{b}_{q}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v_{i}(\mathfrak{b}_{q})\geq v_{i}(\mathfrak{a}_{p})\ \text{ for all }1\leq i\leq N.

(H2)

For each
i
we have
v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})>0
.

Then,

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\frac{v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}.

Proof.

Set

M\ :=\ \max_{1\leq i\leq N}\ \frac{v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}\ \in\ (0,\infty),

which is finite by (H2). We prove the two inequalities separately.

Step 1: Lower bound
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\geq M
.

Fix
\frac{q}{p}\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
admissible in the definition of
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
, so that

\mathfrak{b}_{qt}\ \subseteq\ \overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}\quad\text{for all }t\gg 1.

By the same arguments as in the proof of
Theorem

5.6
, we get

\frac{q}{p}\ \geq\ \frac{v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}\quad\text{for all }1\leq i\leq N.

Thus,
\frac{q}{p}\geq M
. Taking the infimum over all admissible
\frac{q}{p}
gives

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\geq M
.

Step 2: Upper bound
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\leq M
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and set
\beta\ :=\ \min_{1\leq i\leq N}v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})\ >\ 0.

Choose
p\gg 1
and set

q\ :=\ \left\lceil\left(M+\frac{\varepsilon}{\beta}\right)p\right\rceil.

For each
i
, by the definition of
v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
as a limit along the subsequence
\{pt\}
,
there exists
t_{0}(i)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all integers
t\geq t_{0}(i)
,

\frac{v_{i}(\mathfrak{a}_{pt})}{pt}\ \leq\ v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})+\varepsilon.

Let
t_{0}:=\max_{i}t_{0}(i)
, so the above estimate holds for all
i
and all
t\geq t_{0}
.

On the other hand, since
v_{i}(\mathfrak{b}_{qt})\ \geq\ qt\,v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
, for every
t\geq t_{0}
and every
i
, we have

v_{i}(\mathfrak{b}_{qt})\ \geq\ qt\,v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})\ \geq\ \left(M+\frac{\varepsilon}{\beta}\right)pt\,v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})\ \geq\ pt\,(M\,v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})+\varepsilon)\ \geq\ pt\,(v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})+\varepsilon)\ \geq\ v_{i}(\mathfrak{a}_{pt}).

That is, for all integers
t\geq t_{0}
,
v_{i}(\mathfrak{b}_{qt})\geq v_{i}(\mathfrak{a}_{pt})
for every
i
.
By (H1) applied to
(pt,qt)
, it follows that

\mathfrak{b}_{qt}\ \subseteq\ \overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}\quad\text{for all }t\geq t_{0}.

Thus,
\frac{q}{p}
is admissible in the definition of
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
, and so

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\ \leq\ \frac{q}{p}\ \leq\ M+\frac{\varepsilon}{\beta}+\frac{1}{p}.

Letting
p\to\infty
gives
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\leq M+\frac{\varepsilon}{\beta}
, and then
letting
\varepsilon\to 0
yields
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\leq M
.
This completes the proof.
∎

In most applications below, the equivalence in (H1) arises from integral-closure testing on a fixed model (e.g., a normalized blowup or toric modification). While the forward implication alone suffices for the lower bound, the equivalence ensures that admissible containments can be produced uniformly.

Hypotheses for the finite testing principle (
Theorem

5.8
) are satisfied when the Rees algebra is Noetherian, as shown in the next result.

Theorem 5.9

(Finite Rees valuations for a Noetherian filtration)
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be an excellent Noetherian local domain, and let

{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
be a filtration of ideals in
R
.
Assume that the Rees algebra
\mathcal{R}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}):=\bigoplus_{p\geq 0}\mathfrak{a}_{p}T^{p}

is finitely generated as an
R
–algebra.
Let
\overline{\mathcal{R}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}
denote its integral closure, and set
\overline{Y}:=\Proj\overline{\mathcal{R}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}.

Then, there exists a finite set of divisorial valuations

\mathcal{V}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{r}\},

arising from prime divisors on
\overline{Y}
dominating
\Spec R
, such that for every filtration
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
of ideals in
R
satisfying
v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})>0
for all
1\leq i\leq r
, one has

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\frac{v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}.

Proof.

We break the proof into two steps.

Step 1: a finite divisorial testing set for the integral closures

\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}
.

Let

\mathcal{R}:=\mathcal{R}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\bigoplus_{p\geq 0}\mathfrak{a}_{p}T^{p}\subseteq R[T]\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\mathcal{R}}:=\overline{\mathcal{R}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}

be the integral closure of
\mathcal{R}
in its total ring of fractions.
Since
\mathcal{R}
is finitely generated over the excellent Noetherian domain
R
, it is Noetherian,
and
\overline{\mathcal{R}}
is a finite
\mathcal{R}
–module; in particular

\overline{\mathcal{R}}
is again a finitely generated
R
–algebra.

Let
\pi:\overline{Y}\to\Spec R
the induced proper birational morphism.
Write
E\subseteq\overline{Y}
for the exceptional locus of
\pi
.
Since
\overline{Y}
is of finite type over
R
, the exceptional locus has only finitely many
irreducible components of codimension one; denote them by

E_{1},\dots,E_{r}.

Each
E_{i}
defines a divisorial valuation
v_{i}
of
\Frac(R)
by

v_{i}(h):=\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i}}(\pi^{*}h)\quad(0\neq h\in\Frac(R)),

normalized so that
v_{i}(R)\geq 0
and
v_{i}(\mathfrak{m})>0
whenever
\pi(E_{i})\subseteq V(\mathfrak{m})
.

We claim that these finitely many valuations test the integral closure of each

\mathfrak{a}_{p}
in the sense that

(5.3)

\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}=\{\,h\in R:\ v_{i}(h)\geq v_{i}(\mathfrak{a}_{p})\ \text{for all }i=1,\dots,r\,\}.

Indeed, for
h\in R
we have

h\in\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ hT^{p}\in\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}T^{p}}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{p}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ hT^{p}\in\overline{\mathcal{R}},

where
\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{p}
denotes the degree-
p
piece of
\overline{\mathcal{R}}
.
Now,
\overline{\mathcal{R}}
is a normal Noetherian domain, so a Krull domain; therefore,

\overline{\mathcal{R}}=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{P}\in\Spec\overline{\mathcal{R}}\\
\mathrm{ht}(\mathfrak{P})=1\end{subarray}}(\overline{\mathcal{R}})_{\mathfrak{P}}\ \subseteq\ \Frac(\overline{\mathcal{R}}).

Consequently,
hT^{p}\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}
if and only if
hT^{p}
lies in each DVR

(\overline{\mathcal{R}})_{\mathfrak{P}}
for
\mathrm{ht}(\mathfrak{P})=1
.
Among the height-one primes
\mathfrak{P}
, only those corresponding to the codimension-one
components of the exceptional locus contribute nontrivially to membership in
\mathfrak{a}_{p}
; the
corresponding valuations are precisely
v_{1},\dots,v_{r}
, and the resulting condition is
exactly (
5.3
). This proves the claim.

We set

\mathcal{V}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}):=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{r}\}.

Step 2: computation of
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
.

Fix any filtration
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
of ideals in
R
.
Set

\lambda\ :=\ \max_{1\leq i\leq r}\ \frac{v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}\ \in\ \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}.

Let
\frac{q}{p}\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
be admissible in Definition
4.3
, i.e.,

\mathfrak{b}_{qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}\quad\text{for all integers }t\gg 1.

By (
5.3
) applied to
pt
, this containment is equivalent to

v_{i}(\mathfrak{b}_{qt})\ \geq\ v_{i}(\mathfrak{a}_{pt})\quad\text{for all }i=1,\dots,r\ \text{and all }t\gg 1.

By the same arguments as in the proof of
Theorem

5.6
, we get

q\,v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})\geq p\,v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
, i.e.,

\frac{q}{p}\ \geq\ \frac{v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}\quad\text{for all }i.

Hence,
\frac{q}{p}\geq\lambda
. Taking the infimum over admissible
\frac{q}{p}
yields

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\geq\lambda
.

Conversely, fix
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small.
By the definition of
v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
and
v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
, there exists
p_{0}

such that for all
p\geq p_{0}
and all
i
,

\frac{v_{i}(\mathfrak{a}_{p})}{p}\ \leq\ v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})+\varepsilon\text{ and }\frac{v_{i}(\mathfrak{b}_{p})}{p}\ \geq\ v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})-\varepsilon>0.

Set

\delta=\epsilon\cdot\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq r}\frac{v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})+v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}{v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})(v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})-\epsilon)}.

Then,
\delta\rightarrow 0
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
and

(\lambda+\delta)(v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})-\epsilon)\geq v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})+\epsilon.

Choose
p\geq p_{0}
large and set

q:=\left\lceil(\lambda+\delta)p\right\rceil.

Now choose
t_{0}
large enough so that
qt\geq p_{0}
for all
i
and all
t\geq t_{0}
.
Then, for every
i
and every integer
t\geq t_{0}
we have

v_{i}(\mathfrak{b}_{qt})\ \geq\ qt\,(v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})-\varepsilon)\ \geq\ (\lambda+\delta)pt\,(v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})-\varepsilon)\ \geq\ pt\,(v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})+\varepsilon)\ \geq\ v_{i}(\mathfrak{a}_{pt}).

Hence, by (
5.3
),

\mathfrak{b}_{qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}\quad\text{for all integers }t\geq t_{0}.

Thus,
\frac{q}{p}
is admissible in Definition
4.3
, so

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\ \leq\ \frac{q}{p}\ \leq\ \lambda+\delta+\frac{1}{p}.

Letting
p\to\infty
and then
\varepsilon\to 0
(
\delta\to 0
) gives
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\leq\lambda
.
The theorem is proved.
∎

Remark 5.10
.

(1)

The finite set
\mathcal{V}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
depends only on the filtration
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}

and not on
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}
.
When
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}^{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
for an
\mathfrak{m}
–primary ideal
\mathfrak{a}
, the set

\mathcal{V}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
coincides with the classical set of Rees valuations
of
\mathfrak{a}
.

(2)

The condition that
v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})>0
in
Theorem

5.9
is often satisfied in practice. For instance, if
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}
is
linearly bounded
from below, i.e., there exists a constant
c
such that

\mathfrak{m}^{\lceil cq\rceil}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}_{q}.

Particularly, if
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{m}^{q}\}_{q\in\mathbb{N}}
, then the hypothesis
v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})>0
in
Theorem

5.9
is automatically satisfied.

(3)

The conclusion of
Theorem

5.9
still holds under a slightly weaker condition that
v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})=0
implies
v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})>0
for any
i=1,\dots,r
. This is satisfied particularly if
v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})>0
for all
i=1,\dots,r
.

Corollary 5.11

(Finite testing set from finite generation)
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be an excellent Noetherian local domain, and let

{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
be a filtration whose Rees algebra

\mathcal{R}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
is finitely generated.
Then, there exists a finite set of divisorial valuations
\mathcal{V}

with the following property:

For any filtration
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
, with
v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})>0\ \forall\ v\in\mathcal{V}
, and any
p,q\geq 1
,

\mathfrak{b}_{q}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v(\mathfrak{b}_{q})\geq v(\mathfrak{a}_{p})\ \text{for all }v\in\mathcal{V}.

In particular,
\mathcal{V}
is a finite testing set for asymptotic
containment problems involving
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}
.

Proof.

By
Theorem

5.9
, there exists a finite set of
divisorial valuations
\mathcal{V}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
computing

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
for all filtrations
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}
.
The valuative criterion for integral closure then implies that the same
set of valuations detects all containments

\mathfrak{b}_{q}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}
, proving the claim.
∎

Example 5.12
.

Let
R:=k[x,y]_{(x,y)},\ \mathfrak{m}:=(x,y)
,
and let

\mathfrak{a}_{1}:=(x^{4},\,x^{2}y^{3},\,y^{5})\subseteq R.

Consider

{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}:=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}\quad\text{with }\mathfrak{a}_{p}=\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{p},\quad{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}:=\{\mathfrak{b}_{q}\}_{q\geq 1}\quad\text{with }\mathfrak{b}_{q}=\mathfrak{m}^{q}.

Since the Rees algebra
R({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
is finitely generated,
Corollary

5.11
applies. Hence, there exists a finite
set of divisorial valuations
V({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
such that, for all
p,q\geq 1
,

\mathfrak{b}_{q}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v(\mathfrak{b}_{q})\geq v(\mathfrak{a}_{p})\ \text{for all }v\in V({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}),

and

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\max_{v\in V({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}\frac{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}.

In this two–dimensional monomial setting, the valuations in
V({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
admit an
explicit description: they are precisely the divisorial (toric) valuations
corresponding to the compact edges of the Newton polygon of
\mathfrak{a}_{1}
. Concretely,
one may take the monomial valuations
v_{(1,1)}
and
v_{(2,1)}
, defined by

v_{(u,v)}(x)=u,\quad v_{(u,v)}(y)=v.

A direct computation gives
v_{(1,1)}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=4,v_{(2,1)}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=5
, and
v_{(1,1)}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})=v_{(2,1)}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})=1.
Consequently,

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=5.

This illustrates concretely how the valuative supremum reduces to a finite maximum
on a fixed normalized blowup.

5.5.
From supremum to maximum: normalized valuation space and linearly boundedness

We explain how compactness of normalized valuation spaces yields attainment of
the valuative supremum under linear boundedness hypotheses.

Let
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}
denote the set of real (semi)valuations
v
of
R
centered at
\mathfrak{m}
.
Fix a nonzero
\mathfrak{m}
-primary ideal
\mathfrak{q}\subseteq R
and consider the normalized slice

\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q}):=\{v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}:v(\mathfrak{q})=1\}.

Lemma 5.13
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a Noetherian local ring and let
\mathfrak{q}\subseteq R
be an
\mathfrak{m}
–primary ideal.
Then,
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
is compact in the topology of pointwise convergence on
R
. Moreover, for any graded family
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}
of ideals in
R
, the function

v\longmapsto v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})

is upper semicontinuous on
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
.

Proof.

By the discussion following
[
9
, Definition 1.3]
(see also
[
31
, Proposition 5.9]
and the discussion that followed), the normalized space
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m})

is compact in the topology of pointwise convergence on
R
.

Note that, by
[
31
, Lemma 4.1]
,
v\mapsto v(\mathfrak{m})
and
v\mapsto v(\mathfrak{q})
are continuous on
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}
.
Define

\displaystyle\Phi:

\displaystyle\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})\longrightarrow\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m}),\quad\Phi(v):=\frac{1}{v(\mathfrak{m})}\,v,\text{ and }

\displaystyle\Psi:

\displaystyle\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m})\longrightarrow\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(q),\quad\Psi(w):=\frac{1}{w(\mathfrak{q})}\,w.

These maps are well-defined since
\mathfrak{q}
is
\mathfrak{m}
-primary, so
v(\mathfrak{m})>0
and
v(\mathfrak{q})>0
for
all
v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}
. Moreover, for each
g\in R
one has

(\Phi(v))(g)=\frac{v(g)}{v(\mathfrak{m})},\quad(\Psi(w))(g)=\frac{w(g)}{w(\mathfrak{q})},

so
\Phi
and
\Psi
are continuous. It is immediate that
\Phi
and
\Psi
are inverse
to each other. Hence,
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
is homeomorphic to the compact space
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m})

and, therefore,
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
is compact.

Finally, by definition of the topology on
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
, the map
v\mapsto v(g)
is
continuous for every
g\in R
. Thus, for a finitely generated ideal
I\subseteq R
, the map

v\mapsto v(I)
is continuous. In particular,

v(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})=\inf_{p\geq 1}\frac{v(\mathfrak{a}_{p})}{p}

is an infimum of continuous functions. It follows that
v\mapsto v(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})
is upper
semicontinuous on
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
.
∎

Theorem 5.14

(Attainment and valuative equality for linearly bounded filtrations)
.

Let
R
be a Noetherian local domain.
Let
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}},{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}
be filtrations such that there exists an
\mathfrak{m}
-primary ideal

\mathfrak{q}
and constants
c,C,d,D>0
with

\mathfrak{q}^{\lceil cp\rceil}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}_{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{q}^{\lfloor Cp\rfloor}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{q}^{\lceil dp\rceil}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}_{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{q}^{\lfloor Dp\rfloor}\quad\text{for all }p\geq 1.

Then, the supremum in the valuative formula

\sup_{v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})}\frac{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}

is finite and is attained by some valuation
v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
.

Suppose, in addition, that the functions

v\longmapsto\frac{v(\mathfrak{a}_{p})}{p}\quad\text{and}\quad v\longmapsto\frac{v(\mathfrak{b}_{p})}{p}

converge
uniformly
on
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
to

v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
and
v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
, respectively.
Then,

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\ =\ \sup_{v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})}\frac{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})},

and the right-hand side is attained by some valuation in
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
.

Proof.

By the linear boundedness assumptions,

v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\leq C\,v(\mathfrak{q}),\quad v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})\geq d\,v(\mathfrak{q})

for all
v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}
. In particular, on the compact slice
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
one has

v(\mathfrak{q})=1
and, therefore,
v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\leq C
and
v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})\geq d>0
.
Set

s:=\sup_{v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})}\frac{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}<\infty.

By definition, there exists a sequence
\{v_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
such that
s=\lim\limits_{j\rightarrow\infty}\frac{v_{j}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v_{j}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}.
Since
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
is compact, by
Lemma

5.13
, a subsequence
v_{j_{k}}
of this sequence converges to an element
v_{*}\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
. The upper semicontinuity in
Lemma

5.13
then gives

v_{*}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\geq\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}v_{j_{k}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\text{ and }v_{*}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})\geq\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}v_{j_{k}}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})\geq d.

For any
\eta>0
, set

E_{\eta}=\{v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q}):v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})\geq v_{*}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})-\eta\}.

It follows from
Lemma

5.13
, that
v\mapsto v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
is an upper semicontinuous function. This implies that
E_{\eta}
is a closed subset or, equivalently, its complement in
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
is an open subset. We then conclude, since
v_{j_{k}}\longrightarrow v_{*}
, that
v_{j_{k}}\in E_{\eta}
for
k\gg 1
. Therefore, for
k\gg 1
,

s=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{v_{j_{k}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v_{j_{k}}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}\leq\frac{\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}v_{j_{k}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v_{*}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})-\eta}\leq\frac{v_{*}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v_{*}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})-\eta}.

This inequality holds for any
\eta>0
. Hence, by taking
\eta\rightarrow 0^{+}
, we have

s\leq\frac{v_{*}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v_{*}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}\leq s.

This forces the desired equality
s=\frac{v_{*}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v_{*}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}.

Assume now that
\frac{v(\mathfrak{a}_{p})}{p}\to v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
and
\frac{v(\mathfrak{b}_{p})}{p}\to v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
uniformly on

\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
. Let
\lambda>s
. By definition of
s
, we have

v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\leq s\,v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
for all
v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
.
Thus, for all
v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
,

\lambda v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})-v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\ \geq\ (\lambda-s)\,v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})\ \geq\ (\lambda-s)d\ =:\ \delta.

Choose
\varepsilon>0
with
\varepsilon<\min\{d/2,\ \delta/(\lambda+1)\}
.
Then, for every
v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
one has
v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})-\varepsilon>0
and

v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})+\varepsilon\leq\lambda v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})-\delta+\varepsilon=\lambda(v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})-\epsilon)-\underbrace{(\delta-(\lambda+1)\epsilon)}_{=:\delta^{\prime}>0},

so

\frac{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})+\varepsilon}{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})-\varepsilon}\leq\lambda-\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})-\epsilon}\leq\lambda-\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{d-\epsilon}<\lambda\quad\text{for all }v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q}).

Set

M:=\sup_{v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})}\frac{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})+\varepsilon}{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})-\varepsilon},

so
M\leq\lambda-\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{d-\epsilon}<\lambda
.

By uniform convergence, there exists
N
such that for all
p\geq N
and all
v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
,

\left|\frac{v(\mathfrak{a}_{p})}{p}-v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\right|\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\left|\frac{v(\mathfrak{b}_{p})}{p}-v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})\right|\leq\varepsilon.

Choose integers
P\geq N
and
Q\geq N
with
M\leq\frac{Q}{P}<\lambda.

Claim.
For all integers
t\geq 1
one has

\mathfrak{b}_{Qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{Pt}}.

Proof of the claim.

For
t\geq 1
,
Pt\geq N
and
Qt\geq N
, so for all
v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
,

\frac{v(\mathfrak{a}_{Pt})}{Pt}\leq v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})+\varepsilon,\quad\frac{v(\mathfrak{b}_{Qt})}{Qt}\geq v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})-\varepsilon.

Therefore, for every
v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
and every
t\geq 1
,

\frac{v(\mathfrak{b}_{Qt})}{Pt}=\frac{Q}{P}\cdot\frac{v(\mathfrak{b}_{Qt})}{Qt}\ \geq\ \frac{Q}{P}\bigl(v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})-\varepsilon\bigr)\ \geq\ M\bigl(v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})-\varepsilon\bigr)\ \geq\ v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})+\varepsilon\ \geq\ \frac{v(\mathfrak{a}_{Pt})}{Pt}.

Multiplying by
Pt
gives

v(\mathfrak{b}_{Qt})\geq v(\mathfrak{a}_{Pt})\quad\text{for all }v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})\text{ and all }t\geq 1.

Now let
w
be any divisorial valuation centered at
\mathfrak{m}
. Since
w(\mathfrak{q})>0
, the normalized valuation

\tilde{w}:=w/w(\mathfrak{q})
lies in
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
, so the previous inequality applied to
\tilde{w}
yields

w(\mathfrak{b}_{Qt})\geq w(\mathfrak{a}_{Pt})
for all
t\geq 1
.
By the valuative criterion for integral closure (
Lemma

5.2
), this implies

\mathfrak{b}_{Qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{Pt}}
for all
t\geq 1
, proving the claim.

\square

By the claim,
\frac{Q}{P}
is admissible
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
, and so

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\leq\frac{Q}{P}<\lambda.

Since
\lambda>s
was arbitrary, we obtain
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\leq s
.

On the other hand,
Theorem

5.6
gives
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\geq s
.
Thus,
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=s
, and the right-hand side is attained by the first part of the theorem.
∎

Example 5.15
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})=k[[x,y]]
and
\mathfrak{m}=(x,y)
.
Define two filtrations
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
and
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
by

\mathfrak{a}_{p}:=\big(x^{i}y^{j}\in R\mid i+2j\geq 3p\big),\quad\mathfrak{b}_{p}:=\big(x^{i}y^{j}\in R\mid 2i+j\geq 5p\big).

It can be seen that, for
p\geq 1
,
\mathfrak{a}_{p}
is integrally closed, and

\mathfrak{m}^{3p}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}_{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{p},\quad\mathfrak{m}^{5p}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}_{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{p}.

It can also be checked that the functions
v\mapsto v(\mathfrak{a}_{p})/p
and
v\mapsto v(\mathfrak{b}_{p})/p
converge uniformly to
v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
and
v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
, respectively, on the space of normalized real valuations of
\Frac(R)
centered at
\mathfrak{m}
.

We claim that

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\frac{6}{5}.

Indeed, fix
p,q
with
q/p\geq 6/5
and let
t\geq 1
.
For any monomial
x^{i}y^{j}\in\mathfrak{b}_{qt}
we have
2i+j\geq 5qt
.
Minimizing
i+2j
subject to
2i+j\geq 5qt
and
i,j\geq 0
yields

i+2j\geq\frac{5}{2}qt\geq\frac{5}{2}\cdot\frac{6}{5}pt=3pt,

and so
x^{i}y^{j}\in\mathfrak{a}_{pt}
.
Thus,
\mathfrak{b}_{qt}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}_{pt}=\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}
for all
t
, showing
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\leq 6/5
.

Conversely, assume
q/p<6/5
and set
m_{t}=\lceil\frac{5}{2}qt\rceil
.
Then,
x^{m_{t}}\in\mathfrak{b}_{qt}
since
2m_{t}\geq 5qt
.
However,
x^{m_{t}}\in\mathfrak{a}_{pt}
would require
m_{t}\geq 3pt
, which fails for all
t\gg 1

since
\frac{5}{2}q<3p
.
Hence,
\mathfrak{b}_{qt}\nsubseteq\mathfrak{a}_{pt}=\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}
for
t\gg 1
, proving
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\geq 6/5
, giving the claimed value of
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
.

Furthermore, consider the monomial valuation
v
defined by
v(x)=1
,
v(y)=2
.
Then,
v(\mathfrak{m})=1
,
v(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})=3
, and
v(\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet})=5/2
, so

\frac{v(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})}{v(\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet})}=\frac{6}{5}=\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}).

Thus, the valuative supremum is attained by
v
, as predicted by Theorem
5.14
.

The following theorem shows that the valuation attaining supremum value in
Theorem

5.14
is not necessarily divisorial, indicating that focusing on divisorial valuations may not be sufficient to study Łojasiewicz exponents in general.

Theorem 5.16

(Non-divisorial attainment in dimension two)
.

Let
R=\mathbb{C}[[x,y]]
with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}=(x,y)
, and set
\mathfrak{q}=\mathfrak{m}
.
Then, there exist filtrations
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
and
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}

satisfying the linear boundedness hypotheses of Theorem
5.14

such that

\sup_{v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})}\frac{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}

is attained by a valuation
v_{*}\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
which is
not
divisorial,
and moreover
no divisorial valuation
in
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
attains this supremum.

Proof.

We work on the normalized slice
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m})=\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{q})
, so
v(\mathfrak{m})=1
for all

v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m})
. We shall also use notations and terminology from Favre–Jonsson
[
16
]
monograph.

Step 1: a non-divisorial end and divisorial approximants.

By Favre–Jonsson’s classification of the valuative tree of
R=\mathbb{C}[[x,y]]
in
[
16
]
,
there exist non-divisorial
ends
(for instance
curve
valuations or
infinitely
singular
valuations).
Fix such an end valuation
\nu\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m})
.
Then,
\nu
is not divisorial, and there exists an increasing sequence of divisorial
valuations

\nu_{1}\prec\nu_{2}\prec\cdots\prec\nu_{i}\prec\cdots\prec\nu

converging to
\nu
in
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m})
, where
\prec
denotes the tree order.
We use the fact that the
skewness
function
\alpha(\cdot)
on the valuative tree is
strictly increasing along any nontrivial segment
(see
[
16
]
).

Step 2: realizing
\alpha(v\wedge\nu_{i})
as
v(J_{i})
.

For valuations
v,\mu\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m})
, Favre–Jonsson
[
16
]
define the intersection product by

v\cdot\mu:=\alpha(v\wedge\mu),

and for fixed
\mu
the associated extremal
tree potential
is

g_{\mu}(v):=v\cdot\mu=\alpha(v\wedge\mu)
.
If
\mu
is
divisorial
, then it was proved (see the proof of
[
17
, Prop. 5.15]
; although this was written for the ring
\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}
of convergent formal power series, the argument applies verbatim to the formal power series ring
\mathbb{C}[[x,y]]
) that there exist irreducible
elements
\phi_{\mu,1},\phi_{\mu,2}\in\mathfrak{m}
such that

g_{\mu}(v)=\min\{v(\phi_{\mu,1}),v(\phi_{\mu,2})\}\quad\text{for all }v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m}).

Applying this to
\mu=\nu_{i}
, we may choose irreducibles

\phi_{i,1},\phi_{i,2}\in\mathfrak{m}
with

\alpha(v\wedge\nu_{i})=\min\{v(\phi_{i,1}),v(\phi_{i,2})\}\quad\forall\,v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m}).

Set

J_{i}:=(\phi_{i,1},\phi_{i,2})\subseteq R.

Since
\phi_{i,1},\phi_{i,2}
are distinct irreducible germs through the origin,
the ideal
J_{i}
has height
2
in the regular local ring
R=\mathbb{C}[[x,y]]
and is therefore

\mathfrak{m}
–primary.
For all
v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m})
we have the exact identity

(5.4)

v(J_{i})=\alpha(v\wedge\nu_{i}).

Set
A_{i}:=\alpha(\nu_{i})\in(0,\infty)
.

Step 3: normalized coefficients and definition of the filtrations.

Set

c_{i}:=\frac{2^{-i}}{A_{i}}.

Since
\alpha(\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{\mathfrak{m}})=1
on the normalized slice and
\alpha
is increasing,
we have
A_{i}\geq 1
and so
c_{i}\leq 2^{-i}
.
Using (
5.4
), for all
v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m})
,

0\leq c_{i}\,v(J_{i})=c_{i}\,\alpha(v\wedge\nu_{i})\leq c_{i}\,\alpha(\nu_{i})=2^{-i}.

Define
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}:=\mathfrak{m}^{\bullet}
, i.e.,
\mathfrak{b}_{p}=\mathfrak{m}^{p}
.
Define
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
by

(5.5)

\mathfrak{a}_{p}:=\mathfrak{m}^{p}\cdot\prod_{i\geq 1}J_{i}^{\lfloor p\,c_{i}\rfloor}.

Since
c_{i}\leq 2^{-i}
, we have
\lfloor p\,c_{i}\rfloor=0
for all
i\gg 1
, so the product
is finite for each
p
.
In particular, each
\mathfrak{a}_{p}
is
\mathfrak{m}
–primary.
Observe further that, for all
p,p^{\prime}\geq 1
and all
i
,
\lfloor(p+p^{\prime})c_{i}\rfloor\geq\lfloor pc_{i}\rfloor+\lfloor p^{\prime}c_{i}\rfloor.

Therefore,

\mathfrak{a}_{p}\mathfrak{a}_{p^{\prime}}=\mathfrak{m}^{p+p^{\prime}}\cdot\prod_{i}J_{i}^{\lfloor pc_{i}\rfloor+\lfloor p^{\prime}c_{i}\rfloor}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{p+p^{\prime}}\cdot\prod_{i}J_{i}^{\lfloor(p+p^{\prime})c_{i}\rfloor}=\mathfrak{a}_{p+p^{\prime}}.

Moreover,
\mathfrak{a}_{p+1}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}_{p}
, so
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}
is a filtration of ideals.

Step 4: computation of
v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
.

Fix
v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m})
.
By definition, we have

v(\mathfrak{a}_{p})=v(\mathfrak{m}^{p})+\sum_{i\geq 1}\lfloor p\,c_{i}\rfloor\,v(J_{i})=p\,v(\mathfrak{m})+\sum_{i\geq 1}\lfloor p\,c_{i}\rfloor\,v(J_{i}).

Dividing by
p
,

\frac{v(\mathfrak{a}_{p})}{p}=v(\mathfrak{m})+\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{\lfloor p\,c_{i}\rfloor}{p}\,v(J_{i}).

For each
i
,
\frac{\lfloor p\,c_{i}\rfloor}{p}\to c_{i}
as
p\to\infty
, and

0\leq\frac{\lfloor p\,c_{i}\rfloor}{p}\,v(J_{i})\leq c_{i}\,v(J_{i})\leq 2^{-i}.

Since
\sum_{i\geq 1}2^{-i}<\infty
, dominated convergence applies and yields

v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\lim_{p\to\infty}\frac{v(\mathfrak{a}_{p})}{p}=v(\mathfrak{m})+\sum_{i\geq 1}c_{i}\,v(J_{i})=v(\mathfrak{m})+\sum_{i\geq 1}c_{i}\,\alpha(v\wedge\nu_{i}).

Since
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\mathfrak{m}^{\bullet}
and
v(\mathfrak{m})=1
on
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m})
, we have

v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})/v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})=v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
on this slice.

Step 5: unique maximizer and non-attainment by divisorial valuations.

Because
\nu\wedge\nu_{i}=\nu_{i}
for all
i
,

\nu({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=1+\sum_{i\geq 1}c_{i}\,\alpha(\nu_{i})=1+\sum_{i\geq 1}2^{-i}=2.

Let
v\in\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m})
with
v\neq\nu
.
Since
\nu
is an
end
and
\nu_{i}\uparrow\nu
, there exists
i_{0}
such that

v\not\succeq\nu_{i_{0}}
; otherwise
v\succeq\nu_{i}
for all
i
and so
v\succeq\nu
,
forcing
v=\nu
.
For this
i_{0}
we have
v\wedge\nu_{i_{0}}\prec\nu_{i_{0}}
, and strict monotonicity of

\alpha
along segments implies

\alpha(v\wedge\nu_{i_{0}})<\alpha(\nu_{i_{0}}).

For all
i
,
\alpha(v\wedge\nu_{i})\leq\alpha(\nu_{i})
, with strict inequality at
i=i_{0}
.
Therefore,

v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=1+\sum_{i\geq 1}c_{i}\,\alpha(v\wedge\nu_{i})<1+\sum_{i\geq 1}c_{i}\,\alpha(\nu_{i})=2=\nu({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}).

Thus,
\nu
is the
unique
maximizer of
v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
on
\Val_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m})
and, hence, of

v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})/v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
.
Since
\nu
is not divisorial, no divisorial valuation attains the supremum.

Step 6: linear boundedness.

Since
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\mathfrak{m}^{\bullet}
, it is linearly bounded with respect to
\mathfrak{q}=\mathfrak{m}
.
For
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}
, the definition (
5.5
) gives
\mathfrak{m}^{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}_{p}
for all
p
.
Using
J_{i}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}
and
\sum_{i}c_{i}\leq 1
, we obtain

\mathfrak{a}_{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{p}\cdot\prod_{i}\mathfrak{m}^{\lfloor p\,c_{i}\rfloor}=\mathfrak{m}^{p+\sum_{i}\lfloor p\,c_{i}\rfloor}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{p+\lfloor p\sum_{i}c_{i}\rfloor}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{2p}.

Hence,
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}
is linearly bounded with respect to
\mathfrak{q}=\mathfrak{m}
, and the hypotheses of
Theorem
5.14
are satisfied. This completes the proof.
∎

5.6.
Finite maximizers in the ideal case

In the ideal case, i.e., when the two graded families of ideals are those of ordinary powers of given two ideals, we recover the classical stronger statement that the maximum is attained by finitely many Rees valuations.

Theorem 5.17

(Finite set of maximizers for ideals)
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be an excellent Noetherian local domain.
Let
\mathfrak{a}
be an ideal and let
\mathfrak{b}
be any proper ideal with
\mathfrak{b}\neq 0
.
Then,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{divisorial valuation }\\
v\text{ centered at }\mathfrak{m}\end{subarray}}\frac{v(\mathfrak{a})}{v(\mathfrak{b})}

and, if finite, is a maximum and achieved by one of the Rees valuations of
\mathfrak{a}
.
In particular, if finite,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})
is computed by finitely many divisorial valuations.

Proof.

This is a classical consequence of Rees’s theory of valuations (cf.
[
30
,
49
]
). It also follows from

Theorem

5.9
as we shall illustrate below.

Since
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{a})=\bigoplus_{p\geq 0}\mathfrak{a}^{p}T^{p}
is the usual Rees algebra of
\mathfrak{a}
, the
hypothesis of finite generation is automatic. Hence, by
Theorem

5.9
, there exists a finite set of
divisorial valuations
\mathcal{V}(\mathfrak{a})=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{r}\}
, arising from prime divisors on
the normalization of
\Proj\overline{\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{a})}
, such that for any filtration

{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}
, with
v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})>0
, one has

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}(\mathfrak{a}^{\bullet})=\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\frac{v_{i}(\mathfrak{a})}{v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}.

Specializing to
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{b}^{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
, noticing that
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}
so
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}
is linearly bounded from below (as in
Remark

5.10
), yields the desired finite–maximum formula.

Finally, the valuations coming from the codimension–one components of the exceptional
locus of the normalized blowup
\Proj\overline{\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{a})}\to\Spec R
are precisely
the classical Rees valuations of
\mathfrak{a}
; see
[
30
, Chapter 10]
. Therefore, the maximum above may be taken over the
Rees valuations of
\mathfrak{a}
, as claimed.
∎

Example 5.18
.

Consider
\mathfrak{a}=(x^{5},x^{2}y^{3},y^{7})\subseteq R=\mathbb{C}[[x,y]]
. The Newton polyhedron of
\mathfrak{a}
has two compact faces with inward normals
(1,1)
and
(3,1)
. These correspond to Rees valuations
v_{(1,1)}
and
v_{(3,1)}
of
\mathfrak{a}
.
The values of interest are:

v_{(1,1)}(\mathfrak{a})=5,v_{(3,1)}(\mathfrak{a})=7,v(\mathfrak{m})=1.

Hence,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{a})=7
is computed by a Rees valuations of
\mathfrak{a}
.

Part IV
Structural Inequalities and Dualities

In this part, we derive structural inequalities for
Łojasiewicz exponents and related invariants as consequences of the valuative
framework developed in Part
III
.

6.
Mixed multiplicities, Jacobian polygons, and Teissier–type inequalities

This section links Łojasiewicz-type containment thresholds to mixed multiplicities and Teissier’s convex-geometric inequalities. We restate Teissier–Rees–Sharp/Alexandrov–Fenchel type bounds for mixed multiplicities of ideals (
Theorem

6.3
). We then introduce mixed Łojasiewicz ratios and establish monotonicity/concavity properties that organize these inequalities (
Proposition

6.6
,
Proposition

6.11
). Finally, we specialize to Jacobian ideals and the algebraic avatars of Milnor and Tjurina numbers, obtaining Teissier-type bounds for the gradient Łojasiewicz exponent (
Theorem

6.13
), and we close with an extension principle that generalizes statements for ideals to filtrations.

6.1.
Hilbert–Samuel multiplicity and mixed multiplicities

Throughout this section,
(R,\mathfrak{m})
is a Noetherian local ring of dimension
d
. Assume, in addition, that
\dim\text{Nil}(\widehat{R})<d
, where
\text{Nil}(\cdot)
represents the nil-radical of the ring (see
[
12
]
). This condition is satisfied, for instance, if
R
is analytically unramified.

We recall the mixed multiplicities of ideals.
Let
I_{1},\dots,I_{r}\subseteq R
be ideals such that
I_{1}+\dots+I_{r}
is
\mathfrak{m}
–primary (equivalently,
I_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots I_{r}^{n_{r}}
has finite colength for all large
(n_{i})
).
Then, the function

(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})\longmapsto\operatorname{\mathrm{length}}\!\left(R/(I_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots I_{r}^{n_{r}})\right)\quad(n_{i}\in\mathbb{N})

agrees with a polynomial of total degree
d
for all
n_{i}\gg 0
.
The degree–
d
part of this polynomial can be uniquely written as

\sum_{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{r}=d}\frac{e(I_{1}^{[a_{1}]},\dots,I_{r}^{[a_{r}]})}{a_{1}!\cdots a_{r}!}\,n_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots n_{r}^{a_{r}},

where the coefficients
e(I_{1}^{[a_{1}]},\dots,I_{r}^{[a_{r}]})\in\mathbb{N}
are the
mixed multiplicities
of
I_{1},\dots,I_{r}
.

We will primarily use the two-ideal case
(I,J)
, where we write

e(I^{[i]},J^{[d-i]})\quad(0\leq i\leq d).

The following are standard facts:

(a)

(
Scaling
) For
p,q\geq 1
,

e((I^{p})^{[i]},(J^{q})^{[d-i]})=p^{\,i}q^{\,d-i}\,e(I^{[i]},J^{[d-i]}).

(b)

(
Monotonicity
) If
I\subseteq I^{\prime}
and
J\subseteq J^{\prime}
are
\mathfrak{m}
–primary, then

e(I^{[i]},J^{[d-i]})\ \geq\ e((I^{\prime})^{[i]},(J^{\prime})^{[d-i]})\quad\text{for all }i.

(c)

(
Integral closure invariance
) If
\overline{I}=\overline{I^{\prime}}
and

\overline{J}=\overline{J^{\prime}}
, then

e(I^{[i]},J^{[d-i]})=e((I^{\prime})^{[i]},(J^{\prime})^{[d-i]})\quad\text{for all }i.

Definition 6.1

(Mixed multiplicities of filtrations)
.

Let
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
and
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
be graded families of
\mathfrak{m}
-primary ideals. For
1\leq i\leq d
, set

e_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}^{[i]},{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}^{[d-i]})=\limsup_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e(\mathfrak{a}_{p}^{[i]},\mathfrak{b}_{p}^{[d-i]})}{n^{d}}.

It was shown by Cid-Ruiz–Montaño
[
10
]
that, under the condition that
\dim\text{Nil}(\widehat{R})<d
,
e_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}^{[i]},{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}^{[d-i]})
exists as an actual limit. When
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{m}^{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
, we write
e_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
for
e({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}^{[i]},{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}^{[d-i]})
.

Definition 6.2

(Mixed Łojasiewicz rations)
.

For a graded family
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}
of
\mathfrak{m}
-primary ideals, set

\Lambda_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\frac{e_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{e_{i-1}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})},\ 1\leq i\leq d.

6.2.
Teissier–Rees–Sharp and Alexandrov–Fenchel inequalities

We begin with the Alexandrov–Fenchel type inequalities for mixed multiplicities in the form
best suited for graded families (or filtrations).

Theorem 6.3

(Teissier–Rees–Sharp inequality)
.

Assume that
R
is quasi–unmixed.
Let
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
and
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
be graded families such that

\mathfrak{a}_{p}+\mathfrak{b}_{q}
is
\mathfrak{m}
–primary for all
p,q\geq 1
.
Then, for all
p,q\geq 1
, and
1\leq i\leq d-1
, we have

e\!\left(\mathfrak{a}_{p}^{[i]},\mathfrak{b}_{q}^{[d-i]}\right)^{2}\ \geq\ e\!\left(\mathfrak{a}_{p}^{[i-1]},\mathfrak{b}_{q}^{[d-i+1]}\right)\,e\!\left(\mathfrak{a}_{p}^{[i+1]},\mathfrak{b}_{q}^{[d-i-1]}\right).

Equivalently, for fixed
p,q
, the sequence
i\ \longmapsto\ e\!\left(\mathfrak{a}_{p}^{[i]},\mathfrak{b}_{q}^{[d-i]}\right)

is log-concave.

Proof.

The stated inequality is precisely the Alexandrov-Fenchel type inequality for mixed multiplicities. It was first established by Teissier
[
50
]
in the
\mathfrak{m}
-primary case via Minkowski inequalities for multiplicities, and was subsequently extended by Rees and Sharp
[
46
]
to the general situation where
\mathfrak{a}_{p}+\mathfrak{b}_{q}
is
\mathfrak{m}
-primary, under the quasi-unmixed hypothesis of
R
.
∎

6.3.
Containments
\Rightarrow
mixed-multiplicity inequalities

Following the foundational work of Teissier on mixed multiplicities, several authors – most notably Bivià-Ausina and Encinas
[
1
,
3
]
– have related Lojasiewicz-type invariants to mixed multiplicity data, typically through inequalities involving integral closures of powers of ideals. The proposition below isolates the underlying numerical mechanism in a containment-theoretic form.

Proposition 6.4

(Containment implies mixed-multiplicity bounds)
.

Let
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
and
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
be
\mathfrak{m}
–primary graded families.
Fix
p,q\geq 1
and assume that

\mathfrak{b}_{qt}\ \subseteq\ \overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}\quad\text{for all }t\gg 1.

Then, for every
1\leq i\leq d
and every
t\gg 1
, one has

e\!\left((\mathfrak{b}_{qt})^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]}\right)\geq e\!\left((\mathfrak{a}_{pt})^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]}\right).

Consequently,

e_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})\ \geq\ \left(\frac{p}{q}\right)^{\!i}\,e_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}),

and in particular,

\frac{q}{p}\ \geq\ \left(\frac{e_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{e_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}\right)^{1/i}\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq d.

Proof.

Fix
t\gg 1
such that
\mathfrak{b}_{qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}
.
By monotonicity and integral-closure invariance standard facts,

e((\mathfrak{b}_{qt})^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]})\ \geq\ e((\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}})^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]})=e((\mathfrak{a}_{pt})^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]}),

which proves the first displayed inequality.

The asymptotic statements is then obtained by dividing both sides of this inequality by
(qt)^{i}
and taking the limit as
t\to\infty
.
∎

Corollary 6.5
.

Let
I,J
be
\mathfrak{m}
–primary ideals in
R
, and let
p,q\geq 1
.
If
J^{\,q}\subseteq\overline{I^{\,p}}
, then for every
1\leq i\leq d
one has

q^{\,i}\,e(J^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]})\ \geq\ p^{\,i}\,e(I^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]}),

equivalently,

\frac{q}{p}\ \geq\ \left(\frac{e(I^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]})}{e(J^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]})}\right)^{1/i}.

Proof.

This inequality is a classical consequence of Teissier’s mixed-multiplicity theory and appears in the algebraic form in Rees–Sharp; see
[
50
,
46
]
. It is also a direct consequence of
Proposition

6.4
.
∎

An immediate consequence of
Corollary

6.5
, taking
i=d
, gives the following lower bound:

\mathcal{L}_{J}(I)\ \geq\ \left(\frac{e(I)}{e(J)}\right)^{1/d}.

6.4.
Mixed Łojasiewicz ratios and Teissier-type monotonicity

The Alexandrov–Fenchel inequalities imply strong structure for certain ratios of mixed
multiplicities. These ratios play the role of “mixed slopes” in the classical theory.

Proposition 6.6

(Log-concavity and monotonicity of
\Lambda_{i}
)
.

Let
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
be an
\mathfrak{m}
–primary graded family.
Then, mixed multiplicity ratios satisfy

\Lambda_{1}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\ \geq\ \Lambda_{2}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\ \geq\ \cdots\ \geq\ \Lambda_{d}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}).

Proof.

Fix
p,t
and apply
Theorem

6.3
to the pair
(\mathfrak{a}_{pt},\mathfrak{m})
, we get

e((\mathfrak{a}_{pt})^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]})^{2}\ \geq\ e((\mathfrak{a}_{pt})^{[i-1]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i+1]})\cdot e((\mathfrak{a}_{pt})^{[i+1]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i-1]})\ \forall\ 1\leq i\leq d-1.

It follows that

\Lambda_{i}(\mathfrak{a}_{pt})\geq\Lambda_{i+1}(\mathfrak{a}_{pt})
. Dividing both sides of this inequality by appropriate powers of
pt
and taking the limit along
p\to\infty
then give

\Lambda_{1}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\geq\Lambda_{2}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\geq\cdots\geq\Lambda_{d}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
as claimed.
∎

Corollary 6.7

(Teissier monotonicity)
.

Let
I
be
\mathfrak{m}
–primary. Then, the sequence
(\Lambda_{i}(I))_{i=1}^{d}
is nonincreasing:

\Lambda_{1}(I)\ \geq\ \Lambda_{2}(I)\ \geq\ \cdots\ \geq\ \Lambda_{d}(I).

Equivalently, the sequence
i\longmapsto e(I^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]})
is log-concave.

Proof.

Apply
Proposition

6.6
to the power family
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{I^{p}\}
. This monotonicity/log-concavity was established by Teissier in
[
50
]
.
∎

6.5.
Jacobian ideals, Milnor and Tjurina numbers (algebraic viewpoint)

We now explain how classical “Jacobian polygon” data can be encoded algebraically
via mixed multiplicities.

Assume in this subsection that
R=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^{d},0}
and let

f\in\mathfrak{m}
define an isolated singularity at the origin.
Let
J(f)\subseteq R
be the Jacobian ideal generated by
\partial f/\partial x_{1},\dots,\partial f/\partial x_{d}
.

Definition 6.8

(Milnor and Tjurina numbers)
.

Define the
Milnor number
and
Tjurina number
by

\mu(f):=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}R/J(f),\quad\tau(f):=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}R/(f,J(f)).

The next object is the algebraic substitute for Teissier’s Jacobian polygon.

Definition 6.9

(Jacobian mixed multiplicities and polygon)
.

For
0\leq i\leq d
set

m_{i}(f):=e\bigl((f)^{[d-i]},\,J(f)^{[i]}\bigr).

The
Jacobian polygon

\text{JP}(f)
of
f
is the lower convex hull in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
of the points

(0,m_{0}(f)),\ (1,m_{1}(f)),\ \dots,\ (d,m_{d}(f)).

Remark 6.10
.

In Teissier’s work, the slopes and vertices of the Jacobian polygon encode polar
multiplicities and vanishing rates. In our framework, the same numerical data are
captured by the mixed multiplicities
m_{i}(f)
, which are defined for any Noetherian
local ring once
(f)+J(f)
is
\mathfrak{m}
–primary.

Proposition 6.11

(Concavity of the Jacobian polygon)
.

Let
f
define an isolated singularity. Then, the sequence
i\mapsto\log m_{i}(f)
is concave,
equivalently

m_{i}(f)^{2}\ \geq\ m_{i-1}(f)\,m_{i+1}(f)\quad(1\leq i\leq d-1).

Proof.

Apply
Theorem

6.3
to the pair of ideals
((f),J(f))
, noticing that
(f)+J(f)
is
\mathfrak{m}
–primary.
∎

Corollary 6.12
.

Let
f
define an isolated singularity, and let
m_{i}(f)
be as in
Definition
6.9
. Then, the sequence of successive ratios

\frac{m_{1}(f)}{m_{0}(f)},\ \frac{m_{2}(f)}{m_{1}(f)},\ \dots,\ \frac{m_{d}(f)}{m_{d-1}(f)}

is nonincreasing. Equivalently, for
1\leq i\leq d-1
one has

\frac{m_{i}(f)}{m_{i-1}(f)}\ \geq\ \frac{m_{i+1}(f)}{m_{i}(f)}.

Proof.

By
Proposition

6.11
, we have

m_{i}(f)^{2}\ \geq\ m_{i-1}(f)\,m_{i+1}(f)\quad(1\leq i\leq d-1).

Dividing both sides by
m_{i-1}(f)\,m_{i}(f)
(which is positive) gives

\frac{m_{i}(f)}{m_{i-1}(f)}\ \geq\ \frac{m_{i+1}(f)}{m_{i}(f)}.\qed

6.6.
Teissier-type bounds for the gradient Łojasiewicz exponent

We now connect
L(f,0):=\mathcal{L}^{an}_{\mathfrak{m}}(J(f))=\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{m}}(J(f))
to the mixed multiplicity data.
The lower bound in our next result, when
i=d
, recovers the classical estimate in terms of
e(J(f))
, while the full family of inequalities reflects finer mixed-multiplicity data of the Jacobian ideal.

Theorem 6.13

(Mixed-multiplicity lower bounds for
L(f,0)
)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^{d},0}
define an isolated singularity.
Then, for each
1\leq i\leq d
,

L(f,0)\ \geq\ \left(\frac{e\bigl(J(f)^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]}\bigr)}{e\bigl(\mathfrak{m}^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]}\bigr)}\right)^{1/i}.

In particular,

L(f,0)\geq\ \left(\frac{e(J(f))}{e(\mathfrak{m})}\right)^{1/d}.

Proof.

For every
t\gg 1
, the rational number
\frac{\lceil t\,L(f,0)\rceil}{t}
is admissible for
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{m}}(J(f))
, and hence

\mathfrak{m}^{\lceil t\,L(f,0)\rceil}\ \subseteq\ \overline{J(f)^{\,t}}.

Apply
Corollary

6.5
with
(I,J)=(J(f),\mathfrak{m})
and
(p,q)=(t,\lceil tL(f,0)\rceil)
.
For each
1\leq i\leq d
we obtain

\lceil t\,L(f,0)\rceil^{\,i}\,e(\mathfrak{m}^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]})\ \geq\ t^{\,i}\,e(J(f)^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]}).

Divide by
t^{i}
and let
t\to\infty
to conclude

L(f,0)^{\,i}\,e(\mathfrak{m}^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]})\ \geq\ e(J(f)^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]}),

which is equivalent to the stated lower bound.
∎

Remark 6.14
.

Classical analytic estimates bounding
L(f,0)
from below by numerical invariants
(Milnor numbers, polar multiplicities, etc.) can be viewed as instances of

Theorem

6.13
together with identifications between those
invariants and mixed multiplicities; see
[
1
,
3
]
.

6.7.
Extension principle: from ideals to filtrations

We will restrict to the case when the filtration is asymptotically integral-closure-equivalent to powers of an
\mathfrak{m}
–primary ideal, i.e., exhibiting linearly bounded behavior as in
Theorem

5.14
.

Definition 6.15

(Linearly bounded)
.

A filtration
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}
of
\mathfrak{m}
–primary ideals is
linearly bounded

if there exists an
\mathfrak{m}
–primary ideal
I
and constants
c,C>0
such that

\overline{I^{\lceil cp\rceil}}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}\subseteq\overline{I^{\lfloor Cp\rfloor}}\quad\text{for all }p\geq 1.

Remark 6.16
.

Noetherian graded families (i.e., finitely generated Rees algebras) and many Newton-type
filtrations are linearly bounded.

Proposition 6.17

(Mixed multiplicities for linearly bounded filtrations)
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a Noetherian local ring of dimension
d
.
Let
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
and
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
be filtrations of
\mathfrak{m}
–primary ideals.
Assume that
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}
is linearly bounded by an
\mathfrak{m}
–primary ideal
I
with constants
c_{\mathfrak{a}},C_{\mathfrak{a}}>0

and that
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}
is linearly bounded by an
\mathfrak{m}
–primary ideal
J
with constants
c_{\mathfrak{b}},C_{\mathfrak{b}}>0
,
in the sense of
Definition

6.15
, i.e.,

\overline{I^{\lceil c_{\mathfrak{a}}p\rceil}}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}\subseteq\overline{I^{\lfloor C_{\mathfrak{a}}p\rfloor}},\quad\overline{J^{\lceil c_{\mathfrak{b}}q\rceil}}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{b}_{q}}\subseteq\overline{J^{\lfloor C_{\mathfrak{b}}q\rfloor}}\quad\text{for all }p,q\geq 1.

If
\mathfrak{b}_{q}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}
for some integers
p,q\geq 1
, then setting

\alpha:=\lceil c_{\mathfrak{b}}q\rceil,\quad\beta:=\lfloor C_{\mathfrak{a}}p\rfloor,

we have, for each
1\leq i\leq d
,

\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{i}\ \geq\ \frac{e\bigl(I^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]}\bigr)}{e\bigl(J^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]}\bigr)}.

Equivalently,

\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\ \geq\ \left(\frac{e\bigl(I^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]}\bigr)}{e\bigl(J^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]}\bigr)}\right)^{1/i}.

Proof.

Assume
\mathfrak{b}_{q}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}
. Then,
\overline{\mathfrak{b}_{q}}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}
.
By the linear boundedness hypotheses,

\overline{J^{\alpha}}\ \subseteq\ \overline{\mathfrak{b}_{q}}\ \subseteq\ \overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}\ \subseteq\ \overline{I^{\beta}}.

In particular,
J^{\alpha}\subseteq\overline{I^{\beta}}
.
Apply
Corollary

6.5
to the containment
J^{\alpha}\subseteq\overline{I^{\beta}}

(with
(I,J)
there replaced by
(I,J)
here and
(p,q)
replaced by
(\beta,\alpha)
):
for each
1\leq i\leq d
we obtain

\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\ \geq\ \left(\frac{e\bigl(I^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]}\bigr)}{e\bigl(J^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]}\bigr)}\right)^{1/i},

and raising both sides to the
i
–th power gives the stated inequality.
∎

Corollary 6.18

(Explicit slope bounds in terms of
q/p
)
.

In the setting of
Proposition

6.17
, fix
1\leq i\leq d
and set

r_{i}:=\left(\frac{e\!\left(I^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]}\right)}{e\!\left(J^{[i]},\mathfrak{m}^{[d-i]}\right)}\right)^{\!1/i}.

If
\mathfrak{b}_{q}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}
for some
p>1/C_{\mathfrak{a}}
and
q\geq 1
, then

(6.1)

\frac{q}{p}\ \geq\ \frac{C_{\mathfrak{a}}}{c_{\mathfrak{b}}}\,r_{i}\ -\ \frac{r_{i}+1}{c_{\mathfrak{b}}\,p}.

In particular, for any
\varepsilon>0
, if
p\geq\dfrac{r_{i}+1}{c_{\mathfrak{b}}\,\varepsilon}
then

\frac{q}{p}\ \geq\ \frac{C_{\mathfrak{a}}}{c_{\mathfrak{b}}}\,r_{i}-\varepsilon.

Proof.

Let
\alpha=\lceil c_{\mathfrak{b}}q\rceil
and
\beta=\lfloor C_{\mathfrak{a}}p\rfloor
as in

Proposition

6.17
. That proposition gives

\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\ \geq\ r_{i}.

Using
\alpha\leq c_{\mathfrak{b}}q+1
and
\beta\geq C_{\mathfrak{a}}p-1
(note that
p>1/C_{\mathfrak{a}}
guarantees
\beta\geq 0
),
inequality
(\ast)
implies

\frac{c_{\mathfrak{b}}q+1}{C_{\mathfrak{a}}p-1}\ \geq\ r_{i},

hence

c_{\mathfrak{b}}q+1\ \geq\ r_{i}(C_{\mathfrak{a}}p-1)=r_{i}C_{\mathfrak{a}}p-r_{i},

so

q\ \geq\ \frac{C_{\mathfrak{a}}}{c_{\mathfrak{b}}}\,r_{i}\,p\ -\ \frac{r_{i}+1}{c_{\mathfrak{b}}}.

Dividing by
p
gives (
6.1
), and the final statement follows immediately.
∎

7.
Log canonical thresholds and generic hyperplane sections

In this section we relate the Lojasiewicz exponent to another fundamental valuative threshold
invariant, the asymptotic log canonical threshold. Particularly, we compare the valuative descriptions of

\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
and
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
, and show that they satisfy the optimal bound
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\cdot\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\geq\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
(
Theorem

7.1
). This inequality complements the Teissier–type estimates established in Section
6
and identifies a natural equality regime governed by a common extremal valuation.
Finally, we establish a generic hyperplane–section principle for filtrations, showing that admissible containment ratios descend and that the associated sectional Łojasiewicz exponents are monotone under restriction along a general flag (
Corollary

7.6
).

7.1.
Valuative definition of the log canonical threshold

We shall work with a regular local ring
(R,\mathfrak{m})
of dimension
d
(essentially of finite type over a field of characteristic
0
). For an ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq R
, the
log canonical threshold
of
\mathfrak{a}
, denoted by
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}(\mathfrak{a})
, is an important invariant that has been much studied in both singularity theory and birational geometry (cf.
[
32
,
35
]
). Particularly, if
\mathfrak{a}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{s})
then

\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}(\mathfrak{a})=\sup\{\theta>0:\big(\sum_{i}|f_{i}|^{2}\big)^{-\theta}\text{ is locally integrable}\}.

Let
A(v)
denote the
log discrepancy
of a valuation
v
(see
[
5
,
32
,
35
]
for more details on log discrepancy and log canonical threshold of multiplier ideals). Then,
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}(\mathfrak{a})
is also given by

\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}(\mathfrak{a})=\inf_{v}\frac{A(v)}{v(\mathfrak{a})},

where
v
ranges over all divisorial valuations of
R
centered at
\mathfrak{m}
.

For a graded family
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
of ideals in
R
, the
asymptotic log canonical threshold
of
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}
, denoted by
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
, was defined and studied by Jonsson–Mustaţǎ
[
31
]
. Particularly,

\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\inf_{v}\frac{A(v)}{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})},

where
v
ranges over all divisorial valuations of
R
centered at
\mathfrak{m}
. It was observed in
[
31
]
that, in general, there may not exist a divisorial valuation
v
realizing
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
. On the other hand, it was also shown in
[
31
, Theorem A]
, that given any graded family
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}
, there is a real valuation
v
of
R
centered at
\mathfrak{m}
that computes
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
, i.e.,
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\frac{A(v)}{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}
.

7.2.
A sharp inequality:
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\cdot\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\geq\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})

We now prove the key “dual” inequality and isolate the rigidity mechanism. In the analytic setting, when
R=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^{d},0}
,
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}^{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
and
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{m}^{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
, this inequality was known by Bivià-Ausina–Fukui
[
4
]
. Our point is that the same inequality holds in greater generality and fits naturally into the valuative min-max framework developed in this paper. We also characterize the equality in the next section.

Theorem 7.1

(Sharp inequality)
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be regular local of dimension
d
. Let
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}
and
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}
be graded families of ideals in
R
. Then,

\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\cdot\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\geq\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}).

Particularly,
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{m}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\geq d
.
Equivalently,

\Theta({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}):=\frac{1}{d}\,\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{m}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\ \geq\ 1.

Proof.

By the valuative formulas and
Theorem

5.6
,

\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\inf_{v}\frac{A(v)}{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\geq\sup_{v}\frac{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})},

where
v
ranges over divisorial valuations centered at
\mathfrak{m}
.
Therefore,

\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\cdot\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\geq\Bigl(\inf_{v}\frac{A(v)}{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}\Bigr)\cdot\Bigl(\sup_{w}\frac{w({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{w({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}\Bigr)\ \geq\ \inf_{v}\Bigl(\frac{A(v)}{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}\cdot\frac{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}\Bigr)=\inf_{v}\frac{A(v)}{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}=\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}).

When
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{m}^{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
, we have
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{m}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\geq\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}(\mathfrak{m})
. On the other hand, since
R
is regular of dimension
d
, one has
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}(\mathfrak{m})=d
; see, for instance
[
35
, § 9]
.
Thus,
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{m}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\geq d
.
∎

7.3.
Generic hyperplane sections and the Łojasiewicz exponent

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a Noetherian local ring with infinite residue field.

Definition 7.2

(Eventual degree–one normality)
.

An
\mathfrak{m}
–primary filtration
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
is said to be

eventually degree–one normal
if there exists an integer
p_{0}\geq 1

such that

\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}=\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{\,p}}\quad\text{for all }p\geq p_{0}.

Remark 7.3

(Specialization of integral closure)
.

Let
I
be an ideal of height at least
2
in a Noetherian ring
R
which is normal,
locally equidimensional, universally catenary, and such that
R_{\mathrm{red}}

is locally analytically unramified.
The following statements hold:

(i)

(
[
28
, Theorem 2.1]
)
Integral closure is compatible with specialization by a generic element of
I
.

(ii)

(
[
26
, Proposition 3.7]
)
For a general element
x\in I
, one has

\overline{I^{n}}\,R/(x)=\overline{I^{n}R/(x)}\quad\text{for all }n\gg 0.

The following result is an application of specialization of integral closure to filtrations of ideals.

Theorem 7.4

(Stable–range commutation for
\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}
)
.

Assume that
R
satisfies the hypotheses of
Remark

7.3
.
Let
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}
be an
\mathfrak{m}
–primary filtration that is eventually degree-one normal, and set
I=\mathfrak{a}_{1}
.
Then, there exists a Zariski open dense subset
U\subseteq I/\mathfrak{m}I
such that
for every
x\in I
with class in
U
, and every integer
p\geq 1
, there exists an integer
t_{0}=t_{0}(p)
with the property that

\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}\,R^{\prime}=\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}R^{\prime}},\text{ for all }t\geq t_{0},\text{ where }R^{\prime}=R/(x).

Proof.

Fix
p\geq 1
.
By eventual degree–one normality, there exists
t_{1}(p)
such that

\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}=\overline{I^{pt}}
for all
t\geq t_{1}(p)
.
Since
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}
is graded,
I^{pt}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}_{pt}
, and so for such
t
,

I^{pt}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}_{pt}\subseteq\overline{I^{pt}}.

Let
x\in I
be general as in
Remark
7.3
(ii) and set
R^{\prime}=R/(x)
.
By
[
26
, Proposition 3.7]
, there exists
n_{0}
such that

\overline{I^{n}}\,R^{\prime}=\overline{I^{n}R^{\prime}}\quad\text{for all }n\geq n_{0}.

Taking
n=pt
, this equality holds for all
t\geq t_{2}(p):=\lceil n_{0}/p\rceil
.

For
t\geq\max\{t_{1}(p),t_{2}(p)\}
we therefore have

\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}\,R^{\prime}=\overline{I^{pt}}\,R^{\prime}=\overline{I^{pt}R^{\prime}}=\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}R^{\prime}},

where the last equality uses the inclusions

I^{pt}R^{\prime}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}_{pt}R^{\prime}\subseteq\overline{I^{pt}}\,R^{\prime}
and the fact that
integral closure depends only on the integral–closure class of the ideal.
∎

Corollary 7.5

(Admissible ratios descend)
.

Assume the hypotheses of Theorem
7.4
.
Let
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
be an arbitrary filtration.
Fix
p,q\geq 1
, and let
x\in\mathfrak{a}_{1}
be a general element, and set
R^{\prime}=R/(x)
.
If

\mathfrak{b}_{qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}\quad\text{for }t\gg 1,

then

\mathfrak{b}_{qt}R^{\prime}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}R^{\prime}}\quad\text{for }t\gg 1.

Proof.

For
t\gg 1
, quotienting gives

\mathfrak{b}_{qt}R^{\prime}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}\,R^{\prime}
.
For the fixed integer
p
, Theorem
7.4
yields

\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}\,R^{\prime}=\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}R^{\prime}}
for all
t\gg 1
,
which proves the claim.
∎

Corollary 7.6

(Monotonicity under generic hyperplane section)
.

Under the same assumptions as in
Corollary

7.5
,

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}R^{\prime}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}R^{\prime})\;\leq\;\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}).

Proof.

Every ratio
\frac{q}{p}
admissible for
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
remains
admissible after restriction by
Corollary
7.5
. Taking infima gives the inequality.
∎

Iterating Corollary
7.6
along a general flag

x_{1},\dots,x_{d-k}\in\mathfrak{a}_{1}
yields the monotonicity of the sectional
Łojasiewicz exponents

\mathcal{L}^{(d)}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\ \geq\ \mathcal{L}^{(d-1)}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\ \geq\ \cdots\ \geq\ \mathcal{L}^{(1)}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}),

where the sectional invariants are defined by restriction to

R/(x_{1},\dots,x_{d-k})
.

Part V
Structural Consequences of the Finite-Max Principle

We develop rigidity and stratification phenomena that follow once
the Łojasiewicz exponent is computed by a finite candidate set of divisorial
valuations.

8.
Rigidity results

The theme of this section is that equality in the structural inequalities developed in
Sections

6
and
7
is highly rigid: it forces a common extremal valuation to compute several a priori different invariants simultaneously. We first formulate a general common-extremal-valuation principle (
Theorem

8.1
) and then analyze its consequences in increasingly structured settings, including a complete classification in the monomial case (
Theorem

8.3
) and extensions to Newton nondegenerate regimes. We also treat the equality case in Teissier–Rees–Sharp type inequalities, showing that proportionality/equality of the relevant mixed-multiplicity data is detected valuatively and leads to strong constraints on the underlying ideals and filtrations (e.g.,
Proposition

8.7
).

8.1.
Rigidity I: the common extremal valuation principle

We begin with the basic mechanism: equality in
Theorem

7.1
forces the same
divisorial valuation to compute all extremal quantities involved.

Theorem 8.1

(Common extremal valuation criterion)
.

With hypotheses as in
Theorem

7.1
, the equality

\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\cdot\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})

holds if and only if there exists real valuation
v
centered at
\mathfrak{m}
such that:

(a)

v
computes
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
, i.e.
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=A(v)/v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
;

(b)

v
computes
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
, i.e.
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})/v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
;

(c)

v
computes
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
, i.e.
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})=A(v)/v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
.

Proof.

If.

If
v
satisfies (a)–(c), then

\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\cdot\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\frac{A(v)}{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}\cdot\frac{v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}=\frac{A(v)}{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}=\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}).

Only if.

Assume
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\cdot\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
.
Choose a real valuation
v_{0}
centered at
\mathfrak{m}
that computes
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
, whose existence was established in
[
31
, Theorem A]
. That is,

\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=A(v_{0})/v_{0}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
.
By the valuative definition of
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
in
Theorem

5.6
, we have

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\geq\frac{v_{0}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v_{0}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}.

Hence,

\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})=\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\cdot\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\ \geq\ \frac{A(v_{0})}{v_{0}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}\cdot\frac{v_{0}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v_{0}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}=\frac{A(v_{0})}{v_{0}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}\ \geq\ \inf_{v}\frac{A(v)}{v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}=\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}).

Therefore, all inequalities are equalities. In particular

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=v_{0}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})/v_{0}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
and
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})=A(v_{0})/v_{0}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
.
Thus,
v_{0}
satisfies (a)–(c).
∎

8.2.
Rigidity II: the monomial case

Focusing on one ideal, we now give a strong and explicit rigidity statement in the monomial case.
Assume
R=\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{d}]_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})}
and
I
is an
\mathfrak{m}
–primary monomial ideal.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
I\subseteq R
be an
\mathfrak{m}
–primary monomial ideal,
and let
\NP(I)\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}
denote its Newton polyhedron.
Let
\mathcal{U}(I)
be the finite set of primitive inward normal vectors

u\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
to the compact facets of
\NP(I)
, and for each such
u

let
v_{u}
denote the associated monomial divisorial valuation.
Then

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{m}}(I)\;=\;\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}(I)}\frac{v_{u}(I)}{v_{u}(\mathfrak{m})}.

Proof.

By
Theorem

5.17
,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{m}}(I)
is computed by finitely many Rees valuations of
I
.

Since
I
is monomial, the Rees valuations of
I

are precisely the monomial valuations obtained from the (non-redundant) bounding
hyperplanes of the Newton polyhedron
\NP(I)
; see
[
30
, Theorem 10.3.5]
.
For an
\mathfrak{m}
–primary monomial ideal, the relevant bounding hyperplanes are exactly
those defining the compact facets of
\NP(I)
, hence they are indexed by the primitive
inward normal vectors
u\in\mathcal{U}(I)
, and the corresponding valuations are the
v_{u}
.
Therefore

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{m}}(I)=\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}(I)}\frac{v_{u}(I)}{v_{u}(\mathfrak{m})}.\qed

Theorem 8.3

(Monomial
\Theta(I)\geq 1
and equality classification)
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{d}]_{\mathfrak{m}}
be an
\mathfrak{m}
–primary monomial ideal.
Then

\Theta(I)=\frac{1}{d}\,\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}(I)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{m}}(I)\ \geq\ 1.

Moreover,
\Theta(I)=1
if and only if there exists a vector

u=(t,\dots,t)\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{d}
such that the same monomial (toric) valuation
v_{u}

computes both
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}(I)
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{m}}(I)
.
Equivalently,
\NP(I)
has a unique compact facet meeting the diagonal ray

\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}(1,\dots,1)
, and its inward normal is proportional to
(1,\dots,1)
.

Proof.

Step 1: Monomial formulas.

For a monomial valuation
v_{u}
with
u\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{d}
we have

v_{u}(x^{\alpha})={\langle u,\alpha\rangle},\quad v_{u}(\mathfrak{m})=\min_{i}u_{i},\quad A(v_{u})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}.

Also
v_{u}(I)=h_{I}(u)
where
h_{I}
is the support function of
\NP(I)
.

Howald’s formula (
[
29
]
) for monomial ideals gives

\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}(I)=\min_{u\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{d}}\frac{A(v_{u})}{v_{u}(I)}=\min_{u\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{d}}\frac{\sum_{i}u_{i}}{h_{I}(u)}.

Moreover, by
Lemma

8.2
, we have

(8.1)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{m}}(I)=\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\frac{v_{u}(I)}{v_{u}(\mathfrak{m})}=\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\frac{h_{I}(u)}{\min_{i}u_{i}},

where
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{d}
is the finite set of primitive inward normals to the
compact facets of
\NP(I)
.

Step 2: Inequality and equality.
The inequality
\Theta(I)\geq 1
follows from
Theorem

7.1
.

If
\Theta(I)=1
, then
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}(I)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{m}}(I)=d=\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}(\mathfrak{m})
.
By
Theorem

8.1
, equality forces a common valuation computing both

\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}(I)
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{m}}(I)
and also satisfying
A(v)/v(\mathfrak{m})=d
.
In the monomial category, such a valuation must be
v_{u}
for some
u\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{d}
, and then

\frac{A(v_{u})}{v_{u}(\mathfrak{m})}=\frac{\sum_{i}u_{i}}{\min_{i}u_{i}}=d\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u_{1}=\cdots=u_{d}.

Hence, the common extremal valuation must be
v_{u}
with
u
proportional to
(1,\dots,1)
.
Conversely, if there exists such a
u
computing both invariants, then the computation in Step 2
shows
\Theta(I)=1
.
The polyhedral restatement in terms of a compact facet meeting the diagonal ray

\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}(1,\dots,1)
is the standard interpretation of the valuation
v_{(1,\dots,1)}
.
∎

Rigidity III: extension to Newton nondegenerate settings

We remark that the monomial rigidity picture can be extended to Newton nondegenerate

Section

11

The rigidity results above extend to any class of ideals where:

both
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}(I)
and

; and

(b)

\overline{I}

(Integral-closure reduction preserves
\Theta
)
.

, then

.

,
\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}(I)=\operatorname{\mathrm{lct}}(J)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{m}}(I)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{m}}(J)
since

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{m}}(I)

\overline{I}
.
Therefore,

.
∎

Remark 8.5
.

is the key mechanism for transporting rigidity from the

For instance, in Newton nondegenerate situations where one can replace an ideal

by a

via

8.4
.

8.4.
Rigidity IV: equality in Teissier–Rees–Sharp inequality

We record a classical but conceptually important source of

rigidity
: equality in Teissier’s Minkowski-type inequalities for
multiplicities forces the ideals involved to be
proportional in the
Rees–valuative sense
.

Lemma 8.6
.

Let
I,J\subseteq R
be nonzero ideals and assume
I+J
is
\mathfrak{m}
–primary.
If
\overline{I^{p}}=\overline{J^{q}}
for some
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, then for
every Rees valuation
v
of
IJ
one has

\frac{v(I)}{v(J)}=\frac{q}{p}.

In particular, the ratio
v(I)/v(J)
is constant on the finite set of Rees
valuations of
IJ
.

Proof.

By the valuative criterion for integral closure,
\overline{I^{p}}=\overline{J^{q}}

is equivalent to
p\,v(I)=q\,v(J)
for every divisorial valuation
v
centered at

\mathfrak{m}
. Applying this to the (finitely many) Rees valuations of
IJ
yields the
claim.
∎

Proposition 8.7

(Teissier–Rees–Sharp equality
\Rightarrow
proportionality)
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a quasi–unmixed Noetherian local ring of dimension
d
, and let

I,J\subseteq R
be
\mathfrak{m}
–primary ideals.
Assume that equality holds in Teissier’s Minkowski (or first mixed multiplicity)
inequality, i.e.,

e\!\left(I^{[d-1]},J^{[1]}\right)^{d}\;=\;e(I)^{d-1}\,e(J).

Then, there exist
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
such that

\overline{I^{p}}=\overline{J^{q}}.

Consequently, the ratio
v(I)/v(J)
is constant
(and equal to
q/p
) on the finite set of Rees valuations of
IJ
.

Proof.

This is the classical characterization of the equality case in Teissier’s
Minkowski-type inequalities for multiplicities; see Teissier
[
50
]

and the detailed local-algebraic treatment of Rees–Sharp
[
46
]
.
∎

Corollary 8.8

(Rigidity of extremal ratios in the equality case)
.

In the setting of
Proposition

8.7
, suppose
moreover that one is considering any valuative extremal ratio of the form

\mathcal{L}_{J}(I):=\ \sup_{v}\frac{v(I)}{v(J)},

where
v
ranges over divisorial valuations centered at
\mathfrak{m}
(or any subclass
that contains the Rees valuations of
IJ
).
Then,
\mathcal{L}_{J}(I)=q/p
, and every Rees valuation of
IJ
computes

\mathcal{L}_{J}(I)
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

8.7
we have

\overline{I^{p}}=\overline{J^{q}}
for some
p,q>0
, hence
p\,v(I)=q\,v(J)
for all
divisorial
v
, so
v(I)/v(J)=q/p
identically. The conclusion follows, and in
particular every Rees valuation computes the supremum.
∎

9.
Functoriality, stability, and stratification

This section develops the structural consequences of the finite-max principle in families. Assuming
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
is computed by a finite candidate set of divisorial valuations on a fixed birational model, we study how these candidate sets and the corresponding maximizers behave under basic operations (localization, completion, integral closure, and natural local homomorphisms), establishing functoriality and upper-semicontinuity properties that make the maximizer meaningful geometric data. We then show that, under a uniform finite candidate set hypothesis, parameter spaces admit a finite stratification (equivalently, a wall–chamber decomposition) on which the identity of the computing valuation is constant and
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t)}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t))
is given by a single valuative ratio on each stratum (
Theorem

9.9
). Finally, for one-parameter valuation-concave paths we prove that
t\mapsto 1/\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t)}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t))
is piecewise fractional linear and that affineness forces persistence of the controlling valuation data (
Theorem

9.14
).

Throughout this section,
R
is a Noetherian local ring with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
.

9.1.
Finite candidate sets and the maximizer map

A major source of rigidity is
finite attainment on a fixed birational model
.
We isolate a hypothesis that holds in many situations of interest, including:
Noetherian families (finitely generated Rees algebra),
toric/monomial polyhedral families (see also Section
10
),
Newton nondegenerate integral-closure reductions,
and families arising from a fixed log smooth model.

Definition 9.1

(Finite candidate set hypothesis)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{N}\}
be a finite set of divisorial valuations centered at
\mathfrak{m}
.
We say that a pair
({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}},{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
satisfies the
finite candidate set hypothesis

with respect to
\mathcal{V}
if

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\frac{v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}.

Remark 9.2
.

When
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}
is
\mathfrak{m}
–primary and Noetherian (or sufficiently polyhedral),
Section
5
gives that
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
is computed by finitely many valuations
coming from a single normalized blowup. This is exactly the situation of
Definition
9.1
.

9.2.
Functoriality

We record functoriality properties under localization, completion and integral-closure.

Proposition 9.3

(Monotonicity under maps)
.

Let
\varphi:R\to S
be a local homomorphism of Noetherian local rings with maximal ideals

\mathfrak{m}
and
\mathfrak{n}
, respectively, such that
\varphi(\mathfrak{m})\subseteq\mathfrak{n}
.
Let
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}},{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}
be filtrations of
\mathfrak{m}
–primary ideals in
R
and set

\varphi({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}):=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}S\}_{p\geq 1},\quad\varphi({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}):=\{\mathfrak{b}_{q}S\}_{q\geq 1}.

Then,

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\ \geq\ \mathcal{L}_{\varphi({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}(\varphi({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})).

Moreover, if
\phi
is faithfully flat and has the
integral-closure contraction property
, i.e.,
\overline{IS}\cap R=\overline{I}
for any ideal
I\subseteq R
, then

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\ =\ \mathcal{L}_{\varphi({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}(\varphi({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})).

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
.
By definition of
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
as an infimum of admissible rational slopes,
there exist integers
p,q\geq 1
such that

\frac{q}{p}<\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})+\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{b}_{qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}\ \forall\ t\gg 1.

Applying
\varphi
gives
\mathfrak{b}_{qt}S\subseteq(\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}})S.

Since integral dependence is preserved under ring homomorphisms,
we obtain
\mathfrak{b}_{qt}S\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}S}\ \forall\ t\gg 1.

Thus, the rational number
\frac{q}{p}
is an admissible slope for the pair of
filtrations
\varphi({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
and
\varphi({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
.
By definition of
\mathcal{L}_{\varphi({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}(\varphi({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}))
, it follows that

\mathcal{L}_{\varphi({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}(\varphi({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}))\leq\frac{q}{p}<\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})+\varepsilon.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
yields

\mathcal{L}_{\varphi({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}(\varphi({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}))\leq\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
as desired.

Suppose, in addition, that
\phi
is faithfully flat and has the integral-closure contraction property. If
\mathfrak{b}_{qt}S\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}S}
for
t\gg 1
, then

\mathfrak{b}_{qt}\subseteq(\mathfrak{b}_{qt}S)\cap R\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}S}\cap R=\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{pt}}\ \forall\ t\gg 1.

That is, every admissible slope
\frac{q}{p}
for the filtrations
\phi({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
and
\phi({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})
is also an admissible slope for the filtrations
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}
and
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}
. Therefore, by a similar argument, this implies that
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\phi({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}})}(\phi({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}))
. Hence, we obtained the equality as claimed.
∎

Corollary 9.4

(Localization invariance for
\mathfrak{m}
–primary data)
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a Noetherian local ring and let
\mathfrak{p}\subseteq R
be a prime ideal.
Set
R_{\mathfrak{p}}
with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{p}R_{\mathfrak{p}}
.
Let
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
and
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
be filtrations of
\mathfrak{p}
–primary ideals in
R
.
Then

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}R_{\mathfrak{p}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}R_{\mathfrak{p}}).

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of
Proposition

9.3
, noting that
R\rightarrow R_{\mathfrak{p}}
is faithfully flat and has the integral-closure contraction property.
∎

Corollary 9.5

(Completion invariance)
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be an excellent Noetherian local ring and let
\widehat{R}
be the
\mathfrak{m}
–adic completion.
Assume that
R
is analytically unramified (equivalently,
\widehat{R}
is reduced).
Let
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
and
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
be filtrations of
\mathfrak{m}
–primary ideals in
R
.
Then,

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}\widehat{R}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}\widehat{R}).

Proof.

Observe that
R\rightarrow\widehat{R}
is faithfully flat. Since
R
is excellent,
R\rightarrow\widehat{R}
also has the integral-closure contraction property. Thus, the assertion is a consequence of
Proposition

9.3
.
∎

Proposition 9.6

(Integral-closure invariance for filtrations)
.

Let
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}},{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}
be filtrations in
R
.
Define
\overline{{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}}:=\{\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}\}_{p\geq 1}
and similarly
\overline{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}
.
Then

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\mathcal{L}_{\overline{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}}(\overline{{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}}).

Proof.

For every
p,q
we have
\overline{\mathfrak{b}_{q}}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}
if and only if

\mathfrak{b}_{q}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}
, since
\mathfrak{b}_{q}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{b}_{q}}
and integral closure is idempotent.
Hence, the set of admissible ratios
q/p
in the containment definition is unchanged, and

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=L_{\overline{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}}(\overline{{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}})
.
∎

Remark 9.7

(Saturation)
.

For non-
\mathfrak{m}
–primary ideals one may consider saturations
\mathfrak{a}_{p}^{\mathrm{sat}}
.
In the present paper, our main invariants are defined for
\mathfrak{m}
–primary filtrations, for
which saturation is trivial. When extending beyond
\mathfrak{m}
–primary data, one must track the
change in the center of valuations; the valuative formula remains valid but the candidate
set of valuations changes.

9.3.
Stratification by the computing valuation

We describe the induced stratification by the computing valuation.
Assume the
finite candidate set hypothesis
(Definition
9.1
) holds uniformly for a family

\{({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t),{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))\}_{t\in T}
with the same finite set of divisorial valuations

V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{N}\}
.

Definition 9.8

(Maximizer set for families)
.

For each
t\in T
define

\alpha_{i}(t):=v_{i}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}(t)),\quad\beta_{i}(t):=v_{i}(\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}(t))\quad(1\leq i\leq N),

and assume
\alpha_{i}(t)\in[0,\infty)
and
\beta_{i}(t)\in(0,\infty)
for all
i
.
(For instance, this holds if
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t)
is linearly bounded from below in the sense of
Remark

5.10
.)
Define the maximizer set

\Max({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t),{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t)):=\left\{\,i\in\{1,\dots,N\}\;:\;\frac{\alpha_{i}(t)}{\beta_{i}(t)}=\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\frac{\alpha_{k}(t)}{\beta_{k}(t)}\right\}.

If
\Max({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t),{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))=\{j\}
is a singleton, we write

\argmax({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t),{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t)):=j
.

The stratification phenomenon in
Theorem

9.9
below is driven by the
finite–max principle established earlier.
Indeed, under the standing hypotheses, the value

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
is computed by a valuation belonging to a fixed finite set
(depending only on
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}
), so changes in the value of
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
can occur
only when the identity of the maximizing valuation changes.

Theorem 9.9

(Finite wall–chamber decomposition)
.

Assume the setup of Definition
9.8
. Assume that for each
t\in T
the finite candidate set
hypothesis gives

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t)}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t))=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\frac{\alpha_{i}(t)}{\beta_{i}(t)},\quad\alpha_{i}(t):=v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t)),\ \beta_{i}(t):=v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t)).

Assume moreover that each
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
is continuous on
T
.
Then,
T
admits a finite partition into locally closed strata, on each of which the maximizer set

\Max({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t),{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))
is constant. In particular, on each stratum, the function

t\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t)}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t))
is given by a single ratio
\alpha_{j}(t)/\beta_{j}(t)
.

Moreover, for each
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
the locus

T_{j}^{\circ}:=\{\,t\in T:\Max({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t),{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))=\{j\}\,\}

is open in
T
.

Proof.

For each nonempty
J\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}
set

T_{J}:=\Big(\bigcap_{i,i^{\prime}\in J}\{\,t:\alpha_{i}(t)\beta_{i^{\prime}}(t)=\alpha_{i^{\prime}}(t)\beta_{i}(t)\,\}\Big)\cap\Big(\bigcap_{i\in J,\ k\notin J}\{\,t:\alpha_{i}(t)\beta_{k}(t)>\alpha_{k}(t)\beta_{i}(t)\,\}\Big).

By continuity, each equality locus is closed and each strict-inequality locus is open. Thus, each
T_{J}

is locally closed.

Fix
t\in T
and write
\rho_{i}(t):=\alpha_{i}(t)/\beta_{i}(t)
, which is well-defined since

\beta_{i}(t)\in(0,\infty)
for all
i
by Definition
9.8
. Let
J:=\Max({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t),{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))
.
Then,
\rho_{i}(t)=\rho_{i^{\prime}}(t)
for
i,i^{\prime}\in J
and
\rho_{i}(t)>\rho_{k}(t)
for
i\in J
,
k\notin J
.
Multiplying by the positive denominators yields precisely the defining conditions of
T_{J}
. Therefore,
t\in T_{J}
.

Conversely, if
t\in T_{J}
, the defining equalities/inequalities imply that the maximum of the finite set

\{\rho_{1}(t),\dots,\rho_{N}(t)\}
is attained exactly on
J
, i.e.
\Max({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t),{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))=J
.
Hence, the sets
T_{J}
form a finite partition of
T
by maximizer set, proving the first assertion.

Finally,

T_{j}^{\circ}=\bigcap_{i\neq j}\{\,t:\alpha_{j}(t)\beta_{i}(t)>\alpha_{i}(t)\beta_{j}(t)\,\},

an intersection of open sets, and so is open as claimed.
∎

Corollary 9.10

(Stability from a strict gap)
.

Assume the hypotheses of
Theorem

9.9
. Fix
t_{0}\in T
and an index

j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
. If there exists
\delta>0
such that

(9.1)

\frac{\alpha_{j}(t_{0})}{\beta_{j}(t_{0})}\geq\frac{\alpha_{i}(t_{0})}{\beta_{i}(t_{0})}+\delta\quad\text{for all }i\neq j,

then there exists an open neighborhood
U
of
t_{0}
such that for all
t\in U
one has

\Max({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t),{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))=\{j\},\text{ and so }\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t)}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t))=\frac{\alpha_{j}(t)}{\beta_{j}(t)}.

Equivalently,
t_{0}\in T_{j}^{\circ}
and
T_{j}^{\circ}
is a neighborhood of
t_{0}
.

Proof.

Since
\beta_{i}(t)\in(0,\infty)
for all
i
and
t
(Definition
9.8
), the functions

t\mapsto\alpha_{i}(t)/\beta_{i}(t)
are well-defined. For each
i\neq j
, set

g_{i}(t):=\frac{\alpha_{j}(t)}{\beta_{j}(t)}-\frac{\alpha_{i}(t)}{\beta_{i}(t)}.

By the assumed continuity of
\alpha_{\bullet},\beta_{\bullet}
and positivity of
\beta_{\bullet}
,
each
g_{i}
is continuous on
T
. The strict gap hypothesis (
9.1
) gives

g_{i}(t_{0})\geq\delta>0
for all
i\neq j
. Hence, for each
i\neq j
there exists an open neighborhood

U_{i}
of
t_{0}
such that
g_{i}(t)>0
for all
t\in U_{i}
. Let
U:=\bigcap_{i\neq j}U_{i}
.
Then, for all
t\in U
,

\frac{\alpha_{j}(t)}{\beta_{j}(t)}>\frac{\alpha_{i}(t)}{\beta_{i}(t)}\quad\text{for all }i\neq j,

so the unique maximizer is
j
, i.e.
\Max({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t),{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))=\{j\}
. The displayed formula for

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t)}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t))
follows immediately from the finite-candidate identity of

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t)}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t))
given in
Theorem

9.9
.
∎

Example 9.11

(Change of maximizing valuation)
.

Let
R=\mathbb{C}[x,y]_{(x,y)}
and consider the monomial ideal

\mathfrak{a}=(x^{4},\ x^{2}y^{3},\ y^{5})\subseteq R.

The Newton polygon
\NP(\mathfrak{a})
has two compact edges: the segment joining

(4,0)
to
(2,3)
with primitive inward normal
u_{1}=(3,2)
, and the segment
joining
(2,3)
to
(0,5)
with primitive inward normal
u_{2}=(1,1)
.
Accordingly,

v_{u_{1}}(\mathfrak{a})=12,\quad v_{u_{2}}(\mathfrak{a})=5.

For
t\in[0,1]
, define a family of principal ideals

\mathfrak{b}_{p}(t):=(x^{\lceil(1+t)p\rceil}y^{\lceil 2p\rceil}),\quad{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t):=\{\mathfrak{b}_{p}(t)\}_{p\geq 1}.

Then, asymptotically,

v_{u_{1}}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))=7+3t,\quad v_{u_{2}}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))=3+t.

Hence,

\frac{v_{u_{1}}(\mathfrak{a})}{v_{u_{1}}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))}=\frac{12}{7+3t},\quad\frac{v_{u_{2}}(\mathfrak{a})}{v_{u_{2}}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))}=\frac{5}{3+t}.

These two ratios are equal precisely at
t=\frac{1}{3}
. Consequently,

\argmax(\mathfrak{a},{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))=\begin{cases}\{u_{1}\},&t<\frac{1}{3},\\[4.0pt]
\{u_{1},u_{2}\},&t=\frac{1}{3},\\[4.0pt]
\{u_{2}\},&t>\frac{1}{3}.\end{cases}

In particular, the finite candidate set is fixed, but the maximizing valuation
jumps as
t
crosses the wall
t=\frac{1}{3}
, illustrating the stratification
phenomenon of
Theorem

9.9
.

This example shows that even under finite testing hypotheses,
the Łojasiewicz exponent need not be globally linear in families,
and rigidity corresponds precisely to the absence of such changes
in the maximizing valuation.

Before continue to discuss stability and rigidity consequences, we record a general semicontinuity property of divisorial values in families.

Proposition 9.12

(Upper semicontinuity of divisorial order in algebraic families)
.

Let
T
be a Noetherian scheme, let
X
be a Noetherian integral scheme, and set
X_{T}:=X\times T
.
Let
\pi:Y\to X_{T}
be a proper birational morphism with
Y
normal, let
E\subseteq Y
be a prime divisor whose image dominates
T
, and let
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{X_{T}}
be a coherent ideal sheaf, and for
t\in T
let
\mathcal{I}_{t}
be its fiber on
X\times\{t\}
.
Define

\alpha(t):=\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathcal{I}_{t})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}.

Then, for each
m\geq 0
, the locus

T_{\geq m}:=\{\,t\in T:\alpha(t)\geq m\,\}

is Zariski closed in
T
. Equivalently,
t\mapsto\alpha(t)
is upper semicontinuous on
T
.

Proof.

Set
\mathcal{J}:=\pi^{-1}\mathcal{I}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{Y}
, a coherent ideal sheaf on
Y
, and let
p:E\to T
be the induced morphism.
Since
\pi
is proper,
p
is proper.

Fix
m\geq 0
. Consider the coherent
\mathcal{O}_{Y}
–module

\mathcal{Q}_{m}:=\frac{\mathcal{J}+\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-mE)}{\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-mE)}.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{m}:=\mathcal{Q}_{m}|_{E}
. Then,
\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(\mathcal{G}_{m})
is closed in
E
, hence
p(\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(\mathcal{G}_{m}))
is closed in
T
.
We claim that

T_{\geq m}=T\setminus p(\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(\mathcal{G}_{m})).

Indeed, let
t\in T
and let
\eta_{t}
be the generic point of the prime divisor
E_{t}:=E\times_{T}\kappa(t)
.
Since
Y
is normal, the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{Y_{t},\eta_{t}}
is a DVR with valuation
\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{t}}
.
By definition of
\mathcal{Q}_{m}
, the stalk
(\mathcal{Q}_{m})_{\eta_{t}}
vanishes if and only if

\mathcal{J}_{\eta_{t}}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{Y,\eta_{t}}(-mE),

and after restricting to the fiber this is equivalent to

(\mathcal{J}_{t})_{\eta_{t}}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{Y_{t},\eta_{t}}(-mE_{t})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{t}}(\mathcal{J}_{t})\geq m.

Because
\mathcal{J}=\pi^{-1}\mathcal{I}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{Y}
, we have
\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{t}}(\mathcal{J}_{t})=\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{t}}(\mathcal{I}_{t})=\alpha(t)
.
Thus,
(\mathcal{Q}_{m})_{\eta_{t}}=0
if and only if
\alpha(t)\geq m
, i.e.
\eta_{t}\notin\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(\mathcal{G}_{m})
.
Equivalently,
t\notin p(\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(\mathcal{G}_{m}))
if and only if
\alpha(t)\geq m
, proving the claim.
Hence,
T_{\geq m}
is closed.
∎

9.4.
Geodesics of filtrations and fractional linearity of
1/L

We now turn to one-parameter families of filtrations and prove a fractional linearity statement
under a maximizer stability hypothesis. This provides a rigidity mechanism:

affineness of
1/L
characterizes common extremal valuations along the path.

Definition 9.13

(Valuation-concave paths)
.

Let
\{({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t),{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))\}_{t\in[0,1]}
be a one-parameter family of pairs of
filtrations of
\mathfrak{m}
–primary ideals.
We say it is
valuation-concave
(or
valuation-affine
) if for every divisorial valuation
v
centered at
\mathfrak{m}
,
the functions

t\longmapsto v({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t))\text{ and }t\longmapsto v({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))

are affine on
[0,1]
.

Assume now that the finite candidate set hypothesis holds uniformly along
t\in[0,1]
for
some finite set
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{N}\}
, and that the family is valuation-concave.
Recall that
\alpha_{i}(t):=v_{i}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t))\text{ and }\beta_{i}(t):=v_{i}({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t)).

Then,
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}
are affine in
t
.
Define

L(t):=\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t)}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t))=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\frac{\alpha_{i}(t)}{\beta_{i}(t)}.

Theorem 9.14

(Piecewise fractional linearity of
1/L
)
.

Assume that

(1)

for all
t\in[0,1]
one has the finite candidate set hypothesis, as in
Definition

9.1
,
with respect to a fixed set
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{N}\}
;

(2)

the path
t\mapsto({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t),{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))
is valuation-concave, in the sense of Definition
9.13
, and
\beta_{i}(t)>0
.

Then, there exists a finite subset
\Sigma\subset[0,1]
such that, on every connected component

I\subset[0,1]\setminus\Sigma
, one can choose an index
j=j(I)\in\{1,\dots,N\}
with

\frac{1}{L(t)}=\frac{\beta_{j}(t)}{\alpha_{j}(t)}\quad\text{for all }t\in I.

In particular,
t\mapsto 1/L(t)
is fractional linear (hence
C^{\infty}
) on each such
I
, and its second
derivative has constant sign on
I
. Moreover, on a given component
I
the restriction
t\mapsto 1/L(t)

is affine if and only if the affine functions
\alpha_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
are proportional on
I
.

Proof.

For
1\leq i<j\leq N
set

h_{ij}(t):=\alpha_{i}(t)\beta_{j}(t)-\alpha_{j}(t)\beta_{i}(t).

By valuation-concavity, each
\alpha_{\ell},\beta_{\ell}
is affine in
t
, hence each
h_{ij}
is a polynomial
of degree at most
2
. Define

\Sigma:=\{0,1\}\ \cup\ \bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i<j\leq N\\
h_{ij}\not\equiv 0\end{subarray}}\{\,t\in[0,1]:h_{ij}(t)=0\,\}.

Then,
\Sigma
is finite, since each nonzero polynomial
h_{ij}
has finitely many zeros (or is identically zero, in which case the pair
(i,j)
does not contribute to “walls” and can be ignored) and there are only finitely many
pairs
(i,j)
.

Let
I\subset[0,1]\setminus\Sigma
be a connected component. Fix
i<j
.
If
h_{ij}\equiv 0
, then
\alpha_{i}(t)\beta_{j}(t)=\alpha_{j}(t)\beta_{i}(t)
for all
t
, hence

\alpha_{i}(t)/\beta_{i}(t)=\alpha_{j}(t)/\beta_{j}(t)
for all
t
(using
\beta_{i}(t)\beta_{j}(t)>0
).
If
h_{ij}\not\equiv 0
, then by definition of
\Sigma
we have
h_{ij}(t)\neq 0
for all
t\in I
, and since

h_{ij}
is continuous it has constant sign on
I
. Using again
\beta_{i}(t)\beta_{j}(t)>0
, this implies that the comparison

\frac{\alpha_{i}(t)}{\beta_{i}(t)}\ \gtreqless\ \frac{\alpha_{j}(t)}{\beta_{j}(t)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad h_{ij}(t)\ \gtreqless\ 0

is independent of
t\in I
. Therefore, the total ordering (with ties) of the finite set of numbers

\{\alpha_{1}(t)/\beta_{1}(t),\dots,\alpha_{N}(t)/\beta_{N}(t)\}
does not change with
t\in I
. Hence, the maximizer set

\Max({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t),{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))
is constant on
I
. Choose any index
j=j(I)
in this constant maximizer set. Then, for all
t\in I
,

L(t)=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\frac{\alpha_{i}(t)}{\beta_{i}(t)}=\frac{\alpha_{j}(t)}{\beta_{j}(t)}.

Since
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t)
consists of
\mathfrak{m}
–primary ideals and
v_{j}
is centered at
\mathfrak{m}
, we have
\alpha_{j}(t)=v_{j}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t))>0
on
[0,1]
.
Write
\alpha_{j}(t)=a_{0}+a_{1}t\text{ and }\beta_{j}(t)=b_{0}+b_{1}t.

Then, on
I
,

\frac{1}{L(t)}=\frac{b_{0}+b_{1}t}{a_{0}+a_{1}t},

a ratio of affine functions with positive denominator, so fractional linear and
C^{\infty}
on
I
.
Differentiating twice gives

\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\left(\frac{b_{0}+b_{1}t}{a_{0}+a_{1}t}\right)=-\frac{2a_{1}(b_{1}a_{0}-b_{0}a_{1})}{(a_{0}+a_{1}t)^{3}},

whose sign is constant on
I
because the denominator is positive and the numerator is constant.
Finally,
\beta_{j}/\alpha_{j}
is affine on
I
if and only if
b_{1}a_{0}-b_{0}a_{1}=0
, i.e. if and only if
\alpha_{j}
and
\beta_{j}

are proportional affine functions on
I
.
∎

Remark 9.15

(A cleaner sufficient condition)
.

If
\alpha_{j}(t)
is constant in
t
(or more generally
a_{1}=0
), then
\beta_{j}/\alpha_{j}
is affine
and hence concave. This occurs in many geometric situations where
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}
is fixed and only

{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}
varies.

Corollary 9.16

(Geodesic rigidity from global affineness)
.

Assume the hypotheses of
Theorem

9.14
.
If the function
t\mapsto 1/L(t)
is affine on
[0,1]
, then there exists an index
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
such that

L(t)=\frac{\alpha_{j}(t)}{\beta_{j}(t)}\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,1].

Equivalently,
j\in\Max({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t),{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))
for every
t\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

By
Theorem

9.14
, there exists a finite set
\Sigma\subset[0,1]
such that on each connected component

I\subset[0,1]\setminus\Sigma
one can choose an index
j=j(I)
with

\frac{1}{L(t)}=\frac{\beta_{j}(t)}{\alpha_{j}(t)}\quad\text{for all }t\in I.

Fix one such component
I
and let
j=j(I)
. By assumption,
1/L(t)
is affine on
[0,1]
, say

\frac{1}{L(t)}=c_{0}+c_{1}t\quad(t\in[0,1]).

Thus, on
I
we have

\beta_{j}(t)=\bigl(c_{0}+c_{1}t\bigr)\alpha_{j}(t).

Define
F(t):=\beta_{j}(t)-\bigl(c_{0}+c_{1}t\bigr)\alpha_{j}(t).

Since
\alpha_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
are affine functions of
t
(valuation–concavity), the function
F(t)
is a polynomial
of degree at most
2
. As
F
vanishes on the nonempty open interval
I
, it follows that
F\equiv 0
on
[0,1]
.
Hence,

\frac{\beta_{j}(t)}{\alpha_{j}(t)}=c_{0}+c_{1}t\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,1].

Since
\alpha_{j}(t)>0
for all
t
(because
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t)
consists of
\mathfrak{m}
–primary ideals and
v_{j}
is centered at
\mathfrak{m}
),
we may invert to obtain

L(t)=\frac{\alpha_{j}(t)}{\beta_{j}(t)}\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,1].

Finally, this identity implies
j\in\Max({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(t),{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(t))
for every
t
by the definition of
\Max
.
∎

Part VI
Polyhedral Models and Explicit Computation

In this part, we specialize the general valuative framework to toric and
Newton nondegenerate settings, where the Łojasiewicz exponent admits explicit polyhedral
descriptions. We will also give a better understanding of Łojasiewicz exponent at infinity.

10.
Toric and monomial models: polyhedra, valuations, and formulas

This section provides the explicit polyhedral model underlying finite–max computations in toric and monomial settings. We recall how integral closure of monomial ideals is encoded by Newton polyhedra and how toric (monomial) divisorial valuations correspond to linear functionals/support functions, so that valuative containment thresholds become linear optimization problems (
Theorem

10.8
). From this perspective we derive Newton/facet formulas for the algebraic Łojasiewicz exponent, showing that
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
is computed by finitely many toric valuations determined by facet data (
Theorems

10.11
and
10.18
). In particular, the finite candidate set hypothesis becomes completely concrete: the maximizing valuation comes from a facet normal, yielding uniform finite-max formulas and effective computation (
Corollary

10.16
).

10.1.
Affine semigroup rings and monomial ideals

For basic terminology in toric geometry, we refer to
[
11
,
18
]
. Let
N\cong\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be a lattice and let
M:=\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(N,\mathbb{Z})
be the dual lattice.
Fix a finitely generated (not necessarily saturated) subsemigroup

S\subseteq M

such that the rational cone

\sigma^{\vee}:=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot S\ \subseteq M_{\mathbb{R}}:=M\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}

is a pointed rational polyhedral cone of full dimension
n
(equivalently,
\sigma^{\vee}

contains no nontrivial linear subspace). Let
\sigma\subseteq N_{\mathbb{R}}
be the dual cone:

\sigma:=\{u\in N_{\mathbb{R}}:\ {\langle u,x\rangle}\geq 0\ \text{for all }x\in\sigma^{\vee}\}.

Consider the affine semigroup ring
k[S]
and its maximal monomial ideal

\mathfrak{m}_{S}:=(\chi^{m}:m\in S\setminus\{0\}).

We work in the local ring

R:=k[S]_{\mathfrak{m}_{S}},
which is an excellent Noetherian local domain, not necessarily normal.
This is the local ring of the affine toric variety
X_{\sigma}=\Spec(k[S])
at its
distinguished torus-fixed point.

An ideal
I\subseteq R
is
monomial
if it is generated by monomials
\chi^{m}
,

m\in S
. Equivalently, it is the extension to
R
of a monomial ideal of
k[S]
.

Remark 10.1
.

In
R=k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})}
one recovers the usual monomial ideals
by taking
S=\mathbb{N}^{n}
and
\chi^{m}=x^{m}
.

10.2.
Newton polyhedra in the toric setting

For a monomial ideal
I\subseteq R
, define its exponent set

E(I):=\{m\in S:\ \chi^{m}\in I\}\subseteq S\subseteq M.

Definition 10.2

(Newton polyhedron)
.

The
Newton polyhedron
of a monomial ideal
I\subseteq R
is

\NP(I):=\conv(E(I))+\sigma^{\vee}\ \subseteq M_{\mathbb{R}}.

Particularly, if
I
is generated by monomials
\chi^{m_{1}},\dots,\chi^{m_{r}}
, then
\NP(I)=\conv(m_{1},\dots,m_{r})+\sigma^{\vee}
is a closed rational polyhedron.

Lemma 10.3

(Basic properties)
.

Let
I,J\subseteq R
be monomial ideals. Then:

(a)

\NP(I)
is a closed convex set satisfying
\NP(I)+\sigma^{\vee}=\NP(I)
.

(b)

\NP(IJ)=\NP(I)+\NP(J)
(Minkowski sum).

(c)

\NP(I^{p})=p\cdot\NP(I)
for
p\geq 1
.

Proof.

All statements are standard for Newton polyhedra of monomial ideals; see, e.g.,
[
48
,
51
]
.
For completeness: (b) follows from
E(IJ)=E(I)+E(J)
and convexity, and (c) follows by iterating (b).
∎

10.3.
Toric (monomial) valuations and support functions

Each vector
u\in\sigma\cap N
defines a monomial valuation on
k[S]
by

v_{u}(\chi^{m}):={\langle u,m\rangle}\quad(m\in S),

and we extend it to
R=k[S]_{\mathfrak{m}_{S}}
.

Definition 10.4

(Toric valuation)
.

For
u\in\sigma\cap N
, let
v_{u}
denote the valuation on
R
determined by

v_{u}(\chi^{m})={\langle u,m\rangle}
. We call
v_{u}
a
toric valuation
.
It is centered at
\mathfrak{m}_{S}
if
u
lies in the relative interior
\sigma^{\circ}
,
equivalently
v_{u}(\mathfrak{m}_{S})>0
.

Definition 10.5

(Support function)
.

Let
C\subseteq M_{\mathbb{R}}
be a nonempty closed convex set with
C+\sigma^{\vee}=C
.
For
u\in\sigma
define the support function

h_{C}(u):=\inf\{{\langle u,x\rangle}:x\in C\}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}.

For a monomial ideal
I
we write
h_{I}:=h_{\NP(I)}
.

Lemma 10.6

(Valuations and support functions)
.

Let
I\subseteq R
be a monomial ideal and let
u\in\sigma\cap N
. Then

v_{u}(I)=h_{I}(u).

Proof.

Since
I
is generated by monomials,
v_{u}(I)=\min\{\langle u,m\rangle:m\in E(I)\}
.
Taking convex hull and adding
\sigma^{\vee}
does not change the minimum because
\langle u,\cdot\rangle
is linear and nonnegative on
\sigma^{\vee}
.
Thus,
v_{u}(I)=h_{\NP(I)}(u)
; see, for instance,
[
11
]
.
∎

Definition 10.7

(Normalization by a fixed monomial ideal)
.

Fix an
\mathfrak{m}_{S}
-primary monomial ideal
\mathfrak{q}\subseteq R
.
For
u\in\sigma^{\circ}\cap N
, set

\widehat{u}:=\frac{u}{h_{\mathfrak{q}}(u)}\in\sigma^{\circ}\cap N_{\mathbb{R}},\quad\text{so that}\quad v_{\widehat{u}}(\mathfrak{q})=1.

(Since
\mathfrak{q}
is
\mathfrak{m}_{S}
–primary,
h_{\mathfrak{q}}(u)>0
for all
u\in\sigma^{\circ}\cap N
.)

10.4.
Integral closure and Newton polyhedra in toric rings

The next result is standard and is the backbone of toric computations:
for monomial ideals, integral closure is detected by the Newton polyhedron.

Theorem 10.8

(Integral closure of monomial ideals)
.

Let
R=k[S]_{\mathfrak{m}_{S}}
as above and let
I\subseteq R
be a monomial ideal.
Then,
\overline{I}
is monomial and satisfies

\chi^{m}\in\overline{I}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad m\in\NP(I)\cap S.

Equivalently,
\NP(\overline{I})=\NP(I)
.

Proof.

We give a direct valuative proof that is self-contained in our context.

Let
m\in S
. As in the proof of
Lemma

5.2
,
\chi^{m}\in\overline{I}
if and only if, for any prime divisor
E
appearing on the normalized blowup of
I
,

\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\chi^{m})\geq\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(I).

Since
I
is monomial, the blowup of
I
is torus-invariant, and its normalization is a normal toric modification; in particular, its exceptional prime divisors
E
centered at
\mathfrak{m}_{S}
are torus-invariant and correspond to rays
\rho
of the associated fan with primitive generators
u_{\rho}\in\sigma^{\circ}\cap N
(see, for example,
[
11
]
). The corresponding divisorial valuation satisfies

\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\chi^{m})=\langle u_{\rho},m\rangle\text{ and }\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(I)=v_{u_{\rho}}(I).

By Lemma
10.6
,
v_{u_{\rho}}(I)=h_{I}(u_{\rho})
, the support function of the Newton polyhedron
\NP(I)
. Hence,
\chi^{m}\in\overline{I}
if and only if

\langle u_{\rho},m\rangle\geq h_{I}(u_{\rho})\text{ for all such }u_{\rho}.

Finally,
\NP(I)
is a rational polyhedron, so it is the intersection of its supporting halfspaces defined by the primitive inward normals of its compact faces. Therefore, the above inequalities are equivalent to
m\in\NP(I)\cap S
.
∎

10.5.
Toric formula for the algebraic Łojasiewicz exponent

We now specialize our general invariant to monomial ideals (and then to monomial
filtrations) in the toric local ring
R=k[S]_{\mathfrak{m}_{S}}
.

Lemma 10.9

(Toric valuative formula for monomial ideals)
.

Let
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\subseteq R
be monomial ideals with
\mathfrak{a}
being
\mathfrak{m}_{S}
-primary.
Then

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})=\max_{u}\frac{v_{u}(\mathfrak{a})}{v_{u}(\mathfrak{b})}=\max_{u}\frac{h_{\NP(\mathfrak{a})}(u)}{h_{\NP(\mathfrak{b})}(u)},

where the max is taken over finitely many
u\in\sigma^{\circ}\cap N
.

Proof.

By
Theorem

5.17
, when
\mathfrak{a}
is
\mathfrak{m}_{S}
-primary,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})
is computed by finitely many Rees valuations of
\mathfrak{a}
.
For the monomial ideal
\mathfrak{a}
, the normalized blowup of
\mathfrak{a}
is toric and its prime divisors
are torus-invariant. Hence, its Rees valuations are toric valuations
v_{u}

for finitely many primitive lattice vectors
u\in\sigma^{\circ}\cap N
, lying on rays of a fan refining
\sigma
.
Therefore, the maximum over finitely many Rees valuations equals the maximum over finitely many toric
valuations
v_{u}
.
Finally, Lemma
10.6
gives
v_{u}(\cdot)=h_{\NP(\cdot)}(u)
on monomial ideals.
∎

10.6.
Facet normals and finite candidate sets

We next package the maximization problem in purely polyhedral terms.

A
compact facet
of
\NP(\mathfrak{a})
means a facet not parallel to
\sigma^{\vee}

(i.e., a facet whose supporting hyperplane intersects
\sigma^{\vee}
only at the origin).
These are precisely facets
F
whose inward normal vector
u_{F}
lies in
\sigma^{\circ}\cap N
and, thus, gives a valuation centered at
\mathfrak{m}_{S}
.

Definition 10.10

(Facet normals)
.

Let
\mathfrak{a}
be an
\mathfrak{m}_{S}
-primary monomial ideal.
Let
\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})
denote the finite set of primitive inward normals
u_{F}\in\sigma\cap N

to the compact facets
F
of
\NP(\mathfrak{a})
.

Theorem 10.11

(Facet finite–max formula)
.

Let
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq R
be an
\mathfrak{m}_{S}
-primary monomial ideal and let
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq R
be any monomial ideal.
Then

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})=\max_{u\in\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})}\frac{h_{\NP(\mathfrak{a})}(u)}{h_{\NP(\mathfrak{b})}(u)}.

In particular,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})
is attained by a toric divisorial valuation

v_{u}
with
u\in\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})
.

Proof.

By
Theorem

5.17
,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{a})
is the maximum of

v(\mathfrak{a})/v(\mathfrak{b})
over the Rees valuations of
\mathfrak{a}
.
For a monomial ideal
\mathfrak{a}
, the Rees valuations correspond exactly to the torus-invariant
prime divisors on the normalized blowup of
\mathfrak{a}
. Polyhedrally, these correspond to the
compact facets of
\NP(\mathfrak{a})
, and their valuations are the toric valuations
v_{u_{F}}
,
where
u_{F}
is the primitive inward normal to the facet
F
.
(Equivalently,
\NP(\mathfrak{a})
determines a normal fan; the rays corresponding to compact facets
yield precisely these divisors.)
Thus, the maximum is achieved among the finite set
\{v_{u}:u\in\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\}
.
Finally, apply Lemma
10.6
.
∎

Note that, in
Theorem

10.11
, only compact facets contribute since
\mathfrak{a}
being
\mathfrak{m}_{S}
–primary implies
h_{\NP(\mathfrak{a})}(u)>0
precisely along inward normals to compact faces.

10.7.
Monomial filtrations in the toric setting

We now extend the preceding discussion from powers of ideals to monomial filtrations.

Definition 10.12

(Monomial filtration and Newton polyhedra)
.

A filtration
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
in
R
is
monomial
if each
\mathfrak{a}_{p}
is a monomial ideal.
For such a family define

\NP({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}):=\bigcup_{p\geq 1}\frac{1}{p}\,\NP(\mathfrak{a}_{p})\ \subseteq M_{\mathbb{R}}.

Remark 10.13
.

When
\mathfrak{a}_{p}=\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{p}}
, one has
\NP(\mathfrak{a}_{p})=p\,\NP(\mathfrak{a})
and hence

\NP({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\NP(\mathfrak{a})
. For more general families
\NP({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
is a convex region
encoding asymptotic integral closures.

Lemma 10.14

(Support function for a monomial filtration)
.

Let
{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}
be a monomial graded family and
u\in\sigma^{\circ}\cap N
.
Then

v_{u}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})=\inf_{p\geq 1}\frac{v_{u}(\mathfrak{a}_{p})}{p}=\inf_{p\geq 1}\frac{h_{\NP(\mathfrak{a}_{p})}(u)}{p}=\inf_{x\in\NP({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})}{\langle u,x\rangle}.

Proof.

The first equality is Remark
5.1
.
The second is Lemma
10.6
applied to each
\mathfrak{a}_{p}
.
For the last equality, note that
\NP({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}})
is the union of sets
\frac{1}{p}\NP(\mathfrak{a}_{p})
, and

\inf_{x\in\frac{1}{p}\NP(\mathfrak{a}_{p})}{\langle u,x\rangle}=\frac{1}{p}\inf_{y\in\NP(\mathfrak{a}_{p})}{\langle u,y\rangle}=\frac{h_{\NP(\mathfrak{a}_{p})}(u)}{p}.

Taking infima over
p
gives the claim.
∎

Lemma 10.15

(Finite–max on a finite fan)
.

Let
\sigma\subseteq N_{\mathbb{R}}
be a strongly convex rational polyhedral cone and set
\sigma^{\circ}
for its interior.
Let
\Sigma
be a finite rational polyhedral fan supported on
\sigma
with rays
\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{M}
and primitive generators
u_{1},\dots,u_{M}\in N
.
Let
\alpha,\beta:\sigma^{\circ}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be functions such that:

(1)

\alpha
and
\beta
extend to continuous functions on
\sigma^{\circ}
and are positive on
\sigma^{\circ}
;

(2)

for every cone
\tau\in\Sigma
, the restrictions
\alpha|_{\tau^{\circ}}
and
\beta|_{\tau^{\circ}}
are linear forms in
u
.

Then,

\sup_{u\in\sigma^{\circ}\cap N}\frac{\alpha(u)}{\beta(u)}\;=\;\max_{1\leq j\leq M}\frac{\alpha(u_{j})}{\beta(u_{j})}.

In particular, the supremum is a maximum attained at a primitive ray generator of
\Sigma
.

Proof.

Since
\beta(u)>0
on
\sigma^{\circ}
, we may normalize on the compact slice

K:=\{u\in\sigma^{\circ}:\beta(u)=1\}.

On each cone
\tau\in\Sigma
, the set
K\cap\tau
is a compact polytope and
\alpha/\beta=\alpha
on
K
is linear on
\tau
by hypothesis.
Hence, the maximum of
\alpha
on
K\cap\tau
is attained at a vertex of that polytope, which lies on an extreme ray of
\tau
.
Taking the maximum over finitely many cones yields that the global supremum on
K
is attained at one of the finitely many ray generators
u_{1},\dots,u_{M}
of
\Sigma
, giving the claimed formula.
∎

Corollary 10.16

(Polyhedral Newton data
\Rightarrow
finite toric candidate set)
.

Assume that the toric valuation functions

u\longmapsto v_{u}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}),\quad u\longmapsto v_{u}(\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet})

are piecewise-linear on a common finite fan
\Sigma
supported on
\sigma
(for example, when the relevant Newton data is rational polyhedral and one takes
\Sigma
a common refinement of the associated normal fans).
Then, the toric supremum is a finite maximum:

\sup_{u\in\sigma^{\circ}\cap N}\frac{v_{u}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})}{v_{u}(\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet})}\;=\;\max_{u\in\mathrm{Ray}(\Sigma)}\frac{v_{u}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})}{v_{u}(\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet})}.

Proof.

Apply
Lemma

10.15
with
\alpha(u)=v_{u}(a_{\bullet})
and
\beta(u)=v_{u}(b_{\bullet})
.
∎

Remark 10.17
.

If
f
is Newton nondegenerate and
\NP(f)
is fixed, one typically chooses a regular fan
\Sigma
refining the normal fan of
\NP(f)
.
On such a fixed toric model, the relevant toric valuation data attached to
f
(and, in the standard setup, to
J(f)
as well) is determined by linear forms on the cones of
\Sigma
.
In that situation Corollary
10.16
gives a finite candidate set that is independent of parameters as long as the Newton polyhedron is unchanged.

Theorem 10.18

(Toric valuative formula for monomial filtrations)
.

Let
\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
and

\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{q}\}_{q\geq 1}
be graded families of monomial ideals in
R
.

(1) (
Universal toric lower bound
) One has

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})\ \geq\ \sup_{u\in\sigma^{\circ}\cap N}\frac{v_{u}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})}{v_{u}(\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet})}=\sup_{u\in\sigma^{\circ}\cap N}\frac{\inf_{y\in\NP(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})}\langle u,y\rangle}{\inf_{x\in\NP(\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet})}\langle u,x\rangle}.

(2) (
Finite–max among toric valuations
) Suppose that the functions

u\mapsto v_{u}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})
and
u\mapsto v_{u}(\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet})
are piecewise-linear on a common finite
rational fan
\Sigma
supported on
\sigma
. Then

\sup_{u\in\sigma^{\circ}\cap N}\frac{v_{u}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})}{v_{u}(\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet})}=\max_{u\in\mathrm{Ray}(\Sigma)}\frac{v_{u}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})}{v_{u}(\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet})}.

(3) (
Equality with
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})
under toric testing
)
Assume in addition that for all
p,q
the containment
\mathfrak{b}_{q}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{p}}
is equivalent to
the system of inequalities

v_{u}(\mathfrak{b}_{q})\geq v_{u}(\mathfrak{a}_{p})\quad\text{for all }u\in\mathrm{Ray}(\Sigma).

Then,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})=\max_{u\in\mathrm{Ray}(\Sigma)}\frac{v_{u}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})}{v_{u}(\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet})}.

Proof.

(1) By
Theorem

5.6
we have the universal bound

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})\geq\sup_{v}\frac{v(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})}{v(\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet})}

over divisorial valuations
v
centered at
\mathfrak{m}_{S}
.
Restricting to toric divisorial valuations
v=v_{u}
gives the first inequality.
The expression in terms of
\NP(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})
and
\NP(\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet})
follows from Lemma
10.14
.

(2) This is exactly
Lemma

10.15
applied to the functions

u\mapsto v_{u}(\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet})
and
u\mapsto v_{u}(\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet})
.

(3) Under the stated testing equivalence, the finite testing hypothesis (H1) of
Theorem

5.8
holds
with
V=\{v_{u}:u\in\mathrm{Ray}(\Sigma)\}
, hence
Theorem

5.8
yields the desired equality.
∎

Remark 10.19

(Mechanism behind finite testing in the toric case)
.

The finite–max formulas obtained in this section are not a consequence of toric geometry
per se, but of the underlying polyhedral structure.
In the monomial setting, Newton polyhedra encode integral–closure containments by linear
inequalities, toric valuations correspond to linear functionals on these polyhedra, and
support functions are piecewise linear with respect to a finite fan.
As a result, the valuative optimization problem defining the Lojasiewicz exponent reduces
to a finite maximization over extremal directions, namely the primitive inward normals of
compact facets.
This mechanism provides the prototypical example of the general “finite testing” principle
developed earlier in the paper.

Remark 10.20

(Limits of the toric mechanism)
.

The explicit finite testing principle established here relies crucially on the existence of
a polyhedral model controlling all relevant integral–closure containments on a fixed toric
modification.
Outside the toric or monomial setting, such a uniform polyhedral structure need not exist:
although valuative formulas remain valid in great generality, the set of divisorial valuations
that compute the Lojasiewicz exponent may depend on the chosen principalization and need
not admit an a priori finite reduction.
Section
12
investigates to what extent Newton–type hypotheses recover finite testing
and explains why additional verifiable assumptions are necessary beyond the toric case.

11.
Łojasiewicz exponent at infinity and Newton nondegeneracy

This section focuses on the Łojasiewicz exponent
at infinity
for polynomial mappings.
Particularly, we will consider:
(i) a
single fixed birational model
on which the relevant divisor ideals become invertible, yielding
finite-minimum formulas for Łojasiewicz exponent at infinity (
Theorems

11.1
and
11.2
); and
(ii) in Newton nondegenerate settings, a
toric
model that makes the minimizing divisors explicit,
recovering classical Newton-type formulas (
Theorem

11.10
and
Corollary

11.11
).

11.1.
Finiteness of the algebraic Łojasiewicz exponent at infinity

Even though, in Definition
4.8
(and
Theorem

4.9
), the local Łojasiewicz exponent at infinity of a polynomial mapping
F
,
\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)
, is defined in the local ring

\mathcal{O}_{\overline{\Gamma}_{F},\xi}
, there exists a
uniform
description after passing to a single normal
proper birational model on which the divisor ideals
\mathscr{I}_{X}
and
\mathscr{I}_{Y}
become invertible.
On such a model, integral–closure containments are equivalent to finitely many divisorial inequalities,
so the local and global exponents at infinity admit a finite
minima
formulation similar to what we have seen for the exponent at an isolated critical point (
Theorem

5.17
).

Notations.
We keep the notation from
Section

4.3
.
In particular,
\overline{\Gamma}_{F}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}\times\mathbb{P}^{m}_{\mathbb{C}}
denotes the normalization of the
Zariski closure of the graph of
F
, with projections
\pi,\rho
and divisor ideals

\mathscr{I}_{X}=\mathcal{I}_{D_{X}}
and
\mathscr{I}_{Y}=\mathcal{I}_{D_{Y}}
.

Since
\overline{\Gamma}_{F}
is projective over
\mathbb{C}
, there exists a proper birational morphism

(11.1)

\mu:Z\longrightarrow\overline{\Gamma}_{F}

with
Z
normal such that both
\mathscr{I}_{X}\mathcal{O}_{Z}
and
\mathscr{I}_{Y}\mathcal{O}_{Z}
are invertible
(for instance, take a principalization of
\mathscr{I}_{X}\cdot\mathscr{I}_{Y}
).
Write

(11.2)

\mathscr{I}_{X}\mathcal{O}_{Z}=\mathcal{O}_{Z}(-A)\ \text{ and }\ \mathscr{I}_{Y}\mathcal{O}_{Z}=\mathcal{O}_{Z}(-B),

where
A
and
B
are effective Cartier divisors on
Z
.
For any prime divisor
E\subseteq Z
, set

\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{X}):=\text{coeff}_{E}(A)\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{Y}):=\text{coeff}_{E}(B).

Thus,
\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{X})>0
if and only if
E\subseteq\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(A)
.

Local exponents on the fixed model.

Fix
\xi\in D_{X}
and, as before, set
R_{\xi}:=\mathcal{O}_{\overline{\Gamma}_{F},\xi}
,

\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}:=\mathscr{I}_{X}\cdot R_{\xi}
, and
\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}:=\mathscr{I}_{Y}\cdot R_{\xi}
.
Base-changing
\mu
to
\Spec(R_{\xi})
yields a proper birational morphism

\mu_{\xi}:Z_{\xi}:=Z\times_{\overline{\Gamma}_{F}}\Spec(R_{\xi})\longrightarrow\Spec(R_{\xi}),

with
Z_{\xi}
normal, such that
\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}\mathcal{O}_{Z_{\xi}}
and
\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}\mathcal{O}_{Z_{\xi}}

are invertible. Denote by
A_{\xi}
and
B_{\xi}
the pullbacks of
A
and
B
, so that

\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}\mathcal{O}_{Z_{\xi}}=\mathcal{O}_{Z_{\xi}}(-A_{\xi})\text{ and }\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}\mathcal{O}_{Z_{\xi}}=\mathcal{O}_{Z_{\xi}}(-B_{\xi}).

For our purposes, the only prime divisors on
Z_{\xi}
that matter are those lying over codimension–one
points of
Z
whose image contains
\xi
; equivalently, whenever
E\subseteq Z
is a prime divisor with

\xi\in\mu(E)
, its pullback to
Z_{\xi}
has codimension–one points, and testing integral-closure
containments on
R_{\xi}
is reduced to testing the corresponding divisorial orders along such
E
.

Theorem 11.1

(Local finite–minimum formula)
.

Fix
\xi\in D_{X}
. With notation as above, one has

(11.3)

\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)=\min\left\{\frac{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{Y})}{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{X})}\ \middle|\ E\in\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(A)\text{ and }\xi\in\mu(E)\right\}\ \in\ \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}.

Proof.

For simplicity of notation, set
r_{\xi}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}}(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi})
, i.e.,

r_{\xi}=\inf\left\{\frac{q}{p}\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\ \middle|\ \mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}^{\,qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}^{\,pt}}\ \text{ for all }t\gg 1\right\}.

By
Theorem

4.9
, we have
\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)=1/r_{\xi}

(with the conventions
\inf\varnothing=\infty
,
1/\infty=0
, and
1/0=\infty
).

Fix
\frac{q}{p}\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
and
t\geq 1
.
Since
\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}\mathcal{O}_{Z_{\xi}}
and
\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}\mathcal{O}_{Z_{\xi}}
are invertible, their powers are
integrally closed on
Z_{\xi}
.
Hence, by the fixed–model integral–closure criterion (see
Lemma

5.4
),
the containment

\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}^{\,qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}^{\,pt}}\quad\text{in }R_{\xi}

is equivalent to the divisor inequality

qt\,B_{\xi}\ \geq\ pt\,A_{\xi}\quad\text{on }Z_{\xi},

and therefore to the collection of inequalities

(11.4)

qt\cdot\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{Y})\ \geq\ pt\cdot\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{X})\quad\text{for every prime divisor }E\subseteq Z\text{ with }\xi\in\mu(E).

If
E\not\subseteq\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(A)
then
\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{X})=0
and the inequalities (
11.4
) are vacuously true, i.e., such a prime divisor
E
imposes no restriction on
q/p
. If
E\in\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(A)
and
\xi\in\mu(E)
, then
\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{X})>0
.
For such
E
, the inequalities (
11.4
) are equivalent to the ratio inequalities

\frac{q}{p}\ \geq\ \frac{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{X})}{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{Y})}\quad\text{whenever }\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{Y})>0,

and if
\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{Y})=0
then (
11.4
) fails for every
\frac{q}{p}>0
.

It follows that

r_{\xi}=\sup\left\{\frac{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{X})}{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{Y})}\ \middle|\ E\in\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(A)\text{ and }\xi\in\mu(E)\right\}\ \in\ [0,\infty],

where the supremum is
\infty
if some such
E
satisfies
\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{Y})=0
.
Taking reciprocals (with the stated conventions) yields (
11.3
).
∎

Global finiteness and a finite minimum.

Recall that

\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(A)=\{\,E\subseteq Z\text{ prime divisor}\mid\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{X})>0\,\}.

Theorem 11.2

(Global finite–minimum formula)
.

With notation as above, one has

(11.5)

\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty}(F)=\min_{E\in\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(A)}\ \frac{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{Y})}{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{X})}\ \in\ \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}.

In particular,

\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty}(F)>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{Y})>0\ \text{ for every }E\in\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(A).

Proof.

By Definition
4.8
,

\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty}(F)=\inf_{\xi\in D_{X}}\ \mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F).

Applying
Theorem

11.1
gives

\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty}(F)=\inf_{\xi\in D_{X}}\ \min\left\{\frac{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{Y})}{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{X})}\ \middle|\ E\in\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(A)\text{ and }\xi\in\mu(E)\right\}.

Fix
E\in\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(A)
. Since
\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{X})>0
, the image
\mu(E)
is contained in
D_{X}
,
so there exists
\xi\in D_{X}
with
\xi\in\mu(E)
. Therefore, each
E\in\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(A)
occurs in the
inner minimum for some
\xi
. Since
\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(A)
is finite, the outer infimum equals the minimum over

E\in\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(A)
, proving (
11.5
). The final equivalence is immediate.
∎

Positivity and attainment at infinity.

We now specialize the general finite–max and Rees valuations machinery developed in
Section

5
to Łojasiewicz exponents at infinity.

Fix
\xi\in D_{X}\cap D_{Y}
.
By the valuative criterion for integral closure (see
Lemma

5.2
), for fixed
p,q,t\geq 1
the
containment
\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}^{\,qt}\subseteq\overline{\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}^{\,pt}}

holds if and only if

q\,v(\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi})\ \geq\ p\,v(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi})\quad\text{for every divisorial valuation }v\text{ of }R_{\xi}.

Consequently, the optimal exponent is characterized by

(11.6)

\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)=\inf_{v}\ \frac{v(\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi})}{v(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi})},

where the infimum ranges over all divisorial valuations
v
of
R_{\xi}
with
v(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi})>0
. In fact, as pointed out in
Lemma

5.2
, it is enough to let
v
range over finitely many Rees valuations of
\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}
.

Theorem 11.3

(Positivity and attainment)
.

Fix
\xi\in D_{X}\cap D_{Y}
.

(1)

\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)>0
if and only if there is no divisorial valuation
v
of
R_{\xi}

such that

v(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi})>0\quad\text{and}\quad v(\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi})=0.

(2)

If
\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)>0
, then the infimum in
(
11.6
) is a minimum.

(3)

More precisely, if
\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)>0
, then
\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)
is attained by
one of the finitely many Rees valuations of the ideal

\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}\subseteq R_{\xi}
.

Proof.

(1) If there exists a divisorial valuation
v
with

v(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi})>0
and
v(\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi})=0
, then the ratio

v(\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi})/v(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi})
equals
0
, so
(
11.6
) gives
\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)=0
.
Conversely, if no such valuation exists, then every divisorial valuation with

v(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi})>0
also satisfies
v(\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi})>0
, so all ratios in
(
11.6
) are positive, hence

\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)>0
.

(2)–(3)
Assume
\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)>0
.
Taking reciprocals in (
11.6
) gives

\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)}=\sup_{v}\ \frac{v(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi})}{v(\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi})},

where the supremum ranges over divisorial valuations with

v(\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi})>0
.
By (1) this supremum is finite.
Applying the finite–maximum theorem for ideal pairs
(
Theorem

5.17
) to the pair

(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi},\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi})
in the Noetherian local domain
R_{\xi}
,
the supremum is attained by one of the finitely many Rees valuations of the ideal
\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}
.
Taking reciprocals again shows that the infimum in
(
11.6
) is a minimum attained by the same
finite set of valuations.
∎

11.2.
Newton nondegeneracy and toric compactification

We recall the notion of Newton nondegeneracy for polynomial mappings (or ideals) following Saia
[
47
]
. This is slightly different than Kouchnirenko’s
[
34
]
). Particularly, a function
f\in k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is Newton nondegenerate in Kouchnirenko’s sense if and only if the ideal generated by
x_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}
is Newton nondegenerate in Saia’s sense.

Definition 11.4

(Newton polyhedron and nondegeneracy)
.

Write
F=(f_{1},\dots,f_{r})
with
f_{j}\in k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.
For
f=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}
with
c_{\alpha}\neq 0
, set

\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(f):=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}:c_{\alpha}\neq 0\}.

Define the Newton polyhedron of
F
(or equivalently, of the ideal generated by
F
) by

\Gamma(F):=\mathrm{conv}\Bigl(\bigcup_{j=1}^{r}\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(f_{j})\cup\{0\}\Bigr)+\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}\ \subseteq\ \mathbb{R}^{n},

and for
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
define

m_{w}(f):=\min_{\alpha\in\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(f)}\langle w,\alpha\rangle,\quad f_{w}:=\sum_{\alpha\in\operatorname{\mathrm{Supp}}(f)\,:\,\langle w,\alpha\rangle=m_{w}(f)}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}.

We say that
F
is
Newton nondegenerate at infinity
if for every
w\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
the system

(f_{1})_{w}=\cdots=(f_{r})_{w}=0

has no solution in
(k^{*})^{n}
.

We will use a toric compactification adapted to the Newton polyhedron at infinity following Khovanskii
[
33
]
. Let
\Sigma_{\Gamma}
denote the normal fan of
\Gamma(F)
and let
\Sigma_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}
denote the standard fan of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Choose a smooth complete fan
\Sigma
refining both
\Sigma_{\Gamma}

and
\Sigma_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}
(such a refinement exists by toric resolution of singularities;
see
[
33
,
42
]
,
[
11
, Chapter 11]
and
[
18
, Chapter 2]
). Then, the associated toric variety
X_{\Sigma}
is a smooth projective toric compactification of the torus
T=(k^{*})^{n}
, and the refinement

\Sigma\succ\Sigma_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}
induces a proper toric morphism
\pi_{\Sigma}:X_{\Sigma}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}

extending the standard open embedding
T\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
(see
[
11
, Chapter 3]
and
[
18
, Chapters 1–2]
). Moreover, since

\Sigma
also refines the normal fan of
\Gamma(F)
, the toric boundary divisors of

X_{\Sigma}
correspond to weight vectors governing the face systems (initial forms)
of
F
, which control the asymptotic behavior at infinity as in Khovanskii’s
compactification.

Notation 11.5

(Khovanskii compactification)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a smooth complete fan refining both the normal fan of
\Gamma(F)

and the fan of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, and let

\pi_{\Sigma}:X_{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{P}^{n}

be the induced toric morphism. For each cone
\sigma\in\Sigma
, let

O(\sigma)\subseteq X_{\Sigma}
denote the corresponding torus orbit.
Let
\Gamma_{F}^{\Sigma}\subseteq X_{\Sigma}\times\mathbb{P}^{r}
denote the normalization of the
closure of the graph of
F
. Write

\pi_{\Sigma}:\Gamma_{F}^{\Sigma}\to X_{\Sigma}\text{ and }\rho_{\Sigma}:\Gamma_{F}^{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{P}^{r}

for the two natural projections.

Lemma 11.6

(Values along divisors at infinity)
.

Assume that
F:\mathbb{A}^{n}_{k}\to\mathbb{A}^{r}_{k}
is Newton nondegenerate at infinity in the sense of
Definition
11.4
.
Let
\xi\in D_{X}\cap D_{Y}
and set
R_{\xi}:=\mathcal{O}_{\overline{\Gamma}_{F},\xi}
.
Then, for every divisorial valuation
v
of
R_{\xi}
,

v(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi})>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v(\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi})>0.

Equivalently, there is no divisorial valuation
v
with
v(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi})>0
and
v(\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi})=0
.

Proof.

We work on a toric compactification attached to
\Gamma(F)
and then descend to
divisorial valuations of
R_{\xi}
.

Step 1: toric boundary data and face systems.

Let
\Sigma
and
\pi_{\Sigma}:X_{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
be as above (Khovanskii’s compactification).
Write
D_{\tor}:=X_{\Sigma}\setminus(k^{*})^{n}
for the toric boundary.
Each torus–invariant prime divisor
E\subseteq D_{\tor}
corresponds to a primitive
ray vector
w_{E}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.
For every Laurent monomial
x^{\alpha}
one has
\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(x^{\alpha})=\langle w_{E},\alpha\rangle
,
and for every polynomial
f
one has
\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(f)=m_{w_{E}}(f)
with initial form
f_{w_{E}}

on the orbit
O(E):=O(w_{E})
.

Step 2: boundary intersections of the graph and Newton nondegeneracy.

Let
\Gamma_{F}^{\Sigma}\subseteq X_{\Sigma}\times\mathbb{P}^{r}
be as before, and let

\mu_{\Sigma}:\Gamma_{F}^{\Sigma}\to\overline{\Gamma}_{F}
be the induced proper birational morphism.

Fix a torus–invariant divisor
E\subseteq D_{\tor}
and consider the locally closed stratum

O(E)\subseteq X_{\Sigma}
.
Work on the target chart
\{W_{0}\neq 0\}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{r}
, with affine coordinates

y_{j}:=W_{j}/W_{0}
.
Restricting to
O(E)\times\mathbb{A}^{r}
, we work in an affine toric chart
U_{\sigma}=\Spec k[\sigma^{\vee}\cap M]

containing
O(E)
, where
\sigma\in\Sigma
is a cone whose rays include
w_{E}
. In the corresponding toric
coordinates
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}
, each coordinate
x_{i}
is a monomial in the
u_{j}
, and each polynomial
f_{j}(x)

admits a decomposition of the form

f_{j}(x)=u^{m_{j}}\Bigl((f_{j})_{w_{E}}(u)+\sum_{\langle w_{E},\alpha\rangle>m_{j}}c_{j,\alpha}u^{\alpha}\Bigr),

where
m_{j}=\min_{\alpha\in\Gamma(f_{j})}\langle w_{E},\alpha\rangle
, and
(f_{j})_{w_{E}}
is the face polynomial
consisting of those terms achieving this minimum. Consequently, the strict transform of the graph in

U_{\sigma}\times\mathbb{A}^{r}
meets
O(E)\times\mathbb{A}^{r}
if and only if the face system

(f_{1})_{w_{E}}=\cdots=(f_{r})_{w_{E}}=0

admits a solution in the torus
O(E)\cong(k^{*})^{n}
. This is the standard initial-form criterion for strict
transforms in toric modifications; see, for example,
[
33
,
42
]
.
In particular,

(11.7)

\Gamma_{F}^{\Sigma}\cap\bigl(O(E)\times\{W_{0}\neq 0\}\bigr)=\varnothing\Leftrightarrow(f_{1})_{w_{E}}=\cdots=(f_{r})_{w_{E}}=0\ \text{has no solution in }(k^{*})^{n}.

Since
F
is Newton nondegenerate at infinity, the right-hand
condition holds for every
w_{E}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
, and so (
11.7
) implies:
for every boundary divisor
E
with
w_{E}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
,

\Gamma_{F}^{\Sigma}\cap\bigl(O(E)\times\{W_{0}\neq 0\}\bigr)=\varnothing,

i.e. every point of
\Gamma_{F}^{\Sigma}
lying over
O(E)
maps into the hyperplane at infinity

H_{Y}=\{W_{0}=0\}
.
Equivalently,
\rho_{\Sigma}^{-1}(H_{Y})
contains
\mu_{\Sigma}^{-1}(\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(H_{X}))
along all such
boundary strata.

Step 3: descend to divisorial valuations over
\xi
.

Let
v
be a divisorial valuation of the local domain
R_{\xi}=\mathcal{O}_{\overline{\Gamma}_{F},\xi}
.
Choose a normal modification
\nu:W\to\overline{\Gamma}_{F}
and a prime divisor
E^{\prime}\subseteq W

with center
\xi
such that
v=\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E^{\prime}}
on
R_{\xi}
.
Take a common normal refinement
W^{\prime}\to W
and
W^{\prime}\to\Gamma_{F}^{\Sigma}
dominating both models,
and let
\widetilde{E}^{\prime}\subseteq W^{\prime}
be the strict transform of
E^{\prime}
.
Then

v(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi})=\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{\widetilde{E}^{\prime}}(\mathscr{I}_{X}\mathcal{O}_{W^{\prime}})\text{ and }v(\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi})=\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{\widetilde{E}^{\prime}}(\mathscr{I}_{Y}\mathcal{O}_{W^{\prime}}).

Assume
v(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi})>0
. This means that
\widetilde{E}^{\prime}
lies over
D_{X}=\pi^{-1}(H_{X})
,
so its image in
X_{\Sigma}
is contained in the toric boundary lying over
H_{X}
.
Hence,
\widetilde{E}^{\prime}
dominates (after possibly replacing
\Sigma
by a further refinement) a torus–invariant
boundary divisor
E\subseteq D_{\tor}
with ray vector
w_{E}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
.
Indeed, divisors corresponding to rays lying on coordinate faces of the cone
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}

map into the coordinate boundary of
\mathbb{A}^{n}_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
rather than into the hyperplane
at infinity
H_{X}=\{X_{0}=0\}
. Since
\widetilde{E}^{\prime}
lies over
D_{X}=\pi^{-1}(H_{X})
, it must dominate
a torus-invariant divisor corresponding to a ray in the relative interior
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
of the cone.
This is a standard property of toric compactifications attached to Newton polyhedra; see
[
33
,
42
]
.
By Step 2,
\Gamma_{F}^{\Sigma}
over
O(E)
maps into
H_{Y}
, so
\widetilde{E}^{\prime}
is contained in the pullback of

D_{Y}=\rho^{-1}(H_{Y})
.
Equivalently,
\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{\widetilde{E}^{\prime}}(\mathscr{I}_{Y}\mathcal{O}_{W^{\prime}})>0
, i.e.
v(\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi})>0
.

This proves
v(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi})>0\Rightarrow v(\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi})>0
.
The second statement is immediate.
∎

Corollary 11.7
.

Assume that
F
is Newton nondegenerate at infinity.
Then, for every
\xi\in D_{X}\cap D_{Y}
one has

\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)>0.

Proof.

Fix
\xi\in D_{X}\cap D_{Y}
. By Lemma
11.6
, there is no divisorial valuation
v
of
R_{\xi}

with
v(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi})>0
and
v(\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi})=0
. The conclusion follows immediately from
Theorem
11.3
(1).
∎

Remark 11.8
.

At this stage,
Theorem

11.3
guarantees finiteness and attainment by a finite set of
Rees valuations of
\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}
. In the Newton nondegenerate regime,
we can do better: we identify the relevant divisors with explicit toric boundary divisors arising
from a toric compactification adapted to
\Gamma(F)
, leading to concrete Newton polyhedral
formulas.

11.3.
Newton nondegenerate at infinity and Łojasiewicz exponent

We will show how classical formulas for Łojasiewicz exponents at infinity in the Newton nondegenerate case are recovered within our algebraic framework. Let
F:\mathbb{A}^{n}_{k}\to\mathbb{A}^{r}_{k}
denote a polynomial mapping, that is Newton nondegeneracy at infinity as in Definition
11.4
.

Proposition 11.9
.

Assume that
F
is Newton nondegenerate at infinity. Then, there exists a toric compactification

X_{\Sigma}
adapted to
\Gamma(F)
and a proper birational morphism

\mu:Z\longrightarrow\overline{\Gamma}_{F}

with
Z
smooth such that:

(1)

the reduced divisor
(D_{X})_{\text{red}}+(D_{Y})_{\text{red}}
pulls back to an SNC divisor

E_{1}+\cdots+E_{N}\quad\text{on }Z;

(2)

for every
\xi\in D_{X}\cap D_{Y}
, after base change to
\Spec(R_{\xi})
the ideals

\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}
and
\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}
pull back to invertible ideals supported on the
strict transforms of those
E_{i}
meeting
\mu^{-1}(\xi)
.

Proof.

Choose a toric compactification
X_{\Sigma}
adapted to
\Gamma(F)
.
Since
X_{\Sigma}
is proper over
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, the normalization
\Gamma_{F}^{\Sigma}
of the graph closure in

X_{\Sigma}\times\mathbb{P}^{r}
is proper over
\overline{\Gamma}_{F}
, and so there is a proper birational morphism

\Gamma_{F}^{\Sigma}\to\overline{\Gamma}_{F}
.
Now take a log resolution of the reduced divisor

\bigl(\mu_{\Sigma}^{-1}(D_{X})\bigr)_{\text{red}}+\bigl(\mu_{\Sigma}^{-1}(D_{Y})\bigr)_{\text{red}}

on
\Gamma_{F}^{\Sigma}
; composing this resolution with
\Gamma_{F}^{\Sigma}\to\overline{\Gamma}_{F}
produces
a proper birational morphism
\mu:Z\to\overline{\Gamma}_{F}
with
Z
smooth, such that the reduced pullback

(D_{X})_{\text{red}}+(D_{Y})_{\text{red}}
has SNC support. Denote its irreducible components by
E_{1},\dots,E_{N}
, proving (1).

For (2), fix
\xi\in D_{X}\cap D_{Y}
and base change
Z\to\overline{\Gamma}_{F}
to
\Spec(R_{\xi})
.
On the smooth scheme
Z_{\xi}:=Z\times_{\overline{\Gamma}_{F}}\Spec(R_{\xi})
, the pullbacks of

\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}
and
\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}
define Cartier divisors supported on the pullback of

(D_{X})_{\text{red}}+(D_{Y})_{\text{red}}
and, hence, on the strict transforms of those
E_{i}
meeting
\mu^{-1}(\xi)
.
In particular, both ideals become invertible on
Z_{\xi}
with support contained in those components,
as claimed.
∎

Theorem 11.10
.

Assume that
F
is Newton nondegenerate at infinity. Then, for every
\xi\in D_{X}\cap D_{Y}
one has

0<\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)<\infty.

Moreover, on the fixed model
Z
of Proposition
11.9
, one has

\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)=\min\left\{\frac{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i}}(\mathscr{I}_{Y})}{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i}}(\mathscr{I}_{X})}\ \middle|\ 1\leq i\leq N,\ \xi\in\mu(E_{i})\right\}.

Proof.

Fix
\xi\in D_{X}\cap D_{Y}
. By Proposition
11.9
, on the base change of
Z

to
\Spec(R_{\xi})
both
\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi}
and
\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi}
pull back to invertible ideals
supported on the strict transforms of
E_{1},\dots,E_{N}
meeting
\mu^{-1}(\xi)
. Therefore, the local
finite–minimum formula Theorem
11.1
applies on this fixed model and yields

\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)=\min\left\{\frac{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{Y})}{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{X})}\ \middle|\ E\subseteq Z\text{ prime divisor with }\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{X})>0,\ \xi\in\mu(E)\right\}.

Since every such
E
is among the components
E_{i}
, this gives the stated formula.
Positivity follows from Lemma
11.6
: if
\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i}}(\mathscr{I}_{X})>0
and

\xi\in\mu(E_{i})
, then the corresponding divisorial valuation on
R_{\xi}
satisfies

v(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi})>0
, and so
v(\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi})>0
, i.e.
\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i}}(\mathscr{I}_{Y})>0
.
Thus, every ratio in the above minimum is a positive real number. Hence,
0<\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)<\infty
as claimed.
∎

Corollary 11.11

(Complex Newton–nondegenerate polynomial mappings)
.

Let
F:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{m}
be a polynomial mapping that is Newton nondegenerate at infinity in the sense
of Definition
11.4
. Then, the Łojasiewicz exponent at infinity
\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty}(F)
is
finite and is computed by finitely many divisorial valuations coming from a toric compactification
adapted to the Newton polyhedron
\Gamma(F)
.

Proof.

By Newton nondegeneracy at infinity, Lemma
11.6
shows that no divisorial valuation
v

of
\mathcal{O}_{\overline{\Gamma}_{F},\xi}
satisfies
v(\mathscr{I}_{X,\xi})>0
and
v(\mathscr{I}_{Y,\xi})=0
.
Therefore,
\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)>0
for every
\xi\in D_{X}\cap D_{Y}
by
Theorem
11.3
(1). The finite–minimum formula of
Theorem
11.10
then expresses
\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty,\xi}(F)
, and hence
\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty}(F)
,
as a minimum over finitely many divisorial valuations on a fixed toric model associated to

\Gamma(F)
.
∎

When
n=2
, the toric divisorial valuations appearing in Corollary
11.11

correspond bijectively to the edges of the Newton polygon at infinity.

Corollary 11.12

(Plane polynomial mappings)
.

Let
F:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
be a polynomial mapping that is Newton nondegenerate at infinity.
Then,
\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty}(F)
is finite and can be computed explicitly from the Newton polygon at infinity

\Gamma(F)
by a finite set of rational numbers determined by its edges.

Proof.

By Corollary
11.11
,
\mathcal{L}^{\text{an}}_{\infty}(F)
is given by a finite minimum over
divisorial valuations arising from a toric compactification adapted to
\Gamma(F)
.
In dimension two, these divisorial valuations are exactly the toric valuations associated to the
edges of the Newton polygon. By results in
Section

10
,
the relevant orders
\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{X})
and
\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E}(\mathscr{I}_{Y})
are computed by the support
functions of
\Gamma(F)
along those edges. Substituting into the finite–minimum formula of
Theorem
11.10
yields the stated Newton–polygon expression.
∎

Corollaries

11.11
and
11.12
recover classical results on Łojasiewicz exponent at infinity in the Newton nondegenerate case; see, for instance,
[
44
]
.

Remark 11.13
.

Over
\mathbb{R}
, the finiteness of Łojasiewicz exponent at infinity is more subtle. This is mainly because we do not have the equivalence in
Theorem

4.9
nor a single birational model which realizes all relevant valuations. Nevertheless, similar results in Newton nondegenerate situations, where divisors at infinity are torus-invariant and relevant valuations are toric valuations associated to finitely many rays/facets (supporting hyperplanes of the corresponding Newton polyhedra), were obtained under various additional hypotheses (see, for example,
[
24
,
41
,
43
]
).

Part VII
Necessity and Limits of the Hypotheses

In this part, we examine verification, uniformity, and sharpness issues for the
finite–max principle and for the Newton–type formulas arising from the
valuative framework.

12.
Uniformity, verification, and sharpness

This section addresses when the finite testing hypotheses from
Sections

5
and
10
can be verified uniformly in families, and why these hypotheses are close to optimal. On the positive side, in the Newton nondegenerate regime we prove the existence of a single toric modification simultaneously principalizing finitely many ideals, producing a uniform finite divisorial testing set across an entire collection of data (
Theorem

12.1
); as consequences we obtain automatic finite testing for broad Newton-controlled families (
Theorem

12.2
) and a genuine polyhedral wall–chamber structure governing the variation of
\mathcal{L}
(
Theorem

12.3
). On the sharpness side, we construct monomial examples where successive principalizations introduce new relevant facets, showing that outside verifiable finite-testing regimes one cannot expect any a priori finite reduction of the valuative optimization problem (
Theorem

12.4
).

12.1.
Newton polyhedra for ideals

Throughout this section we fix a regular local ring
(R,\mathfrak{m})
of dimension
n

and a regular system of parameters
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
. When convenient for
notation we write
R=\Bbbk[[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]]
, but all constructions below depend
only on the chosen parameters; see
[
22
]
for more details on Newton nondegenerate ideals with respect to a regular system of parameters in a regular local ring.

12.2.
Uniform verification on a fixed toric model

We now explain how, in Newton nondegenerate situations, the hypotheses of the
log-resolution criterion in Section
5
(and its integral-closure reduction

Lemma

5.4
) are
automatically
satisfied for a large
class of filtrations.

Theorem 12.1

(Toric model simultaneously principalizing finitely many Newton nondegenerate ideals)
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be an excellent equicharacteristic regular local ring with chosen parameters

x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
. Let
I^{(1)},\dots,I^{(r)}\subseteq R
be ideals such that each

I^{(j)}
is Newton nondegenerate with respect to
x
(i.e., the relevant face ideals define smooth complete intersections on the torus).
Then, there exists a proper birational morphism
\pi:Y\to\Spec R

such that:

(1)

Y
is regular and the exceptional divisor
E=\sum_{i=1}^{N}E_{i}
has simple normal crossings.

(2)

For each
j=1,\dots,r
, the total transform of
I^{(j)}
is an invertible ideal:

I^{(j)}\mathcal{O}_{Y}\ =\ \mathcal{O}_{Y}(-F_{j}),\quad F_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{ij}E_{i},\ \ a_{ij}\in\mathbb{N}.

(3)

The morphism
\pi
can be chosen toric with respect to the chosen parameters
x
,
coming from a regular subdivision of a fan refining the Newton fans of
\Gamma(I^{(j)})
.

Proof.

We retain the notation and terminology of
[
22
]
for Newton nondegeneracy with respect to a chosen regular system of parameters.

Step 1: reduction to the complete case.

Since
R
is excellent regular local, the
\widehat{R}
is faithfully flat over
R
. All Newton polyhedra and Newton nondegeneracy conditions are expressed in terms of supports with respect to the fixed regular system of parameters
x
, and so pass to
\widehat{R}
without change. Moreover, the birational toric modifications we construct are defined combinatorially from a fan, hence descend to
R
when they exist over
\widehat{R}
.
Thus, we may assume
R=\Bbbk[[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]]
.

Step 2: construction of a common toric modification.

For each
j
, let
\Gamma(I^{(j)})\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
be the Newton polyhedron
with respect to the chosen parameters
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
,
and let
\Sigma^{(j)}
denote its Newton fan.
Since we have finitely many such fans,
there exists a common rational polyhedral refinement
\Sigma^{\prime}

refining all
\Sigma^{(j)}
.
Choose a regular subdivision
\Sigma
of
\Sigma^{\prime}
, and let

\pi=\pi_{\Sigma}:Y=X_{\Sigma}\longrightarrow\Spec R

be the associated toric morphism.

Because
\Sigma
is regular,

Y
is regular and its toric boundary divisor
E=\sum_{i=1}^{N}E_{i}

is a simple normal crossings divisor.
This proves (1).
Property (3) holds by construction.

Step 3: principalization of each
I^{(j)}
.

Fix
j\in\{1,\dots,r\}
.

Since
\Sigma
refines the Newton fan of
\Gamma(I^{(j)})
,
Khovanskii’s toroidal resolution theorem (see
[
33
,
42
]
) for Newton nondegenerate systems applies.
Specifically, for Newton nondegenerate ideals with respect to the parameters
x
,
the toric modification associated to a regular subdivision of the Newton fan
gives a good resolution and principalization.
In particular,
I^{(j)}\mathcal{O}_{Y}

is an invertible ideal sheaf supported on the toric boundary.

Step 4: computation of the coefficients
a_{ij}
.

Let
E_{1},\dots,E_{N}
be the torus-invariant prime divisors of
Y

lying over the closed point,
and let
w_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
be the primitive ray vector corresponding to
E_{i}
.
Then, the order of vanishing of a polynomial
f
along
E_{i}
is given by (see
[
33
,
42
]
):

\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i}}(f)=\min_{\alpha\in\Gamma(f)}\langle w_{i},\alpha\rangle.

Applying this to generators of
I^{(j)}
yields

\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i}}\bigl(I^{(j)}\mathcal{O}_{Y}\bigr)=\min_{\alpha\in\Gamma(I^{(j)})}\langle w_{i},\alpha\rangle=:a_{ij}\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
I^{(j)}\mathcal{O}_{Y}
is invertible and determined by these divisorial orders,
we obtain

I^{(j)}\mathcal{O}_{Y}=\mathcal{O}_{Y}\!\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{ij}E_{i}\right).

This proves (2) for each
j
.
∎

Theorem 12.2

(Automatic finite testing for Newton controlled families)
.

Assume the setup of
Theorem

12.1
, and fix real numbers

\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}\geq 0
. Define a graded family of ideals

\mathfrak{a}_{t}\ :=\ \overline{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\big(I^{(j)}\big)^{\lceil\lambda_{j}t\rceil}}\quad(t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}),

where
\overline{(\cdot)}
denotes integral closure.
Then, on the same model
\pi:Y\to\Spec R
of
Theorem

12.1
,
one has for every
t
:

\mathfrak{a}_{t}\ =\ \pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{Y}\Big(-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big\lfloor t\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}a_{ij}\Big\rfloor\,E_{i}\Big).

In particular, for any other family
{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}
of the same form (possibly with different coefficients),
the containment relations
\mathfrak{b}_{q}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}_{p}
are determined by the finitely many divisorial valuations

\{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, i.e., the hypotheses of the finite-testing criterion
in
Theorem

5.8
are satisfied with testing set
\{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i}}\}
.

Proof.

Let
\pi:Y\to\Spec R
be as in
Theorem

12.1
. As constructed,
Y
is regular, so
Y
is normal. Write

I^{(j)}\mathcal{O}_{Y}=\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-F_{j})

with

F_{j}=\sum_{i}a_{ij}E_{i}
.
Set

D(t)\ :=\ \sum_{j=1}^{r}\lceil\lambda_{j}t\rceil\,F_{j}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}(t)E_{i},\quad d_{i}(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lceil\lambda_{j}t\rceil\,a_{ij}\in\mathbb{N}.

Step 1: compute integral closures on a fixed principalization.

Since
I^{(j)}\mathcal{O}_{Y}
is invertible, so is
\prod_{j}(I^{(j)})^{\lceil\lambda_{j}t\rceil}\mathcal{O}_{Y}
,
and in fact

\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{r}(I^{(j)})^{\lceil\lambda_{j}t\rceil}\Big)\mathcal{O}_{Y}\ =\ \mathcal{O}_{Y}\big(-D(t)\big).

By the fixed-model integral-closure reduction (
Lemma

5.4
),
the integral closure of
\prod_{j}(I^{(j)})^{\lceil\lambda_{j}t\rceil}
equals the pushforward
of the corresponding invertible sheaf:

\mathfrak{a}_{t}\ =\ \overline{\prod_{j=1}^{r}(I^{(j)})^{\lceil\lambda_{j}t\rceil}}\ =\ \pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{Y}\big(-D(t)\big).

Step 2: replace
\lceil\lambda_{j}t\rceil
by linear bounds in
t
.

Write
D(t)=\sum_{i}d_{i}(t)E_{i}
as above. Since
d_{i}(t)
is an integer-valued, piecewise linear
function in
t
with slopes
\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}a_{ij}
, we have

d_{i}(t)\ =\ \Big\lfloor t\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}a_{ij}\Big\rfloor\ +\ \epsilon_{i}(t),\quad\epsilon_{i}(t)\in\{0,1,\dots,r\}.

Absorbing the bounded error into flooring yields the displayed formula; this is the same bookkeeping already used in
Section
5
when passing between integer- and real-indexed families.

Step 3: finite testing.

For any
p,q
, the condition
\mathfrak{b}_{q}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}_{p}
is equivalent to the inequality of divisors
on
Y

D_{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(q)\ \geq\ D_{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(p)

i.e., coefficientwise inequalities along
E_{i}
.
Equivalently,

\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i}}(\mathfrak{b}_{q})\geq\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i}}(\mathfrak{a}_{p})

for all

.
This is precisely the finite-testing hypothesis in
Theorem

5.8
with testing set

\{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i}}\}

∎

12.3.
Uniformity in families and wall–chamber decomposition

We now push the previous verification to a genuine structural statement in parameter families.

Theorem 12.3

(Polyhedral wall–chamber structure)
.

Assume the setup of
Theorem

12.2
, and let
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be
a parameter set. Suppose that for each
s\in S
we have two families

{\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(s)=\{\mathfrak{a}(s)_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\quad{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(s)=\{\mathfrak{b}(s)_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},

each of the form in
Theorem

12.2
, with coefficients

\lambda_{j}=\lambda_{j}(s)
depending
piecewise linearly
on
s
.
Assume moreover that the Newton polyhedra
\Gamma(I^{(j)}_{s})
are independent of
s
and the
nondegeneracy is uniform, so that a single toric model
\pi:Y\to\Spec R
principalizes all

I^{(j)}_{s}
simultaneously.

Then, there exists a finite polyhedral decomposition

S=\bigcup_{\alpha}C_{\alpha}

such that on each chamber
C_{\alpha}
there is an index
i(\alpha)\in\{1,\dots,N\}
with

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(s)}\big({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(s)\big)\ =\ \frac{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i(\alpha)}}\big({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(s)\big)}{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i(\alpha)}}\big({\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(s)\big)}\quad\text{for all }s\in C_{\alpha}.

In particular,
s\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(s)}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(s))
is piecewise rational-linear and the
maximizing divisor is locally constant on chambers.

Proof.

Fix the common model
\pi:Y\to\Spec R
as in
Theorem

12.2
and its exceptional prime divisors
E_{1},\dots,E_{N}
. Write
E=\sum_{i=1}^{N}E_{i}
.

Step 1: linearity of divisor coefficients.

By
Theorem

12.2
, for each
s
we can write

\mathfrak{a}(s)_{t}=\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{Y}\big(-D^{(s)}_{\mathfrak{a}}(t)\big),\quad\mathfrak{b}(s)_{t}=\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{Y}\big(-D^{(s)}_{\mathfrak{b}}(t)\big),

where

D^{(s)}_{\mathfrak{a}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}d^{(s)}_{\mathfrak{a},i}(t)\,E_{i},\quad D^{(s)}_{\mathfrak{b}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}d^{(s)}_{\mathfrak{b},i}(t)\,E_{i}

and each coefficient
d^{(s)}_{\mathfrak{a},i}(t)
(resp.
d^{(s)}_{\mathfrak{b},i}(t)
) is an integer-valued
piecewise linear function whose slope in
t
is a piecewise linear function of
s
.
Hence, for each fixed
i
the asymptotic slopes

\mu_{\mathfrak{a},i}(s):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i}}\big(\mathfrak{a}(s)_{t}\big),\quad\mu_{\mathfrak{b},i}(s):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i}}\big(\mathfrak{b}(s)_{t}\big)

exist and are piecewise linear functions of
s
.

Step 2: finite-max formula on the fixed testing set.

By the finite-testing criterion,
Theorem

5.8
, with testing set
\{\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i}}\}
(we can restrict to those with
\mu_{\mathfrak{b},i}(s)>0
),

\mathcal{L}_{{\mathfrak{b}_{\bullet}}(s)}({\mathfrak{a}_{\bullet}}(s))\ =\ \max_{1\leq i\leq N}\ \frac{\mu_{\mathfrak{a},i}(s)}{\mu_{\mathfrak{b},i}(s)}.

For each ordered pair
(i,j)
, the inequality

\frac{\mu_{\mathfrak{a},i}(s)}{\mu_{\mathfrak{b},i}(s)}\ \geq\ \frac{\mu_{\mathfrak{a},j}(s)}{\mu_{\mathfrak{b},j}(s)}

is equivalent to the linear inequality

\mu_{\mathfrak{a},i}(s)\mu_{\mathfrak{b},j}(s)-\mu_{\mathfrak{a},j}(s)\mu_{\mathfrak{b},i}(s)\ \geq\ 0.

By
Theorem

12.2
, for each fixed exceptional divisor
E_{i}
the functions

\mu_{\mathfrak{a},i}(t)=\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i}}(\mathfrak{a}_{t})\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{\mathfrak{b},i}(s)=\operatorname{\mathrm{ord}}_{E_{i}}(\mathfrak{b}_{s})

are piecewise affine linear functions of
t
and
s
, respectively.
More precisely, there exist real constants
c_{\mathfrak{a},i},c_{\mathfrak{b},i}
and bounded error terms

\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{a},i}(t),\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{b},i}(s)
such that

\mu_{\mathfrak{a},i}(t)=c_{\mathfrak{a},i}\,t+\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{a},i}(t),\quad\mu_{\mathfrak{b},i}(s)=c_{\mathfrak{b},i}\,s+\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{b},i}(s),

where
\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{a},i}(t)
(resp.
\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{b},i}(s)
) is locally constant away from
a finite set of jump points determined by the ceiling functions

\lceil\lambda_{j}t\rceil
(resp.
\lceil\lambda_{j}s\rceil
).
Consequently, on each region of the
(s,t)
–plane where these ceiling functions are fixed,
the functions
\mu_{\mathfrak{a},i}(t)
and
\mu_{\mathfrak{b},i}(s)
are affine linear.
Hence, refining
S
by the finite arrangement of the linearity regions of all

\mu_{\mathfrak{a},i},\mu_{\mathfrak{b},i}
yields a finite polyhedral decomposition on which all comparisons are
given by linear inequalities.

Step 3: chambers and constancy of maximizers.

Intersecting the regions defined by the finitely many pairwise comparison inequalities for the
index attaining the maximum produces the claimed chambers
C_{\alpha}
, and on each chamber the
maximizing index
i(\alpha)
is constant.
∎

12.4.
Sharpness: why one needs verifiable hypotheses

We close with an explicit sharpness statement showing that without a uniform principalization
mechanism, no
a priori
fixed finite testing set can govern all containments in a large class.

Theorem 12.4

(Sharpness via monomial ideals with new facets)
.

Let
R=\Bbbk[[x,y]]
. For each integer
N\geq 2
, set

J_{N}\ :=\ \overline{(x^{N},\ y^{N^{2}})}\ \subseteq R.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be any finite set of real-valued rank-one valuations on
R
centered at
\mathfrak{m}
.
Then, there exist integers
N\geq 2
and
p,q\geq 1
such that:

(1)

For every
v\in\mathcal{V}
, one has
v(\mathfrak{m}^{q})\geq v(J_{N}^{p})
.

(2)

Nevertheless,
\mathfrak{m}^{q}\not\subseteq J_{N}^{p}=\overline{J_{N}^{p}}
.

In particular, no single finite set of valuations can serve as a universal testing set for all
containments of the form
\mathfrak{m}^{q}\subseteq\overline{J_{N}^{p}}
as
N
varies.

Proof.

Fix a finite set
\mathcal{V}
as in the statement. For each
v\in\mathcal{V}
, set

a_{v}:=v(x)>0
and
b_{v}:=v(y)>0
.

Step 1: choose
N
so that
v(J_{N})=Na_{v}
for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
.

For
J_{N}=\overline{(x^{N},y^{N^{2}})}
, the valuative criterion for integral closure gives

v(J_{N})\ =\ \min\{Na_{v},\ N^{2}b_{v}\}.

Choose an integer
N\geq 2
such that

N\ >\ 1+\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\frac{a_{v}}{b_{v}}.

Then,
Nb_{v}>a_{v}
for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
, hence
N^{2}b_{v}>Na_{v}
and, therefore,

v(J_{N})=Na_{v}\quad\text{for all }v\in\mathcal{V}.

Step 2: choose
q
so that
v(\mathfrak{m}^{q})\geq v(J_{N}^{p})
for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
.

Set

M\ :=\ \max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\left\lceil\frac{a_{v}}{\min\{a_{v},b_{v}\}}\right\rceil.

Then,
M\geq 1
, and by the choice of
N
we have
\frac{a_{v}}{b_{v}}<N-1
for all
v
, and so

\frac{a_{v}}{\min\{a_{v},b_{v}\}}\ \leq\ \max\left\{1,\frac{a_{v}}{b_{v}}\right\}\ <\ N-1,

so in particular
M\leq N-1
.

Now choose any integer
p\geq 1
and set
q\ :=\ pNM.

Since
v(\mathfrak{m})=\min\{a_{v},b_{v}\}
and
v(J_{N})=Na_{v}
for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
, we obtain

v(\mathfrak{m}^{q})\ =\ q\,\min\{a_{v},b_{v}\}\ \geq\ pN\cdot\frac{a_{v}}{\min\{a_{v},b_{v}\}}\cdot\min\{a_{v},b_{v}\}\ =\ pNa_{v}\ =\ v(J_{N}^{p})

for every
v\in\mathcal{V}
. This proves (1).

Step 3: explicit witness that
\mathfrak{m}^{q}\not\subseteq J_{N}^{p}
.

We claim that
y^{q}\in\mathfrak{m}^{q}
but
y^{q}\notin J_{N}^{p}
. The first inclusion is clear.

For the second, note that
J_{N}
is a monomial integrally closed ideal, and membership in
J_{N}^{p}

is determined by its Newton polyhedron. The Newton polyhedron of
J_{N}
is given by

\frac{u}{N}+\frac{v}{N^{2}}\ \geq\ 1,

hence that of
J_{N}^{p}
is

\frac{u}{N}+\frac{v}{N^{2}}\ \geq\ p.

For
y^{q}
, the exponent vector is
(u,v)=(0,q)
, so

\frac{u}{N}+\frac{v}{N^{2}}\ =\ \frac{q}{N^{2}}\ =\ \frac{pNM}{N^{2}}\ =\ p\cdot\frac{M}{N}.

Since
M\leq N-1
, we have
\frac{M}{N}<1
, and so
\frac{q}{N^{2}}<p
. Therefore,
y^{q}\notin J_{N}^{p}
.
Consequently,
\mathfrak{m}^{q}\not\subseteq J_{N}^{p}
, proving (2).

This shows that no fixed finite set of valuations can test all containments of the form

\mathfrak{m}^{q}\subseteq J_{N}^{p}=\overline{J_{N}^{p}}
uniformly as
N
varies.
∎

Part VIII
Outlook and the Appendix

In this final part, we collect open questions and directions suggested by the
valuative framework developed in this paper.

13.
Concluding remarks and open problems

This paper develops a valuative framework for understanding the Łojasiewicz exponent, with a focus on finite–max mechanisms.
At the same time, Section
12
shows that finite
testing cannot be expected in full generality: there exist natural filtrations for which no
finite set of valuations suffices to verify containment. This highlights a fundamental
dichotomy between situations where the normalized Rees algebra provides complete control and
those where the valuative supremum remains intrinsically infinite.

Finite testing and birational models.

A central question is to identify intrinsic conditions guaranteeing finite verification.

Question 13.1
.

Which properties of a graded family or filtration ensure that integral–closure containments
can be verified on a fixed birational model? In particular, can finite testing be detected
purely from the structure of the normalized Rees algebra?

Answering this would clarify when the Łojasiewicz exponent is governed by finitely many
divisorial valuations and when more subtle valuation-theoretic phenomena occur.

Extremal valuations and geometric structure.

When finite testing holds, the valuations computing the exponent play a distinguished role.

Question 13.2
.

What geometric or intrinsic properties characterize valuations that compute the
Łojasiewicz exponent? For instance, are such valuations uniquely determined by the
normalized Rees algebra, or do they satisfy stability or minimality properties within the
valuation space?

Understanding these extremal valuations would further clarify the stratification and
rigidity phenomena described in Part
V
.

Variation in families.

Part
V
discusses rigidity and stratification results for the Łojasiewicz exponents of families.

Question 13.3
.

More generally, under what conditions is the Łojasiewicz exponent constructible or
semicontinuous in algebraic or analytic families? Can weaker hypotheses than finite testing
still ensure partial rigidity or controlled variation?

Such results would contribute to a broader algebraic understanding of equisingularity and
asymptotic invariants in families.

Role of non–divisorial valuations.

While divisorial valuations suffice in many situations, Section
12

shows that more general valuations may be needed in principle.

Question 13.4
.

Are there natural geometric or algebraic settings in which non–divisorial valuations
necessarily compute the Łojasiewicz exponent? Conversely, what conditions ensure that
divisorial valuations always suffice?

Clarifying the role of non–divisorial valuations would help delineate the limits of
birational and Rees–algebraic methods.

These questions suggest that the valuative and Rees–algebraic framework developed here
provides not only computational tools, but also a structural foundation for studying
asymptotic containment invariants. We expect that further development of this perspective
will lead to new insights into singularity theory, equisingularity, and related asymptotic
invariants.
