The relationship between effort tolerance, functional fitness, age, and rehabilitation training in women over 60 living in Polish nursing homes
Authors: Małgorzata Fortuna, Antonina Kaczorowska, Zofia Ignasiak, Jacek Szczurowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDecline in physical activity is believed to contribute to reduced exercise tolerance and diminished functional fitness. This underscores the importance of rehabilitation training in maintaining adequate levels of both. The aim of this study was to evaluate the relationship between exercise tolerance, functional fitness, age, and participation in rehabilitation training among women over the age of 60. The study included 106 women aged 60 to 89, of whom 60 participated in rehabilitation training and 46 did not. To assess exercise tolerance and functional fitness, researchers employed the 6-min Walk Test and the Short Physical Performance Battery. No significant relationship was found between age and either functional fitness or exercise tolerance among physically active women. Similarly, no association was observed between age and exercise tolerance in the group of inactive women. However, a significant correlation was identified between age and functional fitness in inactive women (p = 0.001). A notable difference in exercise tolerance between active and inactive participants was observed only in the subgroup of women aged 75 to 89, with those engaged in rehabilitation activities achieving significantly better results (p = 0.008). The level of physical activity among female nursing home residents from the western region of Poland is too low and poorly balanced. Rehabilitation training should be individually selected to bring benefits to the elderly.Keywords: Exercise tolerance, Functional fitness, Older adultsSubject terms: Health care, Health occupations, Physiology

The projected increase in the proportion of people aged 65 and older—from 19% in 2020 to 33% in 2050 poses challenges to the effectiveness of long-term care in Poland. This issue also affects the availability of long-term care through well-functioning social care homes. It is essential to implement public policy measures to ensure that older people have access to appropriate and personalized services1.

The number of people residing in social care homes in Poland is increasing, largely due to the aging of society. However, the availability of caregivers and geriatricians remains a challenge, as there are currently too few professionals employed to meet future demand. Most existing studies focus on the functional fitness, exercise tolerance, and health status of older adults living independently2–4. In contrast, significantly less research has been conducted on older adults living in social care homes5,6, especially in Poland7,8. In nursing homes, older individuals are provided with structured care and activation programs within their place of residence. Further research is needed to develop optimal conditions for dignified aging among older adults, who increasingly reside in social care facilities.

Lifestyle, including regular engagement in appropriately selected physical activity, is a key factor in preventing lifestyle-related diseases and slowing down age-related changes. Numerous studies have confirmed that insufficient physical activity contributes to a decline in physiological parameters. Aging is associated with reduced daily mobility and diminished functional abilities9. Researchers emphasize that improving functional fitness and physical endurance is a crucial preventive factor for many diseases and significantly enhances quality of life. This conclusion applies to all age groups, particularly as age-related regression affects various bodily systems, leading to reduced efficiency7,10,11. The American College of Sports Medicine and the American Heart Association have developed guidelines concerning exercise load, duration, and intensity, which promote health benefits and lead to noticeable adaptive changes in the body. A well designed training regimen balanced in terms of intensity and volume produces the desired adaptations, based on the simple principle that too little is ineffective and too much may be harmful11. The most noticeable beneficial adaptive changes are closely linked to the volume and intensity of physical activity performed. Especially in older adults, physical activity should be purposefully tailored to maximize these adaptations while promoting optimal functional fitness and physical endurance. This approach supports greater autonomy and contributes to dignified aging. Research by various authors has shown that high-intensity interval training (HIIT) induces positive changes in cardiovascular and respiratory fitness, functional capacity, and muscle strength in older individuals, helping to maintain aerobic fitness and slow the progression of sarcopenia10. Physical activity reduces overall morbidity and mortality among older adults12. It also lowers the risk of falls, which pose serious threats to the health and even lives of seniors13. Older individuals should be encouraged to minimize sedentary behavior and avoid prolonged periods of physical inactivity14. The decline in physical activity and exercise tolerance with age is a natural physiological process. However, the rate of this decline varies among individuals depending on their age and level of physical activity. Researchers have found that older adults who engage in regular physical activity and maintain an active family and social life experience a slower rate of decline15.

Accordingly, the following research question was formulated: Do exercise tolerance and functional fitness among women residing in social welfare homes differ depending on age and participation in rehabilitation training?

Aim of the study

The aim of this study was to evaluate the relationship between exercise tolerance, functional fitness, age, and rehabilitation training in women over the age of 60 residing in social welfare homes in Poland.

Materials and methods

Research design and participants

The study involved 106 women, including 60 who participated in rehabilitation training and 46 who did not, aged between 60 and 89 years. All participants were residents of social welfare homes located in the south-western region of Poland. The research was conducted in 13 cities across Poland and took place between 2020 and 2023.

The division into groups A1, A2, B1, and B2 was introduced to enable clear and methodologically consistent comparisons across age categories and levels of physical activity.

The active group (Group A) consisted of younger individuals who regularly participated in rehabilitation training. The inactive group (Group B) included individuals who did not attend any physical activity sessions. Both the active and inactive groups were further divided into two age categories. Groups A1 and B1 included participants aged 60 to 74, while Groups A2 and B2 included those aged 75 to 89. Within the active group, subgroups A1 and A2 were formed, and within the inactive group, subgroups B1 and B2 were established.

Participants in the active groups reported that they had been attending both types of sessions described below for at least six months. Individuals in this group took part in rehabilitation classes regularly, twice a week. Each session lasted between one hour and one hour and twenty minutes, depending on the individual adaptive capacity of the residents. The sessions took place in a rehabilitation room adapted for exercise. The facility was equipped with devices designed to activate both upper and lower limbs, with adjustable resistance settings to suit the level of exertion. The program consisted of individualized and progressive strength exercises, balance training, and aerobic conditioning. Each session began with a warm-up lasting up to 10 min, focused on increasing the range of motion in various joints. The strength training segment, lasting up to 25 min, included exercises targeting both upper and lower body muscle groups using external weights. The balance training, lasting up to 10 min, involved progressively challenging exercises. It began with transitioning from a seated to a standing position. While standing, the number of support points was gradually reduced: starting with both hands, then one hand, followed by both feet, and finally standing on one leg only. The final part of the session, lasting up to 30 min, focused on improving aerobic endurance and was conducted on a stationary bicycle. Each session concluded with breathing and relaxation exercises. Each training session lasted between one hour and one hour and twenty minutes. Additionally, once a week, group classes were held in the exercise room, focusing primarily on physical activities related to everyday functionality. These exercises included: repeated full-range joint movements of the upper and lower limbs, bends in all planes, rotational movements of the torso, standing up from a chair with various arm positions, static and dynamic balance exercises, and fine motor tasks involving small manipulative movements. Most of the exercises were performed in a seated position. These sessions lasted one hour. The physical exercise program for the residents included in the active group maintained the same overall structure throughout the study.

Methods

The assessment of each participant lasted approximately 30 min and was conducted within a single day. Measurements including the 6-min walk test (6MWT) and the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) were carried out in residential care homes. The 6-min walk test was conducted as the final assessment16. The study was carried out in 13 cities across Poland and spanned the years 2020 to 2023.

Methods and procedures of functional fitness tests

Physical fitness was assessed using the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB). Physical endurance was evaluated with the 6-min walk test. The SPPB provides information about physical capabilities, which are reflected in the ability to perform basic activities of daily living. The SPPB test assessed physical fitness in three areas: lower limb strength, static balance, and walking speed. To evaluate lower limb endurance and strength, the participant was asked to rise from a chair with arms crossed over the chest. If the individual successfully completed a single sit-to-stand repetition, they were then instructed to perform five consecutive repetitions as quickly as possible. The total time taken to complete the task was used for result interpretation. To assess static balance, the participant attempted to maintain stability for ten seconds in three different positions:

Standing with feet placed parallel to each other.

Standing with feet placed parallel to each other.

Semi-tandem stance, with the side of one heel touching the big toe of the opposite foot.

Semi-tandem stance, with the side of one heel touching the big toe of the opposite foot.

Full tandem stance, with one foot placed directly behind the other so that the heel of the front foot touched the toes of the back foot. The participant proceeded to the next position only if the previous one was completed without difficulty and could be maintained for ten seconds.

To assess walking speed, the participant walked a straight four-metre distance as quickly as possible. The recorded time to cover the distance was used for result interpretation.

To assess walking speed, the participant walked a straight four-metre distance as quickly as possible. The recorded time to cover the distance was used for result interpretation.

SPPB test results were interpreted using a scoring table based on performance in each individual trial. In each domain of the SPPB test, participants could score from zero to four points, where zero indicated inability to perform the task and four represented the highest level of functional ability. The total score ranged from zero to twelve points and was used to define the overall level of physical fitness17,18.

Physical endurance was assessed using the 6-min walk test

The 6-min walk test was used to indirectly assess aerobic capacity19. Participants were instructed to cover the longest possible distance within 6 min. The distance was recorded in metres. The test was conducted on a flat, non-slippery surface. The walking route was marked around a rectangle measuring twenty metres by five metres.

Discussion

The interpretation of the findings was deliberately limited to the empirical data obtained in this study, without extending conclusions beyond the scope of the results.

Many studies report that the aging process contributes to a decline in functional fitness and aerobic endurance. As a result, this leads to difficulties in performing daily activities and in the normal functioning of older adults. There is a trend in aging associated with a reduction in muscle mass and the efficiency of the cardiovascular and respiratory systems. Muscle mass and strength decrease by 30−50% between the ages of 30 and 80. Muscle strength declines by approximately 12–14% per decade after the age of 50. Resistance training is considered a promising intervention that may slow down the loss of muscle function and mass associated with aging. The ability of older adults to engage in aerobic activities such as walking and running declines with age. This is largely due to a decrease in maximal oxygen uptake by approximately 0.5–1.0% per year22. Differences in the average walking distance achieved in the test across various age groups both among inactive and active individuals examined in this study are not statistically significan. A downward trend associated with aging is evident in both groups (inactive and active). Comparison of the 6-min walk test results between active and inactive individuals within the same age groups mostly did not reveal statistically significant differences. Statistically significant differences in walking distance were observed only in the group of older women (aged 75–89). Women participating in training (group A2) achieved greater walking distances. This group demonstrated a higher level of exercise tolerance. Most likely, only in this age range was a more effective activation applied—though still insufficient. The active women in the study engaged in aerobic capacity-enhancing activities twice a week for up to 30 min. This was dependent on the adaptation process. The training program was based on stationary cycling. Aerobic exercises for older adults may include various forms such as: walking with changes in pace and direction, treadmill walking, step-ups, stair climbing, and dancing. The choice should depend on individual preferences, availability, coexisting cognitive and physical conditions, and specific musculoskeletal issues, in order to achieve the best adaptive effects. According to the International Consensus on Exercise for Older Adults (ICFSR), endurance training may begin with 5–10 min during the initial weeks, progressing to 15–30 min for the remainder of the program, with a frequency of 3 to 7 days per week22. It can be assumed that the training program conducted among the examined female residents was insufficient, with too few sessions per week. Therefore, the expected adaptive changes were not achieved. No significant differences in the distance covered during the 6-min walk test were observed between active and inactive women in the younger age group.

Moreover, when comparing the results of the 6-min walk test for senior women in Poland living independently (not hospitalized, not residing in nursing homes, etc.), significantly lower scores were observed among women living in nursing homes across all age groups. This conclusion is based on a comparison of the values obtained in this study with normative data for Polish older people21. This applies to all compared age groups of women. Nursing home residents achieved significantly lower values. It can be assumed that activation programs and rehabilitation are still applied too infrequently and with insufficient intensity relative to the needs of senior women living in nursing homes in Poland. Aerobic capacity is an important component of physical fitness, and aerobic training should be part of the exercise routine for both fit and frail older adults. Good exercise tolerance is associated with reduced mortality risk and many other chronic diseases, and it determines autonomy22.

Walking ability is a strong predictor of survival in older adults, and its maintenance should be a priority in the oldest age groups23. It can be assumed that the activation programs implemented for older women in Polish nursing homes are insufficient.

In Poland, nursing homes operate based on the Regulation of the Minister of Labour and Social Policy on nursing homes from 2012, with amendments introduced in 2017 and final revisions in 201824. According to this regulation, a nursing home, in addition to providing services related to basic living needs and caregiving, should also offer therapeutic services. These include improving residents’ physical fitness, activating them, and enabling participation in occupational therapy. However, it is often the case that the number of occupational therapists and physiotherapists employed in nursing homes is insufficient relative to the number of residents. Physiotherapists also do not always sufficiently encourage residents to engage in physical activity, often focusing instead on physical treatments. Older residents of nursing homes frequently refuse to participate in activation activities. They do not see the point, are unwilling to make the effort, exhibit passive attitudes, and spend most of the day sitting25,26. Insufficient exercise and excessive sedentary behavior are strong risk factors for mortality and other chronic age-related diseases. Therapists should be able to effectively activate nursing home residents. The more actively an older person participates in therapy, the less assistance they require with self-care and the lower the financial costs needed to maintain their health and independence. Occupational therapy and physical rehabilitation activities for older people living in nursing homes should be intensified in order to improve physical fitness, resilience, and health, allowing people to age with dignity24.

Research results show that physical fitness declines with age among seniors14. Using the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) to assess fitness in older adults, residents can be categorized based on their initial total SPPB score into groups of low performance (LP; 0–6 points), moderate performance (MP; 7–9 points), and high performance (HP; 10–12 points)17,27. The present study shows that female residents aged 60–74 (groups A1, B1) belong to the moderate performance group, while those aged 75–89 (groups A2, B2) exhibit low performance. The older group of women (A2), despite participating in activation programs, falls within the category of low functional fitness. Based on the present study, no significant difference in functional fitness was observed between active and inactive groups of female residents. A significant difference was found only between the younger and older groups of inactive women. The older group showed significantly lower functional fitness, which seems understandable due to the progressive aging of the body. Regular physical activity slows down the process of physical decline. However, this effect was not observed in the studied group of active women. A comparison of SPPB test results between younger and older active women showed no significant differences.

The absence of significant differences in some analyses should be interpreted in light of the high risk of Type II error. The low statistical power observed in many comparisons (1 − β ≈ 0.05–0.40) indicates that non-significant results may stem from insufficient subgroup sample sizes rather than from a true lack of effect. This suggests that several analyses may not have had adequate sensitivity to detect very small effects, which is a typical limitation of studies conducted in populations of nursing home residents.

The results of this study indicate an unsatisfactory level of physical fitness among female nursing home residents in Poland. Studies by other authors confirm a strong relationship between intentional activation of seniors and the resulting improvement in physical fitness levels. Research findings emphasize a close link between physical fitness and the later development of preclinical disability in the aging process14. Most likely, exercise tolerance declines more slowly than physical fitness during the aging process. This may explain why no statistical differences in exercise tolerance were observed between age groups, while differences in physical fitness were evident particularly when comparing younger and older inactive women. Even basic selfcare requires a certain level of functional fitness. However, among nursing home residents, regular long-distance movement is not necessary, which means that aerobic endurance is neither maintained nor strengthened through adaptive changes. Insufficient activation of residents likely due to a lack of encouragement to take regular walks may lead to a decline in exercise tolerance.

With age, the drive for physical activity tends to diminish. The physical fitness assessment results of the examined nursing home residents, based on comparisons with standard results in Poland, are relatively low22. Scientific research shows that regular physical activity has a comprehensive effect on the entire body. It improves functional fitness as well as cardiovascular and respiratory efficiency, which is associated with enhanced aerobic capacity28. Some changes described as part of the aging process are, in fact, caused by a lack or deficiency of physical activity. Implementing regular, appropriately tailored physical exercises may help reduce these changes. Developing optimal yet simple rehabilitation protocols aimed at maintaining autonomy for as long as possible is valuable for aging with dignity.

This study presents the level of physical fitness and endurance among female residents aged 60 − 89 living in nursing homes in Poland. The findings point to a problem of accelerated decline in physical fitness and endurance during aging, despite the fact that some participants were engaged in regular activation programs. Analyzing the results in light of the cited literature suggests that activation efforts for seniors in Polish nursing homes may be inadequate, insufficient, or entirely lacking. Identifying this issue highlights the need for intervention and support through the implementation of tailored activation programs. Such efforts could extend seniors’ independence, reduce the incidence of falls, and help prevent premature disability.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

A major strength of this research is the use of proven and robust methodological approaches. The results have significant clinical relevance for the development and implementation of effective health prevention programs focused on physical activity among nursing home residents. The aim of this initiative is successful aging—maintaining independence for as long as possible while reducing the economic impact of treatment and hospitalization. This study included only female participants. For future research, it would be advisable to expand the analysis to include male residents, which would also increase the overall sample size.

Conclusions

The studied population of female nursing home residents from the western region of Poland, taking into account the activation efforts in these facilities, provided valuable insights. Based on these findings, it can be assumed that the level of physical activity among residents is too low and poorly balanced. This is reflected in the low scores for physical fitness and exercise tolerance across all age groups. It highlights the issue of inadequately designed exercise protocols or even a complete lack of activation. These results serve as a reference point for the need to implement well-developed exercise and rehabilitation programs for seniors living in nursing homes in Poland.
