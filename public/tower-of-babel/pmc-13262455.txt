Epoxyeicosatrienoic acids protect airway epithelial mitochondria by activating the cAMP/PKA–DRP1 Ser637 axis in cigarette smoke–induced lung injury
Authors: Yunxiao Li, Ailin Yang, Ranran Zhao, Bo Wu, Bo Xu, Fang Lin, Xing Hu, Siqi Liu, Xiaona Li, Chunquan Liu, Lixia Fu, Fugui Yan, Ganggang Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCigarette smoke–induced airway epithelial injury is a central pathological feature of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and is closely associated with mitochondrial dysfunction. Excessive dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1)–mediated mitochondrial fission contributes to epithelial oxidative stress and apoptosis; however, the endogenous regulatory mechanisms limiting aberrant DRP1 activation in the airway epithelium remain poorly understood. Epoxyeicosatrienoic acids (EETs), lipid mediators metabolized by soluble epoxide hydrolase (EPHX2), have been implicated in lung protection, but their role in regulating mitochondrial dynamics in cigarette smoke–exposed airway epithelium has not been established.MethodsWild-type and EPHX2-/- mice were exposed to chronic cigarette smoke to evaluate mitochondrial morphology, oxidative stress, epithelial apoptosis, and lung mechanics. In vitro, BEAS-2B airway epithelial cells were exposed to cigarette smoke extract (CSE) with pharmacological or genetic modulation of EET signaling. Mitochondrial structure and function, including reactive oxygen species production, mitochondrial membrane potential, and DRP1 phosphorylation status, were assessed using confocal microscopy, transmission electron microscopy, and immunoblotting.ResultsChronic cigarette smoke exposure induced marked mitochondrial fragmentation, increased oxidative stress, and epithelial apoptosis, accompanied by enhanced DRP1 activation. Genetic deletion of EPHX2 or exogenous EET supplementation preserved mitochondrial network integrity and significantly attenuated DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission in both in vivo and in vitro models. In vivo, restoration of inhibitory DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation was associated with reduced mitochondrial fragmentation, whereas in vitro EETs promoted inhibitory DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation via activation of the cAMP/PKA signaling pathway, thereby limiting pathological mitochondrial fission and mitigating epithelial cell injury.ConclusionsEETs act as endogenous modulators of mitochondrial dynamics in airway epithelial cells. By restraining DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission, EET signaling protects against cigarette smoke–induced mitochondrial dysfunction and epithelial injury, supporting the EPHX2–EET axis as a mechanistically and clinically relevant pathway for further translational investigation in COPD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03657-8.Keywords: Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), Epoxyeicosatrienoic acids (EETs), Mitochondrial dysfunction, DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission, Airway epithelium

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), predominantly caused by cigarette smoking, remains a leading global cause of morbidity and mortality and is characterized by persistent airflow limitation accompanied by chronic inflammation, structural destruction, and oxidative stress–driven tissue remodeling that ultimately results in emphysema and respiratory failure [1]. Oxidative stress is a central driver of COPD pathogenesis, amplifying inflammatory responses and impairing airway epithelial repair. As a major source and target of reactive oxygen species (ROS), mitochondria are particularly vulnerable to cigarette smoke–induced injury. In COPD, mitochondrial dysfunction is manifested by loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and excessive mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) production, which promote epithelial cell injury, apoptosis, and lung remodeling [2, 3].

Disruption of mitochondrial dynamics—especially excessive dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1)–mediated mitochondrial fission—has emerged as a hallmark of smoke-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. ROS-driven DRP1 activation induces mitochondrial fragmentation and apoptosis, a mechanism well established in oxidative stress–induced cell death models [4]. DRP1 activity is tightly regulated by site-specific phosphorylation: phosphorylation at Ser616 promotes mitochondrial fission and pro-death signaling, whereas phosphorylation at Ser637 inhibits DRP1 activation and limits excessive fission [5–7]. Consistent with this regulatory framework, interventions that restore fission–fusion balance or enhance mitochondrial fusion have been shown to alleviate oxidative stress and inflammatory injury in experimental pulmonary disease models [8], underscoring the importance of mitochondrial dynamic homeostasis in maintaining airway epithelial integrity.

In addition to local airway injury, increasing evidence suggests that COPD is associated with systemic metabolic remodeling, including alterations in mitochondrial function, redox balance, and lipid mediator signaling [9]. Although perturbations in lipid metabolic pathways have been reported in COPD patient cohorts [10], epoxy-fatty acids are often underrepresented in untargeted metabolomic analyses, leaving their mechanistic contribution to cigarette smoke–induced mitochondrial injury insufficiently understood [9]. Epoxyeicosatrienoic acids (EETs), cytochrome P450–derived metabolites of arachidonic acid, exert potent anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective effects and participate in cellular stress–response signaling [11]. Our previous studies demonstrated that EETs attenuate airway inflammation in murine COPD models and cigarette smoke extract (CSE)–exposed airway epithelial cells [12–14]. Beyond their extracellular actions, EETs also function as intracellular second messengers capable of activating ERK1/2, Akt, and cAMP/protein kinase A (PKA) signaling pathways [11]. Notably, 11,12-EET has been shown to engage PKA-dependent signaling in vascular systems [15, 16]. However, whether the EET–soluble epoxide hydrolase (sEH/EPHX2) axis regulates cigarette smoke–induced mitochondrial injury—particularly DRP1-dependent mitochondrial fission—in airway epithelial cells remains unknown.

In the present study, we investigated the role of EET signaling in regulating mitochondrial dynamics and epithelial cell survival during cigarette smoke–induced lung injury. Using EPHX2-deficient mice and CSE-exposed airway epithelial cells, we examined mitochondrial morphology, oxidative stress, DRP1 signaling, and epithelial apoptosis, and tested the hypothesis that a cAMP/PKA–dependent DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation pathway mediates the cytoprotective effects of EETs.

Materials and methods

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis of human PBMCs

PBMCs were isolated from peripheral blood of patients with COPD and control subjects by Ficoll density-gradient centrifugation. Total RNA was extracted, reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and subjected to quantitative real-time PCR using SYBR Green chemistry. Relative expression of CYP2J2 and EPHX2 was normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Animals and genotyping

EPHX2−/− mice provided by Dr. Yi Zhu (Tianjin Medical University, Tianjin, China) were maintained at the Beijing Friendship Hospital Animal Center. Age- and weight-matched C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, 20 ± 2 g) were purchased from Sibeifu (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval No. 20–204) and complied with international guidelines. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with free access to food and water on a 12 h light/dark cycle. Genotyping was performed by PCR using genomic DNA from 4-mm tail snips. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Chronic cigarette-smoke exposure

WT and EPHX2−/− mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 20/group): WT-air, KO-air, WT-CS, and KO-CS. Cigarette-smoke exposure was performed for 20 weeks (60 min/day, 5 days/week) in a whole-body smoke chamber using 20 Diamond-brand cigarettes/day (8 mg tar, 0.8 mg nicotine, 10 mg CO; China), with smoke concentration maintained at 80–100 ppm and oxygen at approximately 18%. After exposure, six mice per group underwent lung function testing, and the remaining animals were euthanized for tissue collection. Lung tissues were either snap-frozen or fixed for histology. Group allocation, sampling, imaging, and quantification were performed in a blinded manner according to ARRIVE recommendations.

Lung fixation, histology, and emphysema quantification

After euthanasia, mice were tracheostomized and cannulated with a 20 G angiocatheter (BD Insyte, USA). Lungs were inflated with 10% neutral-buffered formalin (Solarbio, G2161) to 25 cm H₂O, fixed for 24 h, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 5 μm for H&E staining. Emphysematous change was assessed by mean linear intercept (MLI) and mean alveolar number (MAN). MLI reflects the mean free distance between alveolar walls and increases with airspace enlargement [17], whereas MAN reflects alveolar density and decreases with alveolar destruction [18]. Images were acquired at 40× magnification on an Olympus BX51 microscope. MLI was calculated as the total test-line length divided by the number of alveolar-wall intercepts [17], and MAN as the number of alveoli per field [18]. Three sections and six fields per section were analyzed for each mouse in ImageJ, excluding large airways, vessels, and lymphoid tissue.

Lung function measurements

Mice were anesthetized with sodium thiopental (30 mg/kg i.v.; Sigma-Aldrich, T6023), tracheostomized, and connected to a flexiVent™ system (SCIREQ, Montreal, Canada) via a 20 G angiocatheter. Ventilation settings were 150 breaths/min, tidal volume 10 mL/kg, and PEEP 3 cm H₂O. Respiratory system resistance (Rrs), airway resistance (Rn), and quasi-static pressure-volume (PV) loops were recorded. Inspiratory capacity (IC) was calculated as the volume change between end-expiratory volume and 30 cm H₂O, and PV-loop hysteresis as the enclosed loop area [19, 20].

LC-MS/MS quantification of pulmonary EETs

Lung tissue was homogenized in ice-cold PBS (1:5, w/v), spiked with 14,15-EET-d₈ (50 ng/mL; Cayman Chemical), acidified, and extracted using C18 SPE cartridges (Strata-X, 30 mg). Dried extracts were reconstituted in 100 µL of 50% methanol and analyzed by UHPLC-MS/MS on a Dionex Ultimate 3000 system coupled to a Thermo Q Exactive mass spectrometer in negative electrospray ionization mode. Separation was achieved on a Hypersil GOLD C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 3 μm) at 40 °C and 0.3 mL/min using a 7 min acetonitrile gradient. EET regioisomers were quantified by PRM using analyte/internal-standard peak-area ratios and matrix-matched calibration standards (0.5–2000 ng/mL; 1/x weighting). Data are presented as ng·mL⁻¹.

In situ ROS detection in lung cryosections

Unfixed lungs were cryosectioned at 8 μm and incubated with dihydroethidium (DHE, 5 µM; BioLab HR9069) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Images were acquired immediately on an Olympus BX51 microscope using identical exposure settings with a TRITC filter for DHE and DAPI for nuclear counterstaining. Fluorescence intensity was quantified in ImageJ as background-subtracted median fluorescence intensity (MFI).

Cell culture and treatments

BEAS-2B cells (ATCC CRL-9609) and the CRISPR-engineered homozygous DRP1(S637A) line (Ser637→Ala; Hanbio Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, 11965092) supplemented with 10% FBS (HyClone, SH30071.03) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. DRP1-knockdown and DRP1-overexpressing cells were maintained under the same conditions. For pharmacological studies, cells were pretreated for 1 h with 11,12-EET, 14,15-EET, 11,12-EET + 14,15-EEZE, or 14,15-EET + 14,15-EEZE (all 1 µM; Cayman Chemical) before exposure to CSE (300 µg/mL; OD₃₂₀ = 0.75 ± 0.05) for the indicated times. Vehicle controls received 0.1% DMSO.

Adenoviral manipulation of DRP1

For knockdown, DRP1 (DNM1L, NM_012062.4) was silenced using replication-deficient adenoviral shRNA constructs (Obio Technology, Shanghai, China). A non-targeting shRNA served as control. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by RT-qPCR and Western blotting. For overexpression, BEAS-2B cells were infected with an Ad5 vector encoding human DRP1 (DNM1L, NM_012062.5) and an mScarlet reporter under a CMV promoter (Obio Technology) at an MOI of 30 for 4 h and cultured for 48 h before analysis. Overexpression was verified by RT-qPCR and Western blotting under BSL-2 conditions. Additional details regarding the generation and validation of DNM1L-targeting genetic tools are provided in Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Figs. S1-S5.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed by MTT assay (Solarbio, M8180). After incubation with MTT (0.5 mg/mL) for 4 h at 37 °C, formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 µL DMSO and absorbance was measured at 570/630 nm on a SpectraMax M5 plate reader (Molecular Devices). Values were background-subtracted and normalized to vehicle controls.

Intracellular ROS by flow cytometry

Intracellular ROS was measured using DCFH-DA (Solarbio, D6470). BEAS-2B or DRP1(S637A) cells were pretreated with 14,15-EET (1 µM, 1 h), exposed to CSE (300 µg/mL), and loaded with 10 µM DCFH-DA for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Flow cytometry was performed on a BD FACSCalibur, collecting at least 10,000 singlet events per sample. ROS levels are presented as MFI normalized to vehicle controls.

TUNEL staining in mouse lung

Paraffin Sect. (5 μm) were processed using the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit, Fluorescein (Roche, 11684795910), counterstained with DAPI, and imaged on an Olympus BX51 microscope using fixed exposure settings. The apoptotic index was defined as the ratio of TUNEL-positive nuclei to total DAPI-positive nuclei from five non-overlapping fields per section.

CSE preparation and quality control

CSE was prepared by bubbling mainstream smoke from two Diamond cigarettes into 20 mL serum-free DMEM and filtering the extract through a 0.22 μm PVDF membrane (Millex-GV). Quality was monitored by OD₃₂₀, pH, and osmolality. Aliquoted CSE was stored at − 80 °C for up to 1 week. Working CSE (300 µg/mL; ~3% v/v) was freshly prepared and used within 2 h. Vehicle medium was prepared identically without smoke.

Western blotting

Proteins from lung tissues and cells were extracted in RIPA buffer (MedChemExpress, HY-K1001) containing protease/phosphatase inhibitors, and protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay (Beyotime, P0010). Equal amounts of protein (20 µg) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (0.45 μm; Immobilon-P), and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, CST) for 1 h at room temperature, signals were visualized using ECL reagent (Bai Ao Lai Bo, HR0338) on a Clinx imaging system. Densitometric analysis was performed in ImageJ and normalized to β-actin or GAPDH. Target proteins included Bax, Bcl-2, cleaved caspase-3, cleaved caspase-9, cytochrome c, DRP1, p-DRP1(Ser616), p-DRP1(Ser637), and MFN2. Primary and secondary antibodies used for immunoblotting are listed in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

Transmission electron microscopy

Lung tissue (~ 1 mm³) was fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated, embedded in Agar 100 epoxy resin, sectioned (~ 60 nm), stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and imaged on an FEI Morgagni 268D transmission electron microscope at 80 kV with a Morada CCD camera. Non-overlapping fields were analyzed in a blinded manner using Fiji/ImageJ to assess mitochondrial number and ultrastructural changes, including swelling, cristae density, and fragmentation.

Immunofluorescence staining

For lung tissue, paraffin sections were subjected to immunofluorescence staining with antibodies against p-DRP1(Ser616), p-DRP1(Ser637), MFN2, and DRP1, followed by Alexa Fluor 488/594 secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher, A-11008/A-11012, 1:200) and DAPI. Images were captured on an Olympus BX51 microscope using matched exposure settings, and fluorescence intensity was quantified in ImageJ as background-subtracted MFI from 15 alveoli across five random fields per sample. Antibody details for immunofluorescence analyses are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

For cell experiments, mitochondria were labeled with MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Thermo Fisher, M7512; 100 nM, 30 min), followed by immunofluorescence staining for DRP1 and p-DRP1(Ser637). DRP1 mitochondrial recruitment was assessed by line-scan intensity profiling, and p-DRP1(Ser637) fluorescence was quantified by MFI. Mitochondrial network morphology was quantified from MitoTracker-labeled confocal images using the MiNA macro in Fiji/ImageJ under identical preprocessing and threshold settings across groups. Mean branch length, network size, and mitochondrial footprint were analyzed together to evaluate mitochondrial network integrity and fragmentation. At least three independent biological replicates were analyzed for each imaging experiment, and the mean value from each biological replicate was used for statistical analysis.

Mitochondrial membrane potential

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was assessed using JC-1 dye (Beyotime, C2006). After incubation for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark, JC-1 fluorescence was analyzed by microscopy or flow cytometry. ΔΨm was expressed as the red/green fluorescence ratio. Microscopy was performed on an Olympus BX51 microscope, and flow-cytometric analysis was performed on a BD FACSCalibur with FCCP (10 µM, 15 min) as a depolarization control. Flow-cytometric analyses were performed with at least three independent biological replicates. At least 10,000 events per sample were analyzed using FlowJo v10.

Apoptosis by flow cytometry

Apoptosis was assessed by flow cytometry using Annexin V-PE/7-AAD (Beyotime, C1068) or Annexin V-FITC/PI (Beyotime, C1062), as indicated. After staining in Ca²⁺-containing binding buffer for 20 min at room temperature in the dark, cells were analyzed within 1 h on a BD FACSCalibur. Debris and doublets were excluded, and cells were classified as viable, early apoptotic, late apoptotic/secondary necrotic, or primary necrotic. Apoptosis assays were performed with at least three independent biological replicates, and each biological replicate represented an independently prepared sample.

Intracellular cAMP measurement

Intracellular cAMP was measured using the cAMP Parameter Assay Kit (R&D Systems, KGE002B). Cell lysates were analyzed in duplicate by competitive ELISA on a SpectraMax M5 reader. cAMP levels were calculated from a four-parameter logistic curve, normalized to total protein, and expressed as pmol/mg protein.

PKA activity assay

PKA activity was measured using the PKA Colorimetric Activity Kit (Thermo Fisher, EIAPKA). Cell lysates were assayed in substrate-coated wells, and absorbance was measured at 450/570 nm. After background subtraction using PKI controls, activity was normalized to protein input and expressed as U·mg⁻¹ protein.

Pharmacologic modulation of cAMP/PKA signaling

BEAS-2B cells were treated with vehicle, CSE (300 µg/mL), CSE + 14,15-EET (1 µM), CSE + forskolin (10 µM; Sigma-Aldrich, F6886), or CSE + 14,15-EET + H-89 (5 µM; MedChemExpress, HY-15996). Forskolin and H-89 were added 30 min, and 14,15-EET 60 min, before CSE. Final DMSO concentration was ≤ 0.1%. Mitochondrial morphology, p-DRP1(Ser637), ΔΨm, and apoptosis were then assessed.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Comparisons between two groups were made using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, as appropriate. Data distribution and variance were assessed before applying parametric tests; when these assumptions were not met, Kruskal–Wallis tests with Dunn’s multiple-comparison correction were used. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sample sizes represent biological replicates (individual mice or independent cell preparations). For imaging-based analyses, multiple cells or fields were quantified within each biological replicate, and the mean value from each replicate was used for statistical analysis. For flow-cytometric analyses, each biological replicate represented an independent sample preparation and acquisition. Randomization and blinding were applied for animal allocation, imaging, and quantification. No samples were excluded except for predefined quality-control failures. Technical replicates, when performed, were used to ensure assay stability, whereas statistical testing was based on biological replicates.

Results

EPHX2 deficiency preserves pulmonary EETs and attenuates cigarette smoke–induced emphysema, oxidative stress, and apoptosis

Chronic cigarette smoke (CS) exposure for 20 weeks markedly reduced pulmonary epoxyeicosatrienoic acid (EET) levels in wild-type (WT) mice, whereas EPHX2⁻/⁻ mice maintained significantly higher concentrations of 11,12-EET and 14,15-EET (Fig. 1A). Histological analysis revealed severe emphysematous destruction in WT lungs, characterized by enlarged airspaces and reduced alveolar density, while EPHX2 deficiency markedly mitigated alveolar damage, as evidenced by reduced mean linear intercept (MLI) and increased mean alveolar number (MAN) (Fig. 1B–C). Consistently, CS exposure increased respiratory system resistance (Rrs) and airway resistance (Rn) and reduced inspiratory capacity (IC), all of which were significantly improved in EPHX2⁻/⁻ mice (Fig. 1D). CS-induced body weight loss was also attenuated by EPHX2 deficiency (Fig. 1E). At the oxidative stress level, dihydroethidium (DHE) staining demonstrated markedly reduced superoxide generation in EPHX2⁻/⁻ lungs compared with WT under CS exposure (Fig. 1F–G). TUNEL staining further revealed a significant reduction in epithelial apoptosis in EPHX2⁻/⁻ mice (Fig. 1H–I). Immunoblot analysis showed that CS upregulated pro-apoptotic Bax, cleaved caspase-3, cleaved caspase-9, and cytochrome c while downregulating Bcl-2 in WT lungs; these apoptotic alterations were largely prevented in EPHX2-deficient mice (Fig. 1J–K). We next examined CYP2J2 and EPHX2 expression in human PBMCs. qPCR analysis showed significant differences in both CYP2J2 and EPHX2 expression between the COPD and control groups (Fig. 1L). These findings indicate that EPHX2 loss preserves endogenous EETs and protects against CS-induced oxidative stress, apoptosis, and emphysematous lung injury in vivo.

EPHX2 deficiency preserves mitochondrial ultrastructure and fission–fusion balance in vivo

Transmission electron microscopy revealed profound mitochondrial ultrastructural disruption in CS-exposed WT lungs, including matrix swelling, cristae rarefaction, cristae discontinuity, and extensive fragmentation (Fig. 2A–B). In contrast, mitochondria in EPHX2⁻/⁻ lungs largely retained elongated tubular morphology with densely packed cristae, indicating preserved mitochondrial integrity. Consistent with these structural observations, immunoblotting demonstrated that CS increased total DRP1 expression and its fission-promoting phosphorylation at Ser616 while reducing inhibitory Ser637 phosphorylation and suppressing the fusion protein MFN2 in WT lungs, reflecting a shift toward a fission-dominant state. These changes were markedly reversed in EPHX2⁻/⁻ mice, with restoration of p-DRP1(Ser637), reduction of p-DRP1(Ser616), and preservation of MFN2 expression (Fig. 2C–D). Immunofluorescence analysis further confirmed that EPHX2 deficiency limited CS-induced DRP1 and p-DRP1(Ser616) mitochondrial localization while maintaining MFN2 and p-DRP1(Ser637) signals (Fig. 2E–G). Collectively, these data support the in vivo relevance of DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation and indicate that EPHX2 deficiency preserves mitochondrial architecture and counteracts CS-induced mitochondrial fragmentation by maintaining fusion–fission homeostasis in vivo.

EETs restore mitochondrial membrane potential and suppress apoptosis in CSE-exposed airway epithelial cells

In BEAS-2B cells, cigarette smoke extract (CSE, 300 µg/mL) caused a marked loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), indicated by a shift from red to green JC-1 fluorescence (Fig. 3A, C). Treatment with either 11,12-EET or 14,15-EET significantly preserved ΔΨm, whereas co-treatment with the EET antagonist EEZE abolished this protection. Consistent with these mitochondrial effects, Annexin V/PI flow cytometry revealed that EETs significantly reduced CSE-induced early and late apoptosis, an effect reversed by EEZE (Fig. 3B, D). At the molecular level, EET treatment suppressed the intrinsic apoptotic program by decreasing Bax expression, cytochrome c release, and cleaved caspase-3 and caspase-9 while increasing Bcl-2 expression (Fig. 3E–F). EETs also enhanced inhibitory DRP1 phosphorylation at Ser637, linking mitochondrial preservation and anti-apoptotic effects to suppression of DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission. These findings indicate that EETs protect airway epithelial cells from CSE-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and intrinsic apoptosis.

EETs restrain mitochondrial fission by promoting DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation and preserving mitochondrial network integrity through cAMP/PKA signaling

Immunofluorescence imaging showed that CSE markedly increased DRP1 accumulation on mitochondria and induced pronounced mitochondrial fragmentation, whereas EET treatment preserved elongated mitochondrial networks; both effects were abolished by EEZE (Fig. 4A). Line-scan fluorescence profiling further demonstrated increased spatial overlap between DRP1 and mitochondrial signals after CSE exposure, which was reduced by EET treatment and restored by EEZE (Fig. 4B). Representative MitoTracker/p-DRP1(Ser637) staining showed that CSE reduced p-DRP1(Ser637), whereas EET treatment restored Ser637 phosphorylation and maintained tubular mitochondrial morphology (Fig. 4C). Quantitative analysis confirmed that CSE significantly reduced p-DRP1(Ser637) fluorescence intensity, while both 11,12-EET and 14,15-EET restored it (Fig. 4D). MiNA-based analysis further showed that CSE impaired mitochondrial network organization, as indicated by reduced branch length, mitochondrial footprint, and network size, whereas both EETs improved these parameters and EEZE abolished these effects (Fig. 4E). Similar changes were confirmed by representative MitoTracker/skeleton images and the corresponding MiNA quantification (Fig. 4F–G). In parallel, CSE significantly reduced intracellular cAMP levels and PKA activity, both of which were restored by EETs in an EEZE-sensitive manner (Fig. 4H). Together, these data support that EETs inhibit pathological mitochondrial fission by enhancing DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation, limiting DRP1 mitochondrial recruitment, and preserving mitochondrial network integrity through a cAMP/PKA-dependent mechanism.

DRP1 expression is required for EET-mediated mitochondrial protection and anti-apoptotic effects

To determine whether DRP1 is required for EET-mediated protection, BEAS-2B cells with stable DRP1 knockdown or overexpression were exposed to CSE with or without 14,15-EET. The generation and validation of the corresponding DNM1L-targeting genetic tools are summarized in Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Figs. S1-S5. CSE markedly increased apoptosis, whereas EET significantly reduced apoptosis in WT and shNC control cells (Fig. 5A). DRP1 knockdown alone mimicked the anti-apoptotic effect of EET, whereas DRP1 overexpression completely abolished EET-mediated protection. JC-1 staining showed that CSE induced ΔΨm depolarization, which was reversed by either EET treatment or DRP1 knockdown but persisted under DRP1 overexpression despite EET exposure (Fig. 5B). Immunoblot analysis further demonstrated that pro-apoptotic signaling (Bax, cytochrome c release, cleaved caspase-3/9) was suppressed by EET or DRP1 knockdown but remained elevated when DRP1 was overexpressed (Fig. 5C–F). These results establish DRP1 as a critical downstream effector of EET-mediated mitochondrial protection and apoptosis suppression.

DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation is indispensable for EET-mediated cytoprotection

To directly test the requirement of DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation, BEAS-2B cells expressing wild-type DRP1 or the non-phosphorylatable DRP1 S637A mutant were exposed to CSE with or without 14,15-EET. EET markedly reduced CSE-induced ROS accumulation in WT cells, whereas this antioxidant effect was completely lost in S637A-expressing cells (Fig. 6A). Similarly, EET restored ΔΨm and reduced apoptosis in WT cells but failed to rescue mitochondrial depolarization or apoptosis in S637A mutant cells (Fig. 6B–E). Immunoblot analysis showed that EET suppressed Bax expression, cytochrome c release, and caspase activation while increasing Bcl-2 in WT cells, whereas none of these effects were observed in S637A-expressing cells (Fig. 6F–G). These convergent findings demonstrate that DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation is essential for the antioxidant, mitochondrial-preserving, and anti-apoptotic actions of EETs.

Activation of the cAMP/PKA axis is required for EET-mediated DRP1(Ser637) phosphorylation and mitochondrial protection

To establish pathway dependence, BEAS-2B cells were treated with CSE together with 14,15-EET, forskolin, or the PKA inhibitor H-89. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that both EET and forskolin restored elongated mitochondrial networks and increased p-DRP1(Ser637) levels, whereas H-89 abolished these effects (Fig. 7A). MiNA-based morphometric analysis confirmed that mitochondrial network elongation induced by EET or forskolin was lost upon PKA inhibition (Fig. 7B). JC-1 staining showed that EET and forskolin rescued ΔΨm, preventing CSE-induced depolarization, while H-89 blocked this recovery (Fig. 7C–D). Immunoblotting further confirmed that EET- and forskolin-induced DRP1(Ser637) phosphorylation was fully reversed by H-89 (Fig. 7E–F). Annexin V–FITC/PI cytometry demonstrated parallel effects on apoptosis, with EET and forskolin suppressing CSE-induced apoptosis and H-89 negating this protection (Fig. 7G–H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that EET-mediated mitochondrial protection and anti-apoptotic effects require activation of the cAMP/PKA–DRP1(Ser637) signaling axis.

Discussion

Emerging clinical, metabolomic, and multi-omics studies increasingly identify lipid metabolic dysregulation as a central feature of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [9, 10, 21]. Across distinct COPD subphenotypes, pathway-based analyses consistently implicate lipid and oxidized fatty-acid networks among the most perturbed pathways, linking oxylipin remodeling to immune dysregulation, accelerated lung function decline, and increased susceptibility to exacerbations [10, 21]. Recent metabolomic studies further highlight altered lipid mediator profiles as a recurrent signature across patient cohorts [9, 22]. However, classical epoxyeicosatrienoic acids (EETs) and their soluble epoxide hydrolase (sEH/EPHX2)-derived metabolites are often underrepresented in untargeted metabolomic platforms, leaving their contribution to COPD incompletely defined [9, 22].

In the present study, we show that impaired EET signaling is associated with mitochondrial dysregulation and epithelial vulnerability under cigarette smoke stress. In vivo, genetic deletion of EPHX2, which stabilizes endogenous EETs, attenuated emphysematous airspace enlargement, reduced oxidative stress, and decreased epithelial apoptosis. At the mitochondrial level, EPHX2 deficiency preserved ultrastructural integrity and shifted mitochondrial dynamics toward a less fragmented state, as reflected by increased inhibitory DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation, reduced activating DRP1 Ser616 phosphorylation, and maintenance of MFN2. Complementary in vitro experiments further showed that exogenous EETs activated cAMP/PKA signaling, restrained DRP1-dependent mitochondrial fragmentation, preserved mitochondrial membrane potential, and limited intrinsic apoptosis in cigarette smoke extract (CSE)-exposed airway epithelial cells. Together, these findings support an EET–cAMP/PKA–DRP1(Ser637) pathway as a metabolically regulated mechanism linking lipid dysregulation to epithelial injury in smoke-induced lung disease.

EETs are cytochrome P450-derived lipid epoxides with well-established vasodilatory, anti-inflammatory, and cytoprotective properties, but they are rapidly hydrolyzed by sEH/EPHX2 [11, 23]. CYP2J2, a major pulmonary epoxygenase, is broadly expressed in airway and vascular compartments and contributes to epithelial–vascular homeostasis [24]. In the present study, chronic cigarette smoke exposure markedly reduced pulmonary EET levels, supporting disruption of endogenous epoxy-fatty acid signaling under sustained smoke stress. Cigarette smoke-generated oxidative stress is a plausible contributor to this reduction, given the established role of cigarette smoke in oxidative lung injury and the recognized involvement of the EET/sEH axis in smoking-related pulmonary inflammation and tissue damage [13, 25]. However, the present study was not designed to determine whether ROS directly and selectively degrades specific EET regioisomers in vivo.

Notably, EPHX2 deficiency preferentially preserved 11,12-EET and 14,15-EET rather than uniformly increasing all detected regioisomers, suggesting that individual EET species may differ in pulmonary relevance. Among the four detected regioisomers, 11,12-EET and 14,15-EET were prioritized because they constituted the major measurable pulmonary EET species in our dataset, whereas 5,6-EET and 8,9-EET were present at substantially lower levels. This interpretation is also consistent with prior studies showing that 14,15-EET is frequently among the most abundant measurable EET regioisomers, while 11,12-EET exhibits prominent biological activity in pulmonary and airway-related settings [26]. Compared with 11,12-EET and 14,15-EET, 8,9-EET has been less extensively studied in pulmonary systems, whereas 5,6-EET is also more technically challenging to evaluate [27]. In our model, preservation or restoration of 11,12-EET and 14,15-EET was associated with increased DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation, improved mitochondrial morphology, and reduced epithelial apoptosis.

Importantly, pulmonary EET depletion in vivo occurred together with emphysematous change, lung function impairment, oxidative stress, and epithelial apoptosis, whereas preservation of EET signaling in EPHX2-deficient mice was associated with broader protection across these injury phenotypes. This pattern supports the view that disturbed pulmonary EET homeostasis is biologically linked to cigarette smoke-induced tissue injury rather than representing only a secondary metabolic change. The PBMC data further provide preliminary patient-derived support that the EPHX2–EET axis is also altered in human COPD. Together, these findings support the idea that loss of endogenous EET signaling contributes to mitochondrial fragmentation under cigarette smoke exposure, whereas restoration of selected EET species re-engages protective signaling pathways.

Our in vivo data also support a role for coordinated regulation of mitochondrial dynamics in epithelial survival during chronic smoke exposure. Cigarette smoke increased total DRP1 expression and p-DRP1(Ser616), while reducing p-DRP1(Ser637) and MFN2, a pattern consistent with previous reports linking smoke exposure to DRP1-driven mitochondrial fragmentation in pulmonary cells [4, 7]. Restoration of Ser637 phosphorylation is generally considered to restrain excessive fission and favor mitochondrial preservation. However, the functional consequences of DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation may vary depending on cell type and stress context [7]. In this regard, our findings support the in vivo relevance of Ser637 regulation, but the mechanistic requirement of this site was established more directly in vitro using the DRP1 S637A mutant. Therefore, restoration of DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation likely contributes to the protective effects observed in vivo, although these effects are unlikely to be fully explained by this mechanism alone.

Consistent with this interpretation, both 11,12- and 14,15-EET preserved mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), reduced Annexin V/PI-defined apoptosis, and prevented mitochondrial fragmentation in CSE-exposed BEAS-2B cells, whereas antagonism of EET signaling with EEZE abolished these protective effects. Quantitative morphometric analysis further showed that EET treatment improved mitochondrial network integrity, including branch length, network size, and mitochondrial footprint, while reducing DRP1 recruitment to mitochondria [28, 29]. EETs also shifted DRP1 phosphorylation toward a less pro-fission profile by increasing inhibitory Ser637 phosphorylation and reducing activating Ser616 phosphorylation. The accompanying restoration of intracellular cAMP levels and PKA activity suggests that cigarette smoke suppresses this signaling axis and that EETs can reactivate it in airway epithelial cells.

Pharmacologic interventions provided additional support for pathway dependence. Inhibition of PKA with H-89 abolished the protective effects of 14,15-EET on mitochondrial morphology, ΔΨm, and apoptosis, consistent with PKA acting as a major upstream kinase for DRP1 Ser637 [6, 7]. Conversely, activation of adenylyl cyclase with forskolin reproduced several effects of EET treatment, including increased p-DRP1(Ser637), reduced p-DRP1(Ser616), improved mitochondrial morphology, and decreased epithelial apoptosis. Together, these findings identify the cAMP/PKA–DRP1(Ser637) node as a smoke-sensitive signaling module that can be functionally reactivated.

Genetic perturbation experiments further strengthened the specificity of this pathway. DRP1 knockdown recapitulated the effects of EET treatment on mitochondrial morphology and cell protection, whereas DRP1 overexpression aggravated CSE-induced fragmentation and reduced EET efficacy, indicating that DRP1 is a major determinant of epithelial injury responses in this setting. Importantly, expression of the non-phosphorylatable DRP1 S637A mutant rendered cells largely unresponsive to the antioxidant, mitochondrial, and anti-apoptotic effects of EETs, directly supporting a functional requirement for Ser637 phosphorylation in vitro. While previous COPD studies have mainly emphasized Ser616-associated pro-fission signaling [7], our findings extend this framework by highlighting inhibitory Ser637 phosphorylation as an additional regulatory site engaged by endogenous lipid mediators to preserve mitochondrial homeostasis [4, 6, 30].

The upstream mechanism by which EETs enhance cAMP/PKA signaling remains incompletely defined. Recent evidence indicates that, although EETs can elicit GPCR-like responses, including Gαs activation, cAMP generation, and PKA-dependent signaling, a canonical high-affinity EET receptor has not yet been definitively identified. Instead, available data support the possibility that EETs act through context-dependent GPCR-like or membrane-associated mechanisms, with candidate pathways involving GPR40/FFAR1, GPR120/FFAR4, GPR39, prostaglandin EP receptor-sensitive signaling, and ion-channel-associated pathways such as TRP and KATP channels [11, 26, 31]. EET-induced cAMP generation has also been observed in spatially restricted membrane microdomains [32], which may facilitate coupling to mitochondria-associated PKA pools, potentially including AKAP1-anchored complexes that restrain DRP1-dependent fission [33, 34]. In this context, our findings support activation of a downstream cAMP/PKA–DRP1(Ser637) signaling module by EETs in airway epithelial cells, while the precise upstream receptor mechanism remains unresolved.

To strengthen the translational relevance of our findings, we additionally analyzed PBMC samples from patients with COPD and found significant alterations in EPHX2/sEH and CYP2J2 expression, supporting dysregulation of the endogenous EET metabolic axis in human disease. Although PBMCs do not fully recapitulate the pulmonary microenvironment, these patient-derived data suggest that the pathway identified in our experimental models is also perturbed in COPD at the systemic level. This interpretation is further supported by prior human studies showing impaired EET-mediated vascular responses in COPD [35] and partial restoration of smoking-related EET-associated endothelial dysfunction by pharmacologic sEH inhibition [35]. Early clinical pharmacology studies also support the feasibility of sEH inhibition in humans and demonstrate target engagement [36], although disease-specific efficacy in COPD remains to be established.

An additional translational question is whether pharmacologic intervention can reproduce the mitochondrial protection observed with EPHX2 deficiency or exogenous EET treatment in airway epithelial cells. Although this was not directly tested in the present study, our previous work supports the feasibility of using TUDCA in cigarette smoke-related airway injury models [13]. Future studies will therefore evaluate whether GSK2256294, and potentially TUDCA as a related protective intervention, can similarly attenuate cigarette smoke-induced mitochondrial injury. Our previous work also suggests that EET/sEH signaling may influence cigarette smoke-induced airway injury through an ER stress/inflammatory branch involving IRE1α/JNK/AP-1 [13]. However, the present study did not directly test whether this pathway contributes to the regulation of mitochondrial fragmentation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although our lung tissue data support the in vivo relevance of DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation, direct site-specific validation in vivo was not performed. Second, the upstream mechanism by which EETs activate cAMP/PKA signaling remains unresolved, and we did not directly identify the responsible receptor or signaling complex. Third, although cigarette smoke-induced oxidative stress may contribute to pulmonary EET depletion, we did not directly determine whether ROS selectively degrades individual EET regioisomers in vivo. Fourth, we did not directly test whether the IRE1α/JNK/AP-1 pathway contributes to mitochondrial fragmentation in the current model, nor whether pharmacologic sEH inhibition can reproduce the mitochondrial protective effects observed with EPHX2 deficiency or exogenous EET treatment. Finally, although we incorporated PBMC-based patient data, these findings do not substitute for validation in patient-derived airway epithelial cells or lung tissue. Further studies in disease-relevant human pulmonary samples and more direct in vivo mechanistic models will therefore be needed.

In conclusion, our study supports a model in which EETs protect airway epithelial cells from cigarette smoke-induced injury, at least in part, through cAMP/PKA-dependent DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation, thereby limiting excessive mitochondrial fragmentation and intrinsic apoptosis. These findings identify the EPHX2–EET axis as a mechanistically and clinically relevant candidate pathway in COPD that warrants further validation in patient-derived pulmonary samples and translational studies.
