Polytope Division Method: A Scalable Sampling Method for problems with high-dimensional parameters Thanks:
Evie Nielen, Oliver Tse, Karen Veroy

Abstract
Configuration Optimization Problems (COPs), which involve minimizing a loss function over a set of discrete points 𝜸 ⊂ P \boldsymbol{\gamma}\subset P , are common in areas like Model Order Reduction, Active Learning, and Optimal Experimental Design. While exact solutions are often infeasible, heuristic methods such as the Greedy Sampling Method (GSM) provide practical alternatives, particularly for low-dimensional cases. GSM recursively updates 𝜸 \boldsymbol{\gamma} by solving a continuous optimization problem, which is typically approximated by a search over a discrete sample set S ⊂ P S\subset P . However, as the dimensionality grows, the sample size suffers from the curse of dimensionality. To address this, we introduce the Polytope Division Method (PDM), a scalable greedy-type approach that adaptively partitions the parameter space and targets regions of high loss. PDM achieves linear scaling with problem dimensionality and offers an efficient solution approach for high-dimensional COPs, overcoming the limitations of traditional methods.

Introduction

Optimization problems are ubiquitous in computational science, engineering, and machine learning. In this work, we focus on a specific class known as
Configuration Optimization Problems
(COPs), where the objective is to minimize a loss function that depends on configuration of points. Such problems can generally be stated as follows: For a fixed number of points
n\in\mathbb{N}
in a compact set,
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a given
loss function
,
\mathcal{L}\colon\varGamma(P)\to[0,+\infty)
, we want to find

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\varGamma_{n}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\eta}).

(COP)

Here,
\Gamma(P)
is the space of finite configurations, defined by

\Gamma(P)\coloneq\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Gamma_{n}(P),

where
\Gamma_{n}(P)\coloneq\{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\subset P:\#\boldsymbol{\gamma}=n\}
is the family of finite subsets of
P
with cardinality
n
.
In other words, the task at hand is to find a subset
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\subset P
of
n\in\mathbb{N}
points that minimizes the loss function
\mathcal{L}
.

It is, typically, computationally infeasible to solve these problems exactly, but strategies exist to heuristically find (possibly suboptimal) solutions to (
COP
). One family of such strategies is the greedy method
[
8
,
12
,
14
,
15
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
, which iteratively builds
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\subset P
by choosing the (locally) optimal update at each iteration.
In this approach,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{1}=\{p_{1}\}
is chosen arbitrarily; then, in the
j
-th step, we update the configuration with
p_{j}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{j}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{j-1}\cup\{p_{j}\}
, where

\displaystyle p_{j}\in\argmin_{q\in P\setminus\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{j-1}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{j-1}\cup\{q\}),\qquad\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{j-1}\in\varGamma_{j-1}.

(1)

While this procedure does not generally lead to a solution of (
COP
), it does provide a tractable strategy to approximate solutions to (
COP
) by choosing the best possible update in the
j
-th step, given the configuration in the
(j\!-\!1)
-th step.

Unfortunately, (
1
) remains computationally intensive in many practical applications due to the existence of multiple local minimizers. To empirically overcome this, the Greedy Sampling Method (GSM) is often used. GSM aims to approximate the global minimizer of (
1
) by determining a minimizer inside a sample set
S\subset P
. However, GSM suffers from two main problems:
(1) the lack of a sufficient understanding of the selection of method parameters, in particular, the sample size
|S|
, and (2) scalability issues in higher dimensions or the ”curse-of-dimensionality” (see Section
2
for details).

In this paper, we propose an alternative greedy approach called the
Polytope Division Method
(PDM) that is capable of circumventing the aforementioned pitfalls of GSM. The key idea behind PDM—based on a divide-and-conquer strategy—is to recursively divide the parameter set into polytopes where, in each step, polytope configurations are further divided in accordance with the updated configuration
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.
The number of samples required to find an approximate solution
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\varGamma_{n}
for fixed
n\geq 1
scales only linearly with the dimension. Additionally, the number of samples is updated at each step, eliminating the need to choose a sample size
|S|
beforehand.

COPs in Applications

We now consider two examples of COPs.

Example 1: Reduced Basis Methods (RBM)

The first example is taken from Reduced Basis Methods (RBM) in Model Order Reduction (MOR) for parametrized families of partial differential equations (PDEs). RBM is used in many applications, such as material engineering
[
13
,
19
]
, electro-magnetism
[
7
]
, heat flow
[
23
]
and many more
[
9
,
18
,
21
,
24
,
26
]
. It is important to note that this is only a small selection of the extensive literature available on the topic. In RBM, we seek a set of basis vectors that provide good approximations to the solution manifold of parametrized PDEs.

Consider a parameter set
P
and a PDE with solution
u(p)\in\mathcal{V}
depending on the parameter
p\in P
. Then a

made up of a family of solutions at different parameter points

is used to approximate the full solution manifold
\mathcal{M}\coloneq\{u(p)\,|\,p\in P\}
. In this context, the loss function can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textup{RB}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\max_{q\in P}\left\|u(q)-\mathsf{P}_{V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}u(q)\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{V}}.

(2)

Here,
V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\text{span}\{u(p)\,|\,p\in\boldsymbol{\gamma}\}
is the linear span of solutions associated to a configuration of parameter points
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, and
\mathsf{P}_{V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}
is the projection operator onto the reduced basis space
V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
. More details are provided in Section
4.1
.

Example 2: Empirical Interpolation Method (EIM)

The second example is given by the Empirical Interpolation Method (EIM) as introduced in
[
3
]
and further discussed, among others, in
[
6
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
16
]
. EIM is used to find an approximate affine decomposition of a function
s\colon P\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
. In this case, the loss function reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{EIM}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\max_{q\in P}\left\|s(q)-s_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{I}}(q)\right\|.

(3)

Here,
s_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{I}}
is the EIM approximation, which depends on the set of configuration points
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and a set of interpolation points
\mathbf{I}
. Details about the function
s_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{I}}
are given in Section
4.2
.

In this work, we mainly focus on the previous two examples. Still, more examples of this class of optimization problems can be found, for instance, in the context of Optimal Experimental Design (OED)
[

Greedy Sampling Method

We begin this section by providing a more detailed summary of the Greedy Sampling method (GSM)
[
29
]
.
The key idea behind GSM is to compute a heuristic solution to (
COP
) by sequentially selecting points to construct the configuration
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. Ideally, the configuration
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\varGamma_{j-1}
should be updated with the point
p_{j}
given by (
1
). However, even the task of determining this optimal update can be infeasible in many applications. Therefore, the optimizer of the
continuous
optimization problem (
1
) is approximated by the optimizer of a
discrete
optimization problem over a sample set.

The precise implementation of GSM depends on the form of the loss function. We focus on the class of loss functions involving a maximum, i.e., where there exists an objective function such that
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\max_{p\in P}J(p,\boldsymbol{\gamma})
, where
J(p,\boldsymbol{\gamma})
has the following properties

1.

J(p,\boldsymbol{\gamma})\geq 0
,

2.

The function
J(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\gamma})
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
L_{J}
.

3.

For every
p\in\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, it holds that

J(p,\boldsymbol{\gamma})=0.

4.

For every
p\in P
, it holds that if
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{n}\subset\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{m}
, then

\displaystyle J(p,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{m})\leq J(p,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{n}).

In GSM, we then consider a finite sample set
S\subset P
and, in each step, determine

\displaystyle p_{j}\in\argmax_{p\in S\setminus\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{j-1}}J(p,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{j-1}),

(4)

GSM is described by Algorithm
1
(cf.
[
20
]
).

Different variants of GSM exist based on the selection of the sample set
S
. This set could be constructed via random sampling inside the parameter set
P
; we refer to this variant as GSM-Random. Another option is to construct the set
S
via Latin Hypercube Sampling (GSM-LHS). Latin Hypercube Sampling seeks to ensure a better representation of the variability in the parameter set; see e.g.
[
17
]
for details.

GSM is a strategy to compute a heuristic, possibly suboptimal, solution to
COP
, but the method has drawbacks:

1.

In GSM, the sample size
|S|
must be selected a priori, without clear guidelines for a suitable size.
The sample size
|S|
should be chosen to ensure that the sample set
S
adequately represents the set
P
. Nonetheless, we are unaware of any theoretical results that provide a priori guidance regarding the required sample size to guarantee a good approximation of the optimizer.

2.

Commonly used heuristic strategies choose
|S|
by taking, for example,
|S|\approx n^{d}
, thus taking roughly
n
parameter samples per parameter direction. However, such heuristic strategies rapidly face the ”curse of dimensionality”. Since the objective functions (
4
) have to be computed for each sample in every greedy step, the computations quickly become too costly in higher dimensions.

The first drawback of GSM is addressed in
[
8
]
, specifically for the reduced basis setting. Here, sample sizes are given to guarantee convergence with high probability under the assumption that the solution
u(p)
is analytic. However, this assumption is not met in general.

Several adaptations of GSM have been proposed to overcome the second drawback. Particularly in RB methods, the computational burden is decreased in
[
1
]
by formulating the problem in a low-rank tensor format resulting in more efficient computations. Other approaches to reduce the computational costs focus on the reduction of the sample sizes
|S|
. In
[
25
]
, GSM is performed sequentially over smaller, disjoint sample sets. In
[
12
]
, the sample set is updated after each step by discarding irrelevant samples and enriching the set with more relevant samples based on the value of the objective function. In
[
15
]
, the authors consider a surrogate training set constructed via the successive maximization method. Hybrid versions of the aforementioned methods are investigated in
[
14
]
. In
[
11
]
, alternative methods are proposed to reduce the high computational cost associated with large configurations in the context of the empirical interpolation method (EIM)
[
3
]
. In particular,
[
11
]
proposed an
hp
-refinement procedure that subdivides the parameter domain and subsequently constructs smaller configurations for each subdomain. In the EIM context, the smaller configurations result in a reduction in online cost, but comes at the price of a significantly more expensive offline stage
[
11
]
. Furthermore, in each subdomain, the standard greedy sampling method (with its drawbacks) is still employed in each subdomain. Finally,
[
28
]
uses gradient-based methods to compute local optima from a coarse set of starting points.
The maximizer of the largest local maximum is then selected to update the configuration
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.

Although these alternatives lead to faster computations, they eventually suffer from the same scalability issues as the original GSM. To overcome this issue, we propose the Polytope Division Method (PDM), a greedy method that divides the parameter set into polytopes and uses the barycenters as samples. We introduce this method next.

Polytope Division Method

In this section, we introduce the Polytope Division Method. In a nutshell, the key idea behind the Polytope Division Method is to divide the parameter domain
P
into polytopes and recursively compare the objective function values on the barycenters of these polytopes. After selecting the barycenter with maximal value and updating the configuration accordingly, we then refine the polytope division.

As stated in the second point, determining the facets of a general polytope described by its vertices is non-trivial. However, this can still be done in PDM because we start with a hyperrectangle and know how to update the polytope division in every step. This leads to specific polytopes, which allows us to compute the facets quickly.

Theorem 10
.

Let
P
be a

-dimensional hyperrectangle and let
\mathscr{D}^{N}
be the polytope division of

4
COPs in Model Order Reduction

In this Section, we look at different examples in Model Order Reduction and formulate it as a COP.

4.1
Reduced Basis Method

The GSM and PDM can be used to construct a reduced basis. In this section, we provide a brief explanation of the reduced basis method.

We consider a parametrized variational form

\displaystyle a(u(p),v;p)=f(v;p),\qquad\forall v\in\mathbb{V},

(7)

where
\mathbb{V}
is a Hilbert space,
a:\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}\times P\to\mathbb{R}
is a
\mathbb{V}
-coercive bilinear form and
f:\mathbb{V}\times P\to\mathbb{R}
is a linear form. Here,
p
is the parameter, and
\bar{u}(p)
is the solution of (
7
) depending on the parameter
p
. We assume the parameter set
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
d
-dimensional hyperrectangle. In general, we know that
p\in P
, but the precise value of the parameter is unknown.

For a fixed parameter
p\in P
, the exact solution
\bar{u}(p)
could be well approximated by the Finite Element Method (FEM).
Based on the FEM, there exists a matrix
A(p)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}\times N_{h}}
and a vector
f(p)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}
such that the FEM-solution
u(p)
satisfies

\displaystyle A(p)u(p)=f(p),

(8)

where
N_{h}\in\mathbb{N}
is the number of degrees of freedom. Moreover,
A(p)
is commonly referred to as the ”stiffness” matrix, and
f(p)
is the load vector (see e.g.
[
32
]
).

In this setup, we assume that
A(p)
and
f(p)
have an affine decomposition. This means that there exist
Q_{a},Q_{f}\in\mathbb{N}
and scalar-valued functions
\{\theta_{\eta}^{q}(p)\,|\,q=1,\ldots,Q_{\eta}\}
,
\eta\in\{a,f\}
, such that
A(p)
and
f(p)
can be written as

\displaystyle A(p)=\sum_{q=1}^{Q_{a}}\theta_{a}^{q}(p)A^{q},\qquad f(p)=\sum_{q=1}^{Q_{f}}\theta_{f}^{q}(p)f^{q}.

(9)

The benefit of this assumption is that each
A^{q}
and
f^{q}
can be pre-computed offline.

Although FEM could provide a fine approximate solution for known parameters
p\in P
, the typical high-dimensionality of the FE equation (
8
) leads to very high computational costs, particularly in multi-query settings (see, e.g.
[
4
]
for a survey paper). To reduce the complexity of the solution space, we aim to construct a configuration
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\Gamma_{N},\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{N}\}
, leading to a
reduced basis

V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\textup{span}\{u^{p_{1}},...,u^{p_{N}}\}
. After orthonormalization of the elements of
V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, we also write
V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\textup{span}\{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{N}\}
. Then for every parameter
p\in P
, we assume there exist
\lambda_{i},\,i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, such that
u(p)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\,u^{p_{i}}
, where
N
is the number of basis vectors in
V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
and, ideally,
N\ll N_{h}
.

A reduced basis
V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
can be beneficial because it allows us to construct the reduced system

\displaystyle A_{N}(p)u_{N}(p)=f_{N}(p),

(10)

where
A_{N}(p)\coloneq V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}A(p)V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
,
f_{N}(p)\coloneq V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}f(p)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
u_{N}(p)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, thus allowing us to approximate
u(p)\approx u_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(p)\coloneq V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}u_{N}(p)
. Moreover, due to the affine decomposition assumption (
9
), the quantities
A_{N}
and
f_{N}
can be computed via

\displaystyle A_{N}(p)=\sum_{q=1}^{Q_{a}}\theta_{a}^{q}(p)V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}A^{q}V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\qquad f_{N}(p)=\sum_{q=1}^{Q_{f}}\theta_{f}^{q}(p)V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}f^{q},

where the matrices
V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}A^{q}V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
and vectors
V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}f^{q}
can be pre-computed offline.

Since the linear space
V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
consists of orthonormalized solution vectors for certain parameters, the following question arises: which parameters should we choose to construct the reduced basis? We can formulate this problem as a COP with loss function (
2
). Alternatively, we can consider a loss function based on the residual. We define the error function

\displaystyle J(p,\boldsymbol{\gamma})\coloneq\left\|f-A(p)u_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{p}\right\|^{2}_{(A(p))^{-1}}=\left\|f-A(p)\mathbf{V}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}u(p)_{N}\right\|^{2}_{(A(p))^{-1}},

(11)

where
u_{N}(p)
is given by (
10
) and
u_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(p)\coloneq V_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}u_{N}(p)
.
In this case, the loss function reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{res}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\coloneq\max_{p\in P}J(p,\boldsymbol{\gamma}).

(12)

To construct a reduced basis, both GSM and PDM can be applied. In Section
5
, we compare GSM and PDM in a thermal block example.

4.2
Empirical Interpolation Method

The Empirical Interpolation Method (EIM) is another technique in Model Order Reduction that utilizes GSM and, hence, also suffers from the scalability issues of GSM. Here, we explain how PDM can be used for EIM. We start by explaining EIM as introduced in
[
3
]
.

In the reduced basis method, one often assumes that the stiffness
A(p)
and the load vector
f(p)
admit an affine decomposition. In practice, however, this assumption is not satisfied in general. To overcome this problem, methods such as EIM or hyperreduction are applied to construct an approximate affine decomposition. More precisely, suppose
s(x,p)
is a non-affine function with
x\in\Omega_{h}\subset\Omega
and
p\in P
. The aim is then to express
s(x,p)
as

\displaystyle s(x,p)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}(p)\rho_{j}(x)+e_{\text{EIM}}(x,p),

(13)

where
\sup_{p\in P}\left\|e_{\text{EIM}}(\cdot,p)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{h})}\leq\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon>0
is a pre-selected tolerance.

We consider a set of quadrature points
\mathbf{X}\coloneq\{x_{k}\,|\,k=1,\ldots,N_{q}\}
, and we represent the function
s:\Omega_{h}\times P\to\mathbb{R}
as a
vector function
\mathbf{s}:P\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{q}}
, where

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{k}(p)\coloneq s(x_{k},p),\qquad k=1,\ldots,N_{q}.

Hence, we aim to find a configuration
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\subset P
such that
\text{span}\{\mathbf{s}(p)\,|\,p\in\boldsymbol{\gamma}\}
approximates the manifold
\{\mathbf{s}(p)\,|\,p\in P\}
.

Given a configuration
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\Gamma_{n}(P)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we construct an approximation
\mathbf{s}^{n}(p)
of
\mathbf{s}(p)
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}^{n}(p)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}(p)\rho_{j}.

(14)

Here, we need to decide how to determine the vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}(p)=(\beta_{1}(p),\ldots,\beta_{n}(p))
. We do this by selecting interpolation points
\mathbf{I}_{n}=\{x_{\ell}\in X\,|\,\ell\in I_{n}\subset\{1,\ldots,N_{q}\}\}
and require that the approximated function and the original function are equal at the interpolation points. We thus require that

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{i}^{n}(p)\coloneq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}(p)\rho_{j}(x_{i})=\mathbf{s}_{i}(p),\quad\ell\in I_{n}.

(15)

To illustrate how
\mathbf{s}^{n}
can numerically be computed, we let
\mathbbm{Q}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{q}\times n}
be the matrix with columns
\rho_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. The matrix
\mathbbm{Q}_{I_{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is then constructed by restricting the matrix
\mathbbm{Q}_{n}
to the rows indexed by
I_{n}
. Here,
I_{n}
represents the indices of the selected interpolation points after
n
steps of the algorithm. Denoting by
\mathbf{s}_{I_{n}}
the restriction of
\mathbf{s}
to the elements of
I_{n}
, (
15
) can then be written as

\displaystyle\mathbbm{Q}_{I_{n}}\boldsymbol{\beta}(p)=\mathbf{s}_{I_{n}}(p).

(16)

From this, it follows that
\boldsymbol{\beta}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}(p)=\big(\mathbbm{Q}_{I_{n}}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{I_{n}}(p).

Therefore, the approximation
\mathbf{s}^{n}(p)
takes the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}^{n}(p)=\mathbbm{Q}\mathbbm{Q}_{I_{n}}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{I_{n}}(p).

(17)

The question now arises how to determine the functions
\rho_{i}
and the interpolation points
I_{n}
. For the determination of the configuration
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\Gamma_{n}
, we could apply GSM or PDM with
J(\mathbf{s}(p),\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\left\|\mathbf{s}(p)-\mathbf{s}_{n}(p)\right\|_{\sup}
. This provides an
\mathbf{s}(p_{n})
at every step
n\in\mathbb{N}
and we normalize after each step to obtain
\{\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n}\}
. This normalization is based on the interpolation points.

These interpolation points are also determined recursively, where a new interpolation point is selected based on the error estimate in the previous step. In the first step of GSM and PDM, and given the quadrature points
\mathbf{X}
, we select
\mathbf{s}(p_{1})
such that

\displaystyle p_{1}=\argmax_{p\in P}\left\|\mathbf{s}(p)\right\|_{\infty},

if we can determine this value exactly. Otherwise, we select
p_{1}\in P
randomly.
We then set

\displaystyle i_{1}\coloneq\argmax_{k=1,\ldots,N_{q}}|\mathbf{s}_{k}(p_{1})|\qquad\text{and}\qquad r_{1}\coloneq\mathbf{s}_{i_{1}}(p_{1}).

(18)

Then, we set
\rho_{1}\coloneq r_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{s}(p_{1})
.
In the consecutive steps, we select

\displaystyle i_{n+1}\coloneq\argmax_{k=1,\ldots,N_{q}}|\mathbf{s}_{k}(p_{n+1})-\mathbf{s}_{k}^{n}(p_{n+1})|\quad\text{and}\quad r_{n+1}\coloneq\mathbf{s}_{i_{n+1}}(p_{n+1})-\mathbf{s}_{i_{n+1}}^{n}(p_{n+1}).

(19)

In general,
\rho_{n+1}
is defined as

\displaystyle\rho_{n+1}=r_{n+1}^{-1}\big(\mathbf{s}(p_{n+1})-\mathbf{s}^{n}(p_{n+1})\big).

We could prove by induction that, indeed,
\text{span}\{\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n}\}=\text{span}\{\mathbf{s}(p_{1}),\ldots,\mathbf{s}(p_{n})\}
.

After convergence of the objective function,
J(\mathbf{s}(p),\boldsymbol{\gamma})
, we have an affine approximation of
\mathbf{s}(p)
given by
(
14
)
. In Section
5.2
, we consider a numerical example where we first apply EIM to find an affine approximation and then use this to find a reduced basis.

Outlook

In this paper, we introduced the Polytope Division Method. PDM is an algorithm that can be used to find a heuristic solution to Configuration Optimization Problems. The strength of PDM over the Greedy Sampling Method is twofold:

(1)

It requires significantly fewer sample evaluations in high dimensions;

(2)

It does not require a pre-defined training sample size.

PDM could be further explored for various practical applications and theoretical convergence results. In future work, we aim to provide a rigorous foundation for PDM and consider further applications of PDM to COPs, e.g., in the context of context of Optimal Experimental Design (OED) and Active Learning for Regression (ALR).

Here, we provide the proof of Lemma
12
. The proof relies on the following intermediate result:

Lemma 15
.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a polytope. Let
F\in\partial P
be a facet of
P
and
x\in\mathrm{int}(P)
be an interior point of
P
. Consider the polytope
\tau\coloneq\mathrm{Conv}(x\cup F)
. Then,

\partial\tau=\{F\}\cup\bigl\{\mathrm{Conv}(x\cup G):G\in\partial F\bigr\}.

Proof.

Since
x\in\rm{int}{P}
, it is not difficult to see that
F
is indeed a facet of
\tau
. We are then left to prove that
\mathrm{Conv}(x\cup G)
is also a facet of
\tau
.
A facet of
\tau
needs to be a
(d-1)
-dimensional polytope. We note that
F
is a
(d-1)
-dimensional polytope and
G
is
(d-2)
-dimensional.
Since
x\in\rm{int}(P)
, it is not coplanar to the vertices of
F
and is therefore not coplanar to the vertices of
G
. Hence,
\mathrm{Conv}(x\cup G)
is a
(d-1)
-dimensional polytope. In particular, there exist some
c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Conv}(x\cup G)\cap\mathcal{H}=\mathrm{Conv}(x\cup G),\qquad\text{where $\mathcal{H}\coloneq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,|\,c_{1}\cdot y=\alpha\}$},

i.e.,
\mathcal{H}
is a hyperplane through
G
. We show next that
\mathcal{H}
is a separating hyperplane.
First, we show
\tau\cap\mathcal{H}=\mathrm{Conv}(x\cup G)
. The inclusion
\tau\cap\mathcal{H}\supset\mathrm{Conv}(x\cup G)
is trivial.
Now let
y\in\tau\cap\mathcal{H}
. We note that
y\in\tau
implies there exist
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k+1}\geq 0
,
\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\lambda_{i}=1
such that

y=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}v_{i}+\lambda_{k+1}x.

Here,
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
are the vertices of
F
. Without loss of generality, we say
v_{1},\ldots,v_{l}
, are the vertices of
G
for some
l<k
. If for all
i\in\{l+1,\ldots,k\}
it holds that
\lambda_{i}=0
then
y\in\mathrm{Conv}(x\cup G)
. But if there exists at least one
i\in\{l+1,\ldots,k\}
such that
\lambda_{i}>0
, then
\mathcal{H}
must span a
d
-dimensional space, which contradicts the fact that
\mathcal{H}
is a hyperplane. Hence, we conclude that
y\in\mathrm{Conv}(x\cup G)
.

Next, we show that for all
v\in\{v_{l+1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
, it holds that
c_{1}\cdot v>\alpha
(or
c_{1}\cdot v<\alpha)
. In other words, we show that the vertices of
F
not contained in
G
lie on one side of the hyperplane
\mathcal{H}
. Since
G\in\partial F
, there exist
c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
G
is contained in the hyperplane
\mathcal{H}_{G}=\text{span}\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:c_{2}\cdot y=\beta\}
and
c_{2}\cdot y>\beta
for all
y\in\{v_{l+1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
. For the sake of contradiction, suppose there exist
v,w\in\{v_{l+1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
such that
c_{1}\cdot v>\alpha
and
c_{1}\cdot w<\alpha
.
If such
v,w
exist, there is a
\lambda\in(0,1)
such that
c_{1}\cdot(\lambda v+(1-\lambda)w)=\alpha
.
Hence,
\lambda v+(1-\lambda)w\in F\subset\tau
and
\lambda v+(1-\lambda)w\in\mathcal{H}
, implying that
\lambda v+(1-\lambda)w\in G
, i.e.,

c_{2}\cdot(\lambda v+(1-\lambda)w)=\beta.

On the other hand, since
v,w
satisfies
c_{2}\cdot v>\beta
and
c_{2}\cdot w>\beta
, we deduce

\displaystyle c_{2}\cdot(\lambda v+(1-\lambda)w)

\displaystyle=\lambda c_{2}\cdot v+(1-\lambda)c_{2}\cdot w>\lambda\beta+(1-\lambda)\beta=\beta,

leading to a contradiction. Hence, without loss of generality, we suppose that
c_{1}\cdot v>\alpha
for all
v\in\{v_{l+1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
.
But then
\mathcal{H}
is a separating hyperplane of
\tau
. Indeed, for any
y\in\tau
, it holds that there exist
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k+1}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\mu_{i}=1
and
y=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}v_{i}+\mu_{k+1}x

\displaystyle c_{1}\cdot y=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}c_{1}\cdot v_{i}+\mu_{k+1}c_{1}\cdot x\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\mu_{i}\alpha=\alpha.

This proves that
\mathrm{Conv}(x\cup G)
is indeed a facet of
\tau
.

Lastly, we have to prove that
\tau
does not have any other facets. We note that
\tau
is the convex hull of
\{x,v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
with
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
the vertices of
F

(d-1)
-dimensional polytope.
Since
F
is a facet, there exists a separating hyperplane
\mathcal{H}
such that

\tau\cap\mathcal{H}=F
.
Because
F
is
(d-1)
-dimensional, this hyperplane is unique, meaning that if there exists a subset of vertices
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\}\subset\partial_{d-1}F

\mathcal{N}=\text{span}(\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\})
and
\mathcal{N}

(d-1)

\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{H}

The only other candidate for a facet is
\mathrm{Conv}(\{x,w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\})

w_{i}\in\partial_{d-1}F

\mathrm{Conv}(\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\})

(d-2)
-dimensional.
But then
\mathrm{Conv}(\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\})

\mathcal{H}

In particular, the hyperplane through the points

F

\mathrm{Conv}(x\cup G)
with
