pH-responsive R848-loaded Cu MOF NPs potentiate anti-tumor immunity via synergistic photothermal therapy and immunotherapy in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Qiang Tao, Guangquan Zhang, Ruixi Li, Yufu Peng, Mengni Zhu, Zhengqiang Wu, Zixiang Huang, Fenfen Wang, Xianjie Shi, Jianling Xia, Kefei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the most common type of liver cancer, remains a major cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with limited treatment options and poor prognosis in advanced stages. Immunotherapy has shown promise in treating advanced HCC, with the TLR7/8 agonist resiquimod (R848) effectively activating both innate and adaptive immunity. However, its clinical application is limited by poor solubility, rapid clearance, toxicity, and the inherently low immunogenicity of HCC. To enhance therapeutic efficacy, combination strategies are increasingly explored. Here, we developed a multifunctional copper-based metal–organic framework (Cu MOF) nanoparticles encapsulating R848 (Cu MOF/R848) for synergistic photoimmunotherapy against HCC. Cu MOF/R848 achieves efficient pH-responsive release in the tumor microenvironment. Photothermal therapy (PTT) utilizes the light-absorbing properties of Cu MOF to convert near-infrared (NIR) light into heat, thereby disrupting mitochondria, inducing tumor cell apoptosis, and promoting immunogenic cell death (ICD). This photothermal effect facilitates the release of tumor-associated antigens, while R848 acts as an immune stimulant, together eliciting a robust systemic antitumor response. In vitro, Cu MOF/R848 combined with NIR irradiation induced apoptosis in hepatoma cells by modulating pro- and anti-apoptotic proteins, including upregulating Bax and downregulating Bcl-2, thereby effectively inhibiting cell proliferation and promoting dendritic cell maturation. In vivo, the treatment achieved effective tumor ablation, suppressed neovascularization, and enhanced systemic immune responses by activating CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, promoting their infiltration into tumor tissues, and elevating serum levels of TNF-α and IFN-γ. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Cu MOF/R848 integrates photothermal ablation with immune activation, providing a promising nanoplatform for HCC therapy.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the most prevalent form of primary liver cancer and a leading cause of cancer related deaths globally [1,2], continues to see a rising incidence, frequently emerging against a backdrop of chronic liver disease and cirrhosis [[3], [4], [5]]. While surgical resection and liver transplantation offer the potential for cure, most patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, precluding the possibility of radical surgery [6]. This late diagnosis significantly limits therapeutic options and results in poor prognosis. Furthermore, the complex biology of HCC, which includes significant genetic variability and resistance to standard therapies, presents additional challenges for treatment [7]. Immunotherapy has shown considerable promise for treating advanced HCC, achieving substantial clinical progress in recent years [8,9]. The toll-like receptor 7/8 (TLR7/8) agonist resiquimod (R848) exemplifies this potential, capable of activating both innate and adaptive immunity by promoting dendritic cell maturation and robust T-cell responses[[10], [11], [12]]. However, the clinical translation of R848 is hampered by its inherent poor solubility and rapid clearance, which lead to suboptimal tumor accumulation and potential systemic toxicity [13,14]. More critically, as a potent TLR7/8 agonist, systemically administered free R848 can provoke non-specific immune activation and severe cytokine-related toxicities, drastically narrowing its therapeutic window. Moreover, the inherently low immunogenicity of HCC often leads to insufficient immune responses and immune-related adverse effects, reducing the effectiveness of monotherapy [15,16].

These intertwined pharmacokinetic and safety limitations collectively underscore an urgent need for a targeted delivery strategy that actively reprograms the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment-particularly through the modulation of tumor-associated macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and cancer-associated fibroblasts [17]. while confining R848's potent activity specifically to the tumor site. Nanocarrier-based delivery systems offer a viable strategy to meet this demand. Among them, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)-porous materials constructed from metal ions and organic linkers-have emerged as particularly promising drug delivery platforms [[18], [19], [20]]. Their defining features, including high porosity, tunable pore size, facile synthesis, and excellent biodegradability, make them ideally suited for improving the bioavailability and tumor-specific delivery of challenging drugs like R848 [21,22]. Therefore, MOFs also hold great potential to overcome the poor bioavailability of R848. Notably, Cu-based MOFs possess inherent near-infrared (NIR) absorption, enabling them to function as effective agents for photothermal therapy (PTT) [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. PTT employs such agents to convert NIR light into localized heat, inducing tumor ablation [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. Studies have demonstrated that PTT disrupts mitochondrial integrity and activates pro-apoptotic signaling to induce tumor cell apoptosis, while also promoting immunogenic cell death (ICD), thereby enhancing systemic antitumor immunity [[31], [32], [33]]. However, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment can dampen this effect, often making the immune activation from PTT alone insufficient for durable tumor control or preventing metastasis. It is precisely this limitation that creates a powerful rationale for combining PTT with potent immune adjuvants like R848 [34]. The localized hyperthermia and antigen release from PTT can create a pro-inflammatory "hot" tumor, which R848 can then powerfully amplify, leading to a synergistic antitumor effect [[35], [36], [37], [38], [39]].

Based on these considerations, we constructed multifunctional Cu MOF nanoparticles encapsulating R848 (Cu MOF/R848) to achieve synergistic photoimmunotherapy. Upon NIR irradiation, the Cu MOF generates localized hyperthermia, leading to efficient tumor ablation, mitochondrial disruption, and ICD accompanied by the release of tumor-associated antigens. Simultaneously, the acidic tumor microenvironment promotes the controlled release of R848, which then acts to mature dendritic cells and activate T-cells, thereby amplifying the immune response initiated by PTT. This coordinated action is designed to bridge localized tumor destruction with potent, systemic immune activation, presenting a promising combinatorial strategy against HCC.

Experimental sections

Materials

Azobenzene-4,4′-dicarboxylic acid was obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd, the resiquimod (R848) was purchased from Shanghai Naicheng Biotechnology. The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was provided by biosharp, while Calcein acetoxymethyl (Calcein AM) and propidium iodide (PI) were purchased from Solarbio. Antibodies against Bcl-2, Bax, and β-actin were supplied by Affinit. Anti-CD3, anti-CD4, and anti-CD8a antibodies were acquired from Biolegend. Antibodies against CRT, HMGB1, were purchased from Proteintech. The ATP assay kit was supplied by Beyotime. Anti-CD80, and anti-CD86 antibodies for flow cytometry were purchased from Elabscience. Mouse TNF-α and IFN-γ ELISA kits were obtained from Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd and KELU Biotechnology (Wuhan) Co., Ltd, respectively.

Synthesis of the Cu MOF and the Cu MOF/R848

To prepare Cu MOF, 20 mg Cu(OH)2 and 20 mg Azobenzene-4,4′-dicarboxylic acid (ABDC) were dissolved in a mixture of 7.14 mL ethanol and 2.86 mL ultrapure water. The solution was sonicated for 15 min and stirred for 6 h at room temperature. The Cu MOF was subsequently isolated through centrifugation and thoroughly rinsed three times with deionized water to eliminate any unreacted residues.

As-prepared Cu MOF (4 mg) and R848 (2 mg) were added to 0.5 mL of acetone and ultrasonically dispersed for 2 min. Subsequently, 0.1 mL of folic acid solution (10 mg/mL) and 1.4 mL of deionized water were added to the acetone mixture, followed by stirring overnight. Finally, the Cu MOF/R848 was collected by centrifugation and washed with ethanol and deionized water.

Characterization of Cu MOF and Cu MOF/R848

The morphologies of Cu MOF were analyzed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100F, USA) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM, SU1000, Japan). Elemental analysis was conducted with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) attachment on the SEM, and elemental maps were obtained via TEM. The particle size and zeta potential were measured using a Zetasizer Nano Z instrument (Malvern Instruments, UK). Stability studies were carried out by dispersing Cu MOF in PBS with 10% FBS (pH 7.4) and monitoring changes over 3 days using dynamic light scattering (DLS). The copper content in the Cu MOF was quantified by ICP-OES (Agilent 5800, USA). UV-vis absorption spectra of Cu MOF/R848, Cu MOF, and R848 were recorded. The R848 concentration in the supernatant of Cu MOF/R848 was determined by UV-vis spectroscopy, and the drug loading efficiency (DL%) was calculated as follows:DL%=WdrugloadedWnanoparticles+Wdrugloaded×100%where Wdrug loaded represents the weight of R848 successfully encapsulated in Cu MOF, and Wnanoparticles represents the weight of Cu MOF.

Photothermal performance of Cu MOF

To evaluate the photothermal effect, Cu MOF solutions at varying concentrations were exposed to an 808 nm NIR laser for 5 min (1.5 W/cm2 or 2 W/cm2). The temperature changes of the nanoparticle suspensions were monitored during irradiation using a thermal imaging system, and temperature-time curves were obtained for each concentration to assess the photothermal conversion efficiency of Cu MOF.

To assess photothermal stability, the samples underwent four consecutive heating-cooling cycles, where each cycle involved 5 min of laser exposure (1.5 W/cm2), followed by natural cooling. Temperature changes were monitored using infrared thermal imaging. The photothermal conversion efficiency (η) was calculated using Roper's method. Briefly, Cu MOF was irradiated with an 808 nm laser until thermal equilibrium, followed by recording the cooling curve.

Drug release

The release of R848 from Cu MOF/R848 was evaluated at three pH levels (5.0, 6.5, and 7.4) using UV-vis spectroscopy. Cu MOF/R848 (1 mL) was placed in a dialysis bag (Mw = 6000 Da), immersed in PBS at the specified pH, and stirred at 250 rpm at 37 °C. At 0-70 h intervals, 1 mL of release medium was withdrawn and replaced with fresh PBS. R848 release was quantified by UV-vis spectrophotometry.

Cell culture and cell uptake

For cell culture, SK-Hep-1 and HepG2 lines were propagated in DMEM enriched with 10% FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a water-saturated atmosphere of 5% CO2.

SK-Hep-1 and HepG2 cells were seeded in a 12-well culture plates and allowed to adhere overnight. The cells were then treated with Cu MOF/R848-Cy5 (12.5 μg/mL) for 3 or 6 h, after which they were washed three times with PBS. Cells cultured in blank medium served as the control. Subsequently, the cells were detached using trypsin, washed three times with PBS, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry (Becton Dickinson, USA).

Cytotoxicity evaluation in vitro

The in vitro cytotoxicity of Cu MOF and Cu MOF/R848 against SK-Hep-1 and HepG2 cells was determined using the CCK-8 assay. To evaluate cytotoxicity, SK-Hep-1 and HepG2 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells/well and incubated overnight. The medium was then exchanged for fresh medium containing serial concentrations of Cu MOF or Cu MOF/R848 (0, 1, 5, 12.5, 25, 50, 100, and 200 μg/mL). Following 24 h of incubation, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well for an additional 1 h, after which absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a microplate reader to calculate cell viability.

Cell proliferation assay

SK-Hep-1 and HepG2 cells (1 × 104 per well) were plated in 6-well dishes and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were treated with either Cu MOF, Cu MOF/R848, or Cu MOF/R848 combined with NIR irradiation. Following treatment, cultures were fixed in 2 mL methanol/well for 30 min at room temperature, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 5 min, and rinsed with distilled water. Colony formation was documented by digital photography and quantified by manual counting under a light microscope.

Live/dead cell analysis

The live/dead assay of Cu MOF/R848 was performed using the cell-permeable dye propidium iodide (PI) and Calcein AM. SK-Hep-1 and HepG2 cells were distributed in a 12-well culture plates and incubated overnight. Cells were then exposed to Cu MOF, Cu MOF/R848, or Cu MOF/R848 in combination with NIR irradiation. After 12 h, cells were washed with PBS and stained, and fluorescence was observed via CLSM.

Western blot analysis

SK-Hep-1 and HepG2 cells were first cultured for 24 h, followed by treatment with Cu MOF, Cu MOF/R848, or Cu MOF/R848 + NIR for 12 h. After treatment, cells were collected, washed with PBS, and lysed. Protein expression of Bcl-2, Bax, Cleaved-PARP, and β-actin was assessed using the respective antibodies.

Immunofluorescence staining for ICD markers

HepG2 and SK-Hep-1 cells were seeded in 12-well plates and treated with PBS, Cu MOF, Cu MOF/R848, or Cu MOF/R848 + NIR at 12.5 μg/mL for 24 h. Cells were fixed, permeabilized, and blocked, followed by incubation with anti-CRT or anti-HMGB1 primary antibodies and Alexa Fluor-labeled secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI and images were captured by CLSM. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated as described above. After lysis and centrifugation, ATP levels in the supernatant were measured using an ATP assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from treated cells using TRIzol reagent and reverse-transcribed to cDNA. qPCR was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix using specific primers for MyD88, NF-κB, and TNF-α.

BMDC activation assay

Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were isolated from C57BL/6 mice and cultured with GM-CSF and IL-4 for 6 days. Immature BMDCs were treated with PBS, Cu MOF, Cu MOF/R848, or Cu MOF/R848 + NIR for 24 h, then stained with anti-CD80-PE and anti-CD86-FITC antibodies for flow cytometry analysis.

Toxicity study

To assess the in vivo biocompatibility of Cu MOF/R848, healthy 6-week-old C57BL mice (from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center) were intravenously administered varying doses of Cu MOF/R848 (0.5-300 mg/kg) via the tail vein. Mice were closely monitored for general health and behavior throughout the study. After 24 h, mice were sacrificed, and major organs, including heart, liver, kidney, lung, and spleen, were collected for histological analysis. For long-term toxicity evaluation, additional groups of healthy mice were intravenously injected with Cu MOF/R848 at multiple doses (0.5, 1.15, 2.3, 4.7, and 9.4 mg/kg) and monitored for 28 days. Body weights were recorded weekly, and serum biochemical parameters, including total bilirubin (T-bil), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), creatinine (CREA), urea (UREA), and creatine kinase (CK), were measured at the endpoint (day 28). Major organs were also collected for histopathological examination. All experiments involving animals adhered to the guidelines established by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and approved by Ethics committee of the Eighth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University (Reference Number: 202504301).

Biodistribution study

When tumors reached ∼100 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to two groups and injected via tail vein with either free Cy7 or Cu MOF/R848-Cy7. Fluorescence imaging was conducted using an IVIS Lumina II system (PerkinElmer) at 0, 1, 3, 6, 24, and 48 h post-injection. After 48 h, mice were sacrificed, and major organs were collected for fluorescence analysis.

In vivo thermal imaging

C57BL mice were used to establish a tumor model by subcutaneous injection of Heap1-6 cells (2 × 106) into the right armpit region. Starting from day 5 post-injection, tumor growth was monitored, and tumor volume were measured every 2 days. In vivo photothermal performance of Cu MOF/R848 was evaluated in tumor-bearing mice treated with PBS + NIR, Cu MOF + NIR, or Cu MOF/R848 + NIR. Tumors were irradiated with an 808 nm laser (1.5 W/cm2, 5 min) 1 h post-injection, and temperature changes were monitored via thermal imaging.

In vivo anti-cancer efficacy

When tumor volumes reached ∼100 mm3, mice were randomly divided into six groups: (1) PBS + NIR, (2) Cu MOF/R848 (1 mg/kg), (3) Cu MOF + NIR (0.9 mg/kg), (4) Cu MOF/R848 + NIR (1 mg/kg), (5) free R848 (100 μg/kg), and (6) Cu MOF (0.9 mg/kg). Tumors in the laser-treated groups were irradiated with an 808 nm laser (1.5 W/cm2, 5 min) within 0.5-1 h post-injection. Each group received 100 μL of the designated formulation via a single tail vein injection. Tumor volume and body weight were monitored bi-daily, and tumor volume (V) was calculated as described.

At the conclusion of the treatment, mice were euthanized, and tumors along with major organs were collected, weighed, and imaged. The collected tissues were washed three times with PBS, fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Histopathological analyses of tumor sections were performed using H&E, TUNEL, and Ki67 staining by immunohistochemistry, and apoptosis was assessed by TUNEL assay. Immunofluorescence staining was performed to evaluate the expression of Bax, Cleaved-caspase-3, Bcl-2, CRT, and CD31. Additionally, Western blot analysis was conducted to detect HMGB1, MyD88, and p-NF-κB expression in tumor tissues, and commercial ELISA kits were used to measure TNF-α and IFN-γ levels. For long-term safety assessment, additional groups of mice were treated with the six formulations under the same dosing regimen and monitored for 28 days. Body weights, serum biochemical parameters (T-bil, ALT, AST, TP, ALB, CREA, UREA, CK), and H&E staining of major organs were evaluated at the endpoint.

Flow cytometry assay

Single-cell suspensions were obtained from mechanically dissociated spleens. Flow cytometry with anti-CD3, anti-CD4, and anti-CD8 staining was used to evaluate lymphocyte subset distributions.

Statistical analysis

The data were are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical comparisons were conducted using unpaired Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA in GraphPad Prism 9. NS indicates no significant difference; *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Result and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of Cu MOF/R848

The Cu MOF nanoparticles were synthesized via a facile one-step method (Fig. 1A). During sonication, Cu (II) dimers coordinated with the carboxylate groups of the ABDC linker, facilitating the self-assembly of Cu MOF framework [40]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images revealed an irregular bulk-like morphology. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and elemental mapping confirmed the homogeneous distribution of Cu, C, N, and O, which is consistent with the expected chemical composition (Fig. 1B and Fig. S1A). ICP-OES analysis showed a Cu2+ loading capacity of 25.14% (Fig. S1B), suggesting substantial metal incorporation that contributes to structural stability and potential therapeutic functions, including photothermal conversion and catalytic activity. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements indicated a mean hydrodynamic diameter of 158.23 ± 27.32 nm (Fig. 1C). The Cu MOF nanoparticles demonstrated excellent dispersibility in biologically relevant media, including phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, pH 7.4). No visible aggregation or sedimentation was observed over three days under ambient conditions, suggesting good short-term stability (Fig. 1D). The photothermal performance of the Cu MOF nanoparticles was subsequently evaluated in aqueous dispersion. As shown in Fig. 1E–F, PBS exhibited negligible temperature change under 808 nm laser irradiation (2 W/cm2), whereas the Cu MOF solution showed a significant temperature increase of approximately 20 °C, confirming its efficient photothermal conversion. As expected, the temperature elevation exhibited a positive correlation with both laser power density and nanoparticle concentration, providing crucial guidance for optimizing treatment parameters in photothermal therapy. To assess the photothermal conversion stability of Cu MOF as a photothermal agent, cyclic irradiation experiments were conducted (Fig. 1G). The Cu MOF solution was subjected to NIR irradiation followed by natural cooling, repeated for four cycles. The results demonstrated that the solution could be rapidly heated above 50 °C within 5 min of irradiation and cooled back to room temperature in 6-8 min. The heating-cooling curves were highly consistent across cycles, indicating excellent photothermal stability and reproducibility. Using the cooling curve, the photothermal conversion efficiency of Cu MOF was calculated to be 75.37% at 808 nm.

After loading with R848 (denoted as Cu MOF/R848), the hydrodynamic diameter increased to 188.70 ± 15.32 nm, which is likely attributable to the incorporation of R848 molecules within the Cu MOF porous framework and potential surface adsorption. Concurrently, the zeta potential decreased to −22.75 ± 0.84 mV, indicating the influence of negatively charged R848 and improved colloidal stability (Fig. 1H–I). UV-Vis analysis (Fig. 1J and S1C) further verified the presence of R848, as shown by its signature absorption peaks at 226, 246, and 320 nm. The loading capacity of R848 was determined to be 10.04%.

The drug release profile of Cu MOF/R848 was investigated under different pH conditions to simulate physiological (pH 7.4), tumor microenvironment (pH 6.5), and endo/lysosomal (pH 5.0) environments (Fig. 1K). While minimal release (2.82%) occurred at pH 7.4 over 72 h, signifying stability in circulation, a markedly enhanced and pH-dependent release was observed under acidic conditions (26.39% at pH 6.5 and 70.84% at pH 5.0). This pronounced pH-dependent release profile is presumably due to the protonation of the carboxylate groups on the ABDC linkers under acidic conditions, which weakens the coordination bonds between Cu2+ ions and the organic ligands, leading to partial framework collapse and subsequent liberation of the encapsulated R848 from the disrupted pore channels. These findings demonstrate the potential of Cu MOF as a stable nanocarrier for immunomodulatory drug delivery, enabling controlled and tumor microenvironment-responsive release to enhance antitumor efficacy while minimizing systemic side effects.

Thus, we first investigated the internalization of Cy5-labeled Cu MOF/R848 (Cu MOF/R848-Cy5) in HepG2 and SK-Hep-1 cells using flow cytometry. Flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 2A and B) demonstrated that both cell lines exhibited significant time-dependent uptake of Cu MOF/R848-Cy5. The cytotoxic effects of Cu MOF/R848 and Cu MOF were subsequently assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Fig. 2C and D shows that cell viability of both HepG2 and SK-Hep-1 cells decreased in a concentration-dependent manner. Notably, Cu MOF/R848 exhibited significantly enhanced cytotoxicity compared to Cu MOF at equivalent concentrations, underscoring the contribution of the immunotherapeutic agent R848 to cell death. At 12.5 μg/mL, Cu MOF/R848 displayed a significantly stronger inhibitory effect on both cell lines compared to Cu MOF group. Accordingly, 12.5 μg/mL was chosen as the experimental concentration for subsequent studies. Crystal violet staining (Fig. 2E–H) further confirmed that Cu MOF and Cu MOF/R848 effectively suppressed tumor cell proliferation, with the most pronounced inhibition observed in the Cu MOF/R848 + NIR group.

Moreover, the migration ability of HepG2 and SK-Hep-1 cells after different treatments was evaluated using wound healing assays. As shown in Fig. 2I and J, Cu MOF showed a limited effect on cell motility, whereas Cu MOF/R848 and Cu MOF/R848 + NIR significantly inhibited cell migration. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Cu MOF/R848+NIR possesses efficient cellular uptake, potent cytotoxicity, and strong inhibition of HCC cell proliferation and migration, highlighting the synergistic potential of this combined photothermal and immunotherapeutic strategy. To further investigate nanoparticle induced cell death, live/dead staining was conducted using Calcein-AM and PI. As shown in Fig. 2M − N, both the Cu MOF/R848 and Cu MOF/R848 + NIR groups displayed dominant red fluorescence, indicating extensive tumor cell death. Consistently, quantitative flow cytometry analysis confirmed a higher proportion of apoptotic cells in the Cu MOF/R848 + NIR group, with apoptotic cells accounting for approximately 23%, significantly exceeding those in the other groups (Fig. S2). These results demonstrate that Cu MOF/R848 effectively induces apoptosis in tumor cells under NIR irradiation.

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying this enhanced cell death, we examined key markers of apoptosis. A caspase-3 activity assay revealed a significant (∼1.5-fold) increase in fluorescence intensity in the Cu MOF/R848 + NIR group compared to the control in both HepG2 (Fig. 3A) and SK-Hep-1 cells (Fig. 3C), indicating robust activation of the executioner phase of apoptosis. Western blot analysis further demonstrated that this combined treatment synergistically upregulated the expression of the pro-apoptotic protein Bax while downregulating the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 (Fig. 3B–D), suggesting the involvement of the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway [41,42]. To further delineate the downstream apoptotic cascade, we examined cleaved-PARP, a key executioner substrate of Caspase-3. We next investigated whether ICD was induced by Cu MOF/R848 + NIR. Immunofluorescence staining showed that CRT exposure on the cell surface was significantly increased in the Cu MOF/R848 + NIR group, while intracellular HMGB1 signal decreased, indicating its release into the extracellular space (Fig. 3G–J). Intracellular ATP levels were also markedly reduced in the Cu MOF/R848 + NIR group (Fig. S3). These findings confirm that Cu MOF/R848 + NIR effectively induces ICD, characterized by the emission of DAMPs that are critical for initiating antitumor immunity.

To interrogate the downstream signaling cascade, we examined the expression of MyD88, NF-κB, and TNF-α in HepG2 and SK-Hep-1 cells by qPCR. As shown in Fig. 3K–M, Cu MOF/R848 + NIR significantly upregulated all three markers compared to the control group, while Cu MOF alone showed no significant effect. These results confirm that R848 activates the TLR7/8/NF-κB signaling axis, promoting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines essential for initiating antitumor immune responses.

The functional relevance of ICD was further assessed in a BMDC activation assay. Cu MOF/R848 significantly upregulated CD80 and CD86 expression on BMDCs compared to the control group, with the Cu MOF/R848 + NIR group showing the highest expression of both markers. Cu MOF alone exhibited only minimal DC activation (Fig. 3N). These results provide direct in vitro evidence that Cu MOF/R848 promotes DC maturation, a critical step in initiating adaptive antitumor immunity.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the combination of photothermal therapy (from Cu MOF) and immunotherapy (via R848) in our nanoplatform synergistically triggers potent apoptosis in cancer cells. This is achieved by activating caspase-3 and shifting the Bax/Bcl-2 balance in favor of cell death, while simultaneously inducing ICD and activating the TLR7/8/NF-κB pathway to promote DC maturation, thereby achieving superior therapeutic efficacy [43].

In vivo biodistribution and photothermal imaging performance of Cu MOF/R848

The selective accumulation of nanotherapeutics within tumors is crucial for maximizing therapeutic efficacy and minimizing off-target effects. To evaluate the biodistribution of our platform, tumor-bearing mice were intravenously administered with either free Cy7 or Cu MOF/R848-Cy7. As shown in Fig. 4A, the Cu MOF/R848-Cy7 group exhibited rapid and sustained tumor accumulation, with a strong fluorescence signal observed at the tumor site as early as 1 h post-injection. Remarkably, this signal persisted for up to 48 h, demonstrating prolonged tumor retention. Subsequent ex vivo imaging of excised organs and tumors at the 48-h time point (Fig. 4B) revealed a substantially higher fluorescence intensity in the tumors from the Cu MOF/R848-Cy7 group compared to the free dye control, in which the signal had largely dissipated. This confirms the superior tumor-targeting and retention properties of our nanoparticle, which is conducive to localized drug delivery. To determine whether the efficient tumor accumulation translates into a functional photothermal effect, we monitored the temperature change at tumor sites under NIR laser irradiation using an infrared thermal camera. In mice treated with either Cu MOF or Cu MOF/R848, the tumor temperature increased rapidly upon irradiation, reaching approximately 49 °C within 4 min-a level sufficient to induce irreversible tumor cell damage through protein denaturation and necrosis (Fig. 4C and D). Looking forward, the intratumoral retention of such nanoplatforms could be further augmented through intelligent design strategies, which actively reduce back-flow from the tumor interstitium to the peripheral bloodstream [44].

In vivo antitumor efficacy and systemic toxicity of Cu MOF/R848

Prior to therapeutic evaluation, the in vivo biocompatibility of Cu MOF/R848 was systematically assessed. Healthy mice were assigned into groups and intravenously injected with PBS or escalating doses of Cu MOF/R848 (0.5, 1.15, 2.3, 4.7, 9.4, 18.8, 37.5, 75, 150, and 300 mg/kg). Acute toxicity was monitored within 24 h post-administration, and histopathological examinations of major organs were subsequently performed (Figs. S4 and S5). Cu MOF/R848 induced dose-dependent toxicity, with immediate lethality at 150–300 mg/kg, death within 24 h at 75 mg/kg, and severe or mild tissue injury at 37.5 and 18.8 mg/kg, respectively. In contrast, no significant pathological or morphological abnormalities were detected at doses of 9.4 mg/kg or below. Based on these findings, together with the clinically relevant dosage of R848 and its encapsulation efficiency within the Cu MOF carrier, subsequent therapeutic studies were performed using Cu MOF/R848 at doses ≤ 9.4 mg/kg to ensure biosafety. To further evaluate the long-term safety profile, a 28-day subacute toxicity study was conducted in healthy mice at multiple doses (0.5, 1.15, 2.3, 4.7, and 9.4 mg/kg). As shown in Fig. S6A, no significant body weight loss was observed in any treatment group throughout the 28-day period compared to the PBS control group. Serum biochemical analysis at day 28 revealed no significant abnormalities in liver function markers (T-bil, ALT, AST, TP, ALB), kidney function markers (CREA, UREA), or cardiac marker (CK) across all dose groups (Fig. S6B). Histological examination of major organs further confirmed the absence of overt tissue damage or pathological changes at all tested doses (Fig. S6C). These results collectively demonstrate the favorable long-term biosafety profile of Cu MOF/R848 at therapeutically relevant doses.

A subcutaneous Hepa1-6 tumor model was established to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy. When tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomized into five groups: PBS + NIR, R848, Cu MOF + NIR, Cu MOF/R848, and Cu MOF/R848 + NIR (Fig. 5A). Throughout the treatment period, all groups maintained stable body weights, indicating minimal systemic toxicity under the administered doses (Fig. 5B). As expected, PBS-treated mice exhibited rapid tumor growth (Fig. 5C–E). Monotherapies with free R848 or Cu MOF + NIR resulted in modest tumor suppression. Strikingly, while Cu MOF/R848 alone (without laser) showed significant antitumor activity, the combination of Cu MOF/R848 with NIR irradiation led to the complete eradication of tumors within approximately six days. H&E staining revealed no obvious changes in cell morphology, size, or necrosis in the PBS + NIR group, consistent with uncontrolled tumor progression. In contrast, tumor sections from the other treatment groups exhibited clear morphological alterations. Notably, the Cu MOF/R848 group displayed highly irregular tumor cell morphology, with extensive vacuolation. Ki67 immunostaining and TUNEL staining further supported the therapeutic benefit, The Cu MOF/R848 group displayed a moderate reduction in positive signals of Ki67, suggesting that cell proliferation was hindered, while TUNEL images confirmed substantial cell death within the tumor cells of the Cu MOF/R848 group, which was significantly more pronounced compared to the other groups. To further examine treatment effects on the tumor microenvironment, CD31 immunofluorescence was performed to assess angiogenesis [45]. The PBS group displayed strong CD31 expression, indicative of active angiogenesis and rapid progression. Notably, the Cu MOF/R848 group displayed the weakest CD31 signal, demonstrating that the synergistic therapy not only directly kills tumor cells but also potently suppresses tumor-associated angiogenesis, thereby impeding nutrient supply and metastatic potential. The reduced CD31 expression likely reflects both direct thermal damage to tumor vasculature from localized hyperthermia and indirect vascular remodeling mediated by infiltrating CD8+ and CD4+ T cells via anti-angiogenic cytokines such as IFN-γ [46].

In vivo antitumor mechanisms of Cu MOF/R848

To elucidate the underlying mechanism of cell death, immunofluorescence staining was performed, with red fluorescence marking apoptosis-related proteinstumor sections were analyzed by immunofluorescence for key apoptosis-related proteins (Fig. 6A). Compared with the PBS + NIR group, treatment with Cu MOF + NIR, free R848, and Cu MOF/R848 all markedly increased the expression of the pro-apoptotic protein Bax while downregulating the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2. This shift in the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio promotes mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, leading to the release of apoptogenic factors and the subsequent activation of executioner caspase-3. Importantly, the Cu MOF/R848 group demonstrated the most extensive apoptosis, consistent with its complete tumor eradication. We further assessed the functional status of the antitumor immune response by measuring serum cytokine levels. As shown in Fig. 7G–H, the Cu MOF/R848 + NIR group showed markedly elevated levels of TNF-α and IFN-γ, two key effector cytokines produced by activated T cells and involved in tumor cell killing. These results confirm that the infiltrating T cells are functionally active and contribute to a robust systemic antitumor immune response.

To verify whether the combination therapy induces ICD and activates the TLR7/8 signaling pathway in vivo, we examined key molecular markers in tumor tissues. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that Cu MOF/R848 + NIR treatment significantly increased CRT exposure on tumor cells, a hallmark of ICD (Fig. 7B). Western blot analysis further showed decreased intracellular HMGB1, indicating its release into the extracellular space, along with upregulation of MyD88 and p-NF-κB (Fig. 7C–F). These results confirm that R848 released from Cu MOF/R848 activates the TLR7/8/NF-κB signaling axis in the tumor microenvironment, which cooperates with PTT-induced ICD to amplify the antitumor immune response.

In summary, the potent inhibition of hepatocellular carcinoma by Cu MOF/R848 stems from a synergistic loop of photothermal therapy and immunotherapy. Intravenously administered Cu MOF/R848 effectively accumulated in tumor tissues via passive targeting. Upon NIR irradiation, localized hyperthermia induced direct tumor cell ablation and ICD, as evidenced by CRT exposure and HMGB1/ATP release. The released tumor-associated antigens, combined with the TME-responsive release of R848, activated the TLR7/8/NF-κB pathway, promoted DC maturation, and elicited robust systemic and local antitumor T-cell responses, ultimately achieving effective tumor regression.

Finally, the biosafety of Cu MOF/R848 was comprehensively evaluated. In the 28-day safety study, no significant body weight loss was observed in any treatment group throughout the monitoring period (Fig. S8A). H&E staining of major organs further confirmed the absence of overt tissue damage in either the saline or Cu MOF/R848 + NIR groups (Fig. S8B). Serum biochemical analysis at day 28 revealed no significant abnormalities in T-bil, ALT, AST, TP, ALB, CREA, UREA, CK across all treatment groups (Fig. S8C). These results collectively demonstrate the favorable long-term safety profile of the Cu MOF/R848 nanoplatform, an essential prerequisite for its potential clinical translation.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a multifunctional Cu-based MOF nanoparticles encapsulating R848 for NIR-mediated synergistic photoimmunotherapy. Cu MOF/R848 exhibited efficient tumor accumulation and tumor microenvironment-responsive release, enabling precise and controlled delivery of the immunoadjuvant. Upon NIR irradiation, Cu MOF/R848 effectively induced apoptosis and inhibited proliferation of hepatoma cells in vitro. After verifying its biocompatibility and photothermal capability, we validated that Cu MOF/R848 could evoke potent antitumor effects and enhance the immune response in vivo. The combination of Cu MOF/R848 with NIR irradiation induced tumor cell necrosis and ICD. Furthermore, the released R848 cooperated with the PTT-generated antigens to activate a robust tumor-specific immune response, significantly increasing the population of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. Collectively, our work demonstrates that the Cu MOF/R848 platform can significantly suppress hepatocellular carcinoma progression, highlighting its promise as an integrated strategy for cancer therapy.
