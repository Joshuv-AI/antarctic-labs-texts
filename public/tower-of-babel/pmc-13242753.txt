Comparison of the Effects of Local Heat and Massage Using the Flick Application on Pain Caused by Vitamin K Injection in Neonates
Authors: Javad Momenisoroshk, Tabandeh Sadeghi, Seyedhamid Seyedbagheri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeHealthy neonates experience pain during procedures such as vitamin K injections. Pain adversely affects the neonate, so it is important to control it. This study compared the effects of local heat and massage using the flick application on the pain caused by vitamin K injection in neonates.Patients and MethodsThis experimental research was conducted on neonates in Rafsanjan, Iran. The samples were selected using the convenience method and randomly placed in three groups: flick application massage, local heat, and control. In the flick application group, a flick was delivered with the thumb and the index finger on the injection site. In the local heat group, injection site was heated to 40 °C using a hair dryer. The pain score was measured using the Neonatal Infant Pain Scale (NIPS), and data analysis was performed using SPSS software.ResultsBased on the results, there was a significant difference between the average pain score of the neonates in the local heat group and the control group (P<0.001) and between the massage group and the control group (P<0.001). Neonates in the local heat group experienced less pain than those in the massage group. However, there was no difference between the average pain score of the massage group and the local heat group after injection (P = 0.11).ConclusionIn this study, both interventions reduced the pain caused by the intramuscular injection of vitamin K. These findings suggest that either technique can be used in clinical practice, depending on the conditions.Keywords: local heat, massage, pain, intramuscular injection, vitamin K, neonate

Introduction

Neonatal is one of the most critical periods of human life.1 Healthy neonates experience pain during procedures such as blood sampling for metabolic disease screening, vitamin K injection, or hepatitis B vaccination.2 In the past, it was believed that neonates could not perceive pain due to the lack of central nervous system development. However, today, it has been found that the physiological, anatomical, and neurochemical structures related to pain are complete even several weeks before birth.1,3 According to the International Association for the Study of Pain, pain is a physical sensation and an unpleasant experience caused by actual or potential tissue damage.4 The American Nurses Association has identified pain as the fifth vital sign, emphasizing its importance and increasing healthcare providers’ awareness about pain management.5

Neonates show behavioral and physiological responses to painful stimuli, which can be observed and measured.2 The immediate effects of pain include fear, irritability, inadequacy of ventilation and blood circulation, increased oxygen consumption, and sleep and feeding disturbances. Short-term effects include delay in wound healing, changes in the activity of the immune system, and damage to the emotional relationship between mother and neonates. The long-term effects include delay in development, remembering the pain, and a strong response to similar painful experiences in the future.4 Pain control in neonates is very important. There is no routine medical intervention to relieve the pain caused by vitamin K injections in neonates. Nowadays, non-pharmacological pain relief techniques have attracted the attention of nurses and physicians.6 These types of interventions are effective, simple, and safe. They do not need to be performed at specific times using expensive equipment; unlike pharmaceutical interventions, they do not have side effects.7 Hugging,8 non-nutritive sucking,9 shaking, tactile stimulation,10 using solutions with different flavors,11 and hot and cold compresses12 are among the non-pharmacological methods of pain relief that have been mentioned in various studies. One of the non-pharmacological methods is massage therapy, which reduces pain and increases vagal activity.13 The pain gate control mechanism is activated by the large myelinated fibers near the neural pain gates in the environment after performing vibrational stimulation on the skin, increasing beta-endorphin levels. The secreted beta-endorphin raises the pain threshold and reduces or eliminates the pain sensation.14

The results of Fitri et al’s study, “Massage therapy as a non-pharmacological analgesia for procedural pain in neonates” showed that gentle massage (using the foot massage, effleurage massage and leg massage) reduces pain in neonates by activating stress hormone reduction pathways.6 Based on the study by Zargham Borujeni et al, the effect of massage using the effleurage technique (gentle touch) on reducing pain was greater than that of breastfeeding.15 The flick application before injection is a simple, easy, cheap, and safe method compared to other injection area pain management techniques. According to the results of the study of Karaca et al, massage using the flick application reduces pain during injection and the duration of crying in neonates.16 Based on the results of Gol et al’s study, applying manual pressure to the injection area (with an effect similar to the flick application) reduced pain in the injection area.17

Another accepted method of reducing pain in neonates is using local heat. The heat method is based on gate control theory. Vascular changes reduce local pain so that increased blood flow caused by heat can wash away the pain-initiating factors released by tissue damage.18 Local heat relaxes muscles, facilitates blood circulation, and promotes metabolism, thus relieving pain.12 In the study of Ahmadpour-Kacho et al, where a warmer was used to provide heat, the results showed that heat can effectively control pain during painful procedures such as intramuscular injection of vitamin K.2

A hair dryer is a heating device that produces hot, dry air with a monotonous sound, and it has been used in distraction studies. According to the results of the study by Ghobadi et al, heating the neonates heel with a hair dryer reduced pain during and after heel-blood sampling.19

Considering the importance of managing and controlling pain caused by painful procedures in neonates and that the use of local heat, on the one hand, requires devices such as warmers, hair dryers, or hot water bags, and on the other hand, there is a possibility of burns or skin problems. The flick application is an easy, cheap method that does not require special tools and is not likely to cause complications. By comparing these two methods, we would like to know if the flick application is more effective and a suitable alternative for pain control on neonates than applying local heat. Therefore, this study was designed to compare the effect of massage using the flick application and local heat on pain caused by intramuscular injection of vitamin K in neonates.

Materials and Methods

The current research was a semi-experimental study conducted in 2024 on neonates ready for vitamin K injection in Niknafs maternity hospital in Rafsanjan. Neonates who met the inclusion criteria were selected using the convenience method and underwent simple randomization. Participants were randomly allocated to the three study groups using a simple randomization method: a ball-in-bag lottery. This procedure was specifically designed to enhance parental engagement and trust in the allocation process. The inclusion criteria for this study were 37 to 42 weeks of pregnancy, proportional weight to the duration of pregnancy (2500 to 3500 grams), mother’s non-use of painkillers before delivery, absence of contraindications for the use of local heat and massage therapy, temperature between 37.5 °C and 36.5 °C, an APGAR score between 7 and 10 in the first and fifth minutes, no hypoglycemia, no painful procedure before injection, calm and awake, absence of congenital abnormality, and parents’ consent to participate in the study. The exclusion criterion was the parents’ refusal to continue the neonate participation in the research. Neonates were examined immediately after delivery and stabilization of vital signs, and upon confirmation of eligibility criteria, the intervention (injection) was administered in the nursery. Following the study by Ahmadpour-Kacho et al,2 the sample size was 32.12 for each group. Because there were three groups, the sample size had to be 44.97, and considering possible dropouts, finally 50 neonate were determined in each group.

The demographic information questionnaire of the neonate included gestational age (number of days of mother’s pregnancy taking into account the date of the last menstrual period), neonate’s age, weight, gender, height, and head circumference. The Neonatal Infant Pain Scale (NIPS) was published by Laurence in 1993 and has been used in many studies to measure pain in neonates, with a score between 0 and 7.20 Six items are examined in this scale, including facial expression, neonate’s crying, breathing pattern, hand movement, leg movement, and state of consciousness. Generally, a score between 0 to 3 indicates no pain, a score of 4 to 5 indicates moderate pain and a score greater than 5 indicates severe pain. The validity and reliability of the NIPS have been determined repeatedly in different studies.21 The reliability coefficient of this scale in Iran is 0.93 to 0.99.22

After receiving the ethical code ID IR.RUMS.REC.1402.203 from Rafsanjan University of Medical Sciences, the researchers started the sampling process. At the beginning of the study, the research objectives were explained to the mother, and her informed consent was obtained. All procedures were performed in accordance.

Ethical Considerations

With the ethical guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Then, the qualified neonates were placed in the delivery room in one of the three groups of massage using the flick application, local heat, and control. In the massage group, the massage technique based on Karaca’s study is as follows: before the injection, the anterior part of the vastus lateralis muscle (selected leg) was identified for injection, and the injection site was given a quick flicked (according to Figure 1, the thumb should be placed on the index finger) and then the site was disinfected with alcohol and the injection was performed.16

The method of massage intervention using the flick application.

The injection site is shaken with the left hand prior to the invasive procedure. The flicking is performed as follows: the thumb is placed on the middle fingernail, and then the target area is stimulated with a quick strike using the distal part of the middle fingernail.

In the local heat group, the anterior part of the vastus lateralis muscle neonate’s was heated using a hair dryer 3 minutes before the injection. In order to comply with the sound intensity of less than 45 dB (to reduce the negative effects of sound),23 we placed the hair dryer on a fixed handle at a distance of 60 cm inside a chamber, heated to a temperature of 40 °C, and then the injection was performed.19 The temperature of the neonate’s thigh was measured using a strip thermometer four minutes after warming. In the control group, vitamin K injection was done using the standard method without any intervention.

Using an insulin syringe size 30 and a 13 mm needle, 1 mg of vitamin K was injected quickly. Injections were performed in all neonates in the same environment regarding temperature and light by the same person. In order to determine the pain scores, at the same time as the injection was initiated, the researcher’s colleague filmed the neonate’s face and limbs using a mobile camera, and they continued filming until two minutes after the injection. The videos were viewed and scored separately by a colleague for blinding.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using descriptive and analytical tests. Between-group comparisons employed one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test, within-group analysis used paired t-tests, and demographic variables were compared with Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

The present study investigated the effects of local heat and massage using the flick application on pain caused by vitamin K injection in neonates. This study corroborates the results of Bhattacharya et al’s, confirming that the Helfer technique lowers pain levels in neonates during vitamin K injection. Prior to the intramuscular injection, the site was tapped with the finger pads 16 times for 5 seconds, and tapping was repeated concurrently during the injection. The possible reasons for the alignment could be the use of massage intervention and the use of the NIPS in both studies.24 Consistent with the present study, Gray et al’s study showed that the pain level in neonates during IM injection in the heat and sucrose groups was reduced.25 Regarding the effect of massage, the results of the study by Shahroudi et al showed the positive effect of massage on the pain level of neonates during IM injection, which was consistent with the results of the present study.26

In the study by Sapkota et al, heating the heel effectively reduced the pain caused by heel-blood sampling, indicating that perhaps heat applied to neonates in any way can moderate pain intensity.27 The results of the study by Zahedpasha et al showed that there was a statistically significant difference in the average pain score during injection vitamin K in the heat group.2

There was a statistically significant difference between the average pain score of the neonates in all three groups (even the control group) during and after the injection. However, this difference was greater in the massage and local heat groups than in the control group. These results can be justified as it is normal for the neonate’s pain to decrease gradually after the injection. The difference in the average pain was greater in the massage group. The study of Mohamed Elshahat et al showed that the cold therapy intervention had a significantly lower average pain score during and after the injection than the heat therapy group.12 In the study of karabiyikogurlu et al, The participants experienced a significant reduction in pain.28

Consistent with the present study, the results of Karaca et al’s study (2016) showed that in the flick application intervention group, there was a significant difference in the pain score after the injection. The pain was effectively reduced in the flick application group.16 The similarity of the studies is in the use of massage with the flick application on the pain level in neonates, with the difference that in their study, pain was measured during vaccination. In contrast, the present study investigated pain caused by vitamin K injection. As we lack data comparing pain from vaccines and vitamin K injections, this study should serve as a basis for future research on the topic. According to the results of Chik et al’s study (2017), consistent with the present study, the average pain scores during and 30 seconds after vein extraction in neonates who received massage were significantly lower than in neonates who did not.29 A strength of this study is the balanced distribution of key demographic and clinical characteristics between the intervention and control groups. This balance minimizes the potential confounding effect of these variables on pain level outcomes and thereby enhances the validity of the comparisons made. This study had several limitations. First, the nursing staff had no prior experience with the standardized application of the interventions. Although this was addressed through pre-trial training sessions to ensure protocol adherence, it may reflect the real-world challenge of implementing new techniques. Second, the presence of neonates from different nationalities (eg., Afghan) was a consideration; however, we ensured homogeneity in this characteristic across the groups during randomization to minimize its potential confounding effect.

Conclusion

Based on the results of the present study, both local heat intervention and massage using the flick application were effective. They reduced the pain intensity during and after vitamin K injections in neonates. The methods used in this study each have their own advantages. Flick application is easy, safe, and without special equipment. Local heat can also be easily used with easy application and without the need for complex equipment and expert personnel. Therefore, using these methods as non-pharmacological interventions is recommended during painful procedures such as neonate vitamin K injections. Further comparative studies on the Flickard and Helfer techniques are recommended to establish definitive clinical guidelines for neonatal pain management.
