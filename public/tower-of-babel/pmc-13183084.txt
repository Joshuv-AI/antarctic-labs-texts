Apoptosis-related gene model predicts the prognosis in patients with acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Xiang Pei, Wenbing Liu, Yishuang Li, Hui Wei, Min Wang, Jianxiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a hematological malignancy with a high mortality rate and heterogeneous prognosis. Traditional risk stratification is based on the genetic classification in the 2022 guidelines of the European Leukemia Net. However, the risks of some patients remain unclear, and other prognostic assessment methods are required to improve the risk assessment of these patients. Apoptosis-related genes (ARGs) play critical roles in regulating the survival and drug resistance of AML cells. Therefore, we collected gene expression and clinical data from patients with AML from The Cancer Genome Atlas Acute Myeloid Leukemia (TCGA-LAML) datasets to develop a risk assessment model based on 5 ARGs. Using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator Cox regression (LASSO-Cox) model, we identified 5 key ARGs (DDIT4, HSP90B1, ENO1, SOD1, and SLC7A11) and constructed a 5-ARG prognostic model. Using this model, we successfully stratified patients in both TCGA-LAML training and independent external validation cohorts, with high-risk patients consistently exhibiting significantly poorer clinical outcomes. In addition, high-risk patients exhibited significant enrichment in pathways related to TP53 dysfunction, mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signaling activation, and pro-inflammatory responses, which were closely correlated with NPM1c-FLT3 co-mutations. Decitabine, sunitinib, and MK-1775 were identified as potential therapeutic agents. In summary, we established a 5-ARG prognostic model that may facilitate risk stratification and inform therapeutic decision-making in AML.

1. INTRODUCTION

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a hematological malignancy characterized by the massive proliferation and accumulation of undifferentiated and functionally heterogeneous leukemia cells in the bone marrow and other hematopoietic tissues. This pathological process results in the suppression of normal hematopoiesis and infiltration into extramedullary tissues and organs, including the liver, spleen, and lymph nodes. With an in-depth study of the molecular mechanisms and the emergence of targeted therapies, the 5-year survival rate of patients with AML has significantly improved. The 2022 guidelines of the European Leukemia Net (ELN) have refined the AML risk stratification system based on cytogenetic and molecular genetic abnormalities, providing a more scientific framework for risk assessment and precise treatment guidance for clinical diagnosis and treatment decisions.1 However, some patients with AML remain inadequately stratified by the ELN 2022 risk stratification system.2,3 Therefore, a new risk assessment model needs to be established, and novel markers with higher prognostic efficacy should be screened to achieve a more precise risk subgroup division for patients with AML and further improve their long-term survival prognosis.

In recent years, AML prediction models have been established based on m6A-related phagocytosis-related genes,4 ferroptosis- and cuproptosis-related genes,5 and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)-related genes,6 providing an important theoretical basis for future immunotherapy and drug selection of patients with AML. Studies indicate that BCL-2 family proteins7,8 regulate mitochondrial apoptosis, whereas phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase-protein kinase B-mechanistic target of rapamycin (PI3K-AKT-mTOR) pathway overactivation9 and TP53 mutations10 induce defective apoptosis and abnormal malignant cell proliferation, all of which are closely related to AML pathogenesis and treatment outcomes. However, to date, no systematic apoptosis-related prognostic models for AML have been reported. Apoptosis, as a programmed cell death process,11 is a key physiological mechanism that restricts excessive cell proliferation and maintains tissue homeostasis. Its role in tumor immune evasion and drug resistance warrants further in-depth exploration. Increasing evidence has shown that advanced-grade cancers with poor prognosis typically contain relatively high levels of cells in a spontaneous apoptotic state,12 which may have a critical impact on the overall net survival, expansion, and evolution of the tumor cell population.13–17 Moreover, the prognostic model of apoptosis-related genes (ARGs) has demonstrated a robust predictive performance in various malignancies, including diffuse large B-cell lymphoma,18 hepatocellular carcinoma,19,20 and breast cancer.21 Therefore, we hypothesized that ARGs could serve as novel prognostic markers for AML, potentially facilitating the development of effective combination therapeutic strategies for patients with high-risk AML.

This study focused on the effect of apoptosis on the prognosis of AML. Using The Cancer Genome Atlas Acute Myeloid Leukemia (TCGA-LAML) cohort as a foundation, we developed a 5-ARG prognostic model and validated it in an independent cohort. Risk scores (RS) were computed based on the 5-ARG prognostic model. Systematic analyses were performed to characterize the clinical, genomic, and transcriptomic features of the high-risk patients. Additionally, drug sensitivity was compared between high- and low-risk groups. Collectively, these findings provide novel insights for refining AML risk stratification.

2. METHODS

2.1. Data source

Gene expression profiles and clinical data of 179 patients with AML were acquired from the TCGA-LAML cohort using the UCSC Xena database (https://xenabrowser.net). Additionally, 2 independent datasets of patients with AML were used as external validation sets: the HOVON cohort22 (n = 608, non-M3) and our in-house cohort (n = 124). For the in-house cohort, RNA sequencing and analytical methods were as described in our previous article.23 Gene expression profiles and clinical information are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

2.2. Screening of ARGs in AML

Signaling pathways and gene sets associated with apoptosis were retrieved from the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) database. Based on the core annotation term “GO:0006915 (BP_Apoptotic_Process),” an ARG knowledge base comprising 1972 genes was constructed. Subsequently, we employed the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA3) web tool (https://gepia3.bioinfoliu.com/) and identified 666 significantly differentially expressed ARGs between the cohort of patients with AML and the healthy control group.

2.3. LASSO-Cox analysis

Univariate Cox regression analysis was conducted using the R package “survival” to identify 162 ARGs with regression coefficients that were significantly associated with overall survival (OS) (n = 169; p < 0.050) in TCGA-LAML. To refine the candidate gene set, we applied least absolute shrinkage and selection operator Cox regression (LASSO-Cox) (implemented via the R package “glmnet”), which narrowed the 162 OS-related ARGs down to 28 genes with robust prognostic relevance. A stepwise Cox regression was then conducted to further optimize this subset; 5 ARGs (DDIT4, HSP90B1, ENO1, SOD1, and SLC7A11), along with their respective regression coefficients, were identified, and a 5-ARG prognostic model for patients with AML was constructed. The 75th percentile (Q3) of RSs in the training cohort was used as the optimal cutoff value. Patients were stratified into high-risk (RS >Q3) and low-risk (RS ≤Q3) groups. Given that gene expression profiles were generated using different detection platforms and normalization methods across cohorts, the Q3 cutoff was independently determined in each cohort rather than by applying a single fixed threshold derived from the training set (details are provided in Table S1, https://links.lww.com/BS/A149).

2.4. Validation of the prognostic model

Multivariate Cox regression analysis was conducted using the “survival” package in R to include the age, sex, French-American-British (FAB) classification, white blood cell (WBC), hemoglobin (HB), platelet (PLT), and RS to evaluate the independent predictive value of each factor for the prognosis of the patients. In parallel, the diagnostic performance of the model was validated at the Q3 cutoff. We calculated time-dependent specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and sensitivity over the 1- to 5-year follow-up period to assess the predictive efficacy of the model for survival outcomes. Based on the results of the multivariate analysis, a nomogram was constructed using the R package “rms” to predict individualized 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS for patients with AML. To assess the robustness and generalizability of the prognostic model, we performed a 5-fold cross-validation. The cohort was randomized into 5 subsets, and the model was trained and validated iteratively across all folds. The area under the curve (AUC) was computed for each fold to quantify the discrimination.

Survival curves based on the 5-ARG RS were constructed using the “survival” package in R, and OS differences between the high- and low-risk groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals were calculated, and survival curves with confidence intervals were plotted using the “survminer” package in R. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS were calculated using the R package “timeROC” to evaluate prognostic performance.

2.5. Differentially expressed genes and enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the high-risk and low-risk groups were identified using the R package “DESeq2” (criteria: p < 0.050 and |log2FC| ≥1). Functional enrichment analyses, including GSEA, Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, and Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA), were performed using the “ClusterProfiler” and “GSVA” packages in R, based on the AML cell developmental trajectory data from a previous study.24

2.6. Drug sensitivity analysis

Data from AML cell lines in the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (https://sites.broadinstitute.org/ccle) database were utilized to compare drug sensitivity between high-risk and low-risk groups using the “oncoPredict” package in R, predicting potential chemotherapy agents for patients in the high-risk group.

2.7. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2, and result visualization was achieved with the R package “pheatmap” and the Hiplot platform (https://hiplot.com.cn). p < 0.050 was considered statistically significant.

3. RESULTS

3.1. Construction of a classification system based on five ARGs

The analysis of gene signatures related to ARGs was outlined using a systematic workflow, as depicted in Figure 1. To investigate the prognostic value of ARGs in patients with AML, we enrolled 169 AML cases and identified 666 ARGs (Table S2, https://links.lww.com/BS/A150). As shown in Figure 2A, univariate Cox analysis identified 162 ARGs that significantly influenced the prognosis of patients with AML (Fig. 2B). LASSO-Cox regression and stepwise Cox regression analyses identified 5 key prognostic genes (Fig. 2C), including DDIT4, HSP90B1, ENO1, SOD1, and SLC7A11 (collectively termed 5-ARG).

Patients in the TCGA-LAML cohort were categorized into high-risk (>Q3) and low-risk (<Q3) groups based on their 5-ARG RSs. To assess the prognostic performance of this 5-ARG prognostic model, survival time, survival status, and a heatmap of 5-ARG expression were visualized (Fig. 2D). These results indicate that higher 5-ARG RSs are correlated with poorer survival outcomes, including increased mortality and shorter survival times. Additionally, interaction networks among the genes are presented (Figure S1, https://links.lww.com/BS/A152), suggesting that the 5-ARG impacts tumor cell biology through complex molecular interactions.

3.2. Construction of the 5-ARG prognostic model

Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified the RS, age, WBC count, and HB as independent prognostic factors for AML survival, whereas PLT, sex, and some FAB subtypes showed no significant association (Fig. 3A). Model validation at the Q3 cutoff for the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival endpoints demonstrated distinct time-dependent performance characteristics (Fig. 3B). Based on these factors, a nomogram was established to predict the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates of patients with AML (Fig. 3C).

The prognostic model was evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation and showed a stable and reliable discriminative performance (Fig. 3D). ROC curve analysis demonstrated that the 5-gene prognostic model achieved an AUC of 0.748, which was superior to that of the clinical RF model (AUC = 0.637) (Figure S2, https://links.lww.com/BS/A152). The C-indices of the RS and combined models were 0.699 and 0.698, respectively, which were significantly higher than those of the ELN model (Figure S3, https://links.lww.com/BS/A152).

3.3. Validation and clinical-molecular characterization of the 5-ARG prognostic model

To verify the stability and generalizability of the 5-ARG prognostic model, we performed validation in the TCGA-LAML, HOVON, and in-house cohorts (Fig. 3E, p < .001; Fig. 3F, p = 0.029; Fig. 3G, p = 0.014). Time-dependent ROC analysis of the TCGA-LAML cohort further confirmed the robust predictive performance of the RSs (Figure S4, https://links.lww.com/BS/A152).

We analyzed the clinical features of the TCGA-LAML cohort stratified by the 5-ARG RS (Table S3, https://links.lww.com/BS/A151). Significantly higher RSs were observed in patients who died (p < .001, Fig. 4A) and older patients (≥60 years) (p = 0.002, Fig. 4B), while no significant difference was observed between sexes (p = 0.480, Fig. 4C). In the TCGA-LAML cohort, higher RSs were significantly enriched in M1, M5, M6, and M7 subtypes (Fig. 4D); in the HOVON cohort, higher RSs were enriched in M0, M1, and M5 subtypes (Figure S5, https://links.lww.com/BS/A152), with M1 and M5 as the overlapping subtypes. Among the 5-ARG high-risk patients, most fell into the ELN 2022 adverse category, whereas most low-risk patients fell into the favorable and intermediate categories (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, we analyzed the mutation profiles in the TCGA-LAML cohort stratified by the 5-ARG RS (Fig. 4F). Patients with IDH1 R132 and those with NPM1c-FLT3 co-mutations exhibited significantly higher RSs (Fig. 4G). We further validated the expression of the 5-ARG signature in single-cell transcriptomes of patients with NPM1-mutant AML (Fig. 4H).

3.4. Biological pathway characteristics of the 5-ARG high-risk group

To investigate the biological role of the high-risk group defined by the 5-ARG prognostic model in AML, we performed differential expression analysis between the high- and low-risk groups in the TCGA-LAML training set (Figure S6, https://links.lww.com/BS/A152), followed by GSEA, GO, KEGG, and GSVA analyses. GSEA results indicated that the high-risk group was significantly enriched in pathways including tumor necrosis factor-alpha signaling via the nuclear factor-κB pathway, interleukin-2/STAT5 signaling, hypoxia, interferon-gamma response, p53 pathway, and mTORC1 signaling (Fig. 5A–F).

(H) Heatmap of GSVA enrichment scores showing AML developmental arrest-related gene set distribution across 5-ARG risk groups. GSEA = Gene Set Enrichment Analysis, GSVA = Gene Set Variation Analysis, KEGG = Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, NES = normalized enrichment score.

The GO terms and KEGG pathway analysis indicated that the differentially expressed genes were enriched in regulation of myeloid cell differentiation, negative regulation of T-cell apoptotic process, intrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway in response to DNA damage, cellular response to oxidative stress, p53 signaling pathway, transforming growth factor-beta signaling pathway, signaling pathways regulating pluripotency of stem cells, and mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway (Fig. 5G, Figure S7, https://links.lww.com/BS/A152).

GSVA revealed that EoBasoMast-like and ProMono-like cells were significantly enriched in the low-risk group. In contrast, the high-risk group exhibited relatively higher enrichment of monocyte-like, LMPP-like, and early lymphoid-like cells, although the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 5H, Figure S8A–L, https://links.lww.com/BS/A152).

3.5. Immune cell infiltration characteristics of the 5-ARG high-risk group

To investigate the immune cell infiltration patterns associated with the 5-ARG signature in AML, we analyzed the composition of 22 immune cell subsets in the high- and low-risk groups (Figure S9A, https://links.lww.com/BS/A152). We further evaluated the correlation between 5 ARGs (DDIT4, HSP90B1, ENO1, SOD1, and SLC7A11) and immune cell infiltration (Figure S9B–K, https://links.lww.com/BS/A152). The results showed that the 5-ARGs were closely associated with distinct immune cell infiltration profiles. DDIT4, HSP90B1, ENO1, SOD1, and SLC7A11 exhibited specific correlations with multiple immune cell subsets, including T cells, macrophages, mast cells, and natural killer (NK) cells. Collectively, the 5-ARG prognostic model was closely linked to immune cell infiltration and remodeling of the immune microenvironment in AML.

4. DISCUSSION

The conventional risk stratification system for AML relies primarily on cytogenetic and molecular genetic abnormalities. Studies have indicated that approximately 7% of patients with myelodysplastic syndrome-related mutations and 8% of patients requiring reclassification cannot be accurately stratified.2 Therefore, the development of a novel risk assessment model can address the issue of unknown classification and facilitate more efficient risk stratification. Against this background, we constructed a 5-ARG (DDIT4, HSP90B1, ENO1, SOD1, and SLC7A11) prognostic model using the TCGA-LAML dataset. The model was developed using a combination of univariate Cox regression, LASSO regression, and stepwise regression analyses. The RS was calculated based on the correlation coefficients of these genes to classify patients with AML into high- and low-risk groups.

In the AML classification and risk stratification system, the FAB classification is mainly based on morphological features and does not fully incorporate molecular genetic information. Cytogenetic risk stratification focuses on chromosomal abnormalities and cannot capture all the minor genetic variations that influence prognosis. The ELN 2022 risk stratification system, based on risk assessments of genetic mutations, requires dynamic updates to align with advances in clinical practice. Similar to prognostic models based on m6A-related phagocytosis-related genes4 and ferroptosis- and cuproptosis-related genes,5 the 5-ARG prognostic model contributes to further refinement of AML risk stratification. Notably, the RS was significantly correlated with OS in patients with AML. Beyond prognostic prediction, this model provides insights into the biological characteristics of AML, identifies potential therapeutic targets for high-risk patients, and suggests candidate therapeutic agents, thereby offering additional theoretical support for clinical decision-making.

Genes in the 5-ARG prognostic model may serve as key regulators of AML progression. DDIT4 inhibits the mTORC1 signaling pathway,26–28 thereby modulating cell growth, proliferation, metabolism, and protein synthesis,29 which are crucial for cancer cell survival.30 Our previous study using the AML1-ETO9a (AE9a) mouse model confirmed that DDIT4 knockout leads to marked downregulation of the HOXA gene cluster and de-repression of the mTORC1 pathway, alongside impaired hypoxia and autophagy-related signaling. These changes abrogate leukemic stem cell stemness, reducing G0 phase quiescence, self-renewal, and chemoresistance of leukemic cells and ultimately suppressing in vivo leukemogenesis.28 Additionally, HSP90B1 is closely related to the malignant progression of AML, and its aberrant expression helps AML cells evade apoptosis, sustain proliferation, and tolerate oncogenic stress, which correlates with an unfavorable prognosis.31,32 ENO1 promotes AML cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by activating glycolysis-related signaling pathways, and its high expression is linked to poor clinical outcomes in patients with AML.33,34 SOD1 facilitates AML cell evasion of reactive oxygen species-mediated apoptosis and sustains malignant proliferation by enhancing cellular antioxidant capacity, which is closely associated with poor prognosis.35–37 SLC7A11 mediates malignant proliferation and therapeutic resistance in AML by helping leukemia cells resist oxidative stress and evade ferroptosis, and its aberrant overexpression correlates with poor prognosis.38,39 Together, these 5 core genes contribute to the malignant progression and poor prognosis of AML, supporting their potential as prognostic and therapeutic targets.

In this study, the 5 ARGs were regarded as independent prognostic markers to investigate the clinical characteristics of the high- and low-risk groups in the TCGA-LAML training set. We confirmed that OS and age were closely associated with the RSs derived from the 5-ARG prognostic model. Subsequent analyses demonstrated that the 5-ARG high-risk group was closely associated with AML-M1/M5 subtypes, poor differentiation, aggressive phenotypes, and good consistency with the ELN 2022 classification, while identifying additional high-risk patients. High 5-ARG RSs were significantly linked to NPM1c-FLT3 co-mutations and were further validated at the single-cell level. These results confirm that the 5-ARG signature reliably captures the core clinical and molecular features of adverse AML subtypes and provides stable, biologically meaningful risk stratification. Functional enrichment and immune infiltration analyses revealed that the 5-ARG high-risk group was characterized by aggressive clinical subtypes, adverse genetic mutations, disordered hematopoietic differentiation, dysregulated stress responses, and an immunosuppressive microenvironment, all of which contribute to the malignant progression and poor prognosis of AML. Finally, a drug sensitivity analysis identified decitabine, sunitinib, and MK-1775 as potential therapeutic agents for high-risk patients.

In conclusion, we systematically identified ARGs associated with AML prognosis and developed a 5-ARG prognostic model. This model provides a valuable theoretical foundation for refining AML risk stratification and formulating individualized clinical treatment strategies. While our model exhibits promising prognostic performance, this study has several limitations, including its retrospective design, lack of prospective validation, and potential population-specific bias attributable to the largely homogeneous ethnic composition of the TCGA cohort. To mitigate this bias and improve the generalizability of our findings, we performed external validation in a large-sample HOVON cohort and collected clinical data from 124 Asian patients to minimize the influence of ethnic heterogeneity. In the future, we will optimize the model by integrating key clinicopathological features such as age and specific gene mutations and validate the 5-ARG prognostic model in independent multicenter cohorts with large sample sizes to rigorously evaluate its predictive stability and generalizability. We will also investigate the molecular mechanisms by which these 5 ARGs regulate AML pathogenesis using single-cell sequencing and other advanced technologies, focusing on their roles in modulating cancer stem cell properties, immune microenvironment dysregulation, and drug resistance. Additionally, we plan to develop diagnostic kits based on these 5-ARGs to achieve precise patient stratification and personalized treatment guidance.
