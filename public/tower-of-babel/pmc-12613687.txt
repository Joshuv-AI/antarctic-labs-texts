Enhancing nursing ethics education: comparing the PAD model and traditional lecture-based learning
Authors: Heling Wen, Zheng Huang, Rui Zhang, Yifeng Jiang, Lei Peng, Yu Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGiven the widespread occurrence of ethical dilemmas and moral distress in healthcare, it is crucial for educators to strengthen nursing students’ ethical education. However, there is little published research on the best practices for teaching nursing ethics. This study aims to determine whether the Presentation-Assimilation-Discussion (PAD) mode, compared to traditional lecture-based learning (LBL) teaching, increases nursing students’ moral sensitivity and ethical decision-making skills.MethodsA total of 100 eligible nursing students were randomized into two groups: the PAD model group (n = 50) and the LBL group (n = 50). Their moral sensitivity and ethical decision-making skills were evaluated using the Moral Sensitivity Questionnaire-Revised Chinese Version (MSQ-R-CV) and the Chinese Version of Judgment About Nursing Decision (JAND-CV), both before and after the course, and again three months later.ResultsAfter the course, the PAD group showed significantly higher scores in moral sensitivity and ethical decision-making compared to the LBL group, both immediately and at the 3-month follow-up (p < 0.05). Specifically, the PAD group demonstrated greater improvements in total scores and in most subdimensions, including moral responsibility, moral burden, ethical choice, and ethical action. While both groups showed some degree of improvement after the intervention, the changes were more substantial and longer-lasting in the PAD group.ConclusionThis study confirms that the PAD model is an effective method for teaching nursing ethics and developing nursing students’ moral sensitivity and ethical decision-making skills.RegistrationThis randomized trial was registered in Chinese Clinical Registry Center, under code (ChiCTR2400086717) in 07/09/2024.Keywords: PAD, Nursing ethics, Teaching methods, Ethical decision-making skills, Moral sensitivity

Introduction

Nurses have evolved from mere assistants to physicians into comprehensive modern healthcare professionals, providing evidence-based treatment, health education, and compassionate care, with a significant impact on patient treatment outcomes and overall healthcare that should not be underestimated [1–3]. As society ages, healthcare systems adapt to shifting patient needs and evolving care delivery approaches, technology advances, resources become limited, and populations become increasingly diverse in terms of culture, religion, and public expectations of the healthcare system, ethical conflicts in clinical practice are on the rise [4]. The development of artificial intelligence, genetics and genomics, and neuroscience has made medical care more efficient, but it has also introduced more complex emerging ethical issues and decisions [5].

Ethically challenging situations are a complex concept, typically involving moral and ethical dilemmas encountered in nursing practice. These situations often refer to scenarios where nurses face complex ethical issues and are uncertain about what decisions or actions to take [6]. In recent years, as the healthcare model has evolved from a traditionally physician-centered approach to one that emphasizes patient-centered care, and as new medical challenges have emerged, nurses seem to be facing an increased number of ethically challenging situations [6]. The study found that ethically challenging adversely affects nurses both physically and psychologically, leading to increased job burnout, a higher tendency to leave the profession, compassion fatigue, elevated blood pressure, disrupted sleep, mental fatigue, emotional exhaustion, and ultimately a decline in the quality of nursing care [7–11].

Moral sensitivity refers to the ability to perceive and understand moral issues and values in various situations. It helps individuals respond to ethical challenges and serves as a prerequisite for ethical decision-making [12]. Previous studies suggest that nursing students may demonstrate relatively lower levels of moral sensitivity, which could be associated with factors such as developing clinical exposure, evolving professional judgment, the early stage of ethical knowledge acquisition, and the absence of a strongly supportive ethical climate in some clinical settings [13]. Academic ethics education has the potential to enhance students’ moral sensitivity and equip them to navigate ethical challenges in future clinical practice [14, 15]. Nursing educators are actively developing innovative teaching methods to enhance moral sensitivity in nursing education, particularly through visual-auditory-kinesthetic multimodal strategies and interactive teaching approaches, which have shown promising potential to outperform traditional teaching methods. These developments suggest that incorporating some evidence-based pedagogical innovations into ethics course reforms could potentially lead to meaningful improvements in moral sensitivity training for nursing students, although moral sensitivity is also shaped by clinical experience and the informal curriculum [16–18].

Moral sensitivity is positively correlated with ethical decision-making skills, as nurses with high moral sensitivity can identify and understand moral dilemmas more effectively, thus making ethical decisions more efficiently [19]. Nursing ethical decision-making skills, defined as nurses’ capability to make ethical decisions based on ethical knowledge, principles, and their own ethical qualities regarding ethical issues, is considered one of the core competencies nursing student should possess [20, 21]. Nursing students’ future clinical work will inevitably involve ethical dilemmas that reflect the inherent complexity of nursing values. Effective ethics education that improves ethical decision-making abilities can enhance their capacity for dialectical decision-making, enabling them to reconcile these conflicts while minimizing potential adverse effects on their well-being and professional identity [22–24].

High-quality ethics training should become an essential part of nursing education; however, there is still a significant gap between nursing ethics courses and clinical practice [25, 26]. Lecture-based learning (LBL) is a cost-effective method widely used in traditional nursing education to deliver standardized theoretical content to large student cohorts [27]. However, its passive nature limits student engagement and may be inadequate for developing the practical skills and critical thinking essential to nursing practice [28]. Therefore, traditional teaching methods may not sufficiently develop students’ ethical decision-making abilities to meet the actual needs of clinical practice [20].

In recent years, nursing educators have been exploring new teaching methods in nursing ethics, including online synchronous and asynchronous classes, the use of standardized patients, and case analysis. These new teaching modalities have played a positive role in promoting the ethical decision-making skills of students [29, 30]. However, overall, the depth and breadth of research in nursing ethics education remain insufficient. Educators need to further innovate curriculum models and link teaching objectives with measurable outcomes, while tracking longitudinal data of student cohorts trained under new teaching models is essential to enhance the level of nursing education [20, 27].

The Presentation-Assimilation-Discussion (PAD) Class Model employs a two-pronged teaching approach that divides classroom time equally between teacher-led presentations and student-focused assimilation and discussions [31]. This model emphasizes the critical role of instructors while boosting student responsibility and involvement, leading to increased engagement and enthusiasm [31, 32]. Recent studies have shown that the PAD model significantly enhances nursing students’ theoretical knowledge and practical skills in occupational blood exposure (OBE) education, while also demonstrating stronger retention of learning outcomes [33]. Another study on a surgical nursing course revealed that a blended learning approach combining Rain Classroom with the PAD model effectively improved students’ overall academic performance, self-directed learning ability, and self-efficacy [34]. Moreover, this blended mode significantly increased students’ satisfaction with the teaching methods, indicating the broad potential of the PAD model to enhance teaching quality and promote comprehensive student development. Additionally, research in the healthcare field has confirmed that the PAD model not only stimulates students’ enthusiasm for learning but also leads to improved educational outcomes compared to traditional lecture-based teaching methods [32]. However, it is still unclear whether this new educational method effectively promotes ethical education for nursing students. Therefore, we conducted a randomized controlled study aimed at exploring whether the PAD model, compared to traditional LBL methods, can better enhance moral sensitivity and improve ethical decision-making skills in nursing students.

Objective

This study comparatively evaluated the effects of the Presentation-Assimilation-Discussion (PAD) method and the traditional Lecture-Based Learning (LBL) method in ethics education on nursing students’ moral sensitivity and ethical decision-making skills. We tested the following hypothesis:

H1: The PAD method is more effective than the LBL method in cultivating nursing students’ moral sensitivity and enhancing their ethical decision-making skills.

H1: The PAD method is more effective than the LBL method in cultivating nursing students’ moral sensitivity and enhancing their ethical decision-making skills.

Methods

Study design

This randomized controlled trial study was conducted in a training and research hospital during the academic year 2024. The nursing students were divided into two groups: the experimental group, which used the PAD method, and the control group, which used the LBL method for nursing ethics education. The outcomes were measured using Moral Sensitivity Questionnaire (MSQ) and Judgment About Nursing Decision (JAND). Measurements were taken at three time points: before intervention, immediately after intervention, and three months after intervention (Fig. 1).

Sample size calculation

Prior to study initiation, sample size determination was based on preliminary pilot data and published evidence in the field [35]. Power analysis was performed using the total scores of the JAND as the primary outcome measure. Using PASS 2021 software (NCSS, LLC), the analysis was conducted with a two-tailed significance level (α) of 0.05 and a power (1-β) of 0.90. An effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.6 was adopted, assuming a mean difference (D₁) of 9 and a standard deviation (Sigma) of 15. An AR(1) correlation structure with a Rho of 0.7 was applied. The initial calculation yielded a requirement of 44 participants per group. Accounting for an anticipated 10% attrition rate, we inflated the calculated sample size accordingly and rounded up to 50 participants per group (total N = 100).

Participants

Inclusion criteria: Nursing students who are over 18 years old, have at least 3 months of clinical internship experience, and have not taken any ethics courses or ethics training in the past.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Students who missed more than one session in this study. (2) Students who, for various reasons, did not complete all the questionnaires.

Study process

This study recruited a total of 100 volunteer participants. SPSS 26.0 was used for random grouping. Randomization codes were generated and sealed in envelopes. Prior to the commencement of the study, each nursing student was assigned an envelope containing a randomization code. The enrolled nursing students were then randomly divided into two groups: 50 participants in the experimental group using the PAD method, while the other 50 participants in the control group using the traditional LBL method. Following grouping, all participants completed a researcher-designed demographic information questionnaire. The randomization procedure was conducted by a teacher not involved in the study. Only after the random grouping was completed, the researcher became aware of the group assignments.

The research team, based on the learning objectives of the course and the content of nursing ethics, integrated the core training content into four teaching modules: basic knowledge of nursing ethics, clinical practice ethics, research ethics, and ethical decision-making and evaluation. This was achieved after three rounds of discussions with five nursing ethics teachers, each with more than five years of teaching experience. The training course included eight sessions, each lasting 90 min, and was held twice a week for four consecutive weeks. Both groups received the same syllabus and reference materials one week before the training and were allowed to preview them at their own pace based on their individual needs. The entire course was taught by the same instructor.

The PAD method

The experimental group employed the PAD method, which consists of three main components. Below is a detailed explanation of the specific steps of the PAD method, using the clinical practice ethics module as an example.

Presentation (P)

The instructor used PowerPoint to briefly highlight the key basic concepts and theoretical frameworks of each session, allowing space for subsequent independent study and discussion by the students. In the clinical practice ethics module, the focus was on the characteristics of various clinical departments, ethical requirements, and potential ethical dilemmas. This phase aimed to encourage independent thinking and help students build a closed-loop learning system. This stage lasted approximately 40 min, followed by a 10-minute break.

Assimilation (A)

After the instructor’s presentation, each student was tasked with discovering and filling their knowledge gaps through self-study, combining textbook material and additional resources, and creating mind maps based on what they learned. Students were also encouraged to pose their own questions. Through this process, learning and thinking were integrated, promoting the internalization of knowledge. This step took about 15 min.

Discussion (D)

The discussion phase was divided into group and class discussions. In the group discussion phase, students formed groups of 4–6 members and discussed according to the following framework: (1) identify the ethical issue, (2) factors that may influence the ethical issue, (3) discuss possible solutions, (4) potential consequences of each solution, (5) determine the solution, and (6) how would you address the issue if you were the nurse? Each group then formed a consensus and prepared a report. In the class discussion phase, the instructor randomly selected a representative from each group to summarize the highlights and difficulties of their discussion. Following this, other group members or the instructor provided comments and evaluations. Each discussion phase lasted 10 min. Finally, in the last 5 min of the session, the instructor summarized the key points, highlighted areas for improvement, and praised groups that performed well in the discussion phase.

The LBL method

The control group utilized the LBL method. The instructor used PowerPoint to deliver the lecture content, independently analyzing the ethical issues, influencing factors, and solutions within clinical practice ethical dilemmas. Each lecture lasted 90 min, including a 10-minute mid-lecture break and a 5-minute question-and-answer session at the end.

Outcome measures

Moral Sensitivity Questionnaire (MSQ)

The moral sensitivity questionnaire-revised Chinese version (MSQ-R-CV) was used to evaluate the moral sensitivity of nursing students. Originally developed by Swedish scholar Lutzen [36], the MSQ was translated and culturally adapted into Chinese by Huang et al. in 2016 [37]. The MSQ-R-CV includes 2 dimensions and a total of 9 items: moral responsibility and strength (5 items), and moral burden (4 items). It uses a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree), with a total score ranging from 9 to 54. Higher scores indicate greater ethical sensitivity. The reliability and validity of MSQ-R-CV are good among Chinese nurses, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.820.

Judgment about nursing decision

The Chinese version of the Judgment About Nursing Decision (JAND-CV) was used to evaluate the ethical decision-making abilities of nursing students. This questionnaire was originally developed by Ketefian based on the ethical guidelines of the American Nurses Association [38]. Zhang et al. translated and culturally adapted the JAND into Chinese in 2016 [39]. The JAND-CV consists of 6 clinical decision-making scenarios, encompassing 2 dimensions: ethical choice and ethical action, with a total of 37 items. Ethical choice refers to “the actions a nurse should take under ideal circumstances without any limitations”. Ethical action refers to “the actions that nurses in your hospital are likely to take in reality due to various limiting factors”. The questionnaire uses a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with a total score ranging from 110 to 370. Higher scores indicate a greater ability in ethical decision-making. The JAND-CV has demonstrated good reliability and validity among Chinese nurses, with a test-retest reliability coefficient of 0.745.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the 2013 version of the Helsinki Declaration. This randomized controlled trial received approval from the Ethics Committee of the hospital. Before the study began, we explained the purpose and protocol of the study to all participants, who then reviewed and signed informed consent forms. Additionally, all participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences.

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Depending on the data distribution, the results were presented as mean ± standard deviations (SDs) or median values along with the interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were compared with the Chi-square test. Continuous variables with skewed distribution and ordinal variables were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. The scores of the two groups at different time points were evaluated through repeated measures analysis of variance. If a significant interaction effect was observed, the simple effects of the between-group and within-group factors were examined separately, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA), with all tests being two-tailed, and significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

A total of 100 nursing students were enrolled in this study and randomly divided into two equal groups. During the study, one participant in the PAD group withdrew due to a scheduling conflict with clinical practice, and two participants in the control group withdrew due to personal reasons. Thus, the final analysis included data from 49 participants in the PAD group and 48 participants in the LBL group. The distribution of demographic characteristics was balanced between the two groups, including age, gender, only child in family, area of residence, religious belief, life experience with loved ones facing serious illness, interested in being a nurse and their perception of the importance of nursing ethics education (p > 0.05). It is worth noting that the majority of participants (65% in the PAD group and 63% in the LBL group) believed that nursing ethics education is important (Table 1).

Effects of ethics education on moral sensitivity

The interaction between time and group was statistically significant for the total scale scores (F = 7.477, p = 0.001), moral responsibility and strength (F = 5.884, p = 0.003), and moral burden (F = 3.090, p = 0.048). This indicates that the total scores and the dimension scores of MSQ between the two groups exhibited different trends over time.

Prior to the intervention, there were no significant differences in total scale scores, moral responsibility and strength, or moral burden between the PAD and LBL groups (p > 0.05), indicating that the moral sensitivity between two groups was comparable. Immediately and 3 months after intervention, the PAD group’s total scale scores, moral responsibility and strength, and moral burden were significantly higher than those of the LBL group (p < 0.05). Overall, both groups showed varying degrees of improvement in total scale scores, moral responsibility and strength, and moral burden after intervention. And these improvements were maintained at the 3-month follow-up, except for moral burden in the LBL group (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Discussion

Nursing students appear to be particularly vulnerable to moral distress when faced with ethical dilemmas or decision-making in clinical practice, highlighting the need for further research on effective interventions to alleviate such distress [23]. Furthermore, Bordignon et al. conducted a survey of 499 nursing students and found a high frequency of moral distress among nursing students, with senior students experiencing a significantly higher intensity of moral distress [40]. Innovative ethics education for nursing students is of positive significance in improving their ability to handle moral distress. Our pioneering application of the PAD model in the ethics education of nursing students demonstrates that, compared to the traditional LBL method, the PAD model can significantly enhance nursing students’ moral sensitivity and ethical decision-making skills.

Recent studies have shown that most nursing students in clinical practice encounter moral distress, and there is a close association between the intensity and frequency of moral distress and moral sensitivity [41]. While the following findings were derived from studies involving registered nurses rather than students, they provide valuable insight into the potential role of moral sensitivity in mitigating moral distress. Specifically, moral sensitivity has a direct and significant negative effect on the intensity of moral distress, suggesting that higher moral sensitivity can help alleviate the degree of ethical dilemmas nurses experience [42]. The greater the moral sensitivity, the more capable an individual is at perceiving and responding to ethical challenges, thereby more effectively managing or avoiding situations that lead to moral distress [42]. Enhancing nurses’ moral sensitivity through training and education may contribute to alleviating moral distress [43]. Further evidence indicates that increased moral sensitivity helps prevent ethical conflicts from escalating into moral distress and supports a safer healthcare environment [44]. Additionally, by improving nurses’ ethical decision-making abilities, their capacity to handle moral dilemmas is strengthened, effectively reducing their moral distress and thus promoting higher quality nursing care [43].

Moral sensitivity in healthcare refers to the ability to perceive and evaluate ethical issues in clinical practice. It is a core component of ethical nursing practice, encompassing emotional acuteness, ethical knowledge, and the capability to act in morally appropriate ways [36]. Moral sensitivity is crucial for healthcare professionals as they often face complex decisions involving conflicting values and responsibilities that impact patient welfare [36]. Professional ethics education and curriculum optimization can effectively enhance the moral sensitivity of nursing students [45]. Yeom and colleagues’ research indicates that a traditional LBL approach, supplemented by group discussions, case analyses, and video reviews, has a significant impact on patient-oriented care, a sub-domain of moral sensitivity [46]. Another study shows that both standardized patient teaching and in-class case analyses can improve students’ moral sensitivity, with the standardized patient group showing a more significant increase [30]. Upon analyzing these aforementioned new teaching methods, in comparison with traditional teaching methods, they share some common characteristics: (1) They encourage active student participation in the learning process. Participatory teaching enhances students’ understanding and analytical abilities regarding ethical issues. (2) They provide contextual application: by offering scenarios that closely mimic real clinical situations or actual medical ethics cases for training. This helps students apply theoretical knowledge in practice, improving their clinical decision-making skills.

The Moral Sensitivity Questionnaire (MSQ) is an important tool for assessing an individual’s ability to recognize and understand moral issues and dilemmas in clinical practice. It is also used to measure the effectiveness of training programs in enhancing participants’ moral sensitivity. Because moral sensitivity is influenced by cultural values, we use the MSQ-R-CV in our study, which was adapted from the original Moral Sensitivity Questionnaire-Revised (MSQ-R) to ensure it is linguistically and culturally appropriate for Chinese-speaking nurses. The MSQ-R-CV assesses two main dimensions of moral sensitivity: moral responsibility and strength, and sense of moral burden. Multiple studies have applied the MSQ in the field of nursing ethics education to evaluate the impact of various teaching interventions on students’ moral sensitivity. For example, a Korean study using a one-group pretest-posttest design found that ethics education significantly enhanced nursing students’ moral sensitivity in the “patient-centered care” dimension and increased their curiosity in critical thinking [46]. A quasi-experimental study in Taiwan demonstrated that implementing diverse teaching strategies based on visual, auditory, and kinaesthetic learning styles effectively improved students’ moral sensitivity and was significantly associated with teaching satisfaction [16]. These findings support the feasibility and effectiveness of using the MSQ as an evaluation tool for the outcomes of ethics education. Our research shows that both immediately after the training and three months later, compared to the traditional LBL method, the PAD model can significantly improve total scale scores, moral responsibility and strength, and moral burden. In the LBL group, the moral burden scores decreased in the test conducted three months after the training. This suggests that students may overlook some moral issues or fail to recognize their importance, and they may lack sufficient vigilance or response capability when facing certain moral challenges.

Nursing ethical decision-making skills refers to the ability of nursing personnel to effectively assess, select, and implement the best action plans consistent with ethical principles and professional morals when faced with moral distress. There is a positive correlation between moral sensitivity and ethical decision-making, as moral sensitivity enhances nurses’ perceptual abilities in moral distress, helping them to more effectively identify and resolve ethical issues, leading to more reasonable ethical decisions [19].

The Judgment About Nursing Decision (JAND) is an important tool for assessing nurses’ decision-making abilities in ethical dilemmas. The Chinese version of the Judgment About Nursing Decision (JAND-CV) is adapted to the cultural and social context of China, making the assessment more accurate. It includes two dimensions: ethical choices and ethical actions. Ethical choices emphasize judgment and analytical abilities in the decision-making process, while ethical actions emphasize the ability to implement these decisions in actual nursing practice. The JAND was originally designed primarily to assess the moral judgment of clinical nurses when facing ethical situations. However, although our participants were nursing students who had only three months of clinical practice, they also need to engage in reflection and judgment on ethical dilemmas similar to those in real nursing contexts through methods such as scenario simulations and case analyses. A study using the JAND-CV showed that nursing ethics courses significantly improve nursing students’ ethical decision-making abilities, especially in ethical choice, while the ability to translate decisions into ethical actions still depends on ongoing clinical practice [39]. JAND, as a moral judgment tool grounded in specific clinical contexts, demonstrates a certain degree of applicability in educational settings. Clinical experience plays a critical role in the development of nursing students’ ethical decision-making abilities. The study highlighted that experiential knowledge, which is closely tied to work experience, enhances nurses’ intuitive capacity and thereby supports their ethical decisions, suggesting that greater clinical experience may contribute to more confident and effective ethical decision-making in practice [47]. Another study also found that nursing students often feel powerless when facing ethical dilemmas due to lack of experience and recommended that clinical practice be integrated into ethics education interventions to strengthen students’ ethical judgment and consistency between judgment and action in real-world situations [48]. Overall, these findings emphasize that incorporating clinical experience is essential for effectively cultivating ethical decision-making skills in nursing education. Since our participants were nursing students who had only three months of clinical practice, their moral judgments may lean more toward idealism due to the absence of real-world experience, which constitutes a limitation of this study. Future research should explore changes in ethical judgment during clinical internships and examine their relationship with students’ clinical experiences.

Through the PAD model, we can significantly enhance students’ total scale scores, ethical choices, and ethical actions scores at the completion of training as well as three months later. These scores are significantly higher than those of the LBL group. These results suggest that the PAD model is more effective than the traditional LBL method in significantly improving students’ ethical decision-making abilities. The ethical actions dimension in the LBL group did not show significant improvement after training, which may be due to the traditional LBL method’s focus on theoretical knowledge while lacking practical exercises and case analysis related to actual nursing situations.

The aforementioned advantage of the PAD model highlights the importance of a student-centered approach in teaching methods. Recent studies, consistent with our findings, indicate that student-led innovative teaching methods significantly enhance nursing ethical decision-making abilities. The PAD model is also an interactive teaching method incorporating inquiry-based elements. Its assimilation–discussion phases emphasize student initiative and collaboration, aligning with the core principles of inquiry-based learning that promote deep understanding. Students who receive inquiry-based education perform significantly better in ethical decision-making than those in the LBL group [49]. This is due to the inquiry-based approach’s emphasis on student-led learning processes, where the teacher acts as a guide or facilitator. This method encourages students to actively explore and resolve ethical issues through research, interviews, and field visits, allowing them to apply moral principles and critical thinking in real-world scenarios, thus enhancing their ethical decision-making skills [49]. In addition, nursing students underwent a specific “ethical decision-making reasoning scheme,” which is a more systematic approach to ethics education involving case studies, group discussions, and film-based explorations of ethical issues, leading to significant improvements in their ethical decision-making abilities and indicating their enhanced effectiveness in handling moral distress and decisions [50]. Case discussions and film discussions allowed students to apply ethical principles in real-world contexts and engage in critical thinking; group discussions and interactions helped students reason and judge in real-life moral distress, enhancing their decision-making skills [50]. Problem-Based Learning (PBL) is a teaching method that emphasizes active student participation in the learning process, allowing students to explore and solve problems through teamwork and discussion in real or simulated situations. Compared to traditional lecture-based learning (LBL), the PBL approach shows significant improvements in moral development, but it does not demonstrate significant effects in enhancing students’ ethical decision-making [51]. A possible reason is that the duration of the study course was too short to fully develop and improve students’ ethical decision-making abilities; moreover, effective PBL requires students to have a good foundation of prior knowledge and the ability to learn independently, which the students in the study may have lacked [51]. Therefore, in the design of our PAD model, we ensured that the course duration was sufficient. During the “Assimilation” phase, by integrating textbooks and consulting related materials for independent learning, and creating mind maps based on acquired knowledge, students were well-prepared for the “Discussion” phase. By comparing traditional teaching methods, our study results indicate that the PAD model significantly enhances students’ nursing ethical decision-making abilities.

Despite the demonstrated advantages of the PAD model in ethical education, we must acknowledge the limitations of this study. Firstly, the limited sample size may impact the generalizability of the results. Secondly, although nursing interns were included in the sample, their relatively short internship periods mean that the representativeness of the study results is somewhat limited. Thirdly, the evaluation of moral sensitivity and ethical decision-making in this study relied on self-reported data, which may introduce subjective bias. Future research could further validate the effectiveness of the PAD model in nursing ethics education by increasing the sample size and conducting studies across different nursing personnel. Fourthly, The LBL group may not fully reflect the more interactive lecture formats increasingly used in some educational settings. Future studies could adopt more interactive or hybrid lectures as control conditions to better assess the relative effectiveness of the PAD model. Finally, we recognize that, for two randomly assigned groups, it is difficult to completely prevent the exchange of information outside the classroom in real-world educational settings. Future studies may consider using cluster randomization to better control for this type of bias.

Conclusion

Our study introduces the PAD model’s application in ethics courses for nursing students, aiming to enhance their moral sensitivity and ethical decision-making skills. Through the implementation of this model, nursing students demonstrate increased moral sensitivity and ethical decision-making skills. However, further research is needed to address potential biases and to explore the applicability of the model to broader groups. This will help to validate the universality and effectiveness of the PAD model across different contexts and backgrounds.
