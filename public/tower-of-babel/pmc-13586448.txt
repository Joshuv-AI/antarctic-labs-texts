PD‐L1 in Ovarian Cancer: Immune Evasion, Tumor‐Intrinsic Signaling, and Chemoresistance
Authors: Zhiyang Xu, Xiaohui Lv, Yunping Wang, Yanjie Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEpithelial ovarian cancer remains a lethal malignancy, primarily due to late‐stage presentation and the frequent development of platinum resistance. While immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) targeting the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1)/programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1) axis has revolutionized oncology, its success in ovarian cancer has been strikingly limited. This review synthesizes emerging evidence that extends the role of PD‐L1 beyond canonical immune evasion to include tumor‐cell‐intrinsic mechanisms that may contribute to chemoresistance. We explore the “dual shield” function of PD‐L1, detailing how its non‐canonical, intracellular signaling promotes tumor cell survival, stemness, and therapy resistance. Mechanistically, preclinical studies indicate that PD‐L1 can modulate DNA damage responses and may enhance anti‐apoptotic signaling, rewire cellular metabolism, and regulate autophagy. Nuclear PD‐L1 has also been linked to DNA repair machinery in preclinical cancer models, suggesting a potential mechanism of resistance to DNA‐damaging therapy; however, direct mechanistic evidence in ovarian cancer remains limited and is still emerging. The tumor microenvironment further amplifies this axis through cytokine networks and physical barriers. These tumor‐intrinsic survival mechanisms may help explain why PD‐1/PD‐L1 blockade alone has shown limited efficacy in ovarian cancer. Overcoming this requires rational combination strategies that simultaneously disrupt PD‐L1‐mediated immune suppression and its cell‐autonomous pro‐survival functions. Overall, this dual‐function framework positions PD‐L1 as a multifunctional therapeutic node and provides a rationale for combination strategies targeting both immune escape and tumor‐intrinsic resistance mechanisms.

Introduction: The Ovarian Cancer Conundrum and the Unexplored Facets of PD‐L1

Epithelial ovarian cancer remains one of the most lethal gynecologic malignancies despite incremental therapeutic advances, largely because most patients present with advanced‐stage disease and relapse after first‐line treatment [1, 2]. Standard management still centers on cytoreductive surgery followed by chemotherapy, with carboplatin plus paclitaxel representing the principal first‐line cytotoxic backbone in advanced epithelial ovarian cancer [2, 3, 4]. For consistency throughout this review, the term “chemotherapy” is used as an umbrella term encompassing platinum‐based chemotherapy and taxane chemotherapy unless a specific drug or regimen is named because of its distinct mechanistic or clinical relevance. Outcomes are strongly influenced by the completeness of cytoreduction and initial platinum sensitivity; however, more than two thirds of patients recur and a substantial fraction eventually develop platinum resistance with poor survival [2, 3].

Platinum resistance, operationally defined by early relapse after platinum therapy, is clinically vexing because it reflects altered DNA damage responses, drug efflux, redox homeostasis, and tumor cell plasticity that blunt cytotoxic lethality and limit the durability of current regimens [5, 6]. Immune checkpoint blockade targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1)/programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1) has transformed outcomes in several malignancies by reinvigorating anti‐tumor T‐cell immunity. However, in ovarian cancer, single‐agent anti‐PD‐1 or anti‐PD‐L1 therapy has produced low response rates and has not delivered consistent survival benefit in randomized studies [7, 8, 9].

In the Phase II KEYNOTE‐100 study, pembrolizumab monotherapy produced an objective response rate of 8.0% among 376 patients with advanced recurrent ovarian cancer [8]. In the randomized Phase III JAVELIN Ovarian 200 trial, neither avelumab monotherapy nor avelumab plus pegylated liposomal doxorubicin significantly improved progression‐free survival or overall survival compared with pegylated liposomal doxorubicin alone in platinum‐resistant or platinum‐refractory disease [9]. More recently, the Phase III ENGOT‐ov65/KEYNOTE‐B96 trial showed that pembrolizumab plus weekly paclitaxel, with or without bevacizumab, significantly improved progression‐free survival and overall survival in patients with platinum‐resistant recurrent ovarian cancer whose tumors expressed PD‐L1 with a combined positive score (CPS) ≥ 1 [10]. Based on KEYNOTE‐B96, on February 10, 2026, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved pembrolizumab plus paclitaxel, with or without bevacizumab, for adults with platinum‐resistant epithelial ovarian, fallopian tube, or primary peritoneal carcinoma whose tumors express PD‐L1 with CPS ≥ 1 and who have received one or two prior systemic treatment regimens [11]. In contrast, the Phase III AGO‐OVAR 2.29/ENGOT‐ov34 trial found that adding atezolizumab to bevacizumab and nonplatinum chemotherapy did not significantly improve progression‐free survival or overall survival in recurrent ovarian cancer [12]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the benefit of chemo‐immunotherapy in ovarian cancer is not uniform but depends on the treatment backbone and biomarker‐defined patient selection.

To contextualize these clinical shortcomings, it is useful to revisit the canonical biology of the PD‐1/PD‐L1 axis. PD‐1 was originally identified as a gene induced during programmed cell death and later characterized as an inhibitory receptor on activated T cells, while PD‐L1 (initially described as B7‐H1) emerged as a broadly expressed ligand that suppresses T‐cell proliferation, cytokine production, and cytotoxicity after binding PD‐1, thereby establishing a dominant mechanism of adaptive immune resistance in the tumor microenvironment [7, 13, 14]. This receptor–ligand pairing has supported the development of monoclonal antibodies that block PD‐1/PD‐L1 interactions and thereby restore antitumor T‐cell function, a strategy that assumes immune evasion is the principal role of PD‐L1 in cancer. In ovarian cancer, however, checkpoint escape is layered: compensatory upregulation of PD‐1, lymphocyte‐activation gene 3 (LAG‐3), and cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte‐associated protein 4 (CTLA‐4) has been documented in metastatic disease, and recent reviews and early combination studies support CTLA‐4 blockade as part of a broader suppressive network rather than as a stand‐alone alternative pathway [14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. More broadly, PD‐L1 is not only a ligand for an inhibitory receptor but also a signaling‐competent molecule in tumor cells that can transmit reverse or non‐canonical signals independently of T‐cell engagement [5, 19].

In ovarian cancer, direct evidence now supports multiple tumor‐cell‐intrinsic functions of PD‐L1, including tumor‐initiating cell generation, proliferation, autophagy, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT)‐associated phenotypic plasticity, migration and invasion, and transcriptional reprogramming [20, 21, 22, 23]. In particular, genetic manipulation of PD‐L1 in ovarian cancer cells has demonstrated reciprocal regulation of canonical EMT markers and transcription factors, with PD‐L1 loss increasing E‐cadherin and reducing N‐cadherin, vimentin, Slug, Snail, and ZEB1, whereas PD‐L1 overexpression promotes the opposite, EMT‐like phenotype [20]. PD‐L1 has also been shown to promote ovarian‐cancer migration, invasion, and metastatic progression [21], while nuclear PD‐L1 can directly enhance transcription of IL‐8/CXCL8, Bcl3, and STAT1 in ovarian cancer cells [23]. By contrast, the detailed molecular mechanisms linking PD‐L1 to homologous‐recombination/non‐homologous‐end‐joining repair machinery and other DNA‐damage‐response processes remain more extensively defined in non‐ovarian models and should still be presented as mechanistic extrapolations where ovarian‐specific validation is lacking [6]. These non‐canonical functions have been attributed to intracellular PD‐L1 interactions with signal‐transduction machinery, regulation of mRNA stability for genes involved in homologous recombination and DNA repair, and modulation of pro‐survival transcriptional programs. In preclinical cancer models, intracellular PD‐L1 has been shown to stabilize mRNAs encoding DNA‐damage‐response proteins, including NBS1 and BRCA1, thereby increasing resistance to DNA damage; disruption of PD‐L1 can sensitize cancer cells to radiation or chemotherapy [6]. In ovarian cancer cells specifically, nuclear PD‐L1 has been shown to occupy the IL‐8/CXCL8, Bcl3, and STAT1 promoters, enhance their transcription, and support a pro‐survival phenotype; suppression of PD‐L1 increased apoptosis [23]. Separately, in a PD‐L1‐low, immune‐desert‐like murine ovarian tumor model, anti‐vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) treatment enabled antitumor activity of anti‐PD‐L1 therapy and increased intratumoral CD8+ T‐cell infiltration through CXCR3‐ligand induction [24]. These findings do not establish a validated PD‐L1‐high versus PD‐L1‐low chemoresistance taxonomy in ovarian cancer. Rather, they illustrate mechanistically distinct, context‐dependent biology, tumor‐cell‐intrinsic PD‐L1 signaling in some settings and VEGF‐associated immune exclusion in others, and suggest why PD‐L1 IHC alone may not capture the dominant resistance mechanism. This expanding conceptual framework helps reconcile two otherwise discordant observations: tumors with variable PD‐L1 expression may both suppress adaptive immunity and simultaneously acquire cell‐autonomous resistance traits that are not reversed by PD‐1 blockade alone, and therefore single‐agent checkpoint inhibitors may fail in ovarian cancer because they do not target these tumor‐intrinsic, PD‐L1–mediated survival circuits [19, 22].

Consequently, a more nuanced interrogation of PD‐L1 biology that integrates immune‐modulatory and cell‐intrinsic signaling is urgently needed to explain clinical resistance and inform rational combinations that can neutralize immunosuppression while dismantling the tumor's chemoresistance machinery [5, 25]. This review synthesizes contemporary clinical observations and mechanistic data to position PD‐L1 as a dual shield in ovarian cancer and to evaluate whether reverse PD‐L1 signaling is a targetable axis for overcoming platinum resistance and improving immune checkpoint strategies [19, 22, 26]. Literature search methodology and limitations: This narrative review was informed by targeted searches of PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science using combinations of the terms “ovarian cancer,” “PD‐L1,” “immune checkpoint blockade,” “chemoresistance,” “DNA damage response,” “autophagy,” and “cancer stem cells,” together with backward citation screening of relevant reviews and key primary studies. English‐language articles that were directly relevant to PD‐L1 biology in ovarian cancer were prioritized, while the final selection was made narratively rather than through a prespecified systematic protocol. This reporting approach is consistent with SANRA guidance for narrative reviews.

Beyond the Immune Synapse: The Non‐Canonical, Cell‐Autonomous Functions of PD‐L1

The Structure of PD‐L1: Hints at Dual Functionality

Epithelial ovarian cancer comprises several biologically distinct histotypes, including high‐grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC), low‐grade serous ovarian carcinoma (LGSOC), endometrioid, clear cell, mucinous, and carcinosarcoma [27]. Because HGSOC is the most prevalent aggressive histotype and has the most extensively characterized immune microenvironment, it provides the main clinical frame for this review; however, the available mechanistic evidence for tumor‐intrinsic PD‐L1 signaling in ovarian cancer is not uniformly histotype‐resolved. Clark et al. showed in an ovarian cancer model that tumor‐cell PD‐L1 regulates proliferation, mTOR activity, and autophagy [28], and Gao et al. subsequently reported that PD‐L1 promotes ovarian‐cancer cell proliferation and autophagy through AKT–mTORC signaling [29]. Thus, these studies support an ovarian‐cancer cell‐autonomous PD‐L1 program, but they should not be interpreted as HGSOC‐specific proof unless the experimental model is histotype‐defined. In HGSOC patient tissues, Webb et al. found that PD‐L1 expression was associated with tumor‐infiltrating T cells [30], while Jovanović et al. observed greater tumor‐cell PD‐L1 expression at invasive than central tumor regions specifically in HGSOC [31], consistent with spatially adaptive immune resistance. Mateiou et al. further showed that HGSOC displays a stronger immune response and multiple targetable checkpoints, whereas LGSOC was characterized by diffuse immune infiltration and higher stimulator of interferon genes (STING) expression [32]. In ovarian clear cell carcinoma (OCCC), Gao et al. documented clinically relevant but heterogeneous PD‐L1 expression using the 22C3 pharmDx assay [33], and Kristeleit et al. reported clinical activity of pembrolizumab in the Phase II PEACOCC trial, in which most participants had ovarian clear cell cancer [34]. More recently, Frank et al. confirmed histotype‐specific immune programs, identifying STING as a prominent target in LGSOC and intratumoral PD‐L1 as a prognostic marker in HGSOC [35]. Collectively, these studies justify using HGSOC as the principal clinical context while discussing OCCC and LGSOC separately rather than treating epithelial ovarian cancer as immunologically uniform.

Clinical experience similarly shows that the PD‐1/PD‐L1 axis is not uniformly effective across ovarian cancer. Matulonis et al. reported modest single‐agent pembrolizumab activity in KEYNOTE‐100 [8]. Pujade‐Lauraine et al. found that avelumab alone or with pegylated liposomal doxorubicin did not significantly improve progression‐free or overall survival in JAVELIN Ovarian 200 [9], and Monk et al. reported no progression‐free survival benefit for frontline avelumab‐based strategies in JAVELIN Ovarian 100 [36]. By contrast, Zamarin et al. observed a higher response rate and longer progression‐free survival with nivolumab plus ipilimumab than with nivolumab alone in recurrent or persistent ovarian cancer [37], while How et al. showed that pembrolizumab combined with frontline chemotherapy was feasible in a Phase II study, with a signal of greater benefit in tumors with higher PD‐L1 combined positive scores [38]. Chemotherapy can also alter PD‐L1 biology. Sheng et al. showed that cisplatin‐mediated downregulation of miR‐145 increases c‐Myc‐dependent PD‐L1 expression in cisplatin‐resistant ovarian carcinoma cells [39], whereas Zuo et al. found increased PD‐L1 in cisplatin‐resistant SKOV3 and A2780 derivatives and showed that PD‐L1 silencing reduced cisplatin resistance [40].

PD‐L1 is a Type I transmembrane B7‐family protein composed of extracellular IgV‐like and IgC‐like domains, a single transmembrane segment, and a short cytoplasmic tail. Structural work by Lin et al. showed that PD‐1 binds the IgV domain of PD‐L1 through an interface resembling antibody/T‐cell receptor variable‐domain interactions [14]. Unlike PD‐1, the PD‐L1 intracellular domain is only about 30 amino acids long and lacks a catalytic domain and classical ITIM/ITSM‐type signaling motifs, which initially argued against conventional receptor‐like signal transduction [19, 41]. However, Gato‐Cañas et al. identified conserved cytoplasmic motifs, including RMLDVEKC and DTSSK that regulate tumor‐cell resistance to interferon‐mediated cytotoxicity [42]. Lucas et al., working in dermal dendritic cells, independently showed that the TSS sequence within the PD‐L1 tail is required for chemokine‐dependent migration, ERK phosphorylation, and actin polymerization [43]. Together, these studies demonstrate that the short PD‐L1 tail can encode context‐dependent signaling information despite lacking classical receptor motifs.

Post‐translational regulation and intracellular trafficking further expand PD‐L1 function. N‐linked glycosylation contributes to PD‐L1 stability and influences its detection and turnover [44]; separately, Burr et al. showed that CMTM6 binds PD‐L1 and preserves its cell‐surface expression by limiting lysosomal degradation [45]. Orme et al. demonstrated that ADAM10 and ADAM17 cleave surface PD‐L1 to generate soluble PD‐L1 [46]; this shedding pathway should be distinguished from nuclear trafficking. Du et al. showed in non‐small cell lung cancer that KPNB1 interacts with PD‐L1 and facilitates its nuclear import, promoting Gas6/MerTK‐dependent tumor‐cell proliferation [47]. Shu et al. subsequently reported that radiation‐associated PD‐L1 deglycosylation promotes nuclear translocation and interaction with Ku‐containing DNA‐repair machinery, accelerating double‐strand‐break repair [48]. In ovarian cancer, Huang et al. recently showed that PD‐L1 is predominantly intracellular in patient tumors and that disrupting intracellular PD‐L1 in PEO1 and ID8Brca1−/− models impairs tumor growth and increases DNA damage [49]. These findings provide ovarian‐specific support for the biological relevance of non‐surface PD‐L1.

Taken together, PD‐L1 structure is compatible with dual functionality rather than being paradoxical. Its extracellular IgV domain mediates PD‐1 binding, whereas its short cytoplasmic tail contains conserved regulatory motifs, and post‐translational modification plus regulated trafficking can alter PD‐L1 localization and function [14, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48, 49]. For ovarian cancer, the most defensible conclusion is that cell‐autonomous PD‐L1 signaling is supported by ovarian models and emerging patient data, while the precise molecular circuitry remains incompletely resolved at the histotype level [28, 29, 49]. Accordingly, HGSOC will remain the principal clinical context in the following sections, with OCCC and LGSOC treated as biologically distinct comparators.

Mechanisms of PD‐L1‐Driven Intracellular Signaling

Within ovarian cancer, directly demonstrated cell‐autonomous effects of PD‐L1 include proliferation, autophagy, stemness, EMT‐associated phenotypic plasticity, migration and invasion, and chemotherapy resistance [20, 21, 22]. Importantly, interferon‐γ (IFNγ) is the principal upstream inducer of PD‐L1 in ovarian cancer cells, acting through IFNγ/JAK1/STAT1/IRF1 signaling and an IFNγ–Bcl3 axis to upregulate PD‐L1 [50, 51, 52]. By contrast, PD‐1‐dependent reverse signaling, KPNB1‐mediated nuclear import, and several pathway‐rewiring events are supported mainly by non‐ovarian models and should be presented as extrapolations when ovarian data are absent. In HGSOC, the functional output of this network is further shaped by tumor context, including NRF2/KEAP1 activity, stromal composition, and prior chemotherapy exposure, which can shift PD‐L1 distribution between tumor cells and the immune microenvironment and help explain interpatient heterogeneity in PD‐L1 levels and treatment response [19, 41, 48, 53]. The first mode, which we term the inside‐out signal, occurs when PD‐L1 on the cancer cell surface is ligated by PD‐1 on an interacting lymphocyte and transmits a reverse message into the tumor cell. Several independent experimental systems outside ovarian cancer, including dermal dendritic cells and colorectal cancer, have shown that PD‐L1 ligation can activate ERK and mTOR and reduce sensitivity to cytotoxic stress [54, 55, 56]. These observations provide a plausible mechanism for ovarian tumor cells, but they should be described as inferences from non‐ovarian models unless ovarian‐specific validation is available [41, 57].

The second mode is more precisely described as tumor‐cell‐intrinsic PD‐L1 signaling, rather than as an “inside‐in” signal, and should be distinguished from the PD‐1‐ligation‐dependent reverse signaling described above. In ovarian cancer models, genetic attenuation of tumor‐cell PD‐L1 reduced proliferation and altered mTORC1 and autophagy programs; notably, in human ES2 ovarian cancer cells, reduction of PD‐L1 decreased mTORC1 signaling while increasing autophagic flux, supporting cell‐autonomous PD‐L1–mTOR crosstalk but not establishing a direct physical interaction between the PD‐L1 cytoplasmic tail and AKT or mTOR [28]. Gupta et al. further demonstrated that tumor‐cell‐intrinsic PD‐L1 promotes tumor‐initiating cell generation and canonical stemness programs in both murine ID8agg and human ES2 ovarian cancer cells, providing ovarian‐specific evidence for a downstream stemness phenotype without defining the proximal PD‐L1 signaling complex [22]. Direct sequence‐level evidence that the short PD‐L1 cytoplasmic domain itself can transmit intracellular signals instead comes principally from non‐ovarian systems; Gato‐Cañas et al. identified conserved RMLDVEKC and DTSSK motifs within the PD‐L1 intracellular domain that regulate tumor‐cell resistance to interferon‐mediated cytotoxicity [42]. Additional non‐ovarian models demonstrate pathway‐specific forms of this intrinsic signaling: PD‐L1 promotes mTORC1 signaling and autophagy‐dependent chemoresistance in bladder cancer [55], whereas intracellular PD‐L1 can interact with RAS‐family proteins and activate RAS–MEK–ERK signaling in glioblastoma and colorectal cancer models [58, 59]. Thus, these studies identify plausible PD‐L1‐associated signaling modules that warrant testing in ovarian cancer, but they should not be interpreted as evidence that the PD‐L1 cytoplasmic tail has already been shown to directly activate both PI3K–AKT–mTOR and RAS–MAPK pathways in ovarian cancer [28, 55, 59, 60]. For example, bladder cancer cells with elevated PD‐L1 exhibit increased mTOR activity and autophagy flux that support chemoresistance, and manipulation of PD‐L1 levels correspondingly alters downstream phosphorylation of S6 and other mTOR substrates [55]. Likewise, experimental modulation of PD‐L1 alters ERK signaling and stemness programs in models of melanoma and ovarian carcinoma, supporting a model in which PD‐L1 tunes canonical oncogenic pathways indirectly through scaffolded complexes or membrane proximal signaling platforms [19, 22].

Finally, nuclear PD‐L1 represents a qualitatively distinct axis of cell‐autonomous function. The strongest mechanistic demonstrations of PD‐L1 nuclear import and Ku‐associated DNA repair come from pan‐cancer or non‐ovarian systems, including NSCLC, whereas ovarian cancer currently provides emerging correlative and functional support rather than definitive mechanistic proof [47, 48]. This distinction should be made explicit whenever nuclear PD‐L1 is discussed. Beyond repair, nuclear PD‐L1 has been associated with altered transcriptional programs that upregulate survival genes and chemoresistance mediators, and clinical correlative studies link higher nuclear PD‐L1 with recurrence and poor outcome in several tumor types, including emerging evidence in ovarian cancer [48, 61, 62].

NRF2 status further stratifies the PD‐1/PD‐L1 axis in HGSOC. Both NRF2High and NRF2Low tumors contain PD‐1/PD‐L1‐positive compartments, but their distribution differs according to NRF2 status: bulk RNA‐seq showed no significant difference in CD274/PD‐L1 between the two groups, whereas PDCD1/PD‐1 was significantly higher in NRF2High tumors; tissue‐microarray analysis further demonstrated enrichment of both PD‐1 and PD‐L1 within the immune microenvironment of NRF2High compared with NRF2Low tumors, emphasizing the importance of spatial context when interpreting checkpoint expression [53]. Preliminary HGSOC OVCAR8 data also suggest NRF2‐dependent modulation of PD‐L1 by chemotherapy: paclitaxel increased PD‐L1 in NRF2High cells but decreased it in NRF2Low cells, whereas carboplatin reduced PD‐L1, indicating that NRF2 status may influence treatment‐induced remodeling of the PD‐1/PD‐L1 axis [63]. Mechanistically, it remains unresolved whether a discrete PD‐L1 intracellular cleavage product enters the nucleus and functions as a classical transcriptional cofactor, or whether full‐length or membrane‐derived PD‐L1 traffics to the nucleoplasm with partner proteins. Candidate mechanisms include ADAM‐mediated ectodomain shedding, gamma‐secretase or presenilin pathway processing, deglycosylation, and importin‐mediated transport, each supported by fragmentary primary data and by analogies to other membrane receptors whose intracellular domains become signaling entities [46, 48, 58].

In sum, current primary evidence supports a model in which PD‐L1 functions as a dual shield. Extracellularly it engages PD‐1 to blunt immune attack, and intracellularly it interfaces with core survival networks either by transducing signals following ligation, by modulating RAS–MAPK and PI3K–AKT–mTOR axes through ICD‐dependent scaffolding, or by reprogramming nuclear processes that directly enforce DNA repair and chemoresistance. Critical gaps remain, most importantly rigorous biochemical mapping of ICD interactors in clinically relevant ovarian cancer models and definitive demonstration of ICD cleavage products acting as transcriptional regulators. Prioritizing those experiments will be essential if PD‐L1 biology is to be therapeutically decoupled from its immune checkpoint function [19, 48, 55]. Molecular Crosstalk: Deconstructing the PD‐L1/Chemoresistance Axis

The DNA Damage Response (DDR) Alliance

Platinum agents generate covalent DNA lesions and replication‐associated stress that engage the DNA damage response, but the evidence linking this response to PD‐L1 induction should be distinguished from the separate biological effects of the taxane component of ovarian cancer chemotherapy. In a mechanistic study across human cancer‐cell models, Sato et al. demonstrated that DNA double‐strand break signaling increased PD‐L1 expression through an ATM/ATR/Chk1‐dependent pathway; importantly, cisplatin also increased PD‐L1, whereas the non‐DNA‐damaging agent paclitaxel did not produce comparable PD‐L1 induction in this experimental system [69]. In ovarian cancer specifically, Sheng et al. showed that cisplatin‐associated suppression of miR‐145 increased c‐Myc‐dependent PD‐L1 expression in cisplatin‐resistant ovarian carcinoma cells [39]. Complementarily, Zuo et al. found increased PD‐L1 expression in cisplatin‐resistant SKOV3 and A2780 ovarian cancer cells and showed that PD‐L1 silencing reduced cisplatin resistance and increased apoptotic responses [40]. Clinically, these mechanisms should be interpreted within the contemporary first‐line ovarian cancer setting of carboplatin plus paclitaxel, a doublet established as less toxic and non‐inferior to cisplatin plus paclitaxel in the pivotal Gynecologic Oncology Group Phase III trial [70]. However, the two agents should not be mechanistically conflated: the platinum component is the principal inducer of DNA lesions and DDR signaling, whereas paclitaxel acts primarily through microtubule disruption and can independently induce immunogenic cell death in ovarian cancer through TLR4/IKK2/SNARE‐dependent exocytosis [71]. Thus, current evidence supports a mechanistic DDR‐to‐PD‐L1 axis and an ovarian‐specific association between PD‐L1 and cisplatin resistance, while direct demonstration that carboplatin–paclitaxel induces tumor‐cell PD‐L1 specifically through DDR signaling in patients with ovarian cancer remains limited. Mechanistically, the magnitude of this response is influenced by DNA‐repair competence: Sato et al. showed that depletion of homologous‐recombination factors such as BRCA2 or PALB2 further enhanced DNA‐damage‐induced PD‐L1 expression, linking defective repair to amplified checkpoint signaling [69]. Clinically and experimentally, platinum exposure has been observed to increase tumor and microenvironmental PD‐L1 staining, a phenomenon reproducible across tumor types and consistent with checkpoint activation after genotoxic therapy [40, 72]. The magnitude of PD‐L1 induction after platinum is shaped by p53 and repair competence: p53 deficiency and HR defects often shift cells toward replication catastrophe or unrepaired resection and thereby amplify ATR–Chk1 signaling and PD‐L1 expression, whereas intact p53 can blunt some of those responses [69, 73].

Beyond transcriptional upregulation at the membrane, a paradigm shift has emerged in which PD‐L1 acquires nuclear functions that directly interface with DNA repair machinery. Deglycosylation and chaperone‐regulated trafficking permit PD‐L1 nuclear translocation after genotoxic stress, where PD‐L1 physically associates with core repair complexes. In ovarian cancer, nuclear PD‐L1 has emerged as a plausible chemoresistance mechanism, although additional functional validation in ovarian cancer‐specific models is required. Shu and colleagues demonstrated that nuclear PD‐L1 binds Ku and augments Ku recruitment to double‐strand‐break ends, accelerating nonhomologous end joining and radioprotection [48]. Complementary studies reported that tumor‐intrinsic PD‐L1 promotes BRCA1 nuclear foci formation and enhances DNA end resection and homologous recombination efficiency, establishing a broader role for PD‐L1 in promoting high‐fidelity repair when advantageous to the tumor [41, 74]. Recent reports further propose that nuclear PD‐L1 can activate ATR–Chk1 signaling from within the nucleus, creating reciprocal reinforcement of checkpoint signaling and repair factor stability [66].

Taken together, these data support a working model in which chemotherapy produces DNA damage that activates ATR–Chk1 and allied inflammatory transcriptional programs to increase PD‐L1; PD‐L1 then traffics to the nucleus where it stabilizes or recruits DDR effectors, fortifying homologous recombination and end‐joining and thereby diminishing subsequent platinum cytotoxicity. In ovarian cancer, PD‐L1‐linked resistance can be summarized mechanistically as follows: (1) DDR reinforcement: nuclear PD‐L1 enhances repair competence and tolerance to DNA damage; (2) pro‐survival signaling: PD‐L1 can support PI3K–AKT–mTOR and MAPK programs that raise the apoptotic threshold; (3) metabolic adaptation: PD‐L1‐associated metabolic rewiring promotes glycolysis and redox buffering; (4) autophagy support: PD‐L1‐linked autophagy can preserve cell viability under chemotherapy; and (5) stemness enrichment: PD‐L1‐high cells can retain tumor‐initiating and relapse‐seeding features [41, 48, 69, 73, 75].

Inflammatory and redox‐responsive pathways provide an additional upstream layer of CD274 regulation in ovarian cancer. Chemotherapy can induce PD‐L1 through NF‐κB activation, thereby creating local immune suppression [76]. BCL3 provides a complementary NF‐κB‐linked input by promoting PD‐L1 transcription and ovarian‐cancer cell proliferation [52]. IFNγ independently induces PD‐L1 through JAK1/STAT1/IRF1 signaling [77]. The oxidative‐stress arm is more complex: NRF2 contributes to platinum resistance in ovarian cancer through redox and ferroptosis‐defense programs, including the NRF2/GPX4 axis [78]. However, the relationship between NRF2 and PD‐L1 is not a simple positive axis; a recent CRISPR screen that included OVCAR4, CaOV3, and SKOV3 ovarian cancer cells found that KEAP1 loss and NRF2 activation reduced IFNγ‐induced PD‐L1 transcription. Accordingly, future studies should perturb KEAP1 and NRF2 independently in chemosensitive and resistant ovarian models and quantify membrane, cytoplasmic, and nuclear PD‐L1 under basal, IFNγ‐stimulated, and chemotherapy‐treated conditions [79].

Co‐Opting the Anti‐Apoptotic Machinery

Chemoresistant ovarian carcinoma models frequently retain high levels of anti‐apoptotic BCL‐2 family proteins, especially Bcl‐xL and MCL‐1, and dual targeting of these proteins sensitizes cisplatin‐resistant cells to apoptosis [80, 81, 82]. Clinical and preclinical correlations have linked PD‐L1 positivity with higher BCL‐xL and related prosurvival signatures, and multiple mechanistic studies now argue that tumor‐intrinsic PD‐L1 engages intracellular signaling to raise the apoptotic threshold [83, 84]. One proximate route is PD‐L1–driven activation of PI3K–AKT signaling, a connection shown in several tumor contexts in which PD‐L1 overexpression increases p‐AKT and downstream mTOR activity [65, 85]. Canonical AKT outputs include phosphorylation and inactivation of the pro‐apoptotic BH3 protein Bad and indirect stabilization or synthesis of anti‐apoptotic family members, mechanisms long established in apoptosis biology [86, 87].

AKT activation may stabilize MCL‐1 and may increase BCL‐xL levels through both transcriptional and posttranslational effects, thereby neutralizing mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and blunting platinum‐triggered caspase cascades [88, 89]. Tumor‐intrinsic PD‐L1 can activate PI3K/AKT signaling. Downstream, AKT phosphorylates and inactivates Bad and supports MCL‐1‐dependent survival, thereby lowering apoptosis after cisplatin‐induced DNA damage [22, 28, 85, 86, 88, 90]. Importantly, this axis creates synergy with chemotherapy: the apoptotic pressure imposed by platinum selects for clones with heightened PD‐L1/AKT activity and elevated BCL‐2 family expression, while those same survival programs limit immunogenic cell death and the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade [90, 91].

Therapeutically this implies that combinations pairing BCL‐2 family inhibitors or PI3K/AKT pathway antagonists with PD‐L1 blockade or platinum could convert cytostatic survival programs into vulnerability, but the clinical window will depend on tumor lineage and the redundancy among anti‐apoptotic paralogs [92, 93].

The Metabolic Symbiosis of PD‐L1 and Chemoresistance

PD‐L1 expression is linked to a metabolic phenotype that favors aerobic glycolysis and the molecular machinery of the Warburg effect, consistent with primary studies showing that HK2‐dependent glycolysis can induce PD‐L1 expression and immune evasion [94]. Overexpression of PD‐L1 drives AKT–mTOR activation, elevates HIF‐1α protein and transcriptional activity, and increases expression of glycolytic enzymes and transporters in several tumor models [64, 65]. The metabolic consequences of this rewiring include accelerated glucose uptake, augmented lactate production, and creation of an acidic microenvironment that suppresses cytotoxic lymphocytes and fosters therapeutic tolerance [95, 96].

From a chemoresistance perspective, a glycolytic program provides multiple survival advantages against platinum. First, glycolysis feeds the pentose phosphate pathway and generates reducing equivalents that buffer platinum‐induced oxidative stress and support nucleotide synthesis for repair [97, 98, 99]. Second, HIF‐1α‐driven transcription promotes expression of autophagy and stress response genes that help clear damaged organelles produced by chemotherapy [100, 101]. Third, lactate and acidosis remodel immune infiltration and cytokine gradients, compounding PD‐L1‐mediated immune suppression and creating a metabolically privileged niche for resistant clones [102, 103]. These mechanistic intersections position PD‐L1 as a nodal regulator that couples immune evasion to metabolic adaptation. Consequently, targeting metabolic nodes such as HK2 or the LDH/lactate axis in combination with PD‐L1 or platinum may re‐sensitize subsets of ovarian tumors because HK2‐dependent glycolysis can promote PD‐L1 expression, LDHA‐linked glycolysis can upregulate PD‐L1 under glycolytic stress, and ovarian‐cancer data show that LDHB‐mediated histone lactylation can activate PD‐L1 and promote immune escape; however, tumor metabolic plasticity and the observed shifts toward oxidative or fatty acid metabolism in some resistant models warn that single‐agent metabolic targeting will often be insufficient [94, 104, 105, 106, 107].

The Autophagy Connection: A Double‐Edged Sword

Autophagy should be considered a dynamic lysosome‐dependent process rather than a static increase in autophagosome‐associated proteins. In ovarian cancer, cisplatin exposure can induce cytoprotective autophagy, and experimental studies have linked increased autophagic flux and preserved lysosomal function to cisplatin resistance, whereas pharmacological or genetic disruption of autophagy can restore platinum sensitivity [108, 109]. These observations are important because the relationship between PD‐L1 and autophagy is bidirectional but is not adequately described by a simple model in which PD‐L1 invariably stimulates autophagy. In the ovarian ID8agg model and human ES2 ovarian cancer cells, Clark et al. showed that reduction of tumor‐cell PD‐L1 increased LC3 conversion and autophagic activity, whereas PD‐L1 supported basal mTORC1 signaling, indicating that tumor‐intrinsic PD‐L1 can restrain rather than promote basal autophagy in ovarian cancer [28]. A subsequent report proposed that PD‐L1 increased BECN1 expression and autophagy in ovarian cancer; however, that publication now carries an Expression of Concern and should therefore not be used as the principal evidence for a PD‐L1‐driven pro‐autophagic mechanism. Accordingly, the direction of PD‐L1/autophagy crosstalk should be regarded as tumor‐, stress‐, and treatment‐context dependent rather than uniformly feed‐forward.

For readers unfamiliar with autophagy assays, the individual markers also require explicit interpretation. LC3‐II is the phosphatidylethanolamine‐conjugated form of LC3 that becomes associated with autophagosomal membranes; therefore, an increase in LC3‐II indicates greater autophagosome abundance but does not, by itself, distinguish increased autophagosome formation from impaired autophagosome clearance [110]. p62/SQSTM1 is a selective‐autophagy cargo receptor that binds ubiquitinated cargo and LC3 and is commonly degraded together with its cargo after delivery to autolysosomes; consequently, decreasing p62 can support productive autophagic degradation, whereas p62 accumulation may indicate defective downstream clearance. However, p62 is also an active signaling scaffold and can be transcriptionally regulated, so its abundance cannot be interpreted as an isolated flux marker. This limitation is particularly relevant in ovarian cancer because p62 itself contributes to cisplatin resistance through stress‐response pathways such as KEAP1‐NRF2 signaling [111]. LAMP1 and LAMP2, in contrast, are lysosomal membrane proteins that provide information about the late lysosomal compartment; LAMP2 has a demonstrated role in autophagosome‐lysosome fusion, and colocalization of LC3‐positive structures with LAMP1/2‐positive lysosomes can therefore provide evidence that autophagosomes are progressing toward autolysosomal degradation [112]. Nevertheless, LAMP1/2 abundance alone should also not be equated with autophagic flux.

The autophagy‐PD‐L1 relationship is reciprocal at the level of PD‐L1 turnover as well. Outside ovarian cancer, inhibition of autophagy has been shown to cause p62/SQSTM1 accumulation, NF‐κB activation, and increased PD‐L1 expression in gastric cancer [113], whereas p62 can, under other conditions, bind PD‐L1 and direct it toward autophagic‐lysosomal degradation [114]. These apparently opposing observations illustrate why future platinum‐treated ovarian cancer studies should measure dynamic autophagic flux rather than static marker abundance. A practical experimental framework would combine LC3‐II turnover measured in the presence and absence of a late‐stage lysosomal inhibitor such as bafilomycin A1, p62/SQSTM1 turnover, LC3‐LAMP1/2 colocalization or a tandem fluorescent LC3 reporter where experimentally feasible, and simultaneous assessment of membrane, cytoplasmic, and nuclear PD‐L1 [110]. Operationally, increased LC3‐II accompanied by p62 loss and a further rise in LC3‐II after lysosomal blockade is more consistent with productive flux, whereas accumulation of both LC3‐II and p62 with little additional LC3‐II accumulation after lysosomal inhibition is more consistent with a downstream clearance defect. Thus, LC3‐II, p62/SQSTM1, LAMP1/2, and PD‐L1 localization are best interpreted as an integrated autophagy‐PD‐L1 state and flux framework, rather than as interchangeable or isolated biomarkers.

This reciprocity may create a feed‐forward survival loop under chemotherapy: platinum elicits autophagy that temporarily manages proteotoxic stress and, by modulating PD‐L1 stability, shapes both immune visibility and intrinsic PD‐L1 signaling. Conversely, PD‐L1 signaling can potentiate autophagic flux to clear cisplatin‐damaged organelles and preserve viability [100, 115]. In a recent ovarian‐cancer cohort, high PD‐L1 co‐occurred with p62 and LC3 expression, and this pattern was most evident in HGSOC and advanced FIGO stages, supporting the idea that the PD‐L1/autophagy axis is enriched in biologically aggressive HGSOC [116]. Clinically, the double‐edged nature of autophagy argues against simplistic combinations; instead, rational pairing of autophagy modulators with PD‐L1 or DDR inhibitors should be guided by biomarkers of autophagic dependency and by assays that measure PD‐L1 turnover kinetics [117].

The Stem Cell Niche: Fortifying the Root of Resistance

A final and unifying theme is that PD‐L1 may mark and help sustain cancer stem cell states that seed recurrence after chemotherapy. This is supported by ovarian TIC/stemness studies showing that PD‐L1 promotes tumor‐initiating cell generation and that PD‐L1 is associated with CSC markers in ovarian tumors [22, 118]. In ovarian cancer, PD‐L1 expression correlates with CSC markers such as CD44, LGR5, and ALDH1 and with sphere‐forming and tumor‐initiating capacity [118, 119]. Ovarian‐specific mechanistic data show that tumor‐intrinsic PD‐L1 promotes tumor‐initiating cell generation in murine and human ovarian cancer cells, while ovarian spheroids show PI3K‐AKT dependence for stemness and chemoresistance; in parallel, microenvironmental cues such as MDSC‐derived PGE2 increase stem cell‐like properties and PD‐L1 expression in epithelial ovarian cancer [22, 120, 121, 122, 123].

The implication for chemotherapy is stark: platinum may eliminate the bulk while selecting for PD‐L1‐high CSCs that are both immune evasive and intrinsically chemoresistant because of consolidated DDR, anti‐apoptotic, metabolic, and autophagic defenses [41, 124]. Hence, strategies that combine PD‐L1 pathway blockade with agents that target stemness regulators, STAT3, or CHK1 may be uniquely capable of depleting the reservoir that drives relapse. Early preclinical studies combining CHK1 inhibitors with PD‐L1 blockade report promising attenuation of stemness features and sensitization to chemotherapy in ovarian models, but careful translation will require biomarkers that identify PD‐L1‐dependent CSCs [125].

Taken together, these five modules, DNA damage response, anti‐apoptotic signaling, metabolic reprogramming, autophagy, and CSC maintenance, suggest that PD‐L1 functions as a multifunctional hub rather than a single immune checkpoint. Because these pathways reinforce one another, targeting only one arm is unlikely to eliminate the resistant clone population. The most rational strategy is therefore combined inhibition of PD‐L1 intrinsic functions together with DDR‐, survival‐, or metabolism‐oriented partners, guided by biomarkers that reflect nuclear PD‐L1 activity, AKT/MCL‐1 dependence, glycolytic flux, autophagic turnover, and CSC abundance. The Tumor Microenvironment (TME) as an Amplifying Circuit

The Cytokine Milieu

The cytokine landscape of ovarian cancer is not merely a passive backdrop but may act as an active amplifier that couples immune checkpoint induction to canonical survival pathways, and the triad of interleukin 6, interleukin 10 and vascular endothelial growth factor is central to this coupling. These upstream controls complement the IL‐6/STAT3 axis: NF‐κB/Bcl3 and JAK1/STAT1/IRF1 also regulate PD‐L1 expression in OC cells, so the cytokine milieu should be described as one of several converging inputs that set PD‐L1 abundance [52, 77]. In addition to IFNγ released by activated lymphocytes and other immune cells in the TME, PD‐L1 expression in ovarian cancer cells can also be induced by IFNγ generated in response to chemotherapy, radiation, or immune checkpoint blockade, creating a feedback loop that reinforces adaptive immune escape [23, 51]. In ovarian cancer, this cytokine/chemokine program is further shaped by NF‐κB‐, AP‐1‐ and STAT3‐centered transcriptional networks that integrate inflammatory and stromal cues into persistent chemokine production [139, 140, 141]. IL‐6 produced by TAMs, CAFs and tumor cells activates JAK–STAT3 signaling in both malignant and stromal compartments and directly promotes PD‐L1 transcription and surface expression through STAT3‐dependent binding to the PD‐L1 promoter, thereby linking paracrine inflammation with immune checkpoint upregulation [142, 143]. This IL‐6 → STAT3 axis is also pleiotropically pro‐survival: it upregulates anti‐apoptotic factors and drug‐resistance programs including BCL2 family members and drug efflux proteins, which biologically unites immune evasion and chemoresistance within the same signaling node [144, 145].

Concurrently, IL‐10, classically produced by M2‐like TAMs abundant in ovarian tumors, reinforces immunosuppression and may stabilize PD‐L1 expression on both myeloid and tumor cells by blunting antigen‐presenting cell maturation and fostering regulatory programs that preserve checkpoint expression [146, 147]. Importantly, experimental models show that IL‐10 can sustain PD‐L1–dependent tolerance even when PD‐1 blockade perturbs other inhibitory axes, implying that IL‐10 and PD‐L1 operate in parallel, partially non‐redundant circuits that preserve tumor cell survival after immune perturbation [147, 148].

Further, in ovarian cancer, particularly in serous and HGSOC, VEGF upregulation is closely linked to PD‐L1‐associated immune escape. In ovarian serous carcinoma, PD‐L1 and VEGF were co‐overexpressed in biologically aggressive tumors [149], while an independent study of cisplatin‐resistant epithelial ovarian cancer demonstrated a significant positive correlation between PD‐L1 and VEGF expression (r = 0.363, p < 0.001) [150]. These observations provide an ovarian‐cancer‐specific rationale for combining PD‐L1 blockade with antiangiogenic therapy. Consistent with this concept, atezolizumab plus bevacizumab produced greater suppression of cisplatin‐resistant ovarian tumor growth than either treatment alone, and bevacizumab simultaneously reduced STAT3 phosphorylation and PD‐L1 expression in A2780cis cells [150]. Furthermore, in the PD‐L1‐low, immune‐desert‐like OV2944‐HM‐1 ovarian cancer model, VEGF blockade restored sensitivity to anti‐PD‐L1 treatment and increased intratumoral CD8+ T‐cell accumulation together with CXCR3‐ligand expression [24].

Mechanistically, VEGF contributes to immune exclusion in HGSOC in addition to its canonical angiogenic function. High VEGF expression is associated with increased myeloid‐derived suppressor cell (MDSC) infiltration, whereas MDSC abundance is inversely associated with intratumoral CD8+ T‐cell infiltration; experimentally, tumor‐cell VEGF‐A depletion reduces MDSC accumulation while increasing CD8+ T‐cell infiltration [151]. Thus, VEGF can reinforce PD‐L1‐mediated immune escape through at least two complementary mechanisms: establishment of an immunosuppressive myeloid compartment that restricts cytotoxic T‐cell access and STAT3‐associated maintenance of tumor‐cell PD‐L1 expression [150, 151].

The cellular components of this cytokine network are likewise interconnected. TAM–ovarian cancer cell interactions can increase PD‐L1, VEGF, IL‐6, IL‐10, and STAT3‐associated programs and reduce carboplatin sensitivity, while CAF‐derived IL‐6 activates JAK2/STAT3 signaling, promotes epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal plasticity, and increases taxane resistance. These observations indicate that angiogenic, myeloid, and stromal signals converge on partially overlapping PD‐L1‐ and STAT3‐associated resistance programs rather than operating as independent pathways.

From a translational perspective, these data help explain why PD‐1/PD‐L1 blockade alone may be insufficient in an angiogenic and cytokine‐rich ovarian tumor microenvironment. They support biomarker‐guided combinations in which PD‐1/PD‐L1 inhibition is paired with VEGF/angiogenesis blockade, or with selected cytokine/STAT3‐directed interventions when those pathways are demonstrably active, rather than implying that PD‐L1, IL‐6, IL‐10, and VEGF must necessarily be inhibited simultaneously [24, 150, 151].

The Physical Niche

The physical microenvironment in ovarian cancer is an active contributor to immune evasion and therapy failure because the extracellular matrix (ECM) and ascitic milieu create both mechanical and biochemical conditions that increase PD‐L1 and impair drug efficacy. Dense, collagen‐rich ECM and elevated matrix stiffness transduce mechanosignals through YAP/TAZ and related mechanotransduction pathways, and experimental studies show that increased matrix stiffness drives PD‐L1 expression via YAP‐dependent transcriptional programs in carcinoma cells, thereby converting a biophysical cue into an immune‐suppressive phenotype. Beyond stiffness, the ECM acts as a diffusion barrier that quantitatively limits penetration of macromolecular chemotherapeutics and nanomedicines, increases interstitial pressure and produces hypoxic microdomains that further induce HIF‐1α‐dependent PD‐L1 upregulation on tumor and myeloid cells. These combined barriers may foster regions of tumor cells that are shielded from cytotoxic concentrations of platinum‐taxane chemotherapy, including carboplatin and paclitaxel, and that simultaneously enjoy a local immunosuppressive cloak mediated by upregulated PD‐L1 [152, 153].

Beyond stiffness, the ECM acts as a diffusion barrier that quantitatively limits penetration of macromolecular chemotherapeutics and nanomedicines, increases interstitial pressure and produces hypoxic microdomains that further induce HIF‐1α–dependent PD‐L1 upregulation on tumor and myeloid cells [154, 155]. These combined barriers foster pockets of tumor cells that are effectively shielded from cytotoxic concentrations of chemotherapy while simultaneously enjoying a local immunosuppressive milieu mediated by upregulated PD‐L1 [156, 157].

Malignant ascites amplifies this protection by providing a fluid compartment rich in cytokines, growth factors, metabolites, and cellular aggregates that sustain survival signaling and immune escape. Ascitic fluid contains IL‐6, IL‐10, and VEGF at concentrations that reprogram tumor cells toward chemoresistance and elevate PD‐L1, and cell–cell contacts within ascites spheroids further potentiate these effects compared with monolayer culture [158, 159]. In vivo and ex vivo experiments have established that tumor cells exposed to ascites upregulate PD‐L1 and acquire drug efflux and anti‐apoptotic phenotypes, thereby directly linking the acellular ascites milieu to both immune suppression and reduced chemosensitivity [156, 160].

Finally, the ascites–ECM partnership establishes a microanatomy in which physical exclusion of effector T cells, hypoxia‐driven HIF signaling, mechanotransduction and cytokine signaling converge to stabilize PD‐L1 at multiple levels: transcriptional, post‐transcriptional and via reduced protein turnover in stressed, hypoxic niches [153, 161]. Therefore, therapeutic strategies that ignore the physical niche will leave intact the non‐redundant mechanisms that sustain PD‐L1 expression and chemoresistance, and the most promising translational avenues will combine checkpoint inhibition with agents that normalize ECM, abrogate ascites‐derived cytokine signaling or target hypoxia and mechanotransduction [162, 163].

Therapeutic Targeting: From Sequential to Rational Combinations

Limitations of Current Approaches

Multiple large randomized trials that tested the straightforward concept of adding anti–PD‐1/PD‐L1 immune checkpoint blockade to first‐line chemotherapy in unselected ovarian cancer populations have been disappointing, with JAVELIN Ovarian 100 stopped for futility and IMagyn050 failing to show a clinically meaningful progression‐free survival benefit in the intention‐to‐treat cohorts [36, 164]. These negative outcomes reflect a confluence of biologic and trial‐design problems rather than a simple failure of checkpoint biology in ovarian cancer. Biomarker analyses from those same randomized programs reveal that high tumor mutation burden is exceedingly rare in newly diagnosed epithelial ovarian cancer and homologous recombination deficiency and BRCA alterations, while prognostic for PARP inhibitor sensitivity, do not uniformly predict benefit from adding PD‐L1 blockade, blunting the a priori expectation that chemoradiation‐like neoantigen generation would sensitize tumors broadly to immunotherapy [165, 166]. Mechanistically, chemotherapy and anti‐PD‐L1 agents engage opposing short‐term effects: cytotoxic agents can transiently deplete effector lymphocytes or induce myeloid immunosuppression in certain spatial niches, and chemotherapy‐induced wound‐healing programs (including recruitment of MDSCs and TAMs) can in some contexts reinforce PD‐L1‐driven immune escape rather than convert cold tumors to hot ones [167, 168]. Trial‐level heterogeneity in timing, backbone agents, and lack of prospective biomarker enrichment have therefore made many Phase III combinations underpowered to detect a true, biologically restricted signal [165, 169]. Finally, newer data emphasize that PD‐L1 biology in ovarian cancer is complex: a substantial fraction of tumor PD‐L1 is intracellular or post‐translationally modified, which may render conventional IHC scoring a poor proxy for the targetable membrane PD‐L1 fraction and contributes to apparent discordance between PD‐L1 expression and clinical response [49, 67].

Novel Therapeutic Strategies Informed by Crosstalk

Translational and early clinical studies now support a move away from empiric triple‐drug addition toward biology‐driven vertical and horizontal combination strategies that address the crosstalk between PD‐L1 signaling, DNA damage responses, apoptotic priming, metabolism, autophagy, and stemness. Vertical Blockade

Combining PD‐1/PD‐L1 inhibitors with PARP inhibitors is based on a putative DDR‐immune axis because PARP inhibition both activates innate immune sensing via cytosolic DNA and cGAS–STING signaling and paradoxically upregulates tumor PD‐L1 expression, creating a mechanistic rationale for combined blockade [126, 127, 170, 171]. Ovarian‐specific early clinical studies support this strategy, including niraparib plus pembrolizumab in recurrent platinum‐resistant ovarian carcinoma and olaparib plus durvalumab in recurrent ovarian cancer, with activity enriched in biomarker‐defined subgroups such as BRCA‐mutant or HRD‐positive disease [172, 173, 174, 175]. These observations argue for biomarker‐first trial designs in which HRD/BRCA status, tumor immune microenvironment, and readouts of innate sensing govern patient selection and sequencing [165, 166].

Apoptotic Priming

The anti‐apoptotic BCL‐2 family is a core node of chemoresistance, and BH3‐mimetics such as venetoclax can substantially increase mitochondrial priming and sensitize cells to cytotoxic and immune effector–mediated killing [131, 176]. Preclinical models demonstrate that BH3‐mimetics can remodel tumor immunogenicity and NK/T‐cell function, and early clinical work in hematologic malignancies supports the tolerability of venetoclax with immune agents; however, translation into solid tumors, including ovarian cancer, must overcome lineage‐specific resistance mechanisms such as compensatory MCL‐1 upregulation and the risk of clinically meaningful myelosuppression, infection, and tumor lysis syndrome if dosing is not optimized [177, 178]. Safety considerations should accompany these combinations. PARP inhibitors, particularly niraparib, are associated with hematologic toxicity, including thrombocytopenia, anemia, and neutropenia, and niraparib labeling also warns about bone marrow suppression, rare MDS/AML, and non‐infectious pneumonitis [53]. PD‐1/PD‐L1 inhibitors add immune‐mediated pneumonitis, colitis, hepatitis, endocrinopathies, and nephritis, which can arise during treatment or after discontinuation and therefore require serial liver, renal, and thyroid monitoring [179]. In ovarian combination studies, the most common grade ≥ 3 toxicities with niraparib plus pembrolizumab and olaparib plus durvalumab were hematologic, especially anemia and thrombocytopenia, indicating that marrow reserve and schedule selection remain central to safe use [149, 180]. Venetoclax‐based BH3‐mimetic strategies bring additional risks of neutropenia, infections, thrombocytopenia, and tumor lysis syndrome, so ramp‐up dosing and laboratory surveillance are essential when they are layered onto PARP inhibition or other myelosuppressive partners [181, 182]. Rational combinations in ovarian cancer therefore require dynamic BH3 profiling, co‐targeting of MCL‐1 where indicated, and dosing schemas that preserve effector lymphocyte pools while maximally priming tumor mitochondria [183].

Metabolic Disruption

Metabolic reprogramming and hypoxia strongly modulate PD‐L1 expression and immune suppression; HIF‐1α has been reported to drive PD‐L1 transcription in hypoxic niches, while N‐linked glycosylation stabilizes PD‐L1 and supports its immunosuppressive function [67, 132, 161]. Preclinical data show that agents such as 2‐deoxy‐D‐glucose can disrupt N‐glycosylation of PD‐L1 and reverse PARP inhibitor‐induced PD‐L1 stabilization, thereby restoring immune susceptibility [133]. Combining checkpoint blockade with targeted metabolic inhibitors or hypoxia‐modulators is therefore conceptually attractive, but careful pharmacodynamic endpoints are required because metabolic drugs affect both tumor cells and immune effectors [184, 185].

Autophagy Inhibition

Constitutive autophagy is a cytoprotective escape used by ovarian cancer cells to survive chemotherapy and immune attack. Preclinical work shows that autophagy inhibition can enhance antigen release and dendritic cell cross‐presentation and rescue deficient immunogenic cell death, while ovarian‐specific early clinical data now include a randomized Phase II trial of hydroxychloroquine with chemotherapy in platinum‐sensitive relapsed ovarian cancer [137, 167, 186]. The clinical experience to date highlights two lessons: hydroxychloroquine is a blunt tool with limited potency and off‐target effects at the doses safely achievable, and next‐generation autophagy inhibitors with defined pharmacology will be needed to test the concept robustly in combination with PD‐L1 blockade [136, 137, 187].

Stem Cell Eradication

A subset of ovarian tumor cells with stemness markers (e.g., CD44, LGR5, ALDH1) co‐express PD‐L1 and are enriched after therapy, representing a cellular reservoir for relapse and immune escape [119, 188]. Targeting PD‐L1+ cancer stem cells with antibody‐drug conjugates remains an emerging concept; in ovarian cancer, the strongest evidence is still preclinical, including a PD‐L1‐targeted antibody‐polymer‐epirubicin conjugate that prolonged survival in a murine model of advanced ovarian cancer [138, 189]. ADC approaches could therefore complement ICB by directly eradicating the cellular substrate of chemoresistance while preserving or even augmenting antitumor immunity. The Timing and Sequence Conundrum

Whether combinations should be delivered concurrently, sequentially, or as maintenance remains unresolved and is likely context dependent. Frontline concurrent chemo‐ICB regimens failed in trials that enrolled largely unselected patients, suggesting that concurrent addition is not uniformly effective and may sometimes be antagonistic because chemotherapy can transiently blunt adaptive immunity and create suppressive myeloid niches [36, 168]. Conversely, biological work on PARP inhibitors shows that DNA damage can activate innate immune sensing and increase PD‐L1 but that the net effect on immune control is time sensitive: early PARP‐induced STING activation may prime a window of heightened immune susceptibility that is best exploited by near‐concurrent PD‐L1 blockade, whereas later PARP‐driven adaptive PD‐L1 upregulation or intracellular sequestration may necessitate sequential strategies or combination with deglycosylation/metabolic modulators [49, 127]. For apoptotic priming or autophagy blockade, preclinical data favor a priming phase in which tumor cell susceptibility is increased prior to checkpoint engagement, but clinical translation must balance priming intensity against preservation of effector cells [131, 167]. Finally, ADCs that directly eliminate PD‐L1+ cells may function best as induction or consolidation agents to remove resistant clones, followed by maintenance ICB to sustain immune surveillance [190]. Pragmatically, the field should move toward platform trials that embed longitudinal tumor and immune sampling, dynamic pharmacodynamic biomarkers (including membrane versus intracellular PD‐L1, STING activation signatures, metabolic readouts, and BH3 profiling), and adaptive randomization so that timing questions are resolved in biomarker‐defined cohorts rather than by repeating underpowered empiric combinations [165, 173].

Biomarkers and Patient Stratification: Beyond PD‐L1 IHC

The routine use of PD‐L1 immunohistochemistry as a solitary biomarker in ovarian cancer has proven insufficient because staining metrics are variably reported, poorly standardized across assays, and incompletely reflective of the multifactorial mechanisms that govern both immune responsiveness and chemoresistance [33, 191]. Clinical experience and meta‐analyses show that PD‐L1 tumor proportion scores or combined positive scores correlate inconsistently with objective responses to immune checkpoint blockade in ovarian carcinoma, leaving many patients misclassified by IHC alone [180, 192]. A central limitation of PD‐L1 IHC is that it resolves only static protein localization on a single slide and therefore cannot capture dynamic tumor cell intrinsic programs such as homologous recombination deficiency, pro‐survival signaling, autophagy activation, or the biochemical composition of malignant ascites that together determine therapeutic vulnerability [193, 194]. Whole‐genome sequencing (WGS) can complement IHC by defining the tumor's genomic architecture and revealing pathway‐level oncogenic alterations, especially when paired with transcriptomic profiling and immune‐context analysis. WGS is increasingly used in cancer to understand driver alterations and regulatory changes, while transcriptome–proteome studies show that mRNA abundance does not always predict protein abundance, so mRNA‐based markers should not be treated as interchangeable with protein readouts [195, 196, 197, 198].

In HGSOC, NRF2/KEAP1 status may complement PD‐L1‐based stratification because NRF2High tumors show higher PDCD1/PD‐1 in bulk analysis and greater PD‐1/PD‐L1 enrichment within the immune microenvironment than NRF2Low tumors, underscoring the need to interpret checkpoint expression according to both NRF2 status and spatial compartment [53]. A combined readout of NRF2, PD‐L1 spatial localization, CD68, CD3E, and CD80 may therefore be more informative than PD‐L1 IHC alone for selecting patients for immunotherapy or combination immunotherapy [53].

To move beyond this reductionist paradigm we propose a clinically actionable Resistance Pathway Signature that integrates orthogonal data types to stratify patients according to both immune and chemotherapy resistance biology [179, 199]. First, genomic features should include a validated HRD score and high‐confidence BRCA1/2 mutational status because HRD adjudicates DNA repair competence, predicts platinum and PARP inhibitor sensitivity, and therefore provides an essential axis for predicting whether tumor‐intrinsic repair defects will dominate therapeutic response [194, 200].

Second, proteomic nodes that reflect survival signaling and stress responses such as phosphorylated AKT, anti‐apoptotic BCL‐2 family protein levels, and autophagy markers including LC3B supply near‐term readouts of pathways known to mediate platinum tolerance and to interact functionally with PD‐L1 signaling [193, 201, 202]. Phospho‐AKT enrichment has been repeatedly associated with poorer outcomes and therapy resistance in ovarian tumors, making it a mechanistic biomarker candidate for combination strategies targeting the PI3K–AKT axis alongside immune modulation [202, 203]. Elevated expression of BCL‐2 and related anti‐apoptotic proteins confers apoptotic checkpoint escape in ovarian cells and predicts sensitization to BH3 mimetics when combined with DNA damaging agents, thereby providing a rational proteomic component to the signature [130, 204]. Autophagy is increasingly implicated in chemotherapy persistence and PD‐L1‐associated tumor‐cell survival programs, but LC3B abundance alone should not be treated as a direct measure of autophagic flux. In experimental samples, the preferred approach is to quantify LC3‐II turnover in the presence and absence of lysosomal inhibition together with p62/SQSTM1 turnover and, where feasible, LC3‐LAMP1/2 colocalization or tandem fluorescent LC3 reporters [110]. In fixed clinical specimens, where pharmacological flux assays cannot be performed, LC3B, p62/SQSTM1, and LAMP1/2 should instead be interpreted as a multiparametric autophagy‐state signature and integrated with PD‐L1 localization and treatment history.

Third, cellular and spatial metrics that map the topology of PD‐L1–positive tumor cells relative to cytotoxic T lymphocytes, regulatory cells, and tumor‐associated macrophages add critical context because therapies that depend on reinvigorating local T cell function will only succeed if spatial architecture permits effective cell–cell interactions [32, 142]. Multiplex immunohistochemistry and digital spatial profiling approaches have revealed discrete tumor immune microenvironment phenotypes in ovarian carcinoma that correlate with relapse patterns and could reclassify tumors that are PD‐L1 positive by IHC but spatially immune‐excluded and therefore unlikely to respond to checkpoint monotherapy [205, 206].

Fourth, soluble and extracellular compartments provide noninvasive windows into systemic and peritoneal resistance biology because circulating soluble PD‐L1, exosomal PD‐L1, and ascites cytokine milieus modulate distant immune suppression and reflect tumor secretion programs that are invisible to tissue IHC [207, 208]. Soluble PD‐L1 levels in plasma and ascites have been associated with adverse prognosis in ovarian cancer and may better capture the burden of immunosuppressive ligand available to systemically blunt T cell function than a single tumor biopsy [208, 209]. Comprehensive profiling of ascites cytokines and proteomes yields additional predictive information because ascitic fluid contains tumor spheroids, immune cells, and secreted factors that dictate peritoneal dissemination, chemo‐protective niches, and local immunomodulation [158, 210].

Integration of these modalities into a composite Resistance Pathway Signature requires rigorous multi‐omics methods and algorithmic translation, and recent studies demonstrate the feasibility of combining genomic HRD metrics, proteomic phosphorylation states, spatial cell phenotyping, and soluble biomarkers to derive clinically useful subtypes in ovarian cancer [179, 211]. For practical implementation, the signature should be deployed in a tiered workflow rather than as a single all‐in‐one assay. A core panel can be run in routine molecular pathology laboratories using HRD/BRCA testing and plasma or ascites‐based soluble biomarkers, while phospho‐proteomic and LC3B measurements are centralized in a reference laboratory, and spatial profiling is reserved for cases in which tissue architecture is likely to alter treatment selection. This makes the signature more feasible for multicenter use, reduces cost, and preserves the biologic breadth needed to capture immune‐escape and chemoresistance phenotypes [179, 205, 207, 212, 213, 214]. Machine learning frameworks trained on curated cohorts can identify non‐linear interactions among the genomic, proteomic, spatial, and soluble features and can generate probability scores that are more predictive of progression‐free survival and treatment response than any single biomarker alone [179, 215]. For translation, the signature should be operationalized as an assay bundle that combines a standardized HRD CDx, a targeted mass spectrometry or multiplexed immunoassay panel for phospho‐proteins and LC3B, digital spatial profiling or multiplex IHC for cell topology, and validated ELISA or exosome assays for sPD‐L1 and ascites cytokines, with each component analytically validated to CLIA‐equivalent standards [205, 214].

Prospective validation must address preanalytical variability, tumor heterogeneity across metastatic sites, thresholds for clinical actionability and the practical trade‐offs between breadth of profiling and cost [212, 213]. This validation should proceed in three stages: analytical validation of each assay, retrospective validation in an independent locked cohort, and then prospective biomarker‐stratified testing in a multicenter study with prespecified cutoffs, assay reproducibility metrics, and decision‐impact endpoints. If successfully validated, a Resistance Pathway Signature would enable precise matching of patients to combination regimens designed to neutralize immune suppression while simultaneously dismantling tumor‐intrinsic survival networks, thereby overcoming the clinical blind spot created by reliance on PD‐L1 IHC alone [130, 179]. Conclusion and Future Perspectives

This review supports a broader conceptualization of PD‐L1 in ovarian cancer in which its biological significance extends beyond the canonical PD‐1/PD‐L1 immune checkpoint. In addition to suppressing antitumor T‐cell activity at the tumor–immune interface, tumor‐intrinsic PD‐L1 can participate in signaling programs associated with proliferation, autophagy, tumor‐initiating capacity, transcriptional regulation, and treatment adaptation. Ovarian‐specific experimental evidence demonstrates that intrinsic PD‐L1 can promote tumor growth, autophagy, and tumor‐initiating cell generation, while more recent work indicates that nuclear PD‐L1 can occupy promoters of IL‐8/CXCL8, Bcl3, and STAT1 and support transcriptional programs associated with ovarian‐cancer progression [23, 28]. The emerging association between nuclear PD‐L1, chemoresistance, recurrence, and adverse outcome in ovarian‐cancer tissues further supports the biological relevance of subcellular PD‐L1 localization [61]. At the same time, mechanistic findings derived predominantly from non‐ovarian models, particularly the observation that deglycosylated nuclear PD‐L1 interacts with Ku and promotes non‐homologous end joining, should be regarded as compelling mechanistic hypotheses rather than definitive ovarian‐cancer mechanisms until they are reproduced in clinically representative ovarian systems [48].

Viewed collectively, the evidence discussed throughout this review places PD‐L1 at the intersection of several mutually reinforcing dimensions of therapy resistance. DNA‐damage responses, anti‐apoptotic signaling, metabolic adaptation, cytoprotective autophagy, and cancer‐stem‐cell persistence can converge with PD‐L1‐associated signaling, while cytokine networks, VEGF‐driven vascular dysfunction, malignant ascites, stromal architecture, hypoxia, and chemotherapy‐induced remodeling of the tumor microenvironment can further stabilize immune escape and resistant cellular states. Importantly, spatial analyses of high‐grade serous ovarian cancer show that chemotherapy can reorganize myeloid–T‐cell interactions and generate spatially confined T‐cell exhaustion, emphasizing that response to immune checkpoint blockade cannot be predicted from tumor‐cell PD‐L1 expression alone [168, 206]. Recent clinical evidence similarly argues against treating ovarian cancer as uniformly insensitive to checkpoint‐based therapy: ENGOT‐ov65/KEYNOTE‐B96 demonstrated improved outcomes with pembrolizumab plus weekly paclitaxel, with or without bevacizumab, in platinum‐resistant recurrent ovarian cancer, underscoring the importance of therapeutic backbone, clinical context, and biomarker‐informed selection rather than indiscriminate addition of immune checkpoint blockade [10].

Against this background, the first future priority is to establish which tumor‐intrinsic PD‐L1 functions are genuinely causal in ovarian cancer. The intracellular PD‐L1 interactome should be mapped in high‐grade serous and other relevant histotypes using endogenous proximity labeling, quantitative proteomics, phosphoproteomics, and domain‐specific CRISPR editing, followed by functional validation in patient‐derived organoids, ascites‐derived tumor cultures, syngeneic models, and patient‐derived xenografts. Particular attention should be directed toward separating membrane‐initiated reverse signaling from cytoplasmic scaffolding and nuclear PD‐L1 activity. The proposed PD‐L1–DNA‐repair relationship warrants especially rigorous investigation by determining whether chemotherapy induces PD‐L1 deglycosylation and nuclear entry in ovarian cancer, whether nuclear PD‐L1 interacts directly with Ku or other repair complexes in these cells, and whether selectively preventing nuclear localization restores platinum sensitivity without eliminating physiologically important surface checkpoint regulation [48]. Such experiments would convert the current “dual shield” model from a biologically plausible framework into a set of experimentally separable and potentially druggable mechanisms.

A second priority is to translate these mechanisms into interventions that do more than sterically block PD‐1/PD‐L1 binding. Targeting PD‐L1 glycosylation, CMTM6‐dependent stability, nuclear trafficking, intracellular protein interactions, or regulated degradation could theoretically suppress cell‐autonomous PD‐L1 functions that conventional checkpoint antibodies do not adequately address. These approaches should presently be described as investigational because their therapeutic windows and ovarian‐cancer specificity remain undefined. Targeted delivery provides another particularly attractive direction. A recent ovarian‐specific study developed the PD‐L1‐targeted antibody‐polymer‐epirubicin conjugate U6244‐051, which combines PD‐L1 recognition with delivery of an immunogenic cytotoxic payload and produced substantial antitumor activity in a syngeneic advanced ovarian‐cancer model [216]. This proof of concept provides a stronger ovarian‐specific rationale for PD‐L1‐directed antibody–drug or antibody–polymer conjugates, although pharmacokinetics, target heterogeneity, internalization efficiency, payload selection, bystander effects, and on‐target/off‐tumor toxicity must be resolved before clinical translation.

Third, future combination strategies should be derived from the dominant resistance mechanism in each tumor rather than from empirical drug addition. Tumors dominated by homologous‐recombination defects and replication stress may be appropriate for combinations integrating PARP/ATR‐pathway targeting with immunotherapy, particularly because PARP inhibition can activate STING‐dependent immune signaling and create a biological rationale for checkpoint blockade [127]. Conversely, PD‐L1‐high tumors with strong PI3K–AKT–mTOR or anti‐apoptotic signaling may require survival‐pathway or BH3‐mimetic priming, whereas immune‐excluded tumors may benefit more from antiangiogenic, stromal, or myeloid‐directed strategies. Timing must be considered part of the mechanism rather than merely a trial‐design variable, because chemotherapy dynamically alters the ovarian tumor microenvironment and can generate myeloid‐associated T‐cell exhaustion [168]. Prospective trials should therefore directly compare concurrent, sequential, and maintenance strategies while incorporating longitudinal biopsies or liquid sampling capable of demonstrating whether the intended biological state was actually created before the next therapeutic component was administered.

Fourth, biomarker development must progress beyond static total PD‐L1 immunohistochemistry. An actionable biomarker framework should distinguish tumor‐cell from immune‐cell PD‐L1, membrane from cytoplasmic and nuclear pools, and glycosylated from deglycosylated protein, while integrating genomic repair status, survival signaling, autophagic state, immune‐cell topology, and soluble or extracellular markers. This is particularly important because N‐linked glycosylation can interfere with antibody‐based detection of PD‐L1, and experimental deglycosylation improves its analytical recognition [217]. In ovarian cancer, longitudinal immunomic and multi‐omic studies already demonstrate that composite immune, transcriptional, metabolic, and spatial variables can provide information that is not captured by PD‐L1 staining alone [179]. Future biomarker studies should therefore prioritize prospective, locked signatures developed from clinically obtainable specimens and should demonstrate incremental predictive value over established variables such as histotype, BRCA/HRD status, treatment history, and conventional PD‐L1 scoring before complex multi‐omic assays are advanced toward routine use.

Finally, clinical translation will require these mechanistic, therapeutic, and biomarker advances to be developed together rather than sequentially. Early‐phase studies of intracellular PD‐L1‐directed strategies should incorporate pharmacodynamic evidence of target engagement, compartment‐specific PD‐L1 measurements, immune monitoring, and predefined resistance endpoints rather than relying exclusively on radiographic response. Particular attention will be required for therapies that deplete or redistribute PD‐L1 because systemic manipulation of this checkpoint could affect immune homeostasis, while targeted delivery systems will require demonstration that enhanced tumor selectivity translates into an acceptable therapeutic index. Consequently, the immediate goal should not be to assume that every PD‐L1‐associated resistance mechanism is clinically actionable, but to determine which PD‐L1 states are causal, measurable, druggable, and predictive in defined ovarian‐cancer populations.

As a narrative review, this article was informed by targeted searches of PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science using combinations of the terms “ovarian cancer,” “PD‐L1,” “immune checkpoint blockade,” “chemoresistance,” “DNA damage response,” “autophagy,” and “cancer stem cells,” supplemented by backward citation screening of relevant reviews and key primary studies. Priority was given to English‐language studies directly addressing PD‐L1 biology in ovarian cancer; where ovarian‐specific mechanistic evidence was limited, evidence from other tumor models was considered and identified as extrapolative. Because study selection was narrative rather than governed by a prespecified systematic‐review protocol, relevant studies may have been missed and selection or language bias cannot be excluded. The literature‐search approach was reported with reference to the SANRA framework, which includes description of the literature search as a core quality domain for narrative reviews.

In conclusion, the principal implication of this review is that PD‐L1 in ovarian cancer should no longer be considered solely as a static surface biomarker or an extracellular brake on T‐cell activity. Instead, available evidence supports a context‐dependent “dual shield” in which immune checkpoint activity can coexist with tumor‐intrinsic programs that contribute to survival, adaptation, and chemoresistance. The clinical value of this model will ultimately depend on experimentally separating these functions, validating ovarian‐specific mechanisms, matching therapies to the dominant resistance state, and replacing one‐dimensional PD‐L1 assessment with dynamic, spatially and molecularly resolved biomarkers. Such a strategy provides a more realistic path toward converting the complex biology of PD‐L1 from an explanation for therapeutic failure into a framework for precision treatment of resistant ovarian cancer.
