Unlocking nature’s brilliance: using Antarctic extremophile Shewanella baltica to biosynthesize lanthanide-containing nanoparticles with optical up-conversion
Authors: Nia Oetiker, Juan José León, Mark Swihart, Kaiwen Chen, Blaine A Pfeifer, Avisek Dutta, Artem Pliss, Andrey N Kuzmin, José Manuel Pérez-Donoso, Paras N Prasad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBoth lanthanide-containing and fluorine-containing nanomaterials present challenging targets for microbial biosynthesis because these elements are toxic to most bacteria. Here, we overcome these challenges by using an Antarctic Shewanella baltica strain that tolerates these elements and report the first biosynthesis of lanthanide-doped fluoride nanoparticles (NPs) from them. NaYF4 NPs doped with Er3+/Yb3+ are prototypical lanthanide-based upconverting nanoparticles (UCNPs) with upconverted luminescence at visible wavelengths under infrared excitation. However, their synthesis employs high precursor concentrations, organic solvents, and elevated temperatures. Microbial biosynthesis offers a greener alternative but has not been explored for these materials. Here, we harness an extremophile S. baltica strain to biosynthesize UCNPs at room temperature, based upon its high tolerance for fluoride and lanthanide ions and the observation that tolerance of lanthanides increased in the presence of fluoride. Our biosynthesis produces electron-dense nanostructures composed of Na, Y, F, Yb, and Er in the bacterial periplasm, adhered to the outer cell membrane, and dispersed extracellularly, which exhibited up-converted emission under 980 nm excitation. This suggests that extracellular or periplasmic mineralization of lanthanides as fluorides protects the bacteria from lanthanide toxicity. Subsequent heating both enhanced upconverted emission from UCNPs and allowed observation of their crystallinity in transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Background

Lanthanide-doped up-conversion nanoparticles (UCNPs) harness sequential excitation of lanthanide ions by absorption of multiple photons to reach a multiply excited state from which a higher energy photon is emitted. These UCNPs are versatile tools for bioimaging applications, providing enhanced depth penetration and resolution for imaging biological structures and processes using infrared (IR) excitation [1, 2]. Typically, UCNPs comprise a host matrix, sensitizer, and activator. The host matrix is typically a compound of Y3+, La3+, or Lu3+ which has low frequency phonons to minimize multiphonon nonradiative relaxation. Activators, such as Pr3+, Nd3+, Sm3+, Tb3+, Ho3+, Er3+, and Tm3+ are incorporated as luminescence centers, while another rare-earth ion, typically Yb3+, is incorporated as a sensitizer [3]. NaYF4:Yb, Er is widely recognized as one of the most effective compositions for UCNPs [4]. Yb3+ ions are excited by 980 nm near-infrared (NIR) laser illumination, and consecutive energy-transfer processes then excite Er3+ ions. Ultimately, Er3+ ions emit visible photons (red or green) [5, 6].

UCNPs offer a powerful alternative to conventional fluorescent probes like organic dyes and quantum dots. While traditional probes may provide high quantum yields, they often face limitations such as photodegradation, switching between bright and dark states, low tissue penetration of ultraviolet (UV) light needed for excitation, and poor biocompatibility [7]. NaYF4 nanoparticles (NPs) doped with Er3+ and Yb3+ exhibit advantageous properties including narrow luminescence peaks, low background emission, long fluorescence lifetimes, resistance to blinking, and good biocompatibility [8]. Moreover, for IR-to- IR or visible up-conversion, REE (rare earth element)-doped NaYF4 NPs are excited using near-infrared/infrared light, which provides deep tissue penetration and generates no autofluorescence [9]. These properties have led to a wide range of applications, including super-resolution microscopy [10], enhancing solar cell efficiency [11], latent fingerprint detection [12], optogenetics [13, 14], high-resolution bioimaging, photodynamic therapy [15], nanoprobes [16], and sensing [17, 18].

Chemical synthesis of NaYF4:Yb, Er NPs employs thermal decomposition (organometallic precursors heated in organic solvents), hydrothermal/solvothermal synthesis (rare-earth salt precursors in water or organic solvents heated under high pressure), or co-precipitation [19]. However, these routes pose risks due to the potential presence of toxic chemical species adsorbed on the surface of NPs, thereby limiting their suitability for biomedical applications [20].

Considering the limitations and drawbacks of conventional NP synthesis routes, researchers have tested biological systems as viable alternatives. Microorganisms have emerged as environmentally friendly “factories” for NP synthesis [21]. Unlike traditional methods, biological synthesis harnesses the innate mechanisms of living organisms to convert potentially toxic metal species into NPs. Biosynthesis inherently requires lower temperatures and pressures and eliminates the need for toxic reagents or solvents. Furthermore, the resulting NPs often exhibit reduced cytotoxicity and phytotoxicity, rendering them suitable for various biomedical applications [22]. Algae, plants, yeasts, and bacteria are among the diverse range of biological agents capable of performing NP biosynthesis. This diversity of biological systems yields a wide array of NP shapes, sizes, compositions, and surface states, expanding the range of properties and potential applications of these NPs [23].

The genus Shewanella exhibits remarkable ability to reduce a wide range of metal ions, leading to the biomineralization of diverse metal NPs. Molecular mechanisms associated with metal NPs biosynthesis using Shewanella have been extensively documented [24]. While the use of microorganisms for synthesizing NPs is well-established, exploring their potential for synthesizing UCNPs, is a new and essentially unexplored direction that poses a major challenge due to the toxicity (antibacterial activity) of both the lanthanides and the fluoride ions of the host matrix. Fluoride is well known for its antibacterial properties [25]. Its mechanism of action involves direct binding to numerous enzymes crucial for bacterial metabolism, such as enolase, urease, or catalase, at micromolar concentrations [26]. REE can disrupt microbial cell membranes and interfere with essential cellular processes [27]. Muroma was the first to identify the antibacterial properties of REEs and document their dose-dependent effects. At higher concentrations (0.1 mM to 10 mM), these elements primarily inhibit the growth of Gram-negative bacteria [28], while at lower concentrations (10 µM), they can stimulate bacterial growth. Zhang et al. determined that cerium (Ce) at concentrations from 0.001 M to 0.01 M inhibited the growth of Escherichia coli, Bacillus pyocyaneus, Staphylococcus aureus, Leuconostoc, and Streptococcus faecaliss [29]. Liu et al. elucidated the inhibitory effects of lanthanum (La) nitrate on the growth of E. coli. They observed an abnormal “eruption of heat” phenomenon (500 mg/L; 1.5 mM), damaging the outer cell membrane, increasing permeability, and reducing proton-electron potential across the membrane. Consequently, cellular growth was impeded due to a scarcity of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), as energy conversion into ATP during oxidative phosphorylation was inefficient, resulting in increased heat release [30]. Additionally, REEs may induce the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to oxidative stress and damage to bacterial cells [31, 32]. However, there are currently no references available on the effects of REE or fluoride on S. baltica.

Prior reports show that Shewanella species have the ability to bioremediate fluoride and REE. Dwivedi et al., reported the ability of Shewanella putrefaciens to remove fluoride from contaminated water sources. reducing fluoride concentration from 20 to below 1.5 mg/L. They attributed this effect to the bacteria’s capacity for adsorption and accumulation of fluoride [33]. Moreover, studies have highlighted the ability of Shewanella oneidensis MR-12 to absorb REEs, laying the groundwork for investigating their potential for UCNP biosynthesis [34, 35]. Moreover, screenings of S. oneidensis MR-12 knock-out strains have demonstrated their capability to remove a mixture of REEs from aqueous solution, including ytterbium (Yb), a crucial sensitizer in UCNPs [36].

Here, we report the use of bacteria from the Shewanella genus as a biofactory for UCNP biosynthesis through an easy room temperature and low precursor concentration protocol. Specifically, we selected an extremophile S. baltica strain isolated from the Antarctic region, renowned for its resistance to cadmium and tellurium and its ability to biosynthesize cadmium sulfide (CdS) quantum dots [37, 38]. The positive outcomes reported in this study represent a pioneering example of UCNP synthesis through bacterial biosynthesis using extremophiles, overcoming the significant challenges of lanthanide and fluoride toxicity, and producing a class of nanomaterials, rare-earth fluorides, that have not previously been generated by microbial biosynthesis.

Methods

Bacterial strain

The S. baltica strain used in this work was isolated from Antarctic soil by the BioNanotechnology and Microbiology Laboratory of Dr. José Manuel Pérez-Donoso [37, 38].

Effect of REE and fluoride presence on S. Baltica growth

One colony of S. baltica from LB (Luria Bertani) 1.5% agar was grown overnight in LB broth cultured at 37˚C with shaking. From this culture, we transferred 1 mL into 50 mL of fresh LB broth. After 4 h (OD600nm = 0.3–0.4), cells were diluted 1/1000 in LB media. After that, NaF, Y(NO3)3, Yb(NO3)3, and Er(NO3)3 were added independently to obtain final concentrations ranging from 0 to 50 mM in the REEs and 0 to 500 mM in NaF. After 24 h at 28 °C with constant shaking (120 RPM), we analyzed cell growth (via OD600nm) using a multimode microplate reader, Synergy H1 (Biotek). Data were collected from three independent experiments with three technical replicates.

In addition, the survival of S. baltica was analyzed during the NP biosynthesis procedure. After 4 h of growth in LB medium (OD600nm of 0.3–0.4), cells were washed twice with 20 mM MES/150 mM NaCl (pH 5.5; biosynthesis buffer, BB). S. baltica cells were adjusted to an OD600nm of 0.8 in BB in the presence of 0.5 mM of Y(NO3)3, Yb(NO3)3, NaF, and 0.001 mM of Er(NO3)3 separately and in combination. After 60 min, bacteria were seeded on solid LB medium for CFU count.

Biosynthesis of REE UCNPs

To produce REE NPs using S. baltica, one colony from an LB agar plate was used to start an overnight LB broth culture. From this culture, 1 mL was transferred to 50 mL of fresh LB broth. After 4 h (OD600nm reading of 0.3–0.4), cells were washed twice with BB.

After 1 h of incubation in BB with 0.5 mM each of NaF, Y(NO3)3, and Yb(NO3)3, and 0.001 mM of Er(NO3)3, the supernatant was separated from the cells. The cells were washed twice with the same BB without precursors. The supernatant was filtered with a 0.22 μm membrane, and the liquid was dialyzed overnight using a 10 kDa membrane. Dialyzed supernatant was concentrated with a 3 kDa MilliporeSigma™ Amicon™ filter tube; the concentrated NPs were washed with distilled water five times. Supernatant NPs were lyophilized for XRD analysis and vortexed for 30 min for TEM-EDS analysis. The luminescent properties were analyzed in both the cell and supernatant samples.

Characterization of biosynthesized UCNPs by TEM-EDS in cells

Cells resulting from the biosynthesis protocol were fixed with 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer containing 2% glutaraldehyde and sent to the Canadian Centre for Electron Microscopy (McMaster University). The cellular samples were rinsed twice in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer for 5 min each with the last batch of samples post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer for 1 h and then dehydrated in 10 min intervals through a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 70%, 95%, 95%, 100%, 100%). Final dehydration was done in two exchanges of 100% propylene oxide (PO). The samples were mixed with Spurr’s resin through a graded series (2:1 PO: resin, 1:1 PO: resin, 1:2 PO: resin, 100% resin, 100% resin, 100% resin) with rotation of the samples between solution changes. The samples were then transferred to embedding molds filled with fresh 100% Spurr’s resin and polymerized overnight in a 60˚C oven. Thin Sect. (90 nm) were cut on a Leica UCT ultramicrotome and then mounted onto Cu grids and lacy C-grids.

The supernatant liquid samples were sonicated for 30 min and dropped onto a carbon-coated copper grid. Both types of samples (cells and supernatant) were analyzed by a Thermo Fisher Scientific Talos 200X TEM operated at 200 kV. HAADF was performed with a spot size less than 1 nm and a convergence semi-angle of 10.5 mrad. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) data was acquired using the Velox program from Thermo Fisher Scientific. This equipment provided bright and dark field morphology images, and electron diffraction patterns (Selected Area Electron Diffraction or SAED). The shape, size, and interplanar distances of the NPs were determined by processing HR-TEM micrographs with Fiji software [39].

Optical microscopy

After 1 h of incubation, 20 µL of the biosynthesis mixture, the control bacteria without UCNP precursors, and the UCNP precursors without bacteria, were stained with 5 µM of DNA dye Hoechst 33342 for 20 min, dispersed over a glass coverslip, and fixed with 4% formaldehyde in cacodylate buffer for 10 min. Next, coverslips were repeatedly washed in cacodylate buffer, rinsed with water and air-dried. The coverslips were then mounted on microscopy slides using glycerol as an embedding solution. The samples were observed using an inverted Leica Stellaris 8 confocal laser scanning microscope. Hoechst staining of bacterial DNA was excited at 405 nm, transmitted light imaging of bacteria used a 550 nm laser. and UCNPs were excited by a 980 nm laser.

XRD study

Diffracted intensities were measured on a Rigaku XtaLAB Synergy-S equipped with a HyPix-6000HE hybrid photon counting area detector. Powder diffraction experiments were performed using a Cu-target PhotonJet-S source with divergence slits set to provide a beam with 1.2 mrad divergence. A Gandolfi data collection strategy (simultaneously scanning φ and ω) at a single κ orientation was used. Three detector positions were used to collect data to 80° in 2Θ at a detector distance of 60 mm. Samples were mounted using a 200 μm MiTeGen loop with Paratone-N oil. A background scan of the loop with oil was acquired to correct for background scatter.

Upconversion spectroscopy under 980 nm laser excitation

Spectral measurements of up-converted emission were made using a SpectraPro 2500i spectrometer (Acton Research, Acton MA) with Roper Scientific CCD camera. The excitation source was a 975 nm fiber-coupled laser diode (BWT Beijing Ltd., Beijing, China).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad 7.0a. A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s multiple comparison test was used, and results with a p-value < 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Growth of S. Baltica in the presence of UCNP precursors and biosynthesis conditions

We began by establishing the optimal conditions for both the production of REE NPs and the survival of bacteria. This involved assessing the effect on bacteria of each precursor used in the synthesis of NaYF4:Yb: Er. For this, we conducted assays to determine the minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) of each precursor (Fig. 1A-D).

Shewanella baltica growth in the presence of different concentrations of UCNP precursors A) NaF, B) Y(NO3)3, C) Yb(NO3)3, and D) Er(NO3)3

Growth analysis of S. baltica showed similar effects across all REEs, as illustrated in Fig. 1B-D. The MIC value for S. baltica exposed to yttrium (Y) (Fig. 1B) was 4 mM (1532 mg/L, or strictly between 3 and 4 mM), while in the presence of ytterbium (Yb) (Fig. 1C), a MIC of 5 mM (1795 mg/L) was observed. Similarly, exposure to erbium (Er) (Fig. 1D) resulted in a MIC of 5 mM (2216 mg/L). Also, we analyzed the effect of fluoride (F) on S. baltica growth, due to its known antibacterial activity [26, 33, 40–43]. Our results indicated that S. baltica can tolerate high F concentrations, with a MIC of 500 mM (21000 mg/L). In chemical synthesis of NaYF: Yb: Er NPs limiting the erbium concentration is critical due self-quenching of emission at Er3+ concentrations above 1–2% [5, 44, 45]. After initial studies of Er concentration in UCNPs as a function of Er concentration in the synthesis, we set the Er concentration to 0.2% of the Yb concentration.

Interaction of S. Baltica with UCNP precursors

The biosynthesis of NPs using bacteria involves different mechanisms depending on the nature of the NPs. In the case of NaYF4:Yb: Er NPs, bacterial reduction of precursors is not the primary mechanism, because the oxidation state of the elements is the same in the precursor salts and in the product NPs. In this case, the initial stages of NP biosynthesis likely involve the trapping of metal ions on the surface or in the periplasm of microbial cells, so the bacteria can function as a nucleation point in biosynthesis. To analyze bacterial metal capture, we exposed S. baltica to precursors to the biosynthesis conditions and investigated the interaction of these precursors with bacterial cells using Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) in both bacteria and culture supernatant.

By TEM, electron-dense nanostructures were observed within the periplasmic space and adhered to the outer membrane of S. baltica (Fig. 2). Elemental analysis by Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) revealed that these nanostructures comprised Y, Na, F, Yb, and Er (Fig. 2A, B), suggesting the formation of NaYF: Yb: Er NPs. In contrast, no electron-dense nanostructures were detected in control cells that were not exposed to precursors (supplementary Fig.S1).

A) HAADF image from NPs adhered to the outer cell membrane and extracellular region (white arrow). B) EDS of these elements, mapping sodium (yellow), fluorine (green), yttrium (red), ytterbium (purple), and erbium (grey). Note that A and B are different cells. C) Confocal microscopy analysis of cells from UCNP biosynthesis using S. baltica stained with Hoechst 33342 (blue), 980 nm excited up-converted emission from cells (green), and merged image (green + blue)

To investigate whether the amorphous and agglomerated nanostructures adhered to the S. baltica cell exhibit up-converted luminescence characteristic of NaYF4:Yb, Er, we conducted confocal microscopy analysis. Specifically, we examined the emission of Er3+ from fixed cells excited at 980 nm (Fig. 2C).

When cells producing the nanostructures composed of Na, Y, F, Yb, and Er, were excited using a 980 laser, the characteristic green (~ 540 nm) emission of Er3+ was observed, likely representing the Er3+ up-conversion signal from cells; however, the 650 nm Er3+ emission peak was not detected (Supplementary Fig. S3). Two controls were used: (1) cells incubated in BB without precursors, and (2) BB media incubated with precursors but without cells (Supplementary Fig. S2). No green or red emission was detected from either control under excitation at 980 nm. The emission spectra from cells after biosynthesis and from all respective controls, without Hoechst 33342 staining (supplementary Fig. 2 and supplementary videos), was also analyzed; in these cases, only the cells excited with a 980 nm laser after the biosynthesis process showed up-converted emission from Er with a peak at 540 nm. Thus, we have developed the first protocol for biosynthesis of REE based UCNPs, and this advance was made possible by using the extremophile S. baltica isolated from Antarctica as a biofactory.

Furthermore, the presence of UCNPs was analyzed in the extracellular space (Fig. 3). TEM imaging showed the absence of electron-dense nanostructures in the supernatant under conditions without precursors (supplementary Fig. S1). However, upon incubating the cells with precursors, electron-dense nanostructures ranging from 30 to 90 nm in size were detected in the supernatant (Fig. 3A).

A) HAADF image of NPs with EDS mapping showing localization of elements: yttrium (Y), sodium (Na), fluoride (F), ytterbium (Yb) and erbium (Er) and their overall elemental content from EDS (table, other elements excluded). B) XRD analysis of extracellular NPs (blue arrows indicate peaks characteristic of the cubic α-NaYF4, card 077-2042, NaCl card 090-3683 in orange). C) Up-converted emission from NPs synthesized extracellularly by S. baltica (orange line) and a negative control in absence of bacteria and presence of precursors (blue line), excited at 980 nm

EDS analysis gave a composition of 48% fluorine, 34% sodium, 10% yttrium, ~ 4.5% ytterbium, and 2.5% erbium, excluding other elements. Additionally, we conducted an XRD analysis on supernatant samples using filtered supernatant that underwent overnight dialysis followed by concentration using an Amicon 3 kDa filter. XRD analysis revealed distinct peaks characteristic of a cubic phase, aligning closely with the anticipated diffraction pattern for cubic NaYF4 (JCPDS card, No. 77-2042) (Fig. 3B). NaYF4 peaks are shifted to slightly smaller angles due to doping with Yb and Er, which are much larger than Y, thus expanding the lattice. The nanostructures identified in the culture supernatant exhibited up-converted emission with a peak emission observed between 500 and 670 nm when excited at 980 nm (Fig. 3C). In contrast, the negative control including only the precursors exhibited a discernible, albeit weak, peak at 700 nm. The same peak was observed in control cells incubated without precursors, after being washed (Supplementary Fig.S3 and Supplementary Videos).

Furthermore, we monitored the bacterial count throughout the NP biosynthesis process using colony-forming unit (CFU) assays (Fig. 4). This allowed us to track the viability of S. baltica after biosynthesis. We analyzed the viability in the presence of each precursor independently and under biosynthesis conditions where all precursors were present.

The dark gray bar represents the viability of cells incubated in the presence of all precursors at the same time. Asterisks represent values statistically significant (* p < 0.05) compared to the related controls without precursor. ns: represents no statistically significant changes

S. baltica was able to survive incubation for 1 h in BB containing 0.5 mM NaF or 0.001 mM Er independently, without experiencing a significant reduction in cell numbers relative to the initial number of cells (Fig. 4, purple and blue versus black bar). However, the presence of Yb and Y at the concentrations used in synthesis had a detrimental effect on the survival of S. baltica. Notably, the negative impact was less pronounced in the combined presence of all precursors. These results demonstrated that Yb and Y have a significant negative effect on S. baltica incubated in BB for 1 h; however, this effect was reduced when all precursors were present, possibly due to UCNP formation. The results demonstrate for first time a biosynthesis of Er3+-emitting NPs by microorganisms in aqueous media using a low precursor concentration and low-temperature protocol. The improved cell viability when all precursors were added together suggests that the UCNP production is a metabolic response that provides a protective effect to the cells.

Enhancing up-conversion emission of REE-doped nanoparticles

While the direct biosynthesis of REE-doped NPs with up-converted emission marks a significant breakthrough, we also pursued strategies to enhance the up-converted emission brightness and efficiency. Post-biosynthesis heat treatment, conducted at 200 °C for either 1–24 h in an unstirred autoclave reactor within an oven, improved the up-conversion emission of these NPs. Despite not reaching the temperature threshold required for complete organic matter removal via carbonization (i.e., at least 350 °C for 16 h) [46], we believe the post-biosynthesis heat treatment reduces the influence of biomolecules associated with the NPs, which may otherwise hinder emission. Also, we could observe the NPs crystallinity in HRTEM, which was likely obscured by organic matter before heating.

TEM imaging of biosynthesized UCNPs showed NPs from 30 to 90 nm in size before heating (Fig. 5A and B). Following 1 h of heating at 200 °C, NPs with visible crystallinity and a size range of 3 to 14 nm were observed (Fig. 5C, D). This average size remained relatively stable even after 24 h of heating (Fig. 5E and F).

Size analysis of UCNPs produced by S. baltica. (A) TEM image and (B) size distribution of concentrated UCNPs from the supernatant without post-biosynthesis heating. (C) TEM image and (D) size distribution of UCNPs heated at 200 °C for 1 h. (E) TEM image and (F) size distribution of UCNPs heated at 200 °C for 24 h

From each of the heating durations, we acquired the corresponding Selected Area Electron Diffraction (SAED) patterns, as depicted in Supplementary Fig.S4. For NaYF4, the anticipated typical d-spacings are 3.1, 2.7, 1.94, 1.66, 1.38, and 1.05 Å in the cubic phase and 5.1, 3.49, 2.95, 2.25, 2.066, and 1.69 Å in the hexagonal phase [47]. Our analysis revealed characteristic peaks of each phase, albeit without a definitive match, suggesting the presence of a mixture of phases, likely encompassing other materials beyond NaYF4.

After 24 h of heating, higher crystallinity and up-converted emission were observed (Fig. 6). At this point, we expect that most of the organic matter is carbonized (or otherwise reduced) and energy transfer from Yb3+ to Er3+ is thus more efficient, increasing considerably the Er3+ emission. The typical peaks at 550 nm and 650 nm were observed (Fig. 6B). Also, the high-resolution TEM image of UCNPs showed clear lattice fringes with a spacing of approximately 0.375 nm and 0.168 nm (Fig. 6A, C). Finally, the EDS analysis revealed that these electron dense nanoelements (Fig. 6D) were composed of 20% F, 5% Na, 41% Y, 25% Yb and 7% Er in atomic fraction percentage (other elements excluded).

Analysis of UCNPs biosynthesized by S. baltica heated 24 h post-biosynthesis. (A, C) HRTEM images showing lattice spacings in the UCNPs. (B) Up-converted Er3+ emission spectrum under 980 nm illumination. D) EDS elemental maps of yttrium (Y), sodium (Na), fluorine (F), ytterbium (Yb) and erbium (Er)

Altogether, the results presented here highlight the development of a simple, low-temperature protocol free of organic solvents for generating UCNPs. These optical up-conversion properties can be further enhanced through post-biosynthesis heat treatment. Future endeavors will focus on purifying these nanostructures and scaling production to obtain enough NPs for more detailed and quantitative comparative analysis with chemically synthesized counterparts.

Discussion

In the realm of cost-efficiency, biocompatibility, environmental sustainability, and scalability, green NPs synthesis has emerged as a frontier in nanotechnology and a promising alternative to conventional physical and chemical methods of NPs synthesis. Leveraging the diverse array of microbiological resources in nature offers a sustainable and efficient approach to producing inorganic NPs. Bacteria from the Shewanella genus are known to absorb and accumulate inorganic elements, including REEs [36], from their surroundings, and thus have a potential for inorganic NP production [24, 37]. Based upon the scientific and technological value of lanthanide-doped UCNPs and the fact that their green synthesis using bacteria was totally unexplored, we selected Antarctic bacteria from the Shewanella genus as promising microbes for biosynthesis of UCNPs.

Because the MICs of F, Y, Yb and Er for S. baltica had not previously been reported, we began by analyzing the effect of these UCNP precursors on growth (Fig. 1). S. baltica demonstrated similar resistance to each of the REEs (MICs of 3–5 mM) and showed a very high resistance to fluoride ions (MIC of 500 mM). In our biosynthesis process, F was the most critical element, because it is widely recognized as a toxic element and is frequently employed as an antimicrobial agent, e.g., in anti-cavity applications [48]. For instance, the MIC of NaF for cariogenic Streptococcus mutans UA159 is 600 mg/L, equivalent to 14.3 mM, with select oral isolates capable of resisting up to 157.9 mM [49]. Additionally, F can mimic the role of phosphate in cellular metabolism, thereby affecting various enzymes and regulatory phosphatases [26]. Interestingly, we found that our Antarctic Shewanella baltica strain exhibits high resistance to F, withstanding concentrations of up to 500 mM in LB medium (Fig. 1A). This level of tolerance surpasses that observed even in high F resistant bacteria isolated from Natham taluk, South India, which showed MIC values ranging from 200 to 300 mM [43]. From these MIC results, we derived appropriate conditions for UCNP biosynthesis using S. baltica.

Our biosynthesis method used low REE and F concentrations compared to conventional chemical protocols, along with mild, ambient conditions, without organic solvents. After treating the cells in biosynthesis conditions, TEM analysis (Fig. 2) showed electron-dense nanoelements adhered to the outer cell membrane and in the periplasmatic space, showing that bacteria can function as metal-capturing and stabilizing agents. Extracellular NPs were also observed, but none were visible within the cells. In cells, the biosorption, particularly of metal ions, encompasses a broad spectrum of interactions ranging from electrostatic to covalent associations with available functional groups on sorbent materials [50]. Co-localization of these elements suggests the potential biosynthesis of NaYF₄:Yb: Er NPs by S. baltica within the periplasmic space or on the cell surface, with minimal REEs observed in the cytoplasmic space. This could be attributed to sites in the membrane with an affinity for binding REE, which have been identified in both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria [36, 51]. We speculate that these binding sites could promote the formation of REE nanoparticles. Accumulation of these ions on the surface or in the periplasm could promote nucleation and NP formation [52]. Previous reports suggested that carboxylate and phosphate sites are mainly responsible for adsorption of cations onto bacterial cells [51]. Medin et al., identified 130 genes that enhance the biosorption of Eu and 112 genes that diminish it [36]. Notably, disruptions in a key regulatory component of cellular responses to anoxic environments and polysaccharide biosynthesis-related genes consistently enhanced the biosorption. Also, the disruption of a gene associated with capsular polysaccharide (CPS) synthesis resulted in up to a 79% boost in biosorption. Thus, that study offers insights into how genetic factors influence REE biosorption by S. oneidensis and how membrane chemistry is involved in REE binding [36]. These findings suggest that the periplasmic space and membrane play a significant role in nanoparticle biosynthesis, independent of the reductive pathways typically observed in other types of nanoparticle formation within the Shewanella genus [24].

After demonstrating that the electron-dense nanoelements were formed over/in cells, and that these NPs were composed of Na, Y, F, Yb and Er, we determined their up-converting properties by exciting the washed cells at 980 nm. We observed up-conversion from 980 nm to green emission of Er3+, however, we did not detect the typical red emission peak. Prior reports on chemically synthesized UCNPs suggested that energy transfer from Yb3+ to Er3+ can be diminished by surface luminescence quenchers (e.g., organic matter). One possible reason for the reduction of up-conversion luminescence intensity and less distinct peaks compared to chemically made NaYF4:Yb: Er NPs may be due to multiphonon relaxation induced quenching of longer wavelength red emission effected by vibrations modes of organic matter present in high content in these samples [4, 5].

Moreover, we could detect electron-dense nanostructures composed of all elements expected in UCNPs in the supernatant after cell removal (Fig. 3). The commonly used ratio for these dopants in up-conversion NPs is around 20% Yb and 2% Er, relative to the total Y ions present in the lattice. This ratio ensures efficient energy transfer from Yb ions to Er ions, leading to strong up-conversion luminescence [1, 2, 53]. A representative analysis of extracellular NPs showed a composition of 48% fluorine, 34% sodium, 10% yttrium, ~ 4.5% ytterium and 2.5% erbium. Thus, the synthesis did not yield the optimal percentages for efficient up-conversion; however, nanostructures containing all precursors of UCNPs were obtained with emission between 540 and 600 nm when excited at 980 nm (Fig. 3C), showing clear evidence of up-conversion. Further optimization of biosynthesis could be undertaken to vary these ratios to reach a desired composition. Finally, we observed a mixture of phases of NaYF₄ UCNPs in SAED patterns (supplementary Fig. 4), along with evidence of cubic phase NaYF4 in the XRD (Fig. 3B). The literature has established that the hexagonal phase is optimal for upconversion efficiency [53]. Extensive further optimization of our process will be required to achieve the control of phase, core-shell structure, and other factors that have been optimized in chemically-synthesized particles over decades.

Furthermore, under biosynthesis conditions, S. baltica exhibited robust survival in the presence of 0.5 mM NaF and 0.001 mM Er (Fig. 4), with no statistically significant differences compared to the control in the absence of precursors or at the initial inoculum. Though the presence of Yb or Y alone had a detrimental effect on S. baltica, this negative impact was mitigated when all precursors were included simultaneously, suggesting that the biosynthesis processes involved in NP production may alleviate the toxicity of the metal precursors, consistent with findings from previous studies [54–57].

NP production was conducted using cells previously washed from the nutrient medium to exclude active metabolism, suggesting that the physiological activity of the cells did not significantly influence NPs formation. Shewanella genus bacteria are known to actively participate in the formation of various metal nanoparticles (MeNPs), primarily by reducing metals. This reduction process is facilitated by a complex multicomponent branched electron transport system (ETS), consisting of inner membrane-localized dehydrogenases, menaquinone, and numerous cytochromes [58]. Among these electron transport systems, the Mtr (metal reduction) pathway of S. oneidensis is the most extensively studied. It is essential for reducing insoluble metal oxides and also plays a crucial role in the reduction of various soluble electron acceptors [59]. This pathway serves as a central mechanism in the bio-reduction of numerous metals and the subsequent formation of metal NPs [24]. Here, Shewanella bacteria did not primarily act as reducing agents. Rather, we propose that the biosynthesis mechanisms involved macromolecules located in the extracellular matrix or on the outer membrane, that may act as nucleation centers and stabilize NPs by adsorbing metal ions onto their surfaces as has been seen before in silver sulfide NP synthesis by Shewanella oneidensis MR1 [60]. In this biosynthesis, proteins from extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) or the cell membrane may participate in NP formation, suggesting that the involvement of native cell structures is necessary to form stable NPs [60]. The next step in our study was to heat the cells after biosynthesis to eliminate organic matter that could generate a negative effect on up-converted luminescence. The heated UCNPs exhibited enhanced crystallinity and annealing of defect sites to reduce quenching and enhance emission (Fig. 6). Although details of the reasons for observing a smaller size of the UCNPs after heating are not entirely clear, we suggest that the significant reduction in apparent size during the initial hour of heating results from degradation and removal of biomass encapsulating multiple individual crystallites [61, 62].

Conclusion

We conclude that S. baltica, an extremophile, is highly resistant to fluoride ions and can produce REE-doped fluoride NPs in aqueous media, at ambient conditions, with co-incorporation of Er3+ and Yb3+, leading to detectible up-converted emission under 980 nm laser excitation. Post-biosynthesis heating enhances the brightness of their up-converted emission. The results provide the first report of UCNPs produced by microbial biosynthesis, overcoming the major challenges of lanthanide and fluoride toxicity by harnessing an extremophile from Antarctica. Our protocol sets a new precedent for the eco-friendly biosynthesis of lanthanide-containing NPs, representing a significant advance at the intersection of nanotechnology and biotechnology. Furthermore, the development of this protocol lays the groundwork for further efforts to uncover novel bacterial strains or biosynthesis mechanisms that can further enhance the synthesis or manipulation of UCNPs.
