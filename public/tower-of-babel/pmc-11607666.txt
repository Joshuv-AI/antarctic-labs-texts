Delivering therapeutic RNA into the brain using extracellular vesicles
Authors: Paniz Shirmast, Mahdi Abedinzadeh Shahri, Austin Brent, Adi Idris, Nigel A.J. McMillan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
RNA science has experienced a surge, particularly its application to vaccine and therapeutic development. Delivery of RNA molecules into the brain remains one of the biggest therapeutic challenges with the blood-brain barrier excluding most drug-delivery modalities such as lipid nanoparticles. As RNA molecules are predisposed to degradation, harnessing its potential as a therapeutic agent requires protection during delivery into target cells. Several solutions have been offered to protect RNA molecules from degradation, with extracellular vesicles (EVs) garnering attention as naturally occurring nanoparticles released by cells, holding promise as carriers for RNA molecules. These EVs, which offer inherent protection to their cargo and have shown promise in efficiently and safely delivering RNA therapeutics present a promising avenue for advancing RNA-based therapeutics in combating various diseases. Here, we review this fast-developing field looking at the range of delivery strategies developed for RNA/EV brain delivery. We summarize the major issues with conventional RNA delivery techniques while emphasizing the potential benefits of EV-based treatments and suggest promising ways forward to progress the field so that we may realize the promise of delivering therapeutic RNA molecules in EVs as potential treatments for neurological disorders.

Introduction

RNA cargo delivery into the brain is a burgeoning field of research that has the potential to revolutionize the treatment of neurological diseases and disorders. The brain is a complex organ that relies on the precise regulation of RNA expression and function to maintain normal physiological function. Indeed, the RNA dysregulation has been implicated in the pathogenesis of several neurological disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and Huntington’s disease (HD).1 However, obstacles such as the blood-brain barrier (BBB), as well as the inherent instability of free RNA, makes delivering therapeutic RNA into the brain challenging. To protect RNA and allow its delivery many delivery modalities have been developed, including lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), cell-penetrating peptides, dendrimers, and extracellular vesicles (EVs). In addition to the BBB, RNA delivery into the brain also presents an additional set of unique challenges, including the need for precise targeting based on the cell type, and controlled release to ensure optimal therapeutic efficacy. In this review, we provide a comprehensive overview of the current state of RNA delivery into the brain, with a particular emphasis on EV-mediated delivery. We explore the types of RNA molecules being delivered, the formidable challenges associated with crossing the BBB, and the diverse array of strategies employed to facilitate RNA transport into the brain. In addition, we delve into the promising applications of RNA delivery in the treatment of neurological disorders, highlighting the potential impact of EV-based approaches. Furthermore, we examine the future directions and emerging technologies in this rapidly evolving field, aiming to provide insights into the advancement of RNA therapeutics for neurological conditions. By synthesizing the current knowledge and highlighting the gaps in our understanding, we provide insights into the opportunities and challenges associated with RNA delivery into the brain, with the goal of advancing the development of safe and effective therapies for neurological disorders.

Biology and biogenesis of EVs

EVs are lipid bilayer structures that are created through the endocytic process and released by several cell types in both normal physiological conditions and acquired disorders. EVs are discharged into bodily fluids, including blood, urine, and the cerebrospinal fluid.1,2 EV subtypes exhibit variations in size, which can be classified as “small” (sEVs) or exosomes (100–200 nm in size), and “medium/large” (m/lEVs) or microvesicles (>200 nm in size) and apoptotic bodies. According to the Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles 2018 criteria, the classification of EVs can be established based on their density, biochemical composition, or the specific cell type the EVs come from.3,4

EV biochemical composition also depends on its originating cell, the physiological or pathological condition of the cell, and external signals. These vesicles commonly consist of proteins, lipids, nucleic acids such as DNA, RNA, and microRNAs (miRNAs), and diverse metabolites (Figure 1). The precise cargo-sorting mechanisms that determine the incorporation of these biomolecules into EVs are complex and diverse.5 Rab proteins (Ras superfamily of monomeric G proteins), annexin I, II, V, and VI, and integrins decorate the EV surface and play a prominent role in EV docking and fusion.6,7 Indeed, tetraspanins (CD9, CD63, CD81, and CD82), and heat shock proteins (HSP73 and HSP90), are common EV identification biomarkers. The EV lipid bilayer contains multiple sphingolipids and phospholipids, important for docking, signaling, and fusion onto cell the membranes.8 EVs are secreted by both eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells, suggesting a deep evolutionary origin, and play diverse roles in cellular processes, including intercellular communication, blood clotting, maintaining cell balance, managing cellular waste, facilitating the spread of infections, and other important functions that are not yet fully understood.9Figure 1Composition and formation of EVsExosomes are formed inside cells when the endosomal membrane buds inward, leading to the creation of multivesicular bodies (MVBs) that contain intraluminal vesicles (ILVs). Composition and formation of EVs

Exosomes are formed inside cells when the endosomal membrane buds inward, leading to the creation of multivesicular bodies (MVBs) that contain intraluminal vesicles (ILVs). The fusion of MVBs with the cell membrane results in the liberation of exosomes into the extracellular environment. Apoptotic bodies are formed during the process of programmed cell death, or apoptosis, when cells break apart into vesicles enclosed by membranes. These vesicles include cellular components and organelles. Microvesicles, often referred to as shedding vesicles, are created when the cell membrane protrudes outward, resulting in the direct release of vesicles into the extracellular environment. Exosome development is initiated by the creation of a ligand-receptor complex on the cell membrane, which is triggered by platelet-derived growth factor receptor, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), or other signaling molecules, which leads for formation of the early endosomes (Figure 1). The early sorting endosomes develop through the invagination of the plasma membrane, involving the participation of cell surface proteins, the trans-Golgi network (TGN), the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and mitochondria.10 Following this, the membranes of early endosomes undergo budding into lumina, resulting in the creation of late sorting endosomes (LSEs). LSEs distribute their cargo via the TGN and merge with both the TGN and the ER. As a result, LSEs lead to the creation of intracellular multivesicular bodies (MVBs) that contain enclosed intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) by a second inward folding. Proteins such as clathrin are crucial in facilitating this process.11,12

The generation of sEVs from MVBs and the sorting of proteins take place via two pathways: one that is dependent on the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT), and the other independent of ESCRT.13 The ESCRT pathway plays a vital role in controlling the formation of MVBs and sorting proteins that have been ubiquitinated into EVs. Moreover, the process of sorting proteins into EVs is influenced by post-translational modifications such as ubiquitination, SUMOylation, and Neddylation.5 In addition, lipid rafts and tetraspanins such as CD9 and CD63 have a role in protein sorting that is not dependent on the ESCRT process.14 The varied cargo-sorting processes also apply to RNA, which mostly relies on RNA-binding proteins and unique features within miRNAs.15 The complex processes involved in organizing cargo into EVs emphasize the diverse characteristics of EVs and their potential importance in different biological and clinical scenarios.

MVBs have two final fates. Initially, MVBs are transported to the luminal region of the cell membrane by the assistance of the cytoskeletal and microtubule network, as well as docking proteins. In due course ILVs, also known as future sEVs, will be discharged into the extracellular environment by exocytosis.16,17 On the other hand, endosomal recycling takes place by the fusion of MVBs with lysosomes or autophagosomes, resulting in the degradation of its contents through the action of hydrolytic enzymes. Endosomes play a crucial role in determining whether the protein of MVBs undergoes degradation or secretion.16

Apoptotic bodies, often referred to as apoptotic EVs, are formed during the breakdown of apoptotic cells (Figure 1). This process entails meticulously regulated successive alterations in morphology, including the formation of plasma membrane blebs and apoptotic membrane protrusions facilitated by components such as Pannexin 1, Plexin B2, and the cytoskeleton, ultimately resulting in fragmentation into smaller entities.18,19 Apoptosis, which can be triggered by both internal and external factors, initiates a series of biological events within the cell, including the condensation of chromatin, the shrinking of the cell, and the destruction of organelles. Simultaneously, the cellular membrane experiences morphological alterations, resulting in the development of blebs that subsequently transform into budding vesicles that enclose cellular remains. The apoptotic bodies display certain surface marks, such as phosphatidylserine, which help phagocytic cells, especially macrophages, identify and eliminate them. This process allows for the effective elimination of dead cells without causing an inflammatory reaction.20,21 On the other hand, microvesicles are formed when healthy or active cells shed sections of their plasma membrane through outward budding (Figure 1). Microvesicles carry a wide range of biomolecules (proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and other cellular components) and play a crucial role in communication between cells by transporting bioactive molecules. Importantly, microvesicles have an impact on various physiological and pathological processes, including immune modulation and cell signaling cascades.22

Synthetic EVs

While the success of some studies utilizing therapeutic sEVs is promising, the current inability to manipulate them efficiently following their harvest hampers their efficient production, and their broader clinical application is limited by poor production and isolation yields, and a lack of a standard method for sEV isolation and purification.23 This has led to division on how to best utilize and mass produce sEVs for their therapeutic application.24 As a result, LNPs represent the current gold standard for the delivery of genetic therapies, with this most abundantly seen with their use in the vaccines deployed in response to the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic.25 This is in part due to their ease of production and their ability to achieve high encapsulation efficiencies of therapeutic cargo, achieving encapsulation efficiencies of >80%.26 LNPs, however, are currently hindered by the fact that only 1% of their cargo is able to escape the endosome and be delivered to cells,27 and they are unable to distribute to a wide range of tissues with the vast majority localizing within the liver, spleen, and kidney.28,29 Synthetic EVs (SynEVs) attempt to combine the low cost, ease of production and high encapsulation efficiencies of LNPs while attempting to maintain the previously mentioned beneficial characteristics of sEVs. This is achieved by using synthetic lipids, which match or are similar to, those of sEVs. The lipid mixture can then be used to form nanoparticles through standard methods used in LNP production.30,31 synEVs have been used to deliver curcumin to neuronal cells to exert a neuroprotective effect32 and, when packaged with VEGF siRNA, were then able to be taken up successfully by cells and exert a silencing effect. This was in addition to also showing a greater serum stability than traditional liposomes.33 The packaging of VEGF siRNA was achieved by formulating synEVs at a molar ratio of 30:21:17.5:17.5:14 (Chol/DOPC/SM/DOPE/DOPS), which was based on an “average” sEV molar ratio. These showed different uptake rates depending on the cell type and, as there are no surface proteins present on these nanoparticles, this could then be due to the differential lipid composition of the cells interacting with the lipids of the synEVs. Therefore, if a cell-specific sEV molar ratio was used for the synEVs, as opposed to an average, this could possibly allow for an enhanced uptake. Following on from this, Lu et al. utilized cell-free protein synthesis systems to incorporate connexin 43 into the synEVs.34 Although the incorporation of connexin 43 into the synEVs increased uptake efficiency in U87 MG cells, it had no difference in A549 cells and the same result was also seen when comparing standard sEVs to synEVs without connexin 43. Therefore, while proteins can assist in improving the uptake in certain cell types, as similar uptakes in A549 cells are seen in sEVs, synEVs with connexin 43, and synEVs without connexin 43, this could suggest that the proteomic profile may not be crucial for the development of synEVs. However, with the development of cell-free synthesis systems as well as a greater knowledge of the lipid composition of specific sEV populations this could allow for the production of sophisticated nanoparticle systems.

Uptake of EVs by the cells

EV uptake is an essential step in the delivery of their cargo to recipient cells. Although the comprehensive mechanism of EV uptake by recipient cells remains to be elucidated, different pathways are hypothesized including (1) endocytosis via clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME), caveolin-dependent endocytosis (CDE), phagocytosis, or micropinocytosis, (2) direct fusion with the host cell plasma membrane, (3) receptor-mediated interactions, and (4) lipid raft-mediated uptake.17,35,36 In CME, EVs first bind to specific receptors on the cell surface to trigger the recruitment of clathrin, a protein that assembles into a lattice structure beneath the plasma membrane. The clathrin-coated pit then deepens, eventually pinching off to form a clathrin-coated vesicle that contains the EV.36 Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K), actin cytoskeleton, and dynamin 2 have been identified as playing a role in phagocytosis and CME. Dynamin 2 is a GTPase that facilitates membrane binding of EVs and also the activity of dynamin 2 to facilitate the formation and enlargement of caveolar endocytic vesicles.

Caveolae are small, cave-shaped invaginations in the plasma membrane that, akin to clathrin-coated pits, can undergo internalization into the cell. Studies have shown that blocking dynamin 2 can drastically reduce the internalization of the EVs, suggesting an important role for CDE in vesicular uptake.37 On the other hand, macropinocytosis is a non-specific, fluid-phase endocytic process used by cells to take up large volumes of extracellular fluid, along with any vesicles present. This process starts with the formation of membrane ruffles on the cell surface. These ruffles fold back onto the plasma membrane, creating large vesicles called macropinosomes. Unlike CME or CDE, macropinocytosis does not involve specific receptors and is activated in response to growth factors or stimuli. After macropinosomes form, they merge with early endosomes or lysosomes, where their contents, including EVs, undergo processing or degradation. The direct fusion of EVs with the host plasma membrane is another potential method of EV uptake, allowing the direct delivery of EV cargo into the cytosol, bypassing the endosomal process and preventing probable destruction in lysosomes. Fusion is commonly facilitated by particular lipid compositions or proteins present on the surface of EVs that interact with the membrane of the recipient cell.

Receptor-mediated interactions are crucial for the selectivity of EV uptake. EVs frequently display surface proteins or lipids that can bind to matching receptors on target cells, thereby initiating endocytosis or other methods of uptake. This interaction guarantees that EVs transport their payload to the correct cells, which is crucial for preserving tissue balance and coordinating accurate cellular reactions. However, it has been shown that cellular uptake of EVs is highly dependent on the protein corona of the EVs.38,39 Finally, the process of lipid raft-mediated uptake entails the internalization of EVs through specific microdomains located inside the plasma membrane, which are referred to as lipid rafts. These rafts include high levels of cholesterol and sphingolipids, which create a unique environment to promote the grouping together of receptors and other proteins involved in endocytosis. EV particles that engage with these lipid rafts can be effectively taken inside and guided toward particular intracellular pathways.

Targeting the brain with EVs

The brain is safeguarded by a single layer of endothelial cells called the BBB. This barrier controls the movement of biological substances into the central nervous system (CNS) and has limited permeability, effectively preventing the entry of blood cells, pathogens, toxins, and various molecules, including drugs, into the brain.40 Hence, it is crucial to comprehend and consider the compositional, biological, and permeability aspects of the BBB when developing carriers that carry cargos for the goal of treating or diagnosing brain diseases. The emergence of new technologies in the nanoparticle space has opened exciting possibilities for delivering cargo directly to the brain and holds great promise for diagnosing and treating brain diseases and disorders. Considering the successful development of many nanocarriers, the primary obstacle for nanocarriers employed in brain-focused deliveries is its ability to cross the BBB. If not endowed with this ability, these nanocarriers are unable to penetrate the BBB. EVs have attracted interest as potential vehicles for delivering drugs to the brain because of their natural capacity to cross the BBB. Several studies have shown that EVs, specifically small EVs, have inherent qualities that allow them to penetrate the BBB, hence facilitating delivery of their contents into the brain.41 There are three primary reasons why sEVs are suitable for delivering substances to the brain. Firstly, sEVs have the capability to traverse physical barriers. Secondly, sEVs can transport specific proteins from the cells they originate from and transfer them to the recipient cells, simplifying the packaging process. Lastly, sEVs serve as natural carriers, ensuring safe delivery with minimal or no toxicity or immune system responses due to their biocompatibility and stability. Nevertheless, despite the numerous benefits of employing EVs for targeted drug delivery to the brain, drawbacks have been documented.

EVs utilize many mechanisms to cross the BBB, including transcytosis, receptor-mediated uptake, and interactions with specific proteins involved with BBB (Figure 2). Various theoretical pathways by which sEVs cross the brain endothelium at the BBB have been suggested. EVs trigger signaling cascades by attaching to G-protein-coupled receptors located on the brain endothelium.42 Furthermore, sEVs attach to and merge with the endothelial cell membrane, therefore releasing their contents into the cytoplasm, initiating further signaling processes.43 Another method of entry occurs when sEVs are internalized by cells through the process of inward membrane invagination.44 Moreover, certain receptors such as CD46 enhance the cellular uptake of sEVs. In addition, non-specific lipid rafts present in the membrane facilitate the integration and assimilation of sEVs into the cells.44 Nevertheless, the capacity of sEVs to traverse the BBB seems to be contingent upon its inherent dimensions and compactness.45 Morad et al. showed that tumor-derived EVs are capable of crossing the intact BBB using both in vitro and in vivo BBB models.46 This study mechanistically revealed that transcytosis, supported by the endothelium recycling endocytic pathway, is the crucial mechanism involved. This provides new and valuable knowledge on the process of breaching the BBB in brain metastasis. Other works have demonstrated that EVs produced from human neural stem cells (NSCs) and brain endothelial cell lines are capable of traversing the BBB and reaching the damaged cells in a mouse model of stroke.46,47 Moreover, EVs generated from glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) possess a diverse array of pro-angiogenic molecules that facilitate the proliferation of endothelial cells. The exosomes generated from hypoxic GBM, which include elevated levels of VEGF-A, have the ability to enhance BBB permeability by disrupting the expression of proteins including claudin-5 and occludin. The observed disturbance in the integrity of the BBB was noted in both in vivo and in vitro settings, suggesting a possible mechanism by which exosomes from GBM contribute to the advancement of tumors by undermining the barrier function of the BBB.48 Indeed, findings from phase I/II clinical trials to examine the safety and effectiveness of sEVs produced from allogenic human adipose mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) in patients with mild to moderate AD revealed that intranasal delivery of ahaMSCs-Exos was both safe and well tolerated.49 The intervention using a moderate dose resulted in enhancements in cognitive function and decreased emotional suffering among patients but did not alleviate neuropsychiatric symptoms. Several receptors in the BBB have been harnessed to deliver biomolecules to the BBB. Using these receptors presents a hopeful approach to precisely aim and convey therapeutic modalities via the BBB, leading to efficient therapy of neurological conditions and disorders.

Transferrin receptor

The transferrin receptor (TfR), which a component of the receptor-mediated transcytosis (RMT) process, has a crucial function in transporting blood-derived EVs across the BBB (Figure 2).50 RMT is a process by which a ligand binds to a specific receptor, resulting in endocytosis. This process subsequently transfers the internalized endosomal compartments to the opposite side of the membrane. TfRs play a role in promoting the transportation of iron and transferrin to different types of cells, such as brain capillary endothelial cells. Transferrin, which is present in large amounts in the blood plasma, primarily attaches to TfR found on the surface of blood-derived EVs and cerebral vascular endothelial cells.51 Following binding, TfR captures both the receptor and its ligand, moving to the cytosolic side of the cell membrane. This procedure promotes the uptake of EVs, guiding them into either endosomal or lysosomal pathways. The former facilitates the movement of EVs to the basolateral side of the cell membrane for crossing the BBB, whereas the latter guides EVs toward pathways, which results in its destruction.47 Work done in vivo has demonstrated that the process of binding, transporting, and delivering blood-derived EVs through TfRs is regulated by a dynamic cycle of endocytosis.52,53 This process entails the attachment of transferrin to its receptors on the cell membrane, followed by internalization. Afterward, the transferrin receptor releases its payload, which includes the sEVs and transferrin.

Apolipoprotein E

Apolipoprotein E (ApoE) receptors are a group of cell surface receptors that have essential functions in lipid metabolism, cellular absorption of lipoproteins, and other physiological processes. ApoE receptors engage with apolipoprotein E, a protein that plays a role in the conveyance and breakdown of lipids, such as cholesterol (Figure 2).54 ApoE receptors, such as TfRs, are present on the surface of endothelial cells that constitute the BBB. Furthermore, these receptors are present in several cellular populations inside the brain, including neurones, astrocytes, and microglia.50 These receptors have a crucial function in facilitating the passage of LNPs over the BBB.54 Furthermore, previous work has demonstrated that the conjugation of ApoE to nanoparticles can significantly improve its transportation into the brain.55,56 Interestingly, utilizing LNPs with labile polyethylene glycol (PEG)-lipids and co-administering ApoE significantly enhances intracellular mRNA delivery to keratinocytes via ApoE-LDLR-mediated uptake, while coating LNPs with mannose or using stealth-like LNPs shows limited to no transfection efficacy in skin cells.57 Moreover, subcutaneously administered LNPs, complexed with ApoE and modified with high-density PEG-conjugated lipids, effectively target lymphatic endothelial cells via an ApoE-LDLR pathway, enabling efficient siRNA delivery and inhibition of VEGFR3 expression.58 In parallel, studies have shown that syncytiotrophoblast-derived EVs (STBEVs) contain ApoE and are taken up by liver cells through an ApoE-LDL receptor interaction. This induces increased cholesterol release and upregulation of genes involved in lipid biosynthesis, suggesting a potential role for placental STBEVs in modulating maternal hepatic lipid metabolism in the presence of ApoE to support fetal growth.59

Therapeutic RNA cargo for brain diseases

The two most common biomaterials found inside EVs are proteins and RNAs. Various RNA cargos with distinct characteristics and therapeutic potentials have been investigated for their capacity to regulate gene expression and treat brain-related and neurodegenerative disorders, including glioblastoma, AD, PD, and multiple sclerosis (MS).60 Here, we will categorize therapeutic RNAs into two primary groups: those that function via the RNA interference (RNAi) pathway and those that do not (Figure 3). We will provide further details regarding the benefits and drawbacks of different types of RNA cargo, with a particular focus on therapeutic RNAs utilized for brain delivery. Summary of EVs and their therapeutic cargos

RNAi pathway

Functional genomic analysis and treatment development for the neurological system hold significant potential for the highly efficient and potentially precise technology called RNAi.80 The RNAi system, which involves the inhibition of specific gene expression by small RNA molecules is activated by double-stranded RNA molecules that are cleaved by an enzyme called Dicer into 21- to 23-nucleotide-long fragments.81 These siRNAs, which are produced downstream of the Dicer system, are incorporated into a multi-protein complex known as the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC). Within RISC, one strand of the siRNA or miRNA (the guide strand) remains bound to the complex, while the other strand (the passenger strand) is degraded. The Argonaute protein is a key component of RISC and is responsible for the catalytic activity of the complex. In the case of siRNAs, the guide strand of the siRNA directs RISC to its complementary mRNA target. Argonaute then induces cleavage of the target mRNA, leading to its degradation and thus silencing the gene. For miRNAs, the guide strand of the miRNA binds to partially complementary sites in the 3′ untranslated region of target mRNAs. Instead of cleaving the mRNA, miRNAs usually inhibit translation and/or promote degradation of the target mRNA. The two-nucleotide overhang is created by dicer processing of pre-miRNAs/small hairpin RNA (shRNAs). These are generally included in synthetic siRNAs to improve activity and RISC loading.

RNAi-based treatments provide the ability to suppress disease-causing proteins associated with a single gene. Indeed, specific genes involved in the pathophysiology of CNS-related diseases, such as neurodegenerative disorders and brain tumors, can be targeted by RNAi-based approaches.55 RNAi has demonstrated promising outcomes in preclinical and clinical investigations for many CNS-related disorders, including AD, PD, HD, and GBM.82,83,84 For instance, siRNAs that specifically target the β-amyloid protein, a key player in the development of amyloid plaques in AD, can decrease the buildup of this protein and enhance cognitive abilities in AD animal models.85,86 Furthermore, several studies conducted by Davidson’s team using RNAi for HD have yielded promising results.87 For example, they have shown that reducing both normal and mutant forms of HTT gene using RNAi in the striatum effectively prevents behavioral and neuropathological abnormalities in rodent models of HD.88 They have further investigated the safety of preclinical RNAi-based HTT suppression in rhesus macaque models, showing that a reduction of 45% in rhesus HTT expression within the mid and caudal putamen does not lead to motor impairments, neuronal degeneration, astrogliosis, or an immune reaction.89 These findings support the potential development of RNA-i-based therapies for HD in humans.

The key RNAs involved in the RNAi pathway encompass various types of small RNAs, including miRNAs, siRNAs, piwi-interacting RNAs, trans-acting siRNAs, small-scan RNAs, repeat-associated siRNAs, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and shRNAs (Figure 3).90,91

siRNAs

siRNA has demonstrated potential in the management of diverse neurological conditions, such as HD, by specifically targeting the mutant huntingtin gene and GBM.92,93 Nevertheless, successfully transporting siRNAs to the brain is challenging due to the existence of the BBB, which limits the entry of external substances into the brain. Furthermore, to attain therapeutic effectiveness, the siRNA needs to be administered in a precise and targeted manner to cell types within the brain. Jia et al. generated glioma-specific sEVs by incorporating superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles and curcumin into the EVs, and subsequently attached a neuropilin-1-targeted peptide to the EV membrane.61 The modified EVs exhibited efficient penetration of the BBB and effectively delivered targeted imaging and therapy for GBM in both cell cultures and in vivo orthotopic glioma models. Others have used brain endothelial cell-derived EVs as a way for transporting siRNA molecules across the BBB in zebrafish.62 This study showed that the EVs increased the uptake of siRNA in brain endothelial cells, suppressed the expression of VEGF in GBM-astrocytoma cells, improved the delivery of siRNA across the BBB in zebrafish, and decreased the intensity of fluorescence in xenotransplanted brain tumor cells.

Alvarez et al. employed modified dendritic cell-derived EVs that expressed the rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG) peptide and Lamp2b.63 In this study, small sEVs were administered into mice via systemic injection, leading to the successful delivery of GAPDH siRNA to the neurones, microglia, and oligodendrocytes in the brain. Recent work identified a comparable approach to specifically target neuroblastoma cells.94 However, the stability and capacity to control the uptake of unmodified siRNA, which is siRNA without any modifications or carriers, by target cells may limit its usefulness in vivo.95,96 In addition, Cooper et al. administered siRNA-loaded sEVS systemically to both normal mice and transgenic mice expressing a phosphorylation-mimic S129D α-synuclein (α-Syn).64 A significant decrease in both α-Syn mRNA and protein levels throughout the brain in both groups of mice was observed. It is worth noting that α-Syn aggregates are the main component of Lewy bodies in PD. Specifically, this EV-mediated therapy in transgenic mice resulted in significant decreases in intraneuronal protein aggregates, namely in dopaminergic neurones located in the substantia nigra. To effectively treat neurological disorders such as GBM using systemic delivery of unmodified siRNA, the approach must ensure stable and targeted siRNA delivery to brain cells, achieve significant gene silencing, and minimize off-target effects, while avoiding activation of the innate immune system or triggering inflammatory responses. Concerning the delivery of drugs to the brain by the systemic route, Lee et al. showed that the utilization of RNA nanoparticles conjugated with folate ligands (FA-pRNA-3WJ RNP) can deliver siRNA specifically to brain tumor cells in mice and human-derived GBM cells. This modification allows the nanoparticles to exploit the specific interaction between folate and its receptors on glioblastoma cells, facilitating targeted endocytosis. The conjugated nanoparticles demonstrated a strong ability to selectively bind and accumulate in FR-positive glioblastoma cells while minimizing uptake in adjacent normal brain cells.97

miRNAs

miRNAs are a distinct form of small RNA that have a crucial function in controlling gene expression by its interaction with target mRNA molecules, which results in the suppression of their translation. These molecules act as guiding agents that specifically target sequences, binding with the RISC to decrease the production of mRNAs. miRNAs have been linked to the development of many neurological diseases, such as AD and PD, which makes them a potential target for therapeutic treatment.98

While research has examined the expression profiling of miRNAs in brain-related illnesses, there is a scarcity of preclinical studies specifically investigating the delivery of therapeutic miRNAs to the brain. Work by Lang et al. utilized MSCs as natural factories to produce EVs that contained miR-124a (Exo-miR124) at quantities higher than that normally found in the body.65 The vitality and clonogenicity of glioblastoma stem cells were significantly reduced when treated with exosomes expressing miR-124a in vitro compared with the control group. Administering Exo-miR124 systemically to mice with intracranial glioma stem cells in vivo led to a 50% survival rate in the long term. In addition, other work has shown that sEVs decorated with T7-peptide effectively transported microRNA-21 ASO to the brain, leading to a substantial decrease in tumor size in an animal model of GBM.66 Monfared et al.67 demonstrated that modified sEVs containing a miR-21-sponge construct effectively suppressed the expression of miR-21 in glioma cell lines. This resulted in the activation of miR-21 target genes (PDCD4 and RECK), decreased cell growth, increased rates of programmed cell death, and, notably, a significant reduction in tumor size in a rat model of GBM. Furthermore, the utilization of marrow stromal cell sEVs containing anti-tumor miRNA (miR-146b) resulted in a notable decrease in the growth of glioma xenografts in a rat model.68 Studies have also demonstrated that even naturally occurring EVs carrying miRNA derived from glioma stem cells can elicit various gene regulatory reactions that ultimately result in the formation of new blood vessels (angiogenesis).99 Esteves et al. employed EVs derived from mononuclear cells of umbilical cord blood loaded with miR124-3p.69 Their findings demonstrated that EVs stimulate the growth of new neurones and provide protection to the nervous system. The study revealed that the sEVs exhibited a particular affinity for PD, targeting specific brain regions such the substantia nigra and striatum. Furthermore, the sEVs were observed to promote the development of NSCs into fully developed neurones, while simultaneously inhibiting the growth of immature cells.

lncRNAs

lncRNA refers to a specific form of RNA molecule that is longer than 200 bp and does not produce proteins but rather has important functions in regulating gene expression. lncRNA has been associated with a range of physiological processes, such as neural development, nuclear transport, chromatin alterations, and synaptic plasticity. In addition, they may act as regulators of programmed cell death, tumor migration, and drug resistance.100 Certain lncRNAs are present in high quantities within EVs. lncRNAs undergo a selective process to be spontaneously encapsulated within EVs, but the precise mechanisms responsible for this remain unclear. For instance, Michalik et al.101 observed that vascular endothelial cells in cerebral ischemia injury had elevated levels of various lncRNAs, such as linc00493, Meg3, and MALAT1. This study proposes that MALAT1 has a regulatory function in the process of angiogenesis, and the sorting of these lncRNAs occurs in a selective manner. Another study showed that long lncRNAs present in EVs produced from brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs) can regulate the function of glial cells after brain ischemia.102 Hence, EVs originating from BMECs and vascular endothelial cells that contain lncRNAs may be crucial in elucidating the mechanism underlying secondary damage resulting from cerebral ischemia. In summary, the transportation of lncRNAs to the brain via EVs holds great promise for therapeutic intervention and serves as a new means of intercellular communication. This approach could be applied in the treatment of neurological diseases and disorders.

shRNAs

shRNAs are a type of artificial RNA molecules frequently employed in molecular biology and biotechnology to suppress or reduce the activity of genes. They are engineered to replicate the architecture of naturally existing miRNA precursors. Cellular processing of shRNAs follows a similar mechanism as endogenous miRNAs.103 Typically, they are produced from DNA vectors as extended transcripts that encompass the shRNA sequence. Cellular enzymes, such as Dicer, convert these longer transcripts into mature shRNAs.104 After undergoing processing, the fully developed shRNA is integrated into the RISC, which then directs the shRNA to the target mRNA based on sequence complementarity. The interaction between the shRNA and the target mRNA causes the destruction of the mRNA or the inhibition of translation, leading to the suppression of gene expression. One interesting study developed small circles of genetic material called shRNA minicircles that specifically target α-Syn.70 These minicircles were then delivered intravenously into the brain of a transgenic mouse model expressing the phosphomimic human S129D α-Syn-HA under the prion promoter using RVG-sEVs. The findings showed that this approach effectively reduced the levels of α-Syn, prevented its clumping together, protected the neurones responsible for producing dopamine, and improved the clinical symptoms in a model of PD. Utilizing EVs carrying shRNA can offer a new therapeutic strategy for brain-related disorders.

Non-RNAi pathway

Circular RNAs

Circular RNAs (circRNAs), which are single-stranded RNAs that are covalently closed, have lately been identified as new players in gene regulation.71 circRNAs are formed via a molecular mechanism known as back-splicing, in which a 5′ splice site located downstream is connected to an upstream 3ʹ splice site.105 This process leads to the formation of a complete loop structure that is chemically bonded. Circularization confers resistance to exonucleases on circRNAs, resulting in enhanced stability relative to linear RNAs. circRNAs can function as miRNA sponges, by attaching to and sequestering miRNAs, so inhibiting their binding to their target mRNAs. This can modulate the expression of the target genes and potentially impact cellular processes such as differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis.106,107 These molecules can interact with RNA-binding proteins and impact alternative splicing or serve as platforms for the assembly of protein complexes. In some cases, circRNAs can also function as coding molecules for proteins, although this occurrence is infrequent.108

circRNAs have been implicated in various biological processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. Studies have demonstrated that circRNAs are abundant in the brain.105 The disruption of circRNAs has been linked to a range of diseases, such as cancer, neurological disorders, and cardiovascular diseases.108 Work by Yang et al.71 observed lower levels of circRNA SCMH1 (circSCMH1) in the blood of patients with acute ischemic stroke. This finding was confirmed in mouse models. The investigators then used modified RVG-circSCMH1-EVs to specifically transport circSCMH1 to the brain. This approach resulted in improved recovery after stroke in both rodents and nonhuman primates. The improvement was attributed to enhanced neuronal plasticity, reduced glial activation, and decreased infiltration of immune cells from the periphery. These findings suggest that circRNAs could be used as an RNA therapeutic for neurological disorders. Another study demonstrated that the transportation of circDYM to the brain through EVs, specifically RVG-circDYM-EVs, effectively alleviated depressive behaviors caused by chronic unpredictable stress in mice.72 This process is achieved by suppressing neuroinflammation through the circDYM-TAF1 axis, inhibiting microglial activation, preserving the integrity of the BBB, and reducing the infiltration of immune cells from the peripheral system.

mRNAs

A major component of the cellular machinery that produces proteins is mRNA. With the creation of mRNA vaccines against COVID-19, mRNA has attracted a lot of attention in recent years.109 To preserve and aid in its entry into cells, mRNA molecules in these COVID-19 vaccines are encapsulated in LNPs. LNP technology, although highly promising in several applications including medication delivery and gene therapy, does face certain obstacles. One issue pertains to the biocompatibility and potential toxicity of LNPs, as they can trigger immunological responses.110 The task of specifically targeting certain tissues continues to be intricate.111 and LNPs have challenges in surmounting delivery obstacles, including traversing the BBB. In the realm of neurological disorders, EVs differ from LNPs as they are able to penetrate the BBB, making them more suitable for treatment. The innate biocompatibility of EVs mitigates the potential for immunological reactions, thereby resolving one problem linked to LNPs. Moreover, EVs exhibit enhanced stability and can be specifically designed for targeting, rendering them a compelling choice for specific treatments in neurological diseases. The study of Kojima et al.73 focused on developing EXOsomal transfer into cells (EXOtic) to produce designer exosomes in engineered mammalian cells for targeted delivery of specific mRNA cargo. These exosomes are intended for precise delivery of specific mRNA cargo to the targeted cells.

These designer exosomes aimed at potential therapeutic interventions in PD. The EXOtic EVs enabled targeted mRNA delivery to recipient cells, resulting in functional outcomes such as luminescence in target cells expressing specific receptors. Biodistribution analysis revealed accumulation of exosomes in the brain, spleen, and liver, and could reduce neuroinflammation and deliver therapeutic mRNA to target cells in mouse models of PD.73 Similarly, cells were engineered to produce exosomes containing RNAs encoding HIV-1 repressor protein using the EXOtic system. These repressor-loaded exosomes effectively suppress HIV-1 expression, with the mechanism driven by DNA methylation of HIV-1 in humanized NSG mouse models. These findings suggest the potential of an exosome-mediated systemic delivery platform for therapeutically targeting and epigenetically repressing HIV-1 infection.74 Mizrak et al. demonstrated that genetically modified EVs containing the mRNA and protein of a suicide gene, specifically cytosine deaminase (CD) linked to uracil phosphoribosyltransferase (UPRT), effectively suppressed the growth of schwannoma tumors in live organisms. Another study demonstrated that the administration of EVs loaded with CD-UPRT effectively decreased tumor growth in mice.75 Collectively, the results indicate that EVs loaded with therapeutic CD-UPRT mRNA/protein have the potential to serve as a valuable tool for treating GBM.

Aptamers

Short, single-stranded DNA or RNA molecules known as aptamers can attach selectively to target molecules, which can include compounds, proteins, nucleic acids, or even entire cells. It is possible to create aptamers to have characteristics that allow them to pass through the BBB, have better CNS penetration and pharmacokinetics. In addition, aptamers can be coupled to therapeutic agents such as siRNAs, small compounds, or other medications based on nucleic acids. This conjugation makes it possible to deliver therapeutic cargo to the brain specifically, minimizing off-target effects and enhancing treatment efficacy overall. Here, we concentrate on RNA aptamers. Systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment is a method used to select their molecules. It involves iteratively enriching a random pool of nucleic acid sequences for those that bind the target with high affinity and specificity.112,113 However, Rahimi et al. conducted a study that specifically examined RNA aptamers that were developed to target oligomeric Aβ40, a protein that is linked to AD.114 The study discovered that these aptamers have a high level of sensitivity in detecting the production of amyloid fibrils. However, they have low specificity as they can recognize common amyloid structures in different amyloidogenic proteins. Under normal physiological conditions, it is difficult to choose aptamers that can selectively target oligomeric forms because they tend to attach non-specifically to amyloid fibrils. The results suggest that aptamers have the potential to be useful in identifying the production of β sheets, which could be beneficial in the diagnosis and treatment of AD. Aptamers are used in many different areas, including biosensing, medicines, and diagnostics. They can be applied as therapeutic agents, drug delivery systems, or molecular probes. Certain aptamers bind to proteins and prevent them from doing their intended functions, acting as antagonists. Others can be coupled to aptamers that bind to particular cell surface receptors to distribute medications in a targeted pathway.115 The potential of aptamers to treat a range of neurological diseases, such as brain tumors, neuroinflammatory syndromes, and neurodegenerative diseases, have been studied.116 Aptamers have proven to be useful for brain delivery in vivo.117 Target specificity and therapeutic efficacy have shown promise when aptamers are utilized to deliver therapeutic payloads to the brain of animals.116 EVs harboring a surface-bound aptamer that targets myelin significantly reduced demyelination and inflammatory cell infiltration into the CNS in an MS mouse model.79 The covalent conjugation of the nucleolin-targeting aptamer AS1411 to cholesterol-PEG demonstrated that grafting targeting ligands onto cells and generating ligand-functionalized EVs is a fast, simple, and cost-effective method for producing an adequate number of drug delivery vehicles.118

Challenges in delivering RNA therapeutics to the brain

Delivering RNA therapeutics to the brain presents several challenges due to both biological barriers and the complexities of the molecules themselves. One of the most significant hurdles is the BBB, which regulates the passage of substances from the bloodstream into the brain and restricts nearly 98% of small molecules and drugs.60

This barrier, along with active efflux transporters and tight junctions, limits the bioavailability of RNA therapies in the brain. Therefore, getting over the limitations of the BBB remains a major focus for the development of RNA treatment.

In addition to the BBB, the inherent instability of RNA molecules poses another obstacle. Naked RNA is rapidly degraded by nucleases in the bloodstream, making it difficult to reach the brain intact. To overcome these issues, nanoparticles have been developed to encapsulate and protect RNA, allowing it to cross both the bloodstream and the BBB.119

These nanoparticles can be engineered to carry therapeutic RNA as a payload, or they can have surface modifications that increase their ability to pass through barriers.

Once delivered, the next challenge is immunogenicity. RNA molecules encoding foreign or non-self-proteins may trigger an immune response, potentially diminishing their therapeutic effectiveness.120 Specificity in targeting is also crucial, as RNA therapies must be directed to particular brain cell populations while avoiding off-target effects.111 Another challenge is achieving appropriate therapeutic concentrations of RNA in the brain without causing toxicity. RNA can activate innate immune receptors, such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) 3 and 7 in the endosome and RIG-I-like receptors and melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 in the cytosol. While EVs can bypass the endocytic pathway to avoid TLR engagement, RNA in the cytosol may still trigger immune responses. However, siRNAs and shRNAs can be designed without immunostimulatory motifs to mitigate this risk.121 In addition, the cost and scalability of manufacturing RNA therapies will determine their accessibility in clinical settings. Developing reliable and efficient methods for large-scale production remains a challenge, particularly in the EV field.

Challenges with EV-based therapies

Although EV-based therapies have some drawbacks and safety issues, they have a lot of potential for a variety of medicinal applications, but there are issue to be solved (Table2).122 The biggest issue is the absence of standardized protocols for isolating EVs necessary to guarantee consistency and dependability in EV-based treatment methods. These include the issue of ensuring EV purity, how to scale up production, and their stability and storage. A major technical issue is that EVs have a low loading capacity, reducing their ability to transport RNA cargo effectively. To overcome the low loading capacity of EVs, techniques such as membrane fusion, sonication, or electroporation can be used to introduce more cargo into EVs. Also, modifications of the EV membrane such as surface engineering with cationic or ionizable lipids, such as DOTAP or SM-102, may enhance loading of RNA. Moreover, the use of new synthetic EVs (covered above) may overcome poor loading. These include the fusion of EV membranes with synthetic liposomes or other vesicles to increase the overall volume and surface area, or redesigning LNPs based on EV lipids, thereby enhancing the cargo capacity. Another challenge is achieving targeted delivery to specific cells or tissues because the biological heterogeneity of EVs results in variations in surface markers, size, and content. The RNA material may trigger unwanted cytosolic RNA sensor-based innate immune responses, especially the use of long RNAs. Potential solutions and future perspectives

Investigators in this field are actively examining a variety of approaches to get beyond current obstacles with the goal of successfully delivering RNA therapy to the brain using EVs. Engineering EVs to target more specifically by modifying their surfaces with ligands that help them connect with brain cell receptors is one of the main goals of this engineering effort. The intranasal route serves as an attractive non-invasive route for brain-targeted RNA therapies as it bypasses the BBB. Indeed, intranasally delivered EVs loaded with lincRNA-Cox2 siRNA is able to reduce lipopolysaccharide-induced microglial proliferation in the brains of mice to reduce lincRNA-Cox2 expression.139

Another challenge is optimizing regulated cargo release and increasing the loading capacity of EVs for RNA cargo. Techniques to improve the capacity of EVs to cross the BBB are also being investigated; these strategies make use of both specialized modifications and natural mechanisms. To fully utilize EVs as a therapeutic tool, immune responses and evading clearance processes must be addressed. For EV isolation and characterization procedures to be dependable and repeatable in studies and clinical settings, they must be standardized. Achieving a high level of EV purity for clinical use will pose further downstream manufacturing challenges. The use of a combination of current EV purification methods (e.g., ultracentrifugation, size-exclusion chromatography, and tangential flow filtration) as part of the downstream purification process is foreseeable. Large-scale manufacturing of EVs requires bioreactors with hundreds of liters of capacity. To further maximize EV numbers, one can use EV production “boosters,” such as connexin 43,34 in these engineered EV-producing cell lines.

Viral vectors have been used to deliver nucleic acid cargo into the brain. Adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) vectors with tropism for neurons have gained traction as an ideal viral platform for brain delivery. However, AAVs come with safety concerns including their immunogenicity and toxicity issues associated with AAV vectors, especially in the dorsal root ganglia.140 EVs have the benefit of being relatively inert and in some cases anti-inflammatory. Unlike AAVs, EVs can be modified to promote microenvironment modulation, resulting in neuroprotection and tissue regeneration. The advantages of EVs over AAVs for brain-mediated delivery has been extensively reviewed previously by Yom-Tov et al.141

Compared with synthetic vectors and nanoparticles, EVs potentially carry minimal toxicity effects, have better cargo protection, stability in circulation, and better endosomal escape. From a downstream manufacturing point, the manufacture of “synthetic” nanoparticles such as LNPs is more streamlined over naturally produced EVs. EV production is divided into two phases. Engineered cells have to be cultured and amplified before cells are grown in EV-depleted medium to allow EV production. Pre-production methodologies requiring modifying cells to make EVs loaded with RNA and imbued with organ-targeting capabilities may offer a more streamlined approach to post-production methods, where the RNA material is loaded once the EVs are produced and purified. Either way, EV production and purification over synthetic vectors and nanoparticle production carries considerable cost challenges. Therapeutic EV manufacturing costs will most likely override traditional synthetic vector and nanoparticle manufacturing costs owing to additional considerations, including conducting a virus clearance study related to EV properties).142

Despite the possibility of modified EVs to enter the BBB via peripheral circulation, it remains attractive to consider intracranially injecting EVs directly into the brain. Although highly invasive, it is by far the most effective method for delivering EVs into the brain.65 It also bypasses the need to deliver EVs through the BBB via peripheral blood circulation. It is noteworthy to mention that intranasal-mediated delivery can increase the brain delivery efficiency of EVs, as it can prolong the retention time of EVs in the brain. Indeed, EVs from mesenchymal stem cells have been shown to enter the brain when delivered intranasally.143

Prospective avenues for future research encompass merging EV-mediated RNA delivery with additional therapeutic modalities, creating in vivo imaging methods for continuous monitoring, and customizing personalized medicine strategies for individual patients. With the goal of clinical translation and better patient outcomes, these approaches present exciting opportunities for the creation of safe and efficient EV-based RNA therapies for a range of brain diseases.

Conclusion

In this review, we described the utilization of therapeutic RNAs and delivering them into the brain via EVs with the aim of treating neurological disorders, which is a new field of research these days. RNA cargo delivery into the brain may regulate gene expression and cellular function within the CNS. There are different types of therapeutic RNAs such as siRNA, miRNA, mRNA, and ASOs that can be delivered into the brain through the BBB by using EVs. The role of EVs involves the transportation of different cargos such as therapeutic RNAs. These membranous structures have a lipid bilayer and contain various macromolecules. EVs demonstrate subtypes based on size, which are sEVs and m/lEVs. As the brain is the most important part of the body, it is protected by a strict security system known as the BBB. EVs as potential carriers for brain-targeted drug delivery and specially sEVs, which can interact with the BBB, make them the best option for carrying a cargo into the brain. Some of the advantages of using sEVs for brain-targeted delivery are ability to cross physical barriers, and the ability of sEVs to carry the desired proteins from parental cells and transfer them to the host cells. Although, they have some disadvantages, which have been reported before, including immunogenicity due to the heterogeneity of EVs, these challenge production in large scale, are expensive, and there are no optimal isolation and purification methods. The future direction of therapeutic RNA cargo delivery into the brain by EVs could include examining various aspects for more effectiveness, safety, and translational potential. It should guide to continued development and improvement of this approach, contributing to the field’s overall advancement and potential clinical impact. In general, the use of this technique, especially considering the potential of EVs in crossing the BBB into the brain, can be very helpful for curing neurological disorders. However, it presents some challenges that require further research and investigation in this field. There are numerous questions and challenges, particularly regarding the long-term impacts of EVs that have crossed into the brain.

EV-based therapeutics for RNA delivery to the brain present potential opportunities for the development of new treatments for neurological illnesses. It is anticipated that developments in EV engineering for enhanced targeting, cargo loading optimization, and BBB penetration techniques would augment the selectivity and effectiveness of RNA delivery. Research dependability and translational success will benefit from standardizing EV isolation and characterization protocols. Further options for innovation include exploring patient-specific procedures, creating in vivo imaging tools, and combining EV-based RNA delivery with other medicines. All things considered, these developments portend the advent of tailored, safe, and efficient RNA therapies, which will change the direction of neurological therapeutic research in the future.
