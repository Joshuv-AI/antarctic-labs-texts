Behavioural economics intervention to implement a nutrition ranking system in food pantries: Be Well cluster randomised controlled trial protocol
Authors: Pamela D Perez, Katherine C Faulkner, Yingfei Wu, Marlene B Schwartz, Caitlin Caspi, Rachel Burgun, Laura Ortiz, Jenny Jia, Jessica Cheng, Yuchiao Chang, Douglas E Levy, Anne N Thorndike
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFood pantries are critical for addressing food insecurity, but dietary quality of food offered by pantries and selected by clients is often low. Supporting Wellness at Pantries (SWAP) is an evidence-based nutrition labelling strategy (eg, green=choose often; red=choose rarely) that has not been adopted widely due to implementation barriers. Behavioural economics (BE) strategies could help overcome these barriers.Methods and analysisThe Be Well Study is testing a multicomponent BE intervention to increase implementation of SWAP in a 12-month cluster randomised controlled trial, followed by 12-month extended follow-up, in 30 pantries affiliated with a large food bank in eastern Massachusetts. During study months 1–12, intervention pantries receive the BE-enhanced SWAP implementation strategy including: SWAP toolkits, SWAP invoice labelling, dietitian-led learning communities, implementation incentives and the opportunity to earn a seal of approval. Control pantries receive basic SWAP information and invoice labelling. During extended follow-up (months 13–24), control pantries receive the BE intervention. Outcomes are assessed at the pantry (n=30) and client (n=3750) levels using pantry audits, food bank ordering data, client surveys and client basket audits and include changes from baseline to 6 and 12 (primary) months in: SWAP implementation, percent green-labelled foods ordered by pantries and selected by clients, and client dietary quality. During extended follow-up, these outcomes are assessed at 18 and 24 months. Be Well is testing strategies to overcome implementation barriers for healthy eating interventions in the charitable food system. Findings will inform future interventions to support pantry clients’ selection and consumption of healthier food.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval was obtained from the institutional review board of Mass General Brigham (Protocols 2024P000832). Informed consent will be obtained from all participants. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals, conferences and summaries shared with national and local stakeholders in the charitable food system.Trial registration numberNCT06298253.

Introduction

Food insecurity, defined as limited or uncertain access to food, is associated with higher cardiometabolic disease risk.114 The US charitable food system is a network of privately run food banks and pantries supported by federal, state and private funds.15 Centralised food banks distribute food to local food pantries, providing a critical resource for people experiencing food insecurity.1618 Pantries serve as sites where individuals in the community can obtain groceries free of charge, generally as a supplement to other food sources.18 The quality of food offered in pantries and consumed by clients often does not align with US dietary guidelines, suggesting strategies are needed to encourage healthier food choice in this setting.1923

Prior research has demonstrated that behavioural economics (BE) strategies promote healthier food choices.152434 BE leverages psychological insights about mental shortcuts, or heuristics, that influence, and often bias, decision making.35 36 BE strategies to overcome these biases include choice architecture, framing, social norms and incentives.35 36 Choice architecture interventions alter how a set of options, particularly default options, are presented to leverage present-biased preferences and status quo bias, influencing people’s choices in predictable ways. In the charitable food system, choice architecture strategies might involve changing food product placement and prominence to encourage the selection of certain foods.37 Framing provides cues to make a decision more salient to an individual and might take the form of traffic light nutrition labelling (ie, green=choose often; yellow=choose sometimes; red=choose rarely).15 31 Social norms interventions compare an individual’s performance or behaviour to their peers, with the goal of encouraging behaviour change.30 In the charitable food system, these might include feedback comparing a food pantry’s implementation of healthy food initiatives with other pantries in their networks. Incentives provide small financial rewards, recognition or other non-monetary benefits to reward desired behaviour change.35 36 Financial incentives have been shown to increase healthy food choice behaviour in pantries and other settings, such as food retailers and workplace cafeterias.28 30 33

Supporting Wellness at Pantries (SWAP) is a traffic light nutrition ranking and labelling programme that uses evidence-based nutrition guidelines developed for the charitable food system.15 Food banks can use SWAP on ordering systems to encourage pantries to order healthier foods.15 31 38 39 Pantries can purchase SWAP toolkits, consisting of educational materials, labels, posters and handouts, that provide instruction on using the traffic light labelling and another BE strategy, product placement (choice architecture), to guide clients in choosing healthier foods. While nutrition ranking programmes like SWAP have been shown to improve the healthfulness of pantry orders, inventories and clients’ food selection, widespread use of SWAP and other similar programmes has been limited due to logistical challenges, time constraints, competing priorities, lack of space and lack of knowledge.38 40 41 Novel interventions to improve the implementation and maintenance of nutrition ranking by food pantries could have a large impact on improving diet and health for food pantry clients.4244

The Be Well study is a randomised controlled trial (RCT) of 30 food pantries affiliated with a large food bank in Massachusetts that is testing whether a multicomponent BE intervention encourages pantries to implement SWAP and order healthier foods from the food bank, thus improving the healthfulness of foods obtained by clients. Results will inform future strategies in the charitable food system to improve nutrition security and health for millions of US households that use food pantries.

Methods

Study design

This study is a 12-month cluster RCT, with a 12-month extended follow-up period, to determine the effectiveness of a multicomponent BE intervention designed to encourage food pantries to implement SWAP (figure 1). In October 2024, the study began enrolling and randomising 30 food pantries (clusters in this design) affiliated with the Greater Boston Food Bank (GBFB), a large food bank in Massachusetts. All pantries are maximum choice (also called full choice), meaning clients may freely choose food items, similar to a grocery store.45 In the cluster RCT (months 1–12), pantries are randomised to either the intervention (BE-enhanced SWAP implementation strategy, n=15) or control (SWAP information only, n=15). In the extended follow-up period (months 13–24), pantries initially assigned to the intervention will no longer receive the BE intervention and will be monitored to evaluate SWAP maintenance, and pantries initially assigned to the control will start receiving the BE-enhanced SWAP implementation strategy. Outcomes will be examined at 6, 12, 18 and 24 months at both the pantry (food bank ordering data, pantry audits) and client (client surveys, basket inspections) levels. All data collection will be completed by May 2027. This trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06298253) on 1 March 2024.

Intervention

Pretrial pantry staff interviews

Prior to starting recruitment, investigators (ANT, DEL) conducted semistructured interviews from February to May 2024 with staff from 11 GBFB-affiliated food pantries to solicit feedback about the planned intervention (online supplemental document 1). Pantry staff who were 18 years or older and had a leadership role were invited by email from GBFB staff to participate. Interviews were recorded and professionally transcribed.

A rapid qualitative analysis of interview transcripts was conducted to refine the planned intervention and ensure its acceptability to maximum choice food pantries with a broad range of characteristics. By reviewing findings in real time, the study team was able to integrate stakeholder feedback into the study design.4649 Interview transcripts and notes were reviewed using structured templates and organised into an analytic matrix to capture patterns and key themes, tracked across participant responses. A team-based analytic approach with two trained study staff ensured consensus on thematic categories, and regular meetings with lead investigators (ANT and DEL) were held to resolve discrepancies.4649 Table 1 summarises the themes, providing representative quotes that informed final intervention components.

Cluster RCT (months 1–12): intervention arm

The BE-enhanced intervention consists of five components (SWAP toolkit, invoice labelling, learning community, SWAP implementation incentive and Healthy Pantry Seal of Approval) delivered over a 12-month period to support SWAP implementation in food pantries (table 2).

SWAP Toolkit (BE strategy: choice architecture): In the first month of the study, intervention pantries will automatically (ie, by default) receive SWAP Toolkits to guide and assist in SWAP implementation.15 38 50 Toolkits include green, yellow and red shelf labels, signage and informational and instructional materials in English and Spanish. In pretrial interviews, staff expressed enthusiasm for SWAP as a way to support their clients in making healthier food choices. However, they also expressed some concerns about the potential impact on their operations and suggested that additional financial support to implement changes would be helpful (table 1).

Invoice labelling (BE strategy: choice architecture): In pretrial interviews, pantries indicated that invoice labelling would be helpful for staff stocking pantry shelves (table 1). As a result, GBFB invoices will rank items with traffic light colours, making it easier for pantries to assign shelf labels accurately and efficiently. Due to logistical challenges of implementing this for only intervention pantries, GBFB will initiate invoice labelling for all affiliated pantries shortly before the study begins, a change that will remain in place throughout the 24-month study period. Intervention pantries will receive enhanced support for interpreting and using the invoice labelling to implement SWAP through the learning community.

Learning Community (BE strategy: social norms): In pretrial interviews, pantry staff expressed eagerness to learn and collaborate with other pantries through learning communities (table 1). The study team collaborated with GBFB dietitians (RB and LO) to develop an 11-session monthly learning community curriculum to train pantry staff about SWAP and healthy food choices (online supplemental table 1). There will be three cohorts of five intervention pantry staff that participate together in 1-hour monthly videoconference sessions led by a GBFB registered dietitian (LO). Sessions will include approximately 30 min of didactics, focused on SWAP principles, successful implementation practices and relevant health-related topics, with the last 30 min for group discussion in which pantry staff share knowledge and troubleshoot SWAP implementation.

SWAP Implementation Incentive (BE strategy: incentives): Pretrial interviews highlighted the need for flexibility in the types of incentives offered (table 1). The incentive implementation strategy was adapted to allow pantries to choose either of two options. In option 1, pantries are able to use US$250 of a US$500 incentive for two ‘SWAP consultations’ (one in-person, one remote) with the GBFB dietitian, and then use the remaining US$250 for additional materials to assist with SWAP implementation (eg, shelving, refrigeration, lighting and signage). In option 2, pantries are able to use US$500 to purchase SWAP implementation materials. Pantries must use funds before the end of the 12-month intervention period.

Food Bank Healthy Seal of Approval (BE strategy: social norms): Pantries will receive the food bank Healthy Pantry Seal of Approval if they fulfil specific criteria for successful SWAP implementation and specific efforts to promote healthier food choices. Supported by feedback from pretrial interviews (table 1), pantries that earn the seal will be encouraged to highlight this recognition in their marketing and fundraising materials for clients and donors. To obtain the Seal of Approval, pantries must score at least 15 out of 25 points based on four components: learning community attendance (10 points), food prominence (5 points), SWAP labelling accuracy (5 points) and SWAP signage presence (5 points) (online supplemental figure 1). The study team will track learning community attendance; the remaining categories will be scored using pantry audit data (see Pantry-level measures, Pantry audits and online supplemental figures 2 and 3). A preliminary assessment at the 6-month site visit will provide pantries with feedback on their progress, and final assessments at 12 months will determine if the pantry achieves the Seal of Approval.

Cluster RCT (months 1–12): control arm

Pantries assigned to control will be provided with minimal details about the study intervention at baseline, but will be aware they will receive the intervention at the end of 12 months. Control pantries will be provided with basic information about SWAP, including how to purchase SWAP toolkits online (cost US$150, https://swapmaterials.org/). Any toolkit purchases made by control pantries during months 1–12 will not be eligible for retroactive reimbursement during months 13–24, when they receive the intervention (see below). As described above (see Cluster RCT (months 1–12): intervention arm, Invoice labelling), control pantries also receive invoice labelling that ranks items as red, yellow or green. Although it is possible the invoice labels could influence food ordering decisions, control pantries will not be offered assistance with implementing SWAP or interpreting invoice labelling.

Extended follow-up (months 13–24): control pantries receive intervention

Pantries initially assigned to the control arm will receive the intervention, while pantries initially assigned to the intervention arm will stop receiving the intervention (except for invoice labelling). Intervention components during follow-up may be refined based on feedback obtained from previous learning community discussions in months 1–12. Assessments will continue for all pantries during extended follow-up.

Participants and recruitment

Pantry recruitment

Prior to the study start, GBFB had 295 food pantries. GBFB partner pantries are eligible for study participation if they are a maximum choice pantry, obtain at least 40% of their food from GBFB and are located within approximately 1 hour of Boston. Pantries are recruited using several strategies, including GBFB system-wide outreach emails, monthly GBFB videoconferences, in-person meetings with pantries and personal contacts from GBFB staff. All pantries are contacted at least twice until they communicate their decision. Directors from eligible and interested pantries sign a Memorandum of Understanding between the pantry, Massachusetts General Hospital and GBFB to finalise enrolment. Pantries will receive participation remuneration of US$500 for each time point in which they participate (0, 6, 12, 18 and 24 months), totalling US$2500 over the study.

Pantry randomisation

A covariate-constrained randomisation approach51 will be used based on the following pantry characteristics: number of clients served per month (≤300, 301–500, ≥500), client shopping experience (in-person vs menu ordering), use of The Emergency Food Assistance Programme, pre-enrolment SWAP use, proportion of English-speaking and Spanish-speaking clients, proportion of pantry food obtained from GBFB, proportion of food ordered in the prior year that was labelled green, and average pounds of food ordered per month. Information on randomisation covariates is obtained from pre-enrolment pantry interest forms, GBFB order history and direct communication with pantry staff. After generating all possible combinations of 15 pantries per study arm, the combination achieving the best balance of pantry characteristics and baseline purchasing patterns across arms will be determined, defined as an absolute difference within 10%. The randomisation assignments will be determined by the study statistician (YC) who is blinded to pantry identifiers. One of the two groups of 15 will be randomly assigned to the intervention arm. For scheduling and staffing purposes, pantry start dates will be staggered over a 6-month period: each month, five pantries (two or three intervention and two or three control pantries) will begin the study, allowing all 30 pantries to be initiated within the first 6 months.

Client recruitment

Pantry clients will be enrolled from each pantry at 0 (baseline), 6, 12 (end of cluster RCT), 18 and 24 (end of extended follow-up) months. Bilingual study staff will recruit 25 clients from each pantry at each time point. Clients are eligible to participate if they are aged 18 years or older and speak English or Spanish. Study staff will provide and review a study fact sheet with interested clients and obtain verbal consent (online supplemental document 2). Total participation time is estimated at 20 min. There will be 750 clients enrolled at each time point, and clients will not be followed longitudinally. Clients will receive a US$25 grocery store gift card for participation.

Measures and outcomes

Pantry-level measures

ShopGBFB data: Pantries obtain their food from GBFB by ordering through an online platform called ShopGBFB, which maintains an updated list of available items with SWAP labels. The proportion of ordered items that are labelled green, yellow or red by weight (pounds) will be calculated from ShopGBFB ordering data. Weight is used rather than item count because pounds are the most common reporting metric used by food banks and better reflect the volume of food distributed.52 Previous research has demonstrated that SWAP rankings of client food basket contents by weight are associated with the dietary quality of those baskets.53

Pantry audits: The pantry audit tool was adapted from a prior RCT assessing healthy food choices in food pantries.37 To complete the audit, the study team will conduct photo documentation of the food pantry, capturing the nutrition ranking signage and messaging, the pantry layout, and a comprehensive inventory of items available that day. Pantry items are classified into major food groups (eg, fruits and vegetables, grains, protein) for the audits to account for changes in pantry inventory over time. Study team members will complete standardised checklists identifying the following: (a) SWAP implementation: presence of signage or other messaging to educate clients about SWAP and the proportion of pantry shelves with a SWAP label; (b) SWAP labelling accuracy: proportion of labelled items with the correct label; (c) healthy food prominence: for example, foods with green labels are at the front of the pantry, located at eye level or another visible location for clients and (d) de-emphasised unhealthy food prominence: for example, foods with red labels are at the back of the pantry or bottom shelf (online supplemental figure 2). The pantry audit score ranges from 0 to 100 points based on the following components: SWAP implementation (40%), SWAP labelling accuracy (40%), healthy food prominence (12%) and non-prominent placement of unhealthy foods (8%). A sample pantry audit assessment is included in online supplemental figure 3. The pantry audit will be completed at each data collection time point (table 3). At 6 and 12 months, intervention group pantry audits will inform Seal of Approval progress and awards, respectively (online supplemental figure 1).

Pantry surveys: At 12 months and 24 months, a staff member responsible for ordering and/or stocking food at each pantry will be asked to complete a detailed survey assessing the pantry’s experiences, if any, with the SWAP programme. In both study arms, the survey will ask respondents’ attitudes about the utility of the SWAP intervention, barriers and facilitators to implementation, and their impressions of client experiences with pantry changes. Intervention pantry staff will be asked to rate the usefulness of the intervention components and the impact of the SWAP programme on their operations.

Pantry interviews: Intervention pantry staff will participate in in-depth interviews at 12 months (cluster RCT) and 24 months (extended follow-up) about their experiences with the intervention and SWAP implementation. Interview guides will be informed by preliminary analysis of each pantry’s data prior to interviews (pantry audits, participation in Learning Community sessions, SWAP implementation incentive usage), as well as experiences and observations of the study team. Interviews will explore barriers and facilitators of successful implementation and engage participants in a conversation about future intervention adaptations.

Client-level measures

Basket inspections: Each client participant will have their basket inspected for items obtained at the pantry visit. Basket inspection methodology includes carefully unpacking, photographing, weighing and repacking the client’s groceries.22 37 54 Items will be counted and assigned appropriate SWAP traffic light labels.32 This will be conducted concurrently with the other client-level measures to minimise participant burden.

Veggie Meter readings: To objectively measure clients’ recent fruit and vegetable consumption over the prior 2 months, participants will have skin carotenoid levels assessed by finger scan using the Veggie Meter (Longevity Link Corporation, Salt Lake City, Utah, USA). The Veggie Meter uses an LED light to measure carotenoid levels, by taking three measurements in 45 s,55 56 and has been validated at food pantries and among individuals of varying races, ethnicities and skin tones.5660

Client surveys: After completing their shopping, participating clients will complete a survey (in English or Spanish) that includes questions about: demographics (including self-reported race and ethnicity); food and housing insecurity; whether they noticed nutritional messaging in the pantry; frequency and recency of pantry visits; and use of federal food assistance programmes and other food pantries (table 3). Clients are also asked their height, weight and chronic health conditions (eg, diabetes, cardiovascular disease). The survey includes the 13-item rapid Prime Diet Quality Score screener (rPDQS), a validated brief dietary screening tool.61

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes include skin carotenoid levels measured using the Veggie Meter (range 0–800 units, higher scores indicate higher carotenoid levels) and the SWAP implementation audit score measured using the pantry audit tool (range 0–100 points, higher scores indicate more successful SWAP implementation) (online supplemental figure 2). All 6-month pantry and client outcomes will also be assessed in exploratory analyses.

Data analysis

Intervention versus control arms

For outcomes assessed at the pantry level, a Fisher’s exact test will be used to compare the proportion of pantries with successful SWAP implementation, and two-sample t-tests will be used to compare changes in percent green (or red) items ordered and the SWAP implementation pantry audit score between the two arms. For all outcomes assessed at the client level, mixed effect models will be used to compare food choices and dietary quality score between arms, with pantry as a random effect to account for clustering within pantries. Complete case analysis will be used if <5% of data are missing. Otherwise, multiple imputation or sensitivity analyses will be conducted, and results from best-case and worst-case scenarios will be reported.62

Maintenance postintervention

For pantries assigned to the intervention during the cluster RCT, maintenance of the intervention effect will be assessed by comparing outcomes assessed at 12 months to outcomes at 18 and 24 months. For outcomes assessed at the pantry level, a McNemar exact test will be used to compare the proportion of pantries with successful SWAP implementation, and paired t-tests will be used to compare changes in percent green (or red) items ordered and the SWAP implementation pantry audit score between any two time points. For all outcomes assessed at the client level, mixed effect models will be used to compare food choices and dietary quality score between time points, with pantry as a random effect to account for clustering within pantries. Complete case analysis will be used if <5% of data are missing. Otherwise, multiple imputation or sensitivity analyses will be conducted, as described above.

Sample size and power

The total sample size at the pantry level for this study is 30; this was originally determined based on an internal GBFB assessment that about one-third of the estimated 114 maximum choice pantries would accept the recruitment invitation. The study will have 89% power to detect a difference of 53% (ie, n=8 pantries) that successfully implement SWAP between control and intervention arms (eg, successful SWAP implementation in 2 control vs 10 intervention pantries, as defined in Section Primary outcomes). A prior study showed that 53.5% of foods stocked by weight at GBFB pantries were labelled green, with a SD of 11.4%.63 For the percent of foods labelled green at pantries, the current study will have 80% power to detect a 12.1% mean difference with a two-sided significance level of 0.05, anticipating that 1–2 control pantries and 10–15 intervention pantries will implement SWAP. The total sample size for outcomes assessed at the client level is 750 at each time point. For percent green or red labelled foods, dietary quality or Veggie Meter score, the study will have 80% power to detect a small, standardised effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.21.

Dissemination

Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals, conferences and summaries shared with national and local stakeholders in the charitable food system.

Data collection is ongoing. Summary findings from the pretrial interviews are included in the manuscript and supplementary materials. Deidentified survey and observational data will be available from the corresponding author on reasonable request after study completion and ethics approval.

Discussion

This cluster RCT tests a multicomponent intervention delivered at the organisation level that uses BE-enhanced strategies to promote the implementation of an evidence-based nutrition ranking system (SWAP) in food pantries. Be Well encourages food pantries to implement the SWAP programme with the goal of accurately using SWAP labels to increase the healthfulness of food offered by pantries and selected by pantry clients, thereby improving clients’ dietary quality.

Previous interventions using BE strategies at the point of service, such as traffic light labelling, choice architecture, social norms and incentives, have been effective for promoting healthy food choices in workplace and community settings.2430 In a workplace cafeteria RCT, a multicomponent BE intervention improved employees’ purchases of green-labelled foods by 7.3% (95% CI 5.4% to 9.3%) and decreased red-labelled purchases by 3.9% (95% CI −5.0% to −2.7%) during a 12-month intervention period, and these changes were maintained over 12 months postintervention.30 However, the use of BE strategies in pantries remains limited, and few studies have tested pantry-level multicomponent BE interventions to improve the implementation of evidence-based programmes like SWAP that could increase healthy food choices of millions of food pantry clients.39 64 65 Be Well expands on prior studies by using choice architecture, social norms and incentives at the pantry level to encourage them to adopt and implement SWAP independently. This strategy better reflects real-world applications and offers insights into how minimal nudging and support can drive organisational change.

Be Well’s design prioritises flexibility and collaboration with Massachusetts’ largest food bank and local food pantry stakeholders, ensuring the study is relevant to and developed with input from the communities it aims to support. Insights from pretrial qualitative interviews with pantry staff helped tailor Be Well to address the specific challenges that food pantries across Massachusetts face when implementing nutrition ranking or other healthy eating interventions. These interviews informed the intervention prior to launch, helping build trust, strengthen partnerships and improve the likelihood of successful implementation.66 67 Pantry staff interviews and surveys, feedback from learning community sessions and client surveys throughout the trial also reinforce the importance of stakeholder input and support. This community-focused approach enhances the study’s potential for scalability by demonstrating how active engagement with stakeholders can support implementation of programmes like SWAP across communities.

Study limitations should be considered. Implementing an intervention across diverse food pantries is challenging, as no single intervention will fit all settings. Pantry-level variables, such as size, staffing structure, staff nutrition knowledge and healthy food availability, will likely influence the success of implementation and engagement, such as food labelling processes.38 40 41 Individual-level variables, such as language, culture and education, may affect pantry clients’ understanding and adoption of healthy food choices.68 GBFB had been using the SWAP nutrition labels on its ordering platform prior to the study, so participating pantries might be more familiar with nutrition ranking systems than is typical.63 To address this, pantry randomisation is designed to ensure balance across study arms with respect to pantries’ prior experiences with SWAP. Additionally, because this study includes a relatively small sample of food pantries affiliated with one food bank in Massachusetts, findings may not be generalisable to pantries in other locations.

Be Well is an innovative intervention in the charitable food system that combines multiple evidence-based BE strategies to achieve sustainable changes within food pantries. Through a flexible approach that addresses challenges faced by food pantries when implementing nutrition ranking systems, this intervention is designed to be disseminable across the broad spectrum of pantries in the charitable food system.
