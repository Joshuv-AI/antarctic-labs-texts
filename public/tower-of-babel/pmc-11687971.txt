Unexpected Severe Acute Abdominal Pain in a Hemodialysis Patient
Authors: Takashi Uzu, Shota Hirai, Toshiya Nishigaito
Published: 2024

Case Description

A 69-year-old man coughed for 30 minutes while in a sitting position after he completed the hemodialysis session and complained of sudden severe right upper abdominal pain. He had been receiving maintenance hemodialysis through a left arteriovenous fistula for 7 years without complications. He had no history of bleeding disorders. According to the dialysis unit protocol, he received a bolus of 1000 IU of unfractionated heparin at the start of every hemodialysis session, followed by maintenance infusion of 500 IU/h. The patient was not treated with oral anticoagulants or antiplatelet agents. His body weight was 59 kg, and his BP was 120/70 mm Hg. A tender right-sided upper abdominal mass was noted. The patient had no rebound tenderness. Carnett sign was positive, indicating that the abdominal wall was the source of pain. Abdominal computed tomography 2 weeks before presentation performed to screen for cancer showed no tumor on the abdominal wall (Figure 1A). However, a spindle-shaped mass in the area of the right upper rectus abdominis muscle was found on abdominal computed tomography performed immediately after the patient presented with abdominal pain (Figure 1, B and C). These findings revealed a rectus sheath hematoma. A laboratory analysis revealed hemoglobin, 10.3 g/dl; white blood cells, 8830/μl; platelets, 18.5×104/μl; prothrombin time, 1.13 (International Normalized Ratio); activated partial thromboplastin time, 29.1 seconds; and fibrinogen, 276 mg/dl. The patient was hemodynamically stable with no evidence of an expanding hematoma. The patient received conservative treatment with bedrest. The following morning, he was hemodynamically stable, and his hematoma was reduced in size.

(B and C) A heterogeneous spindle-shaped mass (arrows) appeared in the upper right rectus muscle immediately after the patient presented with abdominal pain on axial and sagittal CT scans. CT, computed tomography.

Rectus sheath hematoma is a relatively rare condition caused by rupture of the epigastric arteries or direct damage to the rectus muscle. From case reports and small case series, female sex, older age, CKD, anticoagulation therapy, cough, and abdominal trauma have been reported to be risk factors.1 The clinical course of rectus sheath hematoma is usually self-limiting, but some cases require radiological and/or surgical intervention.2,3 The blood supply to the abdominal muscles is derived from the superior and inferior epigastric arteries, and lower rectus sheath hematoma is reported to be more serious than upper rectus sheath hematoma.1,4 The patient developed upper rectus sheath hematoma, and his clinical course was good with rest alone. Most hemodialysis patients receive systemic anticoagulation therapy during hemodialysis sessions, which increases risk of bleeding. Therefore, nephrologists should add rectus sheath hematoma to the list of differential diagnoses for acute abdominal pain in hemodialysis patients.

Teaching Points
