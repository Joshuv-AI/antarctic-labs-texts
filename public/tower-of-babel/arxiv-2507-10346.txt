Diffusion Monte Carlo calculation of compact T c ​ s ​ 0 T_{cs0} and T c ​ s ¯ ​ 0 T_{c\bar{s}0} tetraquarks
M.C. Gordillo, J. Segovia

Abstract
The LHCb collaboration amplitude analysis of the decays B + → D + ​ D − ​ K + B^{+}\to D^{+}D^{-}K^{+} , B + → D − ​ D s + ​ π + B^{+}\to D^{-}D_{s}^{+}\pi^{+} , and B 0 → D ¯ 0 ​ D s + ​ π − B^{0}\to\bar{D}^{0}D_{s}^{+}\pi^{-} suggested the existence of two new resonant J P = 0 + J^{P}=0^{+} states with minimum quark content u ¯ ​ d ¯ ​ s ​ c \bar{u}\bar{d}sc and s ¯ ​ u ¯ ​ d ​ c \bar{s}\bar{u}dc / s ¯ ​ d ¯ ​ u ​ c \bar{s}\bar{d}uc , named T c ​ s ​ 0 T_{cs0} and T c ​ s ¯ ​ 0 T_{c\bar{s}0} , respectively. In this work, we used the diffusion Monte Carlo (DMC) method to compute the masses of those tetraquarks within the framework of the constituent quark model. We describe the systems as compact structures, not as diquark/antidiquark or meson/meson arrangements. This allows us to deduce the existence of two distinct bound configurations for each composition, corresponding to ground and excited flavor states. In both cases, the excited flavor states are the ones with masses comparable to the ones observed by LHCb. In addition, our analysis allows us to assign unambiguously an isospin value of I I =1 to the experimentally obtained T c ​ s ​ 0 T_{cs0} tetraquark.

This approach allows us to reduce the uncertainty of the numerical calculation and accounts for multi-particle correlations in the physical observables. The potential model used

This allows us to consider both the ground and excited flavor functions for the entire

2
Formalism

Within the framework of the quark model, a system containing
N
quarks (in our case,
N
=4) can be described by a non-relativistic Hamiltonian of the form:

H=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(m_{i}+\frac{p^{\,2}_{i}}{2m_{i}}\right)-T_{\text{CM}}+\sum_{i<j}^{N}V(r_{ij})\,,

(3)

where
m_{i}
is the mass of quark
i
,
\vec{p}_{i}
its momentum, and
T_{\text{CM}}
corresponds to the kinetic energy of the center of mass of the tetraquark.
V(r_{ij})
is a phenomenological two-body potential whose
defining parameters are obtained by a fit to experimental properties of mesons and baryons. In this work, we used the so-called AL1 potential as defined in Refs.
Semay and Silvestre-Brac 1994
;
Silvestre-Brac 1996
:

V(\vec{r}_{ij})=V_{\text{OGE}}(r_{ij})+V_{\text{CON}}(r_{ij})\,.

(4)

The one-gluon exchange (OGE) potential is given by:

\displaystyle V_{\text{OGE}}(r_{ij})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\alpha_{s}\vec{\lambda_{i}}\cdot\vec{\lambda_{j}})\Bigg[\frac{1}{r_{ij}}

\displaystyle-\frac{2\pi}{3m_{i}m_{j}}\delta^{(3)}(r_{ij})\vec{\sigma_{i}}\cdot\vec{\sigma_{j}})\Bigg]\,,

(5)

where
\alpha_{s}
is the strong coupling constant,
\vec{\lambda_{i}}
are the set of SU(3)-
color
Gell-Mann matrices,
\vec{\sigma_{i}}
denote the Pauli spin matrices, and the
\delta^{(3)}(r_{ij})
, as customary, is replaced by:

\delta^{(3)}(r_{ij})\to\kappa\,\frac{e^{-r_{ij}^{2}/r_{0}^{2}}}{\pi^{3/2}r_{0}^{3}}\,,

(6)

with
r_{0}=A\left(\frac{2m_{i}m_{j}}{m_{i}+m_{j}}\right)^{-B}
. On the other hand, the expression for the confining part of the potential is:

V_{\text{CON}}(r_{ij})=(b\,r_{ij}+\Delta)(\vec{\lambda_{i}}\cdot\vec{\lambda_{j}})\,,

(7)

where
b
is the confinement strength and
\Delta
is a global constant fixing the origin of energies. All the parameters in the above expressions for the AL1 potential are given in Table
1
. This potential does not include flavor-dependent interactions, i.e., there is not a term in the Hamiltonian depending on the SU(3)-
flavor
Gell-Mann matrices, that degree of freedom entering only indirectly in the description of the tetraquarks via the symmetry of the total wave function. This is a reasonable approximation that reproduce the experimental masses
of baryons made of
u
,
d
and
s

J.M. Richard 2017

To solve the Schrodinger equation derived from the Hamiltonian in Eq.
3
, we used the
diffusion Monte Carlo (DMC) method
Boronat and Casulleras 1994
. To do so, we write that equation as a standard diffusion equation in imaginary time,
t

(
\hbar=c=1
):

-\frac{\partial\Psi_{\alpha^{\prime}}(\bf{R},t)}{\partial t}=(H_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}-E_{s})\Psi_{\alpha}(\bf{R},t)\,,

(8)

where
E_{s}
is the usual energy shift used in DMC methods,
\bf{R}\equiv(\vec{r}_{1},\ldots,\vec{r}_{N})
stands for the position of the
N
particles and
\alpha
denotes each possible spin-flavor-color channel, with
defined quantum numbers
. The function
\Psi_{\alpha}(\bf{R},t)
can be expanded in terms of a complete set of the Hamiltonian’s eigenfunctions as

\Psi_{\alpha}(\bf{R},t)=\sum_{i}c_{i,\alpha}\,e^{-(E_{i}-E_{s})t}\,\Phi_{i,\alpha}(\bf{R})\,,

(9)

where the
E_{i}
are the eigenvalues of the system’s Hamiltonian operator. The ground state wave function,
\phi_{0,\alpha}(\bf{R})
, is obtained as the asymptotic solution of Eq. (
8
) when
t\to\infty
, as long as there is overlap between
\Psi_{\alpha}(\bf{R},t=0)
and
\phi_{0,\alpha}(\bf{R})
, for any
\alpha
-channel. This technique has been successfully used in several multiquarks sets

Bai et al. 2019
;
Gordillo et al. 2020
;
Gordillo et al. 2021

Ma et al. 2023

;
Alcaraz-Pelegrina and Gordillo 2022
;
Gordillo and Segovia 2024
;
Gordillo et al. 2024
.

The DMC method needs an initial approximation to the many-body wave function of the multiquark, the so-called trial function. That function should include all the information known
a priori
about the system. In accordance with the previous literature, we used the expression:

\displaystyle\Phi_{i,\alpha}(\bf{R})

\displaystyle\equiv\Phi_{i}(\vec{r}_{1},\ldots,\vec{r}_{N};s_{1},\ldots,s_{N};f_{1},\ldots,f_{N_{s}};c_{1},\ldots,c_{N})

\displaystyle=\phi_{i}(\vec{r}_{1},\ldots,\vec{r}_{N})\times\big[\chi_{s}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{N})

\displaystyle\otimes\chi_{f}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{N_{s}})\otimes\chi_{c}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{N})\big]\,,

(10)

where, explicitly,
\vec{r}_{j}
,
s_{j}
,
f_{j}
and
c_{j}
stand for the position, spin, flavor and color of the
j
-quark which is inside the
N
-quark cluster. For this trial function to be a valid, it has to be built as a product of eigenfunctions of the
L^{2}
(angular momentum),
C^{2}
(color),
S^{2}
(spin) and
F^{2}
(flavor) operators for the particular set of eigenvalues considered. Here,
N_{s}
is the number of light quarks, i.e., those with a flavor degree of freedom. In the case of the tetraquarks considered here,
N_{s}
=3, excluding the
c
quark.

In this work, we deal only with hadron states with
L^{2}
=0, corresponding to the experimental data, 0
+
. This implies that that
\phi
should depend only on the distances between pairs of quarks. We chose the form
Gordillo et al. 2020
:

\phi(\vec{r}_{1},\ldots,\vec{r}_{N})=\prod_{j>i=1}^{N}\exp(-a_{ij}r_{ij})\,.

(11)

Once the proper boundary conditions are set, via the

a_{ij}
constants, the DMC algorithm is able to correct the possible shortcomings of this expression to produce the real many-body
function of the multiquark.

As indicated above, the spin, flavor and color terms (
\chi_{s}
,
\chi_{f}
and
\chi_{c}
) have to be eigenvectors of the spin, flavor and color operators, respectively, defined by:

\displaystyle S^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\vec{\sigma_{i}}}{2}\right)^{2}F^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}\frac{\vec{\lambda_{i}^{f}}}{2}\right)^{2}C^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\vec{\lambda_{i}}}{2}\right)^{2},

with fixed eigenvalues.
\vec{\lambda^{f}}
is the corresponding set of Gell-Mann matrices representing the
flavor
degrees of freedom
u
,
d
and
s
instead of the color values
r
,
g
and
b

Amsler 2018
. Since we aim to compare our results with the experimental ones, we fixed
S^{2}
=0 (corresponding to a 0
+
state) and, obviously,
C^{2}
=0 (colorless wavefunction, the only experimentally possible).

On the other hand, the flavor operator,
F^{2}
has other constraints. In principle, we can choose any flavor eigenvector to describe the system, not necessarily the one corresponding to the lowest eigenvalue, as in the color case. However, those functions have to be
simultaneously
eigenvectors of the isospin operator, defined by:

I^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}\frac{\vec{\lambda_{i}^{f}}}{2}\right)^{2},

(12)

isomorphic with the spin operator when we have only
u
and
d
quarks, i.e., we consider only the
\lambda^{f,1}_{i},\lambda^{f,2}_{i}
and
\lambda^{f,3}_{i}
components of the
\vec{\lambda^{f}}_{i}
set. This means that we have to obtain first the eigenvectors of
I^{2}
, and then solve for the eigenfunctions of
F^{2}
in that constrained space.

We are considering only tetraquarks
with composition
\bar{u}\bar{d}sc
, with electric charge equal to 0, and
\bar{s}\bar{u}dc
(identical, in our formalism, to
\bar{s}\bar{d}uc
), with electric charge equal to +2 (0 for
\bar{s}\bar{d}uc
), in order to compare our results with experiments. Those values for the total charges rule out the compositions
\bar{s}\bar{n}nc
and
\bar{n}\bar{n}sc
and with
n=u,d
. In the first case, corresponding to the
T_{c\bar{s}0}(2900)
system, the only flavor eigenfunctions of the
I^{2}
operator (without the
c
quark) are:

|\bar{s}\bar{u}d\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad|\bar{u}\bar{s}d\rangle\,,

(13)

with eigenvalue
I
=1, in accordance with the experimental data. Now, the eigenfunctions of the complete flavor operator
F^{2}
in that basis are:

\displaystyle|\chi_{f1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|\bar{s}\bar{u}d\rangle-|\bar{u}\bar{s}d\rangle\right)\,,

(14)

\displaystyle|\chi_{f2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|\bar{s}\bar{u}d\rangle+|\bar{u}\bar{s}d\rangle\right)\,,

(15)

with eigenvalues 10/3 and 16/3, respectively. Repeating the procedure for the
\bar{u}\bar{d}sc
tetraquark, we have two eigenvectors for the
I^{2}
operator:

\displaystyle|\chi_{f1}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|\bar{u}\bar{d}s\rangle-|\bar{d}\bar{u}s\rangle\right)\,,

(16)

\displaystyle|\chi_{f2}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|\bar{u}\bar{d}s\rangle+|\bar{d}\bar{u}s\rangle\right)\,.

(17)

the first one with eigenvalue
I
=0, and the second one with value
I
=1. Each function is, at the same time, an eigenvector of the flavor operator
F^{2}
, with eigenvalues 10/3 and 16/3, respectively. We have to introduce each of those functions in Eq.
10
to describe the different tetraquarks considered. This set functions correspond to the
T_{cs0}(2870)^{0}
tetraquark.

However, the description of the arrangements is not complete, since we have to consider the fermionic nature of the quarks. This implies that Eq. (
10
) has to be antisymmetric with respect to the exchange of any two
identical quarks. Since Eq. (
11
) is symmetric with respect to those changes, we have to produce spin-flavor-color combinations which are antisymmetric to comply with Pauli statistics. To do so, we apply the antisymmetry operator,

\mathcal{A}=\frac{1}{N_{p}}\sum_{{\alpha}=1}^{N}(-1)^{P}\mathcal{P_{\alpha}}\,,

(18)

to the complete set of spin-flavor-color functions. In Eq. (
18
),
N_{p}
is the number of possible permutations of the set of quark indexes,
P
is the order of the permutation, and
\mathcal{P_{\alpha}}
represents the matrices that define those permutations. Once constructed the matrix derived from the operator in Eq. (
18
), we have to check if we can find any eigenvector with eigenvalue equal to one. Those combinations are the input of the DMC calculation.

4
CONCLUSIONS

We have explored the nature of recently observed
T_{cs0}
and
T_{c\bar{s}0}
tetraquark candidates, using the diffusion Monte Carlo method applied to a non-relativistic quark model. A comparison of our results to the experimental data available indicates that the resonances observed are in both cases excited flavor states with
I
=1. This implies the existence of their ground state flavor counterparts that could be targeted in future experimental searches. We have verified also that all systems are compact structures, with no obvious splitting into meson/meson or quark/antidiquark structures.
