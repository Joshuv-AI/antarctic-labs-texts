Regularity of the Product of Two Relaxed Cutters with Relaxation Parameters Beyond Two
Andrzej Cegielski, Simeon Reich, Rafał Zalas

Abstract
We study the product of two relaxed cutters having a common fixed point. We assume that one of the relaxation parameters is greater than two so that the corresponding relaxed cutter is no longer quasi-nonexpansive, but rather demicontractive. We show that if both of the operators are (weakly/linearly) regular, then under certain conditions, the resulting product inherits the same type of regularity. We then apply these results to proving convergence in the weak, norm and linear sense of algorithms that employ such products. Key words and phrases: Algorithm, demicontraction; metric subregularity; quasi-nonexpansive operator; rate of convergence; regularity. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 47J25, 47J26.

Introduction

The
common fixed point problem
for two operators
T,U\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
defined on a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is to

\text{find a point in }\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U.

(1)

We assume here that such a point exists and that
T
and
U
are
\lambda
and
\mu
relaxations of cutters, respectively, where
\lambda,\mu>0
. Moreover, for the reasons explained below, we will also assume that
\lambda\mu<4
. For the definition of a cutter see Section
2
.

The setting of problem (
1
) is quite general and allows us to encompass various optimization problems. An example of such a situation is when
T
and
U
are
\lambda
and
\mu
relaxations of some metric projections, that is, when
T=(1-\lambda)I+\lambda P_{A}
and
U=(1-\mu)I+\mu P_{B}
, where
A
and
B
are closed and convex subsets of
\mathcal{H}
. In this case (
1
) becomes the convex feasibility problem which is to find a point in
A\cap B
. Another general example is when
T
and
U
are averaged or even conically averaged operators
[
5
]
. Note, however, that in our setting of the common fixed point problem, the operators
T
and
U
do not have to be nonexpansive.

A prototypical iterative method for solving problem (
1
) is defined as follows:

x^{0}\in\mathcal{H},\hskip 20.00003ptx^{k+1}:=x^{k}+\alpha_{k}(UT(x^{k})-x^{k}),\hskip 20.00003ptk=0,1,2,\ldots,

(2)

where
\alpha_{k}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
and
\varepsilon>0
. Iteration (
2
) has its roots in the method of alternating projections and the Krasnosel’skiĭ–Mann method. The first question that we want to address in this paper is as follows:

(Q1) What conditions guarantee weak, norm and linear convergence of method (
2
)?

Note that for
\lambda,\mu\in(0,2)
, the product
UT
is also a
\nu
-relaxed cutter for some
\nu\in(0,2)
and that
\operatorname{Fix}UT=\operatorname{Fix}U\cap\operatorname{Fix}T
; see
[
36
, Proposition 1(d)]
or
[
12
, Theorem 2.1.46]
. It has only recently been shown in
[
14
, Theorem 3.8]
that
UT
enjoys analogous properties when
\lambda\mu<4
; see Theorem
2.15
below. The latter inequality allows one of the relaxation parameters to be greater than two. However, in such a case

and

is no longer quasi-nonexpansive, but rather demicontractive, see Section
2.1
. A result closely related to
[
14
]
has been established in
[
5
]
for conically averaged operators. We comment on the relation between
[
14
]
and
[
5
]
in Remark
2.16
.

One of the possible answers to question (Q1) has been presented in
[
17
, Theorem 6.1]
. Roughly speaking, (weak/linear) regularity of the operators

and

, when combined with some additional assumptions, implies (weak/linear) convergence of method (
2
). We comment briefly on some of these regularities below; see also Section

. There are quite a few results in the literature which fall into the above-mentioned pattern. In particular, for the linear convergence, see
[
2
,
1
,
7
,
11
,
10
,
18
,
20
,

,

,

,

]
, to name but a few. Note, however, that the arguments used in

, as well as those used in the cited works, directly or indirectly, require that

. Can we thus expect that a statement similar to the one above continues to be true when
\lambda\mu<4
? A positive answer was given in
[
14
]
, but only in the case of weak convergence. To the best of our knowledge, the parts of question (Q1) regarding norm and linear convergence have not been considered when
\lambda\mu<4
. We return to this below.

Weak regularity of
T
is oftentimes phrased as the demiclosedness of the operator
I-T
at
0
. This is also known as the demiclosedness principle. Such a property is a quite common assumption in the study of fixed point iterations

,

,

. Some extensions of this property can be found, for example, in
[

,
6
,
8
,
4
,

]
.
Linear regularity of

can also be expressed as the metric subregularity of

[

, Definition 3.17]

. Metric subregularity plays an important role in the study of linear convergence of set-valued fixed point iterations

,

]

[

]

The usefulness of the above-mentioned regularities suggests that a more careful study should be devoted to these properties. This bring us to the main question that we want to address in this paper:

(Q2) Does the (weak/linear) regularity of the individual operators
U
and
T
imply the (weak/linear) regularity of the product

The answer to question (Q2) for all three types of regularity is again positive when

, Corollary 5.6]
. Analogous results addressing some of these regularities can be found in

,

,

,

The main contribution of the present paper is to positively answer questions (Q1) and (Q2) raised above assuming that

2
Preliminaries

In this section we introduce notations and recall some well-known facts which we use in this paper.

In the whole paper
\mathcal{H}
denotes a real Hilbert space with inner
product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and induced norm
\|\cdot\|
. We denote the weak convergence of a sequence
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
to a point
x\in\mathcal{H}
by
x^{k}\rightharpoonup x
as
k\to\infty
. The distance of a point
x\in\mathcal{H}
to a nonempty subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{H}
is given by
d(x,A):=\inf_{a\in A}\|x-a\|
.

Let
T:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
. An operator
T_{\lambda}:=\operatorname{Id}+\lambda(T-\operatorname{Id})
is called a
\lambda
-relaxation
of
T
, where
\lambda\geq 0
. Note that
T_{\lambda}-\operatorname{Id}=\lambda(T-\operatorname{Id})
and
(T_{\lambda})_{\mu}=T_{\lambda\mu}
, where
\lambda,\mu\geq 0
. In particular,
T=(T_{\frac{1}{\lambda}})_{\lambda}
and
T-\operatorname{Id}=\lambda(T_{\frac{1}{\lambda}}-\operatorname{Id})
, where
\lambda>0
.

The subset
\operatorname{Fix}T:=\{x\in\mathcal{H}:T(x)=x\}
is called the
fixed point set
of
T
and each one if its elements is called a
fixed point
. Clearly,
\operatorname{Fix}T_{\lambda}=\operatorname{Fix}T
for
\lambda>0
.

2.1
Relaxed Cutters

Definition 2.1

An operator
T\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
with
\operatorname{Fix}T\neq\emptyset
is called a
cutter
if for all
x\in\mathcal{H}
and for all
z\in\operatorname{Fix}T
, we have

\langle z-T(x),x-T(x)\rangle\leq 0.

(3)

A
\lambda
-relaxation of a cutter, where
\lambda\geq 0
, is called a
\lambda
-
relaxed cutter
.

In the literature a cutter is also called a
\mathcal{T}
-class operator

[
8
]
, a firmly quasi-nonexpansive operator
[
36
,
9
]
or a directed
operator
[
19
]
. We use the term “cutter” following
[
15
,
12
]
.

We relate relaxed cutters to quasi-nonexpansive operators and to demicontractions. First we recall the two aforementioned definitions.

Definition 2.2

We say that an operator
T\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
with
\operatorname{Fix}T\neq\emptyset
is

(a)

quasi-nonexpansive
(QNE), if for all
x\in\mathcal{H}
and all
z\in\operatorname{Fix}T
, we have

\|T(x)-z\|\leq\|x-z\|;

(4)

(b)

\rho
-demicontractive
(
\rho
-DC), where
\rho\in(-\infty,1)
, if for all
x\in\mathcal{H}
and all
z\in\operatorname{Fix}T
, we have

\|T(x)-z\|^{2}\leq\|x-z\|^{2}+\rho\|T(x)-x\|^{2}.

(5)

Proposition 2.3

Let
T\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
and assume that
\operatorname{Fix}T\neq\emptyset
. The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

T
is a
\lambda
-relaxation of some cutter, where
\lambda>0
;

(ii)

T
is a
\theta
-relaxation of some quasi-nonexpansive operator, where
\theta=\frac{\lambda}{2}>0
;

(iii)

T
is
\rho
-demicontractive, where
\rho=\frac{\lambda-2}{\lambda}<1
.

Proof.
See
[
30
, Remark 3.1]
or
[
14
, Theorem 2.5]
. See also
[
12
, Theorem 2.1.39]
for
\lambda\in(0,2]
.

Remark 2.4

The notion of a demicontraction in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
was introduced by Hicks and Kubicek in 1977
[
24
]
. Măruşter
[
31
]
introduced demicontractions using a different property and called it condition (A); see (
7
) below. Moore
[
32
]
proved that these two notions, (
5
) and (
7
), are equivalent.

Remark 2.5

If
T
is
\rho
-demicontractive for some
\rho\in(-\infty,0)
, then this operator is also called
(-\rho)
-
strongly quasi-nonexpansive
; see
[
12
]
. In view of Proposition
2.3
, strongly quasi-nonexpansive operators are
\lambda
-relaxations of cutters, where
\lambda\in(0,2)
. Equivalently, strongly quasi-nonexpansive operators are
\theta
-relaxations of quasi-nonexpansive operators, where
\theta\in(0,1)
.

We provide a few examples of cutters and relaxed cutters. We begin with three very broad classes of operators which are well established in the optimization area.

Example 2.6
(Averaged Operators)

Recall that
T\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is
averaged
if it is a
\theta
-relaxation of a nonexpansive operator for some
\theta\in(0,1)
, that is,
T=(1-\theta)I+\theta N
, where
\|N(x)-N(y)\|\leq\|x-y\|
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{H}
. It is easy to see that if
\operatorname{Fix}T\neq\emptyset
, then
N
is quasi-nonexpansive and
T
is a
\lambda
-relaxed cutter, where
\lambda=2\theta
.

Example 2.7
(Firmly Nonexpansive Operators)

Recall that
T\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is
firmly nonexpansive
if
\langle T(x)-T(y),x-y\rangle\geq\|T(x)-T(y)\|^{2}
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{H}
. It can be shown that if
T
is firmly nonexpasive, then
T
is
\frac{1}{2}
-averaged; see, for example,
[
12
, Theorem 2.2.10]
. Thus, if
\operatorname{Fix}T\neq\emptyset
, then
T
is a cutter.

Example 2.8
(Strict Contraction)

Let
T\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
and assume that
T
is an
\alpha
-strict contraction for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, that is,
\|T(x)-T(y)\|\leq\alpha\|x-y\|
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{H}
. Then
T
is
\theta
-averaged, where
\theta=\frac{\alpha+1}{2}
; see, for example,
[
12
, Theorem 2.2.34]
or
[
9
, Proposition 4.38]
. In particular,
T
is a
\lambda
-relaxed cutter, where
\lambda=\alpha+1
.

We now proceed to specific examples.

Example 2.9
(Metric Projection)

Let
C\subseteq\mathcal{H}
be nonempty, closed and convex. The
metric projection

P_{C}(x):=\operatorname{argmin}_{z\in C}\|z-x\|
is firmly nonexpansive and
\operatorname{Fix}P_{C}=C
; see, for example,
[
12
, Theorem 2.2.21]
. Thus any metric projection is a cutter.

Example 2.10
(Subgradient Projection)

Let
f\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
be a lower semicontinuous and convex function with nonempty sublevel set
S(f,0):=\{x\in\mathcal{H}\colon f(x)\leq 0\}\neq\emptyset
. For each
x\in\mathcal{H}
, let
g(x)
be a chosen subgradient from the subdifferential set
\partial f(x):=\{g\in\mathcal{H}\colon f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle g,y-x\rangle\text{ for all }y\in\mathcal{H}\}
, which, by
[
9
, Proposition 16.27]
, is nonempty. Note here that we apply
[
9
, Proposition 16.27]
to the real-valued functional
f
. The
subgradient projection

P_{f}(x):=\begin{cases}x-\frac{f(x)}{\|g(x)\|^{2}}g(x),&\mbox{if }f(x)>0\\
x,&\mbox{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(6)

is a cutter and
\operatorname{Fix}P_{f}=S(f,0)
; see, for example,
[
12
, Corollary 4.2.6]
.

Example 2.11
(Proximal Operator)

Let
f\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper lower semicontinuous and convex function. The
proximal operator

\operatorname{prox}\nolimits_{f}(x):=\operatorname{argmin}_{y\in\mathcal{H}}(f(y)+\frac{1}{2}\|y-x\|^{2})
is firmly nonexpansive and
\operatorname{Fix}(\operatorname{prox}_{f})=\operatorname{Argmin}_{x\in\mathcal{H}}f(x)
; see
[
9
, Propositions 12.28 and 12.29]
. Thus if
f
has at least one minimizer, then
\operatorname{prox}_{f}
is a cutter; see
[
12
, Theorem 2.2.5]
.

2.2
Product of Two Relaxed Cutters

Below is a very simple but useful characterization of relaxed cutters; see
[
12
, Remark 2.1.31]
, where the case
\lambda\in(0,2]
is considered.

Proposition 2.12

Let
T\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
be an operator with
\operatorname{Fix}T\neq\emptyset
and let
\lambda>0
. Then
T
is a
\lambda
-relaxed cutter if and only if for all
x\in\mathcal{H}
and all
z\in\operatorname{Fix}T
, we have

\lambda\langle z-x,T(x)-x\rangle\geq\|T(x)-x\|^{2}.

(7)

Proof.
Put
U:=T_{\frac{1}{\lambda}}
so that
T=U_{\lambda}
and
\operatorname{Fix}T=\operatorname{Fix}U
. Moreover, let
x\in\mathcal{H}
and
z\in\operatorname{Fix}T
. Then,

\lambda\langle z-x,T(x)-x\rangle-\|T(x)-x\|^{2}=-\lambda^{2}\langle z-U(x),x-U(x)\rangle.

(8)

If
T
is a
\lambda
-relaxed cutter, then
U=T_{\frac{1}{\lambda}}
is a cutter. Thus the right-hand side of (
8
) is non-negative and we arrive at (
7
). On the other hand, when (
7
) holds, then the left-hand side of (
8
) is non-negative, which shows that
U
is a cutter.

We now recall a technical lemma from
[
14
, Lemma 3.5]
.

Lemma 2.13

Let
\lambda,\mu>0
and assume that
\lambda\mu<4
. Then

\nu:=\frac{4(\lambda+\mu-\lambda\mu)}{4-\lambda\mu}\ \begin{cases}<2,&\text{if }\max\{\lambda,\mu\}<2\\
=2,&\text{if }\max\{\lambda,\mu\}=2\\
>2,&\text{if }\max\{\lambda,\mu\}>2.\end{cases}

(9)

Moreover,

\nu\geq\max\{\lambda,\mu\}

(10)

and
\nu
satisfies

4\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}\right)=\left(1-\frac{2}{\nu}\right)^{2}.

(11)

The following lemma can be deduced from the proof of
[
14
, Theorem 3.8]
. Since we rely heavily on this result in the sequel, we sketch its proof for completeness.

Lemma 2.14

Let
T\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
and
U\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
be
\lambda
- and
\mu
-relaxed cutters, respectively, where
\lambda,\mu>0
. Assume that
\lambda\mu<4
and
\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U\neq\emptyset
. Moreover, let
\nu
be defined by (
9
). Then, for each
x\in\mathcal{H}
and
z\in\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U
, we have

\langle z-x,UT(x)-x\rangle\geq\left\|\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}}\Big(T(x)-x\Big)\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}\Big(UT(x)-T(x)\Big)\right\|^{2}\geq 0\text{,}

(12)

where the sign “
\pm
” should be replaced by “
+
” when
\max\{\lambda,\mu\}\geq 2
and by “
-
” when
\max\{\lambda,\mu\}<2
.

Proof.
In order to shorten the notation, put
a:=T(x)-x
,
b:=UT(x)-T(x)
and
c:=UT(x)-x
. Note that
a+b=c
. Using Proposition
2.12
, we have
\langle z-x,a\rangle\geq\frac{1}{\lambda}\|a\|^{2}
and
\langle z-T(x),b\rangle\geq\mu\|b\|^{2}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\langle z-x,c\rangle-\frac{1}{\nu}\|c\|^{2}

\displaystyle\textstyle=\langle z-x,a\rangle+\langle z-T(x),b\rangle+\langle a,b\rangle-\frac{1}{\nu}\|a+b\|^{2}

\displaystyle\textstyle\geq\frac{1}{\lambda}\|a\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\mu}\|b\|^{2}+\langle a,b\rangle-\frac{1}{\nu}\|a+b\|^{2}

\displaystyle\textstyle=\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}\right)\|a\|^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}\right)\|b\|^{2}+\left(1-\frac{2}{\nu}\right)\langle a,b\rangle.

(13)

Assume now that
\max\{\lambda,\mu\}\geq 2
. Then, using (
9
)–(
11
), we get
1-\frac{2}{\nu}\geq 0
and

\textstyle 2\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}=1-\frac{2}{\nu}.

(14)

Consequently,

\textstyle\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}\right)\|a\|^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}\right)\|b\|^{2}+\left(1-\frac{2}{\nu}\right)\langle a,b\rangle=\left\|\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}}a+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}b\right\|^{2}.

(15)

Assume next that
\max\{\lambda,\mu\}<2
. Then, by again using (
9
)–(
11
), we get
1-\frac{2}{\nu}<0
and

\textstyle 2\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}=-\left(1-\frac{2}{\nu}\right).

(16)

Consequently, in this case we get

\textstyle\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}\right)\|a\|^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}\right)\|b\|^{2}+\left(1-\frac{2}{\nu}\right)\langle a,b\rangle=\left\|\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}}a-\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}b\right\|^{2}.

(17)

By combining (
13
), (
15
) and (
17
), we arrive at

\textstyle\langle z-x,c\rangle-\frac{1}{\nu}\|c\|^{2}\geq\left\|\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}}a\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}b\right\|^{2}\geq 0,

(18)

where the sign “
\pm
” should be replaced by “
+
” when
\max\{\lambda,\mu\}\geq 2
and by “
-
” when
\max\{\lambda,\mu\}<2
. This completes the proof.

Theorem 2.15

Let
T\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
and

-relaxed cutters, respectively, where

. Assume that

and

. Then the product

is a

-relaxed cutter with

Remark 2.16

Recall that a conically averaged operator is a
\theta
-relaxation of a nonexpansive operator, where
\theta>0

[
5
, Definition 2.1]
. The result of
[
5
, Proposition 2.15]
shows that if
T
and
U
are
\theta_{1}
- and
\theta_{2}
-conically averaged and
\theta_{1}\theta_{2}<1
, then

is
\theta
-conically averaged with

\theta=\frac{\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}-2\theta_{1}\theta_{2}}{1-\theta_{1}\theta_{2}}.

(19)

On the other hand, it is not difficult to see that

and

are

and

and

of Theorem

satisfies

.

2.3
Regular Sets

Definition 2.17

Let
A,B\subseteq\mathcal{H}
be closed and convex, and such that
A\cap B\neq\emptyset
. Let

S\subseteq\mathcal{H}
be nonempty. We say that the pair
\{A,B\}
is:

(a)

regular
over
S
if for any sequence
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}\subseteq S
, we have

\lim_{k\to\infty}\max\{d(x^{k},A),d(x^{k},B)\}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\Longrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\lim_{k\to\infty}d(x^{k},A\cap B)=0\text{;}

(20)

(b)

\kappa
-linearly regular
over
S
, where
\kappa>0
, if for
for arbitrary
x\in S
, we have

d(x,A\cap B)\leq\kappa\max\{d(x,A),d(x,B)\}\text{.}

(21)

If any of the above regularity conditions holds for
S=\mathcal{H}
, then we
omit the phrase “over
S
”. If any of the
above regularity conditions holds for every bounded subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, then we precede the corresponding term with the adverb
“boundedly” while omitting the phrase
“over
S
” (we allow
\kappa
to depend
on
S
in (b)).

Below we list a few known examples of regular pairs of sets. For an extended list with more than two sets, see
[
7
]
or
[
10
]
.

Example 2.18

Let
A,B\subseteq\mathcal{H}
be closed and convex, and such that
A\cap B\neq\emptyset
.

\mathrm{(i)}

If
\dim\mathcal{H}<\infty
, then
\{A,B\}
is boundedly regular;

\mathrm{(ii)}

If
A
and
B
are half-spaces, then
\{A,B\}
is linearly regular;

\mathrm{(iii)}

If
A\cap\operatorname{int}(B)\neq\emptyset
, then
\{A,B\}
is boundedly linearly regular;

\mathrm{(iv)}

If
\dim\mathcal{H}<\infty
,
A

is boundedly linearly regular.

\mathrm{(v)}

If
\dim\mathcal{H}<\infty

\{A,B\}

\{A,B\}
is boundedly linearly regular.

2.4
Regular Operators

The following definition can be found in
[
17
, Definition 3.1]
.

Definition 2.19

Let
S\subseteq\mathcal{H}
be nonempty. We say that an operator
T\colon\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
is:

(a)

weakly regular
over
S
if for any sequence
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}\subseteq S
, we have

\left(x^{k}\rightharpoonup y\text{ and }\|T(x^{k})-x^{k}\|\rightarrow 0\text{ as }k\rightarrow\infty\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\Longrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pty\in\operatorname{Fix}T\text{;}

(22)

(b)

regular
over
S
if for any sequence
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}\subseteq S
, we have

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|T(x^{k})-x^{k}\|=0\hskip 10.00002pt\Longrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}d(x^{k},\operatorname{Fix}T)=0\text{;}

(23)

(c)

\delta
-
linearly regular
over
S
, where
\delta>0
,
if for arbitrary
x\in S
, we have

\|T(x)-x\|\geq\delta d(x,\operatorname{Fix}T)\text{.}

(24)

The constant
\delta
is called a
modulus
of the linear regularity
of
T
over
S
.

If any of the above regularity conditions holds for
S=\mathcal{H}
, then we
omit the phrase “over
S
”. If any of the
above regularity conditions holds for every bounded subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, then we precede the corresponding term with the adverb
“boundedly” while omitting the phrase
“over
S
” (we allow
\delta
to depend
on
S
in (c)).

Remark 2.20
(Demiclosedness Principle)

Note that the condition “
I-T
is demiclosed at
0
” is an equivalent way of saying that the operator
T
is weakly regular. A weakly regular operator is also called an operator satisfying the
demiclosedness principle
; see, e.g.,
[
13
, Definition 4.2]
. The latter notion is usually applied to nonexpansive operators which are weakly regular; see, e.g.,
[
9
, Theorem 4.27]
.

Remark 2.21
(Metric Subregularity)

Observe that linear regularity of the operator
T\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
over
B(z,r)
, where
z\in\operatorname{Fix}T
and
r>0
, is nothing else but the metric subregularity of
F:=I-T
at
(z,0)
. Recall that a set-valued mapping
F\colon\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
is
metrically subregular
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\operatorname{gph}F
if there are
\delta>0
and
r>0
such that for all
x\in B(\bar{x},r)
, we have

d(\bar{y},F(x))\geq\delta d(x,F^{-1}(\bar{y}));

(25)

see, for example,
[
25
, Definition 3.17]
.

Below we present a few examples of regular operators with an emphasis on the linear regularity.

Example 2.22
(Linear Operators)

Let
T\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
be a bounded linear operator. Then
T
is
\delta
-linearly regular if and only if the range
\mathcal{R}(I-T)
is closed.
In such a case we can put
\delta=\inf\{\|x-T(x)\|\colon x\in(\operatorname{Fix}T)^{\perp},\|x\|=1\}>0
. This follows directly from the closed range theorem; see, for example,
[
22
, Theorem 8.18]
. In particular, when
\dim\mathcal{H}<\infty
, then
T
is linearly regular and
\delta
is the smallest positive singular value of
I-T

[
18
, Lemma 3.4]
.

Example 2.23
(Polyhedral Graph)

Let
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
T\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
be an operator with
\operatorname{Fix}T\neq\emptyset
. Assume that the graph of
T
is a polyhedral set, that is, it can be represented as a finite intersection of closed half-spaces and/or hyperplanes. Then
T
is boundedly linearly regular; see
[
20
, Proposition 1]
.

Example 2.24
(Strict Contraction Cont.)

Following Example
2.8
, let
T\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
be an
\alpha
-strict contraction for some
\alpha\in(0,1)

T
is
(1-\theta)
-linearly regular. Indeed, knowing that
T
has only one fixed point, say
\operatorname{Fix}T=\{z\}

x\in\mathcal{H}
, we get

\|x-T(x)\|\geq\|x-z\|-\|T(x)-T(z)\|\geq(1-\alpha)\|x-z\|=(1-\alpha)d(x,\operatorname{Fix}T).

(26)

Example 2.25
(Subgradient Projection Cont.)

Let
P_{f}
be a subgradient projection as defined in Example
2.10
. Assume that
\partial f
is uniformly bounded on bounded sets (see
[
7

). Then the following statements hold:

\mathrm{(i)}

P_{f}
is weakly regular.

\mathrm{(ii)}

If
f
is
\alpha
-strongly convex, where
\alpha>0
, then
P_{f}
is boundedly regular.

\mathrm{(iii)}

If
f(z)<0
for some
z
, then
P_{f}
is boundedly linearly regular.

For the proof, see
[
18

Example 2.26
(Proximal Operator Cont.)

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper lower semicontinuous and convex function which attains its minimum. Moreover, let
f^{*}
be its minimal value and denote the set of its minimizers by
M
. Let
t>0
and, following Example
2.11
, consider the proximal operator
\operatorname{prox}\nolimits_{tf}(x)=\operatorname{argmin}_{y\in\mathcal{H}}\left(f(y)+\frac{1}{2t}\|y-x\|^{2}\right).
Clearly,
\operatorname{Fix}(\operatorname{prox}_{tf})=M
. Assume the following subdifferential error bound: there are
L>0
and
\varepsilon>0
such that

d(x,M)\leq L\cdot d(0,\partial f(x))

(27)

for all
x\in S(f,f^{*}+\varepsilon)=\{x\colon f(x)\leq f^{*}+\varepsilon\}
. Then, in view of
[
23

, we get

\|x-\operatorname{prox}\nolimits_{tf}(x)\|\geq\frac{t}{L+t}d(x,M)

(28)

for all
x\in S(f,f^{*}+\varepsilon)
. In particular, the proximal operator
\operatorname{prox}_{tf}
is
\delta
-linearly regular over the sublevel set
S(f,f^{*}+\varepsilon)

\delta=\frac{t}{L+t}

Remark 2.27

3
Main Result

We begin with the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1

Let
T\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
and
U\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
be
\lambda
- and
\mu
-relaxed cutters, respectively, where
\lambda,\mu>0
. Assume that
\lambda\mu<4
and
\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U\neq\emptyset
. Then, for each
x\in\mathcal{H}
such that
x\notin\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U
, we have

\|UT(x)-x\|\geq\left(\frac{|\alpha|}{1+\beta\sqrt{\nu}}\right)^{2}\frac{\max\{\|T(x)-x\|^{2},\ \|UT(x)-T(x)\|^{2}\}}{d(x,\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U)},

(31)

where
\nu
is defined by (
9
), and where

\textstyle\alpha:=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}\neq 0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\beta:=\max\left\{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}},\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}\right\}>0.

(32)

Proof.
Similarly to what we did before, we denote
a:=T(x)-x
,
b:=UT(x)-T(x)
and
c:=UT(x)-x
. We first show that

\textstyle\left\|\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}}a\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}b\right\|+\beta\|c\|\geq|\alpha|\max\{\|a\|,\|b\|\},

(33)

where, as in Lemma
2.14
, the sign “
\pm
” should be replaced by “
+
” when
\max\{\lambda,\mu\}\geq 2
and by “
-
” when
\max\{\lambda,\mu\}<2
.

Indeed, assume that
\max\{\lambda,\mu\}\geq 2
. Then the triangle inequality yields

\displaystyle\textstyle\left\|\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}}a+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}b\right\|

\displaystyle=\textstyle\left\|\alpha a+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}(a+b)\right\|

\displaystyle\textstyle\geq|\alpha|\cdot\|a\|-\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}\|c\|

\displaystyle\textstyle\geq|\alpha|\cdot\|a\|-\beta\|c\|.

(34)

Analogously, we get

\displaystyle\textstyle\left\|\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}}a+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}b\right\|

\displaystyle\textstyle\geq|\alpha|\cdot\|b\|-\beta\|c\|.

(35)

This shows (
33
) in the first case.

Assume now that
\max\{\lambda,\mu\}<2
and put
\bar{\alpha}:=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}
. Note that
\bar{\alpha}\geq|\alpha|
. By again referring to the triangle inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\textstyle\left\|\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}}a-\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}b\right\|

\displaystyle=\textstyle\left\|\bar{\alpha}a-\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}(a+b)\right\|

\displaystyle\textstyle\geq|\bar{\alpha}|\cdot\|a\|-\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}\|c\|

\displaystyle\textstyle\geq|\alpha|\cdot\|a\|-\beta\|c\|.

(36)

Analogously, we get

\displaystyle\textstyle\left\|\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}}a-\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}b\right\|

\displaystyle\textstyle\geq|\alpha|\cdot\|b\|-\beta\|c\|.

(37)

This shows (
33
) in the second case.

On the other hand, using Theorem
2.15
, we see that the operator
(UT)_{\frac{1}{\nu}}
is a cutter with
\operatorname{Fix}UT=\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U
. Equivalently,
(UT)_{\frac{1}{\nu}}
is a (
-1
)-demicontraction; see Proposition
2.3
. Thus for any
z\in\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U
, we have

\|c\|=\nu\|(UT)_{\frac{1}{\nu}}(x)-x\|\leq\nu\|z-x\|.

(38)

Consequently, by combining (
12
), (
38
) and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we get

\displaystyle\textstyle\left\|\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\nu}}a\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\nu}}b\right\|+\beta\|c\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\langle z-x,c\rangle}+\beta\|c\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\|z-x\|\cdot\|c\|}+\beta\sqrt{\|c\|\cdot(\nu\|z-x\|)}

\displaystyle=(1+\beta\sqrt{\nu})\sqrt{\|z-x\|\cdot\|c\|}.

(39)

This and (
33
) yield

\|c\|\geq\left(\frac{|\alpha|}{1+\beta\sqrt{\nu}}\right)^{2}\frac{\max\{\|a\|^{2},\|b\|^{2}\}}{\|z-x\|}.

(40)

In particular, the above-mentioned estimate holds for
z:=P_{\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U}(x)
in which case
\|z-x\|=d(x,\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U)
. This completes the proof.

We are now ready to present our main result. We note that a result analogous to (i) was proved in
[
14
, Theorem 3.8]
. We include part (i) for consistency. To the best of our knowledge, parts (ii) and (iii) with
\max\{\lambda,\mu\}>2
of the following theorem are new.

Theorem 3.2
(Regularity)

Let
T\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
and
U\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
be
\lambda
- and
\mu
-relaxed cutters, respectively, where
\lambda,\mu>0
. Assume that
\lambda\mu<4
and
\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U\neq\emptyset
. Moreover, let
z\in\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U
, let
r>0
and put
R:=\max\{r,(\lambda-1)r\}
. The following statements hold:

\mathrm{(i)}

If
T
is weakly regular over
B(z,r)
and
U
is weakly regular over
B(z,R)
, then the operator
UT
is weakly regular over
B(z,r)
.

\mathrm{(ii)}

If
T
is regular over
B(z,r)
,
U
is regular over
B(z,R)
and the family
\{\operatorname{Fix}T,\operatorname{Fix}U\}
is regular over
B(z,r)
, then the operator
UT
is regular over
B(z,r)
.

\mathrm{(iii)}

If
T
is
\delta_{1}
-linearly regular over
B(z,r)
,
U
is
\delta_{2}
-linearly regular over
B(z,R)
and the family
\{\operatorname{Fix}T,\operatorname{Fix}U\}
is
\kappa
-linearly regular over
B(z,r)
, where
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in(0,1]
and
\kappa>0
, then the operator
UT
is
\delta
-linearly regular over
B(z,r)
, where

\displaystyle\delta:=\left(\frac{|\alpha|\min\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\}}{2\kappa(1+\beta\sqrt{\nu})}\right)^{2},

(41)

\nu
is given by (
9
), and
\alpha
and
\beta
are given by (
32
).

Proof.
We begin with some preparatory calculations. First we show that for any
x\in B(z,r)
, we must have

T(x)\in B(z,R).

(42)

Indeed, by Proposition
2.3
,
T
is a
\frac{\lambda-2}{\lambda}
-demicontraction, that is,

\|T(x)-z\|^{2}\leq\|x-z\|^{2}+\frac{\lambda-2}{\lambda}\|T(x)-x\|^{2}.

(43)

If
\lambda\in(0,2)
, then, by definition,
R=r
. Moreover,
\frac{\lambda-2}{\lambda}<0
and we see that
\|T(x)-z\|\leq\|x-z\|\leq r
. Assume now that
\lambda>2
. Then
R=(\lambda-1)r>r
. Since
T_{\frac{1}{\lambda}}
is a cutter, it is a (
-1
)-demicontraction (by Proposition
2.3
) and

\|T(x)-x\|=\lambda\|T_{\frac{1}{\lambda}}(x)-x\|\leq\lambda\|z-x\|.

(44)

Consequently, using (
43
) and (
44
), we get

\|T(x)-z\|^{2}\leq\|x-z\|^{2}+\lambda(\lambda-2)\|z-x\|^{2}\leq(\lambda-1)^{2}r^{2}=R^{2}.

(45)

This shows (
42
). We now proceed to each of the individual statements separetely.

(i) Let the sequence
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}\subset B(z,r)
be such that

x^{k}\rightharpoonup y\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\|UT(x^{k})-x^{k}\|\to 0

(46)

as
k\to\infty
, where
y\in\mathcal{H}
. Note that

d(x^{k},\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U)\leq\|x^{k}-z\|\leq r.

(47)

Then, thanks to Lemma
3.1
, we get

\|T(x^{k})-x^{k}\|\to 0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\|UT(x^{k})-T(x^{k})\|\to 0.

(48)

By assumption, the operator
T
is weakly regular over
B(z,r)
. This implies that
y\in\operatorname{Fix}T
. On the other hand, we see that
T(x^{k})\rightharpoonup y
as
k\to\infty
thanks to the first limit of (
48
). Moreover, by (
42
), we see that
\{T(x^{k})\}_{k=0}^{\infty}\subset B(z,R)
. By applying the weak regularity of
U
over
B(z,R)
to the sequence
\{T(x^{k})\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
, we obtain
y\in\operatorname{Fix}U
. This shows that
y\in\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U
. Note that
\operatorname{Fix}UT=\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U
; compare with Theorem
2.15
. This completes the proof of part (i).

(ii) Let the sequence
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}\subset B(z,r)
be such that

\|UT(x^{k})-x^{k}\|\to 0

(49)

as
k\to\infty
. Analogously to case (i), we get

\|T(x^{k})-x^{k}\|\to 0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\|UT(x^{k})-T(x^{k})\|\to 0

(50)

as
k\to\infty
, where again
\{T(x^{k})\}_{k=0}^{\infty}\subset B(z,R)
. Applying the regularity of
T
over
B(z,r)
and the regularity of
U
over
B(z,R)
, we get

d(x^{k},\operatorname{Fix}T)\to 0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptd(T(x^{k}),\operatorname{Fix}T)\to 0

(51)

as
k\to\infty
. Moreover, the triangle inequality and the definition of the metric projection yield

d(x^{k},\operatorname{Fix}U)\leq\|x^{k}-P_{\operatorname{Fix}U}(T(x^{k}))\|\leq\|x^{k}-T(x^{k})\|+d(T(x^{k}),\operatorname{Fix}U).

(52)

In particular, we obtain

\max\{d(x^{k},\operatorname{Fix}T),\ d(x^{k},\operatorname{Fix}U)\}\to 0

(53)

as
k\to\infty
. Using the regularity assumption of the family
\{\operatorname{Fix}T,\operatorname{Fix}U\}
over
B(z,r)
, we arrive at

d(x^{k},\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U)\to 0

(54)

as
k\to\infty
. This shows that
UT
is regular over
B(z,r)
and completes the proof of part (ii).

(iii) Let
x\in B(z,r)
be such that
x\notin\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U
. By (
42
), we again have
T(x)\in B(z,R)
. Applying the linear regularity of
T
and
U
to
x
and
T(x)
, respectively, we get

\|T(x)-x\|\geq\delta_{1}d(x,\operatorname{Fix}T)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\|U(T(x))-T(x)\|\geq\delta_{2}d(T(x),\operatorname{Fix}U).

(55)

Analogously to (
52
), we have

d(x,\operatorname{Fix}U)\leq\|x-T(x)\|+d(T(x),\operatorname{Fix}U).

(56)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\max\{\|T(x)-x\|,\ \|UT(x)-T(x)\|\}

\displaystyle\textstyle\geq\frac{1}{2}(\|T(x)-x\|+\|UT(x)-T(x)\|)

\displaystyle\textstyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{2}(\|T(x)-x\|+d(T(x),\operatorname{Fix}U))

\displaystyle\textstyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{2}d(x,\operatorname{Fix}U)).

(57)

Using the linear regularity of the family
\{\operatorname{Fix}T,\operatorname{Fix}U\}
over
B(z,r)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\max\{\|T(x)-x\|,\

\displaystyle\|UT(x)-T(x)\|\}

\displaystyle\textstyle\geq\frac{\min\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\}}{2}\max\{d(x,\operatorname{Fix}T)),d(x,\operatorname{Fix}U))\}

\displaystyle\textstyle\geq\frac{\min\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\}}{2\kappa}d(x,\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U)).

(58)

By combining this with (
31
) of Lemma
3.1
, we arrive at

\|UT(x)-x\|\geq\left(\frac{|\alpha|\min\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\}}{2\kappa(1+\beta\sqrt{\nu})}\right)^{2}d(x,\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U).

(59)

This shows that
UT
is
\delta
-linearly regular over
B(z,r)
, which completes the proof

Remark 3.3

r

T

and the linear regularity over
B(z,r)
holds in (iii) with parameter

\delta:=\frac{2-\max\{\lambda,\mu\}}{\max\{\lambda,\mu\}}\cdot\left(\frac{\min\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\}}{2\kappa}\right)^{2}.

(60)

Note, however, that the proofs from
[
17
]
rely on
[
16
, Proposition 4.6]
. The proof of Theorem
3.2
demonstrates the significance of Lemma
3.1
when
\max\{\lambda,\mu\}\geq 2
.

(c)

Other results analogous to Theorem
3.2
focusing on the preservation of various types of regularity under product and convex combination, where
\lambda,\mu\in(0,2)
, can be found, for example, in
[
2
,
1
,
13
,
18
,
16
,
33
]
. See also
[
20
]
.

Corollary 3.5
(Regularity for Projections)

Let
A
and
B
be closed and convex subsets of
\mathcal{H}
. Assume that
\lambda,\mu>0
,
\lambda\mu<4
and that
A\cap B\neq\emptyset
. Let
z\in A\cap B
and let
r>0
. The following statements hold:

\mathrm{(i)}

The operator
(P_{B})_{\mu}(P_{A})_{\lambda}
is weakly regular over
B(z,r)
.

\mathrm{(ii)}

If
\{A,B\}
is regular over
B(z,r)
, then the operator
(P_{B})_{\mu}(P_{A})_{\lambda}
is regular over
B(z,r)
.

\mathrm{(iii)}

If
\{A,B\}
is
\kappa
-linearly regular over
B(z,r)
, then the operator
(P_{B})_{\mu}(P_{A})_{\lambda}
is
\delta
-linearly regular over
B(z,r)
, where

\displaystyle\delta:=\left(\frac{|\alpha|\min\{\lambda,\mu\}}{2\kappa(1+\beta\sqrt{\nu})}\right)^{2},

(61)

\nu
is given by (
9
),
\alpha
and
\beta
are given by (
32
).

Remark 3.6
(Generalized Douglas-Rachford Operator)

Let
A
and
B
be closed and convex subsets of
\mathcal{H}
with
A\cap B\neq\emptyset
, and let
\lambda,\mu>0
. Consider the following operator:

V:=(1-\bar{\alpha})I+\bar{\alpha}\big((P_{B})_{\mu}(P_{A})_{\lambda}-I\big),

(62)

where
\bar{\alpha}\in(0,\infty)
; see, for example,
[
21
]
. The operator
V
reduces to the well-known Douglas-Rachford operator when
\lambda=\mu=2
and
\bar{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}
. Following
[
21
]
, we call
V
the generalized Douglas-Rachford operator.

(a)

Assume that
\lambda\mu<4
. It is not difficult to prove that the (weak/linear) regularity of
V
over
B(z,r)
is equivalent to the (weak/linear) regularity of
(P_{B})_{\mu}(P_{A})_{\lambda}
, respectively. Consequently, with minor adjustments, we can reformulate Corollary
3.5
for the operator
V
. In particular, Corollary
3.5
(iii) recovers part of
[
21
, Proposition 4.9(i)]
and
[
21
, Proposition 4.14]
, where
\lambda,\mu\in(0,2]
and
\min\{\lambda,\mu\}<2
. In these results, the pair of sets
\{A,B\}
is transversal (see Example
2.18
(v)) and boundedly linearly regular, respectively.

(b)

When
\lambda=\mu=2
, both operators correspond to reflections, and Corollary
3.5
no longer applies to
V
. However, an independent argument presented in
[
10
, Theorem 4.4]
shows that when
\mathcal{H}
is finite-dimensional and the family of sets
\{A,B\}
is transversal, then the Douglas-Rachford operator
V
is boundedly linearly regular.

4
An Application

In this section we apply the results of Section
3
to the convergence
properties of method (
2
), where we make the range for
\alpha_{k}
more precise. We note that a result analogous to (i) was shown in
[
14
, Theorem 4.5]
. We include part (i) for consistency. To the best of our knowledge, parts (ii) and (iii) are new when
\max\{\lambda,\mu\}>2
.

Theorem 4.1
(Convergence)

Let
T\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
and
U\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
be
\lambda
- and
\mu
-relaxed cutters, respectively, where
\lambda,\mu>0
. Assume that
\lambda\mu<4
and
\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U\neq\emptyset
. Consider the following iterative method:

x^{0}\in\mathcal{H},\hskip 10.00002ptx^{k+1}:=x^{k}+\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\nu}\big(UT(x^{k})-x^{k}\big),\hskip 10.00002ptk=0,1,2,\ldots,

(63)

where
\alpha_{k}\in[\varepsilon,2-\varepsilon]
,
\varepsilon>0
, and where
\nu
is given by (
9
). Moreover, let
z\in\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U
, let
r:=\|x^{0}-z\|>0
and put
R:=\max\{r,(\lambda-1)r\}
. The following statements hold:

\mathrm{(i)}

If
T
is weakly regular over
B(z,r)
and
U
is weakly regular over
B(z,R)
, then the sequence
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
converges weakly to some
x^{*}\in\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U
.

\mathrm{(ii)}

If
T
is regular over
B(z,r)
,
U
is regular over
B(z,R)
and the family
\{\operatorname{Fix}T,\operatorname{Fix}U\}
is regular over
B(z,r)
, then the sequence
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
converges in norm to some
x^{*}\in\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U
.

\mathrm{(iii)}

If
T
is
\delta_{1}
-linearly regular over
B(z,r)
,
U
is
\delta_{2}
-linearly regular over
B(z,R)
and the family
\{\operatorname{Fix}T,\operatorname{Fix}U\}
is
\kappa
-linearly regular over
B(z,r)
, where
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in(0,1]
and
\kappa>0
, then the sequence
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
converges
Q
-linearly to some
x^{*}\in\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U
at the rate
\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{2\nu}\right)^{2}}
, where
\delta
is given by (
41
), that is,

\|x^{k+1}-x^{*}\|\leq\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{2\nu}\right)^{2}}\ \|x^{k}-x^{*}\|,\hskip 10.00002ptk=0,1,2,\ldots.

(64)

Proof.
Let
V:=I+\frac{1}{\nu}(UT-I)
and put
V_{k}:=I+\alpha_{k}(V-I)
. By Theorem
2.15
, the operator
V
is a cutter with
\operatorname{Fix}V=\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U
. Thus, Proposition
2.3
yields that
V_{k}
is
\rho_{k}
-demicontractive with the same set of fixed points, where
\rho_{k}:=\frac{\alpha_{k}-2}{\alpha_{k}}<0
. Noting that
x_{k+1}=V_{k}(x^{k})
and
x^{k+1}-x^{k}=\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\nu}(UT(x^{k})-x^{k})
, for each
w\in\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U
, we get

\|x^{k}-w\|^{2}-\|x^{k+1}-w\|^{2}\geq\frac{2-\alpha_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}\geq\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\nu}\right)^{2}\|UT(x^{k})-x^{k}\|^{2}.

(65)

Consequently,
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
is Fejér monotone with respect to
\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U
and

\|UT(x^{k})-x^{k}\|\to 0

(66)

as
k\to\infty
. Moreover, for
w=z
, we get
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}\subset B(z,r)
.

(i) Using Theorem
3.2
(i), we see that the operator
UT
is weakly regular over
B(z,r)
. This and (
66
) imply that every weak cluster point of
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
lies in
\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U
. Applying Lemma
2.29
(i), we complete the proof of this part.

(ii) Theorem
3.2
(ii) yields that the operator
UT
is regular over
B(z,r)
. Thanks to (
66
), we see that

d(x^{k},\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U)\to 0

(67)

as
k\to\infty
. It now suffices to apply Lemma
2.29
(ii).

(iii) Thanks to Theorem
3.2
(iii), the product
UT
is
\delta
-linearly regular over
B(z,r)
, where
\delta>0
is defined in (
41
). In particular,

\|UT(x^{k})-x^{k}\|\geq\delta d(x^{k},\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U).

(68)

On the other hand, linear regularity implies regularity over
B(z,r)
. Thus, thanks to (ii), we know that
x^{k}\to x^{*}
for some
x^{*}\in\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U
. Applying Lemma
2.29
(iii), we get

\textstyle d(x^{k},\operatorname{Fix}T\cap\operatorname{Fix}U)\geq\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k}-x^{*}\|

(69)

for
k=0,1,2,\ldots
. By combining (
65
) (with
w=x^{*}
), (
68
) and (
69
), we arrive at

\|x^{k+1}-x^{*}\|\leq\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{2\nu}\right)^{2}}\|x^{k}-x^{*}\|.

(70)

In particular,
\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{2\nu}<1
. This completes the proof.

Remark 4.2
(Reformulation of (
63
))

Assume that
\lambda,\mu>0
and
\lambda\mu<4
. It is sometimes more convenient to write method (
63
) as

x^{0}\in\mathcal{H},\hskip 10.00002ptx^{k+1}:=x^{k}+\bar{\alpha}_{k}\big(UT(x^{k})-x^{k}\big),\hskip 10.00002ptk=0,1,2,\ldots,

(71)

where

\bar{\alpha}_{k}\in[\bar{\varepsilon},1+\rho-\bar{\varepsilon}],\hskip 10.00002pt\bar{\varepsilon}>0,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\rho:=\frac{2-\nu}{\nu}.

(72)

Note that methods (
63
) and (
71
) are equivalent (we demonstrate this below). Moreover, since
\lambda\mu<4
, we obtain

-1<\rho=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2-\lambda}+\frac{\mu}{2-\mu}\right)^{-1},&\text{if }\lambda\neq 2\text{ and }\mu\neq 2,\\
0,&\text{if }\lambda=2\text{ or }\mu=2,\end{cases}

(73)

see (
9
). An upper bound for
\bar{\alpha}_{k}
analogous to (
72
), with
\rho
given by (
73
), can be found, for example, in
[
21
, Theorem 2.14 and Corollary 5.12]
.

Proof.
We show the equivalence of methods (
63
) and (
71
). Indeed, we write

2-\varepsilon=\nu+2-\nu-\varepsilon=\left(1+\frac{2-\nu}{\nu}-\frac{\varepsilon}{\nu}\right)\nu=(1+\rho-\frac{\varepsilon}{\nu})\nu.

(74)

If
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
is defined by (
63
) with
\alpha_{k}\leq 2-\varepsilon
, then
\bar{\alpha}_{k}:=\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\nu}\leq 1+\rho-\bar{\varepsilon}
, where
\bar{\varepsilon}=\frac{\varepsilon}{\nu}
. On the other hand, if
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
is defined by (
71
) with
\bar{\alpha}_{k}\leq 1+\rho-\bar{\varepsilon}
, then
\alpha_{k}:=\bar{\alpha}_{k}\nu\leq(2-\varepsilon)\nu
, where
\varepsilon=\bar{\varepsilon}\nu
.

In this section, we consider a very simple numerical example illustrating Corollary
4.3
. We assume that
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
so that we can visualize the trajectories of the considered methods. We also assume that
A
and
B
are two lines intersecting at the origin
(0,0)
, with the angle
\frac{\pi}{6}
. Thus, the unique solution of the linear feasibility problem is the point
(0,0)
. We examine the following three iterative methods:

•

The method of alternating projections (MAP), where

x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\hskip 10.00002ptx^{k+1}:=P_{B}P_{A}(x^{k}),\hskip 10.00002ptk=0,1,2,\ldots.

The Douglas-Rachford method (DR), where

x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\hskip 10.00002ptx^{k+1}:=x^{k}+\frac{1}{2}\Big((P_{B})_{2}(P_{A})_{2}(x^{k})-x^{k}\Big),\hskip 10.00002ptk=0,1,2,\ldots.

(78)

•

Method (
75
) with
\lambda=1
,
\mu=3
(so that
\nu=4
), and
\alpha_{k}=1
, that is,

x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\hskip 10.00002ptx^{k+1}:=x^{k}+\frac{1}{4}\Big(P_{B}(P_{A})_{3}(x^{k})-x^{k}\Big),\hskip 10.00002ptk=0,1,2,\ldots.

(79)

77
), (
78
), and (
79
), we use the starting point
x_{0}=(1,0)
