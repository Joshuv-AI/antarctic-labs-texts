Temporal changes in sex and race/ethnicity incidence rate ratios for hepatocellular carcinoma in the United States, 1992–2022
Authors: Samantha K Sarlin, Hyeyeun Lim, Xiangnan Li, Yanhong Liu, Aaron P Thrift
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe incidence of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) has increased in the United States. Historically, HCC rates are higher among males than females and among racial and ethnic minorities compared with non-Hispanic White (NHW) individuals. However, HCC incidence patterns have changed over time. We examined how sex and race/ethnicity-specific incidence rate ratios (IRR) for HCC have evolved.MethodsAge-adjusted HCC incidence rates were calculated using the Surveillance Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) 12 Program. Trends in sex- and race/ethnicity-specific IRRs were evaluated overall and by age group (< 50 vs. ≥50 years). IRRs were calculated as the ratio of age-adjusted rates for each comparison group to the reference group (male-to-female; racial/ethnic group-to-NHW), allowing assessment of relative disparities independent of absolute incidence.ResultsFrom 1992 to 2022, 68,023 HCC cases were identified. Incidence increased through the early 2010s before stabilizing and declining after 2015. HCC incidence remained higher among males than females; however, the male-to-female IRR declined from 3.29 to 3.11, with the most pronounced decline among adults aged <50 years. The non-Hispanic Asian/Pacific Islander (NHAPI)-to-NHW IRR declined from 5.86 to 1.87, while non-Hispanic Black (NHB)-to-NHW and Hispanic-to-NHW IRRs declined more modestly. Age-stratified analyses showed more pronounced declines in some relative disparities among younger adults, although several estimates were not statistically significant.ConclusionsSex- and race/ethnicity-specific relative disparities in HCC incidence narrowed over three decades. These findings indicate attenuation of relative disparities, while substantial differences in absolute HCC incidence persisted across demographic groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10552-026-02238-w.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Cancer incidence, Incidence rate ratios, Health disparities, Race and ethnicity, Sex differences

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), which accounts for most primary liver cancers [1], continues to be a major contributor of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide [2]. In the United States alone, it is estimated that more than 41,000 individuals were newly diagnosed with HCC and 30,000 individuals died from HCC in 2024 [3]. Despite advances in vaccination, antiviral therapy, and early detection, HCC incidence and mortality remain substantial and are unevenly distributed across subgroups of the U.S. adult population [4].

Over the years, scientific papers have highlighted sex and racial and ethnic disparities in HCC incidence in the U.S [5]. In particular, the 3- to 4-fold higher incidence rates in males than in females, and 1.5- to 5-fold higher incidence rates among non-Hispanic Asian/Pacific Islander (NHAPI), Hispanic and non-Hispanic Black (NHB) individuals compared with non-Hispanic White (NHW) individuals [6]. Other than suggesting that these differences in HCC burden may be due to differences in prevalence of primary risk factors (i.e., viral hepatitis infection, heavy alcohol use, and metabolic diseases), relatively little attention has been devoted to exploring the discrepancies in HCC rates. Among epidemiological studies of HCC risk factors, sex, race and ethnicity are frequently included as covariates to control for confounding, rather than examined as primary exposures of interest. As a result, relatively less attention has been devoted to evaluating how sex- and race/ethnicity-specific differences in HCC incidence have evolved over time. Despite this, sex and race/ethnicity remain fundamental determinants of HCC risk and are central to understanding its etiology [7].

Descriptive epidemiology constitutes a fundamental component of medical research and can be used to provide essential new insights into patterns of disease incidence. In particular, within cancer epidemiology, we can use descriptive analyses of incidence trends to generate novel hypotheses and guide subsequent etiologic investigations. For HCC, not only can these types of analyses tell us about historical aspects of HCC etiology, but we can also use recent trends to focus on contemporary HCC epidemiology. While prior studies have shown longstanding sex and racial and ethnic disparities in HCC rates [8], recent national analyses suggest that HCC incidence is no longer uniformly increasing, and the direction and timing of shifts (increasing or decreasing) differ by sex, race and ethnicity, and age group [9, 10]. The primary risk factors for HCC have also shifted: hepatitis B virus (HBV)-related HCC has declined with vaccination programs, hepatitis C virus (HCV)-related HCC has decreased following the introduction of direct-acting antivirals, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has become an increasingly dominant driver of new HCC cases [11, 12]. These changes may impact the landscape of HCC in the U.S., since these factors disproportionately affect subgroups of the population, but do not fully explain the discrepancies in HCC rates. Nevertheless, few investigations have explicitly focused on sex-specific or race/ethnicity-specific ratios of HCC incidence using data from the U.S.

This study used population-based data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) cancer registry program to quantify long-term trends in sex and race/ethnicity HCC incidence rate ratios (IRRs). IRRs provide a complementary measure of disparity by quantifying relative differences in incidence between groups independent of absolute incidence rates. By evaluating how these ratios have evolved over time, the analysis provides a detailed picture of shifting demographic patterns in HCC burden and provides insight into potential shifts in HCC etiology. We hypothesized that the magnitude of sex- and race/ethnicity-specific IRRs have narrowed because of divergent trends over time, with steeper declines in some populations than in others, reflecting evolving risk factor distributions and etiologic drivers of HCC in the U.S. It is hoped that this exercise will be hypothesis-generating with regards to sex and race and ethnic differences in the etiopathogenesis of HCC.

Methods

Data source and study population

We analyzed the most recent data from the SEER 12 registry database (November 2024 submission), maintained by the U.S. National Cancer Institute to examine incidence of HCC by demographic characteristics from 1992 to 2022. SEER 12 represents approximately 12% of the U.S. population and includes the following cancer registries: Alaska Native Tumor Registry, Atlanta, Connecticut, Detroit, Hawaii, Iowa, Los Angeles, New Mexico, San Francisco-Oakland, San Jose-Monterey, Seattle-Puget Sound, and Utah. Data were accessed using SEER*Stat version 8.4.3 [13]. SEER 12 was selected because it provides continuous population-based incidence data beginning in 1992, allowing assessment of long-term temporal changes in HCC IRRs over three decades. Although broader databases, such as the United States Cancer Statistics database, provide greater geographic coverage, they do not permit evaluation of trends extending into the early 1990s.

We included primary HCC cases diagnosed between January 1, 1992 and December 31, 2022, among individuals aged ≥ 20 years. HCC cases were defined by International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, 3rd Edition (ICD-O-3) site code C22.0 and histology codes 8170–8175. We excluded cases of unknown sex, race/ethnicity, or age, as well as those first identified through death certificate or autopsy only.

Demographic characteristics

Demographic characteristics of interests for this study included sex (male, female), race/ethnicity (NHW, NHB, Hispanic (all races), and NHAPI), and age group (categorized as <50 vs. ≥50 years). These categories were mutually exclusive and consistent across SEER submissions. American Indian/Alaska Native individuals and other racial/ethnic groups were excluded because annual case counts were insufficient to generate stable age-adjusted incidence estimates and IRRs across the study period. We stratified patients by age <50 versus ≥50 years to evaluate whether relative disparities differed between younger and older adults given recent focus and evidence for divergent trends in those <50 years.

Statistical analysis

We assessed incidence rates, expressed per 100,000 person-years and age-adjusted to the 2000 US Standard Population using the direct method. Corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each rate and IRR were obtained using a gamma distribution-based modification of the standard error formula for directly standardized rates, which provides more accurate interval estimates for smaller case counts and is the method recommended by the SEER program. IRRs were calculated annually as the ratio of the age-adjusted incidence rate for each comparison group to that of the reference group (female for sex and NHW for race/ethnicity analyses) and stratified by age group (< 50 and ≥50 years).

To examine changes in IRRs over time, we applied Joinpoint regression to the log-transformed annual IRRs (National Cancer Institute Joinpoint Regression Program, version 5.2.0) [14]. The Joinpoint method identifies statistically significant inflection points (“joinpoints”) in trend data using a Monte Carlo permutation approach [15]. Joinpoint models were independently fitted for each incidence rate and IRR series, with joinpoints selected by the model based on statistical fit rather than prespecified calendar periods. Accordingly, segment-specific periods and APC estimates could differ across demographic groups and outcomes; however, all analyses used the same overall study period of 1992–2022. We allowed a maximum of two joinpoints with a minimum of four observations per segment. For each linear segment, the annual percent change (APC) of IRR was calculated, and the average annual percent change (AAPC) was estimated for the overall study period as a weighted average of the APCs. When the slope of a segment was statistically significant (p < 0.05; all hypotheses were two-sided), trends were classified as increasing (APC > 0) or decreasing (APC < 0). When no joinpoints were identified, the AAPC represents the constant trend over the study period.

Data availability and ethics approval

The data used in this study are publicly available from the SEER program of the U.S. National Cancer Institute. As the study involved analysis of de-identified, publicly available data, this study was exempt from institutional review board review in accordance with federal regulations (45 CFR 46).

Results

Overall trends in HCC incidence

From 1992 to 2022, a total of 68,023 individuals were diagnosed with HCC across the SEER 12 registries. The annual number of new HCC cases increased from 840 in 1992 to 2,846 in 2022, an increase of 238.8%. The age-adjusted rate for the entire study period was 7.32 per 100,000 (95% CI, 6.90, 7.77), increasing from 4.27 per 100,000 (95% CI, 3.99, 4.57) in 1992 to 7.84 per 100,000 (95% CI, 7.55, 8.14) in 2022. The AAPC in age-adjusted incidence rates of HCC from 1992 to 2022 was 2.03% (95% CI, 1.35, 2.72). HCC rates increased by 4.72% (95% CI, 4.36, 5.23) annually from 1992 to 2009, were stable from 2009 to 2015, and decreased at a rate of 3.41% (95% CI, − 4.85, − 2.61) annually from 2015 to 2022 (Fig. 1A).

Sex-specific trends in incidence and male-to-female IRR

HCC incidence rates remained higher among males than females throughout the study period (Fig. 1). Between 1992 and 2022, incidence increased from 6.97 to 12.24 per 100,000 in males (AAPC, 2.38%; 95% CI, 1.59, 3.20) and from 2.13 to 3.95 per 100,000 in females (AAPC, 2.45%; 95% CI, 1.83, 3.10). Joinpoint regression identified one inflection point for both sexes (2012 for males and 2011 for females); however, APCs were different between females (2011–2022, APC, − 1.05%; Fig. 1C) and males (2012–2022, APC, − 3.11%; Fig. 1B). The male-to-female IRR decreased from 3.29 (95% CI, 3.23, 3.35) in 1992 to 3.11 (95% CI, 3.05, 3.17) in 2022 (Fig. 2A). From 1992 to 2012, the IRR increased by 0.51% (95% CI, 0.05%, 2.71%) annually but then declined thereafter at a rate of 1.64% (95% CI, − 6.83%, − 0.34%) annually (Table 1 and Fig. 2A).

Racial/ethnic trends in HCC incidence and race/ethnic IRR

Across the full study period, HCC incidence was highest among NHAPI individuals (14.27 per 100,000), followed by Hispanic (12.12 per 100,000), NHB (9.30 per 100,000), and NHW (4.37 per 100,000) individuals (Table 1, Fig. 1). Between 1992 and 2022, HCC rates increased among Hispanic (AAPC, 2.54%), NHB (AAPC, 2.37%) and NHW (AAPC, 3.12%) individuals, but decreased among NHAPI individuals (AAPC, − 0.93%) (Fig. 1). The rate of change and inflection points varied between groups (Supplementary Table 1).

Race/ethnicity IRRs (vs. NHW) showed marked temporal variation (Table 1 and Fig. 2). Reflecting the decreasing rates among NHAPI individuals and increasing rates among NHW individuals, the NHAPI: NHW IRR declined sharply from 5.86 in 1992 to 1.87 in 2022 (AAPC, − 3.93%; 95% CI, − 4.23, − 3.62) (Fig. 2D). The NHB: NHW IRR also declined during the study period, with the most significant decline occurring between 2005 and 2022 (APC, − 1.87%; 95% CI, − 4.70%, − 1.02%) (Fig. 2C), while the Hispanic: NHW IRR decreased modestly from 2.62 in 1992 to 2.40 in 2022 (AAPC, − 0.57%; 95% CI, − 0.89%, − 0.24%) (Fig. 2B). Although the overall rate of change in HCC incidence was highest among NHW individuals, segment-specific APCs varied across racial and ethnic groups, with periods of increasing incidence followed by more recent stabilization or decline among NHB, Hispanic, and NHAPI populations contributing to changes in race/ethnic IRRs.

Age-stratified IRRs

Male-to-female IRRs

Among adults aged <50 years, the male-to-female IRR showed no statistically significant change from 1992 to 2012 (APC, − 0.55%; 95% CI, − 1.95 to 11.10), followed by a statistically significant decline from 2012 to 2022 (APC, − 6.17%; 95% CI, − 23.91, − 2.54) (Table 2 and Fig. 3A) Among adults aged 50 years and older, the male-to-female IRR also declined during the most recent period, although this change was not statistically significant (2015–2022; APC = − 2.10%; 95% CI, − 10.54 to 0.07) (Table 2 and Fig. 3B). These findings suggest a potentially greater decline in male-to-female IRR among younger adults, although estimates for some age-stratified comparisons were imprecise.

Race/ethnic IRRs

Among adults aged <50 years, the NHB:NHW IRR declined linearly across the study period (AAPC, − 2.24%; 95% CI, − 4.00, − 0.40). The trend was different among adults aged 50 and over, where the NHB:NHW IRR increased by 1.61% (95% CI, 0.31, 4.93) annually from 1992 to 2005 before declining by almost 2% annually from 2005 to 2022 (APC, − 1.88%; 95% CI, − 3.71, − 1.02) (Table 2 and Fig. 4C and D). Among adults aged 50 and older, the NHAPI:NHW IRR decreased linearly across the study period (AAPC, − 3.93%; 95% CI, − 4.30, − 3.54). In contrast, among adults aged <50 years, the IRR declined more rapidly between 1992 and 2001 (APC, − 7.44%; 95% CI, − 21.97, − 3.04), followed by a slower rate of decline thereafter, which was not statistically significant (APC, − 1.00%; 95% CI, − 2.31, 6.03) (Table 2 and Fig. 4E and F). While the Hispanic:NHW IRR remained stable throughout the study period for adults aged <50 years (AAPC, 0.31%; 95% CI, − 0.94, 1.61), the trend of the IRR among the adults aged 50 and older changed over the study period: increased between 1992 and 2001 (APC, 1.85%; 95% CI, 0.00, 6.25), declined from 2001 to 2008 (APC, − 3.49%; 95% CI, − 9.30, − 1.02), and remained stable from 2008 to 2022 (APC; 0.12%; 95% CI, − 0.97, 5.32) (Table 2 and Fig. 4A and B).

Discussion

In this population-based analysis of U.S. SEER data, we observed that HCC incidence has stabilized or declined in recent years across several demographic groups, accompanied by substantial narrowing of sex- and race/ethnicity-specific IRRs. Importantly, these reductions in IRR represent changes in relative disparities rather than convergence of absolute incidence rates. HCC incidence remained substantially higher among males than females and among NHAPI, NHB, and Hispanic individuals than among NHW individuals at the end of the study period. Therefore, the observed narrowing of relative disparities reflects differences in the magnitude and direction of incidence trends across groups rather than elimination of underlying differences in absolute HCC burden.

Between 2012 and 2022, the male-to-female IRR decreased by 1.64% per year, with particularly pronounced reductions observed among adults aged <50 years, although some age-stratified estimates were not statistically significant. These patterns reflect steeper absolute reductions in HCC incidence among males compared with females during this period. Similarly, racial and ethnic IRRs narrowed over time, driven largely by declining incidence among NHAPI and, more recently, NHB populations, alongside slower declines or continued increases among NHW and Hispanic individuals. Because IRRs capture relative differences between groups, the observed reductions reflect divergent changes in absolute incidence across demographic groups rather than convergence of HCC incidence.

Our findings both corroborate and extend prior population-based studies describing recent trends in HCC incidence in the U.S. Earlier work by Petrick et al. [16], which projected continued increases in HCC incidence through 2030 based on data from 2000 to 2012, highlighted declining rates among NHAPI individuals, younger adults, and later birth cohorts as early indicators of a potential future inflection. Consistent with these observations, we observed sustained declines in incidence among NHAPI individuals and evidence of narrowing relative disparities among adults aged <50 years. However, in contrast to the projected continued overall increases, our extended analysis through 2022 demonstrates that HCC incidence has plateaued and begun to decline, indicating that the inflection anticipated in earlier forecasts has now materialized.

A recent analysis by Abboud et al. [6], which used the CDC United States Cancer Statistics database to examine HCC incidence from 2001 to 2020, is broadly consistent with our findings. This study reported declining HCC incidence among younger men, consistent with our findings of a pronounced narrowing of the male-to-female IRR among adults aged <50 years. Differences in study period, data source, population coverage, and analytic approach may account for differences between the studies. Both studies, however, highlight younger adults and sex-specific trends as important components of the evolving epidemiology of HCC.

Additionally, our findings also build upon the study by Alvarez et al. [17], which used SEER-13 data from 1992 to 2018 to examine HCC incidence and mortality by race/ethnicity, sex, and age. Their analysis found that overall HCC incidence began to decline in 2015, with particularly pronounced declines among NHAPI individuals, women, and younger adults, whereas incidence among NHB individuals did not decline significantly. These findings are broadly consistent with our observations through 2022; however, Alvarez et al. primarily characterized absolute incidence and mortality rates within demographic groups, whereas our analysis specifically examined temporal changes in relative disparities using sex- and race/ethnicity-specific IRRs. Extending the observation period through 2022, we found that these relative disparities continued to narrow, particularly for NHAPI and NHB individuals and among younger adults.

These prior studies primarily characterized absolute incidence and mortality within demographic strata, whereas our analysis specifically quantified temporal changes in relative disparities using sex- and race/ethnicity-specific IRRs. Extending the observation period through 2022 allowed us to assess whether these disparities persisted, widened, or narrowed as HCC incidence entered a period of stabilization and decline. Our findings therefore complement prior work by demonstrating that relative disparities have narrowed despite persistent differences in absolute HCC incidence.

Throughout the study period, males experienced consistently higher HCC incidence rates than females, with a male-to-female IRR exceeding threefold at the start of the study period. However, the male-to-female IRR for HCC has narrowed in recent years, driven primarily by a decline in HCC incidence rates among males after 2012. Despite this relative narrowing, absolute HCC incidence remained substantially higher among males than females in 2022. Thus, the decline in IRR indicates attenuation of relative disparity rather than convergence of absolute incidence rates. Among adults aged <50 years, the male-to-female IRR declined by approximately 6% annually after 2012, although the relatively wide confidence interval warrants cautious interpretation. Racial and ethnic disparities in HCC incidence have also evolved substantially, with our results demonstrating a significant narrowing of these disparities. However, this narrowing did not eliminate differences in absolute incidence. From 1992 to 2022, HCC incidence among NHAPI individuals decreased from 15.2 to 10.2 per 100,000 (AAPC = − 0.93%), while HCC incidence rates increased among NHW (2.6 to 5.5 per 100,000), NHB (5.4 to 7.9 per 100,000) and Hispanic individuals (6.9 to 13.1 per 100,000), albeit at a slower rate for NHB and Hispanic individuals. Correspondingly, the NHAPI-to-NHW IRR declined from 5.86 to 1.87, and the NHB-to-NHW IRR decreased from 2.08 to 1.44. Among adults aged <50 years, some racial/ethnic IRRs showed statistically significant evidence of narrowing, including the NHB-to-NHW IRR, whereas other age-stratified estimates were not statistically significant and should be interpreted as exploratory. These changes illustrate that declining IRRs can reflect both reductions in incidence among historically higher incidence groups and increases or slower declines among reference groups. Substantial absolute differences in HCC incidence persisted in 2022.

Although we cannot directly assess causal mechanisms in this study, the observed temporal patterns may be partly explained by changes in the distribution and management of major HCC risk factors. The narrowing male-to-female IRR, particularly among adults aged <50 years, may reflect sex-specific differences in the evolving burden and impact of metabolic liver disease and diabetes [18]. While overall HCC incidence remains higher in males, accumulating evidence suggests that females with diabetes and MASLD may experience a disproportionately greater relative increase in liver-related risk compared with men [12, 19]. As the prevalence of diabetes and MASLD continues to rise in the U.S., particularly among younger and middle-aged females, these sex-related differences in metabolic disease burden and progression may be contributing to attenuation of the historical male predominance in HCC incidence [20, 21].

The decline in the NHAPI:NHW IRR may be partly explained by long-term changes in HBV prevention and treatment. Universal infant HBV vaccination was added to the U.S. immunization schedule in 1991, with particularly high vaccination uptake among post-1991 birth cohorts and high coverage among NH-Asian children. [22, 23] Because prevention of chronic HBV infection during infancy would be expected to reduce HCC risk only after a substantial latency period, the timing of declining HCC incidence among younger adults is temporally compatible with a contribution from vaccination, particularly in populations with historically high HBV prevalence, such as NHAPI [24]. However, vaccination coverage and individual HBV status were not available in SEER, and the observed trends cannot establish a causal effect of vaccination. Improvements in HBV diagnosis and antiviral treatment may also have contributed to reductions in HBV-related HCC burden, although their population-level effects would be expected to occur gradually because treatment is targeted to individuals with established infection and treatment uptake remains incomplete [25, 26].

The timing of HCV treatment provides another potentially relevant explanation for the more recent decline in HCC incidence. Direct-acting antivirals became available in the United States beginning in late 2013, with subsequent expansion of treatment options and increasing treatment uptake through the latter half of the 2010 s [27]. Although treatment uptake was initially limited, the subsequent increase in HCV treatment preceded the decline in overall HCC incidence observed after 2015 and is therefore temporally compatible with a contribution from reduced HCV-related HCC burden [24]. However, because the latency between HCV eradication and changes in HCC risk varies according to underlying liver disease and because HCV treatment was not measured in this study, this temporal association should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of causality.

In contrast, population-level alcohol consumption has generally increased over recent decades, with particularly notable increases among women and some racial and ethnic subgroups, while alcohol-associated liver disease mortality has also increased [24, 28, 29]. These patterns do not provide a straightforward explanation for the overall decline in HCC incidence and may instead represent a competing or countervailing influence on contemporary HCC risk. The increasing contribution of metabolic and alcohol-related liver disease, occurring alongside declines in viral hepatitis-related disease, may therefore help explain why HCC incidence has not declined uniformly across demographic groups. Future studies incorporating individual-level information on HBV and HCV infection and treatment, alcohol exposure, metabolic disease, and birth cohort will be needed to determine the extent to which these factors contributed to the changing sex- and race/ethnicity-specific patterns observed here.

Taken together, these findings generate several testable hypotheses: long-term HBV prevention may have contributed disproportionately to declining HCC incidence among historically high-risk NHAPI populations; increasing uptake of HCV treatment may have contributed to the broader post-2014 decline; and increasing metabolic and alcohol-related liver disease may increasingly shape residual HCC disparities as viral hepatitis-related disease declines. These hypotheses should be evaluated in future studies incorporating individual-level risk factors and treatment data.

Strengths of this study include the use of high-quality, populated-based SEER data covering three decades and multiple racial and ethnic subgroups. The large sample size enabled precise estimation of age-adjusted rates and rate ratios over time. Limitations include the absence of individual-level data on HCC risk factors, which prevents direct assessment of causal mechanisms. Changes in diagnostic practices, surveillance, and clinical management over the study period may also have influenced observed incidence trends. In particular, the increasing use and standardization of noninvasive imaging-based diagnostic criteria and evolving HCC surveillance practices may have affected case ascertainment over time. However, these factors were not directly captured in our SEER analysis, and their contribution to the observed temporal trends cannot be quantified. Misclassification of race/ethnicity and small sample sizes for some racial and ethnic groups not included in the analysis may have affected estimates of population disparities. In addition, SEER registries cover approximately 13% of the U.S. population and do not include all U.S. geographic regions. We selected SEER 12 rather than the more geographically extensive SEER 21 database because SEER 12 provides data beginning in 1992, allowing evaluation of HCC trends over three decades, whereas SEER 21 data begin in 2000. This longer observation period was important for evaluating long-term changes in sex- and race/ethnicity-specific IRRs. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted within the geographic coverage of SEER 12 and may not reflect patterns in areas included only in more recent SEER releases, including Texas, nor be fully generalizable to the entire U.S. population. Nevertheless, the long-term consistency of the SEER program supports its utility for evaluating temporal trends.

Conclusion

In this national population-based analysis of SEER 12 data, we observed narrowing sex- and race/ethnicity-specific IRRs over time, with the most pronounced statistically significant decline in the male-to-female IRR occurring among adults aged <50 years. These findings indicate attenuation of relative disparities rather than convergence of absolute HCC incidence, as substantial differences in incidence persisted across demographic groups. The observed temporal patterns may reflect changes in viral hepatitis prevention and treatment and the increasing contribution of metabolic and alcohol-related liver disease, although these mechanisms cannot be directly assessed using registry data. Continued efforts to prevent, detect, and manage both viral and metabolic liver disease are needed to reduce the remaining HCC burden across demographic groups.
