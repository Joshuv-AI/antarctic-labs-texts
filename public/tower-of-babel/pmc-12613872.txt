Toward clinically relevant models of complex perianal fistulas: refining preclinical evaluation for exosome-based therapies
Authors: Qianqian Ye, Qingming Wang
Published: 2025

We commend Lu et al. [1]. for their innovative preclinical study demonstrating the therapeutic potential of hUCMSCs-Exo in treating complex perianal fistulas (CPF) through HIF-1α/TGF-β/Smad pathway activation, collagen synthesis, and gut microbiota modulation. Their work addresses a critical unmet need in CPF management, offering a minimally invasive alternative to current therapies with high recurrence rates. The dual focus on molecular mechanisms and microbial ecology provides a robust foundation for translational development.

While this study represents a significant advance, three translational considerations warrant attention. First, anatomical disparities between rodent models and humans—particularly sphincter complexity and microbiota composition—may limit clinical predictability. Validation in large-animal models (e.g., porcine or dog) with anal physiology akin to humans could enhance translational relevance [2–6]. Second, although short-term safety was established, long-term risks such as unintended exosomal cargo effects or immune priming require systematic evaluation. Third, comparative efficacy studies against existing therapies (e.g., anti-TNF biologics or adipose-derived stem cells) would clarify hUCMSCs-Exo’s niche in clinical algorithms.

To refine the experimental model, we propose enhancements in fistula stability and tracking. Chronicity can be improved by extending wire retention to 6–8 weeks to mimic human fibrotic progression, a recommendation grounded in the pathological characteristics of human complex perianal fistulas, where fibrosis typically develops over weeks to months. Current rodent models (e.g., rats), which rely on acute injury protocols, primarily simulate short-term inflammatory responses and fail to replicate chronic fibrotic stages. This limitation is exemplified by Buchanan et al. [5], who demonstrated in a porcine model that 26 days of rubber tube placement induced acute fistulas with tract formation confirmed by MRI. However, their study emphasized that these artificial fistulas were acute compared to chronic human cases, and whether spontaneous closure might occur without prolonged retention remained unresolved. Extended durations are thus critical to ensuring clinically relevant fistula persistence.

To augment chronic inflammation and prevent spontaneous healing, we recommend biweekly intraluminal E. coli injections into the fistula tract. This approach addresses limitations of simpler models, as demonstrated by Li et al. [7], who sustained inflammation in a Crohn’s disease-associated rat anal fistula model through biweekly rectal TNBS instillation. Introducing multi-channel fistulas with accessory tracts would further replicate clinical complexity. Future studies will systematically evaluate histological fibrosis markers across timepoints to validate these refinements.

For longitudinal tracking, contrast-enhanced MRI (e.g., gadolinium-labeled fibrin glue) provides objective monitoring of structural healing. Conventional ultrasound, while non-invasive, faces limitations in differentiating sphincter defects, scar tissue, and active fistulas. Compression during imaging may collapse tracts, leading to underestimation of severity. Hydrogen peroxide-enhanced ultrasound (HPEUS), involving microbubble generation via hydrogen peroxide injection through the external opening, converts hypoechoic signals to hyperechoic ones, improving diagnostic precision [8]. Although HPEUS was not employed in the current rodent model due to technical challenges in small-diameter tracts, it holds promise for future large-animal studies. Given that MRI has demonstrated superior accuracy to non-enhanced ultrasound in multiple studies [9–11], contrast-enhanced MRI is preferable for monitoring fistula healing over ultrasound alone.

Non-human primates and dogs are currently regarded as ideal animal models for anal fistula research. However, porcine models are prioritized due to ethical and practical considerations. To address cost challenges, cost-effective alternatives include using minipigs (smaller size, reduced operational expenses) and collaborative shared-resource facilities. Furthermore, the rubber band ligation method optimized by A Ba-Bai-Ke-Re MMTJ et al. [4] provides a viable strategy for establishing chronic complex porcine fistulas without reliance on expensive instrumentation. Porcine models are particularly advantageous due to sphincter anatomy analogous to humans [6], while minipigs offer a cost-effective alternative. Collaborative use of imaging facilities can further mitigate financial burdens.

We respectfully suggest that the incorporation of these refinements in subsequent studies could further enhance the translational value of this promising approach. By integrating pathophysiologically representative models with advanced imaging modalities, the evidence base supporting hUCMSCs-Exo therapy may be strengthened. We anticipate with interest future work that builds upon these foundational insights to explore clinical translation.
