Evaluating the potential of candidate surrogate endpoints for overall survival in treatment-naive metastatic melanoma treated with anti-PD-1-based regimens with or without anti-CTLA-4 therapy
Authors: Pierre Squifflet, Everardo D Saad, Murat Kurt, James Larkin, Peter Mohr, Andriy Moshyk, Marc Buyse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUsing surrogates for overall survival (OS) may expedite the development of, and patient access to, novel treatments. We assessed potential surrogates for OS in patients with metastatic melanoma treated with nivolumab-containing regimens in the first-line treatment setting.MethodsWe used individual-patient data from 1865 patients enrolled in four randomized controlled trials studying single-agent nivolumab or combinations of nivolumab and ipilimumab against dacarbazine or immunotherapy. Using the two-level meta-analytic framework, we evaluated three candidate surrogates: objective response rate (ORR), progression-free survival (PFS), and time to next treatment or death (TNTD). We measured the patient-level associations between candidates and OS using ORs in the case of ORR and Spearman’s correlation coefficient (ρ) in the case of time-to-event surrogate endpoints. We used R2 to measure the trial-level association between ORs or HRs for each surrogate and the HRs for OS.ResultsFor ORR, at the individual-level, OR of survival was equal to 12.29 (95% CI 9.78 to 14.80), and at the trial-level R2 was equal to 0.62 (95% CI 0 to 1.00). For PFS, at the individual-level ρ was equal to 0.72 (95% CI 0.70 to 0.73), and at the trial-level R2 was equal to 0.73 (95% CI 0.27 to 1.00). For TNTD, at the individual-level ρ was equal to 0.77 (95% CI 0.76 to 0.78), and at the trial-level R2 was equal to 0.77 (95% CI 0.37 to 1.00). In cross-validation, the 95% prediction intervals for HRs for OS predicted by regression models always contained the observed HRs for OS, indicating the stability of the models.ConclusionAt the individual-level, ORR exhibited a strong correlation with OS, whereas PFS and TNTD showed a moderate level correlation with OS. At the trial level, the key requirement for validating surrogates, all candidate surrogates demonstrated moderate predictive abilities for OS in future trials. These findings should be interpreted within the context of anti-PD-1-based therapies, with or without anti-CTLA-4 combinations, consistent with the trial evidence base included in this study.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Analysis of patient-level data from four major CheckMate trials demonstrates a strong patient-level association between objective response rate (ORR) and OS, and moderate patient-level associations for progression-free survival (PFS) and time to next treatment or death (TNTD). At the trial level, ORR, PFS, and TNTD showed moderate predictive abilities for OS benefit. Compared with PFS, TNTD displayed a slightly stronger correlation with OS at both patient and trial levels.

Introduction

Systemic treatment for most patients with metastatic melanoma is currently based on immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs).1 2 In a series of phase III trials in the first line, ipilimumab plus dacarbazine and single-agent nivolumab led to improvements in overall survival (OS) in comparison with the previous standard of dacarbazine,3 4 whereas nivolumab, pembrolizumab, and the combination of ipilimumab plus nivolumab were superior to ipilimumab in terms of OS.5 6 In these trials and in some of the phase III trials of targeted therapy for metastatic melanoma, OS was the primary or a co-primary endpoint.3–8 In some other trials, even though progression-free survival (PFS) was the primary endpoint and was significantly improved, clinically meaningful and significant gains in OS were also observed.9–11 With an increasing number of agents and combinations to be tested, using intermediate endpoints that may reach statistical maturity sooner than OS could expedite drug development and access to novel treatments. A recent large-scale retrospective analysis spanning numerous oncology drugs and indications, including melanoma, demonstrated that the use of surrogate endpoints can substantially accelerate drug development and regulatory decision-making .12 Compared with studies relying on OS as the basis for approval, trials using PFS were associated with an average reduction of approximately 11 months in development timelines, while those using objective response rate (ORR) were associated with reductions of approximately 19 months. Although descriptive in nature, these findings highlight the potential value of validated surrogate endpoints in expediting patient access to effective therapies. Besides improvements in drug development timelines and approval pathways, surrogate endpoints also offer advantages in sample size and power calculations, and facilitate more efficient trial designs. To achieve these goals, candidate intermediate end points need to be statistically validated as surrogates for OS.

Several studies have been carried out in an attempt to validate surrogate end points in metastatic melanoma treated with ICIs.13–24 These studies evaluated a variety of efficacy end points using different statistical methods; in most cases, the analyses were either based on published data which were not exclusive to first-line or ICI trials.13 14 16–18 20 21 2526 27 Studies using individual patient data (IPD) to investigate surrogate predictors of OS either pooled data across different tumor types15 or lines of therapy,22 were not based on randomized data,23 or used data from a single randomized trial only.24 Taken together, these limitations highlight a clear gap in robust surrogacy analyses conducted exclusively for ICIs within the first-line advanced melanoma setting. It is generally accepted that surrogate validation is best undertaken using IPD from several randomized trials in the same treatment setting.28 This validation can be attempted using a two-level meta-analytic approach based on IPD from multiple randomized controlled trials, a robust approach that measures the patient-level and trial-level associations between candidate surrogates and OS.29 We used this approach to evaluate potential surrogates for OS in the first-line treatment of metastatic melanoma using data from CheckMate studies. Our study focused on anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4-based regimens, namely nivolumab and ipilimumab, as they constitute a major part of current standard of care in first-line metastatic melanoma across major healthcare systems, including the USA, Europe, the UK, Canada, Japan, and Australia. Agents such as nivolumab, pembrolizumab, and their combination with ipilimumab also form the backbone of ongoing randomized trials and combination strategies (eg, TILVANCE-301 investigating lifileucel+pembrolizumab, NCT05727904). However, the prolonged follow-up required to obtain mature OS data for these regimens presents challenges for timely trial readouts, regulatory evaluation, and patient access. This has amplified the need for robust surrogate endpoints capable of reliably predicting OS benefit earlier. Accordingly, our focused evaluation of anti-PD-1±anti-CTLA-4 therapies reflects their central clinical relevance and regulatory importance in contemporary advanced melanoma care.

Methods

Study objectives and candidate endpoints

We aimed to evaluate ORR and PFS as potential surrogates for OS using IPD from four randomized trials involving nivolumab for the first-line treatment of patients with metastatic melanoma.4 30–32 We considered the ORR according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors, version 1.1, since this was the definition used in the original trials.33 PFS was defined as the time between randomization and disease progression or death from any cause, censoring patients who were alive and progression-free (or lost to follow-up) on last assessment. OS was defined as the time between randomization and death from any cause, censoring patients who were alive (or lost to follow-up) on last assessment. As an alternative to PFS, due to increasing treatment-free intervals among patients treated with ICIs, there is growing interest in assessing time to next treatment or death (TNTD) as an indicator of treatment benefit.24 Therefore, we evaluated the association between TNTD and death to investigate the additional predictive ability for death of treatment-free interval on top of time to progression for OS. As an alternative definition of this measure, where patients who died before or were alive without receiving subsequent treatment were censored, we also defined time to next treatment (TNT) as the time between randomization and the date of first administration of subsequent systemic treatment and analyzed its association with OS as a sensitivity analysis. In the TNTD definition, unlike the definition of TNT, deaths prior to first administration of subsequent systemic treatment were considered as events. In both cases, the analyses did not consider the potential intercurrent events of radiation therapy or surgery as “next treatment.”

Description of randomized trials

We used IPD from the intention-to-treat (ITT) population of four trials: CheckMate 066 (NCT01721772), a phase III trial of nivolumab versus dacarbazine in BRAF-wild-type (WT) patients4; CheckMate 067 (NCT01844505), a phase III trial of (1) single-agent nivolumab or (2) the combination of nivolumab plus ipilimumab versus single-agent ipilimumab6 30; CheckMate 069 (NCT01927419), a randomized phase II trial of nivolumab plus ipilimumab versus single-agent ipilimumab31 34; and CheckMate 511 (NCT02714218), a phase IIIb/IV trial comparing two different dosing regimens of nivolumab plus ipilimumab (see table 1).32 In order to harmonize the contrasts used in the analyses, we considered nivolumab-containing and nivolumab-free regimens as experimental and control arms, respectively; for CheckMate 511,32 we considered as experimental arm the approved doses of nivolumab (1 mg/kg) plus ipilimumab (3 mg/kg), also investigated in CheckMate 067,30 with the control arm being the alternative regimen (nivolumab (3 mg/kg) plus ipilimumab (1 mg/kg)).

Statistical methods

We used the two-level modeling approach to estimate the associations between candidate surrogates (ORR, PFS, TNT, and TNTD) and OS, as well as between the treatment effects on these endpoints and on OS.28 At the patient level, the association between the individual patient values for a surrogate and for OS indicates the prognostic role of the surrogate; at the trial level, the association between the treatment effects on a surrogate and on OS indicates the extent to which the surrogate is predictive of OS benefit gained by treatment.29

We used “contrasts” as units of analysis corresponding to the ITT population of CheckMate 066, CheckMate 067, CheckMate 069, and CheckMate 511. Since CheckMate 067 had three arms, to maximize the utilization of available data and avoid dependency between the contrasts, we randomly split the single-agent ipilimumab (control) arm. This process generated two contrasts, one for each of the experimental arms (nivolumab or nivolumab plus ipilimumab) against half of the randomly split control arm. At the patient level, we modeled the joint distribution of each surrogate and OS using the copula-based model providing the maximum likelihood value among three different copulas (Clayton’s, Hougaard’s, or Plackett’s). We then quantified the strength of the association between ORR and OS using the statistically best-fitting copula, computing ORs that indicate the risk of death given the absence of a response relative to its presence (ie, ORs>1.00 indicate OS benefit from achieving ORR and higher values of OR indicate stronger associations). We complemented the patient-level assessment of ORR as a potential surrogate with the HR for OS between patients with and without a response. We quantified the strength of the patient-level association between PFS/TNT/TNTD and OS using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ, which ranges from −1.00 to 1.00) derived from copula models where ρ=0 indicates no correlation and ρ=1 indicates perfect positive correlation between the endpoints. For the trial-level assessment of ORR, we used a marginal generalized estimation equation to obtain the coefficient of determination (R², which ranges from 0 to 1.00), which quantifies the association between the natural logarithm of OR for ORR and the natural logarithm of the HR for OS. For a more intuitive interpretation of the association between ORR and OS, analyses used the log-transformed ORR between the control and experimental arms as the predictor of the log-transformed OS HR between the experimental and control arms. This parameterization ensured that a positive association corresponded to higher ORRs being associated with lower OS HRs for the same treatment contrast. For survival-based surrogates (ie, PFS, TNTD, and TNT), we first used Cox stratified proportional-hazards models to jointly estimate the HRs for each of the surrogates and for OS, followed by linear regression, where each contrast was weighted by the corresponding number of deaths. The regression derived a linear relationship between the natural logarithms of the HR of survival-based surrogates (ie, PFS, TNTD, and TNT) and the natural logarithm of the HR for OS. The linear regression between the natural logarithm of the surrogates and the natural logarithm of OS was expressed by an intercept and slope, and derived a coefficient of determination (R², which ranges from 0 to 1.00) for each of the surrogates. In all cases, the R² quantified the proportion of variance in the effects of treatment on OS that is explained by the effect of treatment on the candidate surrogate, where higher values of R² indicated stronger study-level association between the surrogates and OS. We qualified the strengths of individual- and trial-level associations as weak if ρ<0.7 or R²<0.50; moderate if ρ ranged from 0.7 to 0.9 or R² ranging from 0.50 to 0.80; and strong if ρ>0.9 or R²>0.80. We employed a leave-one-out cross-validation strategy to assess model stability and predictive accuracy in the case of PFS, leaving each contrast out once and re-fitting the models with the remaining contrasts. We then applied this new model to the left-out contrast to compare the predicted and observed HR for OS.

In addition to the above analyses, we performed sensitivity analyses for trial-level associations with 100 random splits of CheckMate 067, in order to investigate the influence of the primary random split (presented here as base case) on the results. We also conducted exploratory analyses excluding CheckMate 069, on account of a large amount of crossover between the control and treatment arms and major imbalances in the proportions of patients receiving subsequent systemic therapy between the arms of this study. In particular, in CheckMate 069, BRAF-mutation status was a stratification factor, and the majority (76.8%) of patients were BRAF-WT. Systemic therapy use was 28.4% in the experimental arm and 63.8% in the control arm, whereas immunotherapy use was 3.2% in the experimental arm and 57.4% in the control arm. Compared with other trials in the evidence base, the treatment effect was higher on each surrogate endpoint but lower on OS in CheckMate 069, implying an outlier behavior. Likewise, to explore the impact of BRAF and potential differences in subsequent treatments driven by the presence of this biomarker, we conducted exploratory analyses of patient subgroups defined by BRAF-mutation status (mutant vs WT) when data were available.

We conducted all analyses using SAS, V.9.4 (Cary, NC, USA). We used a two-tailed level of significance of 0.05 throughout, with no formal adjustment for multiplicity and no imputation of missing data.

Results

Characteristics of the four trials

Data were available from a total of 1865 patients enrolled in the four trials; the key design features of these trials, definitions of contrasts used in the analysis, and summary results for each contrast are shown in table 1. No patients were excluded from analysis.

ORR as surrogate for OS

Data for the main analysis were available for all 1865 patients, with responses in 528 (47%) of patients across experimental arms and 166 (22%) across control arms. Corresponding numbers of deaths were 583 (52%) across experimental arms and 505 (67%) across control arms. The HR for OS was 0.15 (95% CI 0.12 to 0.18) favoring patients with at least a partial response for superior OS over those with no response (p<0.01; see online supplemental figure 1). A summary of measures of association is provided in table 2. At the patient level, the OR was equal to 12.29 (95% CI 9.78 to 14.80); at the trial level, R2 was equal to 0.62 (95% CI 0 to 1.00). The correlation measures were very similar when the OR and R2 were computed as the average results obtained using 100 random splits of CheckMate 067. These results indicate a strong patient-level association due to a high OR for OS for responders over non-responders and a moderate trial-level association with OS, but the latter was subject to considerable uncertainty due to wide CI around R2. The bubble plot depicting the trial-level association is shown in figure 1, which also provides the regression equation between ORs for ORR and HRs for OS. The results of exploratory analyses are displayed in table 2. Removing CheckMate 069 had a marginal impact on the patient-level association but considerably improved the trial-level association, with a tighter 95% CI around the corresponding R2. The analyses according to BRAF-mutation status were performed on data from 1826 patients with available information. The patient- and trial-level associations were only modestly affected in the case of BRAF-WT patients compared with the results obtained for the ITT population. For BRAF-mutant, there were insufficient data rendering trial-level analysis infeasible.

These results indicate moderate patient- and trial-level associations between PFS and OS, with the latter subject to considerable uncertainty due to the wide CI around R2. TNTD and TNT as surrogates for OS

In both cases, data were available for all 1865 patients. For TNTD, events (administration of subsequent therapy or death) occurred in 686 (62%) of patients across experimental arms and 613 (82%) across control arms. External trial-level validation for PFS and ORR

With an objective to expand the generalizability of our findings to broader first-line advanced melanoma settings, we evaluated external validity of the derived surrogacy relationships using data from seven independent clinical trials of anti-PD-1-containing regimens, including KEYNOTE-022,35 KEYNOTE-006,36 KEYNOTE-029 Part 1C,37 RELATIVITY-04738IMspire150,39 IMspire170,40 and PIVOT IO-001.41 Except for RELATIVITY-047, TNTD data were not reported across these studies; therefore, trial-level surrogacy could only be assessed for ORR and PFS. In the absence of IPD, individual-level validation could not be performed for these external trials.

In the trial-level external validation, PFS-based predictions closely approximated observed OS HRs in KEYNOTE-006, KEYNOTE-022, and IMspire150, with absolute differences of ≤0.015. Larger deviations were observed in KEYNOTE-029, IMspire170, and PIVOT IO-001. Using ORR as a surrogate, KEYNOTE-006, IMspire150, and IMspire170 demonstrated relatively small differences (≤0.10) between predicted and observed OS HRs. Notably, RELATIVITY-047 showed balanced and closely aligned OS predictions with both the PFS- and ORR-based models, yielding absolute differences of 0.083 and 0.05, respectively. Consistent performance across both surrogate models was also observed in KEYNOTE-006 and IMspire150.

Comparison of surrogacy relationships with RELATIVITY-047

Individual- and trial-level correlation results from RELATIVITY-047 have been reported previously.42 That analysis evaluated ORR, PFS, and TNTD as surrogate endpoints for OS in patients receiving nivolumab plus relatlimab vs nivolumab monotherapy using IPD with a minimum of 21 months of follow-up. Trial-level associations were assessed by grouping patients from both arms of the study into eight non-overlapping clusters based on country of enrollment. Overall, the individual- and trial-level surrogacy patterns observed in RELATIVITY-047 were consistent with those identified in the current analysis. At the individual level, both ORR and TNTD showed strong correlations with OS, while at the trial level TNTD demonstrated a stronger association with OS than both ORR and PFS. Notably, TNTD–OS correlations were higher in RELATIVITY-047 than in the pooled ICI trials evaluated in our study, both at the individual level (Spearman’s ρ: 0.84 vs 0.77) and at the trial level (R²: 0.95 vs 0.77).

Discussion

Unlike analyses based on aggregate published data or on a single trial, the type of evaluation shown in this study simultaneously allows for the assessment of two different types of association and corresponding measures between a potential surrogate endpoint and the final endpoint of interest (in this case, OS).28 29 The patient-level association indicates whether patients with improved results on the surrogate are also more likely to experience prolonged OS, thus being relevant for patient care and denoting a prognostic value to the surrogates. The trial-level association provides predictive information on whether treatments that produce changes of a certain magnitude in the surrogate are likely to produce proportional changes in OS. Statistical validation of candidate surrogate endpoints can strengthen the clinical rationale for their use as primary endpoints in future trials mainly because of the trial-level association.29 Importantly, surrogacy relationships depend on the specific treatment setting and may even change over time, due to the evolution of standard of care with newer agents with different mechanisms of action and the introduction of new salvage treatments.43

The key limitation of our study is the small number of trials and our exclusive access to IPD from trials on nivolumab-based regimens. Nevertheless, the current work has no precedent in the literature in terms of its exclusive focus on ICIs and the use of patient-level data in exploring the comparative potential of multiple surrogates. Another limitation, which is not unique to our study, is the lack of data from patients who had brain metastases. As these patients are also excluded from the analysis sets of registrational trials even when they have measurable disease, their absence in our analyses does not hinder the potential of our results in guiding earlier evaluation of prospective ICI trials. We show that ORR is strongly associated with OS at the patient level, whereas the other endpoints assessed have moderate patient-level associations with OS in this setting. Given the fact that the patient-level association is inherently confounded by baseline prognosis, most studies assessing candidate surrogates for OS have found moderate or strong associations at the patient level both in the metastatic44–47 and in the (neo)adjuvant settings.48–51 Moreover, treatment modifications induced by the observed results on the surrogate during patient care may blunt the association at the trial level.29 Therefore, surrogacy requires strong trial-level associations. Our results indicate that ORR and PFS have moderate trial-level associations with OS in the first-line treatment of patients with metastatic melanoma using nivolumab-based regimens. This level of association does not warrant replacing OS as the final endpoint in future trials, but it suggests that future analyses with a larger number of trials may find stronger associations between these surrogate endpoints and OS and/or narrower CIs associated around the point estimates of correlation. Similarly, moderate-level associations with OS should not undermine the clinical importance of PFS in randomized settings as it offers insight into clinical cure rates and is valued by patients and physicians independent of OS due to improved quality of life experienced prior to progression. In particular, patients who are progression-free for a sufficiently long period of time (eg, 3 years) can be considered to be effectively cured of melanoma. For instance, in the CheckMate 067 study,52 the 10-year follow-up data display a strong association between 3-year PFS rates and long-term survivorship rates. Specifically, 10-year OS rates among patients who were progression-free by year 3 were 86%, 85%, and 79% in the nivolumab plus ipilimumab, nivolumab monotherapy, and ipilimumab monotherapy arms, respectively; whereas in the ITT population, the respective 10-year OS rates across these arms were 43%, 37%, and 19%. Moreover, among the patients who were progression-free by year 3, 10-year melanoma-specific survival (MSS) rates were 96%, 97%, and 88% in the nivolumab plus ipilimumab, nivolumab monotherapy, and ipilimumab monotherapy arms, respectively; whereas in the ITT population, the respective 10-year MSS rates across these arms were 52%, 44%, and 23%. Compared with the ITT population, consistently lower fractions of all-cause and melanoma-related deaths in all arms of the study across 10 years among patients who were progression-free by year 3 are strong indicators of an association between the progression status by year 3 and long-term survivorship rates, underlining the long-term predictive value of PFS. Similar to PFS, ORR demonstrated a moderate association with OS at the trial level, while showing a strong association at the individual level, unlike PFS. The moderate trial-level correlation should not diminish clinical relevance of ORR as a surrogate endpoint, particularly in the context of ICIs, where durable tumor shrinkage may translate into meaningful long-term benefit. As ORR is typically available earlier than other efficacy endpoints and less susceptible to censoring behavior due to follow-up, it can serve as a valuable, immediate indicator of treatment effect.

With regard to TNTD and TNT, trial-level associations were also moderate and only slightly different than the trial-level association between PFS and OS. Despite potential statistical advantages of these measures over PFS, they remain exploratory and have not yet been used as primary or key secondary endpoints in pivotal trials. Arguably, their reliable use as endpoints is compromised by the subjectivity related to introduction of subsequent therapy, often outside of the original clinical trial. Nevertheless, the findings on TNTD and TNT lend additional support to those on PFS, since these measures are closely related.

Our finding of moderate trial-level association between PFS and OS questions the view that PFS is not a reliable endpoint—in terms of its predictivity for OS—with immunotherapy in general, and treatment with ICIs in particular. Evidently, our results reflect the general concordance between PFS and OS results in the four trials investigated. Given that in some ICI trials in non-small-cell lung cancer,53 54 head and neck cancer,55 renal-cell carcinoma,56 and urothelial carcinoma57 discrepancies have been observed between PFS and OS, it remains to be seen if metastatic melanoma constitutes an exception or whether this is a feature of the trials assessed here. Of note, phase III trials with pembrolizumab-containing regimens in metastatic melanoma have also found concordant results between PFS and OS.5 58 59 It is conceivable that the type of control arm (chemotherapy, ICI, or targeted therapy) and the amount of cross-over have an influence on the concordance of PFS and OS results. This is illustrated by the current exploratory analyses excluding CheckMate 069, which led to stronger trial-level associations and narrower CIs around the corresponding correlation measures for all endpoints assessed. On the other hand, the exploratory analyses based on BRAF-mutation status do not allow for consistent conclusions because the direction of changes—with regard to the results from the main analyses—was variable between BRAF-WT and BRAF-mutant patients, which may be caused by the differences in the availability of subsequent treatments between these subgroups, since there are more effective options for BRAF-mutant patients in general.

In summary, our results suggest that ORR is a strong surrogate for OS at the patient level in the setting of metastatic melanoma treated with nivolumab-based, first-line treatment. Likewise, our results suggest that the conventional endpoints, ORR and PFS, are moderate predictors of OS in the design of future trials, and that TNT and TNTD also have moderate predictive validity in that regard, notwithstanding a need to use these measures in an objective and prespecified fashion. More work is needed toward validation of PFS as a surrogate for OS in this setting, thus helping expedite access to novel agents in the future. In this study, because of its gold standard status in the evaluation of oncology trials, our analyses were focused on validation of surrogates for OS. MSS, which measures the time from randomization or first dose of treatment until melanoma-related deaths, has recently gained more clinical relevance and been reported as an exploratory endpoint from the ICI trials. The association between each of the surrogates analyzed in this study and MSS can also be explored using similar approaches to gain insights on how improvements in the surrogates by treatment lead to a reduction in melanoma-related deaths. In another promising future research direction, the surrogates that are individually explored in this analysis can be combined in a predictive framework to jointly estimate the OS benefit. However, the dependency and overlap among the definitions of response outcomes, PFS, and TNTD would pose challenges to the development of a multivariate framework.

Besides the ICI trials, five randomized trials of ipilimumab in first-line metastatic melanoma (CheckMate 742, CA184-248, CA184-169, CA184-024, and CA184-002) could be considered for inclusion in the analyses. These studies evaluated ipilimumab as monotherapy or in combination with dacarbazine, or nivolumab, and included a range of objectives such as registrational efficacy evaluation, subgroup analyses (eg, Chinese patients), safety and dosing/administration strategies in combination therapy, and comparisons of ipilimumab dosing regimens (eg, high- vs low-dose). They were excluded due to falling outside the scope of first-line anti-PD-1-based regimens with a nivolumab backbone, being exploratory rather than registrational in intent, or providing limited-to-no access to individual patient-level data due to their non-registrational design.

Given the transformation of advanced melanoma treatment with ICIs and increasing reliance of regulatory agencies on surrogate endpoints for new therapy approvals, the validation of robust and clinically meaningful surrogate endpoints specific to the indication and line of treatment is essential to ensure that early efficacy signals translate into long-term patient benefit. This need is reinforced by recent evidence that many cancer therapies that have been granted accelerated approval have demonstrated limited improvements in OS or quality of life even within multiple years of approval.1 60 61 In this context, our study provides a comprehensive quantitative comparison of multiple surrogate endpoints in treatment-naïve advanced melanoma. Importantly, the evidence is derived from ICI trials, ensuring strong relevance to contemporary standards of care and supporting its use in informing endpoint selection, trial design, and future surrogate endpoint research in advanced melanoma. In addition, we provide external validation of PFS and ORR across key trials that are not included in our evidence base but have shaped or informed the evolution of the advanced melanoma treatment landscape. While our findings are derived from first-line advanced melanoma treated with anti-PD-1 therapies, with or without anti-CTLA-4 combinations—where improved survival outcomes require prolonged observation—they also provide insights for accelerating treatment evaluation in later-line settings. However, because OS events accrue more rapidly in later-line advanced melanoma settings, the incremental time advantage of surrogate endpoints is likely to be more limited. Analysis of additional measures, including 3-year PFS rates, median duration of response alongside acquired response rates, and complete response, as surrogates for OS may further enhance the early evaluation of long-term clinical benefit and potentially support the utility of ORR as a surrogate for OS. These exploratory directions are particularly relevant in the context of ICIs, where durable responses are common and may not be fully captured by conventional summary measures alone. With their corresponding methodological limitations, these analyses are left for future research.
