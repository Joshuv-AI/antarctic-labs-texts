Compact Circulant Layers with Spectral Priors
Joseph Margaryan, Thomas Hamelryck

Abstract
Critical applications in areas such as medicine, robotics and autonomous systems require compact (i.e., memory efficient), uncertainty-aware neural networks suitable for edge and other resource-constrained deployments. We study compact spectral circulant and block-circulant-with-circulant-blocks (BCCB) layers: FFT-diagonalizable circular convolutions whose weights live directly in the real FFT (RFFT) half (1D) or half-plane (2D). Parameterizing filters in the frequency domain lets us impose simple spectral structure, perform structured variational inference in a low-dimensional weight space, and calculate exact layer spectral norms, enabling inexpensive global Lipschitz bounds and margin-based robustness diagnostics. By placing independent complex Gaussians on the Hermitian support we obtain a discrete instance of the spectral representation of stationary kernels, inducing an exact stationary Gaussian-process prior over filters on the discrete circle/torus. We exploit this to define a practical spectral prior and a Hermitian-aware low-rank-plus-diagonal variational posterior in real coordinates. Empirically, spectral circulant/BCCB layers are effective compact building blocks in both (variational) Bayesian and point estimate regimes: compact Bayesian neural networks on MNIST → \rightarrow Fashion-MNIST, variational heads on frozen CIFAR-10 features, and deterministic ViT projections on CIFAR-10/Tiny ImageNet, spectral layers match strong baselines while using substantially fewer parameters and with tighter Lipschitz certificates.

Related Work

Structured linear operators and circulant layers.

Circulant and block-circulant matrices are classical FFT-diagonalizable operators in signal processing
(
8
;
15
;
32
)
. In deep learning, circulant and block-circulant structure has been used to compress fully-connected and convolutional layers while retaining accuracy
(
5
;
23
;
37
)
. FFT-domain convolution has also been explored to accelerate convnet training and inference
(
29
)
. More broadly, low-displacement-rank structured matrices subsume Toeplitz/circulant-like families and come with universal-approximation-style guarantees
(
47
)
, and deep diagonal–circulant products have been analyzed and trained effectively
(
1
;
11
;
9
)
. These works primarily target compression, fast training, or expressivity, and do not place an explicit GP-compatible prior directly on the nonredundant RFFT parameters optimized in code. They also do not exploit exact FFT diagonalization to obtain closed-form layer spectral norms for inexpensive Lipschitz diagnostics. We instead parameterize directly in the RFFT half/half-plane and endow Fourier coefficients with a GP-compatible spectral prior.

Spectral kernels and Gaussian processes.

Bochner’s theorem characterizes stationary kernels as Fourier transforms of nonnegative spectral measures
(
3
;
35
)
. This underpins spectral GP models, random Fourier features, and scalable approximations
(
34
;
24
;
42
;
43
)
. These constructions are usually phrased at the
kernel
level. Our discrete spectral prior is instead implemented as a weight-space prior directly on the nonredundant RFFT coefficients used by standard real-FFT APIs, so the prior/posterior live on the exact parameters optimized in code. This makes the method a drop-in replacement for FFT-based convolutions (no per-iteration kernel FFTs) and keeps both inference and Hermitian bookkeeping local and transparent: we operate directly on the stored nonredundant RFFT coefficients and enforce Hermitian symmetry via a fixed reconstruction map, rather than relying on implicit constraints or repeated spatial
\rightarrow
frequency transforms of the kernel.

Bayesian neural networks and structured variational inference.

Bayesian neural networks with Gaussian priors and variational approximations are well studied
(
31
;
20
;
22
;
36
)
. Structured posteriors (e.g., matrix Gaussians, low-rank-plus-diagonal) trade flexibility for tractability
(
26
;
46
)
. Our Hermitian-aware low-rank variational posterior lives on the effective real coordinates of the nonredundant RFFT coefficients and is tailored to the FFT-diagonal structure of circulant/BCCB layers.
As a result, we can model meaningful posterior correlations with modest rank while retaining cheap reparameterized sampling and closed-form KL terms, making structured SVI practical for these layers.

Background: Circulant/BCCB Structure and the FFT

A vector
\mathbf{w}=(w_{0},\ldots,w_{d-1})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
determines a

circulant
linear map
\mathrm{circ}(\mathbf{w})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
via
circular shifts (indices modulo
d
):

\mathrm{circ}(\mathbf{w})=\begin{bmatrix}w_{0}&w_{d-1}&w_{d-2}&\cdots&w_{1}\\
w_{1}&w_{0}&w_{d-1}&\cdots&w_{2}\\
w_{2}&w_{1}&w_{0}&\cdots&w_{3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
w_{d-1}&w_{d-2}&w_{d-3}&\cdots&w_{0}\end{bmatrix}.

Equivalently, for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\big(\mathrm{circ}(\mathbf{w})\,\mathbf{x}\big)_{t}=\sum_{s=0}^{d-1}w_{(t-s)\bmod d}\,x_{s},\qquad t=0,\ldots,d-1,

i.e., the map corresponds to a circular convolution.

Let
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
be the unitary DFT matrix with

\mathbf{F}_{k,t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}kt/d}
, and let

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}=\sqrt{d}\,\mathbf{F}\mathbf{w}
.
Circulant matrices are exactly diagonal in the Fourier basis

(
8
;
15
)
:

\mathrm{circ}(\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}\operatorname{diag}(\widehat{\mathbf{w}})\,\mathbf{F},

(4)

so for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

\mathrm{circ}(\mathbf{w})\,\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}\big(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}\odot(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{x})\big),

(5)

with
O(d\log d)
cost using the FFT

(
7
;
32
)
.

For real
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{w}
, the spectrum is Hermitian,

\widehat{w}_{d-k}=\widehat{w}_{k}^{\ast}
, so we only need the
nonredundant half of size

k_{\mathrm{half}}=\lfloor d/2\rfloor+1
.
Real FFTs (RFFTs) implement this by returning the half-spectrum.
We treat these nonredundant RFFT coefficients as the primitive
parameters of our 1D spectral-circulant layers.

On a 2D grid with circular boundary conditions, convolutions correspond
to block-circulant-with-circulant-blocks (BCCB) operators that are
diagonalized by the separable 2D DFT
(
15
)
.
For multi-channel inputs

\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\mathrm{in}}\times H\times W}
and spectral kernels

\widehat{\mathbf{K}}(u,v)\in\mathbb{C}^{C_{\mathrm{out}}\times C_{\mathrm{in}}}
,
the frequency-domain representation is

\widehat{\mathbf{Y}}(u,v)=\widehat{\mathbf{K}}(u,v)\,\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(u,v),

and the spatial output is recovered by an inverse 2D FFT:

\mathbf{Y}=\IRFFT_{2}\big(\widehat{\mathbf{Y}}\big),

where
\RFFT_{2}
and
\IRFFT_{2}
denote the real 2D FFT and its inverse.
While the RFFT half-plane storage itself follows standard real-FFT practice, our key design innovation is to elevate these nonredundant spectral coefficients to the
learnable
parameters and to place both the GP-compatible prior and the Hermitian-aware variational posterior directly in this coordinate system.

A vector
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
defines a circulant linear map

\mathrm{circ}(\mathbf{w})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

Let
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
be the unitary DFT and

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}=\sqrt{d}\,\mathbf{F}\mathbf{w}
.
Circulant matrices are exactly diagonal in the Fourier basis

(

15

\mathrm{circ}(\mathbf{w})\,\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}\big(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}\odot(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{x})\big),

For real

\mathbf{w}

4.3
Band-limiting

The spectral parameterization exposes explicit hyperparameters for compactness.

Band-limiting.

In 1D we may activate only the lowest
K\leq k_{\mathrm{half}}
frequencies, zeroing higher ones.
In 2D we apply a radial mask with normalized cutoff
K_{\mathrm{rad}}\in[0,1]
in frequency coordinates.
These choices directly control the effective capacity of the layer without leaving the RFFT representation.

Prior spectrum (Bayesian only).

When using the spectral GP prior, we parameterize its diagonal variance profile
S(\cdot)
with a simple low-pass envelope controlled by a slope parameter

.

4.4
Backpropagation and complexity

Because FFTs are linear (and unitary under our normalization), gradients through spectral circulant/BCCB layers have the same structure as the forward pass
(
32
)
.
For a 1D spectral-circulant layer, gradients with respect to the weights and inputs can be written as circular correlations, implemented via FFTs with elementwise products and conjugation; see App.
B
for explicit formulas.
Consequently, both forward and backward passes scale as
O(d\log d)
in 1D (and
O(HW\log(HW))
per channel in 2D), rather than
O(d^{2})
for an unstructured dense
d\times d
map.

Overall, the dominant cost of both forward and backward passes is in the FFTs and frequencywise channel mixing.
The number of
trainable
parameters, however, is controlled by the number of RFFT coefficients we choose to activate, which is typically much smaller than the number of entries in the corresponding dense matrix or spatial kernel.
This separation—FFT cost vs. parameter count—is central to the compression and compactness results in Sec.
7
.

Implementation notes

Our implementation mirrors the factorization above by combining one custom variational distribution per spectral layer (handling Hermitian reconstruction in effective real coordinates) with a generic mean-field variational distribution for all remaining parameters.

5.2
Discrete spectral GP prior on filters

Choice of prior spectrum (spectral slope).

In experiments we use a simple low-pass envelope for the diagonal spectral prior variance profile:

S(k)=\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}}{1+\rho(k)^{\alpha}},\qquad\alpha\geq 0,

where
\rho(\cdot)\in[0,1]
is a normalized (wrapped) frequency radius.
In 1D on
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
,
\rho(k)=\min\{k,n-k\}/\max\{1,\lfloor n/2\rfloor\}
.
In 2D on
\mathbb{Z}_{H}\times\mathbb{Z}_{W}
,
\rho(u,v)=\sqrt{\rho_{H}(u)^{2}+\rho_{W}(v)^{2}}
with

\rho_{H}(u)=\min\{u,H-u\}/\max\{1,\lfloor H/2\rfloor\}
and similarly for
\rho_{W}(v)
.

\alpha=0
gives a flat spectrum; larger
\alpha
increasingly suppresses high frequencies.

Consider the discrete circle
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. Let
S:\{0,\ldots,n{-}1\}\to[0,\infty)
be an
even
nonnegative spectrum with
S(n{-}k)=S(k)
. We sample a Hermitian-symmetric spectrum
F\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
by drawing independent
proper
(i.e., circularly symmetric) complex Gaussians on the non-self-conjugate frequencies and real Gaussians on self-conjugate (DC and Nyquist) bins with variance profile
S
, then set

w_{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}F_{k}\,e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}kt/n},\quad t=0,\ldots,n{-}1.

(10)

Theorem 1

(Discrete spectral GP prior)
.

Under the construction above,
\mathbf{w}=(w_{0},\ldots,w_{n-1})^{\top}
is jointly Gaussian, mean-zero, with stationary covariance

k(\tau)=\operatorname{Cov}(w_{t},w_{t+\tau})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(k)e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}k\tau/n},

(11)

which depends only on the difference
\tau
modulo
n
.

Thus any nonnegative even spectrum
S
induces a stationary GP prior over discrete filters, and sampling independent complex-Gaussian coefficients with variance
S
in the RFFT half (plus Hermitian completion) implements this prior. A 2D analogue on
\mathbb{Z}_{H}\times\mathbb{Z}_{W}
yields stationary random fields for BCCB filters; see App.
C
.

2D extension.

On a discrete torus
\mathbb{Z}_{H}\times\mathbb{Z}_{W}
we define an even nonnegative spectrum
S(u,v)\geq 0
with
S(-u,-v)=S(u,v)
modulo
(H,W)
.
Sampling a Hermitian-symmetric field
F_{u,v}
with
\mathbb{E}[|F_{u,v}|^{2}]=S(u,v)
and

w_{x,y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{HW}}\sum_{u,v}F_{u,v}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(ux/H+vy/W)}
yields a mean-zero Gaussian random field with stationary covariance

\kappa(\tau_{x},\tau_{y})=\frac{1}{HW}\sum_{u,v}S(u,v)e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(u\tau_{x}/H+v\tau_{y}/W)}.

This is the natural GP prior over 2D filters for our spectral BCCB layers; details are given in App.
C
.

5.3
Hermitian-aware low-rank variational posterior

The RFFT half/half-plane stores only the nonredundant complex coefficients. Self-conjugate frequencies are real-valued; non-self-conjugate frequencies contribute real and imaginary parts paired by conjugacy.
We collect all
free
real degrees of freedom into an effective coordinate vector

\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}

and define a fixed linear map
T:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}\to\mathbb{C}^{\text{half}}
that reconstructs
the complex RFFT coefficients (including self-conjugate bins) from
\mathbf{a}
, and then
completes the full spectrum by Hermitian symmetry before the inverse FFT.

On
\mathbf{a}
we place a structured Gaussian posterior

q(\mathbf{a})=\mathcal{MVN}\!\Big(\bm{\mu},\;\mathbf{U}\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\lambda}^{2})\mathbf{U}^{\top}+\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\sigma}^{2})+\varepsilon\mathbf{I}\Big).

(12)

Samples are obtained via the reparameterization trick:

\mathbf{a}=\bm{\mu}+\mathbf{U}(\bm{\lambda}\odot\xi)+\bm{\sigma}\odot\zeta,

with
\xi\sim\mathcal{MVN}(0,\mathbf{I}_{r}),\;\zeta\sim\mathcal{MVN}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d_{\mathrm{eff}}})
,
followed by
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{half}}=T(\mathbf{a})
and
\mathbf{w}=\IRFFT(\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{half}})
.
The low-rank term captures global correlations across frequencies and channels; the diagonal part provides per-coordinate flexibility.

Because the spectral GP prior factorizes over frequencies in the complex domain, the induced prior on
a
is a diagonal Gaussian with known variance entries, obtained by viewing each complex coefficient as a 2D real normal with variance
S/2
per component and each real coefficient as a 1D normal with variance
S
. The KL divergence

\operatorname{KL}\big(q(\mathbf{a})\,\|\,p(\mathbf{a})\big)

therefore reduces to a closed-form KL between a low-rank-plus-diagonal covariance and a diagonal covariance, computed efficiently via the matrix determinant lemma and the Woodbury identity (details in App.
D
). In practice we use a small rank (e.g.
r=8
) for all spectral layers.

6
Lipschitz Bounds and Certificates

Lipschitz constants and spectral norms are widely used to characterize and control the sensitivity of neural networks to input perturbations, with connections to stability, margin-based generalization bounds, and certified robustness
(
6
;
30
;
2
;
14
;
41
)
. Here we leverage the fact that circulant and BCCB operators are
exactly
diagonalized by the DFT, so their layer spectral norms can be computed directly from their frequency response (rather than estimated by iterative power methods), making Lipschitz diagnostics essentially free in our parameterization.

The FFT-diagonal structure lets us obtain exact layer spectral norms and simple global Lipschitz bounds.

1D spectral-circulant layers.

For a 1D spectral-circulant layer,

T_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\;=\;\IRFFT\!\big(\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{half}}(\bm{\theta})\odot\RFFT(\mathbf{x})\big),

let
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{full}}(\bm{\theta})\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
denote the Hermitian-completed full spectrum corresponding to
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{half}}(\bm{\theta})
, and let
h_{k}(\bm{\theta})
denote its
k
th (scalar) entry. Then

T_{\bm{\theta}}\;=\;\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}\operatorname{diag}\!\big(\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{full}}(\bm{\theta})\big)\,\mathbf{F}.

Since
T_{\bm{\theta}}
is normal with eigenvalues
\{h_{k}(\bm{\theta})\}_{k=0}^{d-1}
, its operator norm is

\|T_{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2\to 2}=\max_{k}|h_{k}(\bm{\theta})|,

(13)

which can be evaluated directly from the stored RFFT coefficients (the maximum over the half-spectrum suffices since conjugate pairs share magnitude).

A prior typical-case bound (“bad geometry is unlikely”)

The equality in Eq. (
13
) characterizes the
worst-case
Lipschitz behavior of a spectral-circulant layer once its spectrum is fixed.
Under our diagonal spectral prior, however, the nonredundant Fourier coefficients are random and (by construction) independent across active frequency bins, which yields a simple high-probability control on unusually large spectral norms.

Proposition 2

(Prior high-probability bound for spectral-circulant norms)
.

Consider a 1D spectral-circulant layer with an active set of nonredundant RFFT bins
\mathcal{K}
(after any band-limit mask), with
m=|\mathcal{K}|
.
Under the diagonal spectral prior, the active scalar Fourier coefficients
\{h_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
are independent, with non-self-conjugate bins satisfying

(\Re h_{k},\Im h_{k})\sim\mathcal{MVN}\!\big(0,\tfrac{S(k)}{2}\mathbf{I}_{2}\big)
(equivalently
h_{k}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,S(k))
),
and self-conjugate bins (DC and, if applicable, Nyquist) satisfying
h_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,S(k))
.

Let
S_{\max}=\max_{k\in\mathcal{K}}S(k)
. Then for any
t>0
,

\Pr\!\Big[\|T\|_{2\to 2}\geq t\Big]\;\leq\;2m\,\exp\!\Big(-\frac{t^{2}}{2S_{\max}}\Big),

and consequently for any
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\Pr\!\left[\|T\|_{2\to 2}\leq\sqrt{2S_{\max}\log\!\frac{2m}{\delta}}\right]\;\geq\;1-\delta.

In words, under the spectral prior the layer Lipschitz constant typically scales like
\sqrt{S_{\max}\log m}
; extremely large operator norms are exponentially unlikely.

2D multi-channel BCCB layers.

For 2D BCCB layers with multi-channel mixing, the 2D DFT diagonalizes the spatial structure but leaves channel mixing as small matrices. Flattening spatial dimensions, the layer is unitarily equivalent to a block-diagonal map

\displaystyle\widehat{y}_{k}=K_{\bm{\theta}}(k)\widehat{x}_{k},

\displaystyle D_{\bm{\theta}}=\mathrm{blockdiag}(K_{\bm{\theta}}(k_{1}),\ldots,K_{\bm{\theta}}(k_{HW})),

where
K_{\bm{\theta}}(k)\in\mathbb{C}^{C_{\mathrm{out}}\times C_{\mathrm{in}}}
are frequencywise mixing matrices. The operator norm is therefore
(
40
)

\|T_{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2\to 2}=\max_{k}\sigma_{\max}(K_{\bm{\theta}}(k)),

(14)

where
\sigma_{\max}
denotes the largest singular value. Hermitian symmetry does not change singular values, so the maximum can be taken over the RFFT
2
half-plane.

Network-level bounds and certificates.

For a network
f_{\bm{\theta}}
that is a composition of linear layers and 1-Lipschitz activations (e.g.
\tanh
), ignoring biases a standard product bound yields

\displaystyle\operatorname{Lip}(f_{\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{\operatorname{Lip}}(f_{\bm{\theta}}),

(15)

\displaystyle\widehat{\operatorname{Lip}}(f_{\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle:=\prod_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{spec}}}\|T_{\ell,\bm{\theta}}\|_{2\to 2}\;\cdot\;\prod_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dense}}}\|\mathbf{W}_{\ell}(\bm{\theta})\|_{2},

(16)

where for 1D spectral-circulant layers
\|T_{\ell,\bm{\theta}}\|_{2\to 2}=\max_{k}|h_{\ell,k}(\bm{\theta})|
(Eq. (
13
))
and for 2D BCCB layers with channel mixing
\|T_{\ell,\bm{\theta}}\|_{2\to 2}=\max_{k}\sigma_{\max}(K_{\ell,\bm{\theta}}(k))
(Eq. (
14
)).
Under independent spectral priors, Proposition
2
yields a simple simultaneous control of these factors; see Corollary
7
in App.
E
.

Given logits
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
and true label
y
, the logit margin

m_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x},y)=f_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})_{y}-\max_{k\neq y}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})_{k}

yields a standard global
\ell_{2}
robustness certificate

r_{\mathrm{cert}}(x,y;\bm{\theta})=\frac{\max\{m_{\bm{\theta}}(x,y),0\}}{2\,\widehat{\operatorname{Lip}}(f_{\bm{\theta}})}.

(17)

In our Bayesian experiments we sample
\bm{\theta}\sim q(\bm{\theta})
, compute
m_{\bm{\theta}}
and
r_{\mathrm{cert}}
on test inputs, and study their empirical posterior distributions (see App.
E
).

8
Discussion and Limitations

We parameterize circulant/BCCB layers directly by the nonredundant RFFT coefficients used in standard real-FFT APIs, aligning implementation with (i) a GP-compatible diagonal spectral prior, (ii) a Hermitian-aware structured variational posterior with closed-form KL, and (iii) exact layer spectral norms for cheap Lipschitz diagnostics. Empirically, these layers act as compact replacements for dense/conv projections in Bayesian heads and small transformers.

Limitations.

Circular boundary conditions can introduce edge artifacts; padding or hybrid designs may mitigate this but break strict BCCB structure. Our spectral prior uses simple radial/1D profiles; learning richer spectra is a natural extension. At CIFAR scale we apply Bayesian inference only to the head; scaling structured SVI to deep spectral stacks remains challenging. Our Lipschitz analysis is global,
\ell_{2}
-based, and used only as a geometric diagnostic.

Impact Statement

This work develops compact spectral layers and associated probabilistic priors for deep networks. The main anticipated impact is methodological: more parameter-efficient and better-calibrated models for standard supervised tasks, with potential downstream benefits in settings where memory or energy budgets are constrained. As with most work on general-purpose representation learning, the techniques here could be used in both socially beneficial and harmful applications. We do not target safety-critical domains directly and do not propose mechanisms that meaningfully change the risk profile of deploying machine learning systems beyond improving calibration and providing coarse global robustness diagnostics. Any use of these methods in high-stakes domains should be accompanied by domain-specific validation, careful monitoring, and alignment with relevant legal and ethical standards.

Appendix B
Background: Circulant/BCCB and the FFT

This appendix collects additional details on circulant/BCCB structure,
FFT-based implementations, and gradients. The basic circulant matrix
schema and its diagonalization are introduced in
Sec.
3
.

Diagonalization by the DFT.

Let
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
be the unitary DFT
matrix with
\mathbf{F}_{k,t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}kt/d}
, and

\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{F}^{-1}
its conjugate transpose.
Writing
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}=\mathbf{F}\mathbf{w}
, circulant matrices are exactly diagonal
in the Fourier basis
(
8
;
15
)
:

\mathrm{circ}(\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}\,\operatorname{diag}(\widehat{\mathbf{w}})\,\mathbf{F}.

(18)

Thus for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\mathrm{circ}(\mathbf{w})\,\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}\!\big(\,(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{w})\odot(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{x})\,\big),

(19)

where
\odot
denotes elementwise (Hadamard) product. With the Fast
Fourier Transform (FFT)
(
7
;
32
)
,
(
19
) costs
O(d\log d)
.

Real inputs and the real FFT.

When vectors are real, the spectrum exhibits Hermitian symmetry, so we
can store only the nonredundant
k=0,\ldots,\lfloor d/2\rfloor

coefficients, halving memory and flops. In that case,

\mathrm{circ}(\mathbf{w})\,\mathbf{x}=\IRFFT\!\big(\,\RFFT(\mathbf{w})\odot\RFFT(\mathbf{x})\,\big),

(20)

with
\RFFT/\IRFFT
understood as real-valued FFTs on the half-spectrum.

Backpropagation through a circulant layer.

Consider a (pre-activation) vector

\mathbf{a}=\mathrm{circ}(\mathbf{w})\,\mathbf{x}
and a scalar loss
\mathcal{L}
.
Let
\mathbf{g}=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{a}
.
Since FFT/IFTT are linear and unitary (under our normalization), the
adjoint maps are again Fourier transforms.
The gradients can be written as circular
correlations
and admit
the same
O(d\log d)
structure:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{corr}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{x})

(21)

\displaystyle=\IRFFT\!\big(\,\RFFT(\mathbf{g})^{\ast}\;\odot\;\RFFT(\mathbf{x})\,\big),

(22)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{corr}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{w})

(23)

\displaystyle=\IRFFT\!\big(\,\RFFT(\mathbf{g})^{\ast}\;\odot\;\RFFT(\mathbf{w})\,\big),

(24)

where the asterix denotes complex conjugation.
1
1

1

Convolution uses no conjugation in frequency
(cf. Eq. (
20
)); correlation introduces
conjugation on the first argument, yielding
Eqs. (
21
)–(
23
). Library-dependent
FFT normalizations change only constant factors.

Hence the basic computational unit for both forward and backward passes
is

\IRFFT\!\big(\,\RFFT(\cdot)\;(\text{or }\overline{\RFFT(\cdot)})\;\odot\;\RFFT(\cdot)\,\big),

in contrast to
O(d^{2})
dense matvecs in unstructured layers.

2D BCCB operators and multi-channel mixing.

On an
H\times W
grid with circular boundary conditions, 2D
convolutions correspond to block-circulant-with-circulant-blocks (BCCB)
operators, diagonalized by the separable 2D DFT

(
15
)
.

For multi-channel inputs

\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\mathrm{in}}\times H\times W}
and spectral kernels

\widehat{\mathbf{K}}\in\mathbb{C}^{C_{\mathrm{out}}\times C_{\mathrm{in}}\times H\times(W/2+1)}

on the RFFT
2
half-plane, we have

\widehat{\mathbf{Y}}[o]=\sum_{c=1}^{C_{\mathrm{in}}}\widehat{\mathbf{K}}[o,c]\odot\widehat{\mathbf{X}}[c]

and

\mathbf{Y}=\IRFFT_{2}(\widehat{\mathbf{Y}}),

with
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}=\RFFT_{2}(\mathbf{X})
. The per-image complexity is

\underbrace{(C_{\mathrm{in}}{+}C_{\mathrm{out}})\,HW\log(HW)}_{\text{I/O FFTs}}\;+\;\underbrace{C_{\mathrm{in}}C_{\mathrm{out}}\,HW}_{\text{frequencywise mixing}},

and the same structure carries to gradients by linearity of the FFT.

Appendix C
GP–Equivalence of the Spectral–Circulant Prior

This appendix formalizes a key probabilistic fact underlying our spectral prior: drawing independent (proper) complex Gaussian Fourier coefficients with an even nonnegative variance profile and enforcing Hermitian symmetry yields a real-valued stationary Gaussian process on the discrete circle (and analogously a stationary Gaussian random field on the discrete torus). This is a standard instance of the Bochner–Herglotz characterization of positive-definite functions on compact abelian groups; we include the statement and proof in our notation for completeness and to make the GP connection explicit
(
38
;
3
)
.

Conventions.

Indices are taken modulo
n
. We use the unitary inverse DFT

(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\,\mathbf{f})_{t}\;=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}f_{k}\,e^{2\pi ikt/n}.

Definition 3

(Spectral–circulant sampling)
.

Let
S:\{0,\ldots,n{-}1\}\to[0,\infty)
be such that
S(n{-}k)=S(k)
(even on
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
).
Draw independent
proper
complex Gaussians on the Hermitian half:
for
k=1,\ldots,\lfloor(n{-}1)/2\rfloor
let

a_{k},b_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,S(k)/2)
and set
f_{k}=a_{k}+ib_{k}
;
set
f_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,S(0))
and, if
n
is even,
f_{n/2}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,S(n/2))
;
complete by conjugacy
f_{n-k}=\overline{f_{k}}
. Then
\mathbf{w}=(w_{0},\ldots,w_{n-1})
with

w_{t}\;=\;(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\,\mathbf{f})_{t}\;=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}f_{k}\,e^{2\pi ikt/n}

is real by construction.

Remark 4

(Second–order structure)
.

The samples satisfy
\mathbb{E}[f_{k}\,\overline{f_{\ell}}]=S(k)\,\delta_{k\ell}
and

\mathbb{E}[f_{k}f_{\ell}]=S(k)\,\delta_{\ell,n-k}
for all
k,\ell
.

Theorem 5

(GP–equivalence on
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
)
.

\mathbf{w}
is jointly Gaussian, mean zero, with stationary covariance

\displaystyle k(\tau)

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{Cov}\!\big(w_{t},w_{t+\tau}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(k)\,e^{2\pi ik\tau/n},

\displaystyle\tau=0,\ldots,n{-}1.

Proof.

Gaussianity:
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
is real–linear in
(\Re f,\Im f)
, which are jointly Gaussian.
For the covariance, by zero mean and Remark
4
,

\displaystyle k(\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[w_{t}w_{t+\tau}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k,\ell}e^{2\pi i(kt+\ell(t+\tau))/n}\,\mathbb{E}[f_{k}f_{\ell}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(k)\,e^{2\pi i(kt+(n-k)(t+\tau))/n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(k)\,e^{2\pi ik\tau/n},

since
e^{2\pi it}=e^{2\pi i\tau}=1
for integer
t,\tau
.
∎

Corollary 6

(Discrete Bochner/Herglotz)
.

For any
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,

\sum_{t,s}\overline{c_{t}}c_{s}\,k(t{-}s)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(k)\,\Big|\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}c_{s}e^{-2\pi iks/n}\Big|^{2}\geq 0.

Hence
k\succeq 0
iff
S\geq 0
.

2D extension (BCCB).

Let
S(u,v)\!\geq\!0
on
\{0,\ldots,H{-}1\}\times\{0,\ldots,W{-}1\}
with
S(-u,-v)=S(u,v)
modulo
(H,W)
.
Sample a Hermitian–symmetric field
\{f_{u,v}\}
with
\mathbb{E}[|f_{u,v}|^{2}]=S(u,v)
and set

w_{x,y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{HW}}\sum_{u=0}^{H-1}\sum_{v=0}^{W-1}f_{u,v}\,e^{2\pi i(ux/H+vy/W)}.

Then
(w_{x,y})
is mean–zero Gaussian with stationary covariance

\kappa(\tau_{x},\tau_{y})=\frac{1}{HW}\sum_{u=0}^{H-1}\sum_{v=0}^{W-1}S(u,v)\,e^{2\pi i(u\tau_{x}/H+v\tau_{y}/W)}.

Appendix D
Variational Inference Details

We now describe the variational posterior used for spectral layers.
The design goals are:
(i) respect the support implied by Hermitian symmetry (real DC/Nyquist, conjugate pairs);
(ii) model correlated posterior uncertainty across frequencies and channels; and
(iii) retain closed-form KLs against the diagonal spectral prior from Sec.
5
, so the ELBO stays simple.

Effective coordinates.

For a given spectral layer, let
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{half}}
denote the nonredundant complex
RFFT coefficients on the Hermitian half-spectrum (1D) or half-plane (2D) used by the implementation.
Only a subset of real and imaginary parts are free: self-conjugate frequencies
(DC and Nyquist, when present; and the corresponding boundary frequencies in 2D) are real-valued,
with imaginary parts deterministically zero. We collect all free real degrees of freedom into
an effective coordinate vector
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}
and define a fixed linear map

T:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}\to\mathbb{C}^{\text{half}},\qquad\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{half}}=T(\mathbf{a}),

which packs
\mathbf{a}
into the RFFT storage format (inserting the real self-conjugate bins and forming
real/imaginary pairs for the remaining frequencies). The corresponding spatial filter is recovered by

\mathbf{w}=\IRFFT(\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{half}})\qquad(\text{or }\mathbf{W}=\IRFFT_{2}(\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{half}})\text{ in 2D).}

All maps are fixed and linear.

Hermitian-aware low-rank Gaussian.

On
\mathbf{a}
we choose a low-rank + diagonal Gaussian:

q(\mathbf{a})\;=\;\mathcal{MVN}\!\Big(\bm{\mu},\;\mathbf{U}\,\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\lambda}^{2})\mathbf{U}^{\top}\;+\;\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\sigma}^{2})\;+\;\varepsilon\mathbf{I}\Big).

(25)

with rank
r
(we use
r{=}8
), mean
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}
,
factor-loading matrix
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}\times r}
,
scale vector
\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
, diagonal variance
\bm{\sigma}^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}
,
and small jitter
\varepsilon>0
for numerical stability.
Samples are obtained via a standard reparameterization:

\mathbf{a}\;=\;\bm{\mu}\;+\;\mathbf{U}(\bm{\lambda}\odot\xi)\;+\;\bm{\sigma}\odot\zeta,\quad\xi\sim\mathcal{MVN}(0,\mathbf{I}_{r}),\;\zeta\sim\mathcal{MVN}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}),

followed by
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{half}}=T(\mathbf{a})
and
\mathbf{w}=\IRFFT(\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{half}})
. (or
\mathbf{W}=\IRFFT_{2}(\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{half}})
in 2D).
The low-rank term
\mathbf{U}(\bm{\lambda}\odot\xi)
captures global correlations across frequencies and channels; the diagonal term
\bm{\sigma}\odot\zeta
provides per-coordinate flexibility.

Compatibility with the spectral GP prior.

Recall that the prior in Sec.
5
factorizes over frequencies in the complex domain.
Each non-self-conjugate frequency has a proper complex Gaussian prior with variance
S(k)
(or
S(u,v)
in 2D), while self-conjugate bins are real with variance
S
.
Because
T
is linear, the induced prior on effective coordinates
\mathbf{a}
is again Gaussian with a
known
diagonal covariance
C_{\text{prior}}
.
For practical purposes, one can view each complex bin as a 2D real normal with covariance
\frac{S}{2}I_{2}
, and each real bin as 1D normal with variance
S
.
The KL term

\operatorname{KL}\big(q(\mathbf{a})\,\|\,p(\mathbf{a})\big)

in the ELBO is then a closed-form KL between Gaussians with a low-rank + diagonal covariance and a diagonal covariance.
We compute it using the matrix determinant lemma and the Woodbury identity.
This keeps the prior term analytic and cheap.

Spectral hyperparameters.

When learning a spectral slope
\alpha
(Sec.
4
) we introduce an unconstrained scalar
\alpha_{z}
and a monotone map
\alpha=g(\alpha_{z})\in[0,\infty)
, e.g. a softplus.
We either:
(i) treat
\alpha_{z}
as part of a base mean-field guide (with its own Gaussian factor), or
(ii) include it directly in the layer-specific guide.
In both cases the prior on
\alpha_{z}
is Gaussian and contributes another closed-form Gaussian KL term.

Variational distribution factorization.

In a multi-layer network the full variational posterior factorizes as

q(\bm{\theta})\;=\;\prod_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{\text{spec}}}q_{\ell}(\mathbf{a}_{\ell})\;\times\;q_{\mathrm{base}}\big(\bm{\theta}\setminus\{\text{spectral sites}\}\big),

where each
q_{\ell}
is a Hermitian-aware low-rank Gaussian over the spectral layer
\ell
,
and
q_{\mathrm{base}}
is a standard mean-field Gaussian over all remaining parameters (e.g. non-spectral weights, biases, and possibly spectral hyperparameters).
This separation keeps the Hermitian bookkeeping local to each spectral layer while leaving the remaining parameters in a simple variational family.

SVI objective and update.

Let
\mathcal{D}
denote the data and
\bm{\theta}
all parameters, including spectral and nonspectral ones.
Our ELBO is

\mathcal{L}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta})}\!\big[\log p(\mathcal{D}\mid\bm{\theta})\big]-\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{\text{spec}}}\operatorname{KL}\!\big(q_{\phi}(\mathbf{a}_{\ell})\,\|\,p(\mathbf{a}_{\ell})\big)-\operatorname{KL}\!\big(q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{base}})\,\|\,p(\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{base}})\big).

where
\phi
collects all variational parameters and
p(\mathbf{a}_{\ell})
is the spectral GP prior in effective coordinates.
We estimate the expectation with the reparameterization trick, and compute the Gaussian KL terms in closed form.

A single SVI step for one minibatch proceeds as:

1.

For each spectral layer
\ell
:
sample
\mathbf{a}_{\ell}\sim q_{\phi}(\mathbf{a}_{\ell})
,
compute
\mathbf{f}_{\ell,\mathrm{half}}=T(\mathbf{a}_{\ell})
and
\mathbf{w}_{\ell}=\IRFFT(\mathbf{f}_{\ell,\mathrm{half}})
(or
\IRFFT_{2}
in 2D).

2.

Sample nonspectral parameters from
q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{base}})
.

3.

Run a forward pass through the network with these sampled parameters to evaluate
\log p(\mathcal{D}\,|\,\bm{\theta})
.

4.

Accumulate the closed-form KLs and update
\phi
with stochastic gradients of the ELBO.

Complexity.

For a spectral layer with
d_{\mathrm{eff}}
effective coordinates and rank
r
, the parameter cost of (
25
) is
O(d_{\mathrm{eff}}r+d_{\mathrm{eff}})
; sampling and KL evaluation are
O(d_{\mathrm{eff}}r)
.
These costs depend on the number of Fourier coefficients, not on the spatial grid, and are dominated by the FFTs in the forward/backward passes.
We found
r{=}8
to be a robust default across all experiments.

Appendix E
Lipschitz Bounds and Margin Certificates: Details

The FFT-diagonal parameterization of our layers gives exact spectral norms essentially “for free”.
We now spell out the resulting global
\ell_{2}
Lipschitz bounds and the margin-based certificates used in the experiments, and we provide the proof of the prior high-probability bound from the main text.

1D spectral-circulant layers.

Consider a 1D spectral-circulant layer with half-spectrum

\mathbf{h}_{\text{half}}\in\mathbb{C}^{k_{\text{half}}}
, acting on
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

T_{\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{x}\;=\;\IRFFT\!\big(\mathbf{h}_{\text{half}}(\bm{\theta})\odot\RFFT(\mathbf{x})\big).

(26)

Let
\mathbf{F}
denote the unitary DFT matrix on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and

\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{full}}(\bm{\theta})\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
the Hermitian-completed full spectrum corresponding to
\mathbf{h}_{\text{half}}(\bm{\theta})
, with scalar entries
h_{k}(\bm{\theta})
.
Then

T_{\bm{\theta}}\;=\;\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}\operatorname{diag}\!\big(\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{full}}(\bm{\theta})\big)\,\mathbf{F},

(27)

which is normal, with eigenvalues given by the entries
\{h_{k}(\bm{\theta})\}_{k=0}^{d-1}
of
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{full}}(\bm{\theta})
.
Hence the operator norm equals the maximum eigenvalue magnitude:

\|T_{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2\to 2}\;=\;\max_{0\leq k\leq d-1}|h_{k}(\bm{\theta})|\;=\;\max_{0\leq k\leq k_{\mathrm{half}}-1}\big|(\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{half}}(\bm{\theta}))_{k}\big|.

(28)

Prior typical-case bound.

Proposition
2
(main text) shows that unusually large spectral norms are exponentially unlikely under the diagonal spectral prior. We prove it here.

Proof of Proposition
2
.

Let
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{cplx}}
denote the active non-self-conjugate bins and

\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{real}}
the active self-conjugate bins (DC and, if applicable, Nyquist),
so
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{cplx}}\cup\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{real}}
and
m=|\mathcal{K}|
.

For
k\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{cplx}}
, the diagonal spectral prior draws

h_{k}=A_{k}+iB_{k}
with
A_{k},B_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,S(k)/2)
.
Hence
|h_{k}|^{2}/S(k)\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)
and therefore

\Pr\!\big[|h_{k}|\geq t\big]\;=\;\exp\!\Big(-\frac{t^{2}}{S(k)}\Big).

(29)

For
k\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{real}}
, we have
h_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,S(k))
, so the standard Gaussian tail bound gives

\Pr\!\big[|h_{k}|\geq t\big]\;\leq\;2\exp\!\Big(-\frac{t^{2}}{2S(k)}\Big).

(30)

Since
\exp(-t^{2}/S(k))\leq 2\exp(-t^{2}/(2S(k)))
for all
t\geq 0
, both cases satisfy the uniform bound

\Pr\!\big[|h_{k}|\geq t\big]\;\leq\;2\exp\!\Big(-\frac{t^{2}}{2S(k)}\Big),\qquad k\in\mathcal{K}.

(31)

By a union bound over active bins,

\displaystyle\Pr\!\Big[\max_{k\in\mathcal{K}}|h_{k}|\geq t\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\Pr\!\big[|h_{k}|\geq t\big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}2\exp\!\Big(-\frac{t^{2}}{2S(k)}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2m\,\exp\!\Big(-\frac{t^{2}}{2S_{\max}}\Big),

(32)

where
S_{\max}=\max_{k\in\mathcal{K}}S(k)
.
Finally, Eq. (
28
) implies
\|T_{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2\to 2}=\max_{k\in\mathcal{K}}|h_{k}|
, yielding the stated tail bound.
Setting
t=\sqrt{2S_{\max}\log\!\frac{2m}{\delta}}
gives
\Pr\!\big[\|T_{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2\to 2}\leq t\big]\geq 1-\delta
.
∎

Corollary 7

(Prior typical-case control of the product Lipschitz bound)
.

Consider a network with
L
spectral-circulant layers and 1-Lipschitz activations, and let
\widehat{\operatorname{Lip}}(f_{\bm{\theta}})
denote the product upper bound defined above.
Assume the
L
spectral layers have independent diagonal spectral priors, with active bin counts
m_{\ell}
and maxima
S_{\ell,\max}
.
Then for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
the following holds simultaneously for all spectral layers:

\|T_{\ell}\|_{2\to 2}\leq\sqrt{2S_{\ell,\max}\log\!\frac{2m_{\ell}L}{\delta}},\qquad\ell=1,\ldots,L,

and therefore

\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\|T_{\ell}\|_{2\to 2}\;\leq\;\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\sqrt{2S_{\ell,\max}\log\!\frac{2m_{\ell}L}{\delta}}.

2D multi-channel BCCB layers.

For 2D BCCB layers with multiple channels, the same principle holds frequencywise.
Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\mathrm{in}}\times H\times W}
,

\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\mathrm{out}}\times H\times W}
, and spectral kernel

\mathbf{K}_{\text{half}}\in\mathbb{C}^{C_{\mathrm{out}}\times C_{\mathrm{in}}\times H\times(W/2+1)}
.
Using the full complex 2D DFT for clarity, flatten the input/output across spatial dimensions as

\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\mathrm{in}}HW}
and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\mathrm{out}}HW}
, and apply the 2D DFT channelwise via unitary maps

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{in}}:=\mathbf{I}_{C_{\mathrm{in}}}\otimes\mathbf{F}_{2\mathrm{D}},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{out}}:=\mathbf{I}_{C_{\mathrm{out}}}\otimes\mathbf{F}_{2\mathrm{D}},

(33)

where
\mathbf{F}_{2\mathrm{D}}
is the unitary 2D DFT on the spatial grid.
In the frequency domain, the layer acts blockwise as

\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_{k}=K_{\bm{\theta}}(k)\,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\qquad k\in\{0,\ldots,HW{-}1\},

(34)

with channel-mixing matrices
K_{\bm{\theta}}(k)\in\mathbb{C}^{C_{\mathrm{out}}\times C_{\mathrm{in}}}
.
Stacking over
k
yields a block-diagonal map

\widehat{\mathbf{y}}=D_{\bm{\theta}}\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\qquad D_{\bm{\theta}}=\mathrm{blockdiag}\big(K_{\bm{\theta}}(k_{1}),\ldots,K_{\bm{\theta}}(k_{HW})\big),

(35)

and the spatial operator is

T_{\bm{\theta}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{out}}^{\dagger}D_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{in}}.

(36)

Unitary pre-/post-multiplication preserves the operator norm, and the norm of a block-diagonal matrix is the maximum block norm, giving

\displaystyle\|T_{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2\to 2}

\displaystyle=\|D_{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2\to 2}=\max_{k}\,\|K_{\bm{\theta}}(k)\|_{2\to 2}=\max_{(u,v)}\sigma_{\max}\!\big(K_{\bm{\theta}}(u,v)\big),

(37)

where
\sigma_{\max}
denotes the largest singular value.
Hermitian symmetry constrains which frequencies are stored but does not change these singular values, so the maximum can be taken over the nonredundant RFFT
2
half-plane.
In practice, these per-frequency norms involve only small
C_{\mathrm{out}}\times C_{\mathrm{in}}
matrices and can be computed exactly via SVD; a few power-iteration steps are a cheaper approximation when needed.

Network-level Lipschitz bounds.

Consider a network
f_{\bm{\theta}}
obtained by composing linear layers
\{T_{\ell,\bm{\theta}}\}
and 1-Lipschitz activations (e.g.
\tanh
).
Ignoring biases (which are translations), a standard product bound yields

\displaystyle\operatorname{Lip}(f_{\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{\operatorname{Lip}}(f_{\bm{\theta}})

(38)

\displaystyle\widehat{\operatorname{Lip}}(f_{\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle:=\prod_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{\text{spec}}}\biggl(\max_{(u,v)}\sigma_{\max}\bigl(K_{\ell,\bm{\theta}}(u,v)\bigr)\biggr)\cdot\prod_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{\text{dense}}}\|\mathbf{W}_{\ell}(\bm{\theta})\|_{2}.

(39)

Here
\mathcal{L}_{\text{spec}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{dense}}
denote the sets of spectral BCCB and dense layers, respectively.
For spectral circulant (1D) layers, the corresponding factor is
\|T_{\ell,\bm{\theta}}\|_{2\to 2}=\max_{k}|h_{\ell,k}(\bm{\theta})|
(Eq. (
28
) applied to layer
\ell
).

Margin-based
\ell_{2}
certificates.

Given logits
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
and a true label
y
, define the logit margin

m_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x},y)=f_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})_{y}-\max_{k\neq y}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})_{k}.

(40)

A standard Lipschitz–margin argument then yields a global
\ell_{2}
robustness certificate
(
41
;
17
)

r_{\mathrm{cert}}(\mathbf{x},y;\bm{\theta})\;=\;\frac{\max\{m_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x},y),0\}}{2\,\widehat{\operatorname{Lip}}(f_{\bm{\theta}})}.

(41)

For any perturbation
\bm{\delta}
with
\|\bm{\delta}\|_{2}\leq r_{\mathrm{cert}}(\mathbf{x},y;\bm{\theta})
, the predicted label cannot change.
In our Bayesian experiments we sample
\bm{\theta}\sim q(\bm{\theta})
, compute
m_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x},y)
and
r_{\mathrm{cert}}(\mathbf{x},y;\bm{\theta})
on the test set, and study their empirical posterior distributions (Sec.
7.3
).
These certificates are global and
\ell_{2}
-based; they are intended as a diagnostic of the geometry induced by spectral heads rather than a full adversarial-robustness claim.

Appendix F
Experimental Details

F.1
OOD Evaluation Protocol

We evaluate out-of-distribution (OOD) detection using predictive probabilities

p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})\in\Delta^{K-1}
over
K
classes. All logarithms are natural (nats).

Predictive entropy.

H\!\big(p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})\big)\;=\;-\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{\bm{\theta}}(y{=}k\mid\mathbf{x})\,\log p_{\bm{\theta}}(y{=}k\mid\mathbf{x}).

ID metrics.

Negative log-likelihood (NLL), Brier, and calibration follow

16
:

\displaystyle\text{NLL}\;=\;-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p_{\bm{\theta}}(y_{i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{i})

\displaystyle\text{Brier}\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big\|p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}_{i})-\mathbf{e}_{y_{i}}\big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{ECE}\;=\;\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{|B_{m}|}{N}\,\big|\text{acc}(B_{m})-\text{conf}(B_{m})\big|

\displaystyle\text{MCE}\;=\;\max_{m}\big|\text{acc}(B_{m})-\text{conf}(B_{m})\big|.

Here
B_{m}
are
M{=}15
equal-width confidence bins in
[0,1]
,
\text{conf}(B_{m})
is
the average
\max_{k}p_{\bm{\theta}}(y{=}k\mid\mathbf{x})
in the bin, and
\text{acc}(B_{m})
is the
fraction correct in that bin.

OOD scores and thresholds.

Following standard practice
(
19
)
and the survey in

27
, we use
negative entropy
as an ID score:

s(\mathbf{x})=-H\big(p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})\big)
(larger
\Rightarrow
more ID-like).

\text{AUROC}\;=\;\text{AUC}\big(\{(s(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\text{ID}}),1)\}\cup\{(s(\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\text{OOD}}),0)\}\big),

\displaystyle\text{FPR@95\%TPR}\;=\;\Pr_{\mathbf{x}^{\text{OOD}}}\!\big[s(\mathbf{x}^{\text{OOD}})\geq t_{0.95}\big]

\displaystyle\text{where }t_{0.95}\text{ is the }5\text{th percentile of }s(\mathbf{x}^{\text{ID}}).

Entropy KDE plots.

We display kernel density estimates of
H
on
[0,\ln K]
, using boundary
reflection and Scott’s bandwidth; figures report both full range and a zoomed
window (default
[0,0.12]
).

Appendix G
Vision Transformer Experiments

We next ask whether the same spectral circulant primitives are useful in a modern transformer architecture trained deterministically. We consider a compact ViT encoder with patch embedding, multi-head self-attention, and an MLP block per layer. The
dense
baseline uses standard linear projections for
Q
,
K
,
V
, the attention output, and all MLP layers. The
spectral
variant keeps the architecture and training protocol fixed, and replaces all
square
linear projections (
d_{\text{model}}\!\to\!d_{\text{model}}
) by
SpectralCirculant1d
layers; non-square projections (e.g. patch embedding and classifier head) remain dense.

Both models are trained with the same optimizer and schedule described in Sec.
7
: Adan with warmup–cosine decay, peak learning rate
10^{-3}
, weight decay
10^{-4}
, batch size
128
, and up to
200
epochs. For each configuration we select the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss.

Training dynamics and performance on CIFAR-10.

On CIFAR-10 we use
32{\times}32
images, patch size
4
, and a ViT with
d_{\text{model}}{=}128
as described above. Under the shared training recipe, both the dense and spectral models converge stably. The spectral variant attains its best validation loss later in training (epoch 30 vs. 16 for the dense model), but we do not observe optimization instabilities.

On the CIFAR-10 test set, the best checkpoints achieve

\displaystyle\text{Dense ViT (CIFAR-10):}

\displaystyle\text{acc}=0.6851,\;\text{NLL}=0.9660,

\displaystyle\text{Spectral ViT (CIFAR-10):}

\displaystyle\text{acc}=0.7157,\;\text{NLL}=0.8782.

Under identical hyperparameters, the spectral circulant ViT therefore attains higher accuracy and lower negative log-likelihood than the dense baseline.

Tiny ImageNet.

To test whether this behaviour is specific to CIFAR-10, we repeat the experiment on Tiny ImageNet (200 classes,
64{\times}64
images), using patch size
8
so that the number of tokens per image matches the CIFAR-10 setup. We keep the architecture and training hyperparameters fixed and only change the number of classes. Under this matched recipe, both models again converge reliably, and the spectral ViT prefers a substantially later best-validation epoch: epoch 13 for the dense model vs. epoch 58 for the spectral model.

On Tiny ImageNet, the dense ViT achieves
30.7\%
top-1 accuracy and an NLL of
3.06
with
1.25
M parameters, while the spectral ViT attains
34.4\%
top-1 accuracy and an NLL of
2.94
with
0.85
M parameters. While these absolute numbers are below heavily tuned CNN and transformer baselines reported in the literature, they are consistent with small ViT models trained from scratch on Tiny ImageNet without pretraining or aggressive regularization

(
28
;
39
, e.g.)
. Across both datasets the spectral variant selects a substantially later best-validation epoch than the dense model, which is consistent with the hypothesis that the spectral parametrization delays overfitting and allows the model to continue improving for longer under the same schedule. The qualitative trend mirrors our CIFAR-10 results: spectral circulant projections yield better likelihood and higher accuracy at a reduced parameter budget under a shared, untuned training recipe.

Parameter counts.

Replacing each
d_{\text{model}}\!\times\!d_{\text{model}}
linear map by a circulant matrix reduces the number of parameters in those projections from
O(d_{\text{model}}^{2})
to
O(d_{\text{model}})
. In our CIFAR-10 configuration the total parameter counts are

\displaystyle\text{Dense ViT (CIFAR-10): }1{,}205{,}898\text{ parameters}

\displaystyle\text{Spectral ViT (CIFAR-10): }811{,}218\text{ parameters},

while in the Tiny ImageNet setting (with a larger classifier head) we obtain

\displaystyle\text{Dense ViT (Tiny): }1{,}248{,}840\text{ parameters}

\displaystyle\text{Spectral ViT (Tiny): }854{,}160\text{ parameters}.

In both cases the spectral variant uses roughly
1.4
–
1.5\times
fewer parameters overall. Table
4
summarizes performance and model size across datasets.

Overall, these experiments show that spectral circulant layers are not restricted to small Bayesian networks or probabilistic heads: they can be used as drop-in replacements for dense projections in ViT-like architectures, trained with standard first-order optimizers, and provide a favourable accuracy–parameter trade-off without architecture-specific hyperparameter tuning.

Curvature diagnostics.

To better understand how the spectral parametrization affects optimization geometry in the attention projections, we estimate dominant curvature directions using Hessian–vector products and Gauss–Newton vector products, following standard practice for curvature diagnostics in deep networks
(
12
)
. We report the resulting dominant eigenvalues both globally and in an attention-only subspace (restricted to the
Q/K/V
and output projection parameters) at the final selected checkpoints.
Using Hessian–vector products and a small number of power-iteration steps, following standard practice for curvature diagnostics in deep networks
(
12
)
, we approximate the dominant eigenvalues of the Hessian and Gauss–Newton matrices of the validation loss on a small held-out batch at the final checkpoints. Table
10
reports results for both CIFAR-10 and Tiny ImageNet, considering the full parameter space and the same attention-only subspace described above.

On CIFAR-10, in the attention subspace the spectral ViT has a much smaller dominant (largest-magnitude) Hessian eigenvalue than the dense model (approximately
8.5\times
smaller), and an even more pronounced reduction in the top Gauss–Newton eigenvalue (about
21\times
). This indicates substantially flatter curvature around the optimum in the directions where
SpectralCirculant1d
layers are applied. When all parameters are included, the spectral ViT exhibits moderately larger dominant eigenvalues (roughly
1.6\times
for both Hessian and Gauss–Newton), suggesting that sharp directions are less concentrated in attention and more concentrated in the remaining dense layers (MLPs and classifier head). The same qualitative pattern appears on Tiny ImageNet: spectral projections yield significantly smaller dominant eigenvalues in the attention subspace (about
25\times
smaller for the Hessian and
16\times
for Gauss–Newton), while the global top eigenvalues are larger for the spectral model, reflecting stronger curvature elsewhere in the network.

These diagnostics are local and parameterization-dependent, and we use them only for relative comparison between the two ViT variants. Taken together with the improved test accuracy and reduced parameter counts in Table
4
, they support the view that spectral circulant projections offer a favourable compactness–geometry trade-off in transformer architectures.
