Through-the-scope fully covered self-expandable metal stent with biflanged ends for ampullary access in severe duodenal obstruction
Authors: Rahul Karna, Guru Trikudanathan, Nabeel Azeem, Stuart K Amateau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsIndeterminate gastric outlet obstruction can be managed with placement of temporary stents; however, currently available stents are not designed for duodenal placement, and downstream migration is a significant concern.MethodsThis Original Article and accompanying video demonstrate placement of a through-the-scope fully covered self-expandable metal stent (FCSEMS) with biflanged ends across the duodenal stenosis.ResultsIn the case example of a patient with severe duodenal stenosis, despite utilization of a lumen-apposing metal stent, various duodenoscopes, and patient repositioning, the ampulla could not be reached. Subsequently, the FCSEMS with biflanged ends (midbody diameter: 20 mm; flare diameter: 28 mm) was placed across the duodenal stenosis, and ERCP could be successfully performed through the stent.ConclusionsThis case highlights the successful placement of an FCSEMS with biflanged ends in a patient with indeterminate gastric outlet obstruction for eventual ampullary access. The wide caliber of the stent allowed duodenoscope maneuverability and sphincterotome bowing to facilitate ERCP. The biflanged ends on the FCSEMS may potentially provide antimigratory properties, which is a concern for currently available stents for the management of duodenal obstruction.

Introduction

Indeterminate gastric outlet obstruction (GOO) can occur because of benign or malignant etiologies involving the stomach or duodenum. Restoration of the enteral conduit may significantly improve quality of life by improving nutritional status, decreasing frailty, and subsequently allowing biliary access if required. Current strategies for the management of GOO include enteral stenting or creation of gastrojejunostomy via surgical or EUS-guided approaches.1 Enteral stenting using an uncovered self-expandable metal stent (U-SEMS) can be performed for malignant stenosis; however, temporary stents are required for GOO due to indeterminate etiologies. Recently, a through-the-scope (TTS) esophageal fully covered self-expandable metal stent (FCSEMS) with biflanged ends (Resilience; Merit Medical, South Jordan, Utah, USA) has become available. Here, we demonstrate an off-label use of the esophageal FCSEMS with biflanged ends in a patient with indeterminate duodenal stenosis, allowing ampullary access and duodenoscope maneuverability to facilitate biliary access.

Case presentation

An 80-year-old white female with a history of a 31- × 18-mm multiloculated pancreatic neck cyst (Fig. 1A) with elevated CA 19-9 (167 U/mL) and failed Whipple procedure complicated by duodenal stenosis (Fig. 2A) presented for ERCP for evaluation of elevated liver enzymes. She had previously undergone successful EUS-guided radiofrequency ablation of the pancreatic cyst after multidisciplinary evaluation by specialists in medical oncology, pancreas surgery, and gastroenterology (Fig. 1B). Surveillance MRI and EUS demonstrated postablation changes at the pancreatic neck. GOO, due to high-grade duodenal sweep stenosis measuring 15 mm in length, was previously managed with placement of a 15- × 15-mm lumen-apposing metal stent (LAMS) (AXIOS; Boston Scientific, Marlborough, Mass, USA) (Fig. 2B). Laboratory evaluation on the day of the procedure reported aspartate aminotransferase 154 U/L, alanine aminotransferase 207 U/L, total bilirubin 9.3 mg/dL, and alkaline phosphatase 524 IU/L, while other routine blood tests were within normal limits.

Procedure

The patient was placed in the prone position for ERCP under general anesthesia (Video 1, available online at www.videogie.org). Scout films demonstrated a fully expanded previously placed LAMS across the duodenal sweep, from mid bulb to above the ampulla. Despite utilization of a 12.5-mm outer-diameter duodenoscope, an 11.3-mm outer-diameter duodenoscope (TJF-Q190V, Olympus, Center Valley, Pa, USA), a 7.5-mm outer-diameter pediatric duodenoscope, and a 10-mm outer-diameter single-channel therapeutic gastroscope (Olympus) along with patient repositioning to supine and left lateral position, the previously placed LAMS could not be traversed. The LAMS was removed with rat-toothed forceps, revealing a macerated duodenal stricture with engorged villi. Only a single-channel therapeutic gastroscope with a 10.9-mm outer-diameter gastroscope (GIF-1TH190, Olympus) could bridge the stricture, and therefore, a decision was made to place a TTS FCSEMS with biflanged ends (flare diameter: 28 mm; midbody diameter: 20 mm; length: 50 mm) under fluoroscopic and endoscopic guidance (Fig. 3A and B). This was placed to span the stricture, with the distal end just upstream of the ampulla and proximal across the pylorus. The stent was subsequently dilated over a 0.025- × 450-cm guidewire (VisiGlide; Olympus) using a fixed wire balloon dilator (Elation; Merit Medical) to 19 mm with partial effect. Same-session attempts to traverse the stent failed; hence, the decision was made to reattempt ERCP after allowing the FCSEMS with biflanged ends to expand. Subsequent ERCP 2 days later was performed with an 11.3-mm outer-diameter duodenoscope, and an ampullary mass was visualized and biopsied (Fig. 4). Cholangiogram during ERCP demonstrated diffuse mild intrahepatic and extrahepatic diffuse dilation and distal common duct stenosis involving the ampulla suggestive of malignancy involving the common bile duct (Fig. 5). The patient underwent uncomplicated biliary sphincterotomy, sweep removal of debris, and placement of a 10F × 5-cm common bile duct stent with internal and external flaps (Sof-Flex; Cook Medical), a 7F × 7-cm common bile duct stent with an internal flap, an external pigtail (Zimmon; Cook Medical) across the stenosis, and a prophylactic 4F × 3-cm single-pigtail pancreatic duct stent (Hobbs Medical Inc, Stafford Springs, Conn, USA) (Fig. 6A and B). The ampullary biopsies confirmed adenocarcinoma, and eventually, the FCSEMS with biflanged ends was exchanged to a 10- × 60-mm U-SEMS biliary stent (Zilver 635, Cook Medical, Bloomington, Ind, USA) and a 22- × 90-mm duodenal U-SEMS (Evolution, Cook Ireland Ltd, Limerick, Ireland) after multidisciplinary discussion.

Discussion

This case demonstrates successful utilization of a TTS FCSEMS with biflanged ends in a patient with indeterminate GOO for ampullary access, allowing duodenoscope and sphincterotome maneuverability to facilitate ERCP.

Currently, the available options for the management of GOO include enteral stenting, EUS-guided gastroenterostomy (EUS-GE), and surgical gastroenterostomy (GE). The choice of technique depends on patient comorbidities, nature of the stenosis, anatomy, and goals of treatment and often requires multidisciplinary discussion of all available options to determine the right strategy for each individual patient.2 Treatment goals remain the cornerstone consideration, as the goal with benign GOO is durable symptom resolution, while palliative decompression is the goal for patients with malignant GOO.3

Endoscopic balloon dilation (EBD) and placement of covered SEMSs are common endoscopic therapy options for benign GOO. Prior studies have established superior safety and efficacy of EBD compared to SEMSs for benign GOO.4 Surgical GE is often reserved for patients who have failed endoscopic therapy or developed adverse events from endoscopic therapy. However, robust data comparing surgical GE with endoscopic therapy for benign GOO are lacking. EUS-GE is associated with high clinical response rates (90.6%) and low adverse event rate (11%) for benign GOO; however, it is usually performed as a bridge to surgery to improve nutritional status.5 Long-term data on outcomes of EUS-GE as definitive therapy for benign indications are lacking. The comparative data between the available therapies for the management of malignant GOO are more robust. In a systematic review and network meta-analysis, surgical GE (RR, 0.82) and enteral stenting (RR, 0.91) were inferior to EUS-GE for the management of malignant GOO.6 Enteral stenting was associated with a significantly higher risk of reintervention compared to EUS-GE (RR, 7.69) and surgical GE (RR, 6.66).6 In terms of reintervention rate, EUS-GE was the superior treatment approach (surface under the cumulative ranking, 0.81).6

The management of indeterminate stenosis confers additional consideration of the lack of diagnosis. In our patient, despite attempts at sampling the periduodenal mass, the pathology results were benign or nondiagnostic. LAMSs can be placed for short, benign gastroduodenal stenosis; however, it is not recommended for malignant strictures because of risks of tumor ingrowth, migration, and short length of the stent, making it unsuitable for progressive malignant strictures. Overall, clinical success for gastroduodenal strictures is 67.4%, with a migration rate of 10.9%.7 In this case, a 15- × 15-mm LAMS was initially placed for indeterminate GOO and later exchanged to an FCSEMS with biflanged ends to allow ampullary access for the assessment of ampullary pathology. The patient could not be committed to a more durable therapy for palliation of malignant GOO including placement of an U-SEMS, surgical GE, or EUS-GE while awaiting diagnosis.

The major limitation of covered SEMSs is high migration rates. In a meta-analysis of 5 randomized controlled trials, covered SEMSs had a significantly higher migration rate compared to U-SEMSs (26/221, 11.8% vs 6/222, 2.7%; relative difference, 0.09).8 Predictors of covered SEMS migration include extrinsic tumor location, shorter stent length, and chemotherapy after stent placement, although longer stent length and presence of antimigratory design may prevent migration.9, 10, 11 The wide caliber of an FCSEMS with biflanged ends allowed duodenoscope maneuverability and bowing of the sphincterotomy for biliary access. The patient eventually underwent placement of a definitive U-SEMS after the malignant nature of stenosis was confirmed. In principle, the presence of double flanges on the FCSEMS may potentially provide antimigratory properties leading to more durable clinical response for the management of GOO. However, long-term follow-up data on a large cohort of patients are required to ascertain the risk of migration.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we demonstrate successful placement of an FCSEMS with biflanged ends in a patient with indeterminate GOO for eventual ampullary access. The shorter overall stent length allowed precise therapy of duodenal stenosis, and the wide caliber of the stent allowed duodenoscope maneuverability and sphincterotome bowing to facilitate successful ERCP.
