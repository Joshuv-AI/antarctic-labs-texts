Comparative Assessment of Microleakage in Nanoformulated and Conventional Glass Ionomer Cement under Simulated Aging: A Stereomicroscopic Study
Authors: Aleena Alex, Jessy Paulraj, TM Ngoubinah Pretty, Subhabrata Maiti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThe aim of the study is to assess the microleakage of nanoformulated and conventional glass ionomer cements (GICs) under simulated aging.Materials and methodsNanoformulated GIC was synthesized by incorporating chitosan-titanium-zirconia-hydroxyapatite (Ch-Ti-Zr-HA) nanoparticles into GIC powder at concentrations of 3%, 5%, and 10%. The materials were characterized using UV-visible diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV-DRS) and X-ray diffraction (XRD). A total of forty freshly extracted, sound permanent molar teeth were selected for the study and divided into four groups: Group I (3% nanoformulated GIC), group II (5% nanoformulated GIC), group III (10% nanoformulated GIC), and group IV (conventional GIC). Standardized class I cavities were prepared on the occlusal surfaces, restored, and subjected to thermocycling between 5°C and 55°C with a dwell time of 30 seconds in a water bath. To prevent dye penetration, root apices were sealed, and all tooth surfaces—except the restoration and a 1 mm margin around it—were coated with two layers of nail varnish. The specimens were then immersed in a 0.5% methylene blue solution for 24 hours. Following immersion, the teeth were sectioned mesiodistally, and the extent of microleakage was assessed under a stereomicroscope using a predefined scoring system. The collected data were statistically analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis and Bonferroni test.ResultsThe least microleakage was observed in the 5% and 10% nanoformulated GIC groups, with no statistically significant difference between them (p = 0.699). The 3% nanoformulated GIC and the conventional GIC (control group) exhibited similar performance, with no significant difference. However, compared to the control group, both 5% and 10% nanoformulated GICs showed superior results, with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05).ConclusionThe findings of this study indicate that incorporating nanotechnology is a valuable approach to enhancing the clinical performance of GIC. 5% and 10% nanoformulated GIC containing Ch-Ti-Zr-HA demonstrates significant potential as a restorative material, offering microleakage resistance superior to conventional GIC.How to cite this articleAlex A, Paulraj J, Ngoubinah Pretty TM, et al. Introduction

Glass ionomer cements (GIC) are extensively used for restorative procedures, luting applications, and as base materials. Their widespread use is attributed to advantages such as ease of handling, strong adhesion, and fluoride release. However, GIC also exhibits certain limitations, including low wear resistance, opacity, and susceptibility to microleakage.1 Microleakage refers to the infiltration of bacteria, fluids, and ions along the interface, making it a failure of restorative materials.2 Poor marginal adaptation can lead to clinical complications such as marginal discoloration, postoperative sensitivity, bacterial ingress, secondary caries, restoration failure, and potential pulpal inflammation.2 According to a study by Matharu et al., microleakage can occur at both the micron and nanometer scales. While this leakage may not always be visibly detectable in clinical settings, it significantly impacts the longevity of restorations.3 Microleakage at the tooth-restoration interface can result in biological consequences, including recurrent caries, pulp irritation, hypersensitivity, and deterioration of the marginal seal.4

Recent research has shown that once cariogenic bacteria penetrate the tooth-restoration interface, they can multiply and potentially lead to adverse pulp reactions or recurrent caries.5–7 Additionally, studies indicate that the likelihood of recurrent caries increases in direct proportion to the size of the marginal gap.8 Therefore, ensuring a complete seal at the tooth-restoration interface is essential for minimizing microleakage and enhancing the longevity of restorative materials.8,9 In recent years, advancements in dental biomaterials have been significantly influenced by the integration of nanotechnology. Recent advancements suggest nanotechnology as a powerful tool to improve functional properties and clinical performance of GICs.10 By incorporating nanoparticles, investigators have achieved enhancements in the physical performance of GIC, addressing its inherent limitations and improving its interaction with dental hard tissues.11 Studies have demonstrated that the addition of nanoparticles such as chitosan, titanium, zirconia, and hydroxyapatite (Ch-Ti-Zr-HA) to conventional GIC significantly increases its flexural strength and wear resistance compared to traditional formulations.12–15

The clinical success of restorative materials is significantly influenced by their bonding capability with dental structures. A strong bond contributes to an intact marginal seal, which in turn helps prevent microleakage.16 This bond is essential for the material's durability under oral conditions, particularly against challenges such as temperature fluctuations, moisture exposure, and mechanical forces within the oral cavity. Thermocycling, a widely used artificial aging technique, is employed in laboratory studies to replicate the oral environment. During this process, the material undergoes repeated temperature variations, simulating the thermal stresses experienced in the mouth.17 This method helps evaluate the long-term performance and stability of restorative materials. The volumetric changes that occur at the tooth-restoration interface during thermocycling can generate interfacial stresses, particularly if the bonding is inadequate, potentially compromising the restoration's integrity over time.

The goal of this study was to assess the microleakage performance of nanoformulated GICs and compare it with conventional GICs. The primary focus was to determine whether the newly developed nanoformulated GIC offers equal or superior resistance to microleakage compared to conventional GIC. It was hypothesized that the microleakage scores would not differ significantly between the nanoformulated and conventional GIC at the tooth-restoration interface following thermocycling.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Sample Size Determination

This in vitro study was conducted at a University Dental Hospital in Chennai, India, with ethical approval (IRB No. SRB/SDC/UG-1953/23/PEDO/224). A sample of 40 specimens was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.3, with an effect size of 0.80 and α = 0.05.

Sample Collection

The extracted teeth were collected over 1 month from the Tooth Bank, Department of Oral Biology, University Hospital, Chennai. After ultrasonic scaling and root planning to remove organic debris, specimens were stored in a 0.9% NaCl solution to maintain integrity.

Criteria for Tooth Selection and Randomization

Only sound permanent mandibular molars were included; teeth with caries, structural defects, restorations, endodontic treatment, or root caries were excluded. Based on these selection criteria, eligible teeth were chosen and randomly assigned to study groups using the online tool Research Randomizer (https://www.randomizer.org/). An operator blinded to group allocation conducted the process, with the observer alone aware of the assignments, ensuring double blinding.

Preparation of Nanoparticles

Eucalyptus extract was prepared by boiling 1 gm of leaves in 100 mL of water (60–80°C, 5–10 minutes), then cooled and filtered. Chitosan (0.5 gm) was dissolved in glacial acetic acid (0.5 gm) and water (49 mL), mixed with sodium tripolyphosphate (4–5 drops), combined with eucalyptus extract, and incubated at 37°C to form chitosan nanoparticles. Neem leaves (1 gm) were boiled, filtered, and mixed with titanium oxide (0.365 gm in 50 mL water), stirred, and incubated (37°C) to synthesize titanium oxide nanoparticles. Zirconium oxide nanoparticles were formed by dissolving zirconium oxychloride octahydrate (20 mM) in 50 mL of water, mixing with aloe vera extract (1 gm boiled in 100 mL of water at 40°C–50°C), stirring at 340–360 rpm, and incubating overnight. Hydroxyapatite nanoparticles were prepared by calcining eggshells at 1000°C for 2 hours, grinding them into CaO powder, and mixing 0.1 gm of calcium oxide (CaO) with 50 mL of moringa extract, stirring for 2 hours, adding orthophosphoric acid (Ca/P ratio 1.67), and aging for 24 hours. Following Rahman et al.,18 Ch-Ti-Zr-HA nanoparticles were stirred at 80°C (30 minutes), refluxed with ethanol (1.08 mL, 90 minutes), and freeze-dried at–92°C for 48 hours to yield fine powder.

Preparation of Nanoformulated GIC and Grouping

Chitosan, titanium, zirconia, and hydroxyapatite nanoparticles were added to GIC at 3, 5, and 10 wt% (groups I–III); group IV was an unmodified control. Powders were premixed, then liquid was added to prepare cement.

Characterization

Ultraviolet Diffuse Reflectance Spectroscopy (UV-DRS)

The material's reflectance and absorbance in the UV range (200–1100 nm) were analyzed using the PerkinElmer UV WinLab Data Processor. Diffuse reflectance data were recorded under controlled conditions, and the corresponding absorbance spectra were obtained.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD)

X-ray diffraction (XGT 5200 WR, Horiba, Japan) was used to analyze diffraction patterns, referencing the JCPDS database. The detector scanned from 25 to 60 with a 0.02 step size and a 2.5-second step time, maintaining a fixed specimen position.

Procedure

Standardized class I cavities (0.8 mm width, 1.5 mm depth) were prepared on occlusal surfaces with a No. 245 bur under water spray. A single operator performed all preparations. Samples (n = 40) were divided into four groups (n = 10): Group I—3% nano-GIC, group II—5% nano-GIC, group III—10% nano-GIC, group IV—conventional GIC, and restored accordingly.

Thermocycling and Microleakage Testing

Samples underwent 500 thermocycles (5 ± 2°C to 55 ± 2°C, 20 seconds dwell). Root apices were sealed, and teeth coated with nail varnish, leaving 1 mm around restorations. Specimens were immersed in 0.5% methylene blue (24 hours), rinsed, dried, embedded in acrylic, sectioned mesiodistally with a diamond disc, and examined under a stereomicroscope (12×, Carl Zeiss, Italy). Microleakage was evaluated in both halves, with the section displaying the most extensive dye penetration used for grading. Two independent evaluators assessed microleakage along the tooth-restoration interface, and in cases of disagreement, a consensus was reached through reexamination.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS v29.0.2.0 (IBM, 2023). As Shapiro–Wilk showed non-normality (p < 0.05), the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied, with Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons for significant results.

Results

Interpretation of UV-DRS Analysis

The UV-DRS absorbance graph of the tested restorative material displays absorbance (A) values ranging from approximately −0.42 to 0.00669 over a wavelength range of 200 to 1100 nm (Figs 1 and 2A). The low absorbance indicates high transparency or reflectance, highlighting its excellent optical properties, which are beneficial for esthetic dental restorations. The minimal absorption in the visible and near-infrared regions suggests strong reflectance and stability under light exposure, enhancing resistance to discoloration over time. Similarly, the UV-DRS reflectance graph shows reflectance (%R) values between 95% and 327% across the same wavelength range (Fig. 2B). The high reflectance, especially in the visible and near-infrared regions, signifies superior optical stability and minimal light absorption, making the material highly suitable for esthetic dental applications.

Interpretation of XRD Analysis

The XRD analysis of the tested restorative material shows distinct diffraction peaks at 2θ values ranging from 7.665° to 79.164°, with prominent peaks at 7.665°, 8.109°, 25.502°, and 27.575°, indicating a well-crystallized structure. The corresponding d-spacing values, ranging from 11.5252 Å to 1.2089 Å, confirm a stable crystalline phase (Fig. 2C). The sharp peaks and high relative intensities suggest excellent phase purity and structural integrity, making the material highly suitable for dental restorations due to its superior mechanical strength, durability, and resistance to degradation.

Interpretation of Microleakage Scores

Two observers conducted the evaluations, and their external and internal validity, along with reliability, were assessed using the Kappa test. The test yielded a result exceeding 80%, indicating strong agreement between the observers. Once reliability was established, microleakage analysis across the groups was finalized (Fig. 3). The results present the distribution of microleakage scores for class I restorations based on observations from 40 samples across four groups (Table 1). Among them, five samples (12.5%) showed no microleakage (score 0), while 12 samples (30.0%) had mild microleakage (score 1). A total of 13 samples (32.5%) exhibited moderate microleakage (score 2), whereas seven samples (17.5%) showed higher leakage (score 3). The most severe microleakage (score 4) was observed in three samples (7.5%), all from the control and 3% nanoformulated GIC groups. The 5% and 10% nanoformulated GIC groups had no samples with a score of 4, indicating better sealing ability compared to the control. Overall, the 5% and 10% nanoformulated GICs demonstrated lower microleakage, suggesting their potential for improved restoration longevity.

Distribution of microleakage scores

The Kruskal–Wallis test evaluated microleakage differences among the four groups (Fig. 4). The 5% and 10% nanoformulated GICs showed lower median microleakage and narrower interquartile ranges (IQRs), indicating better sealing ability with less variability. In contrast, the 3% and control groups exhibited higher median values and wider IQRs, reflecting greater data dispersion. Overlapping confidence intervals suggest some differences may not be statistically significant. Overall, the results indicate that higher nano concentrations (5% and 10%) improve microleakage resistance compared to conventional GIC. Bonferroni pairwise comparisons showed no significant difference between the 10% and 5% nanoformulated GIC groups (p = 0.699), indicating similar performance in microleakage reduction (Table 2). However, both the 10% and 5% nanoformulated GICs showed significantly lower microleakage compared to the control group (p = 0.002 and 0.007, respectively), suggesting their superior sealing ability. Additionally, the 10% and 5% nanoformulated GICs performed significantly better than the 3% nanoformulated GIC (p = 0.001 and 0.003, respectively), indicating that higher nanoformulation percentages contribute to improved microleakage resistance. No significant difference was observed between the control and 3% nanoformulated GIC (p = 0.804), suggesting that the lower nano concentration did not offer an advantage over the conventional GIC in terms of microleakage prevention.

Discussion

The assessment of microleakage plays a pivotal role in determining the success or failure of restorative materials. Notably, it can cause sensitivity in the tooth due to the interfacial hydrodynamic phenomenon, which in turn can encourage microorganism colonization. The bacterial infiltration resulting from microleakage can lead to secondary caries, an issue that significantly contributes to the failure of dental restorations.20,21 For these reasons, microleakage is considered one of the most significant disadvantages associated with many restorative materials. Prior investigations have largely examined the microleakage of traditional GIC.22–24 However, the current investigation is the first reported to compare the performance of conventional GIC with a novel nanoformulated GIC, which incorporates Ch-Ti-Zr-HA. This modification aims to improve the material's mechanical properties while also enhancing its restorative potential.

The incorporation of these nanoparticles into GIC is considered to increase the mechanical strength of the material, especially in areas subjected to high stress. In addition to enhancing strength, the long-term sustainability of this novel GIC and its fluoride release properties make it a particularly appealing option for patients with a high caries index. Fluoride release is a critical feature in the prevention of caries, making this nanoformulated GIC a promising choice for patients at higher risk of developing cavities.25,26

The study found that adding Ch-Ti-Zr-HA to GIC significantly reduced microleakage, with notable differences observed between groups. This finding is important because it suggests that the inclusion of these nanoparticles, even in relatively low percentages, does not interfere with the ability of the GIC to bond effectively to the tooth structure. The absence of significant changes in 3% and control group can also be attributed to the minimal incorporation of nanoparticles in 3%. The significant difference was noted between 5% and 10% when compared against conventional GIC depicting the increased amount of microleakage in the control group. Previous research by Saida et al.27 found that conventional GIC exhibited higher microleakage values when compared to the nanoformulated GIC. This increased leakage in the conventional GIC could be due to dehydration, which tends to reduce the cohesive strength of the material. Dehydration may alter the properties of the cement, leading to weaker bonds and thus, a greater potential for leakage, which is in accordance with our study, where the highest values of microleakage were noted in conventional GIC when compared to other groups. This observation highlights the importance of material composition and its influence on the performance of restorative materials, particularly in the context of microleakage.

To simulate real-life oral conditions, thermocycling was employed in the study to mimic the temperature fluctuations that restorations experience in the mouth. This procedure is a standard protocol in restorative dentistry, as it evaluates how bonded materials behave under conditions that replicate in vivo aging. The thermocycling process subjects the materials to cyclic exposure to both hot and cold temperatures, allowing evaluation of the materials' durability over time.28,29 Additionally, class I cavities were prepared in the teeth, which takes into account the “C factor” (bonded-to-unbonded surface ratio).30 This factor is an important consideration in restorative procedures because it affects how stress is distributed within the restoration and the tooth structure.

Nanoformulated GIC demonstrated the lowest leakage by ensuring the longevity and effectiveness of the restoration. This outcome aligns with previous studies, which reported that nanoformulated GICs tend to offer better protection against microleakage.31–33 This improved performance is a result of the superior dimensional stability of the nanoformulated GIC, which helps it maintain its integrity over time. Additionally, its reduced thermal conductivity and enhanced chemical adhesion to dentin contribute to the material's superior performance in sealing the restoration and preventing microleakage. Previous research by Petri et al. supports these findings, noting that small amounts of chitosan added to GIC led to improved mechanical properties.34 Chitosan particles interact with polyacrylic acid around the inorganic components, lowering interfacial tension within the GIC. This reduction in tension helps to enhance the overall mechanical performance of the material. In another study by Elsaka et al., it was reported that the addition of chitosan led to improved microtensile bond strength, which is an indicator of the material's adhesive properties.35 This improvement in bond strength is significant because it directly impacts the overall sealing ability of the material, which in turn affects the degree of microleakage. Kini et al. demonstrated that alkali restorative material had the lowest microleakage compared to conventional GICs,36 suggesting alternative restorative materials may provide better marginal sealing. In contrast, a study by Mazumdar et al. found that conventional GIC exhibited the least microleakage among various restorative materials.37 Another study by Abraham et al. reported no significant difference between chitosan-modified and conventional GIC22; however, the present study findings indicate that the incorporation of nanoparticles into GIC formulations may enhance their sealing ability, potentially improving resistance to microleakage beyond that of conventional GIC.

The results from this study, combined with the previously reported benefits such as enhanced strength and fluoride release, suggest that Ch-Ti-Zr-HA nano-modified GIC has the potential to become a promising restorative material. Its ability to perform well in posterior occlusal restorations, as well as its application in minimally invasive techniques, positions it as a versatile material for general clinical use in restorative dentistry. However, it is important to note that this study was conducted in vitro, which means that the findings cannot be directly extrapolated to clinical situations. The clinical performance of any material must be evaluated through well-controlled clinical studies to draw definitive conclusions about its long-term effectiveness and its behavior in real oral conditions. Additionally, future research should involve simulating in vivo conditions more accurately, using thermal cyclic loading in conjunction with the effects of commonly consumed beverages and acidic liquids, as these factors play a significant role in the longevity of restorations.

Conclusion

The 5% and 10% nanoformulated GIC exhibited the least microleakage compared to conventional GIC without a significant difference. Thus, incorporating nanoformulated Ch-Ti-Zr-HA did not adversely affect microleakage, highlighting its potential as a promising restorative material.
