A hybrid LSTM-transformer model for short-term urban electricity load forecasting: a two-city case study
Authors: Hongli Liu, Yang Wei, Han Zhang, Yang Wang, Jie Zhang, Zhongqiang Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate short-term urban power load forecasting is crucial for power system dispatching, peak load management, and data-driven urban energy planning. To improve the forecasting performance of multivariate load time series under complex temporal dependencies and external influencing factors, this paper proposes a hybrid LSTM-Transformer model, combining the temporal representation capabilities of Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks with the global dependency modeling capabilities of Transformer encoders. Based on multi-source 15-minute time series data from two representative cities, the proposed model is evaluated on a short-term power load forecasting task and compared with baseline models including traditional LSTM and DE-LSTM. Experimental results show that, under a given evaluation protocol, the hybrid model achieves lower prediction errors and higher fitting accuracy. These results demonstrate that the proposed structure can effectively capture the local temporal dynamics and long-distance feature interactions in urban load sequences.

Introduction

Against the backdrop of increasingly severe global climate change, sustainable urban development faces growing pressure. The continuous rise in global average temperature and the frequent occurrence of extreme weather events have had a significant impact on socio-economic systems and natural ecosystems, making low-carbon development a recognized strategic response1. As the main carriers of economic and social activities, cities dominate global energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions2,3. With the acceleration of urbanization and industrialization, urban energy demand continues to grow rigidly, while excessive reliance on fossil fuels further exacerbates carbon emissions4–6. In this context, the low-carbon transformation of cities should not be viewed merely as an energy issue, but rather as a complex systemic process involving the coordinated evolution of energy, economy, and environment7. Existing research mainly focuses on coupled measurement, policy design, and coordinated development analysis8–12, but these studies also highlight the urgent need for more effective data-driven tools to provide potential support for urban energy management.

The rapid development of big data technology has created new opportunities to address this challenge. The massive amounts of multi-source data from the power grid, user-side electricity consumption, and environmental monitoring provide a richer empirical basis for analyzing the interactions between urban energy use, economic activities, environmental changes, and low-carbon goals13–17. Therefore, big data analytics and machine learning are increasingly being introduced into the energy field18. For example, the hybrid LSTM-Transformer framework has shown significant advantages in regional residential electricity forecasting19, providing potential support for urban energy management and serving as a reference for future.

Among related tasks, short-term power load forecasting remains a particularly important and active research topic because load dynamics are influenced by a variety of factors, including environmental conditions, social behavior, economic activities, and weather-sensitive demand, resulting in strong periodicity, randomness, and uncertainty. Existing research shows that traditional forecasting methods are often limited by insufficient data preprocessing, limited nonlinear representation capabilities, and dependence on a single model structure. Reference20 systematically reviews intelligent load forecasting methods and points out that ignoring data preprocessing is a common limitation of traditional models. To address this issue, Reference21 proposes a comprehensive forecasting framework that integrates data preprocessing, GAN-based forecasting, and intelligent weighting mechanisms to improve the performance of nonlinear modeling. Reference22 further enhances nonlinear feature extraction by improving the temporal convolutional structure in the IAVOA-EATCN model. For scenarios with high volatility and multi-factor coupling, Reference22 develops a machine learning-based port energy demand forecasting model. Reference23 provides a comparative review demonstrating that hybrid methods can generally provide more reliable and accurate industrial load forecasts. To better address weather-driven abrupt changes, Reference24 proposes a weather-aware multi-scale fusion bidirectional nonlinear spiking neural network model, while Reference25 introduces a deep learning method based on adaptive mode decomposition to achieve more refined short-term regional load forecasts.

To support reliable short-term load forecasting in urban power systems, it is necessary to focus on a more clearly defined technological research gap. Existing research shows that single recurrent neural network models can capture local time dependencies well, but they still have shortcomings in modeling long-range dependencies and complex cross-feature interactions in multivariate load sequences27. In recent years, hybrid deep learning models have improved prediction accuracy to some extent, but many studies either rely on load data from a single source or lack a systematic discussion on how “sequence feature extraction” and “attention-based dependency modeling” work synergistically for urban load forecasting. Against this backdrop, this paper develops and evaluates a hybrid forecasting architecture for short-term urban power load forecasting, oriented towards multi-source time series inputs. This framework is essentially an empirical model for forecasting tasks, providing a methodological foundation for future broader urban energy and low-carbon research.

Preliminaries

LSTM model

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) are a class of artificial neural networks whose core function is processing sequential data. The LSTM model is a significant variant of RNNs, with its most advantage being the ability to retain information over extended periods and effectively capture long-term dependencies within data. The fundamental building block of an LSTM is called a “cell” (as shown in Fig. 1). At each time step, this cell receives three types of inputs: the cell state from the previous time step (representing long-term memory), the hidden state from the previous time step (representing short-term memory), and the input data for the current time step. Ultimately, it outputs two results: the updated cell state (new long-term memory) and the updated hidden state (new short-term memory).

Fig. 1The basic unit of LSTM.

The basic unit of LSTM.

LSTM consists of three gates: the input gate, the forget gate, and the output gate. Its state propagates between units, each capable of performing information addition or subtraction operations. These operations enable information to selectively influence the state through the “gate” structure at each time step. The gate structure includes sigmoid layer operations and vector dot product calculations. Within the LSTM architecture, the first step involves determining which information within the cell state should be discarded—a function performed by the “forget gate.” The forget gate combines the current input with the previous time step’s output to decide which portions of the previous cell state’s information should be forgotten. After the LSTM “forgets” some of the previous state information, the current cell state must learn new information from the current input. Within the LSTM module architecture, multiple LSTM layers are incorporated, each composed of a series of LSTM units. These units are connected in a chain-like structure, where the output of one unit feeds into the next, as illustrated in Fig. 2. As data flows through these layers, each subsequent layer receives input from the preceding layer. It is important to note that the operations within each unit of a specific layer employ identical weights and biases. The output from the final LSTM layer is fed into a fully connected layer (FCL), where the output from each activation function in the preceding layer is input into all activation functions of the FCL. The FCL maps the LSTM output to the desired output dimension T, thereby generating the embedding vector Y. This design ensures consistent feature extraction for time series data, with the LSTM module’s final output serving as the input data’s vector representation.

Transformer model

The core of the Transformer model is an encoder-decoder architecture. Both components incorporate residual connections and “Add & Norm” layers, crucial for enhancing training stability. The encoder transforms the input sequence into a series of representations based on attention mechanisms within this structure. The decoder handles the generation task, utilizing the encoder’s output representations and self-attention mechanism to produce the target sequence. This mechanism enables the model to selectively focus on specific parts of the input sequence, which is essential for power load forecasting.

Specifically, the self-attention mechanism is implemented through the scaled dot-product attention defined in9, formally described as a function mapping queries and a set of key-value pairs to an output. Queries, keys, values, and the generated output are all represented as vectors. In the power load forecasting scenario, the encoder feeds the output of its LSTM component back as its own input. It is important to note that positional encoding was introduced initially because Transformers cannot inherently recognize the order or position of input elements. Positional encodings based on sine and cosine functions are added to the input embeddings to convey positional information to the model. In this research, we adopt the positional encoding implementation consistent with26. The dimension of these positional encodings matches that of the embeddings, uniformly denoted as T.

As shown in Fig. 3, the encoder architecture consists of multiple parallel self-attention layers that collectively form a complex multi-head attention mechanism. Following the multi-head attention layer, the encoder includes a feedforward fully-connected network with residual connections. The outputs from both components are jointly fed into a normalization layer (i.e., Add and Norm operations). Starting from the input sequence (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{Y}}$$\end{document}), the matrices (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{Q}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{K}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{V}}$$\end{document}) are generated and successively processed through both the Multi-Head Attention and the Feed-Forward Network. The final output represents the predicted electricity load for the next time instance. In classification tasks, such predictions typically require the Softmax activation function to map input data to discrete categories. However, in electricity load forecasting task—a regression problem—the linear layer’s output directly provides continuous value predictions without the use of Softmax.

When generating the output sequence, the decoder employs an element-wise generation approach, simultaneously considering both the encoder’s output and previously generated elements in the sequence. Each layer of the decoder comprises three fundamental building blocks: the first is a masked multi-head attention mechanism, which operates similarly to the encoder’s multi-head attention but incorporates an additional masking operation. This ensures each position can only focus on earlier positions within the output sequence. The second building block is the encoder-decoder attention, which consists of another multi-head attention mechanism enabling the decoder to focus on the encoder’s output. The final building block is a feedforward network, structurally similar to the encoder’s FFN, which processes each position individually.

Additionally, each sublayer of the decoder employs residual connections and layer normalization. Due to the more complex structure of Transformer models, training them typically requires more time than training LSTM models.

A hybrid LSTM-transformer-based prediction model

This section details the methodology underlying the hybrid LSTM-Transformer model for electricity load forecasting. The proposed hybrid model integrates the strengths of both LSTM and Transformer architectures. This hybrid framework efficiently captures and leverages temporal dependencies within multivariate time series data, thereby enhancing the performance of electricity load forecasting. At its core, the hybrid model employs a sequence structure capable of effectively processing input multivariate time series data. It primarily consists of two components, with its main structure illustrated in the Fig. 4.

The proposed model adopts a hybrid design that combines LSTM with an encoder-only Transformer. This design is intended for regression-based short-term load forecasting rather than sequence generation. The LSTM module is used to extract short-term sequential patterns from historical observations, while the encoder-only Transformer further captures longer-range dependencies and cross-feature interactions within the multivariate input sequence. Compared with a full encoder-decoder design, this implementation provides a lighter and more task-matched architecture for point forecasting.

Hybrid LSTM-Transformer Model.

In this architecture, the LSTM component occupies a central position and is responsible for processing complex temporal relationships within the input data. Unlike traditional standalone LSTM models typically employed for regression tasks, the LSTM module proposed in this section generates effective embedding vectors of dimension T representing the input data through a series of iterative operations. This approach captures key features and patterns within multivariate time series data.

The LSTM model is designed with K layers containing L LSTM cells. The outputs of these cells are fed into a fully connected layer to generate an embedding vector of dimension T. T, K, and L values were empirically optimized through a systematic trial-and-error process to achieve optimal performance in the load forecasting task.

The LSTM model demonstrates strong capabilities in understanding and learning complex temporal dynamics within sequential data. Incorporating LSTM as the initial component enables the model to effectively capture key sequential features before passing processed information to the Transformer component. This architecture reduces system complexity, shortens training time, and minimizes computational overhead when handling long sequences by eliminating the decoder.

The second component of this architecture is the Transformer encoder, which generates load consumption predictions based on the vector embeddings obtained from the LSTM.

The encoder is a core component of the original Transformer architecture, but does not include a decoder module. First, the encoder adds positional encoding to the embedding vectors to preserve the sequential order of the input data.

Subsequently, these augmented embedding vectors are fed into the main encoder body. The main encoder body consists of P identical stacked layers. Experimental results

Accurate short-term electricity load forecasting is essential for urban energy management and can provide data support for energy system planning and may be indirectly relevant to broader energy-environment research. As a key operating variable in urban energy systems, electricity load reflects the combined influence of socioeconomic activity, environmental conditions, and policy-related factors.

Accordingly, this study focuses on short-term urban electricity load forecasting using real multi-source time-series data from two representative urban areas (Area 1 and Area 2). The dataset spans from January 1, 2012 to January 10, 2015, with a sampling interval of 15 min, resulting in 105,984 samples for each area. It includes five input features: electricity load (MW), average temperature (temp_avg, °C), relative humidity (humidity, %), rainfall (rain, mm), and a quarter-hour code (0–95) to capture intraday periodicity. Missing values were handled by complete case deletion, and all features were standardized using Z-score normalization. To avoid time-series data leakage, the data were divided chronologically into a training set (70%, 74,188 samples, January 2012 to February 2014), a validation set (15%, 15,897 samples, February 2014 to July 2014), and a test set (15%, 15,899 samples, July 2014 to January 2015). A sliding window of 96 time steps (24 h) was used to construct historical input sequences for predicting the load value in the next 15-minute interval.

Based on this dataset, a hybrid deep forecasting model integrating LSTM and Transformer architectures was developed, and its performance was compared with that of a conventional LSTM model and a differential evolution-optimized DE-LSTM model.

Table 3 summarizes the overall prediction results of each model on the test set. From the overall metrics, the proposed LSTM-Transformer model achieved the best performance under the current experimental settings, demonstrating lower prediction errors in RMSE and MAE metrics, and a higher goodness of fit in R². Compared to the traditional LSTM model, the hybrid architecture further reduces the overall error level while maintaining high fitting ability; and compared to DE-LSTM, the proposed model shows more significant performance advantages across all metrics. These results indicate that, under current data conditions, combining the local time-series modeling capabilities of LSTM with the long-range dependency characterization capabilities of Transformer helps improve the prediction accuracy of multivariate urban load sequences.

To provide a more intuitive comparison of model error levels, a bar chart of MAPE for the three models is plotted, as shown in Fig. 5. This metric measures the relative error between the model’s predicted values and the actual values.

Figure 5 shows the comparison results of the three models on the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) metric. It can be seen that the proposed LSTM-Transformer model also maintains the best performance in terms of relative error, with its MAPE significantly lower than that of LSTM and DE-LSTM. This phenomenon is consistent with the conclusions in Table 1 based on absolute error and goodness of fit, indicating that the performance improvement of the proposed model is not limited to a single metric, but exhibits good stability across different evaluation dimensions.

Figure 6 shows a time-series comparison of the actual load and the predicted results of each model in City 1. Overall, the proposed LSTM-Transformer model more closely matches the actual load curve, demonstrating better trend-following ability in most time periods. Especially in areas with rapid load changes and near peak transitions, the proposed model responds more promptly to local fluctuations, and the consistency between the predicted trajectory and the actual curve is higher. In contrast, the traditional LSTM model exhibits a certain degree of smoothing effect in some sections and is insufficient in responding to rapidly changing load patterns; DE-LSTM shows relatively more significant deviations in some local periods. These phenomena indicate that, in the City 1 scenario, the proposed hybrid structure can better balance short-term fluctuation information with the overall time-series evolution trend, thereby improving the fitting ability for complex load dynamics.

Overall, the proposed model exhibits superior predictive capabilities in RMSE, MAE, R², and MAPE metrics. Conclusion

This study develops a hybrid LSTM-Transformer model for short-term urban electricity load forecasting and evaluates its performance using multi-source time-series data from two representative cities. The experimental results indicate that, under the adopted evaluation protocol, the proposed model outperforms the compared baseline models in terms of RMSE, MAE, and R². These findings suggest that combining LSTM-based sequential feature extraction with Transformer-based dependency modeling is effective for multivariate urban load forecasting.

Limitations and future work

This paper still has certain limitations. First, this study only evaluated short-term electricity load forecasting tasks and did not explicitly include carbon emission variables. Second, the dataset is limited to two representative cities. Third, this study focuses on point forecasts and does not provide uncertainty intervals. Finally, it used multi-source input data but did not analyze individual data. Future research will expand this framework by incorporating explicit carbon-related indicators, broader cross-city datasets, stronger forecasting benchmarks, and forecasting methods that consider uncertainty.
