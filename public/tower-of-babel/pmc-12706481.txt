Pial arteriovenous fistula fed by an angular branch of the left middle cerebral artery with associated aneurysm and subdural hematoma: illustrative case

Pial arteriovenous fistulas (pAVFs) are rare vascular anomalies, representing roughly 1.6% of all CNS vascular pathologies.1,2 These malformations are characterized by direct, high-flow shunts between pial arteries and cortical veins, bypassing the capillary network entirely. Unlike arteriovenous malformations (AVMs), pAVFs lack an intervening nidus and exhibit distinct anatomical and hemodynamic features that set them apart from other cerebrovascular malformations. Despite their rarity, pAVFs commonly present with a range of clinical manifestations, including mass effect, seizures, heart failure, or hemorrhage.3

This case report details an unusual presentation of a pAVF in a 68-year-old man, feeding from the angular branch (M4) of the left middle cerebral artery (MCA) and associated with a small feeding artery aneurysm. The patient presented with acute-onset progressive holocranial headache due to a left fronto-temporo-parietal subdural hematoma (SDH) and was subsequently found to have a left pAVF with an associated aneurysm. He underwent successful surgical intervention, including craniotomy, microsurgical ligation of the fistula, and surgical evacuation of the hematoma. This report highlights the importance of individualized care and the integration of advanced surgical techniques in achieving favorable outcomes for complex cerebrovascular anomalies. Through this discussion, we aim to contribute to the growing understanding of pAVFs and provide valuable insights into their presentation, diagnosis, and treatment in adult patients.

Illustrative Case

Case Description

A 68-year-old male presented to the emergency department with a 1-week history of severe left-sided frontal headaches, rated 10/10 in intensity and unrelieved by Tylenol or Motrin. He reported prior episodes of similar headaches that typically resolved with analgesics. His past medical history was notable for bladder pain syndrome and hypertension, controlled with medication. He denied recent trauma, missed doses of antihypertensives, intercurrent illness, neck pain, chest pain, shortness of breath, lightheadedness, visual changes, paresthesia, photophobia, or phonophobia. On examination, he was neurologically intact with no focal deficits.

Noncontrast CT imaging of the head demonstrated a large acute extra-axial hematoma (Fig. 1) located in the left fronto-parieto-temporal region with a thin SDH anteriorly measuring up to 5 mm in thickness. The posterior part of the hematoma was lenticular inshape, measuring approximately 3.7 × 1.6 × 2.8 cm, overlying the posterior temporal lobe with mass effect on the subjacent cerebral cortex and the left lateral ventricle with approximately 3 mm of midline shift toward the right. The patient was transferred to our center for further treatment. FIG. 1.Preoperative noncontrast CT scans of the head obtained at the outside hospital. Repeat CT imaging of the head at our center demonstrated no significant change in the left extra-axial hematoma, which was mainly in the subdural space, with no evidence of epidural component. CT angiography demonstrated a spot sign on the medial aspect of the focal lenticular component of the hematoma. However, no vascular nidus or aneurysm was identified.

The patient was initially observed in the neuro-stepdown unit for 24 hours to ensure neurological and imaging stability. Considering the lack of any significant predisposing factors and the spot sign on the focal lenticular component of the hematoma, a digital subtraction angiography (DSA) study was planned.

A transfemoral diagnostic cerebral angiogram was performed that demonstrated medial displacement of the left MCA cortical branches secondary to mass effect caused by the hematoma. A pAVF supplied by an arterial feeder originating from the angular branch of the inferior division of the left MCA, with an associated saccular aneurysm, and drainage into a pial vein were also noticed (Fig. 2A and B). The aneurysm measured approximately 2 mm in width and 3 mm in height, with evidence of contrast stasis that persisted up to the venous phase of the angiography examination (Fig. 2C). The feeding artery and draining vein measured about 0.5 mm in diameter, rendering the lesion unsuitable to endovascular embolization (Fig. 2D). Craniotomy and microsurgical ligation of the pAVF were planned, with evacuation of the SDH. Given the patient’s stable examination, he was taken to surgery the following day. Surgical Intervention

The patient was brought to the operating room and placed under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation. Stereotactic neuronavigation was utilized to localize the epicenter of the hematoma beneath the left parietal eminence. A straight skin incision centered over the hematoma approximately 2 cm posterior to the left ear was made using a no. 10 scalpel (Fig. 3A). The incision was carried down to the periosteum, which was then dissected using a subperiosteal dissector. A single parietal burr hole was made and a circular craniotomy (5 cm × 5 cm in size) was carried out. The dura mater was carefully separated, and the bone flap was removed. A stellate durotomy was performed using Metzenbaum scissors, and the dural edges were secured using Nurolon 4-0 sutures. An acute SDH compressing the cortical surface was identified, along with motor oil–colored fluid consistent with preoperative CT findings (Fig. 3B). The SDH was meticulously evacuated using suction and irrigation, which resulted in decompression of the pial aneurysm, resulting in rebleeding. Hemostasis was achieved with the application of Gelfoam and cottonoid, followed by thorough irrigation with normal saline. The frontal component of the SDH was subsequently evacuated with saline irrigation and suction. Minor bleeding from the subdural membranes was effectively controlled with bipolar electrocautery. Irrigation continued until the fluid ran clear. The cottonoid covering the ruptured pial aneurysm was then removed, exposing the feeder artery and draining vein at the shunt point (Fig. 3C). Both structures were carefully electrocoagulated to achieve complete hemostasis. Following evacuation of the hematoma, the brain surface appeared relaxed. The dural edges were approximated using Nurolon 4-0 sutures and an overlay dural graft was placed over the dura. The craniotomy flap was repositioned and secured with bone plates, a burr hole cover, and screws. The skin incision was closed in two layers, and a sterile head dressing was applied. The postoperative recovery was uneventful with no immediate complications. Postoperative CT imaging of the head demonstrated a complete evacuation of the SDH with significant improvement in mass effect (Fig. 4). On postoperative day 1, a repeat DSA was performed that demonstrated no evidence of arteriovenous shunting or residual aneurysm. The mass effect on the cortical MCA branches had also improved (Fig. 5). The patient was discharged home on day 3 after surgery. He was doing well with no active symptoms at 1 month of clinical follow-up. Complete resolution of the subdural hemorrhage was noted on follow-up noncontrast CT imaging of the head at 1 month. Discussion

Observations

This case highlights a rare presentation of a left parietal pAVF with associated saccular aneurysm and SDH. Pial AVFs are rare vascular anomalies, accounting for a small fraction of CNS vascular pathologies, and are considered distinct from AVMs due to their unique anatomical and hemodynamic characteristics.3–5 Specifically, pAVFs are characterized by direct high-flow shunts between pial arteries and cortical veins, bypassing the capillary network and lacking an intervening nidus, a hallmark feature of AVMs.2,5–7 The rarity of pAVFs, combined with the associated SDH in this case, underscores the complexity and diagnostic challenges of such cerebrovascular anomalies.

Historically considered a subtype of AVMs, pAVFs are now recognized as unique entities characterized by venous varices and high-flow arterial feeders.8–12 Clinical manifestations of pAVFs differ significantly between pediatric and adult populations. In children, pAVFs are typically congenital, presenting with symptoms of high-velocity blood flow including cardiac decompensation, bruits, and seizures.8–12 Conversely, adults predominantly present with hemorrhage, mass effect, or cerebral ischemia, reflecting vascular fragility and chronic hemodynamic stress.13,14

Most hemorrhagic presentations of pAVFs involve intracerebral hemorrhage, making acute SDHs exceedingly rare. To the best of our knowledge, only one other case of acute SDH associated with pAVF in a 41-year-old patient has been reported previously.5 Our patient presented with an acute SDH in the absence of common predisposing factors such as head trauma, hypertension, advanced age, anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication use, alcohol or substance abuse, or known bleeding disorders. This unusual clinical scenario heightened suspicion for an underlying vascular anomaly, emphasizing the importance of maintaining a high index of clinical suspicion and pursuing advanced diagnostic imaging.

The coexistence of a pAVF with an associated aneurysm, which has been reported in the literature, adds further complexity to this case.13 The formation of such aneurysms likely results from chronic hemodynamic stress, characterized by high-intensity blood flow within the arteriovenous shunt leading to progressive vascular wall dilation.2,15 In our patient, chronic stress and vascular remodeling at the fistula site presumably culminated in aneurysm rupture, causing the SDH. While the primary management focus was on evacuating the hematoma and addressing the aneurysm, the surgical strategy also had to consider the high-flow dynamics of the pAVF to prevent recurrence or further complications.

Optimal management of pAVFs necessitates a multidisciplinary approach involving detailed angiographic imaging and disconnection of the arteriovenous shunt through surgical or endovascular means. Endovascular embolization is a safe, effective treatment for pAVFs and is preferred over surgery due to its minimally invasive nature, particularly for unruptured lesions, shunts involving venous sinuses, and surgically challenging locations.14,16–19 However, factors such as multiple arterial feeders, small vessel size, distal shunt location, and connections to normal cortical veins make endovascular treatment challenging. These complexities increase the risk of recurrence from incomplete embolization and complications like hemorrhage or nontarget embolization. Matsuhashi et al. emphasized the importance of case selection based on these angiographic features, recommending surgery over endovascular treatment when necessary.20

Craniotomy provides a direct approach to the pAVF. Microsurgical disconnection of the arteriovenous shunt can be performed safely with the distinct advantage of hematoma evacuation with immediate relief of mass effect at the same time. When cortical veins are involved, the draining vein can be disconnected near the fistula to preserve normal parenchymal drainage. Venous varices, if present, typically shrink immediately after successful shunt disconnection. A feeder artery aneurysm, as in our case, can also be addressed using this approach. Goel et al. reported successful microsurgical resection of pAVFs in 14 patients, with no residual arteriovenous shunt or procedure-related neurological deficits over a mean follow-up of 32 months.21

In a review of 112 pAVF cases, Yang et al. reported successful treatment with complete obliteration in 90.2% of the patients.3 Treatment modalities included excision (10 patients), shunt disconnection via surgery (21 patients) or endovascular techniques (37 patients), and a hybrid approach (12 patients). The endovascular approach had a higher failure rate (13.5%) compared to surgery (3.2%). The presence of a venous varix was found to significantly influence clinical progression and treatment outcomes.3

The natural history of these lesions remains poorly understood due to their rarity. Spontaneous resolution has been reported, albeit infrequently, particularly following diagnostic cerebral angiography.22 Some authors have postulated that radiographic contrast agents may lead to altered vasomotor balance with predominant vasoconstrictive effect and induce endothelial toxicity with apoptosis, eventually leading to spontaneous fistula closure.23 In the present case, the lesion’s small arterial feeder (< 1 mm in diameter) and distal location made microcatheter navigation challenging. Additionally, the presence of SDH with mass effect and the lesion’s superficial location on the brain surface favored a surgical approach.

Postoperative DSA was critical in confirming complete pAVF resolution and ruling out residual vascular abnormalities. This step is particularly important, as patients undergoing resection remain at risk for complications such as procedure-related thrombosis or recurrence of the vascular anomaly.7,14,24

Lessons

This case underscores the importance of having a low threshold for advanced imaging in a patient with acute SDH without traditional predisposing risk factors such as advanced age, anticoagulation and antiplatelet history, or trauma.
