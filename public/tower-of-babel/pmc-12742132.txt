Essential changes in the Doctor of Pharmacy (Pharm. D) curriculum in Pakistan: an exploratory qualitative study
Authors: Naeem Mubarak, Fatima Rahman Rana, Sabba Kanwal, Muhammad Ahsan Waqar, Che Suraya Zin, Mohamed Hassan Elnaem, Saad S. Alqahtani, Muhammad Zahid Iqbal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe study aims to present recommendations for a revised Doctor of Pharmacy (Pharm. D) curriculum that aligns with regional needs and international standards of pharmacy education.DesignAn exploratory qualitative study involving individual semistructured interviews. Data were collected and reported in accordance with Consolidated Criteria for Reporting of Qualitative Studies.SettingFace-to-face interviews were conducted in the respective offices of the experts and online interviews were conducted on Zoom and Google Meet.ParticipantsPurposive and snowball sampling was used to recruit experts due to the eligibility criteria of including associate professors with a PhD, and snowball sampling facilitated the recruitment of experts from all provinces and internationally. Interviews were transcribed verbatim and data were analysed using an inductive thematic approach using NVivo V.15. All interviews were conducted in English.ResultsThe study engaged 49 experts from national and international settings with an age range of 25–60 years (median=43 years). The researchers came out with six themes and their subthemes from the data including: (a) understanding current Pharm. D curriculum in Pakistan, (b) inevitable changes required in the Pharm. D curriculum, (c) specific-subject based changes, (d) foundational steps to achieve the required changes, (e) barriers to the implementation of these changes and (f) impact of Pharm. D curriculum change.ConclusionThe findings highlighted a clear need to revise the curriculum by incorporating enhanced clinical pharmacy content, integrated learning approaches, elective courses, interprofessional education, mandatory hospital and clinical placements, experiential learning through simulation-based methods and research components through a collaborative approach from policy makers and academic stakeholders.

Background

Pharmacy schools worldwide adopt different approaches to pharmacy education based on their country’s healthcare system, market needs and institutional goals.1 Countries with strong pharmaceutical industries emphasise drug design and development in their curricula while those prioritising patient care focus more on clinical practice.1 These differences in pharmacy education across the world explain the global variation in training, professional roles of the pharmacists and patient-care outcomes.1 However, the global healthcare environment continues to evolve, driven by technological innovations, public health emergencies and integrated pharmaceutical care models.2 3 To meet these emerging demands, many countries have adopted the Doctor of Pharmacy (Pharm. D) reflecting a move towards clinically oriented training.4 5 Initiated by the USA, this transition has been embraced by nations like Saudi Arabia, South Korea, India and China, emphasising the need for Pakistan to align its pharmacy education with international standards.6 7

Pharm. D curriculum in Pakistan

Pharmacy education in Pakistan began at Punjab University in 1948. In 2004, the Higher Education Commission (HEC) and Pharmacy Council of Pakistan (PCP) unified to establish a standardised Pharm. D curriculum for all public and private institutions.8 9 The curriculum has five core disciplines such as basic medical sciences, pharmaceutical chemistry, pharmaceutics and pharmacognosy and pharmacy practice. 8 9However, in Pakistan, pharmacy education is still in its transitional form from an industrial to a clinical and patient-care focused programme.9 A forward-looking Pharm. D curriculum in Pakistan is crucial to developing a pharmacy workforce capable of delivering patient-focused pharmaceutical care.

Research gap

Several studies have reviewed current Pharm. D trends in Pakistan, comparing it with evolving global practices in clinical pharmacy education; however, they were either descriptive reviews, institutional reports or limited to student perspectives and specific areas such as clinical pharmacy education.812 A recent study provided a comparative analysis of Pharm. D curriculum in Pakistan with pharmacy curriculum in other countries which shows that in a standardised scoring system of 15 curriculum quality indicators, Pakistan scored around 5–6 points, whereas the USA and Australia scored 14–15 points, reflecting a significant competency and clinical orientation gap.10 While existing literature provides extensive evidence supporting these reforms, there remains a significant gap in contextual adaptation, implementation feasibility and outcome assessment in low- and middle-income settings such as Pakistan. Therefore, the aim of our study is not to further analyse but to engage national and international experts from all pharmacy disciplines to provide recommendations for the future Pharm. D curriculum which can be put forward to regulatory authorities.

Methodology

Study design

An exploratory qualitative study that involved individual semistructured interviews of national and international academic experts.

Selection and recruitment of experts

Purposive and snowball sampling was used to recruit experts due to the eligibility criteria of including associate professors with a PhD, and snowball sampling facilitated the recruitment of experts from all provinces and internationally. The study determined that the experts must fulfil the following criteria:

Be available to give consent for a minimum 1-hour interview (online/in person) during specified dates.

Be available to give consent for a minimum 1-hour interview (online/in person) during specified dates.

Holds a PhD degree with the designation of associate professor and a minimum of 10 years’ experience in academia at least.

Holds a PhD degree with the designation of associate professor and a minimum of 10 years’ experience in academia at least.

Semistructured interviews

The method for collecting data included semistructured inperson/online interviews. Open-ended interviews enabled experts to share indepth views, helping explore various aspects of required Pharm. D curriculum changes in Pakistan.

Study setting

Face-to-face interviews were conducted in the respective offices of the experts and online interviews were conducted on Zoom and Google Meet.

Interview guide

The interview guide was developed after an extensive literature review89 1317 and pilot-tested for any modifications or improvements with one associate professor (national), two research associates and two clinical pharmacists. Based on their feedback, a few open-ended questions were further added. Online supplemental appendix S1 contains the detailed interview guide.

Interview process

Before the interviews, experts were briefed via email, phone call and an online meeting on 8 November 2023 about the research objectives and plan. The project information sheet was sent to all experts before the scheduled date of the interviews. Verbal and written informed consent was received from the experts prior to both online and face-to-face interviews. FRR and SK conducted all interviews from 15 November to 10 February 2023 after being trained in interview conduct and understanding multiple English languages, especially for Malaysian experts. Interviews were 1 hour in duration and undertaken on a 1:1 basis. A digital interview recorder was used to record the interviews. At the end of an interview, audio files were transferred to a secure, password-protected computer in possession of NM.

Transcription, coding and theme generation

Audio files of interviews were transcribed verbatim (in Microsoft Word) by FRR and SK and counter-checked by NM for any errors in transcription. Transcribed Microsoft Word documents were imported into NVivo V.15 for semantic and interpretivist analysis.

Transcripts were coded by FRR and SK authors independently based on the principles of thematic analysis following the six steps from familiarisation of the text, initial coding, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes and producing the report.18 Coding of the transcripts involved carefully reading and re-reading of all the transcript (data) word by word, linking and connecting texts back to our research questions. The initial coding and generation of themes were conducted by FRR and SK, and any conflict in coding was resolved by NM, the principal investigator,a PhD.

Theoretical saturation

Data saturation was achieved when no new themes were evident after 48 interviews. Interviews followed the plus-one principle, where the process continues until one additional interview is conducted after no new themes emerge.19

Trustworthiness, credibility and respondent validity

Trustworthiness or rigour in qualitative research involves credibility, transferability, conformability and dependability.20 To enhance credibility, data were collected and reported as per COnsolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines given in online supplemental appendix S2.21 The researchers acknowledged their positionality and employed bracketing (reflective technique used in qualitative research to set aside personal biases, assumptions and preconceived notions to minimise personal biases).22 Additionally, negative case analysis was employed to strengthen the credibility of the identified themes. To enhance the transferability, the researchers offered rich, detailed descriptions of the background information on stakeholders such as age, gender, educational qualifications and institutional affiliations as given in table 1. ‘Respondent’s validity’ was used as a technique to ensure conformability, that is, all transcribed files were sent via email to the interviewee to correct and send back if anything was not recorded or transcribed accurately. However, none reported any significant error in transcriptions. Finally, dependability was established through a well-maintained audit trail of all process logs.

Demographics of the experts

The study engaged 30 national and 19 international experts predominantly males, with the majority holding over a decade of academic experience. The majority are affiliated with public institutions (69.4%) and specialise mainly in pharmaceutics (38.8%), clinical pharmacy (28.6%) and pharmacy practice (16.3%). Their professional ranks range from pro vice-chancellors and deans to professors and associate professors, reflecting a highly experienced and academically mature respondent group. Thematic analysis of the interview process

The researchers followed inductive thematic analysis with open coding and emerged with six themes and their corresponding subthemes from the data including: (a) understanding current Pharm. D curriculum in Pakistan, (b) inevitable changes required in the Pharm. D curriculum, (c) specific-subject based changes, (d) foundational steps to achieve the required changes, (e) barriers to the implementation of these changes and (f) impact of Pharm. D curriculum change. The hierarchical framework of themes with their subthemes presented as a coding tree is given in figure 1. The themes and subthemes with participant quotes are detailed in online supplemental table S1.

Theme 1: understanding current Pharm. D curriculum in Pakistan

Experts after reviewing the current Pharm. D curriculum of Pakistan indicated the need for critical revisions. Many called it theoretical, basic and outdated. The majority called it a ‘pharmaceutics based curriculum’ based on the needs of the local pharmaceutical industry and scope of practice in Pakistan. Comparison was made with various other Pharm. D curriculums across the globe such as the USA, UK and Malaysia. The major differences were the presence of a clinical-based curriculum, a decentralised curriculum, experiential training, electives and foundational courses such as in Malaysian curriculum, research, training and apprenticeships. Experts considered the current curriculum lacking far behind the global standards in terms of problem-based learning, knowledge utilisation and patient-oriented approach. However, some regarded it as sufficient to meet global standards as well.

Theme 2: inevitable changes in the Pharm. D curriculum

Curriculum structure, integration and subject positioning

The most common curriculum change suggested was a shift toward a clinical-oriented structure, emphasising evidence-based and problem-based learning. Pharm. D is a clinical degree and hence a suggestion was given to make it patient-oriented and more inclined towards pharmacy practice and clinical pharmacy. The sequencing of courses should be revised to include clinical pharmacy earlier in the curriculum, rather than limiting it to the 9th and 10th semesters. Additionally, basic medical sciences integrated with pharmacy practice to bridge the gap between teaching pharmacology and clinical pharmacy. The need for integrating organic chemistry and medicinal chemistry was also highlighted. A suggestion was also made on the redistribution of practicals to include more space for placements and research in the final year. However, few experts suggested that the placement is in the appropriate order and teaching methodologies should be revised instead.

Inclusion of electives

The majority suggested including electives based solely on students’ choice. Specialised courses currently present in international curriculum such as oncology, infectious diseases management, health promotion, smoking cessation, mental health and digital health were highlighted for inclusion. Some experts also recommended the shifting of some core courses in the Pharm. D curriculum to electives such as mathematics, Islamiyat and Pakistan studies, pharmaceutical management and marketing. However, according to some, electives are not needed.

Experiential education and practical training

The experiential education and practical training was also suggested focusing on three key dimensions such as bridging theory and practice, increasing hospital and industrial collaborations and shifting towards simulation technology for patients and animals in labs. Experts took a balanced stance on shifting to simulation, emphasising the continued importance of animal handling training and recommending a combined approach for student instruction. The barriers to simulation with respect to cost and lack of infrastructure were highlighted and did not suggest prioritising it at the expense of other required changes.

House job/apprenticeships and clinical attachments

A 1-year mandatory house job with rotations in various specialties like internal medicine was recommended, similar to Malaysia’s model where residency leads to pharmacist registration. Some propose internships starting from the 3rd or 5th semester, while others emphasise final-year placements. Training should be flexible, allowing students to choose between clinical and industrial tracks. Paid, structured and specialised house job training should be made a compulsory part of the curriculum. Several barriers for house jobs were also highlighted, such as lack of funds for government and institutions to offer paid positions, lack of hospital affiliations and hospital management issues.

Interprofessional collaboration

Experts highlighted the importance of interprofessional collaboration (IPC) in pharmacy education for fostering teamwork and mutual respect among healthcare professionals. They recommended formally incorporating IPC into the curriculum through seminars, integrated courses and joint training with students from various disciplines, including medicine and economics. Collaboration between academic institutions and hospitals was also suggested to support implementation. Global models from the USA and Malaysia were cited as successful examples to emulate in Pakistan. However, barriers such as limited clinical settings, systemic constraints and lack of respect for pharmacy students were noted. While some opposed IPC at the undergraduate level, others supported flexible approaches like virtual learning and research-based activities to promote IPC.

Professional skills

Experts highlighted a wide range of professional skills that pharmacists must develop to meet evolving healthcare demands. Key skills included clinical reasoning, decision-making and patient-oriented competencies such as counselling and care planning, as practised in systems like the USA. Communication, interpersonal and behaviour development skills were frequently emphasised. Experts also pointed out the importance of managerial and soft skills, including leadership, problem-solving, critical thinking and lifelong learning. Many recommended ethics-based teaching, artificial intelligence and biomedical analytical techniques. The need for technology-related skills including pharmacoinformatics, pharmacogenomics and software proficiency for community pharmacy.

Research in Pharm. D curriculum

Experts shared that while Pharm. D is primarily a patient-care degree, research is still important for skill development. Many suggested including research as a separate credit course, ranging from two to six credit hours, with some preferring it as elective and others recommending it be mandatory. Models from countries like Malaysia and the USA were referenced, and some proposed starting research projects as early as the 3rd or 5th semester. Key research topics such as research methodology and scientific writing were highlighted for inclusion. While offering projects across all five disciplines, concerns were raised about feasibility due to limited supervisors and resources. Some preferred clinical clerkships over research projects at the undergraduate level. Overall, experts emphasised that research should be flexible based on student interest and standardised by the PCP.

Theme 3: subject-based changes for Pharm. D curriculum

Comprehensive changes across multiple subjects in the Pharm. D were suggested. For clinical pharmacy, there was a strong push for experiential and practical training through ward rounds, objective structured clinical examinations, simulation-based learning and integration of pharmaceutical care with clinical cases. Topics like pharmacogenomics, evidence-based medicine and medication review were recommended for inclusion. Experts also highlighted the need to increase the portion of hospital pharmacy, community pharmacy and forensic pharmacy in pharmacy practice. Forensic pharmacy content should be updated with modern techniques such as DNA fingerprinting and nomenclature and jargons used in forensic departments. Experts presented mixed opinions on public health’s role in disease prevention, immunisation and healthcare access, with some seeing it as essential, while others felt it could burden the curriculum or fall outside pharmacy’s domain.

Regarding pharmacognosy, suggestions included making it more value-added through the inclusion of clinical pharmacognosy and hands-on training using tools like microtomes and Ultraviolet lamps. For pharmaceutics, experts emphasised adding topics like novel drug delivery systems, nanotechnology, vaccine development, pharmaceutical calculations and skill development courses involving modern equipment and formulation techniques.

In pharmaceutical chemistry, inclusion of modern drug-design techniques, docking, biotechnology and assessment models for biological potential of drugs was proposed. For basic medical sciences, experts suggested extending physiology to 2 years and incorporating applied therapeutics and clinical pharmacology.

Additionally, there were calls for removing non-pharmacy subjects like Islamiyat and simplifying the curriculum without compromising quality. Overall, experts advocated for a clinically focused, skill-based and globally relevant curriculum that supports critical thinking, IPC and readiness for modern pharmacy practice.

Theme 4: foundational steps to achieve changes in Pharm. D curriculum

Experts emphasised that foundational changes in the Pharm. D curriculum require a coordinated and structured approach. They highlighted the need for collaboration between academia, pharmaceutical industries and non-academic stakeholders, including public and private sectors, regulatory bodies like HEC and PCP and even international advisors. Suggestions included establishing evaluation committees in each department, involving external advisors and building trust with faculty. Faculty development, role modelling and training were identified as critical, along with giving young professionals a voice in decision-making.

Theme 5: barriers in the implementation of changes

Experts identified several barriers to implementing changes in the Pharm. D curriculum. One key challenge is the lack of agreement between stakeholders, particularly due to a centralised curriculum and differing priorities. Bureaucratic hurdles, such as slow decision-making and resistance from institutions like HEC and PCP, were also highlighted. Additionally, there is a lack of communication, coordination and interest in revising the curriculum, with many stakeholders unwilling to move beyond their comfort zones. Resource constraints, especially in the public sector, were also noted as significant barriers. Lastly, a subject-based focus, rather than an interdisciplinary approach, hampers meaningful progress.

Theme 6: impact of changes in Pharm. D curriculum

Experts emphasised that changes in the Pharm. D curriculum could have significant positive impacts. Aligning with global standards would enhance job opportunities and allow pharmacists to compete internationally. This would lead to the development of skilful graduates capable of improving patient outcomes and reducing treatment costs, ultimately enhancing the quality of life for patients. Furthermore, such changes could influence legislation and policy, granting pharmacists greater legal authority and improving the overall perception of the profession. The resulting shift in the pharmacist’s role would reduce the burden on physicians and contribute to economic growth, fostering the progressive development of both students and the pharmacy workforce.

Discussion

The study was designed to systematically explore and report the existing gaps in Pakistan’s decade-old pharmacy curriculum, which remains largely unchanged despite significant global advancements in pharmacy education. Our findings extend beyond Pakistan’s context, offering relevant insights for other neighbouring countries that have transitioned to the Pharm. D structure yet continue to face challenges in aligning curricula with contemporary international standards. Furthermore, the study contributes to the discourse by identifying practical challenges, stakeholder perceptions and policy-level barriers that hinder or facilitate the adoption of these standards. Hence, while the thematic foundation aligns with global accreditation principles, the novelty of this study lies in its localised analytical lens, comparative perspective and implications for policy and quality assurance reform in pharmacy education within emerging academic systems.

The introduction of the Pharm. D programme was intended to enhance clinical components within the curriculum. However, pharmacy practice education remains predominantly theoretical and pharmaceutics based, accounting for approximately 22.07%, with experiential learning comprising a mere 3.03%.17 This gap raises critical concerns about the readiness of Pharm. D graduates for real-world healthcare settings and overall credibility of the Pharm. D qualification in Pakistan.17 One contributing factor to the lack of emphasis on clinical pharmacy education within the curriculum may be the increasing demands of the pharmaceutical industry which ranks as the fourth largest sector within the country’s large-scale manufacturing industry.23 24 However, the magnitude of this gap between the real world clinical pharmacy application and academic preparation appears more pronounced in Pakistan due to bureaucratic hurdles and discipline-based rivalries between subject-specific experts to defend individual subject domains rather than working collaboratively for the transition of entire Pharm. D curriculum to global standards.17

However, many argued that expanding clinical pharmacy content alone is insufficient without systemic demand for clinical pharmacists and available hospital roles.11 16 The increase in curriculum content should also be backed by an increase in clinical pharmacy services in hospitals, community pharmacies and outpatient care facilities.11 16 However, in Pakistan, this is more challenging due to lack of funds, affiliated hospitals, academia-regulatory collaborations and training among undergraduates.11 16 Therefore, periodic meetings through stronger quality assurance checks and mandatory curriculum revision committees are necessary to devise a sustainable curriculum change conforming to local and global contexts.

The findings highlight clear discrepancies between Pakistan’s Pharm. D curriculum and international standards as the development of clinical pharmacy from basement to bedside is significantly slower than in developed countries.11 13 25 26 As a result, there is a need to strengthen competency-based education in order to elevate both the quality and global standing of pharmacy education in the country.10 This can be best achieved through bringing in experts and mentors from high resource settings who have excelled in the implementation of clinical pharmacy in their curriculum and system to teach undergraduates in Pakistan. The need for applying theoretical knowledge through structured internships and training programmes is also essential for improving clinical readiness and developing teaching skills for academic and professional roles.7 26 27 This conforms to a study conducted in the USA, where a 4-year structured pharmacy internship programme led to better training outcomes for interns as full-time pharmacists.28

Training in scientific research is also deemed essential, as it fosters critical thinking, analytical skills and a mindset for lifelong learning qualities that are invaluable in the ever-evolving field of pharmacy.3 27 With the rapid advancement of technology and shifting demands within the profession, future pharmacists must be equipped with a robust foundation in research and evidence-based practice. However, Pharm. D is not recognised as a research-based degree due to the existing curriculum which is a major barrier, and hence, there is a crucial need to offer research projects for every discipline and allow students to choose the projects that best align with their interests.

Integration was widely accepted as an effective approach in pharmacy education, promoting holistic understanding of concepts rather than fragmented knowledge.14 29 When related courses are integrated, students develop creativity and fresh perspectives by exploring a subject from multiple angles within a short timeframe. This approach has been successfully adopted in several pharmacy schools where medicinal chemistry, pharmacology and therapeutics were integrated. A web-based survey of the students indicated that this model significantly enhanced learning outcomes.14 29 However, integration should not only be limited between subjects and extend to IPC among diverse professionals supported through interprofessional education within the Pharm. D curriculum.

The most critical step toward advancing the pharmacy profession requires joint efforts of pharmacists across all sectors and stakeholders from both academic and non-academic backgrounds to assess the current need of Pharm. D in Pakistan.

Comparison with existing literature

Prior research has highlighted the curriculum’s theoretical nature and insufficient clinical focus.8 16 Two notable studies conducted in Pakistan recently evaluated PharmD students’ competencies in antimicrobial resistance and opioid dispensing. Despite differences in focus, both studies converged on the need for curriculum enhancement through innovative pedagogy and stronger integration of experiential learning. These findings underscore the importance of practice-oriented training to better prepare future pharmacists for complex clinical and public health challenges.Our findings support these concerns with experts strongly advocating for experiential learning, early clinical exposure and patient-centred training. 30 31

Internationally, countries like the USA and Malaysia have emphasised integrated learning, simulation, interprofessional education and electives.6 Our study supports adopting similar practices, aligning with International Pharmaceutical Federation (FIP) recommendations for global pharmacy education standards.13

Strengths and limitations

The study involves recruitment of national experts in all disciplines of Pharm. D from four provinces of Pakistan (Punjab, Sindh, KPK and Balochistan) and diverse educational environments to reduce biases based on their positionality in this research. However, the study mainly relied on the suggestions from experts without a consensus-based approach to develop a framework or model for a revised Pharm. D curriculum which can be an important area of research for the future. The focus on associate professors with a PhD degree excluded early-career academics or clinical educators who are actively involved in day-to-day teaching and could be an important part of our stakeholders’ group.

Recommendations

A comprehensive revision of the Pharm. D curriculum in Pakistan is essential to align pharmacy education with contemporary clinical and healthcare needs. The policy brief recommends introducing clinical pharmacy early into the degree programme, inclusion of contemporary elective courses focusing on areas such as mental health, digital health, interprofessional education and professional skill development, mandating hospital clerkships and clinical attachments by affiliation with recognised teaching hospitals for all accredited pharmacy institutes. Curricular integration of pharmacology with pharmacy practice should be included in the revised curriculum, strengthening collaboration between academic institutions, pharmaceutical industries and regulatory bodies such as curriculum revision committees to ensure periodic revision of Pharm. D curriculum in Pakistan, elevating research within the Pharm. D curriculum by training faculty through clinical exchange programmes. Future research should employ consensus-driven methodologies, such as the Delphi technique, to systematically develop recommendations for curriculum reform in Pharm. D curriculum.

Conclusion

This study adds value by empirically mapping the extent of alignment and misalignment between current curricular practices and internationally recognised standards, thus providing evidence-based direction for policy adaptation rather than mere adoption. Continued stakeholder engagement and policy support are essential for implementing these curriculum changes and enhancing the role of pharmacists in the healthcare system.
