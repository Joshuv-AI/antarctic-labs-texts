Exosomes target liver TREM-1 to break the systemic inflammatory cascade and improve spinal cord injury outcomes
Authors: Chengfang Luo, Han Zheng, Xuexiang Wang, Wenxiang Li, Yiming Gao, Zhongze Yuan, Yongfu Lou, Xiaohong Kong, Caorui Lin, Fanshuo Zeng, Ning Ran, Shiqing Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpinal cord injury (SCI) is a catastrophic neurological disorder characterized by irreversible functional deficits and the absence of effective clinical therapies. Increasing evidence suggests liver inflammation has emerged as a critical contributor to the exacerbation of secondary injury by amplifying systemic inflammatory responses and hindering neurological recovery following SCI, suggesting that mitigating hepatic inflammation may alleviate disease progression. Recently, exosomes have gained attention as promising therapeutic agents due to their ability to mediate intercellular communication and deliver bioactive molecules. Whether exosome-based interventions can modulate liver inflammation and improve SCI outcomes remains to be elucidated.MethodsFunctional recovery was assessed using BMS, gait analysis and inclined plane test. Histological and molecular analyses were performed to evaluate spinal cord pathology. Downstream inflammatory signaling pathways were further analyzed through immunohistology staining, flow cytometry and q-RT-PCR to elucidate the cascade involved in liver-mediated secondary injury. Exosomal cargo was characterized by proteomics and western blotting with a focus on heat shock protein 70 (HSP70). Competitive inhibition experiment was used to determine the interaction between exosome-delivered HSP70 and Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid cells-1 (TREM-1) on hepatic macrophages in vitro.ResultsExosomes from different sources significantly improve functional recovery following SCI, as evidenced by enhanced locomotor performance and reduced tissue damage. Importantly, exosome treatment markedly attenuated liver inflammation, characterized by reduced macrophage activation and decreased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Mechanistically, we found that exosomal HSP70 interacts with TREM-1 on hepatic macrophages. This interaction competitively inhibits the binding of HMGB1, a key damage-associated molecular pattern that is persistently elevated after SCI. By blocking the HMGB1-TREM-1 axis, exosomal HSP70 effectively suppresses downstream inflammatory signaling and mitigates the amplification of systemic inflammation originating from the liver.ConclusionThis study uncovers a previously unrecognized systemic mechanism by which exosomes promote recovery after SCI through the modulation of liver inflammation. By targeting the HMGB1-TREM-1 signaling pathway in hepatic macrophages via exosomal HSP70 delivery, exosomes effectively suppress the inflammatory cascade that contributes to secondary injury.The translational potential of this articleTargeting systemic inflammation, particularly liver-mediated immune responses, may complement existing neuroprotective strategies and improve therapeutic outcomes. These findings pave the way for future preclinical and clinical studies aimed at developing exosome-based interventions for SCI and other neuroinflammatory disorders.

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a life-threatening neurological disorder that shows progressive damage at the cord lesion site, triggered by the acute, local inflammation, followed by the systemic inflammation which leads to secondary damage at the injured site. Although a number of different therapeutic approaches are under intensive investigation, including strategies to attenuate the whole-body inflammation such as hormonal therapy [1], to inhibit the neuronal death immediately after SCI [2,3], or to promote axonal regeneration such as removing glial scar by chondroitinase ABC (ChABC) enzyme or cell transplantation [4], there is no one-size-fits-all treatment available in the clinic.

In addition to complications caused by impaired neurological function, SCI can also disrupt the innervation of the entire body. It is not surprising that systemic effects of SCI have received more and more attention. SCI initiates a systemic inflammatory response defined by rapid elevation of chemokines and leukocytes in the circulation, lungs, kidneys, and liver, which evokes local tissue pathology [[5], [6], [7]]. This systemic response in turn contributes to acute and possibly chronic dysfunction in patients with SCI. Studies have shown that SCI causes an influx of leukocytes into the liver as early as 2 h post injury, which can increase the production and release of acute-phase proteins (APRs) and pro-inflammatory cytokines into the circulation [8]. In addition, emerging evidence demonstrates that the injured liver is not merely a passive responder, but also an active regulator of SCI progression through EV-mediated inter-organ communication. In particular, SCI promotes the generation of ribosomal protein S3 (RPS3)-enriched liver-derived EVs, predominantly released from activated Kupffer cells, which can migrate to the injured spinal cord and be internalized by neural stem cells and astrocytes [9]. Depletion of liver Kupffer cell or inhibition of hepatic nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) regulates mobilization of circulating leukocytes to the CNS parenchyma and prevent secondary damage after CNS injury [6,10]. These findings suggested that anti-inflammatory therapy used to treat spinal cord injury need not necessarily cross the blood-brain/spinal cord barrier, and ameliorating hepatic inflammation represents a promising and increasingly validated strategy for SCI treatment [11]. Recently, a groundbreaking study by Friedrich et al. showed that the liver can be repurposed as a “temporary protein factory” to counteract age-related immune decline [12]. These findings enlightened us that controlling the hepatic inflammatory response and modulating peripheral inflammation appear to be efficacious and represent a promising, widely applicable therapeutic approach. However, new strategies that can improve the liver function and ameliorate the systemic inflammation still need to be further investigated.

Exosomes, particularly those derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), have emerged as a highly promising therapeutic strategy for targeting liver inflammation due to their intrinsic hepatic tropism, low immunogenicity, and capacity to deliver anti-inflammatory cargoes directly to liver-resident cells [13]. Following systemic administration, exosomes naturally accumulate in the liver, where they can modulate key inflammatory pathways by transferring bioactive molecules such as miRNAs, proteins, and lipids to hepatocytes, Kupffer cells, and hepatic stellate cells [14]. In models of traumatic injury, including SCI, exosomes have demonstrated therapeutic potential, for instance, by attenuating inflammation, preserving blood-spinal cord barrier integrity, and promoting functional recovery [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. Mesenchymal stromal cell-derived exosomes, in particular, have shown promise in modulating local inflammatory responses and supporting tissue repair in murine SCI models [19,20]. However, the systemic effects of exosomes, their influence on peripheral inflammation, and the underlying mechanisms by which they exert these benefits are not fully understood.

Here, we demonstrate that SCI elicits persistent liver inflammation and repeated administration of exosomes from different sources, especially from human umbilical mesenchymal stromal cells (hUCMSCs) elicits functional recovery and mitigated liver inflammation in SCI mice without detectable toxicity. Mechanistically, exosomes appear to inhibit the TREM1-DAP12 inflammatory amplification pathway, leading to reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine production and diminished leukocyte infiltration into the spinal cord, thus preventing the secondary damage after SCI. Overall, our findings reveal for the first time a novel link among exosome therapy, liver inflammation, and SCI. Notably, the TREM1-DAP12 pathway, never before reported in SCI pathogenesis, plays a critical role in mediating hepatic inflammation that secondarily exacerbates spinal cord damage. To target this pathway, we utilized exosomes derived from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUCMSCs). Exosomes from hUCMSCs offer key translational advantages: non-invasive harvest, high scalability, low immunogenicity, natural hepatic tropism, and off-the-shelf availability. Thus, targeting hepatic inflammation via hUCMSC exosomes through the previously unreported TREM1-DAP12 axis represents a promising, broadly applicable, and translationally attractive strategy for SCI treatment without crossing the blood-spinal cord barrier.

Materials and methods

Exosome extraction and identification

(1)Exosome isolation

(1)Exosome isolation

Exosome isolation

Exosomes were isolated from autologous plasma, neural stem cell-derived conditioned medium, and human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived conditioned medium using sequential centrifugation. For plasma-derived exosomes, autologous peripheral blood was collected and centrifuged at 3000 × g, 6000 × g and 10,000 × g twice at 4°C for 30 min each time to remove the cell debris. The supernatant was diluted 10-fold with PBS, filtered through a 0.22-μm filter, and ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 70 min (Beckman Optima L-100 XP, Beckman Coulter). The exosome pellet was washed with PBS and collected by a second ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 70 min. For neural stem cell-derived exosomes, neural stem cells were cultured in serum-free neural stem cell medium supplemented with B27, EGF, bFGF, L-glutamine, and antibiotics. No fetal bovine serum was used. After reaching the appropriate density, the medium was replaced with fresh serum-free medium, and approximately 100 mL of conditioned medium was collected after 48 h. The medium was sequentially centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min, 2000 × g for 15 min, and 10,000 × g for 30 min, filtered through a 0.22-μm filter, and ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 70 min. The pellet was washed with PBS and ultracentrifuged again under the same conditions. For hUCMSC-derived exosomes, human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells were cultured in low-glucose DMEM containing 10% FBS (Ozfan, China) and antibiotics at 37°C with 5% CO2. Before exosome collection, cells were washed with PBS and cultured for 48 h in serum-free medium. Approximately 100 mL of conditioned medium was collected and processed using the same sequential centrifugation, filtration, washing, and ultracentrifugation procedures described above. The final exosome pellets were resuspended in PBS, aliquoted and stored at −80°C till use [21,22].(2)Morphological characterization of exosomes

(2)Morphological characterization of exosomes

Morphological characterization of exosomes

The morphological features of exosomes were characterized by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). For NTA, the exosomes were diluted in PBS to an appropriate concentration before being loaded into an NS300 NanoSight (ATA Scientific, Australia) equipped with a 488 nm laser. The detection threshold was set to 8, and the camera level was set to 11. Each exosome preparation was measured in triplicate, and the data were analyzed using NTA software 3.0. For TEM, 5 μL of exosome solution was suspended and loaded onto carbon-coated copper grids, air-dried at room temperature for 5 min. The grids were then stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid for 5 min, and washed twice with a drop of ddH2O. Excess solution was removed with filter paper. After air-drying, images were captured using JEOL 1010 plus TEM (JEOL, Japan).(3)Molecular characterization of exosomes

(3)Molecular characterization of exosomes

Molecular characterization of exosomes

The molecular markers of exosomes were detected by Western blotting. In brief, exosomes were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, China). The concentration of exosomal proteins was determined using Bicinchoninic Acid Assay (BCA) Kit (Beyotime, China). Amounts of 20 μg total protein were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred onto 0.2 μm PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA) and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. The membrane was then probed with primary antibodies, anti-CD63 (Proteintech, 25682-1-AP), anti-CD81 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-166029), anti-GM130 (Proteintech, 66662-1-Ig), anti-CD9 (Proteintech, 60232-1-Ig), anti-TSG101 (Proteintech, 67381-1-Ig), anti-Alix (Proteintech, 12422-1-AP) overnight at 4°C and incubated with compatible HRP-labelled secondary antibodies (Thermo, USA) at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Millipore, USA).

Human participants and ethics statement

Patient studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A total of 40 participants were enrolled from a single medical center, including 21 healthy controls and 19 patients with spinal cord injury. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The inclusion of human subjects and supporting documentation was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee ([blinded]). Informed consent was obtained from all participants upon enrollment.

Inclusion criteria for healthy controls and patients with SCI: Participants were generally healthy, with a body mass index between 18.5 and 24 kg/m². No other medications were taken by participants within the past month.

Exclusion criteria for healthy controls and patients with SCI were as follows: 1) exclusion of patients with preexisting neuromuscular disorders; 2) exclusion of spinal cord injuries caused by tumors or other factors; 3) exclusion of patients with mental illnesses; 4) exclusion of patients with autoimmune diseases; 5) exclusion of patients with chronic diseases; 6) exclusion of patients with abnormal white blood cell counts; and 7) exclusion of patients with fever, jaundice, skin or mucosal bleeding, superficial lymphadenopathy, or other symptoms affecting blood counts within the past two weeks.

Mice spinal cord injury model

Female C57BL/6J mice aged 6-8 weeks and weighing 18-25 g used in this study were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd (Jinan, China). All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shandong University (approval no.SDU-IACUC-25022) and were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines. Mice were randomly assigned to three groups: Sham, SCI and EXO treatment groups (n = 8 per group). Randomization was performed using a random number table method. Behavioral assessments and data analyses were conducted by investigators blinded to group allocation. After being anesthetized with isoflurane, mice in the SCI and EXO treatment groups underwent laminectomy at the T8 vertebral level. Spinous process fixators were used to fix the T7 and T9 spinous processes of mice. The NYU Impactor-III (WM Keck, USA) was then used to establish a moderate spinal cord contusion model (5 g × 12.5 mm). After confirming that the contusion was successful, the muscles, fascia, and skin were sutured. Mice in the Sham group underwent laminectomy without spinal cord injury. After surgery, the mice were administered 1 mg cefuroxime sodium intraperitoneally for 5 days to prevent urinary tract infections. Bladders were manually expressed twice daily until spontaneous urination was restored.

Experimental design and administration

Exosomes were administered to mice in the EXO treatment group via tail vein injection at a dose of 1 mg/kg/week, based on total exosomal protein, in a final volume of 200 μL PBS. Exosome administration was initiated on day 1 after contusive SCI and was performed once weekly for 3 or 7 consecutive weeks, depending on the experimental design. Mice in the SCI group received an equal volume of PBS following the same injection schedule. Tissue samples were collected on day 28 post-injury for subsequent histological and molecular analyses.

Behavioral analysis

(1)Basso Mouse Scale (BMS)

(1)Basso Mouse Scale (BMS)

Basso Mouse Scale (BMS)

The BMS was used to evaluate the recovery of hindlimb motor function after SCI. BMS scores were assessed before surgery and once weekly after SCI for 4 or 8 consecutive weeks, depending on the experimental design. The mice were placed individually in an unstimulated open-field arena and allowed to move freely for 5 min. The movement of the hindlimbs was observed and documented. Two trained researchers who were blinded to the group allocation conducted the evaluations. All animals were scored from 0 to 9, where 0 indicated complete paralysis and 9 represented normal function.(2)Catwalk gait analysis

(2)Catwalk gait analysis

Catwalk gait analysis

The CatWalk XT system (Noldus, Wageningen, Netherlands) was used to evaluate gait performance and motor function. Mice were allowed to walk freely across a transparent glass walkway, and footprints were recorded by a high-speed color camera. At least three valid uninterrupted runs were collected for each mouse, and gait-related parameters were analyzed using CatWalk XT software.

Perfusion and sectioning

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and transcardially perfused with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The spinal cord tissues were dissected and post-fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h. Tissues were subsequently dehydrated in 30% sucrose solution for 2 days, embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, and cut into 10-μm-thick sections using a freezing microtome (Leica, CM3050S, Germany).

Immunohistochemistry and histology

The tissue sections were washed with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 10 min to remove excess OCT compound. The sections were then permeabilized and blocked with blocking solution containing 5% BSA and 0.5% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature. The primary antibodies were added to the sections and incubated overnight at 4°C. The primary antibodies used were anti-GFAP (Merck, Cat# MAB360), NF200 (Abcam, Cat# ab8135), F4/80 (Abcam, Cat# ab6604), 5-HT (Immunostar, Cat# 20080). The following day, the sections were washed with TBST for three times to remove residual primary antibody. Corresponding fluorescent secondary antibody was applied to the sections and incubated for 1 h in the dark at room temperature. The fluorescent secondary antibody used were anti-mouse (Thermo Fisher, Cat# A21235), anti-rabbit (Thermo Fisher, Cat# A32731) or anti-rat (Thermo Fisher, Cat# A21247). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI-containing antifade mounting medium and images were captured using a NanoZoomer S60 (Hamamatsu, Japan) microscope at 20× magnification. Routine H&E staining was used to examine the overall injured-spinal cord, heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney morphology and assess the level of infiltrating mononuclear cells.

Plasma biochemical analysis

Five milliliters of venous blood were collected from each participant and centrifuged at 3000 r/min for 10 min. The plasma was collected and subjected to biochemical analysis using a fully automated biochemical analyzer. Liver function-related markers, including albumin (ALB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) and total bilirubin (TBIL) were measured.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from mouse spinal cord samples using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA sequencing was performed by LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China) using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using a threshold of |log2fold change| ≥ 2 and adjusted P < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed based on the identified DEGs. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the ranked gene expression matrix to evaluate pathway-level changes.

timsTOF proteomic analysis

LC-MS/MS analysis was carried out using a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) coupled with a Nanoelute system (Bruker, Germany). The peptides were loaded onto a C18-reversed phase analytical column (Thermo Scientific Easy Column) and analyzed in positive ion mode. The electrospray voltage was set to 1.5 kV. Precursors and fragments were analyzed in the TOF analyzer over an m/z range of 100-1700. The timsTOF Pro was operated in PASEF mode using Compass Hystar software version 5.0.36.0. All raw data from each sample were combined and analyzed by the MaxQuant software version 1.6.14 for protein identification and quantitation.

TREM-1 inhibition analysis

To validate the functional involvement of the TREM-1-DAP12 pathway in EXOhUCMSC-mediated liver protection after SCI, a TREM-1 inhibitory peptide (LP17; sequence: LQEEDAGEYGCM; synthesized by QiangYao, Suzhou, China) was used to block TREM-1 signaling in vivo. Mice were randomly divided into five experimental groups: (1) Sham (normal control), (2) SCI (model control), (3) SCI + LP17 (TREM-1 inhibitor alone), (4) SCI + EXOhUCMSC (exosome alone), and (5) SCI + LP17 + EXOhUCMSC (combination treatment). For LP17 treatment, mice were injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 150 μg of LP17 dissolved in 100 μL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) immediately after spinal cord contusion, followed by daily injections for 7 consecutive days, as previously described [23,24]. For the combination group, EXOhUCMSC (1 mg/kg, in 200 μL PBS) was administered by tail vein injection at 1st day post-injury, with LP17 treatment administered as above. At 7 days post-injury, mice were sacrificed, and liver tissues were collected for analysis of NF-κB signaling activation. Phosphorylation of NF-κB p65 (Ser536) and total NF-κB p65 were detected by Western blotting, with β-actin used as an internal loading control. The results were compared across all five groups to evaluate the effect of TREM-1 blockade on EXOhUCMSC-induced NF-κB suppression.

Depletion of hepatic macrophage

To deplete hepatic macrophages, mice were intravenously injected via the tail vein with 200 μL clodronate liposomes (approximately 5 mg/mL; Targetmall, USA) 3 days before SCI. Control animals received an equal volume of PBS-containing control liposomes. Liposomes were gently mixed before use and administered slowly using a 29G insulin syringe. The efficiency of hepatic macrophage depletion was evaluated 48 h after injection by immunofluorescence staining by F4/80. Successful depletion was confirmed by a marked reduction of F4/80+ Kupffer cells in liver tissues.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Each individual mouse was considered an independent biological replicate, and the specific sample sizes for each experiment are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. All quantitative experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates unless otherwise stated. Data were presented as the mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. For comparisons between two independent groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test was used when the data were normally distributed and showed equal variance. To compare differences among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test was performed. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Animals were randomly assigned to treatment groups based on a set of pre-generated random numbers produced using Microsoft Excel. All analyses were conducted by investigators who were blinded to the experimental groups.

Results

EXONSC and EXOAP elicit functional rescue in SCI mice

Given the benefits brought by the properties of exosomes (EXO) in attenuating neuronal death and improving motor recovery [19,26], we wished to determine whether EXO were beneficial in SCI mice systemically. A previous study has shown that bioactive peptides anchored autologous exosomes facilitate injured spinal cord repair effectively [16]. Interestingly, during the experiment, we observed that exosomes from both autologous plasma (EXOAP) and neural stem cells (EXONSC) exhibited the therapeutic potential for SCI treatment, as evidenced by the improvement in motor function recovery and the reduction of the lesioned area. As shown in Fig. 1, EXOAP and EXONSC were comparable in yield and size distribution, displayed typical saucer-cup shape morphology, and expressed classic exosomal marker CD81, CD9 and CD63 with absence of GM130 expression, a marker for Golgi, confirming the purity of the exosomes (Fig. 1A–C). To test the therapeutic potential of EXOAP and EXONSC on treatment of spinal cord injury, a mouse model of spinal cord contusion was established, followed by weekly intravenous injection of EXOAP or EXONSC beginning at 1 day post injury at a dose of 1 mg/kg per week for 3 times (Fig. 1D), and locomotor recovery was measured using Basso Mouse Scale (BMS) and inclined plate test. After injury, locomotor function was gradually improved 1 week post injury, during 1-3 weeks following SCI, the BMS scores of the three groups were continuously increased, and at the end of treatment, the BMS scores of the vehicle group were decreased slowly to a score of 1, with that of the EXOAP and EXONSC groups increased significantly to a score of > 2 (Fig. 1E). The maximum dip angle of the EXOAP-(43.39° ± 1.09°) and EXONSC (43.12° ± 1.78°)-treated mice was higher than that of vehicle treated mice (40.03° ± 1.96°) (Fig. 1F). Histological analysis of spinal cord sections (Fig. 1G) and quantification of the injured area (Fig. 1H) revealed that exosome treatment significantly reduced tissue damage. EXOhUCMSC improves phenotype rescue and motor function recovery in SCI mice

To determine whether exosomes from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells offer greater efficacy in treating SCI, given their higher clinical relevance and enrichment in functional moieties that promote cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and inflammation attenuation [27,28]. We administered EXOhUCMSC, confirmed by western blot (Fig. 2A), transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) (Fig. 2B), into adult murine SCI mice (Fig. 2C). Similar to previous studies, EXOhUCMSC led to marked repair of injured spinal cord tissue, characterized by a notable reduction in the GFAP-positive injury scar and extension of NF200-positive neurofilaments (Fig. 2D–F), compared to untreated SCI mice. Enhanced axon regeneration was also observed, evidenced by more and longer serotonergic axon sprouting across a distance of 4-5 mm, whereas 5-HT-positive axon fibers in untreated SCI mice reached less than 2 mm across the epicenter scar barrier (Fig. 2G and H). Unsurprisingly, F4/80+ macrophages were reduced following EXOhUCMSC administration (Fig. 2G and I). Furthermore, motor function recovery was assessed using the BMS score, inclined plate test, and gait analysis (Fig. 3). Specifically, animals in the EXOhUCMSC-treated group developed superior ankle movements such as restoring plantar stepping with full hindlimb weight support, followed by lift-off with a BMS score reaching to ∼3 (Fig. 3A) as well as inclined plate angle of up to 43.52° (Fig. 3B). Moreover, EXOhUCMSC-treated mice displayed more regular gait patterns and better hindlimb support capacity, as reflected by maximum contact area, print length, and print area (Fig. 3C–H). Given that all three types of exosomes (EXOAP, EXONSC and EXOhUCMSC) promoted microstructural and functional recovery in mice after SCI with no observable side effects and no significant histopathological changes detected in the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, or kidneys (Fig. S1), we hypothesized that they may share common mechanisms facilitating the repair process. To investigate the general mechanism underlying EXO-mediated SCI repair, we first examined the protein expression profiles of the three exosome types. LC-MS/MS analysis revealed 810 common proteins among EXONSC, EXOAP and EXOhUCMSC (Fig. S2A), most of which were associated with cellular processes and binding functions (Fig. S2B–D). In vivo distribution results showed that each exosome type primarily accumulated in the liver (Fig. S3A–B). Notably, a previous study reported that spinal cord injury could cause chronic liver pathology in rat model [29], and mitigating liver inflammation may help control the inflammatory response following SCI [6,30]. Therefore, we speculate that exosomes may promote recovery from SCI, at least in part, by alleviating liver inflammation.

To better understand how the liver responds to SCI, we performed global transcriptome and proteomics sequencing analysis for the liver of wild-type or SCI mice (28 days post-contusion). Notably, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of livers between control and SCI mice revealed concordant transcriptional changes with 177 upregulated and 339 downregulated genes (p < 0.05) in SCI mice models (Fig. S4A and B). To gain insights into functional, oncogenic and molecular differences in liver between SCI and control groups, we performed global Gene Ontology (GO) analyses of the DEGs between the SCI model mice and the C57BL/6 mice. The majority of the enriched biological process (BP) terms of upregulated DEGs were related to activation of immune response, leukocytes mediated immunity, and neutrophils mediated immunity, etc (Fig. 4G), while the downregulated DEGs were enriched in biological processes such as fatty acid metabolic, small molecular catabolic process (Fig. S4C).

To evaluate the dynamic changes of liver proteins after SCI, we performed mass spectrometry analysis which showed the protein profiles being significantly changed in SCI mouse liver compared with the sham controls (Fig. S4D). Consistent with the results observed at transcriptional level, protein profile enrichment analysis identified inflammatory related pathways including acute-phase response and humoral immune response were upregulated, while lipid- and amino acid metabolic process were downregulated in SCI liver (Fig. 4H, Fig. S4D–E). Furthermore, activation of hepatic macrophages, as indicated by CD68+ macrophages, was significantly elevated 28 days post injury (Fig. 4I and J). To further determine whether the liver inflammation and dysfunction were directly mediated by hepatic macrophages, we injected clodronate liposomes to deplete macrophages in the liver (Fig. S4F) and a significant increase in BMS scores in the liposome-treated group was observed (Fig. S4G), which is consistent with the previous results [6], suggesting that hepatic macrophages play a negative role in the liver response to SCI. However, after exosome administration, CD68+ macrophages in the liver section and serum ALT and AST levels were significantly decreased, while ALB levels in serum were increased compared with the untreated SCI controls (Fig. 4I–K), indicating that exosome treatment could alleviate liver inflammation and dysfunction caused by SCI.

Exosomal HSP70 inhibits TREM-1-DAP12 activation on macrophages

Activation of the TREM-1 signaling pathway is initiated by the receptor-ligand interactions in CNS disease, but no clear TREM-1 ligand has been reported to date. Studies have suggested that high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and heat shock protein (HSP70) may be natural ligands for TREM-1 [36,37]. Interestingly, HMGB1 is either actively or passively released into the extracellular matrix and blood circulation and persists for a long period of time, even throughout the acute, subacute and chronic stages after SCI [36,38]. Interestingly, we found that both HSP70 and HMGB1 protein were highly expressed across the three kinds of exosomes (Fig. S6A–B). Furthermore, we performed molecular docking analysis to predict the binding sites of HSP70 and HMGB1 to TREM-1 [39]. As shown in Fig. 7A, both HSP70 and HMGB1 shared common binding sites on the TREM-1 receptor, with HSP70 exhibiting a broader range of interacting residues. Based on this finding, we hypothesized that exosome-associated HSP70 may competitively bind to TREM-1, thereby blocking the interaction between TREM-1 and damage-associated HMGB1 released after spinal cord injury. To validate this hypothesis, we established an in vitro inflammatory model using Raw264.7 macrophages (Fig. S7) and primary bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDMs) (Fig. 7). The experimental design was illustrated in Fig. 7B: (A) macrophages treated with HMGB1 alone served as the inflammatory control; (B) cells pre-incubated with EXOhUCMSC for 2 h followed by HMGB1 stimulation; and (C) cells pre-incubated with EXOhUCMSC pretreated with an anti-HSP70 antibody [40] before HMGB1 stimulation. After 24 h, downstream signaling and pro-inflammatory cytokine production were assessed. Western blot analysis (Fig. 7C and D) revealed that HMGB1 treatment significantly upregulated the expression of phosphorylated NF-κB p65 (p-NF-κB) and caspase-1 p20 compared with the control group. Pre-incubation with EXOhUCMSC markedly attenuated this activation, as evidenced by reduced levels of p-NF-κB and caspase-1 p20. However, the protective effect of EXOhUCMSC was partially reversed when exosomes were pre-treated with an anti-HSP70 antibody, suggesting that exosomal HSP70 is critical for TREM-1 blockade. Quantitative PCR analysis (Fig. 7E–G) showed that HMGB1 stimulation significantly increased the mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. EXOhUCMSC treatment significantly suppressed the expression of these cytokines, while the anti-HSP70 antibody partially abolished this suppression. Discussion

Exosomes derived from various stem cell sources, including neural stem cells, bone marrow stem cells, human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells have shown broad application prospects for SCI. Although studies have reported that exosome treatment can alleviate the complex pathological changes in secondary injury after SCI by promoting axonal regeneration and angiogenesis while limiting blood-spinal cord barrier (BSCB) disruption, apoptosis, and inflammation [41], the systemic effects and underlying mechanisms remain to be determined as the majority of exosomes accumulate in the liver after systemic administration. Here, we demonstrate that repeated intravenous injection of exosomes derived from hUCMSCs, NSCs and autologous plasma facilitated function restoration and inflammation alleviation and prevented the pathological progression of the secondary injury in SCI mice. These exosomes alleviated liver inflammation caused by SCI, thereby inhibiting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and reducing leukocyte mobilization and infiltration into the spinal cord parenchyma. Furthermore, this alleviated the progression of secondary injury and prevented neuroinflammation. Our findings suggest the clinical applicability of exosomes as a systemic treatment modality for SCI.

Accumulating evidence indicates that the liver, as a central metabolic and immune organ, actively responds to CNS injury. In the acute phase after SCI, the liver contributes to repair process through the secretion of acute-phase proteins [8]. However, our data, consistent with previous studies, show that elevated levels of ALT and AST, decreased ALB levels, increased macrophage infiltration in both chronic SCI patients and mouse models, as well as upregulated inflammatory pathways and downregulated metabolic pathways in the liver 28 days post-SCI [29]. These observations collectively suggest the development of progressive hepatic dysfunction during chronic SCI. However, our clinical data are derived from a cross-sectional observational study. As such, they can only establish an association between chronic spinal cord injury and aberrant liver-function parameters, rather than a causal relationship. Future longitudinal studies and mechanistic investigations in well-controlled cohorts or animal models are warranted to delineate the underlying pathways. Notably, our investigation reveals that liver-derived inflammation may exacerbate secondary spinal cord damage [6,10]. This highlights the potential of targeting hepatic inflammation as a candidate therapeutic strategy for SCI by modulating the systemic environment. A previous study reported that bioactive peptide-painted exosomes targeted to spinal cord and facilitated injury repair. As a control group, naïve autologous plasma-derived exosomes also promoted functional recovery after SCI, but these naïve exosomes predominantly accumulated in the liver [16]. This biodistribution pattern led us to hypothesize that exosomes may mitigate SCI, at least in part, by attenuating hepatic inflammation. To test this, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of three distinct exosomes (EXONSC, EXOAP and EXOhUCMSC). Uniformly, all three types of exosomes accumulated in the liver. Further analysis revealed that these exosomes are primarily taken up by hepatic macrophages. Whole transcriptome analysis of liver tissue demonstrated downregulation of pathways associated with inflammatory cytokine production and granulocyte migration following exosome treatment. Specifically, we observed suppressed expression of TREM-1-DAP12 signaling and its downstream effectors NF-κB and IL-1β, indicating attenuation of inflammatory amplification cascades. Consistent with these findings, exosome treatment resulted in reduced expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6) in both the liver and spinal cord, accompanied by decreased leukocytes infiltration in circulation and at the injury site. These results suggest that alleviating liver inflammation induced by SCI may mitigate secondary inflammatory injury, indicating that the liver could serve as a peripheral target organ for prevention secondary inflammatory damage by improving hepatic function.

Studies have reported TREM-1 is highly upregulated on Kupffer cells, circulating monocytes and monocyte derived macrophages, the lack of TREM-1 restricted Kupffer cell activation, infiltration of inflammatory cells, liver injury and fibrogenesis [32]. Our findings demonstrate that SCI induces significant upregulation of TREM-1 and DAP12 expression on hepatic macrophages, suggesting the activation of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways in the liver following central nervous system trauma. Importantly, exosome administration effectively attenuated this increase, indicating a potential mechanism through which exosomes modulate hepatic immune responses. Based on the established understanding that the TREM-1 signaling pathway can be activated by damage-associated molecular patterns including HMGB1 and HSP70 [42], our findings provide a mechanistic link between systemic inflammation and hepatic immune activation after SCI. The observed persistent upregulation of HMGB1 [38] following spinal cord injury suggests its potential role as a circulating mediator that activates the TREM-1 pathway in hepatic macrophages. Our experimental results demonstrate that exosome treatment effectively suppresses this activation through a competitive inhibition mechanism. Specifically, we found that exosome-derived HSP70 binds to TREM-1, thereby preventing the binding of circulating HMGB1 and subsequent downstream signaling. However, we acknowledge that this competitive inhibition mechanism, while supported by our in vitro data, requires further validation using additional mechanistic studies, including co-immunoprecipitation assays, binding affinity analyses, genetic or pharmacological inhibition approaches in vitro and in vivo. The ubiquity of HSP70 expression across various exosome types suggests this may represent a fundamental mechanism underlying the therapeutic effects of exosome-based treatments for SCI. The previous investigation into Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) revealed that exosome-mediated membrane repair constitutes a key therapeutic mechanism. Notably, we observed that exosomes from different sources consistently produced therapeutic effects and significantly reduced serum ALT and AST levels [43]. These findings led us to hypothesize that beyond membrane repair, exosomes may also mitigate hepatic metabolic disturbances associated with DMD [44]. However, the relevance of the DMD study to the current SCI narrative is indirect, and we have included these observations primarily to highlight the consistency of exosome-mediated hepatic effects across different disease models. In this study, we indeed found that exosomes can improve liver inflammation and metabolic dysfunction caused by SCI. Our data, particularly from in vitro pre-inhibition experiments, support the conclusion that competitive inhibition of the TREM-1 signaling pathway on liver macrophages is a primary mechanism. However, we recognize that exosomes are complex biological vectors, and their full therapeutic potential likely involves multiple effectors. Beyond the TREM-1 pathway, exosomal cargos such as miRNAs represent a compelling parallel mechanism, given their well-established roles in post-transcriptional regulation of inflammation and metabolism. While the present study focused on the protein-mediated pathway, the potential contribution of exosomal miRNAs warrants thorough investigation. Future studies should systematically profile exosomal miRNA signatures and test their functional relevance, as such insights could broaden the therapeutic applications beyond TREM-1-mediated diseases. Nonetheless, our current findings possess broader implications, as the identified pathway may be relevant to other TREM-1-mediated pathologies, such as cardiovascular diseases [45]. Our work thus provides a mechanistic foundation for the clinical development of exosome-based therapeutics. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the link between liver inflammation and SCI recovery, while suggested by our data, is not fully supported by direct causal evidence (e.g., hepatocyte-specific genetic manipulation). Second, the safety, optimal dosing, and scalability of exosome-based therapies remain to be addressed before clinical translation. Third, our study primarily relied on mouse models; further validation in larger animal models and human studies is warranted. All three exosome preparations demonstrated efficacy in our experimental models, offering distinct advantages for clinical translation, including superior quality control and manufacturing scalability [46]. Furthermore, although intravenous administration represents the conventional delivery route, our data suggest that intraperitoneal injection may constitute an alternative approach worthy of further investigation.

In summary, this study demonstrates that exosome treatment can alleviate liver inflammation after SCI, thereby reducing systemic inflammation and secondary spinal cord injury. Our findings provide a foundation for future research but do not establish a viable therapeutic option for SCI without further validation.
