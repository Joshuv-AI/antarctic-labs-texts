Sliding motions on systems with non-Euclidean state spaces: A differential-geometric perspective
Fernando Castaños

Abstract
This paper extends sliding-mode control theory to nonlinear systems evolving on smooth manifolds. Building on differential geometric methods, we reformulate Filippov’s notion of solutions, characterize well-defined vector fields on quotient spaces, and provide a consistent geometric definition of higher-order sliding modes. We generalize the regular form to non-Euclidean settings and design explicit first- and second-order sliding-mode controllers that respect the manifold structure. Particular attention is given to the role of topological obstructions, which are illustrated through examples on the cylinder, Möbius bundle, SO(3), and 2-sphere. Our results highlight how geometric and topological properties fundamentally influence sliding dynamics and suggest new directions for robust control on nonlinear spaces.



1
Introduction

Differential geometry provides the natural mathematical framework for describing and analyzing nonlinear control systems
whose state spaces are smooth manifolds rather than Euclidean spaces
[
7
,
1
,
13
]
. Many control
problems—such as rigid-body stabilization, spacecraft attitude control, and mobile robotics—involve configuration spaces
with rich geometric and topological structures
[
26
,
7
]
. Concepts such as vector fields, flows, tangent bundles,
and Lie group actions are indispensable for understanding the behavior of such systems beyond the scope of traditional Euclidean
analysis
[
18
]
.

In nonlinear control theory, differential geometric methods have profoundly advanced the study of controllability, feedback
equivalence, and stabilization
[
15
,
13
,
26
,
24
,
6
]
. They have also revealed fundamental
limitations, such as the impossibility of global asymptotic stabilization on certain manifolds due to topological
obstructions
[
3
,
14
]
. As engineering applications continue to push the boundaries of performance in
geometrically constrained settings, there is an increasing need to integrate geometric insights into controller design.

Sliding-mode control is a powerful and robust technique for stabilizing nonlinear systems, particularly in the presence of
model uncertainties and external disturbances. Classical formulations of sliding-mode control were developed for systems
evolving on Euclidean spaces, where sliding manifolds are typically linear or affine, and discontinuous feedback can be readily
implemented
[
30
,
27
]
. Extending sliding-mode control to systems evolving on smooth manifolds introduces new
challenges: switching surfaces must be modeled as embedded submanifolds, vector fields must respect the manifold structure,
and topological constraints can fundamentally alter the nature of sliding dynamics.

With few exceptions—most notably the early and pioneering work of Sira-Ramírez
[
28
]
—differential geometric methods
have not been systematically developed within the context of sliding-mode control. In particular, the geometric and topological
aspects of switching manifolds, sliding vector fields, and higher-order sliding motions on manifolds remain largely unexplored.

1.1
Contributions

In this paper, we extend sliding-mode control theory to systems whose state spaces are smooth manifolds. We characterize
well-defined maps and vector fields on quotient manifolds—a result with potential applications beyond the sliding-mode control community.

We reformulate Filippov’s notion of solutions in a geometric setting, provide a consistent definition of higher-order sliding modes,
and point out limitations in some existing formulations
[
32
,
31
]
.

We also generalize the concept of regular form to non-Euclidean spaces and construct explicit first- and second-order sliding-mode
controllers that respect the geometry of the underlying manifold. Special attention is given to topological obstructions, illustrated
through examples on the cylinder, Möbius bundle,
\mathrm{SO}(3)
, and 2-sphere.

Our results underscore the importance of geometric and topological considerations in robust control design and suggest new directions
for sliding-mode control on general nonlinear spaces.

1.2
Paper Structure

Section
2
recalls the necessary background for discussing dynamical systems on smooth manifolds and characterizes
well-defined maps and vector fields on quotient spaces. Geometric definitions of sliding motions are introduced in Section
3
.
In Section
4
, we generalize the regular form and provide design examples of second-order sliding-mode controllers on
non-Euclidean state spaces. Finally, concluding remarks are presented in Section
5
.

2
Global representations for non-Euclidean

state-spaces

In the differential-geometric approach to dynamical systems, it is assumed that the state-space is a smooth

n
-dimensional manifold
X
and that the system’s motions are governed by a vector field taking values on its
tangent bundle
TX
. More precisely, the system is described by differential equations of the form

\dot{x}=f(x,t)\;,

(1)

where
x\in X
is the state,
t\in\mathbb{R}
is time, and
f(x,t)
belongs to the tangent space
T_{x}X
of
X
at
x
.
Since manifolds are formally defined through
local
coordinate charts, the geometric
objects on such manifolds (e.g., maps, vector fields, or differential forms) are usually specified by patching
local coordinate-based definitions. A usual local model for the system is thus

\dot{\xi}=\varphi(\xi,t)\;,

(2)

where
\xi\in W\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state coordinate, that is,
\xi=\psi(x)
with
\psi:U\to W
a homeomorphism
and
U\subset X
a local coordinate domain. Here,
\varphi:W\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a local coordinate-based definition of the time-dependent
vector field
f
. Unfortunately, using multiple coordinate charts in control applications is impractical, so the designer sometimes settles
for a local controller. There are cases, however, in which a
globally defined
control law is essential, and a
coordinate-free representation of
X
becomes mandatory. Throughout,
x
will denote
a point of the abstract manifold
X
, while
\xi
will denote its coordinate representation in a local chart.

This section explores two extrinsic ways to construct a coordinate-free representation of
a state space. In order to define and design sliding motions, we will be required to construct subsets, maps, and vector fields on such state spaces.

2.1
Embedded manifolds

Allow us to begin by recalling some definitions. If
X
and
\tilde{X}
are smooth manifolds and
\Psi:X\to\tilde{X}

is a smooth map, for each
x\in X
we define a map
\Psi_{*}:T_{x}X\to T_{\Psi(x)}\tilde{X}
, called the

pushforward
associated with
\Psi
, by
(\Psi_{*}v)g=v(g\circ\Psi)
, for any tangent vector
v\in T_{x}X
and
any smooth function
g:\tilde{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. A smooth map
\Psi:X\to\tilde{X}
is called
a
submersion
if
\Psi_{*}
is surjective at each point (or equivalently, if
\rank\Psi=\dim\tilde{X}
). It
is called an
immersion
if
\Psi_{*}
is injective at each point (equivalently, if
\rank\Psi=\dim X
).
A
smooth embedding
is a smooth map
\Psi:X\to\tilde{X}
that is both an immersion and a topological
embedding. That is, it maps a manifold
X
smoothly into another manifold
\tilde{X}
such that the image

\Psi(X)
inherits the subspace topology from
\tilde{X}
, and the map is a homeomorphism onto its image. An

embedded submanifold
of a manifold
\tilde{X}
is a subset
X\subset\tilde{X}
that forms a manifold under
the subspace topology. It is equipped with a smooth structure so that the natural inclusion map

X\hookrightarrow\tilde{X}
qualifies as a smooth embedding. See
[
18
]
for details.

One way to obviate the need for multiple charts is to embed
X
in a higher-dimensional real
space
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. While this is always possible due to the Whitney Embedding Theorem
[
18
, Thm. 6.15]
, we note that
the proof of the Theorem is not constructive. Fortunately, explicit embeddings are known for a variety of manifolds relevant to applications
(the sphere, the torus, SO(3), real projective space, etc).

Example 1

(The Group of Rotations
\mathrm{SO}(3)
)
.

In the attitude control problem for rigid bodies, the configuration manifold is the group
\mathrm{SO}(3)
of all
rotations about the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. An effective global control strategy is to embed
\mathrm{SO}(3)

in
\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
,

\mathrm{SO}(3)=\left\{R\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\mid R^{\top}R=I,\;\det(R)=1\right\}\;.

(3)

Note that the representation (
3
) is redundant: Whereas the dimension of
\mathrm{SO}(3)
is only
equal to three, nine parameters are required to specify each rotation matrix
R\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
.
However, the redundancy drawback is easily counterbalanced by the fact that control laws based on (
3
)
are, by design, global and free of unwinding phenomena
[
9
,
12
]
.

\triangle

Example 2

(The Sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
)
.

The 2-sphere can be naturally embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

\mathbb{S}^{2}=\left\{L\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\mid\|L\|=1\right\}\;.

Spherical state-spaces appear, for example, in the kinematic control problem for reduced
attitude
[
5
,
8
]
or the control of energy-conserving electrical circuits
[
28
]
.

\triangle

Given an embedded submanifold
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
, an arbitrary manifold
Y
, and a
k
-times differentiable map

\tilde{s}\in\mathcal{C}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{p},Y)
, one can naturally define a new map
s\in\mathcal{C}^{k}(X,Y)
by restricting
\tilde{s}

to
X
; that is, we set
s=\tilde{s}\big|_{X}
. Similarly, suppose that
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a
time-dependent vector field. Then, a vector field
f
on
X
can be defined by restriction, provided that

\tilde{f}(x,t)\in T_{x}X\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}\;,\quad\text{for all $(x,t)\in X\times\mathbb{R}$}\;.

(4)

In other words,
\tilde{f}
must be tangent to
X
for the restriction to yield a well-defined vector field on the submanifold.
To alleviate the notation, we will drop the distinction between
\tilde{f}=f
when embedding the state space in

\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

2.2
Quotients of manifolds by group actions

While the theory of quotient manifolds is well established
[
18
, Ch. 21]
, comparatively less attention has been given
to the practical construction of geometric objects on such spaces. In this section, we build upon classical results to present a
constructive framework for defining subsets, maps, and vector fields on quotient manifolds. This approach will serve as a
foundation for the development of sliding manifolds, Lyapunov functions, and discontinuous vector fields in subsequent sections.

Let
\tilde{X}
and
\Gamma
be, respectively, a smooth manifold and a group with identity element
e
. Recall that
a
left action of
\Gamma
on
\tilde{X}
is a map
\Gamma\times\tilde{X}\to\tilde{X}
, denoted by

(\gamma,\tilde{x})\mapsto\gamma\boldsymbol{\cdot}\tilde{x}
, such that

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(\gamma_{2}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\tilde{x})

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\tilde{x}

\displaystyle e\boldsymbol{\cdot}\tilde{x}

\displaystyle=\tilde{x}

for all
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma
and
\tilde{x}\in\tilde{X}
. For each
\tilde{x}\in\tilde{X}
, the
orbit of
\tilde{x}
is the set

\Gamma\boldsymbol{\cdot}\tilde{x}=\left\{\gamma\boldsymbol{\cdot}\tilde{x}\mid\gamma\in\Gamma\right\}
.
We consider only smooth left actions in this paper, so we refer to them simply as actions.
The collection of orbits is called the
quotient of
\tilde{X}
by
\Gamma
and is denoted by
\tilde{X}/\Gamma
. The map

q:\tilde{X}\to\tilde{X}/\Gamma
that sends
\tilde{x}
to its orbit is called the
canonical quotient map
.
Although the quotient need not be a smooth manifold in general, it can be given a smooth structure under reasonable
assumptions. Before stating such conditions, recall that a map
g:X\to Y
is said to be
proper

if the preimage of each compact subset of
Y
is compact.

Definition 1

(Free and Proper Actions)
.

An action of
\Gamma
on
\tilde{X}
is said to be
free
if

\gamma\boldsymbol{\cdot}\tilde{x}=\tilde{x}\quad\text{for some $\tilde{x}\in\tilde{X}$}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\gamma=e\;.

The action is said to be
proper
if
(\gamma,\tilde{x})\mapsto(\gamma\boldsymbol{\cdot}\tilde{x},\tilde{x})
is a proper map.

Theorem 1

(Quotient Manifold
[
18
]
)
.

Suppose
\Gamma
is a Lie group acting smoothly, freely, and properly on a smooth manifold
\tilde{X}
. Then, the orbit
space
X=\tilde{X}/\Gamma
is a topological manifold of dimension equal to
\dim\tilde{X}-\dim\Gamma
, and has a
unique smooth structure such that
q:\tilde{X}\to X
is a smooth submersion.

Since
q
is always surjective,
\tilde{X}
can be used to parameterize
X=\tilde{X}/\Gamma
(this is specially
useful when
\tilde{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
). However, because
q
is in general not injective, the parameterization is not unique,
so special care must be taken to ensure that the objects that we define on
X
using
\tilde{X}
are indeed well
defined and inherit the required degree of smoothness.

Theorem 2

(Passing Smoothly to the Quotient
[
18
]
)
.

Suppose
\tilde{X}
and
X
are smooth manifolds and
q:\tilde{X}\to X
is a surjective smooth submersion. If
Y
is a smooth
manifold and
\tilde{s}:\tilde{X}\to Y
is a smooth map that is constant on the fibers of
q
, then there exists
a unique smooth map
s:X\to Y
such that
s\circ q=\tilde{s}
.

Recall that the
fiber
of a map
q:\tilde{X}\to X
is a set of the form
q^{-1}(x)\subset\tilde{X}
for some
x\in X
.
For
q
the canonical quotient map, the fibers of
q
are then precisely the orbits of
\tilde{X}
. This takes us directly to the following.

Corollary 1

(Smooth Map on Quotient Manifold)
.

If
Y
is a smooth manifold and
\tilde{s}:\tilde{X}\to Y
is a smooth map that is constant along orbits, that is, if

\tilde{s}(\tilde{x})=\tilde{s}(\gamma\boldsymbol{\cdot}\tilde{x})\quad\text{for all $\tilde{x}\in\tilde{X}$ and $\gamma\in\Gamma$}\;,

(5)

then there exists a unique smooth map
s:X\to Y
such that

\tilde{s}=s\circ q
:

We now focus on subsets and level sets. Given a quotient map
q:\tilde{X}\to X
, a subset

\tilde{S}\subset\tilde{X}
is said to be
saturated with respect to
q
if
\tilde{S}=q^{-1}(S)

for some subset
S\subset X
. Equivalently,
\tilde{S}
is saturated if

\tilde{x}\in\tilde{S}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Gamma\boldsymbol{\cdot}\tilde{x}\subset\tilde{S}\;.

(6)

Corollary 2

(Level Set of Quotient Manifold)
.

Suppose that
\tilde{s}:\tilde{X}\to Y
satisfies (
5
) and let
c
be any element in

Y
. Then, the level set
\tilde{S}=\tilde{s}^{-1}(c)
is saturated with respect to the
quotient map. Moreover,
\tilde{S}=q^{-1}(S)
with
S\subset X
also a level set.

Proof.

According to Corollary
1
, there exists a map
s
such that
\tilde{s}=s\circ q
.
Thus,
\tilde{S}=q^{-1}(s^{-1}(c))
. The result follows by setting
S=s^{-1}(c)
.
∎

We finally turn our attention to vector fields. Let
q_{*}
be the pushforward of
q
and consider a vector field

\tilde{f}:\tilde{X}\times\mathbb{R}\to T\tilde{X}
. If there exists a vector field
f:X\times\mathbb{R}\to TX

such that

q_{*}\tilde{f}(\tilde{x},t)=f(q(\tilde{x}),t)\quad\text{for all $(\tilde{x},t)\in\tilde{X}\times\mathbb{R}$}\;,

(7)

then we say that
\tilde{f}
and
f
are
q
-related
. Since computing
q_{*}
explicitly is not
always easy, we will use
\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{f}}\tilde{s}
, the Lie derivative of a real-valued function

\tilde{s}
along the flow of
\tilde{f}
, to characterize the vectors fields that can be
q
-related.

Corollary 3

(Vector Field on Quotient Manifold)
.

If
\tilde{f}
is a smooth time-dependent vector field on
\tilde{X}
such that, for any smooth real-valued function
\tilde{s}

satisfying (
5
), we have that

\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{f}}\tilde{s}(\tilde{x})=\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{f}}\tilde{s}(\gamma\boldsymbol{\cdot}\tilde{x})\quad\text{for all $(\tilde{x},t)\in\tilde{X}\times\mathbb{R}$ and $\gamma\in\Gamma$}\;,

(8)

then there exists a unique smooth time-dependent vector field
f
on
X
that is
q
-related to
\tilde{f}
.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{f}
be a vector field satisfying (
8
) and let
x
be any point of

X
. Note that
q
is surjective, so
q^{-1}(x)\neq\emptyset
and we can choose an arbitrary

\tilde{x}\in\tilde{X}
such that
q(\tilde{x})=x
. Take any smooth map
s:X\to\mathbb{R}
and compute

\tilde{s}=s\circ q
. By construction,
\tilde{s}
satisfies (
5
), so

\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{f}}(s\circ q)(\tilde{x})=\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{f}}(s\circ q)(\gamma\boldsymbol{\cdot}\tilde{x})\quad\text{for all $\gamma\in\Gamma$}\;.

Direct application of the definition of the pushforward yields

Geometric definition of sliding-motions

The simplest motivation for Filippov’s notion of solution arises from the following.

Example 5

(The Real Line
\mathbb{R}
)

Consider a one-dimensional system of the form (

Note that
\dot{x}(t)=0

Clearly, the differential equation (

(similar problems arise when

a differential inclusion

Filippov’s theory of differential equations with discontinuous right-hand sides was developed for systems
evolving in Euclidean spaces. In this section, we extend Filippov’s definition to systems defined on smooth manifolds.
The main challenge is to describe the notion of solution entirely in terms of the manifold’s global
geometric/topological structure, not in terms of local representations. Also, particular attention must be paid to the definition
of switching sets. In our setting, we require these sets to consist of collections of embedded submanifolds, which ensures that
well-defined reduced-order dynamics can be constructed on them.

Allow us to begin with the following.

Definition 2

(Piecewise-smooth Vector Field)
.

A vector field
f:X\times\mathbb{R}\to TX

a finite (or locally finite) partition of

into smooth submanifolds

such that:

1.

X=\bigcup_{i}X_{i}
, and the interiors of the submanifolds

are pairwise disjoint;

2.

the restriction
f\big|_{X_{i}\times\mathbb{R}}:X_{i}\times\mathbb{R}\to TX

3.

discontinuities of

on
X
are confined to the boundaries
\partial X_{i}
.

Consider the coordinate-free dynamical model (
1
) with
f
a time-dependent
piecewise-smooth vector field. An

defined on an interval

is called a

\dot{x}\in F(x,t)

almost everywhere. Here,

First, we collect the limits of

\gr_{t,U}(f)=\left\{(x,f(x,t))\in TX\mid x\in X\setminus U\right\}

and its closure in

of

\mathbb{L}(x,t)=\bigcap_{\mu(U)=0}\left\{v\in T_{x}X\mid(x,v)\in\overline{\gr}_{t,U}(f)\right\}\;,

where the intersection runs over all sets

the convex hull in the tangent space

In particular, for (

The existence and uniqueness of solutions to the differential inclusion (

extend naturally to the setting of smooth manifolds.
Here we assume that, for every initial condition

Note that, if the function

reduces to the singleton

and the notion of solution coincides with that of classical differential equations on manifolds. If

is discontinuous on a

from one direction and immediately leaving it by following

trajectory remains within

(Local Weak Invariance)
.

A manifold

Definition 4

(Sliding Vector Field)
.

Consider the Filippov differential inclusion (
18
) and an embedded submanifold

with inclusion map

\imath:S\hookrightarrow X

\sigma:S\times\mathbb{R}\to TS
is said to be

a sliding vector field
if it is smooth in

\imath_{*}\sigma(y,t)\in F^{\circ}(\imath(y),t)\quad\text{for all $(y,t)\in S\times\mathbb{R}$}

with
F^{\circ}(x,t)=\ri\big(F(x,t)\big)=\interior_{\aff(F(x,t))}\big(F(x,t)\big)
,
the
relative interior
of

\aff(F(x,t))\subset T_{x}X
.
Suppose that the dimension of the affine hull of

to
m
for all

the sliding vector field is of order

r
if the dimension of

is equal
to

.

The reason for requiring

that the submanifold

Remark 1
.

The key elements in Definition
4
are the invariance of the manifold
S
and the requirement that solutions along

S
be non-classical (that is, not transversal to
S
). Some authors replace the condition of the
solutions being non-classical with that of finite-time attractivity to
S
(see, e.g.,
[
31
,
32
]
). However, we argue
that—just as equilibria or limit cycles, which are invariant sets, retain their identity regardless of their stability
properties—a sliding orbit should likewise be recognized as such even if it lacks attractivity.
In fact, as we will illustrate below, repulsive sliding orbits arise naturally in non-Euclidean spaces as a consequence of
topological obstructions. Furthermore, due to Brockett’s obstruction, repulsive sliding orbits also appear naturally in systems
with non-holonomic constraints (see, e.g.,
[
25
]
).

We now turn our attention to the case in which the piecewise-smooth nature of

4.2
Second-order sliding-mode control, terminal and

twisting algorithms

Modern methods for designing higher-order sliding-mode controllers are grounded in the concept of homogeneity
of vector fields
[
20

, a property originally formulated for real vector spaces. A geometric definition,
involving the Lie bracket of the control vector field with an Euler vector field, is provided in
[

4

Although Euler vector fields are inherently defined on linear spaces, this geometric formulation opens the door
to possible extensions of homogeneity to non-Euclidean manifolds. Developing such generalizations remains
an open direction for future research. In the remainder of this section, we introduce two ad hoc second-order
sliding-mode control algorithms.

5
Conclusions

In this work, we developed a geometric framework for extending sliding-mode control to nonlinear systems whose state spaces
are smooth manifolds. By generalizing classical constructions—such as Filippov’s solution concept and the regular form—to
accommodate the intrinsic structure of manifolds, we provided a consistent approach to defining and analyzing sliding dynamics
beyond the Euclidean setting.

We showed how embedded submanifolds can serve as switching surfaces, and how sliding vector fields and control laws can be
constructed to respect the geometry of the configuration space. Our examples on the cylinder, Möbius bundle, and 2-sphere
highlight the influence of topology on system behavior, including the emergence of repulsive sliding orbits and the fundamental
limitations to global stabilization.

Future work

An important and challenging problem is trajectory tracking. In the Euclidean case, the first step is to
characterize admissible reference trajectories
x^{*}:\mathbb{R}\to X
for which a nominal
control
u^{*}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
exists, i.e., trajectories satisfying

\dot{x}^{*}=h(x^{*},u^{*})
. One then introduces the tracking error
e=x-x^{*}

(see, e.g.,
[
29
]
) together with the incremental control
v=u-u^{*}
. The next step is to compute
the error dynamics,

\dot{e}=h(e+x^{*},u^{*}+v+d(t))-h(x^{*},u^{*})

and use
v
to robustly stabilize the origin
e=0
.
When the state space is a general manifold, each of these steps requires significant adaptation,
since subtraction and error dynamics are no longer globally defined. Developing an intrinsic
formulation of trajectory tracking on manifolds is therefore a natural and pressing extension of
the present work.

More broadly, the results presented here highlight the importance of incorporating geometric and
topological insights into the design of robust controllers for systems with nonlinear state spaces.
In particular, we believe that a deeper exploration of intrinsic homogeneity, higher-order sliding
modes, and feedback equivalence on manifolds will open promising directions for future research
in geometric control theory.
