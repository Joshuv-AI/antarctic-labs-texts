Geophysical insights from the first geomagnetic field absolute intensity curve for central Asia (2200BCE–2000CE), and implications for archaeomagnetic dating
Authors: R Bonilla-Alba, M Gómez-Paccard, F J Pavón-Carrasco, A Chauvin, E Beamud, V Martínez-Ferreras, J M Gurt-Esparraguera, E Luneau, M L Osete
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUntil now, the lack of high-quality paleointensity data has left Central Asia as a major gap in global and regional reconstructions of geomagnetic field intensity variations. Here, we present the first intensity paleosecular variation curve (PSVC) for Central Asia, spanning the last 4000 years and based on 35 new high-quality paleointensity data obtained from 71 well-dated pottery fragments from Uzbekistan, integrated with previous archaeomagnetic data and direct geomagnetic observations. The new PSVC reveals a long-term increase in field intensity from ~ 32.5 µT (2000 BCE) to a pronounced peak of ~ 79.7 µT (400 BCE), followed by a rapid decline and a distinctive V-shaped minimum between 400 BCE and 400 CE. Cross-correlation with records from the Levant, Greece, and India shows a close match in the timing of intensity changes, suggesting large-scale geodynamo processes behavior across Eurasia. The new PSVC not only provides key constraints on the evolution of the geomagnetic field over the last millennia but also serves as a novel archaeomagnetic dating tool for Central Asia, offering a valuable approach to refine the typological classification of central Asian pottery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23570-2.Keywords: Archaeomagnetism, Paleointensity, Uzbekistan, Paleosecular variation curve, Dating, Central AsiaSubject terms: Geophysics, Palaeomagnetism

Introduction

The Earth’s magnetic field (EMF) is a dynamic feature of our planet, exhibiting significant secular variation on decadal to millennial timescales. While direct measurements from observatories and satellites have provided a detailed picture of secular variation over the past centuries1, reconstructing earlier field behavior relies on archaeomagnetic and paleomagnetic data from well-dated archaeological materials and rocks. Archaeomagnetism is a powerful tool for tracking multi-decadal to centennial changes in the EMF, constraining geodynamo processes, and providing chronological frameworks for archaeological studies.

Over the past four millennia, archaeomagnetic studies have revealed important fluctuations in geomagnetic field intensity. In Europe, two major intensity maxima during the 6th–7th and 8th–9th centuries CE form a distinctive double-peak structure, observed in both regional and global field models2–6. Further back, the Levantine Iron Age Anomaly (LIAA) stands out for exceptionally high intensities in the Levant and neighboring regions during the 1st millennium BCE7,8.The spatial extent, origin, and evolution of this anomaly remain debated, with some studies proposing westward migration8 and others suggesting localized development6,9.

In East Asia, new data from China indicate both extreme lows (ca. 2200 BCE) and a possible high-intensity event around 1300 ± 300 BCE10, raising questions about the relationship between these features and the LIAA or other Eurasian anomalies. For example, this has led to speculation that this maximum may be a precursor to the high-intensity episodes recorded in the Levant and Turkey7,11. However, the lack of robust, high-quality paleointensity records from Central Asia has prevented a full assessment of whether these anomalies are local, regional, or global phenomena, and how they relate to the interplay between dipolar and non-dipolar field components.

Global and regional field models (e.g., ArchKalmag14k, SHAQW family) have attempted to reconstruct Holocene geomagnetic evolution, but their accuracy in Asia is limited by the scarcity and variable quality of available data, especially for the second and first millennia BCE. This gap is particularly critical for understanding the eastern extent of the LIAA, the timing and amplitude of the double peak, and the occurrence of abrupt intensity changes across Eurasia. As a result, Central Asia remains a “blind spot” in geomagnetic field reconstructions.

To shed light on this matter, we present 35 new high-quality paleointensity data from the Iron and Bronze Ages, obtained from 71 pottery fragments from Uzbekistan. Based on these results, and critically integrating previous data and direct geomagnetic observations, we developed the first continuous paleosecular variation curve (PSVC) of geomagnetic intensity for Central Asia (Fig. 1). This new PSVC enables us to: (1) test the presence, timing, and amplitude of major intensity anomalies, including the LIAA, the double peak, and abrupt intensity lows, in Central Asia; (2) compare regional trends with neighboring records from the Levant, Greece, India, and China and global models predictions; and (3) provide new approach for archaeomagnetic dating tool for archaeological research. Our results offer new insights into the spatiotemporal evolution of the geomagnetic field across Eurasia and highlight the need for expanded high-quality datasets to resolve outstanding questions about field behavior over the past few millennia.

Rock-magnetism results

To evaluate the suitability of the ceramic samples for reliable paleointensity determination, we first characterized their magnetic mineralogy and stability using a suite of rock-magnetic experiments (see Methods for full protocols and parameter definitions). Visual inspection of fragments from all three sites revealed minimal variability in color and texture, with most samples displaying a consistent orange-red tone (Fig. 1c).

Rock-magnetic measurements on 19 representative fragments revealed three distinct magnetic behaviors:

Group 1: Twelve samples, primarily from Jarkutan (JKT) and Molali Tepe (MOL), are dominated by low-coercivity minerals. Lowrie tests and thermomagnetic susceptibility curves (Fig. 2a and d) show near-reversible behavior, with Curie temperatures ranging from 380 to 580 °C. These features indicate magnetite and/or titanomagnetite with variable titanium content as the main magnetic carriers. All samples in this group yielded positive paleointensity results.

(a, b, c) Illustrate the results obtained from the Lowrie test on representative samples from the three identified groups. (d, e, f) Thermomagnetic curves of the same samples, with heating curves in red and cooling curves in blue.

Group 2: Three samples from Jarkutan (fragments 476, 477, 479) display both low- and high-coercivity components. In these, the Lowrie test (Fig. 2b) reveals a low-coercivity phase demagnetized near 600 °C (magnetite/maghemite) and a high-coercivity phase with unblocking temperatures above 600 °C, consistent with hematite. Thermomagnetic curves for these samples were noisy due to low susceptibility, but magnetization versus temperature curves were interpretable (Fig. 2e). Notably, these samples did not fully lose their magnetization at high temperatures, preventing accurate Curie temperature determination. During paleointensity experiments, these samples exhibited alteration in the later steps of heating and were excluded from further analysis.

Group 3: Four samples from JKT and MOL exhibit a combination of low-coercivity minerals demagnetized below 450 °C and medium- to high-coercivity minerals persisting up to 600 °C (Fig. 2c). Curie temperatures between 250 °C and 290 °C suggest titanomagnetites with elevated titanium content, while a Curie temperature near 580 °C indicates magnetite. As in Group 2, incomplete demagnetization at high temperatures and the presence of hematite were confirmed by the Lowrie test and thermomagnetic data (Fig. 2f). These samples also showed alteration during thermal treatment and were excluded for paleointensity determination.

In summary, rock-magnetic analyses indicate that magnetite and titanomagnetite are the dominant magnetic carriers in most analyzed ceramics, with hematite present in some samples. The presence of high-coercivity hematite is associated with instability during heating, which negatively affects paleointensity reliability. Only samples dominated by low-coercivity minerals (magnetite/titanomagnetite) were considered suitable for robust paleointensity analysis (see Methods for further details).

Paleointensity results

To reconstruct past geomagnetic field intensity variations in Central Asia, we carried out a comprehensive paleointensity study on 208 specimens from 71 pottery fragments collected at three archaeological sites (see Methods for details). These analyses aimed to generate a robust dataset for constructing the first regional paleosecular variation curve (PSVC) for the area covering the last 4 millennia.

A total of 111 specimens (53%) met the strict quality criteria for paleointensity determination, as outlined in the Methods section. This high success rate, particularly for Bronze Age ceramics from Jarkutan (81.3%) and Molali Tepe (66.2%), reflects the fine-grained texture and technological quality of the fine wares from these sites. In contrast, the more recent ceramics from Kuchuk Tepe exhibited a lower success rate (43.8%), likely due to differences in the manufacturing process. This suggests that even in older populations, the ceramic manufacturing process was highly advanced, producing fine-grained ceramics of exceptional quality, as also visually evident. The main reason for rejection was magnetic alteration during thermal treatment, evidenced by failed pTRM checks, high DANG values, or Zijderveld plots not pointing to the origin (see Fig. 3g–i).

Paleointensity values were determined using linear fits to Arai plots over temperature intervals from ~ 150–200 °C (to avoid viscous overprints) up to 400–580 °C, where samples were fully demagnetized. All accepted determinations corresponded to well-defined single magnetization components, as confirmed by Zijderveld plots. Representative examples of successful and failed experiments are shown in Fig. 3.

Specimen-level intensity values and associated quality parameters, including the number of steps, the TRM fraction used for slope calculation (f), quality factor (q), MAD, DANG, curvature parameter (k), and β ratio, are presented in Table S1 (Supplementary Material) and summarized in Fig. 4. Most accepted samples were determined using more than 10 thermal steps (minimum 5, maximum 19), with f values ranging from 51% to 92% and q values from 11.9 to 137.5. MAD values ranged from 0.4° to 4.6°, and both k and β were always below 0.5 and 0.06, respectively. All specimens were corrected for TRM anisotropy, resulting in differences between uncorrected and corrected intensities ranging from 0.4% to 33.6% (average 14.8%, Fig. 4e). Cooling rate correction factors ranged from 0.04 to 13.6% (average 5.6%, Fig. 4j), consistent with previous studies in the region.

Based on these results, 35 new fragment-mean paleointensity values were obtained, covering the period from 2000 BCE to 300 BCE (Table 1). These are the first high-quality paleointensity results for Central Asia from Bronze and Iron Ages that meet modern standards. The new data indicate a progressive increase in field intensity from 32.7 µT (oldest Jarkutan samples, 2000–1700 BCE) to a maximum of 79.7 µT (Kuchuk Tepe, ~ 400 BCE), with intermediate values for Molali Tepe and older Kuchuk Tepe fragments. Standard deviations for the obtained intensity means are generally low, reflecting the reliability of the determinations.

Overall, our results reveal that geomagnetic field intensity in Central Asia was significantly lower during the first half of the 2nd millennium BCE compared to the late 1st millennium BCE. However, the absence of data between these intervals limits the resolution of the intensity curve for this period, highlighting the need for additional high-quality data to fully characterize geomagnetic field changes across the entire timeframe.

A first geomagnetic field intensity curve for central Asia for the last 4000 years

To provide a robust reference for the evolution of the geomagnetic field in Central Asia, we constructed a new PSVC spanning the last 4000 years. This curve integrates new high-quality paleointensity data and previously published results, covering 2000 BCE to 2000 CE. Details on data selection, quality assessment, and curve construction are described in the Methods section.

The PSVC shows a minimum intensity of ~ 43 µT around 1800 BCE, followed by a gradual increase to a maximum of ~ 76 µT near 400 BCE. The most rapid rise, about + 6 µT per century between 1200 and 900 BCE, is much slower than the abrupt changes reported during the LIAA (35–58 µT per century7. Unlike the sharp intensity spikes seen in the Levant, the Central Asian record displays a steady increase until 400 BCE, with no evidence of abrupt fluctuations in either the input data or the PSVC. However, for the interval 1400–500 BCE, the curve relies mainly on low-quality data (grey dots in Fig. 5), likely obscuring short-term variability. High-quality, well-dated intensity data from this period are needed to resolve the spatiotemporal evolution of the LIAA in Central Asia.

New paleointensity data (red dots) are presented alongside previous data (black, blue, and yellow dots for high-quality data; grey dots for the less robust estimations). Direct measurements are plotted as green squares. The new PSVC for Central Asia between 2200 BCE and 2000 CE is represented as a purple line along with a shaded area corresponding to the error band at a 95% confidence interval (2σ). Data and PSVC are relocated to the coordinates of Termez (37.3°N, 67.2°E).

A well-defined intensity maximum value around 400 BCE, supported by high-quality data, coincides with the peak observed across Europe near 500 BCE, from the Canary Islands to Turkey6. After 400 BCE, the PSVC records a marked decline, reaching a minimum near the turn of the era. This rapid decrease, at about − 9 µT per century, is followed by a recovery (+ 4 µT per century) between 1 CE and 400 CE, forming a pronounced V-shaped pattern. This feature12 has been linked to a reverse polarity patch in the core-mantle boundary’s radial field, which weakens the surface field in this region.

Beyond 400 CE, the PSVC displays a relative minimum around 680 CE, flanked by two maxima at ~ 500 CE and ~ 900 CE. This double peak is similar to the pattern found in European records3,4, though most Central Asian data for this interval are of low quality. Acquiring additional high-quality data is crucial to confirm the presence of this double oscillation in the region.

From 850 to 1600 CE, the curve shows a gradual decline in intensity, which accelerates after 1600 CE, reaching a minimum around 1800 CE13. Subsequently, the geomagnetic field intensity in the Termez area increases, approaching present-day values.

Geophysical insights

To evaluate whether the new regional PSVC enhances our understanding of past geomagnetic field intensity changes in Central Asia, we compared the new PSVC with predictions from the most recent global geomagnetic field paleoreconstructions: the ArchKalmag14k model14 and the SHAWQ-family models15,16 at the coordinates of Termez (Fig. 6). Between ~ 2200 BCE and ~ 600–400 BCE, the PSVC and ArchKalmag14k display a similar trend, both showing a progressive intensity increase following the minimum around 1800 BCE. However, while ArchKalmag14k model shows the next maximum around 650 BCE, our PSVC suggests a later peak, around 400 BCE. In contrast, SHAWQ2k shows a markedly different pattern during this interval, with a peak around 1000 BCE followed by a prolonged decline until the beginning of the Common Era. This difference may be linked to the weighting method employed in SHAWQ2k, which likely produces high-quality data from adjacent regions to exert significant influence where local data are not available. ArchKalmag14k, which does not apply such weighting scheme, aligns more closely with the new PSVC during periods with few or no high-quality dataset (e.g., ~ 900–500 BCE).

New PSVC for Central Asia between 2200 BCE and 2000 CE represented as a purple line, along with a shaded area corresponding to the error band at a 95% confidence interval (2σ). The PSVC and global model results are relocated to the coordinates of Termez (37.3°N, 67.2°E).

From ~ 400 BCE to ~ 400 CE, none of the global models captures the full amplitude of the V-shaped intensity minimum, though both reflect similar large-scale trends. The SHAWQ-family model shows a decline in field intensity starting around 1000 BCE, reaching a minimum near the turn of the era, and followed by a gradual recovery lasting until approximately 100–150 CE. In contrast, the ArchKalmag14k model indicates a later decrease, beginning around 600 BCE, with the minimum also occurring near 100 CE, and a subsequent increase that continues until approximately 500–550 CE.

During the first and second millennia CE, both global models and the new PSVC show the previously described double oscillation in geomagnetic field intensity, which is present at the European scale4, but also seems to appear in further regions such as Guatemala17. The agreement with the models, and the long spatial scale of this phenomenon, could be linked with the dipolar terms of the geomagnetic field, observing the two peaks around all the Earth’s surface. From approximately 850 CE to 1900 CE, there is a good agreement among the PSVC and global field reconstructions. However, it is worth noting that over the past four centuries, global models tend to overestimate field intensity compared to the high-quality archaeomagnetic data available for Central Asia and, consequently, the PSVC presented here. This has already pointed before by Troyano et al. (2021). These authors obtained high-quality intensity estimations from Bukhara lower than expected during the 18th century and not reproduce by global models. They suggest that this is likely linked to a relative minimum of the axial dipole during the late 18th century.

The comparison between our new PSVC and the ArchKalmag14k model predictions using different harmonic degrees shows the temporal evolution in the relative contributions of dipolar and non-dipolar components of the geomagnetic field (Fig. 7).

Comparison of intensity curves for Uzbekistan using different harmonic degrees (Fn). The black dashed line represents the new PSVC, while colored lines show the ArchKalmag14k intensity predictions for different harmonic degree.

For the last 2000 years, the PSVC for Uzbekistan exhibits better agreement with predictions based on low-degree spherical harmonics (Fn = 1 and Fn ≤ 2), suggesting a predominance of the dipolar component in the region. The correlation is particularly notable during the period 0–2000 CE, where the dipolar field variations (Fn = 1) closely follow the general trends observed in our PSVC. This analysis therefore lends further support to the hypothesis that the observed double peak between 600 and 800 CE in intensity, observed in Europa, Asia, and Guatemala, is primarily linked to the dipolar character of the field during the last two millennia.

However, during the first millennium BCE, the maximum agreement is observed with higher-degree harmonics (Fn ≤ 4 to Fn ≤ 10), indicating a greater influence of non-dipolar components. This transition occurs around the BCE/CE boundary, where the behavior of the geomagnetic field intensity variations shifts from predominantly non-dipolar to dipolar dominance. The intensity maximum observed between − 800 and − 400 years12,18, exhibits mixed characteristics. The rapid intensity increase showed around 1000 BCE looks more like a non-dipolar feature, as discussed above, but there is also a rise in the dipolar term during this period. This observation implies that phenomena such as the Levantine Iron Age Anomaly (LIAA) may result from the superposition of dipolar variations with shorter-timescale non-dipolar anomalies. The significant intensity decrease around 500 BCE12,18 also appears to show predominantly non-dipolar characteristics, as evidenced by its better agreement with higher-degree harmonic predictions. This finding supports the hypothesis that rapid geomagnetic field changes are primarily driven by non-dipolar processes, while gradual variations reflect the underlying dipolar field evolution.

The new PSVC shows a greater amplitude and higher variability than the models’ predictions, especially during periods of rapid change. This suggests that regional archaeomagnetic records can capture local geomagnetic features that are not fully represented in global models, highlighting the importance of high-resolution regional data for understanding the full complexity of geomagnetic field behavior across different temporal and spatial scales.

Regional correlation of geomagnetic intensity variations

To better understand the regional and spatial variability of geomagnetic field intensity variations, we perform a cross-correlation analysis of the Virtual Axial Dipole Moment (VADM) well-defined curves from Uzbekistan and three geographically proximate regions: Greece6, the Levant19, and India20. This approach allows us to compare the temporal evolution of paleointensity records across these areas, identifying synchronous trends and possible regional geomagnetic features.

The analysis reveals a clear similarity in both the amplitude and timing of geomagnetic intensity variations across these regions (Fig. 8). The highest correlation coefficients are observed at small lags of 0 years for the Levant, -130 years for India, and − 50 years for Greece, indicating that the main features of the geomagnetic field intensity changes are in phase throughout Western and Central Eurasia. This strong in-phase relationship suggests that regional geomagnetic field variations were governed by large-scale processes.

Bottom panels present the cross-correlation functions, with vertical dashed lines marking the lag at which maximum correlation is observed.

Moreover, the persistence of high cross-correlation values over a range of small lags, particularly for India and Greece, may reflect minor chronological uncertainties in the datasets or regional differences in the timing of intensity maxima and minima. Still, the overall results suggest that the most significant geomagnetic events happened at about the same time across these areas.

Dating applications: A. posteriori intensity-based dating approach applied to potteries from central Asia

One of the most direct applications of PSVCs is their use in archaeomagnetic dating. In this approach, archaeomagnetic data, including both directional and intensity measurements, obtained from baked archaeological materials of unknown age are compared with a reference PSVC corresponding to the region where the samples were collected, in order to estimate their most probable ages21. Several software tools, such as ChronoModel22,23 or Archaeo_dating24, and ArchaeoPyDating25, employ reference PSVCs computed at the geographic coordinates of the archaeological structure to be dated. These PSVCs may consist of locally derived curves, such as the one presented in this study, or synthetic curves constructed from regional or global paleomagnetic reconstructions26,27.

In addition to the application of archaeomagnetic dating based on PSVCs, they can also be used to refine the dating of the archaeomagnetic data that were used to construct it, the so-called a posteriori dating approach. This approach can help determine the most probable age intervals within the period assigned to the archaeological materials22,28 by different chronological methods (e.g., radiocarbon dating, thermoluminescence, typological or historical constraints). During the construction of the curve, a posterior age distribution for the input data is obtained, providing insights into the most likely ages of the materials. This type of analysis involves the convolution of the probability density function (PDF) derived from both the PSVC (the mean curve and its uncertainty band) and the archaeomagnetic datum (the mean value, along with its associated age and measurement uncertainties). It can be applied to the declination, inclination, or intensity geomagnetic field parameters. The methodology of the new approach defined in this work can be found in the Methodology section.

This method can be used to determine the most probable ages of samples within the same age range. To illustrate this, we considered samples JKT500 (Fig. 9a) and JKT496 (Fig. 9b), which were previously associated with a broad age interval between 1700 and 1400 BC. Figure 9 shows the results obtained from this analysis for both samples. The PSVC reveals a noticeable increase in intensity throughout the study period. The intensity value of sample JKT500 (prior intensity: 44.7 µT) is lower than that of sample JKT496 (prior intensity: 51.7 µT). As a result, the posterior age of JKT500 is found to be older than that of JKT496. Specifically, the posterior ages are 1663 BC and 1536 BC, respectively. It is worth noting that the posterior intensities are closely aligned with the a priori values.

Panels a and b show the results of a posteriori dating for two ceramics from the Jarkutan site. Both samples correspond to the same a priori range between 1700 BC and 1400 BC. For further details, refer to the methodology section.

As with classical archaeomagnetic dating, obtaining reliable results using this method requires a well-defined PSVC over time, and the EMF variation recorded in the curve should exhibit high amplitude variability. This allows for the generation of more precise posterior probability density functions (PDFs) for age, providing greater temporal resolution than the prior age alone. However, if the study period shows minimal amplitude variability in an EMF element, this type of posterior analysis would not yield additional information (a phenomenon known as the “plateau effect”), as the posterior PDFs would closely resemble those generated a priori using the known archaeomagnetic data. It is also important to highlight that the primary goal of this method is not to provide a revised chronology for the material under study. Instead, its aim is to offer supplementary insights, particularly within the field of archaeology, to help distinguish different groups within a single assemblage of materials.

Conclusions

This study provides 35 new paleointensity data points from 71 pottery samples collected at three archaeological sites in Uzbekistan, significantly expanding the regional dataset for Central Asia. By integrating these new results with previously published data, we constructed the first geomagnetic field intensity PSVC for Central Asia spanning the last 4000 years.

The new PSVC reveals key features in the regional geomagnetic field: a gradual intensity increase from ~ 1800 BCE, a maximum around 400 BCE, and a V-shaped pattern between 400 BCE and 400 CE. Notably, two distinct intensity peaks between 500 and 1000 CE are observed, consistent with similar features previously documented in Europe and, potentially, Guatemala. These findings suggest that the double-peak structure is a widespread phenomenon, likely linked to the dipolar component of the geomagnetic field.

Our analysis indicates that non-dipolar field behavior predominated in Uzbekistan during the two millennia BCE, while a more dipolar and globally coherent field characterizes the Common Era. Cross-correlation with records from the Levant, India, and Greece demonstrates strong regional coherence and near-synchronous geomagnetic field variations, further supporting the predominance of dipolar behavior over broad spatial scales during the last two millennia.

In addition to providing new insights into geomagnetic field evolution in Central Asia, this work introduces an a posteriori dating approach for baked archaeological materials, offering a valuable tool for refining chronologies where conventional dating is uncertain.

Overall, our findings highlight the need for additional high-quality paleointensity and directional data, particularly for the periods 1400–500 BCE and 350–1500 CE, to further resolve the regional geomagnetic field behavior and its implications for both geophysical and archaeological research.

Methodology

Archaeological context

The samples analyzed in this study were found in three archaeological sites located north of the Amu Darya in the Surkhandarya province of southern Uzbekistan (Fig. 1): Jarkutan (37.6° N, 67.0° E), Molali Tepe (38.1° N, 67.7° E), and Kuchuk Tepe (37.5° N, 66.8° E). These sites were discovered in the early 1960s (Kuchuk Tepe) and early 1970s (Molali, Jarkutan), and have been excavated by various archaeological teams, providing a significant collection of ceramics. Recently, the Spanish-Uzbek IPAEB team conducted archaeometry research on ceramics from these sites as part of the CERAC project (University of Barcelona). A total of 71 pottery fragments from the earlier excavations were sampled for archaeomagnetic research, selected from the ceramics provided by the CERAC project, with preference for fine-grained red-orange-yellow pottery. The samples were officially exported from Uzbekistan to Spain, with the authorization granted by the Institute of Art Studies of the Academy of Sciences of the Republic of Uzbekistan.

Jarkutan and Molali Tepe are affiliated with the Sapalli Culture, a regional variant of the Oxus Civilization dating from ca. 2100 to 1400 BCE, while Kuchuk Tepe represents the subsequent Iron Age period. Sample selection prioritized fine wares with well-constrained archaeological contexts based on typological and stratigraphic criteria. Chronological assignments rely on established typologies and radiocarbon dating where available. A total of 71 fragments were analyzed: 23 from Jarkutan (2000–1400 BCE), 32 from Molali Tepe (1600–1400 BCE), and 16 from Kuchuk Tepe (1000–300 BCE). Further details on excavation history, site descriptions, and sample catalogues are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Rock-magnetic experiments

In order to investigate the main magnetic carriers in our samples, 19 pieces from different ceramic fragments were subjected to rock-magnetism experiments. These measurements were performed at the paleomagnetism laboratories of the Complutense University of Madrid (Spain) and Géosciences-Rennes (France).

Susceptibility versus temperature (χ-T) curves were performed in Madrid and Rennes for 8 samples using a Kappabridge KLY-4 susceptibility meter coupled to a CS4 furnace (AGICO). This device increases the temperature progressively and measures the bulk susceptibility of the powdered sample at each step. In this study, the temperature was increased from room temperature up to 700 °C, and then cooled back to room temperature. In addition, 5 saturation magnetization versus temperature (Ms-T) curves were obtained using a Variable Field Translation Balance (VFTB) (Mag-Instruments29 for samples with low susceptibility. Magnetization was measured in an applied field of 1T, warming up to around 650 °C, and then cooling back to 40 °C in air. These experiments can be used to estimate the Curie/Néel temperature of the magnetic minerals contained in the ceramic samples, and to check for thermally induced magnetic alteration by comparing the heating and cooling branches of the plots. From the Ms-T curves, we calculated the corresponding Curie/Néel temperatures following the second derivative method30 to identify the inflection points and convexities of the curves. These calculations were done using the RockMagAnalyzer 1.1 software31.

Lowrie tests32 were conducted on 27 samples. This experiment consists of the thermal demagnetization of the samples after acquiring three orthogonal Isothermal Remanent Magnetization (IRM) cross components. For IRM acquisition, we used an ASC Scientific IM-10-30 Impulse Magnetizer, applying a magnetic field of 0.12 T along the x-axis of the samples, 0.6 T along the y-axis and up to 2.7 T along the z-axis. After this, the samples were progressively demagnetized by increasing the temperature from room temperature to 600 °C in 50 °C steps. The magnetization acquired after each temperature step was measured with a slow-speed spinner magnetometer Molspin Minispin.

Paleointensity experiments

The 71 pottery fragments were sub-divided into 208 specimens, which were used for paleointensity experiments. Specimens were placed in 2.2 cm3 quartz cubes and secured with a mixture of glass wool and sodium silicate to prevent any movement during the laboratory procedures. Experiments were carried out at the paleomagnetism laboratories of the Complutense University of Madrid (Spain) and Géosciences-Rennes (France).

Paleointensity determination was obtained following the Thellier-Thellier method33, using a thermal demagnetizer (MMTD-24) in Madrid and a locally developed furnace, designated “Ramsés”, at Géosciences-Rennes. Two 2G SQUID magnetometers were employed to measure the magnetization. Each step comprises two heating and cooling cycles: the first, during which a laboratory magnetic field of 50, 60 or 65 µT is applied along the Z + axis, and the second, when the field is applied in the opposite sense (Z-). The cooling of the samples was achieved in about 45 min.

In accordance with the methodology proposed by Bonilla-Alba et al.12,18, partial thermoremanent magnetization (pTRM) checks, TRM anisotropy (ATRM) and cooling rate corrections were conducted to ensure robust estimations of past geomagnetic field intensity. pTRM checks were carried out at regular intervals, every two temperature steps, to assess the stability of the samples throughout the experiment. The determination of the ATRM tensor necessary to apply the anisotropy correction involves six additional heating-cooling cycles, which were conducted when approximately 70% of the magnetization was removed. The specimens were heated and cooled at the same temperature with the magnetic field applied at X+, X-, Y+, Y-, Z + and Z-. Cooling rate factors were tentatively determined experimentally for all the specimens. In Madrid, the cooling rate corrections were applied when about 80–90% of the magnetization was lost. In Rennes, the cooling rate experiment was performed immediately after the ATRM correction. Following the protocol defined in Gomez-Paccard et al.34, four supplementary heating/cooling cycles were required to determine the cooling rate correction and alteration factors. The first cycle involved a TRM acquisition with the applied field along the Z + direction, with samples cooling over approximately 24 h. The second cycle had the applied field in the Z- direction, with samples cooling at the usual laboratory rates (45 min). The third and fourth cycles involved applying the field in the + Z and − Z directions, respectively, with samples cooling at rapid/typical rates.

The paleointensity results were obtained through a linear fit in Arai plots, with only specimens exhibiting a single component in the Zijderveld plots being selected. In order to obtain results that meet high-quality criteria typically applied in modern studies, only specimens corresponding to positive pTRM checks were considered. This includes those with a maximum difference of 10% between the pTRM check and the TRM obtained at the same temperature.

TRM anisotropy and cooling rate corrections were applied at the specimen level. The cooling rate correction was only applied when the rate obtained was higher than the observed mineralogical alteration, given by the alteration factor34. In instances where the degree of alteration exceeded the cooling rate, the value employed was that derived from the analysis of sister specimens. If this was not feasible, a 5% value was employed, in alignment with mean results obtained from archaeological and modern fired clays18,35.

Paleointensity analysis was performed using the StarmacAW3.0 software developed by P. Roperch at Géosciences-Rennes. This software calculates the paleointensity values and provides the different quality parameters used to assess the robustness of the determinations. The quality criteria applied in this study are consistent with those proposed in our recent research12,18. A minimum of five temperature steps and a TRM fraction (f parameter) exceeding 50% are required to determine the paleointensity. Furthermore, the quality factor, q36, should exceed 10, while the angular deviation MAD37 and the deviation angle DANG38 should be less than 5° and 10°, respectively. The fragments’ means were calculated from the specimens results as arithmetic averages, with at least three specimens included in each calculation. The standard deviations of the intensity measurements were also calculated.

Methodology for the PSVC construction

In this work, we present 35 new paleointensity data from Central Asia, with ages spanning the second and first millennia BCE. To build a robust regional PSVC for the last 4000 years, these new results must be integrated with previously published data available in global databases such as GEOMAGIA50.v339. This integration is particularly crucial for certain periods that remain poorly constrained by new data. For instance, only a single new data point is available for the interval between 1400 and 600 BC, precisely when several abrupt geomagnetic intensity changes have been documented in the Levant7. Moreover, this data is associated to a large chronological uncertainty, limiting its reliability for resolving short-term geomagnetic variations during this critical period.

A crucial aspect of integrating previously published data with our new dataset lies in the highly variable quality of older data, which were produced over several decades using diverse protocols and instrumentation40. Therefore, a thorough evaluation of these legacy data is essential before incorporating them into any regional paleointensity reconstruction. For our target region, a 1000 km radius around the city of Termez, 258 paleointensity data points are available for the period between 2200 BCE and 1900 CE (see Fig. 1c). These data originate from various archaeomagnetic studies conducted on fired materials such as bricks and ceramics12,13,18,41–49. While these data are relatively evenly distributed over the last 4000 years (Fig. 1d), the experimental protocols applied vary widely, which can significantly impact the reliability and comparability of the results.

The most recent studies12,13,18 together the new data obtained in this study provide data that meet current quality standards. They were obtained using Thellier-Thellier-type methods, including the Triaxe protocol, which involve the progressive heating of samples in controlled laboratory fields and incorporate partial thermoremanent magnetization (pTRM) checks. In addition, they account for thermoremanent magnetization (TRM) anisotropy at the specimen level, practices that is considered essential for robust for paleointensity determination. They also include corrections for the cooling rate effect upon TRM acquisition. Consequently, the data from Troyano et al.13 and Bonilla-Alba et al.12,18 can be classified as high-quality.

A comprehensive examination of the other available data for our study area, excluding the four aforementioned studies, reveals that most paleointensity estimates were also derived using the Thellier and Thellier33 method or its derivative, the Coe50 protocol (also known as the zero-field–in-field method). However, these studies do not incorporate TRM anisotropy corrections at the specimen level. The importance of TRM anisotropy, particularly in ceramic materials, has long been recognized51, and more recent studies have shown that neglecting this effect can lead to significant errors, in some cases up to 90%, in paleointensity estimates40,52,53. As a result, it is now widely accepted that data lacking TRM anisotropy corrections may introduce substantial uncertainty and are therefore considered technically non-robust or low-quality. In addition, these studies did not incorporate cooling rate corrections, which can also affect the final paleointensity estimates. However, this effect is generally considered less significant than that of TRM anisotropy, with a typical influence of approximately 5%, according to the global compilation by Genevey et al.40 or previous studies performed on pottery from the same region18. To account for this, we applied a uniform 5% reduction to the paleointensity values from these studies to compensate for the lack of cooling rate correction.

Moreover, for our target region and time period, direct geomagnetic observations are also available. Specifically, 324 historical data points dated between 1856 and 1930 have been selected from the HISTMAG compilation54. These records offer a highly precise view of paleointensity variations in the area, although they cover only a relatively short interval, less than a century.

The dataset available for our region is shown in Fig. 5. Together, they cover the last 4 millennia quite homogeneously. However, as previously discussed, the quality of the data is heterogeneous. High-quality data are represented in different colors, while those lacking TRM anisotropy corrections at the specimen level are depicted as grey points. In general, the grey data do not deviate significantly from the high-quality data, particularly during the first and second millennia BCE. However, the differences appear to be more pronounced in the last two millennia. However, a broad agreement of non-robust estimates with high-quality data is observed. Therefore, we believe that the older data can be used to construct the first PSVC curve for Central Asia valid for the last 4000 years, providing an initial approximation for periods where no high-quality data are available and complementing the observations for the other period. However, it is clear that the intensity paleoreconstructions will be refined with future studies of well and precisely dated materials, which will enhance our understanding of past multidecadal variations in the region.

Building on our previous studies in regional18,55 and global scale EMF reconstructions15, the data were categorized into four distinct groups, with each assigned a weight based on specific quality criteria. Historical data from HISTMAG54, the most precise and reliable data, were assigned a weight of 20. The most reliable archeointensity data are considered those obtained from Thellier-type protocols including TRM anisotropy corrections and pTRM checks at the specimen level and derived from at least three specimens. This group is assigned to a weight of 10. In contrast, data corrected for TRM anisotropy, which included pTRM checks but were obtained from two or one specimen, were assigned an intermediate weight of 5. The remaining data, considered the least reliable, were assigned a weight of 1. The difference in weights between the most reliable data (10) and the least reliable (1) is based on the results obtained by Campuzano et al.15. These weights were incorporated as a priori constraints into the modeling approach used to compute the PSVC regional curve.

The curve was created using a bootstrap technique, generating a set of 5000 PSVCs with penalized cubic b-splines in time with knot points every 50 years within the selected time window56. This method makes use of paleointensity data with the associated uncertainties in age and intensity. To ensure the reliability of the PSVC, outliers were identified and removed by detecting data whose residuals (the difference between the actual data and the fitted curve) exceeded three times the root mean square error (RMS) of the curve fitting. One such outlier was the datum associated with an age of 485 BCE44, which was removed from the calculations. The final PSVC for Central Asia, also plotted in Fig. 5, was derived from the mean and standard deviation (1σ or 2σ) of the set of 5000 PSVCs. For further details regarding the curve and associated errors, please refer to Table 2 in the supplementary material.

The combination of both PDFs (Eqs. 1 and 2) generate a new PDF that provides a posteriori information, in terms of probability, of the specific archaeomagnetic datum.

The obtained probability maps (one for the PSVC and one for the data in question) are used to create a new time-dependent probability map, spanning the entire temporal range of the a priori dating. This new map is based on the value of the geomagnetic element being studied, which varies across the full range of values for that element. In this work, the probability distribution maps will be referred to as the probability density function (PDF).

In order to illustrate how this approach works, we apply it to the archaeomagnetic data related to the ceramic fragment TRZ517, which is associated to a prior dating range between 500 BCE and 300 BCE and an intensity of 79.9 ± 2.9 µT. Figure 10a contains the PSVC (in yellow) along with a blue rectangle that corresponds to the cited archaeomagnetic datum. This rectangle defines the area on which the post-dating study will focus. Within this rectangle, the PDFs given by Eqs. 1 and 2 are estimated, which are convolved to obtain the final PDF that defines the probability map within the rectangle (Fig. 10b). This map illustrates the likelihood of the intensity data occurring within the specified region, highlighting areas of lower probability in dark red and those of highest probability in white. Finally, to obtain the posterior PDFs for both age and intensity measurements, the cumulative PDF is calculated over the entire ranges. The cumulative PDF in the range of intensities provides the a posteriori distribution for the time (histogram of Fig. 10c), while the cumulative PDF in terms of time shows the a posteriori distribution of the intensity (histogram of Fig. 10d). These two final distributions provide the posterior information about the intensity data used to construct the PSVC itself. From this analysis it has been determined that the most likely age of the last heating is 403 BCE, and the most likely intensity is 78.8 µT.
