Influence of Mask-Wearing Attitudes on Compliance with Removable Orthodontic Devices: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Ziwei Huang, Guangzhao Guan, Caixia Zhang, Ren Yang, Li Mei, Huang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimTo investigate the impact of mask-wearing on orthodontic patient compliance and to identify factors associated with adherence.MethodsA cross-sectional questionnaire-based survey was conducted among 214 orthodontic patients (64.0% female; predominantly aged 19–30 years) who had used removable orthodontic devices. Data on demographics, treatment duration, appliance type, mask-wearing attitudes and wear times were collected. Key variables included mask-wearing attitudes, which were assessed on a 5-point Likert scale (ranging from “Strongly Disagree” to “Strongly Agree”), actual and recommended wear times of appliances, overall compliance (actual/recommended wear time), and daytime compliance proportion (actual daytime/total wear time).ResultsAlthough 52.2% of participants agreed that masks facilitated acceptance of unaesthetic appliances, this perception was associated with lower daytime (ρ = −0.568) and overall compliance (ρ = −0.245) (both p < 0.01). Daytime wear proportion strongly correlated with overall compliance (β = 0.74, p < 0.01). Patients with neutral mask attitudes showed higher daytime compliance than those with polarized views (p < 0.01). Gender was the only independent predictor of mask-facilitated compliance, with males reporting higher acceptance (coefficient = −0.71, p = 0.03).ConclusionMost orthodontic patients believe that wearing a mask helps them accept less attractive appliances, but this belief did not lead to better treatment compliance. Those with neutral attitudes wore appliances more during the day. Daytime wear strongly predicts overall compliance. For clinicians, this means that patients who count on masks to hide their appliances may need extra attention. Rather than focusing only on reducing aesthetic concerns, efforts should also focus on helping patients build consistent daytime wear habits.

Introduction

The success of orthodontic treatment relies not only on precise biomechanical design and the clinician’s expertise but also, to a large extent, on patient compliance.1,2 In orthodontics, compliance refers to the extent to which a patient follows the prescribed treatment regimen. For patients using removable appliances, such as removable plates, functional appliances, headgear, or facemasks, compliance is primarily assessed by wear time relative to the orthodontist’s recommendation. Both self-reported and objectively measured compliance have been investigated.2 For patients with fixed appliances, compliance encompasses oral hygiene maintenance and appointment attendance.3 Improving patients’ compliance is essential because suboptimal adherence can prolong treatment duration, compromise treatment outcomes, and increase the risk of adverse effects such as decalcification and root resorption.4

However, for the patient-worn devices, numerous studies have confirmed that the actual wear time is generally lower than that recommended, a problem particularly pronounced in children and adolescents.1,2,5,6 Amausi et al7 conducted a survey of patients who had received fixed orthodontic treatment and were prescribed removable retainers, finding that among the 58 respondents only 25.9% always wore their retainers as instructed, while 44.8% reported wearing them only sometimes; the main barriers to compliance were forgetfulness (34.5%), discomfort (24.1%), and aesthetic concerns (13.8%). A systematic review conducted by Nahajowski et al2 also indicated that, regardless of the type of removable appliance, the average wear time is shorter than recommended. Therefore, improving patient compliance with removable appliances remains one of the major challenges in orthodontic clinical practice.

The outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic in early 2020 presented unprecedented challenges to global public health and significantly changed people’s daily lifestyles. As wearing masks in public gradually shifted from a mandatory requirement to a habitual behaviour, especially during cold winters, periods of air pollution, or outbreaks of respiratory diseases, the frequency of patients’ facial exposure has changed significantly. Cherecheș et al8,9 conducted a survey of Romanian children and adolescents undergoing orthodontic treatment and found that 31.3% of children and 22.4% of teenagers believed that mandatory mask-wearing affected their willingness to undergo orthodontic treatment. This finding indicates that wearing masks may be a potential factor influencing the wearing of removable devices.

In orthodontics, patient cooperation is significantly influenced by psychological and social factors, mainly manifested in concerns about their self-image, peer pressure, and sensitivity to social judgment.10,11 During the treatment period, patients may conceal the unattractive orthodontic appliances with masks to create a psychological “safe haven” for themselves, thereby reducing social anxiety associated with visible orthodontic devices. Studies have shown that this psychological impact on the use of orthodontic appliances varies to some extent with age and also exhibits individual differences.8,9,12

It is worth noting that the impact of wearing a mask on orthodontic treatment may have a dual effect. On one hand, the mask may indirectly improve patient compliance by reducing social anxiety. On the other hand, wearing the mask itself may cause physical discomfort, thereby negatively affecting the compliance with removable orthodontic appliances. For example, the ear straps of the mask may overlap with the headgear straps or components of extraoral traction devices, thereby increasing the pressure on the skin behind the ears. Rigid masks might compress the facemask appliances, increasing the discomfort during wearing. For patients who already have difficulty maintaining good compliance, the physical burden caused by prolonged wearing of the mask, such as increased breathing resistance, pressure on the nose bridge and face, and temporomandibular joint pain, may exacerbate the discomfort caused by the orthodontic appliance itself, further reducing their willingness to wear the orthodontic appliances.11,13

This study aimed to investigate whether mask-wearing influences patient compliance with removable orthodontic devices. In the present study, the term “removable orthodontic devices” encompasses both fully removable devices (eg., removable functional appliances, headgear, facemasks) and removable components used in conjunction with fixed appliances (eg., inter-arch elastics for anterior teeth and removable bite plane). All of them share the common feature of being patient-removable and subject to the same adherence challenges during daytime wear. Specifically, it sought to examine the relationship between patients’ perceptions that wearing a mask hides the appearance of their appliance and improves their willingness to wear it, as well as to evaluate how this perception relates to appliance type, treatment duration, demographic characteristics, and the relationship between recommended and actual appliance wear time.

Methods

Participants

Ethical approval of this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee (NJSH-2023NL-067). This study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and for participants who were minors (if the participants are below 16 years of age), informed consent was obtained from their parents/legal guardians. All research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines.

A questionnaire was distributed online between January and April 2023 through Survey Star platform (Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Co., Ltd., Changsha, China) to all the orthodontic patients who were undergoing treatment in the Department of Orthodontics. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients aged 7 to 50 years who were medically healthy (classified as ASA I or II); (2) patients who used removable orthodontic devices, including inter-arch elastics, intraoral removable appliances, headgear and maxillary protraction devices; and (3) patients who completed the questionnaire without obvious logic errors or missing data. Both intraoral and extraoral removable devices were included because the study aimed to capture the full spectrum of removable device-related aesthetic concerns that could potentially be mitigated by mask-wearing. All included devices share the common feature of being removable and visible during social interactions. The exclusion criteria were that patients had diseases affecting nasal ventilation (eg. adenoid hypertrophy, palatine tonsil hypertrophy, asthma and allergies), neuropsychiatric disorders, craniofacial syndrome, hyperthyroidism and pregnant women. According to our preliminary experiment conducted among 50 patients, the correlation coefficient is 0.3. The sample size is 69 calculated by the PASS 15 software (Version 15.0.13, NCSS, LLC., Kaysville, Utah, USA). The power is 0.8 and the confidence level is 0.95.

Questionnaire

A pilot questionnaire was firstly developed and administered to 50 random orthodontic patients to ensure the questions were appropriate and unambiguous, after which several refinements were made based on their feedback to produce the final questionnaire: (1) the wording of wear-time questions was clarified to explicitly distinguish daytime use (defined as waking hours) from nighttime use (defined as sleeping hours); (2) illustrative images of specific unaesthetic appliances were added to the response options to improve comprehension; and (3) the response options for mask-wearing attitude were standardized to a 5-point Likert scale (ranging from “Strongly Disagree” to “Strongly Agree”). The final questionnaire was reviewed by two experienced orthodontists to ensure content validity. The final questionnaire consisted of three sections.

Section 1: Participant demographic information including gender, age, education background, and occupation was collected.

Section 2: Participants’ orthodontic treatment duration and the type of appliance used were investigated, with a particular focus on unaesthetic appliances, including inter-arch anterior elastics, intraoral removable appliances, headgear or facemask. In addition, the extent to which their occupation required regular social interaction was assessed.

Section 3: Data were collected on both the actual and prescribed durations of daytime and nighttime wear of unaesthetic appliances. Participants were asked whether they perceived that mask-wearing facilitated their acceptance of unaesthetic appliances, and whether the use of masks inclined them to choose less aesthetic but more affordable alternatives.

Definitions of Mask-Wearing Attitude

Mask-wearing attitude was assessed using a single question: “Do you agree that wearing a mask facilitates your acceptance of unaesthetic orthodontic appliances?” Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale and subsequently categorized into five groups for analysis: Strongly Agree, Agree, Neutral, Disagree and Strongly Disagree.

Definition of Compliance

In this study, compliance refers to the patient’s self-reported adherence to the prescribed wear of removable orthodontic components. For patients with fixed appliances, compliance was assessed specifically with respect to the prescribed inter-arch elastics or bite plane, which are removable and require active patient cooperation. For patients without fixed brackets, compliance referred to the wear of removable functional appliances, headgear, or maxillary protraction devices. All participants were instructed by their treating orthodontist to wear their prescribed removable components for a specified number of hours per day. The recommended daily wear time varied by appliance type and treatment phase and was recorded individually for each patient through the questionnaire. Overall compliance was defined as the ratio of the patient’s actual total wear time to the prescribed total wear time. Daytime compliance proportion was defined as the ratio of the patient’s actual daytime wear time to their actual total wear time. Effective daytime compliance was calculated by multiplying overall compliance by daytime compliance proportion.

Statistical Analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used to analyze the data. As the data were not normally distributed, Spearman’s rank correlation was used to analyze the relationships between patients’ demographic characteristics, orthodontic treatment duration, appliance type, attitudes toward mask-wearing, and overall compliance as well as the daytime wear proportion. Logistic regression was used to analyze which types of patients are more likely to agree that mask-wearing enhances compliance with removable appliances. Linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate whether daytime wear contributed to overall compliance. Differences in overall compliance, daytime compliance proportion, and effective daytime compliance according to mask-wearing status were assessed using Kruskal–Wallis ANOVA, followed by Dunn’s post hoc tests with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons. A p-value <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Of the 227 questionnaires returned, 13 were excluded for the following reasons: (1) incomplete questionnaires (n=6), (2) obvious logical inconsistencies, such as reported total daily wear time exceeding 24 hours (n=3), daytime wear time greater than total wear time (n=2), or contradictory answers regarding appliance type and wear schedule (n=2). After removing these ineligible questionnaires, a total of 214 valid questionnaires were included in the statistical analysis and their demographic characteristics are detailed in Table 1. The sample consisted of 137 females (64.0%) and 77 males (36.0%). Regarding age distribution, the majority of participants were concentrated in the 19–30 age group (n=123, 57.5%), followed by those aged 12–18 (n=45, 21.0%) and 31–40 (n=29, 13.6%). Participants under 12 years (n=11, 5.1%) or above 41 years (n=6, 2.8%) accounted for relatively low proportions. In terms of education level, more than half of the participants held a university education (n=114, 53.3%), followed by postgraduate education (n=41, 19.2%), intermediate school (n=24, 11.2%), primary school (n=20, 9.3%), and high school (n=15, 7.0%). Regarding occupational distribution, students constituted the largest group (n=112, 52.3%), followed by teachers/doctors/lawyers (n=23, 10.7%), marketing personnel/service staff/front desk staff (n=20, 9.4%), and other occupations (n=59, 27.6%). The participants’ perceptions concerning mask-wearing and orthodontic appliance selection, along with the types of unaesthetic appliances used and their level of social engagement, are presented in Figure 2. Most participants agreed that mask-wearing facilitated their acceptance of unaesthetic appliances, with 32.4% indicating strong agreement and 19.8% expressing agreement. In contrast, only 7.2% disagreed and 9.0% strongly disagreed, while 31.5% remained neutral. Regarding the types of unaesthetic appliances reported, inter-arch elastics for anterior teeth were the most common (81.1%), followed by removable intraoral appliances (10.8%) and orthopedic facemasks or headgear (8.1%). When asked whether mask-wearing increased their inclination to choose cheaper but less aesthetic appliances, responses showed a distinct distribution in which 72.1% disagreed, 23.4% agreed, and 4.5% were neutral. Conversely, most participants (72.1%) did not associate mask-wearing with a greater tendency to choose cheaper appliances. Overall compliance and daytime compliance were expressed as ratios. Spearman correlation analyses (Figure 3) demonstrated that mask wearing was weakly but significantly positively associated with age (ρ = 0.167, p < 0.01) and engagement in regular social activities (ρ = 0.163, p < 0.01), and weakly negatively associated with duration of orthodontic treatment (ρ = −0.181, p < 0.01). No significant associations were observed between mask wearing and gender, education, occupation, or orthodontic appliance type. Notable demographic relationships included a strong positive correlation between age and education (ρ = 0.666, p < 0.01) and a positive association between treatment duration and appliance type (ρ = 0.257, p < 0.01). Further analysis revealed that mask wearing was strongly positively associated with prolonged use of unaesthetic orthodontic appliances (ρ = 0.754, p < 0.01). The use of unaesthetic appliances was also positively correlated with mask wearing (ρ = 0.488, p < 0.01) and with longer appliance use attributed to mask wearing (ρ = 0.458, p < 0.01). In contrast, overall compliance and daytime compliance proportion were negatively associated with both mask wearing (ρ = −0.245 and −0.568, respectively; p < 0.01) and the use of unaesthetic appliances, suggesting reduced compliance among participants who wore masks or opted for less aesthetic appliances. The statistically significant correlations presented in Figure 3 are summarized in Supplementary Table S1 for ease of reference. Perceived use of cheap or unaesthetic appliances due to masks was not significantly correlated with other variables. The ordinal logistic regression model demonstrated good overall fit, with the predictors collectively contributing significantly to explaining variation in mask-related use of orthodontic appliances. However, gender (Coefficients: −0.71; SE:0.33, Wald Test: 4.53; p = 0.03) was the only variable showing an independent statistically significant association, with one gender group being less likely to report higher acceptance of unaesthetic appliances facilitated by mask wearing. Other demographic, social, and clinical variables did not retain independent effects after adjustment. These findings suggest that while mask wearing broadly facilitates acceptance of less aesthetic orthodontic appliances, individual-level predictors are limited, with gender emerging as the most influential factor in the multivariable context.

Linear regression analysis (Figure 4 and Table 2) demonstrated a significant positive association between the proportion of daytime orthodontic appliance wearing and overall compliance (β = 0.74, SE = 0.11, t = 6.63, p < 0.01). The model explained approximately 17% of the variance in overall compliance (adjusted R2 = 0.17). Participants’ mask-wearing attitudes were categorized into four groups, namely Neutral, Agree, Disagree, and Strongly, with the Strongly group comprising participants who answered either Strongly Agree or Strongly Disagree. The distribution of these groups is presented in Figure 2. The descriptive statistics and results of the Kruskal–Wallis test for the three compliance outcomes across the four mask-wearing attitude groups are presented in Table 3. Dunn’s post hoc pairwise comparisons between groups are shown in Table 4. Kruskal–Wallis ANOVA revealed significant differences in compliance outcomes according to mask-wearing attitudes across all three measures assessed. For effective daytime compliance, a strong overall group effect was observed (χ2 (3) = 75.79, p < 0.01). Participants reporting a neutral mask-wearing status demonstrated the highest compliance (median = 1.00, mean rank = 137.56), which was significantly greater than those who strongly agreed, strongly disagreed, agreed or disagreed with mask wearing (all Dunn’s post hoc p < 0.01). No significant differences were detected among the latter three groups (p = 1.00). Similarly, daytime compliance proportion differed significantly between groups (χ2(3) = 74.94, p < 0.01). The neutral group again exhibited the highest compliance (median = 1.00, mean rank = 137.21), with post hoc analyses confirming significantly higher values compared with all other groups (p < 0.01). No pairwise differences were identified among the strongly agree, strongly disagree, agree, and disagree groups. For overall compliance, a smaller but statistically significant group effect was identified (χ2(3) = 13.24, p < 0.01). Although the neutral group showed the highest mean rank (116.23), post hoc Dunn’s tests did not reveal any statistically significant pairwise differences after adjustment (p > 0.23) (Figure 5). Overall, mask-wearing status was significantly associated with multiple compliance metrics, with individuals reporting a neutral stance toward mask wearing consistently demonstrating higher daytime compliance. To evaluate whether the broad inclusion criteria influenced the main findings, two sensitivity analyses were performed by stratifying participants according to appliance type (intraoral vs. extraoral removable appliances) and age group (minors aged ≤18 years vs. adults aged >18 years), respectively (Supplementary Table S2). The direction of all key correlations remained fully consistent with the overall sample findings across all subgroups, with no statistically significant reversals observed. For the negative correlation between mask-wearing attitude and daytime compliance proportion, statistical significance was retained in the larger intraoral appliance subgroup (ρ = −0.14, P < 0.05) and adult subgroup (ρ = −0.17, P < 0.05). The extraoral appliance subgroup (ρ = −0.45, P = 0.22) and minor subgroup (ρ = −0.25, P = 0.06, marginally significant) showed the same negative trend but did not reach conventional statistical significance, likely due to their smaller sample sizes. For the positive correlation between daytime wear proportion and overall compliance, statistical significance was retained in three subgroups including intraoral appliances (ρ = 0.40, P < 0.01), minors (ρ = 0.41, P < 0.01), and adults (ρ = 0.43, P < 0.01). Only the extraoral appliance subgroup (ρ = 0.32, P = 0.39) failed to reach significance due to extremely limited statistical power. The weaker negative correlation between mask-wearing attitude and overall compliance did not reach statistical significance in any subgroup (all P > 0.39). Formal statistical comparison between the subgroups was precluded by the insufficient number of extraoral appliance users and minors. Collectively, these findings confirm that the broad inclusion criteria did not materially bias the main results of this study.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, three principal findings were identified. First, although orthodontic patients generally believe that wearing a mask helps them better accept the unattractive orthodontic appliances, this perception was paradoxically associated with lower daytime and overall treatment compliance. Second, the daytime proportion of wearing orthodontic appliances was identified as a significant influencing factor for overall compliance, highlighting the importance of emphasizing the use of removable appliances during the day for achieving the overall wearing time target. Third, patients who held a neutral attitude toward mask-wearing demonstrated significantly higher daytime compliance compared with those holding polarized views. However, no such group differences were observed for overall compliance, suggesting that the cognitive impact related to masks is more significant on daytime-specific adherence than on overall compliance.

In this study, patients who endorsed the usefulness of mask-wearing in accepting unaesthetic appliances exhibited lower treatment adherence. While some studies have highlighted that masks significantly increased patients’ willingness to undergo orthodontic treatment by concealing aesthetic flaws,14 our results indicate that this initial acceptance does not translate into sustained behavioral adherence. This discrepancy may be explained by motivation externalization.15 Mask-wearing primarily alleviates social and aesthetic concerns, yet this psychological relief does not guarantee sustained adherence. Patients with poor compliance may emphasize this external facilitator to rationalize their insufficient wear time. In contrast, highly adherent patients, driven by stronger intrinsic motivation, maintain consistent wear regardless of mask use. Individuals with weaker intrinsic motivation are more susceptible to external facilitators and, therefore, more likely to endorse their benefits. This aligns with Self-Determination Theory (SDT) in health psychology, which posits that behaviors driven by external contingencies are inherently less stable than those rooted in autonomous, internal values.16,17 Consistent with this interpretation, patients with a neutral attitude toward masks demonstrated the highest daytime compliance, a finding that warrants careful interpretation. We interpret this neutrality as a manifestation of robust intrinsic motivation, wherein treatment persistence is guided by internal goals, such as commitment to therapeutic outcomes, rather than by external conditions like mask availability. This internal drive enables the formation of stable, mask-independent daytime wear habits. In contrast, polarized views including strongly agree or strongly disagree of mask attitude may indicate heightened sensitivity to external judgment or physical discomfort, which can undermine self-regulated behavior. For those who strongly endorsed mask utility, masks may act as a psychological crutch that temporarily relieves appearance-related anxiety but fails to cultivate autonomous adherence. The absence of significant group differences in overall compliance may be explained by a nighttime compensation effect. Patients who strongly agreed with the benefits of mask-wearing may attempt to compensate for insufficient daytime wear by extending nighttime use, thereby attenuating between-group disparities in total wear time. These findings carry direct clinical implications. Patients who strongly endorse the role of mask-wearing in facilitating appliance use warrant closer attention, as they may exhibit excessive reliance on external conditions and limited self-management capacity.

The present study demonstrated a strong positive correlation between the proportion of daytime orthodontic appliance wear and overall compliance with prescribed wear time (β = 0.74). This finding suggests that achieving total wear time targets may largely depend on patients’ active engagement in daytime wear. Our observation corroborates recent studies utilizing objective microsensors (eg., TheraMon), which frequently report a “ceiling effect” in nighttime wear but substantial variability and deficits during daytime hours.6,18 Unlike nighttime use, daytime wear requires patients to navigate aesthetic and discomfort-related concerns in social contexts and to integrate appliance use into their daily routines, reflecting a higher level of treatment commitment and self-regulatory capacity.2 In contrast, a wearing pattern that relies heavily on nighttime “make-up” wear is more susceptible to external disruptions and is often associated with suboptimal overall compliance. These findings have several important clinical implications. First, clinicians should exercise caution when encountering patients who strongly endorse the usefulness of mask-wearing in facilitating treatment acceptance, as such endorsement may indicate an overreliance on external conditions rather than adequate self-management capacity. Second, the strong positive correlation between daytime wear proportion and overall compliance suggests that interventions should prioritize the cultivation of consistent daytime wear habits over merely alleviating aesthetic concerns. Practical strategies may include setting specific daytime wear targets, utilizing smartphone reminders,3 and implementing self-monitoring checklists.19 Third, given that patients with neutral attitudes toward masks exhibit the most favorable adherence behaviors, patient education should focus on fostering mask-independent, intrinsically motivated wearing habits, rather than reinforcing the external attribution that masks conceal aesthetic concerns.

Although the period of universal mask-wearing has subsided, the findings of this study still carry relevance beyond the specific context of the COVID-19 pandemic. In many regions, mask-wearing remains a common practice during influenza seasons, periods of high air pollution, or outbreaks of respiratory illnesses. Moreover, the underlying psychological mechanism revealed by this study, that reliance on external concealment strategies may paradoxically undermine adherence, has broader implications for understanding patient behavior. Patients may substitute other forms of facial concealment, such as high-collared clothing or extended periods at home, or rely on other external justifications to rationalize suboptimal wear. The core clinical message, that fostering intrinsic motivation and consistent daytime habits is more effective than relying on external aids, transcends the specific context of mask-wearing and remains relevant to contemporary orthodontic practice.

Several potential confounding factors should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, compliance behavior is known to differ between minors and adults, reflecting differences in parental supervision and self-motivation.20,21 A sensitivity analysis stratifying participants by age (≤18 years vs. >18 years) showed that the directions of the core correlations were consistent across subgroups. However, the smaller minor subgroup (n = 56) did not reach statistical significance for certain associations, likely owing to reduced statistical power, which precludes a definitive conclusion regarding age-related differences. Second, intraoral and extraoral removable appliances differ in visibility and physical interaction with face masks, which may differentially affect compliance.2 Although a sensitivity analysis stratified by appliance type showed consistent directional trends, the extraoral subgroup was too small (n = 17) to permit formal statistical comparison. Third, we acknowledge that treatment stage can affect how well patients follow wear instructions. A well-known “honeymoon effect” means patients tend to wear appliances more faithfully early in treatment, but adherence often drops over time.5 In this study, the self-reported wear times reflect each patient’s behavior at their current treatment stage. However, because the study focused on the link between mask-wearing attitudes and compliance and used a cross-sectional design, it could not test whether this link changes as treatment progresses. This is an important limitation that future longitudinal studies should examine. Fourth, intrinsic motivation and external supervision can also influence patients’ compliance.20 Future studies may benefit from incorporating validated instruments to assess these psychological and social variables.

In addition to the potential confounding factors discussed above, several other limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationship between mask-wearing attitudes and orthodontic compliance; longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal dynamics. Moreover, the sample was predominantly composed of young, highly educated females and students recruited from a single institution via online distribution, which limits generalizability. Furthermore, all compliance outcomes were based on self-reported wear time and are therefore susceptible to recall bias and social desirability bias. Prior studies comparing self-reports with objective tracking have consistently shown that patients overestimate their adherence by several hours per day.18 Future investigations should incorporate objective monitors such as embedded microsensors. Collectively, future longitudinal studies with objective adherence monitoring and more homogeneous patient samples are warranted to confirm and extend the present findings.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates that although the vast majority of the participants acknowledged the usefulness of mask-wearing in facilitating acceptance of unaesthetic appliances, this perception was associated with lower daytime and overall treatment compliance. Daytime wear proportion was identified as a significant correlate of overall compliance. Notably, patients who held a neutral attitude toward mask-wearing exhibited higher daytime compliance than those who either strongly agreed or disagreed with its benefits. Clinicians should be aware that patients who rely heavily on mask-wearing to mitigate aesthetic concerns may have compromised adherence. Behavioral interventions may benefit from emphasizing the establishment of consistent daytime wear habits rather than focusing solely on alleviating aesthetic concerns. These findings should be interpreted with caution given that compliance was self-reported in a predominantly young, female sample and further validation in broader populations with objective adherence monitoring is warranted.
