Learning spatially adaptive sparsity level maps for arbitrary convolutional dictionaries
Joshua Schulz, David Schote, Christoph Kolbitsch, Kostas Papafitsoros, Andreas Kofler

Abstract
State-of-the-art learned reconstruction methods often rely on black-box modules that, despite their strong performance, raise questions about their interpretability and robustness. Here, we build on a recently proposed image reconstruction method, which is based on embedding data-driven information into a model-based convolutional dictionary regularization via neural network-inferred spatially adaptive sparsity level maps. By means of improved network design and dedicated training strategies, we extend the method to achieve filter-permutation invariance as well as the possibility to change the convolutional dictionary at inference time. We apply our method to low-field MRI and compare it to several other recent deep learning-based methods, also on in vivo data, in which the benefit for the use of a different dictionary is showcased. We further assess the method’s robustness when tested on in- and out-of-distribution data. When tested on the latter, the proposed method suffers less from the data distribution shift compared to the other learned methods, which we attribute to its reduced reliance on training data due to its underlying model-based reconstruction component.

1
Introduction

Learned reconstruction methods using neural networks nowadays, without a doubt, define the state-of-the-art in image reconstruction
[
2
]
. Thereby, an important issue is their present black-box character. Often, one must seek a trade-off between empirical/numerical performance and interpretability/transparency, for example, in terms of the convergence guarantees of the derived reconstruction schemes
[
15
]
. Additionally, learned reconstruction methods are reported to quite consistently suffer from data-distribution shifts
[
6
]
.
To enhance interpretability, it is possible to carefully employ learnable blocks such that the resulting reconstruction can be linked to a model-based variational problem, see e.g.
[
12
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
10
]
and references therein.

Dictionary-learning-based methods constitute another family of data-driven reconstruction approaches
[
8
]
that are highly interpretable, yet so far few studies have connected them to deep learning-based methods to improve their performance
[
17
]
.
In
[
10
]
, an image reconstruction method that leverages spatially adaptive sparsity of the image with respect to a pre-trained convolutional dictionary was introduced. The overall approach consists of an unrolled scheme that can be trained end-to-end, i.e. from the raw data measurements to the image estimate, where the sparsity level map is estimated by a convolutional neural network (CNN)
\mathrm{NET}_{\Theta}
.
The main limitations in
[
10
]
lie in the architectural design of the
\mathrm{NET}_{\Theta}
. The CNN is dictionary-agnostic, meaning that at inference time, it is not possible to use any other dictionary other than the one used for training. A change of the dictionary e.g. in terms of numbers of filters
K
- or even only in terms of their order - results in a loss of reconstruction performance.

Here, we introduce a flexible framework for learning spatially adaptive sparsity level maps that allows for the employment of arbitrary convolutional dictionaries during inference. We do so via a new CNN design customized to this task, training over different numbers of dictionaries and employing efficient dedicated training strategies. More specifically:

(i)

We condition the CNN on the employed convolutional dictionary and adapt the architecture to achieve dictionary filter permutation invariance and to allow the use of dictionaries with a different number of filters.

(ii)

The convolutional dictionaries are varied during training, so that the network learns to estimate appropriate sparsity level maps for different dictionaries of arbitrary sizes.

(iii)

Truncated backpropagation is used during training, which is necessary since the considered reconstruction problem can be very large due to the potential use of a larger number of dictionary filters and larger CNN blocks.

(iv)

We compare against other well-established methods using retrospectively simulated data, including out-of-distribution, showing that our approach is less affected by the data distribution shift. Further, experiments on in vivo low-field MR data show that our approach, which benefits from using a different dictionary at inference,
yields reconstructions that are on par with the other methods, while at the same time being interpretable.

2
Methods

We briefly revise the method in
[
10
]
to present the required concepts. Consider the typical inverse imaging problem

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{true}}+\mathbf{e},

(1)

where
\mathbf{y}
denotes the raw measurement data obtained from the (unknown) image
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{true}}
through the forward model
\mathbf{A}
, and
\mathbf{e}
denotes additive Gaussian noise. Since we focus on MR image reconstruction, here
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{true}}
is complex-valued.

2.1
Reconstruction with Spatially Adaptive Sparsity Level Maps

Reconstructing an estimate of
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{true}}
by solving (
1
) typically requires the use of regularization methods.
A successful regularization mechanism can be designed based on spatially adaptive sparsity with respect to a pre-trained convolutional dictionary
\mathbf{D}

[
8
]
. Here, one assumes that an image can be represented as a linear combination of sparse feature maps
\{\mathbf{s}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
that are convolved with unit-norm convolutional filters
\{d_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
.
For conciseness, we use the following notation:

\mathbf{s}:=[\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{K}]^{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}},\quad\mathbf{D}\mathbf{s}:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}d_{k}\ast\mathbf{s}_{k},\quad\mathbf{B}:=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}.

(2)

As in
[
21
]
, we also aim only to approximate the high-pass-filtered component of the image. Given
\mathbf{x}_{0}:=\mathbf{A}^{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}}\mathbf{y}
, high-pass filtering of
\mathbf{x}_{0}
is achieved by
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{high}}:=\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{low}}
, where

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{low}}

\displaystyle:=\underset{\mathbf{x}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\beta}{2}\|\nabla\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}

(PH)

with
\beta>0
. We strictly enforce the equality
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{high}}=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{s}
with sparse
\mathbf{s}
and consider the reconstruction problem

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\ast}:=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{s}^{\ast}+\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{low}},

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}^{\ast}:=\underset{\mathbf{s}}{\arg\min}\,\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{B}\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\,\mathbf{s}\|_{1},\quad

(PR)

where
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}:=\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{low}}
and the sparsity level maps
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}:=[\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{K}]^{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}
are parametrized as outputs of a deep CNN
\mathrm{NET}_{\Theta}
with parameters
\Theta
applied to some input. The method
[
10
]
(named CDL-
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
; Convolutional Dictionary Learning with
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
-maps), performs the learned high-pass filtering by solving problem (
PH
), the estimation of the sparsity level maps
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, and the final reconstruction of the image by solving (
PR
) by means of algorithm unrolling
[
14
]
of an accelerated proximal gradient descent (FISTA)
[
3
]
with convergence guarantees
[
4
]
. We denote the entire reconstruction network by
\mathcal{N}_{\Theta}^{T}
(see Figure
1
) and the FISTA-scheme to approximately solve (
PR
) with
T\in\mathbb{N}
iterations
\mathbf{s}^{T}:=\mathrm{FISTA}^{T}(\mathbf{s}_{0},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{D},\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
with starting value
\mathbf{s}_{0}=\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{NK}
.

Figure 1:
The reconstruction pipeline of the CDL-
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
method and the three different versions of
\mathrm{NET}_{\Theta}
(
V1
–
V3
)
(orange blocks)
investigated in this study. First, a learned high-pass filtering step solving (

) is performed
(green block)
. Then,
\mathrm{NET}_{\Theta}

(blue block)
. Last, a FISTA algorithm is unrolled to obtain an approximate solution of (

)
(ocher block)
. An estimate of the solution is obtained by adding the low-frequency component that was extracted in the first block.
In contrast to V1 and V2, the proposed improved V3 is permutation invariant and allows for the use of dictionaries with a different number of filters
K
.

2.2
Dictionary-Agnostic vs Dictionary-Aware Learning of the Sparsity Level Maps

We investigate three different possibilities to construct the network
\mathrm{NET}_{\Theta}
for estimating spatially adaptive sparsity level maps
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, see Figure
1
(bottom) for an overview. Complex-valued images
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
are treated as real-valued two-channel images, and we use real-valued dictionary filters, i.e.,

\mathbf{D}\mathbf{s}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}d_{k}\ast\mathbf{s}_{k}:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big(d_{k}\ast\mathrm{Re}(\mathbf{s}_{k})+\mathrm{i}\,d_{k}\ast\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{s}_{k})\big),

implying that for any
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{NK}
, and
\mathbf{z}=[\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2}]^{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}

\mathbf{D}\mathbf{s}=[\mathbf{D},\mathbf{D}][\mathrm{Re}(\mathbf{s}),\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{s})]^{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}},\mathbf{D}^{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}\mathbf{z}:=[\mathbf{D}^{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{D}^{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}\mathbf{z}_{2}]^{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}.

The weighted
\ell_{1}
-norm in (
PR
) is then defined by
\|\mathbf{s}\|_{1}:=\big\|[\mathrm{Re}(\mathbf{s}),\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{s})]^{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}\big\|_{1}.
In
[
10
]
,
\mathrm{NET}_{\Theta}
was constructed as a U-Net that estimates
K
sparsity level maps
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}:=[\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{K}]
(shared between real and imaginary parts of
\mathbf{s}
) from the input image, i.e., upon vectorization of 2D images to vectors, it holds
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\mathrm{NET}_{\Theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
with

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{k}:=\big(\mathrm{NET}_{\Theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\big)_{k}:=t\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}\,\circ\left(\mathbf{u}_{\Theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\right)_{k},

(V1)

with global learnable scalar parameter
t>0
, Softplus activation function
\boldsymbol{\phi}
and a 2-to-
K
2D U-Net
\mathbf{u}_{\Theta}
.
While proven effective, it is evident from (
V1
) that the network is agnostic with respect to the dictionary
\mathbf{D}
as well as only applicable to dictionaries with a fixed number of filters
K
. We refer to the CNN architecture in (
V1
) as ”
\mathrm{NET}_{\mathrm{\Theta}}
V1”.

A straight forward solution to condition the network
\mathrm{NET}_{\Theta}
on the dictionary
\mathbf{D}
is to provide a dictionary-dependent input and use a
K
-to-
K
2D U-Net
\mathbf{u}_{\Theta}
to obtain

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}:=\mathrm{NET}_{\Theta}(\mathbf{D}^{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}\mathbf{x}_{0}):=t\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}\,\circ\mathbf{u}_{\Theta}(\mathbf{D}^{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}\mathbf{x}_{0}),

(V2)

which we refer to as ”
\mathrm{NET}_{\mathrm{\Theta}}
V2”. Although using a dictionary-dependent input,
\mathrm{NET}_{\mathrm{\Theta}}
V2 is nevertheless inherently linked to the number of filters
K
. Thus, we consider the third variant ”
\mathrm{NET}_{\mathrm{\Theta}}
V3”, which, by slight abuse of notation, reads as

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}:=\mathrm{NET}_{\Theta}(\mathbf{D}^{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}\mathbf{x}_{0}):=\mathbf{R}^{-1}t\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}\,\circ\mathbf{u}_{\Theta}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{D}^{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}\mathbf{x}_{0}),

(V3)

where
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{R}^{-1}
are operators that reshape the input tensors by moving the channel’s dimension to the batch-dimension, i.e., they transform inputs with shapes
(\mathrm{batch},2\cdot K,N_{y},N_{x})
to
(\mathrm{batch}\cdot K,2,N_{y},N_{x})
and from
(\mathrm{batch}\cdot K,1,N_{y},N_{x})
to
(\mathrm{batch},K,N_{y},N_{x})
, respectively. Here,
\mathbf{u}_{\Theta}
denotes a 2-to-1 2D U-Net. In contrast to V1 and V2, the
same
2D U-Net is employed to estimate the corresponding sparsity level map
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{N}
from the input
(\mathbf{D}^{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}\mathbf{x}_{0})_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}
. By doing so,
\mathrm{NET}_{\Theta}
can be employed with dictionaries with an arbitrary number of filters
K
at inference.

2.3
Network Training

Instead of a single, fixed dictionary,
here, we employ an entire set of convolutional dictionaries
\mathbf{D}
with different numbers of filters
K
and kernel sizes
k_{f}\times k_{f}
. Given training data, we minimize a mean squared error (MSE)-based loss function

\mathcal{L}(\Theta):=\sum_{\mathbf{y},\,\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{true}},\,\mathbf{D}}\mathrm{MSE}\big(\mathcal{N}_{\Theta}^{T}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{D})\,,\,\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{true}}\big),

(4)

which means that the network
\mathrm{NET}_{\Theta}
is exposed not only to various raw data-target pairs, but also to different reconstruction problems of the form (
PR
).

Being able to employ larger dictionaries in terms of larger
K
and kernel size
k_{f}\times k_{f}
with richer structure implies increased memory consumption when unrolling the FISTA-block.
Therefore, employing truncated back-propagation
[
18
]
is an attractive choice for unrolling a relatively large number of iterations with many large intermediate quantities. More precisely, during training, we first compute
\mathbf{s}^{T^{\prime}}=\mathrm{FISTA}^{T^{\prime}}(\mathbf{s}_{0},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{D},\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
with the FISTA-block with the current estimate of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}

without
tracking gradients. Then, we perform additional
T-T^{\prime}
unrolled iterations (starting the FISTA iteration from
\mathbf{s}^{T^{\prime}}
) by also tracking the gradients of
\Theta
to train by minimizing (
4
). We used
T=64
and
T^{\prime}=36
.

2.4
Datasets and Evaluation Metrics

Here, we conduct experiments on a low-field (LF) MR reconstruction problem.
LF MRI suffers from high noise and low image resolution due to hardware constraints. We therefore model the forward operator
\mathbf{A}
in (
1
) by
\mathbf{A}:=\mathbf{S}_{I}\mathbf{F}
, where
\mathbf{F}
denotes a 2D Fourier transform and
\mathbf{S}_{I}
a binary mask that masks out the outer region of the measurements
\mathbf{y}
, modeling the low-resolution acquisition.
We used brain MR (4875/1393/696 images for training/validation/testing) and 3951 knee MR images from the fastMRI dataset
[
23
]
to retrospectively simulate data according to (
1
). The variance of the noise component
\mathbf{e}
in (
1
) is set relatively high by
\sigma^{2}\in\{0.2,0.3\}
. Further, we show an application of the methods to an in vivo T2-weighted brain image acquired with the Open Source Imaging Initiative (OSI
2
) LF MR scanner
[
20
]
.
For evaluation, we report the MSE, the structural similarity index measure (SSIM), as well as the reference-free blur image metric
[
5
]
, which is especially informative for the in vivo data, where no target image exists.
To focus on the diagnostic content, all metrics are computed by discarding the background of the images, employing masks derived from the target images. The MSE and SSIM were calculated using the
MRpro
library
[
24
]
, which allows for the definition of such masks, while the blur metric implementation is from
Scikit-learn

[
11
]
.
For MoDL, E2E VarNet, SRDenseNet, and CDL-
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, we used a learning rate of
10^{-4}
for the employed CNN-blocks, and
10^{-2}
for the learned step sizes/scalars. CDL-
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
was trained for 48 epochs using a batch size of 1, the others for 128 epochs using a batch size of 4. Code and further training details are available at

here
.

2.5
Pre-Training the Dictionaries

We pre-trained different convolutional dictionaries with different numbers of filters
K
of kernel sizes
k_{f}\times k_{f}
. For doing that, we employed the sparse coding library SPORCO
[
22
]
to solve
a convolutional dictionary learning problem using 360 brain MR images
using the online dictionary learning method described in
[
13
]
for different choices of
\beta=0.1,0.25,0.5
in (
PH
), scalar sparsity level parameters
\lambda=0.1,0.5,2,4
, kernel sizes
k_{f}=9,11
and number of filters
K=16,32,64,128
, resulting in an overall number of 96 different dictionaries.
Note that we do not use the
K=128
-dictionaries when minimizing (
4
) due to GPU-memory constraints.

2.6
Methods of Comparison

We compare against the End-to-End Variational Network (E2E VarNet)
[
19
]
, the model-based deep learning (MoDL)
[
1
]
, and a low-field MRI super-resolution (SR) approach, SRDenseNet
[
7
]
. The first two methods both employ the measured data
\mathbf{y}
, while the latter maps the IFFT reconstruction obtained from the
160\times 160

k
-space data to a
320\times 320
-sized image.
Our implementations of MoDL, E2E VarNet and SRDenseNet contain
113\,413
,
34\,512\,846
and
5\,654\,834
trainable parameters. MoDL is unrolled for
T=10
iterations and alternates between the application of the CNN-block and a data-consistency (DC) step involving a learnable scalar parameter
\lambda>0
that is shared among the iterations. E2E VarNet is unrolled with
T=12
iterations and contains iteration-dependent learned step-sizes.
MoDL and E2E VarNet were accordingly adapted to the here considered single-coil case, i.e., the DC step in MoDL has a closed-form solution instead of requiring a conjugate gradient method, and E2E VarNet does not employ a coil-sensitivity map-refinement module.
CDL-
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
contains
8\,266\,914
and
2\,104\,802
trainable parameters for V1 and V2, respectively, while for V3, we use a more shallow U-Net with only
112\,067
trainable parameters (dictionary filters not included).

4
Conclusion

Together with the improved CNN-block presented here, CDL-
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
yields an interpretable sparsity-based method with convergent guarantees which is less prone to suffering from data distribution shifts compared to MoDL, E2E VarNet and SRDenseNet. This can be explained by the reduced reliance on training data due to its underlying model-based reconstruction component. Indeed, the CNN-block
\mathrm{NET}_{\Theta}
in CDL-
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, solely estimates the
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
-maps, and the regularizational effect comes from the sparsity imposed in (
PR
), whereas in the other approaches, deep CNNs are directly responsible for the reduction of noise and artefacts.

Furthermore, with the improved CNN-block
\mathrm{NET}_{\Theta}
V3, CDL-
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
can now be equipped with a general sparsity level map estimator for arbitrary convolutional dictionaries. We showed that thanks to the use of a larger dictionary at inference, competitive performance can be achieved for in vivo data as well. Our work paves the way to be able to utilize the estimated sparsity level maps to further adapt the dictionary filters as well as to develop rejection and/or replacement strategies for less useful dictionary filters for the image representation, possibly in a zero-shot self-supervised fashion.
