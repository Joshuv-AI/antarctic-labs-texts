Unraveling the heart-brain axis: shared genetic mechanisms in cardiovascular diseases and Schizophrenia
Authors: Jing Shen, Chuang Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe comorbidity between cardiovascular diseases (CVD) and schizophrenia (SCZ) has attracted widespread attention from researchers, with shared genetic causes potentially providing important insights into their association. This study conducted a comprehensive analysis of genetic data from 17 types of CVD and SCZ using genome-wide multi-trait association studies (GWAS), employing statistical methods such as LDSC, MTAG, LAVA, and bidirectional Mendelian randomization to explore global and local genetic correlations and identify pleiotropic single nucleotide variants (SNVs). The analysis revealed a significant genetic correlation between CVD and SCZ, identifying 842 potential pleiotropic single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and multiple associated biological pathways. Notably, genes such as TRIM27, CENPM, and MYH7B played critical roles in the shared genetic variations of both types of diseases. This study reveals the complex genetic relationship between CVD and SCZ, highlighting potential shared biological mechanisms involving immune responses, metabolic factors, and neurodevelopmental processes, thereby providing new directions for future interventions and treatments.Subject terms: Genetics of the nervous system, Psychiatric disorders

Introduction

The comorbidity between cardiovascular diseases (CVD) and mental disorders has been widely reported, drawing significant attention from researchers1,2. Increasing evidence suggests that the existence of the heart-brain axis may be an important mechanism for understanding the interconnection between these two types of diseases3,4. The heart-brain axis refers to the complex interactions between the heart and the brain, involving multiple aspects of physiological, neural, and immune interactions. Existing studies indicate that patients with CVD often experience comorbid conditions such as anxiety and depression, and the occurrence of these mental disorders may exacerbate the progression and prognosis of CVD5–7. This bidirectional relationship suggests a crucial role for the heart-brain axis in regulating both mental health and cardiac health.

Within this conceptual framework, shared genetic causes are considered a potential basis for the association between CVD and mental disorders1,8,9. Through genome-wide association studies (GWAS), researchers have identified shared genetic variants associated with CVD and mental disorders, which may play a role in the development of these two diseases by affecting inflammation, metabolism, and neurobiological pathways10–12. This finding provides new perspectives for exploring the potential mechanisms of the heart-brain axis and may guide future intervention strategies. Additionally, the use of linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) has indicated a significant genetic correlation between these two disease categories13,14. However, it remains unclear whether this overall genetic correlation is driven by a small number of loci or arises from a broad influence across the entire genome. In fact, even in the absence of clear genetic correlation, there may still be instances of genetic overlap15,16. Although previous studies have investigated the genetic overlap, shared susceptibility genes, and causal relationships between CVD and mental disorders, they have identified genetic variants associated with increased risks of both type 2 diabetes and SCZ17,18. Other studies have shown that overlapping loci exist between SCZ and BMI, WHR, blood lipids, and SBP19,20. For example, smoking is highly prevalent among SCZ patients and is also a major risk factor for CVD21,22. Recent data indicate a positive genetic correlation between SCZ and smoking (rg = 0.15)23,24. However, shared variants with a mix of concordant and discordant effect directions may cancel each other out, potentially resulting in negligible genetic correlation. Thus, genetic correlation analysis should be supplemented by methods capable of detecting genetic overlap regardless of effect direction, to identify underlying molecular mechanisms. In summary, current research on CVD and SCZ primarily focuses on specific disease types and mental disorders. There is a lack of studies examining multiple CVDs in relation to schizophrenia (SCZ) and a need for comprehensive research that spans from global genetic associations to local genetic correlations down to individual variant sites13,14,25.

Recent studies suggest that a deeper exploration of shared genetic risks across traits is needed to better understand the complex disorders arising from heart-brain interactions10,26–28. Such exploration not only helps identify specific genetic variants or loci driving genome-wide genetic correlations, but also has important clinical implications, as shared genetic causes may indicate potential pleiotropy, often acting as genetic confounders in trait associations. In recent years, cross-trait analytical methods have been developed to leverage the correlations in GWAS signals to study pleiotropic genetic variants or loci across multiple traits. The identified pleiotropic loci could serve as intervention targets, potentially preventing or treating these diseases simultaneously, offering new perspectives for future research and clinical applications29–31.

In this study, we conducted a genome-wide pleiotropic association analysis, utilizing large-scale GWAS summary data and applying various statistical genetics methods to perform a comprehensive genome-wide pleiotropy analysis of 17 types of CVD and SCZ. Our study aims to explore pleiotropy from the genome-wide level (including global and local genetic correlations), single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and gene levels, as well as from the perspective of biological pathways, to uncover potential shared genetic causes.

Within the framework of pleiotropy analysis, we first conducted an analysis at the SNVs to detect the presence of pleiotropic variants and loci. Subsequently, through pairwise colocalization analysis, we identified colocalized loci and performed gene-level analysis to identify candidate pleiotropic genes. Building on this, we conducted tissue-specific enrichment analysis to characterize tissue specificity and performed gene-level analysis to identify tissue-specific pleiotropic genes. Additionally, we used the Local Analysis of Variance Annotation (LAVA) method to analyze local genetic correlations, allowing us to gain a deeper understanding of the interactions between different traits. At the same time, we employed Summary-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) and Transcriptome-Wide Association Study (TWAS) to analyze transcriptome-level and tissue-specific gene pleiotropy, identifying key biological pathways and mechanisms. Finally, we performed Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis to evaluate pairwise causal associations and partially characterize different types of pleiotropy, including vertical and horizontal pleiotropy. This series of analyses will provide important insights for a deeper understanding of the genetic relationship between CVD and SCZ.

Methods

Data selection

This study included GWAS summary datasets for 17 types of CVD (Ischemic heart disease (IHD), Aortic aneurysm (AA)32, Atrial fibrillation and flutter (AF), Angina pectoris (AP), arrhythmia (AR)33, coronary atherosclerosis (CA), coronary artery disease (CAD)34, Cardiomyopathy (CM), Endocarditis (EC), Heart Failure (HF)35, Hypertensive heart disease (HDD), Myocardial infarction (MI), myocarditis (MYC), Non-rheumatic valve diseases (NRVD), pericarditis (PCD), pulmonary embolism (PE), Rheumatic heart disease (RHD)) (details in Supplementary Table 1). Additionally, we used GWAS data for 90 cardiovascular proteins from the study by Folkersen et al.36, which included 21,758 samples. Furthermore, we obtained SCZ GWAS data from the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC), provided by Vassily Trubetskoy et al., which included 76,755 SCZ patients and 243,649 controls37.

All GWAS resources were approved by relevant ethics committees, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Data collection occurred between July 1, 2024, and September 1, 2024, with the analysis conducted from September 1, 2024, to September 25, 2024. The genome-wide pleiotropy analysis followed the guidelines of the Strengthening the Reporting of Genetic Association Studies (STREGA). The experimental design process is shown in Fig. 1.

Global genetic correlation and genetic overlap analysis

First, we used LDSC38 and high-definition likelihood (HDL)39 to assess the genome-wide genetic correlation between CVD and SCZ. Specifically, we performed LDSC analysis using European ancestry LD scores based on the 1000 Genomes Project40. LDSC is commonly used to analyze genetic correlations between different diseases or phenotypes, helping to reveal the genetic basis of comorbidities. In our LDSC analysis, we did not constrain the intercept. While sample overlap can affect the intercept, it does not influence the slope. Thus, genetic correlation remains unaffected in the presence of sample overlap, which not only accounts for residual confounders but also indicates potential sample overlap between the two GWAS studies38.

HDL is a new method for analyzing GWAS data, designed to better understand the genetic basis of complex traits by precisely estimating genetic correlation and heritability39. The HDL method integrates advanced statistical techniques and computational tools to provide results with higher resolution compared to traditional methods. In terms of estimating genetic correlation, HDL significantly improves precision, reducing variance by about 60% compared to LDSC39. This reduction in variance effectively doubles the sample size, thereby enhancing the reliability of our estimates. Although HDL provides more precise correlation estimates, it lacks the built-in adjustment mechanism for sample overlap that LDSC offers.

Finally, we used Genetic Pleiotropy Analysis (GPA) to examine the overall genetic overlap between traits41. GPA is a method for identifying and quantifying shared genetic effects across traits, particularly useful for understanding how genetic variants influence multiple phenotypes. Unlike LDSC and HDL, where the former estimates heritability and genetic correlation from summary statistics and the latter focuses on dissecting genetic effects at specific loci, GPA directly assesses pleiotropic effects. Adding GPA after LDSC and HDL provides a deeper understanding of genetic architecture, revealing complex relationships between traits that the first two methods may not capture alone. In short, GPA goes beyond traditional single-phenotype analyses, uncovering subtle signals and complex relationships in the genetic architecture.

In the genetic correlation and overlap tests mentioned above, the significance threshold was adjusted for multiple testing using Bonferroni correction and set at P < 0.003 (0.05/17).

In this step, we analyze and cross-validate global genetic correlation using two methods, LDSC and HDL. Additionally, we employ GPA to assess global genetic overlap, aiming to produce results that are both more reliable and informative.

Local genetic correlation analysis

To explore the traits and their local genetic correlations, we used the Local Ancestry Variance Annotation (LAVA) method. LAVA is a method for identifying local genetic correlations within genomic regions by assessing the local genetic relationships between traits to pinpoint significant heritable regions42. Specifically, we performed variance analysis on each SNP and its neighboring region to assess genetic correlation between traits and calculate local genetic correlation for each region, while determining the significance level. To validate the significant regions identified by LAVA, we further employed paired GWAS (GWAS-PW) for verification. This step aimed to confirm the reliability of LAVA’s findings and to refine the analysis results. A posterior probability threshold (PP.A4) > 0.7 was used to determine regions with significant heritability and genetic correlation between traits43.

In this step, we primarily utilized a new method, LAVA, to analyze and identify local genetic correlations within genomic regions, and validated these findings with GWAS-PW. This approach, moving from global to increasingly refined local analyses, helps enhance the reliability of our research outcomes.

Placo and MTAG analysis of shared loci

When trait pairs show significant genetic correlation or overlap, we conducted pleiotropy analysis using the composite null hypothesis (PLACO) to identify potential pleiotropic single nucleotide variants (SNVs)44. To identify potential pleiotropic SNVs with a PLACO significance threshold below 5 × 10 ^ -8, we analyzed these variants using the FUMA tool45. FUMA integrates linkage disequilibrium (LD) information from the 1000 Genomes European population to characterize genomic risk loci and provide functional annotation. We set a p-value threshold of 5 × 10 ^ -8 for lead SNVs and defined independent SNVs as variants with r² < 0.6 relative to the lead SNVs. Additionally, we investigated the overlap of genomic loci between different traits. Genomic loci were defined as merged regions with physical distances <250 kb16. Our analysis results showed the overlap between these loci, providing a better understanding of the distribution of pleiotropic signals across different traits.

MTAG (Multi-Trait Analysis of GWAS) is used to analyze multiple traits simultaneously to improve the estimation of genetic correlation. Building on PLACO, we combined regions with high correlation from LAVA analysis and used the MTAG method to calculate the genetic variance and genetic correlation of shared loci in each region, ensuring maximized statistical power while accounting for interactions between multiple traits. This method helped us uncover potential genetic connections between traits in different regions. In short, we aimed to integrate a cardiovascular-related GWAS dataset, encompassing multiple CVD linked to the heart-brain axis, to more comprehensively and accurately elucidate the genetic relationship of the heart-brain axis. Given that an increase in regional traits in the joint analysis could reduce analytical performance, even though these regional traits were rigorously screened in LAVA, we first filtered pleiotropic loci through PLACO before performing MTAG analysis, ensuring greater reliability and accuracy of the method. Specifically, we used PLINK1.9 to identify independent genome-wide significant pleiotropic loci (r² < .02 and 500 kb window) to ensure that the identified loci were independent and widely distributed across the genome46. The MTAG method allows the estimation of the effect of each SNP in joint analyses to identify genetic variants shared among multiple traits. We then used ANNOVAR47 for functional annotation to gain detailed insights into the functional impact of identified variants, and calculated combined annotation-dependent depletion (CADD) and RegulomeDB scores. Single nucleotide variants with CADD scores >12.37 were considered potentially deleterious48. RegulomeDB scores SNPs, where lower scores indicate stronger regulatory potential49.

For potential pleiotropic loci, a COLOC Bayesian colocalization analysis test was performed. A posterior probability for H4 (PP.H4) >0.7 was used as the criterion to confirm colocalization43.

In addition, we used HyPrColoc (hypothesis prioritization for multi-trait colocalization) to perform multi-trait colocalization analysis in conjunction with 90 cardiovascular protein GWAS datasets, to emphasize the critical role of cardiovascular proteins in the heart-brain axis. HyPrColoc is suitable for multi-trait statistical colocalization analysis, and it is based on the same fundamental statistical model as COLOC, but can perform colocalization analysis across many traits. This is because the colocalization posterior probability for a single causal variant can be accurately approximated by enumerating only a small number of assumed causal configurations50. HyPrColoc avoids repeated pairwise colocalization analysis (which may increase false-negative rates and reduce the power to detect shared causal variants). The HyPrColoc algorithm identifies regions in the genome with evidence of shared causal variants (by segmenting the genome into different regions) and also allows for targeted analysis of specific genomic loci of interest, such as when aiming to identify biological pathway disruptions through the influence of specific genes. Additionally, these region-specific analyses can highlight candidate pathogenic genes, which will contribute to better biological insights and may indicate potential drug targets, informing drug development.

Similarly, in this step, we used a threshold of posterior probability (PP.A4) > 0.7 as the criterion for determining colocalization43.

We progressively refined our analysis down to single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and employed multiple methods for cross-validation to enhance the reliability of the results.

SMR and TWAS analysis of shared pleiotropic genes

Considering that PLACO and MTAG analyses lack the connection between gene expression and pleiotropic loci and cannot directly pinpoint specific tissues, we also used SMR and TWAS analyses. The primary goal of SMR analysis is to assess whether genetic variants at specific gene loci have a shared causal effect on multiple traits51. Specifically, we first used the SMR method to assess the shared genetic effects of target genes across different traits, and then estimated the association between target gene expression levels and multiple traits. SMR analysis can determine whether a significant causal relationship exists. SMR combines data from GWAS and eQTL studies, with LD calculations based on the 1000 Genomes European reference data, including eQTL data from 26 tissues.

We used a Bonferroni-corrected p-value of <0.05 to assess whether the target gene has a significant shared effect with the traits. Further, we performed the HEIDI test to validate the reliability of the SMR results. The HEIDI test is used to assess whether the significance in SMR results may be caused by multiple effects of genetic variants. TWAS analysis was used to evaluate the association between gene expression levels and traits, further revealing potential functional impacts52. We combined GWAS summary data and eQTL data to analyze the relationship between gene expression levels and traits using the TWAS method. Specifically, we used the GTEX V8 dataset to extract gene expression data, which covers 54 tissue types, and calculated the correlation between gene expression and traits for each phenotype. Then, by assessing the association between gene expression and traits, we calculated Bonferroni-corrected p-values to identify significant gene expression-trait associations. In summary, we combined SMR and TWAS results to identify shared pleiotropic genes and their roles across different tissues and traits.

Polytropic gene enrichment analysis

After identifying pleiotropic genes, enrichment analysis can help us understand the roles of these genes in biological pathways and functional categories. To further explore the biological significance of these pleiotropic genes, we used MAGMA at the gene level to analyze genes located at or overlapping pleiotropic loci, aiming to identify candidate pleiotropic genes. The MAGMA gene IDs and positions were obtained from https://ctg.cncr.nl/software/magma, covering 19,427 protein-coding genes based on NCBI build 37.3. Functional enrichment analysis and tissue specificity analysis were subsequently conducted. This type of analysis not only enhances our biological understanding of pleiotropic genes but also provides important information for further functional research and mechanistic exploration.

Bidirectional TSMR analysis and Latent Hidden Causal Mendelian Randomization (lhcMR)

To infer the causal relationship between CVD and SCZ, we employed a bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (TSMR) analysis53,54. This method uses genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to assess the causal effect of the exposure factor on the outcome, while also testing for reverse causality between the outcome and exposure. Selection of instrumental variables (IVs): We first selected single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) significantly associated with CVD and SCZ based on GWAS results as IVs. The selection criterion was a p-value of <5 × 10 ^ -8, indicating that these SNPs are significantly associated with the exposure factor or outcome. Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) filtering: To ensure that the selected SNPs are independent IVs, we filtered out independent SNPs within an LD window of r² < 0.01 and 500 kilobases. We used PLINK1.9 for LD filtering. We employed the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method as the primary MR approach. The IVW method uses weighted linear regression, combining the effect estimates of each SNP and their standard errors, to evaluate the overall causal effect between exposure and outcome. The weights were calculated based on the precision of each SNP’s effect estimate (the inverse of the standard error), making the effect estimates more robust. To ensure the robustness of the TSMR analysis and to exclude potential confounding factors, we conducted sensitivity analyses. We used the Q statistic to assess heterogeneity between the IVs. Heterogeneity refers to whether the effects produced by different SNPs are inconsistent. If the Q_pval is significant (usually p-value < 0.05), it indicates the presence of significant heterogeneity. This suggests that SNPs may influence the outcome not solely through the exposure factor, but potentially through pleiotropy or other confounding effects. The Egger regression intercept was used to detect horizontal pleiotropy in the IVs (horizontal pleiotropy refers to SNPs directly affecting the outcome through pathways unrelated to the exposure factor). If the Egger intercept significantly deviates from zero, this suggests the presence of horizontal pleiotropy, which could affect the reliability of the MR analysis results. The p-value of Egger regression (p_pleiotropy) was used to assess the significance of the intercept. If p_pleiotropy < 0.05, this indicates the potential presence of pleiotropy.

LHC-MR is a structural equation (mixed-effects) model that can account for potential heritable confounding factors in the exposure-outcome relationship, enabling the estimation of bidirectional causal effects between two traits55. This model uses genome-wide genetic marker association summary statistics to estimate various global features, including bidirectional causal effects, confounding effects, direct heritability, polygenicity, and population stratification. In the study by Liza Darrous et al., this method produced significantly lower bias and variance in causal effect estimates in most cases compared to other MR tools45. The direction and magnitude of bias in classical MR methods vary depending on the scenario and sample size. In this study, a key point of including LHC-MR is that in smaller sample sizes, as the kurtosis of the distribution of effect sizes increases, standard MR methods show a reduction in the variance of causal effect estimates, whereas LHC-MR does not show a similar trend and can estimate causal effects with smaller bias55. In summary, the advantage of LHC-MR lies in its ability to deeply analyze the mechanisms by which genetic variants influence complex traits, while accounting for heterogeneity between subgroups. It reduces bias introduced by population stratification and sample size limitations, providing more accurate and detailed results for research55.

Results

Global genetic correlation and genetic overlap analysis

In LDSC, we found suggestive genome-wide genetic correlations between three pairs of traits among the 17 types of CVD and SCZ (SCZ-CAD and SCZ-HHD) (rg_p < 0.05), and one trait pair (SCZ-CA) showed significant genetic correlation (rg_p < 0.003). In HDL analysis, two trait pairs showed suggestive genetic correlation (SCZ-CA and SCZ-HHD), and the directionality was consistent with LDSC. In GPA analysis, 15 out of 17 trait pairs (excluding SCZ-EC and SCZ-RHD) exhibited significant genetic overlap (p < 0.003) (Table 1, Fig. 2).

We found that the GPA analysis demonstrated higher sensitivity, allowing it to detect weak signals that might be missed by LDSC or HDL. This difference in sensitivity can lead GPA to identify very small p-values in some cases, even when the corresponding genetic correlation estimates are not significant. This suggests that the GPA method is better at revealing complex polygenic effects or subtle genetic overlap, while LDSC and HDL may be limited by statistical power in such cases. For trait pairs where the genetic correlation estimates are not significant but the GPA p-values are very small, we speculate that there may be underlying genetic mechanisms or more complex polygenic effects yet to be fully revealed. This difference highlights the importance of combining multiple analytical methods to comprehensively understand the genetic associations between traits. In exploratory studies, GPA can serve as a complementary tool to identify weak but meaningful signals that may be missed by traditional methods.

Localized genetic correlation analysis

Given the differences in the results of global genetic correlation and genetic overlap, to identify significant loci that may have been overlooked in genome-wide analyses, we applied LAVA to 17 trait pairs. This analysis revealed 74 regions across 17 types of CVD that were significantly associated with SCZ in terms of local heritability (Bonferroni-corrected p-value < 0.05) (45 independent regions), and 40 regions had high posterior probabilities of colocalization with SCZ (13 independent regions) (PPA_4 > 0.7) (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table 2). Notably, regions 378 (chr2: 198140351-199654969) and 953 (chr6: 28666365-29529755) showed significant local genetic correlations across more than seven trait pairs. Region 378 primarily involved negative correlations (rho < 0) between MYC, CAD, CA, IHD, MI, and SCZ, and positive correlations (rho > 0) between AF, AR, and SCZ. Region 953 showed negative correlations (rho < 0) between HHD, MYC, CAD, AF, IHD, CM, PCD, and SCZ, and positive correlations (rho > 0) between PE and SCZ (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table 2). It is important to note that region 953 (chr6: 28666365-29529755) lies within the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) region (chromosome 6: 25–35 megabases [Mb]), a region traditionally excluded from heritability analyses due to its complex LD structure. However, previous studies have highlighted the importance of genetic variation in the MHC region for autoimmune diseases, inflammatory markers, and other phenotypes in MR studies56,57. Large-scale GWAS findings have also revealed immune-related SNPs in this region that are strongly associated with psychiatric disorders58,59, suggesting that SNPs in this region should not be excluded60,61. Therefore, in our study, we excluded these regions from global heritability analyses, including TSMR analysis, to facilitate validation across other studies. However, local heritability analysis using LAVA is rare in similar studies. To offer insights into the potential immune-related associations within the brain-heart axis for future researchers, we did not exclude these regions. The same approach was applied in SNVs’ PLACO and MTAG analyses.

PLACO analysis, MTAG analysis and ANNOVAR functional annotation

By performing a cross-trait meta-analysis using MTAG, based on the intersection of LAVA-significant regions and SNPs with P_PLACO < 1*10^-8, we identified 842 independent SNVs as potential pleiotropic variants (Bonferroni-corrected p-value < 0.05) (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Table 3). These SNVs showed significant associations between CVD and SCZ, with coloc colocalization demonstrating high posterior probabilities (PP.H4 > 0.7). Among these, 118 SNVs were identified as significant pleiotropic variants across seven trait pairs, and 9 SNVs were identified across six trait pairs.

Functional annotation using ANNOVAR showed that of the 842 SNVs, 261 were intronic variants, 497 were intergenic, and 8 were exonic variants. Notably, among these shared causal variants, 32 were predicted to have deleterious effects based on Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) scores >12.37. Four of these deleterious variants were exonic, with nearby genes including TRIM27 (rs2230683) and UBD (rs404240) in region 953, CENPM (rs7293091) in region 2485, and MYH7B (rs36003887) in region 2399. Two harmful intronic variants in region 378 were near MOB4 (rs67657812) and ANKRD44 (rs10153881).

Among the 127 SNVs identified as significant pleiotropic variants across more than five trait pairs, 12 were intronic, 94 were intergenic, and 3 were exonic variants. Six of these were predicted to have deleterious effects with CADD scores greater than 12.37. TRIM27 (rs2230683) and UBD (rs404240) in region 953 were detected again, and notably, both are located in the MHC region, highlighting the role of immune factors in the brain-heart axis.

HyPrColoc colocalization analysis for 90 cardiovascular proteins identified 9 pleiotropic loci (4 unique loci: rs9257248, rs3118357, rs9257136, and rs35044849), showing shared causal variants that support the importance of cardiovascular proteins in the brain-heart axis (Supplementary Table 4). Specifically, rs9257248 was identified as a shared causal variant in SCZ-HHD-CX3CL1, rs3118357 in SCZ-IHD-CX3CL1, rs9257136 in SCZ-MYC-CX3CL1 and SCZ-PCD-CX3CL1, and rs35044849 in SCZ-CM-proHB-EGF, SCZ-HHD-proHB-EGF, SCZ-IHD-proHB-EGF, SCZ-MYC-proHB-EGF, and SCZ-PCD-proHB-EGF.

Subsequently, MAGMA analysis found that upregulated pleiotropic genes were predominantly enriched in tissues such as Artery_Tibial, Brain_Anterior_cingulate_cortex, Brain_Nucleus_accumbens_basal_ganglia, Brain_Substantia, Brain_Putamen_basal_ganglia, Artery_Coronary, and Brain_Cortex. Downregulated pleiotropic genes were mainly enriched in Breast_Mammary_Tissue, Minor_Salivary_Gland, and Spleen. Gene set enrichment analysis revealed that these genes were primarily enriched in pathways such as GOBP_GENERATION_OF_NEURONS, GOBP_NEUROGENESIS, GOBP_STRIATUM_DEVELOPMENT, and GOBP_SUBPALLIUM_DEVELOPMENT.

SMR and TWAS analysis of shared pleiotropic genes

Annotating GWAS variants to genes solely based on proximity may be overly simplistic, potentially overlooking pleiotropic effects. Relying only on single nucleotide variants (SNVs) without exploring other genetic variants (e.g., copy number variations) could limit the scope of research findings and fail to address tissue-specific gene expression. To resolve this, we employed SMR and FUSION to identify pleiotropic genes in regions with significant genetic correlations. Using these methods, we identified 105 and 158 genes (adjusted_p < 0.05, p_HEIDI > 0.01) predominantly enriched in tissues such as Blood, Brain, Brain_Caudate_basal_ganglia, Brain_Cerebellar_Hemisphere, Brain_Frontal_Cortex, Cerebellum, Brain_Hypothalamus, and Brain_Cortex. Among them, 12 genes (MARCKSL1P1, ARL3, NT5C2, ARPC3, FAM216A, STN1, VPS29, MYH7B, PCCB, INO80E, ANAPC7, RPS17) were consistently identified by both methods (Fig. 4A–E, Supplementary Tables 5, 6). These genes were primarily enriched in KEGG pathways such as Endocytosis, Glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, Propanoate metabolism, Nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, and Valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation (Fig. 4F, Supplementary Table 7). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed involvement in vesicle-mediated transport to the plasma membrane, protein localization to the ciliary membrane, positive regulation of mitotic metaphase/anaphase transition, IMP metabolic process (GOBP); Arp2/3 protein complex, telomere cap complex, nuclear telomere cap complex (GOCC); and actin filament binding, single-stranded telomeric DNA binding, 5’-nucleotidase activity, GTPase activating protein binding (GOMF) (Fig. 4G, Supplementary Table 7). Bidirectional MR analysis

Under the condition that all instrumental variable F-statistics were robustly >10, TSMR analysis confirmed that SCZ may reduce the risk of MI susceptibility (OR < 1, FDR_IVW < 0.05) and suggested that SCZ may increase the risk of MYC susceptibility (OR > 1, P_IVW < 0.05). Both heterogeneity tests and horizontal pleiotropy checks indicated reliability (Q_pval > 0.05; p_pleiotropy > 0.05). Reverse TSMR analysis confirmed that HHD and NRVD may reduce SCZ susceptibility risk (OR < 1, FDR_IVW < 0.05), and suggested that HF may reduce SCZ susceptibility risk (OR < 1, P_IVW < 0.05). Although the horizontal pleiotropy check showed reliability, we detected heterogeneity in the results (Q_pval < 0.05; p_pleiotropy > 0.05). Moreover, using scatter plots, forest plots, funnel plots, and leave-one-out analyses, we further confirmed the reliability of the results and identified potential outlier SNPs, though these did not substantially affect the overall conclusions (Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 8).

Discussion

In this genome-wide pleiotropy association study, we found extensive genetic correlations and overlaps between CVDs and SCZ. Further integrative analyses highlighted pleiotropic genetic variants and loci, potential shared pathogenic variants, pleiotropic genes, and the underlying genetic basis of biological pathways. All these findings support the role of the heart-brain axis in the shared genetic etiology of these two diseases.

Notably, we aimed to use multiple methods with different model assumptions to provide complementary evidence and allow for in-depth exploration of potential pleiotropic associations from various perspectives. First, GPA identified significant genetic overlap between several trait pairs, even when no significant genetic correlation was detected. This is due to GPA’s focus on more localized signals, providing higher sensitivity. Additionally, the mixed direction of cross-genomic risk loci indicates both concordant and discordant pleiotropy, helping explain cases of polygenic overlap despite non-significant genetic correlation, which we further validated using LAVA in local genetic correlations. PLACO and MTAG analyses further identified pleiotropic variants, followed by colocalization analysis to detect potential shared causal variants within each pleiotropic locus. SMR and TWAS, with their advantages in analyzing cross-tissue and transcriptional expression levels, were used to validate the important genetic role of the heart-brain axis in both diseases. Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis detected causal trait pairs, partially reflecting vertical pleiotropy. It is worth noting that we conducted LDSC to check for potential sample overlap, and used PLACO and MTAG for pleiotropy analysis, as well as LHC-MR for Mendelian randomization analysis to mitigate the issue of sample overlap.

Overall, pleiotropic variants between CVD and SCZ are widely distributed, with several loci being particularly prominent between certain trait pairs, such as TRIM27 (rs2230683) in the 953 region, UBD (rs404240), CENPM (rs7293091) in the 2485 region, MYH7B (rs36003887) in the 2399 region, and MOB4 (rs67657812) and ANKRD44 (rs10153881) in the 378 region.

Studies have shown that TRIM27 is upregulated in heart disease models, particularly under increased pressure load (e.g., in transverse aortic constriction models). TRIM27 promotes cardiac hypertrophy by activating the PTEN/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. Knockdown of TRIM27 significantly reduces the extent of cardiac hypertrophy, highlighting its key role in cardiac adaptive responses62,63. TRIM27 is also believed to be related to neurobiology and may influence the onset of psychiatric disorders. Some studies have found that TRIM27 is upregulated in peripheral blood mononuclear cells of patients with SCZ, suggesting its potential role in neuroinflammation and apoptosis, which may lead to or exacerbate psychiatric disorders62,64.

In a study by Hao Yu et al.65, it was found that the risk genetic variant rs7293091 affects DNAm levels, dysregulates the expression of nearby genes, disrupts cognitive abilities, and increases the risk of SCZ. It is noteworthy that in previous SCZ GWAS, the SNP rs7293091 in the CENPM gene did not show genome-wide significant association with SCZ (P = 5.33 × 10 − 8). Hao Yu et al. believed this was due to insufficient power in previous analytical methods, which might explain why it was not identified in earlier GWAS. With advances in analytical models, they identified this new functional SNP rs7293091 using the SMR method. However, in our study, it was detected by methods like MTAG but excluded in subsequent SMR and TWAS analyses, as we found that although it was significant after correction, its p_HEIDI < 0.01. In addition, multiple studies have shown that MYH7B plays a crucial role in cardiac function, and its variants are associated with the pathogenesis of CVD66,67.

Additionally, HyPrColoc analysis of 90 cardiovascular proteins revealed nine important pleiotropic loci, supporting the critical role of cardiovascular proteins in the heart-brain axis. Notably, four unique loci (rs9257248, rs3118357, rs9257136, rs35044849) showed significant colocalization between CVD and SCZ, suggesting that these genetic variants may simultaneously affect cardiovascular and nervous system functions.

For example, the relationship between rs9257248, rs3118357, rs9257136 and SCZ-HHD with CX3CL1 reveals that this locus may play an important role in the heart-brain axis by regulating CX3CL1 expression. CX3CL1, as a chemokine, is known to be involved in immune responses and neuroinflammation, potentially bridging the gap between CVD and SCZ. Studies have shown that CX3CL1 is upregulated in atherosclerotic plaques, promoting plaque formation and progression by recruiting inflammatory cells (such as monocytes) to the vascular walls68. In heart failure patients, elevated CX3CL1 levels are associated with an increased risk of cardiac dysfunction, potentially acting through its effects on cardiac inflammation and remodeling mechanisms69. In summary, CX3CL1 is considered a potential biomarker for cardiovascular disease, with plasma levels associated with the risk of cardiovascular events, potentially offering new perspectives for clinical risk assessment. Additionally, some studies have shown that CX3CL1 is associated with various mental disorders, such as depression and SCZ, possibly playing a key role in the pathogenesis of these diseases through the regulation of neuroinflammation70.

Similarly, the colocalization of rs35044849 with multiple SCZ phenotypes and proHB-EGF further supports the interaction between cardiovascular proteins and neuropsychiatric diseases. Studies have shown that mice with cardiac-specific overexpression of proHB-EGF exhibit decreased cardiac contractility and interstitial fibrosis, indicating that proHB-EGF plays an important role in the pathological processes of the heart71. This may be due to proHB-EGF affecting physiological and pathological cardiac processes, such as hypertrophy and fibrosis, through activation of the EGFR signaling pathway72. Our study highlights the importance of immune factors in the heart-brain axis, particularly the association between variations in the MHC region and CVD and SCZ. The findings support the neurodevelopmental hypothesis and align with the perspective that the immune system is involved in the pathogenesis of SCZ73. Additionally, brain function regulates behavior, which plays a critical role in lifestyle choices impacting cardiovascular disease. The immune system is also crucial in the development of CVD74. The immune system may play a regulatory role between cardiovascular and mental health by influencing neuroinflammation and metabolic processes. Therefore, further functional studies on these colocalized loci may help uncover the biological mechanisms of the heart-brain axis and provide new therapeutic targets for treating both diseases.

Tissue-specific enrichment in MTAG and other analyses indicates that pleiotropic genes are primarily enriched in the arteries, blood, and brain regions. Pathway analysis shows that these genes are mainly enriched in GOBP pathways related to neurogenesis and neurodevelopment. Moreover, the shared genes identified in SMR and TWAS analyses are predominantly enriched in KEGG pathways related to endocytosis and metabolism. This suggests that endocytosis may play an important role in neurodevelopment. For example, endocytosis is closely related to cellular signal transduction and may influence the proliferation and migration of neural cells, thus promoting the generation and maturation of neurons. Additionally, metabolic pathways such as nicotinate and valine metabolism may support neurodevelopment by regulating neuronal energy supply and signal transmission. Therefore, the potential links between these pathways highlight the significant impact of the intracellular and extracellular environments on nervous system development, emphasizing the necessity of integrating metabolic and developmental pathways in studies related to CVD and SCZ.

Although this study provides some insights into the genetic relationship between CVD and SCZ, it also faces potential limitations. For example, the research primarily relies on populations of European ancestry, raising uncertainty about whether the findings can be generalized to other populations that may lack global diversity. The lack of representation from other ancestries limits the applicability of the findings to more diverse global populations and could lead to biased conclusions. Future research should aim to include a broader range of ancestries to ensure global relevance and explore potential genetic effects in specific populations. Additionally, the study did not account for potential confounding factors such as lifestyle, environmental influences, and socioeconomic status. These factors play a significant role in both cardiovascular diseases and mental disorders and may affect the observed genetic correlations. Ignoring these variables could lead to an incomplete understanding of the genetic basis of these diseases. Future research should integrate these confounders to provide a more comprehensive analysis. Furthermore, some methods, such as HDL and GPA, did not handle sample overlap well, introducing certain biases into the results. These challenges underscore the need for continuous improvement of bioinformatics tools and methods to enhance the accuracy of functional annotations and our understanding of pleiotropic gene functions.

Conclusion

Given the evidence of genetic correlation and genetic overlap between CVD and SCZ in this study, we found that pleiotropic genetic variants, loci, and genes are widely distributed across the genome, showing higher tissue specificity. More importantly, we identified shared biological mechanisms related to immune responses, metabolic factors, neurodevelopmental processes, and potential cardiovascular proteomes. Our findings not only support the shared genetic basis of the heart-brain axis but also provide new insights for the intervention and therapeutic targets of these diseases.
