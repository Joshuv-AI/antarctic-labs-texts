Psychological impacts of APOE genotype disclosure among Latinos in New York City: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Ruth Ottman, John B Wetmore, Cheng‐Shiun Leu, Wendy R Uhlmann, Jill Goldman, Sophia Rodriguez, Daniela Diaz Caro, María Cabán, Sarah McCain, Drew Blasco, Rafael A Lantigua, Ana Abraido‐Lanza, Wendy K Chung, J Scott Roberts, Karolynn Siegel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONLatinos face increased Alzheimer's disease (AD) risk but are underrepresented in studies of APOE genotype disclosure. We evaluated the psychological impacts of APOE disclosure in the Información de la Enfermedad de Alzheimer para Latinos (IDEAL) study, a randomized controlled trial among Latinos in New York City.METHODSLatino northern Manhattan residents without self‐reported AD (mean age 52, 69% women, 49% college graduates) were randomized in the period August 2021 to July 2024 to receive AD risk estimates to age 85 incorporating APOE genotype, family history, and ethnicity (disclosure) or the same factors excluding APOE (non‐disclosure). Bilingual genetic counselors delivered risk estimates to both groups, unmasked to randomization. Follow‐up surveys were completed 6 weeks, 9 months, and 15 months after risk delivery. Primary outcomes were impact of genetic testing in AD (IGT‐AD) and Impact of Event Scale‐Revised (IES‐R). Secondary outcomes were changes from baseline in depression, anxiety, and perceived AD threat. Analyses used intention‐to‐treat with multiple imputation.RESULTSDisclosure (N = 194) and non‐disclosure (N = 180) groups did not differ on IGT‐AD (mean disclosure−non‐disclosure difference [MD] at 6 weeks: −1.5, p = 0.14; 9 months: −1.2, p = 0.35; 15 months: −2.0, p = 0.07), IES‐R (MD at 6 weeks: 0.00, p = 0.98; 9 months: 0.01, p = 0.84; 15 months: 0.03, p = 0.64), or change in secondary outcomes. Occurrence of disclosure‐related adverse events was similar in the disclosure (N = 2) and non‐disclosure (N = 3) groups.DISCUSSIONIn this Latino cohort, APOE disclosure did not have clinically significant adverse psychological effects, addressing an important evidence gap.TRIAL REGISTRATIONClinicalTrials.gov NCT04471779.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, APOE, ethical, genetic testing, Hispanic, legal, Latino, psychology, psychosocial, social implications

BACKGROUND

APOE remains the strongest genetic predictor of late‐onset Alzheimer's disease (AD), with a two‐ to four‐fold increased risk among persons with a single ε4 allele and eight‐ to 12‐fold increased risk among those with two ε4 alleles, compared with the APOE ε3/ε3 genotype. 1 Importantly, these associations vary by ancestral background and are not deterministic: Having an ε4 allele is neither necessary nor sufficient to cause AD. 1

Access to APOE testing is increasing due to direct‐to‐consumer (DTC) testing, participation in AD prevention trials that prioritize ε4 carriers, and evaluation of treatment safety for amyloid‐reducing therapies given the risk of amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities among ε4 carriers (especially homozygotes). 2 Moreover, public interest in APOE testing is strong, reaching 70% to 80% in some surveys. 3 , 4 , 5

Previously, APOE testing of asymptomatic persons was discouraged because of a lack of clinical utility and possible psychological harms. 6 , 7 , 8 However, beginning with the Risk Evaluation and Education for Alzheimer's Disease (REVEAL) study, 9 research has found that testing typically does not lead to clinically significant depression or anxiety. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Testing has benefits for some, such as reduced uncertainty and increased motivation to implement advanced planning (e.g., obtain long‐term care insurance), and adopt risk‐reducing (e.g., improved diet and exercise) and healthcare management behaviors (e.g., symptom monitoring and healthcare utilization). 14 , 15 , 16 , 17

However, previous studies of the impacts of APOE genotype disclosure had limited generalizability. They included primarily highly educated non‐Latino White participants with a family history (in REVEAL) 9 or volunteers for an AD prevention trial, 11 , 17 which likely reflected increased motivation and preparedness to receive genetic findings. Generally, pre‐test and post‐test genetic counseling were included, which likely afforded protection from significant distress. Outcomes may differ in DTC settings or clinical contexts with limited counseling support. 18 , 19 , 20

Latinos are the largest US ethnic minority group, making up 19% of the population in 2022. 21 Compared to other groups, they have higher AD risk, earlier age at onset, and more delayed diagnoses; yet they have been underrepresented in AD research, including studies of the impacts of APOE genotype disclosure. 22 Their responses might differ from those of other groups because of distinct cultural values and beliefs that influence illness perceptions and health behaviors. 23 , 24 For these reasons, improved understanding is urgently needed of responses to the receipt of APOE‐related AD risk information among persons who identify as Latino. We assessed the psychological impacts of APOE genotype disclosure among Latino residents of northern Manhattan, New York, in the Información de la Enfermedad de Alzheimer para Latinos (IDEAL) study. 25

METHODS

Study design

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: The authors searched PubMed for empirical research on the psychological impacts of APOE genotype disclosure. Studies are consistent in finding minimal or only transient psychological impacts but underrepresented Latinos. Research on Latinos is important because they are the largest US minoritized group, face increased AD risk, and could have different responses because of distinct cultural values and beliefs.

Interpretation: As in previous studies, APOE disclosure did not have clinically significant adverse psychological impacts among Latino IDEAL participants, suggesting distinct cultural values and beliefs were unlikely to have increased adverse responses. However, selective participation and specialized education and genetic counseling may have mitigated adverse impacts.

Future directions: Further research is needed on APOE disclosure in settings with limited counseling support and differences in impact according to APOE‐related AD risks conveyed to participants and sociodemographic factors such as gender, education, and AD family history, in addition to ethnicity.

IDEAL is a community‐based, mixed‐methods, Phase 3, parallel‐group, randomized controlled superiority trial of the psychological, cognitive, and behavioral impacts of APOE genotype disclosure among Latino residents of northern Manhattan, New York. This report is limited to the prespecified psychological primary outcomes. The prespecified cognitive primary outcomes are being analyzed separately and will be reported in a subsequent publication.

The study was approved by Columbia University Irving Medical Center's Institutional Review Board (AAAR8269) and is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04471779). IDEAL's design and methods were described in detail previously. 25 The full trial protocol is included in the Supplement.

All data collection and participant communication materials were developed in Spanish and English by a multidisciplinary team with strong representation of Latino bilingual and bicultural researchers. Before beginning data collection, we evaluated the feasibility and acceptability of our methods and obtained feedback from eligible community members through five focus groups with participants recruited with assistance from the Community Engagement Core Resource, Columbia University Irving Institute for Clinical and Translational Research.

Participants

Participants were Latino residents of the same northern Manhattan communities included in the Washington Heights‐Inwood Columbia Aging Project (WHICAP), a 30‐year, community‐based, longitudinal study of the genetic epidemiology of AD. 26 For IDEAL ascertainment, we used address‐based sampling in these communities to identify eligible residents (self‐identification as Latino or Hispanic, age 40 to 64 years, no self‐reported previous AD diagnosis) for a baseline survey. Baseline surveys, accrued June 2021 to September 2023, collected information on demographics, AD family history, AD and genetics knowledge and beliefs, perceived risk of developing AD by age 85, 27 and symptoms of depression (Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 [PHQ‐9]) 28 and anxiety (Generalized Anxiety Disorder‐7 [GAD‐7]). 29 Gender was ascertained by asking “What is your gender?” with three possible options (man, woman, other). Baseline survey completers were excluded from later study steps if they did not allow recontact or their responses suggested a family history consistent with early‐onset, autosomal‐dominant AD, previous APOE genetic testing, or suicidality (i.e., response other than “not at all” to the suicidality item on the PHQ‐9). 28

Remaining participants were invited to complete a narrated and animated introductory session with education about AD, genetics, genetic testing, and study procedures, followed by a 14‐item quiz to assess understanding needed for meaningful informed consent. 25 Most participants completed the session online, and a few (1%) completed it by telephone with a genetic counselor (GC) using materials mailed beforehand. Those who scored ≥67% correct on the quiz and consented were sent a buccal swab DNA collection kit, which they mailed directly to a commercial laboratory for APOE testing.

Among genotyped participants, for the trial we selected all those with ≥1 ε4 allele (ε4+) and approximately the same number of those with zero ε4 alleles (ε4−), frequency‐matched to ε4+ participants by age group (40 to 49, 50 to 59, 60 to 64), gender, and first‐degree family history. Selected participants were asked to complete a pre‐disclosure survey, which included subjective and objective memory assessments and measures that may influence responses to APOE genotype disclosure (e.g., numeracy, intolerance of uncertainty). 25

Randomization and masking

Participants who completed the pre‐disclosure survey were randomized to disclosure (receipt of an AD risk estimate to age 85 incorporating APOE genotype [0, 1, or 2 ε4 alleles], first‐degree AD family history, and identification as a Latino resident of our target communities) or non‐disclosure (estimate based on the same factors excluding APOE genotype). Randomization was carried out from August 2021 to July 2024 using a permuted block design with a mixed block size of four and six and an allocation ratio of 1:1. Randomization was stratified by ε4 allele count, age group, gender, and first‐degree family history, using Excel files containing permuted blocks for each stratification group and a program that sorted participants into the next row of the file that matched their characteristics. Due to ethical concerns about withholding potentially useful information from participants at the highest risk, all ε4 homozygotes were placed in the disclosure group. The allocation sequence was hidden from all participant‐facing study staff until assigned. Research staff who collected outcome data at follow‐up were masked to disclosure group and genotype, but participants and GCs who returned risk information were unmasked.

Procedures

The trial intervention was receipt of an AD risk estimate by age 85 incorporating APOE genotype. Risk estimates were obtained by analysis of data for Latinos from WHICAP, comprising residents of the same communities as in IDEAL. 25 , 26 In the non‐disclosure group, estimates were 25% (no first‐degree family history) and 29% (first‐degree family history). In the disclosure group, estimates for participants with zero ε4 alleles were 21% (no first‐degree AD family history) and 25% (first‐degree family history), and for those with one ε4 allele, 35% (no first‐degree family history) and 40% (first‐degree family history). The estimate for ε4 homozygotes was 55%. (We could not incorporate family history into this estimate because the WHICAP data included only 33 ε4 homozygotes.)

Risk estimates for both groups and genotypes for the disclosure group were delivered in one‐on‐one, semi‐structured risk evaluation sessions with bilingual and bicultural board‐certified GCs, conducted in English or Spanish according to participant preference, by videoconference with almost all participants, and a few in person (N = 8). A PowerPoint presentation was used to review AD, genetics, relations of APOE genotypes to AD risk (emphasizing lack of necessity and sufficiency of ε4), and AD risk reduction options (e.g., healthy diet, social connections). Risk estimates were presented as percentages and numbers out of 20 depicted in 5 × 4 icon arrays (selected because of their demonstrated ability to improve genetic risk communication). 25 , 30

One week after the session, GCs called participants to assess recall and short‐term emotional responses. Follow‐up surveys were completed via computer‐assisted telephone interview (CATI) 6 weeks, 9 months, and 15 months after the risk evaluation sessions and were attempted with all randomized participants regardless of whether they had completed the risk evaluation session or a previous follow‐up survey. In each follow‐up survey, participants’ recall of the estimates they received was assessed by asking, “What estimate were you given as your risk for developing Alzheimer's disease by age 85?”

In IDEAL's qualitative arm, 90‐min interviews were carried out with a subset of trial participants before the risk evaluation session and after completion of each follow‐up survey. Participants not selected for the clinical trial (N = 260) and those in the non‐disclosure group were informed of their genotypes and given a risk estimate (or revised risk estimate) at the end of the study.

Outcomes

Primary measures were the Impact of Genetic Testing in AD (IGT‐AD) 31 scale and the Impact of Event Scale‐Revised (IES‐R). 32 , 33 Both scales assess responses to experiencing a potentially stressful event and were therefore used only in follow‐up surveys.

The IGT‐AD is a 16‐item scale developed to assess the impacts of AD genetic testing specifically, asking about the frequency of specific responses during the past week. 31 The original scale's stem is worded as “… responses you may have had after receiving your genetic test results.” To allow use in both randomization groups, we modified the stem to “…how often you have felt the following about your Alzheimer's disease risk estimate” and replaced “test result” with “risk estimate” in each item (e.g., “feeling upset about my Alzheimer's disease risk estimate” instead of “feeling upset about my test result”). Responses (0 = never, 1 = rarely, 3 = sometimes, 5 = often) are summed for analysis (range 0 to 80). Twelve items assess distress (e.g., upset, sad, anxious) and four positive feelings (“feeling relieved,” “feeling happy,” “feeling my family has been supportive,” and “feeling satisfied with family communication”). Positive items are reverse‐coded, so a higher score reflects more distress. Two subscales assess distress and positive impacts separately, a higher score for the distress subscale indicating more distress and a higher score for the (reverse‐coded) positive scale indicating less positivity. Cronbach's alpha for the IGT‐AD was 0.70 to 0.75 in the three follow‐up surveys.

The IES‐R is a 22‐item screen for test‐related distress commonly used in AD research. 33 Respondents rate how distressing each item has been during the last 7 days in relation to a specific stressful event, with response options scored on a five‐point Likert scale from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely). In our study, the scale stem was “We would like to know how much you have felt distressed or bothered by receiving your estimate of risk for Alzheimer's disease.” Each item referred to “my risk evaluation” (e.g., “Any reminder brought back feelings about my risk evaluation”). As recommended, for analysis we used each participant's mean across all items. 33 For the IES‐R, Cronbach's alpha was 0.90 to 0.93 for the three follow‐up surveys.

Secondary psychological outcomes were changes from baseline in depression (PHQ‐9), 28 anxiety (GAD‐7), 29 and perceived AD threat, measured with a previously validated seven‐item scale focused on concern or worry about AD. 34 AD threat items were scored on a five‐point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree” to 5 = “strongly agree,” with 3 = “neutral”) and summed for analysis (range 7 to 35, with 35 the highest AD threat). Cronbach's alpha for the baseline survey and three follow‐up surveys was 0.84 to 0.88 for the PHQ‐9, 0.87 to 0.92 for the GAD‐7, and 0.79 to 0.83 for AD threat.

Randomization was performed on participants selected for the trial who completed the pre‐disclosure survey and was stratified by age group, gender, first‐degree family history, and ε4 allele count; all ε4 homozygotes were placed in the disclosure group due to ethical concerns about withholding potentially useful information.

Safety monitoring

Definitions of adverse events (AEs) were based on increases to the severe category for PHQ‐9 (≥20) or GAD‐7 (≥15) compared with baseline and mean IES‐R scores > 1.5 (i.e., optimal cut‐off for prediction of post‐traumatic stress disorder). 33 Any response other than “not at all” to the PHQ‐9 suicidality question was considered new‐onset suicidality and defined as a serious AE (SAE) given exclusion of participants with this response at baseline. Participants who met criteria for an AE or SAE were contacted within a week by RAL, a physician well known to community members, who assessed their current level of distress, whether it was related to the study, and whether they had support. One week later, a GC contacted them again. AEs and SAEs were also monitored twice yearly by our study's safety officer.

Statistical analysis

Our original target sample size, based on predicted accrual at each step beginning with the community survey, was 400 randomized participants and 300 (150 per arm) available for the primary analysis at the 15‐month follow‐up. 25 This sample size was estimated to provide 80% power to detect a between‐group difference of ≥0.39 standard deviations (SD) using a two‐sided type 1 error rate of α = 0.05 for each of four prespecified primary outcomes, with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons.

Due to lower‐than‐expected participation in the community survey (possibly because it was carried out near the beginning of the COVID pandemic), baseline survey accrual was lower than predicted. Consequently, the final sample size was 374 randomized participants, 240 of whom completed the 15‐month follow‐up (disclosure 124, non‐disclosure 116). The present analysis focused on two primary outcomes (IGT‐AD and IES‐R), each assessed at three time points, resulting in six primary comparisons (disclosure vs non‐disclosure). Under this revised framework, we had 80% power to detect ≥0.46 SD for each comparison. As originally planned, we applied the Hochberg step‐up procedure 35 across the six planned comparisons to control the family‐wise error rate, preserving strong control of type I error while providing greater power than Bonferroni‐based adjustment for correlated outcomes.

All analyses were carried out under an intention‐to‐treat (ITT) framework and adjusted for prespecified stratification factors: APOE genotype (ε4+ vs ε4−), age group, gender, and AD family history (first‐degree, second‐degree, none/more distant). Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations under a missing‐at‐random assumption. 36 The imputation model included all analysis variables and relevant auxiliary variables. Fifty completed datasets were generated, and parameter estimates and standard errors were pooled using Rubin's rules. As the covariate adjustment and imputation of missing data improved efficiency and reduced bias relative to complete‐case analysis, the effective detectable effect size is likely smaller than 0.46 SD.

Primary outcomes (IGT‐AD and IES‐R), not assessed at baseline, were analyzed cross‐sectionally at each follow‐up visit. Adjusted group means were estimated and compared (disclosure vs non‐disclosure) separately at each time point. The same approach was applied to IGT‐AD subscale scores.

Analyses of the three secondary outcomes focused on change from baseline to follow‐up. Longitudinal analyses used generalized linear models with an identity link, estimated via generalized estimating equations with an exchangeable working correlation structure and robust (sandwich) variance estimators to account for within‐subject correlation due to repeated measures. Models included main effects for group and time and a group‐by‐time interaction term (and all stratification variables); inference focused on the interaction term, representing the group difference in change over time. For graphical presentation of secondary outcomes, we also computed least‐square means at each time point, adjusted for stratification variables.

Prespecified subgroup analyses evaluated IGT‐AD subscales and compared ε4+ and ε4− subgroups within the disclosure arm. Genotype‐defined subgroups within the disclosure arm were also compared with the non‐disclosure arm. For analyses of subgroups and secondary outcomes, no formal multiple comparison adjustment was applied; findings were interpreted conservatively in the context of the overall pattern of results using p < 0.05 to define significance. Analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp.) and SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc.).

RESULTS

Participation and sample demographics

In response to study invitations mailed to 94,433 community residences, 5542 persons were screened for eligibility, of whom 3154 (57%) did not meet initial eligibility criteria and 301 (5%) declined to participate (Figure 1). Among those eligible, 2087 completed the baseline survey, 1423 of whom were excluded from the trial because they did not allow recontact (N = 144), did not meet additional inclusion criteria (N = 315: family history possibly consistent with early‐onset, autosomal‐dominant AD [N = 40], previous APOE genetic testing [N = 90], or suicidality [N = 185]), or did not complete additional steps required for APOE testing (Figure 1).

Among eligible baseline survey participants, those who completed APOE testing (N = 664) were younger (p = 0.01), more educated (p < 0.001), and more likely to have an AD family history (p < 0.001) than those who did not. Among those genotyped, 404 were selected for the trial, including all 194 (29%) who were ε4+ (182 heterozygous, 12 homozygous) and 210 of the 470 who were ε4−. Thirty participants did not complete the pre‐disclosure survey (16 ε4+, 14 ε4−), resulting in a final N = 374 (disclosure 194, non‐disclosure 180) (Figure 1).

Demographic characteristics were similar in disclosure and non‐disclosure participants (Table 1). Mean age was 52 years, most (69%) were women, nearly half (49%) were college graduates, most (71%) identified as either Dominican or Puerto Rican, and 43% selected Spanish in response to the question “In what language can you best and most comfortably express your feelings and thoughts?” Perceived AD risk ranged widely, with 64% of participants responding it was between 10% and 50%. Approximately half of the participants (48%) were ε4+. Thirty percent reported a first‐degree and 29% a second‐degree AD family history. At baseline, PHQ‐9 scores averaged 4.5; fewer than 6% of participants met the criteria for moderately severe or severe depression (score ≥15). GAD‐7 scores averaged 3.6; fewer than 5% met the criteria for severe anxiety (score ≥10). AD threat scores averaged 24.5 (Table 1).

For most risk categories and follow‐up times, participants’ recall of their risk estimates did not differ significantly from those they received, with all differences less than 10 percentage points (Table S1). Recalled risk was higher than the estimate received for all risk categories except 55%, where it was lower (Table S1). At the 6‐week follow‐up, recalled risk was significantly higher among disclosure, ε4+ participants (39.8%, standard deviation [SD] 19.5%, Bonferroni‐corrected p = 0.005) and significantly lower among disclosure ε4− participants (24.1%, SD 14.2%, Bonferroni‐corrected p = 0.025), compared with the non‐disclosure group (31.1%, SD 20.0%) (ANOVA). In paired t‐tests, recalled risks at 9 and 15 months did not differ significantly from those at 6 weeks.

In both disclosure groups, 94% received the allocated intervention (Figure 1). Loss to follow‐up was 11% at 6 weeks, 26% at 9 months, and 36% at 15 months, with no significant difference between disclosure arms. Loss to follow‐up for ≥1 follow‐up survey was not associated with age group, gender, education, Latino origin group, AD family history, APOE‐ε4 carrier status, or perceived AD risk at baseline (all p > 0.05).

Primary outcomes

Mean scores for primary outcomes did not differ significantly between disclosure and non‐disclosure groups at any time point (Table 2 and Figure 2) (IGT‐AD: adjusted mean difference [MD]: −1.5 [6 weeks], −1.2 [9 months], −2.0 [15 months], p = 0.14–0.07), IES‐R (MD: 0.00 [6 weeks], 0.01 [9 months], 0.03 [15 months]), p = 0.98 to 0.64).

Secondary outcomes

Compared with baseline, levels of all three secondary outcomes (PHQ‐9, GAD‐7, and AD threat) declined, with no significant differences between the disclosure and non‐disclosure groups (Table 3 and Figure 3). Within the disclosure group, the change from baseline in GAD‐7 scores at 6 weeks differed significantly according to genotype, with a decrease among ε4− participants (MD = −1.2) and an increase among ε4+ participants (MD = 0.4) (p = 0.016). These differences did not persist at later follow‐ups. Levels of perceived AD threat decreased significantly more among ε4− than ε4+ participants at each time point (p ≤ 0.0079 for each). The decrease in perceived AD threat was also significantly greater among ε4− participants in the disclosure group than among participants in the non‐disclosure group, at each time point (p ≤ 0.030 for each).

Adverse events

Thirty‐nine participants met prespecified criteria for AEs (21 non‐disclosure, 18 disclosure), and 15 for SAEs (nine non‐disclosure, six disclosure). Only five participants with AEs or SAEs (three non‐disclosure, two disclosure) attributed their psychological state to concern about receiving their risk estimate, while the rest cited personal experiences unrelated to the study or a long‐standing history of anxiety or depression.

DISCUSSION

Findings in IDEAL, a randomized controlled trial among Latino northern Manhattan residents, were similar to those of previous studies regarding the psychological impacts of APOE genotype disclosure. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Participants randomized to disclosure and non‐disclosure did not differ in any primary or secondary outcome, despite the higher risk estimates given to those in the disclosure group. AEs related to disclosure were uncommon and did not differ between disclosure groups.

In the disclosure group, levels of test‐related distress (measured by IGT‐AD) were higher in ε4+ than ε4− participants at 6 weeks but not later. Scores on the IGT‐AD distress subscale were also higher among ε4+ than ε4− disclosure participants at 6 ‐weeks and 9 months. Standards for clinical significance are not available for the IGT‐AD, but scores can be interpreted in relation to scale values (0 = never, 1 = rarely, 3 = sometimes, 5 = often). At 6 weeks, the mean IGT‐AD score (summed across 16 items) was 20.4 among ε4+ participants, for an average of 1.3 per item, which is slightly more than “rarely.” For the IGT‐AD distress subscale (12 items), mean scores were 8.8 at 6 weeks and 10.0 at 9 months among ε4+ participants, for an average of 0.7 to 0.8 per item (i.e., less than “rarely”). Thus, although values were higher in ε4+ than ε4− participants, levels of distress do not appear to be clinically significant. Unlike other studies, 9 , 11 we found no differences in scores on the IES‐R in any comparison, suggesting the IGT‐AD had better sensitivity to detect responses related specifically to AD genetic testing, as intended by its creators. 31

Similarly, while anxiety levels (GAD‐7) declined compared with baseline for ε4− participants, they increased at 6 weeks, but not at later follow‐ups, among ε4+ participants. These results are consistent with transient increases in distress observed in many studies of genetic testing impacts for AD 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 and other disorders. 37 The most striking difference between ε4+ and ε4− disclosure group participants was in perceived AD threat. 34 Compared with baseline, scores declined by three to four points among ε4− participants but declined minimally (or not at all) among ε4+ participants at all time points. Again, no standards for clinical significance are available but based on the scale values, a difference of three to four points on this scale is unlikely to be of major concern. The mean score at baseline (24.5) equates to an average of 3.5 for the seven scale items, corresponding to weak agreement (between “neutral” and “agree somewhat”) with the items scored from 1 to 5 (higher values indicating more threat). A decrease of three to four points in ε4− participants corresponds to slightly less agreement, but still in the neutral range.

The AD threat scale, developed to assess the specifically relevant construct of concern or worry about AD, may be more sensitive than depression or anxiety measures for assessing psychological responses to APOE genotype disclosure. Similarly, a recent study assessed the impact of disclosure on another highly relevant construct – AD‐related stigma – and found that levels anticipated among people who had not learned their APOE findings were higher than actual experiences reported by those who learned they were ε4− or ε4+. 38

For three of our measures, IGT‐AD, IGT‐AD positive subscale, and perceived AD threat, outcomes were significantly more favorable among ε4− disclosure participants than non‐disclosure participants. In contrast, ε4+ disclosure participants did not differ from non‐disclosure participants in any outcome measure. This pattern of favorable impacts on persons who learned they were at lower risk, without adverse impacts on those who learned they were at higher risk, suggests that non‐disclosure may carry its own adverse effect, insofar as uncertainty about risk status may maintain elevated threat perceptions. 39 For participants in the non‐disclosure group, uncertainty may have been particularly salient given that enrollment and follow‐up occurred during a period with increasing AD media coverage, ongoing enrollment in AD prevention trials, and approval of anti‐amyloid medications.

Across a range of measures of depression and anxiety, previous studies are consistent in showing no clinically significant differences related to disclosure. 9 , 10 , 11 In treatment trials for depression and anxiety, a clinically significant change has been reported as three to five points for the PHQ‐9 40 and four points for the GAD‐7. 41 The changes we observed were smaller than these thresholds for all subgroups examined, including ε4+ disclosure participants, whose scores at 6 weeks increased significantly more above baseline than those of ε4− participants but were only 0.4 points higher than baseline.

Strengths and limitations

IDEAL's most important strength is its focus on Latino participants, who have been underrepresented in studies of APOE genotype disclosure. Our randomized controlled trial design removed selection bias related to disclosure and controlled for potential confounding by demographic factors through stratified sampling and adjustment in analyses. We had longer follow‐up (15 months) than in previous work. While loss to follow‐up was substantial at final follow‐up (36%), it did not differ between disclosure groups and was not associated with demographic variables possibly related to the outcomes assessed (e.g., age group, gender, education, family history, perceived risk), providing further reassurance against selection bias. Despite the lower number randomized than originally planned (374 vs 400) (due to lower‐than‐expected participation in the initial community survey), we had good statistical power for assessing primary outcomes. Previous studies excluded persons with high scores for depression and anxiety, 9 , 11 whereas we excluded only those with evidence of suicidal ideation, allowing greater generalizability. Participants accurately recalled the risk estimates they received throughout follow‐up, with less than 10‐percentage‐point differences across all risk categories, suggesting the information was received and retained. However, as reported in REVEAL, recall of a genetics‐based risk estimate is not necessarily the same as perceived personal risk. 42

We ascertained participants by address‐based sampling of specific northern Manhattan neighborhoods rather than selecting participants with an AD family history or volunteers for a prevention trial. Previous studies varied in estimated AD risks associated with APOE genotypes among Latinos due to differences in Latino national origin groups, research settings, and other factors. 43 , 44 , 45 Therefore, to obtain appropriate risk estimates for IDEAL participants, we targeted the same New York City neighborhoods studied in WHICAP, a 30‐year longitudinal study of the genetic epidemiology of AD, 26 , 43 and carried out analyses of shared WHICAP data. 25

IDEAL's catchment area is an urban New York City community where most participants identified as Dominican or Puerto Rican, and our results may not generalize to other US Latino communities. Also, trial participants were not fully representative of Latino residents of our target communities. As we reported previously, women and college graduates were overrepresented among baseline survey completers. 25 Further, those who completed APOE testing were younger, more educated, and more likely to have an AD family history than those who did not. These selection biases suggest that adverse psychological impacts of genotype disclosure may have been underestimated in IDEAL because of preferential inclusion of people with greater health literacy, interest in personal risk assessment, and, possibly, greater resilience to the impacts of receiving a high risk estimate.

Adverse impacts were also likely diminished by our extensive pre‐test education and genetic counseling, which emphasized that having an APOE ε4 allele was not deterministic and that risk could be reduced by improving health behaviors. Participants were relatively young (age range 40 to 64, mean 52) and might not have considered AD as an imminent threat.

Since we analyzed average responses, we cannot exclude the possibility of adverse effects in some persons with high vulnerability. In our recent analysis of responses in IDEAL ε4 homozygotes, however, only one of 11 participants showed signs of elevated distress after receiving a risk estimate of 55%, 46 suggesting such responses are likely uncommon. Similarly, among 790 ε4 homozygotes who learned their genotypes during Generation 1 trial screening, fewer than 1% had clinically significant post‐test anxiety. 11

Summary and conclusions

In summary, IDEAL's findings were consistent with those of other studies that underrepresented participants who identified as Latino. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 APOE genotype disclosure did not have clinically significant adverse psychological effects and may have had beneficial effects (e.g., reduced uncertainty) for participants who learned they were at lower risk. Although findings could differ in other US Latino communities, we did not observe greater adverse psychological impacts than in previous studies, suggesting that distinct cultural values and beliefs were unlikely to have increased adverse responses. However, in IDEAL as in other studies, adverse impacts were likely mitigated by participant selection factors and the inclusion of specialized AD education and genetic counseling.

As APOE testing becomes more integrated into AD research and clinical care, understanding its psychological impact in diverse populations and settings will become increasingly important. Further research is needed on the impact of APOE disclosure in settings with limited counseling support (e.g., DTC testing and anti‐amyloid therapy contexts) and possible differences according to AD‐related risks conveyed to participants and sociodemographic factors such as gender, education, and AD family history, in addition to ethnicity. Additional research is also needed on the coping strategies employed when facing high‐risk estimates. IDEAL's qualitative interviews may help explain how participants interpreted and adapted to receiving their risk.
