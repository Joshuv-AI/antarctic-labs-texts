Contrastive adversarial mix-up learning for unsupervised cross-modality image segmentation
Authors: Yichao Shen, Pingjun Gu, Changhui Yu, Bin Yu, Haihua Yang, Minghai Shao, Xiance Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) has been extensively investigated in terms of its ability to address the performance degradation caused by distribution shifts between labeled source-domain images and unlabeled target-domain images. In radiotherapy, this problem is particularly relevant for the transfer of segmentation knowledge from planning computed tomography (CT) to cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), for which target-domain annotations are limited. This study aimed to develop a contrastive adversarial mix-up learning framework for unsupervised cross-modality image segmentation.MethodsA UDA framework integrating mix-up augmentation (MA) and contrastive adversarial learning (AL) was developed. This framework included a shared-weight redistribution network to reduce cross-domain appearance discrepancies, a mix-up consistency strategy to constrain segmentation predictions of mixed-domain images, and contrastive AL to regulate the relationship between redistributed mixture images and mixed redistributed images. The method was evaluated with two in-house CT-CBCT datasets comprising 112 patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma and 112 patients with breast cancer, with 12 patients from each dataset randomly selected for testing. Generalizability was further evaluated in 75 patients from the public Brain Tumor Segmentation 2018 (BraTS2018) dataset, with 80% used for training and 20% for testing. Segmentation performance was assessed via the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), Hausdorff distance (HD), and Jaccard index.ResultsOn the in-house CT-CBCT datasets, the complete UDA + MA + AL framework achieved DSC values of 82.08%, 85.81%, 78.60%, and 60.75% for the breast cancer clinical target volume (CTV), heart, head-and-neck cancer CTV, and parotid glands, respectively; meanwhile, compared with the UDA-only framework, the corresponding absolute DSC improvements of the complete framework were 8.70, 1.02, 3.28, and 3.04 percentage points, respectively. On the BraTS2018 dataset, the proposed framework achieved DSC values of 63.56%, 67.81%, 85.47%, and 74.16% for tasks adapting T2-weighted magnetic resonance image (T2) to T1-weighted magnetic resonance image (T1), T2 to T1 contrast enhanced, T2 to fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and FLAIR to T2, respectively. Overall, the proposed method achieved an average DSC improvement of 3.53 percentage points compared with representative UDA-based segmentation methods.ConclusionsThe proposed contrastive adversarial mix-up learning framework improved unsupervised cross-modality segmentation performance across CT-CBCT and multisequence magnetic resonance imaging adaptation tasks. By combining cross-domain redistribution, mix-up consistency learning, and contrastive AL, the framework can provide enhanced robustness for variations in image appearance and anatomical structure while maintaining segmentation consistency.Keywords: Domain adaptation, semantic segmentation, mix-up data augmentation, contrastive learning

Introduction

Linac-integrated cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) is widely used in image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) to correct setup errors before radiation (1). With the advancements in adaptive radiotherapy (ART), there is a growing need for the evaluation of anatomic changes during radiotherapy to adaptively delineate and replan based on these changes (2,3). Although a mid-radiotherapy computed tomography (CT) rescan is often scheduled for new targets and organs at risk (OARs), with contouring conducted to optimize the treatment plan, this process is inefficient and requires additional time, resources, and manual assessments (4). While on-board CBCT presents a promising solution to improving ART efficiency, its application is currently limited due to its relatively low image quality, which results from poor soft-tissue contrast and high image noise (5). Consequently, direct delineation of targets and OARs on CBCT by radiation oncologists is challenging and prone to errors (6). Deformable image registration (DIR) is often necessary to propagate contours from CT to CBCT (7,8). However, this process is further complicated by the low soft-tissue contrast and a narrow field of view (FOV) of CBCT, leading to significant manual review and editing time (9).

The lack of well-labeled training data is another obstacle to the performance of automatic segmentation with widespread deep learning (DL) methods on CBCT. Convolutional neural network (CNN)-based DL has demonstrated remarkable performance in medical image segmentation with training and test images of the same domain and similar distributions (10). However, studies have shown that the performance of DL models for direct segmentation on CBCT images is poor due tom the poor image quality and lack of well-labeled, large sets of training data (11,12). With the available of manual contours on planning CT (pCT), synthetic CBCT (sCBCT) images can be generated from pCT and mixed with a limited CBCT training dataset to improve the CBCT segmentation results (13,14). However, pairs of pCT and CBCT with contours are needed for this pseudo-CBCT generation. The generalization ability of the segmentation models is also uncertain, as there may be a distribution shift between pCT and CBCT images (15,16).

Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) has been intensively investigated as a solution to the segmentation problem of the distribution shift when CBCT without labels is used as the target domain and labeled CT is used as the source domain (15-26). Aligning the distributions between the source domain and the target domain through some form of transformation is the general objective of UDA. One popular UDA method is using feature-level transfer through focusing on learning the most representative features and inducing alignment between the source and target domains within a shared feature space (10,27-29). In order to enforce the extraction of domain-invariant features in UDA, the follow measures are frequently employed: discrepancy minimization through (I) minimizing the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) between features learned from the source and target domains across one or more adaptation layers (30,31); (II) incorporation of a domain discriminator to assess whether the learned deep features are extracted from the source or the target data, following a domain-adversarial learning (AL) strategy (21,32); and (III) the reduction of the correlation distance between features (33). Disentanglement strategies are also commonly applied to create a shared domain-invariant content space alongside a domain-specific style space (34). However, in feature-level transfer, recognizing invariant features across different domains is challenging due to the limitation in the capacity and representation power of the knowledge pool (35). Furthermore, feature-level transfer techniques entail certain challenges. These include the lack of semantic consistency through the alignment of marginal distributions, in which a mix-up augmentation (MA) might be introduced as an auxiliary measure, and the difficulty in selecting the appropriate level of deep representation for alignment, which is not easily interpretable and results in a tedious tuning process (30,36).

Studies (26,37,38) indicate that sharing the invariance of the target domain is often more important than other factors in determining transfer performance during UDA. However, undesirable invariance may negatively affect representation transfer, especially when domain alignment is performed without sufficient semantic constraints. UDA frameworks usually reduce the source-target domain gap through image-level translation, feature-level alignment, output-space AL, pseudo-label refinement, or disentangled representation learning. Although these methods have shown promise, they may entail certain drawbacks, such as semantic inconsistency, unstable adversarial optimization, or insufficient robustness under large cross-modality appearance and structural variations.

To address these limitations, we developed a contrastive adversarial mix-up learning framework for unsupervised cross-modality image segmentation. Specifically, a weight-shared symmetric redistribution subnetwork was designed to enhance the convergence of semantic knowledge between the source and target domains. Different from conventional UDA methods that mainly focus on direct distribution alignment, the proposed framework integrates MA, AL, and contrastive learning in a unified pipeline. MA is used to construct intermediate cross-domain samples between the labeled source domain and the unlabeled target domain, AL is applied to promote domain-invariant segmentation predictions, and contrastive learning is applied to regulate the redistribution process by modeling the relationship between the redistributed mixture image (Rmix) and the mixed redistribution image (mixR). In this way, the proposed framework enlarges useful cross-domain variations in image illumination and structure while preserving task-relevant segmentation consistency.

The proposed framework has been optimized with a four-stage training strategy: source-domain segmentation pretraining, source-target domain adaptation (DA) with AL, mix-up consistency learning, and contrastive adversarial mix-up learning. The proposed method was evaluated on an in-house CT-CBCT dataset and further validated on the public Brain Tumor Segmentation 2018 (BraTS2018) dataset to assess its generalizability across different cross-modality segmentation scenarios.

The primary contributions of this study are as follows:

❖We developed an end-to-end unsupervised cross-modality image segmentation framework for transferring segmentation knowledge from labeled CT images to unlabeled CBCT images. Unlike conventional UDA methods that mainly focus on image translation, feature alignment, or output-space AL, the proposed framework jointly considers cross-domain image redistribution, mix-up consistency, and adversarial segmentation alignment.

❖We devised a mix-up consistency learning strategy to construct intermediate cross-domain samples and constrain the segmentation prediction of mixed images to ensure consistency with the mixed segmentation predictions of source and target images. This design encourages the segmentation network to learn smoother and more robust decision boundaries across the source-target domain gap.

Methods

Overall design

UDA learning involves two image domains: one contains source images (Xs) with the corresponding pixel-level labels (Ys), and another one contains the target images (Xt) without ground-truth labels. Figure 1 illustrates the overview of the proposed framework, which consists of a three shared-weight network to redistribute the source, target, and mixed images (source + target; three shared-weight networks to segment the source, target, and mixed images; and three discriminators to judge the segmentation results from the source, target domain, and the mixed domains via MA in the output layer). The proposed UDA network with MA is mainly composed of a DA redistribution-segmentation network with contrastive loss-based redistribution and segmentation adversarial training. To control the illumination of redistributed mixed images and mixed redistributed images and to diverge them to the greatest reasonable degree, positive KL divergence loss (LKL) and three other types of negative structure similarity loss (LSSIM) are embedded in contrastive learning, as described in detail in section “Network and objective functions”.

Experimental setup

Initially, the segmentation model was pretrained on the source images with labels for 3,000 iterations. According to baseline source segmentation architecture, the purpose of DA was to obtain the maximum capability of redistribution. Segmentation transfer without MA was conducted for 10,000 iterations, and then mix-up consistency loss was added to segmentation for 10,000 iterations. Finally, mix-up contrastive loss was added for adversarial training for 50,000 iterations. The entire training procedure is shown in Figure 3. All experiments were implemented with Pytorch and performed with a batch size of 4 on a 3090 GPU (Nvidia, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with 24 GB of memory. The network was optimized via a stochastic gradient descent optimizer with an initial learning rate of 5×10−4 and a learning rate decay of 4×10−5.

Four-stage training strategy of the proposed framework. Stage 1 pretrains the segmentation network using labeled source-domain images. Stage 2 performs source-target domain adaptation with adversarial learning. Stage 3 introduces mix-up consistency learning for mixed-domain segmentation. Stage 4 further incorporates contrastive adversarial learning between Rmix and mixR to improve redistribution robustness and segmentation consistency. mixR, mixed redistribution image; Rmix, redistributed mixture image.

Datasets

In-house CBCT dataset

A cohort of 112 patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) with both CBCT and CT images were enrolled for DA segmentation, in which both source CT images and target-source CBCT images were meticulously delineated by a senior radiation oncologist with expertise in determining the clinical target volume (CTV) of radiotherapy for head-and-neck cancer (CTVhn) and the parotid glands. Similarly, 112 patients with breast cancer were included for CBCT and CT DA segmentation with the source CT and target CBCT annotations consisting of the CTV for radiotherapy of breast cancer (CTVb) and the heart. Twelve patients were randomly selected from both datasets as a test set. The in-house datasets were retrospectively collected between 2016 and 2023. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Taizhou Hospital (approval No. K20250861). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the analysis.

BraTS2018 public dataset

The proposed workflow was also assessed and validated for UDA brain tumor segmentation with the BraTS2018 dataset, which includes data from 75 patients used for the multimodality brain tumor segmentation challenge in 2018. Adaptation between two modalities was performed via magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of low-grade glioma cases; specifically, these included T2-weighted magnetic resonance image (T2) to T1-weighted magnetic resonance image (T1), T2 to T1 contrast enhanced (T1ce), T2 to fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and FLAIR to T2. From the dataset, 80% of the patient data were randomly selected as the training set and 20% as the test set.

The Dice similarity coefﬁcient (DSC), Hausdorff Distance (HD), and Jaccard index (Jaccard) metrics were adopted for the evaluation and comparison of the segmentation performance. For the in-house dataset, slices were cropped with a coronal plane size of 128×128 pixels. For the BraTS data, each axial slice was resized to 256×256 pixels. RefineNet and PSPNet were selected for segmentation with the in-house CBCT dataset and BraTS2018 dataset.

Results

UDA for CBCT segmentation

The influence of UDA on CBCT segmentation was assessed and compared with the following: (I) the no-adaption method, in which segmentation model was trained on the source CT images with associated annotations and tested on target CBCT images; and (II) the full supervision method, in which the segmentation model was trained on source CT images with associated annotations and tested on CT images. The DSCs for CBCT without adaption were 70.58%, 75.96%, 73.40%, and 57.87% for CTVb, heart, CTVhn and the parotids, respectively. Introducing the UDA increased the DSCs on CBCT to 73.38%, 84.79%, 75.32%, and 57.71% for the CTVb, heart, CTVhn, and parotids, respectively. Adding MA as an auxiliary branch (UDA + MA) improved the DCSs to 79.75%, 83.62% ,75.75%, and 59.56% for the CTVb, heart, CTVhn, and parotids, respectively, while the further addition of AL (UDA + MA + AL) yielded an increase of 82.08%, 85.81%, 78.60%, and 60.75%, respectively. The metrics of UDA + MA + AL were close to those of the full supervision method, whose DSCs were of 86.49%, 87.05%, 84.31%, and 72.09%, for the CTVb, heart, CTVhn, and parotids, respectively. Test results with the BraTS2018 dataset

The accuracy and feasibility of the suggested UD + MA model were further tested with the BraTS2018 dataset. The DA between T2 and T1, T2 and T1ce, T2 and FLAIR, and FLAIR and T2 was ascertained for cross-phase image segmentation. Similarly, the DSCs of the UDA model were increased to 57.96%, 59.84%, 83.05%, and 70.38% (from 50.49%, 42.97%, 76.08%, and 68.21%, respectively) as compared to the non-DA models in the T2-to-T1, T2-to-T1ce, T2-to-FLAIR, and FLAIR-to-T2 mode segmentation tasks, respectively; UDA + MA improved the DSCs of UDA to 60.04%, 59.23%, 84.25%, and 71.81%, respectively, while UDA + MA + AL provided improvements of 63.56%, 67.81%, 85.47%, and 74.16%, respectively. Comparison with other methods

Other UDA approaches, including AdaOutput, Cycle-Consistent Generative Adversarial Network (CycleGAN), Synergistic Image and Feature Adaptation (SIFA), Dual-Scheme Fusion Network (DSFN), Deep Symmetric Adaptation Network (DSAN), and disentangled learning and transfer segmentation (DLaTS), which leverage image and feature alignment for UDA and disentangled representation learning, were assessed and compared with our method [88], [89], [90], [72], [71], [34]. Overall, the UDA + MA + AL model achieved the best results in terms of both average DSC and Jaccard index. Compared with those achieved by dynamic feature selection network (DFSN) and domain adaptive segmentation network (DASN), the average DSCs achieved by UDA + MA + AL increased by 6.26%, 5.61%, and 6.57% for the T2-T1, T2-T1ce, and T2-FLAIR domains, respectively, and by 5.86%, 5.77%, and 3.68% for the DA settings, respectively; meanwhile, compared with DLaTS, UDA + MA + AL improved the average DSC by 4.21% for the T2-FLAIR DA setting, while the average DSC decreased by 0.97% for the reversed FLAIR-T2 setting. These results indicate the effectiveness of integrating MA and AL for domain shifts. A detailed comparison between UDA + MA + AL and other methods is shown in Table 2.

Ablation study

Ablation experiments on the CBCT-CT datasets and BraTS2018 dataset were conducted to verify the effectiveness of the proposed methods.

Influence of different types of contrastive loss

For the proposed diverse image augmentation, it was important to determine an optimal contrastive loss. On one hand, a shallow layer (e.g., output layer) may limit the diversity of the image augmentation; on the other hand, a deeper layer (e.g., latent layer) may cause the translated images to be too diverse and negatively affect the later segmentation [91]. The inﬂuence of contrastive learning-based MA on image segmentation was assessed using UDA + MA + AL with different types of contrastive loss on the BraTS2018 dataset. In terms of quantitative results (Table 3), UDA + MA + AL achieved the best DSCs of 63.56%, 67.81%, 85.46%, and 74.16% for the four cross-domain segmentation tasks, including T2-T1, T2-T1ce, T2-FLAIR, and FLAIR-T2, respectively.

For the proposed MA, it was crucial to determine an optimal consistency loss. Influence of the segmentation network

With the same dataset, the inﬂuence of different segmentation networks on the performance of UDA + MA + AL was investigated. PSPNet [88] achieved increases in DSCs of 63.56% and 74.16% for T2-T1 and FLAIR-T2, respectively, as compared to RefineNet [87] and DeepLab [86] in the BraTS2018 dataset; in the CBCT dataset, RefineNet achieved a higher DSC of 82.08% and 78.60% for CTVb and CTVhn, respectively, as compared to PSPNet and DeepLab. The detailed quantitative results are presented in Table 4.

Influence of the mix-up mode

The influence of mix-up in the segmentation performance was further evaluated and compared with three consistency regulation mix-up methods: Cutmix [92], Cowmix [93], and Carvemix [94]. Cowmix and Cutmix are region-based mix-up modes for cross-domain augmentation. Carvemix cuts out regions of interest (ROIs) in the source domain image to be pasted into the same position of the target image, which is sampled from a probability distribution. As shown in Table 4, Cowmix achieved the best DSCs of 82.42%, 87.78%, and 78.88% in CBCT CTVb, heart, and CTVhn, respectively, but did not produce superior results for the parotid glands.

Visual interpretability analysis

The impact of contrastive learning on DA was further investigated by comparing the results of UDA + MA + AL on Rmix and mixR with visual and quantitative assessments, as shown in Figure 6. The structural similarity index measure map (SSIM) map between Rmix and mixR showed significant differences in the structural components after the incorporation of contrastive learning. This pronounced variance suggests that contrastive loss substantially enhanced feature learning, allowing the segmentation network to better distinguish between domain-specific structures. By aligning the source and target domains more closely in terms of luminance and contrast and by emphasizing critical differences in structural features through contrastive learning, UDA + MA + AL achieved excellent performance in managing complex domain shifts with sophisticated loss strategies. It amplified the structural differences necessary for MA and effectively balanced the variance and invariance. Thus, UDA + MA + AL is a robust and interpretable solution for UDA in medical imaging.

Discussion

The aim of UDA cross-domain image segmentation is to alleviate the labeling burden for target segmentation. In this study, a mix-up-based contrastive and consistency loss adversarial UDA model was investigated to exploit the potential of mix-up images for cross-domain segmentation by minimizing consistent segmentation loss while reasonably enlarging contrastive loss for the promotion of redistribution and robustness. The proposed UDA + MA framework was intensively trained and validated with an in-house CT-CBCT dataset and a public BraTS2018 dataset with MRI. An average DSC of 82.02% and 78.60% for CTVb and CTVhn was achieved in the test cohort of the in-house CBCT and CT dataset. An average DSC improvement of 3.53% was observed with the BraTS2018 dataset in comparison with the DFSN, DASN, and DLaTS models.

CT-CBCT cross-domain segmentation is of critical clinical value for the CBCT-guided ART. Liu et al. (39) reported a mean CTV DSC of 75.17% and 82.05% with a general model and their proposed personalized DL model by fine-tuning the contours from the pCT on CBCT, respectively. In our study, a CTVhn DSC of 78.60% was achieved with the proposed UDA + MA model. By using intensity-based data augmentation, Brion et al. (40) improved the DSCs for the bladder, rectum, and prostate by 6%, 27%, and 7% on CT-CBCT cross-domain segmentation, respectively. In our study, which included mix-up adversarial augmentation, the DSCs of CTVb, CTVhn, and the parotids were improved by 8.7%, 3.12%, and 3.04%, respectively.

The proposed UDA + MA framework was further validated and compared with other methods under identical conditions in the public BraTS2018 dataset via MRI. As shown in Table 2, the UDA + MA framework achieved superior results in comparison with SIFA, DFSN, DASN, and DLaTS. SIFA is a classical model that combines feature alignment and image appearance adaptation and only requires labels for translated source-domain images (41). DFSN requires labels for both source and target domain images (42), while DASN and DLaTS only require labels for the source domain (34,43). Similarly, the proposed UDA + MA framework needs only labels on the target domain, which is more common in clinical scenarios of ART with CBCT and represents an advantage in practice.

SSIM loss was applied in the proposed framework to maintain the structural similarity between the redistributed images and the original image to improve the segmentation performance. The contrastive loss was combined with KL loss to primarily maintain the SSIM differences in the contrastive learning, which is similar to DLaST with the shared advantage of using different approaches to achieve equally satisfactory results during disentanglement learning (34). Consistency loss only works for partial or minimal improvement in datasets. Contrastive learning involves a proxy task of instance discrimination (44), which maximizes the similarity of representations from similar pairs and minimizes the similarity of representations from dissimilar pairs (45). In this study, contrastive loss provided significant enhancement in some organs or cross-modality situations, such as the parotid sand heart in CT-CBCT and brain tumors in the T2-T1 and T2-T1ce. Negative SSIM loss part was used to slightly adjust the illumination aspect of the redistributed mixture and mixed redistribution.

Although the proposed framework was validated with both the in-house CT-CBCT dataset and publicly available BraTS2018 dataset, the sample sizes from these two datasets were relatively small. Large datasets from multiple centers with various tumor sites and multiple imaging modalities are needed to further validate the feasibility and accuracy of the suggested UDA + MA model for segmentation. Moreover, testing other random data augmentation methods, such as flip and rotation, as well as examining other contrastive loss functions may prove valuable for different cross-modality scenarios.

Conclusions

In this study, a UDA + MA framework was developed for segmenting target CBCT domains without labels. A shared-weight redistribution network was applied to harmonize image intensities across different domains, reduce sensitivity to lighting variations, and improve segmentation accuracy. The introduction of mix-up consistency and contrastive MA further enhanced the framework’s performance. The combination of these techniques allows the framework to handle variations in image illumination and structure to ensure consistent and accurate segmentation results.
