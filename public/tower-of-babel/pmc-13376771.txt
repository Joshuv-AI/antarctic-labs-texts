Computational design and immunoinformatics validation of a T cell multi-epitope vaccine targeting glioblastoma stem cells
Authors: Reza Salahlou, Safar Farajnia, Nasrin Bargahi, Leila Rahbarnia, Elham Kamalkazemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioblastoma multiforme is a highly aggressive and lethal primary brain tumor in adults, with a poor prognosis and high recurrence rate driven by glioblastoma stem cells (GSCs). The primary objective of this study is to design and characterize a novel multi-epitope vaccine, Gliostem-MultiVax, targeting GSC-specific antigens to overcome tumor recurrence and treatment resistance. These biomarkers are essential for tumor initiation, progression, resistance to standard treatments, and the recurrence of cancer. Six well-established GSC-specific markers (CD133, CD44, Sox2, Nestin, CD15, and Musashi-1) were selected as antigen sources. Using comprehensive immunoinformatic tools, CD8 + and CD4 + T-cell epitopes were predicted. The epitopes were linked with appropriate linkers and combined with the adjuvant Rv3628 to construct the multi-epitope vaccine. Physicochemical characteristics, population coverage, and 2D and 3D structures of the vaccine were analyzed. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to evaluate the interaction model of the vaccine–TLR2 complex. The immune simulation to evaluate the immunogenicity and in silico cloning of the designed vaccine were conducted. Nine CD8+ T-cell and seven CD4+ T-cell epitopes demonstrating significant HLA binding, immunogenicity, antigenicity, and the ability to elicit IL-4 and IFN-γ were identified. These epitopes exhibited high affinity for HLA alleles, achieving 94.25% global population coverage. The designed Gliostem-MultiVax was antigenic, non-allergenic, hydrophilic (GRAVY: -0.426), soluble, and stable. The refined 3D model showed 96.4% residues in Ramachandran-favored regions with a Z-score of -7.53. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations revealed that the Gliostem-MultiVax-TLR2 complex is compact and stable (ΔG = − 20.4 kcal/mol, RMSD = 0.65 nm, Rg = 3.36 nm, 31 hydrogen bonds), with an optimal interaction network and flexibility. Immune simulation demonstrated strong humoral and cellular responses, including elevated IgG and IgM levels, proliferation of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, and increased production of IFN-γ and IL-12. These comprehensive findings suggest that Gliostem-MultiVax is a promising candidate for further preclinical and clinical investigation as a therapeutic vaccine against GBM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53415-5.Keywords: Glioblastoma stem cells, Gliostem-MultiVax, Immunoinformatics, T-cell epitopes, Immune simulation, Molecular dynamicsSubject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Immunology

Introduction

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is the most aggressive and treatment-resistant primary brain tumor in adults, with a dismal prognosis despite multimodal therapy including surgery, radiotherapy, and temozolomide (TMZ). A major contributor to this therapeutic resistance and inevitable recurrence is the subpopulation of glioblastoma stem cells (GSCs). These cells possess stemness, self-renewal capabilities, and treatment resistance, allowing them to sustain tumor growth despite intensive therapies. GSCs are notably resistant to conventional therapies such as chemotherapy and radiation, leading to tumor recurrence. Their resistance arises from immune evasion, metabolic alterations, and enhanced DNA repair. Moreover, GSCs contribute to GBM heterogeneity, which greatly complicates uniform therapeutic targeting1–3. A deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying GBM progression is essential for developing more effective treatments. Specifically, strategies that eliminate or reprogram GSCs hold promise for reducing therapeutic resistance, preventing recurrence, and improving patient survival. Because GSCs create a strongly immunosuppressive local microenvironment, targeting them may also enhance the efficacy of immunotherapies. Promising approaches currently under investigation include differentiation therapy, metabolic pathway inhibitors, and novel immunotherapeutic strategies4. Despite advances, key biological challenges, such as extensive cellular heterogeneity, complex tumor microenvironment, and blood-brain barrier, continue to hinder progress in both diagnosis and treatment5,6. In recent years, cancer immunotherapy, particularly multi-epitope vaccines (MEVs), has emerged as a promising strategy to overcome some of these barriers. MEVs are designed to present multiple tumor-derived epitopes simultaneously, eliciting robust, broad CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) and CD4⁺ helper T lymphocyte (HTL) responses, potentially addressing tumor heterogeneity, reducing immune escape, and improving antitumor immunity7. Interestingly, several chimeric peptide-based vaccines for GBM have advanced to clinical trials, including SurVaxM (NCT05163080), EO2401 (NCT04116658), IMA950 (NCT03665545), and SL701 (NCT02078648), demonstrating the growing interest in this approach. Targeting GSCs specifically is considered particularly attractive because eliminating or functionally impairing this resistant subpopulation may substantially reduce recurrence risk and improve long-term outcomes. Several key surface and intracellular markers define GSC identity and function in GBM, including CD133 (Prominin-1), CD44, CD15 (SSEA-1), Musashi-1 (MSI1), Nestin, and SOX2. These markers sustain stemness, drive tumor progression, and confer resistance to therapy8. CD133 promotes self-renewal, chemoresistance, and tumor growth by activating Wnt/β-catenin, Notch/Hedgehog, and JNK pathways. Its expression is strongly upregulated under hypoxia, enhancing GSC formation and GBM aggressiveness9–11. CD44, the main receptor for hyaluronic acid, is highly expressed in the invasive mesenchymal (MES) subtype of GBM. It mediates cell adhesion, migration, and invasion12. CD15 supports tumor plasticity and TMZ resistance. It frequently co-localizes with CD133 and CD44, especially under stress conditions such as hypoxia or chemotherapy12. Musashi-1 (MSI1), an RNA-binding protein, preserves GSC stemness and proliferation by stabilizing mRNAs (particularly CD44) and modifying Notch and Wnt signaling, thereby contributing to chemoresistance13. Nestin, a type VI intermediate filament, is linked to higher tumor grade, increased invasiveness, poorer prognosis, and reduced chemosensitivity across multiple malignancies, including glioma14. SOX2, a master pluripotency transcription factor, enhances GSC self-renewal and invasiveness. It promotes resistance to TMZ mainly by inducing epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and activating anti-apoptotic pathways12,15.

Despite important advances in recent years, most prior MEV designs for GBM have focused on classical tumor-associated antigens (such as EGFRvIII, IL-13Rα2, survivin, WT1)16. For targeting cancer stem cells, most commonly CD133 and/or SOX2 have been used in previous cancer vaccine designs17–19. To the best of our knowledge, no published multi-epitope vaccine construct has simultaneously targeted all six GSC-associated markers (CD133, CD44, SOX2, Nestin, CD15, Musashi-1) within a single design. In particular, CD15 and Musashi-1 have received very limited attention as epitope sources in GBM vaccine development. The advent of bioinformatics and immunoinformatics has revolutionized the design of MEVs. These approaches enable rapid, accurate, and cost-effective identification of epitopes with high HLA-binding affinity, strong immunogenicity, and low toxicity. They also allow the selection of promiscuous epitopes that bind multiple HLA alleles, overcoming population genetic heterogeneity and inducing broad anti-tumor immune responses. Overall, these in silico methods significantly reduce time and cost compared to conventional experimental approaches while improving precision20.

The systematic workflow employed in the current study, which integrates antigen selection, epitope prediction, vaccine construction, and multi-level in silico validation, is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Overall workflow illustrating the immunoinformatics methodology used in this study.

The primary objective of the present study was to design and computationally validate a novel MEV, called Gliostem‑MultiVax, targeting GSCs using reverse vaccinology and immunoinformatic approaches. Specifically, this study aimed to predict highly immunogenic, non‑toxic, and promiscuous T‑cell epitopes from six key GSC‑associated markers and to construct an MEV candidate capable of eliciting strong, broad, and multifaceted CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T‑cell responses. This strategy is designed to address HLA allelic heterogeneity and tumor escape from the immune system, ultimately reducing the risk of GBM recurrence.

Results

Detection and analysis of T lymphocyte epitopes

Altogether, 389 candidate CTL (Table S1 in Excel format) epitopes were initially predicted from the selected proteins using the NetMHCpan-4.1a tool. Following screening based on the described criteria, 93 epitopes were retained as both antigenic and immunogenic. Subsequently, the most favorable candidates were prioritized for potential vaccine development based on their predicted HLA class I binding affinity, antigenicity, and immunogenicity scores. Ultimately, the highest-ranking epitopes from each target protein, based on the integrated evaluation of these three parameters, were selected. These included two top-performing candidates from each of the CD133, Nestin, and Musashi-1 proteins, as well as one representative epitope from each of CD15, CD44, and Sox2, as listed in Table 1.

The study found strong and consistent binding between specific HTL and CTL epitopes and their corresponding HLA alleles in Gliostem-MultiVax construct, indicating their stability and potential for efficient antigen presentation and immunogenic responses, supported by negative binding affinity values. Moreover, the chosen HTL and CTL epitopes exhibited numerous hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds with the receptors (Table 3; Figs. 2, and S1).

Population coverage

The population coverage analysis of the chosen epitopes for both HLA-II and HLA-I alleles revealed that they covered 94.25% of the world’s population, with an average hit rate of 3.12. North America achieved the maximum population coverage for both HLA classes I/II epitopes (96.67%), followed by Central Africa (81.73%), Central America (47.86%), East Africa (86.7%), East Asia (94.28%), Europe (96.33%), North Africa (89.56%), Northeast Asia (78.73%), Oceania (89.26%), South Africa (72.55%), South America (82.87%), South Asia (83.79%), Southeast Asia (85.45%), Southwest Asia (75.41%), West Africa (88.72%), West Indies (94.64%). These data demonstrate that the proposed MEV encompasses a large global population, has significant recognition potential, and broad application (Table S2 and Fig. 3).

Formulation, immunogenic, and physicochemical properties analysis of the designed vaccine

The final formulation of Gliostem-MultiVax, as depicted in Fig. 4, comprises three main components: nine CTL epitopes, seven HTL epitopes, and the adjuvant Rv3628. The resulting polypeptide consists of 412 amino acid residues. The physicochemical properties of Gliostem-MultiVax were evaluated. The chemical formula of Gliostem-MultiVax was found to be C2060H3080N560O591S9. The calculated molecular weight was 45.43506 kDa, representing its mean molecular mass. The aliphatic index was 69.20, and the instability index was 33.37, consistent with thermal and structural stability. The GRAVY score of − 0.426 indicates hydrophilic properties. The antigenicity score of 0.6341 suggests it is likely immunogenic and can stimulate activation of the host immune system. The anticipated half-life is approximately 30 h in mammalian reticulocytes (in vitro), exceeding 20 h in yeast (in vivo), and surpassing 10 h in E. coli (in vivo). The safety of Gliostem-MultiVax was verified through analyses conducted with servers, which confirmed its non-allergenic and non-toxic properties. The solubility score of 0.920 indicates that the protein is soluble in Escherichia coli. Furthermore, the vaccine design showed no TM helices or signal peptides in the predicted results (Figs. S1 and S3).

Secondary structure prediction

The secondary structure composition of Gliostem-MultiVax, as predicted by the PSIPRED 4.0 server, consisted of 31.31% α-helices, 17.23% extended strands, and 48.56% random coils. (Fig. 5A). In contrast, the SOPMA server identified 29.13% α-helices, 18.69% extended strands, 4.85% beta turns, and 47.33% random coils (Fig. 5B).

Modelling, refining, and validation of the tertiary structure of the Gliostem-MultiVax construct

The protein’s tertiary structure was predicted using AlphaFold2, producing five distinct structures. The projected local distance difference test loss (pLDDT) was used to calculate per-position confidence. The tertiary structure was selected as the model, with a pLDDT score of 56.7. The structural integrity of the chosen vaccine model was enhanced by refining it with the GalaxyRefine online tool. This process resulted in five improved models, all derived from the original, unrefined model. These new models showed greater structural stability than the initial model. Based on structural characteristics, such as GDT-HA (0.9345), RMSD (0.491), MolProbity (1.342), clash value (6.2), poor rotamers (0.6), and Rama value (98.0), Model 2 exhibited superior structural metrics (Fig. 6A). This model was selected for further research. By structural validation platforms including PROCHECK, VERIFY-3D, ERRAT, and ProSA-web, the accuracy and reliability of the refined tertiary structure of the vaccine model were evaluated. The Ramachandran plot analysis showed that the structure of the vaccine protein was favorable, with 96.4% of residues in the most preferred regions, 3.3% in additionally allowed regions, 0.3% in generously allowed regions, and no residues (0.0%) in disallowed regions (Fig. 6B). The ProSA-web analysis determined a Z-score of -7.53 (Fig. 6C). Additionally, with ERRAT, the refinement model achieved an overall quality factor of 92.593 (Fig. 6D). The VERIFY-3D analysis revealed enhanced accuracy in the refined model, achieving a score of 84.95% (Fig. 6E). Protein flexibility is crucial for immune interactions, and studying residue surface fluctuations and contact patterns can provide essential insights into the structural stability of the vaccine. The contact map illustrates the interactions between distinct residues in the final vaccine structure. The blue color intensity (from light to dark) indicates the frequency or strength of these interactions, with darker dots indicating more frequent or fundamental contacts, while lighter dots indicate flexible regions such as loops (Fig. 7A). The positional fluctuations of each amino acid in the designed vaccine were depicted by the RMSF plot, where residue 283 had the lowest RMSF at 0.297 Å and residue 309 had the highest at 4.736 Å (Fig. 7B). The occurrence of fluctuations in the vaccine model provides a substantial level of flexibility, supporting its potential as an effectively designed Gliostem-MultiVax.

Molecular docking analysis of Gliostem-MultiVax with TLR2

Molecular docking of the Gliostem-MultiVax construct with TLR2 was conducted using the ClusPro 2.0 server, which generated 30 potential models of the Gliostem-MultiVax -TLR2 complex, organized by their binding energies. The model with a binding energy of − 1217.7 kcal/mol was chosen as the optimal complex for subsequent analysis, indicating the highest binding affinity among the generated clusters (Fig. 9A). These complex exhibits significant molecular interactions, including salt bridges, hydrogen bonds, and non-bonded contacts. We analyzed these interactions utilizing the PDBsum program to improve our understanding of the relationship between Gliostem-MultiVax and the receptor in the docked complex. The analysis showed that the vaccine can form 31 hydrogen bonds (Table S2), three salt bridges (ASP160-ARG14, GLU374-ARG242, LYS422-ASP99), and 280 non-bonded contacts with TLR2 (TLR2-Gliostem-MultiVax) (Fig. 9B). Using the PRODIGY server, the Gibbs free energy (ΔG) was calculated to be -20.4 kcal/mol, along with a dissociation constant (KD) of 1e-15 for the docked complex, thus suggesting a high binding affinity and energetic feasibility of the interaction between the Gliostem-MultiVax candidate and the TLR-2 receptor. Using NMA analysis, the Gliostem-MultiVax–TLR2 complex’s dynamic motions and structural flexibility were described. The complex has distinct regions of deformability, with prominent peaks in flexibility at the chain hinges, which most likely have a significant impact on modulating immune system engagement (Fig. 10A). Analysis of the Factor B complex shows overall structural stability, with low flexibility in the core region (approximately from 0 to 500), and higher flexibility at the termini (around 700–900), suggesting a stable interaction suitable for immune activation (Fig. 10B). Additionally, the computed eigenvalue of 1.956781e − 05 for the docked complex indicates that less energy is required for deformability, implying a more stable and flexible binding conformation as well as effective activation of the immune system (Fig. 10C). The variance plot illustrates mode 1, characterized by a low eigenvalue and high variance contribution, indicating large-scale, flexible motion that facilitates stable engagement with the TLR2. Modes 2–5 account for significant variance, reflecting adaptive flexibility. Modes 10–20, characterized by minimal variance and localized motions, offer structural rigidity for stability without compromising functional dynamics (Fig. 10D). The covariance map reveals a balance between flexible portions (high covariance, red clusters), which are crucial for docking flexibility, and stiffer segments (low covariance, blue spots), which provide structural stability (Fig. 10E). Ultimately, the elastic network model corroborated these results, demonstrating strong interaction networks that preserve structural integrity while permitting essential conformational flexibility in the complex (Fig. 10F).

Molecular dynamics simulation

A molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was performed to gain deeper insight into the structural dynamics, stability, and dynamic interactions of the docked Gliostem-MultiVax-TLR2 complex. The RMSD analysis reveals a steady rise throughout the 200 ns simulation, reaching stability near 90 ns, with an average RMSD of 0.648592 nm, indicating notable conformational changes and equilibrium achieved around 90 ns (Fig. 11A). The study also analyzed the docked complex using RMSF to assess fluctuations at the residue level. Minimal fluctuations were found during the simulation period, attributed to the vaccine’s adjustment to the ideal docking site, without impacting its stability or binding conformation. Additionally, the docked complex was evaluated for SASA, a key parameter for assessing protein surface hydrophobicity. The average SASA for the docked complex was calculated to be 450.33 nm/\S2\N (Fig. 11B). The SASA profile for the complex revealed a modest decline, suggesting that the hydrophobic core became progressively less solvated during unfolding. This reduction in solvation contributed to the protein’s enhanced stability. The Rg is an essential parameter for evaluating molecular compactness, especially in proteins. The Rg of the docked complex initially decreased, stabilizing after approximately 40 ns and consistently maintaining an average value of 3.3641 nm, indicating that the Gliostem-MultiVax-TLR2 complex maintained structural stability and compactness throughout the simulation (Fig. 11C). The analysis of hydrogen bonds reveals that the complex forms an average of approximately 11 dynamic hydrogen bonds throughout the simulation, with fluctuations indicating transient but sustained interactions that are essential for preserving structural stability, specificity, and favorable binding affinity of the complex (Fig. 11D).

Immune response simulation of the Gliostem-MultiVax

The simulation reveals that repeated antigen exposures elicit increasingly stronger and faster antibody responses, beginning with a primary IgM response, followed by enhanced secondary and tertiary responses marked by IgG-mediated immune memory, including heightened levels of IgG + IgM, IgM, and IgG1 + IgG2, which facilitate rapid antigen clearance (Fig. 12A). Following vaccine injection, the B-cell population increased significantly, including plasma and memory B cells, indicating effective B-cell activation, differentiation, and maturation. This was followed by increased antibody production, demonstrating the strength and durability of the vaccine-induced immunological response (Figs. 12B, C, and D). The analysis reveals a peak in CD4 T-helper and CD8 T-cytotoxic lymphocyte activity, followed by a decline and stabilization. CD4 cells exhibit a stronger response, while CD8 cells transition to a balanced state between active and resting phases. Memory Th and Tc development is observed (Figs. 12E, F, G, and H). Additionally, notable elevations in natural killer (NK) cells, along with sustained and enhanced growth of macrophages and dendritic cells (DCs), were observed throughout Gliostem-MultiVax administration (Figs. 12I, K, and L). Further analysis revealed heightened expression of IL-2 and IFN-γ, key pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 12M). These results might help elicit a robust immune response targeting cancer stem cells.

Discussion

Glioblastoma, one of the most aggressive forms of brain cancer, is distinguished by high levels of cellular heterogeneity, which poses a major challenge for effective treatment. New research indicates that a subset of CSCs drives tumor progression. These cells are indispensable for sustaining tumor growth, promoting angiogenesis, aiding invasion and metastasis, and developing resistance to therapies1,21. According to recent research, GBM patients significantly overexpress the proteins CD133, SOX2, CD44, Musashi-1, Nestin, and CD15 when compared to control cohorts. This highlights the potential role of these proteins as both therapeutic targets and diagnostic biomarkers for GBM8,22. In GBM, CD44 is more frequently associated with later-stage, invasive, and therapy-resistant cells. In contrast, markers such as CD133, SOX2, Nestin, Musashi-1, and CD15 are primarily expressed during the early, stem-like, and proliferative phases of tumor development23–25. In response to environmental stressors like hypoxia or chemotherapy, GBM stem-like cells dynamically switch between CD133⁺ and CD44⁺ states, demonstrating their remarkable plasticity. Furthermore, the functional roles and therapeutic relevance of these markers are strongly influenced by their highly heterogeneous expression patterns, which vary across tumor microenvironments and GBM subtypes23. CD133, CD44, and CD15 are membrane-exposed or cell-surface antigens that enable direct immune recognition by antibodies and effector cells, whereas Musashi-1, Nestin, and SOX2 are intracellular proteins that are efficiently processed for MHC class I presentation. This combination addresses intra-tumoral heterogeneity and targets the therapy-resistant GSC population26.

The selected antigens demonstrate high sequence conservation across GBM datasets and patient-derived GSC populations, confirmed by genomic and proteomic analyses27. They are significantly overexpressed in GSCs compared with normal neural stem/progenitor cells, with CD133+ cells constituting about 15–16% in GBM versus 2% in the normal brain28. This conservation and selective overexpression decrease the risk of immune escape due to mutational variability. Aberrant glycosylation, particularly of CD133 and CD44, is more prevalent in the hypoxic tumor microenvironment and may generate neoepitope-like structures or alter MHC presentation23,29. The selected antigens were individually evaluated and have demonstrated immunogenicity in both preclinical and clinical studies. CD133-specific epitopes elicited robust anti-GSC responses through mRNA-DC vaccines and CAR T-cell strategies, enhancing survival in orthotopic GBM models17. It has been shown that a bispecific CAR-T construct effectively targets CD44 and CD15, whereas peptides derived from SOX2, Nestin, and Musashi-1 elicit robust CTL responses in DC-based and MEV systems, demonstrating clinical safety and immunological efficacy in glioma patients30–32. Finally, these tumor-enriched features allow for immunological differentiation between GSCs and normal cells and were selected as suitable vaccine targets and promising antigenic candidates for epitope prediction to inhibit GBM progression and activate the immune system. The integration of reverse vaccinology and immunoinformatics in vaccine development represents a rapidly advancing area of research. Numerous candidates are presently advancing through clinical trial phases, and vaccines developed using these epitope-focused approaches have demonstrated encouraging in vivo efficacy and protective immune responses33,34. Numerous studies have reported encouraging outcomes in the development of MEVs targeting various cancers, including breast cancer, acute myeloid leukemia, colon cancer, and GBM cancer35–37. Peptide-based vaccines are gaining popularity due to their safety, Cost-effectiveness, and ability to elicit specific cancer immune responses38. Immunogenic peptides are presented by HLA class I and II molecules, leading to the activation of CTL and HTL cells, respectively. This dual presentation plays a role in both cellular and humoral immune responses. This study focuses on selecting suitable epitopes for eliciting robust cellular immunity, specifically CD4+ and CD8+ immunogenic epitopes. The CTL and HTL epitopes were carefully screened for immunogenicity, population coverage, antigenicity, IL-4 induction, and IFN-inducing capacity. Epitopes’ binding to HLA alleles is crucial for triggering an effective immune response. Docking simulations revealed robust interactions between the selected T-cell epitopes and HLA alleles, indicating efficient HLA-mediated presentation and subsequent immune activation. At the same time, different linkers were used to connect the chosen top 9 CTL and 7 HTL epitopes to make the final vaccine. Despite the IEDB tool’s reported 94.25% global population-wide epitope coverage, factors such as tumor loss of heterozygosity (LOH) and HLA allele bias can significantly reduce effective coverage in cancer patients. Reduced epitope presentation results from LOH at HLA loci, which is seen in 15–41% of GBM patients39. Because of its inherent intra-tumor heterogeneity, which includes a variety of tumor cell populations, GSCs, and tumor microenvironmental factors that impair cancer hallmark functions, GBM exhibits aggressive behavior. Tumor heterogeneity can be analyzed longitudinally over time or spatially at a single point in time. Tumor subclones often exhibit variable expression of the target antigen, potentially allowing antigen-negative strains to evade the immune system40. MEVs, such as Gliostem-MultiVax, can effectively address this problem by targeting multiple epitopes, thereby enhancing immune responses and coverage in diverse tumor populations.

It has been shown that RV3628 is an effective adjuvant that activates DCs via TLR2. This activation enhances co-stimulatory molecule expression, cytokine production (IL-12p70 and IFN-γ), and antigen cross-presentation to CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, leading to strong Th1-biased immunity and tumor growth inhibition across various preclinical models. It offers advantages over established adjuvants such as flagellin, CpG, and β-defensins by promoting DC maturation, having a favorable safety profile (very low toxicity, no significant inflammation), and effectively activating human DCs, making it a promising candidate for cancer vaccines41,42. The engineered vaccine structure exhibited key features of an effective immunogen: it was non-allergenic, non-toxic, antigenic, and highly soluble, suggesting it could elicit a strong immune response without causing harmful allergic side effects42–45. Its suitability as a multi-epitope vaccine candidate is further supported by its physicochemical characteristics. Strong hydrophilic interactions, remarkable thermal stability, and a low molecular weight (less than 100 kDa) make the construct ideal for formulation and biological activity46–48.

Additionally, the secondary structure analysis of the designed vaccine revealed a prominent random-coil configuration, which is essential for enhancing protein flexibility and promoting efficient antibody binding49. The flexibility of proteins enhances the effectiveness of multiepitope vaccines by exposing a wider range of epitopes and improving antibody binding, thereby increasing binding affinity and supporting an effective immune response essential for vaccine efficacy. The 3D structure of the Gliostem-MultiVax was successfully constructed and refined, demonstrating a high-quality, well-folded structure suitable for immunoinformatics and experimental validation, as indicated by Ramachandran plot analysis. The vaccine’s structural adaptability, with a stable core architecture and flexible loop regions, supports its potential to trigger broad and effective immunological responses, as demonstrated by RMSF and contact map analyses.

TLR activation enhances immune cell activity, including DCs, NK cells, and T cells. DCs initiate adaptive immune responses, while T cells promote maturation and antigen presentation. NK cells enhance cytotoxicity, directly killing tumor cells. T cells, particularly CD8+ T cells, eliminate cancer cells through activation, proliferation, and cytotoxic activity50–52. A thorough molecular docking study revealed that the vaccine’s strong binding affinity for TLR2 is enabled by a variety of stabilizing interactions, including numerous hydrophobic contacts, salt bridges, and hydrogen bonds.

The Gliostem-MultiVax-TLR2 complex, characterized by its core rigidity and hinge-region flexibility, provides stable and adaptive immune engagement, supported by low eigenvalue and covariance patterns, thereby ensuring effective immune activation.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were employed to confirm the Gliostem-MultiVax–TLR2 complex’s dynamic behavior and structural integrity. The simulation results showed minimal structural fluctuations and consistent intermolecular interactions, confirming the formation of a stable complex. These results confirm the molecular stability of the MEV in a cellular environment and its potential ability to stimulate an immune response.

In silico immune simulations using C-ImmSim suggest that the Gliostem-MultiVax construct has the potential to elicit robust humoral and cellular immune responses, including B- and T-cell activation, memory cell formation, and the production of key pro-inflammatory cytokines. The simulation showed persistent elevations in relevant immune cell populations and cytokines following repeated exposures, which are desirable features for a cancer vaccine candidate.

Gliostem-MultiVax is proposed as an adjuvant immunotherapy vaccine targeting GSCs to address resistance and recurrence in GBM. It complements standard care in newly diagnosed GBM (after surgery, during/after chemoradiotherapy and TMZ) to boost immunity and lower recurrence risk, and in recurrent GBM (where options are limited), ideally combined with checkpoint inhibitors or re-irradiation to overcome immunosuppression. This adjunctive approach aligns with trials such as SurVaxM (with TMZ in newly diagnosed) and DCVax-L (in both newly diagnosed and recurrent settings)53,54.

The GC and CAI content values of the vaccine construct indicate efficient expression in the E. coli K-12 strain, supporting recombinant production after codon optimization and in silico cloning for large-scale production. The Computational findings indicate that Gliostem-MultiVax has potential for selective targeting of GSCs. However, comprehensive laboratory validation, including in vitro and in vivo experiments, is necessary to substantiate these initial findings.

Limitations and future directions in T-cell vaccine design

A major challenge for T-cell-based cancer vaccines is overcoming immune tolerance, which limits the strength and quality of vaccine responses and carries a risk of autoimmunity. Central tolerance in the thymus deletes high-avidity self-reactive T cells through negative selection, leaving gaps in the repertoire against non-mutated tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) such as CD133, CD44, Nestin, SOX2, CD15, and Musashi-1, which are targeted in this study. The remaining low-avidity T cells typically fail to generate strong cytotoxic responses, contributing to the limited clinical success of TAA-targeted vaccines55–57.

Peripheral tolerance further suppresses vaccine-induced responses through several mechanisms, including T-cell anergy (inactivation due to lack of co-stimulation), peripheral deletion, and suppression by regulatory T cells (Tregs) in tumor-draining lymph nodes and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. These processes are especially relevant for T-cell-based vaccines, as repeated antigen exposure may reinforce tolerance rather than effectively prime effector T-cell responses58.

Autoimmunity is an additional concern when targeting TAAs, which are expressed in normal tissues; disrupting tolerance may cause off-target tissue damage, as seen in some immunotherapy trials. By comparison, neoantigens arising from tumor-specific mutations evade central tolerance, induce high-avidity T cells with minimal cross-reactivity to normal tissues, and are more immunogenic with a lower risk of autoimmunity56.

Various strategies are used to overcome these limitations, including: (a) selection of peptide epitopes predicted to exhibit favorable immunogenicity, while minimizing potential cross-reactivity. (b) Use of immunostimulatory adjuvants to enhance co-stimulation, promote dendritic cell maturation, and shift the immune response toward immunogenic rather than tolerogenic pathways54. (c) Potential for combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD-1) to prevent T-cell exhaustion and anergy, improve cell migration, and synergize with vaccine-induced priming, potentially enhancing intracranial penetration and persistence of effector cells54,59.

Blood-brain barrier (BBB) constraints. The BBB and blood-tumor barrier (BTB) significantly hinder the efficacy of immunotherapy in GBM, particularly for CSC-targeted vaccinations. The tumor core often shows partial disruption of the BBB, whereas the infiltrative tumor margins, rich in GSCs, usually retain an intact BBB, thereby protecting CSC populations and leading to local recurrence post-treatment. Additionally, brain endothelial cells express high levels of efflux transporters, such as P-glycoprotein and ABCG2, which reduce uptake of various immunotherapeutics, including vaccines and immune cells, into the central nervous system. Strategies such as focused ultrasound show promise for enhancing permeability60.

CNS immune privilege and specialization. The CNS is not isolated but is actively specialized/regulated, with tight BBB control, limited lymphatic drainage, low HLA expression, and suppressive neural-immune crosstalk. In GBM, this fosters an immunosuppressive niche with infiltrating yet dysfunctional immune cells, thereby limiting the efficacy of immunotherapy compared with peripheral tumors61.

Tumor-associated macrophages/microglia (TAMs) dominate. TAMs represent 30–50% of the GBM mass, microglia + bone marrow-derived macrophages, heterogeneous, M2-like immunosuppressive, and with spatial roles, for example, invasion at margins, angiogenesis in the core. Single-cell/spatial studies highlight subsets that drive therapy resistance and a “cold” TME. Targeting holds promise, for instance, with CSF1R inhibition62. GBM-induced T-cell exhaustion in CD8 + T cells is severe and driven by chronic stimulation, inhibitory receptors (PD-1, TIM-3), metabolic dysfunction, and TME suppression (such as IL-10 and TGF-β from TAMs). Epigenetic programs (like TOX) limit the efficacy of checkpoint blockade. Progenitor subsets offer opportunities for reversal63,64. GBM cells downregulate HLA class I expression (via epigenetic/Wnt/β-catenin/and STAT3 signaling) and impair cross-presentation, thereby evading T-cell recognition/priming. Low neoantigen load and immunogenicity further limit the efficacy of vaccines and CAR-T cells. Restoration strategies, including HDAC inhibitors and other epigenetic modulators, are under investigation to improve immunotherapy responses65,66.

Vaccine-induced immune responses are primarily generated systemically. Activated CD8+ and CD4+ T cells (especially vaccine-induced antigen-specific ones) can traffic to different parts of the body, including the CNS, via meningeal lymphatics, disrupted BTB regions, and even relatively intact BBB areas, through integrin-mediated adhesion and chemokine gradients (such as the CXCL9/10–CXCR3 axis)67,68.

Despite these limitations and challenges, several promising strategies offer significant opportunities to enhance the efficacy of T-cell-based vaccines in GBM. Personalized multi-peptide vaccines that combine patient-specific neoantigens and TAAs may provide broader tumor coverage and reduce the risk of antigen escape. This strategy promotes effective T cell recognition of heterogeneous tumor cells, reduces tumor burden, and is expected to enhance intratumoral CD8 + T cell infiltration and survival in patients with newly diagnosed GBM when designed using exome sequencing, transcriptome analysis, and HLA immunopeptidomics69,70.

It has shown that long-length peptides allow appropriate APC processing, cross-presentation on MHC I and MHC II molecules. In addition, both CD4⁺ helper and CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells are primed concurrently with the reduction of T-cell sequestration at the vaccination site71,72. In the other hand, advanced vaccine adjuvants can significantly increase innate immunity, dendritic cell maturation, and antitumor T-cell responses, especially TLR agonists (such poly-ICLC and CpG-ODN) and STING agonists73–75. Effective priming of CD4⁺ helper T cells, which are essential for maintaining CD8⁺ T-cell activity, preventing exhaustion, and mediating cytotoxicity against MHC-deficient tumor cells76.

Combining vaccines with immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as anti-PD-1) is another strategy that can counteract the vaccine-induced increase in inhibitory pathway expression77. Additionally, oncolytic viruses like DNX-2401 can transform the “cold” glioblastoma (GBM) tumor microenvironment into a “hot” one by inducing immunogenic cell death and releasing antigens78. Furthermore, multiphase regimens that combine chemotherapy, oncolytic viruses, and subsequent dendritic cell vaccination may further amplify antitumor immunity76. Looking ahead, future research should prioritize larger randomized controlled trials, the identification of predictive biomarkers, and the optimization of both manufacturing processes and timing for personalized vaccines.

Conclusions

The activation of the immune system is crucial for controlling and eliminating cancer cells. Peptide epitope vaccines have been developed as a novel strategy in cancer immunotherapy to elicit specific immune responses against tumor antigens. The present study demonstrates how immunoinformatic approaches were applied to construct an MEV and fully characterize its immunogenicity, antigenicity, non-allergenicity, and stability, thereby eliciting robust immunogenic responses to GSCs. The construct encodes CD4+ and CD8+ epitopes derived from six distinct proteins associated with GBM development. Structural modeling and molecular docking indicate that the vaccine construct exhibits favorable interactions with TLR2 and stability, suggesting it would have reasonable immunogenic potential. Moreover, immunological simulation data showed favorable activation of B and T cells, as well as increased cytokine production, indicating the vaccine’s capacity to elicit a long-lasting immune response. The results presented here demonstrate the potential of computational vaccine design as a rapid and cost-effective method for targeting GSCs, which are less susceptible to standard treatments. Nevertheless, given that these conclusions based on in silico predictions, which involve immune simulations with tools like C-ImmSim that estimate immune dynamics yet fail to fully address in vivo complexities (such as host variability, immune tolerance, and tumor heterogeneity), they are still considered as preliminary and demand comprehensive experimental validation of safety and effectiveness in both in vitro and in vivo settings to verify their therapeutic potential. The encouraging findings of this investigation provide a solid foundation for further assessment.

Methodology

Retrieval and selection of target protein sequences

The amino acid sequences of the target proteins CD15 (UniProt: P22083), CD44 (UniProt: P16070), CD133 (UniProt: O43490), Nestin (UniProt: P48681), Sox2 (UniProt: P48431), Musashi-1 (UniProt: O43347), and the adjuvant Rv3628 (UniProt: P9WI55) were sourced from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/) in FASTA format for subsequent analysis79. We selected the topological domains of CD133 and CD44, specifically their extracellular regions, and the luminal domain of CD15 for further investigation.

Identification and evaluation of HLA class I epitopes

We employed NetMHCpan-4.1a (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/NetMHCpan-4.1a/), a tool based on advanced artificial neural networks (ANNs), to accurately predict various CTL epitopes by forecasting peptide binding to well-characterized HLA class I alleles. The binding targets were the 12 most common HLA I alleles in humans (HLA-B*07:02, HLA-B*08:01, HLA-B*27:05, HLA-B*39:01, HLA-B*40:01, HLA-B*58:01, HLA-B*15:01, HLA-A*02:01, HLA-A*01:01, HLA-A*03:01, HLA-A*26:01, and HLA-A*24:02)80. On this server, the algorithm achieved optimal sensitivity for epitope identification by setting weak- and strong-binder thresholds at 2% and 0.5%, respectively, ensuring accurate detection of both high- and low-affinity epitopes. Following that, Vaxijen 2.0 (https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/vaxijen/VaxiJen/VaxiJen.html) predicted epitope antigenicity; only epitopes with antigenicity greater than 0.5 will be considered in the subsequent screening phase81. To assess the immunogenicity of CTL epitopes, the Class I Immunogenicity tool (http://tools.iedb.org/immunogenicity/) from the IEDB was used to predict their capacity to elicit an immune response, selecting only those with a positive immunogenicity score (greater than 0)82. For vaccine construction, the IEDB TepiTool (http://tools.iedb.org/tepitool/) utilized the netMHCpan algorithm to predict and validate HLA class I allele binding to CTL epitopes, specifically those with an IC50 ≤ 500 nM, indicating moderate to strong binding83.

Identification and evaluation of HLA class II epitopes

The NetMHCIIpan 4.1 EL method was used to predict 15-mer HTL epitopes from the selected proteins using the IEDB HLA II binding server (http://tools.iedb.org/mhcii/). Predictions were performed for the following human HLA II alleles: DRB1*0301, DRB1*0101, DRB1*0401, DRB1*0405, DRB1*0802, DRB1*0701, DRB1*1101, DRB1*0901, DRB1*1501, DRB1*1302, and DRB1*1201. Epitopes with a percentile rank ≤ 1% were initially selected84. To improve prediction reliability, all identified epitopes were cross-validated using NetMHCIIpan 4.3 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/NetMHCIIpan-4.3/), which employs an ANN algorithm. In this tool, epitopes with a percentile rank below 1% are classified as strong binders85. Only epitopes commonly predicted as binders by both tools were retained for downstream analysis. Epitope antigenicity was evaluated using VaxiJen v2.0 (https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/vaxijen/VaxiJen/VaxiJen.html). Only epitopes with a score > 0.5 were considered further81.

Epitopes were assessed for their potential to induce IFNγ using the IFNepitope tool (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/ifnepitope/design.php) (hybrid Motif + SVM approach) with the model for predicting IFN-γ versus non-IFN-γ and IFN-γ versus other cytokines86 and IL-4 using the IL4pred server (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/il4pred/predict.php) (threshold score ≥ 0.2) 87. Epitopes predicted to be positive inducers of both IFN-γ and IL-4 were strongly preferred, as these cytokines play complementary roles in enhancing antitumor immunity, antigen presentation, and T/B cell activation88,89.

Population coverage analysis of the selected epitopes

Using the IEDB population coverage tool (http://tools.iedb.org/population/), the identified CTL and HTL epitopes and their associated HLA alleles were analyzed for population coverage to assess their potential immunogenic reach across diverse populations90. The study employed default settings and encompassed the entire global population. The assessment included both HLA class I and class II alleles.

Docking of T-cell epitopes

Molecular docking simulations were used to assess the interactions and binding affinities of selected CTL and HTL epitopes with their respective HLA class I and class II alleles. The RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) provided experimentally determined crystal structures for the following HLA alleles: HLA-B*58:01 (PDB ID: 7WZZ), HLA-A*24:02 (PDB ID: 7MJA), HLA-A*02:01 (PDB ID: 7UM2), HLA-B*27:05 (PDB ID: 6VQD), HLA-B*40:01 (PDB ID: 6IEX), HLA-B*07:02 (PDB ID: 5EO0), HLA-DRB1*01:01 (PDB ID: 1AQD), and HLA-DRB1*04:01 (PDB ID: 1J8H). For HLA-DRB1*11:01, HLA-DRB1*07:01, and HLA-DRB1*13:02, for which no experimental structures were available, homology models were constructed using the SWISS-MODEL server (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/)91. Molecular docking studies were performed using the HPEPDOCK 2.0 server (http://huanglab.phys.hust.edu.cn/hpepdock/) to predict protein-peptide binding interactions92. The top-ranked docking poses were then inspected using UCSF ChimeraX v1.8. Binding free energies (ΔG) and dissociation constants (Kd) were calculated utilizing the P-RODIGY server (https://rascar.science.uu.nl/prodigy/)93. Additionally, the LigPlot + v.2.2 program served to examine the docking findings94. Detailed docking parameters are summarized in Table S3.

Design of multi-epitope vaccine construct

To develop a highly immunogenic MEV, key components, including epitopes, linkers, and adjuvant, are precisely integrated into the vaccine construct. The design incorporates CTL and HTL epitopes derived from selected proteins. CTL epitopes are linked using AAY linkers to ensure optimal presentation to the immune system, while HTL epitopes are linked with GPGPG linkers to enhance stability and functionality95,96. An adjuvant, the Mycobacterium tuberculosis Rv3628 protein, a soluble class I pyrophosphatase, and a selective TLR2 agonist, is added to the construct’s N-terminus via an EAAAK linker to increase immunogenicity and stimulate dendritic cell activation41. TLR2 was chosen as the adjuvant target because it plays a key role in activating dendritic cells (DCs) and microglia in the GBM microenvironment. TLR2 stimulation (via HMGB1 from degraded tumor cells) enhances DC infiltration, maturation, antigen presentation, and CD8+ T-cell responses, leading to tumor regression and memory in GBM models97,98. Other TLRs, such as TLR4, often exhibit inconsistent or pro-tumor effects in GBM99, whereas TLR7/8 and cGAS-STING are less relevant to vaccine potentiation in the brain TME, despite showing promise in certain immunotherapy contexts100,101. Thus, TLR2 offers the strongest rationale for boosting anti-GBM vaccine immunity.

Evaluation of physicochemical, antigenicity, and allergenicity properties of the designed vaccine

A detailed evaluation was performed to examine the immunological and physicochemical properties of the vaccine, designated Gliostem-MultiVax, which was generated by fusing selected epitopes with appropriate linkers and an adjuvant sequence. The essential physicochemical characteristics of Gliostem-MultiVax, including theoretical isoelectric point (pI), molecular weight (MW), instability index (II), aliphatic index (AI), grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY), and half-life, were thoroughly calculated and analyzed using the ExPASy ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/)102. The SOLuprot v1.0 server (https://loschmidt.chemi.muni.cz/soluprot/) was employed to evaluate the solubility of the vaccine construct’s protein expression in E. coli, with a predictive accuracy of 58.5%103. This implies high solubility potential, which is necessary for effective purification and manufacturing. Additionally, the VaxiJen v2.0 (https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/vaxijen/VaxiJen/VaxiJen.html) tool was employed to assess antigenicity, achieving 70–89% accuracy at a 0.5 threshold, independent of sequence alignments, and tailored to the target organism81. Furthermore, both the AlgPred 2.0 tool (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/algpred2/batch.html)104, utilizing a hybrid approach (RF+BLAST+MERCI) at a 0.5 threshold, and AllerTOP v2.1 (https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/allertop_test/)105 were applied to assess the allergenicity of the MEV. Toxicity assessment was conducted using ToxinPred 2.0 (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/toxinpred2/batch.html), which employed a hybrid RF+BLAST+MERCI approach with a 0.6 threshold106. Finally, the vaccine construct was analyzed for signal peptides and transmembrane (TM) helices using the SignalP 6.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP-6.0/) and DeepTMHMM 1.0 servers(https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/DeepTMHMM-1.0/), respectively107,108.

Secondary structure prediction

Protein secondary structure is made up of localized folding patterns that are created by interactions between atoms in its backbone. These patterns include random coils, β-turns, Extended strands, and α helices. The online programs PSIPRED 4.0 (https://bioinf.cs.ucl.ac.uk/psipred/) and SOPMA (https://npsa.lyon.inserm.fr/cgi- bin/npsa_automat.pl? page=/NPSA/npsa_sopma.html) were utilized to predict and evaluate the secondary structure of the proposed vaccine. The PSIPRED method has achieved an impressive accuracy rate of 84.2% in its secondary structure prediction findings109. The SOPMA method achieved a prediction accuracy exceeding 80%, delivering highly reliable results110.

Modelling, refining, and validation of the tertiary structure of the vaccine construct

The AlphaFold2 platform (https://colab.research.google.com/github/sokrypton/ColabFold/blob/main/AlphaFold2.ipynb) was employed to predict the tertiary structure of the vaccine candidate. This is a novel machine learning method that uses multi-sequence alignments to integrate physical and biological insights into protein structure111. The highest-ranked predicted 3D structure was further optimized using the GalaxyRefine service (https://galaxy.seoklab.org/cgi-bin/submit.cgi?type=REFINE) and its MD simulation approach to enhance its accuracy and quality112. Subsequently, the accuracy of the refined 3D vaccine model was validated by applying several bioinformatics tools available on the SAVES v6.1 platform (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/), including ERRAT, PROCHECK, and VERIFY-3D, to comprehensively assess the model’s structural integrity and stereochemical characteristics. ERRAT evaluates model quality by analyzing non-bonded interactions between atom types; higher scores indicate more reliable structures, typically exceeding 80113. The PROCHECK analyzes bond lengths, angles, dihedral angles, and Ramachandran plot statistics to evaluate protein stereochemical quality, identifying over 90% of residues in most favored areas114. VERIFY-3D evaluates 3D structure compatibility with the amino acid sequence by comparing it to known structures, with a passing score of 80% indicating model environment alignment115. The ProSA tool (https://prosa.services.came.sbg.ac.at/prosa.php), which determines Z-scores and assesses protein structure quality by analyzing both local residue-level interactions and global structural geometry, was used to validate the predicted model116. Furthermore, the CABS-flex 3.0 (https://lcbio.pl/cabsflex3/) platform was utilized to investigate the structural flexibility of Gliostem-MultiVax, a crucial aspect of the vaccine’s functionality. By mimicking the query protein’s residues, the server provides a comprehensive view of the protein’s flexibility and stability, with default parameters117.

Identification and evaluation of conformational B cell epitopes

The formation of discontinuous B-cell epitopes, which are essential for the immune response, is the result of the complex process of protein folding, which has the amazing capacity to unite amino acid residues that are located at significant distances from one another. Given that the vast majority of B-cell epitopes are discontinuous, we employed the Ellipro algorithm (http://tools.iedb.org/ellipro/) to forecast whether these epitopes would be present in the vaccine’s structural framework118. The threshold parameters, a Maximum distance of 6 Å and a minimum residue score of 0.5, are critical determinants in analyzing the vaccine’s structural characteristics. The findings are based on a modified version of Thornton’s approach that employs algorithms for residue clustering. Neighbor residue clustering, protein shape, and residual protein index (PI) are considered when making the prediction.

Molecular docking of the designed vaccine with TLR2

Protein- protein docking simulations were performed using the Cluspro 2.0 server (https://cluspro.org/login.php) to evaluate the potential interaction between the Gliostem-MultiVax and toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2)119. A high binding affinity of a vaccine candidate for target immune cells leads to an effective immune response. The human TLR-2 crystal structure (PDB ID: 2Z7X), obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/), was docked with the Gliostem-MultiVax construct, acting as the ligand. To validate the interaction between the Gliostem-MultiVax model and TLR2, the complex with the highest docking energy score was selected for further analysis. Furthermore, the binding affinity or Gibbs free energy (ΔG) and the dissociation constant (KD) value of the docked complex at 25 °C were estimated using the Prodigy server (https://rascar.science.uu.nl/prodigy/)120. The docked complex was visualized using UCSF ChimeraX v1.8 software121. Additionally, the interacting residues of the docked complex were visualized using the online database PDBsum Generate (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/thornton-srv/databases/pdbsum/Generate.html)122. Finally, Molecular mobility and structural flexibility were calculated by integrating the coordinates of the docked complex and performing normal mode analysis (NMA) in internal coordinates using the iMODS server (https://imods.iqf.csic.es/)123. B-factors, Deformability, Variance, Eigenvalues, the elastic network, and the covariance matrix were predicted.

Molecular dynamics simulation

A molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was performed to elucidate the conformational dynamics and stability of the TLR2-Gliostem-MultiVax complex using GROMACS. (version 2024.1)124. The system was prepared using the AMBER03 force field125. The complex was placed at the center of a dodecahedral box with a minimum distance of 1.0 nm from the box edge and solvated with TIP3P water molecules126. The system was neutralized by adding Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions as required. Subsequent energy minimization was carried out using the steepest descent method. Equilibration was carried out in two stages: 100 ps of NVT simulation at 298 K, followed by 100 ps of NPT simulation at 1 bar. Long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method, and all bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm. The unrestrained production simulation was run for 200 ns. Structural stability was evaluated by computing radius of gyration (Rg), root mean square deviation (RMSD), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), and the number of hydrogen bonds. Detailed MD simulation parameters are provided in Table S4.

Immune simulation of the proposed vaccine

The immunogenicity and immune response profile of Gliostem-MultiVax were assessed using an immune simulation on the C-ImmSim online platform (https://kraken.iac.rm.cnr.it/C-IMMSIM/index.php), which models the three primary functional compartments of the mammalian immune system (thymus, lymph nodes, and bone marrow)127,128. The simulation employed predefined parameters: a random seed of 12,345, a simulation volume of 50 µl, and 1000 simulation steps. Three vaccine injections were administered at time steps 1, 84, and 168, each corresponding to approximately 8 h in real life, or 4 weeks. The study evaluated immune responses mediated by HLA class I alleles (HLA-B*27:05, HLA-B*07:02, HLA-A*02:01, and HLA-A*24:02) and HLA class II alleles (HLA-DRB1*07:01 and HLA-DRB1*01:01). All other parameter settings remained at their default values.

Codon optimization and plasmid-based cloning of vaccine sequence

The VectorBuilder (https://en.vectorbuilder.com/tool/codon-optimization.html) platform was used to optimize the codons of the engineered vaccine sequence to increase expression efficiency in the selected host, E. coli (strain K12). Protein expression levels were assessed using VectorBuilder’s codon adaptation index (CAI), which ranges from 0 to 1, and GC content, recommended to be between 30% and 70%. Higher CAI values indicate better gene expression. The Gliostem-MultiVax sequence was engineered by incorporating XhoI and BamHI restriction sites at the N-terminus and C-terminus, respectively. To ensure efficient in vitro expression and production, the refined sequence was subsequently cloned into the pET-28a (+) vector using Snapgene software (V6.2.2) for precise molecular cloning and verification.
