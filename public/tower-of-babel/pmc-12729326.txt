Comparison of subxiphoid vs intercostal VATS approaches for anterior mediastinal tumors: a retrospective cohort study with focus on elderly patients
Authors: Huiming Xu, Yihua Lai, Guilong Li, Wenshan Zhang, Rongqiang Shen, Yi Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe best minimally invasive approach for anterior mediastinal tumors in geriatric patients is unknown. The objective of this study was to evaluate clinical outcomes of video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) using subxiphoid (S-VATS) and lateral intercostal (I-VATS) approaches with specific emphasis on perioperative safety and early postoperative recovery in geriatric patients.MethodsA total of 293 VATS resections of anteromedial mediastinal tumors were performed from June 2017 to June 2022; of these, 48 patients were classified as elderly (≥ 65 years). A propensity score matching analysis was performed to balance baseline covariates to compare outcomes between S-VATS (n = 15) and I-VATS (n = 33) patients, as well as additional comparisons to younger cohorts.ResultsElderly patients had significantly more comorbidities than younger patients (58.3% vs 32.7%, p = 0.001). The S-VATS approach in elderly patients resulted in decreased early postoperative pain as compared to the I-VATS approach (24-h VAS-2.53 ± 0.63 vs 3.63 ± 1.04, p = 0.017). Operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and postoperative complication rate were similar across both groups, suggesting that both approaches were equally safe.ConclusionBoth S-VATS and I-VATS for anterior mediastinal tumor resection are safe and feasible in geriatric patients. The S-VATS approach may have the advantage of better early postoperative pain control, although this must be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size and evaluation of pain at a single postoperative point. Further studies are warranted to validate these findings, including larger prospective studies.

Introduction

In recent years, increased public health awareness and widespread adoption of routine chest CT screening have led to a significant rise in the early detection of anterior mediastinal tumors, the majority of which are benign or low-grade malignant lesions [1]. While various treatment options exist, surgical resection remains the gold standard for most cases due to its definitive therapeutic effect [2]. The evolution of minimally invasive techniques has transformed the surgical landscape, with video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) now established as the preferred approach for anterior mediastinal tumor resection [3]. The conventional intercostal approach (I-VATS), performed through either the left or right chest cavity, has been widely adopted in clinical practice [4]. However, accumulating evidence suggests this method may contribute to persistent postoperative intercostal neuralgia and pulmonary complications due to inevitable intercostal nerve manipulation and lung retraction [5, 6].

The emergence of subxiphoid VATS (S-VATS) represents an important technical advancement in thoracic surgery [7]. This novel approach offers several theoretical advantages that address limitations of the traditional method, including superior visualization of critical structures like the upper thymic poles and contralateral phrenic nerves, while simultaneously avoiding intercostal nerve trauma [8]. These benefits may be particularly relevant for elderly patients, who demonstrate increased susceptibility to postoperative complications and chronic pain [9]. With global demographic trends toward aging populations, thoracic surgeons are encountering growing numbers of elderly patients with anterior mediastinal tumors. This patient cohort presents unique clinical challenges due to age-related physiological decline, multiple comorbidities, and heightened psychological vulnerability that can impact treatment adherence and outcomes [10].

Current literature on VATS approaches for anterior mediastinal tumors contains substantial knowledge gaps regarding elderly populations. Available studies have primarily focused on younger patients or mixed-age cohorts without specific age-stratified analyses. Furthermore, direct comparative studies evaluating S-VATS versus I-VATS in elderly patients with anterior mediastinal tumors remain remarkably scarce. This paucity of evidence creates significant uncertainty in clinical decision-making for this vulnerable population.

In the present study, we compared a modified single-port low-intercostal/subxiphoid-assisted VATS technique to the standard lateral intercostal VATS approach to evaluate perioperative safety and clinical outcomes in elderly patients. This term (“subxiphoid-assisted intercostal single-port VATS”) represents the operative access used by the authors and is used intentionally to avoid misunderstanding with the surgical intervention. To date, there have been few studies to compare either standard subxiphoid or subxiphoid-assisted approaches that have consistently included elderly populations who have significantly different postoperative pain tolerance and cardiopulmonary reserve than younger patients in the studies reviewed. Therefore, this study serves to provide a thorough comparison and evaluation of perioperative outcomes in S-VATS and I-VATS approaches in elderly patients with anterior mediastinal tumors, with emphasis on the surgical trauma experienced, postoperative recovery, and incidence of complications.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study included 293 patients who underwent either subxiphoid (S-VATS, n = 99) or intercostal (I-VATS, n = 194) video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery for anterior mediastinal tumors at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Zhangzhou Hospital Affiliated to Fujian Medical University, from June 2017 to June 2022. Patients were stratified into elderly (≥ 65 years) and non-elderly groups (< 65 years) according to World Health Organization age classification criteria to account for potential age-related differences in surgical outcomes. Eligible patients met the following inclusion criteria: (1) well-encapsulated anterior mediastinal lesions (maximum diameter ≤ 8 cm) confirmed by computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) without invasion of adjacent structures; (2) adequate cardiopulmonary, renal, and hepatic function with no contraindications to general anesthesia or single-lung ventilation. Exclusion criteria included: (1) uncontrolled cardiopulmonary dysfunction precluding single-lung ventilation; (2) tumor invasion into critical structures (e.g., superior vena cava, pericardium, or lung) requiring sternotomy or vascular reconstruction; (3) severe myasthenia gravis or unresectable malignant thymoma/thymic carcinoma; (4) prior mediastinal or ipsilateral thoracic surgery.

Given the rarity of anterior mediastinal tumors, diverse histological subtypes were included in this study. To minimize confounding from tumor heterogeneity, subgroup analyses stratified by pathology (thymoma vs. non-thymoma) were performed to verify the consistency of outcomes across tumor types.

Patients and data collection

Demographic and clinical characteristics were retrospectively collected, including age, sex, BMI, smoking history, comorbidities, preoperative pulmonary function (FEV1%, DLCO%), and tumor-related features (location, size, pathological type, and TNM stage if applicable).

For mediastinal tumors, detailed pathological classifications were documented (e.g., thymomas, lymphomas, neurogenic tumors, germ cell tumors, or cystic lesions), along with imaging features (CT/MRI-based density, enhancement patterns, and infiltration of adjacent structures). Tumor size was measured as the maximum diameter on preoperative imaging.

Thymic epithelial tumors were staged according to the Masaoka–Koga classification. The presence and severity of myasthenia gravis (MG) were assessed using the Osserman classification. Preoperative and postoperative MG management, including medical therapy, was also recorded to ensure accurate clinical characterization.

Group stratification

For comparative analysis, all enrolled patients were categorized based on two independent variables: surgical approach (subxiphoid vs. intercostal) and age (elderly vs. non-elderly). Using these classification criteria, we established four distinct study groups: (1) the elderly subxiphoid approach group (S-VATS-E), consisting of 33 patients; (2) the elderly intercostal approach group (I-VATS-E) with 15 cases; (3) the non-elderly subxiphoid approach cohort (S-VATS-NE) comprising 161 individuals; and (4) the non-elderly intercostal approach cohort (I-VATS-NE) including 84 patients. This stratification allowed us to conduct three specific comparative analyses: first, comparing elderly versus non-elderly patients within the subxiphoid approach (S-VATS-E vs S-VATS-NE); second, examining age-related differences in the intercostal approach (I-VATS-E vs I-VATS-NE); and third, evaluating surgical approach differences among elderly patients (S-VATS-E vs I-VATS-E). Each comparison was designed to isolate the effects of either age or surgical approach while controlling for the other variable.

Surgical techniques and operative approach

The S-VATS group in this study denotes a modified single-port low-intercostal approach with a subxiphoid extension, which is functionally similar to a subxiphoid-assisted access rather than a pure subxiphoid route. A 3–4 cm incision was made in the fourth or fifth intercostal space along the anterior axillary line, and no rib spreading was required. Instrument manipulation and camera visualization were achieved through this single port using angled thoracoscopic instruments. All surgeries were performed under low-pressure CO2 insufflation (6 to 8 mmHg), which helped create a good surgical field and improve mediastinal exposure.

The I-VATS operations utilized three separate incisions (one camera port and two working ports) that were located in optimal triangulated positions based on the tumor’s location, typically in the third to seventh intercostal spaces.

The decision for a surgical approach was based on tumor size, tumor anatomy, and surgeon preference. Patients with narrow substernal angles, large cardiomegaly, or limited space under the xiphoid were preferentially assigned to the intercostal VATS group.

All surgeries were performed by senior thoracic surgeons with vast experience (> 50 mediastinal VATS resections) prior to the launch of the study.

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes included operative time (defined as the interval from anesthesia induction to extubation), intraoperative blood loss (calculated by measuring soaked gauze and suction volume), postoperative complications, chest tube drainage volume, postoperative hospital stay, hospital costs, and 30-day mortality. Secondary outcomes encompassed pre-existing conditions such as cardiac comorbidities (e.g., arrhythmias, coronary artery disease) and pulmonary diseases (e.g., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD], pneumonia).

Hospitalization cost was calculated using data from the institutional billing system, encompassing operative, anesthesia, consumable, pharmaceutical, and inpatient stay charges.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., USA). Continuous variables with normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the Student’s t-test; otherwise, median (interquartile range) and the Mann–Whitney U test were applied. Categorical variables were expressed as n (%) and analyzed with Pearson’s χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To minimize selection bias between the S-VATS and I-VATS groups, we performed propensity score matching (PSM). First, we calculated propensity scores using logistic regression, incorporating key baseline covariates including age, sex, BMI, ASA grade, tumor size, clinical stage, pulmonary function (FEV1%), smoking history, and comorbidities (e.g., COPD, diabetes). Patients were then matched 1:1 using nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the propensity score. Post-matching balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD) and p-values, with SMD < 0.1 and p > 0.05 indicating adequate balance. All subsequent analyses were conducted on the matched cohort to ensure comparability between groups.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

This study enrolled 293 patients (162 males, 131 females; mean age 52.25 ± 12.92 years) who underwent mediastinal tumor resection, with 45 elderly and 248 non-elderly patients categorized by surgical approach (S-VATS-E: 33 cases, I-VATS-E: 15 cases; S-VATS-NE: 161 cases, I-VATS-NE: 84 cases). The cohort presented with diverse pathological subtypes, with thymomas being the most prevalent (58.0%), followed by neurogenic tumors (22.5%), lymphomas (9.6%), germ cell tumors (5.8%), and other rare types (4.1%). In elderly patients, the S-VATS-E group comprised 21 thymomas (63.6%), 7 neurogenic tumors (21.2%), 2 lymphomas (6.1%), 2 germ cell tumors (6.1%) and 1 other type (3.0%), while the I-VATS-E group included 10 thymomas (66.7%), 3 neurogenic tumors (20.0%), 1 lymphoma (6.7%) and 1 germ cell tumor (6.7%). Among non-elderly patients, the S-VATS-NE group showed 91 thymomas (56.5%), 37 neurogenic tumors (23.0%), 16 lymphomas (9.9%), 9 germ cell tumors (5.6%) and 8 other types (5.0%), whereas the I-VATS-NE group had 48 thymomas (57.1%), 19 neurogenic tumors (22.6%), 9 lymphomas (10.7%), 5 germ cell tumors (6.0%) and 3 other types (3.6%). Comparative analysis demonstrated comparable tumor subtype distributions between surgical approaches within each age group (elderly: thymoma p = 0.829, neurogenic p = 0.922, lymphoma p = 0.918, germ cell p = 0.918; non-elderly: thymoma p = 0.934, neurogenic p = 0.946, lymphoma p = 0.829, germ cell p = 0.898), confirming balanced pathological characteristics. Preoperative comorbidities

Elderly patients exhibited a significantly higher overall comorbidity rate than their non-elderly counterparts in both surgical approaches (S-VATS: 48.5% vs. 20.5%, p = 0.01; I-VATS: 46.7% vs. 21.4%, p = 0.04). Specifically, hypertension (S-VATS: p < 0.001; I-VATS: p = 0.03) and diabetes (S-VATS: p = 0.01; I-VATS: p = 0.03) were more prevalent in elderly patients. Consistent with the clinical data, myasthenia gravis (MG) incidence was low and comparable between age groups and surgical approaches (S-VATS-E: 3.0%, I-VATS-E: 6.7%, p > 0.05), indicating that MG distribution did not bias the comparison. Notably, preoperative comorbidity rates were comparable between S-VATS-E and I-VATS-E (p = 0.91), indicating no inherent surgical approach-related bias. Intraoperative and postoperative outcomes

No significant differences were observed in operative time (elderly vs. young: S-VATS, p = 0.16; I-VATS, p = 0.28) or intraoperative blood loss (S-VATS, p = 0.41; I-VATS, p = 0.98) between the two surgical approaches (Table 5). Postoperative visual analogue scale (VAS) pain scores at 24 h were significantly lower in the I-VATS group compared to the S-VATS group, with the most pronounced difference seen among elderly patients (I-VATS-E: 2.53 ± 0.63 vs. S-VATS-E: 3.63 ± 1.04, p = 0.01). Pain scores at 24 h were available for all patients; however, longer-term assessments (48 h, 72 h, at discharge, or 1 month postoperatively) were not routinely recorded, representing a study limitation. There were no statistically significant differences in postoperative drainage volume (S-VATS-E vs. I-VATS-E, p = 0.92), length of hospital stays (p = 0.39), or total hospitalization costs (p = 0.81). Regarding postoperative complications, while no significant difference was found between elderly and non-elderly patients undergoing I-VATS (pneumonia: p = 0.63), S-VATS elderly patients demonstrated a significantly higher rate of pneumonia compared to non-elderly patients (p = 0.012). No statistically significant differences were observed in pathological outcomes between elderly and non-elderly patients undergoing either subxiphoid (S-VATS) or intercostal (I-VATS) approaches for anterior mediastinal tumor resection (all p > 0.05). Postoperative pathological findings

Additionally, sub-analysis by pathological type (thymoma vs. non-thymoma) showed no significant differences in operative time, intraoperative blood loss, or complication rates between surgical approaches (all p > 0.05), supporting the reliability of the pooled analysis despite histological diversity.

Postoperative follow-up

A total of 293 patients were followed up via telephone interviews or outpatient visits, with a mean follow-up duration of 36.8 ± 16.4 months. No local tumor recurrence or tumor-related mortality was observed by the last follow-up. These results suggest favorable long-term oncological outcomes for both subxiphoid and intercostal approaches in anterior mediastinal tumor resection.

Discussion

With global population aging and increasing prevalence of anterior mediastinal tumors, surgical management in elderly patients has gained significant attention. While the definition of "elderly" varies across studies, typically ranging from 65 to 80 years, with common cutoffs at 65, 70, 75, or 80 years, our study adopted the ≥ 65-year criterion consistent with major thoracic surgery literature [11, 12].

Elderly patients often exhibit reduced physiological reserve and immunocompromised status, making extensive or prolonged surgical procedures less favorable in terms of treatment efficacy and survival outcomes. In our cohort, a significant proportion of elderly patients (n = 21) presented with pre-existing comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, or both. To optimize surgical outcomes, we implemented strict perioperative blood pressure and glucose monitoring, enforced dietary modification (low-salt and diabetic diets), and collaborated with multidisciplinary teams to ensure preoperative optimization. These measures effectively minimized anesthesia-related risks and postoperative complications such as wound infection or delayed healing. Importantly, our findings confirm that advanced age alone should not preclude surgery in patients with preserved cardiopulmonary function. Follow-up data further demonstrated no cases of local tumor recurrence or tumor-related mortality among surgically treated elderly patients.

This research examined a modified version of the single-port low-intercostal approach with subxiphoid assistance, referred to here as a subxiphoid-assisted intercostal single-port VATS. As indicated in some prior studies [13–17], this variant technique can offer the advantages of midline visualization with decreased intercostal trauma, while still being technically feasible with standard thoracoscopic instrumentation. In light of preventing confusion with the pure subxiphoid route, it is essential to clarify this terminology and accurately interpret our results going forward in the corresponding literature.

All comparisons show there were no significant differences between older patients and younger patients with operative time or interoperative blood loss, nor with any comparison of S-VATS and the I-VATS approach in the elderly cohort. This is in keeping with the findings and conclusions from some studies [13, 14]. The marginal observation that operative time is longer with older patients would suggest, it is probably an artifact of being incautious when managing anesthesia and instrument use, and this is not related to overly complicated changes in the I-VATS technique.

In comparing postoperative outcomes, the subxiphoid-assisted approach provided significantly lower 24-h pain scores, and these findings are aligned with less compression of intercostal nerves and better positioning of an intercostal drain from the xiphoid route [15–17]. However, only pain scores in the early postoperative period (24 h) were collected, and no measures were routinely taken for 48-h, 72-h, or discharge pain scores. Thus, we must interpret these findings only as evidence of short-term pain relief, rather than a better postoperative analgesic effect.

In the subgroup analysis following pathological type (thymoma vs. non-thymoma) and clinical stage (Masaoka–Koga classification), there were no significant differences in operative parameters or complications between surgical approaches. This indicates that histological diversity and tumor stage did not significantly affect perioperative outcomes in our cohort, further underscoring the integrity of the comparative conclusions.

While both approaches produced relatively equal rates of postoperative complications, our data strengthen that the subxiphoid-assisted approach is safe and well tolerated in elderly patients. Additionally, the cost benefit seen in the subxiphoid-assisted group should be interpreted cautiously, as institutional billing practices, resource pricing regionally, and insurance coverage can vary widely. To understand the true economic implications of this approach, standardized, multi-institutional cost analyses will be needed.

There are several limitations of which we should be cognizant. First, this was a retrospective single-center review, which has inherent selection bias potential, and this was merely partially mitigated with propensity score matching. Second, the elderly patient population was small (n = 48), and the sample size limits consideration of statistically significant differences in outcomes for modest effect sizes. Third, the racially heterogeneous population found with tumor pathology may complicate interpretation even further, with the subgroup analyses intended to provide partial ordinal data for etiology levels. Fourth, a limitation of the pain evaluation was that it was confined to a single postoperative assessment time point (24 h), and patients were followed for less than necessary for long-term data on either neuropathic or chronic pain. Finally, institutional cost metrics needed to be viewed with the knowledge that they may not translate across systems of care. To validate these types of findings, future prospective, multicenter studies are needed, considering intervals for pain evaluation, typicalizable cost metrics, and longer oncologic follow-up.

In conclusion, the subxiphoid-assisted intercostal single-port VATS approach is a safe and minimally invasive alternative to conventional intercostal VATS to manage elderly patients with anterior mediastinal tumors. With equivalent perioperative outcomes, the subxiphoid-assisted technique provided improved early postoperative pain control, similar to the VATS technique, with no negative impact on operative safety. Therefore, we believe there is reasonable evidence to continue using this approach under careful consideration in elderly patients with severe thoracic conditions, though this must be validated with larger, prospective studies.

Conclusion

In elderly patients, both subxiphoid-assisted and intercostal VATS are safe and feasible options for anterior mediastinal tumor resection. Although the subxiphoid-assisted technique had lower rates of early postoperative pain, there were no notable differences in significant perioperative outcomes. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes, standardized long-term pain scales, and defined anatomical categorization of surgical techniques are warranted to confirm this study’s results.
