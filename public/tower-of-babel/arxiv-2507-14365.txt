Dynamic annihilation pathways of magnetic skyrmions
Matthew Copus, Ezio Iacocca

Abstract
The investigation of magnetic solitons often relies on numerical modeling to determine key features such as stability, annihilation, nucleation, and motion. However, as soliton sizes approach atomic length scales, the accuracy of these predictions become increasingly sensitive to the details of the numerical model. Here, we study the annihilation of two-dimensional magnetic skyrmions using a pseudospectral approach and compare its performance to that of conventional micromagnetic simulations. A central distinction between the models lies in their treatment of the exchange interaction, which governs the magnon dispersion relation and plays a crucial role to balance the uniaxial anisotropy to stabilise skyrmions. We demonstrate that both the choice of model and spatial discretisation significantly influence the skyrmion dynamics and the magnetic field required for annihilation. The pseudospectral model provides a consistent description across length scales and captures complex behaviours such as skyrmion breathing on its path to annihilation. Our results have direct implications in the state-of-the-art modeling of skyrmions and other two-dimensional textures and will impact the modeling of three-dimensional textures such as hopfions. More broadly, our approach will contribute to the development seamless multiscale model and optimization machine learning approaches for material discovery.

As material dimensions continue to shrink and topological textures approach atomic scales, the discrete nature of solid-state matter can no longer be neglected. Most theoretical investigations of topological textures rely on a continuum model of the magnetic exchange interaction
Kosevich et al. 1990
;
Rohart et al. 2016
;
Büttner et al. 2018
;
Bogdanov and Panagopoulos 2020
. However, this approach introduces assumptions that break down at the atomic scale, e.g., a singular magnetisation vector at a Bloch point. One solution to this problem is to employ discrete numerical models such as atomistic spin dynamics (ASD)
Eriksson et al. 2017
;
Evans et al. 2014
. Such models provide means to accurately describe Bloch points
Andreas et al. 2014
and skyrmion annihilation
Bessarab et al. 2015
;
Bessarab et al. 2018
. However, ASD simulations are constrained to small dimensions due to computational resource limitations. Another solution is to use micromagnetic simulations
Brown 1963
;
Vansteenkiste et al. 2014
;
Donahue and Porter 2015
;
Heil et al. 2019
;
Austrup et al. 2025
. While this method models the exchange interaction using a continuum approximation, it is implemented numerically on a spatial grid of discrete cells. For this reason, micromagnetic simulations are assumed to be valid for cell dimensions larger than the exchange length and may become unphysical as cell dimensions approach the atomic lattice constant.

ASD and micromagnetic simulations can complement each other, thus multiscale approaches have been sought
Andreas et al. 2014
;
Poluektov et al. 2018
. For example, smooth skyrmion motion was achieved in a lattice transitioning from an atomic to a continuum description
Méndez et al. 2020
. Despite such successes, multiscale approaches consider a transition between different models which requires knowledge of the underlying geometry and a sufficiently large computational domain. Alternatively, a pseudospectral Landau-Lifshitz (PS-LL) description of magnetisation dynamics that models atomic and continuum dynamics on equal footing was introduced
Rockwell et al. 2024
. The PS-LL model leverages the fact that the magnon dispersion relation captures the relevant physical interactions at every spatial and temporal scale so that transition to larger cells is seamless in principle. This model has so far proven successful in describing ultrafast remagnetisation due to transient gratings
Rockwell et al. 2024
;
Foglia et al. 2024
. Here, we use the PS-LL to investigate the annihilation pathways of magnetic skyrmions.

In this work, we demonstrate that the choice of numerical model and spatial discretisation can lead to quantitatively different skyrmion dynamics predictions. Using the PS-LL model, implemented with two distinct exchange interaction kernels, alongside results from the widely used micromagnetic simulation package MuMax3
Vansteenkiste et al. 2014
, we examine the contraction and annihilation of a Néel-type skyrmion in a 2D magnetic thin film with uniaxial anisotropy and interfacial Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction (DMI). The dynamics are driven by an increasing out-of-plane magnetic field. While all models show similar behaviour initially, striking differences emerge as the skyrmion radius approaches the exchange length. The PS-LL model accurately describes the dynamics at both the atomic and micromagnetic limits. However, micromagnetic models either overestimate or underestimate the exchange energy in the system, leading to an incorrect prediction of the skymrion’s topological protection and thus its eventual annihilation. Our work demonstrates that, even within the micromagnetic regime, a pseudospectral approach provides a much more accurate description of the magnetisation dynamics because the exchange interaction is described appropriately up to the first Brillouin zone. Such accuracy is essential for modeling the nucleation, stability, and annihilation of skyrmions as well as its manipulation by external stimuli. From a much broader point of view, our investigation showcases the limitation of standard solvers and computational tools that must be carefully considered as physical systems approach their ultimate scales and dynamics escape the standard models and approximations. Dispersion relations can be typically obtained by first principles and experiments, e.g., neutron scattering, for a variety of physical systems, so that they can be directly implemented in pseudospectral approaches with superior accuracy.

Building on the initial configuration described above, we now investigate the dynamic response of a single Néel skyrmion to an applied magnetic field. At
t=0
of each simulation, the system is initialized in a skyrmion state with no external field present. Once the simulation begins, a uniform out-of-plane magnetic field is applied along the
+\hat{z}
direction and increased linearly in time. This ramping field causes the skyrmion to contract and eventually leads to the skyrmion’s annihilation. The evolution of the skyrmion and the required field for its annihilation are influenced by the numerical model, the spatial resolution of the simulation, the rate at which the field is increased, and the selected damping constant.

Generally speaking, during the initial phase of each simulation, the increasing out-of-plane magnetic field induces a contraction of the skyrmion. After reaching a critical size, the skyrmion either annihilates or enters a breathing regime, characterized by periodic oscillations in its radius. Similar breathing behaviour has been reported in Ref.
Austrup et al. 2025
, where the oscillations arise from transformations through different skyrmion configurations prior to collapse. The number and amplitude of these oscillations depend on the simulation parameters. When the skrymion eventually collapses, the stored energy is then released in the form of outward-propagating magnons. The precise moment of annihilation is defined by a topological charge transition from 1 to 0. The methodology used to calculate the topological charge is provided in the Methods.

Instead of annihilation, the skyrmion enters a breathing regime as described earlier. This difference in behaviour reflects a fundamentally different energy landscape. The strong agreement between the PS-LL model with the magnon kernel and MuMax3 can be attributed to their nearly identical dispersion relations, whereas the deviation of the micromagnetic kernel stems from the quadratic approximation of its dispersion.

At a coarser resolution of 5 nm, the behaviour changes. As shown in Fig.

The agreement between the two PS-LL models at larger cell sizes arises because the range of allowed wavevectors is reduced and confined to a small section of the first Brillioun zone where the micromagnetic approximation is largely accurate. Regardless of the differences, skyrmion breathing is observed in all cases.

This deviation shows that the micromagnetic approximation fails to accurately represent short-wavelength magnons. As a result, the PS-LL micromagnetic model overestimates the energy cost of magnons at high

As a result, the exchange energy is released, much like a spring, causing the skyrmion to breathe instead of annihilate. This also explains why the fields required for annihilation are generally larger for the micromagnetic kernel compared to the other two models as this deference to breathing occurs each time the skyrmion reaches its critical size.

Interestingly, the phase diagrams at this discretisation are not divided into two regions like their atomic scale counterparts: skyrmion breathing is always observed. This occurs because a larger cell size assumes that atomic spins are uniformly aligned within each cell, reducing the exchange energy contribution. Because of its topological barrier the exchange nor the external field is able to switch the singularity and breathing ensues. Once the external field is sufficient to switch the singularity, the skyrmion annihilates. For this reason, we observe that the skyrmions tend to annihilate at lower field amplitudes, when a larger cell size is used. In simulations performed by MuMax3, annihilation is observed at fields approximately a factor of two smaller than what was seen at a
0.4

5.0

Further evidence highlighting the importance of the exchange energy description is found in the general behaviour of the annihilation fields as a function of damping. In all cases, the highest annihilation fields occur for high field ramp rates and low damping values. On one hand, the ramp rates used here are comparable to the characteristic timescales of the dynamics themselves, causing the magnetic system to lag in its response. On the other hand, low damping extends the lifetime of fast dynamics. Contrary to naive expectations, this implies that a nonlinear structure like a skyrmion requires more time to stabilize or, in this context, to annihilate. Since the damping is higher, the high-frequency excitations are more rapidly quenched allowing the skyrmion to reach its new equilibrium state more quickly. This interpretation holds insofar as damping is sufficiently low for the explicit Landau-Lifshitz equation to remain valid.

Discussion

Our investigation demonstrates that the choice of numerical model and spatial discretisation has a significant impact on skyrmion annihilation pathways and the physical limits of topological protection. While the PS-LL model offers a consistent description across length scales, the choice of discretisation alters the dynamic pathway, resulting in dramatic differences, including the absence of a direct annihilation. Atomic-scale discretisation is therefore necessary for a faithful description of topological annihilation. The results also showcase the importance of how the exchange interaction is implemented. We examined MuMax3, a widely used micromagnetic solver. It is commonly assumed that such models are valid when the cell size is larger than the exchange length. However, the exchange implementation in MuMax3 leads to a cosine-type dispersion relation, which misrepresents the exchange energy landscape. Interestingly, this implementation turns out to be correct at atomic resolution insofar as only nearest-neighbour interactions are required. More generally, next-nearest-neighbour interactions have been shown to be relevant from density functional theory
Eriksson et al. 2017
. In such cases, the PS-LL kernel would be able to capture the nonlocal interactions because of its Fourier representation.

Our analysis focused primarily on the effect of exchange energy. However, other physical contributions not considered here are important for accurate physical models, e.g., nonlocal dipole fields and temperature. Nonlocal dipolar fields lead to field closures which can reinforce the skyrmion as it contracts. This mechanism is exploited, for example, to stabilize skyrmions in materials lacking DMI but with sufficient thickness
Montoya et al. 2017
. A comprehensive analysis would require a full three-dimensional pseudospectral approach. Temperature can play an important role, as fluctuations may perturb the magnetisation at the skyrmion core and lead to its premature annihilation. In such cases, a statistical analysis would need to be implemented to define a mean skyrmion lifetime.

A fundamental question addressed in this work is the relationship between small-amplitude waves and nonlinear structures. Specifically, the dispersion relation is particular of small-amplitude waves in the sense that the excitation is linear, so that there is no spectral mixing. Regardless, it reflects the structure of the kernel. This kernel then applies to nonlinear structures where mixing is allowed. This is the fundamental reason why the details of the dispersion relation past half the FBZ are relevant for nonlinear problems and why skyrmions are better resolved using the PS-LL method. In other words, the Fourier representation of the kernel comprises all available spatial frequencies in the physical system, which are necessary to define a solitary wave. We expect this principle to extend to other textures in 2D and 3D, as well as many other physical systems where multiple scales are ubiquitous and difficult to encapsulate in a single model or approximation. Therefore, this study is a stepping stone towards future progress to model, understand, and manipulate topological textures in a physically accurate manner. From a broader perspective, the accurate modeling of the energy and momentum space will lead to improved multiscale models, including ultrafast dynamics, while the model of nonlocal interaction will enable further developments in material discovery using machine learning approaches.

Calculation of the topological charge

The topological charge at each timestep was computed via an inverse stereographic projection of the material’s magnetisation onto the surface of a unit sphere. The 2D magnetic lattice was mapped onto the sphere, centered at the origin, with the material positioned in the first quadrant to simplify the calculation of azimuthal angles. Each square cell in the grid was split into two triangles for surface triangulation. To close the sphere, a virtual point was added at the north pole, representing the magnetisation infinitely far from the skyrmion (assumed to be fully out-of-plane), and additional triangles were formed between this pole and each edge segment. To determine the skyrmion radius, we first identified the position of the skyrmion’s core by locating the cell with the most negative
