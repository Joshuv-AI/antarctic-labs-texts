Safety evaluation of a food enzyme containing oryzin and leucyl aminopeptidase activities and a heat‐treated food enzyme containing only leucyl aminopeptidase activity from the non‐genetically modified Aspergillus sp. strain FL 72‐230

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis assessment addressed two food enzymes produced with the non‐genetically modified microorganism Aspergillus sp. strain FL 72‐230 by Shin Nihon Chemical Co., Ltd. The native food enzyme contains leucyl aminopeptidase (EC 3.4.11.1) and oryzin (EC 3.4.21.63) activities. Another food enzyme containing only the leucyl aminopeptidase (EC 3.4.11.1) activity was obtained by heat treatment and filtration of the native food enzyme. Both food enzymes were considered free from viable cells of the production organism. Both food enzymes are intended to be used in 14 food manufacturing processes. Since residual amounts of food enzyme‐Total Organic Solids (TOS) are removed in one process, dietary exposure was calculated for the remaining 13 food manufacturing processes. It was calculated to be up to 0.532 mg TOS/kg body weight (bw) per day in European populations. Genotoxicity tests did not indicate a safety concern. The systemic toxicity was assessed by means of a repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study in rats. The Panel identified a no observed adverse effect level of 585 mg TOS/kg bw per day for the native food enzyme, which when compared with the estimated dietary exposure, resulted in a margin of exposure (MoE) of at least 1100. For the heat‐treated food enzyme, using a single dose tested of 2340 mg TOS/kg body weight (bw) per day, not showing adverse effects, the MoE is at least 4398. A search for the homology of the two amino acid sequences to known allergens was made and matches with two food allergens, three contact allergens and 14 respiratory allergens were found. The Panel considered that a risk of allergic reactions upon dietary exposure to these food enzymes cannot be excluded. Based on the data provided, the Panel concluded that both food enzymes do not give rise to safety concerns, under the intended conditions of use.

INTRODUCTION

Article 3 of Regulation (EC) No 1332/2008 1 provides definition for ‘food enzyme’ and ‘food enzyme preparation’.

‘Food enzyme’ means a product obtained from plants, animals or microorganisms or products thereof including a product obtained by a fermentation process using microorganisms: (i) containing one or more enzymes capable of catalysing a specific biochemical reaction; and (ii) added to food for a technological purpose at any stage of the manufacturing, processing, preparation, treatment, packaging, transport or storage of foods.

‘Food enzyme preparation’ means a formulation consisting of one or more food enzymes in which substances such as food additives and/or other food ingredients are incorporated to facilitate their storage, sale, standardisation, dilution or dissolution.

Before January 2009, food enzymes other than those used as food additives were not regulated or were regulated as processing aids under the legislation of the Member States. On 20 January 2009, Regulation (EC) No 1332/2008 on food enzymes came into force. This Regulation applies to enzymes that are added to food to perform a technological function in the manufacture, processing, preparation, treatment, packaging, transport or storage of such food, including enzymes used as processing aids. Regulation (EC) No 1331/2008 2 established the European Union (EU) procedures for the safety assessment and the authorisation procedure of food additives, food enzymes and food flavourings. The use of a food enzyme shall be authorised only if it is demonstrated that: it does not pose a safety concern to the health of the consumer at the level of use proposed;there is a reasonable technological need;its use does not mislead the consumer.

Background and Terms of Reference as provided by the requestor

Background as provided by the European Commission

Only food enzymes included in the Union list may be placed on the market as such and used in foods, in accordance with the specifications and conditions of use provided for in Article 7(2) of Regulation (EC) No 1332/20081 on food enzymes.

Five applications have been introduced by the Association of Manufacturers and Formulators of Enzyme Products (AMFEP) for the authorisation of the food enzyme consisting of Protease, Leucyl amino‐peptidase, Oryzin and Aspergillopepsin I from Aspergillus oryzae and the companies “BENEO‐Palatinit Gmbh” for the authorisation of the food enzyme Isomaltulose synthase from Protaminobacter rubrum (strain Z12A), “Naganase (Europa) GmbH” for the authorisation of the food enzyme Chitinase from a genetically modified strain of Streptomyces violaceoruber (strain pChi), “Clasado Ingredients Ltd.” for the authorisation of the food enzyme Beta‐galactosidase from a genetically modified strain of Escherichia coli (strain BglA MCB3) and “Meiji Seika Pharma Co., Ltd” for the authorisation of the food enzyme consisting of Aspergillopepsin I and II from Aspergillus niger var. macrosporus (strain DBD‐0406).

Following the requirements of Article 12.1 of Regulation (EC) No 234/2011 3 implementing Regulation (EC) No 1331/2008,2 the Commission has verified that the five applications fall within the scope of the food enzyme Regulation and contain all the elements required under Chapter II of that Regulation.

Interpretation of the Terms of Reference

The present scientific opinion addresses the European Commission's request to carry out the safety assessment of food enzyme protease, leucyl amino‐peptidase, oryzin and aspergillopepsin I from Aspergillus oryzae submitted by AMFEP.

The application was submitted initially as a joint dossier 4 and identified as the EFSA‐Q‐2015‐00619. During a meeting between EFSA, the European Commission and the Association of Manufacturers and Formulators of Enzyme Products (AMFEP), 5 it was agreed that joint dossiers will be split into individual data packages.

The current opinion addresses one data package originating from the former joint dossier. This data package is identified as EFSA‐Q‐2023‐00248 and concerns two food enzymes, one containing oryzin and leucyl aminopeptidase activities and a second one containing only leucyl aminopeptidase activity, both produced with the Aspergillus oryzae strain FL 72‐230 and submitted by Shin Nihon Chemical Co., Ltd.

Although the original mandate refers to the food enzyme as produced from A. oryzae, the new data package identified the production microorganism as A. sojae.

Recent data identified the production microorganism as Aspergillus sp. (Section 3.1). Therefore, this name will be used in this opinion instead of Aspergillus sojae.

The food enzymes are produced with the non‐genetically modified strain FL 72–230, which is deposited as Aspergillus sojae at the National Institute of Technology and Evaluation (NITE, Biological Resource Center Japan) with the deposition number ■■■■■. 6 , 7 , 8

Production of the food enzymes

The food enzymes are manufactured according to the Food Hygiene Regulation (EC) No 852/2004, 10 with food safety procedures based on Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points and in accordance with good manufacturing practice. 11

The production strain is grown as a pure culture using a typical industrial medium in a ■■■■■ fermentation system with conventional process controls in place. After completion of the fermentation, the enzyme is extracted with ■■■■■, and the solid biomass is removed from the suspension by centrifugation followed by filtration. The filtrate containing the enzyme is then further purified and concentrated, including an ultrafiltration step in which enzyme protein is retained, while most of the low molecular mass material passes the filtration membrane and is discarded. 12

The native food enzyme can be subjected to a heat treatment (■■■■■) in order to eliminate the oryzin activity which, after filtration, results in a food enzyme containing only leucyl aminopeptidase activity. Additionally, the heat‐treated food enzyme is further filtrated and spray‐dried. 13

The applicant provided information on the identity of the substances used to control the fermentation and in the subsequent downstream processing of the food enzymes. 14

The Panel considered that sufficient information has been provided on the manufacturing process and the quality assurance system implemented by the applicant to exclude issues of concern.

Purity

The lead content in all batches was below 5 mg/kg 25 which complies with the specification for lead as laid down in the general specifications for enzymes used in food processing (FAO/WHO, 2006). In addition, the concentration of arsenic determined in batches 1–4 was 0.3 mg/kg. 26 , 27 The Panel considered this concentration as not of concern.

The food enzymes comply with the microbiological criteria for total coliforms, Escherichia coli and Salmonella, as laid down in the general specifications for enzymes used in food processing (FAO/WHO, 2006). 28 No antimicrobial activity was detected in any of the tested batches. 29

Strains of Aspergillus species, in common with most filamentous fungi, have the capacity to produce a range of secondary metabolites (Frisvad et al., 2018). The presence of aflatoxins (B1, B2, G1, G2), ochratoxin A, sterigmatocystin, T‐2 toxin, zearalenone, kojic acid, cyclopiazonic acid and 3‐nitropropionic acid was examined in batches 1–4 and all were below the limit of quantification (LoQ) of the applied methods. 30 , 31 Adverse effects caused by the possible presence of other secondary metabolites are addressed by the toxicological examination of the food enzymes.

The Panel considered that the information provided on the purity of the food enzymes was sufficient.

Viable cells of the production strain

The absence of viable cells of the production strain in the native food enzyme was demonstrated in three independent batches analysed in triplicate. Ten grams of product was diluted in 40 mL of sterile water. From this, 5 mL was spread onto non‐selective agar plates and incubated at 30°C for up to 6 days. No colonies were detected. A positive control was included. 32

Considering the further heat treatment and purification steps, the Panel considered that the results can be extended to the heat‐treated food enzyme.

Toxicological data

A battery of toxicological tests including a bacterial reverse mutation test (Ames test), an in vitro mammalian chromosomal aberration test, an in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test and a repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study in rats has been provided.

Batch 4 (Table 1) as one of the batches intended for commercialisation and batch 5 (Table 1) used in these studies have a similar enzyme activity/TOS ratio to the other batches intended for commercialisation and thus are considered suitable as test items.

Batch 4 and 5 are the native food enzymes with two claimed enzyme activities. The native food enzyme was tested in the three genotoxicity tests (batch 4 and 5) and in the repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study (batch 4). In addition, batch 4 was also tested after a heat treatment (without further purification) in the repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study. The heat treatment reduces enzymatic activity, and the additional filtration step applied for the commercial batches allows a further purification of the food enzyme. Therefore, the Panel accepted to use results generated from the heat‐treated batch 4 to evaluate the heat‐treated commercial food enzyme.

Genotoxicity

Bacterial reverse mutation test

A bacterial reverse mutation test (Ames test) was performed according to the Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development (OECD) Test Guideline 471 (OECD, 1997) and following good laboratory practice (GLP). 33 Four strains of Salmonella Typhimurium (TA98, TA100, TA1535 and TA1537) and Escherichia coli WP2uvrA were used with or without metabolic activation (S9‐mix), applying the pre‐incubation method and ‘treat and wash’ method. The experiments were carried out in triplicate.

A dose‐finding study using the pre‐incubation method was carried out using eight concentrations of the food enzyme ranging from 11 to 23,400 μg TOS/plate. Upon treatment with the food enzyme, an increase in the number of revertant colonies was observed in strains TA98, TA100, TA1535 and TA1537 without S9‐mix, and in strains TA100, TA1535 and TA98 and E. coli WP2uvrA with S9‐mix. A spot‐like growth inhibition of background bacteria was seen at ≥ 289 μg TOS/plate in strain TA100 with and without S9‐mix. The growth stimulation of background bacteria with a cloudy medium was observed at ≥ 2600 μg TOS/plate in all S. Typhimurium strains tested and at ≥ 867 μg TOS/plate in E. coli WP2uvrA without S9‐mix, and at ≥ 867 μg TOS/plate in all S. Typhimurium strains tested and at ≥ 96 μg TOS/plate in E. coli WP2uvrA with S9‐mix.

The main study using the pre‐incubation method was carried out using seven concentrations of the food enzyme ranging from 366 to 23,400 μg TOS/plate in E. coli WP2uvrA without S9‐mix and in strain TA1537 with S9‐mix. A growth stimulation of background bacteria with a cloudy medium was observed at ≥ 731 μg TOS/plate in E. coli WP2uvrA without S9‐mix and at ≥ 366 μg TOS/plate in strain TA1537 with S9‐mix. Upon treatment with the food enzyme, there was no biologically relevant increase in the number of revertant colonies above the control values in any strain tested, with or without S9‐mix.

The growth stimulation of background bacteria, resulting in a cloudy medium, observed in the above study was attributed by the study authors to the presence of free amino acids in the medium. Consequently, a second range‐finding study followed by a second main experiment was carried out applying the ‘treat and wash’ method to exclude the effect of free amino acids.

The second dose‐finding study using ‘treat and wash’ method was carried out using six concentrations of the food enzyme ranging from 96 to 23,400 μg TOS/plate in strains TA98, TA100, TA1535 and TA1537 without S9‐mix and in strains TA98, TA100, TA1535 and E. coli WP2uvrA with S9‐mix. Growth inhibition was observed at 23,400 μg TOS/plate in strains TA100 and TA98 with S9‐mix. Upon treatment with the food enzyme, there was no biologically relevant increase in the number of revertant colonies above the control values in any strain tested with or without S9‐mix.

The second main study using ‘treat and wash’ method was carried out using six or seven concentrations of the food enzyme ranging from 366 or 731 to 23,400 μg TOS/plate in strains TA98, TA100, TA1535 and TA1537 without S9‐mix and in strains TA98, TA100, TA1535 and E. coli WP2uvrA with S9‐mix. Growth inhibition was observed at 23,400 μg TOS/plate in strain TA100 without S9‐mix and at ≥ 11,700 μg TOS/plate in TA100 and TA98 strains with S9‐mix. Upon treatment with the food enzyme, there was no biologically relevant increase in the number of revertant colonies above the control values in any strain tested, with or without S9‐mix.

The Panel concluded that the food enzyme did not induce gene mutations under the test conditions applied in this study.

In vitro mammalian chromosomal aberration test

The in vitro mammalian chromosomal aberration test was carried out according to the OECD Test Guideline 473 (OECD, 2016a) and following GLP. 34 An experiment was performed with duplicate cultures of human peripheral whole blood lymphocytes. The cell cultures were treated with the food enzyme either with or without metabolic activation (S9‐mix).

In a dose‐finding test, an inhibition of mitosis of at least 50% was seen at ≥ 9360 μg TOS/mL in the short‐term treatment without and with S9‐mix and at ≥ 240 μg TOS/mL in the long‐term treatment without S9‐mix. The concentrations causing 50% mitotic inhibition were calculated to be 4636 μg TOS/mL in the short‐term treatment without S9‐mix, 5443 μg TOS/mL in the short‐term treatment with S9‐mix and 142 μg TOS/mL in the long‐term treatment without S9‐mix, respectively.

Based on these results, in the main experiment, cells were exposed to the food enzyme and scored for chromosomal aberrations at concentrations of 1920, 3830 and 4790 μg TOS/mL in a short‐term treatment (3‐h exposure and 21‐h recovery period) without S9‐mix, at concentrations of 1920, 3830 and 5990 μg TOS/mL in a short‐term treatment with S9‐mix and at concentrations of 54, 90 and 125 μg TOS/mL in the long‐term treatment (24‐h exposure without recovery period) without S9‐mix.

No cytotoxicity was seen either in the short‐term treatment (with or without S9‐mix) or in long‐term treatment. The frequency of structural and numerical aberrations was not statistically significantly different to the negative controls at any concentration tested.

The Panel concluded that the food enzyme did not induce an increase in the frequency of structural and numerical aberrations under the test conditions applied in this study.

In vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test

The in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test was carried out according to the OECD Test Guideline 487 (OECD, 2016b) and following GLP. 35 An experiment was performed with duplicate cultures of human peripheral whole blood lymphocytes. The cell cultures were treated with the food enzyme with or without metabolic activation (S9‐mix).

In a range‐finding test, cytotoxicity of at least 58% was seen at ≥ 84 μg TOS/mL in the long‐term treatment without S9‐mix.

Based on these results, in the main experiment, cells were exposed to the food enzyme and scored for the frequency of bi‐nucleated cells with micronuclei (MNBN) at concentrations of 1000, 2000 and 5000 μg TOS/mL in a short‐term treatment (3‐h exposure and 21‐h recovery period) either with or without S9‐mix and at concentrations of 40, 50, 80 and 85 μg TOS/mL in a long‐term treatment (24‐h exposure and 24‐h recovery period) without S9‐mix.

No cytotoxicity was seen in the short‐term treatment with and without S9‐mix. In the long‐term treatment, cytotoxicity of 52% was observed at the highest concentration tested. The frequency of MNBN was not statistically significantly different to the negative controls at any concentration tested.

The Panel concluded that the food enzyme did not induce an increase in the frequency of MNBN under the test conditions applied in this study.

Conclusion on genotoxicity.

The Panel concluded that the native food enzyme did not induce gene mutations in bacteria and chromosomal damage.

The Panel extended this conclusion to the heat‐treated food enzyme considering that testing the native form represents a more conservative scenario for the genotoxicity evaluation than testing the heat‐treated form, as the heat treatment reduced enzymatic activity and the additional filtering step allowed a further purification of the food enzyme.

Repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study in rodents

The repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study was performed under GLP and according to the OECD Test Guideline 408 (OECD, 2018). 36 , 37

Groups of 10 male and 10 female Sprague–Dawley (Crl:CD(SD)) [SPF] rats received the native food enzyme with two claimed activities by gavage at doses of 146, 585 or 2340 mg TOS/kg bw per day. Furthermore, a group receiving the heat‐treated food enzyme (conditions of treatment: 70°C for 30 min) at a dose of 2340 mg TOS/kg bw per day was included (comparative high‐dose group). Controls received the vehicle (water for injection).

One high‐dose male was found dead on day 32 of administration. The Panel considered that this animal died of respiratory disorder due to acute inflammation in the lungs caused by mis‐dosing of the test substance, based on the gross and microscopic changes.

The body weight was statistically significantly decreased on day 36 of administration in mid‐dose females (−8%). The Panel considered the change as not toxicologically relevant, as it was only recorded at a single time interval, it was only observed in one sex, there was no dose–response relationship, the change was small and without a statistically significant effect on the final body weight and the final body weight gain.

The feed consumption was statistically significantly decreased in low‐dose females in weeks 3 and 4 (−10% and −14%) and in mid‐dose females in weeks 3–5 (−10% in all) and in weeks 10–12 (−11% in all). The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant, as they were only recorded at single time intervals, they were only observed in one sex, there was no dose–response relationship and there was no statistically significant change in the final feed consumption, the body weight and the body weight gain.

In the functional observations, a statistically significant decrease in the number of movements in the 40‐ to 50‐min interval was observed in high‐dose females (−19%). The Panel considered the change as not toxicologically relevant, as it was only recorded at a single time interval, it was only observed in one sex and there was no change in the total number of movements.

Haematological investigations revealed a statistically significant increase in basophil count in high‐dose males (+100%), a decrease in haematocrit (HCT) and haemoglobin (Hgb) (−4% both) in low‐dose males, a decrease in neutrophil count in high‐dose females (−30%), a decrease in reticulocyte ratio (Ret ratio; −18%) and reticulocyte count (Ret; −19%) in low‐dose females, an increase in prothrombin time (PT) in low‐dose females (+4%) and an increase in activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) in high‐dose males (+13%). The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant, as they were only observed in one sex (all parameters), there was no dose–response relationship (HCT, Hgb, Ret ratio, Ret, PT), there were no changes in other relevant parameters (other red and white blood cell parameters), there were no histopathological changes in bone marrow, spleen, lymph nodes and the changes were within the historical control values (all parameters).

Clinical chemistry investigations revealed a statistically significant decrease in total bilirubin (TBil) in high‐dose males and females (−25% and −20%), a decrease in total protein (TP) and albumin (Alb) (−3% both) and an increase in α2‐globulin ratio and concentration (+16% and +11%) in high‐dose males, a decrease in triglycerides (TG, −4%), creatinine (Crea, −12%), alkaline phosphatase (ALP, −16%) and γ‐glutamyl transpeptidase (γ‐GTP, −40%) in high‐dose females and a decrease in Alb in mid‐dose females (−9%). The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant, as they were only observed in one sex (except for TBil, Alb), there was no dose–response relationship (Alb in females), there were no changes in other relevant parameters (other liver enzymes, BUN, globulin, A/G ratio), there were no histopathological changes in liver and kidneys and the changes were within the historical control values (bilirubin, triglycerides).

The urinalysis revealed a statistically significant decrease in the total excretion of potassium (K) in high‐dose males (−17%) and in low‐, mid‐ and high‐dose females (−23%, −22% and −31%), a decrease in urinary volume in high‐dose females (−29%), a decrease in total excretion of sodium (Na) in low‐, mid‐ and high‐dose females (−18%, −20% and −31%), a decrease in total excretion of chloride (Cl) in low‐ and high‐dose females (−23% and −29%). In addition, the urinalysis revealed a statistically significant increase in the numbers of protein‐ and urobilinogen‐positive animals in mid‐ and high‐dose males and females, an increase in ketone body‐positive animals in high‐dose males and females and similarly observed in the comparative high‐dose group. The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant, as they were only observed in one sex (urinary volume, Na, Cl), there was no dose–response relationship (K in females, Cl), there were no changes in other relevant parameters (blood K, Cl and Na concentrations), there were no histopathological changes in kidneys and the changes were within the historical control values. Furthermore, the differences in the number of protein‐ and urobilinogen‐positive animals were also considered not toxicologically relevant in the absence of changes of urea and creatinine and kidney histopathology.

Statistically significant changes detected in organ weights were an increase in the relative spleen weight in mid‐dose males (+11%), a decrease in the absolute heart weight in low‐dose females (−10%), a decrease in the relative liver weight in mid‐dose females (−4%). The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant, as they were only observed in one sex (all parameters), there was no dose–response relationship (all parameters), the changes were small and there were no histopathological changes in the spleen, liver and heart.

The macroscopic examination revealed lesions in stomach: white patches in one mid‐dose male and in one low‐ and one high‐dose female, brown patches in one high‐dose female, nodules in one control female and in one high‐dose female, and black patches in one female of comparative high‐dose group.

The microscopic examination revealed in forestomach an erosion/ulcer in high‐dose males and females (1/10 and 3/10 vs. 0/10 in the controls) and a squamous cyst in mid‐dose males (1/10) and low‐dose females (1/10). In the glandular stomach, a hypertrophy of mucous cells in high‐dose males (8/9 vs. 0/10 in the control) and in high‐dose females (5/10 vs. 0/10 in the control) and a globule leucocyte infiltration in submucosa of glandular stomach in mid‐ and high‐dose males (4/10 and 5/9 vs. 0/10 in the control) and in mid‐ and high‐dose females (1/10 and 4/10 vs. 0/10 in the control) were observed. In the comparative high‐dose group, slight globule leucocyte infiltration of the submucosa in one male and slight haemorrhage with degeneration of surface mucous cells in one female were observed.

The Panel regarded the histopathological findings observed in the native enzyme‐treated groups as test item‐related and adverse at high dose considering the presence of mucosal ulceration. The findings observed in the comparative high‐dose group were also regarded test item‐related, but not adverse considering the low incidence and minimal severity. Moreover, the Panel considered all changes observed related to the irritant properties of both test items. The differences observed in the nature of changes, incidence and severity at high dose between the native and heat‐treated enzyme groups confirm the proteolytical activity of the enzyme as the origin of gastric findings.

No other toxicologically relevant or statistically significant differences from controls were reported for the native food enzyme. No toxicologically relevant differences from the control were reported for the heat‐treated food enzyme (batch 4) at the single dose tested of 2340 mg TOS/kg bw per day.

The Panel identified a no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) of 585 mg TOS/kg bw per day for the native enzyme. For the heat‐treated food enzyme (batch 4), no adverse effects were identified at 2340 mg TOS/kg bw per day, the only dose tested.

Allergenicity

The allergenicity assessment considered only the food enzymes and not additives, carriers or other excipients that may be used in the final formulation.

The potential allergenicity of the food enzymes containing oryzin and leucyl aminopeptidase from the Aspergillus sp. strain FL 72–230 was assessed by comparing their amino acid sequences with those of known allergens as described in the EFSA GMO Scientific Opinion (EFSA GMO Panel, 2010). Using higher than 35% identity in a sliding window of 80 amino acids as the criterion, no match was found for leucyl aminopeptidase, and matches with two food allergens, three contact allergens and 20 respiratory allergens were found for oryzin in the AllergenOnline database. 38

The matching food allergens were Bac s 1 (46.9%), a nattokinase from Bacillus subtilis and Cuc m 1 (35.6% sequence identity), a subtilisin‐like protease from melon (Cucumis melo).

The matching contact allergens were Tri r 2 (58.8% sequence identity), a subtilisin‐like protease from the Athlete's foot fungus (Trichophyton rubrum) and subtilisin‐like proteases from T. benhamiae and T. schoenleinii (57.5% sequence identity).

The matching respiratory allergens (43.9%–98.8% sequence identity) were serine proteases from different species: Aspergillus flavus, A. oryzae, A. fumigatus, A. versicolor, A. niger, Rhodotorula mucilaginosa, Penicillium chrysogenum, P. oxalicum, P. citrinum, Alternaria alternata, Cochliobolus lunatus, Cladosporium cladosporioides, Fusarium proliferatum, Davidiella tassiana, Bacillus lentus and B. licheniformis.

No reports on oral or respiratory sensitisation or elicitation reactions of the food enzymes under assessment have been published. 39

Bac s 1, the nattokinase from B. subtilis, has been identified as a food allergen. Few case reports of allergic reactions after consumption of natto have been reported (Inomata et al., 2012; Suzuki et al., 2023).

Allergic reactions after consumption of melon have been reported (Hassan & Venkatesh, 2015; Neeharika & Sunkar, 2021).

Concerning the matches with respiratory allergens which were all serine proteases, the Panel notes that several studies have shown that individuals respiratorily sensitised to a food enzyme are usually able to ingest the corresponding enzyme without acquiring clinical symptoms of food allergy (Armentia et al., 2009; Cullinan et al., 1997; Poulsen, 2004).

No allergic reactions to oral ingestion of the contact allergens (alkaline proteases) from Trichophyton rubrum, T. benhamiae and T. schoenleinii, fungi residing on the skin, are expected.

The Panel considered that the results of the sequence homology search and the available literature indicate a risk of allergic reactions for natto and melon allergic individuals upon dietary exposure to the food enzymes under assessment.

The production strain belongs to the Aspergillus genus, which is known to cause respiratory allergy (Kurup et al., 2000; Shen & Han, 1998; Vermani et al., 2015). Allergic reactions upon dietary exposure have been observed, but are rare (Xing et al., 2022). The biomass is removed during the production process; however, allergenic proteins of the production strain can be released into the culture medium from which the food enzyme is obtained.

■■■■■, a product of ■■■■■ that may cause allergies or intolerances (listed in the Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 40 ), is used as raw material. In addition, ■■■■■ and ■■■■■, known sources of allergens, are also present in the culture medium. During the fermentation process, these products will be mostly degraded and utilised by the production strain.

Taken together, concerning the potential allergic reactions due to the production strain and the raw materials in the culture medium, the Panel considered that residual amounts of allergenic proteins could be present in the food enzymes. Taking into account the levels of dietary exposure (see Section 3.5), this would result in minute amounts in the final foods, from which allergic reactions are usually not expected.

In conclusion, when used for the production of distilled alcohols, the Panel considered that a risk of allergic reactions upon dietary exposure can be excluded. For the remaining intended uses, the risk of allergic reactions upon dietary exposure to the food enzymes, particularly for natto and melon allergic individuals, cannot be excluded. However, the likelihood of such reactions will not exceed the risk of reactions after natto and melon consumption.

In the production of flavouring preparations from dairy products, the food enzymes are added to dairy products (e.g. butter, cheese) after the first pasteurisation 52 to enhance the sensory properties. The food enzyme–TOS remain in the final products.

In the production of modified milk proteins, the food enzymes are added to casein or whey prior to pasteurisation 53 to improve solubility and taste. The food enzyme–TOS remain in the modified milk proteins.

In the production of hydrolysed proteins, the food enzymes are added to a variety of protein‐rich materials from plant (e.g. soy, pea) 54 or animal (e.g. animal skin, fish scales) 55 sources. The food enzyme–TOS remain in these protein hydrolysates.

In the production of baked products and in the production of cereal‐based products other than baked, the food enzymes are added to flour during dough and batter formation 56 to catalyse the hydrolysis of gluten proteins, facilitating the handling of the dough. The food enzyme‐TOS remain in the final products.

In the production of brewed products, the food enzymes are added to cereals during the mashing for the production of beer and beer‐like beverages. 57 In the production of fermented beverages such as sake, they may be added before or after the koji fermentation 58 to catalyse the release of free amino nitrogen for the growth of yeast during alcoholic fermentation. The food enzyme–TOS remain in the brewed products.

In the production of distilled alcohol, the food enzymes may be added to cereals during slurry mixing, liquefaction, saccharification or fermentation 59 to improve the yield and to enhance the access of amylolytic enzymes to the starch granules, facilitating the degradation of starch and non‐starch polysaccharides into fermentable sugars. The food enzyme‐TOS are not carried over into the distilled alcohols (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023).

In the production of non‐wine vinegar, the food enzymes are added to cereals or to fruit during the saccharification 60 to enhance alcoholic fermentation and prevent haze formation during storage. The food enzyme–TOS remain in the non‐wine vinegars.

In the production of tea and other herbal and fruit infusions, the food enzymes are added to tea leaves during the extraction 61 to improve the sensory properties of the infusions. The food enzyme–TOS remain in the final products.

In the production of plant extracts, 62 the food enzymes are added to the aqueous slurry before filtration 63 to improve the sensory properties of the final food ingredients, in which the food enzyme–TOS remain.

In the production of plant‐based analogues of milk and milk products, the food enzymes may be added to plant materials after the milling or during the saccharification 64 to increase the yield and to enhance the flavour. The food enzyme–TOS remain in the final products.

In the production of soy sauce, the food enzymes are added after fermentation 65 to improve the sensory properties of the final products. The food enzyme–TOS remain in the soy products.

In the processing of yeast and yeast products, the food enzymes are added to the yeast biomass during the lysis 66 to intensify the savoury taste of the resulting yeast extracts. 67 The food enzyme–TOS remain in the final food products.

Based on the data provided on thermostability of the food enzymes (see Section 3.3.1) and the downstream processing within the respective food manufacturing processes, the Panel considered that the food enzymes are inactivated in most of the food manufacturing processes listed in Table 3 in which the food enzyme–TOS remain. However, they may remain in their active form in baked products, depending on the heat treatment conditions.

Margin of exposure

A comparison of the NOAEL (585 mg TOS/kg bw per day) for the native food enzyme, identified from the 90‐day rat study, with the derived exposure estimates of 0.016–0.182 mg TOS/kg bw per day at the mean and from 0.051 to 0.532 mg TOS/kg bw per day at the 95th percentile resulted in a margin of exposure (MoE) of at least 1100.

For the heat‐treated food enzyme, using a single dose tested of 2340 mg TOS/kg bw per day not showing adverse effects, the MoE is at least 4398.

CONCLUSIONS

Based on the data provided and the derived margin of exposure, the Panel concluded that the native food enzyme containing oryzin and leucyl aminopeptidase activities produced with the non‐genetically modified Aspergillus sp. strain FL 72–230 and the heat‐treated food enzyme containing only the leucyl aminopeptidase activity do not give rise to safety concerns under the intended conditions of use.
