Bounds on the realizations of zero-nonzero patterns and sign conditions of polynomials restricted to varieties and applications
Saugata Basu, Laxmi Parida

Abstract
. We prove upper bounds which are independent of the dimension of the ambient space, on the number of realizable zero-nonzero patterns as well as sign conditions (when the field of coefficients is ordered) of a finite set of polynomials 𝒫 \mathcal{P} restricted to some algebraic subset V V of an affine or projective space. Our bounds depend on the number and the degrees of the polynomials in 𝒫 \mathcal{P} , as well as the degree of V V and the dimension of V V , but are independent of the dimension of the space in which V V is embedded. This last feature of our bounds is useful in situations where the ambient dimension is much larger than dim V \dim V . We give several applications of our results. We generalize existing results on bounding the speeds of algebraically defined classes of graphs, bounding the ε {\varepsilon} -entropy of real varieties, as well as lower bounds in terms of the number of connected components for testing membership in semi-algebraic sets using algebraic computation trees. Motivated by quantum complexity theory we introduce a notion of relative rank (additive as well as multiplicative) in finite dimensional vector spaces and algebras relative to a fixed algebraic subset in the vector space or algebra – which generalizes the classical definition of ranks of tensors. We prove a very general lower bound on the maximum relative rank of finite subsets relative to algebraic subsets of bounded degree and dimension which is independent of the dimension of the vector space or algebra. We show how our lower bound implies a quantum analog of a classical lower bound result of Shannon for Boolean circuits – that almost all Boolean functions require (classical) circuits of size at least Ω ⁡ ( 2 n / n ) \Omega(2^{n}/n) even in the presence of a quantum oracle given by an algebraic subset (of the group of unitary transformations) of fixed degree and dimension.

Summary of main contributions

In this paper we consider varieties and polynomials over real and complex numbers (in fact more generally over arbitrary real closed and algebraically closed fields). We prove bounds on the number of realizable zero-nonzero patterns and sign conditions

These appear as Theorems

The independence of these bounds from

1.3.1.
Methods

Our techniques for dealing with zero-nonzero patterns and sign conditions are different. To prove the upper bound on the number of zero-nonzero patterns of a family of polynomials restricted to an algebraic subset
V
of

\mathbb{A}_{k}^{N}
, we closely follow the proof given in
[
25
,
8
]
,
with the modification that all algebraic subsets that are considered
in the proof are contained in
V
and we can bound their degrees in terms of
\deg V
and the degrees of the given polynomials.
We prove an upper bound on the sum of the degrees of the Zariski closure
of the realizations of all zero-nonzero patterns with non-empty realizations on
V
, which in turn gives an upper bound on the number
of realized zero-nonzero patterns.
An important role in the argument is played by the generalized Bezout inequality (Theorem
I
).

For the problem of bounding realizable sign conditions over ordered fields we
assume without loss of generality that the field
k=\mathrm{R}
is a real closed field. The main technique that is used for proving bounds on the number of realizable sign conditions (or more generally bounding the Betti numbers of these realizations) is via a technique of infinitesimal perturbations
(see for example,
[
6
,
7
]
). The idea is to perturb the given set of polynomials using infinitesimal elements in some real closed extension of
\mathrm{R}
, so that:

(a)

The polynomials in the new set have the same degrees as ones in the given set, but in addition have better algebraic properties – like being in general position. These algebraic properties help control the combinatorial part of the bound.

(b)

The realizations of the various realizable sign conditions are related to those of the given set in some way, so that bounding the number (or more generally the Betti numbers) of
realizations of the sign conditions of the new set of polynomials, suffices to bound the corresponding quantity for the original set.

(c)

Finally, the problem of bounding the number of realizable sign conditions (or more generally the Betti numbers of the realizations) can be reduced to bounding the corresponding quantity for all the non-empty algebraic subsets that one can define using the perturbed set of polynomials.

Our technique for proving upper bounds on the number of realizable
sign conditions follow a similar paradigm but meets two important obstacles.

(a)

Since we are not given the polynomials that define
V
, we are not able to deform
V
infinitesimally maintaining its degree;

(b)

as a result while we are able to reduce using usual techniques to the case bounding the number of semi-algebraically connected components of certain algebraic subsets of
V(\mathrm{R})
, all existing bounds for bounding the number of semi-algebraically connected components of possibly singular real algebraic sets depend exponentially on the dimension of the ambient space.

We overcome these problems by first reducing the problem to the problem of bounding the number of semi-algebraically connected components of an appropriate number of real algebraic subsets
X(\mathrm{R})\subset V(\mathrm{R})
,
of co-dimension one in
V
.
then after taking the image under a generic projection
\pi
to
\mathrm{R}^{p}
(where

p=\dim V
), to the problem of bounding the number of semi-algebraically connected components of the semi-algebraic set consisting of the
\mathrm{R}
-points of
\pi(X)-\mathrm{Sing}(\pi(X))
. We can use the classical bound due to
Oleĭnik and Petrovskiĭ
[
34
]
to bound the latter quantity. This bound depends exponentially on the dimension of the ambient space, but since this is
p
in this situation we can tolerate the exponential dependence
on
p
. The technique described above for obtaining bounds on the number of semi-algebraically connected components of a real variety only in terms of its degree is new and in our opinion the most important technical contribution of the paper.

1.3.2.
Tightness

Our bound (on the sum of the degrees of the realizations) in the algebraically closed case is tight (see Remark
5
).
In the real closed case, our main result applies to a certain slightly restricted class of algebraic subsets of
\mathrm{R}^{N}

that includes most real algebraic sets that occur in practice.
The bound is tight in this case as well up to a factor of
O(1)^{\dim V}

(see Remark

Bounding the number of realizable zero-nonzero patterns of

(see for example

,

,

In fact, if

can be made to depend just on

If we formulate Theorem

an improvement of known lower bound results for algebraic computation trees (Section
3.3
);

(d)

lower bounds on the relative ranks, additive as well as multiplicative
in vector spaces and algebras respectively,
and over algebraically closed as well as real closed fields

and finally,

(e)

how the lower bound on ranks of subsets in an algebra imply

Section

2.
Upper bounds on zero-nonzero patterns and sign conditions

2.1.
Bound on the number of realizable zero-nonzero patterns

For any finite set
\mathcal{P}
of polynomials in
k[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
,
V
an algebraic subset of
k^{N}
, and

\pi\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{P}}
, we denote by

{\mathcal{R}}(\pi,V)=\{x\in V\mid\mathrm{sgn}(P(x))=\pi(P),P\in\mathcal{P}\}.

We will denote

\Pi(\mathcal{P},V)=\{\pi\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{P}}\;\mid\;{\mathcal{R}}(\pi,V)\neq\emptyset\}

and call
\Pi(\mathcal{P},V)
the set of
realizable zero-nonzero patterns of
\mathcal{P}
on
V
.

We are now in a position to state our first result.

Theorem 1
.

Let
V\subset k^{N}
(resp.
V\subset\mathbb{P}_{k}^{n}
) be an algebraic subset
with
\deg V=D
,
\dim V=p
, and

\mathcal{P}\subset k[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]_{\leq d}
(resp.
\mathcal{P}\subset k[X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]_{\leq d}
) be a finite set of polynomials (resp. homogeneous polynomials), with
\mathrm{card}(\mathcal{P})=s
. Then,

\mathrm{card}(\Pi(\mathcal{P},V))\leq\sum_{\pi\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{P}}}\deg\overline{({\mathcal{R}}(\pi,V))}\leq D\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{p}\binom{s}{i}\cdot d^{i}

(where
\overline{({\mathcal{R}}(\pi,V))}
denotes the Zariski-closure of

{\mathcal{R}}(\pi,V)
).

Remark 5

(Tightness)
.

The bound in (the second inequality of)
Theorem
1
is tight. To see this let
N>2p
, and
V
be a union of

D
disjoint affine subspace of dimension
p
in
k^{N}
. Let

\mathcal{P}
be a set of
s
polynomials in
k[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
, where each
P\in\mathcal{P}
is a product of
d
generic polynomials of degree
1
. It is easy to see that with these choices,

\sum_{\pi\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{P}}}\deg\overline{({\mathcal{R}}(\pi,V))}=D\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{p}\binom{s}{i}\cdot d^{i}.

2.2.
Bound on the number of realizable sign conditions

Now suppose
\mathrm{R}
is a real closed field and let
\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{R}[i]
.
For an algebraic subset
V\subset\mathrm{C}^{N}
, we denote by
V(\mathrm{R})=V\cap\mathrm{R}^{N}

the set of its real points. We say that
V
is a
real algebraic set
(or a
real variety
if
V
is irreducible),
if
V
is closed under conjugation taking
z
to
\bar{z}
. It is easy to see that a real algebraic set is the vanishing locus of a finite number of polynomials in
\mathrm{R}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
.

For
x\in V(\mathrm{R})
, we will denote by
\dim_{x}V(\mathrm{R})
to be the local
real dimension (see for example
[
7
, page 191]
)
of
V(\mathrm{R})
at
x
(as a semi-algebraic set). If
V
is a non-singular variety of dimension
p
, then
\dim_{x}V(\mathrm{R})=p
for every
x\in V(\mathrm{R})
.

For any finite set
\mathcal{P}
of polynomials in
\mathrm{R}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
,
S
a semi-algebraic subset of
\mathrm{R}^{N}
, and

\sigma\in\{0,1,-1\}^{\mathcal{P}}
, we denote by

{\mathcal{R}}(\sigma,S)=\{x\in S\mid\mathrm{sign}(P(x))=\sigma(P),P\in\mathcal{P}\}.

We will denote

\Sigma(\mathcal{P},S)=\{\sigma\in\{0,1,-1\}^{\mathcal{P}}\;\mid\;{\mathcal{R}}(\sigma,S)\neq\emptyset\},

and call
\Sigma(\mathcal{P},S)
the set of
realizable sign conditions of
\mathcal{P}
on
S
.

Notation 2.1
.

For
S
a semi-algebraic set, we will denote by
\mathrm{Cc}(S)
the set
of semi-algebraically connected components of
S
.

Remark 6
.

Note that

\mathrm{card}(\mathrm{Cc}_{0}(S))=b_{0}(S)
.

We can now state our main theorems on bounding the number of semi-algebraically connected components of the realizations of realizable sign conditions of a finite set of polynomials restricted to the real points of a given algebraic set
V
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
V=V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{m}\subset\mathrm{C}^{N}
be a union of real varieties, with
\deg(V)=D
,
\dim V=p
, and such that for each
i,1\leq i\leq m
, and
x\in V_{i}(\mathrm{R})
,

\dim_{x}V_{i}(\mathrm{R})=\dim V_{i}
.
Let

\mathcal{P}\subset\mathrm{R}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]_{\leq d}
be a finite set of polynomials, with
\mathrm{card}(\mathcal{P})=s
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{card}(\Sigma(\mathcal{P},V))\leq\sum_{\sigma\in\{0,1,-1\}^{\mathcal{P}}}b_{0}({\mathcal{R}}(\sigma,V(\mathrm{R})))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O(D)^{p}\cdot\left(\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}4^{j}\binom{s}{j}d^{p}\right)+D\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O(D)^{p}((sd)^{p}+D).

Remark 7
.

The hypothesis on
V
is clearly satisfied if each
V_{i}
is non-singular of dimension
p
(see also Remark
4
).

Remark 8

(Tightness)
.

Consider the following example taking
p=1
and the dimension of the ambient space
N=2
.

Let
V
be the algebraic subset of
\mathrm{C}^{2}
defined by
Q=0
, where

Q=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\prod_{j=0}^{D-1}(X_{i}-j)^{2}-{\varepsilon},

where
{\varepsilon}>0
and will be chosen later.
Then,
p=\dim V=1
, and
\deg V=2D
. Moreover,
for all small enough
{\varepsilon}>0,{\varepsilon}\in\mathrm{R}
,

V
is non-singular and
V(\mathrm{R})
consists of
D^{2}
disjoint ovals,

V_{i,j},0\leq i,j,\leq D-1
, where
V_{i,j}
is a oval
with the point
(i,j)
in its interior.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{s}\}
be the set of polynomials, where

P_{i}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(X_{1}-\delta_{i,j}).

Then for

0<\delta_{1,1}\ll\delta_{1,2}\ll\cdots\ll\delta_{1,d}\ll\delta_{2,1}\ll\cdots\ll\delta_{s,d}\ll{\varepsilon}\ll 1,

(where
\ll
means sufficiently small), it is easy to verify that
for each
i,1\leq i\leq s
,

{\rm Z}(P_{i},\mathrm{R}^{2})
consists of

d
vertical lines which meets each of the ovals
V_{0,0},\ldots,V_{0,D-1}
in

2d
distinct points.

Thus,
{\rm Z}(\mathcal{P},\mathrm{R}^{2})
meets each of the ovals

V_{0,0},\ldots,V_{0,D-1}
is
2sd
distinct points, and the complement of these points in each oval is a set of
2sd
disjoint arcs.
Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\{0,1,-1\}^{\mathcal{P}}}b_{0}({\mathcal{R}}(\sigma,V(\mathrm{R})))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(4sdD+D(D-1))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(4sd+D-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega(\deg V)((sd)+\deg V),

matching the upper bound in Theorem
2
with
p=1
.
Similar examples can be created for
p>1
as well.

Theorem
2
may be interesting even in the case

\mathcal{P}=\emptyset
. In this case we just obtain a bound on the number of semi-algebraically connected components of
V
having the top real dimension.
In order to state this formally we introduce a new notation.

Notation 2.2
.

For any semi-algebraic set
S
and
p\geq 0
, we denote

S_{p}=\{x\in S\mid\dim_{x}S\geq p\}.

We have the following slight variant of Theorem
2

in the case of algebraic sets that will be useful later.

Theorem 3
.

Let
V\subset\mathrm{C}^{N}
be a real algebraic set, with
\deg(V)=D
, and

\dim V=p
.
Then,

(2.1)

b_{0}(V(\mathrm{R})_{p})\leq 2^{2p+3}\cdot D^{p+1}=(O(D))^{p+1}.

In particular,
if every semi-algebraically connected component of
V(\mathrm{R})
has (real) dimension equal to
p
,
then

b_{0}(V(\mathrm{R}))\leq 2^{2p+3}\cdot D^{p+1}=(O(D))^{p+1}.

Remark 9
.

As remarked earlier, many naturally occurring real varieties, such as Schubert subvarieties of flag varieties,
rank subvarieties in spaces of matrices, as well as other determinantal varieties, are not non-singular but satisfies the hypothesis of Theorem
2
. These varieties in
general will not satisfy the hypothesis of Theorem
F
because of the presence of singularities.

However, there exist real varieties for which the hypothesis of
Theorem
2
is not satisfied.

(a)

The singular curve
V\subset\mathrm{C}^{2}
in the plane defined by

Y^{2}=X^{2}(X-1)
. In this case the
\dim V=1
, but
V(\mathrm{R})
contains the isolated point
x=(0,0)
, and
\dim_{x}V(\mathrm{R})=0<1
. Thus,
V
does not satisfy the hypothesis of Theorem
2
.

(b)

Another interesting example
is the well-known Whitney umbrella,
V\subset\mathrm{C}^{3}
, defined by the equation
X^{2}-ZY^{2}=0
. In this case
V(\mathrm{R})
has only one connected component but every point
x\in V(\mathrm{R})
on the “stick” of the umbrella (namely the points satisfying
Z<0
) has local dimension
\dim_{x}V(\mathrm{R})=1<2=\dim V
.
The real variety
V
does not satisfy the
hypothesis of Theorem
2
(because of the lower dimensional stick of the umbrella).

We also prove a projective version of Theorem
2
.
It is no longer meaningful to talk about the sign of a (homogeneous)
polynomial in a real projective space. However, one still has the notion of zero-nonzero patterns. We bound the number of semi-algebraically connected components of the realizations on

V(\mathrm{R})
of all zero-nonzero patterns of a finite set of homogeneous polynomials, where
V
is a non-singular real projective variety.

Theorem 4

(Projective case)
.

Let
V=V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{m}\subset\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{C}}^{N}
be a union of real varieties, with
\deg(V)=D
,
\dim V=p
, and such that for each
i,1\leq i\leq m
, and
x\in V_{i}(\mathrm{R})
,

\dim_{x}V_{i}(\mathrm{R})=\dim V_{i}
, and let

\mathcal{P}\subset\mathrm{R}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]_{\leq d}
be a finite set of homogeneous polynomials, with
\mathrm{card}(\mathcal{P})=s
. Then,

\mathrm{card}(\Pi(\mathcal{P},V(\mathrm{R})))\leq\sum_{\pi\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{P}}}b_{0}({\mathcal{R}}(\pi,V(\mathrm{R})))\leq O(D)^{p}((sd)^{p}+D).

Remark 10
.

Aside from the other applications that we discuss in the next section
Theorem
4
can be used to simplify
proofs of certain quantitative results in discrete geometry. For example in
[
21
]
, the authors prove a bound on the number of geometric permutations induced by

. A key role is played by a bound on the number of realizable sign conditions of a certain finite set of polynomials restricted to the Grassmannian

(more accurately, the affine Grassmannian but this is unimportant). In order to get a tight bound on this quantity that does not depend on the dimension of the Plücker emdedding the
authors utilize a fact from real algebraic geometry – that the Grassmannian varieties,

can be embedded semi-algebraically (not scheme theoretically) as an algebraic subset of

[

, Theorem 3.4.4]
. This last fact is somewhat remarkable as there is no such affine embedding of the complex Grassmannian (indeed the complex Grassmannian is compact as a topological space, while every complex affine
algebraic set of positive dimension is necessarily non-compact). However, using Theorem
4
(noting that the Grassmannians are all non-singular projective varieties)
and the known expressions for the degree and the dimension of
\mathrm{Gr}(m,N)

(see (
3.5
)) one can obtain a similar bound
as in
[
21
, Theorem 2]
without resorting to the clever non-standard embedding of the real Grassmannian, and the new bound is a slight improvement over the original result when

is close to
d
.

The bound in Theorem
2
is worse than in Theorem
1
(in the dependence on
D
and

). We can prove a bound with a better dependence on

but with a more stringent assumption on

.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\overline{V}\subset\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{C}}^{N}
be a non-singular complete intersection real variety
with
\deg\overline{V}=D
and
\dim\overline{V}=p
. Let
V=\overline{V}\cap\mathrm{C}^{N}
denote the corresponding affine variety.

Let

\mathcal{P}\subset\mathrm{R}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]_{\leq d}
be a finite set of polynomials with

and

.

Then, for each

,

\sum_{\sigma\in\{0,1,-1\}^{\mathcal{P}}}b_{i}({\mathcal{R}}(\sigma,V(\mathrm{R})))\leq s^{p-i}\cdot D^{2}\cdot(O(d))^{p}.

In particular,

\sum_{\sigma\in\{0,1,-1\}^{\mathcal{P}}}b_{0}({\mathcal{R}}(\sigma,V(\mathrm{R})))\leq D^{2}\cdot(O(sd))^{p}.

We are able to remove the non-singularity restriction
in Theorem
5
.
However, the bound that we prove is on a priori smaller quantity which nevertheless arises in applications
(see for example Theorem

which plays an important role in applications of polynomial partitioning technique in problems of incidence geometry).

For

3.1.
Speeds of algebraic families of graphs

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field and
suppose that
F\subset k^{n}\times k^{n}
be a
fixed

constructible relation (i.e. a constructible subset of
k^{n}\times k^{n}
). A labelled (directed) graph
(V,E\subset V\times V)
, with
V=\{1,\ldots,n\}
is called an
F
-graph if there exists

(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{n})\in(k^{N})^{n}
, such that

(i,j)\in E\Leftrightarrow(\mathbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})\in F.

We call the family of
F
-graphs for a fixed constructible relation

F
, a constructible family of graphs.

Now suppose that
\mathrm{R}
is a real closed field and
suppose that
F\subset\mathrm{R}^{n}\times\mathrm{R}^{n}
be a
fixed

semi-algebraic relation (i.e. a semi-algebraic subset of
\mathrm{R}^{n}\times\mathrm{R}^{n}
). A labelled (directed) graph
(V,E\subset V\times V)
, with
V=\{1,\ldots,n\}
is called an
F
-graph if there exists

(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{n})\in(\mathrm{R}^{N})^{n}
, such that

(i,j)\in E\Leftrightarrow(\mathbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})\in F.

We call the family of
F
-graphs for a fixed semi-algebraic relation

F
, a semi-algebraic family of graphs.

Families of

-graphs, where

is a semi-algebraic relation, have been studied in graph theory from several perspectives such as Ramsey theory, Zarankiewicz problem etc.
(see for example,
[
1
,
17
]
). We will be concerned
with another very well-studied property, called “speed”, of such families.

The function

.

The following theorem (slightly simplified) is a key result in the
theory of semi-algebraic graphs.

Theorem G
.

[
38
, Theorem 1.2]

For every semi-algebraic relation

(where the

).

) or sign conditions (Theorems

and
C
).

A particular case might be of interest in the theory of geometrically defined graphs.

Example 3.2

(Schubert graphs)
.

Let
Z=\mathrm{Gr}(m,N)\subset\mathbb{P}_{k}^{\binom{N}{m}-1}
denote the
Plücker embedding of the Grassmannian of
m
-dimensional subspaces of

k^{N}
, and let
F
be any constructible relation in

\mathbb{P}_{k}^{\binom{N}{m}-1}\times\mathbb{P}_{k}^{\binom{N}{m}-1}
.
For example, the restriction of
F
to
Z\times Z

could be a “Schubert-type” relation – namely
(p,q)\in F
if and only
\dim p\cap q\geq i
for some fixed
i\geq 0
.

Then,

(3.1)

G_{Z,F}(n)=n^{(1+o(1))(m(N-m)+1)n}.

Remark 13
.

Notice that in Example
3.2
if one applies directly Theorem
G
then one would obtain

(3.2)

G_{Z,F}(n)\leq n^{(1+o(1))(\binom{N}{m}-1)n}

which is considerably worse in the exponent for
m\sim N/2
. However, representing
m
-dimensional subspaces in
k^{N}
, using
N\times m
matrices of full rank, it is possible to apply the same techniques as in the proof of
Theorem
G
to get

(3.3)

G_{Z,F}(n)\leq n^{(1+o(1))mNn}

which is better than inequality (
3.2
) but still worse than (
3.1
).

3.2.
Bound on the
{\varepsilon}
-entropy of real varieties

In this section we assume that
\mathrm{R}=\mathbb{R}
.

A quantitative result from real (or more accurately
metric
) algebraic geometry that is now of great interest in applications (in problems originating in machine learning) is a tight upper bound on the volume of an
{\varepsilon}
-tube around a real algebraic variety
V
intersected with a ball of a given radius. A recent result in this direction is
[
9
, Theorem 1.1]
which gives
a tight upper bound on this volume depending on the degree
d
of the polynomials defining
V
, the codimension of
V
.

More recently, using very different techniques (originally due to Vitushkin
[
45
]
), Zhang and Kileel
[
51
]
proved another bound on the volume of such tubes, which matches the bound in
[
9
]
in the regime large
N
and
fixed
d
.
They applied their results to prove new bounds in approximation properties of low rank tensors, on the required dimension for randomized sketching algorithms, and
generalization error bounds for deep neural networks
with rational or ReLU activation functions.
Their technique depends crucially on a bound on the number of connected components of real algebraic varieties and any improvement
on this bound will automatically improve the bound in their paper.
In particular, inequality (
2.1
) is applicable in their context.

We follow the same notation as in
[
51
]
. Let
V(\mathrm{R})\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a real variety, and for
{\varepsilon}>0
denote by
\mathcal{N}(V(\mathrm{R}),{\varepsilon})
the minimum number of
{\varepsilon}
-balls required to cover
V(\mathrm{R})\cap B_{N}(\mathbf{0},1)
. The quantity
\log\mathcal{N}(V(\mathrm{R}),{\varepsilon})
is referred to as the
{\varepsilon}
-entropy of
V(\mathrm{R})\cap B_{N}(\mathbf{0},1)
and an upper bound on
\mathcal{N}(V(\mathrm{R}),{\varepsilon})
implies an upper bound on the volume of the

{\varepsilon}
-tube around
V(\mathrm{R})\cap B_{N}(\mathbf{0},1)
.

The following result appears in
[
51
, page 4, Table 1, entry on real varieties]
.

Theorem H
.

Let
V(\mathrm{R})\subset B_{N}(\mathbf{0},1)
be a real variety defined by polynomials of degrees at most
d
and
\dim V=p
. Then,

(3.4)

\log\mathcal{N}(V(\mathrm{R}),{\varepsilon})\leq p\cdot\log(1/{\varepsilon})+O(N\cdot\log d+p\cdot\log N).

We prove the following theorem.

Theorem 10
.

Let
V\subset\mathrm{C}^{N}
be a real variety with
\deg(V)=D,\dim V=p
, such that
V(\mathrm{R})\subset B_{N}(\mathbf{0},1)
.
Then,

\log\mathcal{N}(V(\mathbb{R})_{p},{\varepsilon})\leq p\cdot\log(1/{\varepsilon})+O(p\cdot(\log\deg(V)+\log N))

(see Notation
2.2
).

In particular, if every point of
V(\mathrm{R})
has local real dimension equal to
p
, then

\log\mathcal{N}(V(\mathrm{R}),{\varepsilon})\leq p\cdot\log(1/{\varepsilon})+O(p\cdot(\log\deg(V)+\log N)).

Note that right-hand side of the inequality in Theorem
10
depends very weakly on the embedding dimension
N
(
\log N
instead of
N
) compared to
the bound in Theorem
H
. Notice also that while the bound in
Theorem
H
is in terms of the degrees of the real
polynomials cutting out

V
, the bound in Theorem
10
is in terms of the degree of
V
(as a complex variety) which is a more intrinsic invariant. It is well known that any affine variety of degree
D
in
\mathrm{C}^{N}
can be cut out by at most

N+1
polynomials of degree at most
D
. However, if we use this fact in conjunction with Theorem
H
, we will end up with a much weaker bound than the one in Theorem
10
(
N
instead of
\log N
in the expression on the right-hand side).

3.3.
Lower bounds for algebraic computation trees

We briefly recall the definition of an algebraic computation tree (see for example
[
19
]
from which the following description is taken).

Definition 3.3
.

An algebraic computation tree
T
with input variables

X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
is a tree having three kinds of vertices:

(a)

computation vertices having outdegree
1
;

(b)

branching vertices with outdegree
3
, and the tree outgoing edges labelled by
0
,
1
and
-1
;

(c)

leaves with outdegree
0
and labelled by “YES” or “NO”.

There is a variable
Y_{v}
attached to each vertex

v
of
T
.Moreover, an expression

Y_{v}=a*b
, where

*\in\{+,-,\times\}
to each computation vertex
v
,
and
a,b
are either real constants, or input variables, or variables
associated with predecessor vertices of
v
, or a combination of these.

At each branch vertex
v
, the variable
Y_{v}
is assigned the value which is either a real
constant, or an input variable, or a variable associated with a predecessor vertex. The
three outgoing edges of a branch vertex
v
labelled
0
,
1
or
-1
, then correspond to signs

Y_{v}=0
,
Y_{v}>0
,
Y_{v}<0
(respectively).

With each leaf
w
,
we associate naturally a
a basic semi-algebraic set
S_{w}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, defined
by a conjunction of inequalities of the type

Y_{v}>0
,
Y_{v}=0
,
Y_{v}<0
,
where
v
varies over all branch nodes on the path from from the root to the leaf
w
and the sign of
Y_{v}
corresponds to the sign of the branch
taken by the path.
The semi-algebraic set
S_{T}
associated to the tree
S
is defined as the union

S_{T}=\bigcup_{w\in\mathrm{Leaves}_{1}(T)}S_{w},

where
\mathrm{Leaves}_{1}(T)
denotes the set of leaf nodes of
T

labelled “YES”.

We say that the algebraic computation tree
T
tests membership in the semi-algebraic set
S
. The height of a tree is the length of the longest path from its root to a leaf node.

The interpretation of this model of computation is as follows. On an input

x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

the input variables
X_{i}
get the corresponding values
x_{i}
,
the arithmetic operations are executed in computation vertices
v
, and

Y_{v}
is assigned the corresponding real value.

At branch vertices, the outward branch is chosen corresponding to the assigned value of
Y_{v}
.
The input
x
belongs to the semi-algebraic set
S_{w}
if this process ends in the leaf
w
. If
w
is a leaf marked “YES”, then
x
is said to be
accepted
by the tree
T
.

It was proved by by Ben-Or
[
12
]
that for any semi-algebraic set
the height of an algebraic computation tree testing membership in a semi-algebraic set
S\subset\mathrm{R}^{N}
, is at least

c_{1}\log b_{0}(S)-c_{2}N
, where
and
c_{2},c_{2}>0
absolute constants.
Yao
[
50
]

proves that for any locally closed and bounded semi-algebraic set

S\subset\mathrm{R}^{N}
, the height of any algebraic computation tree testing membership in
S
is bounded by

c_{1}\log b^{BM}(S)-c_{2}N,

where
b^{BM}(S)
denotes the Borel-Moore Betti numbers of
S
(they can also be defined as the dimensions of the compactly supported cohomology groups
\mbox{\rm H}_{c}^{*}(S)
).
This result was extended recently by Gabrielov and Vorobjov
[
19
]
who proved that the height of an algebraic computation tree testing membership in a semi-algebraic set
S\subset\mathrm{R}^{N}
(not necessarily locally closed), is at least

c_{1}\frac{\log b_{m}(S)}{m+1}-c_{2}\frac{N}{m+1},

where
c_{1},c_{2}>0
are absolute constants.

We prove that the following theorem which in particular improves the original result of
Ben-Or in a direction that is independent of
[
50
]
and
[
19
]
.

Theorem 11
.

Let
S\subset\mathrm{R}^{N}
be a semi-algebraic set contained in an real algebraic set
V
.
Suppose that
V
is a union of
non-singular real algebraic varieties,
then the height of an algebraic computation tree testing membership in
S
, is at least

\frac{1}{(1+(\log\deg V))}(c_{1}\log b_{0}(S)-c_{2}\dim V),

where
and
c_{1},c_{2}>0
absolute constants.

Remark 14
.

Note that the above lower bounds are independent of the ambient dimension
N
.
As in previous remarks this is useful in situations where
\dim V

and
\log\deg V
are very small compared to the dimension of the ambient space.

Example 3.3
.

For example, consider the Grassmannian variety
\mathrm{Gr}(m,N)
of
m
-dimensional subspaces in
\mathrm{C}^{N}
, as a subvariety of
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{C}}^{\binom{N}{m}-1}
.
Let
V=V_{m,N}\subset\mathrm{C}^{\binom{N}{m}}
be the affine cone
over
\mathrm{Gr}(m,N)
. Then, it is well known
(see for example
[
33
, Corollary 3.2.14]
) that

(3.5)

\deg(V_{m,N})=\deg(\mathrm{Gr}_{m,N})=\frac{0!1!\cdots(n-1)!}{(m+1)!\cdots(m+n-1)!}(mn)!,

where
n=N-m
,
while

\dim V_{m,N}=m(N-m)+1.

This implies that

\deg(V_{m,N})=O(1)^{m(N-m)}
, and hence

\log(\deg(V_{m,N}))=O(m(N-m))
.

Now suppose that
S\subset V_{m,N}(\mathrm{R})
is a semi-algebraic subset.
Then, the lower bound in Theorem
11
yields that the
depth of any algebraic computation tree testing membership in
S
is
at least

c_{1}\frac{\log b_{0}(S)}{m(N-m)}-c_{2},

while the result of Ben-Or implies a lower bound of

c_{1}\log b_{0}(S)-c_{2}\binom{N}{m}

(with different constants
c_{1},c_{2}
).

If
m>2
and
b_{0}(S)<\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}\binom{N}{m}
, then the last inequality will not give a non-trivial lower bound, while Theorem
11
will yield a non-trivial bound.

3.4.
Relative ranks in vector spaces and algebras

The notion of rank of tensors play a very important role
in many applications – from understanding the complexity of matrix multiplication, to machine learning and quantum information theory
(see for example
[
30
]
for an overview). We introduce here
a notion of relative rank for finite dimensional vector spaces and algebras (relative to an arbitrary algebraic subset
\Delta
of the vector space or algebra) which generalizes the notion of rank for tensors. We prove lower bounds on the relative rank for algebraically defined subsets
W
of the vector space or algebra, in terms of the dimension and degree of
\Delta
and the cardinality (or the number of connected components) of the set (Theorems
12
,
13
,

14
and
15
below).

Note that while proving upper bounds on the classical rank of specific tensors (such as the matrix multiplication tensor)
[
30
]
is an important and active area of research, proving lower bounds on the rank of sets of
tensors (particularly in the relative setting) has not been equally well studied. We believe the results stated below will prove useful in applications in addition to quantum complexity theory that we discuss here.

3.4.1.
Relative rank in vector spaces

Definition 3.4

(Relative rank in vector spaces)
.

Let
V
be a
k
-vector space, and
\Delta\subset V
. For
v\in V
, we define

\mathrm{rank}_{V,\Delta}(v)

as the smallest number
t
, if it exists, such that there exists
v_{1},\ldots,v_{t}\in\Delta
such that
v=v_{1}+\cdots+v_{t}
,
and
\infty
otherwise. We will call

\mathrm{rank}_{V,\Delta}(v)
the rank of
v
relative to
\Delta
.

For any subset
W\subset V
, we will denote

\mathrm{rank}_{V,\Delta}(W)=\max_{w\in W}\mathrm{rank}_{\Delta}(w).

Remark 15
.

Note that if
\Delta\subset\Delta^{\prime}
, then
\mathrm{rank}_{V,\Delta}(v)\geq\mathrm{rank}_{V,\Delta^{\prime}}(v)
for all
v\in V
, and

\mathrm{rank}_{V,\Delta}(W)\geq\mathrm{rank}_{V,\Delta^{\prime}}(W)
for all subsets

W\subset V
.

Remark 16
.

Notice that Definition
3.4
generalizes the standard notion of rank of a tensor. For example, if
V=V_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes V_{m}
, and
\Delta=\{v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{m}\mid v_{i}\in V_{i},1\leq i\leq m\}
the subset of tensors of rank at most one, then for all
v\in V
,
\mathrm{rank}_{V,\Delta}(v)
is the usual rank of
v
as an element of the tensor product
V_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes V_{m}
.

Theorem 12
.

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field and
V
a finite dimensional
vector space.
Let
\Delta\subset V
be an algebraic subset with
\dim(\Delta)=p
and
\deg(\Delta)=D
, and

\mathcal{P}\subset k[V]_{\leq\delta}
with
\mathrm{card}(\mathcal{P})=s
.
Then, for any finite
\mathcal{P}
-constructible subset
W\subset A

\mathrm{rank}_{V,\Delta}(W)\in\Omega\left(\frac{\log(\mathrm{card}(W))}{p(\log\delta+\log s)+\log D}\right)

(where the constant in the
\Omega(\cdot)
is an absolute constant).

In the real closed case we have a slightly worse lower bound (with
an added non-singularity assumption on
V
).

Theorem 13
.

Let
\mathrm{R}
be an real closed field and
V
a finite dimensional

\mathrm{R}
-vector space.
Let
\Delta\subset V
be an algebraic set,
which is a union of
non-singular real varieties,
with
\dim(\Delta)=p
and
\deg(\Delta)=D
, and

\mathcal{P}\subset\mathrm{R}[V]_{\leq\delta}
with
\mathrm{card}(\mathcal{P})=s
.
Then, for any finite
\mathcal{P}
-semi-algebraic subset
W\subset V

\mathrm{rank}_{V,\Delta}(W)\in\Omega\left(\frac{\log(\mathrm{card}(W))}{p(\log\delta+\log s+\log D)}\right)

(where the constant in the
\Omega(\cdot)
is an absolute constant).

Remark 17
.

Note that we cannot use
Theorem
12
with
k=C=\mathrm{R}[i]

to deduce Theorem
13
as a special case.
A finite
\mathcal{P}
-semi-algebraic set need not be realizable
as a finite
\mathcal{Q}
-constructible set over
\mathrm{C}
, where
the cardinality and the degrees of the polynomials in
\mathcal{Q}
are
proportional to those of
\mathcal{P}
.

3.4.2.
Relative rank in algebras

Let
k
be a field and
A
a finite-dimensional
k
-algebra. The algebra
A
is allowed to be non-commutative or non-associative and we do not require
A
to be unital. For example,
A
could be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra, the algebra of endomorphisms of a finite dimensional
k
-vector space, the group algebra

k[G]
for some finite group
G
(or any sub-algebra of
k[G]
). In each of these cases, it is meaningful to ask
given some subset
\Delta
of
A
and an element
f\in A
, how many elements of
\Delta
does one need to express
f
as a product of elements of
\Delta
.

Definition 3.5

(Relative rank in algebras)
.

Let
A
be a
k
-algebra and
\Delta\subset A
.
For
\alpha\in A
, we define

\mathrm{rank}_{A,\Delta}(\alpha)

as the smallest number
t
, if it exists, such that there exists
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{t}\in\Delta
such that
\alpha=\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{t}
, and
\infty
otherwise.
We will call

\mathrm{rank}_{A,\Delta}(\alpha)
the rank of
\alpha
relative to
\Delta
.

For any subset
W\subset A
, we will denote

\mathrm{rank}_{A,\Delta}(W)=\max_{\alpha\in W}\mathrm{rank}_{A,\Delta}(\alpha).

Remark 18
.

Note that if
\Delta\subset\Delta^{\prime}
, then
\mathrm{rank}_{A,\Delta}(\alpha)\geq\mathrm{rank}_{A,\Delta^{\prime}}(\alpha)
for all
\alpha\in A
, and

\mathrm{rank}_{A,\Delta}(W)\geq\mathrm{rank}_{A,\Delta^{\prime}}(W)
for all subsets

W\subset A
.

Definition 3.6

(Relative max-min rank in real algebras)
.

More generally, let

W
be a semi-algebraic subset of
A
(not necessarily finite).
We define the max-min-rank,
\mathrm{mmrank}_{A,\Delta}(W)
of

W
relative to
\Delta
by

\mathrm{mmrank}_{A,\Delta}(W)=\max_{C\in\mathrm{Cc}(W)}\min_{\alpha\in C}\mathrm{rank}_{A,\Delta}(\alpha).

(Note that if
W
is finite

\mathrm{mmrank}_{A,\Delta}(W)=\mathrm{rank}_{A,\Delta}(W)
.)

Notice that a finite dimensional
k
-algebra
A
has the structure of a vector space (by forgetting the
multiplication) and it makes sense to consider algebraic subsets of
A
. We denote the ring of polynomials on

A
by
k[A]
and the subspace of polynomials in
k[A]
of degree at most
\delta
by
k[A]_{\leq\delta}
.

Theorem 14
.

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field and
A
a finite dimensional
k
-algebra.
Let
\Delta\subset A
be an algebraic subset with
\dim(\Delta)=p
and
\deg(\Delta)=D
, and

\mathcal{P}\subset k[A]_{\leq\delta}
with
\mathrm{card}(\mathcal{P})=s
.
Then, for any finite
\mathcal{P}
-constructible subset
W\subset A

\mathrm{rank}_{A,\Delta}(W)\in\Omega\left(\frac{\log(\mathrm{card}(W))}{p(\log\delta+\log s+\log\log\mathrm{card}(W))+\log D}\right)

(where the constant in the
\Omega(\cdot)
is an absolute constant).

We have the following theorem in the real closed case.

Theorem 15
.

Let
\mathrm{R}
be a real closed field and
A
a finite dimensional
\mathrm{R}
-algebra.
Let
\Delta\subset A
be an algebraic subset,
with
\dim\Delta=p
,
\deg\Delta=D
,
and suppose that
\Delta
is a
finite union of non-singular complete intersection real algebraic varieties.
Let

\mathcal{P}\subset\mathrm{R}[A]_{\leq\delta}
with
\mathrm{card}(\mathcal{P})=s
.
Let
W\subset A
be a
\mathcal{P}
-semi-algebraic subset.
Then,

\mathrm{mmrank}_{A,\Delta}(W)\in\Omega\left(\frac{\log(b_{0}(W))}{p(\log\delta+\log s+\log\log b_{0}(W)+\log D)}\right)

(where the constant in the
\Omega(\cdot)
is an absolute constant).

3.4.3.
Example application of Theorem
12

Example 3.4

(Ranks of tensors)
.

Let
k
be algebraically closed and
let
V_{1},\ldots,V_{m}
be finite dimensional vector spaces with

\dim V_{i}=N_{i}
. Let
V=V_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes V_{m}
. For
1\leq i\leq m
, let
B_{i}=(e_{i,j})_{1\leq j\leq N_{i}}
be a basis of
V_{i}
, and
B=B_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes B_{m}
the induced basis of
V
. The coordinates of an element
v
with respect to the basis
B
is then given by a
m
-dimensional array in
k^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{m}}
. Let
B^{\prime}\subset B
be a fixed set and
W\subset V
,
be defined as the finite subset of elements of
V
, whose coordinates
with respect to the basis elements in
B^{\prime}
belong to
\{0,1\}
and whose coordinates with respect to the basis elements
B-B^{\prime}
are
0
.
Clearly,
\mathrm{card}(W)=2^{\mathrm{card}(B^{\prime})}
.
(In terms of arrays
W
is a finite set of
N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{m}
arrays whose entries in the positions indexed by
B^{\prime}
are
0
or
1
and whose remaining entries are
0
).

Let
\Delta\subset V
be the set of tensors of rank
\leq 1
.
Then,
\Delta
can be identified with the affine cone over the
image of the Segre embedding

\mathbb{P}_{k}^{N_{1}-1}\times\cdots\mathbb{P}_{k}^{N_{m}-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}_{k}^{N_{1}\cdots N_{m}-1},

which yields:

p=\dim\Delta=N_{1}+\cdots+N_{m}-m+1,

and

D=\deg\Delta=\binom{N_{1}+\cdots+N_{m}-m}{N_{1}-1,\ldots,N_{m}-1}

using the multinomial notation (see for example
[
16
, Proposition 2.11]
).
Applying Theorem
12
, and noting that

\mathrm{card}(W)=2^{\mathrm{card}(B^{\prime})}
,
we obtain

\mathrm{rank}_{V,\Delta}(W)=\Omega\left(\frac{\mathrm{card}(B^{\prime})}{p\cdot(\log(N_{1}\cdots N_{m})+\log D)}\right).

Taking
m=2
, and
N_{1}=N_{2}=N
, and
B^{\prime}\subset B
with
\mathrm{card}(B^{\prime})=N^{1+{\varepsilon}}
, for some
{\varepsilon},0<{\varepsilon}<1
, we obtain that

\mathrm{rank}_{V,\Delta}(W)=\Omega\left(\frac{N^{{\varepsilon}}}{\log N}\right).

Thus, in any set of
N\times N
zero-nonzero matrices with non-zero entries allowed only in a fixed set of
N^{1+{\varepsilon}}
positions, there exists a matrix of rank at least
C\cdot\frac{N^{\varepsilon}}{\log N}
, where
C>0
is an absolute constant.

Note that a possible alternative approach to proving the above statement is to use the well known Kővári-Sós-Turán (KST) theorem
(see for example
[
52
, Theorem 1.4.2]
).
Identifying the set
B
with
[1,N]\times[1,N]
,
if the chosen set of positions,
B^{\prime}\subset B

contains a “combinatorial square” of size
s\times s
(i.e.

I\times J
, with
\mathrm{card}(I)=\mathrm{card}(J)=s,I,J\subset[1,N]
), then
clearly,
\mathrm{rank}_{V,\Delta}(W)\geq s
. The KST theorem guarantees
the existence of such a square if
\mathrm{card}(B^{\prime})\geq C(s)\cdot N^{2-1/s}
.
In the KST theorem
s
is constant. For
s
growing with
N
,
the cardinality of
B^{\prime}
needs to be even larger.
Thus, for
s=N^{\varepsilon}/\log N
,
\mathrm{card}(B^{\prime})
needs to be at least
N^{2-o(1)}

to be guaranteed that
B^{\prime}
contains a combinatorial square
with
s=N^{\varepsilon}
. However,
N^{1+{\varepsilon}}\ll N^{2-o(1)}
, and thus
this approach cannot be used to prove the claim above.

3.4.4.
Example of application of Theorem
14

Example 3.5

(Lengths of permutations)
.

Let
V
be an
n
-dimensional
k
-vector space and
A=\mathrm{End}(V)
. Let
B=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
be a vector space
basis of
A
, and
W
the subset of
A
of those endomorphism that permutes the elements of
B
.
Then,
W
is a zero-dimensional algebraic subset of
A
cut out by
n^{2}+2n
equations of degrees at most
2
(
n^{2}
equations of degree
2
and
2n
equations of degree
1
), and

\mathrm{card}(W)=n!
.

If
\Delta\subset A
be any
p
-dimensional variety having degree
D
, then using Theorem
1
we have:

\mathrm{rank}_{A,\Delta}(W)=\Omega\left(\frac{n\log n}{(p+1)\log n+\log D}\right).

This bound is asymptotically tight.

Consider for example
A=\mathrm{End}(V)
, with
V
a
k
-vector space with
basis
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
, and

\Delta
the union of
\mathrm{Id}_{V}
, and
the endomorphisms
\pi_{i},1\leq i\leq n-1
, where
\pi_{i}

acts on

by transposing
v_{i},v_{i+1}
and by the identity on
v_{j},j\not\in\{i,i+1\}
.
Then as is well known each element of

can be expressed as a product of at most

elements of

\mathrm{Id}_{V},\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{N-1}
.

So,
\mathrm{rank}_{A,\Delta}(W)=O(n)
, while in the lower bound above we have

,

, which gives

\mathrm{rank}_{A,\Delta}(W)=\Omega(n)
. Indeed the

lower bound is valid for

and

.

3.5.
Quantum circuit lower bounds

3.5.1.
Shannon’s lower bound for classical circuits

Boolean circuit complexity measures complexity of Boolean functions
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
by the size of the smallest circuit computing
f
.
We assume that the gates of a circuit come from some fixed finite universal set of gates.
We refer the reader to
[
49
, Page 7, Definition 3.1]
for the precise definition of a circuit.
Notice that in this case the set of circuits of bounded size is finite.

Shannon
[
40
]
showed using a counting argument (using the fact that as noted before that the set of circuits of bounded size is finite) very early that almost all Boolean functions need circuits of size
\Omega({2^{n}}/{n})
(see
[
49
, Page 90, Theorem 2.1]
.
Here almost all refers to the fact that the number of Boolean functions that require circuits of size
\Omega({2^{n}}/{n})
is bounded from below by
2^{2^{n}}(1-o(1))
. It is also known (see for instance
[
49
, Page 92, Theorem 2.2]
) that every Boolean
function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
can be computed by a circuit of size
O({2^{n}}/{n})
.

Remark 19
.

In the theorems cited above the set of gates used is assumed to be all possible gates
of arity two (i.e. the finite set of
2^{2^{2}}=16
gates computing all possible Boolean functions

g:\{0,1\}^{2}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
). The arity two is not important. The same asymptotic results will
hold even if we allow all gates of some bounded arity
q\geq 2

(the constants will depend on the arity) .

3.5.2.
Analog of Shannon’s lower bound for quantum circuits

We begin with a brief review of quantum complexity since it motivates our definition.
Recall that in quantum complexity theory a qubit refers to a two-dimensional complex Hermitian
vector space
V
with a fixed orthonormal basis
\mathcal{B}=\{|0{\rangle},|1{\rangle}\}
. A
n
-qubit register refers to
the
n
-fold tensor power,
V^{\otimes n}
of
V
, with an induced Hermitian structure in which the vectors

\mathcal{B}^{\otimes n}=\{|j{\rangle}|0\leq j\leq N-1\}
form an orthonormal basis
(here
N=2^{n}
, and we identify a
0
-
1
string
x_{n-1}\cdots x_{0}
of length
n
, with the integer

x=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}x_{k}\cdot 2^{k},

and denote
|x_{n-1}{\rangle}\otimes\cdots\otimes|x_{0}{\rangle}
simply by
|\mathbf{x}{\rangle}
).

A
q
-ary quantum gate
g
is an element
U_{g}\in\mathbf{U}(V^{\otimes q})
.
In a quantum circuit
C
, with input
n
-qubits, such a gate operates on a subset of
q
of
the
n
-qubits (say labelled by the tuple
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{q}),1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{q}\leq
),
which gives rise to an element
U_{g,\mathbf{i}}
of
\mathbf{U}(V^{\otimes n})
which can be identified with

U_{g}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{V^{\otimes(n-q)}}
.
(after rearranging the order of the copies of
V
in the tensor power
V^{\otimes n}

bringing the ones indexed by
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{q})
to the the positions
(1,\ldots,q)
).

A quantum circuit
C
with
n
input qubits labelled by
1,\ldots,n
having
r

q
-ary gates is determined by the following data:

1.

an ordering of the
r
gates
(lets suppose the ordered tuple of gates is
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}
, and

2.

for each
i,1\leq i\leq r
, an ordered choice,
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{q})
of
q
elements
from amongst
1,\ldots,n
.

We denote

U(C)=\prod_{1\leq i\leq r}U_{g_{i},\mathbf{i}}\in\mathbf{U}(V^{\otimes n})

the unitary transformation implemented by
C
(where
N=2^{n}
), and we denote
\mathrm{size}(C)=r
.

For
q\geq 1
,
we will denote by
\mathcal{C}_{q}
, the set of quantum circuits using
q
-ary quantum gates.

Definition 3.7

(Quantum circuit complexity of a unitary transformation)
.

The
q
-ary quantum circuit complexity of a unitary transformation
\mathbf{U}(V^{\otimes n})
is defined
as

\min_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{q},U(C)=U}\mathrm{size}(C).

In other words, if we denote

\Delta=\Delta_{n,q}=\{U_{g,\mathbf{i}}\mid U_{g}\in\mathbf{U}(V^{\otimes q}),\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{q}),1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{q}\},

then the
q
-ary quantum circuit complexity of a unitary transformation
U\in\mathbf{U}(V^{\otimes n})

is precisely (using Definition
3.5
)

\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{End}(V^{\otimes n}),\Delta}(U)

considering
U
as an element of
\mathrm{End}(V^{\otimes n})
.
This motivates the following definition:

Definition 3.8

(Relative quantum circuit complexity)
.

For arbitrary
\Delta\subset\mathrm{End}(V^{\otimes n})
we will call
\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{End}(V^{\otimes n}),\Delta}(U)

the
quantum complexity of
U
relative to
\Delta
.

Remark 20
.

In analogy with classical complexity,
\Delta
could be thought of as a set of quantum oracles – with unit cost for each application of an element of
\Delta
in an quantum circuit. Notice though we do not assume that
\Delta
is contained in the unitary group

\mathbf{U}(V^{\otimes n})
, but it is allowed to be.

We now explain (following
[
32
]
) what is meant by a quantum circuit computing a Boolean function.
For ease of understanding, we start with a provisional (very stringent) definition
and then give a more general (much less stringent) definition afterwards.

Let
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be a Boolean function. We associate a
unitary transformation
U_{f}\in\mathbf{U}(2^{n+1})
to

which takes for

x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1},y\in\{0,1\}
, the separable state

|\mathbf{x}{\rangle}\otimes|y{\rangle}
to the state

|\mathbf{x}{\rangle}\otimes|f(x)\oplus y{\rangle}
, where
\oplus
denotes “exclusive-or”.
Note that using the measurement postulate of quantum mechanics, if we
set the input qubits to
|\mathbf{x}{\rangle}\otimes|0{\rangle}
and measure
the ancillary bit in the output, we will be left in the state
|1{\rangle}
if
f(x)=1
and
in the state
|0{\rangle}
if
f(x)=0

with probability
1
.

Definition 3.9
.

We will call the quantum circuit complexity of
U_{f}

as the stringent quantum circuit complexity of
f
, and the
quantum complexity of
U_{f}
relative to
\Delta
as
stringent quantum complexity of
f
relative to
\Delta
.

We prove the following theorem.

Theorem 16
.

There exists
C>0
, such that for
all
n>0
,
\Delta\subset\mathrm{End}(V^{\otimes(n+1)})
algebraic subset with
\dim\Delta=p
and
\deg\Delta=D
,
there exists Boolean functions
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, such that the stringent quantum complexity of

f
relative to
\Delta
, is at least

C\cdot\frac{2^{n}}{p\cdot n+\log D}.

We can deduce as a corollary (setting

p=2^{q}\times 2^{q})
and
D=\binom{n}{q}
in Theorem
16
) the following lower bound
which is the quantum analog of Shannon’s lower bound for classical circuits.

Corollary 1
.

For each
q\geq 1
, there exists
C=C_{q},0<c<1
(depending only on
q
), such that for each
n>0

there exists Boolean functions
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, such that the stringent quantum circuit complexity of

f
is least
C\cdot\frac{2^{n}}{n}
.

Remark 21
.

It follows that the fraction of Boolean functions that need quantum circuits in
\mathcal{C}_{q}
of size greater than
c\cdot\frac{2^{n}}{n}
is
1-2^{-2^{n-1}}=1-o(1)
.

Remark 22
.

Notice that since the set of gates
\mathcal{U}_{q}
is infinite (uncountably so), and hence the number of
distinct quantum circuits of any bounded size is also uncountably infinite. So a priori there is no reason for such circuits not being able to compute all Boolean functions on
n
variables.

As mentioned before we will work with a more general notion of what it means for a
quantum circuit to compute a Boolean function. We relax our prior definition in two
ways. First, instead of ending up in the right state depending on the value of the function
f

with probability
1
, we are satisfied if we end at the right state with probability
>1/2
.
We will also allow more than one ancillary bits. The precise definition is as follows.

Definition 3.10

(Unitary transformation associated to a Boolean function)
.

We denote the
n
input qubits by
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}

and

denoted by

Given a Boolean function

,
we denote by

the set of all unitary transfomations

of

satisfying
for each

,

(3.6)

\displaystyle|{\langle}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\;y\;\mathbf{0}\;|\;U\;|\;\mathbf{x}\;0\;\mathbf{0}{\rangle}|^{2}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle 1/2\text{ if $x^{\prime}=x$ and $y\oplus f(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1})=0$},

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 1/2\text{ otherwise}

(

Remark 23
.

Note that
U_{f}\in U_{f,1}^{+}
.
Also note that the inequality appearing
in (

) is the most relaxed possible in as much as we do not insist
on any positive gap between the accepting and rejecting probabilities.

Definition 3.11
.

We will call the

as the quantum circuit complexity of

with

ancillary bits,
and the

.

In the following theorem we consider

as a

-dimensional
\mathbb{R}

.

There exists

, such that for

,
\Delta\subset A

such that

is a real algebraic subset of

and suppose

The following corollary of Theorem

Corollary 2
.

[

For each

Remark 24
.

A more traditional approach would be to consider circuits built out of
only Clifford and Toffoli gates (using results form
[
28
,
39
]
)
that approximate the required unitary matrix sufficiently well.
Since the number of circuits of bounded size built out of a finite set of quantum gates
is finite one can then hope to reproduce the original counting argument of Shannon in the classical case.
This fails because of the following reason.

Our very generous definition of what it means for quantum circuit to compute a Boolean function
gives no room for any approximation, as any error could mean that the new circuit
C^{\prime}
does not compute
f
.
If we strengthen the definition (thus make proving lower bounds easier) by insisting that there be a gap
\delta
between the probabilities of acceptance/rejection i.e. we replace the inequality in Definition
3.10
by

(3.7)

\displaystyle|{\langle}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\;y\;\mathbf{0}\;|\;U(C)\;|\;\mathbf{x}\;0\;\mathbf{0}{\rangle}|^{2}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle 1/2+\delta\text{ if $x^{\prime}=x$ and $y\oplus f(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1})=1$},

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 1/2-\delta\text{ otherwise}

for some fixed
\delta,0\leq\delta<1/2
,
then one can carry through the program sketched earlier using approximation and counting (see for example
[
29
,
28
]
).
In this case one would need to replace
C
by a circuit
C^{\prime}
using Clifford and Toffoli gates which approximates
U(C)
within
\delta/2
in max-norm.
This produces the result that almost all Boolean functions need quantum circuits of size

\Omega(\min(2^{n}/(n\log n),2^{n}/((\log(1/\delta)\log n)))).

This lower bound
goes to
0
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
. So it does not produce any meaningful lower bound for
\delta=0
(which is the case in Definition
3.10
).
We also note that there is recent work in constructing explicit Boolean functions with exponential quantum circuit complexity using sophisticated
tools from transcendental number theory
[
26
]
. Our rank argument does not produce such functions.

.
If

5.
Open problems and future work

We end with some open problems and suggestions for future work.

1.

Is it possible to eliminate the restriction of local constancy of the real dimension in the hypothesis of Theorem
2
?

2.

Is it possible to extend Theorem
1
to a bound on the sum of the Betti numbers of the realizations of all realizable zero-nonzero patterns. The right hand side will definitely have to be larger. For example, the following result is proved in
[
8
]
.

Theorem K
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}\subset\mathrm{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
be a family of polynomials
with
\#{\mathcal{P}}=s
and
\deg(P)\leq d
for all
P\in{\mathcal{P}}
.
Then

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{P}}}b^{BM}({\mathcal{R}}(\pi,\mathrm{C}^{N}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq N}\left(\binom{s}{N-\ell}\binom{s}{\ell}d^{N}+\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell}\binom{s}{N-\ell}\binom{s}{\ell-i}d^{O(N^{2})}\right),

where
b^{BM}(\cdot)
denotes the sum of the Borel-Moore Betti numbers.

Is it possible to prove a similar bound for all realizable zero-nonzero patterns restricted to an algebraic set but where the bound is independent of the ambient dimension
N
and depends only on the degree of the variety?

3.

We believe that the ambient dimension free upper bounds proved in this paper should have other applications (especially in computational complexity theory) than those explored in this paper. We would like to explore such applications.
