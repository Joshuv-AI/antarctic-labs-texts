Secondary Revision With Boot Flap in a Case of Tessier No. 0 Cleft
Authors: Koichi Ueda, Yuka Hirota, Hiromi Kino, Natsuki Seo, Megumi Oe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:The use of de-epithelialized oral vestibular flap (boot flap) for treating free border deformity in unilateral cleft lip and for the correction of whistling lip deformity in bilateral cleft lip is reported. A 22-year-old patient with a Tessier no. 0 median cleft was referred after having undergone median lip repair, facial bipartition for correction of orbital hypertelorism, and nasal reconstruction using a scalping forehead flap at a different hospital. The patient presented with vermilion notching and a reverse V-shaped deformity of the upper lip. The standard bilateral cleft lip repair technique was modified, and satisfactory results were obtained. Specifically, a bilateral boot flap procedure was performed on the oral vestibule along the central suture line of the primary repair. The boot flap was designed to be shorter than that used in standard cases of bilateral cleft lip. The surface mucosa was de-epithelialized before flap elevation, and a subcutaneous pedicle was created beneath the flap, which was then connected to the orbicularis oris muscle. Bilateral subcutaneous pockets were dissected, into which the boot flap was inserted and fixed using absorbable sutures. The donor site was closed by advancing the bilateral mucosal flaps. Full-thickness skin from the nasal dorsum was grafted onto the pigmented forehead donor area of the scalping flap, and direct suturing was performed to narrow the nasal dorsum. The postoperative course was uneventful, and vermilion notching did not recur during a 7-year follow-up.

Deficiency of the free border of the lip is often encountered in secondary repair of unilateral cleft lip, while whistling lip deformity is common after bilateral cleft lip repair. We have previously reported that both of these problems can be treated effectively by a de-epithelialized oral vestibular flap (boot flap).1,2 The advantage of this simple technique is that it does not create any additional donor-site scars in either unilateral or bilateral cleft lip cases. In the years since publishing these reports, we have treated additional cases of secondary repair using this boot flap technique, which we have analyzed and reported after long-term follow-up.3

Direct suturing to correct notching deformity of the free border in the unilateral cleft lip often results in recurrence of the deformity due to scar contracture.3,4 To prevent this, it is important to interrupt the scar plane by incorporating a flap and augmenting tissue volume. We treated a patient with Tessier no. 0 median cleft who presented with notching of the free border after primary upper lip repair. We modified the boot flap method used for bilateral cleft lip and obtained good results. This report describes the case.

CASE REPORT

A 22-year-old patient with a Tessier no. 0 cleft was referred to our hospital with vermilion notching and nasal deformity (Fig. 1). She had previously undergone median lip repair and a facial bipartition procedure for orbital hypertelorism, as well as nasal reconstructions using a scalping forehead flap at a different hospital. She had a deformity of the nose caused by the scalping flap, vermilion notching in the free border, and a reverse V-shaped deformity of the upper lip (Fig. 2).

A 22-year-old woman with a Tessier no. 0 cleft who had undergone primary median cleft lip repair had vermilion notching of the upper lip.

Preoperative condition in the supine position under general anesthesia.

The repair was performed under general anesthesia. The oral vestibule was incised bilaterally along the central suture line of the primary lip repair. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the design of the boot flap and bilateral subcutaneous pockets preoperatively, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/F150.) To address the vermilion notching, we modified the standard boot flap technique for bilateral cleft lip such that the flap was shorter than that used in the standard treatment of bilateral cleft lip3 (Fig. 3). The surface mucosa was de-epithelialized before flap elevation. Instead of creating the standard bilateral subcutaneous pedicles, a single subcutaneous pedicle was fashioned beneath the boot flap and connected to the orbicularis oris muscle. The flap was elevated over the orbicularis oris muscle layer, thus including subcutaneous tissue. The bilateral subcutaneous pockets were then dissected, extending the entire length of the depressed area over the muscle through the primary suture line. The boot flaps were inserted into the pockets and secured with 5-0 polydioxanone absorbable sutures. The donor site was closed by advancing the bilateral flaps. Full-thickness skin obtained from the nasal dorsum was grafted onto the pigmented forehead donor area of the scalping flap, and direct suturing was performed to narrow the nasal dorsum. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays immediate postoperative conditions, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/F151.)

Then, the bilateral nasal alae were removed medially. Postoperatively, the patient was referred to an orthodontist for the management of dental arch disturbance. The postoperative course was uneventful, and no recurrence of vermilion notching was observed during 7 years of follow-up (Fig. 4).

DISCUSSION

Vermilion notching is a deformity often observed after primary surgery for median cleft lip. Notching can be corrected by Z-plasty5; however, it is difficult to increase lip volume with this technique. Other methods, such as V-Y advancement of the labial mucosa,6 scar flap,7 and submucosal back-flipped flap,8 have also been used. In our experience, notching of the free margin in a unilateral cleft lip recurs after repair with direct suturing3,4 likely due to scar contracture. To prevent recurrence, it is therefore important to interrupt the scar plane by incorporating a boot flap and augmenting its volume. In the current median cleft lip case, this technique was especially important, as the upper lip showed a reverse V-shaped deformity under muscle relaxation during general anesthesia, reflecting the effects of scar contracture (Fig. 2).

Our technique has several benefits. Functionally, the boot flap increases the vermilion volume. Furthermore, placing the boot flap along the existing scars left over from the primary cleft lip repair avoids creating any additional scars. Finally, advancing the vermilion to the desired location can be obtained through closure of the donor site. In the present case, the flap volume was small. The bilateral boot flaps do not need to be as long as in standard bilateral cleft lip repair. This is because, in the case of bilateral cleft lip, the flaps must extend into subcutaneous pockets beyond the median prolabium.

In the current case study, we did not observe any new notching throughout the 7-year follow-up. We conclude that the boot flap plays an important role in preventing scar contracture.

CONCLUSIONS

A 22-year-old patient with a Tessier no. 0 median cleft presented with vermilion notching deformity and a reverse V-shaped deformity of the upper lip. In this case, we modified the standard boot flap technique for bilateral cleft lip and obtained satisfactory results.
