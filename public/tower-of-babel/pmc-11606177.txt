Sociodemographic and maternal health-related factors associated with mortality among children under three in Bangladesh: an analysis of data from Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey 2017-18
Authors: Md. Fakhar Uddin, Shanta Akter Mim, Md. Ahshanul Haque, Md. Tariquajjaman, Ishrat Jabeen, Mubassira Binte Latif, Asma-Ul-Husna Sumi, Fatema Aarshe, Md. Hasibul Hossain, Sumaiya Nabi, Md. Alamgir Hossain, Mohammad Niaz Morshed Khan, Akash Saha, Tahmeed Ahmed, Nur Haque Alam, Mohammod Jobayer Chisti
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChild mortality remains remarkably high in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Bangladesh. This study aimed to identify the sociodemographic and maternal health-related factors associated with under three (U3) child mortality in Bangladesh.MethodsWe extracted data of 5299 U3 children from Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS) 2017-18. We used descriptive statistics to summarize the data. The chi-square (χ2) test, simple and multiple Firth logistic regression were performed to test the associations between priori-defined factors and U3 mortality.ResultsIn Bangladesh, the U3 child mortality rate was 35 deaths per 1,000 live births, with a median age at death of less than one month. The adjusted model revealed that the odds of U3 child mortality were higher among children born to mothers aged between 30 and 39 years [adjusted odds ratio (AOR) = 2.01, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.30–3.11; p-value (p) = 0.002], those who did not use any contraceptive [AOR = 2.57, 95% CI: 1.90–3.47; p < 0.001], with first pregnancy [AOR = 14.91, CI: 4.60–48.30; p < 0.001], had birth interval less than 24 months [AOR = 2.10, CI: 1.23–3.60; p = 0.007], children born to mothers who delivered vaginally [AOR = 3.18, 95% CI: 2.07–4.87; p < 0.001]. However, lower odds of mortality were observed among children of mothers with higher education levels [AOR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.28–0.90; p = 0.021] and families with more than five members [AOR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.45–0.83; p < 0.01]. In addition, religion, birth attendant during delivery, and the child’s birth order were significantly associated with U3 child mortality, whereas mortality did not vary significantly across the divisions.ConclusionsHigher odds of U3 child mortality were associated with mothers who did not use contraceptives, delivered vaginally, and were aged 30–39 years in Bangladesh. Conversely, higher maternal education and larger family size were associated with lower odds of U3 child mortality. The findings suggest that community-based family planning awareness programs focused on contraceptive use, as it prevents childbirth and is also a marker of health service usage.

Background

Under five (U5) child mortality is one of the major health indicators for understanding a nation’s overall well-being [1]. The global downward trend of U5 mortality indicates a positive transformation of the healthcare system and quality of life [2–4]. The reduction of U5, including neonatal mortality rates, by 2030 is one of the targets of sustainable development goals (SDGs) 3.2 [5]. Worldwide, U5 mortality has declined 60%, from 93 to 37 deaths per 1000 live births between 1990 and 2022, which illustrates that the number of deaths among children has halved from 12.8 to 4.9 million within these years [6]. The reduction in U5 mortality is not restricted to only wealthy and developed regions; other countries are also experiencing this positive progress [7]. However, compared to that in high-income countries, the U5 child mortality reduction is slower among LMICs and low-income countries [8]. According to the United Nations International Children’s Emergency Fund (UNICEF), the U5 mortality rate in LMICs was 44 deaths/1000 live births, which was approximately 9 times higher than that in high-income countries (5 deaths/1000 live births) in 2021 [7]. Among the LMICs, in the year 2022, Nigeria had the highest U5 mortality, with 835030 deaths, where the U5 mortality rate was 107.17 child deaths/1000 live births [9]. However, Niger had the highest U5 mortality rate (117.34 child deaths / 1000 live births) in the same period [10].

The change in the U5 mortality rate is also noticeable among South Asian countries. The U5 mortality rate in South Asia was 130 deaths per 1000 live births in 1990, which has reduced by 71% in 2022 (36 deaths/1000 live births) [11]. The Maldives, Iran, and Sri Lanka have already achieved SDG 3.2.1 (25 or fewer U5 child deaths per 1,000 live births) [12]. India, Bangladesh, Bhutan, and Nepal are also on track to achieve this goal [12]. According to the annual global childhood report of Save the Children, child mortality rates have fallen dramatically in Bhutan by 60%, Nepal by 59% and India by 57% in the previous twenty years [13]. The U5 mortality rate in Bangladesh has decreased remarkably over the last few decades, from 226.9 deaths per 1000 live births in 1973 to 28.8 in 2022, with current infant and neonatal mortality rates of 24.1 and 17.4 deaths per 1000 live births, respectively [14]. However, reducing neonatal and infant mortality is challenging [8]. A recent BDHS report showed that neonatal and infant mortality rates in Bangladesh are 20 and 25 per 1000 live births, and accounting for 64.5% and 80%, respectively, of all U5 mortalities [15]. Furthermore, data from the 2019 Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS) showed that mortality rates are higher among rural children, with 34.1 infant deaths and 41.6 U5 child deaths per 1,000 live births, compared to 32.3 infant deaths and 36.6 U5 child death per 1,000 live birth in urban areas [16].

A systematic review identified factors associated with U5 child mortality across various regions, including Africa, South-East Asia, the Eastern Mediterranean, and the Western Pacific regions [17]. The most significant covariates of U5 mortality in these regions were maternal education, mother’s age at delivery, place of residence, child size at birth, and birth interval [17]. Multiple studies in LMICs have also identified factors influencing U5 child mortality. For example, a previous study on acutely ill children under 24 months in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia found that the severity of illness, HIV exposure, pre-existing conditions, and limited healthcare access increased the risk of child mortality within 30 days of hospital admission [18]. In Africa, pneumonia, central nervous system (CNS) infections, nutritional inflammation, hypoglycemia, chest indrawing, fever, and diarrhea were significant disease-related factors of U5 child mortality [19, 20]. Additionally, socio-cultural and demographic factors such as family income and size, polygamy, maternal education, place of residence, lack of spousal support as well as structural and functional factors related to hospital bed and equipment shortages further compounded the risk of U5 mortality [19, 21–24]. Maternal factors associated with U5 mortality in South Asia, include maternal age at first childbirth or pregnancy, birth intervals, and education levels. For example, children of mothers under 20 years at their first pregnancy were 11% more likely to die before age five compared to children of older mothers [25, 26]. Similarly, mothers aged between 30 and 32 years at first pregnancy experienced a higher risk of infant mortality [26]. Higher U5 mortality was also associated with lower maternal education levels, short birth intervals, and higher birth-ordered children in South Asia, including Bangladesh [27–34]. In Bangladesh, several factors have been identified as contributors to U5 child mortality, including immunization, infant and young child feeding (IYCF) practices, maternal age, birth interval, child’s sex, household income, and access to antenatal care [30, 35, 36]. Despite significant reductions in U5 mortality rates, rural areas continue to face higher mortality rates compared to urban areas, with persistent disparities across socioeconomic groups [16, 28, 37]. Factors influencing the U5 mortality in rural Bangladesh include lack of trained healthcare professionals, poor sanitation, and unsafe drinking water [24, 38].

While previous studies [18–20, 23, 24] have focused primarily on biomedical and healthcare-related factors associated with U5 mortality, quantitative studies have not showed yet the socio-demographic and maternal factors influencing U3 child mortality in Bangladesh. Additionally, many of these studies were conducted in specific districts or regions, making them less representative of the entire population of Bangladesh [24, 35]. Our study aims to fill these research gaps by using updated data from the BDHS 2017–2018 to identify socio-demographic and maternal health related factors associated with U3 child mortality. This study findings can provide valuable insights for policymakers to develop targeted interventions to further reduce child mortality rates in Bangladesh.

Methods

Sample selection procedure

We utilized nationally representative secondary cross-sectional data derived from the BDHS 2017–2018 for this study. To collect the data, the BDHS used a two-stage stratified sampling method. In the first stage, 675 enumeration areas (EAs) were selected by the BDHS with a sampling probability proportional to size, 250 of which were in urban areas and 425 of which were in rural areas. Systematic random sampling was used in the second stage to choose an average of 30 households (HHs) from each EA. In total, 19,457 HHs consisting of 20,127 eligible women were interviewed. The BDHS Report 2020 indicated the complete methodology of this data collection and design process [39]. After removing the missing values, the sample for this study comprised 5299 mothers whose last birth was a child under three years old, born between October 2014 and March 2018. Variables under study

The study’s outcome variable was children under the age of three who were alive or deceased among Bangladeshi mothers aged 15–49 years. We defined “yes” as an alive child (0) and “no” as a deceased child (1). The independent variables were selected based on our bivariate analysis as well as a literature review. The selected variables were mother’s current age, division, religion, maternal education, use of any contraceptive method, birth interval, mode of delivery, place of delivery, birth attendant during delivery, and number of family members, wealth index.

This analysis considered data collected from all eight divisions of Bangladesh, which included the Dhaka, Rajshahi, Barishal, Chattogram, Khulna, Sylhet, Rangpur, and Mymensingh divisions, and each division consisted of several districts. Religion was categorized into two groups: Muslim and others (Hinduism, Buddhism, Christianity, and others). Maternal education was categorized into three categories: mothers with below secondary (limited/primary education or no literacy), secondary, and higher education. Place of delivery was divided into three groups: home, health facilities (medical college hospital, specialized government hospital, district hospital, MCWC, upazila health complex, other public sectors, UH & family welfare center, community clinic, private medical college hospital, private hospital, private clinic, other private medical sectors, NGO static clinic, delivery hut), and others. Birth attendant during delivery was divided into three groups: medically trained birth attendants (qualified doctors, midwives, nurses or paramedics, sub-assistant community medical officers, community skilled birth attendants, family welfare visitors), trained traditional and untrained traditional birth attendants (births delivered with untrained traditional birth attendants, friends, relatives, or others) [39].

Statistical analysis

Bar diagrams were generated to visualize the data. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies with percentages, were used to summarize the data. Cross tabulation was performed to determine the bivariate distribution of mothers’ current age (in years), division, religion, maternal education, use of contraceptives, birth interval, mode of delivery, place of delivery, birth attendant during delivery, number of family members, birth order, wealth index, child mortality and type of place of residence. We also checked the variance inflation factor (VIF) with a cutoff value 5, to ensure either there was any multicollinearity problem and observed that there was not any multicollinearity among the independent variables. To see the distribution along with the association of respondent’s current age (in years), Division, religion, maternal education, use of contraceptives, birth interval, mode of delivery, place of delivery, birth attendants during delivery, number of family members, birth order, wealth index and child mortality, we performed chi-square/ Fisher’s exact test. To examine the factors associated with child mortality, simple Firth logistic regression which is suitable for a small number of outcomes, was initially applied [40, 41]. Subsequently, based on the results and literature, to identify the relevant factors associated with the outcome, we conducted multiple Firth logistic regression and then presented the regression estimates of simple and multiple models as odds ratios and adjusted odds ratios, respectively. p values < 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals were utilized as inferential statistics. The statistical software packages SPSS Version 20 and Stata Version 16.0 were used for the data analyses.

Results

Characteristics of the study participants

Table 1 displays the background characteristics of the children under the age of three. We observed that 13.13% of young mothers aged 18–19 years give birth in Bangladesh and 61.60% of the mothers were aged between 20 and below 30 years. Almost half (47.82%) of the mothers had completed secondary education. Approximately 66.67% of mothers responded positively to the use of contraceptives. This percentage was higher among mothers who lived in urban areas (72%) than among those who lived in rural areas (63.90%). Additionally, more than half (67.17%) of mothers had vaginal deliveries, and 72.28% of them were from urban areas. In urban areas, the majority (62.28%) of deliveries occurred in health facilities, while more than half of rural mothers (56.30) delivered at home. In rural areas, 43.19% of deliveries were performed by untrained traditional birth attendants or others, whereas this percentage was lower (25.14%) in urban areas. Half (50.41%) of the children were found in families with less than 5 members, with a slightly higher percentage (54.02%) in urban areas. Of all the children, 38.97% were first-order and the majority of the children were from poor families (42.22%), with most of these poor families (54.03) residing in rural areas.

Associations between different factors and U3 child mortality

In the bivariate analysis, we found that child mortality was significantly associated with mothers’ current age (in years), religion, maternal education, use of any contraceptives, birth interval, mode of delivery, and number of family members. The U3 mortality was 4.91% and 4.74% among mothers aged 15–17 years and 18–19 years, respectively. Mothers who did not use any contraceptives experienced 5.6% child mortality. Additionally, child mortality was higher among mothers with below-secondary (4.40%) and secondary (3.50%) education levels than among mothers with higher (2%) education levels. Children born to mothers who delivered vaginally experienced 4.27% child mortality, whereas 2.01% mortality was observed among children born by caesarean section. Children born with a birth interval of less than 24 months experienced 5.39% child mortality and mothers with first pregnancy faced 4.03% child mortality. Families consisting of less than or equal to five members had the highest (4.15%) U3 child mortality than their counterparts. First-order children also had a high percentage of child mortality (3.87%) (Table 2).

Factors associated with U3 child mortality

In the multiple regression model, we included statistically significant and nonsignificant variables of simple regression models. Since statistically nonsignificant variables are also potential predictors of child mortality, we included them in the multiple regression model. The multiple Firth regression model demonstrated that mothers aged 30–39 years were 2 times [AOR = 2.01, CI: 1.30–3.11; p < 0.01] more likely to experience U3 child mortality than 20–29 aged mothers. Younger aged mothers (18–19 years) had 1.39 times [AOR = 1.39, CI: 0.89–2.22; p = 0.137] higher odds of child mortality, although this relationship was not statistically significant. Additionally, mothers who did not use any contraceptive method had more than 2 times [AOR = 2.57, CI: 1.90–3.47; p < 0.001] higher chance of child death than those who used any contraceptive method. With respect to religion, other religious mothers [AOR = 1.94, CI: 1.22–3.08; p = 0.005] faced approximately 2 times more U3 child mortality than Muslim mothers. Mothers with a first pregnancy [AOR = 14.91, CI: 4.60–48.30; p < 0.001] about 15 times and a birth interval less than 24 months [AOR = 2.10, CI: 1.23–3.60; p = 0.007] had 2 times higher chances of child mortality than mothers who had a birth interval greater than 24 months. Children who were born by vaginal delivery [AOR = 3.18, CI: 2.07–4.85; p < 0.001] were approximately 3 times more likely to have child mortality than those who were born by caesarean delivery. Higher educated mothers had 50% [AOR = 0.50, CI: 0.28–0.90; p = 0.021] lower chance of child mortality than below-secondary educated mothers. Children who lived in a family consisting of more than 5 members [AOR = 0.61, CI: 0.45–0.83; p = 0.002] had a 39% lower likelihood of child mortality than their counterparts. Poor [AOR = 1.13, CI: 0.74–1.72; p = 0.568] and rich [AOR = 1.06, CI: 0.68–1.66; p = 0.775] families had 1.13 and 1.06 times more likely to have child mortality than middle-class families but this association was not statistically significant (Table 3).

Discussion

This study demonstrated statistically significant associations between sociodemographic factors such as mother’s current age, maternal education, religion, birth order of the child, family size, and maternal health-related factors, including mood of delivery, use of any contraceptive, and birth interval, and U3 child mortality. In the following section, various factors influencing U3 child mortality are extensively discussed, with particular emphasis on current maternal age, contraception usage, birth spacing, mode of delivery, maternal education, and birth order of the child.

Our study findings highlight that mothers aged 30 to 39 years face a higher risk of child mortality than mothers aged 20 to 29 years. Similar results from Bangladesh show that child mortality rates were higher among children of mothers over 30 or under 20 years old [42]. While many studies emphasize that younger mothers (under 20 years old) have a higher likelihood of U5 mortality than mothers older than 20 years [43, 44], and research also showed that older mother aged 35 to 39 years have an increased risk of fatal or neonatal mortality [45]. This could be attributed to the fact that pregnancies at older ages are often unwanted [46], which can lead to complications at the time of pregnancy, delivery or the postpartum period [47] and less vigilant self-care during pregnancy [48]. Research indicates that older mothers are at a higher risk of experiencing malnutrition, along with other chronic conditions such as diabetes and hypertension during pregnancy, which can negatively affect child survival [49]. Moreover, older mothers are more likely to experience premature births and low birth weights [49, 50], both of which contribute to higher neonatal and infant mortality [51, 52].

Our study demonstrated that the use of any contraceptive is a predictor of U3 child mortality in Bangladesh. Similar findings were also found in Ghana, indicating that mothers who did not use contraceptives had higher chances of experiencing child mortality [53]. The use of contraceptives can help to achieve optimal birth spacing and prevent undesired pregnancies, which in turn reduces the risk of early weaning for children [53]. Early weaning has been linked to mortality among children aged between 4 and 24 months [53]. Additionally, without family planning, the overall number of children increases, which elevates the risk of child mortality due to the difficulty of taking care of each child [54].

In Bangladesh, the birth intervals exhibit a notable association with child mortality. A prior study conducted in Bangladesh found that children born after short birth intervals of 15–17 months and 18–23 months faced 78% and 52% higher risks of post-neonatal mortality, respectively, compared to those born after longer intervals (three to five years) [55]. In our study, we demonstrated that children born without a previous birth interval or firstborns had almost 15 times higher risk of U3 mortality, while those with shorter intervals (< 24 months) had two times higher risk than those born with intervals of ≥ 24 months. This finding aligns with earlier research, including reports from the Philippines’ Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), which indicated that first pregnancies, particularly those with no prior birth interval, were associated with increased child mortality risks, especially when these pregnancies occurred at very early or late maternal ages [56]. Moreover, a meta-analysis [57–60], of 47 low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) confirmed that shorter birth intervals were associated with higher odds of neonatal and U5 mortality than longer birth intervals. In Bangladesh, early childbearing is common, with nearly 70% of mothers had their first child before the age of 20 [61], and early childbearing was significantly associated with U5 child mortality [43]. Short birth intervals limit maternal recovery time, leading to maternal nutrient depletion and insufficient recovery, which can negatively impact foetal growth and increase the risk of foetal malnutrition, infections, and childhood mortality [62]. Limited parental time and resources, and inadequate attention and care due to short birth intervals may exacerbate the risk of child mortality [55]. Potential educational interventions and existing policy solutions are needed into consideration, particularly for first-time mothers during pregnancy and postpartum, combined with strong antenatal and postnatal care from trained providers and well-equipped facilities, which can greatly boost mothers’ skills and confidence, making them feel more prepared to care for their new-borns [63].

This study identified the mode of delivery as a predictor of child mortality. Our findings show that children born via vaginal delivery had a higher likelihood of child mortality compared to those delivered by caesarean section. Child mortality following caesarean deliveries varies by infant weight, with lower risks observed among low birth-weight infants and higher risks among higher birth-weight infants [64]. Moreover, there are differences in child mortality outcomes among pregnant mothers with complications. Our study findings consistent with previous studies that indicating, women who require caesarean delivery due to specific risk factors or complications [65, 66], but underwent vaginal delivery instead, were more likely to experience child mortality than planned caesarean deliveries [67]. Despite clear indications for emergency C-sections in many cases, a significant proportion of deliveries still occur vaginally, with 51% of such deliveries attended by unskilled attendants, and 19.6% taking place at home [68]. Our study found that more than half (67.51%) of children were born via vaginal delivery, with the majority of these births (74.09%) occurring at home. This may contribute to higher child mortality rates. Specifically, the highest mortality rate in our study was found among children born via vaginal delivery assisted by untrained traditional birth attendants during home births, with a rate of 18.8 deaths per 1,000 live births. This highlights the importance of ensuring access to skilled birth attendants and promoting safe delivery practices to reduce child mortality. Although most of the time vaginal delivery is safer than caesarean delivery, there is a need for women to be screened in a timely and accurate manner throughout obstetric treatment, and the choice to perform a caesarean section should be made upon clear, compelling, and well-supported explanations facilitated by skilled birth assistants [69]. Despite the right of women to make informed reproductive choices, many still face restrictions imposed by family members, particularly husbands, which hinder their access to facility-based care [70, 71]. In Bangladesh, the preference among obstetricians for caesarean sections has contributed to higher rates of non-indicated caesareans, as primary healthcare providers and clinic agents often refer women to facilities where these are more common [72]. Vaginal delivery rates in health facilities in Bangladesh are notably lower (26%) than global targets (80%) [73], as rural women were found less likely to deliver in hospitals (43.36%) than urban women (62.28%). Rural mothers often prefer home births due to poor socioeconomic conditions, a perceived lack of necessity, low education levels, and fear of caesarean Sect [71]. Obstetricians in Bangladesh also appear to lack critical knowledge regarding the appropriate use of elective caesareans, that lead to vaginal delivery, further exacerbating risk of child mortality [72]. Policies should focus on improving access to emergency obstetric services, particularly in rural areas, by ensuring that healthcare workers receive proper training and establishing referral systems for managing complex cases effectively.

Our results indicate that higher maternal education levels may significantly reduce the risk of child mortality. This effect may be attributed to the fact that educated mothers typically have higher incomes, better health literacy, greater awareness of child illnesses, and a more informed approach to making decisions regarding their children’s health and well-being [74–76]. Furthermore, higher maternal education is strongly linked to improved knowledge of pregnancy and child healthcare practices [39]. In Bangladesh, while maternal education has been shown to positively influence child mortality rates, it alone may not be enough to fully address the deep-rooted socioeconomic inequalities that contribute to health disparities [77]. This highlights the need for a multifaceted approach that includes education as part of broader efforts to reduce child mortality and improve overall child health outcomes.

Previous research has shown a notable impact of birth order on U5 child mortality [78, 79]. In our study, we found that third or subsequent birth had a higher risk of child mortality than first-born birth, and this result is comparable with other studies carried out in Bangladesh [2, 4]. However, conflicting results have also been found in different studies conducted in South Asia [50–53]. An intriguing discovery emerged from an Indian study indicating that second-born children have a reduced risk during the neonatal period compared to first-born children, but there is no significant difference in the risk during the post-neonatal period in India [80]. In contrast, a recent study in Bangladesh revealed that birth order was not significantly associated with child mortality [53]. The possible reason could be that firstborn children often receive more attention and resources from parents than later-born siblings, which may affect their health and development.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths and limitations. By utilizing nationally representative data from the BDHS 2017-18, the study presents the most recent and updated findings, enhancing the accuracy and applicability of the findings. Additionally, this study stands out for its comparative analysis, which rectifies previous limitations, its emphasis on policy implications, and its comprehensive examination of child mortality in Bangladesh. However, there are several limitations to this study. First, there was no way to establish a causal association between child mortality and the independent variables, as the study was cross-sectional. Second, in the BDHS dataset, maternal healthcare variables (mode of delivery, use of any contraceptive method, birth assistance during delivery) were not available for children aged 36 to 60 months; these children were excluded from the analysis of this study. Third, there is a time gap between the use of contraceptives and child death. Since the data were secondary, we were unable to include a past history of using any contraceptive. Current contraceptive use may not be directly related to past child deaths. However, we did not expect significant changes in mothers’ usage of contraceptives in the three years before the survey. Additionally, some important variables, such as type of place of residence, antenatal care (ANC) and postnatal care (PNC), mothers’ body mass index (BMI), and child morbidity, as well as diarrhea, cholera, and fever, were not considered independent variables in this study.

Conclusions

Findings from the investigated characteristics related to mortality in children younger than three years old in Bangladesh indicated that mothers aged between 30 and 39 years who did not use contraception, had no prior childbirth, had experienced birth intervals of less than 24 months, and who underwent vaginal delivery increased the likelihood of U3 mortality in Bangladesh. Therefore, it may be concluded that additional training is required for unskilled traditional birth attendants, advocating for vaginal deliveries to be overseen by skilled birth attendants, and emphasizing the importance of timely screening for women. The decision to perform a caesarean section should be made for clear, compelling, and well-supported reasons. Additionally, to ensure a significant reduction in child mortality, attention needs to be given not only to mothers with low or limited literacy but also to those with a primary education. Providing mothers and children with health-related seminars that enhance their knowledge is essential. Motivating couples to use contraceptive methods for longer birth intervals and persuading mothers to provide extra care to higher-order children could reduce childhood mortality in Bangladesh.
