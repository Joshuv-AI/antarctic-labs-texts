Custom humeral antibiotic spacers for complex shoulder arthroplasty revision: technical principles and case illustrations
Authors: Fatumastar O Adan, Madeline A Wethington, Audrey L Wethington, Alicia K Harrison, Allison J Rao
Published: 2026

Shoulder arthroplasty utilization continues to increase, and the volume of revision procedures is expected to rise accordingly.3 Although clinical outcomes following primary shoulder arthroplasty are generally favorable, complications such as periprosthetic joint infection (PJI), instability, mechanical loosening, and periprosthetic fracture remain devastating complications that often require implant removal and staged reconstruction.4

Two-stage revision protocols remain the standard approach for chronic shoulder PJI but also may be needed as staging procedures for other complications as well.11 Prefabricated devices offer simplicity and reproducibility; however, their fixed geometry limits their utility in cases of severe canal widening, extended osteotomy, bone loss, instability, narrow canals, instability, or fracture.12 In these complex scenarios, achieving mechanical stability and often infection control within a structurally compromised humerus presents a significant technical challenge.

Custom humeral antibiotic spacers allow intraoperative tailoring of construct length, diameter, offset, and antibiotic composition.5,7 Incorporation of intramedullary reinforcement allows application of fundamental orthopedic principles, including fracture bypass and load sharing across compromised bone segments.6 Despite these theoretical advantages, current literature consists primarily of isolated technical notes, and there remained limited guidance regarding specific indications, biomechanical rationale, and technical consideration for custom spacer use in complex revision shoulder arthroplasty.5,7 The purpose of this study is to describe a technical guide to create of a custom humeral antibiotic spacer including the indications, surgical rationale, and technical considerations in complex shoulder revision scenarios. Five representative cases are presented to illustrate different clinical situations in which prefabricated spacers were inadequate due to fracture, severe bone loss, canal compromise, or instability. Through these cases, practical technical strategies and decision-making principles are highlighted to guide surgeons managing complex shoulder arthroplasty failures.

Materials and methods

This retrospective single-institution technical review with illustrative cases was approved by the institutional review board. Operative records from 2011 to 2025 were reviewed using procedure codes and operative descriptors including “custom spacer,” “Rush rod,” and “antibiotic spacer.” Two independent reviewers performed manual chart review to identify patients who underwent custom humeral antibiotic spacer placement. A total of 306 antibiotic spacers were placed during the study period; 27 of these were custom humeral spacers. Five representative cases were selected to illustrate distinct clinical indications, whereas the remaining custom cases involved overlapping scenarios.

This study was designed as a descriptive technique paper to highlight the clinical indications, operative construction, and biomechanical rationale for custom humeral spacers rather than to evaluate clinical outcomes. The clinical course was reviewed descriptively only to contextualize the representative cases, and no comparative outcome analysis was performed. Clinical information was obtained from operative reports, outpatient follow-up notes, radiographs, and infectious disease documentation in the electronic medical record.

Pre-operative considerations

Patients undergoing custom spacer placement typically present with infection, mechanical loosening, fracture, severe canal widening, or extensive bone loss. Pre-operative planning includes assessment of humeral bone stock, canal diameter, fracture location, and prior implant length. Particular attention is paid to whether the spacer will need to bypass areas of osteolysis, fracture, or prior/anticipated osteotomy.

Operative exposure and implant removal

Procedures are performed in the beach chair position through a standard deltopectoral approach, typically utilizing prior incisions. Antibiotics are commonly withheld until intraoperative cultures are obtained. Dense scar tissue and distorted anatomy are common in revision settings. Careful dissection is required to identify the deltopectoral interval and protect the axillary nerve. A Cobb elevator is often placed directly under the acromion to free the subdeltoid space, while avoiding lateral sweeping to minimize the risk of axillary nerve injury. Multiple deep cultures are obtained prior to antibiotic administration. Cultures are routinely sent for aerobic and anaerobic analysis and held for 21 days, particularly to evaluate for Cutibacterium acnes.2

Existing implants are removed as indicated. Well-fixed humeral stems may require controlled osteotomy or windowing techniques. In cases involving fracture or nonunion, reduction is performed under fluoroscopic guidance prior to spacer construction to allow appropriate alignment and length restoration. Radical débridement of all necrotic tissue, pseudocapsule, cement, and nonviable bone is performed before reconstruction. Copious irrigation was performed using dilute antiseptic solution and saline lavage.

In complex revision cases, the humerus is frequently structurally compromised by fracture, osteolysis, canal widening, and prior osteotomy. Cement alone has limited structural durability and does not reliably distribute load in weakened bone. In these settings, stress concentration at the tip of a short spacer may predispose to fracture propagation or mechanical failure.

Custom spacers allow incorporation of an intramedullary scaffold to facilitate load sharing along the humeral shaft. Extending the construct beyond area of fracture or bone loss applies the principle of fracture bypass, reducing stress risers and improving mechanical stability. Intraoperative molding further permits restoration of humeral length and soft tissue tension while tailoring antibiotic composition to the infectious profile.

Spacer construction followed a consistent technique in the cohort cases presented in this manuscript (Fig. 1). A Rush rod is used as an intramedullary scaffold. Rod length is selected by bypassing areas of structural compromise and achieving distal fixation within intact diaphyseal bone. Fluoroscopy is used to confirm depth, alignment, and central positioning within the canal to avoid cortical stress concentration. Antibiotic-loaded cement is prepared intraoperatively. Vancomycin is routinely added, with dosing adjusted based on organism profile and infectious disease recommendations. The cement is molded around the intramedullary rod to create a humeral stem and spherical head construct. Head size is shaped to approximate native tension while avoiding overstuffing. In cases of severe canal widening, additional cement can be used to increase stem diameter. In fracture of nonunion cases, spacer length is extended to span the fracture site, providing internal splinting. Final fluoroscopic images confirm construct alignment and appropriate bypass length. Layered closure is performed. Drains and negative pressure dressings are used selectively based on soft tissue quality.

Post-operative protocol

Post-operative, the arm is immobilized in a sling. Immediate motion of the hand and wrist is permitted and shoulder motion is restricted until infection control and mechanical stability are confirmed. Infectious disease consultation guides antibiotic therapy. Plans for reimplantation versus definitive spacer retention are individualized based on infection eradication, bone quality, patient comorbidities, and functional goals.

Illustrative cases

Case 1 - stem loosening with distal cortical compromise

A 66-year-old male with rheumatoid arthritis and multiple prior revision of a left reverse total shoulder presented with chronic pain and radiographic stem loosening extending to the distal humerus. His history included a well-functioning reverse until 5 years post-operative, when a fall resulted in a periprosthetic humeral fracture, with subsequent revision reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) and open reduction and internal fixation. After this surgery, he had poor functioning and electromyography demonstrated active denervation with early reinnervation of the deltoid muscle, along with mononeuropathies of the radial and dorsal scapular nerves. On presentation, imaging demonstrated lucency along the entire humeral component with medial cortical thinning near the distal stem tip and proximal nonunion (Fig. 2, A and B).

Case 1 radiographs – stem loosening with distal cortical compromise. (A) Anteroposterior radiograph of the left humerus and shoulder in a 66-year-old man with a history of periprosthetic humeral fracture and multiple revision reverse total shoulder arthroplasties, obtained approximately 3 weeks pre-operatively, demonstrating circumferential lucency surrounding the humeral stem consistent with component loosening, medial cortical thinning at the distal stem tip, proximal humeral nonunion, and dissociation of the humeral tray from the stem. (B) Anteroposterior radiograph of the left humerus and Grashey view of the left shoulder obtained approximately 10 days pre-operatively, demonstrating persistent circumferential lucency surrounding the humeral stem consistent with loosening, medial cortical erosion at the distal stem tip, and dissociation of the humeral tray from the stem. (C) Anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of the Left shoulder obtained 5 weeks post-operative following explantation of the loose reverse total shoulder arthroplasty humeral stem, irrigation, and excisional débridement of the shoulder and humerus including bone, and placement of an antibiotic-impregnated cement spacer.

Because of the distal cortical compromise and need to bypass the weakened segment, a prefabricated spacer was deemed inadequate due to risk of stress concentration and fracture propagation. A custom spacer reinforced with a long intramedullary Rush rod was constructed to bypass the compromised diaphysis and restore humeral length.

The humeral stem was explanted and radial débridement was performed. The glenosphere was removed, while the well-fixed baseplate was retained. A long cement spacer containing vancomycin and gentamicin was molded around the intramedullary rod under fluoroscopic guidance to ensure distal bypass (Fig. 2C).

The patient subsequently underwent reimplantation 1 month later. He later experienced recurrent mechanical failure unrelated to spacer integrity and ultimately required further revision.

Case 2 - iatrogenic periprosthetic fracture and morbid obesity

A 64-year-old female with PJI following total shoulder arthroplasty underwent explantation and spacer placement. An intraoperative midshaft humeral fracture occurred during initial management and progressed to nonunion despite bracing (Fig. 3A). Body mass index was greater than 50. Given the fracture location and high mechanical stresses related to body habitus, definitive reconstruction was considered high risk. A prefabricated spacer would not reliably span the fracture or provide sufficient stability.

(A) Grashey and scapular Y radiographs of the left shoulder in a 64-year-old woman obtained approximately 2 months pre-operatively demonstrate a displaced midshaft periprosthetic humeral fracture with radiographic features concerning for chronic infection and impending nonunion prior to spacer revision, biopsy, irrigation and débridement, and placement of a long-stem antibiotic-impregnated spacer spanning the fracture. (B) Anteroposterior radiographs of the left humerus and scapular Y views of the left shoulder obtained approximately 1 week post-operative demonstrate status post removal of the prior humeral antibiotic spacer, biopsy with culture, irrigation and excisional débridement of the humerus, and placement of a long-stem antibiotic-impregnated cement spacer spanning the midshaft fracture. (C) Anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of the Left humerus obtained approximately 7 weeks post-operative demonstrate maintained alignment following removal of the prior humeral antibiotic spacer, biopsy with culture, irrigation and excisional débridement of the humerus, and placement of a long-stem antibiotic-impregnated cement spacer spanning the midshaft fracture.

A long stem custom spacer reinforced with a Rush rod was constructed to span the fracture, restore alignment, and provide internal splinting while delivering local antibiotics (Fig. 3 B and C). This construct was retained as definitive treatment. The spacer achieved mechanical stability sufficient for activities of daily living without further surgery.

Case 3 - osteomyelitis with stem extraction and intraoperative fracture

A 66-year-old female with recurrent infected total shoulder arthroplasty and humeral osteomyelitis required implant removal (Fig. 4A). The long-cemented stem was rigidly fixed and required anterior humeral osteotomy for extraction. During removal, a distal diaphyseal fracture occurred (Fig. 4B).

Case 3 radiographs – osteomyelitis with stem extraction and intraoperative fracture. (A) Anteroposterior and scapular Y radiographs of the left shoulder in a 66-year-old woman obtained approximately 4 months prior to implant removal demonstrate a long cemented humeral stem with distal diaphyseal fracture and radiographic features concerning for chronic infection, preceding anterior humeral osteotomy, radical débridement, and spacer placement. (B) Intraoperative fluoroscopic images of the left humerus demonstrate open reduction and internal fixation of the humeral shaft fracture with restoration of alignment and appropriate hardware positioning. (C) Anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of the left humerus obtained on post-operative day 1 demonstrate status post removal of the infected total shoulder arthroplasty, radical débridement for humeral osteomyelitis, and open reduction and internal fixation of the distal diaphyseal fracture, with appropriate alignment and stable fixation. (D) Anteroposterior and Grashey radiographs of the left shoulder obtained approximately 4 months post-operative demonstrate maintained alignment and stable fixation without interval hardware complication.

The presence of osteomyelitis, extended osteotomy, and fracture made a prefabricated spacer inadequate due to limited ability to bypass compromised bone. A custom intramedullary rod–reinforced spacer was created to span the osteotomy and fracture site, applying fracture bypass principles and maintaining length and soft tissue tension (Fig. 4C and D). The patient subsequently underwent staged reconstruction with reverse shoulder arthroplasty after infection control.

Case 4 - chronic instability and suspected indolent infection

A 56-year-old female with 6 prior operations presented with a chronically dislocated rTSA and extensive bone loss (Fig. 5, A and B). Intraoperatively, gross fibrinous material and purulence beneath the glenosphere raised concern for indolent infection.

(B) Axillary and external rotation radiographs obtained approximately 6 months pre-operatively demonstrate chronic anterior dislocation of the reverse total shoulder arthroplasty with persistent anterior translation of the humeral component relative to the glenosphere, consistent with mechanical instability and implant malposition. (C) Anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of the left humerus obtained on post-operative day 0 demonstrate status post removal of the chronically dislocated reverse total shoulder arthroplasty, explantation of the allograft–prosthetic composite and associated hardware, and radical débridement of the humerus and scapula including sequestrectomy, with maintenance of humeral alignment. (D) Anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of the left humerus obtained approximately 3 weeks post-operative demonstrate maintained alignment with expected post-operative changes and no acute complication. (E) Grashey and scapular Y radiographs of the Left shoulder obtained approximately 7 weeks post-operative demonstrate maintained alignment with stable post-operative findings and no interval hardware complication.

Given severe bone loss and instability, complete explantation and radical débridement were performed. A prefabricated spacer would not adequately restore humeral length or soft tissue tension in the setting of allograft composite removal. A custom spacer was molded to maintain alignment and deliver high-dose local antibiotics (Fig. 5, C-E). Cultures ultimately returned negative. The spacer served as a bridge to later revision with proximal humeral replacement.

Case 5 - chronic infection, nonunion, and severe bone loss

A 70-year-old female with prior C acnes infection, periprosthetic fracture, nonunion, and repeated hardware failure presented with persistent drainage and humeral bone loss (Fig. 6, A and B).

After plate removal and reverse arthroplasty explantation, extensive débridement revealed structurally compromised bone and chronic nonunion. A prefabricated spacer would not provide sufficient stability across the nonunion site. A long custom spacer reinforced with an intramedullary rod was constructed to span the fracture and deliver local vancomycin (Fig. 6, C and D). Despite spacer placement, the patient experienced persistent mechanical failure and ultimately required conversion to tumor prosthesis reconstruction.

Case summary

Of the 5 cases presented, 2 underwent successful staged reimplantation. One retained the spacer as definitive treatment. Two ultimately required tumor prosthesis reconstruction due to progressive bone loss.

Discussion

As utilization of rTSA continues to expand, surgeons may increasingly encounter complex revision scenarios in which infection is accompanied by substantial mechanical compromise.3 This technical note with illustrative cases highlight 5 distinct clinical situations in which custom humeral antibiotic spacers were used to address both infectious and mechanical challenges when prefabricated implants were insufficient. Although staged revision remains the standard treatment for chronic PJI, these cases are frequently complicated by periprosthetic fracture, osteolysis, extended osteotomy, canal widening, or severe bone loss.4,11 In such settings, the surgeon must address 2 competing objectives: eradication of infection and restoration of mechanical stability within a structurally compromised humerus, a challenge illustrated across the cases presented.

Antibiotic cement spacers play a central role in staged management by delivering high local antibiotic concentrations while maintaining limb length and soft tissue tension.7 However, not all spacers perform equivalently in complex mechanical environments. Prefabricated antibiotic spacers offer convenience and reproducibility but are constrained by fixed geometry.9 In addition, fixed stem diameters may not achieve adequate canal fill in widened osteolytic bone, increasing the risk of instability, migration, or fracture propagation.1,8

Complex revision cases often involve disrupted cortical integrity and stress risers within the humeral shaft, as demonstrated in cases 2, 3, and 5. Cement alone has limited structural durability and does not reliably provide load sharing across weakened bone segments.1 Incorporating an intramedullary scaffold permits application of fundamental fracture principles.6 By extending the spacer beyond areas of fracture, osteolysis, or osteotomy, the construct distributes stress over a longer segment of intact diaphyseal bone.7 This bypass principle reduces stress concentration and may decrease the risk of mechanical failure. In addition, restoration of humeral length and soft tissue tension may improve stability and facilitate later reimplantation. Custom spacers therefore serve not only as antibiotic delivery devices but also as temporary load-sharing constructs.6,7

Based on cases presented and our institutional experience, custom spacers may provide particular value in complex revision scenarios characterized by structural compromise of the humerus. Several of the cases presented, particularly cases 1, 2, and 5 demonstrate scenarios in which a commercially available spacer would not have reliably bypassed compromised bone or restored mechanical alignment. In such circumstances, customization allows the surgeon to adapt the construct to the patient's specific pathology rather than forcing the pathology to conform to implant limitations. These include periprosthetic humeral fractures requiring diaphyseal bypass (cases 1 and 2), severe canal widening or osteolysis that limits stable stem fixation (case 1), and extended humeral osteotomies performed for implant removal (case 3).5,7 Custom constructs may also be advantageous in the setting of chronic infection nonunion (cases 2 and 5), extensive bone loss following multiple revisions (cases 4 and 5), or when no commercially available spacer provides sufficient length to restore alignment and soft tissue tension. In addition, custom spacers may serve an important role in medically frail patients in whom definitive spacer retention is anticipated as an alternative to further reconstructive surgery.1 In contrast, in more routine infection cases with preserved bone stock and diaphyseal support, prefabricated spacers remain an appropriate and efficient option.8, 9, 10

Custom spacers may function either as a bridge to reimplantation or as a definitive treatment. Among the illustrative cases presented, some patients proceeded to successfully staged reconstruction (cases 1 and 3), whereas others required further salvage procedures due to progressive bone loss or mechanical failure (cases 4 and 5), and 1 retained the spacer as definitive treatment (case 2). Importantly, spacer placement should not be viewed as curative for advanced structural compromise but rather as a temporizing or stabilizing intervention within a broader reconstructive strategy. Patient selection remains critical. In elderly or medically complex patients with low functional demands, definitive spacer retention may provide acceptable pain relief and function while avoiding the morbidity of repeated revision surgery.1

This technical review is limited by the small number of illustrative cases and the absence of comparative outcome analysis. The 5 cases presented were selected to illustrate distinct mechanical indications rather than to represent the full institutional experience. No comparative analysis between prefabricated and custom spacers was performed. In addition, several patients experienced continued mechanical failure despite spacer use (cases 4 and 5), underscoring that customization does not eliminate the challenges inherent to severe bone loss and infection. Future investigations should focus on comparative outcomes, mechanical complication rates, reimplantation success, and cost analysis. Standardized reporting of spacer design parameters would also facilitate comparison across studies.

Conclusion

Custom humeral antibiotic spacers provide a flexible strategy for managing complex revision shoulder arthroplasty in the setting of fracture, severe bone loss, or canal compromise. By incorporating intramedullary reinforcement and applying fracture bypass principles, these custom spacers are helpful in providing mechanical stability while delivering local antibiotic therapy. Although not a definitive solution in all cases, custom spacers represent a valuable technique to consider in selected patients.
