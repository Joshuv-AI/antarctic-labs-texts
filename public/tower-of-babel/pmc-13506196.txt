Opioid and Stimulant Coprescriptions among South Carolina Residents
Authors: Alexis Stoner, Lindsay Tjiattas-Saleski, Emma Padgett, Cole Harp, Martin Groke, David Redden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:The South Carolina Department of Public Health (SCDPH) Prescription Drug Monitoring Program (PDMP) is used to monitor the prescribing patterns of controlled substances, including opioids and stimulants. Data collected by the National Center for Health Statistics show that the number of drug overdose deaths involving opioids and stimulants has been increasing since 1999. Although databases such as the PDMP monitor healthcare professionals’ prescribing habits regarding opioids and stimulants individually, there has been little research regarding opioid-stimulant coprescribing patterns. Considering increasing death rates and the lack of research, data from the SCDPH PDMP were used to identify factors that influence opioid-stimulant coprescribing. It was hypothesized that younger age, higher daily morphine milligram milliequivalents (MMEs), increased duration of prescription, and male gender would be associated with increased opioid-stimulant coprescriptions.Methods:Data were gathered from the SCDPH PDMP from 2016 to 2021. The variables assessed included patient age, patient sex, filling date, supply length, daily MMEs, and Lexicomp Drug Classification. A linear regression statistical analysis was then performed to analyze the data.Results:Between January 1, 2016 and December 31, 2021, a total of 9,785,146 opioid prescriptions were filled by 2,158,564 individuals, with 161,103 (1.65%) of them receiving a concurrent stimulant prescription. Patients who were 50 years of age and younger had a 2.74 greater odds of receiving a concurrent stimulant prescription (P<0.001), along with those who had an opioid prescription length of ≥ 30 days (odds ratio [OR] 4.38, P<0.001). Being female (OR 1.58) and a daily MME ≥ 50 mg but ≤100 mg (OR 1.24) were both significantly found to indicate an increased odds of having a concurrent prescription.Conclusions:This study indicates that those with longer opioid prescriptions, younger individuals, higher daily MMEs, and women are at an increased risk of opioid-stimulant coprescription. Future research should investigate other factors associated with opioid-stimulant coprescribing, such as education status, rurality, and whether specific opioid or stimulant agents are linked to coprescribing.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) traces the opioid epidemic to a sharp rise in prescription opioid use beginning in the 1990s, with subsequent waves of overdose deaths in 2011 dominated by heroin and in 2013 driven by synthetic prescription opioids. These events led to the development of national prescribing guidelines aimed at reducing opioid overuse.1 As a result of these regulatory changes and increased awareness, all-cause drug overdose deaths in the United States declined from 110,037 in 2023 to 80,391 in 2024, with psychostimulant-related deaths also decreasing from 37,096 to 29,456 during the same period.2 Despite this overall improvement, however, stimulants remain frequently involved in opioid overdose, with 53.6% of fatal synthetic opioid overdoses involving a stimulant.3

Stimulants and opioids are drug classes that have a high potential for illicit use and abuse.4,5 Although opioid prescribing has declined both nationally and at the state level, stimulant prescriptions have increased, reflecting a rise in attention-deficient/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) diagnosis and corresponding treatment. In 2023, an estimated 15.5 million US adults (6.0%) had a current ADHD diagnosis.6 One-third of these received stimulant medication to manage their symptoms in the previous year.6 According to South Carolina Department of Public Health (SCDPH) Prescription Drug Monitoring Program (PDMP) data, opioid prescriptions decreased by 26.11% from 2015 to 2020 and continued to decline through 2024, whereas during the same period, stimulant prescriptions increased by 13.58% and 16%, respectively.7 Interestingly, dextroamphetamine sulf-saccharate/amphetamine sulf-aspartate were the most frequently dispensed controlled substances from 2022 to 2024.

According to the CDC, the national opioid dispensing rate has continued to decline, falling from 46.8 prescriptions per 100 persons in 2019 to 37.5 prescriptions per 100 persons in 2023.8 Meanwhile, earlier national data showed that stimulant dispensing rates rose from 5.6 to 6.1 prescriptions per 100 persons between 2014 and 2019.9 Another study found that among individuals aged 5 to 64 years with employer-sponsored health insurance, the percentage who filled at least one prescription for a stimulant increased from 3.6% in 2016 to 4.1% in 2021.10 Although opioid prescriptions have declined nationally due to initiatives such as the 2016 CDC prescribing guidelines and increased awareness of addiction risks, ADHD diagnoses and subsequent stimulant treatments have been on the rise.

People who use both opioids and stimulants are at a greater risk of a fatal drug overdose than are patients who use opioids alone.11 Another study showed that among people with a history of incarceration, the hazard of both fatal and nonfatal overdose was the highest with both opioid and stimulant use disorder diagnoses.12 As such, analysis of factors surrounding prescription trends, with the intention of using this information to protect patients, while appropriately treating their medical needs, is important for clinicians when considering coprescriptions of these substances. Although there is a copious amount of literature on prescription opiate abuse and prescription stimulant abuse separately, the literature that examines the two drugs together is limited. Existing research evaluating opioids and stimulants concurrently has focused largely on overdose outcomes rather than on prescribing behaviors or risk factors for coprescription use. For example, prior research has demonstrated that individuals who use both opioids and stimulants have significantly higher rates of fatal overdose compared with those using opioids alone.11 Similarly, other studies have investigated the association between fatal and nonfatal overdose or substance use disorder with concurrent opioid and stimulant exposure, rather than clinical or demographic factors associated with receiving both prescriptions.13,14 These findings highlight the significant health risks associated with opioid-stimulant coprescribing, but they do not address the prescribing patterns that may contribute to the risk. Identifying these patterns represents an important public health objective because understanding which populations are more likely to receive an opioid-stimulant coprescription may allow clinicians to better identify individuals at increased risk of medication misuse and potential overdose.

METHODS

This retrospective descriptive study was approved as exempt by the Edward Via College of Osteopathic Medicine and the SCDPH institutional review boards. The SCDPH tracks stimulant and opioid prescriptions in South Carolina via the PDMP. The PDMP is a tracking system for every stimulant and opioid prescription that is written in South Carolina, filled in South Carolina, or prescribed to a South Carolina resident. Data from 2016–2021 was extracted for this study and included the following variables: age, sex, duration of opioid prescription, and daily morphine milligram equivalents (MMEs). The inclusion criteria included being a South Carolina resident with at least a 30-day concurrent opioid and stimulant prescription. This minimum coprescription length was chosen to exclude patients with prescribed opioids in the short term.

Analysis of the data for this project was performed using SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Because of the potential of repeated observations for a subject within each replicate, generalized estimating equations were used to estimate the effect of the predictors (age, length of prescription, MME, sex) and the outcome of a concurrent stimulant prescription while on an opioid prescription. Given the binary nature of the outcome, generalized estimating equations produce an odds ratio (OR) as the measure of association.

To identify concurrent stimulant prescriptions, 9,785,146 opioid prescriptions were identified, as well as their fill dates and projected end dates. These data were then merged with the records of subjects who had been prescribed stimulants, with the fill dates for each stimulant prescription listed in the records for that subject. The software then scanned across all filled dates for stimulant prescriptions to determine whether that fill date fell between the fill date and the end date of the opioid prescription under investigation for that subject. If any fill dates for stimulant prescriptions fell between fill and end dates for the opioid prescription, then the opioid prescription was classified as having a concurrent stimulant prescription. Otherwise, the opioid prescription was classified as not having a concurrent stimulant prescription. Of the 9,785,146 opioid prescriptions, 161,103 (1.65%) had concurrent stimulant prescriptions.

For the statistical analysis identifying patient and prescription characteristics that are associated with a concurrent stimulus prescription, generalized estimating equations were used to account for the nested nature of the data. Examination of age and the odds of concurrent stimulus indicated a nonlinear relationship with an inflection point at 50 years of age. As such, age was divided into 50 years or younger versus older than 50 years of age for modeling purposes. Finally, the analyses were restricted to include prescriptions indicating a daily MME >0 mg, records indicating gender as male or female, and patient’s age ≤ 90 years of age. The decision to exclude these records was based on two motivations: to exclude medical records with potentially spurious information and to simplify statistical interpretations for sex. Adding these restrictions decreased the number of prescriptions to 9,074,731 opioid prescriptions for 2,068,148 individuals. We conducted a sensitivity analysis to compare the results from a full dataset analysis (including potentially spurious ages and daily MME values) with results of the restricted/reduced analysis dataset. The magnitude and interpretation of ORs and their respective 95% confidence intervals did not meaningfully differ between the two datasets, with the estimated ORs and confidence intervals changing by <0.1. All of the analyses used a two-tailed type I error rate of 0.05.

RESULTS

Opioid prescription length indicated that the majority (55.19%) of prescriptions were prescribed for <30 days (Table 1). Of the individuals with at least one opioid prescription, 1,200,627 (55.62%) were documented as female, 916,902 (42.42%) were male, and 41,035 (1.90%) had unknown sex. The average age of the individuals indicated in this study was 49.40, with a standard deviation of 20.19 years of age (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This study identified several key factors associated with a higher likelihood of concurrent opioid and stimulant prescribing among South Carolina residents. Greater daily MME, longer treatment duration, and being female were strongly linked to combined prescribing. Increase in age, as predicted, appears to play a protective role, with a reduction in the likelihood of prescribing both medication classes simultaneously. These findings highlight distinct demographic and prescribing patterns that characterize individuals who are more likely to experience opioid-stimulant polypharmacy.

Several studies have examined concurrent opioid and stimulant exposure using national insurance claim databases. For example, a recent longitudinal cohort study using MarketScan data evaluated trends in coprescribing and found that stimulant prescriptions were associated with escalating opioid dose trajectories over time.15 Few studies, however, have used state-level prescription drug monitoring program data to evaluate demographic or prescribing factors associated with opioid-stimulant coprescribing. Prescribing practices may vary geographically; therefore, state-specific analysis is needed to better understand regional prescribing trends and identify populations at increased risk.

When placed within the context of the existing literature, some findings align with broader prescribing trends, whereas others diverge from expectations. Previous research has shown that advancing age is generally associated with increased opioid use; however, our results suggest that younger individuals are more vulnerable to concurrent stimulant exposure.16 Increasing age was found to have an inverse relationship with opioid and stimulant coprescriptions because older patients had fewer concurrent prescriptions for both medication classes compared with younger patients. Previous literature has shown that older individuals are more likely to have an opioid prescription, and younger individuals are more likely to have a stimulant prescription. In addition, increasing age was found to be associated with a longer interval between the prescription of an additional medication. This discrepancy may reflect differences in prescribing practices, which are more common in younger populations due to conditions such as attention-related disorders and weight management.17,18 Younger individuals also may already be receiving stimulant therapy before the initiation of opioid treatment, which could explain the shorter interval between prescriptions observed in this group.

Increasing daily MMEs and longer durations of opioid prescriptions were both found to be associated with an increased rate of coprescribing, which reinforces concerns about prescribing patterns in individuals receiving more intensive pain management. The OR for daily MME was only 1.016, whereas the OR for length of opioid prescription was 3.188. As such, there is a stronger association between the duration of opioid prescription and opioid-stimulant coprescriptions than prescription strength. The coexistence of stimulants and opioids may be intentional in some cases because stimulants can mitigate opioid-associated sedation.19,20 Moreover, stimulant medications have been shown to have analgesic effects of their own and even enhance the analgesic properties of opiate medications.21 Some studies advocate for the routine use of a combination of stimulant and opioid medications in responsible and reliable patients.22 Their ability to counteract the side effects of opiates and even enhance the analgesic properties of opiates could be the reason that increasing the daily MME and duration of opiate prescription is associated with opioid-stimulant polypharmacy. In addition, prolonged exposure to opioids inherently increases the opportunity for additional medications to be introduced over time, whether for symptom management or unrelated conditions. Average daily MME, although displaying statistically significant differences for each range, did not demonstrate clinically significant differences in the time between the prescription of an opioid and the addition of a stimulant. The supply of opioid in days, however, was found to have significant differences in the time between the prescription of an opioid and the addition of a stimulant medication. Patients with opioid prescriptions exceeding 30 days experienced a longer interval before stimulant initiation compared with those with shorter prescriptions. A reason for this could be that longer-term prescriptions are written for chronic conditions, and the stimulant is initially not necessary but may be added to potentiate the action of the opioid of adequate pain control is not achieved by opiate alone.

Another plausible explanation lies in the intersection between chronic pain and obesity. Obesity-related pain frequently necessitates extended opioid use, whereas stimulants may be prescribed for weight reduction, creating overlapping therapeutic pathways that culminate in combined prescribing.23 With obesity potentially requiring long-term opiate use to treat pain, stimulants could be prescribed to an obese patient with pain to help him or her lose weight and thus decrease the pain. This phenomenon is another possible explanation for increased rates of opioid-stimulant polypharmacy with longer duration of opioid prescriptions and higher daily MMEs. This dynamic emphasizes the importance of considering broader clinical and behavioral factors when evaluating polypharmacy patterns.

The increased likelihood of combined prescribing among women also mirrors prior findings that women experience higher rates of chronic pain and opioid use.16,24 This statistic remains true across age groups spanning from 18 to 65 years.16 This vulnerability may be attributed to a greater prevalence of pain-related conditions and biological differences in pain perception and modulation. Painful conditions such as fibromyalgia, lupus, and rheumatoid arthritis are more common in women than in men.24 With an increased likelihood of having more painful conditions, women are more predisposed to being prescribed a long-term opioid medication than men. Moreover, research shows that women have lower pain thresholds during menses due to decreased opioid receptor activation during the low estradiol phase of menstruation.24 In addition, as previously discussed, stimulant medications can be used to counteract the sedating effects of opioids, as well as enhance the efficacy of opioids. This increased risk for painful conditions and lower pain thresholds during menses may explain why women were more likely than men to experience opioid-stimulant polypharmacy. There was no statistically significant difference in time with gender as a factor between opioid prescription and addition of a stimulant prescription. Because diagnosis codes were not obtained for patient identification numbers in this study, prescriptions could not be linked to a specific diagnosis.

One limitation to this study was that diagnosis codes were not obtained for patient identification numbers; therefore, prescriptions could not be linked to a specific diagnosis. Future studies could entertain further analysis of diagnosis codes for prescriptions to ascertain whether specific diagnoses or specific conditions linked to female gender drive the increased risk for females to have coprescriptions for opioids and stimulants. Although the dataset is large and all of the results are significant, there is potential for the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic to have skewed the dataset. Stimulant prescription rates were altered during the COVID-19 pandemic, with some segments of the population experiencing declining rates of prescription, whereas others experienced increasing rates.25 During the pandemic, rates of opioid prescriptions for chronic pain increased, while rates of opioid prescriptions for non-chronic pain decreased.26 Future studies could investigate the influence of the COVID-19 on prescription rates. Prior research has demonstrated that socioeconomic factors are associated with differences in opioid prescribing patterns, with rural populations and individuals with lower socioeconomic status experiencing higher rates of opioid prescribing. Although these variables were not available in the PDMP dataset used in this study, future research incorporating these demographic factors and diagnostic codes could help clarify whether specific conditions or populations are associated with a greater likelihood of opioid-stimulant coprescribing. We hypothesize that individuals with conditions commonly treated with stimulant medications who also require long-term opioid therapy for chronic pain may be more likely to receive concurrent prescriptions and that socioeconomic and geographic disparities may further influence these prescribing patterns.

CONCLUSIONS

With increasing rates of drug-related deaths and the risks associated with opioid-stimulant polypharmacy such as a greater risk of drug-related poisoning, it is more vital than ever to study prescription drugs and consider factors related to coprescribing. This study investigated the relationship between opioid-stimulant coprescribing and four variables: age, sex, duration of opioid prescription, and daily MMEs. Results indicated that longer-term opioid prescriptions, higher daily MMEs, younger individuals, and women are at an increased risk of opioid-stimulant coprescription. Future studies could examine demographic or socioeconomic factors and their relationship to simultaneous coprescription of opioids and stimulants. In addition, future studies could use diagnostic codes to assess whether certain diagnoses influence prescription rates. These future studies could help illuminate the relationship between these demographic or socioeconomic factors and opioid-stimulant coprescribing and could help identify risk factors for this phenomenon. Identifying pertinent risk factors can empower new initiatives to form aimed at reducing the frequency of opioid and stimulant coprescribing.
