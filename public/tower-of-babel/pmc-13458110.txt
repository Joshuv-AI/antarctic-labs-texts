Conditional Survivorship of Osteochondral Allograft Transplantation in the Knee
Authors: Nathanial W. Linstrom, William D. Bugbee, Tim Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Osteochondral allograft (OCA) transplantation has demonstrated reliable long-term outcomes in treating chondral and osteochondral lesions of the knee. Although several pre- and intraoperative factors associated with graft failure have been identified, the temporal distribution of graft failures after OCA transplantation has not been described.Purpose:To evaluate the timing and rate of graft failure after knee OCA transplantation and to report conditional survivorship (CS) to inform postoperative patient counseling.Study Design:Case series; Level of evidence, 4.Methods:We identified 288 knees (267 patients) that underwent OCA transplantation by a single surgeon between 1997 and 2015, with a minimum follow-up of 10 years. Graft failure was defined as any reoperation requiring removal of the allograft. Nonfailure reoperation was defined as any surgical procedure on the affected knee that did not involve graft removal. Rates of graft failure and nonfailure reoperation were estimated using piecewise linear regression applied to inverse Kaplan-Meier curves. Kaplan-Meier analysis estimated overall and 10-year CS.Results:The median patient age was 33.1 years (range, 11-68 years), and 58% were male. Overall graft survivorship was 78% at 10 years. Graft failure occurred in 64 knees (22%) at a median of 2.7 years (range, 0.3-23.6) postoperatively. Annual failure rates were 4.9% in the first 3 years postoperatively and decreased to 1.5% from 3 to 10 years. Nonfailure reoperations occurred in 48 knees, with a median of 1.5 years postoperatively, and followed a similar temporal pattern, with most occurring in the early postoperative period. CS analysis demonstrated that grafts remaining in situ at 4 years postoperatively had a 90% probability of survival to 10 years.Conclusion:OCA transplantation of the knee demonstrated an overall 10-year graft survivorship of 78%. Graft failures were not uniformly distributed over time, with the highest failure rates occurring during the early postoperative period and substantially decreasing after 3 years postoperatively to a low steady-state of 1.5% annually. This temporal pattern was also noted with nonfailure reoperations, which occurred predominantly during the early postoperative period. CS analysis offers a patient-centered approach for interpreting these findings and may inform postoperative patient counseling.

Articular cartilage lesions of the knee are common, present in approximately 66% of knee arthroscopies, and have a limited capacity for regeneration.1,24,29 When left untreated, cartilage defects in weightbearing joints may result in pain, functional impairment, and progression of osteoarthritis.21,44 Multiple surgical options have been developed for the treatment of localized articular cartilage defects, including autologous chondrocyte implantation, subchondral bone marrow stimulation, osteochondral autograft transplantation, and osteochondral allograft (OCA) transplantation.

OCA transplantation is a restorative procedure that addresses both the chondral and osseous components of full-thickness defects by transplantation of viable, mature hyaline cartilage and subchondral bone from a donor.9,32 It is indicated for osteochondral defects that are unsuitable for other cartilage repair techniques, revisions of previously failed cartilage resurfacing procedures, and the treatment of a broad range of articular and osteoarticular pathologies.8,17 OCA transplantation offers distinct advantages over other knee restoration procedures, including versatility in treating lesions of varying size, shape, and depth, the ability to be performed in a single stage without donor-site morbidity, and restoration of mature hyaline cartilage.4,36 Long-term follow-up after OCA transplantation in the knee has demonstrated durable graft survival, with reported survivorship of approximately 78% to 91% at 10 years, 69% at 20 years, and 59% at 25 years.14,34 Additionally, patients report high satisfaction and favorable return to sport rates after OCA transplantation.5,18

Despite the consistent success of OCA transplantation, with high patient satisfaction and long-term graft survival, outcomes can vary based on patient- and graft-related factors. Previous studies have identified several risk factors associated with graft failure, including patient sex, age, body mass index (BMI), lesion size, graft location, previous surgery, and the presence of bipolar chondral defects.13,28,30,38-41 Some graft failures can be managed with revision or fixation; however, conversion to total knee arthroplasty is often required. 2

Although preoperative risk factors for graft failure are increasingly well understood, counseling patients regarding long-term postoperative prognosis remains a clinical challenge. 3 Long-term graft survival requires successful osseous integration of the OCA, which can take several years depending on the size of the graft.19,20 This time-dependent process suggests that the risk of graft failure may not be uniform over time. When failure risk changes over time, conditional survivorship (CS), a statistical method used in other fields, notably oncology, can provide updated survival probabilities based on the duration of time already survived. 22 However, to our knowledge, the temporal distribution of OCA failures has not been assessed. We hypothesized that graft failures are not uniformly distributed over time and that an identifiable early postoperative period exists during which grafts are at higher risk of failure. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the timing and rate of graft failure after OCA transplantation of the knee and to report CS to inform postoperative patient counseling.

Methods

Study Population

An institutional review board-approved prospective OCA registry was used to identify 610 primary OCA transplantations of the knee from 1997 to 2023 by a single surgeon (W.D.B.). Of the 610,288 knees (267 patients) included in the analysis, all had undergone OCA transplantation at least 10 years prior and had a minimum follow-up of 10 years or a failure within that time. Before transplantation, all patients had a diagnosis of full-thickness chondral or osteochondral lesions that failed to improve with nonsurgical management and were not considered candidates for arthroplasty.

A total of 288 knees (267 patients) met the inclusion criteria. The median patient age was 33.1 years, the median BMI was 24.9 kg/m2, 58% of patients were male, and 84.7% had undergone previous surgery. Femoral condyles and multifocal grafts comprised 81.7% of cases. The most common diagnoses were osteochondritis dissecans (36.1%) and degenerative chondral lesions (21.9%). Concomitant procedures were performed in 107 knees (37.1%), and the median treated surface area at the time of OCA transplantation was 8.35 cm2 (Table 1).

Allograft Protocol and Surgical Technique

Donor tissue was recovered, processed, and tested in accordance with guidelines from the American Association of Tissue Banks. Allografts were obtained from healthy donors aged 15 to 40 years and were required to meet visual and tactile standards for cartilage quality. Before 2002, grafts were processed at the University of California, San Diego Regional Tissue Bank and stored at 4°C in lactated Ringer's solution, with implantation performed within 7 days of donor death. After 2002, grafts were processed by AlloSource/Joint Restoration Foundation and cold-stored in storage media for up to 28 days. Donor-recipient size matching was performed using the mediolateral width of the tibial plateau, measured from a standard anteroposterior radiograph after adjusting for magnification. 16 Blood or tissue type matching was not performed, and no immunosuppressive therapy was used. The surgical technique for OCA transplantation has been described previously. 43

Data Collection

Demographic variables (age, sex, BMI, primary diagnosis, and previous surgeries on the operative knee) and allograft details (anatomic location, number of grafts, and graft size) were obtained from the OCA registry. Graft survival or failure was determined from the most recent clinical follow-up or, if unavailable in person, through telephone or mail to confirm if any additional surgical interventions had been performed on the allografted knee. Graft failure was defined as any reoperation requiring removal of the graft, such as revision OCA transplantation or conversion to arthroplasty. Nonfailure reoperations were defined as any surgical procedure on the affected knee after OCA transplantation that did not involve graft removal. Procedure details were not available for all reported nonfailure reoperations.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics (age, sex, BMI, primary diagnosis, and previous surgeries on the operative knee), allograft details (anatomic location, number of grafts, and graft size), reoperations, and overall failure rate. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Continuous variables were not normally distributed and are therefore reported as medians and ranges. Graft survivorship was calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with graft failure defined as the endpoint.

To characterize the temporal distribution of graft failures, a linear regression model was fitted to the inverse of the Kaplan-Meier cumulative failure curve. Inspection of the curve suggested a nonuniform distribution of graft failures, with differing rates before and after 3 years postoperatively. Accordingly, piecewise linear regression was used to model separate slopes for 0 to 3 years and 3 to 10 years postoperatively. The 3-year knot was selected based on visual inspection of the cumulative failure curve and clinical interpretability, as the analysis was intended to descriptively characterize temporal failure patterns rather than formally estimate an inflection point. For each segment, the slope of the cumulative failure curve, representing the annual percentage of the cohort experiencing graft failure, was calculated along with the coefficient of determination (R2) and 95% CIs.

A similar descriptive approach was used to characterize the temporal distribution of nonfailure reoperations. Visual inspection of the inverse Kaplan-Meier curve suggested differing rates before and after 2 years postoperatively. Accordingly, piecewise linear regression was used to estimate separate slopes for 0 to 2 years and 2 to 10 years postoperatively. Knees were censored at the time of graft failure, after which nonfailure reoperation is not possible.

Conditional 10-year survivorship was calculated for patients without graft failure at 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 years postoperatively, representing the probability of 10-year graft survival given survival to each postoperative time point. At each time point, patients with previous graft failure were excluded, and Kaplan-Meier survival estimates were recalculated. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp).

Results

Within 10 years of OCA transplantation, 95 knees (33%) underwent at least 1 subsequent reoperation. Graft failure requiring removal or revision occurred in 64 knees (22.2%), with a median time to failure of 2.7 years (range, 0.3-23.6). Of the failed grafts, 43 knees (67%) were converted to unicompartmental or total knee arthroplasty, 20 knees (31%) underwent revision OCA transplantation, and 1 knee (2%) underwent patellectomy. Overall graft survivorship was 93%, 84%, and 78% at 2, 5, and 10 years, respectively (Figure 1).

The inverse Kaplan-Meier cumulative failure curve showed a substantial decrease in the annual graft failure rate after 3 years postoperatively (Figure 2). From 0 to 3 years, the annual rate of graft failure was 4.9% per year (95% CI, 4.7%-5.4%; R2 = 0.96; 34 failure events), whereas from 3 to 10 years, the failure rate decreased to 1.5% per year (95% CI, 1.4%-1.6%; R2 = 0.96; 30 failure events).

Discussion

The most important finding of this study is that the annual rate of graft failure decreases substantially after the initial postoperative period after OCA transplantation, resulting in progressively improved CS over time. After an annual failure rate of 4.9% during the first 3 postoperative years, the failure rate decreased to 1.5% per year from years 3 to 10, reflecting that durable long-term outcomes can be achieved with an OCA.

Importantly, this temporal pattern was not limited to graft failure alone. Nonfailure reoperations showed a similar distribution, with higher rates in the early postoperative period, followed by a lower, stable rate (0.8% per year) after postoperative year 2. Together, these findings suggest that the need for additional surgical intervention decreases after the initial postoperative years after OCA transplantation. Notably, most nonfailure reoperations were arthroscopic, indicating that many early secondary interventions were minimally invasive for symptom management rather than graft-related revision surgeries.

The relevance of the observed temporal distribution of OCA failures to broader cartilage restoration practices is supported by the consistency of our outcomes with those previously reported in the literature. In this cohort of 288 knees, treated by a single high-volume surgeon, overall graft survivorship was 93%, 84%, and 78% at 2, 5, and 10 years, respectively, with an overall reoperation rate of 33%. Similarly, in a 2018 systematic review of OCA outcomes by Familiari et al, 12 including 1036 patients across 19 studies, graft survivorship was estimated at 86.7% at 5 years and 78.7% at 10 years, with a weighted mean reoperation rate of 30.2%. Additionally, Yazdi et al 46 evaluated a single-center cohort of 337 patients with a mean follow-up of 5.6 ± 2.6 years. They reported graft survivorship of 95% at 2 years and 84.1% at 5 years, with an overall reoperation rate of 36.8%. Minor differences in survivorship estimates across studies likely reflect heterogeneity in patient populations, surgical indications, and follow-up duration.

While survivorship and reoperation rates in our cohort are consistent with previous OCA literature, this study is the first to describe how graft failure rates change over the 10 years after OCA transplantation. A notable strength of this study is the large cohort with complete 10-year follow-up, which enabled this analysis by maintaining a substantial number of knees at risk throughout the study period and avoiding attrition-related bias. From a clinical perspective, 10-year graft survival indicates a successful intervention, particularly given the potential role of OCA transplantation in delaying the need for total knee arthroplasty. This is especially relevant for younger patients and for older, active patients who may not yet be ideal candidates for arthroplasty. 27

The temporal distribution of graft failures and nonfailure reoperations in this study, characterized by an initial higher-risk period followed by a stable period, differs from that of other cartilage treatment options. Microfracture (MFX), one of the most frequently performed surgical treatments for focal cartilage lesions, is often used as a baseline against which other cartilage surgeries are compared. First described in 1979, MFX, or variations thereof, is still used because it is a single-stage, minimally invasive, and relatively low-cost procedure.23,35 Although MFX is associated with reliable short-term clinical improvement, mid- to long-term durability varies, particularly outside of small, well-contained lesions, with multiple studies demonstrating plateauing or declining patient-reported outcomes after 2 to 5 years postoperatively.15,31 Previous studies suggest that these differing outcome trajectories may relate to differences between the fibrocartilaginous repair tissue formed after MFX and the native hyaline cartilage restored with OCA.26,37

OCA transplantation follows a bone-healing paradigm with a mature hyaline cartilage layer, such that once osseous integration has occurred, reliable long-term outcomes can be achieved. 19 Before graft integration, short-term complications may involve fibrotic tissue formation, osteonecrosis, delamination, or mechanical collapse.33,45 In comparison, other cartilage treatment options, including cell-based or marrow-based cartilage repair techniques that depend on cellular proliferation and maturation, may not fully mature in situ and therefore be more susceptible to mid- to long-term deterioration.10,11 Although direct comparisons between treatment options must be interpreted with caution, given differences in indications, patient selection, and outcome measures, the present findings further support OCA as a durable restorative option for osteochondral lesions of the knee, with reliable long-term outcomes.

The secondary goal of this study was to report CS as a patient-centered method to communicate the prognostic significance of the timing and rate of graft failure, with 10-year graft survival serving as a clinically meaningful benchmark of long-term success. CS recalculates Kaplan-Meier survival estimates at postoperative time points after excluding patients with prior graft failure (ie, reducing the denominator). Unlike traditional Kaplan-Meier survival estimates, which estimate survival probability from the time of surgery, CS provides individualized survival estimates based on the duration of graft survival already achieved. As a result, when failure risk is concentrated early and decreases over time, CS estimates increase accordingly. This provides patients with a dynamic estimate of their long-term prognosis and has been emphasized as a valuable tool for patient counseling.25,42 In clinical practice, our CS analysis may help clinicians reassure patients once they have passed the initial higher-risk period after OCA transplantation and update them on their likelihood of graft survival at 10 years postoperatively.

Although CS has not previously been reported for OCA transplantation, it has been widely applied in oncology to refine prognostic estimates as patients survive beyond initial high-risk periods. 47 While CS is commonly reported using large registry data, it has also been applied to single-center clinical cohorts of comparable size, supporting its use to estimate 10-year OCA survivorship.6,7,22 Consistent with the reduction in graft failure rate after the early postoperative period, CS analysis showed that patients whose grafts remained in situ at 4 years had a 90% probability of surviving to 10 years.

This study has several limitations. The retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data may introduce selection bias, as patients with available long-term follow-up may differ from those lost to follow-up. Additionally, patients without documented graft failure who were lost to follow-up before 10 years were excluded from analysis, which may introduce survivorship bias if these patients experienced different outcomes than those in the present cohort. The follow-up modality (in-person vs telephone/mail) was also not consistently recorded in the registry. Furthermore, this cohort reflects the experience of a single high-volume cartilage surgeon over more than 2 decades, which may limit generalizability and introduce potential confounding related to evolving surgical experience and changes in graft storage protocols, including the 2002 transition from fresh graft implantation within 7 days of procurement to cold-stored graft implantation up to 28 days after procurement. The cohort was intentionally heterogeneous with respect to diagnosis, lesion size, graft type, and concomitant procedures to reflect the diverse population of patients undergoing OCA transplantation. However, this heterogeneity may limit the applicability of CS estimates to specific patient subgroups. Additionally, 21 patients contributed bilateral knees, introducing potential nonindependence between observations that was not specifically accounted for in the statistical analysis. The piecewise regression analyses were descriptive, with inflection points selected based on observed event patterns rather than formal changepoint estimation, as the modest number of failure events may not support formal breakpoint modeling. Finally, the precision of survivorship estimates at later postoperative time points may be reduced by smaller numbers of knees remaining at risk over time and by incomplete procedural details, which limited nonfailure reoperation data in some cases.

OCA transplantation of the knee demonstrated an overall 10-year graft survivorship of 78%. Graft failures were not uniformly distributed over time, with the highest failure rates occurring during the early postoperative period and substantially decreasing after 3 years postoperatively to a low steady-state of 1.5% annually. This temporal pattern was also noted with nonfailure reoperations, which occurred predominantly during the early postoperative period. CS analysis offers a patient-centered approach for interpreting these findings and may inform postoperative patient counseling.
