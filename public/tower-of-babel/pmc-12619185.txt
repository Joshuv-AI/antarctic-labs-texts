Single-nucleus transcriptome profiling of skeletal muscle in Diqing Tibetan pigs with distinct body sizes
Authors: Suyun Fang, Yuedong Zhang, Ruifei Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Diqing Tibetan pig (DQTP) is a local breed in China, renowned for its high adaptation to plateau climates. However, it has undergone less human selection compared to modern commercial pigs, resulting in smaller but a wide range of body sizes and meat production within its population. The cellular transcriptional variation in this breed related to muscle phenotypes remains unexplored.ResultsIn this study, we performed single-nucleus transcriptome analysis to investigate the muscle cell profiles of 6-month-old pigs with differing body sizes. Cell clustering analysis indicated muscle cells can be categorized into myogenic lineages (myocytes and satellite cells), fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs), endothelial and vascular-related cells (EVRCs), and immune cells (both myeloid and lymphoid). The composition of cell types reflected subtypes of myocytes and mitochondrial metabolism-related FAPs and endothelial cells, with significant differences observed among cell subtypes. We discovered some genes with cell subtype-specific differential expression levels, such as TECRL and MYOZ2 (slow-twitch muscle), and MYL1, AMPD, and FILIP1L (Myocyte-like FAPs). Additionally, ligand-receptor analysis identified significantly stronger cell interaction activities in larger DQTP pigs, suggesting a potential association with the observed the body size variation. The pathways involving COLLAGEN, LAMININ, and PTPRM, which are crucial for extracellular matrix organization and adhesion, were mostly enriched in interactions between FAPs and EVRCs.ConclusionThis work provides a comprehensive overview of muscle-resident cell profiles and identifies differences in cell type composition, cellular gene expression levels, and cell communication intensity in DQTP, which may enhance the understanding of the mechanisms underlying discrepancies in porcine muscle growth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12251-z.

Background

Pork is one of the most favorable agricultural meat products worldwide, bringing great economic benefits to human society. The longissimus dorsi (LD) muscle is a representative muscle tissue for lean meat production assessment in pig industry. Researcher worldwide had studied on this muscle tissue, and had established a large amount of data and knowledge, including genomic, transcriptomic, and epigenetic landscapes [1–4].

Previous literature has performed cellular composition and expression profiling in porcine skeletal muscle [5–7]. Generally, the cellular population in skeletal muscle tissue includes muscle satellite cells, myocytes, fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs), and endothelial cells (ECs). These populations are identified as primary signaling contributors within the muscle environment. Additionally, significant differences in muscle phenotypes exist among pig breeds [8]; for instance, European commercial breeds such as Duroc, Landrace, and Large White exhibit high lean meat yield, whereas Chinese indigenous breeds display lower lean meat yield but higher fat content compared to their European counterparts [9]. Previous studies have illustrated muscle cell composition and conducted comparative analyses of these breeds, revealing distinct cellular transcriptomic profiles [4, 8]. Beyond breed-specific differences, variations in meat phenotypes within populations, such as loin muscle area and depth in commercial populations, indicate that a substantial number of candidate genes are associated with these muscle phenotypes [1].

Diqing Tibetan pigs (DQTP) inhabit the area near Shangri-La City in the Diqing Autonomous Prefecture, Yunnan Province, China, where the altitude exceeds 3000 m in a hypoxic environment, demonstrating their high adaptation to plateau climates [9–11]. This breed has not been through high intensity of human selection as commercial breeds, and the adaptation to variable environmental changes in plateau lead to greater variation of body growth within its population. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) is a powerful technique that enables the transcriptomic profiling at single cell resolution and allows for the dissection of cellular heterogeneity of individual nuclei [12]. A previous study had conducted snRNA-seq from skeletal muscle on purebred Berkshire and Tibetan pigs, and identified differentially expressed genes between breeds that were regulated by trans-elements [13]. However, the cellular transcriptomic expression variation, as well as the molecular basis for phenotypic variations within DQTP population remains unclear.

To decipher the cell-type-specific transcriptional underpinnings of body size variation in pigs, we leveraged snRNA-seq on skeletal muscle from DQTP pigs of differing body sizes (DQTPL: large; DQTPS: small). The snRNA-seq technique was essential for this study as it allows for the precise identification and comparison of all cell subtypes, including myogenic lineage cells, FAPs, endothelial and vascular-related cells (EVRCs), and immune cells. The findings revealed significant cellular heterogeneity and expression differences between DQTPL and DQTPS, thereby providing a landscape of cellular transcriptomic discrepancies in pigs exhibiting divergent body sizes. This cellular atlas constitutes a valuable resource for understanding the genetic basis of traits in local pig breeds, thereby aiding their genetic improvement. The identified cell subpopulations and gene expression signatures offer insights into the genetic resources of the DQTP pig. These findings could inform future strategies for the conservation and utilization of local breeds, aimed at enhancing desirable traits such as carcass yield, meat quality, robustness and adaptation to climate change, thereby aiding in the preservation of genetic distinctiveness.

Materials and methods

Sample Preparation

Four six-month-age Tibetan pigs (male) with two of each had discrepant body size from a breeding farm located near Shangri-La City in the Diqing Autonomous Prefecture of China (altitude exceed 3300 m) were selected in this study, including DQTPs in small size (DQTPS) and large size (DQTPL). These pigs were raised under the same semi-free-ranging conditions on the same farm, which is their traditional rearing system. They were primarily fed a natural diet based on locally available qingke (highland barley) and grasses, without supplementation with concentrated commercial pellets. The DQTPLs weighted 42.6 and 46.5 Kg, with higher body weight, body length and height than DQTPS (weighted 36.05 and 36.35 Kg) (see Additional file 1: Figure S1). Pigs were stunned with electric shock before being slaughtered. LD muscle tissues from the 10th rib were collected from each animal, a part was fixed with 10% neutral buffered formalin for histological verification, and the rest was frozen in liquid nitrogen for the preparation of single nuclear suspension and RNA extraction.

Tissue fixation and staining

LD muscle tissue were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for at least 24 h once animals were slaughtered. Tissues were then washed in PBS (pH 7.4), dehydrated by gradient ethanol elution, embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 5 μm. Sections of muscle tissue were processed with hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining. Images were acquired using a brightfield microscope (Olympus BX61VS) equipped with a digital slide system (VS120).

Nuclei isolation from samples

Nuclei were isolated from fresh-frozen muscle samples as described previously with minor modification [14]. LD sample from liquid nitrogen was placed and dissected in a 2 ml Eppendorf tube containing 1.5 ml homogenization buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 500 mM sucrose, 50 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM DTT, 1% BSA, 0.1% CA630) supplemented with 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, cat. no. 4693132001) and 0.4 U/µl RNase inhibitor (Invitrogen™, cat. no. 10777019). Tissues were transferred into a 2 ml Dounce Tissue Grinder (Kimble Chase, cat. no.#885300-0002) placed on ice for 5–10 min and homogenized with A (“loose”) pestle for 20 strokes, followed with 20 strokes using B (“loose”) pestle on ice. The solution was filtered using a 70-µm cell strainer (Miltenyi Biotec, cat. no.130-098-462), then filtered with a 30-µm cell strainer (Miltenyi Biotec, cat. no.130-110-915) into a 15 ml tube. Nuclei were pelleted with centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min at 4 °C and washed with 1.5 ml PBS supplemented with 1% BSA and 0.2 U/µl RNase inhibitor (Invitrogen™, cat. no. 10777019) twice. The nuclear suspension was centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min at 4 °C and the pellet was resuspended in cold PBS to reach a final concentration of 1 × 106 nuclei per milliliter after counting in AO/PI (Countstar mira-FL), and in trypan blue (Count Star, RE010213) using a Countstar mira-FL cell counter.

snRNA-seq library Preparation and sequencing

Cells were loaded into the microfluidic chip of the Chip A Single Cell Kit v2.1 (MobiDrop (Zhejiang, China) Co., Ltd., cat. no. S050200301) to generate droplets using the MobiNova-100 (MobiDrop (Zhejiang, China) Co., Ltd., cat. no. A1A40001). Each cell was encapsulated in a droplet containing a gel bead linked to millions of oligonucleotides (cell unique barcodes). After encapsulation, the droplets underwent light cutting by the MobiNovaSP-100 (MobiDrop (Zhejiang, China) Co., Ltd., cat. no. A2A40001) while the oligonucleotides diffused into the reaction mix. The mRNAs were captured by the cell barcodes, followed by cDNA amplification within the droplets. After reverse transcription, the cDNAs with barcodes were amplified, and a library was constructed using the High Throughput Single-Cell 3’ Transcriptome Kit v2.1 (MobiDrop (Zhejiang, China) Co., Ltd., cat. no. S050200301) and the 3’ Dual Index Kit (MobiDrop (Zhejiang, China) Co., Ltd., cat. no. S050300301) [15, 16]. The resulting libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 System.

Data processing and quality control

Raw data in fastq format from single-cell 3’ transcriptome were pre-analyzed using MobiVision (v3.2) (https://www.mobidrop.com), with reads aligned to the reference genome, Sscrofa11.1, which was retrieved from the Ensembl database (GenBank accession number: GCA_000003025.6). A filtered cell-gene matrix was generated using MobiVision. For subsequent analysis, low-quality cells were excluded based on established filtering methods [17]. As a result, total number of reads exceed 1.08 G. The efficiency of barcodes ranged between 78.44% and 90.78%, and the Q30 based in UMI varied from 94.79% to 95.05%. The median of UMI counts per gene across all samples ranged from 863 to 1441, and all samples exhibited consistently no mitochondrial RNA in cells (see Additional file 1: Table S1).

Dimensional Reduction, clustering and annotation

“LogNormalize” and “vst” were applied to normalize and find variable features within the snRNA-seq gene expression data using the “FindVariableFeatures” function in the Seurat package (v5.2.1) [18]. The valid value of average expression as a range from 0.05 to 5 and that of dispersion as no less than 0.5. The “RunPCA” function in the Seurat package was used to perform the principal component analysis (PCA) on the single-nucleus expression matrix with genes restricted to highly variable genes (HVGs, defined as genes that exhibit variable expression across cells, characterizing distinct cell populations). Top 15 number of principal components (PCs) for clustering analysis were picked for clustering cells using the “FindClusters” function in the Seurat package (see Additional file 1: Figure S2). Cell clustering visualization was performed using t-SNE or UMAP plots. Cell integration was performed using the “SelectIntegrationFeatures”, “FindIntegrationAnchors”, and “IntegrateData” functions. The sample-level t-SNE analysis was based on average expression value for each cells using the “AverageExpression” function in the Seurat package. For sub-clustering analysis, a similar procedure was applied including the variable genes identification, dimension reduction, cell integration, and the clustering identification to the restricted clusters derived from the overall analysis. The cell populations were annotated based on the expression pattern of HVGs and the well-known cellular markers from previous literatures [6–8, 19]. The identification of HVGs of a cluster relative to all other clusters, the “FindAllMarkers” function in Seurat was used, and the parameters were set as following: differential expression threshold of 0.25 log fold change using Wilcoxon rank sum test with p-value < 0.05 following Bonferroni correction. Gene ontology enrichment analysis were used the Omicshare tools (https://www.omicshare.com).

DEGs detection between pig groups

We extracted the scaled expression values of each gene for each sample within each cell subpopulation. Only those cell subpopulations with more than 10 cells in both DQTPL and DQTPS, and with an absolute log fold change of less than one in cell numbers between these two groups, were included in the analysis. Furthermore, DEGs were identified between DQTPL and DQTPS based on a differential expression threshold of more than one log fold change, utilizing the Wilcoxon rank sum test with a p-value < 0.05, adjusted using Bonferroni correction.

Monocle trajectory analysis

Pseudotime analysis simulates a continuous biological process by arranging cells along a hypothetical timeline, reconstructing their developmental paths from a static snapshot [20]. To map the differentiation of cell subpopulations, we performed pseudotime analysis using Monocle version 3 to elucidate the significant transitional relationships among various cell types and clusters [21]. The variable genes were selected utilizing the Seurat R package. Following dimension reduction and cell ordering with default parameters, the differentiation trajectory of cells was inferred, with UMAP specified as the dimensionality reduction method. We extracted cells derived from DQTPL and DQTPS, applying the same procedure as described above. Subsequently, we used the “plot_cells” function, setting the “color_cells_by” parameter to “pseudotime”. For myogenic lineage cells, FAPs, and EVRCs, the root cells were designated as “Satellite cells”, “VOA-FAPs” (vascular-osteogenesis-associated FAPs), and “EPCs” (endothelial progenitor cells), as these subpopulations have been identified as progenitor cell populations (Satellite cells and EPCs) or associated with a broader range of GO enrichment categories (VOA-FAPs).

Ligand-receptor cell communication analysis

We retrieved homologous genes between humans and pigs from the ENSEMBL BioMart database (https://useast.ensembl.org/biomart/martview), resulting in a total of 16,708 homologous genes available for downstream analysis. Subsequently, we employed the “identifyOverExpressedGenes”, “identifyOverExpressedInteractions”, “computeCommunProb”, “computeCommunProbPathway”, and “aggregateNet” functions from the CellChat R package (v1.5.0) to predict ligand-receptor interactions and cell-cell communication networks based on single-nucleus transcriptome data, using the identified homologous genes (which were converted to their human counterparts using BioMart database) [22]. Finally, we utilized the “netVisual_bubble” and “netAnalysis_signalingRole_network” functions in CellChat to visualize the predictive outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in R (v4.4.1) (https://www.r-project.org/). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test (two-sided) and Chi-square test were used for continuous and categorical data, respectively. All tests with significant differences between each two groups were indicated by p-value < 0.05.

Results

Major cell populations of skeletal muscle

Skeletal muscle cell populations from pigs were profiled using 10× Genomics snRNA-seq. Following stringent quality control (see Materials and methods), a total of 34,112 high-quality nuclei were retained for downstream analysis, comprising 14,820 nuclei from DQTPL and 19,292 nuclei from DQTPS. Unsupervised clustering analysis (tSNE) revealed clear separation between DQTPL and DQTPS based on average gene expression levels (see Additional file 1: Figure S3). Cell clusters were uniformly distributed across all four datasets, indicating minimal batch effects (Fig. 1a and b).We identified 14 clusters spanning seven cell populations in both DQTPL and DQTPS (Fig. 1a-d and see Additional file 2: Table S2): myogenic lineages, including myocytes (cluster 0, 1, 2, 4, and 7) (MYH4, ACTN2 and ACTA1) [8, 23, 24], muscle satellite cells (cluster 9 PAX7) [8]; fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs) (cluster 3 and 6) (DCN and COL1A1) [8]; endothelial and vascular-related cells (EVRCs) (cluster 5, 10, 12, and 14) (ADGRF5, PECAM1, and CDH5) [25, 26]; two immune cell clusters, including myeloid cells (cluster 8) (CD163) and lymphoid cells (cluster 11) (CD8A) [27, 28]; and adipocytes (cluster 13) (DGAT2) [29]. Gene expression tree demonstrated high transcriptional similarity between homologous cell populations in pigs, (Fig. 1c), enabling systematic comparison of cellular expression profiles between these two groups.

We quantified the cellular composition and observed a generally balanced distribution between DQTPL (14,820 nuclei) and DQTPS (19,292 nuclei) (43.45% vs. 56.55%) (Fig. 1e). Most cell populations maintained proportions consistent with this overall distribution, with the exception of adipocytes. Although adipocytes represented a minor fraction in both groups, at 0.25% for DQTPL and 0.63% for DQTPS, it was the only cell type that showed a significantly higher proportion in DQTPL compared to DQTPS (p-value = 0.002, ꭓ² test) (Fig. 1e). Despite this overall compositional similarity at the population level, we observed potential heterogeneity in cellular subtypes between groups. The gene expression showed differences between DQTPL and DQTPS in EVRCs, FAPs, satellite cells, and myocytes (Fig. 1c), exhibiting the cellular heterogeneity between the groups. To systematically investigate these differences, we next performed comprehensive subpopulation-level analyses comparing cellular expression profiles between DQTPL and DQTPS.

Myogenic lineage subpopulation and differences

Myocytes represented the predominant cellular component in porcine skeletal muscle (Fig. 1a and e), which may indicate extensive differentiation into various myocyte subpopulations at the mature stage of pigs. We defined a hierarchy of myogenic lineages using the tSNE clustering method, which includes satellite cells and myocytes. Besides, so as to investigate the relationship between the adipocytes with myogenic lineages, we were also included them into these analyses. Apart from adipocytes (DGAT2) [29], the myogenic lineages can be categorized into seven myocyte subclusters, satellite cells (PAX7) and neuromuscular junction-associated cells (NMJ-ACs) (MUSK) (Fig. 2a and see Additional file 2: Table S3) [8, 30]. Among the myocytes, we detected some HVGs in relation to muscle growth specifically expressed in certain subclusters (ARID5B in myocytes-sub0 [31]; PDLIM3 and MYBPH in myocytes-sub1 [32, 33]; RYR3 in myocytes-sub2 [34]; SLC7A8 in myocytes-sub3 [35]; RB1CC1 in myocytes-sub4 [36]; YWHAE in myocytes-sub5 [37]; MYL2 and MYL3 in myocytes-sub6 [8, 38, 39]; and PGAM2 in myocytes-sub7 [40]) (see Additional file 1: Figure S4 and Additional file 2: Table S3). Besides, some well-known markers for myocytes were also detected among some myocyte subclusters. For example, fast-twitch markers ACTA1 and TNNI2 were highly expressed in myocytes_sub5/7, MYH1 was prominent in myocytes_sub0, MYH4 was found in myocytes-sub1/2/3/7, while MYBPC1 was highly expressed in myocytes-sub4/6. ACTN2 was found to be specifically highly expressed in myocytes-sub6. Additionally, MYL2 and MYL3 were specifically highly expressed in myocytes-sub6 (see Additional file 1: Figure S4), and both genes were reported as marker genes for slow myocytes [8, 38, 39]. Therefore, we inferred that the myocytes_sub6 was the most representative of cells from slow-twitch fibers.

Next, we conducted pseudotime analysis and trajectory inference (Fig. 2c and d). The trajectories indicated that satellite cells maintained a quiescent state in both DQTPL and DQTPS. Consistent with their role as muscle stem cells, some expansion among myocyte subpopulations was observed in DQTPL, indicative of ongoing muscle regeneration processes in this group. This heightened regenerative potential in DQTPL may directly contribute to its larger body size by facilitating more efficient muscle growth and repair. In both DQTPL and DQTPS, the slow-twitch muscle subcluster (myocytes_sub6) did not exhibit any relevance to satellite cells or other myocytes, suggesting that the slow-twitch muscle type had reached a mature stage. This suggests that myocytes_sub6 operates as a distinct functional unit, specialized for sustained, oxidative metabolism and likely plays a key role in postural maintenance and endurance, rather than active growth. Additionally, adipocytes formed an independent cluster with no apparent connection to the trajectories of myogenic lineages in either group, suggesting that this cluster also represented terminally differentiated cells, or was not relevant to myogenic lineage differentiation. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the marker genes associated with this adipocyte population revealed a significant association with thermogenesis-related biological processes (see Additional file 2: Table S4). Additionally, we also identified abundant intramuscular fat (IMF) within the muscle tissue of these pigs (see Additional file 1: Figure S5). The isolated adipocyte population may be related to the IMF phenotype; related marker genes, such as ADIPOQ, have been reported to be negatively correlated with the intramuscular fat content of the longest dorsal muscle in sheep [41]; ACSL1 was identified as potential key genes porcine IMF trait [42]; FABP4 has been found to be associated with marbling and IMF content in pork [43]; THRSP gene was identified as a biomarker for swine intramuscular fat cells [19]. We therefore infer these isolated adipocytes population may be in relation to thermogenesis or IMF trait, which is unrelated with differentiation myogenic lineages at mature stage in DQTP.

The myogenic lineage subtypes exhibited significant heterogeneity among subpopulations of myocytes. Specifically, myocytes-sub0 and myocytes-sub2 predominantly originated from DQTPL, whereas myocytes-sub1, myocytes-sub3, myocytes-sub4, and myocytes-sub5 were primarily derived from DQTPS (Fig. 2e and f and see Additional file 1: Figure S6). The representative marker genes for these subclusters were specifically expressed in fast-twitch muscle fibers (myocytes-sub0/1/2/3/7), such as MYH1 and MYH4, as well as in slow-twitch muscle fibers (myocytes-sub4/6), such as MYBPC1. These reflected the heterogeneity of fast- and slow-twitch myocyte subtypes between DQTPL and DQTPS. Additionally, we identified some cell subclusters relative uniformly distributed between DQTPL and DQTPS (myocytes-sub5, myocytes-sub6 (slow-twitch myocytes), myocytes-sub7, satellite cells, adipocytes, and NMJ-ACs) and therefore conducted a differential gene expression analysis within these clusters between DQTPL and DQTPS (see Additional file 2: Table S5). We discovered 149 DEGs among all myogenic lineage subpopulations between DQTPL and DQTPS. As shown in Fig. 2g, the DEGs exhibited higher expression levels in DQTPL compared to DQTPS in myocytes-sub7, satellite cells, and NMJ-ACs, suggesting greater gene expression activity in myogenic lineage cells in DQTPL. Notably, the number of DEGs in myocytes-sub6 was highest than that of other subclusters (see Additional file 2: Table S5). Two key DEGs, TECRL and MYOZ2, were found to be differentiated expressed between DQTPL and DQTPS and specifically highly expressed in slow-twitch myocytes (Fig. 2h and i). These genes encode known regulators of skeletal muscle repair and differentiation [44, 45], potentially contributing to the observed phenotypic differences.

FAP subpopulation and differences

We further characterized subpopulations of FAPs across DQTPL and DQTPS using tSNE clustering method. Adipocytes were included based on their proposed developmental relationship with FAPs in a previous study [8]. Our result showed adipocytes formed a distinct cluster separate from all FAP subpopulations, indicating a more distant relationship than previously reported in neonatal porcine muscle (Fig. 3a). Using identified marker genes of muscle FAPs alongside previously reported genes [8], we identified seven distinct FAP subpopulations (Fig. 3b; see Additional file 2: Table S6). These FAPs were classified into neuro-associated FAPs (NA-FAPs) (TGFB2, AGTR1, EGFR, and DCC), vascular-osteogenic-associated FAPs (VOA-FAPs) (PLXDC1 and GLI3), vascular-associated FAPs (VA-FAPs) (NRP1), myocyte-like FAPs (ACTA1, TPM1, and NEB), mitochondria (MT)-rich FAPs (CYTB, COX2), interstitial cells (VIM), and tenocytes (MKX, COL1A2, and COL1A1). The classification was further supported by the top enriched Gene Ontology (GO) terms. These subtypes were identified as FAPs associated with neuronal, vascular, and osteogenic processes (see Additional file 2: Tables S7–S9), including NA-FAPs (GO:0030198: extracellular matrix organization; GO:0022008: neurogenesis), VA-FAPs (GO:0030198: extracellular matrix organization; GO:0072359: circulatory system development), and VOA-FAPs (GO:0030198: extracellular matrix organization; GO:0001944: vasculature development; GO:0001501: skeletal system development).

We further conducted pseudotime analysis and trajectory inference. The results indicate both groups shared initial differentiation from VOA-FAPs and VA-FAPs. DQTPL showed predominant progression toward NA-FAPs, while DQTPS exhibited preferential differentiation into MT-FAPs. This suggested distinct differentiation states and trajectories between DQTPL and DQTPS, potentially reflecting metabolic adaptations to their respective physiological demands. Notably, adipocytes formed an isolated cluster completely distinct from all FAP trajectories, indicating either terminal maturation or an independent developmental origin unrelated to the FAP differentiation.

High heterogeneity of cell subtypes was also observed in the FAP subpopulations between DQTPL and DQTPS (Fig. 3c). We calculated the cell composition and found that the differences primarily stemmed from NA-FAPs, interstitial cells, and MT-rich FAPs, with NA-FAPs predominantly enriched in DQTPL, while interstitial cells and MT-rich FAPs were predominated in DQTPS (Fig. 3e). This observation further suggests distinct differentiation states related to the respective physiological demands of DQTPL and DQTPS. Beyond cellular heterogeneity, we also detected differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in subpopulations that were uniformly distributed across DQTPL and DQTPS. Consequently, myocyte-like FAPs showed the most pronounced transcriptional differences, containing most number of DEGs than other subpopulations (see Additional file 2: Table S10), including three particularly informative markers that were specifically highly expressed in myocyte-like FAPs: MYL1, AMPD, and FILIP1L. All three genes exhibited elevated expression levels in DQTPL, with MYL1 recognized as a marker gene for fast-twitch myocytes [8], AMPD1 significantly associated with porcine muscle phenotypes [44], and FILIP1L involved in protein localization to actin cytoskeleton [46].

EVRC subpopulations and differences

In the major cell population clustering result, we detected abundant endothelial vascular-related cells (EVRCs) in the muscle tissue (Fig. 1a). Based on the functionality of HVGs in each subpopulation and marker gene reported previously, EVRCs can be classified into six categories: endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) (FLT1, ELMO1, and IGF1R) [47–49], two subtypes of endothelial cells, namely ECs1 (CDH5 and PECAM1) and ECs2 (PECAM1, COX1, ND3, and ATP6) [26], vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) (ACTA2, PRKG1, PDE1A, PDE3A, and MYH11) [50–54], muscle-associated cells (M-ACs) (MYL1, ACTN2, and ACTN3) [24, 55, 56], and perivascular fibroblasts (PVFs) (FLT4) (Fig. 4a-b and see Additional file 2: Table S11) [57]. While PECAM1 was highly expressed in both endothelial subpopulations, ECs2 uniquely exhibited mitochondrial metabolic characteristics, as demonstrated by GO enrichment analysis of its marker genes (see Additional file 2: Table S12), and specific expression of mitochondrial-related genes, such as COX1, ND3, and ATP6 (Fig. 4b and see Additional file 2: Table S11), suggesting their potential role in mitochondrial energy metabolism. Additionally, we identified an M-ACs subpopulation, which we deduce originates from perivascular muscle-related cells, as GO analysis of marker genes showed significant enrichment in muscle cell development-related processes (see Additional file 2: Table S13).

We next conducted cellular trajectory analysis, revealing that PVFs were unrelated to cell differentiation into EPCs, ECs, or VSMCs (Fig. 4c and d). Comparing DQTPL and DQTPS, both showed transitions from EPCs to other EC subtypes. However, DQTPL exhibited transitions from EPCs to both M-ACs and ECs, along with some transitions to VSMCs, whereas DQTPS displayed transitions to ECs exclusively, with VSMCs completely absent from the EPC/EC differentiation trajectory. These results reflected distinct cellular differentiation states between DQTPL and DQTPS.

The cellular heterogeneity in EVRCs showed no significant differences compared to myogenic lineages or FAPs (Fig. 1e and f). The most notable differences occurred in EC subtypes, with ECs1 and ECs2 predominantly originating from DQTPL and DQTPS, respectively (Figs. 1f and 4e and f, and see Additional file 1: Figure S7). The marker gene CDH5 in ECs1 indicates that this subpopulation represents mature endothelial cells, while ECs2 may represent MT-enrich endothelial cells. These findings suggest a higher proportion of endothelial cells in DQTPS compared to DQTPL, which may enhance oxidative metabolism and mitochondrial function in DQTPS – crucial factors for endurance and hypoxia adaptation. Furthermore, we identified more DEGs in M-ACs than in other subpopulations (see Additional file 2: Table S14), including specifically highly expressed DEGs like GADL1 and IARS1 (see Additional file 1: Figure S8). The GADL1 has been reported as a candidate gene linked to beef quality traits and serves as a hub gene for porcine muscle phenotypes [58, 59], while variations in IARS1 have been associated with growth retardation in humans [60].

Immune cells in the skeletal muscle

Immune cells within muscle tissue significantly influence myogenesis by regulating satellite cell activation, differentiation, and muscle regeneration, playing a crucial role in muscle repair and maintenance. In the analysis of major cell population clustering, immune cells accounted for approximately 5% of the total cell population (Fig. 1e). We clustered immune cells—including myeloid cells and lymphoid cells—into eight subpopulations: proliferating macrophages (pro-MACs) (TOP2A and CDK1), macrophages (MACs) (CSF1R, CD163, and MRC1), dendritic cell-regulatory cells (DCregs) (MERTK, HLA-DRA, CD74, and PDCD1LG2), dendritic cells (DCs) (XCR1, IRF8, and FLT3), mast cells (MCs) (KIT and NFKBIZ), muscle-associated immune cells (M-AICs) (ITGB6, ZBTB16, and MYH1), proliferating NK/T cells (pro-NK/TCs) (KLRK1, CD247, and ITGA4), and NK/T cells (NK/TCs) (PRKCH, IKZF3, and FYN) (Fig. 5a and b, and see Additional file 2: Table S15). Notably, we identified marker genes of a subcluster enriched for muscle cell differentiation-related GO terms (see Additional file 2: Table S16), which we designated as M-AICs.

Our analysis of major cell population clustering revealed no significant differences between DQTPL and DQTPS for either myeloid or lymphoid cells (Fig. 1e). Immune cell subsets similarly showed minimal variation when compared against myocytes and FAPs (Fig. 5c and d). The most pronounced compositional differences occurred in DCregs and MCs, which were predominantly enriched in DQTPS and DQTPL, respectively. Furthermore, there were no significant differences in the subsets of lymphoid cells (pro-NK/TCs and NK/TCs). The most notable cellular heterogeneity was observed in DCregs and MCs, which were predominantly observed in DQTPS and DQTPL, respectively. Additionally, we identified only 45 DEGs across immune cell subpopulations between DQTPL and DQTPS—the lowest DEG count among all examined cell types (myogenic lineages, FAPs, and EVRCs) (see Additional file 2: Table S17). This minimal transcriptional divergence further supports that immune cells maintain the highest degree of conservation between DQTPL and DQTPS relative to other cellular compartments.

The ligand-receptor interaction network across skeletal muscle-resident cell types

Variations in cell type composition may contribute to distinct cellular communication networks. To explore the variations in ligand-receptor networks underlying body size differences between DQTPL and DQTPS, we performed ligand-receptor analysis to identify differentials in cell communications (Fig. 6).

Among major cell populations, FAPs and EVRCs functioned as the principal signal senders and receivers, respectively, exhibiting robust interactions with other cell types. The FAP-EVRC interaction exhibited the greatest strength among all cellular communications (Fig. 6a and b). For the comparison of DQTPL and DQTPS, the FAP-EVRC interaction exhibited the most difference compare to other interacted groups (Fig. 6a and b). Within EVRCs, EC subpopulations demonstrated the highest interaction strength as receivers, with all cellular interactions demonstrating greater intensity in DQTPL compared to DQTPS, indicating enhanced vascular system activity in the former group. Ligand-receptor pathway analysis further confirmed these findings, revealing significantly more enriched interaction pairs in DQTPL (Fig. 6c and d). Among these pathways, COLLAGEN and LAMININ exhibited the highest number of pairs in both DQTPL and DQTPS, with a greater number identified in DQTPL. Additionally, the PTPRM (protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type M) pathway demonstrated the strongest interaction intensity in both groups, with greater intensity observed in DQTPL (Fig. 6e). The EC subpopulation served as the primary receiver in both COLLAGEN and LAMININ pathways. In the COLLAGEN pathway, tenocytes and VA-FAPs were the primary sender subpopulations in DQTPL, while NA-FAPs and interstitial cells served as senders in DQTPS. In the LAMININ pathway, NA-FAPs were the main sender in DQTPL, whereas both NA-FAPs and interstitial cells were the primary senders in DQTPS (Fig. 6f). Additionally, the cell subpopulations in EVRCs functioned not only as receivers, but also as influencers and mediators in DQTPL compared to DQTPS. In the PTPRM pathway, the PTPRM-PTPRM interaction between FAPs and EVRCs was the most enriched ligand-receptor pair in both DQTPL and DQTPS (Fig. 6c and d), with interaction intensity over two-fold greater in DQTPL than in DQTPS (Fig. 6e). PTPRM is a canonical homophilic cell adhesion molecule. Upon engagement, it can transduce signals into the cell that help maintain tissue architecture and homeostasis. The observed strength of the PTPRM-PTPRM interaction observed in DQTPL implied a more robust physical and signaling connection. Within this pathway, EPCs and ECs were identified as the most significant subpopulations in DQTPL and DQTPS, respectively. However, FAPs were more prominent in DQTPS than in DQTPL, serving not only as senders but also as receivers, mediators, and influencers, suggesting complex interaction pathways among these cell subpopulations.

Discussion

Skeletal muscle cellular profiles have been well studied in pigs, little is known about muscle-resident cells relating to porcine body growth due to complex genetic architecture. In this work, we compared data from a Chinese local breed exhibiting body size variation to provide a panorama of adult skeletal muscle cell profiles. Through cell composition comparison, DEGs identification, trajectory, and ligand-receptor interaction analyses, we demonstrated distinct muscle-resident cell profiles between the DQTPL and DQTPS.

Heterogeneity of myocytes

Our findings revealed various cell subpopulations of myocytes (Figs. 1a and 2a). Among these, we identified several subclusters predominantly originating from DQTPL or DQTPS, which exhibited significant heterogeneity in myocyte subpopulations between these groups. These included MYH1+ and MYH4+ fast-twitch muscle fiber subpopulations [23, 61], as well as MYBPC1+ slow-twitch myocytes [62]. We infer that the myocytes with high heterogeneity primarily originated from fast-twitch cell types, given that the fact that porcine LD is predominantly constituted by fast-twitch muscle fibers. Furthermore, cell trajectory analyses demonstrated discrepancies between DQTPL and DQTPS, indicating that myocyte differentiation appears to be more quiescent in DQTPS (Fig. 2d). These results reflect a quiescent stage of myogenic lineage differentiation at maturity, wherein pigs with larger body sizes exhibit some more cellular activity among myocytes. Additionally, we detected DEGs among common cell subclusters that showed a uniform distribution between groups. Notably, DEGs were expressed at higher levels in DQTPL than in DQTPS for some subpopulations, including fast-twitch muscle fibers (ACTA1+ and TNNI2+) [8, 63], NMJ-ACs (MUSK+) [30], and slow-twitch myocytes (MYL2+ and ACTN2+) [8, 64]. However, the number of DEGs in slow-twitch myocytes was the highest compared to other subpopulations. Among them, TECRL has been reported to be involved in the myogenesis of skeletal muscle repair processes [44], while MYOZ2 is recognized as a key protein influencing muscle growth and involved in fiber type regulation [38, 65]. Both genes were identified as the most representative DEGs among these slow myocytes. These results highlight the heterogeneity of myocyte subpopulations and their expression, suggesting that there may be greater gene expression in some certain myogenic lineage cell subtypes and cellular activity related to muscle growth in DQTPL compared to DQTPS.

Differences of FAPs and EVRCs between DQTPL and DQTPS

Among the identified FAP subpopulations, the NA-FAPs, tenocytes, and MT-rich FAPs displayed significant heterogeneity between DQTPL and DQTPS (Fig. 3f). The notable enrichment of NA-FAPs in large pigs indicates a potential role in neuromuscular coordination. Additionally, pseudotime trajectory analysis demonstrated that NA-FAPs represent a terminal differentiation state within the FAP lineage. This finding suggests that NA-FAPs may constitute a functionally mature subpopulation, potentially involved in the regulation or maintenance of neuromuscular functions [66]. The enrichment of terminal cell fate in DQTPL suggests a preferential differentiation trajectory towards NA-FAPs (Fig. 3d), potentially contributing to enhanced muscle development and functional maturation in these pigs. Conversely, we observed a significant enrichment of interstitial FAPs and MT-rich FAP, both identified as terminal states within the pseudotime differentiation trajectory in DQTPS (Fig. 3d). Interstitial FAPs are likely involved in maintaining the homeostasis of the muscle microenvironment [67], suggesting that muscle tissue in small pigs may be experiencing a stress response rather than promoting muscle growth. Meanwhile, MT-rich FAPs, characterized by high mitochondrial content, suggest elevated metabolic activity, and these cell types were identified in a previous study [8]. The enrichment of MT-rich FAPs in DQTPS may indicate a cellular adaptation to increased energetic demands or oxidative stress. Furthermore, in the myocyte-like FAPs, which were also reported as a subpopulation of FAPs previously [8], the number of DEGs in these FAPs was significantly greater than in other cell subpopulations (see Additional file 2: Table S10). This suggests that this subpopulation may represent a critical divergence point underlying the discrepancies in body growth between DQTPL and DQTPS. Notable DEGs, such as MYL1, AMPD1, and FILIP1L, exhibited higher expression levels in DQTPL compared to DQTPS (Fig. 3h-j). Specifically, MYL1 has been reported as a marker gene for fast-twitch myocytes [8, 68], AMPD1 regulation is essential for myocyte differentiation [69], and FILIP1L suppresses FAP proliferation and promotes apoptosis, thereby mitigating fibrosis and fatty infiltration in skeletal muscle [70].

In addition to myogenic lineage cells and FAPs, we identified a significant presence of endothelial and vascular cells in the skeletal muscle tissue (Fig. 1e). This observation may suggest a unique vascular microenvironment that facilitates enhanced nutrient delivery, oxygen exchange, and intercellular communication, potentially contributing to the adaptation of the Diqing Tibetan pig to the hypoxic conditions of the Tibetan Plateau. In the comparison of DQTPL and DQTPS, the proportion of endothelial vascular-related cells (EVRCs) was notably higher in DQTPL (9.71%) than in DQTPS (6.14%) (Fig. 1e). Trajectory analysis revealed a greater transition towards VSMCs observed in DQTPL (Fig. 4d). This may reflect a more coordinated vascular development in larger pigs, potentially enhancing vascular remodeling and perfusion. PVFs appear to be unrelated to the differentiation of ECs, EPCs, and VSMCs as an isolated cell subpopulation (Fig. 1e), suggesting that PVFs may represent a lineage-independent population within the vascular niche, primarily functioning as mediators of vascular stability and homeostasis [71, 72]. Additionally, we observed a significantly higher abundance of MT-rich endothelial cells (EC2) in the DQTPS (Fig. 4f), which may indicate increased mitochondrial activity or oxidative stress within their vascular compartments. Among the subpopulations of endothelial vascular-related cells (EVRCs), we identified a subcluster (M-ACs) associated with muscle development (see Additional file 2: Table S13). We infer that this subpopulation may originate from perivascular muscle-related cells, and further validation of the M-ACs subpopulation is necessary for future research.

MT-rich FAPs and ECs

In our study, we observed a notable enrichment of MT-rich FAPs and ECs within the skeletal muscle tissue of DQTPS. Research indicates that in dystrophic conditions, FAPs exhibit metabolic alterations characterized by impaired mitochondrial oxidative metabolism and a shift towards glycolytic pathways [73]. This metabolic reprogramming leads to increased proliferation and differentiation into adipocytes, contributing to muscle pathology. Conversely, enhancing mitochondrial function in FAPs has been shown to restore their regenerative capacity and support muscle repair. Similarly, endothelial cells rely on mitochondrial function to regulate vascular tone and respond to environmental cues [74]. Mitochondria in endothelial cells act as sensors and integrators of signals, with mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) playing a pivotal role in endothelial signaling pathways. Elevated mitochondrial ROS levels can influence endothelial function, impacting processes such as vasodilation and angiogenesis. The observed increase in MT-rich FAPs and MT-rich ECs in DQTPS may therefore reflect a compensatory mechanism to address the heightened metabolic and regenerative demands associated with their growth retardation. By augmenting mitochondrial content and activity, these cells could enhance energy production and support tissue homeostasis under conditions of metabolic stress.​ This adaptive response aligns with findings in other models of muscle pathology, where metabolic reprogramming of muscle-resident progenitor cells facilitates regeneration and counteracts degenerative processes [75]. Thus, the enrichment of MT-rich cells in the muscle tissue of DQTPS underscores the critical role of mitochondrial adaptations in maintaining muscle function and integrity in response to the hypoxic environment of Tibetan Plateau area. However, the functional consequences and regulatory mechanisms of this enrichment remain unclear, warranting further exploration to elucidate its role in muscle growth and metabolic homeostasis.

Isolated adipocyte population

We identified an isolated mature adipocyte population that exhibited no discernible pseudotime or cellular trajectory in relation to either myogenic lineage cells or fibro/adipogenic progenitors (FAPs) (Figs. 1a and 2d, and 3d). We infer these could be in relation to thermogenesis, as DQTP population inhabits a high-altitude environment characterized by a cold climate. To adapt to these low-temperature conditions, this breed may have evolved thermogenic adipocytes as a key metabolic strategy to maintain body temperature and energy homeostasis. Additionally, these cells may constitute a distinct population of intramuscular adipocytes, which could contribute to both local insulation and energy storage. Related marker genes, such as ADIPOQ, ACSL1, FABP4, and THRSP gene was identified as a biomarker for swine intramuscular fat cells. Notably, the THRSP gene was differentially highly expressed in this cell cluster identified in major cell populations, as well as among myogenic lineage and FAP subpopulations (see Additional file 2: Table S2, S3, and S6). However, the relationship between the identified adipocyte population and intramuscular fat remains unclear. Given the thermogenesis-related gene expression profile of this adipocyte population, it is plausible that these cells contribute to energy metabolism and thermoregulation within muscle tissue. Nevertheless, further investigations are necessary to elucidate their exact functional role in thermoregulation and potential contributions to the observed intramuscular fat content.

Immune cells in skeletal muscle of Tibetan pigs

Muscle-resident immune cells potentially influence myogenesis and muscle regeneration [8]. In this study, we analyzed immune cells in skeletal muscle, which including myeloid and lymphoid cells (Fig. 1a). The proportion of immune cells was found to be 4.81% in DQTPS and 4.68% in DQTPL, which was higher than that reported for other pig breeds in previous studies [8]. This finding may reflect the strong stress resistance of Tibetan pigs to hypoxic and wild environments, as this population was raised in a free-range manner in mountainous areas. However, these figures require further validation with other pig breeds subjected to the same sequencing and analysis protocols. In our study, the immune cell subpopulations were composed of pro-MACs, MAC, DCregs, DCs, MCs, M-AICs, pro-NK/TCs, and NK/TCs (Fig. 5b). Notably, M-AICs, a subcluster of myeloid cells, has been identified in relation to muscle development (see Additional file 2: Table S13). We infer that this subpopulation may exert significant regulatory functions in muscle development and regeneration through interactions with muscle cells and other supporting cells. For instance, macrophages secrete both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory factors that promote the proliferation and differentiation of muscle satellite cells, thereby coordinating the muscle repair process [76]. Additionally, a novel satellite cell subpopulation known as immunomyoblasts, enriched with immune-related genes, has been discovered and may interact with immune cells during muscle regeneration [77]. However, the role of the M-AICs subpopulation as immune cells involved in myogenesis and muscle regeneration requires further validation.

Despite the significant heterogeneity observed in various cell types within the muscle tissues of DQTPL and DQTPS, the composition of immune cells did not exhibit notable differences, and only a limited number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were detected when comparing these two phenotypes (Figs. 1c and 5d and see Additional file 2: Table S14). This finding indicated that muscle-resident immune cells in Diqing Tibetan pigs did not display the same level of heterogeneity as other cell types, suggesting a relatively stable immune cell composition between the two pig phenotypes. This stability may reflect a conserved role of the immune system in muscle tissues, irrespective of body size.

Discrepant ligand-receptor cell interaction

​The observed differences in cell–cell communication networks, specifically between FAPs and EVRCs, highlight potential regulatory mechanisms underlying the phenotypic divergence in body size between DQTPL and DQTPS (Fig. 6a and d). The increased interaction strength and the number of enriched ligand–receptor pairs in DQTPL, particularly within the COLLAGEN, LAMININ, and PTPRM pathways, indicate that FAPs exhibit elevated expression of basal laminar extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins such as COL4A3, COL4A5, and COL4A6, which contribute to ECM remodeling. The laminins are essential for the structural framework of muscle tissue and play a critical role in muscle stem cell (MuSC) quiescence and self-renewal [78]. These results underscored the significance in muscle development and suggested a more active ECM remodeling and vascular signaling environment in DQTPL. Therefore, the interaction between FAPs and EVRCs may have contributed to myogenesis, and the differentiation in interaction intensity between DQTPL and DQTPS could further lead to their discrepancies in body size.

Limitation of this study

A limitation of this study is the small number of skeletal muscle samples sequenced from different groups of DQTP. Consequently, the interpretations of some findings may not be generalizable. However, this research represents the first attempt to analyze cellular expression profiles within the native Tibetan pig breed with discrepant body sizes in a hypoxic plateau. In the future, functional validation of target genes will be conducted, with the initial aim of performing validation on cellular levels.

Conclusions

The results of this manuscript lead to several conclusions: (1) Muscle cells in DQTP can be categorized into myogenic lineages (myocytes and satellite cells), FAPs, EVRCs, and immune cells (myeloid and lymphoid); (2) Significant subtype-level differences exist between DQTPL and DQTPS pigs, including higher abundance of MT-rich FAPs and MT-rich ECs in DQTPS and elevated expression of muscle growth related genes in DQTPL; (3) DQTPL exhibits stronger intercellular communication, specifically through COLLAGEN, LAMININ, and PTPRM signaling between FAPs and EVRCs, potentially promoting muscle growth. Collectively, our study reveals cellular, molecular, and interaction-level differences underlying muscle development and body size variation in DQTP pigs.
