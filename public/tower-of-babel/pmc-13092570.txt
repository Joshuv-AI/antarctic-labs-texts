The regulatory effects of solid-state fermented Rosa roxburghii Tratt. pomace on serum metabolism and intestinal microbiota in rats
Authors: Xiaoli Zhou, Yanli Wang, ShengYan Zhang, Bin Du, Lingshuai Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the in vivo physiological effects of solid-state fermented (SSF) Rosa roxburghii Tratt. pomace (F-RRTP) on host metabolism and gut microbiota. Rats were fed diets supplemented with 1% (w/w, total diet basis) unfermented RRTP or F-RRTP. Compared with unfermented RRTP, F-RRTP showed a relative increase in serum HDL-C and improved antioxidant-related parameters, as indicated by higher serum SOD and hepatic T-AOC activities. Gut microbiota analysis showed that F-RRTP altered the microbial community structure, including a reduced Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio and a lower relative abundance of Proteobacteria, a taxon commonly associated with gut dysbiosis. Notably, F-RRTP enriched putative butyrate-producing genera, including Ruminococcus and Allobaculum, as well as Bifidobacterium, and this was accompanied by a higher fecal butyrate level. Serum metabolomics further revealed distinct metabolic alterations in the F-RRTP group, including changes in flavonoid-related metabolites, tryptophan metabolites such as serotonin and indole-3-lactic acid, and several metabolites associated with antioxidant status. It suggested coordinated associations among gut microbiota, serum metabolites, and host physiological indices, implying that fermentation-induced changes in pomace properties may contribute to the observed effects. Collectively, these findings indicate that SSF may enhance the functional potential of RRTP and support its further development as a value-added food or feed ingredient with potential metabolic health benefits.

Introduction

In the context of the global circular economy, the valorization of food processing by-products represents not only an urgent environmental imperative but also a strategic avenue for developing sustainable food systems. Industrial side-streams constitute a major global environmental challenge, with solid waste production expected to rise from 2.24 billion tons in 2020 to 3.88 billion tons by 2050 (Piwowarek et al., 2025). Fruit and vegetable processing residues, particularly pomace generated from juice extraction, are produced in massive quantities—accounting for 35–60% of the raw material—yet remain severely underutilized (Piwowarek et al., 2025; Torres-León et al., 2019). Despite being discarded, these residues are essentially nutrient-dense matrices rich in dietary fiber (DF), bioactive polyphenols, carotenoids, and minerals (Dulf et al., 2015; Martínez-Martí et al., 2026). Existing literature has well-documented that DF derived from sources like citrus, apple, kiwi fruit and mango pomace possesses significant functional properties, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and lipid-lowering activities, which can mitigate the risks of cardiovascular diseases and type 2 diabetes (Calvete-Torre et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2026; Martínez-Martí et al., 2026; Torres-León et al., 2019). Consequently, transforming these “waste” materials into functional food ingredients offers a dual benefit: alleviating environmental burdens and creating economic value for the agri-food sector (Blejan et al., 2023).

Rosa roxburghii Tratt. (RRT), a unique fruit indigenous to Southwest China renowned for its exceptional antioxidant capacity, generates substantial amounts of by-products during processing. Industrially, RRT is primarily processed into juice via crushing pressing. This process, however, is inefficient in terms of material utilization, as approximately 35–50% of the initial fruit mass remains as pomace. In Guizhou province alone, the annual processing of RRT generates over 100,000 tons of pomace, creating a substantial environmental burden and resource waste. Despite being discarded, this byproduct retains considerable nutritional value, being rich in crude fiber, proteins, minerals (e.g., calcium, phosphorus), and bioactive compounds such as Vitamin C and polyphenols (Li & Jiang, 2016; Zhang et al., 2025). However, the therapeutic potential of RRT pomace is severely constrained by the low bioaccessibility of its phytochemicals. A major proportion of phenolic compounds exists in a “bound” form, covalently linked to the recalcitrant, insoluble cell wall matrix (Zhang et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025). Our previous study demonstrated that the removal of these bound polyphenols (BPs) significantly compromised the functional properties of the DF, including its water/oil-holding capacities and antioxidant activity (Zhou et al., 2025). This structural entrapment hinders the release of polyphenols in the upper gastrointestinal tract, thereby limiting their systemic bioavailability. Current research has predominantly focused on fragmented approaches—either simple extraction or isolated structural modification—often overlooking the synergistic interplay between the fiber matrix and polyphenols, which is critical for remodeling the intestinal microenvironment (Wang et al., 2023). Therefore, unlocking the full health potential of RRT pomace requires a strategy that effectively disrupts the cell wall matrix to liberate these BPs while preserving the functional integrity of the fiber.

Solid-state fermentation (SSF) has emerged as an effective, environmentally friendly bioprocessing strategy to overcome these barriers. By leveraging enzymes secreted by microorganisms (e.g., β-glucosidase, cellulase), SSF can degrade dense cell wall structures and cleave bonds linking polyphenols to the fiber matrix, thereby promoting the conversion of bound forms into more accessible free forms (Ajila et al., 2012; Torres-León et al., 2019). This process not only enhances the release of bioactive compounds but also improves the nutritional profile by increasing certain amino acids and vitamins (Saharan et al., 2020). Previous studies have shown that SSF significantly enhances the antioxidant and hypoglycemic activities of DFs-rich materials, such as kiwi residues and cereal brans, partly through the release of bound phenolics (Liu et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2021). Building on this, we established an optimized SSF system for RRT pomace using Trichoderma viride, and showed that this process remodeled the cell wall matrix and increased total phenolic content by 31.28%, with notable enrichment of rutin, myricetin, 6,7-dimethoxycoumarin, and other phenolic compounds (Wang et al., 2024). These in vitro findings demonstrated the potential of SSF to bioactivate RRT pomace.

However, a critical knowledge gap remains: improved chemical composition in vitro does not automatically translate to physiological efficacy in vivo. To date, research on RRT pomace has mainly focused on solvent extracts or in vitro digestion models, while the biological effects of the whole fermented pomace matrix remain insufficiently understood. In particular, it is still unclear whether SSF-induced modifications in the fiber matrix and phenolic profile can modulate the gut microbiota and thereby influence host metabolic and antioxidant status in vivo. To our knowledge, no previous study has systematically evaluated the in vivo effects of whole SSF-treated RRT pomace in relation to gut microbiota and host physiology.

To bridge the gap between in vitro bioprocessing and in vivo functionality, this study aimed to evaluate the biological activity of F-RRTP in a rat model and to explore a potential “processing–microbiota–host” framework. By integrating serum biochemical and antioxidant indices with 16S rRNA gene sequencing, short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) analysis, and serum metabolomics, we sought to: (1) determine whether SSF enhances the microbiota-modulating potential of RRT pomace; and (2) examine whether these microbial changes are associated with improvements in host metabolism, antioxidant defense, and systemic homeostasis. This study provides new in vivo evidence supporting the value-added utilization of RRT pomace as a functional food ingredient.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Rosa roxburghii Tratt pomace (RRTP) was obtained from Longli County, Guizhou Province, China and processed into powder form before use. Fermented RRT pomace (F-RRTP) was prepared from the same batch of RRTP by SSF using Trichoderma viride according to the optimized method described in our previous study (Wang et al., 2024). Briefly, the moisture content of the substrate was adjusted to 50% with distilled water before sterilization. RRTP was used as the fermentation substrate and inoculated with T. viride (inoculum size: 10%, w/w) in the presence of 1% glucose and 1% yeast extract, followed by incubation at 28 °C for 3 days. After preparation, both RRTP and F-RRTP were sealed and stored at −20 °C until use. Detailed structural and compositional changes of RRT pomace before and after SSF under the same processing conditions were reported in our previous study (Wang et al., 2024). The present study focuses on the in vivo physiological effects of dietary supplementation with RRTP and F-RRTP.

Sodium hydroxide, hydrochloric acid, sodium bicarbonate, potassium dihydrogen phosphate, dipotassium hydrogen phosphate, and acetone were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All chemicals were of analytical grade. SCFA standards, including acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, and isovaleric acid, were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Animal experiment design and sample collection

All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the Chinese national standards Guidelines for Ethical Review of Animal Welfare (GB/T 35892-2018) and Laboratory Animals—Requirements for Environment and Housing Facilities (GB 14925-2022). The study protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guizhou Medical University (SYXK (Gui) 2023-0002). Twenty-four male Sprague–Dawley rats (180–220 g) were obtained from Changsha Tianqin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (SCXK (Xiang) 2022-0011). All rats were acclimatized for one week before the experiment under controlled conditions (22 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 10% relative humidity, and a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle).

After acclimatization, the rats were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 8 per group): control group (CK), RRTP group, and F-RRTP group. Each rat received 30.0 g diet per day throughout the intervention period. The CK group was fed the basal diet, while the RRTP and F-RRTP groups were fed experimental diets containing 1% (w/w) RRTP or 1% (w/w) F-RRTP, respectively. The basal diet was provided in pellet form, whereas RRTP and F-RRTP were powder samples. Therefore, RRTP and F-RRTP were thoroughly premixed with the basal diet before feeding to ensure a more homogeneous diet and more uniform intake by the rats. The final diets used for the three groups had the same moisture content after preparation. The 1% (w/w) supplementation level was selected as a moderate and nutritionally feasible dose for comparing the biological effects of RRTP and F-RRTP under the same dietary background while minimizing potential interference with feed palatability and nutrient balance. The intervention lasted for 14 days. Body weight was recorded weekly, and no significant differences were observed among the groups.

At the end of the experiment, fresh fecal samples were collected, and the rats were then fasted for 12 h and anesthetized. Blood samples were collected from the heart, and the animals were euthanized by exsanguination. Liver tissues were immediately excised, rinsed with normal saline, weighed, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for further analysis. The blood samples were transferred into plain tubes, allowed to clot on ice for 10 min, and then centrifuged at 1000 ×g for 10 min to obtain serum. The serum was transferred into clean tubes and stored at −20 °C until analysis.

Serum biochemical and antioxidant indicators

Serum levels of total protein (TP), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL—C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were measured using an automatic biochemical analyzer (GF2280, China).

The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), and the concentration of malondialdehyde (MDA) in both serum and liver homogenates were determined using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). In short, liver samples were mixed with ice-cold PBS or homogenate (1:9, (w:v)) and the mixtures were triturated in a low temperature homogenate for tissue homogenate. After centrifugation (4000 ×g, 20 min) of the tissue homogenate, the supernatant was collected for the measurement antioxidant indexes and protein content according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Analysis of Fecal SCFAs

Fecal SCFAs produced in vivo were determined based on the previous method (Zheng, Zhao, et al., 2024). The rat fecal samples were mixed with ultrapure water in a ratio of 1: 6 (g:mL). Then the mixture was shocked intermittently on a vortex mixer for 2 min and executed with ultrasonic-processing continuously for 5 min. The processes of the vortex and ultrasound were repeated once. The supernatant was collected after centrifuging (2025 ×g, 10 min) at 4 °C, and then analyzed by gas chromatography (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). For chromatographic analysis, supernatants were acidified with 25% metaphosphoric acid at a 1:0.25 (v:v) ratio. Analytical procedures employed an Agilent 6890 GC-FID system equipped with a 1.82 m × 0.2 mm SP-1200/H₃PO₄-coated Chromosorb W-AW column (80/100 mesh, HP Inc., USA). Following 0.22-μm polysulfone membrane filtration, samples and SCFA standards (acetic/propionic/butyric/isovaleric acids) were subjected to temperature-programmed separation involving an initial hold at 100 °C for 3 min, ramping to 170 °C at 40 °C/min, and a final hold (1 min) with a 1 mL injection volume. The correlation coefficients (R2) for acetic, propionic, butyric, and isovaleric acids were 0.9998, 0.9996, 0.9994, and 0.9998, respectively.

Gut microbiota analysis

Total microbial genomic DNA was extracted from fecal samples using the MagBeads FastDNA Kit (MP Biomedicals, CA, USA). DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and DNA integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3–V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primer pair 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). A barcode sequence was added to the forward primer to distinguish different samples within the same library. PCR amplification was performed in a 25 μL reaction system containing 0.25 μL ABclonal DNA polymerase, 5 μL 5× Reaction Buffer, 5 μL 5× High GC Buffer, 2 μL dNTPs (10 mM), 2 μL template DNA, 1 μL forward primer (10 μM), 1 μL reverse primer (10 μM), and 8.75 μL nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 5 min; 26 cycles of 98 °C for 30 s, 52 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min and holding at 12 °C. PCR products were examined by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, purified using magnetic beads, and quantified using the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, USA).

Sequencing libraries were constructed using the TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Prep Kit (Illumina, USA), quality-checked using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in paired-end 2 × 250 bp mode. Raw reads were processed in QIIME2 (version 2022.11). Briefly, sequences were demultiplexed using the demux plugin, primer sequences were trimmed using cutadapt, and sequence quality control, denoising, paired-end merging, and chimera removal were conducted using the DADA2 plugin. This workflow generated amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and the corresponding abundance table. Taxonomic annotation was performed against the Greengenes database (version 13.8) using the classify-sklearn method with a pre-trained Naive Bayes classifier. Relative abundance profiles at different taxonomic levels were generated for community composition analysis.

To minimize the influence of uneven sequencing depth, the ASV abundance table was rarefied in QIIME2 using the qiime feature-table rarefy function, with the rarefaction depth set to 95% of the sequencing depth of the sample with the lowest read count. Alpha diversity indices, including Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson, were calculated based on the rarefied ASV table. In the QIIME2 output used in this study, a higher Simpson index indicates higher community diversity. Venn diagrams were generated based on the presence or absence of ASVs among groups. Species accumulation curves were generated in R using the vegan package with the specaccum function based on the ASV abundance matrix to evaluate whether the sampling effort was sufficient. Rank-abundance curves were generated in R using the ggplot2 package. A heatmap at the genus level was generated in R to visualize differences in microbial composition among groups. Differentially abundant taxa were identified using LEfSe with thresholds of LDA > 2.0 and P < 0.05. Functional profiles of the gut microbiota were predicted using PICRUSt2 based on the KEGG database, and pathway differences were analyzed at KEGG level 2.

Serum metabolomics analysis

Serum metabolomics analysis was performed by Shanghai BioProf Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Serum samples were thawed at 4 °C, and 100 μL of each sample was mixed with 400 μL methanol. After vortexing, the mixture was sonicated in an ice-water bath for 20 min, incubated at −20 °C for 1 h, and centrifuged at 16,000 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and dried using a vacuum concentrator. The dried extract was reconstituted in 100 μL methanol/water (1:1, v/v), centrifuged at 20,000 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was used for analysis.

Metabolite separation was performed on a SHIMADZU LC-30 UHPLC system equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC® HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). Samples were kept at 4 °C in the autosampler during analysis. The injection volume was 4 μL, the column temperature was 40 °C, and the flow rate was 0.3 mL/min. The mobile phases were 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B). The gradient was as follows: 0–2 min, 0% B; 2–6 min, 0–48% B; 6–10 min, 48–100% B; 10–12 min, 100% B; 12–12.1 min, 100–0% B; and 12.1–15 min, 0% B.

Mass spectrometry was performed on a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) with a HESI source in both positive and negative ion modes. The spray voltage was set at 3.8 kV (+) and 3.2 kV (−), the capillary temperature at 320 °C, the sheath gas at 30 arb, the auxiliary gas at 5 arb, the probe heater temperature at 350 °C, and the S-Lens RF level at 50. Full-scan MS data were acquired over an m/z range of 70–1050 at a resolution of 70,000, followed by data-dependent MS/MS acquisition of the top 10 precursor ions at a resolution of 17,500. HCD fragmentation was applied with stepped normalized collision energies of 20, 30, and 40. Raw data were processed for peak alignment, extraction, and normalization. OPLS-DA variable importance for the projection (VIP) > 1 and p < 0.05 were used as significant difference cutoffs in metabolite screening criteria. Differential metabolites were identified by comparing with standard databases, and pathway enrichment analysis was performed.

Statistical analysis

All chemical and biochemical analyses were performed in triplicate (n = 3). Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance among groups was determined by one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan's test using IBM SPSS Statistics 22.0. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Effects of SSF RRT pomace on serum biochemical indicators in rats

Serum biochemical parameters serve as important indicators of the host's physiological status and are widely used to evaluate the efficacy and safety of nutritional interventions (Sun et al., 2023). In this study, markers related to lipid and protein metabolism were analyzed to assess the physiological effects of RRTP and F-RRTP.

As shown in Fig. 1, dietary supplementation with RRTP or F-RRTP did not significantly alter serum TC, TG, or LDL-C levels compared with the CK group (P > 0.05), suggesting that neither unfermented nor fermented RRT pomace disrupted basal lipid homeostasis under the present experimental conditions. A significant difference was observed only in HDL-C between the RRTP and F-RRTP groups. Specifically, the F-RRTP group showed significantly higher serum HDL-C levels (0.91 ± 0.10 mmol/L) than the unfermented RRTP group (0.77 ± 0.10 mmol/L, P < 0.05). However, neither the RRTP group nor the F-RRTP group differed significantly from the CK group (0.79 ± 0.06 mmol/L). Rather than indicating a definitive improvement in systemic lipoprotein metabolism, the data suggest that SSF may alleviate the slight reduction in HDL-C observed in the unfermented RRTP group and thus confer a relative advantage over the raw pomace. This relative difference may be associated with fermentation-induced changes in matrix structure and/or the release of bioactive compounds during SSF. Our previous study showed that SSF promoted the release and transformation of phenolic compounds in RRT pomace (Wang et al., 2024), and phenolic constituents have been reported to participate in lipid metabolic regulation (Gerwyn et al., 2021; Zheng, Zhao, et al., 2024). As the HDL-C level in the F-RRTP group was not significantly different from that in the CK group, any potential benefit of SSF on HDL-C should be considered preliminary and interpreted as a relative improvement over unfermented RRTP rather than as robust evidence of lipid-lowering or cardioprotective activity.Fig. 1Effects of F-RRTP and RRTP on serum biochemical indicators in rats. Regarding protein metabolism, the RRTP group showed significantly lower serum TP levels than the CK group (P < 0.05), whereas the F-RRTP group (62.50 ± 3.22 g/L) was statistically comparable to the CK group (65.02 ± 3.20 g/L, P > 0.05). Since RRTP and F-RRTP were added to the basal diet at only 1% (w/w), their contribution to total dietary protein intake was limited. Moreover, Rosa roxburghii pomace contains relatively low crude protein but is rich in DF, especially insoluble DF. Therefore, the lower serum TP level observed in the RRTP group is unlikely to be explained solely by differences in protein intake. A possible explanation is that the dense and highly insoluble fiber matrix of raw pomace may physically entrap nutrients or hinder the access of digestive enzymes to dietary substrates, thereby limiting nutrient accessibility and protein utilization to some extent. The improved serum TP profile in the F-RRTP group may be attributed to fermentation-induced structural modification of the pomace matrix. During SSF, enzymes secreted by Trichoderma viride, such as cellulase and hemicellulase, may partially degrade the compact DF network and weaken the cross-linked structure of the cell wall matrix (Wang et al., 2024). Such structural loosening may reduce nutrient encapsulation and improve the accessibility of dietary components to digestive enzymes. This interpretation is also consistent with our previous observation that cellulose content decreased during fermentation, accompanied by a relative increase in lignin proportion, indicating alteration of the structural carbohydrate matrix. Therefore, the restoration of serum TP in the F-RRTP group may reflect an attenuation of the unfavorable effect of the raw pomace matrix on protein utilization. However, because protein digestibility and absorption were not directly measured in the present study, this interpretation should be considered preliminary.

Overall, SSF appeared to improve the nutritional compatibility of RRT pomace, particularly by attenuating the negative impact of the raw high-fiber matrix on protein utilization. While the fermented product also showed a modest relative advantage over unfermented RRTP in terms of HDL—C, the present data do not support a strong conclusion regarding systemic improvement in lipoprotein metabolism. These findings suggest that F-RRTP is a safe dietary ingredient with potential to improve the nutritional functionality of RRT pomace.

Effects of SSF RRT pomace on antioxidant status in rats

Systemic antioxidant homeostasis is important for maintaining metabolic health. In this study, the in vivo antioxidant effects of RRT pomace were evaluated by measuring key indicators, including SOD activity as an enzymatic antioxidant marker, T-AOC as an indicator of overall antioxidant capacity, and MDA as a marker of lipid peroxidation. Considering the central role of the liver in metabolism and detoxification, hepatic antioxidant status was assessed together with serum parameters to provide an integrated view of the oxidative response.

As shown in Fig. 2A, SSF significantly enhanced systemic enzymatic antioxidant defense. The F-RRTP group exhibited significantly higher serum SOD activity than both the RRTP and CK groups (P < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was observed between the latter two groups. SOD acts as a first-line antioxidant enzyme by catalyzing the dismutation of superoxide anions (Sun et al., 2023). The increase observed specifically in the F-RRTP group suggests that fermentation-induced modification of the pomace matrix may be important for improving the accessibility or activity of antioxidant-related components. In contrast, unfermented RRTP did not produce a comparable effect, implying that the bioactive constituents in raw pomace may remain partially constrained within the intact fiber structure.Fig. 2Effects of F-RRTP and RRTP on antioxidant capacity in rats. In the liver, a different but complementary pattern was observed (Fig. 2B). Hepatic T-AOC was highest in the F-RRTP group and was significantly greater than that in both the RRTP and CK groups (P < 0.05). This result suggests that fermented pomace may improve the hepatic antioxidant reserve under the present experimental conditions. Given that the liver is a major metabolic organ and is vulnerable to oxidative challenge, the increase in hepatic T-AOC may reflect improved antioxidant preparedness in response to daily metabolic activity.

The superior antioxidant performance of F-RRTP may be related, at least in part, to fermentation-induced release of BPs from the pomace matrix. Previous studies have shown that DF possesses certain antioxidant properties, but its effects may be limited when bioactive compounds are tightly embedded within a dense structural network (Liu et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2025). Our in vitro results indicated that SSF driven by Trichoderma viride can disrupt the linkage between polyphenols and the fiber matrix, thereby increasing the proportion of more accessible FPs, such as rutin and myricetin (Wang et al., 2024). These released compounds may contribute to antioxidant regulation through multiple pathways in vivo. On the one hand, free polyphenols can directly scavenge reactive species and may also participate in signaling pathways involved in endogenous antioxidant regulation (Lin et al., 2013). These fermentation-induced changes may partly explain the elevated serum SOD activity observed in the F-RRTP group. On the other hand, fermentation-related changes in fiber structure and polyphenol availability may also interact with the gut microbiota and generate metabolites that indirectly support hepatic antioxidant capacity. However, this possibility was not directly verified in the present study and requires further investigation.

It should be noted that the responses of antioxidant defense markers and lipid peroxidation markers were not entirely parallel. Although F-RRTP significantly increased serum SOD activity and hepatic T-AOC activity, no significant changes were observed in serum or liver MDA levels. This may be because SOD and T-AOC primarily reflect antioxidant defense capacity, whereas MDA is a downstream marker of oxidative damage. Since the rats were healthy animals under basal physiological conditions rather than an induced oxidative stress model, baseline lipid peroxidation was likely low and stable, and thus improved antioxidant defense may not necessarily lead to a measurable reduction in MDA. Moreover, while elevated SOD activity can sometimes indicate a compensatory response to oxidative stress, the simultaneous increase in hepatic T-AOC and the absence of increased MDA in the F-RRTP group suggest that the observed SOD elevation more likely reflects enhanced antioxidant defense. Overall, these findings indicate that SSF improved the antioxidant-related functionality of RRTP. By increasing the accessibility of antioxidant-active components in the pomace matrix, F-RRTP enhanced serum SOD activity and hepatic T-AOC under the present conditions. Together with the observed changes in gut microbiota and other metabolic indicators, these results support the view that SSF may improve the physiological compatibility and functional potential of RRTP.

Effects of SSF RRT pomace on Fecal SCFA profiles in rats

SCFAs are major end-products of microbial fermentation of DF and other indigestible substrates and play important roles in intestinal homeostasis and host metabolism (Le et al., 2024; Zheng, Zhang, et al., 2021). Based on the observed differences in metabolic and antioxidant-related parameters, we further examined whether RRTP and F-RRTP differentially affected gut microbial fermentation, as reflected by fecal SCFA profiles.

As shown in Fig. 3, both RRTP and F-RRTP interventions significantly increased total fecal SCFA levels compared with the CK group (P < 0.05), suggesting that RRTP can be utilized by the gut microbiota and may promote overall microbial fermentation activity. Notably, the F-RRTP group exhibited significantly higher total SCFA levels than the RRTP group (P < 0.05). This difference may be related, at least in part, to fermentation-induced changes in pomace structure and composition. In our previous study using the same material source and SSF process, fungal fermentation disrupted ompact cell wall structure, altered fiber–phenolic interactions, and remodeled the pomace matrix into a more accessible substrate, while maintaining the cellulose I crystalline type (Wang et al., 2024). These changes may have increased microbial accessibility to fermentable components and facilitated the utilization of matrix-associated bioactive compounds, thereby contributing to the higher SCFA output observed in the F-RRTP group.Fig. 3Effects of RRTP and F-RRTP on content of SCFAs in rats fecal. Beyond the increase in total SCFAs, a notable compositional difference was observed. While acetate and propionate levels did not differ significantly between the RRTP and F-RRTP groups (P > 0.05), fecal butyrate was significantly higher in the F-RRTP group (P < 0.05). This selective increase suggests that SSF may influence microbial fermentation in a more specific manner than unfermented RRTP. Because butyrate is produced by a more limited range of microbial taxa and metabolic pathways than acetate or propionate (Ashaolu et al., 2020; Deng et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021), the higher butyrate level in the F-RRTP group may reflect more specific shifts in microbial composition or activity. One possible explanation is that fermentation-induced changes in the pomace matrix and phenolic availability may favor butyrate-associated microbial functions; this possibility was further examined through gut microbiota analysis (Maiuolo et al., 2024). The higher butyrate level in the F-RRTP group may be relevant to the physiological differences observed between RRTP and F-RRTP, given the known roles of butyrate in maintaining intestinal barrier function and modulating host metabolism and oxidative status (Li et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2023; Zheng, Zhang, et al., 2021; Sun and O'Riordan, 2013).

Overall, these results indicate that SSF may enhance the fermentability of RRT pomace and alter the fecal SCFA profile, particularly by increasing butyrate. This change may be one of the factors contributing to the distinct physiological effects observed for F-RRTP.

Effects of SSF RRT pomace on gut microbiota diversity in rats

The gut microbiota is an important mediator between dietary components and host metabolic responses. Given the observed differences in fecal SCFA profiles and physiological indicators, we further examined the effects of RRTP and F-RRTP on the intestinal microbial community in terms of diversity, taxonomic composition, and predicted functional potential.

Changes in microbial community diversity

Ecological diversity indices provide an overall view of microbial community structure. As shown in Fig. 4, the limited overlap in ASVs among groups suggests that RRT pomace intervention may have reshaped microbial community composition. Both RRTP and F-RRTP groups exhibited significantly lower species richness (Chao index) than the CK group. Rather than necessarily indicating an unfavorable loss of diversity, this reduction likely reflects dietary selection pressure imposed by the pomace matrix. The DF and polyphenols in the pomace may favorthe growth of microorganisms adapted to these substrates, while reducing the relative representation of other taxa (Huang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2019). Within this selective environment, the F-RRTP group maintained a significantly higher number of observed ASVs than the RRTP group. This difference may be related to fermentation-induced changes in substrate structure and composition, which could provide a broader range of accessible niches for intestinal microbes. As reported in our previous study, SSF altered the structural characteristics and phenolic fractions of RRT pomace (Wang et al., 2024). In addition, the higher Simpson index observed in both pomace groups suggests greater community evenness, which may reflect a more balanced microbial structure (Wang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2019). Changes in key microbial taxa

Taxonomic profiling (Fig. 5, Fig. 6) revealed microbial shifts that align with the observed physiological improvements. All groups were dominated by Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria—consistent with the composition of healthy mammalian gut microbiota (Yang et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2024). At the phylum level, both pomace interventions reduced the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio, a classic marker of improved metabolic health often associated with reduced lipid accumulation and inflammation (Ge et al., 2022). Crucially, F-RRTP specifically suppressed Proteobacteria abundance compared to the RRTP group (P < 0.05). Given that the Proteobacteria phylum encompasses numerous opportunistic pathogens linked to oxidative stress and barrier dysfunction (Ashaolu et al., 2020; Mukherjee et al., 2020; Pham et al., 2017), its suppression underscores the superior safety profile of the fermented product. This effect is likely mediated by the enhanced antimicrobial activity of fermentation-released free polyphenols (e.g., rutin, myricetin). Mechanistically, these bioactive compounds exert a dual inhibitory effect: first, they disrupt the cell membrane integrity of Gram-negative bacteria (including many Proteobacteria), increasing permeability and causing intracellular leakage (Calvete-Torre et al., 2024; Pham et al., 2017); second, their abundant hydroxyl groups can chelate essential metal ions, particularly iron. Differences in predicted pathway abundance among groups may reflect functional consequences of the microbial shifts induced by RRTP and F-RRTP. For example, the enrichment of genera such as Ruminococcus and Prevotella in the F-RRTP group is consistent with a greater predicted potential for carbohydrate metabolism. Their activity likely drives key processes like glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid cycle, facilitating the conversion of pomace fiber into energy substrates and SCFAs. The observed upregulation in the Metabolism of cofactors and vitamins pathway suggests an enhanced microbial capacity for synthesizing essential compounds, including vitamins B and antioxidants. This microbial production may act synergistically with dietary polyphenols to collectively bolster the host's systemic antioxidant status. The activation of the Metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides pathway highlights an increase in microbial secondary metabolism. The bioactive compounds produced through these pathways likely contribute to the maintenance of intestinal homeostasis, potentially explaining the observed suppression of opportunistic Proteobacteria in the F-RRTP group.

These results indicate that SSF transforms RRT pomace from a simple fiber source into a prebiotic modulator. It reshapes the gut microbiota via a “Selective Enrichment - Pathogen Exclusion” mechanism. By fostering a guild of butyrate-producing and antioxidant-related bacteria (Ruminococcus, Bifidobacterium) and suppressing pro-inflammatory taxa (Proteobacteria), F-RRTP establishes a microbial foundation that supports the systemic lipid-lowering and antioxidant benefits observed in the host. This multi-level optimization validates SSF as a potent strategy to upgrade the functional value of fruit by-products.

Correlation analysis of gut microbiota and serum metabolites in rats

To verify the functional coupling between gut microbiota remodeling and the metabolic improvements induced by F-RRTP, Spearman's rank correlation analysis was performed. The resulting heatmap (Fig.12) reveals a multi-dimensional regulatory network driven by the fermentation-optimized microbiome. A primary cluster of beneficial genera enriched in the F-RRTP group—particularly Bifidobacterium and Ruminococcus—exhibited significant positive correlations (P < 0.05) with Sulforaphane, Serotonin, and Perlolyrine. This specific co-occurrence provides molecular evidence for the biotransformation hypothesis of SSF. It suggests that the enrichment of these functional bacteria likely facilitates the release of BPs and their conversion into bioactive metabolites. The analysis further highlighted the role of fiber-degrading bacteria (e.g., Prevotella, Ruminococcus) in lipid regulation. These genera exhibited positive correlations with membrane lipids (e.g., phosphatidylethanolamine) and negative correlations with pro-inflammatory free fatty acids (e.g., 9,12-Octadecadienoic acid). Notably, these SCFA-producing genera showed a negative correlation with serum butyrate, despite the high fecal butyrate levels in the F-RRTP group, which is rapidly consumed by colonocytes for energy or transported to the liver via the portal vein to regulate hepatic lipid metabolism (gut-liver axis), rather than accumulating in the systemic circulation. This efficient metabolic turnover suppresses the accumulation of pro-inflammatory free fatty acids and promotes phospholipid synthesis (essential for HDL assembly), thereby mechanistically explaining the elevated HDL-C levels observed in the F-RRTP group.

In contrast, genera associated with the non-fermented or control groups (e.g., Morganella, Dorea) displayed an inverse metabolic profile. These opportunistic pathogens showed positive correlations with serum butyrate and pro-inflammatory lipids, but negative correlations with the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant metabolites enriched in the F-RRTP group. This suggests that the health benefits of F-RRTP derive not only from the promotion of beneficial bacteria but also from the competitive exclusion of potential pathogens. The suppression of Morganella and Dorea in the F-RRTP group reduces the production of metabolic stressors, thereby lowering systemic inflammatory risks and optimizing overall metabolic homeostasis.

The correlation analysis confirms that SSF establishes a synergistic regulatory network. By promoting beneficial genera that positively correlate with phospholipids, antioxidants, and signaling molecules while negatively correlating with pro-inflammatory markers, F-RRTP exerts a multi-targeted effect. This integrates the findings from lipid metabolism, antioxidant status, and microbiota composition, confirming that the “Fermentation-Polyphenol-Microbiota” axis is the core driver of the observed health benefits in rats.

While this study provides evidence for the beneficial effects of F-RRTP in healthy rats, certain limitations warrant consideration. The present work was performed in a normolipidemic model and therefore primarily reflects the physiological regulatory effects of F-RRTP under normal conditions. Whether these benefits can be maintained or amplified under metabolically stressed conditions remains to be determined. Given the observed increases in HDL-C and butyrate, as well as changes in lipid-associated microbial taxa such as Ruminococcus and Allobaculum, future studies should evaluate F-RRTP in established models of metabolic dysregulation, including high-fat diet-induced obesity and hyperlipidemia. This would help clarify its efficacy in improving features of metabolic imbalance and further support its potential development as a functional food ingredient.

Conclusion

This study suggests that SSF may be an effective approach to improve the utilization value of Rosa roxburghii Tratt. pomace. Compared with unfermented RRTP, F-RRTP was associated with a relative increase in serum HDL-C and higher antioxidant-related indices, including serum SOD and hepatic T-AOC. F-RRTP also altered gut microbiota composition, as reflected by a lower relative abundance of Proteobacteria, enrichment of several beneficial genera such as Ruminococcus, Allobaculum, and Bifidobacterium, and a higher fecal butyrate level. In addition, serum metabolomics revealed distinct changes in lipid-related, flavonoid-related, and tryptophan-related metabolites in the F-RRTP group. Together, these findings suggest coordinated associations among fermentation-induced changes in pomace properties, gut microbiota remodeling, and host metabolic responses. Collectively, this work provides in vivo evidence that SSF upgrades RRT pomace from a simple DF source to a targeted, microbiota-modulating functional ingredient. The findings validate SSF as a sustainable strategy for the high-value conversion of fruit processing by-products into ingredients that support metabolic health, antioxidant status, and gut homeostasis.
