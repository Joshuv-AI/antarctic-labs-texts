I’ve Seen This IP: A Practical Intersection Attack Against Tor Introduction Circuits and Hidden Services
Nicolas Constantinides

Abstract
Tor onion services rely on long-lived introduction circuits to support anonymous rendezvous between clients and services. Although Tor includes some defenses against traffic analysis, the introduction protocol retains deterministic routing structure that can be leveraged by an adversary. We describe a practical intersection attack on Tor introduction circuits that can, over repeated interactions, identify each hop from the introduction point toward the onion service while requiring observation at only one relay per stage. The attack issues repeated probes and intersects destination IP address sets observed within narrowly defined INTRODUCE1 – RENDEZVOUS2 time windows, without assuming global visibility or access to packet payloads. We evaluate feasibility with live-network experiments using a self-operated onion service and relays, and we follow data-minimization and ethical safeguards throughout. The results show reliable convergence in practice, with the rate affected by consensus weight, and time-varying background traffic. We also assess practicality under a partial-global adversary model and discuss implications in light of the geographic concentration of Tor relay weight across cooperating jurisdictions.



1
Introduction

The Internet was not originally designed with privacy as a foundational principle. Although modern communication protocols employ cryptographic methods to secure data, they still expose metadata to potential eavesdroppers. Such metadata may include the identities of communicating parties, the frequency of their exchanges, and the duration of their interactions. Even without decrypting the underlying content, adversaries can exploit these data leaks to infer sensitive information
[
4
]
.

Anonymous communication networks have emerged to mitigate these privacy risks, allowing users to use the Internet without revealing their identities
[
4
,
6
]
. These systems are essential for safeguarding freedom of expression, particularly in environments characterized by pervasive surveillance and censorship
[
11
]
.

Currently, the most widely used anonymous communication network is Tor
[
10
]
, with millions daily active users. Tor’s core mechanism, onion routing, transmits data through multiple relays before reaching its destination, ensuring that no single relay knows the complete path
[
6
]
. This design hinders partial global adversaries from observing the full communication route and makes it harder to find the actual source and the final destination of a packet that travels through the network. Tor also supports hidden services, which enable clients and servers to interact without revealing their IP addresses
[
8
]
. Tor onion services host a wide range of content and applications. Prior work reports substantial legitimate use cases, including activism, journalism, software development, social networking, and whistleblowing, as well as illicit or high-risk activities such as drug trafficking, fraud, hacking, weapons sales, and other forms of cybercrime
[
1
]
. According to Tor Metrics
[
17
]
, there are roughly 900,000 hidden services in operation. However, Tor’s hidden service protocol exhibits static routing patterns, which can still be exploited through intersection attacks, posing ongoing challenges to hidden services operators anonymity.

Contributions

This work makes the following contributions:

•

It explains why introduction points in the hidden-service protocol exhibit deterministic, static patterns that can be exploited by an adversary.

•

It demonstrates how these patterns enable an intersection attack
[
18
]
capable of identifying the successor hop(s) of a relay in an introduction circuit.

•

It demonstrates the attack’s feasibility through live Tor-network experiments, conducted at different times and under varying background traffic conditions, on an introduction circuit of our hidden service composed of relays we operated that simultaneously routed real Tor traffic.

•

It analyzes the practicality of the attack by showing that coordinated state-level adversaries such as intelligence-sharing alliances like the
Fourteen Eyes

[
3
]
could realistically deploy it to deanonymize targeted hidden services.

Scope, Limitations, and Ethics.

The primary objective of this work is to establish the
feasibility
of the attack rather than to design or evaluate comprehensive defenses. Accordingly, our analysis is restricted to demonstrating that the attack converges in practice. In adherence to the Tor Research Safety Board’s data-minimization guidelines
[
15
]
, we deliberately avoid collecting fine-grained traffic data that would enable a detailed characterization of convergence dynamics. Instead, we provide plausible explanations for the observed convergence behavior without relying on sensitive measurements. While we outline a promising mitigation direction, its full design, implementation, and evaluation are intentionally deferred to future work.

2
Background and Preliminaries

This section provides the technical background necessary to understand our threat model and attack methodology. We summarize Tor’s communication model, describe the operation of onion services and the Vanguard defense, and introduce the anonymity-set and intersection-attack concepts used throughout the paper.

2.1
Onion Routing

Onion routing is Tor’s mechanism for anonymizing network traffic. A Tor client retrieves relay descriptors from the directory authorities and constructs a three-hop circuit consisting of an entry (guard) relay, a middle relay, and an exit relay. The client performs a Diffie–Hellman key exchange with each relay, deriving a symmetric key per hop. Traffic is encapsulated in fixed-size encrypted cells
[
6
]
; each relay removes one encryption layer before forwarding the cell to the next hop
[
8
]
. This layered encryption prevents any single relay from learning both the client’s identity and its destination.

2.2
Tor Onion Services

Tor onion services enable mutually anonymous communication between clients and servers. Two protocols underpin this functionality: the
introduction protocol
, which allows a client to signal its intent to communicate with a service, and the
rendezvous protocol
, which establishes an anonymous bidirectional channel.

Publishing an Onion Service

To make an onion service reachable, the operator first selects three relays to act as
introduction points
. The service constructs a long-lived circuit to each of these relays and sends a
RELAY_COMMAND_ESTABLISH_INTRO
cell. If a relay agrees to serve in this role, it responds with
RELAY_COMMAND_INTRO_ESTABLISHED
.

Next, the service constructs a circuit to the responsible Hidden Service Directories (HSDirs), which are selected deterministically based on the service’s public key and additional protocol parameters
[
19
]
. Over this circuit, the service uploads a signed descriptor containing its introduction points.

Finally, the operator distributes the onion address, a special-purpose identifier derived from the service’s public key. Using this address, clients can retrieve the descriptor from the HSDirs, verify its authenticity, and learn the corresponding introduction points.

Client Access to an Onion Service

A client connects to an onion service by first retrieving its descriptor. Using the onion address, the client fetches the descriptor from the responsible HSDirs and verifies its signature
[
19
]
. The descriptor reveals the service’s introduction points.

The client then selects a relay to serve as a rendezvous point and constructs a circuit to it by sending a
RELAY_COMMAND_ESTABLISH_RENDEZVOUS
cell. Upon success, the relay replies with
RELAY_COMMAND_RENDEZVOUS_ESTABLISHED
, confirming that it will act as the rendezvous point.

Subsequently, the client builds a circuit to one of the service’s introduction points and transmits an
INTRODUCE1
cell. This cell contains the rendezvous point identifier, a rendezvous cookie, and Diffie–Hellman data
g^{x}
.

Upon receiving this message, the introduction point encapsulates it into an
INTRODUCE2
cell and forwards it to the hidden service over the pre-established introduction circuit. It then sends a
RELAY_COMMAND_INTRODUCE_ACK
to the client, after which the client tears down the introduction-point circuit.

The hidden service decrypts the
INTRODUCE2
cell, extracts the rendezvous point information and the client’s Diffie–Hellman value
g^{x}
, computes the shared key
g^{xy}
, and establishes a circuit to the rendezvous point. It completes the handshake by sending a
RELAY_COMMAND_RENDEZVOUS1
cell containing
g^{y}
.

Finally, the rendezvous point verifies the cookie, removes it, and forwards the remaining payload to the client as a
RELAY_COMMAND_RENDEZVOUS2
. The client verifies the Diffie–Hellman value
g^{y}
, derives the shared secret
K=g^{xy}
, and confirms it via
H(K)
. Once the handshake completes, communication proceeds over the rendezvous circuit using
RELAY_BEGIN
.

2.3
The Vanguard Defense

Vanguards protects both onion services and Tor clients from guard-relay discovery attacks by adding one or two layers of additional middle relays between the service and its guard set
[
16
]
.

•

Vanguard-Lite
adds a single additional layer (second-layer guards).

•

Full Vanguards
adds both second- and third-layer guards.

These layers reduce the risk that an attacker can identify the service’s entry guard.

2.4
Anonymity Set

An anonymity set
[
5
]
is the set of entities that an adversary cannot distinguish as potential sources or recipients of an observed action, given the observation itself and any auxiliary information available. Equivalently, it is the set of candidates whose behavior remains consistent with the adversary’s evidence. By definition, the true actor is contained in this set, and the adversary’s residual uncertainty can be quantified by the set’s cardinality.

Anonymity Sets in the Introduction Protocol

If an adversary monitors a relay belonging to an introduction circuit and observes the transmission of an
INTRODUCE1
cell followed by the corresponding
RENDEZVOUS2
cell, then the protocol must have executed successfully. Because the monitored relay forwards an
INTRODUCE2
cell to its successor during this interval, any relay that receives traffic from the monitored relay in this window is a candidate for the next hop.

Formally, letting
r_{m}
denote the monitored relay and
\Delta t
the
INTRODUCE1
–
RENDEZVOUS2
interval, the successor’s anonymity set is:

A=\{\,r_{i}\mid r_{i}\text{ receives traffic from }r_{m}\text{ during }\Delta t\,\}.

Importantly, constructing this set requires only
local
visibility at
r_{m}
, not global surveillance of the Tor network. This property makes it exploitable by an adversary capable of monitoring a single relay.

2.5
Intersection Attacks

Intersection attacks reduce anonymity by repeatedly observing anonymity sets that all contain the true target and computing their intersection. Each individual observation is typically too noisy to identify the target, but the intersection of many such sets eventually isolates the entity that appears in all of them. Formally, if each trial yields an anonymity set
A_{t}
that contains the true element
x
, an adversary computes

I_{t}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{t}A_{j},

which forms a monotonically shrinking sequence of sets. Once
|I_{t}|=1
, the adversary identifies
x
with high confidence.

Intersection Attack Against the Introduction Protocol

Consider an adversary that observes traffic at the introduction point of a target onion service. Each time the adversary sends an
INTRODUCE1
cell and subsequently receives the corresponding
RENDEZVOUS2
, they record the set
A_{t}
of all IP addresses that exchanged traffic with the introduction point during that interval. One of these IPs must be the next hop in the introduction circuit. Across repeated trials, background traffic varies, but the true successor appears in every set.

By computing intersections

I_{t}=A_{1}\cap A_{2}\cap\dots\cap A_{t},

the anonymity set for the successor relay shrinks until, eventually,
I_{t}
becomes a singleton. Repeating this procedure hop-by-hop allows the adversary, in principle, to reconstruct the entire introduction circuit.

This is precisely the attack we analyze in this work; its motivation and the structural properties of the introduction protocol that enable it are described in Section
4
.

3
Threat Model

Adversary Goals.

The adversary has both short-term and long-term objectives. The long-term goal is to deanonymize a targeted hidden service by reconstructing the full introduction circuit associated with one of its introduction points. The short-term goal is to identify the next unknown relay that follows the last known relay in the introduction circuit. At the start of the attack, the only known relay is the introduction point itself (IP from
1
); the next unknown relay corresponds to the middle relay that directly succeeds it (M1 from
1
).

Adversary Capabilities.

The adversary is modeled as a
partial-global network observer
with targeted yet incomplete visibility of Tor traffic. Specifically:

•

The adversary can observe traffic at a single relay it targets (e.g., via cooperation with the relay’s hosting ISP).

•

At each stage, the adversary observes a single relay in the introduction circuit and seeks to deanonymize its immediate successor.

•

After identifying a successor relay, the adversary can subsequently observe that relay’s traffic as well, enabling iterative expansion of their visibility along the circuit.

In addition, the adversary can actively transmit
INTRODUCE1
cells to the targeted introduction point and receive the corresponding
RENDEZVOUS2
responses. No other active attacks are assumed.

4
Problem Statement, Hypothesis, and Motivation

We hypothesize that the introduction protocol exhibits static and repeatable patterns that enable an adversary to apply intersection attacks to deanonymize an introduction circuit and, ultimately, the hidden service itself. Four factors motivate this vulnerability:

1.

Persistence of introduction circuits.

Introduction circuits are significantly longer-lived than ordinary Tor circuits: they remain active for 18–24 hours before expiring
[
12
]
. By contrast, other type of Tor circuits are intentionally short-lived (up to 10 minutes) in order to limit the feasibility of intersection attacks
[
7
]
. The extended lifetime of introduction circuits therefore creates a much larger observational window during which an adversary can repeatedly probe, monitor, and correlate traffic associated with a specific introduction point.

2.

Rendezvous churn and large path space.

Rendezvous and clearnet circuits have a lifetime of approximately ten minutes, and roughly 1.4 million circuits are active during any ten-minute interval
[
9
]
.
Using an Onionoo API
[
14
]
snapshot from 18 December , we identified 8,634 eligible middle relays and 6,205 eligible guard relays with non-zero selection probability. This yields a vast space of possible circuit paths, far exceeding the number of circuits active at any given time, making sustained reuse of specific relay paths over long periods statistically unlikely.

Consequently, background clearnet and rendezvous traffic included in the anonymity sets typically changes across trials as circuits expire and are rebuilt. This churn causes unrelated flows to appear only transiently and to drop out over repeated observations. Intersection then becomes effective because repeated trials filter out short lived noise and retain the destination that persists across observations, which corresponds to the next hop.

3.

Short execution time of the introduction protocol.

Instrumented measurements across 16 onion services (ten trials each) show that the
INTRODUCE1
–
RENDEZVOUS2
interval typically lasts approximately between 0.5–1.5 seconds (Table
1
). This brief, well-bounded interval sharply limits which nodes could plausibly interact with the introduction circuit in each trial, thereby reducing the anonymity set of the next hop.

4.

Introduction protocol determinism.

During every
INTRODUCE1
–
RENDEZVOUS2
interval, the successor relay in the introduction circuit
must
exchange traffic with the monitored relay (e.g., the introduction point). Thus, each observed-IP set necessarily contains the true successor relay. Although unrelated background traffic varies across trials, the successor relay appears in all sets, enabling an intersection attack.

Problem Statement.

The combination of the small execution time of the protocol, the deterministic appearance of the successor IP in the anonymity set of the introduction protocol and long-lived introduction circuits creates a promising attack surface for deanonymizing hidden services. This work examines whether an adversary can, by repeatedly collecting the set of IP addresses that exchange traffic with a monitored relay during successive
INTRODUCE1
–
RENDEZVOUS2
intervals,

1.

progressively shrink the successor relay’s anonymity set until only one candidate remains, and

2.

iteratively apply this procedure to identify every hop in the introduction circuit and ultimately recover the hidden service’s network location.

The immediate objective is to determine whether the anonymity set of a successor relay can be reduced to one; the broader objective is to evaluate whether such iterative narrowing enables full deanonymization of the hidden service.

5
Attack Methodology

This section formalizes the intersection-based attack used to reconstruct a hidden service’s introduction circuit.

The adversary proceeds iteratively through four stages, where each stage
k
targets a single unknown relay in the introduction circuit. We assume the hidden service uses the default
Vanguard-Lite
configuration. At each stage, the adversary performs the following steps:

1.

Transmit a
RELAY_COMMAND_INTRODUCE1
cell to the targeted introduction point.

2.

Await the corresponding
RELAY_COMMAND_RENDEZVOUS2
response.

3.

During the
INTRODUCE1
–
RENDEZVOUS2
interval, record the set
A_{t}^{(k)}
of outbound destination IP addresses observed at the monitored relay for trial
t
.
If
k>1
, the previously identified successor relay is removed.

4.

Wait for a configurable inter-trial delay
\delta>0
before initiating the next trial.

Across trials, the adversary accumulates the sets
\{A_{t}^{(k)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
.
The intersection for stage
k
is:

I^{(k)}\;=\;\bigcap_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}^{(k)}.

The stage completes when
|I^{(k)}|=1
; the unique element is accepted as the successor relay at position
k
.
The adversary then moves to stage
k+1
and repeats the procedure until all 4 targeted hops in the circuit are resolved.

The sequence
\{I_{t}^{(k)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
satisfies two monotonicity properties for all
t\geq 2
:

1.

\displaystyle I_{t}^{(k)}\subseteq I_{t-1}^{(k)}
.

Reason:

I_{t}^{(k)}=I_{t-1}^{(k)}\cap A_{t}^{(k)}
, and an intersection cannot enlarge the set.

2.

\displaystyle|I_{t}^{(k)}|\leq|I_{t-1}^{(k)}|
.

Reason:
Subset inclusion implies non-increasing cardinality.

Limitation: IP Co-location of Successive Hops.

The attack assumes that successive relays in the introduction circuit are hosted at distinct IP addresses. If a newly targeted successor relay shares the same IP address as the previously identified relay, that IP is removed from the observed sets by construction, causing the anonymity set to become empty at a certain point and preventing further progress of the attack. In this case, the intersection procedure fails by definition. Because IP co-location of adjacent hops in Tor circuits is highly unlikely in practice, we exclude this scenario from our analysis.

6
Experiment

6.1
Experimental Setup

We conducted controlled experiments to evaluate the feasibility of the intersection-based deanonymization attack using a hidden service and Tor relays that we exclusively operated. The setup is organized into four components: relay deployment, packet logging and pseudonymization, Tor daemon instrumentation, and trial coordination.

•

Relay Deployment.

Four Tor relays were deployed and configured to serve as hops in the introduction circuit of a hidden service under our control.
All relays were hosted on DigitalOcean using the $7/month plan (1 GB RAM, 1 vCPU, 25 GB NVMe storage, 1 TB monthly bandwidth). All relays used in our experiments were active and eligible for path selection throughout the measurement period. Throughout the measurement period, all relays remained active and eligible for path selection. Each relay consistently carried the
Running
,
Valid
,
Stable
,
Fast
,
Guard
, and
V2Dir
flags, and had non-zero guard and middle selection probabilities, indicating eligibility for both entry-guard and middle positions.
The relays were operated in the following order along the introduction circuit: introduction point (IP), Middle 1 (M1), Middle 0 (Vanguard, M0), and entry guard (E–G), as illustrated in Figure
1
. The IP and M0 relays had been operational since 30/10/2025, M1 since 21/09/2025, and the entry guard since 03/09/2025.

•

Packet Logging and Pseudonymization.

To identify successor relays in introduction circuits, when relay was monitored, it ran a Python-based packet-logging service that passively observed transit traffic using
tcpdump

[
13
]
. The logger invoked
tcpdump
as a subprocess configured to emit packet header metadata only, and consumed its output via a unidirectional pipe. Header lines were parsed incrementally using a regular expression to extract only the destination IP address field. Packet processing was activated exclusively when a local measurement flag was enabled, delimiting fixed measurement intervals. During these intervals, each extracted destination address was immediately pseudonymized and stored exclusively in volatile memory within an in-memory set, ensuring that no packet payloads or raw identifiers were ever accessed or persisted. No timestamps, packet counts, or additional metadata were retained, and no data were written to persistent storage. All pseudonymized identifiers were discarded at the end of each measurement interval.

Let
\mathsf{IP}
denote the destination IP address of a packet observed during a trial.
Each
\mathsf{IP}
was transformed using a deterministic pseudonymization function

\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{IP})=\mathrm{SHA256}\!\left(\mathrm{RSA}_{\mathsf{pk}}(\mathsf{IP})\right),

where
\mathrm{RSA}_{\mathsf{pk}}(\cdot)
denotes RSA encryption without padding under a freshly generated public key
\mathsf{pk}
.
The corresponding private key was discarded immediately after key generation, ensuring that
\mathcal{P}
is effectively irreversible.

Traffic collection was coordinated by the packet-logging service via a local socket interface.
Upon receiving a
START
command, which indicated the beginning of an
INTRODUCE1
–
RENDEZVOUS2
interval for trial
t
at stage
k
, the logger applied
\mathcal{P}
to each observed destination IP and inserted the resulting pseudonym into the trial-specific anonymity set

A_{t}^{(k)}.

Upon receiving a
STOP
command, the trial set was finalized and merged into the cumulative intersection.
If
t=1
, the intersection was initialized as

I_{1}^{(k)}=A_{1}^{(k)}.

For
t>1
, it was updated recursively as

I_{t}^{(k)}=I_{t-1}^{(k)}\cap A_{t}^{(k)}.

To validate correctness, we recorded (out of band) the true destination IP address of the successor relay at each stage.
For every trial, we verified that the pseudonym corresponding to this target IP was present in both the newly constructed anonymity set
A_{t}^{(k)}
and the cumulative intersection
I_{t}^{(k)}
until convergence, ensuring that the true successor was never eliminated during intersection.

Trial-specific sets were discarded immediately after intersection.
The public key
\mathsf{pk}
and the cumulative intersection
I_{t}^{(k)}
were retained exclusively in volatile memory for the duration of the experiment and were never written to disk.
When
\lvert I_{t}^{(k)}\rvert=1
, the stage terminated and the number of trials required for convergence was recorded.

•

Tor Daemon Instrumentation.

Each relay host ran two Tor processes:

–

a standard Tor
relay
daemon, and

–

a colocated
client-side
Tor instance used exclusively to trigger introduction requests and issue instrumentation signals.

This co-location ensured that instrumentation control messages incurred minimal network delay, yielding precise request–response windows.
It also enabled strict data minimization, as accurate logging boundaries could be enforced without collecting packet timestamps or auxiliary metadata for each pseudonymous destination IP saved in the anonimity set.

The client-side Tor daemon was modified as follows:

–

Immediately before transmitting an
INTRODUCE1
cell, it issued a
START
command to the local packet logger.

–

Upon receiving the corresponding
RENDEZVOUS2
cell, it issued a
STOP
command.

The hidden-service Tor daemon was modified to deterministically select the four relays we operated when constructing the introduction circuit.

•

Trial Coordination and Automation.

Attack execution was automated using a coordination script deployed on each relay host.
Beginning at the introduction point, the script repeatedly:

–

launched a client-side Tor instance to issue a request to the hidden service,

–

terminated the client process upon completion of the request,

–

checked whether the packet-logging process had converged (i.e., exited when the intersection set reached cardinality one, as defined earlier), and

–

if convergence had not yet been achieved, initiated the next trial after a fixed inter-trial delay
\delta=30s
.

Once a successor relay was identified at a given stage, the script advanced to the next relay in the introduction circuit, which was predefined via SSH and repeated the procedure.
This process continued iteratively until all hops of the introduction circuit, including the hidden service, were deanonymized.

6.2
Ethical Considerations

This study was designed and conducted in accordance with the Tor Research Safety Board’s ethical guidelines
[
15
]
. We explicitly evaluated potential risks to users and the Tor network and adopted safeguards to minimize harm while enabling meaningful security analysis. Although the Board recommends the use of test networks where possible, our evaluation necessarily relied on the live Tor network, as the attack depends on real-world properties such as active user volume and background traffic behavior in different times in the days. These characteristics cannot be faithfully reproduced in existing test networks, nor reliably approximated, and a testbed-only evaluation would therefore preclude assessing the attack’s practical relevance.

To minimize ethical risk, all experiments targeted only the anonimity set of the hidden service that we fully owned and controlled. No third-party users or services were affected beyond normal Tor operation. Any IP addresses observed during experiments were immediately pseudonymized using cryptographic mechanisms designed to be effectively irreversible: re-identification would require simultaneous access to ephemeral in-memory cryptographic material and anonymity/intersection sets and none of them were never written into the disk.

We further adhered to strict data minimization. The system recorded only whether a pseudonymized identifier of the destination IPs of packets appeared within the
INTRODUCE1
–
RENDEZVOUS2
window into the violatile memory; it did not collect counts, precise timestamps, packet contents, or other fine-grained signals per IP. While the methodology introduces limited, controlled risk, these risks are outweighed by the benefit of characterizing a previously underexplored attack surface. Finally, by restricting the attack to our self-operated hidden service, and refraining from collecting user-identifying information beyond heavily pseudonymized indicators, we respect user privacy expectations and reduce the possibility of ethical harm.

6.3
Variables and Factors

We evaluated the impact of two factors on the convergence behavior of the attack:

1.

Time of execution.

We executed the attack at different times of day to assess whether diurnal variations in Tor network load affect convergence.
To this end, we executed the attack repeatedly at fixed daily time windows over multiple days, specifically at 02:00, 10:00, and 18:00 UTC, allowing us to compare convergence behavior under varying background traffic conditions.

2.

Consensus Weight.

Relays with higher consensus weight are likely to induce larger anonymity sets due to increased selection probability, which may slow or impede intersection-based deanonymization.
We therefore examined how each relay’s consenus weight influenced the number of trials required to isolate the true successor relay.

6.4
Results and Analysis

Tables
2
-
7
report the number of trials required to deanonymize each hop of the introduction circuit across multiple dates and time windows, together with the corresponding relay consensus weights. Overall, consensus weight strongly influences convergence: entry guards with higher consensus weight consistently required more trials. On 2026-01-07 (Tables
2
-
4
), entry guards with consensus weights around 9300 required 31–78 trials, compared to 17–30 trials for entry guards with weights between 4000 and 4300 observed on 2026-01-11 and 2026-01-12 (Tables
5
–
7
).

Vanguard and middle 1 relays generally converged faster than entry guards, consistent with their lower selection probability and traffic exposure. However, convergence varied across runs. Notably, the vanguard relay in Table
4
required substantially more iterations, indicating sensitivity to time-varying background traffic and possible repeated selection by other users despite its lower consensus weight. In most cases, convergence for vanguard and middle 1 relay occurred within the expected circuit lifetime of maximum 10 minutes
[
9
]
, with the run illustrated in
2
representing a clear outlier requiring approximately 30.5 minutes. A plausible explanation is that stream circuits corresponding to paths that repeatedly appeared in the anonymity sets eventually expired after their maximum lifetime of approximately 10 minutes. Given Tor’s large combinatorial path-selection space and the high number of concurrently active circuits within any ten-minute window
[
9
]
(see Section
4
), the probability that the same paths were reconstructed after expiration is statistically small. As a result, these previously persistent paths disappear in subsequent trials.

In contrast, in the experiments conducted on 2026-01-07, the introduction
point, despite having the lowest consensus weight, required substantially
more trials, reaching the largest value observed in our study (285 trials).
A plausible explanation is increased unrelated Tor traffic during that
period. For instance, if the relay was repeatedly selected as a long-lived
entry guard by other users, and its successor in the path was also selected
as a guard (e.g., as a second-layer guard in the Vanguard-Lite setting),
the resulting path segment could persist over extended periods. Such
persistent guard–guard path combinations would continuously carry traffic
between the same adjacent relays, creating stable and recurring flows.
These flows would repeatedly appear in the measured anonymity sets across
trials, thereby enlarging and stabilizing the intersection and slowing
convergence.

Overall, these results suggest that convergence depends not only on
consensus weight but also on time-varying background traffic and the
structural persistence introduced by guard selection behavior.

6.5
Limitations

This study has several important limitations that constrain the generality of our results.

•

Incomplete instrumentation of correlated metrics.
We did not collect or report certain correlated measurements that could further characterize the attack, such as the number of active circuits traversing our relays during each trial, per-relay stream-traffic fraction. These data would enable a more detailed analysis of per-stage dynamics and noise sources. Consequently our analysis in the previous subsection is based on assumptions.

•

Scenarios With Larger Per-Second Traffic Volumes and higher Selection Probabilities Not Evaluated.
We have not evaluated the attack in scenarios where introduction circuit relays command substantially larger amount of data per second. The attack’s effectiveness may differ under such conditions.

Despite these limitations, the primary objective of this experiment was to demonstrate the feasibility of an intersection-based deanonymization technique against introduction circuits.

Relay Concentration and Practical Implications

Several Western states participate in intelligence-sharing arrangements
commonly labeled the
Five Eyes
,
Nine Eyes
, and

Fourteen Eyes
alliances
[
3
]
. Five Eyes comprises
the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand;
Nine Eyes additionally includes Denmark, France, the Netherlands, and
Norway; and Fourteen Eyes further includes Germany, Belgium, Italy, Spain,
and Sweden.

We use Fourteen Eyes membership solely to define a conservative set of
jurisdictions that could be
simultaneously observable
by a
coordinated multi-jurisdiction adversary. We make no claims regarding
surveillance, legal authority, or operational cooperation; the set serves
only as an upper bound on potential visibility.

Using Tor Project Onionoo relay statistics from 30 November
2025
[
14
]
, we observe that Tor relays are geographically
concentrated, particularly in the United States, Germany, and the
Netherlands (Figure
2
). Several high-weight
hosting countries fall within the Fourteen Eyes set, resulting in a
non-uniform jurisdictional distribution of relay selection weight.

To quantify concentration by circuit position, we use Onionoo’s
consensus-weighted
guard_probability
and

middle_probability
fields, which approximate relay selection
probabilities given consensus weight, bandwidth weighting, and position
eligibility. Let
p_{G,14}
(resp.
p_{M,14}
) denote the sum of

guard_probability
(resp.
middle_probability
) over
relays geolocated in Fourteen Eyes countries. Interpreted purely as hosting
concentration,
p_{G,14}
and
p_{M,14}
estimate the probability that a
randomly selected guard or middle relay is hosted within the Fourteen Eyes
set (Figure
3
).

We consider a
partial-global adversary
capable of observing traffic
at all relays hosted within a specified jurisdiction set. Under weighted
path selection, relay concentration increases the probability that such an
adversary observes multiple hops of an onion-service introduction circuit.
In the default Vanguard-Lite configuration, an introduction circuit
comprises one entry guard and three middle relays. Although Tor selects
relays without replacement within a circuit, the large eligible relay pools
allow us to approximate hop selection as independent sampling with
replacement to obtain a conservative upper bound. Under this approximation,
the probability that all four hops are hosted in Fourteen Eyes jurisdictions
is

P_{\text{all-14}}^{\text{intro}}\approx p_{G,14}\,p_{M,14}^{3}.

Using the measured values,

P_{\text{all-14}}^{\text{intro}}\approx 0.7706\times(0.7707)^{3}\approx 0.353.

This estimate indicates that, conditional on the stated threat model,
hosting concentration alone can materially increase adversarial visibility.
The result should be interpreted strictly as a structural risk analysis: we
do not assert that any state monitors Tor relays or possesses the legal or
operational capability to do so. Rather, we quantify how a coordinated
multi-jurisdiction observer
could
benefit from the current
distribution of relay selection weight.

Related Work

Biryukov et al.
[
2
]
introduced TorScan, a set of topology-inference techniques and attacks that exploit observable relay-to-relay TLS connectivity in Tor. Their main contribution is a “canonical connectivity scanning” method that turns Tor’s canonical-connection behavior into a practical oracle: by crafting circuit extension requests and observing whether the target relay replies with an EXTENDED or DESTROY cell, an adversary can infer whether a given relay already maintains a TLS connection to another relay. Using repeated scans over time, TorScan demonstrates how connectivity dynamics can shrink the anonymity set for a user’s guard, particularly for long-lived streams where relay connections persist. The authors also describe a noisier timing-based alternative and evaluate feasibility via live-network experiments, reporting fast scans with modest bandwidth overhead and showing that many relay connections decay quickly while a smaller subset remains persistent, enabling guard-candidate reduction in their one-hop and two-hop threat models.

9
Conclusion and Future Work

This work demonstrates the practical feasibility of an intersection attack against Tor onion service introduction circuits. By exploiting deterministic properties of the introduction protocol and the long-lived nature of introduction circuits, an adversary with only local visibility at a single relay per stage can iteratively deanonymize each hop of an introduction circuit. Our live-network experiments on a self-operated onion service show that the attack reliably converges in practice, with convergence influenced by consensus weight and time-varying background traffic. These results expose a previously underexplored attack surface in the introduction protocol and show that meaningful deanonymization is possible without global network visibility.

While our goal is to establish feasibility rather than to design complete defenses, the results motivate potential mitigations. A promising direction is the periodic reconstruction of introduction circuits while retaining the same introduction points. In the current design, introduction circuits are long-lived, often persisting for 18–24 hours, which enables repeated probing of a fixed hop sequence and accumulation of observations for intersection. Periodically rebuilding the internal circuits (e.g., on the order of minutes) while keeping the same introduction point would cause successive
INTRODUCE1
–
RENDEZVOUS2
exchanges to traverse different relay paths, disrupting the stability required for convergence. This approach increases path diversity without requiring descriptor updates or client-side protocol changes, at the cost of additional circuit construction overhead.

Future work includes the systematic design and evaluation of such mitigations.
