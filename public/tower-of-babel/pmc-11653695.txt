Socioeconomic status and sleep duration among a representative, cross-sectional sample of US adults
Authors: Sarah Wetzel, Usama Bilal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSleep is a crucial determinant of physical and mental health outcomes, and insufficient sleep is highly prevalent among United States adults. Although some risk factors of poor sleep have been extensively studied, including substance use, age, health behaviors, and others, the associations between socioeconomic status (SES) and sleep remain inconclusive. There is limited evidence on SES and sleep duration among the US adult population. This study analyzed the relationships between three SES indicators (poverty, education, and food security), and sleep duration.MethodsWe used responses from the 2017-March 2020 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Respondents younger than 25 years old were excluded. Sleep duration was classified using self-reported sleep time and stratified by work vs. non-workdays. SES was operationalized using three indicators: poverty-income ratio, educational attainment, and food security status. We imputed missing data for socioeconomic status and outcome variables using multiple imputation. Weighted Poisson regression models with robust standard errors were used to calculate the crude and adjusted prevalence ratios for insufficient sleep duration (< 7 h of self-reported sleep) on workdays and non-workdays separately by each of the three SES indicators.ResultsWe included a total of 8,457 individuals. In the adjusted model, participants with lower income, educational status, and food security had significantly higher prevalence of insufficient sleep duration on both workdays and non-workdays. For example, low-income individuals (poverty-income ratio < 1) had 1.22 (95% CI 1.04–1.44) and 2.08 (95% CI 1.61–2.67) higher prevalence of insufficient sleep as compared to high income individuals on workday and non-workdays, respectively. In general, we found larger differences by level of SES indicator for the non-workday than for the workday outcome. There were no major differences in gender-stratified analysis. We also found that lower SES was associated with higher prevalence of excessive sleep (≥ 9 h).ConclusionSocioeconomic status indicators are significantly associated with sleep duration in the US adult population. Lower SES correlates with increased prevalence of insufficient sleep duration, which has implications for the overall wellbeing of US adults with lower SES. Targeted interventions and further research are needed to reduce this disparity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20977-w.Keywords: Sleep duration, Sleep health, Socioeconomic status

Introduction

In the US, 33.2% of adults report less than 7 h of sleep per day, categorized as insufficient sleep [1], and as many as 70 million adults nationally have a chronic sleep disorder [2]. Sleep is an established determinant of physical and mental health, and difficulty sleeping can negatively affect a person’s mental well-being across multiple domains [2–4]. Poor sleep is a predictor of cardiovascular health problems [5, 6], medication and substance use [7], mood disorders [8], multimorbidity [9], all-cause mortality [10], and other outcomes [3, 11]. Poor sleep health is also associated with negative social and workplace outcomes [12].

There are multiple causes for poor sleep, including acute stress, medication side effects, substance use, and physical conditions [1, 13]. For instance, alcohol use can cause irregular sleep-wake schedules, and individuals experiencing insomnia may attempt to cope by consuming alcohol [13]. Severe medical conditions, such as those that cause pain, also have a bidirectional relationship with sleep [14]. Other factors that cause poor sleep include employment factors (such as long working hours, irregular or shift schedules, and commute time) [3, 10], being pregnant [15, 16], being single or lonely [13], parenting young children [17], and having symptoms of depression or anxiety [18, 19]. Certain social groups experience higher rates of poor sleep. Older adults experience shorter and less deep sleep [4], with as many as 70% of those older than 65 experiencing chronic sleep problems [20]. Women have higher odds of insomnia [21], while men are more often diagnosed with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and experience more fragmented sleep [4]. Also, non-white individuals are less likely to have sufficient sleep duration and quality [4, 6, 22, 23], which is likely influenced by lifetime racial discrimination and systemic inequities in social and economic opportunity [22, 24–26]. In fact, race likely interacts with income and other socioeconomic factors to influence sleep [25].

There is a proposed association between low socioeconomic status (SES) and poor sleep continuity, although effects on sleep duration are less clear, and higher quality studies are necessary [27, 28]. Lower SES is associated with lower sleep duration, latency, and fragmentation, and greater variability in sleep onset and latency [23, 28, 29]. Individuals with higher neighborhood social cohesion and safety have earlier sleep timing and longer sleep duration [30]. Neighborhood deprivation and household income are significant risk factors for poor sleep [31, 32], and indebted adults are more likely to have difficulties with sleep onset, maintenance, and use sleep medications [33]. Lower and middle income adults may be more likely to report restless sleep compared to high income adults [5]. Notably, the association between education and sleep is inconclusive [30, 34–36]. Finally, very low food security is associated with a higher prevalence of sleep initiation and maintenance problems and shorter sleep duration; [37, 38] some of this relationship may be due to inadequate nutrition [39].

However, there is sparse evidence on socioeconomic disparities in sleep duration among US adults, and existing data is limited by heterogeneity of exposure and outcome definitions, poor inclusion of people with low SES, and lack of control for confounding variables [27, 28, 35]. Self-reported data is generally more accessible and representative but may be inconsistent with objective measures [28, 30]. Additionally, the association between SES and sleep duration is inconsistent and depends on the specific SES indicator used [27]. Furthermore, few studies have examined the distinction between sleep on work days and weekends or non-workdays. To our knowledge, there is limited analysis of the association between sleep duration and specific socioeconomic indicators. Therefore, we aim to assess whether sleep duration among US adults varies by three SES indicators: income, education, and food security status.

Methods

Study design

The present study compares socioeconomic status with sleep duration among US adults, aged twenty-five or older, from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). NHANES is a cross-sectional, nationally representative, cluster-sampled survey of the noninstitutionalized, civilian US population. NHANES uses a complex sample design, with random probability sampling in four nested stages—primary sampling units (PSUs) of counties, groups within counties, or combinations of neighboring counties; from census blocks or their combinations within PSUs; households from the prior stage; and individuals within households [40]. Surveys capture over 5,000 participants annually, collecting data from direct interview and clinical examination.

Analytic sample

This study included participants who were sampled in the cycles from 2017-pre-pandemic 2020 (n = 15,560).41 Survey responses from 2017 to March 2020 were included, as the COVID-19 pandemic caused responses from 2019-March 2020 to be combined with the 2017–2018 dataset. Some populations, including Hispanic and non-white individuals, those at or below 185% of the federal poverty level, and those aged 0–11 or 80 years or greater, were oversampled to improve reliability [40]. Screening is conducted within households to develop oversampling recruitment numbers; the screener response rate was 88.7%.41 The unweighted interview response rates for the two cycles were 51.0%.41 From the n = 15,560 sampled individuals, we excluded respondents younger than 25 years (n = 7,013). We therefore included a total of n = 8,457 (Additional file I).

Measures

Socioeconomic status

Socioeconomic status was categorized according to the poverty-income ratio (PIR), education, and food security status. Respondents self-reported their annual family income for the last year in dollars. If no exact income was provided, the respondent was asked to select an income category from a list, and the income was reported as the midpoint of that category. PIR was calculated by NHANES using the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) annual federal poverty guidelines, which vary by household or family size and location [41]. Compared to other measures, PIR is a more accurate indicator of SES; it is also more reliable for data across survey years, as HHS adjusts income thresholds for inflation [42]. PIR is the ratio of the reported income to the poverty threshold. Thus, PIR is a continuous variable and a PIR of 1 is equal to the family poverty threshold for a given respondent [41]. Respondents were classified as ‘low-income’ if the PIR was < 1, ‘near low-income’ if between 1 and 2, ‘middle-income’ if between 2 and 4, and ‘high-income’ if 4 or greater [41]. Education was categorized as “less than 9th grade” or “9-11th grade (Includes 12th grade with no diploma)” (‘HS’), “High school Graduate/GED or equivalent” (‘HS/GED’), “Some college or AA degree” (‘Some college/AA’), or “College graduate or above” (‘College+’).42 Food security was coded according to the US Food Security Survey Module (FSSM), which includes ten questions related to adult food security [43]. Affirmative responses to each item were totaled, with 0 marked as ‘full food security’, 1–2 as ‘marginal food security’, 3–5 as ‘low food security’, and 6 or greater as ‘very low food security’ [44]. The FSSM includes specific phrases to clarify that food restriction is due to financial circumstances (e.g., “worried whether (my/our) food would run out before (I/we) got money to buy more” or “relied on only a few kinds of low-cost food”) [43]. It also captures a broader range of food insecurity and hunger; for example, it is formulated based on evidence that adults may restrict their food when a household financial situation becomes more challenging, to ensure that children have sufficient food [43].

Sleep

The primary outcomes were usual sleep hours on weekdays or workdays (‘workday sleep’), and usual sleep hours on weekends or non-workdays (‘non-workday sleep’). Respondents were asked “What time [do you] usually fall asleep on weekdays or workdays?” and “What time [do you] usually wake up on weekdays or workdays?” [44] They were asked the same questions with respect to “weekends or non-workdays” [44]. The number of sleep hours was calculated from these responses for each individual. Inclusion of “workdays” vs. “non-workdays” likely accounts for most of the variation introduced by shift workers, unemployment, self-employment, or other types of work schedules [44]. The two outcome variables, sleep on workdays and non-workdays, were categorized as binary, with 7 or more hours per night categorized as ‘sufficient’ and less than 7 h as ‘insufficient’. Clinicians typically recommend 7 or more hours per night, and less than 7 h is associated with chronic health conditions and patient-reported adverse outcomes [45, 46]. Given some evidence about the potential harms of excessive sleep, we conducted a secondary analysis categorizing sleeping 9 or more hours per night as “excessive sleep” [47, 48].

Sociodemographic covariates

Confounders include age, gender, and race or ethnicity. Age was included as a categorical variable (‘0–24’, ’25–39’, ‘40–59’, ‘60–79’, and ‘80+’). We proxied gender using self-reported biological binary sex (“male” or “female”). Respondents categorized their race/ethnicity within the given categories (“Mexican American”, “Other Hispanic”, “Non-Hispanic White”, “Non-Hispanic Black”, “Non-Hispanic Asian”, and “Other Race – Including Multiracial”) [41]. Individual race/ethnicity was included as a proxy for individual lived experiences (disparate access to resources, geographical segregation, interpersonal discrimination, etc.) of those in different racialized groups under a system of structural racism, which likely predicts poorer sleep outcomes [4, 6, 22, 23].

Statistical analysis

The main objective of this analysis was to describe socioeconomic disparities in poor sleep duration in workdays and non-workdays. We conducted this analysis in three steps. First, we found that a high proportion of respondents were missing data on poverty-income ratio (16.11%, n = 1,377) and adult food security status (7.87%, n = 673) (Additional file II). We found fewer missing values for education (0.18%, n = 15) or the outcome (1.40%, n = 120), and no participants were missing age, race, or gender. To address the issue of missing data in the socioeconomic indicators and the outcome [49], we conducted multiple imputation. Multiple imputation (MI) first creates a probability model using complete and missing data to create imputed datasets, which are analyzed separately and then the results are pooled to control for variance introduced by the imputations [50]. Auxiliary variables were identified a priori and assessed with chi-squared analysis of association or generalized linear models, and included using p < 0.01. Variables included total time in the US (for those born outside the US), marital status, the language the survey was completed in, measures of chronic health, smoking status, health insurance status, typical hours worked, and food consumption habits. We used no weights for multiple imputation. All exposure and response variables listed in the prior section were also included in the MI. We then created ten multiply imputed sets [50]. Characteristics for included participants in the original sample (n = 7,179), those excluded (n = 8,381) from the original sample, and those from the imputed sample (n = 8,547) are shown in Additional file III. Generally, the excluded sample had higher proportions of people with Mexican American and ‘other’ race, low-income status, lower educational status, and less than full food security status. Excluded individuals were more likely to have sufficient sleep. Conversely, included respondents were more likely to identify as non-Hispanic (NH) White and had higher PIR, educational, and food security status. Included respondents were more likely to report sleep problems to a clinician. Characteristics do not appear to differ significantly between the multiply imputed and original samples.

Second, we assessed descriptive characteristics of the study population. We calculated frequencies of all co-variates (age, gender, and race/ethnicity) and outcomes (workday sleep and non-workday sleep) by individual exposure status (PIR, education, and FSSM category). Rao-Scott chi-square tests were used to calculate p-values for descriptive statistics. Third, we used Poisson regression with robust standard errors to model the relationship between socioeconomic indicators and sleep. This method allows for the efficient estimation of prevalence ratios [51, 52]. All analyses were weighted with the full-sample interview weights to produce estimates that are “representative of the civilian resident noninstitutionalized U.S. population”, considering the oversampling, nonresponse, and differences between the final sample and US population distribution [40]. Weighted Poisson regression was conducted for the primary outcomes ‘workday sleep’ and ‘non-workday sleep’. We repeated these models for each multiply imputed dataset and pooled results (prevalence ratios and 95% confidence intervals) using Rubin’s formula. We fitted separate models for each exposure (PIR, education, and FSSM) and outcome (workday and non-workday sleep). We started with unadjusted models and then created an adjusted model with age, gender, and race/ethnicity. We also conducted two secondary analyses. We stratified our adjusted model by gender, given the complexity of the gender and sleep relationship [53], and tested for the joint statistical significance of interaction terms by doing a nested model comparison. We also explored the patterning of excessive sleep by SES indicator by comparing those sleeping 7–9 h vs. those sleeping < 7 and ≥ 9 h. For this, we repeated our Poisson model above but excluded those with insufficient sleep for the analysis of normal vs. excessive sleep, and excluded those with excessive sleep for the analysis of normal vs. insufficient sleep.

SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) was used for all analyses.

Results

Characteristics by workday and non-workday sleep status are shown in Table I (characteristics for the original sample shown in Additional file IV). Adults aged 80 and above, females, NH white, NH Asian, and college-educated respondents were less likely to report insufficient workday sleep. NH Black individuals were the most likely to have insufficient workday sleep. Notably, workday sleep did not appear to differ significantly by PIR, while increasing FSSM seemed to correspond with lower proportions of insufficient workday sleep. In general, non-workday sleep was more likely to be sufficient compared to workday sleep; however, adults in the youngest age group, females, and Mexican American, NH white, and NH Asian individuals were less likely to have insufficient non-workday sleep. NH Black individuals were most likely to report insufficient sleep. Notably, increasing PIR, education, and FSSM all appeared to correlate with decreasing proportions of respondents with insufficient sleep status.

The results from the Poisson regression of insufficient workday sleep duration in the multiply imputed sample are shown in Table II; results from the original sample are found in Additional file V. Overall, we found that compared to high-income, college educated, and individuals with full food security, all other categories displayed higher prevalence of workday and non-workday insufficient sleep, with varying magnitudes and uncertainty around associations. We highlight four key findings. First, for poverty, we found that low-income individuals had 22% (95% CI 4 to 44%) and 108% (95% CI 61 to 167%) higher prevalence of insufficient sleep compared to high-income individuals in workday and non-workdays, adjusted for age, gender, and race/ethnicity. This association was consistent across the poverty spectrum for non-workdays, with some variations (i.e., near low-income individuals) for workdays. Second, for educational attainment, we found that all categories had higher prevalence of insufficient sleep compared to college educated individuals after adjustment for covariates, in either workdays or non-workdays. For example, compared to college educated individuals, we found that individuals without completed high school had 33% (95% CI13 to 57%) and 63% (95% CI 27 to 109%) higher adjusted prevalence of insufficient sleep on workdays and non-workdays, respectively. Third, for food security, we found that all individuals experiencing any degree of food insecurity had higher prevalence of poor sleep. For instance, those with very low food security had 50% (95% CI 24 to 82%) and 129% (95% CI 77 to 195%) higher adjusted prevalence of insufficient sleep on workdays and non-workdays. Last, in all cases, we found that the associations for non-workdays were stronger in magnitude but consistent in direction to that of workdays sleep.

We conducted two secondary analysis. First, in our adjusted analysis stratified by gender, we found that, in general, there were no major differences in associations by gender (see coefficients and p-values for the joint test of all interaction terms in Additional file VI). However, we did find more consistent associations between lower SES and lack of sufficient sleep among women, and positive associations were more extreme for women. Second, in our analysis exploring excessive sleep (Additional file VII), we found that the patterning of excessive sleep by SES indicator followed the same pattern as for insufficient sleep: individuals with lower SES had a higher adjusted prevalence of both insufficient and excessive sleep as compared to normal sleep duration.

Discussion

In this study exploring socioeconomic disparities in insufficient sleep duration among US adults, we highlight three key findings. First, socioeconomic status appears to predict insufficient sleep duration among US adults. Higher poverty, education, and food security all predict higher prevalences of insufficient workday and non-workday sleep. Second, these disparities were wider for non-workday sleep as compared to workday sleep. Third, we found no major differences by gender in these associations. These results increase our understanding of the current relationship between SES and sleep duration in the US adult population.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies, which have generally found associations between improved socioeconomic indicators and specific sleep disorders, including insomnia and OSA, as well as sleep outcomes such as duration, continuity, and latency [3, 23, 26, 27, 29]. SES is associated with a multitude of factors that can predict the ability of an individual to have good sleep health—such as access to resources, living and working conditions, neighborhood safety and psychosocial stressors and other predictors of sleep [28, 30]. Sleep and SES are both strongly related to health behaviors, such as alcohol and tobacco consumption, as well as a multitude of mental and physical health indicators and conditions [2, 19, 54, 55].

We observed varying prevalence ratios across the three SES variables, likely reflecting the variations underlying each indicator. For instance, poverty is not static and can vary by year or even month. PIR can reflect an individual’s access to resources and is often correlated with living and working conditions [56]. Our measure of PIR accounts for yearly, family income level and we found significant increases in insufficient sleep among those of low, near-low, and middle income. Separately, though previous research on education has been mixed [23, 27, 34, 35], we found that education level was a significant predictor of insufficient workday and non-workday sleep duration. Education is largely stable over an adult’s life and captures individuals not currently working. However, it may be less sensitive to overall SES, particularly given trends in the labor market and its narrower range [56]. Across almost all analyses, food security had the strongest association with sleep duration. This aligns with prior analyses that have found shorter sleep to correlate with very low food security [37, 57]. Food security likely affects sleep directly, as poor nutrition and inadequate calorie intake are risk factors for sleep difficulties. Indirectly, food insecurity increases psychological distress, chronic stress, rumination, and biologic mechanisms that inhibit sleep hygiene [37, 57].

We also found that for each socioeconomic indicator, there was a stronger association for non-workday than workday sleep. This contrasts with prior work that has found higher SES to correlate with shorter weekend oversleeping [55]. The bulk of the literature has not analyzed the two outcomes separately or has not accounted for shift or weekend workers, and to our knowledge no studies have analyzed the distinction using NHANES data. However, one study found larger differences between workday and non-workday sleep to be associated with socioeconomic disadvantage [58]. SES could account for this disparity through differential occupation, stress and coping, lifestyle factors, disparities in mental health conditions, and much more. For instance, those with lower SES may work less-regular hours, longer hours, or multiple jobs, resulting in less time on non-workdays for other tasks such as sleep. Neighborhood-level factors such as noise and crime may vary on weekends; additionally, those with lower SES may be more likely to face financial and other specific stressors which could interfere with sleep differentially [58]. Our research highlights the need to study this distinction.

Finally, we conducted two secondary analyses. First, we explored how the above-mentioned patterns varied by gender. We found no notable variations between males and females based on poverty status. However, lower educational status was associated with higher adjusted prevalence of insufficient workday and non-workday sleep, while lower food security had larger associations with insufficient non-workday sleep for females. Few studies have examined these differences. Further research on the potential interaction between gender and socioeconomic status, as well as on unique vulnerabilities and stressors faced by women of lower SES, is needed [59, 60]. Second, we conducted an analysis of excessive sleep (defined as 9 or more hours) and found, broadly, that lower SES was associated with increased prevalence of excessive sleep, similarly to insufficient sleep. Adjusted prevalence ratios were larger for excessive than insufficient sleep on workdays, while the reverse was true on non-workdays. Given the potential harms of long sleep duration [47, 48], and the notable variation between workdays and non-workdays shown here, future studies should examine these differences in relation to SES.These findings have implications for the general health of US adults. For instance, sleep may be a mediator between SES and mental health [18, 19]. More research is needed to establish whether sleep mediates socioeconomic and racial differences in physical health [23, 26]. It must also be noted that racial injustice may interact with socioeconomic disparities to affect sleep [25, 61]. While our descriptive results revealed that sleep varied significantly by race/ethnicity, studies with complex indicators of systemic and interpersonal discrimination could elucidate this interaction. More research is also needed to clarify barriers to healthy sleep among racialized groups and those with lower SES, including differential access to care and protective factors, to develop just policies and interventions [27].

We highlight several limitations. First, self-reported sleep data tends to overestimate the true duration; while this bias does not appear to vary race, women tend to report poorer sleep while objective measurements do not confirm this [61, 62]. It is also unclear whether discrepancies between subjectively and objectively measured sleep vary by SES. Thus, the effects seen here may be underestimated, and further research with objective measures, ideally measured longitudinally, of sleep variables is merited in the US population. Additional sleep outcomes, such as latency, efficiency, or restlessness, were also not measured by NHANES and would enrich our understanding of the relationship between SES and sleep. There may also be measurement error on the exposure, and it is important to note that these indicators only approximate SES. The FSSM has been validated to measure diet quality and quantity within the context of income among US adults, though responses may vary by gender [63]. Additionally, all SES indicators and sleep data was measured as self-reported, which may lead to same-source bias [64]. Our measure of gender only reflected one dimension (biological sex); other dimensions of gender (gender identity, perceived gender, internalized gender stigma or discrimination, etc.) [65] may also be relevant modifiers. However, we have used biological sex as collected by NHANES to proxy gender, given the context of social inequities addressed by this paper and different culturally-relevant norms and roles that may relate to disparities in sleep [66]. Furthermore, and given the low response rate [67], individuals who were willing and able to participate may have had differential exposure and outcome characteristics compared to the general population, but the robust survey design of NHANES can support the generalizability of our findings to the US non-institutionalized population. A similar issue may arise with missing data, which we addressed using multiple imputation assuming missingness at random, which may not hold for SES indicators such as income. Last, the cross-sectional nature of this study is a significant limitation, as it precludes analysis of causation or temporality, although our intent was not to establish any causal link. There is also the potential of reverse causality here; sleep is known to affect job performance, for instance, and therefore could influence SES [12]. This would not be an issue with education, which is a more stable indicator, but may affect income and food security.

Conclusion

This study examined SES indicators and sleep duration in a nationally representative sample of the US adult population. We investigated workday and non-workday sleep in the US population separately and found notable differences, particularly that differences in sleep by SES indicator varied more for non-workday sleep. These results have important implications for the sleep health of US adults broadly and demonstrate significant demographic and socioeconomic disparities that may contribute to inequitable distributions of sleep-related outcomes like chronic conditions and mental health. Future research should objectively assess these disparities in nationally representative samples and establish potential mediators or interactions. Interventions must be studied and implemented to reduce these disparities.
