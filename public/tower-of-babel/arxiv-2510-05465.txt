VAL-Bench: Measuring value alignment in Language Models
Aman Gupta, Denny O’Shea, Fazl Barez

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used for tasks where outputs shape human decisions, so it is critical to test whether their responses reflect consistent human values. Existing benchmarks mostly track refusals or predefined safety violations, but these only check rule compliance and do not reveal whether a model upholds a coherent value system when facing controversial real-world issues. We introduce the V alue AL ignment Bench mark ( VAL-Bench ) 1 1 1 Our benchmark code and data can be found here: https://github.com/amangup/VAL-Bench . , which evaluates whether models maintain a stable value stance across paired prompts that frame opposing sides of public debates. VAL-Bench consists of 115K such pairs from Wikipedia’s controversial sections. A well-aligned model should express similar underlying views regardless of framing, which we measure using an LLM-as-judge to score agreement or divergence between paired responses. Applied across leading open- and closed-source models, the benchmark reveals large variation in alignment and highlights trade-offs between safety strategies (e.g., refusals) and more expressive value systems. By providing a scalable, reproducible benchmark, VAL-Bench enables systematic comparison of how reliably LLMs embody human values.



1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are now being evaluated and adopted in settings where their outputs shape human decisions, from classroom teaching (
Appel et al. 2025
,
Kaufman et al. 2025
,
Freeman 2025
) to medical applications (
Chatterji et al. 2025
,
Poon et al. 2025
) and public administration
(
OECD 2025
)
. Because of this reach, it is not enough for models to avoid clear safety violations; they must also apply human values consistently across contexts. Current safety benchmarks mostly measure toxicity (
Hartvigsen et al. 2022
) or high-consensus risk dimensions (
Liang et al. 2022
,
Wang et al. 2023
), but these only test rule compliance. They do not reveal whether a model upholds a coherent value system when confronted with controversial real-world issues. Misaligned systems risk not only giving unsafe advice but also spreading contradictory or incoherent value judgments, which can undermine trust or even be adopted by users themselves
(
Barez & Torr 2023
;
Hubinger et al. 2024
;
Denison et al. 2024
)
.

Measuring alignment is difficult.
Existing benchmarks mostly test refusals or predefined safety violations, which check rule compliance. Recent work has highlighted vulnerabilities that persist even after alignment training
(
Hubinger et al. 2024
;
Denison et al. 2024
)
, and introduced benchmarks for specific risks such as data poisoning or reliability
(
Fu et al. 2025
;
Ghosh et al. 2025
)
. However, systematic evaluation of whether models apply values consistently across different framings remains missing.

We introduce the
V
alue
AL
ignment
Bench
mark (
VAL-Bench
), the first benchmark that evaluates whether LLMs maintain a stable value stance across prompts framed from differing sides of the same issue. VAL-Bench consists of 115K paired prompts mined from Wikipedia’s controversial sections
(
Wikimedia Enterprise 2024
)
, spanning politics, civil rights, intellectual property, and more. Using an LLM-as-a-judge to measure agreement between response pairs, we systematically assess models’ value alignment consistency. Our results show large differences between models: Claude models achieve alignment scores almost three times as high as GPT models, with refusal strategies driving much of the variation. This highlights trade-offs between alignment and expressive value systems.

Figure 1:
Illustration of how VAL-bench assesses alignment. When asked to support opposing views, misaligned AI systems often yield, whereas aligned systems maintain consistency. Both response pairs shown are generated by recent, popular LLMs.

This design has several advantages that make VAL-Bench distinctive as an alignment benchmark:

1.

Grounded and comprehensive:
prompts are drawn from real-world controversial debates across diverse domains, ensuring broad coverage.

2.

Objective and pluralism-friendly:
evaluation measures agreement between paired responses rather than judging “desirable” values. Neutral stances count as consistent, and different models can be aligned in themselves even if they disagree with each other.

3.

Scalable and updatable:
automated construction from Wikipedia makes the dataset large in scale and easy to refresh as societal debates evolve.

Contributions.

•

We introduce
VAL-Bench
, a scalable benchmark for measuring whether LLMs maintain consistent value stances across opposing framings.

•

We construct a dataset of 115K paired prompts from Wikipedia’s controversial sections and derive a taxonomy of values expressed in model outputs.

•

We benchmark leading open- and closed-source models, revealing substantial variation in alignment and systematic trade-offs between refusals and value expressivity.

2
Related Work

Value Alignment.

Early work on measuring value alignment
(
Barez & Torr 2023
)
emphasized the importance of testing whether models reflect human values.
Other recent studies have shown that models can exhibit inconsistent or reward-driven behaviors, such as sycophancy or reward tampering
(
Denison et al. 2024
)
, further motivating the need for benchmarks that directly evaluate value consistency.

Datasets of Human Values and Alignment.

Recent datasets examine how human values are represented in AI systems.
PRISM
(
Kirk et al. 2024
)
collects alignment data from participants in 75 countries, highlighting both diversity of perspectives and frequent discussion of controversial topics such as gender, religion, and politics.
Values in the Wild
(
Huang et al. 2025
)
analyzes over 300K human–AI conversations, building a taxonomy of 3,000 values and showing that models often emphasize helpfulness, professionalism, and transparency.
While these works catalogue values demonstrated in general human-AI interactions, VAL-Bench differs by testing whether models
apply values consistently
specifically on complex real-world issues.

Safety and Robustness Benchmarks.

Large-scale evaluations such as DecodingTrust
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
, HolisticEval
(
Liang et al. 2022
)
, and SALAD-bench
(
Li et al. 2024
)
measure toxicity, bias, or refusal rates in LLMs.
These studies highlight the difficulty of robust evaluation, often depending on fixed categories or external APIs.
Domain-specific analyses show similar risks:
Gourabathina et al. 2025
report that subtle changes in tone can alter medical advice, and
Cheung et al. 2025
find that LLMs are far more framing-sensitive than humans in moral dilemmas.

3
Dataset

In this section, we describe how the
VAL-bench
dataset was created. As mentioned in the abstract, we used the English content of Wikipedia, specifically the structured Wikipedia dataset published by Wikimedia on Hugging Face (
Wikimedia Enterprise 2024
. We followed the following steps to get 114,745 pairs of prompts:

1.

We filtered the Wikipedia sections using common terms used to designate controversial sections in Wikipedia, such as
Criticism
and
Scandals
.

2.

We prompted open-weights model
Gemma-3-27B-it
(
Team 2025
) to assess the
awareness
of the issue, i.e., relevance of the issue to the society. We used that to filter out sections that didn’t represent a divergent issue.

3.

We further prompted
Gemma-3-27B-it
to extract the pair of abductive reasoning prompts from the text of a single controversial section in Wikipedia, each starting with
Explain why
. Each pair of prompts is referred to as
for prompt
and
against prompt
; there is no moral
direction
attached to this naming.

The prompts used are shared in the appendix.

4
Evaluation

We formulate the process of evaluation as follows. The benchmark dataset
\displaystyle{\mathbb{D}}=\{(c_{i},p_{i}^{+},p_{i}^{-})\}_{i=1}^{N}
consists of
N
issues described with an issue description
c_{i}
and paired prompts representing the
for prompt
and
against prompt
. We use an LM
\mathcal{M}
to generate the response dataset
\displaystyle{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{M}}=\{\mathcal{M}(p_{i}^{+}),\mathcal{M}(p_{i}^{-})\}_{i=1}^{N}
. To measure alignment, we use an evaluator
\mathcal{E}
, defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}:(c_{i},p_{i}^{+},r_{i}^{+},p_{i}^{-},r_{i}^{-})

\displaystyle\mapsto\bigl(\sigma_{i},\psi(r_{i}^{+}),\nu(r_{i}^{+}),\psi(r_{i}^{-}),\nu(r_{i}^{-})\bigr)

(1)

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}

\displaystyle\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}

(2)

\displaystyle\psi(r)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $r$ is a refusal response},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(3)

\displaystyle\nu(r)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $r$ is a no-information response},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(4)

Where
\sigma_{i}=-2
implies
complete disagreement
and
\sigma_{i}=2
implies
complete agreement
between generated responses. The
PAC
alignment scores dataset
\displaystyle{\mathbb{S}}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{S}}=\{\,s_{i}\,\}_{i=1}^{N},\quad s_{i}=\begin{cases}\sigma_{i},&\text{if }\psi(r_{i}^{+})=0\text{ and }\psi(r_{i}^{-})=0,\\[6.0pt]
0,&\text{if }\psi(r_{i}^{+})+\psi(r_{i}^{-})=1,\\[6.0pt]
2,&\text{if }\psi(r_{i}^{+})+\psi(r_{i}^{-})=2.\end{cases}

(5)

The score definition in equation
5
recognizes that refusals (responses stating that they can’t provide an opinion or take a stance) are commonly used for achieving alignment. For pairs with no-information responses (responses stating that there is insufficient information), we use the evaluator computed score
\sigma_{i}
.

4.1
Metrics

We use
P
airwise
A
lignment
C
onsistency (
PAC
) as our main metric. For each input, we prompt the model twice: once with a “for” framing and once with an “against” framing. If the model answers consistently (e.g., supporting one side and opposing the other), it scores high. If the answers contradict each other or flip position, it scores low. This approach fits value alignment because there is often no single “correct” answer to compare against, but on the other hand, it’s possible for a model to score high while prioritizing values that many regard as undesirable. PAC works across many domains, avoids cherry-picking single outputs, and produces a clear score between 0 and 100 that can be calibrated across evaluators.

Table
3
describes the notation for all the metrics we list in the results section. We do not define a preference for ideal
NINF
rate, since both overuse (becoming a substitute for refusals) and underuse (causing hallucinations) of no-information responses are problematic. Instead, we report it as an auxiliary metric, enabling future work to interpret its desirability in context.

4.3
Evaluator ablations

We ran two ablations to ensure the accuracy of evaluation scores. Since the ordering of information in the input to LLMs matters, we conducted the evaluation with the paired responses presented in reverse order. To measure the deviation, we computed RMSE using both sets (normal and reversed orderings) of
PAC
,
REF
, and
NINF
metrics, resulting in values of
2.28
,
0.30
, and
0.57
, respectively. We report the mean of these two runs for each model in the results.

In another ablation, we generated paraphrased versions of the responses in the
unaligned-0
and
aligned-100
calibration datasets (using an LLM instructed to make the responses concise and increase readability), and recomputed the metrics. This resulted in a
PAC
score
increase
of
3.71
and
3.63
for
unaligned-0
and
aligned-100
datasets, respectively. This indicates that the evaluator is relatively insensitive to changes in the style of expression.

5.3
Values demonstrated in responses

Using the value annotations of the prompts (Section
3.2
), we directly analyze the values models
demonstrate
. Specifically, we use an LLM to annotate each response with the values it demonstrates from among those
inspired
by the prompt. Remember, this value expectation is implicit - the models demonstrate these values naturally.

To understand a model’s
value system
, we measure
deviation
in the frequency distribution of
expected values
(extracted from prompts) and
demonstrated values
, using the established value taxonomy. The expected distribution, derived from both for and against prompts, represents an inconsistent value system, and high deviation indicates a well-defined value system. We plot the Pearson residuals (Figure
2
) for the ten highest residual values (denoting over-representation) and the ten least residual values (denoting under-representation).

Even with
glm-4.5-air-base
, a pretrained model with no post-training, the responses show a shift towards many morally good values (
prioritizing accountability
,
prioritizing equity
) and against many morally bad values (
evading accountability
,
marginalization
). But, the list of over-represented values includes some morally ambiguous ones, like
pragmatism over principle
and
utilitarianism
.
gpt-5-chat
’s ten highest residual values also include
pragmatism over principle
.
claude-opus-4.1
seems to have the largest absolute residuals among the three, while having subtly different values in the top ten highest/least residuals.

suggests
a bias toward over-prioritizing prominent issues in datasets used in alignment training of these models.

6
Discussion

Our VAL-Bench results highlight the dependence of alignment on the internal choices of each frontier model builder. OpenAI models consistently score low while avoiding refusals. Claude shows significantly higher alignment alonside higher refusal or no-information response rates. This dichotomy clearly illustrates the alignment-expressivity tradeoff, or
alignment tax

(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
. Qwen3
(
Yang et al. 2025
)
appears to pursue alignment through explicit opinions with low refusal rates. Given AI alignment’s societal importance and varying interpretations among frontier labs, we need a common, open process for creating human-annotated alignment datasets.

6.1
Training for value alignment

All LLMs go through a large-scale
pretraining
phase with the cross-entropy objective that can be derived as the Maximum Likelihood Estimator. Modern LLMs are very effective at reducing this loss
(
Dubey et al. 2024
)
, thereby modeling the broad distribution of viewpoints present in their training data, leading to unaligned models as illustrated by
glm-4.5-air-base
’s
PAC
score. It is impractical to “clean” the extraordinarily large pretraining datasets (up to 36T tokens, as per
Yang et al. 2025
) to encode a consistent value system.

Alignment is commonly pursued through RL methods like RLHF (
Ouyang et al. 2022
) or preference-based methods like DPO
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
.
Im & Li 2024
shows that the effectiveness of DPO depends heavily on large, diverse datasets, which helps explain why organizations invest in costly human feedback datasets (
Lee et al. 2023
,
(
Bai et al. 2022
)
). These methods require extensive datasets demonstrating correct behavior across many situations, and our results highlight the challenge of scaling value alignment while maintaining expressivity.

6.1.1
Belief consistency in language model training

In humans, beliefs often underpin values
(
Schwartz et al. 2012
)
. Thus, achieving
belief consistency
also entails, at least in part, achieving value consistency. A training algorithm that is compatible with belief revision while maintaining consistency, like the AGM belief revision theory
(
Alchourrón et al. 1985
)
, could, in principle, reduce the need for large and diverse datasets with explicit examples of alignment behavior; the consistent belief system would be formed during the self-supervised training phase itself. The variation and inconsistency of beliefs in pretraining datasets could actually inform the belief entrenchment (e.g., “vaccines are good for society” is strongly held, whereas “technology is an inherent good” is weakly held), as proposed in the AGM theory
(
Makinson & Gärdenfors 2015
)
. However, currently, we lack operators that measure logical relationships like contradiction or consequence in neural representation spaces
(
Garcez & Lamb 2023
)
, preventing direct implementation of such a method.

7
Conclusion

We introduced
VAL-Bench
, a large-scale benchmark testing whether LLMs demonstrate consistent values across prompts with opposing framings of controversial issues. Unlike prior benchmarks focused on predefined safety violations like toxicity, VAL-Bench directly measures value expression coherence. Leading open and closed-weights models show substantial variation in value alignment consistency; models relying heavily on refusals often avoid expressing values, while more expressive models risk inconsistency across framings. These trade-offs highlight a core challenge in alignment: safety mechanisms can mask, but not resolve, whether models embody stable value systems. By grounding evaluation in real-world controversies and enabling scalable automated assessment, VAL-Bench provides a reproducible tool for tracking value alignment progress and building models with consistent value systems. Future work could extend VAL-Bench beyond English Wikipedia, incorporate more universal value taxonomies, and investigate mechanistic causes of inconsistency.

Ethics Statements

Disclosure of LLM use

We utilize OpenAI ChatGPT, mainly GPT-5 and sometimes GPT-4o, and Anthropic Claude Sonnet 4 to (1) aid and polish writing, and (2) retrieve and discover related literature. We manually verify all related works originally suggested by ChatGPT or Claude. No full paragraphs are completely generated by these LLMs.

Ethics Statement

We propose VAL-Bench to improve alignment and safety evaluation of language models. We do not anticipate direct negative ethical impacts beyond the usual risks of benchmark misuse, which we mitigate by open release and clear intended-use documentation.

To ensure the reproducibility of our results, we made our data, code and benchmark publically available.
