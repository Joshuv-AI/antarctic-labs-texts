Use of passive integrated transponder tags to assess movements and demography of Townsend’s Big-eared Bat (Corynorhinus townsendii) in Colorado
Authors: Daniel J Neubaum, Jeremy L Siemers, Phillip Nyland, Kellen M Keisling, Terutaka Funabashi, Burton Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDetermining bat seasonal roost use, demographic rates (fidelity and survival), and movements are necessary metrics to rigorously evaluate population status and trends. Little is known about these metrics for Townsend’s big-eared bats (Corynorhinus townsendii), a state and federal sensitive species, in Colorado. Therefore, our objectives were to monitor daily use of a large maternity colony (∼ 900 bats) inside a gated mine from 2011 to 2025 by marking bats with PIT tags (n = 840), evaluate their tolerance of the disturbance, assess seasonal patterns, and estimate demographic rates for this species. Bats tagged as adults were regularly detected to reach 10 yr of age, with at least 1 individual reaching a minimum of 15 yr of age. Despite concerns of capture and tagging disturbance for this species, 90% of individuals were detected again at the maternity colony at least once during the same summer of tagging. We detected 46% of females and 41% of males at least once across subsequent years following tagging. Variation by age was apparent with juveniles detected during at least 1 subsequent maternity season at lower percentages than adults. Numbers of tagged individuals present varied seasonally but the pattern of seasonal variation was consistent across years. We confirmed autumn movement to a known hibernaculum in a cave 48 km north of the maternity roost for 31 tagged bats, indicating that summer colonies fragment for winter rather than moving as an intact group. These data represent the first documented summer-to-winter movement of PIT-tagged C. townsendii and suggest that short-distance, localized migrations to hibernacula are an over-wintering strategy for this species where resources permit. We documented loss of PIT tags by C. townsendii and accounted for it by generating minimum tag retention estimates. Apparent annual survival for adult females across all years was 0.74 (95% CI = 0.60–0.87), similar to that for other PIT tag studies of bat species. Adult females and juvenile males showed high site fidelity with yearly return probabilities above 0.95, whereas estimates for juvenile females were lower (0.80). Our data show that PIT tags can be used to mark and monitor C. townsendii safely, providing managers with rigorous demographic data that can guide management actions.Keywords: apparent survival, fidelity, hibernacula, maternity, movements, passive integrated transponder, tag loss, Townsend’s big-eared bat

Monitoring trends in populations of declining, sensitive species is critical to effective management (O’Shea and Bogan 2003). The need for such assessments is more urgent than ever given the large-scale declines of bat species currently underway in North America due to stressors such as the fungal disease white-nose syndrome (Frick et al. 2010a; Brooks 2011; O’Keefe et al. 2019) and climate change (Davy et al. 2022). Bats also face numerous other threats with the potential to cause population declines (O’Shea et al. 2016; Hammerson et al. 2017; Frick et al. 2020). Accurately assessing population status and trends is difficult without the use of rigorous methods such as mark-recapture. Developing rigorous estimates of survival in bats can be particularly challenging given biological traits such as their cryptic nature, small body size, nocturnal activity, and high mobility (Kunz 2003; O’Shea et al. 2004). However, the need for monitoring population trends using rigorous methodology is regularly mentioned as a high priority given its ability to influence management and species listings (Jung et al. 2014; Neubaum et al. 2017).

Several methods to mark bats have been used in field applications over the last century with varying degrees of success. Historic use of bands for mark-recapture efforts of bats provided the first estimates of survival for some species but may have led to population declines due to improper design and application that created a risk of injury (O’Shea et al. 2004; Ellison 2008; Lobato-Bail′on et al. 2023). Bands also require a follow-up in-person observation for the individual to be documented. These limitations to banding may generate biased estimates of survival and associated population trends. Cryan et al. (2004) used stable isotope analysis to investigate seasonal movements, but these techniques cannot be used to assess population trends. Use of passive integrated transponder (PIT) tags, while initially invasive to implant, offers the potential for a permanent mark that can be detected any time the animal passes a continuously powered antenna/reader.

Use of PIT tags to mark bats at maternity roosts and hibernacula, where bats show high fidelity, can enhance detection probabilities and provide more accurate survival estimates than banding. Ellison et al. (2007a) showed that PIT readers have higher daily and annual capture probabilities than conventional techniques, resulting in survival estimates with higher precision. PIT tagging has been used to assess a number of management questions including impacts of contaminants on the survival of juveniles (Frick et al. 2007); movement, survival, and population growth rates of bats in urban settings (Ellison et al. 2007b; O’Shea et al. 2011); and age-specific survival (Frick et al. 2010b; O’Shea et al. 2010).

One species that lends itself to the use of stationary PIT reader technology is Townsend’s Big-eared Bat (Corynorhinus townsendii) because of the high site fidelity it can exhibit at maternity roosts (Humphrey and Kunz 1976). Townsend’s big-eared bat is considered a sensitive species at the federal level by the U.S. Forest Service and Bureau of Land Management, and is a species of concern for most western states (Gruver and Keinath 2006). In Colorado, C. townsendii is listed as a species of concern and considered a Tier 2 species in the State Wildlife Action Plan (Colorado Parks and Wildlife 2025) due to threats such as mining activity, disturbance, and disease. Often tied to mines and caves year-round, the species is known to roost suspended from ceilings and walls of open cavities versus fissures and crevices used by other bats (O’Shea et al. 2018).

The largest aggregation of C. townsendii in Colorado, upward of 1,000 individuals, occurs at a mine in the west-central portion of the state, where adult females form a summer maternity colony and bachelor males roost solo. The roost is situated on public lands and protected by a gate designed to meet bat specifications (Navo 2001), providing an ideal location for long-term monitoring due to its stable setting. Previous efforts have been made at estimating demographics (e.g., survival) for this species on several occasions. Pearson et al. (1952) banded yearling and adult females at a maternity colony in California, estimating survival at 80% for adult female bats, but this assessment did not consider emigration. Ellison (2010) used modern analytical methods that remove bias to assess survival of banded bats from data collected at cave hibernacula in Washington from 1964 to 1975—using model-averaging techniques, estimates of annual apparent survival ranged from 54% to 76%. Banding injuries known to occur at the site were suggested as a disturbance leading to estimates lower than those observed by Pearson et al. (1952). In cases where the species has exhibited lower roost fidelity (Sherwin et al. 2000, 2003; Ives 2015), the continuous detection ability of the PIT reader technology should be more sensitive than haphazard or periodic in-person surveys of individuals.

Despite the advantages of PIT tags, C. townsendii is regarded as being a bat species that is particularly sensitive to disturbance at roost sites (Pearson et al. 1952; Pierson et al. 1999). Over the past several decades, anecdotal observations have suggested declining numbers, generally at roost sites—despite the lack of rigorous trend data—and drawn notable attention to the population status for the western subspecies of Townsend’s big-eared bat, C. t. pallescens (O’Shea et al. 2018). However, Weller et al. (2014) modeled winter count data at caves at Lava Beds National Monument in California and estimated a positive annual growth rate of 1.8%. During this same period, improvements on gating design at abandoned mines and caves were widely implemented across the range of this species (Sherwin et al. 2003, 2009). In addition, the species is thought to avoid mist nets, which accounts for a lack of capture records (O’Shea et al. 2018). These lines of evidence suggest that population trends of C. townsendii may not be in decline and rigorous assessments of demographics would provide clarification on population trends.

In addition to demographic rates, species movement and dispersal abilities undoubtedly affect survival and should influence management plans. With bat species, movement dynamics can be complicated, driven by both male and female dispersal (Moussy et al. 2013). Fidelity to roosts may vary depending on the season and roost type as well (Pearson et al. 1952; Dobkin et al. 1995; Ives 2015). In Colorado, C. townsendii uses abandoned mines (Ingersoll et al. 2010; Hayes et al. 2011) and caves (Neubaum 2018) as maternity roosts and hibernacula. However, the distance of movements individuals make to winter roosts and the seasonality of bat use at maternity roosts, by this and most bat species, are unclear. Dates of parturition can vary widely for this species depending on elevation, ranging between late April and late July (Pearson et al. 1952). Gaining insight into such life history traits and patterns would further assist managers in assessing the vulnerability of this species and allow management actions to be tailored accordingly.

The lack of both demographic rates and life history traits make management difficult for this species. Gruver and Keinath (2006) prepared an extensive conservation assessment of C. townsendii and acknowledged the lack of any recent efforts to estimate apparent survival of this species given conservation concerns. Although Ellison (2010) estimated survival for C. townsendii, recapture probabilities were limited by banding data. The objectives of our study were to: (1) evaluate the tolerance of disturbance-sensitive C. townsendii at a maternity roost; (2) assess seasonal patterns of PIT-tagged C. townsendii; and (3) estimate demographic rates for this species. We hypothesized that PIT tag techniques can be applied safely for this species, resulting in higher apparent survival estimates than previously noted. Pearson et al. (1952) indicated that maternity colonies for this species do not stay intact when dispersing to hibernacula. Given that the largest maternity colony and hibernacula for this species in Colorado are only 48 km apart, we wanted to investigate the plausibility of the colony making this seasonal movement as a more cohesive group. In addition, this work monitors an important colony of C. townsendii in Colorado for potential population declines as Pseudogymnoascus destructans (Pd) and the associated disease white-nose syndrome moves into the region.

Methods

Sample area

We investigated seasonal movements and apparent survival of C. townsendii at an abandoned mine used as a summer maternity and bachelor roost (EM Mine) along the Crystal River Valley and a hibernaculum (Hubbard Cave) along the Colorado River in west-central Colorado from 2011 to 2020 (Fig. 1). EM Mine sits at an elevation of 2,174 m, consists of multiple adits occurring on 2 levels, and intercepts a natural cave system that exhibits geothermal aspects. Temperatures within the roost are buffered from those externally by these subterranean and geothermal characteristics, ranging between 22 °C and 27 °C. Roost locations used by aggregated female bats in summer and early autumn are situated in or near the geothermal cave portions of the roost, which may reach temperatures as high as 31 °C. Adult male bats are presumed to use separate mine adits situated at the cooler end of the temperature range. Hubbard Cave is a large limestone cave with 3 gated entrances that maintains cool temperatures year-round, ranging from 0 °C to 5 °C, making it a suitable hibernaculum for >600 Townsend’s big-eared bats.

Study locations of the summer maternity colony (EM Mine) and winter hibernaculum (Hubbard Cave) used by PIT-tagged Townsend’s big-eared bats (Corynorhinus townsendii) in the Crystal and Colorado River watersheds, west-central Colorado from 2011 to 2025.

The region is characterized by high peaks and deep drainages cut by rivers and streams. Elevations near the mine range from 2,117 m along the Crystal River to 3,950 m near the summit of Mount Sopris directly adjacent to the river valley. The weather station located in the Crystal River Valley, at 2,038 m elevation, recorded a mean annual temperature of 8 °C (ranging from –26°C to 36 °C) from 2009 to 2017. Mean annual precipitation in the study area totals 233 cm with accumulations occurring primarily in the form of snow (195 cm) from October through May (Weather Underground, https://www.wunderground.com/personal-weather-station/dashboard? ID=KCOCARBO1, accessed 31 October 2019). Much like other valleys in the Rocky Mountain Region, the weather patterns for the Crystal River Valley consist of severe winters from October until April and months free from hard frosts between May and September. The valley floor near the mine, which includes the riparian corridor along the Crystal River, was historically converted for agricultural purposes but has been allowed to revert to native meadows and is now managed as open space by Pitkin County. The landscape abruptly transitions to steep flanks of large peaks. Trees and shrubs include cottonwood (Populus spp.), Pinyon Pine (Pinus edulis), juniper (Juniperus spp.), and Gambel Oak (Quercus gambelii) at lower elevations (1,890–2,600 m). Douglas-fir (Pseudotsuga menziesii) and Quaking Aspen (Populus tremuloides) are found at elevations above 2,400 m, and Englemann Spruce (Picea engelmannii) and Subalpine Fir (Abies lasiocarpa) above 2,600 m.

Capture, marking, and data collection

Abandoned mine surveys identified a maternity colony of C. townsendii in 2004 and triggered the initial monitoring efforts. We used infrared (Model HDR-CX560V, Sony Electronics, San Diego, CA) and thermal imaging (Model Q1921-E w/19-mm lens, Axis Communications, Lund, Sweden) camera surveys conducted between 2004 and 2018 to estimate colony size. Capture and tagging methods followed the Colorado Parks and Wildlife’s Capture and Handling Guidelines for Bats (Neubaum and Jackson 2015, ACUC No. 04-2016), which were based on recommendations from the American Society of Mammalogists (Sikes et al. 2016) and American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA 2020). Capture and handling work was conducted under a Colorado Parks and Wildlife scientific collection license CPW004. In 2011, we captured 98 bats outside the mine entrance during summer using a harp trap and placed them in individual cloth holding bags, as part of a pilot effort to see if Townsend’s big-eared bats could handle the stress of tagging without disturbing the colony. We individually tagged bats by subdermal insertion of 12 mm passive integrated transponders (PIT tags; AVID, Norco, CA, United States) on the middorsal side over the lower lumbar region as described by Wimsatt et al. (2005) without the use of anesthesia (Ellison et al. 2006) ≤ 6 hr after capture. We sealed the insertion point by pulling the wound taught to bring the edges together, applied a drop of liquid super glue (Loctite Super Glue, CA), and held the bat for a few minutes to insure drying of the glue before releasing. We assessed our disturbance of the colony by conducting emergence counts to ensure that colony size had not declined and used internal searches of the roost (described below) to identify dead bats if mortalities had occurred. Emergence counts suggested that the colony remained at levels previously measured and mortalities were not confirmed so the project proceeded with efforts to assess demographic metrics.

Subsequent efforts to capture and tag bats between 2014 and 2016 occurred twice each summer using the same techniques but with each individual scanned using a handheld scanner (Power Tracker III, AVID, Norco, CA, United States and 601 Handheld Reader, Biomark, Boise, ID, United States) upon capture to ensure they were not already tagged. We immediately released recaptured individuals and placed untagged bats in individual holding bags to be aged, weighed, and sexed after a settling period of ∼5–20 min. Bats were aged (adults vs juveniles) based on fusion of the phalangeal epiphysis (Brunet-Rossinni and Wilkinson 2009) and tagged using a different type of 12 mm PIT tag (GPT12 Pre-load Sterile, Biomark, Boise, ID, United States).

In 2014, we placed a 0.6 m square antenna attached to a solar-powered PIT reader (FS2001, Biomark, Boise, ID, United States) at the mine portal to passively monitor tagged bats continuously at the roost through 2025. This portal is the only opening where bats can enter or exit the mine. The PIT reader scanned continuously for 24 h a day throughout the entire year. The reader stored date, time, and the unique identification number for tagged bats as they passed by or through the antenna. Data were exported from the reader onsite using a computer, weekly throughout the summer when activity was high, and monthly to bi-monthly when activity was low during the transition and winter seasons. Records of detection were imported into a database where they could be sorted for analysis.

We searched for PIT tags shed by bats in the mine on 3 occasions during winter in January 2016, February 2017, and February 2018, when bats were absent. Searches involved using handheld readers to detect, and magnets to collect, shed PIT tags from the mine floor, focusing on areas below roost sites as indicated by sizeable guano piles. Because the mine has 1 opening and a uniform, stable dirt floor throughout most passages, we were consistently able to find shed tags.

To investigate movements to hibernacula, we scanned for PIT-tagged individuals during 6 winter visits to Hubbard Cave (February of 2012, 2015, 2017, 2018, 2021, and 2024), Colorado’s largest bat hibernaculum, populated by C. townsendii and located 48 km north of the maternity roost (Fig. 1). We used a PIT reader (FS2001, Biomark, Boise, ID, United States) with a 3.7 m cable and racket antenna affixed to an extendable painter’s pole to scan torpid bats hanging from high ceilings and handheld units for individuals within arm’s reach. We did not touch or disturb torpid bats while scanning.

Data analysis

We estimated the minimum tag retention period for bats tagged from 2014 to 2016 that were known to have lost tags between 2014 and 2020. Bats tagged in 2011 were not considered in this analysis; early reader data were inconsistent because the metal mine gate created signal interferences that required equipment refinement, and tag loss was high for bats tagged the first year. If we assume a relatively high detection rate by the reader (i.e., a tagged bat that passes through the antenna is most likely recorded), and that individuals enter/exit the mine on a nightly basis during the active season, and must pass the reader to do so, the last date a bat that lost its tag was detected should be close to the date the bat lost its tag inside the mine.

A total of 840 bats were tagged during 7 sessions over 4 yr (2011, 2014–2016; Table 1). We estimated apparent survival and fidelity only for bats tagged during the summer maternity periods in 2014–2016 (n = 742) and regularly monitored until 2020. Data were downloaded irregularly after 2020, with records collected through to 2025 that referenced only for evidence of longevity for all tagged bats (2011, 2014–2016). For this analysis, we excluded the 98 bats tagged in 2011 because their monitoring was still being refined, as well as 118 bats tagged later and known to have lost their tags, leaving mark-recapture data for 624 individuals. We analyzed these mark-recapture data using the Huggins robust design model (Huggins 1989, 1991) in Program MARK (White and Burnham 1999). Data recorded by the PIT reader were compiled into 3 monthly (June, July, and August) secondary samples per yearly primary sample. We assumed closure, as it relates to survival, for 3 mo during the peak of the maternity season (June–August). Bats were separated into 4 groups based on sex and age class (adult or juvenile). Juveniles transitioned to adults 1 yr after capture.

Our general modeling approach was to first model capture probability (p) and recapture probability (c) and then use the best parameterization of p and c to model apparent survival (φ), temporary movement probabilities—differentiated based upon whether the animal was (𝛾″) or was not (𝛾′) present in the study area during the previous sampling occasion—and site fidelity (the probability of remaining at the site given the bat was present during the last sampling occasion (1–𝛾″)). We developed 8 models of p and c while using highly parameterized (including time and group) structures of φ, 𝛾′, and 𝛾″. We only used models with additive relationships of time (year) and group for p and c. We then used the most parsimonious structure of p and c from the initial model set to model φ, 𝛾′, and 𝛾″. Capture and recapture probabilities were modeled using all combinations of time (year), sex, age, and as constant. Capture probability was set to equal recapture probability in all models. We compared models using Akaike’s Information Criterion for small sample size (AICc) and model weights (Burnham and Anderson 2002). The most parsimonious model for capture and recapture probability varied by age, sex, and year (primary sampling occasion) with capture and recapture probabilities equal: p = c(age+sex+year). To model φ, 𝛾″, and 𝛾′, we used the p = c(age+sex+year) structure for 80 models. Covariates for modeling survival included combinations of sex and age, body condition index (body mass/forearm length) when tagged (Pearce et al. 2008), and variation over time. We only used additive relationships with year when considering time. We modeled 𝛾′ as constant and 𝛾″ as constant or varying by sex and age. Model-averaged estimates of φ and 𝛾″ were calculated using all models in the candidate set that had AICc weight (wi) > 0.

Results

Video surveillance using infrared and thermal imaging cameras between 2005 and 2013 estimated an average initial colony size of 904 (range = 538–1336) bats. Counts to assess tolerance of C. townsendii to capture and tagging disturbance during 2013–2018 ranged from 701 to 1,336 bats depending on the date of survey (pre- or postvolancy) and averaged 1,086 bats. Bats continued to use the maternity site despite the capture, tagging, and monitoring disturbance, with 90% (666/742) of individuals tagged in 2014–2016 detected at least once during any year following tagging. We assessed longevity by considering all years of tagged bats. Seven bats tagged as adults in 2011 were detected by readers in 2020, making those individuals ≥ 10 yr old. In postanalysis years (2021–2025) when we decreased efforts to monitor the PIT reader, 1 female tagged in 2011 as an adult was detected during a winter survey in 2024, making this individual at least 14 yr old (Table 2). In 2025, we detected one additional female tagged in 2011 at the maternity site making this bat a minimum of 15 yr old.

We performed a Pearson’s chi-squared test with Yates’ correction to compare detections following tagging of different groups. Detections varied by sex, with 92% of females (471/514) detected again at least once during any year compared to 82% (186/228) for males (χ2 = 14.768, df = 1, P < 0.001; Table 3). There was also variation within adults with 92% (426/464) of adult females, and 75% (122/162) of adult males detected at least once across years (χ2 = 28.483, df = 1, P < 0.001; Table 3). Within juveniles, 90% (45/50) of females and 97% (64/66) of males were detected at least once across years (Table 3). We detected 43% (222/514) of females and 38% (87/228) of males at least once across subsequent years following tagging (Table 3). For juveniles, 20% (10/50) of females and 32% (21/66) of males were detected at least once across subsequent years following tagging (Table 3). Within adults, 46% of females (212/464) and 41% (66/162) of males were detected at least once across subsequent years following tagging (Table 3).

Activity peaked in July with detections occurring every month except in winter (Fig. 2). Bats began arriving at the maternity colony in March with the number of unique individuals detected continuing to increase into June. Detections remained high through July and August through the parturition phase and period when juveniles become active. Numbers of unique (newly and previously tagged) individuals declined in September and October. During the 6 winter surveys of the Hubbard Cave hibernaculum, we detected 31 tagged bats at least once (Table 2). Of these individuals, both age classes (adult = 28, juvenile = 3) and sexes (male = 3, female = 28) of tagged bats were detected. We detected some individuals at this hibernaculum on multiple visits (10 individuals on 2 visits, 7 individuals on 3 visits, and 3 individuals on 4 visits), suggesting fidelity to this winter site by a distinct subset of the bats tagged at EM Mine in summer.

We identified tag loss after searching internal passages within the summer roost, EM Mine, for dropped tags during the winters of 2016–2018. Seventeen of 18 bats tagged in 2011 and known to have lost tags were never detected after initial tagging. The 1 exception is for an adult male that retained its tag for over 4 yr (1,483 days) before its last detection at the mine in August 2015 and subsequent retrieval of its tag in February 2016. Across all years of tagged bats, we documented 135 (17 = 2011 and 118 = 2014–2016) lost tags during 3 winter searches in the mine when bats were not present (Table 4). The average minimum tag retention length was 64.75 ± 118.05 (x̄ ± SD, range = 9–356) days for juvenile females, 10.33 ± 5.82 (x̄ ± SD, range = 4–20) days for juvenile males, 44 ± 98.83 (x̄ ± SD, range = 0–766) days for adult females, and 56.00 ± 93.33 (x̄ ± SD, range = 2–357) days for adult males. We excluded the adult male that retained its tag for over 4 yr. When that bat is included, average minimum tag retention for adult males is 157.93 ± 391.78 (x̄ ± SD, range = 2–1483) days.

The models of apparent survival with the most support included time (year) variation and group (sex and age; Table 5). None of the models for apparent survival with wi ≥ 0.01 included age or sex alone without time (year) variation, or survival modeled as constant. Body condition index was included in a model with wi = 0.11, although the effect of this covariate was not significant, β = 0.010 (95% CI = –0.206 to 0.227). As expected, precision of survival estimates was best for adult females who accounted for the largest portion of the sample (Fig. 3). In general, sample size was too small (especially with the documented tag loss) to precisely estimate apparent survival for juveniles of either sex as noted by their much larger SEs (Fig. 3). Apparent survival was highest for adult females, averaging 0.74 (95% CI = 0.60–0.87) across all seasons, and ranged between maternity seasons from 0.55 (95% CI = 0.49–0.61) in 2016–2017 to 0.90 (95% CI = 0.82–0.94) in 2019–2020 (Fig. 3). No difference in site fidelity was found for age or sex (adult females = 0.99, SE = ± 0.01; adult males = 0.99, SE = ± 0.01; juvenile females = 0.93, SE = ± 0.18; juvenile males = 0.94, SE = ± 0.11) with the best supported model including constant fidelity (Table 5).

Discussion

Disturbances at roost sites for C. townsendi have been associated with improper gating, or disturbances that occurred inside the roost by researchers (e.g., banding) or by recreational users (Pearson et al. 1952; Graham 1966; Humphrey and Kunz 1976; Pierson and Rainey 1996; Pierson et al. 1999), leading to population declines. Issues at abandoned mines have been addressed through efforts to establish survey protocols prior to closure and bat-friendly gate specifications used during mining reclamation (Sherwin et al. 2009). Awareness of the potential to disturb bats has grown over the last several decades (Elliot 2006; Kennedy 2006), potentially contributing to increases noted in some local populations (Weller et al. 2014).

In our study, after the initial disturbance of placing the PIT reader antenna in front of the mine portal, only 2 capture disturbances occurred each summer (2014–2016) and these all occurred outside the roost. Because the mine in our study is gated to modern, bat-friendly specifications, and situated on public land with seasonal use constraints, no other disturbances were known to have occurred to the roost during the study. In Colorado, a maternity colony of this species also uses an elevated bridge section that is part of a major interstate (Neubaum and Siemers, personal observation). This roost has notable vibrations and noise suggesting that, as with other bat species, C. townsendii may habituate to such disturbance if the benefits outweigh the costs, and if disturbance occurs outside the roost itself (Altringham 2012; Jinhong et al. 2014). We found that C. townsendii tolerated PIT tagging and did not abandon the roost based on several lines of evidence: (1) no dead bats with a PIT tag or wound site were found during multiple searches of the roost site; (2) the number of individuals using the roost remained stable during the study based on video counts; and (3) tagged bats were regularly detected by the reader returning to the roost soon after being tagged. This result suggests that C. townsendii can be marked with PIT tags with low risk of mortality or roost abandonment.

Early-season movements of bats between roosts may account for the staggered arrival and aggregation of the maternity colony that we studied. Arrival of bats in spring occurs in a staggered fashion, starting with small numbers in March and continuing through May and June. The high stable temperatures (31 °C) of geothermal cave passages within the mine provide an ideal setting to be used by C. townsendii for maternity roosting (Pierson et al. 1991). However, these same high temperatures may also explain why some individuals were periodically absent or showed up later in June as Pierson et al. (1991) noted that some individuals use transition roosts with cooler temperatures early in the maternity season until pregnancy has progressed. Similarly, Dobkin et al. (1995) noted lower fidelity of C. townsendii using transition roosts in the spring after departing the hibernacula—a behavior that could also account for some missing PIT-tagged bats if given individuals chose not to return to our study site in lieu of alternate roosts used in the past (Sherwin et al. 2000, 2003). Finally, we found that not all members of the maternity colony that we examined hibernated together. The return of bats from multiple hibernacula on the landscape with different dates of egress can be affected by local snowpack (Neubaum 2018). Therefore, it is reasonable to conclude that dates of return for individual bats to the summer colony will be variable.

The timing of summer arrivals and departures from the maternity colony at EM Mine was similar to seasonal movements noted elsewhere for this species. Corynorhinus townsendii arrived at EM Mine in late May to early June. Pierson et al. (1991) found maternity colonies at lower elevations (∼600 m) in California forming by March and April. At elevations similar to those in this study, bats were still using transition roosts in May (Dobkin et al. 1995). Elevation can lead to dates of parturition varying widely for this species across its range (Pearson et al. 1952). Based on reproductive status of adult females, most pups are born at EM Mine in mid to late July, with young flying by late August. This pattern puts the parturition at our study site toward the later end of the range noted for the species (late July), which is not surprising given the elevation of the roost at over 2,000 m. At the end of summer, tagged individuals began departing the maternity colony at EM Mine in September. Autumn movements of this species in northern California, as confirmed by wing bands, found females arriving at hibernacula in late October with males arriving and departing throughout winter (Pearson et al. 1952).

Pierson et al. (1999) found that this species arrived at hibernacula in California as early as October, which corresponds with the dates on which we saw precipitous declines in detections at the maternity colony in our study. Internal surveys of EM Mine during the winter verified that no bats remained. In addition, we confirmed movements of 31 tagged bats to a known cave hibernaculum that is a 48 km straight-line distance to the north. Although movement over longer distances cannot be excluded, at least some C. townsendii use short, localized migrations as a strategy in Colorado. Regarding use of PIT tagging as a tracking technique, although small relative to the total number of bats we tagged, the number of individuals that we found is large in respect to the number of bats other studies have documented moving from a maternity roost to hibernacula using telemetry or banding for this or other bat species (Pearson et al. 1952; Neubaum 2018). The 48 km distance is similar to that collected in central Oregon where telemetry was used to track spring movements of 24 km from hibernacula to transition roosts (Dobkin et al. 1995). An additional distance from the transition roosts to a known maternity colony in that study may have made total distances noted longer. Females of this species made autumn movements to hibernacula in northern California of 32 km (Pearson et al. 1952). Shorter distance movements (<100 km) between maternity roosts and hibernacula have been noted for other bat species in Colorado (Neubaum et al. 2006, Neubaum 2018) and broader western North America (Blejwas et al. 2023). By contrast, many species in eastern or central portions of North America often undertake longer movements (> 100 km) to reach hibernacula (Winhold and Kurta 2006). However, these movement distances differ between sexes for many bat species, with females usually moving longer distances (Popa-Lisseanu and Voigt 2009). Our sample was biased toward adult females but 3 male C. townsendii (2 juvenile, 1 adult) tagged at EM Mine made the same movement. Consequently, these seasonal movements are likely driven by resource availability in a given region, along with mating opportunities, with bats traveling only as far as needed to breed and reach adequate roosting conditions.

Of the >600 bats that we regularly counted at the Hubbard Cave hibernaculum, only 4% of bats from the EM Mine maternity colony thought to have retained their PIT tag were detected making this movement. Despite confirming the movement of 31 individuals, this number is lower than we initially anticipated based on the unusual sizes of the maternity colony and hibernaculum. Pearson et al. (1952) noted that bats from the maternity colony that they studied moved somewhat independently, intermixing with bats from other maternity colonies to spend the winter. Our data support these findings, suggesting that individuals from the maternity colony that we studied disband and use numerous hibernacula on the landscape rather than moving as 1 intact colony. Winter records for C. townsendii in Colorado show that this species generally occurs in small numbers of single-digit individuals to a couple of dozen in each hibernaculum (Ingersoll et al. 2010; Hayes et al. 2011; Neubaum 2018). This pattern was noted across the western portion of the species range (Weller et al. 2018). Such a strategy would facilitate gene exchange and help maintain healthy populations as at least some reproduction occurs during this period (Pearson et al. 1952; Veith et al. 2004).

The number of bats (7) documented over 10 yr old during the analysis period (2014–2020) is notable. Postanalysis confirmations of 1 bat at least 14 yr of age detected in 2024 and an additional bat at least 15 yr of age detected in 2025 continue to extend this documentation. Longevity records for C. townsendii are minimum ages of 16 (Paradiso and Greenhall 1967) and 21 yr (Perkins 1994), but these were for 1 bat in each case. Our data show that C. townsendii routinely live for more than a decade.

We conclude that PIT tag loss is a concern for this species after retrieving PIT tags shed from 16% of tagged bats despite lengthy minimum tag retention periods for some individuals (Table 3). These results differ from van Harten et al. (2021), who noted only 2.7% tag loss in Gould’s wattled bats for losses that occurred within 2 mo after tagging. Some adhesives may be inferior to others in keeping the injection wound shut during healing. However, with the glue that we used, PIT tags were not lost soon (within hours) after insertion. Most bats tagged in 2014–2016 that lost their tags in the mine and that we recovered appeared to retain their tags from several days to nearly 2 mo (Table 3). In an extreme case, 1 adult male retained its tag for at least 1,483 days before losing it. These data indicate that tags were not simply being lost through the insertion wound of the skin shortly after tagging, but that, somehow, tags are being lost following healing of the insertion site. We used methods for PIT tagging bats that were similar to other studies (e.g., Wimsatt et al. 2005; Rigby et al. 2012) and even shared personnel with the Wimsatt et al. (2005) study that did not show evidence of significant PIT tag loss on Eptesicus fuscus (O’Shea et al. 2004; Ellison et al. 2007b). Although some tag loss may be attributable to inexperienced personnel that were trained on the project, we speculate that there is some characteristic of C. townsendii anatomy (e.g., thinner skin) or behavior (e.g., grooming, roosting) that makes this species more susceptible to PIT tag loss than other species.

As a post hoc assessment to investigate loss of PIT tags by C. townsendii, we investigated the idea of these bats having thinner skin versus other bat species. We measured the dermal thickness of 7 bats collected from western Colorado (including the study site) and compared them to 9 E. fuscus carcasses, a species that did not experience notable PIT tag loss in previous studies (O’Shea et al. 2004) collected in Fort Collins as part of a disease challenge study. We found a high degree of variability within species and overlap between species. Larger sample sizes from more species may help elucidate the influence dermal thickness may have on PIT tag loss, an important consideration for mark-recapture studies (Waag et al. 2025).

Other studies evaluated tag loss and made adjustments within the population models to account for this loss. However, these studies relied on double-marking techniques to establish a rate of tag loss within the study population. We did not double-mark individuals as tag loss was noted later in the study after searching the roost location and finding dropped PIT tags. Tag loss affects both accuracy and precision of estimates of population size and survival in mark-recapture studies (Arnason and Mills 1981). We acknowledge that more bats may have lost their PIT tags elsewhere, which could negatively bias our estimates of survival (Conn et al. 2004). The fate of bats that lose tags is unknown, and with our resight method (PIT reader), cannot be differentiated from death or permanent emigration. This rate of undiscovered tag loss is likely higher in adult males that use this roost in lower numbers than adult females and juveniles. Therefore, adult males would have a higher probability of losing their tags somewhere other than in the mine. Notably, as the time from our last tagging event increased and tag loss rate decreased, our estimates of survival should have become more reliable, especially for adult females.

We generated an apparent survival rate for adult females that was within the range noted for other North American bat species (Frick et al. 2010b; O’Shea et al. 2011). Estimates of survival increased substantially in 2017–2018 and remained high through 2020, which may reflect a reduction in tag loss rate each year following tagging. Most studies on tag loss assume that the rate of loss is greatest shortly after tagging and decreases over time because the tag is lost through the insertion site (Lebl and Ruf 2010). Based upon our data on minimum tag retention, this decrease in tag loss rate over time applies for this study as well, despite some individuals that retained their tags for more than 1 yr before losing them. Our final tagging session was in August of 2016. If most of the bats that were going to lose their tags lost them within a year, our population of tagged bats should have remained constant and tag loss should have been low following the 2017 maternity season. Such a reduction in tag loss is a probable explanation for the increase in annual survival in adults between the first 3 time intervals (2014–2017) and the last 3 time intervals (2017–2020) of the study. In addition, the reduction in disturbance associated with capture, handling, and tagging may have led some individuals to use alternative roost sites.

Pearson et al. (1952) estimated apparent survival for young ranging from 40% to 54% and adults around 80%. Estimates of banded C. townsendii were used to conduct a retrospective analysis using Cormack–Jolly–Seber (CJS) open models that reported adult male apparent survival at 0.54–0.68 and females at 0.60–0.67 (Ellison 2010), which were lower than the last 3 time intervals (2017–2020) of our study and could be due to the potential bias in estimation of apparent survival in CJS models that do not account for temporary emigration.

Although adult females and adult males had the highest site fidelity of the 4 age and sex classes, the overlap of SEs between both sexes and ages suggests no difference in these groups. High site fidelity for adult females was expected, as reported for other bat species at maternity colonies (e.g., Schorr and Siemers 2021). We did not expect the high site fidelity estimate that we observed for adult and juvenile males. Individuals only had to be detected once during the maternity season to be counted as present for that year, so even periodic visits to the site would have been counted as presence. Males tagged as juveniles eventually stopped returning to the site after becoming an adult. Of the 66 males tagged as juveniles, 20 were detected at the site in the following year. Only 1 individual returned after not being detected for a year. Seven of those 20 individuals returned more than 1 yr after tagging and of those, 1 returned every year for 4 yr, and 2 returned every year for 5 yr. O’Shea et al. (2021) found that male big brown bats captured at maternity colonies in Colorado were strongly philopatric, with most returns to the maternity roost occurring during the first year after birth. Further investigation into how individuals, especially returning juveniles and adult males, occupy the EM Mine over the maternity season is of interest.

Demographic data provide a powerful tool for monitoring trends, threat assessment, and to prescribe conservation strategies for wildlife that are under constant pressure from anthropogenic threats. Historically, bats have been under-sampled with respect to these demographic metrics due to the difficulties of marking and following these species. The development of new technologies, such as PIT tags, now allows estimates of survival and fidelity for bat populations at large maternity colonies and hibernacula if vetted carefully. Successfully addressing future threats to C. townsendii and other bat species, whether in the form of disease such as white-nose syndrome, diminishing resources caused by drought, or improper gating of abandoned mines will depend on accurate estimates of population change. Precise estimates of population parameters such as apparent survival and trend allow for informed species assessments to be conducted (Neubaum et al. 2017). These parameters also influence subsequent rankings in state wildlife action plans, which directly affect where agency personnel and resources are focused, how state agencies prioritize management actions, and federal listings. As white-nose syndrome and extreme drought affect Colorado and alter the population dynamics of vulnerable bat species, allocation of these resources will become increasingly important for bat conservation.
