ExclaveFL : Providing Transparency to Federated Learning using Exclaves
Jinnan Guo, Kapil Vaswani, Andrew Paverd, Peter Pietzuch

Abstract
In federated learning (FL), data providers jointly train a model without disclosing their training data. Despite its privacy benefits, a malicious data provider can simply deviate from the correct training protocol without being detected, thus attacking the trained model. While current solutions have explored the use of trusted execution environment (TEEs) to combat such attacks, there is a mismatch with the security needs of FL: TEEs offer confidentiality guarantees, which are unnecessary for FL and make them vulnerable to side-channel attacks, and focus on coarse-grained attestation, which does not capture the execution of FL training. We describe ExclaveFL , an FL platform that achieves end-to-end transparency and integrity for detecting attacks. ExclaveFL achieves this by employing a new hardware security abstraction, exclaves , which focus on integrity-only guarantees. ExclaveFL uses exclaves to protect the execution of FL tasks, while generating signed statements containing fine-grained, hardware-based attestation reports of task execution at runtime. ExclaveFL then enables auditing using these statements to construct an attested dataflow graph and then check that the FL training jobs satisfies claims, such as the absence of attacks. Our experiments show that ExclaveFL introduces a less than 9% overhead while detecting a wide-range of attacks.



1
Introduction

Federated learning (FL)
[
58
,
41
,
38
]
is a multi-party machine learning (ML) technique that allows mutually distrustful
data providers
to train a global ML model. Data providers can contribute their data to a global model without the data ever having to leave their trust domains: they train local models using their data, and share model updates with a centralized
model owner
. The model owner aggregates the local model updates, calculates a new global model and disseminates it to the data providers, who update their local models. To ensure that no information about individual data items is disclosed, local model updates can be protected using differential privacy (DP)
[
26
]
by adding noise to updates
[
6
,
22
,
96
]
.

FL has been shown to be a good fit for many scenarios, because it trains a global model without data providers having to relinquish control over datasets. Its use has been proposed for applications ranging from training large predictive language models on individual user input on mobile devices
[
33
,
74
]
, to training fraud detection models
[
98
]
, while protecting transaction data belonging to competing financial institutions.

Real-world deployments of FL systems
[
77
,
13
,
34
,
85
]
, however, have been hindered by the risk of attacks that compromise data or model integrity. In scenarios in which data providers and model owners are mutually distrusting, different classes of attacks remain possible: in
data poisoning
attacks
[
89
,
35
]
, a data provider deliberately manipulates their training data ahead of time to force specific behaviour of the global model, such as backdoors
[
17
]
; in
model poisoning
attacks
[
67
,
27
]
, a malicious model owner adds bias to the global model, e.g., by excluding particular data providers
[
41
]
.

We observe that the above attacks are possible, because data providers and model owners can
deviate from the correct execution of an FL training job without detection.
Instead of providing point solutions against particular attacks, our goal is therefore to design a new FL platform that offers a principled approach for mitigation against such attacks.

Our key idea is to establish partial trust between data providers and model owners by proving that some security-related properties hold. We achieve this by hosting individual FL computation tasks within an FL job in a new hardware-based environment called
exclaves
. Exclaves ensure that tasks execute with control flow and memory
integrity
, even in the presence of privileged malicious participants that exploit software or side-channel vulnerabilities. This is because exclaves implementations and applications do not rely on hardware support for protecting arbitrary secrets in memory, and instead rely either exclusively on keys managed directly by secure hardware, or application protocols such as differential privacy.

Based on the exclave abstraction, we introduce
ExclaveFL
, a platform for executing FL jobs with end-to-end integrity and transparency.
ExclaveFL
extends existing FL frameworks, such as NVFlare
[
77
]
, with the ability to execute FL tasks within task specific exclaves without requiring changes to FL job specifications or ML frameworks.

ExclaveFL
collects task-specific attestation reports on every exclave invocation that include measurements of all code, inputs and outputs of the FL task being executed. To ensure transparency, participants can register signed statements that include attestation reports in a public and universally verifiable transparency ledger as proof that they execute tasks correctly.

For example, data providers can sign statements to prove to a model owner that the model updates lack outliers, which may otherwise indicate a poisoning attack. Conversely, model owners may show to data providers that the global model update is aggregated from all data providers’ local model without bias by the model owner.

Auditors can use signed statements from the ledger to construct a trustworthy representation of an FL training job, check for attacks such as integrity violations, poisoning and bias, and hold participants accountable for these attacks.

We implement a prototype of
ExclaveFL
using confidential computing hardware and services in Azure
[
78
]
. While exclaves are currently not supported in hardware, we use AMD SEV-SNP VMs as the base platform, because it is the closest to our exclave design. Our experiments show that
ExclaveFL
achieves strong transparency guarantees for FL workloads with a small overhead: it increases training time for compute-intensive models such as VGG9, BERT, and GPT-2 by up to 9%.

We also evaluated the security benefits of exclaves based on known recent vulnerabilities TEEs and FL frameworks. We find that a significant majority of these vulnerabilities result in violations of confidentiality. By design, these vulnerabilities do not impact exclaves.

In summary, we make the following contributions:

(1) Exclaves.
Based on the observation that most attacks on FL jobs are integrity violations, we propose exclaves (§
3
), a new hardware security primitive that ensures integrity of computation. We show how exclaves can be supported with minor extensions to the firmware of CPUs that support TEEs.

(2)
ExclaveFL
.
We describe a new FL platform called
ExclaveFL
(§
4
) that uses exclaves for end-to-end integrity of FL workloads.
ExclaveFL
generates fine-grained attestation reports for each FL task that capture measurements of all inputs and outputs.

(3) End-to-end transparency and accountability.

ExclaveFL
provides transparency by enabling participants in an FL job to register signed statements containing attestation reports with a transparency ledger (§
5
). These signed statements can be used by any relying party to audit that the trained model meets security properties, hold participants accountable for any violations, and create model cards that can be used for various purposes, including providing regulatory compliance.

(4) Implementation and evaluation.
We have implemented a prototype of
ExclaveFL
using existing TEE hardware (§
6
). Our evaluation (§
8
) suggests that
ExclaveFL
introduces low performance overheads (up to 9% on) on real world models, and provides robust protection against strong attackers.

Federated Learning and Transparency

We introduce federated learning (FL) (§
2.1
) and its threat model (§
2.2
). Based on how trusted execution (§
2.3
) is used in FL (§
2.4
), we motivate end-to-end transparency in FL (§

).

2.1
Federated learning

FL enables model training while allowing data providers to retain control over their data. While FL applies to many scenarios, we consider the specific case of collaboration between multiple participants that wish to jointly train models using proprietary datasets, either for commercial gains, or to obtain models for their own use.

As Fig.
1
shows, a set of
data providers
perform training locally using their own datasets and periodically send updates (either gradients or models) to a
model owner
. The model owner aggregates the updates and distributes updated model versions to the providers. This process repeats for a fixed number of iterations, or until the model converges.

To strengthen privacy protection over the dataset, data providers may apply
differential privacy
(DP)
[
26
]
to hide data items from the model owner by adding random noise to the model updates. With DP-protected model updates, the honest-but-curious model owners cannot distinguish one data item from another, which guarantees the dataset’s privacy.

2.2
Threat model

In many real-world FL scenarios, a subset of participants may be adversarial, including data providers and the model owner
[
41
]
. The adversary’s goal is to compromise the trained model yet remain undetected. Adversaries can be actively malicious and do not need to follow the FL protocol.

All honest participants want to make claims about the final model, e.g., that model is in some sense “correct”. Since such claims typically cannot be made by inspecting the model itself, they must be made about the training process, e.g., that expected datasets and training code were used, no participant poisoned the model during training, etc. Thus, honest participants want to
detect
any action during the training process that would undermine their ability to make these claims.

Recent research has shown that FL is inherently vulnerable to various attacks under this threat model:

Poisoning attacks

[
89
,
17
]
can be classified as
untargeted
, which reduce overall model performance, and
targeted
, which focus on specific inputs. For example, targeted poisoning can inject
backdoors
, which modify the model’s behavior for inputs containing an adversary-defined
trigger
, e.g., to misclassify images with specific patterns of pixels.

Poisoning can be carried out differently: in
data poisoning

[
89
]
, an adversarial data provider modifies their training data, e.g., adding, removing, or editing data points, to introduce outliers, or manipulates the output of their local training process; in model poisoning
[
27
]
, an adversarial model provider modifies the received model updates before aggregation, or poisons the updated model.

There are many possible mitigations: data poisoning can be mitigated using data validation and filtering
[
40
]
, norm clipping
[
87
]
, differential privacy
[
67
]
, and participation control
[
89
]
; model poisoning can be mitigated using byzantine resilient aggregation
[
15
]
, and consistency checking
[
29
]
.

Fairness attacks.
Model bias refers to systematic errors that lead to unfair outcomes for certain groups (e.g., gender and race), often due to data skew. In FL, avoiding model bias is challenging due to limited visibility of the global data distribution
[
94
]
. Mitigations rely on pre-processing, e.g., re-sampling, to balance distributions across data providers. Fairness constraints can also be incorporated into the learning, e.g., using regularization and adversarial debiasing
[
7
]
.

The above approaches to mitigate poisoning or reduce bias require trust in one or more potentially malicious participants. For example, data filtering relies on data providers running a filtering task. Similarly, honest data providers interested in preventing model poisoning must rely on the model aggregator to use robust aggregation. However, in the FL threat model, participants can arbitrarily deviate from the protocol without being detected by other participants.

2.3
Trusted execution

One approach for enforcing that participants in FL do not deviate from their protocol is through the use of
trusted execution environments
(TEEs)
[
79
,
21
,
83
]
. TEEs are hardware-backed mechanism that protects code and data against privileged attackers, including malicious administrators, and compromised operating systems (OSs) and hypervisors. TEEs achieve this by
isolating
code and data using hardware and software mechanisms, such as memory encryption, access control, and integrity checks. They also support
remote attestation
for external parties to inspect and establish trust in TEEs.

Intel SGX
[
21
]
was the first practical TEE implementation with strict isolation at process granularity. It, however, suffers from limitations, including a complex programming model, incompatibilities with existing software, and high performance overheads. Therefore, most prior research on the use of TEEs for FL has focused on addressing these limitations (see §
9
).

Recent CPUs from Intel, AMD and ARM support
VM-based TEEs
, which can protecting entire virtual machines (VMs) running a guest OS along with unmodified applications. Examples of VM-based TEEs are AMD SEV-SNP
[
83
]
, Intel TDX
[
97
,
18
]
, and ARM CCA
[
5
,
55
]
.

TEE capabilities have also been integrated into AI accelerators, such as NVIDIA H100 GPUs
[
24
,
77
,
32
]
and Graphcore IPUs
[
93
]
. For example, H100 GPUs support a confidential mode with a unified TEE, spanning the CPU-based TEE and the GPU. All sensitive GPU state, including GPU memory contents and configuration registers, becomes protected from external access using PCIe firewalls, and all communication between a CPU-based TEE and the GPU is encrypted and integrity-protected. These new capabilities allow FL workloads to be hosted in TEEs with low performance overheads.

2.4
TEEs in FL

Prior research proposes the use of TEEs to enhance privacy and security in FL
[
49
,
61
,
65
,
72
,
39
]
. For example, SecureFL
[
49
]
integrates SGX based TEEs with ARM TrustZone on the edge to ensure privacy of models and data; SecFL
[
72
]
adds a policy manager to enforce attestation policies agreed between all clients; CFL
[
31
]
demonstrates how to host FL frameworks in TEEs based on SEV-SNP VMs coupled with NVIDIA Confidential GPUs and train real-world models with low overheads. CFL relies on attested TLS connections to secure communication channels between TEEs.

While the use of TEEs improves security and privacy in FL, it fails to address attacks related to data/model poisoning, bias, and fairness, due to several reasons:

(1) Large TCB.
In existing proposals, the entire computation run by data provider or model owner is hosted in a single, long-running TEE. This TEE generates a single
attestation
, which represents all code within the TEE in a single measurement. This includes an FL framework (e.g., NVFlare
[
77
]
), an ML framework (e.g., PyTorch
[
69
]
), a GPU software stack (CUDA, drivers), and the OS. This makes it challenging for participants and third-parties to audit such a large and evolving TCB with a complex software supply chain.

Code transparency

[
9
,
23
,
36
,
62
]
has emerged as an approach for developing trust in large and complex software. Inspired by
certificate transparency

[
50
]
, it forces developers to be consistent, auditable, and accountable. Code transparency relies on a public, verifiable, append-only ledger in which developers register a
software bill of material
(SBOM). The SBOM states how a software component was built, e.g., , in terms of source files, commits, dependencies, etc. It then allows external auditors to inspect a component after deployment, holding developers accountable for e.g., unpatched vulnerabilities. As we explain below, while code transparency is a necessary step, it is insufficient for FL jobs, because many of its properties of interest are data dependent.

(2) Incomplete attestation.
The attestation produced with the full FL job inside a TEE is also incomplete, because it lacks measurements of datasets, gradient updates, and models, reducing its usefulness for detecting data-dependent attacks, such as poisoning. For example, a data provider can arbitrarily tamper with the dataset during different rounds without changing the attestation, and such changes go undetected.

(3) Over-reliance on confidentiality.
For FL jobs, the primary goal of using TEEs is to ensure
integrity
, i.e., participants must run their FL tasks correctly and not deviate from the protocol. Data confidentiality is already achieved through the decentralized training mechanisms and DP.

Existing TEE applications, however, rely on confidentiality for ensuring integrity. For example, TEE applications commonly use attested TLS connections based on keys generated and held within TEEs
[
31
]
. A compromised key (e.g., through a side-channel, such as memory access patterns or micro-architectural state
[
47
,
68
,
45
,
82
,
92
,
91
,
53
,
54
]
) can be used to impersonate a TEE, and subsequently violate integrity.

Even TEE implementations conflate confidentiality and integrity: in Intel TDX, the integrity of remote attestation requires an attestation key held within an Intel SGX-based Trust Domain Quoting Enclave (TDQE). A compromised attestation key can be used to impersonate a TEE and obtain attestation reports with arbitrary runtime claims, violating both confidentiality and integrity; in AMD SEV-SNP, each SNP VM is provisioned with a set of VM Platform Communication Keys (VMPCKs) in a special secrets page accessible to the guest VM. Guests are required to use these keys to protect communication with the Platform Security Processor (PSP), e.g., requests for attestation reports. If a VMPCK is compromised, an attacker can impersonate an SNP VM and obtain attestation reports with arbitrary runtime claims.

2.5
The need for transparency

In this paper, we argue that FL must be designed with
end-to-end integrity, transparency, and accountability
. Specifically, FL training should generate trustworthy and granular
evidence
that enables any stakeholder, including external auditors, to detect attacks, such as data and model poisoning. This should hold even in the presence of adversarial participants that tamper with the training protocol and compromise TEEs using sophisticated attacks. Next, we describe how
ExclaveFL
achieves these goals using a new hardware primitive for verifiable integrity-protected execution known as an exclave.

3
Exclaves

In this section, we introduce
exclaves
, a new abstraction for ensuring the integrity of computation (§
3.1
), and we discuss how exclaves can be implemented in CPUs (§
3.2
).

3.1
Exclave abstraction

We observe that the main source of attacks in FL comes from participants behaving maliciously and deviating from the tasks they are expected to execute. Such attacks can be mitigated if we could hold participants accountable for their executed tasks. For this, we propose a new abstraction called an
exclave
. An exclave is a special kind of VM-based TEE that ensures
integrity
of all code and data, even from privileged adversaries, including administrators with physical access, and compromised host OSs or hypervisors.

Like most TEEs, exclaves support
remote attestation

[
20
]
based on a hardware root-of-trust (HRoT)
[
28
]
. Code within an exclave can request for a signed attestation report that includes launch measurements of the VM (i.e., measurements of the VM contents at boot time) and initialization-time configuration of the VM. Exclaves also support
runtime attestation
—at any time during VM execution, exclave applications can request an attestation report that includes an arbitrary set of runtime claims. Attestation reports are signed using attestation keys rooted in device-specific keys endorsed by the hardware manufacturer. Relying parties can independently verify authenticity of exclave attestation reports using device certificate chains, and verify that the exclave is hosted on secure hardware in good state, running applications in known good state using measurements of the CPU firmware, CPU configuration, and the initial contents of the VM.

Using exclaves and their attestations reports, participants can prove that they are executing the right set of tasks with the right configurations. For example, an honest data provider may wish to prove that they execute a specific sequence of tasks that first removes bias from the dataset, e.g., using outlier detection
[
40
]
, then, in each training round, uses DP training
[
67
]
, and finally sends the output of the training task to the central aggregator. Similarly, a central aggregator wishes to prove that, in each round, they host aggregation tasks that correctly aggregate all gradient updates received from data providers, and sends the aggregated model to data providers.

A key difference between exclaves and VM-based TEEs is that exclaves do not provide
confidentiality
. As discussed earlier, in existing TEE implementations, integrity properties are tightly coupled with confidentiality—if TEE confidentiality is compromised (e.g., due to side channels), integrity is also compromised. However, exclaves provide integrity
without relying on confidentiality
. This makes exclaves robust against a whole class of CPU and application vulnerabilities such as side channels and memory corruption that can otherwise result in exfiltration of secrets underpinning TEE guarantees.

The lack of confidentiality has significant implications for the exclave programming model. Exclave implementations and applications must not rely on secrets such as keys in exclave memory. While this additional constraint may require redesigning some applications, we believe that the additional security benefits justify the additional effort. In the next section and §
6.1
, we describe how exclaves and applications like FL can be implemented without relying on secrets.

3.2
Exclave implementations

Modern server CPUs, such as AMD EPYC, can be extended easily to support exclaves as an integrity-only mode of TEEs. This mode can reuse primitives used for integrity and attestation of VM-based TEEs. On AMD CPUs
[
83
]
, exclaves can utilize the PSP for exclave provisioning, isolation and integrity of VM memory using the reverse page map (RMP) tables, authenticated encryption of register state during VM enter/exit using VM specific encryption keys, and attestation using VCEK. These mechanisms rely entirely on keys generated and managed within the PSP.

An aspect of AMD SEV-SNP that requires redesign for exclaves is the use of VMPCK keys for secure communication between the SNP VM and the PSP. We propose a different design for exclaves: on exclave creation, the PSP provisions a special
protected
page in the guest VM’s address space, accessible only by the PSP. This protected page may be resident in physical memory and reserved by the PSP during boot with MMU controlling access, or allocated within PSP memory with the IOMMU controlling access. When a guest wishes to communicate with the PSP, it can write the contents of its request to this protected page and notify the PSP through the hypervisor. The PSP can then (i) identify the requesting VM using the address of protected page passed by the hypervisor, (ii) read and validate the request, and (iii) write the response to this page. While this mechanism is less flexible that VMPCK, it is more secure because it does not rely on confidentiality of keys in exclave memory, instead relying only on hardware capability to isolate a page from the host.

GPUs can also be extended to support exclaves as a special mode. Exclaves in GPUs can continue to rely on PCIe firewalls for isolating GPU memory, and the HRoT for attestation. However, instead of relying on authenticated encryption using keys negotiated between a trusted GPU driver and the HRoT for protecting traffic over PCIe, we require that GPUs support TEE Device Interface Secure Protocol (TDISP)
[
70
]
. When paired with a TDISP-capable CPU, the keys for authenticated encryption of PCIe traffic between the CPU and the device are managed entirely in hardware, thereby eliminating all software managed keys.

4
ExclaveFL
Design

We describe
ExclaveFL

, a platform for executing FL workloads while ensuring end-to-end auditability and transparency of trained FL models.

Task integrity.
In
ExclaveFL
, an FL training
job
is specified as a sequence of
tasks
that each participant executes in every epoch of training. Tasks can be orchestrated using an existing FL framework, but
ExclaveFL
extends the underlying FL framework to provision exclaves and execute tasks in exclaves.

Consider the simple FL workflow shown in Fig.
2
. Here, there are two tasks that data providers execute: (i) a local
training task
that receives a global model over the network, reads the dataset from storage, and returns a local model; and (ii) a
DP task
that obtains the local model update, adds DP noise, and outputs the DP-protected local model update. The model owner executes two tasks: (iii) an
aggregation task
that gathers DP-protected local model updates and aggregates them into an aggregated model; and (iv) a
model update task
that converts the aggregated model to a global model.

ExclaveFL
executes each of these tasks in separate exclaves. In each round, the FL framework executing within a data provider’s trust boundary receives model updates, invokes the local training exclave with the latest model, invokes the DP exclave with the locally trained model, and sends the gradient updates to the model owner. The FL framework executing in the model owner’s trust boundary waits for model updates from data providers and invokes the aggregation exclave after it has received a sufficient number of updates. It then invokes the model update exclave to obtain an aggregated model, and sends the model to the data providers for further training.

Note that the FL framework itself is untrusted—it has access to all communication between exclaves in cleartext, it can tamper with the inputs and outputs, e.g., by dropping or reordering inputs, and launch denial-of-service attacks.

Dataset commitments.
In
ExclaveFL
, data providers must package training datasets as integrity-protected disk images
[
44
]
. This constructs a Merkle tree
[
60
]
whose leaf nodes represent measurements of individual blocks on the disk image. When the disk image is mounted,
ExclaveFL
checks the value read against the Merkle tree to ensure integrity. The root hash of the Merkle tree (combined with a random salt) also serves as a private and non-revocable
commitment
to the training dataset.

Task attestation.
The main goal of
ExclaveFL
is fine-grained auditability to capture ML training at sufficient granularity so that auditors can detect attacks, such as data and model poisoning.
ExclaveFL
achieves this by generating
attestation reports
of every instance of an FL task and using
runtime attestation
to capture all inputs and outputs of the task as runtime claims.

For example, in the local training exclave, inputs include the training dataset commitment, i.e., the training dataset’s root hash of its Merkle tree, and a measurement (SHA256 digest) of the current global model, and outputs include a measurement of the locally trained model. For the aggregation exclave, inputs are measurements of all model updates processed by the aggregation enclave in an epoch, and the output is a measurement of the aggregated model. Note that the untrusted FL framework (and hence the participants) control these inputs/outputs, e.g., deciding which inputs are aggregated. Including these measurements in attestation reports, however, enables auditors to detect various kinds of attacks.

Note that the measurement of the exclave’s VM image is extended to HRoT during exclave launch. Thus, attestation reports of an exclave automatically contain this measurement, which we refer to as the exclave’s
code measurement
that uniquely identifies an exclave’s software stack.

End-to-end transparency.
To provide end-to-end auditability of trained models, the model owner can make
claims
about all aspects of the training process that resulted in a given model, starting from the used inputs and encompassing every transformation of these inputs to reach the final model.
ExclaveFL
provides evidence that allows these claims to be
verified
by a relying party, irrespective of whether they were involved in the training process. These claims can be used for various purposes, e.g., to create model cards
[
63
]
for commercial purposes or to demonstrate regulatory compliance.

Verifiable claims in
ExclaveFL
take the form of task-specific signed statements (see Fig.
3
). The payload of each statement contains (i) an attestation report from the exclave; (ii) runtime claims representing all inputs and outputs of the task; and (iii)
endorsements
, such as certificate chains from the hardware manufacturer endorsing the TEE hardware. Participants sign the statement using their long-term identity (
issuer
) for their signing keys e.g., an X.509 certificate chain based on public key infrastructure (PKI) or DID.

Participants then register signed statements in a universally verifiable, append-only transparency
ledger
. As we show in the next section, auditors can construct a fine-grained representation of the entire computation using a copy of the ledger and detect attacks, such as a data provider changing the training dataset between rounds, or the model owner systematically leaving out model updates from specific data providers.

5
Attestation and Auditing

ExclaveFL
uses a transparency ledger to demonstrate the absence of attacks during training and thus provide integrity assurances of the final model. For this,
ExclaveFL
constructs an
attested dataflow graph (ADG)
based on the signed statements in the ledger (§
5.1
). Using the ADG,
ExclaveFL
performs
auditing
to generate the required claims (§
5.2
).

5.1
Attested Dataflow Graph

Upon completion of the FL training, the signed statements with exclave attestation reports from all participants are collated and used to construct an directed acyclic graph, which we refer to as an
attested dataflow graph
(ADG).

A sample ADG is shown in Fig.
4
. Each vertex
v\in V
represents a single execution of an exclave and is therefore defined by the three-tuple of
(\mathit{in},\mathit{code},\mathit{out})
, which refers to the inputs to that specific execution of the exclave, the measurement of the code run on those inputs, and the resulting outputs from that execution of the exclave. Each vertex is constructed from a single signed statement, which contains an attestation report that can be used to verify this vertex.

Each edge
e\in E
represents the (reversed) flow of data between exclaves and points from the downstream (consumer) vertex
v_{1}
to the upstream (producer) vertex
v_{2}
:

e=\{(v_{1},v_{2})\mid v_{1},v_{2}\in V\text{ and }v_{1}.\mathit{in}\cap v_{2}.\mathit{out}\neq\emptyset\}

(1)

The edge label represents the specific data that flows between the two exclave executions. It is defined as the non-empty intersection of the outputs of
v_{2}
and the inputs of

The constructed ADG above is a verifiable record of the steps that led to the creation of the final model. It can be audited and used to make claims about the final model.
ExclaveFL
does not prescribe the types of claims made based on the ADG, because different types of claims might be required for different use cases. In this section, we describe the possible types of claims with examples that prove the absence of attacks during training and thus provide assurance about model integrity.

Detecting dataset poisoning.
A malicious data provider could add, remove, or modify entries in the dataset, which would lead to an error verifying the Merkle tree when reading the data into the training exclave. The data provider could recalculate the Merkle tree for this poisoned dataset, as shown in Fig.
5(b)
. This new data commitment, however, would be recorded as one of the inputs in the ADG. By comparing the ADG inputs against the set of expected inputs (or dataset commitments from the legitimate participants), the auditor can detect this type of dataset poisoning.

Detecting data modifications.
A malicious participant could modify the data in transit, either between exclaves within a participant (see Fig.
5(c)
), or as the data is sent between participants. This would result in a mismatch between the outputs of one exclave and inputs of the next exclave, i.e., a missing edge in the ADG. Since there would be a vertex with an input but no edge, the auditor would identify this as an unintended input, indicating the existence of poisoning.

Detecting modified exclave code.
Any participant could modify the code of the executed exclaves before deployment. For example, a data provider could modify the training and DP exclaves to skip data integrity checks, ignore some training data, inject too much noise, or modify the model updates. A model owner could modify the aggregation or model update exclaves to omit certain inputs, change the aggregation algorithm, directly modify the model weights, or distribute different models to different participants. Although this would still result in an ADG with the correct edges, the modified code would be measured by the exclave and recorded as part of the runtime attestation. During auditing, the auditor would traverse the ADG and check each vertex against a list of acceptable code measurements, thus detecting any modifications.

Detecting unexpected execution order.
A malicious participant could change the order of exclaves or skip exclaves entirely. For example, as shown in Fig.
5(d)
, a data provider could bypass the DP exclave and send the output of the training exclave directly to the model owner to avoid the addition of noise. Alternatively, instead of performing computation in each training round, they could resubmit the results of a previous round. Furthermore, a model owner could omit contributions from certain data owners in its communication with the aggregation exclave to introduce bias. This is more subtle than the preceding attacks, because they would still result in a complete ADG. However, the shape of the resulting ADG would differ from that of the expected ADG (i.e., missing vertices/edges, or edges that unexpectedly span multiple training rounds), which would be detected during auditing.

In this section, we describe a specific implementation of
ExclaveFL
using NVIDIA’s NVFlare in more detail:

FL Framework.
NVFlare
[
77
]
is an open-source, distributed FL framework for orchestrating the FL jobs and providing implementations of typical FL tasks. These FL tasks implement a wide range of FL algorithms (e.g., federated averaging, federated optimization, and secure aggregation) and other algorithmic building blocks (e.g., model trainers, model aggregators, and task filters).

Tab.

shows a set of sample tasks tasks used in a typical FL job. In

Exclave Connectors
are gRPC clients
[
37
]
that enable NVFlare to execue FL tasks to exclaves. Therefore, they are implemented as NVFlare tasks, making them compatible with the NVFlare API. To adopt an exclave into the FL workflow,
ExclaveFL
only needs to include the corresponding Exclave Connector in the job description file. The design using an Exclave Connector integrates
ExclaveFL
with NVFlare without requiring modifications to the NVFlare framework code.

Exclaves.
To provide a hardware-based integrity guarantee for exclaves,
ExclaveFL
implements exclaves using confidential VMs (CVMs) based on AMD SEV-SNP
[
83
]
. Since exclaves execute FL tasks of the FL workflow, they must interact with FL framework to receive FL Tasks. To support such interactions, we implement exclaves as gRPC servers.

Exclave Managers
expose exclave interfaces to the FL framework to support the orchestration of the FL workflow. Exclave Managers receive FL Tasks from the FL framework, deserialize the FL Task payload, and forward the payload to the Task Executor, and vice versa; they take execution results from the Task Executor and send them to the FL framework.

Task Executors
inside the exclaves perform task execution. A Task Executors receives FL Tasks from the Exclave Manager, loads the necessary data/code, and executes it. For example, the Task Executor for large language model (LLM) training initializes the task execution by loading the LLM model, the training code, and the training dataset. Next, the Executor performs model training and validation as part of the FL training task. With Task Executors,
ExclaveFL
reuses specific FL task execution code from NVFlare with minimal changes.

6.3
Runtime attestation

The Runtime Attestor constructs and attests the runtime claim of exclaves. During task execution, it monitors the storage accesses and requests/responses passing through the Exclave Manager to capture exclaves’ input and output data.

To provide the runtime claim with sufficient granularity, the Runtime Attestor records each data item alongside its metadata, which includes both the intended usage (e.g., input or output) and property (e.g., model or dataset). Upon collecting a pair of input/output data, the Runtime Attestor constructs the runtime claim as a nested JSON object, in which the keys are organized hierarchically to contain both the usage and property of the data, and the value is the hash digest of the data.

The Runtime Attestor then submits a runtime attestation request, which includes an SHA256 hash digest of the runtime claim, to the PSP. The PSP creates an attestation report, which contains the above hash in the
REPORT_DATA
field, then signs the attestation report using the hardware-managed
vcek
key. Therefore, the Runtime Attestor generated fine-grained, transparent statements with hardware-signed integrity proofs.

6.4
Secure Storage with commitment

ExclaveFL
’s Secure Storage module provides integrity-preserving and verifiable persistent storage for exclaves, which is used to protect both dataset access and code access.

Dataset protection.

ExclaveFL
uses Linux
dm-verity

[
44
]
to implement the Secure Storage module. Data providers use the dm-verity tool to package the dataset image with its Merkle tree measurement and employ the root hash as a
data commitment
. The generated data commitment becomes a unique measurement over the dataset, which is included by the Runtime Attestor to generate a transparent exclave statement.

While setting up the exclave, the module securely mounts the data disk, checking the expected data commitment against the Merkle tree. The dataset is then exposed as an ext4 file system. When an exclave loads data blocks from the data disk, the module checks the blocks against the Merkle tree, ensuring block-level integrity.

Code protection.
The Secure Storage module also applies dm-verity protection to the exclave’s OS image. It uses the Merkle tree root hash of the exclave image as a
code measurement
. During the exclave launch process, the PSP includes the code measurement into the
HOST_DATA
field of the attestation report. All reports generated by the PSP contain the code measurement. Since it uniquely identifies an exclave’s software stack, it can be used by the auditor to determine whether an FL task was executed by the correct exclave.

7
Security Analysis

This section presents
ExclaveFL
’s security properties with respect to implementation-level attacks, encompassing vulnerabilities arising from
ExclaveFL
’s implementation. We analyze these in terms of the different vulnerabilities classes:

FL framework vulnerabilities.
The complexity of FL frameworks inevitably gives rise to vulnerabilities. For example, in the past two years, there have been three critical severity CVEs in NVFlare
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, arising from insecure deserialization of untrusted data or unsafe YAML loading. By design,
ExclaveFL
’s security properties only depend on the correctness of the code
within
the exclaves, presenting a significantly smaller attack surface than the full framework. Indeed, none of the known NVFlare CVEs would have affected the
ExclaveFL
exclaves in our implementation. A CVE affecting the framework
outside
the exclaves could still be used to mount protocol-level attacks, but these would be detected during auditing (see §
5.2
).

Side-channel attacks
exploit unintended leakage of information from a TEE to break confidentiality. For example, an adversary running on the same physical host (e.g., a malicious hypervisor) could infer information based on architectural or micro-architectural effects, such as memory access patterns
[
30
]
, speculative prefetching
[
47
,
91
]
, cryptographic side-channels
[
53
,
52
]
, or power consumption
[
95
,
56
]
.

In a typical TEE-based FL implementation, remote attestation of participants takes place during the TLS handshake
[
46
,
90
]
, and all further communication is assumed to originate from the attested entity. A malicious participant controlling the TEE could use side-channel attacks to extract the TLS session key, hijack the connection, and send arbitrary data from outside the TEE (e.g., poisoned model updates).

Since
ExclaveFL
only uses exclaves, the security guarantees do not rely on data confidentiality within the exclaves. The only data that must remain confidential is the exclave HRoT, i.e., the signing key for the runtime attestations. In our
ExclaveFL
implementation using AMD SEV-SNP, this is the
VCEK
, which is fully managed by the PSP. Since the PSP is implemented on-die with dedicated SRAM, it is physically isolated from the CPU and DRAM, making the
VCEK
inaccessible to the above side-channel attacks.

TEE implementation flaws.
There may be security flaws in the TEE hardware or firmware implementation. For example, a TLB poisoning attack
[
54
]
exploits a security flaw in AMD SEV/SEV-ES through which a malicious hypervisor injects TLB entries to the victim process, undermining integrity; the WeSee attack
[
4
,
81
]
exploits a design flaw in the handling of
#VC
exceptions through which a privileged attacker can inject malicious code into an AMD SEV-SNP VM.

Such attacks could undermine the integrity guarantees of exclaves. However, they appear to be less common than side-channel attacks (as discussed above) and are more easily mitigated: AMD has stated that the TLB poisoning attack does not affect SEV-SNP VMs
[
10
]
; the WeSee vulnerability resulted in a mitigation in the main Linux kernel
[
11
]
.

]
addresses FL privacy concerns when aggregators are untrusted through cryptographic means. For example, Bonawitz et al.
[
16
]
implement secure aggregation using pair-wise Diffie-Hellman key agreement and t-of-out-n Shamir’s secret sharing, whilst Sharma et al.
[
84
]
use additive secreting sharing. These protocols are complementary to
ExclaveFL
because they provide the application-level confidentiality guarantees whilst
ExclaveFL
provides verifiable integrity guarantees that the correct computation has been performed.
Indeed, secure aggregation protocols can be used with
ExclaveFL
to provide both privacy and integrity guarantees.

Robust aggregation
[
71
]
addresses data and model poisoning attacks in the FL training process. Tolpegin et al.
[
89
]
detect data poisoning attacks using outlier detection.
ExclaveFL
is compatible with such techniques, protecting their integrity using exclaves. To extend the protocol-level protection to model poisoning, Krum
[
14
]
and Trimmed-mean
[
99
]
are Byzantine-robust aggregation protocols for secure aggregation. While such protocols require at least the majority of data providers to be trusted,
ExclaveFL
’s integrity guarantee holds under an arbitrary amount of malicious providers.

Trusted execution for FL.
Mo et al.
[
64
]
assume that both the FL server and clients are honest-but-curious. They use TEEs to prevent participants from observing model data during training, thus preventing inference or data reconstruction attacks. Messaoud et al.
[
61
]
address FL client-side privacy threats by protecting gradient updates inside TEEs. Since a subset of the model layers is prevented from gradient leakage, this mitigates against inference attacks. Both of the approaches assume a weaker honest-but-curious threat, without
ExclaveFL
’s protection against integrity attacks.

CrowdGuard
[
75
]
uses TEEs to mitigate model poisoning attacks. At each training step, data providers use TEEs to evaluate the locally-trained models of every other data provider on their own data and vote regarding poisoned model. Unlike
ExclaveFL
, CrowdGuard assumes that the TEEs ensure confidentiality, which makes them vulnerable to side-channel attacks.

Ensuring integrity.
Proof-carrying code
[
66
]
and model checking
[
19
]
can show that code complies with integrity policies by verifying properties against a specification. Since
ExclaveFL
provides transparent code artifacts with execution integrity, it can be combined with such approaches. Runtime attestation demonstrates code integrity: C-FLAT
[
8
]
and ATRIUM
[
100
]
report both instructions and control flow information in attestation reports. This is similar to
ExclaveFL
’s attestations, but
ExclaveFL
performs these at the granularity of FL data transformations.

10
Conclusions

We described
ExclaveFL
, a novel platform for executing FL jobs with end-to-end transparency.
ExclaveFL
introduces
exclaves
, a new abstraction for the execution of FL tasks with integrity, which allows it to implement fine-grained attestation of all FL tasks. Since all executed FL tasks are attested, it becomes impossible for participants to deviate from the correct execution to mount an attack without detection. Exclaves produce runtime attestation reports of their execution, which can be combined to form an attested dataflow graph (ADG), spanning the entire FL job. An auditor can use the ADG to establish end-to-end security properties of the FL job, discovering violations during execution. We showed that
ExclaveFL
can be integrated with a state-of-the-art FL framework with modest code changes, while mitigating against a wide range of attacks.
