OS-Marathon: Benchmarking Computer-Use Agents on Long-Horizon Repetitive Tasks
Jing Wu, Daphne Barretto, Yiye Chen, Nicholas Gydé, Yanan Jian, Yuhang He, Vibhav Vineet

Abstract
Long-horizon, repetitive workflows are common in professional settings, such as processing expense reports from receipts and entering student grades from exam papers. These tasks are often tedious for humans since they can extend to extreme lengths proportional to the size of the data to process. However, they are ideal for Computer-Use Agents (CUAs) due to their structured, recurring sub-workflows with logic that can be systematically learned. Identifying the absence of an evaluation benchmark as a primary bottleneck, we establish OS-Marathon, comprising 242 long-horizon, repetitive tasks across 2 domains to evaluate state-of-the-art (SOTA) agents. We then introduce a cost-effective method to construct a condensed demonstration using only few-shot examples to teach agents the underlying workflow logic, enabling them to execute similar workflows effectively on larger, unseen data collections. Extensive experiments demonstrate both the inherent challenges of these tasks and the effectiveness of our proposed method. Project website: https://os-marathon.github.io/ .

1
Introduction

Long-horizon, repetitive workflows are prevalent in both professional and personal contexts.
It generally involves processing a collection of data, requiring executing a series of similar sub-workflows to handle each data sample iteratively, as shown in
fig.

1
(Top Left).
Examples include submitting expense reports that require processing numerous receipts with heterogeneous formats, or periodically analysing annual reports from the same set of companies to extract a fixed set of metrics.
These workflows are tedious for humans due to their extended duration and repetitive nature.
In contrast, the inherent repetitiveness and structured sub-workflows of such tasks make them an ideal use case where CUAs can significantly alleviate human burden.

Despite this potential, existing benchmarks for computer-use agents have largely emphasised short-horizon tasks.
Based on OSWorld
28
, the most commonly used community benchmark for CUAs, recent studies
2
;
3
;
19
;
24
have primarily focused on improving CUAs’ efficiency, speed, and accuracy in completing general, short-horizon tasks, such as resizing or repositioning images, reformatting documents, or launching applications.
Because the tasks in OSWorld typically require a relatively small number of steps to complete, long-horizon, repetitive workflows remain largely underexplored, lacking both a clear formal definition and a standardised benchmark to evaluate CUAs’ long-horizon capabilities.

We bridge this gap by formally defining the problem scope and introducing a comprehensive benchmark, OS-Marathon.
OS-Marathon is specifically tailored to evaluate CUA performance in long-horizon, repetitive execution scenarios, comprising 242 tasks across 2 domains and 7 distinct execution environments.
As illustrated in
fig.

1
(Right), OS-Marathon includes expense reporting and transcript processing domains, and execution environments ranging from fully functional web-based systems to local spreadsheet applications.
Tasks in OS-Marathon are further categorised into multiple difficulty levels based on task horizon length and document complexity, enabling fine-grained evaluation of agent performance.

To benchmark the performance of existing approaches, we first evaluate leading CUAs on OS-Marathon.
We discover three primary failure modes due to an extended horizon and increased complexity of the tasks:
First, agents demonstrate a
logical incoherence
, frequently executing tasks in an illogical order.
Second, agents suffer from
hallucination
during action planning, often failing to ground their actions on the state of the current sub-workflow.
For example, it may attempt to fill system fields without retrieving and extracting the source data first, leading to failure.
Third, agents
struggle to maintain long-horizon consistency
in addressing sub-workflows repetitively, typically terminating execution after completing only a few initial sub-workflows.

We further explore methods to mitigate the aforementioned issues through a human demonstration.
However, leveraging a demonstration for long-horizon, repetitive tasks is still challenging: the extensive length of complete workflows prevents direct context integration, as they exceed the effective context limits of current CUAs.
To address this, we propose Few-shot Condensed Workflow Demonstration (
F
C
W
D
).
This strategy constructs a condensed human demonstration from few-shot data by abstracting the workflow into key steps to guide the CUA reasoning, as shown in
fig.

1
(Bottom Left).
This structure provides dual-level instruction:
global planning
, enabling the agent to orchestrate the repetitive loop for sub-workflow processing, and
sub-workflow execution
, teaching the fundamental logic required to complete each sub-workflow.
This approach enables the cost-effective adaptation of state-of-the-art agents to long-horizon, repetitive tasks.

The contributions of this work are as follows:

1. We formalise a definition for long-horizon, repetitive CUA tasks.

2. We introduce the first benchmark tailored for long-horizon, repetitive desktop tasks, comprising 242 tasks across 2 domains and 7 execution environments.

3. We evaluate leading CUAs on our benchmark, showing that existing CUAs tend to fail long-horizon, repetitive CUA tasks due to their inability to comprehend complex workflow structures and maintain consistency across sub-workflows.

4. We introduce a lightweight in-context learning approach, dubbed Few-shot Condensed Workflow Demonstration (
F
C
W
D
), that improves CUA performance on long-horizon, repetitive tasks.

2.1
Task Definition

A general autonomous digital agent task can be formalized as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) (
\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{O}
,
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{T}
,
\mathcal{R}
) with state space
\mathcal{S}
, observation space
\mathcal{O}
, action space
\mathcal{A}
, transition function
\mathcal{T}
:
\mathcal{S}
×
\mathcal{A}
→
\mathcal{S}
, and reward function
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
Following OSWorld
28
, we define the state space
\mathcal{S}
as the current desktop environment, and the observation space
\mathcal{O}
as the multimodal computer signals, including a screenshot, a natural-language instruction, an accessibility (a11y) tree, or any combination thereof, depending on available modalities. The action space
\mathcal{A}
comprises all actions executable by the agent.
The reward function
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow[0,1]
offers a sparse signal, assigning a reward of 1 only at the final step if and only if the task is completed.

Based on this, we further define the long-horizon, repetitive task as involving the sequential processing of a set of independent data samples
\mathbf{D}=\{d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{N}\}
, where each
d_{i},i\in[1,N]
represents a distinct data instance (e.g., a specific receipt) and
N
denotes the total volume of items to be processed.
Under this formulation, the full workflow to process a long-horizon, repetitive task
\mathbf{M}
can be represented as a sequence of
N
repetitive operation patterns, denoted as:

\displaystyle\textbf{M}=\{\mathbf{m}_{1},\mathbf{m}_{2},...,\mathbf{m}_{N}\}.

Here, each
\mathbf{m}_{i}
corresponds to a specific POMDP sub-workflow used to process the data instance
d_{i}
.
Let
l_{i}
be the length of the
i
-th sub-workflow.
We define
\mathbf{m}_{i}
as the sequence of state transitions:

\begin{split}\mathbf{m}_{i}=\{&(S_{i,j},O_{i,j},A_{i,j},T_{i,j},R_{i,j})|j\in[1,l_{i}]\}.\end{split}

where the actions
A_{i,j}
within
\mathbf{m}_{i}
are conditioned on the specific information contained in
d_{i}
(e.g.,
\texttt{ExtractInfo}(d_{i})
for data extraction from a file).
In our formulation, the execution logic of the workflow
\mathbf{m}_{i}
remains invariant for all
i
, while the data
d_{i}
varies for each
i\in[1,N]
.
The reward function assigns a reward of 1 only upon the completion of the final sub-workflow, i.e.,
R_{N,l_{N}}=1
.

Data Composition.

To ensure diverse evaluation, OS-Marathon incorporates a hybrid dataset of real and synthetic data across both domains.
Due to aggregation from heterogeneous sources, the real-world data alone often fails to exhibit internal configuration consistency, such as coherent name, location, currency, and timing information, leading to misalignment with practical use cases.
To bridge this gap, we implemented a synthetic data generation pipeline that produces high-fidelity documents with customisable, consistent information.
By merging these temporally coherent synthetic samples with authentic receipts, we ensure the benchmark offers both visual realism and logical workflow integrity.

For the
Expense Report
domain, our dataset spans 7 common reimbursement categories: Flight, Hotel, Meals, Car Rental, Taxi, Parking, and Fuel.
The documents exhibit significant diversity in format and structure, ranging from file types (Image vs. PDF) and origins (digital-native vs. photocopy) to layouts (printed emails vs. screenshots, single vs. multi-page) and currencies.
Similarly, for the
Transcript
domain, we curate a comprehensive hybrid collection spanning universities in 14 distinct nations.
This subset captures a wide range of document characteristics, ranging from different academic levels (Undergraduate vs. Graduate) and grading systems to diverse layout configurations (single vs. double column, portrait vs. landscape, different fonts) and source qualities (native digital vs. scanned).
Furthermore, the dataset includes both single- and multi-page transcripts with varying course loads, providing a realistic testbed for long-horizon, repetitive CUA tasks.

Real-World Data Collection.

To capture the complexity of real-world long-horizon, repetitive tasks, we curate diverse real-world datasets for both domains.

To ensure data privacy, we strictly adhered to the usage policies of the original datasets and applied privacy-preserving masking manually to personally identifiable information in self-collected samples.

2.3
Long-horizon Repetitive Task Design

We introduce the task design strategy, covering two main aspects: task difficulty level classification based on workflow complexity for fine-grained evaluation and task construction.

Figure

4
shows a long-horizon, repetitive task, i.e. a complex workflow containing multiple sub-workflows that apply invariant execution logic to different data instances.

Task Difficulty Level Design.

We observe that challenges arise primarily from two sources: the volume of data instances to process and the complexity of processing each individual instance.
Specifically, for the latter challenge source, in the
Expense Report
domain, it results in failures when processing multi-page PDFs, as it involves retaining context across multiple captures.
Similarly, in the
Transcript
domain, documents with complex and dense layouts often require zooming for legibility.
This forces the agent to process a sequence of partial, overlapping captures, often leading to failure as the agent cannot effectively retain context across multiple visuals.
To rigorously evaluate CUAs on these challenges, we divide the benchmark into distinct difficulty tiers.

Table

1
shows the statistics of OS-Marathon.

For the
Expense Report
domain, we define four levels classified by receipt volume and page count, as shown in
table

1
.
Levels 1 and 2 focus on fundamental capabilities, while Levels 3 and 4 simulate realistic scenarios and introduce a higher volume of receipts, thereby challenging agents to maintain context over significantly longer execution horizons.
Similarly, the
Transcript
domain comprises three levels according to course number and layout complexity.
Level 1 utilises single-page, single-column PDFs to test basic repetitive course logging.
Level 2 elevates difficulty with dense two-column layouts, requiring retaining context across multiple captures.
Level 3 introduces multi-page documents, demanding cross-page aggregation from multi-page documents with variable layouts.
Concurrently, the workload scales with difficulty, increasing from tens of courses in the lower levels to hundreds in the most advanced.

Task Construction.

We introduce the construction strategy for tasks in each difficulty level.
In the
Expense Report
domain, for Levels 1 and 2, we utilise exclusively real-world data, as these tasks mainly focus on evaluating the fundamental ability for data comprehension and entry.
For each level, we randomly sample six receipt combinations from the real dataset that adhere to the defined difficulty criteria.
For Levels 3 and 4, with a realistic scenario, we employ a hybrid approach to create 10 distinct task profiles per level (6 real, 4 synthetic).
For the real tasks, we construct receipt combinations by manually defining the category distribution (e.g., #hotel, #meals) and randomly sampling corresponding real receipts to form a "trip".
For synthetic tasks, to ensure temporal consistency, we generate 4 synthetic user profiles via LLM (as described in
section

2.2
), rendered with our templates to form temporally coherent timelines.
In total, the expense domain comprises 120 real tasks and 40 synthetic tasks.
For the
Transcript
domain, we classify the collected real data into three levels based on the previously defined difficulty criteria: 6 profiles for Level 1, 6 for Level 2, and 14 for Level 3.
We leverage the pre-built 5 synthetic templates and synthesise 3 student profiles as introduced in
section

2.2
.
This results in 52 real and 30 synthetic transcript tasks.

2.4
Evaluation Metrics

While the standard binary Success Rate (SR) used in OSWorld
(
28
)
allows for effective evaluation of short-horizon tasks, it fails to capture partial progress or reliability in long-horizon workflows.
To address this, we introduce Sub-Workflow Accuracy (SWA), which quantifies the performance of an agent over extended action sequences.
Formally, given a long-horizon, repetitive task
\mathbf{M}
consisting of
N
sub-workflows
\{\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{N}\}
, let
n
denote the number of sub-workflows correctly executed by the agent.
We define SWA as:

\displaystyle\text{Sub-Workflow Accuracy}=\frac{n}{N}.

This provides a fine-grained measurement of agents’ performance on long-horizon, repetitive tasks.
Additional task-specific granular metrics are detailed in
section

A.3.1
.

3
Method

Given a long-horizon, repetitive task
\mathbf{M}
consisting of
N
sub-workflows that process a collection of data instances
\mathbf{D}
within an execution environment
\mathbf{E}
, our goal is to efficiently adapt a CUA to this workflow with a human demonstration.

Figure

5
illustrates the complete workflow of CUA executing long-horizon, repetitive desktop tasks guided by a human demonstration.
Specifically, to balance context usage and demonstration efficacy, we construct a condensed demonstration using only a minimal subset of data samples
\mathbf{D}_{demo}\not\subset\mathbf{D}
to teach the agent the underlying workflow execution logic, termed as Few-Shot Condensed Workflow Demonstration (
F
C
W
D
).
This allows the agent to generalise the learned logic to the unseen collection of data samples
\mathbf{D}
with minimal adaptation cost.

We design a strategy to construct the demonstration using key steps of the workflow, preserving the critical execution logic while significantly reducing the demonstration length.
This addresses the limitation of prior methods by scaling in long-horizon repetitive tasks to avoid heavy computational costs and context window limits.

3.1
Few-shot Condensed Workflow Demonstration

As defined in
section

2.1
, for a long-horizon, repetitive task
\mathbf{M}
containing
N
sub-workflows
\mathbf{m}
, the core execution logic remains invariant across each
\mathbf{m}
.
Thus, the agent can learn the workflow by learning the invariant sub-workflow logic through a demonstration built upon few-shot data and learn to execute it repetitively.
We structure the demonstration as a sequence of
K
semantic key steps
\mathbf{K}=\{k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{K}\}
, where each step
k_{j}
serves as a high-level milestone POMDP transitions.
We standardise this process using a template consisting of four primary phases:

1.
Environment Comprehension (
k_{env}
):
The agent inspects the environment to understand the required input fields (e.g., identifying required fields in an expense system).

2.
Global Planning (
k_{plan}
):
The agent isolates the data unit for each sub-workflow and plans the execution structure for the repetitive loop.

3.
Data Extraction (
k_{data}
):
The agent parses the isolated source unit to interpret and extract the specific entities required by the target environment.

4.
Navigation & Execution (
k_{exec}
):
The agent performs interactions necessary to input the extracted data with the environment to complete each sub-workflow cycle.

3.2
Integrate Demonstration

We employ a state-of-the-art agent, AgentS2.5
3
, to show the effectiveness of
F
C
W
D
.
Leveraging the semantic key step demonstration
\mathbf{K}
, we construct a compact in-context demonstration trajectory
\mathbf{m}_{demo}
derived from a minimal subset of data
\mathbf{D}_{demo}=\{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\}
, where
n\ll N
.

\mathbf{m}_{demo}
begins with an introductory prompt (
p_{start}
) that contextualises the demonstration within the agent’s current task.
Subsequently, key-steps in
\mathbf{K}
are presented sequentially; every key-step
k_{j}
is presented as a pair: an environment observation (represented by a GUI screenshot) and the corresponding high-level human action.
Finally, an ending prompt (
p_{end}
) marks the conclusion of the example.
A demonstration example and more implementation details are provided in
section

A.2
.

Infrastructure and Baselines.

Our experiments are built upon the infrastructure of OSWorld
28
.
To ensure a rigorous evaluation, we select state-of-the-art agents from the OSWorld leaderboard, including 2 CUA specialised models with open weights, i.e. OpenCUA
24
and UI-TARS-1.5
20
and an agentic framework AgentS2.5 powered by GPT-5.
We also report human performance baselines to provide a comparative reference.

Subset Task Selection.

Preliminary experiments indicated that even current state-of-the-art agents struggle significantly on the simplest tasks within our benchmark, often maxing out the step budget or directly failing without reaching the step limit.
Thus, Levels 1 and 2 prove sufficiently challenging to benchmark the long-horizon capabilities of current leading CUAs.
Given the high computational cost of full-scale execution, we balance feasibility with experimental rigour by curating a representative subset of OS-Marathon.
Within the
Expense Report
domain, we select two distinct execution environments covering both web-based and spreadsheet scenarios.
For the
Transcript
domain, we select one web-based environment (both environments are web-based).
Across these environments, we sample 4 tasks for each difficulty level.
This yields a total evaluation set of 24 tasks: 16 in the Expense Report domain and 8 in the Transcript domain.
Levels 3 and 4 are reserved as stretch goals for future agent capabilities.

Evaluation Protocol.

We establish a human performance baseline to define optimal evaluation step counts for each task.
For reference, a human operator is usually able to complete Level 1 and 2
Expense Report
tasks within 150 steps; thus, we evaluate agents at 50, 100, 150, and 200 steps to provide a reasonable margin.
We report the average SWA for all the tasks across the 2 levels for the same execution environment.
For the
Transcript
domain, we adjust the evaluation based on difficulty.
Considering the simplicity of the Level 1 tasks in the
Transcript
domain, Level 1 tasks (completed by humans <100 steps) are evaluated at 50 and 100 steps, while more complicated Level 2 tasks utilise the full 200-step range to ensure a comparable metric against human executors.
Due to the difference in the maximum evaluation steps, we report the average SWA for tasks in the same execution environment at two levels, respectively.
For every agent and human operator, we test the result of 1 run on each task.

However, our agent continues to surpass all baselines in terms of partial progress (SWA).
We provide additional fine-grained results and analysis in
section

A.3
.
We now introduce more challenges caused by the long-horizon tasks and the execution environments.

Common problems for long-horizon, repetitive tasks.

We observe that there are primarily three challenges for the baseline agents as mentioned in
fig.

6
, which are further exemplified by the qualitative result of an AgentS2.5+GPT5 failure case in
section

A.3.3
.

Specifically, UI-TARS-1.5-7B and OpenCUA-7B attempt to execute sub-workflows without logical grounding; this leads to severe hallucinations and results in zero SWA across both domains.
Though the stronger AgentS2.5+GPT-5 baseline outperforms these smaller models, it still suffers from hallucinations.
A primary reason for these failures is that models often prioritise action over comprehension, attempting to populate the system without first locating the necessary source data.
By incorporating demonstrations via
F
C
W
D
, AgentS2.5+GPT5 is guided to first comprehend the execution environment, then locate and verify the required information before populating the system, which significantly improves accuracy.

Challenges from execution environments.

We observe that, in general, populating web systems (in both the expense report and transcript domains) is more manageable for agents than manipulating spreadsheets.
As detailed in
section

A.3
, agents achieve a higher completion rate (Sub-Workflow Finished) for sub-workflows in web domains.
This is largely because web interfaces are more intuitive and possess higher fault tolerance: mistakes are isolated and rarely affect the surrounding data fields.
However, interacting with spreadsheets demands high precision.
A single error in command inputs, such as operating in the wrong mode, can catastrophically wipe out information in adjacent cells.
Consequently, the impact of
F
C
W
D
differs by environment.
In web systems, a demonstration reduces the logic gap, leading to significant accuracy improvements.
However, improvements in spreadsheet environments are constrained by the demand for precise execution, a requirement that extends beyond logic-driven demonstrations.

Related Works

AI agents extend LLMs by perceiving, reasoning, and acting.
We specifically target Computer-Use Agents (CUAs) operating within digital OS environments.
Here, we introduce the background of CUA benchmarks, common approaches, and learning-from-demonstration techniques.

Benchmarks.

Benchmarking for CUAs generally divides into
end-to-end workflow evaluations
and
component-specific evaluations
.
The former measures success rates across desktop
28
;
1
, web
7
, and mobile
21
;
18
;
15
domains, focusing on complex coordination and robustness.
The latter category focuses on fine-grained capabilities, e.g.
13
;
16
assess UI grounding accuracy, and
24
offers trajectory-level analysis.
However, neither adequately captures the challenges of long-horizon, repetitive CUA tasks.

Computer-Use Agents.

Existing computer-use agents follow two primary paradigms:
end-to-end specialised models
and
agentic frameworks
.
End-to-end systems
17
;
9
;
24
train a monolithic model to manage the entire perception-reasoning-action loop, encompassing planning, grounding, execution, and reflection.
Notable examples include AGUVIS
29
for unified vision-based control, UI-TARS
20
for human-like keyboard and mouse input, and OpenAI’s Operator
19
, which combines GPT-4o with RL-based GUI control.
In contrast, agentic frameworks
27
;
31
;
32
;
10
;
5
;
26
decompose the workflow into modular components (planning, grounding, execution) managed by an orchestrator.
For instance, AgentS
2
;
3
sequences these modules explicitly.
CoAct
11
coordinates distinct code and GUI agents for CUA tasks.
Despite this progress, current methods struggle with long-horizon, repetitive tasks, failing to master the logic of individual sub-workflows and sustain the long-horizon execution consistency.

Learning from Demonstration.

Learning from human demonstrations is a well-established paradigm, particularly in robotics
22
;
4
;
6
;
14
;
30
.
In the realm of agents, several recent works have explored leveraging human demonstrations in other contexts.
AdaptAgent
23
utilises few-shot demonstrations to aid web agent generalisation, IFR Agent
25
leverages them to interpret user intent in mobile settings, and Interaction-Driven Browsing
33
explores human-in-the-loop strategies for complex browsing.
Despite these advances, leveraging demonstrations for long-horizon, repetitive tasks faces a unique bottleneck: the extensive duration of a complete workflow for demonstration typically exceeds the effective context limit of current CUAs, preventing the direct context integration of a full task demonstration.

6
Conclusion

This paper investigates long-horizon, repetitive tasks, a complex CUA scenario involving iterative data processing that can extend to extreme lengths.
We propose OS-Marathon, the first benchmark designed to evaluate this capability.
Spanning 242 tasks across 2 domains, OS-Marathon establishes a baseline for current leading agents in this demanding setting.
To tackle the observed performance gaps, we propose a cost-effective method using Few-Shot Condensed Workflow Demonstrations based on semantic key steps, enabling agents to learn and execute these long-horizon tasks effectively.
Extensive experiments demonstrate both the complexity of this task and the effectiveness of our proposed method, laying a foundation for future CUA research on long-horizon, repetitive tasks.

Limitations

Context Window Constraints.

Although
F
C
W
D
significantly reduces context consumption, enabling the inclusion of demonstrations for long-horizon tasks, the inherent context window limits of the underlying model persist.
Consequently, for extremely complex workflows requiring a large number of key steps, fitting the full demonstration may still prove challenging.

Loose Constraints on Execution.

Although context-embedded demonstrations guide agents in global planning and sub-workflow logic, they function as soft suggestions rather than hard constraints.
There is no explicit enforcement mechanism to ensure the agent adheres to the global plan.
Consequently, the agent still struggles to complete the full workflow in higher difficulty levels, as evidenced by the 0% success rate in both the expense report domain and Level 2 of the transcript domain.

In future work, we aim to investigate more robust constraint mechanisms to effectively guide agents through such long-horizon, repetitive tasks.

More Evaluation Results

A.3.1
More Details of the Metrics

We introduce more granular evaluation metrics used in our experiments in this section.
Sub-Workflow Attempted (SWAt) and Sub-Workflow Finished (SWF) are applied to all the tasks to evaluate workflow completeness.
SWAt measures the proportion of sub-workflows initiated, while SWF measures the proportion fully executed, irrespective of data correctness.
Formally, for a long-horizon, repetitive task
\mathbf{M}
consisting of
N
sub-workflows
\{\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{N}\}
, let
p
denote the number of sub-workflows attempted, and
q
denote the number of sub-workflows finished. We define:

\text{SWAt}=\frac{p}{N},\quad\text{SWF}=\frac{q}{N}

Beyond these 2 universal metrics, we introduce metrics specifically tailored for the web and spreadsheet environments within the Expense Report domain.
These allow for a more granular assessment for different environments, necessitated by the higher complexity of sub-workflows in these settings.

Expense Report Domain: Web System.

For web system environments in the expense report domain, we also evaluate the accuracy in each field.
Let
n_{\text{D}},n_{\text{Ca}},n_{\text{P}},n_{\text{Cu}},n_{\text{A}}
be the number of the Date, Category, Payment Method, Currency, and Amount correctly finished for a long-horizon, repetitive task
\mathbf{M}
, the field-level accuracy for each field is
\frac{n_{\text{D}}}{N},\frac{n_{\text{Ca}}}{N},\frac{n_{\text{P}}}{N},\frac{n_{\text{Cu}}}{N},\frac{n_{\text{A}}}{N}
, respectively.

Expense Report Domain: Spreadsheet.

In addition to the universal metrics, we introduce two supplementary metrics specific to the spreadsheet environment within the expense report domain: Table Style Preservation and Meta-Information Accuracy.
Table Style Preservation is a boolean indicator that assesses the agent’s ability to maintain the structural integrity of the spreadsheet.
It evaluates whether immutable cells (e.g., headers, titles, etc.) remain unchanged / unaffected during execution.
This metric indicates agents’ precision in table manipulation.
Meta-Information Accuracy evaluates the correctness of summary cells, such as item totals or sub-category aggregations.
It is calculated as the ratio of correctly populated meta-information cells to the total number of required meta-information cells.

Expense Report Domain (Web Environment).

We observe notably higher accuracy for the

This disparity stems from the lower complexity of these inputs: the category can often be inferred directly from the filename or image preview, while the payment method involves a binary choice, allowing even random guessing to achieve a high baseline accuracy.

Besides this, we observe that performance varies significantly by model capability.
Weaker models, such as OpenCUA-7B and UI-TARS-1.5-7B, exhibit a low Sub-Workflow Finished (SWF) rate; their limited basic entry capabilities prevent them from even populating these fields.
In contrast, AgentS2.5+GPT5 demonstrates superior navigation skills, allowing it to complete sub-workflows and achieve high accuracy in the category field.
However, its accuracy for the payment method remains below 50%, indicating performance worse than random guessing.
For the remaining three fields, which require grounded document understanding, agents’ logical incoherence leads to hallucination, causing them low accuracy.
With

W

Expense Report Domain (Spreadsheet Environment).

Performance deteriorates significantly in the spreadsheet environment compared to the web system.
This decline is driven by two factors: visibility constraints (requiring horizontal scrolling to reveal cells) and a more complicated multi-app coordination (retrieving conversion rates from an external CSV).
Agents also face difficulties with Table Style Preservation.
We observe frequent modal confusion, where agents attempt to input text while in cell-selection mode, inadvertently deleting existing spreadsheet content.
Furthermore, regarding Meta-Information Accuracy, since summary calculations occur at the end of the workflow, the agents’ inability to complete the prerequisite sub-workflows results in a zero completion rate for meta-information.
