Radiotherapy, inflammation, and the myeloid perspective
Authors: Kaiting Yang, András Piffkó, Chuanhui Han, Liufu Deng, Ralph R. Weichselbaum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiotherapy (RT) has long been regarded as a local treatment modality designed to eradicate tumor cells through targeted deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) damage and, more recently, as an in situ vaccine capable of triggering the abscopal effect. However, increasing evidence now defines RT as a systemic immune modulatory intervention that elicits both antitumor and tumor growth-promoting effects. The recently described “badscopal effect”, the paradoxical progression or growth acceleration of non-irradiated disease associated with systemic immunosuppressive and growth-factor programs initiated by the irradiated lesion, highlights that RT may, under certain inflammatory and cellular contexts, foster immune tolerance or accelerate distant disease progression. Central to this duality is the host’s capacity to integrate and balance antitumor and pro-tumor responses following RT. Unlike the relatively binary activation states often attributed to T cells, myeloid cells exhibit high plasticity, integrating signals of DNA damage, hypoxia, and tissue repair to direct the inflammatory responses that inhibit or promote tumor growth. In this review, we examine how RT induces dual inflammatory programs, as a partial explanation as to why classical abscopal models incompletely capture clinical practice and how myeloid plasticity emerges as a central determinant of systemic responses to RT, with important implications for the rational design of combination therapies.

Introduction

Radiotherapy (RT) was historically regarded as a local cytotoxic modality that eradicates tumor cells through the induction of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) double-strand breaks [1]. It is now increasingly recognized that RT also works as a potent inducer of inflammation at both local and systemic levels which differentially affect radiotherapy results. Importantly, these effects cannot be explained by the activation or suppression of a single immune cell type, but instead reflect a plastic and complex host environment that determines how radiation-induced inflammation is resolved. Though RT is increasingly being explored in combination with immunotherapy to enhance systemic antitumor responses, progression at distant, non-irradiated sites remains a major cause of treatment failure. The central question, therefore, is not simply whether RT can induce antitumor immunity, but why this response is so often incomplete, unstable, or diverted into suppressive systemic remodeling. One unfavorable manifestation of this systemic branch is the “badscopal effect”, defined as paradoxical progression or growth acceleration of non-irradiated disease associated with systemic immunosuppressive and growth-factor programs initiated by the irradiated lesion. Myeloid populations rapidly sense the injury, hypoxic, metabolic, vascular, and repair-associated cues generated after irradiation. Depending on context, they may support antigen presentation and T-cell priming or instead drive Treg enrichment, effector T-cell suppression, angiogenesis, extracellular-matrix remodeling, and wound-healing programs. Myeloid plasticity provides a useful perspective for understanding whether RT-associated inflammation is resolved into productive antitumor immunity or pathological immune suppression.

From radiation injury to divergent inflammatory programs

Direct cytotoxic effects of ionizing radiation

Ionizing radiation (IR) induces fundamental biological cellular processes commonly described by the “5 Rs of radiotherapy”-repair, redistribution/reassortment, repopulation, reoxygenation and radiosensitivity [2], 3]. While IR can directly damage DNA, its primary biological impact occurs through the radiolysis of water. This process generates reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to oxidative stress and subsequent protein modifications, including oxidation of critical thiol groups and disruption of redox enzyme activity, ultimately contributing to cell death [4], 5]. Recently, it was reported that RT can engage additional cell-death programs, such as cuproptosis, providing additional evidence of how radiation-induced cellular stress may contribute to actionable vulnerabilities [6]. These cytotoxic effects are determined among others by overall dose, fractionation and the timing of RT delivery, and importantly, they are not limited to tumor cells, but also affect stromal, immune, endothelial, and other host cells, as well as the surrounding normal tissues within and distant from the irradiated field, thereby contributing to vascular injury, tissue-repair responses, and inflammatory remodeling (also reviewed in Barker et al. Nat Rev Cancer 2015 [7]).

Radiation-induced immunogenic inflammation

IR generates nucleic acid danger signals from damaged nuclei and mitochondria, together with damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), calreticulin, and adenosine triphosphate (ATP). These signals collectively activate innate sensing pathways and promote immunogenic cell death (ICD), characterized by the exposure or release of DAMPs, tumor-associated antigens, and neoantigens arising from DNA damage-induced mutations [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]. In particular, radiation-induced DNA damage, including double-strand breaks, can give rise to cytosolic DNA species, such as cytosolic chromatin fragments, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), and DNA associated with unstable micronuclei, that engage cyclic guanosine monophosphate–adenosine monophosphate (GMP-AMP) synthase (cGAS) and activate stimulator of interferon genes (STING), leading to downstream TBK1-IRF3-NF-κB signaling and subsequent production of type I interferons (IFN-I) and inflammatory cytokines [9]. However, the precise cytosolic DNA pool that predominates in cGAS activation after irradiation remains incompletely resolved. Although cGAS association with micronuclei has been widely reported [14], emerging evidence also suggests that mtDNA release, including that associated with MLKL-dependent membrane permeabilization, can contribute to cGAS-STING activation after RT [15], [16], [17]. Concurrently, aberrant ribonucleic acid (RNA) species released following irradiation can engage retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I)-like receptor (RLR) signaling, through receptors including RIG-I, MDA5, and LGP2, and downstream signaling via the mitochondrial adaptor MAVS, providing an additional pathway for type I interferon and inflammatory cytokine production [10], 18].

At the level of the irradiated tissue, these nucleic acid-sensing pathways and ICD together enhance dendritic cell (DC) recruitment, maturation, antigen presentation to promote cross-priming of CD8+ T cells, which is critical for optimal anti-tumor effects of RT [9], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]. Beyond nucleic acid sensing, IR induces a broad spectrum of cytokines, most notably tumor necrosis factor (TNF), IL-6, and IFN-I20 [25], [26], [27]. It also activates NLRP3 and AIM2 inflammasomes, which drive the maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18, triggering downstream apoptosis or pyroptosis [28], 29]. In parallel, IR disrupts endothelial integrity and alters vascular permeability, perfusion, and oxygenation, thereby reshaping cytokine gradients and immune cell trafficking [30], [31], [32].

These local molecular and tissue-level inflammatory programs are not confined to the irradiated lesion, but may extend beyond the local tumor microenvironment and contribute to broader immune remodeling [33]. Consistent with this view, both preclinical models and clinical studies have shown that RT is associated with increased levels of circulating cytokines such as IFN-I, IL-1β, and TNF, as well as altered composition and activation states of peripheral immune cells [34], 35].

Radiation-induced immunosuppression

The immunogenic effects of RT are often counterbalanced by tumor-intrinsic negative feedback loops and broader immunosuppressive mechanisms. High-dose IR triggers the expression of DNA exonuclease TREX1 at doses above 12–18 Gy per fraction, which is reported to degrade cytosolic DNA and thereby attenuates STING activity [36]. This dose-dependent threshold has important implications for fractionation strategies in radioimmunotherapy combinations. Tumor-intrinsic CASP9 signaling also suppresses IFN-I production post IR [16]. In addition, IR can activate tumor-intrinsic NF-κB-related programs that extend beyond cell survival to induce immunoregulatory cytokine and chemokine outputs, thereby limiting productive antitumor immune signaling and setting the stage for broader post-RT immunosuppression [37]. Although the TREX1 threshold provides one mechanistic example of dose-dependent immune regulation, it should not be interpreted as the only relevant dosing principle. Fraction size, total dose, number of fractions, timing relative to systemic therapy, treated volume, and irradiation of organs, or tumor-draining lymph nodes may each shift the balance between productive innate activation and suppressive remodeling.

At the tissue level, these counter-regulatory effects are accompanied by induction of TGF-β, CCL-2, CCL-22 and related chemokine networks, driving the recruitment of regulatory T cells (Tregs), M2-like tumor associated macrophages (TAMs) and myeloid-derived suppressive cells (MDSCs) into the tumor microenvironment (TME) [38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43]. Among these populations, Tregs represent a canonical suppressive arm that restrains antitumor immunity through CTLA-4-dependent attenuation of costimulatory function [44], whereas TAMs and MDSCs further reinforce immune suppression through vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), IL-10, TGF-β, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). Moreover, IR-induced TGF-β may additionally promote peripheral conversion toward induced Treg states and suppressive myeloid programs, thereby contributing to a broader immunoregulatory milieu [45].

In parallel, IR-induced vascular disruption and metabolic stress enhance hypoxia, while purinergic signaling mediated by the ATP-CD39-CD73-adenosine and P2RX7 axes reinforces wound-healing phenotypes that contribute to radioresistance [12], 46], 47]. ATP released during ICD is rapidly converted to adenosine by ectonucleotidases CD39 and CD73, and adenosine binding to A2A and A2B receptors on immune cells suppresses DC and effector T cell function, while promoting Treg expansion and M2 macrophage polarization [48], 49]. Radiation-induced ENPP1 further contributes to immunosuppression by both degrading cGAMP and promoting adenosine production [50].

These tumor-intrinsic and tissue-level counter-regulatory programs extend beyond the irradiated lesion and contribute to broader systemic immune remodeling. Radiation-induced lymphopenia represents one critical systemic immunosuppressive effect, with severity correlating with radiation dose, treatment volume and fractionation; grade ≥3 lymphopenia is associated with significantly worse overall survival across multiple solid tumor types [51]. Single-cell and T cell receptor (TCR)-sequencing analyses in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) further suggest that clinical RT can preferentially deplete tumor antigen-specific T-cell clonotypes, highlighting that post-RT lymphoid impairment may involve not only reduced lymphocyte counts but also loss of functionally relevant antitumor clones [52]. Recent reviews and modeling studies further support the clinical relevance of radiation-induced lymphopenia and emphasize the need to integrate dose-volume and immune-preservation considerations into RT planning [53], 54]. Mechanistically, lymphopenia results not only from direct killing of circulating lymphocytes but also from irradiation of lymphoid organs and tumor-draining lymph nodes. Notably, Tregs appear relatively more radioresistant than many effector T-cell subsets, such that radiation-induced lymphopenia may result in their relative enrichment in the TME [38]. Importantly, emerging evidence suggests that radiation-induced lymphopenia also intersects with suppressive myeloid remodeling, particularly expansion of circulating MDSCs [55].

Together, the dynamic balance between these immunogenic and suppressive programs determines the inflammatory consequences of RT across local, tissue-level, and systemic scales (Figure 1). Thus, the overall immunological consequence of radiotherapy is not immune activation per se, but the induction of a systemic inflammatory state. Radiotherapy-induced inflammation as a dynamic local-to-systemic network. Ionizing radiation (IR) induces multicompartment injury within the irradiated tissue niche, including deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) double-strand breaks, reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, vascular disruption, and injury to tumor, stromal, endothelial, and immune cells. These insults generate cytosolic nuclear and mitochondrial DNA, aberrant ribonucleic acid (RNA) species, and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including adenosine triphosphate (ATP), HMGB1, and calreticulin, thereby converting local tissue injury into an inflammatory signaling network. Within irradiated tumor and host cells, nucleic acid sensing pathways, including cyclic guanosine monophosphate–adenosine monophosphate (GMP-AMP) synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) and retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I)-like receptor signaling, promote type I interferons (IFN-I) and inflammatory cytokine production, which support dendritic-cell recruitment and maturation, antigen presentation, cross-priming, and activation of CD8+ T cells. In parallel, radiation-induced endothelial remodeling alters vascular permeability, perfusion, oxygenation, and immune-cell trafficking, further shaping the local inflammatory milieu and facilitating dissemination of inflammatory cues beyond the irradiated site. This immunogenic program is simultaneously opposed by counter-regulatory suppressive circuits. High-dose irradiation induces TREX1, which limits cytosolic DNA sensing and dampens STING activity. In addition, ATP released during immunogenic cell death can be converted by CD39 and CD73 into adenosine, which suppresses dendritic-cell and effector T-cell function while favoring suppressive myeloid and Treg phenotypes; ENPP1 further restrains antitumor immunity by degrading cGAMP. At the tissue level, TGF-β-, CCL2-, and CCL22-associated networks promote recruitment or expansion of Tregs, M2-like tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), thereby suppressing both priming and effector function. Together, these coupled activating and suppressive circuits extend beyond the local tumor microenvironment to reshape the systemic immune state, including circulating cytokines, lymphopenia, lymphoid and myeloid remodeling, and conditioning of distant non-irradiated lesions. Thus, the principal immunological consequence of radiotherapy is the induction of a systemic inflammatory state, and therapeutic outcome is determined by the balance between immunogenic amplification and counter-regulatory suppression, favoring either productive systemic antitumor immunity and abscopal potential or inflammatory tolerance, immune resistance, and badscopal progression.

Beyond the classical abscopal effect

The classical abscopal effect is a rare but well-documented phenomenon where localized RT to a primary tumor is followed by regression of distant, non-irradiated tumors. Preclinical evidence shows that radiotherapy can increase tumor antigen availability and facilitate the priming and activation of tumor-specific T cells, providing a clear mechanistic rationale for combining RT with immune checkpoint blockade [56], 57]. Clinical experience, however, particularly in the context of metastasis-directed therapies, has revealed a more complex and often paradoxical pattern. Across multiple randomized trials of stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) in oligometastatic disease, although improved outcome is achieved in a subset of patients, durable systemic control remains inconsistent, and progression at distant, untreated sites is still common [58], 59]. These observations suggest that the traditional abscopal framework does not fully explain the complexity of systemic outcomes observed after RT in clinical practice.

One possible explanation for why classical preclinical abscopal models incompletely capture clinical outcomes is that most preclinical abscopal models employ bilateral implantation of the same tumor cell line, creating immunologically homogeneous lesions that amplify antigen-specific T-cell responses while minimizing tumor heterogeneity and competing inflammatory programs. Human cancers typically harbor spatially and temporally distinct metastatic clones that engage divergent immune circuits. Addressing this gap, a recent study by Hou et al. employed a heterogeneous two-tumor model in which two immunologically distinct tumors were implanted in the same host and only one lesion was irradiated [60]. In this setting, RT effectively controlled the irradiated tumor but paradoxically accelerated growth of the unirradiated, heterologous tumor, revealing a badscopal effect that was not apparent in conventional homogeneous models. Although such heterologous models do not fully mirror the clinical setting, where metastatic lesions are rarely completely unrelated and may still be shaped by pre-existing shared immunity, they are particularly useful for exposing systemic suppressive and inflammatory programs that are masked in conventional homogeneous models. Mechanistically, this phenotype was associated with systemic expansion of MDSCs and upregulation of immunosuppressive pathways, and could be mitigated by MDSC depletion or PD-L1 blockade.

Accordingly, “badscopal” should be reserved for paradoxical progression or growth acceleration of non-irradiated disease temporally and mechanistically linked to systemic immunosuppressive and growth-factor programs initiated by the irradiated lesion. It should be distinguished from broader systemic immune perturbations after RT, such as lymphopenia, cytokine changes, inflammatory toxicity, or pharmacodynamic immune-cell shifts, which may occur without constituting a badscopal oncologic outcome.

A recent study from Piffkó and colleagues also provided preliminary hypothesis-generating clinical evidence consistent with a myeloid-dominated systemic response to RT [61]. Analyses of patient samples before and after SBRT revealed that amphiregulin (AREG) expression, either at baseline or induced following SBRT, was strongly correlated with expansion of circulating CD14+CD33+pEGFR+ myeloid populations and poor systemic disease control. Mechanistically, tumor-derived AREG reprogrammed mononuclear phagocytes, impaired phagocytosis, and reinforced CD47-associated anti-phagocytic signaling. These outcomes cannot be attributed solely to impaired T-cell priming, suggesting that RT drives broader systemic immunologic remodeling that is not captured by classical abscopal frameworks centered exclusively on adaptive immunity. This interpretation is also supported by independent evidence from tissue-injury settings, in which inflammation-driven, M2 macrophage-associated EGFR ligands, including AREG, have been shown to awaken dormant cancer cells [62]. These findings do not diminish the importance of T cell-mediated tumor immunity, but instead highlight the inflammatory constraints that often limit its effective engagement following RT.

RT-induced suppressive myeloid plasticity

Myeloid cells are increasingly recognized as major determinants of whether radiation-induced inflammation resolves into antitumor immunity or pathological immune suppression. Across heterogeneous preclinical and clinical RT settings, neutrophil, MDSC-like, and macrophage responses have been repeatedly observed, but their direction and functional consequence depend on dose, fractionation, tissue context, sampling time, and concurrent therapy [63], 64]. Mechanistically, RT-induced myeloid remodeling can be viewed as a response to coupled injury and repair signals rather than as a single pathway (Figure 2). DAMPs, nucleic acid sensing, inflammatory cytokines, hypoxia, vascular injury, and stromal repair cues promote the recruitment and reprogramming of neutrophils, MDSCs, and monocyte-macrophage populations. These cells can then reshape the inflammatory outcome, thereby limiting antigen presentation and effector T-cell function while favoring tissue repair, immune tolerance, and metastatic niche support.

Radiotherapy (RT)-induced suppressive myeloid plasticity. RT reshapes myeloid-cell trajectories rather than simply increasing myeloid abundance. RT-induced cytokines, chemokines, and metabolic cues drive distinct but interconnected programs across neutrophils, myeloid-derived suppressive cells (MDSCs), and monocyte-macrophage lineages. In the neutrophil compartment, G-CSF and CXCR2 ligands, including IL-8/CXCL8, promote recruitment, whereas persistent recruitment biases neutrophils from acute inflammatory states toward pro-tumor phenotypes associated with NET formation, metastatic support, and adverse systemic correlates such as elevated post-RT neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR). RT also promotes the expansion and reprogramming of MDSCs from granulocytic and monocytic progenitors through CSF1, CCL2, IL-6, and GM-CSF, leading to suppressive programs characterized by PD-L1, nitric oxide (NO), reactive oxygen species (ROS), and Arg-1 that inhibit T-cell proliferation and effector function. In parallel, recruited monocytes can differentiate into inflammatory/phagocytic macrophages, but sustained cytokine and metabolic cues bias macrophage states toward immunosuppressive and tissue-repair phenotypes enriched for vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), IL-10, TGF-β, and matrix-remodeling functions. Together, these RT-driven myeloid programs may transiently support inflammatory clearance, but more commonly converge on suppressive remodeling, metastatic progression, and poor clinical outcome.

IR stimulates rapid neutrophil recruitment through induction of G-CSF and CXCR2 ligands, leading to transient or sustained accumulation of neutrophils within the TME [65], [66], [67]. These observations are derived mainly from preclinical tumor-irradiation models using single-dose local RT, including regimens such as 8–20 Gy in different tumor systems, as well as fractionated tumor-irradiation settings such as 2 × 5 Gy. While early neutrophil responses may contribute to acute inflammatory clearance, prolonged or dysregulated neutrophil recruitment following RT frequently favors immunosuppressive and pro-tumorigenic phenotypes, including the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps and support of metastatic niche formation [63], 68], 69]. Recent studies have shown that direct irradiation of the lung or chronic stress can increase neutrophil recruitment to the lungs and promote metastasis [70], 71]. Elevated circulating IL-8/CXCL8 was independently associated with inferior outcome in patients treated with SBRT plus immunotherapy [72]. Consistent with these observations, elevated post-RT neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) is found correlated with poor clinical outcomes across multiple cancer types, underscoring the systemic relevance of neutrophil plasticity [73]. Thereby, therapeutic neutralization of IL-8/CXCL8 is now being explored in clinical trials that combine SBRT with immunotherapy in metastases [74].

A consistent finding across preclinical models and clinical studies is that RT promotes the expansion and functional reprogramming of MDSCs at both local and systemic levels [41], 42]. However, these studies involve heterogeneous treatment contexts, including preclinical local irradiation, definitive radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy, and post-treatment blood immune-monitoring cohorts. MDSCs arise from both monocytic and granulocytic progenitors, and radiation-induced cytokines and chemokines, including CSF1, CCL2, IL-6, and GM-CSF, drive myelopoiesis and facilitate MDSC recruitment following RT. Functionally, MDSCs promote tumor progression by producing nitric oxide (NO) and ROS, and suppress not only T-cell proliferation and effector function, but also broader antitumor immune responses, including antigen-presenting and innate immune functions, by upregulation of arginase-1 (Arg-1), PD-L1 and related suppressive programs [41]. Studies, particularly in non-small cell lung cancer, head and neck cancer, esophageal cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma and glioblastoma, have reported increased levels of circulating MDSCs after RT [55], 64], [75], [76], [77], [78], [79], [80]. Importantly, post-RT MDSC increase correlates with poorer prognosis, increased metastatic progression, and reduced overall survival, highlighting their role as mediators of radiation-induced immune tolerance rather than bystanders of inflammation. These clinical associations should be interpreted cautiously because RT dose and regimen, concurrent systemic therapy, sampling time, and MDSC definitions differ substantially across studies. MDSC accumulation following RT may reflect a combination of expansion and recruitment and altered differentiation trajectories across monocyte-macrophage and neutrophil lineages. Recent reviews of RT-MDSC biology similarly emphasize that MDSC responses to RT are dynamic and context-dependent, varying with radiation modality, dose, fractionation, tumor type, and treatment timing [81].

Tumor-associated monocytes and macrophages further exemplify the plastic nature of myeloid responses to RT. Following irradiation, RT can induce inflammatory macrophage states, characterized by activation of cGAS-STING and NF-κB signaling and upregulation of TNF, IL-12, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and interferon-responsive genes [82], [83], [84], [85]. A recent study identified a radiation-induced CXCL13-CXCR5 axis in which irradiated tumors recruit CXCR5+ monocytes that suppress CD8+ T-cell function through PD-L1 and subsequently differentiate toward M2-like macrophages under GM-CSF signaling [86]. The supporting evidence includes in vitro macrophage irradiation, low-dose irradiation models, and preclinical local tumor-irradiation studies. However, sustained radiation-induced cytokine and metabolic cues subsequently bias monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation toward heterogeneous tissue-repair and immunosuppressive states enriched for TGF-β, VEGF, IL-10, and matrix-remodeling enzymes [42].

Novel therapeutic opportunities targeting myeloid plasticity

Importantly, radiation-conditioned changes within the myeloid compartment are not terminal but remain plastic, thereby offering opportunities for immunomodulatory intervention aimed either at reinforcing productive inflammatory responses or at alleviating myeloid-cell-mediated immunosuppression.

Targeting insufficient innate activation

Several strategies aim to directly enhance innate immune activation following RT. STING agonists represent a particularly promising approach, which have been shown to activate tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, natural killer cell (NK) and DCs in synergy with low-dose radiation and anti-PD-1 and promote vascular normalization and tertiary lymphoid structure formation [97], 98]. Nanoparticle-mediated delivery of STING agonists addresses pharmacokinetic limitations of conventional agonists, recently nanoscale coordination polymers (NCPs) have been shown to directly boost endothelial and macrophage STING activation, and promote antitumor effect in combination with IR [99], 100]. Related materials-based approaches, including STING agonist-conjugated metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) and bifunctional MOFs that couple radiosensitization with sustained STING activation, further support the potential of nanoplatforms to reshape the irradiated tumor microenvironment and augment radioimmunotherapy [101], 102]. However, the translational promise of this strategy has been limited by the modest clinical progress of STING agonists to date, drug delivery problems, as well as by the highly context- and cell-type-dependent consequences of STING signaling. Whereas STING activation in dendritic cells may favor productive antigen priming, activation in T cells or certain myeloid compartments may instead induce cell death or promote counter-regulatory programs, including regulatory B cells (Breg) induction, MDSC infiltration, and broader immunosuppressive remodeling of the TME [40], 103], 104]. Accordingly, the key translational challenge may be not simply whether to activate STING after RT, but how to confine its activation to the appropriate cellular compartments, at the appropriate dose and timing. In this context, TAK-676 has entered clinical evaluation with pembrolizumab following RT and has shown acceptable tolerability with preliminary activity in early-phase testing [105], which should be interpreted primarily as evidence of feasibility rather than validation of efficacy.

CD40 agonists provide another mechanism to directly enhance DC-mediated antitumor effects. Preclinical studies demonstrate that combining CD40 agonists with RT generates CD8+ T-cell-dependent immunity [106] and bispecific antibodies targeting CD40 activation promote DC-mediated anti-tumor effects [107]. Clinically, CD40-directed strategies have now entered early RT-combination testing; for example, a phase I/II trial is evaluating FLT3 ligand, an agonistic CD40 antibody, and SBRT in advanced NSCLC [108], underscoring the translational interest in integrating CD40 activation with radiotherapy, although efficacy data in the RT setting remain preliminary.

Targeting suppressive myeloid remodeling

With the goal of alleviating immunosuppression, recent preclinical studies have shown that pharmacologic or genetic perturbation of TGF-β, CSF1R, or AREG-EGFR signaling can reprogram TAM function and restore antitumor immunity [42], 61], 109], 110]. CSF1R inhibition represents a well-characterized approach to target radiation-recruited immunosuppressive myeloid populations. RT upregulates CSF1 expression in tumors; CSF1R inhibitors (e.g. PLX3397/Pexidartinib, Cabiralizumab) deplete TAMs and prevent monocyte differentiation into immunosuppressive, pro-angiogenic macrophages, substantially enhancing survival in glioblastoma and prostate cancer models when combined with RT [109], 111], 112]. A phase I trial demonstrated acceptable toxicity and modest antitumor activity of Cabiralizumab in combination with Nivolumab and SBRT [92]. The limited clinical advancement of this strategy suggests that blockade of a single TAM-supporting axis may be insufficient in the post-RT setting, and also raises the possibility that broad depletion may be less effective than more selective reprogramming of suppressive myeloid states. Notably, elevated pre-SBRT serum IL-8 was associated with lack of response in this study [92], further supporting the idea that chemokine-driven myeloid programs may represent an additional layer of resistance not fully addressed by CSF1R inhibition alone. Consistent with this possibility, a follow-up trial (NCT04572451) is evaluating SBRT with anti-PD1 and an anti-IL-8 antibody (BMS-986253) [93]. This trial also illustrates the emerging clinical exploration of neutrophil-axis strategies in combination with RT. More broadly, although CXCR1/2-targeted approaches have entered clinical testing, RT-specific combinations remain underdeveloped. Together, these findings highlight that post-RT immunosuppression is unlikely to be governed by a single myeloid axis, and may therefore require biomarker-guided combination strategies rather than one-dimensional depletion approaches.

Among suppressive metabolic circuits, the ATP-CD39-CD73-adenosine axis is a direct bridge between RT-induced immunogenic damage and post-RT immune tolerance. RT can release ATP as a danger signal, but rapid conversion by CD39 and CD73 generates adenosine, which suppresses DC and effector T-cell function while promoting Treg expansion and M2-like macrophage polarization. Thus, CD73 blockade and A2A/A2B receptor antagonism are rational RT-combination strategies because they aim to preserve the immunogenic meaning of radiation-induced danger signals rather than allowing these signals to resolve into a wound-healing program. This logic is now being tested in clinically registered combinations, including SBRT with adenosine signaling modulation and PD-1 blockade in hormone-sensitive oligometastatic prostate cancer (NCT05915442), neoadjuvant chemotherapy plus SBRT with durvalumab and/or oleclumab in luminal B breast cancer (NCT03875573), and neoadjuvant pancreatic cancer treatment incorporating SBRT, checkpoint blockade, and CD73/A2A/A2B pathway inhibition (NCT06048484) [94], [95], [96]. These trials are mechanistically important, but adenosine-axis blockade has not yet been validated as a broadly effective method to reverse RT-induced myeloid suppression.

Complementary strategies also aim to target MDSC accumulation and function, although direct clinical validation in combination with RT remains limited and most current evidence is still preclinical. Pharmacological inhibition of YTHDF2, which mediates radiation-induced MDSC expansion via an IR-YTHDF2-NF-κB circuit, overcomes MDSC-induced immunosuppression and improves RT and anti-PD-L1 treatment in mouse models [42]. All-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) has been used in novel nanomedicine approaches [113] to eliminate MDSCs or in combination with RT to induce inflammatory macrophages [85]. ATRA has clinical precedent as an MDSC-modulating agent, but RT-combination evidence remains largely preclinical. More broadly, cabozantinib has MDSC-modulating potential and is being clinically explored in RT-based combinations, but not as a selective MDSC-targeted radiotherapy strategy [114].

Additional approaches seek to restore the phagocytic function rather than simply deplete suppressive populations. In this regard, CD47 blockade and AREG neutralization have both been shown to enhance macrophage-mediated antitumor responses after IR and, in preclinical settings, to support abscopal effects [61], 115], 116]. Collectively, these findings underscore myeloid plasticity as a therapeutically actionable feature of the post-irradiation immune landscape. RT does not impose a fixed myeloid phenotype but rather initiates a dynamic and context-dependent state transition, providing both challenges and opportunities for therapeutic intervention.

Conclusions

RT induces a spectrum of inflammatory states, shaped by the dynamic interplay between tumor cell death, tissue repair, and host immune plasticity. Although the biological effects of RT are influenced by total dose, fraction size, and treatment duration, the ultimate systemic outcome is determined by how radiation shifts the balance between immunostimulatory and immunosuppressive inflammatory programs operating within the TME and across the peripheral immune system. In this context, radiation-induced reprogramming of MDSCs, macrophages, and neutrophils emerges as a central determinant of whether antitumor immunity can be sustained or is overridden by immunosuppressive programs. From this perspective, abscopal and “badscopal” effects are not separate phenomena, but represent divergent systemic outcomes determined by how well the host restores immune balance.

Thus, achieving a transition from durable local control to systemic tumor eradication therefore requires moving beyond simply “pressing the accelerator” or “releasing the brakes” on T cells, toward actively steering myeloid-driven inflammatory responses. Effective combination approaches should actively restrain immunosuppressive myeloid programs, restore innate effector functions, and redirect inflammatory signaling toward immune-stimulatory states. Potential strategies include limiting myeloid recruitment and survival, reprogramming macrophage and MDSC functional states, and integrating myeloid-targeted agents with radiation regimens optimized for immune modulation. When coupled with biomarker-guided patient selection, this framework offers a path to transform RT from a locally effective cytotoxic modality into a predictable and systemically effective immunomodulatory strategy, capable of achieving durable tumor control beyond the irradiated field.

Future myeloid-steering RT trials should integrate candidate biomarkers according to three purposes, pretreatment stratification, mechanism-matched treatment selection, and pharmacodynamic monitoring. Baseline inflammatory indices, circulating myeloid populations, suppressive cytokine or metabolic programs, and treatment-planning variables related to lymphopenia risk may help define the pre-RT immune context, whereas serial immune profiling can assess whether a selected myeloid-directed intervention has achieved the intended state transition. These markers should be regarded as exploratory until prospectively validated as predictive biomarkers for clinical decision-making.
