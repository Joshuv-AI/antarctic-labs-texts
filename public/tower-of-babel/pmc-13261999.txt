Accounting of provincial horizontal carbon eco-compensation standard under multiple participation scenarios
Authors: Junyu Chen, Ke Wang, Chuanming Yang, Huimin Wang, Yang Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As the world’s largest energy consumer and CO2 emitter, China faces severe sustainable development challenges and intense international pressures. The pronounced spatial heterogeneity of CO2 emissions and sinks creates a dynamic pattern of carbon-deficient and carbon-surplus regions. Horizontal carbon eco-compensation activates the market value of carbon sinks, redistributes sink-related benefits, and internalizes both positive and negative environmental externalities, thereby facilitating the valuation of carbon-sink ecosystem services. In this research, CO2 emission space is treated as a tradable ecological product. Within a government-led, market-coordinated accounting framework, carbon-deficient regions provide ecological compensation to carbon-surplus regions, while the latter are compensated for environmental stewardship and opportunity expenses. The CO2 emission quotas of each province are first determined by the entropy method, and a horizontal carbon ecological compensation recipient and payer identification framework is constructed. Next, the compensation standard accounting models under three scenarios of active participation, passive participation and refusal to participate are constructed respectively. Finally, the empirical analysis is carried out with 30 provinces in China as the object set. The results show that: (1) Regions with high CO2 emissions are mostly economically developed, energy-intensive or densely populated areas, while the regions with high CO2 sequestration often have the characteristics of complete ecological patterns, superior hydrothermal conditions or remarkable ecological restoration results. (2) Among the 30 provinces studied, 18 provinces show the characteristics of carbon surplus, which are mainly distributed in the south, central and western regions and parts of the north; the remaining 12 are carbon-deficient regions, concentrated in the eastern coastal and northern regions of China. (3) Under the framework of the provincial horizontal carbon eco-compensation mechanism, this research identifies three behavioral scenarios: active participation, passive participation and refusal to participate. It is found that active participation compensation is the optimal strategy, which provides a basis for the formulation of differentiated horizontal eco-compensation standards and implementation plans.

Introduction

The trend of global warming has never stopped, and its impacts have gradually intensified. In 2024, the global average surface temperature reached the highest level since 1850. The annual average temperature in China exceeds the normal value of 1.0 °C, making 2024 the warmest year in China since 1901. The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (2023) concluded that human activities, more precisely the large-scale greenhouse gas emissions created by these activities, unequivocally drove the observed warming. Excessive CO2 emissions are recognized as the primary driver of the greenhouse effect. Under the medium emission scenario (SSP2-4.5) proposed by IPCC, it is expected that atmospheric CO2 will reach 500 ppm by the middle of this century. As the world’s largest energy consumer and CO2 emitter, China is facing severe sustainable development challenges and huge international pressure. Whether it is China’s CO2 emissions or China’s CO2 sequestration, there is a significant spatial heterogeneity distribution pattern, which logically forms a dynamic and scattered distribution of carbon-deficient areas and carbon-surplus areas. Carbon-deficient areas are mainly due to the one-sided pursuit of economic and social development, and resulting CO2 emissions exceed the carrying capacity of the ecosystem. Such areas have long faced high CO2 emission reduction pressures. Carbon-surplus areas have strong CO2 sequestration capacity due to their own ecological endowment advantages or greater ecological protection, but the compensation for the opportunity cost is very limited. This phenomenon vividly illustrates the incomplete internalization of environmentally positive externalities. Consequently, an urgent imperative exists to translate ecological advantages into economic momentum through carbon compensation mechanisms [1]. Under carbon peak and neutrality targets, not only is it required that the industrial sector reduce CO2 emissions through industrial structure adjustment, but cities are also encouraged to reduce urban CO2 emissions by taking low-carbon measures [2]. Carbon ecological compensation combines ecological compensation theory with low-carbon practice [3] and regards CO2 emissions as tradable ecological products. Excessive CO2 emitters compensate CO2 sink service owners or ecosystem governance subjects through monetary or non-monetary forms. Specifically, the carbon-deficient area usually acts as a buyer of the CO2 emission quota, which is paid to the carbon-surplus area to obtain the corresponding CO2 emission quota; the carbon-surplus area is usually paid for the expenditure of protection costs and opportunity costs while continuing to provide ecosystem services as a seller.

The key point of the construction of the carbon eco-compensation mechanism lies in the setting and determination of carbon compensation standards [4]. The Contingent Valuation Method [5, 6] primarily involves crafting a detailed sequence of questions concerning various benefits or services, which are then presented through surveys. This approach aims to assess individuals’ willingness to pay for enhancements in public products and services, or their inclination to receive compensation for any losses incurred regarding these offerings [7]. The results of this type of research are often more subjective because the willingness data they use are mostly from surveys [8]. The Opportunity Cost Method [9, 10] determines the eco-compensation standard by evaluating the potential income from the sacrificial social-economic development activities to protect the ecosystem [11], However, this method requires very accurate data for input [12]. Based on the objective classification of ecosystems, the Ecosystem Service Valuation Method [13–15] subdivides ecological benefits into multiple ecosystem service types [16] to assess their contribution to human [17]. This method can fully reflect the value of the regional ecosystem, but due to the different calculation indicators, the results achieved by existing researches are usually higher than the beneficiary’s willingness to pay, which result in low public acceptance [18]. The Market Value Method [19, 20] calculates the amount of carbon eco-compensation by multiplying the reference value of carbon eco-compensation and the unit carbon price [21]. The determination of market value with obvious timeliness is directly linked to the carbon market, and has strong operational feasibility at the provincial level.

Existing research predominantly focuses on carbon eco-compensation accounting methodologies, implicitly assuming active stakeholder participation [22, 23]. However, few of these studies have noted the provincial horizontal carbon ecological compensation standard accounting under complex participation, resulting in an absence of compensation standards for non-voluntary situations. To address this gap, this paper categorizes the strategic behavior of compensation providers and recipients into three scenarios: active participation, passive participation, and refusal to participate. It systematically characterizes behavioral heterogeneity across provincial regions under varying participation constraints, thereby constructing differentiated and operational compensation schemes better suited to the complexities encountered in actual compensation processes. Further analysis of carbon compensation practices reveals that some compensating parties, despite expressing willingness to participate, face a willingness-ability imbalance [24] where compensation prices exceed their fiscal capacity, ultimately causing transactions to stall. To address this, this paper refines compensation standards within the three strategic frameworks. For regions actively participating, compensation amounts are moderately reduced, significantly alleviating fiscal pressure and thereby enhancing participation feasibility and enthusiasm. This provides an affordable and sustainable pricing mechanism to facilitate the continued achievement of compensation agreements.

Definition of recipients and payers

Carbon-surplus regions are defined as those whose actual emissions remain within their allocated carbon budget over a given period. Such regions, characterized by strong carbon sink capacity, act as providers of ecological compensation services to carbon-deficient areas. Conversely, carbon-deficient regions exceed their permitted carbon budget. Their high emissions typically stem from intensive industrial activity, high energy consumption, and extensive land-use change.

CO2 transfer between surplus and deficit regions occurs primarily through two channels: atmospheric advection and industrial relocation. Atmospheric advection is a natural process in which atmospheric circulation physically redistributes greenhouse gases such as CO2 across regions [30]. Industrial relocation, by contrast, is driven by human decisions. Under the combined influence of policy instruments, cost differentials, and market dynamics, carbon-intensive industries tend to migrate from jurisdictions with stringent carbon regulations and high carbon prices to those with more lenient frameworks and lower compliance costs. In the context of horizontal carbon ecological compensation, deficient regions - the compensation payers - remit payments to offset their excess emissions, whereas surplus regions - the compensation recipients - receive transfers that reward their net carbon sequestration. Functioning as an environmental policy instrument, this horizontal scheme enables deficient regions to meet their obligations by purchasing allowances from surplus regions, fosters inter-regional cooperation, and simultaneously incentivizes emission reductions and carbon-sink enhancement. Design of compensation system

In the horizontal carbon ecological compensation system, the central government formulates an institutional framework to provide clear norms for the implementation of compensation and coordinates the enthusiasm of different regions to participate in carbon ecological compensation through reward and punishment mechanisms. Market institutions are participants approved by the central government and have both regulatory and market-oriented functions.

Carbon-deficient areas and carbon-surplus areas are flat-level administrative entities with opposite carbon balances and are direct participants in carbon horizontal ecological compensation. In general, carbon-deficient areas need to pay compensation to ecologically protected areas or ecologically damaged areas due to excessive carbon emissions, while carbon-surplus areas are reasonably compensated according to their contribution to emission reductions to make up for the opportunity cost of their sacrificed development. In the framework of government-led and market-coordinated horizontal carbon ecological compensation, in order to prevent ecological compensation from falling into the extreme situation of completely subsidized by the government or the market, the central government uses the reward and punishment mechanism to make up for the lack of market spontaneity and ensure the sustainable operation of compensation, while the market institutions use market-oriented means to combine the authority of the central government with market efficiency to achieve the purpose of optimizing resource allocation (Fig. 1).

Active participation

Active participation by both sides of carbon eco-compensation occurs when local governments act on their own initiative. Carbon-surplus regions, serving as compensation recipients, voluntarily implement measures to protect and enhance carbon sinks, leveraging their demonstrated CO2 sequestration capacity to secure corresponding payments. At the same time, carbon-deficient regions, acting as compensation providers, proactively remit fees because their emissions exceed their sequestration, thereby offsetting the ecological burden they impose on surplus areas. Guided by their actual emissions, sinks, and respective responsibilities for environmental protection, these sub-national governments initiate cross-regional collaboration on carbon compensation, while the central government and market institutions reinforce the process through differentiated rewards and subsidies.

The amount of carbon eco-compensation consists of two parts: the basic carbon eco-compensation value and the reward. The basic carbon ecological compensation value is determined according to the net carbon emissions and the unit carbon price. The reward part is based on the carbon ecological support coefficient and the carbon emission economic contributive coefficient. The reward subsidy standard is constructed by situation, which is divided into four situations.

Passive participation

The passive participation of both payers and recipients undermines effective carbon compensation: carbon-deficient regions exert negative externalities on their neighbors through the spatial diffusion of CO2, but make no voluntary effort to compensate, while carbon-surplus regions that provide ecological benefits seldom assert their right to recompense. Consequently, the central government must intervene by creating a mandatory compensation framework that clearly identifies who pays and who is paid. The implementation of the compensation mechanism does not involve additional incentives or penalties, and the amount of compensation is the basic compensation value.39\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C_{surplus}}=CM{A_{suri}}$$\end{document}40\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C_{deficit}}=CM{A_{defi}}$$\end{document}

Refusal to participate

Non-participation by both payers and recipients is the core challenge of horizontal carbon ecological compensation. Carbon-deficient regions, whose emissions exceed their sinks, degrade the ecological environment of neighboring areas. However, due to their own interests or other reasons, the carbon-deficient areas refuse to participate in the compensation activities, which poses a serious threat to the regional ecological balance and sustainable development. Carbon-surplus regions, conversely, decline to join because they doubt either the emission-reduction commitments or the payment capacity of deficit regions, or they prefer to pursue higher market returns elsewhere. To guarantee the scheme’s effectiveness, the central government therefore imposes a mandatory compensation relationship. The carbon-surplus areas that refuse to participate will not be able to obtain the basic compensation value, and the carbon-deficient areas will be punished by the central government. The penalty is calculated according to the cost of mitigating the excess CO2 they emit.

The cost of CO2 control represents the total economic resources required to mitigate excess CO2 emissions. To calculate this cost for a specific region, first determine its net CO2 emissions and then establish the unit governance cost. Multiplying net emissions by this unit cost yields the region’s total carbon-governance expenditure. Following Lu [35], the minimum abatement cost for carbon capture and storage technology − 219.4 CNY per tonne - is adopted as the unit governance cost. Results

Carbon dioxide emissions and absorption accounting

CO2 emissions in Henan, Shaanxi, Xinjiang, Zhejiang, Hebei, Liaoning, Jiangsu, Guangdong, Inner Mongolia, Shandong and Shanxi all exceed 500 million tonnes. Shanxi posts the highest emissions, at 1.83 billion tonnes, while Qinghai registers the lowest, at 67.33 million tonnes. Hunan, Henan, Guangxi, Yunnan, Xinjiang, Sichuan, Heilongjiang and Inner Mongolia each report carbon absorption above 300 million tonnes. Inner Mongolia achieves the greatest absorption, at 674.31 million tonnes, and Shanghai the smallest, at 5.77 million tonnes. Regions with high CO2 emissions are typically economically developed, energy-intensive, or densely populated. Conversely, areas with strong CO2 sequestration are characterized by abundant forests, grasslands, wetlands, and other ecosystems, favorable climatic conditions, or notable ecological restoration achievements.

Carbon dioxide emission rights allocation

China has pledged to reduce its carbon emission intensity by at least 65% by 2030 compared with the 2005 level. Accordingly, this study adopts the 65% reduction target to derive China’s total CO2 emission allowance for 2022. In 2005, China’s CO2 emissions were 5554.04 million tonnes1, the gross domestic product was 18731.9 billion CNY2, and the CO2 emission intensity was 29,700 tonnes per billion CNY. Assuming that the CO2 emission intensity decreased steadily from 2005 to 2030, it can be calculated that the CO2 emission intensity in 2030 will be about 10,400 tonnes per billion CNY, with an average annual decline rate of about 4.11%. In light of the average annual growth targets of GDP during the 11th, 12th,13th, and 14th Five-Year Plan periods, the total amount of CO2 emission rights in 2022 was 8318.07 million tonnes.

The calculation of CO2 emission rights allocation for each province is carried out in accordance with the principles of fairness, sustainability and differentiation [36, 37]. The principle of fairness is the foundation of CO2 emission rights allocation, which addresses the issue of equal rights and responsibilities, preventing an unbalanced situation where some regions sacrifice the reasonable development rights of others to achieve their own growth. The principle of sustainability focuses on the dynamic balance between short-term emission reduction and long-term development, ensuring the effectiveness of the allocation plan. The principle of differentiation is based on the reality of diversified regional development in China, making the plan more operational.

The principle of fairness emphasizes that each province has an equal right to exercise CO2 emission rights, fully taking into account the population size and economic strength of different regions, to avoid social and economic problems caused by unfair distribution. Under the principle of population equity, CO2 emission allowances should be distributed in proportion to each province’s share of the national population. Provinces with larger populations thus receive larger quotas to meet the basic living and development needs of their residents. Under the principle of fairness in payment capacity, allowances should be allocated according to the per capita GDP of each province. Economically advanced regions possess stronger fiscal capacity and technology, so they should shoulder greater mitigation responsibilities. Conversely, less-developed regions must be granted additional emission space to safeguard their future growth. This approach reserves essential CO2 headroom for underdeveloped provinces, providing a foundation for their long-term socio-economic development.

The principle of sustainability requires that the distribution mechanism should not only meet the current environmental and social development needs, but also lay the foundation for future sustainable development. From the perspective of environmental sustainability, CO2 emission allowances should be allocated according to the forest coverage rate of each province. Forests act as vital carbon sinks, which absorb and store CO2. A higher ratio signifies greater sink potential, helping regions maintain a long-term carbon balance. Consequently, provinces with more extensive forest cover should receive larger quotas, incentivizing them to sustain and enhance this sink capacity and thereby advance ecological sustainability. From the perspective of social sustainability, CO2 emission allowances should be tied to each province’s urbanization rate. This indicator reflects the concentration of population and economic activity, and higher rates imply denser energy demand and greater carbon pressures. Provinces with elevated urbanization should therefore receive smaller quotas, compelling them to pursue deeper emission reductions through technological innovation and improved management, while ensuring long-term and low-carbon development.

The principle of differentiation in the allocation of CO2 emission rights emphasizes that the heterogeneity characteristics of each subject should be fully considered in the allocation process to achieve accurate and reasonable allocation. Guided by the principle of historical emission responsibility, CO2 emission allowances should refer to the historical CO2 emissions of each province. Regions with higher historical totals have contributed disproportionately to the nation’s carbon burden and must therefore shoulder greater mitigation obligations, thus receiving proportionally smaller quotas to accelerate their transition to low-carbon development. Based on the perspective of emission reduction potential, CO2 emission allowances should be linked to each province’s share of the tertiary industry. Relative to primary and secondary industries, the tertiary sector typically exhibits lower energy intensity and fewer emissions. A higher tertiary share signals a more advanced economic structure, higher energy efficiency, and greater low-carbon potential. Accordingly, provinces with larger tertiary sectors should receive smaller quotas, incentivizing them to further optimize their industrial mix, raise energy efficiency, and steer regional growth toward a green and low-carbon future.

Definition of recipients and payers

The carbon-surplus regions, acting as compensation recipients, are mainly in the southern, central, and western regions, with some in the northern regions. The southern regions include Hainan, Guangxi, Fujian, Jiangxi, and other areas. These regions have abundant water and heat, high forest coverage, and consequently strong vegetation-based carbon sinks. The central and western areas - such as Gansu, Qinghai, Xinjiang, and Sichuan - are largely mountainous, sparsely populated, and industrially limited, while their rich wind and solar resources further curb CO2 emissions.

Carbon-deficient regions, acting as compensation payers, are concentrated in the eastern coastal provinces and northern China. Economically advanced areas - Tianjin, Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, and Guangdong - are highly industrialized and urbanized and consequently consume large amounts of energy. Although these regions can leverage strong technology and talent to improve energy efficiency, their vast industrial output pushes emissions well beyond their carbon sink capacity and allocated emission allowances. Northern heavy industry provinces - Hebei, Shanxi, Liaoning, Shandong, and Shaanxi - have built economies centered on steel, machinery, coal, and chemical engineering, powered by abundant coal and iron ore. Their heavy dependence on coal and oil generates high CO2 emissions and makes energy saving and emission reduction efforts especially challenging, leaving a pronounced carbon deficit. Ningxia, although less developed and short of energy resources, suffers from low energy-use efficiency and limited natural carbon sinks, creating a similar shortfall. Conclusions

The formation of a region’s carbon deficit-carbon surplus state is the superposition effect of the negative externality of economic development and the positive externality of ecological protection. This research profoundly reveals a serious structural imbalance problem: carbon-deficient areas are under huge emission pressure due to the blind pursuit of rapid socio-economic development, while carbon-surplus areas have strong carbon sink capacity, but their ecological protection costs and sacrificed development opportunities have not been fully compensated. A horizontal carbon eco-compensation standard model that incorporates complex participation scenarios was constructed. Then, the optimized eco-compensation standard model makes it more reasonable and more in line with the actual actions of the participants, thus improving the feasibility of the application. An empirical study was conducted on 30 major provinces in China. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) In 2022, Shanxi is the province with the highest CO2 emissions, while Inner Mongolia has the strongest CO2 absorption capacity. It is found that high CO2 emission areas are usually economically developed, energy-intensive or densely populated areas. On the contrary, regions with strong CO2 absorption capacity usually have complete and high-quality ecosystems or significant ecological restoration effects. (2) Guangdong, located in the Greater Bay Area, has the largest proportion of CO2 emission rights distribution, while Shanghai in the Yangtze River Delta region has the smallest. (3) Among the 30 researched provinces in China, 18 provinces show carbon-surplus, which are mainly distributed in the south, central-western regions and northern regions. Among them, Yunnan has the largest carbon surplus amount. The remaining 12 provinces are carbon-deficient areas, mainly concentrated in the eastern coastal and northern regions, of which Shanxi’s carbon-deficient phenomenon is the most serious. (4) Taking 2022 as an example, in the active participation scenario, Yunnan has the highest amount of compensation, and Shanxi has paid the most eco-compensation. In the passive participation scenario, the basic compensation value is equal to the amount paid by the deficit area or the amount received by the surplus area. If the province refuses to participate in eco-compensation, carbon-deficient regions need to bear the penalties from the central government and pay a higher compensation, while carbon-surplus areas do not get any benefits. (5) Through the horizontal comparison between the three scenarios, the compensation received by the carbon-surplus areas decreases with the decline in their willingness to participate. The amount of eco-compensation payment of carbon-deficient provinces has increased with the decline of participation in every three scenarios. In contrast, the amount of compensation paid by Inner Mongolia, Shaanxi and Ningxia - regions with relatively small carbon deficits - shifts from positive to negative; in other words, they shift from the recipients of eco-compensation to payers.

Suggestions

The conclusion showed that the active participation scenario is more conducive to the smooth progress of carbon horizontal eco-compensation. Compared with other scenarios, the participants could obtain a more acceptable distribution of benefits.

Therefore, we believe that it is necessary to establish the orientation of ‘compensation advantage’, and positive incentive guidance measures with the goal of active participation can create favorable conditions such as policies and funds for those who intend to participate. Provide priority support for economic development policies for those who take the initiative to fulfill their compensation responsibilities and invest in protection technology and costs for ecological protection entities to enhance the effectiveness of ecological protection. At the same time, establish an ecological product trading platform to build a trading channel for green elements between development and protection subjects. The subjective initiative of the subject to participate in compensation is stimulated by the manifestation and realization of the value of ecological products.

At the same time, restrictive punishment measures should be implemented to change the negative attitude of each province in the face of ecological compensation. Unlike vertical ecological compensation, horizontal ecological compensation is no longer directly supervised by the central government. Therefore, it is necessary to explicitly incorporate carbon horizontal ecological compensation into the ecological responsibility system and set a punishment mechanism for those who refuse to perform ecological compensation and protect the ecological environment. Link violations to credit, financing and market access to enhance the coordination of ecological protection, ecological compensation and regional development.

Limitations

The definition of the matching relationship between paying and compensated subjects has always been a key link for both research and practice of horizontal eco-compensation. This research has preliminarily clarified the positioning of paying and compensated provinces based on the identification of carbon-deficient and carbon-surplus statuses. However, from the perspective of the interest distribution principle that better aligns with fairness and justice, further research could more accurately determine the matching results of paying and compensated subjects by considering the cross-border flows and spillover effects of CO2 emissions. This will further enable a clearer delineation of the flow direction and volume of compensation funds, which enhances the targeted orientation of the horizontal eco-compensation mechanism, so as to ensure its smooth operation and the achievement of expected outcomes.
