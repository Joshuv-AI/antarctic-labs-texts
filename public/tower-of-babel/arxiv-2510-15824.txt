Blackwell’s Approachability for Sequential Conformal Inference
Guillaume Principato, Gilles Stoltz, [.1cm] EDF R&D

Abstract
We study conformal inference in non-exchangeable environments through the lens of Blackwell’s theory of approachability. We first recast adaptive conformal inference (ACI, Gibbs and Candès 2021 ) as a repeated two-player vector-valued finite game and characterize attainable coverage–efficiency tradeoffs. We then construct coverage and efficiency objectives under potential restrictions on the adversary’s play, and design a calibration-based approachability strategy to achieve these goals. The resulting algorithm enjoys strong theoretical guarantees and provides practical insights, though its computational burden may limit deployment in practice. Keywords: Adaptive conformal inference; Blackwell’s approachability; efficient prediction sets



1
Introduction

1.1
Motivation

Conformal inference
(
Vovk et al. 2005
)
provides a general procedure for constructing prediction sets with guaranteed coverage, under the assumption that the data are exchangeable. This assumption, however, is often too restrictive: it typically fails in sequential or time–dependent settings such as time series forecasting, where the distribution of observations may shift over time.

To address this issue,
Gibbs and Candès 2021
introduced Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI), which extends Conformal Prediction (CP) to adversarial environments. ACI adapts to distribution shifts by updating prediction intervals in response to observed outcomes, ensuring that the empirical coverage converges to the desired level. While effective in maintaining coverage, ACI and its extensions generally lack efficiency guarantees–for instance, there is no control over the average length of prediction intervals in adversarial regimes.

In this work, we study sequential conformal inference as a repeated two-player finite game and invoke Blackwell’s theory of approachability to characterize feasible objectives. Building on this perspective, we design a calibration–based algorithm that ensures asymptotic validity while achieving asymptotic efficiency under mild assumptions. Our approach relies on the notion of
opportunistic approachability

(
Bernstein et al. 2014
)
, which allows the learner to exploit potential restrictions in the opponent’s play. We argue that such assumptions better fit the typical use cases of ACI–such as distributional drift or regime switching–than the fully adversarial setting.

1.2
Related work

Although originally motivated by conformal prediction, ACI relies only marginally on the specific structure of conformal inference
(
Szabadváry and Löfström 2025
)
. At its core, ACI maintains validity by updating the miscoverage level
\alpha_{t}
via a projected gradient descent step. Hence, it can be viewed primarily as a sequential learning algorithm, where coverage control is cast as an online optimization problem. Most extensions of ACI adopt this perspective and focus on the design of the step size used in the gradient update. For instance,
Zaffran et al. 2022
propose an expert aggregation scheme to adapt the learning rate;
Gibbs and Candès 2024
;
Bhatnagar et al. 2023
consider sleeping expert formulations; and
Angelopoulos et al. 2024
study decaying step size schedules. Other extensions include PID-based control
(
Angelopoulos et al. 2023
)
, while
Barber et al. 2023
address non-exchangeability by assigning larger weights to recent observations in the empirical distribution of conformity scores.

Recent work has aimed to generalize the guarantees offered by ACI by asking whether broader classes of sequential learning algorithms might enjoy similar coverage properties. For example,
Angelopoulos et al. 2025
interpret ACI as a gradient equilibrium, while
Ramalingam et al. 2025
investigate the connection between pinball loss regret and asymptotic validity. The notion of regret with respect to the pinball loss is often used as a proxy for efficiency, despite its limitations. Moreover, existing regret analyses bypass the inherent finiteness of conformal inference [see Appendix
A
].

Other lines of work focus more directly on efficiency, understood as the ability to produce narrow prediction intervals. For instance,
Zaffran et al. 2022
analyze the efficiency of ACI in the i.i.d. and autoregressive settings. In the fully adversarial regime,
Srinivas 2025
establish impossibility results showing that no nontrivial efficiency guarantees can be obtained without further assumptions. These results motivate the restricted adversarial framework considered here, where meaningful efficiency objectives become attainable.

Our algorithmic design builds on the theory of Blackwell’s approachability
(
Blackwell 1956
)
, a central result for sequential learning in games. Approachability has notably been used to prove calibration results for online forecasters
(
Foster 1999
;
Mannor and Stoltz 2010
)
, a connection that will play a central role in our analysis. Classical approachability algorithms typically rely on its primal condition to select the player’s action. Our focus, however, lies on the
response-based
framework, which exploits its dual condition. Response-based strategies have been studied by
Bernstein and Shimkin 2013
;
Mannor et al. 2014
, and we build especially on the
opportunistic
approach of
Bernstein et al. 2014
. This framework extends calibration-based approachability algorithms
(
Perchet 2010
)
, allowing the learner to exploit structural restrictions in the adversary’s play.

1.3
Contributions

Our contributions are twofold.

(i) A game-theoretic formulation of sequential conformal inference.

We first formalize sequential conformal inference as a repeated two-player vector-valued finite game. This formulation clarifies the objectives implicit in existing methods and corrects several inconsistencies in prior analyses of Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI). Within this framework, Blackwell’s theory of approachability provides a natural geometric tool to characterize the sets of attainable coverage–efficiency pairs. This perspective unifies the study of validity and efficiency under a single, game-theoretic principle.

(ii) An opportunistic approachability algorithm.

We then design a general algorithm that adapts to potential restrictions in the adversary’s play. Our strategy is
opportunistic
in the sense of
Bernstein et al. 2014
: it recovers the efficiency guarantees of classical conformal prediction in the exchangeable case, while maintaining coverage in fully adversarial regimes. Between these two extremes, the algorithm provides meaningful efficiency guarantees under mild structural assumptions, such as distributional drift or regime switching.

Taken together, these results show that approachability offers a unifying and flexible framework for conformal inference beyond exchangeability, bridging the gap between worst-case validity and practical efficiency.

2.2
Adaptive conformal inference as a repeated two–player finite game

We now consider the case where the sequence of test data
(x_{t},y_{t})_{1\leqslant t\leqslant T}
may be chosen by an adversary. To preserve valid prediction in this setting, we follow the framework from
Gibbs and Candès 2021

\alpha

\alpha_{t}\subseteq[0,1]

Gibbs and Candès 2021

We show below that the sequential nature of these procedures admits a game-theoretic interpretation as a repeated game.

Direct formulation.

We first see ACI as a game where, at stage
t

C_{\alpha_{t}}(x_{t})
while the opponent picks the observation
y_{t}
.
In terms of (mis)coverage, the question is whether
y_{t}\in C_{\alpha_{t}}(x_{t})
or
y_{t}\not\in C_{\alpha_{t}}(x_{t})

In terms of efficiency, we evaluate the prediction set through its length
\Leb\bigl(C_{\alpha_{t}}(x_{t})\bigr)
.
Now, we state two properties that are helpful to reformulate this game into a simpler, finite game.

The discrete nature of the possible lengths of the prediction sets, dictated by the scores in the calibration set,
entails that the learner should pick miscoverage levels

2.3
Blackwell’s approachability theory

To handle the finite game of adaptive conformal inference stated before (
1
),
we will resort to Blackwell’s approachability theory, which we recall now
for general finite action sets
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, and for general vector-valued payoff functions
m:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

The learner wants the average payoff to converge to a closed convex set
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Formally, the aim of the learner is to ensure that

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}m(a_{t},b_{t})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{{=}}\overline{m}_{T}\longrightarrow S\quad\mbox{a.s.}\qquad\mbox{in the sense}\qquad\lim_{T\to\infty}d\bigl(\overline{m}_{T},\mathcal{C}\bigr)=0\,,

(4)

where, for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the distance of
x
to the closed convex set
\mathcal{C}
equals
d(x,\mathcal{C})=\displaystyle{\min_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\Arrowvert x-c\Arrowvert}
.

The aim of the opponent is to prevent this convergence. Actually,
the proof of Blackwell’s theorem (Theorem
1
below) shows that
either the learner can approach
\mathcal{C}
or the opponent can approach the complement of a
neighborhood of
\mathcal{C}
, i.e., make sure that

\liminf_{T\to\infty}d\bigl(\overline{m}_{T},\mathcal{C}\bigr)\geqslant\gamma>0\qquad\mbox{a.s.,}

for some
\gamma>0
, irrespective of the strategy of the learner.

Blackwell’s theorem provides a necessary and sufficient condition for the approachability
of a closed convex set
\mathcal{C}
in a finite game, based on the bilinear extension of
m
:
for all probability distributions
\bm{p}=(p_{a})_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})
and
\bm{q}=(q_{b})_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{B})
,

m(\bm{p},\bm{q})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}p_{a}\,q_{b}\,m(a,b)\,.

Theorem 1
.

[Approachability theorem,
Blackwell 1956
]

A closed convex set
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is approachable by the learner, i.e., (
4
) may hold, if and only if

\displaystyle\forall\bm{q}\in\Delta(\mathcal{B}),\ \exists\bm{p}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad m(\bm{p},\bm{q})\in\mathcal{C}\,.

(5)

The condition (
5
) is referred to as Blackwell’s dual condition.
The proof of Theorem
1
is constructive and we actually provide
(a generalized version of) the sufficient condition in Appendix
B
. The explicit algorithm introduced therein
is based on a geometric argument and is referred to as the primal Blackwell’s approachability strategy:
at round
t
, draw
a_{t}
independently at random according to a distribution

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{t}\in\argmin_{\bm{p}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})}\bmax_{\bm{q}\in\Delta(\mathcal{B})}\langle\overline{m}_{t-1}-\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{C}}(\overline{m}_{t-1}),m(\bm{p},\bm{q})\rangle\,,

(6)

where
\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{C}}
is the projection in Euclidean norm onto the target set
\mathcal{C}
.

Remark 1
.

Theorem
1
mostly ensures whether a sequential-learning problem evaluated through average payoffs
is feasible or not, but in general, the primal Blackwell’s approachability strategy is not a computationally efficient way to achieve these objectives
(often due to the computational complexity in computing the projection) nor is the strategy achieving the optimal rates of convergence.

\displaystyle S=\begin{Bmatrix}c\in[0,1],&\quad c\leqslant\alpha\\
\ell\in[0,2L],&\quad\ell\leqslant 2L(1-\alpha)\end{Bmatrix}\,.

(10)

Proof.

The target set
S
is closed and convex, thus we can apply Theorem
1
. Indeed, for all
\bm{q}\in\Delta(\mathcal{B})
the mixed action
\bm{p}=(1-\alpha)\bm{\delta}_{0}+\alpha\bm{\delta}_{1}
verifies
m(\bm{p},\bm{q})\in S
, which ensures the approachability of
S
.
∎

Moreover, the target set defined in (
10
) is optimal in the following sense:

Lemma 4
.

In the classical adversarial setting, for every horizon
T\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a sequence
\{b_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
such that, for all
\{a_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
,

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{b_{t}\leqslant a_{t}}\leqslant\alpha\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Leb\big(C_{a_{t}}\big)\geqslant 2L(1-\alpha)\,.

Proof.

If at each round
t
, the opponent plays
b_{t}=1/(T_{\calib}+1)
, then any strategy that satisfies
\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{b_{t}\leqslant a_{t}}\leqslant\alpha
must play
a_{t}=0
at least
\lceil T(1-\alpha)\rceil
times and
\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Leb(C_{a_{t}})\geqslant(1-\alpha)\Leb(C_{0})
.
∎

The optimality result of Lemma
4
is actually a negative result because the target set
S
defined in (
10
) is approachable using a trivial strategy where the learner plays either
a_{t}=0
or
a_{t}=1
at each round
t
. This typically leads to uninformative conformal sets and should not be considered as a
efficiency
constraint. As a result, with no further assumption, no approachable
valid
target set are relevant in terms of
efficiency
. Hence, we introduce the following target set for the adversarial setting that does not impose any
efficiency
constraints

\displaystyle S_{\Delta(\mathcal{B})}=\begin{Bmatrix}c\in[0,1],&\quad c\leqslant\alpha\\
\ell\in[0,2L],&\quad\ell\leqslant 2L\end{Bmatrix}\,.

(11)

3.3
Q
–restricted opponent’s play and typical examples

In this section, we present a general framework that enables to see the aforementioned settings as some unknown asymptotic restriction on the opponent’s play. In particular, we provide examples that motivates such generalizations. Following
Bernstein et al. 2014
, we consider the following notion of statistical restriction.

Definition 1

(
Bernstein et al. 2014
)
.

The opponent is said
statistically

Q
–restricted for a given convex subset
Q\subseteq\Delta(\mathcal{B})
if the sequence
(\bm{q}_{t})_{t\geqslant 1}
of mixed actions output by the opponent satisfies,

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}d(\bm{q}_{t},Q)=0\qquad\mbox{a.s.}

Fact 3
.

The adversarial setting corresponds to
Q=\Delta(B)
. Therefore, the assertion that the opponent is
statistically

Q
–restricted for some unknown convex subset
Q\subseteq\Delta(B)
is not an assumption.

Fact 4
.

If the scores are exchangeable, then at each round
t
the opponent plays according to
\bm{q}_{\unif}
(see Lemma
2
). As a result, the opponent is in particular
statistically

Q
–restricted for
Q=\{\bm{q}_{\unif}\}
.

Beyond these two edge cases, the interest of such general framework is that it can be used to consider relaxations of the exchangeable setting. For instance, we introduce the
regime switching
setting. Intuitively, it enables for adversarial distribution shifts, which can typically model time series with seasonal behaviours or changes and the distribution of the conformity scores.
More precisely, it includes the framework of recurrent concept drift, which generalizes exchangeability per blocks–transitions between blocks can be either sudden, gradual, or incremental–and is studied in the context of conformal prediction by
Eliades and Papadopoulos 2025
. Those drifts appear in terms of distribution of data points
(x_{t},y_{t})
and are repercuted in a non trivial way first in terms of scores
s_{t}
and then in terms of distribution of the opponent’s play
\bm{q}_{t}
.
We illustrate this phenomenon with the following toy example, in which we show that similar scores distributions (in terms of total variation distance
\dtv
) lead to similar distributions of the opponent’s play.

Example 2

(the
\epsilon
–ball)
.

Assume that the calibration scores are drawn i.i.d. from a diffuse distribution
F
and that the test scores are drawn i.i.d. from a distribution
G
. Now, if
\dtv(F,G)\leqslant\epsilon
then we have for all
t\in\llbracket 1,T\rrbracket
that
d(\bm{q}_{t},\bm{q}_{\unif})\leqslant 2\epsilon
.

Indeed, consider an ordered sequence
\{k_{i}\}_{i=0}^{T_{\calib}+1}
such that
0=k_{0}<k_{1}<\cdots<k_{T_{\calib}+1}=L
. By elementary properties of histograms and of the total variation distance, denoting by
f
and
g
densities of
F
and
G
with respect to a common dominating probability
distribution
\mu
, e.g.,
\mu=F+G/2
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{T_{\calib}}\Big|\mathbb{P}_{s\sim F}\big(s\in(k_{i},k_{i+1})\big)-\mathbb{P}_{s\sim G}\big(s\in(k_{i},k_{i+1})\big)\Big|\leqslant\int_{0}^{L}\big|f(x)-g(x)\big|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=2\dtv(F,G)\leqslant 2\epsilon\,.

In particular, this property applied to the order statistics ensures
d(\bm{q}_{t},\bm{q}_{\unif})\leqslant 2\epsilon
for all
t\in\llbracket 1,T\rrbracket
. This example corresponds to a
Q
–restricted opponent’s play with
Q=\{\bm{q}\in\Delta(\mathcal{B}):d(\bm{q},\bm{q}_{\unif})\leqslant 2\epsilon\}
, the
2\epsilon
–ball centered at
\bm{q}_{\unif}
.

Example 3
.

Assume that the test scores can follow finitely many distributions, by regimes (with no constraints on the number of regime
shifts). These distributions of test scores induce distributions
\bm{q}^{(n)}
over
\mathcal{B}
, where
n\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket
. This setting corresponds to a
Q
–restricted opponent’s play with
Q=\conv\big(\{\bm{q}^{(n)}:n\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket\}\big)
, the convex hull of the finitely many distributions
induced.

Remark 3
.

As emphasized in Example
3
, this formulation is quite general. As a result, we will see in Section
3.5
that meaningful efficiency guarantees are obtained only for certain kinds of regime switching.

3.4
General target set

Given a unknown convex subset
Q\subseteq\Delta(\mathcal{B})
, we can define a
Q
–target set that will be relevant whenever the opponent is
statistically

Q
–restricted as per Definition
1
. To do so, we introduce the following function that measure efficiency for a given miscoverage level
\alpha\in(0,1)
and mixed action
\bm{q}\in\Delta(\mathcal{B})
.
We define
\phi_{\bm{q}}:\alpha\mapsto\Leb\big(C_{\quant(\alpha,\bm{q})}\big)
where
\quant(\alpha,\bm{q})=\max\big\{a\in\mathcal{A}:\sum_{b\in B}q_{b}\mathbf{1}_{b\leqslant a}\leqslant\alpha\big\}
denotes the highest oracle action that obtains in expectation a lower miscoverage than
\alpha
under
\bm{q}
. The definition of the
\quant
function is such that
\phi_{\bm{q}}(\alpha)
corresponds to the length of the smallest valid conformal set under
\bm{q}
.
To avoid discontinuity issue–which we address in Lemma
8
–we define
\phi_{\bm{q}}^{+}:\alpha\mapsto\min_{\epsilon>0}\phi_{\bm{q}}(\alpha-\epsilon)
, the smallest right continuous upper bounding function of
\phi_{\bm{q}}
. In particular,
\phi_{\bm{q}}^{+}(\alpha)
will be strictly larger than
\phi_{\bm{q}}(\alpha)
if and only if
\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}q_{b}\mathbf{1}_{b\leqslant\quant(\alpha,\bm{q})}=\alpha
.
With these definitions, we can now define a general
efficiency
objective for a given convex subset
Q\subseteq\Delta(\mathcal{B})
, which corresponds to the following bound for (

⋆
⁣
⋆

‣
3
):

\limsup_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Leb\big(C_{a_{t}}\big)\leqslant\max_{\bm{q}\in Q}\phi_{\bm{q}}^{+}(\alpha)\,.

(12)

We make the correspondence between this general
efficiency
objective and the aforementioned settings precise in the Section
3.5
. We can finally express jointly the
validity
and
efficiency
objectives in Blackwell’s form as the approachability of the unknown (as
Q
is unknown) target set

\displaystyle\displaystyle S_{Q}=\begin{Bmatrix}c\in[0,1],&\quad c\leqslant\alpha\\
\ell\in[0,2L],&\quad\ell\leqslant\displaystyle\max_{\bm{q}\in Q}\phi_{\bm{q}}^{+}(\alpha)\end{Bmatrix}\,.

(13)

Remark 4
.

The target set (
13
) is optimal in the following sense: even if the opponent is
statistically

Q
–restricted, he could pick
b_{t}
at each round according to
\bm{q}\in\argmax_{\bm{q}\in Q}\phi_{\bm{q}}^{+}(\alpha)
, in which case,
the average length achieved will be close to
\max_{\bm{q}\in Q}\phi_{\bm{q}}^{+}(\alpha)
.

If
Q
were known in advance–which is a strong assumption we do not want to make–then we could adapt the Primal Blackwell approachability strategy in order to approach the target set
S_{Q}
whenever the opponent’s play is
statistically

Q
–restricted. Formally, we define a
Q
–Primal Blackwell approachability strategy such that the learner plays at each round
t
according to

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{t}\in\argmin_{\bm{p}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})}\bmax_{\bm{q}\in Q}\langle\overline{m}_{t-1}-\operatorname{Proj}_{S_{Q}}(\overline{m}_{t-1}),m(\bm{p},\bm{q})\rangle\,.

(14)

Note that this strategy only differs from Equation (
6
) because the maximum here is over
Q\subseteq\Delta(\mathcal{B})
instead of over
\Delta(\mathcal{B})
and the projection is on
S_{Q}
instead of
S
.

Theorem 3
.

If the opponent’s play is
Q
–restricted, then the
Q
–restricted Primal strategy (
14
) approaches the target set
S_{Q}
. Formally, we have the following convergences:

\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[d\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}m(a_{t},b_{t})\,,\,S_{Q}\right)^{\!\!2}\,\right]=0\qquad\mbox{thus}\qquad d\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}m(a_{t},b_{t})\,,\,S_{Q}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0\,.

The approachability result from Theorem
3
is important because it shows that adversarial algorithm can produce relevant guarantees in favorable settings. However, the Exchangeable Primal strategy (
14
) is not useful in practice as it requires the knowledge of the restriction on the adversary play. In section
4.2
we present a calibration–based procedure that achieve the same approachability result without assuming in hindsight the knowledge of
Q
.

Examples of relevant
Q
–restriction and associated efficiency guarantees

In this section, we establish a precise correspondence between the efficiency objectives in the typical aforementioned settings and the general target sets.

Fact 5
.

In the fully adversarial setting,
Q=\Delta(\mathcal{B})
and
\displaystyle\max_{\bm{q}\in Q}\phi_{\bm{q}}^{+}(\alpha)=2L
. Indeed, for
\bm{q}=\bm{\delta}_{\frac{1}{T_{\calib}+1}}

).

Fact 6
.

In the exchangeable setting,

and if

then
\phi_{\bm{q}_{\unif}}^{+}(\alpha)=\Leb(C_{\alpha})

then

\forall\epsilon\in\big(0,\tfrac{1}{T_{\calib}+1}\big]

and as a result
\phi_{\bm{q}_{\unif}}^{+}(\alpha)=\Leb\big(C_{\alpha-1/(T_{\calib}+1)}\big)
.

As stated in Section
3.3
, the
Q
–restricted framework also enables to deal with intermediate settings such as regime switching. Yet, due to its great generality, we illustrate in the following examples that depending on the distributions involved in the regime switching, the efficiency guarantee can be informative or not.

Example 4
.

Assume the regime switches between two completely different distributions for the observations
\{(x_{t},y_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
. Typically one distribution verifies
s_{t}=\mid y_{t}-\widehat{\mu}(x_{t})\mid\leqslant\epsilon
while the other verifies
s_{t}=\mid y_{t}-\widehat{\mu}(x_{t})\mid\geqslant L-\epsilon
with
\epsilon>0
that is typically small. In that case, the corresponding distribution
\bm{q}_{t}
would respectively be
\bm{\delta}_{1}
and
\bm{\delta}_{\frac{1}{T_{\calib}+1}}
. As a result, the target set induced by

corresponds in terms of target set to
Q=\Delta(\mathcal{B})
and no informative efficiency guarantee can be derived.

Example 5
.

Consider the setting from Example
2
i.e., the calibration scores are drawn i.i.d. from a diffuse distribution
F
of density
f
whereas the test scores are drawn i.i.d. from another continuous distribution
G
of density
g
with
\dtv(F,G)\leqslant\epsilon
. We showed in Example
2
that the opponent’s play is
Q
–restricted with
Q=\{\bm{q}\in\Delta(\mathcal{B}):d(\bm{q},\bm{q}_{\unif})\leqslant 2\epsilon\}
. In that case, if
\epsilon
goes to

, then we can guarantee the same efficiency as in the exchangeable setting. This result is a direct application of Lemma
6
.

4
Blackwell Opportunistic Adaptive Conformal Inference

4.1
General result on opportunistic approachability

In this section, we recall the theoretical framework of opportunistic approachability from
Bernstein et al. 2014
and summarize their general results. Informally, opportunistic strategies achieve the classical guarantees in the fully adversarial setting but can also benefit from potential restrictions on the opponent’s actions and then achieve stronger guarantees. In
Bernstein et al. 2014
, two types of restrictions are considered but for the sake of exposure, we only consider the notion of
statistically

Q
–restricted play as per Definition
1
. We connect these general results with our setting in Section
4.2
.

Opportunistic Approachability

For a given approchable target set
S
, the notion of opportunistic approachability is relative to a given
response function
and
goal function
. In particular, since
S
is approachable, the Dual condition in Blackwell’s Theorem ensures that there exists a
response function

p^{\star}:\Delta(\mathcal{B})\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
such that
\forall\bm{q}\in\Delta(\mathcal{B})
,
m\big(p^{\star}(\bm{q}),\bm{q}\big)\in S
. Moreover, one may consider the smallest target set for which
p^{\star}
satisfies the Dual condition, which naturally leads to considering the
goal function

m^{\star}:\Delta(\mathcal{B})\to S
defined as
m^{\star}(\bm{q})=m(p^{\star}(\bm{q}),\bm{q})
for each
\bm{q}\in\Delta(\mathcal{B})
. Following
Bernstein et al. 2014
, we now introduce the notion of opportunistic approachability for a given
goal function
.

Definition 2

(
Bernstein et al. 2014
)
.

A strategy is
statistically opportunistic
for a given goal function
m^{\star}
if it approaches
S^{+}(Q)
, i.e.,

\lim_{T\to\infty}d\big(\overline{m}_{t},S^{+}(Q)\big)=0\quad\text{(a.s.)}

whenever the play of the opponent is
statistically

Q
–restricted for some convex set
Q\subseteq\Delta(\mathcal{B})
and where
\displaystyle{S^{+}(Q)=\bigcap_{\epsilon>0}\conv\big(\{m^{\star}(\bm{q}):d(\bm{q},Q)\leqslant\epsilon\}\big)}
is the closed convex image of
Q
under the goal function
m^{\star}
.

The calibrated Approachability algorithm

This strategy introduced by
Perchet 2010
requires access to a meta-algorithm, also known as a
forecaster
, that outputs at each round
t
a probabilistic forecast
\bm{z}_{t}\in\Delta(\mathcal{B})
of the opponent’s next pure action
b_{t}\in\mathcal{B}
. The forecaster may be randomized, in which case
\bm{z}_{t}
is drawn from a probability distribution
\bm{\eta}_{t}
over
\Delta(\mathcal{B})
, possibly depending on past observations. In particular, the calibrated approachability algorithm requires the forecaster to be
calibrated
, as defined below.

Definition 3
.

A
forecaster
is said to be
calibrated
if for every Borel measurable set
G\subseteq\Delta(\mathcal{B})
and every strategy of the opponent, its forecasts
\{\bm{z}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
verify:

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{\bm{z}_{t}\in G}(\bm{\delta}_{b_{t}}-\bm{z}_{t})=0\quad\text{(a.s.)}

The calibrated Approachability algorithm consists at round
t
in playing
a_{t}
from
p^{\star}(\bm{z}_{t})
where
\bm{z}_{t}
is the output of the calibrated forecaster at round
t
. This strategy is conceptually simple as its complexity lies entirely in the meta-algorithm responsible for producing the calibrated forecasts
\bm{z}_{t}
. The common approach to obtain a calibrated forecaster as per Definition
3
is based on a sequence of
\epsilon
–calibrated forecaster on a finite
\epsilon
–grid over
\Delta(\mathcal{B})
, which gradually refined. To mitigate this computational burden in practice, we advocate for the use of an
\epsilon
–calibrated forecaster instead.
This calibration-based strategy has been proven to be opportunistic as stated in the above Theorem.

Theorem 4
.

[
Bernstein et al. 2014
, Theorem 4 and Corollary 2
]

Assume that
p^{\star}
is a regular response function (i.e. is piecewise continuous). Then the following implication holds almost surely for the associated calibrated approachability algorithm: if the opponent’s play is
statistically

Q
–restricted as per Definition
1
, then

\lim_{T\to\infty}d\big(\overline{m}_{t},S^{+}(Q)\big)=0\,,

where again,
\displaystyle{S^{+}(Q)=\bigcap_{\epsilon>0}\conv\big(\{m^{\star}(\bm{q}):d(\bm{q},Q)\leqslant\epsilon\}\big)}
. As a result, the calibrated approachability algorithm is
statistically opportunistic
as per Definition
2
.

4.2
Opportunistic Approachability for Adaptive Conformal Inference

In this section, we adapt the general result from
Bernstein et al. 2014
depicted in Section

to achieve the coverage and efficiency objectives introduced for the sequential conformal inference problem. In particular, we design the

–restricted opponent’s play setting for some unknown convex subset

Calibrated approachability algorithm for conformal inference.

We now define our problem specific

\displaystyle p^{\star}:\bm{q}\longmapsto\bm{\delta}_{\quant(\alpha,\bm{q})}\quad\text{ where }\quad\quant(\alpha,\bm{q})=\max\Big\{a\in\mathcal{A}:\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}q_{b}\mathbf{1}_{b\leqslant a}\leqslant\alpha\Big\}\,.

(15)

Intuitively, for a given distribution
\bm{q}
we select the highest pure action in

for which the coverage guarantee (

⋆

‣
3

\bm{q}

represents an efficiency criterion that we deem satisfactory for a each given distribution

We can now define the Blackwell Opportunistic Adaptive Conformal Inference (BOACI) algorithm by adapting the calibrated approachability algorithm to the

Theoretical results.

The response-fonction

Proof.

\alpha\notin\mathcal{A}
,

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}m(a_{t},b_{t})=\begin{pmatrix}r(\alpha)\\
\Leb\big(C_{\alpha}\big)\end{pmatrix}=m^{\star}(\bm{q}_{\unif})\,.

5
Conclusion

Our analysis revisits online conformal inference from a game-theoretic viewpoint, emphasizing the role of Blackwell’s approachability in understanding
validity
and
efficiency
beyond exchangeability. This perspective clarifies the theoretical status of existing algorithms such as ACI and provides a principled path to designing new strategies. From a conceptual standpoint, our results highlight that
efficiency
in non-exchangeable settings is not inherently incompatible with
validity
, but depends critically on the structure of the environment. In particular, opportunistic approachability offers a formal way to quantify how partial regularity in the data-generating process–such as slow drift or regime switching–can be exploited to achieve sharper guarantees than those attainable in the fully adversarial regime.
Future work includes extending the theoretical analysis of the proposed calibration-based approachability strategy to
\epsilon
-calibrated forecasters. The
\epsilon
-calibration requirement is much weaker and enables computationally tractable algorithms. Another promising direction is to consider time-varying vectorial payoff functions, in order to account for updates of the calibration set over time.

A.2
Impossibility results in the adversarial setting

Regret with respect to the Pinball loss.

First, we define the pinball loss
\ell_{\alpha}
(a.k.a. quantile loss) for a given miscoverage rate
\alpha\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\ell_{\alpha}:(a,b)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\longmapsto(\alpha-\mathbf{1}_{b\leqslant a})(b-a)\,,

(21)

and the associated regret with respect to the constant pure action
a\in\mathcal{A}
as

\displaystyle R_{T}^{\ell}(a)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{\alpha}(a_{t},b_{t})-\ell_{\alpha}(a,b_{t})\,.

(22)

To cast simultaneously the coverage and no-regret objectives in the Blackwell’s approachability framework we follow the regret matching formulation from
Hart and Mas-Colell 2001
. This formulation is based on the fact that a no-regret algorithm verifies for all
a\in\mathcal{A}\,\limsup_{T\to\infty}R_{T}^{\ell}(a)\leqslant 0
. Hence, we define the following vector-valued payoff function
m_{\operatorname{reg}}:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\to[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{T_{\calib}+2}
,

\displaystyle m_{\operatorname{reg}}:(a_{t},b_{t})\longmapsto\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{1}_{b_{t}\leqslant a_{t}}\\
\Big(\ell_{\alpha}(a_{t},b_{t})-\ell_{\alpha}(a,b_{t})\Big)_{a\in\mathcal{A}}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(23)

Theorem 6
.

No sequential strategy can ensure simultaneously
validity
and
no–regret
with respect to the pinball loss for miscoverage
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Formally, the target set
S_{\operatorname{reg}}=[0,\alpha]\times\mathbb{R}_{-}^{T_{\calib}+2}
is not approachable.

Proof.

Here, we use the necessary condition of Theorem
1
and it suffices to find
\bm{q}\in\Delta(\mathcal{B})
such that for all
\bm{p}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})
we have
m(\bm{p},\bm{q})\notin S_{\operatorname{reg}}
. Actually, for each
b\in\mathcal{B}
, the pure action
\bm{q}=\bm{\delta}_{b}
verifies the property. Indeed, by definition the pinball loss
\ell_{\alpha}(a_{t},b_{t})=0
if and only if
a_{t}=b_{t}
but in that case
\mathbf{1}_{b_{t}\leqslant a_{t}}=1>\alpha
, which concludes.
∎

Remark 8
.

This impossibility result does not directly contradict existing results in the literature (e.g.
Bhatnagar et al. 2023

Gibbs and Candès 2024

Ramalingam et al. 2025
). Indeed, Theorem
6
is due to the fact that we take into account discontinuities in the empirical distribution of conformity scores by viewing sequential conformal inference as a finite game. In particular, the aforementioned results are idealized results not taking into account the finiteness of conformal inference in the analysis.

Regret with respect to the best valid constant strategy.

We now construct a natural regret-based efficiency criterion that involves the length of the conformal sets. In particular, we aim for
valid
conformal sets that are as small as possible compared to the best constant strategy. However, it would not be fair to compare against

because this constant strategy does not achieve

\displaystyle R_{T}^{\Leb}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Leb(C_{a_{t}})-\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{T}^{\star}}\Leb(C_{a})\,.

Assume

and the score’s upper bound

\displaystyle\text{if }\quad\limsup_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{b_{t}\leqslant a_{t}}\leqslant\alpha\quad\text{ then }\quad\liminf_{T\to\infty}\sup_{\{b_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 1}}R_{T}\geqslant 2\big(\max_{t\in\mathcal{D}_{\calib}}s_{t}-\min_{t\in\mathcal{D}_{\calib}}s_{t}\big)>0\,.

Assume that
