Climate-driven flood hazard assessment in data-scarce mountainous basins using a GIS-based machine learning and hydrodynamic modelling under CMIP6 SSP scenarios
Authors: Shahbaz Khan, Afed Ullah Khan, Abdullah Alodah, Ahmad Azeem, Muhammad Waqas, Faten Nahas, Nazih Y. Rebouh, Youssef M. Youssef
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Floods pose increasing risks in mountainous regions such as the Swat River Basin, where climatic variability, glacial influences, and data limitations hinder conventional flood risk assessment. This study introduces a hybrid framework that integrates explainable SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)-based XGBoost for Global Climate Model (GCM) ranking, Random Forest (RF) ensemble modeling, and coupled hydrologic–hydraulic simulations (HEC-HMS–HEC-RAS) for multi-scenario flood hazard mapping. The approach provides an interpretable, data-driven, and physically based method for assessing climate-induced flood hazards in data-scarce basins. Daily precipitation, maximum temperature (Tmax), and minimum temperature (Tmin) data from eleven CMIP6 (Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6) Global Climate Models (GCMs) were bias-corrected using the linear scaling approach. These GCMs were ranked using XGBoost regression with SHAP interpretation, achieving high predictive accuracy (R2: 0.934/0.926 for precipitation, 0.953/0.949 for Tmax, and 0.947/0.943 for Tmin). A Multi-Model Ensemble built with RF regression further improved performance (R2: 0.74/0.71 for precipitation; 0.97/0.963 for Tmax; 0.965/0.958 for Tmin). These datasets were used to drive the HEC-HMS model, calibrated (1993–2013) and validated (2014–2019) with satisfactory results (NSE: 0.612/0.603; PBIAS: + 3.96%/− 6.75%). Flood frequency analysis using distribution fitting and the VIKOR method identified Log-Logistic (historical), Gumbel (SSP245), and GEV (SSP585) as optimal models. Simulated hydrographs for different return periods were input into a 2D HEC-RAS model to estimate flood depth, extent, and velocity. Flood hazard was quantified using a composite index of depth and velocity. Under SSP585, high to very high hazard zones expanded to 78% of the floodplain for the 100-year event, compared to 69% under historical conditions. This integrated, explainable, and scalable framework enhances climate-driven flood hazard prediction in complex mountainous terrains.

Introduction

Floods are among the most frequent and destructive natural hazards worldwide, causing extensive socio-economic and environmental damage. Their frequency and magnitude have increased over recent decades due to rapid urbanization, land-use change, and climate variability. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) reports that between 2000 and 2019, floods constituted 44% of global disaster events, impacting over 1.65 billion individuals1. In South Asia, floods remain a critical hazard, posing ongoing risks to human lives, infrastructure, and economic stability.

The Swat District in northern Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan, is highly vulnerable to flooding due to its mountainous terrain, monsoonal rainfall, snowmelt influence, and deforestation2,3. The catastrophic 2010 flood, Pakistan’s most devastating natural disaster, displaced nearly 500,000 people and damaged 3350 km of roads, 137 bridges, and 33,000 houses in Swat alone4,5. Subsequent flood events, notably in 2016 and 2022, reaffirmed the region’s high susceptibility. The 2022 monsoonal flooding caused widespread destruction, resulting in over 1700 fatalities nationwide and displacing millions, including residents of the Swat District6. The disaster severely damaged transportation infrastructure, disrupted communication networks, and devastated agricultural lands, thereby increasing the vulnerability of communities dependent on farming and tourism. Moreover, both the 2010 and 2022 floods affected over 800 schools across Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, causing substantial interruptions in education7. Under the high-emission SSP585 scenario, projections indicate a rise in extreme rainfall and more frequent flood events in the Swat Valley. Therefore, robust data-driven flood hazard mapping and risk assessments are imperative for effective disaster risk reduction, informed decision-making, and sustainable regional development.

Conventional flood risk assessment methods—primarily dependent on historical hydrological records and static assumptions—are increasingly insufficient for representing evolving flood dynamics under changing climatic conditions, especially in data-limited mountainous regions. Recent advances combine machine learning (ML), climate model projections, and hydrologic–hydraulic simulations to improve the spatial and temporal accuracy of flood hazard prediction. For instance, Sadiqzai et al.8 integrated CMIP6-based climate projections with 2D hydraulic modeling to evaluate flood risks in Peshawar, while Ahmad et al.9 applied HEC-HMS, HEC-RAS, remote sensing, and ML techniques to assess hazards in the Hunza–Nagar basins of Pakistan. Hajji et al.10 employed Google Earth Engine and interpretable AI models—Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting, and AdaBoost—achieving an AUC of 1.0 for flood prediction in data-scarce basins of northern Morocco. Similarly, Vemula et al.11 demonstrated that a graph transformer network outperformed traditional algorithms such as XGBoost and RF by effectively capturing spatial dependencies in France, while Fereshtehpour et al.12 confirmed that even coarse-resolution DEMs can support reliable flood mapping using deep learning in Carlisle, UK. Lee et al.13 proposed an ensemble framework (MaxFloodCast) achieving an R2 of approximately 0.95 for peak flood predictions. Further studies include CMIP6-driven RF and SVM flood modeling in Iran’s Tajan Basin14, validation of HEC-HMS across Iranian watersheds15, and ANN-based streamflow forecasting under future climate scenarios in the Hunza Basin16. Collectively, these studies highlight the growing potential of climate-informed and interpretable ML approaches integrated with physically based simulations to enhance flood risk prediction.

In this context, the present study develops a novel hybrid flood modeling framework for the Swat River Basin (SRB) that integrates explainable AI, ensemble climate modeling, and coupled hydrologic–hydraulic simulations to assess both current and future flood risks under SSP245 and SSP585 scenarios. Climate projections from ten CMIP6 Global Climate Models (GCMs) were first bias-corrected using the Linear Scaling method17. To ensure model interpretability and reliability, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)-based XGBoost was employed to evaluate and rank GCM performance, after which the top-ranked models were integrated into a Multi-Model Ensemble (MME) using Random Forest regression18, minimizing individual model biases and enhancing predictive stability19. The resulting ensemble climate datasets were used to drive a calibrated and validated HEC-HMS hydrologic model, from which simulated peak discharges were statistically analyzed using probability distribution fitting and optimized through the VIKOR decision framework20. These derived design discharges were then transformed into synthetic hydrographs and used in a 2D HEC-RAS hydraulic simulation to estimate flood depth, extent, and flow velocity. Finally, a dynamic hazard index integrating hydraulic parameters was applied to delineate and classify flood risk zones into four hazard levels across the basin.

This study is the first to unify SHAP-informed GCM selection, ML-based ensemble modeling, statistical bias correction, frequency-based flood estimation, and 2D physically-based hydraulic simulation into a single interpretable framework. It is tailored specifically for the SRB—a snow-fed, topographically complex, and data-limited watershed. Unlike previous research that applied these techniques separately or in data-rich settings, this integrated, explainable, and scalable workflow enables transparent flood hazard assessment for complex, data-scarce mountain basins under climate change.

Study area characteristics

The Swat District, situated in northern Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KP), Pakistan, has an average elevation of around 980 m and experiences a cold and humid climate. Spanning approximately 5337 km2 and home to around 2.3 million people3, Swat has faces recurrent and severe flood episodes. Geographically, it is positioned at the foothills of the Hindu Kush Mountains, between latitudes 34° 40′ to 35° N and longitudes 72° to 74° 6′ E, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Average summer temperatures vary between 20 and 30 °C, decreasing toward the northern mountainous terrain21.

Data collection

Daily precipitation along with minimum and maximum temperature data for the period 1993–2020 were obtained from the Pakistan Meteorological Department (PMD), while daily streamflow observations for the same period were provided by the Water and Power Development Authority (WAPDA). Future streamflow conditions were retrieved using daily climate variables—namely precipitation, minimum temperature, and maximum temperature from 11 GCMs within the CMIP6. These models include NESM3 (China), CMCC-ESM2 (Italy), CNRM-CM6-1 and CNRM-ESM2-1 (France), EC-Earth3-Veg-LR (Europe), GFDL-ESM4 (USA), INM-CM4-8 and INM-CM5-0 (Russia), MIROC6 and MRI-ESM2-0 (Japan), and Nor-ESM2-MM. A 30 m resolution Digital Elevation Model (DEM) was obtained from NASA’s Earth Data Portal. For detailed hydraulic modeling, a higher-resolution 10 m DEM was also retrieved via the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform, which was specifically employed in the development of 2D computational meshes. Data pre-processing

Bias correction

To enhance the fidelity of climate projections for both historical (1993–2014) and future (2015–2099) periods under SSP245 and SSP585 scenarios, bias correction was applied to daily precipitation, maximum, and minimum temperature data from 11 CMIP6 GCMs. Observations from the Kalam Station served as a reference. The Linear scaling method was employed to adjust model means to match observed values due to its computational efficiency, simplicity, and ability to maintain climatic variability17. The corrected datasets were subsequently used as inputs in HEC-HMS for hydrological simulations.

Model ranking using XGBoost and SHAP

The XGBoost regression model, integrated with SHAP, was employed to evaluate and rank the reliability of the GCMs. Observed data for temperature and precipitation from 1993 to 2014 were used as targets, while bias-corrected outputs from the 11 GCMs served as predictors. Model performance was evaluated using R2 and RMSE metrics, while SHAP values were computed to quantify the contribution of each GCM19. The top three GCMs, based on SHAP importance and performance metrics, were selected for future simulations to minimize uncertainty and ensure consistency with historical climate behavior.

Multi-model ensemble using random forest

An RF-based MME approach was developed to enhance the reliability of downscaled climate projections. The three top-ranked GCMs were used to predict precipitation and temperature based on historical performance (1993–2014). Data were divided into training (80%) and validation (20%) subsets. The RF model captured nonlinear dependencies between variables and was evaluated using R2, RMSE, and MSE22. The trained model was then applied to project daily climate variables for 2015–2099 under SSP245 and SSP585 scenarios, yielding improved hydrological input datasets and enhanced reliability of flood and streamflow simulations23.

Hydrological modeling

HEC-HMS v4.12, a semi-distributed hydrologic model by the US Army Corps of Engineers (USACE, 2021), was employed to simulate streamflow in the Swat River basin. Sub-basins were delineated from a 30 m DEM. Eight sub-basins were defined (Fig. 1), with input data comprising daily precipitation, maximum, and minimum temperatures. The SCS Curve Number (CN) method was used for infiltration loss24, while runoff transformation was modeled using the SCS Unit Hydrograph method. Baseflow was simulated using a monthly constant flow. CN values were extracted using zonal statistics from a CN raster overlaid with the Swat District shapefile (https://hydrologydata.org). Time of concentration was derived using the SCS lag time formula:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{T}}_{{\mathrm{c}}} = \frac{{L^{0.8} \cdot (S + 1)^{0.7} }}{{1140 \cdot (Y \cdot 100)^{0.5} }}$$\end{document}where L is the flow length, S is the maximum retention, and Y is the average watershed slope. Watershed baseflow was represented by the monthly constant method. In this approach, the base flow remains fixed throughout every simulation time step within a particular month. The approach is to be used in the long-term simulations, and it has to have an independent value that belongs to every month which is comprised by the total simulation time. Mean flow in its lowest setting was used as the pre-calibration value. Each reach in the watershed network had been provided with a value of lag time in order to show how long it takes runoff to travel through one sub-basin to the other. The Lag Time was calculated at first by using the physical features of the watershed, slope, length of the flow, and land use and then fine-tuned using the observed data of stream flow during the process of model calibration.

The data was split into a 75 percent calibration set (1993–2013) and a 25 percent validation set (2014–2019). Calibration optimized CN, lag time, baseflow, and routing parameters through iterative manual adjustment to minimize deviation between observed and simulated hydrographs26. Flood frequency analysis

Flood frequency analysis was conducted to estimate the streamflow discharges for different return periods at Chakdara Discharge Station River Swat, using Easyfit software. Three datasets, including observed annual maximum flows (1993–2019) and projected flows under SSP245 and SSP585 climate scenarios (2015–2099), were analyzed using a set of probability distributions, including Log-Logistic, GEV, Gumbel, Log-Pearson III, Weibull, Gamma, Normal, and Pareto27. The performance of these distributions was evaluated by using goodness-of-fit (GoF) tests, namely Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS), the Anderson–Darling (AD), and the Chi-square tests.

The VIKOR multi-criteria decision-making model was used to combine the test results to make model selection more objective. The historical data set identified Log-Logistic as the most appropriate and then followed by GEV and Log-Pearson III. Gumbel distribution performed better under SSP245 scenario, whereas with the high-emission SSP585 scenario, the GEV distribution performed better. These results are consistent with the findings of the recent works seeking to popularize hybrid frameworks that combine statistical models with decision-making tools to create useful flood models28.

Design hydrograph for hydraulic modeling

The study generated synthetic triangular hydrographs for the observed, SSP245, and SSP585 scenarios to define upstream boundary conditions in the HEC-RAS 2D hydraulic model. Each hydrograph spanned 100 days, beginning and ending with zero discharge on days 0 and 100, respectively, with a peak discharge occurring on day 50. These hydrographs represented the discharge corresponding to different return periods of 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, and 200 years as previously predicted by flood frequency analysis. This symmetric structure was responsible and reasonably good for capturing the rising and recession limbs of different floods, which falls in line with the expectations of classical flood model principles29. Linear interpolation was adopted for interpolation between base and peak values to generate a continuous and smooth transition. Recent literature showed that the use of synthetic triangular hydrographs is a more effective approach for flood hazard mapping, especially for climate stress testing and flood risk mapping30.

HEC-RAS 2D hydraulic modeling

HEC-RAS 2D was applied for hydraulic simulation across the Swat River basin using a 10 m DEM, projected to the UTM Zone 42N. The computational mesh was initially defined at 100 m and subsequently refined to 50 m, 30 m, and finally 10 m to align with the DEM resolution near floodplains and breaklines, thereby improving hydraulic precision31. Boundary conditions were established at both upstream and downstream limits. At the upstream boundary, triangular hydrographs were assigned for the observed, SSP245, and SSP585 scenarios, each representing 100 h of simulation with peak discharges corresponding to return periods of 10, 50, 100, 200, and 500 years. The peak discharge occurred at hour 50, while the starting and ending discharges were set to zero to represent the rising and recession limbs of the flood29. A normal depth assumption was applied to downstream boundary conditions, using the average bed slope of the river reach and elevation data through the DEM using the recommendations of the32.6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{S }} = \frac{{{\mathrm{H}}_{{\text{upstream }}} {\text{ - H}}_{{{\mathrm{downstream}}}} }}{{\mathrm{L}}}$$\end{document}where S is the channel slope, H represents elevations derived from the DEM, and L is the channel length. This method is consistent with practices outlined by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers32.

Initial Manning’s roughness coefficients were assigned based on literature values: 0.030 for the main gravel-bed channel and 0.040–0.050 for floodplains characterized by varying vegetation and surface roughness33. These were narrowed in the model calibration in a bid to accurately incorporate the observed behavior of floods. The Unsteady Flow Simulation module was used to carry out the simulation during a 100-h duration as input hydrographs have shown. Time steps were also chosen with great care in such a way that the modeled dynamic aspects of the flood wave propagation were properly reflected and numerical stability was established. The final configuration allowed detailed modeling of the flood in both past and future climates which do play pivotal roles in the establishment of the possible extents of inundation and flow behavior throughout the Swat River flood plain.

Hazard assessment

The flood depth and velocity outputs obtained from the 2D hydraulic simulations in HEC-RAS 6.0 for six scenarios—Observed, SSP245, and SSP585 under 10- and 100-year return periods—were generated and subsequently imported into a GIS environment for spatial flood hazard assessment across the SRB. To ensure consistency and comparability, both depth and velocity rasters were reclassified into standardized hazard categories based on established thresholds (Smith et al., 2014; USACE, 2016). Flood depth was classified as Low (< 0.5 m), Moderate (0.5–1.5 m), High (1.5–3.0 m), and Very High (> 3.0 m)34, while flow velocity was categorized as Low (< 0.5 m/s), Moderate (0.5–1.0 m/s), High (1.0–2.0 m/s), and Very High (> 2.0 m/s). To provide an integrated assessment of flood hazard, a Flood Hazard Index (H) was subsequently calculated using the following equation:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{H }} = {\text{ D}} \times {\text{ V}}$$\end{document}where D is the flood depth (in meters) and V is the flow velocity (in m/s), both at the raster cell level. This index effectively captures the combined influence of shallow high-velocity and deep low-velocity flows, both of which can be destructive35. The resultant hazard index raster was further classified into four categories—Low (< 0.5), Moderate (0.51–1.0), High (1.01–2.0), and Very High (> 2.0)—following DEFRA and Environment Agency guidelines36,37.

Subsequently, GIS-based spatial analysis was performed in ArcGIS to quantify the extent of each hazard class using zonal statistics. The procedure included: (1) extraction of class-specific cell counts via Reclassify and Zonal Histogram tools, (2) area computation based on the raster resolution (9.6 × 9.6 m2), and (3) conversion to hectares using:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{Area}} \left( {{\mathrm{ha}}} \right) \, = {\text{ Cell}} \;{\text{Count }} \times \, \left( {{9}.{6} \times {9}.{6}} \right)/{1}0,000$$\end{document}

This quantitative method allows us to compare the exposed areas by the hazard in various climate scenarios and the frequency of days. The findings demonstrate how hazard zones change and increase under future circumstances, giving useful and implementable data to zoning in floodplains, disaster preparedness, and climate adaptation planning. The combination of a high-resolution hydraulic model identified hazard metrics, and the superiority of GIS analytics provides a powerful flood hazard analysis. This methodology is consonant with the best international practices in mitigating flood risk science38.

Results and discussion

Bias correction

The linear scaling method effectively reduced systematic biases in the raw outputs of GCMs for daily precipitation, maximum, and minimum temperatures relative to observed data from Kalam Station (1993–2014). Post-correction, the mean precipitation values from the eleven CMIP6 models closely aligned with observed records, significantly enhancing the reliability of climate inputs for hydrological simulations in HEC-HMS. The same correction approach was extended to future projections (2015–2099) under SSP245 and SSP585 scenarios. By adjusting baseline values while preserving the intrinsic climate change signals of the GCMs, linear scaling improved the regional representativeness and plausibility of the hydrological projections. These outcomes affirm the suitability of linear scaling for bias correction in climate–hydrology studies, consistent with the findings of Mahmood et al.17.

Model ranking using XGBoost and SHAP

A hybrid machine learning framework integrating XGBoost regression and SHAP interpretation was applied to rank GCMs and quantify the contribution of each climate predictor. This combination ensured both high predictive accuracy and model interpretability, revealing the relative influence of individual input variables on model performance. Precipitation, maximum temperature, and minimum temperature were modeled independently due to their distinct roles in controlling snowmelt and runoff dynamics in the SRB.

During the historical evaluation period (1993–2014), bias-corrected daily data from the 11 CMIP6 GCMs were used as predictors, and observed records from the Kalam Station were used as target variables. The XGBoost models demonstrated strong capability in capturing nonlinear relationships between climate variables and observations, achieving high R2 across all parameters (Table 4). This confirms their robustness in replicating observed climatic behavior. SHAP-based analysis further decomposed the predictive contributions of each GCM, enhancing model transparency and supporting data-informed climate adaptation strategies.

Hydrological modeling

In the HEC-HMS, a semi-distributed hydrological model of the SRB was created that can forecast the streamflow in both historic and the climate conditions in the future. A DEM of 30 × 30 m was founded to partition the basin into eight sub-basins. The major model inputs were the daily precipitation, maximum and minimum temperature, land use/land cover, hydrologic groups of soil, and observed streamflow (1993–2019). Data on temperature was very crucial in modeling evapotranspiration and snowmelt, which was crucial in the mountainous SRB region. Future conditions of SSP245 and SSP585 were projected to be simulated using bias-corrected precipitation and temperature.

Flood frequency analysis

Flood frequency analysis was carried out to calculate discharge values of different return periods at the Chakdara Discharge Station in the Swat District using observed and future climatic datasets (SSP245 and SSP585). Observed streamflow (maximum annually) 1993–2019 was used as well as future projections (2015–2099 and 2015–2099) in SSP245 and SSP585. In the case of every dataset, there were several theoretical probability distributions that were fitted, including the distributions tested were Log Logistic, Generalized Extreme Value (GEV), Log Pearson Type III, Exponential, Normal, Gumbel, Weibull, Gamma and Pareto. The statistical analysis of goodness-of-fit of each distribution was performed by the Kolmogorov Smirnov (K–S) test, by the Anderson–Darling (A–D) test, and by the Chi-square test, which taken together yield the quantification of the closeness of the distribution to matching the empirical data. The results were further combined through the VIKOR technique of multi-criteria decision-making, which permitted the generation of an overall ranking to determine which distribution was the most appropriate for each predictor dataset.

The best-fitting distribution for the observed dataset was identified as Log Logistic, followed by generalized extreme value (GEV), Log Pearson Type III, Exponential, and Normal (Table 7). For the SSP245 scenario, the Gumbel distribution was the most appropriate, while for the SSP585 scenario, the GEV distribution ranked highest. These selections were made based on minimum VIKOR_Q values, indicating the best compromise among the goodness-of-fit tests shown in 4.

Design hydrograph

Triangular hydrographs representing the observed data, SSP245, and SSP585 scenarios were constructed to serve as upstream boundary conditions in the 2D HEC-RAS hydraulic model. Each hydrograph was formulated over a 100-day period, starting at zero discharge on day 0, reaching peak discharge—corresponding to the return period—at day 50, and returning to zero discharge by day 100, forming a symmetrical triangular shape. The peak discharges correspond to the maximum values for 10-, 50-, 100-, 200-, and 500-year return periods. Missing discharge values between the start, peak, and end points were filled using linear interpolation to ensure smoothness and accurate simulation of flood wave propagation. This triangular hydrograph method is widely accepted in hydraulic modeling due to its ability to simplify flood waveforms while realistically capturing flood dynamics, making it effective for evaluating flood extents and inundation patterns across various flood scenarios.

Hydraulic modeling

The SRB was simulated using a 2D hydraulic modeling framework in HEC-RAS 6.0 to generate high-resolution flood depth and flow velocity maps under observed and projected climate conditions (SSP245 and SSP585). Simulations were performed for 10-year and 100-year return periods, and the resulting outputs were reclassified into four hazard levels, low, moderate, high, and very high. This classification enabled a clearer interpretation of the basin’s hydrodynamic behavior and its implications for flood risk assessment and management42.

Flood depth categories were defined as follows: low (< 0.5 m), moderate (0.5–1.5 m), high (1.5–3.0 m), and very high (> 3.0 m). As shown in Fig. 11, the 10-year return period simulation primarily exhibited Low and Moderate flood depths, with inundation largely confined to the active floodplain. Under the SSP245 scenario, Moderate-depth zones became more extensive, suggesting broader inundation even under lower-frequency events. In contrast, the SSP585 scenario showed a substantial increase in High and Very High flood depths, reflecting intensified flood hazards driven by climate change.

Hazard assessment

Flood hazard evaluation in this study combines hydraulic simulation results with GIS-based hazard indexing to quantify flood risks under current and future climatic conditions in the SRB. Six distinct scenarios—observed 10- and 100-year return periods—were analyzed along with projected floods under SSP245 and SSP585 for the same intervals. The assessment utilizes flood depth, flow velocity, and a composite hazard index to characterize spatial and temporal variations in flood hazard. For each scenario, hazard maps were developed (Fig. 13) by reclassifying the computed hazard index into four categories—low (< 0.5), moderate (0.5–1.0), high (1.0–2.0), and very high (> 2.0)—in accordance with the classification framework of y Kvočka et al.36. These maps illustrate the depth of floods and velocity, highlighting areas with elevated risks of structural damage, erosion, and other hazards.

Limitations and uncertainties of the current study

Pakistan faces considerable data constraints, particularly in hydrological observations, which limit the precision of modeling efforts. The lack of recent streamflow records constitutes a key limitation of this study. Future investigations should integrate updated discharge data, as pronounced climatic fluctuations—especially the 2022 and 2025 flood events—have likely altered hydrological regimes. Additionally, uncertainties arising from CMIP6 model outputs, bias-correction techniques, and emission scenario assumptions introduce variability into flood projections. Simulated streamflow results also retain uncertainty due to the coarse spatial resolution of input datasets. Furthermore, future studies should account for rainfall distribution patterns and the spatial extent of agricultural, urban, and wetland areas within the catchment, as these factors influence streamflow dynamics and may support nature-based flood mitigation strategies46,47. Despite these limitations, the present study delivers a reliable assessment of climate variability impacts on streamflow, providing a foundation for climate-resilient water resource management.

Conclusion

Floods represent a growing hazard in the SRB, where climatic variability, glacial dynamics, and data scarcity constrain the accuracy of conventional flood risk assessments. This study develops a novel hybrid framework that integrates SHAP-based XGBoost for GCM ranking, RF ensemble modeling, and coupled hydrologic–hydraulic simulations to map multi-scenario flood hazards. The framework offers an interpretable, data-driven, and physically grounded approach for evaluating climate-induced flood risks in the SRB. The main conclusions can be summarized as follows:The developed methodology enables high-resolution flood hazard assessment under both historical and projected climate scenarios (SSP245 and SSP585) for multiple return periods.The results reveal a substantial increase in flood hazard under future scenarios—particularly SSP585—where high and very high hazard zones expand to nearly 78% of the floodplain during the 100-year event, compared with 69% coverage for the 10-year flood under baseline conditions. This emphasizes the growing susceptibility of mountainous catchments like the SRB to intensified hydro-meteorological extremes.Beyond its methodological innovation, the framework provides a practical decision-support system for climate-resilient spatial planning. Its capacity to quantify GCM-driven uncertainties in flood depth, velocity, and extent offers valuable input for risk-informed zoning, infrastructure design, and disaster preparedness.The approach is also adaptable to other data-limited or topographically complex basins, broadening its relevance to diverse hydroclimatic contexts. Overall, this study highlights the necessity of advancing toward proactive, climate-informed flood risk management to enhance the resilience of vulnerable riverine communities and to guide policy-driven adaptation strategies at regional and national scales.
