Quantum thermal state preparation for near-term quantum processors
Jerome Lloyd, Dmitry A. Abanin

Abstract
Preparation of quantum thermal states of many-body systems is a key computational challenge for quantum processors, with applications in physics, chemistry, and classical optimization. We provide a simple and efficient algorithm for thermal state preparation, combining engineered bath resetting and modulated system-bath coupling to derive a quantum channel approximately satisfying quantum detailed balance relations. We show that the fixed point σ ^ \hat{\sigma} of the channel approximates the Gibbs state as ‖ σ ^ − σ ^ β ‖ ∼ θ 2 \|\hat{\sigma}-\hat{\sigma}_{\beta}\|\sim\theta^{2} , where θ \theta is the system-bath coupling and σ ^ β ∝ e − β ​ H ^ S \hat{\sigma}_{\beta}\propto e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{S}} . We provide extensive numerics, for the example of the 2D Quantum Ising model, confirming that the protocol successfully prepares the thermal state throughout the finite-temperature phase diagram, including near the quantum phase transition. Our algorithm provides a path to efficient quantum simulation of quantum-correlated states at finite temperature with current and near-term quantum processors.



I
Introduction

In experimental quantum systems, one frequently observes that the system tends naturally toward a near-equilibrium distribution, so long as heat can be efficiently dissipated into the wider environment. For electronic degrees of freedom in solid state systems, the atomic lattice acts as an effective heat bath, while in atomic and molecular setups, energy typically dissipates into electromagnetic field modes. In contrast, the task of sampling on a quantum computer from the equilibrium (Gibbs) distribution with inverse temperature
\beta
,

\hat{\sigma}_{\beta}\propto e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{S}},

(1)

is a formidable algorithmic challenge with a wide range of applications
Dalzell et al. 2025
;
Bergamaschi et al. 2024
;
Lemieux et al. 2021
;
Cao et al. 2019
;
Brandao and Svore 2016
. Given the efficiency of cooling in real-world quantum systems, where does the difficulty arise in designing practical cooling algorithms for use in quantum simulators and quantum processors?

In particular, two main difficulties have frustrated efforts to design efficient quantum algorithms mimicking cooling processes in nature. Firstly, the basic assumption in statistical mechanics is that the reservoir (or ‘bath’) to which the system is coupled to is macroscopic, with
N_{B}\gg N_{S}
, where
N_{S}
(
N_{B}
) is the number of degrees of freedom in the system (bath). Under this assumption, the bath density of states approaches a continuous function, which allows for irreversible decay of the system towards a steady state set by the coupling with the bath
Cohen-Tannoudji et al. 2024
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
. A system coupled to a finite bath, on the other hand, will exhibit recurrences in the late-time limit, and does not tend toward a steady state. With present-day quantum simulators of modest sizes, and often restricted geometries, it is not immediately clear how to get around this issue.

Figure 1:

The system density matrix evolves under the repeated map
\hat{\rho}_{n+1}=\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho}_{n})
, consisting of three stages: (i) initializing a bath of auxiliary qubits in the
\ket{0}
state, (ii) joint unitary evolution and (iii) reset of the bath qubits. The unitary stage consists of evolution under a time-dependent unitary
\mathcal{U}_{\tau}
, defined in Eq. (
4
), with
-M_{T}\leq\tau\leq M_{T}
,
M_{T}\propto\sqrt{\beta}
.
The coupling to the bath is smoothly switched on with an interaction strength modulated by the filter function
f_{\tau}
, to ensure the resulting map approximately satisfies detailed balance. An optional randomization step
\mathcal{R}
(iv) grants an additional suppression of unwanted system coherences. Our protocol is designed with near-term analog and digital processors in mind.

Secondly, cooling processes are inherently limited by the quantum energy-time uncertainty relation,
\Delta\epsilon\Delta t\gtrsim\hbar
. In order to accurately resolve the energy levels of the system Hamiltonian, one requires times of order of the inverse level spacing, which is typically exponentially large in the system size. This hinders quantum algorithms which depend on quantum phase estimation
Kitaev 1995
(QPE) routines, such as the quantum Metropolis sampling algorithm introduced in
Temme et al. 2011
, since the QPE resolution scales inversely proportional to the runtime. This limit is clearly unphysical, since real-life macroscopic systems do not take exponentially long times to reach equilibrium, in the general case. Nevertheless, on a formal level the finite energy resolution leads to the breaking of
detailed balance
relations in the evolution equations, which makes proving convergence to the Gibbs state technically difficult
Chen et al. 2023a
.

Recent works have aimed to surmount these obstacles, with progress in several directions. On the experimental side, recent experiments on a digital Google quantum processor
Mi et al. 2024
have demonstrated preparation of low-energy states of up to 35 qubits, by coupling to a small
resettable
bath of auxiliary qubits. By periodically resetting the bath, the issues associated with recurrences are mitigated, and the system settles into a steady state at late times. A theoretical breakthrough was made in the paper by Chen et al.
Chen et al. 2023b
, where a Lindblad equation was derived which has the Gibbs state as an exact steady state, but with provably efficient runtime and resource costs. The total Hamiltonian simulation time depends only on the ‘mixing time’ of the Lindbladian, a quantity which essentially measures the worst case time to reach the steady state starting from
any
initial state. The key insight was that the conditions of quantum detailed balance can be satisfied for certain Lindbladians with a specially tailored coherent term added. We refer to
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Chen and Brandão 2021
;
Shtanko and Movassagh 2021
;
Gilyén et al. 2024
;
Ding et al. 2025
;
Guo et al. 2025
;
Hahn et al. 2025
for related works, as well as
Lin 2025
for a pedagogical overview. Unfortunately, the proposed algorithmic techniques are rather high-level and likely out of reach for simulators in the near future. The mixing time entering the bounds in
Chen et al. 2023b
is also a quantity which is notoriously difficult to bound even in simple cases
Levin and Peres 2017
, although recent efforts have led to impressive new results in this area, in particular proving efficient mixing in certain weakly-interacting or high temperature systems
Bardet et al. 2023
;
Rouzé et al. 2025
;
Rouzé et al. 2024
;
Bakshi et al. 2024
;
Tong and Zhan 2024
;
Šmíd et al. 2025
.

With near-term applications in mind, the current authors put forward a simple algorithm
Lloyd et al. 2025
aimed at efficiently preparing low-energy states of many-body Hamiltonians. The algorithm combined two main features: a small resettable bath as in the experiment
Mi et al. 2024
, and a coupling between system and bath qubits which is
modulated
in time, again with the aim of achieving detailed balance. The algorithm was shown to be effective at preparing ground states of several quantum systems, supported by heuristic arguments for cooling via quasiparticle processes at low temperatures. Other works based on similar principles have helped establish dissipative ground state preparation as a practical and efficient method for future applications
Kraus et al. 2008
;
Verstraete et al. 2009
;
Roy et al. 2020
;
Metcalf et al. 2020
;
Raghunandan et al. 2020
;
Matthies et al. 2024
;
Kishony et al. 2025
;
Ding et al. 2024
;
Zhan et al. 2025
;
Molpeceres et al. 2025
;
Stadler et al. 2025
.

In this work, we generalise the modulated coupling protocol of Ref.
Lloyd et al. 2025
(also dubbed ‘quasiparticle cooling algorithm’) to efficiently prepare quantum thermal states, applicable to present-day digital and analog simulator platforms. There are three essential ingredients to our algorithm, diplayed schematically in Fig.
1
, all of which are accessible on today’s hardware: (i) unitary Hamiltonian evolution,
U=e^{-i\delta\hat{H}_{S}}
, implemented either “exactly” or via a suitable Trotterisation of the evolution, (ii) fast reset of auxiliary qubits, i.e. reinitialization to the empty state
\ket{0}
, (iii) time-dependent coupling between the system and auxiliary qubits. The coupling needs only to be of the form
\hat{V}(t)=\theta f(t)\hat{A}_{i}\hat{R}_{j}
, where
\hat{A}_{i}
(
\hat{R}_{j}
) is a local operator acting on the system (bath),
\theta
is the overall system-bath coupling strength, and
f(t)
is a time-dependent modulation. We do not require the operators entering the coupling to vary in time.

In addition, we propose a
randomized
modification of the cooling protocol, which further suppresses off-diagonal elements in the Hamiltonian eigenbasis. The extra randomization step involves Hamiltonian evolution for a random time
\sim T_{0}
, and does not change the overall resource-cost scalings of the algorithm.

For weak system-bath coupling
\theta
, we show, through a combination of perturbative and numerical methods, that the modulated coupling protocol prepares the Gibbs state to an accuracy
\|\hat{\sigma}_{\beta}-\hat{\sigma}\|\sim\theta^{2}
in the system-bath coupling, where
\hat{\sigma}
is the steady state of the cooling protocol. Thus the errors in both populations and coherences (off-diagonal elements) in the eigenbasis of
\hat{H}_{S}
scale to zero as
\theta^{2}
, in the limit of small system-bath coupling. The randomized protocol leads to a further dephasing of the coherences, giving an overall suppression
\hat{\sigma}_{ab}\approx\theta^{2}/(T_{0}\omega)
, where
T_{0}
is the (average) randomization time, and
\omega
the splitting between energy levels. Our results hold for any digital or analog platform capable of implementing the simple steps displayed in Fig.
1
.

We numerically demonstrate the efficiency of our protocol by preparing thermal states of the two-dimensional quantum Ising model, for a range of temperatures and interaction strengths, and lattice sizes of up to 16 spins. We benchmark the steady state by comparing several observables against exact values for the thermal state, finding close agreement in all cases. Of particular note is the system heat capacity, which displays clear signatures of the thermodynamic phase transition near the critical values of
\beta
and
J
(the ferromagnetic coupling), suggesting that our protocol can be used to prepare quantum-critical thermal states without additional complications. The accuracy of our results increases as the system-bath coupling is decreased, verifying the predicted perturbative scaling.

in the system-bath coupling. Turning to numerical results, we present in Section
IV.1

IV.2
our results for cooling in the 2D Quantum Ising model. We verify the perturbative scaling and show that our protocol accurately prepares the thermal state throughout the finite-temperature phase diagram. In Section
V

VI

II
Modulated coupling protocol

In this section, we describe our modulated coupling protocol for preparing quantum thermal states. The idea of modulating the coupling to bias cooling transitions was suggested in
Lloyd et al. 2025
, while the idea of engineering a resettable qubit bath goes back to
Terhal and DiVincenzo 2000
.

We consider
N_{S}
system qubits coupled to
N_{B}
bath qubits (‘auxiliaries’). The bath qubits are prepared initially in the product state
\hat{\Phi}=\ket{0}\bra{0}^{\otimes N_{B}}
. For the initial state of the system, we take the maximally mixed (infinite temperature) state,
\hat{\rho}_{0}\propto\hat{I}
. Experimentally, initial states of the system can be uniformly sampled from any complete basis set.

In each step of the cooling protocol, the system and bath are evolved under a joint unitary
\mathcal{Q}
, specified below, and then the bath qubits are reset to the initial state
\hat{\Phi}
. The effect of coupling and reset can be expressed as a quantum channel acting on the system density matrix
\hat{\rho}
,

\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})=\text{tr}_{B}[\mathcal{Q}(\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\Phi})\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger}].

(2)

We refer to one application of the above channel as the “reset cycle”. The system density matrix then evolves as
\hat{\rho}_{n+1}=\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho}_{n})
. The system converges to a fixed point of the channel, defined by
\mathcal{E}(\hat{\sigma})=\hat{\sigma}
, in the limit of a large number of cycles.

So far the specified channel is completely general. We now explain how to choose the unitary
\mathcal{Q}
to prepare quantum thermal states. We focus on the setup for digital platforms, while the setup for analog platforms requires minor modifications which we comment on at the end of this section. We decompose
\mathcal{Q}
into a total of
2M_{T}+1
unitary layers, followed by an optional ‘randomisation’ unitary
\mathcal{R}
,

\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{R}\ \mathcal{U}_{M_{T}}\ldots\mathcal{U}_{0}\ldots\mathcal{U}_{-M_{T}},

(3)

where each unitary
\mathcal{U}
is in turn reduced into system (S), bath (B), and coupling components,

\mathcal{U_{\tau}}=U_{\theta,\tau}U_{B}U_{S}\equiv e^{-i\delta\theta\hat{V}_{\tau}}e^{-i\delta\hat{H}_{B}}e^{-i\delta\hat{H}^{\prime}_{S}}.

(4)

Here
\theta
is a small-coupling parameter (the system-bath coupling) and
\delta
the Trotter angle, also assumed to be small
in order to avoid errors from Floquet heating effects. The Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{S}
can be viewed as an effective prethermal Hamiltonian for the system evolution, which differs from the target system Hamiltonian
H_{S}
by an error of order
\delta^{2}
. We will primarily be interested in the Hamiltonian cooling limit obtained as
\delta\to 0
,
M_{T}\to\infty
, and
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{S}\to\hat{H}_{S}
, with the ratio
T\equiv M_{T}\delta
kept constant. In the rest of the paper we write
U_{S}=e^{-i\delta\hat{H}_{S}}
for the system unitary, with the above limit assumed. We refer to parameter
T
, which measures the overall depth of the unitary evolution before the bath reset takes place, as the “reset time”.

The randomisation unitary
\mathcal{R}
consists of a random-depth unitary acting only on the system,

\mathcal{R}=e^{-i\delta\hat{H}^{\prime}_{S}M_{R}}=U_{S}^{M_{R}},

(5)

where
M_{R}
is an integer of the same order as
M_{T}
, chosen at random for each new reset cycle. The reason for the extra randomisation step will be explained in detail in Section
III.3
: essentially, it acts as a further dephasing map on the density matrix coherences. In our results below, we will make use of both the protocol with the extra randomization step (randomized protocol) and without (unrandomized protocol), in which case we set
M_{R}=0
in Eq. (
5
).

For the bath Hamiltonian, a sufficient choice is to take non-interacting qubits according to

\hat{H}_{B}=-\frac{h}{2}\sum_{\mu=1}^{N_{S}}\hat{Z}_{\mu},

(6)

where parameter
h
enters as the single bath energy scale. It is also possible, and may be advantageous in some settings, that each bath spin is subject to an independent field
h_{\mu}
, as long as the system-bath couplings are adjusted accordingly (see Eq. (
8
)).

The
\tau
-dependent coupling operator
\hat{V}_{\tau}
is specified by

\hat{V}_{\tau}=f_{\tau}\sum_{\mu=1}^{N_{B}}\ \hat{A}_{\mu}\hat{Y}_{\mu},

(7)

where
f_{\tau}
is a modulating function which will be referred to as the ‘filter function’,
\hat{Y}_{\mu}
is the Pauli-
Y
matrix acting on the
\mu
-th auxiliary qubit (
\hat{X}_{\mu}
can also be used), and
\hat{A}_{\mu}
is a local system operator associated with the
\mu
-th auxiliary coupling, which is assumed to be Hermitian. The precise choice of
\hat{A}_{\mu}
does not affect arguments regarding the form of the steady state (after making the Hermiticity assumption), but a good choice of the operators is important for controlling the ergodicity of the dynamics, as well as ensuring fast approach to the steady state. For preparing low-temperature states, it is beneficial to choose
\hat{A}_{\mu}
to target quasiparticle-like excitations of the system, while still remaining local
Lloyd et al. 2025
. Frequently, a single Pauli operator suffices, e.g.
\hat{A}_{\mu}=\hat{Y}_{i_{\mu}}
.

The choice of the filter function is the critical element in designing the protocol such that the steady state approaches the Gibbs state. The class of allowed functions is restricted according to the principle of quantum detailed balance
Ding et al. 2025
, as will be explained in Section
III.2
. This still leaves some flexiblity, and a convenient choice is to take a Gaussian filter,

f_{\tau}\propto\exp\Big(-\frac{a^2\delta^2\tau^2}{2}\Big)

(8)

with a normalisation
\delta\sum_{\tau=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}|f_{\tau}|=1
, and width set by

a=\sqrt{\frac{4h}{\beta}}.

(9)

While at this level
\beta
enters as a free parameter of the protocol, the association with the inverse temperature of the Gibbs state is not accidental. The bath splitting
h
enters to fix the overall width of the filter function. In order to avoid truncation effects from the finite
M_{T}
, we require
T\gg\sqrt{\beta/4h}
. Thus low temperatures (large
\beta
) require longer periods of unitary evolution, in line with the expectation from the energy-time uncertainty principle. In practice, we find
T\approx 5a^{-1}
sufficient for effective cooling, due to the fast decay of the Gaussian tails. The bath parameter,
h
, should also be chosen judiciously, as it fixes the resonance centre for the cooling processes (see Eq. (
25
)) and thus effects the rate at which the steady state is approached. Normally we should choose
h\gtrsim\Delta
, where
\Delta
is the many-body energy gap. In applications the system gap is usually not known precisely. However, simple estimates are often available and the parameter
h
may be varied experimentally to find approximately optimal values of
h
.

Above we defined the cooling protocol for use on digital quantum simulators. The protocol can be defined similarly for analog machines. In this case we replace the unitary evolution by

\mathcal{Q}=e^{-iT_{R}\hat{H}_{S}}\mathcal{T}e^{-i\int_{-T}^{T}dt\ (\hat{H}_{S}+\hat{H}_{B}+\hat{V}_{\theta}(t))},

(10)

and define the filter function according to Eq. (
8
) by setting
t=\delta\tau
. In the next section, we will work exclusively in the
\delta\to 0
limit to simplify our derivations, so the results hold for both analog protocols and digital protocols (in the limit of small Trotter step). Experimentally, implementing our protocol on analog simulators requires the capability for reset of the bath qubits, which is available e.g. in the recently developed digital-analog superconducting qubit platform
Andersen et al. 2025
, or trapped ion architectures
Barreiro et al. 2011
. Digital processors allow a rapid reset on timescales comparable to two-qubit gates operation times, see e.g.
McEwen et al. 2021
;
Miao et al. 2022
;
Mi et al. 2024
.

In summary, our protocol requires: (i) time evolution under the system unitary
U_{S}=e^{-iT\hat{H}_{S}}
, for a time
T=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\beta/h})
, with
h\gtrsim\Delta
and
\Delta
the many-body gap (ii) a small (
N_{B}\geq\mathcal{O}(1)
) bath of resettable qubits (in practice
N_{B}\propto N_{S}
is likely needed to efficiently compete with device noise) and (iii) weak local coupling between system and bath, with time-dependent interaction strength set by the filter function, Eq. (
8
).

III
Approximately thermal steady states

To which extent do the steady states of the cooling protocol
\mathcal{E}
approximate the target thermal state,
\hat{\sigma}_{\beta}
? To answer this question, we will first show that the modulated coupling protocol leads to small violations of ‘detailed balance’ in the interaction frame defined with respect to the system Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{S}
. These violations cause the steady state to deviate from the exact Gibbs state. We then calculate the errors introduced in the steady state by means of a perturbation theory around the Gibbs state, and show that the errors generically scale as
\|\hat{\sigma}-\hat{\sigma}_{\beta}\|\sim\theta^{2}
, in terms of the system bath coupling.

III.1
Expansion in weak coupling

Intuitively, we expect errors in the thermal state to arise due to finite coupling between the system and the bath. We therefore consider the weak-coupling limit
\theta^{2}/a\ll 1
where we may
expand the reset channel, Eq. (
2
), in powers of the system-bath coupling. Note that
1/a
, not
T
, measures the effective time of interaction between the system and bath.

The interaction picture is defined with respect to the free system-bath evolution by

\tilde{O}_{\tau}=U_{0}^{-\tau}(\hat{O}_{\tau})U_{0}^{\tau},\hskip 14.22636ptU_{0}=U_{B}U_{S},

(11)

such that

\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}=U_{S}^{-M_{R}}U_{0}^{-M_{T}}\mathcal{Q}U_{0}^{-(M_{T}+1)}=\tilde{U}_{\theta,M_{T}}\ldots\tilde{U}_{\theta,-M_{T}}.

(12)

We note that this unconventional definition of the interaction picture is due to our choice of symmetrising the reset protocol with respect to
-M_{T}\leq\tau\leq M_{T}
, and is purely convention. The interaction picture allows us to rewrite Eq. (
2
) as the concaternation of channels

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})=\mathcal{S}^{M_{T}+M_{R}}\circ\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\circ\mathcal{S}^{M_{T}+1}(\hat{\rho}),

(13)

where

\mathcal{S}(\hat{\rho})=U_{S}(\hat{\rho})U_{S}^{-1},

(14)

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\hat{\rho})=\text{tr}_{B}\big[\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}(\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\Phi})\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}\big].

(15)

Note that the Schrödinger map in Eq. (
13
) is a random channel depending on the timestep
M_{R}
(with
M_{R}=0
for the unrandomized protocol). We refer to
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
as the interaction-picture map.

Next, under the weak-coupling assumption, we expand
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
to second order in
\theta
(we leave the details to Appendix
A
) to arrive at the interaction-picture map in Lindblad form:

\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\hat{\rho})-\hat{\rho}}{\theta^{2}}=-i[\hat{G}^{\text{LS}},\hat{\rho}]+\{\hat{K},\hat{\rho}\}+\sum_{\mu}\hat{L}_{\mu}\hat{\rho}\hat{L}_{\mu}^{\dagger}.

(16)

Here,

\hat{L}_{\mu}=\delta\sum_{\tau=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}f_{\tau}e^{i\delta h\tau}\tilde{A}_{\mu\tau},

(17)

is the jump operator associated to interactions with the
\mu
-th auxiliary,
\hat{K}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu}\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{\mu}\hat{L}_{\mu}
, and

\hat{G}^{\text{LS}}=\frac{\delta^{2}}{2i}\sum_{\mu,\tau,\tau^{\prime}}\text{sgn}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})e^{i\delta h(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}f_{\tau}f_{\tau^{\prime}}\ \hat{A}^{\dagger}_{\mu\tau}\hat{A}_{\mu\tau^{\prime}}

(18)

is a Hamiltonian correction traditionally referred to as the “Lamb shift”.

Since
T\gg a^{-1}
, the limits on the Gaussian sums may be extended to infinity with marginal error. Using also the assumption of small
\delta
(we require
\delta a\ll 1)
, we replace the summation formulae with continuous-time integrals according to

\hat{L}_{\mu}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\ g(t)\tilde{A}_{\mu}(t),

(19)

\hat{G}^{\text{LS}}=\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{\mu}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}dtdt^{\prime}\ \text{sgn}(t-t^{\prime})g^{*}(t)g(t^{\prime})\tilde{A}^{\dagger}_{\mu}(t)\tilde{A}_{\mu}(t^{\prime}),

(20)

where
g(t)=e^{iht}f_{t/\delta}.
The error encurred in this replacement is
\text{max}[\mathcal{O}(e^{-T^{2}a^{2}}/aT),\mathcal{O}(e^{-\pi^{2}/2\delta^{2}a^{2}})]
as shown in Appendix
B
). The final form of the interaction-picture map is the Lindblad equation specified in Eq. (
16
), with the matrices defined according to the integral formulae (
19
,
20
).

III.2
Violations of quantum detailed balance

As we will show below, the steady states of the Lindblad equation, Eq. (
16
), are not exact Gibbs states with respect to the system Hamiltonian
H_{S}
. The error can be traced to an ‘incorrect’ choice of the Lamb-shift Hamiltonian,
\hat{G}^{\text{LS}}
. It was shown in
Chen et al. 2023b
that, surprisingly, there exists a different choice of Hamiltonian,
\hat{G}^{\text{DB}}
, which ensures that the steady state is
exactly
thermal. Below we give a simple proof of this statement. Note that in our case, the Lamb-shift is fixed by the cooling protocol, and it is not clear how to derive a similar protocol where
\hat{G}^{\text{DB}}
appears naturally in the interaction picture.

We first introduce the concept of quantum detailed balance (QDB)
Guo et al. 2025
, following the definition in
Temme et al. 2011
, which holds for a general quantum channel
\mathcal{E}
. This coincides with the definition of ‘KMS’ detailed balance used in other works, defined at the level of the Lindbladian
Chen et al. 2023b
;
Ding et al. 2025
. For a given basis,
\ket{\psi_{a}}
, the QDB condition requires that
\mathcal{E}
satisfies the relation

\frac{\bra{\psi_{a}}\mathcal{E}(\ket{\psi_{c}}\bra{\psi_{d}})\ket{\psi_{b}}}{\bra{\psi_{d}}\mathcal{E}(\ket{\psi_{b}}\bra{\psi_{a}})\ket{\psi_{c}}}=\sqrt{\frac{p_{a}p_{b}}{p_{c}p_{d}}},

(21)

where
p_{a}
are probabilities satisfying
\sum_{a}p_{a}=1
. Then,

\hat{\pi}=\sum_{a}p_{a}\ket{\psi_{a}}\bra{\psi_{a}}

(22)

is a fixed point of the channel
\mathcal{E}
, since

\displaystyle\bra{\psi_{a}}\mathcal{E}(\hat{\pi})\ket{\psi_{b}}=\sqrt{p_{a}p_{b}}\sum_{c}\bra{\psi_{c}}\mathcal{E}(\ket{\psi_{b}}\bra{\psi_{a}})\ket{\psi_{c}}

\displaystyle=p_{a}\delta_{ab},

(23)

where we used the fact that
\mathcal{E}
is trace-preserving. We say that
\mathcal{E}
satisfies quantum detailed balance with respect to the state
\hat{\pi}
.

Our choice of filter function in Eq. (
8
) is made in order to approximately satisfy the QDB criteria with respect to the Gibbs state,
\hat{\sigma}_{\beta}
. To see this, consider the action of
\hat{L}_{\mu}
between eigenstates of the system Hamiltonian,
\ket{\phi_{a}}
. For the jump operators defined in Eq. (
19
),

\displaystyle\bra{\phi_{a}}\hat{L}_{\mu}\ket{\phi_{b}}=A_{\mu ab}\bar{f}_{h}(\omega_{\alpha\beta}),

(24)

where
\omega_{ab}=\epsilon_{b}-\epsilon_{a}
are the transition (Bohr) frequencies,
A_{\mu ab}=\bra{\phi_{a}}\hat{A}_{\mu}\ket{\phi_{b}}
, and

\bar{f}_{h}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\ e^{i\omega t}g(t)=e^{-\frac{(\omega-h)^{2}}{2a^{2}}}.

(25)

It is easy to check that the function
\bar{f}_{h}(\omega)
satisfies the
classical
detailed balance condition,

\bar{f}_{h}(\omega)=e^{\beta\omega/2}\bar{f}_{h}(-\omega)

(26)

using our choice of
a
in Eq. (
9
). This fixes the jump operators to obey the relation

\bra{\phi_{a}}\hat{L}_{\mu}\ket{\phi_{b}}=e^{\beta\omega_{ab}/2}\bra{\phi_{a}}\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{\mu}\ket{\phi_{b}}.

(27)

We then separate the Lindblad equation in (
16
) into two parts, the dissipative part

\mathcal{W}=\sum_{\mu}\hat{L}_{\mu}\bullet\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{\mu},

(28)

and a part

\mathcal{G}=-i[\hat{G},\bullet]+\{\hat{K},\bullet\}

(29)

which depends on a general Hamiltonian term
\hat{G}
in place of
\hat{G}^{\text{LS}}
. From Eq. (
27
), QDB is satisfied for the dissipative part:

\frac{\bra{\phi_{a}}\hat{L}_{\mu}(\ket{\phi_{c}}\bra{\phi_{d}})\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{\mu}\ket{\phi_{b}}}{\bra{\phi_{d}}\hat{L}_{\mu}(\ket{\phi_{b}}\bra{\phi_{a}})\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{\mu}\ket{\phi_{c}}}=\sqrt{\frac{e^{\beta\epsilon_{d}/2}e^{\beta\epsilon_{c}/2}}{{e^{\beta\epsilon_{a}/2}e^{\beta\epsilon_{b}/2}}}}

(30)

with
p_{a}\propto e^{-\beta\epsilon_{a}}
the Gibbs weights. On the other hand,
\mathcal{G}
satisfies QDB only if

\displaystyle\sqrt{p_{b}}\bra{\phi_{a}}\hat{X}\ket{\phi_{b}}=\sqrt{p_{a}}\bra{\phi_{a}}\hat{X}^{\dagger}\ket{\phi_{b}},

(31)

with the non-Hermitian operator

\hat{X}=\hat{K}-i\hat{G}.

(32)

Solving this equation for
\hat{G}
, we find

\hat{G}^{\text{DB}}_{ab}=-i\tanh\bigg(\frac{\beta\omega_{ab}}{4}\bigg)\hat{K}_{ab}.

(33)

For this choice of the coherent term, the Lindblad equation

{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{DB}}=\mathcal{G}^{\text{DB}}+\mathcal{W},\hskip 14.22636pt\mathcal{G}^{\text{DB}}=-i[\hat{G}^{\text{DB}},\bullet]+\{\hat{K},\bullet\}

(34)

satisfies QDB and hence
\mathcal{L}^{\text{DB}}(\hat{\sigma}_{\beta})=0
. Note also that
\hat{G}^{\text{DB}}
is only unique up to the addition of a term
\hat{\Lambda}
which is diagonal in the eigenbasis of
\hat{H}_{S}
, since any such term commutes with the Gibbs state.

Unfortunately, our Lamb-shift does not have the form of Eq. (
33
). We give an explicit expression for
\hat{G}^{\text{LS}}
in Appendix
C
, from which it is readily seen that the two differ. We denote the error as

\Delta\hat{G}=\hat{G}^{\text{LS}}-\hat{G}^{\text{DB}},

(35)

and for non-zero
\Delta\hat{G}
, the QDB conditions are not satisfied. While it is possible that quantum channels may exist which have the Gibbs state as their fixed point despite not satisfying the detailed balance conditions (this statement is at least true for classical Markov chains), this is not true in our case, as we show in the next section.

III.3
Approximately thermal steady states

Due to the violations of detailed balance, the steady state of the protocol differs from the Gibbs state. We will perform a perturbative expansion about the Gibbs state, from which it will be seen that the errors scale with the system-bath coupling
\|\hat{\sigma}-\hat{\sigma}_{\beta}\|\sim\theta^{2}
. Therefore, for weak coupling, the steady state is approximately thermal. We note that it is important to carry out the perturbation theory in the Schrödinger picture, since the steady states of
\mathcal{E}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
are generally different, and the small parameter
\theta
enters only in the steady state of channel
\mathcal{E}
.

In fact only the coherences in the Hamiltonian eigenbasis can be determined explicitly from the perturbative expansion. The correction to the populations can be shown to be
\mathcal{O}(\theta^{2})
, but requires solving a higher order Lindblad equation in order to be accurately determined. This statement is related to the fact that the coherent term satisfying QDB is only specified up to a term diagonal in the Hamiltonian eigenbasis.

To perform the perturbative expansion, we rewrite Eq. (
16
) as

\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\hat{\rho})-\hat{\rho}}{\theta^{2}}={\mathcal{L}}^{\text{DB}}(\hat{\rho})-i[\Delta\hat{G},\hat{\rho}],

(36)

and move back to the Schrödinger picture via

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})=\mathcal{S}^{2M_{T}+M_{R}+1}(\hat{\rho})+

(37)

\displaystyle\theta^{2}\mathcal{S}^{M_{T}+M_{R}}\circ\Big({\mathcal{L}}^{\text{DB}}\circ\mathcal{S}^{M_{T}+1}(\hat{\rho})-i[\Delta\hat{G},\mathcal{S}^{M_{T}+1}(\hat{\rho})]\Big).

The above equation depends on the randomization step
M_{R}
. In order to obtain the steady state after repeated application of the random channel, we consider the averaged map: we set
T_{R}=\delta M_{R}
and for simplicity consider the case where the probability
p_{T_{R}}
follows the exponential distribution
p_{T_{R}}\propto e^{-T_{R}/T_{0}}
, with characteristic time-scale
T_{0}=\mathcal{O}(T)
. The unitary channel
\mathcal{S}^{M_{R}}
, averaged over
M_{R}
, now implements a dephasing channel in the eigenbasis of the Hamiltonian,
\mathcal{D}\equiv\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}^{M_{R}}]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\hat{\rho})=\frac{1}{T_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dT_{R}\ e^{-T_{R}/T0}e^{-i\hat{H}_{S}T_{R}}(\hat{\rho})e^{i\hat{H}_{S}T_{R}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ab}\frac{\ket{\phi_{a}}\bra{\phi_{a}}\hat{\rho}\ket{\phi_{b}}\bra{\phi_{b}}}{1-i\omega_{ab}T_{0}}.

(38)

We then decompose the steady state of
\mathcal{E}
as
\hat{\sigma}=\hat{\sigma}_{0}+\theta^{2}\hat{\zeta}
, absorbing higher-order corrections into
\hat{\zeta}
. The zeroth-order equation for the steady state requires

\hat{\sigma}_{0}=\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{S}^{2M_{T}+1}(\hat{\sigma}_{0}),

(39)

which is satisfied for any
\hat{\sigma}_{0}
diagonal in the Hamiltonian eigenbasis. At second order, we require

\hat{\zeta}=\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{S}^{M_{T}}\circ\Big(\mathcal{S}^{M_{T}+1}(\hat{\zeta})+\mathcal{L}^{\text{DB}}(\hat{\sigma}_{0})-i[\Delta\hat{G},\hat{\sigma}_{0}]\Big).

(40)

This gives two conditions, for the diagonal and off-diagonal elements respectively. For the diagonal elements, the terms depending on
\hat{\zeta}
vanish, leaving us with

\Big(\mathcal{L}^{\text{DB}}(\hat{\sigma}_{0})-i[\Delta\hat{G},\hat{\sigma}_{0}]\Big)_{aa}=0.

(41)

It is easy to see that a solution to this equation is provided by the Gibbs state,
\hat{\sigma}_{0}=\hat{\sigma}_{\beta}
. The fact that the second-order perturbative equations are only sufficient to determine the zeroth-order diagonal elements is well known
Mori and Miyashita 2008
;
Fleming and Cummings 2011
;
Lee and Yeo 2022
, and stems from the fact that solving for the steady state populations is always a problem of degenerate perturbation theory. Consequently, accurately determining the second-order population corrections requires that the interaction-picture channel is expanded to fourth-order in
\theta
. This problem is intractable in general and we do not attempt it here
1
1

1

In certain cases
Lee and Yeo 2022
;
Thingna et al. 2012
, correct second-order populations are obtained after taking the limit
\omega_{ab}\to 0
in the second-order equation for the off-diagonal elements, which in our case gives

\hat{\zeta}_{aa}=\frac{e^{-\beta\epsilon_{a}}\beta}{Z(2T+T_{0})}\hat{G}^{\text{LS}}_{aa}.

(42)

We have found that this equation is inaccurate even for the single-spin cooling case, although it reproduces some features of the exact solution.
. However, Eq. (
41
) is sufficient to show that the corrections to the Gibbs state populations scale as
\theta^{2}
.

The leading order correction to the coherences can be explicitly determined from Eq. (
40
). Setting
\hat{\sigma}_{0}=\hat{\sigma}_{\beta}
, we solve for
a\neq b
(we replace
M_{T}\delta=T
and assume
\delta\omega\ll 1
to simplify the final expressions):

\displaystyle\hat{\zeta}_{ab}=-\frac{e^{-\beta\bar{\epsilon}_{ab}}}{Z}\frac{2i\sinh(\beta\omega_{ab}/2)e^{iT\omega_{ab}}}{[1-e^{2iT\omega_{ab}}-i\omega_{ab}T_{0}]}(\Delta\hat{G})_{ab},\hskip 5.69046pt

(43)

where
\bar{\epsilon}_{ab}=\frac{\epsilon_{a}+\epsilon_{b}}{2}
and
Z=\text{tr}\ \hat{\sigma}_{\beta}
is the partition function of the thermal state. It is now also easy to see the role played by the randomization step. Setting
T_{0}=0
in Eq. (
43
) – corresponding to the unrandomized protocol – leads to divergences when
\omega_{ab}
is a multiple of the drive frequency
\omega_{0}=\pi/T
(this effect survives for
\delta\to 0
, and is not related to the discretization of the protocol). The randomization step guarantees that no frequencies are resonant with the drive period, thus ensuring the smallness of the coherences in the steady state.

The steady state corrections described above can be understood from a complementary point of view, by rewriting
\hat{\sigma}
in the form of a modified Gibbs state

\hat{\sigma}\propto e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{S}^{\prime}}
, defined with respect to a
renormalised
system Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{S}=\hat{H}_{S}+\theta^{2}\hat{C}
. In order to determine the matrix
\hat{C}
, we make use of the Kubo formula

\displaystyle e^{-\beta(\hat{H}+\theta^{2}\hat{C})}=e^{-\beta\hat{H}}\bigg(1-\theta^{2}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\beta^{\prime}\hat{C}(\beta^{\prime})+\mathcal{O}(\theta^{4})\bigg)

(44)

where

\hat{V}(\beta^{\prime})=e^{\beta^{\prime}\hat{H}}\hat{V}e^{-\beta^{\prime}\hat{H}}

(45)

is the imaginary-time evolved perturbation. To order
\theta^{2}
, equating the Kubo formula and Eq. (
43
), we find

\hat{C}_{ab}=-\frac{i\omega_{ab}e^{i\omega_{ab}T}}{1-i\omega_{ab}T_{0}-e^{2i\omega_{ab}T}}(\Delta\hat{G})_{ab}+\delta_{ab}\Lambda_{a},

(46)

where
\Lambda_{a}
is the diagonal part, undetermined in the second-order scheme.

The fact that the interaction with the bath leads to a renormalisation of the system Hamiltonian hints that detailed balance may in fact be restored by considering the QDB equations in the ‘correct’ basis (here, the basis of
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{S}
). We show in Appendix
D
that this principle is correct, giving an alternative derivation of Eq. (
46
) by rotating to the interaction frame defined with respect to
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{S}
, which is then solved for self-consistently. We expect that this technique of moving to the ‘detailed balance’ frame may be of use more generally in finding steady state solutions of quantum master equations, a question we leave for future work.

Finally, let us give a brief comment on the Gibbs state corrections in two experimentally relevant regimes, of high and low temperature. When the parameter
a=\sqrt{\frac{4h}{\beta}}
is small,
\Delta\hat{G}
is ‘almost’ diagonal in the eigenbasis of the Hamiltonian. This can be seen by writing
\Delta\hat{G}
(see Appendix
C
) as

\Delta\hat{G}=2\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\hat{A}^{\dagger}_{\omega^{\prime}}\hat{A}_{\omega}e^{-(\omega-\omega^{\prime})^{2}\beta/16h}y(\omega,\omega^{\prime}),

(47)

where
|y(\omega,\omega^{\prime})|\leq 1
is a function defined in Appendix
C
, which is not important here. Thus the matrix elements of
\Delta\hat{G}
should be suppressed like
\Delta\hat{G}_{ab}\approx e^{-\omega_{ab}^{2}\beta/16h}
for large
\omega_{ab}\beta
. In the low-temperature regime, we therefore expect coherences between eigenstates
\phi_{a}
and
\phi_{b}
to be small whenever
\omega^{2}_{ab}\beta\gg 1
(assuming
h=\mathcal{O}(1)
), and further suppressed as
e^{-\beta\bar{\epsilon}_{ab}}
in the average of the energy. In particular, we expect to be able to accurately prepare the ground state of
\hat{H}_{S}
in many cases, by tuning
\beta\Delta\gg 1
, where
\Delta
is the many-body gap. Ground state preparation using variants of the cooling protocol has been demonstrated in several recent works
Lloyd et al. 2025
;
Ding et al. 2024
;
Zhan et al. 2025
.

In the opposite regime of high-temperatures, where
\beta\omega_{ab}\ll 1
, we can expand the hyperbolic function in Eq. (
43
), from which
\hat{\zeta}_{ab}=\mathcal{O}(\beta\omega_{ab})
(the case of large frequency should not change the argument according to Eq. (
47
)). Thus, coherences are expected to vanish in the high temperature regime, consistent with previous works studying thermalisation in the case of physical baths
Lee and Yeo 2022
;
Timofeev and Trushechkin 2022
.

V
Discussion

As we have seen in Sections
II
and
IV
, the accuracy of our protocol improves as the magnitude of the system-bath coupling
\theta
is lowered, relative to the energy scales of the system Hamiltonian. This is due inherently to the fact that our protocol is defined in the Schrödinger picture, see Eq. (
2
). In contrast, other proposals for exact
Chen et al. 2023b
;
Gilyén et al. 2024
or approximate
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Hahn et al. 2025
quantum Gibbs samplers have worked primarily at the level of the Lindbladian in the interaction picture i.e. without the term
-i[\hat{H}_{S},\hat{\rho}]
. The advantage of working directly in the interaction picture is that rigorous bounds can be applied
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Hahn et al. 2025
which depend only on the mixing time of the Lindbladian and properties of the correction
\Delta\hat{G}
.

It is easy to see that our perturbation theory fails if one tries to solve directly for the steady state in the interaction picture: since the
\mathcal{O}(1)
term
-i[\hat{H}_{S},\hat{\rho}]
doesn’t enter the Lindblad equation in Eq. (
16
), there is no sense in which
\theta^{2}
can be treated as a small-coupling parameter. The errors in the steady state can only be considered small if the matrix
\Delta\hat{G}
is approximately diagonal, which is untrue for finite values of
\beta
and
h
(we recall that
h
cannot be scaled arbitrarily as it sets the resonance frequency for cooling transitions (
25
), and making it too small will lead to a divergence in the timescales associated with the approach to steady state).

The recently proposed algorithm in Ref.
Hahn et al. 2025
closely mirrors our cooling protocol, but with the presence of an additional ‘rewinding’ step,
\mathcal{Q}^{\text{rew}}=U^{-M_{\text{rew}}}_{S}\mathcal{Q}
(in our notation), with
M_{\text{rew}}=2M_{T}+1
. The point of this extra step is to algorithmically implement the interaction-picture map Eq. (
15
), rather than the Schrödinger-picture map Eq. (
2
). The authors were able to show that the error between the interaction-picture steady state
\tilde{\sigma}
and the Gibbs state (assuming the dominant error comes from the breaking of detailed balance due to
\Delta\hat{G})
scales as
\|\tilde{\sigma}-\sigma_{\beta}\|_{1}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(a\beta\tau_{\text{mix}}N_{B})
, where
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}
denotes an upper bound, neglecting poly-logarithmic factors, and
\tau_{mix}
is the relevant mixing time of the protocol. By dropping the rewinding step, the bound is formally loosened by another power of the mixing time.

Our perturbative results derived in Section
III.3
suggest that, contrary to this bound, the Schrödinger channel should perform
better
in cases where the perturbation theory is valid: although we cannot solve the perturbative equations in the case of full rewinding (
M_{\text{rew.}}=2M_{T}+1
), we can consider a ‘partial’ rewinding with
M_{\text{rew.}}=2M_{T}+1-L
. In this case the perturbative correction for the off-diagonal elements derived in Eq. (
43
) becomes

\displaystyle\hat{\zeta}_{ab}=-\frac{e^{-\beta\bar{\epsilon}_{ab}}}{Z}\frac{2i\sinh(\beta\omega_{ab}/2)e^{-i(T-\delta L)\omega_{ab}}}{[1-e^{i\delta L\omega_{ab}}]}(\Delta\hat{G})_{ab}.

(54)

In the limit of small
\delta L
(approaching full rewinding), we find the correction diverges as
\hat{\zeta}_{ab}\propto(\delta L)^{-1}
. Therefore, while it appears harder to rigorously bound the accuracy of protocols in the Schrödinger picture, protocols defined in the interaction picture miss a physically important effect coming from the action of the system evolution
e^{-iT\hat{H}_{S}}
. It would be desirable to extend and potentially strengthen rigorous bounds as used in
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Hahn et al. 2025
to protocols like the one considered here.

VI
Conclusions

In summary, we have presented an algorithm for preparing Gibbs states of quantum many-body Hamiltonians. Our protocol relies on engineering Lindblad evolution using auxiliary qubits, which
approximately
satisfies the quantum detailed balance condition, thereby driving the system towards the Gibbs state. Compared to recently proposed exact Gibbs samplers
Chen et al. 2023b
;
Ding et al. 2025
;
Gilyén et al. 2024
which require significant resources for implementation on quantum hardware, our algorithm offers a straightforward and efficient path to near-term experiments. Indeed, a closely related algorithm for ground state preparation in correlated systems has been recently demonstrated with a superconducting quantum processor
Mi et al. 2024
.

While this paper focused on establishing a theoretical basis for approximate Gibbs state sampling, a promising future direction is to investigate cooling dynamics and mixing times in specific examples of correlated phases, especially in two dimensions, as well as to evaluate the potential of dissipative-engineering algorithms for quantum chemistry applications in molecular systems. Our results for the 2D Ising model, though limited to modest system sizes, show the potential for thermal state preparation of non-trivial quantum phases and critical points. In this setting, many open questions exist concerning the timescales involved in state preparation and the stability to breaking of detailed balance. So far, rigorous results on mixing times have been limited to high temperature systems
Rouzé et al. 2025
;
Rouzé et al. 2024
;
Bakshi et al. 2024
, or weakly-interacting spin and fermion systems
Tong and Zhan 2024
;
Šmíd et al. 2025
;
Zhan et al. 2025
. Despite the usefulness of these results, it is unlikely that quantum advantage can be found in these settings. Understanding the fate of Gibbs samplers in preparing highly correlated quantum states will require further efforts and insight on a case-by-case basis, as in classical Monte Carlo applications
Levin and Peres 2017
.

A further open question concerns the stability of cooling protocols to the presence of unwanted device noise. A basic estimate for our protocol suggests that that for noise rates
\gamma
exceeding the bound
\gamma\tau\gg 1
, where
\tau
is the relevant timescale for approaching the steady state (we distinguish from the mixing time which represents the worst case timescale), the protocol will fail
Lloyd et al. 2025
. However, this naive estimate does not take into account the stability of many ordered phases (e.g. 2D Ising model) to certain types of noise. A more complete understanding of how weak noise affects the detailed balance relations, and the ensuing stability of quantum state preparation algorithms, is required.

Turning to experiments, one immediate application will be to simulate phases and quantum-critical points of spin models in 2d, such as the quantum Ising model considered above and previously studied experimentally on a digital processor in Ref.
Mi et al. 2024
, as well as XY and Heisenberg models in the analog-digital setting
Andersen et al. 2025
. The latter case may allow for reaching lower temperatures due to reduced effects from environmental noise. Furthermore, exploring realizations of cooling algorithms for many-body Gibbs state preparation in other platforms such as neutral atom arrays
Henriet et al. 2020
;
Bluvstein et al. 2024
and trapped ions
Monroe et al. 2021
;
Foss-Feig et al. 2024
, would be desirable. While early proposals exist for simulating open quantum systems on analog platforms
Diehl et al. 2008
;
Barreiro et al. 2011
, it would be timely to revisit these questions in light of the recent progress in efficient thermal state preparation.

Appendix A
Weak-coupling derivation of interaction-picture map

In this appendix, we provide a step-by-step derivation of the Lindblad form of the interaction-picture map, by keeping leading order terms in the weak-coupling expansion. While the derivation mirrors standard ones in the open systems literature
Lidar 2019
, the derivation for discrete-time processes is uncommon and we reproduce it here for completeness.

The interaction-picture map was defined in Eq. (
15
) in the main text. We expand the unitary
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}
entering the interaction-picture map to second order:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}=\tilde{U}_{\theta,M_{T}}\ldots\tilde{U}_{\theta,0}\ldots\tilde{U}_{\theta,-M_{T}}

\displaystyle=1-i\theta\delta\sum_{\tau=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}\tilde{V}_{\tau}-\frac{\theta^{2}\delta^{2}}{2}\sum_{\tau=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}\tilde{V}_{\tau}^{2}-\theta^{2}\delta^{2}\sum_{\tau={-M_{T}}}^{M_{T}}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}=-M_{T}}^{\tau-1}\tilde{V}_{\tau}\tilde{V}_{\tau^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=1-i\theta\delta\sum_{\tau=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}\tilde{V}_{\tau}-\frac{\theta^{2}\delta^{2}}{2}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}\mathcal{T}(\tilde{V}_{\tau}\tilde{V}_{\tau^{\prime}}).

(55)

In the last line we introduced the discrete-time time-ordering operator
\mathcal{T}
which is defined (along with the anti time-ordering operator
\mathcal{T}^{*}
) by:

\mathcal{T}(O_{\tau}O_{\tau^{\prime}})=\begin{cases}O_{\tau}O_{\tau^{\prime}}&\tau>\tau^{\prime}\\
O_{\tau^{\prime}}O_{\tau}&\tau\leq\tau^{\prime}.\end{cases}\hskip 28.45274pt\mathcal{T}^{*}(O_{\tau}O_{\tau^{\prime}})=\begin{cases}O_{\tau}O_{\tau^{\prime}}&\tau\leq\tau^{\prime}\\
O_{\tau^{\prime}}O_{\tau}&\tau>\tau^{\prime}.\end{cases}

(56)

Substituting into the definition of the interaction-picture map, and observing that the linear term in
\theta
vanishes due to
\text{tr}\big(\tilde{V}_{\tau}\hat{\Phi}\big)=0
, we arrive at the second-order map

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\hat{\rho})\approx\hat{\rho}+\theta^{2}\delta^{2}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}\bigg[\text{tr}_{B}\Big(\tilde{V}_{\tau^{\prime}}[\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\Phi}]\tilde{V}_{\tau}\Big)-\text{tr}_{B}\Big(\mathcal{T}(\tilde{V}_{\tau}\tilde{V}_{\tau^{\prime}})[\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\Phi}]\Big)-\text{tr}_{B}\Big([\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\Phi}]\mathcal{T}^{*}(\tilde{V}_{\tau^{\prime}}\tilde{V}_{\tau})\Big)\bigg].

(57)

We now use the bath correlation function

\text{tr}_{B}(\tilde{Y}_{\mu,\tau}\tilde{Y}_{\mu^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}}\hat{\Phi})=\delta_{\mu,\mu^{\prime}}e^{ih\delta(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}

(58)

and the definition of
V_{\tau}
(Eq.
7
) to write

\displaystyle\text{tr}_{B}(\tilde{V}_{\tau}\tilde{V}_{\tau^{\prime}}\hat{\Phi})=f_{\tau}f_{\tau^{\prime}}e^{ih\delta(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}\sum_{\mu=1}^{N_{B}}\tilde{A}_{\mu,\tau}\tilde{A}_{\mu,\tau^{\prime}}.

(59)

This equation takes a more familiar form after introducing the jump operators

\hat{L}_{\mu}=\delta\sum_{\tau=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}f_{\tau}e^{ih\delta\tau}\tilde{A}_{\mu,\tau}.

(60)

The first term entering the square brackets in Eq. (
57
) then becomes
\sum_{\mu}\hat{L}_{\mu}\hat{\rho}L_{\mu}
. The time-ordered term can be decomposed into Hermitian and anti-Hermitian parts with the Hermitian matrices
\hat{K}
and
\hat{G}^{\text{LS}}
:

\hat{J}=\delta^{2}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}\text{tr}_{B}\Big(\mathcal{T}(\tilde{V}_{\tau}\tilde{V}_{\tau^{\prime}})\hat{\Phi}\Big)\equiv\hat{K}+i\hat{G}^{\text{LS}}.

(61)

The new matrix
\hat{K}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{K}=\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\sum_{\mu}\bigg(\sum_{\tau=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}f_{\tau}^{2}(\tilde{A}_{\tau,\mu})^{2}+\sum_{\tau=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}={-M_{T}}}^{\tau-1}\Big(e^{ih\delta(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}f_{\tau}f_{\tau^{\prime}}\tilde{A}_{\tau,\mu}\tilde{A}_{\tau^{\prime},\mu}+e^{ih\delta(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}f_{\tau}f_{\tau^{\prime}}\tilde{A}_{\tau^{\prime},\mu}\tilde{A}_{\tau,\mu}\Big)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\sum_{\mu}\bigg(\sum_{\tau=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}f_{\tau}^{2}(\tilde{A}_{\tau,\mu})^{2}+\bigg(\sum_{\tau=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}={-M_{T}}}^{\tau-1}+\sum_{\tau=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}={\tau}+1}^{M_{T}}\bigg)e^{ih\delta(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}f_{\tau}f_{\tau^{\prime}}\tilde{A}_{\tau,\mu}\tilde{A}_{\tau^{\prime},\mu}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\sum_{\mu}\sum_{\tau^{\prime},\tau=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}e^{ih\delta(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}f_{\tau}f_{\tau^{\prime}}\tilde{A}_{\tau,\mu}\tilde{A}_{\tau^{\prime},\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu}\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{\mu}\hat{L}_{\mu}.

(62)

In going from the first to the second line, we performed a change of variable
\tau\leftrightarrow\tau^{\prime}
, and used
\sum_{\tau}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}<\tau}=\sum_{\tau^{\prime}}\sum_{\tau>\tau^{\prime}}
.

The matrix
\hat{G}^{\text{LS}}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{G}^{\text{LS}}=\frac{\delta^{2}}{2i}\sum_{\mu}\sum_{\tau=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}={-M_{T}}}^{\tau-1}\Big(e^{ih\delta(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}f_{\tau}f_{\tau^{\prime}}\tilde{A}_{\tau,\mu}\tilde{A}_{\tau^{\prime},\mu}-e^{ih\delta(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}f_{\tau}f_{\tau^{\prime}}\tilde{A}_{\tau^{\prime},\mu}\tilde{A}_{\tau,\mu}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{2}}{2i}\bigg(\sum_{\tau=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}={-M_{T}}}^{\tau-1}-\sum_{\tau=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}={\tau}+1}^{M_{T}}\bigg)e^{ih\delta(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}f_{\tau}f_{\tau^{\prime}}\tilde{A}_{\tau,\mu}\tilde{A}_{\tau^{\prime},\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{2}}{2i}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}\text{sgn}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})e^{ih\delta(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}f_{\tau}f_{\tau^{\prime}}\tilde{A}_{\tau,\mu}\tilde{A}_{\tau^{\prime},\mu},

(63)

where the sign function is defined with
\text{sgn}(0)=0
. The anti-time-ordered term is given by

\hat{J}^{\dagger}=\delta^{2}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}\text{tr}_{B}\Big(\mathcal{T}^{*}(\tilde{V}_{\tau}\tilde{V}_{\tau^{\prime}})\hat{\Phi}\Big)\equiv\hat{K}-i\hat{G}^{\text{LS}}.

(64)

Combining Eqs. (
60
,
62
,
63
) we arrive at the Lindblad form given in the main text, Eq. (
16
):

\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\hat{\rho})-\hat{\rho}}{\theta^{2}}=-i[\hat{G}^{\text{LS}},\hat{\rho}]+\{\hat{K},\hat{\rho}\}+\sum_{\mu}\hat{L}_{\mu}\hat{\rho}\hat{L}_{\mu}^{\dagger}.

(65)

It is interesting to note that our Lindblad equation has the same form as the so-called ‘Universal Lindblad Equation’, derived in an attempt to restore complete positivity to the Redfield equation
Nathan and Rudner 2020
. In that case, the ‘filter function’ in the frequency domain enters as the square root of the bath spectral function,
\bar{f}(\omega)=\sqrt{J(\omega)}
. We leave a detailed investigation of the equivalence between the two to future work.

Appendix B
Approximations in replacing jump operators with operator Fourier transforms

The jump operators in Eq. (
60
) were derived for the digital channel with a finite reset time
M_{T}
. For analytical purposes it is convenient to replace this expression with the continuous-time operator Fourier transform,

\hat{L}_{\mu}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\ f(t)e^{iht}\tilde{A}_{\mu}(t),

(66)

and similarly for the Lamb-shift

\hat{G}^{\text{LS}}=\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{\mu}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}dtdt^{\prime}\ \text{sgn}(t-t^{\prime})f(t)f(t^{\prime})e^{ih(t^{\prime}-t)}\hat{A}^{\dagger}_{\mu}(t)\hat{A}_{\mu}(t^{\prime}).

(67)

The error in this substitution is controlled by the smallness of the two parameters,
\xi_{T}=1/aT
,
\xi_{\delta}=a\delta
, as we show here. Let

\hat{L}(M_{T},\delta)=\delta\sum_{\tau=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}}f_{\tau}e^{ih\delta\tau}\tilde{A}_{\tau}.

(68)

First we show the bound

\|\hat{L}(M_{T},\delta)-\hat{L}(\infty,\delta)\|\leq 2(aN)^{-1}\|\hat{A}\|\frac{e^{-T^{2}a^{2}/2}}{aT},

(69)

with
N
the filter function normalisation (
N\sim a^{-1}
):

\displaystyle\|\hat{L}(M_{T},\delta)-\hat{L}(\infty,\delta)\|=\Big\|\delta\sum_{\tau={M_{T}+1}}^{\infty}(f_{\tau}e^{ih\delta\tau}\tilde{A}_{\tau}+f_{-\tau}e^{-ih\delta\tau}\tilde{A}_{-\tau})\Big\|

\displaystyle\leq 2N^{-1}\|\hat{A}\|\Big\|\delta\sum_{\tau=M_{T}+1}^{\infty}e^{-\delta^{2}a^{2}\tau^{2}/2}\Big\|

\displaystyle\leq 2N^{-1}\|\hat{A}\|\int_{T}^{\infty}dt\ e^{-a^{2}t^{2}/2}=\sqrt{2\pi}(aN)^{-1}\|\hat{A}\|\text{erfc}\bigg(\frac{aT}{\sqrt{2}}\bigg),

(70)

where
T=\delta M_{T}
,
erfc(x)
is the complementary error function, and we used the fact that the Gaussian is monotonically decreasing for
\tau>0
, hence the sum can be bounded by the integral. The error function satisfies the known bound

\text{erfc}(x)\leq\frac{e^{-x^{2}}}{x\sqrt{\pi}},

(71)

leading to

\|\hat{L}(M_{T},\delta)-\hat{L}(\infty,\delta)\|\leq 2(aN)^{-1}\|\hat{A}\|\frac{e^{-T^{2}a^{2}/2}}{aT}.

(72)

Next, we consider the frequency domain decomposition of
\hat{L}
,

\hat{L}(\infty,\delta)=\delta\sum_{\tau=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{\tau}e^{ih\delta\tau}\tilde{A}_{\tau}=\sum_{\omega}\hat{A}_{\omega}\bigg(\delta\sum_{\tau=\infty}^{\infty}f_{\tau}e^{i(h-\omega)\delta\tau}\bigg).

(73)

Using the Poisson summation formula
Ding et al. 2024
we can replace the infinite time sum with a sum over shifted copies of the continuous-time Fourier transform of the filter function:

\delta\sum_{\tau=\infty}^{\infty}f_{\tau}e^{-i\omega\delta\tau}=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\bar{f}(\omega-2\pi k/\delta),

(74)

where
\bar{f}(\omega)=e^{-\omega^{2}/2a^{2}}
. Restricting to the range
\omega\delta\ll\pi
, which defines the prethermal regime we work in, the error from keeping only the
k=0
term in the above sum is of the order
\mathcal{O}(e^{-\pi^{2}/2\xi_{\delta}^{2}})
. Combining the two approximations above, we can replace
\hat{L}(M_{T},\delta)
with
\hat{L}(\infty,0)
(Eq. (
66
)).
A similar analysis may be performed for the Lamb-shift term. This gives the final integral-form operators appearing in Eqs. (
19
,
20
), and the error involved in these replacements in exponentially small in the parameters
\xi_{T}
and
\xi_{\delta}
.

Appendix C
Explicit form for the Lamb-shifts

Here we derive an explicit formula for the Lamb-shift Hamiltonian,
\hat{G}^{\text{LS}}
, which can be expressed in terms of the error-function, and compare to the detailed balance function
\hat{G}^{\text{DB}}
.

To simplify notation we consider a single auxiliary spin,
\hat{A}_{\mu}\equiv\hat{A}
, and work with the continuous time formula given in Eq. (
67
). We first decompose
\hat{A}=\sum_{\omega}\hat{A}_{\omega}
in terms of cooling transitions of frequency
\omega
, such that
\hat{A}(t)=\sum_{\omega}\hat{A}_{\omega}e^{-i\omega t}
. The Lamb-shift is

\hat{G}^{\text{LS}}=\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\hat{A}^{\dagger}_{\omega}\hat{A}_{\omega^{\prime}}K^{\text{LS}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime}),

(75)

with the kernel

\displaystyle K^{\text{LS}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2i}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}dtdt^{\prime}\ \text{sgn}(t-t^{\prime})e^{i[(\omega-h)t-(\omega^{\prime}-h)t^{\prime}]}f(t)f(t^{\prime}).

(76)

Using the Fourier transform of
\text{sgn}(t-t^{\prime})
, we can write this as a standard principal value integral

\displaystyle K^{\text{LS}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\mathcal{P}\int\frac{d\nu}{2\pi\nu}\bar{f}_{h}(\omega-\nu)\bar{f}_{h}(\omega^{\prime}-\nu).

(77)

For our choice of Gaussian filter function,
\bar{f}
restricts
\omega
to the range
\omega\approx h+\nu\pm\mathcal{O}(a)
within the integral; in turn, this restricts the kernel to be finite only when
|\omega-\omega^{\prime}|\lesssim\mathcal{O}(a)
. To see this explicitly, we may factor

\displaystyle K^{\text{LS}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\frac{d\nu}{\nu-h}e^{-(\omega-\nu)^{2}/2a^{2}}e^{-(\omega^{\prime}-\nu)^{2}/2a^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-(\omega-\omega^{\prime})^{2}/4a^{2}}}{2\pi}\int\frac{d\nu}{\nu+\bar{\omega}-h}e^{-\nu^{2}/a^{2}},

(78)

where we defined
\bar{\omega}=\frac{\omega+\omega^{\prime}}{2}
. The latter integral is the Hilbert transform of a Gaussian, which has a known solution:

K^{\text{LS}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-(\omega-\omega^{\prime})^{2}/4a^{2}}F\bigg(\frac{\bar{\omega}-h}{a}\bigg).

(79)

The function

F(x)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2i}e^{-x^{2}}\text{erf}(ix),

(80)

is a real-valued antisymmetric function known as Dawson’s function, and erf is the error function. The main points to note are that
F(x)\approx\frac{1}{2x}
for large
x
, and
F(x)\approx x
for small
x
. In total, Eq. (
79
) shows that the Lamb-shift kernel is a real-valued function which is symmetric in the argument
\omega-\omega^{\prime}
, and suppressed as a Gaussian of width
2a
for large
|\omega-\omega^{\prime}|
.

This can be compared to the kernel of the Hamiltonian required for exact detailed balance, derived in Eq. (
33
),

\displaystyle K^{\text{DB}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=i\tanh\bigg(\frac{\beta(\omega-\omega')}{4}\bigg)e^{-(\omega-\omega^{\prime})^{2}/4a^{2}}e^{-(\bar{\omega}-h)^{2}/a^{2}}.

(81)

In contrast to (
79
),
K^{\text{LS}}
is an imaginary function, antisymmetric in
\omega-\omega^{\prime}
, but with the same Gaussian decay for large values of the argument
\omega-\omega^{\prime}
. The difference between the Lamb-shifts,
\Delta\hat{G}
, can be written as

\Delta\hat{G}=\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\hat{A}^{\dagger}_{\omega}\hat{A}_{\omega^{\prime}}\Delta K(\omega,\omega^{\prime}),

(82)

where

\Delta K(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=2e^{-(\omega-\omega^{\prime})^{2}/4a^{2}}y(\omega,\omega^{\prime}),

(83)

and

y(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}F\bigg(\frac{\bar{\omega}-h}{a}\bigg)-\frac{i}{2}\tanh\bigg(\frac{\beta(\omega-\omega')}{4}\bigg)e^{-(\bar{\omega}-h)^{2}/a^{2}}

(84)

is a function satisfying
|y(\omega,\omega^{\prime})|\leq 1
. Although the matrix element
\Delta\hat{G}_{ab}
involves an internal summation over intermediate states which is difficult to bound, we expect the behaviour at large
\omega_{ab}
is dominated by the Gaussian suppression in Eq. (
83
).

Appendix D
Hamiltonian renormalisation

The corrections to the Gibbs state derived in Section
III.3
were derived using standard perturbation theory around the Gibbs state in the Schrödinger picture. In this appendix, we show how the same formulas can be obtained through an alternative method, guided by the following intuition: on physical grounds, we expect that the presence of the bath modifies (‘renormalises’) the bare system Hamiltonian (leading to e.g. level shift phenomena known from atomic theory
Cohen-Tannoudji et al. 2024
), hence there is no reason to expect exact thermalization into the Gibbs state set by
\hat{H}_{S}

Trushechkin et al. 2022
. While detailed balance is therefore broken in the interaction picture with respect to the
bare
Hamiltonian, it may be that it is restored in the interaction frame with respect to a
renormalised
Hamiltonian,
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{S}
. We show below that this intuition is correct, and that the renormalised system Hamiltonian can be solved for self-consistently at second order in the coupling
\theta
.

We define the renormalised Hamiltonian with the correction expanded in powers of the system-bath coupling

\hat{H}^{\prime}_{S}=\hat{H}_{S}+\theta^{2}\hat{C},\hskip 28.45274pt\hat{C}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\theta^{2k}\hat{C}_{k},

(85)

where
\hat{C}
is an operator acting only on the system Hilbert space. We assume throughout that the Trotter angle is small such that

e^{-i\delta\theta^{2}\hat{C}}e^{-i\delta\hat{H}_{S}}\approx e^{-i\delta\hat{H}^{\prime}_{S}}\equiv U_{S}^{\prime}.

(86)

Then, by making use of the identities

\mathcal{U_{\tau}}=e^{-i\delta\theta\hat{V}_{\tau}}e^{i\delta\theta^{2}\hat{C}}e^{-i\delta\hat{H}_{B}}e^{-i\delta\theta^{2}\hat{C}}e^{-i\delta\hat{H}_{S}}=e^{-i\delta\theta\hat{V}_{\tau}}e^{i\delta\theta^{2}\hat{C}}U_{B}U^{\prime}_{S},

(87)

\mathcal{R}=\prod_{\tau=1}^{M_{R}}e^{-i\delta\tau\hat{H}_{S}}=\prod_{\tau=1}^{M_{R}}e^{i\delta\theta^{2}\hat{C}}U^{\prime}_{S}

(88)

we define the interaction picture with respect to the renormalised Hamiltonian as

\breve{O}_{\tau}=Y_{0}^{-\tau}(O_{\tau})Y_{0}^{\tau},,\hskip 14.22636ptY_{0}=U_{B}U^{\prime}_{S},

(89)

and
\breve{\mathcal{Q}}=U^{\prime-M_{R}}_{S}Y_{0}^{-M_{T}}\mathcal{Q}Y_{0}^{-(M_{T}+1)}
. The Schrödinger map is then written in the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})=\mathcal{S^{\prime}}^{M_{T}+M_{R}}\circ\breve{\mathcal{E}}\circ\mathcal{S^{\prime}}^{M_{T}+1}(\hat{\rho}),

(90)

\mathcal{S^{\prime}}(\hat{\rho})=U^{\prime}_{S}(\hat{\rho})U^{\prime-1}_{S},\hskip 28.45274pt\breve{\mathcal{E}}(\rho)=\text{tr}_{B}\big[{\breve{Q}}(\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\Phi})\breve{Q}^{\dagger}\big].

(91)

The weak-coupling derivation follows the same steps as laid out in Appendix
A
. The only difference at leading order in
\theta
is the appearance of an additional coherent term:

\frac{\breve{\mathcal{E}}(\hat{\rho})-\hat{\rho}}{\theta^{2}}=-i[\hat{G}^{\text{LS}}-\hat{G}^{C},\hat{\rho}]+\{\hat{K},\hat{\rho}\}+\sum_{\mu}\hat{L}_{\mu}\hat{\rho}\hat{L}_{\mu}^{\dagger},

(92)

where

\hat{G}^{C}=\delta\sum_{\tau=-M_{T}}^{M_{T}+M_{R}}\breve{C}_{0,\tau}\approx\int_{-T}^{T+T_{R}}dt\ \breve{C}_{0}(t).

(93)

We next seek the frame in which the quantum detailed balance equations are satisfied. Denote by
\phi_{a}^{\prime}
the basis of eigenstates of
H^{\prime}_{S}
. The filter function guarantees that Eq. (
24
) holds with the replacement
\phi_{a}\to\phi^{\prime}_{a}
, and hence the dissipative part of Eq. (
92
) still satisfies QDB. To satisfy QDB for the coherent terms, we require that

\hat{G}^{\text{LS}}-\hat{G}^{\text{DB}}-\hat{G}^{C}+\hat{\Lambda}=0,

(94)

where
\hat{\Lambda}
is a term diagonal in the eigenbasis of
\hat{H}_{S}^{\prime}
. If we can find
\hat{C}
such that the above condition is satisfied, then the steady state of the channel
\breve{\mathcal{E}}
is given by the QDB solution

\hat{\sigma}^{\prime}_{\beta}\propto e^{-\beta\hat{H}^{\prime}_{S}}.

(95)

Returning to the Schrödinger picture,
Y_{0}
commutes with
\hat{\sigma}^{\prime}_{\beta}
, and therefore
\hat{\sigma}^{\prime}_{\beta}
is also the
exact
steady state for the cooling channel,
\mathcal{E}(\hat{\sigma}^{\prime}_{\beta})=\hat{\sigma}^{\prime}_{\beta}
.

The solution which satisifes QDB is simply found in the basis of eigenstates of
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{S}
. Ignoring for a moment the randomization step and setting
T_{R}=0
, we have

(\hat{C}_{0})_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}=\frac{(\Delta\hat{G})_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}}{\int_{-T}^{T}dt\ e^{-i\omega^{\prime}_{ab}t}}+\delta_{ab}\frac{\Lambda_{a}}{2T}=\frac{(\Delta\hat{G})_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}}{2T\text{sinc}(\omega^{\prime}_{ab}T)}+\delta_{ab}\frac{\Lambda_{a}}{2T},

(96)

where the primes remind us that we are working in the eigenbasis of
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{S}
. Returning to the eigenbasis of
\hat{H}_{S}
requires a non-trivial perturbation expansion. However, since we only require
\hat{C}_{0}
to leading order, we can substitute the original eigenbasis in place above
\phi^{\prime}_{a}\to\phi_{a}
i.e. dropping the primes (assuming that the perturbation
\hat{C}
did not lead to degeneracy splitting).

The above expression is problematic due to the resonances at
\omega_{ab}T=k\pi
, as we observed in Section
III.3
. To include the randomization step, we should consider the channel averaged over the random time
T_{R}
. Note that now both the interaction-picture map and the Schrödinger-picture map depend on
T_{R}
. The correct average is performed by fixing

\mathbb{E}\ \int_{-T}^{T+T_{R}}dt\ (U^{\prime}_{S})^{T_{R}}\breve{C}_{0}(t)(U_{S}^{\prime-1})^{T_{R}}=\mathbb{E}\ (U^{\prime}_{S})^{T_{R}}\Delta\hat{G}(U_{S}^{\prime-1})^{T_{R}},

(97)

which after a little algebra gives

(\hat{C}_{0})_{ab}=\frac{i\omega_{ab}e^{i\omega_{ab}T}}{1-i\omega_{ab}T_{0}-e^{2i\omega_{ab}T}}(\Delta\hat{G})_{ab}+\delta_{ab}\frac{\Lambda_{a}}{2T+T_{0}}.

(98)

\frac{\hat{\Lambda}}{2T+T_{0}}\to\hat{\Lambda}
, we find the same correction as we derived in Eq. (
46
) from the Kubo formula. It is unfortunate that the diagonal correction
\hat{\Lambda}
remains undefined in this scheme.
