Exposure to zinc oxide nanoparticles inhibits preimplantation embryonic development by disrupting zygotic genome activation
Authors: Xuemei Liu, Chenshi Li, Sicheng Zhang, Xiaoping Zhang, Ling Zhu, Bei Liu, Yanhua Zhao, Jiayu Huang, Jingyu Li, Shimeng Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The potential adverse effects of zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONPs) on human reproductive health may arise from their increasing industrial and commercial applications. However, their effects on preimplantation embryonic development and the related molecular mechanisms are still not well understood. Here, we demonstrate that ZnONPs exposure exhibit toxicity to a critical developmental period in mice. We observed that sustained exposure to ZnONPs in vitro resulted in embryonic development arrest at the 2-cell stage. To identify the susceptible stage, we controlled experiments to treat embryos with ZnONPs in the different processes of early embryonic development and determined that ZnONPs mainly to affect 2-cell stage embryos. According to the RNA-seq and EU (5-ethynyl uridine) analysis, the transcriptional activity of minor ZGA genes increased in the late 2-cell embryos following ZnONPs exposure. Subsequently, we employed multi-omics assays, including CUT&Tag and ATAC-seq. We found that ZnONPs exposure led to increased enrichment of H3K27ac (Histone H3 acetylated lysine 27) in late 2-cell embryos and enhanced chromatin accessibility, which led to abnormal upregulation of minor zygotic genome activation (ZGA) genes. In addition, the direct occupancy of ZnONPs at H3K27ac modification sites was verified through pulldown and immunoprecipitation. In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that ZnONPs exposure disrupting minor ZGA by interfering with H3K27ac erasure on the embryonic genome and ultimately impairing the developmental potential of embryos.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10565-025-10127-2.

Introduction

The expanding utilization of ZnONPs in industrial and commercial has raised concerns about their potential adverse effects on human reproductive health. ZnONPs have been reported to induce toxicity in the female reproductive system and early embryonic development (Xu et al. 2022; Zhai et al. 2018). Our recent study demonstrated that low concentrations of ZnONPs (0.1 μg/mL) disrupt impairs embryonic development by suppressing the expression of pluripotent genes in blastocysts (Liu et al. 2024). Notably, embryonic developmental arrest becomes more severe as ZnONPs concentration rise (Liu et al. 2024), which also far below the toxic levels for other researches (Zhao et al. 2013; Huang et al. 2022; Kteeba et al. 2017). However, the impact of ZnONPs on earlier embryonic stages remains not well understood.

The activation of ZGA refers to the onset of embryonic transcription at specific developmental stages. It initiates the maternal-to-zygotic transition, which is essential for embryonic development. In mice, ZGA proceeds through two key phases: a minor wave in zygotes and a major wave at the 2-cell stage. Both phases are fundamental to embryogenesis (Jukam et al. 2017; Schulz et al. 2019). Histone modifications are crucial in orchestrating ZGA (Xu et al. 2021, 2022). After fertilization, H3K27 acetylation decreases in early 2‐cell embryos (minor ZGA) and is reestablished at the late 2-cell stage (major ZGA) (Wang et al. 2022). Failure to erase H3K27ac in embryos can result in sustained activation of RNA polymerase II and prolonged expression of minor ZGA genes. Therefore, it disrupts the timely transition to major ZGA and leading to developmental arrest (Li et al. 2022). Precise temporal regulation these histone modifications is vital for normal preimplantation embryos development.

Emerging evidence indicates that nanoparticles can directly influence epigenetic modifications. For instance, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) reduced histone methylation patterns in erythroid cells (Qian et al. 2015), while titanium dioxide nanoparticles (TiO2NPs) affect global DNA methylation in human cell lines (Pogribna et al. 2020). Our previous study demonstrated that ZnONPs increase H3K9me3 in the inner cell mass (ICM) of blastocysts (Liu et al. 2024). Except for methylation modification, nanoparticles exhibit affinity for core histones and can modulate acetylation states, potentially leading to chromatin remodeling (Conroy et al. 2008; Zhao et al. 2025). Another study verified that arsenite exposure disturbs ZGA by attenuating H3K27ac enrichment in mouse preimplantation embryos (Yu et al. 2023). Nevertheless, whether ZnONPs affect other histone modification that interfere with embryonic development at earlier stage awaits further exploration.

In this study, we further explored ZnONPs-induced toxicity in preimplantation embryos. Using multi-omics techniques, we show that ZnONPs participate in the dynamic changes of H3K27ac enrichment in 2-cell embryos, which resulted in the abnormal activation of minor ZGA genes expression. Our findings provide mechanistic insights into ZnONPs reproductive toxicity and highlight the importance of epigenetic regulation in early embryogenesis.

Materials and methods

Mice and ethics statement

The relevant ethical guidelines were strictly followed in the performance of all experiments. All experiments involving animals were conducted following approval (No. 2023037) from the Chongqing Health Center for Women and Children's IRB. Female and male, 6–8 weeks old ICR strain mice were used for the experiments (Beijing SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd).

Embryo collection and culture

Unless otherwise stated, all reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Embryo collection commenced with superovulation induction in ICR females via i.p. injection of 6.5 IU PMSG (Sansheng Biological, China). After 46–48 h, 5.5 IU hCG (China) was administered, followed by mating with ICR males. Oviductal cumulus oocyte complexes (COCs) collection was performed 17 h after hCG treatment utilizing M2 medium (M7167). Hyaluronidase (37,326,333) was employed to dissociate granule cells prior to embryo culture in KSOM medium (MR-121-D) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and humidity. Embryos were placed in droplets of KSOM under oil for culture. Each dish (Thermo, 150,460) contained a maximum of nine 30 μL droplets, and each droplet accommodated no more than 30 embryos. For ZnONPs treatment at different developmental stages, specific time points after hCG injection were selected. Specifically, embryo transfer was performed at the zygote stage (24 h), mid-2-cell stage (40 h), and late 2-cell stage (48 h). During transfer, embryos were rinsed through three sequential washing droplets to replace the original culture medium. Notably, collection of embryos was primarily focused on the early (30 h) and late 2-cell stage.

Culturing mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs)

The mESCs were maintained on mitotically inactivated mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) under standard conditions of 37 °C, 5% CO₂, and high humidity. The culture medium consisted of KnockOut™ DMEM (Gibco, 10,829,018) supplemented with the following supplements: 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS; FSP500, ExCell), 1 × KnockOut™ Serum Replacement (10,828,028, Gibco), 10 ng/mL mouse leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF, PMC9484, Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (15,140,122, Gibco), 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (21,985–023, Gibco), 1 × L-glutamine (25,030,081, Gibco) and 1 × MEM Non-essential Amino Acids Solution (11,140,050, Gibco). The medium was replaced daily.

ZnONPs and nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) treatment

For consistent nanoparticle distribution, the ZnONP powder, purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (677,450, CAS-No: 1314–13-2) were vortex-mixed in double-distilled Water (ddH₂O) to 3 mg/mL, followed by 20 min pulsed sonication (0.9 s ON: 0.9 s OFF) at 100 W using a sonicator (science-IID, China). Subsequently, ZnONPs were diluted to 0.3 μg/mL using KSOM medium. For inhibitor treatment, NMN (Sigma, N3501, USA) was dissolved in ddH2O and further diluted to 50 μM in the culture medium. The selection of concentration in 50 μM NMN was based on previous studies and its efficacy in promoting deacetylation in early embryos without inducing toxicity (Li et al. 2022). Embryos were subsequently cultured in medium containing ZnONPs with or without NMN for further experiments.

Pull-down assay

The mESCs were cultured to a density of 90% in 10 cm cell-culture dish and subsequently lysed with RIPA (Solarbio, R0010) supplemented with 1 × PMSF (Solarbio, FJP0100, China). To ensure complete lysis, the cells were lysed for a total of 30 min on ice with vortexing (30 s for every 10 min). Following lysis, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to eliminate cellular debris. For pull-down assay, the lysate was incubated with or without 20 μg/mL of ZnONPs (30 °C, 2 h). Subsequently, the mixture underwent centrifugation (17,000 × g, 4 °C, 60 min) to pellet the ZnONPs and their bound proteins. The supernatant was carefully discarded. Pellets were washed with PBS (Gibco, C10010500BT) and was resuspended in 10 μL 1 × loading buffer, which was prepared by diluting a commercial 5 × protein loading buffer with deionized water. Finally, the resuspended proteins underwent 5-min denaturation at 100 °C. For western blotting, denatured proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE using 12% separating and 5% stacking gels in 1 × Tris–Glycine Running Buffer (Solarbio, T1070). Electrophoresis was conducted at 80 V for a duration of 20 min, utilizing the stacking gel, followed by a 120 V application the separating gel. The resolved proteins were then transferred electrophoretically onto PVDF (Merck Millipore, ISEQ010) membranes at 300 mA. Then, the membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20; biosharp, BL315B) for 1 h at room temperature. Following blocking, it was trimmed to the appropriate molecular weight range based on the protein marker (PageRuler Prestained Protein Ladder, 26,616), then incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-H3K27ac primary antibody (Active Motif, 39,133) diluted 1:1000 in 5% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, 9,048,468) prepared in TBST. The next day, membranes were washed thoroughly with TBST, then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the HRP-conjugated Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 31,460), 1:1000 diluted in 5% non-fat milk in TBST. After a final wash step, immunoblot signals were detected via a luminol-based enhanced chemiluminescence (New Cell & Molecular Biotech Co., Ltd, #P10300A) and imaged with Imaging System (Bio-Rad Gel Doc XR, Hercules, CA).

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy

Firstly, embryos were fixed in 100 μL 4% paraformaldehyde buffer (FB002; Invitrogen, USA) for 0.5 h, then permeabilized using 100 μL 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 0.5 h. Unspecific reactions were blocked by incubating the embryos with a blocking solution supplemented with 1% of BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, 9,048,468, St. Louis, MO). Subsequently, embryos were incubated with antibody against H3K27ac (Rabbit, monoclonal, 39,133, Active Motif, 1:1000) at 4 °C overnight. Embryos were washed every 5 min for 3 times with PBS-PVA (0.01% Triton X-100 and 0.1% Tween-20). Finally, embryos were stained with an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher, A-11008, 1:500) for 1 h and counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (Beyotime, C1022, Jiangsu, China) at a dilution of 1:1000 for 15 min.

EU (5-ethynyl uridine) staining was according to the protocol provided with the Click-iT® RNA Imaging Kits (Invitrogen, C10329). In brief, embryos were maintained for 2 h in medium containing EU. Then, the embryos were fixed with 100 μL 4% paraformaldehyde buffer, permeabilize with 100 μL 0.5% Triton X-100 and washed embryos with Click-iT® reaction rinse buffer. Embryos were stained with Hoechst 33,342 (1:1000) to visualize nuclei.

Finally, embryos were positioned on glass slides after being gently flattened with a coverslip, they were observed under a fluorescence microscopy (TCS SP8, Germany). The relative fluorescence expression value was defined by the TCS SP8 software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Immunoprecipitation and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) analysis

The 1 × 104 mESCs were seeded in the 6-well plates. After 24 h, ZnONPs at the indicated concentrations were added to the culture medium. Following a 48-h incubation, cells were collected for co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays by using a commercial kit (Protein A/G Magnetic IP/Co-IP Kit, Vazyme, PB201, China). Briefly, cells were lysed and the supernatant was collected. Subsequently, 40 μL pre-washed Protein A/G beads together, 5 μg of anti-H3K27ac antibody and lysate were incubated at 4 °C overnight. For the negative control, normal IgG was used instead of the specific antibody. Then, the beads were collected and the supernatant was discarded. Finally, the eluate was divided into two equal volumes: one portion was used for Western blot (WB), and the other was subjected to ICP-MS analysis for zinc detection with the PerkinElmer NexIONTM 300X ICP-MS (Norwalk, CT).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

After trypsinization, 1 × 107 mESCs with control groups and ZnONPs-treated groups were collected. Subsequently, fixed cells with 4% glutaraldehyde for 10 min, and then transferred to 4 °C for storage. Then the cell was prepared to Chengdu Lilai Biology Co., Ltd. for TEM (JEOL JEM-1400 Flash). In brief, after fixed in 2% osmium tetroxide solution (2 h, 4 °C). The mESCs were subjected to gradient dehydration using ethanol, with each gradient lasting 15 min. The mESCs were then infiltrated with Epon 618 resin. Followed by embedding and sectioning, the samples were stained with 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate for 10 min each. Finally, using the transmission electron microscope (TEM) to observe the samples.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

In accordance with the previously reported methods for preparing RNA sequencing libraries (Li et al. 2022; Picelli et al. 2014), the procedure was as follows: Two biological replicates were performed. For each replicate, three early 2-cell embryos of control group and three late 2-cell embryos of control and ③ groups were used per library preparation reaction. To avoid potential contamination, all embryos were treated with 3% hydrochloric acid to remove the zona pellucida. After being washed three times with 0.1% PBS-PVA, the embryos were transferred to a lysis buffer containing RNase inhibitors. Then, full-length cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription system which contained Super Script III (Invitrogen, cat. no. 18080044), T4 gene 32 protein (Roche, cat. no. 10972991001) and Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) (Invitrogen, 10,533,073). The resulting cDNA was amplified via PCR for 20 cycles using Phusion HS II High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (F-565S; Thermo Fisher) and an oligo (dT) primer. Furthermore, the amplified cDNA was purified using VAHTS® DNA Clean Beads, (Vazyme, N411-02). Sequencing libraries were then constructed from the purified cDNA using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, E7805S), following the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, all libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

CUT&Tag

The Commercial Kit of TD901-02 (Vazyme Biotech, China) was employed to conduct the CUT&Tag procedure. In brief, a total of 150 early 2-cell embryos of control group and 150 late 2-cell embryos of control and groups ③ embryos were washed with Dig-wash Buffer, which prepared with 10 × Wash Buffer, 5% digitonin, PI (Sigma-Aldrich, 5,056,489,001) and ddH2O, and incubated with anti-H3K27ac antibody (1:50) in 96-well plates at 4 °C overnight. After incubated with the secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher, A-11008, 1:50) for 1 h, the samples were supplemented with 1 μL of pA/G-Tn5 in 49 μL of dig-300 buffer for 1 h. The samples were fragmentated by 1 × TTBL mixture (37 °C, 1 h). For DNA, to halt the fragmentation process, the buffer 0.5 M EDTA (Beyotime, ST066, China), 10% SDS (Servicebio, G2055) and proteinase K were added to the reaction mixture, then vortexed and incubated at 50 °C for 1 h. Following this, 50 µL of DNA extraction buffer (pre-cooled at 4 °C) was added, vortexed and subsequently centrifuged at 13,000 rpm, 10 min. The DNA was extracted by using a glycogen-assisted method and precipitated with isopropanol. The following reaction system was employed for amplification after product purification: an initial denaturation at 72 °C for 3 min, 95 °C for 3 min, and then 98 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s in 20 cycles, 72 °C for 5 min as final. DNA was purified using the VAHT (Vazyme Biotech, N411). Libraries were bound to beads (RT, 5 min), washed with 80% ethanol, and eluted in 20 μL nuclease-free water. Subsequently, sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

ATAC-seq

The Commercial Kit of TD711 (Vazyme Biotech, China) was utilized to conduct ATAC-seq, according to the established protocol and the manufacturer’s guidelines. For the preparation of nuclei, 100 late 2-cell embryos of control and groups ③ were treated with 50 μL of lysis buffer (RS buffer supplemented with 10% NP-40, 10% Tween 20, 1% digitionin) for 10 min on ice. The samples were fragmentated by mixing for 1 h (37 °C). DNA was subsequently extracted using a glycogen-assisted method and precipitated with an equal volume of isopropanol. Following PCR conditions to library amplification: 72 °C for 3 min, 95 °C for 30 min and then round 98 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 5 s in 20 cycles, 72 °C for 1 min. Subsequently, all libraries were performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

RNA-seq data processing

Using the parameters "fastp -M 25 -q 20 -u 20 -f 15 -F 15," adaptor sequences and bases with a sequencing quality below Q20 were trimmed from the reads in embryos (Chen et al. 2018). Subsequently, HISAT2 aligned all qualified reads to the mm10 reference genome on a per-sample basis (Kim et al. 2019). Raw counts for each sample were obtained using feature Counts (Liao et al. 2014), followed by gene differential expression analysis with DESeq2 (|Log2 FC|> 1, P-value < 0.05) (2014). FPKM-normalized expression data informed DEG identification. Subsequent GO and KEGG enrichment of DEGs applied Benjamini-Hochberg (BH)-corrected significance thresholds (P < 0.05).

CUT&Tag and ATAC data analysis

Utilizing BWA(Li et al. 2013) with the 'mem' command, all ChIP-seq and input data reads were aligned to the mm10 reference genome after trimming with fastp. Eliminated PCR duplicate bias in the paired Bam files byPicard software (https://github.com/broadinstitute/picard) was employed to. H3K27ac peaks were then identified by MACS2(Feng et al. 2012) using the parameters “-f BAMPE -g mm –broad –broad-cutoff 0.01”. Sample-wise signal tracks (RPKM-normalized) were derived using deepTools (Park et al. 2013) bamCoverage. Resultant bigWig files were visualized in Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) [36]. Profile plots and H3K27ac density heatmaps across annotated genomic regions were generated via deepTools.

Statistical analyses

Prism 10.6.0 software (USA) was carried to analyzed statistical significance. Continuous variables were depicted as the mean ± standard deviation, whereas categorical variables were reported as frequencies (%). For comparisons between two groups of continuous variables, the normality of the data distribution was first assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. If this assumption was met, the Student’s t-test was employed. Otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. For comparisons among three or more groups, if the data satisfied both normality and homogeneity of variance, one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) was applied. If not, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was used. Statistical significance defined as P < 0.05.

Results

ZnONPs causes 2-cell arrest in mouse preimplantation embryos

Firstly, we assessed the embryotoxic effects induced by ZnONPs. ZnONPs exposure at 0.3 μg/mL significantly impaired preimplantation embryos, with more than half of 2-cell stage embryos were developmental retardation (Fig. 1A-B). The concentration of 0.3 μg/mL was chosen based on our prior dose–response experiments, which indicated significant developmental arrest at this level without embryo lethality (Liu et al. 2024). Considering the significative developmental arrest at the 2-cell stage and that ZGA mainly occurs from the middle 1-cell to late 2-cell stages (Abe et al. 2018), we proposed that ZnONPs primarily affect ZGA. To test this, we switched the in vitro culture medium between control and ZnONPs treatment at the 1- and late 2-cell stages. As expected, embryos treated with ZnONPs from the 1- to 2-cell stages had similar developmental arrest as those continuously treated with ZnONPs. Moreover, time-restricted exposure to ZnONPs during the 1-cell to mid-2-cell stages triggered developmental arrest, which resulted in nearly half of embryos being arrested, whereas developmental competence remained unaffected outside this critical window (Fig. 1C-E). ZnONPs affect the transcriptional profiles of preimplantation embryos in vitro

To investigate how ZnONPs affect preimplantation embryonic development, RNA-seq profiling in ZnONPs-exposed versus control 2-cell embryos was performed. Cluster analysis clearly distinguished the ZnONPs-treated and control groups across early and late 2-cell stages, with high within-group Pearson correlation coefficients (r > 0.92 and r > 0.93, respectively) (Fig. S1A-C). At late 2-cell stage, 1473 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified compared control and ZnONPs-treated embryos (P < 0.05; Fig. 2A). Notably, we identified a high proportion of upregulated genes, with 976 showing upregulated expression in the ZnONPs-treated group. Gene ontology (GO) analysis identified that upregulated genes in the ZnONPs-treated late 2-cell group were enriched in RNA transcription processes (Fig. 2B), while downregulated genes were enriched in nucleic acid binding and macroautophagy regulation (Fig. 2C). Notably, ZnONPs treatment significantly increased the regulation of transcription regulatory region DNA binding at late 2-cell stage (Fig. 2D). In early 2-cell stage embryos, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis of 786 DEGs, which identified the PI3K-Akt and cAMP metabolic pathways, but did not reveal enrichment of transcription-related pathways (Fig. 2E-F). As observed in control embryos, a cohort of 1571 genes was downregulated during the transition from early to late stage in 2-cell embryos (Fig. 2G). ZnONPs exposure inhibited the downregulation of these genes (Fig. 2H & S1D), indicating a disruption of the normal transcriptional profile in late 2-cell embryos. Minor ZGA is severely impacted by ZnONPs

Transcription in cleavage-stage embryos plays a key role in establishing developmental programs. Total de novo transcription was quantified via 5ʹ-ethynyluridine (EU) labeling in late 2-cell embryos, revealing significantly elevated transcriptional activation levels in ZnONPs-treated groups (Fig. 3A-B), consistent with RNA-seq data. These results suggest that ZnONPs exposure disrupts the transcriptional levels in late 2-cell stage. Given that ZGA represents the predominant genomic reprogramming event in 2-cell embryos, these transcriptional disruptions induced by ZnONPs exposure prompted investigation into its potential impact on ZGA. Transcriptomic profiling detected negligible ZGA alterations in early 2-cell embryos (Fig. 3C), but significant upregulation of ZGA genes during the late 2-cell stage (Fig. 3D). Notably, minor ZGA genes comprised 28.18% (275/976) of upregulated transcripts (Fig. 3E). In addition, part of minor ZGA genes were still expressed in ZnONPs groups, which should be decreased in late 2-cell embryos (Fig. 3F). This suggests that ZnONPs exposure disrupts the minor ZGA profile in early 2-cell mouse embryos. GSEA further confirmed abnormal upregulation of minor ZGA genes in ZnONPs-treated late 2-cell embryos (Fig. 3G), such as Mis18bp1 (Fig. 3H). ZnONPs exposure induces aberrant H3K27ac in 2-cell embryos

Our previous research has demonstrated that the appropriate erasure of H3K27ac is crucial for the completion of minor ZGA (Li et al. 2022). We speculated whether ZnONPs induce excessive minor ZGA through H3K27ac modification. As expected, immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated a marked elevation in H3K27ac enrichment in late 2-cell embryos following ZnONPs treatment (Fig. 4A-B). Furthermore, CUT&Tag analysis demonstrated a pronounced increase in H3K27ac enrichment across the promoter and distal intergenic regions at late 2-cell stage from ZnONPs-treated group compared with the control group (Fig. 4C & S2A-C). GO analysis showed that genes with upregulated H3K27ac enriched in transcription and ribosome biogenesis, such as Dhx9 (Fig. S2D-E). Integrated analysis of RNA-seq and CUT&Tag datasets revealed that ZnONPs treatment led to increased H3K27ac enrichment at genes showing downregulation during the progression from early to late 2-cell stage (Fig. 4D). Moreover, we distinguished genes of late 2-cell embryos into 4 groups (Q1-Q4) based on expression level. We found that gene expression levels strongly correlated with H3K27ac enrichment (Fig. 4E). Upregulated include minor ZGA gene expression in ZnONPs-treated embryos correlates with H3K27ac enrichment (Fig. 4F). As H3K27ac facilitates chromatin accessibility (Smith et al. 2014), ATAC-seq was employed to assess ZnONPs-induced alterations in chromatin accessibility. The global increase ATAC-seq signals aligned with the upregulation of H3K27ac in ZnONPs-treated late 2-cell embryos (Fig. S3A-B), such as Blvra and Gm39922 (Fig. S3C). Notably, ZnONPs treatment significantly increased the accessibility of upregulation minor ZGA genes, consistent with enhanced H3K27ac enrichment (Fig. 4G), such as Anxa7 and Mss51 (Fig. 4H). Discussion

The widespread application of ZnONPs can traverse various bodily systems, including the reproductive system, and may introduce health risks. Given that embryos are highly sensitive to environmental pollutants, it is important to evalute the toxicity and mechanisms of ZnONPs during the preimplantation embryonic development. Although our previous study demonstrated the inhibitory effect of ZnONPs on pluripotency genes expression (Liu et al. 2024), their impact on ZGA in embryos remains unexplored. Here, we verified the impact of ZnONPs on ZGA in mice. Our results indicate that ZnONPs impeding H3K27ac erasures, leading to the failed exit of minor ZGA in late 2-cell stage and ultimately impairing preimplantation embryonic development (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Schematic diagram illustrates the mechanism of ZnONPs exposure effects on preimplantation embryonic development. ZnONPs predominantly influence the 2-cell stage, which aligns with the transition from minor to major ZGA in this study. By limiting ZnONPs exposure to certain stages, we identified that the minor ZGA stage is the most susceptible. RNA-seq analyses revealed that ZnONPs treatment caused an elevation in minor ZGA gene expression at the late 2-cell stage. This was accompanied by increased transcriptional activity and chromatin accessibility. These findings suggest that ZnONPs prevent the proper silencing of minor ZGA, which is required for the embryonic development (Li et al. 2022; Abe et al. 2018). We identified H3K27ac as a crucial epigenetic target of ZnONPs, which should be erased during early 2-cell stage to allow proper ZGA progression (Wang et al. 2022). However, ZnONPs treatment resulted in disposition of H3K27ac at promoter and distal regions of minor ZGA genes in late 2-cell embryos. This was associated with increased chromatin accessibility and persistent transcription. These findings are consistent with previous reports that dynamic H3K27ac remodeling is critical for ZGA (Yu et al. 2023). This suggests that the impact of ZnONPs on gene expression may be mediated through effects on histone modification.

We next to explored the underlying molecular mechanisms of H3K27ac hyperacetylation induced by ZnONPs exposure. Our previous study demonstrated that ZnONPs can enter the nucleus to depositing at H3K9 methylation sites (Liu et al. 2024). Similarly, our results suggest a potential association between ZnONPs and H3K27ac. Such interaction is supported by studies about nanoparticles can associate with core histones (Conroy et al. 2008) and alter histone modification landscapes (Zhang et al. 2020). Importantly, NMN treatment fails to reverse the H3K27ac accumulation caused by ZnONPs exposure. This indicates that ZnONPs could physically bind to H3K27ac sites, blocking the removal of acetylation mediated by NMN. However, it is possible that ZnONPs affect histone acetyltransferases or deacetylases like HATs or HDACs, which regulate acetylation during ZGA stage (Wang et al. 2022). Moreover, the selectivity of ZnONPs for H3K27ac remains unclear. It may relate to the surface properties of ZnONPs, which could favor binding to acetylated lysines. Previous studies have shown that nanoparticles can exhibit tropism toward specific histone modifications based on their physicochemical properties (Conroy et al. 2008). Alternatively, the open chromatin state associated with H3K27ac-marked regions may make them more accessible. Further structural and biophysical studies are needed to clarify the basis of this selectivity.

It is also important to consider other potential mechanisms of ZnONPs toxicity. For instance, ZnONPs are known to induce oxidative stress and inflammation in somatic cells (Patil et al. 2016; Long et al. 2019), which could indirectly affect epigenetic states. However, the rapid onset of defects within one cell cycle and the specificity of the transcriptional and epigenetic changes observed here suggest a direct mechanism. Moreover, the concentration used in this study (0.3 μg/mL) is far below those typically required to induce broad cytotoxicity or oxidative stress in other cell types (Zhao et al. 2013; Kteeba et al. 2017; Qin et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2017), reinforcing the notion that preimplantation embryos are exceptionally vulnerable to nanoscale perturbations. However, previous literature suggests that Zn ions can disrupt gene expression and cause developmental arrest in mouse 2-cell embryos, attributable to abnormal ZGA (Yao et al. 2025; Erbach et al. 1995). Moreover, the cellular effects of ZnONPs are often attributed to dissolved Zn ions (Brun et al. 2014). However, our previous study indicated that zinc ions from ZnONPs partially contribute to embryotoxic (Liu et al. 2024), implying a special role of the nanoparticles. Furthermore, the binding of ZnONPs to H3K27ac also provide a nanoparticle-specific mechanism that may function alongside ionic effects. Whatever, future studies should compare the effects of Zn ions and ZnONPs directly, in order to further understand their respective contributions.

In summary, our study explored the toxicological mechanism of ZnONPs leading to preimplantation embryonic development arrest due to the overactivation of minor ZGA genes by H3K27ac erasures failure. However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The in vitro embryo culture model may not fully reflect conditions in vivo. Although we show that ZnONPs could bind to H3K27ac in vitro, further validation is needed to confirm the way how this interaction occurs. It also remains unclear whether ZnONPs influence other histone modifications or epigenetic regulators during ZGA. Finally, translating findings from mouse embryos to human reproductive risk requires caution due to known species differences in early development and ZGA timing. Future study needs to verify these interactions on human embryos, and exploring strategies to mitigate their embryotoxic effects.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that ZnONPs exposure disrupts preimplantation embryonic development by occupying H3K27 modification sites and then impairing H3K27ac erasure, which lead to aberrant activation of minor ZGA genes and subsequent 2-cell arrest. These findings highlight the reproductive toxicity of ZnONPs and underscore the critical role of epigenetic regulation in early embryogenesis.
