Optimal Structural Design to Improve Cycling Stability of SiO x ‑Spherical Porous CNTs Composite Anode for Lithium-Ion Battery
Authors: Taeyong Choi, Subin Jo, Aneel Pervez, Juhyeong Kim, Duck Rye Chang, Pilgun Oh, Ji-Hun Cha, Khalid Javed, Jin Won Kim, Yoonkook Son
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Silicon suboxide (SiO x ) has emerged as a viable anode material for lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) because of its high theoretical specific capacity and structural stability. However, its practical application is restricted by inadequate cycling stability and poor electrical conductivity. Herein, plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PE-CVD) was utilized to synthesize SiO x -SPC composites, in which the optimized spherical porous CNTs (SPC) provided a well-defined porous framework that facilitated uniform SiO x deposition. The resulting SiO x -SPC composite (SSC) exhibits high electrical conductivity, Li-ion diffusivity, and mechanical stability, which remarkably enhance the cyclic stability and rate capability. As a result, the SSC electrode exhibits a high initial specific capacity of 1032.26 mAh g–1 and achieves exceptional cycling performance, considerably surpassing microsized SiO x (MSiO x ) particles (∼102% vs ∼41% retention after 100 cycles at 0.5C). Moreover, SSC shows enhanced Li-ion diffusion (4.66 × 10–10 cm2 s–1) as evaluated by cyclic voltammetry. This work demonstrates the essential role of the SiO x coating on optimized SPC via PE-CVD and enables the development of long-lasting, high-capacity anode materials for advanced lithium-ion battery technologies.

Introduction

The significant growth of consumer electric vehicles (EVs) and portable devices has increased the demand for higher energy density and large capacity in lithium-ion batteries (LIBs). − However, the limited theoretical capacity (372 mAh g–1) of typical graphite anodes remains a major hurdle in meeting the required application expectations. , Silicon (Si) has attracted significant interest for next-generation LIBs owing to its high capacity (4200 mAh g–1), suitable potential (0.4 V vs Li+/Li), cost-effectiveness, and abundance. Nevertheless, the large volume changes (>300%), unstable SEI formation, structural degradation, and loss of electric contact hinder its commercialization. −

As an alternative to Si, SiO x (0 < x < 2) has gained considerable attention because of its moderate theoretical specific capacity (2680 mAh g–1), volumetric stability (∼200%), and long-term structural stability. − Despite these benefits, SiO x still faces several challenges, such as poor electrical conductivity, inefficient charge transfer kinetics, and irreversible capacity due to the formation of Li2O and lithium silicates during the first lithiation. , To address these limitations, different strategies have been presented, including the design of porous nanostructures and SiO x -based composites such as SiO x /C, SiO x @G, SiO x @C nanorods, porous SiO x @C, SiO x /C yolk–shell structures, and hollow Si/SiO x . − Although these design strategies have achieved some progress, they still face significant challenges related to fabrication complexity. Thus, extensive efforts have focused on establishing a low-cost, scalable approach for the development of high-quality SiO x /C composites. Among various strategies, incorporating carbon nanotubes (CNTs) has emerged as a promising approach due to their exceptional mechanical strength and excellent conductivity (102–106 S cm–1). CNTs provide excellent mechanical durability, electrical conductivity, and high surface area. − Although the CNT matrix offers a conductive network, its high surface area leads to an unstable SEI formation. Depositing SiO x onto the CNT framework resolves these drawbacks by buffering volume expansion, enhancing Li-ion diffusivity, and facilitating stable SEI formation.

Generally, silicon–carbon composite materials for LIB anodes are synthesized via vapor deposition through the decomposition of silane (SiH4) gas. However, the synthesis of SiO x -carbon composites with the same process is difficult because SiH4 is extremely pyrophoric and can react explosively with oxygen, causing serious safety concerns. In contrast, silicon tetrachloride (SiCl4) provides a safe alternative method for the fabrication of SiO x -carbon composites. SiCl4 forms a uniform and stable SiO x thin film when reacted with O2 in a plasma environment without any safety hazards. PE-CVD has been used as an effective method for depositing thin layers onto carbon frameworks at low temperatures. PE-CVD provides multiple advantages over conventional fabrication processes, such as physical vapor deposition (PVD), low-pressure CVD, and atomic layer deposition (ALD). These include low cost, rapid processing, precise thickness control, high deposition rates, and conformal, strongly adherent coatings over large areas. , Therefore, in this study, SiCl4 was selected as the silicon precursor to deposit conformal SiO x coatings onto the spherical porous CNTs (SPC) framework via PE-CVD. In this work, a unique hierarchical composite structure, SiO x -spherical porous CNTs composite (SSC), was developed via low-temperature processing. To evaluate the performance of SSC, comparative samples, including MSiO x , MSiO x blended SPC (MSSB), and MSiO x blended graphite (MSGB), were also prepared. These comparison groups provide a basis for evaluating the structural integrity and performance of uniform SiO x -coated-SPC synthesized by PE-CVD.

The blended samples were prepared by physically mixing MSiO x with SPC and graphite. The blending method and typical degradation mechanism of the MSSB electrode over extended cycles are illustrated in Figure a. The volume expansion of the MSiO x particles during repeated cycles causes them to pulverize and lose electrical contact with the SPC matrix. This leads to the formation of isolated, inactive SiO x particles, resulting in a rapid capacity fade and poor cycling stability. For the SSC structure, the fabricated SPC scaffold was used to deposit a SiO x nanolayer via PE-CVD. The deposition process, along with the unique structural characteristics of the SiO x -SPC composite (SSC), is schematically illustrated in Figure b. The SSC design has several advantages: (1) the CNT framework not only provides a conductive pathway but also maintains the structural integrity over long cycling; (2) the SPC porous structure after SiO x deposition provides enough space for volume accommodation; (3) the thin SiO x nanolayer promotes the stable SEI formation and fast Li-ion kinetics; and (4) due to the strong adhesion between the SPC matrix and the SiO x layer via PE-CVD, the structure experiences less fracture during long-term cycling as compared to bare SiO x particles. Because of these advantages, the novel composite structure facilitates enhanced electrochemical performance across prolonged cycling. Consequently, the SiO x -SPC composite delivers excellent initial discharge capacity (1032.26 mAh g–1) and 102% retention after 100 cycles in a half cell. The unique architecture of the SSC supports strong interfacial contact, mechanical buffering, and uniform active material distribution. Using comprehensive electrochemical evaluations, including cycling tests, rate capability, EIS, Li-ion diffusivity by CV, and GITT-derived IR drop/overpotential, we reveal that the SSC exhibits significantly lower resistance, faster Li-ion diffusion, reduced polarization, and outstanding electrochemical performance compared to blended counterparts. These findings highlight the role of SPC frameworks in enhancing charge transport and cycling stability of SiO x anodes, providing design guidelines for developing high-capacity and energy density LIBs.

Experimental Section

Fabrication of Spherical Porous CNTs (SPC)

The SPC was fabricated by using the spray-drying method. Specifically, a uniform solution with 5% solid content was prepared by dispersing CNTs in an ethanolic solution, which was then introduced into the spray-drying chamber via a liquid pump. The liquid droplets sprayed by a nozzle were fully dried in the drying chamber, where the inlet and outlet temperatures were 120 and 100 °C, respectively. After passing through a cyclone, the dried SPC sample was collected and pyrolyzed at 900 °C for 2 h under an argon environment. The experimental procedure is illustrated in Figure S1.

Preparation of Blended Samples

The blended samples were prepared by physically mixing MSiO x with SPC and graphite, as illustrated in Figure a. To ensure a uniform mixture, the powders were mixed using a Thinky mixer at 2000 rpm for 3 min. The resulting blended samples, MSSB and MSGB, were used as a comparative group to evaluate the electrochemical behavior of blended versus composite materials.

Preparation of SiO x -SPC Composite

The SSC was synthesized via the PE-CVD method, as illustrated in Figure a. This conventional process is crucial for the conformal and uniform deposition of the SiO x layer on the SPC network, which provides a stable and highly integrated structure. The synthesis was carried out in a PE-CVD chamber by using silicon tetrachloride (SiCl4) as the precursor and oxygen (O2) as the reactant gas. The SPC sample was placed into the reaction chamber, and the chamber was evacuated to the base pressure. The radio frequency (RF) plasma was turned on, and the SiCl4 and O2 precursors were introduced into the chamber. The plasma dissociates the precursor into Si and O radicals. These species then react on the surface of the SPC and form a uniform and thin SiO x layer. The use of plasma not only facilitates this reaction at lower temperatures but also ensures a strong interfacial bond between the SiO x coating and the SPC matrix, creating a robust and conductive network.

Material Characterization

A Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FE-SEM, S4700, Hitachi) with backscattered electrons (BSEs) and EDS was used to analyze the structural morphology and elemental mapping. Physicochemical characterizations were examined by an X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku D/Max-2200) analyzed with Cu Kα radiation and Raman spectroscopy (LabRAM HR Evolution, Horiba). Pore size, pore volume, and specific surface area were measured by a nitrogen adsorption–desorption analyzer before and after SiO x deposition. The chemical states of the elements were examined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, NEXSA, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA 2050, TA Instruments) was measured at a heating rate of 3 °C/min under an air atmosphere to evaluate the thermal stability and composition of the samples. The elemental composition was further verified by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES, iCAP7400DUO, Thermo Scientific). The carbon and silicon contents of the samples were determined using elemental analysis (EA, FlashSmart, Thermo Fisher Scientific). To examine the surface morphology of the electrode after cycles, the cells were carefully opened in a glovebox, washed with DMC, and evaluated by cross-sectional SEM. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were measured using an automated analyzer for specific surface area and pore size distribution (BELSORP-mini II, MicrotracBEL). Specific surface area and pore characteristics were assessed with Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) and nonlocal density functional theory (NLDFT) approaches.

Electrochemical Characterization

The electrode was fabricated by using active material (SiO x -SPC) 60 wt %, carbon black (Super P) 10 wt %, and 30 wt % poly­(acrylic acid) (PAA) binder. The slurry was made and cast on copper foil by the conventional doctor blade method. The fabricated electrodes were dried at 100 °C for 30 min. For the blended electrodes, MSiO x particles were first physically mixed with graphite and SPC by using a Thinky mixer. The electrodes were then fabricated by the same method described above for pristine SPC, MSiO x , MSiO x -SPC blending, and MSiO x -graphite blending. The electrodes were cut into 14Φ disks, and the separator was cut into 16Φ disks and used for making cells.

The electrochemical performance of the electrode was measured by 2032 coin-type cells. All half-cells were assembled in an Ar-filled glovebox with prepared anodes, a 1 M electrolyte (LiPF6) in EC:EMC 3:7 (v/v) + VC 1% + FEC 10%, a separator (Celgard 2325), and lithium metal foil as the reference electrode. The electrochemical analysis was conducted at a voltage range of 0.005 to 1.5 V at a rate of 0.1C for two formations and 0.5C for the rest of the cycles. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was executed at different scan rates using the same cycler. The internal resistance of electrodes was evaluated by Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) within a frequency range of 0.1 Hz to 1 MHz at an amplitude of 10 mV. The Galvanostatic Intermittent Titration Technique (GITT) was employed to measure the IR drop and overpotential of the electrodes, providing insights into internal resistance and kinetic polarization during cycling.

Results and Discussion

The morphological evolution of SPC and SSC was investigated by using surface and cross-sectional SEM and BSE images, as illustrated in Figure a–h. SEM and BSE images of pristine SPC (Figure a,b) show a spherical and porous structure formed by an interconnected CNT network. After SiO x deposition via PE-CVD, the CNT matrix displays a smoother morphology owing to pore filling and conformal coating. The BSE image shows a brighter visual, verifying the successful deposition of uniform SiO x (Figure c,d). Furthermore, the structural transformation was illustrated by cross-sectional SEM and BSE. The pristine SPC exhibits a porous interior structure (Figure e,f), while after deposition, the SEM and BSE images reveal SiO x coating in pores and outer surface of SPCs. The BSE clearly shows a SiO x rich brighter region, suggesting well-defined coating on SPC (Figure g,h). The SEM and BSE images of SSC, pristine SPC, MSSB, and MSGB are illustrated in Figure S2a-j. The blended samples demonstrate aggregated and nonuniform morphology, in contrast to the conformal coating via PE-CVD. This highlights the dominance of the deposited SSC structure in attaining structural stability and interfacial adhesion. The elemental composition of SSC was verified by EDS mapping (Figure S3a,d), which clearly demonstrates the distribution of Si and O on the surface of spherical CNTs.

Figure a demonstrates the XRD patterns of pristine SPC, SSC, MSiO x , and blended samples. The pristine SPC displays a broad diffraction peak at 2θ = 25°, associated with the (002) plane of the amorphous carbon CNT framework. Upon SiO x deposition via PE-CVD, the XRD pattern of SSC retains a featureless and broad peak, indicating the deposition of amorphous SiO x on SPC. The main peaks of SPC and SiO x overlap, resulting in the absence of distinct peaks for SiO x , which confirms the successful deposition and effective interaction between SPC and SiO x . , In contrast, the MSiO x , MSSB, and MSGB samples exhibit sharp diffraction peaks, indicating the presence of crystalline SiO x domains (ICDD card no. 00-030-1127). The MSGB shows a sharp peak representing the (002) plane at 2θ = 26.5°, reflecting higher crystallinity of the graphite (ICDD card no. 01-089-7213). The structural characteristics of pristine SPC and graphite were further investigated by Raman spectroscopy (Figure S4). For pristine SPC (Figure S4a), the two distinct peaks around 1340 cm–1 and 1580 cm–1 correspond to D and G bands, respectively. The calculated I D/I G ratio is 1.33, showing a high degree of disordered structure representative of CNT-based materials. Conversely, graphite displays D- and G-bands at 1340 cm–1 and 1561 cm–1 (Figure S4b) with a significantly lower I D/I G ratio of 0.43, indicating an ordered crystalline structure.

To further corroborate the SiO x composition in the SSC, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed. Figure b and S5a-e show the Si 2p and O 1s spectra for SSC, MSiO x, and SiO2. Figure b displays the deconvoluted five peaks of SSC corresponding to Si0, Si1+, Si2+, Si3+, and Si4+ with binding energies of 99.21, 100.62, 101.70, 102.41, and 103.04 eV, respectively, indicating an incomplete oxidation process. In contrast, MSiO x (Figure S5d) exhibits three fitted peaks corresponding to Si0 (99.73 eV), Si2+(101.80 eV), and Si4+ (103.36 eV). The absence of intermediate states (Si1+ and Si3+) and the coexistence of Si0 and Si4+ in MSiO x indicate the disproportion of MSiO x due to heat treatment during the fabrication process. − The presence of all oxidation states in the SSC indicates the amorphous and heterogeneous nature of the deposited SiO x layer on the SPC via PE-CVD, which is optimal for accommodating volume expansion and advancing electrochemical performance during the cycling process. For comparison, the XPS analysis of SiO2 (Figure S5e) was also conducted, which presents a sharp single peak associated with the Si4+ oxidation state, as expected for oxidized and stoichiometric silica. The respective peaks for the O 1s spectra (Figure S5a-c) support these observations. SSC and MSiO x (Figure S5a,b, respectively) demonstrate intense and sharp O 1s peaks at ∼532.6 eV, associated with lattice oxygen (O2–) in Si–O bonds. On the other hand, SiO2 shows a less intense and broader peak, which may occur from lower electronic conductivity, structural consolidation, or differential charging effects.

The surface area and porous nature of pristine SPC and SSC were analyzed by nitrogen adsorption–desorption measurements. Figure S6a,b presents the nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherm of pristine SPC and SSC. Figure S6a shows a typical IV type isotherm, indicating the mesoporous nature of pristine SPC. Conversely, SSC (Figure S6b) also shows a similar isotherm but a significantly increased hysteresis, suggesting a reduction in available mesopores. This originates from the deposition within the internal pores as well as a thin layer on the surface of CNT matrix, which partially blocks or fills the pore. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis (Figure c) demonstrates that the specific surface area decreases from 317.82 m2 g–1 (pristine SPC) to 1.35 m2 g–1 (SiO x -SPC composite) after SiO x deposition via PE-CVD. Likewise, the total pore volume is declined from 0.7237 cm3 g–1 to 0.00349 cm3 g–1, further representing a reduction in porosity after SiO x deposition. Additionally, these findings are further supported by the pore size distribution curve (Figure d). The pristine SPC exhibits a broad distribution of pores ranging from 2 to 30 nm, showing mesoporous characteristics. The pore volume indicates a gradual decrease with an increasing pore diameter, revealing a heterogeneous pore structure dominated by smaller mesopores. The incorporation of SiO x into SPC results in a noticeable reduction in pore volume across all pore sizes, confirming the effective coating of SiO x layer and filling of pores. The Si content and thermal stability of all samples were evaluated by TGA. As illustrated in Figure a, the TGA curve of pristine SPC exhibits sharp weight loss between 400 and 600 °C because of the complete oxidation of the CNT framework with negligible weight retention, while the SiO x -SPC composite displays improved thermal stability up to 600 °C. SSC shows an increase in weight (5%) at a higher temperature, indicating the coexistence of SiO x and SPC. This is attributed to the oxidation of SiO x in the air atmosphere during the TGA process. The SiO x reacts with oxygen to form SiO2, causing weight gain due to oxygen incorporation. Additionally, the outer SiO x layer is first oxidized at elevated temperatures, followed by slow oxidation of the inner carbon framework. Therefore, the coexistence of SiO x and SPC results in the oxidation of both components, collectively causing a slight increase in the weight. On the other hand, MSiO x shows gradual weight gain of ∼20% at higher temperatures in air atmosphere due to oxidation of pure SiO x . These findings are additionally supported by an inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis (Table S1). MSiO x contains 54.16 wt % of silicon, while the SSC carries 48.45 wt % silicon, validating the dilution of silicon content due to SPC incorporation. Furthermore, elemental analysis (EA) of SSC (Table S2) confirms 19.27 wt % carbon content. In contrast, MSSB and MSGB samples display a two-step weight loss, with initial weight loss due to the oxidation of carbon material, followed by weight gain at higher temperatures because of SiO x oxidation, which confirms the physical mixing of SiO x with SPC and graphite. Figure b represents the ICP and EA data, which show a small difference in silicon wt % compared to TGA results, such as for SSC, the TGA shows 49.29 wt %, while the ICP/EA presents 48.45 wt %, which may be due to the different nature of analysis techniques. The silicon and oxygen ratios in MSSB and MSGB were calculated based on the ICP and EA analyses of SSC and MSiO x shown in Tables S1 and S2. The calculation method is illustrated in the Supporting Information (note 1). TGA measurements are based on residual mass, which may slightly exaggerate the silicon content, while ICP/EA directly quantifies the elemental composition accurately. The SSC exhibits a lower oxygen-to-silicon ratio compared to the blended samples, indicating a more Si-rich composition and stronger interfacial bonding between the SiO x coating and the SPC framework formed during the PE-CVD process. Cumulatively, these results verify that the SSC synthesized via PE-CVD attains the chemical integration of SiO x and SPC instead of physical mixing, aligning well with the findings of XRD and XPS analyses.

The electrochemical performance of pristine SPC, SSC, MSiO x , MSSB, and MSGB electrodes was evaluated in a half-cell. Figures a and S7a-d present the voltage profile of the prepared electrodes within the voltage window (0.005–1.5 V) by using the Li metal as the counter electrode. Instead of pristine SPC, all other samples show a prominent plateau at a potential below 0.5 V during lithiation, corresponding to the c-Li15Si4 phase during the alloying reaction. The key parameters of voltage profiles for all samples are summarized in Table S3. The SSC exhibits a discharge capacity of 1032.26 mAh g–1 with an initial Coulombic efficiency (ICE) of 55.06%. Its cyclic stability remarkably reaches 102.8% after 100 cycles (Figure b) at 0.5C, indicating exceptional structural integrity and rapid Li+ diffusivity. This excellent capacity retention is attributed to the gradual electrode activation, which occurs due to the slow activation reaction of SiO2 and lithiation of inner silicon during charging/discharging. , The pristine SPC shows a high charge capacity of 1479.19 mAh g–1 and a discharge capacity of 335.52 mAh g–1 (Figure S7a) with an ICE of 22.68% due to a large surface area and porous structure. Table S3 represents 76.20% retention upon 100 cycles for pristine SPC, indicating a large SEI formation and less Li-ion diffusivity due to its high surface area. MSiO x exhibits the highest discharge capacity (1433.57 mAh g–1) and highest ICE (70.54%). This higher ICE is attributed to the disproportionation of SiO x , which mitigates the irreversible capacity loss through the evolution of Li2O and Li4SiO4. However, it displays a rapid decrease in capacity during the first few cycles (Figure b), and after 100 cycles, the retention drops to 41.62% because of unstable SEI formation and pulverization caused by a huge volume expansion. The MSSB and MSGB samples (Figure S7b,d) exhibit moderate discharge capacities as represented in Table S3, with ICE of 51.51% and 61.04%, respectively. These blended samples also show subpar long-term stability, retaining capacities of only 60.96% and 76.30%, respectively, after 100 cycles, likely due to mechanical degradation and poor electrical conductivity. Noticeably, the lower ICE of SSC and MSSB originates from the SPC matrix. Nevertheless, the ICE of SSC is higher than that of MSSB, indicating that SiO x deposition effectively mitigates the irreversible reactions associated with SPC and improves the initial efficiency compared to MSSB. Overall, the excellent performance of SSC is ascribed to the deposition of a thin SiO x layer on the SPC matrix, which prevents volume changes, maintains electrical conductivity, provides an excellent pathway for Li-ion diffusion, and suppresses continuous SEI growth. For reference, the electrochemical performance of a nonoptimized SSC sample over 300 cycles is included in Figure S8. This illustrates that the enhanced cyclability of the current SSC is a direct result of its structural optimization through the deposition of a thin SiO x film. Differential capacity (dQ/dV) was performed to analyze the electrochemical reaction during lithiation/delithiation, as shown in Figure S9. SSC exhibits a pronounced cathodic peak around 0.2 V and anodic peaks at 0.3 and 0.46 V associated with the alloying/dealloying reaction of SiO x derived Si. , In contrast, the pristine SPC (Figure S9b) exhibits a sloping peak characteristic of carbon materials and corresponds to the intercalation of Li+ into interlayers. The broad cathodic peak at 0.9 V is ascribed to the formation of SEI on pristine SPC. The MSSB and MSiO x (Figure S9c,d) samples display a slopy peak associated with the lithiation process of SiO x , while the broad peaks at 0.3 and 0.48 V correspond to the discharging of both electrodes. However, the MSGB electrode (Figure S9e) exhibits the same sloping behavior during lithiation at a lower potential, confirming the utilization of both silicon alloying and intercalation mechanisms in the MSiO x -graphite electrode, whereas the delithiation peaks at 0.16, 0.31, and 0.49 V show deintercalation and dealloying behavior. The attenuated peaks during delithiation in MSSB and MSGB electrodes as compared to MSiO x indicate the coexistence of MSiO x with SPC and graphite as active materials. The cyclic stability of electrodes was additionally evaluated by Coulombic efficiency (CE) over 100 cycles, as illustrated in Figure S10a. All electrodes show lower CE during initial cycles, mainly due to SEI formation. The SiO x -SPC composite displays a stable CE (over 99%) after the second cycle; this exceptional stability is due to the deposition of a thin SiO x layer on SPC via PE-CVD, which mitigates the volume changes in the electrode, thereby hindering the continuous fracture and regeneration of the SEI layer. The blended electrodes also exhibit stable cyclic performance, attributed to the buffering role of pristine SPC and graphite in volume accommodation. In contrast, pristine SPC and MSiO x manifest the fluctuating Coulombic efficiency, indicating unstable SEI formation caused by continuous cracking and reformation due to volume expansion.

The rate capability of SSC, pristine SPC, MSSB, MSiO x , and MSGB was evaluated at different c-rates ranging from 0.2 to 3C, as represented in Figure c. MSiO x exhibits the highest capacity at the lowest (0.2C) rate; however, as the c-rate increases, the performance deteriorates, reflecting poor kinetics due to inadequate electrical conductivity. The SSC displays an excellent rate capability among all electrodes at high current densities. The spherical porous CNT matrix provides a highly conductive network for electron transportation, while the conformal thin deposition of SiO x on SPC via PE-CVD allows rapid Li+ diffusion in the SSC electrode. This unique structure mitigates the limitations of both pristine SPC and SiO x , leading to outstanding performance at high c-rates. Upon reverting to 0.2C, the SSC electrode recovered its initial capacity, illustrating structural stability and high reversibility of electrochemical reactions. Conversely, MSSB and MSGB samples also show better rate performance with respect to MSiO x , but at higher rates, their capacities are lower than SSC because of reduced utilization of active materials and kinetic limitations. Pristine SPC displays the lowest but stable capacities at all rates owing to rapid Li intercalation kinetics.

For a better understanding of electrochemical kinetics, the lithium-ion diffusion coefficient (DLi+) was calculated for all electrodes by cyclic voltammetry (CV). The CV analysis was conducted at scan rates between 0.1 mV s–1 and 0.8 mV s–1, as shown in Figure S11a-e. The peak current in all electrodes increases progressively with scan rates, indicating diffusion-controlled behavior. Figure d shows the calculated diffusion coefficient for all samples. SSC displays the highest DLi+ (4.66 × 10–10 cm2 s–1) compared to those of all other samples. Pristine SPC, MSiO x , MSSB, and MSGB electrodes exhibit a lower diffusion coefficient, indicating these strategies do not mitigate the problems of volume expansion and SEI formation arising from their structural characteristics, in contrast to the deposited thin SiO x layer on SPC. The superior Li-ion diffusivity in the composite electrode is attributed to the unique structure of SiO x -SPC, in which the CNT network provides better electron transport, and the conformal coating of SiO x allows excellent Li diffusion over short distances. , The interface between the SiO x and spherical porous CNT matrix minimizes interfacial resistance and enhances charge transfer kinetics, which align with its exceptional rate performance at high rates and cyclic stability.

Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) was further used to investigate the dynamic behavior of electrodes. Nyquist plots and the fitted equivalent circuit model (inset images) consisting of surface resistance (R s), the resistance of the solid electrolyte interface passivation layer (R SEI), and charge transfer resistance (R ct) are shown in Figure S13a-e. The Nyquist plots show a typical shape, consisting of a compressed semicircle at middle to higher frequencies, followed by an inclined line at lower frequencies. The intercept of the semicircle with the real axis (Z′) in the high-frequency domain reflects the surface resistance (R s), which covers the internal resistance of the cell, including the current collector and electrolyte resistance. The semicircle in the middle range is attributed to R SEI and R ct. The inclined line at low frequencies corresponds to the Warburg impedance (Z W), which indicates the lithium-ion diffusion behavior within the electrode material.

The fitted values of resistance parameters are illustrated in Table S4. The SSC displays the lowest R s (2.8 Ω), R SEI (2.8 Ω), and R ct1­(2.5 Ω). R SEI and R ct are lower due to stable SEI formation, enhanced electronic conductivity, and charge transfer kinetics due to thin conformal coating and intimate contact between SiO x and the SPC conductive network. In contrast, pristine SPC and MSGB samples show the highest R SEI values of 35.8 and 48.7 Ω, respectively. This indicates that a more resistive and thicker SEI layer formed due to the large surface area and mechanical instability due to volume expansion. The MSiO x particles and the MSSB show intermediate values, but the overall performance is inferior to that of the SSC. The EIS analysis confirms that the unique architecture of the SSC effectively mitigates the internal and interfacial resistances, resulting in a more stable and kinetically favorable environment for lithium-ion storage. The GITT analysis was conducted to examine the long-term stability and electrochemical polarization of SSC, MSSB, and MSGB electrodes over 100 cycles. The IR drop and overpotential were measured to determine the internal resistance of cells and the kinetic polarization of composite and blended electrodes, shown in Figure S14a,b. The SSC exhibits the lowest IR drop and overpotential over 100 cycles, ranging between (8–17 mV) and (65–100 mV), respectively. The inferior IR drop in SSC indicates stable and lower internal resistance, which is consistent with EIS data due to a conductive SPC matrix and uniform SiO x coating via PE-CVD, which facilitates the stable and less resistive SEI layer. Similarly, the overpotential also remains lower in SSC, signifying suppressed kinetic polarization. The increase in the IR drop and overpotential observed at 40 and 60 cycles in SSC is a temporary phenomenon caused by volume expansion and contraction of the SiO x particles during cycling, which can temporarily increase internal resistance. As cycling continues, the structure gradually stabilizes, and the IR drop and overpotential decrease or recover, indicating that there is no permanent structural failure. These results match well with our previous results on high Li-ion diffusivity and excellent rate performance, further corroborating that the composite framework of SiO x and SPC promotes efficient charge transfer and fast Li-ion transportation. In contrast, MSSB and MSGB electrodes display higher IR drop and overpotential with a gradual increase over cycles. The IR drop is higher in both blended samples due to the loss of electric contact between the particles and unstable SEI formation, while the increased overpotential reflects a less efficient and more kinetically hindered electrochemical process over cycling. The GITT investigation validates that the SSC possesses superior electrochemical and structural stability as compared to other electrodes. The lower IR drop and overpotential values indicate a stable structure with minimized internal resistance and highly Li-ion kinetics.

The structural and morphological changes during prolonged cycling were observed by cross-sectional SEM images of all of the electrodes. Figure a-e displays the compact and uniform morphologies of SSC, pristine SPC, MSSB, MSiO x , and MSGB electrodes before cycling. The SSC electrode shows excellent structural stability even after 150 cycles (Figure f), and the thickness changes from 27.4 to 52.2 μm without indication of cracking or pulverization due to the uniform coating of SiO x on the SPC network, which mitigates the volume changes and prevents structural degradation. In contrast, MSSB (14.9 to 42.5 μm), MSiO x (20.5 to 39.5 μm), and MSGB (16.1 to 38.7 μm) show considerable swelling of electrodes, illustrated in Figure h-j, respectively, corresponding to large volume expansion. The signs of cracks and pulverization are also observed in these electrodes, which is the main reason for the internal resistance and capacity fading over cycling. Pristine SPC exhibits the lowest structural expansion (29.4 to 31.3 μm), as shown in Figure g. The smaller thickness increase in pristine SPC as compared to SiO x containing electrodes is consistent with a stable intercalation reaction. These cross-sectional SEM analyses present a strong connection between the structure of electrodes and electrochemical performance. The results confirm that the SSC structure is crucial for mitigating the effects of volume expansion for long-term cycling stability and a superior rate capability.

Conclusion

In summary, the SSC anode was successfully synthesized by depositing a thin and conformal SiO x layer onto optimized spherical porous CNTs via the PE-CVD method. The fabricated structure efficiently mitigates the drawbacks of SiO x , including poor electrical conductivity, poor Li+ kinetics, and mechanical integrity, by providing a conductive network, improved electrolyte accessibility, and mechanical buffering against volume expansion. This strategy not only ensures robust contact between the active material and the conductive scaffold but also facilitates durable SEI formation, further contributing to the excellent electrochemical stability of the SiO x -SPC composite. The SSC exhibits better initial discharge capacity (1032.26 mAh g–1), superior cycling stability, and rate capability, significantly outperforming the performance of MSiO x and blended samples. In a broader context, our findings highlight that rational nanostructure engineering through the integration of spherical CNTs and thin SiO x deposition via PE-CVD reflects a potential approach to develop high-performance materials for next-generation LIBs.
