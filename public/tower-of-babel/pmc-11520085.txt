Optimization of sowing dates for enhanced rice yield: insights from field experiments in the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River, China
Authors: Yalan Ji, Youzun Xu, Xueyuan Sun, Muhammad Ahmad Hassan, Yongjin Zhou, Huawen Zou, Zhong Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn efficacious strategy to adapt to climate change involves optimizing the planting season, a technique that has been extensively utilized to enhance the use of solar radiation and temperature resources in rice cultivation. Field experiments were executed in the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River, China, employing three distinct rice cultivars and seven disparate sowing periods spanning 2019 to 2021. The objective of assessing the impact of sowing date on apparent radiation use efficiency (RUEA), accumulated temperature use efficiencies (TUE), and overall rice yield. Subsequent to the delay of sowing dates, the duration of the comprehensive growth period initially exhibited a declining trajectory before subsequently escalating, with the reduction predominantly ascribed to a decrease in the number of days preceding heading. Furthermore, there was a tendency for both the mean daily and effective cumulative solar radiation to decline over the course of the growing period. The yield of the three rice varieties demonstrated an initial surge, which was then followed by a subsequent decline in reaction to the delay of sowing dates. A correlation analysis disclosed that solar radiation and effective cumulative temperature (EAT) were the predominant elements impacting grain yield. The outcomes of the path analysis indicate that EAT exerts the most substantial influence on yield, succeeded by cumulative total solar radiation (TSR), while photothermal quotient (PTQ) demonstrates the least impact on yield. There was a significant positive correlation between EAT and cumulative TSR with spikelets per panicle (0.237** and 0.218**), grain filling (0.753** and 0.576**), and grain weight (0.339** and 0.359**), respectively. The findings of this study indicate that an increase in yield is facilitated when the EAT after heading exceeds 594.9 ℃, the EAT surpasses 2016.7 ℃, the cumulative TSR before heading is above 1548.7 MJ m− 2, the cumulative TSR after heading is over 603.0 MJ m− 2, and the cumulative total radiation throughout the entire growth period is more than 2151.8 MJ m− 2. Furthermore, the most optimal sowing date, as identified by this study, is June 6. This study provides key insights into boosting rice productivity in the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River, China by analyzing the impact of temperature and solar radiation on yield and identifying optimal growth conditions. Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is the primary staple food for over 50% of the world’s population [1]. China is a major contributor to global rice production, possessing approximately one-fifth of the world’s rice cultivation area [2]. With an average of 29.90 million hectares dedicated to rice cultivation, this crop comprises 25.59% of the total area sown with grain crops in China. Furthermore, between the years 2019 and 2021, rice production represented 31.46% of the total grain production in the country (data source: National Bureau of Statistics of China). The prominence of food security concerns has been heightened by the dual pressures of climate change and population growth [3]. Yield, a critical factor in rice production, is inherently limited by the finite resources offered by the Earth’s terrestrial, marine, and atmospheric environments [3]. The decline in crop yields, attributable to a succession of warming trends and escalating extreme climatic events, has surfaced as a formidable challenge that necessitates human intervention [4]. The detrimental effects of climate change have already led to a reduction in rice production ranging from 2 to 26% in Thailand, Vietnam, Philippines and Indonesia [5]. Furthermore, apprehensions have been articulated concerning the potential implications of mitigation and adaptation strategies on the food system [6]. It is projected that rice production during the period 2005 to 2050 will decrease by approximately 2.55% and 2.48%, respectively, compared to the baseline period of 1960 to 2004 [7]. It has been noted that elevated temperatures can accelerate the growth cycle of numerous crops, thereby diminishing the duration for biomass accumulation [8–10]. Excessive heat during the panicle initiation phase or the initial stage of grain filling can obstruct spikelet differentiation, augment the incidence of empty grains, reduce the proportion of filled grains, and ultimately contribute to a decline in yield [11, 12]. Correspondingly, suboptimal temperatures at the initial phase of rice cultivation can hinder the development of the crop group and accumulation of materials, resulting in a deficient cumulative temperature during the grain-filling period, thereby causing a decrease in yield [13]. Insufficient solar radiation during the vegetative growth stage can lead to a reduction in the number of rice tillers, effective panicle count, leaf area index, and dry matter accumulation, all of which can collectively contribute to a decrease in yield [14]. Moreover, diminished solar radiation during the rice grain-filling stage results in less plump grains, suboptimal grain-filling, and ultimately, a reduction in yield [15, 16]. Consequently, understanding the impact of temperature and solar radiation on rice yield is of paramount importance.

The prevalent consensus in the academic community is that crop growth and yield are often impacted by factors such as temperature and solar radiation. It is observed that temperature accumulation and cumulative incident solar radiation have a direct correlation with the duration of growth. It is incumbent upon different regions to determine a suitable sowing period that aligns with their specific climatic conditions. The objective is to circumvent periods of extreme high-temperature weather during the rice tasseling and grain filling stage, while simultaneously ensuring that the cumulative temperature requirements for the filling period are met, thereby optimizing yield potential. As a result, the preservation of optimal temperature and solar radiation conditions can augment rice growth and efficiently alleviate the effects of natural elements such as pests, diseases, and frosts. This, in turn, can prevent a decrease in the seed filling rate, yield, and quality. Influenced by climate change, China is subject to recurrent regional climate catastrophes, which contribute to heightened instability in agricultural production and an irrational distribution and utilization of natural resources. In the contemporary multifaceted context, influenced by a myriad of factors, the predictability of temperature and solar radiation patterns, crucial for year-round rice cultivation, has become uncertain. The extensive implementation of superior and productive annual rice-wheat cropping systems has imposed limitations on conventional agricultural practices. These include machine-inserted rice transplanting or direct seeding, which are now constrained due to reduced seedling age or even its complete elimination. These changes are further compounded by the restrictions imposed by the management of wheat stubble. As a result, there is a requirement for a delay in the sowing of rice, which directly influences the maturation period of the seeds [17].

Consequently, seven unique environments, characterized by varying temperatures and solar radiation levels, were created by employing diverse sowing dates. This was done to examine the efficiency of temperature and light resource utilization in rice cultivation within China. The findings from this study bear significant implications. The aims of the present research encompass: (i) the comparison of temperature and solar radiation across distinct phenological stages within three indica rice varieties; (ii) the identification of optimal combinations of high-yielding varieties, taking into account the specific temperature and solar radiation conditions in the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River, China; (iii) the identification of optimal temperature and solar radiation ranges that result in relatively high yields, thereby informing appropriate sowing dates to enhance and stabilize indica rice production for rice farmers and agricultural policy-makers.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and experiment design

Field experiments were undertaken at the Anhui Academy of Agricultural Sciences, situated in Hefei City, Anhui Province, China (31°89΄N, 117°25΄E), during the agricultural seasons of 2019 and subsequently replicated in 2021. The geographical location of the experimental area is within the Yangtze River, characterized by a temperate climate and extending from the northern region of the lower Yangtze River to the Huai River. Soil analysis was conducted on the topmost 20 cm layer of the experimental field. The composition includes 16.35 g kg-1 of organic matter, 1.38 g kg-1 of total nitrogen (N), 17.29 mg kg-1 of available phosphorus (P), 176.27 mg kg-1 of available potassium (K), and exhibits a pH level of 7.62.

This study employed a randomized complete block design incorporating a split-plot arrangement, wherein sowing dates were treated as the primary plots and varieties as the secondary plots. The selection of varying sowing dates was intended to investigate the influence of assorted environmental conditions on the growth and development of rice. The experimental design incorporated a schedule of sowing dates, specifically: April 27 (S1), May 7 (S2), May 17 (S3), May 27 (S4), June 6 (S5), June 16 (S6), and June 26 (S7). This was implemented across two years, 2019 and 2021, with the application of three distinct indica rice varieties: Taoyouxiangzhan (V1), Fengliuyouxiang 1 (V2), and Wandao 153 (V3). It should be noted that the 2020 experimental trial did not yield any results due to the incidence of flooding. Each experimental unit encompassed a space of 10 m × 20 m (200 m2), with an interplant and interrow spacing of 12 cm×30 cm, respectively. The process of transplanting was conducted utilizing a transplanter machine, whereby two rice seedlings were positioned within each hole. Each experimental treatment was subjected to three replications for accuracy and consistency.

The nutrient ratio of Nitrogen (N), Diphosphorus Pentoxide (P2O5), and Potassium Oxide (K2O) has been calibrated to 1:0.4:0.8 (kg ha-1). The allocation ratio of the fertilizer for the base, tiller, and spike fertilizer has been optimized to 5.5:1.5:3.0. Specifically, 15% of the nitrogen fertilizer is applied as tiller fertilizer 10 d post-planting, while 30% is applied as panicle fertilizer. All of the phosphorus fertilizer and 60% of the potassium fertilizer are utilized as base fertilizer. The remaining 40% of the potassium fertilizer is applied as panicle fertilizer along with the nitrogen fertilizer. The application of deep water irrigation is utilized during periods of high temperature to maintain a regulated climate, succeeded by intermittent irrigation. The cessation of irrigation occurred 7 d prior to the harvesting period. Pest and weed management strategies were implemented in accordance with the prescribed technical guidelines for the cultivation of high-yield rice.

Meteorological data collection

Data pertaining to the climate were procured from regional meteorological stations, encompassing daily peak temperature, daily mean temperature, and daily cumulative solar radiation (Fig. 1). Comprehensive records were meticulously maintained during the entire rice development cycle, encompassing stages such as sowing, transplanting, jointing, heading, and maturity.

Statistical analysis

The analysis of statistical data was conducted utilizing IBM SPSS Statistics (Analytical Software, USA), with the comparison of treatment means being based on the least significant difference test at the probability levels of 0.05 and 0.01. The graphical representations were generated using the software package Origin Pro 8.0 (Origin Lab, Northampton, MA, USA).

Results

The characteristics of meteorological indexes

The diurnal variations of the three temperature indices, namely average daily temperature, maximum temperature, and minimum temperature, during the rice growing season at the experimental site exhibited consistent patterns (Fig. 1). From April onwards, all indicators showed a gradual increase trend, reaching peak values in July and August before gradually declining. The trend of cumulative temperature and the trend of radiation change are consistent.

Rice growth duration

The duration of growth before heading, subsequent to heading, and throughout the entire growth period was significantly impacted by the variety of rice, the date of sowing, and the interaction between these two factors, as illustrated in Table 1. The cumulative growth duration across three different rice varieties was found to decrease with later planting dates, a trend primarily evidenced by a decrease in the number of days during the nutritional growth stage (Refer to Table 1 and Table S1). When compared to T1, the period from seeding to maturity in T2 through T7 exhibited changes of 3.3 d, 0.2 d, -1.7 d, -4.0 d, -4.3 d, and − 4.0 d, respectively. The duration from the sowing to the heading phase was observably reduced, with an average decrease of 0.5 d, 2.8 d, 4.3 d, 7.0 d, 8.7 d, and 10.0 d in comparison to the T1 phase, respectively. The heading to maturity phase, on the other hand, displayed a non-linear reaction to the prolongation of the sowing period, initially diminishing and subsequently augmenting. Specifically, the T7 treatment manifested an average increment of 1.5 d relative to the T1 phase, while the remaining treatments (T2 ~ T6) exhibited average reductions of 0.7 d, 1.5 d, 1.8 d, and 0.2 d, respectively, when compared to the T1 treatment.

Solar radiation and temperature conditions

The sowing date significantly impacted both the daily TSR and the cumulative TSR of rice, as indicated in Table S2. However, the interaction between the sowing date and variety did not exhibit a significant effect. The cumulative TSR before heading in 2019 and 2021 ranged from 1357.8 to 1697.2 MJ m-2 and 1357.0 to 1671.6 MJ m-2, respectively, as detailed in Tables S2 and S3. Initially, the cumulative TSR before heading demonstrated an ascending trend, which subsequently descended, reaching its peak across three varieties in 2019 and 2021. The cumulative TSR following heading in 2019 and 2021 varied from 454.3 to 775.4 MJ m-2 and 519.2 to 626.1 MJ m-2, respectively. The cumulative TSR throughout the entire growth seasons of 2019 and 2021 ranged from 1894.9 to 2456.0 MJ m-2 and 1965.3 to 2252.6 MJ m-2, respectively. When examining different varieties, the cumulative TSR for variety V1 from TD to ES was found to be 113.1 to 161.7 MJ m-2 and 85.3 to 109.8 MJ m-2, which was lower than the other treatments in 2019 and 2021, respectively, as indicated in Table S3.

The sowing date significantly influenced both the daily mean temperature and the EAT, as evidenced in Tables S4 and S5. A delay in the sowing date corresponded with a gradual decrease in the EAT before heading, after heading, and throughout the entire growth season in 2019. Specifically, the EAT for V1, V2, and V3 ranged from 1817.7 to 2043.8 ℃ d (1776.3 to 2044.5 ℃ d), 1782.4 to 2115.6 ℃ d (1781.2 to 2175.4 ℃ d), and 1864.2 to 2187.3 ℃ d (1853.7 to 2153.8 ℃ d) in 2019 (2021), respectively. Furthermore, the pattern of cumulative solar radiation variation was found to be consistent with that of the EAT, as shown in Table S4.

The PTQ before heading was significantly affected by the sowing date (Table S6). However, the interactions of variety, sowing date and variety do not affect it. The PTQ of V1, V2 and during 2019 and 2021 were 0.63 ~ 0.70 MJ m-2 d-1 ℃ and 0.63 ~ 0.67 MJ m-2 d-1 ℃.

Correlation analysis

The outcomes of the path analysis indicate that EAT exerts the most substantial influence on yield, succeeded by TSR, while PTQ has the least impact on yield, as illustrated in Table 3. Concurrently, a significant interaction effect between EAT and cumulative TSR on rice yield was observed. The yield was influenced by several factors including cumulative TSR before and after heading, TSR, EAT after heading, and total EAT. A positive correlation was identified among these variables, suggesting that enhancing these factors could potentially facilitate yield formation. Subsequent research revealed a significant positive correlation between EAT and cumulative TSR with spikelets per panicle (0.237** and 0.218**), grain filling (0.753** and 0.576**) and grain weight (0.339** and 0.359**). These findings suggest that a moderate increase in EAT and cumulative TSR could potentially enhance the spikelets per panicle, grain filling, and grain weight, thereby promoting yield substitutes (Table 4).

Discussion

Suitable sowing date ranges for growth duration and rice yield

The duration of growth plays a crucial role in augmenting grain yield, due to its positive impact on environmental conditions and the photosynthetic duration of the plant [20]. Apart from the variety of rice, factors such as air temperature and solar radiation significantly influence the growth duration and yield [21]. Our research yielded varying climatic conditions during the growth duration of rice, brought about by a delayed sowing period. This delay correspondingly impacted the growth period of each rice variety, leading to a notable decrease in the number of the nutritional growth period. Rice is a high-temperature, short-day crop. In comparison to the initial sowing date (T1), subsequent sowing dates were delayed by 7 d, resulting in an average reduction in growth duration of 2 to 11 d for V1, 0 to 17 d for V2, and 0 to 9 d for V3. The elevated temperatures associated with delayed sowing expedite the developmental phase from sowing to tasseling, thereby requiring a lower cumulative temperature than earlier sowing dates, which ultimately leads to a shorter fertility period [22]. It is essential to maintain the rice growth duration within an optimal range to ensure proper maturation, thereby enhancing grain yield and efficiently utilizing climatic resources.

Significant yield losses in rice due to early or late sowing have been documented in previous studies [22, 23]. Variations in yield across different studies can be attributed to differences in climatic conditions, cultivars, and sowing dates. The high yields observed in S3, S5, and S6 were primarily due to increased spikelets per square meter and grain weight, which were significantly higher during these sowing periods. The low grain yield in 2021 was mainly attributed to reduced temperatures and solar radiation. These findings are consistent with other experiments [24]. The maximum yield was predominantly observed from May to early June, followed by a decline in mid to late June. These findings indicate that the adverse impacts of low temperatures on early-season rice grain yield can be mitigated during this period. Variations in grain yield, whether due to early or delayed transplanting, as well as increases in yield, were attributed to changes in the number of spikelets. The number of spikelets per panicle is a crucial determinant of grain yield formation, primarily influencing the number of spikelets, grains per panicle, seeding rate, and grain weight [25, 26].

Conversely, S1 and S7 exhibited relatively low yields under the sowing treatment. Both reduction in grain yield due to early or delay transplanting and increase in grain yield were explained by the number of spikelets. The change in sowing date also modified other climatic factors like humidity, which in turn had an effect on spikelet sterility. Reduced yield associated with late sowing is attributed to shortened fertility periods, decreased spike density, and diminished biomass accumulation during the tassel stage [27]. The increased yield observed in V3 across all treatments can likely be attributed to the relatively stable temperature and light conditions following the heading phase among the various varieties, which consequently exerted minimal influence on the seed filling process.

Suitable sowing date ranges for temperature and solar radiation

Regulating temperature and solar radiation within an optimal range throughout the growth period enhances the accumulation of both biological and economic yield. Additionally, it contributes to disaster risk reduction and decreases the incidence of pests, thereby reducing input costs [28, 29]. The growth and development of rice exhibit heightened sensitivity to temperature and solar radiation, particularly during the early reproductive stage of seedling differentiation and the spike development process prior to tasseling [30]. Temperature and solar radiation are critical limiting factors influencing rice growth, development, and yield formation. Specifically, temperature and solar radiation during the grain filling and setting periods significantly impact the structural characteristics of the spike and grain filling [31]. The yields of three rice varieties demonstrated substantial correlations with EAT and exhibited minimal differences in response to cumulative solar radiation across the seven environmental condition treatments. The observed variations in yield are predominantly attrubutable to the interaction between average daily temperature and radiation levels, alongside the inconsistent growth and grain set of rice, which result from climatic disparities during the sowing period and varietal differences. Specifically, the yields of treatments S3, S5, and S6 were marginally higher compared to other treatments, which can be attributed to a greater number of spikelets per panicle. Previous studies have indicated that an extended growth duration is associated with increased EAT and, consequently, higher grain yield [32, 33]. The PTQ quantifies the ratio of solar radiation received to the average daily temperature. Consequently, it encapsulates the synergistic interaction between solar radiation, the principal driver of carbon accumulation, and temperature, a critical determinant of carbon accumulation [34, 35]. Numerous prior studies have established a strong correlation between PTQ and grain yield [36, 37]. The findings of the present study corroborate this relationship. The findings of this study demonstrated a significant positive correlation between PTQ and growth duration, suggesting that PTQ can effectively prolong the growth period of rice. This extension is advantageous for the grain filling stage, particularly by increasing the number of effective panicles. Consequently, by strategically adjusting the sowing date, an optimal combination of temperature and solar radiation was achieved across various phenological stages, resulting in enhanced grain yield and improved resource use efficiency.

The rice yield, TUE and RUEA varied across different sowing dates. This study highlighted the critical importance of optimizing TUE and RUEA at various growth stages. The interaction between the year, sowing date, and rice variety exhibited highly significant effects on TUE and RUEA from transplantation to maturity. Additionally, the availability of increased warmth and light resources during the grain-filling period was identified as a crucial factor in enhancing rice productivity [38]. The TUE and RUEA tendencies of the S5 and S6 environmental condition treatments were significantly higher than those of the other treatments. However, the yield of S6, in comparison to S5, was primarily affected by a significantly reduced seed-setting rate due to low EAT during grain filling. Additionally, the continuous low temperature after heading impeded the distribution of assimilates to the grains [39]. The increased yield of V3 in 2019 under conditions S1 to S3 can be primarily attributed to the enhanced efficiency of temperature and light resource utilization during both the early (jointing to heading) and middle to late (heading to maturity) stages of rice development. This improvement in TUE and RUEA during the phases of young spike differentiation and grain filling facilitates the formation of robust stalks and large spikes, thereby increasing the fruit set rate and thousand-grain weight [38]. The elevated yield of V1 under S4 and S5 conditions in 2021 can be primarily attributed to the stable TUE and RUEA during the early growth stages, coupled with enhanced efficiency in the utilization of temperature and solar radiation resources, as well as increased material accumulation capacity during the middle and late stages of fertility [40]. Consequently, higher EAT throughout the reproductive period, along with greater TUE and RUEA of meteorological resources during the early and late reproductive stages, are critical characteristics and foundational elements for achieving high yield. Furthermore, the processes of growth and development are governed by intricate interactions among factors such as light, temperature, soil moisture, and nutrients. In rice cultivation systems, additional elements such as soil health, water quality, and irrigation water demand significantly influence rice yields [5]. Therefore, effective weed control and water management practices are likely to enhance yields under changing climatic. With the anticipated rise in global temperatures in the coming decades, it becomes imperative to investigate the optimal sowing periods to mitigate heat damage in rice production and ensure an adequate water supply to minimize such damage [41].

Recommendations on optimal sowing date ranges for rice cultivation

The efficiency of resource utilization was enhanced by optimizing sowing dates to achieve the ideal synergy of temperature and solar radiation. Taking into account the timing of harvest, land preparation, and other agricultural inputs, the growth and sowing schedule for medium indica rice can be effectively managed through mechanical transplanting in the Yangtze River from mid to late May. Consequently, the optimal sowing date ranges for achieving relatively high yields are between May 27 and June 6. Several studies reached similar conclusions [42]. In conclusion, to achieve a synergistic increase in bioaccumulation and yield, it is essential to maximize high yield potential through rational sowing practices based on a steady increase in TUE and RUEA. This approach promotes efficient filling and fruit set, thereby obtaining high fruit set and thousand-grain weight. Furthermore, by optimizing the sowing date, the population number of spikes can be increased, efficient grouting can be facilitated, and overall yield can be enhanced. Ding et al. (2020) identified that the escalating frequency of heat events is projected to constrain the sowing windows for farmers in Chinese rice-producing areas, such as the Yangtze River Basin, by the end of the century [43]. To mitigate the adverse effects of climate change on rice yields, it is imperative to implement effective strategies, including optimizing precipitation utilization, adopting rational cultivation practices, and minimizing resource wastage.

Conclusion

A good-quality indica rice yield in China’s lower reaches is mainly determined by temperature and solar radiation. In conclusion, under the main cropping systems, May 27 ~ June 6 (S5) was found to be the optimal sowing date for indica rice to obtain relatively high yields in China. This study analyzed the influence of temperature and light factors on grain yield in China, clarified the temperature and light demand of grain yield formation, and recommended a suitable cultivation period to make full use of temperature and light factors in China to achieve high rice yield. To effectively mitigate the adverse impacts of climate change and population growth on rice yield, and to enhance resource efficiency, the implementation of emerging technologies, such as remote sensing and precision agriculture, to optimize the sowing period represents a superior adaptation strategy.
