In vitro development and evaluation of chitosan–cellulose nanocrystal microspheres for controlled dexamethasone delivery in rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Veda B Hacholli, Samar M Patil, Łukasz Szeleszczuk, B H Prabhanjan, H Sindhu, M R Shubha, M Lavanya, S Pramod, V Kusum Devi, Marcin Gackowski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSystemic glucocorticoids are effective in rheumatoid arthritis, but long-term exposure carries dose-dependent risks. We developed a “nano-in-micro” platform in which cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs; <100 nm) are embedded within polymeric microspheres (about 100–400 μm) to modulate dexamethasone release while preserving drug identity and matrix integrity. Microsphere formulations varying in CNC and chitosan content were prepared and characterized at a fixed drug load. FT-IR and UV–Vis spectroscopy confirmed drug identity and assay linearity. Optical microscopy and SEM assessed particle size and morphology; CNCs were examined by TEM. Process yield, encapsulation efficiency (EE), equilibrium swelling, and cumulative drug release over 10 h were measured. Release data were fitted to zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, Hixson–Crowell, and Korsmeyer–Peppas models. Microspheres (predominantly 100–150 μm within a 100–400 μm design space) were produced with high process yields (approximately 85–89%) and moderate encapsulation efficiency (approximately 52–63%). Increasing cellulose nanocrystal (CNC) content reduced equilibrium swelling (from about 180% to about 150%) and slowed dexamethasone release over 10 h (from approximately 100% for chitosan-only to approximately 87% at the highest CNC loading). UV–Vis quantitation was highly linear (R² = 0.9876). Diffusion-based models best described release profiles (Higuchi, Korsmeyer–Peppas; R² ≥ 0.97), whereas first-order kinetics were comparatively stronger in the fastest-releasing, chitosan-rich formulations. Embedding CNCs within polymeric microspheres provides a controllable lever to tune swelling and diffusional pathways, enabling short-to-medium dexamethasone release profiles without compromising analytical identity or process robustness. The platform’s reproducible yields and predictable CNC-dependent slowing of release support further optimization (e.g., cross-link density, medium conditions) and in vivo evaluation toward dosing concepts that minimize systemic glucocorticoid exposure. Clinical applicability has not yet been established; in vivo biocompatibility, pharmacokinetic, and efficacy studies—aligned with medicinal‑product guidance—are required before any translational claims can be made.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00296-025-06030-y.Keywords: Rheumatoid arthritis, Dexamethasone, Microspheres, Chitosan, Cellulose, Drug delivery systems

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic autoimmune disease marked by chronic synovial inflammation, progressive cartilage destruction, joint deformity, and disability [1–6]. It is the most prevalent systemic autoimmune rheumatic disease (SARD), with an estimated prevalence of 0.63% in females and 0.24% in males in Western Europe, reaching up to 2% in individuals over 60 years of age [3]. Symptoms include tender and swollen joints, morning stiffness, fatigue, and appetite loss, typically starting in smaller joints before progressing to larger ones [4]. The etiology of RA is multifactorial, involving both genetic and environmental risk factors such as age, female gender, family history, smoking, and obesity [5]. If untreated, RA may lead to serious complications, including bone loss, cardiovascular and pulmonary involvement, nerve compression, and increased infection risk [6]. Diagnosis relies on clinical examination, laboratory testing (RF, anti-CCP), and imaging methods such as X-ray, ultrasound, or MRI [7]. While not curative, current treatment strategies aim to suppress symptoms and slow disease progression. These include nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), corticosteroids, conventional disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), and biologic agents. Lifestyle modification, physiotherapy, and in severe cases, surgical interventions complement pharmacological therapy [8, 9]. Current EULAR recommendations emphasize a treat-to-target strategy with minimal exposure to systemic glucocorticoids, favoring short-term bridging where needed and tapering once remission is sustained [10].

Corticosteroids such as dexamethasone remain an essential option for rapid control of inflammation and symptom flares in RA. However, chronic systemic use is constrained by well-known adverse effects, including osteoporosis, metabolic disturbances, increased infection risk, and adrenal suppression. The challenge in RA therapy is therefore to maintain the anti-inflammatory benefits of corticosteroids while minimizing systemic toxicity. This therapeutic gap has stimulated growing interest in advanced drug delivery systems that enable localized, sustained release at inflamed joints [11]. Dexamethasone is a synthetic fluorinated glucocorticoid with potent anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive activity. It is approximately 25 times more powerful than hydrocortisone and has negligible mineralocorticoid effects, which makes it suitable for long-term management of chronic inflammatory conditions [12, 13]. The compound is a white crystalline powder with a melting point of about 262 °C and exhibits poor aqueous solubility, leading to variable oral bioavailability. Dexamethasone is well absorbed after oral administration, with a plasma half-life of 3–4.5 h, but its biological half-life (36–54 h) is prolonged due to sustained receptor binding and genomic effects. The drug is metabolized primarily in the liver via CYP3A4 and excreted in the urine. Clinically, dexamethasone is administered orally, intravenously, intramuscularly, or intra-articularly, depending on the indication. Its poor solubility and systemic adverse effects highlight the importance of novel delivery systems, including nanoformulations, to improve solubility, prolong release, and reduce systemic toxicity [14].

Nanotechnology-based drug carriers can improve the therapeutic index of anti-rheumatic agents by prolonging systemic circulation, enhancing accumulation in inflamed synovial tissue, and reducing off-target effects [8, 15, 16]. Natural polymers such as chitosan are desirable due to their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and mucoadhesive properties. Cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs), derived from renewable sources, provide structural reinforcement within polymeric matrices, decrease swelling capacity, and modulate diffusional pathways, enabling more sustained release profiles [17, 18].

We hypothesized that integrating CNCs into chitosan microspheres would yield a polyelectrolyte complex capable of slowing dexamethasone diffusion, thereby achieving sustained anti-inflammatory activity with reduced systemic exposure. This study aimed to develop and characterize chitosan–CNC microspheres for dexamethasone delivery, assess their physicochemical and morphological properties, and evaluate their in vitro release kinetics as a function of CNC content. This approach provides a rational nanotechnology-based strategy for optimizing corticosteroid therapy in rheumatoid arthritis [19]. This work delineates a CNC-tunable, nano-in-micro chitosan platform that offers a short-to-medium dexamethasone release profile (about 10 h), distinguishing it from week-scale intra-articular depots and systemic liposomal carriers [20–23].

Materials and methods

Reagents

Dexamethasone (≥ 99% purity) was obtained from Cipla Pvt. Ltd. (Mumbai, India). Chitosan (medium molecular weight, 75–85% deacetylated, analytical grade) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Bangalore, India). Gelatin (Type B, 240 Bloom, pharmaceutical grade) was obtained from S D Fine Chemicals (Mumbai, India). Other reagents—including petroleum ether, potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KH₂PO₄), disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na₂HPO₄), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), potassium chloride (KCl), and light liquid paraffin—were provided by S D Fine Chemicals (Mumbai, India). All solvents and salts were of analytical grade. Additional materials included spectroscopic‑grade potassium bromide (KBr; Sigma‑Aldrich, Bangalore, India) for FT‑IR pellet preparation; sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄, 30% w/w; S D Fine Chemicals, Mumbai, India) for CNC hydrolysis; barium chloride (BaCl₂; S D Fine Chemicals, Mumbai, India) for qualitative sulfate testing after dialysis; and deionized water produced in‑house (Milli‑Q system, Merck Millipore). A gold sputtering target (99.99%; Ted Pella, USA) was used for SEM sample preparation.

Experimental procedures

In this study, the drug carriers are polymeric microspheres (~ 100–400 μm) that contain dispersed cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs) (nanoparticles, < 100 nm). This nano-in-micro architecture forms nanocomposite microspheres. CNCs act as nano-reinforcements within the polymer matrix—attenuating swelling and modulating diffusional pathways—thereby tuning encapsulation efficiency and drug-release kinetics. Accordingly, “nano-enabled microspheres” denotes microscale carriers incorporating nanoparticles, not nanoparticle-sized carriers.

FT-IR spectroscopy

Identification of a pure drug

Dexamethasone was identified using a Bruker Alpha series instrument using FTIR spectroscopy with the KBr pellet method. Dexamethasone’s FTIR spectrum was obtained by mixing it with KBr, compressing it at 600 kg/cm², and scanning within 4000–500 cm⁻¹. The spectra were then compared with standard references for confirmation.

Drug–excipient compatibility study

Assessing dexamethasone’s compatibility with excipients is essential for formulation stability. FTIR analysis (4000–500 cm⁻¹) was performed on pure drug and excipient mixtures to identify interactions. Samples were triturated with KBr, analyzed, and compared with standard spectra. Early compatibility testing aids in selecting excipients that enhance stability, bioavailability, and formulation quality [24].

UV spectroscopy and analytical method for dexamethasone

Pre-formulation begins with developing a simple analytical method for quantitative analysis. Most drugs absorb UV light (190–390 nm) due to aromatic rings or double bonds. A Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan) was used to determine the maximum absorbance wavelength (λ_max) of dexamethasone in phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). A standard calibration curve was constructed to quantify drug loading and release. Dexamethasone’s λmax was determined as 242 nm using a pH 7.4 buffer, with various concentrations analyzed for calibration [25].

Preparation

Preparation and purification of cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs)

CNCs were obtained in-house by sulfuric acid hydrolysis of cellulose, followed by repeated washing, sonication, and dialysis to neutral pH. The resulting suspension was freeze-dried to yield CNC powder with high crystallinity. Bleached surgical cotton was washed with deionized water and hydrolyzed using 30% sulfuric acid at 60 °C for 4 h with sonication. The pH was adjusted to 6–7 using NaOH, followed by probe sonication for 45 min. The CNC suspension was dialyzed for 24 h to remove residual salts, confirmed by BaCl₂ testing. After purification, the nanosuspension was concentrated and analyzed using TEM [26]. Preparation of dexamethasone‑loaded microspheres

Microspheres were prepared by spray-drying polyelectrolyte complexes of chitosan and CNCs containing dexamethasone. Process parameters (inlet temperature, feed rate, and airflow) were optimized to achieve uniform spherical morphology and reproducible yields. The qualitative and quantitative composition of formulations F1–F7 is provided in Table 2. The formulated nanocomposite was processed using a spray dryer to obtain microspheres. The solution was fed into the spray dryer under controlled conditions, with an inlet temperature of 140 °C and an outlet temperature of 70 °C. The instrument was operated at a variable frequency drive (VFD) setting of 32, an atomization pressure of 1.8–2 bar, and a feed rate of 4 mL/min [27].

The dosage ranges in Table 2 were selected a priori to maintain a feed viscosity compatible with stable two-fluid atomization (4 mL/min; 1.8–2 bar) and to prevent nozzle clogging; to promote formation of a chitosan–CNC polyelectrolyte complex while avoiding phase separation; to yield spherical, non-collapsed microspheres within a target 100–400 μm size distribution; to deliver robust process yields (approximately 85–89%) and moderate drug entrapment efficiency (approximately 52–63%); and to span a CNC gradient sufficient to modulate swelling and diffusional release over about 10 h while retaining an all-aqueous process. Chitosan grade, solvent system, and spray-drying conditions were held constant across F1–F7 to isolate the effect of CNC loading. Compositions outside this range produced unstable feeds (excess viscosity) or brittle, low-yield powders (excess CNC) and were not advanced in this exploratory set.

Characterization (SEM)

SEM analysis with a JEOL JSM-6360 microscope was performed to evaluate microsphere size and surface morphology. Samples were mounted on a brass stub, gold-coated via ion sputtering, and scanned randomly for detailed surface characterization in drug delivery studies [29].

In vitro dissolution study

Drug release from PEC microspheres with varying drug-polymer concentrations was assessed using a USP XXIII dissolution apparatus. Testing was conducted in a pH 1.2 buffer for 2 h, then in a pH 7.4 buffer for 10 h at 100 rpm. Release testing employed a two-stage screen (pH 1.2 followed by pH 7.4) commonly used for preliminary assessment and cross-study comparability. This design brackets acidic and near-neutral conditions and was not intended to mimic synovial fluid. Protein- and hyaluronan-rich synovial media (near-neutral pH, physiological ionic strength, albumin/globulins, hyaluronan/lubricin) can alter diffusion and matrix hydration; evaluating release in simulated synovial fluid will be the focus of follow-on work [30]. A 100 mg sample was dissolved in 900 mL of medium at 37 °C, with 5 mL samples collected hourly and replaced. Drug concentration was analyzed at 242 nm using a UV spectrophotometer, with experiments performed in triplicate [31].

Kinetic modeling and analysis

Model fitting of in‑vitro release data

Drug release kinetics were analyzed using the models of zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, Hixson–Crowell, and Korsmeyer–Peppas. Data fitting and regression analyses were performed using standard least-squares procedures; the model with the highest coefficient of determination (R²) was considered the best fit for the release mechanism [32].

Analysis of release kinetics

To analyze the release kinetics and mechanism of drug release from microcapsules, the in vitro release data were fitted to standard mathematical models, including zero-order (Qt = K0t), first-order (lnQt = lnQ0 − K1t), and the diffusion-based Higuchi model (Qt = Kht1/2) [33]. Qt represents the percentage of drug released at time t, Q0 is the initial drug content, and K0, K1, and Kh are the rate constants for zero-order, first-order, and Higuchi models, respectively. Because such models do not fully capture release influenced by matrix swelling and/or relaxation, the data were also analyzed with the Korsmeyer–Peppas power law, which is widely applied to polymeric systems to distinguish Fickian vs. non-Fickian behavior [34, 35].

Statistical analysis

Unless stated otherwise, all experiments were performed in independent triplicate (n = 3). Data are summarized as mean ± SD with n indicated for each assay. Given the exploratory, in vitro design and limited replication, no formal between-formulation hypothesis testing was performed; differences between formulations are described qualitatively, and p-values are not reported. No data were excluded, and no outliers were removed. For comparability, cumulative dexamethasone release at 2, 6, and 10 h is reported as mean ± SD (n = 3). Ordinary least squares fitted release kinetics to zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, Hixson–Crowell, and Korsmeyer–Peppas models; R² summarizes goodness-of-fit, and model parameters are reported where appropriate. No inferential comparison between models was undertaken. Analyses (curve fitting and plotting) were performed in R (version 4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Reporting guideline

In line with EQUATOR Network’s recommendations for using an appropriate reporting standard, this in vitro laboratory study was prepared per the CRIS Checklist for Reporting In-vitro Studies [36]. We also aligned key transparency items with the MDAR (Materials, Design, Analysis, Reporting) framework to improve clarity on reagents, blinding/randomization applicability, replication, statistics, and data availability [37].

Discussion

Contemporary rheumatoid arthritis management increasingly prioritizes minimizing systemic glucocorticoid exposure while preserving disease control, with non-pharmacologic and pharmacologic strategies converging on steroid-sparing care. Despite the rapid anti-inflammatory effects of glucocorticoids, the risk–benefit balance of chronic therapy remains debated, reinforcing the rationale for delivery approaches that reduce systemic exposure while preserving efficacy [38]. A feasibility randomized trial of a physiotherapist-led behaviour-change programme improved physical activity in RA, illustrating how adjunctive interventions can contribute to global risk reduction [39]. Emerging patient-centred evidence also highlights how more explicit physical-activity guidance within routine rheumatology can support risk-reduction strategies that minimize cumulative glucocorticoid exposure while maintaining disease control [40]. At the mechanistic level, a recent pilot study reported that JAK inhibitors enhanced ATP production and mitochondrial function in RA, aligning to normalize cellular bioenergetics and further supporting steroid-sparing paradigms [41]. In parallel, clinical guidance on foot and ankle management emphasizes functional, joint-focused outcomes and reducing treatment burden, which resonates with targeted, sustained local delivery approaches such as chitosan–CNC microspheres [42].

Positioned against current efforts to minimize systemic glucocorticoid exposure while preserving disease control, our work adds a materials-based, nano-in-micro microsphere platform that offers a tunable, short-to-medium dexamethasone release profile (approximately 10 h). By varying cellulose nanocrystal (CNC) content across otherwise matched formulations, we demonstrate head-to-head, causal structure–property–performance relationships: higher CNC loadings reproducibly reduce swelling and slow diffusional release, while maintaining moderate encapsulation efficiency (approximately 52–63%) and robust process yields (approximately 85–89%). Mechanistically, CNCs function as rigid, hydrogen-bonding nano-reinforcements that densify the chitosan network, limit solvent ingress, and increase diffusional tortuosity, in line with prior observations for CNC-polymer systems [43–46]. Such controllability could flatten systemic peaks and reduce overall steroid exposure—for example, within local/compartmental delivery concepts or multiparticulate oral regimens—without sacrificing rapid anti-inflammatory action. In this context, tunable short-to-medium release profiles are directly relevant to strategies that limit cumulative glucocorticoid burden without sacrificing rapid flare control in RA [47]. Although the absolute difference in 10-h cumulative release across the CNC gradient is modest (≈ 99% vs. ≈ 87%), the shift in early-time release rate and reduction of the burst fraction are consistent with increased diffusional tortuosity; such control is expected to flatten systemic peaks and extend local exposure in joint-targeted use. However, whether this magnitude is clinically meaningful cannot be determined from the present in vitro data and will require in vivo PK/PD and comparative efficacy studies.

Compared with other glucocorticoid carriers, liposomal dexamethasone—including targeted constructs—has shown enhanced joint targeting and anti-inflammatory efficacy in arthritis models [20]. Long-acting intra-articular depots based on PLGA microspheres, exemplified by triamcinolone acetonide extended-release, prolong synovial residence and lower systemic exposure over weeks [21, 22]. In situ–forming phospholipid gels can achieve sustained joint exposure while remaining injectable [23]. Against this backdrop, our chitosan–CNC “nano‑in‑micro” microspheres occupy a complementary space: by adjusting CNC content, they offer a short‑to‑medium, tunable release window (about 10 h) with high process yield and moderate encapsulation efficiency, which may be helpful when shorter steroid exposure is desirable (e.g., local/compartmental dosing or as modular building blocks toward longer‑acting depots) while aiming to limit systemic peaks and overall exposure.

SEM and particle-size data confirm that the carriers fall within the intended microsphere design space (100–400 μm). At the same time, TEM shows rod-like cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs) with high aspect ratios embedded in the matrix. As CNC loading increases, equilibrium swelling decreases (e.g., from ~ 180% to ~ 150% across formulations; see Table 6), consistent with nanofilled chitosan systems in which CNC–polymer hydrogen bonding and percolating rigid domains limit matrix relaxation and water uptake. In turn, cumulative release curves separate monotonically by CNC content, with CNC-rich batches releasing more slowly at matched time points (e.g., ~ 100% → ~87% at 10 h; see Table 7 / Fig. 8). This pattern aligns with prior reports that CNCs stiffen polysaccharide networks and increase diffusional tortuosity, thereby restricting solvent ingress and drug transport [43, 44, 46].

Kinetic fitting (Table 8; Figs. 9 and 10) showed that Higuchi and Korsmeyer–Peppas models best captured the data for most formulations, with typical R² ≥ 0.97, indicating diffusion-dominated or anomalous transport through a hydrated, porous matrix. First-order fits were comparatively competitive in faster-releasing, chitosan-rich batches (e.g., F1–F2), consistent with higher early-time drug liberation. This pattern matches established behavior in chitosan-based matrices, where Fickian diffusion and polymer relaxation govern release [46, 48]. FT-IR spectra corroborated drug identity, and the UV–visible assay in phosphate buffer at λ_max = 242 nm showed good linearity (R² = 0.9876), supporting routine quantitation (Figs. 1 and 2; Table 3). These analytical characteristics underpinned drug-content determinations and % cumulative drug release measurements across all formulations and are consistent with established UV–Vis workflows for small-molecule assays in phosphate media [45].

Glucocorticoids remain pivotal for the rapid control of rheumatoid arthritis activity. Still, contemporary cohort data show a dose- and duration-dependent increase in major cardiovascular events, detectable even at ≤ 5 mg/day, underscoring the need to minimize systemic exposure and taper promptly when disease control is achieved [49, 50]. Real-world practice patterns likewise indicate a shift toward more cautious use and earlier discontinuation of glucocorticoids in routine RA care [51]. Against this backdrop, carrier-enabled steroid delivery is attracting attention as a way to preserve anti-inflammatory benefit while reducing systemic peaks: targeted liposomal dexamethasone has demonstrated enhanced joint accumulation and efficacy in arthritis models [14], and randomized data with long-circulating liposomal prednisolone have shown superior flare control versus intramuscular methylprednisolone [52]. Our CNC-reinforced microspheres contribute to this rationale by providing short-to-medium release windows compatible with multiparticulate oral concepts or combination regimens designed to flatten peak–trough exposure and lower systemic burden. At the same time, the CNC-driven reduction in swelling and diffusivity observed here supports a tunable platform that could be adapted to different clinical constraints on glucocorticoid dosing.

This work combines reproducible fabrication (process yield approximately 85–89%) with internal analytical confirmation (FT‑IR/UV; calibration R² = 0.9876) and a clear structure–property–performance link: increasing CNC loading reduces swelling and proportionally slows dexamethasone diffusion while maintaining encapsulation efficiency approximately 52–63%. Across otherwise matched formulations, multi‑model kinetic fits (zero‑, first‑order, Higuchi, Korsmeyer–Peppas) showed consistently high R², supporting mechanistic interpretability and tunability over a short‑to‑medium release window (about 10 h). From a materials and translational perspective, the use of biocompatible, readily available components (chitosan, cellulose nanocrystals) and a fully aqueous workflow supports scalability and regulatory plausibility while aiming to limit systemic peaks and cumulative glucocorticoid exposure without sacrificing rapid anti‑inflammatory effects in RA.

This study was exploratory and focused on descriptive head-to-head comparisons across formulations; accordingly, no formal between-group inference was performed, and kinetic-model selection relied on R². In future confirmatory work, we will pre-specify between-group tests and additional model-selection metrics, and undertake formal design-of-experiments optimization with multi-objective criteria (e.g., release targets, EE/yield thresholds) across a broadened design space. To strengthen the evidence base, pre-specified ANOVA (or Kruskal–Wallis) with appropriate post-hoc procedures (e.g., Dunnett/Tukey) will be applied to EE, swelling, and selected % cumulative drug-release time points with multiplicity control; and R² will be complemented by adjusted R² and information criteria (preferably AICc for small n). Future studies should also report the Korsmeyer–Peppas exponent (n) with confidence intervals to distinguish Fickian vs. non-Fickian regimes, map the influence of cross-link density and medium conditions (pH/ionic strength) on swelling and diffusional tortuosity, and extend to in vivo models to benchmark against state-of-the-art steroid nanotherapies and targeted carriers [14, 43, 44, 46]. Additionally, the present dissolution media (pH 1.2/7.4) were intended for screening and do not reproduce the macromolecular/rheological milieu of inflamed synovial fluid; planned studies will quantify release in synovial-like media containing albumin/globulins and hyaluronan at near-neutral pH and physiological ionic strength [53]. No in vivo biocompatibility or efficacy data were collected in this work; future nonclinical studies will follow established medicinal‑product guidance (e.g., ICH M3(R2) and relevant EMA/FDA expectations) and will include single‑ and repeat‑dose intra‑articular studies with local tissue histopathology, biodistribution/pharmacokinetics, and comparative efficacy in RA‑relevant models.

Conclusions

This study establishes a nano-in-micro platform in which cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs; <100 nm) are embedded within polymeric microspheres (about 100–400 μm) to tune dexamethasone delivery. The formulations were fabricated reproducibly (process yield approximately 85–89%) and quantified using a UV–Vis assay with excellent linearity (R² = 0.9876). Across batches, increasing CNC content reduced equilibrium swelling and slowed release over 10 h while maintaining moderate encapsulation efficiency (approximately 52–63%). Kinetic fits favored Higuchi and Korsmeyer–Peppas models, consistent with diffusion-dominated/anomalous transport in a hydrated, CNC-reinforced matrix. These results demonstrate that CNC loading is a controllable lever for programming short-to-medium release profiles without compromising drug identity or matrix integrity, highlighting a practical route to modulate glucocorticoid exposure. Future directions include in vivo studies to define pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, joint accumulation, and safety; optimization of cross-link density and medium sensitivity (pH/ionic strength) to broaden the tunable design space; stability and scale-up assessments (process robustness, storage); and benchmarking against established steroid carriers in models relevant to rheumatoid arthritis. Collectively, these steps will clarify translational performance and the potential of this platform to support dosing strategies that limit systemic glucocorticoid burden while preserving therapeutic efficacy.
