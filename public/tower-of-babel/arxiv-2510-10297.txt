A continued fraction approximation for the effective elasticity tensor of two-dimensional polycrystals as a function of the crystal elasticity tensor
Graeme W. Milton

Abstract
. For two-dimensional polycrystals the effective elasticity tensor 𝐂 ∗ {\bf C}_{*} as a function 𝐂 ∗ ​ ( 𝐂 0 ) {\bf C}_{*}({\bf C}_{0}) of the elasticity tensor 𝐂 0 {\bf C}_{0} of the constituent crystal is considered. It is shown that this function can be approximated by one with a continued fraction expansion resembling that associated with a class of microstructure known as sequential laminates. These are hierarchical microstructures defined inductively. Rank 0 sequential laminates are simply rotations of the pure crystal. Rank j j sequential laminates are obtained by laminating together, on a length scale much larger that the existing microstructure and with interfaces perpendicular to some direction 𝐧 j {\bf n}_{j} , rank j − 1 j-1 sequential laminates with a rotation of the pure crystal. The continued fraction approximation for arbitrary polycrystal microstructures typically takes a more general form than that of sequential laminates, but has some free parameters. It is an open question as to whether these free parameters can always be adjusted so the continued fraction approximation matches exactly that of a sequential laminate. If so, one would have established that the elastic response of two-dimensional polycrystals can always be mimicked by that of sequential laminates. Our analysis carries over to the more general case where the strain is replaced by a field 𝐄 ⁡ ( 𝐱 ) {\bf E}({\bf x}) that is the gradient of a vector potential 𝐮 ⁡ ( 𝐱 ) {\bf u}({\bf x}) , i.e. 𝐄 = ∇ 𝐮 {\bf E}=\nabla{\bf u} and the stress is replaced by a matrix valued field 𝐉 ⁡ ( 𝐱 ) {\bf J}({\bf x}) that need not be symmetric but has zero divergence ∇ ⋅ 𝐉 = 0 \nabla\cdot{\bf J}=0 . The tensor 𝐋 ⁡ ( 𝐱 ) {\bf L}({\bf x}) entering the constitutive relation 𝐉 = 𝐋𝐄 {\bf J}={\bf L}{\bf E} is locally a rotation of the tensor 𝐋 0 {\bf L}_{0} of the pure crystal that need not have any special symmetries and has 16 independent tensor elements.

1.
Introduction

It is a pleasure writing this article in honor of Luc Tartar who, beside many other works, made pioneering contributions to the subject of homogenization theory, to bounds on the effective tensors that according to homogenization theory govern the macroscopic response of composites, and to obtaining formulas for the effective tensors of laminates, often in collaboration with Francois Murat.

This article concerns two-dimensional polycrystals, a composite of grains of a single anisotropic pure crystal rotated according to the grain orientation, as illustrated in Figure 1. Our primary interest is the function
{\bf C}_{*}({\bf C}^{(0)})
giving the effective elasticity tensor
{\bf C}_{*}
as a function of the elasticity tensor
{\bf C}^{(0)}
of the pure crystal. The goal is to show that it can be approximated to an arbitrarily high degree of approximation by a continued fraction having a close relation with the continued fraction for microstructures known as sequential laminates, which will be described in the next section. After approximating the Hilbert space to a finite dimensional one,
the crux of our analysis will be a dimension counting argument showing that certain subspaces have a non-trivial intersection. This reduces the problem to a simpler one, where the dimension counting argument can be employed again, and so forth, until one arrives at an elementary problem that is easily solved. The effective tensors at successive stages are linked, and elimination of the intermediate effective tensors gives rise to the continued fraction.

To be clear, it still remains an open question as to whether in general
{\bf C}_{*}({\bf C}^{(0)})
can always be approximated by the function for some sequential laminate, rather than only by continued fractions having a more general form.
Our continued fraction expansion provides a representation of
{\bf C}_{*}({\bf C}^{(0)})
. Besides the intrinsic interest, this itself is useful as representation formulas
for effective tensors as a function of the tensors of the constituent materials allow one to obtain bounds on the response of
composites: see, for example, Chapters 18, 27, 28 in
[
28
]
and
accompanying references. There is a connection between many of these bounds and bounds on Herglotz and Stieltjes functions. These have a long history dating back to works of Nevanlinna and Pick: see
[
20
]
and references therein.

2.
Motivation

Some background is needed to motivate this study. As composites can exhibit different properties and sometimes strikingly different properties than their constituent materials, there is considerable interest in establishing the range of properties a composite may have and identifying microstructures that achieve desired properties in this range.
A beautiful example is that of Murat and Tartar
[
34
]
and independently Lurie and Cherkaev
[
22
]
, who completely characterized the set of effective conductivity tensors
\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}_{*}
, allowing for anisotropic ones, of three-dimensional composites of two isotropic conducting phases mixed in fixed proportions. They obtained bounds and then showed that any effective conductivity tensor compatible with the bounds is realized by a hierarchical laminate geometry whose conception goes back to Maxwell
[
23
]
.
Hierarchical laminates are multiscale structures defined recursively. At the first stage one defines the constituent phases as rank 0 laminates. To obtain a hierarchical laminate of rank
r\geq 1
one laminates together laminates of rank
r-1
with laminates of rank at most
r-1
. The direction of lamination varies at the different stages of lamination, and there is a wide separation of length scales between laminations so one can use the effective tensors of the constituent hierarchical laminates
to obtain the effective tensor at the next stage. Alternatively, any effective conductivity tensor attaining the bounds of Tartar, Murat, Lurie and Cherkaev, is realized by an assemblage of coated ellipsoids, a geometry whose conception goes back to the papers
[
24
,
25
]
and which generalizes the coated sphere assemblage construction of Hashin
[
13
]
and Hashin and Shtrikman
[
14
]
. Assemblages of doubly coated ellipsoids attain points inside the bounds.

A constant theme is that in many instances hierarchical laminates can have unusual properties and often achieve bounds. In fact, it is hard to identify microstructures whose properties of interest cannot be replicated by hierarchical laminates built from
the same constituent materials. Personally, I initially thought it was impossible to find hierarchical laminates having a Poisson ratio arbitrarily close to
-1
until I found one incorporating chevron type elements
[
27
]

(a related construction was later independently discovered by Larsen, Sigmund, and Bouwstra
[
21
]
and has been used as a protective layer in Bontrager bicycle helmets, where the negative Poisson ratio assists in getting the desired positive curvature of the layer). Similarly, I had thought
[
30
]
it impossible
to find hierarchical laminates that achieve a sign change of the effective Hall coefficient, a counterexample to the standard belief that the sign of the charge carriers determines the sign of the Hall coefficient. Certain interlocking ring structures were proven to have the sign inversion property
[
2
]
, then the design was simplified and verified numerically

[
15
]
and experimentally
[
18
]
. An alternative knotted structure having the sign inversion property was later conceived
[
19
]
. Much to my surprise, Christian Kern found a hierarchical laminate exhibiting the sign change
[
17
]
. On the other hand, an example of Sverak
[
35
]
has led to
a seven phase composite whose effective elasticity tensor cannot be replicated by a hierarchical laminate composed from the same phases (Section 31.9 in
[
28
]
) and Grabovsky has an example of an exact relation for effective tensors that is satisfied by hierarchical laminate geometries, but not for some microstructures
[
11
]
.

A much deeper question is to look at the effective tensor as a function of the tensors, or relevant moduli, of the constituent phases and ask whether this effective tensor function for an arbitrary microstructure can always be replicated by a suitable
hierarchical laminate microstructure. An example is the proof
[
31
]
that sequential laminates can reproduce to an arbitrarily high degree of approximation the effective conductivity tensor
\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}_{*}
as a function
\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}_{*}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
of the conductivities
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
of two isotropic phases for two-dimensional, possibly anisotropic composites of these phases. Sequential laminates are a subclass of hierarchical laminates, again defined recursively: one begins with a trivial sequential laminate which is simple laminate of the constituent phases
then at each successive stage one laminates one of the constituent phases with a sequential laminate obtained at a previous stage.
Key to this correspondence was that the function
\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}_{*}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
in two dimensions satisfies
Keller’s phase interchange identity
[
16
]
.

(2.1)

\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}_{*}(\sigma_{2},\sigma_{1})=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}_{*}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})/\det\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}_{*}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}),

in addition to analyticity, homogenity, normalization, and Herglotz properties analogous to those mentioned in the last section.
Related continued fraction expansions
[
32
]
can be obtained more generally without assuming (
2.1
), though in this case one cannot generally make a correspondence with sequential laminate geometries.

There are also related results concerning two-dimensional conducting polycrystals, comprised of grains of anisotropic pure crystal with conductivity
\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}^{(0)}
rotated according to the grain orientation. Dykhne
[
5
]
showed that the effective tensor

of the polycrystal satisfies
\det\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}_{*}=\det\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}^{(0)}

then elementary variational principles imply
\lambda^{+}{\bf I}\geq\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}_{*}\geq\lambda^{-}{\bf I}
. Putting these together, if
\lambda^{+}_{*}
,
\lambda^{-}_{*}
are the maximum and minimum eigenvalues of
\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}_{*}
then these satisfy
\lambda^{+}_{*}\lambda^{-}_{*}=\lambda^{+}\lambda^{-}
and

\lambda^{-}_{*}\leq\lambda^{+}_{*}\leq\lambda^{+}
. This is fact characterizes all possible effective conductivity tensors
\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}_{*}
. It is easy to see that a
simple laminate of the crystal with conductivity
\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}^{(0)}
layered in the direction of the eigenvector of
\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}^{(0)}
with the same crystal rotated by
90^{\circ}
achieves any pair of
\lambda^{+}_{*}
,
\lambda^{-}_{*}
compatible with these constrains. (Subsequently a complete characterization was
given of the possible effective conductivity tensors

[

A deeper result was obtained by Clark and Milton
[
4

and Clark
[
3

who showed
the underlying Hilbert space structure, and hence the effective conductivity tensor
\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}_{*}
as a function
\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}_{*}(\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}^{(0)})
of the pure crystal conductivity tensor
\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}^{(0)}
could be mimicked to an arbitrary degree of approximation by sequential laminates, as for example illustrated in Figure 2. Sequential laminates are a subset of hierarchical laminates: at the first stage one still defines the constituent phases as rank 0 laminates, and then to obtain a sequential laminate of rank
r\geq 1
one laminates sequential laminates of rank
r-1
with one or more of the constituent

This correspondence then led to a continued fraction expansion that approximates the function
\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}_{*}(\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}^{(0)})

Our hope was to obtain a similar result as Clark and Milton
and Clark but for elasticity in two-dimensional polycrystals, i.e., characterizing all possible functions
{\bf C}_{*}({\bf C}^{(0)})
of the effective elasticity tensor
{\bf C}_{*}
as a function of the elasticity tensor
{\bf C}^{(0)}
of the constituent crystal. Specifically, we hoped to show that the function
{\bf C}_{*}({\bf C}^{(0)})
can be approximated to an arbitrarily high degree of approximation by the function for a sequential laminate geometry and such functions are easily characterized through continued fraction expansions.
Some indication that this might be true comes from a result of Avellaneda Cherkaev, Gibiansky, Milton and Rudelson

[
1
]
,, who obtained a complete characterization of all possible isotropic
{\bf C}_{*}
for a fixed real
{\bf C}^{(0)}>0
having orthotropic symmetry. They found that
the effective bulk modulus
\kappa_{*}
and effective shear modulus
\mu_{*}
are confined to a rectangular box in the
(\kappa_{*},\mu_{*})

plane and that any point within this rectangle is realized by a hierarchical laminate geometry. For crystals having more general symmetry
the moduli are confined to a box, with two corners being attained by hierarchical laminate geometries, and numerical results strongly indicating that a third corner is attained by a hierarchical laminate geometry
[
29
]
. The attainability of the fourth corner by a hierarchical laminate geometry was not established, but seems likely.

What we accomplish is more modest. We obtain a continued fraction approximation to
{\bf C}_{*}({\bf C}^{(0)})
that generalizes the form of the continued fractions associated with
sequential laminates.

The physical relevance of this result applies to three dimensional materials with a columnar structure with columns perpendicular
to a plane each made from a single crystal rotated in the plane. Provided the single crystal has sufficient symmetry and has the right orientation relative to the direction of each column the three-dimensional elasticity equations decouple into antiplane elasticity equations and the two-dimensional elasticity equations that are the focus of our study (see, for example, Section 2.7 in
[
28
]
). Furthermore, if the applied fields are time varying at a frequency
\omega
and quasistatic (i.e. such that the macroscopic wavelengths and attenuation lengths are much larger than the microstructure) then
{\bf C}^{(0)}={\bf C}^{(0)}(\omega)
depends on
\omega
and may be complex if viscoelasticity is present. The function
{\bf C}_{*}({\bf C}^{(0)})
then gives the dependence of
{\bf C}_{*}
on
\omega
:
{\bf C}_{*}(\omega)={\bf C}_{*}({\bf C}^{(0)}(\omega))
.

\begin{pmatrix}b_{11}\cos\theta+b_{12}\sin\theta\cr b_{21}\cos\theta+b_{22}\sin\theta\cr-b_{11}\sin\theta+b_{12}\cos\theta\cr-b_{21}\sin\theta+b_{22}\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}=({\bf I}\cos\theta+{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\sin\theta)\begin{pmatrix}b_{11}\cr b_{21}\cr b_{12}\cr b_{22}\end{pmatrix}.

In other words, the full rotation acting on the matrix
{\bf A}
is represented by

(3.12)

({\bf I}\cos\theta+{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\sin\theta)({\bf I}\cos\theta+{\bf R}_{\perp}\sin\theta)

acting on the vector
{\bf A}
.
The constitutive law is

(3.13)

{\bf J}({\bf x})={\bf L}({\bf x}){\bf E}({\bf x}),\quad{\bf J}\in{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal U}$}}\oplus{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal J}$}},\quad{\bf E}\in{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal U}$}}\oplus{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}},

where, as we have a polycrystal, the
4\times 4
matrix

takes the form

(3.14)

{\bf L}({\bf x})={\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}({\bf x}){\bf R}({\bf x}){\bf L}^{(0)}{\bf R}^{T}({\bf x}){\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}^{T}({\bf x}),

in which
{\bf L}^{(0)}
is the tensor of the pure crystal while
{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}({\bf x})
and
{\bf R}({\bf x})
are the rotations.

(3.15)

{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}({\bf x})={\bf I}\cos\theta({\bf x})+{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\sin\theta({\bf x}),\quad{\bf R}({\bf x})={\bf I}\cos\theta({\bf x})+{\bf R}_{\perp}\sin\theta({\bf x}).

In this representation
{\bf L}^{(0)}
is represented by a
8\times 8

{\bf L}^{(0)}=\begin{pmatrix}L^{(0)}_{11}&L^{(0)}_{12}&L^{(0)}_{13}&L^{(0)}_{14}\cr L^{(0)}_{21}&L^{(0)}_{22}&L^{(0)}_{23}&L^{(0)}_{24}\cr L^{(0)}_{31}&L^{(0)}_{32}&L^{(0)}_{33}&L^{(0)}_{34}\cr L^{(0)}_{41}&L^{(0)}_{42}&L^{(0)}_{43}&L^{(0)}_{44}\cr\end{pmatrix}.

Here
{\bf L}^{(0)}
could be the elasticity tensor
{\bf C}^{(0)}

a_{21}=-a_{12}

{\bf C}^{(0)}

We define

(3.17)

\chi({\bf x})={\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}({\bf x}){\bf R}({\bf x})\chi^{(0)}{\bf R}^{T}({\bf x}){\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}^{T}({\bf x}),

where

is the projection

(3.18)

\chi^{(0)}={\bf t}\otimes{\bf t},\quad\quad{\bf t}=\begin{pmatrix}1\cr 0\cr 0\cr 0\end{pmatrix}.

It has the properties that

(3.19)

\chi\chi=\chi,\quad\chi{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi=0,\quad\chi{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\chi=0,\text{ and }\chi{\bf R}_{\perp}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\chi=0.

(3.20)

\displaystyle\chi{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}({\bf x}){\bf R}({\bf x}){\bf t}\otimes{\bf t}{\bf R}^{T}({\bf x}){\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}^{T}({\bf x}){\bf R}_{\perp}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}({\bf x}){\bf R}({\bf x}){\bf t}\otimes{\bf t}{\bf R}^{T}({\bf x}){\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}^{T}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}({\bf x}){\bf R}({\bf x}){\bf t}({\bf t}\cdot{\bf R}_{\perp}{\bf t})\otimes{\bf t}{\bf R}^{T}({\bf x}){\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}^{T}({\bf x})=0,

and similar analysis implies to the third and fourth identities. Another relation follows from

(3.21)

{\bf t}\otimes{\bf t}-{\bf R}_{\perp}{\bf t}\otimes{\bf t}{\bf R}_{\perp}-{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf t}\otimes{\bf t}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}+{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}{\bf t}\otimes{\bf t}{\bf R}_{\perp}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}={\bf I},

which upon applying the rotations implies

(3.22)

\chi-{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi{\bf R}_{\perp}-{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\chi{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}+{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi{\bf R}_{\perp}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}={\bf I},

where the four terms on the left project onto four orthogonal subspaces.
Next note that
{\bf L}^{(0)}
can be expressed as a linear combination of 16 operators:

\displaystyle{\bf L}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L^{(0)}_{11}\chi^{(0)}+L^{(0)}_{12}\chi^{(0)}{\bf R}_{\perp}+L^{(0)}_{13}\chi^{(0)}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}+L^{(0)}_{14}\chi^{(0)}{\bf R}_{\perp}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle L^{(0)}_{21}{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi^{(0)}-L^{(0)}_{22}{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi^{(0)}{\bf R}_{\perp}-L^{(0)}_{23}{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi^{(0)}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}-L^{(0)}_{24}{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi^{(0)}{\bf R}_{\perp}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle L^{(0)}_{31}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\chi^{(0)}-L^{(0)}_{32}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\chi^{(0)}{\bf R}_{\perp}-L^{(0)}_{33}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\chi^{(0)}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}-L^{(0)}_{34}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\chi^{(0)}{\bf R}_{\perp}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle L^{(0)}_{41}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi^{(0)}+L^{(0)}_{42}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi^{(0)}{\bf R}_{\perp}+L^{(0)}_{43}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi^{(0)}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}+L^{(0)}_{44}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi^{(0)}{\bf R}_{\perp}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L^{(0)}_{11}\chi+L^{(0)}_{12}\chi{\bf R}_{\perp}+L^{(0)}_{13}\chi{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}+L^{(0)}_{14}\chi{\bf R}_{\perp}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle L^{(0)}_{21}{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi-L^{(0)}_{22}{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi{\bf R}_{\perp}-L^{(0)}_{23}{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}-L^{(0)}_{24}{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi{\bf R}_{\perp}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}

\displaystyle L^{(0)}_{41}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi+L^{(0)}_{42}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi{\bf R}_{\perp}+L^{(0)}_{43}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}+L^{(0)}_{44}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}\chi{\bf R}_{\perp}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}.

Correspondingly,

gets replaced by

\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}_{\theta_{0}}=(\cos\theta_{0}{\bf I}+\sin\theta_{0}{\bf R}_{\perp})(\cos\theta_{0}{\bf I}+\sin\theta_{0}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp})\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}(\cos\theta_{0}{\bf I}+\sin\theta_{0}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp})^{T}(\cos\theta_{0}{\bf I}+\sin\theta_{0}{\bf R}_{\perp})^{T},

Now, in Fourier space, the projection onto

(3.28)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\frac{{\bf k}\otimes{\bf k}}{|{\bf k}|^{2}}&0\cr 0&\frac{{\bf k}\otimes{\bf k}}{|{\bf k}|^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{if}\quad{\bf k}\neq 0

\displaystyle 0\quad\text{if}\quad{\bf k}=0,

while the projection onto

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{2}

\displaystyle 0\quad\text{if}\quad{\bf k}=0,

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\quad\text{if}\quad{\bf k}\neq 0

{\cal U}
,
{\cal E}

(3.31)

\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{0}+\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{2}={\bf I}.

These three projection operators commute with

{\bf R}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}=\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{2}

and

\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$}=\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$}=\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}-{\bf R}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\bf R}_{\perp}={\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}({\bf x})\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}&0\cr 0&0\end{pmatrix}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}^{T}({\bf x}),

we see that

(3.33)

\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{0}=\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{0}\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$},\quad\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}=\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$},\quad\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{2}=\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{2}\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$}.

The projection
{\bf P}
onto antisymmetric matrix valued fields takes the form

(3.34)

{\bf P}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0\cr 0&\frac{1}{2}&-\frac{1}{2}&0\cr 0&-\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}&0\cr 0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix},

which can be written alternatively as

(3.35)

{\bf P}=-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\bf R}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}^{(0)}{\bf R}_{\perp}+{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\bf R}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}^{(0)}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}+{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}^{(0)}{\bf R}_{\perp}-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}^{(0)}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp},

where
\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}^{(0)}={\bf t}\otimes{\bf t}
. The projection
{\bf P}
has the properties that

(3.36)

{\bf P}{\bf t}=0,\quad{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}{\bf P}={\bf P}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}={\bf P},\quad({\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}+{\bf R}_{\perp}){\bf P}={\bf P}({\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}+{\bf R}_{\perp})=0.

These imply that
{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}({\bf x}){\bf R}({\bf x})
commutes with
{\bf P}
,

(3.37)

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}({\bf x}){\bf R}({\bf x}){\bf P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[{\bf I}\cos^{2}\theta({\bf x})+({\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}+{\bf R}_{\perp})\sin\theta({\bf x})\cos\theta({\bf x})+{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}\sin^{2}\theta({\bf x})\right]{\bf P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf P}\left[{\bf I}\cos^{2}\theta({\bf x})+({\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}+{\bf R}_{\perp})\sin\theta({\bf x})\cos\theta({\bf x})+{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}\sin^{2}\theta({\bf x})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf P}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}({\bf x}){\bf R}({\bf x}),

as may be expected from the fact that these rotations preserve the symmetry, or antisymmetry, of matrices.

Using this commutivity we see that
{\bf P}={\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}({\bf x}){\bf R}({\bf x}){\bf P}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}^{T}({\bf x}){\bf R}^{T}({\bf x})
, and
substituting (
3.35
) into the last expression gives

(3.38)

{\bf P}=-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\bf R}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\bf R}_{\perp}+{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\bf R}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}+{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\bf R}_{\perp}-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}.

4.
The abstract setting

Throughout the paper we will successively truncate the Hilbert space
{\cal H}
making no changes at any stage to the operators
\chi
,
{\bf R}_{\perp}
,
{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}
and hence
{\bf L}
, and only changing

{\cal U}
,
{\cal E}
, and
{\cal J}
and the operators
\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{0}
,
\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}
, and
\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{2}
that project onto these spaces. Consequently,
\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}
and
\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{2}
will be
no longer given by (
3.28
) and (
3.29
) and there is no reason to believe that they remain local operators in Fourier space. As
{\cal U}
will change we need to introduce the abstract setting of effective operators in the theory of composites, called the
Z
-problem. One has a Hilbert space
{\cal H}
, equipped with an inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
,
that has a decomposition into three orthogonal subspaces
{\cal U}
,
{\cal E}
, and
{\cal J}
:

(4.1)

{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal H}$}}={\mbox{\boldmath${\cal U}$}}\oplus{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}\oplus{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal J}$}}.

Given an operator
{\bf L}:{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal H}$}}\to{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal H}$}}
one considers the equation

(4.2)

{\bf j}_{0}+{\bf j}={\bf L}({\bf e}_{0}+{\bf e})\text{ with }{\bf j}_{0},{\bf e}_{0}\in{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal U}$}},\quad{\bf j}\in{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal J}$}},\quad{\bf e}\in{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}.

If for each
{\bf e}_{0}\in{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal U}$}}
this has a unique solution for
{\bf j}_{0}
,
{\bf j}\in{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal J}$}}
, and
{\bf e}\in{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}
,
then since
{\bf j}_{0}
depends linearly on
{\bf e}_{0}
one may write

(4.3)

{\bf j}_{0}={\bf L}_{*}{\bf e}_{0},

which defines the effective operator
{\bf L}_{*}:{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal U}$}}\to{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal U}$}}
. We emphasize that
{\bf L}_{*}
is an operator mapping
{\cal U}
to
{\cal U}
. In particular, for our problem, it is not the case that after truncation
{\bf j}_{0}({\bf x})={\bf L}_{*}{\bf e}_{0}({\bf x})
for some
{\bf L}_{*}
, possibly dependent on
{\bf x}
.
To guarantee existence and uniqueness of the solutions to (
4.2
)
for all
{\bf e}_{0}\in{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal U}$}}
it suffices [see, for example, Section 2.4 of
[
33
]
] that
{\bf L}
be bounded, i.e.
there exists a constant
\beta>0
such that for all
{\bf a}\in{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal H}$}}
,

(4.4)

|{\bf L}{\bf a}|\leq\beta|{\bf a}|\quad\text{where}\quad|{\bf a}|=\sqrt{({\bf a},{\bf a})},

and additionally that
{\bf L}
is
\phi
-coercive for some angle
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
by which we mean that there exists a constant
\alpha>0
such that
for all
{\bf a}\in{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal H}$}}
,

(4.5)

\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits[(e^{i\phi}{\bf L}{\bf a},{\bf a})]\geq\alpha|{\bf a}|^{2}.

For our polycrystal these are satisfied if
{\bf L}_{0}
is bounded and the self-adjoint part of the matrix
e^{i\phi}{\bf L}_{0}
has a positive definite real part. Without loss of generality one may set
\phi=0
by making a rotation of
{\bf L}
in the complex plane if necessary.

As stated, we will be truncating
{\cal H}
making no changes at any stage to the operators
\chi
,
{\bf R}_{\perp}
,
{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}
and hence
{\bf L}
. Therefore, these operators retain their
properties which we relist here:

(4.6)

\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}^{2}=\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$},\quad\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}^{\dagger}=\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$},

(4.7)

{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}^{2}=-{\bf I},\quad{\bf R}_{\perp}^{2}=-{\bf I},\quad{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}^{\dagger}=-{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp},\quad{\bf R}_{\perp}^{\dagger}=-{\bf R}_{\perp},\quad{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}={\bf R}_{\perp}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp},

(4.8)

\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\bf R}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}=0,\quad\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}=0,\text{ and }\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\bf R}_{\perp}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}=0,

(4.9)

\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}-{\bf R}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\bf R}_{\perp}-{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}+{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\bf R}_{\perp}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}={\bf I},

At each truncation stage we want the new spaces

{\cal U}
,
{\cal E}
and
{\cal J}
to be such that the projections
\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{0}
,

\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}
and
\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{2}
onto them retain their essential properties:

(4.10)

\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{0}+\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{2}={\bf I},\quad\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{j}=\delta_{ij}\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{i},\quad\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{i}^{\dagger}=\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{i}.

(4.11)

{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{i}=\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{i}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp},\text{ for }i=0,1,2,\quad{\bf R}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}=\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{2}{\bf R}_{\perp},\quad{\bf R}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{0}=\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{0}{\bf R}_{\perp}.

and

(4.12)

\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{0}=\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{0}\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$},\quad\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}=\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$},\quad\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{2}=\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{2}\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$}.

where
{\Psi}
is the projection

(4.13)

\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$}=\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}-{\bf R}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\bf R}_{\perp}={\bf I}+{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}-{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\bf R}_{\perp}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp},

in which the last identity follows from (
4.9
).
From (
4.8
)
{\Psi}
satisfies

(4.14)

\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}=\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}=\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$},\quad\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$}({\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp})=0,

and additionally,

(4.15)

{\bf R}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$}={\bf R}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}+\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\bf R}_{\perp}=\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$}{\bf R}_{\perp},\quad{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$}=({\bf I}-\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$}){\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}.

The latter implies
{\Psi}
and
{\bf I}-\mbox{\boldmath${\Psi}$}
project onto spaces of equal dimension.
We may introduce

(4.16)

{\bf P}=-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\bf R}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\bf R}_{\perp}+{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\bf R}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}+{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\bf R}_{\perp}-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp},

which according to this definition and (
4.8
) satisfies

(4.17)

{\bf P}\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}=\mbox{\boldmath${\chi}$}{\bf P}=0,\quad{\bf P}^{2}={\bf P},\quad{\bf P}^{\dagger}={\bf P},

the latter two equations implying that
{\bf P}
is an orthogonal projection, and

(4.18)

{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}{\bf P}={\bf P}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\bf R}_{\perp}={\bf P},\quad({\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}+{\bf R}_{\perp}){\bf P}={\bf P}({\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}+{\bf R}_{\perp})=0.

The first equation is in fact a corollary of the second. Indeed, multiplying the last equation on the left by
{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}
gives

(4.19)

0={\bf P}({\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}+{\bf R}_{\perp}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp})=-{\bf P}+{\bf P}{\bf R}_{\perp}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp},

and taking adjoints establishes the first equation in (
4.18
).

The first step in the truncation is done in the appendix. It reduces the Hilbert space to a finite dimensional vector space with only minor perturbations of the operators
\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}
and
\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{2}
and no change to the other operators. We use the same notations for the relevant operators, and relevant subspaces, despite the fact that
{\cal E}
,
{\cal J}
and the projections
\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}
and
\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{2}
onto them have changed. The changes in the appendix ensure that
{\bf L}_{*}({\bf L}_{0})
remains almost unchanged on the domain of
{\bf L}_{0}
’s that are of main physical interest. For the remainder of the paper we assume that all spaces are finite dimensional.

The next truncation step is reducing the dimension of the Hilbert space by
4
. The spaces
{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}^{\prime}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal J}$}}^{\prime}

that replace
{\cal E}
and
{\cal J}
will each have dimension 2 less than those spaces.
The new space
{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal U}$}}^{\prime}
, replacing
{\cal U}
, will still be
4

{\bf L}^{\prime}_{*}
can be represented by a
4\times 4
matrix if we choose a basis for
{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal U}$}}^{\prime}
. The truncation needs to be done in such a way that (
4.10
), (
4.11
), and (
4.12
) remain satisfied
when
\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{0}
,
\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}
, and
\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{2}
are replaced by
\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{0}^{\prime}
,
\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}^{\prime}
, and

for some choice of angles

would have the factorization (

) and a correspondence with sequential laminates could be made.

In summary, with an
appropriate choice of bases, solving (

important distinction is that the change of basis requires a

that needs to have the form (

\phi_{j}
is an open problem. If it is always possible then the function

for a general polycrystal can be mimicked, to an arbitrary high degree of approximation, by the function for a sequential laminate.

10.
Alternative continued fraction expansions for sequential laminates

Naturally it follows from our analysis that (
8.16
), (
8.17
), (
8.18
), and (
9.8
) provide a continued fraction expansion for the effective tensor of sequential laminates.
There are many alternative expansions based on
formulae giving the effective tensor
{\bf L}_{*}
of a laminate of two phases with tensors
{\bf L}_{1}
and
{\bf L}_{2}
. For conductivity, with one phase being isotropic an elegant formula was obtained by Tartar
[
36
]
, generalized to elasticity by Frankfort and Murat
[
6
]
, and subsequently formulated for a wide variety of problems. For two dimensional elasticity, with one phase being isotropic a more concise formula can be obtained
[
8
]
.

One lamination formula
[
26
,
37
]
that is convenient for developing continued fraction expansions for sequential laminates
(and underpins the theory of exact relations in composites, as reviewed in Chapter 17 of

[
28
]
,
[
9
]
and
[
10
]
) is

(10.1)

[{\bf K}_{*}-\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}({\bf n})]^{-1}=\langle[{\bf K}-\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}({\bf n})]^{-1}\rangle,

where the angular brackets denote a volume average,

(10.2)

{\bf K}_{*}=\sigma_{0}(\sigma_{0}{\bf I}-{\bf L}_{*})^{-1},\quad{\bf K}({\bf x})=\sigma_{0}(\sigma_{0}{\bf I}-{\bf L}({\bf x}))^{-1},

and in our setting

(10.3)

\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}({\bf n})=\begin{pmatrix}{\bf n}\otimes{\bf n}&0\cr 0&{\bf n}\otimes{\bf n}\end{pmatrix}.

Here we are free to choose the constant
\sigma_{0}
and for a given
{\bf L}({\bf x})
,
{\bf L}_{*}
does not depend on the choice.
Applying this formula to a two-phase sequential laminate gives

(10.4)

[{\bf K}_{*}^{(j)}-\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}({\bf n}_{j})]^{-1}=f_{j}[{\bf K}^{(j)}-\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}({\bf n}_{j})]^{-1}+(1-f_{j})[{\bf K}_{*}^{(j-1)}-\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}({\bf n}_{j})]^{-1},

where

(10.5)

{\bf K}_{*}^{(j)}=\sigma_{0}(\sigma_{0}{\bf I}-{\bf L}^{(j)}_{*})^{-1},\quad{\bf K}^{(j)}={\bf R}_{j}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{j}\sigma_{0}(\sigma_{0}{\bf I}-{\bf L}^{(0)})^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{j}^{T}{\bf R}_{j}^{T},

in which now

(10.6)

{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{j}={\bf I}\cos\psi_{j}+{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{\perp}\sin\psi_{j},\quad{\bf R}_{j}={\bf I}\cos\psi_{j}+{\bf R}_{\perp}\sin\psi_{j}.

The directions
{\bf n}_{j}
of lamination need not be correlated in any way with the set of crystal orientations
\psi_{j}
.
Equivalently, we may write

(10.7)

{\bf K}_{*}^{(j)}=\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}({\bf n}_{j})+\left\{f_{j}[{\bf K}^{(j)}-\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}({\bf n}_{j})]^{-1}+(1-f_{j})[{\bf K}_{*}^{(j-1)}-\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}_{1}({\bf n}_{j})]^{-1}\right\}^{-1}.

To develop the continued fraction expansion starting with
{\bf L}_{*}^{(m)}={\bf L}_{*}
one sets

(10.8)

{\bf L}_{*}=\sigma_{0}{\bf I}-\sigma_{0}[{\bf K}_{*}^{(m)}]^{-1},

and then recursively makes the substitutions (
10.4
), eliminating
{\bf K}_{*}^{(j)}
for
j=m,m-1,\ldots,1
as one goes, until at the last stage one substitutes

(10.9)

{\bf K}_{*}^{(0)}=\sigma_{0}{\bf R}_{0}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{0}(\sigma_{0}{\bf I}-{\bf L}^{(0)})^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal R}$}}_{0}^{T}{\bf R}_{0}^{T}.

Unless there is a correspondence with sequential laminates it seems that for general polycrystals there is no such continued fraction expansion for
{\bf L}_{*}
having an analogous simple form.
A more complicated formula is available that simplifies to (
10.1
) for laminates, but it does not simplify in general: see

[
12
]
.
