Organ-specific modulation of antioxidant functions by combined voluntary exercise and radon inhalation in mice
Authors: Reiju Takenaka, Ayumi Tanaka, Shogo Miyanaga, Shota Naoe, Shuna Goto, Mitsuki Nagano, Kotaro Tano, Norie Kanzaki, Akihiro Sakoda, Kiyonori Yamaoka, Takahiro Kataoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Radon inhalation as well as voluntary exercise increase antioxidant function in experimental animals. However, the combined effects of radon inhalation and exercise on antioxidant functions have not yet been investigated. In this study, we examined the effects of combined voluntary wheel running (VWR) and radon inhalation on antioxidant functions in various organs of mice. Mice were individually housed in cages equipped with running wheels, and allowed voluntary exercise for 5, 15, or 25 days, followed by radon inhalation at 2,000 Bq/m3 for 24 ‍h. Antioxidant function was enhanced by the combined treatment in the kidneys, pancreas, spleen, and stomach, with responses varying according to exercise duration. In contrast, antioxidant function was reduced in the lungs and heart. No clear interaction effects of the combined treatment were observed in the liver, small intestine, colon, and brain. These findings suggest that the combined effects of voluntary exercise and radon inhalation on antioxidant functions are organ-specific and can be categorized into distinct response patterns across tissues.

Introduction

Radon (222Rn) is a radioactive noble gas with an atomic number of 86 that emits α-particles. Radon exposure is well recognized as a risk factor for lung cancer; however, radon therapy has been reported to be effective in alleviating pain-related conditions, such as rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis,(1) as well as respiratory diseases, including bronchial asthma.(2) Despite its reported therapeutic effects, the precise mechanisms underlying radon therapy remain incompletely understood. In a previous study, we demonstrated that radon inhalation enhances antioxidant function in various mouse organs(3) and that this enhancement contributes to the suppression of oxidative stress–related diseases caused by reactive oxygen species (ROS) and free radicals. For example, enhanced hepatic antioxidant function mitigates carbon tetrachloride-induced liver injury(4) and alcohol-induced liver damage.(5) Moreover, radon inhalation has been reported to exert inhibitory effects on several oxidative stress-related diseases, including dextran sulfate sodium-induced acute colitis,(6) carbon tetrachloride-induced kidney injury,(4) and diabetic nephropathy.(7)

In vascular tissues, voluntary wheel running (VWR) increases superoxide dismutase (SOD) expression and activity, thereby attenuating age-related decline in endothelium-dependent vasodilation.(8) In a broader perspective, exercise reduces oxidative damage and enhances antioxidant capacity across multiple organs. In rodent models, exercise decreases malondialdehyde levels and ROS while increasing antioxidant enzymes, such as SOD and catalase (CAT), as well as glutathione (GSH), in the lungs, liver, heart, spleen, gastrointestinal tract, hippocampus, and pancreas under conditions of chemical injury, inflammation, irradiation, neurodegeneration, and chronic stress.(9–18) Collectively, these findings indicate that exercise exerts systemic antioxidant and cytoprotective effects across diverse tissues.

The abovementioned studies have demonstrated that radon inhalation and exercise independently enhance antioxidant function and reduce oxidative stress. However, to the best of our knowledge, the combined effects of radon inhalation and exercise have never been evaluated. We hypothesized that combined radon inhalation and spontaneous exercise enhance antioxidant function in various organs. To test this hypothesis, in the present study, we evaluated the combined effects of radon inhalation and VWR on oxidative stress and antioxidant function in multiple mouse organs, by assessing endogenous antioxidant systems, such as SOD, CAT, and total GSH (t-GSH).

Materials and Methods

Animals

Eight-week-old male BALB/cJ mice (Jackson Laboratory Japan, Yokohama, Japan) were acclimated for one day under controlled environmental conditions with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle (lights on at 8:00 AM). Standard chow and tap water were provided ad libitum. Mice were assigned to 12 experimental groups (n = 6 per group): non-VWR + sham inhalation, non-VWR + radon inhalation, VWR + sham inhalation, and VWR + radon inhalation. The mice in each group were allowed voluntary exercise for 5, 15, or 25 days, followed by radon inhalation. All procedures were approved by the Animal Experiment Committee of Okayama University (approval no. OKU-2024310).

VWR

After acclimation, mice were individually housed in cages equipped with a rotating activity meter (SN-450-A; Shinano Seisakusho Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) for 5, 15, or 25 days. Non-VWR mice were housed in identical cages but were prevented from accessing the running wheel to ensure comparable housing conditions. Voluntary running activity was recorded daily at 8:30 AM, and running distance was calculated from the number of wheel rotations over a 24-h period. Following the VWR period, the mice were assigned to sham or radon inhalation groups, with group allocation adjusted to equalize mean running distance between groups.

Radon inhalation

Mice assigned to the radon inhalation group were exposed to radon at an average concentration of 2,000 Bq/m3 for 24 ‍h, whereas sham inhalation mice were exposed to ambient air under identical conditions. Immediately after exposure, the mice were euthanized with a carbon dioxide overdose. The target organs (lungs, liver, kidneys, spleen, stomach, small intestine, pancreas, heart, colon, and brain) were removed and stored at −80°C until analysis.

Biochemical assays

Tissue samples were homogenized in 10 ‍mM phosphate-buffered saline (pH ‍7.4), and the homogenates were used for the measurement of SOD, CAT, and t-GSH following the methods described previously by us.(19,20)

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as the mean ± SEM. A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was first performed to identify significant main and interaction effects. Subsequently, only those cases that showed a statistically significant increase or decrease according to Tukey’s post-hoc test were categorized as an increase or decrease among interaction effects, with statistical significance defined as p<0.05.

Results

Running distance

Daily running distance increased progressively with longer VWR durations. For each 10-day increase in the VWR period, daily running distance increased by approximately 1 ‍km, and total running distance increased by approximately 100–110 ‍km (Table 1, 2).

No significant differences in daily running distance among VWR durations were observed between the radon and sham inhalation groups. In both groups exposed to 15- and 25-day VWR periods, running distances were low during the initial 4–5 days and increased thereafter (Fig. 1).

Effects of VWR and radon inhalation on antioxidant function in the kidneys, pancreas, spleen, and stomach

In the kidneys, the SOD activity was significantly elevated in the VWR + radon inhalation group exposed to VWR for 15 days compared with those in all other groups. Additionally, t-GSH level showed a significant increase in the VWR + radon inhalation group exposed to 5 days of VWR compared with that in the non-VWR + sham inhalation group; however, it decreased significantly in the combined treatment group exposed to 25 days of VWR (Fig. 2).

In the pancreas, the SOD activity increased significantly after 15 days of VWR in the combined exposure group compared with those in the other three groups, but decreased significantly after 25 days of VWR compared with that in the non-VWR + radon inhalation group. The levels of t-GSH were significantly decreased in the VWR + radon inhalation group exposed to 5 and 15 days of VWR compared with those in the non-VWR + sham inhalation and non-VWR + radon groups, respectively (Fig. 3).

In the spleen, the SOD activity decreased significantly in the combined treatment group subjected to 15 days of VWR but increased significantly after 25 days of VWR compared with those in the remaining three groups. The CAT activity was significantly increased after both 5 and 25 days of combined exposure compared with that in the non-VWR + sham group (Fig. 4).

In the stomach, the SOD activity was significantly reduced in mice subjected to 15 days of VWR followed by radon inhalation compared with those in mice subjected to other treatments. However, after 25 days of VWR, the activity increased significantly in the VWR + radon inhalation group compared with that in the non-VWR + radon inhalation group. The CAT activity was significantly reduced in both the VWR + sham inhalation and VWR + radon inhalation groups receiving 5 days of VWR compared with that in the non-VWR + radon inhalation group (Fig. 5).

Effects of VWR and radon inhalation on antioxidant function in the lungs and heart

In the lungs, the SOD activity increased significantly in the VWR + radon inhalation group exposed to 5 days of VWR compared with those in all other groups, whereas the increase was not significant between the VWR + radon inhalation group exposed to 15 days of VWR and the non-VWR + radon inhalation group. In contrast, the SOD activity decreased significantly after 25 days of VWR exposure in the VWR + radon inhalation group compared with those in both the non-VWR + sham inhalation and non-VWR + radon inhalation groups. The CAT activity also decreased significantly in both the VWR + sham inhalation and VWR + radon group compared with that in the non-VWR + radon inhalation group after 25 days of VWR (Fig. 6).

In the heart, the SOD activity was significantly reduced in the VWR + radon inhalation group compared with those in the non-VWR + radon inhalation and the VWR + sham inhalation groups after 25 days of VWR. The level of t-GSH was significantly increased in the VWR + radon inhalation group exposed to 25 days of VWR compared with that in the non-VWR + radon inhalation group (Fig. 7).

Effects of VWR and radon inhalation on antioxidant function in the liver, small intestine, colon, and brain

In the liver, only t-GSH levels were significantly increased after 15 days of VWR in both the VWR + sham inhalation and VWR + radon inhalation groups compared with those in the non-VWR + radon inhalation group (Supplemental Fig. 1*).

In the small intestine, only t-GSH levels were significantly decreased after both 5 and 25 days of VWR in the combined treatment group compared with those in the non-VWR + sham inhalation group (Supplemental Fig. 2*).

In the colon, the SOD activity significantly increased in the VWR + sham inhalation group compared with that in the non-VWR + sham inhalation group, but decreased in the VWR + radon inhalation group compared with that in the VWR + sham inhalation group exposed to 5 days of VWR. Similarly, t-GSH levels showed similar trends in both VWR groups exposed to 15 days of VWR (Supplemental Fig. 3*).

In the brain, no significant differences were observed among groups for any indicator after combined VWR and radon inhalation (Supplemental Fig. 4*).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the effects of combined radon inhalation and VWR on antioxidant function and oxidative stress markers across multiple organs in mice. By assessing key indicators of redox balance, we aimed to determine whether the combined intervention produces interaction effects compared with either treatment alone. Our findings demonstrate that combined radon inhalation and VWR modulate antioxidant function in an organ-specific manner, with enhanced responses observed in selected tissues and variable, time-dependent effects depending on both organ type and exposure duration.

In the present study, individual housing was employed to enable precise quantification of VWR activity, as well as food and water intake, while minimizing variability arising from social hierarchy or aggressive interactions. Mouse housing strategies are selected according to experimental objectives and outcome measures, with individual and group housing each presenting distinct advantages and limitations. However, rodents are social animals, and social isolation has been reported to induce stress and influence immune and neuroendocrine functions.(21) Previous studies have shown that irregular chronic mild stress can increase spontaneous locomotor activity, including exercise duration, distance, and speed.(22) Accordingly, although a progressive increase in running distance was observed with prolonged VWR duration, the contribution of housing-related stress to this effect cannot be determined in the present study, as stress-related parameters were not directly assessed. Non-VWR mice were housed under identical conditions to control for environmental factors. Nevertheless, the potential influence of housing condition-related stress on antioxidative functions in organs cannot be excluded.

Exercise activates MAP kinases in the gastrocnemius muscle of rats, which is followed by the activation of the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) pathway and subsequent expression of enzymes associated with defense against ROS such as Mn-SOD.(23) VWR also modulates antioxidant and inflammatory responses. For example, treadmill-based exercise has been shown to suppress lipopolysaccharide-induced acute lung injury, potentially through increased anti-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-10 and enhanced antioxidant capacity.(24) Moreover, we previously reported that radon inhalation exerts immunomodulatory effects in lipopolysaccharide-induced systemic inflammatory models.(25) These findings suggest that both VWR and radon inhalation share the ability to enhance biological defense mechanisms, including antioxidant and anti-inflammatory pathways. Accordingly, their combined application was hypothesized to exert interaction effects on antioxidant function. However, the present findings indicate that these effects are not uniformly additive and instead exhibit organ-specific and exposure-dependent variability.

Radon inhalation increases the brain NF-κB content, which regulates the induction of Mn-SOD production, in nuclear compartments.(26) The inhibitor of κB kinase-β, which activates NF-κB, was slightly increased by radon inhalation.(26) These phenomena are similar to those observed in exercise, supporting the involvement of shared ROS-mediated signaling pathways.

Furthermore, enhancement of antioxidant function is triggered by moderate oxidative stress, such as low levels of ROS. For instance, in a previous study, we demonstrated that thoron (220Rn, the radioisotope of radon 222Rn) inhalation at 500 Bq/m3 resulted in early recovery from alcohol-induced liver damage in mice, whereas its inhalation at 2,000 Bq/m3 did not inhibit the liver damage, probably because of the decrease in GSH content in the liver.(27) This suggests the existence of an optimal dose for stimulating antioxidant defenses. Similarly, exercise generates moderate levels of ROS, and the cumulative ROS generated by both radon inhalation and VWR likely determines the magnitude of antioxidant responses. We have reported about optimal radon concentrations and inhalation durations for enhancing antioxidant functions.(3) In this study, the SOD activity in the pancreas increased after 15 days of voluntary exercise combined with radon inhalation, which indicates that the subjects were exposed to an optimal level of moderate oxidative stress. In contrast, the stimulation probably exceeded this optimal range when radon inhalation was combined with 25 days of exercise. Furthermore, low-dose X-ray irradiation has been reported to cause a temporary decrease in activity before an eventual increase.(28) The phenomena observed in the pancreas and stomach in this study are apparently similar to these previously reported trends. In the pancreas (Fig. 3), for example, the SOD activity increased after 15 days of VWR but decreased after 25 days, indicating a non-linear, potentially hormetic response pattern. However, given that antioxidant capacity varies across organs,(20) the optimal oxidative stress threshold is likely organ-specific. These observations support the notion that combined exposures may elicit bidirectional and context-dependent responses.

Analysis of baseline redox status in various organs of normal mice revealed substantial differences in intrinsic antioxidant capacity and lipid peroxidation levels, allowing classification into groups with high antioxidant function (e.g., the liver and kidney), low antioxidant function with low lipid peroxidation (e.g., the brain, pancreas, and stomach), and low antioxidant function with high lipid peroxidation (e.g., the lungs, heart, small intestine, and large intestine). Furthermore, radon inhalation has been reported to alter correlations among oxidative stress-related markers, with t-GSH and SOD playing particularly important roles in tissues with lower antioxidant capacity.(20) These findings suggest that the responses of individual antioxidant systems vary under moderate oxidative stress. In the present study, changes in antioxidant function differed depending on the indicator, consistent with our previous findings. The radiation dose from radon inhalation was estimated based on our previous studies(29,30); specifically, the doses ranged from 86 nGy to 1.4 μGy, depending on the organ. These doses, together with the baseline antioxidant capacity, may influence the biological responses to radon. However, the mechanistic basis underlying these organ-specific differences remains to be elucidated.

Previous research from our group revealed that combined radon inhalation and antioxidant vitamin supplementation effectively suppresses alcohol-induced oxidative liver injury in mice.(31) Clinical studies have also shown that radon therapy can reduce the required doses of anti-inflammatory and analgesic medications in patients with rheumatoid arthritis.(32) In addition, several studies have examined the combined effects of exercise and antioxidant supplementation on oxidative stress. However, results remain inconsistent and highly dependent on experimental conditions, including exercise intensity, antioxidant type, and duration of intervention.(33,34) Collectively, these findings underscore the complexity of combined interventions targeting redox regulation. Based on our observation of organ-specific, non-linear patterns, we speculate that the combined ROS load from both sources determines the magnitude of the response.

Based on the findings of the present study, the combined effects of VWR and radon inhalation can be categorized into three organ-specific response patterns: organs exhibiting enhanced antioxidant function (the kidneys, pancreas, spleen, and stomach); organs showing reduced antioxidant function (the lungs and heart); and organs showing no clear response to the combined treatment (the liver, small intestine, colon, and brain). The most pronounced combined effects were observed in the kidneys and pancreas after 15 days, and in the spleen and stomach after 25 days. No clear interaction effects of the combined treatment were detected in the liver, small intestine, colon, or brain. These patterns highlight the importance of considering organ-specific and temporal variability when interpreting combined treatment effects.

This study had some limitations. Long-term VWR effects could not be fully evaluated due to constraints associated with individual housing, limiting exercise duration. As a result, the findings cannot be directly compared with studies investigating prolonged exercise exposure. Additionally, the radon inhalation protocol did not fully replicate real-world exposure scenarios, such as radon hot spring therapy in humans. Moreover, because the experimental design involved sequential rather than simultaneous exposure, the observed interaction effects should be interpreted with caution and may not fully represent true biological interactions. Furthermore, disease models were not directly assessed in this study; therefore, the potential of combined VWR and radon inhalation to mitigate oxidative stress–related pathologies remains unclear. Future studies using disease models and varied exposure regimens are warranted.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that combined VWR and radon inhalation modulate antioxidant function in an organ-dependent manner. The combined intervention produces enhanced antioxidant responses in selected tissues, whereas other organs exhibit limited or marker-dependent effects, indicating that the magnitude of the response is influenced by tissue-specific redox status and exposure duration. Overall, these findings indicate that the combined effects are complex, non-linear, and organ-specific, rather than uniformly additive, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation and further mechanistic investigation.
