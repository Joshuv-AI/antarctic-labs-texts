Secular trends of morbidity and mortality of thyroid cancer in five Asian countries from 1990 to 2019 and their predictions to 2035
Authors: Shi‐Di Tang, Hao Zi, Hua Tao, Qiao Huang, Xing‐Pei Guo, Tong Deng, Fei Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe rising burden of thyroid cancer (TC) is a public health problem in Asia. Predicting the future burden of TC in Asian countries is essential for disease prevention.MethodsData were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease 2019 for five Asian countries. We applied Bayesian age‐period‐cohort models to predict morbidity and mortality to 2035 and calculated age‐standardized incidence rate (ASIR) and age‐standardized mortality rate (ASMR). Furthermore, the estimated annual percentage change was calculated to evaluate the variation of ASIR and ASMR.ResultsBy 2035, predictions suggest that cases of TC will reach 75.56 × 103 in China, 70.22 × 103 in India, 15.78 × 103 in the Republic of Korea, 9.01 × 103 in Japan and 5.55 × 103 in Thailand, respectively. Except Japan, a significant upward trend of ASIR of TC will be observed in five Asian countries. The deaths from TC will increase in five countries and India will become the highest reaching 14.07 × 103. The ASMR will rise to 0.83/100 000 in India and 1.06/100 000 in the Republic of Korea, while it will drop to 0.35/100 000 in China, 0.43/100 000 in Japan and 0.50/100 000 in Thailand. In further predictions projected by sex, the growth rate of ASIR is reported higher in males than in females among most countries. ASMR of male will exceed that of females in China and Thailand by 2035.ConclusionThe disease burden caused by TC will further increase in five Asian countries, especially for men. It is necessary to develop more rational and timely disease prevention and manage strategies facing this disease trend.Keywords: Asian countries, morbidity, mortality, prediction, thyroid cancer

INTRODUCTION

Thyroid cancer (TC) has become an important public health problem because of its increasing incidence. As one of the most prevalent endocrine malignancies, TC caused 586 000 cases worldwide in 2020, and the global incidence rate in women even reached 10.1 per 100 000, which was three‐fold higher than that in men. 1 , 2 Research based on the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019 have reported a 167% increase in the new cases of TC in 2019 compared to 1990. Moreover, the age‐standardized incidence rate (ASIR) showed an upward trend in the past three decades, especially in high SDI regions. 3 An increase in incidence rates has been observed initially in very high‐income countries, and these increases have been subsequently reported in some middle‐income Asia countries, such as China 4 and India. 5 Further assessment and prediction of the epidemiological trend of TC is essential to the disease prevention and optimal allocation of medical resources.

In 2019, the highest incidence of cases and the highest disability‐adjusted life year (DALY) regions of TC were East Asia and South Asia. Furthermore, China had the highest incidence of cases per country followed by USA and India. 3 A previous study projected that the incidence of TC in China will rise substantially over the next two decades, and that new cases will reach 47 820 in 2039. 6 According to the world population prospects 2022 reported from the United Nations, Asia carried more than 4.5 billion populations approximating 60% of the global population. Additionally, the total population of Asia will continue to increase over the next 20 years. Consequently, these Asian countries face a huge public health challenge and there should be more focus on exploration of the potential reasons for this disease trend.

Currently, most studies focus on the evaluation and prediction of disease trend in a country or globally, and there is a lack of comprehensive analysis of disease burden of TC in Asian countries. In this study, we selected five representative Asian countries to investigate, with similar features in sociocultural backgrounds and increased population trend, which included China, Japan, South Korea, Thailand and India. We extracted data from the GBD 2019 database to analyze the epidemiological features of TC from 1990 to 2019. Furthermore, incidence and mortality of TC up to 2035 were predicted in these five Asian countries.

METHODS

Data source

The GBD 2019 provides a systematic assessment of the burden of 369 diseases and injuries in 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2019. Data were obtained from the Global Health Data Exchange query tool (https://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool). Details on methods and estimations have been published in previous studies. 7 , 8 , 9 In this study, we extracted data of incidences and mortality for TC in five Asian countries, including China, India, Public of Korea, Japan, and Thailand. Population predictions were obtained from the Department of Economic and Social Affairs (United Stations). Population data was organized by five Asian countries and sex.

Statistical analysis

Projection model

In this study, the Bayesian age‐period‐cohort (BAPC) model was used for the projection of the incidence and mortality of TC up to 2035 in five Asian countries. The BAPC model has been widely used in analyzing and projecting age stratified cancer incidence and death. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Model comparison based on our TC data showed the advantage of the BAPC model (Table S1). Moreover, projections from the BAPC model have been well calibrated with a narrow uncertainty interval. 14 The BAPC model based on integrated nested Laplace approximations (INLA) has been proven to be a reasonable method to achieve projections. 14 This method assumes that parameters were from appropriate prior distributions instead of parametric assumptions. Age, period and cohort effects can be modeled using either first‐order random work (RW1, fixed effect) or second‐order random work (RW2, linear time effect). A previous study demonstrated that RW2 priors provide more reliable forecasts than RW1 priors. 15 Second‐order random effects (RW2) were modeled for age, period and cohort to restrict their second‐order differences towards zero. Moreover, an additional random effect (independent and identically distributed, iid) was used to adjust for over‐dispersion. The BAPC model can be expressed as,

Crude rate and age‐standardized rate

Crude rate was calculated by a ratio of the number of new cases (morbidity) or deaths (mortality) to the number of populations at risk. The age‐standardized rate (ASR) (per 100 000 population) was calculated by the sum of the products of age‐specific rates (ai, where i denotes the ith age) and the number of population (or weight Wi) in the same age group i of the selected reference standard population, divided by the sum of the standard population weights: ASR = ∑i=1AaiWi∑i=1AWi×100000. The GBD world population 2019 was used as the reference population to calculate ASIR and age standardized mortality rate (ASMR). Then, raw ASR were compared with predicted ASR.

Temporal trend estimation

The estimated annual percentage changes (EAPC) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were estimated by a linear regression model: y = α + βx + ε, where y is ln (ASR), x represents the calendar year, and ε expresses the error term. EAPC is used to estimate the amplitude of variation in ASR during the survey time period. 16 , 17 In this study, the EAPC were calculated for the 1990–2019 and 2020–2035 intervals, respectively. The ASR exhibited an upward trend when the estimated value of EAPC and its lower 95% CI were both greater than zero. On the contrary, it showed a downward trend.

All statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using the R program (version 4.0.3, R Core team) with apc package (APC model), bapc package (BAPC model), and ggplot2 package (data visualization). A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Projections from 2020 to 2035

Compared with 2019, the projected number of new cases in 2035 will increase in five Asian countries. The highest projected total cases will occur in China, India and the Republic of Korea will reach 75.56 × 103 (95% UI: 0.00–170.53), 70.22 × 103 (95% UI: 0.00–151.36) and 15.78 × 103 (95% UI: 0.00–137.03), respectively. In 2035, the highest cases with males (36.13, 95% UI: 0.00–90.57) and females (47.46, 95% UI: 0.00–96.91), will respectively be presented in China and the Republic of Korea among five Asian countries (Table 1).

By 2035, the total ASIR in the Republic of Korea will have increased by 2.64 times and is expected to be 18.56/100 000 (95% UI: 0.00–161.63) being the highest among five countries. The highest ASIR for both male and females will be in the Republic of Korea and will reach 11.95/100 000 (95% UI: 0.00–197.08) and 30.63/100 000 (95% UI: 0.00–336.05), respectively (Table 1). According to this prediction, there will be an upward trend of ASIR of TC in most countries and the increasing speed of ASIR in males will be higher than in females (Table 1, Figures 1 and S1). The largest upward trend of ASIR will be the Republic of Korea, including the total population with an EAPC of 5.53 (95% CI: 4.22–6.85), for males with an EAPC of 7.90 (5.85–9.98) and for females with an EAPC of 5.92 (4.57–7.28) (Table 1, Figures 1c and S1e,f). However, the ASIR in Japan will present a downward trend with an EAPC of −0.94 (95% CI: −1.00 to −0.89) (Figure 1d).

DISCUSSION

This study provides projections of TC burden in five Asian countries up to 2035 using a BAPC model with integrated nested Laplace approximations building on the GBD 2019 database. By 2035, the ASIR of TC will reach 3.34/100 000 for China, 3.99/100 000 for India, 18.56/100 000 for the Republic of Korea, 3.65/100 000 for Japan, and 4.51/100 000 for Thailand, respectively. Meanwhile, the ASMR will reach 0.35/100 000 for China, 0.83/100 000 for India, 1.06/100 000 for the Republic of Korea, 0.43/100 000 for Japan, and 0.50/100 000 for Thailand, respectively. Our study showed a significant upward trend of ASIR in China, India, the Republic of Korea and Thailand. For ASMR, downward trends were observed in China, Japan and Thailand. A concerning finding is the rapidly rising burden in Asian countries. These findings have implications for health policy planners, physicians, healthcare professionals, and the public.

Previous studies have shown that the ASIR of TC has been increasing in Central, East, South, and Southeast Asia. 3 , 4 Our study showed increasing trends of ASIR in Asia from 1990 to 2035. The highest cases of total population and male groups will occur in China and of female groups will occur in India. Meanwhile, the highest ASIR will occur in the Republic of Korea. The upward trend of ASIR will be in most countries except Japan. Similarly, another study has found that TC incidence in China will increase significantly from 2020 to 2039. 6 The possible reasons for the general increase in the number of TC cases in these countries are as follows. First, the increasing population inevitably leads to an increase in the cases of TC. Second, the popularization and application of advanced diagnostic techniques, such as ultrasonography and fine‐needle techniques, is conducive to the early detection of TC. 18 , 19 , 20 Third, overdiagnosis could also be one of the reasons for the increase. 1 More and more studies have found that overdiagnosis of papillary TC, which has almost no effect on patients' life expectancy, has led to a dramatic increase in TC incidence. 21 , 22

Overdiagnosis is generally defined as the diagnosis of disease that would never cause symptoms or death during a given patient's lifetime. 23 Previous studies have confirmed the indolent growth pattern of TC, especially small papillary carcinomas, which are characterized by slow growth, or not at all, and rarely metastasizes. 24 , 25 , 26 Meanwhile, a growing body of research suggests that overdiagnosis due to thyroid screening is an important cause of the rising incidence of TC. 27 A study based on the population of China, Japan and South Korea also showed significantly positive correlations between ASIR of TC and proportions of overdiagnosis for both men and women. 28 The major consequence of overdiagnoses is overtreatment. Because patients are often young when they are diagnosed with TC they have excellent long‐term and disease‐specific survival, and accumulate substantial associated healthcare costs when undergoing surveillance and medical treatment. 29 In cost‐effective analysis, the cost of detecting recurrence in low‐risk patients is even more than six and seven times greater than the cost for intermediate‐ and high‐risk papillary TC patients. 30 These not only pose a serious challenge to medical resources and the public health system, but are also seriously detrimental to quality of life and endure financial burden to survivors. Consequently, some measures should be explored to deal with this issue. First, the optimized thyroid screening strategy for a specific population is essential. Second, clinicians should approach each patient having preciseness and personalized healthcare plans during diagnosis, treatment and follow‐up stage. Third, a more comprehensive risk assessment system for TC should be constructed to assist in clinical decision‐making. Fourth, enhancing health education of the general public is also an indispensable aspect to reduce overtreatment.

Our findings indicate that the disease burden caused by TC will further increase in five Asian countries, especially in males. Previous studies have also shown that the incidence of TC in male has increased more than in females. 3 Some studies have shown that the incidence of TC growth in Chinese males is higher than that in females, which is the same as the conclusions of this study. 4 , 6 Some potential reasons may partly explain this phenomenon. First, the more frequent exposure of radiation and chemical, especially from medical sources, may increase the risk of developing TC. 31 , 32 The thyroid is a radiosensitive organ and radiation exposure will increase the incidence of TC, especially in infancy and childhood. 33 , 34 Chemical exposure, such as endocrine disrupting chemicals, can induce abnormal proliferation of thyroid cells. 35 Flame retardant exposure will significantly increase the risk of papillary TC. 36 Benzene and formaldehyde exposure are also significantly associated with TC. 37 Nowadays, environmental and exposure factors are increasing, with the development of economy and society. Men may be more likely to undertake these high exposure risk jobs, in some developing countries. This may be the important causes for the rapidly rising incidence for TC in men. Second, the differences in healthcare awareness have led to a lag in the rise in morbidity. Current studies have shown that the incidence of TC in females is much higher than that in males, but the detection rate of TC in males will gradually increase, with the popularization of the healthcare concept. Although autopsy studies have shown that the incidence rate is higher in females than in males, the gender disparity is mostly confined to the detection of small subclinical papillary TCs. When the more lethal types of TC were included, the gender ratio of incidence for TC approached 1:1. 38 Consequently, while there are many specific factors, such as pregnancy, history of infertility, abnormal menstrual cycles, and history of breast cancer could make females more likely to develop to TC, 39 , 40 we should not ignore the issue of TC in males.

The ASMR will rise in India and the Republic of Korea, while it will fall in China, Japan and Thailand. This study reported that the highest deaths (14.07 × 103) and ASMR (1.06/100 000) will be shown in India and the Republic of Korea in 2035, respectively. The ASMR in China, Japan and Thailand will decrease, but the number of cancer deaths will increase. It is suggested that risk of mortality of TC in China, Japan and Thailand is mainly caused by aging. In contrast, we observed an elevated number of TC deaths accompanied by a rise in ASMR in India and the Republic of Korea from 2020 to 2035. This finding indicated that the disease burden caused by TC will further increase in these countries. Adaptation of disease prevention and management strategies and prioritization of resources are major issues that should be considered by local health policy makers.

In addition, we predicted that by 2035 the mortality rates will higher for males than for females in China and Thailand. In particular, China will become the only country to report the deaths and ASMR of TC for males higher than females. Based on epidemiological studies, the ASMR of TC is projected to decrease globally between 2020 and 2030, but this decrease has geographic heterogeneity. 41 Epidemiological studies of TC in China have shown a downward trend in overall mortality, and the mortality of males is not significantly reduced compared with that of females. 4 , 6 This trend suggests that the disease prevention and public health system will also face new challenges. The specific reasons for this trend are unclear. However, the later diagnosis opportunity may play a critical role in this trend. Women are more likely to receive medical care than men due to reproductive and perimenopausal factors. 42 , 43 More active health screening leads to early detection of TC. Moreover, previous studies have emphasized the high risk to women, leading to unequal screening opportunities for men and women. Clinicians or healthcare doctors pay more attention to the screening of TC in women, 38 which may lead to the risk of later stage of TC in men at diagnosis. Therefore, clinicians should pay more attention and screening to TC in males.

There were several limitations to this study. Trend‐based projections should be interpreted with caution as they are based on the assumption that trends previously observed will continue into the future. Furthermore, this study was based on the GBD 2019 data source and has the limitation of secondary data, which has been reported in previous studies. 3 , 7 Finally, we did not consider changes in risk factors of TC, so the prediction results can be interpreted under the assumption of a constant spectrum of risk factors.

In conclusion, in the next decades, the disease burden caused by TC will further increase in five Asian countries. We observed a signifcant upward trend of cases and ASIR for TC in China, India, the Republic of Korea and Thailand. The most pronounced increases of ASIR are projected in males. The ASMR will rise in the Republic of Korea and India, while it will fall in China, Japan and Thailand. This study predicted morbidity and mortality trends of TC in five Asian countries and provides evidence for prioritizing resources and future research.
