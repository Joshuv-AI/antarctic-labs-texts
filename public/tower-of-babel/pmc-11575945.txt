Comparison of a fixed-dose combination of Celecoxib/PG201 [Layla®] versus co-administration of individual formulations in healthy participants: A randomized trial
Authors: Ji Hye Song, Hyunsook Koh, Hyun-Young Moon, Jin-Gyu Jung, Jang Hee Hong, Jung Sunwoo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Osteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent joint disease affecting the spine, hands, hips, knees, and feet. However, definitive drugs for OA are lacking, and current treatments are limited owing to inconvenient administration, inadequate functional improvement, and long-term side effects including gastrointestinal and cardiovascular adverse events. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to assess the pharmacokinetics and safety profiles of PK101, a fixed-dose combination (FDC) comprising PG201, a 12-herb extract used in OA treatment in traditional East Asian medicine, and celecoxib, a selective cyclooxygenase-2 inhibitor, by comparing its administration as an FDC and the corresponding individual formulations in healthy subjects.Patients and methods:A randomized, open-label, single-dose, 2 × 2 crossover design with a cohort of healthy participants. All subjects received a single FDC tablet (405.4 mg PG201 and 100 mg celecoxib) or the individual formulations, with 7-day washout period between administrations. The estimation of maximum plasma concentration and area under the plasma concentration–time curve from time zero to the time of the last quantifiable concentration of celecoxib involved determining the geometric mean ratios and 90% confidence intervals of the FDC compared to its individual formulations.Results:Forty-six participants were enrolled; however, only 44 completed the study. The geometric mean ratios (90% confidence intervals) for the area under the plasma concentration–time curve from time zero to the time of the last quantifiable concentration and maximum plasma concentration of celecoxib were 1.1124 (1.0601–1.1672) and 1.2788 (1.1708–1.3969), respectively. The time of maximum plasma concentration range was 1.0 to 4.0 hours and 1.0 to 6.0 hours (minimum–maximum) for the FDC and individual formulations, respectively. Seven adverse events occurred in 6 subjects.Conclusion:The systemic exposure and safety profiles of the individual and FDC formulations were similar, supporting their potential as an innovative and effective therapeutic approach for OA treatment. All relevant data are within the paper and its Supporting Information files.

1. Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most prevalent joint disease and represents one of the most frequent and symptomatic health challenges among middle-aged and older adults.[1] Characterized by chronic degeneration of movable joints, OA mainly affects the spine and peripheral joints, specifically the hands, hips, knees, and feet.[2] Recent insights have revealed diverse etiological factors contributing to OA onset, manifesting in various symptomatic presentations due to a multifaceted interplay of age, genetic predisposition, obesity, joint structure, and hormones. Once OA develops, patients are subjected to life-long disease, typically marked by increased pain and severe functional impairment. The frequency and chronicity of OA, coupled with limited effective preventive measures and therapeutic interventions, poses a significant economic burden on patients, healthcare systems, businesses, and countries.

Arthritis is a serious health concern worldwide. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates that 1 in 5 (or 53.2 million) adults in the US experience some form of arthritis.[3] Amidst the plethora of over 100 arthritis types, OA emerges as the predominant variant, affecting 32.5 million adults in the US.[4] OA primarily targets articular cartilage, with matrix metalloproteinase (MMP), a pivotal contributor to cartilage damage, influencing cartilage tissue destruction and resulting in degenerative changes in the surrounding bone. In addition, cytokines such as interleukin-1 and tumor necrosis factor-α are induced, affecting the muscles, tendons, and ligaments, thereby causing severe pain.[5,6]

The overarching objective of OA treatment is the protection and maintenance of cartilage and bone tissues in the affected joint coupled with effective pain suppression. Currently, definitive drugs for the prevention and treatment of OA are lacking. Numerous analgesic and anti-inflammatory drugs have been developed, with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) being the predominant choice. Opioids, a synthetic analgesic anesthetic, and compounds such as glucosamine and chondroitin sulfate, purportedly involved in cartilage degradation and synthesis, are used as over-the-counter drugs and dietary supplements.[7] Among OA treatments, oral NSAIDs take precedence and are employed to control moderate to severe pain. However, existing OA treatments are suboptimal owing to cumbersome administration, poor functional improvement in cartilage and bone, and long-term side effects such as gastrointestinal and cardiovascular adverse events (AEs) associated with NSAIDs.[8]

Although NSAIDs have been demonstrated to reduce inflammatory responses in systemic and regional synovial membranes, there is insufficient evidence to support their efficacy in improving the processes associated with these limitations. Most NSAIDs inhibit both cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) and COX-2, it is the inhibition of COX-2 that is responsible for the anti-inflammatory effects of NSAIDS. On the other hand, inhibition of COX-1 by these agents causes damage to the GI tract.[9] Celecoxib has been implicated in the ‘alleviation of symptoms or signs of OA (degenerative arthritis)’, and as a COX-2 inhibitor, it exerts anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects by selectively inhibiting COX-2, thereby impeding the synthesis of prostaglandin H2.[10,11]

Herbs have been commonly used to treat OA in Asia for centuries.[12] Although the precise mechanisms of action of oral medicinal herb products remain incompletely elucidated, their interaction with inflammatory and cartilage-destructive mediators provides a basis for their use in OA treatment.[13] PG201, an extract derived from 12 herbs, has been historically used to treat OA in traditional East Asian medicine. Among the botanical constituents, Chaenomeles sinensis, Achyranthes japonica, Carthamus tinctorius, Cnidium officinale, and Cinnamomum cassia, which maintain and promote blood circulation. Angelica gigas and Gastrodia elata strengthen the blood and promote blood circulation. Saposhnikovia divaricata addresses headache, dizziness, and joint pain. Gentiana macrophylla is used for the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis. Acanthopanax sessiliflorus strengthens muscles and bones, thereby alleviating joint pain. Clematis chinensis and Dipsacus asperoides have been used to prevent cartilage degeneration and regeneration of damaged tissues.[14]

Twelve plant sources of PG201 have demonstrated efficacy in the treatment of several human diseases. For example, the Clematidis Radix extract has been identified for its ability to inhibit nuclear factor kappa B binding activity, resulting in an anti-inflammatory effect. Phlomidis Radix, another component, exhibits a protective effect against cartilage breakdown and facilitates the regeneration of damaged tissue. Previous animal experiments have demonstrated the strong anti-inflammatory activity of PG201 through the suppression of proinflammatory cytokine expression. Additionally, it demonstrates cartilage-protective effects by regulating the production of tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMP) and MMP. The mechanism of action of PG201 involves inhibiting the production of inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α, and promoting the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-4. Suppression of phospholipase A2 and inducible nitric oxide synthase lowers prostaglandin E2 and nitric oxide production, thereby alleviating inflammation and pain. Furthermore, PG201 inhibits glycosaminoglycan release from cartilage tissue and protects cartilage by downregulating MMP-2 expression and upregulating TIMP-2 expression.[8,14,15]

Recent studies have identified fourteen key compounds isolated from PG201 (Layla®) through activity-guided fractionation. These compounds include coumarin, demethylsuberosin, xanthotoxin, psoralen, decursinol, decursin, decursinol angelate, chikusetsusaponin IVa, chikusetsusaponin IVa methyl ester, ethyl caffeate, syringaresinol, cnidilide, farnesol, and linoleic acid. The effects of these compounds on lipopolysaccharide-induced nitric oxide and prostaglandin E2 production in RAW 264.7 cells were evaluated. All compounds, except linoleic acid, demonstrated significant inhibitory effects, with IC50 values ranging from 8 to 60 μM.[16] Herbal medicines typically consist of a complex mixture of multiple chemical constituents, each of which may contribute to the overall therapeutic effect. Due to this complexity, unlike conventional drugs that usually contain a single active pharmacological ingredient, herbal medicines often lack a clearly defined main compound, making comprehensive analysis challenging. Therefore, in this study, we focused solely on analyzing the concentration of celecoxib, excluding the herbal medicine PG201.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate and compare the safety and pharmacokinetic (PK) properties of the PK101 complex, comprising PG201 and celecoxib, when administered as a fixed-dose combination (FDC) versus when administered as individual drugs in a cohort of healthy participants. While the PK analysis focused exclusively on celecoxib, the safety evaluation included both components.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Ethics

All investigators adhered to the principles outlined in the Korean Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Food and Drug Safety and Institutional Review Board of Chungnam National University Hospital (CNUH 2019-05-028). The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov under the identifier NCT03876210. Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects.

2.2. Subjects

In this study, we recruited healthy adult males aged 19 years or older with a body weight of ≥50 kg. Prospective participants were screened based on their medical history, vital signs, physical examination, clinical laboratory tests, and 12-lead electrocardiogram conducted within 4 weeks before the initial administration.

Key exclusion criteria included alanine transferase and aspartate transaminase levels more than 2-fold of the upper normal limit and creatinine clearance below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2.

2.3. Study design

This study was a phase 1, randomized, open-label, single-dose, 2 × 2 crossover design study conducted to evaluate and compare the PK and safety profiles of individuals administered 2 doses of individual formulations and those administered a single-dose of FDC. The study was conducted at Chungnam National University Hospital Clinical Trials Center. Subject screening was performed for 28 days prior to the first investigational product administration. Subjects were randomly assigned to order sequences A and B in a 1:1 ratio, with a 7-day washout period between the study treatments. PK blood samples were collected before dosing and 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.33, 2.67, 3, 3.5, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 36, and 48 hours after administration (6 mL, EDTA K2 tube).

2.4. Sample size determination

This study aims to evaluate the safety and PK characteristics of PK101 when administered as a combination drug and PG201 and celecoxib when administered as single agents, the nature of the research is explorative and descriptive rather than hypothesis-testing. Therefore, it differs from typical studies designed for statistical hypothesis-testing. Consequently, the study can achieve its objectives with a sufficient number of subjects that meet the empirically required level.

Previous studies reported intra-subject variability for celecoxib maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) and area under the plasma concentration–time curve from time zero to the time of the last quantifiable concentration (AUClast) as 30.8% and 15.7%. Based on these, 46 subjects (23 per group) are needed, considering potential dropouts.

Considering these variabilities and a T/R ratio of 0.95 to 1.05 with 80% power, 46 subjects (23 per sequence group) are needed, accounting for potential dropouts.

The randomization was conducted using the SAS (SAS Institute Inc., NC: Ver. 9.4) software. To eliminate the period effect based on the order of clinical trial drug administration, simple randomization using a random number table was employed to carry out sequence-randomization.

2.5. Investigational products

The test drug consisted of an FDC containing 405.4 mg PG201 and 100 mg celecoxib (PMG Pharm Co., Ltd., Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea). The reference drugs included PG201 (405.4 mg PG201; PMG Pharm Co., Ltd., Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea) and Celcok Tab. (100 mg celecoxib; Whan in Pharm Co., Ltd., Seoul, Republic of Korea).

2.6. Determination of plasma concentrations

The collected blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4 °C. Plasma samples were transferred to Eppendorf tubes and stored at –70 °C until subsequent analysis.

Given the inherent challenges associated with PG201 as a natural drug to determine its concentration, quantitative analysis was performed only for plasma celecoxib. Plasma concentration analysis was conducted using calibrated liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC system: Waters ACQUITY UPLCTM System, Waters; MS/MS: Waters Micromass Quattro PremierTM XE Mass Spectrometer). For the column, Waters ACQUITY UPLCBEH C18, 1.7 µm (2.1 mm ID × 50 mm L) was used for the stationary phase, and in the mobile phase, 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in DW: methanol (A: B, B%: 65→90→65 v/v) was used at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. Positive electrospray ionization and multiple reaction monitoring were used for the concentration measurements, with ion transitions at m/z 382.0 to 362.0. The range of the calibration curve was 10 to 2000 ng/mL, and quality control samples were established at concentrations of 30, 800, and 1500 ng/mL.

2.7. PK analysis

The primary PK endpoints encompassed the AUClast and Cmax for celecoxib. The secondary PK endpoints included the area under the plasma concentration–time curve from zero to infinity (AUCinf), time of maximum plasma concentration (Tmax), terminal elimination half-life (t1/2), apparent clearance (CL/F), and AUClast/AUCinf ratio for celecoxib. PK analyses were performed using the non-compartmental method with WinNonlin software (version 8.3; Pharsight, Mountain View, CA). The AUC was calculated using the linear trapezoidal method. The Cmax and Tmax were determined directly from the observed concentration–time data.

2.8. Safety

Safety and tolerability were assessed based on AEs, vital signs, physical examination, clinical laboratory tests (complete blood cell count, blood chemistry tests, including liver and kidney function tests, and urinalysis), and 12-lead electrocardiography. AEs were coded using the system organ class and preferred terms according to MedDRA version 21.1.

3. Results

3.1. Subject demographics

A total of 46 participants were enrolled and randomized, and all participants were administered at least 1 dose of the experimental drug. Two participants discontinued the study: 1 due to AEs, as assessed by the principal investigator/co-investigator, and the other following withdrawal of consent. As a result, 44 participants successfully completed the study. The age, height, and weight and BMI of participants were 24.5 ± 2.2 years, 173.8 ± 6.3 cm, 69.7 ± 8.1 kg, and 23.1 ± 2.4 kg/m2, respectively (mean ± standard deviation). No significant differences in demographics were observed between individual sequence groups (Table 1).

3.2. Pharmacokinetics

The geometric mean ratios (GMRs) with corresponding 90% confidence intervals (CIs) for the AUClast and Cmax of celecoxib were 1.1124 (1.0601–1.1672) and 1.2788 (1.1708–1.3969), respectively. The mean plasma concentration–time profiles of celecoxib (Fig. 1) revealed Cmax values of 342.0 ± 118.0 ng/mL and 438.3 ± 146.6 ng/mL for the individual formulations and FDC, respectively. Corresponding AUClast values were 2661.0 ± 913.1 hour ng/mL and 2956.6 ± 992.6 hours ng/mL, respectively (mean ± standard deviation). The Tmax range was 1.0 to 6.0 hours and 1.0 to 4.0 hours (minimum–maximum) for the individual formulations and FDC, respectively. The AUClast/AUCinf ratio was 0.9 ± 0.1 for both treatments, and the PK sampling time was confirmed to be appropriate for assessing the PK profile based on the Tmax and AUClast/AUCinf ratio (Table 2).

3.3. Safety

The safety analysis set comprised 46 participants who were administered at least 1 dose of the drug. Seven AEs were reported in 6 participants (13.0%, 6/46). Five AEs occurred in 4 participants in the individual formulation group and 2 in 2 participants in the FDC group.

An increase in blood creatine phosphokinase level was the most frequently reported AE, with 4 instances in 4 participants (8.7%, 4/46). Except for procedural dizziness, all AEs observed in this study had been reported in previous studies.[8,17] Procedural dizziness was a transient dizziness caused by the blood sampling needle. No serious AEs were reported. All AEs resolved without any sequelae (Table 3).

4. Discussion

In this study, we investigated PK101 the PK properties and safety profiles when administered as an FDC and as an individual drug (PG201 and celecoxib). The observed t1/2 of 9.2 ± 4.3 hours validated the 7-day washout period, exceeding 5 times the t1/2. The 90% CI for the GMR and mean difference in the AUClast of celecoxib was 1.1124 (1.0601–1.1672), falling within the equivalence range. However, the GMR and mean difference of the Cmax of celecoxib were 1.2788 (1.1708–1.3969), falling outside the equivalence range (log 0.8–log 1.25). This discrepancy may be attributed to the low solubility of celecoxib in aqueous solution. Celecoxib is a weak acid with low solubility, ranging from 3 to 7 μg/mL at pH 7.0 and 40 °C in vitro.[18–20] Although no documented evidence exists in the literature regarding a physical drug–drug interaction between celecoxib and PG201, the complex composition of PG201 may influence the gastrointestinal environment or interact with enzymes involved in drug absorption. The possibility of such interactions cannot be completely excluded. This, along with celecoxib’s physicochemical properties, could potentially lead to variability in Cmax and affect absorption.

Regarding PK101, the biological equivalence between its single administration and the concomitant administration of individual formulations has not been established. Therefore, patients administered celecoxib (100 mg) and PG201 (405.4 mg) concomitantly were not transitioned to monotherapy with PK101. However, this caution is based on celecoxib concentration alone, and not on the analysis and comparison of both drug concentrations. As PG201 is a combination of herbs, the concentration of PG201 cannot be verified. Therefore, further research on the analytical methods and related herbal combinations is necessary. Notably, systemic exposure to celecoxib was not significantly altered by the concomitant administration of A gigas root, Chaenomelis fructus, Saposhnikovia root, Dipsaci radix, Acanthopanax root bark, Clematidis radix, Cinnamon bark, Gentianae macrophyllae radix, Cnidium rhizome, Gastrodia rhizome, and C tinctorius ethanol extract in a previous study.[21]

The PK101 FDC used in this study represents an innovative combination of herbal and chemical components developed for OA treatment for the first time. Moreover, among the NSAIDs frequently administered to patients with OA, the combination of celecoxib and PG201, which are COX-2 inhibitors with minimal gastrointestinal side effects, has emerged as an optimal therapeutic approach for patients with OA because of its concurrent enhancement of pain suppression and protection of cartilage and bone tissue through different mechanisms.

Despite the various types of drugs available for OA, their efficacies in relieving pain are similar, and preference leans toward drugs characterized by easy administration and minimal upper gastrointestinal side effects.[22] Therefore, considering the anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects of PG201, which acts on inflammatory factors and cartilage tissue protection, in conjunction with celecoxib, which selectively inhibits COX-2, the analgesic effect can be enhanced through different mechanisms. Simultaneously, the protective effects of the cartilage and bone tissue can be achieved. The formulation of an FDC incorporating these 2 components has the potential to enhance pain management, ensure stable functional improvement, and increase patient compliance in OA treatment.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, we compared the effects of PK101 administration as an FDC or individual drugs (PG201 and celecoxib) on PK properties and safety. Systemic exposure and safety profiles of the individual and FDC formulations were similar. PK101 FDC represents an innovative blend of herbal and chemical components, providing a promising therapeutic option for OA through distinct mechanisms of action.
