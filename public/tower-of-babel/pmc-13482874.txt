Joint association of frailty and social isolation with depressive symptoms among middle-aged and older Chinese adults: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Deyan Yang, Bingru Luo, Zide Wang, Yiwen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Frailty and social isolation are common in later life and each has been linked to poor mental health. However, their joint association with incident depressive symptoms, and whether co-occurrence confers risk beyond either factor alone, remains unclear. Participants aged ≥ 45 years without baseline depression and with complete exposure data were included (n = 7330). Frailty was assessed using a 30-item frailty index and defined as ≥ 25. Social isolation was measured by a composite scale (range 0–6) capturing marital status, living alone, frequency of contact with children, recent social participation, and residence; scores ≥ 3 denoted isolation. Incident depressive symptoms was defined as CESD-10 ≥ 10 at follow-up. Cox models estimated adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) for frailty, isolation, and their joint categories. Interaction was examined on multiplicative and additive scales using cross-product terms and RERI/AP/SI, respectively. Over follow-up, 2007 incident depressive symptoms cases occurred. Compared with non-frail participants, frailty was associated with depression risk (adjusted HR 1.47, 95% CI 1.24–1.74). Social isolation was also associated with increased risk (adjusted HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.02–1.28). Joint analyses showed a graded pattern versus non-frail and non-isolated: non-frail+isolated HR 1.11 (95% CI 0.98–1.25), frail + non-isolated HR 1.36 (95% CI 1.10–1.67), frail+isolated HR 1.86 (95% CI 1.43–2.42). Additive interaction was suggestive but not definitive (RERI 0.39; 95% CI − 0.14–0.93; AP = 0.21; − 0.03–0.46; SI = 1.85; 0.81–4.21); multiplicative interaction was not evident (interaction HR 1.24, 95% CI 0.89–1.72). Subgroup analyses by gender and cardiometabolic comorbidities showed broadly consistent patterns (all P for interaction >0.05). Frailty and social isolation independently predict incident depressive symptoms among middle-aged and older Chinese adults, with co-occurrence identifying the highest-risk subgroup. Introduction

Depression is a leading cause of disability worldwide and a major contributor to the global burden of disease among middle-aged and older adults1. In rapidly aging societies, including China, identifying high-risk subgroups and modifiable upstream determinants of depression is a public health priority2. Two gerontologic constructs-frailty and social isolation-have emerged as salient, multifaceted risk factors for adverse mental health outcomes in later life3–5. However, their joint association with depression, and the extent to which co-occurrence confers risk beyond either factor alone, remains insufficiently understood in longitudinal, population-based settings.

Accumulating evidence also links frailty with incident depressive symptoms and major depression, potentially through shared pathways involving chronic inflammation, neuroendocrine dysregulation, sarcopenia, multimorbidity, pain, and reduced activity and engagement6,7. Conversely, depression may exacerbate frailty via behavioral mechanisms (e.g., physical inactivity, poor nutrition), neurocognitive changes, and stress-related physiologic alterations, suggesting a bidirectional relationship8. Clarifying the prospective contribution of frailty to depression is essential for prevention strategies that integrate physical and mental health in aging9.

Social isolation-objective deficits in social contacts, participation, and support-is likewise common and consequential among older adults10. Distinct from loneliness (a subjective appraisal), social isolation is associated with elevated risks of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease, cognitive decline, and depression11,12. In China, demographic transitions characterized by internal migration, urbanization, and changing family structures may intensify isolation risks among middle-aged and older adults, particularly those with limited education or living alone in rural areas13. Notably, social isolation may cluster with frailty, as frail individuals are less likely to maintain social participation due to mobility limitations and fatigue, and socially isolated individuals may be less likely to engage in health-promoting activities that preserve physiologic reserve14.

Despite the plausibility of intersectional risk, relatively few studies have investigated the joint association of frailty and social isolation with depression. It remains unclear whether the co-occurrence of frailty and social isolation is simply additive-reflecting the sum of independent risks-or synergistic, indicating interaction that amplifies depression risk beyond additivity.

In this study, we evaluate the joint association of frailty and social isolation with depressive symptoms among middle-aged and older Chinese adults. We hypothesize that both frailty and social isolation are independently associated with elevated odds or risk of depressive symptoms and that their co-occurrence confers greater-than-additive risk, consistent with synergistic interaction.

Methods

Study population

We used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS)15, a nationally representative cohort of Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years enrolled in 2011 with biennial follow-up through 2018. Of 17,708 participants with baseline data, we first excluded those with missing CESD-10 data at baseline (n = 1671), participants with prevalent depression at baseline (n = 5465) or missing depression score (n = 2762), or with missing frailty or social isolation variables (n = 480), yielding an analytic sample of 7330 participants (Fig. S1). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

This study followed Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines (Table S1 STROBE Statement)16.

Assessment of frailty and social isolation

Frailty was assessed using the frailty index, which was calculated based on the accumulation of age-related health deficits17. Following previous studies18,19, we selected 30 available items from standardized CHARLS questionnaires and performance tests, including chronic diseases, self-reported health conditions, functional limitations, depressive symptoms, and cognitive function (Table S2 The items used to construct the frailty index). All items were drawn from core CHARLS modules that have been widely used in Chinese populations, and deficit-accumulation frailty indices derived from CHARLS have demonstrated good predictive validity for mortality and other adverse outcomes20,21. A detailed description of each deficit, its source scale or questionnaire, and supporting validity/reliability evidence is provided in Table S3 (Description, source module, type, and supporting evidence for the 30 deficits used to construct the frailty index).

Participants with missing data for any item were excluded from the frailty index calculation. Most items were dichotomized as 0 or 1, where 0 indicated no deficit and 1 indicated the presence of a deficit. Some items, including self-reported general vision, hearing, health status, and cognitive ability, were scored on a scale from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater severity of deficits. In our study, the frailty index was calculated by dividing the sum of existing deficits by 30 and multiplying by 100. Thus, the frailty index was a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 100. Following recommendations from previous studies20,21, frailty was defined as a frailty index ≥ 25, while non-frail was defined as a frailty index < 25.

In CHARLS, social isolation was assessed using a previously validated scale22, which included being unmarried (including separated, divorced, widowed, or never married), living alone, having less than weekly contact with their children (via phone, or email), not participating in any social activities over the past month (including interacting with friends, playing chess or cards, attending sports, social, or other clubs), residence (rural or urban), and see children less than once a week (1 point each; range, 0–6 points)23. A composite score was calculated, with a score of ≥ 3 indicating social isolation24.

Ascertainment of incident depressive symptoms

Incident depressive outcomes were identified at follow-up using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10), which has been validated in Chinese populations25. The CESD-10 consists of 10 items, each rated on a 4-point scale from 0 (rarely or none of the time) to 3 (most or all of the time). The CESD-10 is a screening tool for depressive symptoms rather than a diagnostic instrument for major depressive disorder. In accordance with previous CHARLS-based studies, a total score of 10 or more was used as the cut-off point for depressive symptoms26,27. In this study, “incident depression” refers to the first occurrence of elevated depressive symptoms defined by CESD-10 ≥ 10 among participants who were below this threshold at baseline, and does not represent physician-diagnosed depressive disorder.

Covariates

Potential confounders were selected based on prior literature and their relevance to both social isolation and frailty. The following covariates were included in the analysis: age (continuous), gender (male or female), marital status (married/other), educational attainment (less than middle school, high school/vocational, higher), body mass index (BMI, continuous), smoking status (current, former, or never), alcohol consumption (current, former, or never), and the presence of chronic conditions, included hypertension, defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg or self-reported history of hypertension28; diabetes, defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, glycated hemoglobin ≥ 6.5%, self-reported physician diagnosis29; chronic kidney disease, defined as estimated glomerolar filtration rate < 60 ml/min/1.73 m2 or self-reported physician diagnosis30; cardiovascular disease (CVD), defined as self-reported having a heart disease or stroke)31 and hyperlipidemia, defined as self-reported physician diagnosis. All covariates were measured at baseline and included in the models as potential confounders. Detailed information on the missing covariates in CHARLS cohorts is presented in Table S4 Proportion of missing covariables. We used complete-case analysis, which may introduce selection bias if missingness is not completely at random.

Statistical analysis

We summarized baseline characteristics overall and by joint frailty–isolation status using means with standard deviation or counts with percentages; group differences were assessed with one-way ANOVA or Pearson χ2 tests, as appropriate. Incidence rates (per 1000 person-years) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI) were estimated. Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs for frailty, social isolation, and their joint categories. Mode1 was crude mode, model 2 adjusted for age and sex; Model 3 additionally adjusted for education, marital status, lifestyle factors, BMI, baseline CESD-10 score, and comorbidities. Proportional hazards assumptions were evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. Prespecified subgroup analyses tested multiplicative interaction by gender and comorbidities using cross-product terms; interaction P values were derived from likelihood ratio tests.

To evaluate the interaction between social isolation and frailty, we also included an interaction term in the Cox proportional hazards models. We calculated three measures of interaction on the additive scale: (1) Relative Excess Risk due to Interaction (RERI) = HR11 − HR10 − HR01 + 1; (2) Attributable Proportion due to interaction (AP) = RERI/HR11; (3) Synergy Index (SI) = HR11/(HR10 × HR01)32. We calculated 95% CIs for RERI, AP, and SI using the delta method, which accounts for the variability in the estimated HRs. RERI < 0 or AP < 0 and SI < 1 indicate negative (antagonistic) interaction; RERI > 0 or AP > 0 and SI > 1 indicate positive (synergistic) interaction33,34.

We conducted a sensitivity analysis in which we reconstructed a reduced frailty index by excluding items related to doctor-diagnosed emotional, nervous, or psychiatric problems, doctor-diagnosed memory-related disease, and cognitive performance (items 8, 9, and 30 in Table S2). Frailty was redefined using the same cut-off of frailty index ≥ 0.25, and the Cox models were re-estimated.

Two-sided P < 0.05 denoted statistical significance. Analyses were performed using Stata 17 (StataCorp) or R 4.2.2.

Results

Patient demographics and baseline characteristics

Among 7330 participants (mean age 57 ± 9 years; 49.0% female), 5508 (75.1%) were non-frail and not socially isolated, 1358 (18.5%) were non-frail with social isolation, 328 (4.5%) were frail without social isolation, and 136 (1.9%) were frail with social isolation. Age increased across groups (56 ± 8 to 64 ± 8 years; P < 0.001). Women were more prevalent in frail groups (65.2–65.4%; P < 0.001). Lower education and non-married status were concentrated in socially isolated and frail groups (both P < 0.001). Comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, CKD, and CVD, were more frequent in frail groups (all P < 0.001). CESD-10 scores were highest in frail with social isolation (5.92 ± 2.48) and lowest in non-frail without isolation (4.17 ± 2.75; P < 0.001). Mean frailty index and social isolation scores differed markedly across groups (both P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Risk of incident depressive symptoms by frailty, social isolation, and their joint status

Over follow-up, 2007 incident depression cases occurred. Incidence rates were higher among frail versus non-frail participants (62.43 vs. 37.68 per 1000 person-years) (Table 2). In fully adjusted model, frailty was significantly associated with risk of depressive symptoms (HR 1.47; 95% CI 1.24–1.74). Social isolation was also associated with risk (HR 1.14; 95% CI 1.02–1.28), with incidence 46.50 versus 37.38 per 1000 person-years. Joint analyses showed graded risks relative to non-frail, non-isolated participants. Incidence rates increased from 36.18 to 74.00 per 1000 person-years across joint categories. In fully adjusted models, HRs were 1.11 (95% CI 0.98–1.25) for non-frail with isolation, 1.36 (95% CI 1.10–1.67) for frail without isolation, and 1.86 (95% CI 1.43–2.42) for frail with isolation, indicating the highest risk when frailty and social isolation co-occur.

In sensitivity analyses using a reduced frailty index that excluded mental and cognitive components (emotional/psychiatric problems, memory-related disease, and cognitive performance), the associations between frailty and incident CESD-10-defined depressive symptoms remained robust. The adjusted HR for frailty (vs. non-frailty) was 1.42 (95% CI 1.21–1.68), compared with 1.47 (95% CI 1.24–1.74) in the main analysis. The joint effects of frailty and social isolation and the measures of additive interaction were also similar to the primary results (Table S5 Sensitivity analysis using a reduced frailty index (excluding mental and cognitive components) and risk of incident depressive symptoms).

Interaction of frail and social isolation on depressive symptoms

Among middle-aged and older adults, frailty and social isolation were jointly associated with higher depressive symptoms risk (Table 3). Compared with those neither frail nor isolated, the hazard was higher for frailty alone (HR 1.36; 95% CI 1.10–1.67), social isolation alone (HR 1.11; 95% CI 0.98–1.25), and highest for co-occurrence (HR 1.86; 95% CI 1.43–2.42). The association of social isolation was stronger among the frail (HR 1.37; 95% CI 1.00-1.87). Additive interaction was suggested (RERI 0.39; AP 0.21; SI 1.85), whereas multiplicative interaction was not (HR 1.24; 95% CI 0.89–1.72).

Discussion

In this nationally representative cohort of Chinese adults aged 45 years and older, frailty and social isolation were each associated with higher risk of incident depressive symptoms, and their co-occurrence identified the subgroup with the highest incidence and hazard. After multivariable adjustment, frailty remained a robust predictor, while social isolation showed a more modest but significant association overall. Joint-category analyses revealed a stepwise gradient from neither exposure to both, consistent across most subgroups. Formal tests suggested no multiplicative interaction, whereas additive-scale indices pointed toward possible supra-additivity. These findings underscore the value of integrating physical function and social connectedness in strategies to prevent late-life depression in rapidly aging settings such as China.

Our results extend prior evidence that frailty increases vulnerability to adverse mental health outcomes, potentially through chronic inflammation, hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis dysregulation, pain, sarcopenia, multimorbidity, and reduced activity-pathways that may precipitate or sustain depressive symptoms35. The stronger and more consistent association for frailty relative to social isolation in fully adjusted models aligns with literature linking frailty to incident depression and poorer recovery trajectories36,37. At the same time, social isolation-an objective deficit in social ties and participation distinct from perceived loneliness-was independently associated with depression risk, complementing research showing that restricted networks and low engagement elevate risk of depressive symptoms, cognitive decline, and mortality38,39. In our data, the combined presence of frailty and isolation captured a particularly vulnerable phenotype: individuals with limited physiologic reserve who also lack buffering social resources.

The suggestive indication of positive interaction on the additive scale has practical implications. Additive interaction is often viewed as more relevant for public health prioritization because it reflects excess risk attributable to the combination beyond the sum of individual effects34. Although our interaction estimates were not statistically definitive, the pattern implies that interventions addressing both functional vulnerability and social connectedness could deliver greater absolute risk reductions than single-component approaches. This dovetails with trials and implementation studies demonstrating that multicomponent physical reconditioning (e.g., resistance exercise, protein supplementation) can mitigate frailty and depressive symptoms, while social prescribing, community clubs, and group-based programs enhance social engagement and mental health outcomes in older adults40–42. Embedding social connection components within frailty management-particularly for individuals with mobility limitations-may be a pragmatic path toward synergy in real-world settings.

From a clinical and policy perspective, these findings support systematic screening for both frailty and social isolation in primary care and community health programs. Brief, validated tools-such as short frailty indices and succinct social network/participation scales-could identify high-risk individuals for stepped interventions43,44. Given the small but meaningful association for social isolation after adjustment, resource-efficient, low-intensity social interventions (e.g., telephone befriending, structured group activities) may yield benefits at scale, especially when targeted to persons with early or moderate frailty who remain community-dwelling45,46. At the health-system level, integrating social care into chronic disease management and leveraging community organizations may help address upstream determinants of depression47.

Limitations warrant consideration. First, despite adjustment, residual confounding is possible, including unmeasured variables such as pain severity, cognitive impairment, or caregiving burden. Second, depressive outcomes were ascertained using the CESD-10, a self-report screening tool for depressive symptoms rather than a diagnostic instrument. Our definition of “incident depression” therefore reflects the onset of depressive symptoms (CESD-10 ≥ 10) and does not capture physician-diagnosed depressive disorders. Misclassification relative to clinical diagnoses may bias associations toward the null48. Third, frailty and social isolation were assessed at baseline; although we used prospective follow-up, time-varying changes and potential bidirectionality (e.g., depressive symptoms promoting social withdrawal and functional decline) could influence associations. Fourth, the subgroup with both frailty and isolation was relatively small, yielding wider confidence intervals and less precise interaction estimates. Fifth, competing risks (e.g., mortality in frail participants) may attenuate associations. Last but not least, our measure of social isolation was a composite index that included residential location. While urban-rural residence in China is strongly related to opportunities for social contact and community participation, it also reflects broader structural and socioeconomic differences, raising concerns about construct validity and potential confounding. We adjusted for multiple sociodemographic factors, which yielded similar results. Nonetheless, the extent to which residential location should be treated as a component of social isolation versus an upstream determinant remains an important conceptual consideration for future research.

Future research should test integrated interventions that jointly target mobility, strength, and social participation, ideally using adaptive or stepped-care designs and implementation frameworks that account for resource constraints. Mechanistic studies linking biological aging markers (inflammation, hormonal profiles) with network structures and activity patterns could illuminate pathways linking physical and social vulnerability to depression49,50. Given urban-rural and socioeconomic heterogeneity in China, examining contextual modifiers and the role of community infrastructure may guide equitable deployment of preventive services.

Conclusion

In conclusion, frailty and social isolation independently predict incident depressive symptoms among middle-aged and older adults, and their co-occurrence marks a subgroup at particularly high risk. While interaction findings were suggestive rather than definitive, they support integrating physical and social interventions in prevention strategies. As populations age, aligning geriatric care with social care may offer a scalable path to reduce depression burden and promote healthy aging trajectories.
