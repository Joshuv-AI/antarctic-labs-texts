Entropic Selection Principle for Monge’s Optimal Transport
Shrey Aryan, Promit Ghosal

Abstract
We investigate the small regularization limit of entropic optimal transport when the cost function is the Euclidean distance in dimensions d > 1 d>1 , and the marginal measures are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Our results establish that the limiting optimal transport plan is supported on transport rays . Furthermore, within each transport ray, the limiting transport plan uniquely minimizes a relative entropy functional with respect to specific reference measures supported on the rays. This provides a complete and unique characterization of the limiting transport plan. While similar results have been obtained for d = 1 d=1 in [ DML18 ] and for discrete measures in [ PC20 ] , this work resolves the previously open case in higher dimensions d > 1 . d>1.

Introduction

Optimal transport has emerged as one of the most prominent mathematical fields in recent decades, forging deep connections with various branches of mathematics. The Monge-Kantorovich optimal transport problem aims to transform one probability measure into another while minimizing a specified transportation cost. In Monge’s original formulation, the objective was to minimize the average distance transported. This leads to the challenging problem of finding an optimal transport map that pushes one distribution onto another while preserving mass. The existence and properties of Monge’s optimal transport map have been established under various assumptions through seminal works of Caffarelli, Feldman, and McCann
[
CFM02
]
and Evans and Gangbo
[
EG99a
]
. In particular, Evans and Gangbo constructed Monge’s map by considering the limits of solutions to relaxed optimal transport problems that approximate Monge’s original formulation.

While the classical optimal transport theory has achieved considerable success, its direct implementation is often computationally prohibitive in high-dimensional settings due to the non-smooth nature of the transport problem. A significant driving force behind the recent interest in optimal transport has been the development and popularization of entropic optimal transport, which offers a computationally efficient framework for approximating classical optimal transport problems. The entropic optimal transport problem, pioneered by Cuturi
[
Cut13
]
, modifies the Kantorovich formulation by penalizing the coupling’s deviation from a reference measure through a relative entropy term, enabling faster and more stable numerical algorithms. When the regularization parameter
\varepsilon>0
is small, the entropic optimal transport provides an approximation of the classical optimal transport map.

Given its computational advantages, a natural theoretical question arises: which optimal transport map is selected as the entropic regularization parameter
\varepsilon
approaches zero, especially in Monge’s optimal transport problem? Understanding this limiting behavior is crucial for bridging the gap between the entropic and classical optimal transport theories. While the existence of limits has been established under various settings, the precise characterization of the limiting transport map remains an active area of research, especially when the transport cost lacks strict convexity or smoothness. This paper seeks to address this question by providing a detailed variational characterization of the entropic limit, offering new theoretical insights into the interplay between entropic regularization and Monge’s optimal transport map.

Given two probability measures,
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathds{R}^{d})
, the Monge-Kantorovich formulation of Monge’s optimal transport problem seeks to find an optimal coupling
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathds{R}^{d}\times\mathds{R}^{d})
satisfying

\displaystyle\pi:=\argmin_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int\|x-y\|\ \mathrm{d}\pi(x,y),

(1.1)

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm and
\Gamma(\mu,\nu):=\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathds{R}^{d}\times\mathds{R}^{d}):(\operatorname{proj}_{X})_{\#}\pi=\mu,\ (\operatorname{proj}_{Y})_{\#}\pi=\nu\}.

Caffarelli, Feldman, and McCann
[
CFM02
]
, along with Evans and Gangbo
[
EG99a
]
, constructed a solution to (
1.1
) in the form
\pi=(Id\otimes T)\#\mu
, where
T
is an optimal transport map that pushes
\mu
to
\nu
along straight-line paths, commonly referred to as transport rays connecting
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{Y}
(see Section
2.2
). The entropic regularization of the above optimal transport problem is

\displaystyle\pi^{\varepsilon}:=\argmin_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|\mathrm{d}\pi+\varepsilon\mathsf{H}(\pi\mid\mu\otimes\nu)\right),

(1.2)

where
\mathsf{H}
represents the relative entropy defined by

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}(\pi\mid P):=\begin{cases}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi}{\mathrm{~d}P}\right)\mathrm{d}\pi,&\text{ if }\pi\ll P\\
\infty,&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}.

(1.3)

The variational problem described above is equivalent to stating that
\pi^{\varepsilon}
minimizes the relative entropy
\mathsf{H}(\pi\mid e^{-\|x-y\|/\varepsilon}\mu\otimes\nu)
among all couplings
\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
.
As
\varepsilon\to 0
, the entropically regularized coupling
\pi^{\varepsilon}
weakly converges to a coupling
\tilde{\pi}_{\text{opt}}
that minimizes (
1.1
). Since the cost
c(x,y):=\|x-y\|
is not strictly convex, the Monge-Kantorovich problem generally admits infinitely many minimizers (cf. Remark 2.7.4 in
[
FG23
]
), leaving the selection of
\tilde{\pi}_{\text{opt}}
unclear as
\varepsilon
vanishes. While Marino and Louet
[
DML18
]
addressed this issue when
d=1
, the higher-dimensional case has remained an important open problem. In this paper, we resolve this question by providing a variational characterization of the limiting coupling
\tilde{\pi}_{\text{opt}}
when
d\geq 2.

To state our main theorem, we introduce some notation. Let
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be compact sets, and let
\mu
and
\nu
be probability measures with densities
p:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
q:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
respectively. Let
\mathcal{R}
be a
maximal
transport ray, and denote by
\mu_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}}
and
\nu_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}}
the restrictions of
\mu
and
\nu
to
\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{Y}
, respectively (see Section
2.3
for more details about their definitions). The limiting coupling
\tilde{\pi}_{\text{opt}}
is then characterized as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
\pi^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\tilde{\pi}^{\text{opt}}
be the weak limit of the optimal entropic coupling as
\varepsilon\to 0.
Then
\tilde{\pi}^{\text{opt}}
can be characterized as the minimizer of the following cost functional

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}^{\text{opt}}_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}}=\operatorname{argmin}\left\{\mathsf{H}\big(\pi|\mathcal{F}(x,y)\mu_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}}\otimes\nu_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}}\big):\pi\in\Gamma(\mu_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}},\nu_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}})\right\}

(1.4)

for any transport ray
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is defined as

\mathcal{F}(x,y):=(2\pi\|x-y\|)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}e^{c\|x-y\|}

where
c\geq 0
is a constant which depends on
\mu
,
\nu
and the transport ray
\mathcal{R}
.

Remark 1.2
.

Since the set of couplings
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
is compact in the weak topology, and any limiting coupling of the sequence
\pi^{\varepsilon}
is uniquely characterized by (
1.4
), Theorem
1.1
establishes that
\pi^{\varepsilon}
indeed converges to the limiting coupling
\tilde{\pi}^{\text{opt}}
as described by (
1.4
). It is important to note that Theorem
1.1
does not explicitly specify the value of the constant
c
in
\mathcal{F}(x,y)
. This constant depends implicitly on the limiting behavior of the derivatives of the entropic optimal transport potentials. Further details regarding this dependency will be provided in Section
1.1
.

1.1
Proof Sketch

The primary objective of this section is to demonstrate how the entropic optimal transport approach selects a unique optimal transport plan as the regularization parameter
\varepsilon\to 0
. For norm cost
\|x-y\|
, which is not strictly convex, the corresponding optimal transport map is not unique. However,
[
CFM02
]
constructed a “potential” Monge map, which transports mass along transport rays. These transport rays were first introduced by
[
EG99b
]
and are defined as line segments connecting points in the supporting domains of the marginal measures. Along these rays, the Kantorovich potentials decrease linearly.

The work in
[
EG99b
]
shows that the union of all transport rays contains the union of the supporting domains, and further, these rays are disjoint (see Section
2.2
). Moreover, optimal transport plans with respect to the norm cost are supported on these transport rays (see Lemma
5.3
for proof). Thus, to establish Theorem
1.1
, it suffices to investigate how
\pi^{\varepsilon}
, the entropic optimal transport plan, aligns with the transport rays as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
For a transport ray
\mathcal{R}
, consider cylinders
\mathfrak{C}\subset\mathcal{X}
and
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{Y}
of radius
\omega\ll 1
around the segments
\vec{aa^{\prime}}\subset\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{X}
and
\vec{dd^{\prime}}\subset\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{Y}
respectively. We show that

\displaystyle\pi^{\varepsilon}(\mathfrak{C}\times\mathfrak{C}^{\prime})\sim\omega^{d-1}\int_{\vec{aa^{\prime}}\times\vec{dd^{\prime}}}d\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}}(x,y),\qquad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

(1.5)

Here
\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}}
is same as in Theorem
1.1
. Now the final result follows by the weak convergence of
\pi^{\varepsilon}
to
\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{opt}}
as
\epsilon\to 0
and further, letting
\omega\to 0
. Now we explain how we obtain the above approximation as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Since
\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}}
solves an entropic optimal transport problem between
\mu_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}}
and
\nu_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}}
as revealed in (
1.4
), we know

\frac{d\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}}}{d(\mu_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}}\otimes\nu_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}})}(x,y)=(2\pi\|x-y\|)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}e^{c\|x-y\|}e^{\mathfrak{f}(x)+\mathfrak{g}(y)}

where
\mathfrak{f}:\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\mathfrak{g}:\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
are the potential functions of the entropic optimal transport problem in (
1.4
). We explain here how we obtain the form of the Radon-Nikodym derivative in the above display from the density of
\pi^{\varepsilon}
with respect to
\mu\otimes\nu
, i.e.,

\frac{d\pi^{\varepsilon}}{d(\mu\otimes\nu)}(x,y)=\exp\Big(-\frac{\|x-y\|}{\varepsilon}+\frac{(f_{\varepsilon}(x)+g_{\varepsilon}(y))}{\varepsilon}\Big).

We define
\tilde{I}(x,y):=\|x-y\|-u(x_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}})+u(y_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}})
for all
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
,
u
is the Kantorovich potential of the optimal transport plan between
\mu
and
\nu
,
(f_{\varepsilon},g_{\varepsilon})
are entropic optimal transport potential and
(x_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}},y_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}})
are the orthogonal projection of
(x,y)
onto
\mathcal{R}
. With
I(x,y)
, we rewrite

\|x-y\|-(f_{\varepsilon}(x)+g_{\varepsilon}(y))=I(x,y)-(f_{\varepsilon}(x)-u(x_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}})+g_{\varepsilon}(y)+u(y_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}})).

Along the transport ray
\mathcal{R}
,
I(x,y)
vanishes for any
(x,y)\in(\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{X})\times(\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{Y})
. Furthermore, for any
x+\sqrt{\varepsilon}x^{\perp}\in\mathcal{X}
and
y+\sqrt{\varepsilon}y^{\perp}\in\mathcal{Y}
with
(x,y)\in\mathcal{R}
and
x^{\perp},y^{\perp}
are orthogonal to the direction of the transport ray
\mathcal{R}
,
I(\cdot,\cdot)
expands as

I(x+\sqrt{\varepsilon}x^{\perp},y+\sqrt{\varepsilon}y^{\perp})\sim\varepsilon(1+o(1))\frac{\|J(x)x^{\perp}-J(y)y^{\perp}\|^{2}}{\|x-y\|}

where
J(x)
is the Jacobian matrix of transformation of local coordinates around transport rays. A detailed discussion of this expansion can be found in Lemma
3.1
. As
\varepsilon\to 0
, the entropic optimal transport potentials expand as

\displaystyle\frac{f_{\varepsilon}(x+\sqrt{\varepsilon}x^{\perp})-u(x)}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{f}(x)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(1+o(1))\langle v_{\varepsilon},J(x)x^{\perp}\rangle,

\displaystyle\frac{g_{\varepsilon}(x+\sqrt{\varepsilon}x^{\perp})+u(x)}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{g}(y)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(1+o(1))\langle v_{\varepsilon},J(y)y^{\perp}\rangle

for some
v_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Combining these expansion of
I(\cdot,\cdot)
and
(f_{\varepsilon},g_{\varepsilon})
yields

\displaystyle\frac{d\pi^{\varepsilon}}{d(\mu\otimes\nu)}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(-(1+o(1))\frac{\|J(x)x^{\perp}-J(y)y^{\perp}\|^{2}}{\|x-y\|}

(1.6)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(1+o(1))\langle v,J(x)x^{\perp}-J(y)y^{\perp}\rangle\Big)\exp(\mathfrak{f}(x)+\mathfrak{g}(y)).

(1.7)

Furthermore, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
v_{\varepsilon}/\sqrt{\varepsilon}
converges. See Theorem
4.1
for its proof. Notice that the above reductions show that the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\pi^{\varepsilon}
with respect to
\mu\otimes\nu
locally behaves as a multivariate Gaussian distribution with respect to
x^{\perp}
and
y^{\perp}
. Integrating this density around a local neighborhood of
\mathcal{R}
of width
\omega
shows

\pi^{\varepsilon}(\mathfrak{C}\times\mathfrak{C}^{\prime})\sim\omega^{d-1}\int_{\vec{aa^{\prime}}\times\vec{dd^{\prime}}}(2\pi\|x-y\|)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}e^{c\|x-y\|}e^{\mathfrak{f}(x)+\mathfrak{g}(y)}d(\mu_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}}\otimes\nu_{\llcorner\mathcal{R}})(x,y).

Here, the constant
c
is equal to
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|v_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}/\varepsilon.
The proof of the above display is primarily contained in Lemma
3.1
, where a more general result is established. The proof of Lemma
3.1
relies on a multi-scale argument, distinguishing between contributions from regions near the transport ray and those farther away. Specifically, the contribution from a close neighborhood of the transport ray is analyzed using the expansion of the entropic optimal transport potential, as explained earlier. In contrast, the contribution from regions farther from the transport ray is estimated using Lemma
5.2
, which serves as a critical component of the proof of Lemma
3.1
.
By proving the above display via Lemma
3.1
, we establish the validity of (
1.5
), thereby completing the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

1.2
Background

The Monge-Kantorovich problem arises as a relaxation of the Monge problem, first proposed by Monge in 1781
[
Mon81
]
. Given probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathds{R}^{d})
, the goal of the Monge problem is to find an optimal transport map
T
minimizing

\displaystyle\inf\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\|x-T(x)\|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

(1.8)

under the constraint that
T
preserves mass, i.e.,
T_{\#}\mu=\nu
.
While simple examples (e.g., when
\mu
is a Dirac measure and
\nu
is the standard Gaussian) show that (
1.8
) does not always admit a minimizer, Monge’s insight introduced the concept of transport rays, which became central to subsequent developments. Sudakov
[
Sud79
]
was the first to attempt solving the Monge problem by decomposing
\mathds{R}^{d}
into affine regions where the transport problem could be solved explicitly, and then gluing these solutions to construct an optimal transport map. However, this approach relied on the incorrect assumption that the absolute continuity of
\mu
with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{d}
implied the absolute continuity of the measures induced by the decomposition (cf. Section 6 in
[
Amb03
]
). Several years later, Evans and Gangbo
[
EG99a
]
introduced new analytical tools and resolved the Monge problem under the assumptions
\mu,\nu\ll\mathcal{L}^{d}
with Lipschitz densities and
\operatorname{supp}\mu\cap\operatorname{supp}\nu=\emptyset
. Subsequently, Caffarelli–Feldman–McCann
[
CFM02
]
and Trudinger–Wang
[
TW01
]
independently resolved the Monge problem for absolutely continuous measures with compact support in
\mathds{R}^{d}
. Ambrosio
[
Amb03
]
later established the existence of an optimal transport map under the very general assumption
\mu\ll\mathcal{L}^{d}
without requiring additional regularity assumptions. Furthermore, Feldman and McCann
[
FM02
]
extended the results of
[
CFM02
]
and
[
TW01
]
to the Riemannian setting. On the other hand, when the cost function is squared distance, the seminal works of Brenier
[
Bre91
]
and McCann
[
McC01
]
provide a complete characterization of the transport map as the gradient of a convex potential. Later this result was generalized for any strictly convex cost function by Gangbo and McCann
[
GM96
]
.

In recent years, optimal transport has garnered significant attention for its computational applications in fields such as statistics, machine learning, and economics. For a comprehensive overview, see
[
PC20
]
. Among various computational approaches, the entropic regularization method popularized by Cuturi
[
Cut13
]
has become a particularly effective tool for approximating optimal transport plans efficiently, especially in large-scale settings. By incorporating a relative entropy term into the classical Kantorovich formulation, this method reformulates the problem into a computationally tractable form. It enables the use of scalable iterative algorithms, such as Sinkhorn’s algorithm, which are widely adopted due to their robustness and efficiency in high-dimensional applications.

Beyond its computational applications, entropic optimal transport has been the focus of significant theoretical developments. While it is not possible to list all contributions in this rapidly growing field, we highlight a few results that are particularly relevant to our work. Gamma-convergence techniques have demonstrated that weak limits of entropic optimizers correspond to solutions of the classical Monge-Kantorovich problem
[
L1́2
,
CDPS17
]
. This result has been extended to more general settings with non-integrable cost function via
(c,\varepsilon)
-cyclical invariance in
[
BGN22
]
where large deviation principles have been used to quantify convergence rates. Furthermore, asymptotic expansions of the entropic cost as
\varepsilon\to 0
have been explored in
[
Pal24
,
CT21
]
, while
[
NW22a
]
examined the convergence of Schrödinger potentials in Polish spaces. Regarding stability,
[
GNB22
]
established the robustness of entropically regularized solutions under variations in both the marginals and the cost function. We refer to
[
Nut21
,
CNR24
]
for more details.

Despite significant progress, understanding the precise behavior of entropically regularized couplings as
\varepsilon\to 0
remains a challenging open problem, particularly when the cost function lacks strict convexity. This question has been partially addressed in the discrete setting by
[
PC20
, Proposition 4.1]
, where the authors demonstrated that the limit of the entropic optimal transport maximizes entropy among all optimal transport couplings.

In the continuous setting, this question has been resolved only in one dimension with the linear cost function
c(x,y)=|x-y|
. Marino and Louet
[
DML18
]
provided a unique characterization of the limiting optimal transport coupling as the solution to a variational problem. However, extending this result to higher dimensions
d>1
has remained an open problem. In this work, we address this gap by establishing a variational
characterization of the limiting coupling in
our main result, Theorem
1.1
.

Entropic optimal transport is also known as the (static) Schrödinger bridge problem. The Schrödinger problem explores the most likely evolution of a particle system in thermal equilibrium, given a reference law
R
for its configuration at initial and final times
t_{0}
and
t_{1}
, and observed marginals
(\mu,\nu)
that differ from those of
R
. The solution minimizes the relative entropy
H(\cdot|R)
over all couplings
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
, providing the optimal joint law
\pi^{*}
. The minimization of
H(\cdot|R)
over
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
aligns with the entropic optimal transport problem when the cost function
c
is defined as
c:=-\varepsilon\log(\alpha^{-1}dR/d(\mu\otimes\nu))
, where
\varepsilon>0
and
\alpha
is a normalizing constant. While this is the static formulation, the dynamic Schrödinger bridge problem extends this framework to model the continuous evolution of the particle system over
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
, with the static problem serving as a projection to the marginals. For detailed surveys and historical context see
[
Nut21
]
.

A large deviations principle arises in the small-noise or small-time limit, where the reference measure
R(\varepsilon)
collapses to a deterministic coupling, mapping a particle at
x
to a fixed destination
f(x)

[
BGN22
]
. Theorem
1.1
extends this analysis by characterizing the small-noise limit of the static Schrödinger bridge problem when the cost function
c
corresponds to the distance cost. Investigating whether this principle can be generalized to the dynamic Schrödinger bridge problem on the path space remains an intriguing open question.

A fundamental question in the study of any regularization scheme in the calculus of variations is understanding the behavior of minimizers as the regularization parameter tends to zero. This question resonates across many areas of mathematics, where regularization often plays a crucial role in selecting meaningful solutions. In fluid dynamics, the inviscid limit problem (cf.
[
CKV15
]
) investigates the asymptotic behavior of solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations as the viscosity parameter approaches zero. In differential geometry, the seminal work of Sacks and Uhlenbeck
[
SU81
]
introduced a perturbed Dirichlet energy to construct minimal immersed spheres, illustrating how regularization schemes can lead to geometrically significant minimizers. Similarly, in game theory, recent works
[
HL19
,
BZ20
]
have shown that in cases where mean field games exhibit non-unique Nash equilibria, the equilibria that arise as limits of finite
N
Nash equilibria, as
N
tends to infinity, correspond precisely to the entropy solution. See also
[
BCN18
]
for the connection between Cournot-Nash equilibria and entropic optimal transport. Building on this foundation, our work contributes to the broader research landscape by providing a rigorous variational characterization of the limiting coupling
\tilde{\pi}_{\text{opt}}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. This addresses fundamental theoretical questions within the framework of the entropic Monge-Kantorovich problem, advancing the understanding of how entropic regularization selects specific optimal transport plans.

1.3
Structure of the Paper

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we provide the necessary preliminaries, including the formulation of the entropic optimal transport problem and its connection to the classical Monge-Kantorovich problem. We also review key results on transport rays and define the restriction of measures to these rays. In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
1.1
, by establishing a variational characterization of the limiting coupling
\tilde{\pi}_{\text{opt}}
as the entropic regularization parameter
\varepsilon\to 0
. Finally, Section
4
examines the asymptotic behavior of entropic potentials around transport rays. Through precise expansions, we derive their limiting form, which plays a crucial role in the proof of our main result.

Recall that
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
denote the space of all probability measures supported on
\mathcal{X}
. Additionally, recall that
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
. We now outline the following assumptions on the measure spaces
(\mathcal{X},\mu)
and
(\mathcal{Y},\nu)
.

1.

\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that are quantitatively disjoint, i.e.
\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{Y}=\emptyset
and
\operatorname{dist}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})>c_{0}

2.

\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=p(x)\mathrm{d}x

2.1
Dual Formulation

Let
v_{0}
be the optimal transport cost function in (
1.1
).
Given
\varepsilon>0
, let
v_{\varepsilon}
be the entropic optimal transport cost function in (
1.2
).
Alternatively,
v_{0}
and
v_{\varepsilon}
can be characterized using a dual formulation

\displaystyle v_{0}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}\left(\mathcal{X}\right)\\
g\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}\left(\mathcal{Y}\right)\end{subarray}}\left\{\int_{\mathcal{X}}f\ \mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}g\ \mathrm{d}\nu:f(x)+g(y)\leq\|x-y\|,\forall(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\right\},

(2.1)

and

\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{\varepsilon}\in L^{1}_{\mu}\left(\mathcal{X}\right)\\
g_{\varepsilon}\in L^{1}_{\nu}\left(\mathcal{Y}\right)\end{subarray}}\left\{\int_{\mathcal{X}}f_{\varepsilon}\ \mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}g_{\varepsilon}\ \mathrm{d}\nu-\varepsilon\log\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}e^{\frac{f_{\varepsilon}(x)+g_{\varepsilon}(y)-\|x-y\|}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}\nu(y)\right\}

(2.2)

where
\operatorname{Lip}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
denotes the space of all real-valued Lipschitz functions on
\mathcal{X}
with Lipschitz constant
\leq 1

denotes the space of real-valued integrable functions with respect to the measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}.
Any pair
(f_{K},g_{K})
minimizing (
2.1
) is called a Kantorovich potential whereas the pair
(f_{\varepsilon},g_{\varepsilon})
minimizing (
2.2
) is called an entropic optimal transport (EOT) potential.

2.2
Transport Rays

We present some relevant facts about transport rays following
[
CFM02
]
. There exists
u\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}(\mathds{R}^{d})
such that the Kantorovich potential
(f_{K},g_{K})
in (
2.1
) can be written as

\displaystyle u(x)=-f_{K}(x)\text{ on }\mathcal{X},\quad u(y)=g_{K}(y)\text{ on }\mathcal{Y}.

(2.3)

A transport ray
\mathcal{R}
is a segment with endpoints
a
,
b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

1.

a\in\mathcal{X},b\in\mathcal{Y},a\neq b
,

2.

u(a)-u(b)=\|a-b\|
, and

3.

for any

Limit Theory of EOT Potentials

In this section, we establish the expansion of the entropic optimal transport potentials in the vicinity of a transport ray. Earlier works, such as
[

]

\varepsilon\to 0

\varepsilon

\varepsilon

Using the relation

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\log\int_{\mathcal{Y}}e^{\frac{g_{\varepsilon}(y)-\|x-y\|}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}\nu(y),\quad\mu\text{-a.s. }

(4.25)

we get

\displaystyle\frac{f_{\varepsilon}(x)+u(x)}{\varepsilon}+\log\int_{\mathcal{Y}}e^{\frac{-I(x,y)+g_{\varepsilon}(y)-u(y)}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}\nu(y)=0

(4.26)

where
I(x,y)=\|x-y\|+u(x)-u(y).
For any given
x
, we denote
y_{x}
to be any
y\in\mathcal{R}_{x}\cap\mathcal{Y}
such that
I(x,y)\equiv 0
. Furthermore, for points outside a
\delta
-neighbourhood of the transport ray, there exists
c_{0}=c_{0}(\delta)>0
such that
I(x,y)\geq c_{0}>0.
Therefore, if we assume that
x\in S^{i}_{p}
for some
i\in\mathbb{N},p\in\mathbb{Q}
, it suffices to estimate the integral on the domain
D_{\delta}=\bigcup_{z\in B_{\delta}(x)\cap S^{i}_{p}}\mathcal{R}_{z}\cap\mathcal{Y}
,

\displaystyle\frac{f_{\varepsilon}(x)+u(x)}{\varepsilon}=-\log\int_{D_{\delta}}e^{\frac{-I(x,y)+g_{\varepsilon}(y)-u(y)}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}\nu(y)+O(\varepsilon^{-1}c_{0}(\delta)),

(4.27)

where we also used the fact that the potentials
g_{\varepsilon}
and
u
are bounded on compact sets. Consider variations of
x
along the level set
S^{i}_{p},
i.e.
x_{\delta}=\operatorname{Proj}_{S^{i}_{p}}(x+\delta\chi^{\perp})
where
\chi^{\perp}
is an unit vector orthogonal to the direction of transport ray
\mathcal{R}_{x}
. We estimate the derivative in the normal direction of the equation (
4.27
) as follows

\displaystyle\frac{f_{\varepsilon}(x_{\delta})+u(x_{\delta})-(f_{\varepsilon}(x)+u(x))}{\varepsilon}

(4.28)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon}\langle\nabla f_{\varepsilon}(x),\chi^{\perp}\rangle+O(\varepsilon^{-1}\delta)

(4.29)

where we used the fact that
u(x_{\delta})-u(x)=O(\delta)
since
\langle\chi^{\perp},\nabla u(x)\rangle=0.
From the integral term in (
4.27
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{D_{\delta}}\left(e^{\frac{f(x_{\delta},y)}{\varepsilon}}-e^{\frac{f(x,y)}{\varepsilon}}\right)\ \mathrm{d}\nu(y)

(4.30)

\displaystyle\int_{D_{\delta}}e^{\frac{f(x,y)}{\varepsilon}}\left(e^{\frac{f(x_{\delta},y)-f(x,y)}{\varepsilon}}-1\right)\ \mathrm{d}\nu(y)

(4.31)

\displaystyle=\int_{D_{\delta}}\left(\frac{f(x_{\delta},y)-f(x,y)}{\varepsilon}+O(\varepsilon^{-1}\delta)\right)e^{\frac{f(x,y)}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}\nu(y),

(4.32)

where
f
is defined as follows

\displaystyle f(x,y)

\displaystyle:=-I(x,y)+g_{\varepsilon}(y)-u(y).

(4.33)

Recall that Lemma
2.1
gives local coordinate function
G
mapping a ray cluster to
\mathds{R}^{d-1}\times\mathds{R}
along with its inverse map
F.
Thus, we can express
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
y\in D_{\delta}
as

\displaystyle\tilde{x}=G(x),\quad\tilde{x}=\mathfrak{x},\quad\tilde{y}=G(y),\quad\tilde{y}=\mathfrak{y}+\mathfrak{y}^{\perp}

(4.34)

where
\mathfrak{x}
and
\mathfrak{y}
are orthogonal projections of the points
\tilde{x},\tilde{y}
onto the transport
\mathcal{R}_{x}
. Expanding
I
and
g_{\varepsilon}
as in (
3.22
) and (
3.23
) we get

\displaystyle f(x,y)=-\frac{\|(\overline{\tfrac{\partial F(\mathfrak{y})}{\partial\mathfrak{y}}})\mathfrak{y}^{\perp}\|^{2}}{2\|F(\mathfrak{x})-F(\mathfrak{y})\|}+g_{\varepsilon}(F(\mathfrak{{y}}))+\langle\nabla g_{\varepsilon}(F(\mathfrak{y})),(\overline{\tfrac{\partial F(\mathfrak{y})}{\partial\mathfrak{y}}})\mathfrak{y}^{\perp}\rangle-u(F(\mathfrak{y}))+O(\delta).

(4.35)

Similarly, we can write

\displaystyle\widetilde{x_{\delta}}=G(x_{\delta}),\quad\widetilde{x_{\delta}}=\mathfrak{\bar{x}},\quad\tilde{y}=\mathfrak{\mathfrak{\bar{y}}}+\mathfrak{\bar{y}}^{\perp}

(4.36)

where
\mathfrak{\bar{x}},\mathfrak{\bar{y}}
are orthogonal projections of the points
\widetilde{x_{\delta}}
and
\tilde{y}
on the transport ray
\mathcal{R}_{x_{\delta}}
. Thus, we get

\displaystyle f(x_{\delta},y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\|(\overline{\tfrac{\partial F(\mathfrak{\bar{y}})}{\partial\mathfrak{{y}}}}){\mathfrak{\bar{y}}}^{\perp}\|^{2}}{2\|F(\mathfrak{\bar{x}})-F(\mathfrak{\bar{y}})\|}+g_{\varepsilon}(F(\mathfrak{\bar{y}}))+\langle\nabla g_{\varepsilon}(F(\mathfrak{\bar{y}})),(\overline{\tfrac{\partial F(\mathfrak{\bar{y}})}{\partial\mathfrak{y}}})\mathfrak{\bar{y}}^{\perp}\rangle-u(F(\mathfrak{\bar{y}}))+O(\delta).

(4.37)

By Lemma
2.2
the distance between the transport rays emanating from
x
and
x_{\delta}
is of order
O(\delta)
, i.e.
\|\mathfrak{y}-\mathfrak{\bar{y}}\|+\|\mathfrak{\bar{y}}^{\perp}-\mathfrak{y}^{\perp}\|=O(\delta)
. Furthermore, since
\|x_{\delta}-x\|=O(\delta)
and
G
is a Lipschitz map we have
\|\mathfrak{x}-\mathfrak{\bar{x}}\|=O(\delta).
Thus, if we further decompose
\mathfrak{\bar{y}}^{\perp}=\mathfrak{z}+\mathfrak{z}^{\perp}
, where
\mathfrak{z}^{\perp}
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{R}_{x}
, and omit higher order terms, we get

\displaystyle f(x_{\delta},y)-f(x,y)=\delta\langle\nabla g_{\varepsilon}(F(\mathfrak{y})),\tilde{\chi}^{\perp}\rangle+o(\delta)

(4.38)

where
\tilde{\chi}^{\perp}=\frac{\mathfrak{\bar{z}}^{\prime\perp}-\mathfrak{{y}}^{\prime\perp}}{\|\mathfrak{\bar{z}}^{\prime\perp}-\mathfrak{{y}}^{\prime\perp}\|}
,
\mathfrak{\bar{z}}^{\prime\perp}=(\overline{\tfrac{\partial F(\mathfrak{\bar{y}})}{\partial\mathfrak{y}}})\mathfrak{\bar{z}}^{\perp}
and
\mathfrak{y}^{\prime\perp}=(\overline{\tfrac{\partial F(\mathfrak{{y}})}{\partial\mathfrak{y}}})\mathfrak{{y}}^{\perp}.
This implies that

\displaystyle=\int_{D_{\delta}}\left(e^{\frac{f(x+\delta\chi^{\perp},y)}{\varepsilon}}-e^{\frac{f(x,y)}{\varepsilon}}\right)\ \mathrm{d}\nu(y)

(4.39)

\displaystyle=\int_{D_{\delta}}\left(\frac{f(x+\delta\chi^{\perp},y)-f(x,y)}{\varepsilon}+O(\varepsilon^{-1}\delta)\right)e^{\frac{f(x,y)}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}\nu(y)

(4.40)

\displaystyle=\int_{G(D_{\delta})}\left(\frac{\delta\langle\nabla g_{\varepsilon}(F(\mathfrak{y})),\tilde{\chi}^{\perp}\rangle}{\varepsilon}+O(\varepsilon^{-1}\delta)\right)d\zeta^{g_{\varepsilon}}(\mathfrak{y}),

(4.41)

where
d\zeta^{g_{\varepsilon}}(\mathfrak{y})\sim e^{\frac{f(x,y)}{\varepsilon}}\ q(F(\mathfrak{y}))J(F(\mathfrak{y}))(1+O(\delta))\ \mathrm{d}\mathfrak{y}_{1}^{\perp}\cdots\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{y}_{d-1}^{\perp}\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{y}_{d}
. Combining (
4.28
) and (
4.39
) we get

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon}\langle\nabla f_{\varepsilon}(x),\chi^{\perp}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\log\frac{\int_{D_{\delta}}e^{\frac{f(x+\delta\chi^{\perp},y)}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}\nu(y)}{\int_{D_{\delta}}e^{\frac{f(x,y)}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}\nu(y)}+O(\varepsilon^{-1}\delta)

(4.42)

\displaystyle=-\log\left(1+\frac{\int_{D_{\delta}}\left(e^{\frac{f(x+\delta\chi^{\perp},y)}{\varepsilon}}-e^{\frac{f(x,y)}{\varepsilon}}\right)\ \mathrm{d}\nu(y)}{\int_{D_{\delta}}e^{\frac{f(x,y)}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}\nu(y)}\right)+O(\varepsilon^{-1}\delta)

(4.43)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon}\frac{\int_{G(D_{\delta})}\langle\nabla g_{\varepsilon}(F(\mathfrak{y})),\tilde{\chi}^{\perp}\rangle d\zeta^{g_{\varepsilon}}(\mathfrak{y})}{\int_{D_{\delta}}e^{\frac{f(x,y)}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}\nu(y)}+O(\varepsilon^{-1}\delta^{2})+O(\varepsilon^{-1}\delta)

(4.44)

and therefore the above equation yields

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f_{\varepsilon}(x),\chi^{\perp}\rangle=-\mathbb{E}_{\zeta^{g_{\varepsilon}}}\left[\langle\nabla g_{\varepsilon}(y_{x}),\tilde{\chi}^{\perp}\rangle\right]+O(\delta)

(4.45)

where we abuse notation and denote the normalized measure by
\zeta^{g_{\varepsilon}}
. Repeating the argument with
g_{\varepsilon}
we get

\displaystyle\langle\nabla g_{\varepsilon}(y),\chi^{\perp}\rangle=-\mathbb{E}_{\zeta^{f_{\varepsilon}}}\left[\langle\nabla f_{\varepsilon}(x_{y}),\bar{\chi}^{\perp}\rangle\right]+O(\delta)

(4.46)

where the above expectation is with respect to the same measure where instead of
g_{\varepsilon}
in (
4.33
) we use
f_{\varepsilon}.
Then

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f_{\varepsilon}(x),\chi^{\perp}\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{\zeta^{f_{\varepsilon}}}\mathbb{E}_{\zeta^{g_{\varepsilon}}}\left[\langle\nabla f_{\varepsilon},\chi^{\prime}\rangle\right]+O(\delta)

(4.47)

where
\chi^{\prime}
is some vector orthogonal to the transport ray
\mathcal{R}_{x}.
Then we first claim that
\langle\nabla f_{\varepsilon},\chi^{\perp}\rangle
converges to a constant when
\delta\to 0.
This follows from the fact that the minimum value of the function
F_{\varepsilon}(x,\chi^{\perp})=\langle\nabla f_{\varepsilon}(x),\chi^{\perp}\rangle
on a compact set
(x,\chi^{\perp})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
, denoted by
F^{\operatorname{min}}_{\varepsilon}
satisfies

\displaystyle\left|F^{\operatorname{min}}_{\varepsilon}-\mathbb{E}_{\zeta^{f_{\varepsilon}}}\mathbb{E}_{\zeta^{g_{\varepsilon}}}\left[F_{\varepsilon}\right]\right|\lesssim\delta\to 0

(4.48)

when
\delta=\varepsilon^{1/2-\kappa}
,
\kappa\in(0,1/2)
and
\varepsilon\to 0.
The same argument implies that
\langle\nabla g_{\varepsilon},\chi^{\perp}\rangle
converges as
\varepsilon\to 0.
The fact that the derivatives of
f_{\varepsilon}
and
g_{\varepsilon}
converge to the same constant up to a sign follows from sending
\delta=\varepsilon^{1/2-\kappa}\to 0
in (
4.45
).

where
D_{\delta}=\bigcup_{z\in B_{\delta}(x)\cap S^{i}_{p}}\mathcal{R}_{z}\cap\mathcal{Y}
for some
\delta>0
small enough where

2.2

\displaystyle\tilde{x}:=G(x)=\mathfrak{x}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\mathfrak{x}^{\perp},\quad\tilde{y}:=G(y)=\mathfrak{y}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\mathfrak{y}^{\perp},

(4.51)

for some
\mathfrak{x}^{\perp},\mathfrak{y}^{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}

\chi
of
\mathcal{R}_{x}

(4.52)

\displaystyle\times J(F(V(\mathfrak{y}_{1}.\ldots,\mathfrak{y}_{d-1})+\mathfrak{y}_{d}\nu(V(\mathfrak{y}_{1}.\ldots,\mathfrak{y}_{d-1}))))(1+O(\delta))d\mathfrak{y}^{\perp}_{1}\ldots d\mathfrak{y}^{\perp}_{d-1}d\mathfrak{y}_{d}

(4.53)

\displaystyle=\|F(\tilde{x})-F(\tilde{y})\|-\|F(\mathfrak{x})-F(\mathfrak{y})\|=\varepsilon\frac{\|(\overline{\tfrac{\partial F(\mathfrak{x})}{\partial\mathfrak{x}}})\mathfrak{x}^{\perp}-(\overline{\tfrac{\partial F(\mathfrak{y})}{\partial\mathfrak{y}}})\mathfrak{y}^{\perp}\|^{2}}{2\|F(\mathfrak{x})-F(\mathfrak{y})\|}+O(\varepsilon\delta)

\displaystyle=-\log\int_{D_{\delta}}e^{-I(x,y)/\varepsilon+\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(y)}\ \mathrm{d}\nu(y)+O(e^{-O(1/\varepsilon)})

\displaystyle\quad\times q(y)e^{\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(F(\mathfrak{y}))+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\langle\nabla\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(F(\mathfrak{y})),\bar{\mathfrak{x}}^{\perp}\rangle}\ \mathrm{d}\bar{\mathfrak{y}}^{\perp}_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathfrak{y}}^{\perp}_{d-1}d\mathfrak{y}_{d}+o_{\varepsilon}(1).

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{\varepsilon}(x)=-\log\varepsilon^{(d-1)/2}\int_{\mathcal{R}_{x}^{\circ}\cap\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle(2\pi\|\bar{\mathfrak{x}}^{\perp}_{d}-\bar{\mathfrak{y}}^{\perp}_{d}\|)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}e^{\frac{\|\nabla\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(F(\mathfrak{y}))\|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\|\bar{\mathfrak{x}}^{\perp}_{d}-\bar{\mathfrak{y}}^{\perp}_{d}\|}

\displaystyle\times e^{\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(y)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\langle\nabla\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(F(\mathfrak{y})),\bar{\mathfrak{x}}^{\perp}\rangle}q(y)\ \mathrm{d}\mathfrak{y}_{d}+o_{\varepsilon}(1)

(4.61)

and thus, we define
\Theta^{1}_{\varepsilon}
as a function of the distance along the

\displaystyle\Theta^{1}_{\varepsilon}:=(2\pi\|\bar{\mathfrak{x}}^{\perp}_{d}-\bar{\mathfrak{y}}^{\perp}_{d}\|)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}e^{\frac{\|\nabla\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(F(\mathfrak{y}))\|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\|\bar{\mathfrak{x}}^{\perp}_{d}-\bar{\mathfrak{y}}^{\perp}_{d}\|}e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\langle\nabla\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(F(\mathfrak{y})),\bar{\mathfrak{x}}^{\perp}\rangle}.

(4.62)

Repeating the same argument yields a similar relation for (

4.3
Proof of Lemma
4.4

We begin by using the dual formulation (
2.2
) with the potentials
(f_{K},g_{K})
attaining the supremum in (
2.1
) to get the following lower bound

\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathcal{X}}f_{K}\ \mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}g_{K}\ \mathrm{d}\nu-\varepsilon\log\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}e^{-\frac{I}{\varepsilon}}\mathrm{~d}\mu\otimes\mathrm{d}\nu\right)

(4.63)

\displaystyle=v_{0}-\varepsilon\log\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}e^{-\frac{I}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}\mu\otimes\mathrm{d}\nu\right),

(4.64)

where
I(x,y)=\|x-y\|-f_{K}(x)-g_{K}(y)
for
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. We claim that for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
C>0
(independent of
\varepsilon
) such that the following holds

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}e^{-I/\varepsilon}\ \mathrm{d}\mu\otimes\mathrm{d}\nu\leq C\varepsilon^{(d-1)/2}.

(4.65)

To prove this we consider the covering described in Lemma
3.1
. By Lemma
5.3
it suffices to prove this estimate on
\bigcup_{p\in\mathbb{Q},i,j\in\mathbb{N}}(T^{0}_{pij}\cap\mathcal{X})\times(T^{0}_{pij}\cap\mathcal{Y}).
Define for any
p\in\mathbb{Q}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{T}_{p}:=\bigcup_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}}(T^{0}_{pij}\cap\mathcal{X})\times(T^{0}_{pij}\cap\mathcal{Y}).

(4.66)

Then to avoid double counting we refine this family of sets
\{\tilde{T}_{p}\}_{p\in\mathbb{Q}}
by defining a disjoint family of sets

\displaystyle\bar{T}_{p_{1}}

\displaystyle:=\tilde{T}_{p_{1}}

(4.67)

\displaystyle\bar{T}_{p_{k}}

\displaystyle:=\tilde{T}_{p_{k}}\setminus\bigcup_{q=1}^{k-1}\bar{T}_{p_{q}},\quad k\geq 2

(4.68)

where
\{{p_{n}}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is an enumeration of the rational numbers. This gives us a disjoint family of sets and therefore it suffices to estimate

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}e^{-I/\varepsilon}\ \mathrm{d}\mu\otimes\mathrm{d}\nu\leq\limsup_{m\to\infty}\int_{\cup_{n=1}^{m}\bar{T}_{p_{n}}}e^{-I/\varepsilon}\ \mathrm{d}\mu\otimes\mathrm{d}\nu.

(4.69)

Now observe that using the covering described in Lemma
3.1
of the
T^{0}_{pij}
by

\displaystyle A^{k}_{\delta}:=\bigcup_{z\in B_{\delta}(x_{k})}\mathcal{R}_{z}\cap\mathcal{X},\quad D^{k}_{\delta}:=\bigcup_{z\in B_{\delta}(x_{k})}\mathcal{R}_{z}\cap\mathcal{Y}

(4.70)

where
\{B_{\delta}(x_{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
covers
S^{i}_{p}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\cup_{n=1}^{m}\bar{T}_{p_{n}}}e^{-\frac{I}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}\mu\otimes\mathrm{d}\nu

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\tilde{T}_{p_{m}}}e^{-\frac{I}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}\mu\otimes\mathrm{d}\nu

(4.71)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k}\int_{A^{k}_{\delta}\times D^{k}_{\delta^{1-\kappa}}}e^{-\frac{I}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}\mu\otimes\mathrm{d}\nu

(4.72)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k}\int_{A^{k}_{\delta}\times\left(D^{k}_{\delta^{1-\kappa}}\right)^{c}}e^{-\frac{I}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}\mu\otimes\mathrm{d}\nu.

(4.73)

Then arguing as in Lemma
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\nu(y)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{(d-1)/2}q(V(\mathfrak{y}_{1}.\ldots,\mathfrak{y}_{d-1})+\mathfrak{y}_{d}\nu(V(\mathfrak{y}_{1}.\ldots,\mathfrak{y}_{d-1})))

(4.74)

\displaystyle\times J(F(V(\mathfrak{y}_{1}.\ldots,\mathfrak{y}_{d-1})+\mathfrak{y}_{d}\nu(V(\mathfrak{y}_{1}.\ldots,\mathfrak{y}_{d-1}))))(1+O(\delta))d\mathfrak{y}^{\perp}_{1}\ldots d\mathfrak{y}^{\perp}_{d-1}d\mathfrak{y}_{d}

(4.75)

\displaystyle I(x,y)

\displaystyle=\|F(\tilde{x})-F(\tilde{y})\|-\|F(\mathfrak{x})-F(\mathfrak{y})\|=\varepsilon\frac{\|(\overline{\tfrac{\partial F(\mathfrak{x})}{\partial\mathfrak{x}}})\mathfrak{x}^{\perp}-(\overline{\tfrac{\partial F(\mathfrak{y})}{\partial\mathfrak{y}}})\mathfrak{y}^{\perp}\|^{2}}{2\|F(\mathfrak{x})-F(\mathfrak{y})\|}+O(\varepsilon\delta)

(4.76)

where
\overline{\tfrac{\partial F(\cdot)}{\partial\cdot}}
denotes
(d-1)\times(d-1)
principle submatrix of
\tfrac{\partial F(\cdot)}{\partial\cdot}
. We use the following notation
\bar{\mathfrak{x}}^{\perp}:=(\overline{\tfrac{\partial F(\mathfrak{x})}{\partial\mathfrak{x}}})\mathfrak{x}^{\perp}
and
\bar{\mathfrak{y}}^{\perp}:=(\overline{\tfrac{\partial F(\mathfrak{y})}{\partial\mathfrak{y}}})\mathfrak{x}^{\perp}
. Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\int_{A^{k}_{\delta}\times D^{k}_{\delta^{1-\kappa}}}e^{-\frac{I}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}\mu\otimes\mathrm{d}\nu

(4.77)

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{(d-1)/2}\int_{A^{k}_{\delta}\times(D^{k}_{\delta^{1-\kappa}}\cap\mathcal{R})}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\int_{\mathds{R}^{d-1}}e^{-\frac{\|\bar{\mathfrak{x}}^{\perp}-\bar{\mathfrak{y}}^{\perp}\|^{2}}{2\|x-y\|}}q(y)d\bar{\mathfrak{y}}^{\perp}_{1}\ldots d\bar{\mathfrak{y}}^{\perp}_{d-1}d{\mathfrak{y}}_{d}

(4.78)

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{(d-1)/2}\sum_{k}\operatorname{Vol}(A_{\delta}^{k})\lesssim\varepsilon^{(d-1)/2}\operatorname{Vol}(\mathcal{X}).

(4.79)

On the other hand, using Lemma
5.2
with the assumption that
\operatorname{dist}(d,\mathcal{R}_{a})\geq\delta^{1+\kappa}
for any

with

for

small enough to get

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\int_{A^{k}_{\delta}\times\left(D^{k}_{\delta^{1-\kappa}}\right)^{c}}e^{-\frac{I}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}\mu\otimes\mathrm{d}\nu

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k}\int_{A^{k}_{\delta}\times\left(D^{k}_{\delta^{1-\kappa}}\right)^{c}}e^{-\frac{c_{0}\delta^{2+2\kappa}}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}\mu\otimes\mathrm{d}\nu\quad

(4.80)

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-\frac{c_{0}\delta^{2+2\kappa}}{\varepsilon}}\sum_{k}\operatorname{Vol}\left(A^{k}_{\delta}\times(D^{k}_{\delta^{1-\kappa}})^{c}\right)

(4.81)

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-\varepsilon^{-\frac{c_{0}(1+2\kappa)^{2}}{2}}}\operatorname{Vol}\left(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\right)

(4.82)

which shows that this term decays exponentially faster than
\varepsilon^{(d-1)/2}
when
\varepsilon\to 0
. Thus combining (
4.77
) and (

) we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}e^{-I/\varepsilon}\ \mathrm{d}\mu\otimes\mathrm{d}\nu

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{m\to\infty}\int_{\cup_{n=1}^{m}\bar{T}_{p_{n}}}e^{-I/\varepsilon}\ \mathrm{d}\mu\otimes\mathrm{d}\nu

(4.83)

\displaystyle\lesssim\limsup_{m\to\infty}\int_{\tilde{T}_{p_{m}}}e^{-I/\varepsilon}\ \mathrm{d}\mu\otimes\mathrm{d}\nu

(4.84)

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{(d-1)/2}

(4.85)

as desired.

Remark 4.5
.

The above computation is simpler when the source and target measures are unit rectangles in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, i.e.
\mu=\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]^{2}}\mathrm{d}x
and

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\delta}(x)\times B_{\delta}(y)}e^{-E/\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}\mu\otimes\nu

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{\delta}(x)\times B_{\delta}(y)}e^{-\frac{|x_{2}-y_{2})^{2}}{2\varepsilon|x_{1}-y_{1}|}+\frac{O(\delta^{2})}{\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}y_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\mathrm{d}y_{2}

(4.86)

(4.87)

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{1/2}\delta^{4}\leq C\sqrt{\varepsilon}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{\delta}(x)\times B_{\delta}(y)).

(4.88)

Summing this over all such balls we get

\displaystyle\int_{\cup_{i=1}^{n}B^{i}_{\delta}(x)\times B^{i}_{\delta}(y)}e^{-E/\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}\mu\otimes\nu\leq C\sqrt{\varepsilon}

(4.89)

since the volume of
\delta
-balls are controlled by the total volume of the support of the measures
\mu
and
\nu.
We estimate the remainder
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{n}B^{i}_{\delta}(x)\times B^{i}_{\delta}(y)
by a crude estimate of the form
e^{-\frac{I(x,y)}{\varepsilon}}\leq e^{-\frac{C}{\varepsilon}}
which then gives the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}e^{-E/\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}\mu\otimes\mathrm{d}\nu\leq C\sqrt{\varepsilon}

(4.90)

as desired.

Lemma 5.3
