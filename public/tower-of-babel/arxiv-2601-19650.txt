Efficient Application of Tensor Network Operators to Tensor Network States
Richard M. Milbradt, Shuo Sun, Christian B. Mendl, Johnnie Gray, Garnet K.-L. Chan

Abstract
The performance of tensor network methods has seen constant improvements over the last few years. We add to this effort by introducing a new algorithm that efficiently applies tree tensor network operators to tree tensor network states inspired by the density matrix method and the Cholesky decomposition. This application procedure is a common subroutine in tensor network methods. We explicitly include the special case of tensor train structures and demonstrate how to extend methods commonly used in this context to general tree structures. We compare our newly developed method with the existing ones in a benchmark scenario with random tensor network states and operators. We find our Cholesky-based compression (CBC) performs equivalently to the current state-of-the-art method, while outperforming most established methods by at least an order of magnitude in runtime. We then apply our knowledge to perform circuit simulation of tree-like circuits, in order to test our method in a more realistic scenario. Here, we find that more complex tree structures can outperform simple linear structures and achieve lower errors than those possible with the simple structures. Additionally, our CBC still performs among the most successful methods, showing less dependence on the different bond dimensions of the operator.

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{\vec{\sigma},\vec{\alpha}}T^{[1]}_{\sigma_{1}\vec{\alpha}_{1}}\cdots T^{[L]}_{\sigma_{L}\vec{\alpha}_{L}}\ket{\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{L}},

We will now examine a few special TTNS used throughout this work.

II.2
Special Tree Structures

The matrix product state (MPS)
Schollwöck 2011
, also known as tensor train
Oseledets 2011
, is the most commonly used tensor network to represent a quantum state. It is a chain of tensors with two virtual and one physical leg each, where the one virtual leg of the first and last tensor is trivial. This structure can be viewed as a TTNS, where every site has at most one child. We will use a slightly different notation compared to
Eq. (2)
and represent a quantum state
\ket{\psi}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{L}}
of
L
sites by

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{\vec{\sigma},\vec{\alpha}}T^{[1]}_{\sigma_{1}\alpha_{0}\alpha_{1}}\cdots T^{[L]}_{\sigma_{L}\alpha_{L-1}\alpha_{L}}\ket{\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{L}}

(3)

where the outermost legs
\alpha_{0}
and
\alpha_{L}
are of dimension
1
. Note that we changed the notation for the virtual legs: The leg to the left and right of site
i
are denoted
\alpha_{i-1}
and
\alpha_{i}
respectively. Since it has been so widely used in research over the last two decades, one cannot hope to state all its use cases. Examples include condensed matter physics, quantum information, open quantum systems, and quantum chemistry. A graphical depiction of the MPS structure is given in
Fig. 1(a)
. Note that in this work, we will refer to the underlying structure as the tensor train structure, but when used to represent a quantum state, we will refer to the state as an MPS.

The second important tree structure is the T3NSs. A T3NS is a tree tensor network state for which no node has more than three non-trivial legs
Gunst et al. 2018
. Notably, this means that only a site with a maximum of two non-trivial virtual legs can also have a non-trivial physical leg. This limitation of legs tends to leave the scaling of algorithms acceptable while still allowing for the full flexibility in creating topologically adapted tree tensor network structures. However, especially in cases where physical dimensions are small, it can sometimes be advisable to allow multiple open legs per tensor, while still restricting the number of virtual legs, which tend to have a much higher dimension
Tagliacozzo et al. 2009
;
Cheng et al. 2019
;
Seitz et al. 2023
;
Dubey et al. 2025
;
Krinitsin et al. 2025
. Thus, T3NS have mainly been used in quantum chemistry
Gunst et al. 2018
;
Gunst et al. 2019
;
Ke and Assan 2025
;
Ke 2025
;
Sun et al. 2025
or in a setting with mostly non-physical sites neighboring each other
Silvi et al. 2010
;
Magnifico et al. 2021
;
Ceruti et al. 2021
;
Okunishi et al. 2023
;
Ceruti et al. 2023
;
Sulz et al. 2024
. An example of a T3NS is depicted in
Fig. 1(c)
.

These are the two main tree structures needed for now. We will explain additional structures as needed, though we will mostly refrain from drawing the leg indices into the tensor network diagrams to avoid unnecessary clutter.

II.3
Tree Tensor Network Operators

Another kind of tensor network, essential for this work, is the tree tensor network operator (TTNO)
Fröwis et al. 2010
;
Milbradt et al. 2024a
. Analogously to TTNS representing quantum states, TTNOs represent linear operators acting on a quantum state. Thus a linear operator
\hat{O}:\mathbb{C}^{\times d_{i}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times d_{i}}
acting on a multi-site quantum system can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{O}=\sum_{\vec{\sigma}^{\prime},\vec{\sigma},\vec{\beta}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{[1]}_{\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\sigma_{1}\vec{\beta}_{1}}\cdots\mathcal{T}^{[L]}_{\sigma^{\prime}_{L}\sigma_{L}\vec{\beta}_{L}}

\displaystyle\cdot

\displaystyle\ket{\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\cdots\sigma^{\prime}_{L}}\bra{\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{L}}.

(4)

This resembles the definition of the TTNS in
Eq. (2)
, apart from the additional physical leg on each tensor. We will also use the same notation for the legs and indices of TTNO tensors as well as the special notation for the tensor train equivalent of the TTNO, the matrix product operator (MPO). Both an MPO and a T3NO, i.e., the operator equivalent of a T3NS, are depicted in
Fig. 1(b)
and
Fig. 1(d)
, respectively. Use cases for a TTNO are ground state search
Murg et al. 2010
;
Nakatani and Chan 2013
and time-evolution algorithms
Bauernfeind and Aichhorn 2020
;
Ceruti et al. 2021
;
Ren et al. 2022
;
Ceruti et al. 2023
;
Ceruti et al. 2024
. In these algorithms, the TTNO represents the system’s Hamiltonian, which can be constructed efficiently for arbitrary tree structures
Milbradt et al. 2024a
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Çakır et al. 2025
. However, most of these algorithms merely require something akin to the expectation value
\braket{\psi|\hat{O}|\psi}
and not the state
\ket{\psi^{\prime}}=\hat{O}\ket{\psi}
resulting from the application of
\hat{O}
to
\ket{\psi}
. Algorithms requiring this are currently rarely used with TTN, cf.
Sun et al. 2025
for an example. However, the algorithms that require the application of MPO to MPS, as highlighted in
I
, are easily generalizable to TTN. Thus, we will now show how to obtain
\ket{\psi^{\prime}}
for tensor trains and more general tree structures.

III
Cholesky Based Compression

We will now state the general idea for our new compression algorithm, coined the Cholesky Based Compression (CBC). A step-by-step description can be found in Sec .
III.1
and Sec .
III.2
for tensor-trains and general TTN, respectively. The general idea is based on the fact that any TTNS is split into two disconnected subsystems
A
and
B
by cutting any virtual bond
\alpha_{i}
. The required dimension of this bond can then be obtained by performing the eigendecomposition of the resulting density matrix, which is obtained by performing the partial trace over all sites connected to site
i
only via leg
\alpha_{i}
. To avoid exponential scaling, the physical degrees of freedom for which the tensor was already compressed are removed through a projection, compressing them into a single virtual degree of freedom. In the end, one obtains the new and compressed TTNS as a projection change of the original TTNS into the compressed form. This idea is known as the density matrix compression algorithm (DMC). It was originally introduced for MPS only
DMC
, but a version for general TTNS was recently introduced
Ma et al. 2024
. However, when constructing the tensor representing the reduced density matrix, we build many local positive definite tensors explicitly by evaluating a product of the form
G=M^{\dagger}M
, which is a Cholesky decomposition of
G
. In the CBC, we utilize this fact by constructing only
M
and immediately truncating the legs that would connect to the adjoint. Avoiding the full construction of the tensor
G
leads to a more efficient algorithm as the involved tensors tend to be much smaller, as we will see in the explicit statement of the algorithm.

III.3
Other Compression Methods

We will now provide an overview of other methods commonly used to apply an MPO to an MPS and restrict the resulting bond dimension. Note that, even though for some of these methods, there is no explicit version for general TTN in the literature. However, while not trivial, the method’s extension follows a similar scheme to that of the CBC: moving up and down the tree for precomputation and then performing a second sweep from the leaves to the root. We will also refrain from mentioning the DMC again, as it was already covered at the beginning of this section, c.f.
Sec. III
.

The simplest way of performing the application and compression is to do both as separate steps. Here, the TTNO is fully contracted with the TTNS, and the two virtual legs between every two nodes are combined into one larger leg. Then the compression is performed as a second step. This compression takes the form of a sweep through the entire network, performing truncated SVDs for each leg encountered during the sweep to compress it down to the desired dimension. The sweep can be performed either from leaves to root, requiring the movement of the orthogonality center
Oseledets 2011
;
Schollwöck 2011
;
Ma et al. 2024
, or recursively from the root to the leaves with the root as the orthogonality center
Ceruti et al. 2023
. The compression can also benefit from using randomized SVD if the difference between the current and desired bond dimension is large
Al Daas et al. 2023
. While this approach is the most accurate one
Oseledets 2011
, it has a worse analytical scaling, also evidenced in numerical benchmarks, compared to the more involved methods
Paeckel et al. 2019
;
Al Daas et al. 2023
;
Camaño et al. 2025
;
Ma et al. 2024
.

The other end of the spectrum is the ZipUp method
Stoudenmire and White 2010
. It is very fast, but has a small accuracy. The method sweeps through the network only once and compresses every site locally, using the result of already compressed sites. However, the fact that it only utilises the information of the already compressed parts of the tensor network is the cause of the smaller accuracy. The state that is part of this contraction can be brought into canonical form before running the ZipUp method to slightly improve its performance
Paeckel et al. 2019
. For a graphical depiction of this method on MPS, consider any of
Paeckel et al. 2019
;
Ma et al. 2024
;
Tensornetwork.org
contributors 2021a
.

A recent improved algorithm is the successive randomized compression (SRC)
Camaño et al. 2025
. It utilizes multiple randomly generated tensors to find the compressed product, employing the same underlying ideas as random matrix decompositions
Halko et al. 2011
;
Murray et al. 2023
. For this algorithm, a single random matrix of size
d\times\bar{D}
is drawn per site. They are combined via the Khatri-Rao product and contracted to build environment blocks similar to our CBC algorithm. However, instead of an SVD, the compression is obtained through contraction with random matrices. Performance-wise, the obtained state is comparable to that of the naive application and DMC algorithms, while beating the Zip-Up algorithm in runtime
Camaño et al. 2025
.

Finally, there is an entire class of algorithms that variationally fit the result to a TTNS of desired bond dimension. This can be done by first contracting and then performing the fitting, using the contraction result as a reference
Schollwöck 2011
;
Paeckel et al. 2019
. A way to perform the application without the explicit contraction can be achieved via DMRG-like sweeps
Verstraete and Cirac 2004
;
Stoudenmire and White 2010
. However, these variational approaches can take a long time to converge or get stuck in local minima without an educated starting guess, for example, obtained via a Zip-Up run
Paeckel et al. 2019
;
Camaño et al. 2025
. Therefore, we will exclude it from the following numerical exploration.

A simplified scaling of the operation count is presented in
Tab. 1
, and a simplified scaling for the required memory in
Tab. 2

Additionally, we observe that the DM-based algorithm loses some of its advantage over the direct method when going from MPS to the more complex T3NS. We note that this behavior persists for trees with higher degrees, and, in particular, the memory requirements can already become problematic for the T3NS case.

•

As a second experiment, we conduct a more realistic simulation that is closer to an actual application by simulating a quantum circuit
U_{\text{full}}
. To avoid the need for a reference calculation, we consider a quantum circuit of the form

U_{\text{full}}=UU^{\dagger}.

(16)

Accordingly, the output state should be equal to the input state for an exact circuit simulation. The circuit
U
itself consists of
N

U=\prod_{i=1}^{N}U_{i},

(17)

each batch
U_{i}
has the form shown in
Fig. 6
and is thus a tree-like circuit
Seitz et al. 2023
. In the circuit, every two-qubit gate
G
is of the form

G=\text{CNOT}\cdot(V_{1}\otimes V_{2}),

(18)

where all the single qubit gates
V_{i}
are drawn Haar randomly. Note that the
V_{i}
are only placed in front of the CNOT, as they do not have any entangling power. Thus, multiple

The first thing to note is that the Zip-Up method fails to converge to an error much below
1
for any of the three structures, even for the largest bond dimensions. However, the other methods converge to numerical error for a bond dimension of
\bar{D}=50
for both T3NS structures, but not for the MPS. The simulations using the MPS cannot achieve an error below

This supports that long-range interactions cause problems for less optimized structures, thereby demonstrating that TTN can provide an advantage over the simpler tensor train structure. When examining methods other than the Zip-Up method, we observe them performing within a similar range. However, the SRC performs slightly worse, requiring more memory and runtime to achieve a desired error. For the other three methods, there is no relevant difference in performance when considering the memory requirement. Surprisingly, the performance regarding runtime is the opposite of our initial expectation. The contraction and truncation algorithm performs best, followed by the DM-based method and then our CBC method. This is likely due to the very low bond dimension of the TTNO, which represents the quantum circuit.

V
Conclusion

We found that our newly introduced CBC method, which applies a TTNO to a TTNS, performs similarly to current state-of-the-art methods, such as SRC and DM. We also found that the correct choice of method can be impacted significantly by the relative bond dimension of TTNO to TTNS. In all our numerical studies, the CBC method consistently performed among the best. Another advantage of our method over the SRC is the simpler implementation of an automatically adaptive bond dimension by setting a tolerance for the SVD. The flip side of this is that the SRC only requires contractions and QR decompositions, which allows a much greater performance boost when running the simulations on GPUs
Unfried et al. 2023
. Additionally, our results support the utility of more complicated TTN structures adapted to the problem at hand, improving once more the classical methods to simulate quantum systems. We can also conclude that the Zip-Up method performs significantly worse than the CBC and SRC methods in every aspect and should therefore no longer be considered state-of-the-art. Additionally, we found that the DM bases compression method, while useful for MPS, is unsuited for general T3NS.
