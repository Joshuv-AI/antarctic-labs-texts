Mechanical forces of beating heart suppress cancer growth
Authors: Tingting Li, Yiyao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe cardiac vasculature is characterized by high pressure, high blood flow, and dense vascular branching, which may increase its exposure to circulating tumor cells (CTCs). However, both primary cardiac tumors and metastases are remarkably rare. This mechanism has remained poorly understood. A recent report by Ciucci and co-workers show that mechanical load in the heart inhibits cancer cell proliferation. Their work proposes that physiological mechanical stimuli is not merely a regulator of cancer progression, but also an intrinsic anticancer mechanism.

The physical microenvironment influencing cancer cell fate including proliferation, metastasis, drug resistance and etc. by mechanotransduction.1,2 Previous work established that the mechanical properties of extracellular matrix (ECM), especially stiffness regulates cancer cell migration, metabolism, mitophagy and therapeutic resistance through pathways involving integrins, YAP/TAZ and mitochondria associated mechanotransduction 3, 4, 5, 6. Most studies in tumor mechanobiology have focused on how the mechanical properties of tumor microenvironments promote malignancy. By contrast, Ciucci et al.7 reveal a strikingly different standpoint that sustain physiological mechanical load generated by the beating myocardium suppresses cancer growth.

Adult mammalian cardiomyocytes lose their proliferative capacity after birth. Prolonged mechanical unloading of the human heart induces cardiomyocyte proliferation. Mechanical stress has therefore been proposed as one of the major forces limiting cardiac regeneration.8 Ciucci and colleagues7 speculated that if mechanical load inhibits cardiomyocyte proliferation, it might also suppress the growth of cancer cells in myocardium. To test this hypothesis, they employed a genetically engineered mouse model driven by oncogenic K-Ras activation and p53 deletion. Despite efficient recombination in multiple tissues, expression of mutated K-Ras and loss of p53 resulted in development of tumor in skeletal muscle and other organs but never in the heart. This observation suggests that the myocardium possesses intrinsic antitumor properties rather than simply being spared from oncogenic mutations. A particularly innovative aspect of the study is use of heterotopic heart transplantation (HHT) as model of mechanical unloading.7 In this model, the authors transplanted a donor heart into the neck of a recipient syngeneic mouse. The donor aorta and pulmonary artery were anastomosed to the recipient carotid artery and external jugular vein, respectively, thereby restoring coronary perfusion while mechanically unloading the left ventricle (LV). This experimental design intelligently isolates mechanical load from other influencing factors such as vascularization, systemic metabolism, and immune surveillance. They injected lung adenocarcinoma cancer cells, one of the cancer types that frequently metastasize to the heart, into the LV of both loaded and unloaded hearts. The results indicate that lung cancer cells form larger tumors in unloaded hearts than in control (loaded) hearts. Importantly, this difference was not attributable to altered engraftment or apoptosis, but rather to a substantial increase in cancer cell proliferation under unloaded conditions.

To further analyze the contribution of mechanical stress, the authors developed engineered heart tissues (EHTs) with tunable load conditions. Mechanical load and compressive forces generated by cardiomyocyte (CM) contraction inhibit cancer cell proliferation, whereas mechanical unload enhances it, independently of major changes in nutrient availability. Mathematical simulations further revealed that load modulation in EHTs reproduces the effect of cardiac unloading in vivo.

To uncover how the mechanical stimuli in the beating heart constrains tumor growth, the authors interrogated cardiac metastases arising from lung adenocarcinoma, colon carcinoma, and cutaneous melanoma using spatial transcriptomics.7 Strikingly, cardiac lesions shared a common transcriptional signature irrespective of tumor origin, highlighting the dominant influence of the myocardial niche over intrinsic tumor identity. The most enriched pathway in cardiac metastases was histone demethylation, accompanied by a reduction in histone 3 lysine 9 trimethylation (H3K9me3) and chromatin compaction. H3K9me3 is a hallmark of heterochromatin and transcriptional repression.9 Changes in chromatin compaction have increasingly been linked to nuclear mechanics and mechanotransduction 10, 11, 12. These observations suggest that mechanical stimuli in the beating heart drives extensive epigenetic reprogramming in metastatic cancer cells. By linking mechanical stimuli to chromatin remodeling, the study identifies histone demethylation as a key molecular axis through which the beating heart imposes a proliferative barrier against tumor growth. By combining single-cell ATAC-seq (scATAC-seq) and H3K9me3 ChIP-seq on cancer cells injected in both loaded and unloaded hearts. This study demonstrates that mechanical load reduces H3K9me3 and increases chromatin accessibility for genes involved in cell cycle arrest, extracellular sensing, mechanosensing, cytoskeleton, and calcium homeostasis, consistent with reduced cancer cell growth. These findings suggest that cardiac mechanical forces actively reprogram tumor cell fate through chromatin remodeling, ultimately restricting proliferative capacity.

Previous studies in mechanobiology have largely focused on membrane associated signaling pathways. Cancer cells perceive mechanical cues, including matrix elasticity, viscoelasticity, and fluid shear stress through mechanosensors such as integrins and mechanosensitive ion channels.13,14 These signals are subsequently transmitted intracellularly via mechanotransduction pathways to regulate proliferation, migration, therapy resistance, and other aspects of cell fate.15,16 This work demonstrate that mechanical load directly remodels chromatin architecture in cancer cells. One of the most important advances of the study is its transition from classical mechanotransduction to nuclear mechanobiology. The myocardium imposes cyclic compression, rhythmic deformation, and contractile stress. Rather than serving as a pro-tumorigenic stimulus, these forces appear to create a biomechanical barrier that restricts cancer cell expansion. In this study, the key mechanotransducer is Nesprin-2, a core component of the linker of nucleoskeleton and cytoskeleton (LINC) complex. Nesprins mechanically connect the cytoskeleton to the nuclear envelope and mediate force transmission into the nucleus.17 Silencing Nesprin-2 restored tumor growth in mechanically loaded hearts. This finding identifies that Nesprin-2 as a critical mediator of the mechanotransduction pathway through which cardiac mechanical forces suppress cancer cell proliferation. This finding substantially advances the emerging field of mechanogenomics, in which physical forces directly regulate genome accessibility and transcriptional identity. It also suggests that the nucleus itself may function as a mechanosensitive tumor suppressor organelle.

Taken together, this study reveals that unique mechanical environment of the beating heart suppresses cancer cell proliferation, offering a plausible explanation for the low incidence of cardiac tumors. Mechanistically, the authors uncover a mechanotransduction pathway involving Nesprin-2 mediated nuclear mechanosensing, H3K9me3 remodeling, and chromatin decompaction, thereby linking tissue-scale mechanical forces to epigenetic regulation of tumor cell fate (Fig. 1). It raises the intriguing possibility that mechanical forces can be harnessed as therapeutic regulators of tumor growth, which open a new frontier in the emerging discipline of cancer mechanotherapy.

These biomechanical cues are perceived through Nesprin-2. Mechanical signaling subsequently reduce histone methylation and chromatin compaction in cancer cells, and ultimately restrains tumor cell proliferation within the cardiac microenvironment. This study suggests that controlled mechanical stimulation may suppress tumor growth, which is profound significance for cancer treatment. Future cancer therapeutic strategies could involve cyclic compression systems, ultrasound-mediated mechanical stimulation, magnetically actuated biomaterials, or engineered matrices designed to impose tumor-suppressive mechanical states. Furthermore, mechanical modulation may synergize with existing cancer therapies. Mechanical stress may alter immune cell infiltration, enhance sensitivity to chemotherapy, or interfere with metastatic colonization. Given the increasing interest in epigenetic therapies, the possibility of manipulating chromatin accessibility through biomechanical inputs represents an exciting new therapeutic frontier. Several important questions remain unresolved. Why do other mechanically active tissues, such as skeletal muscle and blood vessels, not display the same degree of tumor resistance as the myocardium? How are histone demethylases mechanistically regulated by force transmission? Finally, do different tumor types possess distinct thresholds of mechanical tolerance? Despite these questions, the conceptual impact of this study is undeniable. Ciucci et al.7 move the field beyond the traditional view that mechanics merely modulates tumor progression. Instead, they position physiological mechanical force as an active and evolutionarily conserved anticancer mechanism. The beating heart, long regarded as a hostile metastatic niche, now emerges as a natural biomechanical tumor suppressor system. In doing so, this work may inaugurate a new era in oncology, one in which mechanics is no longer viewed simply as a property of the tumor microenvironment, but as a therapeutic principle in its own right.
