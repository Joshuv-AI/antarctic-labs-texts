Shelling and Sinking Graphs on the Sphere Thanks: An extended abstract of this paper was presented at the 41st International Symposium on Computational Geometry [ 41 ] .
Jeff Erickson, Christian Howard

Abstract
We describe a promising approach to efficiently morph shortest-path embeddings of planar graphs on the sphere, extending earlier approaches of Awartani and Henderson [ Trans. AMS 1987] and Kobourov and Landis [ JGAA 2006]. Specifically, we describe two methods to morph spherical triangulations by moving their vertices along longitudes into the southern hemisphere; we call a triangulation sinkable if such a morph exists. Our first method generalizes a longitudinal shelling construction of Awartani and Henderson; a triangulation is sinkable if a specific orientation of its dual graph is acyclic. We describe a simple polynomial-time algorithm to find a longitudinally shellable rotation of a given spherical triangulation, if one exists; we also construct a spherical triangulation that has no longitudinally shellable rotation. Our second method is based on a linear-programming characterization of sinkability. By identifying its optimal basis, we show that this linear program can be solved in O ⁡ ( n ! / 2 ) O(n^{\omega/2}) time, where ! \omega is the matrix-multiplication exponent, assuming the underlying linear system is non-singular. In addition to these main results, we describe a reduction from morphing shortest-path embeddings of 3-connected planar graphs on the sphere to morphing triangulations, and we describe an efficient algorithm that constructs morphs where each intermediate edge has at most one bend. Finally, we pose several conjectures and describe experimental results that support them.

1.1
What About the Sphere?

In light of the long history of results for morphing planar graphs and their recent generalizations to higher-genus surfaces, it is surprising how little is known about morphing graphs on the sphere. Although embeddings on the sphere are
topologically
equivalent to embeddings in the plane, the different geometries of the two surfaces induce significantly different behavior.

In 1944, Cairns
[
21
]
proved that any two isomorphic shortest-path triangulations of the sphere are connected by a continuous family of shortest-path triangulations, using essentially the same edge-contraction strategy that he used for planar triangulations (but with more complex case analysis). A direct translation of Cairns’s spherical morphing proof leads to an exponential-time algorithm; unlike his planar result, this is still the fastest algorithm known. Neither of the primary tools that underlie more efficient planar morphing algorithms—barycentric embeddings
[
44
,
45
,
97
]
and convex hierarchical graph drawing
[
28
,
63
,
24
,
67
,
68
]
—have appropriate generalizations to spherical graphs. In fact, as far as we are aware, this is the
only
algorithm known for morphing arbitrary spherical triangulations.

More efficient morphing algorithms are known for a few special cases of spherical triangulations. Almost all of these algorithms operate by moving vertices along longitudes into the southern hemisphere, projecting the resulting “southern” triangulation into the plane, and applying a planar morphing algorithm. We call a sphere triangulation
sinkable
if it can be morphed along longitudes into the southern hemisphere.

Awartani and Henderson
[
4
]
describe an algorithm to morph spherical triangulations that satisfy a certain longitudinal shelling condition using this strategy. A spherical triangulation is
longitudinally shellable
if its faces can be ordered so that the union of any prefix of faces has connected intersection with every longitude. In short, Awartani and Henderson prove that every longitudinally shellable triangulation is sinkable. They also prove that any triangulation with a
longitudinal seam
, meaning there is a longitude that does not cross the interior of any edge, is longitudinally shellable. (We study Awartani and Henderson’s longitudinal shelling condition in more detail in Section
3
.)

Kobourov and Landis
[
69
]
describe an algorithm to morph between
Delaunay
triangulations via longitudinal morphs; their algorithm easily generalizes to
coherent
triangulations, which are central projections of arbitrary convex polyhedra. Every coherent triangulation is longitudinally shellable and therefore sinkable. The same method is also implicit in Richter-Gebert’s proof
[
83
, Theorem 13.3.3]
of classical theorems of Eberhard
[
37
]
and Steinitz
[
87
]
describing morphs between isomorphic convex polyhedra.

Awartani and Henderson
[
4
, Figure 3.1]
also give an example of a triangulation that is not sinkable; it is not possible to move the vertices of their triangulation into the southern hemisphere along longitudes without inverting at least one face.

1.3
Our Results

We describe and study a promising approach to efficiently morph shortest-path triangulations on the sphere, extending the previous results of Awartani and Henderson
[
4
]
and Kobourov and Landis
[
69
]
. At a high level, we propose finding suitable rotations of the sphere that make the source and target triangulations both sinkable, moving vertices of the rotated triangulations into the southern hemisphere along longitudes, and finally reducing to a planar morphing problem.

We emphasize that we do
not
develop this strategy into a complete algorithm; the existence of an efficient spherical morphing algorithm remains an open problem. We also note that sinking is not
required
to construct spherical morphs; for example, we can directly morph any Schönhardt or six-beaked-shaddock triangulation into any other by twisting the faces.

We begin in Section
2
by reviewing relevant definitions, describing our proposed morphing strategy in detail, and proving some preliminary results. In particular, we prove that a triangulation
\Gamma
is sinkable if and only if there is an isomorphic
weak
triangulation
\Gamma^{\prime}
(intuitively, a triangulation in which some faces may be degenerate) whose vertices are on the same longitudes but below the equator. We also show that any efficient algorithm for morphing spherical triangulations implies an efficient algorithm to morph shortest-path embeddings of 3-connected planar graphs on the sphere.

In Section
3
, we formally define the
longitudinal shelling
condition introduced by Awartani and Henderson. We provide several combinatorial characterizations, in terms of different directed versions of the triangulation or its dual graph, each of which can be tested in
O(n)
time. We also reiterate Awartani and Henderson’s proof that every longitudinally shellable triangulations is sinkable. Finally, by combining Awartani and Henderson’s results with more recent planar morphing algorithms
[
2
,
68
]
, we describe an efficient algorithm to construct morphs in which each edge of each intermediate embedding has at most one
bend
.

Rotating a longitudinally unshellable triangulation can make it longitudinally shellable. In Section
4
, we present an algorithm that either finds a rotation of a given triangulation
\Gamma
that is longitudinally shellable, or reports correctly that no such rotation exists, in
O(n^{5/2}\log^{3}n)
time. Our algorithm searches each of the
O(n^{2})
cells in the arrangement of great circles determined by the edges of
\Gamma
, using a dynamic reachability data structure of Diks and Sankowski
[
34
]
. We also construct a spherical triangulation that has no longitudinally shellable rotation.

In Section
5
, we describe an efficient algorithm to determine whether a given sphere triangulation is
sinkable
. First we prove that sinkability is equivalent to the feasibility of a linear program with
O(n)
variables and
O(n)
constraints. By identifying the optimal basis for this linear program, we then show that it can be solved in
O(n^{\omega/2})
time, assuming the underlying linear system is non-singular.

As a tool for building intuition, we implemented a suite of algorithms to construct sphere triangulations, test for shellability and sinkability, construct Awartani–Henderson embeddings, and visualize longitudinal morphs. In Section
6
, we present results of some lightweight experiments with “ugly” spherical triangulations, which suggest that “in practice”, one can find a longitudinally shellable or sinkable rotation of a triangulation by testing only a small constant number of random rotations.

Finally, we conclude in Section
7
by presenting several directions for further research, including several conjectures suggested by our experimental results.

Background and Preliminary Results

2.1
Cartography

We consider drawings of graphs on the unit sphere
S^{2}=\{(x,y,z)\mid x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=1\}
.
The
north pole
is the point
(0,0,1)
; the
south pole
is the point
(0,0,-1)
; the
equator
is the intersection of the unit sphere with the plane
z=0
. A
longitude
is an open great-circular arc whose endpoints are the north and south poles. Every point except the poles lies on a unique longitude.

Throughout the paper, we fix an arbitrary simple maximal planar graph
G
with
n\geq 4
vertices, arbitrarily indexed from
1
to
n
. An
embedding
of
G
on the sphere is an injective map from
G
to
S^{2}
, or less formally, a drawing of
G
where edges are arbitrary simple curves that intersect only at their shared endpoints. Because
G
is maximal planar, every embedding of
G
on the sphere is a
triangulation
, meaning every face is bounded by a cycle of three distinct edges.

Unless explicitly indicated otherwise, we consider only
generic shortest-path
triangulations, meaning (1) every edge is a great-circular arc with length less than
\pi
; (2) no great circle contains more than two vertices, and (3) no great circle through the poles contains more than one vertex. Condition (1) is the natural analogue on the sphere of straight-line embeddings in the plane. Condition (3) implies that vertices lie on distinct longitudes.

Classical theorems of Steinitz
[
86
,
87
,
57
]
and Whitney
[
99
,
16
]
imply that
G
can be embedded as a generic shortest-path triangulation, and that this embedding is unique up to homeomorphism. A
face of
G
is a face of this essentially unique embedding. We sometimes identify each face as a triple
(i,j,k)
of vertices (or their indices) in counterclockwise order around its interior.

Every generic shortest-path triangulation has a unique
north face
that contains the north pole in its interior and a unique
south face
that contains the south pole in its interior. The remaining
non-polar
faces are of two types: An
up-face
has one vertex (called its
apex
) that is directly north of its opposite edge (called its
base
); symmetrically, a
down-face
has one vertex (its apex) directly south of its opposite edge (its base). The
legs
of a non-polar face
f
are the edges of
f
incident to the apex of
f
. The faces immediately north and south of any vertex
i
are respectively denoted
f_{\textsf{above}}(i)
and
f_{\textsf{below}}(i)
. For each vertex
i
,
f_{\textsf{above}}(i)
is either the north face or a down-face, and
f_{\textsf{below}}(i)
is either the south face or an up-face. See Figure
2.1
.

Figure 2.1:
An up-face
f_{\textsf{above}}(i)
, a down-face
f_{\textsf{below}}(j)
, and the north face
f_{\textsf{north}}=f_{\textsf{above}}(j)
of a spherical triangulation. Edge
ij
is a leg of both
f_{\textsf{above}}(i)
and
f_{\textsf{below}}(j)
.

A face of a (generic) triangulation is
everted
if it has area greater than
2\pi
, or equivalently, if it contains an open hemisphere, or equivalently, if it contains a pair of antipodal points. Every triangulation contains at most one everted face. We call a triangulation
full
if no face is everted, and
southern
if every vertex lies below the equator (which implies that the north face is everted).

A connected region
R
on the sphere is
\theta
-monotone
if every longitude is either disjoint from
R
or has connected intersection with
R
.

2.2
Coordinates

Throughout the paper, we represent spherical triangulations
implicitly
as projections of certain polyhedra onto the unit sphere. Specifically, we represent each vertex using signed homogeneous coordinates
[
88

89
,
90

—for any scalar
\lambda>0
, the coordinate vectors
(x,y,z)
and
(\lambda x,\lambda y,\lambda z)
represent the same point on the sphere. We can freely rescale the vertices, each independently, without changing the underlying spherical triangulation; although it is never actually necessary, this rescaling is sometimes convenient for purposes of discussion and intuition. For example, we can implicitly interpret any southern triangulation as a
planar
triangulation by scaling vertices onto the tangent plane
z=-1
; this scaling is equivalent to the
gnomonic
or
central
projection
(x,y,z)\mapsto(-x/z,-y/z,-1)
.

2

Gnomonic projection should not be confused with
stereographic
projection
(x,y,z)\mapsto(\frac{x}{1-z},\frac{y}{1-z})
, which maps shortest paths on the sphere to circular arcs in the plane. We use stereographic projections to visualize graphs on the sphere in Figures

.

Every full triangulation is the projection of a
star-shaped
polyhedron whose visibility kernel contains the origin. Every non-full triangulation is the projection of a polyhedron that is star-shaped except for one facet.

2.3
Longitudinal Morphing

Two embeddings
\Gamma\colon G\to S^{2}
and
\Gamma^{\prime}\colon G\to S^{2}
are
isomorphic
if they define the same rotation system, or equivalently, if there is an orientation-preserving homeomorphism
H\colon S^{2}\to S^{2}
such that
\Gamma^{\prime}=h\circ\Gamma
. Two isomorphic embeddings are
\theta
-equivalent
if each vertex of
G
lies on the same longitude in both embeddings.

A
homotopy
between two embeddings
\Gamma\colon G\to S^{2}
and
\Gamma^{\prime}\colon G\to S^{2}
is a continuous function
H\colon[0,1]\times G\to S^{2}
such that
H(0,\cdot)=\Gamma
and
H(1,\cdot)=\Gamma^{\prime}
. Any pair of isomorphic embeddings
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
are connected by an
isotopy
, which is a homotopy
H
such that every intermediate drawing
\Gamma_{t}=H(t,\cdot)
is an embedding. (The edges of these intermediate embeddings can be arbitrary simple curves, not necessarily shortest paths.) A
morph
is an isotopy in which the edges of every intermediate embedding
\Gamma_{t}
are shortest paths. Finally, a
longitudinal morph
is a morph in which vertices move only along their longitudes.

Lemma 2.1
.

Any two
\theta
-equivalent triangulations are connected by a longitudinal morph.

Let
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
be two
\theta
-equivalent triangulations. By scaling coordinate vectors as described in Section
2.2
if necessary, we can assume that each vertex has the same
x
- and
y
-coordinates in both embeddings. Let
(x_{i},y_{i},z_{i})
and
(x_{i},y_{i},z^{\prime}_{i})
denote the coordinate vectors of each vertex
i
in
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
, respectively.

We define a longitudinal morph by linearly interpolating the
z
-coordinate of each vertex from
z_{i}
to
z^{\prime}_{i}
. For each
t\in[0,1]
, let
\Gamma_{t}
denote the shortest-path
drawing
where each vertex
i
has coordinates
(x_{i},y_{i},(1-t)z_{i}+t\cdot z^{\prime}_{i})
. To complete the proof, it remains only to show that each drawing
\Gamma_{t}
is an embedding, or equivalently, that no face collapses to an arc during the morph.

For any three vertices
i,j,k
and any
t\in[0,1]
, let
\operatorname{vol}_{t}(i,j,k)
denote the determinant

\det\begin{pmatrix}x_{i}&y_{i}&(1-t)\cdot z_{i}+t\cdot z^{\prime}_{i}\\[2.15277pt]
x_{j}&y_{j}&(1-t)\cdot z_{j}+t\cdot z^{\prime}_{j}\\[2.15277pt]
x_{k}&y_{k}&(1-t)\cdot z_{k}+t\cdot z^{\prime}_{k}\end{pmatrix}

This volume determinant is a linear function of
t
; specifically,

\operatorname{vol}_{t}(i,j,k)~=~(1-t)\cdot\operatorname{vol}_{0}(i,j,k)~+~t\cdot\operatorname{vol}_{1}(i,j,k).

Suppose
i,j,k
are the vertices of a
non-polar
face of
\Gamma
in counterclockwise order, so that
\operatorname{vol}_{0}(i,j,k)>0
. Because
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
are isomorphic, we have
\operatorname{vol}_{1}(i,j,k)>0
; linearity immediately implies that
\operatorname{vol}_{t}(i,j,k)>0
for all
t\in[0,1]
.

Finally, for all
t
, the north pole lies in the interior of the north face of
\Gamma_{t}
, and the south pole lies in the interior of the south face of
\Gamma_{t}
. Thus, these faces may become (un)everted, but they always have non-empty interiors.

Corollary 2.2
.

A triangulation
\Gamma
is sinkable if and only if there is a southern triangulation that is
\theta
-equivalent to
\Gamma
.

We extend this argument slightly by considering
weak triangulations
, which are drawings of
G
that may include degenerate faces, but no inverted faces or crossing edges. (Weak triangulations are special cases of
weak embeddings
studied by Akitaya, Fulek, and Tóth
[
1
]
and Fulek and Kyncl
[
49
]
.) A weak triangulation
\Gamma^{\prime}
is
\theta
-equivalent to a triangulation
\Gamma
if vertices lie on the same longitudes in
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
and every non-degenerate non-polar face of
\Gamma^{\prime}
has the same orientation as the corresponding face of
\Gamma
.

Corollary 2.3
.

A triangulation
\Gamma
is sinkable if any only if there is a southern weak triangulation that is
\theta
-equivalent to
\Gamma
.

Fix a triangulation
\Gamma
and a
\theta
-equivalent southern weak triangulation
\Gamma^{\prime}
. For each
t\in[0,1]
, let
\Gamma_{t}
denote the shortest-path drawing where each vertex
i
has coordinates
(x_{i},y_{i},z_{i}(t))
, where
z_{i}(t)=(1-t)z_{i}+t\cdot z^{\prime}_{i}
. The proof of Lemma
2.1
implies that
\Gamma_{t}
is an embedding for all
0\leq t<1
.

Let
\tau
be the smallest value such that
z_{i}(\tau)\leq 0
for all
i
. Because
z_{i}(1)=z^{\prime}_{i}<0
for all
i
, we must have
\tau<1
, and therefore
z_{i}((\tau+1)/2)<0
for all
i
. We conclude that
\Gamma_{(\tau+1)/2}
is a southern triangulation (with no degenerate faces) that is
\theta
-equivalent to
\Gamma_{0}
, and the retriction of
H
to
[0,(\tau+1)/2]
is a longitudinal morph from
\Gamma
to
\Gamma_{(\tau+1)/2}
.

2.4
Morphing Strategy

In this section, we propose a strategy for morphing between isomorphic shortest-path triangulations on the sphere that slightly generalizes strategies previously used by Awartani and Henderson
[
4
]
and Kobourov and Landis
[
69
]
. Recall that a triangulation is
sinkable
if it can be longitudinally morphed to a
\theta
-equivalent triangulation with all vertices below the equator. A
rotation
of a spherical triangulation
\Gamma
is the triangulation
\rho\circ\Gamma
for some rigid motion
\rho\colon S^{2}\to S^{2}
of the sphere.
3
3

3

Rotations should not be confused with
rotation systems
, which encode the cyclic order of edges around each vertex in a planar or spherical embedding.
Our strategy rests on the following (admittedly optimistic) conjecture:

Conjecture 2.4
.

Every shortest-path triangulation
\Gamma
of the sphere can be rotated to obtain a sinkable triangulation.

Conjecture
2.4
implies an efficient algorithm to morph any triangulation
\Gamma_{0}
into any isomorphic target triangulation
\Gamma_{1}
through an intermediate coherent triangulation
\Gamma_{1/2}
. Steinitz’s theorem
[
86
,
87
,
57
]
implies that an isomorphic coherent triangulation
\Gamma_{1/2}
exists; moreover, we can compute such a triangulation in
O(n)
time using an algorithm of Das and Goodrich
[
33
]
. Given any coherent triangulation, we can project its underlying convex polyhedron from a point on the positive
z
-axis just outside the polyhedron to the plane
z=-1
, and then centrally project the resulting planar triangulation back to the southern hemisphere, to obtain a
\theta
-equivalent
southern
triangulation. It follows that
every
rotation of
\Gamma_{1/2}
is sinkable.

We construct a morph from
\Gamma_{0}
to
\Gamma_{1/2}
in several stages as follows. First, we rotate
\Gamma_{0}
so that the resulting triangulation
\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}
is sinkable (as guaranteed by Conjecture
2.4
) and then longitudinally morph
\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}
to a southern triangulation
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}_{0}
.
4
4

4

If the triangulation
\Gamma_{0}
is not full, we can rotate it directly to a southern triangulation
\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}=\Gamma^{\prime\prime}_{0}
.
Similarly, we rotate
\Gamma_{1/2}
so that the resulting triangulation
\Gamma^{\prime}_{1/2}
has the same north face as
\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}
and
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}_{0}
, and then longitudinally morph
\Gamma^{\prime}_{1/2}
to a southern triangulation
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}_{1/2}
. Projection from the center of the sphere maps
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}_{0}
and
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}_{1/2}

2.5
Morphing 3-Connected Embeddings

To close this section, we describe how to extend any efficient morphing algorithm for spherical triangulations to shortest-path embeddings of 3-connected graphs.

Our reduction relies on an algorithm to triangulate the faces of a 3-connected embedding with additional shortest paths. We can triangulate any face that fits in a hemisphere by projecting it to a simple polygon to the plane
[
5
]
, but triangulating faces that do not fit in a hemisphere requires more specialized algorithms
[
81
,
15
]
, some of which have never been published
[
58
]
. See O’Rourke
[
81
]
for a thorough discussion of subtleties in triangulating spherical polygons.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\Gamma
be a shortest-path embedding of a 3-connected planar graph on the sphere. We can triangulate the faces of
\Gamma
, using at most one Steiner point, in
O(n\log n)
time.

We adapt an algorithm of Fournier and Montuno
[
47
]
for triangulating planar polygons, which is also described by O’Rourke
[
80
, Chapter 2]
, using techniques developed by Halperin
[
58
]
. We provide only a sketch of our algorithm here, omitting several straightforward proof and implementation details.

If necessary, we rotate
\Gamma
so that the north and south poles are in different faces and, if any face of
\Gamma
contains a hemisphere, that face also contains the north pole. We define the
longitudinal decomposition

\mathcal{L}(\Gamma)
of
\Gamma
by extending longitudinal segments north and south from each vertex to the next intersection with
\Gamma
, or to one of the poles if there is no such intersection. Each face of
\mathcal{L}(\Gamma)
is a (possibly degenerate) spherical trapezoid that is contained in a hemisphere. We can construct
\mathcal{L}(\Gamma)
in
O(n\log n)
time, either by sweeping the sphere with a moving longitude
[
85
,
8
]
or using a randomized incremental algorithm
[
29
,
78
]
.

The rest of the triangulation algorithm requires only
O(n)
time. We call a face
f
of
\mathcal{L}(\Gamma)

boring
if two vertices of
\Gamma
lie on the boundary of that face but not on the same edge of
\mathcal{L}(\Gamma)
; we call the shortest path between those two vertices a
boring diagonal
. Inserting all boring diagonals into the original embedding
\Gamma
partitions its faces into
\theta
-monotone pieces. Two of these
\theta
-monotone pieces contain the poles; each of the others is a
monotone mountain
, whose boundary consists of a
\theta
-monotone polygonal chain and a single shortest path. Each monotone mountain can be triangulated in
O(n)
time by repeatedly removing convex ears
[
47
]
. We can similarly remove convex ears from each polar piece until either the piece is fully triangulated or there is one remaining fragment that has no convex vertices. We can triangulate the final “anti-convex” fragment by extending shortest paths from the north pole to each vertex.

Theorem 2.6
.

Suppose we can construct a morph between two isomorphic shortest-path triangulations of the sphere in
O(T(n))
time. Then we can construct a morph between two isomorphic embeddings of the same 3-connected planar graph in
O(T(n)+n\log n)
time.

Let
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
be any two isomorphic shortest-path embeddings of the same 3-connected planar graph on the sphere. As in the previous section, we compute an isomorphic coherent triangulation
\Gamma_{1/2}
in
O(n)
time
[
33
]
and then construct a morph from
\Gamma_{0}
to
\Gamma_{1}
through this intermediate coherent triangulation.

We construct a morph from
\Gamma_{0}
to
\Gamma_{1/2}
as follows. First, we triangulate the faces of
\Gamma_{0}
in
O(n\log n)
time, as described by Lemma
2.5
. Then we triangulate each face of
\Gamma_{1/2}
isomorphically to the corresponding face of
\Gamma_{0}
; this is always possible because the faces of
\Gamma_{1/2}
are convex. In particular, if the north face of
\Gamma_{0}
contains a hemisphere, we introduce one new vertex inside the north face of
\Gamma_{1/2}
. Finally, we construct a morph from the triangulation of
\Gamma_{0}
to the triangulation of
\Gamma_{1/2}
, in at most
T(n+1)=O(T(n))
time, and simply ignore the vertices and edges that are not part of the original embeddings.

We similarly construct a morph
\Gamma_{1}\rightsquigarrow\Gamma_{1/2}
, and finally assemble a morph from
\Gamma_{0}
to
\Gamma_{1}
by concatenating
\Gamma_{0}\rightsquigarrow\Gamma_{1/2}
and the reverse of
\Gamma_{1}\rightsquigarrow\Gamma_{1/2}
.

We conjecture that similar reductions can be developed for spherical embeddings that are not 3-connected, mirroring reductions from morphing arbitrary planar straight-line graphs to morphing planar triangulations
[
2
,
96
,
93
,
95
,
55
]
. We leave the development of these more general reductions to future work.

3
Longitudinal Shelling

A
shelling
of a spherical triangulation
\Gamma
is an ordering
f_{1},f_{2},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{2n-4}
of the faces of
\Gamma
such that the union of any prefix
U_{k}=\bigcup_{i\leq k}f_{i}
is either empty (
k=0
), the entire sphere (
k=2n-4
), or a topological disk. We call a shelling
longitudinal
if every disk
U_{k}
is
\theta
-monotone. The triangulation
\Gamma
is
longitudinally shellable
if it has a longitudinal shelling.

Bruggesser and Mani’s line shelling
[
17
]
implies that every
coherent
triangulation is longitudinally shellable. Awartani and Henderson
[
4
]
proved that a triangulation is longitudinally shellable if it has a
longitudinal seam
, that is, a longitude
\ell
such that for any edge
e
, the intersection
\ell\cap e
is either empty, an endpoint of
e
, or the entire edge
e
.

3.1
Characterization

We characterize the longitudinal shellability of
\Gamma
in terms of three directed graphs, which are illustrated in Figure
3.1
.

•

The directed dual graph
{\Gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\shortdownarrow}}
contains a directed edge from face
f_{i}
to face
f_{j}
if and only if
f_{i}
and
f_{j}
share an edge in
\Gamma
, and some point in
f_{i}
is due north (on the same longitude) of some point in
f_{j}
. The north face of
\Gamma
has out-degree
3
in
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
; each down-face has in-degree
1
and out-degree
2
; each up-face has in-degree
2
and out-degree
1
, and the south face has in-degree
3
.

•

The oriented primal graph
{\Gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\shortrightarrow}}
is the directed dual of
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
. For every undirected edge
ij
of
\Gamma
, the graph
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}
contains the directed edge
i\mathord{\shortrightarrow}j
if and only if vertex
j
is east of vertex
i
, or more formally, if the determinant

\det\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\
x_{i}&y_{i}&z_{i}\\
x_{j}&y_{j}&z_{j}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\det\begin{pmatrix}x_{i}&y_{i}\\
x_{j}&y_{j}\\
\end{pmatrix}

is positive, where
(x_{i},y_{i},z_{i})
is the coordinate vector for vertex
i
.

•

Finally,
{\Gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\ssearrow\!\sswarrow}}
is a directed proper subgraph of
\Gamma
whose edges are the legs of each down-face, each directed toward its apex.

Lemma 3.1
.

For every generic shortest-path triangulation
\Gamma
, the following conditions are equivalent:

(a)

\Gamma
is longitudinally shellable.

(b)

{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
is acyclic.

(c)

{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}
is strongly connected.

(d)

{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}
contains directed paths from
f_{\textsf{north}}
to
f_{\textsf{south}}
and from
f_{\textsf{south}}
to
f_{\textsf{north}}
.

(e)

{\Gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\ssearrow\!\sswarrow}}
is acyclic.

To prove (a)
\Rightarrow
(b), let
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{2n-4}
be a longitudinal shelling of
\Gamma
, and consider any two faces
f_{k}
and
f_{l}
that share a common edge, where
k<l
. The disk
U_{k}
is
\theta
-monotone, and therefore includes every point due north of
f_{k}
, but does not include
f_{l}
. It follows that
f_{l}
is south of
f_{k}
, and thus
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
contains the directed edge
f_{k}\mathord{\shortrightarrow}f_{l}
. We conclude that
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
is acyclic.

On the other hand, to prove (b)
\Rightarrow
(a), suppose
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
is acyclic. Let
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{2n-4}
be any topological ordering of
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
.
The north face is the only vertex of
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
with in-degree
0
, so it must be
f_{1}
.
Consider the prefix union
U_{k}=\bigcup_{i\leq k}f_{i}
for some index
k
, and suppose some longitude
\ell
intersects two faces
f_{i}
and
f_{j}
such that
i\leq k<j
. The intersection
\ell\cap f_{i}
must be north of
\ell\cap f_{j}
, since otherwise
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
would contain a path from
f_{j}
to
f_{i}
, contradicting the topological order. Thus,
U_{k}
is a
\theta
-monotone disk. It follows that
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{2n-4}
is a longitudinal shelling of
\Gamma
.

The equivalence (b)
\Leftrightarrow
(c) follows from the duality between directed cycles and directed edge-cuts in directed planar graphs
[
66
,
79
]
.

The implication (c)
\Rightarrow
(d) is trivial. To prove the converse (c)
\Leftarrow
(d), suppose
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}
contains directed paths
\pi_{\downarrow}
from
f_{\textsf{north}}
to
f_{\textsf{south}}
and
\pi_{\uparrow}
from from
f_{\textsf{south}}
to
f_{\textsf{north}}
. Consider an arbitrary vertex
i
. Because every edge in
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}
is oriented from west to east, following edges of
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}
starting at
i
must eventually reach some vertex of
\pi_{\downarrow}
. Thus,
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}
contains a directed walk from
i
to
f_{\textsf{south}}
. Symmetrically,
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}
contains a directed walk from
f_{\textsf{south}}
to
i
, starting with some prefix of
\pi_{\uparrow}
. We conclude that
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}
is strongly connected.

The implication (b)
\Rightarrow
(e) is straightforward. If
i\mathord{\shortrightarrow}j
is an edge of
{\Gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\ssearrow\!\sswarrow}}
, then
f_{\textsf{above}}(i)
is north of
f_{\textsf{above}}(j)
, so there is a directed path from
f_{\textsf{above}}(i)
to
f_{\textsf{above}}(j)
in
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
. Thus, if
{\Gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\ssearrow\!\sswarrow}}
contains a directed cycle, then
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
also contains a directed cycle.

It remains only to prove the implication (e)
\Rightarrow
(b). Suppose
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
contains a directed cycle. Let
C
be any strongly connected component of
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
with more than one vertex, and let
S
denote the union (or “sleeve”) of faces of
\Gamma
whose dual vertices are in
C
. The north and south faces are respectively the unique source and sink vertices of
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
, so they cannot be contained in
C
or in
S
. The following claims imply that
S
is a
\theta
-monotone annulus that separates the polar faces of
\Gamma
.

Claim 3.2
.

S
intersects every longitude.

We prove this claim by adapting an argument of Palazzi and Snoeyink
[
82
]
. For any two faces
f
and
f^{\prime}
in
C
, let
f\Downarrow f^{\prime}
indicate that some longitude
\ell
intersects both
f
and
f^{\prime}
, and
f\cap\ell
is north of
f^{\prime}\cap\ell
. Let
f_{1}\Downarrow f_{2}\Downarrow\cdots\Downarrow f_{r}\Downarrow f_{1}
be the minimum-length cycle through the faces in
C
with respect to this relation. We trivially have
r\geq 3
, since no face can be both north and south of another face. If any longitude does not intersect
S
, then some face
f_{i}
in this cycle is furthest east, which implies
f_{i-1}\Downarrow f_{i+1}
, contradicting the minimality of the cycle.

Claim 3.3
.

S
is
\theta
-monotone.

Consider any longitude
\ell
, and let
a
and
b
respectively denote the northernmost and southernmost point in
\ell\cap S
. Let
f^{\prime}_{a},f^{\prime}_{a+1},\dots,f^{\prime}_{b}
denote the sequence of faces of
\Gamma
that intersect the longitudinal arc from
a
to
b
, in order from north to south; in particular,
f^{\prime}_{a}
is the face of
\Gamma
just south of
a
, and
f^{\prime}_{b}
is the face of
\Gamma
just north of
b
. We easily observe that
f^{\prime}_{a}\in C
and
f^{\prime}_{b}\in C
, and that
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
contains the edge
f^{\prime}_{i}\mathord{\shortrightarrow}f^{\prime}_{i+1}
for each index
a\leq i<b
. It follows that
C
contains all faces
f^{\prime}_{i}
, which implies that
S\cap\ell
is connected.

It follows that
S
has two boundary curves, each of which is a simple
\theta
-monotone cycle in
\Gamma
. Let
\sigma
denote the southern boundary of
S
. Any face
f
in
S
that is incident to
\sigma
has at least one out-neighbor in
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
that is is not in
C
. On the other hand, because
C
is a strongly connected component of
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
, every face in
S
has at least one out-neighbor in
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
that is in
C
. It follows that every face in
S
that is incident to the southern boundary of
S
has out-degree
2
in
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
, and thus must be a down-face. Moreover, every edge of
\sigma
is a leg of a
unique
down-face; in particular, every edge of
\sigma
has a corresponding directed edge in
{\Gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\ssearrow\!\sswarrow}}
.

Finally, consider the set
{\sigma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\ssearrow\!\sswarrow}}
of all such directed edges. If any two edges in
{\sigma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\ssearrow\!\sswarrow}}
were directed toward each other, they would be the legs of a single down-face, which we just argued is impossible. Thus,
{\sigma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\ssearrow\!\sswarrow}}
is a directed cycle in
{\Gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\ssearrow\!\sswarrow}}
. This completes the proof of Lemma
3.1
.

Each of the conditions (b), (c), (d), and (e) in Lemma
3.1
can be tested in
O(n)
time using textbook graph algorithms.

Awartani and Henderson
[
4
]
explicitly consider spherical triangulations with edges that lie along longitudes. It is straightforward to extend Lemma
3.1
to such triangulations by
including
both orientations of longitudinal edges in
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}
,
excluding
both orientations of their dual edges from
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}
, and
excluding
both orientations of vertical edges from
{\Gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\ssearrow\!\sswarrow}}
. With this extension, Lemma
3.1
immediately implies a key result of Awartani and Henderson:

Corollary 3.4

(Awartani and Henderson
[
4
]
)
.

If
\Gamma
has a longitudinal seam, then
\Gamma
is longitudinally shellable.

Suppose
\Gamma
has a seam along some longitude
\ell
. Then the edges of
\Gamma
that are contained in
\ell
must comprise a single undirected path the north and south faces of
\Gamma
. It follows that
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}
contains
directed
paths along
\ell
from each polar face to the other. We conclude that
\Gamma
meets condition (d) of Lemma
3.1
.

3.2
The Awartani–Henderson Embedding

Theorem 3.5

(Awartani and Henderson
[
4
]
)
.

Every longitudinally shellable triangulation of the sphere is sinkable. Moreover, given any longitudinally shellable triangulation
\Gamma
, we can compute a longitudinal morph from
\Gamma
to a southern triangulation in
O(n)
time.

Let
\Gamma
be any longitudinally shellable triangulation. Corollary
2.3
implies that to prove
\Gamma
is sinkable, it suffices to construct a southern weak triangulation
\Gamma^{\prime}
that is longitudinally equivalent to
\Gamma
, in
O(n)
time. By projecting or scaling to the plane
z=-1
, we can think of
\Gamma^{\prime}
as a weak
planar straight-line
triangulation that is
\theta
-equivalent to
\Gamma
, meaning each vertex of
\Gamma^{\prime}
must lie on the ray from the origin determined by its longitude in
\Gamma
. Because the edges of
\Gamma^{\prime}
are straight line segments, our algorithm only needs to specify the location
p^{\prime}_{i}
of each vertex
i
in
\Gamma^{\prime}
.

Following Awartani and Henderson
[
4
, Theorem 3.3]
, we construct
\Gamma^{\prime}
by embedding the
faces
of
\Gamma
one at a time, following an arbitrary longitudinal shelling order
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{2n-4}
. Throughout the construction, we maintain a weakly convex polygon
W
—that is, a polygon with convex interior whose vertices have interior angle
at most

\pi
—whose interior contains the origin. This polygon satisfies the following invariants for each vertex
i
, after faces
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}
have been embedded:

•

If
f_{\textsf{above}}(i)
has been embedded, then
p^{\prime}_{i}
lies outside the interior of
W
.

•

If in addition
f_{\textsf{below}}(i)
has
not
been embedded, then
p^{\prime}_{i}
is a vertex of
W
.

To start the construction, we embed the north face
f_{1}
by fixing its vertices
arbitrarily
on their respective rays, for example on the unit circle. We also initialize
W
to be the convex hull of these three points. Then for each index
k
from
2
to
2n-5
, we proceed as follows:

•

If
f_{k}
is an up-face, then all three vertices of
f_{k}
have already been placed on the boundary of
W
, so we embed
f_{k}
as the convex hull of its vertices. Because
W
is weakly convex, it contains the base segment of
f_{k}
. After embedding
f_{k}
, the apex of
f_{k}
is no longer a vertex of
W
, and the base of
f_{k}
becomes an edge of
W
. (The vertices of
f_{k}
may be collinear, in which case the interior of
W
does not change.)

•

On the other hand, if
f_{k}
is a down-face, the base vertices of
f_{k}
have already been placed on the boundary of
W
, but not the apex. We place the apex of
f_{k}
at the intersection of its ray and the base of
f_{k}
; thus,
f_{k}
is embedded as a degenerate triangle. The apex of
f_{k}
becomes a new vertex of
W
, and the interior of
W
does not change.

In both cases, it is easy to verify that the invariants on
W
hold after
f_{k}
is embedded. Finally, when we consider the south face
f_{2n-4}
, all of its vertices have already been placed.

Awartani and Henderson’s construction can be described more concisely as follows. We construct a southern weak triangulation
\Gamma^{\prime}
that is longitudinally equivalent to
\Gamma
, by considering vertices in any topological order of
{\Gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\ssearrow\!\sswarrow}}
and assigning a new coordinate
z^{\prime}_{i}
to each vertex. (This topological order is an example of a
canonical order
for
\Gamma

[
48
,
22
]
.) The first three vertices lie on the north face and thus can be placed anywhere below the equator on their respective longitudes. For each later vertex
i\geq 4
, we make
z^{\prime}_{i}
as large as possible, such that no face induced by vertices
1
through
i
(except the north face) is inverted. Awartani and Henderson’s argument implies that every down-face in the resulting triangulation
\Gamma^{\prime}
is degenerate.

3.3
One-Bend Morphing

Several authors have developed morphing algorithms for planar graphs that allow (or require)
bends
in intermediate edges
[
72
,
10
,
11
,
43
]
. To close this section, we describe an efficient morphing algorithm that introduces at most one bend into each edge; that is, we can efficiently construct an isotopy between any two isomorphic spherical triangulations, such that each edge of every intermediate triangulation is either a shortest path or the concatenation of two shortest paths. We call such an isotopy a
one-bend morph
.

Our algorithm uses a mild generalization of longitudinal morphing. A
rotated longitudinal morph
moves all vertices move along great circular arcs through some pair of antipodal points, called the
poles
of the morph. Rotated longitudinal morph are the natural spherical analogue of
unidirectional morphs
in the plane, which move the vertices of a planar straight-line graph along parallel lines at constant speeds
[
6
,
2
]
. Any rotated longitudinal morph can be implemented by linearly interpolating the homogeneous coordinate vectors of vertices along parallel lines in
\mdmathbb{R}^{3}
, as described in the proof of Lemma
2.1
.

Corollary 3.6
.

Every pair of isomorphic
n
-vertex spherical triangulations is connected by a one-bend morph consisting of
O(1)
rotations and
O(n)
rotated longitudinal morphs. Moreover, this one-bend morph can be computed in
O(n^{2})
time.

Let
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
be arbitrary isomorphic triangulations with the same underlying planar graph
G
. If necessary, we rotate both triangulations so that they have the same north face, and so that every great circle through the poles contains at most one vertex of each. We then construct a one-bend morph from
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
through an intermediate
southern
coherent triangulation
\Gamma_{1/2}
, similarly to our strategy in Section
2.4
. Again, the intermediate triangulation
\Gamma_{1/2}
can be constructed in
O(n)
time
[
33
]
.

To construct a one-bend morph from
\Gamma_{0}
to
\Gamma_{1/2}
, we start by refining
\Gamma_{0}
so that it contains a longitudinal seam, as shown in Figure
3.2
. Let
\ell
be the longitude through an arbitrary vertex of the north face of
\Gamma_{0}
. We refine
\Gamma_{0}
by introducing bend vertices at the intersection of
\ell
with each edge of
\Gamma_{0}
, and then adding edges that split the south face into two smaller triangles and each non-polar face intersecting
\ell
into three smaller triangles. Call the resulting refined triangulation
\tilde{\Gamma}_{0}
. Awartani and Henderson’s results (Corollary
3.4
and Theorem
3.5
) imply that we can longitudinally morph
\tilde{\Gamma}_{0}
to a southern triangulation
\tilde{\Gamma}^{\prime}_{0}
in
O(n)
time.

Figure 3.2:
Refining a triangulation along a longitude.

We then refine
\Gamma_{1/2}
into a southern triangulation
\tilde{\Gamma}_{1/2}
isomorphic to
\tilde{\Gamma}^{\prime}_{0}
, by bisecting the same edges and subdividing the same faces as we did to refine
\Gamma_{0}
. Finally, we can construct a morph between the southern triangulations
\tilde{\Gamma}^{\prime}_{0}
and
\tilde{\Gamma}_{1/2}
using any planar morphing algorithm. In particular, an algorithm of Alamdari
et al.

[
2
]
computes a planar morph consisting of a sequence of
O(n)
unidirectional morphs; an improvement by Klemz
[
68
]
computes this morph in
O(n^{2})
time. Lifting a unidirectional morph in the plane
z=-1
to the sphere yields a rotated longitudinal morph whose poles lie on the equatorial plane
z=0
.

Putting the pieces together, we obtain a morph from the refined triangulation
\tilde{\Gamma}_{0}
to the refined coherent triangulation
\tilde{\Gamma}_{1/2}
that consists of
O(n)
rotated longitudinal morphs. Ignoring the refinement edges yields a morph from
\Gamma_{0}
to
\Gamma_{1/2}
where each intermediate edge has at most one bend. We can extend this morph to
\Gamma_{1}
by concatenating the reversal of a similar morph from
\Gamma_{1}
to
\Gamma_{1/2}
. The overall running time is dominated by two invocations of Klemz’s planar morphing algorithm
[
68
]
.

Theorem
2.6
immediately generalizes this result to 3-connected embeddings:

Corollary 3.7
.

Every pair of isomorphic shortest-path embeddings of the same 3-connected planar graph on the sphere is connected by a one-bend morph consisting of
O(1)
rotations and
O(n)
rotated longitudinal morphs. Moreover, this one-bend morph can be computed in
O(n^{2})
time.

4
Longitudinally Shellable Rotations

When a given triangulation
\Gamma
is not longitudinally shellable, we can still apply our morphing strategy if we can find a rotation of the sphere that transforms
\Gamma
into a longitudinally shellable triangulation. In this section, we describe an efficient algorithm that either finds such a rotation or correctly reports that no such rotation exists. Experimental evidence (described in Section
6
) suggests that “in practice”, even for triangulations with many long skinny triangles, a significant fraction of rotations of
\Gamma
are longitudinally shellable, so it suffices to consider a small number of random rotations. In fact, without exception, every one of the thousands of random adversarial triangulations we generated had shellable rotations. Nevertheless, in Section
4.2
, we construct a triangulation with no longitudinally shellable rotation.

4.1
Finding a Shelling Direction

Instead of considering different rotations of
\Gamma
, it is convenient to keep
\Gamma
fixed and consider different locations for the “north pole”. We call a unit vector
p
a
shelling direction
for
\Gamma
if any (and therefore every) rotation of the sphere that takes
p
to the standard north pole
(0,0,1)
also takes
\Gamma
to a longitudinally shellable triangulation. We similarly define the directed graphs
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}(p)
and
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}(p)
and
{\Gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\ssearrow\!\sswarrow}}(p)
. For example, for any unit vector
p=(x,y,z)
, the graph
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}(p)
contains the edge
i\mathord{\shortrightarrow}j
if and only if

\operatorname{vol}(p,i,j)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\det\begin{pmatrix}x&y&z\\
x_{i}&y_{i}&z_{i}\\
x_{j}&y_{j}&z_{j}\\
\end{pmatrix}>0,

and
p
is a shelling direction for
\Gamma
if and only if the graphs
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}(p)
and
{\Gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\ssearrow\!\sswarrow}}(p)
are acyclic.

Theorem 4.1
.

Given any shortest-path triangulation
\Gamma
on the sphere, we can either compute a shelling direction for
\Gamma
or report correctly that no such direction exists, in
O(n^{2.5}\log^{3}n)
time.

Each edge
ij
in
\Gamma
lies on a unique great circle; let
\mathcal{A}(\Gamma)
denote the arrangement of all
3n-6
such great circles. For any two unit vectors
p
and
q
, the graphs
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}(p)
and
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}(q)
are identical if and only if
p
and
q
lie in the same cell of
\mathcal{A}(\Gamma)
. Thus, for any cell
C
, we can write
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}(C)
to denote the graph
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}(p)
for any
p\in C
. If
C
and
C^{\prime}
are adjacent two-dimensional cells of
\mathcal{A}(\Gamma)
, then
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}(C)
and
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}(C^{\prime})
differ in the direction of exactly one edge.

We can find a shelling direction for
\Gamma
in
O(n^{3})
time by constructing the great-circle arrangement
\mathcal{A}(\Gamma)
, and then for each two-dimensional cell
C
, check whether
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}(C)
is strongly connected. (This arrangement is centrally symmetric, and the gnomonic projection of either hemisphere to any tangent plane is an arrangement of
lines
. Thus, we can use any classical algorithm to construct line arrangements
[
27
,
38
,
39
]
instead of more sophisticated algorithms to construct arrangements of more general circles on spheres
[
23
]
.)

We can speed up this naive algorithm using a data structure of Diks and Sankowski for reachability queries in dynamic directed plane graphs
[
34
]
. Diks and Sankowski’s data structure maintains a directed planar graph with a fixed embedding, supports edge insertions and deletions that do not change the embedding in
O(\sqrt{n}\log^{3}n)
time, and supports queries of the form “Is there a directed path from vertex
i
to vertex
j
?” in
O(\sqrt{n}\log^{2}n)
time.

To use this data structure, we traverse the dual graph of the arrangement
\mathcal{A}(\Gamma)
. At each two-dimensional cell
C
, we perform two reachability queries in
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}(C)
between the two polar faces. If both reachability queries succeed, then by Lemma
3.1
(d), cell
C
contains a shelling direction. When we move from a cell
C
to a neighboring cell
C^{\prime}
, we delete one edge of
{\Gamma}^{\shortrightarrow}(C)
and insert its reversal. Altogether, we perform
O(n^{2})
queries and
O(n^{2})
updates.

4.2
Unshellable From Every Direction

Regard each edge of the undirected dual graph
\Gamma^{*}
as a pair of opposing directed edges, which we call

∗
of darts in
\Gamma^{*}
, we define the

to be the set of all north poles

contains every dart in

if and only if

for

Lemma 4.2
.

For every directed cycle
∗

is the interior of a convex spherical polygon, that is, the intersection of

with a (possibly empty) open convex polyhedral cone.

Let
i\mathord{\shortrightarrow}j
be any dart in
\Gamma

and

be the faces incident to

on the left and right, respectively. The directed graph

. The set of all points

bounded by the great circle through

A

is the subset of faces of
\Gamma
dual to any directed cycle

. We sketch the construction of a triangulation

First we define a simple

around a great circle using

and with all edges at angle

sufficiently far from the rotor, the rotor defines a cycle in the directed dual graph

from the rotor itself. In particular, in the limit as

.

Nothing in our construction prevents our bad triangulation

from being

. In particular, just before we add the final rotors to cover the “holes” in the square faces of

satisfies this condition.

5
Sinking

Finally, we present an exact characterization of sinkable triangulations. Let
\Gamma
be any shortest-path triangulation of the sphere. Recall from Corollary
2.3
that
\Gamma
is sinkable if and only if there is a weak triangulation
\Gamma^{\prime}
, with all vertices below the equator, that is
\theta
-equivalent to
\Gamma
.

By scaling coordinate vectors if necessary, as described in Section
2.2
, we can consider only weak triangulations
\Gamma^{\prime}
where each vertex has the same
x
- and
y
-coordinates as in
\Gamma
. Let
(x_{i},y_{i},z_{i})
and
(x_{i},y_{i},z^{\prime}_{i})
denote the coordinates of vertex
i
in
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
, respectively. Then
\Gamma^{\prime}
is
\theta
-equivalent to
\Gamma
if and only if
{\operatorname{vol}}^{\prime}(i,j,k)\geq 0
for every non-polar face
(i,j,k)
, where

{\operatorname{vol}}^{\prime}(i,j,k)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\det\begin{pmatrix}x_{i}&y_{i}&z^{\prime}_{i}\\
x_{j}&y_{j}&z^{\prime}_{j}\\
x_{k}&y_{k}&z^{\prime}_{k}\end{pmatrix}

We reiterate that because all
x
- and
y
-coordinates are fixed, the volume determinant
\operatorname{vol}^{\prime}(i,j,k)
is a linear function of the vector
z^{\prime}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

A spherical triangulation
\Gamma
is sinkable if and only if the following linear program is feasible:

\begin{array}[]{r@{\quad}r@{~}l@{\qquad}l}\text{maximize}&\sum_{i}z^{\prime}_{i}\\
\text{subject to}&z^{\prime}_{i}&=-1&\text{for every vertex $i$ of $f_{\textsf{north}}$}\\
&{\operatorname{vol}}^{\prime}(i,j,k)&\geq 0&\text{for every non-polar face $(i,j,k)$}\end{array}

(5.1)

Suppose
z^{\prime}=(z^{\prime}_{1},\dots,z^{\prime}_{n})
is the
z
-coordinate vector of a weak triangulation
\Gamma^{\prime}
that is
\theta
-equivalent to
\Gamma
, such that
z^{\prime}_{i}<0
for all
i
. Without loss of generality, assume the vertices of the north face of
\Gamma^{\prime}
are vertices
1
,
2
, and
3
. We can move these three vertices to the plane
z=-1
by applying the linear transformation
(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,y,ax+by+cz)
, where
a
,
b
, and
c
satisfy the linear system

\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&y_{1}&z^{\prime}_{1}\\
x_{2}&y_{2}&z^{\prime}_{2}\\
x_{3}&y_{3}&z^{\prime}_{3}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b\\
c\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\
-1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}.

This linear transformation has positive determinant. Let
z^{\prime\prime}
denote the transformed
z
-coordinate vector and
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
the corresponding weak triangulation. Because no face of
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
is inverted, the coordinate vector of every other vertex
i
is a positive linear combination of the north-face coordinate vectors; thus, we have
z^{\prime\prime}_{i}<0
for all
i
.

Lemma
5.1
immediately implies an algorithm to compute a sinking longitudinal morph, or report correctly that none exists, in weakly polynomial time, provided the input
x
- and
y
-coordinates are rational
[
56
]
. We obtain a simpler and faster algorithm by identifying the optimal basis of linear program (
5.1
), subject to a mild technical condition.

First we need a small extension of a result used in many planar morphing algorithms
[
64
,
36
,
68
,
28
,
67
]
. Recall that a polygon is
weakly convex
if its interior is convex and each of its vertices has interior angle
at most

\pi
. Let
T
be any straight-line plane graph whose outer face is a simple
x
-monotone polygon and whose inner faces are triangles. A weakly convex polygon
W
is
compatible
with
T
if there is a homeomorphism from the boundary of
T
to the boundary of
W
that preserves
x
-coordinates. A weak planar triangulation
T^{\prime}
is
x
-equivalent
to

if it has the same underlying graph, every nondegenerate face of
T^{\prime}
has the same orientation as the corresponding face of
T
, and corresponding vertices have equal
x
-coordinates.

Lemma 5.2
.

Given a triangulation
T
of an
x
-monotone polygon in the plane, and a weakly convex polygon
W
that is compatible with
T
, there is a weak planar triangulation
T^{\prime}
that is
x
-equivalent to
T
and whose outer face is

.

When

is a

convex polygon (all interior angles are strictly less than

), the lemma follows immediately from algorithms of Chrobak, Goodrich, and Tammassia
[
28
, Theorem 3.5]
and Kleist, Klemz, Lubiw, Schlipf, Staals, and Strash
[
67
, Lemma 11]
. Both of these algorithms first compute positive weights
\lambda_{i\mathord{\shortrightarrow}j}>0
for each dart

of

so that the
x
-coordinate of each interior vertex

is the weighted average of the
x
-coordinates of the neighbors of

, the incoming dart weights satisfy the constraints

x_{j}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i\mathord{\shortrightarrow}j}x_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i}\lambda_{i\mathord{\shortrightarrow}j}=1,

where for notational simplicity we define
\lambda_{i\mathord{\shortrightarrow}j}=0
if

is not an edge of

et al.
compute the weights
\lambda_{i\mathord{\shortrightarrow}j}
using flows
[

et al.
use a simpler averaging computation at each vertex

[
67

.

Then, to compute the new embedding
T^{\prime}
, both algorithms fix the boundary vertices to the corresponding vertices of
W
, and then compute the interior
y
-coordinates by solving the linear system

y^{\prime}_{j}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i\mathord{\shortrightarrow}j}y^{\prime}_{j}\qquad\text{for every interior vertex $j$}

(5.2)

Floater’s extension
[
45
]
of Tutte’s spring embedding theorem
[

]
guarantees that the resulting

A straightforward limiting argument implies that when

of polygons, where

and

is strictly convex for all

is well-defined and varies continuously with

T^{\prime}=T^{\prime}(0)
is a weak triangulation

We emphasize that our algorithms never

If

of
B
maps to a vertex in the closed interior of
\tau
, which implies
z^{\prime}_{i}\leq z^{\prime\prime}_{i}\leq z^{\prime}_{i}/(1+\varepsilon)
.

We assemble the overall weak triangulation
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
by applying this construction to every bar in
\Gamma^{\prime}
. (Processing each bar requires projecting to a different vertical tangent plane.) Because
z^{\prime\prime}_{i}\geq z^{\prime}_{i}
for every vertex
i
, and
z^{\prime\prime}_{i}>z^{\prime}_{i}
for at least one vertex
i
, the original weak triangulation
\Gamma^{\prime}
cannot be an optimal solution to our linear program (
5.1
).

Corollary 5.4
.

Given a shortest-path triangulation
\Gamma
of the sphere, we can either compute a longitudinal morph from
\Gamma
to a southern triangulation, or report correctly that no such morph exists, in
O(n^{\omega/2})
time, assuming linear system (
5.3
) is non-singular.

The support graph of linear system (
5.3
) is precisely the directed planar graph
{\Gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\ssearrow\!\sswarrow}}
. Thus, assuming the system is non-singular, it can be solved in
O(n^{\omega/2})=O(n^{1.1864})
time (in the real RAM model) via nested dissection and fast matrix multiplication
[
70
,
3
]
. If the solution
z^{\prime}
is a feasible point for linear program (
5.1
), the corresponding drawing
\Gamma^{\prime}
is a weak southern triangulation longitudinally equivalent to
\Gamma
. If the system is non-singular, and the solution
z^{\prime}
is not a feasible point for linear program (
5.1
), then by Theorem
5.3
, the linear program is infeasible, so we can correctly report that
\Gamma
is not sinkable.

Theorem
5.3
is a strict generalization of the Awartani–Henderson embedding described in Section
3.2
. Recall from Lemma
3.1
that
\Gamma
is longitudinally shellable if and only if its support graph
{\Gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\ssearrow\!\sswarrow}}
is acyclic. Thus, we can permute the rows and columns of (
5.3
) to obtain an upper-triangular system, which we can then solve by back-substitution. Each step of back-substitution assigns a
z
-coordinate to the apex of a single down-face, exactly mirroring one step of Awartani and Henderson’s construction.

6
Experimental Results

We implemented a suite of algorithms to construct spherical triangulations, test shellability and sinkability, construct Awartani–Henderson embeddings, and visualize longitudinal morphs. (This implementation was invaluable in identifying the objective function for our linear program (
5.1
) and leading us to conjecture Theorem
5.3
.) In particular, to stress-test our algorithms, we implemented several randomized heuristics to construct “ugly” triangulations with long edges and skinny triangles, including generalizations of Schönhardt’s polyhedron

We tested several thousand random triangulations, with dozens to hundreds of vertices. Without exception, every triangulation we generated had at least one
longitudinally shellable
rotation. Even in the most pathological families we generated, we could find a longitudinally shellable rotation for most triangulations by trying at most four random directions, and a sinkable rotation by trying at most three.

7
Conjectures and Open Problems

Our results suggest several directions for further research. The most obvious open problem is to prove or disprove Conjecture
2.4
: Every shortest-path triangulation of the sphere has a sinkable rotation. More concretely, is there a polynomial-time algorithm that either finds a sinkable rotation of a given spherical triangulation or correctly reports that no such rotation exists?

A fast algorithm to find sinkable rotations would imply an efficient spherical morphing algorithm, but the resulting morphs have one undesirable feature: Even when we are morphing between full triangulations, most of the intermediate triangulations are not full. In contrast, Cairns’s edge-collapsing argument
[
21
]
actually yields morphs in which every intermediate triangulation is full. We conjecture that techniques used to efficiently construct piecewise-linear planar morphs, which are based on Cairns’s strategy, can be generalized to the sphere to yield
full
morphs. The most significant obstacle appears to be morphing a shortest-path triangulation with one missing edge, so that the unique quadrilateral face becomes convex. This task is complicated by the fact that the initial quadrilateral face could have more than one reflex vertex.

Even much simpler questions about sinkability remain open. Experiments with thousands of random triangulations, similar to those reported in Section
6
, are consistent with the following conjectures:

Conjecture 7.1
.

If every edge of a shortest-path triangulation
\Gamma
of the sphere has length at most
\pi/2
, then
\Gamma
(and therefore every rotation of
\Gamma
) is sinkable.

Conjecture 7.2
.

For any shortest-path triangulation
\Gamma
, if there is a great circle that does not cross any edge of
\Gamma
, then
\Gamma
is sinkable.

Either Conjecture
7.1
or Conjecture
7.2
would imply the following conjecture about the symmetry of sinkability, which is also consistent with our experimental observations. A shortest-path triangulation is
floatable
if it can be longitudinally morphed into the
northern
hemisphere.

Conjecture 7.3
.

A shortest-path triangulation is sinkable if and only if is it floatable.

The corresponding claim about longitudinal shellability follows directly from Lemma
3.1
; for any unit vector
p
, the graph
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}(p)
is the reversal of
{\Gamma}^{\shortdownarrow}(-p)
, and the reversal of a directed acyclic graph is a directed acyclic graph.

Two somewhat more technical conjectures concern the linear system (
5.3
) introduced in Theorem
5.3
. The only triangulations we have found where this linear system is singular lie on the boundary between sinkable and non-sinkable triangulations. For example, the linear system associated with the critical Schönhardt triangulation
\bar{S}_{\pi/6}
is singular, but for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, the linear systems for the sinkable triangulation
\bar{S}_{\pi/6-\varepsilon}
and the unsinkable triangulation
\bar{S}_{\pi/6+\varepsilon}
are both non-singular.

Conjecture 7.4
.

If the linear system (
5.3
) defined by a shortest-path triangulation
\Gamma
is singular, then
\Gamma
is not sinkable.

Conjecture 7.5
.

Linear system (
5.3
) is
generically
non-singular; either an arbitrarily small random rotation of
\Gamma
or an arbitrarily small random perturbation of its
x
- and
y
-coordinates yields a nonsingular system (
5.3
) with probability
1
.

Ho
[
61
,
60
]
proved that the space of all embeddings of a maximal planar graph, with a fixed outer triangular face, is topologically trivial. Ho’s result was generalized to planar triangulations with convex outer faces by Bloch, Connelly, and Henderson
[
14
]
and Bloch
[

]
; more recent proofs have been given by Cerf
[
24
]
and Luo
[
73
]
. Connelly, Henderson, Ho, and Starbird
[
32
]
conjectured that every isotopy class of geodesic triangulations on
any
surface
S
with constant curvature is homotopy-equivalent to the group
\emph{Isom}_{0}(S)
of isometries of
S
that are homotopic to the identity. Their conjecture was recently proved both for the flat torus
[
74
,
42
]
and for arbitrary negative-curvature surfaces
[
75
]
; all of these proofs rely on nontrivial extensions of Tutte and Floater’s planar barycentric embedding theorem
[
30
,
54
,
59
,
75
]
. The only closed surface for which this conjecture remains open is the sphere! Cairns actually announced a proof of this conjecture for the sphere in 1941
[

]
but later retracted it
[

,

]
; Awartani and Henderson also posed this conjecture as a motivation for their work
[

.

Finally, essentially nothing is known about morphing the more general class of
long geodesic embeddings
, whose edges are great-circular arcs that may be longer than a semicircle. Even representing such embeddings is nontrivial; in particular, vertex coordinates and a rotation system do not necessarily specify a unique long geodesic embedding. Using ad-hoc arguments, we have been able to prove that any two long geodesic embeddings of
K_{4}
are connected by a continuous family of such embeddings. Do such morphs exist for all planar graphs? Can they be constructed efficiently?
