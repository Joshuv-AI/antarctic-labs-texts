Intrapartum glucose metrics associated with adverse neonatal outcomes among people with gestational diabetes mellitus
Authors: Alexandra C Gallagher, Alyssa R Hersh, Lucy Ward, Amy M Valent
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHyperglycemia is a recognized risk factor for adverse neonatal outcomes. However, the impact of achieving glucose goals during labor on the risk of such outcomes remains poorly understood for individuals with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). We sought to compare continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) metrics between laboring GDM pregnancies complicated by adverse neonatal outcomes to those without complications.MethodsSecondary analysis of pregnant people with GDM randomized to using CGM versus capillary blood glucose (CBG) for management of GDM. Among all participants wearing a CGM during labor, we compared pregnancies that were complicated by a significant neonatal outcome as defined by a composite including one or more of the following: hypoglycemia, hyperbilirubinemia, respiratory distress syndrome, or neonatal intensive care unit admission to those without this composite adverse outcome. Continuous variables were compared with two‐sample t‐tests, and categorical variables were tested with Fisher's Exact tests. We correlated antepartum to intrapartum glucose management by measuring mean glucose and glucose percent time‐in‐range (TIR; primary intrapartum range 70–120 mg/dL, with candidate ranges 70–110 and 70–140 mg/dL), stratified by composite adverse neonatal outcome.ResultsOf the 111 in the primary trial, 59 were included in this secondary analysis. A total of 52 of 111 patients in the primary trial were excluded, as 23 were missing intrapartum data and 29 had no trial of labor prior to cesarean delivery. The cohort with the composite adverse neonatal outcome spent significantly less intrapartum TIR between 70 and 120 mg/dL (62.7% vs. 80.7%, p = 0.012) and more time‐above‐range (TAR) ≥ 120 mg/dL (26.7% vs. 10.6%, p = 0.007) compared to those with no composite adverse neonatal outcome. The mean glucose for those with adverse neonatal outcomes was non‐significantly higher than those without (103.9 vs. 94.6, p = 0.059). There was a weak‐to‐moderate positive correlation between antenatal and intrapartum glucose management.ConclusionIntrapartum participants with GDM and a composite adverse neonatal outcome spent less TIR, more TAR, with a higher mean glucose in labor than those without a composite adverse outcome. A weak‐to‐moderate positive correlation between antenatal and intrapartum glucose management was observed.Keywords: continuous glucose monitoring, gestational diabetes, intrapartum, neonatal hypoglycemia

INTRODUCTION

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a condition of carbohydrate intolerance which affects 6%–9% of US patients in pregnancy [1]. GDM is associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes such as hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, cesarean section, maternal type 2 diabetes (T2D) risk, large for gestational age (LGA), and small for gestational age (SGA) neonates [2]. In addition, neonates born to patients with GDM are more likely to require neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission due to various indications [3]. One of the most common is neonatal hypoglycemia, which occurs in up to 45% of GDM patients [4, 5]. At a minimum, NICU admission separates neonates from their mothers and contributes to increased healthcare utilization costs. In severe cases, hypoglycemia is associated with neonatal seizures and long‐term sequela [4].

As optimal glucose management during pregnancy has been demonstrated to lower rates of adverse outcomes, patients are encouraged to maintain pregnancy blood glucose targets and are monitored closely antenatally [6]. However, the relationship between maternal glucose management and adverse neonatal outcomes in the intrapartum period for patients with GDM remains a topic of debate with a heterogeneity of published results. A systematic review published in 2017 designed to address this question states that while there is a pathophysiologic mechanism for suggesting that maternal hyperglycemia in labor may increase rates of neonatal hypoglycemia, future studies are needed to help clarify whether lower intrapartum glucose goals are superior to broader glucose targets for patients with different diabetes types in pregnancy [7]. The lack of clarity regarding the impact of intrapartum maternal hyperglycemia on neonatal adverse outcomes is reflected in the varying intrapartum practice patterns observed between institutions [4]. Closing this knowledge gap is critical to facilitate standardization of intrapartum diabetes management and improve perinatal outcomes.

Traditionally, the data available to address this clinical question have been limited to fingerstick capillary blood glucose (CBG) levels and among pregnant people with type 1 diabetes (T1D) [8]. In recent years, there has been a shift from traditional CBG testing to continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) in nonpregnant and pregnant populations with T1D and GDM [8, 9, 10]. The use of CGM is increasing among pregnant people since the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) clearance for use in pregnancy was approved in 2022–2023 [1]. CGM provides a novel opportunity to better understand the association of CGM profiles with neonatal outcomes among pregnancies complicated by GDM. Our objective for this secondary analysis was to investigate the association of maternal hyperglycemia intrapartum and neonatal adverse outcomes. We plan to address a key clinical knowledge gap and hypothesize that pregnancies with both antenatal and intrapartum glycemia meeting pregnancy targets will have lower adverse neonatal outcomes than those with either period out of range.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We performed a secondary analysis of a parent randomized controlled trial (RCT) which randomized pregnant patients with GDM to either CGM or CBG use during the antenatal period [10]. For this secondary analysis, we included all patients who underwent a trial of labor and had intrapartum CGM data available. We chose not to exclude patients based on the amount of CGM data available to maximize our sample size. We excluded those with a scheduled cesarean or emergency cesarean without a trial of labor. The intrapartum period began at time of admission for delivery or patient‐reported start of labor symptoms until the time of delivery. We compared participants whose pregnancies were complicated by a significant neonatal outcome to those without. The parent RCT was approved by the institutional review committee, received a Federal Drug Administration investigative device exemption, and was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04605497).

Institutional policies require CBG testing to drive intrapartum glucose management. Patients with long‐acting insulin are continued on their regimen intrapartum with dose adjustments at the discretion of the clinical team as indicated by labor progress, parity, and epidural status. During latent labor, patients are allowed to eat and CBG testing is performed fasting and 1 h postprandial. Following placement of an epidural, patients are transitioned to a clear liquid diet and CBG monitoring is transitioned to every 4 h for the remainder of latent labor, every 2 h in active labor, and every 1 h in the second stage of labor. If a patient has two independent CBG values >120 mg/dL, an insulin infusion is initiated to achieve glucose goals.

Our neonatal outcome of interest was a composite including one or more of the following: hypoglycemia (defined by glucose <40 mg/dL or a neonate requiring glucose therapy beyond breastmilk/formula), hyperbilirubinemia requiring phototherapy, respiratory distress syndrome, or NICU admission. We sought to determine if there are glucose differences characterized by CGM metrics between these groups. As there is limited data on intrapartum CGM glucose targets for pregnancies complicated by GDM, we aimed to evaluate multiple candidate ranges. Our primary intrapartum range was 70–120 mg/dL, as this reflects our institutional protocol. Candidate ranges of 70–110 mg/dL, as recommended by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), and 70–140 mg/dL, as suggested by prior studies, were also investigated [11]. During the intrapartum period to delivery, glucose percent time‐in‐range (TIR), TIR > 90%, time‐above‐range (TAR) for each range, time‐below‐range (TBR < 70 mg/dL), and mean glucose were measured.

We characterized the antenatal and intrapartum demographics of the cohort by maternal age, parity, randomization status, body mass index (BMI) > 30, gestational age at time of GDM diagnosis, gestational age at time of trial enrollment, type of GDM (A1 vs. A2), average medication dose at delivery, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP: gestational hypertension, preeclampsia without severe features, preeclampsia with severe features, hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count (HELLP) syndrome, posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES), eclampsia, and postpartum preeclampsia), induction of labor, mode of delivery, and the presence of intraamniotic infection (IAI). We additionally characterized individual neonatal outcomes in both groups, including macrosomia (birthweight >4000 g), birth injury, LGA (birthweight >90th percentile for gestational age), SGA (<10th percentile for gestational age), Apgar score ≤6 at 5 min, average birthweight, and average gestational age at delivery. Continuous variables were compared with two sample t‐tests, and categorical variables were tested with Fisher's Exact tests and chi‐square tests. Given the repeated tests on multiple glycemic ranges, a Bonferroni adjustment for multiple testing was performed for TIR, TIR > 90%, and TAR. We also compared antenatal glycemic management to intrapartum glycemic management by mean glucose and % TIR, stratified by composite adverse neonatal outcome. Data cleaning, analysis, and visualization were performed using R Statistical Software [12]. p value <0.05 and confidence intervals that did not cross the null were considered statistically significant. Adjustment for potential confounders was not performed due to the small sample size.

RESULTS

Of the 111 patients in the parent RCT, 59 were included in this secondary analysis (Figure 1). Of the subjects included in this analysis, 40 were randomized to CGM while 19 were randomized to CBG (blinded to CGM), which was similar to the parent trial (n = 74 CGM and n = 37 CBG). Intrapartum glycemic characteristics were compared between those with adverse neonatal outcomes and those without (Table 2). Differences in demographic and pregnancy characteristics were of small magnitude, and none were statistically significant (Table 1). Among pregnancies complicated by adverse neonatal outcomes, maternal age, HDP, unscheduled cesarean delivery, and insulin‐requiring gestational diabetes mellitus (A2GDM) were not significantly different. For those on insulin, the average insulin dose at time of delivery for those with adverse neonatal outcomes was higher compared to those without. This relationship did not achieve statistical significance due to high variance. The gestational age at time of GDM diagnosis, gestational age at enrollment in the parent trial, and induction of labor rates were similar between groups.

Twenty pregnant patients (34%) in our cohort experienced the composite adverse neonatal outcome. Among those who experienced an adverse neonatal outcome, the most common was neonatal hypoglycemia (65%) followed by NICU admission (40%), respiratory distress syndrome (30%), and hyperbilirubinemia (15%). The cohort with adverse neonatal outcomes spent significantly less TIR between 70 and 120 mg/dL (62.7% vs. 80.7%, p = 0.012) and more TAR compared to those without an adverse neonatal outcome (Table 2). There was also a statistically significant difference in the proportion achieving TIR > 90% between 70 and 120 between the two groups (0% vs. 35.9%, p = 0.004) with no cases complicated by an adverse outcome achieving >90% TIR. The intrapartum mean glucose for those with adverse neonatal outcomes was 9.3 mg/dL higher than those without on average (p = 0.059).

We correlated antepartum to intrapartum glucose management, measuring mean glucose (Figure 2A). We found a moderate positive correlation between antepartum and intrapartum mean glucose levels based on a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.48 and p < 0.001. In a linear regression model, an increase of 1 mg/dL in antepartum mean glucose was associated with a 0.76 mg/dL increase in intrapartum mean glucose on average. We also compared antepartum TIR (63–140 mg/dL) to intrapartum TIR (70–120 mg/dL) which demonstrated a weak positive correlation (Figure 2B). A 1% increase in antenatal %TIR was associated with a 0.54% increase in intrapartum %TIR on average (p value = 0.162). Participants with >90% TIR during the antenatal period were more likely to achieve optimal TIR during the intrapartum period. No patients with an adverse outcome achieved >90% TIR during the antenatal and intrapartum period. We compared other pertinent confounders between groups. While there were differences, the outcome incidence was uncommon, and none were statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

We found that patients with adverse neonatal outcomes spent significantly less intrapartum TIR, more TAR, and had a higher mean glucose during labor compared to those with no adverse neonatal outcome. Additionally, we found a positive correlation between antepartum TIR and average glucose, as expected. Nevertheless, even among those with optimal antepartum management (e.g., >90% TIR between 63 and 140 mg/dL), there were adverse neonatal outcomes, suggesting incomplete protection by antepartum glucose management alone. This is an important observation, as antepartum glucose management is often prioritized above intrapartum glucose management in practice given robust literature which demonstrates that glucose optimization throughout pregnancy reduces adverse neonatal outcomes [2, 13].

Intrapartum glucose optimization may continue to provide a benefit for pregnancies complicated by GDM as those with lower intrapartum TAR and more TIR had lower rates of adverse neonatal outcomes. A previously published RCT investigated tight versus liberalized glucose management in labor and found no difference in initial neonatal glucose concentrations but the antepartum glucose achievements were unknown [14]. Additionally, the study was not powered to adequately assess differences in NICU admission rates or infrequent neonatal outcomes. The authors ultimately concluded that blood glucose surveillance every 4 h in labor, with intervention only if the maternal glucose level was lower than 60 mg/dL or greater than 120 mg/dL, was best supported by their findings [14]. Our findings similarly support that patients with adverse neonatal outcomes spent more time >120 mg/dL than those without. Antepartum glucose management alone was not sufficient to reduce intrapartum glucose levels or prevent adverse neonatal outcomes. Thus, both the antepartum and intrapartum periods should be recognized as critical targets for glucose optimization, as achieving and maintaining glucose within a defined intrapartum range and reducing TAR may be associated with fewer adverse neonatal outcomes.

While there were not statistically significant differences noted between the demographics and pregnancy characteristics of each group, subjects with an adverse neonatal outcome tended to have a higher maternal age, rates of HDP, unscheduled cesarean delivery, and higher insulin doses at delivery. In addition, birthweight and gestational age at delivery were slightly lower and IAI rates were higher in the adverse outcome group. Considering this, it is possible that intrapartum glucose may reflect the severity of maternal metabolic and obstetric conditions rather than independently driving neonatal outcomes. These variables may be both drivers and modifiers of the higher neonatal adverse outcome rate observed, but our sample size was too small to adjust for these potential factors. Residual confounding may be contributing to the associations observed between intrapartum glucose metrics and neonatal outcomes in our analysis, independent of intrapartum glucose, and future prospective studies are warranted to address this. Regardless, these characteristics may be considered to identify patients who could benefit most from intrapartum glycemic optimization to mitigate compounded risk.

Our study has several strengths. It is observational but nested within a RCT, which lends methodological rigor to data collection. In addition, intrapartum CGM data are granular and enable refined metrics such as TIR, TAR, mean glucose, and glucose variability beyond single time‐point values. We used a composite neonatal outcome which reflects clinically meaningful endpoints frequently encountered in practice and investigated a wide range of maternal and neonatal pregnancy characteristics. Having antepartum CGM data is a benefit to understand the potential influence of antepartum glucose achievement and intrapartum data on outcomes. Prior studies have primarily focused on CBG data that are different from CGM data, and more studies are needed to determine CGM metrics for the intrapartum period. We examined the relationship of glucose management from the antepartum period of enrollment to labor to intrapartum glucose. Regarding limitations, our population represents a single institution with a distinct protocol for intrapartum glycemic management, which may limit generalizability to similar populations. Additionally, the analytic sample is relatively small which limits the ability to detect modest effects. Given the limited sample size and absence of multivariable adjustment, residual confounding by age, parity, hypertensive disorders, IAI, and delivery factors remains a plausible explanation for our observed associations and should be considered in future larger studies.

CONCLUSION

In this secondary analysis, we found that patients with GDM and a composite adverse neonatal outcome spent less TIR and more TAR in labor by CGM metrics compared to those without. Antepartum glucose achievement was weakly to moderately positively correlated with intrapartum glucose achievement, but some participants with optimal antepartum glucose management experienced an adverse neonatal outcome, emphasizing the continued importance of both antepartum and intrapartum glucose management. While our analysis suggests an association between suboptimal intrapartum glucose management and adverse neonatal outcomes, a higher‐risk obstetric phenotype was noted among those in the adverse neonatal outcome group and small sample size limited our ability to perform multivariable adjustment. Thus, residual confounding remains a plausible explanation for the observed associations between intrapartum glucose and adverse neonatal outcomes. Clarifying these observed associations should be a focus of future investigation.

As CGM use becomes more prevalent among patients with GDM, this study represents a meaningful addition to the literature in this population. Obstetric clinicians will be tasked with more frequent interpretation of CGM data to guide patient care, and this includes the Labor and Delivery setting. We utilized meaningful pregnancy targets in our analysis and noted a statistically significant difference in our primary intrapartum range of interest, %TIR between 70 and 120 mg/dL, between those with and without adverse outcomes. This may suggest that optimizing %TIR 70–120 mg/dL is a reasonable threshold to further investigate for risk mitigation in future studies. ACOG presently suggests maintaining glucose <110 mg/dL in labor; however, as previously mentioned, a recent RCT questioned the benefit of such tight intrapartum control for those specifically with GDM [14, 15]. Greater clarification of this target utilizing CGM metrics is a logical next step. Until then, patients with GDM may benefit from prioritizing euglycemia intrapartum just as euglycemia is prioritized antepartum, given the observed association between suboptimal glycemic control prior to delivery and adverse neonatal outcomes.
