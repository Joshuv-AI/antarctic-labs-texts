Optimality of meta-converse for channel simulation
Aadil Oufkir, Omar Fawzi, Mario Berta

Abstract
We study the effect of shared non-signaling correlations for the problem of simulating a channel using noiseless communication in the one-shot setting. For classical channels, we show how to round any non-signaling-assisted simulation strategy — which corresponds to the natural linear programming meta-converse for channel simulation — to a strategy that only uses shared randomness. For quantum channels, we round any non-signaling-assisted simulation strategy to a strategy that only uses shared entanglement. Our main result is for classical and classical-quantum channels, for which we employ ideas from approximation algorithms to give a guarantee on the ratio of success probabilities of at least ( 1 − e − 1 ) (1-\mathrm{e}^{-1}) . We further show this ratio to be optimal for the purely classical case. It can be improved to ( 1 − t − 1 ) (1-t^{-1}) using O ⁡ ( ln ⁡ ln ⁡ ( t ) ) O(\ln\ln(t)) additional bits of communication.

Notation

For a positive integer

We use standard quantum information theory notation.
Hilbert spaces are denoted
A,B,...

A

denote the set of linear maps from
A

\rho\in\mathrm{L}(A):\;\rho\succcurlyeq 0\;\text{and}\;\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho\right]=1,

where

\rho
is positive semidefinite. The set of quantum states on
A
is denoted by
\mathrm{D}(A)
.
For an integer
N\geq 2
, we denote the
N

A_{1}A_{2}\cdots A_{N}=A_{1}\otimes A_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes A_{N}
.

The partial trace
\mathrm{Tr}_{B}\left[.\right]
is a quantum channel from
AB
to
A

\forall\rho\in\mathrm{L}(A)\otimes\mathrm{L}(B):\mathrm{Tr}_{B}\left[\rho\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{|B|}\left(\mathbb{I}_{A}\otimes\langle i|_{B}\right)\,\rho\,\left(\mathbb{I}_{A}\otimes|i\rangle_{B}\right).

Quantum channels are linear maps
\mathcal{N}:\mathrm{L}(A)\rightarrow\mathrm{L}(B)
that can be written in the form

\forall\rho\in\mathrm{L}(A):\mathcal{N}(\rho)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}K_{x}\rho K_{x}^{\dagger}

where the Kraus operators

are linear maps from
A
to
B
and satisfy
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}K_{x}^{\dagger}K_{x}=\mathbb{I}_{A}
.

For a quantum channel
\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow B}
, we define the Choi matrix

J_{\mathcal{N}}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{|A|}|i\rangle\langle j|_{A^{\prime}}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow B}(|i\rangle\langle j|_{A})=(\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow B})(|w\rangle\!\langle w|_{A^{\prime}A})\quad\text{where}\quad|w\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{|A|}|i\rangle_{A^{\prime}}\otimes|i\rangle_{A}.

A quantum channel is completely positive and trace preserving:

•

completely positive:
for all

,

•

trace preserving:

\mathrm{Tr}_{B}\left[J_{\mathcal{N}}\right]=\mathbb{I}_{A^{\prime}}
.

A quantum channel
\mathcal{N}
with classical input system is called a classical-quantum (CQ) channel, and is described by a set of quantum states
\{\mathcal{N}(x)\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\in\left(\mathrm{D}(B)\right)^{\mathcal{X}}
. A measurement is described by a POVM (positive operator-valued measure)
\{\Lambda_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\in\left(\mathrm{L}(A)\right)^{\mathcal{X}}

. After performing the measurement on a quantum state
\rho\in\mathrm{D}(A)

.

The one norm of an operator

\|\xi\|_{1}=\mathrm{Tr}\left[|\xi|\right]

The trace norm between two quantum states

The diamond distance between two quantum channels

\|\mathcal{N}-\mathcal{M}\|_{\diamond}=\sup_{\sigma\in\mathrm{D}(RA)}\|(\mathrm{id}_{R}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow B})(\sigma_{RA})-(\mathrm{id}_{R}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{A\rightarrow B})(\sigma_{RA})\|_{\operatorname{Tr}}.

The diamond distance between two CQ channels

\|\mathcal{N}-\mathcal{M}\|_{\diamond}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|\mathcal{N}(x)-\mathcal{M}(x)\|_{\operatorname{Tr}}.

The fidelity between two quantum states

\rho\in\mathrm{D}(A)

\sigma\in\mathrm{D}(A)

F(\rho,\sigma)=\|\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\sigma}\|_{1}^{2}.

The purified distance between two quantum states

\sigma\in\mathrm{D}(A)

P(\rho,\sigma)=\sqrt{1-F(\rho,\sigma)}.

The purified distance between two quantum channels

The relative entropy between two quantum states

and

D(\rho,\sigma)=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho(\log(\rho)-\log(\sigma))\right].

Finally,
A\succcurlyeq B
stands for
A-B

2
Simulation of classical channels

2.1
Shared-randomness assistance

Given a classical channel
W_{Y|X}
of input alphabet
\mathcal{X}
and output alphabet
\mathcal{Y}
and an integer
M
, our goal is to simulate the channel
W
using a classical communication of at most
M
distinct messages and with an error probability as small as possible. More formally, we can describe a size
M

\varepsilon
-simulation code for the channel
W_{Y|X}
by a triple
(\{\mathcal{E}_{s}\}_{s\in\mathcal{S}},\{\mathcal{D}_{s}\}_{s\in\mathcal{S}},\{p_{S}(s)\}_{s\in\mathcal{S}})
such that the synthesized channel

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{Y|X}(y|x)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}p_{S}(s)\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{E}_{s}(i|x)\mathcal{D}_{s}(y|i)

is
\varepsilon
close to the actual channel
W_{Y|X}
in the worst case total variation distance. Here, it is allowed to use a shared random variable
S
on an arbitrary discrete set
\mathcal{S}
. This choice is motivated by the fact that strategies assisted by shared-randomness (SR) are proven to achieve the optimal channel simulation capacity
[
5
]
, whereas strategies without assistance require a simulation rate strictly greater than the capacity
[
2
,
8
]
. In the following, we describe a possible approximation algorithm given by the meta-converse.

2.2
Non-signaling assistance in the classical setting

SR strategies are special case of the so called non-signaling (NS) strategies. In this latter, the joint encoder-decoder map
N_{IY|XJ}
satisfies

\displaystyle N_{IY|XJ}(i,y|x,j)

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall i,y,x,j,

\displaystyle\sum_{i,y}N_{IY|XJ}(i,y|x,j)

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\forall x,j,

\displaystyle\sum_{i}N_{IY|XJ}(i,y|x,j)

\displaystyle=N_{Y|J}(y|j)

\displaystyle\forall x,

\displaystyle\sum_{y}N_{IY|XJ}(i,y|x,j)

\displaystyle=N_{I|X}(i|x)

\displaystyle\forall j.

We denote the set of non-signaling maps by
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{S}(IY|XJ)
. Non-signaling strategies prove to be useful for simplifying the computation of the maximal success probability which is obtained by solving the following program

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{NS}}(W,M)=\max_{N}

\displaystyle\;1-\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|\widetilde{W}(\cdot|x)-W(\cdot|x)\right\|_{\TV}

\displaystyle\rm{s.t.}\;\;

\displaystyle N\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{S}(IY|XJ),

(1)

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}(y|x)=\hskip-3.00003pt\sum_{i=1}^{M}N(i,y|x,i).

So, when we relax the constraints of the encoder-decoder to be non-signaling, we obtain a linear program. After a symmetry-based reduction, the program (
1
) becomes
[
21
,
5
]
(see Lemma
A.1
for a proof)

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{NS}}(W,M)=\max_{\widetilde{W},\,\zeta}\;\;

\displaystyle 1-\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|\widetilde{W}(\cdot|x)-W(\cdot|x)\right\|_{\TV}

\displaystyle\rm{s.t.}\;\;

\displaystyle\sum_{y}\widetilde{W}(y|x)=1\quad\forall x,

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}(y|x)\geq 0\quad\quad\;\;\,\forall x,y,

(2)

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}(y|x)\leq\zeta(y)\quad\;\,\forall x,y,

\displaystyle\sum_{y}\zeta(y)=M.

Observe that a strategy assisted by shared-randomness
N(iy|xj)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}p_{S}(s)\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{E}_{s}(i|x)\mathcal{D}_{s}(y|j)
is non-signaling, so the non-signaling (NS) simulation success probability is greater than the shared-randomness (SR) simulation success probability

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{NS}}(W,M)\geq\suc^{\rm{SR}}(W,M).

These values might differ in general, even when comparing them with entanglement-assisted strategies (see Appendix
B
). Since non-signaling strategies are computationally accessible and shared-randomness strategies are of interest, a natural question arises:

How large is the gap between the success probabilities

of shared-randomness and non-signaling strategies?

2.3
Rounding

In the following, we round the solution of the non-signaling program (
2
) to a strategy that requires only shared-randomness. This allows us to prove a meta inequality between the channel using non-signaling (NS) resources and the constructed channel using only shared randomness (SR).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
M,M^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\widetilde{W}^{\rm{NS}}
be a feasible solution of the program (
2
) of size
M
. Then, there exists a shared-randomness assisted strategy
\widetilde{W}^{\rm{SR}}
of size
M^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}^{\rm{SR}}(y|x)\geq\left[1-\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{M^{\prime}}\right]\widetilde{W}^{\rm{NS}}(y|x)\quad\forall x,y.

To construct the strategy
\widetilde{W}^{\rm{SR}}
with shared-randomness, we use a standard tool from statistics
[
22
,
28
]
that has been applied previously to channel simulation
[
5
,
29
,
25
]
namely the
rejection sampling technique

[
30
,
23
]
. However, the one shot bound we obtain in this work (Proposition
2.1
) is conceptually different and highly flexible.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

Let
(\widetilde{W}^{\rm{NS}},\zeta)
be a feasible solution of the program (
2
).

Shared randomness

Since
\sum_{y}\zeta(y)=M
and
\zeta(y)\geq 0
for all
y
,
\{\frac{\zeta(y)}{M}\}_{y}
is a probability distribution. Let
\mathbf{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{M^{\prime}}}
be i.i.d. samples from
p_{\rm{initial}}=\{\frac{\zeta(y)}{M}\}_{y}
, which acts as the shared-randomness assistance of the protocol
\widetilde{W}^{\rm{SR}}(y|x)
to be constructed.

Encoding

For an input
x
, we run the rejection sampling algorithm
[
5
]
with
M^{\prime}
steps for
p_{\rm{initial}}=\{\frac{\zeta(y)}{M}\}_{y}
and
p_{\rm{target}}=\widetilde{W}^{\rm{NS}}(\cdot|x)
and obtain
\mathbf{\widetilde{Y}}=\mathbf{Y_{i}}
where
\mathbf{i}
is the first index (or
M^{\prime}
if it does not exist) such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{U_{i}}\leq\frac{1}{M}\cdot\frac{p_{\rm{target}}(\mathbf{Y_{i}})}{p_{\rm{initial}}(\mathbf{Y_{i}})}=\frac{\widetilde{W}^{\rm{NS}}(\mathbf{Y_{i}}|x)}{\zeta(\mathbf{Y_{i}})},

where
\mathbf{U_{i}}\sim\unif([0,1])
, then encode
\mathcal{E}_{s}(m|x)=\delta_{m=\mathbf{i}}
.

Decoding

Decode as
\mathcal{D}_{s}(y|m)=\delta_{y=\mathbf{Y_{m}}}
. For finite
M^{\prime}
, we follow
[
5
]
and show, with a simple calculation, that the distribution of
\mathbf{\widetilde{Y}}
satisfies for
y\in\mathcal{Y}
that

\displaystyle p_{\mathbf{\widetilde{Y}}}(y)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{M^{\prime}-1}\left(p_{\rm{initial}}(y)-\frac{1}{M}\cdot p_{\rm{target}}(y)\right)+\left[1-\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{M^{\prime}}\right]p_{\rm{target}}(y).

Indeed, let us denote
\mathbf{E}
the binary random variable such that
\mathbf{E}=1
if and only if there is an
\mathbf{i}\leq M^{\prime}
such that
\mathbf{U_{i}}\leq\frac{1}{M}\cdot\frac{p_{\rm{target}}(\mathbf{Y_{i}})}{p_{\rm{initial}}(\mathbf{Y_{i}})}
. We have by letting
\lambda=\frac{1}{M}
that

\displaystyle p_{\mathbf{\widetilde{Y}E}}(y,0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y_{1},\dots,y_{M^{\prime}-1}}\prod_{i=1}^{M^{\prime}-1}p_{\rm{initial}}(y_{i})\left(1-\lambda\cdot\frac{p_{\rm{target}}(y_{i})}{p_{\rm{initial}}(y_{i})}\right)\cdot p_{\rm{initial}}(y)\left(1-\lambda\cdot\frac{p_{\rm{target}}(y)}{p_{\rm{initial}}(y)}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{M^{\prime}-1}\sum_{y_{i}}\left(p_{\rm{initial}}(y_{i})-\lambda\cdot p_{\rm{target}}(y_{i})\right)\cdot\left(p_{\rm{initial}}(y)-\lambda\cdot p_{\rm{target}}(y)\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)^{M^{\prime}-1}\left(p_{\rm{initial}}(y)-\lambda\cdot p_{\rm{target}}(y)\right).

Similarly, we get

\displaystyle p_{\mathbf{\widetilde{Y}E}}(y,1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{M^{\prime}}\sum_{y_{1},\dots,y_{j-1}}\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}p_{\rm{initial}}(y_{i})\left(1-\lambda\cdot\frac{p_{\rm{target}}(y_{i})}{p_{\rm{initial}}(y_{i})}\right)\cdot p_{\rm{initial}}(y)\left(\lambda\cdot\frac{p_{\rm{target}}(y)}{p_{\rm{initial}}(y)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{M^{\prime}}(1-\lambda)^{j-1}\cdot\lambda\cdot p_{\rm{target}}(y)

\displaystyle=\left(1-(1-\lambda)^{M^{\prime}}\right)p_{\rm{target}}(y).

Hence, we find

\displaystyle p_{\mathbf{\widetilde{Y}}}(y)

\displaystyle=p_{\mathbf{\widetilde{Y}E}}(y,0)+p_{\mathbf{\widetilde{Y}E}}(y,1)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{M^{\prime}-1}\left(p_{\rm{initial}}(y)-\frac{1}{M}\cdot p_{\rm{target}}(y)\right)+\left[1-\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{M^{\prime}}\right]p_{\rm{target}}(y).

Moreover, the second constraint of the program (
2
) reads

\displaystyle\forall y:\;p_{\rm{initial}}(y)=\frac{\zeta(y)}{M}\geq\frac{1}{M}\widetilde{W}^{\rm{NS}}(y|x)=\frac{1}{M}\cdot p_{\rm{target}}(y).

Therefore, we get

\displaystyle p_{\mathbf{\widetilde{Y}}}(y)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{M^{\prime}-1}\left(p_{\rm{initial}}(y)-\frac{1}{M}\cdot p_{\rm{target}}(y)\right)+\left[1-\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{M^{\prime}}\right]p_{\rm{target}}(y)

\displaystyle\geq\left[1-\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{M^{\prime}}\right]p_{\rm{target}}(y).

By observing that
p_{\mathbf{\widetilde{Y}}}(y)=\widetilde{W}^{\rm{SR}}(y|x)
, where
\widetilde{W}^{\rm{SR}}(y|x)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}p_{S}(s)\sum_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}\mathcal{E}_{s}(m|x)\mathcal{D}_{s}(y|m)
, we deduce the required inequality.
∎

As a direct corollary of Proposition
2.1
, we can control the gap between the success probabilities of strategies assisted by shared-randomness and non-signaling assistance. Although we focus on the worst-case total variation distance, it is noteworthy that Proposition
2.1
also permits to control the gap between the success probabilities of SR and NS strategies under average-case total variation, as well as under average and worst-case Bhattacharyya distance.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
M,M^{\prime}\geq 1
, and
W
be a channel. Then, we have

\displaystyle 1\geq\frac{\suc^{\rm{SR}}(W,M^{\prime})}{\suc^{\rm{NS}}(W,M)}\geq\left[1-\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{M^{\prime}}\right].

Proof of Corollary
2.2
.

Observe that for two probability distributions
p
and
q
,
1-\|p-q\|_{\TV}=\sum_{i}\min(p_{i},q_{i})
. Let
x\in\mathcal{X}
, by Proposition
2.1
we have

\displaystyle 1-\left\|\widetilde{W}^{\rm{SR}}(\cdot|x)-W(\cdot|x)\right\|_{\TV}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}\min\left(\widetilde{W}^{\rm{SR}}(y|x),\;W(y|x)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{y}\left[1-\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{M^{\prime}}\right]\min\left(\widetilde{W}^{\rm{NS}}(y|x),\;W(y|x)\right)

\displaystyle=\left[1-\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{M^{\prime}}\right]\left(1-\left\|\widetilde{W}^{\rm{NS}}(\cdot|x)-W(\cdot|x)\right\|_{\TV}\right),

where we used
\left(1-(1-1/M)^{M^{\prime}}\right)\leq 1
. Choosing the optimal feasible solution
\widetilde{W}^{\rm{NS}}
of the program (
2
) and taking the minimum on

on both sides give the desired inequality.
∎

In particular, when

\suc^{\rm{SR}}(W,M)

is at most
1-\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}}
. Furthermore, by choosing
M^{\prime}=\ln(t)M
, we can show that the gap is at most
1-\frac{1}{t}
and thus approaches
1
as
t\rightarrow\infty
. This implies the fact that the non-signaling assistance does not help to reduce the asymptotic simulation capacity of a classical channel
[
8
,
5
]
. In words, the simulation capacity of a classical channel is the asymptotic minimum rate of communication required to simulate this channel with high success probability in the presence of shared randomness (SR), shared entanglement (EA), or non-signaling correlations (NS).

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
W
be a channel. Then, we have

\displaystyle C^{\rm{SR}}(W)=C^{\rm{EA}}(W)=C^{\rm{NS}}(W).

Proof.

Since NS contains EA and EA contains SR, we have that
C^{\rm{SR}}(W)\geq C^{\rm{EA}}(W)\geq C^{\rm{NS}}(W)
so it suffices to show that
C^{\rm{SR}}(W)=C^{\rm{NS}}(W)
.

From Corollary
2.2
we have for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
M=2^{t}
and
M^{\prime}=\ln(t)2^{t}
that

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{SR}}(W^{\otimes n},\ln(t)2^{t})

\displaystyle\geq\left[1-\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{t}}\right)^{\ln(t)2^{t}}\right]\suc^{\rm{NS}}(W^{\otimes n},2^{t})

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{t}\right)\suc^{\rm{NS}}(W^{\otimes n},2^{t}).

Suppose by contradiction that there is
R
such that

\displaystyle C^{\rm{NS}}(W)<R<C^{\rm{SR}}(W).

So, by taking
t=Rn
we have

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{NS}}(W^{\otimes n},2^{Rn})\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}1

and as
1-\frac{1}{t}=1-\frac{1}{Rn}\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}1
we deduce that

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{SR}}(W^{\otimes n},\ln(Rn)2^{Rn})\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}1,

hence we find that
R=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log(\ln(Rn)2^{Rn})\geq C^{\rm{SR}}(W)
, which contradicts the assumption
R<C^{\rm{SR}}(W)
.
∎

2.4
Tightness

Finally, we show that the bounds of Corollary
2.2
are in fact tight.

Lemma 2.4
.

For integers
n\geq k
and
M=\left\lceil\frac{n}{k}\right\rceil
, consider the channel

\displaystyle W:\binom{n}{k}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\{1,2,\dots,n\},\;W(y|x)=\frac{1}{k}\;\mathbf{1}\{y\in x\}.

Then, we have that

\suc^{\rm{NS}}(W,M)=1
, while
\suc^{\rm{SR}}(W,M^{\prime})\leq 1-\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\binom{n-M^{\prime}}{k}
.

This shows that the ratio in Corollary
2.2
cannot be improved in general. In fact, let
M^{\prime}=k
and
M=\gamma k
for some constant
\gamma
. Then, we immediately have

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{kM-M^{\prime}}{k}}{\binom{kM}{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(kM-M^{\prime})\cdots(kM-M^{\prime}-k+1)}{(kM)\cdots(kM-k+1)}

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{k}{\gamma k^{2}-k+1}\right)^{k}\underset{k\to\infty}{\sim}\left(1-\frac{1}{\gamma k}\right)^{k}\underset{k\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{e}^{-1/\gamma}.

In particular, for the channel
[
17
]

\displaystyle W:\binom{M^{2}}{M}\rightarrow\{1,2,\dots,M^{2}\},\;W(y|x)=\frac{1}{M}\;\mathbf{1}\{y\in x\}

(3)

we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\suc^{\rm{SR}}(W,M)}{\suc^{\rm{NS}}(W,M)}\underset{M\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}1-\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}}.

Proof of Lemma
2.4
.

For the first equality, we can choose
\zeta(y)=\frac{1}{k}
for all
y\in[n]
, hence
\widetilde{W}=W
satisfies the constraints of the program (
2
). For the second inequality, the proof is similar to an argument in
[
17
]
. We use
1-\|p-q\|_{\TV}=\sum_{i}\min(p_{i},q_{i})
to upper bound the success probability as

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{SR}}(W,M^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\inf_{x}\sum_{y}\min\left(\widetilde{W}^{\rm{SR}}(y|x),\frac{1}{k}\;\mathbf{1}\{y\in x\}\right)

\displaystyle=\inf_{x}\sum_{y\in x}\min\left(\widetilde{W}^{\rm{SR}}(y|x),\frac{1}{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{x}\sum_{y\in x}\widetilde{W}^{\rm{SR}}(y|x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{x\in\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{y\in x}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}p_{S}(s)\sum_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}\mathcal{E}_{s}(m|x)\mathcal{D}_{s}(y|m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}p_{S}(s)\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{x\in\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{y\in x}\sum_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}\mathcal{E}_{s}(m|x)\mathcal{D}_{s}(y|m).

We may suppose without loss of generality that
\mathcal{D}_{s}
is deterministic (any stochastic map can be written as a convex combination of deterministic maps
[
31
]
:
\mathcal{D}_{s}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{s}}p_{s}(i)\mathcal{D}_{i,s}
where
\mathcal{D}_{i,s}
is deterministic, then we can consider the shared randomness
\{p_{S}(s)p_{s}(i)\}_{s\in\mathcal{S},i\in\mathcal{S}_{s}}
). So, for a fixed
s\in\mathcal{S}
, the set
\mathcal{Y}_{s}=\{y:\mathcal{D}_{s}(y|m)\neq 0\text{ for some }m\}
is of size at most
M^{\prime}
. Hence, we find

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{SR}}(W,M^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}p_{S}(s)\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{x\in\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{y\in x}\sum_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}\mathcal{E}_{s}(m|x)\mathcal{D}_{s}(y|m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}p_{S}(s)\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{x\in\binom{n}{k}:x\cap\mathcal{Y}_{s}=\emptyset}\sum_{y\in x}\sum_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}\mathcal{E}_{s}(m|x)\cdot 0

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}p_{S}(s)\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{x\in\binom{n}{k}:x\cap\mathcal{Y}_{s}\neq\emptyset}\sum_{y\in x}\sum_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}\mathcal{E}_{s}(m|x)\mathcal{D}_{s}(y|m)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}p_{S}(s)\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{x\in\binom{n}{k}:x\cap\mathcal{Y}_{s}\neq\emptyset}1

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}p_{S}(s)\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\left(\binom{n}{k}-\binom{n-M^{\prime}}{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\left(\binom{n}{k}-\binom{n-M^{\prime}}{k}\right).

So, the gap between the NS and SR success probabilities can be as large as

\displaystyle\frac{\suc^{\rm{SR}}(W,M^{\prime})}{\suc^{\rm{NS}}(W,M)}\leq 1-\frac{\binom{n-M^{\prime}}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}

for all integers
n,k
such that
\left\lceil\frac{n}{k}\right\rceil=M
.
∎

These simulation results are analogous to the known rounding results for channel coding
[
10
]
. However, while even the rounding factors are similar (although slightly stronger here!), we emphasize that the techniques needed are fundamentally different. In fact, the simulation meta-converse is related to the smoothed max-divergence
[

]
, while the coding PPV meta-converse can be phrased in terms of the hypothesis testing divergence
[

3
Non signaling assistance in the quantum setting

In this section we describe the non-signaling assistance of Section
2.2
in the general quantum setting.
Non-signaling assistance represents shared correlations that do not permit communication without external help. Let
\mathcal{W}_{A_{i}\rightarrow B_{o}}
be the quantum channel to be simulated and let
A_{o},B_{i}
be quantum systems of dimension
M
where
M
is the communication size. More precisely, a non signaling super map
\Pi
is a super channel of input systems
A_{i},B_{i}
and output systems
A_{o},B_{o}
that maps a channel
\mathcal{W}_{A_{o}\rightarrow B_{i}}
to a channel
(\Pi\circ\mathcal{W})_{A_{i}\rightarrow B_{o}}
. The Choi matrix of a valid non-signaling super channel
\Pi
satisfies
[
21
]
:

\displaystyle J_{\Pi}\succcurlyeq 0,\quad\mathrm{Tr}_{A_{o}B_{o}}\left[J_{\Pi}\right]=\mathbb{I}_{A_{i}B_{i}}

\displaystyle\text{(CP), (TP)},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{A_{o}}\left[J_{\Pi}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{I}_{A_{i}}}{|A_{i}|}\otimes\mathrm{Tr}_{A_{o}A_{i}}\left[J_{\Pi}\right]

\displaystyle(A\nrightarrow B),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{B_{o}}\left[J_{\Pi}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{I}_{B_{i}}}{|B_{i}|}\otimes\mathrm{Tr}_{B_{i}B_{o}}\left[J_{\Pi}\right]

\displaystyle(B\nrightarrow A).

We denote the set of non-signaling superchannels by
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{S}(A_{o}B_{o}|A_{i}B_{i})
.
The importance of non-signaling assistance lies in its ability to simplify the computation of the maximum success probability, which can be obtained by solving the following program:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{W},M)=\max_{\Pi}&\;1-\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{A_{i}\rightarrow B_{o}}-\mathcal{W}_{A_{i}\rightarrow B_{o}}\right\|_{\diamond}\\
\rm{s.t.}\;\;&\Pi\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{S}(A_{o}B_{o}|A_{i}B_{i}),\\
&\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{A_{i}\rightarrow B_{o}}=\Pi\circ\mathrm{id}_{A_{o}\rightarrow B_{i}}.\end{aligned}

(4)

So, for simulating quantum channels with non-signaling strategies, the success probability can be expressed as an SDP program. Using
[
21
, Corollary
2
]
, this program can be further simplified to:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{W},M)=\max_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{A\rightarrow B},V_{B}}&1-\left\|\mathcal{W}_{A\rightarrow B}-\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{A\rightarrow B}\right\|_{\diamond}\\
\rm{s.t.}\quad&\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{A\rightarrow B}\quad\text{quantum channel,}\\
&J_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}_{A^{\prime}}\otimes V_{B},\quad\\
&\mathrm{Tr}\left[V_{B}\right]=M^{2}.\end{aligned}

(5)

Notably, when only classical instead of quantum communication is allowed, with the classical identity channel

\displaystyle\mathrm{id}_{c}(\rho)=\mathrm{id}^{c}_{A_{o}\rightarrow B_{i}}(\rho)=\sum_{x=1}^{N}\langle x|\rho|x\rangle|x\rangle\!\langle x|,

the non-signaling program is similar to (
5
) except that
M^{2}
is replaced by
N
(see Lemma
A.2
for a proof). Since EA strategies are non-signaling, the success probability of NS strategies is at least as its EA counterparts

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{NS}}({\mathcal{W}},M)\geq\suc^{\rm{EA}}({\mathcal{W}},M).

In the following we study the gap between these success probabilties in the case of simulating classical-quantum and quantum channels.

4
Simulation of classical-quantum channels

We are able to generalize the classical results of Section
2
to the classical-quantum setting. In this section, we provide rounding protocols to the simulation of classical-quantum channels with classical communication
and with entanglement-assisted strategies.

4.1
Entanglement-assistance

Classical-quantum (CQ) channels can be seen as special cases of quantum channels where the input consists of commuting states. Since the output is quantum, it is natural to consider entanglement assisted strategies. The difference between SR and EA for CQ channel simulation remains open, even when considering asymptotic capacities. So, we focus on EA and define the task of simulating a CQ channel
\mathcal{W}_{X\rightarrow B}
, with classical input system
X
and quantum output system
B
, as follows.

A size-
M
EA
\varepsilon
-simulation code for
\mathcal{W}_{X\rightarrow B}
is a triple
(\{E^{m}_{x}\}_{m\in[M]},\{\mathcal{N}^{m}_{K\rightarrow B}\}_{m\in[M]},\sigma_{K^{\prime}K})
consisting of a measurement POVM, a family of quantum decoding channels, and a shared entanglement state such that the channel:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{X\rightarrow B}:x\mapsto\sum_{m\in[M]}\mathcal{N}^{m}_{K\rightarrow B}\left(\mathrm{Tr}_{K^{\prime}}\left[E^{m}_{x}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{K}\cdot{\sigma}_{K^{\prime}K}\right]\right)

(6)

approximates to desired channel
\mathcal{W}_{X\rightarrow B}
in the diamond distance, i.e. it satisfies:

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{X\rightarrow B}(x)-\mathcal{W}_{X\rightarrow B}(x)\right\|_{\operatorname{Tr}}\leq\varepsilon.

By considering a probability distribution
p_{X}
as classical state
\rho_{X}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p_{X}(x)|x\rangle\!\langle x|
, we can see that the task of simulating CQ channels is the same as the task of simulating quantum channels with classical input and classical communication. Hence the non-signaling program is an SDP and follows from Lemma
A.2
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{W},M)=\max_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{X\rightarrow B},V_{B}}&1-\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|\mathcal{W}_{X\rightarrow B}(x)-\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{X\rightarrow B}(x)\right\|_{\operatorname{Tr}}\\
\rm{s.t.}\quad&\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{X\rightarrow B}\quad\text{CQ channel,}\\
&\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{X\rightarrow B}(x)\preccurlyeq V_{B}\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{X},\quad\\
&\mathrm{Tr}\left[V_{B}\right]=M,\end{aligned}

(7)

where we used the equivalence

\displaystyle J_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}_{X^{\prime}}\otimes V_{B}\Leftrightarrow\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|x\rangle\!\langle x|_{X^{\prime}}\otimes\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{X\rightarrow B}(x)\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}_{X^{\prime}}\otimes V_{B}\Leftrightarrow\forall x\in\mathcal{X}:\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{X\rightarrow B}(x)\preccurlyeq V_{B}.

4.2
Rounding

Since entanglement assisted strategies are non-signaling, the non-signaling success probability is at least as its entanglement assisted counterpart:

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{W},M)\geq\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{W},M).

We can inquire about the extent to which these quantities can differ. Importantly, the answer to this question is similar to Corollary
2.2
although the context and the proof strategies are slightly different. We round a feasible solution of the program (

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied approximation algorithms for simulating classical, classical-quantum, and quantum channels. We provided rounding results relating the success probabilities of shared-randomness assistance and entanglement-assistance to non-signaling assisted strategies. In particular, we deduced that non-signaling assistance is not proving any further advantage for asymptotic i.i.d. simulation capacities. Yet, non-signaling assisted values are linear or semi-definite programs and are therefore computationally more tractable for approximating the optimal success probabilities. We further proved that our rounding results are tight in the classical case with respect to shared-randomness assistance, but had to leave open the tightness question when comparing entanglement-assistance with non-signaling assistance (cf. Appendix
B
). This relates to the notoriously difficult problem of bounding the power of entanglement in a more refined way (see, e.g.,
[
35
,
36
]
).

It would be interesting to study the impact of our one-shot result on more refined information-theoretic asymptotic i.i.d. limits, such as error exponents or strong converse exponents (which are largely unknown for the task of channel simulation). Further generalizations to network topologies might also be possible. Finally, investigating computational hardness results of achieving an improved approximation ratio of
(1-\mathrm{e}^{-1}+\varepsilon)
for some
\varepsilon>0
also remains an open question.

Lemma A.1
.

The non-signaling program of simulating a classical channel can be written as

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{NS}}=\max_{\widetilde{W},\zeta}

\displaystyle 1-\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|\widetilde{W}(\cdot|x)-W(\cdot|x)\right\|_{\TV}

\displaystyle\rm{s.t.}\;\;

\displaystyle\sum_{y}\widetilde{W}(y|x)=1\quad\forall x,

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}(y|x)\geq 0\quad\quad\;\;\,\forall x,y,

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}(y|x)\leq\zeta(y)\quad\;\,\forall x,y,

\displaystyle\sum_{y}\zeta(y)=M.

Proof.

Recall the non-signaling program of simulating a classical channel

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{NS}}=\max_{N_{IY|XJ}}

\displaystyle 1-\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|\widetilde{W}(\cdot|x)-W(\cdot|x)\right\|_{\TV}

\displaystyle\rm{s.t.}\;\;

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}(y|x)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}N_{IY|XJ}(i,y|x,i)

\displaystyle N_{IY|XJ}(i,y|x,j)\geq 0\quad\forall i,y,x,j,

\displaystyle\sum_{i,y}N_{IY|XJ}(i,y|x,j)=1\quad\forall x,j,

\displaystyle\sum_{i}N_{IY|XJ}(i,y|x,j)=N_{Y|J}(y|j)\quad\forall x,

\displaystyle\sum_{y}N_{IY|XJ}(i,y|x,j)=N_{I|X}(i|x)\quad\forall j.

Given a non-signaling super map
N_{IY|XJ}
, we define
\zeta
as

\displaystyle\forall y:\zeta(y)=\sum_{j}N_{Y|J}(y|j).

We have by using the non-signaling properties of
N_{IY|XJ}
that

\displaystyle\forall x,y:\zeta(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}N_{Y|J}(y|j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}N_{IY|XJ}(i,y|x,j)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i}N_{IY|XJ}(i,y|x,i)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{W}(y|x),

\displaystyle\sum_{y}\zeta(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}\sum_{i,j}N_{IY|XJ}(i,y|x,i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\sum_{i,y}N_{IY|XJ}(i,y|x,j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}1=M.

Conversely if
\zeta
satisfies

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}(y|x)

\displaystyle\leq\zeta(y)\quad\;\,\forall x,y,

\displaystyle\sum_{y}\zeta(y)

\displaystyle=M,

we define the non-signaling super map
N_{IY|XJ}
as

\displaystyle N_{IY|XJ}(i,y|x,j)=\begin{cases}\frac{\widetilde{W}(y|x)}{M}&\text{if}\;i=j,\\
\frac{\zeta(y)-\widetilde{W}(y|x)}{M(M-1)}&\text{if}\;i\neq j.\end{cases}

We check the non-signaling properties

\displaystyle N_{IY|XJ}(i,y|x,j)

\displaystyle\geq 0,

\displaystyle\sum_{i}N_{IY|XJ}(i,y|x,j)

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta(y)}{M},

\displaystyle\sum_{y}N_{IY|XJ}(i,y|x,j)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M},

\displaystyle\sum_{i,y}N_{IY|XJ}(i,y|x,j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{M}=1.

Hence, the two programs are equal.
∎

Similarly, we prove the symmetry based reduction of the non-signaling program of simulating a quantum channel with classical communication. Note that this program is similar to the one with quantum communication (
5
).

Lemma A.2
.

The non-signaling program of simulating a quantum channel with classical communication of size
N
can be written as

\displaystyle\suc_{C}^{\rm{NS}}=\max_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{A\rightarrow B},V_{B}}

\displaystyle 1-\left\|\mathcal{W}_{A\rightarrow B}-\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{A\rightarrow B}\right\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\rm{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{A\rightarrow B}\quad\text{quantum channel,}

(17)

\displaystyle J_{\widetilde{W}}\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}_{A^{\prime}}\otimes V_{B},\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[V_{B}\right]=N.

Proof.

The general form of the non-signaling program of simulating a quantum channel
\mathcal{W}
using a quantum channel
\mathcal{N}
is
[
21
]

\displaystyle\suc_{\mathcal{N}}^{\rm{NS}}=\max_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{A\rightarrow B}}

\displaystyle 1-\left\|\mathcal{W}_{A\rightarrow B}-\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{A\rightarrow B}\right\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\rm{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{A\rightarrow B}\quad\text{quantum channel,}

\displaystyle J_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}=\mathrm{Tr}_{A_{o}B_{i}}\left[(J_{\mathcal{N}}^{\top}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{A_{i}B_{o}})\cdot J_{\Pi}\right],

\displaystyle J_{\Pi}\geq 0,\quad\mathrm{Tr}_{A_{o}B_{o}}\left[J_{\Pi}\right]=\mathbb{I}_{A_{i}B_{i}},

(18)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{A_{o}}\left[J_{\Pi}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{I}_{A_{i}}}{|A_{i}|}\otimes\mathrm{Tr}_{A_{o}A_{i}}\left[J_{\Pi}\right],

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{B_{o}}\left[J_{\Pi}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{I}_{B_{i}}}{|B_{i}|}\otimes\mathrm{Tr}_{B_{i}B_{o}}\left[J_{\Pi}\right].

The setting of simulation using classical communication corresponds to
\mathcal{N}=\mathrm{id}_{c}(\rho)=\sum_{x=1}^{N}\langle x|\rho|x\rangle|x\rangle\!\langle x|
. Using the symmetry of
J_{\mathrm{id}_{c}}
, we can conjugate the Choi matrix
J_{\Pi}
with the unitaries for
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
:

\displaystyle P^{\sigma}_{A_{o}}\otimes P^{\sigma}_{B_{i}}=\sum_{x=1}^{N}|x\rangle\langle\sigma(x)|_{A_{o}}\otimes\sum_{x=1}^{N}|x\rangle\langle\sigma(x)|_{B_{i}}.

If
J_{\Pi}
is an optimal solution of the program (
18
) then for
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
, the Choi matrix
\left(P^{\sigma}_{A_{o}}\otimes P^{\sigma}_{B_{i}}\right)\cdot J_{\Pi}\cdot\left(P^{\sigma^{-1}}_{A_{o}}\otimes P^{\sigma^{-1}}_{B_{i}}\right)
satisfies the constraints of the program (
18
) hence
\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\left(P^{\sigma}_{A_{o}}\otimes P^{\sigma}_{B_{i}}\right)\cdot J_{\Pi}\cdot\left(P^{\sigma^{-1}}_{A_{o}}\otimes P^{\sigma^{-1}}_{B_{i}}\right)
is also an optimal solution.
Hence, we can write

\displaystyle J_{\Pi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}|x\rangle\langle x|_{A_{o}}\otimes|x\rangle\langle x|_{B_{i}}\otimes\rho^{0,0,0,0}_{A_{i}B_{o}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{x\neq y}|x\rangle\langle x|_{A_{o}}\otimes|y\rangle\langle y|_{B_{i}}\otimes\rho^{1,1,0,0}_{A_{i}B_{o}}

\displaystyle\quad+\qquad\cdots

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{x\neq y\neq z\neq t}|x\rangle\langle y|_{A_{o}}\otimes|z\rangle\langle t|_{B_{i}}\otimes\rho^{3,2,1,0}_{A_{i}B_{o}}.

Denote by
H_{A_{i}B_{0}}=\rho_{A_{i}B_{0}}^{0,0,0,0}
and
K_{A_{i}B_{0}}=\rho_{A_{i}B_{o}}^{1,1,0,0}
then we have

\displaystyle J_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\langle xx|\otimes\mathbb{I}_{A_{i}B_{o}}\;J_{\Pi}\;|xx\rangle\otimes\mathbb{I}_{A_{i}B_{o}}=N\rho_{A_{i}B_{0}}^{0,0,0,0}=NH_{A_{i}B_{0}}.

Moreover, the constraint
\mathrm{Tr}_{A_{o}}\left[J_{\Pi}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{I}_{A_{i}}}{|A_{i}|}\otimes\mathrm{Tr}_{A_{o}A_{i}}\left[J_{\Pi}\right]
implies

\displaystyle N\left(\rho^{0,0,0,0}_{A_{i}B_{o}}+(N-1)\rho^{1,1,0,0}_{A_{i}B_{o}}\right)=\frac{\mathbb{I}_{A_{i}}}{|A_{i}|}\otimes N\left(\rho^{0,0,0,0}_{B_{o}}+(N-1)\rho^{1,1,0,0}_{B_{o}}\right).

Denoting

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{|A_{i}|}\left(\rho^{0,0,0,0}_{B_{o}}+(N-1)\rho^{1,1,0,0}_{B_{o}}\right)=\frac{N}{|A_{i}|}\left(H_{B_{o}}+(N-1)K_{B_{o}}\right),

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{A_{i}}\otimes V

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}_{A_{i}}\otimes\frac{N}{|A_{i}|}\left(\rho^{0,0,0,0}_{B_{o}}+(N-1)\rho^{1,1,0,0}_{B_{o}}\right)=N\left(\rho^{0,0,0,0}_{A_{i}B_{o}}+(N-1)\rho^{1,1,0,0}_{A_{i}B_{o}}\right).

On the other hand, we have
J_{\Pi}\succcurlyeq 0
so
K\succcurlyeq 0
, and hence

\displaystyle J_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}

\displaystyle=NH_{A_{i}B_{0}}\preccurlyeq N(H_{A_{i}B_{0}}+(N-1)K_{A_{i}B_{0}}).

Since
H_{A_{i}B_{0}}=\rho^{0,0,0,0}_{A_{i}B_{o}}
and
K_{A_{i}B_{0}}=\rho^{1,1,0,0}_{A_{i}B_{o}}
it follows that

\displaystyle J_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq N\left(\rho^{0,0,0,0}_{A_{i}B_{0}}+(N-1)\rho^{1,1,0,0}_{A_{i}B_{0}}\right)=\mathbb{I}_{A_{i}}\otimes V.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle|A_{i}|\cdot|B_{i}|

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathbb{I}_{A_{i}B_{i}}\right]=\mathrm{Tr}\left[J_{\Pi}\right]=N\mathrm{Tr}\left[H\right]+N(N-1)\mathrm{Tr}\left[K\right]=|A_{i}|\cdot\mathrm{Tr}\left[V\right]

and consequently

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[V\right]=|B_{i}|=N.

Therefore, we find
\suc_{\mathrm{id}_{c}}^{\rm{NS}}\leq\rm{Opt}\;\eqref{ns-program-qq-cl}
.

Conversely, given
V_{B_{o}}
such that
J_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}_{A_{i}}\otimes V_{B_{o}}
and
\mathrm{Tr}\left[V_{B_{o}}\right]=N
, we can choose the Choi matrix of the non-signaling strategy as

\displaystyle J_{\Pi}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x}|x\rangle\!\langle x|_{A_{0}}\otimes|x\rangle\!\langle x|_{B_{i}}\otimes J_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{x\neq z}|x\rangle\!\langle x|_{A_{0}}\otimes|z\rangle\!\langle z|_{B_{i}}\otimes\left(\mathbb{I}_{A_{i}}\otimes V_{B_{o}}-J_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}\right).

Both
\Pi
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
satisfy all the constraints of the program (
18
) for
\mathcal{N}=\mathrm{id}_{c}
, hence
\rm{Opt}\;\eqref{ns-program-qq-cl}\leq\suc_{\mathrm{id}_{c}}^{\rm{NS}}
, and finally

\displaystyle\rm{Opt}\;\eqref{ns-program-qq-cl}=\suc_{\mathrm{id}_{c}}^{\rm{NS}}.

∎

Appendix B
One shot SR, EA and NS success probabilities

Here we follow
[
36
]
and show that the SR, EA and NS success probabilities are in general different (see the discussion at the end of Subsection
2.2
). Consider the channel
[
17
,
36
]
, which is a special type of the channels (
3
) we used to prove the tightness of our rounding results:

\displaystyle W:\binom{4}{2}\rightarrow\{1,2,3,4\},\;W(y|x)=\frac{1}{2}\;\mathbf{1}\{y\in x\}.

We have from Lemma
2.4

\suc^{\rm{NS}}(W,2)=1
and
\suc^{\rm{SR}}(W,2)\leq\frac{5}{6}
. We use the same techniques of
[
36
]
to show that

\suc^{\rm{EA}}(W,2)\gtrsim 0.908.

Define the shared quantum state

|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{4}|i\rangle\otimes|i\rangle.

Upon receiving the message
m\in\{1,2\}
, the decoder performs a measurement with the computational basis if
m=1
and otherwise in the orthonormal basis corresponding to
U
with

U=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&-1&1\\
1&0&1&1\\
-1&1&0&1\\
-1&-1&1&0\end{pmatrix}.

In other words, we set
D(y|1)=|y\rangle\!\langle y|
and
D(y|2)=U|y\rangle\!\langle y|U^{\dagger}
for
y\in\{1,2,3,4\}
. For an input
x\in\binom{4}{2}
, the encoder performs the measurement
\{E(m|x)\}_{m\in\{0,1\}}
on the first system of the shared state. The EA success probability satisfies

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{EA}}(W,2)

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{x\in\binom{4}{2}}\sum_{y}\min\left(\widetilde{W}^{\rm{EA}}(y|x),\frac{1}{2}\;\mathbf{1}\{y\in x\}\right)

\displaystyle=\inf_{x\in\binom{4}{2}}\sum_{y\in x}\min\left(\widetilde{W}^{\rm{EA}}(y|x),\frac{1}{2}\right).

By the definition and description of the entangled strategy above, we have that

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}^{\rm{EA}}(y|x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{2}\langle\psi|E(m|x)\otimes D(y|m)|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi|E(1|x)\otimes D(y|1)|\psi\rangle+\langle\psi|[\mathbb{I}-E(1|x)]\otimes D(y|2)|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi|E(1|x)\otimes(|y\rangle\!\langle y|-U|y\rangle\!\langle y|U^{\dagger})|\psi\rangle+\langle\psi|\mathbb{I}\otimes U|y\rangle\!\langle y|U^{\dagger}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{Tr}\left[E(1|x)^{\top}\cdot(|y\rangle\!\langle y|-U|y\rangle\!\langle y|U^{\dagger})\right]+\frac{1}{4}\leq\frac{1}{2}

using the fact that
0\preccurlyeq E(1|x)\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}
. Hence, we find

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{EA}}(W,2)

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{x\in\binom{4}{2}}\sum_{y\in x}\min\left(\widetilde{W}^{\rm{EA}}(y|x),\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\inf_{x\in\binom{4}{2}}\sum_{y\in x}\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{Tr}\left[E(1|x)^{\top}\cdot(|y\rangle\!\langle y|-U|y\rangle\!\langle y|U^{\dagger})\right]+\frac{1}{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\inf_{x\in\binom{4}{2}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[E(1|x)^{\top}\cdot\left(\sum_{y\in x}|y\rangle\!\langle y|-U\sum_{y\in x}|y\rangle\!\langle y|U^{\dagger}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\inf_{x\in\binom{4}{2}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\sum_{y\in x}|y\rangle\!\langle y|-U\sum_{y\in x}|y\rangle\!\langle y|U^{\dagger}\right\|_{1},

where we maximize over
\{E(1|x)\}_{x}
in the last step. Since we have that
\left\|\sum_{y\in x}|y\rangle\!\langle y|-U\sum_{y\in x}|y\rangle\!\langle y|U^{\dagger}\right\|_{1}\simeq 3.2660
for all
x\in\binom{4}{2}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{EA}}(W,2)

\displaystyle\gtrsim 0.908>\frac{5}{6}.

On the other hand, observe that we have for
\widetilde{W}^{\rm{EA}}(y|x)=\sum_{m=1}^{2}\langle\psi|E(m|x)\otimes D(y|m)|\psi\rangle
:

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{EA}}(W,2)

\displaystyle=\sup_{|\psi\rangle,E,D}\inf_{x\in\binom{4}{2}}\sum_{y\in x}\min\left(\widetilde{W}^{\rm{EA}}(y|x),\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{|\psi\rangle,E,D}\frac{1}{\binom{4}{2}}\sum_{x\in\binom{4}{2}}\sum_{y\in x}\widetilde{W}^{\rm{EA}}(y|x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{|\psi\rangle,E,D}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=1}^{2}\sum_{x\in\binom{4}{2}}\sum_{y\in[4]}\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{1}\{y\in x\}\langle\psi|E(m|x)\otimes D(y|m)|\psi\rangle.

The latter expression can be identified as the optimal entanglement assisted coding success probability for the channel
N(x|y)=\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{1}\{x\ni y\}
where the encoder is represented by
D
and the decoder is represented by
E
. In
[
36
]
, it is shown that this optimal success probability can be upper bounded by approximately
0.908
and thus

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{EA}}(W,2)

\displaystyle\lesssim 0.908.

Finally, we find that

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{SR}}(W,2)\lneqq\suc^{\rm{EA}}(W,2)\lneqq\suc^{\rm{NS}}(W,2).
