Implementing telemedicine for the care of women with gestational diabetes mellitus in a rural hospital: an observational retrospective study
Authors: Mauro Tancredi, Jan Chrusciel, Sarah Zaouia, Virginie Mergey, David Laplanche, Stéphane Sanchez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) can adversely impact pregnancy outcomes. LGA is a common complication of GDM. Telemedicine is increasingly used for the follow-up of chronic diseases. The objective of this study was to evaluate if implementing a telemedicine solution for GDM could decrease the frequency of large for gestational age (LGA) newborns in a rural hospital.MethodsThis retrospective interrupted-time-series study was conducted in a rural French hospital. An LGA newborn was defined as a newborn with weight ≥ 90th percentile. The intervention period was defined as starting 45 days after the initial introduction of the telemedicine solution. The two timeframes were: 1 January 2015 to 28 April 2017 (baseline period) and 12 June 2017 to 31 December 2021 (intervention period).ResultsBetween 2015 and 2021, 14,382 single births were registered in the hospital and 1,981 births from women with GDM were included. The mean age of mothers was 31.71 ± 5.54 and 32.30 ± 5.14 in women with newborns with birthweights lower and higher than the 90th percentile respectively (p=0.09). LGA births were reduced from 76/533 (14.3%) in the baseline period to 170/1,448 (11.7%) in the intervention period. This reduction became statistically significant in the multivariate analysis (protective OR: 0.541, 95%CI [0.311 to 0.930],p=0.13). Obesity was associated with LGA (OR: 1.877, 95%CI [1.394 to 2.558]).ConclusionsThe implementation of a telemedicine solution for GDM care in a rural general hospital was associated with a decrease in the adjusted odds of LGA births.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11726-1.Keywords: Gestational diabetes mellitus, Obesity, Telemedicine, Large for gestational age, Telehealth

Introduction

Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) is a form of glucose intolerance initially diagnosed during pregnancy that leads to hyperglycemia of variable severity [1]. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), GDM affects 7.8% of pregnancies in Europe [2]. A meta-analysis published in 2021 found a prevalence of 10.7% in Western Europe, with a high heterogeneity between study results, possibly due to geographic differences in incidence [3]. In France, GDM is a rising public health concern. According to the latest nationwide survey, the incidence of GDM was 16.4% in 2021 [4] compared to 10.8% in 2017 [5].

The condition is characterized by relative insulin deficiency particularly in the third trimester of pregnancy. Although developing some degree of insulin resistance during the late stages of pregnancy is common [6], most women produce enough insulin to overcome the resistance and the remainder who do not, experience GDM. Risk factors for developing GDM include age, obesity, a previous episode of GDM (occurring in a prior pregnancy), and a family history of type 2 diabetes [1].

GDM increases the risk of complications for both mother and child. Mothers with GDM may develop gestational hypertension and preeclampsia as well as a higher recourse to caesarean Sect. [7]. Moreover, an increase in the weight of the newborn is common. Newborns with a weight above the 90th percentile are considered large for gestational age (LGA). These newborns are at risk of neonatal and maternal morbidity, including a higher risk of shoulder dystocia [8].

Current evidence suggests that the detection and management of GDM before 20 weeks of pregnancy improves outcomes for both mother and child [9]. The management of this condition is to optimize blood glucose levels through a multidisciplinary approach primarily based on lifestyle modifications (such as food intake and physical activity) [10, 11], with the possible addition of an insulin treatment [12]. Although metformin can be considered as a therapeutic option [13], current American guidelines have not recommended the use of this drug as a first-line agent, citing the fact that it crosses the placenta to the fetus [14, 15].

Women with GDM were traditionally cared for by nurses and diabetologists with the results of blood sugar testing recorded by hand in paper diaries. They needed frequent outpatient visits to review these results which may have been inconvenient for pregnant women and their families. Access to health services in rural areas presents a challenge for patients with GDM. Telemedicine, the use of information communications technologies, is a promising tool to improve their care. However, despite encouraging results from previous studies [16], the benefits of telemedicine for women with GDM remain uncertain [17, 18]. The objective of this study was to investigate whether implementing a telemedicine solution for women with GDM in a rural hospital setting can improve birth outcomes and in particular if it can reduce the percentage of LGA births.

Methods

Study design

An observational retrospective interrupted-time-series study [19] was conducted in Troyes, France from 1 January 2015 to 31 December 2021. Births included were by women with GDM as identified in the hospital medico-administrative database of the Centre Hospitalier de Troyes which contains the diagnoses of all patients treated at the hospital. The period before the implementation of the telemedicine solution was the baseline period. The intervention period was defined as starting 45 days after the initial introduction of the telemedicine solution. The two study timeframes were: 1 January 2015 to 28 April 2017 (baseline period) and 12 June 2017 to 31 December 2021 (intervention period). Births in the transition period between both compared periods (from 29 April 2017 to 11 June 2017) were excluded from the study.

In order to be coded in the database, GDM had to be diagnosed by a physician according to current clinical guidelines. The diagnosis was usually made through screening for GDM between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation, using a positive oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) with 75 g (2.64oz) of glucose, according to the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) guidelines. The test was positive if the glucose level was above 1.80 g/L (10.0 mmol/L) 1 h after ingestion, or ≥ 1.53 g/L (8.5 mmol/L) two hours after ingestion. A fasting glucose of 0.92 g/L (5.1mmol/L) during the second or third trimesters also led to a positive diagnosis of GDM. Patients were not required to have started insulin to be included. Insulin was started if the introduction of dietary measures and adapted physical activity did not result in acceptable glycemic control after 10 days. Insulin therapy was typically continued until birth, and stopped thereafter. Women with GDM were followed-up three months after birth with another OGTT to check for the resolution of impaired glucose tolerance.

Inclusion/exclusive criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (i) live births from women with GDM (diagnosed from a 24 to 28 weeks OGTT, or a fasting glucose of 0.92 g/L), (ii) those that occurred within the study period, in the hospital under study (Centre Hospitalier de Troyes, France), (iii) age of mother ≥ 18 years. Exclusion criteria were: (i) twin or multiple births, and (ii) history of type 2 diabetes in mothers prior to the current pregnancy.

Study intervention

The telemedicine solution used in this study was myDiabby (myDiabby Healthcare, Bordeaux, France). The application was free of charge for the patients in the intervention period, without any changes to other healthcare-related expenses. During the intervention, women auto-tested their glucose levels six times per day (before each meal and two hours after each meal) using a portable capillary blood glucose monitor. The testing frequency was theoretically the same as in the baseline period. However, the telemedicine application could schedule reminders, via icons on the screen, to ensure that blood glucose tests were carried out as planned. The solution recorded and transmitted the blood glucose readings of women with GDM to a clinical care team. The clinical care team was composed of well-trained nurses in diabetes management, dieticians and diabetologists. The telemedicine solution was based on web-based data collection in the form of diaries and enabled real-time interaction between the patients and the hospital-based care team.

The choice of glucose meter was left to the patient (common choices included Contour next ONE, ACCU-CHEK Guide or OneTouch Verio). If the glucose meter had Bluetooth capabilities, readings were automatically transferred from a glucose meter (via Bluetooth) to the phone (Android or iPhone) and from the phone to the MyDiabby solution.

All web-based diaries were verified by the care team four times per week. Nurses remotely checked transmitted glucose readings daily, and physicians reviewed them once per week. The team could contact the patient by phone or using a chat function embedded in the application to comment on observance or glycemic control. The telemedicine solution could in theory be used to monitor the dietary intake of a patient based on records entered by the patient. However, we did not apply the diet-related telemedicine follow-up in our study. Videoconferencing (teleconsultation with video) was not used in the intervention. GDM-related consultations took place once per week before the telemedicine solution was implemented. Once the use of telemedicine was in place, the patient visited the outpatient department once per month. Nurses called the patient once a week (via telephone) to discuss remotely transmitted glucose readings.

Observational study periods

The telemedicine solution was implemented on 28 April 2017. However, the outcomes of the intervention on birth weight were not expected to be immediate or to occur in a few days. For example, pregnancies that were already LGA in utero would not be expected to revert to a normal weight in the course of a few days. This is because the outcome of the telemedicine intervention is indirect and expected to be mainly mediated by improved glycemic control (compared to women without the intervention), which can take a long time to achieve. A transition period had to be defined to avoid the inclusion of births from women who used telemedicine for a short time, which would lead to an underestimation of the true intervention effect. Therefore, a minimum length of exposition to telemedicine had to be defined, so that any effect of the intervention would have time to be translated to weight changes in the newborn. Accordingly, the start date of the intervention period was defined as 45 days after the solution was first implemented at the hospital.

Women whose birth was analyzed in the intervention period theoretically had the opportunity to use the solution for 45 days (which may have varied due to delays in consultations or uptake). The 45-day window following the implementation of telemedicine ensured that those who used the solution would be exposed to the intervention effects for a time sufficient for slow changes in fetal growth to have time to occur before birth. The 45 days window would also ensure that teams using the solution would have sufficient time to learn its functions and how best to integrate them in their routines. This window of 45 days was set a priori based on clinical considerations, before any statistical analysis was conducted.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was a birth considered large for gestational age (LGA) defined as a birth weighting more than the 90th percentile. French reference weights by gestational age and sex were retrieved using morphometric data provided by the AUDIPOG network [20]. Individual newborn weights at the hospital were retrieved for each birth from the hospital medico-administrative database.

Data collection

Data were extracted from the hospital’s medico-administrative database. This database is used for financing purposes, and therefore it contains information on the diagnoses, procedures, and demographic characteristics of all patients. Diagnoses are encoded using the International Classification of Disease, 10th Edition (ICD-10). Since this database is used for the day-to-day functioning of the hospital, no hospitalized patients were lost to follow-up.

Regarding socioeconomic data, markers of unfavorable socioeconomic conditions are found in the ICD-10 under the headers Z55-Z65. Language barriers and acculturation problems are present among these codes and were also available and collected for analysis. The socioeconomic deprivation index [21] based on the mother’s place of residence was also collected. The socioeconomic indicator used was the French Deprivation Index (FDI), an indicator of the socioeconomic conditions of an area (postal code, equivalent of a zip code), based on the average education levels within an area, unemployment rate, percentage of blue-collar workers, and the income of the inhabitants [22]. Higher values of FDI correspond to worse socioeconomic conditions of the place of residence.

Statistical analysis

Maternal and newborn characteristics potentially associated with the newborn weight were compared according to the two groups defined by the newborns’ birth weights (“GDM-related births LGA” if weight was ≥ 90th percentile according to national reference morphometric data, or “GDM-related births not LGA” if the weight was less than the 90th percentile). We examined the relationship between the rate of LGA births and the study period, baseline (without telemedicine) and intervention (telemedicine solution in use for all patients with GDM in the hospital, except those with a major language or comprehension barrier). If the adjusted Odds Ratio (OR) corresponding to the intervention period (compared with the baseline period) indicated a protective effect (OR less than one), then the intervention period would be considered associated with lower odds of LGA. Therefore, the model for the effect of the solution was a level change model [19].

Other comparisons (comparison of birth weight as a continuous variable expressed in grams according to study period, comparison of birth weight ≥ 4500 g according to study period) were secondary analyses and considered exploratory. The characteristics of births were compared according to LGA, and also according to the study period (baseline period vs. intervention period).

Continuous variables were described with the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were summarized as absolute frequencies and percentages (%). Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t test or the Mann-Whitney U test for asymmetric variables. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Since the variables used for modelling did not have missing values, no specific methods were required for the management of missing data. P-values were not adjusted for multiple testing. A p-value inferior to 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R Version 4.2.3 (The R Foundation of Statistical Computing, Vienna Austria).

Multivariable analyses

To account for confounding in the analysis of the primary outcome (LGA birth), the relationship between the study period and the primary outcome of an LGA birth was estimated by multivariable analysis with a logistic regression model [23], estimating the probability of an LGA birth. The model was estimated on individual birth data.

Variables considered to be potentially related to the primary outcome based on clinical significance were included in the multivariable model analysis. The variables used for adjustment were: the age of the mother [24], gestational age at birth, the sex of the newborn, obesity [25] (Body Mass Index ≥ 30), the presence of an active COVID-19 infection during birth [26], the presence of socioeconomic difficulties based on ICD-10 diagnoses [21], the socioeconomic deprivation FDI index [27], time since the beginning of the study (in years since the start of the study, included as a continuous variable), and the season of birth [28]. A subset of maternal diseases potentially associated with birth weight (eclampsia or preeclampsia [29], anemia [30], liver disease [31], sleep apnea [32]) were not included in the model due to low sample sizes for positive cases. Parity [33] and gestational excessive maternal weight gain [25] were not present in the database.

Ethical considerations

This study was based on routinely collected data and declared to the publicly available French Registry of Studies using healthcare data (Ref No. F20221011164247). In accordance with French legislation pertaining to retrospective studies, non-opposition to the use of the participants’ data or their legal representatives for this research was sought and obtained from all subjects. The study was conducted within the MR004 legal framework, in accordance with the French Public Health Code (L1121-1, Law No. 2012 − 300, March 5, 2012).

Results

Between 2015 and 2021 (excluding the transition period during year 2017), we registered 14,382 single births (Fig. 1). Overall, 1981 out of these single births were GDM-related (13.8%). From comparison of each birth to the national French reference growth curves, there were 76 LGA newborns for 533 births (14.3%) during the baseline period (Supplementary Table 1), and this rate dropped to 11.7% in the intervention period (170 out of 1,448). The difference did not reach statistical significance in the bivariate analysis (p = 0.13). However, an exploratory post hoc analysis showed that newborns weighing ≥ 4500 g were significantly less frequent in the intervention period (2.4% vs. 0.3%, bivariate OR: 0.139; 95%CI [0.047 to 0.409], p < 0.001), and that the average birth weight was lower in the intervention period (Fig. 2). The rate of LGA births in the general obstetric population (single births without gestational diabetes) remained stable (9.1% vs. 8.6% for baseline and intervention periods respectively).

The number of newborns weighing less than the 10th percentile remained stable between the baseline period and the telemedicine intervention period: from 52 out of 533 (9.8%) to 155 out of 1,448 newborns (10.7%; p = 0.54). Cesarean sections were more frequently practiced for LGA births (p < 0.001). There was no statistically significant change in the frequency of cesarean sections between the baseline and intervention periods (p = 0.85) (Supplementary Table 1).

The mean age of mothers was 31.71 ± 5.54 and 32.30 ± 5.14 in women with newborns with birthweights lower and higher than the 90th percentile respectively (p = 0.09) (Table 1). In bivariate analysis the probability of LGA births was associated with obesity (< 0.001), and a higher socioeconomic deprivation index (p = 0.03). The main socioeconomic predictor within this index was median revenue of the residence area (p = 0.02). In multivariable analysis, after adjusting for potential confounders, a lower risk of LGA births was observed for the period after the implementation of the telemedicine solution (OR: 0.541; 95%CI [0.311 to 0.930] (Table 2). Obesity was an independent risk factor for LGA (OR: 1.877; 95%CI [1.394 to 2.558]).

Discussion

This observational study found that LGA births were less frequent after a telemedicine solution was implemented for the care of women with GDM in a large rural hospital, and the rate decrease became statistically significant in multivariate analysis. An exploratory post hoc analysis also showed that the effect was highly significant for the largest newborns (weight ≥ 4500 g).

The incidence of GDM observed in our study (13.8% of single births) was close to the incidence reported in the most recent available perinatal survey performed in France (16.4%) [4]. The observed 2.6% difference was not unexpected since wide temporal variations have been observed in France, and geographical gradients are possible. For example, a past perinatal survey published by Blondel et al. in 2017 [5] reported a lower incidence of 10.8%. The LGA birth rates were higher in this study than in the French reference population (10% by definition) and especially in low gestational age categories. This may have been because the morphometrics curves were established prior to the study or because of regional variations. Nevertheless, the use of national morphometric data has been considered more informative than comparison to international charts [34].

While most observational studies in literature have failed to show an association between telemedicine use and a decrease in LGA, this may have been due to low sample size. An Italian study conducted in Ferrara, Italy, included 60 consecutive patients with GDM, and showed an improvement in perceived control and a better lifestyle fit for telemedicine use, without apparent improvements in clinical outcomes [35]. One study conducted in Puerto Real University Hospital (Cadiz, Spain) showed that applying a telemedicine solution decreased the need for real-life consultations [36]. A recent systematic review on the effectiveness of telemedicine in GDM primarily focused on maternal outcomes (BMI and weight) rather than LGA [37]. Most of the studies identified in a review [38] conducted in 2018 were pilot studies, and therefore did not add substantial quantitative evidence to the literature.

Interventional studies have also been conducted to evaluate the efficacy of telemedicine on perinatal outcomes. A Randomized Controlled Study (RCT) conducted in a large United Kingdom (UK) tertiary referral center failed to show differences in the rate of change of blood glucose measures, although glycemic monitoring was significantly more frequent in the remote glucose monitoring group [39]. Another study published in the same year found improved glucose control in the telemedicine group [40]. The study had an intensive feedback program based on data entered in a smartphone application [40]. An RCT study conducted in Slovenia showed an 18% decrease in the cesarean section rate in the telemedicine group, without showing a decrease in LGA newborns, probably because of the low sample size (n = 106) [41]. A Norwegian study using a dedicated mobile application also showed a decreased cesarean section rate in unadjusted analysis (n = 238) [42]. A slightly larger RCT conducted in China included 309 women [43]. The intervention was simpler than the one found in our study, as it consisted of weekly lifestyle advice provided through WeChat. Better glycemic control was achieved in the intervention group, without any significant differences in neonatal or birth-related complications. Three studies reviewed by Rasekaba et al. (2015) [17] also had sample size limitations, which made them very unlikely to show an effect of the intervention on LGA. The intervention in the study by Homko et al. [44] was very similar to the one implemented in our hospital. Due to a low sample size (n = 40 in each group), no statistically significant differences in the newborn LGA rate could be found in that RCT.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study were the large sample size and multivariate statistical methods allowing for the inclusion of known confounders. The study included all known GDM patients who were referred to a large hospital with more than 2000 single births per year. Furthermore, the study was carried out in a real-life rural setting where the use of telemedicine solutions might be useful for diabetes care.

Regarding limitations, although our data adds evidence to the literature regarding an effect of telemedicine on the odds of LGA newborns, the retrospective observational design may not be sufficient to prove causality. The lower rate of LGA births in the intervention period could have been theoretically due to other hospital-specific or external factors (such as parity, which was not present in our database). Moreover, since this study was based on medico-administrative data, other outcomes such as maternal weight gain prior to birth, the percentage of women treated by insulin or the quality of diabetes control after GDM diagnosis, were not available.

Compliance was not measured since it is not part of the standard medico-administrative database. Compliance to treatment is a major issue, since women with GDM often lack information on their condition and are therefore less motivated to carry out treatment or care. Literature has supported the hypothesis that the use of telemedicine could increase compliance with treatment and glycemic monitoring [40]. This could make telemedicine solutions potentially useful in developing countries, where a shortage in healthcare staff means healthcare providers have less time to deliver health-related information to patients [45]. Regarding directions for future research, RCTs with a larger sample size are necessary to assess the impact of telemedicine on LGA births, as most studies available in the literature were probably underpowered to estimate an effect of the intervention on LGA.

Conclusions

This study found that the implementation of telemedicine as an intervention for GDM care in a rural general hospital was associated with a decrease in the adjusted odds of LGA births.

This dataset makes it possible to reproduce the main result of the study in descriptive and bivariate analysis (additional data is required for multivariable analysis). Although the remaining data analyzed for this study are strictly routinely collected medical data and do not contain patient names, these data are not available to the general public for confidentiality reasons (as their joint information could constitute indirectly reidentifying information). Requests for further information on these data, used within the MR004 legal framework, should be directed to: stephane.sanchez@hcs-sante.fr. Academic requests may be accepted upon review; requests for commercial use will not be accepted.
