Biogenic Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles enhance carotenoid pathway gene expression and suppress verticillium root rot in marigold (Tagetes erecta)
Authors: Hira Saleem, Fethi Ahmet Ozdemir, Liu Qunlu, Syed Waqas Hassan, Rabia Nawab, Farhana, Urooj Haroon, Ibrar Ullah, Hassan Javed Chaudhary, Muhammad Farooq Hussain Munis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVerticillium dahliae is the most destructive root rot pathogen of marigold. For eco-friendly management of this disease, iron oxide nanoparticles (Fe2O3 nanoparticles) were synthesized with the seed extract of Trachyspermum ammi.ResultsSynthesis of Fe2O3 NPs was confirmed by ultraviolet-visible (UV) spectrum absorption peak at 275 nm. FTIR validated functional groups on the nanoparticle surface, XRD showed a crystalline structure with an average size of 42 nm, EDX proved elemental composition (Fe 71.6%, O 15.9%), and SEM showed a spherical morphology. Variable mycelial growth inhibition of V. dahliae was observed at different concentrations (0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.0, and 1.5 mg/mL) of synthesized Fe₂O₃ NPs, in vitro. The greatest mycelial growth inhibition (88.5%) was observed at 0.75 mg/mL concentration. This concentration was further used to control root rot disease of marigold. Root priming of NPs significantly improved plant growth parameters such as root and shoot lengths, fresh and dry weights, increasing root length by 32%, shoot length by 34%, fresh weight by 42%, and dry weight by 18%. NPs treatment also enhanced photosynthetic pigments (chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids), relative water content, and osmolytes (proline and sugars) accumulation. Furthermore, nano-priming significantly decreased H2O2 and malondialdehyde levels, indicating less oxidative stress, as well as decreased relative electrolyte leakage. Histological analysis revealed a decreased mycelial colonization of V. dahliae in the root vascular tissues of marigold. Application of Fe2O3 NPs upregulated the expression of carotenoid biosynthesis pathway genes including Phytoene synthase, Phytoene desaturase, Beta cyclase and Epsilon cyclase, indicating their role in plant defense.ConclusionThe biosynthesized Fe2O₃ nanoparticles improved physiological and biochemical parameters and activated defense-related gene expression, which successfully inhibited the V. dahliae growth and increased resistance in marigold plants. These results imply that Fe₂O₃ NPs produced from T. ammi seed extract could be a viable and environmentally friendly method of controlling Verticillium root rot in marigolds.Keywords: Tagetes erecta, XRD, SEM, Verticillium dahlia, Trachyspermum ammi, Carotenoid biosynthesis, Plant defense, Green nanotechnology

Introduction

Marigold (Tagetes erecta L.), a member of the Asteraceae family, originated in the Mediterranean region and is now grown extensively throughout Europe and Asia [1]. It is a notable ornamental and economically valuable plant that provides a significant natural source of lutein, which is used in food, medicines, and feed additives [2]. Furthermore, marigold has antioxidant, phenolic, and anti-nematicidal qualities, which contribute to its medical and agricultural importance [3], and its blooms are widely utilized in social and religious rites [4].

Despite its repelling properties, marigold is prone to several fungal infections including stem rot (Phytophthora cryptogea), flower blight (Alternaria zinniae), damping-off (Pythium spp.), collar rot (Phytophthora spp.; Pythium spp.), Fusarium wilt (Fusarium oxysporum), Cercospora leaf spot (Cercospora megalopotamica), and Alternaria leaf spot (Alternaria tagetica) [2, 5]. Root rot at the seedling stage, caused by Rhizoctonia solani. is the most reported disease of marigold. Plant roots are also colonized by the soil-borne ascomycete fungus Verticillium dahlia, which can survive without a host by developing extremely resilient structures known as microsclerotia. Root exudates prompt the germination of these microsclerotia, which are inactive in the soil [6]. V. dahliae has a large host range, infecting a diverse variety of flowers, vegetables, and agricultural crops. Cineraria, chrysanthemum, dahlia, geranium, heather, gerbera, marigold, pelargonium, rose, peony, statice, snapdragon, strawflower, and stock are among the ornamental plants that are sensitive to this fungus [7].

For the efficient control of fungal infections in marigold, synthetic fungicides have been used extensively [8]. Synthetic fungicides harm plant growth because excessive use of fungicides disrupts soil microbial balance, ozone layer depletion, develops resistance in fungal pathogens which has widely been reported [9–12], and harms plant and ecosystem health. These issues make it important to investigate and create safer and more sustainable substitutes for treating fungal diseases [13].

Nanotechnology is a recent field that has currently acquired popularity among phytopathologists due to its potential in plant disease management, particularly through dsRNA-loaded nanoparticles that enable spray-induced gene silencing and targeted pathogen control [14–16]. Based on their significant antimicrobial properties, nanoparticles (NPs) are being investigated as potential solutions for managing a variety of plant diseases [17]. Owing to their nanoscale dimension, NPs have strong antifungal and antibacterial properties that allow them to be used as non-toxic substitutes for traditional fungicides. Various studies have documented NPs as an innovative, targeted, and eco-friendly alternative to control plant diseases by enhancing pathogen inhibition, plant defense activation, and precise delivery of protective agents [18–20]. Since metal oxide NPs are assumed to be safe for human health, researchers are mainly concentrating on their manufacture to prevent fruit diseases. Both intracellular and extracellular techniques have been used to biosynthesize these nanoparticles using a variety of pyto-extracts and microorganisms, such as bacteria, fungi, and algae. Over the last decade, many metals have been used to synthesize NPs, such as, platinum, silver, zirconia, gold, silica, iron, zinc, and titanium [21]. Nano fungicides are gaining popularity in agriculture, and a lot of research has focused on synthesizing powdered nanoparticles, which can be easily transported to the field and applied in the form of water-based solution [22]. There are several benefits of eco-friendly synthesis of metal and metal oxide NPs using plant extracts and microorganisms. These plant extracts are affordable, easily useable, non-toxic and broadly available [23]. Iron oxide nanoparticles (Fe2O3 NPs) are the most researched transition metal oxides, due to their environmental friendliness, magnetic properties, low cost, varied crystal structures, and multiple oxidation states [24, 25]. A variety of studies have described the successful antimicrobial properties of Fe2O3 NPs [26, 27].

Trachyspermum ammi L. (Ajwain), is an herbaceous species, which belongs to the family Apiaceae. It is grown extensively in Pakistan, India, Egypt, Iran, and different regions of Europe [28, 29]. Essential oils found in T. ammi seeds have hepatoprotective, diuretic, antibacterial, antiviral, broncho-dilatory, and antiseptic properties [30]. T. ammi is acknowledged as a valuable medicinal plant with great usage in a variety of fields [31]. T. ammi seed extract has already been used for the biogenic synthesis of various nanoparticles, including iron NPs [32], zinc NPs [33] and silver NPs [34]. Important components of T. ammi including thymol, γ-terpinene, and p-cymene have been reported to serve as reducing and capping agents to synthesize NPs. Furthermore, its essential oil exhibits strong antifungal and antibacterial properties, and its roots are well-known for its diuretic qualities [35]. NPs are usually applied on seeds (nano-priming) to improve seed storage and longevity, promote plant growth, improve germination rates and uniformity, and boost crop resilience to a variety of abiotic and biotic stresses [36].

This study has been designed to use Fe₂O₃ NPs for treating root rot disease of marigold plants. Fe₂O₃ NPs were synthesized using T. ammi seed extract as a reducing and stabilizing agent and characterized using sophisticated approaches like XRD, FTIR, SEM, EDX, and UV spectroscopy. To fully comprehend the response of marigold plants to treated and stress conditions, plant defense mechanisms and the expression of defense-related genes involved in carotenoid production pathways were also investigated.

This study sheds new light on nanoparticle-mediated control of defense- and carotenoid-related gene expression, relating better plant growth and stress tolerance to underlying molecular mechanisms. It is hypothesized that Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles may improve plant development and reduce disease severity in marigold (T. erecta L.) by modulating defense and carotenoid-related gene expression.

Materials and methods

Chemical and reagents

Chemicals used in the experiment were analytical reagent grade and purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Germany).

Preparation of seed extract

Seeds of T. ammi were procured from the National Agricultural Research Centre (NARC), Islamabad. After being carefully cleaned with tap water and then distilled water, the seeds were dried at 50 °C in a dry oven. To obtain the aqueous extract, 100 g of seeds were added in 1000 ml of distilled water and placed in the water bath at 80 °C for 30 min [37]. After cooling to room temperature, the mixture was strained through muslin cloth and filtered using a sterilized Whatman filter Paper No. 1. The pH of the solution was recorded and stored at 4 °C, till further use.

Green synthesis of Fe2O₃ nanoparticles

Fe2O₃ NPs were synthesized using T. ammi seed extract with 1 mM solution of FeCl3 in 1:2 ratio. The mixture was heated at 80 °C for 120 min on a hot plate, until a visible change in color, indicating the reduction of Fe2O₃ NPs. The solution was centrifuged for 10 min at 6000 rpm, and the pellet was carefully washed with the deionized water. The pellet was dried for 3–4 h at 100 °C, in an incubator and calcined for 2–3 h at 500 °C to produce crystalline Fe2O₃ NPs [37].

Characterization of Fe₂O₃ NPs

As described by Zubair et al. (2022) successful synthesis of Fe2O₃ NPs was confirmed by the following analyses [38].

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

The functional groups present on the surface of Fe2O₃ NPs were determined using FTIR spectroscopy. The sample was prepared using the KBr pellet method, which involved mixing 100 mg of potassium bromide (KBr) with 10 mg of Fe2O₃ NPs, to create pellet. At a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹, spectral analysis was performed in the range of 400–4000 cm⁻¹.

X-ray diffraction

Scanning electron microscopy and energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy

Fe2O₃ NPs were characterized for their shape and elemental makeup using SEM and EDX spectroscopy. SEM analysis was performed at ×5,000 magnification. Fe2O₃ NPs were sonicated for five minutes in distilled water and the suspension was placed on conductive carbon tape and dried using a lamp. A thermionic emission scanning electron microscope (VEGA3 TESCAN) was used for the analyses.

Ultraviolet-visible spectrum

The spectra were obtained using Labtronics LT 2800 V UV-vis spectrophotometer. The absorption spectra of Fe2O₃ NPs were acquired by scanning their aqueous dispersions between 200 and 800 nm wavelength.

Assessment of dynamic light scattering

Measurement of particle size and its structure was performed by DLS method, using the BeNano180 Zeta pro (Bettersize Instruments Ltd. China). The powder of Fe2O3 NPs was suspended in water and the DLS was assessed at the following conditions: 0.01 absorption, 1.59 refractive index, 0.89 mPa.s (cP) viscosity, 90º scattering angle, 25 °C temperature, and 671 nm wavelength. Fe2O3 NPs present in the aqueous suspensions were quite homogeneous and well dispersed. To reduce agglomeration, the powdered Fe2O₃ NPs were dissolved in distilled water and sonicated for half an hour before measurement.

Fungal growth inhibition assay, in vitro

Verticillium dahliae was procured from the molecular plant pathology laboratory, Quaid-i-Azam University, Islamabad, and purified in Petri plates containing Potato dextrose agar (PDA) media. The effect of different concentrations of Fe2O3 NPs on mycelial growth inhibition was determined using ‘poisoned food” method [39]. In this experiment, 15 mL of PDA medium was supplemented with varying concentrations of Fe2O3 NPs (0.25 mg/mL, 0.5 mg/mL, 0.75 mg/mL, 1.0 mg/mL and 1.5 mg/mL). In control treatment, no NPs were added in PDA medium. To observe mycelial growth inhibition, 5 mm diameter discs of V. dahliae were placed in the center of each Petri plate and incubated at 25°C. Disease control assay, in vivo.

This in vivo assay was performed in the following way.

Preparation of fungus inoculum

Sorghum seeds were placed inside a conical flask and inoculated with the mycelial plug of V. dahliae. The pathogen inoculum was set to 1 × 10⁶ spores/mL. These inoculated seeds were incubated at 25 °C, for 5–6 days. For uniform dispersal of fungal mass, the flask was vigorously agitated, twice a day.

Soil inoculation

Soil was collected from the agriculture field of Quaid-i-Azam University, Islamabad, Pakistan (33°44′N, 73°09′E), at a depth of 5–25 cm. The collected soil was dried for one week at room temperature and sieved through 2 mm mesh, to remove larger debris. Soil was analyzed and mixed with sand and peat moss in a 1:1:1 ratio. This soil mixture was autoclaved and mixed with V. dahlia-inoculated sorghum seeds. The prepared inoculated and control soil (with no inoculated sorghum seeds) was filled in pots and kept for 15 days before sowing.

Root priming of marigold seedlings

Marigold seedlings (4 weeks old) were obtained from the flower nursery of Islamabad, Pakistan. Based on the findings of “in vitro” analysis, the best concentration of Fe2O3 NPs (0.75 mg/mL) was used for disease control (in vivo). The experiment was conducted with the following four treatments, each consisting of three replicates. In the control treatment (C), the roots of marigold seedlings were hydro-primed and planted in the normal soil. In NP treatment, roots were nano-primed with a concentration of 0.75 mg/mL of Fe2O3 NPs. In F treatment, hydro-primed roots were sown in fungus-infected soil. In F + NP treatment, roots were primed with Fe2O3 NPs and planted in fungus infested soil.

Measurement of growth attributes

Physiological growth parameters of harvested plants including root and shoot length were observed, using measuring tape.

Biochemical parameters

The following biochemical assays of the collected seedlings were carried out.

Estimation of photosynthetic content

In a mortar and pestle, 0.1 g samples of fresh leaves were ground using 6 mL of 80% acetone. The mixtures were centrifuged, and the optical densities of supernatants were measured. Carotenoids, chlorophyll a, and chlorophyll b contents were measured at 470 nm, 645 nm, and 663 nm, respectively [40].

Measurement of osmolytes

Following stress related osmolytes were observed from the leaf samples.

Proline content

Proline was determined by the following procedure of Troll and Lindsley, (1955), with little modifications [41, 42]. The method for proline estimation was based on the principle that the proline forms a coloured complex with the ninhydrin. The amount of colour determines the concentration of proline in the sample. Samples were observed at 528 nm wavelength and proline contents were determined.

Total soluble sugar

Content of soluble sugars in plant leaves were determined by the procedure of Dubois et al. (1956) [43]. Optical densities of the samples were determined at 620 nm wavelength, and the total soluble sugar (TS) was measured in µg/g FW on a standard curve, which was calculated with 0–10 mg Dex sugar, as a reference sample.

Relative water content

To assess RWC, leaves were collected and weighed for fresh weight (FW). After being incubated for four hours at 4 °C in distilled water, the turgid weight (TW) was measured. These leaves were dried thereafter in an oven to 80° C for 12 h and their dry weight (DW) was noted. The RWC was calculated using the following formula [44].

Electrical conductivity (EC1) of the liquid was measured after incubating test tubes in a water bath at 32 °C for 2 h. Antioxidants enzymes assay

In an ice-cooled mortar and pestle, fresh samples (0.5 g) were homogenized with a solution of 1.0 mM EDTA (pH 7.8), 5 mL of ice-cold 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer, and 2% (w/v) polyvinylpyrrolidone. Following the instructions of Zhang et al. (2008) and Zulfiqar et al. (2024), the homogenate was centrifuged at 4 °C for 15 min at 10,000 g, and the superoxide dismutase (SOD), and peroxidase (POD) enzymes were measured [46, 47].

Oxidative stress parameters

Malondialdehyde

To measure MDA content, 1.0 mL of a 20% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid and 0.5% (w/v) thiobarbituric acid solution were combined with 0.5 mL of the enzyme extract, and the mixture was agitated for 30 s [44]. After 30 min of heating the combination at 95 °C in a water bath, the tube was quickly cooled in an ice-water bath. After centrifuging the extract for 10 min at 5000 g, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 532 and 600 nm wavelengths. Non-specific turbidity at 600 nm was subtracted to determine the concentration of MDA, and its molar extinction value of 155 mM−1cm− 1 was used.

Hydrogen peroxide

H2O2 levels were determined, following a previously described method [48]. Leaf samples were homogenized with 5% TCA and the mixture was centrifuged twice for 20 min at 13,000 rpm. The reaction mixture was combined with the reagent, comprising of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 8.4), 0.6 mM 4–(2–pyridylazo) resorcinol, and 0.6 mM potassium-titanium oxalate in a 1:1 ratio. The H2O2 concentration was determined in the supernatant by spectrophotometer at an absorbance of 508 nm [49].

Histopathological assessment of T. erecta

Following an authentic protocol, the histopathological study of the roots of infected and control seedlings was accomplished [50]. From each treatment, microtomy was performed to get transverse slices of the root tissues (10 mm thick) and placed for 36 h in a fixative solution, comprising of 5% acetic acid, 10% formalin, 35% distilled water, and 50% ethanol [51]. These root specimens were further stored for extended stabilization in ethanol acetic acid solution (75% ethyl alcohol and 25% acetic acid). Following fixation, marigold root specimens were manually cut into extremely thin transverse slivers (3 mm). These slivers were then dehydrated, diluted with ethyl alcohol and stained with safranin. Finally, compound microscopy was used to visualize the slides at 100× magnification.

Expression analysis of genes involved in carotenoid biosynthesis pathway

In this study, a higher concentration of carotene was observed in disease-resistance of marigold. To further understand the defense mechanism of marigold plant, differential expressions of genes involved in carotenoid biosynthesis pathway were observed. Following a standard protocol, total RNA was extracted from marigold leaves and cDNA was synthesized [52]. The gene expression levels were standardized against the internal reference gene ß-Actin, and relative expression was estimated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt technique, with control samples as calibrators. Statistical analysis

All the experiments were conducted in triplicate. Microsoft Excel was used to compile and process results. The analysis of variance was performed using Statistic 8.1, and the mean and standard deviation were calculated [53]. The difference between mean values (p < 0.05) was determined using Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference test (HSD). Pearson’s component analysis was carried out using R-Studios and displayed as a corrplot of the mean values of all the variables.

Results

Characterization of Fe2O3 NPs

FTIR analysis of synthesized Fe2O3 NPs

FTIR analysis revealed the presence of viable reducing and capping biomolecules on the surface of Fe₂O₃ NPs (Fig. 1; Table 2). In wavelength range of 500–4000 cm− 1, the presence of aliphatic primary amine (N–H stretch) was indicated by the characteristic peak at 3401.00 cm− 1. The presence of alcohol molecules was determined by significant broad appearance and stretching of O-H at 3181.40 cm− 1. Strong C ≡ N (nitrile) stretching was reflected by the peak at 2161.23 cm− 1, whereas N–H bending of the amine salt was indicated by the peak at 1595.10 cm− 1. C-H bending was shown by the peak at 1405.33 cm− 1, indicating the existence of aliphatic group during the reduction process. The peak at 1040.70 cm− 1 demonstrated CO–O–CO stretching of the anhydride. The C–I stretching of halo compounds was strongly reflected by the peaks at 583.41 cm− 1 and 597.1 cm− 1. C–Br stretching of halo compounds was found at 528.28 cm− 1 and 524.01 cm− 1. The existence of organic molecules and amide groups suggests that they function as capping and reducing agents.

UV-visible spectroscopy

UV-visible spectroscopy described the successful synthesis of Fe2O3 NPs (Fig. 2). The production of Fe2O₃ nanoparticles was shown by a prominent absorption peak at 275 nm [54].

UV-visible spectrum of synthesized Fe2O3 NPs

XRD analysis

The constructive interference of monochromatic X-rays, interacting with a crystalline material was efficiently examined by XRD technique. Atomic electrons fluctuate and release secondary radiation at the same frequency as the incident X-rays hit the crystal and produce a distinctive diffraction pattern [55] (Fig. 3). In this study, key characteristics such as crystalline nature, crystallite size, and phase composition of materials were efficiently determined by XRD. Tetragonal peaks of the Fe2O3 were represented by XRD analysis. Since there were no additional impurity peaks and all the XRD peaks were crisp, thin, and strong, the synthesized product was clearly crystalline. According to the QUALX2 software database, the main XRD peaks were found at 11.0°, 16.03°, 20.83°, 21.53°, 30.02°, 31.12°, 32.38°, 33.98°, 37.65°, 42.43°, 43.25°, 50.35°, and 54.36° 2θ. These XRD diffraction patterns matched the JCPDS reference number 01-076-0958. Using the Scherrer equation, the average particle size of the Fe2O₃ nanoparticles was calculated to be 42 nm. The obtained XRD results agree with previous studies [31].

SEM and EDX analysis

The surface morphology of the produced nanoparticles was examined by SEM (Fig. 4). According to the micrographs, the Fe2O₃ NPs were primarily spherical and ranged in size from 30 to 50 nm, which is consistent with the size determined by the XRD study. The spherical shape of synthesized Fe₂O₃ NPs was noticeable. Additionally, EDX analysis verified the successful production of Fe2O₃ NPs by confirming the elemental composition and displaying distinctive peaks of iron (Fe) and oxygen (O). Iron, oxygen, and chlorine weight percentages were 71.6%, 15.9%, and 4.8%, respectively (Fig. 5).

DLS analysis

DLS analysis gave comprehensive details on the size distribution of the produced Fe2O₃ NPs and validated their dispersion quality. The average particle size of the Fe2O₃ NPs as determined by DLS was 118.5 nm, which is significantly greater than the size estimated by XRD (42 nm) (Fig. 6). Unlike XRD, which measures the crystalline core, DLS reflects the hydrodynamic diameter, typically looking 10 to 100 times bigger due to particle aggregation in suspension. This disparity is related to the reduced electrostatic repulsion between NPs in aqueous solutions, leading to agglomeration and the development of bigger clusters. These findings support the assessment of Fe₂O₃ NPs in colloidal systems [56].

DLS shows particle size distribution of Fe2O3 NPs

Furthermore, the polydispersity index (PDI), which may be derived from DLS data, gives information about the uniformity of particle size distribution. A moderately homogenous suspension is indicated by PDI values below 0.4, whereas a very heterogeneous system is suggested by values above 1 [57]. The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) states that NPs are classified as monodisperse if their PDI value is less than 0.05 and as polydisperse if it is greater than 0.7 [58]. The Fe2O₃ NPs in this investigation showed a PDI of 0.35, indicating a homogeneous and monodisperse distribution. These results are consistent with earlier studies [59].

Antifungal assay, in vitro

Green-synthesized Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles were examined for antifungal activity against Verticillium dahliae at various concentrations (0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00, and 1.50 mg/mL) to define inhibitory concentration metrics. All experiments were carried out in triplicate, and mycelial growth inhibition (%) was determined relative to the untreated control. There was a distinct concentration-response pattern (Fig. 7). 0.75 mg/mL was the most effective inhibitory concentration, resulting in the greatest mycelial growth suppression (88.5% inhibition), while slightly less inhibition (84%) was observed at while at 1.00 mg/mL concentration of Fe2O₃ NPs. Minimal inhibition (7.84% and 12.4%, respectively) was seen at lower concentrations (0.25 mg/mL and 0.50 mg/mL), indicating insufficient nanoparticle interaction with the fungal hyphae at suboptimal concentration. Interestingly, the greatest concentration of Fe2O₃ NPs (1.5 mg/ mL) was also less beneficial, as the higher concentration might suppress oxidative stress responses [37], and activate fungal stress tolerance systems [60]. Based on these findings, 0.75 mg/mL concentration of Fe2O₃ NPs was used for pot experiment.

Disease control assay, in vivo

Fe2O₃ NPs successfully prevented wilt disease by diminishing root rot of marigold. Marigold seedlings of same age (4 weeks old, 10 ± 1 cm height) were used for each treatment. To reduce native microbial impacts, sterilized potting soil (121 °C, 30 min) with low microbial counts (< 10² CFU/g) was utilized. Each seedling’s root zone received 10 mL of an inoculum containing 1 × 106 spores/mL of V. dahliae. The statistically significant decrease in disease severity in F + NP plants, the absence of disease in NP-only and control plants, and the correlation with in vitro antifungal activity collectively support that Fe₂O₃ NPs contributed to disease suppression, consistent with other greenhouse studies using disease reduction as the primary efficacy indicator, even though direct visualization or quantification of Fe₂O₃ NPs uptake (e.g., via ICP-MS or TEM-EDX) would provide mechanistic insight.

In control (C) and NPs treated plants (NP treatment), no wilting symptoms were observed (Fig. 8A-B). In F + NP treatment, considerable root rot (8.2 ± 0.58 mm²) and moderate wilting symptoms were observed (Fig. 8C). However, severe wilting and root rot (36 ± 2.12 mm2) symptoms were prevalent in F treatment (Fig. 8D). The disease incidence, wilt severity score, and root rot area in marigold seedlings under different treatments are presented in Table 3. Fungus inoculated plants displayed wilting and poor growth of plant seedlings and they turned yellow (F + NP treatment) and brown (F treatment). Fe2O₃ NPs effectively inhibited root rot disease of marigold seedlings.

Measurement of osmolytes

Increased stress conditions were indicated by the marked increase in osmolyte accumulation i.e., sugar and proline levels in infected plants (Fig. 10A-B). Conversely, plants exposed to Fe2O₃ NPs exhibited a decrease in proline and sugar levels, indicating a reduction in stress. Proline and sugar levels in the fungus inoculated plants (F treatment) were about 25% higher than the control plants, resulting in the greatest accumulation of these osmolytes. In comparison to the F treatment, Fe2O₃ NPs treated plants (NP treatment) showed significant decreased accumulation of proline (47%) and sugar (44%). Similarly, NP + F treatment showed lower proline and sugar concentration than F treatment alone (Fig. 10).

Expression analysis of carotenoid biosynthesis pathway genes

Variable expressions of four carotenoid biosynthesis pathway genes were observed in all four treatments (Fig. 16). The greatest expressions of all genes were observed in NP treatment, showing the role of NPs in upregulating the synthesis of carotenoids and improving plant defense mechanism. Fungal infection downregulated the expression of these genes and indicated stressful conditions.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) of all treatments to show the relative expression of Psy, Pds, Lcy-b and Lcy-e genes

By the application of Fe2O3 NPs, the expression of these genes increased in NP and F + NP treatments. Higher expression in F + NP treatment indicated the synthesis of carotenoid in marigold plants to survive and tolerate fungus-stress conditions. (Fig. 16). The reference gene (ß-actin) was chosen based on prior validation in marigold and demonstrated consistent Ct values across treatments. Primer amplification efficiency ranged from 95 to 105%, and melt curve analysis revealed single, unique amplification products without primer-dimers. Three independent biological replicates, each measured in technical triplicates, were used for each qPCR experiment. These methodological techniques ensure that observed expression patterns reflect transcriptional changes generated by Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles and fungal stress.

Carotenoids play very important roles in plant development and survival under biotic and abiotic stress conditions [60], and the upregulation of carotenoid biosynthesis genes in NP and F + NP treatments supports the protective role of Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles in enhancing plant resilience.

Pearson correlation and principal component evaluation

PCA was used to investigate the association between several attributes in marigold plants exposed to varying concentrations of Fe2O3 NPs under biotic stress. A biplot of the charted statistics (Dim 1 and Dim 2) revealed 98.5% inconsistencies of the dataset, with Dim 1 explaining 96.9% and Dim 2 explaining 1.8% of the variation (Fig. 17). Variables like H₂O₂, MDA, Proline, Sugar, and REL have strong positive loadings on Dim1, demonstrating their significant role in stress responses. Conversely, SOD, POD, RWC, Chl a, Chl b, and carotenoids showed negative loadings on Dim1, indicating improved physiological condition and increased antioxidant defense in plants treated with nanoparticles. Dim2 made a small contribution to the overall variation, mostly due to slight changes in the relative water content and chlorophyll concentration.

Discussion

In Pakistan, the fast increase of commercial marigold harvesting has resulted in the introduction of several plant diseases. There is little socioeconomic information available on marigold production and impact, even though it has become an economically important crop [61]. Biotic stress, especially fungal diseases, is a serious concern and one of the main obstacles in its cultivation. Verticillium wilt, caused by V. dahlia, is an important disease of marigold [62]. This disease is controlled by several chemical fungicides, which pollute the environment [63]. Our findings are in agreement with previous studies reporting that nano-priming enhances plant growth and disease resistance. Earlier studies using iron-based nanoparticles (Fe₂O₃/Fe₃O₄) have shown improved antioxidant defense, increased germination, and reduced disease severity in different crops. Similarly, our results demonstrated significant suppression of verticillium wilt disease and improved merigold plant health following Fe₂O₃ nanoparticle treatment. For the treatment of fungal diseases, NPs have potential to substitute hazardous fungicides and control diseases in an environmentally friendly way [64]. Plant-mediated fabrication of metallic NPs is thought to be simpler than other methods, since plant metabolites naturally act as reducing and stabilizing agents during the synthesis of NPs [65].

In the present study, seed extract of T. ammi, also known as ajwain, has been successfully used for the environmentally friendly synthesis of Fe₂O₃ NPs. It has previously been accounted that T. ammi-mediated nanoparticles have antifungal properties [31]. The main bioactive component responsible for ajwain’s potent antifungal action is thymol. Apart from thymol, it is also thought that additional phenolic components including carvacrol, p-cymene, and γ-terpinene contribute to its antifungal qualities [66].

FTIR, XRD, SEM, EDX, and DLS were among the analytical methods used to fully characterize the synthesized Fe₂O₃ NPs. FTIR spectroscopy was used to identify different functional groups linked to bioactive substances. According to the study, all samples contained organic compounds and amine salts, which are known to contribute to the stabilization and reduction of NPs. FTIR spectrum also revealed that the surfaces of the NPs have free O-H stretching vibrations from alcohols and N-H groups from proteins, which increase their antimicrobial properties by preventing microbial growth [67]. Furthermore, the antifungal potential of Fe2O₃ NPs was further supported by the identification of alkyl halides on their surface [68]. The crystalline nature and nanoscale size of the particles were verified by XRD analysis. According to earlier research, these tiny, distinct crystalline structures greatly increase the antimicrobial efficacy of NPs [29]. Fe2O₃ NPs were found to have a mostly spherical shape by SEM analysis, with diameters ranging from 20 to 50 nm, which is according to the size estimations derived from XRD analysis (Fig. 6). According to Saqib et al. (2019) electrostatic interactions between the surface layers of NPs are probably the cause of the agglomeration seen in SEM images [69]. Some particles had unusual shapes, although the majority seemed spherical. The existence of iron (Fe) and oxygen (O) peaks in the T. ammi seed extract was confirmed by elemental composition analysis using EDX, indicating the successful production of Fe2O₃ NPs. According to earlier research, the oxygen and chlorine components needed to synthesize Fe2O₃ NPs come from polyphenols and iron found in the plant extract, respectively [70].

Using UV-visible spectroscopy, the effective green synthesis of FeNPs was verified. Both α-Fe2O₃ and γ-Fe2O₃ nanoparticles tend to form large aggregates in aqueous conditions, just like many other metal and metal oxide nanoparticles [56, 71]. DLS examination verified that Fe2O₃ NPs were free of impurities and dust particles and were in the nanoscale region, with a size of roughly 78–80 nm. Hydrodynamic diameter, which is often bigger than those determined by TEM imaging, is reflected in particle sizes measured by DLS [72]. According to a recent study, biometallic Ag/Fe2O₃/TiO₂ nanocomposites made with neem and tulsi oils had an average size of 25 nm according to SEM, and a hydrodynamic size of 270.9 nm, according to DLS analysis [73]. It has also been demonstrated that Fe2O₃ nanoparticles have potent antifungal qualities against a variety of diseases (Saleem et al., 2024). In the current investigation, V. dahliae growth was successfully inhibited by green-synthesized Fe2O₃ NPs in both in vitro and in vivo study. In accordance with prior study, larger nanoparticle concentrations were found to lower antifungal efficacy [74]. Nanoparticles tend to aggregate into bigger bulk particles at high concentrations [75].

Our research showed that marigold growth was positively impacted by nano-priming using Fe2O₃ NPs. Previous studies have reported similar improvements in marigold’s morphological, physiological, biochemical, and enzymatic characteristics [76]. According to earlier research, NPs have a beneficial effect on fresh and dry weight, shoot and root lengths, relative water content, and the formation of proteins and sugars [67]. Similar results have previously been documented, wherein Fe2O₃ NPs improved several plant physiological indices [77]. Furthermore, increased proline and total soluble sugar levels could be biochemical markers of V. dahliae-induced stress and damage. Plants with verticillium wilt typically have higher levels of organic solute concentration in their leaves, including proline and sugars [78]. The amounts of these chemicals were considerably decreased in our study after treatment with Fe2O₃ nanoparticles. This decrease reflects a reduced stress response, as the concentration of proline and sugars is often linked with plant reactions to both abiotic and biotic stresses.

Chlorophyll plays a crucial role in photosynthesis and is intimately related to plants’ total photosynthetic efficiency. Consequently, variations in chlorophyll levels are valid markers of plant health and environmental adaptability. In the current study, leaves treated with Fe2O₃ NPs showed improved photosynthetic characteristics and increased chlorophyll content. The structure and function of the photosynthetic machinery depend on iron, which helped significantly to the rise in chlorophyll levels in marigold seedlings. Iron deficiency has been linked to severe reductions in chlorophyll content and impairments in chloroplast structure [79]. Plants naturally contain large amounts of carotenoids, which perform as non-enzymatic scavengers of reactive oxygen species [80]. In our study, carotenoid levels significantly increased when NPs were applied under stressful conditions. On the other hand, seedlings infected with V. dahliae showed a decrease in both carotenoid and chlorophyll levels, suggesting a particular stress response that led to chlorophyll breakdown and reduced photosynthetic activity [81]. Improved iron availability using Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles may explain the observed increase in carotenoid-related gene expression. Iron is essential for pigment biosynthesis enzymatic activities. Furthermore, nanoparticles can be absorbed by plant roots and moved through vascular tissues, where they affect cellular signaling pathways such as stress-related reactions and reactive oxygen species (ROS) [66]. In addition to improving plant defense and physiological function, these signaling pathways may control gene expression. Previous studies have found similar results revealing nanoparticle-mediated control of plant growth and gene expression [31].

Antioxidant enzyme activation is a crucial defense mechanism in plants that decreases damage from reactive oxygen species (ROS). Production of ROS impose stress, which causes disruption in electronic and ionic transport chains due to higher affinity of iron NPs with the membrane of microbes [54]. Enzymes that assist neutralize ROS and shield cells from oxidative damage, such as peroxidase (POD) and superoxide dismutase (SOD), are upregulated in response to pathogen-induced stress. Peroxidase is essential for lowering oxidative stress by turning hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) into water [82]. In contrast to the plants under pathogen stress, marigold plants treated with Fe2O₃ NPs showed a substantial rise in the activity of the antioxidant enzymes. To maintain normal growth and development under stressful conditions like salt, plants employ a variety of defense mechanisms, such as increased activity of antioxidant enzymes that scavenge reactive oxygen species [49].

The relative water content (RWC) of plants is significantly decreased as a result of osmotic stress caused by fungal infections [83]. In comparison to the control and F + NPs treatments, our results showed a significant increase in RWC in marigold seedlings treated with Fe₂O₃ NPs. These findings are in line with past research that found a link between decreased RWC and the severity of the disease [84]. In our study, plants treated with NPs displayed greater RWC levels than the infected plants. These findings align with the research put forward by Satti et al. (2021) [85].

The development of the V. dahliae infection in T. erecta resulted in increased H2O₂ and MDA levels. But in our study, the increase of these oxidative stress indicators was considerably decreased by nano-priming with Fe2O₃ NPs. These findings align with previous study on different plant species [86]. Both the correlation matrix and the PCA results supported the findings of Naseem et al. (2025) [87], who documented the impact of Fe2O₃ NPs on antioxidant levels and plant growth. According to earlier studies, metal-based compounds have a significant negative correlation with photosynthetic indicators and biomass production, but a high positive correlation with MDA and other antioxidant indicators [80].

Conclusion

This work reveals novel insights into the use and efficacy of green-synthesized NPs to manage verticillium root rot disease in marigold plants in a simple, efficient, and eco-friendly manner alternative to standard chemical treatments with prospective applications in agricultural disease management. Strong antifungal activity against V. dahliae was shown by Fe2O₃ NPs, and the physiological and biochemical attributes of marigold plant were significantly improved by root priming with these NPs, while also decreasing disease severity, showing a significant role in activating plant defense mechanisms. These findings imply that Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles could be developed as a nano-priming agent for sustainable disease management in ornamental crops under field settings. Since these NPs are produced in powdered form, they can be easily transferred to the field for their application.
