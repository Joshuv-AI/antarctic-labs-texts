Stable Thompson Sampling: Valid Inference via Variance Inflation
Budhaditya Halder, Shubhayan Pan, Koulik Khamaru, Department of Statistics, Rutgers University

Abstract
We consider the problem of statistical inference when the data is collected via a Thompson Sampling-type algorithm. While Thompson Sampling (TS) is known to be both asymptotically optimal and empirically effective, its adaptive sampling scheme poses challenges for constructing confidence intervals for model parameters. We propose and analyze a variant of TS, called Stable Thompson Sampling, in which the posterior variance is inflated by a logarithmic factor. We show that this modification leads to asymptotically normal estimates of the arm means, despite the non-i.i.d. nature of the data. Importantly, this statistical benefit comes at a modest cost: the variance inflation increases regret by only a logarithmic factor compared to standard TS. Our results reveal a principled trade-off: by paying a small price in regret, one can enable valid statistical inference for adaptive decision-making algorithms.



1
Introduction

Multi-armed bandit algorithms form a foundational framework for sequential decision-making under uncertainty, wherein a learner repeatedly selects among competing actions in order to maximize cumulative reward. A key statistical challenge in this setting is the inherent exploration–exploitation trade-off: how to balance the need to acquire information about uncertain actions (exploration) with the goal of leveraging current knowledge to accrue reward (exploitation). Owing to their conceptual simplicity and wide applicability, bandit methods have been deployed across a range of domains, including personalized recommendation
[
61
,
8
,
9
]
, online advertising
[
54
,
51
]
, adaptive clinical trials
[
5
]
, and financial portfolio optimization
[
43
,
25
]
, to name a few. For a comprehensive overview of applications of bandit algorithms, see the survey by Bouneffouf et al.
[
10
]
.

A variety of algorithmic strategies have been developed to address the exploration–exploitation dilemma. Among the most prominent are the Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) family
[
33
,
34
,
4
]
, which constructs high-probability upper confidence bounds on the mean rewards of each arm and selects the arm with the highest such bound;
\epsilon
-greedy approaches
[
46
]
, which inject random exploration with fixed or decaying probability; and exponential–weighted algorithms
[
3
]
, which assign probabilities to actions via multiplicative updates, among other commonly used strategies. Within this landscape, Thompson Sampling (TS), originally proposed by Thompson
[
48
]
, occupies a distinct position due to its Bayesian formulation. At each decision point, TS samples a mean-reward parameter for each arm from its posterior distribution of arm means given the observed data, and selects the arm with the highest sampled mean parameter. In practice, Thompson Sampling has gained widespread adoption not only due to its Bayesian interpretability but also because of its algorithmic simplicity and favorable empirical performance
[
11
]
.

While bandit algorithms are traditionally motivated by the goal of reward maximization, many contemporary applications demand more than just optimal decision-making—they require valid statistical inference
[
6
,
49
,
50
,
40
,
12
]
. In such settings, the adaptive nature of data collection presents a fundamental challenge, as the resulting data sequence no longer satisfies the standard i.i.d. assumptions that underpin much of classical statistical theory. In particular, in the multi-armed bandit problem, sample means—computed from data collected by adaptive algorithms—may not converge to an normal distribution asymptotically
[
60
]
. This lack of normality complicates the use of classical tools such as confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.

In this paper, we study how to construct confidence intervals for model parameters in multi-armed bandit problems where actions are selected using the Thompson Sampling algorithm.

1.1
Related work

The difficulties of conducting valid inference under adaptive data collection are not new; analogous issues have been documented in time series and econometrics, where autoregressive dependencies can invalidate classical asymptotic approximations

,
55
,
56
,

[

. Most relevant to our work are recent developments in

In this work, we extend this perspective to a modified variant of Thompson Sampling, showing that a similar deterministic analysis yields rigorous asymptotic inferential guarantees—albeit via proof techniques that differ substantially from those used in the UCB setting.

2
Multi-armed bandit and Thompson sampling

We consider a Gaussian multi-armed bandit problem with
K
arms. At each time step
t
, the player selects an arm
A_{t}\in[K]
and receives a random reward
X_{t}
from the distribution
\mathscr{N}(\mu_{A_{t}},\sigma^{2}_{A_{t}})
. The goal of a bandit algorithm is to select a sequence of actions
\{A_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
such that the expected-regret over
T
rounds, defined as,

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}_{T}=T\mu^{\star}-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}\right]

(1)

is minimized. Here,
\mu^{\star}:=\max\limits_{a}\mu_{a}
denotes the largest arm mean.

Several algorithms have been proposed to address this problem, including Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) methods
[
4
,
32
]
, which leverage optimism in the face of uncertainty, and
\epsilon
-greedy strategies, which balance exploration and exploitation. Among these, Thompson Sampling (TS)
[
48
]
has emerged as a particularly effective approach due to its empirical performance and strong theoretical guarantees
[
2
,
28
]
. TS operates by maintaining a posterior distribution over the arm means and sampling actions according to their probability of being optimal, seamlessly integrating exploration and exploitation. We detail the algorithm in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Thompson Sampling

Inputs
: (a) Number of epoch
T

Initialize
: Pull each arm
a\in[K]
once and set
\widehat{\mu}_{a,K}
as the associated reward, and set
n_{a,K}=1
.

for

t=K+1,\ldots,T

do

For each arm
a\in[K]
, sample
\theta_{a,t}\sim\mathscr{N}\left(\widehat{\mu}_{a,t-1},\frac{1}{n_{a,t-1}}\right)
.

Pull arm
A_{t}=\arg\max\limits_{a}\theta_{a,t}
and observe associated reward
X_{t}
.

For
a\in[K]
, update

n_{a,t}=n_{a,t-1}+\bm{1}_{\{A_{t}=a\}}\qquad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\mu}_{a,t}=\frac{n_{a,t-1}\widehat{\mu}_{a,t-1}+X_{t}\bm{1}_{\{A_{t}=a\}}}{n_{a,t}}

end

for

3
Statistical inference with Thompson Sampling

Beyond minimizing regret, there is growing interest in constructing confidence intervals (CIs) for the arm means
\{\mu_{a}\}_{a\in[K]}
. Such inference is critical in applications like clinical trials, where quantifying the uncertainty of treatment effects is essential
[
39
]
, or in A/B testing, where precise effect-size estimation informs decision-making
[
17
,
24
]
. However, standard methods for constructing CIs—such as those based on asymptotic normality of sample means—fail in the bandit setting because the data-collection process is inherently non-i.i.d. Due to adaptive sampling, the central limit theorem (CLT) does not directly apply, and naive estimators can exhibit significant bias
[
41
,
44
]
.

To address this challenge, recent work has focused on the stability of bandit algorithms
[
22
,
30
,
19
,
27
]
, a notion introduced by Lai and Wei
[
36
]
.

Definition 1
.

A bandit algorithm
\mathscr{A}
is called stable if for all arm
a\in[K]
there exist non-random scalars
\{n^{\star}_{a,T}(\mathscr{A})\}_{a\in[K]}
, which depend on the problem parameters
T,\{\mu^{2}_{a}\}_{a\in[K]},\{\sigma^{2}_{a}\}_{a\in[K]}
, such that

\displaystyle\frac{n_{a,T}(\mathscr{A})}{n^{\star}_{a,T}(\mathscr{A})}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}1\qquad\text{and}\qquad n^{\star}_{a,T}(\mathscr{A})\to\infty\qquad\text{as}\quad T\to\infty.

(2)

Here,
n_{a,T}(\mathscr{A})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\{A_{t}=a\}}
denotes the number of times arm
a
is pulled in
T
rounds by algorithm
\mathscr{A}
.

At a high level, the stability of an algorithm ensures that the arm pull-behavior becomes less noisy as
T
becomes large. Following an argument from Lai and Wei
[
36
,
Theorem 3
]
it is easy to show that for any stable algorithm
\mathscr{A}
, the arms means
\{\widehat{\mu}_{a,T}(\mathscr{A})\}_{a\in[K]}
are asymptotically normal. Formally,

Lemma 1

(Lai and Wei
[
36
]
)
.

Given any stable algorithm
\mathscr{A}
, let
\widehat{\mu}_{a,T}(\mathscr{A})
and
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{a,T}
, respectively, denote the sample mean and variance of arm
a
rewards at time
T
. Then for all
a\in[K]

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{n_{a,T}(\mathscr{A})}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{a,T}}\cdot\left(\widehat{\mu}_{a,T}(\mathscr{A})-\mu_{a}\right)\overset{\mathscr{D}}{\longrightarrow}\mathscr{N}(0,1)

(3)

. In a recent work, Fan and Glynn

\frac{n_{1,T}}{T}

\frac{n_{2,T}}{T}

[0,1]

4
Stable Thompson Sampling

In this section, we propose and analyze a modification of Thompson Sampling, which we refer to as Stable Thompson Sampling. Our approach is motivated by a recently established stability property of the Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) algorithm. A series of recent works
[
19
,
27
,
22
]
attribute this stability to UCB’s characteristic bonus term,
\sqrt{\frac{2\log T}{n_{a,t-1}}}
, which inflates reward estimates to account for statistical uncertainty in estimating the sample mean. The UCB estimate for arm
a
at time
t
takes the form

\displaystyle\text{UCB}_{a,t}=\widehat{\mu}_{a,t-1}+\sqrt{\frac{2\log T}{n_{a,t-1}}}

(4)

where
\widehat{\mu}_{a,t-1}
is the empirical mean,
n_{a,t-1}
is the number of times arm
a
has been pulled and
T
is the total number of rounds. The bonus term exceeds the typical deviation of the sample mean, which is only of the order
\sqrt{\frac{2\log\log T}{n_{a,t-1}}}
under sub-Gaussian noise, as implied by the Law of iterated logarithm
[
18
,
Theorem
8.5.2
]
. This deliberate inflation guards against underestimation of the true mean and contributes to stability (cf. definition (
2
)). Motivated by this insight, we incorporate a similar safeguard into Thompson Sampling:

We inflate the posterior variance in Thompson Sampling by a factor
\gamma_{T}\gg 2\log\log T
to guard against uncertainty in estimating the arm-means.

We refer to this variant as
Stable Thompson sampling
; the full algorithm is presented in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Stable Thompson Sampling

Inputs
: (a) Number of epoch
T\;\;
(b) Tuning parameter
\gamma_{T}

Initialize
: Pull each arm
a\in[K]
once and set
\widehat{\mu}_{a,1}
as the associated reward. Set
n_{a,K}=1
.

for

t=K+1,\ldots,T

do

For each arm
a\in[K]
, sample
\theta_{a,t}\sim\mathscr{N}\left(\widehat{\mu}_{a,t-1},\frac{\gamma_{T}}{n_{a,t-1}}\right)
.

Pull arm
A_{t}=\arg\max_{a}\theta_{a,t}
and observe associated reward
X_{t}
.

Set

n_{a,t}=n_{a,t-1}+\bm{1}_{\{A_{t}=a\}}\qquad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\mu}_{a,t}=\frac{(n_{a,t}-1)\widehat{\mu}_{a,t-1}+X_{t}\bm{1}_{\{A_{t}=a\}}}{n_{a,t}}

end

for

4.1
Theoretical guarantees

In this section, we state our main theoretical guarantees on Stable Thompson Sampling. We show that Stable Thompson algorithm is stable for a two-armed bandit problem. Throughout, we assume that the tuning parameter
\gamma_{T}
in Algorithm
1
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{T}}{\log\log T}\rightarrow\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\sqrt{\log T}}{\gamma_{T}}\rightarrow\infty\quad\text{as}\;\;T\rightarrow\infty.

(5)

Under this setup, we have:

Theorem 1

(Stability in two-armed bandit)
.

Assume arm 1 is one of the optimal arms in a two armed bandit problem, and we run Algorithm
1
with tuning parameter
\gamma_{T}
satisfying condition (
5
). Then, Algorithm
1
is stable. Concretely, the following hold

\displaystyle\frac{n_{1,T}}{T}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{n_{2,T}}{T}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{2}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{if}\quad\Delta=0

\displaystyle\frac{n_{1,T}}{T}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{n_{2,T}}{\gamma_{T}\cdot\log T}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{2}{\Delta^{2}}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{if}\quad\Delta>0

We prove Theorem
1
in Section
5
. An immediate consequence of this theorem, together with Lemma
1
, is that sample means of both arms are asymptotically normal. As a result, given any target level
\alpha>0
, we have following asymptotically exact confidence intervals. Specifically, for arms
a=1,2
we have:

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\text{CI}_{a,\alpha,T}\ni\mu_{a}\right)=1-\alpha,\qquad\text{where}\quad

\displaystyle\text{CI}_{a,\alpha,T}=\left[\widehat{\mu}_{a,T}-z_{1-\alpha/2}\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{a,T}}{\sqrt{n_{a,T}}},\quad\widehat{\mu}_{a,T}+z_{1-\alpha/2}\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{a,T}}{\sqrt{n_{a,T}}}\right].

(6)

Here,
\widehat{\mu}_{a,T}
and
\widehat{\sigma}_{a,T}
are the sample mean and sample standard deviation of the rewards from arm
a
, respectively.

Regret comparison:

While Stable Thompson Sampling enables valid inference despite adaptive data collection, it is natural to ask how much we lose in terms of expected regret. The following regret bound addresses this question in the general
K
-armed bandit setting.

Proposition 1
.

For
K
-armed bandit problem, the regret of Algorithm
1
is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}(T)\leq\sum_{j:\Delta_{j}>0}\left[\kappa_{1}\cdot\frac{\gamma_{T}\cdot\log\left(\frac{T\Delta_{j}^{2}}{\gamma_{T}}+\kappa_{2}\right)}{\Delta_{j}}+\frac{\kappa_{3}\gamma_{T}}{\Delta_{j}}\right],

(7)

where
\Delta_{j}=\mu^{*}-\mu_{j}
, and
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3}
are positive universal constants.

Comparing this bound with the regret guarantee for standard Thompson Sampling
[
31
,
Theorem 2
]
, we see that the regret of the Stable Thompson Sampling algorithm is at most a factor of
\gamma_{T}
worse, up to universal constants.

Proof of Theorem
1

We prove the theorem in two parts; first for
\Delta=0
and then for
\Delta>0
. Throughout, we assume the reward variance
\sigma^{2}=1
.

5.1
Case 1:
\Delta=0

Let us define
U_{t}:=\bm{1}\{A_{t}=1\}
and
p_{1}\left(n_{1,t-1}\;,t\right):=\mathbb{E}\left[U_{t}\;\big|\;\mathscr{F}_{t-1}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left(A_{t}=1\;\big|\;\mathscr{F}_{t-1}\right)
. For convenience, we denote
p_{1}\left(n_{1,t-1}\;,t\right)
as simply
p_{1,t}
. Rewrite

\displaystyle\frac{n_{1,T}}{T}=\frac{1+\sum_{t=3}^{T}U_{t}}{T}=\frac{1}{T}+\mathfrak{G}_{1}^{(T)}+\mathfrak{G}_{2}^{(T)}

where
\mathfrak{G}_{1}^{(T)}=\frac{\sum_{t=3}^{T}\left(U_{t}-p_{1,t}\right)}{T}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}^{(T)}=\frac{\sum_{t=3}^{T}p_{1,t}}{T}
. We prove that
\mathfrak{G}_{1}^{(T)}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}^{(T)}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{2}
.

By Markov Inequality, for any
\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\sum_{t=3}^{T}\left(U_{t}-p_{1,t}\right)}{T}\right|>\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{t=3}^{T}\left(U_{t}-p_{1,t}\right)\right|>\varepsilon T\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\left[\sum_{t=3}^{T}\left(U_{t}-p_{1,t}\right)\right]^{2}\right]}{\varepsilon^{2}T^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\left(\sum_{t=3}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[(U_{t}-p_{1,t})^{2}\right]\right)+2\left(\sum_{3\leq i<j\leq T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(U_{i}-p_{1,i}\right)\left(U_{j}-p_{1,j}\right)\right]\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}T^{2}}

Observe that
|U_{t}-p_{1,t}|\leq 1
. For
i<j
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(U_{i}-p_{1,i}\right)\left(U_{j}-p_{1,j}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\left(U_{i}-p_{1,i}\right)\left(U_{j}-p_{1,j}\right)\Big|\mathscr{F}_{j-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(U_{i}-p_{1,i}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\left(U_{j}-p_{1,j}\right)\Big|\mathscr{F}_{j-1}\right]\right]

Since
p_{1,j}
is
\mathscr{F}_{j-1}
–measurable, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(U_{j}-p_{1,j}\right)\Big|\mathscr{F}_{j-1}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[U_{j}\Big|\mathscr{F}_{j-1}\right]-p_{1,j}=0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\sum_{t=3}^{T}\left(U_{t}-p_{1,t}\right)}{T}\right|>\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T-2}{\varepsilon^{2}T^{2}}

which converges to
0
as
T\to\infty
. This proves
\mathfrak{G}_{1}^{(T)}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0
. Next, we prove
\mathfrak{G}^{(T)}_{2}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}1/2
by showing that
p_{1,t}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}1/2
.

Recalling the posterior sample at time
t
is
\theta_{a,t}=\widehat{\mu}_{a,t-1}+\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{T}}{n_{a,t-1}}}Z_{a,t}
for
Z_{a,t}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathscr{N}(0,1)
and letting
\widehat{\Delta}_{t}:=\widehat{\mu}_{1,t}-\widehat{\mu}_{2,t}
, we have

\displaystyle p_{1,t}

6
Discussion

This work takes a step toward bridging the gap between adaptive decision-making and valid statistical inference in bandit settings. We introduced a stabilized variant of Thompson Sampling, called Stable Thompson Sampling, and established its stability and asymptotic normality in the canonical two-armed Gaussian bandit problem. Our theoretical results, supported by empirical evidence, demonstrate that a modest variance inflation enables confidence intervals with nominal coverage while preserving near-optimal regret performance.

When extended to a general
K
-armed bandit problem, the Stable Thompson Sampling algorithm retains favorable performance: Proposition
1
shows that regret increases only logarithmically relative to standard Thompson Sampling. This raises a natural question: does the stability property established in the two-armed case persist when
K>2
? The main technical challenge lies in controlling the allocation of arm pulls when multiple arms are optimal or nearly optimal. How variance inflation interacts with these allocation dynamics remains an open and important question. Additionally, our current analysis assumes Gaussian reward distributions. It would be natural to ask whether similar stability and inferential guarantees can be established under more general reward models.

Ultimately, our work underscores that statistical inference and decision-making need not be at odds: with careful algorithmic design, randomized posterior-based methods – such as Thompson Sampling – can be stabilized to provide valid confidence guarantees while maintaining strong learning performance. We view this as an initial step toward extending such guarantees to broader classes of adaptive algorithms.

Lemma 6
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\left\{p_{j}^{(n)}\right\}_{j=1}^{m}

(0,1)

First observe that for all

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq j\leq m}\log\left(G_{j}^{(n)}p_{j}^{(n)}\right)\geq\log\left(G_{1}^{(n)}p_{1}^{(n)}\right)\geq\log(c)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq m}\log\left(G_{j}^{(n)}p_{j}^{(n)}\right)>n^{a/2}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq m}\log\left(G_{j}^{(n)}p_{j}^{(n)}\right)\leq n^{a/2}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\left(\log\left(G_{j}^{(n)}p_{j}^{(n)}\right)\leq n^{a/2}\;\;\text{for all}\;\;\;j\in[m]\right)

\displaystyle=1-\prod_{j\in[m]}\mathbb{P}\left(\log\left(G_{j}^{(n)}p_{j}^{(n)}\right)\leq n^{a/2}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\prod_{j\in[m]}\mathbb{P}\left(G_{j}^{(n)}p_{j}^{(n)}\leq\exp\left\{n^{a/2}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\prod_{j\in[m]}\left[1-\left(1-p_{j}^{(n)}\right)^{\left\lfloor\frac{\exp\left\{n^{a/2}\right\}}{p_{j}^{(n)}}\right\rfloor}\right]
