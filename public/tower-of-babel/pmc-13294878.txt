Highly Efficient Complete Hydrogenation of (Polystyrene‑b‑Poly(styrene-alt-1,3-pentadiene)‑b‑Polystyrene) Triblock Copolymer Using a Low-Cost Ni-Based Catalyst
Authors: Kun Liu, Xupeng Han, Jiahao Zhou, Yao Long, Min Zhang, Yayan Wang, Fengli Xie, Zhe Xiao, Wenjun Yi, Lijun Li, Yali Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The design and control of the chain architecture are crucial for the development of polyolefins with defined properties. Herein, we synthesized a type of symmetrical and well-defined triblock copolymer (poly­(styrene)-b-Poly­(styrene-alt-1,3-pentadiene)-b-Poly­(styrene), (Sm-b-(SP)n-b-Sm = SSPS)) with gradient compositions F ST and constant molecular weights (M n(designed) = 100 kDa) as well as narrow molecular weight distributions (1.03 < Đ M s < 1.07) by living sequential anionic copolymerization. Subsequently, the highly efficient complete hydrogenation over the low-cost Ni-based catalyst (including Raney nickel and Ni 0.2@Al2O3) was carried out to produce a novel kind of Poly­(vinylcyclohexane)-b-Poly­(vinylcyclohexane-alt-ethylene/propylene)-b-Poly­(vinyl-cyclohexane) (VCHm-(VCH-alt-EP)n-VCHm = fhSSPS) multiblock copolymers containing strictly VCH-alt-EP alternating sequences and with narrow Đ Ms and complete degree of hydrogenation (HD). Moreover, the complete hydrogenation kinetics was investigated in detail and the hydrogenation process conditions (including reaction temperature and time, H2 pressure, polymer concentration, polymer molecular weight, and catalyst dosage) were optimized. In addition, the resulting copolymers before and after complete hydrogenation modification were well characterized by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR), differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), and gel permeation chromatography (GPC). Finally, the effect of the block ratio of these triblock copolymers before and after modification on the impact strength, flexural strength, and yield strength of transparent resin was investigated. The impact strength and flexural strength reached a good balance value at F ST = 0.80 for SSPS, while at F ST = 0.76 for fhSSPS.

Introduction

It is well-known that conjugated dienes such as butadiene (B) and isoprene (I) are representative monomers capable of living anionic polymerization (LAP), as well as styrene (S) and methyl methacrylate (M) under suitable conditions. , This living and controllable polymerization enables the synthesis of a variety of well-defined block copolymers and postmodification functional polymers with designed molecular weight M ns and rather narrow dispersity index Đ Ms. Previously, our group has succeeded in the LAP of 1,3-pentadiene (P) and styrene derivatives, resulting in well-defined homopolymers, , alternating copolymers − and block copolymers, as well as other sequence-controlled copolymers. Interestingly, the polymerization controllability of the two isomers of P (trans-isomer is better than cis-isomer) under homopolymerization conditions is rather different. In contrast, both trans-P and cis-P isomers can undergo copolymerization with Styrene derivatives to give strictly alternating copolymers with predicted M ns and low Đ Ms (<1.10). In addition, the trans-1,4-structure of the P structural unit in poly­(S-alt-P) is superior up to 90%. Moreover, the degree of monomer alternation in the case of copolymerization in the nonpolar solvent (cyclohexane or toluene) is rather high, and the glass transition temperature (T g) of this unique alternating sequence can be easily adjusted by changing the substituent groups of benzene rings as well as some postmodification methods. , As for the pure poly­(P)-derived block sequence, the living nature of anionic polymerization allows the preparation of a new thermoplastic elastomer (SPS like SIS (poly­(S)-b-poly­(I)-b-poly­(S))) by the sequential copolymerization of S and trans-P monomers, which has similar elongation at break and tensile strength as SIS, but much higher hardness. Recently, a novel symmetrical triblock copolymer, poly­(S)-b-poly­(S-alt-P)-b-poly­(S) (SSPS), was synthesized in cyclohexane by the classical three-step sequential feeding method and subsequently modified by epoxidation using m-chloroperoxybenzoic acid in cyclopentyl methyl ether (CPME). Due to the T g (close to room temperature) of the poly­(S-alt-P) middle block, it exhibited the properties of a highly transparent thermoplastic resin below room temperature, whereas it was given classical thermoplastic elastomer properties (similar to SIS) above room temperature. To enhance the mechanical properties of SSPS, optimization work was carried out on the block ratio and molecular weight of SSPS. Although good modification effects were achieved, the heat resistance and oxidation resistance of this resin-like material still need to be further improved. The purpose of this paper is to overcome these defects of the transparent resin while maintaining its excellent mechanical properties through a complete hydrogenation process using a low-cost Ni-based catalyst.

On the other hand, polystyrene is a commercial plastic with significant brittleness and a low T g (≈100 °C), which severely limits its application scenarios. Hydrogenation modification is an important way to improve its thermal performance (Route 2 in Scheme ). In fact, the hydrogenated product (Poly­(vinylcyclohexane), PVCH) has many other interesting properties, such as high modulus and excellent optical properties, but PVCH is still a very brittle material, which further hinders its commercialization. Due to the low reactivity of vinylcyclohexane (VCH) monomer (Route 1 in Scheme ), it is rather difficult to obtain impact-resistant resin materials by copolymerizing VCH with olefins or 1,3-dienes. In contrast, the preparation of prepolymers through controlled sequence polymerization of styrene and 1,3-dienes, followed by an efficient complete hydrogenation strategy, provides a new approach (Route 3 in Scheme ) for customizing tough PVCH copolymers.

Routes to Obtain Poly (VCH) Polymers

Over the past few decades, several studies have proved that this limitation can be overcome by block copolymerization of PVCH with rubber or semicrystalline blocks, including polyethylene, poly­(ethylene-butene), and poly­(ethylene-propylene). More than a decade ago, Gehlsen and Bates first successfully demonstrated a hydrogenation process that does not degrade PS. However, this method requires a large amount of catalysts, making the process uneconomical. Around the same time, Hucul and Hahn developed a significantly more efficient hydrogenation catalyst on a nanoporous silica support by depositing platinum and other transition metals (such as rhenium) in large pores (around 300 Å, as reported). This new catalyst allows almost complete hydrogenation of polystyrene (>99%) without chain degradation, with a total loading (metal and support) of about 0.07 g of catalyst per gram of polymer. Nevertheless, the difficulty lies in the design and control of the sequence structure and the construction of the hydrogenation catalytic system.

At the end of the 20th century, single-site catalyst technology was used by Dow Chemical Company to produce commercialized and cost-effective polyolefin elastomeric materials based on styrene block plastomers and elastomers. Extensive research conducted over the past three decades has focused on improvements in the complete hydrogenation of a class of PVCH-based copolymers. For example, completely hydrogenated styrenic block copolymers (SBS to CEC) reacted over a Pt/Re catalyst supported on macroporous silica particles and exhibited lower shear viscosity compared to partially hydrogenated styrenic block copolymers. In addition, the completely hydrogenated hydrocarbon block copolymer thermoplastic elastomers possessed competitive mechanical properties and attractive processing features. Bates reported that poly­(vinylcyclohexane-ran-ethylene) copolymers, synthesized by random copolymerization of styrene and butadiene followed by heterogeneous catalytic hydrogenation, can toughen isotactic polypropylene. The optically clear blends exhibit superior tensile mechanical properties with up to a 20-fold increase in elongation at break, even when the amount of hydrogenated copolymer is as low as 5%. Bates’ group also described the preparation and complete catalytic hydrogenation of styrene/1,1-diphenylethylene copolymer (with T g up to 200 °C) using supported Pt or Pt/Re catalysts. Overall, these materials add to an expanding portfolio of high-temperature hydrocarbon plastics available in homopolymer or copolymer forms.

As thermoplastic elastomers, styrenic multiblock copolymers (SDSs) possess the mechanical properties of vulcanized rubber and the processing characteristics of thermoplastics, which depend on their microphase separation structure. Their unique segmental architecture, controllable mechanical strength, and ease of processing allow for versatile applications despite some oxidative and environmental stability issues. In addition, selective hydrogenation of the polydiene midblock not only increases the oxidative resistance of the product but also increases the thermal stability of the chain segments due to the disappearance of double bonds. Furthermore, if this type of sequence-controlled copolymer can be completely hydrogenated to fully convert the benzene rings to saturated cyclohexyl groups, it will definitely improve the heat resistance and light transmission of the product. However, what effect would this have on the final mechanical properties of the typical triblock copolymer? In particular, changes in the chemical structure of the middle block affect its compatibility and degree of phase separation, which directly influence the mechanical properties of the material. For example, if the middle block becomes more rigid, this can lead to increased phase separation between the blocks, improving the material’s strength and modulus but potentially reducing its toughness and ductility. Conversely, making the middle block more flexible may improve toughness and ductility but reduce strength and modulus. Ultimately, the specific effect of these chemical modifications on the mechanical properties of the triblock copolymer must be determined through experiments, as the actual effect depends on many factors, including the chemical structure of the blocks, molecular weight, block ratio, and processing conditions.

In this paper, we designed and anionically copolymerized S and P monomers to synthesize a new symmetrical and deformed triblock copolymer, poly­(S)-b-poly­(S-alt-P)-b-poly­(S) (SSPS), in cyclohexane using n-BuLi as an initiator. We also attempted to obtain a novel highly saturated hydrocarbon resin, poly­(vinylcyclohexane)-b-poly­(vinylcyclohexane-alt-ethylene/propylene)-b-poly­(vinylcyclohexane) (fhSSPS) via the subsequent complete hydrogenation of the prepolymer with a predominant linear 1,4-repeating unit, as shown in Scheme . Compared with the common and effective selective hydrogenation of CC double bonds, complete hydrogenation modification is an attractive method for the synthesis of novel saturated polyolefins by introducing cyclohexyl ring structures onto their side chains. How to quickly and effectively convert the benzene ring into a rigid cyclohexyl ring using a low-cost hydrogenation catalyst is an important scientific problem to be solved in polymer chemistry. Therefore, we focus our attention on this postpolymerization modification based on Ni-series catalysts to obtain novel highly transparent thermoplastic resins with a complete degree of hydrogenation (HD). We strongly believe that this approach enables the creation of libraries of functional macromolecules with novel microstructures, sequence distributions, and topologies, allowing the easy and precise study of structure–property relationships.

First, gas chromatographic analysis confirmed that both P and S monomers were completely consumed, indicating an efficient polymerization process. Second, the quantitative 1H NMR analysis (Figure S2) showed that the composition of the synthesized copolymer was consistent with the initial feeding ratio, indicating that the polymerization process was well controlled and that the monomers were incorporated into the polymer in the desired proportions. Moreover, the 1H NMR spectrum revealed distinct peaks for the PS block (δ = 6.2–6.6 ppm) and the alternating structure of P (δ = 4.6–5.1 ppm), confirming that the copolymer has a well-defined block structure with P and S units arranged in a specific order in the middle block. As expected, the presence of alternating linkages between S and P units in the midblock was also confirmed by 13C NMR spectrum (Figure S3) and real-time 1H NMR analysis. This is significant because it demonstrates the precision of the polymerization process in creating the desired alternating structure.

Generally speaking, the preparation process for the SSPS triblock copolymer containing an intermediate alternating block is similar to the traditional synthesis of SBS triblock copolymers. However, the polymerization temperature for the second step, where the alternating copolymerization of S and P occurs, was slightly higher than that for the butadiene segment. This was attributed to the relatively lower reactivity of the S/P alternating copolymerization compared to the B monomer, but it was still comparable to that of the S homopolymerization. ,,

On the other hand, the microstructure of the P units in this novel triblock copolymer was rather different from that of the polypentadiene homopolymer. The P monomer in the alternating midblock was mainly composed of linear 1,4-repeating units, accounting for over 90%, which is about 15%∼20% higher than that of the P-based homopolymer. These significant differences in microstructure may reflect different underlying mechanisms. The S/P (including trans or cis isomers) copolymerization not only produced the unique alternating sequence (as shown in MALDI-TOF MS analysis, Figure S4) but also exhibited perfectly living controllability. The molecular weight was in accordance with the design value, and the molecular weight distribution was in the range of 1.03–1.07.

In summary, the synthesis of this triblock copolymer with a special alternating sequence and a high 1,4-structure offers several advantages for both the material itself and for scientific research. For example, their well-defined structures provide a perfect basis for a systematic investigation of the relationship between structure and performance, which can lead to a deeper understanding of how molecular architecture influences the bulk properties of polymers. In addition, this type of copolymer serves as an ideal model prepolymer for further chemical modifications, as its well-defined structure allows chemists to predict how modifications would affect the properties of the final product. By studying the effects of the alternating sequence and 1,4-structure on the performance of the copolymer, researchers can optimize these features to enhance specific properties such as toughness, heat resistance, or chemical resistance. Thus, the synthesis of this triblock copolymer not only results in a material with potentially superior properties but also provides a valuable tool for advancing polymer science and technology.

The Postmodification StrategyComplete Hydrogenation

Complete Hydrogenation Mechanism

In our previous work, , we focused on the epoxidation of SSPS to produce a unique polar triblock copolymer with a strictly alternating middle block synthesized via the LAP mechanism. Herein, we introduced another postmodification strategy: the complete hydrogenation of the CC bonds and benzene rings using a hydrogenation catalyst to obtain a saturated C–H resin with pendant cyclohexane groups on the side chains. In this part, the commercially supported Ni 0.2@Al2O3 catalyst was used as the hydrogenation agent for heat-resistance modification. Due to the solubility of the polymer, cyclohexane (CH) was chosen as the solvent, and the hydrogenation reaction was carried out in a heterogeneous catalytic system. The symmetrical triblock copolymers with an -(SP)m- alternating midblock (F ST = 80%) together with the SP alternating sequence copolymer were completely hydrogenated at high temperatures of 453–493 K and under high pressure of 4–8 MPa. The complete hydrogenation reaction mechanism of the SSPS triblock copolymer with an SP alternating sequence as the midblock is shown in Scheme . The outer surface of the Ni 0.2@Al2O3 catalyst preferentially adsorbs unsaturated double bonds and benzene rings, activates Ni–H, reacts with the addition of unsaturated bonds, and then desorbs and diffuses into the solution after saturation. Due to the low selectivity of the nickel-supported catalyst, adsorption and hydrogenation occur randomly, so that the CC double bonds of the P units and the benzene rings of the S units are hydrogenated simultaneously. It is very interesting to note that the quantitative hydrogenation efficiency of SSPS easily reaches 100% for both CC bonds and benzene rings. To prevent side reactions such as chain breakage, it is necessary to strictly control the hydrogenation temperature, the concentration of the polymer solution, the hydrogen pressure, and the amount of catalyst. Thus, another saturated triblock copolymer with a novel alternating midblock sequence can be obtained via this efficient postmodification method.

At the beginning of the complete hydrogenation experiments, the effects of known hydrogenation catalysts on the hydrogenation performance of SP alternating copolymers were preliminarily investigated. The initial results showed that supported nickel and Raney nickel catalysts were better choices for complete hydrogenation modification (Figure S5). In addition, as shown in Figure , the complete hydrogenation of SP resin with a molecular weight of 90 kDa was investigated using supported nickel (Figure a) and Raney nickel catalysts (Figure b), respectively, at 210 °C. The hydrogenation pressure was controlled at 6–8 MPa, the polymer concentration was 4.5%, and the amount of catalyst was 1% (1 g catalyst per 4.5 g polymer). The hydrogenation degree (HD) at different reaction times was tracked and determined via real-time 1H NMR spectra. It was found that the hydrogenation rate of the CC double bonds was higher than that of the benzene ring. With the supported nickel catalyst, the HD of the CC bond exceeded 95% within 2 h and reached 98% within 4 h. The HD of the benzene ring was over 97% after 8 h of reaction. With the Raney nickel catalyst, the HD of the CC double bond was similar to that of the benzene ring under the same conditions, indicating that this catalyst has much lower hydrogenation selectivity. Specifically, the HD values for both the CC bond and the benzene ring were over 90% within 4 h and over 97% within 8 h, respectively. However, considering the safety and low cost of the hydrogenation catalyst, the supported nickel catalyst was used in the subsequent process optimization.

Kinetic analysis of the complete hydrogenation of SP at 210 °C: (a) nickel-supported catalyst and (b) Raney nickel (conditions given in the figure).

Although the use of a commercial high-activity supported nickel catalyst can yield completely hydrogenated transparent resin materials within 2 h under conditions exceeding 210 °C, tracking the state of the solution in the reactor revealed that the hydrogenation process goes through a special stratification phase. Density analysis indicated a significant difference between the two layers. In addition, 1H NMR analysis suggested that the top layer of the solution had higher transparency and saturation, while the bottom layer was similar in structure to the original polymer. GPC analysis indicated that the 100% completely hydrogenated product undergoes some degree of chain scission, resulting in a slight broadening of the molecular weight distribution (Figures S6 and S7). This trend increases with increasing polymerization temperature. To ensure that the product does not experience significant chain scission, we found that hydrogenation can be achieved at temperatures below 180 °C, but under these conditions, the hydrogenation efficiency is significantly reduced. Furthermore, we also found that the hydrogenation efficiency of CC double bonds and benzene rings was very close under low-temperature conditions (Figure ), and the catalyst showed almost no selectivity. In contrast, CC double bonds exhibited slightly higher hydrogenation activity at relatively higher temperatures.

Real-time 1H NMR analysis of SP hydrogenation process (a) and kinetic data (b) at 180 °C.

Additionally, it is worth noting that the selective hydrogenation of SBS (Figure S8) and SIS (Figure S9) under similar hydrogenation conditions proved to be inefficient and easily generated a large number of gel-like substances. The reason may be closely related to the alternating sequence of S-alt-P, and the uniform dispersion of CC double bonds is conducive to complete hydrogenation modification.

Hydrogenation Process Optimization

In this part of the work, we mainly discuss the optimization of the complete hydrogenation process of the SP strictly alternating copolymer (Figure a–f). First, we examined the impact of reaction temperature on the complete hydrogenation reaction while controlling the amount of catalytic dosage at 1 g per 100 g of polymer solution, hydrogenation pressure at 6 MPa, polymer concentration at 4.5%, SP alternating polymer M n = 90,000 g/mol, and hydrogenation time at 4 h. It was found that the most suitable temperature was above 210 °C, at which both the benzene rings and CC double bonds reach up to 98% conversion. The HD value at 180 °C was less than 80%, and it dramatically decreased to only 33% at 140 °C. This indicates that the complete hydrogenation temperature has a great influence on the conversion of the unsaturated degree in the polymer, further suggesting that the activation energy of the complete hydrogenation reaction is rather high.

We then optimized the reaction pressure under similar conditions: catalyst amount 1 g per 100 g of polymer solution, hydrogenation temperature at 210 °C, polymer concentration at 4.5%, polymer molecular weight of 90,000 g/mol, and hydrogenation time of 4 h. The results showed that the relationship between the complete hydrogenation reaction and the pressure is closely related. The conversion rate at P H2 = 4 MPa was nearly 90%. To achieve an excellent HD value above 98%, the hydrogen pressure should be controlled above 6 MPa.

Additionally, the amount of the hydrogenation catalyst was also a key factor. For 100 g of polymer solution at 4.5%, the catalyst amount must be controlled above 1 g to reach the ideal complete hydrogenation effect (HD > 98%). The complete hydrogenation effect is greatly reduced with a decrease in the number of hydrogenation activity sites. Interestingly, the impact of the polymer concentration was relatively less obvious. When similar hydrogenation conditions were applied but the polymer concentration was lower than 6.0%, the complete hydrogenation effect was essentially unaffected. However, the efficiency of the complete hydrogenation showed a significant decreasing trend with a further increase in the polymer concentration. The polymer molecular weight had a similar effect. The higher the molecular weight of the polymer, the higher the viscosity of the system. Of course, this introduces certain heat and mass transfer obstacles that will affect the HD value to some extent.

Good catalyst circulation is critical for the industrial realization of the hydrogenation performance of polyolefin materials, because the cost and added value directly determine the feasibility of the process route. From this perspective, Ni-supported series catalysts have a great advantage over those of precious metal catalysts. The results of the three-cycle experiments showed good reproducibility and industrial feasibility.

Using similar hydrogenation conditions, a complete hydrogenation study was conducted on a high molecular weight SSPS triblock copolymer (M n = 237 kDa), and it was found that the hydrogenation degree of benzene rings and CC double bonds was higher than 99% within 4 h (Figure S10). This indicates that this type of catalyst has the same hydrogenation activity for SP alternating sequence and alternating-block sequence copolymers, making it suitable for the synthesis of hydrogenated polyolefin products.

Complete Hydrogenation Products: FTIR and DSC Analysis

SSPS triblock copolymers with different HD were analyzed and characterized by FTIR, and the results are shown in Figure . In the middle alternating sequences, P mainly existed as a trans-1,4-structure in the main chain. A comparative study showed that the absorption peak strength of the CC double bond at 967 cm–1 in the corresponding infrared spectrum gradually weakened and disappeared with increasing HD. Moreover, the characteristic absorption bands at 800–670 cm–1 (blue region) and 2000–1700 cm–1 (gray region), corresponding to the monosubstituted benzene ring, were completely absent when HD > 99%, together with the bands at 3130–3030 cm–1 (pink region), 550 cm–1 (red region), and 1600 cm–1 (yellow region), all attributed to unsaturated bonds, including CC bonds and benzene rings. All these bands disappeared when the complete hydrogenation process was completed. The above infrared results, combined with the 1H NMR results, indicate the successful synthesis of a completely hydrogenated triblock copolymer.

FTIR spectrum of SSPS triblock copolymers before and after complete hydrogenation

To determine whether the complete hydrogenation modification had any effect on the thermal stability of the novel alternating and triblock copolymers, DSC analysis was carried out on the products before and after hydrogenation. For the S-alt-P alternating copolymer, Figure shows that the glass transition temperature (T g) of the hydrogenated copolymer was slightly lower than that of the corresponding alternating prepolymer. Although the thermal stability was significantly improved after hydrogenation of the pendant benzene ring side groups, the flexibility of the main chain also increased after hydrogenation of the CC bonds in the P units. The overall results showed that the magnitude of change on both sides was similar, resulting in an improvement in the flexibility of the polymer.

DSC of the SP (a) before and after complete hydrogenation and SSPS (b) after complete hydrogenation.

As for the SSPS triblock copolymers, the T g of the PS end-block was rather difficult to determine, especially in the case of F ST < 70%. However, the T g of the corresponding S-alt-P alternating sequence was about 30 °C before complete hydrogenation. After modification, the T g corresponding to the completely hydrogenated alternating sequence with 99% HD value was only 16.38 °C. In contrast, another T g corresponding to the poly­(vinyl cyclohexane) block sequence increased dramatically to 130.75 °C, which was much higher than that of the PS block sequence. These results showed that the change in HD had an obvious influence on the thermal stability of the completely hydrogenated product due to the formation of rigid pendant cyclohexane groups on the side chains. Meanwhile, the completely hydrogenated product maintained much more excellent transparency and good toughness; of course, the composition of the P monomer should be controlled above 24%. The completely hydrogenated product showed higher light transmission properties (T% up to 90% at F ST = 0.76), which may be due to its refractive index being similar to that of poly­(vinyl cyclohexane) and ethylene-alt-propylene copolymer.

Mechanical Property Analysis

To investigate the relationship between the sequence structure and mechanical properties of the novel symmetrical triblock copolymers, a series of triblock copolymers with different block ratios and styrene compositions (F ST) were designed and synthesized. The influence of their impact resistance and flexural strength before and after complete hydrogenation is clearly shown in Figure . Simply speaking, the linear symmetrical triblock copolymers had excellent impact resistance and transparency, and the unnotched Izod impact strength reached up to 3.91 kJ/m2 when the copolymer composition was F ST = 80%. In addition, the flexural strength was highly dependent on the polystyrene composition, F ST. When F ST exceeded 80%, the flexural strength was close to 25 MPa, which was comparable to that of the commercial K resin. Although the yield strength of the SSPS with F ST = 85% was above 27 MPa, the impact strength decreased dramatically. In general, the balance point of each performance should be around 80% of the styrene content, that is, the proportion of alternating sequences was about 50%. As for the completely hydrogenated products, it was evident that the reduction in the glass transition temperature after alternating block hydrogenation in the middle was beneficial for improving the toughness of the product. However, the poly­(VCH) blocks at both ends significantly improved the flexural strength compared to that of the unhydrogenated series with similar F ST. In contrast to the SSPS prepolymer, the toughness reached its maximum at F ST = 76% while maintaining an excellent mechanical strength of 24 MPa.

Conclusions

Polystyrene-b-poly­(styrene-alt-1,3-pentadiene)-b-polystyrene (SSPS) triblock copolymer, together with the strictly alternating copolymer poly­(styrene-alt-1,3-pentadiene) (SP), was synthesized by LAP and then subsequently underwent complete hydrogenation to afford a novel high-performance thermoplastic resin (poly­(vinylcyclohexane)-b-poly­(vinylcyclohexane-alt-ethylene/propylene)-b-poly­(vinylcyclohexane), fhSSPS) using a commercial nickel-supported catalyst. The low-cost Ni0.2Al2O3 catalyst exhibited excellent hydrogenation activity in the complete hydrogenation of SSPS and SP prepolymers, with HD values for benzene rings and CC double bonds reaching up to 99% within several hours under relatively mild reaction temperature (180–210 °C), with a low amount of catalyst (0.2–0.4 g cat/g polymer) and a high concentration of PS (4.5–6.0 wt % in CH solution). This work analyzed the hydrogenation kinetics through real-time 1H NMR tracing, while GPC analysis showed that although the low-temperature hydrogenation rate was relatively slower, the side reaction of polymer chain degradation could be completely avoided. The comprehensive mechanical performance indicated that the mechanical strength and toughness of the SSPS triblock copolymer reached an optimal balance when the styrene content F ST was 80%. As for the completely hydrogenated series, although the mechanical strength was significantly better than that of the SSPS, the toughness was at an optimal value when F ST was 76%. Generally, this work has significant practical value for developing new thermoplastic resins for medical applications, optical materials, and high-end packaging materials. At the same time, we are also very interested in studying the degradation behavior at high temperatures. In the next stage, we will directly investigate the degradation behavior of SSPS under high-temperature conditions.
