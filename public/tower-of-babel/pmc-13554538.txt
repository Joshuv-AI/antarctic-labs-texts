Emergency Department Utilization after Initiation of Intermittent Hemodialysis: A Retrospective Cohort Study in Regina, Saskatchewan
Authors: Aarti Garg, Arghya Podder, Peter Smylie, Shirmin Kader, Ahmed M Soliman, Bhanu Prasad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmergency department (ED) use is common among patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) initiating hemodialysis (HD), but potentially modifiable predictors of these visits remain poorly defined.ObjectiveTo identify factors associated with ED visits among patients newly initiated on intermittent HD in Regina, Saskatchewan, Canada.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingRegina General Hospital in Saskatchewan, Canada.Patients360 incident adult patients who received at least 90 days of HD between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2022.MeasurementsPatients were followed for one year from dialysis initiation to capture the number and causes of all-cause ED visits.MethodsMultinomial logistic regression was used to examine associations between patient characteristics and ED utilization, categorized as no visits, a single visit, 2–3 visits, or >3 visits, with no visits as the reference group.ResultsHalf of the cohort (n=180, 50%) had at least one ED visit during the first year, totaling 453 encounters. Gastrointestinal (GI) complaints were the most common reason for presentation (14.1%), followed by fluid overload (11.5%) and cardiovascular causes (8.3%). Dialysis non-adherence was independently associated with ED utilization across all categories, with the strongest association for >3 visits (RRR 7.34, 95% CI 2.81-19.20, p<0.001). Arteriovenous fistula use at dialysis initiation was associated with a lower risk of a single ED visit (RRR 0.28, 95% CI 0.09-0.90, p=0.033). In contrast, age, sex, diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease were not independently associated with ED utilization.LimitationsThis single-center, retrospective study is limited by generalizability and could not analyze non-adherence factors. ED visits were grouped broadly, without assessing severity or preventability. Although ED visits were more frequent during the winter months, we did not examine whether missed dialysis treatments also varied by season. The study period from 2018 to 2022 overlaps with the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have affected ED utilization, dialysis attendance, outpatient access, and patient health-seeking behavior. This limits direct comparison with studies conducted entirely before or after the pandemic.ConclusionsEarly ED utilization among incident HD patients may be reduced by improving dialysis adherence, ensuring timely permanent vascular access placement, and better management of GI symptoms.Keywords: incident hemodialysis, dialysis adherence, vascular access, arteriovenous fistula, end-stage kidney disease

Introduction

Patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) initiating hemodialysis (HD) are often older, frail, and burdened with multiple comorbidities.1,2 Though they represent less than 0.1% of the Canadian population, dialysis patients place a disproportionate demand on acute care resources. They present to the emergency department (ED) 8.5 times more frequently than age- and sex- matched controls, with ED utilization increasing nearly tenfold around dialysis initiation.1,3 The cumulative impact of this repeated acute care use is substantial. According to a national estimate, ESKD care in Canada accounted for approximately $1.3 billion in direct healthcare expenditures in the year 2000, with total costs reaching $1.9 billion when indirect costs related to morbidity and mortality were included. 4

The high rate of ED utilization in this population reflects the clinical complexity of kidney failure and the risks inherent to dialysis therapy. Admission rates are often driven by complications intrinsic to ESKD, such as electrolyte disturbances, volume overload, and accelerated cardiovascular disease, along with treatment-specific complications such as vascular access dysfunction, intradialytic hypotension, and infection.5-7 The conventional thrice-weekly HD schedule further contributes to risk, as the 72-hour interdialytic interval allows accumulation of fluid and uremic solutes, contributing to predictable peaks in adverse events observed on Monday and Tuesday.8,9 In addition to physiological factors, non-adherence to treatment and social barriers, including transportation difficulties, limited health literacy, and inadequate support systems, are important contributors to avoidable ED encounters.

Although ED utilization among dialysis patients has been described in other Canadian and US cohorts, local program-level data remain important because dialysis delivery, geography, transportation barriers, rurality, and access to pre-dialysis care vary across regions.1,10,11 Saskatchewan shares several features with Manitoba, including a dispersed population, rural and remote dialysis catchment areas, and a substantial burden of travel for many patients. However, Saskatchewan-specific data on ED use after HD initiation have not been well described. This study aims to identify factors associated with ED visits among patients newly initiated on intermittent HD in Regina, Saskatchewan, Canada. By focusing on an incident HD cohort and using chart-level review of ED presentations, this study aimed to identify local patterns and potentially modifiable factors that could inform quality improvement within our dialysis program.

Methods

Study Design and Population

We conducted a retrospective observational cohort study using existing chart data for patients who initiated intermittent HD between 2018 and 2022 at the main dialysis unit of Regina General Hospital, Saskatchewan, Canada. We restricted the cohort to incident maintenance HD patients because dialysis initiation is a clinically important transition associated with high acute care use. Starting from a common index date allowed uniform one-year follow-up and assessment of early factors such as initial vascular access type and dialysis adherence. Including prevalent patients would have introduced heterogeneity related to dialysis vintage, access history, comorbidity burden, and survival bias. This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines and was approved by the Saskatchewan Health Authority Research Ethics Board (REB 23-41).

We included all incident adult HD patients (≥18 years) who received at least 90 days of HD between January 1st, 2018, and December 31st, 2022. Our unit operates three shifts per day, seven days per week. We excluded patients younger than 18 years and those receiving short-term HD for acute kidney injury. All eligible patients were followed for one year from the date of HD initiation to capture the number and reasons for ED visits.

Descriptive and Dialysis-Related Variables

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics at HD initiation were obtained from electronic health records and included age, sex, and comorbid conditions, such as diabetes, hypertension, smoking status, cardiovascular disease, cancer, and liver disease. Dialysis-related variables included the cause of ESKD, type of vascular access at initiation, prescribed dialysis frequency, adherence to dialysis treatments, and chronic kidney disease-related laboratory parameters, including lipids, hemoglobin, phosphate, calcium, and parathyroid hormone. The primary reason for each ED visit was determined from the ED discharge summary. Where available, the final ED impression or discharge diagnosis was used rather than the triage complaint, as it reflected the clinical picture after investigations and treatment. The triage complaint was used only when discharge documentation was incomplete or nonspecific. Each visit was then grouped into predefined clinical categories relevant to HD patients. The number of ED visits were also recorded.

Outcome Variables

Each patient was followed for one year from HD initiation to determine the number and reasons for all-cause ED visits, and whether the visit resulted in hospitalization. We followed patients for one year after HD initiation as this is a high-risk period when patients are adjusting to treatment, vascular access may still be maturing, and early complications are most common. Extending follow-up beyond one year would have introduced additional variability from changes in access type, comorbidity burden, transplantation, or death. Patients could experience multiple events during follow-up. Deaths occurring during follow-up were also recorded.

Definition

All-cause emergency visit: Each ED visit was reviewed and assigned a primary reason for presentation based on clinical judgment, using a predefined classification tailored to HD patients. (Supplementary Table 1). The categories included electrolyte abnormalities, gastrointestinal (GI)-related causes, fluid-related complications, vascular access-related issues, infections, musculoskeletal causes, neurological conditions, genitourinary causes, falls/syncope, cardiovascular events, endocrine causes, vascular conditions, pulmonary causes, other medical issues, ear, nose, and throat (ENT)-related complaints, social concerns or non-compliance, drug-related problems, and psychiatric causes. Data on ED visits were obtained from Sunrise Clinical Manager, the provincial electronic health record, capturing all clinical encounters in Saskatchewan.

Dialysis schedule: The dialysis schedule was defined according to the initial recorded prescription at HD initiation and categorized as once weekly, twice weekly, three times weekly or more than three times weekly.

Seasonal variation was defined a priori as winter (November- March), spring (April- May), summer (June- August), and fall (September- October).

Compliance with dialysis treatments: Dialysis adherence was defined as attendance at and completion of prescribed HD sessions without missed or shortened treatments. Poor adherence was defined as missing two or more dialysis sessions per month. Adherence was assessed over a one-year follow-up. Adherence data were obtained from dialysis unit attendance records.

Type of vascular access: Vascular access at HD initiation was categorized as tunneled catheter, arteriovenous fistula (AV fistula), or non-tunneled catheter based on the primary access used at the start of dialysis.

Statistical Analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables were summarized as the mean with standard deviation, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test, and normally distributed continuous measures using Student’s t-test. Missing data were assessed for all variables. The variables with less than 5% missing data were handled using complete case analysis, and those with more than 5% missing data were handled with multiple imputation under the assumption that data were missing at random.

Multinomial logistic regression analysis was used to examine factors independently associated with ED utilization. ED visits were categorized as no visit, single visit, 2-3 visits, and more than 3 visits, with “no visit” as the reference category. A multinomial model was selected to allow estimation of factors associated with increasing levels of ED utilization without assuming proportional effects across outcome categories. Covariates were selected a priori and included age, sex, smoking status, dialysis compliance, comorbidities, and type of vascular access. Adjusted estimates are reported as a relative risk ratio with a 95% confidence interval (CI). Model assumptions and multicollinearity were assessed prior to model estimation. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using StataNow 18.5 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA).

Results

Demographic Characteristics

Of 404 adults who initiated intermittent HD between 2018 and 2022, 360 met the inclusion criteria and were included in this analysis. Half of the cohort (n=180, 50%) had at least one ED visit within the first year of dialysis initiation. The mean age was 62.7 ± 15.0 years; nearly half of the patients were aged ≥65 years (49.4%), followed by those aged 45–64 years (38.9%) and 18–44 years (11.7%). Females comprised 60.6% of the study population, and the distribution of ED visits did not significantly differ by sex. Among the patients with no ED visits, around 62% were non-smokers, compared to current and former smokers (p = 0.001) (Table 1).

Among patients with no ED visits in the first year after HD initiation, around 89% of patients had good dialysis adherence. ED utilization varied by vascular access type; among patients with >3 ED visits, 59% had started dialysis with non-tunneled catheters, and 38.5% with tunneled catheters. ED use was lowest among patients with AV fistula, with only 2.6% having more than three visits, and most had no visits (68.1%; p = 0.038). Diabetic nephropathy was the most common cause of ESKD (43.3%) and did not differ by ED utilization (p = 0.583) (Table 1).

The most common comorbidities were hypertension (n=292, 81.1%), diabetes mellitus (n=225, 62.5%), dyslipidemia (n=234, 65%), coronary artery disease (n=104, 28.9%), and peripheral arterial disease (n=83, 23.1%). None of these conditions were significantly associated with ED utilization. However, GI disease was significantly associated with higher ED use (p = 0.047) (Supplementary Table 2). Among the 360 patients included in this analysis, 155 (43.1%) initiated dialysis with a tunneled catheter, 158 (43.9%) with a non-tunneled catheter, and 47 (13.1%) with an AV fistula. Subgroup analysis by vascular access type showed that infection-related ED visits were highest in patients with tunneled catheters (12.3%), followed by non-tunneled catheters (10.1%), while AV fistula patients had the lowest infection rate (2.1%).

Reasons for ED Use

Among 180 patients who visited the ED, there were 453 encounters during the one-year follow-up period. The most frequent reasons for presentation were GI-related (n=64, 14.1%), followed by fluid overload (11.5%), cardiovascular causes (8.3%), infections (8.3%), and syncopal episodes or vertigo (7.9%) (Figure 1).

Dialysis Adherence

Of the 360 patients, 287 (80.2%) demonstrated high adherence to their prescribed HD schedules. This group had significantly lower ED utilization, with 89.9% reporting no ED visits (p < 0.001). In contrast, among patients with non-adherence (n = 71, 19.8%), ED use was higher across all categories, including a greater proportion with more than three visits (18, 46.2%, p < 0.001) (Table 1). There was a significant association between the number of scheduled dialysis sessions per week and treatment adherence (p = 0.004). Poor adherence increased as the number of scheduled sessions increased, rising from 5.3% among patients prescribed once-weekly dialysis to 41.4% among those scheduled for more than three sessions per week (Table 2).

In multivariable multinomial logistic regression, dialysis non-adherence was independently associated with higher ED utilization across all visit categories. Compared to adherent patients, non-adherent patients had a higher relative risk of a single ED visit (RRR 3.44, p=0.002), 2-3 visits (RRR 2.35, p=0.034), and >3 visits (RRR 7.34, p<0.001), indicating a graded relationship between poor dialysis adherence and increasing ED utilization.

The type of vascular access was associated with ED utilization for single visits. Using non-tunneled as the reference category, patients who initiated dialysis with an AV fistula had a significantly lower relative risk of a single ED visit (RRR 0.28, 95% CI 0.09-0.90, p=0.033). However, this association was not significant for a higher number of ED visits.

Smoking status was associated with frequent ED utilization. Patients in the past smoking category had a higher relative risk of >3 ED visits (RRR 2.41, p=0.040). GI disease was strongly associated with frequent ED use (>3 visits) (RRR 7.86, p=0.012). Age group, sex, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and coronary artery disease were not significantly associated with ED visits across any visit category.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of incident HD patients in Regina, Saskatchewan, ED utilization during the first year following dialysis initiation was substantial. Half of the patients visited the ED at least once during their first year on dialysis. Interestingly, dialysis non-adherence and vascular access type drove ED utilization, rather than traditional risk factors like age, sex, or comorbidities. This suggests that early ED visits may be partly preventable through better vascular access planning and closer attention to dialysis adherence.

Among 180 patients who visited the ED, there were 453 encounters during the one-year follow-up. GI complaints were the most common reason for presentation, followed by fluid overload and cardiovascular issues. This pattern differs from large administrative database studies, where cardiovascular conditions tend to dominate. Of these 453 ED visits, 30 were for fluid overload and 8 for hyperkalemia, both considered potentially avoidable. By comparison, an Alberta study found hyperkalemia and heart failure as the most common preventable ED presentations. 6 Similarly, a US HD cohort showed cardiovascular and infection-related visits as leading causes, especially after the long interdialytic interval. 9

However, our findings align with the literature that has shown a high burden of GI symptoms in dialysis patients. A systematic review of over 5,000 patients found that constipation, indigestion, abdominal pain, and reflux were the most common symptoms. 12 Compared to matched controls, dialysis patients have significantly higher rates of abdominal pain, laxative use, functional vomiting, and irritable bowel syndrome. Gastritis and gastroesophageal reflux are also frequent in this population. 13 Our data suggest that GI complaints are a real driver of ED utilization and point to an underrecognized opportunity to address these symptoms more effectively during routine HD care.

Non-adherence was the strongest predictor of ED utilization in our study. Patients with poor adherence had a 7-fold higher relative risk of frequent ED use (>3 visits). Missed sessions cause volume overload, electrolyte abnormalities, and uremic symptoms, all of which may lead to ED presentations. A US study of 44 million HD treatments found that the risk of ED visits doubled within two days of a missed session (OR 2.00; 95% CI, 1.87–2.14). 14 The Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study (DOPPS) similarly showed the same pattern, with higher hospitalization rates for patients who missed treatments. 15 Our retrospective design meant we were unable to identify causes of non-adherence in our study. However, prior studies have shown several modifiable factors, such as transportation barriers, inclement weather, psychiatric illness, pain, and GI symptoms, as modifiable factors.14,16,17

We also observed that dialysis adherence declined as the number of prescribed weekly treatments increased. This finding should be interpreted cautiously. It may reflect confounding by indication, as patients prescribed more frequent dialysis may have been clinically more unstable or had greater comorbidity, volume overload, or biochemical derangements. Alternatively, more frequent dialysis schedules may themselves increase treatment burden, with greater demands on transportation, time, caregiver support, and patient motivation. Our retrospective dataset could not distinguish between these explanations. Future studies should examine the clinical and social reasons for non-adherence.

Several interventions have been shown to reduce non-adherence and its downstream effects. Transportation challenges have been linked to more missed treatments and increased mortality in HD patients. 18 Psychosocial interventions, including cognitive behavioral therapy delivered during dialysis sessions, have been shown to improve fluid adherence and reduce depression. 19 A meta-analysis of 25 randomized controlled trials found that adherence programs worked best when they included educational interventions and ran for at least 12 sessions. 20 Digital engagement strategies, such as automated text messaging and digital engagement, have improved patient appointments and reduced unintended hospitalization. Together, these studies support a combined approach that addresses transportation access, mental health, and patient engagement. 21

Vascular access type was also a significant predictor of ED use. Patients who started dialysis with an AV fistula have nearly 80% lower relative risk of a single ED visit compared to those with a non-tunneled catheter. This finding is consistent with prior studies showing that non-tunneled catheters had significantly higher rates of complications compared to AV fistula. 22 A study from the Dominican Republic found that patients with non-tunneled catheters were 85.5 times more likely to be hospitalized than those with AV fistula. 23 In our cohort, infection-related ED visits were highest with tunneled catheters (12.3%), followed by non-tunneled catheters (10.1%), and lowest with AV fistulas (2.1%). This finding is consistent with prior studies demonstrating that catheter-based vascular access, regardless of tunneled or untunneled, carries a substantially higher risk of infection compared to AV fistulas. Data from the National HealthCare Safety Network, which included 6,005 outpatient facilities, reported bloodstream infection rates for non-tunneled catheters of 2.16 per 1000 patient months compared to 0.26 for AV fistulas. 24 Similarly, a Scottish cohort study found infection rates of 3.11 per 1,000 HD days for non-tunneled catheters, 1.10 for tunneled catheters, and 0.29 for AV fistulas. 25 These findings underscore the importance of transitioning patients from catheter-based access to AV fistulas when feasible to reduce infection-related complications and ED utilization.

Several evidence-based strategies can reduce reliance on non-tunneled catheters at dialysis initiation. More intensive predialysis nephrology care has been associated with increased permanent access placement and reduced two-year mortality. 26 The KDOQI 2019 guidelines recommend vein preservation strategies through limiting venipuncture and avoiding peripherally inserted central catheters in CKD patients. 27 Predialysis education in multidisciplinary clinics has doubled the likelihood of initiating dialysis with permanent access.28,29 Additionally, preoperative ultrasound vein mapping, early endovascular intervention, and external support devices have all contributed to improved AV fistula maturation and cannulation rates.30,31 Together, these strategies highlight the importance of early nephrology referral and proactive vascular access planning to reduce catheter dependence at dialysis initiation and potentially lower ED utilization.

The association between AV fistula use and lower ED utilization should be interpreted cautiously. While this may partly reflect fewer catheter-related complications, AV fistula use at dialysis initiation may also be a marker of earlier nephrology referral, better pre-dialysis planning, and a less urgent dialysis start. Conversely, patients initiating dialysis with catheters may have been more clinically unstable or less prepared for dialysis. Our retrospective dataset could not separate the effect of access type itself from these upstream factors. Future studies should examine whether lower ED utilization among patients with AV fistulas is driven by the access, the pre-dialysis care pathway, or differences in patient complexity at dialysis initiation.

Beyond patient-level factors, dialysis scheduling and environmental conditions influence ED utilization patterns. In our cohort, ED visits peaked on Tuesday and Friday, with the highest visits occurring on Tuesday for Monday, Wednesday, Friday (MWF) patients and Friday for Tuesday, Thursday, Saturday (TTS) patients. This pattern differs from larger studies that show the highest ED utilization following the long 2-day interdialytic interval. A Canadian study found ED utilization was 33.6% higher on Mondays and 19.5% higher on Tuesdays. 1 A US study of 241,093 patients identified a consistent “post-weekend effect” and “interdialytic-gap effect”, with the highest rates occurring after the long interval. 9 This discrepancy likely reflects our smaller sample size and shorter follow-up and needs to be tested in larger cohorts.

Although dialysis non-adherence and vascular access type were associated with ED utilization, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that the ED visits were preventable. We did not classify individual ED encounters as preventable or non-preventable, and the reasons behind missed treatments or catheter-based starts were not fully captured. Poor adherence may reflect illness burden, transportation barriers, psychosocial factors, or other structural determinants rather than patient choice alone. Similarly, AV fistula use may be a marker of earlier nephrology engagement and more comprehensive pre-dialysis preparation rather than a direct protective factor against ED utilization. These associations should therefore be viewed as signals warranting further investigation and potential quality-improvement efforts, rather than as evidence of preventable ED use.

Our findings should be interpreted in the context of prior Canadian and US studies showing high ED utilization among dialysis patients. The novelty of this study is not that dialysis patients use the ED frequently, but that it provides Saskatchewan-specific, incident HD data with chart-level classification of ED presentations. This is relevant because program structure, geography, transportation, rurality, and access to pre-dialysis care may influence both missed treatments and ED use. The findings are therefore most useful as local quality-improvement data, identifying areas where intervention may be possible, including dialysis adherence, vascular access planning, GI symptom management, and winter-related barriers to care.

Our study has several strengths. It was conducted within Canada’s universal healthcare system, which reduced selection bias and captured nearly all incident HD patients regardless of socioeconomic status. By focusing on incident rather than prevalent HD patients, we avoided survival bias and captured the high-risk period following dialysis initiation. Manual chart review gave us better clinical detail than administrative datasets that rely on diagnostic codes alone. The use of multinomial regression enabled us to look at graded ED utilization and separate predictors of frequent versus occasional ED use.

Our study has several limitations. The single-center, retrospective design limits generalizability to different dialysis practices or patient populations. Our retrospective design prevented us from detailed analysis of factors that drove non-adherence, such as transportation barriers, psychiatric illness, or socioeconomic circumstances. A further limitation of this study is that ED visits were categorized by broad clinical groups instead of by severity or preventability. For example, within the GI category, visits can range from severe conditions like GI bleeding or pancreatitis that require hospital admission, to less critical issues such as nausea or abdominal pain without a clear diagnosis. Our analysis did not differentiate between preventable visits and those that require urgent care. Future studies should categorize ED visits by severity and admission status, as well as examine factors predicting potentially preventable visits.

Although ED visits were more frequent during the winter months, we did not examine whether missed dialysis treatments also varied by season. This is an important limitation, particularly in Saskatchewan, where winter weather, transportation barriers, and long travel distances may affect dialysis attendance. The study period from 2018 to 2022 overlaps with the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have affected ED utilization, dialysis attendance, outpatient access, and patient health-seeking behavior. This limits direct comparison with studies conducted entirely before or after the pandemic.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort of incident HD patients, ED utilization in the first year was common, with half of the patients having at least one visit. Non-adherence was the strongest predictor of ED use, while initiation with an AV fistula was associated with low risk of early ED utilization. The traditional risk factors, including age, sex, diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease, were not independently associated. GI complaints were the predominant presenting diagnoses, with a seasonal winter peak. These findings suggest that early ED utilization in new dialysis patients can be reduced by implementing comprehensive predialysis programs emphasizing permanent vascular access placement, multimodal interventions addressing modifiable causes of non-adherence, and proactive symptom management during routine care.
