On bounds of entropy and total curvature for ancient curve shortening flows
Wei-Bo Su, Kai-Wei Zhao

Abstract
. Bounds of total curvature and entropy are two common conditions placed on mean curvature flows. We show that these two hypotheses are equivalent for the class of ancient complete embedded smooth planar curve shortening flows, which are one-dimensional mean curvature flows. As an application, we give a short proof of the uniqueness of tangent flow at infinity of an ancient smooth complete non-compact curve shortening flow with finite entropy embedded in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} .



1.
Introduction

A family of planar curves
M_{t}
is a solution to the
curve shortening flow
(CSF) if there exists a smooth family of immersions
\gamma:\mathbb{L}\times I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
I\subset\mathbb{R}
is an interval and
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, such that
M_{t}=\gamma(\mathbb{L},t)
and satisfies the evolution equation

(1)

\gamma_{t}=\gamma_{ss}=\boldsymbol{\kappa}=\kappa\mathbf{n}.

Throughout this paper,
s
denotes the arc-length parameter of a planar curve
\gamma
,
\mathbf{t}=\gamma_{s}
is the unit tangent vector and
\mathbf{n}=J\mathbf{t}
is the normal vector, where
J:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the counterclockwise rotation by an angle of
\pi/2
, and
\kappa=\langle\gamma_{ss},\mathbf{n}\rangle
is the (signed) curvature.
We say that a flow
\mathcal{M}=\cup_{t\in I}(M_{t},t)
is
ancient
if
(-\infty,t_{0})\subset I
for some
t_{0}
. Simple examples of ancient flows are self-similarly shrinking circles and translating grim-reapers. See
[
1
,
8
,
2
,
4
,
16
]
for more complicated examples.

We say that a curve shortening flow
\mathcal{M}
has
finite total curvature
if

(2)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in I}\,\int_{M_{t}}\lvert\kappa\rvert\,ds<+\infty.

For closed curves, it is well known that the total curvature is non-increasing along the curve shortening flow according to the first variation formula
[
12
, Section 4.3 page 99]
:

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{M_{t}}\lvert\kappa\rvert\,ds=-2\sum_{p\in M_{t}:\kappa(p,t)=0}\lvert\kappa_{s}(p,t)\rvert\,ds.

Here, the set
\{p\in M_{t}:\kappa(p,t)=0\}
is finite by Sturm’s theory proved by Angenent
[
3
]
(see also Subsection
2.1
for summary).

Next, we recall Colding-Minicozzi’s
Entropy

[
6
]
.
We denote the
Gaussian weighted length functional

F_{x_{0},\lambda}
at center
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and scale
\sqrt{\lambda}
(
\lambda>0
) for a curve
\Gamma
by

(4)

\displaystyle F_{x_{0},\lambda}[\Gamma]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\lambda}}\int_{\Gamma}e^{-\frac{\lvert x-x_{0}\rvert^{2}}{4\lambda}}\ ds.

Then the entropy of
\Gamma
is defined as

(5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ent}[\Gamma]=\sup_{x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\lambda>0}F_{x_{0},\lambda}[\Gamma].

If
\mathcal{M}=\cup_{t\in I}(M_{t},t)
is a curve shortening flow, we define the
Entropy
of
\mathcal{M}
by

(6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ent}[\mathcal{M}]:=\sup_{t\in I}\mathrm{Ent}[M_{t}],

and we say that an ancient flow
\mathcal{M}
has
finite-entropy
if
\mathrm{Ent}[\mathcal{M}]<+\infty
.
One important motivation to define
F
-functional and entropy is the well known monotonicity formula found by Huisken
[
10
]
: for any spacetime point
X_{0}=(x_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
, for any
t<t_{0}

(7)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{M_{t}}\Phi_{X_{0}}(x,t)\ ds\leq-\int\left\lvert\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\frac{(x-x_{0})^{\perp}}{2(t_{0}-t)}\right\rvert^{2}\Phi_{X_{0}}(x,t)\ ds\ .

Here,
\Phi_{X_{0}}(x,t)=\left(4\pi(t_{0}-t)\right)_{+}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\big(-\frac{\lvert x-x_{0}\rvert^{2}}{4(t_{0}-t)}\big)
is the backward heat kernel where
(\cdot)_{+}=\max\{\cdot,0\}
.
Note that the integral on the left-hand side of the monotonicity formula is exactly
F_{x_{0},(t_{0}-t)}[M_{t}]
.
Thus,
t\mapsto\mathrm{Ent}[M_{t}]
is non-increasing (see
[
6
, Lemma 1.11]
for detail.)

For any immersed
C^{2}
planar curve, finite total curvature implies finite entropy (Theorem
3.3
).
The converse is generally not true. For instance, the graph of
y=\sin(x)
and the logarithmic spiral have finite entropy but infinite total curvature (see the appendix for the detail).
Nevertheless, we show that when restricting to the class of ancient complete smooth embedded curve shortening flows, these two conditions are equivalent.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose
\mathcal{M}
is an ancient complete smooth embedded curve shortening flow. Then
\mathcal{M}
has finite total curvature if and only if
\mathcal{M}
has finite entropy.

Angenent and You
[
4
]
constructed examples of ancient solutions by gluing together a finite number of grim reapers, called the
ancient trombones
. They showed that their ancient trombones have finite total curvature. In the
2
-dimensional case, Lambert–Lotay–Schulze
[
11
]
showed that the special Lagrangian surfaces (considered as static ancient solutions) with finitely many planar ends have finite total curvature, by utilizing the correspondence between special Lagrangians and algebraic curves in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
via hyperkähler rotations. It would be interesting to investigate the relation between the entropy and the total curvature in higher dimensions.

Assuming well-definedness and boundedness of Lagrangian angle, Neves
[
13
]
used the Huisken’s monotonicity formula to argue that the tangent flows of a Lagrangian mean curvature flow must be a union of Lagrangian cones
C_{1},\cdots,C_{N}
with a
unique
set of constant angles
\overline{\theta}_{1},\cdots,\overline{\theta}_{N}
.
Similar argument was used by Lambert–Lotay–Schulze
[
11
]
to show the analogous result for tangent flow at infinity for ancient Lagrangian mean curvature flows.
In curve shortening flow, namely the
1
-dimensional Lagrangian mean curvature flow, the uniqueness of the set of constant Lagrangian angles of the tangent flow directly leads to the uniqueness of tangent flow. Using the simple fact that finite total curvature implies the boundedness of the Lagrangian angle, we give a short proof of
[
5
, Theorem 1.1]
in the complete non-compact case:

Corollary 1.2
.

The tangent flow at infinity of a smooth, complete, non-compact, embedded ancient solution of the curve shortening flow with finite entropy is unique.

This paper is organized as follows: In Section 2, we recall Sturm’s theory and its applications to geometric functions: the squared-distance function and the angle function. In addition, we recall the rough convergence theorem
2.7
from
[
5
]
that gives the sheeting structure for every very-old time-slice of CSF in our setting.
The combination of Strum’s theory and the rough convergence theorem gives a uniform bound of the number of local minimum/maximum points of the squared-distance functions (Proposition
2.8
).
This is the most crucial observation in this paper.
In Section 3.1, we prove that the finite total curvature condition implies the finite entropy condition for curves (Theorem
3.3
).
In Section 3.2, we prove the bound of the number of preimages of (mod
\pi
)-angle function (Proposition
3.6
).
The proof involves a contradiction argument together with the uniform bound of local maximum points of squared-distance functions (Proposition
2.8
) and a point selection trick (Lemma
3.4
) to find a squared-distance function that violates the bound.
The control of preimages of (mod
\pi
)-angle function allows us to find the bound of the total curvature for finite entropy CSF in our setting (Theorem
3.7
) and a uniform bound of angle function for the non-compact case (Corollary
3.8
).
In Section 4, we give a short proof of the uniqueness of the tangent flow for the non-compact case (Theorem
4.1
) by employing the uniform bound of the angle function.

2.1.
Sturm’s Theory

For a function
u:[A_{1},A_{2}]\to\mathbb{R}

u^{-1}(0)
of zeros the

of

is called a

is called a

of

if
u(x_{0})=u_{x}(x_{0})=0
.

Proposition 2.1

(Sturm’s theory
[
3
]
)
.

Let
u:[A_{1},A_{2}]\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth nontrivial solution to the parabolic equation
u_{t}=au_{xx}+bu_{x}+cu
, where
a,b,c\in C^{\infty}([A_{1},A_{2}]\times[0,T])
and
a>0
. Suppose
u
satisfies Dirichlet, Neumann, the periodic boundary condition, or the non-vanishing boundary condition:
u(A_{i},t)\neq 0
holds for all
t\in[0,T]
and
i=1,2
. Let
z(t)
denote the number of zeros of
u(\cdot,t)
in
[A_{0},A_{1}]
with
t\in[0,T]
. Then

(a)

for all
t\in[0,T]
,
z(t)
is finite,

(b)

if
(x_{0},t_{0})
is a multiple zero of
u
, i.e.,
u
and
u_{x}
both vanish at
(x_{0},t_{0})
, then for all
t_{1}<t_{0}<t_{2}
we have
z(t_{1})>z(t_{2})
. More precisely, there exists a neighborhood
N=[x_{0}-\varepsilon,x_{0}+\varepsilon]\times[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}+\delta]
such that

(i)

u(x_{0}\pm\varepsilon,t)\neq 0
for
\lvert t-t_{0}\rvert\leq\delta
,

(ii)

u(\cdot,t+\delta)
has at most one zero in the interval
[x_{0}-\varepsilon,x_{0}+\varepsilon]
,

(iii)

u(\cdot,t-\delta)
has at least two zeros in the interval
[x_{0}-\varepsilon,x_{0}+\varepsilon]
.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
u
be assumed as in the above proposition. Then the set

\{t\in[0,T]:u(\cdot,t)\mbox{ has a multiple zero}\}

is finite.

2.3.
The angle function

Let
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{t}\}_{t\in I}
be an ancient complete
non-compact
embedded smooth solution to CSF parametrized by
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\times I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
We can choose a smooth global function
\theta:\mathbb{R}\times I\to\mathbb{R}
which is an extension of the angle measured from
e_{1}=(1,0)
to
\mathbf{t}(p,t)
counterclockwisely mod
2\pi
.
It is well-known that
\theta_{s}=\kappa
, where the sign is positive in our convention of curvature.
Equivalently, this is the well-known identity for the Lagrangian angle

\boldsymbol{\kappa}=J\nabla\theta.

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}
is a complete non-compact embedded smooth solution to CSF. The angle function satisfies the heat equation

(14)

\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\theta=\tfrac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\theta.

Moreover,

(15)

\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\theta^{2}=\tfrac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\theta^{2}-2\theta_{s}^{2}.

Proof.

Note that
[
12
, Remark 2.3.2 page 34]
the commutation rule
\partial_{t}\partial_{s}=\partial_{s}\partial_{t}+\kappa^{2}\partial_{s}
on CSF implies that

(16)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{t}=\kappa_{s}\mathbf{n}.

By definition of
\theta
, we can write
\mathbf{t}=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
. Plugging this into (
16
) and comparing the coefficients gives
\theta_{t}=\kappa_{s}=\theta_{ss}
. Then (
15
) follows from chain rule for heat operator
[
9
, Lemma 3.14]
.
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}
is an ancient complete non-compact embedded smooth solution to CSF. Then the range of the angle of
M_{t}

(17)

\mathcal{A}(t):=\{\theta(s,t):s\in\mathbb{R}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}

is non-increasing in the sense that
\mathcal{A}(t_{1})\supseteq\mathcal{A}(t_{2})
for
t_{1}<t_{2}
.

Proof.

We may assume that
\mathcal{M}
is not a quasi-static line; otherwise, the claim holds trivially. Proposition
2.1
applied to the solution
\theta-c
of the same heat equation in Lemma
2.5
implies that for any
c\in\mathcal{A}(t_{2})
, the zero of
\theta(\cdot,t)-c
exists for all
t<t_{2}
. Then the claim follows.
∎

3.1.
Finite total curvature implies finite entropy

Lemma 3.1

(entropy of graph with finite slope)
.

Let
f:(a,b)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function satisfying
\lvert f^{\prime}(x)\rvert\leq\eta
for all
x\in(a,b)
for some constant
\eta
. Then the entropy of the graph
y=f(x)
is less than or equal to
\sqrt{1+\eta^{2}}
.

Proof.

We can always extend
f
to
\mathbb{R}
with
\lvert f^{\prime}(x)\rvert\leq\eta
everywhere. Denote by
\Gamma_{f}
the graph of
y=f(x)
.
Using the derivative bound, for all
\lambda>0

(18)

\displaystyle F_{0,\lambda^{2}}[\Gamma_{f}]=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\big(-\frac{x^{2}+\lambda^{-2}f(\lambda x)^{2}}{4}\big)\,\sqrt{1+\lvert f^{\prime}(\lambda x)\rvert^{2}}\,dx\leq\sqrt{1+\eta^{2}}.

In the last inequality, we simply throw away non-positive
-\lambda^{-2}f^{2}
term in the exponent, so
the inequality is invariant under translation.
Therefore,
\mathrm{Ent}[\Gamma]\leq\sqrt{1+\eta^{2}}
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose that
\Gamma
is an immersed, connected,
C^{2}
planar curve with total curvature
\int_{\Gamma}|\kappa|\,ds\leq\tfrac{\pi}{4}
.
Then
\mathrm{Ent}[\Gamma]\leq\sqrt{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma:(a,b)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an arc-length parametrization for
\Gamma
where
(a,b)\ni 0
.
Since the entropy is invariant under rotation on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, applying a suitable rotation we may assume that
\gamma(0)=P
and
\theta(0)=0
for some interior point
P\in\Gamma
.
From the assumption on total curvature, we readily see that
\lvert\theta(s)\rvert\leq\pi/4
.
Thus,
\Gamma
can be expressed as a (part of) graph of an entire function
f(x)
over the
x
-axis satisfying
\lvert f^{\prime}(x)\rvert\leq 1
everywhere.
By Lemma

,
\mathrm{Ent}[\Gamma]\leq\sqrt{2}
.
∎

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose that
\Gamma
is an immersed, connected,
C^{2}
planar curve with total curvature

.

By the total curvature condition, we can find a partition of

\displaystyle F_{x_{0},\lambda}[\Gamma]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{4\alpha}F_{x_{0},\lambda}[\Gamma_{i}]\leq 4\alpha\sqrt{2}.

3.2.
Finite entropy implies finite total curvature

Lemma 3.4

\displaystyle 0=\big((0,Y)-\gamma(s)\big)\cdot\mathbf{t}(s)=-x(s)\cos\theta(s)+(Y-y(s))\sin\theta(s).

\displaystyle Y=y(s)+x(s)\cot\theta(s).

We only focus on

. From (

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\kappa_{0}s\leq\theta(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\kappa(s)\,ds\leq 2\kappa_{0}s.

We can make

Lemma 3.5

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

Proof.

By a proper translation of
\Gamma
, we can always assume that
x_{0}
is the origin.
Suppose that the claim is not true as
s\to\infty
; that is, there exist
R>0
and a sequence
s_{i}\to\infty
such that
\lvert\gamma(s_{i})\rvert\leq R
.
By compactness of closed ball
\overline{B}_{R}
, after passing to a subsequence and relabeling, we may further assume that
\lvert\gamma(s_{j})-\mathbf{p}\rvert\leq 1
for some
\mathbf{p}\in\overline{B}_{R}
and
s_{i+1}-s_{i}\geq 2
for all
i=1,2,\ldots
.
Note that the curved segments
\gamma\big([s_{i}-1,s_{i}+1]\big)
are mutually disjoint due to embeddedness and all have intrinsic length equal to
2
.
Furthermore, for any
s\in[s_{i}-1,s_{i}+1]
, by triangle inequality,

(26)

\displaystyle\lvert\gamma(s)-\mathbf{p}\rvert\leq\lvert\gamma(s)-\gamma(s_{i})\rvert+\lvert\gamma(s_{i})-\mathbf{p}\rvert\leq 1+1=2.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ent}[\Gamma]\geq F_{\mathbf{p},1}[\Gamma]\geq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\int_{s_{i}-1}^{s_{i}+1}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{\lvert\gamma(s)-\mathbf{p}\rvert^{2}}{4}}\,ds\geq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\tfrac{e^{-1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{s_{i}-1}^{s_{i}+1}\,ds=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\tfrac{e^{-1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot 2=\infty,

which contradicts the finite entropy condition. For the case
s\to-\infty
, we just change the orientation of
\gamma
.
∎

Proposition 3.6

(bounded number of parallel normal lines)
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{t}\}_{t\in I}
is an ancient complete non-compact
(
resp. closed
)
embedded smooth solution to CSF with
\mathrm{Ent}(\mathcal{M})=m<\infty
. Then

(a)

For all
t\in I
, there are at most
m-1

(
resp.
m

)
distinct points on
M_{t}
having nonzero parallel curvature vectors with the same sign.

(b)

For all
t\in I
, there are at most
2m-1

(
resp.
2m+1

)
distinct points on
M_{t}
having parallel normal lines.

(c)

For all
t\in I
, for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
,
\theta(\cdot,t)\equiv\xi\mod\pi
has at most
2m-1

(
resp.
2m+1

)
solutions.

Proof.

We first consider the non-compact case. Suppose (a) were false. Up to a proper rotation and a change of orientation, we may assume that there exist
q_{1}<q_{2}<\ldots<q_{m}
for some
t\in I
at which the unit normal vector
\mathbf{n}(q_{i},t)=(1,0)
and curvature
\kappa(q_{i},t)>0
for all
i=1,\ldots,m
.
Applying Lemma
3.4
to all
q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}
, there exists
R>0
depending on the position
\gamma
and curvature around these points so that for any
Y\geq R
, for each
q_{i}
we can find
q_{i}^{\prime}
near
q_{i}
, at which the normal line to
M_{t}
passing through the common intersection point
(0,Y)
.
According to Lemma
2.3
, by choosing
Y
even larger (depending on the curvature at all
q_{i}^{\prime}
), the squared-distance function
\varphi^{(x_{0})}
with respect to the center
x_{0}:=(0,Y)
defined on
M_{t}
achieves strict local maximums at all
q_{i}^{\prime}
.
By Lemma
3.5
,
\varphi^{(x_{0})}(s)
diverges to
+\infty
as
s\to\pm\infty
. There must be
m
distinct local minimum points
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}
of
\varphi^{(x_{0})}(s)
such that
p_{i}<q_{i}<p_{i+1}
for
i=1,\ldots,m-1
. This contradicts Proposition
2.8
.

Note (b) and (c) are equivalent, so it suffices to show (c). Suppose there are distinct
s_{1},\ldots,s_{2m}
zeros of
\theta(s_{i},t)=\xi\mod\pi
for some
\xi
and
t\in I
. Recall that
\theta(s,t)
satisfies the heat equation in Lemma
2.5
.
By Proposition
2.1
and Corollary
2.2
, by perturbing
t
backward slightly, we still have
2m
distinct zeros
s_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,s_{2m}^{\prime}
of the equation
\theta(\cdot,t)=\xi\mod\pi
and
\kappa(s_{i}^{\prime},t)=\theta_{s}(s_{i}^{\prime},t)\neq 0
for all
i=1,\ldots,2m
. At least
m
of
s_{i}
’s have the same sign of curvature. This leads to a scenario that contradicts (a).

For the closed case, the only difference is the maximal number of local maximum points in Proposition
2.8
and the proof proceeds similarly as the non-compact case.
∎

Theorem 3.7

(bounded total curvature)
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{t}\}_{t\in I}
is an ancient complete non-compact
(
resp. closed
)
embedded smooth solution to CSF with
\mathrm{Ent}(\mathcal{M})=m<\infty
. Then

(27)

\sup_{t\in I}\int_{M_{t}}|\kappa|\,ds\leq(2m-1)\pi\quad(\mbox{resp.}\;(2m+1)\pi).

Proof.

We first prove the result in the non-compact case. For any fixed
t\in I
, applying area formula to the angle function
\theta(\cdot,t):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\theta_{s}(s,t)|\,ds=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{H}^{0}\big(\theta(\cdot,t)^{-1}(\xi)\big)d\xi,

where
\mathcal{H}^{0}
is the counting measure.
Using
\theta_{s}(\cdot,t)=\kappa(\cdot,t)
and Tonelli’s theorem, we get

\displaystyle\int_{M_{t}}|\kappa|\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{H}^{0}\big(\theta(\cdot,t)^{-1}(\xi)\big)\,d\xi=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{k\pi}^{(k+1)\pi}\mathcal{H}^{0}\big(\theta(\cdot,t)^{-1}(\xi)\big)d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{0}\big(\theta(\cdot,t)^{-1}(\xi+k\pi)\big)d\xi\leq(2m-1)\pi.

In the last inequality, we use Proposition
3.6
(c).

Note that the above proof does not really need a globally defined smooth angle function, since we only used the (mod
\pi
)-angle in Proposition
3.6
. In closed case, we can find a (discontinuous) angle function
\theta:[0,\ell)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
on
M_{t}
parametrized by arclength having a jump of value
2\pi
at
s=0
, where
\ell
is the length of
M_{t}
. The proof proceeds similarly as the non-compact case.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Combining Theorem
3.3
and Theorem
3.7
completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.8

(uniform bound for the angle)
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{t}\}_{t\in I}
is an ancient complete
non-compact
embedded smooth solution to CSF with
\mathrm{Ent}(\mathcal{M})=m<\infty
and suppose that
\theta(0,t_{0})\in[0,2\pi)
for some
t_{0}\in I
.
Then
|\theta(s,t)\rvert\leq(2m+1)\pi
for all
s\in\mathbb{R},t\in I
.

Proof.

Let
\theta_{0}=\theta(0,t_{0})
. By Proposition
2.6
, it suffices to show that
\theta
is bounded for
t<t_{0}
and for any
t<t_{0}
, there exists
s_{t}
such that
\theta(s_{t},t)=\theta_{0}
. Then Theorem
3.7
implies that for any
s
,

\displaystyle\lvert\theta(s,t)\rvert=\left\lvert\theta_{0}+\int_{s_{t}}^{s}\kappa\,ds\right\rvert\leq 2\pi+(2m-1)\pi=(2m+1)\pi.

∎

4.
Application: uniqueness of tangent flow at infinity for non-compact curves

We prove a uniqueness result for tangent flow at infinity, making use of the well-definedness of the angle function
\theta
in the complete non-compact case. Note that by Corollary
3.8
,
\theta
is uniformly bounded in terms of the entropy, so the result is exactly the same as Neves’ result
[
14
]
and Lambert–Lotay–Schulze’s
[
11
]
in the
1
-dimensional case. We remark that our uniqueness result below does not provide a convergence rate, whereas the uniqueness result in
[
5
, Theorem 1.2]
gives an exponential convergence of the rescaled flow
\overline{M}_{\tau}
as
\tau\to-\infty
.

Theorem 4.1

(unique tangent flow at infinity)
.

An ancient finite-entropy smooth complete non-compact curve shortening flow embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
has a unique tangent flow at infinity. Moreover, the tangent flow at infinity is a straight line with multiplicity
m\geq 2
, unless the flow is a static line.

Proof.

To show the uniqueness of tangent flow at infinity, from rough convergence theorem, it suffices to show that the rotation function
S(\tau)
converges as
\tau\to-\infty
.
Let
\theta
be a choice of angle function defined in Subsection
2.3
such that
\theta(0,t_{0})\in[0,2\pi)
for some
t_{0}\in I
.
Suppose that
\mathrm{Ent}[\mathcal{M}]=m
.
By Corollary
3.8
, the angle function
\lvert\theta(s,t)\rvert
is uniformly bounded by
(2m+1)\pi
for all
t\in I
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
.
By rough convergence theorem
2.7
, there exist
R\gg 0
,
T\ll-1
, and some fixed
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that for all
\tau\leq T
, for all
i
,

(28)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\Sigma_{\tau}^{i}\cap B_{R}}\theta^{2}e^{-\frac{\lvert x\rvert^{2}}{4}}\,ds-\big(S(\tau)+a_{i}\pi\big)^{2}\right\rvert<\varepsilon.

where
\Sigma_{\tau}^{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{\tau}^{m}
are the connected components of
\overline{M}_{\tau}\cap B_{R}
.
Here, we identify the smooth rotation function
S(\tau)
with a real-valued angle function.
From
[
7
, Lemma 2.4]
,
F_{0,1}[\overline{M}_{\tau}\setminus B_{R}]\leq Cme^{-\frac{R^{2}}{8}}
for some numerical constant
C
.
As a result,

(29)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\overline{M}_{\tau}\setminus B_{R}}\theta^{2}e^{-\frac{\lvert x\rvert^{2}}{4}}\,ds\right\rvert\leq Cm(2m+1)^{2}\pi^{2}e^{-\frac{R^{2}}{8}},

which can be made arbitrarily small by choosing a sufficiently large
R
.
Combining (
28
) and (
29
) gives

(30)

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to-\infty}\left\lvert\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\overline{M}_{\tau}}\theta^{2}e^{-\frac{\lvert x\rvert^{2}}{4}}\,ds-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big(S(\tau)+a_{i}\pi\big)^{2}\right\rvert=0.

By weighted monotonicity formula
[
9
, Theorem 4.13]
and Lemma
2.5
(
15
),

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{M_{t}}\theta^{2}\Phi_{(0,0)}\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{M_{t}}\Big[(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}-\tfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s^{2}})\theta^{2}-\Big\lvert\kappa+\frac{x^{\perp}}{2t}\Big\rvert^{2}\theta^{2}\Big]\Phi_{(0,0)}\,dt

\displaystyle=-\int_{M_{t}}\Big[2\kappa^{2}+\Big\lvert\kappa+\frac{x^{\perp}}{2t}\Big\rvert^{2}\theta^{2}\Big]\Phi_{(0,0)}\,dt\leq 0.

It follows from the above monotonicity, uniform bounds of angle and entropy, and (
30
) that the limit

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to-\infty}\int_{M_{t}}\theta^{2}\Phi_{(0,0)}\,ds=\lim_{\tau\to-\infty}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\overline{M}_{\tau}}\theta^{2}e^{-\frac{\lvert x\rvert^{2}}{4}}\,ds=\lim_{\tau\to-\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(S(\tau)+a_{i}\pi)^{2}

exists. This implies that
\lim_{\tau\to-\infty}S(\tau)
exists.
∎

Appendix A
The entropy bound for the logarithmic spiral

The converse of Theorem
3.3
is generally not true for
C^{2}
embedded curves in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For example, by Lemma
3.1
the graph of
y=\sin(x)
has finite entropy but infinite total curvature. Another interesting example is the logarithmic spiral
[
15
, Logarithmic_spiral]
, which is self-similar: a scaled logarithmic spiral is congruent to the original curve by a rotation.
Given real constants
a>0
and
k\neq 0
, the logarithmic spiral can be written as

(31)

\displaystyle S_{a,k}:=\{(r\cos\varphi,r\sin\varphi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,r=ae^{k\varphi},\varphi\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Since the pitch angle of
S_{a,k}
is a constant
\alpha=\arctan k
, the angle function
\theta
defined in Section
2.3
at point
(r\cos\varphi,r\sin\varphi)
is given by
\varphi+\pi/2-\alpha
.
Then unbounded
\theta
implies that the total curvature of
S_{a,k}
is infinite.
For any
0\leq r_{1}<r_{2}
, the length of
S_{a,k}\cap(B_{r_{2}}(0)\setminus B_{r_{1}}(0))
is given by

(32)

\displaystyle L[S_{a,k}\cap(B_{r_{2}}(0)\setminus B_{r_{1}}(0))]=\frac{r_{2}-r_{1}}{\lvert\sin\alpha\rvert}.

Note that
S_{a,k}\cap B_{1}(0)
has finite length, which implies that the logarithmic spiral
S_{a,k}
is not a complete curve.
Finally, we will show that the entropy of
S_{a,k}
is finite.

Proposition A.1

(entropy bound of logarithmic spiral)
.

Let
a>0,k\neq 0
,
\alpha=\arctan k
, and let
S_{a,k}
be defined as above.
Then
\mathrm{Ent}[S_{a,k}]\leq 1/\lvert\sin\alpha\rvert<\infty
.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\lambda>0
.
By (
32
), the arclength element is
ds=\frac{dr}{\lvert\sin\alpha\rvert}
in
the paramaterization by
r
.
Using triangle inequality and the substitution
u=(r-\lvert x_{0}\rvert)/\lambda
,

\displaystyle F_{x_{0},\lambda^{2}}[S_{a,k}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda^{2}\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-\tfrac{\lvert x-x_{0}\rvert^{2}}{4\lambda^{2}}\big)\,\frac{dr}{\lvert\sin\alpha\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lvert\sin\alpha\rvert\sqrt{2\lambda^{2}\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-\tfrac{(r-\lvert x_{0}\rvert)^{2}}{4\lambda^{2}}\big)\,dr

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lvert\sin\alpha\rvert\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\lvert x_{0}\rvert/\lambda}^{\infty}\exp\big(-\tfrac{u^{2}}{4}\big)\,du

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lvert\sin\alpha\rvert}.

Thus,
\mathrm{Ent}[S_{a,k}]\leq 1/\lvert\sin\alpha\rvert
.
∎
