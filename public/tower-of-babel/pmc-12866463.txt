Perspectives of final-year medical students in Singapore on clinical role models: a mixed methods study
Authors: Isaac KS Ng, Qin Xiang Ng, Wilson GW Goh, Christopher Thong, Kevin SH Teo, Desmond B Teo, Li Feng Tan, Gerald Choon-Huat Koh, Manjari Lahiri, Adrian CL Kee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical role modelling is an essential part of medicine’s “hidden curriculum”, which deeply influences trainees’ professional development. Yet, learners’ perspectives of both positive and negative clinical role models remain less well-known.MethodsWe surveyed a cohort of final-year medical students in Singapore with a comprehensive questionnaire on role model profile, impact, and key attributes, with face and content validation performed as required. Specifically, our study adopted a mixed-methods approach, with collection of quantitative data on respondents’ perceptions of positive and negative role models, and qualitative thematic analysis from free-text survey responses.ResultsMajority of role model encounters were with senior clinicians (registrar and above). Most students (> 75%) reported positive role model encounters in clinical settings, although one-third and one-half reported negative role model encounters in medical and surgical rotations respectively. Students highly rated the importance of role models across surveyed domains of personal/professional development. Core role model attributes identified largely pertained to interpersonal skills and professional conduct. Qualitative free-text thematic analysis revealed three major themes: (1) Role modelling humanity: compassion, patience and empathy, (2) Teaching as moral leadership: grounded confidence and professionalism in action, and (3) Negative role modelling: disrespect, humiliation, and their impact.ConclusionsMedical students placed great value on relational/interpersonal attributes (e.g. empathy, compassion, and professional conduct) in role modeling that forms an integral part of medicine’s enduring hidden curriculum. Further qualitative studies to interrogate personal and real-world contextual factors that influence role modeling behaviors would be helpful to inform pedagogical and workplace-based interventions that foster positive clinical role modeling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08488-7.Keywords: Role model, Medical education, Medical students

Background

Role modeling is an indispensable aspect of medical training, which has long adhered to a clinical apprenticeship pedagogical model that emphasises the importance of cultivating clinical and non-clinical soft skills through observation and emulating the behaviors and practices of clinical preceptors [1]. Consequently, physicians practising in teaching hospitals are expected to concurrently be educators and actively demonstrate their clinical skills and expected professional conduct to their learners [1].

It is known that clinical role models can exert both positive and negative influences in a powerful manner on their trainees [2]. Drawing on Bandura’s social learning theory [3], role modeling is understood as a process through which learners acquire both positive and negative behaviors in real-world socialized clinical settings [4]. Under this educational construct, learning is embedded in a social environment and occurs in four sequential stages of directing attention towards observed behaviors of a ‘role model’, internalization/retention of these external cues, having an opportunity to reproduce intended behaviors, and subsequent motivation to persist in these actions [2]. Importantly, vicarious reinforcement (both positive and negative) takes place when learners observe the reactions of others (e.g. other learners, or patients) to the behavior of the clinical role model that influences their appraisal of them [2].

In contrast to the extensive literature on the importance/implications of role models in clinical and para-clinical spaces (as part of the “hidden curriculum”) [4], the specific key defining attributes of positive [5] and negative [6] role models from the learners’ perspectives are not often directly explored. Particularly, there are a paucity of studies in Asian settings [7], where cultural, hierarchical, and educational contexts may differ. In Singapore, a Southeast Asian, multi-ethnic country, Koh et al. (2015) had previously published a study on the perceptions of newly graduated local medical students across two different cohorts (2005 and 2009) on the defining attributes of a “role model” physician [8], which were extracted from a 1998 study [9]. At that time, the authors found that there were significant differences in perspectives of medical students from the two cohorts (2005, 2009) separated by 5 years on characteristics that define a “role model”/“ideal physician” [8]. In the more recent cohort, students seemed to place greater value on cognitive attributes such as “intelligence”, “keeping up-to-date with medical advances”, and “independent thinking”, as well as interpersonal attributes/soft skills such as “being an understanding person”, “being likeable”, “being a good team player”, “being considerate”, “ability to communicate in a patient’s language/dialect”, and “understanding of patients’ cultural beliefs” [8].

Understanding students’ views on clinical role models is vital for informing faculty development, shaping learning environments, and enhancing the “hidden curriculum”. Therefore, in this study, we sought to examine the perspectives of final-year medical students in Singapore on the attributes of positive and negative clinical role models, the profiles of these role models, and their perceived impact on students’ professional development and career choices. By employing a mixed methods approach, albeit with open-ended survey responses as our source of qualitative data, we sought to integrate quantitative ratings with free-text narratives to provide a richer, triangulated understanding of role modeling in contemporary medical education.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a convergent mixed methods study integrating quantitative and qualitative data to explore final-year medical students’ perceptions of positive and negative clinical role models and their impact on professional development. Quantitative and qualitative data were collected concurrently, analyzed separately, and integrated through triangulation of findings.

Setting and participants

The study was conducted at the Yong Loo Lin School of Medicine, National University of Singapore (NUS) which runs a 5-year undergraduate medical degree programme that comprising a 2-year pre-clinical phase followed by 3-year clinical rotations [10]. All 305 final-year medical students in the graduating class of 2025 were invited to participate after completion of their final MBBS examinations in March 2025. A physical copy of the survey questionnaire was distributed to medical students by two members of the research team (IKSN, WGWG), both of whom were not involved in the conduct or assessment of their MBBS examinations. They were permitted to be in the post-examination holding area to conduct the research study, having received prior approval from the medical school deanery and final year undergraduate education director. Recruitment was solely done through verbal briefing by the on-site research team members with all final year medical students invited to participate in this study. The survey was timed after the completion of the final examinations with the aim of improving participation rates given less time pressure and examination-related stress. A participant information sheet with relevant information about the study was appended at the front of each distributed survey questionnaire. It was emphasised that participation in the study was completely voluntary with non-participation having no bearing on their examination grades, and all survey questionnaires were anonymised with no participant identifiers collected. Consent to participate was implied through the return of a completed survey questionnaire. Ethics approval was obtained from the NUS Institutional Review Board (NUS-IRB-2024-887).

Survey instrument

We developed a survey questionnaire to study the perspectives of final-year medical students on clinical role models (Appendix). Broadly, the survey questionnaire comprised 5 main sections (participant demographic information, perception of positive role model attributes, perception of negative role model attributes, profile, and impact of clinical role models in professional development, and free-text description of clinical role model/real-life encounters). Question types included Likert scale, multiple choice, check-box style (permitting multiple selections) and free response questions. The full survey questionnaire is made available in the Appendix.

The questionnaire section on positive role model attributes was adapted from Jochemsen-van der Leeuw et al. (2014)’s recently developed role model apperception tool (RoMAT) [5] which was validated for clinical trainer appraisal amongst a group of Dutch general practitioner trainees. In our adaptation, we extracted the role model attributes reflected in the original survey into a list of 18 individual traits. Separately, Burgess et al’s (2015) qualitative study findings [6] to generate a 14-item list of negative clinical role model attributes. While Koh et al. (2015)’s study [8] previously used Fones’ 1998 role model constructs [9], we decided to adopt more updated role model attributes developed within the last decade, and with validation of studied items. The survey was anonymous with no participant identifier collected. Upon completion of the survey questionnaires, the students deposited them in a central collection box in no particular order, with no reasonable means of tracing back the participant identity.

Face validation was performed for the negative role model attributes and profile/impact of clinical role models survey sections using the QQ-10 face validation survey (comprising 6 value items, and 4 burden items) [11] on 13 house officers who had recently graduated from medical school, as these sections were not adapted from previously validated surveys. In general, 5–15 participants for preliminary face validity assessments of survey questionnaires as part of a “cognitive interviewing” process is adequate, balancing the sensitivity in capturing issues with survey design, and avoiding excessive sampling with diminishing returns [12]. The 13 house officers were selected through convenience sampling, based on word-of-mouth and invitation of past acquaintances of members of the research team. While some of them may have had past working relationships with members of the research team, none of them had an active or ongoing supervisory relationships with the investigators. The QQ-10 comprises ten items scored on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = strongly disagree, 1 = mostly disagree, 2 = neutral (neither agree/disagree), 3 = mostly agree, 4 = strongly agree), with six items pertaining to positive values (addresses issues of importance/value, relevant to the topic, easy to complete, comprehensive in covering all areas of the topic, enjoyment in filling questionnaire, happy to complete questionnaire in future), and four items on negative burdens (questionnaire being too long, too embarrassing, too complicated, or upsetting) [11].

Content validation was further performed for the negative role model attributes using the content validation index (CVI) [13] on 10 experienced clinical faculty members who have been highly involved in undergraduate medical education for at least 5 years. Each survey item was scored on a Likert scale (1 = not relevant, 2 = somewhat relevant, 3 = quite relevant, 4 = highly relevant), and key CVI indices are generated as follows: I-CVI (item-level content validity index) = proportion of experts giving relevance rating of 3 or 4, and S-CVI/Ave (scale-level content validity index based on the average method) = average I-CVI score of all the items [13].

Lastly, we assessed the general profile (seniority, specialty) of these positive and negative role models that students encountered in their clinical rotations, as well as the implications of these role models on their professional development and career perceptions.

Analysis plan

Quantitative data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS version 30. Descriptive statistics were summarised as follows: mean/standard deviation or median/ interquartile range for numerical data, and frequencies/percentages for categorical data. Univariable analyses were performed using Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test (if presence of cell count < 5 in a 2 × 2 table) for comparison between categorical variables. Multivariable analyses were performed using a binary logistic regression model (where categorical covariates with p < 0.1 on univariable analyses were included) to identify potential independent predictor variables. Missing or inappropriately entered data (e.g. multiple or ambiguous selections) were ascribed as either system-missing or user-defined missing values in SPSS and were not included in quantitative data analyses. Statistical significance was set at p-value < 0.05, with effect estimate shown as prevalence odds ratio (POR) and 95% confidence interval (CI).

Qualitative data were analyzed thematically following Braun and Clarke’s six-step process for reflexive thematic analysis [14]. Two investigators (IKSN and QXN) independently coded the data, compared coding decisions, and resolved discrepancies through iterative discussions. Both investigators brought distinct but complementary perspectives to the analysis. At the time of the study, IKSN was an internal medicine trainee with a strong interest and research background in medical education, with lived experience navigating clinical hierarchies and training under clinical apprenticeship models. He had informally taught some of the final year medical students during their clinical rotations, but had no supervisory roles or involvement in formal assessments. QXN has graduate and doctoral training in public health, psychology, and the social sciences, with prior experience in qualitative and mixed methods research. He had no prior contact in teaching or assessing the medical students. Their differing disciplinary lenses enriched the reflexive process, as they continuously challenged and questioned their assumptions about role modeling, professional identity formation, and hierarchical dynamics in medical education. Codes were grouped into themes and subthemes reflective of students’ views.

Quantitative analyses were conducted by the statistical lead (IKSN) before qualitative coding commenced. Qualitative coders (IKSN and QXN) conducted initial familiarisation, coding, and theme generation without access to the quantitative findings. Integration occurred only after both analyses were completed, consistent with convergent mixed-methods design principles (Fetters et al., 2013) [15]. [15] Integration was achieved through a side-by-side comparison of quantitative findings and qualitative themes, with development of a joint display table mapping numerical ratings to themes and representative quotes. Areas of convergence, complementarity and divergence were identified to provide a richer understanding of the data and enhance interpretation of the findings. QXN did not review any quantitative summaries or statistical results prior to completing coding and initial theme development, to maintain analytic independence and minimise interpretive bias.

Results

Instrument validity testing

A total of 13 recent medical graduates (house officers who had completed their final-year student rotations within the past year) were invited to pilot the instrument, and all consented to participate (n = 13). These individuals were chosen because they had recent, relevant experience of medical student training and role modeling. The mean composite score for value items was 85.6% (SD 12.7%), indicating high perceived value, and the mean burden score was 11.2% (SD 11.3%), indicating low burden.

Content validation of the negative attributes section with 10 senior clinical faculty members was also performed. Nine items achieved an I-CVI of 1.0, one item scored 0.9, two items 0.8, and two items 0.7. The S-CVI/Ave was 0.92, indicating good overall content validity. Items with I-CVI below 0.78 were retained following discussion among the research team, as they were judged to reflect important elements of negative role modeling identified in prior literature [16] and pilot feedback.

Survey participant characteristics

Out of 305 eligible final-year medical students, 218 completed the survey, yielding a response rate of 71.5%. The mean age of participants was 24.0 (SD 1.1) years, with near-equal gender distribution (50.5% male, 49.5% female). The majority (95.8%) had rotated through at least three tertiary teaching hospitals during their final-year clinical postings, with rotations in National University Hospital (90.2%), Tan Tock Seng Hospital (69.2%), and Singapore General Hospital (61.2%) being the most common (Table 1).

Quantitative findings on positive and negative role model attributes

Students rated empathy for patients (median 4, IQR 3–4), communication with patients/relatives (median 4, IQR 3–4), understanding learners’ needs (median 4, IQR 3–4), commitment to learner growth (median 4, IQR 3–4), building rapport with learners (median 4, IQR 3–4), holding a positive attitude towards learners (median 4, IQR 3–4), honesty/integrity (median 4, IQR 3–4), positive interactions with healthcare workers (median 4, IQR 3–4), and competence in difficult clinical situations (median 4, IQR 3–4) as the most important positive role model attributes (Table 2). On the other hand, humiliation of students (median 4, IQR 4–4) and disrespect toward patients (4, IQR 4–4) were viewed as the most important negative attributes, with poor bedside manners, lack of empathy, inauthenticity in patient interactions, and showing negative regard for the medical profession also frequently identified.

In terms of role model profiles, students most frequently identified associate consultants/consultants (38.1%, n = 69) and senior consultants (20.4%, n = 37) as positive role models, and senior consultants (36.1%, n = 70) and registrars (32.0%, n = 62) as negative role models (Table 1). For medical rotations, 84.8% of students (n = 185) had encountered many positive role models, while 33.9% of respondents (n = 74) encountered many negative role models. For surgical rotations, 75.2% of students (n = 164) reported encountering many positive role models, while 53.9% (n = 117) encountered many negative role models.

Nearly all students agreed that positive clinical role models were important for the development of clinical reasoning (94.4%, n = 206), applying knowledge to practice (91.7%, n = 200), self-directed learning (92.6%, n = 202), and professionalism (90.8%, n = 198) (Fig. 1). Similarly, 90.3% (n = 197) reported that role models influenced their perception of medicine as a career, and 88.5% (n = 193) indicated an impact on their future subspecialty or residency choice.

Medical student-rated impact of having positive clinical role models for their personal/professional development

Multivariable analysis showed that students who identified excellent clinical reasoning as a defining positive attribute were significantly more likely to report frequent encounters with positive role models during medical rotations (POR 16.2, 95% CI 1.4–187; p = 0.026). Those who emphasized self-confidence as a key positive trait were more likely to report positive role models in surgical rotations (POR 5.91, 95% CI 1.2–29.1; p = 0.029). In addition, negative role model encounters in surgical rotation seems to be particularly linked to higher perceived impact on subspecialty/residency choice (POR 3.89, 95% CI 1.18–12.9; p = 0.026).

Qualitative findings on role models

For the free-text question on definition of a clinical role model, the response rate was 149 out of 218 (68.3%), and for the question asking respondents to share experiences with clinical role models, the response rate was 82 out of 218 (37.6%). All qualitative responses were written in English; therefore, no translation or back-translation procedures were required. After sieving through the collated responses, reflexive thematic analysis of 150 substantive free-text responses was done which yielded several key themes.

Theme 1: role modelling humanity – compassion, patience, and empathy

Students consistently emphasised relational and humanistic qualities as central to positive role modelling. Clinicians admired by students were described as demonstrating genuine compassion, communicating sensitively, and taking deliberate steps to include students in clinical activities. Small acts—such as spending extra time with patients, speaking gently, or checking in on students’ understanding—were frequently highlighted as meaningful.

Doctor spent extra time counselling a patient, showing me that there is always time to care for patient.

Doctor was very kind, always checked in on me during rounds to make sure I understood what was going on.

Theme 2: teaching as moral leadership – grounded confidence and professionalism in action

Students valued role models who conveyed confidence grounded in clinical competence and professionalism. Such clinicians were viewed as steady, reassuring presences who communicated clearly, demonstrated humility, and upheld high standards of patient care. Students differentiated between confidence that inspires and condescension that alienates, describing how tone, demeanour, and interpersonal style shaped their perceptions.

We had a tutorial with a doctor who spent 1.5 h teaching us about the difficult cases we had seen in the ward… he was always a reassuring presence to patients and they trusted him a lot.

Some doctors were extremely condescending towards students for lack of medical knowledge, but others were encouraging, dedicated to teaching, firm but empathetic.

Theme 3: negative role modelling – disrespect, humiliation, and their impact

Students also described negative role modelling behaviours, primarily involving humiliation, disrespect, and unprofessional interpersonal conduct. Such behaviours included belittling comments, disrespect toward patients or junior staff, and poor bedside manners. Students noted that these encounters affected their confidence and shaped their perceptions of professionalism.

Doctor was derogatory during an assessment and did not practise constructive criticism.

Doctor was rude to junior staff and disrespectful toward patients.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

As shown in Table 3, there was strong convergence between the quantitative data and qualitative narratives on key positive attributes, such as empathy and communication skills. Students who rated these attributes often described role models who exemplified compassion, patience, and effective interpersonal interactions. For example, high ratings for empathy paralleled accounts of clinicians who took extra time to counsel patients or offered thoughtful feedback to students.

Similarly, negative attributes such as humiliation and disrespect for patients were consistently reflected in students’ qualitative accounts. Those who rated these traits as highly problematic shared narratives of condescending remarks, verbal abuse, and unprofessional conduct.

Complementarity was noted where qualitative data enriched the understanding of certain quantitative findings. For instance, while self-confidence received moderately high quantitative ratings as a positive attribute (median 3, IQR 2–4), qualitative responses elaborated on how confidence inspired trust and provided reassurance to both students and patients.

Discussion

This mixed methods study examined final-year medical students’ perceptions of positive and negative clinical role models, their defining attributes, and their perceived impact on professional development and career intentions.

Our survey response rate was high at 71.5%. The average age of our participating students was 24 years, with an even gender distribution. From integration of quantitative and qualitative findings on role model attributes, convergent themes largely pertained to interpersonal or relational attributes – with empathy and good communication skills highlighted as positive traits, and humiliation and poor bedside manners forming the negative traits. There was an instance of complementarity, where qualitative findings clarified the perceived importance of self-confidence as a role model trait – where it should be grounded in actual competency, and serve as a form of reassurance to patients and students. The distinction between self-confidence and arrogance was also made, as students repeatedly mentioned unacceptable, condescending behaviors.

Clinical reasoning as an important trait was specifically associated with positive role modeling in medical subspecialties, presumably due to their hallmark emphasis on analytical and elaborate diagnostic and decisional processes [17]. On the other hand, grounded self-confidence was another valued trait, especially in surgical contexts, where students admired role models who provided reassurance and inspired trust through calm decisiveness and humility. Importantly, students distinguished between authentic confidence and arrogance, noting that the former inspired while the latter alienated. Negative role models were characterized by behaviors such as humiliation of students, disrespect toward patients, and poor bedside manners, findings that strongly converged across data streams. Such behaviors were not merely seen as unpleasant; they were described as damaging to students’ confidence, sense of belonging, and enthusiasm for the profession.

More broadly, our study reinforces the critical role of informal learning in shaping students’ professional development. As seen in other contexts, students primarily learn professionalism, ethics and interpersonal norms not through formal instruction, but through observation and participation in the day-to-day practices of clinical teams, the so-called “hidden curriculum” [4, 18]. Similar to studies by Jochemsen-van der Leeuw et al. [5] and Burgess et al. [19], students in our cohort placed greater value on relational and moral attributes over technical or cognitive skills. The qualities students valued most in role models (empathy, communication, inclusion, and moral integrity) were those enacted through relational interactions rather than taught through lectures or guidelines. Our integrated data suggest that these relational attributes not only inspire and motivate learners but also serve as the foundation for students’ emerging professional identities.

Role modelling in the hidden curriculum is not solely a function of explicit clinician behaviours; it is co-constructed through interactions shaped by learners’ identities, expectations, and sociocultural norms [20]. In our context, students’ perceptions are likely influenced by the hierarchical structures of Asian clinical environments, prior experiences with authority, and differing expectations of professionalism and communication. These contextual factors help explain why relational and moral attributes were consistently prioritised.

In terms of the profile and impact of role models in clinical training, it was re-assuring that majority of students reported positive role model encounters in both medical and surgical rotations. However, there remains a significant proportion – approximately one in three students in medical postings and one in two in surgical rotations – who had many negative role model encounters. We postulate that this could be due to several reasons. Firstly, it is known that clinicians generally work in highly pressurizing environments dealing with high patient load, high work intensity, and a myriad of other non-clinical responsibilities, often with suboptimal structural/systemic support [21]. This undoubtedly poses significant challenges to clinicians/preceptors in having the time and space to teach and engage with medical students attached to them [22]. This problem might be further exacerbated in procedural-heavy specialties where there is often little time for teaching activities in the wards [23], as well as lack of a proper structure for intra-procedural teaching [24]. Moreover, clinicians have high rates of job burnout which can lead to both uninspiring [25], and uncollegial behaviors [26, 27]. Recently, a qualitative study found that medical students embedded in multidisciplinary healthcare teams often find themselves “caught in the crossfire” of “interprofessional and interpersonal conflicts” in the course of delivering patient care [28]. Nonetheless, further qualitative studies would be helpful to tease out the major drivers of negative clinical role model behaviors encountered by medical students.

Interestingly, we observed that the majority of clinicians whom students consider as positive role models were associate consultants/consultants and senior consultants, while most negative role models were senior consultants and registrars. This may suggest that medical students generally benchmark their clinical role models at senior doctors (registrars and above), and therefore pay more attention and take more seriously their exhibited attitudes and behaviors. In contrast, junior doctors may still be perceived as in the nascent phase of their professional development and far from the “end product”. However, with near-peer teaching becoming increasingly popular due to perceived enhanced cognitive/social congruence [29], it is surprising to an extent that junior physicians are seen as lesser role models than their senior counterparts. Further qualitative studies to interrogate the respondents’ understanding of role models and whether there are sociocultural factors that drive this perception (e.g. greater hierarchial differences and reverence accorded to senior physicians in Asian settings).

Of note, it is intriguing that registrars are frequently perceived to be negative role models. We postulate that this might be attributed to several reasons. Firstly, registrars are in a difficult transitional phase of their careers, as they move on to subspecialty-specific higher training, take on new roles/responsibilities, and are expected to now supervise juniors in various clinical tasks [30]. Consequently, they may lack the professional maturity/clarity and experience to be effective role models for what is expected of a senior clinician. Secondly, registrars like junior physicians have high rates of burnout [31, 32], presumably attributed to high levels of work-related stress, role conflicts and transitions that they may find great difficulty in coping with. Indeed, previous studies have consistently shown that the workload of a medical registrar is often deemed as “unmanageable” 33. In fact, a free-text response from a prior qualitative study summed up the medical registrar as “an undervalued, overworked, stressed individual, fighting waves of referrals and sick patients with little senior support and criticism from all directions” 33. Consequently, registrars may not have the capacity to portion time out from clinical duties to attend to the learning needs of medical students and constantly laboring under high work intensity and stress may predispose them to displaying uncollegial/unprofessional conduct to others, especially juniors seeking advice/consult [33].

From our survey responses, it is evident that medical students value the presence of role models in their clinical rotations, perceiving that they would impact their ability to develop the necessary clinical and soft skills, as well as influence their career/specialty choice. Our study findings echo previous studies that have consistently found that role model encounters can influence career aspirations amongst trainees, seemingly more pronounced in surgical subspecialties [24, 34], presumably attributed to a greater perceived need for apprenticeship training for procedural opportunities and supervision for honing of surgical craft.

Given the wide-ranging implications of clinical role models on trainees’ personal/professional development, it is imperative that clinicians practising in public/teaching hospitals are adequately equipped, supported, and assessed on their role modeling behaviors.

Firstly, faculty development programs that help clinical faculty internalise their role model responsibility and be more attuned to the impact of their role modeling behaviors that could have wittingly or unwittingly influenced their learners [35]. Practical forms of such programmes include simulation practice for faculty members to learn how to provide constructive feedback [36], observed structured teaching evaluation [37], and reviewing of exemplars or anonymised student feedback. In addition, faculty engagement with students during their clinical rotations could be enhanced to obtain narrative feedback on personal experiences in clerkship training and tease out actionable points for faculty improvement. A recent Dutch study reported the use of Feedback and Feedforward Conversations (FFCs), which was inspired by healthcare ‘mirror meetings’, to promote bidirectional feedback and dialogue between clinical faculty and medical students in a psychologically safe environment [38]. Briefly, there were 3 phases in such a meeting, comprising an input phase with facilitator-guided student reflections on their clinical experiences with faculty members listening in with their backs facing the students, time-out phase for both groups to reflect on what they have heard, and dialogue phase where faculty members and students meet in a group to go through the core issues raised with the help of a facilitator to attain mutual understanding and generate actionables [38].

Secondly, provision of formalised training for clinician-educators to learn evidence-based pedagogies [39], and recognise “teachable moments” amidst busy clinical practice [40], as well as allocation of protected teaching time [41] are important as well. Thirdly, we must continue to tackle the physician burnout crisis through individual and systemic changes [21, 26], as well as actively promote collegial practices at the workplace (e.g. cultivating a blame-free culture, flattening of professional hierarchies) [42]. Lastly, although student feedback on clinical preceptors is increasingly incorporated in faculty appraisal, it may be helpful to re-calibrate included assessment items and their respective weightages to better reflect the importance of key role model attributes alongside measures of teaching quality and clinical competence. In addition, medical students may also be encouraged to share direct, anonymous feedback with assigned posting coordinators, so as to recognise clinicians who display exemplary conduct, and provide remediation to negative role models.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is its mixed methods design, which enabled the integration of numerical data with students’ personal reflections to provide a more holistic understanding of clinical role modeling. Face and content validation of our survey instrument, and a high response rate (71.5%), enhance the overall reliability and credibility of our findings.

Nonetheless, there are several limitations merit consideration and further studies that build on our initial findings. Firstly, we adapted our positive role model attributes survey section from the RoMAT questionnaire that was developed and validated in a cohort of Dutch general practitioner trainees for use in appraisal of clinical preceptors [5]. However, there were inherent differences in sociocultural backgrounds of the respondents, different levels (undergraduate vs. postgraduate) and settings (primary vs. tertiary care) of training, and intent of survey (for tutor appraisal vs. establishing role model traits) between the original RoMAT study and our cohort study. Secondly, both the RoMAT and negative attributes survey appear to feature more questions on teaching abilities, interpersonal skills and professional/ethical behaviors, as compared to clinical and technical skills, which could potentially skew our study findings. As such, we avoided making conclusive statements about the relative importance of attributes in the role model domains. Thirdly, our qualitative analyses relies solely on survey free-text responses, which admittedly lack the depth obtainable through interviews or focused-group discussions which can be performed until data saturation. This limits the quality obtainable of our qualitative findings, which is in contrast with our quantitative data. Finally, the study reflects the experiences of a single medical school cohort in Singapore, which potentially limits overall generalizability to other settings, given that there could be sociocultural nuances to role model perceptions attributed to differences in value/belief systems. For instance, there are intrinsic differences between Asian and Western cultures in how they perceive professional hierarchy, separation of personal and professional lives, and the relative importance of medical ethical constructs (e.g., autonomy) as compared to collectivism and relational considerations [35, 43]. Gender could also shape perceptions of role modelling, although exploring this was beyond the study scope, this is a potential avenue for future research (e.g., possible gendered expectations of professionalism, communication, or leadership).

Conclusion

In summary, our study found that final-year medical students placed great value on relational/interpersonal attributes (e.g. empathy, compassion, and professional conduct) in clinical role modeling. The presence of positive role models was seen as necessary for professional development, while encounters with negative role models were linked to diminished confidence and dissuasion from certain career paths. These findings collectively affirm the enduring impact of the hidden curriculum of medical education. Further qualitative studies, including interviews, focused group discussions, and ethnographic assessments, are needed to identify personal and real-world contextual factors that influence positive and negative role modeling behaviors. This would inform pedagogical and workplace-based interventions that would foster positive role modeling behaviors in the clinical training and practice environment.
