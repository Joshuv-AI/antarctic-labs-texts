Effect of active noise-cancelling headphones combined with music therapy on postoperative recovery quality after general anaesthesia for ankle fracture ORIF: a study protocol for a single-centre, four-arm 2×2 factorial randomised controlled trial in China
Authors: Tongtong Li, Yukai Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOperating room noise levels during ankle fracture open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) can reach levels well above the WHO recommended 35 dB(A) threshold for hospital treatment rooms. Neuroimaging evidence confirms that primary auditory cortex remains responsive to acoustic stimuli even under general anaesthesia (bispectral index 40–60), suggesting intraoperative noise may trigger stress responses via subcortical pathways. Active noise-cancelling (ANC) headphones combined with music therapy represent a promising non-pharmacological intervention for enhanced recovery after surgery protocols, yet no study has employed factorial design to isolate the independent and interactive effects of noise reduction and music therapy on postoperative recovery.Methods and analysisThis prospective, randomised, participant and outcome-assessor-blinded, four-arm, 2×2 factorial design trial will enrol 224 adult patients (aged 18–65 years) scheduled for elective ankle fracture ORIF under standardised anaesthesia combining saphenous nerve block with popliteal sciatic nerve block plus general anaesthesia at Deyang Sixth People’s Hospital, Sichuan, China. Patients will be randomised 1:1:1:1 to: (1) NC-MT group: ANC plus personalised music; (2) NC group: ANC only; (3) MT group: passive isolation plus personalised music and (4) Sham group: passive isolation only. All groups will wear identical Soundcore Space One headphones, applied after anaesthesia induction (loss of consciousness) and removed before emergence (Bispectral Index<60), so that patients cannot perceive between-group difference in device operating mode. The primary outcome is the 15-item Quality of Recovery score (QoR-15) at 24 hours postoperatively. Secondary outcomes include pain (Visual Analogue Scale), anxiety (6-item State-Trait Anxiety Inventory), sleep quality (Richards-Campbell Sleep Questionnaire), melody recognition test, opioid consumption and adverse events.Ethics and disseminationThis study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Deyang Sixth People’s Hospital, Sichuan, China, on 23 December 2025 (DSLYIRB-2025-11) and is conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice of the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH-GCP) guidelines. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants prior to enrolment. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication and academic conference presentations.Trial registration numberChiCTR2600123100.

Introduction

Operating room noise represents an underestimated perioperative stressor. The WHO Guidelines for Community Noise recommend that noise levels in hospital treatment and observation rooms should not exceed 35 dB(A), where dB(A) denotes A-weighted decibels that simulate human ear frequency response per IEC 61672-1:2013.1 The Occupational Safety and Health Administration mandates hearing protection programmes when 8-hour time-weighted average noise exposure reaches 85 dB(A).2 However, substantial evidence demonstrates that operating room noise levels systematically violate these safety standards. Katz’s seminal review in Anesthesiology documented that operating room noise frequently exceeds 80–100 dB(A), with levels maintained above 100 dB(A) for over 40% of surgical time during orthopaedic and neurosurgical procedures, with peaks exceeding 120 dB(A).3 Orthopaedic surgery represents a particularly high-exposure environment. Stephanie et al systematically measured 300 procedures across six orthopaedic subspecialties, finding mean peak levels of 96.9–102.0 dB, with 84% of procedures exceeding 85 dB and 35% exceeding 100 dB.4 During ankle fracture open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF), orthopaedic power tools generate particularly prominent noise levels: oscillating saws average 99.2 dB (range 81–131 dB), bone hammers can reach 113 dB, with peaks exceeding 140 dB.5 Critically, the causal relationship between intraoperative noise exposure and adverse patient outcomes has been established through high-quality evidence. You et al demonstrated in a prospective study of 200 patients under general anaesthesia that the high-noise group (>70 dB for ≥40% of surgical time) had significantly elevated postoperative Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) pain scores and increased analgesic consumption; an accompanying mouse model confirmed noise-induced hyperalgesia mechanisms.6 In a prospective, non-randomised, three-phase controlled study of paediatric operations, Engelmann et al reported that a team-directed operating-theatre noise-reduction programme was associated with a lower postoperative complication rate (10/56 (17.9%) vs 20/58 (34.5%); p<0.05), although the authors emphasised that the study was not powered for this endpoint, that the reduction was driven predominantly by minor complications and that complication severity was unchanged.7

A key theoretical premise of this study is that intraoperative noise may adversely affect patients through auditory pathways that remain active even under general anaesthesia. Krom et al provided direct evidence through intracranial electrode recordings from human patients: following propofol-induced loss of consciousness, neuronal firing and local field potentials in primary auditory cortex remained responsive to sound, with only higher-order auditory responses suppressed.8 Davis et al’s functional MRI study similarly confirmed that bilateral temporal lobe auditory responses persist at all propofol sedation levels, while prefrontal semantic processing is suppressed.9 Complementary electrocorticographic and thalamocortical evidence further supports preservation of core auditory processing during propofol anaesthesia.10 11 These landmark studies indicate that loss of consciousness primarily disrupts cortico-cortical signal transmission rather than thalamo-cortical relay—meaning sound continues to reach and activate brain auditory mechanisms, potentially triggering subcortical stress responses via thalamo-amygdalar pathways. Perioperative music therapy is among the most evidence-based non-pharmacological interventions. Hole et al’s landmark meta-analysis in The Lancet, incorporating 73 randomised controlled trials (RCTs), concluded that music significantly reduces postoperative pain, anxiety and analgesic requirements, whether initiated preoperatively, intraoperatively or postoperatively.12 Kühlmann et al extended this analysis with 92 RCTs (7385 patients), finding moderate-to-large reductions in anxiety (Mean Difference (MD) −0.69; 95% CI −0.88 to −0.50; p<0.001) and moderate reductions in pain (MD −0.50; 95% CI −0.66 to −0.34; p<0.001).13 de Witte et al’s two meta-analyses (104 RCTs, 9617 participants) reported a small-to-medium effect on physiological stress outcomes (d=0.380) and a medium effect on psychological stress outcomes (d=0.545), with the strongest physiological effect on heart rate (d=0.456), consistent with autonomic modulation.14 Clinical evidence for active noise-cancelling (ANC) headphones in perioperative care is rapidly accumulating. You et al conducted the most relevant trial: 90 patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery under general anaesthesia were randomised to noise-cancelling headphones or standard care, with movement-evoked pain significantly lower in the ANC group (2.7±1.0 vs 4.0±1.0; p<0.001) and opioid consumption reduced (44.2±12.8 vs 51.3±17.5 mg; p=0.032).15 The most critical evidence gap this study addresses is the lack of factorial design studies separating the independent contributions of ANC and music therapy. Tran et al randomised knee replacement patients to ANC headphones with silence versus ANC headphones with music—but since both groups received ANC, the independent effect of noise reduction could not be isolated.16 Dağcı et al employed a four-arm design comparing ANC, passive noise control, music therapy and control—but this was not a factorial design and did not include ANC+music or passive isolation+music groups, meaning interaction effects could not be assessed.17 This study is therefore explicitly positioned as an ‘explanatory trial’ rather than a ‘pragmatic trial’. According to Schwartz and Lellouch’s classic framework and the subsequent PRECIS-2 tool, explanatory trials prioritise internal validity, assessing biological effects under highly controlled conditions; pragmatic trials prioritise external validity, assessing clinical effectiveness under real-world conditions.18 19 This study’s core objective is to answer a mechanistic question: ‘Under minimising key confounding influences (including patient expectation effects and perceptual differences), do ANC and/or music therapy exert measurable biological effects on postoperative recovery in unconscious patients via subcortical auditory pathways?’ This positioning necessitates prioritising methodological rigour (participant and outcome-assessor blinding), even at the cost of not intervening during some clinical intervention windows.

Given the above-mentioned research gaps, a suitable surgical model is required to minimise confounding and reliably test our hypothesis. Ankle fracture ORIF was selected as the primary surgical model due to its nearly universal adoption of general anaesthesia, standardised operative procedures, high intraoperative noise exposure, stable epidemiological distribution and a relatively young patient population with low comorbidity burden, which minimises confounding factors and optimises trial feasibility and result interpretability.20 21

This study aims to employ a 2×2 factorial design to evaluate the main effects, combined effects, and potential synergistic effects of ANC (Factor A) and personalised music therapy (Factor B) on postoperative recovery quality (15-item Quality of Recovery score (QoR-15)) in patients undergoing ankle fracture ORIF under general anaesthesia.

Participant recruitment strategies

Eligible participants will be consecutively recruited from the outpatient and inpatient departments of our study centre. Physicians in charge will screen potential participants according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Detailed trial information will be introduced to eligible participants verbally and with written informed consent documents. After sufficient time for consideration, participants who agree to participate and sign the written informed consent form will be enrolled into this trial. Recruitment advertisements will not be used in this study.

Randomisation and blinding

A computer-generated randomisation sequence will be created by an independent statistician using R software, permuted blocks of variable size (details of block sizes are withheld from investigators until recruitment is complete in order to protect allocation concealment). Allocation ratio will be 1:1:1:1. Stratification factors include: (1) fracture complexity (unimalleolar vs bimalleolar vs trimalleolar) and (2) age group (18–40 vs 41–55 vs 56–65 years). A central web-based randomisation system will ensure allocation concealment. Blinding strategy: This study employs a ‘post-induction application, pre-emergence removal’ strategy. Patients and outcome assessors are blinded to group allocation; the therapists and physicians who apply the intervention are necessarily unblinded because the four device operating modes differ, but they do not participate in outcome assessment. After randomisation, both participants and outcome assessors are blinded to group allocation. The therapists/physicians who perform the intervention remain unblinded due to the inherent difference of interventions.

Post-randomisation withdrawal, protocol deviation and handling of missing data: if a participant withdraws consent after randomisation, data collected up to the point of withdrawal will be retained and included in the intention-to-treat analysis unless the participant requests complete data removal. Participants who are withdrawn by the investigator for safety reasons or who undergo an urgent change of intervention will remain in the intention-to-treat analysis in the group to which they were randomised. All withdrawals and reasons (where provided) will be reported in the final Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) flow diagram. Post-randomisation discontinuations will be summarised by treatment arm; group allocation will not be revealed to the assessor at the time of any withdrawal-related decision. Missing data patterns will be examined and handled as described in the Statistical analysis section.

Circumstances and procedures for unblinding: unscheduled unblinding is only permitted when a serious adverse event (AE) occurs or when urgent medical treatment adjustment is required and cannot be properly managed without knowing the group allocation.

Once an unblinding situation occurs, the principal investigator must be notified immediately. The unblinding request will be reviewed and approved by the research ethics committee. After approval, the designated researcher will complete the official unblinding procedure according to the standard operating procedure. The reason, time, person in charge and results of unblinding will be fully documented in the trial record. Unblinded participants will remain in the trial wherever possible, unless clinical circumstances warrant withdrawal.

Data collection and management

All study participants will be assigned a study identification number. Case Report Form (CRF) will be completed on paper forms. Data will then be entered and stored in a password-protected electronic database. All raw data were independently entered into the database by two researchers using double data entry. The original paper copies of CRFs and all study data will be stored in a locker within the study site, accessible to the involved researchers only.

Local anaesthetic: 0.33% ropivacaine, total volume 30 mL (saphenous nerve 10 mL, sciatic nerve 20 mL).

Block assessment: sensory block assessed 15 min post-injection using pinprick; successful block defined as diminished or absent pinprick sensation in blocked territories.

Emergence: sevoflurane/propofol discontinued after surgical closure; headphones removed while BIS <60 (before discontinuing anaesthetics); extubation after return of spontaneous respiration and consciousness; transfer to Post-Anaesthesia Care Unit.

Postoperative analgesia: patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA): sufentanil 100 µg+tropisetron 15 mg+normal saline to 100 mL; background infusion 2 mL/hour; bolus 0.5 mL; lockout 15 min; rescue analgesia: oxycodone hydrochloride 3 mg intravenous for Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) >4.

Eligibility criteria for study site and intervention deliverers: this is a single-centre trial. Intervention procedures will be performed by qualified attending physicians/nurses who hold valid professional practising certificates, have more than 2 years of clinical working experience and receive unified standardised training before trial implementation. Operators without corresponding qualifications and standardised training are not allowed to participate in intervention delivery.

Interventions

Music selection

The music selection is performed once, during the preoperative anaesthesia consultation (1 day before surgery), by the recruiting research physician together with the patient, before randomisation. Patients choose one of four pre-validated relaxing music styles: (1) classical instrumental (Mozart, Bach, Vivaldi; 60–80 beats per minute [BPM]); (2) light/new-age music (piano, guitar, harp); (3) nature soundscapes with music and (4) slow-tempo jazz instrumental. Volume is set at 50–60 dB; playback is in loop mode; playlist duration is ≥1 hours. In accordance with prior published research, all music tracks are instrumental without vocals, with a target tempo range of 60–80 beats/min.13 14 22–24 For nature soundscapes combined with background music, the embedded musical components were selected to meet this tempo target, whereas non-rhythmic natural ambient sounds are not subject to BPM constraints.

Existing studies have demonstrated that multiple forms of music interventions produce positive perioperative effects, yet individual musical preference plays a critical role in determining the effectiveness of music therapy.13 Personalised music was used to prevent bias arising from a standardised uniform playlist.

All recordings are drawn from public-domain or licensed music libraries. The same selected playlist is used for the participant if they are subsequently randomised to a music-containing arm; participants randomised to a non-music arm receive no audio playback, but complete the selection procedure identically so that all groups undergo the same preoperative process. This ensures that music selection cannot introduce a between-group perceptual difference before allocation.

Intervention allocation

Group allocation: all groups wear identical Soundcore Space One wireless Bluetooth headphones (ANC capability up to 40 dB reduction; passive isolation approximately 15–20 dB).

Infection control: headphone ear cushions will be disinfected with 75% isopropyl alcohol wipes before and after each use, following CDC guidelines for low-level disinfection of non-critical medical devices.25 26 Alcohol is effective against most bacteria, fungi and enveloped viruses, with rapid action and no residue.27

Group configurations of the factorial design are summarised in table 1.

NC-MT group (ANC+personalised music): ANC activated (maximum level); Bluetooth-connected audio device plays patient-selected playlist (60–80 BPM, instrumental); volume 50–60 dB; loop playback.

NC group (ANC only): ANC activated (maximum level); Bluetooth connected but no audio output (silent mode).

MT group (music only): ANC deactivated; plays same patient-selected music; passive isolation only (~15–20 dB).

Sham group (sham control): headphones powered off (no ANC, no music); passive physical isolation only (~15–20 dB).

Critical device settings (all groups): disable ‘transparency mode’; disable ‘wearing detection’; disable ‘adaptive ANC’; disable ‘auto power-off’ (critical to prevent NC group headphones from auto-shutting down due to no audio input); reset device before pairing; verify battery >50% before each use.

Intervention timing: application immediately after anaesthesia induction (confirmed loss of eyelash reflex); removal when surgical closure complete and BIS <60.

AE definition and assessment methods

An AE is defined as any untoward medical occurrence in a participant during the trial period, regardless of its causal relationship with the study intervention, including abnormal clinical symptoms, physical signs and abnormal laboratory test results.

Systematic safety assessment will be performed at every scheduled follow-up visit throughout the trial. Researchers will actively inquire about participants’ subjective discomfort, conduct physical examinations, and review laboratory indicators to screen for potential AEs. Unscheduled passive safety monitoring will also be conducted throughout the entire study period: all spontaneously reported discomfort or abnormal physical conditions from participants will be recorded and assessed timely.

Once an AE is identified, investigators will evaluate the severity, duration, outcome, and causal relationship with the trial intervention. All AEs will be fully documented in the case report form, including occurrence time, manifestation, intervention measures, and prognosis. All serious AEs will be immediately reported to the principal investigator and institutional ethics committee following standard regulatory procedures.

On postoperative day 1, participants will listen to six musical excerpts, consisting of three old tracks played intraoperatively and three novel tracks that have never been presented previously. Participants will rate subjective liking for each excerpt on a 1-to-5-point scale (1=strongly dislike; 5=strongly like). The signal-detection index d′ will be calculated based on liking ratings for old versus novel melodies as the primary summary measure for implicit-memory performance.

The melody-recognition test serves dual purposes: as an anaesthetic-depth safety indicator and for mechanistic exploration. At group-level observation of near-zero mean d′ (no detectable group-level evidence of implicit-memory formation), together with statistically superior QoR-15 scores in music-receiving groups compared with non-music groups, would provide suggestive evidence that postoperative benefits from music therapy are independent of implicit-memory formation.

Sample size

Primary endpoint: QoR-15 at 24 hours.

Design: 2×2 factorial.

Assumptions based on literature: expected marginal means: ANC effect+5 points; music effect+5 points; interaction+2 points. Within-group SD: 14 points. Minimal Clinically Important Difference: 6.0 points.28–30

Sample size calculation: effect size f=5/(14×√2)≈0.25; α=0.05 (two-sided); power (1−β)=0.80. G*Power 3.1.9.7 indicates a total of 180 participants (45 per arm). Because the two factors (ANC, music) are analysed at the margins using the full sample, no additional inflation for multiple comparisons is required for main-effect testing.33 34 To retain 50 evaluable participants per arm as a pragmatic target, we set the base sample at 200 (50 per arm). Adjusting for an anticipated 10% loss to follow-up using the standard formula N=n/(1−d) gives 200/0.90=222.2, rounded up to 224 to preserve balance across the four arms (56 per arm).34 Final sample size: 224 participants (56 per group) Power analysis: ANC main effect: 85% power; music main effect: 85% power; interaction effect (f=0.15): 65% power (exploratory); NC-MT versus Sham (d=0.86): 90% power.

Several strategies will be implemented to ensure adequate participant enrolment to attain the target sample size of 224 participants.

Preoperative anaesthesia outpatient clinics and orthopaedic inpatient wards will serve as the primary recruitment sites. Eligible patients will be screened consecutively by trained research physicians during pre-anaesthetic assessment.

Potential participants will be fully informed of trial information during preoperative consultation. Research staff will screen patients daily according to pre-specified eligibility criteria.

Statistical analysis

Analysis populations: intent-to-treat (ITT): all randomised patients; per-protocol (PP): completers without major protocol deviations; safety: all patients receiving any intervention. Primary analysis in ITT population.

Primary analysis: two-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) factorial model: QoR-15 = μ+α(ANC)+β(Music)+(αβ)(ANC×Music)+ε.

Main effects calculation: ANC main effect=((NC-MT+NC)/2)-((MT+Sham)/2); Music main effect=((NC-MT+MT)/2)-((NC+Sham)/2).

If p ≥0.10: no significant interaction; simplify to main-effects model.

If p ≥0.10: no significant interaction; simplify to main-effects model.

Covariate analysis: to control for confounding by anaesthetic depth on auditory cortex suppression, Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) will include: mean MAC value (sevoflurane) or mean effect-site concentration (propofol TCI); surgical duration; tourniquet time; vasoactive drug use.

Effect sizes: partial η² for main/interaction effects; Cohen’s d with 95% CI for pairwise comparisons.

Secondary analyses: VAS pain: repeated-measures mixed-effects model; STAI-6: factorial ANCOVA with baseline STAI-6 and Surgical Fear Questionnaire as covariates; RCSQ: factorial ANOVA; melody recognition: one-way ANOVA for d′; opioid consumption: factorial ANOVA; PONV: logistic regression.

Multiple comparisons: main-effect tests for ANC and music are analysed at the margins and each performed at two-sided α=0.05 without additional multiplicity adjustment, consistent with standard analysis of factorial trials.33 For any post-hoc pairwise comparisons among the four cells, Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference will be applied to control the family-wise error rate.

Sensitivity analyses: covariate-adjusted (including anaesthetic concentration); PP; completers only.

Missing data: <5% complete case; 5%–20% multiple imputation (m=20); >20% pattern-mixture models. Software: SPSS V.26.0, R V.4.3.0. Significance: two-sided p<0.05.

Data collection schedule and procedure: all baseline, outcome and safety data will be collected strictly in accordance with the predefined trial time points. Trained researchers will complete data collection during scheduled follow-up visits. All clinical indicators, scale scores and AE information will be recorded truthfully and timely. Missing data will be confirmed with participants immediately to reduce data omission and recording errors.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not directly involved in study design. Results will be disseminated to participants through lay summaries.

Monitoring

The study will be carried out in accordance with the protocol, as it will be registered. No changes in the study protocol are expected to be made after the study starts. However, in case of any unexpected circumstances requiring alterations of the protocol, changes will be immediately applied to the protocol registry site at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR), and, if relevant enough, reported to the institutional ethics committee.

This study is assessed as a low-risk clinical trial with non-invasive and low-hazard intervention. No severe, irreversible or life-threatening safety risks are anticipated for enrolled participants.

Comprehensive ongoing safety surveillance and trial quality supervision will be conducted by the institutional review board/ethics committee through routine semi-annual monitoring audits, together with regular internal quality control performed by the principal investigator and research team. Existing monitoring mechanisms are sufficient to guarantee participant safety, trial integrity and protocol compliance. Therefore, establishment of a Data Monitoring Committee (DMC) is not necessary for this study. No relevant DMC charter is required.

Interim analyses and stopping guidelines

No pre-specified statistical interim efficacy analysis is planned for this study. The interim periodic safety review and assessment during trial conduct are undertaken by the ethics committee.

Based on routine ethical monitoring conducted once every 6 months, the ethics committee will periodically review cumulative safety data, serious AEs, protocol deviations, participant drop-out rate and recruitment progress, and conduct ongoing interim benefit-risk assessment.

The final decision authority for trial continuation, suspension or early termination rests jointly with the ethics committee and the principal investigator.

The ethics committee is authorised to issue a trial termination recommendation and initiate termination procedures if a negative benefit-risk profile emerges, frequent serious AEs occur, major uncorrectable protocol deviations persist, recruitment is prolonged and stagnant or identifiable participant safety risks arise.

No unblinded interim efficacy data will be generated or shared, no external access to interim data is applicable, and all interim assessments rely on routine clinical and ethical monitoring data only.

Ethics and dissemination

Research ethics approval

This study will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and Good Clinical Practice of the International Council for Harmonisation guidelines. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Deyang Sixth People’s Hospital, Sichuan, China, on 23 December 2025 (DSLYIRB-2025-11). This study was registered on chictr.org.cn on 21 April 2026 (ChiCTR2600123100).

Protocol amendments

All substantial protocol amendments will be submitted to the institutional ethics committee for review and approval prior to implementation. Once approved, amendments will be timely communicated to all research staff, investigators and relevant clinical departments. Any amendments that may affect participant safety, risk benefit profile or informed consent content will be re-explained to enrolled participants and re-signed informed consent will be obtained where necessary.

Informed consent process

Written informed consent will be obtained from each eligible participant before any trial-specific procedures are performed. Qualified research physicians are responsible for introducing the trial purpose, interventions, potential risks, benefits and participants’ rights of voluntary participation and withdrawal. The informed consent document will be signed and dated by both the participant and the authorised researcher. No participant will be enrolled without valid written informed consent. A copy of the participant information sheet and consent form is provided as online supplemental material.

No additional informed consent for biological sample collection or storage is required in this trial. Only routine clinical data generated during standard clinical care will be collected and analysed.

Dissemination policy

Results of this RCT will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication and academic conference presentations.

Trial oversight and safety monitoring

No steering committee, endpoint adjudication committee or independent data monitoring committee was established for this single-centre, low-risk clinical trial. The study is a small-sample exploratory intervention trial with no planned interim analysis, no early stopping rules and minimal potential risk to participants. All study conduct, participant safety, trial progress and endpoint judgement are overseen and reviewed by the principal investigator and the institutional ethics committee throughout the research period. Therefore, these independent external committees are not required for this trial.

Privacy and confidentiality

All identifiable personal information will be de-identified immediately after collection. Participant-related data will be securely stored in encrypted electronic files and locked paper files during and after the trial. All research staff shall strictly abide by local privacy regulations. Identifiable personal data will not be publicly shared or disclosed without official authorisation.

Post-trial care and compensation for trial-related harm

All participants will receive routine clinical care throughout the trial period. No additional specialised post-trial medical arrangement is planned for this study. If any AE or trial-related harm occurs, timely medical intervention will be provided, and reasonable compensation will be offered in accordance with institutional rules and relevant clinical regulatory requirements.
