Real-world management and in-hospital outcomes of foreign body-associated esophageal perforations in the United States: a national retrospective cohort study
Authors: Stephanie Rodriguez, Spencer R. Goble, Gaurav Suryawanshi, Khalid Ahmed, Nabeel Azeem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimsEsophageal perforations can be managed conservatively, endoscopically, or surgically. We aimed to characterize the outcomes of these approaches and assess the impact of intervention timing.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed hospitalizations in the National Inpatient Sample from 2016 to 2022 for foreign body-associated esophageal perforations. Patients were grouped into endoscopic, surgical, or conservative management. Outcomes included mortality, respiratory failure, sepsis, hemorrhage, parenteral nutrition, and gastrostomy.ResultsAmong the 2,045 patients, 145 (7.1%) underwent endoscopic management, 190 (9.3%) underwent surgical management, and 1,710 (83.6%) received conservative treatment. Food impaction accounted for 76% of foreign bodies. The mortality rates were 0%, 5.3%, and 2.9%, respectively. Respiratory failure occurred in 17.2% of endoscopically managed patients and 42.1% of surgically managed patients. Surgical cases had higher use of parenteral nutrition (15.8%) and gastrostomy (31.6%), whereas conservative management had the highest hemorrhage rate (18.1%). Intervention timing was not significantly associated with outcomes.ConclusionsMost foreign body-associated esophageal perforations are managed conservatively. Among patients selected for intervention, endoscopic management was associated with lower rates of mortality, selected complications, and healthcare utilization than surgery. These findings reflect clinical triage pathways and should be interpreted cautiously, given potential selection bias, confounding by indication, and administrative data limitations.

INTRODUCTION

Esophageal perforation is rare but associated with high morbidity and mortality.1,2 Serious adverse events such as mediastinitis, sepsis, and multiorgan failure can develop after esophageal perforation.2,3 Foreign body ingestion remains a common etiology, especially in high-risk populations such as individuals with psychiatric disorders or with predisposing esophageal pathologies, including strictures or eosinophilic esophagitis.3-6

Management of esophageal perforations fall into three main categories: surgical repair, endoscopic therapy, and conservative management. Treatment choice is typically guided by clinical factors, including the time since perforation, perforation size and location, presence of contamination, and patient comorbidities.1,7,8 Surgical repair has historically been the cornerstone of management, as it offers the ability to debride necrotic tissue, drain mediastinal or pleural contamination, and directly close the defect.9 Endoscopic techniques are rapidly evolving and include standard clipping, endoscopic suturing, over-the-scope clips (OTSC) for focal defects, fully covered self-expanding metal stents (SEMS) to bridge larger perforations, and endoscopic vacuum therapy (EVT) for continuous drainage and tissue remodeling.10-13 These endoscopic approaches offer potentially less-invasive procedures that are better tolerated in frail patients.14 Conservative management, including bowel rest, intravenous antibiotics, and close clinical monitoring, is frequently used in patients with leaks, hemodynamic stability, and no signs of systemic infection.7,9

Despite position statements by the World Society of Emergency Surgery and the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy, there is no universally accepted algorithm for treatment selection.5,6 Both societies emphasize the importance of individualized treatment decisions based on hemodynamic stability, timing, contamination, and local expertise.5,6 Many of the recommendations are consensus-based rather than evidence-based. For foreign body-associated perforations, in particular, large-scale nationally representative data describing real-world management patterns and in-hospital outcomes after clinical triage are lacking.15

To address this gap, we conducted a national retrospective cohort study using the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) to examine how foreign body-associated esophageal perforations are managed in routine practice and how outcomes vary across management strategies. We compared in-hospital outcomes among patients treated endoscopically, surgically, or conservatively, and evaluated whether the timing of procedural intervention was associated with differences in clinical outcomes. Given the observational design and the limited clinical details available in the administrative data, this study aimed to describe practice patterns and associated outcomes, rather than establish comparative effectiveness.

METHODS

Study design and database description

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from 2016 to 2022 in the NIS. The NIS is the largest publicly available all-payer inpatient care database in the United States. It captures a stratified 20% sample of hospital discharges and is designed to generate national estimates after standardized weighting. All reported results are weighted.

Study sample and variables

Adult patients (≥18 years) hospitalized with foreign body-associated esophageal perforations were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), Clinical Modification diagnosis codes (Supplementary Table 1). The inclusion criteria were both an esophageal foreign body and a perforation diagnosis, with at least one listed as the primary discharge diagnosis. In the NIS, the primary discharge diagnosis is considered chiefly responsible for admission. This approach was applied to reduce misclassification by excluding hospitalizations in which foreign body ingestion or esophageal perforation occurred as secondary complications during admission for unrelated conditions. Patients with esophageal cancer (n=10) were excluded because the underlying cancer substantially alters management strategies, prognosis, and indications for interventions, introducing clinical heterogeneity that cannot be addressed within the administrative data. A flow diagram illustrating cohort selection and exclusion is shown in Supplementary Figure 1.

Patients were categorized into three management groups: endoscopic, surgical, or conservative (non-procedural), based on ICD-10-Procedure Coding System procedure codes recorded during the admission. Both open and minimally invasive percutaneous approaches were used in the surgical group. To be classified as endoscopic, documentation of defect closure (e.g., stent placement, clipping, or suturing) was required; cases with diagnostic endoscopy only were not considered for definitive endoscopic management. The NIS does not allow direct identification of treatment crossover (e.g., progression from conservative or endoscopic to surgical intervention), although the timing of intervention and rates of upper endoscopy (Table 1) may provide indirect insight.

Demographic variables (age, sex, race/ethnicity) and clinical characteristics, including the Charlson comorbidity index (CCI), were compared across the management groups. Food impaction, achalasia, and eosinophilic esophagitis were treated as associated conditions and not considered mutually exclusive etiologies. Among patients undergoing endoscopic or surgical management, timing of repair was categorized as same-day or one or more days after admission based on calendar-day procedure variables available in the NIS, rather than exact symptom-to-intervention intervals.

Study objectives

The primary objective of this study was to determine whether clinical outcomes varied among patients with foreign body-associated esophageal perforations managed endoscopically, surgically, or conservatively. The clinical outcomes included in-hospital mortality, respiratory failure, sepsis, hemorrhage, parenteral nutrition initiation, and gastrostomy tube placement. These objectives were intended to describe the associations between management strategies and outcomes, rather than to establish comparative effectiveness.

Respiratory failure was defined as mechanical ventilation for 24 or more consecutive hours to avoid misclassification due to procedural sedation. Sepsis and hemorrhage were defined using secondary diagnostic codes, whereas parenteral nutrition initiation and gastrostomy were defined using procedure codes. Hemorrhage was subclassified using ICD-10 code into gastrointestinal, respiratory (including hemothorax), and unspecified categories. No cases of unspecified hemorrhage were detected in this cohort.

The secondary objectives included determining the impact of intervention timing on clinical outcomes and the impact of intervention type on healthcare utilization outcomes. In the assessment of timing, patients who underwent repair on the first day of admission were compared to those who underwent repair one or more days after presentation. The assessed utilization outcomes included discharge-to-home rates, length of stay (LOS) in days, and hospital costs in U.S. dollars. Discharge to home and LOS assessments were restricted to patients who survived until discharge.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to compare the baseline characteristics across the management groups. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests, and continuous variables were compared using Student t-tests, as appropriate.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate the associations between the management strategy and in-hospital mortality, respiratory failure, sepsis, hemorrhage, parenteral nutrition initiation, gastrostomy tube placement, and discharge to home. Models were adjusted for patient age and CCI, as reliable measures of acute illness severity were unavailable in the NIS, and event counts were insufficient to support more complex models. Multivariable regression analyses were included to describe the adjusted associations rather than to imply causal inferences or treatment superiority. Prior research has consistently identified esophageal malignancy (cases excluded from the present study), age, and comorbidity burden to be associated with poor outcomes in patients with esophageal perforations.16-18 The results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

A negative binomial regression model was used to assess LOS associations and a generalized linear model was used to assess cost associations. The results were expressed as adjusted rate ratios (aRRs) with 95% CIs. Absolute risk differences (ARDs) were calculated for descriptive purposes, but were not emphasized when events were rare or zero in the reference group. Adjusted mortality estimates involving the endoscopy group were not calculated because of zero observed events.

Univariable regression analyses were also performed to explore unadjusted associations and are reported in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3. Analyses evaluating intervention timing were underpowered and should be considered hypothesis-generating. No imputation was performed and hospitalizations with missing dispositions, LOS, or cost data were excluded from the analyses. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value of <0.05. All analyses were conducted using Stata ver. 17.0 (StataCorp. LLC).

Ethical statements

As a de-identified and publicly available dataset, the use of the NIS is exempt from institutional review board oversight.

RESULTS

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 2,045 adult patients hospitalized for foreign body-associated esophageal perforations were identified in the NIS from 2016 to 2022. Of these, 145 (7.1%) underwent endoscopic management, 190 (9.3%) underwent surgical management, and 1,710 (83.6%) were treated conservatively without procedural intervention. The burden of comorbidities was similar across the management groups (Table 1). The mean age for all hospitalizations was 57.6 years, and 35.2% of the patients were female. The majority (76.0%) of the foreign bodies had food impaction. The endoscopic group had the highest proportion of patients with eosinophilic esophagitis (20.7%) and achalasia (6.9%); these differences, however, were not statistically significant. Among patients who underwent endoscopic repair, 50 (34.5%) received esophageal stents. The remaining patients underwent non-stent closure using clips or sutures, although procedural details were limited to the closure code.

Clinical outcomes based on intervention type

The overall in-hospital mortality was 2.9%. Mortality and complication rates varied according to management strategy. No deaths were reported among patients receiving endoscopic treatment, versus 5.3% in the surgical group and 2.9% in the conservative group.

The rate of respiratory failure was significantly higher among surgically managed patients (42.1%) compared to those managed endoscopically (17.2%) or conservatively (12.6%) (p<0.05). Sepsis occurred in 29.0% of surgical patients, compared to 10.3% and 15.5% in the endoscopic and conservative groups, respectively. The odds of sepsis were significantly higher in the surgical group compared to conservative management (aOR, 2.39; 95% CI, 1.12–5.09; p=0.024), whereas the difference between endoscopic and surgical groups was not statistically significant.

Hemorrhage was most frequently observed in the conservative treatment group (18.1%), although the differences across the groups did not reach statistical significance (Table 2). No episodes of gastrointestinal hemorrhage occurred in the endoscopic group and no respiratory hemorrhage was reported in the surgical group. No cases were identified as unspecified hemorrhage, and codes were not detailed.

Nutritional interventions were most common in surgical patients; parenteral nutrition was initiated in 15.8%, 8.8%, and 3.4% of surgical, conservative, and endoscopic patients, respectively (all pairwise, p>0.05). Gastrostomy tube placement occurred in 31.6% of surgical patients, 10.3% of endoscopic patients, and 5.8% of conservative patients, with a significant difference between surgical and conservative management (p<0.001) and a borderline difference between surgical and endoscopic management (p=0.055).

Clinical outcomes based on timing of intervention

Of the 335 patients who underwent intervention, 205 (61.2%) underwent the procedure on the day of admission. There were no significant differences in mortality (2.4% vs. 3.9%, p=0.403) or respiratory failure (31.7% vs. 30.8%, p=0.619) between the early and delayed intervention groups. All hemorrhage events occurred in patients who received the same-day intervention (9.8%), with no cases observed in the delayed group. The rate of sepsis was comparable between the same-day (19.5%) and delayed (23.1%) intervention groups (p=0.957). Similarly, no significant differences were observed in the rates of parenteral nutrition or gastrostomy between the same-day and delayed intervention groups (Table 3).

Healthcare utilization outcomes

Of the 1,980 patients who lived until discharge, 1,945 (98.2%) had available disposition information. The discharge-to-home rates were highest in the endoscopic group (93.1%), followed by the conservative group (65.2%) and the surgical group (63.2%). The difference between the endoscopic and surgical group was statistically significant (aOR, 11.86; 95% CI, 2.40–58.85; p=0.002) as was the difference between endoscopic and conservative management (p=0.008). The mean LOS was lower in the endoscopic group than in both the conservative and surgical groups (7.3 vs. 9.4 vs. 15.7 days, respectively). The difference between the endoscopic group and surgical group was statistically significant (aRR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.41–0.82; p=0.002) although the difference between the endoscopic and conservative group was not (p=0.147). The mean cost was the lowest in the conservative group ($24,503) compared to $32,612 in the endoscopic group and $62,437 in the surgical group. The difference in cost between endoscopic and surgical management was statistically significant (aRR, 0.50; 95% CI, 0.35–0.70; p<0.001).

DISCUSSION

In this large, nationally representative cohort study, over 80% of patients with foreign body-associated esophageal perforations were managed conservatively without procedural intervention, which is consistent with prior reports indicating that nonoperative management continues to be the default strategy in many U.S. hospitals. This approach is supported by the literature for patients with perforations, stable vital signs, and no evidence of systemic infections. However, nearly 20% of conservatively treated patients in our cohort experienced hemorrhage—the highest rate among all groups— raising concerns that some patients managed conservatively may still develop clinically significant complications related to underlying mucosal injury, food impaction, or pre-existing esophageal disease, rather than failure of conservative management itself.7,9,19-21 Because temporal sequencing cannot be determined, the hemorrhage may precede, prompt, or follow management decisions. Importantly, the conservative cohort likely represented a heterogeneous population, including both low-risk patients with perforations and higher-risk patients deemed unsuitable for invasive intervention because of comorbidities, frailty, or physiological instability. This heterogeneity may explain the observed complication patterns and underscores the need for more precise risk stratification when selecting nonoperative management.

Because treatment selection reflects clinical triage decisions rather than random assignment, differences observed across management strategies should be interpreted as outcomes within established care pathways, rather than as evidence of intrinsic modality superiority. Among patients who underwent procedural management, in-hospital mortality, respiratory failure, and sepsis were the lowest in the endoscopic group. No deaths were observed in the endoscopic group, versus 5.3% and 2.9% mortality in the surgical and conservative groups, respectively. Given the relatively small size of the endoscopic cohort, the absence of observed mortality should be interpreted cautiously, as it does not imply the inherent superiority of endoscopic therapy. Nonetheless, the observed pattern is consistent with prior literature, suggesting that endoscopic therapies—such as OTSC, SEMS, and EVT—may achieve successful closure without the physiological burden of open surgery.12,19,22-24 These findings reinforce the evolving role of therapeutic endoscopy as a first-line strategy for patient selection.

Historically, esophageal perforation has a high mortality rate of 10%–30%, driven by factors such as delayed diagnosis and the need for surgical repair.1,25,26 More recent data suggest that mortality has declined, likely due to earlier detection and broader use of minimally invasive therapies.27 Our findings add to the existing evidence describing favorable outcomes among selected patients managed endoscopically, although causal inferences cannot be made.

Patients undergoing surgery in our cohort were younger and had a similar comorbidity burden, but experienced the highest rates of respiratory failure and sepsis. This likely reflects a selection bias, in which operative management was reserved for patients with more severe, delayed, or refractory disease. Independent predictors of mortality in patients with esophageal perforation include noncontained leaks, sepsis at presentation, and delayed intervention, all of which may have been overrepresented in the surgical group.28 These patterns emphasize the need for structured triage criteria to identify the ideal candidates for early endoscopic or surgical intervention.

Beyond these primary outcomes, real-world patterns of nutritional support reflect the downstream impact of the intervention type. Patients who underwent surgical repair had markedly higher rates of parenteral nutrition and gastrostomy tube placement, consistent with the physiological disruption and prolonged recovery associated with open surgical repair.5,16,29 In contrast, endoscopically managed patients had significantly lower rates of parenteral nutrition and gastrostomy tube placement. These findings describe the differences in nutritional support and resource utilization across management strategies.

The timing of intervention has consistently been emphasized in guidelines, with early treatment, typically within 24 hours, linked to reduced mortality and adverse event rates.1,2,30,31 However, in our study, the same-day and delayed-intervention groups had similar mortality and sepsis rates. While this may reflect adequate stabilization with antibiotics, bowel rest, and supportive care, it could also suggest clinical differences between the groups. Importantly, patients undergoing same-day versus delayed interventions may differ systematically in baseline severity and urgency of presentation, which cannot be fully accounted for using administrative data. Hemorrhage occurred only in the same-day intervention group, which could suggest that bleeding prompted more urgent management; however, the sequence of events, whether hemorrhage preceded or followed the intervention—could not be determined from the dataset. Some prior studies similarly reported that delaying intervention for more than 24 hours may not adversely affect outcomes if appropriate supportive measures are in place.1,2,32

Although our results support the benefits of endoscopic therapy, they must be interpreted in the context of potential indication-related confounders. Surgical patients may have had more severe disease or failed prior therapies, whereas conservatively managed patients may have been considered too high risk for intervention. These unmeasured differences likely contributed to outcome variation and underscore the need for more prospective data to guide treatment selection in this heterogeneous population.

This study has several limitations inherent to the use of NIS data. The diagnosis and procedure coding may be subject to coding errors or misclassification. Nasoenteric feeding was excluded because of insufficient documentation in administrative coding, which limited the ability to reliably identify this intervention. The database does not include certain clinical variables, such as perforation size, location, extent of contamination, or hemodynamic status, which may affect treatment selection and outcomes. As noted above, this limits adjustment for disease severity and introduces potential confounding and selection bias. Procedural details were also limited; specific endoscopic and surgical techniques could not be distinguished, thereby limiting insights into best practices within each modality.

In this nationally representative analysis of foreign body-associated esophageal perforations, conservative management was the most common approach, yet carried the highest rate of hemorrhage. Although used in a relatively small number of cases, endoscopic intervention has demonstrated lower rates of mortality, respiratory failure, and sepsis. These findings demonstrate favorable outcomes among selected patients managed endoscopically and are consistent with the growing role of therapeutic endoscopy in contemporary practice. They also highlight the need for more structured triage pathways to guide early interventions. Future studies incorporating detailed clinical variables are required to refine patient selection and improve outcomes in this high-risk population.
