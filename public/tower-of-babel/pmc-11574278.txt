The effects of structured coordinative exercise protocol on physical fitness, motor competence and inhibitory control in preschool children
Authors: Ali Mert Şendil, Umut Canlı, Bodor Bin sheeha, Nouf H. Alkhamees, Alexios Batrakoulis, Sameer Badri Al-Mhanna
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The aim of the study was to evaluate the effects of a structured coordinative exercise intervention based on motor skill elements on physical fitness (PF), motor competence (MC) and inhibitory control (IC) in preschool children. A total of 41 kindergarten children (27 boys and 17 girls) aged between 5 and 6 years participated and were divided into exercise (EG; n = 18) and control (CG; n = 23) groups. The exercise group participated in a structured coordinative exercise programme focusing on locomotor and balance skills such as side-stepping, galloping, jumping and running for 30 min, 2 days a week for 8 weeks. The control group followed the normal curriculum. Children participated in measurements before and after 8 weeks, respectively; motor competence was assessed using the Körperkoordinationstest für Kinder (KTK3+) test battery, which includes eye-hand coordination and other coordinative skills. Physical fitness was measured by balance, agility and vertical jump tests. Inhibitory control was assessed using the Go/NoGo test in the Early Years Toolbox. As a result of the two-factor analysis of variance for mixed measures to determine whether being in the exercising group had a significant effect on the test scores, group by time interaction effect showed that the increase in the scores of the exercise group was significantly higher than that of the control group in the parameters of static balance, KTK sideways jump and KTK eye-hand coordination [respectively: F(1−39) = 6.993, p = 0.012; 6.443, p = 0.015; 8.180, p = 0.007). The results show that structured coordinative exercises improve PF and MC but have no significant effect on IC. This study targets the development of motor and cognitive skills considered to be important in preschool children and is one of the few studies to evaluate these parameters simultaneously. It also provides a comprehensive perspective on interventions aimed to positively affect health and development in early childhood.Trial Registration: NCT06631248. Registered on October 07, 2024.

Introduction

The preschool period is critical for children’s physical, cognitive and motor skill development1,2. Studies revealed that physical activity (PA) participation is important for children at this period, which positively affects health factors and developmental processes3–7. At this point, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that children, including the preschool age range, participate in at least 60 min of moderate-intensity PA every day8. However, current research results have determined that children participate below the recommended PA levels9–11. Low PA participation in children leads to health problems such as obesity and cardiovascular risk factors12,13 and negatively affects motor skill development14.

Regular PA participation in the preschool period has a significant effect on physical fitness (PF) and motor competence (MC)15–17. PF and MC elements are also key for healthy development18,19, as they are recognised as important health markers that influence not only physical performance but also long-term health20,21. Furthermore, many studies emphasise the importance of the interplay between PF and MC and show that improvement in one parameter leads to gains in the other22–24. Moreover, it has been reported that these two parameters are closely linked to cognitive processes such as executive functions, which are of major importance during early development25–30. The relationships between these parameters are complex and the holistic development of these processes in early childhood is of great importance31. Therefore, it is recommended that PA and exercise experiences in preschool period should be organised as “deliberate preparation” in preschool education institutions32,33. It is thought that this purposeful approach will contribute to the development of readiness level34, motor and cognitive skills35,36, and PF level37 in the preschool period and will contribute to children to maintain a more active lifestyle and to the formation of healthy foundations for the future38–41.

Coordinative exercises, which we designed in our study and which are included in structured physical activity types, are characterised by multifaceted processes that include gross and fine motor movements as well as motor planning and goal-oriented behaviours in harmony with body components within the scope of Newell’s (1985) definition of coordination42,43. However, these exercises should not be confused with Bernstein’s theory of ‘coordinative structure’; Bernstein’s theory explains how the degrees of freedom of body parts such as muscles and joints are managed and how motor control is achieved44. The structured coordinative exercise protocol focuses on supporting motor skills through these control mechanisms. Operationally, the protocol was applied two days a week for 8 weeks with sessions lasting thirty minutes and consisted of progressive exercises involving locomotor and balance skills. Cognitive performance may also be positively affected by such coordinative interventions45–48. The reason for this is that motor and cognitive development are thought to have similar developmental timelines in this period, and the relationship between the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and the cerebellum in the brain structure may be fundamentally intertwined with motor and cognitive performance48–50. In particular, coordinative exercises have focussed on a set of cognitive processes located in the DLPFC called executive functions51,52. The reason is that the complex, controlled and adaptive movement elements inherent in coordinative exercises fit the definition of executive functions, which includes cognitive processes responsible for regulating, controlling and organising goal-directed behaviours53–55.

Inhibitory control (IC) within executive functions includes self-control against the strong attraction of external stimuli and internal impulses, selective attention and intelligent behaviour56,57. IC undergoes a significant development from preschool to eight years of age58,59. It is especially important in the preschool period in terms of enabling children’s self-control, giving correct responses to environmental variables in a short time, adapting to complex situations and providing a successful learning condition56,60–62. In addition, longitudinal studies on the importance of IC have observed that children with good IC levels have higher levels of well-being, a better quality of life and lower crime rates in adulthood compared to those with low IC levels63–65. At the same time, IC is also important for academic success66–68. Therefore, interventions for IC in the preschool period, supported through coordinative exercises that offer rich environmental features and learning environments, can promote IC development and make important contributions to children’s positive development, health and social well-being56,63,69–71. In the light of all this evidence, the study aimed to determine the simultaneous changes in PF, MC and IC parameters of the coordinative exercise protocol designed according to the developmental needs of preschool children. While these parameters are usually addressed separately in the literature, the focus of our study is to present a comprehensive and innovative perspective that evaluates the contributions of a specific exercise programme to motor and cognitive development in a holistic framework by examining three critical developmental areas with an integrated approach.

Methods

Participants

This study was carried out with the voluntary participation of a total of 41 children, 27 boys and 14 girls, aged 5–6 years (M = 5.75, SD = 0.23), attending a kindergarten. The power calculation, conducted under a one-tailed hypothesis and considering a large effect size, a significance level of 0.05, and a power of 0.80, suggests that a total sample size of 40 participants across both groups is required72. Study inclusion criteria were: (a) typically developing children; (b) participants should not be taking medication; (c) they should not have any cardiovascular, neurological, orthopaedic or psychiatric disease. Study exclusion criteria were: (a) having any cardiovascular, neurological, orthopaedic or psychiatric disease; (b) having an intellectual disability. Inclusion and exclusion criterias were determined by completing an informed consent form after all participants and parents were explained the study procedure in detail. Participants who did not fulfil the inclusion criteria were excluded from the experimental procedure. Before the study started, the ethics committee protocol was approved by the Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee of Tekirdag Namik Kemal University (2022.197.10.21). The experimental study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Experimental procedure

The research was divided into groups according to the convenience sampling method. Convenience sampling is a non-random sampling method in which the sample to be selected from the main mass is determined by the judgement of the researcher. In convenience sampling, data are collected from the main mass in the easiest, fastest and most economical way73,74. At this point, two classes in the same kindergarten, which accepted to participate in the study and met the conditions for participation in the study, was assigned as the control group (CG; n = 23) and the other class as the exercise group (EG; n = 18). In the first session of the measurements, a form containing the demographic information of the participants was filled out by the researchers and explanations about the measurement protocols were made. Anthropometric tests including body weight and height measurements were performed. Afterwards, Go/No-Go measurement was performed to determine the IC parameter in a quiet environment. Finally, the KTK3 + test battery was measured to determine the MC levels of the participants. In the second session of the measurements, PF elements were measured. Among these, static and dynamic balance performance, agility test (pro-agility) and vertical jump test (countermovement jump test) were performed. All children were assessed individually and the measurements were carried out by the same researchers. Before the determination of PF measurements, a typical warm-up including five minutes of dynamic stretching and running was performed. The structured coordinative exercise intervention for the exercise group lasted eight weeks without any interruption. The control group continued the standard education curriculum in kindergarten and did not receive any intervention. At the end of the eight weeks, all measurement methods were administered to the participants as a posttest in the same order and by the same researchers as in the pretest. The order of administration of the measurements is shown in Fig. 1. Data on MC and PF were gathered in the kindergarten’s sports hall. In a calm classroom setting, the IC parameter was measured.

Structured coordinative exercise protocol

During the eight-week period, all children in the structured coordinative exercise intervention group participated in 30-minute sessions twice a week on the same day and time. The purpose-oriented basic movement patterns created in the coordinative exercise content were organised in a play-oriented manner suitable for the preschool age range. The basic movement patterns included open-ended tasks that followed a path from simple to complex, with motor planning as a prerequisite75. These open-ended tasks covered all locomotor and balance skills at the point of basic movement patterns (Table 1). As the exercise intervention was game-based, the intervention sessions were conducted in a fun, active and social learning environment for the children.

KTK3 + Test battery

The KTK3 + test battery supplemented with a eye-hand coordination task was used to assess children’s motor abilities76–79. The KTK3 provides a measure of general gross motor coordination80. A throwing and catching task was added to the KTK3 test form and the KTK3 + test battery covering three basic motor skill areas (movement, balance and object control) was introduced77,79. The KTK3 + test items are backward balancing (BB), movie sideways (MS), jumping sideways (JS) and eye-hand coordination task (EHC). All test items showed good test-retest reliability; BB 0.80, MS 0.84, JS 0.95, and EHC 0.8779,81,82.

Go/No-Go task

The Go/No-Go task is a protocol that is frequently used in young children to measure the IC parameter59,83,84. Children’s IC parameter was determined with the Go/No-Go task in Early Years Toolbox, an iPad-based application developed for the preschool period85. The convergent validity of the inhibitory control measure showed a good relationship with the NIH Toolbox86,87, which is frequently used in this age group and has the same structure (r (80) = 0.40, p < 0.001). Moreover, internal consistency analyses of the Go/No-Go task revealed good reliability for the “Go” stimulus (Cronbach α = 0.95) and the “No-Go” stimulus (Cronbach α = 0.84)85. In the Go/No-Go task, children were instructed to respond (press the screen) to the “go” stimulus (catch fish) and not to respond (do not press the screen) to the “no-go” stimulus (avoid sharks). Since the “go” stimulus constitutes the majority of the trials (80% fish), it creates a strong tendency to respond, and they should prevent the “go” stimulus trials (20% sharks). Before starting the measurement, an appropriate verbal explanation was given to the children and then the practice trials were started. The 20-trial practice trials consisted of a block of 5 “catch fish” trials, 5 “avoid sharks” trials, followed by a mixed block of 10 trials. After a short rest, the task is completed with 75 stimulus trials divided into three test blocks (25 trials of 1 min each). The order of the stimuli is presented randomly. A block does not start with a “don’t go” stimulus, and no more than two consecutive trials consist of a “don’t go” stimulus. Each trial is set such that the fish and shark stimuli appear on the screen for 1500 ms with an interval of 1000 ms each. The ‘go’ stimulus (catching the fish) and the reaction time to the ‘go’ stimulus were used in statistical analyses.

Counter movement jump test

Jump performance was measured using an accelerometer system (IVMES Athlete, Ankara, Turkey). The device was attached to a belt and vertically attached to the centre of the waist of the participants. All participants were instructed to avoid any involuntary movement in the vertical plane during jumps that could affect jump height. During the test, participants were warned to jump as high as possible. The test was administered twice and a 30-second rest was given between administrations. The highest jump measurement in the two test trials was used in statistical analysis. The CMJ test showed the highest relationship with the explosive power factor (r = 0.87), that is, the greatest factorial validity88.

Balance performances

Balance was assessed using a moveable platform (Sensbalance MiniBoard; Sensamove®, Utrecht, The Netherlands), which provides an interactive training tool. While on the balance board, the participant was required to bring or hold the ball in the position required by the measured balance variable on the screen, for 30 s. It was tested twice while doing two activities involving balance in two different scenarios. During the first challenge, the MiniBoard could tilt about 10◦ in all directions while the participant had to hold the ball steady at a certain location displayed on the screen (static balancing). The second assignment required the participant to use dynamic balance (staying balanced on the MiniBoard) to move the ball to a new location. The MiniBoard is a robust wooden device with the ability to tilt in both directions and increase the complexity of balancing tasks. First, the device uses innovative, non-invasive technology for real-time data recording. It offers storage in the form of notepad data files, excel, and graphical files. The apparatus allowed for tests on static and dynamic balance and ankle joint mobility89. The balance device automatically calculated the scores of the static and dynamic balance performances of the participants in terms of %.

Agility test (Pro-Agility test)

The test track is determined by placing markers (markers were motivating due to the age of the participants) 5 yards (4,57 m) to the left and right of the starting line. A photocell gate is placed at the start line. Repeated transition times were taken in this way. The participant took his/her place at the starting line before the application started. When he/she was ready, he/she first touched the pointer on the right, then the pointer on the left, and finally crossed the baseline to end the test. The measurement was made in two trials. A 3-minute rest interval was given between the measurements to avoid performance decreases, and the measurement with the lowest second of the two trials was used in statistical analysis90. Time recorded in seconds91. Stewart et al.92 study reported higher reliability values for the pro-agility test (r = 0.90) in comparison with Mayhew et al.93 (r = 0.80; 2010).

Statistical analysis

The data was analysed using SPSS v.18.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA) statistical software, and a significance level of p < 0.05 was determined. The homogeneity and normality of the data were evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively, before any analysis was conducted. The mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage are the presentation formats for descriptive statistics. Intragroup comparison of the parameters of physical fitness, motor competence and inhibitory control variables was performed using Paired Samples T-test. An analysis of variance (ANOVA) using repeated measures (group x measurement) was carried out for parameters of physical fitness, motor competence and inhibitory control variables. Prior to executing the ANOVA, the prerequisites for the analysis were verified. To determine whether there were any statistically significant variations in the covariances between the measurement groups for pairwise combinations of the groups, Box’s Test of Equality of Covariance Matrices was employed. Furthermore, the partial eta squared effect size was employed to assess the magnitudes of the variances among the values. According to Fröhlich et al.94, the partial eta squared coefficient (ηp2), was introduced to show how much of the variance of the dependent variable can be explained by the independent variable.

As a result of the two-factor analysis of variance for mixed measures to determine whether being in the exercising group had a significant effect on the test scores, group by time interaction effect showed that the increase in the scores of the exercise group was significantly higher than that of the control group in the parameters of static balance, KTK jumping sideways and KTK eye-hand coordination [respectively: F(1−39) = 6.993, p = 0.012; 6.443, p = 0.015; 8.180, p = 0.007). However, no statistically significant difference was found in all other parameters as a result of group by time interaction effect comparison (p > 0.05) (Table 4).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate the concurrent effects of a structured coordinative exercise programme with motor skills on PF, MC and IC parameters in kindergarten children. In the exercise group, significant improvements were achieved in the agility, static and dynamic balance parameters, KTK jumping sideways, KTK balancing backwards and KTK eye-hand coordination parameters of the children in the eight-week coordinative exercise programme. In addition, significant changes were observed in agility, vertical jump and KTK jumping sideways parameters in the control group who continued their standard education in kindergarten and participated in the PA lessons included in the normal education curriculum. In line with these results, it can be said that kindergarten children who participate in PA and coordinative exercises can improve in various aspects of PF elements and MC. When the literature is examined, the observation of improvements in PF elements and MC at the point of structured PA and coordinative exercises on the development of motor skills supports the literature36,38,51,52,95–98. For example, Alesi et al.97, emphasised that football-based coordination exercises may be important in the formation of positive changes in agility and dynamic balance parameters along with eye-hand and arm-leg coordination, and thus MC may improve positively.

Another aspect we focused on in our research involved assessing the IC parameter, a critical element of executive functions. Contrary to previous findings suggesting the advantages of structured PA and coordinative exercise interventions on IC, our results revealed no significant alteration in the IC parameter following the coordinative exercises51,52,99–102. In a meta-analysis study conducted by Alvarez-Bueno et al.71, it was concluded that structured PA and coordinative exercises, especially cognitively enriched programmes, provided positive effects on the IC parameter. Similarly, Giordano and Alesi100, concluded that cognitively engaging structured exercises designed for preschool children showed favourable performance improvement in the IC parameter compared to the normal play group. The findings obtained in our study contradict the findings of the existing literature reporting that positive effects on IC parameters were observed with PA and exercise interventions in preschool period. In addition, Malambo et al.31, in a comprehensive systematic analysis, reported that there was weak evidence for a moderately weak relationship between MC and IC in preschool children. In line with these findings, we confirm that structured PA and coordinative exercises for preschool children positively support MC in children, but more specific research is recommended to determine the level of effect on IC and the links with MC.

As a result of the group-time interaction between the exercise and control groups, it was determined that the increase in scores was significantly higher in the coordinative exercise group compared to the control group in the parameters of static balance, KTK sideways jumping and KTK eye-hand coordination. In line with the findings, significant improvements were achieved in the motor skill parameters of static balance, KTK jumping sideways and KTK eye-hand coordination as a result of coordinative exercise interventions structured with motor skills. These findings support the findings of a recent meta-analysis study. Zhang et al.95, that intervention programmes aiming to improve motor skills in preschool children with normal development can be effective in increasing their MC. When the group-time interaction was evaluated on the IC parameter, which is another element in the focus of the study, no significant difference was found between the exercise group and the control group. This shows that the coordinative exercise intervention did not provide any significant increase in the performance level in the IC parameter. In contrast to these findings, Chang et al.51 and Alesi et al.52 observed significant improvements in the IC parameter after football-based coordinative exercise intervention in two different studies. The focal point of both studies was that football exercises were designed to stimulate coordination and cognitive parameters. It is stated that kicking a moving ball (object control) requires a high level of motor coordination and perceptual skills. Therefore, the reason why there was no significant change in the performance level of the IC parameter in our study may be due to the fact that the coordinative exercise intervention applied was more dominant on locomotor and balance skills. On the other hand, supporting this hypothesis, Han et al.103, in a cross-sectional study, observed that object control skills were a significant predictor of the IC parameter when the relationships between basic motor skills and executive functions were evaluated. The findings suggest that while structured PA and exercise practices and especially coordinative exercise interventions contribute to the benefits of preschool children in PF and MC, the lack of any significant performance difference in the IC parameter, which is an important component of executive functions, is thought to make an important contribution to expand the scope of the literature.

Strengths and limitations

Our study was carried out on a sample group covering the preschool period. Especially the limitation of the researches conducted in this period is considered important in terms of expanding the scope of the literature. However, the implementation of a structured coordinative exercise programme based on motor skills elements, which was prepared by going beyond the kindergarten curriculum and considering the developmental needs of children, stands out as the strength of the study. At the same time, the focus of our study constitutes another strength of the study by providing a more comprehensive evaluation by examining the simultaneous effects of such exercises on three critical parameters important for children’s development: PF, MC and IC. An important limitation of the study is that the coordinative exercise intervention was applied two days a week and the PA status of the children was not monitored on other days and hours. Nevertheless, conditions that may affect early childhood, such as socioeconomic level, nutrition, family lifestyle, parents’ level of education and the content of the education programme in kindergarten, were not taken into account. In addition, the fact that the number of males in the sample group in the study is higher than the number of females and the effect levels investigated are not analysed separately according to gender constitute another important limitation of the study. These situations may have a potential impact on the results. Therefore, it is important to consider these factors in future research.

Conclusions

Structured coordinative exercise interventions resulted in significant improvements in agility, static and dynamic balance parameters and MC among preschool children. These findings suggest that structured exercises targeting motor skills can significantly improve PF and MC. Specifically, significant improvements in static balance, jumping sideways, and eye-hand coordination were observed in the exercise group compared to the control group, emphasizing the effectiveness of such interventions in fostering diverse aspects of MC. These findings underscore the importance of integrating coordinative exercise programs enriched with motor skills into early childhood education, offering valuable insights for preschool educators and researchers aiming to optimize MC development.
