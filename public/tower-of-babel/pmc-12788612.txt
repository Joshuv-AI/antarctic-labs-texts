Influence of Salivary Parameters in Teenagers Undergoing Orthodontic Treatment in Caries Development
Authors: Vijender Kumar, Ashim Roy, Apna Yadav, Ubaid Kureshi, Sanket Patel, Roshni Mahajan, Abraham Thomas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aim:To assess the link between ACLs and salivary parameters (CA VI, pH, buffering capacity, α-amylase, and SFR) in adolescents (12–17 years) with fixed orthodontic appliances.Methodology:A longitudinal observational study with 100 healthy adolescents. Stimulated saliva samples were collected at baseline, 1, 3, 6, and 12 months post-application. Parameters measured included SFR, pH, buffering capacity, α-amylase, and CA VI. ACLs were diagnosed using Nyvad’s criteria.Results:SFR increased at one month in non-ACL patients (P ≈ 0.0283) and correlated with CA VI at three months. Buffering capacity dropped at three months (P < 0.0001) and partially recovered by 12 months. α-Amylase decreased at one and six months (P = 0.0003). CA VI positively correlated with SFR at three months and negatively with pH at one month. New caries appeared around three months, aligning with reduced enzymatic and buffering defenses.Conclusion:Orthodontic treatment triggers early salivary flow increases, but declines in buffering capacity, α-amylase, and CA VI between three and six months coincide with ACL onset. This high-risk phase highlights the need for early preventive care, despite parameter recovery by 12 months.

INTRODUCTION

Adolescent dental care often involves fixed orthodontic appliances (brackets, bands, wires), which significantly alter the oral environment. These appliances increase plaque retention, disturb oral homeostasis, and elevate the risk of enamel demineralization and dental caries (Cardoso et al., 2017).[1] Saliva serves as the first line of defense by buffering acids, aiding remineralization, clearing food, and maintaining microbial balance. Key parameters, like pH, buffering capacity, and stimulated flow rate (SFR), are linked to caries risk, particularly in teenagers (Bonetti et al., 2013).[2] Reduced flow, low pH, and weak buffering are strongly associated with higher caries rates (Jakavičė et al., 2023).[3] Although appliance placement may temporarily boost flow and pH, long-term protective effects are uncertain, with some studies suggesting declines (Bonetti et al., 2013; Jakavičė et al., 2023).[23] Enzymes, like α-amylase and CA VI, are emerging biomarkers of caries susceptibility, with elevated CA VI linked to increased risk in children (Picco et al., 2017; Frasseto et al., 2012).[45] Notably, 30–70% of patients may develop active caries lesions (ACL) within three months of treatment (Cardoso et al., 2017),[1] often alongside reduced salivary defenses. This approach seeks to clarify how salivary physiology evolves during orthodontic treatment and its role in caries development, guiding prevention for this at-risk group.

METHODOLOGY

Samples were collected between 9:00 a.m. and 12 p.m., two hours post-eating/brushing to minimize diurnal variation. UFR and SFR (after paraffin chewing) were measured in mL/min. Salivary pH was assessed using strips or a pH meter; buffering capacity via the Ericsson method or kits. α-Amylase and CA VI activity were analyzed by spectrophotometry/enzyme assays and ELISA/zymography. Using Nyvad’s criteria, new ACL were assessed in 100 healthy adolescents (12–17) receiving fixed orthodontic treatment. Ethical approval and informed consent were obtained. Inclusion: good health, no active caries, or past orthodontic treatment. Exclusion: pregnancy, smoking, illness, poor periodontal health, or salivation-affecting drugs. Evaluations occurred at baseline, 1, 3, 6, and 12 months. A single calibrated examiner ensured intra-observer reliability (Cronbach’s alpha, ICC). The Shapiro–Wilk test assessed normality. Parametric/nonparametric tests and correlation analyses were applied. Significance was set at P < 0.05 (SPSS v16).

RESULT

Stimulated salivary flow rate (SFR) significantly increased at one month post-appliance placement (P = 0.0283), likely due to reflex mechanical stimulation from brackets and wires, and remained elevated in non-ACL patients for at least three months, positively correlating with carbonic anhydrase VI (CA VI) activity, indicating a possible protective role. By three months, buffering capacity showed a significant decline (P < 0.0001), reducing saliva’s acid-neutralizing ability and increasing caries risk. While limited 12-month data exist, some studies suggest buffering capacity trends back toward baseline without maintaining statistical significance. α-Amylase activity declined significantly at both one month (P = 0.0003) and six months, potentially weakening digestion and biofilm modulation. CA VI activity showed an inverse relationship between salivary pH and a positive correlation with SFR in patients without active caries, suggesting enzymatic regulation of pH in the early phase. Importantly, the emergence of new active caries lesions (ACL) was first noted at three months, coinciding with diminished enzymatic defenses and buffering capacity, highlighting this period as a critical window of increased caries susceptibility. DISCUSSION

This longitudinal study highlights a clear early vulnerability window for caries development, particularly between three and six months post-appliance placement, while offering insights into salivary physiology during orthodontic treatment. In line with earlier studies, the significant rise in SFR at one month likely reflects a physiological response to mechanical stimulation.[25] Bonetti et al. also reported temporary increases in salivary flow and pH in early treatment.[2] However, these protective factors tend to return to baseline by one year—an “adaptation effect” that limits long-term caries protection. Buffering capacity, vital for acid neutralization and remineralization, significantly declined at three months (P < 0.0001), coinciding with ACL appearance. This aligns with findings that prolonged plaque exposure may reduce buffering capacity,[6] despite earlier reports of short-term increases.[7] CA VI, a zinc-containing enzyme tied to oral homeostasis, showed a compensatory response: its activity inversely correlated with pH and positively with SFR at one month in non-ACL patients. This supports its role as a caries-risk biomarker, as noted by Picco et al.,[4] Al Mahdi et al.,[8] and Vasudevan et al.[9] Other studies also linked CA VI activity to dietary and microbial factors; Souza et al.[10] showed sucrose rinse affected buffering and CA VI levels, while Esberg et al.[6] linked CA VI gene variation to oral microbiota composition. Amylase levels significantly dropped at one and six months (P = 0.0003), potentially reducing saliva’s starch breakdown and antimicrobial functions. This supports Borghi et al.’s[11] findings on the role of enzymatic shifts in early childhood caries and plaque virulence. As in previous studies where 30–70% of patients developed early lesions,[57] ACLs in our study emerged around three months, accompanied by enzymatic and buffering declines, marking this as a high-risk phase. Although initial increases in flow and pH may offer temporary protection, they diminish over time. Orthodontists should prioritize preventive care—fluoride, sealants, and education—especially during the first three to six months. Monitoring markers, like amylase and CA VI, may improve caries risk assessment and guide personalized prevention.[310] Strengths of this study include its longitudinal design, Nyvad criteria, and combined salivary parameter analysis. Limitations include a small sample size (n = 22), lack of a non-orthodontic control, and no CA VI/amylase data at 12 months. Future studies should include larger, multicenter adolescent samples with microbiome and long-term enzyme tracking.

CONCLUSION

In adolescents aged 12–17, fixed orthodontic treatment initially increases salivary pH and flow, enhancing early oral defense. However, buffering capacity, amylase, and carbonic anhydrase VI activity drop significantly within three to six months, coinciding with the onset of active caries lesions. Despite eventual adaptation over 12 months, the early phase remains the most vulnerable. Enhanced preventive care, including strict hygiene and topical supplements, should be prioritized during this period.
