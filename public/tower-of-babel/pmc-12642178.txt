Neutrophil lymphocyte ratio as a marker of skin disease activity in older children with juvenile dermatomyositis
Authors: Shreya Swaminathan, Nidhi Naik, Susan Shenoi, Sarah Vandenbergen, Jessica L. Turnier, Zilan Zheng, Jessica Neely, James S. Andrews, Christian Lood
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeutrophils contribute to the pathogenesis of many autoimmune diseases. While the neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR) is associated with disease activity in adult dermatomyositis, its clinical utility in juvenile dermatomyositis (JDM) is unknown.MethodsDemographic, medication use, autoantibody profile, disease activity markers, and laboratory data from 97 JDM patients was collected using retrospective chart review. Patients were age-stratified (0–9 vs. 9–21 years of age), as white blood cell count stabilizes at 9 years. The relationships between NLR with JDM disease activity measures were analyzed using spearman’s correlation.ResultsPatients were primarily white (56.7%) and female (71.1%). Older children had lower physician global assessment (PGA) (2.0 vs. 4.0, p = 0.015) and lymphocyte count (1700 vs. 2320, p = 0.01), and higher manual muscle testing (MMT-8) (80 vs. 73, p < 0.001) and childhood myositis assessment score (CMAS) (49 vs. 45, p = 0.012). Cutaneous dermatomyositis disease area and severity index (CDASI) (r = 0.39, p = 0.007) and PGA skin (r = 0.52, p = 0.004) were associated with NLR in older children, independent on prednisone use. In younger children, skin involvement was negatively associated with NLR (r=-0.41, p = 0.024). No associations with muscle activity, including CMAS (r = 0.021, p = 0.91 vs. r = 0.25, p = 0.22), CK levels (r = 0.23, p = 0.11 vs. r=-0.041, p = 0.79), and MMT-8 (r = 0.152, p = 0.31 vs. r=-0.132, p = 0.377) were seen in older or younger children respectively.ConclusionsAge-related variability in lymphocytes may complicate the interpretation and assessment of NLR in young children. NLR may serve as a cutaneous disease activity marker in older JDM patients. Larger multi-center cohorts are necessary to validate these findings and assess the role of treatment on NLR.

Background

Juvenile dermatomyositis (JDM) is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy characterized by proximal muscle weakness and pathognomonic skin rashes [1]. The etiology of JDM remains unclear. One hypothesis is that JDM may be caused by an immune-mediated vasculopathy affecting muscle tissue and organ systems of genetically susceptible individuals [1]. Current biomarkers for JDM disease activity, such as serum creatine kinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), aspartate transaminase (AST), and alanine transaminase (ALT), are often unreliable as muscle enzymes may not be elevated in patients with chronic disease or lower disease activity [2]. Identifying a more reliable disease activity biomarker would facilitate clinical care and research.

Neutrophils, the main immune cell in circulation, are essential to host defense. However, neutrophils are also involved in the pathogenesis of systemic autoimmune conditions, including JDM [3, 4]. Neutrophils infiltrate skeletal muscle and skin in JDM and adult DM [3]. In peripheral blood, JDM neutrophils demonstrate aberrant gene expression profiles as compared to healthy controls. Functional modeling of neutrophilic gene expression suggest pathologic hubs centered around TNFα and IFNγ [5]. A study by Baechler et al. reports an interferon signature noted in peripheral blood of JDM patients [6].

Further, serum levels of calprotectin, a marker of neutrophil activation, are elevated in JDM blood and associate with disease activity [3]. However, calprotectin levels are not routinely measured in most clinical settings.

The neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR) has been identified as a clinically accessible marker of inflammation and disease activity in various autoimmune conditions [7, 8]. Recent studies of adult DM have proposed the NLR as a cost-effective marker of inflammation [9, 10]. While the NLR has been shown to positively correlate with the myositis disease activity assessment visual analogue scale (MYOACT) in adult DM [11], its clinical utility in JDM has not yet been assessed. The relationship between NLR and disease activity may differ in JDM due to age related changes in total and differential white blood cell counts [12]. In general, neutrophil count increases, while lymphocyte count decreases with age in children and stabilizes around 9 years of age. In this study, we evaluate the relationship between the NLR and disease activity in a cohort of JDM patients.

Methods

Patient cohort

This multicenter retrospective cohort study was approved by the review boards at the University of Washington (UW), University of California San Francisco (UCSF), and University of Michigan (UM). Ninety-seven patients under the age of 21 diagnosed with JDM at UW (n = 40), UM (n = 31), and UCSF (n = 26) were included. Exclusion criteria were age > 21 years and lack of complete blood count (CBC) data.

Demographic, medication use, auto-antibody profile, disease activity markers, and laboratory data (CBC, comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP), and muscle enzymes including creatine kinase (CK)) paired with study visit data were collected from medical records. CBC data were recorded the day of clinical visit. Myositis specific antibodies were analyzed using the comprehensive myositis autoantibody panel at Oklahoma Medical Research Foundation (OMRF) for the majority of the samples from UW and USCF, whereas UM analyzed the samples using Myomarker Panel 3 (Mayo Clinic Laboratories), as well as OMRF when equivocal or negative testing using the Myomarker Panel 3. Disease activity markers assessed included physician global assessment (PGA), manual muscle strength testing of 8 muscles (MMT8), childhood myositis assessment scale (CMAS), and cutaneous dermatomyositis disease area and severity index (CDASI). CBC values were used to calculate the NLR by dividing absolute neutrophil count (cells/µL) by absolute lymphocyte count (cells/µL).

Statistical analyses

Because white blood cell count levels stabilize around 9 years of age [12], analyses were stratified by age (0–9 vs. 9–21 years). For the primary analysis, Spearman’s rho measured the correlations between the NLR and disease activity markers. For secondary analyses, Whitney U test and Fisher’s exact test compared demographic data, disease activity markers, cell counts, and cell count ratios between the two groups. Spearman’s correlation determined the correlations between cell counts, cell count ratios, and disease activity markers. IBM SPSS statistics software version 26 (SPSS Inc. Chicago, IL) was used for statistical analysis.

Results

Cohort description

The majority of children in this cohort were white (56.7%) and female (71.1%) (Table 1). Median age at the time of data collection was 10 years (range: 1–21 years) and median age of diagnosis was 6.0 years (range: 0.9–17.0 years). The median disease duration was 2.0 years (range: 0.0-16.1 years). Median lymphocyte and neutrophil counts were 1950 cells/µL (range: 572–8710) and 3497 (870-15800), respectively. Median PGA total was 3 (range: 0–9) and median PGA skin and PGA muscle were 0.5 (range: 0–8) and 1 (0-8.5) respectively. The median CDASI was 4 (range: 0–40) and median CMAS was 48 (13–52). The most commonly used medication was IVIG (37.1%), followed by systemic glucocorticoids (36%) and methotrexate (27.8%).

Forty-seven children were 0–9 years old, and 50 children were 9–21 years old. There was no significant difference in race or sex between groups. Older children had a higher age of diagnosis (10 (range 1.6–17) vs. 4.1 (0.9–8.5), p < 0.001), longer disease duration (4.8 vs. 1, p < 0.001), lower overall PGA (2 vs. 4, p = 0.015), less muscle disease activity (lower PGA muscle (0 vs. 2, p = 0.002), higher MMT-8 (80 vs. 73, p < 0.0001), higher CMAS (49 vs. 45, p = 0.012)), lower muscle enzymes (AST: 31 vs. 44.5, p = 0.003; LDH: 301.5 vs. 421, p = 0.002), and lower lymphocyte count (1700 vs. 2320, p = 0.010). Younger children had higher global and muscle disease activity (higher PGA, lower MMT8 and CMAS scores). There was no significant difference in PGA skin (0.1 vs. 1.3, p = 0.18), CDASI (3.0 vs. 5.0, p = 0.086), CK (p = 0.37), neutrophil count (3100 vs. 4178, p = 0.18), and NLR (1.72 vs. 1.50, p = 0.46) between older and younger children. There was a similar rate of glucocorticoid (26% vs. 38%, p = 0.20), IVIG (40.0% vs. 34.0%, p = 0.67), mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) (21.6% vs. 14.7%, p = 0.55), and rituximab (8.0% vs. 8.5%, p = 1.0) usage amongst older and younger children. Older children were less likely to have been on methotrexate (18% vs. 38%, p = 0.04).

Fifty-six out of 87 children with testing were ANA positive (p = 0.372). Only 2/20 children with antibody testing were anti-dsDNA positive, one from the younger cohort and one from the older group (p = 1.0). Twenty-eight out of 80 children with antibody testing were anti-p155 positive, including 18/41 children in the younger cohort and 10/39 children in the older cohort (p = 0.105). Twenty-two out of 81 children with antibody testing were anti-MJ/NXP2 positive, including 9/41 in the younger cohort and 13/40 children in the older cohort (p = 0.326). Four out of 75 children with testing had positive anti-RNP, and 3/38 of these children were from the younger cohort and 1/37 from the older cohort (p = 0.615). Three out of 73 children with testing had a positive anti-MDA5, all from the younger cohort (3/26, p = 0.241). Positive Anti-Mi2 was noted in 11/84 patients with testing, with 7/45 patients from the younger cohort and 4/39 patients from the older cohort testing positive (p = 0.533).

Association between NLR and disease activity

In the whole cohort, there was no association between NLR and skin disease activity markers, PGA skin (r= -0.011, p = 0.936) and CDASI (r = 0.112, p = 0.289). Additionally, there was no association between NLR and muscle disease activity markers (PGA muscle (r= -0.002, p = 0.988), CMAS (r = 0.082, p = 0.545), CK (r = 0.086, p = 0.412), LDH (r = 0.014, p = 0.893), AST (r=-0.062, p = 0.551), ALT (r = 0.153, p = 0.140), aldolase (r = 0.042, p = 0.704), and MMT-8 (r= -0.050, p = 0.804)). Total PGA was also not associated with NLR (r= -0.031, p = 0.764).

In older children, two markers of skin involvement, CDASI and PGA skin, were positively associated with NLR (r = 0.39, p = 0.007; and r = 0.52, p = 0.004, respectively). Of note, prednisone dose did not correlate with NLR (r = 0.17, p = 0.25), nor did patients on prednisone have higher levels of NLR (p = 0.26) as compared to those without prednisone. As a result, the correlation between CDASI and NLR remained even when stratifying patients based on prednisone use (prednisone: r = 0.62, p = 0.03; no prednisone: r = 0.35, p < 0.05). The correlation with PGA skin remained in the no prednisone use group (r = 0.53, p = 0.006). However, we were unable to evaluate this correlation in the prednisone group given the low number of older patients for which we had data on PGA skin and that were also on prednisone (n = 4).

In younger children, PGA skin negatively associated with NLR (r=-0.41, p = 0.024) (Table 2). Neither neutrophil (r = 0.25, p = 0.19) nor lymphocyte (r=-0.31, p = 0.11) count correlated with PGA. No associations for NLR with muscle activity, including CMAS (r = 0.021, p = 0.91 vs. r = 0.25, p = 0.22), CK (r = 0.23, p = 0.11 vs. r=-0.041, p = 0.79), or MMT-8 (r = 0.152, p = 0.31 vs. r=-0.132, p = 0.377) were seen in either older children or younger children respectively.

There were no associations between NLR and distinct MSA (myositis specific antibodies) (p > 0.05) or nail capillary changes (p = 0.404). The limited numbers of calcinosis and pulmonary involvement disallowed for further analyses for those manifestations. Overall, no distinct clinical phenotype was seen with NLR, other than skin involvement within older children.

Discussion

This is one of the first, and largest, cohorts to explore NLR as a marker of disease activity in pediatrics, including 97 JDM patients from multiple medical centers throughout the US. In our study, the NLR positively associated with cutaneous disease activity in older children with JDM, highlighting that the NLR may be an organ-specific disease activity marker in a JDM subgroup. Of note, this association was independent on steroid use. Interestingly, the NLR had a negative association with cutaneous disease activity in younger children with JDM. Though the current study was not designed to explore the reason for the diverse findings in young versus old children, potential hypotheses include a difference in disease pathophysiology in JDM subgroups related to either age of diagnosis, disease duration, or disease severity. No associations were seen between NLR and any MSA subtypes or nail capillary changes.

It is often difficult to differentiate active versus inactive skin disease in JDM clinically, particularly with chronic disease. Additionally, skin findings in JDM patients can be non-specific and related to other processes that may be indistinguishable from JDM skin disease activity, such as malar erythema noted after exercise, rigorous activity or steroid exposure. Hence an objective marker to differentiate cutaneous JDM activity would be helpful. Serum interferon I-score has been proposed as a biomarker for cutaneous disease in JDM. However, this is not routinely collected and may be difficult to obtain [13]. In a study by Khojah et al., JDM patients with greater skin disease activity had an elevated neopterin. However, elevated neopterin was also associated with greater total disease and muscle activity, making it non-specific [14]. Our study shows that in older children with JDM, NLR may be a cutaneous marker of disease but not muscle involvement. NLR correlated with PGA skin and CDASI in older children, but not with measures of muscle activity, such as PGA muscle, CMAS, and CK. Although PGA skin correlated positively with NLR in older children, the correlation was negative among younger children. It is unclear why, although this may be due to persistent skin disease activity accompanying chronic disease, which may be harder to treat. In our cohort, older children had a longer disease duration and increased methotrexate usage which may have caused differences in the cell counts that contribute to these trends. Further, in younger children, the association was only between NLR and PGA skin, but not with CDASI, whereas in older children NLR correlated with PGA skin and CDASI. The inverse association between NLR and PGA skin (and not CDASI) in younger children with JDM may be a random finding. Further studies are warranted to validate these data. It is unclear why NLR may be a better marker of skin over muscle disease in our cohort. Prior research has highlighted a predominant innate immune signature population in the skin of patients with JDM [15]. The stimulation of the innate immune system response in the skin may lead to stimulation of the neutrophilic response in the skin over muscle. JDM is a heterogeneous disease, with certain phenotypes presenting with predominantly skin manifestations [16].

Prior studies have noted lymphocyte counts to be lowest during infancy with increase and peak values at 6 months before continuously reducing until 9 years [12]. The hypothesis for these differences is the increased exposure to foreign antigens with age. Consistently, we noted significantly higher lymphocyte counts in the younger cohort than the older cohort. This highlights the challenges with using NLR in children, and the need of establishing a cut-off, such as 9 years as used in this and prior work, when age-dependent cell count variability is sufficiently stable to allow meaningful interpretation of NLR. Future studies are warranted to explore these hypotheses.

Weaknesses of this study include its retrospective nature and relatively small sample size, which precluded adjusting for potential confounders (e.g. disease duration and medication use, such as glucocorticoids). However, these data serve as an important baseline to inform future JDM cohort studies.

In conclusion, NLR may serve as a marker of cutaneous disease activity in older children with JDM. Despite age related variability in neutrophil and lymphocyte counts, the NLR is a readily available marker that may allow for a more efficient and non-invasive measure of skin disease activity than other currently available clinical measures. Future larger studies should validate our observed trends and examine how other clinical factors, such as disease duration and treatment, may affect the relationship of NLR with JDM cutaneous disease activity.

Conclusions

NLR may serve as a clinically accessible cutaneous disease activity marker in older JDM patients. Age-related variability in lymphocyte levels in children may complicate the interpretation and, assessment of NLR making it a less reliable marker in young children. Larger multi-center cohorts are necessary to validate these findings and assess the role of treatment on NLR.
