Calabi-Yau techniques for Namikawa-Weyl groups
Jasper van de Kreeke

Abstract
. The field of symplectic singularities aims to build a 21st century Lie theory. A key development is the Namikawa-Weyl group, which generalizes the classical Weyl group of Lie algebras. Another cornerstone is the integration of categorical Calabi-Yau techniques, capturing the rich algebraic structure of these singularities. In this paper, we develop a systematic strategy to determine Namikawa-Weyl groups for representation spaces of Calabi-Yau-2 algebras, leveraging local-to-global functors, symmetry analysis, and A ∞ A_{\infty} -methods. Applying this approach to quiver varieties, we carry out the detailed calculations and recover Yaochen Wu’s result.

Strategy

Our strategy for determining the Namikawa-Weyl groups is to explicitly identify the entire fiber bundle
\pi:\pi^{-1}(L)\to L
for every codimension-2 leaf

of
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
. The implementation is best sketched as follows. For every point
x=S_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus S_{k}
in
L
, there is an astonishing similarity between the vertex representations
\tilde{S}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{S}_{k}
of the local quiver
(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
and the simples
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
of
(Q,\alpha)
. We bundle this similarity into an
A_{\infty}
-functor
F
which allows us to transform representations in the exceptional locus of Kleinian singularities into representations lying in
\pi^{-1}(L)
. This strategy provides an explicit identification of the bundle
\pi:\pi^{-1}(L)\to L
and therefore an explicit description of the Namikawa-Weyl group of
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
.

In
section 2
, we recapitulate preliminaries on symplectic singularities, quivers and

-structures. In
section 3
, we construct the desired functor

. In
section 4
, we investigate the symmetry group of the local quiver. In
section 5
, we identify the action of the symmetry group on the exceptional fibers of partial resolutions of Kleinian singularities. In
section 6
, we identify the fiber bundle explicitly and read off its monodromy. In
section 7
, we collect the main results and provide an outlook on the the analogous situation with other Calabi-Yau-2 algebras. The appendices contain examples and several deferred proofs.

2.1.
Symplectic singularities

We start by recalling the notion of symplectic singularities and their Namikawa-Weyl groups, following

\pi:Y\to X

\pi:\pi^{-1}(X^{\mathsf{reg}})\to\,X^{\mathsf{reg}}

Let

It is useful to note that the Poisson structure on

2.2.
Quiver varieties

Next, let us recapitulate basic properies of quivers, quiver varieties and preprojective algebras. During the entire paper, we work with algebras and representations over the field of complex numbers. Let us start by recalling quivers and their representations.

A
quiver

Q
is a finite directed graph. We denote by
Q_{0}
its set of vertices and by
Q_{1}
its set of arrows. We denote by
h:Q_{1}\to Q_{0}
and
t:Q_{1}\to Q_{0}
the head and tail functions. A
dimension vector
on
Q
is an element
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{Q_{0}}
. A
quiver setting

(Q,\alpha)
is a quiver
Q
together with a dimension vector
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{Q_{0}}
.

A
representation
or
module
of
Q
of dimension
\alpha
consists of assigning to every vertex
i\in Q_{0}
a vector space
V_{i}
of dimension
\alpha_{i}
and to every arrow
a:i\to j
a linear mapping
\rho(a):V_{i}\to V_{j}
. The
path algebra

\mathbb{C}Q
is the algebra with basis given by the paths in
Q
, and the product of paths
p\cdot q
being the concatenation of
p
and
q
if
h(q)=t(p)
and zero else.

The
double quiver

\overline{Q}
of
Q
is obtained from
Q
by adding for every arrow
a:i\to j
an arrow
a^{*}:j\to i
in opposite direction. The
preprojective algebra

\Pi_{Q}
is defined as

\Pi_{Q}=\frac{\mathbb{C}\overline{Q}}{\big(\sum_{a\in Q_{1}}aa^{*}-a^{*}a\big)}.

The representations of a quiver can be bundled into a vector space. When enforcing the preprojective condition, we obtain an affine variety. We fix notation as follows:

\operatorname{Rep}(\overline{Q},\alpha)=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\overline{Q}_{1}\\
a:i\to j\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{C}^{\alpha_{i}},\mathbb{C}^{\alpha_{j}}).

The group
\operatorname{GL}_{\alpha}=\prod_{i\in Q_{0}}\operatorname{GL}_{i}(\mathbb{C})
acts on
\operatorname{Rep}(\overline{Q},\alpha)
by conjugation:

(g.\rho)_{a}=g_{h(a)}\rho(a)g_{t(a)}^{-1}.

The orbits of this action are the isomorphism classes of quiver representations of
(\overline{Q},\alpha)
. Recall that an affine variety
X
with an algebraic action of a reductive group
G
comes with an associated affine quotient
X\sslash G
, whose coordinate ring is the invariant ring
\mathbb{C}[X]^{G}
.

This construction gives rise to quiver varieties: Let
(Q,\alpha)
be a quiver setting. The
moment map

\mu:\operatorname{Rep}(\overline{Q},\alpha)\to(\operatorname{Lie}\operatorname{GL}_{\alpha})^{*}\cong\operatorname{Lie}\operatorname{GL}_{\alpha}
of the double quiver is defined by

\mu(\rho)=\bigg(\sum_{h(a)=v}\rho(a)\rho(a^{*})-\sum_{t(a)=v}\rho(a^{*})\rho(a)\bigg)_{v\in Q_{0}}.

The
quiver variety

\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
is the affine quotient variety
\mu^{-1}(0)\sslash\operatorname{GL}_{\alpha}
. The points in
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
are in one-to-one correspondence with the closed
\operatorname{GL}_{\alpha}
-orbits in
\mu^{-1}(0)
. In other words, these are the
\operatorname{GL}_{\alpha}
-orbits of semisimple representations in
\mu^{-1}(0)
.

The structure of the varieties
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
has been investigated for more than 30 years, going back to work of Nakajima, Crawley-Boevey, King, and others. We shall base our inspection on the modern treatment presented by Bellamy and Schedler
[
3
]

Theorem 2.3

(
[
3
, Theorem 1.2]
)
.

Let
(Q,\alpha)
be a quiver setting. Then
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
is an irreducible symplectic singularity.

2.3.
Local structure of quiver varieties

Quiver varieties have an interesting local structure. The systematic treatment requires a few combinatorial notions: A (positive)
root
of
Q
is a dimension vector
\alpha
of an indecomposable representation of
Q
. A root
\alpha
is
real
if there is (up to isomorphism) a unique indecomposable representation with dimension vector
\alpha
. A root
\alpha
is
imaginary
if there are (up to isomorphism) multiple indecomposable representations with dimension vector
\alpha
. The set of roots is denoted
R^{+}
.

The
Ringel form

\langle-,-\rangle
on
\mathbb{C}^{Q_{0}}
is given by

\langle e_{i},e_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}-\#(a:i\to j).

Its symmetrization is the
Cartan form

(x,y)=\langle x,y\rangle+\langle y,x\rangle
which can also be seen as
(-,-)=2I_{Q_{0}}-A
, where
I_{Q_{0}}
denotes the identity and
A
denotes the adjacency matrix of the oriented graph
\overline{Q}
. We write
p(\alpha)=1-\kern 1.00006pt\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{\scriptsize 1}\kern-1.00006pt/\kern-1.49994pt\lower 1.07639pt\hbox{\scriptsize 2}(\alpha,\alpha)
.

Not all positive roots are created equal. Rather, there is a set
\Sigma_{0,0}
of “good roots” defined by

\Sigma_{0,0}=\big\{\alpha\in R^{+}~|~p(\alpha)>\sum_{i=1}^{r}p(\beta_{i})\\
\text{for any nontrivial decomposition }\alpha=\beta_{1}+\ldots+\beta_{r},~(\beta_{i})\subseteq R^{+}\big\}.

An element
\alpha\in\Sigma_{0,0}
is
minimal
if it cannot be written as a nontrivial sum of other elements of
\Sigma_{0,0}
.

There are quiver settings which yield empty quiver varieties. In fact, an explicit criterion states that the quiver variety is non-empty if and only if the dimension vector lies in the
\mathbb{N}
-span of the positive roots:

Theorem 2.4

(
[
5
, Theorem 4.4]
)
.

Let
(Q,\alpha)
be a quiver setting. Then
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)\neq\emptyset
implies
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}R^{+}
.

Thus, for nontrivial quiver varieties
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
we can always assume
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}R^{+}
.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
3
, Proposition 2.1, Theorem 1.3]
)
.

Let
Q
be a quiver and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}R^{+}
. Then
\alpha
admits a decomposition

for some

and

and

, such that any other decomposition with these properties is a refinement. This decomposition is called the

of

. In terms of the canonical decomposition, we have an isomorphism of symplectic varieties

\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)\cong S^{n_{1}}\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha_{k})\times\ldots\times S^{n_{k}}\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha_{k}).

Thus, we can focus on the case that the dimension vector
\alpha
itself lies in the set
\Sigma_{0,0}
. If
\alpha\in\Sigma_{0,0}
, then

is its own canonical decomposition. Bellamy and Schedler also show that any dimension vector

if and only if there is a simple representation of

has dimension

Let us now recall the description of the symplectic stratification of
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
due to Bellamy and Schedler. In particular, we recall the construction of local quivers associated with decompositions of

We are now ready to recall the local structure of

. It is possible to phrase the structure theory in étale local or formal scheme theory, but we shall work in the analytic setting for simplicity. We start with a point

In particular, a stratum is of codimension 2 if and only if

is two-dimensional. This is the case precisely when

is Kleinian quiver setting. We can make a few further observations. For instance, every element

is either a real or an imaginary root. It is known that if

consists of a single point. Therefore if

is non-empty, we necessarily have that all real roots among

are pairwise distinct. To ensure that we enumerate only the non-empty codimension-2 leaves, we fix the following terminology, following the account of Bellamy and Schedler.

Definition 2.7
.

Let

be a quiver setting with

. Then a decomposition

.

(2)

\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}
are pairwise distinct.

(3)

2.4.
Resolutions of quiver varieties

In this section, we recall the result of Bellamy and Schedler on symplectic resolutions of quiver varieties. We start from a quiver variety
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
where
Q
is a quiver and
\alpha
does not necessarily in
\Sigma_{0,0}
. Bellamy and Schedler provide an explicit criterion for
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
to have a symplectic resolution. We recall the criterion and provide basic insight into the resolution itself.

Theorem 2.8

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
(Q,\alpha)
be a quiver setting and
\alpha=n_{1}\alpha_{1}+\ldots+n_{k}\alpha_{k}
be its canonical decomposition. Then
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
admits a projective symplectic resolution if and only if every vector
\alpha_{i}
is indivisible or satisfies
(\gcd(\alpha_{i}),p(\gcd(\alpha_{i})^{-1}\alpha_{i}))=(2,2)
.

The theorem demands that every
\alpha_{i}
either be indivisible or satisfy a combinatorial condition. We refer to these cases as the “indivisible case” and the “(2, 2) case”. Let us now assume that
\alpha\in\Sigma_{0,0}
and describe the symplectic resolution explicitly.

A symplectic resolution can often by found by installing a stability parameter. For quiver varieties
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
, this boils down to studying the Mumford quotient
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\alpha)=\operatorname{Rep}(\Pi_{Q},\alpha)\sslash_{\theta}\operatorname{GL}_{\alpha}
. Here
\theta\in\mathbb{Z}^{Q_{0}}
is a stability parameter with
\theta\cdot\alpha=0
. The points of the quotient
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\alpha)
are in one-to-one correspondence with
\theta
-polystable representations of
\Pi_{Q}
of dimension
\alpha
.

The variety
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\alpha)
is sometimes smooth. The smoothness heavily depends on the choice stability parameter
\theta
. We fix the following terminology:

Definition 2.9
.

Let
(Q,\alpha)
be a quiver setting with
\alpha\in\,\Sigma_{0,0}
. Let
\theta\in\mathbb{Z}^{Q_{0}}
be a stability parameter with
\theta\cdot\alpha=0
. Then

•

\theta
is
generic
if
\theta\cdot\beta\neq 0
for all
0<\beta<\alpha
.

•

If
\alpha
is indivisible, then
\theta
is
pseudo-generic
if it is generic.

•

If
(\gcd(\alpha),p(\gcd(\alpha)^{-1}\alpha)=(2,2)
, then
\theta
is
pseudo-generic
if
\theta\cdot\beta\neq 0
for all
0<\beta<\alpha
with
\beta\neq\alpha/2
.

The existence of generic stability parameters depends heavily on the dimension vector
\alpha
. In the indivisible case, the linear inequalities
\theta\cdot\beta\neq 0
cut out finitely many hyperplanes from the space orthogonal to
\alpha
and almost every stability parameter
\theta
with
\theta\cdot\alpha=0
is generic. In the (2, 2) case, there is not a single generic stability parameter, but almost every stability parameter
\theta
with
\theta\cdot\alpha=0
is pseudo-generic.

We are now ready to recall the description of the symplectic resolution of

2.5.
Calabi-Yau structure

The module category
\operatorname{Mod}_{\Pi_{Q}}
has the structure of an
A_{\infty}
-category and its precise structure is well-known. To start with, recall from e.g.
[
4
]
that an
A_{\infty}
-category

\mathcal{C}
consists of a set of
\mathbb{Z}
-graded hom spaces together with higher products
\mu^{k}
for
k\geq 1
of degree
2-k
satisfying the
A_{\infty}
-relations

\sum_{k\geq l\geq m\geq 1}(-1)^{\|a_{m}\|+\ldots+\|a_{1}\|}\mu(a_{k},\ldots,\mu(a_{l},\ldots,a_{m+1}),\ldots,a_{1})=0.

The category
\mathcal{C}
is
cyclic
of degree
n
if it is equipped with a bilinear nondegenerate pairing
\langle-,-\rangle:\operatorname{Hom}(X,Y)\times\operatorname{Hom}(Y,X)\to\mathbb{C}
of degree
-n
such that
\langle x,y\rangle=(-1)^{|x||y|}\langle y,x\rangle
and

\langle\mu^{k}(a_{k+1},\ldots,a_{2}),a_{1}\rangle=(-1)^{\|a_{k+1}\|(\|a_{k}\|+\ldots+\|a_{1}\|)}\langle\mu^{k}(a_{k},\ldots,a_{1}),a_{k+1}\rangle.

We can now regard the specific
A_{\infty}
-category
\operatorname{Mod}_{\Pi_{Q}}
. The objects of this category are the finite-dimensional
\Pi_{Q}
-modules. The hom spaces are
\mathbb{Z}
-graded and given by
\operatorname{Hom}(M,N)=\operatorname{Ext}^{\bullet}(M,N)
. The differential
\mu^{1}
vanishes, and the product
\mu^{2}
is the signed (Yoneda) product of morphisms and extensions. The algebra
\Pi_{Q}
has a Calabi-Yau property. The exception of Dynkin quivers here refers to the non-extended (finite type) Dynkin quivers.

Theorem 2.11

(
[
7
]
)
.

If
Q
is not a Dynkin quiver, then the algebra
\Pi_{Q}
is Calabi-Yau-2.

The CY-2 property of
\Pi_{Q}
induces a Calabi-Yau structure on the category
\operatorname{Mod}_{\Pi_{Q}}
. Given that the category
\operatorname{Mod}_{\Pi_{Q}}
arises from a minimal model construction and is only defined up to isomorphism, it is tricky to make its Calabi-Yau structure explicit. However, there exists a model of
\operatorname{Mod}_{\Pi_{Q}}
whose
A_{\infty}
-structure is cyclic. We are not aware of a specific proof of the cyclicity in the literature, but it appears to be well-known among experts and we shall simply assume it:

Theorem 2.12
.

If
Q
is not a Dynkin quiver, then the
A_{\infty}
-category
\operatorname{Mod}_{\Pi_{Q}}
is cyclic.

Our next step is to describe the
A_{\infty}
-structure of
\operatorname{Mod}_{\Pi_{Q}}
. We shall at this point not aim to describe the entire structure, but focus on a collection of pairwise non-isomorphic simples
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
associated with a point in a codimension-2 leaf. Vanishing of higher products in an
A_{\infty}
-category is also known as formality. Fortunately, Davison has already investigated this question and found that any
\Sigma
-sequence in a Calabi-Yau-2 algebra is formal. While this formality theorem in principle only asserts that there exists a model with vanishing higher products, we will simply assume that this model agrees with the cyclic model:

Theorem 2.13

(
[
8
, Theorem 1.2]
)
.

Let
Q
be a non-Dynkin quiver and let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
be simple finite-dimensional representations of
\Pi_{Q}
. Then the higher products
\mu^{\geq 3}
on
\operatorname{Mod}_{\Pi_{Q}}
vanish.

The dimensions of ext spaces between non-isomorphic representations can be determined in a combinatorial way, which we record as follows:

Theorem 2.14

(
[
6
, Lemma 1]
)
.

If
M
and
N
are finite-dimensional representations of
\Pi_{Q}
, then we have

\dim\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(M,N)=\dim\operatorname{Hom}(M,N)+\dim\operatorname{Hom}(N,M)-(\dim M,\dim N).

3.
Local-to-global functor

Thanks to the description of the
A_{\infty}
-structure of
\operatorname{Mod}_{\Pi_{Q}}
, we can immediately construct a correspondence between the local and the global representations. More precisely, we can construct an

-functor

\tilde{S}_{i}
to
S_{i}

\operatorname{Hom}_{\Pi_{Q}}(S_{i},S_{j})

to actual modules. We call such twisted objects

behaves nicely with respect to honest modules, and we collect the important statements in

Let

6.
Identification of monodromy

We are now ready to describe the monodromy of the bundle
\pi:\pi^{-1}(L)\to L
. We start with a quiver setting
(Q,\alpha)
with
\alpha\in\Sigma_{0,0}
such that
\alpha
is indivisible or
(\gcd(\alpha),p(\gcd(\alpha)^{-1}\alpha))=(2,2)
. By Namikawa’s description, we are supposed to pick a symplectic resolution
\pi:Y\to\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
and read off the monodromy of the fiber bundle
\pi:\pi^{-1}(L)\to L
over every codimension-2 leaf
L
. It is our aim to analyze the monodromy of the bundle
\pi:\pi^{-1}(L)\to L
, where
\pi
is a symplectic resolution of
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
. If
\alpha
is indivisible, then a symplectic resolution is given by
\pi:\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\alpha)\to\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
where
\theta
is a generic stability parameter. If
(\gcd(\alpha),p(\gcd(\alpha)^{-1}\alpha))=(2,2)
, then a symplectic resolution is given by the blowup of
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\alpha)
along the remaining singularity, where
\theta
is a pseudo-generic stability parameter.

If
(\gcd(\alpha),p(\gcd(\alpha)^{-1}\alpha))=(2,2)
, then the partial resolution
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\alpha)
with
\theta
pseudo-generic is already smooth enough to read off the desired monodromy. Indeed, the special locus is examined in
Appendix E
and it suffices to trace the two or three remaining singular points around the leaf.

The second core ingredient in our identification of the monodromy is the functor
F
from
section 3
. We start with a codimension-2 leaf
L
, given by the isotropic decomposition
\alpha=m_{1}\beta_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}\beta_{k}
.
Let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\in\operatorname{Mod}_{\Pi_{Q}}
be a collection of simples of dimensions
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}
and let
\tilde{S}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{S}_{k}\in\operatorname{Mod}_{\Pi_{Q^{\prime}}}
be the vertex simples of the Kleinian local quiver
(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
. Then we have the functor

F_{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}}:\operatorname{Tw}\{\tilde{S}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{S}_{k}\}\to\operatorname{Tw}\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}.

We bundle these functors
F
together into a single function whose input is a collection
(S_{1},\ldots,S_{k})
. We write

M\coloneqq\big(\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{s}}(Q,\beta_{1})\times\ldots\times\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{s}}(Q,\beta_{k})\big)\setminus\Delta.

Here

denotes the subset of tuples where at least two representations are isomorphic. Note that the space

is not the same as the leaf

, but rather

where

acts naturally on

.

\Gamma

7.
Main result and further applications

Let us now generalize to the case
\alpha\notin\Sigma_{0,0}
. We use the following simple lemma for the Namikawa-Weyl groups
W
of products and symmetric products of symplectic singularities, which is proven in
Appendix F
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
X,X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
be symplectic singularities with good
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action. Then
W(X_{1}\times\ldots\times W_{k})=W(X_{1})\times\ldots\times W(X_{k})
and
W(S^{n}X)=W(X)
.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
(Q,\alpha)
be a quiver setting with canonical decomposition
\alpha=n_{1}\alpha_{1}+\ldots+n_{k}\alpha_{k}
. Assume that for every
i=1,\ldots,k
, the dimension vector
\alpha_{i}
is indivisible or satisfies
(\gcd(\alpha),p(\gcd(\alpha)^{-1}\alpha))=(2,2)
. Then the Namikawa-Weyl group of
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
is given by

W(\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha))=W(\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha_{1}))\times\ldots\times W(\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha_{k})).

Here
W(\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha_{i}))
denotes the Namikawa-Weyl group of
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha_{i})
, which can be determined according to
Theorem 6.2
.

While this paper focuses on quiver varieties, the same strategy extends naturally to moduli spaces of representations of Calabi-Yau-2 algebras. Specifically, for a Calabi-Yau-2 algebra
\Pi
and a dimension vector
\alpha
, consider a moduli space
\mathcal{M}(\Pi,\alpha)
of
\alpha
-dimensional representations. If
\mathcal{M}(\Pi,\alpha)
is a symplectic singularity, the codimension-2 strata take the form

L=(\mathcal{M}^{o}(\Pi,\beta_{1})_{c_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathcal{M}^{o}(\Pi,\beta_{k})_{c_{k}})/\Gamma.

Here
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}
are the local dimension vectors. The letters
c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}
denote additional dimensional data which serve to specify individual connected components of
\mathcal{M}^{o}(\Pi,\beta_{k})
. The letter
\Gamma
denotes the permutation group which fixes the dimensional inputs
(\beta_{i},c_{i})
. The monodromy of the stratum
L
is then equal to the action of
\Gamma
on the Kleinian exceptional fiber. This strategy determines the Namikawa-Weyl group for
\mathcal{M}(\Pi,\alpha)
.

B.1.
Honest modules

In this section, we recapitulate basic properties of honest modules.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
Q
be a quiver and let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\in\operatorname{Mod}_{\Pi_{Q}}
be a sequence of modules. Then we have the following statements:

•

Any twisted complex without shifts
T=(M_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus M_{k},\delta)\in\operatorname{Tw}\operatorname{Mod}_{\Pi_{Q}}
is an honest module.

•

If
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
are simple, then any honest module in
\operatorname{Tw}\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}
is isomorphic to a shift-free twisted complex of
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
.

•

If
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
are simple and
X\in\operatorname{Tw}\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}
is an honest module, and
Y\subseteq X
is a submodule, then
Y
can be realized as a subcomplex of
X
.

Proof.

Let us comment on all three statements after each other.

Regard the first statement. This is in fact a foundational property of
A_{\infty}
-theory. In its simplest form, we have
k=2
and

\delta=\begin{pmatrix}0&e\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Then
T=(M_{1}\oplus M_{2},\delta)
is simply quasi-isomorphic to the extension of
M_{2}
by
M_{1}
given by
\delta
. In particular,
T
is an honest module. The general case proceeds by induction.

Regard the second statement. Let
X\in\operatorname{Tw}\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}
be an honest module, quasi-isomorphic to a module
M\in\operatorname{Mod}_{\Pi_{Q}}
. Let
M_{1},\ldots,M_{l}
be the simple composition factors of
M
. Then
M
is quasi-isomorphic to a shift-free twisted complex
Y=(M_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus M_{l},\delta)
. In particular,
X
and
Y
are quasi-isomorphic and we have maps
f:X\to Y
and
g:Y\to X
of degree zero and maps
h_{X}:X\to X
and
h_{Y}:Y\to Y
of degree
-1
such that
\mu^{2}(f,g)=\mathrm{id}_{Y}+\mu^{1}(h_{Y})
and
\mu^{2}(g,f)=\mathrm{id}_{X}+\mu^{1}(h_{X})
. Since
Y
is shift-free, we necessarily have
h_{Y}=0
. Thanks to the twisted complex formalism, the maps
f
and
g
are matrices of morphisms between the components of
X
and
Y
, and
\mu^{2}(f,g)
is given by matrix multiplication. Let us split the twisted complex
X
into a shift-free part
Y_{1}
and a shifted part
Y_{2}
:

X=\big(X_{1}\oplus X_{2},\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{11}&\delta_{12}\\
\delta_{21}&\delta_{22}\end{pmatrix}\big).

Let us also split
f=(f_{1}~~f_{2})
and
g=(g_{1},g_{2})
along this decomposition. Then we the composition
\mu^{2}(f_{2},g_{2}):Y\to X_{2}\to Y
vanishes, given that a morphism
M_{i}\to S_{j}[s]
and
S_{j}[s]\to M_{l}
can be nonzero but not both since the hom spaces of
\operatorname{Mod}_{\Pi_{Q}}
are concentrated in nonnegative degrees. We conclude that

\mathrm{id}_{Y}=\mu^{2}(f,g)=\mu^{2}(f_{1},g_{1}).

Since
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
and
M_{1},\ldots,M_{l}
are simples, the matrices
f_{1}
and
g_{1}
are scalar matrices, and we conclude they have equal size and are inverse to each other. We conclude that
f_{1}:X_{1}\to Y
and
g_{1}:Y\to X_{1}
provide an isomorphism between
X_{1}
and
Y
. Moreover, since there are no degree zero morphisms between non-isomorphic simples, we conclude that every summand
M_{i}
lies in
\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}
and hence
X_{1}
is a shift-free twisted complex of
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
. Finally, since
X
was assumed to be quasi-isomorphic to
Y
, we conclude that
X
is quasi-isomorphic to
X_{1}
, a shift-free twisted complex of
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
. This proves the second statement.

The proof of the third statement is similar. This finishes the proof.

Appendix D
Comparison of symmetry groups

In this section, we describe the possible symmetries which can be present in the local quiver. In particular, we prove
Proposition 4.1
. We start with an arbitrary codimension-2 leaf and look at the local quiver together with the roots assigned to all vertices. It is possible that some of these roots are equal, which gives rise to a permutation action among the vertices, a priori not preserving the quiver structure. Simultaneously, we can consider the automorphisms of the local quiver with vertices labeled by the roots, and this gives rise to an a priori smaller group. In this section, we show that the two groups are in fact equal.

To start our investigation, regard the example of a local quiver with roots depicted in
Figure D.1
. In this example, two roots appear twice, and we shall therefore define the naive symmetry group of this labeled quiver to be the permutation group
S_{2}
. However, mirroring over the vertical axis does not preserve the other roots, and we shall therefore define the automorphism group of this labeled local quiver to be the trivial group.

.

The naive symmetry group and the automorphism group of the labeled local quiver agree:

Proof.

We split the proof into cases depending on the type of the leaf. For the

For the

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{M}_{\theta^{\prime}}(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{M}_{\theta^{\prime}}(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{M}(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{M}(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{\varphi}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{\theta^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}

Lemma D.4
.

Let
(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
be any Kleinian quiver setting and let
\sigma
be an automorphism of
(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
. Let
\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{Q^{\prime}_{0}}
be any
\sigma
-symmetric parameter. Then the map
\varphi_{\theta^{\prime}}
acts by actually flipping a representation according to
\sigma
, even on the exceptional fiber.

Proof.

Let us regard the flipping map
\operatorname{Rep}(\pi_{Q^{\prime}},\alpha^{\prime})\to\operatorname{Rep}(\pi_{Q^{\prime}},\alpha^{\prime})
. As we can see from the upper commutative square in
Remark D.3
, the map
\varphi_{\theta^{\prime}}
is the map induced from flipping the representation. At the same time, since the lower square commutes, the map
\varphi_{\theta^{\prime}}
is necessarily the desired lift
\tilde{\varphi}
. This proves the claim.
∎

Appendix E
Description of the monodromy

In this section, we describe the monodromy of the bundle
\pi:\pi^{-1}(L)\to L
for the symplectic codimension-2 leaves
L
in case
\alpha\in\Sigma_{0,0}
. In particular, we prove
Proposition 6.1
. The idea is that our functor
F
from
section 3
produces representations which lie above the leaf. As we walk around a closed path in the leaf, we may have the chance to swap the roles of two simples if a symmetry from
section 4
is present. We return to a point above the leaf which we can again describe with the help of the functor. This time, the role of the two simples has swapped, which is equivalent to swapping the building plan of the representations. It is precisely the action of the symmetry on the Kleinian exceptional fiber, which we have described in
Proposition 5.1
and elaborated in
Appendix C
. Combining these steps, we arrive at an explicit description of the monodromy.

We start with a quiver setting
(Q,\alpha)
with
\alpha\in\Sigma_{0,0}
such that
\alpha
is indivisible or
(\gcd(\alpha),p(\gcd(\alpha)^{-1}\alpha))=(2,2)
. Let
L
be a codimension-2 leaf with labeled local quiver
(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime},(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}))
. We perform a case distinction, depending on whether
\alpha
is indivisible or not, and depending on the symmetry present in the local quiver. Recall that “generic” and “pseudo-generic” refers to the precise notions listed in
Definition 2.9
.

The case of indivisible
\alpha

By Namikawa’s description, we are supposed to pick a symplectic resolution
\pi:Y\to\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
and read off the monodromy of the fiber bundle
\pi:\pi^{-1}(L)\to L
over every codimension-2 leaf
L
. In the case that
\alpha
is indivisible, a symplectic resolution is given by
\pi:\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\alpha)\to\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
where
\theta
is a generic stability parameter. The monodromy of this fiber bundle
\pi:\pi^{-1}(L)\to L
is completely well-defined and is precisely Namikawa’s monodromy.

The case
(\gcd(\alpha),p(\gcd(\alpha)^{-1}\alpha))=(2,2)

We claim that in this case the partial resolution
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\alpha)
with
\theta
pseudo-generic is already smooth enough to read off the desired monodromy. Let us elaborate this principle in detail. Let
\theta
be a pseudo-generic stability parameter and
L
be a codimension-2 leaf. Then the local quiver
(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})
at
L
can only have one of the symmetries described in
Figure 4.1
. In the two cases treated in
section C.3
, the variety
\mathcal{M}_{\theta^{\prime}}(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
is only a partial resolution of
\mathcal{M}(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
and the exceptional fiber of the variety
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\alpha)
above any point in
L
consists of a single projective line with two or three singular points, respectively. As we walk around the leaf, the arrangement of this projective line with three singular points is preserved, and we obtain a well-defined action of the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(L)
on the components and special points of the exceptional fiber in
\mathcal{M}_{\theta^{\prime}}(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
.

In conclusion, in both the indivisible case and the (2, 2) case we have a well-defined notion of “naive monodromy” of
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\alpha)
over any codimension-2 leaf. It is given by tracking the projective lines and potentially the two or three singular points. The monodromy is by Dynkin automorphism, once an identification between the Dynkin diagram and the projective lines and potentially singular points in the exceptional fiber has been chosen. In
Lemma E.1
, we explain that this naive monodromy agrees with Namikawa’s monodromy.

Lemma E.1
.

Let
(Q,\alpha)
be a quiver setting with
\alpha\in\,\Sigma_{0,0}
such that
\alpha
is indivisible or
(\gcd(\alpha),p(\gcd(\alpha)^{-1}\alpha))=(2,2)
. Let
\theta
be a pseudo-generic stability parameter and let
L
be a codimension-2 leaf of
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
. Then the naive monodromy of
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\alpha)\to\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
over
L
agrees with Namikawa’s monodromy.

Proof.

Namikawa’s monodromy is the monodromy which we obtain from an actual symplectic resolution. A symplectic resolution
\widetilde{M_{\theta}(Q,\alpha)}
is given by further blowing up along the remaining singular locus, which inflates the two or three singular points per
x\in L
to further projective lines. It is obvious that these points in
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\alpha)
are interchanged when we move around a loop
\gamma:[0,1]\to L
if and only if the projective lines in
\widetilde{M_{\theta}(Q,\alpha)}
are interchanged along the loop
\gamma
. In other words, the naive monodromy which we read off from
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\alpha)
agrees with Namikawa’s monodromy.
∎

The second core ingredient in our identification of the monodromy is the functor
F
from
Appendix B
. Let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\in\operatorname{Mod}_{\Pi_{Q}}
be a collection of simples and
\tilde{S}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{S}_{k}\in\operatorname{Mod}_{\Pi_{Q^{\prime}}}
be the vertex simples of the Kleinian local quiver
(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
. Then we have the functor

F_{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}}:\operatorname{Tw}\{\tilde{S}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{S}_{k}\}\to\operatorname{Tw}\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}.

We shall now bundle these functors
F
together into a single function whose input is a collection
(S_{1},\ldots,S_{k})
. We start with a codimension-2 leaf
L
, given by the isotropic decomposition
\alpha=m_{1}\beta_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}\beta_{k}
. Recall that the representations in
L
are precisely those which decompose as
S_{1}^{m_{1}}\oplus\ldots\oplus S_{k}^{m_{k}}
with
S_{i}\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{s}}(Q,\beta_{i})
. We write

M\coloneqq\big(\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{s}}(Q,\beta_{1})\times\ldots\times\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{s}}(Q,\beta_{k})\big)\setminus\Delta.

Here
\Delta\subseteq\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{s}}(Q,\beta_{i})
denotes the subset of tuples where at least two representations are isomorphic. Note that the space
M
is not exactly the same as the leaf
L
. Instead, we have the natural action of
\Gamma=\operatorname{Aut}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k})
on
M
, and the quotient
M/\Gamma
is precisely the leaf
L
.

Let now
\theta\in\mathbb{Z}^{Q_{0}}
be a pseudo-generic stability parameter and
\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{Q^{\prime}_{0}}
its localized version. Denote by
E
the exceptional fiber of
\pi:\mathcal{M}_{\theta^{\prime}}(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})\to\mathcal{M}(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
. Then we can interpret the family of functors as a continuous function

F:M\times E\to\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\alpha).

Remark E.2
.

The group
\Gamma
acts naturally on
M
and acts on
E
by the lift of the action on
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha)
thanks to
Lemma D.4
. This way,
\Gamma
also acts on the product
M\times E
and we notice that the function
F:M\times E\to\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\alpha)
is in fact
\Gamma
-invariant. This can be explained easily as follows: An element
m\in\,M
determines the building plan of a representation, and an element
e\in E
determines the building substance of the representation. When we permute the building plan and simultaneously permute the substance, we obtain the same representation. This is analogous to the fact that when you rotate the building plan of a house and you simultaneously permute the bricks, you obtain the same house. We conclude that
F
is
\Gamma
invariant and therefore descends to a map
F:(M\times E)/\Gamma\to\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\alpha)
.

Remark E.3
.

In the statement of
Proposition 6.1
we make use of the canonical map
\pi_{1}(L)\to\Gamma
. To describe this map, let
\gamma:[0,1]\to L
be a closed path and take a lift
\tilde{\gamma}:[0,1]\to M
. Then determine the
g\in\Gamma
such that
\tilde{\gamma}(1)=g\tilde{\gamma}(0)
. Simply speaking, the map
\pi_{1}(L)\to\Gamma
forgets the entire trajectory of the path, and remembers only which simples are swapped along the way.

We are now ready to prove
Proposition 6.1
, which claims that
F:(M\times E)/\Gamma\to\pi^{-1}(L)
is an isomorphism. This identifies the fiber bundle
\pi:\pi^{-1}(L)\to L
and tells us that the desired monodromy is nothing else than the action of
\Gamma
on
E
.

Proof of
Proposition 6.1
.

Let us explain all three statements after each other. Regard the first statement. The projection
M\times E\to M
is a trivial fiber bundle with fiber
E
. The action by the finite group
\Gamma
is free, therefore its quotient is still a fiber bundle with fiber
E
.

Regard the second statement. Let us observe that
F
indeed maps to
\pi^{-1}(L)
. Indeed, let
m=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{k})\in M
and let
e\in E
. Then
F(m,e)
is a
\theta
-polystable representation by
Lemma 3.2
which semisimplifies to
S_{1}^{m_{1}}\oplus\ldots\oplus S_{k}^{m_{k}}
. Therefore we have
F(m,e)\in\pi^{-1}(L)
. Moreover the diagram is commutative as claimed.

Let us explain that the map
F:(M\times E)/\Gamma\to\pi^{-1}(L)
is an isomorphism. It suffices to check the bijectivity fiberwise. Recall that the functor
F_{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}}:\operatorname{Tw}\{\tilde{S}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{S}_{k}\}\to\operatorname{Tw}\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}
is a strict
A_{\infty}
-equivalence. This makes
F:E\to\pi^{-1}([S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}])
injective. Since any
\theta
-polystable representation in
\pi^{-1}([S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}])
lies in
\operatorname{Tw}\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}
, the map
F:E\to\pi^{-1}([S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}])
is also surjective. This proves fiberwise bijectivity and finishes the second statement.

Regard the third statement. Let
\gamma:[0,1]\to L
be a closed path and
\tilde{\gamma}:[0,1]\to L
is its lift to
L
with
\tilde{\gamma}(1)=g\tilde{\gamma}(0)
. We need to show that the monodromy action of
\gamma
on
\pi^{-1}(\gamma(0))
is equal to the action of
g
on
E
. Thanks to the second statement, this is immediate, but we spell out the instructive detail. Write
\gamma(0)=F(\tilde{\gamma}(0),e)
with
m\in M
and
e\in E
. Then the lift of
\gamma
to the exceptional fiber
\pi^{-1}(L)
can be described explicitly by
\rho(t)=F(\tilde{\gamma}(t),e)
. In particular, we have

\rho(1)=F(\tilde{\gamma}(1),e)=F(g\tilde{\gamma}(0),e)=F(\tilde{\gamma}(0),g^{-1}e).

In other words, while
\rho(0)
lies in the projective line or singular point given by
e
, the representation
\rho(1)
lies in the projective line or singular point given by
g^{-1}e
. This proves the third statement and finishes the proof.
∎

Appendix F
Products and symmetric products

In the present section, we show how to determine the Namikawa-Weyl groups of symmetric and direct products. Together with the analysis of the Namikawa-Weyl groups of the individual quiver varieties
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha_{i})
in the previous section, this lets us determine the Namikawa-Weyl groups of arbitrary quiver varieties that have a symplectic resolution. For brevity, we denote the Namikawa-Weyl group of
X
by
W(X)
.

Lemma F.1
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
be symplectic singularities with good
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action. Then
W(X_{1}\times\ldots\times W_{k})=W(X_{1})\times\ldots\times W(X_{k})
.

Proof.

Choose symplectic resolutions
\pi_{i}:Y_{i}\to X_{i}
. Then the codimension-2 leaves of
X_{1}\times\ldots\times X_{k}
are given by
X_{1}^{\mathsf{reg}}\times\ldots\times L\times\ldots\times X_{k}^{\mathsf{reg}}
where the
L
is at the
i
-th index and
L
runs through the set of codimension-2 leaves of
X_{i}
. A symplectic resolution
\pi
of the product variety is given by the product of the individual resolutions
\pi_{i}
. We recall that all individual resolutions
\pi_{i}
are trivial fiber bundles over their smooth locus. To read off the monodromy, we regard the fiber bundle

\pi:X_{1}^{\mathsf{reg}}\times\ldots\times\pi_{1}^{-1}(L)\times\ldots\times X_{k}^{\mathsf{reg}}\to X_{1}^{\mathsf{reg}}\times\ldots\times L\times\ldots\times X_{k}^{\mathsf{reg}}.

It is the direct product of the trivial bundles
\pi_{i}:X_{i}^{\mathsf{reg}}\to X_{i}^{\mathsf{reg}}
and of
\pi_{i}:\pi_{i}^{-1}(L)\to L
, and therefore has the same monodromy as
L
. This proves the claim.
∎

In the lemma below we perform a similar analysis for the symmetric products. It is slightly trickier than the case of direct products, but still relies only on classical observations.

Lemma F.2
.

Let
X
be a symplectic singularity with good
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action. Then
W(S^{n}X)=W(X)
.

Proof.

We differentiate the case whether
X
has dimension
2
or dimension at least
4
.

Let us start with the case that
X
has dimension
2
. Then
X
is in fact a Kleinian singularity. The variety
S^{n}X
has two types of codimension-2 leaves. The first is the leaf
L
which contains the points
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in S^{n}X
where
x_{1}=0
and
x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\in X^{\mathsf{reg}}
are pairwise distinct. A resolution of a neighborhood of such a leaf is given by the product
\tilde{X}\times L\to X\times L
. In particular, the monodromy is the same as the monodromy of the codimension-2 leaf
L\subseteq\,X
.

The second type of codimension-2 leaves can be described as follows. Let
1\leq i<j\leq n
, then the leaf
L_{ij}
consists of points
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in S^{n}
where
x_{i}=x_{j}
and all other entries are pairwise distinct and lie in the smooth locus of
X
. Locally around this leaf, the singularity looks like
(\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{2})/C_{2}
, which is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}^{2}\times(\mathbb{C}^{2}/C_{2})
and thus of
A_{1}
type. This gives no additional monodromy.

Let us now treat the case that
X
has dimension
\geq 4
. Similar to the case of dimension
2
, every codimension-2 leaf of
X
now reappears as codimension-2 leaf for
S^{n}X
, with the same monodromy. The second type of leaves does not appear, given that any connected component of the open locus already has dimension
4
.

Finally, we conclude that
S^{n}X
has the same Namikawa-Weyl group as
X
in both cases. This finishes the proof.
∎
