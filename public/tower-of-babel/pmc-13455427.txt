ISP25: health crisis management training for public health residents in France, a pilot and feasibility study

Introduction

Effective management of health crises is a critical aspect of public health, necessitating specialized skills and knowledge to address complex and often unpredictable situations [1–3]. The unique challenges posed by health crises, ranging from natural disasters to pandemics, highlight the importance of robust training and preparation framework for public health professionals.

While Crisis Resource Management (CRM) training is well-established in clinical healthcare settings since the 1990s, it has traditionally focused on interdisciplinary teams managing patients at the bedside [4]. However, public health residents operate in a fundamentally different context: they must make decisions at the population level, often under strict administrative and political constraints, rather than focusing on individual clinical care. There is currently a lack of data on the application of CRM principles specifically for public health administrative leadership [5, 6]. This creates a specific need to adapt existing CRM models to the institutional realities faced by future public health officials.

In France, public health residents are medical doctors specializing in public health who are frequently integrated into public administrations, such as the Regional Health Agencies (ARS). These agencies play a pivotal role in coordinating health policies and emergency responses. Consequently, these residents require training that bridges theoretical knowledge with the practical application of non-technical skills—such as leadership, communication, and resource utilization—within this specific administrative framework.

This study presents the development and pilot implementation of a health crisis management training program for public health residents, conducted in collaboration with the ARS Île-de-France. Given the exploratory nature of this work, our objectives are: (i) to describe a training model specifically adapted to the French administrative context and the profile of public health residents; (ii) to assess the feasibility and acceptability of this training through preliminary participant feedback rather than definitive effectiveness metrics; and (iii) to provide a translation and cultural adaptation of two recognized assessment tools (the Ottawa CRM Global Rating Scale and the Modified Simulation Effectiveness Tool) to facilitate future research in the Francophone community.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was designed as a prospective, single-group, pre-post intervention pilot feasibility study. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling from a cohort of public health residents in the Île-de-France region. In the French medical education system, public health residents are medical doctors undergoing specialized training in epidemiology, health policy, and administration; unlike clinical residents, they primarily operate within government agencies rather than at the patient’s bedside. Six residents voluntarily participated in the training session. The sample size was determined by the availability of residents within the department at the time of the study, rather than by an a priori power calculation, consistent with the exploratory nature of this pilot. Participants had a broad range of backgrounds and were in their first to fourth years of residency. The session was held at the Regional Health Agency (ARS) in the Île-de-France region and was led by the Agency’s Defense and Security Department.

Training session and simulation exercise

The training session aimed to introduce participants to Crisis Resource Management (CRM) principles specifically adapted to the context of public health administration. The program consisted of two distinct components:

The morning session included a series of theoretical lectures addressing the fundamental principles of health crisis management and the specific organizational structure of the health administration system in France. The objective was to furnish participants with the essential conceptual knowledge and foundational tools necessary for effective crisis response at a regional level.

The afternoon session featured a simulation exercise designed to replicate a realistic health crisis scenario requiring population-level decision-making rather than individual patient care. Participants were required to engage in real-time strategic planning, interagency coordination, and complex problem-solving tasks under administrative constraints. Each participant was assigned a specific functional role in the crisis room. To mimic the hierarchical reality of a crisis center, roles requiring higher-level management skills (e.g., Room Manager) were assigned to the most senior residents, while operational roles were assigned to less experienced residents.

Assessment questionnaires

We used two established Likert scales to assess participant performance and perceptions regarding the simulation experience. Following the ISPOR (Professional Society for Health Economics and Outcomes Research) principles of good practice, we performed a translation and cultural adaptation of both questionnaires [7–10]. The adaptation process was conducted by two medical doctors specialized in public health and one security officer to ensure the terminology accurately reflected the French administrative crisis management context rather than a standard clinical setting. Psychometric validation was not performed given the pilot nature of the study.

The Ottawa Global Rating Scale for Crisis Resource Management (CRM) is an internationally recognized tool that measures essential non-technical skills involved in crisis response, including leadership, teamwork, communication, situational awareness, decision-making, and resource utilization [11]. Each criterion is measured using a seven-level Likert scale [12, 13]. We used our French-adapted version (see Supplementary S1.1) to assess participants’ self-perceived skills both before and after the simulation to observe potential trajectories in self-efficacy.

The Modified Simulation Effectiveness Tool (SET-M) is a questionnaire designed to gather participants’ perspectives on the quality and educational impact of the simulation, specifically addressing the briefing, debriefing, and immersive aspects [14, 15]. Each criterion was measured using a three-level Likert scale, ranging from “disagree” to “totally agree.” A linguistically adapted French version was distributed for feedback collection (see Supplementary S2.1). Crucially, items related to clinical knowledge and patient communication were modified or omitted to align with the population-level decision-making focus of the exercise, as opposed to patient bedside management [8, 16].

To encourage honest feedback, questionnaire responses were de-identified. Participants completed the CRM scale before and after the practical session, and the SET-M immediately after the debriefing. While efforts were made to maintain confidentiality, we acknowledge that true anonymity is difficult to guarantee in a small group setting with specific assigned roles.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis

Data management and visualization were performed using R software (version 4.4.2; R Core Team, 2024) and the Tidyverse package [17]. Consistent with the study’s design as a pilot feasibility assessment with a limited sample size (n = 6), no inferential statistical hypothesis testing was conducted. We relied exclusively on descriptive statistics to summarize participant characteristics and identify trends in the data.

For the CRM scale, the analysis focused on:

Ethics approval and consent to participate

According to local regulations and institutional guidelines, formal ethics committee approval was not required for this educational training simulation. However, the study was conducted in strict accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participation was strictly voluntary. Residents were explicitly assured that their participation—or refusal to participate—would have no impact on their academic grading or evaluations, thereby ensuring that no coercion was involved. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the session, following a comprehensive explanation of the exercise’s nature, expectations, and potential psychological stressors. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the simulation at any time without penalty should they experience undue distress.

Regarding data privacy, participants were informed that their data would be de-identified and used exclusively for publication purposes. Strict confidentiality was maintained during data processing and reporting. While every effort was made to ensure confidentiality, the authors acknowledge that complete anonymity could not be fully guaranteed given the small group size and the specific functional roles assigned to each resident.

Results

Participants

A total of six public health residents participated in this pilot study. The cohort consisted of five male residents and one female resident. The group possessed varying levels of seniority: three participants were in their first year of residency, one was in their second year, and two were in their fourth year. Regarding prior exposure to the field, while the majority of participants (n = 4) reported having some prior experience in health crisis management, five out of the six residents had no prior professional experience working within a Regional Health Agency (ARS) (Table 1).

SET-M questionnaire

Participant satisfaction with the training session was high. Notably, no participant disagreed with any of the statements in the SET-M questionnaire, indicating strong acceptance of the pilot format (Fig. 2).

Regarding the prebriefing, four participants “totally agreed” that it was beneficial for their learning, while the remaining two “somewhat agreed.” However, regarding the impact on their confidence, the majority (n = 4) “somewhat agreed” that the prebriefing helped, with two participants stating they “totally agreed.”

For the simulation scenario, participants expressed the highest confidence in their theoretical understanding and communication. Four participants “totally agreed” that they developed a better understanding of health crisis management and felt more confident communicating with colleagues. Conversely, regarding specific operational skills—such as prioritizing tasks, applying knowledge acquired during the morning session, and overall preparedness to respond—ratings were positive but moderate; for these items, five participants “somewhat agreed” and one “totally agreed.”

The debriefing phase received the strongest endorsement from the group. All six participants “totally agreed” that the debriefing provided a constructive evaluation of the simulation. Similarly, five participants “totally agreed” that the debriefing contributed to their learning and allowed them to express their feelings before focusing on the analytical aspects of the scenario.

Finally, qualitative feedback from the open-ended question highlighted a structural observation: some participants reported periods of inactivity toward the end of the session, noting that engagement levels varied depending on the specific functional role assigned in the crisis room.

Discussion

Main results and feasibility

This pilot study demonstrates the feasibility of implementing a simulation-based Crisis Resource Management (CRM) training program specifically adapted for public health residents. Six residents with varying levels of seniority participated. The high acceptability of the training is evidenced by the SET-M results, where all participants agreed or totally agreed with the utility of the prebriefing, the scenario, and the debriefing.

Regarding skill acquisition, the analysis of individual trajectories reveals a nuanced picture. While overall confidence in crisis management increased for all participants—suggesting the training successfully empowered residents to engage in crisis response—specific complex domains showed different trends. Notably, while leadership and communication scores generally improved, scores for problem-solving and situational awareness remained stable or even decreased for some participants.

This divergence may reflect a “recalibration” of self-efficacy. One possible explanation is that the theoretical session in the morning may have initially inflated participants’ confidence (providing conceptual knowledge), while the immersive simulation in the afternoon confronted them with the practical difficulty of applying these concepts under pressure. The decrease in self-rating for problem-solving observed in some participants could potentially be interpreted through the lens of the Dunning–Kruger effect [18, 19]. This cognitive bias suggests that individuals with limited experience may initially overestimate their ability (unconscious incompetence). The simulation likely facilitated a transition to “conscious incompetence,” where participants recognized the true complexity of the task and their own limitations. If this is the case, this realization, while numerically represented as a lower score or stagnation, would represent a critical pedagogical step in professional development, as noted by similar studies in medical education.

This study is subject to several methodological limitations inherent to its design as a pilot feasibility project.

Perspectives

Despite these limitations, this study addresses a critical gap identified in the literature: the lack of CRM training specifically tailored to the administrative and population-based decision-making context of public health, as opposed to the clinical “bedside” focus of traditional CRM. By providing a translated and culturally adapted version of the Ottawa CRM Scale and SET-M, this work offers a methodological foundation for future Francophone research. Future iterations should aim for larger, multi-centric cohorts and utilize objective external observers alongside self-assessment to provide a more rigorous evaluation of training effectiveness.

Transferability and contextualization

Transferability of the training model

To date, this initiative represents a novel proposal for crisis management training specifically tailored to public health residents acting in administrative capacities. While Crisis Resource Management (CRM) is well-established in clinical settings, its application to the specific constraints of the French health administration—where decisions are made at a population level rather than at the bedside—constitutes a specific pedagogical niche [3]. Consequently, the specific roles and scenarios designed for this study may not be directly transferable to countries with different health system architectures without local adaptation.

Nevertheless, the core pedagogical approach—combining theoretical instruction with high-fidelity simulation—aligns with the broader literature. Established models, such as the Health Crisis Management Framework, consistently show that simulation enhances non-technical skills like decision-making and teamwork. Although our sample size (n = 6) prevents generalizing our findings on effectiveness, our observations of high participant satisfaction mirror results from larger studies. For instance, Escribano et al. demonstrated significant improvements in resilience and self-efficacy among health science students following CRM training, and Gartland et al. found similar benefits for leadership skills in emergency medicine residents [20, 21]. These parallels suggest that the feasibility and acceptability observed in our French pilot could potentially be replicated in other contexts, provided the scenario is adapted to local institutional needs [2].

Looking forward, the ongoing global health crisis has underscored the need for flexible educational models. As noted by a study from Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, personalized, technology-enhanced education is essential for meeting pandemic-related challenges. Integrating virtual reality or remote simulation components into future iterations of this training could not only enhance its accessibility but also improve its external validity by facilitating multi-center studies with larger cohorts.

Relevance of the adapted evaluation tools

A key contribution of this study is the translation and cultural adaptation of the CRM scale and SET-M for the Francophone public health context [7]. While the original English versions are internationally recognized and validated for clinical environments, their application to public health leadership remains limited [9, 14, 16]. Unlike clinicians managing individual patients, public health residents operate under distinct administrative and political constraints that require a specific lexicon and skill set [22].

By adapting these tools to exclude purely clinical items (e.g., patient communication) and focusing on population-level coordination, we provide a resource for the broader community. The SET-M, in particular, proved valuable for collecting structured feedback on the simulation design, identifying specific areas for pedagogical refinement [23–26]. This combined approach—adapting established instruments to a new linguistic and professional context—serves as a model for other competency-based public health training programs seeking to evaluate non-technical skills rigorously [8, 14–16].

Perspectives

The preliminary results of this pilot study suggest that a simulation-based training model specifically adapted for public health residents in an administrative context is both feasible and highly acceptable to learners. While the small sample size precludes definitive conclusions regarding effectiveness, these findings generate several hypotheses to guide future research and development efforts.

Expanding study design and sample size

To address the limitations of generalizability and statistical power identified in this pilot, future research must transition from descriptive analysis to rigorous evaluation. Expanding the sample size through multi-centric collaborations with other Regional Health Agencies would mitigate the homogeneity bias inherent in a single-center study. Furthermore, as recommended by the reviewers, future studies should employ a controlled design with a comparison group to isolate the specific impact of the training from general professional development or social desirability biases.

Technological integration

Incorporating advanced technology and virtual components into training sessions represents a key avenue for development, as highlighted by recent literature on pandemic preparedness. Virtual simulations and e-learning modules could offer flexible training options, overcome geographical constraints and allowing for the standardization of scenarios across different regions. This would also facilitate the implementation of large-scale studies necessary for robust statistical analysis.

Longitudinal assessment

A major limitation of the current study was the immediate post-training assessment. To determine the true pedagogical value of the program, future iterations should incorporate long-term follow-ups. Assessing participants several months after the training would allow researchers to evaluate skill retention and, crucially, the transfer of these non-technical skills to actual professional practice during real health crises.

Psychometric validation of tools

Finally, this study provided the initial step of translating and culturally adapting the Ottawa CRM scale and SET-M for the French administrative context. However, full psychometric validation (assessing internal consistency, factor analysis, and inter-rater reliability) was not possible with the current sample size. A dedicated methodological study with a larger cohort is now required to formally validate these French versions, thereby providing the Francophone public health community with robust metrics for future educational research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study describes the development and pilot implementation of a health crisis management training model specifically tailored to public health residents acting in administrative capacities—a population distinct from the clinical teams traditionally targeted by Crisis Resource Management (CRM).

The initial findings demonstrate that this pedagogical approach is feasible and meets the felt needs of residents for practical, decision-based training. While the descriptive data suggests positive trends in learner confidence and engagement, the exploratory nature of this work requires caution in interpreting efficacy. This pilot serves as a foundational step, providing adapted assessment tools and a tested scenario design. Future research must now build on this groundwork by conducting larger, controlled studies with longitudinal follow-up to definitively establish the effectiveness of this training in improving the resilience and response capabilities of the future public health workforce.
