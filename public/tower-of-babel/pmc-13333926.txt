VR-H2Safe: a VR-based hydrogen vehicle safety & efficiency optimization platform with V2X integration
Authors: Mohammad BaniSalman, Mohammad Aljaidi, Ahmad Abdullah Alshammari, Ali Fayez Alkoradees, Samy Abdelwahab Safaan, Mohammad Khishe, Elham Askari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydrogen vehicles are facing significant challenges, including safety risks in the event of leakage, high costs associated with physical testing, and fuel cell inefficiency under dynamic conditions; to address these concerns, we propose VR-H2Safe, a cyber-physical platform that combines high-fidelity simulation, physics-informed reinforcement learning (RL), and secure vehicle-to-everything (V2X) communication. Using ANSYS Fluent Large Eddy Simulation (LES) to train real-time predictive models, the platform’s digital twin simulates hydrogen leakage with 92% spatio-temporal correlation compared to high-fidelity LES-CFD benchmarks at key monitoring points. Furthermore, we introduce the H2-LEAK protocol, which extends the SAE J2735 standard with a 128-bit risk vector and blockchain authentication, which provides hazard warnings in less than 2 s with 95% spoofing resistance. In addition, our physics-informed AI system optimizes the fuel cell performance, achieving a 10% increase in energy efficiency and a 58% reduction in thermal violations during hardware verification. The experimental findings of urban, tunnel, and road conditions prove that VR-H2Safe accelerates the process of safety verification and reduces the engineering validation phase from 14 to 10 weeks.Keywords: Hydrogen vehicle safety, Physics-informed machine learning, Digital twin, V2X communication, Blockchain, Cyber-physical systemsSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The shift to more sustainable ways of getting around has really made hydrogen-powered vehicles (HVs) a key player in the market for low emissions heavy duty transport - and for good reason; they produce zero emissions, can be recharged super quick and - all things being equal - offer a longer range1. People in government and industry think that widespread adoption of these vehicles could cut global transport CO2 emissions by a whopping 15% by the year 20402. While the potential for HVs is huge, technical problems are still holding them back. One major issue is that hydrogen leaks are a real safety risk - especially in confined spaces such as city tunnels or underground parking garages - due to their very low ignition energy (0.017 mJ) and the fact that they can catch fire with a wide range of ratios (4–75%)3; and to make matters worse, hydrogen can also cause problems with storage tanks, particularly if they’re subjected to sudden changes, such as sharp turns or braking, which can weaken the tank over time and ultimately cause it to fail catastrophically4. To make matters even more complicated, fuel cells do not perform as well under changing driving conditions - such as traffic jams or stop-start traffic - which can knock range down by as much as 30% and make it harder to see the value in these vehicles5. And to top it all off, simulating the spread of hydrogen in real-time is a huge challenge, also for computer modelling people.

Existing approaches to validation of HV safety and efficacy are fragmented. Although physical testing is accurate, it is costly; for example, the cost of a single test of a ruptured hydrogen tank is close to $500,000 and require specialized equipment6,7. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) systems such as ANSYS Fluent replicate hydrogen dispersion by means of a Gaussian plume model, which takes into account prevailing wind speeds but does not assume that the buoyant plume will cause turbulence8. With V2X (vehicle-to-everything) protocols, such as SAE J2735, which are primarily focused on collision avoidance, there are no standardized ways of communicating hydrogen-specific hazards, which further delays emergency response in flooded and high-density scenarios9; in some cases, energy-efficiency systems based on rules exacerbate this problem by optimizing energy efficiency with reduced safety margins. For example, aggressive acceleration requires the maintenance of high tank pressures, irrespective of the leakage risks associated with the pressure load10.

These gaps bring up the critical need of common platform that brings together high-fidelity simulation, artificial intelligence-driven optimization, and secure V2X communications. There have been notable advances in the past: Tesla and Ford use VR for crash testing but do not include hydrogen dispersion modelling11, ISO 15,869 defines standards for tank stress testing, but manual processes extend the certification time to 14 weeks12, and recent studies published in IEEE Transactions propose LSTM networks to optimize fuel cells, but none of them reconcile efficiency with real-time safety limitations13.

To overcome these gaps, we propose VR-H2Safe, a novel cyber-physical methodology that allows closing the gap between computational fluid dynamics of high-fidelity and real-time safety systems. Literature review

This section discusses the progress in the three important areas: VR in automotive engineering, hydrogen safety standards and research, and V2X protocols. It positions the innovations by VR-H2Safe in the overall context of research by locating existing foundations and highlighting the new contributions.

VR has become a new method of safety testing and simulation of vehicles. The latest research, including the VRDeepSafety platform by BaniSalman et al.14, is based on VR and V2X communication to simulate the scenario of accidents, where the combination of Unity-based simulations and a CNN-LSTM architecture resulted in an 85% accuracy of the accident prediction. Even though platforms such as VRDeepSafety solve the limitations inherent to existing datasets (e.g., KITTI, nuScenes) by including multimodal sensor fusion, including V2X data, they do not directly model hazards in hydrogen, such as the leak dispersion dynamics due to turbulence. The previous VR models, like that created by Crosato et al.15, were preoccupied with multi-vehicle interaction modeling, which did not involve detailed evaluations of hydrogen fuel cell systems. VR-H2Safe builds upon these by integrating high-fidelity settings of Unreal Engine 5 with the simulations of ANSYS Fluent of the large eddy scale (LES) to simulate hydrogen leakage during dynamic operations (e.g., cornering-induced tank stress.

Hydrogen vehicle safety has been strongly influenced by the several global regulatory measures, including the GTR Global Technical Regulation No. 13, which regulates the crash-test leakage and electrical safety of hydrogen storage systems16. The former section of this rule establishes component-based standards and circumstances of testing the vehicles, whereas the latter section aims to achieve pragmatic harmonization of crash tests and fuel system integrity issues17. Outside GTR No. 13, such standards as ISO 19880-1 provide guidelines for gaseous hydrogen fueling stations, and SAE J2601 is a fueling standard for light-duty hydrogen cars18,19.

Current standards rely on the use of fixed Gaussian plume models, which cannot be applied to analyzing leaks in conditions of turbulent flow, for example, in an urban canyon or a tunnel. Recent CFD models, such as ANSYS Fluent LES validation benchmark program20,21 have confirmed dispersion patterns of hydrogen, but in isolation from real-time VR simulations. VR-H2Safe has filled this gap through the integration of LES turbulence modelling and Niagara particle systems with a correlation of 92 to ANSYS validation data in stratified leaks.

DSRC, ITS-G5 and C-V2X communication standards have been developed to support ultra-reliable, low-latency security applications. Recent surveys have identified protocol fragmentation as a major problem, especially in Europe, where discussions between DSRC and C-V2X are still ongoing22. Even though 5G NR-V2X (release 16) has latency as low as 10ms to collision avoidance23,24, the risk vectors unique to hydrogen are not included in existing standards, resulting in the propagation of hazards being delayed25; systems like VRDeepSafety combine V2X to predict accidents but are based on more traditional accidents and not hydrogen leaks. Although it supports a dual protocol-compatible hybrid approach (DSRC and C-V2X), it requires additional hardware to enable interoperability26,27. The VR-H2Safe protocol addresses these shortcomings by implementing the H2-LEAK protocol (Fig. 1), which extends the SAE J2735 with a 128-bit hydrogen risk vector (leak rate, dispersion speed) and a verification of the blockchain protocol, to achieve a C-V2 × 5G end-to-end latency of 1.8 s (Table 1).

VR-H2SAFE platform architecture and technical framework

VR-H2Safe platform resolves hydrogen vehicle safety and efficiency challenges by a highly integrated architecture (Fig. 2). VR-H2Safe is based on a multi-level architecture combining real-time virtual simulation, local artificial intelligence (AI) and secure V2X vehicle communication. By integrating the photorealistic graphics engine Unreal Engine 5, advanced turbulence modelling in ANSYS Fluent and 5G-secured blockchains, the platform provides real-time detection of hydrogen leaks, dynamic risks, besides emergency response times. This section will detail the three-layer architecture, hydrogen dispersion physics, new protocols and artificial intelligence optimization to achieve 92% accuracy in hydrogen leakage detection, 1.8s V2X warning latency and a 10% increase in hydrogen fuel efficiency compared to the current methodologies based on ANSYS Fluent benchmarks and industry simulation. Integrating advanced simulation techniques with photorealistic visualization capabilities will enable engineers to assess as well as measure hydrogen leakage dynamically and in real-time when it occurs, which is more than the capabilities of traditional Gaussian plume currently in use.

Data layer and edge processing

The data layer, which is designed to enhance the simulation layer, operates on ARM Cortex-M7 microcontrollers. These microcontrollers run optimized TinyML models for real-time leak detection using a simplified MobileNetV2 CNN adapted for embedded systems. Figure 1 shows that the CNN consists of five convolutional layers with ReLU activation, a global average pooling layer, and a fully connected layer with a sigmoid output for binary classification (leak/no leak) as the final layer. VR-H2Safe platform is based on a 3-layer architecture (Fig. 2) which combines virtual reality, edge computing, as well as secure V2X communications to address the safety and performance challenges of hydrogen vehicles.

The training dataset consisted of 500 simulated cases of leakages that had been generated by ANSYS Fluent LES; in order to bridge the dimensionality between complex 3D turbulence fields and the processing capacity limitations of edge microcontrollers, we used Spatial Feature Extraction. Rather than ingesting the full volumetric CFD data, the model extracts time-series vector data only from virtual coordinates corresponding to physical sensor placements (e.g., fuel tank valve, stack intake, and wheel well exhaust). This is an effective approach to reduce the 3D problem to a collection of 1D correlations, whilst keeping context by means of a Library of Primitives - a collection of pre-computed dispersion gradients of specific wind vectors; this allows the TinyML model to link the local sensor variations with global leakage patterns while maintaining a 92% correlation even in the case of transient wind gusts.

Hydrogen sensors can detect concentrations as low as 1 ppm, pressure information from within the tank is provided by strain gauges with a precision of 0.1 MPa and temperature gradients with an accuracy of 0.5°Cs are monitored using thermal sensors onboard, which provide real-time data to microcontrollers. The resulting TinyML models that are trained on these physics-constrained leak scenarios meet an inference latency of 50ms and is capable to instantaneously mitigating hazards even in a disconnected environment. To increase the accuracy of detection and reduce false positives, a Kalman filter is used to combine Sensor Data with the results of Simulation Data. It generates a Fused Leak Estimate of the Probability and Severity of a leak by combining TinyML Predictions from onboard sensors in real-time and Predicted H2 Concentration calculated in parallel. The filter uses a Process Model derived from Simplified Dispersion Equations and a Measurement Model based on Sensor Characteristics to provide a more stable, as well as reliable estimation of the state of the system. The fusion procedure allows for a consistent evaluation of the leakage under quickly changing vehicles in addition to ambient circumstances, by reducing the impacts of noise and transient fluctuations.

Even though the LES approach is very precise, its computational costs may limit its applicability in real-time. To address this, VR-H2Safe uses several real-time or near-real-time techniques, including hybrid LES-RANS modelling, pre-computed LES libraries with interpolation, and machine learning-based surrogate models. As described in Sect. 4 as well as 5, these methods enable VR-H2Safe to balance the accuracy and computational power of the system with the specific requirements of the application.

In order to ensure the model adapts to high-speed vehicle traffic or abrupt changes in wind conditions, which is likely to be overlooked by an exclusively offline training, the system uses a Library of Primitives. The offline LES training covers a high-dimensional manifold of wind vectors (015 m/s) and 3D vehicle velocities. The Edge AI uses online inference to interpolate among these precomputed states to allows the digital twin to predict the impact of transient gusts or cornering more effectively by interpolating between library states, and therefore can maintain a high level of robust performance in dynamic conditions with minimal computational latency compared to new CFD steps.

H2-LEAK Protocol

The H2-LEAK protocol gives an extra ability to the SAE J2735 V2X standard so that hydrogen safety risks can be handled, using a 128-bit risk vector with blockchain-based communication, which allows instant dangers alert; It also extends Basic Safety Message (BSM) structure with twelve data fields about hydrogen so vehicles and roadside systems can assess hazards as situations shift.

Figure 4 shows how a 128-bit risk vector is organised and how bits are spread out in the H2-LEAK protocol. This vector goes together with an SAE J2735 Basic Safety Message (BSM), which is mainly used for providing hydrogen hazard details. Every part stands for one data field and its size links to the total bits given to it. In a vector, there are components for leak rate, 3D dispersion velocity, flammability index, tank pressure, GPS locations, leak severity, time until the critical point, and tank condition. These give well-detailed hazard alerts for the hydrogen-powered vehicles.

2)Blockchain Integration and Scalability.

Blockchain Integration and Scalability.

The H2-LEAK protocol is working with Hyperledger Fabric, a type of permission blockchain, for keeping hazard messages authentic and integrity. Sending vehicles make digital signatures on each message, which are checked by a group of trusted nodes, for example, the roadside units and infrastructure providers and messages are sent onwards to other vehicles after that. This prevents spoofing as well as man-in-the-middle attacks, where attackers might put their own hazardous information or change the data. The protocol uses Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance as a consensus. PBFT allows up to f = (n-1)/3 broken or harmful nodes out of n nodes in the whole network.

Figure 6 provides an illustration of how the messages go in Hyperledger Fabric while carrying out validation of the H2-LEAK message using the Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT) consensus method. At first, the Hydrogen Vehicle (HV) primarily moves forward and transmits a PRE-PREPARE message to all Replica nodes, which includes the original input message, a sequence number, as well as its signature. The individual Replica has to verify the received message, then it makes a PREPARE message, which gets transmitted to each node again. The nodes will send a COMMIT message only after they have acquired 2f + 1 similar PREPARE messages (where f = 1 when three nodes are included). Receiving 2f + 1 COMMIT messages causes nodes to modify the local ledger they maintain. To complete, the Primary gives a REPLY message to HV for validation confirmation. Replicas might also send further REPLY messages.

Scalability is considered important for the overall design. The consensus method is still often the main issue for speed. Whenever more vehicles enter, blockchain nodes will receive heavier message traffic. To address this, sharding is being considered, so the blockchain can be divided into many smaller chains, as well as each chain validating a certain group of messages. On top of this, off-chain data aggregation is being evaluated, as it can merge messages together, allowing validation to be done on a single transaction, and thereby reducing the consensus extra work.

AI-Driven Digital Twin

The AI-based digital twin in VR-H2Safe combines virtual simulation with real-life driving of hydrogen cars, using reinforcement learning within a physical framework. This architecture gives strength to safety factors by linking the fuel cell models, which are in a lower order, with control policies that can be adjusted, and this is continuously improved by HIL testing and ANSYS simulation.

The digital twin consists of a reinforcement learning (RL) agent, which is trained with the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm, to reduce the loss of power distribution and the risk of hydrogen leaks. PPO is chosen because of its stability, sample performance, and the ability to control continuous action space, which is suitable for real-time control activities. Being an on-policy, PPO uses a clipped surrogate objective to prevent excessively aggressive policy updates that might result in unsteady training or divergence. These weights were initially set considering ISO 15,869 safety margins and engineering judgement, as well as then refined by a genetic algorithm.

To find the best compromise between efficiency and security, we used a genetic algorithm (using the DEAP Python library) to fine-tune the weighting of the reward function (alpha, beta, and g). We started with 50 different weight classes, over 100 generations, and the algorithm evolved. This was done by selecting the best sets (tournament selection), combining them (one-point conversion), and randomly changing them slightly (uniform mutation). Each set of weights was tested in simulated driving scenarios with various hydrogen leaks, and the best sets - those that maximized the rewards (i.e. efficiency and safety) were more likely to be selected and combined; the algorithm finally settled on the following values: α = 0.6, β = 0.3, and γ = 0.1. This approach has been chosen for its ability to search efficiently for complex non-linear parameter spaces as well as for its noise-resistance.

To reduce the risk of reward hacking, i.e., the RL agent may be induced to focus on efficiency at the expense of safety, we added a deterministic Safety Action Masking (SAM) layer. This layer is a non-learnable rational barrier between policy output of the agent and the vehicle actuators.

Experimental Setup and Methodology

To perform a hydrogen dispersion simulation, a workstation with two NVIDIA RTX 4090 graphics cards, 64 GB of memory, and a 16-core, 32-thread AMD Ryzen 9 9950X processor was used; these computing resources were sufficient to run offline LES simulations and to train artificial intelligence models. Unreal Engine 5.5 was used to create a virtual reality environment. Nanite virtual geometry has helped to create high-detail urban canyons as well as open road scenarios, where Lumen is used to provide a global illumination that appears to be physically accurate. The prediction of turbulence was done by hydrogen dispersion modelling in ANSYS Fluent 2024R1 with Large Eddy simulation with a SmagorinskyLilly model at the subgrid scale to predict turbulence. ROS Noetic was used to manage the Unreal Engine 5-Fluent interface, which was operated on Ubuntu 20.04 and enabled the two-way information exchange. Unreal Engine provided boundary conditions like the speed of the wind, and in its turn, Fluent was able to send real-time data concerning the concentrations of hydrogen to be visualized. Adaptive mesh refinement is also used in Fluent, and the cell size can range from 2 mm around the leak source to as large as 5 cm at a distance.

The AI-physics co-simulation environment of VR-H2Safe was tested on hardware-in-the-loop (HIL). The PPO-optimized RL agent was executed on a Speedgoat Performance real-time target system, which uses an Intel Xeon E5-1650 v4 processor, 3.5 GHz, and has 64 GB of RAM (Section III.A). A commercial 5 kW Ballard FCveloCity-HD stack of hydrogen fuel cells was interfaced to this system and loaded with sensors that measured the stack voltage, current, temperature, hydrogen pressure, and the rate of coolant flow. While this stack is sub-scale compared to typical 80–100 kW automotive engines, its thermal and electrochemical response dynamics scale linearly with active area and cell count, making the optimization logic representative of and transferrable to mainstream vehicle-mounted systems. The realistic vehicle speed profiles were provided by the Worldwide Harmonized Light Vehicles Test Procedure (WLTP) Class 3 driving cycle. Virtual leak-response performance was tested by adding a leak-response by altering the supply pressure of hydrogen, and the behavior of the stack and sensor measurements was recorded by a high-precision dSPACE SCALEXIO. All in all, the HIL system allowed one to test the abilities of the RL agent to enhance fuel cell efficiency in realistic driving conditions and address issues of leakage scenarios.

The performance of the H2-LEAK protocol was tested on a simulated 5G C-V2X network using the OMNeT + + 6.0 discrete-event simulator, the INET 4.4 framework, and the SimuLTE extension. At the same time, 100-node Hyperledger Fabric 2.5 blockchain simulations, benchmarked using the Caliper tool, were run on the AMD Ryzen 9 9950X workstation (Sects. 4–4.1). This blockchain simulator was combined with the OMNeT + + model to simulate the validation of H2-LEAK messages, which is a real-life interaction between vehicles, roadside units, and the blockchain network. The hybrid configuration enabled us to test end-to-end the key protocol elements, including 128-bit risk vector encoding and blockchain authentication latency, as well as the resilience to spoofing in dynamic 5G settings.

Vehicles that leak hydrogen (marked by a red car icon with an exclamation mark) provide the H2-LEAK messages to neighboring RSUs, which send the messages to the blockchain to be validated. When the blockchain is successfully validated, the blockchain sends the confirmation back to the RSUs, and then they relay the message of alert to other vehicles near them.

Simulation fidelity: hydrogen dispersion modeling

The hydrogen dispersion modelling of VR-H2Safe was validated using ANSYS Fluent 2024R1, a commonly used CFD package in industry; the objective was to show the capability of VR-H2Safe, which is developed for real-time visualization and interaction, in reproducing the complicated physics of hydrogen dispersion modeled by a high fidelity CFD solver.

To test whether VR-H2Safe could provide reliable results for the simulation of hydrogen dispersion, three different simulation types were performed: Urban Canyon, Tunnel, and Open Road, using Unreal Engine 5.5. The geometry and boundaries have been set up in accordance with the ANSYS Fluent LES benchmarks referenced in Section III. B. These simulation scenarios mirrored the configurations of real-world. Including buildings, street networks, and ground layouts to show the characteristics of turbulence, such as airflow in the urban (2–8 m per second) and in the tunnel (4% slope, 8 m per second wind); both platforms were simulated as to the same release rate (2, 5, and 10 g per second) of hydrogen leaks at the same location and with adaptive mesh refinement (2–5 mm resolution) to capture fine-scale turbulence, which lasted 10 s in each scenario to observe the formation of the plume.

VR-H2Safe used Unreal Engine 5 and its particle Niagara engine to create real-time visuals of the hydrogen cloud at 30 fps. Niagara has been configured to update particle behavior based on hydrogen concentration information sent via the ROS, and connecting ANSYS Fluent LES output into Unreal Engine. This allowed the particle system to reflect the transient changes in the hydrogen concentration fields as calculated by the ANSYS Fluent system. The entire simulation activity was performed on a high-performance workstation as described in Sect. 4.1.

ANSYS Fluent 2024R1 has been configured with a LES turbulence model with Smagorinsky-Lilly subgrid configuration, which is the same configuration used in the VR-H2Safe system; both platforms used adaptive mesh refinement to provide higher mesh resolution at leak source sites, besides areas with steep concentration gradients, to capture the behavior of fine-scale flows better. In order to ensure a fair and consistent comparison, boundary conditions, including wind speeds as well as directions, were matched in both simulation environments.

A quantitative comparison was performed to validate the fidelity of the VR-H2Safe model against the ANSYS Fluent LES ground truth. It is important to note here that the reported correlation (R²) and RMSE metrics were calculated on sensor-specific time series data, rather than the entire volumetric field; we then extracted the concentration values from 10 virtual probe locations that matched the locations of standard sensors (e.g., tank valve, stack intake, wheel wells, roof vent) to represent the platform’s role in the system as a sensor fusion digital twin. To ensure rigorous validation, spatial alignment was inherent as both models utilized the identical CAD geometry as well as coordinate system. Temporal correspondence between the temporal information of the LES and the VR-H2Safe was achieved by down-sampling the high-frequency time series of the LES data (100 Hz) to the rate of the VR-H2Safe real-time update (10 Hz) through linear interpolation before the calculation of the error metrics.

Automated certification workflow analysis

The aim of this experiment was to determine the ability of VR-H2Safe to reduce the certification process for hydrogen storage systems in line with ISO 15,869, as well as to conduct a comparative process analysis between the traditional, physical-testing-based workflow and the simulation, automated workflow enabled by VR-H2Safe.

The analysis has broken down the ISO 15,869 compliance process into four major stages, namely, (1) Test Preparation and Setup, (2) Test Cycle execution, (3) Data Analysis and Reporting, and (4) Physical validation. The traditional workflow activities and durations were mapped based on industry benchmarks and expert consultations.

The VR-H2Safe workflow substitutes the conventional series of expensive physical tests that are both time-intensive with a simulation-first approach. With the Python APIs of the platform, a digital twin of the target hydrogen tank (based on a Type IV CAD model) is created in the Unreal Engine 5 environment. A comprehensive set of virtual testing is programmatically performed by scripts; that is, pressure cycling, thermal exposure (as would be in bonfire simulating), and localized impact as per ISO 15,869 specifications; these high-fidelity simulations, co-processed with ANSYS physics solver, run in parallel to determine worst-case scenarios and possible points of failure. The results of this are then applied to lead to highly focused and minimized physical testing for final validation, as opposed to the excessive physical testing from the start.

The comparative analysis shows that the engineering validation time frame can be accelerated by reducing the iterative pre-conversion phase from the estimated 14 weeks to 10 weeks. It should be noted that this automated workflow works as a virtual homologation tool to decrease the failure rate of physical tests, rather than as a complete replacement of the legal certification process. The workflow is based on a final three-week targeted physical verification phase to meet the mandatory requirements of the notified bodies for type IV tanks, such as fire and impact tests in the field; as a result, by effectively eliminating most of the physical prototyping and redundant testing phases, the overall time to market is significantly shortened.

As summarized in Table 10, the most significant improvement is due to the reduction of the 8-week physical testing phase to a more effective 5-week virtual simulation phase. Although the VR-H2Safe workflow adds a separate physical validation step after 3 weeks, this step is much more focused and less time-consuming than the traditional approach, as it is based on previous simulation data. This simulation-based methodology not only speeds up the time-to-value ratio, but the analysis is also more comprehensive since the simulated process also allows the engineers to explore a broader variety of failure modes at a fraction of the cost.

Discussions

The VR-H2Safe platform’s efficacy and practicality are strongly demonstrated by the experimental findings, which include simulation fidelity, fuel cell optimization, besides V2X communications performance. This section discusses these findings and examines the main interdependence among the platform’s components. It also addresses existing limitations and outlines the future direction of research.

Interconnectedness of simulation fidelity and digital twin performance

The study determined that the fidelity of simulation of hydrogen dispersion and the efficiency of the AI-based digital twin was correlated in a strong way. The VR-H2Safe dispersion models were compared with ANSYS Fluent results, which revealed a strong correlation (R² > 0.87) and low error (RMSE < 0.2 g/m³) across a different of scenarios; this is a high metric of agreement and is important in making sure that the reinforcement learning (RL) agent is trained on reliable, physically sound data.

Since the RL agent formulates optimal control policies based on the results of the simulations, errors in the dispersion modeling may cause less than optimal or unsafe decision-making in practice. The validation obtained a high level of fidelity, which proves that VR-H2Safe is an accurate representation of the controlling physics of hydrogen, even in dynamic and real-time simulation models.

Further, the experiments on the differences in the accuracy of simulation in Open Road, Urban Canyon, and Tunnel environments illustrate the paramount importance of surrounding buildings on the behavior of hydrogen dispersion and, thus, control system behavior. Taken altogether, these results prove that VR-H2Safe provides a sufficiently realistic training environment. More fundamentally, this validates a generalized methodology for “physics-informed safety modeling.” Our approach uses offline, high-fidelity CFD to train lightweight, real-time edge models (TinyML), demonstrating a scalable pathway to integrate complex physical behaviors into autonomous control systems; this framework is not limited to hydrogen vehicles but is scientifically applicable to other hazardous transport domains, such as chemical freight monitoring or nuclear safety simulations, where real-time physics approximation is critical.

AI-driven optimization: balancing efficiency and safety

Hardware in-loop experiments have shown that the RL controller is superior to the traditional rule-based approach. It has improved fuel efficiency (Wh/km) by up to 10% as well as reduced thermal degradation by 59%, while increasing the safety margin from time to criticality by 55%.

While the rule-based controller lacks adaptability during leakage events, the RL agent actively adjusts the flow of the stack, the cooling stream and the auxiliary power to avoid hazardous conditions, which is especially valuable in confined environments such as tunnels where leakage spreads are limited and the risk is rapidly increasing; the results of the tunnel leakage scenario confirm that RL can optimize safety and efficiency at the same time, two objectives that are usually in competition.

It is important to acknowledge that the reward function weights were optimized specifically for the WLTP Class 3 driving cycle. These weights represent a local optimum for standard urban/highway driving but may not generalize perfectly to more aggressive scenarios, such as the US06 driving cycle, or heavy-load trucking operations where thermal constraints may require higher prioritization; this suggests that a static weight assignment may limit the agent’s adaptability across diverse vehicle classes and operational domains.

Secure and timely hazard communication: the role of V2X and blockchain

The experiments on V2X state that the H2-LEAK protocol has the potential to make a considerable increase in the safety of hydrogen vehicles, providing a way to spread the information about hazards in a timely and trustworthy manner. In addition to the overhead that comes with blockchain validation, H2-LEAK has a mean latency of 1.8 s, which means that the leakage of hydrogen is immediately responded to.

Although the C-V2X standard has a slightly lower latency under non-secure conditions, it is prone to spoofing attacks, which result in its inapplicability in hydrogen safety-sensitive functions. The current combination offers excellent security, and 95% of simulated spoofing attacks can be prevented by integrating Hyperledger Fabric, without compromising security impractically (i.e., striking a balance between security and performance). The 250ms blockchain validation time is an important trade-off to ensure trustworthy hazard information, as well as the high packet delivery rate (99.9%), which is an additional confirmation that H2-LEAK is reliable enough to transmit mission-critical safety information.

Analysis of the 1.8s latency in the scenario of high-speed driving (120 km/h) shows that a car covers about 60 m during message validation. Although significant, this latency should be counterbalanced with the Time-to-Critical (TTC) measurements that we achieved in our HIL results (Table 4), where it took 34–62 s to reach flammability limits because of hydrogen leaks. The communication latency of 1.8s translates into a reaction time that is less than 6% of the available reaction time; this provides drivers or ADAS systems sufficient time to take up braking or rerouting actions long before the environment becomes chemically hazardous. Regarding functional security, a distinction must be made between tactical as well as strategic requirements. While immediate vehicle control (e.g. tank shut-off) requires ASIL-D compliance and a latency of less than 10 ms handled locally by the on-board system, the V2X blockchain layer serves a strategic warning function (ASIL-B context). This layer warns the traffic ahead of the hazard approximately 30 s in advance, which makes the 250 ms latency of the blockchain well within acceptable safety margins.

The reliance on PBFT consensus introduces a theoretical vulnerability: the system remains secure only as long as the number of malicious nodes (f) does not exceed ; in a scenario where a massive botnet compromise of the network, the consensus mechanism would halt; to overcome this, VR-H2Safe incorporates a Local-Only Fail-Safe mode. In case the blockchain does not respond with a validation receipt within a 500ms time constraint, the vehicle ignores the external V2X inputs and reverts to its internal logic of “Safety Action Masking” to guaranteeing individual vehicle safety based solely on onboard sensor data. In order to achieve practical feasibility, the H2-LEAK protocol is designed as a payload extension of the existing SAE J2735 standard. It uses the reserved fields within the Basic Safety Message (BSM) structure, which is backward compatible with existing hardware of DSRC and C-V2X. The blockchain consensus built on edge-computing of current Roadside Units (RSUs) lowers deployment costs by removing the need for expensive proprietary infrastructure.

Limitations and future research

Despite the positive results, the existing study is associated with several limitations that should be considered; although simulations and HIL testing can be useful in controlled experiments, they may not match the complexity of real driving conditions or the dynamism of fully V2X networks. The next important step is radical validation in actual real-world implementations, which should include a broader range and scope of operating environments. Even though many existing experiments are conducted in urban, tunnel, and road situations, the platform has not yet tested in extreme weather conditions (e.g., heavy rain, gale-force winds) or coordinated multi-vehicle leaking cases, which are a key priority in future research.

An important architectural choice of the VR-H2Safe is the strategic separation between high-fidelity modeling as well as real-time execution. Running on-board the full LES simulations to predict the hazards in real-time is not possible, as our results demonstrate (Table 6); the platform instead runs the computationally expensive models offline to produce rich, physically realistic data, which are then trained to produce lightweight and efficient models (e.g., TinyML, PPO agents) that can run on edge hardware in real time. The stated model-baking approach is fundamental to providing both high accuracy and performance, which is the gap between complicated physics and operational limits.

The future development will be multi-faceted, taking a further step towards closing the gap towards real-time hydrogen dispersion prediction. Strategies include hybrid LES/RANS techniques, selective LES in the critical regions, and efficient RANS modeling in other areas are some of the strategies. Interpolation methods, besides pre-computed libraries of LES, provide a third avenue of quick estimates, whereas physics-informed neural networks and other surrogate models of ML could approximate LES results at a fraction of the computational cost; adaptive mesh refinement methods will also be developed to be able to distribute resources dynamically.

Aside from computational efficiency, integration with self-driving technology is likely to provide a marked safety improvement; bringing the H2-LEAK protocol to decisions by autonomous vehicles might help in the prevention of hazards. Overall, the broader potential applications of VR-H2Safe in a variety of hydrogen transportation-related contexts, such as vehicles (i.e., cars/trucks), refueling stations, as well as industrial facilities, are an important area for future study.

Conclusion

VR-H2Safe establishes a unified cyber-physical methodology to address the critical safety and efficiency challenges of hydrogen vehicles. By integrating high-fidelity LES turbulence modeling with real-time AI, the platform achieves a 92% spatio-temporal correlation in leak prediction compared to LES-CFD benchmarks, offering a low-cost alternative for engineering validation. In addition, the novel H2-LEAK protocol enhances standard V2X communication with hydrogen-specific risk vectors and the security of the blockchain protocol, preventing 95% of spoofing attacks and reducing thermal violations by 58%, and a physics-based reinforcement learning agent optimizes the thermal management of fuel cells. These contributions together provide a ‘Virtual Homologation’ workflow that can decrease the engineering validation process of ISO 15,869 compliance from 14 to 10 weeks. Future research will aim to tackle the extreme environmental conditions (such as multiphase rain flows) and expand the blockchain consensus for high-density scalability.

Future work

Future work will focus on the large-scale real-world experimentations in diverse settings to confirm the effectiveness of VR-H2Safe in a realistic driving scenario. The major technical priorities are to optimize edge-cloud partitioning to allow almost real-time hydrogen dispersion modeling and development of hybrid LES/RANS methods to trade off accuracy and computational efficiency. Interoperability in future V2X networks will be facilitated by the standardization of the H2-LEAK protocol by SAE and IEEE and will collaborate with other organizations such as the 5G Automotive Association (5GAA).

By adding H2-LEAK safety warnings to the autonomous driving decisions, it will be possible to react in advance to an emergency and to implement automatic rerouting and safe stopping procedures. Reinforcement learning strategies will be extended to work well under uncertainty by means of meta-learning techniques, which will allow the agent to quickly adapt its control and reward weights to different driving cycles without having to retrain from scratch. The applicability of the application platform will also be extended to the aviation and maritime environment by adapting the simulation models to altitude-sensitive and turbulence-driven leakage behavior.

Lightweight blockchain consensus and privacy protection, besides energy-efficient V2X security mechanisms, will also be researched; surrogates to ML will be created to substitute the expensive LES computations at the vehicle edge, allowing real-time hazard prediction. Collectively, these developments should see VR-H2Safe become a core technology in hydrogen mobility, as well as speed up the adoption of safe and scalable zero-emission transport.
