Introduction

Oxygen is essential for maintaining proper bodily functions and is closely regulated within the body. It is a critical parameter in assessing respiratory function and guiding treatment decisions. The normal oxygen saturation in a healthy individual is ≥94%[1]. Hypoxemia defined as low oxygen saturation in blood is a physiological sign of respiratory failure[2]. Hypoxemic patients, who have lower than normal oxygen levels in their blood, require precise monitoring to ensure appropriate oxygen therapy[3,4]. Patients with acute and chronic illness that includes chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), pneumonia, bronchiectasis, heart failure, septicemia, and other severe systemic illness often present with hypoxemia requiring supplemental oxygen to maintain oxygen saturation[5–7]. Different disease conditions have different target oxygen saturation. In COPD, oxygen is titrated to achieve target oxygen saturation of 88–92%, while target oxygen saturation range for other medical conditions like asthma, pneumonia, and acute coronary syndrome is 92–96%[8–11].

Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis and pulse oximetry (SpO2) are two widely used methods to assess oxygen saturation of hemoglobin in blood.

ABG analysis is a gold standard and frequently used to evaluate the oxygenation, ventilation, and acid–base status of critically unwell individuals. In critically ill patients, ABG analysis is crucial for making the correct diagnosis and selecting the best course of treatment[12–14]. ABG analysis provides accurate data regarding the oxygenation saturation of hemoglobin in blood (SaO2) but is an invasive procedure and is not suitable for continuous monitoring[15].

A pulse oximeter is a non-invasive device used to monitor oxygen saturation in critically ill patients[16]. It is a small handy pocket device, which measures blood oxygen when placed onto a fingertip, or other peripheries like earlobe or foot tip. It is commonly used by health care providers to check for vital signs and to evaluate the patients for further management[1]. Use of pulse oximeter helps in early determination of weaning a patient from mechanical ventilation and extubation, and it also reduces the frequency of ABG for estimation of oxygen saturation[17,18]. It has advantages of being convenient to use, non-invasive, and accurate to certain extent in determination of oxygen saturation with advantage of being used for continuous monitoring[18–20].

Several studies have compared the accuracy of pulse oximetry with ABG analysis. A study conducted by Elhossieny et al on 70 patients showed that SpO2 is accurate relative to SaO2 in ABGs as the difference between SpO2 and SaO2 was less than ±3% and SaO2 could be reliably predicted from pulse oximeter saturation, and it remains a valuable tool in the intensive care patients[13]. In developing countries like Nepal where ABG machines are not available in every health care facility, this study aims to compare oxygen saturation by ABG and pulse oximeter and look for the reliability of pulse oximeter as an alternative to ABG.

Method

This was a prospective cross-sectional observational study conducted in Medical Intensive Care Unit (MICU) of Chitwan Medical College Teaching Hospital (CMCTH), Nepal. Data collection was done over a span of 3 months from April 2025 till June 2025 after the study proposal was approved by the Institutional Review Committee of CMCTH. All the patients admitted in MICU, requiring supplemental oxygen therapy, were included in the study.

After patients were admitted in MICU, informed consent was obtained for including patients’ data in the study. Data were collected in a structured proforma. All patients admitted to the MICU have routine monitoring of vitals and ABG sampling is taken when clinically indicated as part of routine clinical management. No investigations were done solely for the purpose of the study. Paired recording of SpO2 and SaO2 were taken simultaneously.

The SpO2 value on the monitor at the time when the blood was seen to enter the ABG collection syringe was noted for simultaneous reading and the sample was immediately analyzed using blood gas analyzer, after removing air bubbles. The blood gas analyzer was properly calibrated. The SpO2 recordings were taken using the monitor with adult SpO2 sensor probes. The monitors were calibrated by the biomedical department of our institution. Data were then entered in IBM SPSS for analysis. Statistical analysis

The collected data were entered into IBM SPSS for statistical analysis. Categorical variables were expressed in percentages. Numeric variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation for normally distributed variables and median and interquartile range for non-normally distributed variables. Discussion

Accurate assessment of oxygen saturation is vital for managing critically ill patients. Inaccurate readings may lead to inappropriate clinical decisions, potentially compromising patient outcomes. ABG analysis and pulse oximetry (SpO2) are two widely used methods to assess oxygen saturation of hemoglobin in blood. ABG analysis has always been gold standard to obtain information on oxygenation, ventilation and acid–base status of critically ill patients. It is an invasive procedure that is painful to a patient with repeated punctures, has chances of infection, and is not a continuous monitoring process. Pulse oximeter is noninvasive, patient friendly, and painless, and it can be used for continuous monitoring of the patient’s oxygen saturation, enabling immediate attempts to correct the hypoxemia.

Pulse oximeters are commonly used in healthcare settings in resource-limited country like Nepal due to their non-invasive nature and cost-effectiveness for the measurement of oxygen saturation (SpO2). However, limited research has been conducted to validate their accuracy against SaO2 measurements in the local population. This study aimed to fill this gap by providing evidence on the reliability of SpO2 readings in a Nepalese tertiary care setting, ultimately contributing to better clinical decision-making and patient care.

The demographic characteristics of the patient revealed median age of the patients was 73.50 (IQR 16) years, with higher male predominance (51.3%), which was similar to the study done by Munoz et al[22], whereas the study done by Wilson et al[23] and Abraham et al[23,24] had lower mean age of patient compared to our study. The higher mean age in our study was due the disease process, as COPD was the most common cause of hypoxemia in our patients.

Most of the hypoxemic patients 132 (86.84%) admitted to MICU had shortness of breath as the most prominent symptom. The other common symptoms were cough in 117 (76.97%), fever in 74 (48.68%), and pedal edema in 19 (12.5%) cases. The study done by Tiwari et al[25] in a tertiary center in India had similar findings.

During the study period, the most common disease leading to MICU admission with hypoxemia was acute exacerbation of COPD in 78 (51.32%), which was more than half of the number of cases followed by pneumonia in 44 (28.94%), acute exacerbation of post tubercular bronchiectasis in 10 (6.58%), chronic kidney disease under maintenance hemodialysis in 8 (5.26%), acute decompensated heart failure in 5 (3.29%), and others in 7 (4.61%) and the findings were similar to the study done by Tiwari et al[25].

The most common comorbidities in the study were COPD 88 (57.89%), hypertension 53 (34.87%), diabetes mellitus 27 (11.76%), hypothyroidism 14 (9.21%), history of pulmonary tuberculosis 13 (8.55%), valvular heart disease 12 (7.89%), chronic kidney disease 12 (7.89%), dilated cardiomyopathy 8 (5.26%), and others 5 (3.29%).

Various modes of respiratory support were provided to the patients after they were admitted to MICU. The most common mode of respiratory support was in the form of nasal prongs in 90 (59.21%) patients followed by non-invasive ventilation in 23(15.13) patients, venturi mask in 15 (9.86%) patients, high-flow nasal canula in 14 (9.21%) patients and invasive mechanical ventilation in 10 (6.57%) patients, similar to the study done by Tiwari et al[25].

In this study, a strong correlation was found between SaO2 and SpO2 (r = 0.880, P-value < 0.01). However, the utility of pulse oximeter oxygen saturation (SpO2) to estimate arterial oxygen saturation (SaO2) in critically ill patients has yielded mixed results in various medical literatures. Some studies have found SpO2 underestimates SaO2[16,26], while other studies have shown opposite results[27]. A meta-analysis was done by Jensen et al[28] on monitoring of arterial oxygen saturation by pulse oximetry in adult population. It included 74 studies from 1976 to 1994 that met the inclusion criteria. The study showed that pulse oximeters were found to be accurate within 2% in the range of 70–100% SaO2.

In our study, median SaO2 was 93.8 (IQR 3.175) and median was SpO2 93 (IQR 3), and study showed that the difference between SaO2 and SpO2 was less than 1%. Another similar study was conducted by Elhossieny et al[13] on accuracy of pulse oximetry in comparison with ABG. The study was conducted on 70 patients admitted in emergency and surgical ICU for a period of 1 year. This study shows highly significant correlation between hypoxia and SpO2. The study concluded that SpO2 is accurate relative to SaO2 in ABG as the difference between SpO2 and SaO2 was less than ±3% and SaO2 could be reliably predicted from pulse oximeter saturation. In a study conducted by Rauniyar et al[29], oxygen saturation was measured by ABG and pulse oximetry in ICU patients. The mean SaO2 and SpO2 values were 93.22% and 92.85%, respectively. The study suggests that pulse oximetry is highly accurate for measuring oxygen saturation (SpO2 ≥ 90%) and can replace ABG measurements. Similarly, another study was conducted by Razi et al[30] on “Comparison of Arterial Oxygen Saturation Measured Both by Pulse Oximeter and Arterial Blood Gas Analyzer in Hypoxemic and Non-hypoxemic Pulmonary Diseases.” The hypoxemic and non-hypoxemic patients had good correlation between Sp02 and SaO2 (in hypoxemic group: r = 0.865, P < 0.001; in non-hypoxemic group: r = 0.95, P < 0.001). This study also showed that in patients with pulmonary diseases with Sp02 > 80%, pulse oximetry has high accuracy in estimating SaO2 and may be used instead of ABG.

Conclusion

Our study concluded that a strong correlation was found between SaO2 and SpO2 (r = 0.880, P-value < 0.01) in patients with hypoxemic respiratory failure requiring supplemental oxygen, and pulse oximeter can be used as a reliable tool for the estimation of oxygen saturation.

The strength of our study was that the SpO2 value on the monitor was recorded at the time when the blood was seen to enter the ABG collection syringe and the sample was immediately analyzed using blood gas analyzer. So, there was no time difference between the readings of ABG and pulse oximeter. Similarly, all patients in the study population were hypoxemic patients requiring supplemental oxygen therapy, and study population was adequate.

That said, our study also had few limitations. It was a single center study, and the outcome of the study cannot be generalized. Sample size may limit the strength of the conclusions. Potential confounding factors (such as comorbidities, hemodynamic status, and finger abnormalities) could influence pulse oximetry readings. Very severe cases were also excluded from the study due to the difficulty for consent from the patient party.
