Multidimensional Assessment of Nutritional Status Among Hospitalized Older Adults With Pneumonia at a Tertiary Hospital in Ghana: A Descriptive Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Theresa Andoh, Justina S. Owusu, Husein Mohammed, Eliza M. Kwesi‐Maliepaard, Augustina Frimpong, Beverly Egyir, Nicholas T.K.D. Dayie, Nana Yaa K. Frimpong, Alfred Toppar, Kwadwo A. Kusi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionMalnutrition is a common but often overlooked problem among hospitalised older adults, particularly those admitted with acute infections such as pneumonia. Pneumonia can worsen nutritional status through inflammation, fever, poor appetite, breathlessness, fatigue, and reduced food intake, while malnutrition can, in turn, weaken immunity, reduce respiratory muscle strength, and delay recovery. This study assessed the spectrum of nutritional status among hospitalised older adults with pneumonia at the 37 Military Hospital in Accra, Ghana.MethodsA descriptive cross‐sectional study was conducted among 74 hospitalised adults aged 60 years and above with a confirmed diagnosis of pneumonia. Data were collected on socio‐demographic and health‐related characteristics, dietary intake, anthropometry, and haemoglobin status. Nutritional status was assessed using Body Mass Index (BMI), the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA), mid‐upper arm circumference (MUAC), calf circumference, dietary diversity score, and haemoglobin concentration. Associations between nutritional indicators were examined using chi‐square and Monte Carlo exact tests, and multivariable prevalence‐ratio regression was used to identify factors independently associated with MNA‐defined malnutrition and BMI‐defined underweight.ResultsParticipants had a mean age of 72.3 ± 8.6 years, with 38 males and 36 females. Based on BMI, 66.2% were underweight, 31.1% had a healthy weight, and 2.7% were overweight. The MNA classified 39.2% of participants as malnourished, 50.0% were at risk of malnutrition, and only 10.8% as well nourished. Among participants with haemoglobin data, 77.5% were anaemic. MUAC classified 9.5% as underweight and 32.4% as at risk, while 31.1% had a low calf circumference; 56.8% had inadequate dietary diversity. MUAC and calf circumference were significantly associated with both MNA classification (p = 0.0047 and p = 0.0309) and BMI category (p = 0.010 and p = 0.035), whereas MNA classification was not significantly associated with BMI category (p = 0.142). In multivariable analysis, each 5‐year increase in age was associated with a 21% higher prevalence of MNA‐defined malnutrition (aPR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.01–1.46, p = 0.040); no factor was independently associated with BMI‐defined underweight.ConclusionMalnutrition was highly prevalent and manifested in multiple, only partly overlapping forms among hospitalised older adults with pneumonia. Because BMI, MNA, MUAC, calf circumference, and dietary diversity captured related but distinct dimensions of nutritional risk, reliance on a single indicator particularly BMI alone may underestimate the true burden of malnutrition. Routine screening with multiple, low‐cost indicators should be integrated into pneumonia care for older adults to support early detection, targeted intervention, and improved recovery.

Introduction

Population ageing is an immediate public health reality that is reshaping health systems globally. As people live longer, there is a growing need to ensure that later life is accompanied by good health, independence, and dignity. The World Health Organization's Decade of Healthy Ageing emphasises the need for health systems that preserve functional ability and respond to the complex needs of older adults [1]. In Ghana, the 2021 Population and Housing Census shows a steady increase in the older population, making geriatric health and nutrition increasingly important in national health planning [2]. As a result, hospitals are likely to manage older adults whose recovery is shaped not only by acute illness, but also by nutritional, functional, and social factors.

Ageing is often associated with reduced appetite, altered taste and smell, poor dentition, impaired chewing or swallowing, limited mobility, chronic disease, polypharmacy, loneliness, and financial dependence. These factors can reduce food intake and dietary diversity, increasing the risk of undernutrition [3]. During acute illness, inflammation and increased metabolic demands may further worsen nutritional status, contributing to weight loss, muscle wasting, reduced strength, micronutrient deficiencies, and poor functional recovery [4]. These consequences can affect immunity, wound healing, respiratory muscle function, length of hospital stay, readmission risk, and quality of life [5, 6]. Malnutrition is a significant concern among the elderly in the community, with prevalence rates between 1.3% and 47.8% [7]. Research indicates that malnutrition is more prevalent in developing countries than in developed countries. The prevalence of malnutrition in hospitalized older adults ranges from 23% to 62%, whereas among nursing home residents, it can reach up to 85% [8, 9].

Malnutrition among hospitalised older adults is a major but often under‐recognised problem [10]. In busy clinical settings, nutritional status may be overlooked unless deliberate screening is conducted. This is concerning because malnutrition may exist before admission, worsen during hospitalisation, and persist after discharge if not identified and managed. Recent evidence shows that poor nutritional status among older adults with community‐acquired pneumonia is associated with functional decline, complications, institutionalisation, and mortality [11, 12, 13, 14]. Nutritional assessment should therefore be considered an essential component of care for older adults with acute infections.

Pneumonia is particularly relevant because it has a bidirectional relationship with malnutrition. Pneumonia may worsen nutritional status through fever, inflammation, reduced appetite, breathlessness, fatigue, and difficulty eating [14]. Conversely, malnutrition may increase susceptibility to infection by weakening immune function, reducing respiratory muscle strength, and impairing recovery. This interaction can create a cycle in which infection accelerates nutritional decline, while poor nutrition delays clinical recovery [15]. Because malnutrition is multidimensional, no single measure fully captures its burden in older adults. Body Mass Index provides useful information on weight status but may not reflect recent weight loss, muscle depletion, or functional vulnerability. The Mini Nutritional Assessment offers a broader assessment of nutritional risk, while mid‐upper arm circumference, calf circumference, dietary diversity, and haemoglobin status provide additional information on body reserves, muscle mass, diet quality, anaemia, and possible micronutrient deficiency or inflammation [16].

Despite growing attention to healthy ageing in Ghana, evidence on the nutritional status of hospitalised older adults remains limited, particularly among those with pneumonia. This study therefore sought to describe the prevalence and pattern of nutritional status using multiple nutritional indicators, among hospitalized adults aged 60 years and above with pneumonia at the 37 Military Hospital in Accra, Ghana. Using multiple indicators including the Body Mass Index, Mini Nutritional Assessment, Mid Upper Arm Circumference, calf circumference, dietary diversity, and haemoglobin status, the study provides a broader picture of nutritional vulnerability and contributes evidence to guide nutrition screening and intervention in routine hospital care.

Methods

Study Site

This study was carried out at the Public Health Unit of the 37 Military Hospital located in the Greater Accra region of Ghana. This hospital is the premier military healthcare facility in the country, which serves as a referral and tertiary hospital, it houses a Public Health Unit that is equipped to manage infectious diseases and bacterial infections with a high level of medical proficiency.

Study Design and Participants Selection

This study utilized a descriptive cross‐sectional, which allows for a simultaneous evaluation of both outcome and exposure variables. The research was conducted among a sample of 74 patients, all of whom were 60 years of age or older and had been diagnosed with pneumonia.

Study Population, Sample Size Determination and Sampling Technique

Study Population

The study population comprised older adults aged 60 years and above who were diagnosed and confirmed with pneumonia for less than 72 h and who were on admission at the 37 Military Hospital. To ensure the validity of the study results, participants who showed signs of lung malignancies or upper respiratory tract infections at the time of data collection were excluded. Potential participants were informed about the details and purpose of the study and were then asked for their written or verbal informed consent. Once consent was obtained, participants were recruited from the hospital after undergoing a rapid test for pneumonia infection to confirm their diagnosis.

Sample Size Determination

The prevalence of pneumonia carriage among the elderly in Ghana was not previously established, but the overall incidence of influenza and pneumonia in the country is recorded as 9.29% [17]. To determine the sample size for this study, the formula put forth by [18] was utilized, which states that n = Z P (1−P) ÷ d^2. This calculation resulted in a sample size of 74 participants, all of whom were recruited from the 37 Military Hospital and diagnosed with pneumonia through rapid testing. The selected patients were then provided with informed consent before being included in the study.

Population Sampling

A convenience sampling approach was used to recruit the 74 participants from the 37 Military Hospital in the Greater Accra region of Ghana. Eligibility required that a participant be 60 years or older, have a confirmed diagnosis of pneumonia, and have been admitted to the hospital for less than 72 h.

Data Collection

A semi‐structured questionnaire was pre‐tested among five participants at the Mother Love clinic to assess its validity, reliability, and clarity before use in the main study. Data were collected using structured questionnaires covering socio‐demographic characteristics and the full Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA‐full); interviews were conducted in the respondent's preferred language at the hospital.

Anthropometric Assessment

A calibrated electronic scale was used to measure each participant's weight to the nearest 0.1 kg. Height was measured using an adult stadiometer for participants who could stand; recumbent length was used for participants who could not stand. Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). BMI was categorised using the older‐adult cut‐offs of Winter et al. [19], shown in Table 1, selected because they better account for unintentional weight loss, lean‐mass preservation, and comorbidity in this age group than conventional adult cut‐offs.

Mid‐Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC)

MUAC was measured with a non‐stretchable tape at the midpoint between the acromion process and the olecranon on the left arm, with the arm extended, and recorded in centimetres.

Calf Circumference

Calf circumference was measured at the widest point of the calf using a non‐stretchable tape.

Dietary Assessment

A single 24‐h dietary recall was used to assess dietary intake, chosen to reduce recall bias associated with longer recall periods. Participants who consumed foods from fewer than five food groups were classified as having inadequate dietary diversity, whereas those who consumed foods from five or more food groups were classified as having adequate dietary diversity.

Biochemical Assessment

Haemoglobin was measured using a haemoglobin meter (URIT‐12); where recent laboratory haemoglobin results were available, these values (in g/dL) were recorded instead. Anaemia was defined using sex‐specific World Health Organization cut‐offs of < 13.0 g/dL for males and < 12.0 g/dL for females; participants at or above these thresholds were classified as non‐anaemic.

Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA)

Nutritional status was additionally assessed using the MNA®, a validated screening tool for older adults comprising 18 items across four domains: anthropometry, general health, dietary habits, and self‐perceived health and nutrition. Using the scoring system of Vellas et al. (2006), an MNA score of 24 or higher indicates a normal nutritional status, a score of 17–23.5 indicates risk of malnutrition, and a score below 17 indicates malnutrition.

Data Analysis

All analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26.0. Continuous variables were first assessed for approximate normality; normally distributed continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and were compared between sexes using independent‐samples t tests. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies with percentages, accompanied by the numerator and denominator from which each percentage was derived, and were compared using the chi‐square test, where expected cell counts were small (any expected cell count < 5), the Monte Carlo exact test based on the Pearson chi‐square statistic. Binomial 95% confidence intervals are reported for key prevalence estimates. Associations between the anthropometric and dietary indicators of malnutrition (MUAC, calf circumference, BMI category, dietary diversity) and both the MNA classification and BMI category were pre‐specified analyses defined at the design stage of the study. Multivariable prevalence‐ratio regression (Poisson regression with robust standard errors) was subsequently used, as an exploratory analysis, to identify factors independently associated with MNA‐defined malnutrition and with BMI‐defined underweight; models were adjusted for age, sex, multi‐morbidity, and education, selected a priori based on their established relevance to nutritional status in older adults. All statistical tests were 2‐sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Consistent with recommended reporting conventions, p values are reported as p ≥ 0.01, as “p < 0.001” when below 0.001.

Ethical Issues

The study was granted ethical approval by the institutional review board of the Noguchi Memorial Institute of Medical Research (NMIMR‐ IRB 006/21‐22) and the Ethics and Protocol Review Committee of the 37 Military Hospital (37MH‐ IRB/MAS/IPN/538/21). Data collection took place in at the 37 Military hospital, Accra Ghana from June 2021 to September 2021. Written or verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment, after they had been informed of the purpose, procedures, risks, and benefits of the study.

Malnutrition Indicators and Their Association With the Body Mass Index

MUAC and calf circumference were significantly associated with BMI classification (Monte Carlo exact p = 0.01 and p = 0.04, respectively). In contrast, MNA classification was not significantly associated with BMI category (p = 0.14). Among participants classified as malnourished by MNA, 22/29 (75.9%) were underweight by BMI, compared with 24/37 (64.9%) of those at risk and 3/8 (37.5%) of those well‐nourished.

Multivariable Analysis of Factors Associated With MNA‐Defined Malnutrition and BMI‐Defined Underweight Among Hospitalised Older Adults With Pneumonia

After adjustment for sex, multi‐morbidity, and education, each 5‐year increase in age was associated with a 21% higher prevalence of MNA‐defined malnutrition (aPR 1.21, 95% CI 1.01–1.46; p= 0.04). Sex, multi‐morbidity, and education were not significantly associated with MNA‐defined malnutrition. None of the examined predictors was significantly associated with BMI‐defined underweight, although multi‐morbidity showed a non‐significant positive association (aPR 1.30, 95% CI 0.94–1.78; p= 0.11). Discussion

This study demonstrates a substantial and multidimensional burden of nutritional vulnerability among hospitalised older adults with pneumonia in Ghana. Based on the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) classification, 39.2% of participants were malnourished, 50.0% were at risk of malnutrition, and only 10.8% were well nourished. In addition, 66.2% had a study‐defined low body mass index (BMI), 31.1% had a reduced calf circumference, 41.9% were underweight or at risk according to mid‐upper arm circumference (MUAC), 56.8% had inadequate dietary diversity, and 77.5% of participants with available haemoglobin measurements were anaemic. Collectively, these findings indicate that nutritional vulnerability in this population extended beyond low body weight to encompass reduced peripheral body reserves, poor dietary variety, anaemia, and broader MNA‐defined nutritional impairment.

The principal contribution of this study therefore lies not simply in estimating the prevalence of malnutrition, but in comparing several low‐cost nutritional indicators and identifying the factors independently associated with malnutrition in a resource‐constrained hospital setting. The combined prevalence of MNA‐defined malnutrition and nutritional risk observed here is comparable to recent findings from a hospital‐based study in Malawi, in which 40.3% of older adults were malnourished and 39.7% were at risk according to the MNA‐Short Form [20].

Socio‐Demographic Characteristics

The socio‐demographic profile of the participants provides important context for interpreting these findings. Seventy‐four older adults were assessed, with a mean age of 72.28 years and a nearly equal distribution of males and females; slightly more than half of participants were male. This may reflect the higher burden or severity of pneumonia often reported among older men, a pattern that has been linked to life‐course exposures such as smoking, alcohol use, occupational risk, and underlying cardiopulmonary disease [13, 14]. The age distribution also revealed notable sex differences: most male participants were aged 60–74 years, whereas a larger proportion of female participants were aged 75 years and above. This pattern is consistent with the generally longer life expectancy of women, although prolonged survival may also heighten exposure to frailty, widowhood, chronic illness, dependency, and functional decline, each of which can compromise food intake and nutritional status. Such vulnerabilities may become more pronounced during acute illness, when appetite, mobility, and functional reserve are further reduced (Bellanti et al., 2022b [6, 8]).

Health‐Related Characteristics and Multi‐Morbidity

Chronic disease was common in this population. Hypertension affected 62.2% of participants, diabetes mellitus 45.9%, stroke 18.9%, and coronary heart disease 5.4%, and almost half (48.6%) had multi‐morbidity, defined as at least two of the four assessed conditions. Nevertheless, multi‐morbidity was not independently associated with MNA‐defined malnutrition or study‐defined low BMI after adjustment for age, sex, and education. This finding is consistent with contemporary geriatric evidence describing malnutrition as a multifactorial syndrome that arises from the interaction of disease burden, inflammation, reduced intake, impaired function, medication effects, and social vulnerability, rather than from the number of diagnoses alone (Cruz‐Jentoft & Volkert, 2025).

Anaemia was similarly common, affecting 77.5% of participants. In acutely ill older adults, anaemia may reflect inflammation, chronic disease, iron or other micronutrient deficiencies, renal impairment, blood loss, or a combination of these factors; its underlying cause cannot be determined from the present cross‐sectional data. Nonetheless, the coexistence of anaemia and nutritional impairment observed in this study is clinically relevant, as a recent study of older adults with community‐acquired pneumonia identified low haemoglobin as an independent correlate of malnutrition, alongside older age and neurological disease [14].

Nutritional Status and Comparison of Assessment Indicators

The MNA results showed that nearly nine in ten participants were either malnourished or at risk of malnutrition, a burden that is clinically important given that malnutrition in older adults is associated with impaired immunity, reduced muscle function, delayed recovery, increased readmission, functional decline, and mortality. Among older adults hospitalised with community‐acquired pneumonia, recent studies have similarly linked malnutrition with longer hospital stays, higher readmission, institutionalisation, functional deterioration, and mortality [11, 14].

The prevalence of study‐defined low BMI was correspondingly high, at 66.2%. The threshold used in this study, BMI ≤ 24 kg/m2, was set substantially above the conventional adult underweight threshold, reflecting evidence from a meta‐analysis by Winter et al., [19] reporting increased mortality risk among older adults with a BMI below approximately 23 kg/m2. This higher threshold, together with the influence of acute illness, recent weight loss, hydration status, and difficulty obtaining standing height, likely contributed to the elevated prevalence observed. This figure also exceeds the 48% undernutrition previously reported among community‐dwelling older adults in Northern Ghana Aganiba et al., [21], plausibly because the present study involved hospitalised patients with acute pneumonia, in whom illness‐related inflammation, breathlessness, fatigue, poor appetite, and reduced food intake may further compound nutritional risk.

By comparison, the MNA identified 39.2% of participants as malnourished and 50.0% as at risk of malnutrition. The divergence between the BMI‐ and MNA‐based estimates illustrates that these two measures capture related but non‐identical dimensions of nutritional status. BMI primarily reflects body size and does not directly assess recent weight loss, reduced food intake, mobility, psychological stress, cognition, or self‐perceived health, whereas the MNA incorporates several of these domains and may therefore identify nutritional vulnerability before marked weight loss occurs. Consistent with this distinction, no statistically significant association was found between MNA classification and BMI category using the Monte Carlo exact test, reinforcing the conclusion that the burden of malnutrition should not be inferred from BMI alone and underscoring the value of multidimensional nutritional assessment.

In contrast, MUAC and calf circumference were both significantly associated with MNA classification and BMI category. All participants classified as underweight by MUAC were also malnourished according to the MNA classification, and those with reduced calf circumference were more frequently classified as malnourished. These findings align with recent validation studies showing that calf circumference can serve as a practical surrogate indicator of reduced muscle mass in hospitalised older adults, and that both MUAC and calf circumference can usefully contribute to hospital malnutrition assessment when more advanced body‐composition methods are unavailable [22, 23].

Inadequate dietary diversity was identified in 56.8% of participants, indicating that limited food‐group variety constituted a further important dimension of nutritional vulnerability in this population. Breathlessness, fatigue, poor appetite, hospital food availability, and treatment‐related interruptions may all have reduced dietary intake during admission. This finding is consistent with recent African evidence demonstrating a high burden of low diet quality and food insecurity among older adults at hospital admission, and with longitudinal evidence linking persistently low dietary diversity to frailty in later life [20, 24].

Factors Associated With MNA‐Defined Malnutrition and BMI‐Defined Underweight Among Hospitalised Older Adults With Pneumonia

In multivariable analysis, age was the only variable independently associated with MNA‐defined malnutrition: each 5‐year increase in age was associated with a 21% higher prevalence of malnutrition after adjustment for sex, multi‐morbidity, and education. This result is consistent with evidence linking increasing age to reduced appetite, sensory impairment, oral‐health problems, dysphagia, functional dependence, loss of muscle mass, and diminished physiological reserve, and corroborates findings by Zheng et al., [14], who similarly identified older age as an independent risk factor for malnutrition among hospitalised older adults with community‐acquired pneumonia. By contrast, none of the examined predictors was significantly associated with BMI‐defined low weight. This divergence between the two sets of regression findings further reinforces the distinction between MNA‐defined malnutrition and BMI classification and suggests that age may influence several MNA domains including intake, mobility, neuropsychological status, recent stress, and self‐rated health without producing an equivalent change in BMI.

Clinical Implications

Current findings support the routine nutritional screening of older adults with pneumonia soon after hospital admission. A practical pathway would begin with a validated screening tool designed for older adults, followed by comprehensive nutritional assessment for those who screen positive. The MNA‐Short Form may be well suited to rapid initial screening, while the full MNA can provide a more detailed assessment when time and patient condition permit. MUAC and calf circumference may serve as useful adjunctive bedside measures, particularly when accurate weight or height cannot be obtained, whereas BMI should not be relied upon as the sole indicator, given its potential to miss recent weight loss, reduced intake, muscle depletion, and functional vulnerability.

Where feasible, patients who screen positive should undergo further assessment of recent weight change, food intake, muscle mass or strength, swallowing function, functional status, anaemia, food security, and barriers to eating. The Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM) framework may then be applied to support diagnosis, while referral to a dietitian or nutrition professional can guide individualised food fortification, feeding assistance, oral nutritional supplementation, and discharge planning [8, 25].

Strengths and Limitations

A major strength of this study was its use of several complementary nutritional indicators, including the MNA, BMI, MUAC, calf circumference, dietary diversity, and haemoglobin concentration. The analysis further incorporated sex‐specific denominators, anaemia cut‐offs, Monte Carlo exact tests for sparse contingency tables, and multivariable prevalence‐ratio models, providing greater methodological and clinical depth than descriptive prevalence estimates alone.

Several limitations should nonetheless be acknowledged. The cross‐sectional design precludes determination of the causal relationships among pneumonia, reduced intake, anaemia, low body reserves, and malnutrition. The convenience sample, small sample size, single‐centre setting, and absence of a comparison group limit representativeness and generalisability. The study did not assess frailty, functional dependence, cognition, depression, polypharmacy, smoking, alcohol consumption, food insecurity, swallowing impairment, or several other clinically important chronic conditions, so residual confounding is likely. The single 24‐h dietary recall may have been affected by recall error and acute changes in intake, while anthropometric measurements may have been influenced by acute illness, hydration status, oedema, posture, or difficulty measuring height in participants unable to stand. Finally, the study did not examine length of stay, complications, functional decline, readmission, or mortality; consequently, the findings identify nutritional vulnerability and its associated factors but do not establish prognostic value or demonstrate that nutritional impairment caused adverse clinical outcomes.

Future research should draw on larger, multicentre, prospectively recruited cohorts that incorporate comprehensive geriatric variables, standardised malnutrition diagnosis, disease severity, longitudinal weight and intake data, and clinically relevant outcomes. Including a comparison group of hospitalised older adults without pneumonia would also help determine whether the observed nutritional burden is specific to pneumonia or reflects the broader vulnerability of older medical inpatients more generally.

Conclusion

Hospitalised older adults with pneumonia at the 37 Military Hospital exhibited a high and multidimensional burden of nutritional vulnerability. Based on the MNA classification, 39.2% were malnourished and 50.0% were at risk of malnutrition, a picture corroborated by study‐defined low BMI, reduced MUAC and calf circumference, inadequate dietary diversity, and a high prevalence of anaemia. Rather than converging on a single picture of risk, these indicators captured related but distinct dimensions of nutritional status, demonstrating that BMI alone does not adequately characterise nutritional vulnerability in older hospitalised adults.

Increasing age was independently associated with MNA‐defined malnutrition, whereas sex, education, and the restricted multi‐morbidity measure were not independently associated with either MNA‐defined malnutrition or BMI‐defined low weight. MUAC and calf circumference showed concordance with broader nutritional classifications and may provide useful bedside information, although their inclusion within the full MNA limits their interpretation as fully independent validation measures.

These findings support the implementation of systematic nutritional screening at hospital admission, followed by comprehensive assessment and appropriate nutrition care for patients identified as malnourished or at risk. Nevertheless, given the cross‐sectional design and the absence of clinical outcome data, causal and prognostic conclusions cannot be drawn. Larger prospective studies are therefore required to determine whether these nutritional indicators predict recovery, functional decline, length of stay, readmission, and mortality among older adults hospitalised with pneumonia.
