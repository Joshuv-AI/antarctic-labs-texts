Mediating role of self-efficacy for appropriate medication use in the relationship between medication literacy and medication adherence among patients with atrial fibrillation: a longitudinal study
Authors: Chu-xin Fang, Yan Fang, Xiao-juan Zhou, Li-xiang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to assess the level of medication adherence among patients with atrial fibrillation through a longitudinal follow-up study design. Additionally, it seeks to examine the impact of baseline medication literacy on long-term medication adherence and to investigate the mediating role of self-efficacy for appropriate medication use over time. MethodsA convenience sampling method was employed to select 579 patients with atrial fibrillation from a tertiary first-class hospital in Hefei, Anhui Province, between May 2023 and June 2024. Data collection occurred at three time points: baseline (T1), where general demographic data and a medication literacy questionnaire were administered; at 3 months (T2), where self-efficacy for appropriate medication use was assessed; and at 6 months (T3), where medication adherence was evaluated. The primary endpoints were measured using the medication adherence score, and the longitudinal relationships were evaluated through mediation analysis.Results At time point T3, the medication adherence score for patients with atrial fibrillation was 34.53 ± 14.19 (total score: 60). Mediation analysis revealed that medication literacy at T1 positively predicts self-efficacy for appropriate medication use at T2, which in turn positively predicts medication adherence at T3. Notably, 13.38% of the total effect of T1 medication literacy on T3 medication adherence is mediated through T2 self-efficacy, functioning as a longitudinal intermediary variable.ConclusionThere is a need for improvement in the long-term medication adherence of patients with atrial fibrillation. Early enhancement of medication literacy not only is associated with patients' adherence to medication regimens but also may indirectly be associated with long-term adherence by improving their self-efficacy mid-study.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Medication literacy, Medication adherence, Self-efficacy for appropriate medication use, Mediation effect

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most prevalent form of rapid atrial arrhythmia, markedly elevating the risk of stroke, cognitive impairment, dementia, heart failure, and mortality [1, 2]. The incidence of AF is progressively increasing with the aging population, and its severe complications adversely affect both the long-term prognosis and economic burden on patients [3]. The utilization of antiarrhythmic drugs constitutes a crucial therapeutic approach for managing atrial fibrillation. Nevertheless, the efficacy of these drugs and patient adherence remain suboptimal, resulting in the persistence of AF-related symptoms, hospitalizations, stroke, progression of atrial fibrillation, and AF-mediated cardiomyopathy [4].

While pharmacological management remains a cornerstone of therapy, the landscape of atrial fibrillation (AF) treatment is rapidly evolving to include sophisticated surgical, interventional, and biological strategies. Recent research has highlighted the potential of exosomes and their microRNA cargos as critical biomarkers and therapeutic targets [5, 6], offering new insights into the underlying mechanisms and diagnosis of AF. In the realm of surgical intervention, the Cox-maze IV procedure has demonstrated significant clinical efficacy and safety, even in complex scenarios such as patients with aortic valve calcification [7] or rare anatomical conditions like situs inversus dextrocardia [8], where 3D printing techniques are increasingly utilized for pre-operative planning [9]. Furthermore, recent evidence indicates that first-line cryoablation significantly improves quality of life and reduces serious adverse events compared to traditional antiarrhythmic drug therapy [10]. The integration of electrophysiological mapping during these procedures further optimizes outcomes by predicting long-term AF recurrence [11]. Given that enhancing medication adherence is vital for ensuring patient safety, these clinical advancements underscore the increasing complexity of AF management, which underscores the importance of high levels of medication literacy and self-efficacy in potentially supporting optimal long-term adherence and clinical prognosis.

Research indicates that enhancing an individual’s self-efficacy for appropriate medication use can lead to improved patient compliance and self-management in medication usage [12]. Studies have demonstrated a positive correlation between patients’ medication literacy and medication adherence (P < 0.01) [13]. Furthermore, the implementation of medication literacy programs by some scholars has significantly enhanced patients’ adherence to medication regimens [14]. Another study corroborated the existence of a significant positive correlation between individual medication literacy and self-efficacy for appropriate medication use (β’ =0.386, P < 0.001) [12].

Currently, research has investigated the correlation between medication literacy, self-efficacy in appropriate medication use, and medication adherence; however, the interrelationships among these factors are seldom reported. Consequently, this study examines the mechanisms underlying medication adherence in patients with atrial fibrillation, focusing on medication literacy and self-efficacy in appropriate medication use. The findings aim to offer novel insights for advancing the study of medication adherence in this patient population.

Methods

Study design and objectives

This study employs a prospective longitudinal design to examine the mechanisms underlying medication adherence in patients with atrial fibrillation. The primary objective was to investigate the longitudinal relationship between baseline medication literacy (T1) and long-term medication adherence (T3), while specifically evaluating the mediating role of self-efficacy for appropriate medication use (T2) over a six-month follow-up period.

Participants

Utilizing a convenience sampling approach, patients diagnosed with atrial fibrillation and admitted to the Department of Cardiology at a tertiary hospital in Hefei, Anhui Province, between May 2023 and December 2023, were selected for investigation. This sampling strategy was adopted primarily to ensure the clinical feasibility of the study and to facilitate a high retention rate during the six-month longitudinal follow-up period within a specialized cardiac setting. In this study, medication-related assessments encompassed all pharmacological treatments prescribed for atrial fibrillation management, including anticoagulants, antiarrhythmics, and medications for associated comorbidities. The inclusion criteria for the study participants are as follows: (1) Individuals diagnosed with atrial fibrillation based on electrocardiogram (ECG) findings, as outlined in the 2020 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for the diagnosis and management of atrial fibrillation [15]. (2) Participants must be over 18 years of age. (3) Participants must be conscious and capable of comprehending and completing the questionnaire. (4) Participants must provide informed consent and voluntarily agree to participate in this longitudinal follow-up study. The exclusion criteria include: (1) Presence of complications involving the brain, kidneys, or other vital organs. (2) A history of mental illness or cognitive dysfunction. (3) Concurrent diagnosis of malignant tumors or severe cardiovascular diseases.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their enrollment in the study.

Research instruments

General information questionnaire

The general information questionnaire was developed by the researchers and encompassed variables such as age, gender, educational attainment, and other socio-demographic characteristics. While the majority of the data in this study were collected through the research questionnaire, electronic health records (EHR) were utilized to supplement or verify information that could not be accurately obtained from the participants. Additionally, it collected disease-related information, including the duration of atrial fibrillation, types of atrial fibrillation, and associated complications such as hypertension, diabetes, stroke, and coronary heart disease.

Chinese version of the 12-item medication adherence scale

The 12-item medication adherence scale developed by Ueno et al. [16] was formulated through a comprehensive literature review and interviews with chronic patients and clinicians. Medication adherence is defined as the capacity of patients to take medications accurately and punctually as prescribed by their healthcare provider [16]. It is designed to assess medication adherence among chronic patients, including those with coronary heart disease, stroke, and diabetes. The scale demonstrates a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.780. The Chinese version of the scale, translated and culturally adapted by Zhang et al. [17] in 2020, has been utilized to evaluate patients with atrial fibrillation. This version exhibits a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.802 and a content validity index of 0.950. The Chinese version of the scale comprises 12 items distributed across four dimensions: regular medication (3 items), medication cooperation (3 items), medication willingness (3 items), and medication integration into daily life (3 items). A 5-point Likert scale is employed, ranging from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”), with item 12 being reverse-scored. The total score ranges from 12 to 60, with higher scores indicating greater adherence.

Chinese version of the self-efficacy for appropriate medication use scale (SEAMS)

Self-efficacy in appropriate medication use, defined as patients’ confidence in adhering to prescribed drug regimens, is a critical determinant of sustained, rational medication use long term [18]. The scale, developed by Risser et al. [19], is designed for research and development concerning patients with chronic diseases and was subsequently adapted for Sinicization by Dong Xiaofang et al. [20]. The Chinese version of the SEAMS encompasses two dimensions and 13 items, requiring participants to report their confidence in medication adherence across 13 different scenarios. The scale employs a three-level rating system, where 1 denotes no confidence, 2 signifies a little confidence, and 3 represents great confidence. The total score is the aggregate of the individual item scores, ranging from 13 to 39, with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy for appropriate medication use. According to Dong Xiaofang et al. [20, 21], the Chinese version of the SEAMS demonstrated a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.934 and a test-retest reliability of 0.932.

Chinese version of the medication literacy questionnaire (MLQ)

Medication literacy is defined as the ability of individuals to acquire, accurately comprehend, and judiciously utilize basic drug information—encompassing their understanding of pharmacological knowledge, proficiency in drug administration, and ability to access drug-related information—thereby facilitating appropriate evaluation and decision-making [22]. This scale was originally developed by Maniaci et al. [23] and subsequently adapted for Sinicization by Zheng Feng et al. [24]. The Chinese version of the MLQ comprises nine items, each evaluated using a binary scoring system, where 1 point is awarded for a correct response and 0 points for an incorrect one. Notably, items 7 and 9 are excluded from scoring. The cumulative score ranges from 0 to 7, with higher scores indicating greater medication literacy among patients. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the Chinese version of the scale is 0.850 [24].

Data collection methods

In this study, a multi-point follow-up survey methodology was employed, with data collection conducted by two uniformly trained nurses from the cardiology department. The specific procedure was as follows: (1) Baseline Assessment (T1, upon group entry): During the patient’s hospitalization, the researcher elucidated the purpose and content of the follow-up study, emphasizing the importance of long-term follow-up. Following the acquisition of informed consent, general demographic information and responses to a medication literacy questionnaire were collected. (2) Mid-term Follow-up (T2, three months post-admission): Follow-up was performed via telephone or outpatient visits, utilizing the self-efficacy scale for rational drug use to assess the patient’s confidence in medication management post-discharge. (3) End-point Evaluation (T3, six months post-enrollment): Follow-up was conducted again through telephone or an online questionnaire system to assess the patient’s long-term medication adherence using the Chinese version of the 12-item Medication Adherence Scale.

The questionnaire was typically completed by the patients themselves; for patients who were unable to independently complete the questionnaire, the researcher read the questions to them and recorded their responses on the participant’s behalf once they had fully understood and selected their choice. Upon completion of the questionnaire at each stage, the researcher promptly reviewed it to ensure the longitudinal integrity and accuracy of the data. In accordance with the study protocol, participants with incomplete follow-up data or those who missed assessments at T2 or T3 were excluded from the final analysis to maintain a complete-case dataset (listwise deletion).

Statistical methods

The data were subjected to statistical analysis using SPSS software version 26.0. Univariate analysis was conducted to identify the factors influencing medication adherence among the participants. For this purpose, independent sample t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were employed to compare medication adherence scores across various demographic and clinical variables (as detailed in Table 2), including gender, education level, type of residence, employment status, monthly income, duration of atrial fibrillation, types of atrial fibrillation, the number of medications taken daily, and comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, and stroke. The sample size was determined using Kendall’s rough estimation method [25], which suggests that the sample should be at least 5 to 10 times the number of independent variables. To account for potential attrition during the longitudinal follow-up, the study initially recruited 600 participants. Qualitative data are presented as frequencies and percentages, while quantitative data following a normal distribution are expressed as mean and standard deviation. Pearson correlation analysis was employed to examine the relationships between the total scores of the three scales. Additionally, the mediating effect of self-efficacy in appropriate medication use on the relationship between medication literacy and medication adherence among patients with atrial fibrillation was analyzed using Hayes et al.‘s Model 4 within the SPSS macro program PROCESS version 3.5 [26].

A mediation model comprising medication literacy (T1), self-efficacy for appropriate medication use (T2), and medication adherence (T3) was developed by the authors. Initially, the scores for these three variables were standardized. Mediation among the variables was assessed using stepwise regression and the bias-corrected bootstrap method within the PROCESS plug-in (Model 4), with the number of bootstrap samples set to 5,000. Covariates, which were selected based on univariate significance, including monthly income, the number of medications taken daily, and hypertension, were included in the model to control for potential confounding effects.

Before conducting the mediation analysis, statistical assumptions, including normality, linearity, and homoscedasticity, were rigorously tested and met to ensure the validity of the estimates. PROCESS Model 4 was selected as the analytical framework because it is highly effective for assessing simple mediation paths across three time points (T1-T2-T3). This approach allows for the rigorous testing of the longitudinal temporal sequence while providing robust, bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals for the indirect effects.

To ensure the robustness of the mediation findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted by comparing models with and without the inclusion of covariates. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. Missing data handling involved listwise deletion for participants with incomplete follow-up. Statistical analyses were conducted to compare baseline characteristics, such as age, sex, and duration of AF, between participants who were lost to follow-up and those who completed the study to assess potential attrition bias.

The mean scores for medication literacy at baseline (T1), self-efficacy for appropriate medication use at the 3-month follow-up (T2), and medication adherence at the 6-month follow-up (T3) were 3.82 (SD = 2.26), 25.40 (SD = 8.05), and 34.53 (SD = 14.19) points, respectively. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the Chinese version of the 12-item medication adherence scale(measured at T3) was 0.846, indicating good internal consistency in this population. Additionally, the Chinese version of the self-efficacy for appropriate medication use scale (measured at T2) demonstrated a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.889. The Chinese version of the medication literacy questionnaire (measured at T1) demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.792.

Pearson correlation analysis indicated a positive correlation between medication literacy and self-efficacy for appropriate medication use. Specifically, medication literacy (T1) demonstrated a positive correlation with medication adherence (T3) (r = 0.426, P < 0.01), and self-efficacy for appropriate medication use (T2) was also positively correlated with medication adherence (T3) (r = 0.336, P < 0.01). However, no significant correlations were found between age and self-efficacy for appropriate medication use, medication literacy, or medication adherence (r = -0.024, -0.058, 0.006; P = 0.568, 0.165, 0.878, respectively).

The mediating effect of self-efficacy for appropriate medication use between medication literacy and medication adherence in patients with atrial fibrillation

As detailed in Tables 3 and 4, and Fig. 1, the mediation analysis revealed significant relationships among the independent variable (medication literacy at T1), the mediating variable (self-efficacy at T2), and the dependent variable (medication adherence at T3) after controlling for monthly income, the number of medications taken daily, and hypertension (P < 0.05). Specifically, the inclusion of self-efficacy as a mediator resulted in a significant decrease in the direct effect of medication literacy on adherence. The bootstrap analysis confirmed a significant indirect effect, with self-efficacy accounting for 13.38% of the total impact of medication literacy on adherence.

Discussion

As a prevalent arrhythmia, AF requires long-term pharmacological management [27]. Sustained adherence to prescribed treatment regimens is essential for managing AF symptoms and, most crucially, for reducing the risk of serious complications such as ischemic stroke. In this context, patients with higher medication literacy are better equipped to comprehend complex treatment instructions, supporting more informed decision-making [28]. Furthermore, rational drug use and self-efficacy are key determinants of consistent behavior, as patients confident in managing medications are more likely to maintain adherence over time [14].

Current situation of medication adherence, medication literacy and self-efficacy for appropriate medication use in patients with atrial fibrillation

The medication literacy score of the participants in this group was 3.82/7.00, indicating that their knowledge and understanding of pharmaceuticals is at an upper-middle level. Medication literacy typically encompasses comprehension of drug names, dosages, usage, side effects, and interactions [29]. Higher medication literacy implies that patients are better equipped to understand physicians’ prescriptions and use medications correctly [28]. Conversely, the self-efficacy score for rational drug use among the subjects was 25.40/39.00, suggesting that their confidence in their ability to use medications appropriately is at a middle to lower level. Low self-efficacy may indicate a lack of confidence or uncertainty about the correct use of medications. Furthermore, the medication adherence score was 34.53/60.00, which also falls within the middle to lower range. Inadequate adherence to prescribed treatment regimens can result in suboptimal therapeutic outcomes, ineffective disease management, and potentially heightened healthcare costs and increased risk of hospital readmission [30].

The comprehensive findings indicate that, despite possessing substantial knowledge regarding their medication, patients with atrial fibrillation encounter challenges in effectively applying this knowledge in practical settings. This difficulty may be attributed to several factors, including a lack of confidence in medication administration, concerns about potential side effects, or misinterpretations of medical advice. Furthermore, diminished self-efficacy may exacerbate non-adherence to medication regimens, as insufficient confidence can lead to patients disregarding medical recommendations or failing to take medications as prescribed. To enhance medication adherence among patients, healthcare professionals could consider strategies including increased education and support, potentially bolstering patients’ self-efficacy regarding proper medication use, and ensuring comprehensive understanding of the significance of their treatment regimens. Furthermore, improving doctor-patient communication has the potential to foster patients’ confidence in their treatment, thereby potentially further promoting adherence to prescribed medications.

The mediating role of self-efficacy for appropriate medication use between medication literacy and medication adherence in patients with atrial fibrillation

The correlation analysis of this study reveals a positive association between medication literacy and self-efficacy in the context of appropriate medication use, indicating that enhancing patients’ medication literacy may improve their self-efficacy in this regard. Concurrently, a positive correlation is observed between medication literacy and medication adherence, suggesting that improvements in medication literacy may be associated with patients’ drug use behaviors, potentially promoting more rational adherence to medical advice. Furthermore, the positive correlation between self-efficacy for appropriate medication use and medication adherence underscores the pivotal role of self-efficacy in drug use behavior, as patients with higher self-efficacy are more likely to exhibit optimal medication adherence.

The above findings thus elucidate the intrinsic relationships among medication literacy, self-efficacy for appropriate medication use, and medication adherence. First, the positive correlation identified between medication literacy and self-efficacy for appropriate medication use suggests that enhancing patients’ knowledge and skills regarding medication may significantly contribute to their confidence in their ability to use medications rationally. This increased confidence not only encourages patients to engage more actively in their treatment processes but also motivates them to seek and acquire additional information about medications, thereby establishing a positive feedback loop [31].

Second, the positive correlation between medication literacy and medication adherence further demonstrates that patients’ comprehension of pharmaceuticals directly influences their medication-taking behavior. Patients with high medication literacy are more likely to appreciate the significance of medical advice, thereby adhering to prescribed instructions and minimizing instances of non-compliance [32]. This enhancement in adherence is crucial for the long-term therapeutic outcomes and management of conditions such as atrial fibrillation. Furthermore, the study indicates that 13.38% of the impact of medication literacy on adherence is mediated by the variable of self-efficacy for appropriate medication use. This finding suggests a potential direction for interventions aimed at improving patients’ medication adherence, which could not only focus on directly enhancing medication literacy but also on fostering and strengthening patients’ self-efficacy. Educational and psychological support may serve as effective strategies to help patients develop accurate self-awareness and improve their capacity for self-management in medication use.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the sample was recruited from a single center in Hefei using a convenience sampling method, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings to patients with atrial fibrillation in other regions or healthcare settings. As such, the socio-demographic and clinical profile of our cohort may not fully represent the broader population of AF patients in different geographical or socioeconomic contexts. Second, although this study employed a longitudinal design, the follow-up duration was relatively short; future research should implement multi-center studies with extended follow-up periods to capture the temporal dynamics of medication adherence. Finally, while the quantitative approach provided robust statistical evidence, future investigations could benefit from a mixed-methods design. Combining qualitative interviews with quantitative data would offer deeper insights into the subjective barriers and facilitators of medication adherence, ultimately providing a more comprehensive basis for developing tailored intervention strategies. Furthermore, although we adjusted for significant covariates such as monthly income and hypertension in our analysis, the potential for residual confounding remains. Other unmeasured variables, such as healthcare accessibility, psychological resilience, or the quality of social support systems, may also influence the interrelationships between medication literacy, self-efficacy, and adherence.

Conclusion

This study indicates that medication adherence among patients with atrial fibrillation is at a suboptimal level. The results highlight that self-efficacy for appropriate medication use serves as a significant mediator, accounting for 13.38% of the total effect of medication literacy on adherence. While the longitudinal design provides a temporal sequence that supports the proposed mediation pathways, these findings should be interpreted with caution as they represent observed associations rather than definitive causal relationships. Consequently, healthcare providers should implement interventions that not only enhance patients’ medication literacy but also systematically foster their self-efficacy to optimize long-term medication adherence and clinical outcomes.
