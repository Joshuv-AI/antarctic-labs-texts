Frankl’s diversity theorem for permutations
Eduard Inozemtsev, Andrey Kupavskii

Abstract
In 1987, Frankl proved an influential stability result for the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem, which bounds the size of an intersecting family in terms of its distance from the nearest (subset of) star or trivial intersecting family. It is a far-reaching extension of the Hilton–Milner theorem. In this paper, we prove its analogue for permutations on { 1 , … , n } \{1,\ldots,n\} , provided n n is large. This provides a similar extension of a Hilton–Milner type result for permutations proved by Ellis.

Introduction

We denote by
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
the standard
n
-element set and by
{[n]\choose m}:=\{F\subset[n]:|F|=m\}
the set of its
m
-element subsets. A
family
is a collection of sets. A family is
intersecting
if any two sets from the family intersect.

The famous theorem of Erdős, Ko and Rado (EKR)
[
9
]
states that for
n\geq 2m
an intersecting family in
{[n]\choose m}
has size at most
{n-1\choose m-1}.
For
n>2m
we have a series of stability results for the EKR theorem that can be conveniently formulated in terms of the diversity of a family. For a family
\mathcal{F}\subset{2^{[n]}}
we define its maximum degree
\Delta(\mathcal{F}):=\max_{x\in[n]}|\mathcal{F}[x]|
. Then
diversity

\gamma(\mathcal{F})
of the family
\mathcal{F}
is
\gamma(\mathcal{F})=|\mathcal{F}|-\Delta(\mathcal{F}).
That is, diversity is the number of sets not containing the element of maximum degree. It can be thought of as the (edit) distance to the closest star.

The result of Hilton and Milner
[
19
]
states that
|\mathcal{F}|\leq{n-1\choose m-1}-{n-m-1\choose m-1}+1
if
\gamma(\mathcal{F})\geq 1.
The matching example is the Frankl family
\mathcal{A}_{m}
, where the Frankl families
\mathcal{A}_{k}
are defined for
k=3,\ldots,m
below:

\mathcal{A}_{k}=\Big\{F\in{[n]\choose m}:1\in F,F\cap[2,k+1]\neq\emptyset\Big\}\bigcup\Big\{F\in{[n]\choose m}:[2,k+1]\subset F\Big\}.

The following theorem was proved by Frankl
[
16
]
in the degree form and then by Kupavskii and Zakharov
[
27
]
in the diversity form.

Theorem 1

(Frankl,
[
16
]
; Kupavskii and Zakharov,
[
27
]
)
.

Let
n>2m>0
be integers. Take an intersecting family
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose m}
with
\gamma(\mathcal{F})\geq{n-k-1\choose m-k}
for some real
3\leq k\leq m
. Then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq{n-1\choose m-1}+{n-k-1\choose m-k}-{n-k-1\choose m-1},

and equality is possible only if
\mathcal{F}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{A}_{k}.

Intersection problems are studied for other objects: permutations, graphs
[
6
]
, partitions
[
31
]
,
[
22
]
, simplicial complexes
[
2
]
,
[
23
]
and vector spaces
[
12
]
. In this paper, we focus on permutations.
We denote by
\Sigma_{n}
the family of all permutations
[n]\rightarrow[n]
. We say that two permutations
\sigma,\pi\in\Sigma_{n}

intersect
if there exists
i\in[n]
such that
\sigma(i)=\pi(i)
. It is convenient to identify a permutation
\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}
with an
n
-element subset of
[n]^{2}
consisting of pairs
(x,\sigma(x))
. This way, a family of permutations can be treated as a subfamily of
{[n]^{2}\choose n}
. The intersection of two permutations then becomes the usual intersection of
n
-element sets.

Deza and Frankl
[
4
]
initiated the study of
t
-intersecting families of permutations. They observed that if
\mathcal{F}\subset\Sigma_{n}
is intersecting, then
|\mathcal{F}|\leq(n-1)!
. Let us recite the simple proof of this bound below. Take the standard
n
-cycle
\pi
,
\pi(k)=k+1
, with addition modulo
n
. Let
G
be the cyclic group of order
n
generated by
\pi
. Take a permutation
\sigma
. Consider its left coset
\sigma G
. Any two permutations in
\sigma G
do not intersect. Hence,
\sigma G
contains at most
1
permutation from
\mathcal{F}
. At the same time, we have
(n-1)!
different cosets.

This proof is simple, but it does not say anything about the case when equality
|\mathcal{F}|=(n-1)!
is achieved. Deza and Frankl conjectured that
|\mathcal{F}|=(n-1)!
if and only if
\mathcal{F}
consists of all permutations with some fixed element
(x,y)
. Note that this family has diversity
0
. It was proved by Cameron and Ku
[
3
]
and Larose and Malvenuto
[
29
]
. Larose and Malvenuto conjectured that the largest intersecting family permutations
\mathcal{F}
with
\gamma(\mathcal{F})\geq 1
is isomorphic to

\{\sigma\}\cup\big\{\pi\in\Sigma_{n}:\pi(1)=1,\pi\cap\sigma\neq\emptyset\big\},

(1.1)

where
\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}
is some permutation with
\sigma(1)\neq 1
. By
isomorphic to
\mathcal{F}
we mean that a family has the form
\pi\mathcal{F}\rho
, where
\pi,\rho\in\Sigma_{n}
. This Hilton–Milner type result was proved by Ellis
[
7
]
for large
n
. The results mentioned in this paragraph were proved using algebraic techniques such as Hoffman spectral bounds and representation theory of symmetric groups.

Let
\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}
be a permutation. We denote by
\Sigma_{n,\sigma,\overline{\pi}}
the family of permutations that intersect
\sigma
and do not intersect
\pi
. If
\sigma
is the identity permutation, then by
\mathcal{D}_{n}:=\Sigma_{n,\overline{\sigma}}
we denote the family of derangements. Similarly, we use
\mathcal{D}_{n,\sigma}
to denote permutations from
\mathcal{D}_{n}
that intersect
\sigma
.
We also denote
d_{n}=|\mathcal{D}_{n}|
.
Using the inclusion-exclusion principle, it is easy to see that
|\mathcal{D}_{n}|=n!\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{i+1}}{i!}
. It is well-known that
|\mathcal{D}_{n}|=\lceil\frac{n!}{e}\rfloor
, i.e.
|\mathcal{D}_{n}|
is the nearest integer to
\frac{n!}{e}
.

In this paper, we get an analogue of Theorem
1
for permutations. First, we need to find the correct analogue of the Frankl families
\mathcal{A}_{k}
. It will be given by
\mathcal{E}_{k}
below.

Recall that, for a given permutation
\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}
, its
support

S(\sigma)
is the subset of
[n]
of all non-fixed points of
\sigma
. That is,
S(\sigma)=\{i:\sigma(i)\neq i\}.

For
k=2,\ldots,n-2
consider a family

\mathcal{H}_{k}:=\Sigma_{n}[\overline{(1,1)},\{(k+1,k+1),\dots,(n,n)\}]

of all permutations
\sigma
with
S(\sigma)\cap[k+1,n]=\emptyset
and
1\notin S(\sigma)
. (Cf. the notation in the next section.) We have

|\mathcal{H}_{k}|=k!-(k-1)!.

For a given
\mathcal{G}\subset\Sigma_{n}
define
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{G})\subset\Sigma_{n}[(1,1)]
to be the family of permutations from
\Sigma_{n}[(1,1)]
that intersect each permutation from
\mathcal{G}
.
Define

\mathcal{E}_{k}:=\mathcal{H}_{k}\cup\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k}).

It is easy to see that
\mathcal{H}_{k}
and
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})
are disjoint and together form an intersecting family. Also,
\gamma(\mathcal{E}_{k})=|\mathcal{H}_{k}|=k!-(k-1)!.
Note that
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is isomorphic to the family (
1.1
). One can check that
|\mathcal{E}_{n-3}|=|\mathcal{E}_{n-2}|=3(n-2)!-2(n-3)!
using the inclusion-exclusion formula (see (
2.6
)). It is also not difficult to see that
|\mathcal{E}_{k}|-|\mathcal{E}_{k-1}|\geq d_{n-2}
for
3\leq k\leq n-3
.

The main result of this paper is given below. It provides a generalization of a result by Ellis
[
7
]
analogous to Theorem
1
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
n
be a large enough integer. Take an intersecting family
\mathcal{F}\subset\Sigma_{n}
of permutations. If for some
k\in[2,n-3]
we have
\gamma(\mathcal{F})\geq k!-(k-1)!
then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq|\mathcal{E}_{k}|=(n-1)!-\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose i}d_{n-1-i}+k!-(k-1)!

Moreover, for
k\leq n-4
equality is only possible for
\mathcal{F}
isomorphic to
\mathcal{E}_{k}
, and for
k=n-3
equality is only possible when
\mathcal{F}
is isomorphic to either
\mathcal{E}_{n-3}
or
\mathcal{E}_{n-2}
.

In the following section, we introduce some notation and give exact and asymptotic formulas for the size of
\mathcal{E}_{k}
. We note that, for any
k\in[2,n-3],
it is dominated by the
|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})|
term. In Section
3
we prove Theorem
2
.

A related question that was studied for sets is how large the diversity of an intersecting family can be. Lemons and Palmer
[
30
]
proved for
n>n_{0}(m)
that if
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose m}
is intersecting, then
\gamma(\mathcal{F})\leq{n-3\choose m-2}
. Frankl
[
11
]
improved the bound on
n
to
n\geq 6k^{2}
, then Kupavskii
[
21
]
improved it to
n\geq Ck
for some absolute constant
C
. Recently, Frankl and Wang
[
18
]
proved that for
n\geq 36k
, which is currently the best known bound.

For permutations, Wang and Xiao
[
35
]
, using spread approximations, recently proved that if
\mathcal{F}\subset\Sigma_{n}
is intersecting, then
\gamma(\mathcal{F})\leq(n-2)!-(n-3)!.
(We note that the authors of the present note, while working on the proof, also got the same result via the same technique. We, however, omit it and simply rely on
[
35
]
instead.)

Our proof combines several combinatorial ingredients with the spread approximation method. An interesting aspect of this application of the spread approximation technique is that we need its highly asymmetric variant for two cross-intersecting families, with a carefully chosen set of parameters.

2
The family
\mathcal{E}_{k}

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of sets. For sets
X,Y
and a family
\mathcal{S}
, we use the following standard notation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(X):=

\displaystyle\ \{F\setminus X:X\subset F,F\in\mathcal{F}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[X]:=

\displaystyle\ \{F:X\subset F,F\in\mathcal{F}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[X,\overline{Y}]:=

\displaystyle\ \{F:X\subset F,Y\cap F=\emptyset,F\in\mathcal{F}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[\mathcal{S}]:=

\displaystyle\ \bigcup_{A\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{F}[A].

Recall that, whenever more convenient, we treat permutations
\sigma
as sets in
[n]^{2}
. That is, we have
\sigma=\{(i,\sigma(i)):i\in[n]\}
. We will also work with
partial permutations,
i.e., sets of the same form and size
\leq n
that could be extended to a permutation.

For convenience in this and the following proofs, we introduce a partial permutation
\alpha_{i,j}:=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}i&i+1&\cdots&j\\
i&i+1&\cdots&j\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)=\{(i,i),(i+1,i+1)\dots,(j,j)\}
and use notation like
\Sigma_{\alpha_{k+1,n}}[(1,1)]
, which stands for all permutations from
\Sigma_{n}
that intersect
\alpha_{k+1,n}
and fix
(1,1).
We will typically omit
n
from the subscript in the notation like
\Sigma_{n}
etc., simply writing
\Sigma
instead.

We will need the following formula for the size of
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})
.

Lemma 3
.

We have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})|

\displaystyle=(n-1)!-\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose i}d_{n-i-1},

(2.1)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{k}|

\displaystyle=(n-1)!-\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose i}d_{n-1-i}+k!-(k-1)!.

(2.2)

Proof.

We prove (
2.1
). Then (
2.2
) follows. It is easy to see that a permutation
\pi\in\Sigma[(1,1)]
intersects each permutation of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
if and only if
S(\pi)\cap[k+1,n]\neq\emptyset.
In our notation, we have
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})=\Sigma_{\alpha_{k+1,n}}[(1,1)]
. We decompose

|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})|=|\Sigma_{\alpha_{k+1,n}}[(1,1)]|=|\Sigma[(1,1)]|-|\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{k+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|.

(2.3)

We find the size of
\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{k+1,n}}}[(1,1)]
by specifying the intersection of its permutations with
\alpha_{2,k}
:

\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{k+1,n}}}[(1,1)]=\bigcup_{i=0}^{k-1}\bigcup_{\pi\subset\alpha_{2,k},|\pi|=i}\Sigma_{\pi,\overline{\alpha_{2,n}\setminus\pi}}[(1,1)].

Note that for
|\pi|=i
we have
|\Sigma_{\pi,\overline{\alpha_{2,n}\setminus\pi}}[(1,1)]|=d_{n-i-1}
. This gives

|\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{k+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose i}d_{n-i-1}.

Together with (
2.3
) this implies (
2.1
).
∎

Lemma 4
.

For
n\to\infty
we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})|

\displaystyle=(n-2)!\cdot\Big((n-k)+O\big((n-k)^{2}/n\big)\Big).

(2.4)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})|

\displaystyle=(n-1)!\cdot\Big(1-e^{-1}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{k-1}+O(k\log^{2}n/n^{2})\Big).

(2.5)

The first equality is applicable for
k=(1-o(1))n,
while the second one is applicable for, say,
k<n-n^{1/2}
. For
k=cn
with
c\in[0,1)
it gives
|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})|=(n-1)!\cdot(1-e^{c-1}+o(1))
.

Proof.

To get (
2.4
), we use the representation

|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})|=\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}(-1)^{i+1}{n-k\choose i}(n-1-i)!,

(2.6)

which could be obtained via exclusion-inclusion in the same vein as the formula for derangements. More precisely, it is implied from the following calculations

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}(-1)^{i+1}{n-k\choose i}(n-1-i)!

\displaystyle={n-k\choose 1}(n-2)!-{n-k\choose 2}(n-3)!+\sum_{i=3}^{n-k}(-1)^{i+1}{n-k\choose i}(n-1-i)!

\displaystyle=\Big((n-k)(n-2)!+O\Big((n-k)^{2}/2\Big)\cdot(n-3)!+O\Big((n-k)^{3}/6\Big)\cdot(n-4)!\Big),

where in the last equality we used

{n-k\choose i+1}/{n-k\choose i}\cdot(n-2-i)!/(n-1-i)!\leq 1/(i+1).

In order to obtain (
2.5
), we use (
2.1
) and replace
d_{n-1-i}
by
(n-1-i)!/e
:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{H}_{k}|

\displaystyle=(n-1)!-\frac{1}{e}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose i}(n-i-1)!+O(2^{k})

\displaystyle=(n-1)!\left(1-\frac{1}{e}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose i}\frac{(n-i-1)!}{(n-1)!}+O\Big(\big(2e/n\big)^{n}\Big)\right).

Next we estimate the sum
\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose i}\frac{(n-i-1)!}{(n-1)!}
in the following way

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose i}\frac{(n-i-1)!}{(n-1)!}=\sum_{i=0}^{10\log{n}}{k-1\choose i}\frac{(n-i-1)!}{(n-1)!}+\sum_{i=10\log{n}+1}^{k-1}{k-1\choose i}\frac{(n-i-1)!}{(n-1)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{10\log{n}}{k-1\choose i}n^{-i}\Big(1+O\Big(i^{2}/n\Big)\Big)+O\left({k-1\choose 10\log{n}}\frac{(n-10\log{n}-1)!}{(n-1)!}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{10\log{n}}{k-1\choose i}n^{-i}\Big(1+O\Big(i^{2}/n\Big)\Big)+O\left(\Big(\frac{(k-1)e}{10\log{n}}\Big)^{10\log{n}}\cdot\Big(\frac{1}{n-10\log{n}}\Big)^{10\log{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{10\log{n}}{k-1\choose i}n^{-i}\Big(1+O\left(i^{2}/n\Big)\right)+O\left(1/n^{C}\right)

for any constant
C
, where in the first sum we used
\frac{(n-i)!}{n!}=n^{-i}\Big(1+O(i^{2}/n)\Big)
for
i=o(\sqrt{n})
and the second sum decreases at least as the geometrical progression with common ratio
\frac{1}{10\log{n}}
, since

{k-1\choose i+1}\frac{(n-i-2)!}{(n-1)!}/{k-1\choose i}\frac{(n-i-1)!}{(n-1)!}=\frac{k-1-i}{(i+1)(n-i-1)}\leq\frac{1}{10\log{n}}.

We continue the above as follows, noting that for term
i=0
there is no error,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{10\log{n}}{k-1\choose i}n^{-i}\Big(1+O\Big(i^{2}/n\Big)\Big)=\sum_{i=0}^{10\log{n}}{k-1\choose i}n^{-i}+\sum_{i=1}^{10\log{n}}{k-1\choose i}n^{-i}\cdot O\Big(i^{2}/n\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{10\log{n}}{k-1\choose i}n^{-i}+O(\log^{2}{n}/n)\cdot O(k/n)=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose i}n^{-i}+O\Big(k\log^{2}{n}/n^{2}\Big),

where in the second equality we used
i\leq 10\log{n}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{10\log{n}}{k-1\choose i}n^{-i}=O(k/n),
and in the last inequality we again used that
\sum_{i=10\log{n}+1}^{k-1}{k-1\choose i}n^{-i}=O(1/n^{C}).

Finally, we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{H}_{k}|

\displaystyle=(n-1)!\cdot\Big[1-\frac{1}{e}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose i}n^{-i}+O(k\log^{2}n/n^{2})\Big]

\displaystyle=(n-1)!\cdot\Big[1-\frac{1}{e}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{k-1}+O(k\log^{2}n/n^{2})\Big].

∎

From here after, we replace
d_{n}
by
n!/e
, since

\sum_{i=0}^{m}{m\choose i}d_{n-i}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}{m\choose i}(n-i)!/e+O(2^{m})

and the big-O term is negligible.

Proof of Theorem
2

Permutations from
\Sigma_{n}[\overline{(1,1)}]
can be viewed as
(n-2)
-partial permutations
[2,n]\to[2,n]
: for
\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}[\overline{(1,1)}]
, delete
(1,\sigma(1))
and
(\sigma^{-1}(1),1),
and get an
(n-2)-
partial permutation. Conversely, such an
(n-2)
-partial permutation can be uniquely extended to a permutation in
\Sigma_{n}[\overline{(1,1)}]
. Note that

\Big|\bigcap\mathcal{H}_{k}\Big|=n-k

as permutations and also as partial permutations. (Here and in what follows,
\bigcap\mathcal{X}
is a shorthand for
\bigcap_{X\in\mathcal{X}}X
.) Note, that we want to maximize
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{G})
for given
\mathcal{G}
. Therefore, if
X=\bigcap\mathcal{H}_{k}
contains elements
(1,i)
or
(i,1)
, then permutations from
\Sigma[(1,1)]
can not intersect
X
by these elements. Hence, w.l.o.g. we may assume that
\bigcap\mathcal{H}_{k}\subset[2,n]^{2}
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\Sigma_{n}=\Sigma
be the largest intersecting family of permutations satisfying the conditions of Theorem
2
. W.l.o.g. assume that
(1,1)
has the largest degree in
\mathcal{F}
: i.e., that
|\mathcal{F}[(1,1)]|=\Delta(\mathcal{F})
. We decompose

\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}\sqcup\mathcal{F}_{\gamma},

where
\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}=\mathcal{F}[(1,1)]
is the degree part and
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}=\mathcal{F}[\overline{(1,1)}]
is the diversity part.
We have
|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}|=\gamma(\mathcal{F})\geq k!-(k-1)!
by the assumption of the theorem. However, a bulk of the family is
\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}.
In the proof, we use the aforementioned result of Wang and Xiao
[
35
]
that for
n
large enough and any intersecting
\mathcal{F}\subset\Sigma_{n}
we have

|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}|\leq(n-2)!-(n-3)!

Compared with the bounds from Lemma
4
on the size of
\mathcal{E}_{k}
, it is typically a negligible part.

Put
X:=\bigcap\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}
and suppose that
|X|=n-t
, for some
t
. That is, using
|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}|\geq k!-(k-1)!
, we have

\Big|\bigcap\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}\Big|=n-t\leq n-k.

If
t=k
, equivalent to
|X|=n-k
, then we have
|\Sigma[\overline{(1,1)},X]|=k!-(k-1)!
. We have
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}\subset\Sigma[\overline{(1,1)},X]
. The lower bound on the size of
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}
implies that
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}=\Sigma[\overline{(1,1)},X],
which in turn is isomorphic to
\mathcal{H}_{k}.
This and the fact that
\mathcal{F}
is inclusion-maximal implies that
\mathcal{F}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{E}_{k}.

In what follows, we assume that
|X|=n-t<n-k
. For simplicity and w.l.o.g., in what follows we assume that
X=\alpha_{t+1,n}
. The equality
\bigcap\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}=\alpha_{t+1,n}
implies that for any
x\in[2,t]^{2}
there exists a partial permutation
\pi
from
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}
such that
x\notin\pi
.

Our goal is to show that for
t>k
we have
|\mathcal{F}|<|\mathcal{E}_{k}|
. We distinguish three cases.

3.1
Case 1.

t\geq 4k

We begin with the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\sigma,\pi
be two
partial permutations on
[n]
, and suppose that

(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\notin\sigma,\pi
and
\{(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\}
is a partial permutation of size
2
.
Then the number of permutations avoiding both
\sigma
and
\pi
, but containing
(x_{1},y_{1})
and
(x_{2},y_{2})
satisfies

|\Sigma_{\overline{\sigma},\overline{\pi}}[(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})]|\geq\frac{(n-2)!}{e^{2}}-\frac{2(n-3)!}{e^{2}}.

Proof.

For simplicity, we assume that
(x_{1},y_{1})=(1,1)
and
(x_{2},y_{2})=(2,2)
. We lower bound
|\Sigma_{\overline{\sigma},\overline{\pi}}[(1,1),(2,2)]|
using the permanent of the corresponding matrix. First, we form disjoint partial permutations
\sigma^{\prime},\pi^{\prime}
, where
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma\cap([3,n]\times[3,n])
and
\pi^{\prime}=(\pi\setminus\sigma)\cap([3,n]\times[3,n])
is defined analogously. Consider the
(0,1)
-matrix
A
with rows and columns indexed by
[3,n]
and with
0
’s exactly on positions corresponding to the elements of
\sigma^{\prime},\pi^{\prime}
. This matrix has at most two zeros in each row and each column. Henderson proved
[
33
]
that the minimum possible permanent of such matrix equals to the
(n-2)
-th menage number
U_{n-2}
for
n
even (and
U_{n-2}-1
for
n
odd). We have

U_{n}\sim\frac{n!}{e^{2}}\Big(1-\frac{1}{n-1}+\frac{1}{2!(n-1)(n-2)}-\dots\Big)\geq\frac{n!}{e^{2}}\cdot\frac{n-2}{n-1}

.

∎

We want to lower bound the size of

. Obviously,

But we could say more.
For any

\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}
cannot contain permutations from

. By Lemma

|\Sigma_{\overline{\sigma\setminus x},\overline{\rho_{x}}}[(1,1),x]|\geq\frac{(n-2)!}{e^{2}}-\frac{2(n-3)!}{e^{2}}.

Note, that
\Sigma_{\overline{\sigma\setminus x},\overline{\rho_{x}}}[(1,1),x]\cap\Sigma_{\overline{\sigma\setminus y},\overline{\rho_{y}}}[(1,1),y]=\emptyset
. Therefore, summing up over all

\displaystyle|\Sigma[(1,1)]|-|\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}|

\displaystyle\geq|\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}[(1,1)]|+(t-2)\frac{(n-2)!}{e^{2}}-(t-2)\frac{4(n-3)!}{e^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(n-1)!}{e}\Big(1+\frac{t}{en}+O(t/n^{2})\Big).

(3.1)

Using (

\displaystyle|\Sigma[(1,1)]|-|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})|

\displaystyle=(n-1)!\cdot\Big(e^{-1}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{k-1}+O(k\log^{2}n/n^{2})\Big)

Taking the difference of the RHS of (

|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})|-|\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}|\geq\frac{(n-1)!}{e}\Big(1+\frac{t}{en}-e^{k/n}+O(k\log^{2}n/n^{2}+t/n^{2})\Big).

We have

|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})|-|\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}|\geq(n-1)!\cdot\frac{t}{Cn}

Consequently,

|\mathcal{E}_{k}|-|\mathcal{F}|\geq|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})|-|\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}|-|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}|\geq(n-1)!\cdot\frac{t}{Cn}-|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}|.

If

3.2
Case 2.

t\leq 4k,k\leq\frac{\sqrt{n}}{100}

Recall that
\bigcap\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}=\alpha_{t+1,n}
and

. Hence, we compare the remainders,

and
|\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}\cap\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|.

In order to upper bound
|\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}\cap\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|
, we count the number of hitting sets of different sizes for the family of partial permutations
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(\alpha_{t+1,n})
. Recall that
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(\alpha_{t+1,n})=\{\pi\setminus\alpha_{t+1,n}\mid\pi\in\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}\}
and that a
hitting set
for
\mathcal{X}
is a set that intersects all sets from
\mathcal{X}
.

Note that each permutation in
\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}\cap\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]
corresponds to a hitting set of size at most
t-1
for
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(\alpha_{t+1,n})
. We count inclusion-minimal hitting sets only. If
\mathcal{B}
is the family of all such inclusion-minimal hitting sets, then, by maximality of
\mathcal{F},

\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}\cap\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]=\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1),\mathcal{B}].

The following lemma in the spirit of Erdős and Lovàsz provides the necessary bound.

Lemma 6
.

The family
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(\alpha_{t+1,n})
has no
1
-hitting sets. The number of minimal
i
-hitting sets from
[2,t]^{2}
for the family
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(\alpha_{t+1,n})
is at most
(t-2)^{i}
for each
i\in[2,t-1]
.

Proof.

Take an arbitrary
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(\alpha_{t+1,n})
. Any hitting set
H
contains an element from
\rho_{0},
say
x_{1}
. Take a permutation from
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(\alpha_{t+1,n})
that avoids
x_{1}
. We see that, first, there are no
1-
hitting sets and, second, any
(\geq 2)
-hitting set contains an element from
\rho_{1}
. Given that
|\rho_{0}|,|\rho_{1}|\leq t-2
(as a partial permutation on
[2,t]
), the number of
2-
hitting sets is at most
(t-2)^{2}
.

We proceed inductively. For each prospective partial hitting set
x_{1},\ldots,x_{i}
that is not yet a hitting set, there is a permutation
\rho_{i}
that avoids all of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{i}
. Any inclusion-minimal
(i+1)-
hitting set
H
extending
x_{1},\ldots,x_{i}
must contain an element
x_{i+1}\in\rho_{i}
. By induction, the number of partial sets
x_{1},\ldots,x_{i}
is at most
(t-2)^{i},
and there are
(t-2)
options for
x_{i+1}.
Moreover, each inclusion-minimal
(i+1)
-hitting set must arise this way. Thus, there are at most
(t-2)^{i+1}
inclusion-minimal
(i+1)
-hitting sets.
∎

Define
\mathcal{B}
to be the family of such hitting sets. Note that the number of sets from
\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]
containing an
i
-hitting set
B\in\mathcal{B}
is equal to

\sum_{j=i}^{t-i-1}{t-i-1\choose j-i}d_{n-j-1},

by running the same argument as in Lemma

. This and the previous lemma imply

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}\cap\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|

\displaystyle\leq|\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1),\mathcal{B}]|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=2}^{t-1}(t-2)^{i}\sum_{j=i}^{t-i-1}{t-i-1\choose j-i}d_{n-j-1}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-1}\sum_{i=2}^{t-1}\sum_{j=i}^{t-i-1}t^{j}(n-j-1)!

\displaystyle\leq t^{2}\frac{(n-3)!}{e}+2t^{3}(n-4)!+3t^{4}(n-5)!+\dots

\displaystyle\leq t^{2}\frac{(n-3)!}{e}+2t^{2}/25\cdot\sqrt{n}(n-4)!+3t^{2}/25^{2}\cdot(\sqrt{n})^{2}(n-5)!+\dots

\displaystyle\leq t^{2}\frac{(n-3)!}{e}+t^{2}\sqrt{n}(n-4)!,

(3.3)

where we used
t\leq 4k\leq\frac{\sqrt{n}}{25}.

At the same time, we bound
|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})\cap\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|=|\Sigma_{\alpha_{k+1,t},\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|
as follows

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})\cap\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|

\displaystyle=|\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|-|\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{k+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{t})|-|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})|

(3.4)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eqsizehk}}{=}\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}{t-1\choose i}d_{n-i-1}-\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose i}d_{n-i-1}

\displaystyle\geq(t-k)\frac{(n-2)!}{e}.

(3.5)

Finally, compare the expressions in the RHS of (
3.5
) and (
3.3
). We have

(t-k)\frac{(n-2)!}{e}-t^{2}\frac{(n-3)!}{e}-t^{2}\sqrt{n}(n-4)!\geq\frac{(n-2)!}{e}\left(t-k-\frac{2t^{2}}{n-2}\right)\geq\frac{(n-2)!}{e}\left(1-\frac{1}{10}\right).

where we used that
t\leq\sqrt{n}/25
and
k<t
. It is clear that the RHS of the displayed chain of inequalities is bigger than
|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}|,
since in the case

we have
|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}|\leq(n-t)!\leq\Big(n-\frac{\sqrt{n}}{25}\Big)!
. Thus we conclude that
|\mathcal{F}|<|\mathcal{E}_{k}|
again.

Case 3.

k\geq\sqrt{n}/100
,
t\leq 4k

As in Case 2, we compare the sizes of

From now on, we treat permutations from

, and permutations from

as permutations

To prove the theorem in this case, we shall use the spread approximation technique, introduced by the second author and Zakharov
[

]
and developed in a series of recent works of the second author and coauthors
[

,
24
,
17
,

.

We say that a family
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
is
r
-spread for some
r\geq 1
if
\frac{|\mathcal{F}(X)|}{|\mathcal{F}|}\leq r^{-|X|}
for any
X\subset[n].
The following statement is a variant due to Stoeckl
[
34
]
of the breakthrough result that was proved by Alweiss, Lovett, Wu and Zhang
[
1
]
. It is central for the spread approximation method.

Theorem 7

(Spread Lemma,
[
1
]
)
.

If for some
n,k,r,m\geq 1
and
\delta>0
a family
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose\leq k}
is
r
-spread and
W
is an
m\delta
–random subset of
[n]
, then

\Pr[\exists F\in\mathcal{F}:F\subset W]\geq 1-\left(\frac{2}{\log_{2}(r\delta)}\right)^{m}k.

For us, it will be convenient to work with the following modified version of spreadness. Fix an
arbitrary family of sets
\mathcal{A}
and a real number
\tau>1
. A subfamily
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{A}
is called
(\mathcal{A},\tau)
-homogeneous if for any set
S
we have

\frac{|\mathcal{F}(S)|}{|\mathcal{F}|}\leq\tau^{|S|}\frac{|\mathcal{A}(S)|}{|\mathcal{A}|}.

Observation 8
.

Given a family
\mathcal{A}
,
\tau>1
and a family
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{A}
, let
X
be a maximal set that satisfies
|\mathcal{F}(X)|\geq\tau^{|X|}\frac{|\mathcal{A}(X)|}{|\mathcal{A}|}|\mathcal{F}|
. Then
\mathcal{F}(X)
is
(\mathcal{A}(X),\tau)
-homogeneous.

Proof.

Indeed, for any
B\supsetneq X
of size
b
we have

|\mathcal{F}(B)|\leq\tau^{b}\frac{(n-b)!}{n!}|\mathcal{F}|\leq\tau^{b-|X|}\frac{(n-b)!}{n!}|\mathcal{F}(X)|.

∎

Also we need the following notion.
Given a family
\mathcal{A}\subset 2^{[n]}
of sets and
q,r\geq 1
, we say that
\mathcal{A}
is
(r,q)
-spread
if for each
S\in{[n]\choose\leq q}
, the family
\mathcal{A}(S)
is
r
-spread. Note that putting
S=\emptyset
implies that
\mathcal{A}
is
r
-spread, so
(r,q)
-spreadness is a stronger condition than the usual
r
-spreadness.

For any partial permutations
S,X
, such that
S\subset X
, we have
\frac{|\Sigma_{n}(X)|}{|\Sigma_{n}(S)|}=\frac{(n-|X|)!}{(n-|S|)!}
, and simple calculus shows that for any
|S|<n/4
and any
X\supset S
, where
X,S
are partial permutations, we have

\frac{|\Sigma_{n}(S)|}{|\Sigma_{n}(X)|}=\frac{(n-|S|)!}{(n-|X|)!}\geq\big((n-|S|)!\big)^{\frac{|X|-|S|}{n-|S|}}\geq\Big(\frac{n-|S|}{e}\Big)^{|X|-|S|}>\Big(\frac{n}{4}\Big)^{|X|-|S|}.

That is,
\Sigma_{n}
is
(\frac{n}{4},\frac{n}{4})
-spread.

We will apply the following spread approximation result for
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma},\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}
. One of the unusual aspects of this result is a highly asymmetric choice of parameters for the two families. In what follows, we write
\log{n}:=\log_{1.01}{n}
for shorthand, we put

and omit integer parts whenever not essential to simplify presentation.

Theorem 9
.

Consider a family
\mathcal{F}_{1}
of permutations on
[2,n]
and a family
\mathcal{F}_{2}
of partial permutations on
[2,t]
, such that
\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}
are cross-intersecting.
Let
\tau_{1}:=1.01,\tau_{2}:=\frac{k}{\log^{2}{n}}

Then there exist families
\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2}
of partial permutations on
[2,n]
and
[2,t]
of sizes at most
q_{1},q_{2}
, respectively, and families
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{F}_{i}
for

such that the following holds.

(i)

We have
\mathcal{F}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\prime}\subset\Sigma_{n_{i}}[\mathcal{S}_{i}]

(ii)

for any

there is a family
\mathcal{F}_{i,B}\subset\mathcal{F}_{i}
such that
\mathcal{F}_{i,B}(B)
is
(\Sigma_{n_{i}}(B),\tau_{i})

(iii)

|\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\prime}|\leq\tau_{i}^{-q_{i}-1}n_{i}!

Proof.

The first guiding observation to make is that
\mathcal{F}
cannot be
\tau
-homogeneous.
Indeed, the same application of Spread Lemma as we will use later imply that there are
A_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{1},A_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{2}
such that
A_{1}\cap A_{2}=\emptyset
. We avoid calculations here, since we do them below in a more general scenario.

1.

Find an inclusion-maximal
S_{i}^{j}
that
|\mathcal{F}_{i}^{j}(S_{i}^{j})|\geq\tau^{|S_{i}^{j}|}_{i}\frac{|\Sigma_{n_{i}}(S)|}{|\Sigma_{n_{i}}|}|\mathcal{F}_{i}^{j}|
.

2.

If
|S_{i}^{j}|>q_{i}
or
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{j}=\emptyset
then stop. Otherwise, put
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{j+1}:=\mathcal{F}_{i}^{j}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{i}^{j}[S_{i}^{j}]
.

Let us show that
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{j}(S_{i}^{j})
is
(\Sigma_{n_{i}}(B),\tau_{i})
-homogeneous. Indeed, for any set
X
disjoint with
S_{i}^{j}
we have

|\mathcal{F}_{i}^{j}(S_{i}^{j}\cup X)|<\tau_{i}^{|S_{i}^{j}|+|X|}\frac{|\Sigma_{n_{i}}(S_{i}^{j}\cup X)|}{|\Sigma_{n_{i}}(X)|}|\mathcal{F}_{i}^{j}|\leq\tau_{i}^{|X|}\frac{|\Sigma_{n_{i}}(S_{i}^{j})|}{|\Sigma_{n_{i}}|}|\mathcal{F}_{i}^{j}(S_{i}^{j})|,

where the first inequality uses the maximality of
S_{i}^{j}.

Let
N_{i}
be the step of the procedure for
\mathcal{F}_{i}
at which we stop. The families
\mathcal{S}_{i}
are defined as follows:
\mathcal{S}_{i}:=\{S_{i}^{1},\ldots,S_{i}^{N_{i}-1}\}
. Clearly,
|S_{i}^{j}|\leq q_{i}
for each

. The families
\mathcal{F}_{B}
promised in (ii) are defined to be
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{j}[S_{i}^{j}]
for
B=S_{i}^{j}
. Next, note that if
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{N_{i}}
is non-empty, then

|\mathcal{F}_{i}^{N_{i}}|\leq\tau_{i}^{-|S_{i}^{N_{i}}|}\frac{|\Sigma_{n_{i}}|}{|\Sigma_{n_{i}}(S)|}|\mathcal{F}_{i}(S_{i}^{N_{i}})|\leq\tau_{i}^{-|S_{i}^{N_{i}}|}|\Sigma_{n_{i}}|.

We put
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{F}_{i}^{N_{i}}
. Since either
|S_{i}^{N_{i}}|>q_{i}
or

, we have
|\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\prime}|\leq\tau^{-q_{i}-1}_{i}\cdot n_{i}!
.
∎

Next, we verify that, thanks to the choice of the parameters, the families

9

is small.
Note that the approximation
\mathcal{S}_{2}
may be empty, in which case
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\Delta}
is empty as well.

Take
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
of maximal size, and put
|\pi|=\ell
. We may assume that
\pi=\alpha_{t-\ell+1,t}
. Any permutation from
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\Delta}
cross-intersects
\pi.
We also know that for each
(i,i)\in\alpha_{t-\ell+1,t}
, there is a permutation
\rho_{i}
from
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}
that does not contain it. Any permutation from
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
intersects
\rho_{i}
as well. Therefore,
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
cannot contain any permutation of the form
\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t-\ell+1,n}\setminus\{(i,i)\}},\overline{\rho_{i}}}[(1,1),(i,i)]
for each
i\in[t-\ell+1,t].

Using Lemma
5
, we have

|\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t-\ell+1,n}\setminus\{(i,i)\}},\overline{\rho_{i}}}[(1,1),(i,i)]|\geq\frac{(n-2)!}{e^{2}}-\frac{2(n-3)!}{e^{2}}.

(3.6)

Hence, we have

|\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\Delta}\cap\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|\leq|\Sigma_{\alpha_{t-\ell+1,t},\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|-\ell\frac{(n-2)!}{e^{2}}+\ell\frac{2(n-3)!}{e^{2}}=:X.

Recall that

|\Sigma_{\alpha_{t-\ell+1,t},\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|=\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}{t-1\choose i}\frac{(n-i-1)!}{e}-\sum_{i=0}^{t-\ell-1}{t-\ell-1\choose i}\frac{(n-i-1)!}{e}.

We compare
X
with the size of
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})\cap\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]
, given by the formula

|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})\cap\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|=\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}{t-1\choose i}\frac{(n-i-1)!}{e}-\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose i}\frac{(n-i-1)!}{e}.

First, assume that
\ell\geq t-k
.
We compare the difference between
|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})\cap\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|
and
|\Sigma_{\alpha_{t-\ell+1,t},\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|
:

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose i}\frac{(n-i-1)!}{e}-\sum_{i=0}^{t-\ell-1}{t-\ell-1\choose i}\frac{(n-i-1)!}{e}

\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(k-t+l){k-1\choose i-1}\frac{(n-i-1)!}{e}+O((n-3)!)\leq(k-t+\ell)\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{i!}\frac{(n-2)!}{e}=(k-t+\ell)(n-2)!,

where we used
{n\choose k}-{n-m\choose k}\leq m{n-1\choose k-1},
valid for any
n\geq k,m
, and in the first inequality we used
t-\ell-1>(k-1)/2
, meaning that the remaining tail is in the
O((n-3)!)
. Using this calculation, we get

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})\cap\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|-X

\displaystyle\geq\ell\frac{(n-2)!}{e^{2}}-\ell\frac{2(n-3)!}{e^{2}}-(k-t+\ell)(n-2)!

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{\ell}{e}-e(k-t+\ell)-O((t-k)/n)\right)\frac{(n-2)!}{e}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\ell(1/e-e)-e(k-t)-O((t-k/n))\right)\frac{(n-2)!}{e}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{\lceil 1.1(t-k)\rceil}{e}-e\lceil 0.1(t-k)\rceil-O((t-k)/n)\right)\frac{(n-2)!}{e},

where we used
\ell\leq\lceil 1.1(t-k)\rceil
. This is at least
1.2(n-2)!
for
t\geq k+35.

Second, assume that
\ell\leq t-k-1
. In this case, we get that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})\cap\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|-X

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{t-\ell-1}{t-\ell-1\choose i}\frac{(n-i-1)!}{e}-\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose i}\frac{(n-i-1)!}{e}+l\frac{(n-2)!}{e^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq(t-k-\ell)\frac{(n-2)!}{e}+\ell\frac{(n-2)!}{e^{2}}.

.

This is also at least, say,
1.2(n-2)!
for
t\geq k+35.

Hence, for
t\geq k+35
we have

|\mathcal{E}_{k}|-|\mathcal{F}|\geq|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})\cap\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]|-X-|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\Delta}|-|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}|\geq 1.2(n-2)!-|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\Delta}|-|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}|>0,

since
|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}|\leq(n-2)!-(n-3)!
and
|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\Delta}|=|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{1}|\leq(n-11)!
. The theorem is proved in this case. Note that, we have the following bound on
\mathcal{F}
:

|\mathcal{F}|\leq|\mathcal{E}_{k}|-1.2(n-2)!+|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\Delta}|+|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}|.

Next, we deal with the case
t\leq k+35
. Consider
\sigma:=\bigcap\mathcal{S}_{2},|\sigma|=\ell^{\prime}
. W.l.o.g.
\sigma=\alpha_{t-\ell^{\prime}+1,t}
. Then
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\gamma}:=\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\gamma}\subset\Sigma[\alpha_{t-\ell^{\prime}+1,n}]
. At the same time,
|\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\gamma}|\geq k!-2(k-1)!>(k-1)!
by our bound on the remainder. It means that
(t-\ell^{\prime})>(k-1)
, hence
\ell^{\prime}\leq t-k
. Consider

\mathcal{S}_{2}(\sigma)=\{\pi\setminus\sigma\mid\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{2}\}\subset{[2,t]^{2}\setminus\sigma\choose\leq 1.1(t-k)-\ell^{\prime}}.

Note that it may be empty.

If
\mathcal{S}_{2}(\sigma)
is non-empty, then for any
x\in[2,t]^{2}\setminus\sigma
there is a partial permutation
\rho_{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}(\sigma)
such that
x\notin\rho_{x}
. By the same argument as in the Lemma
6
we have that the number of inclusion-minimal
i
-hitting sets of
\mathcal{S}_{2}(\sigma)
is at most
(1.1(t-k)-|\sigma|)^{i}\leq 38^{i}
for
i\geq 2
. Then
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\Delta}
cross-intersects
\mathcal{S}_{2}
either by cross-intersecting its common part
\sigma
, or by cross-intersecting
\mathcal{S}_{2}(\sigma)
. We bound the former part as in Case 2 by plugging
38
instead of
t
in
3.3
:

38^{2}\frac{(n-3)!}{e}+o((n-3)!)=O((n-3)!).

We could do the same calculations as above to bound the latter part, but now plugging the value
\ell^{\prime}=t-k
in the bounds above for the case
t\geq k+35
(it is easy to see that this value of
\ell^{\prime}
gives the worst bound). It actually makes
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{k})\cap\Sigma_{\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]
and
\Sigma_{\alpha_{t-\ell^{\prime}+1,t},\overline{\alpha_{t+1,n}}}[(1,1)]
coincide, we have

|\mathcal{F}|\leq|\Sigma_{n,\alpha_{k+1,n}}[(1,1)]|-(t-k)\frac{(n-2)!}{e^{2}}+(t-k)\frac{2(n-2)!}{e^{2}}+O((n-3)!)+|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\Delta}|+|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}|,

which gives

|\mathcal{E}_{k}|-|\mathcal{F}|\geq(t-k)\frac{(n-2)!}{e^{2}}-(t-k)\frac{2(n-3)!}{e^{2}}-O((n-3)!)+k!-(k-1)!-|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\Delta}|-|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\gamma}|-|\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\gamma}|>0,

when
|\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\gamma}|<\frac{(n-2)!}{100}
.

Hence, the remaining case is
|\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\gamma}|\geq\frac{(n-2)!}{100}
. Since
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\gamma}\subset\Sigma[\alpha_{t-\ell^{\prime}+1,n}]
, we must have

t-\ell^{\prime}+1\geq n-1
. Since
\mathcal{E}_{k}
,
k=2,\ldots,n-3
is the smallest for
k=n-3,
we may w.l.o.g. compare
|\mathcal{F}|
with
\mathcal{E}_{n-3}.

Recall that, using the inclusion-exclusion formula, we have

|\mathcal{E}_{n-3}|=\big(3(n-2)!-3(n-3)!+(n-4)!\big)+(n-3)!-(n-4)!=3(n-2)!-2(n-3)!

We bound
|\mathcal{F}|
using (
3.6
) as follows

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}|

\displaystyle\leq|\Sigma_{\alpha_{t-\ell^{\prime}+1,n}}[(1,1)]|-|\sigma|\left(\frac{(n-2)!}{e^{2}}-\frac{(n-3)!}{e^{2}}\right)+|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\Delta}|+|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}|

\displaystyle\leq\left((n-t+\ell^{\prime})(n-2)!-{n-t+\ell^{\prime}\choose 2}(n-3)!\right)-\ell^{\prime}\frac{(n-2)!}{e^{2}}+(n-2)!+O((n-3)!)

\displaystyle\leq\big(n-t+\ell^{\prime}+1-e^{-2}\ell^{\prime}\big)(n-2)!+O((n-3)!)

where in the second inequality we used the inclusion-exclusion formula for
|\Sigma_{\alpha_{t-\ell^{\prime}+1,n}}[(1,1)]|
, the fact that
n-t+\ell^{\prime}\leq 2
and the fact that
|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}|\leq(n-2)!-(n-3)!
.
Since
n-t+\ell^{\prime}\leq 2
, we have
n-t+1+\ell^{\prime}(1-e^{-2})\leq 3-e^{-2}
unless
t=n-2
and
\ell^{\prime}=0
. Unless the latter happens, we have
|\mathcal{F}|<|\mathcal{E}_{n-3}|
by comparing the coefficients in front of
(n-2)!
and, third,
\ell^{\prime}=0.

We are left with the last case when, first,
t=n-2,
that is, all permutations in
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}
have two common elements
(n-1,n-1)
and
(n,n)
, second,
|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}|\geq(n-2)!/100
. We may actually assume that

|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}|\geq\frac{99(n-2)!}{100},

since otherwise, substituting this improved bound on
|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}|
in the calculation above, we again get

|\mathcal{F}|=\big(n-t+\ell^{\prime}+99/100-e^{-2}\ell^{\prime}\big)(n-2)!+O((n-3)!)=\big(2+99/100\big)(n-2)!+O((n-3)!)<|\mathcal{E}_{n-3}|.

We claim that then all permutations from
\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}
must contain either
(n-1,n-1)
or
(n,n)
. This will conclude the proof, since then
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{E}_{n-2},
and we know that
|\mathcal{E}_{n-2}|=|\mathcal{E}_{n-3}|.

Assume that there is a permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}
such that
\pi(i)\neq i
for
i=n-1,n.
The number of permutations from
\Sigma[\overline{(1,1)},\alpha_{n-1,n}]
that do not intersect
\pi
is asymptotically the number of derangements on a set of size
n-2
, that is,
(1+o(1))(n-2)!/e
. All these permutations must be excluded from
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}
, which implies that
|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}|\leq(1-e^{-1}+o(1))(n-2)!
, a contradiction with the last displayed bound. This concludes the proof of the theorem.
