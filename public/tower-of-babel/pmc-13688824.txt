The role of salivary lactobacilli in early childhood caries increment
Authors: Yu Zhang, Yating Xu, Liangzhi Tu, Minyi Xu, Jingyu Zhan, Xiping Feng, Xi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough lactobacilli are frequently detected in children with early childhood caries (ECC), the roles of specific lactobacilli species remain unclear. Streptococcus mutans, a well-established cariogenic species, was included as a reference organism. Limosilactobacillus fermentum, Ligilactobacillus salivarius, Lactobacillus acidophilus, and Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus were selected in the study aiming to investigate the prospective associations between lactobacilli species and caries increment based on machine learning approaches.MethodsA prospective cohort of 282 children aged 3–4 years was established in Shanghai, China. Participants received clinical examinations at baseline and 1-year follow-up. Baseline saliva samples were collected. The detection and quantification of the targeted species in salivary samples were performed by quantitative real-time PCR. Logistic regression and machine-learning analyses were conducted to evaluate associations with caries increment.ResultsL. fermentum, L. salivarius, L. rhamnosus, and S. mutans were positively correlated to caries increment. L. fermentum was highly detectable (82.6%) in the saliva and significantly correlated with S. mutans. Furthermore, positive correlations existed between L. fermentum, and L. acidophilus, L. rhamnosus and L. salivarius. The ratio of L. salivarius to S. mutans was significantly correlated to caries increment. In the logistic regression model, caries experience and high levels of caries activity test were significantly associated with caries increment, whereas the associations between salivary bacterial abundance and caries increment were no longer statistically significant after adjustment. SHAP analysis based on light gradient boosting machine identified S. mutans and L. fermentum as the top important bacterial features contributing to the prediction of caries increment.ConclusionsSalivary detection and abundance of specific lactobacilli species were prospectively associated with caries increment in preschool children. Among the species examined, L. fermentum may contribute to the overall microbial predictive profile in machine-learning models rather than acting as an independent predictor of caries increment. The observed association involving L. salivarius and S. mutans suggests a potential ecological relationship that warrants further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09425-w.Keywords: Cohort study, Early childhood caries, Lactobacilli, Saliva, Streptococcus mutans

Introduction

Early childhood caries (ECC) represents the most prevalent condition that affects young children globally. ECC is often indicated when one or more deciduous tooth surfaces are either decayed, missing, or filled in a child by the age of 71 months [1]. At present, ECC has affected the oral health of nearly 1.76 billion children, causing a serious public health burden worldwide [2]. The Fourth National Oral Health Epidemiological Survey conducted in 2015 reported that the ECC prevalence in China was 50.8%, and 71.9% in 3-year-old and 5-year-old groups, respectively. Compared to the Third National Oral Health Epidemiological Survey data, the prevalence rate has increased by approximately 5.0% [3]. Once tooth decay begins, it cannot be reversed, and these children will remain at an increased risk of tooth demineralization and the development of new carious lesions, which may lead to loss of teeth.

In order to evaluate the ECC risk, several factors, including oral microbial count (e.g. salivary lactobacilli and Streptococcus mutans), saliva biochemistry (e.g. pH and buffering capacity), baseline status of caries, behaviors, oral hygiene status (e.g. visible plaque levels), socioeconomic status, and dietary factors (e.g. frequency of sugar intake), have been investigated either as single predictors or variables in multivariate models [4]. Nevertheless, forecasting future occurrences of ECC has been proven challenging. Furthermore, few studies have reported the successful oral microbiota-based prediction of future ECC outcomes, particularly for caries progression [5].

Oral lactobacilli have been reported to be associated with caries progression. Compared to the well-established cariogenic bacteria, S. mutans, less information is known about lactobacilli, especially regarding its dental caries-related characteristics, and relationship with the increment of ECC in children. In most clinical studies, the specific lactobacilli species remain unidentified [6]. Consequently, its association with dental caries and its ecological role in caries-related microbial communities have not been fully elucidated. In addition, a variety of lactobacilli species have been shown to exert protective and beneficial effects against other cariogenic bacteria, including S. mutans [7]. Therefore, it is crucial to differentiate among various lactobacilli species based on its cariogenic potential.

Previous culture-based and molecular studies have consistently identified a limited number of lactobacilli species in ECC-associated microbiota, among which Limosilactobacillus fermentum, Ligilactobacillus salivarius, and Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus are frequently detected in saliva, dental plaque, and carious lesions of children with ECC [8, 9]. Lactobacillus acidophilus has historically been one of the most extensively studied oral lactobacilli species because of its documented association with dental caries and its widespread use as a probiotic organism [10, 11]. These species, therefore, were chosen as target species in the present study to represent clinically relevant lactobacilli taxa with potentially different ecological roles in the oral cavity. Hereafter, the term “lactobacilli” is used to collectively refer to the four investigated species, following the 2020 taxonomic reclassification of the former genus Lactobacillus [12].

In the present study, a cohort design of 282 children for 12 months was employed to evaluate the role of four lactobacilli species (L. fermentum, L. salivarius, L. acidophilus, and L. rhamnosus) in the increment of caries lesions. Then, the salivary lactobacilli abundance was further compared between groups of children with and without caries increment during follow-up, and the associations between oral lactobacilli species, S. mutans, a range of caries risk factors, and the ECC of children were evaluated.

Materials and methods

Study population and design

The present report conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) regulations. The sample size was computed using a cohort formula with a power of 0.80 and an alpha of 0.05, and the proportions of 63.5% and 40.0%, respectively [13]. Considering a margin of data loss (10.0%), the minimum required sample size was 150.The guardians of the selected participants provided a signed informed consent before enrollment and the clinical oral examination of children.

The present study recruited a total of 302 children at baseline (age: 3–4 years old) between October 2023 and December 2023, from eight kindergartens in Shanghai (three kindergartens from the central region and five kindergartens from the suburbs). Participants who suffered from other oral or systemic diseases, or those who consumed antibiotics in the past three months were excluded. Professional dentists visited the selected kindergartens for clinical examination, caries evaluation, and sample collection. All guardians completed the survey questionnaire at baseline, including details on the demographics, oral health behaviors, and dietary habits (Supplementary material). All children underwent comprehensive clinical oral examinations and sample collection at the time of enrollment. At the 12-month follow-up, the children underwent oral examinations. The flow diagram and reasons for dropouts are presented in Fig. 1. The primary outcome of this study was caries increment, defined as an increase in the dmfs score (Δdmfs ≥ 1) during the 12-month follow-up period. Accordingly, the 282 participants who completed follow-up were classified into a caries increment group (CG, Δdmfs ≥ 1) and a no-caries-increment group (NG, Δdmfs = 0).

Clinical examination and sample collection

During the study period, the participants shared similar living settings, including the maintenance of identical oral health practices and dietary intake during the kindergarten’s daytime hours.

All clinical assessments and samples collection were carried out in the morning between 8:30 and 10:30 a.m. ECC examinations were performed under artificial white lighting using the Community Periodontal Index (CPI) “ballpoint” periodontal probe and standard dental mirror to record the following indices: decayed, missing, and filled surface (dmfs), and decayed, missing, and filled tooth (dmft). In addition, the plaque index (PLI) was calculated. The Cariostat kit (GangDa Medical Technology Co. Ltd., Beijing, China) was used to conduct the caries activity test (CAT), according to manufacturer’s instructions. The facial surfaces of the mandibular incisors and maxillary molars were gently scrubbed (3–5 times) using sterile cotton swabs, which were immersed into ampules that contained 2.5 ml of culture medium. All samples were placed in an incubator at a constant temperature of 37 °C for 48 ± 4 h. Using the standard color chart, one examiner analyzed the color of the culture medium under natural light, according to manufacturer’s instructions, and recorded the caries activity index scores (0 to 3.0): scores within 0–1.0 were categorized as low caries activity, indicating low caries risk; a score of 1.5 was classified as medium caries activity, indicating medium caries risk; scores within 2.0–3.0 were categorized as high caries activity, indicating high caries risk [14, 15].

The same dentist performed the corresponding examination for the initial and follow-up visits, who received prior training and calibration through training courses and clinical-epidemiological practice, following the World Health Organization’s manual for epidemiological surveys [16]. In order to evaluate the intra-examiner reliability, randomly chosen participants (10.0%) were reexamined. The Cohen’s kappa (κ) value for all clinical measurements was 0.85 to 0.95, demonstrating good reliability.

At two hours before saliva collection, all subjects were requested to refrain from drinking or consuming any food. Unstimulated saliva was obtained by instructing the participants to drool 2–3 mL of saliva into a sterile 5-mL tube with the funnel. Then, the collected saliva specimens were placed on ice, and transferred to a freezer (−80 °C) within three hours for storage, until further experimentation.

DNA extraction and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was conducted to identify and analyze certain species in salivary specimens. The unstimulated whole saliva (2 mL) was treated with lysis buffer (2 mL), which contained 20 mM of Tris (pH: 8.0), 1.2% Triton X-100, 2 mM of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), and 20 mg/mL of lysozyme for cell lyses. Then, this was incubated for 30 min in a water bath at 37 °C. Afterwards, DNA extraction was carried out using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood & Tissue DNA kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), according to manufacturer’s guidelines. Next, the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to analyze the purity and concentration of DNA (A260 nm/A280 nm), according to manufacturer’s guidelines, with a purity threshold of 1.8 or higher for further amplification. Then, DNA integrity was evaluated by electrophoresis in ultraviolet light. The extracted DNA specimens were kept at −80 °C prior to subsequent experiments.

The standard plasmids and primers were designed from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) library. All primer sequences utilized for the qPCR are presented in Supplementary Table 1. The qPCR assays were conducted using a 20 μL aliquot of reaction mixture (3 μl of extracted DNA from the saliva sample, 10 μl of SYBR Green Master Mix [Yeasen, China], 6.2 μL of nuclease-free water, and 0.4 μL of forward and reverse primers). The qPCR parameters were, as follows: 120 s at 95 °C for pre-denaturation, followed by 40 cycles of 10 s at 95 °C for denaturation, and 30 s at 60 °C for annealing/extension. Serial dilutions from 4 × 10–4 to 4 × 10–8 ng (tenfold) of plasmids were utilized to quantify the targeted species. For each primer set, standard DNA amplification curves and a melting-point product curve were obtained. The experiments were performed for at least three times using the SYBR Green Master Mix to quantify the mean values of the cycle threshold (Ct) values. The Ct values from the amplification of extracted DNA samples were plotted to generate the standard curves, and the absolute concentration of bacteria in saliva was thereby determined. Samples with Ct values below the detection limit of the DNA standard curves were regarded as negative for the corresponding bacterial species.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (v20; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism (v.5.0; GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Significance level was defined at 5.0%. Descriptive statistics were adopted for the sociodemographic features and clinical measurements of children. Student’s t-test was used to explore the significance of the differences in continuous variables in both groups. Chi-squared test and Fisher’s test were employed to determine the differences in categorical or hierarchical variables between the two involved groups, such as the comparison of the proportion of bacteria detected. The correlation of bacterial counts, and the newly developed dmft and dmfs was evaluated by Spearman’s correlation. Furthermore, Python 3.6 (www.Python.org) was used to process the machine learning (ML) of data. The data were divided into two sets (training set and testing set) at a ratio of 7:3. ML algorithms, which included random forest (RF), light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), and adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), were employed to construct the prediction models using the training set, and these were validated using the testing set [17–19]. To identify the optimal classifier and enhance the model accuracy, a fivefold cross-validation technique was applied to the training set, which tuned the hyperparameters during training, and prevented overfitting. It is noteworthy that data from the testing set were not utilized during the training phase. The bacterial concentration data were standardized using the logarithmic function and Z-score. Model performance was evaluated using discrimination metrics, including the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, the area under the curve (AUC) analysis, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1 score. The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of the AUC were calculated by bootstrapping, using 1000 iterations. In addition, the Shapley values for the most performing predictive model were calculated using the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) algorithm, aiming to interpret the contribution of each variable to the outcome prediction [20].

Results

General characteristics of participants

Among the 302 participants, 20 children were excluded due to transfer to other schools (n = 6), and absence during the follow-up survey (n = 14) (Fig. 1). Thus, children who finished the follow-up (n = 282) were considered for the data analysis. At baseline, 135 of 282 children (47.9%) were female (mean age: 3.5 ± 0.3 years old). Furthermore, at baseline, the mean dmfs and dmft was 2.2 ± 6.8 and 1.2 ± 2.7, respectively, with 209 caries-free children (74.1%). This increased to 3.2 ± 8.0 and 1.6 ± 3.0, respectively, at follow-up, with 172 caries-free children (61.0%).

Considering the variations in caries state for 12 months, the 282 children were classified into two groups: CG, with caries increment (Δdmfs ≥ 1); NG, without caries increment (Δdmfs = 0). A total of 77 (27.3%) children had a new increment of dental caries over the last 12 months. The Δdmfs was significantly correlated to the baseline caries experience estimated (p < 0.001).

There were no statistical differences between the caries increment rate and gender (p = 0.444). Furthermore, the PLI was significantly higher in children in the CG, when compared to the NG, regardless of whether at baseline (0.73 ± 0.38 vs. 0.60 ± 0.40, p = 0.016) or follow-up (0.71 ± 0.39 vs. 0.38 ± 0.40, p < 0.001). Moreover, children with new caries were more likely to be at high risk, based on the CAT (p < 0.001). Table 1 presents the main sociodemographic and behavioral information of the study sample. Children in the CG had a significantly lower rate of fluoride toothpaste use (p = 0.018).

Cariogenic bacteria detection and caries status

For the qPCR analysis at baseline, the bacterial concentration data was standardized using a logarithmic function. L. fermentum, L. salivarius, L. acidophilus, L. rhamnosus, and S. mutans were detected in 233 (82.6%), 128 (45.4%), 225 (79.8%), 125 (44.3%) and 229 (81.2%) subjects, respectively. Next, the correlations of bacterial abundances between these species in saliva were analyzed (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table 2). Significant positive correlations were identified between S. mutans and L. fermentum (p < 0.001), and S. mutans and L. rhamnosus (p = 0.024). Interestingly, L. fermentum was also positively correlated to L. acidophilus (p = 0.009), L. rhamnosus (p < 0.001), and L. salivarius (p < 0.001).

In the saliva obtained from 77 children in the CG (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Table 3), it was found that L. fermentum was positively correlated to S. mutans (p < 0.001), L. acidophilus (p = 0.004) and L. rhamnosus (p < 0.001). Notably, in the 205 children without new caries, significant negative correlations were found between L. acidophilus and S. mutans (p = 0.024; Fig. 2D and Supplementary Table 4).

To further evaluate the associations between salivary bacterial abundance and caries increment, logistic regression analyses were performed (Supplementary Table 5). In the logistic regression model, baseline dmfs (OR = 1.113, 95% CI: 1.039–1.193, p = 0.002) and high CAT level (OR = 4.162, 95% CI: 1.551–11.167, p = 0.005) were significantly associated with caries increment. Notably, compared with children who used fluoride toothpaste, those who did not use fluoride toothpaste had significantly higher odds of caries increment (OR = 2.485, 95% CI: 1.096–5.636, p = 0.029). However, after adjustment for these factors, the associations between salivary bacterial abundance and caries increment were attenuated and were not statistically significant (all p > 0.05).

Co-detection of lactobacilli species and S. mutans

The Venn and upset diagrams revealed the following bacterial co-detection information (Fig. 3A and B): 209 (74.1%) children were co-detected with both S. mutans and L. fermentum, 113 (40.1%) children were co-detected with both S. mutans and L. salivarius, 186 (66.0%) children were co-detected with both S. mutans and L. acidophilus, and 112 (39.7%) children were co-detected with both S. mutans and L. rhamnosus. In the 77 children with new caries, the co-detection of S. mutans with L. fermentum, L. salivarius, L. acidophilus, and L. rhamnosus was detected in 53 (68.8%), 34 (44.2%), 49 (63.6%) and 31 (40.3%) children, respectively (Fig. 3C). For the 205 children in the NG, the co-detection of S. mutans with L. fermentum, L. salivarius, L. acidophillus and L. rhamnosus was detected in 156 (76.1%), 79 (38.5%), 137 (66.8%) and 81 (39.5%) children, respectively (Fig. 3D). Next, a series of new caries classification models was established from each single species, as well as two-species (lactobacilli and S. mutans). The AUC value for models derived from single lactobacilli species of L. fermentum, L. acidophilus, L. salivarius and L. rhamnosus was 0.55, 0.57, 0.52 and 0.54, respectively. All four two-species models built with lactobacilli and S. mutans demonstrated a relatively higher ECC predictive power, when compared to the single lactobacilli models (Figs. 3E-3H).

In order to further understand the role of lactobacilli species in ECC, the ratio of the four lactobacilli species to S. mutans, which is a recognized pathogen, was calculated using bacterial concentration data, and the correlation between the ratios and Δdmfs index was analyzed. The ratio of L. salivarius to S. mutans was significantly higher in children in the CG, when compared to the NG (p = 0.027, Supplementary Table 6), and this ratio was positively correlated to Δdmfs (p < 0.001, Supplementary Table 7). There were no statistical differences or correlations in other species ratios in the present study.

Weights of lactobacilli species in the caries increment prediction model

The weight of lactobacilli species at caries increment was estimated using RF, LightGBM and Adaboost (Fig. 4A). The LightGBM model performed the best among the three ML algorithms. The optimal decision threshold was determined to be 0.503 through the ROC curve under fivefold cross-validation, with an AUC value of 0.73 (95% CI: 0.59–0.87, Fig. 4B), an accuracy rate of 0.76, a sensitivity rate (recall) of 0.39, a specificity rate of 0.90, a precision rate of 0.60, and an F1 score of 0.47, indicating good discriminatory ability. The LightGBM SHAP summary plot was illustrated to clarify the characteristics that affected the probability of ECC progression (Fig. 4C). Among the five leading predictors, the SHAP algorithm indicated S. mutans and L. fermentum concentrations in saliva as the top important bacterial features, followed by L. salivarius. The weight of the concentration ratio for the four lactobacilli species to S. mutans was also estimated by the three ML models (Fig. 4D). The LightGBM model perfo rmed the best, with an AUC value of 0.70 (95% CI: 0.53–0.85, Fig. 4E). The ratio of L. salivarius to S. mutans demonstrated maximal contribution to caries progression in the LightGBM-based SHAP analysis (Fig. 4F).

Discussion

The present study explored the role of lactobacilli species in caries increment by developing and testing caries increment prediction models that considered various parameters, including psychosocial behavior, socioeconomic status, and clinical predictors, using data collected in a 12-month follow-up cohort study.

Several lactobacilli species and S. mutans were associated with an increase in caries in the present study. S. mutans has been implicated as a key feature in the pathogenesis of caries [21]. However, it is important to note that not all subjects with caries had detectable levels of S. mutans, clearly demonstrating that the presence of other acid-producing bacteria leads to dental caries [22]. Numerous studies have demonstrated the strong acidogenic capacity of lactobacilli. Meanwhile, these are capable of attuning to fluctuating conditions and survive for prolonged time in a low pH environment along with S. mutans [23]. Furthermore, previous studies have explained the characteristics of lactobacilli in ECC, but most of these studies focused on demonstrating the general features, rather than clarifying specific species [24]. Moreover, the relationship between lactobacilli and ECC may be species-specific.

The present study revealed that L. fermentum was highly detectable in the saliva of children, achieving a positive rate of 82.6%. Furthermore, a significant positive correlation was found between L. fermentum and S. mutans across all participants. Moreover, L. fermentum had a strong correlation with Δdmfs. S. mutans had the highest predictive weight in the LightGBM-based prediction model for caries increment, while L. fermentum ranked as the second most significant microbial feature in the machine learning model. These findings suggest that L. fermentum is associated with caries increment, representing a candidate microbial feature for ECC risk assessment, and may share ecological relationships with S. mutans. However, this association was not retained after adjustment for established caries-risk factors in the logistic regression model, indicating that the observed association may be partly influenced by the overall caries-risk environment, which is consistent with the ecological plaque hypothesis [25]. Emerging evidence has suggested that L. fermentum is often isolated from the dentitions of children with caries, and is associated with the severity of caries, which agrees with the findings of the present study [9, 13]. Notably, nearly half of the L. fermentum oral genomes were recorded to contain glucosyltransferase genes (gtfs). This species has been reported to exhibit type-I collagen binding activity, enabling L. fermentum to sustain colonization in exposed, collagen-rich dentin caries, and continuously ferment various carbohydrates, which may facilitate its persistence in dentin caries lesions [26]. Yet, no individual bacterial species has been proven beyond doubt to be a necessary and sufficient cause of ECC [27]. The ecological plaque hypothesis states that dental caries arises from the collective action of multiple oral microorganisms [28]. Interestingly, the present study revealed that positive correlations existed between L. fermentum and L. acidophilus, as well as L. rhamnosus and L. salivarius (all, p < 0.05). L. fermentum showed multiple positive correlations with other lactobacilli species, suggesting a potentially important position within the observed salivary microbial network, which warrants further investigation, since few studies have mentioned these.

Our findings demonstrated a positive association between L. salivarius and the progression of dental caries. Interestingly, although the association was attenuated after adjustment for established caries-risk factors, L. salivarius remained one of the most influential microbial features in the LightGBM model, suggesting that it may contribute to the overall predictive microbial profile rather than acting as an independent risk factor. These results aligned with some epidemiological studies that attempted to characterize the oral microbiota in ECC children and identify L. salivarius as a caries-related bacterium [6, 29, 30]. In addition, in vitro studies indicated L. salivarius is one of the strongest acid producers among lactobacilli species, consistent with its observed association with caries increment in the present study [31]. Given that S. mutans is a well-recognized culprit pathogen of dental caries and the progression of dental caries often involves the imbalanced distribution of different species in the microbial community, we further assess the relationship between ECC and the proportion of the pathogen. An interesting finding of the present study is that the ratio of L. salivarius to S. mutans emerged as an important predictive feature for caries increment. Our results demonstrated that the ratio of L. salivarius to S. mutans was significantly higher in children in the CG, when compared to the NG, and that this ratio was positively correlated to Δdmfs. Furthermore, this ratio revealed the maximal contribution to prediction of new carious lesions in the LightGBM-based model. These results further suggest a potential ecological relationship between L. salivarius and S. mutans in the oral community. The current oral biofilm paradigm posits that S. mutans and other acidogenic pathogens create a niche that can mechanically retain lactobacilli [9]. Despite these intriguing observations, there is a notable scarcity of studies that directly investigated the potential ecological relationship between L. salivarius and S. mutans. Future experimental studies are required to determine whether direct biological interactions exist between L. salivarius and S. mutans, which is a possibility raised by the present analytical results. However, it should also be noted that L. salivarius includes strains that have been widely investigated as oral probiotics, acting as a good complement to individual preventive methods in preschool children [32].

In the present study, L. rhamnosus was detected in 44.3% of children and its salivary abundance was positively associated with caries increment during the 12-month follow-up. These findings indicate that L. rhamnosus may be associated with caries progression. Previous studies have consistently found that the high load of L. rhamnosus in the oral cavity of children with dental caries [33, 34]. Li et al. pointed out that among children with severe ECC, the detection rate of L. rhamnosus ranked second among lactobacilli species [35]. While certain L. rhamnosus strains have been associated with caries-related microbial communities, others, especially the LGG strain, have been widely investigated as probiotics and reduce caries [36, 37]. Therefore, the positive association observed in this study should not be generalized to all L. rhamnosus strains, highlighting the importance of strain-level investigations in future studies.

The present study identified positive associations between several lactobacilli species and caries increment, whereas different patterns were observed for L. acidophilus. In contrast, some strains of L. acidophilus were considered probiotics and may contribute to oral microbial homeostasis. Experimental studies reported that the metabolic byproducts of certain strains of L. acidophilus decrease the biomass production and survival of S. mutans, thereby creating a protective barrier to cariogenic bacteria [38]. The present study revealed the negative association between L. acidophilus and S. mutans in the NG, preliminarily suggesting a possible ecological relationship between L. acidophilus and S. mutans. Although some previous studies have reported the negative association between L. acidophilus and dental caries, its exact role in caries progression remains controversial and ambiguous [11]. Thus, there is a need for further longitudinal, mechanistic, and intervention studies to clarify the specific ecological role of L. acidophilus in the progression of dental caries.

The present study constructed prediction models for ECC using data gathered from a one-year follow-up cohort study. Numerous ML algorithms were implemented, and LightGBM demonstrated the best performance. These promising results indicate that the LightGBM model holds substantial potential in caries increment prediction. While previous studies have been carried out to link the microbiome with ECC, our current work further extends this line of inquiry by incorporating both microbial and traditional predictors, including some well-known factors, such as previous caries experience and the infectious level of S. mutans [17, 39]. In this study, the SHAP algorithm was employed to evaluate the weight of each feature, representing its contribution to predicting ECC. The Cariostat test was the top significant predictor of the increment of new caries lesions in children, agreeing with the reports of other researchers [40]. In addition, although none of the bacterial variables remained independently associated with caries increment in the adjusted logistic regression model, the SHAP analysis consistently identified L. fermentum and L. salivarius as important contributors to model performance. This discrepancy may reflect differences in the analytical objectives of the two approaches. Machine-learning approaches could capture and identify complex patterns and interactions among predictors that may not be specified a priori in traditional regression models [41]. Therefore, variables identified as important contributors in predictive models should not necessarily be interpreted as independent risk factors. In a word, this machine-learning model could help identify microbial and clinical features related to ECC increment, even when these bacteria do not emerge as independent risk factors in conventional regression analyses. Such an approach may support the potential value of integrating salivary microbial information with established clinical indicators for caries-increment risk assessment.

To the best of our knowledge, our study was one of the few prospective studies to investigate the associations between specific lactobacilli species and caries increment while simultaneously evaluating their predictive value using machine-learning approaches and logistic regression models. These findings provide further insights into microbial features associated with caries increment. However, combined with logistic regression analysis, these findings should be interpreted as prospective salivary associations and predictive features rather than independent risk factors for caries increment.

However, as limitations, bacterial detection was performed only at baseline, precluding assessment of temporal microbial changes during follow-up. In addition, bacterial measurements were obtained from saliva rather than dental plaque or carious lesions. Ideally, the findings should be validated using supragingival plaque samples, plaque from active lesions, or dentinal caries samples. Finally, the observational nature of this study precludes causal inference. Further longitudinal investigations with repeated sampling in diverse parts of the oral cavity and mechanistic studies are needed to clarify the biological roles of these bacterial species in ECC progression.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the salivary detection and abundance of specific lactobacilli species were prospectively associated with caries increment in preschool children. However, these bacterial associations were attenuated and were no longer statistically significant after adjustment for established caries-risk factors. Among the species examined, L. fermentum contributed to the overall microbial predictive profile in the machine-learning models rather than being an independent predictor of caries increment. In addition, the observed association between L. salivarius and cariogenic S. mutans suggests a potential ecological relationship that warrants further investigation, while the inverse association between L. acidophilus and S. mutans may indicate distinct ecological characteristics of this species. The interspecific interaction mechanisms in lactobacilli warrant exploration through future longitudinal and mechanistic studies.
