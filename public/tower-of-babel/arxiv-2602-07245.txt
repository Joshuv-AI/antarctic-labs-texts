Computing with Continued Logarithms
Michael J. Collins

Abstract
Gosper developed an algorithm for performing arithmetic on continued fractions (CFs), and introduced continued logarithms (CLs) as a variant of continued fractions better suited to representing extremely large (or small) numbers. CLs are also well-suited to efficient hardware implementation. Here we present the algorithm for arithmetic on CLs, then extend it to the novel contribution of this paper, an algorithm for computing trigonometric, exponential, and log functions on CLs. These methods can be extended to other transcendental functions. As with the corresponding CF algorithms, computations are entirely in the domain of the CL representation, with no floating-point arithmetic; we read one CL input term at a time, producing the next CL term of the result as soon as it is determined. The CL algorithms are in fact simpler than their CF counterparts. We have implemented these algorithms in Haskell.

1.1
Dynamical System Representation

There is another way of writing CLs which is trivially equivalent, but better suited to the computational algorithms of this paper.
We can define a dynamical system on

g(x)=\begin{cases}x/2&\mbox{if }x\geq 2\\
\frac{1}{x-1}&\mbox{if }1<x<2\\
\mbox{terminate}&\mbox{if }x=1\end{cases}

We now consider how to do arithmetic on CLs. The algorithm is implicit in

How would we, for example, derive the continued logarithm
[z_{0},z_{1},\cdots]=\pi/e=1.155727\cdots

We have now read enough terms to imply

, and it is easy to observe that the above expression must lie between 1 and 2 whenever
[p_{2},\cdots]
and
[e_{2},\cdots]
are both greater than 1; so the first term of

is

, and

[z_{1},z_{2},\cdots]=\left(\frac{4[p_{2},\cdots][e_{2},\cdots]+2[e_{2},\cdots]}{2[p_{2},\cdots][e_{2},\cdots]+[p_{2},\cdots]+2[e_{2},\cdots]+1}-1\right)^{-1}

i.e.

[z_{1},z_{2},\cdots]=\frac{2[p_{2},\cdots][e_{2},\cdots]+[p_{2},\cdots]+2[e_{2},\cdots]+1}{2[p_{2},\cdots][e_{2},\cdots]-[p_{2},\cdots]-1}

This is not sufficient to determine

and
[e_{2},\cdots]
independently range from one to infinity.
So we must make further substitutions, beginning with
[p_{2},\cdots]=[0,p_{3},\cdots]=(1+\frac{1}{[p_{3},\cdots]})
and so on.

The key idea of the arithmetic algorithm is that, as we continue to make such substitutions, the still-undetermined output terms

are always given by an expression of the form

M(x,y)=\frac{axy+bx+cy+d}{exy+fx+gy+h}

and

are integers. Such an expression is called

, and can be
identified with the matrix
M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b&c&d\\
e&f&g&h\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. It is straightforward

Transcendental Functions on Continued Logarithms

We now extend the ideas of the arithmetic algorithm to compute
\sin,\arcsin,\exp

\log

3.1
Exponentials

We first consider how to obtain the CL terms of

e^{x}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{k}}{k!}

where
x
itself is given as a continued logarithm. We will assume
0\leq x\leq 1
; otherwise we can use the identity
e^{x}=(e^{x/2})^{2}
. Let

M^{x}_{n}(y)=1+\frac{xy}{n}\ .

(8)

This is a bihomographic expression with matrix
M_{n}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&0&n\\
0&0&0&n\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
The
n^{th}
degree Taylor approximation

1+x+x^{2}/2+\cdots+x^{n}/{n!}
can be written using Horner’s rule as

1+x\left(1+\frac{x}{2}\left(1+\frac{x}{3}\left(1+\cdots\left(1+\frac{x}{n}\right)\right)\right)\cdots\right)=M^{x}_{1}(M^{x}_{2}(\cdots M^{x}_{n}(1)\cdots))

so we define

y_{n}(x)=1+\frac{x}{n}+\frac{x^{2}}{n(n+1)}+\cdots=\lim_{m\to\infty}M^{x}_{n}(M^{x}_{n+1}(\cdots M^{x}_{m}(1)\cdots))\ .

(9)

Now
y_{n}(x)=M^{x}_{n}(y_{n+1}(x))
, and
y_{1}(x)=e^{x}
.
Since
x\leq 1
we have

y_{n}(x)\leq 1+\frac{e^{x}-1}{n}<1+\frac{2}{n}\ .

For
n\geq 2
this gives
y_{n}(x)<2
, so the first CL term of
y_{n}(x)
is zero. Furthermore

\frac{1}{y_{n}(x)-1}>\frac{n}{2}

so the initial zero term is followed by
\lfloor\log_{2}(n)-1\rfloor
ones. We get the subsequent terms of
y_{n}(x)
by applying the arithmetic algorithm to

\hat{M_{n}}(x,y_{n+1}(x))

where
\hat{M_{n}}
is
\mbox{prod}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n)-1\rfloor}_{1}(\mbox{prod}_{0}(M_{n}))
, with
\mbox{prod}_{0},\mbox{prod}_{1}
as defined in section
2
.
The terms of
y_{n+1}(x)
are obtained recursively in the same manner.

3.1.1
Termination

We have seen that we can apply the arithmetic algorithm to an infinitely nested expression like
9
,
because we always know the first few terms of
y_{n}(x)
without recursing into
y_{n+1}(x)
.
It remains to show that we will in fact obtain every term of
y_{n}(x)
eventually.
In other words we must show that, given any
\varepsilon>0
,
we eventually confine
y_{n}(x)
to an interval of radius

.
From

we see that we will have

K-\varepsilon<y_{n}(x)<K+\varepsilon

for some

if, for some

,

K^{\prime}-\frac{n\varepsilon}{x}<y_{n+1}(x)<K^{\prime}+\frac{n\varepsilon}{x}

and thus if

K^{\prime\prime}-\frac{n(n+1)\cdots(n+r-1)\varepsilon}{x^{r}}<y_{n+r}(x)<K^{\prime\prime}+\frac{n(n+1)\cdots(n+r-1)\varepsilon}{x^{r}}

for some

. Since
\frac{n(n+1)\cdots(n+r-1)}{x^{r}}
approaches infinity,
we will eventually get the required bound from the known initial terms of
y_{n+r}(x)
,
and will not need to recurse into
y_{n+r+1}(x)

(assuming we have also ingested enough terms of
x
to get a sufficiently tight bound on

).

Similar observations apply to computation of the other transcendental functions we now consider.

3.2
Inverse Trigonometric Functions

We can apply a similar approach to

\frac{\arcsin(x)}{x}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2n)!}{4^{n}(n!)^{2}(2n+1)}x^{2n}=1+\frac{x^{2}}{6}+\frac{3x^{4}}{40}+\cdots

with
|x|\leq 1
. Let
t_{n}=\frac{(2n)!}{4^{n}(n!)^{2}(2n+1)}
and

M^{x}_{n}(y)=M_{n}(x,y)=1+\frac{t_{n}}{t_{n-1}}xy=1+\frac{(2n-1)^{2}}{2n(2n+1)}xy

i.e.
M_{n}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}(2n-1)^{2}&0&0&2n(2n+1)\\
0&0&0&2n(2n+1)\end{smallmatrix}\right)

Let

and define

a_{n}(w)=M^{w}_{n}(M^{w}_{n+1}(\cdots))=1+\frac{t_{n}}{t_{n-1}}w+\frac{t_{n+1}}{t_{n-1}}w^{2}+\cdots

so

and
a_{n}(w)=M^{w}_{n}(a_{n+1}(w))

1+\frac{(2n-1)^{2}}{2n(2n+1)}w\leq a_{n}(w)\leq 1+\frac{(2n-1)^{2}w}{2n(2n+1)(1-w)}\ .

The lower bound is always between 1 and 2. If the upper bound is less than 2, let
[p_{0},p_{1}\cdots p_{k}]=[0,p_{1}\cdots p_{k}]

.

The rest of

is obtained by applying the arithmetic algorithm to

is obtained by starting with

If the upper bound is greater than 2, then (

Trigonometric Functions

The cosine

\cos(x)=1-\frac{x^{2}}{2!}+\frac{x^{4}}{4!}-\cdots=1-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\left(1-\frac{x^{2}}{12}\left(1-\frac{x^{2}}{30}\left(1+\cdots\right.\right.\right.

3.4
Logarithms

To compute

we use the series

\log(x)=2\left(\left(\frac{x-1}{x+1}\right)+\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{x-1}{x+1}\right)^{3}+\frac{1}{5}\left(\frac{x-1}{x+1}\right)^{5}\cdots+\right)

g(w)=1+\frac{w}{3}+\frac{w^{2}}{5}+\frac{w^{3}}{7}+\cdots=1+\frac{w}{3}\left(1+\frac{3w}{5}\left(1+\frac{5w}{7}\left(1+\cdots\right.\right.\right.\ .

The series for

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2n-1&0&0&2n+1\\

Define

g_{n}(w)=1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2n-1}{2n+2k-1}w^{k}=\lim_{m\to\infty}M_{(n,w)}(M_{(n+1,w)}(\cdots M_{(m,w)}(1)\cdots))\ .

Then
g_{1}(w)=g(w)
, and
g_{n}(w)=M^{w}_{n}(g_{n+1}(w))
. Since

is nonnegative, we have

1\leq g_{n}(w)\leq 1+\frac{2n-1}{2n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w^{k}=1+\frac{2n-1}{2n+1}\frac{w}{1-w}\ .

(13)

If

\frac{w}{1-w}\leq\frac{1}{8}
, so

4
Termination

As noted previously, the arithmetic algorithm is not guaranteed to produce an output term after reading a finite amount of input. Arithmetic on continued fractions has precisely the same problem. In
collins2025transcendentalfunctionscontinuedfractions
, we addressed the problem by extending the internal representation of the continued fraction data type to include upper and lower bounds on
M(x,y)
; we include these bounds as part of the output, and nested arithmetic can use such bounds on
x
and
y
to determine the next bound on
M(x,y)
. When we obtain
n-\varepsilon<M(x,y)<n+\varepsilon
, and
\varepsilon
is less than a specified error threshold, we consider the next term of the CF to be
n
. But we retain the bounds, in case subsequent computations require greater precision.

Such an approach is less appealing with continued logarithms, since part of the motivation for studying CLs is their amenability to very compact hardware implementation; manipulating arbitrary rational bounds adds considerable complexity to the prod/con manipulations defined in section
2
. One option might be to have a parallel error channel which detects and reports stalled computations.
