Effective electromagnetic Lagrangians in the derivative expansion method
R. Martínez von Dossow, Luis F. Urrutia

Abstract
We calculate the effective electromagnetic Lagrangian up to the lowest-order corrections in the derivatives for two fermionic systems of interest in condensed matter physics in the linearized approximation of the tight-binding Hamiltonian near the Fermi level in the Brillouin zone: (i) the ( 3 + 1 ) (3+1) description of the simplest Weyl semimetal and (ii) the massive ( 2 + 1 ) (2+1) electrodynamics, which can serve as a model for the interface between two ( 3 + 1 ) (3+1) topological insulators. We employ the derivative expansion method which directly provides local effective Lagrangians and allows selecting from the outset both the powers of the electromagnetic potential to be considered together with the number of relevant derivatives. We find new higher-order derivative corrections to Carroll-Field-Jackiw electrodynamics. In general, the new terms we find either have a similar structure or constitute a relativistic generalization of some recent phenomenological proposals found in the literature. In this way, they should be incorporated into these proposals for assessing the relative significance of all the terms included up to a given order.



I
Introduction

The rise of topological materials exhibiting a Dirac-like behavior of their energy bands in regions close to the Fermi energy in the Brillouin zone has fostered a revival of many techniques already developed in
quantum field theory, in order to obtain their electromagnetic response codified in an effective action. The starting point is the condensed matter tight-binding Hamiltonian for the fermionic excitations, coupled to an external electromagnetic field, and linearized around the Fermi energy in the Brillouin zone.
This coupled Hamiltonian can be embedded in a Dirac-like theory similar to that described in the fermion-photon sector of the Standard Model Extension (SME)
[
1
,
2
]
, designed to study Lorentz and CPT violations in high-energy physics. The electromagnetic effective action is subsequently obtained by “integrating” the fermion fields, which yields corrections to the standard Maxwell’s equations that encode the electromagnetic response of the material.

Besides the intrinsic interest in effective electromagnetic Lagrangians, additional motivation to investigate higher-order derivative contributions to them stems from recent publications introducing phenomenological additions of this kind to well-studied systems such as axion electrodynamics
[
3
]
and
2+1
Maxwell-Chern-Simons (MCS) electrodynamics
[
4
]
, for example. The first reference considers the extension of electrodynamics to a medium with constitutive relations
\mathbf{D}=\epsilon\mathbf{E}+\psi(x)\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2}\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{H}=\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{B}-\psi(x)\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2}\mathbf{E}
, dubbed
\psi
-electrodynamics, and studies its realization together with its possible applications in metamaterials. In reference
[
4
]
an extra relativistic term proportional to
\xi\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}A_{\mu}\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2}F_{\nu\rho}
is added to the MCS Lagrangian to account for the Hall viscosity
\xi
of the system, which plays a fundamental role in elucidating the topological electromagnetic phases of matter. Originally, the viscosity parameter was introduced as a geometric response of the energy-momentum tensor arising from deformations in the underlying metric of the fluid
[
5
]
. Later it was shown that for Galilean invariant quantum Hall states the odd contribution to the viscosity tensor (yielding the Hall viscosity) appears in the electromagnetic response of the material as a momentum expansion in the conductivity
[
6
,
7
]
. For a review, see, for example Ref.
[
8
]
. As anticipated, contributions of this type naturally emerge in higher-order derivative corrections within the framework of the effective action. This is what motivates our interest to investigate some of these contributions in order to compare them with those introduced in the cited references.

There is an ample variety of methods to deal with the determination of the electromagnetic response
S_{\rm eff}(A)
of fermionic systems, and each of them has its own advantages and drawbacks, especially regarding the coordinate dependence of the external electromagnetic fields.

For example, many calculations in condensed matter directly focus on the resulting current, which is obtained either via the Kubo linear response theory
[
9
]
or by a semiclassical Boltzmann approach including an anomalous velocity which takes care of the topological characteristics of the material
[
10
]
. These methods are perturbative and start directly from the electromagnetic fields, thus being gauge invariant
ab initio
. Nevertheless, they are mostly restricted to constant (slowly varying) or harmonic time-dependent fields.

The quantum field theory-motivated calculations yielding an effective action usually start from a Dirac-like action coupled to the electromagnetic field via the electromagnetic potentials. In this way, gauge invariance is not manifest, and it is mandatory to make it explicit at the end of the calculation.
These effective actions are known to all orders in the
electromagnetic fields only in a restricted family of backgrounds, such as constant fields or plane wave fields
[
11
]
,
to name some well-known cases. For a review, see, for
example,
[
12
]
. The extension to the case of nonhomogeneous electromagnetic fields has remained a subject of investigation and several advances have been reported
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
. Also, further developments on non-perturbative methods have received much attention recently. A detailed review of this topic, emphasizing advances from the last decade, is presented in Ref.
[
18
]
. Among the new theoretical tools providing an alternative to standard Feynman diagram calculations, the semiclassical worldline instanton method, deriving from the “first quantized”
approach to field theory, has proven particularly useful in
calculating effective actions and related quantities in
the study of QED processes in external fields
[
19
,
20
]
.

In this work, we employ one of the many versions of the so-called derivative expansion (DE) method , which provides an alternative approach for calculating effective Lagrangians in the low-energy limit as an expansion in derivatives of the involved fields.
Our focus is on the electromagnetic interaction of fermions
\Psi
with the electromagnetic field
A_{\mu}
through a generalized coupling

e{\bar{\Psi}}\Gamma^{\mu}A_{\mu}\Psi
. The early version of the DE technique to be discussed in this article was introduced in
Refs.
[
21
,
23
,
22
,
24
]
.
Some previous works employing the same version of the DE approach that we follow in this manuscript include: (i) the study of higher-derivative corrections in Lorentz-violating massive QED theories, incorporating different parameters of the Standard Model extension
[
25
,
26
]
, (ii) the work of Ref.
[
27
]
dealing with the inclusion of non-minimal terms appearing in a further extension of the SME
[
28
]
and (iii) the work of Ref.
[
29
]
, where the author analyzes the same Lorentz-breaking massless theory that we study in this manuscript, focusing on the generation of the Carroll-Field-Jackiw term using different regularization methods. Unlike our approach, in these references, the calculations are performed perturbatively in the Lorentz-violation parameter. Finally, in Ref.
[
30
]
, higher-derivative corrections in
QED_{2+1}
are calculated, but only for the axial part.

The effective action
S_{\rm eff}(A)
yielding the electromagnetic response is a sum of terms of the form

\frac{e^{n}}{n}{\rm Tr}\Big(S\Gamma^{\mu}A_{\mu}\Big)^{n},\qquad n=1,2,\dots

(1)

where
S
is the free fermion propagator (i.e., without the electromagnetic interaction) and
{\rm Tr}
denotes the trace in coordinate as well as matrix space. Diagrammatically, this is represented by the sum of closed fermion loops ( since the fermions have been integrated out) with a number of
n
photon external legs. The calculation of each loop yields a non-local expression which can be subsequently expanded in derivatives of the electromagnetic fields to produce local expressions, identified as contributions to the effective Lagrangian. The DE procedure
reverses this situation by allowing to select, from the very beginning, the number of fields
A_{\mu}
, as well as the number of derivatives one chooses to include in the desired contribution to the effective Lagrangian, yielding a local expression from the outset. Nevertheless, it goes without saying that increasing these numbers makes the calculation very involved. Also, since we are adding different combinations of terms depending on
A_{\mu}
, usually in these cases it is a hard task to exhibit the required gauge invariant result. However, finding the correct combination provides a consistency check of the calculation.

The underlying concept in this version of the DE program is an alternative method for calculating generalized traces of products of momentum- and coordinate-dependent operators, as suggested by Eq. (
1
). To be precise, we will
deal with objects of the form

T^{(n)}\equiv\mathrm{Tr}\Big(M_{1}(\hat{p})C_{1}(\hat{x})\,M_{2}(\hat{p})C_{2}(\hat{x})\dots M_{n}(\hat{p})C_{n}(\hat{x})\Big),

(2)

where
\hat{x}
and
\hat{p}
denote coordinate and momentum operators of
arbitrary dimension and metric. For the purpose of our applications we will mainly consider four dimensions,
with the metric
\mathrm{diag}(+,-,-,-)
such that

[{\hat{x}}^{\mu},{\hat{p}}_{\nu}]=-i\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu},

(3)

where
{\hat{p}}_{\mu}=i\partial_{\mu}
in the coordinate representation. We
assume that each
\hat{p}
-dependent function in (
2
) can be expanded in a power series
of the argument, while we deal with the exact expressions for the
\hat{x}
-dependent functions.

The basic idea is to use the commutation relations (
3
) to rearrange the expression (
2
) in such a way that it can be written as a sum of terms where, by
convention, the momentum operators are written to the left and the
coordinate operators are written to the right

T^{(n)}=\sum_{N}T^{(n)}_{N}\equiv\sum_{N}\mathrm{Tr}\Big(L^{(n)}_{N}({\hat{p}})\,R^{(n)}_{N}({\hat{x}})\Big).

(4)

Once this is achieved, each contribution to the full trace can be
immediately calculated as the sum of

\displaystyle T^{(n)}_{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{4}x\langle x|L^{(n)}_{N}({\hat{p}})\,R^{(n)}_{N}({\hat{x}})|x\rangle=\int d^{4}x\langle x|L^{(n)}_{N}({\hat{p}})|x\rangle\,R^{(n)}_{N}({x}),

(5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{4}x\,\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,L^{(n)}_{N}({\ p})\,R^{(n)}_{N}({x}),

in terms of the numerical functions
L_{N}(p)
and
R_{N}(x)
. We are using the
scalar product

\langle x|p\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{4}}}e^{-ip_{\mu}x^{\mu}},\qquad\langle p|x\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{4}}}e^{+ip_{\mu}x^{\mu}},

(6)

together with the completeness of each orthonormal basis.

Before going into the details, we indicate the organization of the paper. In Section II, we explain the basic strategy for moving coordinate-dependent operators to the right and momentum-dependent operators to the left in expressions with the generic structure (
2
). To simplify the notation, we use one-dimensional operators at this stage, but we also indicate the direct generalization to arbitrary dimensions, focusing on the four-dimensional case in particular. Section III is divided into two parts. In the first subdivision, we provide a review of the calculation of the effective electromagnetic action
S_{\rm eff}(A)
of a Dirac field as a power series in the electromagnetic potential. In the second part, by restricting to the contribution of second order in
A_{\mu}
, we compare the DE method with the standard procedure for calculating this term. To show the equivalence, we express both alternatives in momentum space. Next, we present two applications where the DE technique is applied, emphasizing derivation of higher-order derivative corrections to the effective Lagrangians. In Section IV, we discuss
(3+1)
-dimensional massless electrodynamics with an additional coupling which is relevant to the effective description of Weyl semimetals in condensed matter. We recover Carroll-Field-Jackiw electrodynamics together with its lowest-order derivative correction. The second application, in section V, deals with massive electrodynamics in
(2+1)
dimensions, which can serve as a model for the interface between two topological insulators in
(3+1)
dimensions. In this case, we calculate the effective action up to terms of fourth order in the derivatives. Finally, we close with section VI, which summarizes and discusses our results. Three appendices are included, containing the detailed evaluation of some integrals used throughout the manuscript. Our signature is
(+,-,-,-)
, we take
\epsilon^{0123}=+1
, and we follow the electrodynamics conventions of Ref.
[
31
]
. We work in natural units
\hbar=c=1
.

II
The Method

The basis of the method can be easily explained in the one-dimensional case. Let
us consider the simplest situation

T^{(2)}=\mathrm{Tr}\Big(M_{1}(\hat{p})C_{1}(\hat{x})\,M_{2}(\hat{p})C_{2}(\hat{x})\Big),

(7)

where we need to interchange
C_{1}M_{2}
. Assuming
M_{2}
has a power expansion in
\hat{p}
, we examine the contributions of
{\hat{p}}^{n}
to the commutator
[C_{1}(\hat{x}),M_{2}(\hat{p})]
. We start from the basic commutator

\displaystyle[C_{1}(\hat{x}),\hat{p}]=-i\frac{dC_{1}(\hat{x})}{d\hat{x}},

(8)

which we reorganize in the convenient form

\displaystyle C_{1}(\hat{x})\,\hat{p}=\Big(\hat{p}-i\frac{d}{d\hat{x}}\Big)C_{1}(\hat{x}).

(9)

To firmly establish the notation and conventions we calculate in detail the next contribution

\displaystyle[C_{1}(\hat{x}),\hat{p}^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{p}[C_{1}(\hat{x}),\hat{p}]+[C_{1}(\hat{x}),\hat{p}]\hat{p},

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\hat{p}[C_{1}(\hat{x}),\hat{p}]+[[C_{1}(\hat{x}),\hat{p}],\hat{p}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2i\hat{p}\frac{dC_{1}(\hat{x})}{d\hat{x}}-\frac{d^{2}C_{1}(\hat{x})}{d\hat{x}^{2}},

where we choose to write the
\hat{p}
operators to the left and the
\hat{x}
operators to the right.
Next we identify the term
C_{1}({\hat{x}}){\hat{p}}^{2}
as

C_{1}(\hat{x})\hat{p}^{2}=\Big(\hat{p}^{2}-2i\hat{p}\frac{d}{d\hat{x}}-\frac{d^{2}}{d\hat{x}^{2}}\Big)C_{1}(\hat{x}).

(11)

This is an exact operator expression and Eq. (
10
) makes it clear that
\frac{d}{d\hat{x}}
acts only in the operator
C_{1}
. The crucial step in the notation arises after rewriting (
11
) as

\Big(\hat{p}^{2}-2i\hat{p}\frac{d}{d\hat{x}}-\frac{d^{2}}{d\hat{x}^{2}}\Big)=\Big(\hat{p}-i\frac{d}{d\hat{x}}\Big)^{2}.

(12)

In other words, from now on we are assuming that the operator
\hat{p}
commutes with
\frac{d}{d\hat{x}}
,
which naturally act to the right upon the corresponding
C_{1}(\hat{x})
. That is to say,
\frac{d}{d\hat{x}}
will be considered a
{C}
-number in the following. Having established this convention we immediately obtain the relations

\displaystyle C_{1}(\hat{x})\hat{p}^{n}=\Big(\hat{p}-i\frac{d}{d\hat{x}}\Big)^{n}\,C_{1}(\hat{x}),

(13)

obtained by multiplying the
(n-1)
equation to the right by
\hat{p}
and using (
9
). This yields the general result

C_{1}(\hat{x})M_{2}(\hat{p})=M_{2}\left(\hat{p}-i\frac{d}{d\hat{x}}\right)C_{1}(\hat{x}).

(14)

Here we recall that the derivative
\frac{d}{d\hat{x}}
acts only upon the exchanged operator
C_{1}
. Let us emphasize that the notation
M({\hat{p}}-i\frac{d}{d{\hat{x}}})
indicates that the operator
{\hat{p}}
has been shifted to
{\hat{p}}-i\frac{d}{d{\hat{x}}}
in the power expansion of
M({\hat{p}})
. Equation (
14
) is the key identity of the method, with the appropriate generalization to arbitrary dimensions.

Extending the above construction to more factors let us consider
T^{(3)}
, for example,

T^{(3)}=\mathrm{Tr}\Big(M_{1}(\hat{p})\Big[C_{1}(\hat{x})\,M_{2}(\hat{p})C_{2}(\hat{x})\,M_{3}(\hat{p})\Big]C_{3}(\hat{x})\Big),

(15)

and focus on the term in square brackets

\Big(C_{1}(\hat{x})\,M_{2}(\hat{p})\Big)\Big(C_{2}(\hat{x})M_{3}(\hat{p})\Big)=M_{2}\left(\hat{p}-i\frac{d\;}{d\hat{x}_{1}}\right)\left[C_{1}(\hat{x})\;M_{3}\left(\hat{p}-i\frac{d\;}{d\hat{x}_{2}}\right)\right]C_{2}(\hat{x}),

(16)

where we use the prescription (
14
) to move
C_{1}
through
M_{2}
and
C_{2}
through
M_{3}
. We have labeled de derivatives as
\frac{d}{d\hat{x}_{i}}
to make sure that that they only act upon the corresponding
C_{i}(\hat{x})
.

We still have to move
C_{1}(\hat{x})\;\;
across
M_{3}(\hat{p})
in order to have all
\hat{x}
-depending operators to the right. Again we start from the first non-trivial combination arising from the power expansion of
M_{3}

C_{1}({\hat{x}})\left(\hat{p}-i\frac{d\;}{d\hat{x}_{2}}\right)^{2}=\Big(C_{1}(\hat{x})\,\hat{p}^{2}-2iC_{1}(\hat{x})\,\hat{p}\frac{d}{d\hat{x}_{2}}-C_{1}(\hat{x})\,\frac{d^{2}}{d\hat{x}_{2}^{2}}\Big).

(17)

Using the relations (
13
) in the first two terms and the
C
-number property of
\frac{d}{d\hat{x}_{2}}
yields

\displaystyle C_{1}({\hat{x}})\left(\hat{p}-i\frac{d\;}{d\hat{x}_{2}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\left(\hat{p}-i\frac{d\;}{d\hat{x}_{1}}\right)^{2}-2i\left(\hat{p}-i\frac{d\;}{d\hat{x}_{1}}\right)\frac{d\;}{d\hat{x}_{2}}-\frac{d^{2}}{d\hat{x}_{2}^{2}}\right)C_{1}({\hat{x}}),

(18)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\hat{p}-i\frac{d}{d\hat{x}_{1}}-i\frac{d\;}{d\hat{x}_{2}}\Big)^{2}C_{1}({\hat{x}}),

which enforces the
C
-number character of the derivatives
\frac{d}{d\hat{x}_{i}}
. The general expression results

C_{1}({\hat{x}})M_{3}\left(\hat{p}-i\frac{d\;}{d\hat{x}_{2}}\right)=M_{3}\left(\hat{p}-i\frac{d\;}{d\hat{x}_{2}}-i\frac{d\;}{d\hat{x}_{1}}\right)\,C_{1}({\hat{x}}),

(19)

and the final result for the product in Eq. (
16
) is

M_{2}\left(\hat{p}-i\frac{d\;}{d\hat{x}_{1}}\right)M_{3}\left(\hat{p}-i\frac{d\;}{d\hat{x}_{2}}-i\frac{d\;}{d\hat{x}_{1}}\right)\,C_{1}({\hat{x}})C_{2}(\hat{x}),

(20)

The final result for
T

{}^{(3)}\;
is

\displaystyle T^{(3)}=\mathrm{Tr}\left(M_{1}(\hat{p})M_{2}\left(\hat{p}-i\frac{d\;}{d\hat{x}_{1}}\right)M_{3}\left(\hat{p}-i\frac{d\;}{d\hat{x}_{2}}-i\frac{d\;}{d\hat{x}_{1}}\right)C_{1}(\hat{x})C_{2}(\hat{x})C_{3}(\hat{x}).\right)

(21)

Since the product of the operators
M_{1}
,
M_{2}
and
M_{3}
involve only the operator
\hat{p}
together with commuting objects
\frac{d\;}{d\hat{x}_{i}}
and the operators
C_{i}(\hat{x})
are all displaced to the right,
T^{(3)}
has just the form indicated in Eq. (
5
) yielding the final value

T^{(3)}=\int dx\int\frac{dp}{(2\pi)}\left(M_{1}(p)M_{2}\left(p-i\frac{d\;}{dx_{1}}\right)M_{3}\left(p-i\frac{d\;}{dx_{2}}-i\frac{d\;}{dx_{1}}\right)C_{1}(x)C_{2}(x)C_{3}(x)\right).

(22)

The procedure is easily generalized to products including more terms. Besides, the generalization to higher dimensions is also direct. For example, extending
T^{(3)}
to four dimensions yields

\displaystyle T^{(3)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[M_{1}(\hat{p}_{\mu})C_{1}(\hat{x}^{\nu})\,M_{2}(\hat{p}_{\mu})\left(C_{2}(\hat{x}^{\nu})M_{3}(\hat{p}_{\mu})\right)C_{3}(\hat{x}^{\nu})\right]

\displaystyle T^{(3)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(M_{1}(\hat{p}_{\mu})M_{2}\left(\hat{p}_{\nu}-i\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{x}_{1}^{\nu}}\right)M_{3}\left(\hat{p}_{\rho}-i\frac{\partial\;}{\partial\hat{x}_{2}^{\rho}}-i\frac{\partial\;}{\partial\hat{x}_{1}^{\rho}}\right)C_{1}(\hat{x})C_{2}(\hat{x})C_{3}(\hat{x})\right),

(23)

where we have deleted the super-index labeling the coordinate operators and functions. The final trace evaluation in coordinate space yields

T^{(3)}=\int d^{4}x\Big[\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,\left(M_{1}(p_{\mu})M_{2}\left(p_{\nu}-i\frac{\partial\;}{\partial x_{1}^{\nu}}\right)M_{3}\left(p_{\rho}-i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}^{\rho}}-i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}^{\rho}}\right)\Big]C_{1}(x)C_{2}(x)C_{3}(x)\right).

(24)

Defining the operator

\Pi\Big(\,i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}^{\nu}},\,\,i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}^{\rho}}\Big)=\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,M_{1}(p_{\mu})M_{2}\left(p_{\nu}-i\frac{\partial\;}{\partial x_{1}^{\nu}}\right)M_{3}\left(p_{\rho}-i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}^{\rho}}-i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}^{\rho}}\right),

(25)

we present Eq. (
24
) in the compact form

T^{(3)}=\int d^{4}x\,\Pi\Big(\,i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}^{\nu}},\,\,i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}^{\rho}}\Big)\,C_{1}(x)C_{2}(x)C_{3}(x).

(26)

The final goal now is to determine the local function of
x
inside the integral (
26
). The standard strategy is to expand the functions
M_{i}
in powers of the derivatives times functions of the momentum, thus giving the name of “the derivative expansion” to the method. The general form of the resulting expression is

T^{(3)}=\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}d^{4}x\Big(N_{0}+N^{\mu}_{1}(p)\partial_{\mu}+N^{\mu\nu}_{2}(p)\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}+\cdots\Big)C_{1}(x)C_{2}(x)C_{3}(x),

(27)

where we must make sure that each derivative
\partial/\partial x^{\nu}_{i}
acts on the corresponding function
C_{i}(x)
. The
p
-integration can now be performed in the coefficient of each term, and the gradient operators end up yielding a local dependence on
C_{i}(x)
and their derivatives.

III
The effective electromagnetic action

III.1
The general setup

We illustrate the use of the DE method in the calculation of the electromagnetic response of Dirac fermions in different settings. The main advantage of the method is that one can select from the very beginning the type of effective action one is interested in by selecting the number of electromagnetic potentials involved together with the number of derivatives required.

Let us start with the fermionic action in an external electromagnetic field
\;A_{\mu}

S=\int d^{4}x\bar{\Psi}(\left(\Gamma^{\mu}i\partial_{\mu}-M\right)-e\Gamma^{\mu}A_{\mu})\Psi,\quad{\hat{p}}_{\mu}=i\partial_{\mu},\quad A_{\mu}=A_{\mu}({\hat{x}}^{\nu}),

(28)

where
\Gamma^{\mu}
and
M
can be taken in the general form described by the fermionic sector of the Standard Model Extension
[
1
,
2
]
, for example. The action (
28
) is frequently used as a benchmark in the field theory calculation of the electromagnetic response of Dirac-like materials in condensed matter physics .The resulting effective electromagnetic action
S_{\rm eff}(A)
is given by

\displaystyle\exp[iS_{\rm eff}(A)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int D\bar{\Psi}D\Psi\exp[i\int d^{4}x\bar{\Psi}(\left(\Gamma^{\mu}{\hat{p}}_{\mu}-M\right)-e\Gamma^{\mu}A_{\mu})\Psi],

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det(\left(\Gamma^{\mu}{\hat{p}}_{\mu}-M\right)-e\Gamma^{\mu}A_{\mu}).

Let us introduce the non-interacting Green function
S({\hat{p}_{\mu}})={i}/{\left(\Gamma^{\mu}{\hat{p}}_{\mu}-M\right)}
and write

(\left(\Gamma^{\mu}{\hat{p}}_{\mu}-M\right)-e\Gamma^{\mu}A_{\mu})=\left(\Gamma^{\mu}{\hat{p}}_{\mu}-M\right)\left[1-S({\hat{p}_{\mu}})\,\Gamma^{\alpha}\left(-ieA_{\alpha}\right)\right].

(30)

such that

\det(\left(\Gamma^{\mu}{\hat{p}}_{\mu}-M\right)-e\Gamma^{\mu}A_{\mu})=\det\left(\Gamma^{\mu}{\hat{p}}_{\mu}-M\right)\det\left[1-S({\hat{p}_{\mu}})\,\Gamma^{\alpha}\left(-ieA_{\alpha}\right)\right].

(31)

Here we discard the irrelevant normalization factor
\det\left(\Gamma^{\mu}{\hat{p}}_{\mu}-M\right)
and write

iS_{\rm eff}(A)=\ln\det\left[1-S({\hat{p}_{\mu}})\,\Gamma^{\alpha}\left(-ieA_{\alpha}\right)\right]={\rm Tr}\ln\left[1-S({\hat{p}_{\mu}})\,\Gamma^{\alpha}\left(-ieA_{\alpha}\right)\right],

(32)

after using the identity
\det M=\exp{\rm Tr}\ln M
. Here,
{\rm Tr}
indicates the trace in coordinate space which includes the trace
\rm tr
in the Dirac matrix space. The above expression
provides the basis for a perturbative calculation of
S_{eff}(A)
, starting
from the power expansion of the logarithm

\ln(1-x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}-\frac{1}{n}x^{n}.

(33)

In this approach we initially keep the operator-valued expressions of the propagator
S(\hat{p}_{\mu})
together with that of the vector potential
A^{\mu}(\hat{x}^{\alpha})
such that
[\hat{x}^{\mu},\hat{p}_{\nu}]=-i\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu}
, so that

iS_{\rm eff}(A)=Tr\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\Big(S({\hat{p}}_{\mu})\,\left(-ie\Gamma^{\alpha}A_{\alpha}({\hat{x}}^{\mu})\right)\Big)^{n}.

(34)

In the following we restrict ourselves to an expansion to second order in
A^{\mu}
, having up to four derivatives in the electromagnetic fields. The starting point is

\displaystyle iS_{\rm eff}^{(2)}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle+\frac{e^{2}}{2}{\rm Tr}\left[S({\hat{p}})\,\Gamma^{\mu}A_{\mu}({\hat{x}})\,S({\hat{p}})\,\Gamma^{\nu}A_{\nu}({\hat{x}})\right].

(35)

III.2
Contact with the Feynman diagram calculation to one-loop

In this subsection, we establish the equivalence between the following two alternative methods for calculating the one-loop effective electromagnetic action: (1) the use of the DE method which we follow in this work and (2) the usual Feynman diagram calculation as shown in Ref.
[
32
]
, for example.
On the one hand, the DE approach we have discussed naturally operates in coordinate space, yielding local results at each stage of the calculation. On the other hand, the standard calculation of
S_{\rm eff}^{(2)}(A)
in coordinate space yields a non-local result. The point of contact is achieved going to the momentum representation in both cases, as we now show.

Let us start with the more familiar case of the Feynman diagram calculation, where we use the coordinate basis to evaluate (
35
) by taking the coordinate trace as
\int d^{4}x\,\langle x|\,i\,S^{(2)}_{\rm eff}(A)|x\rangle
, and subsequently inserting a unity as
\int d^{4}x^{\prime}|x^{\prime}\rangle\langle x^{\prime}|=I
immediately before
\Gamma^{\mu}
. Following this method, we obtain the following one-loop expression in coordinate space
[
32
]

iS_{\rm eff}^{(2)}(A)=\frac{e^{2}}{2}\int d^{4}x\;d^{4}x^{\prime}\;A_{\mu}(x^{\prime}){\rm tr}\left[S(x-x^{\prime})\Gamma^{\mu}S(x^{\prime}-x)\Gamma^{\nu}\right]A_{\nu}(x),

(36)

which is a non-local expression.
Here,
\langle x|S({\hat{p}})|x^{\prime}\rangle=S(x-x^{\prime})

is the free fermion propagator (i.e. without the electromagnetic interaction) denoted by
S(p)
in momentum space. The only trace remaining is that in the Dirac matrix space. Notice that this way of taking the coordinate trace is a different operation, when compared to the prescription in the DE program discussed in section
II
, where we moved the coordinate-dependent operators to the right and the momentum-dependent operators to the left.

To make contact with the DE approach we introduce the Fourier transform

A_{\mu}(x)=\int\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}e^{-ik_{\mu}x^{\mu}}A_{\mu}(k),

(37)

in Eq. (
36
), with
i\partial_{\mu}=k_{\mu}
and the notation
k_{\mu}x^{\mu}=k\cdot x
,
obtaining

iS_{\rm eff}^{(2)}(A)=\frac{e^{2}}{2}\int d^{4}x\;\frac{d^{4}k^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\;\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\;\Pi^{\mu\nu}(k^{\prime})\,e^{-ix\cdot(k^{\prime}+k)}A_{\mu}(k^{\prime})A_{\nu}(k).

(38)

with

\Pi^{\mu\nu}(k^{\prime})=\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}{\rm tr}\Big(S(p)\,\Gamma^{\mu}\,S(p-k^{\prime})\,\Gamma^{\nu}\Big),

(39)

which is the standard form of vacuum polarization tensor. Usually the result (
38
) is presented after the
d^{4}x
integration is performed, which in turns allows a further integration in one of the momenta. We keep the integration over
x
because it is mandatory in the DE formulation as a consequence of its local character.
In the next step of the calculation following Eq. (
38
), one further combines the denominators of the propagators
S(p)
and
S(p-k^{\prime})
to produce an exact result for
\Pi^{\mu\nu}(k^{\prime})
.

Now let us consider the alternative DE calculation which
starts from the key identity Eq. (
14
), that allows to rewrite the effective action (
35
) as

iS_{\rm eff}^{(2)}(A)=+\frac{e^{2}}{2}{\rm Tr}\left[S({\hat{p}})\,\Gamma^{\mu}\,S({\hat{p}-i\partial^{\prime}})\,\Gamma^{\nu}A^{\prime}_{\mu}({\hat{x}})A_{\nu}({\hat{x}})\right],

(40)

where the superscript “prime” indicates where the derivative acts. Then, evaluating the trace as in (
24
), we obtain

iS_{\rm eff}^{(2)}(A)=+\frac{e^{2}}{2}\int d^{4}x\,\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}{\rm tr}\Big(S(p)\,\Gamma^{\mu}\,S(p-i\partial^{\prime})\,\Gamma^{\nu}\Big)A^{\prime}_{\mu}(x)A_{\nu}(x).

(41)

Here we recognize what we call the vacuum polarization operator, where
k^{\prime}
in Eq. (
39
) has been replaced by
-i\partial^{\prime}
, yielding

\Pi^{\mu\nu}(i\partial^{\prime})=\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}{\rm tr}\Big(S(p)\,\Gamma^{\mu}\,S(p-i\partial^{\prime})\,\Gamma^{\nu}\Big),

(42)

together with

iS_{\rm eff}^{(2)}(A)=+\frac{e^{2}}{2}\int d^{4}x\,\Big[\Pi^{\mu\nu}(i\partial^{\prime})A^{\prime}_{\mu}(x)\Big]A_{\nu}(x).

(43)

As a final step to make the comparison, let us now rewrite (
43
) in momentum space with

A^{\prime}_{\mu}(x)A_{\nu}(x)=\int\frac{d^{4}k^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}e^{-ik^{\prime}\cdot x}e^{-ik\cdot x}A^{\prime}_{\mu}(k^{\prime})A_{\nu}(k).

(44)

Since
\partial^{\prime}
acts only in
A^{\prime}_{\mu}(x)
we can replace the operator
i\partial^{\prime}
by
k^{\prime}
in the vauum polarization operator (
42
) acting on
e^{-ik^{\prime}\cdot x}
,
such that the Fourier transformed version of Eq. (
43
) reads,

iS_{\rm eff}^{(2)}(A)=+\frac{e^{2}}{2}\int d^{4}x\,\frac{d^{4}k^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,\Pi^{\mu\nu}(k^{\prime})\,e^{-ik^{\prime}\cdot x}e^{-ik\cdot x}A_{\mu}(k^{\prime})A_{\nu}(k),

(45)

in terms of the momentum representation of the operator
\Pi^{\mu\nu}(k^{\prime})
, given in Eq. (
39
), which arises in the Feynman diagram calculation.

Summarizing, we have shown that the full expression for the effective action
S_{\rm eff}^{(2)}(A)
is the same as calculated by either of the two methods, as depicted in Eqs. (
38
) and (
45
). In other words, the same expression in coordinate space
can be achieved in terms of an expansion in
k_{\mu}^{\prime}
of the Feynman diagram calculation of
\Pi^{\mu\nu}(k^{\prime})
, after going back to coordinate space taking care that the powers in
k^{\prime}_{\alpha}
would end up acting only as derivatives on
A^{\prime}_{\mu}
.

When the main goal of a calculation is to obtain a local expression for the effective action (
34
) in coordinate space,
we highlight the specific prescription for taking the trace in the DE method, described in section
II
, as an important advantage, particularly in the case when we are interested in higher derivative corrections to this effective action containing additional powers in
A_{\mu}
.

IV
First application: the efective action in an extended massless QED

In this section, we choose the additional coupling
b_{\mu}\gamma_{5}\gamma^{\mu}
to massless QED in
(3+1)
dimensions to show the calculation of the effective action. We start from the fermionic action

S=\int d^{4}x\bar{\Psi}(\left(\gamma^{\mu}i\partial_{\mu}-b_{\mu}\gamma_{5}\gamma^{\mu}\right)-e\gamma^{\mu}A_{\mu})\Psi,

(46)

where we identify
\Gamma^{\mu}=\gamma^{\mu}
and
M=b_{\mu}\gamma_{5}\gamma^{\mu}
in the general Eq. (
28
).
The additional coupling belongs to the fermionic sector of the SME and violates Lorentz and CPT symmetries. In condensed matter, the fermionic action describes the linearized approximation for the Hamiltonian of a Weyl semimetal with two cones, having energy separation
b_{0}
and momentum separation
\mathbf{b}
in the Brillouin zone. The general case, including tilting, anisotropy, and chemical potential without higher-order derivative corrections, is reported in Refs.
[
33
,
34
]
.
Additional work dealing with the use of fermionic actions like (
28
), which violate Lorentz invariance, to obtain the electromagnetic response of Dirac-like materials can be found in Refs.
[
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
.

As we will show next, the effective electromagnetic action resulting from Eq. (
46
) describes Carroll-Field-Jackiw electrodynamics
[
39
]
plus higher-order derivative corrections. Since
b_{\mu}
is a constant vector, the effective action is given by the Eq. (
34
)
with the propagator

S(\hat{p})=i/\left(\gamma^{\mu}\hat{p}_{\mu}-b_{\mu}\gamma_{5}\gamma^{\mu}\right).

(47)

where
M
replaces the standard mass term.
We are interested in the effective action to second order in
A_{\mu}
, which is given by Eq. (
35
). In this case, Eqs. (
42
), (
) and (
38
) indicate that the result is given by the vacuum polarization tensor in any of its representations. Then, we focus on the calculation of this object.

Using the relation (
14
) and following the basic idea of the DE technique, we move
A_{\mu}({\hat{x}})
to the right, obtaining Eq. (
40
).
Next, we calculate the trace over the coordinate space as shown in Eq. (
5
), arriving at Eq. (
41
),
which is written as

S_{\rm eff}^{(2)}(A)=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{4}x\Pi^{\mu\nu}(\partial^{\prime})A^{\prime}_{\mu}(x)A_{\nu}(x),\qquad\Pi^{\mu\nu}(\partial^{\prime})=ie^{2}{\rm tr}\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\left[S({p})\,\gamma^{\mu}\,S(p-i\partial^{\prime})\,\gamma^{\nu}\right],

(48)

where
\Pi^{\mu\nu}(\partial^{\prime})

is the polarization tensor operator.

Since we are dealing with a massless theory and the matrix
\gamma_{5}
appears in the propagator (
47
),
we find it convenient to use chiral projectors.
We define the operators

P_{\chi}=\frac{1+\chi\gamma_{5}}{2},\hskip 14.22636pt\gamma_{5}^{2}=1,\hskip 14.22636ptP_{+}+P_{-}=1,\hskip 14.22636ptP_{\chi}^{2}=1,\hskip 14.22636ptP_{+}P_{-}=P_{-}P_{+}=0,

(49)

that project onto the right-handed (R) and left-handed (L) subspaces, with
\chi=+1
and
\chi=-1
, respectively
[
40
,
41
,
42
,
34
]
. Therefore, we can write the polarization tensor as the sum

\Pi^{\mu\nu}=\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{L}+\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{R},

(50)

with

i\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{\chi}=e^{2}{\rm tr}\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\left[S({p})\,\gamma^{\mu}\,S(p-i\partial^{\prime})\,\gamma^{\nu}P_{\chi}\right].

(51)

The action of the projectors on the propagator allows for the replacement of the matrix
\gamma^{5}
in the denominator by the respective eigenvalues, yielding

\frac{i}{\gamma^{\mu}p_{\mu}-b\gamma_{5}\gamma^{\mu}}\gamma^{\nu}P_{\chi}=P_{\chi}S_{\chi}(p)\gamma^{\mu},

(52)

where

S_{\chi}(p)=\frac{i}{\gamma^{\mu}p_{\mu}+\chi\gamma^{\mu}b_{\mu}}=\frac{i}{(p_{\mu}+\chi b_{\mu})\gamma^{\mu}}=i\frac{(p+\chi b)_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}}{(p+\chi b)^{2}}.

(53)

Using the cyclic property of the trace, we obtain

i\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{\chi}=e^{2}{\rm tr}\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\left[S_{\chi}({p})\,\gamma^{\mu}\,S_{\chi}(p-i\partial^{\prime})\,\gamma^{\nu}P_{\chi}\right].

(54)

In the following, we consider only the axial contribution to the vacuum polarization, which amounts to retaining only the
\gamma^{5}
piece of the projector
P_{\chi}
in Eq. (
54
). This restriction is motivated by some interesting results which emerge from this sector of the
\Pi^{\mu\nu}
in condensed matter, such as the anomalous
Hall effect, together with contributions to the chiral magnetic effect
[
43
]
.
Also, this sector allows for a simpler illustration of the DE method in connection with the derivation of gauge-invariant results. Then, the vacuum polarization operator reduces to

i\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{\chi}(\partial^{\prime})=-\frac{\chi e^{2}}{2}{\rm tr}\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\left[\frac{(p+\chi b)_{\alpha}\gamma^{\alpha}}{(p+\chi b)^{2}}\,\gamma^{\mu}\,\frac{(p-i\partial^{\prime}+\chi b)_{\beta}\gamma^{\beta}}{(p-i\partial^{\prime}+\chi b)^{2}}\,\gamma^{\nu}\gamma^{5}\right].

(55)

Defining
\pi_{\alpha}=(p+\chi b)_{\alpha}
, we have that

(\pi-i\partial^{\prime})^{2}=\pi^{2}-(2i\pi_{\rho}\partial^{\prime}\rho+\partial^{2})
, so we can rewrite

i\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{\chi}(\partial^{\prime})=-\frac{\chi e^{2}}{2}{\rm tr}\left(\gamma^{\alpha}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{\beta}\gamma^{\nu}\gamma^{5}\right)\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\left[\frac{\pi_{\alpha}}{\pi^{2}}\,(\pi_{\beta}-i\partial^{\prime}_{\beta})\frac{1}{\pi^{2}-(2i\pi_{\rho}\partial^{\prime\rho}+\partial^{\prime 2})}\right],

(56)

where the trace is given by
{\rm tr}\left(\gamma^{\alpha}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{\beta}\gamma^{\nu}\gamma^{5}\right)=-4i\epsilon^{\alpha\mu\beta\nu}
with
\epsilon^{0123}=+1
. The notation is
\pi^{2n}=(\pi_{\alpha}\pi^{\alpha})^{n}
.

Following the main objective of the DE technique, we now expand
\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{\chi}(\partial^{\prime})
in powers of the derivatives
\partial^{\prime}_{\mu}
. The last factor in Eq. (
56
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi^{2}-(2i\pi_{\rho}\partial^{\prime\rho}+\partial^{\prime 2})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}+\frac{1}{\pi^{4}}(2i\pi_{\rho}\partial^{\prime\rho}+\partial^{\prime 2})+\frac{1}{\pi^{6}}(2i\pi_{\rho}\partial^{\prime\rho}+\partial^{\prime 2})^{2}+\frac{1}{\pi^{8}}(2i\pi_{\rho}\partial^{\prime\rho}+\partial^{\prime 2})^{3}+...,

(57)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{0}+\mathcal{D}_{1}+\mathcal{D}_{2}+\mathcal{D}_{3}+...,

where the lower index
i
in
\mathcal{D}_{i}
indicates the number of derivatives. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{0}=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\hskip 14.22636pt

,

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\mathcal{D}_{1}=\frac{2i\pi_{\rho}\partial^{\prime\rho}}{\pi^{4}},

(58)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{2}=\frac{\partial^{\prime 2}}{\pi^{4}}-\frac{4\pi_{\rho}\pi_{\eta}\partial^{\prime\rho}\partial^{\prime\eta}}{\pi^{6}}\hskip 14.22636pt

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\mathcal{D}_{3}=\frac{2i\pi_{\rho}\partial^{\prime\rho}{\partial^{\prime 2}}}{\pi^{6}}-\frac{8i\pi_{\rho}\pi_{\eta}\pi_{\gamma}\partial^{\prime\rho}\partial^{\prime\eta}\partial^{\prime\gamma}}{\pi^{8}}.

Up to four derivatives, we expand

\displaystyle i\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{\chi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2i\chi e^{2}\epsilon^{\alpha\mu\beta\nu}\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\left[\frac{\pi_{\alpha}}{\pi^{2}}\,(\pi_{\beta}-i\partial^{\prime}_{\beta})\left(\mathcal{D}_{0}+\mathcal{D}_{1}+\mathcal{D}_{2}+\mathcal{D}_{3}+...\right)\right],

(60)

which further simplifies to

\displaystyle i\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{\chi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\chi e^{2}\epsilon^{\alpha\mu\beta\nu}\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\left[\frac{\pi_{\alpha}}{\pi^{2}}\,\partial^{\prime}_{\beta}\left(\mathcal{D}_{0}+\mathcal{D}_{1}+\mathcal{D}_{2}+\mathcal{D}_{3}+...\right)\right],

(61)

due to the antisymmetry of the Levi-Civita tensor, which makes zero the term proportional to
\pi_{\alpha}\pi_{\beta}
.

IV.2
Higher-derivative contribution in Carroll-Field-Jackiw electrodynamics

The leading order high-derivative term is given by

S_{{\rm HD-CFJ}}(A)=C^{\prime}\,e^{2}\,\int d^{4}x\,\epsilon^{\alpha\nu\beta\mu}\,b_{\alpha}A_{\nu}\,\partial^{2}\,\partial_{\beta}A_{\mu},

(67)

where
C^{\prime}
is a finite but undetermined numerical factor, similarly to the case of the CFJ term.

The rise of higher-order derivative terms deriving from the effective action has been studied in the literature for massive QED, starting from different contributions in the fermionic sector of the SME, corresponding to parameters like
b_{\mu}

[
25
]
and
g_{\alpha\beta\gamma}

[
26
,
55
]
, for example. Also, non-minimal terms
[
27
]
and combinations of
b_{\mu}
and non-minimal terms
[
56
]
have been considered. Recently,
S_{{\rm HD-CFJ}}
has been proposed as part of the effective action for certain types of metamaterials
[
57
]
.

In this work, we consider, for the first time, the emergence of
S_{{\rm HD-CFJ}}
from a massless fermionic theory.
To generate this term, we need three derivatives acting on the field
A_{\mu}
. Therefore, we next consider only such contributions in Eq. (
61
), i. e.

\displaystyle i\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{\chi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2i\chi e^{2}\epsilon^{\alpha\mu\beta\nu}\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\left[\frac{\pi_{\alpha}\partial^{\prime}_{\beta}}{\pi^{2}}\mathcal{D}_{2}\right]=2\chi e^{2}\epsilon^{\alpha\mu\beta\nu}\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\left(\frac{\pi_{\alpha}}{\pi^{6}}\partial^{\prime}_{\beta}\partial^{\prime 2}-4\frac{\pi_{\alpha}\pi_{\rho}\pi_{\eta}}{\pi^{8}}\partial^{\prime}_{\beta}\partial^{\prime\rho}\partial^{\prime\eta}\right).

(68)

Solving these integrals in spherical coordinates (see Appendix
A
), we obtain

i\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{\chi}=i\frac{\chi^{2}e^{2}}{48\pi^{2}b^{2}}\epsilon^{\alpha\mu\beta\nu}\,b_{\alpha}A_{\nu}\,\partial^{\prime 2}\,\partial_{\beta}A^{\prime}_{\mu}.

(69)

Therefore, the effective action is given by

S_{\rm eff}^{(2)}(A)=-\frac{e^{2}}{48\pi^{2}b^{2}}\int d^{4}x\,\epsilon^{\alpha\nu\beta\mu}\,b_{\alpha}A_{\nu}\,\partial^{2}\,\partial_{\beta}A_{\mu}.

(70)

which coincides with the expression (
67
), choosing
C^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{48\pi^{2}b^{2}}
. The calculation in cylindrical coordinates yields zero, showing no generation of a higher-derivative CFJ term of order two.

V

In this case, we consider massive quantum electrodynamics in

(73)

is the usual free fermionic propagator.
Again, using the relation (

), we move all momentum operators to the left, as in Eq. (
40

41
) where

is now given by (
73

where we recall that the momentum

acts as the derivative
\partial_{\mu}
only in the field

when going back to coordinate space. The polarization tensor takes the form

\displaystyle i\Pi^{\mu\nu}(k^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-e^{2}\mathrm{tr}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left(\frac{\left(\gamma^{\alpha}p_{\alpha}+m\right)\gamma^{\mu}\left(\gamma^{\beta}p_{\beta}-\gamma^{\beta}k^{\prime}_{\beta}+m\right)\gamma^{\nu}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)\left((p-k^{\prime})^{2}-m^{2}\right)}\right),

\displaystyle-e^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{tr}\mathcal{N}^{\mu\nu}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)\left((p-k^{\prime})^{2}-m^{2}\right)}\right).

Calculating the trace, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\mathcal{N}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left[\left(\gamma^{\alpha}p_{\alpha}+m\right)\gamma^{\mu}\left(\gamma^{\beta}p_{\beta}-\gamma^{\beta}k^{\prime}_{\beta}+m\right)\gamma^{\nu}\right],

(76)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4p^{\mu}p^{\nu}-2(p^{2}-m^{2})\eta^{\mu\nu}-2p^{\mu}k^{\prime\nu}+2p_{\alpha}k^{\prime\alpha}\eta^{\mu\nu}-2p^{\nu}k^{\prime\mu}+2im\epsilon^{\mu\beta\nu}k^{\prime}_{\beta},

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{\mu\nu}_{0}+\mathcal{N}^{\mu\nu}_{1}(k^{\prime}).

Here, we have split
{\cal N}^{\mu\nu}
into the terms

indicates the number of external momenta

(77)

\mathcal{N}_{1}(k^{\prime})=-2p^{\mu}k^{\prime\nu}+2p_{\alpha}k^{\prime\alpha}\eta^{\mu\nu}-2p^{\nu}k^{\prime\mu}+2im\epsilon^{\mu\beta\nu}k^{\prime}_{\beta}.

(78)

Now, we perform an expansion of the denominator in (
75
) in terms of the momentum
k^{\prime}_{\alpha}
, up to terms of order four

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(p-k^{\prime})^{2}-m^{2}}\

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p^{2}-m^{2}}+\frac{1}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{2}}(2p_{\lambda}k^{\prime\lambda}-k^{\prime 2})+\frac{1}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{3}}(2p_{\lambda}k^{\prime\lambda}-k^{\prime 2})^{2}

(79)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{4}}(2p_{\lambda}k^{\prime\lambda}-k^{\prime 2})^{3}+\frac{1}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{5}}(2p_{\lambda}k^{\prime\lambda}+k^{\prime 2})^{4}+...,

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{0}+\mathcal{D}_{1}+\mathcal{D}_{2}+\mathcal{D}_{3}+\mathcal{D}_{4}.

We have separated the above expression as a sum of terms

, where

indicates the number of external momenta in each term. Collecting the terms yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{0}=\frac{1}{p^{2}-m^{2}}\hskip 14.22636pt

,

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\mathcal{D}_{1}=\frac{2p_{\lambda}k^{\prime\lambda}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{2}},

(80)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{2}=-\frac{1}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{2}}k^{\prime 2}+\frac{4p_{\lambda}p_{\sigma}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{3}}k^{\prime\lambda}k^{\prime\sigma}\hskip 14.22636pt

,

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\mathcal{D}_{3}=-\frac{4p_{\lambda}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{3}}k^{\prime 2}k^{\prime\lambda}+\frac{8p_{\lambda}p_{\sigma}p_{\eta}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{4}}k^{\prime\lambda}k^{\prime\sigma}k^{\prime\eta},

(81)

and

\mathcal{D}_{4}=\frac{k^{\prime 4}}{(p^{2}-m^{2})^{3}}-12\frac{p_{\lambda}p_{\kappa}}{(p^{2}-m^{2})^{4}}k^{\prime 2}k^{\prime\lambda}k^{\prime\kappa}+16\frac{p_{\lambda}p_{\kappa}p_{\sigma}p_{\eta}}{(p^{2}-m^{2})^{5}}k^{\prime\lambda}k^{\prime\kappa}k^{\prime\sigma}k^{\prime\eta}.

(82)

Therefore, the vacuum polarization tensor takes the form

\Pi^{\mu\nu}=ie^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\mathcal{N}_{0}+\mathcal{N}_{1}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)}\left(\mathcal{D}_{0}+\mathcal{D}_{1}+\mathcal{D}_{2}+\mathcal{D}_{3}+\mathcal{D}_{4}+...\right),

(83)

where it is easy to identify each term by the number of external momenta,
k^{\prime}_{\mu}
. Next, we will calculate all the terms in the polarization tensor that contain up to 4 external momenta. i.e.,

\Pi^{\mu\nu}=\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{0k^{\prime}}+\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{1k^{\prime}}+\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{2k^{\prime}}+\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{3k^{\prime}}+\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{4k^{\prime}}.

(84)

Substituting the above expression in Eq. (
43
), adapted to
2+1
dimensions,
yields an effective action with higher-order derivative corrections up to order four. Next we calculate each separate contribution to
\Pi^{\mu\nu}
.

V.1
Terms without external momentum

They are included in

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{0k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ie^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\mathcal{N}_{0}\mathcal{D}_{0}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)},

(85)

which has the explicit expression

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{0k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ie^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left(\frac{4p^{\mu}p^{\nu}-2(p^{2}-m^{2})\eta^{\mu\nu}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{2}}\right)=ie^{2}\eta^{\mu\nu}\left(\frac{4}{3}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{p^{2}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{2}}-2\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)}\right),

(86)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle ie^{2}\eta^{\mu\nu}\left(\frac{4}{3}I^{1}_{2}-2I^{0}_{1}\right).

In reducing
\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{0k^{\prime}}
, we have used the identity (
143
). The last line in Eq. (
86
) is presented in terms of the master integral
I_{\alpha}^{\beta}
, defined in Eq. (
153
), and calculated in Appendix
C
. The result is

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{0k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ie^{2}\eta^{\mu\nu}\left(\frac{4}{3}\frac{3i|m|}{8\pi}-2\frac{i|m|}{4\pi}\right)=0.

(87)

In other words, there is no contribution without external momentum to the polarization tensor. This is expected to ensure gauge invariance.

V.2
Terms with one external momentum

The contributions are summarized in

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{1k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ie^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\mathcal{N}_{0}\mathcal{D}_{1}+\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{D}_{0}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)}

(88)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ie^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)}\left((4p^{\mu}p^{\nu}-2(p^{2}-m^{2})\eta^{\mu\nu})\frac{2p_{\lambda}k^{\prime\lambda}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\left(-2p^{\mu}k^{\prime\nu}+2p_{\alpha}k^{\prime\alpha}\eta^{\mu\nu}-2p^{\nu}k^{\prime\mu}\right)\frac{1}{p^{2}-m^{2}}+2im\,\epsilon^{\mu\beta\nu}\,k^{\prime}_{\beta}\frac{1}{p^{2}-m^{2}}.\right)

Note that only the term proportional to the Levi-Civita tensor survives, because integrals with an odd number
p_{\mu}
cancels due to symmetric integration, as shown in Eq. (
142
). Therefore, we end up with

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{1k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2me^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{2}}\,\epsilon^{\mu\beta\nu}\,k^{\prime}_{\beta}=-2me^{2}\,I^{0}_{2}\,\epsilon^{\mu\beta\nu}\,k^{\prime}_{\beta}.

(89)

Using the master integral (
153
), we obtain

\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{1k^{\prime}}=-i\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi}\,\mathrm{sgn}(m)\,\epsilon^{\mu\beta\nu}\,k^{\prime}_{\beta},

(90)

where
\mathrm{sgn}(m)
is the sign function of
m
. This is the usual Chern-Simons term.

V.3
Terms with two external momenta

These contributions arise from

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{2k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ie^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)}\left(\mathcal{N}_{0}\mathcal{D}_{2}+\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{D}_{1}\right)

(91)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ie^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)}\left(\left(4p^{\mu}p^{\nu}-2(p^{2}-m^{2})\eta^{\mu\nu}\right)\left(-\frac{1}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{2}}{k^{\prime}}^{2}+\frac{4p_{\lambda}p_{\sigma}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{3}}{k^{\prime}}^{\lambda}k^{\prime\sigma}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\left(-2p^{\mu}k^{\prime\nu}+2p_{\alpha}k^{\prime\alpha}\eta^{\mu\nu}-2p^{\nu}{k^{\prime}}^{\mu}\right)\left(\frac{2p_{\lambda}{k^{\prime}}^{\lambda}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{2}}\right)+2im\textasciiacute\,\epsilon^{\mu\beta\nu}\,k^{\prime}_{\beta}\left(\frac{2p_{\lambda}{k^{\prime}}^{\lambda}}{(p^{2}-m^{2})^{2}}\right)\right).

Disregarding all odd terms in
p_{\mu}
, using the identities (
143
) and (
144
), and organizing the terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{2k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-ie^{2}\left(\frac{8}{3}I^{1}_{3}-\frac{16}{15}I^{2}_{4}-2I^{0}_{2}\right)\eta^{\mu\nu}k^{\prime 2}+\left(\frac{8}{3}I^{1}_{3}-\frac{32}{15}I^{2}_{4}\right)k^{\prime\mu}k^{\prime\nu}.

(92)

In terms of the master integral (
153
), we arrive at

\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{2k^{\prime}}=-\frac{e^{2}}{12\pi|m|}\left(\eta^{\mu\nu}k^{\prime 2}-k^{\prime\mu}k^{\prime\nu}\right).

(93)

Note that this term is explicitly gauge invariant. We consider a highly non-trivial consistency check of the calculation: the fact that all five integrals, with their weird coefficients in Eq.(
93
) manages to produce a manifestly gauge-invariant result. In fact, the difficulty in making gauge invariance manifest is one of the drawbacks of the method when applied to more complicated scenarios. Since we do not start from an explicitly gauge-invariant expression, the different independent contributions provided by the DE prodedure may complicate their necessary rearrangement, finally yielding a manifestly gauge-invariant result. A possible way to alleviate this complication could be to use the Fock-Schwinger gauge for
A_{\mu}

[
58
]
, as has been shown in different contexts in Refs.
[
59
]
.

V.4
Terms with three external momenta

They are included in

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{3k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ie^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)}\left(\mathcal{N}_{0}\mathcal{D}_{3}+\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{D}_{2}\right)A_{\nu}

(94)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ie^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left(\frac{4p^{\mu}p^{\nu}-2(p^{2}-m^{2})\eta^{\mu\nu}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)}\left(\frac{-4p_{\lambda}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{3}}k^{\prime\lambda}k^{\prime 2}+\frac{8p_{\lambda}p_{\sigma}p_{\eta}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{4}}k^{\prime\lambda}k^{\prime\sigma}k^{\prime\eta}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{2im\epsilon^{\mu\beta\nu}k^{\prime}_{\beta}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)}\left(\frac{1}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{2}}k^{\prime 2}-\frac{4p_{\lambda}p_{\sigma}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{3}}k^{\prime\lambda}k^{\prime\sigma}\right)\right).

Disregarding the odd terms in
p_{\mu}
, we have

\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{3k^{\prime}}=2me^{2}\epsilon^{\mu\beta\nu}k^{\prime}_{\beta}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left(\frac{1}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{3}}k^{\prime 2}-\frac{4p_{\lambda}p_{\sigma}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{4}}k^{\prime\lambda}k^{\prime\sigma}\right).

(95)

Now, using the identity (
143
) and reorganizing terms, we arrive at

\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{3k^{\prime}}=2e^{2}m\,\epsilon^{\mu\beta\nu}\left(I^{0}_{3}-\frac{4}{3}I^{1}_{4}\right)k^{\prime 2}k^{\prime}_{\beta}.

(96)

Using the master integral (
153
), we finally obtain

\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{3k^{\prime}}=-\frac{ie^{2}}{48\pi}\frac{\mathrm{Sgn}(m)}{m^{2}}\,\epsilon^{\mu\beta\nu}k^{\prime 2}k^{\prime}_{\beta}.

(97)

This term is the lowest-order higher-derivative counterpart of the Chern-Simons term.

V.5
Terms with four external momenta

Finally, we consider the contribution in

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{4k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ie^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)}\left(\mathcal{N}_{0}\mathcal{D}_{4}+\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{D}_{3}\right)

(98)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ie^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left((4p^{\mu}p^{\nu}-2(p^{2}-m^{2})\eta^{\mu\nu})\left(\frac{k^{\prime 4}}{(p^{2}-m^{2})^{4}}-12\frac{p_{\lambda}p_{\kappa}}{(p^{2}-m^{2})^{5}}k^{\prime\lambda}k^{\prime\kappa}k^{\prime 2}+16\frac{p_{\lambda}p_{\kappa}p_{\sigma}p_{\eta}}{(p^{2}-m^{2})^{6}}k^{\prime\lambda}k^{\prime\kappa}k^{\prime\sigma}k^{\prime\eta}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+(2p^{\mu}k^{\prime\nu}-2p^{\alpha}k^{\prime}_{\alpha}\eta^{\mu\nu}+2p^{\nu}k^{\prime\mu}-2im\,\epsilon^{\mu\beta\nu}\,k^{\prime}_{\beta})\left(\frac{4p_{\lambda}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{4}}k^{\prime\lambda}k^{\prime 2}-\frac{8p_{\lambda}p_{\sigma}p_{\eta}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{5}}k^{\prime\lambda}k^{\prime\sigma}k^{\prime\eta}\right)\right).

Disregarding the odd terms in
p_{\mu}
, using identities (
143
), (
144
) and (
145
), and reorganizing terms, we arrive at

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{4k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ie^{2}\left[\left(\frac{20}{3}I^{1}_{4}+\frac{192}{105}I^{3}_{6}-2I^{0}_{3}-\frac{96}{15}I^{2}_{5}\right)\eta^{\mu\nu}k^{\prime 4}+\left(\frac{768}{105}I^{3}_{6}-\frac{192}{15}I^{2}_{5}+\frac{16}{3}I^{1}_{4}\right)k^{\prime\mu}k^{\prime\nu}k^{\prime 2}\right].

(99)

Finally, using the master integral (
153
), and, in spite of the complicated structure of the coefficients in Eq. (
99
) we obtain

\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{4k^{\prime}}=\frac{e^{2}}{120\pi|m|^{3}}\left[k^{\prime\mu}k^{\prime\nu}-\eta^{\mu\nu}k^{\prime 2}\right]k^{\prime 2}.

(100)

This term is the higher-derivative counterpart of the term with two derivatives in Eq. (
93
) and turns out to be wonderfully gauge invariant, as it should.

V.6
The polarization Tensor up to
\mathcal{O}(k^{\prime 4})

Summarizing our results, we write the polarization tensor as

\Pi^{\mu\nu}(k^{\prime})=\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{even}+\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{odd}
. The even and odd parts are

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{even}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{2k^{\prime}}+\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{4k^{\prime}}=(k^{\prime\mu}k^{\prime\nu}-\eta^{\mu\nu}k^{\prime 2})\left(1+\frac{1}{10}\frac{k^{\prime 2}}{m^{2}}\right)\frac{e^{2}}{12\pi|m|}\equiv(k^{\prime\mu}k^{\prime\nu}-\eta^{\mu\nu}k^{\prime 2})\,\pi_{0}(k^{\prime}),

(101)

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{odd}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{1k\textasciiacute}+\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{3k^{\prime}}=-i\epsilon^{\mu\beta\nu}\,k^{\prime}_{\beta}\left(1+\frac{1}{12}\frac{k^{\prime 2}}{m^{2}}\right)\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi}\mathrm{Sgn}(m)\equiv-i\epsilon^{\mu\nu\beta}\,k_{\beta}\,\pi_{\rm CS}(k^{\prime}),

(102)

respectively, which defines
\pi_{0}(k^{\prime})
and
\pi_{\rm CS}(k^{\prime})
.
The final result is

\Pi^{\mu\nu}(k^{\prime})=(k^{\prime\mu}k^{\prime\nu}-\eta^{\mu\nu}k^{\prime 2})\pi_{0}(k\textasciiacute)-i\epsilon^{\mu\beta\nu}\,k^{\prime}_{\beta}\pi_{\rm CS}(k^{\prime}),

(103)

with

\pi_{0}(k^{\prime})=\frac{e^{2}}{12\pi|m|}\,\left(1+\frac{1}{10}\frac{k^{\prime 2}}{m^{2}}\right),\qquad\pi_{\rm CS}(k^{\prime})=\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi}\mathrm{Sgn}(m)\,\left(1+\frac{1}{12}\frac{k^{\prime 2}}{m^{2}}\right).

(104)

It is easy to see that the full polarization tensor is gauge-invariant, i.e.,

k^{\prime\mu}\Pi_{\mu\nu}(k^{\prime})=k^{\prime\nu}\Pi_{\mu\nu}(k^{\prime})=0.

(105)

As a final consistency check of our calculations, we start from the non-perturbative result of the complete polarization tensor (see for example Ref.
[
60
]
)

\pi_{0}(k^{\prime})=-\frac{|m|}{4\pi k^{\prime 2}}+\frac{1}{8\pi\sqrt{k^{\prime 2}}}\left(\frac{4m^{2}}{k^{\prime 2}}+1\right)\sinh^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{k^{\prime 2}}}{\sqrt{4m^{2}-k^{\prime 2}}}\right),\qquad\pi_{CS}(k^{\prime})=\frac{m}{2\pi\sqrt{k^{\prime 2}}}\sinh^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{k^{\prime 2}}}{\sqrt{4m^{2}-k^{\prime 2}}}\right),

(106)

and perform a power series expansion in the low-energy regime
|k^{\prime 2}|<<m^{2}
, retaining terms up to order
k^{\prime 2}/m^{2}
. We recover exactly the expressions shown in Eq. (
104
).

Finally, we go back to the expression for the effective action in coordinate space. Since we have two powers of
A_{\beta}
, and only one type of derivative (
k^{\prime}_{\alpha}=i\partial^{\prime}_{\alpha}
), it is a simple exercise to recover the final expression for the local effective action. The result is

S_{\rm eff}^{(2)}=\frac{e^{2}}{2}\int d^{4}x\,A^{\nu}(x)\Big[\frac{1}{12\pi m}\Big(1-\frac{1}{10}\frac{\partial^{2}}{m^{2}}\Big)\,\Big(-\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}+\eta_{\mu\nu}\partial^{2}\Big)+\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(m)}{4\pi}\epsilon_{\nu}{}^{\mu\beta}\,\partial_{\beta}\Big(1-\frac{1}{12}\frac{\partial^{2}}{m^{2}}\Big)\Big]A_{\mu}(x),

(107)

where all the derivatives are acting on
A_{\mu}(x)
.

VI
Summary and Discussion

With an eye on exploring alternative methods to calculate effective electromagnetic Lagrangians in Dirac-like models, we have used an early version of the derivative expansion method
[
21
,
23
,
22
,
24
]
, focusing on obtaining the corresponding lowest-order derivative corrections. This approach directly provides local effective Lagrangians and allows selecting from the outset both the powers of the electromagnetic potential to be considered, together with the number of relevant derivatives. We highlight these features as an advantage over the non-local effective actions resulting from the standard calculation of the vacuum polarization tensor, for example.

For the benefit of the reader, a brief description of the method was presented along with a summary of how the general effective electromagnetic action is obtained. The first case considered is
(3+1)
massless electrodynamics with an additional coupling
b_{\mu}\gamma^{5}\gamma^{\mu}
, which is relevant to the description of the simplest Weyl semimetal with two Dirac cones separated in momentum by
\mathbf{b}
and in energy by
b_{0}
, in the Brillouin zone.
[
43
]
. Restricting ourselves to the axial contribution of the vacuum polarization tensor, we recovered Carroll-Field-Jackiw (CFJ) electrodynamics in the zeroth order of the derivative expansion. As is well known, the numerical coefficient of this action is finite but undetermined, which is regularization dependent. Choosing alternative regularizations, we match our result with some in the literature, as shown in Table
1
. The lowest-order derivative correction is calculated for the first time in a massless theory, and the coefficient is again finite but regularization dependent. The calculation in spherical coordinates yields

J_{\rm CFJ}^{\mu}=\frac{e^{2}}{48\pi^{2}b^{2}}\,\epsilon^{\mu\alpha\beta\gamma}\,b_{\alpha}\partial^{2}F_{\beta\gamma},

(108)

which constitutes an addition to the CFJ equations. Notice that for
b^{\mu}=(0,\mathbf{b})
the above current is a covariant generalization (
\boldsymbol{\nabla^{2}}\rightarrow\partial^{2}
) of the additional terms in the Maxwell equations proposed in Ref.
[
3
]
when
\psi(x)\sim\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{x}
.

The second case we dealt with is
(2+1)
massive (
m
) electrodynamics, which can be used as a model to describe the plane interface between two topological insulators, for example. The full effective action up to fourth order in the derivatives is given by Eq. (
107
) and the resulting current is given by

J_{(2+1)}^{\mu}=\frac{e^{2}}{12\pi}\frac{1}{m}\Big(1-\frac{1}{10}\frac{\partial^{2}}{m^{2}}\Big)\partial_{\alpha}F^{\alpha\mu}-\frac{e^{2}}{8\pi}{\rm sgn}(m)\Big(1-\frac{1}{12}\frac{\partial^{2}}{m^{2}}\Big)\epsilon^{\mu\alpha\beta}F_{\alpha\beta}.

(109)

Let us observe that, in our case, we do not have a superimposed magnetic field.
From the above equation, the relativistic transverse conductivity resulting from the Hall current (the contribution proportional to
\epsilon^{\mu\alpha\beta}
) is

\sigma_{xy}(k)={\rm sgn}(m)\frac{e^{2}}{(4\pi)}\Big(1+\frac{1}{12}\frac{k^{2}}{m^{2}}\Big).

(110)

The first term,
\sigma_{xy}(k=0)
, corresponds to the usual Chern-Simons contribution to the transverse conductivity. When reintroducing units, the prefactor in Eq. (
110
) simplifies to
{\rm sgn}(m)e^{2}/(4\pi\hbar)={\rm sgn}(m)e^{2}/(2h)
. The second term provides a relativistic generalization of the
\xi/\kappa
-term introduced in Ref.
[
4
]
, in the ratio
\sigma_{xy}(k)/\sigma_{xy}(k=0)
, for the case of a Galilean invariant theory. In the latter case, the ratio
\xi/\kappa
is related to the Hall viscosity
\eta^{H}
. In single-component Galilean-invariant fluids and solids with particle number symmetry,
the momentum density is proportional to the particle number current, resulting in a Ward identity that links the viscosity and conductivity tensors
[
6
,
8
,
7
,
61
,
62
,
63
]
.

The relativistic case in a Chern insulator modeled as a free massive Dirac fermion has been considered in Refs.
[
64
,
65
]
from the perspective of the response of the material due to strain deformations induced by a curved metric. In particular, the Hall viscosity appears in the corresponding torsional response. Up to our knowledge, the possible relation between the Hall conductivity and the Hall viscosity remains an open problem in the case of relativistic systems.

The lesson we learn is that these naturally emerging correction terms in the effective action should be incorporated into the phenomenological proposals for higher-order derivative corrections to assess the relative significance of all the terms included up to a given order.

Since the scope of this work is more on a technical description of the derivative expansion method we have not considered specific physical applications of the corrections we have found in some effective electromagnetic actions. However, in the Introduction we have mentioned important applications already developed or envisaged in the literature. This is particularly noteworthy in the case of the anomalous Hall conductivity
\sigma_{xy}
arising at the interface between two 3D topological insulators, and resulting from a
2+1
QED at the boundary. As mentioned in the introduction, higher order derivative corrections are crucial for an alternative simpler way to measure the Hall viscosity, an additional topological property of the quantum states at the boundary, for a particular class of materials. Additionally, as shown in Ref.
[
66
]
, the momentum dependence of the optical parameters (particularly of
\sigma_{xy}
), obtained through higher-order derivative corrections, for example, is mandatory to explain the existence of topological bosonic phases, in particular the photonic ones. This is a topic much less studied in the literature in comparison with the fermionic case. In a forthcoming publication
[
67
]
we will consider the effect of the derivative corrections on
\sigma_{xy}
upon two physical effects: (i) the Kerr and Faraday rotation angles induced when an electromagnetic wave impinges on a topological insulator and (ii) corrections to the resulting magnetoelectric effect when a point charge is placed near a topological insulator, which is related to the generation of an image magnetic charge.

Appendix A
Integrals in Four-Dimensional Spherical Coordinates

In this appendix, we explicitly calculate the integrals appearing in equations (
63
) and (
68
). To begin with, let us compute the following integral

I^{(n)}=\int\frac{d^{4}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{4}}\frac{1}{\left[\left(p+\chi b\right)^{2}\right]^{n}}=\int\frac{d^{4}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{4}}\frac{1}{\left(p^{2}+b^{2}+2\chi p_{\mu}b^{\mu}\right)^{n}}.

(111)

We perform a Wick rotation to move into Euclidean space, choosing

\displaystyle p_{0}

\displaystyle\,\,\rightarrow

\displaystyle\,\,ip_{0}\qquad\implies\qquad d^{4}p\rightarrow id^{4}p\hskip 28.45274pt\mathrm{and}\hskip 28.45274ptp^{2}\rightarrow-p^{2},

(112)

\displaystyle b_{0}

\displaystyle\,\,\rightarrow

\displaystyle\,\,ib_{0}\qquad\implies\qquad b^{2}\rightarrow-b^{2}\hskip 28.45274pt\mathrm{and}\hskip 28.45274ptp_{\mu}b^{\mu}\rightarrow-p_{\mu}b^{\mu},

(113)

which change
I^{(n)}
into

I^{(n)}_{E}=(-1)^{n}i\int\frac{d^{4}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{4}}\frac{1}{\left(p^{2}+b^{2}+2\chi p_{\mu}b^{\mu}\right)^{n}},

(114)

where all the scalar products are now in the Euclidean metric
\delta^{\mu\nu}={\rm diag}(1,1,1,1)
.
We solve this integral in 4-dimensional spherical coordinates, where the differential volume element is

d^{4}p\,\,\rightarrow\,\,dp\,d\theta_{1}\,d\theta_{2}\,d\theta_{3}\,p^{3}\,\sin^{2}\theta_{1}\,\sin\theta_{2},

(115)

with the ranges

0\leq p<\infty\hskip 17.07164pt,\hskip 17.07164pt0\leq\theta_{1}\leq\pi\hskip 17.07164pt,\hskip 17.07164pt0\leq\theta_{2}\leq\pi\hskip 17.07164pty\hskip 17.07164pt0\leq\theta_{3}\leq 2\pi.

(116)

In this coordinate system,
p
represents the radial distance from the origin in four-dimensional Euclidean space,
\theta_{1}
is the first polar angle, measuring the inclination of
p_{\mu}
with respect to the fourth coordinate.
\theta_{2}
is the second polar angle, describing the orientation of the vector within the three-dimensional subspace perpendicular to the fourth coordinate. Finally,
\theta_{3}
is the azimuthal angle, representing rotation around the remaining axis in the three-dimensional subspace. Without loss of generality, we fix the coordinate system so that the four-vector
b_{\mu}

is aligned along the fourth coordinate. This choice simplifies the calculation of the integral, as
b_{\mu}

will then form a well-defined angle
\theta_{1}
with the radial four-vector
p_{\mu}
, thereby reducing the integral expression in terms of this angle.
Hence, the integral (
111
) takes the form

I^{(n)}_{E}=\frac{(-1)^{n}i}{\left(2\pi\right)^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dpp^{3}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta_{1}\,\sin^{2}\theta_{1}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta_{2}\,\sin\theta_{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta_{3}}_{=4\pi}\frac{1}{\left(p^{2}+b^{2}+2\chi\,p\,b\,\mathrm{cos}\theta_{1}\right)^{n}},

(117)

I^{(n)}_{E}=\frac{(-1)^{n}i}{4\pi^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dpp^{3}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta_{1}\frac{\sin^{2}\theta_{1}}{\left(p^{2}+b^{2}+2\chi\,p\,b\,\mathrm{cos}\theta_{1}\right)^{n}}.

(118)

The general result for the integral over
d\theta_{1}
can be found in Ref.
[
68
]
, and it is

\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sin^{2\mu-1}(x)}{\left(1+2a\cos(x)+a^{2}\right)^{\nu}}dx=B\left(\mu,\frac{1}{2}\right)F\left(\nu,\nu-\mu+\frac{1}{2};\mu+\frac{1}{2};a^{2}\right),\qquad\mathrm{Re}(\mu)>0\,,\qquad|a|<1,

(119)

where
B\left(\mu,\frac{1}{2}\right)
is the beta function, defined as

B\left(\alpha,\gamma\right)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\alpha\right)\Gamma\left(\gamma\right)}{\Gamma\left(\alpha+\gamma\right)},

(120)

and
F\left(\nu,\nu-\mu+\frac{1}{2};\mu+\frac{1}{2};a^{2}\right)
is the hypergeometric function, given by

Appendix C
The Master Integral

We solve the integrals over
p
which are required in section
V
, when dealing with the calculation of the effective action in
2+1
QED. The master integral is

I^{\beta}_{\alpha}=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{p^{2\beta}}{\left(p^{2}-m^{2}\right)^{\alpha}}.

(146)

To solve this integral, we first go to Euclidean space through a Wick rotation given by

p_{0}\,\,\rightarrow\,\,ip_{0}\quad\implies\quad p^{2}\,\,\rightarrow\,\,-p^{2},

(147)

such that the integral takes the form

I^{\beta}_{\alpha}=(-1)^{\alpha+\beta}i\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{p^{2\beta}}{\left(p^{2}+m^{2}\right)^{\alpha}},

(148)

where now the scalar products are in the Euclidean metric
{\rm diag}=(1,1,1,1)
.
Passing to spherical coordinates, we have

I^{\beta}_{\alpha}=i\frac{(-1)^{\alpha+\beta}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\sin\theta\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,p^{2}\frac{p^{2\beta}}{\left(p^{2}+m^{2}\right)^{\alpha}}.

(149)

Given the spherical symmetry of the integrand, we can directly integrate over the angular variables, obtaining

I^{\beta}_{\alpha}=i\frac{(-1)^{\alpha+\beta}}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\frac{p^{2(\beta+1)}}{\left(p^{2}+m^{2}\right)^{\alpha}}.

(150)

Now, we perform the change of variables
p^{2}=xm^{2}\,\,\implies\,\,dp=\frac{|m|x^{-1/2}}{2}dx
, therefore

\displaystyle I^{\beta}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\frac{(-1)^{\alpha+\beta}}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dxx^{-1/2}|m|x^{\beta+1}|m|^{2(\beta+1)}\left(x|m|^{2}+|m|^{2}\right)^{-\alpha},

(151)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\frac{(-1)^{\alpha+\beta}|m|^{-2\alpha+2\beta+3}}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,x^{\beta+\frac{3}{2}-1}\,(x+1)^{-\alpha}.

The integral in
x
is a standard Beta function

B(\gamma,\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,x^{\gamma-1}(x+1)^{-\gamma-\lambda}=\frac{\Gamma(\gamma)\Gamma(\lambda)}{\Gamma(\gamma+\lambda)},

(152)

which leads to the final result

I^{\beta}_{\alpha}=i\frac{(-1)^{\alpha+\beta}|m|^{-2\alpha+2\beta+3}}{4\pi^{2}}B\left(\beta+\frac{3}{2},\alpha-\beta-\frac{3}{2}\right).

(153)

Now, we evaluate this integral for the different values of the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
that occur in section
V

(154)
