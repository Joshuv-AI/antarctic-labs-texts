Serotypic and Genomic Diversity of Vibrio anguillarum in Rainbow Trout Farms in Turkey: Implications for Vibriosis Control and Vaccine Candidate Selection
Authors: Soner Altun, Muhammed Duman, Jesús L. Romalde, Nihed Ajmi, Gorkem Tasci, Cuneyt Ozakin, Nazmiye Ulku Tuzemen, Bekir Akça, Sebahattin Ergün, Sevdan Yılmaz, Izzet Burcin Saticioglu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOutbreaks of vibriosis caused by Vibrio anguillarum are a persistent constraint on rainbow trout ( Oncorhynchus mykiss ) aquaculture. However, information on the population structure of field strains in Turkey has been lacking. Here, we report the first systematic serotypic, proteomic, and genomic characterization of 23 V. anguillarum isolates collected over 10 years from rainbow trout farms located in six major aquaculture regions of Turkey. Serological analyses based on microagglutination, supported by ELISA characterization of hyperimmune sera, identified a clear predominance of serotype O1, whereas isolate V12 exhibited a non‐agglutinating, atypical O‐antigen profile. Protein profiling (SDS‐PAGE) and immunoblotting showed largely conserved whole‐cell protein patterns among the isolates, but distinct immunogenic bands at 14, 18, and 40 kDa were detected in isolates V18 and V21. Long‐read whole‐genome sequencing revealed that most Turkish isolates grouped within the global O1 clade, while V12, V25, and V28 isolates occupied more distant branches. Comparative genomics demonstrated a conserved core virulence gene set (RTX toxins, siderophore and iron‐uptake systems, motility and adhesion factors, Type VI secretion system), with strain‐dependent variation in accessory loci such as anguibactin and T6SS‐I. Experimental infections of rainbow trout demonstrated significant differences in virulence among isolates (p < 0.05), with the V18 isolate showing high, the V15 intermediate, and the V12 low‐mortality rates. By elucidating the relationship among the serotype, immunogenic protein profiles, virulence gene repertoires, and in vivo pathogenicity, this study provides a comprehensive overview of the antigenic and genomic diversity of Vibrio anguillarum isolates from Turkey. Notably, the identification of V18 and V21 as promising candidate strains for further vaccine evaluation, characterized by high virulence and unique immunogenic features, provides a scientific foundation for the development of serotype‐specific vaccination strategies to mitigate vibriosis‐associated losses in aquaculture.

Introduction

Globally, the decline in natural fish stocks and the growing human population have positioned aquaculture as an increasingly critical source of animal protein. However, intensive production systems facilitate the spread of infectious diseases, particularly those of bacterial origin, leading to high mortality rates, increased treatment costs, and reduced product quality (FAO 2022). Among these pathogens, Vibrio anguillarum (synonym Listonella anguillarum ) is recognized as one of the most important Gram‐negative bacteria affecting numerous farmed fish species in freshwater, marine, and brackish environments (Zorrilla et al. 2003; Frans et al. 2011; Mohamad et al. 2019). Today, V. anguillarum remains one of the most frequently reported bacterial pathogens in economically important species such as rainbow trout, Atlantic salmon, seabass, and seabream across Europe, Asia, the Americas, and the Mediterranean basin. The agent typically causes acute hemorrhagic septicemia characterized by external ulcers, splenomegaly, abdominal distension, and internal haemorrhages, and often progresses rapidly, resulting in high mortality (Toranzo et al. 2005; Salleh et al. 2023).

To date, more than 23 serotypes of V. anguillarum have been described with O1, O2a, and O2b being the variants most commonly associated with clinical outbreaks (Pedersen et al. 1999; Plumb 2018). These serotypes differ in their antigenic structures and in their strategies for evading host defences; for example, the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) O‐antigen of O1 strains contributes to resistance to phagocytosis and supports a more invasive course of infection (Naka and Crosa 2011; Lindell et al. 2012).

Antimicrobial therapy has traditionally been used to manage vibriosis, but the emergence and dissemination of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in aquaculture systems is a growing global concern (Schar et al. 2020; Duman et al. 2025). These findings underscore the urgent need for alternative, site‐specific preventive measures. Experimental work using in vivo bioluminescent imaging has shown that V. anguillarum rapidly colonizes fish skin and intestinal mucosa during infection, and that the LPS O‐antigen is critical for evading epithelial phagocytosis and resistance to lysozyme and antimicrobial peptides (Weber et al. 2010; Lindell et al. 2012). Together, these studies suggest that identifying key virulence determinants and exploiting attenuated strains as live vaccine candidates represent complementary strategies for controlling vibriosis. Among available control measures, vaccination is considered one of the most effective approaches for vibriosis, providing long‐term protection while reducing the need for antibiotics (Colquhoun and Lillehaug 2014; Monaghan et al. 2016). However, the efficacy of commercial vaccines may be compromised by the genetic and antigenic diversity of local field isolates. In Turkey, previous work has demonstrated substantial phenotypic and genotypic variability among V. anguillarum strains (Onuk et al. 2018), highlighting the need for site‐specific vaccines based on locally prevalent serotypes and genotypes.

In this context, V. anguillarum strains isolated between 2013 and 2023 from aquaculture facilities across six regions of Turkey were comprehensively characterized at phenotypic (hemolysis type, protease, lipase, and DNase activities), serological (agglutination tests and ELISA), molecular (detection of virulence and antimicrobial resistance genes, whole‐genome sequencing), and antigenic (protein profiling by SDS‐PAGE and Western blot) levels, and their virulence was evaluated in vivo. We hypothesized that V. anguillarum populations circulating in Turkish rainbow trout ( O. mykiss ) farms display considerable serotypic and genomic diversity, and that integrating serological, proteomic, and genomic data with infection outcomes would enable the identification of local vaccine candidate strains and support the development of effective, site‐specific vaccination strategies against vibriosis in Turkey.

Materials and Methods

Bacterial Strains

In this study, V. anguillarum isolates obtained between 2013 and 2023 from rainbow trout ( O. mykiss ) farms located in different regions of Turkey (Western Black Sea/Marmara, Central Anatolia, Eastern Anatolia, Mediterranean, Aegean, and Black Sea) were used. Bacterial isolation was performed from internal organs (kidney, liver, spleen, heart, brain) and/or specific organs harbouring lesions. For isolation, Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA) supplemented with 1% NaCl medium was used, and the plates were incubated at 22°C for 24–48 h. Pure colonies obtained after incubation were transferred into stock cultures containing 15%–20% glycerol for long‐term preservation and stored at −80°C (Buller 2014; Austin and Austin 2016). Bacterial Identification and Genomic Characterization

Classical Biochemical Identification

Colonies grown on culture media were subjected to classical biochemical tests including Gram staining, oxidase, catalase, motility, oxidation/fermentation (O/F), and vibriostat (O/129), as well as growth characteristics on species‐specific selective media for identification (Buller 2014; Austin and Austin 2016).

DNA Extraction and Whole‐Genome Sequencing

DNA extractions from bacterial isolates were performed using a spin‐column‐based extraction kit (Nucleogene, Istanbul, Turkey) according to the manufacturer's protocol. DNA concentrations were measured using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) while purity ratios (260/280) were assessed with a Thermo Scientific Multiskan GO spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For whole‐genome analysis, 50 ng of bacterial DNA from each isolate was used and the genome sequencing was carried out using the PromethION 2 Solo platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK). A 24‐h sequencing run was performed using the MinKNOW control software (version 0.46.1.9, flow cell type R9.4; Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK).

Genome Assembly and Taxonomic Classification

De novo genome assembly was performed using the Flye assembler (version 2.9.1‐b1780), optimized for long‐read Nanopore data. Assembly polishing was carried out using Minimap2 (version 2.24‐r1122) for read alignment and Racon (version 1.5.0) for consensus correction. Assembly quality metrics such as N50, total length, and number of contigs were assessed using QUAST (version 5.2.0). Read alignment processing was conducted using Samtools (version 1.13; HTSlib 1.13 + ds). All assembly and analysis steps were performed on the BV‐BRC platform (Bacterial and Viral Bioinformatics Resource Center). The assembled draft genomes were taxonomically classified using the Type Strain Genome Server (TYGS) (https://tygs.dsmz.de/) (Meier‐Kolthoff and Göker 2019). Genome annotation was performed using the NCBI Prokaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline (PGAP v2024‐03) (Tatusova et al. 2016).

Virulence and Antimicrobial Resistance Gene Profiling

For virulence gene detection, a custom reference dataset was compiled based on previously published studies involving V. anguillarum strains, including 240 virulence‐associated genes from strain 775 and 10 genes from strain RV22 (Naka et al. 2011; Castillo, Alvise, et al. 2017; Balado et al. 2018). Homologous genes in the isolates were identified using BLAST, applying minimum thresholds of 80% sequence identity and 60% coverage.

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) gene profiling was performed using the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD). For this purpose, the Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI) tool available on the CARD platform (https://card.mcmaster.ca/analyze/rgi) was used, and analyses were conducted with default parameters (Alcock et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2022).

Genome‐Based Phylogenetic Analysis

Core‐genome phylogeny was inferred from 3800 single‐copy orthologous genes identified via the BV‐BRC pipeline. Protein sequences were aligned with MAFFT v7.525 (Katoh and Standley 2013), and a maximum‐likelihood (ML) tree was reconstructed in IQ‐TREE v2.3.3 under the LG + G model with 1000 bootstrap replicates (Nguyen et al. 2015). Turkish isolates were analysed alongside reference strains representing distinct serotypes (O1: ATCC 68554 and 775; O2a: ATCC 43306; O3: CNEVA NB 11008). The resulting topology was independently validated on the Type Strain Genome Server (TYGS) using Genome BLAST Distance Phylogeny (GBDP) and digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) metrics (Meier‐Kolthoff and Göker 2019). Comparable phylogenomic strategies have been applied in recent studies of V. anguillarum (Castillo, Alvise, et al. 2017; Hansen et al. 2020; Saticioglu et al. 2025).

Determination of Phenotypic Virulence Traits of V. anguillarum Isolates (Hemolysis, Protease, Gelatinase, DNase, and Lipase Activities)

Phenotypic virulence traits of the isolates were assessed by caseinase and gelatinase hydrolysis, DNase, lipase (tributyrin and Tween 80 hydrolysis), and hemolysin activity. All assays were performed as previously described (Saticioglu et al. 2025) with minor modifications, and each isolate was tested in four to five independent experiments.

Caseinase, gelatinase, DNase, lipase, and hemolytic activities were determined using standard plate‐based assays as previously described, with minor modifications (Romalde and Toranzo 1993; Farrar and Reboli 2006; Austin and Austin 2016; Frans et al. 2013; Huang et al. 2013; Gao et al. 2018). Briefly, caseinase and gelatinase activities were assessed on TSA supplemented with skim milk or gelatin, respectively; DNase activity on DNase agar; lipase activity on tributyrin agar and Tween 80‐supplemented TSA; and hemolytic activity on sheep blood agar. Hemolysis was classified as α, β, or γ according to standard criteria; proteolytic activity was estimated as the ratio of the clearing zone diameter to the colony diameter; and the presence of a clear halo around colonies was considered a positive reaction for DNase and lipase activities.

Production of Hyperimmune Serum and ELISA‐Based Determination of Antibody Levels for V. anguillarum Serotyping

Hyperimmune sera against V. anguillarum reference strains O1 (NCIMB 2131), O2a (NCIMB 6 T), and O2b (NCIMB 2130) were produced in New Zealand white rabbits following the intravenous immunization protocol described by Sørensen and Larsen (1986), with minor modifications, under local ethical approval (17.05.2023, decision no. 2023–01). High‐titre antisera were obtained by repeated injections of formalin‐inactivated bacteria followed by live bacterial suspensions, and sera were collected, separated, and stored at −70°C until use (Altun et al. 2025). Total bacterial antigens for ELISA were prepared from TSA cultures by heat inactivation, washing, and sonication as described by Dudek et al. (2020). Indirect ELISA was performed according to Sørensen and Larsen (1986). Preliminary checkerboard titration experiments were used to determine the optimal working serum dilution and antigen coating dilutions for the V. anguillarum reference serotypes. Based on these analyses, rabbit sera were used at a working dilution of 1:100 in the comparative assays. Plates were coated with serotype‐specific antigens (10 μg well−1), incubated with rabbit sera, followed by HRP‐conjugated anti‐rabbit IgG and TMB substrate, and absorbance was measured at 405–450 nm using a microplate reader. The assay was used to determine specific antibody levels and to assess cross‐reactivity among serotypes.

Slide and Microwell Plate (Quantitative) Agglutination

Serological relationships among the isolates were assessed by slide and microwell agglutination tests using rabbit whole‐cell antisera and standardized antigen suspensions (~109 cells mL−1; McFarland no. 3), as previously described (Larsen et al. 1994; Santos et al. 1995, 1997). O‐antigens prepared by heat treatment were used for slide agglutination, and rapid visible agglutination was recorded as a positive reaction. Microwell agglutination assays were performed in duplicate using twofold serial dilutions of antisera, and titers were defined as the highest serum dilution showing macroscopic agglutination after incubation.

Whole‐Cell Protein Profiles by SDS‐PAGE

Whole‐cell protein profiles were analysed by SDS‐PAGE according to Laemmli (1970). Proteins were separated on 12% polyacrylamide gels, visualized with a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), and molecular weights were estimated using Precision Plus Protein Dual Colour Standards (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Banding patterns were analysed with Quantity One software (v4.6.5, Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and further compared in BioNumerics v7.6 (Applied Maths, Sint‐Martens‐Latem, Belgium) after gel normalization and band matching using standard optimization/tolerance settings. Similarity matrices were calculated with the Dice coefficient, and dendrograms were generated by UPGMA, as previously described (Santos et al. 1995).

Determination of Antigenic Profiles

Antigenic profiles were determined by Western blotting as previously described, with minor modifications (Altun et al. 2025). Briefly, whole‐cell proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE, transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes, and probed with hyperimmune rabbit sera (1:200), followed by HRP‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG (1:10,000). Immunoreactive bands were visualized using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio‐Rad) and Clarity Max substrate. Non‐immune rabbit serum was included as a negative control, and whole‐cell proteins of Escherichia coli were used to assess serum specificity (Sepúlveda et al. 2022).

In Vivo Virulence Assays

In vivo virulence assays were conducted at the Fish Experimental Unit of Çanakkale Onsekiz Mart University, Turkey, following local ethical approval (No. 2022–01/12). A total of 180 rainbow trout ( O. mykiss ; mean body weight 15.2 ± 0.3 g) were acclimated for 15 days under standard recirculating aquaculture conditions, and fish health status was verified bacteriologically before challenge. Isolates V12, V15, and V18 were selected on the basis of their prior phenotypic, serological, proteomic, and genomic characterization in order to represent distinct biological profiles within the Turkish collection: V12 as an atypical non‐agglutinating and genomically divergent isolate, V15 as a representative O1 isolate with an intermediate virulence‐related phenotype, and V18 as an O1 isolate showing strong enzymatic and immunogenic features suggestive of higher virulence. Bacterial suspensions were prepared from 24 h cultures, quantified by plate counts and optical density, and standardized to approximately 1 × 107 CFU fish−1, corresponding to a previously determined LD60 dose, as described by Li et al. (2024). Fish were anaesthetised with phenoxyethanol and intraperitoneally injected with 0.1 mL of the bacterial suspension, whereas control fish received sterile 0.85% NaCl. Mortalities were recorded daily for 21 days, and V. anguillarum infection was confirmed bacteriologically from kidney samples of dead fish. Statistical Analysis

Survival data obtained from the in vivo challenge experiments were analysed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves. Differences in survival among experimental groups were evaluated using the Mantel‐Cox (log‐rank) test, and pairwise comparisons were performed where appropriate. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Production of Hyperimmune Serum for V. anguillarum Serotyping and Determination of Antibody Levels by ELISA

The ELISA optimization assays demonstrated dilution‐dependent decreases in absorbance values for all 3 V. anguillarum reference serotypes, while negative serum absorbance values remained below 0.150. Based on these quantitative checkerboard analyses, a serum dilution of 1:100 was selected as the optimal working dilution for subsequent assays. The corresponding optimal coating antigen dilutions were 1:204800 for serotype O2b, 1:51200 for serotype O2a, and 1:204800 for serotype O1. At the selected working dilution, homologous antigen‐serum combinations showed strong reactivity and clear discrimination from negative controls, confirming the specificity of the hyperimmune sera. Whole‐Cell Protein Profiles of V. anguillarum Strains

The whole‐cell protein profiles of 23 field isolates of V. anguillarum , together with three reference strains (NCIMB 2131 [Serotype O1], NCIMB 6 T [Serotype O2a], and NCIMB 2130 [Serotype O2b]), were analysed using the SDS‐PAGE method. The resulting protein bands were comparatively evaluated with BioNumerics 7.0 software (Applied Maths, Belgium). A dendrogram constructed on the basis of protein similarity ratios is presented in Figure 1, while whole‐cell protein type (WCPT) groupings are shown in Table S2.

UPGMA dendrogram of V. anguillarum isolates based on SDS‐PAGE whole‐cell protein profiles analysed in BioNumerics after gel normalization and band matching. The overall clustering pattern was confirmed in repeated analyses.

Protein similarity among isolates ranged between 96.6% and 100%. Notably, 73.91% of the field isolates exhibited 99%–100% similarity to the O1 and O2a reference strains, indicating a high degree of homogeneity at the protein level. The similarity between the O1 and O2a reference strains themselves was 99%–100%, whereas the similarity between the O1 and O2b reference strains ranged from 97% to 100%.

Across all V. anguillarum isolates, major common bands were consistently detected at approximately 95, 77, 47, 35, 26, and 14 kDa. In general, protein bands were observed in the range of 14–110 kDa, with the total number of bands varying between 16 and 19 among isolates. These shared bands were considered putative immunodominant proteins and were regarded as potentially important for vaccine development studies. The dendrogram constructed at a 97% similarity threshold revealed that field and reference strains clustered into three distinct groups (Figure 1). This finding indicates that while field isolates can be classified based on their protein profiles, they remain genetically and serotypically closely related.

Comparison of Immunogenic Protein Profiles of V. anguillarum Strains by Western Blot Analysis

Western blot analyses revealed distinct immunogenic differences between field isolates and reference strains of V. anguillarum . When probed with antiserum against serotype O1 (NCIMB 2131), most field isolates displayed multiple bands ranging from 10 to 110 kDa (Figure 2). Notably, bands between 12 and 18 kDa were observed exclusively in O1 reference and field strains, suggesting that these proteins may represent serotype‐specific antigenic markers. In contrast, the field isolate V12 was differentiated from the reference strains of serotypes O2a and O2b by the absence of the 48 kDa band.

Genomic Analysis

Whole‐genome sequences of 23 V. anguillarum isolates of Turkish origin were generated using Oxford Nanopore Technology. Genome sizes ranged between 4.32 and 4.57 Mb with GC contents of 44.3%–44.8%. Assemblies consisted of 4–34 contigs with N50 values of 2.4–3.1 Mb. On average, 3900–4100 CDSs, 75–105 tRNAs, and 8–11 rRNAs were annotated per isolate. (Table S3).

Core‐genome phylogeny (based on amino acid sequences of 3800 single‐copy genes via BV‐BRC) revealed that most Turkish isolates clustered within a monophyletic clade together with O1 serotype reference strains, particularly V. anguillarum 775, ATCC 68554 (O1, USA), LMG 12010 (O1, Greece), and VA1 (sea bass). Isolates V28 and V152 grouped within this O1 clade, whereas V12 and V25 branched on more distant lineages from the main O1 cluster. In contrast, Turkish isolates exhibited greater phylogenetic distances to reference strains of other serotypes, such as ATCC 43306 (O2a), 030305–1/5 (O2b), and CNEVA NB 11008 (O3) (Figure 4).

Overall, 261 genes (256 virulence‐associated and 5 antimicrobial resistance genes) were investigated, and their distribution across functional categories is summarized in Tables S4 and S5. Iron acquisition systems were well represented, with 61 genes related to vanchrobactin, anguibactin, and heme/iron transport. Vanchrobactin and heme uptake systems were conserved in all isolates, whereas the anguibactin cluster was absent in a subset of strains, including V12 and V100. Toxin‐ and enzyme‐related genes were also widely distributed. All isolates carried six hemolysin genes, including vah1 and delta‐VPH. The seven genes associated with RTX toxins and their auxiliary components were conserved across all genomes, indicating the presence of a complete RTX system in the studied population. Eight genes encoding secreted enzymes such as phospholipase, collagenase, lipase, and metalloproteases were detected in all isolates, while a non‐hemolytic enterotoxin gene was uniquely identified in V12.

Motility‐ and adhesion‐related functions formed another major block of the virulence repertoire. A total of 88 genes associated with flagella, motility, and chemotaxis were identified, including an expanded mcp1‐20 family, suggesting a high chemotactic and motile potential. All isolates carried an apparently intact Type IV pilus (T4P) system, with msh pili consistent with roles in adhesion and colonization. Similarly, 31 genes encoding components of the Type VI secretion system (T6SS) were detected: T6SS System II (vstA‐vstK) was intact in all isolates, whereas elements of T6SS System I were partially absent in some strains, notably V12 and V25.

Quorum sensing and regulatory functions were broadly conserved. 11 quorum sensing genes, including luxS, luxR, and vanT, were present in all isolates, alongside four global or virulence regulators (hns, hfq, hlyU, and vpsT). In addition, three genes classified as “other” virulence‐related factors encoded fimbrial proteins and multimodular transpeptidases, likely contributing to cell surface structure and adhesion. The antimicrobial resistance (AMR) gene profile comprised five genes. The quinolone resistance determinant qnrVC6 was detected in all isolates, while tet (34) was present in 95.6% of strains and absent only in V85. Notably, qnrS2, sul2, and tet(B) were uniquely identified in V28, suggesting that this isolate may harbor additional resistance determinants associated with mobile genetic elements.

In Vivo Virulence Tests

Experimental infections of rainbow trout with V. anguillarum O1 isolates resulted in clinical manifestations characteristic of vibriosis. Infected fish exhibited darkening of skin pigmentation; petechial haemorrhages around the mouth, the bases of paired fins, and the anus; as well as marked erythema and haemorrhages in the ventral region (Figure 6).

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive characterization of field isolates of V. anguillarum from Turkey at phenotypic, antigenic, proteomic, and genomic levels, thereby filling a gap in the literature and contributing to aquaculture pathogen control strategies. Approximately 87% of the isolates expressed at least three phenotypic virulence markers, a pattern previously associated with high mortality in vibriosis outbreaks (Frans et al. 2013). The concurrent presence of β‐hemolysis, gelatinase, caseinase, DNase, and lipase in isolates V15, V18, and V21 agrees with evidence that DNase promotes bacterial dissemination through degradation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) (Gao et al. 2018) and that lipase activity correlates with elevated virulence (Huang et al. 2013). These enzyme profiles support the use of phenotypic virulence traits as practical indicators when selecting strains for vaccine development and challenge experiments.

Serological analyses based on hyperimmune rabbit sera against V. anguillarum serotypes O1, O2a, and O2b allowed robust serotyping of field isolates and assessment of antigenic relationships. Indirect ELISA at 1:100 serum dilution clearly distinguished homologous from heterologous antigen‐serum combinations, confirming pronounced antigenic differences among serotypes and validating hyperimmune sera as reliable serotyping tools, in line with the extended scheme of Pedersen et al. (1999). This strong serotype specificity is consistent with the view that serotype matching is critical for protective immunity and vaccine performance in fish (Colquhoun and Lillehaug 2014) and supports the emphasis on antigen specificity and controlled cross‐reaction in DIVA‐compatible vaccination strategies (Monaghan et al. 2016).

Serotyping revealed a clear predominance of O1 among Turkish field isolates: 22 of 23 reacted only with O1 antiserum, while no reactions were obtained with O2a or O2b antisera. Microplate agglutination confirmed O1 positivity with titers between 40 and 640 (Larsen 1983; Pedersen et al. 1999), whereas O2a and O2b reference strains reacted exclusively with homologous antisera. These observations reinforce the high antigenic specificity of O‐serogroups and help explain why serotype compatibility is a key determinant of vaccine efficacy (Colquhoun and Lillehaug 2014). In contrast, isolate V12 did not agglutinate with any reference antisera, consistent with non‐agglutinating or atypical O‐antigen profiles previously linked to uncharacterized O‐serogroups (Grisez and Ollevier 1995; Pedersen et al. 1999). Such atypical variants are relevant for both serological diagnostics and future vaccine formulations.

Proteomic and immunoproteomic analyses supported a picture of highly conserved but differentially immunogenic surface structures. SDS‐PAGE revealed 97%–100% similarity among whole‐cell protein profiles of field and reference strains, with consistent bands at approximately 95, 77, 47, 35, 26, and 14 kDa. Conserved outer membrane and surface proteins have long been recognized as key targets of the host immune response (Chart and Trust 1984), and our findings agree with functional studies showing that outer membrane porins such as the 38 kDa OmpU contribute to bile resistance and biofilm formation (Wang et al. 2023) and that antimicrobial proteins in the skin mucus of Scophthalmus maximus are important for defence against V. anguillarum (Wang et al. 2023). Serotype‐dependent immune responses observed in Hippoglossus hippoglossus (Hoare et al. 2002) further support the dual importance of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and conserved outer membrane proteins in shaping protective immunity.

Western blotting refined these observations by identifying both conserved and serotype‐linked immunogenic bands. O1 reference antisera recognized dominant bands at 38–42 kDa and 60–70 kDa across most field isolates, consistent with omics‐based identification of outer membrane proteins such as OmpK (70–110 kDa) with cross‐protective potential (Jonsson et al. 2025). Sera raised against field isolates revealed bands at 28–32 kDa and 45–50 kDa in homologous and heterologous strains, which may correspond to band sizes for flagellin A, hemolysin, and OmpK, respectively (Mikkelsen et al. 2011; Baliga et al. 2018). These patterns illustrate how immunoproteomics complements genomics in antigen discovery, as highlighted by Mishra et al. (2023) and Natnan et al. (2021). In contrast, O2a and O2b antisera detected serotype‐specific bands only in the reference strains, and V12 again showed weak reactivity, reinforcing its atypical antigenic status (Grisez and Ollevier 1995; Pedersen et al. 1999). Collectively, the proteomics data support conserved outer membrane proteins as promising broad‐spectrum vaccine antigens, while serotype‐specific bands point to components relevant for serotype‐matched formulations.

Whole‐genome sequencing provided a genomic framework for interpreting these phenotypic patterns. Genome sizes (4.32–4.57 Mb) and GC contents (44.3%–44.8%) closely resembled those of well‐characterized strains such as NB10 and pJM1 plasmid‐bearing virulent isolates (Naka et al. 2011; Holm et al. 2015), indicating overall genomic stability within the species. No plasmids were detected in the Turkish isolates, suggesting that their pathogenicity is largely chromosomally encoded rather than dependent on pJM1‐like plasmids. Comparative analyses showed conserved iron uptake systems (e. g, vabA–E, tonB, exbB/D, feoABC, fbpABC), flagellar motility, type IV and Msh pili and quorum‐sensing systems, consistent with core virulence repertoires described for V. anguillarum from other regions (Castillo, Christiansen, et al. 2017; Hansen et al. 2020). At the same time, accessory virulence factors varied: the anguibactin siderophore cluster and Type VI secretion system T6SS‐I were incomplete or absent in some strains, particularly V12, whereas isolates V15 and V18 carried complete sets of RTX toxins, hemolysins, T6SS genes, siderophore transporters, and adhesins. These results align with the central role of RTX toxins and associated systems in V. anguillarum virulence (Balado et al. 2018) and suggest that variation in siderophore and T6SS loci contributes to the observed differences in virulence potential. A more focused comparison of the three strains used in the challenge assays further supports the link between accessory virulence composition and in vivo outcome. Isolate V12 was distinguished by its non‐agglutinating phenotype, weaker immunoreactivity, and more divergent genomic position relative to the main O1 cluster. In addition, it lacked the anguibactin cluster and exhibited an incomplete or reduced T6SS‐I repertoire, features consistent with its markedly attenuated phenotype in vivo. By contrast, V15 had a more typical O1 background and combined a strong phenotypic virulence profile with a broader virulence‐associated gene complement than V12, reflected in its intermediate mortality pattern. Among the three strains, V18 showed the most pronounced virulence‐associated phenotype, including lipase positivity, strong immunogenic banding patterns, and a broader accessory virulence repertoire, consistent with the highest mortality observed in the challenge experiments. Taken together, these findings suggest that differences in mortality among V12, V15, and V18 were not associated with a single determinant, but rather with the combined contribution of antigenic profile, accessory virulence loci, and phenotypic virulence traits.

The antimicrobial resistance (AMR) gene profiles have direct implications for treatment choices. All isolates carried the quinolone resistance determinant qnrVC6, and nearly all harboured tet (34), indicating a widespread potential for reduced susceptibility to quinolones and tetracyclines. The unique combination of qnrS2, sul2, and tet(B) in V28 suggests the presence of mobile genetic elements carrying multiple resistance determinants. In the broader context of increasing AMR in aquaculture, these findings support the need for prudent antibiotic use and for incorporating genomic AMR surveillance into routine monitoring when designing therapeutic strategies.

Phylogenetic analysis placed most Turkish isolates within the O1 serotype clade, whereas V12, V25, and V28 formed more divergent branches. The non‐agglutinating, weakly immunoreactive phenotype of V12 correlated with its genomic divergence, illustrating good concordance between antigenic and genomic variation. Turkish isolates clustered separately from hypervirulent European strains such as NB10 (Holm et al. 2015), suggesting local adaptation in rainbow trout aquaculture systems and reinforcing the need for region‐specific vaccines rather than relying solely on antigens derived from non‐local strains.

Integration of genomic, phenotypic, and in vivo data provided a coherent view of strain‐level differences in pathogenic potential. In experimental infections, V18 caused the highest mortality (70.4%), V15 showed intermediate virulence (35.2%), and V12 exhibited markedly attenuated virulence (5.6%). Kaplan–Meier survival analyses confirmed significant differences among isolates, and mortality patterns closely matched their virulence gene repertoires and phenotypic virulence traits profiles. The attenuated phenotype of V12 is consistent with loss of anguibactin and T6SS‐I components and echoes reports of weakened strains lacking key virulence determinants (Li and Ma 2017). Conversely, the broad virulence complement and strong enzymatic activity in V15 and V18 agree with the concept that multi‐factorial virulence, rather than single markers, better predicts disease outcome (Balado et al. 2018; Baliga et al. 2018).

From a vaccine candidate identification perspective, isolates V18 and V21 emerge as promising candidates for further vaccine evaluation. Both exhibit a broad virulence gene repertoire, robust immunogenic protein profiles, and an O1 background, warranting further vaccination and protection studies of formalin‐inactivated or subunit vaccines tailored to Turkish aquaculture. This conclusion is consistent with omics‐based vaccine strategies proposed for fish pathogens (Natnan et al. 2021; Mishra et al. 2023), which advocate genomics‐ and proteomics‐guided antigen selection. Conserved outer membrane proteins identified in this study, together with serotype‐specific antigens revealed by Western blotting and omics analyses (Mikkelsen et al. 2011; Jonsson et al. 2025), provide a rational antigen panel for future cross‐protective and serotype‐matched formulations.

In summary, Turkish V. anguillarum isolates possess a conserved core genome and virulence gene set, but display marked heterogeneity in accessory virulence and AMR determinants, which underlies variation in virulence among strains. These findings indicate that vaccines and therapies developed in other regions may not be uniformly effective in Turkish rainbow trout farming. Comprehensive genomic, phenotypic, and immunoproteomic characterization in the present study highlights V18 and V21 as promising candidates for region‐specific vaccine development and underscores the importance of genomics‐based surveillance, targeted vaccination, and informed antibiotic stewardship in aquaculture health management in Turkey. Because vaccination/challenge‐protection trials are not included in the present study, the protective efficacy of these candidate strains remains to be demonstrated.
