Electrical discharge machining of Ti6Al4V in deionized water: surface integrity and wear response for medical applications
Authors: Hamidullah Yaşar, Nihal Ekmekci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Titanium alloys are widely used in medical applications, particularly in dentistry and orthopedics, due to their biocompatibility, corrosion resistance, and low density. Electrical Discharge Machining (EDM) offers an effective method for producing microrough and porous surfaces, which are valued in the medical sector for supporting stable cell integration. However, EDM also generates surfaces with pronounced wear-relevant features, such as craters, microstructural changes, and common defects, including voids and cracks, which can significantly reduce wear performance. Research on the wear characteristics of EDM-processed surfaces and their relationship to surface integrity remains limited, highlighting the need for further investigation to optimize operational conditions for medical industry applications. In this study, medical-grade Ti6Al4V alloy was processed using EDM in deionized water with controlled pulse-on durations and currents. The resulting surfaces were evaluated under dry sliding conditions using a pin-on-disk tribometer to compare process parameters and wear responses. Comprehensive analyses of both worn and unworn surfaces, as well as subsurface features, were conducted using a surface profilometer, Scanning Electron Microscopy with Energy-Dispersive Spectroscopy (SEM/EDS), X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) and micro-hardness testing. To enhance the analytical framework and emphasize a principal contribution of this research, multiple Machine Learning (ML) techniques were employed to model and predict wear rates and layer thicknesses based on experimental parameters and data. Among the evaluated methods, CatBoost and KNN achieved the highest coefficients of determination for predicting the wear characteristics of the Ti6Al4V alloy, demonstrating the potential of ML to provide robust and generalizable analyses in biomedical surface engineering.

Introduction

Titanium alloys dominate biomedical applications due to their distinct properties, notably their enhanced cell adhesion on Electrical Discharge Machining (EDM) modified surfaces. These features compellingly demonstrate why EDM-processed titanium is essential for medical use, particularly in optimizing tribological performance. EDM imparts a macro-rough, isotropic morphology defined by interlocking craters. Micro-rough features within these craters and narrow, deep channels resembling cracks decisively support stable cell integration1. Strictly defined ranges of surface roughness and porosity are vital to achieving biocompatibility and osseointegration in implant materials. For titanium, studies conclusively identify a surface roughness (Ra) of 2.81–16.68 μm as optimal for biocompatibility, cell differentiation, and proliferation2. Furthermore, bioactive titanium oxides unequivocally improve cell viability, adhesion, and proliferation on EDM-modified surfaces with a surface roughness of 2.9–5.4 μm3. However, EDM creates surfaces with pronounced wear-relevant features, including craters, microstructural alterations, and common defects such as voids and cracks, which significantly reduce wear performance. These characteristics, determined by electrical parameters, directly dictate tribological performance and serve as the central focus of this study’s experimental investigation.

EDM operates by creating and controlling electrical discharges between oppositely charged electrodes in a dielectric liquid, leading to complex changes on the electrode surfaces. These discharges generate a molten zone, resulting in surface craters and distinct subsurface layers: a Resolidified Layer (RSL), a Heat Affected Layer (HAL), and an unaffected base. The RSL is typically harder yet brittle, containing debris, oxides, and metallurgical mixing. High thermal cycles induce residual stresses and hardness variations, leading to cracks that degrade wear performance. The HAL lies beneath the RSL, where tempered/martensitic changes occur in the solid state.

The thickness and continuity of the layers vary with pulse-on duration and current4. Wear tests and tribological performance evaluations on EDM surfaces are relatively rare in the literature and have been performed using reciprocating or pin-on-disc setups. Bonny et al.5 conducted dry reciprocating sliding tests on ZrO2–TiN composites against WC–Co cemented carbide. They observed that microstructure and surface defects lead to higher wear rates and increased Coefficients of Friction (CoF) on the modified surface.

Ekmekci and Güngör6 studied reciprocal sliding friction with alumina (Al2O3) pins on EDM-treated surfaces of duplex, austenitic, and ferritic steels. They found that tribological properties differ depending on the types of dielectric, electrode, and substrate materials used. Arun et al.7 obtained a composite coating layer on tool steel by using a semi-sintered nickel and tungsten-containing tool electrode in the EDM process, which showed superior wear resistance.

The wear response of coatings produced by Co-based tool electrodes in EDM on AISI 304 stainless steel was compared with that of traditional laser-cladded coatings. The dry-sliding wear resistance of the EDM surface was found to be 3–4 times higher than that of the laser-cladded surface8. The tribological performance of the RSL formed during EDM of AISI 304 L was examined, and it was found that pulse current significantly affects tribological results, regardless of the dielectric and electrode materials. The best tribological performance was achieved with a copper electrode in distilled water, compared with a graphite electrode in a paraffin oil dielectric9. Nitrogen gas-assisted EDM was used to form a TiN-enriched, hardened surface on Ti6Al4V, resulting in a significant improvement in hydrophobicity and a notable reduction in surface defects, wear rate, and average CoF compared with conventional EDM10.

Hydrocarbon-based oils are the most commonly used as dielectric liquids for EDM in commercial applications. However, it emits carcinogenic and toxic substances, such as benzene and carbon monoxide11. In this context, using water as a dielectric fluid provides a healthier, safer environment12. Furthermore, using water as a dielectric in EDM reduces surface roughness and recast layer thickness compared to oil. The higher thermal conductivity and specific heat of water facilitate more rapid cooling, while its lower viscosity allows for a more uniform formation of the resolidified layer13. Water promotes oxidation, which decreases recast layer thickness, whereas oil encourages carbonization, leading to increased surface roughness. Collectively, these factors contribute to improved wear characteristics when water is used as the dielectric medium9.

Titanium and its alloys are also preferred in the biomedical field due to their high strength-to-weight ratio, high corrosion resistance, and good biocompatibility properties14. Additionally, porous and rough surfaces promote cell differentiation and mineral accumulation. The machined surface microtexture and the formation of oxide, nitride, and phosphite phases on the RSL also make EDM a valuable option for biomedical applications15. The formation of a thick titanium oxide film on machined Ti alloy facilitates proper cell adhesion and viability by allowing oxygen to accumulate on the surface3.

The specific microstructural alterations and their linkage with the tribological performance of EDM surfaces have received limited evaluation. Previous research indicates that EDM parameters and materials strongly influence surface defects, wear, and friction behavior. Water-based dielectrics have emerged as safer, yielding improved surface uniformity and wear characteristics, making them increasingly significant for advanced EDM studies focused on wear16.

This study addresses the limited understanding of EDM wear characteristics and surface integrity features for medical applications. We investigate Ti6Al4V under different EDM settings in deionized water, assessing wear via pin-on-disk tests, surface and subsurface features by Scanning Electron Microscopy equipped with Energy Dispersive Spectrometry (SEM/EDS), X-ray Diffraction (XRD) and microhardness, and employ Machine Learning (ML) to clarify links between EDM parameters, surface structure, and tribological performance. Therefore, samples were processed using four different pulse currents and pulse-on durations in a parametric sequence. The wear responses of the modified surfaces were evaluated using a pin-on-disk tribometer. Subsequently, worn and unworn surfaces were analyzed using SEM/EDS and XRD. Subsurface features and microhardness around worn and unworn regions on sectioned samples were also examined to relate pulse current, pulse-on duration, surface topography, and microstructure to wear rate and CoF.

Experimental procedure

Materials and EDM

Material transfer from the electrode and dielectric liquid to the machined surface can occur during EDM17–19. To preserve biocompatibility after machining, a pure titanium (grade 2) tool electrode with a 25 mm diameter and deionized water as the dielectric fluid were selected. EDM was performed using an Ajan CNC 983 4-axis machine. A custom dielectric circulation system was implemented, incorporating a low-capacity tank and water-compatible circulation components (Fig. 1). The tool electrode was rotated to enhance circulation at the interelectrode gap during machining. Both sample and tool electrode surfaces were polished prior to EDM.

Measurements

The average roughness (Ra) of machined surfaces was measured using a Taylor Hobson Surtronic device with a 4 mm evaluation length. Each roughness value represents the mean of 10 repeated measurements per sample.

Surface features and topography of EDM-processed samples were analyzed using a SEM (FEI Quanta 450) equipped with EDS. Morphology of EDM and wear surfaces was examined with a profilometer (Huvitz, HRM-300) at 20X magnification. Phase composition analysis was performed using a Panalytical Empyrean XRD equipped with Co-Kα radiation source (λ = 1,78901 A⁰), operated at 40 kV and 40 mA. The measurements were conducted with a step size of 0,01 and total collection time of 31 min. Standard metallographic procedures, including sectioning, molding, grinding, polishing, and etching, were applied to the samples. Layer thicknesses were measured from cross-sectional images using ImageJ software.

Vickers microhardness tests were conducted on sample cross-sections using a Shimadzu microhardness tester with a 0.025 kg (245.25 mN) load applied for 25 s20. To evaluate hardness variation along the wear track, 12 measurements were taken at distinct points from the RSL to the bottom of the wear track. Measurements were taken from the machined surface through the wear cavity to the midpoint, along the sample’s axis of symmetry (Fig. 2).

Wear test parameters for EDM-processed surfaces were determined based on preliminary test results and recommendations from previous studies6,8. Wear tests were performed using a pin-on-disk tribometer with a 6 N load, a 540 m sliding distance, a 3 mm track radius, and a 50 mm/s speed. Machine learning models

Several supervised ML regressors, including Decision Tree (DT), Random Forest (RF), K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN), AdaBoost, and CatBoost, were employed to predict wear rate, HAL, and RSL thickness from experimental data. Python (version 3.13) served as the implementation platform. The scikit-learn library22 was used for hyperparameter optimization, performance evaluation, and model development. Data manipulation and numerical analysis were performed using the Numpy23 and Pandas24 libraries. Data visualization was performed using Matplotlib25 and Seaborn26. Given the small dataset size, the leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) procedure was selected, as the number of k-folds equals the number of data points, making it suitable for limited data27.

Experimental data were collected and prepared for analysis. The wear rate, HAL thickness, and RSL thickness datasets were evaluated separately by running the same code for each set. These data sets were partitioned into two-thirds for training and one-third for testing using the “train_test_split” function from scikit-learn. Input features were standardized during preprocessing, and the selected regression models were then applied. Each model underwent optimization using “RandomizedSearchCV” following selection. Parameter configurations for the machine learning models are provided in Table 2. Each model has distinct hyperparameters. The optimal parameters for the Decision Tree were min_samples_split = 2 and max_depth = 8. For Random Forest, the optimal parameters were n_estimators = 200, min_samples_split = 2, and max_depth = 6. The best configuration for KNN was n_neighbors = 2, weight = ‘uniform’, and p = 1. For the boosting models, the learning rate was set to 0.1, n_estimators to 100 for AdaBoost, and iterations to 200 and depth to 2 for CatBoost.

Several statistical evaluation metrics were used to compare the predictive performance of the machine learning models. These metrics include the coefficient of determination (R2), which measures the goodness of fit between the model and the data points; Mean Absolute Error (MAE), representing the mean absolute difference between actual and predicted values; Mean Squared Error (MSE), the average of the squared errors; and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), the square root of MSE. A higher R2 value indicates a better fit, with 1 representing a perfect fit; lower values for the error metrics are preferable, with zero being optimal28.

Results and discussions

Surface Topography of EDM’ed Surfaces

Crater size and depth are determined by pulse-on duration and current during EDM. The average crater diameter increases as the pulse-on duration is extended, while the crater depth increases with higher current. The thickness of the resolidified and heat-affected layers also increases in the surface sublayers. Surface texture and microstructure are affected by machining mechanisms, including secondary discharges, which may result in local remelting, rapid solidification, or non-uniform material removal.

The metal rapidly melts and solidifies upon contact with the dielectric liquid at the end of the pulse, resulting in distinct orientation traces. Rapid cooling produces surface crests and troughs with rounded structures on the crater rim, indicating the abrupt solidification of boiling material. Dense cracks form on the surface due to thermal expansion differences between the solidified layer and the substrate, as well as the generation of high stress. These cracks are particularly evident in crater troughs. Splitting of sparks, caused by variations in the discharge gap, leads to the formation of porous regions (Fig. 4a). High-magnification backscattered electron detector (BSED) images reveal dendritic structures, confirming high cooling rates during machining with an elevated pulse current and an extended pulse-on duration (Fig. 4b). Debris that remains unflushed or loosely attached to the surface indicates an inability to traverse the crater rims during discharge with low pulse current and short pulse-on duration (Fig. 4c).

Surface roughness measurements (Fig. 5) show an increase with higher pulse current and pulse-on duration, primarily due to increased pulse energy. However, this trend does not continue at the maximum pulse current of 42 A. The roughness stabilizes at 22 A and decreases at 42 A with a 400 µs pulse-on duration. These findings indicate a shift in the machining mechanism, likely related to changes in surface topography and subsurface layer characteristics.

Fig. 5Standard deviation and mean of surface roughness measurements of EDM’ed surfaces obtained from ten successive measurements for each sample.

Standard deviation and mean of surface roughness measurements of EDM’ed surfaces obtained from ten successive measurements for each sample.

An increase in pulse current and pulse-on duration also results in a larger plasma channel diameter and interelectrode gap. The crater diameter increases, forming a shealow shape. Additionally, the number of uneven electrical fields encountered during discharge increases due to the channel’s expanded volume. The channel propels and warms the debris ahead of it, mainly originating from the tool electrode. Meanwhile, the boundaries of the discharge channel become uneven, branching into secondary discharges. Debris particles caught among the secondary discharges heat up during the pulse-on duration. Suddenly, they are drawn toward the work material and adhere to its surface due to the negative pressure generated when the discharge ends. As a result, the machined surface exhibits particles bonded around the crater edges, which are released from the tool electrode (Fig. 6). The phenomenon also explains the saturation of roughness when high pulse current and pulse-on duration are used during EDM. The branching of discharge channels reduces surface roughness, even with high-energy pulses. The secondary or tertiary sparks created by dividing the main discharge channel lead to the formation of shallow, small craters rather than deep, splayed ones.

3D profiles (a), SEM(b)-BSED(c) images of EDM surfaces using 7 A pulse current 100 µs pulse-on time.

Surface XRD patterns indicate the formation of complex oxide phases on modified surfaces. The predominance of oxide peaks suggests elevated dissolved oxygen levels resulting from extended pulse-on durations (Fig. 7). Building on this, titanium oxides, particularly TiO₂, are widely recognized as leading biomedical materials due to their superior biocompatibility, chemical stability, and corrosion resistance. This is significant because, as a passive, non-toxic layer on implants, TiO₂ facilitates osseointegration. Additionally, its nanostructured forms provide drug-delivery capabilities and antibacterial properties through photo-induced reactive oxygen species29. However, it should be noted that the incidence of surface cracks, pores, and irregular appendages also increases with prolonged pulse-on time, which compromises the mechanical strength of the modified layer with respect to wear response30.

Evaluation of the coefficient of friction and wear rate

Figure 8 presents the evolution of the CoF over time for EDM samples, with the ground-to-polished specimen included for comparison. The CoF versus time is displayed on a logarithmic scale to highlight the initial sliding behavior. The unmachined ground sample shows a CoF of 0.4–0.55, with minor fluctuations during the initial sliding stage. After approximately 40 s of wear, the CoF stabilizes and fluctuates around 0.4. EDM-treated surfaces subjected to a 7 A pulse current and pulse-on durations below 400 µs display similar CoF trends, although surfaces modified with a 400 µs pulse-on duration exhibit a noticeably delayed response (Fig. 8a). Increasing the pulse current to 12 A results in trends comparable to those at 7 A, with a slight delay in response observed for the surface treated with a 6 µs pulse-on duration (Fig. 8b). At a pulse current of 22 A, the delay exceeds that observed for the surface treated with a 400 µs pulse-on duration (Fig. 8c). Increasing the pulse current to 42 A revealed a similar trend, with the largest delay at 400 µs, followed by 6 µs. These results indicate a complex relationship between the coefficient of friction and the energy released during sparking, as well as material migration from both the tool electrode and the dielectric medium (Fig. 8d).

Figure 9 presents the wear rate and wear depth of samples that were EDM-treated and polished to a ground finish. The wear rate increases with longer pulse-on duration, regardless of pulse current level, and reaches a peak at the maximum tested pulse-on duration. Notably, despite being the highest tested, the 42 A pulse current results in lower wear rates than the other pulse currents at comparable pulse-on durations. Among the 16 EDM-treated surfaces, only 2 showed a slightly lower wear rate than the polished-to-ground surface: one treated with 7 A and the other with 42 A, with a 6 µs pulse-on duration.

Worn EDM surfaces display grooves, debris accumulation, and varying levels of delamination. The samples shown in Fig. 10 were selected as representative examples because they illustrate the extremes of surface roughness and wear rate. The EDM-treated surface in Fig. 10a, produced with a 7 A pulse current and 6 µs pulse-on duration, demonstrates lower surface roughness and wear rate, with only minor grooves and small debris clusters present. In contrast, the sample in Fig. 10b, machined with a 400 µs pulse-on duration, exhibits some of the highest roughness and wear rates observed. This surface features deep grooves, where dislodged particles accumulate as debris clusters within the worn region. Moreover, EDS analysis indicates a reduction in oxygen content in the worn area compared to the unworn region (Fig. 10b). Hard particles detached from sliding components act as abrasive agents, leading to pronounced abrasive wear. Debris clusters are distributed randomly and exhibit smearing-like morphologies, suggesting the simultaneous operation of both abrasive and adhesive wear mechanisms.

The results for CoF and wear rates show distinct responses to surface topographical features and subsurface microstructures. Each of these characteristics is influenced by pulse-on duration and current in complex ways. During sliding, asperities are initially removed from the surfaces. The observed delay in CoF reflects the hardness and surface bonding strength associated with specific topographical features, such as globular attachments and crater crests. Additionally, the thickness and microstructure of subsurface features, such as the resolidified and heat affected layers, significantly influence the wear mechanism.

The subsurface and wear

The thickness and properties of subsurface layers are strongly influenced by the pulse-on duration and current applied during EDM, which, in turn, affect the wear response. Specifically, RSL exhibits the machined surface microtexture and the formation of hard oxide phases. Both features are advantageous for biomedical applications. However, the texture of this layer contains multiple discontinuities, such as bumpy, piled-up regions and numerous cracks, which may decrease mechanical strength16 Importantly, these discontinuities can also serve as cell-adhesion sites, thereby enhancing osseointegration. Therefore, evaluating wear responses and subsurface characteristics necessitates a comprehensive understanding of both mechanical and biomedical considerations. At the onset of sliding, worn hard particles from the RSL interact with both the counter body and the work material. Consequently, the structure, thickness, and crack density of the RSL contribute to the initial wear response. HAL beneath the RSL forms due to a rapid temperature gradient that exceeds the solid-state phase transformation temperature. The thickness of the HAL is determined by the pulse energy applied and the phase transformation temperatures of the work material. This layer typically displays tempered and over-tempered microstructures that are more complex than those of the base material but softer than the RSL. After the RSL is worn away during sliding, the HAL’s wear response becomes predominant.

Samples were sectioned from the EDM-processed and worn surfaces following the Pin-on-Disk Test. The cut specimens were then molded to facilitate analysis of the symmetric wear track produced by wear and to examine subsurface characteristics resulting from EDM, utilizing SEM/EDS (Fig. 11). Subsequently, the thickness of layers formed by the EDM process, wear track features, and micro-hardness profiles in depth were measured for each sample.

The layers are distinguished by their appearance in transitional regions observed under the optical microscope. Figure 12 shows the optical images of the unworn regions of the 16 analyzed samples, arranged in a parameter matrix. The RSL exhibits the characteristic appearance, with irregularities and complex microstructures in the vicinity of the surface. It is clearly distinguished from the HAL, which appears relatively lighter. RSL and HAL thicknesses increase with longer pulse-on durations, as well as with greater uniformity and higher crack density. An increase in pulse current during EDM significantly increases discharge energy, leading to greater melting of the workpiece material and higher temperature gradients. This localized thermal input produces a thicker, non-uniform RSL and a deeper, expanded HAL. These modifications increase thermal stresses and promote the formation of microcracks, porosity, and raised surface asperities, as demonstrated by Kumar et al.3 and Rajhi et al.31.

Sectional EDS line analysis was conducted on all affected layers of the EDM-modified surface, employing a 400 µs pulse-on time and a 42 A pulse current. The analysis revealed that Ti concentration increases progressively from the surface toward the interior. Ti content declines within the RSL and subsequently increases at the boundary between the RSL and the HAL, which marks the transition from resolidified to thermally altered but unmelted material. Beyond the RSL-HAL interface, Ti concentration stabilizes within the base material. Al and V exhibit similar distribution patterns across the RSL-HAL region. In contrast, O content exhibits an inverse trend. Elevated O levels indicate dissolution and the predominant formation of oxide phases within the RSL (Fig. 13).

Increased RSL thickness is therefore inferred to produce a thicker surface layer containing complex O phases, primarily TiO, with smaller quantities of AlO and VO. Aluminum oxide is widely recognized as a bio-inert ceramic, suitable for medical implants due to its excellent corrosion resistance, high hardness, and minimal adverse biological responses in both in vitro and in vivo studies32. In contrast, vanadium oxide generally demonstrates limited biocompatibility, often displaying concentration-dependent cytotoxicity, increased reactive oxygen species production, and the potential to induce harmful cellular responses. However, low-dose engineered forms are under investigation for antibacterial properties or specialized coatings33. Therefore, RSL thickness is a primary factor in assessing biocompatibility concerns for medical applications and in determining wear response.

The zero point denotes the surface position after adjustment for RSL thickness. In this analysis, HAL thickness is defined as the region extending from the RSL to the total affected layer. A more precise comparison can be achieved by assessing the total affected layer thickness, defined as the sum of the RSL and HAL, relative to the total wear depth, as well as by considering the RSL-to-HAL ratio. Therefore, the ratios are indicated by dotted lines.

Fig. 14Layer thicknesses, wear depth ratios, and operational parameters in EDM. a) 7 A, b) 12 A, c) 22 A, d) 42 A.

The results indicate that the total affected layer thickness increases with both pulse-on duration and current. The RSL-to-HAL ratio remains approximately 0.7, though with considerable deviation. For pulse currents of 7, 12, and 22 A, the ratio of total affected layer thickness to wear depth closely follows the trend observed for RSL thickness (Fig. 14a-c). At a pulse current of 42 A, the RSL-to-HAL ratio becomes stable, while the total affected layer-to-wear depth ratio exhibits greater variability (Fig. 14d). In particular, measurements of RSL and HAL thickness in thicker layers provide increased precision when observed under an optical microscope, leading to a more accurate determination of the RSL-to-HAL ratio for the 42 A pulse current. Based on these findings, it is plausible to assume that the RSL-to-HAL ratio remains constant. However, variations in the total affected layer-to-wear depth ratio suggest alterations in RSL microstructure, as both properties are now measured with increased precision. In conclusion, the RSL demonstrates greater sensitivity to pulse-on duration, indicating the need for further investigation beyond thickness. Consequently, hardness measurements of the individual layers are performed to clarify the relationship with observed wear rates and to establish a connection with surface oxide formation.

Microhardness variations in the subsurface

Microhardness measurements serve as reference points for layer identification and depth profiling. Measurements were performed on each wear-track segment, including unworn regions, to evaluate changes. Vickers microhardness values were determined by measuring the distance from the center of the worn cavity to the machined surface (Fig. 15). EDM with low pulse-on durations of 6 and 25 µs produces relatively thin layers, which may lead to edge effects and associated hardness variations. Therefore, additional hardness measurements were conducted using low indentation loads to validate these observations. The results demonstrate a clear distinction between the RSL and HAL. The RSL hardness is significantly greater than that of the HAL, especially at the longest pulse-on duration of 400 µs. For instance, the RSL attains a hardness of 1550 HV, indicative of the formation of complex titanium oxide (TiO).

In comparison, the HAL exhibits a maximum hardness of 466 HV near the RSL and gradually decreases toward the base metal’s hardness of approximately 360 HV. RSL hardness increases with pulse-on duration, ranging from 567 to 1550 HV. These results indicate that mean RSL hardness is primarily influenced by pulse-on duration rather than pulse current. At elevated current levels, shorter pulse-on durations lead to reduced plasma expansion and increased electron bombardment during sparking. The limited contact area of the spark limits its interaction with the dielectric liquid, thereby slowing the formation of TiO after the spark terminates. As a result, the RSL exhibits decreased hardness by the end of the sparking cycle.

Moreover, hardness values near the base of the wear cavity range from 360 to 413 HV. Despite wear extending to the base metal, the measured hardness remains elevated. Gain et al.34 attribute this phenomenon in Ti6Al4V work material under dry sliding conditions to surface plastic deformation under high load, which increases temperature, reduces grain size, and increases nano-hardness at the worn base. Low surface roughness, thin heat-affected layers, and EDM under these conditions exerts minimal influence on wear response. When the pulse-on duration increases, the wear rate rises rapidly, indicating a negative response of the RSL. Therefore, hard asperities initially removed from the surface subsequently contribute to abrasion during sliding, as the HAL is relatively thin and cannot withstand grinding. At a pulse-on duration of 100 µs and pulse current of 7 A, a reduction in wear rate is observed. This phenomenon is supported by backscattered electron images, which show traces of secondary discharges as particles at crater edges that have migrated from the tool electrode. These fine particles act as solid lubricants and are removed during sliding, thereby slightly reducing the wear rate. Further increasing the pulse-on duration to 400 µs results in a marked increase in the wear rate (Fig. 9). At this stage, the RSL exhibits an average thickness of approximately 20 μm and contains numerous defects, including cracks and micro-voids (Fig. 12). CoF results indicate a significant delay in saturation time, suggesting increased resistance to the removal of RSL asperities under high contact stresses (Fig. 8). These findings confirm that the hard RSL features are deeply embedded in the surface. Following the saturation delay, abrasive wear predominates, resulting in the highest wear rate observed in the study.

In conclusion, wear rates under fixed-pulse current conditions remain consistent across different pulse-on durations. Increasing the pulse current to 42 A results in a significant reduction in wear rates, demonstrating the advantage of higher current. Elevated pulse current produces thicker Resolidified layers (RSLs) and heat-affected layers (HALs), along with a notable decrease in wear rates at 42 A. The resulting surfaces (Fig. 16) display a macro-rough, isotropic morphology characterized by interlocking craters and narrow, deep channels with cracks. These surface features facilitate stable cell integration and achieve the surface roughness and porosity necessary for medical applications. Furthermore, bioactive titanium oxides have been reported to enhance cell viability, adhesion, and proliferation on EDM-modified surfaces. Thus, a high-pulse current of 42 A is appropriate for fulfilling medical requirements and improving surface wear resistance. However, additional in vivo and in vitro studies are necessary to validate the suitability of these surfaces.

Machine learning results

Multiple machine learning techniques were employed to model and predict wear rates and layer thicknesses based on the analyzed experimental parameters and collected data, thereby strengthening the analytical framework and underscoring a principal contribution of this study.

A prediction-real values plot, in other words, a scatter plot, provides a visual comparison of each ML regressor’s efficiency, and the ideal is a tight clustering of data around the line. Figures 17, 18 and 19 compares predicted and actual values for the training and test sets of five ML models for each output of wear rate, HAL, and RSL thickness. As shown in Fig. 17, DT, AdaBoost, and CatBoost regressors performed well, accurately predicting the wear rate on unseen data. The KNN regressor shows noticeable deviation from the diagonal for both the training and test data. The RF model fits the training data better than KNN, but, as with KNN, testing performance decreases with increasing distance from the diagonal line. Figures 18 and 19 presents the scatterplots of the predicted and actual HAL and RSL thicknesses. Integration of all layer measurements ensured a more robust estimation. From the scatterplots, the ML regressors perform well at predicting both thicknesses, so most points are close to the ideal diagonal line.

Table 3 presents the predictive performance of the models, including R2, MAE, MSE, and RMSE for the 3 outputs mentioned above. When considering the wear rate in Table 3, the CatBoost regressor achieves the highest test R2 of 0.88, making it the most accurate model. The train R² of the same regressor is also 0.97. This relatively small difference between the test and training scores indicates that the model generalizes to unseen data. The AdaBoost regressor achieved a test R2 of 0.82 and a training R2 of 0.99. Although the prediction performance is lower than CatBoost’s, it still demonstrates successful generalization. Interestingly, the DT regressor achieved a perfect fit on the training data (train R2 = 1), but the test score (R2) is significantly lower at 0.81. In this case, the DT regressor may overfit to the training data due to the limited data. However, the test score shows that the model can recall the training data but fails to generalize to new data. RF has lower test and train R2 scores, which may need further hyperparameter tuning. Another interesting result is the KNN score. The test score of R2 is 0.74, which is higher than the train score of 0.66. The regressor may underfit the training data. Besides, it has the lowest test score, and it is less prone to overfitting than the DT regressor.

Furthermore, the error metrics MAE, MSE, and RMSE also confirm these results. CatBoost generated the lowest error in all metrics (MAE = 9.96 × 10⁻⁵, MSE = 1.51 × 10⁻⁸, RMSE = 1.23 × 10⁻⁴). On the contrary, the KNN regressor exhibits the highest. In conclusion, among the evaluated ML regressors, CatBoost provided the best results, including predictive and generalization performance, making it more applicable to this specific work and data. In contrast to the wear rate predictions, KNN is the best model for predicting HAL and RSL thicknesses, along with Catboost. In terms of predictive performance, the R2 scores exceeded 0.9 for HAL and 0.8 for RSL, demonstrating strong model fit across all regressors. Error values for layer thickness predictions are larger than those for wear rate, which explains the presence of outlier data points. A MAE of 2 ± 1 indicates that our model is off by 2 ± 1 units in predicting thicknesses.

Overall, this section provides detailed evaluations using multiple regression models, including hyper-parameter tuning via RandomizedSearch, cross-validation with the Leave-One-Out (LOOCV) technique, and a train-test split. However, evaluating ML models on a small experimental dataset may lead to overfitting or underfitting. Moreover, limited data may limit regressors’ ability to learn. Adding more input parameters, such as wear load, distance, and dielectric type, may increase data capacity. Furthermore, hyperparameter tuning, subject to CPU capacity, can be further improved, and grid-based procedures may be used with various ML regressors beyond those employed in this research.

According to the CatBoost feature importance, which has one of the highest coefficients of determination with KNN, pulse-on duration is the most effective parameter for wear rate, HAL, and RSL thicknesses. The method involves various parameters that are simultaneously effective, including pulse-on duration, pulse current, workpiece properties, and dielectric content. Different spark forms occur due to branching during machining. Additionally, short circuits caused by discontinuities may result in local surface differences. Simultaneous control of these parameters is difficult, and it is possible to obtain different surface integrity under the same machining conditions. These differences do not contradict the basic rules of the process. Although the high or low pulse current affects surface integrity, it is also necessary to allow the time required for completion. Sudden alterations in high current may overheat the surface, creating irregular forms, while low-current, long-continued pulse currents provide more controlled surface changes by stabilizing heat transfer35.

The results of this study provide a better understanding of the wear properties of Ti6Al4V. Despite its chaotic nature, EDM exhibits numerous nonlinear relationships among its determinants36. In the literature on ML/EDM, some papers have reported R2 values exceeding 0.9. These papers address the MRR and TWR, which are direct outputs of the process. On the other hand, wear resistance of a material is based on a combination of numerous factors such as volume, depth, humidity, rate, etc37. Taking these into consideration, a coefficient of determination above 0.8 is therefore a significant outcome, meaning a powerful generalization capacity.

Conclusions

The development of functional surfaces has received significant attention due to the increasing demand for efficient medical devices in the biomedical industry. To address both mechanical and biomedical requirements, researchers are advancing commercial machining techniques. EDM has become a leading method for roughening implant surfaces. Although EDM inherently produces a porous surface with overlapping craters, effective surface roughening depends on activating specific mechanisms and establishing optimal processing conditions. The experimental results and analyses from this study are summarized as follows:

Surface roughness increases with higher pulse current and longer pulse-on duration, then stabilizes at elevated pulse energies. Surface topography, subsurface properties, pulse timing, and current are closely associated with the coefficient of friction and wear.The RSL exhibits a microtexture defined by hard oxide phases, particularly TiO₂, with characteristics influenced by pulse-on duration. This microtexture contains discontinuities, such as bumpy, piled-up regions and numerous cracks, which can reduce mechanical strength. However, these same features -by creating a rough, porous surface- directly promote the formation of stable cell-integration regions that meet the surface roughness and porosity requirements essential for medical applications.The findings suggest that the use of high-pulse current is an effective strategy to meet biomedical requirements and improve wear resistance.Further analysis of supervised machine learning algorithms showed that CatBoost, an ensemble boosting model, and the instance-based k-nearest neighbors (KNN) model achieved the highest coefficients of determination for predicting wear rate and layer thicknesses of the Ti6Al4V alloy. These results indicate their suitability for future research in medical applications.

Limitations and furher studies

EDM exhibits complex, chaotic behavior, characterized by multiple nonlinear interactions among influencing factors. The current findings enhance the understanding of Ti6Al4V wear properties and elucidate the relationships among these properties, wear response, and specific medical requirements. However, additional in vitro and in vivo analyses of osseointegration and corrosion are required to validate the suitability of Ti6Al4V for medical applications. Results from these further investigations could yield new data to be integrated into the machine learning model, thereby increasing its accuracy and effectiveness as an optimization tool for targeted medical applications.

Furthermore, the observed material migration from both the tool electrode and the dielectric medium suggests the potential to generate diverse functional surfaces by selecting appropriate combinations of work material, tool electrode, and dielectric liquid. Building on this, the use of powder-metallurgical electrodes and powder mixing in EDM represents a promising strategy for developing a new generation of biomaterials with functional surfaces that can better meet specific performance requirements in medical applications.
