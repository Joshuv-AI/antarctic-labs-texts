BRASH Syndrome: A Vicious Yet Reversible Cause of AV Nodal Blockade
Authors: Anuj Subedi, Jose Luis Aparicio, Haaris Siddiq, Nofil Syed, Komal Paudyal, Sajjad Ahmed Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEarly recognition of BRASH syndrome is crucial, as the synergistic interplay of AV nodal blockade, renal failure, and hyperkalemia can rapidly lead to circulatory collapse. Prompt identification and withdrawal of offending agents enable complete recovery from this reversible condition.Keywords: bradycardia, diagnosis, hyperkalemia, management, renal failure

Introduction

BRASH syndrome is a rare clinical phenomenon observed in patients taking atrioventricular (AV) nodal blockers. To date, approximately 70 cases have been reported in the literature [1]. The pathophysiology of Bradycardia–Renal Failure–Atrioventricular Nodal Blockade–Shock–Hyperkalemia (BRASH) syndrome involves impaired clearance of AV nodal agents and potassium. The synergistic interaction between AV nodal blockade and hyperkalemia‐induced cardiac dysconduction can lead to circulatory collapse, even at lower‐than‐expected doses of either agent [2]. Hyperkalemia alone can precipitate bradycardia, as elevated potassium levels reduce membrane excitability and interfere with cardiac action potentials by preventing repolarization, ultimately resulting in isolated bradycardia [3]. Conversely, AV nodal blockers act on β₁‐receptors in cardiomyocytes, suppressing cardiac impulse conduction and leading to bradycardia [4]. BRASH syndrome is therefore not merely a manifestation of severe hyperkalemia or AV nodal blocker toxicity but rather a vicious cycle in which each component exacerbates the other, potentially resulting in worsening bradycardia and renal failure due to reduced renal perfusion [5]. Patients may present with a wide range of symptoms, from asymptomatic bradycardia to multiorgan failure [1]. Here, we present a case of a patient who developed BRASH syndrome while on verapamil therapy.

Case Description

Case History/Examination

A 64‐year‐old female with a history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, pulmonary embolism on apixaban, asthma, diabetes mellitus, and hypothyroidism on levothyroxine presented to the emergency department with acute shortness of breath since morning. Over the preceding 3–4 days, she had experienced progressively worsening low back pain and abdominal pain. The abdominal pain was described as severe, diffuse, and cramping in nature, associated with nausea that began the previous evening. Since then, she had felt increasingly dizzy and short of breath.

She denied chest pain, hemoptysis, syncope, palpitations, recent surgery, recent travel, melena, hematochezia, or sick contact. On arrival, she appeared in significant respiratory distress with bradycardia and hypotension. She was initially placed on BiPAP for presumed pulmonary edema but was later weaned to a nasal cannula as her breathing improved.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatments)

On evaluation, initial laboratory results revealed profound hyperkalemia (8.3 mmol/L), hyperglycemia (553 mg/dL), and metabolic acidosis (TCO2 15 mmol/L, ABG 7.17/35 mmHg with a base deficit of −14.3). She was initially suspected to have diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and was started on an insulin drip; however, her anion gap was modest at 18, and β‐hydroxybutyrate was normal, leading to the discontinuation of the insulin infusion. Her lactate level was elevated at 5.7 mmol/L. Liver function tests showed marked elevation with AST in the 1000s and ALT around 500, consistent with shock liver (Table 1). BUN and creatinine indicated acute renal failure, and she remained anuric in the emergency department despite two liters of crystalloid resuscitation.

Her thyroid function test revealed a TSH of 6.7 μIU/mL, consistent with mild hypothyroidism. She was treated for hyperkalemia with insulin, calcium, and albuterol. Blood pressure improved following intravenous fluids, with lactate trending downward. Due to persistent hypotension and bradycardia (Figure 1), she was initiated on a dobutamine infusion, which was successfully tapered after four hours. After discontinuation of dobutamine, telemetry demonstrated a normal sinus rhythm (Figure 2). She was placed on BiPAP for perceived pulmonary edema; however, she was weaned down to nasal cannula later (Figure 3). Given the initial presentation with hyperkalemia, hyperglycemia, and metabolic acidosis, DKA was appropriately considered. However, the normal β‐hydroxybutyrate level and only modestly elevated anion gap (18) argued against DKA as the primary etiology. Sepsis was considered due to elevated lactate levels, yet the absence of fever, leukocytosis, or an identifiable infectious source, combined with rapid hemodynamic improvement following fluid resuscitation, made this less likely. Similarly, cardiogenic shock was entertained as a cause of hypotension and bradycardia; however, echocardiographic findings were unremarkable, and the rapid resolution of bradycardia and hypotension after correction of hyperkalemia supported a functional, rather than structural, cardiac cause. Concomitant medication review revealed the use of β‐blockers and an ACE inhibitor, both of which are known precipitating factors for BRASH syndrome by promoting bradycardia, hyperkalemia, and renal hypoperfusion. The transient mild hypothyroidism (TSH 6.7 μIU/mL) was deemed subclinical and unlikely to account for the acute presentation. Collectively, these findings favored a diagnosis of BRASH syndrome over isolated hyperkalemia or other metabolic and hemodynamic derangements.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Overnight, the patient's bradycardia resolved, renal function improved, and she began producing urine. Her serum potassium normalized, and her vital signs stabilized. She was transferred to the medical ward for continued monitoring, and her verapamil was discontinued in view of suspected BRASH syndrome.

She was discharged on a revised medication regimen, excluding verapamil. On follow‐up, the patient remained clinically stable with no recurrence of bradycardia or hyperkalemia, and her renal function returned to baseline. She continues to be followed closely by her cardiologist and has remained symptom‐free since discharge.

Prevention of recurrence in patients with BRASH syndrome requires a multifaceted approach aimed at correcting underlying precipitating factors and optimizing long‐term management. Regular monitoring of renal function and serum electrolytes is essential, particularly in patients on combinations of AV nodal blocking agents and renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors. Dose adjustments or temporary discontinuation of these medications should be considered during periods of dehydration, acute illness, or worsening renal function to minimize the risk of recurrent hyperkalemia and bradycardia. Alternative rate‐control strategies, such as switching from β‐blockers to calcium channel blockers with shorter half‐lives (e.g., diltiazem), or vice versa, may be employed depending on comorbidities and tolerance. Clinicians should also educate patients to promptly report symptoms of dizziness, fatigue, or syncope, as early recognition of bradyarrhythmia can prevent progression to hemodynamic instability. Ultimately, individualized medication reconciliation and close outpatient follow‐up are critical to mitigating recurrence risk and ensuring safe cardiovascular management.

Discussion

BRASH syndrome can be a challenging diagnosis to establish. Our patient presented with concurrent use of an AV nodal–blocking agent and renal failure, leading to severe bradycardia and multiorgan dysfunction, as evidenced by pulmonary edema, hypotension, and shock liver (Figure 4). With an elevated TSH level, hypothyroidism was also considered as a potential contributing factor to her bradycardia. Isolated hyperkalemia may precipitate bradycardia, which in turn can worsen renal failure; however, bradycardia typically occurs with severe hyperkalemia (potassium levels ≥ 7 mmol/L) [1]. An electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrating bradycardia without the classic ECG features of hyperkalemia serves as an additional clue pointing toward BRASH syndrome [6].

The vicious cycle of BRASH syndrome.

Currently, there is limited evidence regarding the safety of restarting AV nodal–blocking agents after an episode of BRASH syndrome. A review of the available literature suggests that a shared decision‐making approach between healthcare providers and patients is warranted when considering the risks and benefits of resuming beta‐blocker or non‐dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker therapy [7]. A systematic review conducted in 2023 reported that almost none of the patients required permanent pacemaker implantation. Most patients with BRASH syndrome responded favorably to medical management, while 20% required renal replacement therapy (RRT) and 33% required temporary transvenous pacing (TVP). No patients underwent permanent pacemaker placement, and the overall in‐hospital mortality rate was 5.7% [2].

Given the potential severity of this condition, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion in any patient presenting with unexplained severe bradycardia accompanied by hyperkalemia [8]. Prompt recognition and management of BRASH syndrome are crucial, as it represents a serious yet reversible clinical entity.

Conclusion

BRASH syndrome represents a rare but important clinical entity characterized by the synergistic interaction of bradycardia, renal failure, AV nodal blockade, shock, and hyperkalemia. Early identification is critical, as this vicious cycle can rapidly progress to multiorgan failure if unrecognized. In our case, prompt withdrawal of the offending agent, correction of hyperkalemia, and supportive care led to full clinical recovery without the need for pacing or renal replacement therapy. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for BRASH syndrome in patients on AV nodal–blocking agents who present with unexplained bradycardia and hyperkalemia, even in the absence of classic ECG changes. Awareness of this reversible condition and timely intervention can prevent unnecessary invasive procedures, reduce morbidity, and improve patient outcomes. Future studies and case series are warranted to establish standardized diagnostic and management guidelines for BRASH syndrome.
