GraphDL: A visibility graph-based structural representation framework for flow-based cyber-attack detection
Authors: Ali Melih Kanca, İlker Türker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn modern network environments, the increasing diversity and volume of cyber-attacks have highlighted the need for advanced intrusion detection approaches capable of modeling complex traffic behaviors. In particular, capturing temporal and structural dependencies within flow-based network traffic remains a significant challenge for conventional feature-based methods. In this study, a graph-based structural representation framework named GraphDL is proposed for flow-based cyber-attack detection. In the proposed approach, network flow data are segmented into temporal frames using a sliding window mechanism, and Natural Visibility Graphs (NVGs) are constructed for each traffic feature. Various graph-theoretical metrics, including degree centrality, closeness centrality, betweenness centrality, and clustering coefficient, are extracted from the generated visibility graphs to construct structural feature representations. These graph-derived descriptors are subsequently processed using a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)-based classification architecture for intrusion detection. The proposed framework is evaluated on the CICIDS2018 dataset under different temporal window and feature configurations. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed approach achieves Accuracy values ranging from 75.81% to 96.73% and weighted F1-score values between 72.32% and 96.65%. In addition, an ablation study comparing the proposed GraphDL framework with a Raw-CNN baseline using the original flow features was conducted to analyze the contribution of visibility graph-based representations. The results indicate that although the Raw-CNN baseline provides strong predictive performance with substantially lower computational cost, the proposed GraphDL framework offers an alternative graph-oriented representation for modeling temporal and nonlinear traffic behaviors. Furthermore, the study analyzes the trade-off between structural representation capability and the computational overhead introduced by visibility graph construction and graph metric extraction. Introduction

The widespread adoption of digital infrastructure has brought significant convenience to users, ranging from individuals to global enterprises. However, the expansion of these technologies has also given rise to new types of threats, making cybersecurity a critical area of focus. Cyber threats such as data breaches, denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks, ransomware, phishing, and advanced persistent threats (APTs) are now encountered across organizations of all sizes, from large-scale enterprises to small businesses. These threats, which have the potential to cause significant harm at both individual and organizational levels, pose serious risks to computer systems, networks, and critical infrastructures.

As cyber threats continue to grow in complexity and sophistication, the importance of cyber-attack detection methods has become increasingly prominent. These methods play a key role in identifying existing threats and predicting potential ones. Thus, modern detection strategies adopt not only a reactive but also a proactive approach. Given the complex and dynamic nature of cyber threats, traditional methods are often insufficient, necessitating the development and adoption of more innovative analytical techniques. Indeed, conventional cybersecurity methods typically excel at identifying threats with known patterns but often fall short in addressing emerging and evolving cyber threats.

There are two primary approaches to cyber-attack detection: signature-based and anomaly-based methods. Signature-based techniques work by matching known patterns of malicious activity, while anomaly-based approaches aim to detect abnormal behaviors within network traffic. Signature-based detection systems can successfully identify known attacks using predefined signatures; however, they require frequent updates and are limited to identifying only previously encountered threats. In contrast, anomaly-based detection systems can identify unusual or suspicious traffic that may indicate previously unknown or evolving attacks1. In this regard, signature-based detection methods are often inadequate in identifying complex and dynamic cyber-attack types, underscoring the need for more advanced and adaptive detection approaches to ensure early threat identification and prevention. Network traffic data typically contains information about communication between network devices and users, offering critical insights that can assist in the early detection of cyber-attacks. However, the large volume and inherent complexity of this data significantly hinder accurate detection using traditional algorithms. Effective analysis of network data requires considering not only individual data points (such as packets or connections) in isolation but also their interrelationships. Therefore, it is essential to complement conventional methods with more advanced analytical techniques.

Graph-based approaches have emerged as powerful tools for understanding complex structures and relationships. They offer the ability to more effectively manage such complexity and enable more meaningful modeling of network traffic data. Graph structures allow the modeling of relationships between data points and support multidimensional analysis. Network traffic, broadly defined as all data flows occurring within a network, usually consists of communication between network devices, each flow corresponding to a specific connection, which can be represented as a node in a graph. Cyber-attacks often manifest as anomalies or distinct patterns within normal traffic behavior. Graph representations, comprising nodes and edges, help visualize these relationships and facilitate deeper analysis.

Accordingly, leveraging graph representations to model and interpret these complex interactions can enhance network traffic analysis. Given the limitations of traditional detection methods, a deeper understanding of network data and its interactions is increasingly critical. Thus, graph-based representations are practical tools for modeling network structures and detecting attacks. The integration of graph theory with deep learning techniques holds great potential for understanding the complex and dynamic nature of cybersecurity scenarios. Network traffic is largely shaped by the relationships among flows and packets, which often serve as key indicators of cyberattacks. Complex network topologies can be modeled using graph structures, where analysis of relationships enables anomaly detection. Graph-based cyber-attack detection techniques provide a significant advantage by enabling the analysis of such network structures to identify cyber threats2. Moreover, graph-based network analysis enables the examination of structural dependencies between data points, enabling the detection of complex attack types by analyzing the relationships between specific nodes3. Graph-based methods employed deep learning algorithms to model relationships within the data and to detect anomalies and cyber-attacks in network traffic. Cyber-attacks typically resulted in specific traffic patterns and irregularities, which graph structures could identify, distinguishing malicious traffic from expected flows more effectively. This approach leveraged topological traffic representations to support cyber threat analysis.

Furthermore, graph-based cyber-attack detection techniques revealed that attacks do not necessarily target a single system, but may affect multiple points within the network. Such attacks often disrupted data flow across the network, leading to abnormal connection patterns observable in the graph structure. For instance, a DDoS attack occurred when many devices simultaneously sent traffic to a single target, causing notable node density and traffic increases within the network structure. Graph representations allowed these anomalies to be tracked over time, enabling earlier detection of potential threats through learned patterns. In this context, a graph-oriented deep learning framework is proposed for cyber-attack detection in flow-based network traffic, and its performance is evaluated using the CICIDS2018 dataset generated with the CICFlowMeter tool4. The CICFlowMeter tool monitored bidirectional (forward and backward) traffic flows and extracted various statistical features related to these flows. The data generated by CICFlowMeter offered rich interactions across different protocols and flow types. These bidirectional flow records included detailed network traffic information such as source and destination IP addresses, source and destination ports (e.g., TCP, UDP), flow duration, packet counts, byte counts, packet sizes, and time intervals. In this context, it was considered that modeling such flow data using one of the graph representation techniques would enable a more interpretable analysis of the relationships between devices and their interactions. Accordingly, in this study, each selected flow feature within a temporal window was interpreted as a time series, where individual temporal observations were represented as graph nodes and edges were established according to the Natural Visibility Graph (NVG) visibility criterion. Network graph structures were utilized to visualize these connections, and the NetworkX library in the Python programming language was employed for implementation5. While conventional deep learning-based intrusion detection approaches primarily emphasize classification performance, this study focuses on investigating the potential of visibility graph-based structural representations to provide an alternative and topology-aware modeling perspective for capturing temporal and nonlinear characteristics of flow-based network traffic. In this context, the proposed framework is designed not only to consider predictive performance but also to account for representational capacity and structural interpretability.

Theoretical background

Network traffic analysis is a method used to examine communication networks and ensure their security. It involves monitoring, recording, and analysis of data packets transmitted over a computer network. In network analysis, the primary objective is to identify abnormal activities and cyber threats by examining packet flows and network behavior. Network traffic analysis enables the monitoring of data flows within a network to optimize performance, detect anomalies, and take preventive measures against cyber-attacks. To examine network traffic, two main techniques are commonly employed: Deep Packet Inspection (DPI), which involves thoroughly analyzing the contents of data packets to detect malicious elements, and Flow-Based Analysis, which utilizes packet metadata to summarize traffic and identify anomalies. These methods facilitate the early detection of potential attacks or security breaches within the network. Additionally, traffic analysis is commonly conducted using essential tools such as Wireshark, Tcpdump, and NetFlow.

Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) are technologies used to identify unusual activity in a network and maintain its security. IDSs typically monitor network traffic to detect potential threats based on known attack signatures or deviations from normal behavior. While signature-based IDSs effectively detect previously known attacks, anomaly-based IDSs analyze irregular activity to uncover previously unknown or emerging threats7. In this context, it is evident that Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) play a critical role in cyber-attack detection. In recent years, IDS systems integrated with explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) models have enhanced threat detection capabilities and provided transparent insights into the rationale behind detections, offering security teams greater control and interpretability. This advancement has further emphasized the importance of IDS systems in cybersecurity efforts8. IDS systems are categorized based on their detection methods, data sources, and the type of traffic they analyze. According to the detection method, they are classified as Signature-Based IDS and Anomaly-Based IDS. Based on the data source, they are divided into Network-Based IDS (NIDS) and Host-Based IDS (HIDS). Finally, based on the type of traffic analyzed, they are classified as Packet-Based IDS and Flow-Based IDS. Signature-Based Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) detect threats based on predefined attack signatures. These systems analyze network traffic to identify known attacks by applying specific detection rules (rulesets) tailored to particular threat patterns. While this method effectively identifies previously encountered attacks, it often falls short in detecting new or unknown threats9. Anomaly-Based Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) are designed to detect abnormal network activities and are capable of identifying previously unknown threats10. Signature-based methods are characterized by their ease of implementation, reliability, and high processing speed. However, they have several limitations, including lower robustness, limited scalability, and a relatively low detection (alert) rate. In contrast, anomaly-based methods, although more complex to implement and generally weaker in terms of reliability and speed, offer significant advantages in robustness, higher alert rates, and better scalability11.

Network-Based Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) monitor all network traffic to detect suspicious activities and can identify attacks occurring within the network. In contrast, Host-Based Intrusion Detection Systems (HIDS) operate on individual machines by monitoring system logs, file integrity, and user activity, and are primarily used to detect attacks targeting a specific device12. Packet-Based Intrusion Detection Systems (PIDS) analyze network traffic at the level of individual packets, whereas Flow-Based Intrusion Detection Systems (FIDS) examine aggregated traffic flows—such as source and destination IP addresses, port numbers, total packet counts, and byte volumes—typically using tools like Snort and Zeek. In packet-based systems, information such as source and destination IPs and port numbers is used to identify communication patterns within the network. These systems can also inspect packet content, making them particularly effective in detecting signature-based attacks. While packet-based systems provide deep inspection and enable detection of content-level attacks, they often face scalability limitations in large-scale networks due to their high computational overhead. On the other hand, flow-based IDS approaches are more suitable for analyzing high-volume traffic, as they focus on traffic flows rather than individual packet contents. These systems offer more efficient and faster detection in networks with heavy traffic loads13. In such datasets, the packet payload is not stored directly. Instead, only the statistical properties of the traffic are analyzed, making this approach more suitable for graph-based analysis (e.g., CICFlowMeter, NetFlow). In flow-based IDS methods, traffic patterns between connections are examined, enabling more accurate and scalable cyber-attack detection. Indeed, these systems effectively perform key security processes such as anomaly detection, attack classification, and real-time monitoring14.

For this reason, a flow-based IDS approach was adopted in this study, as it provides more effective results in anomaly detection. Since the focus is on detecting anomalies, this method was preferred for its suitability. Table 1 presents a summary comparison of the key characteristics of packet-based and flow-based IDS systems. In this context, the present study aims to perform cyber-attack detection using a graph-based deep learning model, which requires the analysis of connections and interactions within the network. Furthermore, since the Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) model intended for use focuses on analyzing traffic flows rather than individual packets, the flow-based IDS approach is considered to be more effective for this study. The dataset used, CICIDS2018, is constructed based on flow-based traffic and does not contain individual packet data; instead, it consists of network flows. This study intends to transform these network flows into graph structures and compute relevant network metrics. By integrating deep learning into a flow-based IDS framework, the ultimate goal is to develop a more robust and accurate intrusion detection model.

Graph representation techniques

The graph-based representation approach is a powerful method that models complex relationships by structuring data through network nodes and edges. This approach facilitates the understanding of intricate interactions in networks and offers significant advantages in the visualization and analysis of data. It has been widely adopted in various domains, including social network analysis, bioinformatics, computer networks, and recommendation systems18. Graph representations, which are also used in analyzing network-based and time series data, serve as a critical tool for understanding the structural characteristics of complex systems. In graph theory, nodes and the relationships between these nodes are analyzed to provide a more interpretable and visual representation of the data. Owing to their ability to easily model numerous node and edge relationships, network-based graph representations allow for examining each data point’s role, interactions within the network, and evolution over time. Modeling network flows enables a deeper understanding of the relationships between nodes. For instance, attributes such as IP addresses and protocol numbers from network traffic flow data can be represented as nodes, while their connections can be expressed as edges. Although the graph-based approach can be employed to model various types of data, specialized graph structures are developed for more domain-specific applications. One such structure is the Visibility Graph, which is used to analyze time series data. Time series data consist of sequential observations over a specific period, where each observation corresponds to a value measured at a particular time point. Various dynamic and static analyses can be performed on such data. Time series analysis typically aims to uncover patterns, trends, or anomalies within the data points. The visibility graph is a powerful graph-based method developed to better analyze time series data by representing them within a network structure. It is widely used to understand and analyze the dynamics of complex systems and has been applied in a broad range of domains, including financial markets and biomedical signal analysis19. In a visibility graph, each data point in a time series is treated as a node, and the connections between these nodes are established according to a specific geometric criterion. These connections are based on visibility rules that aim to reveal the relationships within the time series visually. Visibility graphs represent the positions of a sequence of points in the Euclidean plane, where each point tests whether it has a direct linear connection with other points to determine whether it can “see” them20. The visibility graph proposed by Lacasa provides a simple and efficient computational framework that bridges the gap between time series and complex networks21. Moreover, graph-based representation techniques offer a general approach that allows modeling various types of relationships between nodes. These relationships can range from structural analysis of networks to interactions in social networks. However, the visibility graph is a specialized technique developed to examine the unique relationships in time series data, based on specific geometric and linear rules. While general graph representations are typically used to analyze overall relationships between nodes, the visibility graph focuses on uncovering the dynamic properties and patterns of time series by examining their internal structure in greater detail. For this reason, the visibility graph differs from other graph representations by visually revealing the relationships between each data point in the time series. Visibility graph approaches used in time series analysis consist of two main variants: the Natural Visibility Graph (NVG) and the Horizontal Visibility Graph (HVG). Both methods are designed to model the relationships in time series data graphically and are based on the concept of visibility. However, there are notable differences in the visibility criteria between these two methods. The Natural Visibility Graph (NVG) is the basic form of a visibility graph, which models time series data through nodes and edges. Each data point is represented as a node in this method, and the connections between nodes are established according to geometric visibility rules. The Horizontal Visibility Graph (HVG), on the other hand, is considered a subset of NVG. In HVG, connections between nodes are restricted to horizontal visibility along the time axis. This method relies on a simpler visibility criterion than NVG. In HVG, a connection between two points exists only if all intermediate data points (xk, i < k < j) are lower than both xi and xj:

To explain the construction of NVG more concretely, each data point in a given time series is represented as a node. The edges between these nodes are then determined based on whether the intermediate values act as obstacles. If two points can “see” each other, if none of the intermediate points obstruct their linear visibility, an edge is established between them. In this way, the internal structure of the time series is transformed into a network form. These processes are illustrated in Figs. 1 and 2, where examples of NVG and HVG constructions from sample time series are presented22,23.

When constructing a visibility graph, each value in the time series is considered as a node, and the existence of edges between these nodes is determined based on visibility criteria. Visibility refers to the geometric relationships between points, and these rules are employed to analyze the nonlinear dependencies within time series data. In a visibility graph, each column (i.e., data point) can be visualized as a node, and each interaction as an edge. The intensity or continuity of traffic flows can be represented through edge weights, thereby supporting intrusion detection. In this context, the visibility graph is considered a powerful tool for analyzing network traffic data that has been transformed into a time series format within the scope of cybersecurity. It serves as a visualization method used to represent the flow and interactions of data in a network more meaningfully, helping to analyze connections occurring in the network, relationships between devices, and data transmission based on flow information. Deep neural networks and convolutional neural network

Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) are powerful learning models characterized by multilayer architectures that process data in stages to transform it into meaningful representations. These models consist of multiple hidden layers and can learn complex structures within data. Deep neural networks are widely used across various disciplines, including healthcare, finance, natural language processing, image analysis, autonomous systems, and cybersecurity. Their success is largely attributed to their ability to learn abstract step-by-step through hierarchical learning. They can effectively identify complex patterns and make predictions when trained on large datasets. While traditional artificial neural networks typically consist of only one or two hidden layers, deep neural networks include numerous hidden layers that process data at different hierarchical levels. These layers transform raw input data into increasingly abstract representations, enabling the extraction of high-level features. The learning process in these networks is performed through feedforward neural networks (FNNs), the backpropagation algorithm, and various optimization techniques. The success of deep neural networks has been greatly facilitated by advancements in computational hardware (e.g., GPUs) and the availability of large-scale datasets (Big Data)24.

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are a subclass of artificial neural networks designed to work with image and spatial data. The key characteristic of CNNs their ability to capture low-level features (such as edges, corners, and textures) by applying convolution operations with local filters over the input data. Due to their capability to abstract information across deep layers and perform multi-dimensional learning, CNNs have achieved remarkable success in image classification, object detection, segmentation, facial recognition, and medical image analysis tasks. A visual representation of the CNN architecture is presented in Fig. 3, adapted from Beri et al.25. A typical CNN structure consists of convolutional, pooling, and fully connected layers. Commonly used CNN architectures today include models such as LeNet, AlexNet, VGG, ResNet, and EfficientNet. These architectures are trained on large-scale datasets such as ImageNet to learn universal visual features, which can then be utilized for various tasks through transfer learning.

Typical architecture of the CNN model.

CNN models have also been employed in cybersecurity research. For instance, in a study conducted by Beri et al., a CNN architecture was utilized to detect anomalies in network traffic. The model was trained using the CICIDS2017 dataset and included a Convolutional Layer (Activation: ReLU), Max Pooling Layer, Flatten Layer, Fully Connected (Dense) Layer, and an Output Layer with Softmax activation for classification. The study reported an accuracy rate of 93.65%25. In the study conducted by Kirubai et al., a CNN-based algorithmic framework was proposed for the detection of malware, phishing, and DDoS attacks. The framework employed Convolution Layer, Max Pooling, Dropout, Dense Layer (ReLU), and Output Layer (Softmax), and it was emphasized that an accuracy rate of 95% was achieved26.

Considering the aforementioned studies, it is evident that models trained with CNNs have achieved high accuracy rates. Therefore, this study adopted a CNN architecture to classify fixed-length feature vectors constructed from metrics derived from visibility graphs. The strength of CNNs in capturing local patterns facilitates learning meaningful relationships among the metrics extracted from time series data. By applying separate Conv1D layers for each column, the structural information carried by each variable was processed independently by the model. Considering parameter sharing, low computational cost, and resistance to overfitting, CNN was evaluated as the most suitable model for this study from both theoretical and practical perspectives.

Related works

In recent years, graph-based approaches have gained prominence in the analysis of network traffic data. These approaches leverage graph structures to more effectively model the complex relationships inherent in network data. This section focuses on recent studies published between 2020 and 2024 that specifically address graph-based network traffic analysis and intrusion detection. The reviewed publications are indexed in major academic databases such as Web of Science, Scopus, IEEE Xplore, SpringerLink, and the ACM Digital Library. Graph-based methods offer significant advantages in intrusion detection by modeling the temporal relationships in network traffic. Studies employing this approach utilize graph structures and network metrics to more accurately analyze the dynamic characteristics of cyber-attacks. Various studies in the literature have adopted graph-based techniques for network traffic analysis. In a study conducted by Zola et al. a temporal graph-based approach was proposed for detecting cyber-attacks within network traffic data. The proposed method consists of three main stages: (1) graph construction from the data, (2) application of data preprocessing techniques on the constructed graph-based data (R-hybrid and SM-hybrid), and (3) classification using a Neural Network (NN) and a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN). During the feature extraction phase, several network metrics were calculated to represent the structural properties of the graphs. Specifically, degree centrality was used to measure the connectivity of nodes, betweenness centrality to capture the extent to which nodes act as intermediaries in information flow, and closeness centrality to quantify how efficiently a node can reach other nodes in the graph. These metrics provide detailed insights into graph topology and contribute significantly to the classification performance. The type of graph employed in this study is referred to as a Traffic Dispersion Graph (TDG), where nodes represent IP addresses and edges denote communication flow observed within fixed temporal intervals. TDGs highlight “who talks to whom” and illustrate how communication patterns evolve over time. In contrast, the visibility graphs adopted in our study are constructed directly from time series data, where each data point is treated as a node and edges are established according to geometric visibility rules. Thus, while TDGs capture structural host interactions, visibility graphs emphasize temporal and nonlinear dependencies within traffic features27.

In the study conducted by Zhu et al., a Graph Neural Network (GNN)-based Intrusion Detection System (IDS) was proposed to investigate the structural characteristics of the network and the relationships among network traffic flows. The study introduced a two-part Graph-Based Behavior Analysis model (GBA-IDS), and it was emphasized that the proposed model demonstrated improvements in both binary and multi-class classification tasks28. In the study conducted by Su et al., a graph kernel-based deep learning architecture was adopted to exploit the relationships among network flows. A graph kernel matrix was constructed from the network flow metadata and subsequently used as input to a CNN algorithm. In addition, the same data were directly fed into an LSTM algorithm. The results obtained from both methods were then combined and classified in the final step using a softmax function29.

In the study conducted by Yao et al.30, a hybrid deep neural network architecture called STDeepGraph was proposed, which combines Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks to learn high-dimensional representations from network traffic. The methodology involves constructing temporal communication graphs from network flow data, in which nodes represent IP addresses and edges denote communication flows observed during specific time intervals. From these graphs, shortest-path graph kernel matrices are computed to capture structural similarities between graphs. These kernel matrices are then further processed using graph signal decomposition to derive compact vector representations, which are fed into CNN and LSTM models for classification. Compared to our approach, which directly extracts classical network metrics (e.g., degree, betweenness, closeness) from visibility graphs constructed over time series data, STDeepGraph relies on kernel-based similarity embeddings to model structural and temporal dependencies. This distinction highlights the different perspectives of graph representation: while STDeepGraph emphasizes structural similarity through kernel functions, our method focuses on visibility-based graph metrics that capture temporal and nonlinear dependencies in traffic features.

In another study, Yao et al. proposed Deep Graph Feature Learning (DeepGFL) for network traffic modeling. In their work, the CIC-IDS-2017 dataset containing over 2.8 million flows was used. Flow graphs were constructed by grouping every 1000 flows, and high-level features were learned from these graph representations. From the raw flow features generated using CICFlowMeter, eight discriminative attributes were identified via Random Forest Regressor. Their experimental results demonstrated that DeepGFL outperformed traditional feature-based and machine learning approaches, particularly by achieving higher Recall and F1-Score values. Unlike our approach, which systematically varies the window length to assess its impact on detection accuracy and runtime, Yao et al. relied on a fixed block size of 1000 flows31.

Botnet detection and network anomaly analysis have become increasingly effective through the adoption of graph-based approaches. These methods enable the identification of central nodes and dense neighborhood structures within botnet architectures by modeling network entities and their interactions. Moreover, advanced techniques such as Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) facilitate the analysis of temporal variations, thereby allowing for more accurate detection of botnet activities. Several notable studies have been conducted in this domain. For instance, in the study by Alharbi et al., a graph-based machine learning model was proposed for botnet detection. In this approach, a graph was constructed from network traffic data, followed by feature extraction and normalization steps. The resulting features were then provided as input to a machine learning algorithm. The study utilized various graph metrics for feature extraction, including in-degree, out-degree, in-degree weight, out-degree weight, clustering coefficient, in-degree centrality, out-degree centrality, node count, betweenness, authority, hub, Katz, PageRank, closeness, and eigenvector centrality32.

Similarly, a study conducted by Wang et al. proposed a model called BotMark, which detects botnets through a hybrid analysis of flow-based and graph-based network traffic behaviors. In this study, a wide range of flow-based features were considered, such as flow duration, the number of transmitted packets, and payload length distributions, while graph-based features included the number of neighbors, the number of edges, and the total edge weights. In addition, anomaly detection techniques such as the Local Outlier Factor (LOF) were employed to capture deviations in node neighborhoods. By integrating these heterogeneous feature spaces, BotMark was able to achieve very high accuracy rates (up to 99.9%), outperforming methods that rely solely on either flow or graph features. Compared to our approach, which focuses exclusively on graph-theoretic metrics extracted from visibility graphs of traffic features, BotMark represents a hybrid strategy that leverages both statistical flow characteristics and structural graph information. This difference emphasizes two complementary perspectives: while BotMark aims to combine heterogeneous features for broad coverage, our method provides a deeper analysis of temporal and nonlinear dependencies through visibility graph representations33.

In a study conducted by Jing et al., the characteristics of DDoS attacks were analyzed in detail, and a graph-based model was employed to represent the structural properties of traffic data between victims and bots. For feature extraction, graph-based metrics such as in-degree, out-degree, in-degree weight, out-degree weight, eigenvector centrality, and local clustering coefficient were utilized. In addition, flow-based metrics, including flow duration, standard deviation of backward packet length, average packet size, standard deviation of flow interval time, and mean active time, were also used34. Similarly, Shinan et al. proposed a graph-based bot detection system named BotSward, where network traffic was converted into graph form and features such as closeness centrality, degree centrality, and PageRank were computed for use in a machine learning model35. Furthermore, Millar et al. introduced a method called BiSAGE, a graph-based machine learning approach aimed at identifying devices involved in botnet activities. This method leveraged the neighborhood information of nodes for feature extraction36.

In the study conducted by Daya et al., a two-stage graph-based bot detection system was proposed by employing both supervised and unsupervised machine learning algorithms. In this approach, network traffic was first converted into a graph, followed by feature extraction and normalization. The extracted features were then fed into both unsupervised and supervised learning models. The study employed several graph-based metrics for feature extraction, including In-Degree (ID), Out-Degree (OD), In-Degree Weight (IDW), Out-Degree Weight (ODW), Betweenness Centrality (BC), Local Clustering Coefficient (LCC), and Alpha Centrality (AC)37.

Similarly, Chowdhury et al. presented a novel graph-based method for botnet detection. In their study, a range of graph metrics was utilized for feature extraction, such as in-degree, out-degree, the sum of incoming edge weights, the sum of outgoing edge weights, clustering coefficient, node betweenness, and eigenvector centrality38. Sinha et al. proposed a graph-based approach for botnet detection in 2019. In this study, network traffic was transformed into a graph, and features were extracted using fundamental graph metrics, including out-degree, in-degree, out-neighbors, in-neighbors, PageRank centrality, betweenness centrality, eigenvector centrality, and local clustering coefficient. The extracted features were then used as input to a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network for classification39.

Graph-based approaches have been extensively adopted not only in botnet detection and network anomaly analysis but also in modeling spatial and temporal patterns, anomaly detection, and malware analysis. These approaches leverage the structural properties of networks and their dynamic behaviors to support traffic analysis and interpretability. Below is a summary of prominent hybrid and graph-based studies in these domains.

In the study by Fu et al., a method named ST-Graph was proposed to investigate spatial and temporal features of network behavior using a graph representation learning algorithm. The study employed features such as character count, character ratio, and vowel/consonant ratio, which were extracted using Domain Generation Algorithms (DGA)40. Liu et al. combined graph structures with deep learning techniques to conduct predictive analysis of Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs), highlighting the potential of hybrid models in capturing complex attack sequences41. Karrar et al. proposed a graph-based machine learning algorithm to identify critical features for malware detection. The extracted features included command signatures, API calls, notification permissions, and other system-level behavioral indicators42. Baru et al. introduced a graph-based outlier detection framework for the Internet of Things (GODIT), which modeled IoT traffic as real-time graph flows. The system utilized the Random Walk Path algorithm for feature extraction and demonstrated the ability to detect real-time DDoS attacks43.

Khalaef et al. proposed a multi-agent system using Graph-Based Layered Learning Focused (GLLD) for anomaly detection. The method captured the complexity of node relationships and dependencies within IoT networks. The system was designed in four stages: feature extraction, graph construction, learning, and evaluation. Features such as start time, duration, protocol, source/target device, IP address, connection direction, packet count, and transmitted bytes were used. The model was evaluated using the BoT-IoT and Code Red datasets44.

Cuzzocrea et al. developed a graph-based technique for extracting control flow graphs from Android APK binary files. The approach introduced a new graph representation model, inferential SIR-GN, preserving structural similarities. A structural vector was generated for each node, followed by the construction of an adjacency matrix using XGBoost for classification45. Yu et al. proposed the Adaptive Feature Graph Update (AFGU) model, which focuses on data leakage prevention through graph merging, reduction, and matching strategies. In this model, all data segments were represented as graphs, with sensitive data modeled as graph nodes46.

Leichtnam et al. presented Sec2graph, a graph-based anomaly detection method that leverages Security Objects’ Graphs (SOGs) combined with an autoencoder. In this approach, heterogeneous log data such as TCP, DNS, HTTP, and certificate-related events are translated into Security Objects, which serve as graph nodes, while semantic relations among them form the edges. This unified representation enables the correlation of diverse log events into a single comprehensive graph structure. To process these graphs, Sec2graph encodes each edge as a feature vector that integrates the edge type, attributes of the source and destination nodes, and statistical summaries of their neighborhoods. Continuous attributes are categorized using Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM), and categorical attributes are encoded through one-hot encoding, resulting in compact binary vectors. These vectors are then fed into an autoencoder that learns to reconstruct normal patterns; anomalies are flagged when the reconstruction error exceeds a predefined threshold. Experimental evaluation on the CICIDS2017 dataset demonstrated that Sec2graph, despite being unsupervised, achieved a recall of 99% with competitive.

precision, outperforming many supervised baselines. Compared to our approach, which focuses on graph-theoretical descriptors (e.g., degree, betweenness, closeness) from visibility graphs of time series data and applying supervised classification, Sec2graph emphasizes novelty detection on unified log-derived graphs using unsupervised autoencoder models47.

Jing et al. developed a machine learning method to predict DDoS attacks, utilizing Principal Component Analysis (PCA) for feature extraction after graph construction, by applying a covariance matrix approach48. Finally, in the study by Alwasel et al., a graph-based experimental framework was proposed. Network traffic data were transformed into graph representations using adjacency matrices, followed by feature extraction, dimensionality reduction using PCA, and classification with machine learning models. While the model achieved 90%–96% accuracy without graph representation, performance improved to 97%–99% when graph-based features were incorporated49.

In another recent study, graph-oriented and deep learning-based methodologies were investigated for intrusion detection and anomaly analysis in network environments52. Proposed method

This section presents the proposed GraphDL framework, which combines visibility graph-based structural representations with deep learning for flow-based cyber-attack detection. The proposed methodology aims to model temporal and nonlinear traffic behaviors by transforming network flow features into graph-based structural representations and analyzing the resulting graph descriptors using a CNN architecture. The detailed workflow of the proposed methodology is illustrated in Fig. 4, including sliding window segmentation, feature visibility graph construction from temporal flow windows, graph-based descriptor extraction, and CNN-based classification. As part of the preprocessing stage, raw network traffic data are transformed into a format suitable for graph construction and deep learning analysis. During this process, missing and infinite values are removed, and feature scaling is applied to normalize traffic attributes and improve model learning stability. After preprocessing, network traffic records are segmented into overlapping temporal windows using a sliding window mechanism. Each temporal window contains consecutive flow records that preserve the temporal ordering of traffic behavior. This windowing strategy enables the proposed framework to capture short-term temporal dynamics and evolving traffic patterns within flow-based network data.

Overview of the proposed GraphDL framework, including preprocessing, sliding window-based temporal segmentation, feature-wise visibility graph construction, graph-theoretical descriptor extraction, and CNN-based cyber-attack classification.

Within each temporal window, each selected flow feature is interpreted as an independent time series. The Natural Visibility Graph (NVG) algorithm is then applied separately to each temporal feature sequence in order to construct visibility graph-based structural representations. In the constructed visibility graphs, each temporal observation, namely each feature value within the corresponding temporal window, is represented as a graph node. Edges between nodes are established according to the NVG visibility criterion described in the theoretical background section. Therefore, unlike communication-centric graph approaches, graph nodes in the proposed framework do not represent hosts, IP addresses, or communication entities. Instead, each visibility graph models the structural behavior of a single flow feature within a temporal window.

After visibility graph construction, graph-theoretical descriptors are extracted from each graph in order to characterize the structural properties of temporal traffic behavior. In this study, several classical graph metrics are utilized, including degree centrality, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, clustering coefficient, modularity, diameter, average path length, and average degree. These graph-derived descriptors provide structural information regarding node connectivity, local neighborhood density, and graph topology. By transforming temporal flow-feature behaviors into graph-theoretical representations, the proposed framework aims to model complex and nonlinear traffic dynamics that may not be directly observable from raw flow features alone.

Subsequently, feature selection and dimensionality reduction techniques are applied to identify the most informative graph-derived descriptors and reduce computational complexity prior to classification. The most effective features are selected using the MLP-SimpleClassifier feature selection strategy. The selected graph-derived descriptors are then standardized and prepared as input for the CNN classifier.

Finally, the extracted graph-derived descriptors are processed using a CNN classifier for cyber-attack detection. The CNN architecture is designed to learn discriminative structural patterns from graph-based representations extracted from temporal flow windows. The architecture consists of Conv1D layers followed by Batch Normalization and Dropout operations to improve generalization performance and reduce overfitting. Outputs obtained from multiple feature branches are concatenated and processed through fully connected dense layers, and a softmax activation function is employed to perform multi-class cyber-attack classification.

The performance of the proposed GraphDL framework is evaluated using Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and Confusion Matrix metrics. Furthermore, the proposed framework is comparatively evaluated against a Raw-CNN baseline using the original flow features in order to analyze the contribution of visibility graph-based structural representations and the trade-off between representation capability and computational overhead. Data and methods

This section outlines the experimental procedures carried out to evaluate the proposed GraphDL framework for flow-based cyber-attack detection. It includes detailed descriptions of the dataset, preprocessing steps, experimental setup, visibility graph construction, graph-derived descriptor extraction, CNN-based classification, and evaluation metrics. In addition, a Raw Flow Feature-Based CNN baseline is introduced to evaluate whether the visibility graph transformation and graph metric extraction provide meaningful representational advantages over directly using the original flow features as CNN input.

Data

In this study, the CICIDS2018 dataset was used, which was developed by the Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity to support research in cyber-attack detection, the development of Intrusion Detection and Prevention Systems (IDS/IPS), the training of machine learning and deep learning models for network traffic analysis, and the examination of both attacker and benign user behaviors. The dataset was collected over five days during which network traffic was simulated to reflect a realistic corporate environment. Over these five days, approximately 16 million flow records were generated, averaging around 3.2 million flows per day and 35 to 40 flows per second. In enterprise environments, it is common for thousands of packets to be transmitted per second, resulting in millions of flow records daily. The number of flows typically depends on network size, user count, the type of applications in use, and overall traffic intensity. For instance, in a large-scale corporate network, a single user can generate between 20 and 50 flows per second, leading to approximately 1 to 4 million flows per hour. In this context, CICIDS2018 reflects the characteristics of a medium to large-scale network, particularly under conditions of heavy or attack-induced traffic. A high-traffic corporate environment is expected to produce between 100,000 and 1 million flows per hour. The dataset comprises around 16 million rows and 80 columns, with approximately 12 million instances labeled “Benign.” The distribution of classes in the dataset is presented using logarithmic scaling in Fig. 5. The dataset distribution consists of 83% benign traffic and 17% other attack types.

Logarithmic distribution of the number of attack types in the dataset.

The dataset was created using CICFlowMeter-V3, a Java-based flow generator that extracts features from network traffic at the flow level. Unlike packet-level data that captures detailed, per-packet interactions, flow-level data provides a more aggregated view, summarizing a series of packets into a single communication session. This enables more scalable and generalizable analysis. The CICIDS2018 dataset provides a comprehensive set of flow-level features that capture different aspects of network traffic behavior. Among these are the fundamental flow properties, such as flow duration, total number of packets, and total number of bytes in both forward and backward directions. In addition, packet length statistics are provided, including minimum, maximum, mean, and standard deviation values for each direction. The dataset also includes inter-arrival time (IAT) features, which measure the time between packets or flows and provide important insights into temporal traffic patterns. Various TCP flag-based features (e.g., FIN, SYN, RST, PSH, ACK, URG, CWE, ECE) are also included, often indicating malicious or anomalous behaviors. Content- and segment-level features cover header lengths, average segment sizes, and bulk transmission rates for both forward and backward directions. Furthermore, the dataset provides subflow-based attributes, such as the number of packets and bytes in forward and backward subflows, and window-based features like the number of bytes sent in the initial TCP window. Finally, active/idle time features describe how long a flow remains active or idle before changing state, which is important in detecting abnormal behaviors or attack traffic. These features allow the dataset to comprehensively represent both normal and attack traffic, thereby enabling the development of effective machine learning- and deep learning-based intrusion detection models. In the data structure used in this study, each row of the CICIDS2018 dataset represents a single network flow, while each column corresponds to a specific feature of that flow (e.g., Source IP, Destination Port, Flow Duration, Total Fwd Packets). To enable the proposed model to learn temporal dependencies, the data were not fed directly on a row-by-row basis; instead, consecutive rows were grouped into frames, which were then used as model inputs. For this purpose, a sliding window mechanism was applied, segmenting the data stream into portions of data predefined window size. For example, when WL = 50 is selected, each frame consists of 50 consecutive flow records, and these frames are shifted across the dataset with partial overlaps. This approach allows the model to capture not only individual flow characteristics but also the temporal dependencies and traffic patterns embedded in the sequence of flows. In this context, window length denotes the number of rows (flows) contained in each frame, serving as a key parameter that determines both the input size of the model and the contextual range it can learn from.

Testbed

The graph-based deep learning model for cyber-attack detection was implemented and tested on a Lenovo E14 system equipped with 2 CPU cores and 16 GB of RAM. The experiments were conducted using Python, with NetworkX (v3.1), Scikit-learn (v1.3.2), and TensorFlow (v2.13.0). All experiments were conducted on a 64-bit Windows 10 operating system.

Measurements

Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and the Confusion Matrix were employed as the primary performance evaluation metrics in this study. In addition, both macro-averaged and weighted evaluation metrics were analyzed in order to provide a more comprehensive assessment of model performance under class-imbalanced conditions. A series of experiments were conducted by varying the window lengths from 10 to 80 and the number of columns from 10 to 80 in order to determine the most optimal parameter configurations for the proposed model.

Data preprocessing

Data preprocessing plays a critical role in model design, as it helps normalize input ranges and enhances the performance of deep learning models. Therefore, before applying the proposed models to the dataset, it was essential to convert the raw data into a suitable format for analysis. In the preprocessing phase of this study, missing and infinite values were removed from the dataset. Extremely large or small outliers were scaled, non-numeric columns were excluded, and categorical variables were converted into numerical representations. These steps ensured that the data were clean, consistent, and compatible with machine learning frameworks.

Due to the large scale of the CICIDS2018 dataset, a representative subset consisting of 100,000 rows and 80 columns was selected for the experiments in order to ensure computational feasibility. No oversampling technique was applied in the preprocessing stage. In particular, SMOTE was not used in order to avoid any potential risk of data leakage between training and testing subsets. As a result, the original class distribution of the dataset was preserved, providing a more realistic evaluation scenario for intrusion detection.

Experimental design

In the experimental phase of the study, graph-based features were calculated by applying visibility graph techniques within defined time windows. Specifically, the dataset was segmented into overlapping temporal windows with window lengths ranging from 10 to 80 flow records. For each temporal window, each selected flow feature was interpreted as an independent time series, and a visibility graph was constructed separately for each feature sequence.

ii.Since the proposed framework operates on sliding temporal windows, a window-labeling strategy is required to assign a single class label to each window. Initially, a standard majority-vote labeling approach was considered, in which each temporal window is assigned the label of the most frequently occurring class within that window. These findings further support the use of the proposed relabeling-based strategy for preserving minority attack behaviors within temporal windows.

The Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) is one of the most widely used types of artificial neural networks, characterized by a feed-forward architecture composed of an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. Information flows in a single direction, from the input layer through the hidden layers to the output layer, without feedback connections. Nonlinear activation functions, such as sigmoid or tanh, enable MLPs to learn complex and nonlinear mappings, making them powerful universal approximators. Training is typically performed using the backpropagation algorithm, which iteratively adjusts the weights to minimize the error between predicted and actual outputs. In this context, it was decided to use the MLP model to determine the most effective columns due to the higher success rate it achieved. Accordingly, in order to perform feature selection with the MLP model, the data was divided into frames so that the model could analyze and classify the data over a time window after the visibility graph was created. For each data group divided into frames and for each column, two-dimensional (node number * node number) neighborhood matrices were constructed.

Subsequently, this process was applied to all frames in the dataset. The resulting data were stored in a multidimensional array. If the number of frames is denoted as f and the number of columns as c, a four-dimensional array with the shape (n × n × f × c) was obtained. In parallel, a label was assigned to each frame by analyzing the distribution of labels within it. Specifically, the label that appeared most frequently in a given frame was selected as the representative label. In cases where two or more labels appeared with equal frequency, the first occurring label among them was assigned. After this operation was completed for all frames, a corresponding list of frame labels was generated. In the final step, the constructed array and the list of labels were combined and used as input to a simple artificial neural network model based on Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP - SimpleClassifier) for the purpose of feature extraction and identifying the most informative columns.

During the analysis, the MLP - SimpleClassifier model was trained and evaluated by systematically removing each column from the dataset in separate iterations. Initially, all columns were used as input. One column was excluded in each iteration, and the model’s accuracy was measured. If removing a specific column resulted in a significant drop in accuracy, that column was considered to have high importance. Model selection

In this study, visibility graphs were constructed from network traffic data, and the network metrics derived from these graphs were analyzed in conjunction with time series-based data. Given the temporal nature of the data and the interdependencies between extracted features, the Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) model was selected due to its demonstrated effectiveness in learning temporal patterns and feature relationships. Specifically, the use of Conv1D layers enables the model to detect both local and global dependencies among graph-derived descriptors, capturing structural patterns over a specified window length. Conv1D can effectively model the internal structure of each feature by applying filters over time, making it particularly suitable for processing sequences of graph-based metrics. Although alternative approaches were considered, CNN was ultimately chosen as the most appropriate model because of its local filtering capability, computational efficiency, and its ability to learn complex relationships among metrics derived from the visibility graph representation. In particular, the CNN architecture enables learning discriminative structural patterns from graph-derived descriptor sequences extracted from temporal flow windows.

The Weighted Sum Method computes a composite evaluation score by multiplying each normalized metric with a predefined weight coefficient representing its relative importance and subsequently summing the weighted values. In this study, weighted F1-score was assigned a higher positive weight because it directly reflects classification performance, whereas runtime was assigned a negative weight since lower execution time is preferred. Following metric normalization, the final composite scores were calculated using the corresponding weight coefficients. This approach enabled the identification of configurations that simultaneously provide strong classification performance and improved computational efficiency. Conclusions

In this study, the proposed GraphDL framework was introduced for flow-based cyber-attack detection using visibility graph-based structural representations together with deep learning techniques. The proposed framework transforms temporal flow-feature sequences into visibility graph structures and extracts graph-theoretical descriptors in order to model temporal and nonlinear characteristics of network traffic behavior.

Experimental evaluations conducted on the CICIDS2018 dataset demonstrated that the proposed framework can achieve strong classification performance across different temporal window and feature configurations. The results further showed that visibility graph-based structural descriptors are capable of capturing meaningful temporal and topological characteristics within flow-based network traffic data. In addition, comparative experiments conducted with a Raw-CNN baseline demonstrated that conventional deep learning models operating directly on raw flow features provide highly efficient and strong classification performance, particularly under smaller temporal window configurations.

Although the proposed GraphDL framework did not consistently outperform the Raw-CNN baseline in terms of classification accuracy or computational efficiency, the study demonstrates that graph-oriented structural representations can still provide an alternative perspective for modeling temporal traffic behavior in intrusion detection problems. In this respect, the primary contribution of the study lies not only in predictive performance, but also in investigating the feasibility of visibility graph-based structural representations for flow-based cyber-attack detection.

The experimental findings also revealed that visibility graph construction and graph-theoretical descriptor extraction introduce substantial computational overhead, particularly under larger temporal window settings. Therefore, the current implementation of the proposed framework appears more suitable for offline or semi-offline traffic analysis scenarios rather than fully real-time deployment environments.

Future studies may focus on reducing the computational cost of graph construction and graph metric extraction, as well as evaluating the proposed framework under larger-scale datasets and more diverse network environments in order to further analyze its scalability and generalization capability.
