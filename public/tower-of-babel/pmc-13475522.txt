TRPA1 deficiency alters brown adipose tissue proteome and thermogenic gene expression during cold exposure in mice
Authors: Cristhian D Sua-Cespedes, Otoniel Gonçalves Lima, Letícia Menezes Vasconcelos, Giovanna Zanetti, Miriane Avelino da Silva, Maria Nathalia Moraes, Guilherme Gomes, William Tadeu Festuccia, Ana Maria L Castrucci, José Thalles Lacerda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTRPA1 channels function as cold sensors and have been implicated in energy metabolism. We investigated the impact of TRPA1 on thermoregulation. Trpa1−/− mice exhibited body weight loss and increased food intake when transitioning from 30°C to mild cold (14 days at 22°C), indicating increased energy metabolism in response to cold. Trpa1−/− mice exhibited elevated oxygen consumption, while their core temperature remained unaffected. The BAT proteome in WT mice after 14 days at 22°C showed increased proteins related to glucose utilization and reduced proteins related to lipolysis, whereas Trpa1−/− mice revealed increased PRDM16 and proteins associated with peroxisomal/mitochondrial β-oxidation. Transcripts of Cidea, Ucp1, Cpt1a, and Lcad were higher in Trpa1−/− mice at 22°C compared to 30°C, relative to WT mice, with Prdm16 expression uniquely increased in Trpa1−/− mice. Thermographic images revealed higher interscapular surface temperature in Trpa1−/− mice exposed to 22°C. These data suggest that the TRPA1 channel reduces metabolic stress and contributes to thermogenic regulation in mice.Keywords: TRPA1 channel, Brown adipose tissue, Cold exposure, Metabolism, Thermogenesis

Introduction

Transient receptor potential (TRP) permeable channels are sensory receptors that respond to physical, chemical, and thermal stimuli, allowing calcium influx in cells [[1], [2], [3]]. Among these, TRPA (ankyrin)-1, the only member of the TRPA family identified in mammals, is a sensor for diverse noxious external stimuli such as intense cold, irritating compounds, mechanical stimuli, reactive chemicals, and endogenous signals associated with cellular damage [4,5]. TRPA1 senses and mediates behavioral and physiological responses to low environmental temperatures below 17°C [6] and to noxious heat stimuli [7]. In contrast, heat sensing by the TRPA1 channel has been reported in mouse neurons [5] and appears to be modulated by the cellular redox state [7,8].

Although TRPA1 is not considered the molecular basis of cold sensitivity in dorsal root ganglion and trigeminal neurons, it can still be activated by cold at a slow rate, supporting the notion that TRPA1 is a cold-activated channel [5]. Indeed, the overlap between cold-induced responses and TRPA1 activation suggests that this channel may play a role in thermogenesis. Notably, TRPA1 activation with allyl isothiocyanate (AITC), a pungent compound found in mustard, horseradish, and wasabi, is associated with adrenaline secretion potentially via activation of the adrenal sympathetic nerve, as well as upregulation of thermogenesis and brown adipose tissue (BAT) uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1) expression [9]. UCP1-expressing brown and beige adipocytes play a central role in nonshivering thermogenesis, a cold-induced heat-generating process that dissipates energy from oxidative metabolism as heat to prevent hypothermia [10].

Cold exposure activates UCP1-mediated thermogenesis in brown and beige adipocytes via sympathetic nervous system release of norepinephrine and adenosine, both of which increase cAMP through β3-adrenergic and A2a adenosine receptor activation, respectively [10,11]. Intracellular cAMP accumulation leads to protein kinase A (PKA)-mediated activation of intracellular lipolysis, releasing fatty acids that, after entering mitochondria, act as allosteric activators of UCP1 and serve as substrates for β-oxidation, thereby energetically supporting thermogenesis. In addition to lipolysis, PKA also enhances the transcription of UCP1 and thermogenic genes in brown adipocytes via signaling through cAMP response element binding protein (CREB), p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma co-activator 1a (PPARGC1A) [12]. Upon activation by fatty acids, UCP1 uncouples the proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane, diverting energy from ATP synthesis and releasing it as heat [13]. In addition to fatty acids, glucose and branched-chain amino acids are also important substrates oxidized to fuel BAT thermogenesis [14,15].

To investigate the role of TRPA1 in body thermoregulation, whole-body Trpa1 knockout (Trpa1−/−) and wild-type (WT) mice were evaluated for their thermoneutral zone, as well as energy balance and BAT molecular remodeling using label-free proteomics and quantitative PCR upon exposure to either thermoneutral (30°C) or mild cold (22°C) conditions. Our main findings indicate that whole-body TRPA1 deficiency is associated with an extended thermoneutral zone from 24 to 33°C and increased energy expenditure at 30, 23, or 10°C, but with unaltered core body temperature. TRPA1 deficiency also promoted, at 22°C, a negative energy balance characterized by a reduction in body mass, despite increased food intake.

Materials and methods

Mice

The experiments were performed with 3 to 6- month-old male mice, all on a C57BL/6 J background, bearing the following genotypes: C57BL/6 J wild type (WT) and global whole-body Trpa1 knockout (Trpa1−/−), from the Vivarium of the Department of Physiology, University of São Paulo. The C57BL/6 J WT strain was originally purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA); Trpa1−/−mice on a B6; 129 background (B6; 129P-Trpa1tm1Kykw/J) were also purchased from Jackson Laboratory and crossbred for 10 generations into the C57BL/6 J background. The C57BL/6 J background of the knockout mice was validated by genetic background monitoring (Central Vivarium of the School of Medicine, University of São Paulo). C57BL/6 J wild-type mice were used as controls for Trpa1−/−. Mouse genotypes were confirmed by PCR analysis of tail genomic DNA.

Mice were offered water and a commercial chow (3.8 kcal/g, 50 g/kg fat, 220 g/kg protein, and 540 g/kg carbohydrates, Nuvilab® CR-1 Irradiated, Quimtia S.A., Chapecó, SC, Brazil) ad libitum. Five main cohorts/groups of mice were used according to the experiments described below: 1) thermoneutrality determination; 2) weight gain, food intake, gene and protein expression; 3) whole-body indirect calorimetry; 4) telemetry of core temperature and locomotor activity; 5) thermography. All protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Use (CEUA IB/USP, number 350/19).

Thermoneutrality and whole-body indirect calorimetry

Thermoneutrality and whole-body indirect calorimetry assays were performed in a Promethium System (Sable Systems, Las Vegas, NV, USA) at the Rodent Metabolic Phenotyping Center (RMPC), Institute of Biomedical Sciences, University of São Paulo. For evaluation of the thermoneutral zone, mice acclimated at 30°C were evaluated for energy expenditure at different ambient temperatures during 4 h between 08:00 and 16:00 h on 4 consecutive days. The following order was applied: day 1-36°C from 08:00 to 12:00 h and 33°C from 12:00 to 16:00 h; day 2 - 30°C from 08:00 to 12:00 h and 27°C from 12:00 to 16:00 h; day 3- 24°C from 08:00 to 12:00 h and 21°C 12:00 to 16:00 h; day 4- 18°C from 08:00 to 12:00 h and 15°C from 12:00 to 16:00 h. From 16:00 until 08:00 h, which comprises the dark period, mice were maintained at 30°C. The average energy expenditure from the last hour at each temperature was plotted to establish the thermoneutral zone, minimizing possible carryover effects. Repeated measures one-way ANOVA was used for thermoneutrality determination.

For circadian metabolic rate, O2 consumption and expired CO2 volumes were measured for both genotypes using the Promethium system (Sable Systems, North Las Vegas, NV, USA). Mice were acclimated for 4 days at 23°C and maintained on a 12:12 light-dark cycle, with chow and water ad libitum. The oxygen consumption rate (VO2), carbon dioxide production (VCO2), and RER were continuously quantified for 48 h under each of the three controlled environmental temperatures: 10°C, 23°C, and 30°C, in randomly distributed mice. The results were analyzed using CalR [16]. Results were extracted as the average for 4 h and expressed as mL/h. Since WT and Trpa1−/− mouse weights were not statistically different at each temperature after 48 h, differences in O2 consumption and RER were compared by repeated measures two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post-test (p < 0.05).

Body weight gain and food intake

Mice were acclimated in thermoneutrality (30°C) for 14 days and then kept at 30°C or 22°C for an additional 14 days, under a 12:12 light/dark cycle (600 lux white LED light, ranging from 420 to 750 nm, lights on at 7 a.m. and off at 7 p.m. Body weights were assessed daily on a digital scale (0.1-2000 g, I-2000, Brazil), and weight gain was calculated by delta (day 14 minus day 0). Food intake was assessed weekly and expressed as kilocalories. Measures were compared by two-way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni post-test for p < 0.05.

Core temperature and locomotor activity

Mice were anesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (15 mg/kg) for intraperitoneal implantation of probes (E-Mitter telemetry implants, Starr Life Sciences, Oakmont, PA, USA), after which, mice were single-housed at 30°C for 14 days under a 12:12 light-dark cycle (600 lux white LED lights, lights on at 7 a.m.), followed by two weeks at 22°C. During the 4 weeks, core temperature and spontaneous locomotor activity were continuously recorded at 1-min intervals using the VitalView® Data Acquisition System (Software version 5.1, Starr Life Sciences, Oakmont, PA, USA). The data were exported to ClockLab Analysis (Software version 6.1.02, Actimetrics, Lafayette, IN, USA), grouped in 1 h intervals, and analyzed for circadian core temperature and activity, and amplitudes (calculated as 50% of the distance from trough to peak). Differences between genotypes at each time point and between amplitudes were compared by two-way ANOVA (p < 0.05), followed by Bonferroni post-test.

Proteomics

Sample preparation for proteomics analysis

Mice were kept as described above (section 2.3). Brown adipose tissue from mice (n = 3 per group, euthanized at 9 a.m.) was mechanically homogenized in lysis buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.8, 4% SDS, 150 mM NaCl) supplemented with protease inhibitor (Complete™ ULTRA Tablets, Mini, EASYpack Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, Roche, USA), using a bead blaster refrigerated homogenizer (Benchmark Scientific, Sayreville, NJ, USA) at a maximum speed of 3650 rpm with three cycles of 30 s on-and-off at 4°C. Samples were kept on ice and sonicated for three cycles (10 s bursts with 30 s pauses) at 20% power using a Pulse 150 Ultrasonic Homogenizer (Benchmark Scientific, Sayreville, NJ, USA), then centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C.

The supernatants were collected, and protein concentration was measured using a BCA protein assay. Aliquots of 200 μg of total protein were reduced using 200 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) added to each sample at 1:1 (v/v), heated at 90°C for 10 min, and then cooled to room temperature (RT). Subsequently, samples were diluted to <0.3% SDS with urea buffer (8 M urea in 100 mM NH4HCO3) and loaded onto 10 kDa Microcon centrifugal filter units using the filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) method operated at 10,000 x g for 50 min at 20°C [17,18]. Filter units and collection tubes used in FASP were previously passivated by overnight incubation in 5% (v/v) TWEEN®−20 to reduce peptide and protein loss due to non-specific surface binding [19].

The filter-collected samples were centrifuged for protein concentration. Subsequently, the samples were washed once with 200 μL urea buffer. Then, 200 μL of 50 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) in urea buffer were added and mixed for 5 min at 600 rpm. Alkylation was carried out for 25 min at RT in the dark. The filters were centrifuged and washed twice with 200 μL of urea buffer, followed by three washes with 100 mM NH4HCO3. The filters were placed into fresh collection tubes, and 100 μL of 50 mM NH4HCO3 were added to the filters. Trypsin/Lys-C Mix (Promega, USA) was added to the filters at an enzyme-to-protein ratio of 1:100 (w/w), and incubated for 12 h at 37°C; a second digestion was carried out with trypsin (Promega, USA) at an enzyme-to-protein ratio of 1:100 (w/w) at 37°C for 4 h.

The peptides were filtered, and the filters were rinsed three times with 100 μL of 3% acetonitrile (ACN) in 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) to maximize the number of retrieved peptides. Samples were acidified to a final concentration of 2% TFA, and peptides were purified with reversed-phase chromatography using C18 micro-pipette tips (TopTip™, PolyLC, Columbia, MD, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Peptides were vacuum dried and stored at −20°C. All samples were processed in the same experimental batch.

Mass spectrometry

Dried peptides were recovered in 20 μL of aqueous 0.1% formic acid. The peptide mixture was analyzed by online nanoflow LC–ESI–MS/MS using an ACQUITY UPLC system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) coupled online to a maXis 3G quadrupole time-of-flight (Q-TOF) mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany), equipped with a Captive Spray nanoelectrospray source. Two microliters of sample were injected, and the peptide mixture was loaded onto a trap column (nanoAcquity UPLC® 2G-V/M Trap 5 μm Symmetry® C18, 180 μm × 20 mm, Waters, Milford, MA, USA) for 3 min at a flow rate of 7 μL/min of aqueous 0.1% formic acid.

Peptides were separated on an analytical column (nanoAcquity UPLC® 1.8 μm HSS T3, 75 μm × 200 mm, Waters, Milford, MA, USA) using a 240 min gradient from 2% to 85% of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (1 min at 2%, 209 min 2–30%, 10 min 30–85%, 5 min wash at 85%, 5 min 85–2%, 10 min equilibration at 2%). The flow rate was set at 200 nL/min. Eluted peptides were analyzed on a maXis 3G (Q-TOF) mass spectrometer operated in positive ion mode under data-dependent acquisition in the m/z range of 150–2200. Precursor ions were fragmented with collision-induced dissociation (CID).

Label-free quantification (LFQ) and statistical rationale

The raw data (mzXML files) were loaded into PEAKS Studio v.11 (Bioinformatics Solution Inc., Waterloo, ON, Canada), and the LFQ workflow was applied for protein identification (PEAKS DB), followed by relative quantification (PEAKS Q – de novo-assisted quantification). First, the MS/MS spectra were submitted to a database search against the Mus musculus database, which contains 17,234 reviewed protein entries from UniProt/Swiss-Prot (Reference proteome ID: UP000000589), downloaded on September 2, 2025. The following parameters were applied: a precursor mass tolerance of 15 ppm, a fragment mass tolerance of 0.02 Da, and trypsin as the enzyme with semi-specific cleavage, allowing up to three missed cleavages. Carbamidomethylation (C) was set as a fixed modification, and oxidation (M) and acetylation (protein N-terminus) were set as variable modifications, with a maximum of three modifications per peptide in the database search results. A False Discovery Rate (FDR) threshold of 1% was applied at both the peptide-spectrum match (PSM) and protein levels.

The protein identification results were subsequently processed using the PEAKS Q quantification add-on module. The LFQ method was based on identified LC/MS features by retention time alignment, feature vector contribution, and match between runs (ID transfer) for protein quantification using the following parameters: mass error tolerance (shift between different runs) of 20 ppm, retention time shift tolerance of 2 min, and feature intensity ≥10E2. Normalization of datasets was performed by total-ion count (TIC), and the normalization factor obtained for each sample was applied to calculate normalized areas of proteins (top-3 peptides). Since the peptide detection limits were set by the alignment windows of retention times (RTs) in samples where they were successfully detected, some protein areas may result in a zero (0) value due to the absence of suitable peaks from unique peptides to integrate into the results. In such cases, zero values were considered missing and replaced with “NA”.

Normalized protein areas were log2-transformed. Missing values in the dataset were attributable to Missing At Random (MAR)/Missing Completely at Random (MCAR), low expression/missing-not-at-random, and Missing Not At Random (MNAR) [20]. Missing values were imputed for each experimental group using a two-step sequential imputation approach. First, missing values for proteins detected in at least two out of three replicates (2/3) were imputed using k-nearest neighbor (kNN) imputation [21], with k equal to 15 per group to minimize the root mean squared error (RMSE) across repeated simulations over 50 iterations. Next, missing values for proteins detected in only one replicate per group (1/3) were imputed using MinProb (Probabilistic Minimum Imputation, q = 0.01) [22]. While kNN is suitable for MCAR and more generally MAR values, MinProb is specifically designed to impute MNAR values [23].

Differential protein abundance was assessed using two-tailed Student's t-tests for pairwise comparisons between temperature conditions within each genotype (WT: 30°C vs 22°C; KO: 30°C vs 22°C). Proteins showing nominal p < 0.05 and fold change ≥ 1.2 [log2(FC) ≤ −0.26 or ≥ 0.26] were selected for downstream biological interpretation. Diagnostic plots summarizing the Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-values and Storey q-value analyses are provided in Supplementary Data 1 (Fig. S1–S3). These diagnostics support the statistical rationale for prioritizing proteins using nominal p-values and fold-change thresholds in this exploratory proteomic study. The resulting temperature-responsive protein sets were subsequently analyzed using pathways and functional enrichment approaches to identify biological processes associated with thermal adaptation.

This pairwise comparison strategy was selected based on the biological characteristics of the experimental model. Because TRPA1 deletion is constitutive, each genotype represents a distinct physiological context in which temperature responses occur. The resulting proteomic profiles may reflect both the consequences of TRPA1 loss and long-term developmental and compensatory adaptations accumulated throughout the organism's lifetime. Therefore, temperature-associated proteomic changes were evaluated independently within each genotype to characterize the thermal adaptation response of each genetic background. Accordingly, a formal genotype × temperature interaction model was not performed.

Bioinformatics analysis

Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the DAVID web tool (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) based on KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathways and GO (Gene Ontology) biological processes (GOTERM BP ALL). Functional annotation clustering was applied with the lowest classification stringency and an enrichment threshold of 0.5. Enriched terms were selected using a p-value cutoff of < 0.05. Bioinformatic and statistical analyses were performed using custom scripts in R (64-bit version 4.3.1) with packages reshape2 (1.4.4), readr (2.1.4), Tidyverse (2.0.0), tidytext (0.4.2), and stringr (1.5.1). All analyses were performed after log2 transformation, and kNN imputation was implemented using the kNN function of the “Impute” R package (1.80.0). MinProb imputation was performed using the imputeLCMD R package (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=imputeLCMD).

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Mice were acclimated and challenged with mild cold temperature as described above. At 9 a.m. on the last day, mice were euthanized with CO2 followed by cervical dislocation. BAT was harvested, cleaned of adherent tissue, and mechanically homogenized in TRIzol (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA), using a bead blaster refrigerated homogenizer (Benchmark Scientific, Sayreville, NJ, USA) at 3650 rpm with 1 run of three cycles (30 s on-and-off at 4°C). RNA extraction was performed according to the manufacturer's protocol (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA concentration and quality were determined by the OD260/OD280 ratio using a spectrophotometer (NanoDrop ND-1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and integrity was assessed by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel. One μg of RNA, random hexamer primers, and reagents according to the Superscript III manufacturer's instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were used for the reverse transcriptase reaction.

The mRNA content of thermogenic genes peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator a (Ppargc1a), PR-domain containing 16 protein (Prdm16), carnitine palmitoyltransferase Ia and Ib (Cpt1a), long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (Lcad), uncoupling protein 1 (Ucp1), and cell death-inducing effector a (Cidea), was evaluated in a solution containing 12.5 ng of cDNA, 300 nM of each primer and PowerUp SYBR® Green qPCR Master Mix (2X) (Applied Biosystems, USA) in a QuantStudio 6 Applied Biosystem thermocycler (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA) with the following protocol: 2 min at 50°C, 10 min at 95°C, followed by 50 cycles of 15 s at 95°C, 1 min at 60°C, and then a melting curve from 60°C to 95°C with 0.05 °C/s with continuous fluorescence acquisition. Rplp0 (Ribosomal protein lateral stalk subunit P0), encoding a protein component of the 60S large ribosomal subunit) was used as the housekeeping gene. Primers (Table 1) were designed on https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/and synthesized by IDT DNA (Coralville, IA, USA). Results were analyzed using relative expression [24], normalized to the mean value of WT at 30°C, and plotted as log(2^-ΔΔCt). Data were expressed as the mean value ± SEM and compared by two-way ANOVA (p < 0.05) followed by Bonferroni post-test. All statistical analyses were carried out in GraphPad Prism 8.0 (San Diego, CA, USA).

Thermography

Thermographic images of the body were taken at the end of the 2nd week at 30°C and at the end of the 2nd week at 22°C from non-anesthetized mice used in the telemetry assays, between 9 and 10 a.m. Images were obtained with an infrared camera SC640 (detector 640 × 480 px, sensitivity: <30 mK, IR: 7.5–13 μm, FLIR Systems, Wilsonville, OR, USA), and the average of surface temperature of different body regions was analyzed using the software Thermacam 2.9 (FLIR Systems), Python and ImageJ (emissivity 0.98)), following established thermographic procedures for non-invasive thermal physiology studies [25,26]. Infrared thermography has been widely recognized as an important non-invasive tool for monitoring thermoregulatory and thermogenic responses in experimental animals, allowing repeated measurements without the physiological interference associated with invasive thermal probes or surgical implantation procedures [26,27]. The emissivity area (1 × 1 cm) and the temperature obtained with a pair of thermal probes (4-channel data logger, RDXL4SD Omega) were used as the background reference (outside the animal body).

Mean surface temperatures were calculated from eight predefined anatomical regions of interest (ROIs): the interscapular region (centered between the inferior angles of the scapulae, corresponding to the major brown adipose tissue depot and a well-established thermogenic site [28]), the dorsal thoracolumbar region, the periocular region (centered over the medial canthus of the eye, reflecting superficial ocular blood flow and autonomic thermoregulatory responses), the bilateral inguinal region, the acoustic region (centered on the external ear/pinna, a highly vascularized thermal window for peripheral heat dissipation), the nostril region (centered over the external nares, reflecting nasal mucosal perfusion and respiratory heat exchange), the plantar surface of the hind paw, and the proximal third of the tail, another major thermal window for heat dissipation and thermoregulation in rodents27. ROIs were positioned using standardized anatomical landmarks and identical dimensions across all animals. Thermographic images were acquired with animals in the same prone position, at a fixed camera-to-subject distance, with the camera oriented perpendicular (90°) to the body surface under identical acquisition conditions. Each ROI comprised approximately 1000 temperature pixels, and the mean ROI temperature was calculated to minimize local thermal heterogeneity and improve measurement reproducibility. To reduce observer bias, ROI selection and temperature extraction were performed on coded images by an investigator blinded to genotype. Mean ROI temperatures were analyzed using multifactorial analysis of variance (ANOVA), with genotype and body region as experimental factors, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Trpa1−/− mice have an extended thermoneutral zone and lower body weight gain, despite increased food intake under mild cold stress

To begin our exploration of the role of the TRPA1 channel in thermoregulation, we first evaluated the thermoneutral zone of C57BL/6 J (WT) and Trpa1−/− mice by measuring their oxygen consumption at different ambient temperatures. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, Trpa1−/− mice displayed higher rates of oxygen consumption than WT mice in some of the ambient temperatures investigated (15, 21, 27, 33 and 36°C). Furthermore, while WT mouse thermoneutral zone ranged from 27 to 33.5°C, which is consistent with the current literature for wild-type C57BL6J mice [29,30], Trpa1−/− mice showed a lower thermoneutral zone (3°C below the WT), ranging from 24 to 33.5°C (Fig. 1a). Thus, room temperature (≤22°C) represents a moderate cold stimulus for both genotypes, with a greater impact on Trpa1−/− mice. Based on these findings, we further evaluated WT and Trpa1−/− mice for energy balance, body temperature and BAT proteome and gene expression after acclimation to either 30 ± 1°C as the optimal thermoneutral ambient temperature, where mice show the lowest rates of oxygen consumption suggesting reduced activation of thermogenic processes, or 22 ± 1°C as a mild cold stress to elicit thermogenic processes and mechanisms of heat conservation. Since the 90's, it was demonstrated that vivarium temperatures could be considered as a thermal stressor for rats and mice, being 26-34°C the optimum temperature for C57BL/6 J (similar to the range found in our results) [[29], [30], [31]]. In addition, and corroborating our observations, Carstens and colleagues [32] defined 29°C as the thermal comfort temperature for triple-knockout mice lacking Trpv1, Trpa1, and Trpm8 channels. Upon acclimation at 30°C, there were no changes in body weight gain and food intake between genotypes (Fig. 1b and c), whereas at 22°C, Trpa1−/− mice featured reduced body weight gain, despite a marked increase in food intake when compared to WT mice (Fig. 1b and c). Altogether, these findings indicate that while WT mice maintain body weight at 22°C, perhaps at the expense of a small increase in food intake (t-test p = 0.049), Trpa1−/− mice display a negative energy balance characterized by reduced body weight gain, despite a significant increase in food intake when challenged by a mild cold stress.

Trpa1−/− mice exhibit elevated rates of oxygen consumption, but circadian core temperature is not affected

Next, to evaluate the causes of this negative energy balance, we measured WT and Trpa1−/− mice oxygen consumption and respiratory exchange rate (RER) at 30, 22 and 10°C. As depicted in Fig. 2, Trpa1−/− mice displayed higher rates of oxygen consumption and therefore energy expenditure than WT mice, mostly during the dark phase at both 30 and 10°C, and at both the light and dark phases at 23°C. Also, Trpa1−/− mice exhibited higher RER values at 30°C and at 23°C during the dark phase, suggesting increased carbohydrate use as fuel alongside lipids. Levels of CO2 expired volume are presented in Supplementary Data 1 (Fig. S4).

O2 consumption and RER. (a) 30°C; (b) 23°C; (c) 10°C. The mean values (n = 6) ± SEM were compared between the genotypes at each temperature by repeated measures two-way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni post-test, p < 0.05.

Aerobic capacity (volume O2) is closely related to temperature in mammals: cold exposure increases oxygen consumption during thermogenesis [33]. Here, we confirmed the expected increase at lower temperatures and observed significantly higher metabolic rates in the absence of the TRPA1 channel compared to WT across all three temperatures, including the thermoneutral temperature (30°C). In other words, even under basal metabolic conditions without thermal stress, the Trpa1−/− genotype was already metabolically more active than WT. Our RER values for both mouse lines at all temperatures suggested the use of a mix of carbohydrates and lipids as fuel sources for energy production. It has been described that TRPA1 mediates respiratory physiology by regulating ventilatory responses to mild hypoxia, and disruption of TRPA1 enhances in vivo vagal discharges induced by hyperoxia and hypoxia [34].

Bearing in mind that weight loss and food consumption of knockout animals were higher at 22°C compared to knockout animals at 30°C or WT at 22°C (over a 14-day experiment), higher O2 consumption of the knockout mice at lower temperatures than WT animals was to be expected.

Circadian core temperature did not differ statistically between 30°C and 22°C in WT or Trpa1−/− mice (Fig. 3a). Nevertheless, the amplitude of core temperature in Trpa1−/− animals was lower than the WT group at 30°C (Fig. 3b). Interestingly, a similar pattern was observed in the analysis of spontaneous locomotor activity, with no differences between 30°C and 22°C in either genotype (Fig. 3c). However, the amplitude of locomotor activity in Trpa1−/− mice seemed also lower than in WT mice at 30°C (Fig. 3d).

Amplitudes of core temperature (c) and of locomotor activity (f) at 30°C and 22°C. The mean values (n = 4-11) + SEM were compared between genotypes at each time point and between amplitudes by two-way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni post-test. Asterisks indicate significance for p < 0.05.

A clear correlation between locomotion and core temperature was observed. Trpa1−/− mice exhibited reduced locomotor activity amplitude at thermoneutrality compared to WT mice, leading to a lower amplitude in core temperature at 30°C. Although no statistically significant differences in core temperature were observed between 30°C and 22°C in either genotype, we reasoned that the Trpa1−/− genotype had to intensify its metabolism at 22°C, as indicated by O2 consumption, to maintain body temperature similar to thermoneutral conditions, while increasing it sufficiently to match the amplitude of core temperature observed in WT mice at 22°C.

It is important to mention that, unlike our study, the literature has recently reported increased BAT thermogenesis, core body temperature, and energy expenditure in response to TRPA1 agonists [35]. The discrepancy is probably due to the following factors: (i) their experiments were performed at 24°C; (ii) the photoperiod and irradiance were not mentioned for either the vivarium or the experimental setup, which usually means that the light source originates from the ceiling, irradiating a wide range of light intensities; (iii) the time of day when euthanasia was performed was not mentioned; (iiii) body and BAT temperature were monitored in anesthetized animals.

As a polymodal channel, TRPA1 is required for sensing cold in the skin, modulating both vasodilatation and vasoconstriction [36]. However, its role in core body temperature regulation in C57BL/6 animals was not demonstrated when using pharmacological antagonists [37].

Absence of the TRPA1 channel affects BAT proteome remodeling and the thermoregulation mechanism during prolonged mild cold exposure

The overarching goal of this analysis was to understand reprogramming of the BAT proteome following adaptation to prolonged mild cold exposure. Although proteins represent central functional intermediates between genotype and phenotype, and their presence and abundance are fundamental determinants of biological system function [38], we emphasize that the proteomic findings should be interpreted as associative and hypothesis-generating regarding potential mechanisms, rather than as direct mechanistic evidence.

A total of 2665 unique proteins were identified and quantified across all samples. We identified 214 differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) in WT mice and 172 in Trpa1−/− mice in the comparison between 30°C and 22°C for each genotype (Fig. 4a) (Supplementary Data 2, Table S1). The overlap of only 15 DAPs between the genotypes suggests that adaptive thermoregulation proceeds through different mechanisms. Nonetheless, understanding this small set of shared proteins could provide insights into cold-induced changes that are independent of genotype.

Both genotypes showed a significant reduction in the levels of the proteins PLIN4, SPART, ABHD5, and members of the 14-3-3 family (YWHAQ and YWHAG) at 22°C (Fig. 4b). These proteins are associated with the accumulation and turnover of lipid droplets (LDs), playing a direct role in maintaining LD stability and regulating lipolysis by controlling the recruitment of lipases to LDs, as well as lipid droplet biogenesis and degradation [[39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44]].

The reduction of these key biomarkers suggests that LDs may be reduced in BAT under cold stress. However, it remains unclear whether this is related to the depletion of fat stores in BAT, leading to reduced lipophagy and a shift in substrate generation toward more rapid provision of resources for thermogenesis, such as glucose and circulating non-esterified fatty acids (NEFAs).

On the other hand, we also identified the increased abundance of NDUFA13 and HTRA2 at 22°C in both genotypes. These proteins have been previously associated with ATP synthesis and maintenance of mitochondrial quality control under stress conditions, respectively [45,46]. In this line, it could indicate enhanced mitochondrial metabolism through NADH oxidation in both genotypes in our study.

Additionally, we analyzed the most abundant proteins based on spectral count (peptide-spectrum matching, PSM) to identify the DAPs with the highest abundance (Supplementary Data 3, Table S2). This approach allowed us to assess changes in the dynamic range of BAT and detect other differences between genotypes subjected to cold exposure (Fig. 4c). The top 20 DAPs identified in the dynamic range were detected predominantly in the WT genotype, with up-regulation of UCP1, FABP4, TKT, and ATP2A1 and down-regulation of ECHS1, ETFA, PYGM, and TUFM. Surprisingly, UCP1, a canonical marker of adaptive thermogenesis, did not show a significant change in the Trpa1−/− genotype, although it showed a trend toward increased abundance at 22°C (Fig. 4d).

Our findings on BAT reprogramming in the WT genotype were similar to those reported by Cutler and colleagues in mice subjected to 4°C for 3 weeks [47]. They reported increases in UCP1 and transketolase (TKT), an enzyme in the non-oxidative branch of the pentose phosphate pathway. At the same time, there were reductions in enzymes involved in short-chain fatty acid oxidation (ECHS1). These proteins transport electrons from fatty acid breakdown into the electron transport chain (ETFA), and decreased glycogen phosphorylase (PYGB).

TKT appears to be a hub protein in BAT metabolism of the WT genotype in our study. It plays a vital role in shuttling carbon intermediates between the pentose phosphate pathway and glycolysis, and it is a crucial source of NADPH [48]. It dynamically responds to cellular energy demands by converting d-ribose-5-phosphate (R5P) into d-glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P), which then proceeds to lower glycolysis, where ATP and pyruvate are generated [49]. Furthermore, TKT can also regulate the gene expression of lipolytic enzymes, thereby controlling lipolysis [50].

It has previously been reported that ribose and xylose carbons are incorporated into glycogen assembly, with TKT participation [51]. Glycogen accumulation is necessary for the production of reactive oxygen species, which leads to the activation of p38 MAPK and drives UCP1 expression [52]. Notably, we detected the down-regulation of glycogen phosphorylases PYGB (p-value = 0.036, log2FC = −0.68) and PYGM (p-value = 0.029, log2FC = −1.62). The downregulation of these glycogen phosphorylases is consistent with reduced glycogen degradation and suggests glycogen accumulation at 22°C in WT mice.

In parallel, we evidenced the increase in protein abundance of FABP4 in the WT genotype. FABP4, an adipocyte-associated protein, binds free fatty acids (FFAs) and is essential for FFA uptake into BAT, with its BAT expression increasing upon cold exposure [53]. Moreover, it has been demonstrated that NEFAs are not efficiently absorbed by BAT in FABP4/5 double knockout (DKO) mice [54]. Therefore, the increase in FABP4, as observed at 22°C in the WT genotype, may indicate that NEFAs are taken up into BAT in a scenario with low lipolysis to support thermogenesis. This process is further highlighted by a decrease in TUFM at 22°C in the WT genotype, as we observed. TUFM is a protein that enhances lipolysis [55], another key protein whose down-regulation indicates a lower rate of lipolysis. Additionally, ATP2A1 (SERCA1), an endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase 1 that transports Ca2+ into the endoplasmic reticulum and regulates intracellular Ca2+ levels, represents another source of heat production contributing to BAT thermogenic function [56].

We then performed a functional enrichment analysis using KEGG pathways and GO biological processes to further understand the signaling pathways altered by cold exposure in each genotype. Our data robustly suggest an increase in glucose utilization via glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway with pyruvate cycling, accompanied by enhanced proteolysis/proteasome activity and a reduction in lipolysis, with decreased fatty acid oxidation in the WT genotype (Fig. 4e). We detected up-regulation of proteolysis mediated by the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) and mitochondrial translation, as evidenced by the clustering of up-regulated proteins such as ubiquitins UBB and UBC, as well as proteasomal components (PSMA4, PSMD11, PSMD14, PSMD8) and mitochondrial ribosomal proteins (MRPS14, MRPS21, MRPS22) in BAT from WT mice. UPS plays an important role in mitochondrial energy metabolism [57].

In contrast, some proteins of the MICOS complex (mitochondrial contact site and cristae organizing system), including APOO, CHCHD3, and CHCHD6, as well as other proteins related to mitochondrial organization, were down-regulated in WT mice. In this context, we hypothesized that reduced abundance of MICOS-associated proteins may indicate adaptive remodeling of mitochondrial cristae architecture during prolonged cold exposure, although the functional implications of this response remain to be established.

To gain insight into the fuel preferences of cold-adapted BAT in the WT genotype, we focused on three key pathways altered by the DAPs, including the electron transport chain (ETC), carbohydrate metabolism associated with pyruvate cycling, and lipid metabolism associated with reduced lipolysis (Fig. 4f). We observed the up-regulation of NADH dehydrogenase subunits (NDUFA13, NDUFA2, NDUFA8, NDUFB2, NDUFS4, NDUFV3) in Complex I, as well as proteins in Complex V (ATP5PO).

In contrast, there was down-regulation of flavoprotein dehydrogenases (ETFA) and succinate dehydrogenase (SDHA) in Complex II. Additionally, ETFRF1, a regulatory protein of the electron-transfer flavoprotein ETFA [58], was also down-regulated. Ubiquinol-cytochrome C reductases (UQCRH, UQCC4, UQCC5) in Complex III were similarly down-regulated in BAT from WT mice. These alterations in the mitochondrial respiratory chain complex proteins are consistent with metabolic remodeling and support the possibility of shifts in substrate preference in cold-adapted BAT in the WT genotype. Complex I primarily facilitates the entry of carbohydrate-derived electrons into the ETC, whereas Complex II accepts electrons from fatty acid oxidation [47].

We investigated alterations in carbohydrate metabolism by examining the up-regulation of transketolase (TKT), glycogenin 1 (GYG1), and the NADP-dependent malic enzyme (ME1), alongside down-regulation of lactate dehydrogenases (LDHB and LDHD), glycogen phosphorylases (PYGB and PYGM), and pyruvate dehydrogenase protein X component (PDHX). Beyond TKT, pyruvate cycling may also be supported by the up-regulation of ME1, a cytosolic enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of malate and NADP+ to pyruvate and NADPH [59].

Concurrently, the down-regulation of LDHB and LDHD in BAT from WT mice suggests maintenance of elevated intracellular pyruvate levels relative to lactate. Lactate dehydrogenases catalyze the reversible interconversion of pyruvate and lactate, with the reaction equilibrium generally favoring lactate formation regardless of isoform [60]. LDHB is preferentially localized near the inner mitochondrial membrane, and local pyruvate depletion may promote lactate oxidation [61]. Despite this, LDHB has been reported to have higher affinity for lactate and preferentially catalyzes lactate-to-pyruvate conversion (see reviews by Refs. [62,63].

In our study, one plausible interpretation is that the coordinated down-regulation of LDH isoenzymes reflects an overall suppression of the canonical pyruvate-to-lactate conversion pathway, thereby preserving pyruvate levels. Another mechanistic possibility is that lactate levels are reduced due to limited conversion of pyruvate into lactate, which would preserve pyruvate for cycling and favor gluconeogenic flux and glucose replenishment for glycogen synthesis. Consequently, reduced lactate availability may limit the abundance of LDHB protein. Notably, pyruvate cycling is marked by the down-regulation of PDHX, a component of the PDH complex that catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA [64], along with a potential increase in glycogen biosynthesis via GYG1 [65], and a reduction in degradation through PYGM/B isoenzymes.

Connected to lipid metabolism in in BAT from WT mice, we detected the down-regulation of proteins related to lipolysis and enhanced lipid turnover (PNPLA2, also known as adipose triglyceride lipase - ATGL) [66,67], mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation (ECHS1) [68], fatty acid biosynthesis (ECHDC3) [69], and an enzyme involved in converting cholesterol to cholesteryl esters for storage in lipid droplets or secretion as major components of lipoproteins (ACAT1) [70], as well as a protein associated with chylomicron assembly and cholesterol transport (APOAIV) [71].

The changes promoted by the absence of TRPA1 after prolonged mild cold exposure followed entirely different pathways compared to those observed in the WT genotype, particularly regarding the type of fuel used in thermogenesis. We observed in Trpa1−/− mice that lipid metabolic processes displayed both up- and down-regulation, albeit in distinct functional directions. Up-regulated proteins were primarily associated with peroxisomal and mitochondrial β-oxidation, whereas down-regulated proteins were linked to lipogenesis and cholesterol homeostasis. Up-regulated proteins were also related to chemotaxis and DNA damage processes, cell development, and the conversion of pyruvate to lactate via LDHA. In contrast, there was down-regulation of proteins involved in responses to toxic substances, oxidative stress, chemical homeostasis, and hormone signaling (Fig. 4g).

Among the up-regulated proteins in Trpa1−/− mice, we evidenced the transcriptional regulator PRDM16, the main player stimulating differentiation into brown fat cells (adipogenesis) [72], while proteins associated with lipogenesis and lipid storage were down-regulated (AACS, ABHD5, ACAT2, MCAT, FECH, SPART, THRSP) [[73], [74], [75], [76]]. In this way, lipogenesis should not be confused with adipogenesis [77].

We also observed the up-regulation of peroxisomal β-oxidation proteins (ECI2, EHHADH, ACAA1B) and mitochondrial β-oxidation (ACOT11). Peroxisomal β-oxidation significantly contributes to total cellular degradation of fatty acids (FAs) and promotes the production of reactive oxygen species (e.g., H2O2), which in turn results in a decrease in lipolysis rates as a negative feedback mechanism [78].

Surprisingly, acyl-CoA oxidase 1 (ACOX1), a key peroxisomal matrix protein, was down-regulated after cold exposure in BAT from Trpa1−/− mice. Previous study has shown that in the absence of ACOX1, the Acox1-LKO mice exhibited enhanced browning in cultured white adipocytes [79]. Similarly, a mutation in Acox1 (Acox1Lampe1) led to higher energy metabolism and increased oxygen consumption with increased Ucp1 expression compared to WT controls, suggesting enhanced BAT activity via low activity of ACOX1 [80].

The relationship between the down-regulation of ACOX1 and increased energy metabolism remains unclear, as ACOX1 is a key component in peroxisomal β-oxidation. Based on our data, we hypothesize that the high energy demand favors the utilization of less complex FAs, which may influence the abundance of specific enzymes in the peroxisomes. In this context, up-regulated EHHADH acts on long-chain fatty acids in the peroxisomal β-oxidation pathway [81] and is essential for medium-chain dicarboxylic fatty acids [82], while up-regulated ACAA1B plays a role in the β-oxidation of medium and long-chain fatty acid derivatives [83].

The potential increase in ROS in the BAT metabolism of Trpa1−/− mice may be suggested by the up-regulation of enzymes involved in mitochondrial and peroxisomal β-oxidation, together with the down-regulation of enzymes with antioxidant roles (GPX4, PRDX5, PRDX6, CP, MAPK3, LONP1, STX2). The enzymes GPX4, PRDX5, and PRDX6 play a key role in detoxifying lipid hydroperoxides, helping to mitigate oxidative stress [84,85]. Previous studies have shown that excessive increases in ROS lead to the production of lipid peroxides [86], while simultaneously, increased superoxide production has been shown to enhance UCP1 activity [87]. In this way, the proposed involvement of ROS could be considered a secondary consequence of the metabolic remodeling observed in Trpa1−/− mice, although their precise contribution remains to be further elucidated.

Although a significant increase in UCP1 protein abundance was not observed in the proteomic data for Trpa1−/− mice, the mechanisms underlying increased UCP1 activity may be linked to the up-regulation of β-oxidation, and metabolic alterations potentially related to ROS generation, together with the down-regulation of proteins involved in antioxidant and detoxification processes as well as the conversion of pyruvate to lactate via LDHA. During adaptation to severe cold exposure, BAT simultaneously metabolizes both FAs and glucose, with FAs primarily used as a fuel source for thermogenesis, whereas glucose-derived carbons are mostly released as lactate [88]. In this context, lactate enters the mitochondrial matrix, stimulating ETC activity and ATP production [89], and has previously been reported as essential for the activation of BAT thermogenesis [90]. In fact, LDHA was up-regulated in BAT of Trpa1−/− mice (p-value = 0.001; log2FC = 1.82).

BAT proteome remodeling in Trpa1−/− mice after cold exposure suggests a shift in fuel sources for thermogenesis compared to the WT genotype. However, classical key players linked to the thermogenic pathway were not detected in the proteomic analysis. To deepen our understanding of the differences in thermoregulation due to the absence of the TRPA1 channel, we explored gene expression analysis of these markers.

Other possible mechanisms for lipid utilization by BAT have been proposed by Heeren's group. Following cold stimulation, norepinephrine stimulates insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells, increasing lipolysis in white adipose tissue and consequently the release of free fatty acids, which may be utilized by BAT [91]. If insulin secretion is reduced in Trpa1−/− mice, fatty acid uptake could be enhanced, promoting the β-oxidation observed above. In addition, the group demonstrated increased lipoprotein absorption and subsequent lipid accumulation by BAT endothelial cells [92]. Since TRPA1 is highly expressed in brain endothelial cells [93], we might hypothesize that this channel could also regulate lipid uptake in BAT's endothelium, complementing the hypothetical substrate preference mentioned before. However, both pathways/mechanisms must be further explored.

The thermogenic pathway is activated in the absence of the TRPA1 channel

Here, we evaluated the expression of Prdm16, Cidea, Ucp1, Ppargc1a, Cpt1a, and Lcad to elucidate potential changes complementary to the proteome data. Regarding Prdm16 mRNA, only Trpa1−/− mice showed higher expression at 22°C than at 30°C, consistent with the proteomics data. At 22°C, knockout animals also displayed higher expression of this gene than WT mice (Fig. 5a). The expression of Cidea (Fig. 5b) and Ucp1 (Fig. 5c) exhibited significant differences between temperatures for each genotype and a remarkable increase in transcripts at 22°C in knockout mice compared to WT animals. There was no significant difference in Ppargc1a expression between genotypes at each temperature or between temperatures (Fig. 5d). As with Prdm16, Cpt1a (Fig. 5e) transcripts were higher in Trpa1−/− mice at 22°C, and Lcad mRNA exhibited a similar tendency, although it was not statistically significant (Fig. 5f).

Thermogenic pathway. Gene expression of (a)Prdm16; (b)Cidea; (c)Ucp1; (d)Ppargc1a;(e)Cpt1a;(f)Lcad in both genotypes at 30°C and 22°C. The mean values (n = 4) ± SEM were compared among genotypes at each temperature and between temperatures for each genotype by two-way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni post-test, p < 0.05.

In summary, with the exception of Ppargc1a and Lcad, all thermogenic genes evaluated in this study showed a remarkable increase in Trpa1−/− mice compared to WT animals at 22°C, suggesting that in the absence of the TRPA1 channel, there was a marked increase in cold sensitivity resulting in a substantial stimulation of transcripts related to non-shivering thermogenesis.

PRDM16 regulates BAT adipogenesis and, together with PPARGC1A, induces UCP1 expression [94,95]. Cohen and colleagues [96] reported that Prdm16 knockout mice had impaired thermogenesis and, indeed 4°C exposure of WT mice induced an increase in this gene expression in BAT, subcutaneous (iWAT), and gonadal (gWAT) white adipose depots [97]. Therefore, the challenge of 22°C appears to be insufficient to promote significant activation of Prdm16 in WT, but is sufficient to markedly increase its transcripts in the absence of the TRPA1 channel. This coincides with the up-regulation of PRDM16 identified in our Trpa1−/− mouse proteome.

CIDEA is a lipid droplet-associated protein highly expressed in brown and beige adipocytes, promoting the enlargement of lipid droplets by inhibition of lipolysis [[98], [99], [100], [101]]. According to de Jong and co-workers [102] and Fischer and collaborators [103], Cidea is expressed continuously and invariably. However, in our study, Cidea mRNA was affected by both genotype and temperature. It should be borne in mind that our control animals were kept at 30°C and cold-challenged at 22°C. However, those authors described that protein levels are remarkably higher in thermogenically recruited brown fat, as seen here for the transcripts.

The role of Cidea in adipose tissue physiology is still controversial in the context of UCP1 activity and expression. Zhou et al. demonstrated, using Cidea-null mice, that CIDEA has a role in regulating fat metabolism in BAT by binding to UCP1 and inhibiting its uncoupling activity, in which a higher level of Ucp1 mRNA was observed in the absence of Cidea [104]. In contrast, in humans, during the browning process (or the conversion of white to brown fat), CIDEA translocates from lipid droplets to the nucleus, where it interacts with the LXRa and PPARγ receptors, removing the repression exerted by LXRa on the UCP1 enhancer, thereby promoting UCP1 transcription [105].

Our data demonstrate an increase in Cidea and Ucp1 in both genotypes exposed to 22°C, with a higher increase in Ucp1 in Trpa1−/− mice. Under cold exposure, an increase in both CIDEA and UCP1 protein levels was reported in interscapular brown adipose tissue (iBAT) [103]. In an aP2-hCidea transgenic model, which overexpresses CIDEA, elevated levels of CIDEA were described to suppress UCP1-specific activity in mitochondria without affecting UCP1 abundance. However, under cold conditions, this inhibition is compensated by enhanced BAT recruitment, resulting in increased mitochondrial mass and a greater total UCP1 content within the tissue [103]. Accordingly, we propose that the elevated Cidea expression observed in our study, under cold exposure, may promote a compensatory hyper-recruitment of BAT and, consequently, higher total UCP1 levels in the tissue, as evidenced.

It is worth noting that, as the data were collected at the beginning of the light phase (resting phase, ZT2), Ucp1 mRNA already exhibited an increase, whereas the protein was still at basal levels, as it rises during the activity phase. Importantly, the correlation between mRNA and protein abundance is not always direct and may reflect post-transcriptional, translational, and protein degradation regulatory mechanisms, resulting in divergences between RNA and protein expression profiles depending on the cell type, tissue, and biological context [[106], [107], [108]].

In addition, both genotypes showed no variation in BAT Ppargc1a, indicating that mitochondrial biogenesis was not stimulated after a mild cold challenge. Its participation may be required in response to more extreme cold or to acute exposure. In fact, an increase in this gene is observed in response to acute cold challenge, resulting in browning of white adipose tissue and induction of genes related to mitochondriogenesis and thermogenesis, including Ucp1 [109,110].

Cpt1a and Lcad encode mitochondrial enzymes that, respectively, catalyze the transfer of acyl groups from carnitine coenzyme A to long-chain fatty acids [111] and participate in the β-oxidation of unsaturated and ramified chain fatty acids [112,113]. LCAD catalyzes the initial step of mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation. Polyunsaturated fatty acids and their metabolites are known to modulate TRPV1 function [114,115], but unfortunately, the relationship between the TRPA1 channel and LCAD remains unclear.

It is clear that thermogenic activity is enhanced in WT mice subjected to 22°C, since Ucp1 and Lcad expression was increased, suggesting that β-oxidation and thermogenesis are optimized. On the other hand, genes associated with mitochondriogenesis and adipogenesis were not stimulated in WT animals at 22°C. However, components of the lipid metabolism regulatory loop (lipolysis and lipogenesis), such as Cidea, and of mitochondriogenesis, such as Ucp1 and Lcad, exhibited significant expression increases in the absence of the TRPA1 channel. Indeed, with the exception of Ppargc1a, the thermogenic response to a 22°C challenge was much more robust in the absence of the TRPA1 channel than in WT mice for all evaluated genes.

Increased body temperatures in Trpa1−/− mice during cold exposure

To partially support our hypothesis, we evaluated body surface temperatures. At thermoneutrality, no differences were observed in the infrared thermography analysis of the measured body regions. However, at 22°C, knockout animals exhibited higher interscapular (average and maximal) and back (average) temperatures than WT mice, which can be visualized in the body images (Fig. 6a) and quantified in Fig. 6b. To understand whether this increase was related to brown adipose tissue alone, BAT thermometry should be performed. Accordingly, in Trpa1−/− mice, body weight gain was lower, food consumption was higher, and the cost to maintain homeostasis (evaluated by O2 consumption) was significantly greater at 30°C, 22°C, and 10°C. Since the TRPA1 channel has been implicated in cold-induced peripheral vascular responses and blood flow regulation, acting as a primary vascular cold sensor [36], altered heat dissipation may also contribute to the phenotype observed in Trpa1−/− mice, without changes in core body temperature.

Conclusions

The absence of the TRPA1 channel resulted in a broader thermoneutral zone compared to WT mice, and prolonged mild cold exposure at 22°C led to weight loss and increased food intake in Trpa1−/− mice. Additionally, oxygen consumption rates indicated that Trpa1−/− mice were metabolically more active than WT mice. Despite the increased metabolism, core temperature remained similar between 30°C and 22°C for both genotypes. However, the ability of Trpa1−/− mice to regulate core temperature may differ from that of WT mice, whose thermoregulation is more finely tuned to manage thermal stress. This suggests that, under cold exposure, Trpa1−/− mice may rely on different mechanisms to maintain core temperature, achieving levels comparable to those of WT mice at 22°C. These mechanisms could include altered cold sensing, altered vasomotor control and heat dissipation, and altered neural regulation (not evaluated in our study) associated with BAT thermogenesis.

Analysis of temperature-associated proteomic changes within each genotype revealed distinct remodeling patterns in WT and Trpa1−/− mice after 14-day cold adaptation. In WT mice, the proteomic profile was consistent with increased glycolysis and pentose phosphate pathway activity, suggesting metabolic reprogramming associated with thermogenesis. In contrast, the proteomic profile of Trpa1−/− mice was consistent with greater reliance on fatty acid β-oxidation and was accompanied by alterations in proteins potentially associated with ROS generation, as well as reduced abundance of proteins involved in antioxidant and detoxification pathways. Gene expression analysis further supported these findings, showing increased Prdm16 expression coupled with increased Ucp1 expression in Trpa1−/− mice, correlating with their heightened thermogenic response. Although thermographic imaging did not directly measure BAT temperature, the increased interscapular temperature observed could be associated with this phenotype. Collectively, our findings support a role for TRPA1 in adaptive thermogenesis. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms inferred primarily from the differential protein abundance data, together with the associated gene expression changes, remain associative and hypothesis-generating, and their causal involvement will require targeted functional validation.

Limitations of study

Although our study addresses the role of the TRPA1 channel in thermogenesis, some considerations are necessary. We have not used littermates as controls, which might have led to differences not attributable to the absence of the TRPA1 channel, but to subtle genetic differences accumulated across breeders, in addition to different maternal and cage environments. Also, although animals of different ages were randomly distributed among the groups, the age range (3–6 months old) was wider than desired and may have been a variable to consider. Due to methodological considerations, we used whole-body knockout mice. Consequently, other pathways involved in thermoregulation (including the vascular and/or nervous systems) may be affected, resulting in effects beyond brown adipose tissue. Future studies involving tissue-specific knockout or overexpression of Trpa1 would help refine the findings presented here. Another limitation relates to the potential sex differences, since female KO mice may respond differently. Some of our results are based on a single time point during the light phase. We understand that processes such as gene and protein expression can fluctuate over 24 h; therefore, including additional time points in future assessments will make the results more robust and better reflect the role of TRPA1 on thermogenesis. Finally, to demonstrate that BAT thermogenesis is enhanced by β-oxidation and a possible involvement of ROS as a secondary consequence of the metabolic remodeling observed, further functional studies are needed to measure FAO flux and UCP1-dependent respiration.

An additional limitation relates to the statistical power of proteomic analysis. Although limited biological replication may have reduced the precision of variance estimates, the lack of proteins remaining significant after multiple-testing correction is likely to be mainly related to the overall distribution of p-values across the large number of simultaneous hypothesis tests. Specifically, the dataset showed an enrichment of small p-values relative to the null expectation, but relatively few extremely small p-values, making multiple-testing correction particularly stringent under the present experimental conditions (see Fig. S1–S3 in Supplementary Data 1 for the corresponding p-value distribution and Benjamini–Hochberg/Storey q-value diagnostics). Therefore, although the data support the presence of a biological signal, the evidence was insufficiently concentrated among the lowest-ranked p-values to satisfy conventional genome-scale significance thresholds after multiple-testing correction. Consequently, the proteomic findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Nevertheless, the proposed biological interpretation is strengthened by its consistency with the independent physiological and phenotypic findings presented in this study.
