The association between mean arterial pressure and memory decline in preeclampsia patients: a mediation analysis of serum amyloid-β 1-42 and phosphorylated tau 181
Authors: Tao Chen, Yuanyuan Wang, Kun Wang, Ping Zhu, Yilin Guo, Linfeng Yang, Wenying Nie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreeclampsia (PE) can damage the central nervous system of pregnant women, leading to a decline in cognitive function, and this effect can persist for several decades after childbirth. Amyloid-β 1-42 (Aβ1-42) had neurotoxicity and excessive deposition can damage neural synapses. Excessive production of phosphorylated tau 181 (P-tau181) can reduce the transport efficiency of axons in nerve cells, leading to degeneration of nerve cell protrusions and cell death. The aim of our study was to compare memory function status and serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 levels in PE patients and healthy controls to explore the related factors affecting memory function in PE patients and the possible pathogenic mechanism leading to the decline in memory function.MethodsOne hundred and fifteen PE patients, forty-eight nonpregnant healthy controls (NPHCs) and forty-seven pregnant healthy controls (PHCs) were included. The clinical data of each participants, included blood pressure, were collected. Neurological examinations and neuropsychological tests effectively evaluate all participants’ memory status. Among these, the Auditory Verbal Learning Test–Huashan Version (AVLT-H) is particularly useful. The levels of serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 were detected by enzyme linked immunosorbent assay. The associations between the mean arterial pressure (MAP), total AVLT-H score and serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 levels were investigated by mediation analysis. The contribution ratio of each factor affecting the total AVLT-H score was obtained by principal component analysis.ResultsThere were obvious differences in the total AVLT-H score and the levels of serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 among the PE patients and healthy controls (p < 0.05). Mediation analysis revealed that the MAP affected the total AVLT-H score in three ways. The principal component analysis showed that the main factors affecting memory function were the serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 levels and the MAP, in that order of contribution.ConclusionsThese findings highlight that the elevated blood pressure can lead to decreased memory function in PE patients through the mediating effect of serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 levels. Blood pressure monitoring and early detection of memory decline should be strictly and regularly carried out in PE patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08553-5.

Background

Preeclampsia (PE), which complicates 4%−6% of pregnancies, refers to the development of new hypertension in pregnant women after 20 weeks of gestation and is accompanied by one of the following symptoms: proteinuria or complications involving the brain, kidneys, liver, lungs, or platelets [1]. PE can lead to eclampsia, placental abruption, hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes and low platelet (HELLP) syndrome and other adverse consequences [2] and is an important cause of maternal and fetal death. The brain is a common potential organ involved in PE, and PE can lead to organic and functional brain lesions. Imaging studies suggest that central nervous system damage caused by PE is not completely reversible. Compared with those in healthy women without a history of hypertension, the volume of the temporal white matter and cortical gray matter of the brain in women who have previously had a history of PE is reduced [3], and the neural network connections in the brain are altered. This may be one of the reasons why women with a history of PE have an increased prevalence of vascular dementia, epilepsy, stroke, and cognitive dysfunction years after pregnancy. In addition, compared with healthy pregnant women, PE patients have more working memory and language learning problems in both the short and long term postpartum [4], and their offspring’s cognitive function, especially working memory, is also significantly reduced [5]. Nevertheless, there is relatively limited research on changes in cognitive function, especially memory function, in PE patients.

Hypertensive disorders are associated with poor memory and cognitive functions. Studies have shown that diminished functional connectivity in the brain, especially in the hippocampus and its related regions, including the prefrontal cortex, parahippocampal gyrus, posterior cingulate cortex, and temporal cortex, is related to hypertension history and mediates prospective memory impairment [6]. Mediation analysis showed that the differences in functional connectivity explained 11.5% of the association between hypertension history and prospective memory impairmen [6]. In PE patients, automatic cerebral blood flow regulation is weakened, while cerebrovascular resistance and blood perfusion are altered. These can damage the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and lead to vasogenic edema. Finally, secondary neurological symptoms occur, and the risk of cognitive dysfunction increases.

The pathogenic mechanism of memory impairment in PE patients is still poorly defined. Misfolded proteins can be found in the serum, urine and placenta of patients with PE [7, 8], suggesting that nervous system damage may have pathological characteristics similar to those of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and both PE and AD are protein conformational diseases characterized by impaired autophagy [9].

The key pathological hallmarks of AD are the abnormal accumulation of amyloid-β (Aβ) in extracellular amyloid plaques and the development of intracellular neurofibrillary tangles resulting from the overphosphorylation of tau [10]. Amyloid-β 1-42 (Aβ1-42) is a kind of Aβ protein, a hydrolysate of amyloid precursor protein, which exists in small amounts in cerebrospinal fluid and peripheral blood. It is neurotoxic, can damage synapses and is associated with memory decline and cognitive dysfunction [11]. Tau is the most abundant microtubule-related protein in nerve cells and primarily exists in neuronal axons. The overphosphorylation of tau, especially phosphorylated tau 181 (P-tau181), can hamper the efficiency of neuronal axon transportation and lead to synaptic degeneration and even nerve cell death, ultimately leading to cognitive impairment [12]. In addition, the misfolding of tau protein can also promote neurofibrillary tangles, causing the degeneration and apoptosis of neurons and leading to cognitive decline [13]. However, the role of serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 levels in influencing memory by blood pressure is still unclear.

The objectives of our study were to (1) evaluate memory function in PE patients during pregnancy and explore the relationship between blood pressure and memory decline; (2) explore the role of serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 in the process of memory decline; and (3) investigate whether the abovementioned serum proteins mediate the relationship between blood pressure and memory decline.

Materials and methods

Participants and inclusion criteria

From June 2022 to October 2023, 115 PE patients, 48 nonpregnant healthy controls (NPHCs) and 47 pregnant healthy controls (PHCs) were enrolled in this study. This was a retrospective study, that was approved by the ethics review board before initiation of the study. All participants fully understood the detailed procedures of the experimental process, and signed the written informed consent form. They were recruited from Jinan Maternity and Child Care Hospital affiliated with Shandong First Medical University, which is located in Jinan City, Shandong Province, China. All PE patients were from the outpatient clinics and inpatient wards of the obstetrics department and had to be clinically stable to complete the data collection and neuropsychological examination. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) PE patients who were strictly screened according to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists diagnostic criteria [14]. PE is defined as new-onset hypertension (defined as systolic blood pressure of 140 mmHg or more or diastolic blood pressure of 90 mmHg or more on two occasions at least 4 h apart) after 20 weeks of gestation and proteinuria (300 mg or more per 24 h urine collection) and/or evidence of end-organ compromise, such as thrombocytopenia, renal insufficiency, impaired liver function, pulmonary edema, or central nervous system symptoms (such as new-onset headache unresponsive to medication or blurred vision); (2) had no confirmed history of eclampsia throughout the entire pregnancy; (3) patients with a gestational age ranging from 20 weeks to 41+ 6 weeks, with pregnancies involving a single fetus, and a matenal age of 22–45 years; (4) patients with complete data from clinical examination records; and (5) patients who had no personal history or family history of hypertension before pregnancy. The exclusion criteria were (1) pregnancy-related diseases other than PE, such as diabetes (including gestational diabetes mellitus) or existing kidney diseases; (2) smoking, drinking, long-term drug use or other conditions during pregnancy; (3) obvious anxiety, depression or intellectual disability; and (4) combined malignant tumors, immune system diseases, infectious diseases or known neurological diseases such as cerebrovascular diseases, dementia and epilepsy. PHCs voluntarily enrolled in this project through poster information in the prenatal clinic. PHCs were matched for PE in terms of basic information, such as age and gestational age. PHCs who developed PE or had a prior history of chronic hypertension, who delivered prematurely (< 37 weeks), or who gave birth to a baby whose birth weight was less than normal were excluded. NPHCs were recruited from the physical examination center in the form of posters for age-matched healthy women (aged from 20 to 45). All healthy controls had no history of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy or chronic hypertension. None of the participants received antihypertensive drugs or magnesium sulfate treatment at the time of enrollment, and they also did not undergo epidural anesthesia. The detailed recruitment process of the participants was shown in Additional file 1.

Data collection

Clinical data such as age, gestational age, body mass index (BMI), weight gain during pregnancy, blood pressure and the years of education were collected from all participants and recorded. Before measuring blood pressure, the participants need to rest for 10-minute or longer and maintain an upright position. The mean arterial pressure (MAP) could be calculated using the formula: MAP = diastolic blood pressure + [(systolic blood pressure - diastolic blood pressure)/3]. Neurological examination and neuropsychological tests were conducted by a professional neurologist within 12 h after the enrollment of all participants. Blood and urine samples were collected by professional medical technicians. For each participant, 2 ml of blood was collected in an EDTA-K2 tube, 3 ml of blood was collected and allowed to coagulate, and the serum was separated within 30 min.

Laboratory testing

All participants underwent routine blood tests and liver and kidney function, lipid, blood glucose, glycated hemoglobin, urinary microalbumin/creatinine ratio (ACR), and 24-hour total urinary protein measurements to confirm the diagnosis of PE and exclude other diseases. An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay was utilized to detect the serum Aβ1-42, total tau (T-tau) and P-tau181 levels (Wuhan Saipei Biotechnology Co.,Ltd. Wuhan, China, Lot: SP11457, SP11917, SP32631), which used double antibody sandwich method. All operations strictly followed the operating instructions of the kits. Added the sample to the well three times, ensuring each addition is done separately. The sample was incubated for 30 min at 37℃, after washing, the enzyme-conjugated detection antibody was incubated for 30 min at 37℃. After adding tetramethylbenzidine for colorimetric development, the reaction was terminated, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. The protein concentration of the experimental samples was the average of three repeated measurements.

Neurological examination and neuropsychological testing

The gestational weeks of PE patients were recorded during neurological examinations and neuropsychological tests. PHC participants with matching gestational weeks were then selected to undergo the same examinations and tests. All participants underwent neurological examinations and the Auditory Verbal Learning Test-Huashan version (AVLT-H), which assesses memory. The AVLT-H, adapted by Chinese scholars based on the California Verbal Learning Test and the Hong Kong Auditory Word Learning Test, was designed to evaluate memory function in Chinese mainland residents. The AVLT-H test was conducted in a quiet and undisturbed environment by the same physician who had been trained in neuropsychological scales. The assessment was mainly based on the number of correct memories and the types of errors made by the subjects during the learning stage and the delayed recall stage.

The AVLT-H test consisted of 12 common Chinese words that were read to the participants on three consecutive trials (AVLT-H-1 to AVLT-H-3, immediate memory); participants were asked to remember as many words as possible. After a 5-minute interval, participants were required to recall the above 12 words for the fourth time (AVLT-H-4), which was used to measure short-term delayed recall. Twenty minutes later, participants were again asked to recall the words list for the fifth time (AVLT-H-5), which was used to measure long-term delayed recall. Then, the participants were given specific clues designed to facilitate the sixth recall (AVLT-H-6), referred to as the clue recall. Finally, they were asked to identify the 24 words from the list, including 12 target words and 12 words that were similar in category and served as distractors, to determine if they had learned the words and to record the number of target words for which they made the correct judgment (AVLT-H-7, recognition). The number of correctly recalled words in each session was recorded, with each correct word scoring 1 point. The maximum score for each session was 12 points. The total AVLT-H is the sum of the scores of the seven items listed above. The AVLT-H scale for assessing memory function in pregnant women was verified by statistician for its internal consistency and construct validity.

Statistical analysis

SPSS (version 26.0 for Windows), Origin 2021, GraphPad Prism 8, and MedCalc software were used for statistical analysis. First, descriptive analyses of the three groups were performed. The quantitative clinical data and the results of laboratory tests and neuropsychological tests were analyzed by the Kruskal‒Wallis test, two independent samples t tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The relationships between variables in this study were assessed by Pearson bivariate correlation analysis. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to determine the factors that influenced the total AVLT-H score in women of childbearing age. The causal relationships among serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 levels, MAP, and the total AVLT-H score were investigated using mediation analysis. The ratios of the contributions of the factors affecting the AVLT-H in PE patients were obtained via principal component analysis. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to determine the area under the curve (AUC), which was used to evaluate the ability of the MAP, and serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 levels to predict memory decline. The changes in AUC were tested by the DeLong test utilizing MedCalc software.

Laboratory characteristics and neuropsychological test results

The results of laboratory and neuropsychological tests in the three groups are summarized in Table 1. As expected, significant differences were found in the serum P-tau181 and Aβ1-42 levels among the three groups (F = 6.353, p = 0.002; F = 60.747, p < 0.001). The AVLT-H 1–7 and total AVLT-H scores were significantly different among the three groups (F = 24.663, p < 0.001; F = 24.987, p < 0.001; F = 32.130, p < 0.001; F = 45.702, p < 0.001; H(K) = 54.844, p < 0.001; F = 5.863, p = 0.003; F = 16.266, p < 0.001; F = 37.603, p < 0.001).

Pearson bivariate correlation analysis

The results of the Pearson bivariate correlation analysis of the MAP, serum Aβ1-42 levels and total AVLT-H score are shown in Table 2. Several factors were significantly correlated with the MAP, including the total AVLT-H score and serum P-tau181 and Aβ1-42 levels (r=−0.404, p < 0.001; r = 0.181, p = 0.009; r = 0.351, p < 0.001), and the differences were significant. The factors that were associated with the total AVLT-H score were the serum P-tau181 and Aβ1-42 levels and the years of education (r=−0.165, p = 0.017; r=−0.260, p < 0.001; r = 0.523, p < 0.001). The factors significantly associated with serum Aβ1-42 levels were serum T-tau and P-tau181 levels and the years of education (r = 0.724, p < 0.001; r = 0.855, p < 0.001; r=−0.159, p = 0.021). The correlation between the above factors was visualized by scatter linear correlation plots, as shown in Fig. 1.

Associations between the MAP and total AVLT-H score by serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 levels

Mediation analysis was used to explore the effects of serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 levels and the MAP on the AVLT-H score (Fig. 2A). This study revealed three kinds of mediating effects among these factors: (1) the MAP can affect the total AVLT-H score through direct effects (SE = 0.0365, t=−6.361, p < 0.001) and indirect effects (SE = 0.0220, t=−5.297, p < 0.001), and the serum Aβ1-42 levels mediate the indirect effect; (2) the MAP can affect the serum P-tau181 levels through direct effects (SE = 0.1052, t = 2.654, p = 0.009) and indirect effects (SE = 0.0374, t=−3.643, p < 0.001), and the serum Aβ1-42 levels mediate the indirect effect; and (3) the MAP can affect the serum Aβ1-42 levels through direct effects (SE = 0.0710, t = 5.4074, p < 0.001) and indirect effects (SE = 0.1255, t = 6.001, p < 0.001), and the serum P-tau181 levels mediate the indirect effect (Supplementary Material). Combined analysis (2) and (3) revealed that serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 levels had a synergistic effect on the mediating effect of MAP on the total AVLT-H score, and each factor promoted elevated serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181. Notably, the serum P-tau181 level cannot be directly involved in the effect on the total AVLT-H score. Therefore, multiple factors are involved in the effect of the MAP on the total AVLT-H score, and an increase in the serum Aβ1-42 level is the central link in many of the above models. Receiver operating characteristic curve of the total AVLT-H score

The evaluation criteria for memory level in this study were as follows: The average value of the total AVLT-H score in the NPHC and PHC groups was used as the basis for judgment. If the score was equal to or higher than this value, it indicated a normal memory level; otherwise, it indicated memory decline (average total AVLT-H score = 52.09). The ROC curve was used to assess the diagnostic capability of the MAP, serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 levels, and the combination of the MAP and serum Aβ1-42 level in terms of memory decline in women of childbearing age (AUC = 0.719, 0.680, 0.594, 0.732). The DeLong test showed that there were no significant differences among the AUC values using the serum Aβ1-42 levels, or a combination of serum Aβ1-42 levels and MAP using logistic regression, which indicates their similar abilities to discriminate women of reproductive age with and without hypomnesis (p > 0.05). In other words, the combination of the MAP and serum Aβ1-42 level is the preferred method for predicting memory decline in PE patients, which is more steady than the MAP and more accurate than the serum Aβ1-42 levels alone.

Discussion

This study is the first to report the mediating role of serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 levels in the relationship between an elevated MAP and memory decline in PE patients. Previous studies have confirmed that PE involves the central nervous system and can lead to acute and chronic cognitive decline. For example, acute cognitive impairment can occur with eclampsia or pulmonary edema [15], and women with a history of PE are at significantly increased risk of memory decline and cognitive impairment months to decades after delivery. However, few articles have reported on changes in memory function in PE patients. Our study revealed that the total AVLT-H score of the PE group was lower than that of the control groups, and PE patients experienced some degree of memory impairment during pregnancy. Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 are closely related to memory impairment, and Aβ1-42 is often injected to establish animal models of memory impairment. We also measured the participants’ serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 levels and found that the serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 levels of the PE group were significantly greater than those of the control groups. We speculate that Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 may be important causes of the decline in cognitive and memory functions in PE patients and subsequent symptoms in the central nervous system.

PE and AD are both defined as proteinopathies characterized by abnormal protein aggregation and impaired autophagy function. In particular, both diseases involve the aggregation of pathological proteins such as Aβ1-42, P-tau, α-synuclein, and transthyretin (TTR). Defects in autophagy impair the clearance of these proteins, thereby promoting neurotoxicity and vascular damage. Misfolded toxic proteins which are found in the brains of AD patients, have also been detected in the placental tissue of patients with PE [8]. There was a significant relationship between Aβ1-42 and memory function. It has been found that misfolding and aggregation of the Aβ protein occur in PE patients and cause damage to the nervous system through mechanisms such as peroxidative damage to neurons, neuroinflammation, induction of nerve cell apoptosis, and synaptic dysfunction, leading to a decline in early learning and memory [16, 17]. Learning and memory may depend on the activation of the cAMP/MAPK/CREB transcriptional pathway in the hippocampus [18], and the Aβ protein can induce the phosphorylation of p38 MAPK to increase reactive oxygen species, resulting in neuronal cell death and a decline in learning and memory function. A recent study in a mouse model treated with Aβ1-42 showed that Aβ1-42 induced significant learning and memory impairment, which was associated with hyperactivity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis [19]. All studies described above suggest that the excessive deposition of Aβ proteins, especially Aβ1-42, in the brain can lead to a decline in memory function. The expression and modification of tau protein affect the function of neurons. Hyperphosphorylated tau protein destabilizes microtubules, disorders neurocellular transmitter transmission, and ultimately leads to learning and memory dysfunction. Through Pearson correlation analysis and multifactor linear regression analysis, we found that memory function was closely related to the MAP and serum Aβ1-42 levels.

This study revealed that increased MAP in PE patients is an important factor leading to memory decline during pregnancy. In recent decades, it has been found that arterial pressure is negatively correlated with memory function. The impairment of arterial vessels in hypertensive patients leads to a decrease in vascular compliance, the microcirculation of small vessels and cerebral blood flow. Both mouse and human studies have shown that continuously high cerebral artery pulsation can cause a decrease in the volume of the hippocampus, which can lead to a decrease in related functions [20, 21]. The hippocampus is a bilateral structure located deep in the human temporal lobe and is a core region that controls memory and cognitive functions. In an experimental PE rat model, the hippocampus arterioles (HAs) were remodeled by inward malnutrition and capillary sparseness [22], and vasodilation was reduced, while the blood oxygen and glucose supplies were insufficient, which affected the function of nerve cells and led to a decline in memory function. Increased MAP also disrupts the BBB and brain microenvironment by affecting the neurovascular unit (NVU), which was proposed by the National Institutes of Health in 2001. The NVU emphasizes the symbiotic relationship between brain cells and cerebral blood vessels. The two key functions of the NVU are maintaining neurovascular coupling and maintaining the BBB [23]. In PE animal models, HAs impair endothelial cells and vascular smooth muscle function, resulting in neuronal dysfunction [24], which subsequently leads to decreased memory function. The BBB is maintained and regulated by pericytes in the NVU. An increase in arterial pulsatility damages pericytes and disrupts the BBB, leading to neurovascular decoupling and failure to maintain normal neural activity, resulting in a decline in memory function [25]. Studies have shown that the BBB is damaged in women with PE [26], which may be one of the reasons for memory decline in these women.

In addition, increased arterial pressure can cause protein deposition by affecting the metabolism of Aβ and P-tau181. Increased arterial pulsatility damages the NVU, leading to neurovascular decoupling, and disrupts important brain metabolite clearance pathways (e.g., vascular pathways and cerebrospinal fluid flow pathways), leading to the accumulation of potentially toxic byproducts (e.g., Aβ and tau proteins) generated by brain metabolism. Chronic hypoxia caused by increased arterial pressure can increase the production of Aβ protein by enhancing the activity of β- and γ-secretase and activating the autophagy pathway [27]. These findings led us to propose that Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 have a mediating effect on the association between the MAP and memory function. Strikingly, through mediation analysis, we found that Aβ1-42 significantly regulated this process, while P-tau181 acted on Aβ1-42 to mediate the effect of MAP on memory. There was a synergistic interaction between Aβ1-42 and P-tau181. It has been previously confirmed that the presence of tau protein can promote the aggregation and deposition of Aβ in individuals with AD [28], while Aβ1-42 can induce tau protein phosphorylation, aggregation and cleavage to accelerate the pathological effect of tau protein [10]. In fact, both the direct mediating effect of Aβ1-42 and the indirect mediating effect of P-tau181 provide clues to the potential mechanisms underlying the relationship between elevated arterial pressure and memory decline in PE patients.

Principal component analysis revealed the contribution ratio of various factors affecting memory decline in PE patients, and the order from highest to lowest was the serum Aβ1-42 level, serum P-tau181 level, MAP and the years of education. Finally, we evaluated the diagnostic ability of the MAP, Aβ1-42 level and P-tau181 level for memory decline in PE patients by ROC analysis. This analysis revealed that the MAP and Aβ1-42 levels were greater than the P-tau181 level. Therefore, we believe that the increase in MAP and serum Aβ1-42 levels in PE patients is associated with a decrease in memory function and can be used to evaluate memory function in PE patients. Higher MAP and Aβ1-42 levels may be simple indicators for evaluating memory function in PE patients in clinical practice. The MAP can be greatly influenced by the site, environment, exercise, measurement instrument and measurement method, while serum Aβ1-42 measurements are relatively accurate and efficient. In the research of neurodegenerative diseases, Aβ, tau and P-tau can be measured by positron-emission tomography (PET) or cerebrospinal fluid. These methods are invasive, expensive, and require high levels of radiation, making them unsuitable for use in research involving pregnant women [29, 30]. We reviewed the literature and found that the concentrations of serum Aβ1-42, T-tau, and P-tau181 proteins were all strongly correlated with the concentrations in cerebrospinal fluid [31, 32]. Therefore, we believe that the serum Aβ1-42 concentration can be used as a serum biomarker to evaluate the decline in memory function in PE patients.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to analyze the associations between serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181 levels and memory function in PE patients. More importantly, we are the first to report a mediating role of serum Aβ1-42 levels in the association between MAP and memory function. However, due to certain unavoidable limitations of this study, the results of this study should be interpreted with caution. First, the sample size was not large enough, and prospective studies with more participants are needed in the future. Moreover, another limitation was that the memory function test is relatively simple. In future studies, the memory function of the enrolled participants needs to be evaluated in more detail and comprehensively. We will commit to long-term follow-up studies on memory function combined with brain functional imaging characteristics and the measurement of more relevant serum protein biomarkers in PE patients in the future. Finally, while our study has focused on mechanisms within the central nervous system, it is also pertinent to consider the potential contribution of the peripheral nervous system, particularly sensory neurons, to the pathophysiology of preeclampsia. We think it is a fascinating and emerging area of research. This research could provide a new research perspective and approach for further exploring the physiology and etiology of PE.

Conclusion

In our study, the MAP and serum Aβ1-42 level were strongly correlated with the total AVLT-H score. We speculated that memory loss during pregnancy in PE patients was related to an increase in the MAP and the accumulation of Aβ1-42 in the body. In addition, mediation analysis showed that the MAP could affect the levels of serum Aβ1-42 and P-tau181, thereby reducing memory function in PE patients. Therefore, patients with PE should undergo strict and standardized blood pressure monitoring and early detection of memory decline. In addition, serum Aβ1-42 levels can be used as a noninvasive, accurate and efficient serum biomarker to evaluate the decline in memory function in PE patients.
