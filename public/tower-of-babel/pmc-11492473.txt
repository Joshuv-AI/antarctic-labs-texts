Comprehensive multi-omics integration uncovers mitochondrial gene signatures for prognosis and personalized therapy in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Wenjia Zhang, Lei Zhao, Tiansheng Zheng, Lihong Fan, Kai Wang, Guoshu Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe therapeutic efficacy of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), the most prevalent histological subtype of primary lung cancer, remains inadequate, with accurate prognostic assessment posing significant challenges. This study sought to elucidate the prognostic significance of mitochondrial-related genes in LUAD through an integrative multi-omics approach, aimed at developing personalized therapeutic strategies. Utilizing transcriptomic and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data, alongside clinical information from publicly available databases, we first applied dimensionality reduction and clustering techniques to the LUAD single-cell dataset, focusing on the subclassification of fibroblasts, epithelial cells, and T cells. Mitochondrial-related prognostic genes were subsequently identified using TCGA-LUAD data, and LUAD cases were stratified into distinct molecular subtypes through consensus clustering, allowing for the exploration of gene expression profiles and clinical feature distributions across subtypes. By leveraging an ensemble of machine learning algorithms, we developed an Artificial Intelligence-Derived Prognostic Signature (AIDPS) model based on mitochondrial-related genes and validated its prognostic accuracy across multiple independent datasets. The AIDPS model demonstrated robust predictive power for LUAD patient outcomes, revealing significant differences in responses to immunotherapy and chemotherapy, as well as survival outcomes between risk groups. Furthermore, we conducted comprehensive analyses of tumor mutation burden (TMB), immune microenvironment characteristics, and genome-wide association study (GWAS) data, providing additional insights into the mechanistic roles of mitochondrial-related genes in LUAD pathogenesis. This study not only offers a novel approach to improving prognostic assessments in LUAD but also establishes a strong foundation for the development of personalized therapeutic interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at10.1186/s12967-024-05754-y.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma, Mitochondrial-related genes, Machine learning, Prognostic model, Single nucleotide variation

Introduction

Lung cancer (LC) ranks among the deadliest diseases worldwide, with approximately 1.8 million LC-related deaths in 2020, accounting for about 18% of all cancer mortality [1]. Smoking and exposure to environmental pollutants are the primary etiological factors for lung cancer. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), the most prevalent histological subtype of primary lung cancer, is classified under the non-small cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC) category and accounts for approximately 40% of all lung cancer cases, a proportion that continues to rise. LUAD originates from type II alveolar epithelial cells in the small airways, which are responsible for secreting substances such as mucus. The prognosis of LUAD is heavily influenced by its histological subtype, with adenocarcinomas featuring a solid component that invades alveolar spaces or lymphovascular structures being associated with a significantly poorer prognosis [2]. Over the past few decades, significant advancements in research and development have yielded a range of therapeutic modalities, including radiotherapy, immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and minimally invasive surgical techniques. These multimodal approaches have shown progress in the management of LUAD. However, despite these advancements, the 5-year survival rate for LUAD patients remains dismally low, with treatment outcomes often falling short of expectations [3]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to further explore the molecular mechanisms underlying LUAD, identify reliable biomarkers, and discover novel therapeutic targets. Such insights will be crucial for improving patient prognosis and developing more effective, personalized treatment strategies.

Mitochondria, essential and dynamic organelles responsible for regulating differentiation and survival in eukaryotic cells, harbor their own distinct genome, known as mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). Due to its circular structure and the absence of chromosomal end protection, mtDNA is particularly vulnerable to oxidative damage and mutations. These mitochondrial alterations not only disrupt cellular metabolism but also contribute to the initiation and progression of various diseases by influencing epigenetic modifications and modulating cellular signal transduction pathways [4]. Examples include cancer [5] with instances in breast cancer [6], adult acute myeloid leukemia [7], neurodegenerative diseases [8], and diabetes [9]. Cancer cells exhibit high rates of glycolysis and lactate production in the presence of oxidative phosphorylation [10]. Given that both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation are intricately linked to mitochondrial biochemistry, mitochondrial dysfunction is likely to play a pivotal role in cancer progression. A hallmark of mitochondrial dysfunction is oxidative damage, which can result in intracellular metabolic imbalances, aberrant macromolecule synthesis, and uncontrolled tumor growth. Despite its significance, the regulatory mechanisms governing the expression of mitochondria-associated cancer genes remain underexplored. Thus, investigating the roles and underlying mechanisms of mitochondria-related genes in LUAD could provide critical insights into novel therapeutic targets and prognostic markers, ultimately improving treatment outcomes for patients.

With the advancements in relevant technologies, machine learning algorithms have increasingly integrated into the in-depth analysis of biological data, revolutionizing our approach to unraveling the intricate mechanisms underlying diseases [11]. This synergy stems from the substantial improvement in computational power and the refinement of algorithms tailored specifically for biological data [12]. Moreover, the proliferation of public biological databases has drastically democratized access to diverse datasets, enabling researchers to acquire and analyze samples from various conditions effortlessly. The vast array of data, encompassing transcriptomics, proteomics, and single-cell sequencing profiles, contributes significantly to capturing the multifaceted nature of diseases like lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) [13]. Integrating multiple machine learning algorithms within an ensemble framework offers a powerful approach to capitalize on the strengths of individual models while minimizing their respective limitations. A more robust and generalizable prognostic model can be constructed by combining diverse algorithms, each tailored to specific data analysis aspects. This ensemble methodology mitigates the risk of methodological bias, resulting in more comprehensive and balanced insights. Additionally, leveraging a broad array of relevant datasets enhances the reliability of the findings, as a larger sample size increases the representativeness and generalizability of the results, thereby improving their clinical relevance and translational potential.

This study employs bioinformatics approaches to investigate the roles and mechanisms of mitochondria-related genes in LUAD, with the aim of constructing a prognostic prediction model. Transcriptomic and single-cell datasets were sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), and subsequently underwent preprocessing. Single-cell data were subjected to dimensionality reduction, clustering analysis, and cell type annotation, followed by a comparative assessment of cell composition and distribution between tumor and normal samples. Epithelial cells, T cells, and fibroblasts were then isolated for further subclassification. For epithelial cells, we conducted dimensionality reduction, trajectory analysis, transcriptional activity analysis, Copy Number Variation (CNV) analysis, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). For T cells and fibroblasts, clustering and transcriptional activity analyses were performed. Mitochondria-related prognostic genes were identified using univariate Cox regression analysis, and a consensus clustering algorithm was applied to stratify TCGA-LUAD into distinct molecular subtypes based on tumor characteristics. We then explored the prognostic significance, gene expression profiles, and distribution of clinical features across these subtypes. A machine learning-based ensemble method was utilized to develop a mitochondrial gene-based prognostic risk model, termed the Artificial Intelligence-Derived Prognostic Signature (AIDPS). The model’s predictive performance was validated across multiple datasets, demonstrating its stability and robustness. Patients were classified into high- and low-risk groups according to AIDPS scores, and differences in immunotherapy and chemotherapy responses, as well as survival outcomes, were analyzed between the risk groups. Additionally, Single Nucleotide Variation (SNV) analysis and immune microenvironment profiling were performed on TCGA-LUAD data. Finally, gene co-localization and Mendelian Randomization (MR) analyses were conducted using Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) data related to LUAD.

Materials and methods

Data downloading and preprocessing

The research process of this study was showed in Fig. 1. Obtain the LUAD (lung adenocarcinoma) entire transcriptome dataset al.ong with clinical information from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov), comprising 513 samples. Transcriptome data and single-cell datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). The transcriptome datasets included GSE81089 (n = 107), GSE13213 (n = 117), GSE14814 (n = 71), GSE26939 (n = 115), GSE31210 (n = 226), GSE30219 (n = 85), GSE37745 (n = 106), GSE50081 (n = 127), GSE42127 (n = 133), GSE72094 (n = 398), and GSE68465 (n = 442) LUAD microarray data. The single-cell datasets comprised GSE131907 [14] (11 adjacent non-tumor samples, 11 tumor samples, and 4 Stage IV tumor samples) and GSE127465 (18 tumor samples), totaling 44 samples. Immunotherapy data (IMvigor210) were obtained using the R package “IMvigor210CoreBiologies”. Additionally, 2030 mitochondrial-related genes were collected from relevant literature. Preprocessing of the transcriptome data involved the following steps: firstly, all data from GSE81089 were converted to transcripts per million (TPM) format and log2 transformed for subsequent analysis [15]. Subsequently, the “normalizeBetweenArrays” function from the “limma” package was used to correct the chip data. The data of single-cell RNA-seq were processed using “Seurat” package for cytoplasmic quality control and analysis. Cytoplasmic quality control criteria included mitochondrial content less than 20%, with restrictions on Unique Molecular Identifier (UMI) counts and gene counts within the ranges of (200-30000) and (200–6000), respectively. Next, data normalization, selection of highly variable genes (2000), and data normalization to eliminate cell cycle effects were performed using the “NormalizeData”, “FindVariableFeatures”, and “ScaleData” functions, respectively, from the “Seurat” package. We used the “harmony” function to operate batch effect correction.

The workflow of this study

Single-cell sequencing analysis

In this study, we utilized the Seurat R package to perform clustering, dimensionality reduction, and subclassification analyses on single-cell data from the GSE131907 dataset [16]. The primary objective was to visualize cell clusters and their distribution using t-SNE plots. We applied the ‘FindAllMarkers’ function to identify differentially expressed genes across clusters, which were subsequently visualized as heatmaps and used as marker genes for cell type annotation. The accuracy of cell annotation for each cell type was validated by generating additional heatmaps for marker gene expression. We then compared the composition and distribution of various cell types between tumor and adjacent normal samples, visualizing these differences with bar charts and box plots. Epithelial cells, T cells, and fibroblasts were further isolated for dimensionality reduction and clustering, following the standard Seurat workflow. Subclassifications within these cell types were distinguished based on specific marker genes, and the results were visualized using t-SNE plots. A bar chart was used to represent the composition of epithelial cell clusters, and a heatmap was generated to visualize the differentially expressed marker genes in epithelial cell clusters. Pseudotime analysis on epithelial cell subgroups was conducted using the Monocle2 package, yielding trajectory plots for different cell states, subgroups, and differentiation paths, which were visualized using the DDRTree method. Genes exhibiting expression changes along the pseudotime trajectory were identified and visualized in heatmaps. To further characterize malignant cells within epithelial subgroups, we performed copy number variation (CNV) analysis using InferCNV software, with normal epithelial cells serving as a reference. CNV scores were calculated for each subgroup and state and compared using box plots. Pearson correlation analysis was performed between CNV scores and pseudotime, with the results visualized in scatter plots.Additionally, we compared the scores of cytotoxic, resident, costimulatory, and exhausted T cells across different sample sources using box plots. The scores were derived from the average expression levels of specific signatures, including resident markers (NR4A3, CXCR6, CD69, NR4A1, and RUNX3), cytotoxic markers (IFNG, PRF1, NKG7, GNLY, GZMA, GZMB, CST7, and TNFSF10), exhaustion markers (CTLA4, LAG3, PDCD1/PD-1, TIGIT, and HAVCR2/TIM-3), and costimulatory molecules (TNFRSF14, TNFRSF25, ICOS, CD226, CD28, and TNFRSF9).

Transcriptional activity analysis of fibroblasts, T cells, and epithelial cells

The “decoupleR” software was utilized for analysis of transcription factor (TF) across cell subgroups, employing Dorothea. Scores of activity were computed at the cellular level, describing the enrichment of each cell in TFs and their downstream targets (regulons).

GSEA and functional annotation

To assess the functional characteristics of epithelial cell subpopulations and tumor subtypes, the “clusterProfiler” package were employed to perform GSEA on differentially upregulated genes in epithelial cell subpopulation and tumor subtype. Enrichment was conducted using the Hallmark gene set of Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) 3.0 [17], with enrichment visualization achieved using the “enrichplot” package when Benjamini & Hochberg (BH) adjusted p-values were < 0.05.

Consensus clustering-related analysis

Analysis of single-univariate Cox regression was employed on collected mitochondrial-related genes, and genes with prognostic significance (p < 0.01) were selected, with results visualized via forest plots. Subsequently, the “ConsensusClusterPlus” package was applied in the TCGA-LUAD cohort using a consensus clustering algorithm, assuming a cluster number of k, and a consensus matrix heatmap was generated at the optimal k value [18]. The optimal k value was determined comprehensively through consensus score matrices and the cumulative distribution function (CDF) curve. Specifically, the optimal k value needed to meet two criteria: firstly, the consensus matrix heatmap exhibited characteristics of “high cohesion and low coupling”; secondly, the slope of the CDF curve was minimized, indicating that the CDF approached its maximum value. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted for different tumor subtypes, compressing data dimensions by projecting all data onto a direction vector while minimizing the projected error [19]. TCGA-LUAD cohort was also conducted the survival analysis, with survival curves plotted to demonstrate prognostic differences among subtypes. For prognostic genes identified through single-factor Cox regression analysis, a gene expression heatmap was generated to analyze and compare their differential expression among tumor subtypes. Additionally, the results of consensus clustering analysis were combined with some clinical indicators and subjected to chi-square tests, with the composition proportions of clinical indicators in different tumor subtypes displayed via bar charts.

Risk model constructed based on machine learning ensemble methods

To develop a prognostic model with high precision and stability performance, we integrated 10 machine learning algorithms and their 101 combinations to construct the AIDPS model. The ensemble algorithms comprised Elastic Net (Enet), Random Survival Forest (RSF), Ridge, Stepwise Cox, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), CoxBoost, Supervised Principal Component (SuperPC), Generalized Boosted Regression Model (GBM), Survival Support Vector Machine (survival-SVM), and Cox Partial Least Squares Regression (plsRcox) [20]. The differentially expressed Mitochondrial-related Genes (DEMRGs) between C1 and C2 were computed using the “DESeq2” package. Single-factor Cox analysis was performed on DEMRGs across 12 datasets including TCGA-LUAD to identify prognostic genes, retaining those with prognostic significance in at least 8 datasets. Subsequently, 101 algorithm combinations were applied to the prognostic genes to fit predictive models based on the Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) framework within the TCGA-LUAD cohort. All models were applied to 11 validation datasets (GSE14814, GSE13213, GSE31210, GSE37745, GSE26939, GSE30219, GSE42127, GSE50081, GSE72094, GSE68465, and GSE81089), and the Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) was calculated for each model. The model with the highest average C-index was deemed optimal. Using the optimal model, we computed risk scores for all samples in the 12 datasets and the meta-cohort of merged samples. The TCGA-LUAD cohort utilized median values, while cutoff values for the remaining cohorts were acquired based on “surv_cutpoint” function. Subsequently, thirteen cohorts were divided into high- and low-risk categories using these threshold values. Subsequently, survival analyses were conducted for each cohort, and survival curves were generated to visualize prognostic differences between different risk groups.

Evaluation of the AIDPS model

To validate the predictive performance and robustness of AIDPS, analysis of Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) was applied to 12 datasets and the merged samples’ meta-cohort, visualizing the values of area under the curve (AUC) corresponding to three time points(1-year, 3-year, and 5-year) through bar plots. For the TCGA-LUAD cohort and 11 validation sets, C-index values were calculated for AIDPS and several clinical indicators, and error bar plots were generated to visually compare different C-index values. To assess how AIDPS fares in comparison to other prognostic models, we comprehensive searched literatures which conducted for prognostic models. Prognostic models based on miRNA were excluded due to a lack of miRNA information in validation datasets. Ultimately, 52 prognostic models (including mRNA and lncRNA prognostic models) were collected. For each prognostic model, single-factor Cox regression analysis was performed across 12 datasets including the TCGA training set, and C-index was calculated. Prognostic relevance was visualized through a heatmap, and the C-index for each model was compared visually using error bar plots.

Analysis of predicted response to chemotherapy and immunotherapy

To assess disparities in chemotherapy response across distinct groups within the TCGA-LUAD cohort, we calculated the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of several traditional chemotherapy drugs based on “oncoPredict” package [21]. Additionally, Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) analysis was utilized for online prediction of immune response and TIDE score for TCGA-LUAD. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were conducted to ensure statistical significance of differences, and results were visualized using violin plots. Based on chemotherapy response (response [R] or non-response [NR]), the high-risk group was divided into High-NR and High-R subgroups, while the low-risk group was divided into Low-NR and Low-R subgroups. Survival analysis results comparing the prognosis differences of the four subgroups were generated by plotting ensemble survival curves, and the composition of chemotherapy response in different groups was visualized using bar plots. Subsequently, we analyzed the immune therapy response in both risk groups using the IMvigor210 cohort. Similarly, AIDPS was applied to the IMvigor210 dataset, and patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score. Survival analysis and immune response composition analysis were then conducted for both risk groups, with corresponding survival curves and bar plots generated. Finally, correlation analysis between risk scores and immune checkpoint gene expression levels was carried out relying on “ggcorrplot” package, and a correlation heatmap was plotted.

Tumor SNV and immune microenvironment analysis

We retrieved SNV data from the TCGA database and conducted a detailed analysis of SNV mutation types, gene mutation rates, and mutation differences among different groups. The findings were presented through waterfall plots. Subsequently, the “maftools” package was employed to calculate Tumor Mutation Burden (TMB), Homologous Recombination Defects (HRD), and Mutant-Allele Tumor Heterogeneity (MATH) values for different groups. Differential analysis was conducted using the Wilcoxon test, considering differences as significant when p-value < 0.05. For the immune cell infiltration in the LUAD immune microenvironment, the CIBERSORT algorithm was utilized to obtain the proportions of 22 immune cell types [22], visualized through box plots. Additionally, using the ESTIMATE algorithm, ESTIMATE Score, Stromal Score, and Immune Score were calculated for both groups, and differences were visualized using violin plots.

GWAS data analysis and MR analysis for LC

The GWAS data for LC were sourced from the Genome-wide Complex Trait Analysis (GCTA) website, and the Manhattan plot for GWAS was created using the “CMplot” package. Simultaneously, the SMR software was employed, using the European population’s thousand-genome data as a reference. Gene co-localization analysis was conducted based on cis-eQTL data from the eQTLGen website (https://eqtlgen.org/cis-eqtls.html) and LC-GWAS data. Subsequently, the “TwoSampleMR” package in R was utilized for MR analysis, focusing on exploring the correlation between genes associated with the prognostic model and Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNP) loci, as well as the association between Interstitial_lung_disease (ebi-a-GCST90018643) and LC. The results were visualized through scatter plots, forest plots, and funnel plots.

Statistical analysis

Data processing, statistical analyses, and visualization were executed utilizing R software (version 4.1.3). Pearson correlation coefficient was used to assess the correlation between two continuous variables. Chi-square tests were employed for comparing categorical variables, while Wilcoxon rank-sum tests or T-tests were used for comparing continuous variables. The “survminer” package was utilized to determine optimal cutoff values. Cox regression and Kaplan-Meier analyses were conducted using the “survival” package. The “CompareC” package was employed to compare C-index values for different variables. Figures were generated using the “ggplot2” package, and a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant (* p < 0.05; ** p < 0.01; *** p < 0.001; **** p < 0.0001).

Results

LUAD single-cell expression atlas

Following dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis of the single-cell data from the GSE131907 dataset, we identified 31 distinct cell clusters (Fig. 1A). The t-SNE plots revealed differential distributions between cells originating from normal and tumor tissues (Fig. 1B). We subsequently analyzed the expression patterns of 31 marker genes across these clusters and visualized the results using bubble plots (Fig. 1C). These marker genes included epithelial cell markers (KRT18, KRT19, EPCAM, CDH1), fibroblast markers (THY1, COL1A1, DCN, COL1A2), endothelial cell markers (CLDN5, FLT1, PECAM1, RAMP2), T cell markers (CD3E, CD3G, CD3D, TRAC), NK cell markers (GNLY, NCAM1, NKG7, KLRD1), B cell markers (IGHM, IGHG3, CD79A, IGHA2), myeloid cell markers (MARCO, CD68, LYZ, FCGR3A), and mast cell markers (KIT, GATA2, MS4A2). Based on the expression profiles of these marker genes, we annotated the clusters into eight distinct cell types: fibroblasts, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, T cells, B cells, myeloid cells, mast cells, and NK cells (Fig. 1D). The bubble plots effectively validated the accuracy of this classification by displaying the expression profiles of marker genes across these eight cell types (Fig. 1E). Significant differences in cell type composition were observed between normal and tumor samples, with tumor tissues showing higher proportions of T cells and B cells, whereas normal tissues contained relatively higher proportions of endothelial cells, NK cells, and myeloid cells (Fig. 1F-G). These observations were further illustrated through bar plots and box plots, providing a comprehensive visualization of cell type distribution.

Constructing and validating PRGs

Subsequently, we isolated epithelial cells for independent dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis. The tSNE plot displayed 24 clusters of epithelial cells along with their sample origins (Fig. 2A-B). Additionally, we conducted bar plot analysis to examine the composition of epithelial cells in each cluster, revealing that clusters 7, 8, and 12 predominantly comprised normal epithelial cells, while the remaining clusters were enriched with tumor cells (Fig. 2C). Differential expression genes (DEGs) were computed for each cluster and utilized as markers to generate heatmaps illustrating marker gene expression for each cluster (Fig. 2D). In the heatmap, clusters 16, 21, 20, 10, 18, and 22 clustered together, suggesting functional similarities, hence unified as S2; clusters 1, 13, and 23 were grouped as S3; and the remaining clusters were designated as S1, thus resulting in three subgroups. Cell trajectory analysis of epithelial cells revealed correlations between cell trajectories across different subgroups. State1 (normal cells) was postulated as the starting point of tumor differentiation, diverging towards either State5 or State6, representing two tumor types (Fig. 2E). Subgroup trajectory plots showed distinct cell distributions, with S1 containing the majority of epithelial cells covering all states, S2 predominantly consisting of tumor cells, especially enriched in State5, and S3 characterized by a relatively higher proportion of normal cells, particularly enriched in State1 (Fig. 2F). Comparison between tumor and normal samples revealed that tumor-derived trajectories contained more epithelial cells distributed across all six states, while most cells in normal samples were normal cells, with tumor cells enriched in State5 (Fig. 2G). Moreover, we selected 100 genes exhibiting expression changes over pseudo-time and generated expression heatmaps (Fig. 2H). Transcription factor analysis identified enrichment of RXRB, TCF7L1, RARB, ZNF197, and SP4 in S1; FOXJ2, CTCF, KLF3, NFKB2, and TCF7L2 in S2; and NFIC, SNAI1, HNF4G, HFYA, and ZEB2 in S3. To visualize these results, a heatmap of transcription factor activity was generated (Fig. 2I). Additionally, CNV analysis was conducted separately for each subgroup and state, with results presented using box plots. S2 exhibited higher CNV scores compared to S1 and S3, with S3 displaying the lowest CNV score (Fig. 2J). CNV scores for each state indicated that State5 was approximately equal to State6 but higher than the other four states, suggesting that State5 and State6 may represent two distinct tumor subtypes. Furthermore, the observation that tumor cell CNV was higher than that of normal cells corroborated the hypothesis that State1 serves as the starting point. Afterwards, we conducted analysis of Pearson correlation between pseudo-time and CNV score. From the correlation scatterplot, we observed a significant positive correlation between the two variables (R = 0.78, p < 0.05, Fig. 2L). Based on the results of GSEA, S1 was associated with ESTROGEN_RESPONSE_LATE and HYPOXIA functional pathways (Fig. 2M), while S2 was associated with APICAL_JUNCTION, EPITHELIAL_MESENCHYMAL_TRANSITION, ESTROGEN_RESPONSE_LATE, HYPOXIA, KRAS_SIGNALING_DN, P53_PATHWAY, and TNFA_SIGNALING_VIA_NFKB pathways (Fig. 2N).

Similarly, we isolated T cells and conducted dimensionality reduction and clustering analyses independently. The t-SNE plot revealed 14 distinct clusters, indicating that the majority of T cells originated from tumor samples (Fig. 3A-B). Using the ‘SCType’ software for cell annotation, the t-SNE plot identified three T cell subtypes: CD4 + NKT-like cells, Memory CD4 + T cells, and Naive CD4 + T cells (Fig. 3C). We then performed transcription factor activity analysis on these T cell subtypes, with heatmaps showing that Memory CD4 + T cells were enriched for YY1 and RUNX1, CD4 + NKT-like cells were enriched for JUND and SOX6, and Naive CD4 + T cells were enriched for NR1I2 and RXRB (Fig. 3D). Additionally, we assessed the phenotypic scores for resident, cytotoxic, exhausted, and costimulatory profiles across T cells from both tumor and normal samples. It was observed that resident and cytotoxic scores were significantly higher in T cells from normal tissues compared to tumor tissues, whereas exhausted and costimulatory scores were notably elevated in T cells from tumor samples (p < 0.05, Fig. 3E-H).

Subsequently, fibroblasts were isolated and analyzed using dimensionality reduction followed by clustering analysis separately. The t-SNE plot revealed 10 clusters, with the majority of cells originating from tumor samples (Fig. 3I-J). Through cell annotation, fibroblasts were categorized into myofibroblasts and inflammatory phenotypes, comprising three types of Cancer Associated Fibroblasts (CAFs). The distribution of these three types of CAFs was shown on the t-SNE plot. cluster5,6 were identified as myofibroblast CAFs (myoCAFs); while the other clusters expressed inflammatory phenotypes, among which cluster0,7,8,9 cells originated from normal samples, named as iNAFs; cluster1,2,3,4 belonged to tumor cells, named as iCAFs (Fig. 3K). Myofibroblast markers were upregulated in myoCAFs, including contractile proteins (TAGLN, MYLK, MYL9) and α-smooth muscle actin (αSMA, also known as ACTA2), while inflammatory phenotype markers such as CFD, DCN, and MFAP5 were upregulated in iNAFs and iCAFs (Fig. 3L). Transcription factor enrichment analysis revealed the enrichment patterns of transcription factors in the three types of CAFs, with SATB1, TEAD4, HOXB7 enriched in iCAFs, ZHX2, POU4F2, SNAI1 enriched in iNAFs, and FOXK1, NCOA1, RXRB enriched in myoCAFs (Fig. 3M).

Acquisition and Consistency Clustering Analysis of Mitochondrial-Associated Gene Set of Prognosis.

We performed univariate Cox regression analysis on 2,030 mitochondrial-associated genes, identifying 220 genes with significant prognostic value (p < 0.01, Fig. 4A). Based on the expression profiles of these 220 genes, consensus clustering analysis was conducted on the TCGA-LUAD dataset. The CDF curve and Proportion of Ambiguous Clustering (PAC) statistics indicated that the optimal clustering solution was achieved at k = 2, resulting in two distinct clusters (C1 and C2). The consensus clustering heatmap demonstrated high intra-cluster cohesion and low inter-cluster separation (Fig. 4B). Principal component analysis (PCA) illustrated the distribution of samples across the two subtypes (Fig. 4C). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that patients in cluster C2 had significantly worse overall survival compared to those in cluster C1 (p < 0.0001, Fig. 4D). Examination of the mitochondrial-associated gene expression heatmap showed that while a minority of genes had higher expression in C1, the majority were more highly expressed in C2 (Fig. 4E). Integration of clustering results with clinical parameters, including age, M stage, N stage, T stage, overall stage, and survival status, using a chi-square test, revealed significant differences in the distribution of clinical features between the two clusters, with the exception of age (p < 0.05, Fig. 4F-K). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) conducted on the marker genes from both clusters showed that C1 exhibited downregulation of pathways such as G2M checkpoint, protein secretion, reactive oxygen species (ROS) pathway, and unfolded protein response, while C2 demonstrated upregulation of pathways including G2M checkpoint, E2F targets, mitotic spindle, and spermatogenesis (Fig. 4L-M).

Prognostic risk model constructed via machine learning integrated approach

In the TCGA-LUAD dataset, 101 predictive models were fitted using the LOOCV framework, followed by computation of the C-index for each model across 11 validation datasets. The optimal model, Enet[a = 0.2], with a highest average C-index (0.655), exhibited superior performance across all validation datasets (Fig. 5A). Utilizing the optimal model, risk scores for each patient were computed based on the prognostic genes’ expression levels, and samples were classified into high and low-risk groups using a cutoff value. In both the TCGA-LUAD dataset and the remaining 11 validation datasets, patients in the high-risk group demonstrated significantly poorer Overall Survival (OS) compared to those in the low-risk group (p < 0.05, Fig. 5B-M). We observed similar trends in the meta-cohort combining all samples (p < 0.05, Fig. 5N).

Discussion

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is a prevalent malignant tumor, accounting for approximately 40% of all lung cancer cases. Lung cancer remains one of the most lethal diseases globally, with around 1.8 million deaths reported in 2020, representing approximately 18% of all cancer-related fatalities. Despite advancements in therapeutic modalities such as surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies, the clinical efficacy of current LUAD treatments remains suboptimal, and the accurate prediction of patient outcomes continues to pose significant challenges. As a result, identifying reliable molecular biomarkers is critical to improving diagnosis, therapeutic stratification, and prognostic evaluation in LUAD. Mitochondria, fundamental organelles involved in energy metabolism, apoptosis, and oxidative stress regulation, have been increasingly recognized as playing a central role in cancer biology. Recent studies suggest that mitochondrial dysfunction can promote oncogenesis through mechanisms such as increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, metabolic reprogramming, and evasion of apoptosis, all of which contribute to tumor progression. Despite these emerging links, the specific roles of mitochondria-related genes in LUAD remain underexplored. Understanding how these genes contribute to LUAD development and progression could provide new insights into disease pathophysiology and reveal novel therapeutic targets. In particular, the investigation of mitochondria-related genes could lead to the identification of prognostic biomarkers that stratify patients based on their predicted response to treatments, including chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapies. Additionally, elucidating the mechanisms by which mitochondrial dysfunction influences the tumor microenvironment, including immune cell infiltration and response, may enhance the understanding of tumor-immune interactions and identify potential avenues for improving immunotherapy efficacy. In this study, we utilized comprehensive bioinformatics approaches to investigate the roles of mitochondria-related genes in LUAD. We integrated multi-omics data, including transcriptomics and single-cell RNA sequencing, to identify key mitochondrial genes associated with disease progression and prognosis. Our study culminated in the development of a robust prognostic model that provides valuable insights for predicting disease outcomes. This work underscores the potential of mitochondria as a critical area of focus for advancing both our understanding of LUAD pathogenesis and the development of more personalized treatment strategies. Further experimental validation and clinical trials are needed to translate these findings into clinical practice, potentially offering new hope for improving the survival rates of LUAD patients.

To explore the cellular landscape of LUAD, we leveraged a comprehensive dataset comprising transcriptomic profiles and clinical data from LUAD patients within the TCGA, which served as our primary training set. For validation, we additionally accessed 11 LUAD microarray datasets from the GEO and incorporated two single-cell RNA sequencing datasets for further analysis. Dimensionality reduction and clustering techniques were first applied to the single-cell data, identifying 31 distinct cellular clusters. Marker gene expression profiling enabled the classification of these clusters into eight major cellular phenotypes: epithelial cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, B cells, myeloid cells, and macrophages. Notably, a comparative analysis between tumor and normal samples revealed a higher prevalence of B cells and T cells in tumor tissues, whereas endothelial cells, NK cells, and myeloid cells were more abundant in normal tissues, indicating differential immune cell dynamics in LUAD.

Subsequent analysis focused on the epithelial cell population, which was subdivided into 24 clusters. Clusters 7, 8, and 12 predominantly comprised normal epithelial cells, while the remaining clusters were enriched with tumor cells. Differential expression analysis of these clusters identified the top 50 genes that distinguished them, enabling the classification of epithelial cells into three distinct states (S1, S2, and S3). Cell trajectory analysis suggested that State1, representing normal cells, likely corresponds to the initial stage of tumor cell differentiation, with progression toward States 5 and 6, indicative of two divergent tumorigenic pathways.

Transcription factor analysis revealed that State1 was enriched for factors such as RXRB, TCF7L1, RARB, ZNF197, and SP4, while State2 was characterized by the enrichment of FOXJ2, CTCF, KLF3, NFKB2, and TCF7L2, and State3 was associated with NFIC, SNAI1, HNF4G, HNFYA, and ZEB2. Copy number variation (CNV) analysis across these subgroups further corroborated these findings, demonstrating a positive correlation with pseudo-time progression, thus supporting the hypothesis that normal cells transition into tumor cells.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) indicated that State1 is likely involved in pathways such as ESTROGEN_RESPONSE_LATE and HYPOXIA, while State2 was associated with pathways including KRAS_SIGNALING_DN, APICAL_JUNCTION, and TNFA_SIGNALING_VIA_NFKB. Existing research suggests that estrogen may exert anti-tumor effects in specific contexts by inhibiting NF-κB-mediated tumor-promoting inflammation [23]. These enriched pathways offer valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying LUAD progression and provide potential avenues for further exploration of tumor development.

Additionally, we conducted a similar subclassification and transcription factor analysis for isolated T cells and fibroblasts from tumor samples. T cells were categorized into CD4 + NKT-like cells, naive CD4 + T cells, and memory CD4 + T cells; fibroblasts were classified into three types of cancer-associated fibroblasts (myoCAF, iCAF, and iNAF). Transcriptional profiling indicated distinct enrichments: memory CD4 + T cells for YY1 and RUNX1, CD4 + NKT-like cells for JUND and SOX6, naive CD4 + T cells for NR1I2 and RXRB; iCAF for SATB1, TEAD4, and HOXB7; iNAF for ZHX2, POU4F2, and SNAI1; and myoCAF for FOXK1, NCOA1, and RXRB. Furthermore, immune functionality scores, specifically exhaustion and costimulatory markers, were elevated in tumor-derived T cells, while resident and cytotoxic scores were higher in cells from normal tissue, underscoring distinct immunological states associated with tumor and normal contexts.

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), we performed univariate Cox regression analysis on a dataset of 2,030 mitochondrial-associated genes, identifying a set of 220 genes with significant prognostic value. Using consensus clustering, we stratified the TCGA-LUAD cohort into two distinct clusters, C1 and C2, with the optimal separation determined at this bifurcation. Survival analysis revealed that patients in cluster C2 had significantly worse overall survival compared to those in cluster C1. Expression profiling of mitochondrial-related genes showed differential expression patterns between the clusters, with a small subset upregulated in C1 and the majority exhibiting elevated expression in C2. This differential expression suggests a potential association between increased mitochondrial gene activity and poorer clinical outcomes in LUAD. Moreover, significant differences were observed between the two clusters in several clinical parameters, including M stage, N stage, T stage, overall stage, and survival status, further emphasizing the prognostic relevance of these clusters. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the two clusters revealed distinct molecular pathways: in C1, pathways such as G2M checkpoint, protein secretion, reactive oxygen species (ROS) pathway, and unfolded protein response were downregulated, whereas C2 exhibited upregulation in E2F targets, G2M checkpoint, mitotic spindle, and spermatogenesis pathways. These findings provide insights into the potential role of mitochondrial-related genes in LUAD progression and prognosis.

In the subsequent analysis, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between various tumor subtypes were identified, and those with prognostic significance were selected through univariate Cox regression analysis. Genes demonstrating prognostic relevance in at least eight independent datasets were shortlisted for further evaluation. To construct a robust predictive model, we employed a combination of 101 machine learning algorithms, with performance assessed using a leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) framework. The optimal algorithm, identified as Enet [α = 0.2], achieved the highest mean concordance index (C-index) and consistently ranked first across all validation datasets. Using this algorithm, we calculated risk scores for each patient in the TCGA-LUAD and 11 external validation cohorts, stratifying them into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score. Survival analyses across all cohorts consistently demonstrated significantly worse outcomes for the high-risk group, validating both the predictive power and stability of the model. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis further supported the model’s predictive efficacy, with most area under the curve (AUC) values exceeding 0.6.

Additionally, we compared the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of chemotherapy drugs between risk groups. Violin plots revealed significantly lower IC50 values in the high-risk group, suggesting greater chemotherapy sensitivity. This group also exhibited higher Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) scores, indicating a greater likelihood of tumor immune evasion and potentially reduced efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors. While the high-risk group within the immunotherapy cohort demonstrated poorer outcomes, the proportions of immune responses were similar between the high- and low-risk groups.

Lastly, we explored the mutational characteristics and tumor immune microenvironment features of LUAD. A waterfall plot of single nucleotide variants (SNVs) revealed that TP53 and TTN had the highest mutation rates, predominantly of the missense type and Multi_Hit mutations. Violin plot analyses showed that the Tumor Mutational Burden (TMB), Mutant-Allele Tumor Heterogeneity (MATH), and Homologous Recombination Deficiency (HRD) scores were significantly higher in the high-risk group, suggesting that genetic alterations may play a crucial role in the disease’s progression. Using the CIBERSORT algorithm for immune infiltration analysis, we found differential levels of immune cells between the risk groups, which could contribute to their distinct clinical behaviors.

Through genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis of lung cancer, we investigated the association between significant SNPs and the prognostic genes identified in our model. While no significant association was found with interstitial lung disease, SNPs such as rs1794002 and rs244320 showed significant correlations with lung cancer risk, providing potential targets for future genetic and therapeutic studies.

Haibo Yu and colleagues applied machine learning techniques to identify six mitochondrial autophagy-related signature genes (SRC, UBB, PINK1, FUNDC1, MAP1LC3B, and CSNK2A1) for the development of a diagnostic model for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Through unsupervised consensus clustering, they stratified NSCLC patients into two molecular subtypes and conducted a comprehensive analysis of differences in clinical characteristics, immune cell infiltration, and immunotherapy response between these subtypes. Moreover, they investigated the interactions between mitochondrial autophagy-related genes (MRGs) and immune cells using single-cell sequencing data, providing further insights into the immune landscape of NSCLC [24]. Ke Mi and colleagues focused on macrophage polarization-related subtypes and identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) to develop a prognostic risk model for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) patients. Using univariate Cox regression and LASSO analysis, they incorporated genes such as RGS13, ADRB2, DDIT4, MS4A2, ALDH2, CTSH, and PKM into the model, which was subsequently validated. Additionally, they explored correlations between trait-associated genes and significantly mutated genes, evaluated immune cell infiltration across different risk groups, and conducted pseudotime trajectory analysis of macrophage clusters using Monocle software. Finally, the expression levels of the prognostic signature genes in LUAD were validated using quantitative PCR (qPCR) technology [25]. While previous studies have utilized similar datasets, our research surpasses existing analyses in both dataset diversity and sample size. By leveraging the comprehensive TCGA dataset for model construction and rigorously validating it across 11 GEO datasets, each comprising over 50 samples, we have significantly improved the reliability and generalizability of our findings. This extensive validation process highlights the robustness of our model and its applicability across diverse conditions, providing a strong foundation for advancing personalized therapeutic strategies in lung adenocarcinoma. Additionally, the incorporation of multiple machine learning algorithms into an ensemble framework offers a powerful approach to capitalize on the strengths of individual algorithms while mitigating their limitations. By integrating diverse algorithms, each tailored to specific aspects of data analysis, we have developed a more robust and universally applicable prognostic model. This ensemble strategy ensures that no single methodological bias dominates the analysis, resulting in more comprehensive and balanced insights.

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive analysis of the roles and prognostic significance of mitochondria-related genes in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). The integration of multi-omics data, including transcriptomics and single-cell RNA sequencing, allowed for the identification of key mitochondrial genes associated with disease progression. The development of the Artificial Intelligence-Derived Prognostic Signature (AIDPS) model offers a promising tool for predicting patient outcomes and tailoring personalized therapeutic strategies based on mitochondrial gene expression profiles. Despite these promising findings, there remain several areas that warrant further exploration. First, while this study highlights the association between mitochondrial dysfunction and LUAD prognosis, additional experimental validation is needed to confirm the causal relationships between specific mitochondrial genes and tumor progression. Functional studies using in vitro and in vivo models could provide deeper insights into how mitochondrial dysfunction directly influences key oncogenic processes, such as metabolic reprogramming, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance. Moreover, the tumor microenvironment plays a critical role in shaping cancer progression and response to treatment. Our findings suggest that mitochondrial alterations may influence the immune landscape in LUAD, but further investigations are necessary to elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying these interactions. For example, understanding how mitochondrial dysfunction affects immune cell infiltration, immune checkpoint expression, and overall immunogenicity of the tumor could lead to improved strategies for combining mitochondrial-targeted therapies with immunotherapy. Another important consideration is the potential clinical application of our findings. While the AIDPS model demonstrated robust predictive performance across multiple datasets, its clinical utility must be validated in prospective clinical trials. Factors such as patient heterogeneity, varying therapeutic regimens, and differences in clinical settings could affect the model’s predictive accuracy. Therefore, large-scale, multi-center studies are needed to evaluate the generalizability of the AIDPS model and to determine its potential for integration into routine clinical practice. Finally, given the rapid advancements in mitochondrial research, it is crucial to continue exploring the broader implications of mitochondrial dynamics in cancer. Future studies could focus on mitochondrial DNA mutations, mitochondrial-nuclear communication, and the role of mitochondrial metabolites in influencing both tumor biology and the tumor microenvironment. Understanding these complex interactions may unlock new therapeutic avenues, offering novel interventions that target mitochondrial pathways in LUAD.

Overall, this study underscores the importance of mitochondria as a key player in LUAD pathogenesis and prognosis. By advancing our understanding of the molecular mechanisms involving mitochondria, we move closer to developing more effective, individualized therapies that could significantly improve the survival and quality of life for LUAD patients.

Conclusion

This study performed an in-depth analysis of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) through the integration of multiple methodologies, including the acquisition and processing of transcriptomic and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data, followed by cell classification and subclassification analyses. In addition, immune infiltration profiling, prediction of responses to immunotherapy and chemotherapy, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), and Single Nucleotide Variant (SNV) analysis were conducted. The comprehensive application of these bioinformatics approaches yielded a robust and multifaceted dataset, providing valuable insights into the molecular landscape and immune dynamics of LUAD.
