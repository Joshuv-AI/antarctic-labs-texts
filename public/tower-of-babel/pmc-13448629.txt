Preparing residents and fellows for armed conflict and war settings: a scoping review protocol of educational programmes
Authors: Reine Obeid, Ali Bou Melhem, Ola El Zein, Joseph Abourjeili, Carmen Assi, Nizar El Bcherawi, Marianne El Khoury, Patrick Maroun, Fatima Msheik-El Khoury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionArmed conflict and war increasingly strain healthcare systems worldwide, particularly in regions affected by protracted violence and instability. Physicians practising in war settings must deliver care under conditions of insecurity, resource scarcity and health system disruption, yet graduate medical education (GME) largely prepares trainees for stable, high-resource environments. Residents and fellows are often underprepared for the clinical, ethical and operational challenges of armed conflict. This scoping review aims to map and synthesise the existing literature on educational programmes designed to prepare residents and fellows for clinical practice in armed conflict and war settings.Methods and analysisThe review protocol follows the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley, with refinements by Levac et al and the final review will be reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines (PRISMA-ScR). Guided by the Population-Concept-Context (PCC) framework, this scoping review will include articles that describe educational and training programmes (Concept) involving medical residents and fellows (Population), and which are intended to prepare these physicians-in-training for armed conflict and war-related clinical practice environments (Context). We developed the search strategy with the support of an experienced librarian and will execute the database searches on 3 August 2026, across the following databases: MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase (Elsevier) and ERIC (EBSCOhost), with no start-date restriction. No language restriction will be applied; with translation support to be used where required for screening and data extraction. Additional grey literature searches, citation tracking and search updates will be conducted between July and August 2026 and will be repeated before the review is completed. Two reviewers will independently screen eligible studies for inclusion and extract data using a piloted data charting form. Findings will be synthesised through descriptive mapping and narrative analysis to examine programme characteristics, curriculum content, teaching strategies, assessment approaches and reported barriers and facilitators.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval is not required for this scoping review. The professional stakeholder consultation component will be conducted in accordance with institutional guidance and, if required, will be submitted for ethics review or exemption determination. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations, with the aim of informing GME curricula and strengthening physician preparedness for armed conflict and war settings.

Introduction

In recent decades, the frequency and severity of crisis events, including armed conflicts and war, have escalated, placing unprecedented demands on healthcare systems.1 The Middle East and North Africa region remains one of the most volatile and conflict-prone areas in the world, plagued by protracted wars, forced displacement and humanitarian emergencies.2 In such settings, physicians may be required to manage trauma, mass-casualty triage, infection risks, resource scarcity, communication breakdowns, psychological distress and ethical decisions under conditions of uncertainty and personal risk. Yet medical education and graduate medical training have largely been designed for relatively stable, resource-available healthcare environments. As a result, physicians working in conflict-affected settings may be underprepared for the realities of infrastructure collapse, prolonged instability and rapidly evolving clinical demands.3 Many physicians in settings such as Gaza, Libya, Syria, Iraq and Yemen acquire war surgery, trauma management and crisis-response skills in real time, while caring for patients during mass-casualty and public health emergencies, rather than through structured training.3 This reactive model may contribute to delayed or suboptimal trauma care, increased preventable morbidity and mortality and additional strain on already overburdened healthcare systems. Physicians are often forced to improvise and make rapid clinical decisions under uncertainty, in circumstances for which medical education has not adequately prepared them.4 5 Where conflict is recurrent or protracted, war-related clinical practice may become an expected part of healthcare delivery rather than a temporary exception. It is therefore important to synthesise the current training programmes described in the literature to understand how residents and fellows are being prepared for conflict and war settings, what educational approaches are being used and where gaps remain.

Conflict medicine and emergency preparedness are not standardised components of graduate medical education (GME) curricula outside of emergency medicine, and no relevant universal requirements exist, resulting in highly variable training approaches across specialties.6 Surveys with residents revealed that a majority were not aware of institutional disaster management plans and over two-thirds had not received disaster management training in the past year.7 The COVID-19 pandemic further exposed these preparedness gaps, disrupting medical training and underscoring the need for improved preparedness education.8 In conflict-affected settings, this shortfall is even more pronounced, as current medical education often does not prepare trainees to respond to the sustained insecurity, infrastructure damage, displacement, infectious risks, psychological trauma and disrupted systems that characterise war-related clinical practice.4 9

Several systematic reviews have addressed adjacent areas of disaster and mass-casualty preparedness education, but none answer the specific population-concept-context combination targeted by this review. Some focus on undergraduate medical students rather than residents and fellows; others address broad disaster or pandemic education rather than conflict-focused and war-focused training within GME.10 11 Other reviews have examined prehospital disaster response among medical first responders, evaluating training programmes and their effectiveness across traditional, simulation-based and technology-enhanced approaches. One review specifically addresses extended-reality simulation for mass-casualty incident training.12 13

To address these deficiencies, a patchwork of specialised programmes has emerged outside the standard curriculum. Numerous courses, certifications and just-in-time training initiatives aim to build crisis readiness in healthcare professionals, but they are often elective, variably integrated or developed in response to acute needs rather than embedded longitudinally within residency or fellowship training. For instance, the Advanced Trauma Life Support and Advanced Trauma Care for Nurses (ATCN) courses provide a structured approach to the assessment and life-saving management of acute trauma emergencies.14 The Hospital Emergency Response Training (HERT) programme teaches hospital staff, including residents, to manage mass-casualty incidents through incident command, decontamination drills and patient triage.15

Academic institutions have also introduced certificate programmes designed to prepare physicians for practice in conflict settings. The Online Certificate in Conflict Medicine, developed by the Global Health Institute at the American University of Beirut,16 aims to strengthen healthcare practitioners’ capacity to deliver context-appropriate care and conduct research in fragile and conflict-affected environments. Other initiatives, such as the Disaster Medicine Certificate Series at the University of Louisville,17 provide training in mass-casualty triage and clinical improvisation during emergencies. More recently, conflict-specific virtual training initiatives have been deployed to support healthcare workers in active war settings, including a rapidly developed Armed Conflict Trauma Training programme in Ukraine and an emergency Extension for Community Healthcare Outcomes programme in Sudan focused on trauma care and public health emergency response.18 Collectively, these programmes address key competencies, including trauma management, crisis leadership and chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear (CBRN) preparedness, reflecting the broad skill set required for effective response in conflict settings. Despite these efforts, available training opportunities remain fragmented.

In light of the growing threat of armed conflict and the evident gaps in GME, a scoping review is necessary to map and synthesise the existing literature on training programmes that prepare residents and fellows for clinical practice in armed conflict and war settings. This review aims to consolidate what is known about these educational programmes, including their formats, curricular content, teaching strategies, assessment approaches, reported outcomes and barriers and facilitators to implementation. Thus, the review will provide a foundation for strengthening residency and fellowship curricula and for informing future educational development in conflict medicine. To our knowledge, and based on a preliminary search of MEDLINE, PROSPERO and the Open Science Framework, no existing review has specifically mapped educational programmes designed to prepare residents and fellows, as distinct from medical students, the broader health workforce or fully trained physicians, for practice in armed conflict and war settings.

Method

Protocol registration

This scoping review protocol has been registered with the Open Science Framework (OSF) (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/8H3ED).

Protocol design

This study will employ a scoping review methodology to systematically map the existing literature on training programmes designed to prepare resident physicians and fellows for practice in armed conflict and war settings. A scoping review is the most appropriate approach given the heterogeneity of educational interventions, variability in terminology and the exploratory nature of the research question. The review will be conducted in accordance with the methodological framework originally proposed by Arksey and O’Malley,19 with refinements by Levac et al,20 and will be reported according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines (PRISMA-ScR).21 This approach consists of six stages: (1) formulating the research questions, (2) identifying relevant studies, (3) selecting eligible studies, (4) charting the data, (5) collating, summarising and reporting the results and (6) expert consultation. The review commenced in November 2025 and is anticipated to be completed by June 2027; database searches will be executed on 3 August 2026, with no start-date restriction.

Defining armed conflict and war settings

For the purposes of this scoping review, armed conflict and war settings are defined in accordance with established international legal and public health literature. Armed conflict is broadly understood as the use of armed force between organised parties that results in sustained violence and disruption of civilian life.22 The International Committee of the Red Cross distinguishes armed conflict from internal disturbances by the presence of organised armed groups, protracted hostilities and the application of international humanitarian law (IHL). Armed conflicts may be classified as international armed conflicts, occurring between two or more states, or non-international armed conflicts, involving state and non-state armed groups within a state’s territory.22

War settings represent environments affected by active or protracted armed conflict in which healthcare delivery occurs under conditions of insecurity, violence and systemic disruption. The WHO describes conflict-affected settings as those characterised by weakened or collapsed health systems, shortages of essential supplies, threats to health workers, population displacement and barriers to access to care.23 In such settings, health facilities and personnel may be directly targeted, and routine standards of care are frequently compromised.

Within the medical education literature, war and armed conflict settings are further characterised by recurrent mass-casualty events, severe resource scarcity, ethical dilemmas related to triage and allocation of care, disrupted referral pathways and the need for clinical decision-making in unstable and insecure environments.4 24 These conditions fundamentally differ from disaster or emergency contexts occurring in otherwise stable systems, as armed conflict introduces prolonged insecurity, legal constraints and intentional harm to civilians and healthcare infrastructure.

Accordingly, in this review, armed conflict and war settings refer exclusively to environments affected by international or non-international armed conflict where residents and fellows are required to provide clinical care amid sustained violence, insecurity and health-system disruption.

Stage 1: identifying the research question

An iterative process was followed to refine the research question and align the review scope with its educational purpose. This process was informed by familiar literature in conflict medicine and adjacent fields, including disaster medicine, pandemic preparedness, humanitarian health and crisis-response training. Although this literature describes preparedness initiatives for medical students, healthcare workers and medical first responders, training programmes situated within GME have not been specifically mapped.10–13 We therefore focused on residents and fellows because they are still within formal supervised training, where curricula, competencies, teaching strategies and assessment approaches can be intentionally developed and embedded. Physicians who have already completed residency or fellowship may receive conflict-related preparation through continuing professional development, humanitarian deployment training, military medicine or institutional capacity-building initiatives; however, these represent distinct educational contexts and were outside the scope of this review. Thus, the research question was developed using the Population-Concept-Context (PCC) framework to align with the study objectives and clearly delimit the scope of inquiry.

Population: Medical residents and fellows enrolled in GME programmes across all specialties.

Stage 2: identifying relevant studies

Search strategy and information sources

Identification of studies relevant to this review will be achieved by searching three electronic databases: MEDLINE (Ovid), EMBASE (Elsevier) and ERIC (EBSCOhost). The database searches will be executed on 3 August 2026, with no start-date restriction. Following the initial search, email alerts will be established across the bibliographic databases, where available, to notify the review team of newly indexed records meeting the search criteria and to support ongoing surveillance of emerging literature throughout the review period. The proposed search strategy was developed in consultation with an experienced medical librarian, who is also the director of the evidence synthesis unit at our institution, to ensure sensitivity and reproducibility. These databases were selected to capture literature spanning medical education, health professions training, humanitarian health and conflict-related clinical education.

Predefined search terms combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH, Emtree and ERIC descriptors) with free-text keywords, structured around two core concept blocks, Population (residents and fellows within GME) and Context (armed conflict and war settings), combined with the Boolean operator AND and adapted to each database’s syntax. The Concept component of the PCC framework (education and training) was deliberately not implemented as a separate mandatory search block, to increase sensitivity. Educational and training concepts are instead captured structurally within the Population block through graduate-medical-education vocabulary and concept-level eligibility is confirmed during title/abstract and full-text screening rather than enforced at the search stage. Search terms were applied within the title, abstract, keyword and other relevant fields, as supported by each platform.

Full line-by-line search strategies for all three databases (MEDLINE, Embase and ERIC) are provided in online supplemental appendix 1 to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Grey literature

Given that many conflict-focused and war-focused training programmes are developed and delivered outside traditional academic publishing, a targeted grey-literature search will be conducted to capture relevant non-peer-reviewed sources. Searches will be conducted between 15 July 2026 and 15 August 2026, with a final search update completed within 4 weeks of manuscript submission to capture newly available programmes, course pages, competency documents or training requirements.

We will search Google, using a private/incognito browser session with location-based personalisation disabled, screening the first 100 results (approximately 10 pages) per query, consistent with established grey-literature guidance for evidence synthesis,25 as relevance beyond this threshold declines sharply.

Search terms will combine conflict- and war-related terms, educational terms, learner-population terms and country-specific or context-specific terms for active armed conflicts and war-affected settings. Country-specific and context-specific terms will be drawn from the Uppsala Conflict Data Program/Peace Research Institute Oslo (UCDP/PRIO) Armed Conflict Dataset, version 26.1 (released June 2026),26 rechecked at the final search update to capture newly emergent contexts. Examples of exact search strings include “conflict medicine” AND “training”; “conflict medicine” AND “course”; “war medicine” AND “curriculum”; “armed conflict” AND “medical education”; “humanitarian health” AND “certificate”; “disaster medicine” AND “residency”; “mass casualty” AND “training” AND “physicians”; “emergency preparedness” AND “residents”; “trauma training” AND “conflict settings”; “Ukraine” AND “trauma training” AND “physicians”; “Sudan” AND “emergency preparedness” AND “training”; and “Gaza” AND “medical education” AND “conflict.” No language restriction will be applied; publicly available sources in any language are eligible, including programme descriptions, curricula, course pages, certificate descriptions, competency frameworks, conference materials and training requirements.

To increase sensitivity, we will handsearch the websites of organisations, academic and training centres, professional bodies and governmental or humanitarian platforms relevant to conflict and war medicine training, identified through the initial search results and the authors’ familiarity with the field. For each organisation, we will apply the same search-string combinations listed above, using either the site’s internal search function or a domain-restricted Google query (eg, site:icrc.org “trauma training” AND “physicians”), where supported. Examples include humanitarian and global health organisations such as the WHO and its OpenWHO platform, the WHO Health Emergencies Programme, the International Committee of the Red Cross, the International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies, Médecins Sans Frontières, RedR UK and Medical Peace Work; academic and training platforms such as the American University of Beirut Global Health Institute, HarvardX, the Johns Hopkins Center for Humanitarian Health and the Center for Conflict and Health Security at King’s College London; governmental and accreditation bodies as the Federal Emergency Management Agency Center for Domestic Preparedness, the National Center for Disaster Medicine and Public Health, the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education, the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada (CanMEDS) and the General Medical Council; and professional societies or course providers such as the National Association of Emergency Medical Technicians, the American College of Surgeons, Advanced Life Support Group, World Extreme Medicine and Advanced Hazmat Life Support. This list is not exhaustive and predominantly reflects North American, UK and international humanitarian bodies; we acknowledge this as a limitation of geographic breadth in the discussion.

Additional search methods

To ensure comprehensive coverage, the reference lists of all included studies will be manually screened for additional relevant publications not captured through database or grey-literature searching. Scoping and systematic reviews identified during the search will not be treated as primary sources, consistent with the eligibility criteria in online supplemental appendix 2, but their reference lists will also be hand-searched for potentially eligible primary studies. Forward citation tracking (identifying subsequent publications that cite included studies) will be performed for all included articles using Google Scholar and Web of Science.

Search limits

No language restriction is applied; publications in any language are eligible, and records in languages other than English will be screened and charted with translation support (including professional or verified translation) to ensure accurate assessment. No restrictions will be placed on the year of publication, recognising that conflict-medicine training has evolved over time and that older programmes may provide important contextual insights.

Scope control

To maintain alignment with the study objectives, search results will be screened to ensure that included studies explicitly address armed conflict or war settings. Literature focused exclusively on natural disasters, pandemics or emergency preparedness in otherwise stable settings will be excluded unless armed conflict or war-related practice is explicitly described as part of the intended context.

Given the overlap between crisis preparedness domains, no search limits were applied to exclude disaster or pandemic terminology; relevance to armed conflict or war settings was assessed during screening.

Stage 3: study selection

Articles of empirical or descriptive study designs will be eligible for inclusion, including qualitative, quantitative, mixed-methods, observational and methodological studies. Systematic and scoping reviews will be used for reference mining but will not be included as primary sources unless they provide extractable descriptions of eligible training programmes not otherwise available from primary reports. Grey literature describing educational or training programmes will also be included. Commentaries, perspectives, editorials, letters and book chapters will be excluded, as these sources typically do not provide sufficient detail to address the review objectives.

The eligibility criteria for this review are guided by the PCC framework (online supplemental appendix 2).

Population

This review focuses on residents and fellows as the primary population of interest. Residents are defined as physicians enrolled in accredited residency training programmes across any medical or surgical specialty. Fellows are defined as physicians pursuing subspecialty training following completion of residency.

Residents and fellows are the focus of this review because they occupy a distinct, formative stage of professional development in which clinical competence and professional identity are still being shaped within accredited, competency-based curricula, rather than consolidated through independent practice.27 28 Within GME, conflict-preparedness training also remains variable and largely non-standardised across specialties and residents themselves report feeling inadequately prepared for crisis conditions.29–32 Fully trained physicians who engage in conflict-related care typically do so through continuing professional development or humanitarian-deployment pathways that lie outside GME33; including them would shift the review from curriculum design toward workforce deployment, introduce substantial population heterogeneity and dilute the PCC focus. The review therefore retains a deliberate focus on residents and fellows enrolled in GME, for whom conflict-preparedness competencies can be embedded directly into formal training.

To be eligible for inclusion, studies must involve residents and/or fellows who are engaged in, exposed to or affected by educational or training programmes delivered within or formally linked to, GME. Studies of programmes delivered to mixed-learner groups, including fully trained physicians or other health professionals, will be included if residents and/or fellows are explicitly identified among the target participants or if the programme is formally linked to residency or fellowship training. When trainee-specific findings are reported, these will be extracted separately. When outcomes cannot be disaggregated by learner type, programme-level data will be charted, and the mixed-learner composition will be documented.

Context

In this review, context refers to the intended clinical practice environment for which trainees are being prepared. This includes armed conflict and war-related healthcare environments, such as active combat zones, post-conflict settings and humanitarian crises resulting from political violence.

Educational programmes may be delivered in a range of GME–affiliated settings, including academic institutions, teaching hospitals, simulation centres, civilian training facilities or residency-linked or fellowship-linked humanitarian training programmes. The physical location of training delivery is not a criterion for inclusion/exclusion. Rather, the intended practice environment should be explicitly related to armed conflict or war.

Contexts limited to natural disasters, pandemics or humanitarian emergencies without an underlying armed conflict are explicitly excluded. Contexts relevant to both armed conflict and other crises (eg, pandemics or disasters) will be included provided that preparation for armed conflict or war-related practice is an explicit component of the programme’s intent or scope.

Military medicine is excluded from this review, as it constitutes a distinct and well-established discipline with its own dedicated training pathways, competency frameworks, accreditation structures and doctrine (eg, tactical combat casualty care), supported by a substantial parallel literature.34 The orientation of armed-forces medical services toward combatant care within a military command structure differs fundamentally from the preparation of civilian residents and fellows who may be required to deliver care in war-affected health systems. Incorporating military medicine would introduce a heterogeneous body of literature governed by different objectives and institutions and would dilute the review’s focus on civilian GME. Programmes that prepare civilian residents and fellows in conflict-relevant clinical competencies (war surgery, blast and ballistic injury management, mass-casualty triage) remain eligible, provided they are not delivered as part of military medical training.

Studies conducted in any geographical region or healthcare system will be considered to capture the global scope of conflict-related GME.

Study selection process

All search results will be exported into Covidence for reference management and duplicate removal. Following de-duplication, records will be screened and data extracted.

Title and abstract screening will be conducted independently by two reviewers to identify studies that meet the predefined PCC eligibility criteria. Full-text screening will then be performed independently by the same reviewers. Any disagreements at either stage will be resolved with the consultation of a third reviewer.

Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage will be documented. The study selection process will be reported using a PRISMA flow diagram.

Stage 4: charting the data

Data from all included studies will be systematically charted using a predefined and piloted data extraction form (online supplemental appendix 3) developed specifically for this scoping review. The purpose of charting data is to extract, organise and summarise key information related to educational programmes designed to prepare residents and fellows for practice in armed conflict and war settings, in accordance with the review objectives. For programmes delivered to mixed-learner groups, where outcomes cannot be disaggregated by learner type, programme-level data will be charted and the mixed-learner composition documented, consistent with the eligibility approach described in stage 3.

Prior to full data extraction, the form will be piloted on a subset of included studies to ensure clarity, consistency and completeness. Refinements will be made as needed through team discussion.

Data extraction process

Data extraction will be performed independently by two reviewers to enhance rigour and reduce bias. Any discrepancies in extracted data will be resolved with the involvement of a third reviewer. Extracted data will subsequently be compiled in Microsoft Excel for validation, coding and analysis. Both descriptive data (example: programme characteristics, settings) and interpretive data (example: authors’ conclusions regarding effectiveness, relevance or limitations) will be charted to support comprehensive synthesis in subsequent stages of the review.

As the purpose of this scoping review is to map the extent, range and nature of available evidence rather than assess intervention effectiveness, a formal quality appraisal of included studies will not be conducted.

Stage 5: collating, summarising and reporting the results

Extracted data will be synthesised using descriptive mapping and narrative synthesis, structured around the review’s four subquestions. First, included sources will be summarised quantitatively using frequencies and cross-tabulations for year of publication, source type (peer-reviewed vs grey literature), country (and World Bank income status), region, conflict or war-related setting, specialty, training level, programme format, duration, delivery mode and study design. These findings will be presented in tables and charts.

Second, the educational content of each programme will be coded using a deductive–inductive approach. An initial content-domain framework will be developed from prior literature on conflict medicine, conflict-health education, healthcare education during war and healthcare provider competencies in armed conflict settings.4 5 9 18 24 35 36 Initial domains will include trauma and surgical management, blast and ballistic injury care, mass-casualty triage, CBRN and explosive preparedness, security and personal safety, communication, surge capacity, infectious disease and public health, mental health and psychosocial support, medical ethics and IHL, and health-systems or operational management. During data extraction, this framework will be refined inductively to capture additional domains reported in included sources.

Teaching and learning strategies will be categorised by instructional methods, including didactic teaching, simulation, experiential or clinical placement, e-learning or blended learning, problem-based learning, drills and tabletop exercises. Assessment approaches will be mapped using the Kirkpatrick model of educational outcomes,37 supplemented by Miller’s pyramid38 where competence or performance is assessed, and by assessment type, such as knowledge tests, objective structured clinical examinations, simulation-based assessments, workplace-based assessments and self-reported confidence. Barriers and facilitators to the design, implementation, and sustainability of programmes will be organised thematically across learner, curricular, institutional and contextual or system levels.

Findings will then be integrated in a narrative synthesis to map how residents and fellows are being prepared for armed conflict and war settings, what competencies and teaching approaches are emphasised, how outcomes are assessed, and where gaps remain across specialties, training levels, geographic regions and conflict-related contexts.

Results will be reported in accordance with PRISMA-ScR guidelines21 using a combination of tabular, graphical and narrative formats.

Stage 6: expert consultation

Consistent with the sixth (optional) stage of the Arksey and O’Malley framework,19 20 a stakeholder consultation will be undertaken to validate and contextualise the review findings. Stakeholders will comprise a purposive sample spanning (1) medical educators and GME programme directors; (2) experts in conflict, humanitarian or disaster medicine; (3) healthcare leaders from institutions in or serving conflict-affected settings and (4) early-career physicians with experience of training or practising in war-affected health systems. Approximately 8-12 stakeholders will be recruited through purposive and snowball sampling,39 selected to ensure diversity in specialty, geographic region (including conflict-affected settings) and professional role.

Preliminary findings will be shared with stakeholders in advance, and input will be collected through a structured online consultation meeting guided by brief questions set addressing the credibility and completeness of the findings, the relevance and prioritisation of identified competencies and gaps and the implications for GME. Consultations will be documented through detailed notes. Stakeholder feedback will be analysed and used to (1) verify the plausibility and comprehensiveness of the synthesis, (2) refine the classification of competencies and gaps, (3) identify priorities for future research and curriculum development and (4) inform the framing of recommendations. The consultation process and its influence on the final review will be reported transparently, and contributing stakeholders will be acknowledged with their consent.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved. Professional stakeholder consultation will be conducted to contextualise the review findings.

Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations. First, although a comprehensive search of multiple databases and grey literature sources will be conducted with no language restriction, some relevant sources, particularly informal or locally published training initiatives from conflict-affected regions, may remain unindexed and could be missed despite the use of translation support. Second, considerable heterogeneity is expected in how armed conflict and war settings are defined, conceptualised and operationalised across educational programmes, which may result in variability during data extraction and synthesis. Finally, this review focuses on formally described educational programmes and may not capture informal, ad hoc or undocumented training practices that occur in conflict settings, particularly those developed in response to acute or evolving conditions. Despite these limitations, this review will provide a structured synthesis of the existing literature and identify important gaps to inform future educational development and research in GME for conflict and war settings.

Ethics and dissemination

Since the scoping review methodology consists of reviewing and collecting data from publicly available materials, this study does not require ethics approval. The professional stakeholder consultation component will be conducted in accordance with institutional guidance and, if required, will be submitted for ethics review or exemption determination. Findings of this study will be disseminated through publication in peer-reviewed journals and at national and international conferences and can inform curricula and educational programmes in residency and fellowship programmes globally.
