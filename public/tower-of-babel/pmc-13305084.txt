Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains a common and lethal disease, with the majority of patients presenting with locally advanced or metastatic disease and experiencing progression after first-line treatment. Historically, outcomes with first-line chemotherapy only were poor, and effective later-line options were limited. The introduction of immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting the programmed death-1 (PD-1) pathway has substantially changed the treatment landscape of advanced (locally advanced or metastatic) NSCLC (aNSCLC). Nivolumab is an established PD-1 inhibitor approved for the treatment of aNSCLC after prior chemotherapy, with a well-characterized efficacy and safety profile [1–3]. Randomized phase 3 trials demonstrated improved overall survival (OS) and durable benefit with nivolumab compared with docetaxel in patients treated with prior chemotherapy, with a favorable safety profile [1, 2]. Long-term follow-up confirmed the persistence of a survival benefit in a minority of patients, highlighting the potential for durable responses [1]. In addition, real-world data from the prospective, non-interventional EVIDENS study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03382496) further corroborate the effectiveness and safety of nivolumab in less-selected real-world populations with greater clinical heterogeneity. However, the approval of immunotherapy for the first-line treatment of aNSCLC has led to emerging questions about second-line nivolumab use, treatment sequencing and rechallenge strategies [4–6].

Real-world studies suggest that outcomes of immunotherapy rechallenge are heterogeneous and may depend on factors such as duration of prior benefit, reasons for prior treatment discontinuation and intervening therapies, but available data are largely retrospective [7–9]. Consequently, there remains a need for prospective, real-world evidence that evaluates nivolumab effectiveness, safety and treatment-discontinuation patterns in patients with different immunotherapy histories, with particular attention to the feasibility of rechallenge and treatment sequencing in routine clinical care.

The Lung Initiative on Sequence Therapy (LIST) study provided the first prospective, real-world assessment of nivolumab in patient cohorts defined by prior immunotherapy exposure [10, 11]. The interim analysis demonstrated broadly similar time-to-event outcomes across immunotherapy-naïve patients and those previously exposed to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Median time to treatment discontinuation (TTD), progression-free survival (PFS) and OS were consistent with expectations for later-line aNSCLC. Treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) were generally consistent with the known safety profile of nivolumab, and quality of life (QoL) measures appeared broadly maintained among patients with available data [10, 11].

Herein we present results from the final analysis of the LIST study, which was designed to evaluate long-term treatment durability, safety and QoL outcomes.

Methods

Study Design

The LIST study was a multicenter, longitudinal, observational study conducted in routine clinical practice at 99 sites in France. The study design and overall methodology have been described previously [10, 11]. Briefly, patients were enrolled at the time of nivolumab initiation, with retrospective collection of baseline characteristics and prior treatments and prospective longitudinal collection of effectiveness, safety, QoL outcomes and subsequent anticancer therapies.

Patients were followed according to routine clinical practice for a minimum of 24 months and up to 36 months from the initiation of nivolumab therapy, until death, withdrawal of consent, loss to follow-up or end of study, whichever occurred first. No study-mandated interventions, additional procedures or visits were required. Data were entered into electronic case report forms at prespecified time points using information from the visit closest to each scheduled assessment.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments, the International Society for Pharmacoepidemiology Guidelines for Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practices and applicable regulatory requirements. All required approvals from ethics committees, independent review committees, regulatory authorities and/or other local governance bodies were obtained prior to study initiation (EudraCT/ID RCB no.: 2020-A00769-30). All patients provided oral informed consent and were permitted to withdraw from the study at any stage.

The use of the European Quality of Life 5 Dimensions 3 Level version (EQ-5D-3L) questionnaire was approved by the EuroQol Group (Rotterdam, The Netherlands). The study was registered with EuroQol in April 2020, in accordance with the applicable licensing agreement.

Patients

Eligible patients were adults (≥18 years) with pathologically confirmed aNSCLC who had initiated nivolumab after ≥ 1 prior chemotherapy-based regimen, in accordance with the approved indication and the treating physician’s independent clinical decision. Patients already receiving nivolumab as part of an interventional clinical trial at the time of enrollment were excluded. Other exclusion criteria included receipt of immunotherapy within 12 weeks prior to nivolumab initiation in the context of this study, diagnosis of another primary malignancy within the preceding 5 years and pregnancy.

Patients were stratified into three cohorts according to prior immunotherapy exposure: cohort 1, comprising immunotherapy-naïve patients; cohort 2, comprising immunotherapy-experienced patients who discontinued prior immunotherapy for reasons other than immunotherapy-related toxicity; and cohort 3, comprising immunotherapy-experienced patients who discontinued prior immunotherapy due to immunotherapy-related toxicity.

Endpoints and Outcomes

The primary endpoint was TTD, defined as the time from the initiation of nivolumab therapy to permanent discontinuation of this therapy for any reason, including disease progression, toxicity, patient decision or death. Secondary endpoints included PFS, OS, best overall response (BOR), safety outcomes and QoL.

Clinical response was assessed by the investigator according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST). BOR was defined as the highest level of response observed during follow-up under nivolumab treatment. Response rates were calculated over the total number of patients in each cohort, including patients for whom BOR was not reported. Although no formal definition or minimum treatment duration for prolonged benefit was provided to investigators, a duration of 2 years was generally regarded as the minimum treatment duration in France at the time.

Safety outcomes included all-cause adverse events (AEs) and TRAEs, which were recorded by investigators at each visit and coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA), version 19.1 or higher. QoL was assessed using the validated EQ-5D-3L questionnaire at baseline and during follow-up, using available data only (no imputation performed).

Statistical Analyses

All analyses were descriptive. Effectiveness and QoL outcomes were assessed in the analyzable population, defined as patients who were enrolled and treated with nivolumab without major inclusion or exclusion criterion deviations. Safety outcomes were assessed in the safety population, defined as patients who received ≥ 1 dose of nivolumab.

Categorical variables were summarized using counts and percentages, and continuous variables using medians and ranges. Time-to-event endpoints (TTD, OS, PFS) were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and reported using medians, discontinuation-free rates (DFR) and event-free rates at prespecified time points and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Patients were censored at the date of last available assessment or record if lost to follow-up, or at the date of enrollment into an interventional clinical trial, if applicable.

No formal hypothesis testing was performed. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 or higher (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R ® Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) software.

Results

Patients

A total of 522 patients were enrolled over a 30‑month period (September 2020–March 2023) and included in the analyzable population. Among these, 280 were immunotherapy-naïve (cohort 1) and 242 had received prior immunotherapy, which they had discontinued for reasons other than immunotherapy-related toxicity (cohort 2; n = 197) or because of immunotherapy-related toxicity (cohort 3; n = 45).

Briefly, the patient population had a median age of 70 years in cohort 1 and 66 years in cohorts 2 and 3, and the majority of patients were male (68.6%, 62.9% and 77.8% in cohorts 1, 2 and 3, respectively; Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM] Table S1). Fewer patients had non-squamous histology in cohort 1 (60.0%) than in cohorts 2 and 3 (76.1% and 75.6%, respectively). Approximately 25% of each cohort had Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) ≥ 2. The majority (60.1%) of patients in cohort 1 had PD-L1 expression < 1% at baseline, while 30.9%, and 22.2% of patients in cohorts 2 and 3, respectively, had ≥ 50% PD-L1 expression. The majority of patients were former/current smokers (89.6% of cohort 1, 88.3% of cohort 2 and 95.6% of cohort 3).

Treatment History

All patients had received prior chemotherapy, either for non-advanced disease, advanced disease or both (ESM Table S2). In cohort 1, most patients had received one line of prior systemic therapy for advanced disease (82.5%), whereas patients in cohorts 2 and 3 had been more heavily pretreated, with most patients (95.4% and 80.0%, respectively) having received ≥ 2 prior systemic therapies (ESM Table S1). Among patients who received chemotherapy for advanced disease, platinum-based doublets were the most common regimens, with carboplatin-containing combinations most frequently reported (77.5%, 80.7% and 82.2% in cohorts 1, 2 and 3, respectively). Most patients in cohorts 2 and 3 had received prior immunotherapy, mainly with pembrolizumab (ESM Table S2).

Time to Treatment Discontinuation

In the TTD analysis, the median follow-up times were 35.6, 31.4 and 30.4 months in cohort 1, 2 and 3, respectively.

At 24 months, the median TTD was 3.8 (95% CI 3.0, 4.8) months in cohort 1, 3.2 (95% CI 2.4, 3.7) months in cohort 2 and 3.4 (95% CI 2.3, 5.6) months in cohort 3 (Table 1). At 24 months, overall DFRs were 7.9% in cohort 1, 6.0% in cohort 2 and 7.1% in cohort 3. A minority of patients in all cohorts remained on nivolumab treatment at 24 months (ESM Fig. S1).

Effectiveness Outcomes

In this final analysis, the median OS in cohort 1 was 12.3 (95% CI 8.6, 13.7) months, 9.5 (7.3, 11.4) months in cohort 2 and 9.8 (7.6, 20.1) months in cohort 3 (Table 1); the corresponding 24-month OS rates were 25.8% (95% CI 20.6, 31.3), 19.0% (13.4, 25.3) and 32.2% (18.8, 46.3).

The median PFS in this final analysis was 3.2 (95% CI 2.7, 4.1) months in cohort 1, 2.6 (2.2, 3.4) months in cohort 2 and 3.9 (2.2, 7.4) months in cohort 3 (Table 1); the corresponding 24-month PFS rates were 9.5% (95% CI 6.2, 13.6), 4.8% (2.2, 8.9) and 9.3% (3.0, 20.2).

In cohort 1, complete response (CR; RECIST criteria was observed in four patients (1.4%), partial response (PR) was observed in 30 patients (10.7%) and stable disease (SD) was observed in 95 patients (33.9%). Progressive disease (PD) was reported in 117 patients (41.8%), while BOR was not reported in 34 patients (12.1%). In cohort 2, CR occurred in three patients (1.5%), PR in 15 patients (7.6%) and SD in 60 patients (30.5%). PD was observed in 82 patients (41.6%), and BOR was not reported in 37 patients (18.8%). In cohort 3, CR was reported in two patients (4.4%), PR in four patients (8.9%) and SD in 14 patients (31.1%). PD occurred in 15 patients (33.3%), while BOR was not reported in 10 patients (22.2%).

Nivolumab Discontinuation and Subsequent Anticancer Therapy

Most patients discontinued nivolumab during follow-up and the proportions of patient discontinuations were similar across the cohorts: 95.0% in cohort 1, 93.9% in cohort 2 and 91.1% in cohort 3 (ESM Table S3). The most commonly reported reason for discontinuation was disease progression, followed by toxicity, death and other reasons.

Among patients who discontinued nivolumab and regardless of the reason for nivolumab discontinuation, subsequent anticancer therapy was administered to 54.5% of patients (n = 138) in cohort 1, 53.1% of patients (n = 93) in cohort 2 and 42.5% of patients (n = 17) in cohort 3. The most frequent systemic therapy received after nivolumab was chemotherapy, which was administered to 46.6% (n = 118), 44.0% (n = 77) and 40.0% (n = 16) of patients in cohorts 1, 2 and 3, respectively. The most commonly used agents were gemcitabine (27.7% [n = 70], 22.3% [n = 39] and 22.5% [n = 9], respectively), paclitaxel (17.0% [n = 43], 12.6% [n = 22] and 10.0% [n = 4], respectively) and vinorelbine (7.9% [n = 20], 9.7% [n = 17] and 15.0% [n = 6], respectively). Subsequent immunotherapy was administered to 4.0% (n = 10), 4.6% (n = 8) and 10.0% (n = 4) of patients in cohorts 1, 2 and 3, respectively, with the most commonly administered immunotherapy being pembrolizumab (1.2% [n = 3], 2.9% [n = 5] and 7.5% [n = 3], respectively) or nivolumab readministration (2.4% [n = 6], 0.6% [n = 1] and 2.5% [n = 1], respectively). Antiangiogenic therapy (bevacizumab) was used in 3.6% (n = 9), 8.6% (n = 15) and 7.5% (n = 3) of patients in cohorts 1, 2 and 3, respectively, while targeted therapy was infrequent (5.5% [n = 14], 6.3% [n = 11] and 2.5% [n = 1], respectively). Radiotherapy was administered after nivolumab discontinuation in 15.8% (n = 40), 12.6% (n = 22) and 7.5% (n = 3) of patients in cohorts 1, 2 and 3, respectively, whereas surgery was rarely performed (0.4% [n = 1], 0.6% [n = 1] and 0%, respectively).

Safety

All-cause AEs were reported in 73.6%, 63.0% and 76.1% of patients in cohorts 1, 2 and 3, respectively; grade 3–4 all-cause AEs occurred in 41.8%, 41.0% and 43.5% of patients, respectively (Table 3).

Quality of Life

Across cohorts, median changes in EQ Visual Analogue Scale (EQ-VAS) and EQ-5D-3L index scores from baseline at 12 and 24 months were generally small among patients with available data (Table 4). At 12 months, changes in the median EQ-VAS score ranged from 0.0 to 10.0 points across cohorts, while changes in the median EQ-5D-3L index score were close to zero. At 24 months, median changes remained modest, with small positive or negative shifts observed across cohorts. The number of patients with available QoL assessments decreased during follow-up, with 66.7–85.7% of participants completing assessments of QoL at baseline and 44.3–63.6% of participants completing these assessments at 24 months; thus caution is warranted in interpreting these results.

Discussion

This final 24-month analysis of the LIST study describes the long-term use of nivolumab in aNSCLC in real-world practice, including in patients previously exposed to immunotherapy. Overall effectiveness and safety outcomes remained consistent with those reported in the interim analysis [10, 11]. The LIST study was initially designed to capture outcomes with 36 months of follow-up; however, premature termination of the study limited the availability of 36-month data for all patients. Consequently, in the present report we used the minimum of 24 months of follow-up.

Across all cohorts, median TTD was consistent with later-line treatment outcomes in routine clinical practice. As observed in other real-world nivolumab cohorts, most patients discontinue treatment within the first few months, predominantly due to disease progression or death [4, 10, 11]. However, longer follow-up demonstrated that a proportion of patients remained on treatment or were event-free at 12 and 24 months. This pattern is consistent with long-term follow-up from both clinical trials and real-world studies, which indicates that a proportion of patients demonstrate prolonged benefit [12].

In immunotherapy-naïve patients (cohort 1), the real-world OS and PFS results of nivolumab monotherapy were consistent with the findings of the CheckMate 017 and 057 clinical trials and numerically similar to those reported in the interim LIST analysis [11]. Moreover, more than half of the patients in cohort 1 had a PD‑L1 expression < 1%, suggesting that PD‑L1 status may have played a role in first‑line treatment selection, favoring chemotherapy alone over immunotherapy. Numerically higher 24-month DFRs were observed in specific subgroups, and DFR findings generally suggested nivolumab monotherapy as a suitable later-line treatment for patients who do not receive immunotherapy as first-line therapy.

Outcomes in patients previously treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors, including those with prior immunotherapy-related toxicity, suggest that rechallenge with nivolumab could be possible in selected patients, in line with the findings of the interim analysis [10]. The 24-month DFR in cohort 3 was similar to that of immunotherapy-naïve patients in cohort 1. In cohort 2, the 24-month DFR was higher in patients with prolonged benefit from prior immunotherapy (27.8%) than in patients who had discontinued prior immunotherapy due to disease progression (4.8%). Median OS and PFS values remained within the expected range for previously treated aNSCLC in cohorts 2 and 3. Subgroup analyses of DFRs support the patterns observed at the interim analysis across clinically relevant subgroups, notably numerically higher DFRs in younger patients, in those with better ECOG PS and in those who demonstrated prolonged benefit with prior immunotherapy, reinforcing the view that these factors may influence rechallenge results [11]. These observations are also consistent with retrospective series reporting non-negligible benefit from immunotherapy rechallenge in carefully selected populations [9, 13].

The safety profile observed with extended follow-up remained consistent with the established experience of nivolumab in aNSCLC. Overall rates of all-cause AEs and TRAEs were comparable with those reported at the interim analysis and aligned with long-term real-world and clinical trial data [10, 12, 14]. Tolerability was acceptable across all the cohorts, even in cohort 3 for which, despite a marginal increase in grade 3 and 4 TRAEs, the overall TRAEs were similar to those recorded in the immunotherapy-naïve group (cohort 1). This finding suggests that, although patients in cohort 3 discontinued prior immunotherapy due to immunotherapy-related toxicity, immunotherapy rechallenge could potentially still be feasible for these patients. However, the observed lack of excessive toxicity may be partly explained by the under-reporting of AEs in routine clinical practice.

The most frequently reported TRAEs reflected known PD-1-associated toxicities, including endocrine, gastrointestinal, dermatologic and constitutional effects. Importantly, longer observation did not reveal new or unexpected safety signals. TRAEs leading to death remained rare and unchanged from those recorded in the interim analysis, providing reassurance that extended nivolumab exposure in routine practice does not appear to be associated with emergent late toxicities.

Among patients with available EQ-5D data, median scores appeared to be generally stable over time, suggesting preservation of QoL in those patients remaining in the study. However, the number of evaluable patients and completion rates decreased with longer follow-up. Thus, the findings of the present study should be viewed as descriptive and reflecting outcomes in the subset of patients who were still in the study at 12 and 24 months and actively filled the questionnaires.

The strengths of this study include its prospective, multicenter real-world design; stratification by prior immunotherapy exposure; use of pragmatic endpoints relevant to routine practice; and extended follow-up allowing assessment of durability, discontinuation patterns and post-nivolumab sequencing. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The observational design precludes causal inference and is subject to confounding and selection bias. Missing data, particularly for BOR and QoL assessments at later time points, limit interpretability. Cohort 3 was small, warranting caution in interpreting rechallenge-related results, and findings may not be fully generalizable beyond the French healthcare setting. Furthermore, dynamic changes in PD‑L1 expression and tumor immune microenvironment-related parameters were not prospectively collected in the context of this study, limiting the feasibility of analyses on mechanisms underlying nivolumab discontinuation due to disease progression. As the tumor immune microenvironment is thought to play a role in shaping immunotherapy resistance and responses to rechallenge, further investigation of prognostic immune-related biomarkers reflecting immune infiltration levels, which have been shown to correlate with outcomes in solid tumors such as lung cancer, would be of interest [15].

Finally, this study used a purely descriptive approach, and no statistical tests were conducted. Therefore, the results of the subgroup analyses are only suggestive, and additional investigation is required to draw solid conclusions regarding factors affecting rechallenge results.

Conclusions

This final 24-month analysis of the LIST study suggests that the real-world effectiveness and safety of nivolumab in patients with aNSCLC is consistent with the outcomes of clinical trials in immunotherapy-naïve patients. Additionally, immunotherapy rechallenge with nivolumab appeared to be a feasible option in patients with prior immunotherapy exposure, with consistent tolerability in patients who experienced prior immunotherapy-related toxicity. Moreover, specific subgroups of patients seemed to demonstrate a particular benefit, warranting further investigation to determine the factors and patient/disease characteristics associated with optimal outcomes of immunotherapy rechallenge with nivolumab.
