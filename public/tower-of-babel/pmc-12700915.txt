Elucidating the molecular compatibility mechanism to guide the optimization of Straw-Derived asphalt
Authors: Weishuai Ji, Shuangshuang Liang, Wenyuan Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Amid the growing focus on sustainable development, the integration of renewable materials, such as straw liquefied residue oil (SLRO), into asphalt engineering is gaining significant attention. However, the underlying compatibility mechanisms and their impact on performance remain inadequately understood. This study offers a thorough investigation of the SLRO-asphalt system by combining molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with multi-scale experimental techniques. MD simulations revealed that intermolecular interactions, primarily hydrogen bonding, increase with higher SLRO content, peaking at 10 wt.%, which facilitates the formation of the most cohesive molecular network. Experimental results confirmed that this enhanced network exerts a dual influence on performance. Dynamic shear rheometer (DSR) tests demonstrated that the 10% SLRO blend significantly improved high-temperature rutting resistance following short-term aging. In contrast, bending beam rheometer (BBR) tests on long-term aged samples indicated that the same stiffening mechanism reduced low-temperature thermal cracking resistance. This study clarifies the microscopic origins of this performance trade-off, suggesting that the optimal SLRO content is context-dependent, requiring a balance between rutting and cracking resistance rather than an absolute value. These findings provide a crucial mechanistic foundation for the rational design and performance-driven optimization of sustainable bio-asphalts.

Introduction

The rising cost and finite nature of petroleum-based asphalt have driven extensive research into alternative and modified binders to ensure the long-term sustainability of road infrastructure1–4. Current asphalt modification efforts primarily focus on three main categories: (1) polymeric modifiers, including styrene–butadiene–styrene block copolymer (SBS), polyethylene, and thermoplastic polyurethane elastomers (TPU)5–10; (2) recycled materials, such as waste plastics (PE, PP, PVC) and rubber powder11–16, along with innovative approaches like the encapsulation of waste oils or the utilization of industrial by-products17–19; and (3) bio-based modifiers, encompassing waste edible oils, palm oil, castor oil, and their epoxidized, esterified, or acrylic acid derivatives20–25. Although these modifiers have significantly advanced asphalt performance, certain challenges persist. For instance, while polymer modifiers can greatly enhance the mechanical properties of asphalt, they remain costly and vulnerable to aging under specific environmental conditions26. The use of recycled materials facilitates resource recycling; however, their performance stability remains suboptimal, and they may introduce impurities. Additionally, the large-scale application of bio-based modifiers still requires further technical and economic validation.

In the context of global sustainable development and carbon neutrality, the use of plant-based materials in road engineering has garnered increasing attention27,28. Bio-oils, a novel category of road construction materials, offer benefits such as low carbon emissions, environmental sustainability, and renewability29. This innovative material not only reduces dependence on conventional petroleum-based resources but also contributes significantly to the reduction of carbon emissions28–30. However, compatibility issues between plant-based oils and traditional asphalt binders remain a significant barrier to their widespread adoption31,32. Recent studies utilizing advanced characterization techniques and molecular simulations have begun to uncover the complex interactions between bio-oil composition and asphalt compatibility33,34. Despite this progress, a consensus on the underlying mechanisms is still evolving. A deeper understanding of the physicochemical interactions between these materials is crucial for ensuring the stability, performance, and durability of modified asphalts.

In recent years, straw liquefied residue oil (SLRO)—an important byproduct of the plant-based chemical alcohol process using straw as the raw material—has attracted significant research interest, primarily due to its substantial economic potential35–38. China is endowed with abundant straw resources, characterized by high yields, stable availability, and relatively uniform composition, which exerts minimal influence on the composition of SLRO39. Consequently, this study focuses on SLRO, derived from the straw-to-alcohol process, as the primary subject of investigation. In contrast to traditional petroleum-based modifiers, such as styrene–butadiene–styrene (SBS) and waste plastics, research on plant-based modifiers remains relatively underexplored, highlighting the importance of this study in addressing the existing gap in the field. Significant progress has been made in the study of straw-based asphalts. Ding Zhan et al.40 incorporated straw liquefaction products into matrix asphalt to produce bio-asphalt, whose penetration, softening point, and ductility met the required standards, while also improving its low-temperature performance. Lei Yong41 found that bio-asphalt, prepared by blending straw cracking oil with rubber powder, significantly enhanced the compatibility of the rubber powder, as well as the high-temperature performance and fatigue resistance. Fang Jinmiao et al.42 demonstrated that high-temperature extraction of biomass heavy oil from straw notably improves the rheological properties of aged asphalt.

However, current research on straw-derived asphalts, particularly those derived from the straw-to-alcohol process, primarily focuses on their physical properties, with limited attention given to their compatibility. Specifically, a systematic investigation linking the overall chemical nature of SLRO to the resulting intermolecular network, and subsequently connecting this network to the binder’s multifaceted, temperature-dependent performance, remains unexplored. Furthermore, the majority of research approaches are predominantly experimental, which inherently limits their scope. The rapid advancement of MD simulation technology has provided materials science with powerful computational tools43. In recent years, MD simulations have become indispensable in asphalt research, frequently incorporated into multi-scale modeling frameworks to bridge molecular behavior with macroscopic performance44,45. MD simulations can reveal the microscopic behavior of materials at the molecular scale, offering novel insights into the interaction mechanisms between asphalt and modifiers. By leveraging MD simulations, researchers are now able to conduct comprehensive investigations into key parameters of the asphalt-modifier system, such as solubility parameters, intermolecular interaction energies, and diffusion characteristics, thereby providing theoretical guidance for the selection of efficient and sustainable modifiers.

Building upon this background, this study adopts an integrated approach combining MD simulations with multi-scale experiments to systematically investigate the compatibility mechanism of SLRO in asphalt. The primary novelty of this study lies in establishing a “theory-to-performance” linkage that elucidates the dual role of the intermolecular network formed by SLRO. Specifically, we demonstrate how this network simultaneously enhances high-temperature rutting resistance while compromising low-temperature flexibility, thereby revealing a critical performance trade-off. This mechanistic insight provides a scientific foundation for true performance-based optimization of bio-asphalts, offering a guiding framework for the rational design of sustainable pavement materials.

Materials and methods

Materials

Matrix asphalt

The matrix asphalt used in this study was a 90-grade penetration petroleum asphalt, commonly employed in highway construction in northern China. It was sourced from Panjin, Liaoning Province. The asphalt’s fundamental properties were characterized following the “Standard Test Methods of Bitumen and Bituminous Mixtures for Highway Engineering” (JTGE20-2011). The asphalt exhibited a penetration of 82.5 (0.1 mm) at 25 °C, a softening point of 47.8 °C, and a ductility of 122 cm at 15 °C, all of which conform to the standard specifications for 90-grade asphalt.

Straw liquefied residue oil (SLRO)

The modifier, straw liquefied residue oil (SLRO), is a viscous, dark liquid produced as a co-product in an industrial facility that manufactures bio-polyols through the catalytic hydrogenation of hydrolyzed corn straw. Its density at 25 °C was measured to be 1.254 g/cm3. The appearance of the SLRO is depicted in Fig. 1.

Preparation of straw-derived asphalt

The SLRO-derived asphalt blends were prepared using a high-shear mixer. The matrix asphalt was first heated in a container until it became fluid. A specified amount of SLRO was then added to the heated asphalt to achieve the target weight percentages (e.g., 5, 10, or 15 wt.%). The mixture was subsequently subjected to mechanical stirring at 6500 rpm for 60 min to ensure uniformity.

For the initial process optimization study, blending was conducted at various temperatures (80, 100, 120, and 150 °C). For all subsequent systematic performance evaluations, the blends were prepared at a constant, predetermined optimal temperature.

Simulation details

All MD simulations were performed in Materials Studio with the COMPASS III force field. Prior to data collection, each asphalt system underwent a rigorous relaxation procedure: first, a geometry optimization was performed, followed by a 1 ns (1000 ps) simulated annealing process. Subsequently, a 500 ps dynamic simulation in the isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensemble was conducted to achieve a stable density. The final production run for data analysis was a 500 ps simulation in the canonical (NVT) ensemble.

Throughout the simulations, the temperature was controlled by the Nosé-Hoover thermostat and the pressure by the Berendsen barostat. A time step of 1.0 fs was used, and a cutoff distance of 12.5 Å was applied for van der Waals interactions, while the Ewald method handled long-range electrostatics. Hydrogen bonds were defined by a distance cutoff of 2.5 Å and an angle cutoff of 90° (Donor-H-Acceptor). The Radial Distribution Function (RDF), g(r), between the centers of mass of SLRO molecules was calculated with a cutoff radius of 20.0 Å and an interval of 0.02 Å to analyze aggregation behavior.

By analyzing the molecular mean displacement curve, it is possible to determine whether the system has reached equilibrium. During the early stages of relaxation, the displacement of molecules may increase rapidly over time, resulting in a steep slope of the curve, indicating that the molecules are still adjusting their positions. As simulation time progresses, the system’s energy gradually stabilizes, and molecular movement becomes increasingly stable. The free volume characteristics of the asphalt model were quantitatively assessed using the Atom Volumes & Surfaces module within the Materials Studio software suite. The fraction of free volume (FFV) is defined as the ratio of the unoccupied free space volume within the system to the total model volume. FFV is a crucial parameter for characterizing the microscopic structural features of materials, as it provides insight into the packing density of molecules and the spatial constraints governing molecular motion. It plays a significant role in influencing the diffusion, mechanical properties, and thermodynamic behavior of materials.

The Connolly Surface method was employed in the calculation process to characterize the free volume, with the probe radius set at 1.0 Å to simulate the diffusion behavior of typical small molecules within the asphalt system. Figure 7 illustrates the distribution of free volume within the asphalt model, where the blue region represents the spatial distribution of free volume. The calculation results indicate that under room temperature conditions (298 K), the fraction of free volume within the asphalt model is 17.56%. This value is consistent with the reported range of free volume fractions in asphalt systems (typically 15–20%) found in the literature49,50, further validating the accuracy and reliability of the molecular model constructed in this study.

Experimental characterization

FTIR spectroscopy

The chemical functional groups of the pure SLRO, matrix asphalt, and the 10 wt.% straw-derived asphalt were analyzed using a Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (model Bruker Tensor 27). The spectra were recorded in the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1.

Rheological performance tests

The rheological properties of all asphalt binders were evaluated using a Dynamic Shear Rheometer (DSR) and a Bending Beam Rheometer (BBR).

An Anton Paar SmartPave 102 DSR was used to characterize the high and intermediate-temperature performance in accordance with AASHTO T315. Temperature sweep tests were conducted from 20 to 80 °C at a constant angular frequency of 10 rad/s (1.59 Hz) to determine the high-temperature performance grade (PG). For these tests, a 25 mm parallel plate geometry with a 1 mm sample gap was used. The complex shear modulus (G*) and phase angle (δ) were recorded.

The low-temperature creep behavior was assessed using a Bending Beam Rheometer (BBR) following the AASHTO T313 standard. The tests were conducted at − 12 °C and − 18 °C to measure the creep stiffness (S) and the creep rate (m-value) at a loading time of 60 s. These parameters were used to determine the low-temperature PG of the binders.

Artificial aging procedure

To simulate the aging of the asphalt binders during production and service life, all samples were subjected to standardized short-term and long-term aging procedures. Short-term aging was performed using the Rolling Thin-Film Oven (RTFO) test according to AASHTO T240. Subsequently, the RTFO-aged residues were subjected to long-term aging in a pressurized aging vessel (PAV) following the AASHTO R28 standard. The rheological properties of the unaged, RTFO-aged, and PAV-aged binders were then evaluated.

Effect of SLRO content on molecular dynamics and structure

To systematically investigate the mechanistic effects of SLRO content, the asphalt systems were simulated at 298 K and analyzed from multiple perspectives. Initially, we examined the molecular mobility and dispersion state of the modifier using mean square displacement (MSD) and radial distribution function (RDF) analysis. Subsequently, the strength of the intermolecular interactions was quantified by analyzing the cohesive energy density (CED), interaction energy, and hydrogen bonding.Molecular diffusion and dispersion state

As shown in Fig. 8a, the mobility of molecules in the system exhibits a non-monotonic trend with increasing SLRO content. The diffusion coefficient (DC), derived from the slope of the MSD curve, initially increases from 5.75 cm2/s at 2.5% to a peak of 19.55 cm2/s at 7.5%. This suggests that at lower concentrations, SLRO molecules may function as plasticizers or “lubricants,” disrupting the strong, ordered stacking of asphaltene molecules, thereby increasing the overall free volume and enhancing the molecular mobility of the system.

Paradoxically, when the SLRO content is further increased to 10 wt.%, the diffusion coefficient sharply decreases to 2.54 cm2/s, the lowest value observed among all systems. This significant reduction in mobility might initially suggest poor compatibility, such as the self-aggregation of SLRO molecules. To test this hypothesis, the radial distribution function (RDF) between the centers of mass of SLRO molecules was calculated (Fig. 8b).

However, the RDF results directly refute the self-aggregation hypothesis. An RDF peak signifies a higher probability of finding molecules at a specific distance from one another. As shown in Fig. 8b, the intensity of the first RDF peak decreases monotonically as the SLRO concentration increases from 2.5 to 10%. The 10 wt.% system exhibits the lowest and broadest peak, providing clear evidence that the SLRO molecules are, in fact, most effectively dispersed and least aggregated at this concentration.

Combining these findings, a more comprehensive mechanism emerges. At 10 wt.%, the SLRO molecules appear to be optimally dispersed, acting as molecular “bridges” or crosslinkers, forming an extensive and robust intermolecular network with the polar components of the asphalt matrix. This powerful, system-wide network likely restricts the mobility of all molecules (both SLRO and asphalt), resulting in the observed minimum in the diffusion coefficient. Therefore, the anomalous decrease in diffusion at 10% is interpreted not as a sign of poor compatibility (phase separation), but rather as an indicator of maximum intermolecular interaction and the formation of a highly structured, cohesive system. Interaction energy is a key measure of material compatibility and is commonly used to characterize the strength of interactions between different molecules. In this study, the interaction energy of straw-derived asphalt with varying SLRO concentrations was calculated using molecular dynamics simulations, as shown in Fig. 10.

As illustrated in Fig. 10, the interaction energy between SLRO and asphalt remains negative across all concentrations. With increasing SLRO content, the strength of non-bonding interactions demonstrates a notable upward trend, with van der Waals forces predominantly contributing to the total interaction energy. This phenomenon can be attributed to the robust intermolecular attraction between the polar SLRO molecules and the polar components within the asphalt matrix, including asphaltenes and resins. In contrast, electrostatic interactions exhibit negligible variation across the doping range. Given that the absolute value of the interaction energy is positively correlated with the stability of the composite system, the results suggest that structural stability is optimized when the SLRO mass fraction reaches 10%.

Effect of temperature on the compatibility of straw-derived asphalt

In the preparation of straw-derived asphalt, both SLRO and matrix asphalt must be heated to a specified temperature range before uniform mixing. This heating process significantly affects the compatibility of the two components, with the underlying mechanism primarily manifested in changes to molecular motion, interphase diffusion rates, and other pertinent physicochemical properties. Given the critical role of temperature parameters in material compatibility, this section examines the compatibility behavior of SLRO and matrix asphalt at 353K, 373K, 393K, and 413K (equivalent to 80, 100, 120, and 140 °C), with the goal of elucidating the intrinsic relationship between temperature and compatibility, thereby laying a theoretical foundation for optimizing the preparation process of straw-derived asphalt.Analysis of SLRO molecular diffusion behavior

Temperature is a key thermodynamic parameter that governs the diffusion behavior. In this study, molecular dynamics simulations were employed to systematically investigate the molecular motion characteristics of the straw-derived asphalt system under varying temperature conditions. The diffusion kinetic parameters were derived by analyzing the Mean Square Displacement (MSD) curves of the system. Fig. 12MSD curves and diffusion coefficients of straw-derived asphalt systems with different SLRO blends under temperature gradients.

Diffusion curves represent the variation in substance concentration as a function of time and space. A rapid and uniform diffusion rate typically indicates good compatibility, whereas a slow or irregular rate may suggest poor compatibility. The diffusion coefficient is a parameter used to quantify the diffusion capacity of a substance, with a larger value signifying a higher diffusion rate. A higher diffusion coefficient is typically associated with enhanced compatibility, whereas a lower diffusion coefficient may suggest poor compatibility.

The results presented in Fig. 12 show that increasing temperature significantly enhances the MSD and diffusion coefficient (DC) of the system, suggesting that molecular motion constraints are reduced at elevated temperatures, thereby enhancing diffusion capacity. This is due to the increase in temperature, which raises molecular kinetic energy and promotes molecular motion. At low SLRO content (2.5 wt.%), SLRO molecules display a higher diffusion coefficient due to increased available free space; however, this value stabilizes when the temperature reaches 393K. In contrast, the diffusion coefficient of 10 wt.% SLRO continuously increases with temperature, exhibiting the most significant increase at 413K, indicating that higher SLRO content significantly enhances molecular motion and improves the compatibility of the asphalt system.(2)Solubility parameter

According to the Hildebrand Solubility Parameter Theory, the closer the δ values of two substances, the more similar their intermolecular forces, thereby enhancing compatibility. As shown in Fig. 13, the difference in solubility parameters (Δδ) between SLRO and matrix asphalt first increases and then decreases nonlinearly as the SLRO mass fraction increases. When the SLRO content reaches 10 wt.%, Δδ reaches its minimum value, indicating that the straw-derived asphalt system achieves optimal compatibility at this point. Further analysis reveals that increasing temperature significantly promotes the reduction of Δδ, which can be attributed to enhanced molecular thermal motion and facilitated molecular diffusion between components due to the temperature rise. However, simulation results demonstrate that even under optimal conditions (high temperature, high SLRO content), Δδ remains relatively high, suggesting that relying solely on thermodynamic factors is insufficient for achieving full compatibility within the system. Interaction energy

Temperature, as a key thermodynamic parameter regulating molecular dynamics, plays a significant role in modulating the intermolecular interaction energy within the asphalt system. Figure 14 illustrates the quantitative results of the SLRO binding energy to matrix asphalt, obtained through molecular dynamics simulations, as a function of SLRO mass fraction (0–10 wt.%) and temperature (353–413 K).

Fig. 14Interaction energy of SLRO to matrix asphalt at different temperatures.

Interaction energy of SLRO to matrix asphalt at different temperatures.

Research indicates a significant positive correlation between interaction energy and the SLRO mass fraction, consistent with previous findings. Thermodynamic theory suggests that an increase in temperature leads to higher molecular kinetic energy, thereby weakening intermolecular non-covalent interactions, such as van der Waals forces and electrostatic interactions, ultimately reducing the overall interaction energy of the system. Notably, the straw-derived asphalt with 10 wt.% SLRO shows the greatest reduction in interaction energy (4.94%) within the temperature range from 373 to 393K. This phenomenon suggests the existence of an optimal temperature range for the effect of temperature on system performance, beyond which excessively high temperatures may disrupt the intermolecular interaction network. Therefore, temperature control should follow the principle of moderation, rather than merely pursuing high-temperature conditions.

In summary, molecular dynamics simulations reveal a positive correlation between SLRO content and the structural strength of straw-derived asphalt. Specifically, as the SLRO mass fraction increases, the molecular diffusion coefficient of the system at elevated temperatures increases significantly, leading to a marked improvement in compatibility. However, it is important to note that while high temperatures promote molecular diffusion and enhance compatibility, excessively high temperatures can excessively reduce intermolecular interactions, potentially hindering system performance optimization. The simulation results indicate that the optimal SLRO content is 10%, and the temperature should not exceed 393K. Based on the aforementioned findings, this study will further investigate, through experimental work, the effect of different stirring temperatures on the compatibility of straw-derived asphalt, with the goal of determining the optimal process parameters.

Experimental validation and microscopic analysis

MD simulations predict that adding 10 wt.% SLRO forms the strongest intermolecular interaction network with the asphalt matrix. To translate this theoretical advantage into macroscopic performance, it is essential to first determine the optimal preparation process parameters. Therefore, this section investigates the effects of different mixing temperatures on the physical properties and stability of 10 wt.% SLRO-derived asphalt. The goal is to identify the optimal temperature range that promotes full compatibility while preventing thermal damage to the material. The data in Table 5 indicate that as the mixing temperature increases, the binder’s penetration decreases, while its softening point increases, reflecting a high-temperature hardening effect. Notably, the ductility—a key indicator of material flexibility—first increases and then decreases, peaking at 9.1 cm at 100 °C. When the temperature is further raised to 150 °C, the ductility decreases sharply to 2.9 cm, indicating significant thermal damage and embrittlement.

A more critical observation pertains to the thermal stability of the blend. Trace bubbles began to appear in the mixture at 120 °C, and this phenomenon became more pronounced at 150 °C. This macroscopic observation strongly supports our MD simulation results, which predicted that excessively high temperatures (> 120 °C) would weaken intermolecular interaction energies, resulting in system instability. The bubbles observed experimentally are likely direct evidence of the volatilization of low-boiling-point compounds or the thermal degradation of heat-sensitive components in SLRO, serving as a macroscopic manifestation of molecular-level instability.

Considering both ductility and thermal stability, the ideal mixing temperature should be close to the ductility peak while remaining well below the onset temperature for bubble formation. Therefore, the temperature range of 100 °C to 110 °C was identified as the optimal processing window for preparing the straw-derived asphalt. All subsequent samples for systematic performance evaluation were prepared at 100 °C to ensure the accuracy and comparability of the results.

Chemical interaction analysis by FTIR spectroscopy

To experimentally validate the microscopic interaction mechanisms predicted by the MD simulations, Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) was utilized. Based on the simulation results indicating that the 10 wt.% SLRO blend exhibited the strongest intermolecular interactions, we selected matrix asphalt (0%), pure SLRO, and the representative 10 wt.% SLRO-derived asphalt for comparative analysis. The primary objectives were to determine the nature of the blending process (physical or chemical) and to identify direct evidence of the functional group interactions responsible for the enhanced compatibility. The resulting spectra are shown in Fig. 15.

Fig. 15Infrared spectra of (a) SLRO, (b) matrix asphalt, (c) and 10wt.% straw-derived asphalt, along with a (d) comparative infrared spectrum diagram.

Infrared spectra of (a) SLRO, (b) matrix asphalt, (c) and 10wt.% straw-derived asphalt, along with a (d) comparative infrared spectrum diagram.

The FTIR spectrum of pure SLRO (Fig. 15a) exhibits a prominent broad absorption band between 3000 and 3500 cm−1, characteristic of hydroxyl (-OH) and amine (–NH) stretching vibrations. It also presents peaks for methyl/methylene groups (2800–3000 cm−1), C=C stretching (around 1600 cm−1), and C–O–C asymmetric stretching (around 1087 cm−1), indicating a complex chemical composition rich in polar functional groups. The spectrum of matrix asphalt (Fig. 15b) is dominated by aliphatic C–H stretching (2800–3000 cm−1) and bending (1455 and 1374 cm−1) vibrations, with a minor peak for aromatic C=C stretching (1604 cm−1), reflecting its primarily non-polar, hydrocarbon nature.

Notably, the comparative spectrum in Fig. 15d reveals two key findings. First, the spectrum of the 10 wt.% SLRO-derived asphalt appears to be a combination of the spectra of matrix asphalt and SLRO. No new characteristic absorption peaks are observed, strongly indicating that the blending process at 100 °C is predominantly physical, without significant chemical reactions. This confirms the chemical stability of the blend, which is a vital characteristic for pavement materials.

Second, and more importantly, a significant intensification of the broad –OH/–NH band (3000–3500 cm−1) is observed in the 10 wt.% blend compared to matrix asphalt. This directly confirms the introduction of a high concentration of hydroxyl and other polar groups into the asphalt system by SLRO molecules. This experimental observation provides compelling evidence that strongly supports the MD simulation results, which predicted a substantial increase in the number of hydrogen bonds at the 10 wt.% SLRO content. The abundance of these hydrogen bond acceptors from SLRO molecules promotes the formation of a robust intermolecular hydrogen bonding network with the polar components (e.g., heteroatoms in asphaltenes and resins) of matrix asphalt. This enhanced intermolecular crosslinking is a key microscopic mechanism responsible for the increased cohesive energy density and improved compatibility of the system, as predicted by the simulations.

To experimentally investigate the influence of SLRO content on binder performance, a series of blends with varying SLRO dosages (0, 5, 10, and 15 wt.%) were prepared at the optimal temperature of 100 °C. These specific dosages were selected for the experimental phase to cover a practical range around the 10 wt.% content, identified as the most promising by the MD simulations. As shown in Fig. 16, the G*/sinδ values of the unaged binders are relatively similar, with all modified binders exhibiting slightly lower or comparable rutting factors to the matrix asphalt (1.40 kPa). This suggests that, before aging, SLRO functions as a softening agent. However, a significant shift is observed after RTFOT aging, which simulates the binder’s state after the plant mixing and paving process. The addition of SLRO significantly improves the high-temperature performance of the aged binders. This stiffening effect upon aging may be attributed to the potential polymerization of certain compounds within the SLRO at high temperatures, consistent with the strong intermolecular interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonding) predicted by the MD simulations.

Low-temperature creep behavior by BBR

The binder’s resistance to thermal cracking at low temperatures was evaluated using the BBR on PAV-aged samples, in accordance with the AASHTO T313 standard. The tests were conducted at two distinct temperatures, − 12 °C and − 18 °C, to evaluate performance under both moderate and severe cold conditions. Two key parameters were analyzed: creep stiffness (S), which measures resistance to deformation, and creep rate (m-value), which indicates the ability to relax thermal stresses. For adequate performance, Superpave specifications require S ≤ 300 MPa and m ≥ 0.300.

The influence of SLRO on the binders’ low-temperature properties is shown in Fig. 17. At − 12 °C, all samples performed adequately, meeting the stiffness (S ≤ 300 MPa) and creep rate (m ≥ 0.300) criteria. However, at the more severe temperature of − 18 °C, all binders failed to meet the criteria. Notably, even the unmodified matrix asphalt failed to meet the requirements at this temperature. The addition of SLRO further worsened this performance, causing a consistent increase in stiffness (Fig. 17a) and a general decrease in the m-value (Fig. 17b), which indicates an increased risk of thermal cracking.

These results clearly demonstrate the detrimental effect of SLRO on the binder’s long-term, low-temperature durability, reinforcing the central thesis of a performance trade-off. This phenomenon, where high-temperature stiffness improves at the expense of low-temperature flexibility, aligns with findings for other bio-oils51,52. This study suggests that the same strong intermolecular network responsible for superior rutting resistance—predicted by MD simulations—also restricts molecular mobility at low temperatures, leading to embrittlement. This classic trade-off highlights that, while SLRO is an effective rutting-resistance enhancer, its optimal content must be carefully selected. Interestingly, some studies have shown that this limitation can be overcome through co-modification. For instance, Zhang et al.53 found that a composite of bio-oil and lignin improved both high- and low-temperature properties, suggesting a promising direction for future optimization.
