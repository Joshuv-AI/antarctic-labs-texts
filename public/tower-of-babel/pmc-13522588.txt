Data-driven catalyst design for direct catalytic N2O decomposition
Authors: Chenxi He, Shinya Mine, Yuan Jing, Tsz Lok Wan, Jialei Zhang, Junxian Qin, Ningqiang Zhang, Koichiro Taketoshi, Akihiko Anzai, Ryo Toyoshima, Hiroshi Kondoh, Ichigaku Takigawa, Ken-ichi Shimizu, Takashi Toyao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Catalytic N2O decomposition in the presence of O2 is a key process for addressing environmental challenges, such as greenhouse gas emissions and ozone layer depletion. However, the identification of efficient catalysts for this reaction remains challenging owing to the limitations of conventional methods. In this study, we employ a machine learning approach designed to accelerate the discovery of effective direct N2O decomposition catalysts. Starting with 51 catalysts and conducting 37 cycles of a closed-loop discovery system (machine-learning prediction + experiment), 633 catalysts are experimentally tested. Over 10 multi-elemental catalysts exhibiting superior activity are identified, surpassing the performance of the originally identified best catalyst. Among them, Rh(1)–Pd(2)/ZrO2_EP exhibits the highest catalytic performance for N2O decomposition. Through control experiments and a combination of ex situ and in situ characterizations, we identify the essential role of each component within the catalyst system.

Introduction

Nitrous oxide (N2O) is a potent greenhouse gas with a global warming potential that is approximately 300 times greater than that of carbon dioxide (CO2) on a per-mass basis. In addition to its contribution to climate change, N2O is a major ozone-depleting substance because it can reach the stratosphere and undergo photolytic decomposition, releasing nitrogen oxides (NOx) that catalyze ozone breakdown1,2. Given a rapid increase in N2O emissions from agricultural practices, industrial activities, and fossil fuel combustion, controlling its level in the atmosphere has become a pressing environmental priority3,4. Therefore, the development of efficient strategies for N2O abatement is of great significance to both climate protection and ozone layer preservation.

Direct decomposition5–9 and reduction approaches10–21 are generally considered the two main catalytic strategies for N2O emission control. Direct catalytic decomposition is particularly attractive because of its efficiency and the absence of external reducing agents, which lowers the operational costs. Over the past few decades, a wide range of catalysts, including supported noble and metal oxides22–25, composite oxides such as perovskites26–28, hydrotalcites7,29, spinels30–33, and zeolite-based catalysts17,34–37, have been explored for direct N2O decomposition. However, these catalysts typically suffer from reduced activity under practical conditions where common inhibitors such as O2 are present31,38–40. This limitation highlights the need for robust catalysts that can maintain a high activity in the presence of O2, making them viable for practical applications.

Among the catalysts studied, Rh is generally considered one of the most effective for N2O decomposition, especially in the presence of O238,41–44. Our previous research has demonstrated that supported RhOx catalysts are particularly effective at a relatively low temperature (~350 °C), with RhOx/ZrO2 containing 5 wt.% Rh identified as one of the best performers45. However, Rh-based catalysts still require temperatures above 350 °C to achieve sufficient activity under practical conditions. In addition, the high cost of Rh, which exceeds that of most other platinum-group metals, remains a significant barrier to its widespread application. These limitations underscore the need for the development of more active and cost-effective catalytic systems.

Recent developments in molecular and materials informatics have led to a paradigm shift in molecular and materials science, enabling the rational design of functional molecules and materials46–53. Although artificial intelligence (AI) has demonstrated proof-of-concept success in accelerating catalyst discovery and significantly reducing the associated time and cost, most studies have been limited to benchmark problems, with few cases leading to the discovery of fundamentally new molecules, materials, or synthetic transformations54,55. A major challenge is that machine learning (ML) models typically focus on interpolation within known data rather than extrapolation to unknown high-performance candidates. This limitation is particularly pronounced in catalysis informatics, where the underlying reactions involve dynamic and time-dependent phenomena46,54,56. Furthermore, compared with materials informatics, catalysis research suffers from a lack of large, high-quality datasets because of the prohibitive computational costs of accurately modeling heterogeneous catalysts57–60. High-throughput experimental methods for catalysis that have successfully advanced other scientific fields remain underdeveloped61–63. Moreover, the scarcity of negative results in catalytic research poses a significant challenge for data-driven approaches64. To overcome these challenges, it is critical to develop ML models that can effectively identify novel catalysts within diverse chemical spaces using experimental catalytic data56,62,65–68.

In this context, our group has adopted an ML framework that represents catalyst compositions exclusively through continuous, physically meaningful elemental descriptors, such as electronegativity and melting point, rather than using explicit element-identity representations (e.g., one-hot encoding or categorical compositional vectors)69,70. Each catalyst is represented as a weighted combination of elemental descriptors based on the proportion of each element69,70. As a result, the model can propose catalyst compositions containing elements that were not present in the original training dataset. The predictions made here are not strictly “true extrapolation” in a data-science sense; rather, they are largely interpolative within the continuous descriptor space defined by physicochemical parameters. However, from a materials and catalysis perspective, this capability can be regarded as “extrapolative”, because it enables the suggestion of promising elements that were not explored in the original dataset. Other studies have confirmed the effectiveness of this strategy using relevant feature engineering and selection techniques71. In addition to this theoretical foundation69,70,72, we recently applied this approach to the development of multi-elemental reverse water-gas shift catalysts73. The composition of the optimal catalyst was Pt/Rb–Ba–Mo–Nb/TiO2. Notably, Nb was not originally present in the dataset, and the resulting catalyst composition was beyond expectations, even by experts. This success demonstrated the ability of ML-driven catalyst design to overcome the limitations of conventional intuition-based exploration. However, although this approach successfully identified novel high-performance catalysts, it was limited to a single catalytic system, focusing only on the effect of additive oxides while keeping Pt and TiO2 fixed as the support metal and support, respectively. More recently, we extended our extrapolative ML strategy to develop new multi-elemental catalysts for the selective catalytic reduction of NOx using H2 (H2-SCR)74 and low-temperature methanol synthesis from CO2 and H275. The optimal catalysts for these reactions were found to be Pt–Ir/Ba–Co/H-ZSM-5 and Pt/Mo–Re–W/TiO2, respectively. These studies further highlighted the potential of ML for identifying unexpected and highly efficient catalytic systems. There is a strong demand to apply this ML approach to other valuable catalytic reaction processes.

In this study, we developed efficient N2O decomposition catalysts using this approach. Using 51 catalysts as the initial dataset, we performed 37 cycles of the closed-loop discovery system (ML prediction + experiment) and experimentally tested 633 catalysts, thereby establishing a large and internally consistent dataset for direct N2O decomposition under practical co-feed constraints (O2, H2O, and CO2). This process led to the identification of more than ten multi-elemental catalysts with superior activity compared to the previously identified best catalyst, with Rh(1)–Pd(2)/ZrO2_EP emerging as the best catalyst for N2O decomposition in the presence of O2, H2O, and CO2. In addition, the iterative exploration progressively identified improved non-Rh-containing candidates, and the promotion effect of Pd on Rh is further examined by combining kinetics, in situ spectroscopy, and DFT-guided analysis.

Results

ML-assisted discovery of catalysts for N2O decomposition

We explored up to four types of platinum group metal (PGM) elements, up to three types of additive elements, and one type of support for PGM1(XP1)–PGM2(XP2)–PGM3(XP3)–PGM4(XP4)/Add1(XA1)–Add2(XA2)–Add3(XA3)/Support N2O decomposition catalysts. Each PGM and added element had a unique loading amount (X) for each catalyst. The numbers of PGM elements, additive elements, and supports explored were 7, 56, and 17, respectively. We began by constructing an initial dataset of 51 data points, labeled as iteration 0. These catalyst compositions were determined manually by the authors without using ML. Based on this initial dataset, we performed predictions using the base ML model via a grid search employing extra-tree regression (ETR) (see the “Methods” section for detailed information). We then selected a batch of catalyst candidates for the next iteration by combining ML-based ranking with a human-in-the-loop selection step. Specifically, the ML system generated a list of the top 100 promising candidates ranked by expected improvement (EI), and this list was obtained after clustering the candidates with the highest EI values using K = 100 to ensure compositional diversity and avoid duplicate formulations69. The catalysts for experiments were subsequently selected by experts from this top 100 set by considering practical and ambiguous criteria such as novelty, diversity, cost, availability, and safety76. Because no candidates outside the ML-suggested list were considered, this procedure, to this extent, makes the human selection practical while maintaining systematic exploration. The selected catalysts were synthesized via a sequential impregnation method and evaluated for N2O decomposition activity. The T20 value, which is defined as the temperature required for 20% N2O conversion during the light-off experiment, was used as the ML prediction target. Note that all the samples were calcined at 700 °C in air to ensure thermal stability, as N2O decomposition catalysts are expected to require high thermal durability42. The performance data from the newly tested catalysts were then added to the dataset to complete one iteration of the closed-loop system. Thus, the ML model was retrained using the updated dataset, and the next batch of promising candidates was selected for synthesis and evaluation. In each subsequent iteration, the EI values were recalculated across all grid points using an ML model trained on all the data obtained up to the previous iteration, and the most promising candidates were suggested to the researchers to perform actual experiments in the next cycle.

Through experimental testing of 633 ML-predicted new catalysts corresponding to 37 cycles of the closed loop discovery system (ML prediction + experimental validation), we found several catalysts that showed higher activity (lower T20 value) than the originally identified high-performance catalyst (Rh(1)–Pd(2)/ZrO2_RC-100) with a T20 value of 378 °C, as shown in Fig. 1b. The composition of the best catalyst identified by this approach was Rh(1)–Pd(2)/ZrO2_EP with a T20 of 355 °C. Despite the simple components of our best catalyst, more than 10 multi-elemental catalysts with superior activity were identified using this approach, including Rh(1)/Sr(0.5)/Al2O3_G07 (T20 = 363 °C), Rh(1)–Pd(0.5)–Pt(0.5)–Ir(0.7)/Ta(1.6)–W(0.4)/ZrO2_RC-100 (T20 = 369 °C), Rh(1)–Ir(0.7)/Ba(4)–Nd(4)/SnO2 (T20 = 370 °C), and Rh(1)–Pd(0.8)–Pt(1)/Ba(2)/Al2O3_G07 (T20 = 371 °C). Catalytic reactions using these catalysts were tested at least three times, and the average values are reported. Notably, these catalysts included elements not included in the initial dataset, as shown in the periodic tables in Fig. 2a. Although many high-performance catalysts contain Rh, our iterative exploration strategy has gradually led to the discovery of effective non-Rh-containing catalysts. Among PGM catalysts, Rh is particularly expensive; therefore, developing catalysts that do not contain Rh is highly important. Against this background, the best non-Rh-containing catalyst initially identified was Ir(1)/ZrO2_RC-100 with T20 of 509 °C. As the iterations progressed, catalysts with improved performance were found, such as Ru(2)–Pd(1)/ZrO2_EP (T20 = 448 °C) at iteration 6, Pd(2)–Ru(1)/MgO (T20 = 423 °C) at iteration 13, and Pd(2.6)–Ru(0.2)–Pt(0.2)/Nd(0.5)/Al2O3_G07 (T20 = 383 °C) at iteration 36. This trend highlights the capability of our ML-guided approach to expand the catalyst design space beyond Rh-based systems and to continuously discover non-Rh-containing catalysts with enhanced performance, even though they have not yet surpassed Rh-based benchmarks. It should be noted that the present closed-loop workflow was designed to optimize catalytic activity (T20), and material cost was not explicitly considered in the optimization process. Therefore, the identification of non-Rh-containing catalysts should be interpreted as an outcome of activity-guided exploration in materials space rather than a result of cost-optimized design. Incorporating economic considerations such as material cost into the closed-loop framework (e.g., via multi-objective optimization) will be an important direction for future work and may enable the discovery of more cost-effective catalysts. The appearance of new elements and supports during iterative exploration is also visualized in Supplementary Fig. 4, which shows that components absent from the initial dataset appeared predominantly during the early stages, particularly within the first ten iterations. Our approach employs continuous-valued elemental descriptors characterized by user-selected properties such as electronegativity and atomic radius, rather than discrete categorical representations of elements. Each element is represented by a vector of continuous properties, enabling model inference within the continuous descriptor space and facilitating interpolation therein. Consequently, this approach can propose catalyst compositions containing elements or supports not present in the initial dataset, which may be regarded as materials-space “extrapolative” discovery (i.e., extrapolative in terms of elemental/support combinations explored in the dataset, rather than statistical extrapolation). This allows the model to consider chemical-feature-based similarities among elements, thereby enhancing the generalization and exploration capacity of the approach. The prediction accuracy (R2, MAE, RMSE, and MSE) was estimated via a tenfold cross-validation based on the accumulated data at each iteration. In the actual exploration process, we used 222 for the first ten iterations. Subsequently, 000, 111, 222, and the best model at each iteration were employed according to the estimated prediction accuracies. In the early phase (iterations <10), the model exhibited limited predictive accuracy (R2 < 0.6). As experimental data accumulated through successive iterations, the prediction accuracy improved steadily. Notably, this improvement in model accuracy closely correlates with the evolution of the T20 distributions obtained at each iteration (Supplementary Fig. 6). When the model accuracy was low (R2 < 0.6), the experimentally tested catalysts exhibited a very broad activity distribution (T20 ≈ 400–900 °C), reflecting largely exploratory sampling. As the model accuracy improves with increasing iterations, the per-iteration T20 distributions progressively narrow and shift toward lower temperatures. Quantitatively, the first ML-assisted iteration (iteration 1) yielded catalysts with T20 values ranging from 403 to 730 °C (mean 608 °C), whereas the final iteration (iteration 37) yielded catalysts in a much narrower and lower range of 370–464 °C (mean 431 °C). This simultaneous narrowing and downward shift of the T20 distribution provides clear evidence of learning-guided candidate selection rather than blind sampling. Taken together, these changes reflect the classic exploration–exploitation trade-off in ML, which emphasizes the need to balance between “exploration”, that is, probing uncertain regions of chemical space, and “exploitation”, capitalizing on accumulated knowledge to refine predictions64. Accordingly, descriptor representation 222 was employed during the early phases and after 10 iterations we switched to the best models based on prediction accuracy. Descriptor representation 121 was frequently selected as optimal in later iterations, which was confirmed in our post-analysis (Supplementary Fig. 7). Combined with descriptor representation 121, ETR was confirmed to yield the best prediction accuracy for predicting the T20 of the 633 catalysts (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9).

To assess the practical relevance of the developed catalysts, we evaluated their stability and tolerance under representative co-feed and impurity conditions. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 11, the best catalyst, Rh(1)–Pd(2)/ZrO2_EP, maintained stable catalytic performance at 390 °C for 30 h under reaction conditions, demonstrating excellent resistance to deactivation in the presence of common co-feeds, including O2, H2O, and CO2. In addition, we further examined the tolerance toward NOx by conducting an NO co-feeding experiment. Specifically, 500 ppm NO was introduced after 1 h on stream. Although the N2O conversion partially decreased upon NO addition, the catalyst retained excellent stability without noticeable deactivation for at least 60 h under NO co-feeding, highlighting the robustness of Rh(1)–Pd(2)/ZrO2_EP under impurity-containing feeds.

To place the performance of the identified catalyst in the context of previous studies, we compared its activity in the presence of O2 with representative N2O decomposition catalysts reported in the literature. Direct quantitative comparison is generally difficult because reported activities are obtained under different experimental conditions, including calcination temperature, feed composition, space velocity, contact time, etc. Nevertheless, to provide a practical benchmark, we evaluated the T20 and T50 values of the best catalyst identified in this study, Rh(1)–Pd(2)/ZrO2_EP, under a range of reaction conditions and compared them with literature-reported catalysts tested under similar conditions (Supplementary Table 5). To further position the performance within a broader landscape of N2O decomposition catalysts, including non-noble-metal systems, we also evaluated representative spinel-type oxides (e.g., Co3O4) and Fe-zeolite catalysts using the same test protocol; these results are also included in Supplementary Table 5. Thus, Rh(1)–Pd(2)/ZrO2_EP exhibits higher activity than representative benchmark catalysts reported in the literature, placing its performance among the most active N2O decomposition catalysts reported to date.

ML-based statistical analysis

Although multi-element catalysts have been identified through ML-assisted research in other reactions, such as Pt/Ba–Rb–Mo–Nb/TiO2 for the reverse water–gas shift reaction73, Pt–Ir/Ba–Co/H-ZSM-5 for the selective catalytic reduction of NOx with hydrogen (H2-SCR)74, and Pt/Mo–Re–W/TiO2 for low-temperature methanol synthesis from CO2 and H275, the most active catalyst identified for N2O decomposition in this study was a relatively simple system composed of only two elements and a support. Although the simplicity of this catalyst might appear boring at first glance, clarifying which types of reactions benefit from multi-element catalyst systems and which do not is an important challenge in the field of catalysis. This highlights the need for detailed mechanistic studies and a deeper understanding of each reaction as well as a comprehensive understanding of different reaction systems.

While ML is frequently used as a black box, supervised ML models can uncover important chemical insights influencing the prediction, even without any explicit knowledge of its underlying principles. Extrapolative ML can reveal not only effective catalyst composition, but also the necessary elemental features and electronic properties for precise catalyst design. In the present study, SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) analysis was used as a global interpretive and diagnostic tool for the trained model, allowing us to identify descriptors that contribute strongly to the model prediction across the explored catalyst space and to assess whether these trends are robust or may simply reflect dataset composition. The importance of descriptors in the best-performing model (121 representation combined with ETR) is shown in Fig. 4a. Among the element/support components, Al2O3_AKP-G07, Rh, and TiO2_P25 were identified as highly influential features. For the element/support descriptors, the atomic radius, molecular weight of the most stable oxide form (MW_Oxide), and molecular weight of the metal precursor (MW_Precursor) for the Additive elements were identified as the most important factors for predicting T20. This was confirmed by the feature-importance scores of the descriptors (Fig. 4b). SHAP can be used to visualize the dependence of the model output (e.g., T20) on the value of each descriptor. For instance, relatively high feature values (red color) for Rh and Al2O3_AKP-G07 are correlated to a low T20 (SHAP value), indicating that these two inputs give positive impacts on obtaining good activity. On the other hand, a high feature value for TiO2_P25 correlates to a high T20, indicating that TiO2_P25 has a negative impact on the activity. To further interpret these support-level trends, we analyzed the SHAP distributions across support categories and their relationship to support frequency (Supplementary Fig. 12). The results show that the prominence of supports such as Al2O3_AKP-G07 cannot be explained solely by its frequency in the dataset; rather, it is associated with a systematically favorable SHAP distribution. It is important to note that these SHAP results describe the average contribution patterns learned by the model across the dataset and are not intended to provide a direct causal explanation of why the final best catalyst emerged on ZrO2_EP. In particular, ZrO2_EP does not appear as a globally dominant support factor in the SHAP analysis. We therefore carried out additional ZrO2_EP-focused analyses, including whole-dataset, subgroup-level and interaction analyses (Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14), which suggest that the optimum Rh(1)-Pd(2)/ZrO2_EP is better interpreted as a localized, composition-dependent outcome arising from the combined influence of multiple factors, rather than as the consequence of a single globally favorable support effect or a single dominant Rh/Pd interaction. This interpretation is consistent with the fact that SHAP provides an average explanation of model behavior, whereas the final optimum identified during closed-loop optimization may lie in a relatively sparse region of the design space. Importantly, experimental comparison of Rh–Pd catalysts across alternative supports (Supplementary Fig. 15) showed that the catalyst on ZrO2_EP gave the lowest experimental T20 among the tested Rh–Pd/support systems, confirming that the favorable performance of the ZrO2_EP-supported catalyst is experimentally real.

The SHAP values were analyzed using waterfall plots for the best catalyst, Rh(1)–Pd(2)/ZrO2_EP, as shown in Fig. 4c. The waterfall plot analysis highlights the descriptors responsible for deviations from the dataset’s average value (T20 = 538.12 °C) relative to the predicted value for each catalyst. Although the absence of Al2O3_AKP-G07 negatively contributes to the T20 (low catalytic activity), the presence of Rh and several properties of the support positively contribute to the high activity of our best catalyst. It is well-known that Rh is an effective active metal for direct N2O decomposition reactions in the presence of O238,41–44. The results of this ML-based statistical analysis demonstrate that, even without domain knowledge of N2O decomposition catalysts, such information can be extracted from the dataset created in the present study.

To examine the robustness of the interpretation and reduce potential element-identity bias, we additionally conducted SHAP and feature-importance analyses using the fully explorative 222 representation, which excludes any direct elemental identity information (Supplementary Fig. 16). The important physicochemical descriptors remain qualitatively consistent, supporting that the model captures intrinsic property–activity relationships rather than relying on element-specific identifiers. Specifically, for additive elements, atomic radius, MW_Oxide, and MW_Precursor are still identified as important descriptors. For PGM elements, group number and adsorption energy of an O atom on metallic surfaces (Eads(O)) are consistently highlighted. For supports, adsorption energy of an N2O molecule on support surfaces (Eads(N2O)) and the strength of Lewis acid sites on the support obtained from pyridine adsorption IR measurements (IR L-acid cm−1) remain among the key descriptors. In addition, the 222-based interpretation provides further chemical insights. For instance, band gap energy (EBand Gap), surface energy (Esurf), and specific surface area without calcination (SSA_w/o cal) are also recognized as important descriptors for supports.

Catalyst characterization

With the best catalyst composition in hand, structural analyses and mechanistic studies were conducted. This is important because the investigation of extraordinary materials can provide new scientific insights. Supplementary Fig. 17 shows the XRD pattern of Rh(1)–Pd(2)/ZrO2_EP, which is essentially identical to that of ZrO2_EP, without the peaks corresponding to Rh oxide or Pd oxide. This suggests that both the RhOx and PdOx species are well dispersed in the catalysts. To further investigate how Pd influences the redox behavior of Rh under different conditions, we performed additional kinetic studies comparing Rh-only and Rh–Pd catalysts. Kinetic measurements were conducted under controlled conditions, including experiments in the absence of O2 and H2O, to isolate the intrinsic behavior of N2O decomposition. The apparent reaction orders and their temperature dependence were systematically evaluated (Fig. 6b).

Temperature-dependent kinetic analysis reveals that the apparent reaction order for N2O decomposition increases with increasing temperature. This result indicates that at low temperatures, O2 desorption is rate-limiting, whereas at higher temperatures, N2O activation becomes increasingly important. For the Rh–Pd catalyst, the apparent reaction order with respect to N2O was consistently higher than that of the Rh-only catalyst over the investigated temperature range. This indicates that Pd addition enhances the ability of the catalyst to undergo thermal reduction, facilitating oxygen removal from the surface. Such behavior is consistent with PdOx promoting the redox of the catalytically active species. The apparent reaction order with respect to O2 was also measured to investigate the effect of O2 on N2O decomposition, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 18. The apparent reaction order of O2 changes from −0.15 to −0.07 with increasing temperature, indicating that O2 suppresses N2O decomposition, particularly at low temperatures.

Figure 7 shows in situ Rh K-edge and Pd K-edge XANES spectra of Rh(1)–Pd(2)/ZrO2_EP and the Rh K-edge XANES spectra of Rh(1)/ZrO2_EP during N2O decomposition. For Rh(1)–Pd(2)/ZrO2_EP, the absorption edge of the Rh K-edge XANES spectra shifts to a higher energy after the initial introduction of 0.5% N2O, indicating that the Rh species is oxidized by N2O. In situ UV-vis spectroscopic measurements were conducted to investigate the redox behavior of Rh(1)–Pd(2)/ZrO2_EP and Rh(1)/ZrO2_EP during N2O decomposition. Although the spectral contributions of Rh and Pd cannot be individually resolved because of their overlapping absorption features in the visible range, the overall redox activity of the combined Rh and Pd species can still be monitored. To quantify this, the intensity at 798 nm was monitored over multiple redox cycles (Supplementary Fig. 20). In contrast, Rh(1)–Pd(2)/ZrO2_EP shows a larger fluctuation in intensity at 798 nm during the initial cycles, which gradually diminishes and stabilizes. This observation is in good agreement with the AP-XPS and in situ XAS results, which revealed that PdOx initially participates in the redox cycle but ultimately enhances the redox activity of the Rh species as a promoter.

We also performed a density functional theory (DFT) based analysis to rationalize how Pd enhances the activity of Rh species. Based on our previous work and the aforementioned results45, we propose a redox mechanism for catalytic N2O decomposition over Rh-oxide-based catalysts. This mechanism involves the desorption of O2 from the catalyst surface to generate oxygen vacancy (Ov), followed by reoxidation of Ov by N2O molecules to recover the original structure. Under low-temperature conditions, O2 desorption is recognized as the rate-limiting step. Thus, the Ov formation energy (EOvac) is an important descriptor of activity.

Accordingly, we calculated EOvac by modeling using surface models of Rh2O3 and Pd-substituted Rh2O3 (Pd–Rh2O3), focusing on the most labile surface oxygen species (For detailed information on DFT calculations, see Supplementary Note 2). The optimized structures are shown in Supplementary Figs. 21 and 22. As summarized in Supplementary Table 6, the calculated EOvac for Pd–Rh2O3 (1.37 eV) is lower than that for pristine Rh2O3 (2.95 eV), suggesting that Pd incorporation facilitates oxygen vacancy formation and, consequently, O2 desorption, which is a key step in the N2O decomposition reaction and is also consistent with our kinetic analysis (Fig. 6b).

To further rationalize why Pd lowers EOvac and enhances Rh activity, we examined the electronic structures of pristine Rh2O3 and Pd–Rh2O3 prior to vacancy formation. Upon Pd incorporation, a slight narrowing of the band gap is observed (Supplementary Fig. 23). Previous theoretical studies have shown that EOvac in oxides is closely related to intrinsic electronic properties such as band gap and electron affinity82, as these govern how efficiently the lattice accommodates the excess electrons left behind after oxygen removal. In general, a wider band gap is associated with a higher energetic penalty for charge accommodation and thus a higher EOvac. In addition, comparison of the Bader charge analysis and charge density distribution before and after oxygen removal (Supplementary Fig. 24) indicates that, in the Pd-substituted system, the excess electrons generated upon vacancy formation are preferentially localized at the Pd site. This suggests that Pd acts as an effective electron acceptor and stabilizes the defect-induced charges, thereby lowering the energetic penalty associated with vacancy formation. This electronic stabilization is consistent with the reduced EOvac calculated for Pd–Rh2O3 and provides a mechanistic basis for the experimentally observed decrease in apparent activation energy upon Pd addition.

Because the exact structure of the catalytically active surface and active sites under reaction conditions remains unclear, the most stable Rh2O3 surface termination was employed as a representative model to enable systematic comparison of the effect of Pd substitution. Although the surface model employed here is simplified, the DFT results nevertheless provide consistent and qualitative theoretical support for the experimentally observed decrease in apparent activation energy upon Pd addition. These findings support a bimetallic promotion effect of Pd on Rh-based catalysts in N2O decomposition.

Discussion

Using the ML approach, 633 catalysts were experimentally tested through iterative cycles of ML prediction and validation starting from 51 initial data points. As a result, more than ten catalysts with activity superior to that of the best originally identified catalyst were discovered. Rh(1)–Pd(2)/ZrO2_EP was the best catalyst for N2O decomposition in the presence of O2. In addition to guiding experimental discovery, our ML model provides insights into the important physical and chemical properties governing catalytic performance. Specifically, the model identified both effective catalyst compositions and elemental/support descriptors that correlate with high activity. Experimental mechanistic studies using in situ techniques were also performed to elucidate the role of each catalyst component and the reaction mechanism. The obtained results indicated that RhOx acts as a redox species, whereas PdOx facilitates the redox reaction of RhOx under steady-state conditions. Although this study focused on the influence of catalyst composition under fixed preparation and reaction conditions to limit the search space, we acknowledge that preparation methods can significantly impact the catalyst structure and, consequently, performance. Future studies should extend the ML approach to include process variables; however, this will require a substantially larger experimental dataset.

Methods

Catalyst preparation

Chemicals and materials were purchased from commercial suppliers and used without further purification. The sources and purities of the chemicals used for catalyst preparation are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The sources and Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface areas (SBET) of the supports are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

The catalysts in this study can be described by the general formula PGM1(XP1)–PGM2(XP2)–PGM3(XP3)–PGM4(XP4)/Add1(XA1)–Add2(XA2)–Add3(XA3)/Support (where PGM = platinum group metal, namely, Pt, Pd, Ru, Rh, Ir, Au, or Re in the present study. Au and Re are included in this category because of their similar chemical properties and high cost; AddX = additive element; XPX = loading amount of PGMX, and XAX = loading amount of AddX). The catalysts were prepared using the sequential impregnation method. Note that elements with atomic numbers (AN) from 3 (Li) to 83 (Bi), except for Be, B, C, N, O, F, Cl, As, Br, Tc, Pm, Hg, Tl, and noble gases, were used as the catalyst components in this work. First, the additive-loaded support (Add1(XA1)–Add2(XA2)–Add3(XA3)/Support) was prepared via an impregnation process. A mixture of the related amount of support and corresponding sources of the Add elements was added to a 100 mL glass vessel containing an appropriate amount of deionized water and stirred for 30 min with 200 rpm agitation at 50 °C. The mixture was evaporated to dryness at 50 °C, dried at 110 °C for 1 h and calcined in air at 700 °C for 3 h to give Add1(XA1)–Add2(XA2)–Add3(XA3)/Support. The formed additive-loaded support was then added to an aqueous solution containing the PGM precursors. The mixture was stirred for 15 min with 200 rpm agitation at 50 °C, evaporated to dryness at 50 °C and further dried in air at 110 °C for 1 h, then calcined in air at 700 °C for 3 h to obtain PGM1(XP1)–PGM2(XP2)–PGM3(XP3)–PGM4(XP4)/Add1(XA1)–Add2(XA2)–Add3(XA3)/Support. Calcination at high temperature (700 °C) was conducted to ensure the thermal stability of the catalysts. Note that the maximum Rh loading was set to 1 wt.% and the maximum PGM loading was set to 3 wt.%.

Characterization

Details of the characterization are presented in the Supplementary Methods section.

Catalytic reactions

Catalytic tests were carried out using 20 mg of the catalyst in a fixed-bed flow reactor by feeding a gas mixture containing 0.1% N2O, 10% O2, 5% CO2, 1% H2O, and He balance, at a flow rate of 100 mL/min. After pretreating the catalyst under a flow of reaction gas at 500 °C for 30 min, the catalyst was cooled to 250 °C and light-off tests (i.e., monitoring catalytic activity during a controlled temperature ramp) were performed at a heating rate of 3 °C/min. For NO co-feeding experiments, the catalyst was first stabilized under the standard reaction feed for 1 h, after which 500 ppm NO was introduced into the feed while keeping other conditions unchanged. The concentration of N2O in the effluent was monitored using an online IR spectrometer (JASCO FT/IR-4600) equipped with a triglycine sulfate (TGS) detector and a homemade gas cell (CaF2 window).
