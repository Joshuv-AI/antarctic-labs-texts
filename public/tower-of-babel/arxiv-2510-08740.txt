Light-induced pseudo-magnetic fields in three-dimensional topological semimetals
Arpit Raj, Swati Chaudhary, Martin Rodriguez-Vega, Maia G. Vergniory, Roni Ilan, Gregory A. Fiete

Abstract
In this work, we show that suitably designed spatially varying linearly polarized light provides a versatile route to generate and control pseudo-magnetic fields in Weyl semimetals through Floquet engineering. Within a high-frequency expansion, we derive an effective axial gauge potential 𝐀 5 ​ ( 𝐫 ) \mathbf{A}_{5}(\mathbf{r}) whose curl gives the pseudo-magnetic field 𝐁 5 ​ ( 𝐫 ) \mathbf{B}_{5}(\mathbf{r}) . By mapping the light profile to 𝐀 5 ​ ( 𝐫 ) \mathbf{A}_{5}(\mathbf{r}) , we establish design principles for pseudo-magnetic field textures that mimic strain-induced gauge fields while offering key advantages like dynamic control, full reversibility, spatial selectivity, and absence of material deformation. We compare the Landau-level spectra produced by uniform real and pseudo-magnetic fields and also analyze both their linear optical conductivity and the second-order dc responses. Our results enable real-time manipulation of pseudo-magnetic fields and predict clear experimental signatures for optically engineered gauge fields in topological semimetals.



I
Introduction

Three-dimensional topological semimetals have emerged as fascinating platforms for exploring exotic quantum phenomena, where electrons behave as relativistic fermions and exhibit linear energy dispersions near isolated band crossings
Armitage et al. 2018
. These materials include Dirac semimetals, featuring four-fold degenerate crossings protected by crystalline symmetries, and Weyl semimetals, characterized by pairs of two-fold degenerate nodes with opposite chiralities that are topologically protected and act as monopoles of Berry curvature in momentum space. These systems are highly sensitive to external perturbations including strain and other inhomogeneities which couple to the low-energy fermions as if electromagnetic fields were applied. Unlike conventional electromagnetic fields, these emergent pseudo-gauge fields act with opposite sign on the two chiralities
Ilan et al. 2020
.

The most widely studied route to pseudo-gauge fields in electronic materials is strain engineering
Cortijo et al. 2015
;
Guinea et al. 2010
;
Levy et al. 2010
;
Grushin et al. 2016
;
Pikulin et al. 2016
. Elastic deformations shift Weyl (or Dirac) nodes in momentum space, generating an axial vector potential
\mathbf{A}_{5}(\mathbf{r})
whose curl produces a pseudo
-
magnetic field
\mathbf{B}_{5}(\mathbf{r})=\gradient_{\mathbf{r}}\times\mathbf{A}_{5}(\mathbf{r})
. When
\mathbf{A}_{5}
also varies in time, a pseudo
-
electric field
\mathbf{E}_{5}=-\partial_{t}\mathbf{A}_{5}
arises. The resulting strain
-
induced chiral anomaly has been proposed and analyzed for both type
-
I and type
-
II Weyl semimetals
Pikulin et al. 2016
;
Sabsovich et al. 2020
. While strain-induced pseudofields have been clearly demonstrated in two-dimensional systems such as graphene
Guinea et al. 2010
;
Levy et al. 2010
and in thin FeSn kagome films
Zhang et al. 2023
, their realization in bulk three-dimensional topological semimetals remains scarce
Kamboj et al. 2019
. Beyond strain engineering, magnetic textures offer another route to generate pseudo-gauge fields
Araki 2020
;
Rogers et al. 2024
;
Ozawa et al. 2024
in magnetic Weyl semimetals where
\mathbf{A}_{5}
depends on magnetization and resulting fields can reach hundreds of Tesla
Ozawa et al. 2024
. However, both strain and magnetic textures are material-specific and challenging to control dynamically, motivating the search for alternative approaches.

Floquet engineering, the use of time-periodic driving to modify material properties, has been extensively studied as a route to realize and control topological semimetal phases
Oka and Kitamura 2019
;
Wang et al. 2014
;
Hübener et al. 2017
;
Bucciantini et al. 2017
;
Chan et al. 2016
;
Zhu et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2024
. Notably, it has also been proposed as a means to generate axial gauge fields in these systems
Ebihara et al. 2016
. This axial gauge field leads to light-induced anomalous Hall conductivity which has been recently reported in the Dirac semimetal Co
3
Sn
2
S
2

Yoshikawa et al. 2025
. We can expect that the spatial modulation of light intensity which controls
\mathbf{A}_{5}
should lead to non-zero
\mathbf{B}_{5}
fields which in turn can affect the electronic transport and optical responses of the system.

The experimental probes of pseudo
-
magnetic fields remain challenging because their signatures can be obscured by those of real magnetic fields (
\mathbf{B}
). Platforms such as photonic and acoustic metamaterials circumvent this difficulty where chiral acoustic Landau levels (LLs) have been reported
Li et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2025
. In electronic systems, several theoretical proposals leverage the distinct symmetry properties and coupling mechanisms of
\bf B
and
{\bf B}_{5}
fields. The photovoltaic Hall effect
Oka and Aoki 2011
and cubic Hall viscosity
Robredo et al. 2021
have been identified as potential discriminating probes. Several studies have shown that LLs from real magnetic fields manifest directly in optical conductivity
Morimoto et al. 2009
;
Ikebe et al. 2010
;
Shimano et al. 2011
;
Shimano et al. 2013
;
Okamura et al. 2020
;
Jeon et al. 2014
;
Ashby and Carbotte 2013
;
Stålhammar et al. 2020
;
Yadav et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Yadav et al. 2023
, which suggests that optical probes can offer a promising route to detect LLs generated by pseudo-magnetic fields.
Recent works propose that real magnetic fields induce giant-nonlinear optical responses in Weyl semimetals
Bednik and Kozii 2024
and we can expect to see similar effects with
\mathbf{B}_{5}
.

In this work, we investigate the generation and manipulation of pseudo-magnetic fields in Weyl semimetals using linearly polarized light within the Floquet formalism. We demonstrate that spatially modulated laser profiles induce effective axial gauge potentials that generate pseudo-magnetic fields. We focus on a particular laser profile that generates a uniform
\mathbf{B}_{5}
leading to a distinctive Landau level structure in the effective Floquet Hamiltonian. These light-induced pseudo-magnetic fields manifest clear experimental signatures: quantum oscillations in the linear optical conductivity and a characteristic low-energy hump in the second-order nonlinear conductivity, features that are analogous to those produced by real magnetic fields. Crucially, this implies that in non-magnetic Weyl semimetals without any external magnetic fields, these optical conductivity measurements provide an unambiguous probe for detecting and characterizing pseudo-magnetic fields. Our approach offers significant advantages over strain-induced methods: dynamic control, complete reversibility, spatial selectivity, and the ability to switch pseudo-magnetic fields on ultrafast timescales without material deformation. This opens pathways for real-time manipulation of topological properties and time-resolved studies of emergent gauge phenomena in quantum materials.

-

-
magnetic field. We present results for linear conductivity and second-order DC conductivity in Sec.
V
. Finally, we conclude with a summary of our main results in Sec.
VI
.

III
Effective Floquet Hamiltonian

We now study the effect of light with different incidence direction, polarization, frequency
\Omega
and driving strength
A
on the band structure of
\mathcal{H}({\bf\it k})
. We assume a classical limit, and use the minimal substitution
{\bf\it k}\rightarrow{\bf\it k}+\frac{ea}{\hbar}\mathbf{A}(t)
, where
\mathbf{A}(t)
is the vector potential and
e
is the magnitude of electron’s charge. This leads to the time-dependent Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}({\bf\it k},t)=\mathcal{H}({\bf\it k},t+2\pi/\Omega)
, which we treat with Floquet theory that we briefly describe below
Floquet 1883
;
Sambe 1973
;
Rodriguez-Vega et al. 2021
.

The remaining discrete time-translation symmetry allows us to employ the Floquet theorem
Floquet 1883
to decompose the wave function’s time-dependence as
|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{i\epsilon t/\hbar}|\phi(t)\rangle
, where
|\phi(t+2\pi/\Omega)\rangle=|\phi(t)\rangle
and
\epsilon
is the quasienergy. Then, the periodic piece obeys the Floquet-Schrödinger equation

\left[\mathcal{H}(t)-i\hbar\partial_{t}\right]|\phi(t)\rangle=\epsilon|\phi(t)\rangle.

(5)

We can obtain the quasienergies and steady states from the Floquet evolution operator
U_{F}=\mathrm{\hat{T}}\exp\left\{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{2\pi/\Omega}\mathcal{H}(s)ds\right\}=e^{-i\mathcal{H}_{F}T/\hbar}
, where
\mathrm{\hat{T}}
denotes time ordering,
T=2\pi/\Omega
is the period, and
\mathcal{H}_{F}
is the effective Floquet Hamiltonian governing stroboscopic dynamics. Alternatively, we can employ the extended-state picture which follows from a Fourier expansion of the steady states
|\phi(t)\rangle=\sum_{n}e^{in\Omega t}\left|\phi_{n}\right\rangle
. This leads to

\sum_{m}\left(\mathcal{H}^{(n-m)}+\delta_{n,m}\hbar\Omega m\right)\left|\phi_{m}\right\rangle=\epsilon\left|\phi_{n}\right\rangle,

(6)

with Hamiltonian Fourier modes given by
\mathcal{H}^{(n)}=\frac{\Omega}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi/\Omega}\differential{t}\mathcal{H}(t)e^{-in\Omega t}
. The latter representation is particularly useful to obtain an effective Floquet Hamiltonian in the high-frequency regime. In particular, we perform a Van Vleck approximation
Eckardt and Anisimovas 2015
;
Rodriguez-Vega et al. 2021
. In this work, we consider the effective Hamiltonian up to first order in
1/\Omega
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{F}\approx\mathcal{H}^{(0)}+\sum_{m\neq 0}\frac{{\mathcal{H}}^{(m)}{\mathcal{H}^{(-m)}}}{m\hbar\Omega},

(7)

where
\mathcal{H}^{(0)}
is the averaged Hamiltonian over one cycle.

In the next section we derive the effective Hamiltonians near the special points
\Gamma
and
\rm R
for different light polarization.

III.1
Circularly polarized light

First, we study the effect of circularly polarized light on the band structure. We consider the vector potential

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(t)=A_{0}[\sin(\Omega t)\hat{\mathbf{y}}+\cos(\Omega t)\hat{\mathbf{z}}].

(8)

As a starting point, and to gain initial insight into the effect of light in this case, we obtain and analyze the linearized effective Floquet Hamiltonians near the
\Gamma

\rm R

Near the

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{F,C}_{R}({\bf\it k})

\displaystyle=t((k_{x}+\tfrac{\gamma^{2}}{\hbar\Omega/t})\tau_{0}\sigma_{y}+k_{y}\tau_{y}\sigma_{z}+k_{z}\tau_{y}\sigma_{x}),

(9)

The quasienergies are given by

\displaystyle E_{\Gamma,1}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=-2t\pm t\,\sqrt{\left(k_{x}-\tfrac{\gamma^{2}}{\hbar\Omega/t}\right)^{2}+k_{y}^{2}+k_{z}^{2}},

\displaystyle E_{\Gamma,2}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=2t\pm t\,\sqrt{\left(k_{x}-\tfrac{\gamma^{2}}{\hbar\Omega/t}\right)^{2}+k_{y}^{2}+k_{z}^{2}+16},

IV
Landau levels

IV.1
Pseudo-magnetic field

Before looking at the Landau levels, we briefly describe how the model in Eq. (
15
) gives rise to pseudo-magnetic fields. In the typical Floquet approach, one assumes that the vector potential
\mathbf{A}(t)
is spatially constant. However, in experimental implementations, we can expect to obtain a position-dependent vector potential amplitude. This can arise from engineered laser beam profiles using various beam shaping techniques
Dickey 2014
;
Laskin 2013
, or the attenuation of the light inside a material. If these variations are on length scales large compared with the unit cell size, we can define a local band structure with a position-dependent vector potential.

For this study, we consider a linearly polarized light described by a position dependent vector potential

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(y,t)=A(y)\sin(\Omega t)(\hat{\mathbf{y}}+\hat{\mathbf{z}}),

(20)

with

\displaystyle A(y)=\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}\sqrt{\sin(\tfrac{\alpha+A_1 y}{2})},

(21)

where we have defined
\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}=\frac{\hbar}{ea}\gamma_{0}
and
\alpha=2\sin^{-1}(\frac{A_{0}^{2}}{\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}^{2}})
with
A_{0}<\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}
(hence,
0\leq\alpha<\pi
). We take the system to have
N_{y}
unit cells along the
y
-axis, with the origin situated midway, and consider open boundary conditions. Since the system is centered at
y=0
,
A(y)
in Eq. (
21
) is well defined if
|A_{1}|<{\rm min}\left(\frac{2\alpha}{N_{y}a},\frac{2(\pi-\alpha)}{N_{y}a}\right)
. Note that when
A_{1}=0
, we get
A(y)=A_{0}
.

Having a position dependent amplitude gives rise to a pseudo-magnetic field along
\hat{\mathbf{z}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{5}(\mathbf{r})=\gradient_{\mathbf{r}}\times\mathbf{A}_{5}(\mathbf{r}),

(22)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{5}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{2\hbar}{e\,a}\,\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{J_{0}\left(\tfrac{aA(y)}{\hbar/e}\right)-1}{2J_{0}\left(\tfrac{aA(y)}{2\hbar/e}\right)}\right)\hat{\mathbf{x}}.

(23)

Our main motivation behind the particular choice of
A(y)
in Eq. (
21
) is to generate a near uniform pseudo-magnetic field for better comparison with a uniform real magnetic field. With this choice, we get
\mathbf{B}_{5}\approx-\frac{\hbar}{ea^{2}}\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{8}A_{1}a\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}
. For
A_{1}=0.004/a
, this gives
B_{5}\sim 10\,\rm T
which corresponds to pseudo-magnetic length
l_{B_{5}}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{eB_{5}}}\sim 10\,\rm nm
.

One can also consider other spatial profiles for the amplitude, for example,
A(y)=A_{0}\left(1+\frac{\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}^{2}A_{1}}{4A_{0}^{2}}y\right)

VI
Conclusions

In summary, we have shown that spatially varying linearly polarized light could provide a versatile route to engineer pseudo-magnetic fields in Weyl semimetals via Floquet band renormalization. Compared with strain engineering, the optical route is dynamically tunable and spatially selective, enabling on-demand control without material deformation. The model we studied has a charge-2 multifold Weyl point at
\rm R
which could be split into two charge-1 Weyl points of the same chirality by shining linearly polarized light. We found that their separation depends on the light amplitude which can be given various spatial profiles to create desired
\mathbf{A}_{5}
and
\mathbf{B}_{5}
, including the possibility of an optically generated dynamically tunable pseudo-magnetic domain wall.

In this study, we chose a specific light profile that resulted in a uniform pseudo-magnetic field. We computed the resulting Landau level spectrum and compared it to that of a real uniform magnetic field of the same strength. We found that the zeroth Landau level disperses in opposite directions for the former but in the same direction for the latter. We also examined the linear and injection conductivities for both
\mathbf{B}_{5}
and
\mathbf{B}
and found them to be qualitatively similar. We observed oscillations in the longitudinal linear optical conductivity coming from transitions between the lower Landau levels for both cases. For the injection conductivity, we found a low-energy hump followed by a plateau whose height was consistent with that of circular photogalvanic effect quantization. Together, these results provide a promising route for optically generating pseudo-gauge fields in topological semimetals and detecting them through optical conductivity measurements.

Appendix E
Landau levels and optical conductivities

We consider a Weyl node with finite tilt in the
z
-direction and described by

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\hbar\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix u_zk_z + u_tk_z - \frac{\mu}{\hbar} & u(k_x-ik_y)\\
u(k_x+ik_y) & -u_zk_z + u_tk_z - \frac{\mu}{\hbar}\endlx@physics@matrix).

(59)

The chirality of the node is given by
\chi=\textrm{sgn}(u_{z})
. We will assume
u>0
. Throughout this section,
k_{x}
,
k_{y}
,
k_{z}
have the units of inverse length.

A uniform magnetic field,
\mathbf{B}=B\hat{\mathbf{z}}
, is applied to the system (we will assume
B>0
). In Landau gauge this is effected by the vector potential
\mathbf{A}=(-By,0,0)
which couples by making
{\bf\it k}\to{\bf\it k}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\mathbf{A}
. Note that if we were to do this analysis for
\mathbf{A}_{5}=(-By,0,0)
which produces
\mathbf{B}_{5}=B\hat{\mathbf{z}}
, the only difference would be in the way the pseudo-vector potential couples to the system:
{\bf\it k}\to{\bf\it k}+\chi\frac{e}{\hbar}\mathbf{A}_{5}
. This implies that for
\chi=+1
all the results we obtain hereafter for
\mathbf{B}
will directly apply whereas for
\chi=-1
the results for
B<0
will apply.
Since
\mathbf{A}
has a
y
dependence,
k_{y}
is no longer a good quantum number. Presence of the magnetic field changes the Hamiltonian to

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\hbar\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix u_zk_z + u_tk_z - \frac{\mu}{\hbar} & u(k_x-\frac{eB}{\hbar}y-\partial_y)\\
u(k_x-\frac{eB}{\hbar}y+\partial_y) & -u_zk_z + u_tk_z - \frac{\mu}{\hbar}\endlx@physics@matrix).

(60)

We now define

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\frac{l_{B}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{y-y_{0}}{l_{B}^{2}}+\partial_{y}\right),

(61)

\displaystyle b^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\frac{l_{B}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{y-y_{0}}{l_{B}^{2}}-\partial_{y}\right),

(62)

where
l_{B}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{eB}}
is the magnetic length and
y_{0}=k_{x}l_{B}^{2}
. These operators satisfy
[b,b^{\dagger}]=1
. Since
b,b^{\dagger}
behave as the lowering and raising operators, respectively, we can define

\displaystyle b\ket{n}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}\ket{n-1}

(63)

\displaystyle b^{\dagger}\ket{n}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n+1}\ket{n+1}

(64)

where
\ket{n}
are the eigenstates of the number operator,
b^{\dagger}b\ket{n}=n\ket{n}
. The eigenstates are easily found to be
\ket{n}=\frac{(b^{\dagger})^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\ket{0}=\frac{2^{-n/2}}{\sqrt{n!}}H_{n}\left(\frac{y-y_{0}}{l_{B}}\right)\ket{0}
where
H_{n}(x)
are the Hermite polynomials and
\ket{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{\pi l_{B}^{2}}}e^{-\frac{(y-y_{0})^{2}}{2l_{B}^{2}}}
is obtained by requiring
b\ket{0}=0
.

In terms of these operators, the Hamiltonian can be rewritten as

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\hbar\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix u_zk_z + u_tk_z - \frac{\mu}{\hbar} & -\frac{\sqrt{2}u}{l_B}b \\
-\frac{\sqrt{2}u}{l_B}b^\dagger& -u_zk_z + u_tk_z - \frac{\mu}{\hbar}\endlx@physics@matrix).

(65)

It is easy to see that
\psi=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\A\ket{n-1}\\ \B\ket{n}\endlx@physics@matrix)
can be eigenstate of
H
for a suitably chosen
\alpha,\beta
(with
\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}=1
). Solving for
H\psi=E\psi
we get the following system of coupled linear equations

\displaystyle\left(u_{z}k_{z}+u_{t}k_{z}-\frac{\mu}{\hbar}-\frac{E}{\hbar}\right)\alpha-\frac{\sqrt{2n}u}{l_{B}}\beta

\displaystyle=0,

(66)

\displaystyle-\frac{\sqrt{2n}u}{l_{B}}\alpha+\left(-u_{z}k_{z}+u_{t}k_{z}-\frac{\mu}{\hbar}-\frac{E}{\hbar}\right)\beta

\displaystyle=0.

(67)

Here, we need to consider two cases:

1.

n=0
: We are forced to set
\alpha=0
which results in the eigenstate
\psi_{0}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 0\\ \ket{0}\endlx@physics@matrix)
with the eigenenergy

\displaystyle E_{0}(k_{z})=-\hbar(u_{z}-u_{t})k_{z}-\mu.

(68)

2.

n\neq 0
: Solving for the eigenenergy gives

\displaystyle E_{n}^{\pm}(k_{z})=-\mu+\hbar u_{t}k_{z}\pm\hbar|u_{z}|\sqrt{k_{z}^{2}+\frac{2n}{l_{B}^{2}}\frac{u^{2}}{u_{z}^{2}}}.

(69)

The corresponding eigenstates are given by
\psi_{n}^{\pm}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\A_n^{\pm}\ket{n-1}\\ \B_n^{\pm}\ket{n}\endlx@physics@matrix)
with

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\frac{\sqrt{2n}u}{l_{B}}}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{\sqrt{2n}u}{l_{B}}\right)^{2}+\left(u_{z}k_{z}\mp\sqrt{u_{z}^{2}k_{z}^{2}+2n\frac{u^{2}}{l_{B}^{2}}}\,\right)^{2}}},

(70)

\displaystyle\beta_{n}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{u_{z}k_{z}\mp\sqrt{u_{z}^{2}k_{z}^{2}+2n\frac{u^{2}}{l_{B}^{2}}}}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{\sqrt{2n}u}{l_{B}}\right)^{2}+\left(u_{z}k_{z}\mp\sqrt{u_{z}^{2}k_{z}^{2}+2n\frac{u^{2}}{l_{B}^{2}}}\,\right)^{2}}}.

(71)
