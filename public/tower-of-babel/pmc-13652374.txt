Aortic occlusion requiring surgery in a patient with autoimmune hemolytic anemia and severe anemia: a case report

Introduction

Peripheral arterial occlusive disease is a major cause of morbidity worldwide and may progress to chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI), the most severe form of limb ischemia[1,2]. Aortoiliac occlusive disease (AIOD), involving the infrarenal aorta and iliac arteries, can result in critical reductions in lower-extremity perfusion, requiring urgent revascularization[3]. Open surgical reconstruction remains the reference standard for extensive Trans-atlantic inter-society consensus (TASC II) D AIOD because of its excellent long-term patency[4]. However, perioperative optimization becomes considerably more challenging when severe anemia and suspected autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) coexist, requiring careful balancing between hematologic optimization and timely limb salvage.

AIHA complicates CLTI management by increasing the destruction of red blood cells through autoantibodies, causing persistent anemia, reducing oxygen delivery, impairing wound healing, and increasing perioperative risk[5]. Systemic inflammation from AIHA may also affect vascular health and surgical outcomes. Proper preoperative evaluation and management are essential to address AIHA-induced anemia, which poses risks for surgical complications and impaired wound healing[6].

We report a patient with CLTI secondary to complete distal aortoiliac occlusion and suspected AIHA who underwent successful, expedited, planned aortobifemoral bypass surgery despite severe transfusion-refractory anemia.

Case presentation

A 72-year-old man presented to the emergency department in Surabaya, Indonesia, with leg pain, bilateral lower limb weakness, and numbness. The patient was hemodynamically stable on admission. His medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus (random blood glucose: 402 mg/dL) and suspected AIHA. The patient reported no known allergies.

As shown in Supplemental Digital Content Figure S1, available at: https://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A133, physical examination demonstrated conjunctival pallor; bilateral, cold lower extremities; absent pulse oximetry signals in the left foot, except in the third digit; and necrosis of the first, second, and fourth toes of the left foot, consistent with Fontaine stage IV CLTI. Laboratory results showed severe anemia (hemoglobin, 6.1 g/dL), hyperglycemia, leukocytosis (15 800/µL), and hypoalbuminemia (3.06 g/dL).

Computed tomography angiography, as shown in Fig. 1, demonstrated complete distal aortoiliac occlusion with extensive calcification extending from the infrarenal aorta into the bilateral iliac and femoral arteries, with detailed reconstruction visualized in Supplemental Digital Content Figure S2, available at: https://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A133, consistent with the TASC II D classification for aortoiliac lesions. Doppler ultrasonography showed monophasic flow in the left femoral and popliteal arteries and biphasic flow in the right femoral and popliteal arteries, as shown in Supplemental Digital Content Figures S3–S6, available at: https://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A133. The patient underwent an expedited, planned open aortobifemoral bypass on the sixth day after admission, following 5 days of preoperative hemoglobin optimization. Although the operation was not performed as an emergency procedure, it was scheduled within a short timeframe because of Fontaine stage IV CLTI with tissue necrosis and complete distal aortoiliac occlusion. Further delay was considered inappropriate because of the risk of progressive ischemia and limb loss.

Following hematology consultation for suspected AIHA, the patient received packed red blood cell transfusions, folate, and oral iron, with a target hemoglobin greater than 8 g/dL. Despite repeated transfusions, hemoglobin remained unstable, increasing transiently to 13 g/dL after a leukodepleted transfusion before declining to 7 g/dL preoperatively. Given ongoing tissue loss and the risk of limb loss, surgery proceeded despite suboptimal hemoglobin levels.

Under general anesthesia, the patient underwent an expedited, planned, open aortoiliac revascularization through a midline laparotomy. After entering the abdominal cavity, the infrarenal abdominal aorta and bilateral iliac arteries were carefully exposed with limited dissection to reduce the risk of bleeding. As shown in Fig. 2, a heavily calcified occlusive plaque was identified at the aortic bifurcation. Figure 2.Presence of severe calcification and thrombus formation proximal to the aortoiliac junction.

Intraoperative anticoagulation was administered according to standard vascular surgery practice, with closer intraoperative monitoring because of the patient’s high bleeding risk from severe anemia secondary to suspected AIHA. An initial dose of 5000 IU of unfractionated heparin was given, with intraoperative monitoring of the activated clotting time (ACT) and a target ACT of approximately 200 seconds.

After systemic heparinization, the aorta was cross-clamped proximally, and the distal vessels were controlled. The occluded aortoiliac segment was opened; calcified plaque and thrombotic material were removed; and adequate inflow and outflow were confirmed. A bifurcated Y-graft was anastomosed proximally to the abdominal aorta in an end-to-end fashion, as demonstrated in Fig. 3. The distal limbs of the graft were then tunneled and anastomosed to both femoral arteries in an end-to-side fashion, as demonstrated in Supplemental Digital Content Figures S7 and S8, available at: https://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A133. Anastomoses were performed using vascular sutures, and clamps were released sequentially to assess graft patency and hemostasis. Because of the patient’s low hemoglobin level, meticulous hemostatic control was prioritized throughout the operation. No topical hemostatic agents or specialized anesthetic blood-conservation techniques were used. Hemostatic control was achieved primarily through limited tissue dissection, precise vascular clamping, careful handling of friable or calcified vessels, and targeted electrocauterization to address small-vessel oozing. After satisfactory graft flow and hemostasis were confirmed, heparin was reversed with protamine. The patient was extubated in the operating theater. Estimated intraoperative blood loss was 200 mL.

Postoperatively, bilateral femoral pulses were restored, and hemoglobin improved to 8.9 g/dL. No bleeding, hematoma, or graft-related complications occurred. Aspirin, 100 mg daily, was resumed 24 hours after surgery, and the patient was transferred to the ward in stable condition.

Discussion

AIOD represents the most severe manifestation of aortoiliac atherosclerotic disease and may progress to CLTI when arterial perfusion becomes critically compromised. Patients presenting with rest pain, tissue loss, or gangrene require timely revascularization because delayed restoration of blood flow substantially increases the risk of limb loss and mortality[6,8,9]. In the present case, complete distal aortic occlusion accompanied by digital necrosis indicated advanced ischemia requiring prompt revascularization.

Although endovascular techniques have become increasingly utilized for AIOD, open aortobifemoral bypass remains the reference standard for extensive calcific occlusions, particularly TASC II D lesions, because of its superior long-term patency[4,10,11]. In this patient, complete distal aortic occlusion with heavy circumferential calcification extending into the iliac arteries made durable endovascular reconstruction less favorable. Open aortoiliac endarterectomy followed by aortobifemoral bypass, therefore, represented the most appropriate strategy for definitive inflow reconstruction[12,13].

The principal challenge in this case was not the operative technique itself but determining the appropriate timing of surgery despite persistent severe anemia. Although preoperative optimization is generally recommended before major vascular procedures, a prolonged delay risked irreversible ischemic progression. Following a hematology consultation, the patient underwent serial transfusions; however, the limited and transient hemoglobin response, together with the clinical suspicion of AIHA, suggested that further postponement was unlikely to provide meaningful hematologic benefit. In view of the ongoing tissue loss and risk of limb loss, expedited revascularization was therefore undertaken.

Although the operative procedure represents the standard management for advanced AIOD, this case demonstrates that successful limb salvage depends on individualized perioperative decision-making when significant hematologic comorbidity coexists. Rather than introducing a novel surgical technique, this report illustrates how timely revascularization can still be achieved through multidisciplinary planning and meticulous operative execution despite suspected AIHA and persistent anemia.

The present report is limited by its single-patient design, lack of confirmatory immunohematologic testing, and absence of long-term follow-up regarding graft patency, limb salvage, and hematologic outcomes.

Conclusions

AIOD causing CLTI requires timely revascularization to prevent progressive tissue and limb loss. This case demonstrates that open aortobifemoral bypass remains an effective treatment for extensive AIOD, even when perioperative management is complicated by severe anemia and suspected AIHA. Rather than illustrating a novel operative technique, this report highlights the importance of individualized perioperative decision-making that balances hematologic optimization against the need for timely limb salvage. Further studies are warranted to better define perioperative management strategies for patients with severe anemia undergoing major vascular reconstruction.
