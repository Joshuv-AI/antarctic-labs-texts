Eye-related emergency calls and prehospital management in region Zealand, Danmark: a register-based cohort study
Authors: Natalie Jensen, Stig Nikolaj Fasmer Blomberg, Torben Lykke Sørensen, Amalie Elisabeth Thiele-Nygaard, Thea Palsgaard Møller, Magnus Rafner, Astrid Merete Blomberg, Helle Collatz Christensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEye-related symptoms ranging from mild irritation to acute vision loss can lead to emergency calls. While many cases are benign, others involve chemical exposures or trauma requiring urgent care to prevent permanent damage. Prehospital characteristics, dispatch categorization, and early management of eye-related emergencies remain unexplored. The aim of this study was to provide a systematic description of eye-related emergency calls, including patient characteristics, dispatch categorization, and prehospital management, within the framework of the Danish Index for Emergency Care.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of all emergency calls [1–1–2] in Region Zealand, Denmark, from November 2017 to June 2025, describing eye symptoms. We linked data from the Computer-Aided Dispatch (CAD) system, the Prehospital Patient Records (PPJ), and the Danish National Patient Registry (NPR). Patient demographics, paramedic assessment of primary problem, on-scene symptoms, prehospital treatments and hospital outcomes were analysed using descriptive statistics.ResultsA total of 376 patients were identified calling 1-1-2 and registered with eye related symptoms as the main complaint. Of these, 36 (9.6%) were excluded due to missing patient identifiers or prehospital patient records, leaving 340 in the cohort. Median age was 49.5 years (IQR 29–68), and 62.1% were male. Most calls were categorized as priority B (urgent situation not assessed as acute life-threatening) (70.9%). The leading primary problems were chemical exposures (31.5%), vision loss (25.9%), and ocular trauma (18.2%). Vision disturbance (54.4%) was the most frequently prehospital reported symptom. Prehospital interventions were rarely documented, with irrigation being the most reported procedure (27.1%). Notably, 39 of 340 patients (11.5%) were suspected of stroke prehospitally, and among 283 admitted to hospital, 21 (7.4%) received a confirmed TIA or stroke diagnosis. The median length of stay was 3.7 h (IQR 2.0–7.7). Most frequent discharge diagnoses were injury of the eye and orbit (17.7%), burn and corrosion confined to eye and adnexa (9.9%), and foreign body on external eye (7.8%).ConclusionEye-related emergency calls were primarily caused by chemical exposures, vision loss, and trauma, with relatively short hospital stays. Several patients classified as eye-related problems were later diagnosed with stroke, highlighting the need for better dispatcher recognition of visual stroke symptoms.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13049-026-01618-0.

Background/Introduction

Eye-related symptoms, ranging from mild irritation and redness to severe complaints such as blurred or acute vision loss can result in contact with emergency medical services, even though this is a rare occurrence [1, 2]. National data support this, showing approximately 850 eye-related contacts out of nearly 440,000 total emergency dispatches in Denmark in 2023 [2]. While many of the underlying conditions are benign, others represent serious ocular injury or potentially sight-threatening disease that requires timely intervention [1, 3]. Moreover, ocular symptoms may occasionally constitute early manifestations of systemic disease, including stroke(4–6].

Ocular trauma and chemical exposures represent a substantial proportion of acute eye emergencies. Chemical eye injuries, most often caused by alkali agents, are especially injurious and disproportionately affect young men [1, 7]. Such injuries are associated with long‑term visual impairment or blindness, particularly in younger individuals, and entail a significant societal and personal burden [1].

Even though emergency department presentations for eye conditions have been described in the literature [8], prehospital management of eye‑related emergencies remains poorly characterized. In Denmark, emergency [1, 2] calls are categorized using the Danish Index for Emergency Care [1, 9]. Yet no studies have explored the prehospital symptom profile, dispatch categorization, or immediate care pathways for these patients. This knowledge gap motivates the present study, which aims to inform improvements in dispatch accuracy, early triage, and patient outcomes.

The aim of this study was to describe the population of patients categorized under eye-related symptoms in the Danish Index for Emergency Care with dispatch priority A (acute situation assessed as potentially life-threatening) or B (urgent situation but not assessed as acute life-threatening) [9] in Region Zealand, Denmark, including the reported symptom at call, dispatch categorization, and subsequent prehospital and hospital management.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective registry-based cohort study describes 1-1-2 emergency calls categorized as eye-related conditions according to the Danish Index for Emergency Care.

Setting

The study is conducted in Region Zealand, Denmark, which covers an area of 7,227 km² with an estimated population of 855,000 in 2025, corresponding to a population density of about 118 inhabitants per km² [10]. In Denmark, the healthcare system, including prehospital emergency medical services (EMS) [11], is tax-funded, free of charge and equally accessible for all [9, 12]. Citizens are encouraged to call 1-1-2 in life-threatening situations, where the calls are initially answered by medically trained dispatchers (e.g. nurses or paramedics), who assess the degree of urgency using the Danish Index for Emergency Care, a national criteria-based dispatch protocol, which guides the dispatchers in determining the appropriate response and level of care needed [9, 11, 12]. In addition, Denmark has a nationwide out-of-hours primary care (OOH-PC) system, which together with EMS provides telephone triage around the clock. The patients are advised to contact OOH-PC in less urgent cases that cannot wait until the patient’s own general practitioner is available [12].

The linkage between data sources was enabled through the Danish Civil Registration System (CPR number), a unique identifier used across all healthcare records in Denmark [13].

Data sources and variables

Data were collected retrospectively from three sources

The Computer-Aided Dispatch (CAD) system provides information on call timestamps, dispatch category, and priority level assigned by medical dispatchers using the Danish Index for Emergency Care [14].

The Prehospital Patient Record (PPJ) comprises the national prehospital patient records with paramedic assessments, treatments administered, and free-text clinical notes [9].

A structured predefined data collection form was created in REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) [15, 16] to enable systematic manual review of all PPJ entries. This secure, web-based platform is hosted by Region Zealand and was specifically tailored for this study. Data on paramedic assessment of primary problem, on-scene symptoms, and prehospital treatments were collected. All variables were categorized retrospectively based on free-text documentation from ambulance personnel. The form included overarching symptom categories with relevant subcategories (e.g. vision disturbances subdivided into vision loss, blurred vision, and other visual symptoms), as well as systemic symptoms (altered consciousness, dizziness, hemiparesis, or headache). Prehospital treatments were documented (such as irrigation, removal of foreign bodies, medical treatment, eye bandaging, or no treatment), along with advice from a medical professional (e.g. the poison hotline), if applicable. The form allowed checkbox registration of multiple symptoms per patient to accurately capture complex presentations, as well as open text fields for “other” entries to ensure data completeness. Variables were defined based on preliminary review of patient records and existing literature [1, 5, 17] to ensure clinical relevance and consistency during data collection. Among all identified cases, a particular note was made of patients presenting with stroke-like symptoms that were potentially misclassified as eye-related problems within the dispatch system [4, 17–19]. Stroke suspicion was determined retrospectively based on paramedics’ assessment on-scene and subsequent documentation in the PPJ.

Finally, the Danish National Patient Registry (NPR) includes data on hospital admission dates, discharge diagnoses coded in ICD-10, and length of stay [20].

Study population

The study population included all individuals in the Region of Zealand who, during the period from November 1, 2017, to June 30, 2025, were registered under Chap. 37 (eye-related problems) in the Danish Index for Emergency Care. There were no age restrictions. Patients dispatched with priority A (acute situation assessed as potentially life threatening) or B (urgent situation but not assessed as acute life threatening) were included in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed using RStudio version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Categorical variables are presented as absolute numbers and percentages. Continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). No inferential statistics were performed, as the study is exploratory and descriptive in nature.

Ethics approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, using registry data, and all information was pseudonymised prior to analysis. Data handling complied with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and national regulations for health data governance. No formal ethical approval is needed for register-based studies, according to the regional scientific ethical committee in Region Zealand, who waived need for approval. The project was approved as a quality assurance study by Region Zealand (ID: EMN-2025-07436) and reported to the Danish Data Protection Agency. In accordance with Danish law, individual patient consent was not required for registry-based quality assurance projects [21].

Results

During the study period, 376 patients were identified through CAD data under Chap. 37 of the Danish Index for Emergency Care. Of these, 36 (9.6%) were excluded due to missing CPR numbers or otherwise inaccessible prehospital patient records, leaving a final cohort of 340 patients (Fig. 1). During data validation, three patients initially included as ocular cases under Chap. 37 were identified as misclassified. These patients presented with thorax trauma, overdose, and COPD, respectively. As the study population includes all patients identified through the Danish Index for Emergency Care, regardless of subsequent verification, misclassified patients were retained in the analysis. In the same period, the EMS in Region Zealand received approximately 555,782 emergency calls in total, of which eye-related contacts represented only 0.07%, highlighting that such cases constitute a very small fraction of overall dispatches.

In the full cohort (N = 340), the median age was 49.5 years (IQR 29–68), and 62.1% were male (Table 1). Most cases were assigned dispatch priority B (70.9%), while 29.1% received priority A. Calls occurred most frequently in the morning (50.0%), followed by evening (34.1%) and night (15.9%). Nearly three-quarters of calls were made on weekdays (73.2%).

Incidents most often occurred in the home (32.4% of the 340 patients), at work (12.9%), or in public locations (12.1%). Location was undocumented in 42.6% of cases. The distribution of paramedic assessments of primary problems among all 340 patients showed that chemical exposures accounted for 31.5% of cases, vision loss for 25.9%, and ocular trauma for 18.2%. Foreign bodies (6.2%), red eye (2.1%), and other causes (15.3%) were less frequent. Notably, 39 patients (11.5% of the cohort) were retrospectively identified as suspected stroke based on PPJ data, and among the 283 admitted patients, 21 (7.4%) were diagnosed with TIA (transient ischemic attacks) or stroke at hospital discharge according to NPR data. Of the remaining 57 non-admitted patients, all were non-conveyed and left on-scene after assessment and, where applicable, treatment by the paramedics.

The most frequently reported prehospital symptoms among the 340 patients were vision disturbance (54.4%), abnormal eye appearance (40.6%), and pain (34.4%) (Fig. 2). Systemic symptoms were present in 17.9% of cases, and other symptoms in 5.6%. Laterality was most often unilateral, affecting the left eye in 31.2% and the right eye in 29.1%, while 23.5% of patients presented with bilateral involvement. In the remaining 16.2%, laterality was not documented.

In total, 283 patients were admitted to hospital. The median length of stay was 3.7 h (IQR 2.0–7.7). Among these admitted patients, the most frequent discharge diagnoses were injury of eye and orbit (17.7%), burn and corrosion confined to eye and adnexa (9.9%), and foreign body on external eye (7.8%). Neurological diagnoses included transient cerebral ischemic attacks and related syndromes (3.9%) and cerebral infarction (3.5%), together 7.4% of the admitted patients. Less frequent diagnoses were visual disturbances (6.4%), blindness or low vision (2.8%), and superficial injury of head (2.8%) (Table 2).

Discussion

This study provides the first systematic description of eye-related emergency calls categorized under the Danish Index for Emergency Care, drawing on linked patient information from CAD, PPJ, and the National Patient Registry, thereby filling a previously unaddressed gap in prehospital research. In total, 376 emergency dispatches with eye-related symptoms were identified out of 555,782 missions in the study period. Thirty-six cases were excluded due to missing or inaccessible prehospital patient records, leaving 340 patients in the final cohort. Although constituting only a small fraction, this proportion is consistent with national figures from the Prehospital Database, where 850 eye-related dispatches were recorded out of approximately 440,000 (0.19%) total dispatches in 2023 [2]. Chemical exposures, ocular trauma, and vision loss were the predominant paramedic assessments of primary problem, while visual disturbance was the most frequently reported on scene symptom. Among all 340 patients, most calls occurred during daytime on weekdays, hospital stays were relatively short, and notably, 39 patients (11.5%) were suspected of stroke based on prehospital records, although only 21 patients (7.4%) of those admitted (N = 283) received a confirmed diagnosis of TIA or stroke at hospital discharge.

In comparison with existing emergency department literature, 1.5–3.4% of all ED visits including walk-in patients of varying acuity are reported to have an ophthalmic principal diagnosis [8, 22, 23], most frequently among younger men and related to ocular trauma and chemical exposure [1, 8]. However, this comparison must be interpreted with caution, as our study focuses exclusively on 1-1-2 dispatched cases, which inherently represent a higher-acuity and more selected population than the broader ED population. In emergency department studies, chemical eye injuries account for only 3.3% of all eye-related emergencies [8]. In contrast, this prehospital cohort (N = 340) demonstrated a markedly higher proportion of chemical exposures (31.5%). Since chemical eye injuries can be severe and time-critical and immediate irrigation is required as the most important determinant of visual outcome in chemical eye injuries [1], patients and bystanders are likely to seek urgent ambulance assistance. This explains why these cases are disproportionately represented in the prehospital setting whereas less acute conditions such as ocular trauma and e.g. conjunctivitis is more commonly reported in emergency department studies [8, 22, 23].

Another key finding was the - while small in absolute numbers - considerable proportion of strokes hidden under eye-related dispatch codes. This aligns with growing evidence that atypical or non-classical presentations, such as isolated visual disturbances, are frequently overlooked in dispatcher assessments. In a comparable Danish region, the Capital Region of Denmark, only 30% of stroke patients with visual symptoms as the primary complaint are correctly triaged to hospital by a high-priority ambulance [4] and the dispatcher sensitivity for stroke has been reported as 64% in 1-1-2 calls and 25% in out-of-hours service calls [6], underscoring the difficulty of recognizing visual symptoms as stroke manifestations. Since Danish citizens are generally advised to contact out-of-hours primary care when experiencing non-life- or non-limb-threatening emergencies and reserve 1-1-2 for life- or limb-threatening emergencies [12], some patients with visual stroke symptoms that could seem non-life- or limb-threatening may contact out-of-hours primary care instead of the EMS. Thus, the true prehospital burden of stroke patients initially categorized under eye-related problems may be even greater than the 39 of 340 patients (11.5%) suspected of stroke in this study. Of these, 21 patients (7.4% of those admitted) ultimately received a confirmed diagnosis of TIA or stroke at hospital discharge. Correct dispatcher recognition of acute ischemic stroke has been associated with significantly shorter prehospital delays, higher rates of direct admission to stroke centres, and greater likelihood of receiving reperfusion treatment [24]. Taken together, these findings emphasize that dispatcher misclassification can have profound clinical consequences. Our results add to the existing literature by demonstrating that the “eye symptoms” chapter of the Danish Index for Emergency Care represents a specific context in which such misclassification occurs. These findings highlight the need for improved dispatcher training to ensure that stroke patients presenting with visual symptoms are categorized under stroke rather than eye-related categories.

A marked increase in the number of patients categorized under eye-related symptoms was observed from 2021 to 2022 (Fig. 3). This rise coincided with a doubling of staff at the AMK Emergency Medical Dispatch Centre, introducing many new employees. This organizational change may have influenced triage practices, potentially leading to a lower threshold for dispatching ambulances for ocular complaints, although this remains speculative.

This study has several strengths. A key strength was the linkage of administrative data from the dispatch centre with prehospital patient records (PPJ) and the National Patient Registry (NPR), which enabled patient trajectories to be followed from the initial emergency call through to hospital diagnosis. The use of a complete regional dataset with pseudonymized registry data reduced selection bias and ensured both high validity and methodological robustness.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the dispatch data were collected for administrative and clinical rather than research purposes, which may introduce information bias due to misclassification and incomplete documentation. The 8-year study period (2015–2022) may also have introduced temporal bias, as changes in dispatch protocols, EMS staffing, or documentation practices could affect classifications over time. The Danish Index Chap. 37 reflects the symptom prompting the call, not necessarily the final diagnosis, and three patients were entirely misclassified as non-ocular cases. Second, 36 patients were excluded due to missing prehospital records, which may contribute to selection bias. Third, prehospital interventions were often poorly documented, further limiting the analysis. Additionally, by including only priority A and B calls, we excluded less urgent eye-related contacts (e.g., priority C or D), limiting our ability to characterize the full spectrum of ocular emergency cases. Finally, the regional design may limit the generalizability of findings to other EMS systems.

Our findings demonstrate substantial variability in the accuracy of prehospital classification of ocular cases, as evidenced by the discrepancy between dispatch categories and eventual hospital diagnoses. The observed variability, including potential over-triage after staffing changes, highlights opportunities to optimize dispatch protocols.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that eye-related emergency calls occurred mainly during daytime and were primarily caused by chemical exposures and ocular trauma. Although such calls accounted for only a very small proportion of all emergency calls (approximately 0.07% of all EMS contacts during the study period), a substantial subset of suspected strokes was concealed under eye-related categories, underscoring the vulnerability of patients presenting with visual symptoms. These findings highlight the need for improved categorization of visual symptoms to prevent stroke cases from remaining hidden within eye-related dispatch categories.
