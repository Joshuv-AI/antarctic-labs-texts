Economic Impact of Timely Systemic Treatment of Psoriatic Disease in Comorbidity Risk Reduction: Literature Review and (Early) Economic Assessment
Authors: Nicholas Norton, Zoltan Nemeth, Soumya Nanda, Abhishek Dhirta, Kirk Geale, Raquel Vaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPsoriatic disease, including psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis, is associated with increased risk of comorbidities (cardiovascular disease, mental health disorders, type 2 diabetes mellitus and obesity), placing substantial burden on patients and health systems. We aimed to quantify the lifetime economic burden of these four comorbidities in populations with moderate-to-severe psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis. We subsequently estimated the potential comorbidity-related gross cost offsets from timely systemic therapy across three diverse country settings (Denmark, the USA and Vietnam).MethodsWe conducted a literature review to identify data on prevalence, comorbidity risk, treatment-associated risk reduction and cost inputs, followed by development of a static cohort-based economic model from a societal perspective. The model compared untreated/undertreated moderate-to-severe psoriasis/psoriatic arthritis; and systemic treatment scenarios (conventional systemic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs [csDMARDs]; biologic DMARDs [bDMARDs]; and targeted synthetic DMARDs [tsDMARDs]) over a lifetime horizon in Denmark, the USA and Vietnam, reporting total comorbidity cost offsets across direct (healthcare) and indirect (societal) costs.ResultsSystemic treatment reduced lifetime comorbidity-related direct and societal costs across settings. In the USA, patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis incurred annual direct costs of approximately 4.6 billion USD (indirect 0.6 billion USD), and untreated lifetime burden of about 153.0 billion USD. Treating all patients with bDMARDs yielded an estimated cost offset of 62.9 billion USD in the direct costs and an offset of 8.1 billion USD in the societal costs compared with a fully undertreated population. Equivalent analyses in Denmark and Vietnam reflected similar effects on comorbidity-related costs. The model shows that early systemic therapy yields substantial effect on the direct and societal costs via reduced comorbidity incidence.ConclusionsTimely and efficacious systemic treatment of psoriatic disease can significantly reduce lifetime economic burden of comorbidities across diverse healthcare settings. These findings underscore the value of early, proactive management of psoriatic disease and highlight the need for further cost-effectiveness research, including treatment uptake and real-world comorbidity prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-026-01814-x.

Introduction

Psoriatic disease is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory condition encompassing psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis, which share systemic pathogenic pathways [1]. Approximately 30% of patients with psoriasis develop psoriatic arthritis, underscoring the close clinical relationship between cutaneous and musculoskeletal involvement [2]. As diagnosis and surveillance improve, global psoriatic disease incidence continues to rise, having increased by an estimated 26.5% between 1990 and 2019 [3–5], revealing a growing burden for patients and health systems.

Psoriatic disease is associated with elevated risks of several comorbidities, including cardiovascular disease (CVD) [6, 7]; mental health disorders (MHDs) encompassing depression, anxiety and suicidality [8–10]; type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [11]; and obesity [12], each compounding morbidity, mortality and costs [13–21]. In the USA, patients with psoriasis and a comorbidity experience roughly double the hospital use and costs of non-comorbid patients [22]; those with both psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis incur about 9900 USD in additional annual costs, while those with CVD add 8275 USD annually [23]. Moreover, patients with psoriatic disease face higher comorbidity-related costs than non-psoriatic disease populations [24].

Beyond financial burden, psoriatic disease and its comorbidities reduce quality of life, work ability and mental well-being. Psoriatic disease and some of its comorbidities share inflammatory pathways, including tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-17, IL-23 and Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK–STAT) signalling, that drive cutaneous and systemic manifestations [25, 26]. Modern biologics and JAK inhibitors target components of these pathways [26–28], while conventional systemics such as methotrexate exert broader immunomodulatory effects that reduce overall inflammatory activity [29, 30]. Effective systemic treatment may lower systemic inflammatory load, providing a biologically plausible basis for reduced risks of cardiovascular, metabolic and mental health comorbidities observed in emerging evidence. Recent studies suggest that timely systemic therapy for moderate-to-severe psoriasis can lower risks of developing psoriatic arthritis [31], CVD [32, 33], MHDs [34, 35], T2DM [34] and obesity [36]. Here, ‘timely’ refers to initiating appropriate systemic therapy early in the disease course, before prolonged inflammation increases likelihood of irreversible joint damage and comorbidities. Consistent with this, international guidelines recommend early detection and management of comorbidities [13, 14, 37].

Previous studies have examined the overall economic burden of psoriatic disease [24, 38–42], but few have directly assessed the economic impact of systemic treatment on psoriatic disease-related comorbidities. This study quantifies the potential effect of timely systemic treatment on the cost of specific comorbidities in psoriatic disease. We conducted [1] a literature review to collect data on epidemiology and costs of four comorbidities (CVD, MHDs, T2DM and obesity) and [2] an economic model estimating lifetime comorbidity costs among patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis in Denmark, the USA and Vietnam. The model evaluates the potential offset in comorbidity-related gross costs that timely treatment with one of three systemic therapy classes could achieve, through a reduction in comorbidity risks. Improving access to effective systemic therapies remains a key priority, as delays in appropriate treatment contribute to preventable burden. Quantifying downstream comorbidity impacts can inform resource allocation, strengthen arguments for early intervention and support efforts to reduce global disparities in psoriatic disease care.

Methods

This study combined a literature review with a static economic burden model. A protocol was defined a priori, and the review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, including a flow diagram, two-stage study selection and comprehensive referencing. The review and model were developed iteratively to allow available data to inform model structure and ensure methodological transparency and reproducibility.

Literature Review

A structured, protocol-driven targeted literature review (TLR) was conducted to identify published and grey literature relevant to comorbidity burden and model input parameters in psoriatic disease. The search strategy was predefined and implemented primarily in Embase, with supplementary searches conducted via Google to capture grey literature and additional relevant sources.

Eligibility criteria were prospectively defined within the study protocol and refined as appropriate for each model variable. All retrieved records underwent systematic screening by one reviewer, with independent random quality checks performed by a second reviewer to ensure consistency and minimize selection bias. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion.

Data were extracted in a standardized extraction grid to ensure consistency in capturing study characteristics and parameter estimates. Identified evidence was assessed for methodological quality, relevance and applicability to the predefined modelling objectives. While all eligible sources were reviewed, final parameter selection followed predefined prioritization criteria, emphasizing recency, methodological robustness, demographic comparability to the target population, and country-specific relevance where applicable.

In instances where direct evidence was unavailable, the most clinically and methodologically comparable relative risk or cost estimates were applied using a structured hierarchy of evidence to preserve internal consistency across comorbidity risks, treatment effects and economic parameters.

The target population was defined as adults with moderate-to-severe psoriasis or psoriatic arthritis, representing individuals at elevated risk of comorbidities and the primary candidates for systemic therapy [13, 37, 43].

Economic Model

A cohort-based simulation model estimated the excess lifetime burden of four psoriatic disease comorbidities (CVD, MHDs, T2DM and obesity) and potential comorbidity-related gross cost offsets associated with timely systemic treatment with csDMARDs (methotrexate, cyclosporine and acitretin), bDMARDs (TNF- α, IL 12/12, IL 17 and IL 23) or tsDMARDs (apremilast). The analysis used a simple decision tree comparing systemic treatment versus topical/no intervention. This static structure applied treatment-associated relative risk reductions that may translate into lower comorbidity incidence. The analysis extrapolated comorbidity-related costs over a lifetime horizon, without modelling time-dependent transitions, disease progression or treatment discontinuation. Given the simplified structure and absence of longitudinal disease dynamics, the projected differences in comorbidity burden should be interpreted as scenario-based estimates rather than definitive real-world estimates.

On the basis of the shared inflammatory pathways affected by systemic treatments, two comparison groups were defined:Topical/no intervention: all patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis or psoriatic arthritis, untreated or receiving only topical therapy.Systemic treatment: all patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis or psoriatic arthritis, receiving, and adhering to csDMARDs, bDMARDs or tsDMARDs.

This comparison provides a biologically grounded contrast for estimating the comorbidity-related cost offset attributable to systemic therapy.

We modelled the treatment effect of csDMARDs, bDMARDs and tsDMARDs using an overall relative risk reduction (RRR) applied at the drug-class level. The csDMARD RRR was based on estimates for methotrexate, cyclosporine and acitretin. The bDMARD RRR was based on biologics targeting TNF-α, IL-12/23, IL-17 and IL-23. The tsDMARD RRR was based on apremilast. These classes include treatments with different mechanisms and effect sizes, so the groups are heterogeneous. We did not model the distribution of treatments within each class and therefore assumed equal weighting across included therapies when estimating the RRR.

Some treatment effects were taken directly from the literature, while others were estimated using differences in comorbidity prevalence between treatment groups reported in observational studies. Relative risks for treatments without direct evidence were estimated by scaling a known relative risk using the ratio of observed comorbidity prevalence between treatment groups.

The T2DM risk reductions for bDMARDs and tsDMARDs were then derived by adjusting the csDMARD estimate according to the differences in T2DM prevalence observed between treatment groups. This approach assumed that treatments associated with lower T2DM prevalence correspond to greater reductions in T2DM risk.

The obesity risk reductions for csDMARDs and bDMARDs were derived from the tsDMARD obesity estimate (25%) [36]. Specifically, the tsDMARD estimate was adjusted using the same proportional differences between treatments observed for T2DM prevalence, assuming that the relative differences between treatments for obesity follow a similar pattern to those observed for T2DM.

Finally, the CVD risk reduction for csDMARDs was estimated by combining the bDMARD CVD effect with the difference in CVD prevalence between bDMARD and csDMARD groups, and the tsDMARD CVD effect was derived by adjusting the bDMARD estimate using the difference in myocardial infarction prevalence between tsDMARD and bDMARD groups. This approach allowed treatment effects to be estimated consistently across therapies despite limited direct comparative evidence.

This approach enables estimation of treatment effects when direct evidence is lacking by using observational prevalence data. However, differences in prevalence may reflect confounding rather than causal treatment effects, and assuming similar patterns across comorbidities introduces additional uncertainty.

The analysis did not incorporate condition-specific prevention of comorbidities, such as statin treatment for CVD, glucose-lowering treatment for diabetes or targeted interventions for obesity and depression. The model therefore focused solely on psoriasis-directed treatment and its associated relative risk reductions. As a result, the estimated comorbidity burden reflects a simplified, psoriasis-centred scenario and may not fully represent real-world risk modification achieved through standard preventive care. By isolating psoriasis treatment-associated risk effects, the analysis aims to clarify the extent to which psoriasis-directed therapy alone may influence projected comorbidity costs.

Example populations were defined using prevalence data from Denmark, the USA and Vietnam, selected for data availability and geographic diversity. In Denmark and Vietnam, we estimated the annual direct costs attributable to comorbidities in patients with psoriasis (CVD, T2DM, obesity and MHDs) using a two-step, top-down approach owing to limited country-specific data on comorbidity-attributable costs.

First, we used published estimates of the total annual direct cost of psoriasis in each country and inflated these values to 2025 price level. Subsequently, we estimated the proportion of costs attributable to the selected comorbidities on the basis of Feldman et al. (2017), who reported the annual direct costs for patients with psoriasis, with or without comorbidities [23]. We estimated that, on average, patients with psoriasis and comorbidities have 33% higher annual costs. This proportion was applied to the Danish and Vietnamese total psoriasis-related costs to estimate the direct costs attributable to the comorbidities in Denmark and Vietnam. Lastly, we distributed this total comorbidity-related cost across individual conditions using the relative incremental cost contribution of each comorbidity reported in the same study.

This method provides pragmatic and internally consistent estimates but assumes that USA-derived comorbidity cost distributions are transferable to Denmark and Vietnam; therefore, the results should be interpreted as modelled projections rather than empirically observed comorbidity-attributable costs.

In the absence of country-specific comorbidity risk estimates for Denmark and Vietnam, baseline prevalence inputs were sourced from Germany and China, respectively. China was selected as a comparator for Vietnam primarily owing to geographic proximity. Germany was selected as a comparator for Denmark because both are high-income European countries. These proxy risk estimates were applied without country-specific recalibration. Although this approach enabled model parametrisation in the absence of local data, differences in epidemiology, healthcare utilisation and diagnostic practices may limit direct comparability; therefore, the results should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

A lifetime horizon captured comorbidity-related costs over remaining life expectancy, assuming equal survival across treatment groups. Outputs included total and per-capita lifetime costs for each treatment group, and direct and indirect gross cost offsets by treatment type. Model assumptions and inputs were informed by the literature review and are detailed in the Results section.

Ethical Considerations and Authorship

No human subjects or animals were involved; ethical approval was not required. The study was conceptually informed by patient advocacy stakeholders. All sources are cited, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) standards (flowchart and checklist) were followed and funding, conflicts of interest and author contributions (per ICMJE criteria) are disclosed.

Country-specific estimates were used where possible; otherwise, global or proxy data were applied with assumptions documented. Owing to limited evidence for MHDs as a group, depression was used as a representative condition.

Economic Model Results

Psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis prevalence for each country was estimated by applying prevalence rates to 2025 populations, followed by proportions with moderate-to-severe psoriasis. Comorbidity prevalence was calculated using comorbidity-specific rates (local or proxy). A one-time sustained relative risk reduction reflected systemic treatment effects. Direct (healthcare) and indirect (societal, captured as productivity loss) costs were estimated separately for systemic and non-systemic/no treatment groups. The annual cost reduction represents the difference between the estimates for these groups. Lifetime comorbidity-related cost reduction was calculated by multiplying the annual cost reduction by the remaining life expectancy. Survival gains from reduced comorbidity prevalence were not modelled to avoid added uncertainty.

Key Model Assumptions

Treatment effects were assumed to produce immediate and sustained reductions in comorbidity prevalence over the lifetime horizon, with full adherence to therapy and no contraindications or adverse events. Effects for csDMARDs, bDMARDs and tsDMARDs were estimated from available data on specific treatments within each class and treated as representative averages. Treatment effects on the risks were assumed equal across psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis and consistent across countries. No discontinuation or switching between systemic treatments was modelled; instead, each treatment type was assumed to apply uniformly to all patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis or psoriatic arthritis. Consequently, the model does not incorporate treatment mix within countries. With that, comorbidity-related gross cost offsets calculations were performed assuming the entire patient population would be given the specific DMARD being considered. This is not a realistic real-world scenario but provides an estimate of the magnitude of the impact of treatment on comorbidity-related costs.

Model Findings: Denmark

Our calculations estimated that annual direct and indirect costs for the four comorbidities among Danish patients with psoriasis were 24.0 million euros and 30.9 million euros, corresponding to a 29-year burden of 1.4 billion euros. For psoriatic arthritis, annual costs were 58.5 million euros (direct) and 80.4 million euros (indirect), totalling 3.5 billion euros. Systemic treatment substantially reduced this comorbidity-related burden. Treating all patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis with bDMARDs yielded lifetime gross cost offsets of 283.5 million euros (direct) and 330.6 million euros (indirect), a 43.9% offset of the comorbidity-related cost. For psoriatic arthritis, 1.2 billion euros represented a 34.4% offset. The average direct and indirect lifetime cost offsets per comorbid patient were 35,753 € and 43,199 €, respectively. The effect of DMARDs on comorbidity costs in Denmark was lower for csDMARDs compared with bDMARDs (Table 6). Discussion

This study provides an assessment of how timely and efficacious systemic treatment for psoriatic disease could reduce the economic burden of the four comorbidities studied. Our model synthesized evidence on psoriatic disease comorbidity burden and showed that a proportion of lifetime costs may be preventable through timely systemic therapy, thus potentially offsetting comorbidity-related direct and societal gross cost, depending on the setting. The analysis focused on moderate-to-severe psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis, which are both well-studied and primary candidates for systemic therapy. The effects on the comorbidity-related costs were estimated by comparing an undertreated population with one receiving full systemic treatment; real-world treatment mixes would yield intermediate results.

The three modelled countries (Denmark, the USA and Vietnam) reflected diverse health economic contexts. Across all examples, systemic therapy reduced comorbidity-related costs, with bDMARDs showing the greatest effect on comorbidity costs, followed by tsDMARDs and csDMARDs. Some country-specific observations emerged: Denmark’s high societal burden reflects comprehensive national registers and a publicly financed healthcare that reduces the burden on patients; in the USA, direct costs dominate, suggesting that data sources under-report productivity loss, underestimating indirect burden. High out-of-pocket costs highlight affordability challenges in the USA among the 25 million uninsured and 21% underinsured adults [47, 48]. Vietnam’s relatively low societal costs likely reflect data gaps; for example, evidence on caregiver burden for T2DM suggests under-reported indirect impacts [49, 50].

Stakeholder Perspectives

For patients, reducing comorbidity risks improves both quality and length of life. Early systemic therapy may reduce additional illnesses, lower medication needs and enhance well-being [39–41, 51]. Multidisciplinary care further improves outcomes and mental health [52–54], while delayed or insufficient treatment can worsen morbidity and mortality [55]. For policy-makers, the findings support early, comprehensive psoriatic disease management. Although systemic therapies have upfront costs, the downstream cost reductions due to the effects of systemic treatment may offset these [56] and should be reflected in Heatlh Technology Assessment (HTA) processes. Expanding access to biosimilars [57–60] may further reduce treatment costs, and strengthening screening for cardiovascular, metabolic and mental health risks remains important for holistic psoriatic disease care and disease prevention.

Study Strengths

To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify the effect of systemic psoriatic disease treatment on comorbidity-related costs, due to reduced comorbidity risks. Integrating a literature review with an economic model enabled synthesis of existing evidence and projection of population-level impacts. By including multiple comorbidities, treatment types, and both direct and indirect costs, this study offers a broad view of the health and economic implications of psoriatic disease. Similar findings in other diseases, such as cardiovascular risks in sleep apnea [61], late-life depression [62] and diabetes [63], reinforce the value of early intervention.

Study Limitations

The review, while structured, was not fully systematic, and included studies varied in quality and outcome definitions. Limited evidence on long-term treatment effects required assumptions of similar relative risk reductions across treatments and geographies, with durability over a lifetime, which may not accurately reflect real-life experiences. Most included studies were observational and cannot reliably provide causal estimates. Approximately 30% of patients with psoriasis develop psoriatic arthritis, so overlap between populations could affect risk estimates.

The model is a simplified representation of economic benefit from systemic treatment, assuming linear, sustained treatment effects, and disease progression. The side effects of systemic treatment were not modelled. We also assume immediate onset of treatment effect, perfect adherence and sustained reductions in comorbidity risk over the entire time horizon. Real-world treatment patterns, such as delayed response, partial adherence, treatment switching or waning effect, could result in smaller or more variable reductions than those estimated here. The model therefore does not estimate real-world incremental value but rather the comorbidity-related cost offsets attributable to systemic treatment under a full-uptake scenario. Formal deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses were also not conducted, as several parameters were based on pragmatic assumptions without sufficient underlying evidence to define reliable parameter distributions or plausible ranges.

The model also did not account for concomitant standard preventive care commonly used in patients with psoriatic disease and cardiometabolic risk factors, including statins, antihypertensive therapy, glucose-lowering medications, weight-management interventions or structured mental health treatment. These background interventions can independently manage or prevent the comorbidities modelled and may interact with systemic psoriasis therapies in real-world care pathways. By isolating psoriasis-directed therapy effects, the analysis was intended to estimate the potential contribution of systemic treatment alone; however, excluding broader preventive management may inflate the incremental benefits attributed to systemic therapy.

Taken together, these structural assumptions mean that the model should be interpreted as an exploratory scenario analysis designed to illustrate the possible scale of avoidable comorbidity burden rather than a definitive prediction of real-world cost offsets.

While uncertainty intervals were not calculated, the model highlights potential underestimation of indirect costs due to data limitations. Importantly, the model and calculations are dependent on data available, such as psoriatic disease prevalence data. Differences in prevalence have been shown across multiple sources. These differences arise primarily from the use of different methodologies to assess how many people live with psoriatic disease. In the USA, for example, the second edition of the Global Psoriasis Atlas estimates 1.12% prevalence of psoriasis [64], while sources such as the National Psoriasis Foundation and Armstrong et al. (2021) estimate it at 3% [65, 66]. It is likely that the real prevalence falls in between these sources, suggesting that the total comorbidity-related burden and gross cost offsets may be higher than we conservatively estimated using the Global Psoriasis Atlas estimate.

Most of the identified evidence on treatment-associated risk offsets come from observational studies in which patients receiving systemic therapy often have more severe disease and higher baseline comorbidity risk than untreated groups. Moreover, different treatments within the same group of DMARDs show a different risk reduction estimate, and for some treatments, there is no information on whether and how they may impact comorbidities. As a result, treatment effects derived from these populations may not perfectly reflect risk profiles across the full spectrum of moderate-to-severe psoriatic disease. While this could mean that absolute risk reductions vary across subgroups, the directionality of effect is unlikely to differ, and our population-level model appropriately reflects average expected impacts under a full-uptake scenario.

Generalisability of the results is limited; data inputs were derived from a small number of countries, and healthcare structures, access or population characteristics may differ elsewhere. Because lifetime cost projections accumulate effects over extended time horizons, even modest variation in baseline comorbidity prevalence can materially influence the magnitude of estimated cost offsets. In the absence of country-specific epidemiologic data, baseline comorbidity prevalence for Vietnam and Denmark was informed by proxy estimates from China and Germany, respectively, without country-specific recalibration. As a result, even small differences between the proxy and true national prevalence patterns may compound over time and affect projected economic outcomes. The findings should therefore be interpreted as model-based projections generated under stated epidemiological assumptions rather than precise forecasts of country-specific comorbidity-related cost offsets.

Only four comorbidities were modelled, leaving others (e.g. hypertension, cancer and inflammatory bowel disease [IBD]) outside of scope. This analysis also did not include the costs of systemic treatments themselves, as these vary substantially across settings, may involve confidential pricing agreements and require jurisdiction-specific inputs to assess total net value. Incorporating treatment costs alongside the avoided comorbidity burden would therefore be an important next step for estimating the full economic value of systemic therapy. Finally, the model assumes 100% systemic treatment uptake with a single therapy type. It is an unrealistic but necessary simplification for early decision modelling. Future analyses incorporating actual treatment mixes, treatment costs or untreated proportions would refine these estimates for more real-world application.

Future Research

We believe that this work provides an important first perspective on the wider impact of early systemic treatment in reducing the burden of psoriatic disease beyond the skin and joint manifestations. This is particularly relevant when there is still a lack of understanding from many physicians about the increased risk for comorbidities for people living with psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis.

Future research using state-transition or microsimulation approaches with longitudinal real-world data would help better capture disease progression, adherence patterns, competing risks, preventive care pathways and temporal changes in treatment effectiveness. Future work should use real-world longitudinal data to better quantify treatment effects on comorbidity risks and improve model accuracy. Cost-effectiveness analyses incorporating Quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) and regional data are needed to evaluate long-term value. Expanding evidence in diverse populations will support localized decision-making [67].

Conclusions

Our model offers a pragmatic approach to explore the potential economic implications of comorbidity risk reduction associated with timely systemic treatment in psoriatic disease, in settings where longitudinal data remain limited. The findings suggest that timely systemic therapy may offer more than symptom control; it may be associated with reductions in psoriatic disease-related comorbidities and their related healthcare and societal costs. While the analysis relies on necessary epidemiological and structural assumptions, it indicates that systemic treatment may reduce the risk of comorbidities and translate into reduced comorbidity incidence in psoriatic disease, leading to meaningful comorbidity-related cost offsets for the health systems and society. The model development process also highlighted gaps in the available evidence. Future research is needed to strengthen the evidence on the magnitude, durability and causal nature of systemic treatment effects on comorbidity risks, and to support more refined economic evaluations incorporating real-world management patterns. While psoriatic disease remains lifelong condition, its complications may be modifiable, highlighting the importance of further investigation into the long-term burden of psoriatic disease and its comorbidities.
