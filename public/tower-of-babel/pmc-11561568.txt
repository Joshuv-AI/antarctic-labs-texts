Risks associated with evacuation and disaster-related death after a radiation disaster: summary of research results from Hamadori region, Fukushima
Authors: Tianchen Zhao, Masaharu Tsubokura
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiation disasters can lead to a myriad of direct and indirect health effects. The term ‘disaster-related deaths’ is commonly used in Japan; however, comprehensive studies in regions severely impacted by nuclear accidents are scarce. Our research primarily focuses on Minamisoma City, situated north of the Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant (FDNPP), and aimed to study the risks associated with evacuation and disaster-related death. We introduce the following studies: (1) risks from evacuation in elderly care facilities after the FDNPP accident; (2) detailed case reports on hospital evacuation and (3) detailed reports on disaster-related deaths. A critical lesson from the FDNPP accident is the significant risk to life posed by evacuation actions taken to avoid radiation exposure. Despite evacuation being an effective measure to reduce exposure, our preparation and knowledge are insufficient for safe execution. This paper ensures that the lessons from Fukushima are leveraged in future radiation protection measures.

Introduction

The adverse health effects of radiation/nuclear disasters can take various forms, including direct consequences due to radiation exposure as well as indirect repercussions caused by evacuations, disruptions to the health care system and changes in social structure.(1) The indirect adverse effects of radiation disasters may sometimes result in death. While the term ‘disaster-related deaths’ is used to refer to indirect deaths in Japan, studies on disaster-related deaths in areas heavily affected by nuclear power plant accidents are limited.(2)

Our research team has primarily focused on Minamisoma City, located north of the Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant (FDNPP), to reveal the actual conditions of medical facilities during the acute phase of the accident, as well as to comprehensively study the risks of evacuation and disaster-related death.(3) This paper summarises the results of these studies.

Detailed case reports on hospital evacuation

We conducted extensive interviews with healthcare professionals who experienced the evacuation during the nuclear power plant accident and compared the situation of hospital and facility evacuations during disasters in other regions (Kumamoto earthquake, Saga flooding and Miyagi Prefecture during the Great East Japan Earthquake). We compiled case reports summarising the difficulties faced by hospitals in the areas surrounding the nuclear power plant during evacuation at the time of the FDNPP accident, as well as reports summarising legal issues and agreements from a medical perspective.

Summary of difficulties during the evacuation of Futaba Hospital (within a 5 km radius)

Of the 338 patients at Futaba Hospital, 39 (11.5%) critically ill or bedridden patients died before evacuation completion.(11) Staff shortage and infrastructure disruption led to inadequate care, causing premature deaths during the emergency evacuation. Actual case reports of patients have also been reported.(12)

Summary of difficulties during the evacuation of Futaba Kosei Hospital (within a 5 km radius)

On the day of the FDNPP accident, 136 patients were in Futaba Kosei Hospital.(13) After receiving information from the local disaster task force, the director requested immediate evacuation, during which four patients, including those in end-stage conditions, died. Early intervention from an external organisation, such as the Japan Self-Defense Forces, may have prevented death during evacuation.

Comparison of the situation and difficulties during the evacuation of three hospitals within the PAZ (comparing the time, method and duration required for evacuation and considering the causes)

An analysis found four themes: inadequate disaster preparation, challenges in patient evacuation, lack of materials and information scarcity.(14)

Summary of difficulties during the evacuation of Odaka Akasaka Hospital (a psychiatric hospital, within a 20 km radius)

At the time of the FNDPP accident, 104 patients who were admitted to the hospital were instructed to evacuate.(15) Despite the challenging evacuation, the staff’s professionalism prevented significant health deterioration in patients.

Summary of difficulties during the evacuation of Minamisoma Municipal General Hospital (outside of the 20 km radius)

Data from medical and administrative records from 11 to 20 March 2011 showed that the hospital treated 622 outpatients and 241 inpatients, including those injured and with chronic diseases.(16) After the earthquake, the hospital faced a lack of communication, medical supplies and food for 4 d. On Day 4, hospital directors allowed employees to leave, and 71 of 239 full-time employees stayed to manage patient care and transfers. Details have also been compiled regarding the difficulties faced at the evacuation centers where people were subsequently relocated to.(17)

Reports regarding a hospital that chose not to evacuate despite being within the evacuation directive area

Even without evacuation, there could be an increase in mortality if sufficient resources are not provided.(18, 19, 20) There are also case reports regarding the life risks after the evacuation for individuals with disabilities and patients with mental illness.(21, 22)

Detailed reports on disaster-related deaths

Data regarding disaster-related deaths, while aggregated and publicly available, cannot be disclosed without the support of local administrations for detailed database access, and a thorough analysis is still largely underway. Our team, with the cooperation of Minamisoma City, is conducting research to analyse the detailed data and clarify the actual situation of disaster-related deaths. Issues related to the legal origins of disaster-related deaths and problems when adapting it to medical and health issues

The word, disaster-related deaths, was created for the condolence payment system and is not a legal or medical term.(23, 24) Therefore, there are many discrepancies when using its definition from a medical perspective during a disaster.

Differences in disaster-related deaths between radiation disasters and natural disasters

The most common reason for certification of death as disaster-related was ‘displacement owing to evacuation’ (25.8%) followed by ‘lack of appropriate treatment’ (20.4%).(2) Disaster-related deaths that occurred >6 months since the incident accounted for 37.8%. According to the Nagaoka criterion, which is one of the current guidelines for disaster-related deaths in Japan, a death must occur within 6 months to be recognised as disaster-related. However, delayed deaths occurring >6 months after the incident may still be disaster-related, particularly when involving nuclear/radiation disasters.

Association between disaster-related deaths and the pre-disaster long-term care certificated level

Of the 520 people whose deaths were certified as disaster-related by the Minamisoma City Committee for Certification of Disaster-Related Deaths, 467 (90.0%) responded to the long-term care (LTC) item on the survey form: 200 (38.5%) were not certified for LTC and 266 (51.3%) were certified for LTC.(25) People with high levels of LTC certification needs during the acute phase of the earthquake were more likely to die from circulatory and respiratory diseases. The mean time between the earthquake and the death since the earthquake was shorter for those with higher levels of LTC and significantly shorter for those at care level 3 or higher. Therefore, concentrating resources on people with high LTC certification during the acute phase of the disaster may lead to a reduction in earthquake-related deaths.

Conclusion

One of the major lessons learned after the FNDPP accident is that the action of evacuation, taken to avoid radiation exposure, posed a significant risk to life for many residents, although the above results do not necessarily support the conclusion that it would have been better not to evacuate following the Fukushima nuclear accident. Judgments vary depending on the scale of the accident and the status of resources.

While evacuation is one of the most effective measures to reduce exposure, our level of preparation and accumulation of knowledge remain insufficient to carry it out safely. Indirect risks mean it is difficult to infer causality, making it difficult to develop a quantitative argument; however, from the valuable experiences of the affected people, as demonstrated in this paper, the authors hope that the lessons from Fukushima introduced in this paper will be utilised in future radiation protection measures.
