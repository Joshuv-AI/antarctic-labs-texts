Integrated fuzzy MCDM for consumer-driven ceramic manufacturing sustainability within the UN SDGs framework
Authors: Shengyan Xue, Chia-Liang Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAchieving sustainable development in the ceramic industry necessitates aligning production practices with evolving consumer expectations and the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). This study evaluates sustainability practices from a customer perspective, aiming to foster more sustainable manufacturing within the China’s product manufacturing sector. We introduce a novel hybrid fuzzy multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) framework by integrating the Fuzzy Full Consistency Method (FUCOM-F) and Fuzzy Measurement of Alternatives and Ranking according to COmpromise Solution (FMARCOS). This approach, leveraging expert evaluations with linguistic fuzzy numbers, rigorously determines the importance of various sustainability criteria (aligned with SDGs) and ranks potential sustainable alternatives for the ceramic industry. Our findings demonstrate the framework’s robustness in assessing consumer priorities for sustainability, highlighting “Environment” and “Humanity” as the most critical dimensions from the consumer viewpoint. Furthermore, “The Branding of Cultural and Creative Industries” and “Sustainable Materials Use and Environmental Impact Reduction” emerged as the top-ranked solutions. This research offers significant managerial implications for ceramic product manufacturer, managers, and policymakers by providing a structured approach to prioritise sustainability initiatives that resonate with consumer values. Ultimately, it guides the industry towards sustainable pro- duction pathways, bridging the gap between awareness and facilitating a transition to more sustainable manufacturing models and public policies, with relevance for the ceramic product manufacturing sector and beyond.Subject terms: Business and management; Business and management; Engineering; Environmental social sciences; Environmental studies; Operational research; Science, technology and society

Introduction

With origins traceable to archaeological eras, the celebrated Chinese ceramic industry is characterized by its extensive history. The evolution of Chinese porcelain, as detailed by Crabbe1 for the Han Dynasty period, underscores the prominence of Jingdezhen. This city has been widely recognised as the ‘porcelain capital’, received imperial status during the Song Dynasty. A distinctive blend of art, innovation, and utility defines the porcelain from Jingdezhen; this fusion has markedly propelled the craftsmanship of Chinese ceramics to international esteem.

By the Yuan Dynasty, Chinese ceramics attained global prominence, largely due to the widespread production and export of its blue and white porcelain which shaped international markets. Subsequently, during the Ming and Qing eras, these ceramics became synonymous with exceptional skill and creative advancement, viewed globally as icons of aesthetic beauty and cultural value2. The twentieth century introduced modernisation via industrial manufacturing methods and engineering breakthroughs, allowing the sector to preserve its competitive edge in the global arena3. Shui4 underscored the ceramic sector’s role as a vital part of China’s economy, illustrating the nation’s capacity to integrate technological advancement with the safeguarding of cultural traditions, thereby reflecting both artistic skill and industrial strength.

Despite these significant historical and industrial achievements, the contemporary ceramic sector, like many manufacturing industries, faces the challenge of aligning its practices with growing global concerns for sustainability. While fostering customer loyalty remains a primary goal for enterprises amidst fierce international competition and evolving consumer preferences5–7, this loyalty is increasingly influenced by perceptions of a company’s environmental and social responsibility. Yu et al.8 highlighted how consumer shopping experiences and perceptual differences in Jingdezhen impact ceramic product manufacturing. Likewise, Mirfakhradini et al.5 explored consumer opinions’ role in new ceramic product development. Similarly, Lin9 and Guo et al.10 investigated and discussed the sustainable development factors and strategies for Jingdezhen’s ceramic industry by considering variations in consumer perspectives to underscore the need to integrate consumer insights into the industry’s sustainability transition.

In addition, enhancing sustainable customer satisfaction in China’s ceramic industry requires a focus on service quality factors11. This perspective is crucial, as service quality, in the context of promoting sustainable consumption and cleaner production, can extend to transparent information about product origins, the use of eco-friendly materials, ethical sourcing and end-of-life product management. Supporting this, Zhu et al.12 utilised an integrated fuzzy MCDM approach to assess service quality, emphasising customer-centric practices. Wan et al.13 also applied a hybrid MCDM methodology for service quality assessment of Yue Kiln Celadon products. These studies collectively advance the understanding of how service quality, when viewed through a sustainability lens, contributes to customer satisfaction and can drive more responsible manufacturing practices within the ceramic sector.

Furthermore, evaluating and prioritising sustainability initiatives in the ceramic sector, particularly when incorporating multifaceted consumer perspectives and aligning with the UN SDGs, presents a complex decision-making environment fraught with uncertainty and subjectivity. Fuzzy logic offers a powerful means to address such challenges, enabling decision-makers to effectively manage inherent vagueness in problem contexts and expert opinions14–16. For this study, a hybrid MCDM framework was developed, leveraging the strengths of two advanced fuzzy techniques: the Fuzzy Full Consistency Method (FUCOM-F) and the Fuzzy Measurement of Alternatives and Ranking according to COmpromise Solution (FMARCOS).

The FUCOM-F ensures more consistent criteria weighting by effectively capturing expert judgment under imprecise conditions, requiring fewer comparisons while maintaining accuracy17,18. This is crucial for determining the relative importance of various SDG-aligned sustainability criteria from the consumer viewpoint. Complementing this, the FMARCOS excels at handling uncertain information in ranking alternatives, providing a reliable ranking by evaluating options against both ideal and anti-ideal solutions, reducing bias19. Interestingly, The integrated method of FUCOM-F and FMARCOS was proved for offering a robust and suitable approach for resolving sustainable decision-making problems, specifically for assessing and ranking cleaner production strategies or sustainable material choices within the ceramic industry based on consumer-defined priorities20.

While existing literature has explored the impact of UN SDGs on customer satisfaction across various industries, and integrated MCDM models like FUCOM-F and FMARCOS have effectively addressed general sustainability problems, a specific research gap remains. There is a notable lack of studies focusing on how consumer perceptions of UN SDGs can directly inform and influence the adoption of specific sustainability practices and within China’s ceramic product manufacturing industry. This study aims to fill this void by employing the proposed hybrid FUCOM-F and FMARCOS approach. The primary goal is to assess the importance of various UN SDG-aligned sustainability dimensions from the perspective of consumers and to rank potential sustainable alternatives. The outcomes are intended to provide relevant decision-making suggestions for practitioners seeking to achieve enhanced ceramic product manufacturing sustainability in China’s ceramic sector. Ultimately, it guides the industry towards sustainable production pathways, bridging the gap between awareness and facilitating a transition to more sustainable manufacturing models and public policies, with relevance for the ceramic product manufacturing sector and beyond.

Literature review

This investigation utilises an integrated Multi-Criteria Decision-Making (MCDM) model, which combines the Fuzzy Full Consistency Method (FUCOM-F) and the Fuzzy Measurement of Alternatives and Ranking according to COmpromise Solution (FMARCOS). The purpose is to assess how the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (UN SDGs) influence sustainability practices within the ceramics product manufacturing industry, as viewed from the consumer perspective. Accordingly, the literature pertinent to this study will be reviewed and discussed below.

First, it is important to define the core concept of manufacturing sustainability. Although often used interchangeably with “sustainable production”, it refers to the creation of manufactured products through economically sound processes that minimise negative environmental impacts while conserving energy and natural resources. Crucially, manufacturing sustainability enhances the safety and well-being of employees, communities and consumers21. This holistic approach is based on three pillars: environmental stewardship (e.g. reduced emissions and waste valorisation), economic viability (e.g. process efficiency and long-term profitability) and social equity (e.g. fair labour practices and community engagement)22. Within the context of the ceramic industry, this translates to addressing challenges such as high energy consumption during firing, responsible sourcing of raw materials like clay and glazes, and preserving the cultural heritage and economic livelihood of artisan communities. This study adopts this multi-faceted understanding of sustainability as its foundational framework.

The relationship between customer satisfaction, sustainability principles and the UN SDGs

The notion of customer satisfaction was first applied to marketing by Caardozo23 who theorised that consumers’ repurchase behaviour was influenced by positive prior experiences. Howard et al.24 subsequently applied this to consumer theory, defining customer satisfaction as a psychological state that reflects whether the price paid is perceived as reasonable when compared to the actual price received. Oliver25 further characterised customer loyalty as an emotional response, affirming that consumer “expectations” and “purchase intentions” are shaped by pre-purchase attitudes, whereas post-purchase satisfaction is affected by “product performance” and its alignment with those initial expectations.

Johnson et al.26 contended that customer satisfaction can be understood as the user’s evaluation of the entire consumption experience resulting from a service process or product manufacturer. Spreng et al.27 proposed that it involves the customer’s rational and emotional perception following consumption. Anderson et al.28 further elaborated that complete customer satisfaction arises from a comprehensive evaluation of accumulated customer experiences, divisible into post-purchase assessment and the overall experiential evaluation based on satisfaction.

As awareness of environmental continues to grow, Dubrovski29 reported a re-definition of customer satisfaction in the pursuit of business excellence, with sustainability emerging as a central element. The concept of sustainable customer satisfaction requires a holistic view, encompassing not merely the immediate customer needs but also the broader environmental and social consequences of consumption. This involves considering aspects such as product durability, recyclability, ethical sourcing, and fair labour practices.

Grover et al.30 emphasised that research concerning consumer satisfaction is increasingly incorporating sustainability considerations, highlighting the growing significance of this idea in recent decades. Prud’homme et al.31 have indicated that sustainable customer satisfaction is based on the premise that genuine customer satisfaction must include the long-term well-being of both the customer and the planet. It relies on creating value for customers in a way that does not hinder the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. This is realised through the integration of sustainable practices throughout the value chain, from sourcing raw materials to product design and subsequent disposal.

Pingali et al.32 noted that the sustainability concept began to shape business practices. Companies started to evaluate the environmental and social impact of their operations, including the entire lifecycle of their products and services. This movement towards responsible business practices started to intersect with customer satisfaction, leading to the development of the sustainable customer satisfaction concept.

The United Nations (UN) established 17 global Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) in 201534, which are further detailed into 169 specific targets outlining each goal’s objectives In the context of the ceramic industry, this involves addressing issues such as the high energy consumption associated with firing, the responsible sourcing of raw materials such as clay and glazes, and the preservation of the cultural heritage and economic livelihoods of artisan communities. This study adopts this multifaceted understanding of sustainability as its foundational framework. Consequently, many scholars have begun to explore and discuss the connection between UN SDGs and customer behaviours. For example, Khalufi et al.35 pointed to the positive effect of retailers’ sustainable practices on customer relationships, suggesting that SDG-related concepts can aid in improving customer brand loyalty. Stauropoulou et al.36 revealed that banks’ commitment to UN SDGs is positively associated with increased customer trust, loyalty, and perceptions of fair pricing; integrating these goals has thus become an important strategic approach for banks to fortify their customer relationships. Lin et al.37 investigated the impact of UN SDGs on customer satisfaction, brand image, and customer loyalty within the green hotel industry. They discovered that SDG-centric strategies enhance a hotel’s environmental profile and market standing by aligning with evolving customer values, a crucial aspect in the hospitality sector.

FUCOM-F: a fuzzy approach to consistent criteria prioritisation

The Full Consistency Method (FUCOM), introduced by Pamučar et al.38, and its subsequent fuzzy extension, FUCOM-F39 which incorporates fuzzy triangular numbers to model linguistic uncertainty, represent significant advancements in MCDM for criteria weighting. Ilieva40 underscored FUCOM-F’s utility in managing the inherent uncertainties and vagueness often present in real-world decision-making evaluations by decision-makers. A key advantage, as reported by Badi et al.41, is FUCOM-F’s ability to achieve superior consistency ratios with fewer pairwise comparisons compared to established fuzzy MCDM techniques like the fuzzy analytic hierarchy process (FAHP) and the fuzzy best worst method (FBWM). This efficiency renders it particularly well-suited for sustainability assessments that frequently involve extensive sets of criteria and require input from diverse, interdisciplinary stakeholders, a common scenario when evaluating complex manufacturing alternatives or environmental performance metrics.

Several studies have demonstrated FUCOM-F’s applicability in the sustainability domain. For example, Tang et al.42 developed a novel framework utilizing FUCOM-F to assess the sustainability of sewage sludge-to-energy scenarios, praising its capacity for reliable weighting in ambiguous assessment environments – a context relevant to waste valorisation and resource recovery and key tenets of a circular economy. Ocampo18 further applied FUCOM-F to evaluate the sustainability of agro-tourism sites, an area interfacing agricultural practices with environmental conservation and economic viability.

In addition, the hybrid models integrating FUCOM-F with other MCDM techniques have also been effectively applied to diverse sustainability challenges. Demir et al.43 employed a FUCOM-F and the fuzzy combined compromise solution (F-CoCoSo) model for evaluating sustainable urban mobility plans. In a similar vein, Ayadi et al.44 integrated FUCOM-F with the fuzzy multi-attribute ideal-real comparative analysis (F-MAIRCA) and the fuzzy preference ranking organization method for enrichment evaluation (F-PROMETHEE) to identify sustainable logistics platforms, navigating the trade-offs between industrial development and ecological preservation, which is a central concern in the sustainability of logistics industry. Agrawal et al.45 proposed a FUCOM-F and fuzzy Bonferroni mean operator model to analyze barriers within the Indian healthcare circular supply chain, highlighting the role of regulatory efficiency and fiscal incentives in fostering circular economy principles in specific sectors.

Furthermore, Ecer et al.46 utilised FUCOM-F with the comprehensive normalisation technique (MACONT) to prioritise sustainable circular suppliers in the Turkish textile industry, focusing on criteria crucial for achieving circularity and sustainability in manufacturing supply networks.

FMARCOS: ranking alternatives under uncertainty using a fuzzy compromise solution

The Fuzzy Measurement of Alternatives and Ranking according to COmpromise Solution (FMARCOS), introduced by Stanković et al.47, builds upon the traditional MARCOS framework Stević et al.48 by integrating fuzzy logic principles. This enhancement significantly improves its capacity to manage uncertainty and vagueness inherent in complex decision-making processes. Shanmugasundar et al.49 highlighted that the FMARCOS proves particularly useful in contexts demanding qualitative assessments, making it highly appropriate for sustainability evaluations which often depend on subjective criteria and expert judgments.

In the view of this, FMARCOS has seen growing application in various sustainability contexts. For instance, Puška et al.50, Wang et al.51 and Olabanji52 applied the FMARCOS model to the critical task of selecting sustainable suppliers, a decision with direct implications for a firm’s overall environmental performance and the integrity of its green supply chain. Kovač et al.53 broadened its use to renewable energy assessments and drone-based urban logistics, both fields with significant sustainability dimension.

Additionally, the hybrid MCDM models incorporating FMARCOS have further demonstrated its versatility. For example, Wang et al.54 successfully used an Ordinal Priority Approach (OPA) and FMARCOS model to assess sustainable suppliers in Vietnam’s garment sector, considering factors like environmental impact. A similar approach was employed by Wang et al.55 o evaluate sustainable last-mile delivery solutions during the COVID-19 pandemic, addressing economic, social, and environmental dimensions. More recently, Lin56 presented a comprehensive analysis of sustainable design factors using a hybrid approach of the fuzzy analytical hierarchy process (FAHP) and FMARCOS model in the graphic design industry, showcasing the model’s robustness in addressing complex sustainability challenges strategically.

Summary

The foregoing literature review highlights a distinct research gap. While existing studies have explored sustainability in the ceramic industry, there is a notable lack of research that systematically evaluates and prioritises sustainability initiatives directly through the lens of consumer perception, particularly within the comprehensive framework of the UN SDGs. Furthermore, the inherent subjectivity and uncertainty in assessing consumer values require a sophisticated decision-making tool.

To address this gap, a methodology is needed that can both consistently weigh subjective criteria and robustly rank potential strategies under conditions of uncertainty. The review of MCDM methods indicates that while many tools exist, the combination of the FUCOM-F and the FMARCOS is particularly well-suited for this challenge. FUCOM-F offers a highly consistent method for determining criteria weights with fewer pairwise comparisons, while FMARCOS excels at ranking alternatives in a fuzzy environment. Therefore, this study proposes an integrated FUCOM-F and FMARCOS approach to provide a novel and robust framework for enhancing ceramic product manufacturing sustainability based on consumer priorities.

The establishment of the evaluation structure

To construct the evaluation framework, we began by conducting an in-depth literature review focusing on the 17 UN SDGs. The main challenge was to organise these goals into a set of core dimensions that would be comprehensive and meaningful to consumers within the context of the ceramic industry.

To validate and refine this structure, a questionnaire was distributed to a broad pool of industry professionals, resulting in 129 valid responses. Notably, feedback from this initial stage revealed that standard, high-level sustainability frameworks were inadequate for capturing the nuances of the ceramic sector. Many experts suggested refining the classification of sub-criteria and explicitly including distinct economic and human-related dimensions when analysing the impact of the SDGs on the sustainability of manufacturing ceramic products.

In light of this expert feedback, a final, tailored evaluation structure was developed. This structure groups all 17 UN SDGs innovatively into five custom dimensions that more accurately reflect consumers’ holistic perspectives on sustainability in this industry: Environment (D1), Society (D2), Management (D3), Economy (D4) and Humanity (D5). For instance, although SDG 5 (Gender Equality) and SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities) are both social goals, they were categorised under the specific dimension of “Humanity” based on expert feedback indicating that consumers are more personally, ethically and humanistically engaged with these issues. This tailored approach ensures that the subsequent evaluation is finely tuned to the specific priorities of the research context, rather than being based on a generic template.

Numerical analysis

The determination of criteria weight using FUCOM-F

In this study, ten senior experts in ceramic product manufacturing related industries were invited to serve as decision makers (DMs). The panel size of ten is considered appropriate for MCDM studies, as it is large enough to provide diverse and reliable judgements while being small enough to ensure the detailed engagement required for complex comparisons106. The experts were purposely selected based on two key criteria: a minimum of 15 years’ professional experience and their position in the industry. The final panel comprised a balanced cross-section of professionals, including three senior executives from leading Jingdezhen ceramic enterprises, two product designers who had won international awards, three marketing directors who specialised in cultural consumer goods and two academic researchers who focused on ceramic product manufacturing and management. This diverse composition was crucial for ensuring a holistic viewpoint.

A critical aspect of this study’s methodology is the rationale behind using industry experts to represent consumers’ viewpoints. Rather than being surveyed as end consumers, the panel was surveyed as “expert proxies” for consumer perception. With their extensive experience in product development, marketing and sales, these professionals have a deep understanding of consumer trends, purchase drivers and evolving sustainability expectations. This is insight that a survey of individual consumers might not capture as cohesively or with as much forward-looking perspective. They excel at translating abstract consumer desires into tangible product and manufacturing strategies. Therefore, their perspective is essential for the decision-making model employed here, providing a strategic synthesis of consumer expectations.

Implications for research

Beyond its practical application, this work offers several contributions to the academic literature on sustainability and multi-criteria decision-making.

First, our findings challenge conventional, often Western-centric, sustainability frameworks that primarily focus on ecological and economic dimensions. The high weighting of the “Humanity” dimension (D5) – second only to “Environment” (D1) – and the prominence of sub-criteria such as “Gender Equality” (S16) and “No Poverty” (S13) suggest that, in certain cultural and industrial contexts, socio-humanistic aspects of sustainability are central to consumer perception, rather than being secondary considerations. This indicates a need for sustainability models to be more adaptable and culturally attuned, potentially incorporating a fourth pillar of “cultural and ethical purpose” alongside the traditional triple bottom line.

Second, this study highlights the usefulness of integrated MCDM models in revealing subtle consumer priorities that could be hidden by traditional survey methods. The FUCOM-F model’s ability to establish a robust weight hierarchy, combined with the FMARCOS model’s capacity to rank alternatives under uncertainty, was crucial in revealing the preference for intangible values (e.g. cultural branding) over more conventional business strategies (e.g. market expansion). This affirms the value of such hybrid approaches for researchers tackling complex problems at the intersection of consumer behaviour and sustainable development.

Finally, this research opens up several avenues for future scholarly enquiry. One logical next step would be to conduct a comparative study of Chinese and Western consumer perspectives, investigating whether the high premium placed on cultural branding and social equity is unique to the Chinese market or indicative of a broader global trend in artisanal sectors. Furthermore, qualitative research employing case studies of ceramic enterprises could explore the mechanisms through which concepts such as “gender equality” are implemented in supply chains and communicated to consumers effectively, providing a rich empirical complement to our quantitative findings.

Implications for management

The outcomes of this research provide a clear, data-driven strategic roadmap for managers, practitioners, and policymakers within China’s ceramic manufacturing sector.

The most significant managerial insight is the ascendancy of “The Branding of Cultural and Creative Industries” (A5) as the premier strategy. This finding resonates powerfully with the prevailing “Guochao” (National Trend) movement in China, where consumers are actively seeking products that embody cultural authenticity and national heritage113–115. This implies that for the modern ceramic industry, sustainability is perceived not merely through an ecological lens, but through a cultural one. The high ranking suggests consumers are drawn to brands that tell a compelling story, where sustainability is a core chapter of that narrative. Manufacturers are thus advised that investing in storytelling, connecting products to historical narratives, and celebrating artisanal craftsmanship are not peripheral marketing activities, but are central to a sustainability strategy that aligns with contemporary consumer values. This directly validates our model’s finding that the “Humanity” dimension (D5) is a critical driver of consumer perception, second only to the environment itself.

Additionally, the results advocate a dual-focus strategy. The high ranking of both cultural branding (A5) and “Sustainable Materials Use and Environmental Impact Reduction” (A1) suggests that the most effective approach is to combine an engaging brand narrative with concrete evidence of environmental responsibility. Consumers expect both a clean conscience and a cultural connection. This requires a holistic approach in which marketing departments and production teams work together. Managers should therefore recognise that investments in low-impact glazes or transparent supply chains are essential components of the brand’s narrative and value proposition, not mere operational costs.

Limitations and future research

While this study provides valuable insights through a robust methodological framework, it is important to acknowledge its limitations, which in turn suggest avenues for future research.

First, as this study’s primary contribution is its deep contextual analysis, its findings are closely tied to the Chinese market. The high importance placed on cultural branding and specific social SDGs may be influenced by unique cultural factors and current market trends (e.g., “Guochao” movement in China). Therefore, the direct generalisability of the results to other cultural or economic contexts, such as the European ceramic market, is limited. Future research should involve a cross-cultural comparative study to test the model and its outcomes in different regions.

Second, the methodology relies on the judgments of a panel of experts. While the “expert proxy” approach is a valid and insightful method for strategic analysis, the outputs are inherently subjective and contingent on the expertise of the selected panel. Additionally, while a limited sample size of ten experts is conventional for ensuring high-quality engagement in in-depth MCDM studies, the results represent a snapshot of high-level expert opinion rather than a statistically generalisable view of the entire industry.

Third, a key methodological consideration is the composition of the expert panel. The evaluation of broad societal and environmental SDGs was conducted by a panel of industry experts. While their role was to act as proxies for consumer perception, it is acknowledged that their perspectives may be inherently shaped by their industry background. A significant enhancement for future research would be the inclusion of a multi-stakeholder panel, comprising not only industry experts but also representatives from environmental NGOs (“nature representatives”) and community organisations (“society representatives”), to provide a more holistic evaluation.

Finally, the MCDM methods used, while robust, have their own characteristics. Although the combination of FUCOM-F and FMARCOS offers high consistency and robust rankings, it does not capture the dynamic interplay or potential trade-offs between criteria over time. Future research could use system dynamics modelling to examine how investing in one area of sustainability might affect others in the long term.

Conclusion

This study introduces a new integrated FUCOM-F and FMARCOS approach to analyse and select sustainability strategies for China’s ceramic manufacturing industry from the crucial standpoint of the consumer. Meanwhile, we conclude that achieving meaningful sustainability in this sector requires a dual approach: tangible environmental and social responsibility must be combined with the powerful vehicle of cultural and creative branding.

Also, our findings reveal a clear hierarchy of consumer priorities, with the “Environment” and “Humanity” dimensions being of paramount importance. Subsequent analysis of the ranking of solutions confirms that consumers are most receptive to strategies that combine a product’s cultural heritage with demonstrable ethical and ecological integrity. This provides a vital strategic insight for an industry navigating the complexities of tradition and modernity: to secure its future, the industry must sell more than just porcelain; it must also sell principles. The combined model enhances decision-making efficiency and provides a holistic tool for strategic evaluation, its credibility being strengthened through rigorous empirical validation.

Whilst this research provides a robust framework, its findings are primarily situated within the context of the Chinese market. Further research would therefore be beneficial in order to test its applicability in other regions.

Ultimately, this study offers a cohesive framework for the systematic identification and prioritisation of sustainability initiatives. It demonstrates that by aligning production practices with the deeper cultural and ethical values of consumers, the Chinese ceramic industry can not only enhance its sustainability but also fortify its unique brand identity on the global stage.
