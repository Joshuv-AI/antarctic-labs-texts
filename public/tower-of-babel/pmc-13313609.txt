Electronic nose technology in bronchial asthma diagnosis and management
Authors: Lushuo Zhang, Mengnan Guo, Hui Wang, Anna Dong, Ning Song, Binghan Zhong, Xiaying Li, Chenqi Liu, Yunxia Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electronic nose (e-nose) technology, as an emerging non-invasive diagnostic tool, has demonstrated considerable potential in the diagnosis and management of bronchial asthma. By mimicking the human olfactory system, this technology utilizes gas sensor arrays combined with pattern recognition algorithms to enable rapid analysis and identification of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in the exhaled breath of patients with asthma. Accumulating evidence suggests that e-nose systems can discriminate between asthma patients and healthy individuals with relatively high accuracy, and they have also shown promising performance in asthma phenotyping. Compared with conventional pulmonary function tests, e-nose technology offers distinct advantages, including cost-effectiveness, rapid response, and non-invasiveness. These features make it particularly suitable for populations requiring repeated dynamic assessment or those unable to perform high-quality pulmonary function testing. However, current evidence is primarily derived from exploratory studies, and heterogeneity exists across studies in terms of population characteristics and methodologies. This highlights the need for further standardized research to improve the consistency and reproducibility of findings.

Bronchial asthma is a heterogeneous disease typically characterized by chronic airway inflammation, with diverse clinical phenotypes and endotypes. It is clinically manifested by recurrent episodes of wheezing, dyspnea, chest tightness, and cough, while atypical cases may present predominantly with cough or chest tightness. The disease is also associated with airway hyperresponsiveness and variable airflow limitation, and prolonged disease duration may lead to airway remodeling [1, 2]. The global burden of asthma remains substantial. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, the age-standardized prevalence of asthma worldwide was 3340.1 per 100,000 population in 2021, corresponding to approximately 260 million affected individuals. In the same year, the age-standardized mortality rate was 5.2 per 100,000, with an estimated 436,000 asthma-related deaths [1, 2]. Since 2010, the prevalence of asthma among adolescents and adults in China has shown an increasing trend, placing a considerable burden on the public health system [1].

Currently, the clinical diagnosis of bronchial asthma primarily relies on medical history assessment and pulmonary function testing, including bronchodilator reversibility testing, peak expiratory flow (PEF) variability, and bronchial provocation tests. However, these conventional approaches have notable limitations. Misdiagnosis and underdiagnosis are more likely to occur in populations with poor cooperation (e.g., young children and elderly individuals) as well as in patients with contraindications to testing. Moreover, conventional pulmonary function tests mainly reflect the severity of airflow limitation but are insufficient to assess the type and extent of airway inflammation. Accurate identification of inflammatory phenotypes is crucial for guiding individualized therapy.

In this context, there is growing interest in developing novel technologies capable of non-invasively and rapidly characterizing asthma-related inflammatory phenotypes. E-nose has thus emerged as an intelligent sensing system that mimics the biological olfactory system. By analyzing breathprints, e-nose systems can detect disease-specific patterns of VOCs, thereby providing supportive information for disease diagnosis and phenotyping. This review summarizes the current applications and future perspectives of e-nose technology in the diagnosis and management of bronchial asthma. From a clinical perspective, current asthma management continues to face several key challenges, including the lack of non-invasive approaches for assessing inflammatory phenotypes, difficulties in precise phenotyping due to disease heterogeneity, limited ability to predict treatment response, and the absence of effective tools for dynamic monitoring and early exacerbation prediction. By enabling pattern recognition of exhaled VOCs, e-nose technology has the potential to provide novel solutions to these challenges. However, its incremental value over existing biomarkers remains to be fully established.

Overview of electronic nose technology

Definition of electronic nose technology

Human exhaled breath contains thousands of biomarkers, typically present at micromolar or nanomolar concentrations, many of which are of significant interest to respiratory clinicians and researchers [3, 4]. These molecules originate from two primary sources. Some are inhaled from the external environment and subsequently exhaled, while environmental compounds may also be absorbed by the body, where they can interact with endogenous VOCs or modulate their production.

The second group consists of VOCs released endogenously by the human body, including those derived from respiratory tract cells, reflecting underlying metabolic processes. The release of certain endogenous VOCs is influenced by inflammation and oxidative stress [5]. Collectively, these VOCs represent byproducts of metabolism, cellular activity, inflammatory responses, and microbial processes. Therefore, exhaled breath can be regarded as a “window” reflecting the physiological and pathological status of the human body.

Currently, the precise detection and quantification of these compounds primarily rely on gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). However, this technique is limited by long analysis times, high costs, and operational complexity, which restrict its widespread clinical application [6].

In contrast, e-nose technology has emerged as a rapid, user-friendly, and cost-effective alternative. The e-nose is a biomimetic device that simulates the biological olfactory system and typically consists of a gas sampling system, a sensor array, and a data processing unit (Figure 1). Unlike GC–MS, the e-nose does not perform qualitative or quantitative analysis of individual chemical compounds. Instead, it recognizes composite response patterns generated by complex gas mixtures— referred to as “breathprints”—to differentiate between samples [7–9].

Accordingly, while conventional e-nose systems are generally not intended for the identification of individual molecules [10], they enable sample classification and discrimination based on overall VOC profiles [11]. It should be noted that advanced configurations, such as MS-based e-nose systems, may allow the detection of specific VOCs. Medical e-nose devices are most commonly used for analyzing VOCs in exhaled breath, but they have also been applied to other biological samples, including feces, biopsy specimens, saliva, and skin [8, 9].

Principles of electronic nose technology

The fundamental principle of e-nose technology is based on the interaction between VOCs and sensor arrays. An e-nose system typically consists of multiple cross-reactive sensors, each exhibiting varying degrees of sensitivity to different chemical compounds, thereby generating a characteristic response pattern.

Sensor technology forms the core of e-nose systems. Common sensor types include metal oxide semiconductors, quartz crystal microbalances, surface acoustic wave sensors, conductive polymers, nanosensors, and photoionization detectors [12]. When VOCs interact with the sensor surface through physical or chemical mechanisms, measurable changes occur in the electrical or physicochemical properties of the sensors, such as resistance, frequency, or mass, which are subsequently converted into electrical signals.

These signals are then processed through signal conditioning and data analysis modules. Pattern recognition techniques, including principal component analysis (PCA), linear discriminant analysis (LDA), and artificial neural networks (ANNs), are applied to enable classification and identification of gas samples [7–9]. Through this process, complex chemical information is transformed into recognizable digital patterns, allowing the e-nose system to mimic the human olfactory recognition process.

Following data acquisition, preprocessing steps— including baseline correction, normalization, and feature extraction—are performed. Classification models based on PCA combined with machine learning algorithms, such as support vector machines (SVM) and random forest (RF), are then applied for pattern recognition. Model performance is typically evaluated using cross-validation and independent test datasets.

Based on different sensing technologies, a variety of e-nose devices have been developed, including Cyranose 320, BIONOTE, PEN3, Aeonose, and Spiro Nose. Among these, Cyranose 320 is one of the most widely used systems and has been extensively applied in studies of respiratory diseases, such as lung cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and asthma, owing to its portability and rapid response capabilities [13].

However, differences exist among sensor systems in terms of sensitivity and selectivity toward various VOCs, signal stability, and susceptibility to environmental factors such as temperature and humidity. These variations should be carefully considered when comparing data obtained from different e-nose platforms.

Application of electronic nose technology in bronchial asthma

Diagnosis and differential diagnosis of bronchial asthma

In most studies, e-nose technology has demonstrated favorable diagnostic performance in distinguishing patients with bronchial asthma from healthy controls, as well as from other chronic respiratory diseases presenting with respiratory symptoms, such as COPD, obstructive sleep apnea, and interstitial lung disease [14–16]. To facilitate comparison across studies, Table 1 summarizes key characteristics, including study populations, control settings, e-nose devices, analytical methods, and diagnostic performance metrics (e.g., sensitivity, specificity, and validation approaches).

As a non-invasive breath analysis tool, e-nose technology shows potential value in both the identification and stratification of bronchial asthma. In a prospective study, Rutjes et al. [17] investigated the ability of an e-nose to predict asthma development in preschool children with wheezing. By analyzing parameters such as differences in exhaled breath resistance ratios, maximum resistance slope, and baseline resistance, combined with linear discriminant analysis and cross-validation, the study demonstrated that, in the subgroup with rhinovirus infection, e-nose-derived principal component analysis could identify children who later developed asthma, with a sensitivity of 78.6% and a specificity of 54.5%. When restricted to comparisons between confirmed asthma and non-asthma cases, the area under the curve (AUC) increased to 0.779. These findings suggest that integrating e-nose analysis with viral detection may improve early identification of high-risk pediatric populations. In a cross-sectional study, Farraia et al. [18] analyzed exhaled breath samples from 199 patients with respiratory symptoms using the Cyranose 320 e-nose and demonstrated that this approach could rapidly and non-invasively identify uncontrolled asthma in real-world clinical settings, thereby providing supportive information for individualized treatment strategies. Similarly, Tenero et al. [19] reported that e-nose technology could effectively discriminate between controlled and uncontrolled asthma in children, with a sensitivity of 79%, a specificity of 84%, and a cross-validated accuracy of 0.80. These findings indicate that e-nose systems may not only assist in the initial diagnosis of asthma but also reflect disease control status.

E-nose technology, based on differences in exhaled VOC profiles, also provides a novel approach for the differential diagnosis of bronchial asthma and other respiratory diseases. Differentiating asthma from COPD remains a common clinical challenge. Although both conditions are characterized by airflow limitation, asthma is typically associated with eosinophilic inflammation, whereas COPD is predominantly driven by neutrophilic inflammation. Fens et al. [20] reported that analysis of VOC profiles using an e-nose could distinguish asthma from COPD with relatively high accuracy, suggesting underlying differences in metabolic signatures between the two diseases. In addition, several studies have shown that e-nose technology can differentiate asthma from other conditions, including lung cancer and interstitial lung disease [14–16]. However, it should be noted that the ability of e-nose systems to distinguish between diseases does not imply that they can serve as standalone diagnostic tools for asthma. A more appropriate clinical role may be as a complementary modality alongside pulmonary function testing, fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO), and other inflammatory biomarkers, particularly in scenarios requiring rapid, non-invasive, and repeatable assessment.

Phenotyping of bronchial asthma

According to the Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Bronchial Asthma (2024 Edition) [1], asthma is a heterogeneous disease with multiple classification frameworks. Clinically, it can be categorized into typical asthma; atypical asthma predominantly presenting with cough or chest tightness (e.g., cough-variant asthma and chest tightness-variant asthma); and specific subtypes such as aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, exercise-induced asthma, and occupational asthma.

From a pathophysiological perspective, asthma is broadly classified into type 2 (T2) inflammation—characterized by eosinophilia and elevated FeNO, commonly observed in allergic and eosinophilic asthma—and non-type 2 (non-T2) inflammation. In terms of disease management, asthma can be stratified into mild, moderate, and severe forms based on the level of treatment required to achieve disease control. Additionally, according to disease status, asthma may be divided into acute exacerbation, chronic persistent, and controlled phases.

Given the heterogeneity in classification approaches and phenotype definitions across studies, Table 2 summarizes the relevant literature to facilitate comparison of e-nose performance under different phenotyping frameworks.

E-nose technology, by capturing differences in exhaled VOC profiles, has shown potential in identifying inflammatory phenotypes, allergic phenotypes, and disease control status. In a multicenter study, de Vries et al. [21] applied an integrated e-nose platform to perform unsupervised clustering analysis of exhaled breath from 278 patients with asthma and 157 patients with COPD, identifying five distinct clusters independent of conventional diagnostic labels: (1) obese, non-eosinophilic, poorly controlled phenotype; (2) eosinophilic inflammation–dominant phenotype; (3) non-Caucasian, impaired lung function with mild symptoms; (4) neutrophilic inflammation with frequent exacerbations; and (5) benign, well-controlled phenotype.

These clusters were driven by clinical inflammatory characteristics, including systemic eosinophil and neutrophil counts, body mass index (BMI), and FeNO levels. Furthermore, regression analyses demonstrated that e-nose signals could predict peripheral blood inflammatory cell levels, supporting its capability for inflammatory phenotyping.

In addition, Abdel-Aziz et al. [22] integrated data from four independent cohorts comprising 655 patients with asthma and applied multiple machine learning algorithms. Their results demonstrated that e-nose technology could distinguish atopic from non-atopic asthma with high accuracy, outperforming FeNO, and that the e-nose signal was independent of other clinical confounding factors, highlighting its robustness in allergic phenotype identification.

In real-world clinical settings, Farraia et al. [18] utilized the Cyranose 320 e-nose in outpatient clinics and successfully identified patient clusters with different levels of symptom control using unsupervised clustering methods, further supporting its utility in detecting uncontrolled asthma.

For severe asthma, Brinkman et al. [23] employed a composite e-nose platform for breath metabolomic analysis in 78 patients with asthma. Their study identified three phenotypes associated with systemic inflammation and oral corticosteroid use—eosinophil-dominant, neutrophil-dominant, and moderate inflammation phenotypes. Moreover, longitudinal analysis revealed that phenotype transitions were significantly associated with changes in sputum eosinophil counts (p = 0.045), underscoring the potential of e-nose technology for monitoring dynamic disease evolution.

Overall, these findings suggest that e-nose systems, functioning as composite biomarkers, have the capacity to characterize inflammatory and allergic phenotypes and may provide non-invasive support for precision medicine in asthma management.

Van der Schee et al. [24] investigated 25 patients with mild-to-moderate asthma and 20 healthy controls, demonstrating that e-nose technology could predict corticosteroid responsiveness with a sensitivity of 90.9% and a specificity of 71.4%. Notably, its predictive performance exceeded that of conventional biomarkers, such as sputum eosinophil counts and FeNO. These findings suggest that e-nose systems are capable of capturing complex metabolic signatures associated with steroid responsiveness, thereby enabling the identification of patients who are more likely to benefit from corticosteroid therapy prior to treatment initiation.

Although direct applications of e-nose technology in therapeutic drug monitoring are still emerging, metabolomics-based evidence provides important complementary insights. Brinkman et al. [25], using GC–MS analysis of exhaled VOCs in patients with severe asthma, demonstrated strong concordance between breath VOC profiles and urinary detection of salbutamol and oral corticosteroids, suggesting the potential feasibility of non-invasive drug monitoring via breath analysis.

In another study, real-time mass spectrometry analysis revealed that inhalation of salbutamol (200 μg) induced alterations in lipid metabolism, particularly increased fatty acid levels, reflecting its physiological effects [26]. Furthermore, in pediatric asthma, Zeng et al. [27] utilized real-time breath metabolomics to identify metabolic phenotypes associated with bronchodilator responsiveness. Cluster analysis classified patients into three distinct subtypes, which were significantly correlated with clinical bronchodilator response, further supporting the role of breath-based metabolic profiling in treatment stratification.

Beyond treatment response prediction, e-nose technology also demonstrates considerable potential in dynamic disease monitoring, assessment of asthma control, and early identification of exacerbation risk. Brinkman et al. [28] employed a combined e-nose and GC–MS platform in 23 patients with asthma and found that e-nose signals could sensitively detect changes in disease control status, achieving an accuracy of 95% in distinguishing baseline from uncontrolled states and 86% in distinguishing uncontrolled from recovery phases. These findings indicate that e-nose systems can effectively reflect temporal fluctuations in disease activity.

Consistent with these findings, Farraia et al. [18] demonstrated in a real-world outpatient setting that e-nose technology could differentiate patient clusters with varying symptom burden and control levels through unsupervised clustering, highlighting its value in patient stratification and objective disease assessment. In addition, emerging evidence suggests that regular monitoring of breathprints, particularly in pediatric populations, may enable early detection of impending exacerbations prior to the onset of clinical symptoms, underscoring its potential as a predictive and preventive tool.

Overall, e-nose technology, as a non-invasive and rapid analytical approach, shows promise not only in predicting treatment response but also in monitoring disease progression and identifying exacerbation risk, thereby offering a novel tool for long-term asthma management and precision medicine.

Factors influencing electronic nose detection

Subject-Related factors

Exhaled VOC profiles are highly susceptible to individual-related factors, including lifestyle and baseline characteristics. Among these, smoking is one of the most extensively studied variables. Cigarette smoke is a complex mixture of over 5,000 compounds, hundreds of which are toxic and/or carcinogenic [29]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that smoking status, intensity, duration, and inhalation depth can significantly influence the composition of exhaled VOCs [30–35].

On the one hand, smoking introduces a substantial amount of exogenous VOCs, generating a strong background signal. On the other hand, it induces oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and airway remodeling, thereby altering endogenous VOC profiles and potentially masking asthma-specific signatures [36, 37]. However, some studies have reported that e-nose systems are unable to reliably distinguish smoking status in healthy individuals, suggesting that short-term smoking exposure may not substantially alter global breath patterns, whereas long-term smoking-related airway changes may exert a more pronounced effect [38].

Therefore, it is recommended that smoking conditions be standardized prior to breath sampling, and detailed smoking histories should be recorded.

In addition to smoking, diet, fasting status, and recent physical activity can also influence metabolic processes and, consequently, exhaled VOC composition over short time scales. Studies have shown that exercise can induce measurable changes in VOC profiles in healthy individuals, whereas these changes tend to be more pronounced in patients with asthma [39–41]. This may be attributable to exercise-induced broncho-constriction, inflammation, or metabolic alterations.

Similarly, dietary intake and fasting status are recognized sources of variability in VOC analysis, as they can modulate systemic metabolism and gastrointestinal-derived volatile compounds [42–44]. Therefore, standardized pre-test conditions—such as fasting and avoidance of strenuous exercise—are recommended.

Age and BMI may also influence VOC profiles through their effects on metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammatory status, and should therefore be considered in study design and data interpretation [45, 46].

Effects of medication and comorbidities

Medication use represents an important confounding factor in e-nose-based analysis. In particular, bronchodilators and their washout periods require careful consideration. Bronchodilators may alter airway tone, ventilation patterns, and the local inflammatory microenvironment, thereby affecting exhaled VOC composition. Similarly, inhaled corticosteroids and other anti-inflammatory agents may modify VOC profiles through their regulatory effects on airway inflammation [24, 47, 48].

Accordingly, medication use should be clearly documented and, where possible, standardized across study participants, especially regarding the discontinuation timing of short-acting and long-acting bronchodilators prior to testing.

Comorbidities also represent a significant source of variability in VOC analysis. As exhaled VOCs reflect systemic metabolic processes, both respiratory and non-respiratory diseases may introduce additional signals that overlap with asthma-specific profiles, thereby reducing diagnostic specificity.

Non-respiratory conditions, such as diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, liver disease, and coronary artery disease, have all been associated with distinct VOC alterations [49–53]. Similarly, respiratory comorbidities—including gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), COPD, respiratory infections, and lung cancer—can also influence exhaled breath patterns [54–56]. Notably, e-nose technology itself has demonstrated the capability to identify some of these disease-specific signatures, further highlighting the complexity of interpretation.

Therefore, careful consideration and, where feasible, stratification of comorbid conditions are essential in e-nose studies.

Sampling procedures and environmental factors

In addition to subject-related variables, sampling methodology and environmental conditions are critical determinants of e-nose performance and reproducibility. Factors such as breath collection protocols (e.g., standardized expiratory flow rates), exclusion of dead space air, sampling containers, number of repeated measurements, and time intervals between sampling can all influence VOC composition and sensor responses [57, 58].

Environmental factors, including ambient temperature, humidity, and background air pollution, may also affect VOC concentrations and sensor performance, thereby introducing variability into measurement results [8, 9, 59].

Given the sensitivity of e-nose systems to external conditions, it is essential to adopt standardized sampling protocols and controlled environmental conditions. In addition, appropriate background air correction should be implemented to enhance reproducibility and comparability across studies.

Technical challenges and future directions

Overall, e-nose technology, as an emerging noninvasive analytical tool, has demonstrated considerable potential in the diagnosis, phenotyping, and treatment response assessment of bronchial asthma [60]. By capturing disease-related metabolic alterations as composite “breathprint” signatures of exhaled VOCs, e-nose systems provide a novel approach for non-invasive characterization of asthma. Compared with conventional biomarkers, such as FeNO and eosinophil counts, which primarily reflect single inflammatory pathways, e-nose technology integrates multidimensional metabolic and inflammatory information. This integrative capability may better capture the complexity and heterogeneity of asthma, particularly in the context of overlapping phenotypes and dynamic disease states. In addition, e-nose systems offer several practical advantages, including rapid analysis, ease of operation, cost-effectiveness, and suitability for repeated dynamic monitoring, thereby partially addressing the limitations of conventional pulmonary function testing in specific populations. However, several limitations remain. The reliance on pattern recognition algorithms limits direct biological interpretability, and the lack of standardized protocols across devices and analytical workflows constrains reproducibility and cross-study comparability. These challenges, together with the influence of multiple confounding factors on VOC profiles, currently hinder the clinical translation of e-nose technology. Therefore, the clinical positioning and real-world utility of e-nose systems have yet to be fully established and require further validation [61].

At present, several key challenges need to be addressed. First, from a methodological perspective, most existing studies are exploratory and involve relatively small sample sizes. Substantial heterogeneity exists in study populations, control settings, and analytical workflows, and many studies lack independent external validation, which may lead to overestimation of model performance and limit generalizability. Second, regarding reporting standards, current studies often provide insufficient evaluation of model performance, with limited reporting of confidence intervals, calibration analyses, and prospective validation. This compromises the robustness and interpretability of the evidence. Third, from biological and technical perspectives, exhaled VOC profiles are influenced by multiple confounding factors, including inflammatory phenotypes, disease stages, medications, diet, and environmental exposures. In addition, variability across e-nose platforms—such as differences in sensor materials, signal processing algorithms, and device calibration—further hinders reproducibility and cross-platform consistency.

Looking forward, the successful clinical implementation of e-nose technology will depend on methodological standardization and the accumulation of high-quality evidence. Future research should focus on identifying clinically relevant application scenarios, establishing standardized protocols for breath sampling, data analysis, and reporting, and conducting large-scale, multicenter prospective studies to enable robust external validation and comparison with established biomarkers.

Furthermore, integration with artificial intelligence and multi-omics approaches is expected to enhance the interpretation of high-dimensional signal data and improve biological interpretability. Advances in device miniaturization and portability may facilitate long-term monitoring and early detection of disease exacerbations in real-world settings. Ultimately, only through rigorous validation of reproducibility, interpretability, and clinical utility can e-nose technology be effectively incorporated into precision medicine frameworks for bronchial asthma management.
