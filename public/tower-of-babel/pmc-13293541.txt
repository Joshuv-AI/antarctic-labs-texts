The effect of using silver diamine fluoride at two different times on the microshear bond strength of adhesives to sound and caries-affected dentin
Authors: Kimiya Sadeghpour, Mohammadreza Malekipour, Farzaneh Shirani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:The aim of this laboratory study was to investigate the immediate and delayed effects of silver diamine fluoride (SDF) on the microshear bond strength (μSBS) of two types of sixth-generation self-etch and universal etch-and-rinse (ER) adhesives to sound dentin (SD) and Caries-Affected Dentin (CD).Materials and Methods:In this experimental study, 120 human third molars were selected, half of which had occlusal caries. First, the occlusal enamel was removed, and SD and CD surrounded by SD were exposed. The samples were divided into three groups. In the first group, SDF was not used; in the second group, SDF was applied, and in the third group, after applying SDF, the samples were incubated in artificial saliva for 1 month. The sixth-generation self-etch adhesive was used in half of the samples, and the universal ER adhesive was used in the other half. The μSBS of the samples was measured, and the strength and type of failure were investigated. The data were analyzed using three-way analysis of variance (α = 0.05).Results:The highest mean shear bond strength was observed in the SD without SDF and Clearfil Liner Bond (LB), and the lowest mean shear bond strength was observed in the CD, LB, and Aged SDF. The bond strength was lower in the CD than in the SD (P < 0.001). In CD, SDF caused a slight increase in the immediate μSBS, which was not significant in any of the bonding systems. Bond strength in the SD with Aged SDF was significantly higher in All Bond (AB) Universal than in LB (P = 0.03).Conclusion:Applying SDF to SD, regardless of the type of bond, decreases the bond strength, while it increases the bond strength of CD. If SDF is used in SD, it is better to use AB and ER bonding systems.

INTRODUCTION

Silver diamine fluoride (SDF), a colorless solution containing fluoride ions, inhibits the demineralization of carious lesions, facilitates remineralization, and prevents the destruction of the organic matrix of the tooth.[1] The mechanism of action is that silver ions (Ag+) are reported to have antimicrobial effects, and metallic silver (Ag or Ag°) which is relatively inert. The metallic silver can interact with moisture in an oral environment and would release silver ions.[2] SDF can also increase the mineral density of enamel lesions and the microhardness of dental caries.[34] SDF inhibits matrix metalloproteinase and cysteine cathepsin and limits dentin collagen degradation.[56] Recently, it has been shown that SDF has an antimicrobial effect and reduces caries biofilm formation.[7] However, SDF may cause some side effects. For example, carious lesions turn black during the reaction of SDF products with dental tissues. Therefore, due to its inherent properties and aesthetic problems, SDF has not been widely accepted by patients.[8] In addition, SDF may be a useful anticaries pretreatment compound for dental tissue before placing restorative materials and preventing the formation of recurrent caries.[910]

Most of the studies have investigated the immediate effect of SDF on glass ionomer bond strength[11] and etch-and-rinse (ER) and self-etch adhesives on sound dentin (SD).[12] However, this study investigated not only the effect of SDF on natural caries affected dentin (CD) but also the effect of SDF aging on bond strength.

Considering the effectiveness of SDF in reducing the caries progress rate, using this substance in patients with many progressing caries can be a great help in caries control. Yet, since cavities, depending on the position in the mouth in terms of function and beauty, require restorative interventions with composites, this study was carried out to investigate the immediate effect of SDF on the microshear bond strength (μSBS) of sixth-generation self-etch and universal ER adhesives to sound and CD and also to examine the effect of 1-month SDF aging on the bond strength of sound and CD. The null hypothesis ran as follows: Neither the SDF application nor the water storage period would have any effect on the bond strength to CD and SD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

In this experimental study (ethic code: IR.IAU.KHUISF.REC.1398.053), 120 human third molars (60 sound and 60 caries-affected teeth) were used. The teeth were mounted in acrylic (Acropars, Iran) using polyethylene generators with an inner diameter of 14 mm and a height of 20 mm. Then, the acrylic block was fixed to the holding jig of the trimmer so that the occlusal surface of the tooth was parallel to the transverse plane. Next, a low-speed trimmer with plenty of water cooling (Cutting Machine, Krupp Dental, Germany) was used to remove the occlusal enamel and expose a uniform layer of dentin.

According to the International Caries Detection and Assessment System, half of the teeth were caries-free,[13] and the other half had occlusal caries, which involved about two-thirds of the dentin in periapical radiography. After removing the occlusal enamel, a caries detector solution (1.0% acid red in propylene glycol, Kuraray Medicine Inc., Tokyo, Japan) was applied to the trimmed surfaces of the dentin.[1415]

Because the conventional caries detection methods cannot detect a small amount of demineralization, quantitative caries detection methods were used. The DIAGNOdent device (KAVO DIAGNOdent 2095, Germany) is capable of receiving, analyzing, and quantifying the fluorescence emitted from bacterial products. To evaluate the quantity and sameness of the samples, the extension of the caries on each tooth was evaluated by the DIAGNOdent device.

According to Figure 1, the teeth were randomly divided into 12 groups based on the type of dentin (SD or CD), application of SDF, and the type of adhesive, Clearfil Liner Bond (LB, Kuraray Noritake Dental Inc., Tokyo, Japan) or All Bond (AB) Universal (Bisco Inc., Schaumburg, USA), in ER mode.

In the second 20 samples, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, SDF was applied to the tooth surface for 3 min and allowed to absorb, which was then washed and dried. Immediately after applying the SDF, AB universal adhesive was applied to half of the samples, and LB self-etch adhesive was applied to the other half.

The third 20 samples were kept in artificial saliva (20 mmol/l NaHCO3, 3 mmol/l NaH2PO4, and 1 mmol/l CaCl2) in an incubator at 37°C for 1 month after applying SDF. Then, the AB universal adhesive was applied to half of the samples and the LB self-etch adhesive to the other half. The classification of CD samples was done exactly like the SD samples.

It should be noted that in the groups that used AB with etch and rinse instructions, were etched with 32% phosphoric acid for 15 s, then washed for 15 s, and then gently blot-dried with cotton pellets.

The desired composite (Amelogen Plus, ULTRADENT PRODUCTS, INC, USA) with A2 color was placed in a transparent plastic tube with an inner diameter of 0.9 mm and a height of 2 mm. The composite plastic tubes were filled with a plugger (Joya, Iran) so that a convex semi-circle was created on the bonding side to prevent bubbles and a gap between the composite and the tooth surface. Then, the plastic tube was placed on the surface of the dentin, and the composite was cured for 20 s on each side by a Valo light curing device (Ultradent CO, USA) with an intensity of 700–800 Mw/cm2 (at a light intensity of 700–800 Mw/cm2), which had been previously calibrated using a radiometer (Demetron LED Radiometer, SDS, Kerr, Orange, CA, USA). The plastic cylinder was then gently cut using a scalpel blade and removed from the small composite cylinder. Then, the diameter of each composite cylinder was measured by a digital caliper (Mitutoyo 500-1710-6, Digimatic Caliper, Mitutoyo Corp, Tokyo, Japan).[16] The composite was post-cured again for 40 s. The samples were kept in an incubator (Norsanat Ferdous, Iran) for 24 h at 37°C (100% humidity). Then, the μSBS was measured using a universal testing device (SANTAM, IRAN), and all the samples were broken by this device. To apply force, a chisel-like mandrel was used; the force was applied to the closest sample-dentin connection point, and the samples were subjected to a shear force at a speed of 0.5 mm/min until fracture occurred. The maximum force applied to the sample was recorded in Newton on the device monitor, and the bond strength was calculated in MPa by dividing the maximum force at the time of separation of the sample by the cross-sectional area of the composite cylinder (mm2).

The type of failure was examined under a stereo microscope (OLYMPUS, SDF PLAPO 1XPF, JAPAN) at ×40 magnification. Failures included adhesive failure (bond failure at adhesive-substrate interface), cohesive failure (failure occurs inside the material, and some material remains on the surface), or combined failure (a combination of cohesive and adhesive failures).

Data analysis was done by SPSS-22 software (IBM Co., Armonk, NY, USA) using three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Bonferroni post hoc test, and Duncan’s post hoc test. The significance level was set at 0.05.

DISCUSSION

The results of the present study showed that bond strength was lower in CD than in SD in all the studied groups, which is in line with the results of other studies, regardless of the type of adhesive system (ER or self-etch; 1-, 2-, or 3-stage bonding process).[171819202122] Wei et al.[21] reported that the hardness of CD was lower than that of normal dentin because CD is partially demineralized and has more porous intertubular dentin. This demineralization and reduced hardness can justify the reduction of bond strength to CD. In addition to the altered organic and inorganic phases of CD, collagen can change under substructural changes during the caries process.[23] The cross-linking of collagen fibers in CD decreases,[24] apatite crystals are larger, and the space between crystals enlarges in intertubular dentin.[25] The smear layer in the carious tooth may be more resistant to acid etching or self-etch primers because of the acid-resistant crystals and foreign proteins that have penetrated into the mineral phase during the demineralization cycle.[26] According to Wang’s study,[23] the reason for the lower bond strength of CD is the presence of a thick hybrid layer into which the resin has not completely penetrated.

However, a number of studies have reported no difference between the strength of the adhesive bond to CD and SD.[2728] This difference can be due to the difference in the adhesive system used. In addition, CD has a variable nature inherently and can be different from one sample to another. Further, the diagnostic criteria used in various studies include visual observation, touch, and the use of a caries detector solution, which is largely subjective and can be the reason for the differences in the results of the studies.

According to the results of the present study, the mean μSBS in SD without SDF was significantly lower in AB than in LB, which is consistent with the results of Muñoz et al.[29] Furthermore, in the present study, the mean μSBS in SD with SDF was higher in AB than in LB, although it was not statistically significant. In self-etch systems containing 10-MDP, the better bond is the result of the chemical bond created between this molecule and calcium, so the presence of any contamination prevents this chemical bond. Therefore, the self-etch system has a higher sensitivity to impurities than the ER system. The alkaline pH of alkaline SDF, to some extent, hinders the proper and sufficient performance of self-etch systems.[303132] This problem, to some extent, hinders the proper and sufficient performance of acid etching of ER systems and primers of self-etch systems.

According to the results of the present study, the SDF solution in SD decreased the immediate μSBS, although this difference was significant in the LB system and not significant in the AB system, which was consistent with the results of a number of studies.[303132] However, some other studies have shown that SDF has no effect on the immediate bond strength of the composite to SD,[12333435] and the reason for this difference can be the time of using acid etching in the ER system before application of SDF and the self-etch system without MDP. The number of samples and rinsing time can also affect the results.

In Lutgen et al.’s study,[31] the bond strength of SD decreased after the application of SDF, and the μSBS depended on the SDF application protocol. The control group without SDF had the highest bond strength. The surface-polished SDF before the application of bonding, the SDF rinsing group, and finally the group without SDF rinsing had the lowest bond strength. In this study, the self-etch adhesive was used.[36] The high pH of SDF (about 10) can have a negative effect on the poor acidity of the self-etch adhesive.[37] Rinsing removes only the surface layer of SDF, while silver and fluoride ions penetrate into a depth of 450 μm.[31] Also, when SDF is applied, due to the bonding of silver nitrate to dentin, cubic crystals containing phosphate, silver, and calcium are formed, which cannot be removed by rinsing.[38] Markham et al.[32] indicated that the bond strength of the composite to enamel and dentin decreased with the use of universal adhesive in the self-etch mode after the application of SDF. For this reason, the universal adhesive was used in the ER mode in the present study. Moreover, like these studies, SDF was used before the etch and prime process to match the existing clinical conditions. The time of application and rinsing was according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Unlike SD, SDF slightly increased the immediate μSBS in the CD group, which was not significant in any of the bonding systems and was consistent with the results of the studies of Firouzmandi et al.[33] and de Siqueira et al.[39] This increase can be due to the reaction of SDF with the mineral hydroxyapatite of the tooth and the formation of calcium fluoride and silver fluoride. These compounds are responsible for the remineralization of carious lesions.[38] The increase in the bond strength of demineralized dentin after applying SDF can be attributed to the improvement of the mechanical properties of demineralized dentin due to the use of SDF. This solution can increase the hardness and strength of CD.[404142]

In the present study, the bond strength in SD with delayed SDF was significantly higher in AB than in LB. The self-etch primer has a pH of about 2 and has enough strength to remove the smear layer resulting from 600 grit SiC; however, it may not be acidic enough to remove the smear layer containing SDF. This residual smear layer can prevent the penetration of resin monomer and the formation of the self-etch-adhesive interface seal.[314344]

As for the effect of SDF on the bond strength of CD in the present study, the type of bond and the use or non-use of SDF did not create a statistically significant difference in the bond strength in the CD, which is consistent with the results of Van Duker et al.’s study.[37]

Applying SDF to CD and giving it a chance to age for 1 month has no effect on composite bond strength. Due to high porosity, CD absorbs more water than SD. This can cause the mineral sediments of SDF to be washed out over time. With the removal of minerals from the CD, its mechanical properties decrease, which can be considered a reason for the decrease in bond strength compared to the immediate test.

The analysis of the nature of the failure in the interfacial area showed no significant difference among the twelve groups in the present study. The highest frequency in both CD and SD was of mixed failure type. In Torres’s study, most of the failures were of mixed type.[45]

CONCLUSION

The application of SDF to healthy dentin, regardless of the type of bond, decreases the bond strength, while it increases the bond strength of CD. If SDF is used in SD, it is better to use ER and AB bonding systems.
