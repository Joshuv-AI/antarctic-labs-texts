Schur-Agler class and Carathéodory extremal functions
Anindya Biswas

Abstract
. We study the role of Carathéodory extremal functions in the Schur-Agler class generated by a collection of test functions. We show that under certain conditions, 𝔻 \mathbb{D} and 𝔻 2 \mathbb{D}^{2} are the only domains where finitely many test functions can generate the Schur class. As applications, we give a description of the Carathéodory extremals in the unit ball of the multiplier algebra of the Drury-Arveson space and give operator-theoretic Herglotz representations for any Carathéodory hyperbolic domain.

1.
Introduction.

Suppose that
\Omega
is a Carathéodory hyperbolic domain in some
\mathbb{C}^{m}
. For the well-studied domains the open unit disc
\mathbb{D}
and the bidisc
\mathbb{D}^{2}
, any element of the Schur class (the closed unit ball of the Banach algebra of all bounded holomorphic function on
\Omega
, denoted by
\mathscr{S}(\Omega)
) has a structure known as the
realization formula
, which states that given any
f\in\mathscr{S}(\Omega)
, there is a Hilbert space
\mathscr{X}
, a unitary operator
U:\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathscr{X}
and an operator
z\in B(\mathscr{X})
which is identified with the identity function on
\Omega
such that with

U=\bordermatrix{&\mathbb{C}&\mathscr{X}\cr\mathbb{C}&a&B\cr\mathscr{X}&C&D},

one can express
f
as
f(z)=a+Bz(I_{\mathscr{X}}-Dz)^{-1}C
for all
z\in\Omega
.

In the theory of interpolation, the realization formula has certain important implications. For instance, it is the realization formula which leads to the fact that every solvable Pick interpolation problem on
\mathbb{D}^{2}
has a rational inner solution (
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
). Due to such importance, the property of having a realization formula has been investigated further and it has been found that in the case of the annulus (
[
9
]
,
[
13
]
) and the symmetrized bidisc (
[
12
]
) similar form can be established, though not as convenient as in the case of
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{D}^{2}
, but still provides useful and far reaching consequences (
[
11
]
).

In the case of
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{D}^{2}
, in a certain sense, only a finite number of elementary functions can generate the whole Schur class, namely, the identity function on
\mathbb{D}
and the coordinate functions on
\mathbb{D}^{2}
, although this is not the case for the other domains. For the annulus and the symmetrized bidisc, one requires infinitely many functions to provide a realization formula, and in this article we will see that beyond the domain
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{D}^{2}
, it is indeed necessary to consider such an infinite collection.

Generalizing the idea that a certain collection of functions has the property of generating the whole Schur class leads us to a notion known as the
Schur-Agler class
, or more precisely, the
\Psi
-Schur-Agler class
for a given collection of functions
\Psi
(
[
9
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
13
]
). We call such a collection
\Psi
a set of test functions and they are introduced in the following way.

Definition 1.1
.

A collection
\Psi
of
\mathbb{C}
-valued functions on
\Omega
is called a set of
test functions
if the following conditions hold.

(1)

\sup\{|\psi(z)|:\psi\in\Psi\}<1
for each
z\in\Omega
.

(2)

For each finite subset
F
of
\Omega
, the collection
\{\psi|_{F}:\psi\in\Psi\}
together with the constant function generates the algebra of all
\mathbb{C}
-valued functions on
F
.

We can topologize
\Psi
by considering it as a subspace of
\overline{\mathbb{D}}^{\Omega}
equipped with the product topology. For every
z\in\Omega
, we define an element
E(z)
in
\mathcal{C}_{b}(\Psi)
,
the
C^{*}
-algebra of all bounded continuous functions on

\Psi
, such that
E(z)(\psi)=\psi(z)
. Clearly,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\|E(z)\|=\text{sup}_{\psi\in\Psi}|\psi(z)|<1

for each
z\in\Omega
. These functions
E(z)
have a key role in the development of the theory of
\Psi
-Schur-Agler class.

Given such a set
\Psi
, we next introduce a collection of positive semidefinite functions or kernels
k:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
satisfying the property

\displaystyle\big((1-\psi(z)\overline{\psi(w)})k(z,w)\big)_{z,w\in\Omega}\geq\mathbf{0},\,\ \text{for all}\,\,\psi\in\Psi.

These functions
k
are called the
\Psi
-admissible kernels and we will denote the set of all
\Psi
-admissible kernels by
\mathcal{K}_{\Psi}
.

Now we can describe the
\Psi
-Schur-Agler class.

Definition 1.2
.

Given a collection of test functions
\Psi
on a domain
\Omega
, the
\Psi
-Schur-Agler class is the collection of functions
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
for which

\displaystyle\big((1-f(z)\overline{f(w)})k(z,w)\big)_{z,w\in\Omega}\geq\mathbf{0},\,\ \text{for all}\,\,k\in\mathcal{K}_{\Psi}.

The
\Psi
-Schur-Agler class on
\Omega
will be denoted by
\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\psi}(\Omega)
, or
\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}
when the underlying domain is clear from the context.

One should note that, we do not require
\Psi
to be a subset of
\mathscr{S}(\Omega)
, but then
\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\psi}(\Omega)
, which also contains
\Psi
, is not a subset of
\mathscr{S}(\Omega)
. On the other hand, if we take
\Psi
from
\mathscr{S}(\Omega)
,
\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\psi}(\Omega)
is then contained in
\mathscr{S}(\Omega)
and it inherits a number of important properties from
\mathscr{S}(\Omega)
. For instance, since to check positivity of
\big((1-f(z)\overline{f(w)})k(z,w)\big)_{z,w\in\Omega}
one needs to consider only finitely many points from
\Omega
at a time,
\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\psi}(\Omega)
is closed under pointwise convergence. For the development with holomorphic test functions, an interested reader may consult
[
11
]
. The main result providing the structure of
\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\psi}(\Omega)
is the following (see
[
9
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
13
]
).

Theorem 1.3
.

For a function
f:\Omega\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, the following statements are equivalent.

(1)

f\in\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\psi}(\Omega)
.

(2)

f
has an Agler decomposition, that is, there exists a completely
positive kernel
\Gamma:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{b}(\Psi)^{*}
such that

(1.2)

\displaystyle 1-f(z)\overline{f(w)}=\Gamma(z,w)(1-E(z)E(w)^{*})\,\,\,\text{for all $z,w\in\Omega$.}

(3)

There is a Hilbert space
\mathscr{X}
, a unitary
U:\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathscr{X}
and a unital
*
-representation
\rho:\mathcal{C}_{b}(\Psi)\rightarrow B(\mathscr{X})
such that writing
U
as

\displaystyle U=\bordermatrix{&\mathbb{C}&\mathscr{X}\cr\mathbb{C}&a&B\cr\mathscr{X}&C&D},

one can express
f
as
f(z)=a+B\rho(E(z))(I_{\mathscr{X}}-D\rho(E(z)))^{-1}C
for all
z\in\Omega
.

(4)

There is a Hilbert space
\mathscr{X}
, a unital
*
-representation
\rho:\mathcal{C}_{b}(\Psi)\rightarrow B(\mathscr{X})
and a map
h:\Omega\rightarrow\mathscr{X}
such that

(1.3)

\displaystyle 1-\overline{f(z)}f(w)=\langle\rho(1-E(z)^{*}E(w))h(w),h(z)\rangle_{\mathscr{X}}\,\,\,\text{for all $z,w\in\Omega$.}

By the completely positive kernel
\Gamma
in (
1.2
) we mean that for any
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\Omega
,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{b}(\Psi)
, the matrix
\big(\Gamma(z_{i},z_{j})(a^{*}_{i}a_{j})\big)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
is positive semidefinite. It is known (
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
) that, for any such
\Gamma
, there is a Hilbert space
\mathscr{X}
, a unital
*
-representation
\rho:\mathcal{C}_{b}(\Psi)\rightarrow B(\mathscr{X})
and a map
g:\Omega\rightarrow\mathscr{X}(=B(\mathscr{X},\mathbb{C}))
such that

(1.4)

\displaystyle\Gamma(z,w)(\delta)=\langle\rho(\delta)g(w)^{*},g(z)^{*}\rangle.

In this article, we will always assume that our collection
\Psi
of test functions are elements of
\mathscr{S}(\Omega)
and that there is a point
w_{0}\in\Omega
such that
\psi(w_{0})=0
for all
\psi\in\Psi
. As a consequence,
\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}\subset\mathscr{S}(\Omega)
and
E(w_{0})=0
. The fact that this assumption does not alter the
\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}
can be found in
[
11
]
.

Let us now describe our work in this article. In Section
2
we establish a necessary condition for a collection of test functions to generate the Schur class and show that under certain conditions
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{D}^{2}
are the only domains such that finitely many test functions can generate the Schur class. We show that the collection of test functions and the admissible kernels are closely related to certain extremal functions. In the next section we give two applications of our results. The first one describes the range of Carathéodory extremals in the unit ball of the multiplier algebra of the Drury-Arveson space. As a second application, we give an operator theoretic Herglotz representation for any Carathéodory hyperbolic domain.

2.
Extremal functions and
\Psi

We consider any two points
z_{1},z_{2}\in\Omega
and define

(2.1)

\displaystyle d_{\Psi}(z_{1},z_{2})=\sup\Big\{m(f(z_{1}),f(z_{2}))=\Big|\frac{f(z_{1})-f(z_{2})}{1-f(z_{1})\overline{f(z_{2})}}\Big|:f\in\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}\Big\}.

Since
\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}
is closed under pointwise convergence and it is contained in the Schur class, using Montel’s theorem, we can say that there is an
f\in\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}
such that
d_{\Psi}(z_{1},z_{2})=m(f(z_{1}),f(z_{2}))
.

Theorem 2.1
.

(2.2)

\displaystyle d_{\Psi}(z_{1},z_{2})=\inf

\displaystyle\Big\{\sqrt{1-\frac{|k(z_{1},z_{2})|^{2}}{k(z_{1},z_{1})k(z_{2},z_{2})}}:k(z_{1},z_{1})\neq 0\neq k(z_{2},z_{2}),

\displaystyle k\,\text{is an admissible kernel, i.e., }k\in K_{\Psi},\Big\}.

Proof.

We have that there is an
f\in\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}
such that

d_{\Psi}(z_{1},z_{2})=m(f(z_{1}),f(z_{2})).

So, for any
k\in\mathcal{K}_{\Psi}
we have

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}(1-|f(z_{1})|^{2})k(z_{1},z_{1})&(1-f(z_{1})\overline{f(z_{2})})k(z_{1},z_{2})\\
(1-f(z_{2})\overline{f(z_{1})})k(z_{2},z_{1})&(1-|f(z_{2})|^{2})k(z_{2},z_{2})\end{pmatrix}\geq\mathbf{0}.

Using the fact that the above matrix has non-negative determinant, we get

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\frac{|k(z_{1},z_{2})|^{2}}{k(z_{1},z_{1})k(z_{2},z_{2})}}\geq m(f(z_{1}),f(z_{2}))=d_{\Psi}(z_{1},z_{2}).

Now, we consider the following function on
\Omega\times\Omega

(2.3)

\displaystyle\tilde{k}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-f(x)\overline{f(y)}},\,x,y\in\{z_{1},z_{2}\}

\displaystyle=0,\,\,\text{otherwise}.

It is easy to see that
\tilde{k}
is an admissible kernel and

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\frac{|\tilde{k}(z_{1},z_{2})|^{2}}{\tilde{k}(z_{1},z_{1})\tilde{k}(z_{2},z_{2})}}=m(f(z_{1}),f(z_{2}))=d_{\Psi}(z_{1},z_{2})

and the proof is complete.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

(2.4)

\displaystyle d_{\Psi}(z_{1},z_{2})=\sup\Big\{m(\psi(z_{1}),\psi(z_{2})):\psi\in\Psi\Big\}.

Moreover, if
\Psi
is closed under pointwise convergence, then
d_{\Psi}(z_{1},z_{2})
is attained by some element of
\Psi
.

Proof.

Since each element of
\Psi
vanishes at a point of
\Omega
, there is a non-constant holomorphic function
\tilde{\psi}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{D}
such that

\sup\Big\{m(\psi(z_{1}),\psi(z_{2})):\psi\in\Psi\Big\}=m(\tilde{\psi}(z_{1}),\tilde{\psi}(z_{1})).

If we construct a function like
\tilde{k}
as in (
2.3
) using
\tilde{\psi}
, then we get an admissible kernel. When the collection
\Psi
is closed in the topology of pointwise convergence, we have
\tilde{\psi}\in\Psi\subset\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}
. This completes the proof.
∎

We now recall the Carathéodory pseudodistance on
\Omega
. Given two points
z_{1},z_{2}\in\Omega
, it is defined by

(2.5)

\displaystyle c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{1},z_{2})=sup\Big\{m(f(z_{1}),f(z_{2}))=\Big|\frac{f(z_{1})-f(z_{2})}{1-f(z_{1})\overline{f(z_{2})}}\Big|:f\in\mathscr{S}(\Omega),f(\Omega)\subset\mathbb{D}\Big\}.

A few words are in order. It is known that the Carathéodory pseudodistance is always attained and the functions that attain it are called
Carathéodory extremals
. Here are a few examples of domains and their Carathéodory extremals.

Example 2.3
.

(1)

For the open unit disc
\mathbb{D}
, the Carathéodory extremal is the identity function on
\mathbb{D}
and it is unique upto compositions with elements of the automorphism group of
\mathbb{D}
.

(2)

In the case of bidisc, the extremals are the coordinate functions and again they are unique (
[
15
]
).

(3)

On the symmetrized bidisc
\mathbb{G}=\{(z_{1}+z_{2},z_{1}z_{2}):z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{D}\}
, a set of Carathéodory extremals is given by (
[
7
]
,
[
15
]
)

\displaystyle\{\mathbb{G}\ni(s,p)\mapsto\frac{2zp-s}{2-zs}\in\mathbb{D}:z\in\mathbb{D}\}.

It turns out, the collections we described above serve as test functions that generate the whole Schur class (
[
5
]
,
[
12
]
). The next result shows that containing Carathéodory extremals is necessary, not sufficient though (
[
2
]
), for a collection of test functions to generate the Schur class.

Corollary 2.4
.

A necessary condition for a collection
\Psi
of test functions to satisfy
\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}=\mathscr{S}(\Omega)
is that for any two points
z_{1},z_{2}\in\Omega
, the Carathéodory pseudodistance
c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{1},z_{2})
must be equal to

\sup\Big\{m(\psi(z_{1}),\psi(z_{2})):\psi\in\Psi\Big\}.

Proof.

If
\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}=\mathscr{S}(\Omega)
, then we must have
d_{\Psi}=c^{*}_{\Omega}
, and hence, the result follows from this.
∎

It is known that upto some extent the Carathéodory extremals carry the data sufficient to characterize the underlying domain (
[
3
]
,
[
16
]
). When the domain
\Omega
is the polydisc
\mathbb{D}^{m}
, it is known that there are exactly
m
Carathéodory extremals, namely, the coordinate functions (
[
15
]
). But in general it does not hold, that is, a universal set for the Carathéodory extremal problem (a set consisting of functions from
\mathscr{S}(\Omega)
such that
c_{\Omega}^{*}
between any two points is attained by a function of that collection) is generically infinite. Thus, the problem of generating a Schur class with finitely many
\mathbb{C}
-valued test functions has very little hope. However, the importance of the realization formula asks for further development and, in the next result, we show that there is a workaround for this problem.

Proposition 2.5
.

For any Carathéodory hyperbolic domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
, there is a collection
\Psi
of test functions such that
\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}=\mathscr{S}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Let
w_{0}\in\Omega
and let
\Psi=\{\psi_{f}(z)=\frac{f(w_{0})-f(z)}{1-\overline{f(w_{0})}f(z)}:f\in\mathscr{S}(\Omega)\}
. Then clearly, this
\Psi
satisfies the second defining condition for test function, as it separates the points of
\Omega
. Also, for any point
z\in\Omega

\displaystyle|\psi_{f}(z)|=\bigg|\frac{f(w_{0})-f(z)}{1-\overline{f(w_{0})}f(z)}\bigg|\leq c^{*}_{\Omega}(w_{0},z)<1.

Since
\Psi\subset\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}
and, for any
a\in\mathbb{D}
and
\psi\in\Psi
,
(a-\psi)(1-\overline{a}\psi)^{-1}\in\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}
we have
\mathscr{S}(\Omega)\subset\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}
. This completes the proof.
∎

We end the section with a result which says that under certain conditions, the disc and the bidisc are the only two domains whose Schur class can be generated by finitely many functions. Before we go into the result, let us observe the following: suppose that
\Psi=\{\psi_{j}:1\leq j\leq n\}
is finite and for each
j
, we consider the function
\tau_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{b}(\Psi)
satisfying
\tau_{j}(\psi_{k})=\delta_{jk}
(the Kronecker delta). Then, the collection
\{\tau_{j}:1\leq j\leq n\}
generates
\mathcal{C}_{b}(\Psi)
and for any fixed
z\in\Omega
we can write
E(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\psi_{j}(z)\tau_{j}
. In this case, we conclude from (
1.3
) that for a given
f\in\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}
there is a Hilbert space
\mathscr{X}
, a unital
*
-representation
\rho:\mathcal{C}_{b}(\Psi)\rightarrow B(\mathscr{X})
and a map
h:\Omega\rightarrow\mathscr{X}
such that

(2.6)

\displaystyle 1-\overline{f(z)}f(w)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(1-\overline{\psi_{j}(z)}\psi_{j}(w))\langle\rho(\tau_{j})h(w),h(z)\rangle_{\mathscr{X}}\,\,\,\text{for all $z,w\in\Omega$.}

To state the result, we need the following two notions.

(1)

The first one is
\gamma
-hyperbolicity
. We call a domain
\gamma
-hyperbolic if its Carathéodory–Reiffen pseudometric is nondegenrate (
[
15
]
).

(2)

Secondly, we call a domain
\Omega

c^{*}
-finitely compact
if for each
z_{1}\in\Omega
and
r\in(0,1)
the set
\{z_{2}\in\Omega:c^{*}(z_{1},z_{2})<r\}
is a compact subset of
\Omega
(
[
16
]
).

Theorem 2.6
.

For a
\gamma
-hyperbolic and
c^{*}
-finitely compact domain
\Omega
, the following are equivalent.

(1)

\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{m}
and there is a collection
\Psi=\{\psi_{j}:1\leq j\leq n\}
consisting of
m
functions such that
\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}=\mathscr{S}(\Omega)
.

(2)

m=1
or
2
and,
\Omega
is biholomorphic with
\mathbb{D}^{m}
.

Proof.

(2)\implies(1):
We prove it for the case
m=2
. Let
F:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{D}^{2}
be a biholomorphism and let
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
denote the coordinate functions on
\mathbb{D}^{2}
. Then clearly,
\psi_{j}=z_{j}\circ F
are test functions on
\Omega
. For an
f\in\mathscr{S}(\Omega)
, we find that
f\circ F^{-1}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
, and hence, has an Agler decomposition of the form (
2.6
) in terms of
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
. From this we conclude that
f
has an Agler decomposition in terms of
\psi_{j},j=1,2
.

(1)\implies(2):
When
\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}=\mathscr{S}(\Omega)
, we see that
\Psi
is a universal set for the Carathéodory extremal problem. By Theorem 4.4 in
[
16
]
, the map
\Phi:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{D}^{m}
sending
w
to
(\psi_{1}(w)\ldots,\psi_{m}(w))
is a biholomorphism. Invariance of the Carathéodory pseudodistance says that
\{\psi_{j}\circ\Phi^{-1}:1\leq j\leq m\}
is a universal set for the Carathéodory extremal problem on
\mathbb{D}^{m}
. By uniqueness Carathéodory extremals on
\mathbb{D}^{m}
, we see that the set
\{\psi_{j}\circ\Phi^{-1}:1\leq j\leq m\}
is just the set
\{z_{j}:1\leq j\leq m\}
of
m
coordinate functions. If
f\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{D}^{m})
, then
f\circ\Phi\in\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}
, and hence, has an Agler decomposition of the form (
2.6
) in terms of the functions in
\Psi
. Consequently,
f
has an Agler decomposition in terms of the coordinate functions
z_{j}
. But, this is false unless
m=1
or
2
(
[
2
]
,
[
13
]
).
∎

3.
Applications

3.1.
Carathéodory extremals and the multiplier algebra of the Drury-Arveson space

Following the description in
[
5
]
, we consider the kernel
K_{m}:\mathbb{B}_{m}\times\mathbb{B}_{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined by

\displaystyle K_{m}(z,w)=\frac{1}{1-\langle z,w\rangle_{m}},

where
\mathbb{B}_{m}
is the Euclidean unit ball in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
and
\langle.,.\rangle_{m}
is the usual inner product in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
.
The reproducing kernel Hilbert space generated by
K_{m}
is known as the
Drury–Arveson space
. Obviously, following the proof of Proposition
2.5
, we can say that
K_{m}
serves as an admissible kernel for the Drury-Arveson space, so does any kernel
K
of the form

\displaystyle K(z,w)

\displaystyle=K_{m}(z,w),\,\,\text{for all}\,\,z,w\in\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{l}\}

\displaystyle=0,\,\text{for all}\,\,z,w\in\mathbb{B}_{m}-\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{l}\}

for any fixed collection of points
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{l}\}
in
\mathbb{B}_{m}
.

We observe that for any
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{B}_{m}

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\frac{|K_{m}(z_{1},z_{2})|^{2}}{K_{m}(z_{1},z_{1})K_{m}(z_{2},z_{2})}}=c^{*}_{\mathbb{B}_{m}}(z_{1},z_{2}).

So, the interpolation problem
z_{1}\mapsto 0
and
z_{2}\mapsto c^{*}_{\mathbb{B}_{m}}(z_{1},z_{2})
has a solution in the closed unit ball of the multiplier algebra of the Drury-Arveson space, that is, the closed unit ball of the multiplier algebra contains a Carathéodory extremal. We will describe the possible values of this Carathéodory extremal (upto compositions with elements of the automorphisms of
\mathbb{D}
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{B}_{m}
and
f:\mathbb{B}_{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{D}
is a solution to the interpolation problem
z_{1}\mapsto 0
and
z_{2}\mapsto c^{*}_{\mathbb{B}_{m}}(z_{1},z_{2})
in the closed unit ball of the multiplier algebra of the Drury-Arveson space. Then, for any
z_{3}\in\mathbb{B}_{m}
we have

\displaystyle f(z_{3})\in\Big\{w\in\mathbb{D}:\frac{1-c^{*}_{\mathbb{B}_{m}}(z_{1},z_{2})\overline{w}}{|1-c^{*}_{\mathbb{B}_{m}}(z_{1},z_{2})\overline{w}|}=\frac{1-\langle z_{2},z_{3}\rangle_{m}}{|1-\langle z_{2},z_{3}\rangle_{m}|}\Big\}.

In particular,
f
is always real valued on the set
\{z\in\mathbb{B}_{m}:\langle z,z_{2}\rangle=0\}
.

Proof.

Let
z_{3}\in\mathbb{B}_{m}
and
f(z_{i})=w_{j},j=1,2,3
. We write

k_{i,j}=\frac{K_{m}(z_{i},z_{j})}{\sqrt{K_{m}(z_{i},z_{i})K_{m}(z_{j},z_{j})}}

and
m_{i,j}=m(w_{i},w_{j})
.

Since the matrix
\big((1-w_{i}\overline{w_{j}})K_{m}(z_{i},z_{j})\big)
is positive semidefinite, so is the matrix
\big((1-w_{i}\overline{w_{j}})k_{i,j}\big)
. Taking the Schur product of this matrix with

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&e^{i\theta}&e^{i\sigma}\\
e^{-i\theta}&1&e^{i(\sigma-\theta)}\\
e^{-i\sigma}&e^{i(-\sigma+\theta)}&1\end{pmatrix},

we may assume that
k_{1,2}
and
k_{1,3}
are positive. Now, using the fact that
\big((1-w_{i}\overline{w_{j}})k_{i,j}\big)
has non-negative determinant, we find

\displaystyle\frac{k_{1,2}|k_{2,3}|k_{3,1}}{\sqrt{(1-m_{1,2}^{2})(1-m_{2,3}^{2})(1-m_{3,1}^{2})}}

\displaystyle 2Re\Biggl\{e^{i\theta}\frac{(1-w_{1}\overline{w_{2}})(1-w_{2}\overline{w_{3}})(1-w_{3}\overline{w_{1}})}{|(1-w_{1}\overline{w_{2}})(1-w_{2}\overline{w_{3}})(1-w_{3}\overline{w_{1}})|}\Biggr\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{k_{1,2}^{2}}{(1-m_{1,2}^{2})}+\frac{|k_{2,3}|^{2}}{(1-m_{2,3}^{2})}+\frac{k_{3,1}^{2}}{(1-m_{3,1}^{2})}-1.

We have
k_{1,2}=\sqrt{1-c^{*}_{\mathbb{B}_{m}}(z_{1},z_{2})^{2}}=\sqrt{1-m_{1,2}^{2}}
,
w_{1}=0
and
w_{2}=c^{*}_{\mathbb{B}_{m}}(z_{1},z_{2})
. Using these together with the AM-GM inequality gives that
w_{3}
must satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{1-c^{*}_{\mathbb{B}_{m}}(z_{1},z_{2})\overline{w_{3}}}{|1-c^{*}_{\mathbb{B}_{m}}(z_{1},z_{2})\overline{w_{3}}|}=\frac{1-\langle z_{2},z_{3}\rangle_{m}}{|1-\langle z_{2},z_{3}\rangle_{m}|}.

∎

3.2.
A Herglotz Representation

For a domain
\Omega
, the
Herglotz class
on
\Omega
is the class of all holomorphic functions
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
that satisfy
Re(f(z))\geq 0
for all
z\in\Omega
. The classical Herglotz representation theorem (
[
14
]
) states that for any such function
f
on the
\mathbb{D}
, we can find a real number
b
and a finite
non-negative Borel measure
\mu
on the circle
\partial\mathbb{D}
such that

f(z)=ib+\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\frac{1+tz}{1-tz}d\mu(t),\,\,\text{for all}\,\,z\in\mathbb{D}.

There are a few generalizations of this result, and among them, our focus will be on the operator-theoretic ones. Our work will closely follow the proof described in the book
[
6
]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

For any holomorphic function
h:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, the following are equivalent.

(1)

Re(h(z))\geq 0
for all
z\in\Omega
.

(2)

There is a real number
b
, a Hilbert space
\mathscr{X}
, a unitary operator
U\in B(\mathscr{X})
, and a
\gamma\in\mathscr{X}
such that with
\Psi
as in Proposition
2.5
, we can find a unital
*
-representation
\rho:\mathcal{C}_{b}(\Psi)\rightarrow B(\mathscr{X})
such that one can write

(3.1)

\displaystyle h(z)=ib+\Big\langle\gamma,(1+U\rho(E(z)^{*}))(1-U\rho(E(z)^{*}))^{-1}\gamma\Big\rangle\,\,\,\text{for all}\,\,\,z\in\Omega.

Proof.

It easily follows that
(2)
implies
(1)
. So we suppose that
(1)
holds. We choose
w_{0}
and
\Psi
as in the proof of Proposition
2.5
. First, we assume that
h(w_{0})=1
. Now with our choice of
\Psi
, we have
\frac{h-1}{h+1}\in\mathscr{S\mspace{-5mu}A}_{\Psi}
and by Theorem
1.3
, there is a Hilbert space
\mathscr{X}
, a unital
*
-representation
\rho:\mathcal{C}_{b}(\Psi)\rightarrow B(\mathscr{X})
and a map
g:\Omega\rightarrow\mathscr{X}
such that

(3.2)

\displaystyle 1-\frac{h(z)-1}{h(z)+1}\frac{\overline{h(w)}-1}{\overline{h(w)}+1}=\Big\langle\rho(1-E(z)E(w)^{*})g(w),g(z)\Big\rangle_{\mathscr{X}},\,\,\text{for all}\,\,z,w\in\Omega.

We write
k(z)=(\overline{h(z)}+1)g(z)
and deduce from (
3.2
) that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\Bigg\langle\begin{pmatrix}\overline{h(w)}-1\\
k(w)\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\overline{h(z)}-1\\
k(z)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\Bigg\rangle_{\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathscr{X}}=

\displaystyle\Bigg\langle\begin{pmatrix}\overline{h(w)}+1\\
\rho(E(w)^{*})k(w)\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\overline{h(z)}+1\\
\rho(E(z)^{*})k(z)\end{pmatrix}\Bigg\rangle_{\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathscr{X}}.

Therefore, we can construct a unitary operator
V:\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathscr{X}
such that
V
can be written as

\displaystyle V=\bordermatrix{&\mathbb{C}&\mathscr{X}\cr\mathbb{C}&a&B\cr\mathscr{X}&C&D},

and it sends
\begin{pmatrix}\overline{h(z)}+1\\
\rho(E(z)^{*})k(z)\end{pmatrix}
to
\begin{pmatrix}\overline{h(z)}-1\\
k(z)\end{pmatrix}
for all
z\in\Omega
. Since
B\in B(\mathscr{X},\mathbb{C})
and
C\in B(\mathbb{C},\mathscr{X})
, there exist unique
\beta
and
\gamma
in
\mathscr{X}
such that
B=1\otimes\beta
and
C=\gamma\otimes 1
where we follow the convention
(u\otimes v)(t)=\langle t,v\rangle u
.

Since
h(w_{0})=1
and
E(w_{0})=0
, we deduce from

a(\overline{h(z)}+1)+\langle\rho(E(z)^{*})k(z),\beta\rangle=\overline{h(z)}-1

that
a=0
. Using the fact that
V
is a unitary we also find that
||\gamma||=1
and the operator
U=\gamma\otimes\beta+D
is a unitary on
\mathscr{X}
.

In terms of the components of
V
, we now have

(\overline{h(z)}+1)\gamma+D\rho(E(z)^{*})k(z)=k(z)

and
\langle\rho(E(z)^{*})k(z),\beta\rangle=\overline{h(z)}-1
, and we deduce

(3.4)

\displaystyle k(z)

\displaystyle=U\rho(E(z)^{*})k(z)+2\gamma=2(1-U\rho(E(z)^{*}))^{-1}\gamma.

Putting
w=w_{0}
in (
3.3
) we find
2(1+h(z))=\langle 2\gamma,k(z)\rangle
and then using the formulas for
k(z)
from (
3.4
) one can easily deduce the formula (
3.1
) with
b=0
.

For the general case we take
h(w_{0})=a+ib
with real
a
and
b
. If
a=0
, then open mapping theorem says that
h
is constant and the formula (
3.1
) holds trivially. When
a\neq 0
, we have
a>0
. We replace
h
by
g=\frac{h-ib}{a}
and apply the above conclusion to get the required form.

This completes the proof.
∎
