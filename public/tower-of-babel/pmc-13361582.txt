Elevated serum TNF-α in patients with immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy correlates with muscle damage
Authors: Xingyu Han, Huizhen Ge, Qing Zhang, Mengge Yang, Bitao Bu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate serum levels of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) in patients with immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) and to explore its correlation with disease activity.MethodsSerum TNF-α levels were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). TNF-α mRNA expression in muscle tissues from IMNM patients was detected by reverse transcription quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR). In vitro experiments were performed on human muscle cells stimulated with TNF-α to assess cell viability and the expression of downstream molecules.ResultsSerum TNF-α levels were significantly elevated in IMNM patients compared to healthy controls (p = 0.0002). In IMNM patients, TNF-α levels showed a significant positive correlation with serum creatine kinase (CK; p = 0.0004) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH; p = 0.0137). TNF-α mRNA was also overexpressed in muscle biopsies from patients with IMNM. In vitro, TNF-α stimulation significantly reduced muscle cell viability and upregulated the mRNA expression of downstream inflammatory mediators, including IP-10, MCP-1, IL-6, and phosphorylated p65.ConclusionElevated serum TNF-α in IMNM patients is associated with muscle damage and may contribute to disease pathogenesis by amplifying the inflammatory response. These findings suggest that TNF-α could serve as a potential biomarker for assessing disease severity in IMNM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12865-026-00834-8.

Introduction

Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) is a unique subtype of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIMs) [1–3], typically characterized by rapid and severe disease progression accompanied by markedly elevated serum creatine kinase (CK) levels. Histopathologically, it is defined by prominent muscle fiber necrosis with relatively mild inflammatory cell infiltration. Based on autoantibody profiles, IMNM is classified into three major subtypes: anti-signal recognition particle antibody-positive IMNM (SRP-IMNM), anti-3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase antibody-positive IMNM (HMGCR-IMNM), and seronegative IMNM. Patients with SRP-IMNM generally present with more profound muscle weakness and higher CK levels, whereas those with HMGCR-IMNM are more likely to exhibit inflammatory infiltration [4]. Without timely and effective treatment, IMNM can lead to severe and persistent muscle damage [5].

Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) is a pleiotropic proinflammatory cytokine involved in the regulation of diverse pathophysiological processes, including immune responses, inflammation, and apoptosis [6]. Its role has been extensively investigated in various inflammatory diseases [7–11]. Additionally, TNF-α has been implicated in the pathogenesis of multiple myopathies [12, 13].While persistent and excessive TNF-α signaling contributes to muscle wasting and inflammation, transient and mild expression of TNF-α may promote satellite cell activation and muscle injury repair [14].

TNF-α is closely associated with IIMs [12, 13]. Its levels have been shown to correlate with disease activity in patients with dermatomyositis and with the presence of interstitial lung disease (ILD) [15]. Preuße et al. reported that TNF-α expression is upregulated in the muscle tissue of IMNM patients, and that an active Th1-M1 immune response—triggered by IFN-γ stimulation of CD4 + T cells—leads to TNF-α production by effector cells such as macrophages [16]. However, subsequent studies have shown that serum TNF-α levels do not significantly differ between IMNM and other IIM subtypes, including inclusion body myositis, dermatomyositis, and polymyositis [17]. Studies on animal models of IIMs have shown that inhibiting the infiltration of macrophages into the inflammatory site can induce the degradation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reduce the expression of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6 [18]. These findings support the hypothesis that TNF-α may contribute to muscle injury through mechanisms involving M1-type macrophage polarization and subsequent necrotic myopathy. It is worth noting, however, that TNF-α also plays a role in muscle regeneration. Kuru et al. measured TNF-α mRNA and protein levels in muscle tissues from patients with polymyositis, dermatomyositis, and Duchenne muscular dystrophy, and found that TNF-α is expressed in regenerating muscle fibers, but only during a limited differentiation stage of the regenerative process, rather than throughout all phases of muscle regeneration [14]. This temporal specificity further highlights the context-dependent and dual role of TNF-α in both muscle damage and repair.

The objective of this study was to quantify serum TNF-α levels and analyze their correlation with clinical severity in IMNM patients.

Materials and methods

Research subjects and serum sample

A total of 78 patients with IMNM were enrolled at Tongji Hospital between January 2017 and January 2025. All patients met the 2016 clinico-pathologic diagnostic criteria for IMNM revised by the European Neuromuscular Center (ENMC) [19]. Among them, 45 patients were positive for anti-SRP antibodies, 17 were positive for anti-HMGCR antibodies, and 16 were seronegative.

The control group for serum Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) consisted of 15 age- and sex-matched healthy volunteers. All controls met the following criteria: normal serum CK levels; absence of clinical symptoms such as muscle weakness or myalgia; and no abnormalities in rheumatologic indicators. For muscle tissue analysis, muscle biopsies were obtained from 33 of the 78 IMNM patients (18 anti-SRP-positive, 10 anti-HMGCR-positive, and 5 seronegative). The control group for muscle tissue analysis included 11 individuals who presented with mild muscle pain, weakness, or elevated serum CK of unknown cause, but in whom no evidence of primary muscle or neurological diseases was found after comprehensive evaluation. Hematoxylin and eosin staining of muscle biopsies from these controls revealed no signs of muscle necrosis, atrophy, hypertrophy, or degeneration.

Exclusion criteria for all participants included: muscle diseases induced by immune checkpoint inhibitors, infections, drugs, or toxins; cancer-associated myositis; metabolic myopathies; muscular dystrophies; congenital myopathies; and other myopathies related to connective tissue diseases. All participants underwent testing for serum myositis-specific antibodies (MSAs) and myositis-associated antibodies (MAAs), including anti-Mi-2α, -Mi-2β, -TIF1γ, -MDA5, -NXP2, -SAE1, -Ku, -PM-Scl100, -PM-Scl75, -Jo-1, -SRP, -PL-7, -PL-12, -EJ, -OJ, -HMGCR, -cN-1 A, -Ro-52, -Ha, -Ks, -Zo, and ANA. All antibody tests were performed using the German OUMON immunodetection method.

Blood samples from IMNM patients were collected during the acute attack phase prior to immunotherapy. All serum samples were stored at − 80 °C until analysis. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital (TJ-IRB20230708) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians.

Clinical data collection

Demographic and clinical data for patients with IMNM—including age, sex, disease duration, clinical manifestations, laboratory findings (serum CK and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)), and myositis antibody profiles—were collected from the hospital database. Muscle strength was assessed using the manual muscle testing (MMT-8) scale [20], which evaluates eight muscle groups encompassing proximal, distal, and axial muscles, with scores ranging from 0 to 150 [21].

Measurement of serum cytokines

Serum TNF-α levels were measured using a commercially available ELISA kit (Ruixin Biotechnology, Quanzhou, China), and all procedures were performed strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Muscle cells culture

Human myoblasts (Applied Biological Materials, Richmond, BC, Canada) were cultured in DMEM medium (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and gentamicin/chloramphenicol at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. After three days of culture, when the cells exhibited healthy morphology and reached optimal growth conditions, 1 mL of TNF α at a concentration of 50 ng/mL (Proteintech, China) was added to the culture dishes of the experimental group, while the control group received the same volume of fresh culture medium. Following 48 h of co culture, cell viability was assessed using a CCK 8 kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Cell pellets were then collected, lysed in TRIzol reagent, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent RT qPCR analysis.

Muscle biopsies and human myoblast cells reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Muscle tissue and human myoblast samples were thoroughly lysed and homogenized using TRIzol reagent (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). RT-qPCR analysis was performed using primers targeting TNF-α, IP-10, IL-6, MCP-1, RANTES, p65 (nuclear factor kappa B p65), phosphorylated p65 (p-p65), and GAPDH (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase). The primer sequences are listed in Appendix Table 1 of the supplementary materials.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous data are expressed as the median with interquartile range (IQR) or the mean with standard deviation, as appropriate. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk, Anderson–Darling, D’Agostino–Pearson, or Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the unpaired t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, depending on the data distribution. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.01 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). To evaluate the diagnostic performance of serum TNF α, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed by plotting sensitivity against 1 specificity across all possible cut off values. The efficacy of differentiating the disease group from the control group was determined by calculating the area under the curve (AUC). A two-sided p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Elevated serum TNF-α in patients with IMNM correlated with disease severity

Serum TNF-α levels were significantly elevated in IMNM patients compared to healthy controls (mean: 75.06 pg/mL; p = 0.0002) (Table 1; Fig. 1A). To investigate the potential relationship between TNF-α and disease severity, we analyzed correlations between serum TNF-α levels and clinical markers of muscle damage, including CK, LDH, and manual muscle testing (MMT-8) scores. TNF-α levels showed a significant positive correlation with both CK (p = 0.0004) and LDH (p = 0.0137) (Fig. 1B-C). However, no significant correlation was observed between TNF-α levels and MMT-8 scores (Fig. 1D).

The ROC curve analysis demonstrated that serum TNF-α had good performance for distinguishing IMNM patients from healthy controls, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.8234 (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1E). At the optimal cut-off value, TNF-α exhibited a high specificity of 89.19% (Detailed data can be found in Appendix Table 2 of the supplementary materials.)

Serum TNF-α levels in patients with different IMNM subtypes

ELISA analysis revealed that serum TNF-α levels were significantly elevated in both SRP antibody-positive IMNM patients (p = 0.0063) and antibody-negative IMNM patients (p = 0.0005) compared to controls. In contrast, no significant difference in serum TNF-α levels was observed between HMGCR antibody-positive IMNM patients and controls (p = 0.9528). Furthermore, when comparing IMNM subtypes, SRP antibody-positive patients and antibody-negative patients both exhibited significantly higher serum TNF-α levels than those with HMGCR antibody-positive IMNM (p < 0.0001 for both comparisons) (Fig. 2).

In HMGCR antibody-positive IMNM cases, there were no significant differences in TNF-α levels compared to CK, LDH, and MMT(Fig. 3E-G). TNF-α mRNA expression in IMNM muscle tissue and its effects on myoblasts

Consistent with the ELISA findings, TNF-α mRNA expression was significantly elevated in the muscle tissue of patients with IMNM relative to healthy controls (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4A). Subgroup analyses further demonstrated significantly increased TNF-α expression in SRP-positive (p = 0.0438), HMGCR-positive (p < 0.0001), and antibody-negative IMNM patients (p = 0.0102) compared with control subjects (Fig. 4A).

To investigate the direct effects of TNF-α on muscle cells, human myoblast cells were stimulated with TNF-α protein. Cell viability and the expression of downstream factors mRNA, including IP-10, RANTES, MCP-1, IL-6, p65, and phosphorylated p65 (p-p65) were assessed. TNF-α stimulation significantly reduced myoblast viability (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4B). Additionally, TNF-α-treated myocytes showed markedly increased expression of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6 mRNA (p < 0.0001) and the chemokines IP-10 (p = 0.0004) and MCP-1 (p = 0.0039) mRNA. A significant increase in p-p65 mRNA levels (p = 0.0389) was also observed, indicating activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway downstream of TNF-α (Fig. 4C).

Discussion

Our findings indicate that serum TNF α, particularly in anti SRP antibody positive IMNM patients, may serve as a valuable biomarker for assessing disease activity. It may also contribute to the pathogenesis by promoting inflammatory responses and muscle cell damage. Given the particularly strong association observed in the anti SRP positive subgroup, the potential efficacy of anti TNF α therapy in these patients warrants exploration in future studies.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate a correlation between serum TNF α levels and markers of muscle damage in patients with IMNM. Our main findings can be summarized as follows. First, serum TNF α levels were significantly positively correlated with CK and LDH, two established markers of muscle injury, suggesting that TNF α may reflect disease activity or the extent of muscle destruction. However, no significant correlation was observed with MMT 8 scores, indicating that TNF α may be more closely associated with the pathological process of muscle necrosis than with immediate functional status. ROC curve analysis further showed that serum TNF α exhibited good discriminatory ability between IMNM patients and healthy controls, with notably high specificity, supporting its potential utility as an adjunct biomarker for disease activity.

Subgroup analysis revealed that the role of TNF-α varies substantially across IMNM subtypes. In anti-SRP antibody-positive patients, the positive correlation between serum TNF-α and muscle injury markers (CK and LDH) was more pronounced, and TNF-α showed excellent ability to distinguish this subtype from healthy individuals (specificity: 97.62%) as assessed by ROC curve analysis. In contrast, no significant correlations were observed between serum TNF-α levels and CK, LDH, or MMT-8 scores in both antibody-negative and HMGCR antibody-positive subgroups. Consistent across these two subsets, neither muscle injury markers nor functional status was meaningfully associated with circulating TNF-α. These results suggest that TNF-α may play a more central role in the pathogenesis of anti-SRP antibody-positive IMNM, whereas alternative inflammatory pathways may predominate in both antibody-negative and HMGCR antibody-positive cases, thereby highlighting the critical importance of subtype stratification in IMNM research.

We further confirmed that TNF α mRNA expression was upregulated in the muscle tissues of IMNM patients. It is worth noting that qPCR analysis confirmed that the expression of TNF-α mRNA in the muscle tissues of IMNM patients who were positive for SRP and negative for antibodies was significantly increased. These local expression patterns were consistent with our ELISA results. In these two subgroups, the serum TNF-α levels were significantly higher than those in the healthy control group and HMGCR-positive patients. Although HMGCR-positive IMNM also showed an increase in TNF-α mRNA in the muscle tissues, given the very small sample size (n = 4) and the trend of increased serum TNF-α levels but without significant differences in this subgroup, this result should be interpreted with caution. Overall, these observations support the important role of local TNF-α production in the pathogenesis of SRP-positive and antibody-negative IMNM, and indicate that the inflammatory characteristics of TNF-α in HMGCR-positive diseases are different, and its systemic impact is limited. In vitro experiments demonstrated that TNF α directly reduced human myoblast viability and upregulated the expression of multiple pro inflammatory factors, including IP 10, MCP 1, IL 6, and phosphorylated p65. These findings, taken together, provide a chain of evidence linking circulating TNF α levels, local tissue expression, and cellular responses, supporting the hypothesis that TNF α participates in the disease process of IMNM, potentially through activation of inflammatory pathways such as NF κB.

Consistent with our findings, previous studies have shown that TNF α can induce muscle atrophy in vitro [22], and that inhibition of TNF α may improve muscle strength, reduce inflammation, and attenuate apoptosis in injured skeletal muscle [23]. TNF α has also been reported to enhance local inflammatory responses in human skeletal muscle cells by inducing the secretion of IP 10 [24]. Previous studies have shown that human myoblasts can produce IL-6 [25], which serves as an important cofactor for T cell proliferation and cytotoxicity; in our study, TNF α significantly upregulated IL 6 mRNA expression in myoblasts. Ma et al. further demonstrated that IL 6 trans signaling may enhance the inflammatory response by regulating MCP 1 expression in IMNM, and that the IL 6/sIL 6R complex can induce MCP 1 expression in human muscle cells via the STAT3 pathway [26], which aligns with our observations. Collectively, these studies depict a complex inflammatory network in which TNF-α likely plays a role.

Conclusion

This study, through comprehensive serological testing, clinical assessment, and in vitro functional experiments, explored the clinical significance and potential mechanism of TNF-α in IMNM, indicating that TNF-α may serve as a serological biomarker for assessing disease activity of IMNM.
