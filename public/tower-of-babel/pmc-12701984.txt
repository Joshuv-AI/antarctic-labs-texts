Melatonin attenuates atherosclerotic plaque vulnerability through SIRT6-dependent regulation of vascular smooth muscle cells senescence
Authors: Yue Wang, Wenxin Zhao, Leli Zhang, Pengrong Guo, Yi Zou, Zhenbai Qin, Yuan Wang, Xiaofan Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rupture of vulnerable atherosclerotic plaques is a major cause of acute cardiovascular events. Vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) senescence promotes plaque vulnerability by impairing fibrous cap integrity. Although melatonin exhibits atheroprotective potential, its capacity to stabilize plaques by targeting VSMC senescence along with the underlying mechanisms, remains unclear. In this study, a vulnerable plaque model was established in ApoE−/− mice by partial ligation of the left carotid artery combined with a high-fat diet. Melatonin treatment substantially enhanced plaque stability, as indicated by decreased plaque burden, increased fibrous cap thickness, and an elevated collagen-to-lipid ratio. These effects were prevented by the MT membrane receptor antagonist luzindole. Melatonin markedly suppressed VSMC senescence in both plaques and hydrogen peroxide-stimulated VSMCs; this suppression was similarly abolished by luzindole. Transcriptomic analysis identified SIRT6 as the most significantly upregulated sirtuin in response to melatonin treatment. Melatonin upregulated SIRT6 expression in a MT membrane receptor-dependent manner. Notably, VSMC-specific SIRT6 knockdown eliminated the beneficial effects of melatonin on plaque stabilization and VSMC senescence attenuation. Mechanistically, melatonin alleviated oxidative stress by activating the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway-an effect nullified by SIRT6 knockdown or inhibition. This study uncovers a novel mechanism by which melatonin mitigates atherosclerotic plaque vulnerability through inhibition of VSMC senescence. This protective action is mediated via MT membrane receptor-dependent activation of the SIRT6/Nrf2 signaling axis, culminating in reduced oxidative stress.

Introduction

Rupture of vulnerable atherosclerotic plaques is a major cause of acute cardiovascular events, such as myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke, contributing significantly to the rising global burden of cardiovascular mortality [1]. Histopathological and clinical imaging studies have established that vulnerable plaques are typically characterized by a large lipid-rich necrotic core covered by a thin, collagen-poor fibrous cap [2]. The thickness and structural integrity of this fibrous cap are critical determinants of plaque stability. Vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) help maintain plaque stability by secreting extracellular matrix components that strengthen the fibrous cap [3]. However, growing evidence indicates that VSMC senescence—whether replicative or stress-induced—impairs collagen synthesis and promotes a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which fosters a pro-inflammatory microenvironment [4]. These changes contribute to fibrous cap thinning and increase the risk of plaque rupture, underscoring the therapeutic potential of targeting VSMC senescence to stabilize atherosclerotic plaques.

Melatonin (N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine) is a neuroendocrine hormone primarily secreted by the pineal gland and also produced in extrapineal tissues such as the retina, lymphocytes, and cardiovascular system. It exhibits a wide range of biological effects, including the regulation of circadian rhythms, as well as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-aging properties [5]. Increasing evidence suggests that melatonin exerts protective effects on the progression and stabilization of atherosclerotic plaques [6]; however, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Previous studies have reported that melatonin stabilizes vulnerable plaques through multiple pathways, such as modulating macrophage polarization, activating anti-inflammatory signaling, and regulating VSMC phenotypic switching [[7], [8], [9]]. Nevertheless, whether melatonin attenuates plaque instability by mitigating VSMC senescence remains unclear. Although in vitro studies have shown that melatonin alleviates oxidative stress-induced VSMC senescence [10], it is still unknown whether this anti-senescent effect translates into improved plaque stability in vivo. In addition, melatonin is known to exert its pleiotropic functions through both receptor-dependent pathways—involving membrane-bound MT1/MT2 receptors (MTNR1A/MTNR1B) and nuclear retinoid acid-related orphan receptors—and receptor-independent mechanisms [11]. While the anti-atherosclerotic effects of melatonin mediated by nuclear receptors have been reported, the role of MT membrane receptor-dependent signaling in plaque stabilization, particularly in the context of VSMC senescence, is not well defined.

The sirtuin family (SIRT1-SIRT7) comprises a class of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)-dependent class III histone deacetylases, which play a critical role in the regulation of cellular metabolism, DNA repair, oxidative stress, and aging [12]. Notably, SIRT1, SIRT3, and SIRT6 are particularly recognized for their atheroprotective functions, including suppression of macrophage inflammation, amelioration of endothelial dysfunction and attenuation of VSMC senescence [[13], [14], [15]]. Interestingly, several cardiovascular protective effects of melatonin have been linked to sirtuin activation [16,17], indicating that sirtuins may serve as important downstream effectors of melatonin's atheroprotective actions. To elucidate the specific mechanisms underlying melatonin's plaque-stabilizing effects, we performed transcriptomic sequencing on atherosclerotic plaques from a mouse model. This unbiased approach identified SIRT6 as the most significantly upregulated sirtuin following melatonin treatment. Despite this compelling correlation, the functional contribution of SIRT6 activation in this process—as well as the detailed molecular mechanisms connecting melatonin to SIRT6 and the downstream pathways—remains largely unexplored. Oxidative stress is a well-established driver of both cellular senescence and atherosclerosis progression [18,19]. Emerging evidence from other disease models suggests that SIRT6 can deacetylate and activate Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), thereby enhancing the expression of antioxidant genes and reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels [20]. However, whether the SIRT6/Nrf2 pathway contributes to melatonin's anti-senescent effects on VSMCs and plaque stabilization has not been investigated.

Based on existing evidence, we hypothesized that melatonin attenuates atherosclerotic plaque destabilization by mitigating VSMC senescence via a mechanism dependent on MT membrane receptor and SIRT6 signaling. In this study, we investigated the protective effects of melatonin against plaque destabilization and its association with the suppression of VSMC senescence. Furthermore, we examined whether melatonin's protection is mediated through the MT membrane receptor-dependent activation of the SIRT6/Nrf2 signaling pathway. Our work provides a novel insights into targeting VSMC senescence and highlights the potential of melatonin as a promising therapeutic agent for stabilizing vulnerable plaques.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Capital Medical University. Male ApoE−/− mice (four- or eight-weeks-old) were obtained from Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The mice were housed under a 12-h light/dark cycle in a temperature-controlled barrier facility with ad libitum access to food and water.

Adeno-associated virus administration

For VSMC-specific SIRT6 knockdown, four-week-old male ApoE−/− mice received a tail-vein injection of 1 × 1011 viral genomes (vg) of serotype 9 adeno-associated virus (AAV9) vectors diluted in 100 μL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). These AAV9 vectors carried a VSMC-specific SM22α promoter driving either shRNA targeting SIRT6 (sh-SIRT6) or a non-targeting negative control (NC) sequence. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and immunofluorescence in carotid arteries four weeks post-injection. The remaining mice were then used to establish the vulnerable plaque model. All AAV9 viruses were sourced from HanBio, Inc. (Shanghai, China).

Establishment of vulnerable plaque model

The vulnerable plaque model was established in eight-week-old male ApoE−/− mice by partial ligation of the left carotid artery combined with a high-fat diet, as previously reported [21]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized by an intraperitoneal injection of 1 % sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg). The left common carotid artery and its bifurcation were exposed, and all branches except the superior thyroid artery were permanently ligated using 6-0 silk sutures. After surgery, the mice were fed a high-fat/high-cholesterol diet (21 % fat, 0.15 % cholesterol) for eight weeks.

Experimental group assignment

Mice with established carotid plaques were randomly assigned to the following groups: (a) ApoE−/−+ V (vehicle control); (b) ApoE−/−+ Mel (melatonin treatment); (c) ApoE−/−+ Mel + Luz (melatonin and luzindole co-treatment); (d) AAV9-NC + V (negative control virus infection + vehicle control); (e) AAV9-NC + Mel (negative control virus infection + melatonin treatment); (f) AAV9-SIRT6 shRNA + V (SIRT6-knockdown virus infection + vehicle control); (g) AAV9-SIRT6 shRNA + Mel (SIRT6-knockdown virus + melatonin treatment). Melatonin (HY–B0075, MCE, USA) and the melatonin receptor antagonist luzindole (HY-101254, MCE, USA) were first dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; ID9010, Solarbio) and then diluted in a mixture of PEG300 (HY–Y0873, MCE), Tween 80 (HY–Y1891, MCE), and saline to obtain the working solutions. Starting on the day of surgery, drugs or vehicle were administered daily via intraperitoneal injection for eight weeks. Melatonin and luzindole were injected at doses of 20 mg/kg/day and 10 mg/kg/day, respectively, prior to the dark phase. The dosages were selected based on previous literature [22,23].

Ultrasonography monitoring

Eight weeks after surgery, ultrasound imaging of the left carotid artery was performed using a Vevo770 system equipped with a 55-MHz linear array transducer (Visual Sonics, Canada). Anesthetized mice were positioned supine on a heated platform (37 °C). The neck region was shaved, and ultrasound gel was applied. B-mode imaging was employed to measure the intimal-medial thickness (IMT) at maximal and minimal plaque regions. Pulse-wave Doppler mode was used to determine the maximal flow velocity (Vmax) proximal to the stenosis. The eccentricity index (EI) was calculated as (maximal IMT - minimal IMT)/maximal IMT to evaluate the degree of plaque eccentricity.

Tissue collection

After terminal anesthesia, blood was collected via cardiac puncture. The common carotid arteries were isolated after systemic perfusion with ice-cold heparinized saline and photographed. The left carotid arteries were dissected, fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 24 h, and embedded in either paraffin or optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (Sakura, Netherlands).

Histopathology, immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded sections (5 μm thick) were prepared for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to assess general morphology and Masson's trichrome staining to quantify collagen content. Frozen sections (7 μm thick) were used for Oil Red O staining to assess lipid deposition.

For immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence, serial paraffin-embedded sections (5 μm) were deparaffinized in xylene, blocked with 5 % bovine serum albumin(BSA, Sigma, USA), and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against: anti-α-SMA (α-smooth muscle actin; 1:200, ab7817, Abcam, USA), anti-IL-1β (Interleukin 1β; 1:200, 12242, Santa Cruz Biotech, USA), anti-TNF-α (tumor necrosis factor-α; 1:200, ab1793, Abcam, USA), anti-MMP-2 (matrix metalloproteinase-2; 1:200, ab86607, Abcam, USA), anti-MTNR1A (1:200, bs-0027R, Bioss, China), anti-MTNR1B (1:200, bs-0963R, Bioss, China), anti-P16 ARC (1:100, ab51243, Abcam, USA), anti-P21 (1:200, ab188224, Abcam, USA), anti-SIRT6 (1:200, ab191385, Abcam, USA) and anti-Nrf2 (1:200, 12721, Cell Signaling Technology, USA). After rewarming for 30 min, sections for immunofluorescence were incubated with species-appropriate FITC-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, USA) for 1 h, with nuclear counterstaining using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; P0131, Beyotime, China). For immunohistochemistry, sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies, followed by color development with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB; P0202, Beyotime, China) and hematoxylin counterstaining.

All histological assessments were performed on cross-sections from the left common carotid artery proximal to the bifurcation. Three serial sections per mouse from this standardized location were analyzed, and quantitative data represent the mean of these replicates. All images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX41, Japan) and analyzed with ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

In situ senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining

In vivo SA-β-Gal staining was performed using a Senescence Detection Kit (ab65351, Abcam, China). Cryosections were fixed in 1X Fixative Solution for 10 min and then washed with PBS. Subsequently, the sections were incubated overnight at 37 °C in a non-CO2 humidified chamber with a staining solution mix, which contained 20 mg/ml X-gal in DMF, 1X Staining Solution, and 100X Staining Supplement. After PBS rinsing, sections were blocked with 5 % BSA and immunostained with α-SMA antibody (Abcam, ab7817; 1:200) followed by appropriate secondary antibodies. Analysis was performed on cross-sections of the left common carotid artery just before the bifurcation, using three serial sections per mouse. Images were captured using a light microscope (ECLIPSE 90i, Nikon, Japan). The percentage of SA-β-Gal+/α-SMA+ dual-positive cells was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, USA).

In situ dihydroethidium (DHE) staining

ROS levels in the plaque were evaluated using a DHE staining kit (S0064S, Beyotime, China). Fresh frozen sections (7 μm thick) were washed with PBS, incubated with 10 μM DHE for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark, and counterstained with DAPI. Evaluations were performed on cross-sections from the left common carotid artery near the bifurcation, using three serial sections per mouse. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope (BX41 model, Olympus, Japan).

RNA-sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from mouse carotid arteries using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and used to generate a cDNA library. RNA sequencing was then conducted using the Solexa high-throughput sequencing service (Beijing Novogene Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 (|log2FC| ≥1, adjusted p < 0.05).

Molecular docking

The interaction between SIRT6 and Nrf2 was predicted using AlphaFold3 (version 1.0) with default parameters. The sequences of mouse SIRT6 (UniProt ID: P59941) and Nrf2 (UniProt ID: Q60795) were submitted to the server for complex structure prediction. The model with the highest confidence score was selected and visualized using PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (version 3.0, Schrödinger, LLC).

Primary mouse VSMCs isolation and culture

VSMCs were isolated from the thoracic aortas of four-week-old male C57BL/6 mice as described previously [24]. Briefly, aortas were isolated and rinsed in cold calcium and magnesium-free Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (Gibco, USA). After the removal of adipose and connective tissues, the aortas were bisected longitudinally and incubated in collagenase type I (1 mg/mL; 17100017, Gibco, USA) at 37 °C for 15 min. The tissues were then sectioned into ∼1 mm2 pieces and explanted in six-well plates. The explants were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Sigma, USA) supplemented with 20 % fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA), 1 % penicillin/streptomycin, 0.1 % Epidermal Growth Factor (EGF; E9644, Sigma, USA) and Insulin-transferrin-sodium selenite media supplement (ITS Supplement; I1884, Sigma, USA) at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. After 7 days, outgrown VSMCs were passaged and identified by α-SMA immunohistochemistry. Cells were maintained in the same culture medium, which was refreshed every 2–3 days. Experiments were performed using the cells between passages 2 and 6.

Cell treatment and transfection

To induce cellular senescence, VSMCs were treated with 10 μM hydrogen peroxide (H2O2; 7722-84-1, Sigma, USA). After 12 h of H2O2 exposure, the cells were treated with either 10 μM melatonin or vehicle for 72 h. Where indicated, 10 μM luzindole was added to the culture medium 4 h prior to melatonin. To assess the involvement of SIRT6 in cellular senescence, 100 μM OSS_128167 (HY-107454, MCE, USA), a selective SIRT6 inhibitor, was applied 4 h before melatonin or vehicle treatment.

For SIRT6 knockdown, VSMCs were transduced with lentiviral particles carrying either SIRT6-specific or control shRNA (Beijing Likeli Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 50 for 16 h. The medium was then replaced with virus-free medium containing 20 % FBS. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by qRT-PCR and Western blot for SIRT6 expression. Subsequently, cells were treated with either 10 μM H2O2 alone or in combination with 10 μM melatonin for 72 h.

In vitro SA-β-gal staining

SA-β-Gal activity in VSMCs was detected using a senescence detection kit (ab65351, Abcam, China). Cells were plated in 24-well plates at a low density of 2 × 10^4 cells per well and subjected to experimental treatments for 72 h. After PBS washes, cells were fixed using a 1X fixative solution for 10 min and incubated in a staining solution mix for 24 h at 37 °C. After washing with PBS again, cells were preserved in 70 % glycerol. Images were captured from at least five random fields per well under a bright-field microscope, and the percentage of SA-β-Gal-positive cells was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from VSMCs using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA). RNA concentration and purity were measured spectrophotometrically (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg total RNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (RR037A, Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer's specifications. qRT-PCR was conducted to detect the mRNA levels of the target genes using SYBR Green Master Mix (RR820A, Takara, Japan) and specific primers on a LightCycler® 480 System (Roche, Germany). Relative gene expression was calculated via the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method, with GAPDH as the endogenous control. All primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

Western blotting

VSMCs were lysed in RIPA buffer (Applygen, China) containing protease inhibitors (Applygen, China). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay (23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Proteins (30–50 μg per sample) were separated by 10 % SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5 % non-fat milk in Tris Buffered Saline with Tween-20 (TBST; Solarbio, Beijing) for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-p16 ARC (1:2000, ab51243, Abcam, USA), anti-p21 (1:1000, ab188224, Abcam, USA), and anti-SIRT6 (1:1000, ab191385, Abcam, USA), anti-Nrf2 (1:1000, 12721, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) and anti-β-actin (1:1000, GB15003, Servicebio, China). After washing, membranes were incubated with an IRDye-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10000, 926–32211, LI-COR Biosciences, USA) for 1 h. Protein blots were visualized and analyzed using Odyssey infrared imaging system (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, USA).

Measurement of SASP levels

After being subjected to different treatments, culture supernatants of VSMCs were collected. The concentrations of SASP, including IL‐1β, TNF-α and MMP-2, were quantified using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Shanghai Enzyme linked Biotechnology, China) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

In vitro DHE staining

ROS levels in VSMCs were determined using a DHE staining kit (S0064S, Beyotime, China). Cells were seeded in a 24-well plate at a density of 2 × 10^4 cells per well. After 72 h of treatment, cells were incubated with 5 μM DHE at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Fluorescence images were acquired from at least five random fields per well using a fluorescence microscope (Model BX41, Olympus, Japan).

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical data as frequencies (percentages). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were analyzed by an unpaired Student's t-test (for two groups) or one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni's post hoc test (for three or more groups). Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using non-parametric tests. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (Version 9.0, CA, USA), with P < 0.05 indicating a statistically significant difference.

Results

Melatonin enhances stability of vulnerable plaques in ApoE−/− mice via an MT membrane receptor-dependent manner

To elucidate the effect of melatonin on plaque vulnerability in vivo, we established a mouse model of carotid plaques and initiated treatment eight weeks post-surgery (Supplementary Fig. 2). ApoE−/− mice were administered melatonin alone, melatonin combined with luzindole, or vehicle daily for 56 days (Fig. 1A). Micro-ultrasonography analysis demonstrated that IMT, EI and Vmax were significantly reduced following melatonin treatment compared to the vehicle control (Fig. 1B–E). Melatonin also markedly reduced the carotid plaque burden (Fig. 1F). Histological analyses further demonstrated that plaques from melatonin-treated mice displayed a smaller necrotic core, increased fibrous cap thickness, and a higher collagen-to-lipid ratio—each indicative of enhanced plaque stability—relative to the vehicle group (Fig. 1G–I and M). Co-administration of luzindole abolished these protective effects, despite the absence of significant differences in the expression of MT membrane receptors (MTNR1A/MTNR1B) between the melatonin- and vehicle-treated groups (Fig. 1N–P). Melatonin inhibits VSMC senescence in plaques via an MT membrane receptor-dependent manner

To further investigate how melatonin inhibits vulnerable plaque formation by modulating cellular senescence, we first assessed the proportion of senescent VSMCs, as they constitute the primary cellular component of the fibrous cap. Notably, senescent VSMCs accounted for over half of all senescent cells (Supplementary Fig. 3), which directed our research focus towards VSMC senescence. Melatonin administration significantly reduced the percentage of senescent VSMCs within plaques, as determined by SA-β-Gal staining (Fig. 2A–B). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that the expression of cellular senescence markers P16 and P21 was significantly lower in the melatonin-treated group than in the vehicle-treated group (Fig. 2C–F). Moreover, immunohistochemistry revealed that melatonin-treated mice exhibited lower levels of SASP, including IL-1β, TNF-α, and MMP-2 (Fig. 2G–J). Notably, the ameliorative effects of melatonin on VSMC senescence were inhibited by co-treatment with the MT membrane receptor antagonist luzindole. Melatonin attenuated VSMC senescence via an MT membrane receptor-dependent manner in vitro

To further validate the role of melatonin in VSMC senescence, mouse VSMCs were isolated and treated with 10 μM H2O2 to induce senescence. The cells were then incubated with varying concentrations of melatonin (1, 10, 50, and 100 μM) or vehicle. We found that 10 μM melatonin most effectively reduced the proportion of SA-β-Gal-positive cells (Supplementary Fig. 4) and was therefore selected for subsequent experiments. As shown by SA-β-gal staining, the ability of melatonin to decreasing the percentage of senescent VSMCs was blocked by 10 μM luzindole (Fig. 3A–B). Similarly, melatonin effectively counteracted the H2O2-induced upregulation of the senescence markers P16 and P21(Fig. 3C–E) and the increase in SASP factors (Fig. 3F–H); these effects were also abolished by luzindole. Although seven sirtuin family members have been identified, it remains unclear which one contributes most significantly to the melatonin-mediated suppression of vulnerable plaques. To address this, we first performed bulk RNA-seq to analyze the expression of all sirtuin genes in carotid tissues from melatonin-treated mice. Notably, SIRT6 exhibited the most pronounced upregulation among all sirtuins following melatonin treatment (Fig. 4A). In situ immunofluorescence staining further showed that SIRT6 expression in VSMCs was markedly reduced during vulnerable plaque progression (Supplementary Fig. 5). This decrease was significantly reversed by melatonin, whereas co-administration of luzindole blunted this restorative effect (Fig. 4B–C). Furthermore, qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis confirmed that melatonin reversed H2O2-induced downregulation of SIRT6 expression in VSMCs, an effect that was inhibited by luzindole (Fig. 4D–F). The SIRT6 signaling pathway contributed to the effects of melatonin against oxidative stress in vivo and in vitro

Discussion

This study elucidates a novel mechanism by which melatonin attenuates atherosclerotic plaque vulnerability. In a murine model of vulnerable plaques, melatonin administration enhanced plaque stability and inhibited VSMC senescence in an MT membrane receptor-dependent manner. Transcriptomic analysis identified SIRT6 as the most significantly upregulated sirtuin family member following melatonin treatment, and subsequent experiments confirmed that melatonin upregulates SIRT6 expression in VSMCs via membrane receptor signaling. Critically, VSMC-specific genetic knockdown of SIRT6 abolished the beneficial effects of melatonin on both plaque stabilization and the attenuation of VSMC senescence. Mechanistically, we demonstrated that the melatonin–membrane receptor–SIRT6 axis functions by activating Nrf2-mediated antioxidant pathways and reducing ROS levels. Collectively, our findings reveal that melatonin mitigates atherosclerotic plaque vulnerability by suppressing VSMC senescence, at least in part through MT membrane receptor-dependent activation of the SIRT6/Nrf2 pathway.

Our work provides the first comprehensive in vivo evidence that melatonin stabilizes atherosclerotic plaques by inhibiting VSMC senescence. While previous in vitro studies indicated that melatonin alleviates cellular senescence in VSMCs [10], its functional significance in plaque stabilization remained unclear. The present research extends these earlier cellular observations by directly demonstrating that this anti-senescent mechanism translates into improved plaque stability in vivo. VSMC senescence has emerged as a critical contributor to plaque vulnerability through multiple mechanisms, including impaired extracellular matrix synthesis, increased secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators, and the promotion of a local microenvironment conducive to plaque rupture [4,25,26]. In our study, the reduction in senescent VSMC burden—evidenced by decreased SA-β-Gal activity, reduced expression of senescence markers p16 and p21, and suppressed secretion of SASP factor following melatonin treatment—offers a plausible explanation for the observed plaque phenotype improvements, particularly the enhanced fibrous cap integrity. Together, these findings significantly advance our understanding of melatonin's cardioprotective properties by establishing a crucial link between its cellular anti-senescent effects and its impact on plaque architecture, thereby firmly establishing cell senescence as a promising therapeutic target for plaque stabilization strategies.

Previous studies have demonstrated that both MT membrane receptors and nuclear receptors mediate melatonin's protective effects against cardiovascular diseases [7,16,17,27]; however, the specific role of MT membrane receptors in its anti-atherogenic action has remained less defined. Here, we provide new evidence that melatonin-induced stabilization of vulnerable plaques, suppression of cellular senescence, and upregulation of SIRT6 are strictly dependent on MT membrane receptor activation. These results complement earlier work emphasizing nuclear receptor pathways in melatonin-mediated anti-atherosclerotic effects and underscore the importance of membrane-initiated signaling in modulating VSMC senescence. Two MT membrane receptor subtypes, MT1 and MT2, are known to be expressed in vascular tissues [28]. In this study, we used luzindole—a non-selective MT receptor antagonist with 15- to 25-fold higher binding affinity for MT2 over MT1—to block melatonin's effects [29]. Therefore, the protective actions of melatonin observed here are likely mediated predominantly by the MT2 receptor. Nevertheless, the precise receptor subtype involved and their potential functional interplay in vascular protection require further investigation using subtype-selective agents or genetic models.

Among the diverse downstream effectors of melatonin, the sirtuin family has attracted considerable interest [30]. Accumulating evidence supports the involvement of several sirtuins in the pathogenesis and progression of atherosclerosis [[13], [14], [15]]. A key innovative finding from our study is that SIRT6 showed the most pronounced upregulation among all seven sirtuin members in melatonin-treated mice, identifying it as the most responsive sirtuin to melatonin administration. SIRT6 is predominantly localized in the cell nucleus and functions as an NAD+-dependent deacetylase, playing crucial roles in maintaining genomic stability, regulating metabolic homeostasis, and counteracting aging-related processes [31]. Although SIRT6 has been reported to inhibit atherosclerotic development [15,32,33], our study newly establishes its profound beneficial effect on plaque stability. We observed that SIRT6 expression in VSMCs declined during vulnerable plaque progression, and that melatonin restored SIRT6 levels via MT membrane receptor activation. Importantly, VSMC-specific knockdown of SIRT6 abolished melatonin's beneficial effects on both VSMC senescence and plaque stability. The essential role of SIRT6 was further corroborated by both genetic and pharmacological inhibition in VSMCs in vitro. These results establish MT membrane receptor-mediated SIRT6 activation as a critical molecular switch through which melatonin exerts its anti-senescent effects in VSMCs and promotes plaque stabilization. It should be noted, however, that our study does not provide direct evidence of a physical interaction between MT membrane receptors and SIRT6. Prior research indicates that MT membrane receptors, as G protein-coupled receptors, modulate various intracellular signaling cascades—such as cAMP-PKA, cGMP-PKG, and PLC—dependent pathways [[34], [35], [36]]. Interestingly, Kim et al. reported that cAMP activation promotes ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation of SIRT6 via suppression of the Raf-MEK-ERK axis in non-small cell lung cancer cells [37]. These insights suggest that secondary messengers, kinase cascades, and transcription factors may link MT receptor activation to SIRT6 upregulation in VSMCs—a compelling direction for future research.

At the molecular level, we identified the SIRT6/Nrf2/ROS signaling axis as a crucial downstream pathway executing melatonin's anti-senescent effects. Oxidative stress is a fundamental driver of cellular senescence [38], and Nrf2 serves as the master regulator of cellular antioxidant responses by controlling the expression of key detoxifying and antioxidant enzymes [39,40]. Our data demonstrate that melatonin, through MT membrane receptor activation and subsequent SIRT6 upregulation, enhances Nrf2 activity and reduces ROS accumulation in VSMCs. The absence of this antioxidant effect in SIRT6-knockdown mice and VSMCs strongly suggests that SIRT6 is indispensable for melatonin-mediated Nrf2 activation. Several mechanistic possibilities may explain how SIRT6 regulates Nrf2 signaling. First, as a deacetylase, SIRT6 may directly deacetylate Nrf2, potentially enhancing its transcriptional activity or promoting its nuclear retention [41]. Second, SIRT6 might influence Nrf2 signaling through epigenetic mechanisms, such as by deacetylating histone H3K9 at the promoters of Nrf2-target genes, thereby facilitating their transcription [42]. Third, SIRT6 could indirectly regulate Nrf2 by modulating the expression or activity of its negative regulator Keap1 [43]. Our molecular docking analysis provides structural evidence supporting a potential direct interaction between SIRT6 and Nrf2, offering an intriguing mechanistic hint for their functional relationship. However, the exact nature of this regulation—whether through direct deacetylation, epigenetic modification, or other indirect mechanisms—requires further experimental validation.

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, while we focused primarily on VSMC senescence, melatonin likely also regulates other cellular components within atherosclerotic plaques, including endothelial cells and macrophages. Notably, the potential crosstalk between these diverse cell types, as well as their collective role in melatonin-mediated plaque stabilization, remain inadequately understood. Second, although we identified SIRT6 as a key mediator, melatonin's cardioprotective effects are known to involve other sirtuins, notably SIRT1 and SIRT3 [16,17,44,45]. While preliminary evidence indicates a potential functional interaction between the SIRT6 and SIRT1/SIRT3 pathways [46,47], their crosstalk in modulating melatonin's impact on plaque vulnerability require further elucidation. Third, the relative contributions of different MT membrane receptor subtypes to melatonin's therapeutic effects on vulnerable plaques have not been fully delineated. Future studies using subtype-selective pharmacological tools or receptor-specific knockout models are needed to address this question systematically. Fourth, the molecular signaling cascade linking MT receptor activation to SIRT6 upregulation, as well as the precise biochemical mechanism by which SIRT6 regulates Nrf2 activity, requires further characterization. Finally, future studies examining the therapeutic efficacy of melatonin in more complex animal models and, ultimately, in human subjects will be essential for translating these preclinical findings into clinical practice.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings demonstrate that melatonin significantly attenuates atherosclerotic plaque vulnerability by suppressing VSMC senescence. The MT receptor-dependent activation of SIRT6 and its downstream Nrf2/ROS signaling pathway played a pivotal role in this process (Fig. 10).
