Factors associated with student satisfaction in a chiropractic program at a South African university: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Sharné Naidoo, Justin Paul Brown
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify factors associated with student satisfaction in a South African chiropractic program and to determine how these factors related to overall student satisfaction. Comparisons between the year of study and overall satisfaction were also investigated.MethodsA cross-sectional, quantitative study design was adopted. Data were collected via an online survey distributed to chiropractic students in undergraduate and postgraduate programs (n = 242). The adapted questionnaire rated satisfaction factors on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = completely dissatisfied to 5 = completely satisfied). Data analysis included frequency counts, descriptive statistics, exploratory factor analysis, and reliability analyses, along with normality and comparison analyses. Regression analysis determined the relationship between overall satisfaction and the identified factors.ResultsA 50.83% response rate was achieved (n = 123). The exploratory factor analysis yielded 3 key factors: course, teaching and evaluation, and clinical training, as well as career guidance. Respondents expressed satisfaction with the course delivery and clinical training (median = 4.00) but were neutral regarding career guidance (median = 3.00). Course, Teaching and Evaluation Methods (P = .77), Clinical Training (P = .59), and Career Exploration After Graduation (P = .49) were statistically significant with General Satisfaction. All factors were positively associated with overall satisfaction, which was satisfactory (median = 4.00).ConclusionsOverall, students’ satisfaction with the chiropractic program was high across the course, teaching and evaluation methods, and clinical training. Improvements are required in the career guidance domain, and student satisfaction declined between the academic years.

INTRODUCTION

Student satisfaction in higher education is crucial for their academic performance and subsequent success in the workplace. Additionally, student satisfaction affects their ability to grasp concepts and master their skills.1 There is increased uptake of these skills, knowledge, and values among students who are more satisfied with their academic program.2

Students come from a variety of backgrounds and learning styles, which may be linked to how they view teaching and learning in their chosen discipline. However, factors within the higher education program are controlled by the curriculum design and delivery, thereby affecting students’ levels of satisfaction.3 This can include aspects such as teaching strategies, the structure of the content in each module, and the competencies that students feel they gain during their academic journey.3 Clinical training, an essential component in health sciences programs, can be related to student satisfaction and is linked to their overall professional competencies.4 The practical component exposes students to real-life scenarios they may encounter in practice upon graduation.5,6 Vocational guidance in higher education is an avenue for students’ personal and professional development.7 Furthermore, early experience within the health care environment can influence career exploration opportunities.8 Health care professions, including chiropractic, rely on higher education institutions to develop competent graduates, and this competency may be shaped by students’ level of satisfaction with their educational experience.1,3

Chiropractic training programs are offered at over 50 institutions and is increasing globally.9–11 These programs encompass both theoretical and practical components, as chiropractors are health care providers who diagnose, treat, and prevent musculoskeletal conditions using manual therapies.10,12 The competencies and structure of the programs are based on accreditation from the region’s respective accreditation bodies and the legal scope of practice.9,13–15 This is based on the competencies outlined in national education standards upon graduation.16 Globally, this can be defined differently among institutions.9,15

In South Africa, chiropractic programs are accredited by the Allied Health Professions Council of South Africa (AHPCSA), the statutory body for complementary and alternative medicine professions, and chiropractors are registered with it. International accreditation is awarded by the European Council on Chiropractic Education (ECCE), as there are only 2 institutions in Africa that offer the chiropractic program, both in South Africa.9,15,17 The chiropractic program in South Africa consists of a 4-year Bachelor of Health Sciences and a 2-year Master of Health Science degree. The AHPCSA requires all registered chiropractors to hold a Master’s qualification as a minimum.13

Understanding student satisfaction is essential for enhancing the quality and effectiveness of higher education programs. Insights into student satisfaction enable programs and institutions to identify areas where students require improvements in teaching, learning, and assessment approaches; thereby strengthening the overall educational environment.18 Although previous student satisfaction surveys have been conducted at the institutional level at the chosen university, there is currently a gap in program-level evaluation. While accreditation bodies such as the AHPCSA and ECCE conduct program reviews, students remain the central stakeholders in the institutional process.11 Incorporating their perspectives on satisfaction can therefore contribute meaningfully to improving program quality, particularly in how students experience and perceive their education. Moreover, education standards are dynamic and evolve as the educational landscape improves.14 Consequently, the enhancement of educational programs must be viewed as part of an ongoing, continuous quality improvement process, reinforcing the need for studies that systematically explore student satisfaction levels.9,15

Therefore, the aim of this study was to identify the primary factors associated with the satisfaction of South African chiropractic students and to examine how these factors are related to overall student satisfaction. The objective was to compare the year of study with general levels of satisfaction and the factors affecting student satisfaction.

METHODS

Study Design

A cross-sectional, correlational, quantitative study was employed for this research, following the STROBE guidelines for reporting.19

Study Setting

A higher education institution in South Africa was selected as the study setting. The survey (Supplemental File 1) was hosted on an online platform, namely Google Forms (Google LCC). This platform was chosen because it provided easy access to the questionnaire, which could be distributed to participants, and allowed for effective data collation. Data were collected during the first semester of the academic year (March–April 2024).

Participants

Convenient sampling was employed in this study, as the participants were registered at the same institution where the researchers were based. The population consisted of only Bachelor’s and Master’s in Health Science students enrolled in the chiropractic program. Students were included in the study if they were over 18 years old and provided consent to participate. If the students did not meet the aforementioned criteria, they were excluded from participation.

Study Size

At the time of the study, 242 students were enrolled in the undergraduate and postgraduate chiropractic programs at the selected South African higher education institution. Therefore, with a 95% confidence level and 10% margin of error, 69 responses were required. However, to ensure meaningful statistical analysis, a minimum of 100 responses needed to be collected.

Measurement

The online survey link contained the information letter, consent form and questionnaire. The information letter provided details about the study, the benefits and risks of participation, and the researcher’s contact information, should they require further assistance.

If participants provided consent, they had to tick the 3 boxes in the link to proceed to the questionnaire. Participant consent entailed the following: (1) The participant read and understood the information letter; (2) their participation was voluntary, and they could withdraw prior to submission of the questionnaire due to the anonymous nature of the study; (3) they agreed to participate in the study.

The questionnaire was modified from 2 original studies in health care professions education.20,21 The authors adapted the questionnaire in consultation with the institutional statistician to ensure alignment with the chiropractic education context. Adaptations involved modifying terminology and aligning it with the chiropractic program, and refining item wording to ensure relevance and clarity for chiropractic students. Additionally, to support content and face validity, the adapted questionnaire underwent a pilot study with 5 chiropractic students, who were subsequently excluded from data collection. Feedback from the pilot study included clarity of wording and ensured that each item was understandable and appropriate for the chiropractic context.

For ease of use, the questionnaire was separated into 3 distinct sections. Section A collected participants’ socio-demographic characteristics. General satisfaction of the chiropractic program was obtained from 4 items in Section B. Section C measured the factors associated with student satisfaction with 19 items spread across 5 categories (satisfaction within the course, satisfaction with lecturers and teaching methods, satisfaction with evaluation methods and academic performance, satisfaction with clinical and practical training, satisfaction with career exploration after graduation). The satisfaction questions (Sections B and C) were answered based on participants’ opinions and used a 5-point Likert scale measuring satisfaction (1- Completely dissatisfied, 2- somewhat dissatisfied, 3- neither satisfied nor dissatisfied, 4- somewhat satisfied, 5- completely satisfied). The questionnaire took approximately 20 minutes to complete.

Ethics

Ethical approval was received from the University of Johannesburg’s Faculty of Health Sciences Research Ethics Committee (REC-2583-2024). All ethical considerations were adhered to in this study. Participants were informed of the study with the information letter and signed the consent form prior to completing the survey. All information was confidential and anonymous, ensuring participants’ privacy throughout the study. Anonymity was maintained throughout the study, as no personal information was collected. Participants could complete the questionnaire on their own time and on their own mobile devices. There were no direct benefits for the participants as voluntary participation was emphasized.

Data were collected and stored on a password-protected device and in a multifactor-authentication cloud storage service. Data handling was only between the researchers and the statistician involved in the study.

Statistical Analysis

Frequency and descriptive statistics were used to analyze the questionnaire responses, yielding counts, percentages, means, medians, SDs, maximums, and minimums.

To test the items and group them into the same categories, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted. The principal axis factoring method can be applied to the analysis of Likert-scale questions (Sections B and C). To ensure the validity of the analysis, a correlation matrix, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy, Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity, and commonalities were also assessed.

Cronbach alpha was used to assess the internal consistency of the questionnaire items and determine if they measured the same thing consistently. Normality and subscales were used to compare the different demographics in Section C factors and subscales. For normality testing, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (undergraduate) and Shapiro-Wilk (postgraduate) tests were performed, given the group size by year of study. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the groups, as the data were not normally distributed.

Regression analysis was performed using the 3 factors (Course, Teaching and Evaluation Methods; Clinical Training and Career Exploration After Graduation) from Section C, along with the year of study, to predict General Satisfaction (Section B), which was the dependent variable. An outlier was identified in the analysis and subsequently removed from the standardized residual analysis so that the data were normally distributed for the correlational analysis.

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)

Section B: Factor 1 - General Satisfaction

All the factors had correlation coefficients above 0.3 in the correlation matrix, indicating meaningful correlations. Items B2/B3 were 0.28, but most items were above the threshold; therefore, the factor analysis was still conducted. The KMO for General Satisfaction yielded a measure of sampling adequacy (MSA) of 0.73, indicating that all items were suitable for this factor. The Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity had a P-value of 0.00, demonstrating a significant correlation between the factors. The anti-image matrix for MSA was above the recommended 0.6 threshold, indicating strong items. For the communalities, B2 was 0.29, which is slightly below the recommended value of 0.3. This item was deemed strong in the MSA and was therefore not excluded from the analysis. One factor was extracted from the total variance of the general satisfaction, with an eigenvalue of 2.58 and a total cumulative percentage of 64.59 (Supplemental File Fig. 1). Since only one factor was extracted, the rotated factor matrix could not be computed. From the factor matrix, all items had loadings over 0.4; therefore, they all measured general satisfaction (Supplemental File Table 2).

Section C: Factors Associated with Satisfaction

For the communalities, items C3 and C16 were omitted from the analysis because their threshold values were below 0.3 (0.25 and 0.27, respectively). Conceptually, item C3 reflects disciplinary knowledge rather than focusing on the course's teaching and learning. Additionally, item C16 differed from the other career-related items as it focused on professional identity rather than institutional or departmental support. As a result, only these 2 items were omitted from the exploratory factor analysis. The remaining communalities ranged from 0.39 to 0.88.

The retained items in this section had values greater than 0.3 in the correlation matrix, indicating that the factors could be analyzed. Exploratory factor analysis was conducted using principal axis factoring, as the data were ordinal and not normally distributed. KMO was a high value of 0.9, and the Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity had a p-value of 0.00, indicating a significant correlation among the factors. All the MSA items were above 0.75.

Factor retention was guided by eigenvalues greater than 1 and was inspected on the scree plot. The total variance for the Factors that affect Satisfaction suggested 3 distinct factors from the items in the section (Supplemental File Fig. 2). Although parallel analysis was not conducted, these criteria and the conceptual coherence of the factor structure supported factor retention. To aid interpretability, a Varimax rotation with Kaiser normalization was applied. The full rotated factor matrix grouped the items into the most suited of the 3 factors, with no problematic cross-loading (Supplemental File Table 3). Nine items (C1, 2, 4–10) were grouped in Factor 1, which was focused on the Course, Teaching and Evaluation Methods. Factor 2 consisted of 4 items (C11–14) that were based on the Clinician Training. The last factor comprised only 2 items (C15 and C17) specific to Career Exploration after Graduation.

Reliability on Factors

All 4 factors identified in the EFA showed good internal consistency: General satisfaction = 0.808; Course, Teaching and Evaluation Methods = 0.892; Clinical Training = 0.902; and Career Exploration After Graduation = 0.899.

Descriptives on Factors

It was noted that the Career Exploration After Graduation was neutral in terms of satisfaction compared to the other factors, with a median of 3 (M = 3.01, SD = 1.14). General satisfaction (M = 3.75, SD = 0.88), Course, Teaching and Evaluation Methods (M = 3.83, SD = 0.74), and Clinical training (M = 3.88, SD = 0.88) were all indicated as somewhat satisfied factors, with a median of 4.

Comparisons between Undergraduate and Postgraduate

From the nonparametric testing, Table 3 shows the mean per academic group.

Statistical significance was indicated with 2 factors between the undergraduate and postgraduate groups: General Satisfaction (P = .002) and Career Exploration After Graduation (P = .000). Postgraduate students reported significantly smaller to moderately lower general satisfaction compared to their undergraduate counterparts (U = 1166, r = 0.28). Additionally, postgraduate students also reported lower satisfaction with career exploration compared to the undergraduate students, with a moderate to large difference between the groups (U = 915, r = 0.40).

Regression Analysis

A multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the combined association between the Academic Group, Course, Teaching and Evaluation Methods, Clinical Training and Career Exploration After Graduation on General Satisfaction (Supplemental File Table 4). The regression model was statistically significant (F (4,117) = 55.209, P < .001) (Supplemental File Table 5), explaining approximately 65% of the variance in satisfaction (R2 = 0.65, adjusted R2 = 0.64) (Supplemental File Table 6).

Scatterplots indicated a positive linear relationship between Course, Teaching and Evaluation Methods and General Satisfaction (Supplemental File Fig. 3), with Clinical Training and General Satisfaction having a positive relationship with variations in the middle and lower ranges (Supplemental File Fig. 4). Career Exploration After Graduation indicated a weaker positive association with General Satisfaction (Supplemental File Fig. 5). Lastly, the Academic Group and General Satisfaction show a weaker negative relationship (Supplemental File Fig. 6).

Bivariate correlation analysis supports these findings. Academic Group showed a negative correlation with general satisfaction (r = −0.28, P = .001), while Course, Teaching, and Evaluation Methods (r = 0.77, P < .001) and Clinical Training (r = 0.59, P < .001) showed strong correlations with the dependent variable. There was a moderate correlation between Career Exploration After Graduation and General Satisfaction (r = 0.49, P < .001).

DISCUSSION

The purpose of this study was to identify the main factors associated with student satisfaction in a chiropractic program at a South African University. From the findings, 3 key factors were identified, namely: (1) Course, Teaching and Evaluation Methods; (2) Clinical Training; and lastly, (3) Career Exploration After Graduation. Overall, these 3 factors showed positive correlations with the program's overall satisfaction, as indicated by responses from the chiropractic students. However, the results suggest that student satisfaction decreases among the academic student cohorts. Nonetheless, no correlation was done between the year of study and each factor to determine if there was a relationship between these variables.

The first factor identified from this study was the course, teaching and evaluation methods. This factor had a strong positive correlation with general satisfaction. A study in Australia supports this, as student satisfaction was linked to the quality of the course, teaching and assessment strategies.22 Similar to the results of this study, it was also found that the course, teaching, and evaluation methods were the most influential factors in students’ overall satisfaction.22 As of 2020, the chiropractic program at this particular university had re-curriculated from a Bachelor’s in Technology (BTech) and Master’s in Technology (MTech) to a Bachelor’s in Health Science (BHSc) and Master’s in Health Science (MHSc) in Chiropractic. This was not only a change in the qualification's nomenclature but also a rearrangement of the curriculum and its outcomes. Students were introduced to chiropractic spinal assessment and manipulation from their second year of study, which had previously been introduced in the third year of the old qualification. Another contributing factor to satisfaction with the course, teaching, and evaluation methods is the evolution of teaching methods since the COVID-19 pandemic, which has led to an increased emphasis on online, blended, and hybrid approaches rather than traditional face-to-face/didactic methods.23,24 Blended learning was found to increase student satisfaction.25 Students tend to lean towards the digital or technological aspects of learning, as they make it easier for them to access and understand large volumes of content.26 The quality of education has thus improved with innovative teaching methods that make it engaging for students, encouraging them to become active learners in the classroom and grasp concepts and knowledge.27 This becomes apparent through the use of discussion forums, peer learning, and interactive activities. Assessment fairness and outcomes are readily available to students, allowing them to understand how they will be evaluated and graded. This approach could help boost academic performance, manage student expectations for their modules, and improve the overall perception of the chiropractic program.

Clinical training was identified as the second factor. This factor shows a positive relationship with participants' overall satisfaction; however, some participants reported low to moderate satisfaction with this aspect. Clinical training is mainly focused on the final year of the BHSc program and the 2 years of the MHSc qualification. In the BHSc program, students have basic interactions with patients by observing consultations conducted by the Master’s students. This provides them with the soft skills and operational knowledge necessary for consultations and working in the Chiropractic Clinic, in preparation for their Master’s registration and subsequent qualification. In another African study involving physiotherapy students, the respondents reported low satisfaction with their clinical training, which was linked to a lack of information, year of study, and the institution's infrastructure.21 An Ethiopian study found that student satisfaction with clinical training is based on perceptions of academic selves, the university and the program’s curriculum. Moreover, structured clinical training plays a critical role in health sciences education.23,28 Clinical training provides students with the initial career experience they need, integrating theoretical knowledge with the practical application of the specific discipline and laying the foundation for entry into professional work.28 Although participants had positive views of the clinical training in the chiropractic program, further investigation is required to identify areas of improvement to increase student satisfaction.

Career exploration after graduation was the last factor identified from the questionnaire. In contrast to other factors, satisfaction with career exploration after graduation was relatively low. This dissatisfaction was more pronounced among postgraduate students, reflecting a potential gap in career guidance services. These findings align with 2 studies that identified insufficient career support as a key factor contributing to student dissatisfaction in health sciences programs.29,30 Dissatisfaction with career guidance at the postgraduate level can hinder students’ motivation to find employment or explore entrepreneurial opportunities, particularly in professions such as chiropractic. In South Africa, chiropractors only work in the private sector and are required to open a private practice or seek employment as an associate or locum.31 Uncertainty with career options after graduation may leave students unemployed once they complete their academic requirements, and delay their professional growth while they learn and discover which professional path to take. It is recommended that chiropractic programs include curriculum or courses on chiropractic-specific practice management and equip students with the knowledge and skills to thrive in the professional workforce upon graduation.

General Satisfaction within the chiropractic student body at the South African University was high. This correlates with a study on Italian students, which found a high level of satisfaction.32 There is an inverse relationship between satisfaction and academic years.32 When comparing satisfaction levels, undergraduate students reported significantly higher satisfaction across most factors compared to postgraduates. The authors are of the opinion that these differences suggest that postgraduate students may have higher expectations or face more complex academic and career-related challenges, which can lead to lower satisfaction levels. Similar trends have been observed in other studies, highlighting the need for targeted support for postgraduate students.33,34

Future Studies

A longitudinal study tracking student satisfaction over multiple years of study could provide deeper insights into how satisfaction evolves and identify critical periods when interventions may be most beneficial. Conducting this study across both chiropractic programs in South Africa may provide a comprehensive overview of student satisfaction within the country and yield a generalized satisfaction level. Socio-economic factors can be included in the analysis, as they may affect experiences and perceptions of the chiropractic program.

Additionally, this study highlights that student satisfaction is multi-factorial and can be related to the overall satisfaction with the educational program. Future studies can be conducted at other institutions by using the identified factors from this study as a theoretical underpinning to test for similar relationships within their educational environments. The findings can be relevant for institutional improvement and quality assurance with programs of a similar nature.

Strengths

This study is the first to explore the student satisfaction levels in a South African chiropractic program. The findings provide context-specific insights to establish an understanding of factors associated with student satisfaction. The outcomes can drive program-specific improvements that inform future institutional, faculty, and departmental initiatives aimed at enhancing student experiences.

Limitations

While this study offers valuable insights into student satisfaction within the chiropractic program at this particular institution, several limitations were noted. The response rate of 50.83%, although sufficient for statistical analysis, may not fully capture the diversity of student perspectives, and a higher response rate could have yielded more representative results (reducing nonresponse bias). Additionally, as the sample was limited to chiropractic students at one institution and used convenience sampling, the findings may not be generalizable to the broader chiropractic student population nationally or internationally (sampling bias). The noticeable sex disparity, with a predominance of female respondents, further limits the study, as it may not accurately reflect the experiences of male students, restricting the ability to assess satisfaction across different demographic groups. Furthermore, although the questionnaire was adapted from previously published instruments and refined through expert consultation and pilot testing, it did not undergo comprehensive psychometric validation within the target population. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

CONCLUSIONS

This study identified 3 primary factors associated with overall student satisfaction within a South African chiropractic program. Students reported satisfactory experience with course content, teaching and evaluation methods, and clinical training. Career guidance emerged as an area that requires improvement. Additionally, students in advanced years of study tended to report lower levels of general satisfaction, indicating a negative association between these factors.
