Psychologists should study basic social cognition processes within the context of sexual interactions
Authors: Noam Markovitch, Anat Perry, Tali Kleiman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractResearch in psychology has long underscored the significance of contextual influence on social cognition processes and behavior. However, the exploration of sexual interactions as a unique context affecting these processes has largely been neglected by previous research, despite their prominent role in our daily lives and potentially consequential outcomes. We outline the relevance of various basic social cognition processes to sexual interactions and propose a line of research integrating theoretical insights and methodologies from social cognition research with those from sex and relationship research. We elaborate on theoretical and applied contributions to both fields.Subject terms: Human behaviour, Society

Introduction

At the end of 2017, a short story, “Cat Person”1, published in The New Yorker, depicted the thoughts and feelings of a young woman, Margot, who goes out on a date with a male partner, Robert, and ends up having sex with him. The story further described how Margot decides to have sex with Robert despite not wanting to (“and she knew that her last chance of enjoying this encounter had disappeared, but that she would carry through with it until it was over”). The story quickly went viral as it provided a unique insight into the potentially complex individual experience as a sexual interaction unfolds. Moreover, the story shone a rare spotlight on the idea that even consensual sex can result in a negative experience. A research psychologist reading through this portrayal of a sexual interaction could have easily identified numerous basic social cognition processes that are operating throughout it. They could have, for example, recognized that Margot is ambivalent about the interaction and that she is experiencing an internal conflict between two courses of action (“she felt a twinge of desire pluck at her belly… But the thought of what it would take to stop what she had set in motion was overwhelming”). They could have also seen that Margot is motivated to understand her partner’s thoughts and feelings (“she saw that Robert was watching her closely, observing the impression the room had made”). Notably, it could also be evident that the context that Margot and Robert find themselves in, that of a sexual interaction, is characterized by specific features, such as sexual arousal, limited time to make judgments and decisions, and use of mainly nonverbal cues to communicate.

In this paper, we advocate for a line of research focused on studying basic social cognition processes within the context of sexual interactions (Fig. 1). We argue that integrating theoretical concepts and methodologies from social cognition research with those from sex and relationship research can result in theoretical insights and important applied contributions to both fields (for a related claim see ref. 2). By examining the operation of social cognition processes within the context of sexual interactions, we may uncover overlooked mechanisms underlying the dynamics of sexual interactions and their role in both positive and negative psychological outcomes. Furthermore, exploring the contextual influences of sexual interactions on basic social cognition processes can provide insights into the similarities and differences in the operation of these processes across different social contexts. On the applied front, research on social cognition processes within the context of sexual interactions can aid in promoting sexual well-being specifically and life satisfaction more generally3. Importantly, such research can help in the understanding of how negative outcomes in sexual interactions can be reduced. We strongly emphasize that recognizing the role of social cognition processes in the negative outcomes of sexual interactions in no way justifies violence, coercion, or any other actions leading to nonconsensual sex. Instead, this recognition is a critical step toward developing empirically-based tools to reduce negative outcomes in sexual interactions.

Our proposed line of research integrates theoretical insights and methodologies from social cognition research with those from sex and relationship research.

Social cognition processes can have a role in shaping sexual interactions and their psychological outcomes. Understanding these effects can contribute to the body of sex and relationship research. In addition, the unique features of the context of sexual interactions can have an influence on social cognition processes. Examining these effects can help bring insights into social cognition research, in elucidating the processes and understanding whether they are domain-specific or rather domain-general across social contexts.

It is important to note that whereas nonconsensual sex is an extremely important issue in its own right, throughout this paper we only refer to consensual sexual interactions; For the purpose of this piece, we define sexual consent as a freely given verbal or nonverbal communication of the conscious willingness to engage in sexual activity4. Consensual sexual interactions can be wanted, unwanted, or ambivalent. Wanted consensual sex refers to sexual interactions that are based on an inner desire or interest in the sexual interaction5,6. In contrast, we define unwanted consensual sex as sexual interactions where an individual communicates agreement or willingness to participate in a sexual act without an inner desire or interest in it5,6. Ambivalent consensual sex refers to sexual interactions with uncertainty regarding one’s own wishes concerning the sexual interaction.

Sexual interactions occur across different types of relationships such as those with strangers, new dating partners, or long-term committed partners, and between partners of the same or different sex and/or gender. Some sexual interactions, such as unwanted or ambivalent ones, and with strangers or new partners, may increase the probability for negative outcomes. In addition, it is typically found that it is disproportionately women who are vulnerable to such negative experiences7–10. As such, many of the examples we provide focus on these types of interactions and relationships. Although the psychological outcomes may vary, we believe that the basic social cognition processes discussed apply to all types of sexual interactions, relationships, genders/sexes, and sexual orientations, regardless of their specific context.

Sexual interactions as a unique context in studying social cognition processes

Research in psychology has long underscored the significance of contextual influences on psychological processes and behavior11. Contextual influences are abundant and relate to factors such as available resources12–15, the mere presence of others16, the presence of a person from another gender17, ingroup status18,19, and situational information20,21, to name just a few. Contextual factors affect a range of processes spanning from low-level perception22,23, through higher-level functions such as social cognition and judgments14,19,24–26, to complex social behaviors27–30. Though every context affects psychological processes and behavior to some extent, some contexts might have a stronger, or a unique, influence and more consequential outcomes. Here, we suggest that one such context is the sexual context.

Sexual interactions are an integral part of human daily life, motivated by various basic needs, from physical drives to the need for love and belonging31,32. Sexual interactions inherently involve interaction with another person, and so they may be considered a form of social interaction, and thus share some basic features with other types of interpersonal social interactions. However, compared to other forms of social interactions, the sexual context is unique as it is driven by a strong evolutionary need for reproduction, supported by a biological system for sexuality that is distinct from other systems (e.g., the attachment system)33. Moreover, and especially important when considering social cognition, sexual interactions involve several strong situational factors that are simultaneously active. First, sexual arousal is often an inherent part of sexual interactions, which can affect the availability of mental resources. Second, in sexual interactions people often rely on nonverbal and indirect cues34–37; yet, sexual interactions necessitate a high level of communication, thereby amplifying the importance of perceptual precision. Third, processes like social perception, judgment, and decision-making are all operating under time constraints. Fourth, sexual interactions are continuously evolving by nature, and thus require individuals to repeatedly and frequently update the assessment of the situation as it unfolds, creating an ongoing decision-making process38. Lastly, sexual interactions can be high-stakes situations, as they can significantly improve well-being, on the one hand, and hold the potential for grave psychological and social outcomes, on the other. Furthermore, the highly dynamic nature of sexual interactions can shift experiences swiftly from positive to negative and vice versa. While some of these features may overlap with other interpersonal contexts (e.g., conflict resolution between partners or joint decision-making in parenting), the intensity and simultaneous presence of these features in sexual interactions sets them apart from other interpersonal contexts.

Despite their prevalence and importance, their unique characteristics, and the potential gravity of their outcomes, interpersonal sexual interactions have rarely been studied as a context affecting basic social cognition processes (with notable exceptions39–44). Some studies have applied social cognition concepts to sexual content to gain insights into sexual processes. For instance, the concepts of implicit attitudes and beliefs have been used to study romantic partner evaluations45 and sexual desire46,47 (e.g., assessing implicit attitudes related to general evaluations or sexual desire directed specifically at one’s romantic partner); the concepts of approach and avoidance goals have been borrowed to examine why individuals engage in sex with their partners (e.g., to feel closer versus to avoid upsetting a partner)48; and the concept of bias has been applied to study perceptions of romantic partners’ sexual advances and sexual rejection (e.g., the accuracy in perceiving daily sexual rejection by one’s partner)49,50. This approach is important for understanding sexual processes, and these studies demonstrate how social cognition concepts can be used to investigate processes specific to the sexual domain. However, our focus within the interplay between the two fields of research is different in several ways. First, our focus is on how the sexual context influences not only sex-specific interactions but also basic social cognition processes that are not sex-specific and apply across various social interactions (e.g., general emotion perception rather than perceiving a specific partner’s emotions within a relationship). Second, we suggest a feedback loop between the sexual context and social cognition, such that the sexual context can shape social cognition processes operating within it, and these processes, in turn, can influence the unfolding of the highly dynamic interaction itself and its psychological outcomes. The unfolding interaction can then further impact the social cognition processes (Fig. 1). Finally, we believe that by integrating the existing literature on social cognition in the sexual context together with the dynamic perspective we propose, and the introduction of overlooked psychological concepts and interdisciplinary methods, we can advance a comprehensive understanding of the dynamic interplay between social cognition and sexual interactions.

In the next sections, we elaborate on the components comprising our argument, we review some of the pertinent social cognition processes and explore how they may be influenced by sexual interactions, as well as how they might affect the unfolding of such interactions.

Social cognition processes in the context of sexual interactions

In what follows, we review several different social cognition processes that we believe operate within sexual interactions, organized under three main themes: Arousal, Internal Conflict, and Understanding Others. We believe that these processes are prominent and important features of sexual interactions, and thus discuss them in this context, with an emphasis on how they might play a role in the unfolding of sexual interactions and their psychological outcomes, as well as how they might be affected by the contextual characteristics. We also mention other concepts and psychological constructs that are related to these three main processes and are of relevance in the context of sexual interactions. Table 1 consists of a short explanation of each construct, as well as references to research demonstrating ways in which the construct can be operationalized (in some cases beyond the scope of sex research) for each of these concepts and processes. Note that the processes we discuss are often intertwined, as we try to highlight throughout the paper. Importantly, we do not presume to cover here all the relevant social cognition processes, or all possible sexual experiences. Instead, we wish to inspire psychology researchers by providing several examples that attest to the relevance of experimental research on social cognition processes to the understanding of the dynamics of sexual interactions, and the potential influences of the sexual context on the processes themselves.

(Sexual) arousal

Sexual arousal is “an appraisal of the dynamic integration of psychological (e.g., cognition, emotion, perception) and both generalized and sex-specific physiological arousal responses”51. Previous research on sexual arousal has mainly focused on its role in certain behaviors such as coercive behavior52–55 or on its effects on straightforward sexual behaviors such as practicing safe sex41,52,56,57 and facilitating satisfying sexual experiences58,59. However, research on whether and how sexual arousal affects more basic social cognition processes, namely, individuals’ cognitions, emotions, and behaviors during consensual sexual interactions, is scarce (with a few important exceptions; see below). Nonetheless, to the extent that sexual arousal shares some mechanistic and procedural properties with, for example, physiological arousal and affective arousal, research on sexual arousal can be informed by research on other forms of arousal60–62.

Recall that in the “Cat Person” story1, Margot feels flickers of sexual arousal while needing to decipher whether she is interested in the unfolding sexual interaction or not. Sexual arousal during a sexual interaction might make the task of understanding one’s own wishes especially difficult. Indeed, a hallmark theory of emotions suggests that context can influence how physiological arousal is interpreted63. That is, different types of arousal are often misattributed to a range of different emotions and experiences, depending on the context. For example, sexual arousal might be attributed as emotional arousal (e.g., stress) given certain contextual cues, and emotional arousal (e.g., anxiety) can be interpreted as sexual arousal given other contextual cues64. This can make it harder for the individual to figure out what they want, which in turn can increase ambivalence. Also of relevance is research suggesting that when an individual makes decisions, physiological arousal increases “herding” tendencies (i.e., one’s tendency to choose a more popular option65). Thus, if one believes, for instance, that most people would consent to a sexual interaction once it has begun, when aroused one might choose to do so, despite having ambivalent thoughts.

Furthermore, abundant research has shown that both physiological and affective arousal affect various cognitive functions and judgments (for meta-analyses and reviews see refs. 60–62). Similarly, sexual arousal was found to affect several executive and related functions, such as working memory capacity42, inhibition39,43, and risk-taking41. Thus, sexual arousal might be a source of cognitive load. Another key process that may be affected by the unfolding of the sexual interaction is social perception. Research suggests that arousal induces biases in the perception and judgment of facial expressions. For example, a study comparing high and low physiological arousal found that high physiological arousal led participants to judge expressions of positive emotions as expressing strong-arousal positive emotions (i.e., joy) whereas low physiological arousal led participants to judge the same expressions as expressing weak-arousal positive emotions (i.e., serenity)66. Along these lines, within the context of a sexual interaction, sexual arousal may bias one’s processing and interpretation of their partner’s emotions, especially if they are not communicated explicitly, and need to be inferred from nonverbal cues. Indeed, studies find that sexual activation (induced by the likes of sexual or romantic stimuli, speed-dating, and supraliminal sexual primes) affects perception of sexual interest or intent (at least in some cases) in speed dates partners, descriptions of hypothetical behaviors, and even in facial expressions40,53,67–70.

If future research provides experimental evidence that sexual arousal indeed affects core psychological functions, several important follow-up questions will ensue. One such question relates to target specificity in social perception. That is, does sexual arousal influence one’s social perception of a specific desired target, of all sexually relevant targets (e.g., targets of the gender one is sexually attracted to), of all social targets (i.e., all people), or perhaps even more generally of non-social perception as well. Another question relates to whether the effects of sexual arousal, similarly to those of physiological arousal, depend on timing. Specifically, the direction of the effects of physiological arousal on cognitive functions was found to depend on the time elapsed since its onset (i.e., since the beginning of a physical exercise), impairing cognitive functions at first, but improving them later61. Thus, it may also be the case that in some stages of the sexual interaction, sexual arousal improves cognitive functioning (e.g., focusing attention on relevant social cues), whereas in other stages it impairs it (e.g., misinterpreting relevant social cues).

Internal conflict

An internal conflict entails the clashing of thoughts, attitudes, emotions, or courses of action (behaviors)71. Different features of the conflict, some of which are present during sexual interactions, can make it either easier or more difficult to resolve.

First, recall Margot, the “Cat Person”1 protagonist, who is ambivalent72 regarding the sexual interaction, and is thus conflicted between continuing with the sexual interaction and stopping it. A recent qualitative analysis of cases where unwanted (both consensual and nonconsensual) sex occurred73 revealed that women indeed experienced a conflict between not wanting to be perceived as “leading someone on” or a “bitch,” on the one hand (inclining them to go along with the interaction), and not wanting to be perceived as a “slut” or “easy,” on the other (inclining them to stop the interaction). Although not usually framed this way, this conflict can be thought of as an example of an avoidance–avoidance conflict74. Avoidance–avoidance conflict is especially difficult to resolve because each of the negative options pushes the decision maker away from it, and so the decision maker is trapped between the options, unable to resolve the conflict74–76. Relatedly, this type of conflict can also be thought of as a high-stakes decision, in which there is a large prospective loss outcome and a high cost to reversing the decision once made77. Looking at internal conflict in sexual interactions through the lens of difficult, high-stakes, possibly stressful decisions may facilitate the understanding of why one might decide to stay in an unwanted sexual interaction. First, research suggests that in difficult-to-resolve conflict, the tendency to choose the default option increases78. The default option is to keep the status quo (referred to as status quo bias79), that is, to take no action (for reviews see refs. 77,80). In the context of sexual interactions, taking no action effectively means continuing what has already started despite having conflicting thoughts. There is a need for studies examining whether status quo bias also occurs in the context of sexual interactions. Second, research shows that in high-stakes decisions individuals tend to focus only on the immediate consequences of their choices77. In conflict occurring within a sexual interaction, individuals might focus only on the immediate difficulty of stopping the ongoing situation, and not on the (possibly negative) emotional consequences that might unfold later on. In addition, when individuals are uncertain about their decisions, which is often the case in high-stakes decisions, they rely on social norms regarding what others would do in the same situation77. If the social norm is perceived to be that individuals tend to desire sexual interactions, or “go with the flow,” more individuals might choose this option when they face conflict within sexual interactions.

In addition, sexual interactions evolve and change as they unfold. This requires the individual to continuously update their assessment of what is currently going on38 and necessitates making multiple decisions either simultaneously or in close succession. Crucially, these decisions are governed by situational characteristics, which make the conflict even more difficult to resolve, and the decisions more prone to biases. One such situational characteristic is sexual arousal. Another is limited time to make decisions. Time pressure can have several relevant effects on internal conflict that might influence the unfolding of sexual interactions. First, research shows that individuals tend to make more concessions when negotiating under time pressure15. In the sexual context, if an individual perceives a conflict between their own wishes and what they believe to be their partner’s wishes, time pressure might increase the likelihood that they will concede to their partner’s wishes instead of focusing solely on their own. Second, if an individual makes a decision under time pressure they often tend to avoid information even if it is available, possibly to avoid being conflicted between alternatives, and this information avoidance often leads to self-serving choices12. Thus, time pressure might hinder individuals from getting verbal, clarifying, information about their partner’s wishes, which might be in conflict with their own.

Understanding others

In interpersonal interactions, understanding the partner’s perspective, inferring their motives and objectives, and predicting what they are planning to do are key components of a smooth and pleasant interaction. In the sexual context, being attuned to the other’s needs, emotions, and desires has further important implications for sexual and relationship satisfaction81. Emotion perception and perspective-taking (as well as other terms such as mentalization, cognitive empathy, empathic accuracy, and affective Theory of Mind82,83) are terms that have been used in past research to study people’s ability to recognize and understand emotional or mental states, from verbal and nonverbal expressions, within a certain context or as a personality tendency.

Despite the essential role that an accurate interpretation of others’ mental states plays in social interactions, individuals are often biased in how they judge other people’s emotional expressions, perspectives, and intentions. One of the most theory-driven consistent findings in the literature is that people’s judgments of other people’s mental states are anchored in their own mental state, a phenomenon coined egocentric bias84,85. Very briefly, whereas considering one’s own perspective is an automatic, effortless process, taking the perspective of another requires cognitive resources and effort85. The more incongruent the two perspectives are, the more difficult and less likely it is for one to take the other’s perspective85, or to judge the other’s emotional expression as expressing a different emotion from one’s own86,87. The egocentric bias may have major implications when one is trying to decipher the mental state of their partner in a sexual interaction. This is especially the case because most cues in such interactions are nonverbal cues34–38,88, which can be further accompanied by a high level of uncertainty, which in turn increases egocentrism in perspective-taking89. The individual’s egocentric assumption might be that their partner desires the interaction as much as they do. This assumption in turn might lead the individual to interpret ambiguous expressions of the partner as reflecting a state more similar to their own. In addition to the egocentric bias, the confirmation bias may also affect one’s judgment. If an individual believes that their partner desires the interaction, they might only attend to cues that support this belief, and miss cues that contradict it, such as the partner’s ambivalent expressions (a similar, but more specific concept, is the concept of positive illusions in close relationships90, where individuals see their partners more positively than their partners see themselves). The odds of an individual accurately understanding the mental state of another might decrease even more if they are unaware of potential considerations that can highly influence their partner. For instance, in heterosexual relationships, men might not be aware that women sometimes consent to sexual interactions because they feel they are expected to73,91.

Sexual arousal itself may also bias our understanding of the other. First, visceral states influence social prediction, causing people to self-project. For example, thirsty individuals predict that others who lack access to water are thirsty to a greater degree compared to the prediction of non-thirsty individuals92. Similarly, when individuals are strongly (versus weakly) physiologically aroused, they perceive other individuals’ positive facial expressions as conveying stronger rather than weaker arousal (i.e., joy rather than serenity)66. In the case of the visceral state of sexual arousal, individuals might perceive others to be more aroused than they actually are (research on the role synchrony plays in social and sexual interactions can also be of relevance93–96). Second, sexual arousal might be a source of cognitive load42,60,61 that could impair the ability to interpret the perspective of the partner, which is an effortful process to begin with85. Third, sexual arousal might affect one’s understanding of others by serving as a contextual cue. In non-sexual contexts, it was repeatedly found that context has a large effect on emotion perception26,97, such that when individuals received information about the situational context, it had a greater effect on their judgment of the emotion expressed in the face than did the facial expression itself20, and even changed the judgment of the emotional valence21. In the context of sexual interactions, when men were primed with a sexually/romantically arousing (compared to a non-arousing) context, they judged women’s behaviors as expressing more willingness to have sex69, and attractive women’s faces to express more sexual arousal (but not other emotions40).

Biases in perceiving another individual’s perspective may also occur because of the situation’s inherent uncertainty, as one can seldom truly and fully know what the other is experiencing. When people need to judge the probability of an uncertain event they rely on heuristic principles, which can lead to an inaccurate assessment98. Take for example the availability heuristic whereby individuals judge an event as more probable if it is easily retrievable, regardless of its actual frequency. If it is easier for an individual to think of a mutually wanted sexual interaction, they might deem this possibility more probable, even if it is not necessarily so. That is, the possibility that one of the partners is ambivalent about the unfolding of the sexual interaction might be common (a subject for future research to address), but if it is not very retrievable, there is a higher probability that this possibility will not be taken into consideration.

Finally, if future studies find empirical support for biases in the interpretation of partners’ mental states in sexual interactions, there remains the question of the mechanism underlying such biases. That is, it needs to be ascertained whether such biases are due to a lack of available resources needed to understand the partner’s perspective, a lack of motivation to do so, especially if it leads to the conclusion that the partner’s desires are different from one’s own, or both.

Potential implications

Theoretical implications

Potential theoretical implications for social cognition research

What can we learn about the processes themselves by studying them in the context of sex? Let us take arousal, for example. Sexual arousal is jointly composed of both physiological and psychological arousal. While it would be tempting to assume that all arousal types share at least some features and have similar implications, this is not necessarily the whole picture. A meta-analysis of the effects of exercise-induced arousal on cognitive performance found that even two activities that stimulate physiological arousal, cycling, and treadmill running, differently affected cognitive performance, with cycling enhancing cognitive performance and running impairing it61. If general physiological arousal affects cognitive performance in opposite directions depending on the specific activity, it is likely that activities that evoke different types of arousal (e.g., running on a treadmill, walking around a haunted house, or engaging in a sexual interaction, stimulating physiological, affective, and sexual arousal, respectively) will have different effects on basic social cognition processes. Research is needed that empirically compares different types of arousal. If different types of arousal are shown to have different effects, that is, if their effects differ qualitatively and not only quantitatively, it suggests that there are domain-specific mechanisms for arousal in different social contexts, rather than a domain-general system of arousal across all social contexts. Such questions do not pertain only to arousal, but also to other psychological processes more broadly. For example, is emotion perception the same across different social contexts, or does it vary across different social contexts, such as when relating to different types of targets (e.g., sexual partners vs. colleagues)?

Empirically addressing such theoretical issues would mark the initial stage in the development of new research questions. Are social cognition processes either domain-general or domain-specific, or do different processes exhibit distinct patterns across different social contexts? Are there specific social contexts (e.g., the sexual context) with unique effects on social cognition processes, and other contexts that influence the same processes similarly (e.g., the work context, the friend context)? If so, which contexts cause qualitatively different effects on social cognition processes? And of course, what are those unique effects? Finally, does the potential difference between social contexts stem from physiological, cognitive, motivational, or social factors? Thus, studying social cognition processes within the unique and strong context of sexual interactions can pave the way for addressing many questions of interest to the field of social cognition, and social psychology more generally (see the interesting related discussion about questions of generalizability of judgment and decision-making processes to the context of romantic relationships2).

Potential theoretical implications for sex research

Sexual interactions can be a wonderful experience, enjoyed by all involved99. However, partners can experience varying levels of ambivalence regarding the situation100, such as varying wantedness, desire, or ambiguity regarding their own (and their partner’s) wishes, and so on, resulting in a range of positive and/or negative experiences. Whereas some instances of such ambivalence might be trivial, like feeling conflicted due to an early morning commitment, others can be considerably more distressing, such as consenting to unwanted sexual activity7,8,10,101–103 (even though unwanted consensual sex does not always have to be experienced as negative, especially within committed relationships8,10,102,104–106). The range of potential experiences and psychological outcomes one can have within the context of sexual interactions emphasizes the importance of exploring relevant processes that might contribute to negative outcomes, as well as facilitate positive experiences.

Throughout the previous section, we emphasized the role of social cognition processes as potential mechanisms underlying the dynamics of sexual interactions, and how they might help determine whether a consensual sexual interaction will be experienced as more positive or negative. That is, understanding social cognition processes operating during sexual interactions can provide additional insights into central concepts traditionally explored in research on sex processes. For example, researchers studying the mechanisms underlying the act of consenting to unwanted sex have been focused on the role of sex-related mechanisms such as gendered sexual scripts73,91,107–109, sexual self-control102, feelings about first sexual experience110, and attachment styles106, or they merely asked individuals to report their reasons for consenting6–8,10,104–107,109. Here, we suggest several additional mechanisms that might cause ambivalent individuals to consent to sexual interactions by using concepts such as avoidance–avoidance conflict and high-stakes decisions. In addition, we consider how situational factors such as arousal, time pressure, and uncertainty might make it more difficult for an individual to decipher their own wishes or increase the possibility that their choices will be influenced by the perception of norms or expectations. Furthermore, whereas most research on consent and unwanted consent focuses on the person consenting (with some research on how individuals perceive signals of consent34,35,111), our analysis of sexual interactions as interpersonal interactions emphasizes the second person in the equation (i.e., the partner), and how even basic processes such as emotion perception and perspective-taking might be biased in sexual interactions, in myriad ways. Such biases in trying to understand the other’s mental state might be especially important if one partner desires the interaction while the other is conflicted about it, with the sexual context potentially causing them to miss ambivalent signals that they might not have missed in a non-sexual interaction. More broadly, studying the underlying complex social cognition processes operating within sexual interactions (e.g., ambivalence, conflict, uncertainty) can help to further move the scientific discussion about consent away from the binary distinction between “no means no” and “yes means yes” toward a more nuanced and complex view of the range of possible outcomes of consensual sexual interactions. Finally, as social cognition research is typically studied using experimental manipulations (see Box 1), conducting such research will provide important insights into the causality of relevant mechanisms underlying the varying dynamics and psychological outcomes of sexual interactions.

The potential contribution of integrating social cognition research and sex research pertains also to what we can learn by using methodologies typically used in social cognition research in the context of sexual interactions, and vice versa. Within the sex research literature, researchers interested in the psychological mechanisms underlying phenomena like unwanted consensual sex typically study individuals’ introspection about their past experiences either through interviews6,73,109 or surveys5,7,8,10,104,105,107 or they explore its correlations with other constructs, mostly psychosexual (e.g., sexual self-perceptions8,102,104,106,107,110). While insights from such introspection and correlational studies are key components in reaching a comprehensive picture about a phenomenon, experimental research, typically employed when studying social cognition, adds important components. First, it can tap processes that one might be less aware of (e.g., the influence of biases). Second, it is the only way to reach conclusions about causality. It might not be easy or straightforward to come up with ways to conduct experimental research to study processes relating to sexual interactions. However, previous research has already experimentally manipulated sexual context, by, for example, exposing participants to sexual content or to situational prompts41,53,56,103. We therefore suggest that testing the effects of these manipulations on various social cognition processes, several of which have been discussed here, is feasible. Furthermore, we believe that employing state-of-the-art paradigms and techniques is important in order to gain more fine-tuned insights into such processes. For example, researchers can use mouse-tracking (monitoring motor trajectories as a decision unfolds185) or eye-tracking186 to better depict the dynamics of internal conflict when a sexual interaction is simulated (i.e., by prompts), or create a virtual reality environment to simulate the evolving nature of sexual interactions. Research on social cognition processes, on the other hand, is typically experimental and often focuses on individual level analysis at a specific moment in time, rather than on dyadic interactions and dynamic, over-time patterns—a perspective more commonly employed in sex and relationship research. While insights from experimental research are important due to the reasons we detailed above, social cognition does not occur in a vacuum. Integrating methods from sex and relationship research, such as dyadic methods or repeated measures designs (e.g., experience sampling)187, can offer a more comprehensive understanding of social cognition processes and may lead to new conclusions. Importantly, the understanding of both the dynamics of sexual interactions and the mechanisms of social cognition requires a comprehensive perspective, best achieved through the integration of diverse research methods—experimental and ecological. We implore researchers to think about how their methods of expertize can help tap new mechanisms underlying the dynamics of sexual interactions and their psychological outcomes or social cognition processes.

We would be remiss to highlight the potential contributions of conducting experimental research on sexual interactions without discussing ethical considerations, which are crucial in conducting any psychological research, but perhaps even more so in the domain of sex. While it might be more challenging to find ethical ways to test questions relevant to the sexual context, psychologists are experienced in finding solutions to such issues. For example, when Burger188 attempted to replicate Milgram’s189 seminal findings on obedience, he not only reduced the maximum voltage used but also implemented several precautionary measures to prevent participant distress, such as thorough participant screening and repeated reminders of their right to terminate participation without forfeiting compensation. Researchers also found solutions when studying the sexual context, like manipulating sexual arousal by exposing participants to sexually explicit content in the privacy of their homes53,56. Nonetheless, for each potential study, researchers would need to carefully consider the best ways to test the psychological question at hand, while carefully weighing ethical issues against the potential scientific gains.

Applied implications

As we strive for psychological well-being, promoting sexual well-being is important as it is found to be related to life satisfaction3. However, the story “Cat Person”1 and the #MeToo movement highlighted the potential for negative outcomes in different types of sexual interactions such as nonconsensual sex and consensual unwanted sex. The public discussion and the increased awareness that followed emphasized that preventing emotionally negative consequences ensuing from sexual encounters is a goal of the utmost importance. Conducting research from the perspective of the underlying mechanisms (i.e., social cognition processes) that lead to negative outcomes in sexual interactions will be a critical step toward changing societal norms and conventions through awareness, interventions, and educational programs that are grounded in empirical research112.

The social cognition literature we reviewed above can be an important resource informing such programs. For example, assuming that future studies do find empirical evidence for the influence of biases either on decisions to stay in unwanted sexual interactions or on the interpretation of the partner’s mental states, intervention programs can draw from the extensive literature on debiasing techniques113,114. Intervention programs can also be informed by the literature on goals and motivation115–118, especially literature that concerns processes relevant for a partner of someone who is ambivalent about the sexual interaction at hand. For instance, increasing the motivation for social desirability was found to facilitate empathizing with others119). Importantly, social cognition research has taught us that accurately understanding others is often quite difficult120,121, which might be especially true in sexual interactions due to their nonverbal nature. Instead, asking for feedback122,123 and direct questioning (“perspective-getting”120, as opposed to perspective-taking) were both found to be helpful strategies for improving predictions of another person’s emotions and attitudes. Such findings also seem to support recent sexual assault prevention policies requiring “affirmative consent,” i.e., an active agreement to engage in sexual activity. But as consent does not guarantee a mutually positive experience, intervention programs should aim not only for direct questioning, but also for active communication of wishes and internal experiences by both partners, reducing the need to interpret ambiguous cues, and promoting a positive experience for both124. Encouraging such active communication is especially important given the sometimes unrealistic representations of sex created through the increasing exposure of adolescents and adults alike to explicit sexual content online125.

Throughout this paper, we have highlighted how social cognition processes contribute to the understanding of consensual sexual interactions. However, the same processes may also play a crucial role in understanding the risk factors that may contribute to the occurrence of nonconsensual sexual interactions. We strongly emphasize that recognizing these cognitive biases as risk factors in no way justifies any form of violence, coercion, or any other action resulting in nonconsensual sex. Instead, understanding that people may be susceptible to these biases in the sexual context further stresses the critical need for open and active communication of wishes and experiences between partners in all sexual interactions.

The impact of the potential outcomes of sexual interactions is not limited to the individual level but may extend to the societal level as well. With regard to the potentially negative outcomes of unwanted consensual sex, for example, the public discussion on the topic, as well as previous research, has made it abundantly clear that women are more likely to consent to unwanted sexual interactions7–10 (note that most studies included only or mostly heterosexual samples, or did not differentiate the sample based on sexual orientation). When the potential for negative outcomes affect women disproportionately, coupled with other potentially dire consequences for women (e.g., unwanted pregnancy), it maintains under-the-surface gender inequality in one of the most basic human experiences (sex), preserves women as a vulnerable population, and constitutes a basic sociocultural norm that may enable sexual violence against women126. Creating norms where the wishes of both partners in a sexual interaction are seen and communicated is an important step in advancing mutual and equal sexual interactions and, in the context of heterosexual sexual interactions, in reducing women’s vulnerability.

Conclusions

Research in psychology has long underscored the significance of contextual influence on social cognition processes and behavior. However, the exploration of sexual interactions as a unique context affecting these processes was largely neglected by previous research, despite the prominent role sexual interactions play in our daily lives. We proposed a line of research integrating theoretical insights and methodologies from social cognition with those from sex and relationship research. Social cognition processes are highly relevant, and essential, to the dynamics of sexual interactions, and can be key mechanisms contributing to potential psychological outcomes, both positive, promoting well-being, and negative, like some cases of unwanted consensual sex. Furthermore, studying social cognition processes within the context of sexual interactions could produce new insights into these processes, by addressing questions relating to their domain-general or domain-specific nature. Our aim is to inspire psychology researchers to contemplate how their respective areas of theoretical and methodological expertise can be related to and viewed through the lens of sexual interactions, encouraging a line of research which more closely integrates these two fields, and looks at the dynamic interplay between them.
