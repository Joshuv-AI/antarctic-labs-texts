High precision acoustofluidic synthesis of stable, biocompatible water-in-water emulsions

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Emulsions, colloidal systems consisting of one liquid dispersed within another immiscible liquid [1], [2], find widespread application across diverse industries such as pharmaceuticals, food, cosmetics, textiles, paints, and coatings, for their ability to stabilize formulations, control release, and enhance product efficacy [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. They are integral in drug delivery to improve bioavailability [8], [9] and facilitate targeted release of drug compounds [10], [11]. In the food industry, emulsions are used to encapsulate flavors [12] and nutrients [13], [14] to create fat replacers [15], as well as to provide to provide texture and stability [16] In cosmetics they are essential for formulating creams, lotions, and other skincare products, where they help to deliver active ingredients, improve texture, and provide desirable sensory properties.

The most common procedure to achieve stable oil in water (o/w) or water in oil (w/o) emulsions is to use an emulsifier, which can introduce undesirable interactions with other formulations, impacting purification [17], performance [18], [19], [20], and biocompatibility [21], [22], [23], leading to complications and unwanted side effects [24], such as long-term exposure. Creating stable and emulsifier-free o/w or w/o emulsions is challenging [25]. The oil phase is not desirable in skin products that require a lightweight, non-greasy texture that can be rapidly absorbed [26] or when treating patients with specific allergies [27]. Another important consideration is the sustainability of the developed products, in which certain oils derived from non-renewable sources are likely to have a negative environmental impact [28].

The issues associated with emulsifiers and the presence of oil phases can be addressed using water-in-water (w/w) emulsions. These w/w emulsions consist of water droplets dispersed in a continuous water phase. The resulting formulations offer improved purity, controlled release, and fast absorption of active ingredients, and (when formulated with biocompatible polymers) a significantly reduced potential for toxicity or adverse effects [29], all of which are critical for reliable pharmaceutical or biomedical applications. In food science, micron-sized w/w emulsions can deliver water-soluble bioactive compounds, such as probiotics or enzymes [30], serving as thickeners, rheological texture modifiers or bio-based packaging materials [31]. However, the controlled scalable production of w/w emulsions [32] and ensuring their stability are two key unmet challenges that hinder their widespread implementation.

The formation of w/w emulsions requires one of the aqueous polymeric phases to break down into droplets and disperse into another immiscible phase. This can be achieved through high-energy mixing of the immiscible phase to increase the interfacial area between the dispersed and continuous phases using impellers [33]. Furthermore, accurately obtaining the required size distribution [34] of w/w emulsions requires process parameters, including mixing times, homogenization conditions, and temporal fluctuations, to be accurately controlled, which is often extremely challenging [35] in bulk systems. Active microfluidic mixers can control the processing conditions during the generation of w/w emulsions. However, they struggle to perform optimally when the solutions are highly viscous, like polymer solutions [36]. Thermal bubble-actuated diffuser pumps and pulsed laser-induced cavitation bubbles have been shown to facilitate mixing at high viscosities [37]. However, these methods are not suitable for emulsion generation because the thermal and mechanical forces exerted during the process can compromise the integrity of the encapsulated active compounds. There is an unmet need for innovative approaches that do not compromise the uniformity of droplet generation or the stability of the encapsulated active and labile materials.

The second challenge associated with w/w emulsions is their stability, which depends on the inherent interfacial tension between the inner and outer aqueous phases. Traditional emulsifiers, which are mostly amphiphilic molecules [38], are poorly adsorbed and cannot be used. Sustaining emulsion stability without compromising functionality is often difficult [39], [40]. The thermodynamic instability of w/w emulsions causes rapid coalescence, leading to immediate phase segregation and the formation of two poorly defined water phases.

Various strategies have been proposed to address this issue, including the utilization of block polymers that adsorb at the emulsion droplet interface, nanoplates, and colloidal particles for Pickering stabilization [41], [42], [43], as well as linear homopolymers [44] that prevent coalescence by forming a protective layer. All these methods are effective only for a limited duration [45], [46], [47]. Recently, several studies have reported crosslinking of the interface of w/w pickering emulsions to enhance the protection of encapsulated bioactive compounds. This strategy is required because the colloidal particles forming pickering emulsions are only effective until they cover the emulsion interface [48], [49], [50]. However, most studies have focussed on generation and stabilization of w/w emulsions separately and consequently, a novel one-step approach is required to generate and enhance the stability of water-in-water (w/w) emulsion droplets.

Here, we address these two challenges using an integrated microfluidic platform, in which an acoustically actuated microfluidic micromixer [51], [52], [53] rapidly homogenizes highly viscous aqueous polymers in several milliseconds and generates uniform w/w emulsions with precisely controlled diameters. Immediately after their formation, the emulsions are encapsulated in larger droplets consisting of an alginate gel matrix, which is cross-linked to provide additional stability (Fig. 1). The three-dimensional colloidal gel structure makes it suitable for protecting and delivering labile active ingredients. Our approach does not use toxic surfactants, and instead relies only on food-grade polymers, which can be easily absorbed while maintaining emulsion stability. The gel medium can sufficiently prevent aggregation and leakage of emulsions when undisturbed. The release of the emulsions can be controlled by varying the alginate concentration, which changes the shear sensitivity of the emulsion-gel matrix. Tests performed with kidney cell lines indicate that the gel medium is fully biocompatible, thereby enhancing the potential therapeutic effectiveness of the system, with a broad range of potential applications in controlled drug delivery, wound healing, and tissue regeneration.Fig. 1Schematic showing the overview for generation of emulsion encapsulated gels by acoustic micromixer.

Materials and methods

All materials were purchased and used as received. Dextran (Mw ∼ 500,000) and Polyethylene glycol PEG (Mw ∼ 120,000) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Australia). Medium viscosity sodium alginate and calcium chloride were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Dextran labelled with FITC (Mw ∼ 450,000 – 500,000) was obtained from Signa Aldrich Glass, and Capillaries were procured from Vitrocom. The HEK293T cell lines, Gibco™ DMEM (high glucose, pyruvate, no glutamine, 10313039) and Gibco™ qualified fetal bovine serum (FBS), 26140079) were obtained from Thermo Scientific. Penicillin-streptomycin (10,000 U/mL, 15140122) was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. L-glutamine (200 mM, 25030081) and MEM non-essential amino acid solution (100X, 11140050) were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. PrestoBlue™ Cell Viability Reagent (A13261) was obtained from Invitrogen.

Device fabrication

The microfluidic mixer was constructed with three layers: top channel, silicon chip, and bottom channel. The key component is a silicon chip with a variable-thickness through-hole oscillator. The silicon chip's fabrication started with applying a 50 µm thick layer of SU8 (Negative Photoresist, MicroChem Corp) to a 500 µm thick double-side polished (DSP) silicon wafer. Photolithography was used to pattern the SU8 layer to create octagram-shaped holes. The Deep Reactive Ion Etching (DRIE) technique was then applied to etch through the silicon chip, with patterned SU8 acting as a masking layer. Due to the octagram-shaped holes non-uniform exposure area, differential etching rates occurred, resulting in a circular hole encircled by freestanding structures on the wafer's opposite side [54], as shown in Figure S1. After the fabrication of the silicon chip, it was sandwiched between two PDMS microchannel layers using the plasma bonding method. Both PDMS layers had identical microfluidic channels, with dimensions of 5 mm length, 2 mm width, and 0.1 mm depth, fabricated using a standard soft-lithography technique. The top channel incorporated holes at both ends, produced by a 1.5 mm biopsy punch, whereas the bottom channel was designed with a singular hole at one of its ends. During the assembly phase, with the circular hole side of the silicon chip facing upward, the through-hole on the silicon chip, the center of the top channel, and the closed end of the bottom channel were aligned. A PZT piezoelectric transducer was then attached to the assembled device using two-part epoxy glue (Selleys, Araldite).

Experimental Details

Fluorescently tagged FITC-dextran mixed with varying concentrations of sodium alginate was delivered to the mixer through inlet a, whereas poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) was delivered through inlet b. Both the fluids were delivered at the same flow rate using a syringe pump (ChemyX Fusion 4000). A signal generator (Stanford research systems DS345) and an amplifier (T&C power conversion, Inc. The optimal actuation frequency was evaluated using a Vector Network Analyzer (VNA) (Mini-Circuits), and the mixing performance of the device was studied at flow rates ranging from 1 μL/min to 10 μL/min.

Characterization of emulsion and emulsion gel formation

Bright-field and fluorescent microscopy

Bright-field and fluorescence microscopy were utilized to evaluate the effects of actuation and viscosity of the polymeric solutions on the size of the emulsion droplets. Emulsion droplets were prepared by mixing 20 wt% PEG and 12 wt% dextran in the continuous PEG phase along with different concentrations of alginate (0 %, 0.5 %, 1 %, 1.5 %, and 2 %). After actuation, the emulsion droplets formed from each sample were collected and images of the emulsion droplets were captured using an Olympus BX51 microscope. The size distribution data were analyzed and fitted using Gaussian curves. In addition, the fluorescence images of the emulsion droplets were captured for morphological analysis using a Basler pylon camera.

Rheological behaviour of the emulsion gels

The rheological properties of the emulsion encapsulated alginate gels were analyzed using an Anton Paar rheometer (MCR 702e). The emulsion-encapsulated gels were prepared by mixing 20 wt% PEG and 12 wt% dextran with varying concentrations of alginate viz. 0.5 %, 1 %, 1.5 %, and 2 %, and then dripped into a calcium chloride solution using a glass capillary. The samples were placed on a 25 °C substrate, and a parallel plate arrangement with a 0.2 mm gap was employed to assess the rheological characteristics of the emulsion encapsulated gels. Paraffin oil was applied along the edges of the materials to prevent moisture loss during the testing.

Shear rheological tests were conducted by rotating the parallel plate at increasing speeds to determine the viscosity and shear stress of the samples as a function of shear rate (ranging from 0.01 s−1-–100 s−1). Oscillatory rheological tests were performed by oscillating a parallel plate with increasing oscillatory strain amplitudes (from 0.1 % to 100 %) while maintaining a constant frequency of 1 Hz. This enabled the measurement of the storage (G') and loss (G’) components of the shear modulus, as well as tan δ (δ = G''/G') values, as a function of the oscillatory strain amplitude.

Cell viability assay

To assess cell viability following exposure to emulsion encapsulated gels, we performed a PrestoBlue® assay [55]. The day before the treatment administration, HEK293T cells were seeded at a density of 5000 cells per well in 96-well plates, and each well was supplemented with 100 μL of DMEM cell culture medium supplemented with 10 % FBS. After allowing the cells to adhere to the plates for 24-hour, 10 μL of each treatment solution, namely, w/w emulsions and emulsion-encapsulated gels with varying alginate concentrations (0.5 %, 1 %, 1.5 %, and 2 %), were added to the wells. Cells treated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were used as controls. Subsequently, 24-hour post-treatment, the existing cell culture medium was aspirated and replaced with 100 μL of cell culture medium containing PrestoBlue® cell viability reagent (comprising 10 μL of PrestoBlue® reagent and 90 μL of culture medium). The plates were incubated again in a 37 °C, 5 % CO2 environment for 4-hour. Following incubation, the fluorescence intensity was measured (bottom read) using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy HTX plate reader) at excitation and emission wavelengths of 540 nm and 590 nm, respectively. The resulting data are represented as the percentage change in viability, with the PBS-treated group serving as the reference point at 100 %.

Results and Discussion

Mixer properties

Aqueous two-phase system emulsions were prepared using an acoustically actuated ultrafast microfluidic mixer that rapidly and controllably homogenizes the solutions within several milliseconds. The mixer was previously used to homogenize liquid solutions with comparable viscosities [51], synthesize nanoparticles [52], and lyse blood cells [56] in less than several milliseconds. In the current iteration, the device assembly is employed to mix highly viscous polymer solutions, a task that has thus far been challenging.

In the absence of any external perturbations, the flow profile in a microfluidic device is laminar and mass transfer is dominated by diffusion. A geometric constraint for achieving effective mixing in this scenario is given by ld≥Pe, where l is the mixing length, d is the hydraulic diameter of the channel, and Pe is the Peclet number [52]. When dealing with high viscosity fluids, where diffusion coefficient is low and Peclet number is high, the required mixing length in the channel becomes exceptionally long. This leads to an impractically high flow resistance in the system. The mixing length can be reduced if sufficiently large external perturbations, analogous to those achieved with impellers used in bulk systems [57] can be applied without compromising the flow conditions.

The device employed in this study incorporates unique features that enable efficient mixing of samples with exceptionally high viscosities, surpassing previous capabilities, as shown in Fig. 2a. The key feature is a silicon micro-oscillator that consists of stiff variable-thickness overhanging structures with sharp features (Fig. 2b). The oscillator is sandwiched between two PDMS microfluidic channels. In the present configuration, the solutions to be mixed are fed through two inlets from the opposing sides of the device. The geometry of the device was optimized to ensure large vibrational amplitudes at sufficiently high frequencies [51].

Upon application of a MHz-level oscillatory signal to a piezoelectric transducer attached to the device assembly, the sharp features start vibrating and generate localized pressure variations. This induces a strong streaming field within the fluid in the channel, efficiently perturbing the fluid flow, even at increasing flow rates. As a result, the interfaces between the different phases were disrupted, the interfacial contact between the fluids was increased, and diffusion was enhanced, leading to the formation of emulsions, as shown in Fig. 2f.

Mixing characterisation

During the experiments, 20 wt% PEG and 12 wt% FITC-labelled dextran solutions were introduced from the two inlets at the top and bottom of the device, respectively, at flow rates ranging from 1 µl/min to 10 µL/min. The viscosity of the PEG solution was maintained at 42.081 mPa · s throughout the experiment. However, the viscosity of the 12 wt% dextran solution was varied through addition of alginate at increasing concentrations (0.5 %, 1 %, 1.5 %, and 2 %) from 38.16 mPa.s to 84.66 mPa.s (Figure S2).

The device was actuated for each combination through a 1.064 MHz oscillatory signal at 0.4Vpp amplitude, initiating the mixing process and triggering emulsion formation. The FITC dye in the dextran phase was used to quantify the mixing efficiency.

Fluorescence intensities were measured at varying flow rates and solution viscosities to calculate the relative mixing index (RMI) for each case [58], [59]. Upon actuation, the oscillation of the sharp features instantly destabilizes the interface between the coflowing polymers, generating multiple bands. Owing to the high viscosities employed, there is initially a delay for the mixing to start for the entire solution to be homogenized. After this point, the solutions were effectively mixed in less than three milliseconds, as long as the device was in operation, as shown in Fig. 2e and Supplementary Video.

Owing to the stiffness of the oscillating structure and optimized actuation parameters, the oscillator can overcome the damping effect resulting from an increase in the backflow pressure at increasing flow rates. Additionally, the mixing efficiency was calculated using the unmixed and mixed region of interest (ROI), as shown in Fig. 2c. Notably, the mixing efficiency remained consistently high, even when the viscosity of the dextran solution was substantially increased, with the computed RMI exceeding 90 % for all viscosities and flow rates, as shown in Fig. 2d.

Effect of acoustic emulsification on the size of emulsion droplets

As soon as the device is actuated, the high-frequency, high-amplitude oscillation of the silicon structure at MHz frequencies generates a rapid streaming flow and excessive shear stresses, destabilizing the interface between the coflowing polymers and maximizing the surface area between them. Consequently, the intermolecular forces between the two polymer phases were overcome and the dextran phase broke into smaller droplets that were dispersed in the continuous PEG phase. Continuous mechanical agitation, localized micro-vortices and shear stresses arising from high-amplitude oscillations reduce the size of emulsion droplets. (Figure S3) [17]. In contrast to the conventional bulk emulsion synthesis method, the acousto-fluidic mixer presented here offers real-time microscopic monitoring of emulsion formation and changes in the polymer concentrations. Fluorescence micrographs confirmed dextran emulsion droplets formation in the PEG solution's continuous phase. To evaluate the effects of the viscosity on the emulsion droplets size, 20 wt% PEG, 12 wt% dextran and different concentrations of sodium alginate were used, and bright-field and fluorescence images of the emulsions were recorded throughout the mixing process. The size distribution graphs of the emulsion droplets are shown in Fig. 3c. Optical micrographs revealed the presence of spherical droplets with well-defined structures, and their average sizes were monitored using various concentrations of sodium alginate (Fig. 3b). Previously, the findings demonstrated that smaller droplet sizes were observed with increasing viscosity, as coalescence was suppressed. As the mobility of the droplets decreased, along with a reduction in their collision frequency, the macromolecular stabilizers played a vital role in enhancing stability through hydrodynamic effects. The sodium alginate concentrations added to the dextran phase acted as stabilizers and prevented the rapid coalescence of freshly formed emulsions [60].

As a result, the average size of the emulsions was reduced and their uniformity improved substantially with increasing alginate concentration (Fig. 3).In the absence of sodium alginate, the droplet size is measured to be 63.8 ± 1.4 μm, indicating larger and less uniform particles. However, upon the addition of 0.5 wt% sodium alginate, the emulsion exhibited a reduced particle size of 51.9 ± 1.2 μm. Furthermore, with the incorporation of 1 wt% and 1.5 wt% sodium alginate, there was a continued decrease in particle size and an increase in uniformity, giving values of 31.9 ± 1.1 μm and 17 ± 0.8 μm, respectively. Including 2 wt% sodium alginate in the polymeric solution and actuation yielded even smaller particle sizes and narrower size distributions. These particles exhibited remarkable uniformity, with an average size of 7.2 ± 0.6 μm.

Emulsion gels

A major concern regarding the water-in-water emulsions is their long-term stability. To prevent unwanted aggregation, immediately after their formation, the emulsions were introduced into a glass capillary, and the dextran emulsion droplet in a continuous PEG phase with the alginate solution was solidified by calcium chloride crosslinking, resulting in emulsion-encapsulated alginate gels (Fig. 4a). In this system, the function of alginate was two-fold: during the mixing stage, it was used to vary the droplet size, whereas, during the final encapsulation stage, it formed a stable gel matrix, preventing the w/w emulsion droplets from establishing contact. The gel matrix was formed by crosslinking the polymer chains using a calcium chloride solution. The presence of calcium chloride is crucial for stabilizing emulsion droplets, as it induces Na-alginate gelation through ionic cross-linking with calcium ions, forming a robust network around the droplets. This crosslinking process not only enhances emulsion stability by preventing coalescence, but also expels water, leading to a relatively dehydrated gel structure. After the crosslinking step, the gel network provides a solid-like structure to the overall system as shown in Fig. 4b. The images of the emulsion-encapsulated gels clearly show a solid gel structure, as depicted in Fig. 4d. The effect of different alginate concentrations after crosslinking with calcium chloride was assessed for its influence on the morphology. Distinctive patterns were observed within the various gels in the entrapped emulsion droplets at varying alginate concentrations. Consequently, the gel network imparted structural stability to the emulsion droplets, preventing coalescence over an extended period. This attribute makes them promising candidates for use as structuring agents in the food and cosmetic industries [61].

It should be emphasized that, in the absence of actuation, the emulsion-encapsulated gel displayed a distinct phase, with no observable emulsion formation upon entrapment. Conversely, when actuation was applied at flow rates of up to 10 μL/min, uniformly distributed emulsion-encapsulated gels were obtained (Figure S4).

Rheological behaviour of the emulsion gels

Emulsion gel suspensions containing 0.5 %, 1 %, 1.5 %, and 2 % alginate exhibited shear-thinning behavior, with the viscosity decreasing as the shear rate was increased from 0.01 s−1 to 100 s−1 (Fig. 5a) [62]. This observation suggests that even 0.5 % emulsion encapsulated alginate gels form a non-dilute suspension with sufficient gel interactions. Viscosities of 0.5 % and 1 % emulsion encapsulated alginate gel suspensions were 170 Pas and 327 Pa.s, respectively, at a shear rate of 0.01 s−1, while a notable increase in viscosity was observed for 1.5 % and 2 % emulsion encapsulated alginate gel suspension, increasing to 401 Pa.s and 846 Pa.s, respectively, indicating stronger gel interactions as depicted in Fig. 5b. The shear moduli (storage and loss moduli) and damping factors are plotted as functions of oscillatory amplitude in Fig. 5c and 5d, respectively. Within the linear viscoelastic (LVE) region, defined by a constant shear modulus and damping factor, suspensions with lower microgel concentrations demonstrated a larger oscillatory amplitude required to end the LVE regions, but smaller storage and loss moduli. The storage modulus of the emulsion encapsulated alginate gel samples within the LVE region increased with increasing concentration, and was approximately 5.5 Pa, 63.3 Pa, 93 Pa, and 250 Pa for 0.5 %, 1 %, 1.5 %, and 2 % EAl gels, respectively. The similar damping factors (approximately 0.23) among the suspensions suggest the formation of a strong elastic network within the emulsion gels in all suspension samples [63]. Beyond the LVE region, the storage and loss moduli decreased, whereas the damping factors increased, indicating disruption and collapse of the microgel dispersion structure. The absence of a loss modulus peak suggested that no bond breakage occurred within the emulsion encapsulated alginate gel [65]. Shear-thinning and storage modulus reduction at high oscillatory amplitudes is likely due to emulsion gel deformation into ellipsoidal shapes, reducing suspension flow resistance [66]. Damping factors for 1.5 % and 2 % alginate emulsion gel samples continued to increase, while those for 0.5 % and 1 % alginate emulsion gel reached a plateau at approximately 0.2 oscillatory amplitude. This may be attributed to the reduced microgel deformation in suspensions with lower concentrations, resulting from diminished emulsion gel interactions. To further monitor the stability of the gels over time, we evaluated the viscosity of the emulsion-encapsulated alginate gels stored in a room-temperature laboratory environment after a 7-day period. The 0.5 % EAl (emulsion encapsulated alginate gel) stored at room temperature exhibited a viscosity of 63.70 Pa·s when subjected to a shear rate of 0.01 s−1, indicating a minor reduction in gel interaction over time. However, the 1 %, 1.5 %, and 2 % emulsion-encapsulated alginate gel concentrations maintained consistent viscosity levels of 320 Pa·s, 405.02 Pa·s, and 824.15 Pa·s, respectively, suggesting more robust gel interactions, as depicted in Figure S6. In contrast, viscosity measurements conducted on day 1 revealed strong gel interactions across all alginate concentrations (0.5 %, 1 %, 1.5 %, and 2 %), as shown in Fig. 5b. This is likely due to the increased cross-linking density and mechanical stability at higher alginate concentrations, which contribute to the enhanced viscosity and structural integrity of the gels. This effect is consistent with findings from similar studies that report the role of alginate in improving mechanical strength [67], [68].

The fluorescence intensity remained similar, even after treatment with the emulsions for 24 h. As shown in Fig. 6 b, the percentage change in the viability of cells treated with PBS (control) and the cells treated with 0.5 – 2 % emulsion formulations was not significant (p ≥ 0.05). Furthermore, cell viability did not differ significantly after dextran, PEG, or alginate treatment alone. The compatibility of the w/w emulsion and emulsion-encapsulated gels with the kidney cell line confirmed to be unaffected by the concentration of alginate, as no significant difference was observed between the control and exposed cells.

Conclusion

This paper demonstrates the successful generation of biocompatible, stable w/w emulsions, the size of which can be tuned through a solvent and emulsifier-free method. The conventional process of generating w/w emulsions typically requires considerable time to achieve proper and uniform mixing of the two immiscible phases, which result in inconsistent and nonuniform droplet sizes. Additionally, the low interfacial tension and poor adsorption of surfactant molecules present challenges for stabilizing these emulsions. The technique proposed in this paper overcomes these challenges using an acoustically actuated micromixer which effectively and rapidly mixes highly viscous polymers to form w/w emulsions, which were subsequently stabilized by alginate crosslinking with an integrated device without using surfactants. The size of the emulsions can be reduced by increasing the alginate concentrations while maintaining the actuation parameters.

The present study has several major advantages over existing methods. It utilizes a one-step approach for producing food-grade w/w emulsions with real-time monitoring and stabilization, without harmful impacts on the environment (Table S1) [38], [69], [70], [71]. The effectiveness of the microfluidic mixer for the homogenous mixing of aqueous two-phase systems (ATPS) polymers make a microfluidic mixer with hanging features suitable for mixing and synthesizing various materials. For instance, it can be used to produce oil-in-water, water-in-oil emulsions, and microparticles by utilizing emulsions through the solvent evaporation method. Moreover, this application will be advantageous for encapsulating proteins in water-in-water emulsion-encapsulated gels for tissue engineering applications. This approach will also have potential applications in the food industry as an edible food packaging material, where emulsions can be used to encapsulate labile components, such as enzymes and probiotics.
