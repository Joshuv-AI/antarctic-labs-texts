SuperFlow : Training Flow Matching Models with RL on the Fly
Kaijie Chen, Zhiyang Xu, Ying Shen, Zihao Lin, [4pt] Yuguang Yao, Lifu Huang

Abstract
Recent progress in flow-based generative models and reinforcement learning (RL) has improved text–image alignment and visual quality. However, current RL training for flow models still has two main problems: (i) GRPO-style fixed per-prompt group sizes ignore variation in sampling importance across prompts, which leads to inefficient sampling and slower training; and (ii) trajectory-level advantages are reused as per-step estimates, which biases credit assignment along the flow. We propose SuperFlow , an RL training framework for flow-based models that adjusts group sizes with variance-aware sampling and computes step-level advantages in a way that is consistent with continuous-time flow dynamics. Empirically, SuperFlow reaches promising performance while using only 5.4 % ∼ 56.3 % 5.4\%\sim 56.3\% of the original training steps and reduces training time by 5.2 % ∼ 16.7 % 5.2\%\sim 16.7\% without any architectural changes. On standard text-to-image (T2I) tasks, including text rendering, compositional image generation, and human preference alignment, SuperFlow improves over SD3.5 - M by 4.6 % ∼ 47.2 % 4.6\%\sim 47.2\% , and over Flow - GRPO by 1.7 % ∼ 16.0 % 1.7\%\sim 16.0\% . The code will be available at https://github.com/jkkjjj/superflow .

Introduction

First, GRPO
12
constructs per-prompt baselines on the fly by generating multiple images for each prompt within a fixed group size, which leads to three key challenges that directly motivate more sample-efficient, stable, and adaptive RL training for flow models:

(1)
Sampling Inefficiency.

In practice, each prompt may require the policy to generate dozens of images (e.g., up to
64
), causing training time and computational cost to grow rapidly. This uniform multi-sample requirement motivates approaches that can reduce redundant sampling while preserving learning signal.

(2)
Advantage Collapse and Training Instability.

When all responses in a group receive similar rewards (e.g., all zero), the relative advantages collapse toward zero, which weakens the learning signal and accelerates entropy collapse
11
. This issue becomes particularly pronounced in later training stages, where the diversity of the policy outputs tends to diminish quickly
1
. This motivates advantage estimation and optimization mechanisms that remain informative even when reward variance is low.

(3)
Inflexible Sampling Allocation.

The fixed group size ignores variation in sampling importance across prompts
51
. At a given step, some prompts exhibit high reward variance while others yield nearly constant rewards. From the GRPO objective, the expected gradient magnitude increases with the variance of returns, so high-variance prompts contribute more to policy improvement. Allocating the same number of samples to both low-variance and high-variance prompts therefore wastes sampling budget on uninformative prompts and under-samples the prompts that matter most for learning, motivating variance-aware and adaptive sampling strategies.

Second, current RL training pipelines for flow models suffers from inaccurate advantage estimation
24
;
49
.
In GRPO, trajectory advantages are computed by z-score normalization of rewards within each group. DeepSeekMath
46
alleviates this issue by incorporating process-level rewards under GRPO for language generation tasks
59
. However, for flow-based image generation, process-level rewards are difficult to obtain because intermediate states along the denoising trajectory are visually noisy and semantically ambiguous
60
. Recent methods attempt to estimate the contribution of intermediate states via metrics such as cosine similarity
61
or auxiliary networks
63
, but these approaches yield only marginal improvements and do not fundamentally resolve the bias in advantage estimation.

To address these challenges, we propose
SuperFlow
. We name it
SuperFlow
since it turns streaming rollouts into effective group-wise comparisons that are well-suited for flow models.
SuperFlow
is a Streaming-to-Group Sampling framework that improves Single-Stream Policy Optimization (SPO)
57
for flow matching models in text-to-image (T2I) generation. As illustrated in Figure
1
, compared to standard GRPO training of a flow matching model denoted
Flow-GRPO
,
SuperFlow
introduces two strategies that directly resolve the above limitations.
First, we propose
Streaming-to-Group Sampling
. Instead of using a fixed group size, we dynamically adjust the number of samples per prompt based on reward variance. This allocates more samples to high-variance prompts and fewer samples to low-variance prompts. As a result, it reduces redundant sampling and improves efficiency, and it removes the fixed, prompt-agnostic sampling rule that causes sampling rigidity. The resulting variance-aware groups also provide more reliable normalization, so relative advantages are less likely to collapse to near-zero. This helps mitigate training instability and entropy collapse in later stages.
Second, we propose
Step-level Advantage Re-estimation
. The second challenge is the bias from trajectory-level advantages in flow training, where one trajectory-level advantage is reused across all denoising steps even though reward statistics change across steps. To address this, we re-estimate advantages at each denoising step using step-dependent statistics, including reward standard deviation. This yields more accurate and consistent credit assignment to intermediate states and improves both training stability and reward alignment.

Extensive experiments demonstrate that
SuperFlow
attains comparable or better performance than prior RL methods while requiring fewer training steps and reduced compute time. We evaluate

Related Work

Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models

Reinforcement learning has become a standard tool for improving large language models under preference-based or verifiable rewards. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
45
and its variants are widely used for RLHF-style training, but often require a learned critic and careful tuning to maintain stability. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
46
offers a critic-free alternative by constructing per-prompt baselines from multiple responses and normalizing rewards within each group to obtain relative advantages. This group-based normalization has led to strong improvements in instruction following and reasoning in large-scale LLM training
35
, and it has motivated a line of work that extends group-based online RL to broader settings. Recent work has explored single-stream online optimization that reduces the need for fixed-size, per-prompt groups while retaining stable learning signals. Single-Stream Policy Optimization (SPO)
57
is a representative approach that performs single-sample updates and relies on streaming statistics or tracking mechanisms to stabilize advantage estimation and normalization over time. These methods are closely related to our setting because they provide a path to reduce the generation overhead of group-based methods while still enabling effective reward-driven learning, which is particularly important for expensive generative models.

Reinforcement Learning for Diffusion and Flow Matching

Reinforcement learning has also emerged as a powerful tool for steering diffusion and flow-based text-to-image models toward task- or preference-aligned objectives. Since the initial noise configuration strongly influences the final image quality, some methods learn improved noise priors such as Noise Hypernetworks
16
. Other approaches integrate RL signals directly into model training. Direct Reward Fine-Tuning
9
and Q-score Matching (QSM)
39
optimize generative models against explicit reward signals
4
, often using vision–language models as outcome reward models, while DPOK
19
introduces an online RL framework for diffusion. These ideas have been instantiated in diffusion+DPO
50
;
6
and diffusion+PPO
43
, demonstrating that moving beyond pure likelihood training can improve image fidelity and semantic alignment.

GRPO-Style RL for Diffusion and Flow Matching

Motivated by the success of GRPO in LLMs, several works adapt GRPO-style updates to the structure of diffusion and flow matching. Flow-GRPO
36
converts the deterministic ODE to an equivalent SDE, preserving marginals while enabling reliable sampling for RL. Dance-GRPO
58
, Mix-GRPO
29
, and Temp-GRPO
23
further tailor GRPO updates to different aspects of the denoising process. Orthogonal approaches optimize the noise itself. ReNO
17
optimizes initial noise using human-preference rewards, Golden Noise
63
builds a noise preference distribution and trains NPNet to map random noise to higher-quality noise, and LRM
61
fuses U-Net and text features for similarity-based scoring. Test-time scaling of SDE diffusion has also been cast as a Markov decision process or contextual bandit, enabling MCTS or
\epsilon
-greedy search over noise sequences
40
;
54
. Finally, Step-level Reward
33
measures per-step contribution via embedding changes between intermediate and final images, providing finer-grained feedback along the denoising trajectory.

Although GRPO-based methods for diffusion and flow matching report consistent improvements over purely supervised baselines, they inherit limitations from the underlying group-based formulation. Fixed-size per-prompt groups lead to inefficient and inflexible sampling allocation, and on-the-fly per-group baselines increase generation cost and synchronization overhead in distributed settings. In addition, trajectory-level advantages obtained via simple group-wise normalization can be biased for continuous-time flows, where intermediate states are noisy and difficult to credit accurately, even when augmented with heuristic step-level rewards
33
;
63
;
61
. Our work addresses these challenges for flow matching by introducing a Streaming-to-Group sampling scheme that adaptively allocates samples across prompts while retaining the benefits of group-wise normalization, and a step-level advantage re-estimation mechanism that provides more accurate credit assignment along the denoising trajectory.

Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the mathematical formulation of flow matching and explain how the denoising process can be represented as a multi-step Markov Decision Process (MDP).

Flow Matching.

Let
\bm{x}_{0}\sim X_{0}
be a data sample from the target data distribution, and let
\bm{x}_{1}\sim X_{1}
denote a noise sample. Recent high-performance image generation models adopt the Rectified Flow framework
37
, which defines the noised sample at continuous time
\tau\in[0,1]
as

\bm{x}_{\tau}=(1-\tau)\,\bm{x}_{0}\;+\;\tau\,\bm{x}_{1}.

(1)

A transformer model is trained to regress the velocity field
\bm{v}_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{\tau},\tau)
by minimizing the flow matching objective
34
;
37
:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau,\;\bm{x}_{0}\sim X_{0},\;\bm{x}_{1}\sim X_{1}}\bigl[\;\|\bm{v}\;-\;\bm{v}_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{\tau},\tau)\|^{2}\bigr],

(2)

where the target velocity field is
\bm{v}=\bm{x}_{1}-\bm{x}_{0}
.

Denoising as an MDP.

Following
(
3
)
, we view the reverse-time denoising procedure as a finite-horizon MDP
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\rho_{0},P,R)
by discretizing
\tau
into
T{+}1
steps.
Let
k\in\{0,1,\dots,T\}
index the reverse denoising steps and define a decreasing schedule
\tau_{k}=1-\frac{k}{T}
.
We denote
\bm{x}_{k}\triangleq\bm{x}_{\tau_{k}}
, so
\bm{x}_{0}=\bm{x}_{\tau_{0}}
is pure noise and
\bm{x}_{T}=\bm{x}_{\tau_{T}}
is the final generated sample.

At step
k
, the state is
\emph{vs}.\hbox{}_{k}\triangleq(\bm{c},k,\bm{x}_{k})
, where
\bm{c}
is the text prompt. The action is the next denoised sample
\bm{a}_{k}\triangleq\bm{x}_{k+1}
, and the policy is

\pi_{\theta}(\bm{a}_{k}\mid\emph{vs}.\hbox{}_{k})\triangleq p_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{k+1}\mid\bm{x}_{k},\bm{c}).

The transition is deterministic:

P(\emph{vs}.\hbox{}_{k+1}\mid\emph{vs}.\hbox{}_{k},\bm{a}_{k})\triangleq(\delta_{\bm{c}},\delta_{k+1},\delta_{\bm{a}_{k}}),

and the initial state distribution is

\rho_{0}(\emph{vs}.\hbox{}_{0})\triangleq\bigl(p(\bm{c}),\delta_{0},\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\bigr).

The reward is given only at the final step:

R(\emph{vs}.\hbox{}_{k},\bm{a}_{k})\triangleq\begin{cases}r(\bm{x}_{T},\bm{c}),&k=T-1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

GRPO on Flow Matching.

We optimize the policy
\pi_{\theta}
using KL-regularized returns:

\max_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{(\emph{vs}.\hbox{}_{k},\bm{a}_{k})\sim\pi_{\theta}}\Bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\Big(R(\emph{vs}.\hbox{}_{k},\bm{a}_{k})-\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\emph{vs}.\hbox{}_{k})\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid\emph{vs}.\hbox{}_{k})\big)\Big)\Bigg].

(3)

GRPO
46
employs a group-relative advantage. Given a prompt
\bm{c}
, the flow model
p_{\theta}
generates
G
samples
\{\bm{x}_{T}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}
through reverse-time trajectories. The group-normalized advantage for trajectory
i
is defined as

\hat{A}^{i}_{k}\;=\;\frac{r(\bm{x}^{i}_{T},\bm{c})-\mathrm{mean}(\{r(\bm{x}^{j}_{T},\bm{c})\}_{j=1}^{G})}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(\bm{x}^{j}_{T},\bm{c})\}_{j=1}^{G})}.

(4)

The policy is updated by maximizing

\mathcal{J}_{\text{Flow-GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{c}\sim\mathcal{C},\,\{\bm{x}^{i}\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot\mid\bm{c})}\big[f(r,\hat{A},\theta,\varepsilon,\beta)\big],

(5)

\begin{aligned} f(r,\hat{A},\theta,\varepsilon,\beta)&=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\Big(\min\!\big(r^{i}_{k}(\theta)\hat{A}^{i}_{k},\;\operatorname{clip}(r^{i}_{k}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\hat{A}^{i}_{k}\big)-\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\Big),\\
r^{i}_{k}(\theta)&=\frac{p_{\theta}(\bm{x}^{i}_{k+1}\mid\bm{x}^{i}_{k},\bm{c})}{p_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\bm{x}^{i}_{k+1}\mid\bm{x}^{i}_{k},\bm{c})}.\end{aligned}

(6)

Methodology

We introduce
SuperFlow
, a method for policy optimization of flow matching models under verifiable feedback.
Section
4.1
revisits Single-Stream Policy Optimization (SPO)
57
and specifies the components we reuse in our setting.
Section
4.2
then presents our first contribution, Streaming-to-Group Sampling, which adapts streaming updates to flow-based text-to-image training and reduces entropy collapse by constructing variance-aware normalization signals.
Section
4.3
presents our second contribution, step-level advantage re-estimation along the denoising trajectory, which improves credit assignment and accelerates training without sacrificing performance.

4.1
Revisiting SPO for Flow Matching

We first summarize the SPO ingredients that we build upon.
SPO performs online updates using streaming rollouts and maintains a per-prompt tracker to stabilize advantage normalization without requiring a fixed group size.
In our setting, the prompt
c
defines a rollout distribution over denoising trajectories produced by the flow model, and the verifiable feedback provides a scalar reward for each final image.
We adapt SPO by defining prompt-level running statistics and by using them to compute normalized advantages for policy updates.

Initialization.

For each prompt
c
in the training set
\mathcal{C}
, we maintain three prompt-specific statistics.
The value tracker
\hat{v}(c)
stores the running estimate of the expected reward for prompt
c
.
The two scalars
\alpha(c)
and
\beta(c)
are the parameters of a Beta prior that we use to track uncertainty of the reward mean, which will later serve as a stable normalization signal for streaming advantages.

We initialize these statistics using
n_{0}
rollouts generated by the initial policy
\pi_{0}
.
Let
\{r_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n_{0}}
be the rewards obtained from these rollouts for prompt
c
.
We set the initial value estimate as the empirical mean

\hat{v}_{0}(c)=\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}r_{k}.

We then initialize the Beta parameters using an effective sample size
N_{0}

\alpha_{0}(c)=N_{0}\,\hat{v}_{0}(c),\hskip 16.38895pt\beta_{0}(c)=N_{0}\left(1-\hat{v}_{0}(c)\right).

Here
N_{0}
is the initial effective sample size and we choose it as
N_{0}=1/(1-\rho_{\min})
.

Training.

After initialization, we optimize the policy
\pi_{\theta}
in episodes.
Each episode consists of selecting a prompt batch, generating rollouts with the current policy, receiving verifiable rewards, and updating the prompt-level trackers that are used for normalization and sampling in subsequent episodes.

KL-Adaptive Value Tracker Updating.

After observing a new reward
r(c,y)
for prompt
c
and generated image
y
, we update the prompt-level tracker.
The scalars
\alpha(c)
and
\beta(c)
are the running Beta parameters, and
\hat{v}(c)
is the corresponding running estimate of the expected reward.
We update

\displaystyle\alpha(c)

\displaystyle=\rho(c)\,\alpha_{i-1}(c)+r(c,y),

(7)

\displaystyle\beta(c)

\displaystyle=\rho(c)\,\beta_{i-1}(c)+\bigl(1-r(c,y)\bigr),

(8)

\displaystyle\hat{v}(c)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(c)}{\alpha(c)+\beta(c)}.

(9)

The discount factor
\rho(c)
controls how fast the tracker forgets old data.
It depends on the KL divergence
D(c)
between the current policy and the last policy that acted on prompt
c
, so that larger policy shifts lead to faster forgetting:

\rho(c)=2^{-D(c)/D_{\text{half}}}.

We clip
\rho(c)
to
[\rho_{\text{min}},\rho_{\text{max}}]

4.2
Mitigating entropy collapse across T2I tasks

In flow-based T2I training, rewards can differ substantially across prompts and tasks
48
.
This heterogeneity makes fixed sampling rules brittle.
In particular, late in training the policy entropy often decreases quickly
11
;
42
;
41
.
With a single rollout per prompt, it becomes common to observe nearly identical rewards for many prompts, which yields near-zero normalized advantages and stalls learning.
We therefore propose
Streaming-to-Group Sampling
that combines two components.
It prioritizes prompts that are more informative for learning and it allocates multiple rollouts only when needed, balancing the efficiency of streaming updates with the stability of group-based normalization.

Prioritized Prompt Selection.

To improve sample efficiency and reduce wasted updates on low-information prompts, at the beginning of each episode we sample a prompt batch
\mathcal{B}_{i}\subset\mathcal{C}
using prompt-dependent weights.
For prompt
c
, we define an uncertainty score based on the tracker
\hat{v}_{i-1}(c)
and set

w_{i}(c)\propto\sqrt{\hat{v}_{i-1}(c)\bigl(1-\hat{v}_{i-1}(c)\bigr)}+\epsilon,

(10)

where
\epsilon
is a small constant that ensures every prompt has nonzero sampling probability.
This score is larger when the reward is uncertain, and it encourages more frequent visitation of prompts that are likely to provide larger learning signal.

Adaptive Rollout Allocation.

Beyond selecting which prompts to train on, we also adapt how many rollouts to generate per prompt.
For each epoch, we compute the score
s(c)
from Eq. (
10
) for all prompts and partition
\{s(c)\}
into
K
empirical quantile bins with thresholds
\{q_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}
.
We assign each prompt a bin index

b(c)=\min\{\,k\in\{1,\dots,K\}:s(c)\in[q_{k-1},q_{k})\,\}.

We then map
b(c)
to a per-prompt rollout count
m(c)
.
With a linear rule,

m(c)=M_{\max}-b(c)+1,

so prompts with lower scores receive more rollouts to stabilize group normalization, while prompts with higher scores receive fewer rollouts to save computation.
We replicate each prompt
c
exactly
m(c)
times to form a multiset, shuffle it, and take the first
B
elements as the batch.
If the multiset has fewer than
B
elements, we sample with replacement to fill the batch.
We then update each prompt’s visitation count by its replication count.

4.3
Advantage re-estimation and fast sampling

Flow-GRPO-style training typically computes a single trajectory-level advantage and reuses it across all denoising steps.
This introduces biased credit assignment because reward uncertainty and action variability change substantially along the denoising trajectory.
We therefore propose a lightweight
step-level advantage re-estimation
rule that better matches step-dependent statistics while keeping computation low.

We start from a Monte Carlo advantage with a baseline
10
:

A_{t}=\sum_{s=t}^{T}\gamma^{s-t}r(y_{s})-b,

(11)

where
\gamma\in[0,1]
is a discount factor and
r(y_{s})
is the reward at step
s
.
Common choices of
b
include learned value baselines
(
55
)
, group mean rewards
(
46
)
, and leave-one-out averages
(
2
;
28
)
.

To approximate step-wise advantages without explicitly evaluating rewards at intermediate denoising steps, we redistribute a trajectory-level signal using step-dependent uncertainty.
We define

\hat{A}_{t}\;=\;w_{t}\cdot A^{\tau},\hskip 20.00003ptw_{t}\;=\;\eta\,\sigma_{t}^{\gamma},

(12)

where
A^{\tau}
is the trajectory-level advantage computed from the final reward, and
\sigma_{t}
is the standard deviation of the conditional action distribution
p_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{t-1}\mid\bm{x}_{t},\bm{c})
at step
t
.
In the isotropic case,
\Sigma_{t}=\sigma_{t}^{2}I
.
In the diagonal case, we aggregate element-wise standard deviations to a scalar
\sigma_{t}
, for example by taking the mean.
Appendix
B.3
shows that Eq. (
12
) provides a closer mean-squared approximation to the true step advantages than the uniform rule
w_{t}=1
used in prior work
36
under standard small-noise and local-linear assumptions.

5.1
Experimental Setup

Following standard practice, we describe the experimental setup in terms of training prompts and evaluation benchmarks, T2I tasks and reward definitions, baselines, and implementation details. Hyperparameters and additional compute details are deferred to the Appendix.

Implementation Details.

We post-train all models on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU with 141 GB of memory. Unless stated otherwise, all methods use the same backbone model, prompt pool, reward computation, and training budget to ensure a controlled comparison.

T2I Tasks and Reward Definitions.

We consider three verifiable-feedback T2I post-training tasks. For each task, we specify the prompt construction, the reward definition used for training, and the evaluation metric reported.

Visual Text Rendering.

Text appears in many images, such as posters, book covers, and memes, so accurate and readable rendering is important for T2I models.
Each prompt follows
‘‘A sign that says ‘text’’’
, where
‘text’
is the exact string that should appear.
We use GPT-4o to generate 20K training prompts and 1K test prompts.
Following
22
, we define the reward as

r=\max\bigl(1-N_{\text{e}}/N_{\text{ref}},0\bigr),

where
N_{\text{e}}
is the minimum edit distance between the rendered and target text, and
N_{\text{ref}}
is the number of characters inside the quotation marks in the prompt.
We report the same quantity as the evaluation metric.

Compositional Image Generation.

GenEval
21
evaluates compositional prompts that involve counting, spatial relations, and attribute binding across six sub-tasks.
We use the official evaluation pipeline, which detects object boxes and colors and infers spatial relations.
Training prompts are generated with the official scripts using templates and random combinations.
We deduplicate the test set so that prompts that differ only by object order, such as
‘‘A and B’’
versus
‘‘B and A’’
, are treated as the same.
We remove such variants from training.
Based on the base model’s initial accuracy, we use the following prompt ratio during training:

Position : Counting : Attribute Binding : Colors : Two Objects : Single Object = 7 : 5 : 3 : 1 : 1 : 0.

Rewards are rule-based.
For
Counting
, we use
r=1-|N_{\text{gen}}-N_{\text{ref}}|/N_{\text{ref}}
.
For
Position
and
Color
, we first require the count to be correct, then assign a partial reward when the position or color condition is satisfied.

Human Preference Alignment.

We use
PickScore

27
as the reward model.
It is trained on human-annotated pairwise preferences for images generated from the same prompt.
For each prompt–image pair,
PickScore
outputs a scalar reward that reflects prompt alignment and visual quality.

Training Prompts and Evaluation Benchmarks.

For each task, we train on the corresponding prompt set described above and evaluate on its held-out benchmark split using the task-specific metric.
All methods are evaluated under the same prompts, reward computation, and inference settings unless stated otherwise.

Baselines.

We compare
SuperFlow
with GRPO-based post-training for flow matching models and other representative post-training baselines.
All baselines use the same backbone, task prompts, and reward models to ensure fair comparisons.

5.3
Analysis

Entropy Collapse.

A key motivation of our design is to prevent entropy collapse during late-stage post-training, where policy outputs become less diverse and many prompts receive nearly identical rewards.
To this end, we use
Streaming-to-Group Sampling
to increase the number of samples per prompt only when needed, so that group-based normalization remains informative while retaining the efficiency of streaming updates.
This behavior is reflected by the consistent improvements across three tasks in Table
2
, Table
1
, and Table
3
.
Empirically, it delays early saturation of the reward distribution and maintains informative gradients across diverse prompts.

Training Efficiency.

Appendix B
Theoretical Analysis

B.1
SDE Sampling as an Episodic MDP

We cast a single denoising trajectory of a flow (SDE) model as a finite-horizon
Markov Decision Process (MDP) with horizon
T
.
A conditioning variable (e.g., a text prompt) is denoted by
c
and treated as fixed context.
The induced MDP is characterized by
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T},r,\pi_{\phi})
as follows.

State.

At timestep
t
, the state is the latent variable

s_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}.

When needed, we augment the state with the timestep and context,

\tilde{s}_{t}=(s_{t},t,c)
; for brevity we continue to write
s_{t}
.

Action and Policy.

Actions are additive increments, consistent with discrete SDE/flow updates.
At each step, the agent samples
a_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}
from a stochastic policy

a_{t}\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid s_{t})\;\triangleq\;\mathcal{N}\!\big(\mu_{t}(s_{t};\phi),\,\Sigma_{t}(s_{t};\phi)\big),

(13)

where
\Sigma_{t}
may be isotropic (
\Sigma_{t}\approx\sigma_{t}^{2}I
), diagonal, or full covariance.

Transition (Dynamics).

Conditioned on
(s_{t},a_{t})
, the next state evolves as

s_{t+1}\;=\;\Phi_{t}(s_{t};\theta)\;+\;a_{t},

where
\Phi_{t}(\cdot;\theta)
denotes the deterministic drift term parameterized by
the flow/score network (e.g., an Euler–Maruyama or ODE discretization step).
The corresponding transition kernel is the degenerate distribution

\mathcal{T}(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{t})\;=\;\delta\!\big(s_{t+1}-(\Phi_{t}(s_{t};\theta)+a_{t})\big).

Reward.

The cumulative return is

R_{0:T}\;=\;\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})\;+\;r_{T}(s_{T}).

In our default setting, only the terminal reward
r_{T}(s_{T})
is provided by the reward model,
so
R_{0:T}=r_{T}(s_{T})
.

Value, Action Value, and Advantage.

Under policy
\pi_{\phi}
, the value functions at time
t
with respect to the remaining return
R_{t:T}
are

\displaystyle V_{t}(s)

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\phi}}\!\big[R_{t:T}\mid s_{t}=s\big],

\displaystyle Q_{t}(s,a)

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\phi}}\!\big[R_{t:T}\mid s_{t}=s,\,a_{t}=a\big],

\displaystyle A_{t}(s,a)

\displaystyle\triangleq Q_{t}(s,a)-V_{t}(s).

By construction,

\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid s)}[A_{t}(s,a)]=0,\quad\text{since}\quad V_{t}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid s)}[Q_{t}(s,a)].

For variance reduction we also consider the centered trajectory-level signal

A^{\tau}\;\triangleq\;R_{0:T}-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\phi}}[R_{0:T}],

which serves as an episode-level scalar baseline that can be redistributed across steps.
Any step weight
w_{t}
that is independent of
a_{t}
preserves unbiased policy gradients,
since
\mathbb{E}_{a_{t}}[w_{t}A^{\tau}]=w_{t}\,\mathbb{E}[A^{\tau}]=0
.

B.2
One-Step Reverse SDE Update and Log-Probability

We formalize the single-step update used by our scheduler.

Scheduler indices and step size.

Let
\{\sigma_{j}\}_{j=0}^{J}
be a monotone noise schedule. A discrete timestep maps to index
i=\texttt{index\_for\_timestep}(\text{timestep})
and its previous index
i^{-}=i+1
. Define

\sigma_{t}=\sigma_{i},\quad\sigma_{t^{-}}=\sigma_{i^{-}},\quad\Delta t=\sigma_{t^{-}}-\sigma_{t}\ (<0\ \text{typically}).

Noise scale.

With
\texttt{noise\_level}\in(0,1]
, set

\kappa_{t}=\texttt{noise\_level}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{t}}{1-\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}}},\quad\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}=\begin{cases}\sigma_{\max},&\sigma_{t}=1,\\
\sigma_{t},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Deterministic mean.

For
s_{t},m_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\bar{s}_{t^{-}}=\Bigl(1+\frac{\kappa_{t}^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}}\Delta t\Bigr)s_{t}+\Bigl(1+\frac{\kappa_{t}^{2}(1-\sigma_{t})}{2\sigma_{t}}\Bigr)m_{t}\Delta t.

Stochastic update.

Sample
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
:

s_{t^{-}}=\bar{s}_{t^{-}}+\kappa_{t}\sqrt{-\Delta t}\,\varepsilon,\quad s_{t^{-}}\mid s_{t},m_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{s}_{t^{-}},\kappa_{t}^{2}(-\Delta t)I_{d}).

Log-probability.

The conditional log-density of
s_{t^{-}}
is

\log p(s_{t^{-}}\mid s_{t},m_{t})=-\frac{\|s_{t^{-}}-\bar{s}_{t^{-}}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\kappa_{t}^{2}(-\Delta t)}-\frac{d}{2}\log\bigl(2\pi\kappa_{t}^{2}(-\Delta t)\bigr).

In practice, we average the element-wise log-probability over non-batch axes to obtain a per-sample scalar.

B.3
Step-level Advantage Estimation via Standard Deviation

GRPO: trajectory-level signal for step-wise updates.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) aggregates a trajectory-level signal and uses it
to approximate step-wise advantages. Given a group of
G
trajectories sharing the same
conditioning
c
, a per-trajectory
z
-scored return is

\hat{A}^{\,i}_{\mathrm{traj}}\;\triangleq\;\frac{R(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\,i},c)-\mathrm{mean}\big(\{R(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\,j},c)\}_{j=1}^{G}\big)}{\mathrm{std}\big(\{R(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\,j},c)\}_{j=1}^{G}\big)}.

(14)

The baseline distributes
\hat{A}^{\,i}_{\mathrm{traj}}
uniformly to all steps,
i.e.,
\hat{A}^{\,i}_{t}=w_{t}\hat{A}^{\,i}_{\mathrm{traj}}
with
w_{t}\equiv 1
.
However, the true per-step advantages can vary strongly over time: early-step perturbations
often produce larger changes in the final image than late-step perturbations.

Standard-deviation-based step scaling.

To better approximate step-wise advantages with low cost, we redistribute the
trajectory-level signal using standard-deviation weights:

\hat{A}_{t}\;=\;w_{t}\cdot A^{\tau},\qquad w_{t}\;=\;\eta\,\sigma_{t}^{\gamma},

(15)

where
A^{\tau}=R_{0:T}-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\phi}}[R_{0:T}]
and
\sigma_{t}
is the per-step
standard deviation of the action distribution in (
13
).
In the isotropic case,
\Sigma_{t}=\sigma_{t}^{2}I
; in the diagonal case, we take element-wise
standard deviations from
\Sigma_{t}
and aggregate to a scalar (e.g., by mean).
We show in Sec.
B.4
that (
15
) yields a smaller
mean-squared error (MSE) to the true step advantages than the uniform rule
w_{t}=1
,
under standard small-noise and local-linear assumptions.

B.4
Proof: Variance-Weighted Trajectory Advantage Reduces Stepwise MSE

Setting.

Consider discrete-time flow/SDE dynamics with additive actions

\displaystyle s_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{t}(s_{t};\theta)+a_{t},

\displaystyle a_{t}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t},\Sigma_{t}),\qquad t=0,\dots,T-1.

Let the terminal reward be
R=r(s_{T})
and define the trajectory-centered signal

A^{\tau}:=R-\mathbb{E}[R]
.
The true stepwise advantage is

\displaystyle A_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})

\displaystyle:=Q_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})-V_{t}(s_{t}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{a_{t}\sim\pi_{t}(\cdot\mid s_{t})}[A_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})]

\displaystyle=0.

Write the centered action noise as
\Delta_{t}:=a_{t}-\mu_{t}
.

Assumption (local linearization and stepwise independence).

Along the realized trajectory, approximate the trajectory-level and step-level advantages
by a first-order influence expansion:

\displaystyle A^{\tau}

\displaystyle\;\approx\;\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}G_{k}^{\top}\Delta_{k},

(16)

\displaystyle A_{t}

\displaystyle\;\approx\;G_{t}^{\top}\Delta_{t},

where
\{\Delta_{k}\}
are independent across steps.
Here
G_{t}
is the sensitivity of
R
to
a_{t}
:

G_{t}\;\triangleq\;\Big(\tfrac{\partial s_{t+1}}{\partial a_{t}}\Big)^{\!\top}\Big(\tfrac{\partial s_{T}}{\partial s_{t+1}}\Big)^{\!\top}\nabla r(s_{T}).

Estimators and objective.

Compare
\widehat{A}_{t}^{\mathrm{plain}}:=A^{\tau}
with
\widehat{A}_{t}^{\mathrm{var}}:=w_{t}\,A^{\tau}
,
where
w_{t}
is independent of
a_{t}
. Define the MSE

\mathcal{E}_{\bullet}(t):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[(A_{t}-\widehat{A}_{t}^{\bullet})^{2}\right].

Optimal one-dimensional projection.

Approximating
A_{t}
by
\alpha A^{\tau}
yields

\alpha_{t}^{\star}=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(A_{t},A^{\tau})}{\mathrm{Var}(A^{\tau})}=\frac{G_{t}^{\top}\Sigma_{t}G_{t}}{\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}G_{k}^{\top}\Sigma_{k}G_{k}}.

For isotropic
\Sigma_{t}=\sigma_{t}^{2}I
,
\alpha_{t}^{\star}\propto\sigma_{t}^{2}\,\|G_{t}\|^{2}
.

MSE identity.

For any scalar
w
,

\mathbb{E}\!\big[(A_{t}-wA^{\tau})^{2}\big]=\mathbb{E}\!\big[(A_{t}-\alpha_{t}^{\star}A^{\tau})^{2}\big]+\mathrm{Var}(A^{\tau})\,(w-\alpha_{t}^{\star})^{2}.

Thus the MSE is minimized at
w=\alpha_{t}^{\star}
.

Choice of weights.

Since
G_{t}
is not available, we use policy variance as a surrogate:

w_{t}=c\,\sigma_{t}^{\gamma}\ \ \text{(isotropic)},\qquad w_{t}\propto\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_{t})\ \ \text{(anisotropic)}.

In common schedules,
\sigma_{t}
decreases with
t
and
\|G_{t}\|
does not increase,
so
w_{t}
correlates with
\alpha_{t}^{\star}
, whereas
w_{t}\equiv 1
does not.

Comparison to the baseline.

If
|w_{t}-\alpha_{t}^{\star}|<|1-\alpha_{t}^{\star}|
, then

\mathbb{E}\!\left[(A_{t}-w_{t}A^{\tau})^{2}\right]\;<\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[(A_{t}-A^{\tau})^{2}\right].

Therefore, variance-weighted trajectory advantages reduce stepwise MSE relative to the uniform rule.

Unbiasedness and variants.

Because
w_{t}
is independent of
a_{t}
,

\mathbb{E}_{a_{t}}[\widehat{A}_{t}(w_{t})]=w_{t}\,\mathbb{E}[A^{\tau}]=0,

so REINFORCE/GRPO gradients remain unbiased.
If a sensitivity proxy
\widehat{I}_{t}\approx\|G_{t}\|^{2}
is available, a closer surrogate is

w_{t}\propto\sigma_{t}^{\gamma}\widehat{I}_{t}
.
