Describing patterns of delirium in alcohol withdrawal and in non-alcohol withdrawal: An observational study in a tertiary care rural hospital
Authors: Shobhit Mathur, Kshirod K. Mishra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Delirium is an acute neuropsychiatric syndrome with variable presentations depending on etiology. Alcohol withdrawal delirium (AWD) typically presents as hyperactive subtype, while non-alcohol withdrawal delirium (NAWD) is more heterogeneous.Aim:To compare clinical patterns and severity of delirium in AWD and NAWD in a rural tertiary care hospital.Methods:A cross-sectional study of 120 patients with delirium referred for psychiatric evaluation was carried out. Delirium Etiology Checklist, Richmond Agitation Sedation Scale (RASS), and Delirium Rating Scale-Revised-98 (DRS-R-98) were the tools used. Statistical analysis employed SPSS v 27.0.Results:AWD accounted for 17% of cases, all hyperactive, while NAWD showed mixed patterns of presentation. DRS-R-98 scores were significantly higher in AWD. Hypoactive delirium correlated with sepsis and cardiac decompensation.Conclusion:AWD demonstrates severe hyperactive features, while NAWD is varied and frequently hypoactive, underscoring the need for tailored approaches in resource-limited settings.

INTRODUCTION

Delirium is a severe acute-onset neuropsychiatric syndrome characterized by fluctuating disturbances in attention, awareness, and cognition.[12] It is prevalent among hospitalized patients, particularly the elderly, and is associated with increased morbidity, prolonged hospitalization, and mortality.[13] Despite its clinical importance, delirium often remains under-recognized, especially in rural settings with limited resources and awareness.[2]

Subtypes of delirium comprise of hyperactive, hypoactive, and mixed forms. Hyperactive delirium is marked by agitation, restlessness, and hallucinations, whereas hypoactive delirium manifests with lethargy and reduced responsiveness.[345] Alcohol withdrawal delirium (AWD), resulting from abrupt cessation in chronic alcohol consumption, is driven by GABAergic and glutamatergic dysregulation and typically presents as hyperactive delirium.[67] Untreated AWD can lead to seizures, autonomic instability, and increased risk of mortality.[68]

Non-alcohol withdrawal delirium (NAWD) encompasses diverse etiologies such as metabolic disturbances, sepsis, and organ dysfunction.[910] NAWD has heterogenous patterns, having both hypoactive as well as hyperactive form. Hypoactive forms predominate in systemic illness and older patients, complicating recognition, and management.[410] This study aimed to compare patterns and severity of AWD and NAWD in a rural tertiary healthcare setting for early screening and management strategies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

It was a cross-sectional observational study conducted over a period of 18 months (June 2022–December 2023) at a rural tertiary care hospital. Adults aged 18 years and above, diagnosed with delirium referred from various clinical departments for psychiatric evaluation were included in the study. While intubated patients and caregivers unwilling to give consent, were excluded from the study.

Each patient was assessed by a trained psychiatry resident and consultant psychiatrist, diagnosed as per ICD-10. Data on sociodemographic characteristics, medical history, alcohol use history, and clinical presentation were documented. Laboratory investigations, including liver function tests, renal function tests and serum electrolytes, were analyzed to determine underlying medical conditions.

The study hypothesized the frequency of delirium in the population to be 24% as observed by Grover et al. (2014),[11] the population size was taken to be 100000 with a standard error of 8%, having a design effect of 1, and the confidence level of 95%. Considering all these parameters the total sample size came out to be 114 and for convenience total 120 samples were collected.

Assessment tools

Delirium Etiology Checklist (DEC): A tool used to identify and categorize the underlying medical causes of delirium. Each patient underwent a thorough clinical and laboratory evaluation to establish contributing etiologies.[12]

Richmond Agitation Sedation Scale (RASS): Used to subtype delirium into hyperactive or hypoactive subtypes. A RASS score of +1 to +4 was indicative of hyperactive delirium, while a score of −1 to −4 signified hypoactive delirium.[13]

Delirium Rating Scale-Revised-98 (DRS-R-98): Applied to assess delirium severity and symptomatology, including perceptual disturbances, cognitive impairment, and motor activity changes.[14]

Data were compiled and analyzed using SPSS version 27.0. Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, and frequency distribution, were utilized to summarize demographic and clinical data as well as Fisher’s Exact Test was used to compare categorical variables like type of delirium and underlying etiological factors, especially where subgroup sizes were less than 5. Unpaired t test was used to compare continuous variables, particularly DRS-R-98 symptom scores, between alcohol withdrawal and non-alcohol withdrawal groups, P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee prior to data collection (MGIMS/IEC/PSY/25/2023). Informed consent was obtained from patient’s primary caregivers before participation. Confidentiality was maintained, and no identifying information was included in the analysis.

RESULTS

The study population had a mean age of 48.2 ± 15.96 years, the mean age of AWD was found to be 38.0 ± 11.89 years and non-alcohol withdrawal group was 52.67 ± 15.74 years respectively, with a male predominance (89.5%). Most patients were in the age group of 30–49 years (46.6%), followed by 50–69 years (31.7%) and only 13.3% were above 70 years of age. Most patients were referred from the Medicine ICU (41.7%), followed by the Psychiatry Ward (20.8%), and Surgery ICU (15.8%). Among the 120 patients, 17% had alcohol withdrawal as the primary etiological factor of delirium, while 83% had non-alcohol-related causes. The leading non-alcohol-related etiological factors included metabolic disturbances (22.8%), renal derangement (11.6%), hepatic dysfunction (9.8%), anemia (12.5%), and sepsis (2.2%).

Subtyping of delirium was conducted by using RASS as the positive values were taken as hyperactive and negative values were taken as hypoactive. Hyperactive delirium was the most common subtype, occurring in all of alcohol withdrawal cases and 64% of non-alcohol withdrawal cases. It was characterized by symptoms such as severe agitation, restlessness, hallucinations, and increased psychomotor activity. Hypoactive delirium, which presented with lethargy, reduced motor activity, and diminished responsiveness, was seen in 17% of non-alcohol withdrawal cases and none in alcohol withdrawal cases. AWD was strongly associated with hyperactive delirium (P = 0.0047). Cardiac decompensation (P = 0.0127) and sepsis (P = 0.0112) were significantly correlated with hypoactive delirium, as described in Table 1.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates clear differences in delirium patterns based on etiology. AWD consistently presented as hyperactive delirium with higher DRS-R-98 scores, featuring prominent perceptual disturbances and motor agitation. Grover et al. (2013)[6] reported similar findings in patients of AWD, highlighting severe agitation, hallucinations, and autonomic hyperactivity. Kattimani and Bharadwaj (2013) also emphasized the need for early recognition and benzodiazepine-based management due to hyperactive presentation of AWD.[7]

Conversely, NAWD in this study displayed heterogeneous subtypes, with hypoactive delirium significantly associated with systemic illnesses such as sepsis and cardiac decompensation. Inouye et al. (2014)[1] similarly reported that hypoactive delirium is more common in medically ill and older patients, often leading to under-recognition and worse outcomes. Saxena and Lawley highlighted that hypoactive forms are frequently missed in clinical practice, contributing to increased morbidity and mortality.[2]

Van Der Mast (1998) described the role of systemic inflammation caused by septic foci in disrupting neurotransmission causing intraneuronal signal transduction by the second messenger systems, and secretion of cytokines in the brain, leading to delirium having hypoactive presentations.[9] Atterton et al.[15] (2020) also identified sepsis as a key driver of hypoactive delirium in critical care. Ampadu and Morley (2015) suggested that cardiac dysfunction reduces cerebral perfusion, worsening cognitive impairment and contributing to hypoactive delirium.[16] Renal dysfunction has a link to hypoactive delirium in this study aligns with Pang et al. (2022),[17] suggesting that uremic toxins and systemic inflammation contribute to neurocognitive decline. Hepatic dysfunction was associated with perceptual disturbances in AWD, consistent with Planas-Ballvé et al. (2017)[18] findings on neurotoxicity in hepatic encephalopathy causing hallucinations and confusion.

These findings underscore the need for tailored management strategies. Grover et al. (2013)[6] highlighted that AWD requires vigilant monitoring and sedative management to prevent complications. Rosen et al. (2024)[19] have recommended proactive screening and early mobilization for hypoactive delirium to improve outcomes. Zhou et al. (2025)[20] demonstrated that early mobilization can significantly reduce the incidence of hypoactive delirium in critically ill patients.

Some of the limitations of the study included a single-centric design and cross-sectional nature, and overlapping etiologies that may have had hypoactive, hyperactive as well as mixed presentations.

Future multicentric longitudinal studies may have different categorizations for different etiological variables causing the delirium, to be more precise in designating a specific cause for it, leading to a better understanding of delirium trajectories and standardize screening and treatment protocols.
