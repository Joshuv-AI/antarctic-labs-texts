A systematic review of the clinical correlates of delusions in Parkinson's disease.
Authors: Tarini Sharma, Abhishek Lenka, Emma McLachlan, Jennifer Foley, Suzanne Reeves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionResearch within Parkinson's disease (PD) psychosis has mostly focused on hallucinations due to their higher prevalence, limiting our understanding of the clinical correlates of delusions. This review aims to systematically review the content and clinical correlates of delusions in PD, including demographic, motor and non-motor symptoms and medication.MethodFollowing PRISMA Guidelines, a search on Ovid Medline, PsycINFO, Web of Science and Embase was performed to include English language studies evaluating delusions in PD. Data extraction included study and patient characteristics, clinical factors, outcome measures and cognitive correlates. Risk of bias was assessed for each study.ResultsSeven studies were eligible. Persecutory delusions were most commonly reported; and one study focused solely on delusional misidentification. Delusional jealousy was associated with younger age and shorter disease duration, and a single study reported a higher prevalence in men. Delusions were associated with motor symptoms but less strongly than hallucinations, neither were delusions associated with cognitive deficits, apart from delusional misidentification, which was associated with deficits in verbal memory and language. A diagnosis of depression and the severity of anxiety symptoms predicted the presence and number of delusional types. Dopamine agonists were commonly prescribed, but there was no association with prescribed dose.ConclusionThe small number and heterogeneity of the included studies limited interpretation and generalizability of our findings and emphasize the need for further investigation of the intersection of age, sex and dopamine agonist use in delusional jealousy, neuropsychological correlates of delusional misidentification and affective and psychological correlates of delusions.Keywords: Parkinson's, Delusions, Psychosis, Dopamine agonists, Cognition, Affective, Delusional jealousy, Delusional misidentification

Introduction

Overview and clinical features of parkinson's disease

Parkinson's disease (PD) is the second most common neurological illness linked with ageing [1] and is characterized by degeneration of the substantia nigra pars compacta, loss of dopaminergic neurons [2], [3] and accumulation of misfolded α-synuclein (α-syn), a neuronal protein forming intraneuronal Lewy bodies (LB) and Lewy neurites PD [4]. Key motor symptoms for diagnosis include bradykinesia, rigidity, and resting tremor, as described in the updated International Parkinson's and Movement Disorder Society (MDS) criteria. However, PD is not solely a motor disorder, as a range of non-motor symptoms manifest across the disease course [3], [5] . This includes neuropsychiatric disorders (psychosis, mood disorders, apathy, impulse control disorders) [5], [6] cognitive decline (memory, language, attention, executive functions, sleep disorders (rapid eye movement sleep behaviour disorder (RBD), daytime somnolence and insomnia), autonomic dysfunction and sensory disturbances such as pain, abnormal sensations, and olfactory deficits (hyposmia) [7], [8]. Non-motor symptoms have a significant impact on quality of life and the social and economic burden on patients and families [9] .

Parkinson's disease psychosis (PDP)

PD has an estimated prevalence of 40% [10] and encompasses illusions, minor hallucinations (sense of passage and presence) [11] complex visual hallucinations (commonly animals, objects, or people), and other perceptual disturbances [12], [13] . These experiences can be distressing, particularly when they persist or when insight is lost due to deteriorating cognitive function [14] Delusions (fixed false beliefs) occur in 5–15% [15] , and may involve persecution, jealousy (spousal infidelity), theft, harm, and grandiosity [16] . PDP is associated with a poorer prognosis, in terms of quality of life, carer burden, emergent dementia, and earlier institutionalization [17], [18] .

Current research on delusions in PD and gaps in the literature

Research within PDP has mostly focused on hallucinations due to their higher prevalence, limiting our understanding of the nature and clinical correlates of delusions. Published reviews provide information largely from case reports [19], [20], [21]. Others have included delusions in the broader context of non-motor symptoms or psychosis, reporting limited information on delusions and/or small sample sizes (<20) [22], [23], [24] . The exception to this is a large case series of 67 patients with ‘Othello’ syndrome (delusional jealousy, belief spousal infidelity), who were younger and had a shorter illness duration than those without [19] ; and a systematic review by De Michele et al. [21] reported a greater frequency of visual hallucinations and a trend toward a greater frequency of depression in those with Othello syndrome. The impact of psychosis on caregiver burden in PD is well documented [25], [26] , but there is a dearth of research focusing specifically on delusions.

Objectives

This study aimed to systematically review the content and clinical correlates of delusions in PD, including relationships with demographic variables, motor and non-motor symptoms, and use of dopaminergic treatments. A secondary aim was to investigate the relationship between delusions and levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD), and the impact of delusions on carers.

Methodology

Protocol and registration

The review adhered to the guidelines in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement (see Fig. 1) [27] . The protocol was made available in the PROSPERO registry: CRD42024529413 before the review's official commencement. No ethical approval was required.

PRISMA CHART.

Electronic databases and search strategy

A systematic electronic search (25th April 2024) was carried out using Ovid MEDLINE®, Embase (Ovid), PsycINFO (Ovid), and Web of Science, for English language publications and no limits imposed on publication dates. Only original research published in peer-reviewed publications was selected. The search syntax was optimized for each database, centering on three concepts: Delusions, Parkinson's disease, and non-motor symptoms, including anxiety, apathy, memory, sleep, fatigue, depression, appetite, impulsivity, and mood. Using Boolean operators, these terms and their permutations were entered into a search matrix. Reference lists of articles included in the studies were further reviewed for additional eligible studies (Refer to Supplementary material for the complete electronic search strategy for all databases).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) Males and females aged ≥18 years, (2) documented active or past delusions, (3) formal PD diagnosis (E.g., MDS clinical diagnostic criteria or UK brain bank criteria). Studies were excluded if (1) participants had other neurodegenerative disorders, (2) delirium, (3) a prior diagnosis of schizophrenia or psychotic illness, or psychosis characterized solely by hallucinations (4) had fewer than 10 participants with delusions. This threshold was selected as small number of cases were unlikely to provide meaningful evaluation of clinical correlates.

We included studies using any research design (longitudinal, cross-sectional, case-control and observational studies) with both aggregate and single-subject data. No restrictions were placed on considering PD or delusions as either the exposure or outcome variables.

Primary outcomes of interest were the clinical correlates of delusions, with a focus on demographics, duration and severity of motor symptoms, medication, and non-motor symptoms (anxiety, depression, sexual function, sleep disturbance, impulsivity, and cognition). Secondary outcomes included the impact of delusions on caregivers and overall quality of life. Studies that reported at least one of these outcomes were included.

Screening and assessment for eligibility

After duplicates were removed, publications were screened independently by TS and SR. Those not meeting eligibility criteria included editorials, dissertations/theses, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses. Full-text articles were similarly screened and reviewed by TS and SR.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Using a standardized data extraction form, TS and co-rater AL independently extracted data on: bibliographic information (authors, year of publication, and country of origin), demographics (sex, age, age of onset of Parkinson's disease), study characteristics (sample size, study design, follow up time, outcome measures and assessment tools), delusional content, comorbidities, and key findings. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with SR.

Risk of Bias assessment

TS and AL independently rated the methodological quality and risk of bias of studies using the Quality Assessment Tool for Quantitative Studies created by The Effective Public Health Practice Project (EPHPP) (Project, 1998a). Sections (A) Selection bias, (B) Study design, and (C) Confounders. (E) data collecting methods, and specific questions 3 and 4 in the (H) analysis were assessed. An overall grade of’ strong’, ‘moderate’, or ‘weak’ was determined based on the results in each subsection (Project, 1998b). Sections D, F, G, and parts of H were excluded as their focus on randomized controlled trials was not relevant.

Data synthesis

Due to the small number and variability of the studies, we performed a narrative synthesis. All included studies were listed in a tables, along with their extracted variables.

Results and discussion

Study inclusion

The initial database search in April 2024 yielded 5445 records. After removing 430 duplicates, 5015 articles were screened. Of these, 102 were selected for full-text review, with an additional article (n = 1) identified through manual reference searching of the included studies. During the full-text review, 96 articles were excluded (detailed in Fig. 1). Ultimately, seven studies were included.

Study characteristics

As shown in Table A.1, six cross-sectional studies [28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33] and one prospective cohort study were included [34] . Two studies reported outcomes based on the same sample of participants [28], [33]. Six studies recruited participants from outpatient, movement disorder clinics, and one [29] involved subjects from previous epidemiological research. All involved participants with PD, and one [29] also included a sample of patients with dementia with Lewy bodies. UK Parkinson's Disease Society Brain Bank criteria and Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) (III, IV, and IV-TR) were the most commonly used diagnostic criteria. Five studies employed psychosis rating scales (see Table A.1), to measure type, content and severity of psychotic symptoms [28], [29], [30], [33], [34] including the carer-rated Neuropsychiatric Inventory [29], [30], [32] and six investigated comorbid dementia, depression, anxiety and RBD [28], [29], [30], [32], [33], [34]. All investigated the associations between concomitant medication (levodopa and dopamine agonists) and the severity and onset of psychosis. All but one study [30] investigated global cognitive function, and four [28], [29], [32], [34] evaluated specific cognitive domains.

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The mean age of participants ranged from 63 (SD = 9.3) [28], [31], [33] to 80 (SD = 5.8) years [29], [30], [32], [34]. The mean age of illness onset ranged from mean 57 (SD = 10.0) [33] to 63 years (SD = 9.7) [32], mean illness duration ranged from 7 (SD = 4.3) [28] to 10 (SD =7.2) years [30] and the proportion of males varied from 53.0 [32] to 63% [28], [33].

Of the three studies reporting educational attainment, one [34] reported a mean of 7 years (SD = 2.0) in the PD-dementia (PDD) group with delusional misidentification; and two reported an average of 15 years (SD = 2.5) in patients with psychosis [28], [33].

Six studies assessed motor symptom severity Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS Part III), while five used the Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) scale to evaluate the disease stage. UPDRS-III scores ranged from 19 (SD = 7.0) [28], [33] to 44 (SD = 11.0) [34], with [30] not reporting the values. H&Y stages ranged from 2.5 (SD = 0.8) [30] to 3.8 (SD = 0.8) [29].

Delusional content

The prevalence and content of delusions is shown in Table A.2. The proportion of patients with persecutory/paranoid delusions was reported in three studies [28], [29], [31], while two studies focused solely on content. Pacchetti et al. [31] documented delusions in 19/226 (6.6%) patients with PD, of which 12/19 (63.2%) were persecutory in nature. Factor et al., reported delusions in 23/144(16%) patients with PD, of which 8/23 (35%) experienced delusions of sin/guilt, 8/23 (35%) grandiose delusions, 7/23(30%) delusions of reference, and 5/23 (22%) persecutory delusions. Similarly, 9/48 (19%) patients reported paranoid delusions in the PDD group [29].

Delusional jealousy, the focus of one study, was present in 20/805 (2.5%) of patients [30], while one study found that, of 39/349 (11.2%) who experienced delusions, 14 (4.0%) had delusional jealousy [32]. One study of 289 patients found that 8/19 patients with delusions (42.1%) reported delusions of jealousy [31].

In a prospective study Pagonabarraga et al. described delusional misidentification in 5/30 (16.7%) patients with PDD [34]: 2 experienced reduplicative paramnesia (belief that they had been replaced in an identical/ duplicated place), 2 described Intermetamorphosis (belief that others had been transformed into different persons), and 1 described reduplication of his wife. In the group without delusional misidentification, 1 patient experienced delusional parasitosis (infestation). Aarsland et al. reported phantom border syndrome (delusional misidentification involving the belief that an intruder is living in the house) among 8/48 (17%) patients with PDD [29].

Assessment of delusions

Five studies used established or structured instruments (Table A.3), including the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI), Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms (SAPS), Parkinson Psychosis Questionnaire (PPQ), and Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) [28], [29], [30], [32], [33]. The extent to which individual delusional themes were identified and assessed differed. Some evaluated multiple predefined delusional subtypes [28], [29], [33] and others focused on specific subtypes (delusional jealousy and misidentification) [30], [31], [32], [34]. In studies involving people with dementia or cognitive impairment, assessments were predominantly caregiver/informant-rated while clinician administered scales were used in studies examining phenomenology and delusional severity. Severity ratings were reported in two studies [28], [33].

Clinical correlates

The clinical correlates of delusions are described in Table A.2.

Demographic and motor correlates

Younger age was associated with delusions, primarily delusional jealousy, in two studies [30], [32], while two studies reported older age to be associated with delusions [29], [31]. Disease duration was a predictor of psychosis [31] but no direct evidence of an association with delusions or, more specifically, delusional jealousy [28], [30], [32], [33].

A single study evaluating delusional jealousy reported that it was more common in men [32] and delusions of all types were more common in patients with motor complications (13.7% vs. 3.4%, p < 0.01), including those with delusional jealousy (subgroup analysis) (10.7% vs. 2.3%, p < 0.05) [32]. Dyskinesias were strongly associated with psychosis (p < 0.001) and, while delusions were more common in those with dyskinesia (21.2%) than those with motor fluctuations (14.2%), there was no association specifically reported between dyskinesia and delusional jealousy or other types of delusions [32]. Pacchetti et al. [31] shared a similar finding, reporting that a UPDRS III score ≥ 30 was associated with an increased risk of psychosis (OR = 2.065), in a sample who largely presented with hallucinations (30% vs. 2–6.6%),

Medication

Several studies reported an association between dopamine agonists and delusions. One study [30] found that all those with delusional jealousy were prescribed dopamine agonists (Odds Ratio = 18.1, p < 0.001) but provided no information on dosage. One study [33] reported an association between dopamine agonists and psychosis (p = 0.02) but did not differentiate between delusions and hallucinations. Of note, pramipexole and ropinirole were commonly prescribed among patients with delusional jealousy, but no statistical comparisons were made. There was less evidence of an association between overall dose of dopaminergic medication and delusions. Several studies found no association between delusions and levodopa dose, DA agonist ‘levodopa equivalent daily dose’ (DA-LEDD) or the summed dopaminergic load (total levodopa equivalent daily dose) [29]. This was observed for delusions in general and specific subtypes, including delusional jealousy [32] and delusional misidentification [34], arguing against a direct dose-response relationship. Similarly, one study [31] found no relationship between delusions and recent medication changes. Other drugs such as amantadine, monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs), SSRIs, and benzodiazepines were not associated with the presence of delusions [28], [30].

Other neuropsychiatric symptoms

Visual hallucinations were the most commonly reported non-motor symptoms. Other types of hallucinations included auditory, tactile, and olfactory hallucinations. The prevalence varied widely, ranging from 23.98% in PD (Poletti et al., 2012) to 54% in PDD [29]. One study found that 100% of their patients with delusions also experienced visual hallucinations, and these hallucinations were more severe; however, the difference in frequency between the groups was not statistically significant [34].

Factor et al. [33] used multivariate regression analysis to investigate the affective correlates of psychosis in PD, accounting for potential confounding variables (Table A.2). They found that a diagnosis of major depression (p = 0.001) was associated with both hallucinations (OR = 6.12, p = 0.001) and delusions (OR = 7.14, p = 0.001); while the severity of depression assessed using the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-II) did not predict the presence or severity of delusions, it was significantly associated with the total number of delusions (p = 0.02).

A clinical diagnosis of anxiety was not associated with the presence of delusions, but higher anxiety severity, measured using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), was associated with the presence of hallucinations (OR = 1.08, p = 0.03) and delusions (OR = 1.09, p = 0.03) and the total number of delusions (p = 0.005) [33]. Solla et al. [32] found that motor complications were associated with the presence of delusions, hallucinations, cognitive impairment (lower MMSE) and generalised anxiety disorder. However, they did not investigate or report a direct relationship between anxiety and delusions.

Pacchetti et al. [31] also controlled for several confounders (Table A.2) using multivariate logistic regression and noted that patients with delusions (n = 13) exhibited behavioural disturbances during hallucinations, including agitation (68.4%), disorientation, and aggression (31.6%) with ‘clouded sensorium’ (calling out for someone, crying out for help, shouting). They also found that RBD was associated with both hallucinations (OR = 2.73) and delusions (OR = 2.73).

Cognitive correlates

Global cognition

Five studies assessed global cognition function, using the MMSE [28], [29], [31], the Mattis Dementia Rating Scale (MDRS) [34] and the Montreal Cognitive assessment (MoCA) [33]. Pacchetti et al. [31] found that lower MMSE was an independent predictor of psychosis (OR = 3972, p = 0.0001) after adjusting for age, disease duration, motor severity, RBD, and duration of dopamine agonist treatment, whereas Factor et al. [33] found no association between MoCA scores and the presence of psychosis, in a regression analysis which adjusted for confounders. Factor et al. [28] found that lower MMSE was associated with the frequency (r = −0.53, p < 0.01) and severity (r = −0.42, p < 0.05) of hallucinations, but not delusions. Aarsland et al. [29] also reported lower MMSE scores in those with hallucinations than those without (20.8 vs 27). Lower MDRS scores were reported in those with delusional misidentification (89/144, SD = 19) than those without (105/144, SD = 14) [34], but findings were based on unadjusted group comparisons (Table A.3).

Specific cognitive domains

Of the three studies assessing specific cognitive domains [28], [29], [34], only two [28], [34] reported scores and neither adjusted for global cognition. One [28] included those with hallucinations and delusions, and one [34] focused specifically on delusional misidentification (see Table A.4).

One study reported poorer performance on language (p = 0.002), semantic fluency (p = 0.003) and both immediate (p < 0.001) and delayed recall (p = 0.03) on the RAVLT among patients with delusional misidentification but not visuospatial function [34]; and one study, which separated hallucinations and delusions [28] reported poorer performance on verbal memory, visuospatial and executive function in those with hallucinations but not delusions (Table A.4).

Risk of bias (quality assessment)

All stated their research aims and were rated as having a moderate risk of selection bias, apart from one cohort study [34]. Formal psychosis instruments were used in 5 studies, and assessment approaches varied (refer to Table B.1). Five studies [28], [30], [31], [33], [34] reported controlling for potential confounders while the remaining two did not adjust for confounding. All studies received a strong rating for data collection, indicating that the data were consistently and rigorously gathered. Although appropriate statistical methods were used across all studies, none provided power calculations to guide sample size determination.

Discussion

This review examined the clinical correlates of delusions in PD and PDD, focusing on delusional content, demographics, motor and non-motor symptoms and medication.

Aligning with previous literature [20], [35], [36] , persecutory delusions were more commonly reported in the included studies than delusional misidentification. Delusional jealousy was suggested to be associated with younger age and shorter disease duration, consistent with previous case series [37], [38] and one study reported a higher frequency of delusional jealousy among men [32]. There was however inconsistent evidence of similar associations in the context of other types of delusions, and thus findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Delusions as a whole were more common in those with motor fluctuations [32]. However, the association with motor fluctuations was more marked in those with hallucinations, consistent with the fact that hallucinations are typically associated with a longer duration of illness. Delusions did not correlate with the total daily dose of dopaminergic therapy (levodopa, dopamine agonist LEDD, or total LEDD) [29], [31], [32], [34], and neither were modifications in medication associated with the onset of delusions. Importantly, our review reflects only current dopaminergic doses and does not assess long-term exposure. These findings are not contradictory to the model of dopaminergic kindling which refers to gradual sensitization over long times but suggests other factors such as receptor sensitization, serotonergic changes, and psychological vulnerability [39] may be critical. The potential confounding effects of dopamine agonist medication need to be considered, as they are preferentially used in younger patients, to delay motor complications [40], [41] and have been previously implicated in Othello syndrome [22], [42]. Thus, the observed association between younger age and delusional jealousy may partly reflect differences in dopamine agonist exposure, rather than age alone.

Previous studies have reported an association between depression and psychosis [43], [44] , but Factor et al. [33] are the first to demonstrate the presence and extent of the association specifically between depression and delusions: A diagnosis of depression increased the likelihood of delusions around sevenfold, and the severity of depressive symptoms was correlated with the total number of types of delusions. These findings are important as depression occurs in around one-third of people with PD and is associated with a poorer prognosis, including increased disability and reduced quality of life [35], [45], [46] . The severity of anxiety was independently associated with both the presence and the number of types of delusions [33] .

Factor et al. [33] reflected on the shared pathophysiology of affective and psychotic symptom, in terms of monoaminergic neurocircuitry. Viewed from a psychological perspective contemporary models of delusion formation in the broader literature such that worry, negative cognitions regarding self and others, and perceived vulnerability may increase the propensity toward paranoid ideation in the context of anticipated criticism or rejection [47], [48] . It is tempting to speculate that the higher reported prevalence of delusional jealousy in males may reflect the cultural and socialized narratives of masculinity, which position men as protectors and link their self-esteem to sexual dominance, reinforcing the gendered-beliefs of possessiveness and power-imbalanced relationship dynamics [49] . Certainly, evolutionary researchers have noted men's greater sensitivity to beliefs around sexual infidelity [49], [50], [51] . This interpretation is however exploratory and, given the limited evidence and small sample sizes (20 cases in Poletti et al., 2012 [30]; 14 cases in Solla et al., 2011) [32], it is possible that the reported demographic patterns occurred due to chance or variability in sampling.

Consistent with the published literature, global cognitive impairment was consistently associated with hallucinations, but not delusions. Similarly, hallucinations but not delusions were associated with deficits in memory, executive, visuospatial, and language functions. This is consistent with the published literature which has highlighted the fact that cognitive impairment is less pronounced in patients with isolated delusions in PD [20], [52] . Cognitive vulnerabilities, particularly deficits in executive function (suppressing inappropriate or automatic thoughts, planning, and organizing action), may still occur even with preserved intellectual function and can reinforce delusional beliefs. Foley et al. [[53], [54]] suggests that, when these vulnerabilities interact with the overactivity of the medial limbic dopaminergic system, delusion content emerges through the excessive meaning assigned to neutral stimuli.

The finding of poorer performance on verbal memory and language in those with delusional misidentification warrants further consideration, as all patients also experienced hallucinations. Pagonabarraga et al., [34] linked these deficits to temporal lobe dysfunction, noting that PDD involves neuronal loss in paralimbic and temporal associative cortex. Neuroimaging evidence however consistently implicates frontal and right-hemispheric dysfunction in misidentification delusions, particularly Capgras as these regions support conflict resolution, familiarity signalling and semantic memory [55], [56] . In the context of Alzheimer's disease (AD), emerging evidence suggests that delusional misidentification, with or without hallucinations may represent a distinct subtype of psychosis, characterized by greater cognitive dysfunction and more marked pathology in functional networks involved in recognition and context processing compared to those with isolated persecutory delusions [57] . Future studies should consider approaching this transdiagnostically AD, DLB and PD/PDD.

The small number of included studies, heterogeneity in methodology including outcomes and assessment tools and small sample sizes is a key limitation and our findings should thus be viewed as exploratory. For example, one study demonstrated a relationship between RBD and increased risk of psychosis, but did not investigate whether earlier onset or longer duration influenced the likelihood of psychosis or more specifically delusions. This requires further investigation as previous studies have reported similar findings of persistent RBD being associated with an increased risk of both dementia and psychosis [58], [59] . Similarly, associations observed in relation to delusional jealousy require further replication in larger samples. Differences in the approach to assessment of delusions may have influenced the identification and classification of specific delusional themes. Our findings should thus be interpreted with caution.

Several studies lacked detailed information on phenomenology or on cognitive assessments, or did not account for potential confounding factors. Although medication was considered, none adjusted for the use of antipsychotic medications or cognitive enhancers, which have been shown to worsen [60] or improve [61] cognitive function in PD respectively. No studies reported on ethnicity, affecting comparability and generalizability, and the hospital-based context limits the applicability of findings to the broader population. No studies explored the impact of delusions on caregivers and this needs further investigations as qualitative studies (which were beyond the scope of this review) have highlighted the distressing impact of PD delusions, describing how loss of control, identity disruptions and fear of uncertainty contribute to distress [62], [63] .

Prospective studies are needed to overcome methodological and conceptual limitations and adequately address confounding factors. The intersection of sex, age and use of dopamine agonists needs to be investigated, particularly in relation to delusional jealousy. Similarly, the interplay between depression, anxiety and delusions requires exploration, including psychological constructs that may contribute to delusions, and their impact on spousal relationships and caregiver burden. Cognitive deficits in those with delusional misidentification should be investigated in larger samples, to distinguish between those with and without comorbid hallucinations.
