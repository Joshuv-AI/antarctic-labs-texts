Empathy as a catalyst in patient-physician communication: a moderated-mediation analysis of shared decision-making and diabetes outcomes
Authors: Maryam Peimani, Anita L. Stewart, Ensieh Nasli-Esfahani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo examine a moderated mediation model in which shared decision-making (SDM) mediates the association between patient–physician communication (PPC) and two key diabetes outcomes—medication adherence and diabetes distress— while physician empathy moderates the pathways from PPC to SDM and from SDM to outcomes.MethodsA cross-sectional survey of 1300 adults with diabetes was conducted at a specialty clinic in Tehran, Iran. Participants completed validated measures of PPC, SDM, physician empathy, medication adherence, and diabetes distress. Analyses used latent moderated structural equation modeling in Mplus to test direct, indirect, and conditional indirect effects.ResultsSDM significantly mediated the effects of both ‘hurried communication’ and ‘explained results’ (two aspects of PPC) on adherence and distress (all 95% CIs excluded zero). Empathy significantly moderated these indirect effects. The negative indirect effect of hurried communication on adherence through SDM was weaker at high empathy (B= -0.83, p < 0.001) than at low empathy (B= -1.13, p < 0.001). A similar pattern was found for distress, where the positive indirect effect of hurried communication was weaker at high empathy (B = 0.29, p < 0.001) than at low empathy (B = 0.39, p < 0.001). Conversely, the positive indirect effect of explained results on adherence through SDM was stronger at high empathy (B = 2.95, p < 0.001) than at low empathy (B = 1.35, p = 0.001).ConclusionPhysician empathy may act as a relational resource that sustains SDM when communication is hurried and enhances its benefits when treatment rationales are clearly explained. These findings have important implications for the design of communication and empathy-training interventions, suggesting that enhancing physician empathy may be a key target for improving self-management and emotional well-being in diabetes care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15203-9.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) requires sustained self-management, and consistent adherence to medications and lifestyle modifications is the key to decelerating disease progression and complication risk [1, 2]. However, the relentless attention required for these daily regimens takes a significant emotional toll, frequently manifesting as diabetes distress. This distress can reduce patient engagement, creating a cyclical relationship of diminished self-efficacy and deteriorating clinical outcomes, illustrating the importance of relational interventions that foster patient resilience and empowerment [3, 4].

Patient-physician communication (PPC) is the cornerstone of effective T2DM care, profoundly influencing patients’ understanding and motivation for sustained self-care [5]. Robust evidence demonstrates that high-quality PPC enhances medication adherence, mitigates distress and improves glycemic control [6–8]. While PPC has direct effects on patient outcomes, a complete understanding of its influence requires elucidation of the intermediary mechanisms that transmit its benefits.

Shared decision-making (SDM), a collaborative process in which healthcare providers and patients jointly make medical decisions based on the best available evidence and patients’ preferences [9], is a key intermediary in the patient-physician dynamic, with effective PPC facilitating this process [10]. For example, a study in China demonstrated that communication and trust were significant factors influencing SDM, with trust itself mediating the relationship between communication and SDM [11]. Enhancing SDM has been shown to improve decision quality, patient self-efficacy in self-care, and glycemic control [12]. Adherence is more likely when patients and physicians engage in open dialogue, share their preferences, and actively participate in decision-making [13]. Moreover, promising evidence indicates that patients with experience of SDM report significantly lower diabetes distress [4, 14]. Building on its role as a key intermediary, emerging evidence further supports SDM as a mechanism transmitting the benefits of PPC to patient outcomes. A study in a general healthcare context found that SDM mediated the impact of the patient-physician relationship on treatment compliance [15], underscoring its potential mediatory role. However, the specific pathways through which communication fosters SDM and how SDM subsequently influences T2DM outcomes such as adherence and distress, require further elucidation.

Empathy is an important component of patient-centered care and is essential for understanding and responding to patients’ emotional and experiential perspectives [16]. In T2DM, where emotional burden and relational trust are paramount, empathy has been consistently linked to greater patient satisfaction, treatment adherence, and reduced diabetes distress [3, 4, 6]. However, prior research has predominantly examined empathy as an independent predictor, overlooking its capacity to moderate the transformation of communicative input into decisional collaboration and, subsequently, into sustained behavioral enactment. In the iterative, high-stakes context of T2DM, characterized by recurrent consultations and compounding psychosocial demands, the moderating function of empathy warrants explicit examination as a scalable intervention target.

While medication adherence (a behavioral domain) and diabetes distress (an emotional domain) are conceptually distinct, they are highly intertwined in the context of chronic disease management. Drawing from Self-Determination Theory [17], which posits that psychological need satisfaction simultaneously influences both behavioral engagement and emotional well-being, we conceptualize them as parallel, simultaneous responses to the clinical encounter. High-quality PPC, translated through SDM and conditioned by physician empathy, serves as a shared upstream mechanism that concurrently supports the patient’s cognitive-behavioral needs (adherence) and emotional needs (distress mitigation). Modeling these outcomes simultaneously allows us to acknowledge their shared variance and better reflects the holistic, dual-pathway impact of SDM in real-world diabetes care.

To our knowledge, the present study is the first to integrate PPC, SDM, and physician empathy within a moderated mediation framework for T2DM. We tested a model in which SDM mediates the relationship between PPC and patient outcomes (medication adherence and diabetes distress) while physician empathy moderates both the antecedent path (from PPC to SDM) and consequent paths (from SDM to the respective outcomes). Specifically, we hypothesized that (1) PPC has positive indirect effects on medication adherence and negative indirect effects on diabetes distress via SDM and (2) physician empathy strengthens these indirect effects by moderating both the path from PPC to SDM and the path from SDM to patient outcomes (adherence and distress). By delineating these interdependent mechanisms, this study provides a resilience-oriented blueprint for enhancing self-management support and mental well-being in chronic diabetes care, with direct implications for communication training and clinical practice.

Methods

Study design, setting, and participants

This study utilized a cross-sectional design to test a hypothesized moderated mediation model. The model proposed that the relationship between patient-physician communication (PPC) and patient outcomes, including medication adherence and diabetes distress, is mediated by shared decision-making (SDM). Furthermore, it proposed that patient perception of physician empathy moderates the strength of the pathways from PPC to SDM and from SDM to the patient outcomes. The conceptual model, shown in Fig. 1, builds upon and extends the theoretical framework established by Deniz et al. 2021 [15] by incorporating empathy as a central moderator and by examining two distinct outcome domains (behavioral and psychological) simultaneously in the context of diabetes care.

Participants were recruited from a diabetes specialty clinic at Tehran University of Medical Sciences (Tehran, Iran) from November 2024 to June 2025. To be eligible, patients needed to meet the following criteria: diagnosed with T2DM ≥ 1.5 years previously; had been treated at these clinics for at least one year with at least 4–6 visits, current use of diabetes medication (oral or insulin), and ability to provide informed consent.

Sample size and data collection

Following recommendations by Westland [18] for achieving adequate statistical power in structural equation modeling (SEM), a minimum sample size of N = 1300 was determined to be necessary to detect effect sizes as small as standardized path coefficients ≥ 0.10–0.15 for the key paths in the model (including interaction effects) with 90% power at a significance level of α = 0.05. This sample size also accounts for the model’s complexity, ensuring robust parameter estimation.

Data collection occurred at a diabetes clinic with approximately 50–60 daily patients. On each working day, the researcher approached patients in the waiting area. Eligible patients were identified based on the study’s inclusion criteria, and a daily sampling frame was prepared. Participants were then selected using simple random sampling via a computer-generated random number list. Selected patients were provided with a detailed explanation of the study purpose and invited to participate. Those who agreed provided written informed consent, which assured confidentiality, anonymity of responses, and the right to withdraw at any time. Participants completed the anonymous paper-based questionnaires in private rooms. To ensure data quality and completeness, each returned questionnaire was immediately checked by the researcher, and participants were politely asked to answer any missing items before leaving the room.

This study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the medical research ethics committee of the Tehran University of Medical Sciences (IR.TUMS.EMRI.REC.1401.105).

Measurements

Patient-physician communication

Patient-physician communication was assessed using the communication domain of the validated Persian version of the Interpersonal Processes of Care (IPC) Survey [19, 20]. This domain includes three independent scales: Hurried Communication (5 items), Elicited Concerns/Responded (3 items), and Explained Results/Medications (4 items). Patients report the frequency of these interactions with their diabetes physician over the preceding 12 months. All items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never to 5 = always), and scores are averaged for each scale. The Hurried Communication scale was scored such that higher scores indicate more frequent hurried communication (i.e., poorer perceived communication quality). No items in this scale required reverse coding. In contrast, higher scores on the Elicited Concerns and Explained Results scales reflect better-quality communication (see definitions in Fig. 2). The Persian-IPC (P-IPC) has demonstrated strong construct validity and reliability in a previous study of Persian-speaking T2DM patients [20]. In the current study, the internal consistency (McDonald’s ω) for the three scales of communication domain was as follows: Hurried Communication (ω = 0.84), Elicited Concerns (ω = 0.80), and Explained Results (ω = 0.87).

Shared decision-making

The nine-item Shared Decision-Making Questionnaire (SDM-Q-9) was used to measure SDM [21]. The SDM-Q-9 is a self-report tool that evaluates a patient’s perception of the extent to which they were involved in the treatment decision-making process during a medical consultation (see Fig. 2). It comprises nine items, each rated on a 6-point Likert scale from 0 (“completely disagree”) to 5 (“completely agree”). The total score is calculated by summing all item responses, yielding a range of 0–45. Higher scores indicate more SDM. In this study, we employed the validated Persian version of the SDM-Q-9, which has demonstrated excellent psychometric properties, including high internal consistency and confirmed validity, in an Iranian oncology setting [22]. In the current study, the estimate of internal consistency was ω = 0.89.

Empathy

The Jefferson Scale of Patient Perceptions of Physician Empathy (JSPPPE-5) was used [23]. The JSPPPE-5 contains 5 items assessing patients’ perceptions of their physician’s empathy on a 7-point Likert scale (from 1 = Strongly Disagree to 7 = Strongly Agree). The total score is calculated by summing all the item responses, ranging from 5 to 35. Higher scores indicate a greater perceived level of physician empathy (see definitions in Fig. 2). Researchers utilized the validated Persian version of the JSPPPE-5, which has demonstrated strong psychometric properties in an Iranian inpatient population [24]. The scale has shown excellent internal consistency and a unidimensional factor structure, confirming its validity for use in this cultural context. In the current study, omega coefficient was obtained 0.85.

Medication adherence

Medication adherence was assessed using the 5-item Medication Adherence Report Scale (MARS-5) [25]. The MARS-5 evaluates non-adherent behaviors, including forgetting, altering doses, stopping, skipping, and taking less medication than prescribed (Fig. 2). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (always) to 5 (never); therefore, the total score ranged between 5 and 25. Higher scores indicate higher adherence. A total score < 24 points was defined as low adherence to the medication (24 and 25 points = high adherence). We used the validated Persian version of the MARS-5, which has shown satisfactory internal consistency in previous studies [26]. In this study, the internal-consistency reliability was ω = 0.80.

Diabetes distress

We employed the two-item Diabetes Distress Scale (DDS2) to assess diabetes-related distress [27]. This instrument measures the degree of distress caused by (1) feeling overwhelmed by the demands of living with diabetes and (2) feeling often failing with one’s diabetes routine. The items are rated on a 6-point Likert scale. The total score is calculated as the mean of the two items, with higher scores indicating greater distress. An average score of < 2 indicates little or no distress, 2–2.9 indicates moderate distress, and ≥ 3 indicates high distress. In this study, DDS2 demonstrated adequate reliability, with a Spearman-Brown coefficient of 0.79 (appropriate for two-item measures), well above the conventional acceptable threshold of 0.70.

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Respondents provided information on their age, gender, education level, occupation, and length of their relationship with their physician. Data on T2DM clinical history, including diagnosis, diabetes duration, treatment regimen type, presence of diabetes complications, and the most recent HbA1c levels (within the past 6 months), were extracted from electronic diabetes medical records.

Statistical analysis

Before conducting the main analyses, we examined whether the data met normality assumptions. Skewness for all individual items ranged from − 1.23 to 1.80 and kurtosis from − 1.12 to 2.54, indicating approximately normal distributions. Because all Likert-type items had five or more response categories and showed no severe skewness, we treated them as continuous using the robust Maximum Likelihood (MLR) estimator. Methodological research supports this approach, demonstrating that ordinal items with five or more categories can be safely modeled as continuous when data are not severely skewed [28]. MLR also provides robust standard errors and a scaled chi-square test statistic, making it resilient to potential deviations from multivariate normality.

Descriptive statistics were then computed for all study variables, presented as means with standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Bivariate correlations among the primary study variables (Hurried Communication, Elicited Concerns, Explained Results, shared decision-making, medication adherence, diabetes distress, and empathy) were examined using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation coefficients based on the distribution of the data. The dataset was complete for all analyzed variables, with no missing data.

A confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) employing the MLR estimator was conducted using Mplus (version 8.0) to evaluate the validity of the proposed measurement model. The model specified seven latent constructs: patient-physician communication indicated by three latent factors, including Hurried Communication (5 items), Elicited Concerns (3 items), and Explained Results (4 items); SDM (9 items); medication adherence (5 items); empathy (5 items); and diabetes distress (2 items).

Model fit was assessed using multiple indices: chi-square/df ratio ≤ 5, Comparative Fit Index (CFI) ≥ 0.90, Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) ≥ 0.90, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) ≤ 0.06 [29].

After establishing an acceptable measurement model, researchers first estimated the mediating effect of SDM on the predictive effects of PPC dimensions on patient outcomes (medication adherence and diabetes distress). Because medication adherence and diabetes distress were modeled simultaneously as parallel endogenous variables, the covariance between their residual errors was freely estimated in the structural models. This approach accounts for unobserved shared variance between the two outcomes beyond what is explained by the predictors, ensuring more robust parameter estimates.

To examine the potential moderating role of physician empathy, we employed a Latent Moderated Structural Equations (LMS) framework in Mplus to test moderated mediation models [30]. This involved estimating and comparing competing models for each dimension of PPC as predictors. For each predictor, researchers specified a baseline model that included all direct effects and the mediation pathway but no interaction terms. Researchers then specified competing models that added latent interaction terms using the XWITH command to test whether empathy moderated the a-path (communication→ SDM) and/or the b-paths (SDM→ outcomes). These models were estimated using TYPE = RANDOM, ALGORITHM = INTEGRATION, and the MLR estimator, following standard LMS procedures. Model fit was compared using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the − 2 log-likelihood (-2LL) [30]. In LMS models with numerical integration, the absolute values of −2LL are influenced by integration scaling and are not directly comparable across models. Therefore, we rely on the change in −2LL (Δ − 2LL) and AIC (ΔAIC) for model comparison, which is the standard recommended approach.

The significance of indirect effects was tested using bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples. For models showing significant moderation, conditional indirect effects were probed at low (− 1 SD), mean, and high (+ 1 SD) levels of the latent empathy variable using the MODEL CONSTRAINT command in Mplus. An effect was considered statistically significant if the 95% bias-corrected confidence interval (CI) did not include zero.

All analyses were controlled for the following covariates: age, sex, education level, communication duration, treatment regimen, and diabetes duration, which were regressed on the mediator and outcome variables. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was conducted incorporating HbA1c levels and the number of diabetes complications as further covariates to test the robustness of the model.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

A sample of 1300 participants (663 men, 637 women; mean age = 59.7 ± 12.5 years) was included in this study. The mean diabetes duration was 12.2 years, and the mean duration of patient-physician communication was 4.5 years. Nearly half of the participants (48.5%) were managed with oral antidiabetic drugs alone. The mean HbA1c was 7.58% (SD = 1.35), indicating near-target glycemic control on average. For the MARS-5 scale, 58% of the sample had scores of 24 or 25, indicating high medication adherence. The mean DDS was 2.79 ± 0.75, indicating a moderate level of diabetes distress. The mean SDM-Q-9 score was 26.19 ± 8.7, reflecting a mild-to-neutral level of perceived involvement in SDM. The average scores of the communication subscales were 3.50 for Elicited Concerns, 2.93 for Explained Results, and 1.82 for Hurried Communication, indicating fairly good distributions. The mean JSPPPE-5 score was 26.8 ± 3.8, suggesting a high level of perceived empathy (Table 1).

Correlations

Table 2 presents the bivariate correlations among the primary study variables. Hurried Communication was negatively correlated with SDM (r= -0.538) and medication adherence (r= -0.456), and positively correlated with diabetes distress (r = 0.409). In contrast, both Elicited Concerns and Explained Results were positively correlated with SDM (r = 0.304 and r = 0.689, respectively) and medication adherence (r = 0.512 and r = 0.532, respectively), and negatively correlated with diabetes distress (r= -0.518 and r= -0.351, respectively). Furthermore, SDM was positively correlated with empathy (r = 0.602) and medication adherence (r = 0.399), and negatively correlated with diabetes distress (r= -0.507). Similarly, empathy was positively correlated with medication adherence (r = 0.462) and negatively correlated with diabetes distress (r= -0.591).

Confirmatory factor analysis

A confirmatory factor analysis was performed to assess the measurement model comprising Hurried Communication, Elicited Concerns, Explained Results, empathy (JSPPPE-5), shared decision-making (SDM-Q-9), medication adherence (MARS-5), and diabetes distress (DDS-2). The model demonstrated an acceptable to good fit to the data: chi-square/df = 3.2, CFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.93, RMSEA = 0.044. All factor loadings were statistically significant (p < 0.001) and ranged from 0.53 to 0.83, indicating medium to strong relationships between the items and their respective latent factors (Supplementary Table S1).

Discriminant validity

Given the high bivariate correlation between Explained Results and SDM (r = 0.689), we further assessed their discriminant validity. Although the constructs are correlated at a surface level, their item content reveals conceptual distinctness. The items for Explained Results assess unidirectional information transfer (e.g., explaining test results, side effects, and consequences of non-adherence), whereas the SDM items assess bidirectional collaborative deliberation (e.g., eliciting patient preferences, jointly weighing options, and reaching agreement). The Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratio was 0.71, below the recommended threshold of 0.85, supporting discriminant validity. Correlations between SDM and the other communication dimensions were notably lower, indicating that the moderate-to-high correlation between Explained Results and SDM reflects a specific and theoretically expected link rather than a general measurement artifact.

Mediation analysis

As expected, the structural model indicated a significant residual covariance between medication adherence and diabetes distress (residual correlation = -0.42, p = 0.001).

We tested a mediation model to examine the indirect effects of patient-physician communication dimensions (Hurried Communication, Elicited Concerns, Explained Results) on patient outcomes (medication adherence and diabetes distress) through SDM. The model indicated that all direct pathways between the communication dimensions and outcomes were statistically significant. Path coefficients showed that Hurried Communication was significantly and negatively associated with SDM (B= -7.25, 95% CI [-10.80, -3.70], p < 0.001), and medication adherence (B= -1.850, 95% CI [-2.760, -0.940], p < 0.001), and was significantly and positively associated with diabetes distress (B = 0.46, 95% CI [0.237, 0.683], p < 0.001). Elicited Concerns was significantly and positively associated with SDM (B = 2.76, 95% CI [0.76, 4.76], p < 0.01), and medication adherence (B = 3.180, 95% CI [1.794, 4.566], p < 0.001), and was negatively associated with diabetes distress (B= -0.64, 95% CI [-0.918, -0.362], p < 0.001). Similarly, Explained Results was significantly and positively associated with SDM (B = 9.04, 95% CI [5.50, 12.58], p < 0.001) and medication adherence (B = 2.741, 95% CI [1.462, 4.018], p < 0.001), and was significantly and negatively associated with diabetes distress (B= -0.43, 95% CI [-0.640, -0.220], p < 0.001). Furthermore, SDM itself was strongly and significantly associated with both higher medication adherence (B = 0.151, 95% CI [0.085, 0.217], p < 0.001) and lower diabetes distress (B= -0.049, 95% CI [-0.068, -0.030], p < 0.001).

The results of the mediation analysis are presented in Table 3 (for medication adherence) and Table 4 (for diabetes distress). The indirect effects of Hurried Communication on both outcomes through SDM were significant, leading to lower medication adherence (B= -1.088, 95% CI [-1.778, -0.398], p = 0.002) and higher diabetes distress (B = 0.355, 95% CI [0.138, 0.572], p = 0.001). Similarly, the indirect effects of Explained Results through SDM were also significant, contributing to higher medication adherence (B = 1.356, 95% CI [0.460, 2.252], p = 0.003) and lower diabetes distress (B= -0.443, 95% CI [-0.660, -0.226], p < 0.001). In contrast, the indirect effects of Elicited Concerns on medication adherence (B = 0.414, 95% CI [-0.131, 0.959], p = 0.136) and diabetes distress (B= -0.135, 95% CI [-0.282, 0.012], p = 0.072) through SDM were not statistically significant.

Moderated mediation analysis

In this part, we conducted separate analyses per predictor for Hurried Communication and Explained Results scales, excluding Elicited Concerns due to being non-significant in mediation analysis.

Hurried communication

Following the framework of LMS, we tested a latent moderated mediation model in which empathy moderated the indirect effect of Hurried Communication on patient outcomes (medication adherence and diabetes distress) through SDM. We first estimated model 0 including all related variables but not latent variable interactions (see Fig. 3a). The model fit indices demonstrated that model 0 fitted acceptably with RMSEA = 0.052, CFI = 0.91, and TLI = 0.92. Then, model 1 was tested including all related variables and the latent variable interactions (Hurried × empathy→ SDM, SDM × empathy→ adherence, SDM × empathy→ distress) (see Fig. 3b). As a means of selecting between nested models, we used AIC and − 2LL, where lower values indicate a better-fitting model. Compared to Model 0, the value of AIC decreased by 12,744 and the value of -2LL decreased by 12,750, which showed a significant difference and indicated that Model 1 fit better than Model 0.

Explained results

In this part, we tested the latent moderated mediation model in which empathy moderated the indirect effect of Explained Results on patient outcomes (medication adherence and diabetes distress) through SDM. We first estimated model 2 including all related variables but not latent variable interactions (Fig. 3c). The model fit indices demonstrated that model 2 fitted acceptably with RMSEA = 0.051, CFI = 0.92, and TLI = 0.91. Then, model 3 was tested including all related variables and the latent variable interactions (Explained × empathy→ SDM, SDM × empathy→ adherence, SDM × empathy→ distress) (Fig. 3d). Compared to Model 2, the value of AIC decreased by 13,135.65 and the value of -2LL decreased by 13,139.65, which showed a significant difference and indicated that Model 3 fit better than Model 2.

Following this, we examined the individual interaction terms within Model 3. Only the interaction SDM × empathy→ adherence was significant (B = 0.15, SE = 0.04, p < 0.001), suggesting that empathy moderated the path between SDM and medication adherence. The other two interactions, Explained × empathy→ SDM (p = 0.37) and SDM × empathy→ distress (p = 0.21) were not significant (Table 5).

We also estimated the conditional indirect effect of Explained Results on medication adherence under different levels of empathy. The results demonstrated that the positive indirect effect of Explained Results on medication adherence through SDM was stronger at higher levels of empathy (B = 2.95, 95% CI [2.07, 3.83], p < 0.001) and weaker at lower levels of empathy (B = 1.35, 95% CI [0.76, 1.94], p = 0.001) (see Table 6).

In summary, empathy significantly moderated the association between SDM and medication adherence. Consequently, the positive indirect effect of Explained Results on medication adherence through SDM was stronger at higher levels of empathy.

Discussion

The present study examined the complicated relationship between patient-physician communication and patient outcomes in T2DM, testing the mediating role of SDM and the moderating influence of physician empathy at the same time. Research findings are consistent with a model in which patient-physician communication is associated with patient outcomes in T2DM through a dual-process mechanism. SDM is a crucial mediator that channels effective communication into enhanced medication adherence and reduced diabetes distress. Nonetheless, physician empathy emerges not as a consistent enhancer but as a context-dependent one. When communication quality is suboptimal, empathy may act as a protective buffer, reducing negative impacts on SDM. However, when communication is effective, empathy may amplify the translation of shared decisions into successful self-management and psychological well-being. This, consistent with previous research, suggests that empathy is key to fostering resilience against communication challenges [31] and optimizing the benefits of positive clinical interactions, highlighting its potential value in patient-centered diabetes care [32].

To further elucidate this dual-process mechanism, we first investigated the potential mediating role of the SDM in linking patient-physician communication and patient outcomes. The results suggest that SDM acts as a significant mediator, although its function varies by communication style. The Hurried Communication dimension of PPC was associated with a detrimental indirect effect on patient outcomes. This style of communication was negatively related to the SDM process, which, in turn, correlated with both poorer medication adherence and greater diabetes distress. Conversely, the Explained Results dimension exhibited a beneficial indirect pathway. Here, the provision of clear, informative explanations positively correlated with SDM, which was associated with enhanced adherence and reduced distress. These results underscore SDM’s central role as a potential mechanism linking specific communication behaviors to concrete health outcomes. This finding expands previous research that established a general association between SDM and outcomes in diabetes [4, 12, 33]. Our study therefore suggests that this proposed mediating pathway is responsive to communication quality: it appears to be supported by clear information-sharing (Explained Results) but hindered by interactions perceived as rushed and inattentive (Hurried Communication).

Notably, the Elicited Concerns dimension influenced adherence and distress through direct pathways, with no significant mediation via SDM. While Explained Results and the absence of Hurried Communication provide the actionable information and time necessary for cognitive deliberation (the core of SDM), Elicited Concerns represents a different mechanism—one rooted in affective, rather than instrumental, communication. Eliciting concerns primarily facilitates patient-to-physician disclosure, which builds relational trust [11] and emotional relief [34], but does not inherently generate the reciprocal exchange of medical options required to trigger collaborative decision-making.

Viewed through Self-Determination Theory, eliciting concerns directly satisfies the need for Relatedness (feeling understood), exerting a direct, independent effect on distress and adherence. In contrast, the other communication dimensions actively facilitate the Autonomy and Competence required for SDM. This interpretation aligns with theoretical models separating affective and instrumental dimensions of patient-centered communication [35], placing Elicited Concerns on the affective, relationship-building side that bypasses the cognitive SDM pathway.

Having explored these distinct mediation pathways, we next examined how physician empathy might moderate these associations. Physician empathy played two distinct moderating roles in the pathways examined. First, it acted as a buffer by significantly moderating the a-path from Hurried Communication to SDM. Second, it functioned as an amplifier by significantly moderating the b-path from SDM to medication adherence in the context of Explained Results.

In line with the idea that empathy is a multifaceted resource in clinical practice [36], our findings showed that empathy’s function was context-dependent rather than uniform. In the context of Hurried Communication, empathy was associated with a protective role, significantly buffering its negative relationship with the SDM process. This finding aligns with studies suggesting that emotional support can mitigate the detrimental correlates of negative interpersonal encounters in healthcare settings [37, 38]. As a result, the negative indirect associations of rushed conversations with both medication adherence and diabetes-related distress were notably attenuated for patients who perceived their physicians as more empathetic.

Conversely, in the context of Explained Results, empathy was associated with a different but equally important pattern of association, suggesting an amplifying relationship. Specifically, empathy did not appear to moderate the association between Explained Results and SDM. Instead, it appeared to moderate the subsequent pathway, where the positive association between SDM and medication adherence was significantly stronger for patients perceiving higher physician empathy. This finding suggests that when patients feel understood and cared for, the positive relationship between co-created care plans and self-management behaviors is more robust. This is consistent with studies linking physician empathy to higher levels of patient motivation and commitment to treatment plans [3, 39]. In other words, empathy in this context seems less connected to the decision-making process itself and more to the translation of that decision into daily life, a potential factor in addressing the well-documented intention-behavior gap in chronic disease management [40].

These findings collectively suggest physician empathy may function as a form of ‘relationship insurance’ in diabetes care, an interpretation consistent with its association with a more resilient therapeutic alliance in the face of pressures inherent in real-world practice [41, 42]. More fundamentally, our results suggest that empathy is associated with relational capital that is linked to sustained SDM even when technical communication falters. While much previous research has emphasized achieving communication skill perfection [5, 43], our results indicate that the emotional connection built through empathy may be equally critical for weathering inevitable human imperfections—speaking too fast, using jargon, appearing distracted, or seeming bothered by patients’ questions. This empathic foundation potentially provides a dual benefit: compensating for the flaw of hurried communication by preserving SDM, and amplifying the yield of sound explanations by strengthening the link between shared decisions and patient adherence. This ultimately helps explain the clinical puzzle of why a patient’s perception of being understood is such a powerful factor in their resilience and ultimate success in managing their condition.

This study is not without limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes definitive causal inferences. Crucially for our core conclusions, this implies that the directional pathways we modeled could potentially be bidirectional. For instance, while we posit that effective communication and SDM reduce diabetes distress, it is also plausible that patients experiencing lower baseline distress are more proactive and receptive, thereby eliciting better communication and more collaborative decision-making from their physicians. Therefore, the temporal precedence of these relationships should be validated through longitudinal studies.

A second consideration is that the use of self-report measures for all constructs, while necessary to capture patient perceptions, introduces the potential for common-method variance and social desirability bias. Importantly, the absence of independent clinical markers (such as objective pharmacy refill records) implies that our findings reflect patients’ perceptions of their adherence and distress rather than objectively verified clinical behaviors. Consequently, the modeled impact of SDM and empathy on adherence may be slightly inflated by shared method variance, and we cannot conclusively claim that these communicative processes directly translate into improved physiological disease control. Future studies incorporating objective data (e.g., coded audio of consultations) would strengthen the findings.

Third, our measure of empathy assessed the patient’s global perception of their physician’s understanding and affective concern. While this captures a crucial aspect of the clinical relationship, it does not cleanly distinguish between the cognitive (e.g., perspective-taking) and affective (e.g., compassionate concern) components of empathy. Future research could utilize more differentiated scales to investigate whether these specific sub-components have distinct moderating effects on the pathways between communication, SDM, and patient outcomes.

Fourth, while we controlled for diabetes duration and treatment regimen as proxies, specific data regarding the clinical severity of complications were not recorded in the electronic medical records, limiting our ability to control for precise disease burden. However, a sensitivity analysis including the number of diabetes complications and HbA1c levels as additional covariates did not meaningfully alter the significance or magnitude of our main findings, suggesting our model is robust.

Fifth, we recruited from a single specialty university clinic, which may not be representative of the general diabetic population. The direct implication of this is that the observed moderating role of empathy and the mediating role of SDM we identified might be specific to a well-resourced, specialized environment. These pathways might operate differently, or be attenuated, in fast-paced, high-volume primary care settings where consultation times are strictly limited.

Furthermore, another limitation concerns statistical independence. Patients in this study were nested within physicians, with an estimated average of 26 patients per physician. However, physician identifiers were not collected to protect patient anonymity and encourage honest reporting of potentially sensitive evaluations. Consequently, we could not apply cluster-robust standard errors or multilevel modeling to account for this nesting. The absence of such adjustments may lead to underestimated standard errors and inflated type I error rates. Future studies should balance anonymity with the need for cluster correction by collecting de-identified physician codes that allow for statistical adjustment while protecting patient confidentiality.

Final limitation concerns the conceptual proximity between Explained Results and SDM. Although discriminant validity tests supported their distinctness (HTMT = 0.71) and item content analysis revealed conceptual differences, the moderate-to-high correlation indicates some shared variance remains. This is theoretically expected—clear information transfer facilitates collaborative deliberation—but it implies that the mediated pathway may partly reflect this interdependence rather than a completely independent sequential process. We therefore encourage cautious interpretation of the indirect effect and note that future research using observer-coded communication could further disentangle these constructs.

Practice implications and conclusion

This study points to a dynamic, triadic model of patient-centered diabetes care, where patient-physician communication sets the conditions, SDM acts as the central mechanism, and physician empathy serves as a key regulator. We demonstrated that Hurried Communication eroded SDM, thereby undermining adherence and exacerbating diabetes distress, whereas Explained Results fueled it to improve outcomes. Crucially, empathy was not a mere virtue but a strategic moderator: it buffered SDM from the harm of hurried exchanges and amplified its translation into adherence when explanations were sound. These findings call for a paradigm shift in clinical training and practice—from pursuing communicative perfection to building strategic resilience. Curricula should prioritize three synergistic competencies: (1) mitigating relational risk by minimizing hurried communication and inattentiveness; (2) maximizing informational clarity to activate SDM; and (3) deliberately deploying empathy as relational capital through evidence-based micro-behaviors (validating emotions, conveying understanding, expressing holistic concern, inquiring into daily life, and adopting the patient’s perspective; each drawn from our validated empathy scale [23]). This framework presents empathy as potentially more than a passive trait, but rather as a modifiable factor that could serve as a high-leverage intervention target. Future randomized trials must now test whether operationalizing this communication–SDM–empathy framework in practice, e.g., through simulation and reflective training, improves glycemic control and patient outcomes. Longitudinal studies are also needed to determine whether sustained alignment of these elements across serial encounters cultivates cumulative resilience, transforming episodic coping into durable self-management mastery. By defining these elements as interdependent components of a coherent patient-centered framework, we provide a robust, human-centered model for diabetes care that can guide clinicians in navigating clinical pressures through strategic attention to their interplay.
