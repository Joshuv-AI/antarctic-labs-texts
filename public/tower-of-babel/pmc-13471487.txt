The association between objective visual quality, retinal structure, and dynamic visual acuity
Authors: Ruoyu Li, Zesong Wang, Yuexin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe dynamic visual acuity (DVA) is crucial to assess dynamic visual function in clinical ophthalmology. The impact of objective ocular parameters on DVA remained unknown, which limits the clinical applicability and interpretability of the DVA test. Thus, the present research aims to investigate the impact of objective visual quality and retinal structure on DVA.MethodsHealthy young myopic adults aged 18 to 35 years were recruited. Binocular dynamic visual acuity (DVA) was evaluated at a 1-meter distance with habitual spectacles. The test comprised four sequential assessments of horizontal motion DVA at increasing velocities of 20, 40, 60, and 80 degrees per second (dps). Modulation transfer function (MTF) and higher-order aberrations (HOA) were measured to evaluate visual quality. Retinal structure, including retinal thickness and capillary density in the optic disc and macula was measured with optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA).ResultsThirty participants were enrolled with a mean age of 23.0 ± 3.7 years. DVA differed significantly across velocities (P < 0.001). DVA at 20 dps (r = -0.537, P = 0.002) and 60 dps (r = -0.430, P = 0.018) were negatively correlated with the binocular mean sphere. DVA at 20 and 60 dps was significantly associated with MTF value from 5 to 30 cycle/degree (c/d), and 80 dps DVA was associated with MTF value at spatial frequencies from 10 to 20 c/d (P < 0.05, respectively). The Z9 aberration showed a positive association with DVA at 20 dps (P = 0.047), and the Z7 aberration showed a significant positive association with DVA at 80 dps (P = 0.030). Limited associations were observed between DVA and retinal thickness or capillary density in the optic disc and macula.ConclusionsIn healthy myopic adults, better objective visual quality, as measured by MTF, might be associated with better DVA at both low and high speeds, whereas retinal structural parameters had a limited association with DVA. Background

Dynamic visual acuity (DVA) refers to the capacity to discern subtle spatial details within a visual target during relative motion between the observer and the target [1]. Our daily activities frequently require the perception and interaction with moving objects, and dynamic visual functions significantly affect performance in various tasks, such as reading, sports, driving, and walking [2–4]. There are various DVA tests, including the dynamic-object DVA test, where participants identify or assess moving targets with their head stabilized, and the static-object DVA test, where participants view stationary targets while their head or body is in motion [1, 5, 6]. The dynamic-object DVA (abbreviated as DVA in the following text) test has been applied to assess dynamic visual function in clinical ophthalmology, including cataract, dry eye, myopia and refractive surgery [7–10].

The visual signal transduction pathway of dynamic vision is different from static vision [11]. The difference in the transduction pathway determines different influential factors between dynamic and static vision, including ocular functional and structural parameters. Investigating the impact of objective ocular parameters on DVA could enhance its clinical applicability and improve the interpretability of test results. Previous research demonstrated that myopia severity affects DVA when corrected to normal static vision with spectacles [9]. In addition to refractive error, objective visual quality, including higher-order aberration (HOA) and modulation transfer function (MTF), was also quantified to evaluate static visual quality [12–14]. However, the impact of HOA and MTF on DVA has not been investigated. The retina, with its complex multi-layer, is the key structure for visual signal transduction. Apart from the P ganglion cell, the M ganglion cell-related pathway is critical in transmitting visual signals with high temporal and low spatial frequency, which is related to dynamic vision [15]. Thus, the retinal structural parameters, including retinal thickness and vascularity, could potentially impact DVA, which remains to be fully explored.

Thus, the present study included healthy young adults to test DVA at different velocities and assessed total eye MTF and HOA as objective measures of visual quality with an aberrometer, as well as measured macular and optic disc retinal thickness and capillary density using optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA). OCTA are widely used optical imaging modalities that allow in vivo structural and functional assessment of the neural retina [16]. This study aimed to investigate the influence of objective visual quality and retinal structural parameters on DVA across different velocities. The findings indicate potential factors affecting DVA and provide a foundation for utilizing DVA testing as a tool for assessing visual function.

Methods

Participants

This is a cross-sectional study. Healthy young adults aged 18 to 35 years were recruited for the study, with the following exclusion criteria: (1) monocular best-corrected visual acuity worse than 0 LogMAR (20/20) (subjective refraction); (2) monocular corrected distance visual acuity worse than 0.4 LogMAR (20/50) (habitual spectacles); (3) high myopia (subjective refraction ≤ -6.00D, indicating severe nearsightedness), high hyperopia (subjective refraction ≥ 4.00D, indicating severe farsightedness) or high astigmatism (subjective refraction ≤ -3.00D, indicating severe astigmatism), considering the potential optical artifacts caused by severe refractive error [9]; (3) history of severe ocular diseases, including glaucoma, corneal diseases, retinal disorders, or macular degeneration; (4) Systemic conditions that could potentially affect visual acuity; (5) vestibular or cognitive impairments that may interfere with the accuracy of the test; (6) inability to properly track dynamic visual stimuli or follow instructions provided by the examiner. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, received approval from the Institutional Review Board (No.S2024779), and all participants provided written informed consent before the study.

Ocular assessment

The ocular assessment was performed in the following order: corrected distance visual acuity assessed with habitual spectacles (LogMAR visual chart), automatic refraction (Topcon, KR-800, Japan), subjective refraction, slit lamp biomicroscopy, OCTA (Cirrus, Zeiss, Germany) and iTrace analyzer (Tracey Technologies, United States).

Subjective refraction was performed monocularly with the following steps: first maximum plus to maximum visual acuity (MPMVA) was achieved after fogging, followed by refinement of the cylinder axis and power with the Jackson-cross cylinder device, and a second MPMVA to tune the spherical diopter.

A SD-OCTA device with a wavelength of 840 nm and 100,000 A-scans/second was utilized to quantify retinal thickness and capillary density automatically, with a 3 mm × 3 mm scan performed for the macula and a 4.5 mm × 4.5 mm scan for the optic disc. Only images with a signal strength index greater than 8/10 were included. Images with significant motion artifacts, segmentation errors, or poor clarity were excluded. For the retinal thickness, the peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), fovea (1.0 mm × 1.0 mm), parafovea (1.0 mm to 3.0 mm) thickness, ganglion cell layer thickness (3.0 mm × 3.0 mm) and macular avascular zone size were quantified. For the vascularity, the inside disc (1.5 mm × 1.5 mm) and peripapillary capillary density, superficial and deep fovea (1.0 mm × 1.0 mm) and parafovea (1.0 mm to 3.0 mm) capillary density were quantified.

The iTrace analyzer was applied to measure ocular aberration and the MTF curve. The device combines the Placido corneal topography and ray-tracing aberrometry to precisely quantify ocular aberrations. The total eye higher-order aberration at a pupil size of 4.00 mm was recorded and presented in Zernike polynomial coefficients, including Z6 (), Z7 (), Z8 (), Z9 () and Z12 (). The total eye MTF value at a pupil size of 4.00 mm was collected for the analysis, including the spatial frequency of 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30 cycles per degree (c/d).

Dynamic visual acuity test

Four DVA tests were performed sequentially, with horizontal motion velocities set at 20, 40, 60, and 80 degrees per second (dps). The velocities were selected according to our previous research, considering the variety of eye movement strategies [9]. DVA was examined binocularly with habitual spectacles at 1 m. Habitual spectacles were defined as the participant’s daily-wear corrective glasses. The test optotypes were presented on a 24-inch 120 Hz in-plane switching screen. Custom software, created with MATLAB 2017b (MathWorks, United States), was employed to generate a moving optotype letter E at predefined speeds and sizes. The configuration and size of the optotype letter E were designed based on the standard logarithmic visual chart, and the moving velocity was quantified as the viewing angle changed per second. During the test, the letter E moved across the screen, starting from the midpoint of the left edge and proceeding to the right. Participants were required to identify the opening direction of the letter E in a four alternative forced choice regime.

The DVA test began with the optotype size, three steps larger than the subjects’ static visual acuity. Following a preliminary round of five randomly selected optotypes for familiarization, the formal testing phase commenced. During the formal test, eight optotypes of the same size were presented individually at two-second intervals, featuring a randomly oriented opening. If the participant correctly identified at least five out of eight optotypes, the size was decremented by one logMAR step. The smallest optotype size (A, presented in logMAR, range from 0 to 1) at which participants could reliably identify greater than 5/8 optotypes and the count (b, range from 0 to 8) of correctly identified optotypes at the next smaller size were documented. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with IBM SPSS Statistics (version 23.0, IBM Corp., United States). Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. The Shapiro-Wilk test was performed to determine the normality of the distribution.

A linear mixed model with a random intercept for the participant was applied to compare the DVA of different velocities, followed by a Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc pairwise comparison. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the associations between binocular DVA at various velocities and mean refraction results for normally distributed data. Otherwise, Spearman’s correlation was performed. Generalized linear models with an identity link function and normal distribution were applied to analyze the impact of binocular mean MTF value, higher-order aberration, retinal thickness, and capillary density on DVA at various velocities, adjusting for mean sphere and visual acuity as covariates to control for potential confounding. Because DVA was measured binocularly, optical and structural parameters were averaged across both eyes for primary analyses. Effect sizes are reported as regression coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Exploratory subgroup analysis was further conducted by individual eye with the same generalized linear model specification. However, these should be interpreted cautiously because measurements from the two eyes of the same participant are correlated. No correction was applied for multiple tests to the generalized linear model. P < 0.05 was determined to be significant.

Discussion

Dynamic visual acuity (DVA) is crucial for tasks involving moving objects and serves as a promising metric for assessing visual function [17]. However, the factors influencing DVA have not been fully investigated. This study aimed to explore the impact of objective visual quality, including higher-order aberrations and MTF, and retinal structural parameters on DVA across different velocities in healthy young adults. The goal was to identify key influential factors and enhance the clinical utility of the DVA test.

Velocity is a significant influential factor for DVA. The present research demonstrated that DVA at 20 dps was significantly better than at higher speeds, and no significant differences were observed between DVA at 40 dps and 80 dps, or between 60 dps and 80 dps. These results are consistent with our previous research on horizontal motion DVA [18]. As velocity increases, the ability to track moving targets diminishes, significantly impacting DVA outcomes [19, 20].

Refractive errors significantly influence DVA, including uncorrected and corrected refractive errors [9, 21]. For uncorrected refractive errors, previous studies have shown that minor retinal defocus may not significantly affect athletic performance. However, higher refractive errors are likely to impair DVA [22]. Additionally, Jorge et al. identified a significant correlation between DVA and astigmatism [17]. In the present research, all participants had their static visual acuity (SVA) corrected with habitual spectacles during the DVA test. The binocular mean sphere and SE had significant correlations with DVA at 20 and 60 dps, while the monocular sphere and SE in the right eye were significantly correlated with DVA at all tested velocities. These results suggest that the further the refractive state deviates from emmetropia, the worse the DVA, even after correction. This finding aligns with our previous research [9]. There are several potential reasons. First, for participants with more severe myopia, the prism effect of the spectacles became more pronounced. The prism effect might cause spatial disorientation, which impairs the ability to track moving targets and leads to worse DVA performance. Second, the defocus effect in the peripheral regions of spectacles reduces visual clarity outside the central visual field, essential for tracking objects moving across a wide range of visual angles [23]. Considering the instinct flaws of spectacles, the application of contact lens or refractive surgery might minimize the spectacle-related artifacts. The subgroup analysis revealed that the right eye’s refractive error had significant correlations with DVA at 40 and 80 dps, while the left eye did not. In our study, the right eye was more frequently the dominant eye among participants. Previous studies have found that, compared to the nondominant eye, the dominant eye is more sensitive and performs better in visual target detection [24]. The visual sensitivity difference between the dominant and nondominant eye might explain the greater influence of the right eye’s refractive error on DVA.

The MTF value is a widely recognized metric for evaluating objective visual quality. It quantifies the resolution of an optical system at different spatial frequencies [14]. The present research found negative associations between MTF values and DVA at 20, 60 and 80 dps, suggesting that superior visual quality contributes to better DVA performance. Significant associations between DVA at 20 and 60 dps and MTF values were observed across low to high spatial frequencies. In contrast, DVA at 80 dps was associated with MTF values at spatial frequencies from 10 to 20 c/d but not at high spatial frequencies. The results might be attributed to the difference between static and dynamic visual signal transduction, which becomes more pronounced at higher velocities. The M ganglion cell-related pathway mainly transmits visual signals with low spatial and high temporal frequencies, which is associated with dynamic vision [15]. At 40 dps, no significant association was observed between MTF values and DVA. One possible explanation is perceptual learning or transfer effects from the preceding 20 dps test [18]. Future studies employing randomized or counterbalanced velocity sequences are needed to disentangle the learning effect.

The retinal structure, including retinal thickness and vascularity, might influence visual signal transduction. In the present research, higher capillary density in the peripapillary area was associated with better DVA at 80 dps. However, no significant relationship was observed between DVA and macular thickness or vascularity. These findings suggest that increased vascular density in the optic nerve head, rather than in the macular region, may enhance visual perception during rapid motion. Previous studies have demonstrated that M ganglion cells, which are primarily linked to rod cells, are related to the transduction of dynamic visual signals [15]. The mid-peripheral retina, rather than the macular region, contains the highest density of rod cells, which might explain the lack of association between macular structure and DVA. Further research is required to investigate the potential impact of peripheral retinal structure and rod cell density on DVA. In contrast to high-speed DVA, low-speed DVA was not associated with retinal structure in this study. These results indicate that higher-speed DVA might serve as a more promising indicator of subtle retinal structural damage than lower-speed DVA. Consistent across velocities, higher MTF values were associated with better DVA. In contrast, isolated significant associations for specific Zernike terms or single OCTA parameters should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect chance findings given the number of comparisons and modest sample size.

While the present study identifies statistically significant associations between objective optical quality (MTF) and DVA in healthy young myopic adults wearing habitual spectacles, the translation of these findings into routine clinical practice requires careful consideration. Currently, DVA testing remains primarily a research tool, and normative thresholds for clinical decision-making have not been established. The observed associations suggest that objective metrics like MTF may complement traditional static visual acuity measures in assessing visual function, particularly for activities involving motion perception such as driving or sports. However, given the use of habitual spectacles and the restricted demographic, these findings cannot be directly generalized to clinical populations with different refractive profiles or to those wearing contact lenses or with refractive surgeries. Future research should establish standardized DVA protocols, validate these associations in larger prospective cohorts with diverse refractive statuses, and determine specific clinical scenarios where DVA assessment coupled with optical quality metrics would provide actionable diagnostic or prognostic information beyond current standard examinations.

This study has several limitations that constrain the interpretation and generalizability of the findings. First, DVA was evaluated using habitual spectacles. While this approach reflects real-world conditions, it introduces a systemic optical confounder. The habitual prescription may deviate from the subjective refraction. Unfortunately, the exact habitual spectacle prescription was not recorded, precluding quantification of the discrepancy. Although static vision and refractive error were adjusted using the generalized linear model, their influence could not be completely ruled out. The optical limitation precludes isolation of pure retinal or neural factors and restricts generalizability to other populations. Standardized correction (e.g., trial frames or contact lenses) would better isolate these factors in future studies. Second, only horizontally moving DVA was assessed in this study. Other motion patterns, such as vertical and diagonal motion, may be influenced by different factors, which require further investigation. Third, the sample size was not supported by a formal prior power calculation and the study remains underpowered for small effects and vulnerable to model instability, highlighting the need for larger-scale studies in the future to validate these findings. Fourth, potential confounding factors were not considered in the subject inclusion that might influence the visual processing or cognitive function, such as high-performance sports, educational attainment, or reading habits. Fifth, the failure to assess ocular dominance represents a significant limitation, as the observed asymmetry in right and left eyes may suggest that dominant eye performance may differentially drive binocular DVA outcomes, and unrecorded dominance status prevents stratified analysis of this potential confounder. Sixth, the study only assesses retinal thickness in the macular region, which primarily reflects the parvocellular pathway. Dynamic vision predominantly engages the magnocellular pathway and mid-peripheral retina. The absence of peripheral retinal structural assessment and evaluation of rod-cell function limits mechanistic interpretation of the velocity-dependent findings. Seventh, the four DVA tests were conducted sequentially in a fixed order, and the learning or fatigue effect might impact the results. Including randomized velocity orders would reduce these effects in future studies. Eighth, the generalized linear model analysis examines multiple predictors across velocities. The approach may increase the risk of false positives, and the isolated significant association should be interpreted cautiously. Ninth, eye-level subgroup analyses treated right and left eyes as independent units, which should be interpreted cautiously because measurements from the two eyes of the same participant are correlated and standard generalized linear models do not account for this hierarchical dependency.

Conclusions

The cross-sectional study investigates the relationship between DVA and objective visual quality and retinal structure in healthy young myopic adults. The findings suggest that a higher modulation transfer function (MTF) value was associated with better DVA at both low and high velocities. However, only weak or nonsignificant associations were observed between DVA and retinal thickness or capillary density in the optic disc and macula. The findings indicate that objective optical quality metrics may be relevant to dynamic visual performance. However, these conclusions are restricted to healthy young myopic adults wearing habitual spectacles and clinical translation requires validation in larger, prospectively designed studies with standardized protocols and diverse refractive populations.
