Mortality risk ranking after medical emergency team review: External validation and redevelopment of predictive models
Authors: Anthony Batterbury, Aaron Conway, Mark Mattiussi, Alanna Geary, Clint Douglas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Most adult patients reviewed by a medical emergency team (MET) remain on the ward,1,2 often in clinical states comparable to high-dependency or intensive care patients.3,4 In-hospital mortality among MET-reviewed patients is consistently high. Across contemporary cohorts, 20–35% of patients receiving at least one MET or rapid response team review die before discharge, with rates approaching 40% in those with sepsis, respiratory failure, or pre-existing treatment limitations.5 These figures reflect the underlying acuity and multimorbidity of this population. Approximately 40% of patients have pre-existing treatment limitations, which contribute disproportionately to mortality.6,7 Recognising this distinction is essential, as risk ranking supports care planning that is proportionate to risk, whether escalation focused or comfort focused. Ward staffing, patient placement, and resource allocation typically do not change after the MET leaves,3,4 creating a mismatch between clinical complexity and ward capability: a patient safety problem that rapid response systems have not solved.

Decisions at MET sign-off, including adjustments to escalation triggers, monitoring frequency, resource allocation, and patient placement, are largely based on clinical judgement supported by tools designed to detect deterioration rather than prioritise patients according to subsequent risk. Mortality prediction models and other decision-support aids for deteriorating ward patients rely on early warning score–based systems, although machine learning approaches are increasingly being adopted to enhance predictive performance.8 Numerous models have been developed to predict short-term in-hospital mortality; however, performance varies across settings, and few have been shown to improve patient care. Reviews have highlighted concerns regarding calibration, methodological inconsistencies, and limited evidence of clinical impact.5,9 Models often perform worse in hospitals other than the one where they were built, so they must be tested elsewhere before being used in practice. No externally validated instrument measures patient complexity or estimates mortality risk among patients already under review by the MET.10,11 Ward-level interventions strengthening nursing surveillance can redistribute MET activations and adverse outcomes by patient complexity,12 but without evidence to identify which patients sit where on that gradient.

We previously examined the acuity and dependency of patients who remained on the ward after MET review (the REMAIN Study), identifying substantial patient complexity and demonstrating that mortality and unplanned escalation could be predicted.[13], [14], [15] Although the REMAIN models were internally validated at a single hospital, predictive performance in hospitals with different case-mix, capability, and post-MET care processes is unknown.

As part of a broader co-design program to develop a Collaborative Decision-Making Framework for Safer Patient Placement After MET Review (the COMFORTER Study), in this multicentre study, we aimed to externally validate the REMAIN in-hospital mortality model in a larger and more diverse population and explore whether alternative bedside measures of illness severity could improve its clinical utility.

Methods

Study design, setting, and participants

This cohort study was conducted across four Australian metropolitan public hospitals/study sites. Sites 2 and 3 are specialist state-wide referral centres, and Sites 1 and 4 are tertiary suburban centres. Study sites operate a 24/7 MET service capable of responding to clinical emergencies. Patients requiring MET review are escalated by ward staff in response to abnormal vital signs, respiratory/cardiac arrest, or staff concern. The MET dose, a standardised measure of MET activations, was 24.2 per 1000 inpatient separations.

All MET activations for admitted adults (aged ≥18 years) who remained on a general ward at the end of the review between 1 January 2024 and 31 December 2024 were screened. Activations outside ward areas, responses to nonadmitted individuals, and specialist-led reviews (mental health or obstetric) were excluded. Eligible cases were selected using simple random sampling. Sample size calculations assumed a 20% event rate, 13 candidate predictors, and a target mean absolute prediction error of 0.05.16 The target cohort was 1940 unique patients (485 per site).

Outcome and predictor variables

The outcome was in-hospital mortality. Predictor variables included mean-centred age (patient's age minus 67 years, linear and quadratic terms), sex (female, male), comorbidity burden (nil, single, multiple), resuscitation preference (resuscitate, do-not-attempt-resuscitation [DNAR]), and hospital admission, surgery, and intensive care unit discharge status (yes, no) within 24 h of the sampled event. Illness severity variables were the National Early Warning Score-2 (NEWS),17 modified Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (mSOFA),18,19 Nursing Activities Score (NAS),20,21 and modified Index of Care Dependency (mICD).22 In REMAIN model development, an acuity-dependency profile variable derived from mSOFA and NAS was used; NEWS and mICD were not. In COMFORTER redevelopment, age in years and the instrument-specific scores from NEWS, mSOFA, and mICD were applied; comorbidity was not included as a predictor (Supplement_S1).

Collectively, these instruments capture complementary dimensions of deterioration and complexity. NEWS reflects acute physiological instability, summarising deranged vital signs and supplemental oxygen use (range 0–20). mSOFA, using the SpO2:FiO2 ratio for respiratory assessment,19 quantifies organ dysfunction summarising severity across six organ systems (range 0–24). NAS represents workload and functional complexity by aggregating 23 nursing activities into a proportional workload score (range 16.8%–175%). mICD captures patient dependency across eight care domains, expressed as an aggregate complexity score (range 8–32). Across all instruments, higher scores indicate greater acuity, dependency, and clinical complexity.

Data collection and preparation

Physiology, intervention, and resuscitation status data were manually extracted from clinical information systems and electronic/paper medical records across all sites by a single investigator (AB) into a REDCap23 instrument. Missing mSOFA values were coded as no dysfunction; undocumented dependency-measure items were coded as normal/independent or absent. Demographic variables, hospital events, and in-hospital mortality were extracted from the health service data warehouse after site-level data collection, ensuring investigators were blinded to outcomes until analysis. Extracted variables were coded and transformed into predefined predictor and outcome variables.

Model specification and validation framework

We assessed generalisability of the published REMAIN in-hospital mortality model by applying it to the complete cohort without refitting or changing its coefficients7 (Supplement_S1). Site 2 provided temporal validation, while Sites 1, 3, and 4 provided external validation. Site-specific external validation results were pooled using random-effects meta-analysis to assess performance across hospitals.

We evaluated five machine learning approaches—grouped penalised regression (lasso, ridge), random forest, tabular ensembles (AutoGluon), and Tabular In-Context Learning (TabICL)—to identify the optimal modelling framework for COMFORTER. Models were compared using leave-one-site-out internal–external cross-validation, in which three hospitals were used for model development and the remaining hospital for testing. All model development steps were restricted to training hospitals. The final model was selected based on overall performance and compared with a NEWS-only model representing current escalation practice. Performance was evaluated using measures of discrimination (receiver operating characteristic [ROC]–area under the curve [AUC] and precision–recall AUC), calibration (calibration-in-the-large and calibration slope), and prediction error (Brier score), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

As a sensitivity analysis, we examined whether performance differed by resuscitation status.

Ethical approval

Ethical approvals were obtained from the Health Service Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/2024/MNHA/112718). There was no patient or public involvement in the development and conduct of this study.

Results

Sample characteristics

Between 1 January 2024 and 31 December 2024 METs responded to 7865 deterioration events across four hospitals. After exclusions and random selection of a single index event per patient, 1937 adults were analysed (Fig. 1).

Participant flow diagram.

The COMFORTER cohort comprised older, comorbid adults reviewed for multiple clinical triggers, with moderate illness severity and a 20.7% in-hospital mortality rate. Across sites, smaller hospitals had older patients with more frequent DNAR orders, whereas larger hospitals had younger cohorts, higher comorbidity, and nursing dependency. Clinical triggers and MET activity varied, but acuity measures and mortality were broadly similar. Compared with REMAIN, COMFORTER patients were older, more comorbid, and more frequently DNAR and had higher overall mortality (20.7% vs 16.0%). REMAIN patients demonstrated greater acute organ dysfunction and nursing dependency, reflected in higher mSOFA and NAS scores (Table 1).

External validation of the REMAIN in-hospital mortality model

Across all four hospitals (1937 patients; 400 deaths; mortality 20.7%), the REMAIN model demonstrated moderate discrimination (ROC-AUC = 0.80, 95% CI 0.75–0.84) and low overall prediction error (Brier = 0.13, 95% CI 0.11–0.15; Fig. 2). Discrimination was consistent across hospitals, with similar ROC-AUC estimates observed at each site and a pooled ROC-AUC of 0.80 (95% CI 0.75–0.84). Although predicted risk estimates varied between hospitals, these differences were not sufficiently consistent to be addressed by a single recalibration approach (Supplement_S1).

Site performance of the REMAIN model during external validation. The panels summarise model discrimination (receiver operating characteristic curves) and agreement between predicted and observed mortality (calibration plots). Differences in discrimination between sites suggest that transportability may be influenced by variation in case-mix, escalation and patient-management practices, outcome prevalence, and data quality, with these same determinants also contributing to site-specific patterns of over- and under-prediction observed in the calibration plots.

Model selection and development of the COMFORTER in-hospital mortality model

All machine learning approaches demonstrated similar discrimination (pooled ROC-AUC 0.83–0.84), suggesting that performance was driven largely by the available predictors rather than the specific modelling approach (Supplement_S1). TabICL demonstrated the most consistent between-site performance and was therefore selected.

Across the combined sample from all four hospitals, the redeveloped COMFORTER model using TabICL demonstrated good discrimination based on demographic, clinical, and illness severity measures. The pooled ROC-AUC was 0.84 (95% CI 0.76–0.89), compared with 0.75 (95% CI 0.67–0.82) for a NEWS-only model (Fig. 3). When analysed separately by resuscitation status, COMFORTER distinguished between patients at higher and lower risk of death, both among those with a DNAR order (pooled ROC-AUC 0.78, 95% CI 0.73–0.83) and those without a DNAR order (pooled ROC-AUC 0.82, 95% CI 0.70–0.90). This suggests that the model's discrimination was not explained solely by identifying patients with a DNAR order. Calibration was generally improved compared to REMAIN, although the extent of improvement varied between hospitals (Supplement_S1).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and calibration plots comparing TabICL and National Early Warning Score-2 (NEWS) logistic regression models. ROC and calibration plots for the COMFORTER (TabICL) and NEWS logistic models based on pooled out-of-site predictions from all held-out sites in the internal–external validation. The plots show that COMFORTER discriminates patients more effectively and provides more accurate risk predictions than NEWS, with clearer separation of outcomes and closer alignment between predicted and observed mortality.

Discussion

In this multicentre study, REMAIN successfully identified patients at increased risk of death after MET review across four metropolitan hospitals, although the accuracy of predicted mortality risk varied between sites. Redevelopment produced COMFORTER, which improved the identification of patients at highest risk using fewer available clinical variables. However, because the accuracy of absolute mortality estimates varied between hospitals, predicted probabilities of death should be interpreted cautiously. The model may be most useful for prioritising patients for clinical review.

At MET stand-down, clinicians must decide whether ward-based care remains appropriate and what ongoing monitoring, treatment, or escalation is required. Patients who remain on the ward after MET review are heterogeneous and have widely varying risks of adverse outcomes.15,24 In the absence of validated decision-support tools, post-MET decisions rely largely on clinical judgement, as existing prediction models focus on MET activation rather than management after review. This study addresses that gap by externally validating and redeveloping a risk stratification model for patients who remain on the ward following MET review.

Post-MET care also occurs within the reality of finite clinical resources, including ward staffing levels, and availability of critical care outreach services and close observation or higher-acuity beds. Clinicians must prioritise these resources across multiple competing patients, often without objective tools to support risk assessment.7 Risk classification is intended to complement, not replace, clinical judgement. The purpose of risk ranking is to ensure that care is proportionate to risk, rather than to imply that all deaths are preventable. Risk stratification may therefore help inform post-MET care planning across different resuscitation statuses and goals of care.

Patients identified as high-risk warrant senior clinical review to determine whether deterioration is potentially reversible or reflects progression towards the end of life. This distinction cannot be made by the model alone. Depending on the clinical context, this review may lead to closer surveillance, patient relocation, specialist palliative or multidisciplinary review, or reassessment of goals of care (Fig. 4). By providing a shared assessment of risk, COMFORTER may also support situational awareness among ward staff, multidisciplinary teams, MET clinicians, and patients and families during post-deterioration care planning. This aligns with findings from the ENCORE cluster randomised trial, in which strengthening ward-level nursing surveillance redistributed MET activations and serious adverse events by patient complexity.12

Potential application in clinical practice.

Compared with REMAIN, the COMFORTER model had improved performance with more consistent risk ranking across hospitals. This was achieved using fewer variables that are directly available at the bedside. More importantly, unlike NEWS, COMFORTER was designed to support clinical decision-making after MET review rather than detect deterioration before review. Increasing integration of electronic clinical records with vital sign monitoring systems, care planning tools, and pathology databases may further support the feasibility of model-informed reassessment by enabling automated data capture and near-real-time risk ranking without increasing documentation burden. However, this study assessed predictive performance only, and prospective studies are required to determine the feasibility, acceptability, and clinical impact of integrating COMFORTER into routine post-MET workflows, including effects on care processes, resource allocation, and patient outcomes.

Limitations and future work

Vital sign and dependency predictors were derived from routine clinical documentation, which may be susceptible to incomplete or inconsistent recording. Resuscitation status was modelled as a binary variable and did not capture nuanced treatment preferences or ceilings of care. As such, COMFORTER predicts all-cause in-hospital mortality and cannot distinguish potentially preventable death from death during an irreversible or palliative trajectory. Future research should examine frailty, multimorbidity, and treatment limitations and evaluate whether integrating COMFORTER into rapid response workflows improves patient placement, resource allocation, or clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

Among patients who remained on the ward following MET review, COMFORTER demonstrated adequate discrimination for in-hospital mortality across multiple hospitals, with improved performance compared with both NEWS and the REMAIN models. These findings support the potential value of structured risk ranking as an adjunct to post-MET clinical reassessment and may support team communication and decision-making regarding escalation of care. Prospective studies are now required to determine whether implementation of COMFORTER influences resource utilisation and patient outcomes.
