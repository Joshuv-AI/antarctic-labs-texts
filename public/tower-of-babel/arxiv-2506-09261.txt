Chains without regularity
Alessandro Della Corte, Marco Farotti

Abstract
We study chain-recurrence and chain-transitivity in compact dynamical systems without any regularity assumptions. We prove that every compact system has a chain-recurrent point and a closed, invariant, chain-transitive subsystem. We also prove that, in fact, the same results hold for a strengthened form of chain-recurrence, which we identify. KEYWORDS: Topological dynamics; Chain-recurrence; Discontinuous dynamical systems. MSC2020: 37B20, 37B65.



1
Introduction

Consider a dynamical system
(X,f)
, with
X
a metrizable space. When
X
is compact and
f
is continuous, a classical result by Birkhoff (
[
1
]
) ensures that there is at least one
x\in X
that revisits each of its neighborhoods within a bounded number of iterations, that is there exists an almost-periodic point. If we drop the continuity assumption, this is obviously false in general.
However, one can wonder if a point verifying some more general form of self-recurrence always exists even without continuity, and in fact without any assumption on the map. Searching for a suitable candidate generalization of almost-periodicity, let us review the well-known
topological dynamical relations

\mathcal{O},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{N},\mathcal{C}\subseteq X^{2}
, as introduced by E. Akin (
[
2
]
), that can be defined as follows (see for instance
[
3
, Def. 2.2, p. 47]
):

Definition 1.1
.

1.

Orbit relation:

x\,\mathcal{O}\,y
iff
\exists\,k\in\mathbb{N}\,|\,f^{k}(x)=y
;

2.

Recurrence relation:

x\,\mathcal{R}\,y
iff
\forall\epsilon>0\,\exists\,k\in\mathbb{N}\,|\,f^{k}(x)\in B_{\epsilon}(y)
;

3.

Non-wandering relation:

x\,\mathcal{N}\,y
iff
\forall\epsilon>0\,\exists\,z\in B_{\epsilon}(x)\,\exists\,k\in\mathbb{N}\,|\,f^{k}(z)\in B_{\epsilon}(y)
;

4.

Chain-recurrence relation:

x\,\mathcal{C}\,y
iff
\forall\epsilon>0\,\exists\,n\in\mathbb{N},\,\exists\,x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\,|x_{0}=x,x_{n}=y,\,d(f(x_{i}),x_{i+1})<\epsilon\ \ (i=0,\dots,n-1)
.

By compactness, every infinite orbit has an accumulation point, which is in fact non-wandering, in the sense that it verifies
x\,\mathcal{N}\,x
. Indeed, any open neighborhood of the accumulation point
x
of an infinite orbit
\mathcal{O}(y)
contains two points of type
f^{k}(y)
,
f^{h}(y)
for some distinct natural numbers
h,k
, and thus the claim in Def.
1.1
-3 is verified with
y=x
.
Since finite orbits contain periodic (hence non-wandering) points, we have the following, easy

Fact
∗
.

If
X
is compact, the non-wandering set
\{x\in X|x\,\mathcal{N}\,x\}
of
(X,f)
is nonempty, independently of any assumption on
f
.

However, the significance of this result is problematic if we cannot guarantee that
f
is continuous at the non-wandering point
x
. Specifically, the statement
x\,\mathcal{N}\,x

is independent of the value of
f
at
x
, so it provides insight into the dynamics of
f
only when the behavior of
f
at
x
can be inferred from its values at sufficiently close points — that is, when
f
is continuous.

Luckily, it is easy to modify slightly the definition of
\mathcal{N}
so that: 1. it stays unchanged in case
f
is continuous; 2. becomes better suited for discontinuous maps. If
f
is continuous, indeed, it does not matter whether we make the
\epsilon
-correction before or after the first iteration of the map, so in this case the relation
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
defined below is just equivalent to
\mathcal{N}
:

Definition 1.2
.

For
x,y\in X
, we write
x\,\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}\,y
if for every
\epsilon>0
there exist
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
z\in B_{\epsilon}(f(x))
such that
f^{k}(z)\in B_{\epsilon}(y)
.

In case of a discontinuous map, Def.
1.2
is not anymore equivalent to Def.
1.1
-3. In fact, the new version fits better with the other topological dynamical relations. In particular, the statement
x\,\mathcal{A}\,x

depends
on the behavior of
f
at
x
when
\mathcal{A}
is any of the relations
\mathcal{O}
,
\mathcal{R}
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
,
\mathcal{C}
.

Moreover, without any regularity assumption, in general we have
\mathcal{N}\not\subseteq\mathcal{C}
, so that, in particular, it does not immediately follow from Fact
∗
that every compact system has a chain-recurrent point. On the other hand, we have always
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{R}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
(this is graphically represented in the scheme of Fig.
1
), which resembles what happens in the continuous case, when
\mathcal{N}=\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
.

Figure 1:
Topological dynamical relations. It is graphically emphasized that, if we replace
\mathcal{N}
by
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
, additional
\epsilon
-corrections are allowed whenever one goes down in the table (right).

It seems therefore that
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
is a more flexible definition for the non-wandering relation: it is equivalent to the usual one with continuity, and generalizes better for discontinuous maps.

Let us now come back to our original problem: the existence of non-wandering points. If we do not assume continuity, it is false that a compact system has always an
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
-recurrent point. Indeed, an elementary counterexample to the claim that every compact metrizable dynamical system has a point
x
such that
x\,\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}\,x
is given by
([0,1],f)
where
f
is defined as:

f(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{3}{4}&\quad\text{ if }x=0\\
\\
x(x+\frac{1}{2})&\quad\text{ if }x\in(0,\frac{1}{2})\\
\\
\frac{1}{4}&\quad\text{ if }x=\frac{1}{2}\\
\\
\frac{1}{2}x+\frac{1}{4}&\quad\text{ if }x\in(\frac{1}{2},1]\end{cases}

(1)

In fact, since all the topological relations
\mathcal{O},\mathcal{R},\widetilde{\mathcal{N}},\mathcal{C}
are sensitive to what happens pointwise, one may think that there is little hope of finding any relevant recurrence phenomenon if the map is completely general.

Perhaps surprisingly, this is not the case. In fact, the chain-recurrence relation
\mathcal{C}
is sufficiently robust to guarantee key existence results even without assumptions. Indeed, we will show that, without any assumptions on the map:

1.

every compact system has a chain-recurrent point;

2.

every compact system has a closed, invariant, internally chain transitive subset;

3.

every point in a compact system is in chain relation with a chain-recurrent point;

4.

every point in a compact system is in chain relation with a closed, invariant, internally chain transitive subset.

Notice that points 2. and 4. are respectively stronger than 1. and 3., but their proof builds on their weaker versions, and thus for clarity we preferred to present them as separate, warm-up results.
Finally, we will also show that, in fact, the chain-recurrent point whose existence is established is also recurrent with respect to a stronger relation than chain-recurrence, which we identify.

2
Preliminaries

We say that
(X,f)
is a compact dynamical system (or simply a dynamical system) if
X
is a compact, metrizable space and
f
is a self-map of
X
.

For
x\in X
, we indicate by
\mathcal{O}(x)
the orbit of
x
, that is the set
\mathcal{O}(x)=\{f(x),f^{2}(x),\ldots\}
. We set
\mathcal{O}^{0}(x):=\{x\}\cup\mathcal{O}(x)
.
We will indicate by
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, respectively, the set of positive and non-negative integers.
Let us recall the notion of
\epsilon
-
chain
or
\epsilon
-
pseudo-orbit
(
[
3
, p. 48]
).

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(X,f)
be a dynamical system and
d:X^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
be a metric on
X
. Given two points
x,y\in X
and
\epsilon>0
, an
\epsilon
-
chain
from
x
to
y
is a finite set of points
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
in
X
, with
n\geq 1
, such that

i)

x_{0}=x
and
x_{n}=y
,

ii)

d(f(x_{i}),x_{i+1})<\epsilon
for every
i=0,1,\ldots n-1
.

The
chain relation

\mathcal{C}\subseteq X^{2}
, the binary relation mentioned in the Introduction, can be equivalently defined as follows (
[
3
, Def. 2.2, p. 47]
): given
x,y\in X
,

x\,\mathcal{C}\,y\iff\forall\epsilon>0\text{ there exists an $\epsilon$-chain from $x$ to $y$}.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq X^{2}
be a binary relation on
X
. For
M\subseteq X
and
y\in X
, we write
M\,\mathcal{A}\,y
if
x\,\mathcal{A}\,y
for every
x\in M
. A point
x\in X
is called
chain-recurrent
if
x\,\mathcal{C}\,x
.
The set of all the chain-recurrent points of a dynamical system
(X,f)
is denoted by
CR(X,f)
.
We write simply
CR_{f}
when the space
X
is clear from the context.

Let us also recall the notion of strong chain-recurrence introduced by Easton
[
4
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
(X,f)
be a dynamical system and
d:X^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
be a metric on
X
.
Given two points
x,y\in X
and
\epsilon>0
, we say that a finite sequence of points
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
of
X
, with
n\in\mathbb{N}
, is a
strong
(\epsilon,d)
-chain
from

x
to
y
if

i)

x_{0}=x
and
x_{n}=y
,

ii)

\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}d(f(x_{i}),x_{i+1})<\epsilon
.

The
strong chain relation

\mathcal{SC}_{d}\subseteq X^{2}
is the binary relation defined as follows: given
x,y\in X
,

x\,\mathcal{SC}_{d}\,y\iff\forall\epsilon>0\text{ there exists a strong $(\epsilon,d)$-chain from $x$ to $y$}.

A point
x\in X
is called
strong chain-recurrent
if
x\,\mathcal{SC}_{d}\,x
. Let
\mathcal{SCR}_{d}(f)
be the set of all the strong chain-recurrent points of
(X,f)
. We write simply
\mathcal{SCR}_{d}
when the map
f
is clear from the context.

The relation
\mathcal{SC}_{d}
depends, in general, on the metric
d
(while
\mathcal{C}
can be defined in purely topological terms if the space is compact). Intersecting over all topological equivalent metrics on
X
, we obtain the generalized recurrent set
GR(f)
introduced by Auslander for flows (
[
5
]
) and then extended to maps (
[
2
,
6
,
7
]
). More precisely, we give the following

Definition 2.3
.

The
generalized recurrent set
, denoted by
GR(f)
, is defined as

GR(f)=\bigcap_{d}\mathcal{SCR}_{d}(f),

where the intersection is over all metrics
d
compatible with the topology of
X
.

The following inclusions hold without any assumptions on
f
:

GR(f)\subseteq\mathcal{SCR}_{d}(f)\subseteq CR_{f}.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
S
be a subset of a topological space
X
. The
derived set
of
S
, denoted by
S^{\prime}
, is the set of the limit points
x
of
S
, that is, the set of points
x
such that for every neighborhood
U
of
x
we have that

S\cap(U\setminus\{x\})\neq\emptyset
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
(X,f)
be a dynamical system, and
S\subseteq X
. We say that the set
S
is
f
-invariant
(or simply invariant) if
f(S)\subseteq S
.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
(X,f)
be a dynamical system. For
Y\subseteq X
, we say that
(Y,f|_{Y})
is a
subsystem
of
(X,f)
if
Y
is closed and invariant.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
(X,f)
be a dynamical system, and
M\subseteq X
. We say that
M
is
chain-transitive
(
\mathcal{C}
-
transitive
) if for every
x,y\in M
we have that
x\,\mathcal{C}\,y
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
(X,f)
be a dynamical system and
d:X^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
be a metric on
X
.
We say that
M
is
strong chain-transitive
(
\mathcal{SC}_{d}
-
transitive
) if for every
x,y\in M
we have that
x\,\mathcal{SC}_{d}\,y
.

The following is a strengthening of chain-transitivity which will prove useful later on.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
(X,f)
be a dynamical system, and
M\subseteq X
. We say that
M
is
internally chain transitive
(see e.g.
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
, who use the concept in the case of continuous maps and flows) if for every
x,y\in M
and for every
\epsilon>0
there exists an
\epsilon
-chain form
x
to
y
whose points belong to
M
.

The concept of internal chain transitivity is related to what is called, in case of continuous maps, the
restriction property
for topological dynamical systems (see for instance
[
11
]
, Prop. 2.7.21), which ensures that, setting
CR(X,f)=S
, we have

CR(S,f|_{S})=S.

This means that, for every
x\in S
, the
\epsilon
-chains from
x
to itself can be built with points belonging to
S
. Notice, however, that this does not imply, even in the continuous case, that two distinct points
x,y\in S
that are in chain relation can necessarily be connected by
\epsilon
-chains built with points of
S
, as is shown elementarily by
([0,1],f)
with
f=x^{2}
. Indeed, here,
1\,\mathcal{C}\,0
but the
\epsilon
-chains must of course leave the chain recurrent set
\{0,1\}
.

3
Existence results

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system. Then
CR_{f}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

If there is
x\in X
such that
|\mathcal{O}(x)|<\aleph_{0}
, then at least one point
y
in the orbit of
x
is periodic, so that
y\,\mathcal{C}\,y
. We can therefore assume that every point in
X
has an infinite orbit.
Let
d:X^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
be a metric on
X
.

Step 1

Pick
x\in X
and set
x_{0}:=x
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, set
x_{k}:=f(x_{k-1})
. Pick
y\in\mathcal{O}(x)^{\prime}
(which is nonempty by compactness) and set
x_{\omega}:=y
.
Let us now define a set of points by transfinite induction as follows.
Let
\alpha>\omega
be an ordinal number.
Assume that
x_{\beta}
has been defined for every
\beta<\alpha
and set:

\{x_{\eta}\}_{\beta\leq\eta<\alpha}:=S_{\beta,\alpha}(x)\quad,\quad S_{\alpha}(x):=S_{0,\alpha}(x).

1.

If
\alpha
is a successor ordinal, set
x_{\alpha}:=f(x_{\alpha-1})
.

2.

If
\alpha
is a limit ordinal, then, recalling that every orbit in
X
has infinite cardinality, we have
|S_{\beta,\alpha}(x)|\geq\aleph_{0}
for every
\beta<\alpha
, so
\left(S_{\beta,\alpha}(x)\right)^{\prime}
is closed and, by compactness, nonempty. Therefore,

S^{*}_{\alpha}(x):=\bigcap_{\beta<\alpha}\left(S_{\beta,\alpha}(x)\right)^{\prime}

(2)

is nonempty as well (see for instance
[
12
, Th. 26.9, p. 169]
). Pick then any
y\in S^{*}_{\alpha}(x)
and set
x_{\alpha}:=y
.

Step 2

For every ordinal
\alpha
, we have

x_{\beta}\,\mathcal{C}\,x_{\eta}\quad\text{ whenever }\quad 0\leq\beta<\eta\leq\alpha.

(3)

Let us prove this by induction on
\alpha
.
If
\alpha=1
, the claim is obviously true. Assume then that claim (
3
) is true for every
\eta<\alpha
.
If
\alpha
is a successor ordinal, then
x_{\alpha}=f(x_{\alpha-1})
, and thus, for
0\leq\beta<\eta\leq\alpha
, we have

x_{\beta}\,\mathcal{C}\,x_{\alpha-1}\implies x_{\beta}\,\mathcal{C}\,x_{\alpha}.

Assume that
\alpha
is a limit ordinal and take
\beta<\alpha
and
\epsilon>0
.
Since
x_{\alpha}\in S^{*}_{\alpha}(x)
, there is
\beta<\eta<\alpha
such that
d(x_{\eta},x_{\alpha})<\frac{\epsilon}{2}
.
By the induction hypothesis, we have
x_{\beta}\,\mathcal{C}_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}\,x_{\eta}
, so by the triangle inequality
x_{\beta}\,\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}\,x_{\alpha}
, and since
\epsilon
was arbitrary, we have
x_{\beta}\,\mathcal{C}\,x_{\alpha}
.

Step 3

Now, if for every
\eta<\alpha
we have
x_{\eta}\notin S_{\eta}(x)
, then
x_{\beta}\neq x_{\gamma}
for every distinct
\beta,\gamma<\alpha
, so that the application

\eta\mapsto x_{\eta}\quad\quad(0\leq\eta<\alpha)

(4)

is a bijection between
\alpha=\{\eta:0\leq\eta<\alpha\}
and
S_{\alpha}(x)
, so
we have
|S_{\alpha}(x)|=|\alpha|
.
Assuming
x_{\alpha}\notin S_{\alpha}(x)
for every ordinal
\alpha
, there would be an ordinal
\lambda
so large that
|S_{\lambda}(x)|>|X|
, which is impossible since
S_{\alpha}(x)\subseteq X
.
Therefore,
x_{\alpha}\in S_{\alpha}(x)
for some ordinal
\alpha
, so that:

x_{\alpha}=x_{\beta}\text{ for some }\beta<\alpha.

(5)

This implies, using claim (
3
) with
\eta=\alpha
, that
x_{\alpha}
is chain-recurrent.

∎

Remark 3.2
.

Notice that, since in Step 2 we built every chain using only points indexed by ordinals, for every
\beta<\eta\leq\alpha
and every
\epsilon>0
, there is an
\epsilon
-chain from
x_{\beta}
to
x_{\eta}
whose points belong to
S_{\beta,\eta+1}(x)
.

Remark 3.3
.

Since a compact space has at most cardinality continuum (see
[
13
, Corollary 3.1.30]
), assuming the Generalized Continuum Hypothesis we can take, in the previous proof,
\lambda\leq\aleph_{2}=2^{\mathfrak{c}}
.

If we replace the chain relation
\mathcal{C}
with the relation
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
, the argument in the previous proof is no longer applicable. In fact, in Step 2, for
\alpha=1
claim (
3
) is also true by replacing
\mathcal{C}
with
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
, so we can assume that it is true for
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
for every
\eta<\alpha
. However, when
\alpha
is a successor ordinal, if we have
x_{\beta}\,\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}\,x_{\alpha-1}
for some
0\leq\beta<\eta\leq\alpha
, then, when
f
is discontinuous at
x_{\alpha-1}
, it is not true, in general, that
x_{\beta}\,\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}\,f(x_{\alpha-1})
. This of course is consistent with the example given in Eq. (
1
) in the Introduction.

The following result is an easy consequence of the previous theorem.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system. Then for every
x\in X
there is
y\in X
such that
y
is chain-recurrent and
x\,\mathcal{C}\,y
.

Proof.

In the previous argument, and specifically in Eq.(
5
), take
y=x_{\alpha}
. Since
x=x_{0}
was arbitrary, the result follows.
∎

Notice that, when the map is continuous, the result above can be strengthened by replacing the last relation
x\,\mathcal{C}\,y
by
x\,\mathcal{R}\,y
, and it is a consequence of the existence of a complete Lyapunov function (a seminal work is
[
14
]
).

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system. Then
GR(f)\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

Again we can assume that every orbit in
X
is infinite (otherwise the result follows immediately). Let
\mathcal{D}
be the set of all metrics compatible with the topology on
X
, and pick
d\in\mathcal{D}
.
Pick
x\in X
and set
x_{0}:=x
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, set
x_{k}:=f(x_{k-1})
.
For any ordinal
\alpha
, we define the sets
S_{\alpha}(x)
and
S^{*}_{\alpha}(x)
as done in Step 1 of the proof of Theorem
3.1
. Notice that this construction depends only on the topology and on the map
f
, but does not depend on the metric
d
.

Following the steps in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we want to prove by induction on
\alpha
that

x_{\beta}\,\mathcal{SC}_{d}\,x_{\eta}\quad\text{ whenever }\quad 0\leq\beta<\eta\leq\alpha.

(6)

If
\alpha=1
, the claim is obviously true; therefore, we assume that claim (
6
) is true for every
\eta<\alpha
.
If
\alpha
is a successor ordinal, then
x_{\alpha}=f(x_{\alpha-1})
, and thus, for
0\leq\beta<\eta\leq\alpha
, we have

x_{\beta}\,\mathcal{SC}_{d}\,x_{\alpha-1}\implies x_{\beta}\,\mathcal{SC}_{d}\,x_{\alpha}.

Assume that
\alpha
is a limit ordinal and take
\beta<\alpha
and
\epsilon>0
.
Since
x_{\alpha}\in S^{*}_{\alpha}(x)
, there is
\beta<\eta<\alpha
such that
d(x_{\eta},x_{\alpha})<\frac{\epsilon}{2}
.
By the induction hypothesis, there is a strong
(\frac{\epsilon}{2},d)
-chain
x_{\beta}=x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}=x_{\eta}
from
x_{\beta}
to
x_{\eta}
.
By the triangle inequality, we have:

\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}d(f(x_{i}),x_{i+1})+d(f(x_{n-1}),x_{\alpha})\leq\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}d(f(x_{i}),x_{i+1})+d(x_{\eta},x_{\alpha})<\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}=\epsilon.

Therefore, the points
x_{\beta}=x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1},x_{\alpha}
form a strong
(\epsilon,d)
-chain from
x_{\beta}
to
x_{\alpha}
.
Since
\epsilon
was arbitrary, it follows that
x_{\beta}\,\mathcal{SC}_{d}\,x_{\alpha}
.
Then, by an identical argument as in Step 3 above (with (
6
) in place of (
3
)), we have that there is some ordinal
\alpha
such that
x_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{SCR}_{d}
.

Since claim (
6
) is valid for every choice of the metric
d
(compatible with the topology),
we have

x_{\alpha}\in\bigcap_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{SCR}_{d}(f)=GR(f).

∎

We prove now that every compact system has a chain-transitive subsystem. For this we need a preliminary lemma, which deepens our understanding of the construction in the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system. Then
X
has an invariant,
\mathcal{C}
-transitive subset.

Proof.

If some point is periodic, the periodic orbit is clearly an invariant,
\mathcal{C}
-transitive subset of
X
.
We can then assume that every orbit is infinite.
We will use the notation defined in the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
For every
\alpha
limit ordinal and
\beta\leq\alpha
we set

\Gamma_{\beta,\alpha}:=\{\beta\}\cup\{\beta<\eta<\alpha:\text{$\eta$ is a limit ordinal}\}.

Pick
x\in X
.
Let
\lambda
be an ordinal such that
|\lambda|>|X|
and
S_{\lambda}(x)
a set defined by means of the transfinite procedure described in Step 1 of the proof of Theorem
3.1
. Let
\alpha\leq\lambda
be the least ordinal greater than 0 such that
x_{\alpha}\in S_{\alpha}(x)
.
Then there is an ordinal
0\leq\beta<\alpha
such that
x_{\alpha}=x_{\beta}
and
x_{\alpha}\in CR_{f}
.

If
\alpha
is a limit ordinal, then the set
S_{\beta,\alpha}(x)
can be written as:

S_{\beta,\alpha}(x)=\bigcup_{\eta\in\Gamma_{\beta,\alpha}}\mathcal{O}^{0}(x_{\eta}),

that is, it is a union of orbits and therefore is invariant. Analogously, if
\alpha
is a successor ordinal, that is
\alpha=\gamma+k
for some limit ordinal
\gamma\geq\beta
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
the set
S_{\beta,\alpha}(x)
can be written as:

S_{\beta,\alpha}(x)=\bigcup_{\eta\in\Gamma_{\beta,\gamma}}\mathcal{O}^{0}(x_{\eta})\cup\bigcup_{h=0}^{k-1}f^{h}(x_{\gamma}),

which is again invariant, since in this case
f^{k}(x_{\gamma})=x_{\alpha}=x_{\beta}
.
In both cases, since claim (
3
) holds, that is,

4
Nested chains

Looking closer at the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we can observe that in fact something stronger can be said. Indeed, as we will prove (see Theorem
4.6
), we obtained a point
x
belonging to the diagonal of a relation
\mathcal{C}_{{}_{\subseteq}}
on
X^{2}
, verifying in general
\mathcal{C}_{{}_{\subseteq}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
, defined as follows:

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system and
d:X^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
be a metric on
X
.
We say that
x\,\mathcal{C}_{{}_{\subseteq}}y
if, for a non-increasing sequence of positive reals
\{\epsilon_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to zero, there is a collection
\{C_{\epsilon_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\epsilon_{n}
-chains from
x
to
y
such that
C_{\epsilon_{n}}\subseteq C_{\epsilon_{n+1}}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We call
\mathcal{C}_{{}_{\subseteq}}
the
nested-chain
relation and
\{C_{\epsilon_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
the nested
\epsilon
-chains.

A point
x\in X
is called
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
-recurrent
if
x\,\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}\,x
.

It is easy to see that, if
x\,\mathcal{C}_{{}_{\subseteq}}y
, then the sequence of nested
\epsilon
-chains
\{C_{\epsilon_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
works (possibly up to subsequence) for
every
positive sequence
\{\epsilon_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to zero, in the sense that, if
\delta_{j}\to 0
, then there is a subsequence
\{n_{j}\}
such that, for every
j
,
C_{\epsilon_{n_{j}}}
is a
\delta_{j}
chain from
x
to
y
and we have
C_{\epsilon_{n_{j}}}\subseteq C_{\epsilon_{n_{j+1}}}
.
The relation
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
can be formulated, just like
\mathcal{C}
, in pure topological terms:

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system. Then,

x\,\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}\,y
if and only if, for a sequence of open neighborhood
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\Delta X
such that:

1)

\overline{U_{i+1}}\subseteq U_{i}
for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
,

2)

\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\overline{U_{i}}=\Delta X
,

for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
and a set
C_{i}=\{x=x_{0},\dots,x_{n_{i}}=y\}
such that:

3)

(f(x_{j}),x_{j+1})\in U_{i}\text{ for every }j=0,\ldots,n_{i}-1
,

and the family
\{C_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
verifies:

4)

C_{i}\subseteq C_{i+1}
for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Let
d
be a metric compatible with the topology on
X
.
Let us assume that
x\,\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}\,y
. Therefore, there are a non-increasing sequence of positive reals
\{\epsilon_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to zero and a collection
\{C_{\epsilon_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\epsilon_{k}
-chains from
x
to
y
such that
C_{\epsilon_{k}}\subseteq C_{\epsilon_{k+1}}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then, there exists a strictly decreasing subsequence
\{\epsilon_{k_{i}}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{\epsilon_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
.
For every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we set
U_{i}:=B_{\epsilon_{k_{i}}}(\Delta X)
.
Since
\epsilon_{k_{i}}\to 0
as
i\to\infty
, we have
\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\overline{U_{k_{i}}}=\Delta X
and since the sequence
\{\epsilon_{k_{i}}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is strictly decreasing, it follows that
\overline{U_{i+1}}\subseteq U_{i}
for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
Set
C_{i}:=C_{\epsilon_{k_{i}}}
for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

C_{i}=C_{\epsilon_{k_{i}}}\subseteq C_{\epsilon_{k_{i+1}}}=C_{i+1}\quad\text{for every }i\in\mathbb{N}.

Moreover, since
C_{\epsilon_{k_{i}}}
is an
\epsilon_{k_{i}}
-chain from
x
to
y
we have
C_{i}=C_{\epsilon_{k_{i}}}=\{x=x_{0},\ldots,x_{n_{i}}=y\}
for some
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
and

(f(x_{j}),x_{j+1})\in B_{\epsilon_{k_{i}}}(\Delta X)=U_{i}\quad\text{for every }j=0,\ldots,n_{i}-1.

Let us prove the converse implication.
Suppose that for
x,y\in X
there are a sequence of open neighborhoods
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\Delta X
with properties 1) and 2) and a collection of sets
\{C_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with properties 3) and 4).
Since

is compact and exploiting the properties of

(see
[

, Prop. A.28]
),
we can define the sequence
\{\epsilon_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
by induction as follows.

Set
k_{0}:=1
. By
[
3
, Prop. A.28-(2)]
there is
\epsilon_{1}>0
such that

.
By
[

there exists
k_{1}>k_{0}
such that
U_{k_{1}}\subseteq\overline{U_{k_{1}}}\subseteq B_{\epsilon_{1}}(\Delta X)
.
Assume that
\epsilon_{i}
and

have been defined for every

such that
U_{k_{i}}\subseteq B_{\epsilon_{i}}(\Delta X)\subseteq U_{k_{i-1}}
.
By
[

, Prop. A.28-(2)]
there exists
\epsilon_{n+1}>0
such that
B_{\epsilon_{n+1}}(\Delta X)\subseteq U_{k_{n}}
. By
[

there exists

sufficiently large that

By construction, the sequence

is non-increasing and by 2) it follows that

as

Set

for every

. Since

, and

Example 4.3

Let

\sigma:\{0,1\}^{\omega}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\omega}

\left(\sigma(u)\right)_{n}=u_{n+1},\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N},

Set

d(u,v)=2^{-(n-1)},\ \text{where $n:=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}:u_{n}\neq v_{n}\}$}.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\omega}
be the subshift defined by the following countable set of forbidden words:

\{01^{j}0\text{ such that }j\in\mathbb{N}\}\quad\cup\quad\{1^{j}0^{h}\text{ such that }j>h\}.

Defining the words:

w_{k}:=1^{k}0^{k}1^{\infty},

the subshift
\Sigma
consists of the words

\sigma^{j}(w_{k})\quad(j\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\ k\in\mathbb{N})

and the limit points

\lim_{k\to\infty}w_{k}=1^{\infty}\quad,\quad\lim_{k\to\infty}\sigma^{k}w_{k}=0^{\infty}.

Consider the subshift
\Sigma
. We want to show that
(1^{\infty},0^{\infty})\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
.

Take
\epsilon>0
, and let
n>0
be such that
2^{-n}<\epsilon
. If we set
x_{i}:=\sigma^{(i-1)}(w_{n})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, then the points
1^{\infty}=x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},x_{n+1}=0^{\infty}
form an
\epsilon
-chain from
1^{\infty}
to
0^{\infty}
. Indeed, we have that

•

d(\sigma(1^{\infty}),x_{1})=d(1^{\infty},1^{n}0^{n}1^{\infty})=2^{-n}<\epsilon
;

•

d(\sigma(x_{i}),x_{i+1})=d(x_{i+1},x_{i+1})=0
for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
;

•

d(\sigma(x_{n}),0^{\infty})=d(\sigma^{n}(w_{n}),0^{\infty})=d(0^{n}1^{\infty},0^{\infty})=2^{-n}<\epsilon
.

Hence
1^{\infty}\,\mathcal{C}\,0^{\infty}
.
It remains to be proven that
(1^{\infty},0^{\infty})\notin\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
.
Suppose, towards a contradiction, that
1^{\infty}\,\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}\,0^{\infty}
.
Let
\{\epsilon_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a non-increasing sequence of positive reals converging to zero such that
2^{-(n+1)}<\epsilon_{n}<2^{-n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then, there is a collection of
\epsilon_{n}
-chains,
\{C_{\epsilon_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
from
1^{\infty}
to
0^{\infty}
such that, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
C_{\epsilon_{n}}\subseteq C_{\epsilon_{n+1}}
.
Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
1^{\infty}\,\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon_{n}}\,0^{\infty}
, there are
m\geq h>n
such that
u=1^{h}0^{m}1^{\infty}\in C_{\epsilon_{n}}
.
We will prove that
u\notin C_{\epsilon_{2m}}
, contradicting the inclusion
C_{\epsilon_{n}}\subseteq C_{\epsilon_{2m}}
.
Notice that since
1^{\infty}
is a fixed point, without loss of generality, we can assume that the point
x_{1}
of the
\epsilon_{2m}
-chain
C_{\epsilon_{2m}}
is equal to
1^{k}0^{p}1^{\infty}
for some
p\geq k>2m
.
The orbit of
x_{1}
is given by

\mathcal{O}(x_{1})=\{1^{k-j}0^{p}1^{\infty}\}_{j=1}^{k-1}\cup\{0^{p-j}1^{\infty}\}_{j=0}^{p-1}\cup\{1^{\infty}\}.

Since
p\geq k\geq 2m+1
, we have that
1^{h}0^{m}1
is not a subword of any element of
\mathcal{O}(x_{1})
, thus we have:

d(u,\mathcal{O}(x_{1}))\geq 2^{-2m}>\epsilon_{2m}.

By the definition of
\Sigma
, it is clear that
(x,u)\notin\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon_{2m}}
for all
x\in\mathcal{O}(x_{1})
.
Then
u\notin C_{\epsilon_{2m}}
and we can conclude.

Remark 4.4
.

Notice that in the previous example we have shown something stronger, that is,
1^{\infty}\,\mathcal{N}\,0^{\infty}
. This proves that in general, even for continuous maps,
\mathcal{N}\not\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}

Let us now prove that the chain-recurrent point whose existence is proved in Theorem
3.1
is, in fact, a
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
-recurrent point.
For this, we start with the following lemma showing that claim (
3
) also applies with respect to the relation
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system and
x\in X
.
For ordinals
\zeta<\lambda
, let
S_{\zeta,\lambda}(x)
and
S_{\lambda}(x)=S_{0,\lambda}
be sets defined as in Step 1 of the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
Then

x_{\eta}\,\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}\,x_{\xi}\quad\text{whenever}\quad 0\leq\eta<\xi\leq\lambda.

Proof.

Let
d
be a metric compatible with the topology on
X
and take
0\leq\eta<\xi\leq\lambda
. Let
\{\epsilon_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
be a non-increasing sequence of positive reals converging to zero.
By Theorem
3.1
and Remark
3.2
there is an
\epsilon_{1}
-chain
C_{\epsilon_{1}}\subseteq S_{\eta,\xi+1}(x)
from
x_{\eta}
to
x_{\xi}
.

Proceeding by induction, assume that, for every positive integer
m\leq n
, there is an
\epsilon_{m}
-chain
C_{\epsilon_{m}}\subseteq S_{\eta,\xi+1}(x)
from
x_{\eta}
to
x_{\xi}
, and that, for all
m<n
, one has
C_{\epsilon_{m}}\subseteq C_{\epsilon_{m+1}}
.
Assume that
C_{\epsilon_{n}}
consists of the points:

x_{\eta}=x_{\beta_{0}},x_{\beta_{1}},\dots,x_{\beta_{k}}=x_{\xi}\quad\quad(\eta=\beta_{0}<\beta_{1}<\dots<\beta_{k}=\xi\leq\lambda).

By Remark
3.2
, for every
0\leq j<k
, there is an
\epsilon_{n+1}
-chain
C_{\epsilon_{n+1}}^{j}\subseteq S_{\beta_{j},(\beta_{j+1}+1)}(x)
from
x_{\beta_{j}}
to
x_{\beta_{j+1}}
, consisting of the points:

x_{\beta_{j}}=x_{\beta_{j_{1}}},x_{\beta_{j_{2}}},\dots,x_{\beta_{j_{h_{j}}}}=x_{\beta_{j+1}}\quad\quad(\beta_{j}=\beta_{j_{1}}<\beta_{j_{2}}<\dots<\beta_{j_{h_{j}}}=\beta_{j+1}).

Therefore, the points

x_{\eta}=x_{\beta_{0}}=x_{{\beta_{0}}_{1}},x_{{\beta_{0}}_{2}},\dots,x_{{\beta_{0}}_{h_{0}}}=x_{{\beta_{1}}_{1}},\dots,x_{{\beta_{1}}_{h_{1}}}=x_{{\beta_{2}}_{1}},\dots\dots\dots,x_{{\beta_{k-1}}_{h_{k-1}}}=x_{\beta_{k}}=x_{\xi}

form an
\epsilon_{n+1}
-chain
C_{\epsilon_{n+1}}
from
x_{\eta}
to
x_{\xi}
such that

C_{\epsilon_{n}}\subseteq C_{\epsilon_{n+1}}=\bigcup_{j=0}^{k-1}C_{\epsilon_{n+1}}^{j}\subseteq S_{\eta,\xi+1}(x),

and we are done.
∎

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system. Then there is a chain-recurrent point
x\in X
such that
x\,\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}\,x
.

Proof.

Pick
x\in X
.
Let
\lambda
be an ordinal such that
|\lambda|>|X|
and
S_{\lambda}(x)
a set defined by means of the transfinite procedure described in Step 1 of the proof of Theorem
3.1
. Let
\alpha\leq\lambda
be the least ordinal greater than 0 such that
x_{\alpha}\in S_{\alpha}(x)
, which means that
x_{\alpha}=x_{\beta}=:z
for some
0\leq\beta<\alpha
.
Therefore, we have
z\in CR_{f}
.
Applying Lemma
4.5
with
\eta=\beta
and
\xi=\alpha
, we obtain that
z\,\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}\,z
.
∎

Definition 4.7
.

Let
(X,f)
be a dynamical system, and
M\subseteq X
. We say that
M
is
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
-transitive
if for every
x,y\in M
we have that
x\,\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}\,y
.

We now prove that every compact system has a
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
-transitive subsystem. For this, we need a preliminary lemma.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system and
M
be a subset of
X
.
If
M
is internally chain transitive, then
M
is
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
-transitive.

Proof.

Let
d
be a metric compatible with the topology on
X
. Pick
x,y\in M
and let
\{\epsilon_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
be a non-increasing sequence of positive reals converging to zero.
Since
M
is internally chain transitive, there is an
\epsilon_{1}
-chain
C_{\epsilon_{1}}\subseteq M
from
x
to
y
.

Proceeding by induction, assume that, for every positive integer
m\leq n
, there is an
\epsilon_{m}
-chain
C_{\epsilon_{m}}\subseteq M
from
x
to
y
, and that, for all
m<n
, one has
C_{\epsilon_{m}}\subseteq C_{\epsilon_{m+1}}
.
Assume that
C_{\epsilon_{n}}
consists of the points:

x=x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{k}=y.

Since
C_{\epsilon_{n}}\subseteq M
and
M
is internally chain transitive, for every
0\leq j<k
, there is an
\epsilon_{n+1}
-chain
C_{\epsilon_{n+1}}^{j}\subseteq M
from
x_{j}
to
x_{j+1}
, consisting of the points:

x_{j}=x_{j_{1}},x_{j_{2}},\dots,x_{j_{h_{j}}}=x_{j+1}.

Therefore, the points

x=x_{0}=x_{{0}_{1}},x_{{0}_{2}},\dots,x_{{0}_{h_{0}}}=x_{{1}_{1}},\dots,x_{{1}_{h_{1}}}=x_{{2}_{1}},\dots\dots\dots,x_{{k-1}_{h_{k-1}}}=x_{k}=y

form an
\epsilon_{n+1}
-chain
C_{\epsilon_{n+1}}
from
x
to
y
such that

C_{\epsilon_{n}}\subseteq C_{\epsilon_{n+1}}=\bigcup_{j=0}^{k-1}C_{\epsilon_{n+1}}^{j}\subseteq M,

and we are done.
∎

In general, the converse implication does not hold, as elementary examples show.
Consider indeed the set
S=\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
and the map
f:S\rightarrow S
defined as
f(x)=(x+1)\mod n
.
Then, for any proper subset
M\subsetneq S
we see that
M
is
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
-transitive but not internally chain transitive.
It is also easy to give examples in which
M
is invariant. For instance, consider the logistic map with parameter 4 (that is the system
([0,1],f
, with
f(x)=4x(1-x))
) and take
M=\{0,\frac{3}{4}\}
, or the identity map on
[0,1]
and take
M
equal to any subset of
[0,1]
containing an isolated point in the relative topology. Since
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
, this also shows that in general chain-transitivity does not imply internal chain transitivity.

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system.
If
X
is
\mathcal{C}
-transitive, then
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}=\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{C}=X^{2}
, we have that
X
is internally chain transitive.
By Lemma
4.8
,
X
is
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
-transitive, which means that
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}=X^{2}=\mathcal{C}
.
∎

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
X
be a compact dynamical system. Then
(X,f)
has a
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
-transitive subsystem.

Proof.

We will use the notation defined in the proof of Theorem
3.9
.

By Theorem
3.9
, the dynamical system
(X,f)
has a
\mathcal{C}
-transitive subsystem. More precisely, there exists a least ordinal
\nu
such that the set
\{x^{\gamma}\}_{\gamma<\nu}
is defined, while it is impossible to define the point
x^{\nu}
.

Let us assume that
\nu
is a successor ordinal. Then one of the following verifies:

1.

The set
S_{\beta_{\nu-1},\alpha_{\nu-1}}(x^{\nu-1})
is a closed, invariant and internally chain transitive subset.
By Lemma
4.8
, it follows that
S_{\beta_{\nu-1},\alpha_{\nu-1}}(x^{\nu-1})
is
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
-transitive and we are done.

2.

The set
S_{\beta_{\nu-1},\alpha_{\nu-1}}(x^{\nu-1})
is not closed and there exists an ordinal
\xi<\nu
such that the set
M_{\xi,\nu}
is closed, invariant and internally chain transitive.
By Lemma
4.8
, it follows that
M_{\xi,\nu}
is
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
-transitive and we conclude.

On the other hand, assume that
\nu
is a limit ordinal.
By the argument in the proof of Theorem
3.9
, if we cannot define the point
x^{\nu}
, then there exists an ordinal
\xi<\nu
such that
M_{\xi,\nu}
is a closed, invariant and internally chain transitive subset, that is a chain transitive subsystem.
By Lemma
4.8
, it follows that
M_{\xi,\nu}
is
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
-transitive and we conclude.
∎

The relation
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
is clearly transitive. In the next example, we show that the relation
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
is, in general, not closed even if the map is continuous.

Example 4.11
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\omega}
be the subshift defined by the following countable set of forbidden words:

\{01^{j}0\text{ such that }j\geq 2\}\quad\cup\quad\{1^{j}0^{h}\text{ such that }j>h\}.

Set
w_{k}:=1^{k}0^{k}10^{\infty}.

The subshift
\Sigma
is given by the words

\{\sigma^{j}(w_{k}):j,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}\cup\{1^{\infty}\}\cup\{0^{\infty}\}

since

\lim_{k\to\infty}w_{k}=1^{\infty}\quad,\quad\lim_{k\to\infty}\sigma^{k}w_{k}=0^{\infty}.

We want to show that the relation
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
is not closed as a subset of
\Sigma^{2}
.
Since
\sigma^{2k+1}(w_{k})=0^{\infty}
, it is clear that
w_{k}\,\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}\,0^{\infty}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, since
1^{\infty}
is a limit point of the sequence
\{w_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, it is sufficient to prove that
(1^{\infty},0^{\infty})\notin\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
.

Let us proceed by contradiction, assuming that
1^{\infty}\,\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}\,0^{\infty}
. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we take
\epsilon_{n}
such that
2^{-(n+1)}<\epsilon_{n}<2^{-n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
1^{\infty}\,\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}\,0^{\infty}
, there is a family
\{C_{\epsilon_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\epsilon_{n}
-chains from
1^{\infty}
to
0^{\infty}
such that
C_{\epsilon_{n}}\subseteq C_{\epsilon_{n+1}}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Take
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
1^{\infty}\,\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon_{n}}\,0^{\infty}
, there are
m\geq h>n
such that
u=1^{h}0^{m}10^{\infty}\in C_{\epsilon_{n}}
.
We will prove that
u\notin C_{\epsilon_{2m}}
, contradicting the inclusion
C_{\epsilon_{n}}\subseteq C_{\epsilon_{2m}}
.
Since
1^{\infty}
is a fixed point, without loss of generality, we can assume that the point
x_{1}
of
C_{\epsilon_{2m}}
is equal to
1^{k}0^{p}10^{\infty}
for some
p\geq k>2m
.
The orbit of
x_{1}
is given by

\mathcal{O}(x_{1})=\{1^{k-j}0^{p}10^{\infty}\}_{j=1}^{k-1}\cup\{0^{p-j}10^{\infty}\}_{j=0}^{p}\cup\{0^{\infty}\}.

Since
p\geq k\geq 2m+1
, we have that
1^{h}0^{m}1
is not a subword of any element of
\mathcal{O}(x_{1})
, thus we have:

d(u,\mathcal{O}(x_{1}))\geq 2^{-2m}>\epsilon_{2m}.

By the definition of
\Sigma
, it is clear that
(x,u)\notin\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon_{2m}}
for all
x\in\mathcal{O}(x_{1})
. Therefore,
u\notin C_{\epsilon_{2m}}
and we can conclude.
