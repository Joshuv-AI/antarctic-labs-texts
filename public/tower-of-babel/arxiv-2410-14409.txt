Abstract
. We consider the problem of sampling from the ferromagnetic q q -state Potts model on the random d d -regular graph with parameter β > 0 \beta>0 . A key difficulty that arises in sampling from the model is the existence of a “metastability” window β ∈ ( β u , β u ′ ) \beta\in(\beta_{u},\beta_{u}^{\prime}) , where roughly the distribution has two competing modes, the so-called disordered and ordered phases. This causes classical Markov-chain algorithms to be slow mixing from worst-case initialisations. Nevertheless, Helmuth, Jenssen and Perkins (SODA ’19) designed a sampling algorithm that works for all β \beta , when d ≥ 5 d\geq 5 and q = d Ω ⁡ ( d ) q=d^{\Omega(d)} , using polymers and cluster expansion methods; more recently, their analysis technique has been adapted to show that a Markov chain (random-cluster dynamics) mixes fast when initialised appropriately, in the same regime of q , d , β q,d,\beta . Despite these positive algorithmic results, a well-known bottleneck behind cluster-expansion arguments is that they inherently only work for large q q , whereas it is widely conjectured that sampling on the random d d -regular graph is possible for all q , d ≥ 3 q,d\geq 3 . The only result so far that applies to general q , d ≥ 3 q,d\geq 3 is by Blanca and Gheissari who showed that the random-cluster dynamics mixes fast in the “uniqueness” regime β < β u \beta<\beta_{u} where roughly only the disordered mode exists. For β ≥ β u \beta\geq\beta_{u} however, a second subdominant mode emerges creating bottlenecks and giving rise to correlations which have been hard to handle, especially for small values of q q and d d . Our main contribution is to perform a delicate analysis of the Potts distribution and the random-cluster dynamics that goes beyond the threshold β u \beta_{u} . We use planting as the main tool, a technique used in the analysis of random CSPs to capture how the space of solutions is correlated with the structure of the random instance. While planting arguments provide only weak sampling guarantees generically, here we instead combine planting with the analysis of random-cluster dynamics to obtain significantly stronger guarantees. We are thus able to show that the random-cluster dynamics initialised from all-out mixes fast for all integers q , d ≥ 3 q,d\geq 3 beyond the uniqueness threshold β u \beta_{u} , all the way to the optimal threshold β c ∈ ( β u , β u ′ ) \beta_{c}\in(\beta_{u},\beta_{u}^{\prime}) where the dominant mode switches from disordered to ordered. A more involved analysis also applies to the ordered regime β > β c \beta>\beta_{c} where we obtain an algorithm for all d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 and q ≥ ( 5 ​ d ) 5 q\geq(5d)^{5} , improving significantly upon the previous range of q , d q,d by Carlson, Davies, Fraiman, Kolla, Potukuchi, and Yap (FOCS’22).



1.
Introduction

The Potts model is a weighted model
assigning probabilities to
(non-proper)
q
-colourings. The model originated in statistical physics but has since been a central object of study in various contexts; here, we focus on the computational problem of sampling from the model.

For an integer
q\geq 3
and a graph
G=(V,E)
, the
q
-state Potts model on
G
with parameter
\beta
is a probability distribution
\mu=\mu_{G,q,\beta}
on
[q]^{V}
where
[q]=\{1,\ldots,q\}
. Each configuration
\sigma\in[q]^{V}
has weight
\mu(\sigma)=\mathrm{e}^{\beta m(\sigma)}/Z_{G}
where
m(\sigma)
is the number of monochromatic edges under
\sigma
; the normalising factor
Z_{G}
is the partition function. Throughout, we consider the “ferromagnetic” case
\beta>0
, where configurations with many monochromatic edges are favoured in the distribution.

From a computational complexity perspective, sampling from the ferromagnetic Potts model has various twists relative to other similar models and the complexity of the sampling problem is widely open. On general graphs, the problem is #BIS-hard for any fixed
\beta>0

[
23
]
; for graphs of max degree
d
(where
d
is a fixed integer), the problem becomes #BIS-hard when
\beta>\beta_{c}

[
19
]
, where
\beta_{c}=\ln\frac{q-2}{(q-1)^{1-2/d}-1}
is known as the ordered/disordered threshold (we will discuss this in more detail shortly). It is also conjectured that the problem admits a polynomial time algorithm when
\beta<\beta_{c}
but this is open in general; in fact, many of the standard tools that are used to analyse Markov chains provably fail well below the
\beta_{c}
threshold. The quintessential example here is the random
d
-regular graph, which underpins all the relevant phenomena behind this picture. For
dn
even, we let
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{G}_{n,d}
be a graph chosen uniformly at random from the set of all
d
-regular graphs with
n
vertices. We use “whp” as a shorthand for “with probability
1-o(1)
as
n
grows large”.

For a typical random graph
\mathbb{G}
, it is known that
a sample from the Potts distribution
\mu_{\mathbb{G}}
is “disordered” for
\beta<\beta_{c}
, and “ordered” for
\beta>\beta_{c}
; roughly, this means that the colours in the former case appear equally often whereas in the second case one of the colours strictly dominates over the rest (see Lemma
2.1
for the formal statement).
The intricate feature of the Potts model on the random
d
-regular graph is that these competing modes are both present
as “local maxima”
throughout a window
(\beta_{u},\beta_{u}^{\prime})
containing
\beta_{c}

even though only one of them has the vast majority of the mass (except at

\beta=\beta_{c}
itself, where both modes appear with constant probability). This already poses problems to standard MCMC algorithms such as Glauber and Swendsen-Wang dynamics since the simultaneous presence of the modes causes exponentially slow mixing from worst-case initialisations, see
[
25
,
14
]
. At a more conceptual level, any sampling algorithm has to take into account the presence of the other mode which obliterates from the very start standard analysis tools (e.g., correlation decay/spectral independence).

Helmuth, Jenssen and Perkins
[
25
]
introduced a cluster-expansion technique that allowed them to control more precisely how much a typical sample differs from the corresponding mode, and obtained an algorithm based on the interpolation method for all
\beta>0
when
d\geq 5
and
q\geq d^{\Omega(d)}
; their algorithm applies more generally to expander graphs (under some mild conditions). See also the works
[
16
,
11
,
12
]
for various refinements of their method. Following a series of developments
[
20
,
21
]
, this cluster expansion expansion argument has been converted into an MCMC algorithm (for large
d
and
q
)
[
18
]
using the random-cluster dynamics with appropriate initialisation that avoids the bottlenecks in the distribution (see Section
2.2
for details). See also
[
10
,
8
,
22
]
for closely related results on the random
d
-regular graph (and lattices) that apply for large
\beta
.

Despite these positive algorithmic developments, the cluster expansion technique that underpins the analysis of these algorithms is inherently “perturbative”, relying roughly on controlling how much a typical configuration differs from the max-energy configurations and arguing that the difference is relatively small. In the case of the Potts model, the resulting estimates are accurate only when
q
is quite large relative to
d
. By contrast, it is conjectured that sampling on the random regular graph should be possible for all
q,d\geq 3
(and all
\beta>0
), and hence more precise tools are needed to settle the picture for small
q,d
. The only result so far that works for general
q,d\geq 3
is by Blanca and Gheissari
[
9
]
who showed that random-cluster dynamics mixes in
O(n\log n)
time when
\beta<\beta_{u}
, for arbitrary initialisation. Note that for
\beta>\beta_{u}
the results of
[
14
]
give worst-case initialisations that slow down the mixing time of the Glauber dynamics to
\mathrm{e}^{\Omega(n)}
; more generally, for
\beta>\beta_{u}
, the presence of the ordered mode imposes correlation phenomena that are hard to handle, especially for small values of
q,d
.

1.1.
Results

Our main contribution is to do a delicate analysis of the Potts distribution and the random-cluster dynamics that goes beyond the threshold
\beta_{u}
, using planting. Planting is a technique used in the analysis of random CSPs to capture how the space of solutions is correlated with the structure of the random instance, see
[
1
,
15
,
5
]
for some applications. In terms of sampling however, there is no recipe for converting planting methods to efficient sampling algorithms, and typically some extra sampling step is needed on top, see for example
[
2
,
17
]
for such approaches; even so, the resulting sample usually has some accuracy limitation, i.e., the TV-distance from the target distribution cannot be made arbitrarily small (typically
it is required to be at least an inverse polynomial in
n
). Here, we instead use planting as a means to obtain sharp estimates that are used in the analysis of random-cluster dynamics; we thus show fast convergence of the latter, yielding automatically strong sampling guarantees. A different sampling idea that uses planting in a somewhat related manner has recently been considered in
[
26
]
, in the context of sampling from spherical spin glasses.

Using this planting framework, we give an algorithm to sample from the Potts distribution on the random
d
-regular graph
\mathbb{G}
that covers the optimal range of “high temperatures”
\beta<\beta_{c}
for all integers
q,d\geq 3
; as we will explain in more detail later the algorithm is running random-cluster dynamics for
O(n\log n)
steps, initialised appropriately (from the “empty” configuration).

Theorem 1.1
.

For integers

and real

, the following holds whp over
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{G}_{n,d}
.

There is an MCMC algorithm that, on input

satisfies

.

Our analysis framework also applies to the ordered regime

. For example,

1.2.
Proof outline

Our algorithms are based on a well-known edge representation of the Potts model, the random-cluster (RC) model, where configurations are edge subsets weighted by the number of edges present and the number of induced components. We run the random-cluster dynamics (the analogue of Glauber dynamics in this edge setting), starting from the empty set of edges in the disordered regime
\beta<\beta_{c}
and the full set of edges in the ordered regime
\beta>\beta_{c}
. The reason for working in the RC representation is that, in contrast to the Potts model, the RC model enjoys certain monotonicity properties that facilitate tracking the evolution of Glauber dynamics. In the next Preliminaries section we define the RC model and the relevant random-cluster chain. We also give a more detailed overview of the phases of the Potts model, and how planting gives a handle for the disordered and ordered settings. Most of these parts are largely imported from
[
14
,
19
]
. In Section
3
, we convert the planting for Potts into suitable planting for the RC model, giving the analogue of the ordered and disordered phases in that setting. Then, we state the main contribution of this work which is establishing weak spatial mixing within the disordered and ordered phases (a notion that originated in
[
20
]
). Sections
4
and
5
give the technical core of our arguments.

Roughly, in the disordered regime (Section
4
), the key ingredient is to show that the dynamics converges to a set of configurations that do not form large components. For
\beta<\beta_{u}
, Blanca and Gheissari
[
9
]
accomplished this by a very careful random-graph revealing process coupled with the evolution of random-cluster dynamics. Using planting, we are able to capture precisely the correlation between the random graph and a sample from stationarity for
\beta<\beta_{c}
, reducing the study of the component structure to showing that the planted random graph is subcritical. Using the monotonicity of the RC model, this implies that the RC dynamics initialised from the empty set (“all-out”) enjoys the same component structure as the stationary distribution (for exponentially many steps). Having this in place (Lemma
4.2
), the rest of the proof is more streamlined after some small reworking of certain correlation decay estimates up to the ordered/disordered threshold
\beta_{c}
(Lemma
4.3
).

In the ordered regime (Section
5
), the planted model is instead supercritical and a typical configuration has a “giant” component, whose presence complicates the arguments significantly. The goal now is roughly to show that bichromatic edges do not typically form large components; more precisely, the key ingredient is to show that every vertex
v
is surrounded by close-by vertices (each at some distance
\ell<\tfrac{1}{2}\log_{d-1}n
) that belong to the giant component, a so-called “wired” boundary. Planting here allows us to do a head-on analysis of the probability that, in a typical sample from stationarity, there exists a path of length
\ell
that does not include any vertex in the giant. We get a tight bound for this using careful first moment arguments and percolation bounds; then, for
q\geq(5d)^{5}
, a union bound over the paths starting at
v
gives the existence of the desired wired boundary (in a typical configuration). Relative to the earlier works
[
25
,
16
,
11
,
18
]
, bichromatic components roughly correspond to so-called polymers; our techniques show that a smaller lower bound on the size of the giant component suffices in order to ensure that the polymers are not large.

1.3.
Further Discussion

The planting arguments that we use in this paper follow by careful first and second moment considerations in
[
19
]
, which have been carried out for the ferromagnetic Potts model, see also
[
4
]
for similar-flavored “silent” planting results. The moment analysis has previously been used in
[
13
]
for sampling independent sets and proper colourings in random bipartite graphs, though there the approach ultimately relies on cluster-expansion type of arguments
[
28
,
12
]
. Our more direct planting approach can be carried out for sampling independent sets on random bipartite graphs (that shares similar monotonicity and planting properties) and obtain sharper bounds for sampling using Glauber dynamics. Note however that, as pointed out in
[
13
, Lemma 10]
, it is unlikely that current polymer-based techniques will achieve better asymptotic estimates in terms of the degree
d
. Our technique should also apply to the
G(n,d/n)
setting (for arbitrary
q,d>1
) provided that the analogous “silent” planting is in place; this seems more easily feasible in the disordered regime but more difficult for the ordered regime where the Poisson-tree neighbourhoods are expected to cause bigger variance.

It is also relevant to note that the results we stated earlier for the cluster-expansion technique and fast mixing of the random-cluster dynamics in the literature apply to non-integer
q
, in the random-cluster representation. It is conceivable that a similar planting to the one we consider here for the Potts model can be carried out for non-integer
q
in the random-cluster representation, though the related first and second moment computations will likely require careful arguments for the distribution of the number of components in random graph percolation; see
[
6
]
for some related developments.

Preliminaries

2.1.
The configuration model

To obtain a handle on the random

2.2.
The random-cluster dynamics

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
and real parameters

For integer

Let

The random-cluster (RC) dynamics initialised from a configuration

(1)

First choose an edge

(2)

If

, then with probability

. With all remaining probability set
X_{t+1}:=X_{t}\setminus\{e\}

(3)

Otherwise, with probability
p

X_{t+1}:=X_{t}\cup\{e\}
. With all remaining probability set
X_{t+1}:=X_{t}\setminus\{e\}
.

It is a standard fact that the distribution of

converges to
\pi_{G}
, regardless of the initialisation. To work around the slow convergence in the metastability window when

) and the all-in

(\hat{\boldsymbol{G}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\mathrm{o}}),\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\mathrm{o}}\big)
as the disordered and ordered planted distributions respectively.

The following lemma allows us to relate the planted distributions with the distributions of the (conditional) disordered

and ordered

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
d,q\geq 3
be integers and
\beta>0
be real. Let

be

functions with

be an arbitrary event.

(1)

\beta\leq\beta_{c}
: if
\mathbb{P}\big[\big(\hat{\boldsymbol{G}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\mathrm{d}}),\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\mathrm{d}}\big)\in{\mathcal{E}}\big]\leq g(n)

\beta\geq\beta_{c}

\mathbb{P}\big[(\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{G},\mathrm{o}}\big)\in{\mathcal{E}}\mid\boldsymbol{G}\big]\leq\frac{g(n)}{f(n)}

The proof is given in Appendix

. To utilise Lemma

G\in\mathcal{G}_{n,d}
and a configuration

, let

be vertex colour statistics under
\sigma

for a colour

\nu^{\sigma}_{i}=|\sigma^{-1}(i)|/n
.

Similarly, let

\mbox{for colours $i,j\in[q]$, $\rho^{G,\sigma}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2|E(G)|}\sum_{u,v\in V(G)}\mathbf{1}\big\{\{u,v\}\in E(G),\sigma(u)=i,\sigma(v)=j\big\}$}.

Recall that

So, for a colour class

.
Observe further that, conditioned on
\nu^{\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\mathrm{d}}}=\nu
and
\rho^{\hat{\boldsymbol{G}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\mathrm{d}}),\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\mathrm{d}}}=\rho
the pair
(\hat{\boldsymbol{G}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\mathrm{d}}),\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\mathrm{d}})
is uniformly distributed over the pairs

with

. Similarly for the pair

conditioned on

and

.

To put this observation into use, we will need more quantitative information on the vertex and edge colour statistics in the planted models. By definition of

\rho^{\hat{\boldsymbol{G}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\mathrm{d}}),\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\mathrm{d}}}

are concentrated around the vectors

and

\mbox{for $i,j\in[q]$},\quad\rho_{\mathrm{d},ij}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta\mathbf{1}\{i=j\}}}{\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in[q]}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\mathbf{1}\{i^{\prime}=j^{\prime}\}}},\quad\rho_{\mathrm{o},ij}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta\mathbf{1}\{i=j\}}(\nu_{\mathrm{o},i}\nu_{\mathrm{o},j})^{(d-1)/d}}{\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in[q]}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\mathbf{1}\{i^{\prime}=j^{\prime}\}}(\nu_{\mathrm{o},i^{\prime}}\nu_{\mathrm{o},j^{\prime}})^{(d-1)/d}}.

We have the following concentration statement.

(

)
.

Let

be integers

3.
Mixing within the disordered and ordered phases for the RC model

3.1.
Phases for the random cluster model via Potts

For a graph
G\in\mathcal{G}_{n,d}
and
\sigma\in[q]^{n}
, let
\boldsymbol{F}(G,\sigma)
denote the random subset of edges obtained by keeping each monochromatic edge of
G
under
\sigma
with probability
p
. It is a standard fact (see, e.g.,
[
24
]
) that, for integer
q\geq 3
, we can obtain a sample
\boldsymbol{F}\sim\pi_{G}
by first sampling
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\sim\mu_{G}
and then keeping each monochromatic edge of
G
under
\sigma
with probability
p=1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}
(the so-called percolation step).

We use this for the random graph
\boldsymbol{G}
to get a handle on the random cluster model on
\boldsymbol{G}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{\sigma})
has distribution
\pi_{\boldsymbol{G}}
. From Lemma
2.3
, we can figure out in particular how many monochromatic edges we should expect from each phase, so we can translate the phases for the Potts model to the random cluster model. More precisely, set

\mathfrak{m}_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)=p\frac{d}{2}\sum_{i\in[q]}\rho_{\mathrm{d},ii}\mbox{ and }\mathfrak{m}_{\mathrm{o}}(\beta)=p\frac{d}{2}\sum_{i\in[q]}\rho_{\mathrm{o},ii}.

With a bit of algebra, we can derive the following expressions for
\mathfrak{m}_{\mathrm{d}}
and
\mathfrak{m}_{\mathrm{o}}
from (
2.5
): for
\beta<\beta_{u}^{\prime}
, we have
\mathfrak{m}_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)=\frac{d}{2}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta}-1}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta}+q-1}
, while, for
\beta>\beta_{u}
, we have
\mathfrak{m}_{\mathrm{o}}(\beta)=\frac{d}{2}\frac{(\mathrm{e}^{\beta}-1)(x^{2}+\frac{(1-x)^{2}}{q-1})}{1+(\mathrm{e}^{\beta}-1)(x^{2}+\frac{(1-x)^{2}}{q-1})}
with
x=\frac{t^{d-1}}{t^{d-1}+q-1}
and
t>1
defined from
\mathrm{e}^{\beta}-1=\frac{(t-1)(t^{d-1}+q-1)}{t^{d-1}-t}
, as in (
2.1
).

We are now ready to define the ordered/disordered phases for the random-cluster model. Due to monotonicity properties (and in contrast to the phases of the Potts model), we can just use the values of
\mathfrak{m}_{\mathrm{d}}
and
\mathfrak{m}_{\mathrm{o}}
at the critical temperature
\beta=\beta_{c}
; this will be convenient later on.

Definition 3.1

(The ordered/disordered phases)
.

Let
q,d\geq 3
be integers. For a graph
G\in\mathcal{G}_{n,d}
and
\rho:=\mathfrak{m}_{\mathrm{o}}(\beta_{c})-\mathfrak{m}_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta_{c})>0
, define

\Omega_{G,\mathrm{d}}=\{F\subseteq E(G):|F|\leq n\mathfrak{m}_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta_{c})+n\rho/4\}
and
\Omega_{G,\mathrm{o}}=\{F\subseteq E(G):|F|\geq n\mathfrak{m}_{\mathrm{o}}(\beta_{c})-n\rho/4\}
.

Let also
\pi_{G,\mathrm{d}}=\pi_{G}(\cdot\mid\Omega_{G,\mathrm{d}})
and
\pi_{G,\mathrm{o}}=\pi_{G}(\cdot\mid\Omega_{G,\mathrm{o}})
.

Using the translation between the Potts and random-cluster models, we have the following.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
d,q\geq 3
be integers and
\beta>0
be real. Then, whp over
G\sim\mathcal{G}_{n,d}
, we have
\pi_{G}\big(\Omega_{G,\mathrm{d}}\big)=1-\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega(n)}
when
\beta<\beta_{c}
and
\pi_{G}\big(\Omega_{G,\mathrm{o}}\big)=1-\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega(n)}
when
\beta>\beta_{c}
.

For
\beta=\beta_{c}
,
\pi_{G}\big(\Omega_{G,\mathrm{d}}\cup\Omega_{G,\mathrm{o}}\big)=1-\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega(n)}
and
\pi_{G}\big(\Omega_{G,\mathrm{d}}\big),\pi_{G}\big(\Omega_{G,\mathrm{o}}\big)\geq 1/\ln\ln n
.

Proof of Corollary
3.2
.

This follows from Lemma
2.1
, and the correspondence between
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\sim\mu_{G}
and
\boldsymbol{F}\sim\pi_{G}
stated at the start of the section, using
f(n)=1/\ln\ln n
for the lower bounds at
\beta=\beta_{c}
.
∎

To analyse the ordered/disordered phases of the random cluster model using the planted distributions, we will need the following intermediate distributions. Namely, for a graph

, let

be the distribution on

obtained by first sampling

. The proof of the following is given in Appendix

.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
d,q\geq 3
be integers and
\beta>0
be real. Then, whp over
G\sim\mathcal{G}_{n,d}
, for any event
\mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^{E(G)}
, the following holds.

(1)

if

, then
\pi_{G,\mathrm{d}}(\mathcal{A})\leq\hat{\pi}_{G,\mathrm{d}}(\mathcal{A})+\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega(n)}

(2)

3.2.
Mixing and Weak Spatial Mixing (WSM) within a phase

Treading a path initiated in
[

]
, to prove Theorems

and Theorem

Theorem 3.4
.

Let

be integers and
\beta\in(0,\beta_{c}]
. Then, whp over

.

Let

be integers and

be a real. Then, whp over

, for every
\varepsilon\geq\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega(n)}
the RC dynamics

\big\|X_{T}-\pi_{G,\mathrm{o}}\big\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\varepsilon
for

.

Proof of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
.

We first do the proof of Theorem
1.1
. By Corollary
3.2
, it holds that
\big\|\pi_{G}-\pi_{G,\mathrm{d}}\big\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega(n)}
and by Theorem
3.4
we can obtain a sample from
\pi_{G,\mathrm{d}}
in
T
steps using random-cluster dynamics. Then, using the translation between the Potts and RC models
[
24
]
, this gives a sample

whose distribution is within TV-distance
\leq\varepsilon
from
\mu_{G}
, as wanted. The proof of Theorem
1.2
is analogous using Corollary
3.2
and Theorem
3.5
.
∎

It was shown in
[
20
]
that, for the ferromagnetic Ising model, a sufficient condition for fast convergence to equilibrium within the phase is the property of
weak spatial mixing (WSM) within a phase
, paired with some mixing-time estimates in local neighbourhoods. The same condition applies for the random-cluster model and, more generally, for monotone models. In the case of the random regular graph, the local neighbourhoods resemble the
d
-regular tree and the required mixing-time results have been established in
[
7
]
. So, the main piece missing in order to obtain Theorems
3.4
and
3.5
is showing WSM within the corresponding phase. Roughly, the notion captures that, when we condition on the phase, the influence of a boundary configuration at distance
\ell
is small.

More formally, for a graph
G=(V,E)
, an integer
\ell
and a vertex
v
, let
B_{\ell}(v)
be the graph induced by vertices at distance
\leq\ell
from
v
, whose vertex and edge sets are going to be denoted by
V(B_{\ell}(v))
and
E(B_{\ell}(v))
, respectively. A boundary condition
\tau
on
B_{\ell}(v)
is a subset of the edges in
E\backslash E(B_{\ell}(v))
; then
\pi^{\tau}_{G,v,\ell}
denotes the RC distribution induced on
B_{\ell}(v)
conditioned on
\tau
, i.e.,
for
S\subseteq E(B_{\ell}(v))
it holds that
\pi^{\tau}_{G,v,\ell}(S)=\frac{\pi_{G}(S\cup\tau)}{\sum_{S^{\prime}\subseteq E(B_{\ell}(v))}\pi_{G}(S^{\prime}\cup\tau)}
. There are two extreme boundary conditions on

which will be most relevant: (i) the
all-in
or
wired
condition
+
where all edges in
E\backslash E(B_{\ell}(v))
are included in the boundary condition, and (ii) the
all-out
or
free
boundary condition
-
where none of the edges in
E\backslash E(B_{\ell}(v))
are included. We use
\pi^{+}_{G,v,\ell}
and
\pi^{-}_{G,v,\ell}
to denote the corresponding conditional RC distributions.

For an edge
e\in E
, let
1_{e}
denote the subsets of
E
that include
e
. Following
[
18
]
, we say that we have WSM within the disordered phase (respectively, ordered) at distance
\ell
if for each vertex
v
and each edge
e
incident to
v
it holds that
\big|\pi^{-}_{G,v,\ell}(1_{e})-\pi_{G,\mathrm{d}}(1_{e})\big|\leq\frac{1}{100m}
(respectively,
\big|\pi^{+}_{G,v,\ell}(1_{e})-\pi_{G,\mathrm{o}}(1_{e})\big|\leq\frac{1}{100m}
) with
m=|E|
.

Our main technical results for establishing Theorems
3.4
and
3.5
are the following.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
d,q\geq 3
be integers and
\beta\leq\beta_{c}
be a positive real. Then, there exists
\delta>0
such that, whp over
G\sim\mathcal{G}_{n,d}
,

has WSM within the disordered phase at a distance

. That is, with

, for every

and every edge
e
incident to
v
, it holds that
\big|\pi^{-}_{G,v,\ell}(1_{e})-\pi_{G,\mathrm{d}}(1_{e})\big|\leq\frac{1}{100m}
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
d\geq 3,q\geq(5d)^{5}
be integers and
\beta\geq\beta_{c}
be a real. Then, there exists
\delta>0
such that, whp over
G\sim\mathcal{G}_{n,d}
,

has WSM within the ordered phase at a distance

. That is, with

, for every

and every edge

incident to

Using these, the proofs of Theorems

5.

Lemma D.2

We will also use the following lemma.

Lemma D.3
.

Consider a supercritical Galton-Watson process and let

be the size of the surviving population at generation

\mathbb{P}(Z_{r}\geq K\mid Z_{r}>0)=1-o_{r}(1)
.

Proof.

Let

and

be the probability of no offspring in one step; since the process is supercritical we have
\phi,q_{0}<1
. Suppose for the sake of contradiction that there is

\mathbb{P}(\mbox{extinction}\mid Z_{r}>0)\geq\delta

\delta=\varepsilon q_{0}^{K}>0

Hence, for every

\mathbb{P}(\mbox{extinction})\geq\delta(1-\mathbb{P}(Z_{r}=0))+\mathbb{P}(Z_{r}=0).

As

∎

Corollary D.4
.

Let
0<d_{0}\leq d
be integers, and
p\in(\tfrac{1}{d-1})
be a real. Then consider a Galton-Watson process where the root has offspring distribution

be the size of the surviving population at generation

\mathbb{P}(Z_{r}\geq K\mid Z_{r}>0)=1-o_{r}(1)
.

Proof.

Note that once at second or later generation, the process is supercritical, so then we can apply Lemma
D.3
. Thus we need to consider the case of the root.

Note that if

, then there is a non-empty set

of children of the root whose branching process survive

\mathbb{P}(Z_{r}<K\mid Z_{r}>0,\mathcal{S}\text{ survive})\leq\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{C}}}\mathbb{P}(Z_{r-1}^{i}<K\mid Z_{r-1}^{i}>0)=o_{r}(1)

Summing over the possible (non-empty) sets of surviving children, we obtain that the bound holds unconditionally.
∎

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{o}},\boldsymbol{F}^{+}\text{ disagree on }e)\leq

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{o}}\text{ does not have a wired boundary})+\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathcal{O}})

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}(e\not\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{o}},e\in\boldsymbol{F}^{+},\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{o}}\text{ has a wired boundary}\mid\mathcal{O})

Let
\mathcal{S}_{B}

is at most

\sum_{\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{S}_{B}}\mathbb{P}(e\not\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{o}},e\in\boldsymbol{F}^{+}\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{o}},\mathcal{S}},\mathcal{O})\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{E}}_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{o}},\mathcal{S}}\mid\mathcal{O})

Conditional on

\boldsymbol{F}^{+}

Lemma
5.7
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph, let
v\in V
be an arbitrary vertex, and let
\ell\geq 1
an integer. For any
F\subseteq E
,
let

F_{v}
be the set of edges in
F
that are incident to
v
and let

G_{v,F}:=(V\setminus\{v\},F\setminus F_{v})
.
Suppose that all simple length-
\ell
paths from
v
in
G
intersect the largest component of
G_{v,F}
.
Then
F
has a wired boundary around
v
in
B_{\ell}(v)
.

Proof of Lemma
5.7
.

Consider the set of paths
\mathcal{P}
of length
\ell
in
G
that start in
v
.

By assumption, each of these paths have a vertex in the largest component of
G_{v,F}
, denoted
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
. For each path in
\mathcal{P}
, consider the
first
vertex on the path belonging to
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
(we think of the path as starting in
v
). Let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of these vertices. We next show that
\mathcal{S}
is a wired boundary around
v
in
B_{\ell}(v)
.

We can further assume
\mathcal{S}
is not empty: if
\mathcal{S}
were empty, then
\mathcal{P}=\emptyset
, which only happens if
v
is not connected to
G\setminus B_{\ell}(v)
, and thus an empty set is a trivial wired boundary.

Claim 1.

\mathcal{S}\subseteq V(B_{\ell}(v))\setminus\{v\}
is a cut-set of
G
separating
v
from
V\setminus B_{\ell}(v)

Proof of Claim 1.

The paths in
\mathcal{P}
are contained in the ball
B_{\ell}(v)
. Also,
v
is not in the graph
G_{v,F}
, hence
v\not\in\mathcal{S}
.
Consider any path from
v
to
V\setminus V(B_{\ell}(v))
in
G
,
v=w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{r},\dots,w_{\ell^{\prime}}\not\in B_{\ell}(v)
. Clearly,
w_{0},\dots,w_{\ell}
is a path in
\mathcal{P}
and thus there is
1\leq i\leq\ell
such that
w_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
. Therefore any path from
v
to the outside of the ball is disconnected in
G\setminus\mathcal{S}
, proving Claim 1.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{v}
be the component of
v
in
G\setminus\mathcal{S}
. To conclude that
\mathcal{S}
is a wired boundary, we need to show vertices of
\mathcal{S}
belong to a single component in
(V\setminus V({\mathcal{C}}_{v}),\{e\in F:e\cap V({\mathcal{C}}_{v})=\emptyset\})
. In particular, we show that this component is
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
.

Claim 2.
No vertices of
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
belong to
{\mathcal{C}}_{v}
.

Proof of Claim 2.

Suppose for contradiction
u\in V({\mathcal{C}}_{v})\cap V({\mathcal{C}}_{1})
. By Claim 1,
{\mathcal{C}}_{v}
is a subgraph of
B_{\ell}(v)
, and thus there exists a path
v=w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{\ell^{\prime}}=u
of length
\ell^{\prime}\leq\ell
from
v
to
u
in
{\mathcal{C}}_{v}
. Since we assumed
\mathcal{S}
is not empty and
\mathcal{S}\subseteq V({\mathcal{C}}_{1})
, wlog we can extend this path to be length
\ell
, containing such
u
, say to
w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{\ell^{\prime}}=u,w_{\ell^{\prime}+1},\dots,w_{\ell}
. But this is a path in
\mathcal{P}
. By the choice of
\mathcal{S}
and
u
, there is
1\leq i\leq\ell^{\prime}
such that
w_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
. But then
w_{i}\not\in{\mathcal{C}}_{v}
, which contradicts that
w_{0},\dots,w_{\ell^{\prime}}
is in
{\mathcal{C}}_{v}
.
Thus no such
u
can exist and Claim 2 follows.

Claim 2 implies that
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
is connected in
(V\setminus V({\mathcal{C}}_{v}),\{e\in F:e\cap V({\mathcal{C}}_{v})=\emptyset\})
, thus in particular all vertices of
\mathcal{S}
belong to it in this graph as well, so these vertices are all in the same component.

Thus it follows that
\mathcal{S}
is a wired boundary around
v
in
B_{\ell}(v)
.
∎

Theorem
3.4
.

Let
d,q\geq 3
be integers and
\beta\in(0,\beta_{c}]
. Then, whp over
G\sim\mathcal{G}_{n,d}
, for every
\varepsilon\geq\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega(n)}
the RC dynamics
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
initialised from all-out satisfies
\big\|X_{T}-\pi_{G,\mathrm{d}}\big\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\varepsilon
for
T=O(n\log n\log\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon})
.

Proof.

First, we have that, whp
G\sim\mathcal{G}_{n,d}
, satisfies:

(1)

Corollary
B.2
so that, for
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{d}}\sim\pi_{G,\mathrm{d}}
,

\mathbb{P}(|\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{d}}|\geq nm_{d}(\beta_{c})+\delta n)=\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega(n)}
,

(2)

Theorem
3.6
, i.e., there exists
\delta>0
so that
G
has WSM within the disordered phase at a distance
\ell\leq(\tfrac{1}{2}-\delta)\log_{d-1}n
.

Also, for
\ell
as above, there exists a constant
L>0
such that for every vertex
v
,
B_{\ell}(v)
is
L
-treelike, see, e.g.,
[
9
, Fact 2.3.]
. Consider any graph
G
that satisfies these properties.

We next outline the theorem for
\varepsilon=\tfrac{1}{4}
, the full details for this part can be found in
[
18
]
. Then, we prove that probability amplification applies for any
\varepsilon=\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega(n)}
. Consider two copies of the random-cluster dynamics: (1)
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
starting from all-out, and (2)
(\hat{X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
starting from
\pi_{G,\mathrm{d}}
which is restricted to the disordered phase by ignoring updates that would make the configuration not disordered. Note that the stationary distribution of
\hat{X}_{t}
is
\pi_{G,\mathrm{d}}
, and thus for all
t\geq 0
,
\hat{X}_{t}\sim\pi_{G,\mathrm{d}}
.

We couple the two chains by choosing the same edge in each step, and same
U[0,1]
random variable to decide whether to keep the edge in the next configuration. Note that if
\hat{X}_{t}
has not ignored any updates by the time
t
, by monotonicity
\hat{X}_{t}\supseteq X_{t}
. Since
\hat{X}_{t}\sim\pi_{G,\mathrm{d}}
, we can bound
\left\|{X_{T}-\pi_{G,\mathrm{d}}}\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\mathbb{P}(X_{T}\neq\hat{X}_{T})
, which will next show is at most
\tfrac{1}{4}
for
T=Cn\log n
for some constant
C>0
.
Let
{\mathcal{E}}_{<t}
be the event that no transitions have been ignored by time
t
. Since we assumed
G
is such that Corollary
B.2
applies, the probability that
\hat{X}_{t}
is in a state from which it is possible to leave the disordered phase is
\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega(n)}
, thus by the union bound
\mathbb{P}(\overline{{\mathcal{E}}_{<T}})=T\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega(n)}
.

Next, we upper bound
\mathbb{P}(X_{T}\neq\hat{X}_{T})
by summing over disagreements on individual edges. We have in particular that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{T}\neq\hat{X}_{T})\leq

\displaystyle m\mathbb{P}(\overline{{\mathcal{E}}_{<T}})+\sum_{e\in E(G)}\mathbb{P}(\mathds{1}\{e\in X_{T}\}\neq\mathds{1}\{e\in\hat{X}_{T}\}\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{<T}).

(F.1)

Fix an arbitrary edge
e
and let
v
be a vertex incident to
e
. We will bound
\mathbb{P}(\mathds{1}\{e\in X_{T}\}\neq\mathds{1}\{e\in\hat{X}_{T}\}\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{<T})
by considering another random-cluster dynamics
(X_{t}^{v})_{t\geq 0}
, which starts from all-out and for which where we restrict all updates to be inside the ball
B_{\ell}(v)
. Formally,
X_{t}^{v}
is coupled with
X_{t}
and
\hat{X}_{t}
using the same coupling as above, ignoring updates on edges outside the ball
B_{\ell}(v)
. By monotonicity, conditional on
{\mathcal{E}}_{<T}
,
X^{v}_{T}\subseteq X_{T}\subseteq\hat{X}_{T}
, thus also
\mathbb{P}(\mathds{1}\{e\in X_{T}\}\neq\mathds{1}\{e\in\hat{X}_{T}\}\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{<T})\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathds{1}\{e\in X^{v}_{T}\}\neq\mathds{1}\{e\in\hat{X}_{T}\}\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{<T})\leq\mathbb{P}(e\in X^{v}_{T}\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{<T})-\mathbb{P}(e\in\hat{X}_{T}\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{<T})
, where the last inequality follows by monotonicity. Using the triangle inequality on the last bound, and the inequality
|\mathbb{P}(A)-\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)|\leq 2\mathbb{P}(\overline{B})
to remove the conditioning, we obtain that

\mathbb{P}(\mathds{1}\{e\in X_{T}\}\neq\mathds{1}\{e\in\hat{X}_{T}\}\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{<T})\leq 4\mathbb{P}(\overline{{\mathcal{E}}_{<T}})+|\mathbb{P}(e\in X_{T}^{v})-\pi_{G,v,\ell}^{-}(1_{e})|+|\pi_{G,v,\ell}^{-}(1_{e})-\mathbb{P}(e\in\hat{X}_{T})|,

where
\pi^{-}_{G,v,\ell}
is the
distribution on
B_{\ell}(v)
conditioned on an all-out boundary.
Since
\mathbb{P}(e\in\hat{X}_{T})=\pi_{G,\mathrm{d}}(1_{e})
, WSM within the disordered phase bounds the third term by
\frac{1}{100m}
.
The second term is also bounded by
\frac{1}{100m}
using mixing time estimates, in this case log-Sobolev inequalities. We don’t give the full details here (which, as mentioned before, can also be found in
[
18
,
20
,
9
]
) but the idea is that, since the neighbourhood of
v
is treelike (up to removing a constant number of edges), the log-Sobolev constant for
\pi_{G,v,\ell}^{-}(1_{e})
is a constant factor away from that on the regular tree. This in turn is just percolation so the log-Sobolev constant for the chain
X_{v}^{t}
is at most
C_{\mathrm{d}}/|V(B_{\ell}(v))|
(in the ordered regime, the bound for the log-Sobolev constant on the tree with + boundary condition is given in
[
7
]
). Since
\pi_{G,v,\ell}^{-}
is the stationary distribution of the chain

, the term
|\mathbb{P}(e\in X_{T}^{v})-\pi_{G,v,\ell}^{-}(1_{e})|

where

is the number of updates inside the ball
B_{\ell}(v)
by time
t
, which ultimately gives the claimed bound
\frac{1}{100m}
.

Combining the above and going back to (

), we obtain

\mathbb{P}(X_{T}\neq\hat{X}_{T})\leq 5Cmn\log n\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega(n)}+\frac{m}{50m}\leq 1/4

for all
n
large enough. This concludes the proof for
\varepsilon=1/4
.

In the remainder of the proof, we denote the
T
established above for
\varepsilon=1/4
by
T_{1/4}
.
To get the claimed TV-distance bound for all
\varepsilon\geq\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega(n)}
, we modify the standard probability amplification argument using monotonicity. Formally, we prove the following inequality for all integer
k\geq 0
, whenever
T^{\prime}\geq kT_{1/4}
(the second inequality follows from induction and the definition of
T_{1/4}
).

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{kT_{1/4}}\neq\hat{X}_{kT_{1/4}}\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{<T^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}(X_{(k-1)T_{1/4}}\neq\hat{X}_{(k-1)T_{1/4}}\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{<T^{\prime}})\mathbb{P}(X_{T_{1/4}}\neq\hat{X}_{T_{1/4}})}{\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{E}}_{<T^{\prime}})}

(F.2)

\displaystyle\leq[4\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{E}}_{<T^{\prime}})]^{-k}

(F.3)

We introduce another copy of coupled Glauber dynamics,
(X^{\prime}_{t})_{t\geq T}
starting from all-out at the time
T_{(k-1)T_{1/4}}

t

X^{\prime}_{t}
is coupled with the

X_{t}
and
\hat{X}_{t}
(using the standard monotone coupling). Now, we use two facts: first
\hat{X}_{(k-1)T_{1/4}}\sim\pi_{G,\mathrm{d}}

\hat{X}_{(k-1)T_{1/4}+t}\neq X^{\prime}_{t}
is the same as the probability of
X_{t}\neq\hat{X}_{t}
. Second, that conditional on

(for any

.

Thus, conditionally on
{\mathcal{E}}_{<T^{\prime}}
, it must be the case that both
X_{t}
,
\hat{X}_{t}
has not coupled by time
(k-1)T_{1/4}
(if they have, by the conditioning they would not disagree before time

), and also that
\hat{X}_{(k-1)T_{1/4}+t}

has not coupled by time

, hence l.h.s. of (

\mathbb{P}(X^{\prime}_{T_{1/4}}\neq\hat{X}_{kT_{1/4}}\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{<T^{\prime}})\mathbb{P}(X_{(k-1)T_{1/4}}\neq\hat{X}_{(k-1)T_{1/4}}\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{<T^{\prime}}),

We conclude (

\mathbb{P}(X^{\prime}_{T_{1/4}}\neq\hat{X}_{kT_{1/4}}\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{<T^{\prime}})\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}(X^{\prime}_{T_{1/4}}\neq\hat{X}_{kT_{1/4}})}{\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{E}}_{<T^{\prime}})}=\frac{\mathbb{P}(X_{T_{1/4}}\neq\hat{X}_{T_{1/4}})}{\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{E}}_{<T^{\prime}})}

To finish, recall that
