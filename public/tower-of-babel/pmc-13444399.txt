Ac‐SDKP modulates apoptosis via HSP27 and the FAS/FASL and mitochondrial axes
Authors: Wenxin Guo, Jinyin Yan, Wenli Li, Jin Wang, Lu Liu, Chengmei Zhang, Wei Cao, Shanshan Yao, Haibo Gong, Yi He, Zihan Zhou, Siyan Chen, Li Feng, Haijing Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSilicosis is a progressive, irreversible fibrotic lung disease caused by long‐term exposure to crystalline silica. Although the anti‐fibrotic tetrapeptide Ac‐SDKP has shown promise in reducing fibrosis, the specific molecular mechanisms through which it modulates apoptosis in silica‐induced lung injury remain unclear, particularly the role of heat shock protein 27 (HSP27).MethodsWistar rats were divided into groups including control, model (2‐week, 3‐week, and 4‐week), and Ac‐SDKP prevention/treatment groups. In vitro, MEF and A549 cells were treated with TGF‐β1 and Ac‐SDKP to observe the effects on apoptosis‐related proteins. Additionally, HSP27 interference vectors were constructed to study its role in regulating apoptosis via FAS/FASL and mitochondrial pathways in both in vivo and in vitro models.ResultsIn vivo, Ac‐SDKP administration alleviated silica‐induced pulmonary fibrosis and reduced apoptosis in lung tissue. In contrast, in vitro, TGF‐β1 stimulation suppressed apoptosis in A549 and MEF cells, whereas Ac‐SDKP restored apoptotic activity by regulating the HSP27‐mediated FAS/FASL and mitochondrial pathways. Moreover, AAV9‐mediated knockdown of HSP27 in mice enhanced apoptosis and attenuated fibrosis, confirming the anti‐apoptotic role of HSP27 in silicosis.ConclusionThese findings reveal that Ac‐SDKP exerts context‐dependent modulation of apoptosis through HSP27—protecting acutely injured alveolar epithelial cells in vivo while reinstating apoptotic signaling in EMT‐adapted A549 and MEF cells in vitro. Thus, targeting HSP27 may offer a promising therapeutic strategy to restore apoptotic–fibrotic equilibrium in silicosis and related pulmonary fibrotic disorders.Keywords: apoptosis, FAS/FASL pathway, HSP27, mitochondria, silicosis

INTRODUCTION

Silicosis is currently an occupational disease that causes suffering in China and has a high incidence rate. 1 , 2 It is primarily the result of prolonged exposure to silica dust, which accumulates in the lungs, leading to extensive fibrosis of lung tissue. The resulting pathological manifestations include infiltration of inflammatory cells, deposition of extracellular matrix, damage to the alveolar walls, and formation of silica nodules. In severe cases, it may also lead to impaired lung function. Pulmonary fibrosis is the most common manifestation of silicosis, characterized by a complex progression involving inflammation, immune response, tissue damage, and collagen deposition. 3 , 4 Even after silica exposure is terminated, silicosis continues to progress, leading to lung degeneration and respiratory failure, as fibrosis remains irreversible and untreatable.

N‐Acetyl‐serine‐aspartyl‐lysine‐proline (Ac‐SDKP) is a naturally occurring polypeptide found in various tissues. It is recognized for its ability to regulate the immune system, promote angiogenesis, and exhibit anti‐fibrotic properties. 5 , 6 Ac‐SDKP has been shown to mitigate organ damage by inhibiting inflammation and fibrosis, while also promoting angiogenesis. It has shown promise in the treatment of pulmonary, hepatic, renal, and cardiac fibrosis. Ac‐SDKP has demonstrated antifibrotic properties in silicosis models by inhibiting epithelial‐mesenchymal transition and myofibroblast transformation, as well as by reducing lung inflammation. It plays an antifibrotic role in silicosis models, where the induction of apoptosis is associated with tissue fibrosis. 7 , 8 , 9 Previous studies have demonstrated that Ac‐SDKP attenuates SiO₂‐induced apoptosis of type II alveolar epithelial cells by suppressing endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, 10 In addition, Ac‐SDKP has been shown to reduce collagen deposition by upregulating α‐tubulin acetyltransferase 1 (α‐TAT1), thereby reversing DNA double‐strand break damage. 11 Moreover, recent evidence indicates that Ac‐SDKP alleviates silicosis‐associated pulmonary fibrosis through the heat shock protein 27 (HSP27)‐mediated p38/MAPK–mTOR autophagy signaling pathway. 12 However, the role of HSP27 in the regulation of apoptosis induced by Ac‐SDKP in silicosis remains poorly understood.

Heat shock protein 27 (HSP27) is a member of the small heat shock protein family. It has a low molecular weight and functions as an ATP‐independent molecular chaperone, widely distributed across various organisms. Its primary function is regulated by the phosphorylation and dephosphorylation of three critical N‐terminal serine residues at positions 15, 78, and 82. 13 , 14 Under normal physiological conditions, HSP27 functions as a molecular chaperone, regulating protein synthesis, folding, stretching, translocation, localization, oligomerization, and depolymerization. Additionally, it assists in the removal of misfolded proteins, promotes protein denaturation, enhances cellular resistance to external stressors, and acts as a cellular protector. However, under pathological conditions, it may exacerbate disease progression. 15 , 16 Studies have shown that HSP27 plays a significant role in fibrosis and tumor progression by influencing the proliferation and differentiation of lung fibroblasts and tumor cells. 17 , 18 Inhibiting HSP27 presents a promising therapeutic strategy for treating pulmonary fibrosis and tumor‐related diseases.

Apoptosis occurs through two main pathways: the extrinsic and intrinsic pathways. 19 The FAS/FASL signaling pathway is a classical extrinsic apoptotic pathway. The interaction between Fas cell surface death receptor (FAS) and Fas ligand (FASL) generates apoptotic signals, promoting the expression of downstream apoptotic factors and leading to apoptotic cell death. 20 Studies have shown that the absence of FAS signaling in fibroblasts leads to persistent pulmonary fibrosis. Promoting FASL expression in lung fibroblasts reverses their resistance to apoptosis. 21 FAS ligand‐induced cell death is typically associated with epithelial injury in pulmonary fibrosis. Paradoxically, certain fibrotic lung diseases, such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), exhibit apoptosis resistance in fibroblasts, 22 while alveolar epithelial cells display excessive apoptosis, thereby contributing to disease progression. 23 Therefore, elucidating how Ac‐SDKP modulates the FAS/FASL pathway to exert its therapeutic effects is of significant importance. The mitochondrial apoptotic pathway is an intrinsic process and is considered the primary mechanism of cellular apoptosis. 24 This pathway is characterized by the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, disruption of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, and the release of apoptotic proteins. 25 , 26 Research has demonstrated that HSP27 protects against homocysteine‐induced endothelial cell apoptosis by regulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and the mitochondrial caspase‐dependent apoptotic pathway. 27 Studies have also shown that HSP27 can sequester cytochrome c (Cyt C), thereby preventing apoptosome formation and the subsequent activation of caspase‐9. 28 Thus, elucidating how HSP27 regulates apoptosis through the mitochondrial pathway represents another key focus of our investigation. To date, no reports have been published on whether Ac‐SDKP modulates apoptosis via HSP27 and the FAS/FASL and mitochondrial axes.

Based on the above background, this study proposes the following central hypothesis: Ac‐SDKP produces differential apoptotic regulatory effects across distinct pathological contexts through downregulation of HSP27 expression. In fibroblasts and epithelial cells in an apoptosis‐resistant state, it relieves HSP27‐mediated apoptosis resistance and restores cellular sensitivity to death signals. In complex tissue microenvironments, it may attenuate inflammation‐driven tissue damage through integrated cell‐autonomous and immunomodulatory mechanisms, ultimately exerting anti‐fibrotic effects. Although prior studies have confirmed the anti‐fibrotic activity of Ac‐SDKP in silicosis, 29 these studies focused on different molecular mechanisms and did not elucidate how Ac‐SDKP simultaneously regulates the apoptotic balance of epithelial cells and fibroblasts. The innovative aspects of this study include: (1) revealing for the first time that HSP27 is the key mediator through which Ac‐SDKP regulates apoptosis, (2) establishing a novel mechanistic framework for the “HSP27‐dual apoptotic pathway”, and (3) elucidating the context‐dependent effects of the Ac‐SDKP‐HSP27 axis – protecting acutely injured cells in vivo through anti‐inflammatory action, and exerting pro‐apoptotic effects in vitro by relieving apoptosis resistance in EMT‐adapted cells, thereby providing experimental evidence for targeting HSP27 in the treatment of pulmonary fibrosis.

METHODS

Preparation and administration of Ac‐SDKP

Ac‐SDKP (N‐acetyl‐seryl‐aspartyl‐lysyl‐proline, molecular weight 487.5 Da) is a chemically synthesized tetrapeptide. The powder form was purchased from Bachem AG, Switzerland, with a purity of ≥98%.

In vivo experiments

Ac‐SDKP powder was dissolved in physiological saline containing 0.1% glacial acetic acid to prepare a stock solution at 5 mg/mL, which was aliquoted and stored at −20°C. Prior to use, the stock solution was diluted to the working concentration with sterile physiological saline. The in vivo administration dose was 800 μg/kg/d, delivered via subcutaneously implanted mini‐osmotic pumps for a continuous period of 4 weeks.

In vitro experiments

For the preparation of Ac‐SDKP stock solution, 5 mg of Ac‐SDKP powder was dissolved in 1 mL glacial acetic acid to give a stock solution concentration of approximately 10−2 mol/L. Two sequential 100‐fold dilutions were performed (each time, 10 μL was added to 990 μL glacial acetic acid), yielding a final concentration of 10−6 mol/L. The solution was aliquoted and stored at −20°C. Prior to use, the solution was thawed at 4°C and diluted to the working concentration of 10−8 mol/L in serum‐free culture medium.

Experimental animal model of silicosis

Experimental animals were purchased from Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Healthy male Wistar rats of SPF grade, weighing 180 ± 20 g, were used. Prior to grouping, all animals were stratified by body weight and randomly allocated by non‐experimental personnel who were unaware of the subsequent treatment protocols. Animals were numbered using picric acid marking; experimental operators were aware only of animal numbers and had no knowledge of group allocation. This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of North China University of Science and Technology [SQ2022036]. The experiments were conducted in accordance with the 8th edition of the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals [SYXK (JI) 2020‐007]. The rats were randomly assigned to eight experimental groups using a random number table: (1) C2: intrabronchial instillation of 1.0 mL saline (n = 10); (2) Si2: intrabronchial instillation of 50 mg/mL SiO2 suspension (1.0 mL per rat) (n = 10); (3) C3: intrabronchial instillation of 1.0 mL saline (n = 10); (4) Si3: intrabronchial instillation of 50 mg/mL SiO2 suspension (1.0 mL per rat) (n = 10); (5) C4: intrabronchial instillation of 1.0 mL saline (n = 10); (6) Si4: intrabronchial instillation of 50 mg/mL SiO2 suspension (1.0 mL per rat) (n = 10); (7) Ac‐ pre: pre‐treatment with 50 mg/mL SiO2 suspension via intrabronchial instillation (1.0 mL per rat), followed by implantation of an osmotic mini‐pump containing Ac‐SDKP (800 μg/(kg·day)) for 4 weeks (n = 10); (8) Ac‐post: pre‐treatment with 50 mg/mL SiO₂ suspension via intrabronchial instillation (1.0 mL per rat), followed by 2 weeks of observation, and then implantation of an osmotic mini‐pump containing Ac‐SDKP (800 μg/kg day) for an additional 2 weeks (n = 10).

To suppress the expression of HSP27, the shHspb2 gene was delivered to the lungs using AAV9‐Hspb2‐RNAi(136011‐1) vectors. The empty vector AAV9, without the target knockdown, was used as a control. Mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 5 per group): Control + AAV9‐NC, Control + HSP27 knockdown (AAV9‐Hspb2‐RNAi), SiO2 + AAV9‐NC, SiO2 + AAV9‐shHspb2. The viral concentration was 1 × 1011 vg/mL per dose. After the feeding period, mice were euthanized in a CO2 chamber, and lung tissues were collected under sterile conditions for further analysis. This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of North China University of Science and Technology [SQ2022036].

Cell culture and treatments

The human type II alveolar epithelial A549 cell line and Mouse Embryonic Fibroblasts MEF cell line were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences cell library (TCHu150, Shanghai, China). Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. MEF cells were cultured in Ham's F‐12K (SH3002401B, GIBCO, USA) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (10099141, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, NY, USA) and 1% antibiotics (penicillin and streptomycin). A549 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (C11995500BT, GIBCO, USA) containing 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics. To induce myofibroblast differentiation, MEF and A549 cells were serum‐starved for 24 h and then treated with 5 ng/mL of TGF‐β1 for 6, 12, 24, and 48 h. Four experimental groups were cultured for the respective time points. The experimental groups included: (1) C:a control group, (2) T: a TGF‐β1 (5 ng/mL) induction group, (3) Ac: an Ac‐SDKP intervention group (pre‐incubation with Ac‐SDKP at 10−8 mol/L for 2 h followed by co‐incubation with TGF‐β1 at 5 ng/mL), (4) J2: a J2 intervention group (pre‐incubation with J2, a known HSP27 inhibitor, at 2 μmol/L for 2 h followed by co‐incubation with TGF‐β1 at 5 ng/mL). This design aimed to determine whether Ac‐SDKP exerts effects similar to those of HSP27 inhibition.

Lentivirus transfection

A549 cells were cultured in 60 mm dishes at a density of 4 × 105 cells per dish and incubated for 24 h. Once cells reached 20%–30% confluence, the old medium was removed and replaced with complete medium containing Hitrans GP. Lentiviral infection (LV‐HSPB2‐RNAi 110681‐2) was performed with a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 50, and virus was added to the culture. After 10 h, the culture medium was replaced with complete medium. Forty‐eight hours after lentiviral infection, when cells reached 70%–80% confluence, they were placed in medium containing 1 mg/mL of puromycin. After 72 h, the percentage of cells expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) was observed through fluorescence microscopy. Stable expression cell lines were acquired after three generations of culture in medium containing 1 mg/mL puromycin.

MEF cells were seeded in 60‐mm culture dishes at a density of 4 × 105 cells per dish and incubated for 24 h to achieve approximately 30% confluence overnight. Based on preliminary experiments, an appropriate transduction enhancer (P) and a viral dose of 5 × 107 were selected (LV‐Hspb1 87343‐2). Cells were cultured in medium at 37°C for 48 h. After replacing the medium, puromycin at the optimal concentration determined from preliminary screening (10 μg/mL) was added to eliminate non‐transduced cells. Seventy‐two hours later, the percentage of green fluorescent protein (GFP)‐positive cells was assessed under a fluorescence microscope, and successful viral transduction was further confirmed by Western blot analysis. Stable cell lines were established after three consecutive passages in puromycin‐containing medium.

Histopathology by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

As previously described, 30 the cell pad was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently stained with hematoxylin–eosin. The lung tissue was also fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by paraffin embedding. Hematoxylin–eosin staining was used to observe the cell morphology in a 4 μm thick section.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence staining

Immunocytochemical and immunohistochemical analyses of MEF cells, A549 cells, and lung tissue sections were performed according to a previously described protocol. 31 The cells or tissues were incubated in citrate buffer for antigen retrieval, followed by quenching endogenous peroxidase activity with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Lung tissue slivers, MEF cells, and A549 cells were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: anti‐HSP27 (1:100), anti‐FAS (1:150), anti‐FASL (1:100), anti‐Cyt C (1:200), anti‐APAF‐1 (1:200), anti‐BCL‐2 (1:200), anti‐BAX (1:450), anti‐Caspase‐9 (1:200), anti‐Caspase‐8 (1:450), and anti‐Caspase‐3 (1:500). The following day, the samples were incubated with a secondary antibody for 20 min at room temperature. Immune response detection was performed using DAB (Beijing Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd). Brown‐stained cells were considered positive. Counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin, and slides were mounted with neutral balsam.

Western blot

Total proteins from lung tissue and cells were collected by lysis in RIPA buffer containing a protease inhibitor. After a 30‐min ice bath, the extracts were centrifuged at 12 000× g and 4°C for 30 min, and the supernatants were collected. Proteins (20 μg/lane) were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate 10% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and electrotransferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was sealed with 5% skimmed milk powder and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: anti‐β‐actin (Bosher Bioengineering Co., Ltd., China), anti‐β‐tubulin (HUABIO, Hua'an Biology), anti‐GAPDH (Affinity, China), anti‐HSP27 (Bausch Tech Bioengineering Co., China), anti‐FAS (Affinity, China), anti‐FASL (Wanleibio, China), anti‐Caspase‐8 (Wanleibio, China), anti‐Caspase‐3 (Wanleibio, China), and anti‐HSP27 (HUABIO, Hua'an Biology). Next, a rabbit/mouse immunoglobulin affinity‐purified peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibody was incubated, followed by washing, chromogenic reaction, and detection using a gel imaging analysis system (Bio‐Rad Co., Ltd). The image was scanned with ImageJ software.

Apoptosis analysis

Apoptosis rates of MEF and A549 cells were determined using Annexin V‐FITC/PI flow cytometry. As described earlier, each group of cells was digested with 0.25% trypsin (500 μL), followed by the addition of 1.5 mL DMEM high glucose medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum to stop digestion. After centrifugation (1000 rpm for 5 min), the cells were washed twice with cold PBS buffer, then suspended in 100 μL binding buffer. Cells were stained with 5 μL Annexin V‐FITC and 10 μL propidium iodide. After 20 min of incubation in the dark, 300 μL PBS buffer was added, and apoptosis was detected by flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, USA). 32

Co‐immunoprecipitation (co‐IP)

Lung tissue was minced and homogenized on ice in lysis buffer containing 1 mmol/L PMSF and 1% NP‐40 (Beyotime, China), followed by centrifugation at 14 000× g for 30 min at 4°C. A total of 1.0–3.0 mg protein lysate was incubated with 3 μg rabbit anti‐HSP27 antibody (HUABIO, China) or normal rabbit IgG (Beyotime, China) overnight at 4°C with rotation. Protein A/G magnetic beads (Beyotime, China) were then added and incubated overnight at 4°C. The beads were washed five times with lysis buffer (the last two washes were performed with high‐salt buffer containing 300–500 mmol/L NaCl if necessary), and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 1× SDS loading buffer. Input samples were prepared with 50–100 μg total protein. After SDS‐PAGE and transfer to PVDF membranes, the membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 2 h and incubated overnight at 4°C with mouse primary antibodies against HSP27, Cyt C, and FAS (HUABIO, China). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL). The IP antibody and Western blot primary antibodies were derived from different species (rabbit vs. mouse) to avoid heavy/light chain interference.

Statistical analyses

To ensure the stability and reliability of the results, all experiments were independently repeated three times under identical conditions. The obtained data were analyzed and graphed using SPSS and GraphPad Prism software. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Differences among multiple groups were analyzed by one‐way ANOVA, and pairwise comparisons between groups were performed using the least significant difference (LSD‐t) test. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Ac‐SDKP effectively inhibits the progression of silicosis fibrosis

To investigate whether Ac‐SDKP effectively suppresses the progression of silicosis‐induced pulmonary fibrosis, we examined the macroscopic morphology of the fibrotic lungs, focusing on the deposition of silicotic nodules. The control group exhibited a uniform lung tissue surface, free of nodules. In contrast, the silicosis model group showed a gradual accumulation of nodules on the lung tissue surface, which increased in size over time. Granular nodules were observed on the lung tissue surface in both the prevention and treatment groups, although they were smaller than those in the silicosis model group over the same duration (Figure 1A).

HE staining was employed to assess the progression of silicosis. The findings showed that, in the control group, the alveolar architecture remained intact and easily discernible, with no evidence of alveolar wall dilation or hyperemia, absence of inflammatory exudates in the alveoli, and no proliferative fibers or tissues in the pulmonary interstitium. In contrast, the silicosis model group showed progressive destruction of alveolar integrity over time, infiltration of inflammatory cells, and accumulation of silicon nodules. The Ac‐SDKP prevention and treatment groups displayed reduced levels of inflammatory cell infiltration, aggregation of silicon nodules, and smaller nodules (Figure 1B).

Western blot analysis showed that the expression of α‐SMA protein gradually increased over time following prolonged exposure to dust instillation. The levels of α‐SMA protein in both the Ac‐SDKP prophylaxis and treatment groups were significantly lower than those in the 4‐week model group (Figure 1C).

Ac‐SDKP suppresses the expression of HSP27, FAS, and FASL in silicosis tissues

To examine the differential expression of HSPB1 in patients with pulmonary fibrosis, we screened the GEO database and obtained the GSE24206 and GSE24988 datasets. The dataset GSE24206 comprises whole‐lung samples from 17 patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) who underwent diagnostic surgical biopsy or lung transplantation, along with six control samples obtained from healthy donor lungs during volume reduction procedures in lung transplantation. This sampling strategy, encompassing specimens from different lobes of the lung, enables the analysis of spatial gene expression variations across the lungs of IPF patients, while the inclusion of healthy controls provides a robust basis for comparative analysis. Gene expression was measured using microarray technology, and top‐ranked genes were fitted into a Bayesian probabilistic regression model to identify gene signatures and construct a predictive molecular feature model with high accuracy. The GSE24988 dataset was also generated using microarray technology. RNA was extracted from freshly frozen lung tissue samples obtained from 116 lung transplant recipients diagnosed with pulmonary fibrosis (PF) and hybridized to Affymetrix microarrays. The resulting gene expression profiles were used to identify and validate molecular clusters based on gene signature patterns. Both datasets focused on clinically confirmed pulmonary fibrosis cases, ensuring their clinical relevance. As microarray‐based platforms, they allow for simultaneous quantification of thousands of genes, offering an integrated view of the molecular alterations underlying IPF—a disease characterized by complex, multi‐gene, and multi‐pathway dysregulation. Moreover, these datasets are complementary, covering idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis as well as major fibrotic complications. In this study, we specifically examined the expression of the HSPB1 gene and compared its expression differences between the two datasets. The datasets were normalized using the RAW algorithm to eliminate dimensional discrepancies and ensure cross‐sample comparability. Data were then processed and visualized using R software, and gene expression levels were presented as boxplots. The results showed strong consistency between GSE24206 and GSE24988, supporting their suitability for differential gene expression analysis. Dimensionality reduction via principal component analysis (PCA) projected the high‐dimensional gene expression data into a two‐dimensional space. The PCA plots and statistical analyses revealed a significant difference in HSPB1 expression between IPF and normal control groups (Figure 2A–D).

Immunohistochemical staining was used to investigate the effects of Ac‐SDKP on the expression of caspase‐3 and caspase‐8 in the silicosis model. Ac‐SDKP inhibits the morphological transformation of fibroblasts and epithelial cells induced by TGF‐β1, as well as the expression of HSP27

To investigate the morphological effects of Ac‐SDKP on MEF and A549 cells following TGF‐β1 stimulation, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed. In the context of pulmonary fibrosis, the cytokine transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF‐β1) plays a key role in promoting fibroblast differentiation into myofibroblasts and accelerating the progression of pulmonary fibrosis. To investigate the potential inhibitory effects of Ac‐SDKP on fibroblast proliferation and differentiation induced by TGF‐β1, we analyzed morphological alterations using HE staining. After TGF‐β1 induction for 24 and 48 h, both MEF and A549 cells exhibited a morphological shift toward a spindle shape. This morphological change intensified with prolonged exposure to the inducing agent. The introduction of Ac‐SDKP effectively suppressed the spindle shape transformation observed (Figure 3A,B).

Ac‐SDKP modulates HSP27 and FAS expression across in vivo and in vitro models of pulmonary fibrosis

To evaluate the interaction between HSP27 and the target proteins FAS, we retrieved the corresponding protein files from the PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/) (FAS: PDB ID = 1DDF; HSP27: PDB ID = 4MJH). FAS were used as the receptors, and HSP27 as the ligand. We prepared the receptor and ligand protein files, performed protein docking using the GRAMM online server, and obtained the best docking conformation. 33 The protein complex was visualized using PyMOL. 34 Simultaneously, the protein complex surface was uploaded to PDBePISA for visualization (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/msd‐srv/prot_int/). 35 Molecular docking analysis predicted that HSP27 may interact with FAS through hydrogen bonding and other non‐covalent interactions (Figure 4A).

Immunofluorescence staining was employed to investigate the effect of Ac‐SDKP on the colocalization of HSP27 and FAS in TGF‐β1‐induced MEF and A549 cells at 48 h, thereby elucidating their interaction. Under unstimulated conditions, HSP27 protein exhibited faint red fluorescence in the cytoplasm of MEF and A549 cells, while FAS protein displayed minimal green fluorescence. Following TGF‐β1 induction, the red fluorescence intensity of HSP27 protein increased compared with the control group, whereas the green fluorescence intensity of FAS protein decreased. The predominance of red over green fluorescence indicated that TGF‐β1‐induced HSP27 may exert anti‐apoptotic effects by reducing FAS protein expression in MEF cells at 48 h. In the Ac‐SDKP and J2 intervention groups, HSP27 red fluorescence was diminished and FAS green fluorescence was enhanced, with green fluorescence becoming more prominent than red. These results suggest that Ac‐SDKP may inhibit HSP27 protein expression, thereby dismantling HSP27‐mediated apoptosis resistance and restoring the expression of the pro‐apoptotic protein FAS (Figure 4B,C). A notable discrepancy emerged with the in vivo experiments at this juncture: immunofluorescence histochemistry revealed that FAS (green) and HSP27 (red) fluorescence were markedly elevated in the silicotic fibrosis group compared with the control group, and this enhancement was selectively reversed by Ac‐SDKP preventive and therapeutic treatment (Figure 4D).

Ac‐SDKP enhances activation of the FAS/FASL apoptotic pathway by downregulating HSP27 expression in MEF cells

To investigate the effect of Ac‐SDKP on apoptosis in MEF and A549 cells, the following experiments were performed. The effect of Ac‐SDKP on the FAS/FASL‐mediated apoptotic pathway was examined by immunocytochemistry (Figure 5A,B) and Western blot analysis. Compared with the control group, HSP27 was upregulated in MEF cells after TGF‐β1 induction for 6–48 h as detected by Western blot, and this upregulation was reversed by Ac‐SDKP and J2 intervention. DISCUSSION

Silicosis, the classic pneumoconiosis, results from prolonged inhalation of respirable crystalline silica or silicate‐laden dusts (e.g., talc) and leads to nodular pulmonary fibrosis. 38 , 39 Here, we replicated this pathology in rats; silica‐instilled lungs showed time‐dependent expansion of fibrotic nodules, confirming model accuracy. Central to this response is heat‐shock protein 27 (HSP27), a stress‐inducible molecular chaperone that regulates cytoskeletal dynamics, redox homeostasis, and apoptosis evasion. 17 , 40 , 41 We observed robust upregulation of HSP27 in both silica‐exposed rat lungs and TGF‐β1‐stimulated MEF and A549 cells, consistent with previous reports that identify HSP27 as a key mediator—and potential therapeutic target—in pulmonary fibrosis. 42 Concurrently, α‐SMA expression increased in vivo (silicosis model) and in vitro (TGF‐β1 group), highlighting HSP27‐driven myofibroblast differentiation and epithelial–mesenchymal transition during fibrogenesis.

Ac‐SDKP is a tetrapeptide with anti‐inflammatory properties. Evidence suggests that Ac‐SDKP has the potential to protect against various models of pulmonary, renal, and cardiovascular injuries, displaying anti‐inflammatory properties, reducing macrophage and neutrophil infiltration, and combating fibrosis. 6 , 43 Previous studies have successively revealed that Ac‐SDKP alleviates SiO₂‐induced apoptosis of type II alveolar epithelial cells by suppressing endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, 10 and attenuates collagen deposition by upregulating α‐tubulin acetyltransferase 1 (α‐TAT1) to reverse DNA double‐strand break damage. 11 However, whether Ac‐SDKP possesses additional key targets that regulate cell fate remains largely unknown. In this study, we identified for the first time that Ac‐SDKP combines with heat shock protein 27 (HSP27), concomitantly activating both the mitochondria‐mediated intrinsic apoptotic pathway and the FAS/FASL death receptor pathway. This dual activation induces apoptosis and blocks phenotypic transformation in alveolar epithelial cells and fibroblasts. Distinct from previous studies focusing on ER stress or DNA damage, our work systematically elucidates a novel “HSP27–dual apoptosis axis.” This mechanism was comprehensively validated through multiple approaches, including shRNA‐mediated knockdown and overexpression assays, protein–ligand docking analysis, and in vivo AAV9‐based intervention. Collectively, these findings establish a new molecular target and provide a mechanistic rationale for the therapeutic application of Ac‐SDKP in silicosis. Consistent with these mechanistic insights, both Ac‐SDKP and the HSP27 inhibitor J2 reduced HSP27 expression, suppressed α‐SMA induction in MEF and A549 cells exposed to TGF‐β1, and decreased silicotic nodule formation in vivo. Ac‐SDKP administration further attenuated myofibroblast differentiation, collagen deposition, and fibrosis progression, highlighting its therapeutic potential in silicosis. Together, these results identify HSP27 as a key molecular hub linking stress signaling and fibrotic remodeling, and establish Ac‐SDKP as a promising candidate for targeting epithelial–mesenchymal imbalance in fibrotic lung disease.

The induction of apoptosis through the binding of the FASL homotrimer to three FAS molecules results in the aggregation of the receptor death domain. The FAS/FASL‐mediated apoptotic process is tightly regulated, maintaining internal homeostasis and facilitating cellular turnover. 44 Apoptosis demonstrates intrinsic autonomy, reflecting resilience to persistent stimuli. For example, in our study, TGF‐β1 was administered in vitro as a defined apoptotic trigger. In parallel, SiO2 suspension was introduced in vivo, causing prolonged structural damage to lung tissue and perpetuating programmed cell death. However, with sustained injury, apoptotic tolerance increases, leading to a slower rate of cell death. Thus, apoptosis levels appear to be consistently maintained at a threshold. Research has demonstrated that FAS/FASL signaling contributes to the apoptosis of myofibroblasts and macrophages, 45 , 46 and pulmonary fibrosis is thought to be closely linked to dysregulated apoptosis. Studies show that FAS expression increases in renal and liver fibrosis but decreases after pharmacological intervention, thereby alleviating apoptosis. 47 , 48 , 49 We observed similar trends in the FAS/FASL signaling pathway in both the Ac‐SDKP prevention and treatment groups. In our study, Ac‐SDKP's effects on the FAS/FASL pathway were evaluated by analyzing FAS, FASL, Caspase‐3, and Caspase‐8. In vivo, FAS, FASL, Caspase‐3, and Caspase‐8 levels were elevated in the silicosis model group but reduced after Ac‐SDKP treatment. Conversely, previous research indicates that HSP27 upregulation inhibits apoptosis. 50 These findings led us to investigate the role of HSP27 in apoptosis. In vitro, HSP27 protein expression gradually increased in MEF and A549 cells following TGF‐β1 induction for 6–48 h. Studies have shown that TGF‐β1 can induce apoptosis. 51 In the early stage of the TGF‐β1 stimulation experiment (6 h), MEF cells underwent acute injury and stress, thereby activating the FAS/FASL apoptotic pathway and inducing apoptosis. After a 12‐h transitional phase, the cells entered an adaptive stage during the late experimental phase (24–48 h), wherein upregulated HSP27 established a defense mechanism and acquired apoptosis resistance. 52 Upon EMT occurrence, Ac‐SDKP exerted its effect by downregulating HSP27, dismantling apoptosis resistance, and manifesting as pro‐apoptotic. Similarly, A549 cells underwent acute injury and stress at the early stage of TGF‐β1 stimulation (6–12 h), thereby activating the FAS/FASL apoptotic pathway; Ac‐SDKP inhibited the excessive activation of FAS and FASL by downregulating HSP27, thus manifesting as anti‐apoptotic. Only in the late phase (24–48 h) did cells similarly enter an adaptive stage, wherein upregulated HSP27 established a defense mechanism and acquired apoptosis resistance. This temporal dynamic suggests that the anti‐apoptotic effect observed in vivo may correspond to the early protective stage, rather than directly contradicting the pro‐apoptotic effect observed in vitro at the late phase. Lentivirus‐mediated HSP27 silencing confirmed that Ac‐SDKP regulates apoptosis by downregulating HSP27, activating the FAS/FASL and Caspase‐3 signaling pathways, analogous to the effect of the HSP27 inhibitor J2.

Intrinsic apoptosis is initiated by mitochondrial dysfunction triggered by oncogene activation, DNA damage, hypoxia, or growth factor deprivation. 53 , 54 During this process, cytochrome c is released into the cytoplasm, where it combines with APAF‐1 to form the apoptotic complex with the assistance of ATP and dATP. This complex recruits and activates pro‐Caspase‐9, leading to the formation of holoenzyme Caspase‐9, which subsequently activates Caspase‐3 and Caspase‐7, thereby initiating the caspase cascade. 55 , 56 The BCL‐2 protein family plays a critical role in regulating mitochondrial outer membrane permeability and cytochrome c release. Anti‐apoptotic members of the BCL‐2 family inhibit mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and cytochrome c release, thereby preventing apoptosis, whereas the pro‐apoptotic protein BAX promotes permeabilization and cytochrome c release, thereby inducing apoptosis. 57 Compared with healthy lungs, AEC2s in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) exhibit increased apoptosis during the early stages of fibrosis. In established fibrosis, alveolar epithelial cells from IPF lungs display reduced BCL‐2 expression and increased BAX expression. 58 In vivo, the silicosis model group exhibited decreased BAX expression and increased BCL‐2 expression, while BAX expression was further reduced in the Ac‐SDKP prevention and treatment groups compared with the silicosis model group. In vitro, α‐SMA was markedly upregulated in MEF and A549 cells following TGF‐β1 intervention for 6–48 h compared with the control group. Similarly, in the early stage of TGF‐β1 stimulation (6 h), MEF cells underwent mitochondria‐mediated apoptosis induced by stress; after a 12‐h transitional phase, at the late stage (24–48 h), cells established a defense mechanism, acquired apoptosis resistance, and underwent EMT, wherein Ac‐SDKP exerted its effect by downregulating HSP27 and dismantling apoptosis resistance. In A549 cells in the early stage of TGF‐β1 stimulation (6–12 h), the observed apoptosis could be inhibited by Ac‐SDKP through downregulating HSP27 and suppressing the excessive activation of pro‐apoptotic proteins APAF‐1 and BAX, thus manifesting as anti‐apoptotic; only at the late stage (24–48 h) did cells similarly enter an adaptive stage, wherein upregulated HSP27 established a defense mechanism and acquired apoptosis resistance. The discrepancy between the SiO₂‐induced in vivo silicosis model and the TGF‐β1‐stimulated in vitro cell model may be attributed to differences in the nature of stimulation and temporal scales between the two models. In the in vitro TGF‐β1 stimulation model, cells undergo an adaptive response of “early apoptosis → transitional phase → late apoptosis resistance + EMT”. In contrast, in the in vivo silicosis model, a single intratracheal instillation of SiO₂ particles, which are difficult to clear and persist in the lungs, causes repeated alveolar epithelial injury and a sustained inflammatory response. Previous studies have demonstrated that after a single intratracheal instillation of 50 mg SiO₂ in rats, day 7 represents a pronounced inflammatory phase characterized by significant neutrophil and macrophage infiltration and elevated pro‐inflammatory cytokines including IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α 59 ; at day 14, granulomatous lesions appeared in lung tissue, accompanied by increased TUNEL‐positive cells and significant upregulation of apoptotic markers including Caspase‐3 60 ; Not until day 28 did the model enter the typical fibrotic phase, with formation of silicotic nodules and collagen deposition. 61 Therefore, the significant upregulation of pro‐apoptotic proteins including FAS, FASL, APAF‐1, BAX, and Caspase‐3/8/9 observed at 2–4 weeks in this study likely reflects persistent alveolar epithelial injury and inflammatory stress induced by sustained SiO2 retention, rather than the adaptive apoptosis‐resistance transition process in response to single‐dose TGF‐β1 stimulation observed in the in vitro model. This is consistent with reported studies: Ac‐SDKP attenuates injury by inhibiting the TLR4/MyD88/NF‐κB inflammatory pathway, 62 which aligns with the protective effect of Ac‐SDKP observed in vitro in the early stage (6–12 h). Second, the inconsistency between in vivo and in vitro results may also be attributed to the inability of a homogeneous cell culture to replicate the spatiotemporal complexity of intact tissue. Variables such as drug concentration, exposure duration, inflammatory milieu, immune surveillance, metabolic flux, and tissue‐specific regulation cannot be faithfully reproduced ex vivo. Within this broader limitation, the FAS/FASL axis provides a representative example. In vivo, activated T and NK cells cleave membrane‐bound FASL into soluble FASL (sFASL) during inflammatory stress, generating a diffusible ligand pool with substantially higher apoptotic potency than its membrane‐bound counterpart. 63 , 64 The resulting surge of sFASL in fibrotic lungs amplifies alveolar epithelial apoptosis, whereas monocultures, lacking activated lymphocytes, fail to generate soluble FASL and thus shift the balance toward the less efficient juxtacrine isoform. Moreover, the ultimate impact of sFASL—whether direct epithelial cytotoxicity or T‐cell deletion that facilitates immune evasion—is determined by paracrine networks, extracellular matrix interactions, metabolic gradients, and integrated inflammatory, immunoregulatory, and tissue‐specific signaling circuits, all of which are absent in in vitro platforms. 65

To further investigate this, we employed lentivirus‐mediated knockdown to silence HSP27 expression in A549 cells. The results showed that FAS, FASL, BAX, APAF‐1, Caspase‐3, Caspase‐9, and Caspase‐8 were upregulated in both the Ac‐SDKP intervention group and the HSP27‐silenced group, whereas BCL‐2 expression was reduced. However, in the present study, no further reduction in HSP27 expression was observed when Ac‐SDKP treatment was combined with lentiviral‐mediated HSP27 knockdown compared with Ac‐SDKP treatment alone. We speculate that this phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that cells cannot survive when HSP27 expression falls below a critical threshold. Previous studies have demonstrated that HSP27 plays an essential role in maintaining cytoskeletal stability, preserving mitochondrial integrity, and preventing the activation of apoptotic pathways; excessive depletion of HSP27 disrupts these processes, ultimately leading to cell death. 66 Similarly, it has been reported that silencing HSP27 alone is sufficient to induce lethality in multiple oncogene‐addicted cancer cell lines, underscoring its indispensable function in cellular survival. 67 These findings suggest that cells may maintain a minimal basal level of HSP27 expression through self‐protective regulatory mechanisms to sustain essential survival functions. Subsequently, gene amplification was used to increase HSP27 expression in MEF cells. Similarly, pro‐apoptotic protein expression increased in the Ac‐SDKP intervention group compared with the TGF‐β1 group, whereas anti‐apoptotic protein expression decreased. After HSP27 overexpression, the expression of FAS, FASL, BAX, APAF‐1, Caspase‐3, Caspase‐9, and Caspase‐8 decreased in the viral intervention group compared with the untreated group, while BCL‐2 expression increased. To further verify the regulatory role of HSP27 in the pathogenesis of silicosis and in the FAS/FASL and mitochondrial apoptotic pathways in vivo, mice and control mice were infected with AAV9 carrying Hspb2 within 48 h of establishing the silicosis model to reduce HSP27 expression. We found that reducing HSP27 expression in silicosis model mice alleviated fibrotic pathology, increased the expression of pro‐apoptotic proteins (FAS, FASL, BAX, Caspase‐3, and APAF‐1), and decreased BCL‐2 expression. This indicates that HSP27 exerts an anti‐apoptotic role in mice, and that reducing its expression weakens this effect, thereby activating the FAS/FASL and mitochondrial apoptotic pathways and enhancing cell apoptosis. Therefore, these findings suggest that Ac‐SDKP may act as a potential inhibitor of HSP27, and that its pro‐apoptotic effect is at least partly mediated by activation of the FAS/FASL axis and the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway via suppression of HSP27. To further validate these findings, protein interaction prediction was performed. The results revealed that HSP27 interacts with FAS and Cyt C through specific hydrogen bonds, providing a structural basis for the potential direct binding between HSP27 and these target proteins. To experimentally verify these interactions, co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) assays were conducted, confirming the interactions between HSP27 and FAS as well as Cyt C in primary tissue lysates. This is consistent with established conclusions: Cyt C has been demonstrated to directly associate with HSP27 through Co‐IP experiments and targeted metabolomics 68 ; a weak positive interaction was observed between FAS and HSP27, suggesting that their association may be indirect and mediated by other factors. This is consistent with existing research reports: silencing Hsp27 can promote FAS‐induced apoptosis by regulating the PEA‐15 molecular switch. 50 Subsequently, JC‐1 monomer staining of MEF and A549 cells and flow cytometry‐based apoptosis analysis demonstrated that at 12 h, the low apoptotic activity may indicate that in the early stage of TGF‐β1 stimulation cells underwent stress and acute injury, thereby inducing the FAS/FASL apoptotic pathway and the mitochondria‐mediated apoptotic pathway; whereas at 48 h, persistent TGF‐β1 induction drove cells into an adaptive stage, wherein upregulated HSP27 established a defense mechanism and acquired apoptosis resistance. These findings align with previous evidence that elevated HSP27 suppresses apoptosis and promotes tumorigenesis. 69 In contrast, Ac‐SDKP inhibits HSP27 expression and dismantles HSP27‐mediated apoptosis resistance, highlighting its therapeutic significance.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that Ac‐SDKP exerts anti‐fibrotic effects in silica‐induced rats and regulates HSP27‐mediated apoptosis both in vivo and in vitro. Targeting HSP27 may represent a promising therapeutic strategy to halt pulmonary fibrosis progression. Additionally, Ac‐SDKP dismantles apoptosis resistance by downregulating HSP27, thereby preventing EMT and limiting fibrogenesis. These findings provide new molecular insights into the anti‐fibrotic actions of Ac‐SDKP and support its potential as a candidate for the development of lung‐protective therapeutics or preventive interventions against pulmonary fibrosis.

CONCLUSIONS

These findings indicate that Ac‐SDKP exerts stage‐dependent regulation of apoptosis through HSP27—protecting acutely injured alveolar epithelial cells in vivo while reactivating apoptotic signaling in EMT‐adapted A549 and MEF cells in vitro. Therefore, targeting HSP27 may offer a promising therapeutic strategy to restore the balance between apoptosis and fibrosis in silicosis and related pulmonary fibrotic diseases.
