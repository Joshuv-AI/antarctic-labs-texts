Imaginary modules arising from tensor products of snake modules Thanks: The work of M.B. and A.M. was partially supported CNPq grant 405793/2023-5. The work of A.M. was also partially supported by Fapesp grant 2018/23690-6.
Matheus Brito, Adriano Moura

Abstract
. Motivated by the limitations of cluster algebra techniques in detecting imaginary modules, we build on the representation-theoretic framework developed by the first author and Chari to extend the construction of such modules beyond previously known cases, which arise from the tensor product of a higher-order Kirillov–Reshetikhin module and its dual. Our first main result gives an explicit description of the socle of tensor products of two snake modules, assuming the corresponding snakes form a covering pair of ladders. By considering a higher-order generalization of the covering relation, we describe a sequence of inclusions of highest- ℓ \ell -weight submodules of such tensor products. We conjecture all the quotients of subsequent modules in this chain of inclusions are simple and imaginary, except for the socle itself, which might be real. We prove the first such quotient is indeed simple and, assuming an extra mild condition, we also prove it is imaginary, thus giving rise to new classes of imaginary modules within the category of finite-dimensional representations of quantum loop algebras in type A A .



1.
Introduction

This paper is dedicated to the study of certain aspects of the tensor product of two prime snake modules for a quantum loop algebra
U_{q}(\tilde{\mathfrak{g}})
in the case that the finite-dimensional simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is of type
A
. We assume
q
is neither zero nor a root of unity. Since the discovery by Hernandez-Leclerc
[
12
,
13
]
that the Grothendieck rings of certain proper subcategories of the abelian category of finite-dimensional
U_{q}(\tilde{\mathfrak{g}})
-modules are cluster algebras, the task o classifying the prime and the real simple objects of the category became one of the most studied topics in the area. Since a module is real if its tensor square is simple, the task of classifying the real modules is equivalent to that of classifying the simple modules which are not real. Such modules were termed imaginary in
[
19
]
, where the first example of an imaginary simple module was given. The results of
[
1
,
16
,
24
,
26
]
imply that, in principle, the real modules can be determined using cluster algebra machinery. In practice, however, determining if a given Drinfeld polynomial arises from a cluster monomial is not a simple task, which motivates the search for alternative techniques. For instance, this is the spirit of
[
3
]
. Furthermore, the combinatorial framework of cluster algebras does not provide representation theoretic insights for the existence of imaginary modules. Seeking for such insights is the spirit of the present paper.

The class of snake modules introduced in
[
23
]
has been proven to be particularly important in many aspects. In particular, their
q
-characters can be computed by a relatively simple combinatorial data, which we refer to as Mukhin-Young (MY) paths, and the connection with cluster monomials can be made explicitly, thus showing snake modules are real. The class of Kirillov-Reshetikhin (KR) modules and, more generally, that of minimal affinizations, are proper subclasses of that of (prime) snake modules.
In
[
2
]
, the authors considered the subclass of prime snake modules whose highest weight is a multiple of a fundamental weight, thus terming such modules “higher order KR modules”. Suppose
V
is such a module. By studying
V\otimes V^{*}
, a new family of imaginary modules was described in
[
2
]
, assuming
N={\rm rank}(\mathfrak{g})>2
. The pioneering example of imaginary modules from
[
19
]
is recovered by this construction. The main results of the present paper substantially extends this construction of imaginary modules by studying much more general tensor products of two prime snake modules. The assumption
N>2
is implied by the assumptions of the construction. We remark that
[
2
]
also gives a different construction specific to the case
N=2
(and it is well known that all simple modules are real if
N=1
).

Before continuing, let us recall that the isomorphism classes of simple modules of the category of (type 1) finite-dimensional
U_{q}(\tilde{\mathfrak{g}})
-modules are classified in terms of Drinfeld polynomials. We denote by
\mathcal{P}^{+}
the set of all Drinfeld polynomials, which is a multiplicative monoid in a natural way. For
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}^{+}
, we let
V(\boldsymbol{\pi})
be any element in the corresponding isomorphism class. The trivial Drinfeld polynomial will be denoted by
\boldsymbol{1}
.

As a “preliminary” step towards our construction of imaginary modules, the first of our main results,
Theorem

2.5.1
, describes explicitly the socle of the tensor products of two prime snake modules, assuming the corresponding snakes form what we termed a covering pair of ladders. Let us explain what this means. Without loss of generality, we restrain ourselves to the subcategory consisting of modules which have simple factors whose Drinfeld polynomials have roots in
q^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Let
\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
be a parameterizing set for the isomorphism classes of the corresponding fundamental modules and let
\mathbb{S}=\tilde{\mathbb{S}}\cup\{\emptyset\}
. In the main part of the text, we chose to work with
\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
being the set of certain “segments”. For
s\in\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
, let
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s}
denote the corresponding Drinfeld polynomial, while
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\emptyset}=\boldsymbol{1}
. Given
s,s^{\prime}\in\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
, we say
s
covers
s^{\prime}
, and write
s\rhd s^{\prime}
, if

(1)

V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s^{\prime}})\ \ \text{is reducible and highest-$\ell$-weight.}

Recall that a module
V
is highest-
\ell
-weight if its set of weights contains a unique maximal element, the corresponding weight space is one-dimensional, and
V
is cyclically generated by this weight space. In the language of segments, (
1
) can be phrased in a completely intrinsic manner, with no mention to representation theory (see
Section

2.4
). It is well-known that, if (
1
) is satisfied, then
V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s^{\prime}})
has length
2
and its socle is a simple module of the form

V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s\cap s^{\prime}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s\cup s^{\prime}})\cong V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s\diamond s^{\prime}})\ \ \text{where}\ \ \boldsymbol{\omega}_{s\diamond s^{\prime}}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s\cap s^{\prime}}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s\cup s^{\prime}}

and
s\cap s^{\prime},s\cup s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}
. In the language of segments,
s\cap s^{\prime}
is indeed the intersection of the corresponding segments and similarly for
s\cup s^{\prime}
, thus the choice of notation. A prime snake of length
l
corresponds to a sequence
\boldsymbol{s}=s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{l}
of elements in
\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
such that

s_{k+1}\rhd s_{k}\ \ \text{for all}\ \ 1\leq k<l.

Such sequences are also called ladders. In that case,
V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{l}})\otimes\cdots\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}})
is highest-
\ell
-weight and the associated prime snake module is the simple quotient of this tensor product. Setting

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}}=\prod_{k=1}^{l}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{k}},

it follows that this simple quotient is isomorphic to
V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})
. If
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
are ladders of length
l
, we say
\boldsymbol{s}
covers
\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
and write
\boldsymbol{s}\rhd\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
if

s_{k}\rhd s^{\prime}_{k}\ \ \text{for all}\ \ 1\leq k\leq l.

Theorem

2.5.1
says that

\boldsymbol{s}\rhd\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\quad\Rightarrow\quad{\rm soc}(V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}))\cong V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}\diamond\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}})\quad\text{where}\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}\diamond\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}=\prod_{k=1}^{l}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{k}\diamond s^{\prime}_{k}}.

A closely related result was obtained in
[
17
, Corolary 6.16]
in the framework of complex smooth representations of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
with
\mathbb{F}
being an nonarchimedian field. Using a Schur-Weyl type duality
[
5
]
(we refer the reader to
[
9
]
for a deeper discussion), this result gives rise to an algorithm for computing the socle of a tensor product of a snake module with a fundamental module. In the setting of representations of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
, Lapid and Mínguez
[
18
]
proved a necessary and sufficient condition for an irreducible module
V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})
to be imaginary, provided
\boldsymbol{s}
is any sequence (not necessarily a ladder) satisfying a certain regularity condition. The results of
[
2
]
and, more generally,
Theorem

2.6.3
, describe families of imaginary modules which do not satisfy this regularity condition.

For the next step of our construction, we introduce “higher order” versions of the covering relation
\boldsymbol{s}\rhd\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
. Given a sequence
\boldsymbol{s}=s_{1},\dots,s_{l}
of elements of
\mathbb{S}
and
0\leq m<n<l
, set

\boldsymbol{s}(m,n)=s_{m+1},\dots,s_{n}.

We say
\boldsymbol{s}
is a
p
-cover of
\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
and write
\boldsymbol{s}\rhd_{p}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
if

\boldsymbol{s}(0,l-k)\rhd\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}(k,l)\quad\text{for all}\quad 0\leq k\leq p.

In particular, we can consider

\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}(0,l-k)\diamond\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}(k,l)}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \boldsymbol{\pi}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}(0,k)}\,\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{k}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}(l-k,l)}

for all
0\leq k\leq p
. Our main conjecture is:

\boldsymbol{s}\rhd_{p}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\quad\Rightarrow\quad V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k})\ \ \text{is imaginary for all}\ \ 1\leq k\leq p.

Our main result,
Theorem

2.6.3
, confirms this holds for
k=1
with a mild extra assumption. We do not believe this assumption is necessary. For instance, if either
l=2
or both
V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})
and
V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}})
are KR modules, we prove the conjecture for
k=1
even in the cases that the extra condition fails (and such failing cases exist). We also remark this assumption is trivially satisfied in the context of
[
2
]
, so
Theorem

2.6.3
is a proper generalization of the corresponding result from
[
2
]
. Regarding
V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{0})\cong{\rm soc}(V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}))
, we obtained examples for which it is real as well as for which it is imaginary. These examples, alongside a more systematic study of the reality of
V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{0})
, will appear in a future publication. It is also interesting to mention that, in
Example

2.6.5
, we show that, if
\boldsymbol{s}\rhd_{1}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
so that
V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1})
is defined, then
V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1})
can be obtained as the socle of a tensor product of snake modules in the context of
Theorem

2.5.1
. We also leave a similar discussion for
V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k}),k>1
, for a future publication.

We also prove an intermediate result towards the proof of the conjecture for all
k
which is relevant on its own right (
Proposition

2.6.1
). Namely, we prove there exists a chain of submodules

{\rm soc}(V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}))=M_{0}\subseteq M_{1}\subseteq\cdots M_{p}\subseteq V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}})

where
M_{k}
is a highest-
\ell
-weight module whose Drinfeld polynomial is
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k}
. In fact, we prove these are the only highest-
\ell
-weight submodules whose highest
\ell
-weights are smaller than
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{p}
with respect to the partial order on
\mathcal{P}^{+}
induced by the simple
\ell
-roots. We conjecture

M_{k}/M_{k-1}\cong V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k})\ \ \text{for all}\ \ 1\leq k\leq p

and prove this is true for
k=1
in complete generality. In
[
10
]
, Gurevich gives an algorithm for computing the simple factors of a tensor product of any two ladder representations of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
where
\mathbb{F}
is a nonarchimedean field. In principle, the result does not explicitly describe a composition series, only the simple factors.
Via the aforementioned Schur–Weyl type duality, this can be used to compute the simple factors of the tensor product of any two snake modules for
U_{q}(\tilde{\mathfrak{g}})
if
N\gg 0
. On the other hand,
Proposition

2.6.1
, together with the above two conjectures, captures part of the simple factors in a stronger manner: it gives part of a composition series for such tensor products. Moreover, our proof works for any
N>2
.

.

, while

is proved in

. The latter is rather lengthy, so the section has several subsections. In

, we show the

-weight spaces corresponding to

are one-dimensional both in

The logical structure of the proof of

Preliminaries and Main Statements

Throughout the paper, let
\mathbb{C},\mathbb{Q}
, and
\mathbb{Z}
denote the sets of complex, rational, and integer numbers, respectively. Let also
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq m},\mathbb{Z}_{<m}
, etc., denote the obvious subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
. Given
a,b\in\mathbb{Q}
, we will use the notation
b\leq a
to mean

a-b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},

and similarly for
b<a
.

The symbol
\cong
means “isomorphic to”. We shall use the symbol
\diamond
to mark the end of remarks, examples, and statements of results whose proofs are postponed. The symbol ∎ will mark the end of proofs as well as of statements whose proofs are omitted.

2.1.
Cartan Data and Basic Notation

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a simple Lie algebra of type
A_{N}
over a characteristic zero algebraically closed field
\mathbb{F}
and let
I
be the set of nodes of its Dynkin diagram. We let
x_{i}^{\pm},h_{i},i\in I
, denote generators as in Serre’s Theorem and let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{n}^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{+}
be the corresponding triangular decomposition.
We shall identify
I
with the integer interval
[1,N]
as usual. To shorten notation, we will often use the notation
h=N+1
for the Coxeter number of
\mathfrak{g}
.
For
i\in I
, let
i^{*}=w_{0}(i)
, where
w_{0}
is the Dynkin diagram automorphism induced by the longest element of the Weyl group. More precisely,
i^{*}=h-i
.

We let
P
denote the weight lattice of
\mathfrak{g}
and
P^{+}
be the subset of dominant weights. The root system, set of positive roots, root lattice and the corresponding positive cone will be denoted, respectively, by
R,R^{+},Q,Q^{+}
, while the fundamental weights and simple roots will be denoted by
\omega_{i},\alpha_{i},i\in I
. For convenience, we set
\omega_{i}=0
for
i\in h\mathbb{Z}
. The notation
V(\lambda),\lambda\in P^{+}
, denotes a simple
\mathfrak{g}
-module of highest weight
\lambda
.

Consider the quantum affine (actually loop) algebra
U_{q}(\tilde{\mathfrak{g}})
over
\mathbb{F}
, where
q\in\mathbb{F}^{\times}
is not a root of unity, where
\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}=\mathfrak{g}\otimes\mathbb{F}[t,t^{-1}]
. We use the presentation in terms of generators and relations and the Hopf algebra structure as in
[
20
]
. In particular, the generators are denoted by
x_{i,r}^{\pm},k_{i}^{\pm 1},h_{i,s},i\in I,r,s\in\mathbb{Z},s\neq 0
.

2.2.
Finite-Dimensional Modules

For
i\in I
,
a\in\mathbb{Z}
, we let
\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{i,a}
denote the corresponding fundamental
\ell
-weight, which is the Drinfeld polynomial whose unique non constant entry is the
i
-th which is equal to
1-q^{a}u\in\mathbb{F}[u]
. For convenience, given
i\in h\mathbb{Z}
, we let
\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{i,a}
denote the tuple
\boldsymbol{1}
whose entries are all constant.
We let
\mathcal{P}^{+}
denote the monoid multiplicatively generated by such elements and let
\mathcal{P}
be the corresponding abelian group. We also let
\mathcal{C}
be the full subcategory of that of fintie-dimensional
U_{q}(\tilde{\mathfrak{g}})
-modules whose simple factors have highest
\ell
-weights in
\mathcal{P}^{+}
and, hence,
\ell
-weights in
\mathcal{P}
. It was proven in
[
12
]
that
\mathcal{C}
is a rigid tensor category.
For
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}^{+}
,
V(\boldsymbol{\pi})
will denote a simple
U_{q}(\tilde{\mathfrak{g}})
-module whose highest
\ell
-weight is
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, while
W(\boldsymbol{\pi})
will stand for the corresponding (local) Weyl module. In particular, if
V
is a finite-dimensional highest-
\ell
-weight
U_{q}(\tilde{\mathfrak{g}})
-module with highest
\ell
-weight
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, we have an epimorphism

W(\boldsymbol{\pi})\to V.

For an object
V\in\mathcal{C}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{P}
,
V_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
will denote the associated
\ell
-weight space and we set

\operatorname{wt}_{\ell}(V)=\{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{P}:V_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\neq 0\}\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{wt}_{\ell}^{\pm}(V)=\operatorname{wt}_{\ell}(V)\cap(\mathcal{P}^{+})^{\pm 1}.

It was proved in
[
8
]
that, if
V^{\prime}
is another object of
\cal C
, then

(2.2.1)

\operatorname{wt}_{\ell}(V\otimes V^{\prime})=\operatorname{wt}_{\ell}(V)\operatorname{wt}_{\ell}(V^{\prime}).

We also let
V^{*}
and
{}^{*}V
be the dual modules such that the evaluation maps

V^{*}\otimes V\to\mathbb{F}\quad\text{and}\quad V\otimes{}^{*}V\to\mathbb{F}

are module homomorphisms (cf.
Section

3.1
), where
\mathbb{F}
is regarded as the trivial representation
V(\boldsymbol{1})
.

2.3.
Multisegments and
\ell
-weights

It will be convenient to use a different index set for the simple objects of
\cal C
. Given
i,j\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
with
i\leq j
, let

[i,j]=\{k\in\mathbb{Q}:i\leq k\leq j\}.

Equivalently,
k\in[i,j]
if, and only if,
j-k,k-i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. We shall say
[i,j]
is an
N
-segment (or simply a segment for short) if
j-i\leq h
. If
0<j-i<h
, we will say it is a proper segment. We denote by
\mathbb{S}
and
\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
the sets of segments and proper segments, respectively. Letting
\mathcal{F}=\{\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{i,a}:i\in I,a\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, there exists a bijective map

(2.3.1)

\mathcal{F}\to\tilde{\mathbb{S}},\quad\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{i,a}\mapsto[(a-i)/2,(a+i)/2]

whose inverse is given by

(2.3.2)

\tilde{\mathbb{S}}\to\mathcal{F},\quad[i,j]\mapsto\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{j-i,i+j}.

Set

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,j}=\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{j-i,i+j}\quad\text{for}\quad[i,j]\in\mathbb{S},

and note
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,i}=\boldsymbol{{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}}}_{i,i+h}=\mathbf{1}
. We shall also use the notation

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,j}\quad\text{if}\quad s=[i,j]\in\mathbb{S}.

It follows that
\mathcal{P}
is the free abelian group generated by
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,j}
with
[i,j]\in\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
. Hence, the isomorphism classes of simple objects in
\mathcal{C}
are in bijection with the free abelian group generate by
\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
.

Set

\mathbb{M}=\bigcup_{l\geq 0}\mathbb{S}^{l}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\mathbb{M}}=\bigcup_{l\geq 0}\tilde{\mathbb{S}}^{l}.

Here, for convenience, we have set
\tilde{\mathbb{S}}^{0}={\mathbb{S}}^{0}=\{\emptyset\}
. We shall refer to an element
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{S}^{l}
as a multisegment of length
\ell(\boldsymbol{s})=l
. It will be said to be a proper multisegment if
\boldsymbol{s}\in\tilde{\mathbb{S}}^{l}
. Given
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{l})\in\mathbb{M}
, set also

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}}=\prod_{k=1}^{l}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{k}},

where we understand
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\emptyset}=\boldsymbol{1}
. One easily sees that, if
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\tilde{\mathbb{M}}
, then
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}
if and only if
\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
is obtained from
\boldsymbol{s}
by a permutation of its entries.
Hence, the isomorphism classes of simple objects in
\mathcal{C}
are in bijection with the equivalence classes of the corresponding equivalence relation
\boldsymbol{s}\sim\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
.

Given
s=[i,j]\in\mathbb{S}
, set

(2.3.3)

s^{*}=[j-h,i]\quad\text{and}\quad{}^{*}\!s=[j,i+h].

One easily checks

\quad s^{*}\in\mathbb{S}\quad{\rm and}\quad{}^{*}\!s\in\mathbb{S}.

If
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{l})\in\mathbb{S}^{l}
, set

\boldsymbol{s}^{*}=(s_{1}^{*},\dots,s_{l}^{*}),\quad{}^{*}\!\boldsymbol{s}=({}^{*}\!s_{1},\dots,{}^{*}\!s_{l}),

and

(2.3.4)

\boldsymbol{s}(k,m)=(s_{k+1},\dots,s_{m})\ \ \text{for}\ \ 0\leq k<m\leq l.

The Hopf algebra structure on
U_{q}(\tilde{\mathfrak{g}})
is chosen so that, if
V=V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})
, then
V^{*}\cong V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{*}})
and
{}^{*}V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})\cong V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{{}^{*}\!\boldsymbol{s}})
. We also recall that the lowest
\ell
-weight of
W(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})
is
(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{{}^{*}\!\boldsymbol{s}})^{-1}
.

Given
s=[i,j]\in\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
, the following elements are known as simple
\ell
-roots:

(2.3.5)

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{s}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i,j}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,j}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i+1,j+1}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i+1,j}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,j+1})^{-1}.

The subgroup of
\mathcal{P}
generated by them is called the
\ell
-root lattice of
U_{q}(\tilde{\mathfrak{g}})
and will be denoted by
\mathcal{Q}
. It is well-known that
\mathcal{Q}
is freely generated by the simple
\ell
-roots. Let also
\cal Q^{+}
be the submonoid generated by the simple
\ell
-roots.
Consider the partial order on
\mathcal{P}
given by

\boldsymbol{\varpi}\leq\boldsymbol{\omega}\quad{\rm if}\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{\varpi}^{-1}\in\cal Q^{+}.

Inspired by (
2.3.2
), let us introduce the following terminology and notation. If
s=[i,j]\in\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
, by the (Dynkin) support and the spectral parameter of
s
we mean, respectively, the numbers

(2.3.6)

\operatorname{supp}(s)=j-i\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{spar}(s)=i+j.

For
\boldsymbol{s}\in\tilde{\mathbb{M}}
, set also

(2.3.7)

\operatorname{supp}(\boldsymbol{s})=\{\sup(s_{k}):1\leq k\leq\ell(\boldsymbol{s})\}\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{spar}(\boldsymbol{s})=\{\operatorname{spar}(s_{k}):1\leq k\leq\ell(\boldsymbol{s})\}.

2.4.
Coverings and Ladders

Two segments
s_{1},s_{2}
will be said to be overlapping if

(2.4.1)

s_{1}\cap s_{2}\neq\emptyset\quad\text{and}\quad s_{m}\neq s_{1}\cup s_{2}\ \text{for}\ m\in\{1,2\}.

If
s_{1}=[i_{1},j_{1}]
and
s_{2}=[i_{2},j_{2}]
, this is equivalent to saying that there exists
k,l\in\{1,2\},k\neq l
, such that

i_{k}<i_{l}\leq j_{k}<j_{l}.

In that case, note

s_{1}\cup s_{2}=[i_{k},j_{l}]\quad\text{and}\quad s_{1}\cap s_{2}=[i_{l},j_{k}].

It is important to note that, if
i_{2}-i_{1}\notin\mathbb{Z}
or, equivalently,
j_{2}-j_{1}\notin\mathbb{Z}
, then
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
do not overlap since
s_{1}\cap s_{2}=\emptyset
.

We will say
(s_{1},s_{2})
is a covering pair
if it is formed by an overlapping pair of segments such that

(2.4.2)

s_{1}\cup s_{2}\in\mathbb{S}\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{spar}(s_{2})>\operatorname{spar}(s_{1}).

In that case, we may also say
s_{2}
covers
s_{1}
and write
s_{2}\rhd s_{1}
. Moreover,
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
are necessarily proper segments and
s_{1}\cap s_{2}\in\mathbb{S}
. One also easily checks

(2.4.3)

s_{2}\rhd s_{1}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\operatorname{spar}(s_{1})<\operatorname{spar}(s_{1}\cap s_{2}),\,\operatorname{spar}(s_{1}\cup s_{2})<\operatorname{spar}(s_{2})

as well as

(2.4.4)

s_{2}\rhd s_{1}\quad\Rightarrow\quad(1+{}^{*}\!s_{1})\rhd s_{2},

where
(1+s)=[i+1,j+1]
if
s=[i,j]
.
Furthermore,

(2.4.5)

s_{2}\rhd s_{1}\ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \ s_{2}^{*}\rhd s_{1}^{*},\quad(s_{1}\cap s_{2})^{*}=s_{1}^{*}\cup s_{2}^{*},\quad(s_{1}\cup s_{2})^{*}=s_{1}^{*}\cap s_{2}^{*},

and similarly for left duality.

We shall say the set
\{s_{1},s_{2}\}
is
h
-connected (or simply connected for short) if

(2.4.6)

\text{either}\ \ s_{1}\rhd s_{2}\ \ \text{or}\ \ s_{2}\rhd s_{1}

or, equivalently, if
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
are overlapping intervals such that
s_{1}\cup s_{2}\in\mathbb{S}
.
We say
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\cdots,s_{l})\in\mathbb{M}
covers
\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}=(s^{\prime}_{1},\dots,s^{\prime}_{l})\in\mathbb{M}
and write
\boldsymbol{s}\rhd\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
, if

(2.4.7)

s_{k}\rhd s^{\prime}_{k}\ \ \text{for all}\ 1\leq k\leq l.

The multisegment
\boldsymbol{s}
is said to be a ladder if, writing
s_{k}=[i_{k},j_{k}]
, we have

(2.4.8)

i_{1}<\cdots<i_{l}\ \ {\rm and}\ \ j_{1}<\cdots<j_{l}.

In that case, it is straightforward from the definition that

(2.4.9)

1\leq k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots<k_{r}\leq l\quad\Rightarrow\quad(s_{k_{1}},\dots,s_{k_{r}})\ \text{is a ladder.}

It is not difficult to check that (
2.4.8
) is equivalent to

(2.4.10)

\operatorname{spar}(s_{k_{2}})-\operatorname{spar}(s_{k_{1}})>|\operatorname{supp}(s_{k_{2}})-\operatorname{supp}(s_{k_{1}})|\quad\text{for all}\quad 1\leq k_{1}<k_{2}\leq l.

A connected ladder is a ladder satisfying

(2.4.11)

s_{k+1}\rhd s_{k}\quad\text{for all}\quad 1\leq k<l.

We shall denote by
\mathbb{L}^{l}
the set of ladders of length
l
and by
\mathbb{L}
the set of all ladders. The following lemma is easily established.

Lemma 2.4.1
.

If
(s_{1},s_{2})\in\mathbb{L}
and
s\rhd s_{k},k=1,2
, then
\{s\cap s_{2},s\cup s_{2}\}\subseteq\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
.∎

We shall need the following (cf.
[
21
, Lemma 5.1.3]
).

Lemma 2.4.2
.

If
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{l})
is a ladder and
s_{l}\rhd s_{1}
, then
\boldsymbol{s}
is a connected ladder.

Proof.

We need to show
s_{k+1}\rhd s_{k}
for each
1\leq k<l
. Write
s_{k}=[i_{k},j_{k}]
and note the assumptions that
s_{l}\rhd s_{1}
implies

i_{1}<i_{l}\leq j_{1}<j_{l}.

In particular, since
j_{1}\leq j_{k}<j_{l}
and
i_{1}<i_{k+1}\leq i_{l}
for all
1\leq k<l
, we get

0\leq j_{1}-i_{l}\leq j_{k}-i_{k+1}<j_{l}-i_{1}

and, hence,

s_{k}\cap s_{k+1}=[i_{k+1},j_{k}]\neq\emptyset\quad\text{and}\quad s_{k}\cup s_{k+1}=[i_{k},j_{k+1}]\subseteq s_{1}\cup s_{l}.

It immediate follows that
s_{k}
and
s_{k+1}
overlap and, since
s_{1}\cup s_{l}\in\mathbb{S}
, we also have
s_{k}\cup s_{k+1}\in\mathbb{S}
. Moreover, the assumption that
\boldsymbol{s}
is a ladder implies
\operatorname{spar}(s_{k})<\operatorname{spar}(s_{k+1})
and, hence,
s_{k+1}\rhd s_{k}
as desired.
∎

If
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{l})
and
\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}=(s^{\prime}_{1},\dots,s^{\prime}_{m})
, set

(2.4.12)

\boldsymbol{s}\vee\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{l},s^{\prime}_{1},\dots,s^{\prime}_{m}).

If
\boldsymbol{s}\rhd\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
, let
\boldsymbol{s}\cap\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{s}\cup\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
be the multisegments whose
k
-th entries are, respectively,

s_{k}\cap s^{\prime}_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad s_{k}\cup s^{\prime}_{k}.

One easily checks

(2.4.13)

\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{L}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \boldsymbol{s}\rhd\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\boldsymbol{s}\cup\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s}\cap\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{L}.

Set also

(2.4.14)

\boldsymbol{s}\diamond\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}=(\boldsymbol{s}\cap\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})\vee(\boldsymbol{s}\cup\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}).

2.5.
Coverings and Socles of Certain Snake Tensor Products

A necessary and sufficient condition for a tensor product of fundamental modules to be reducible is well-known. It can be stated in terms of segments as follows.

(2.5.1)

V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{2}})\ \ {\rm is\ reducible}\ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \ \{s_{1},s_{2}\}\ \ {\rm is\ connected}.

Moreover, in that case,

(2.5.2)

V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}\cap s_{2}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}\cup s_{2}})\cong V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}\cap s_{2}}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}\cup s_{2}})

and, if
s_{2}\rhd s_{1}
, we have the following short exact sequences

(2.5.3)

\displaystyle 0\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}\cap s_{2}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}\cup s_{2}})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{2}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{2}})\to 0,

\displaystyle 0\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{2}})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{2}})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}\cap s_{2}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}\cup s_{2}})\to 0.

If
\boldsymbol{s}
is a ladder, the module
V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})
is known as a snake module. In that case,
V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})
is prime if and only if
\boldsymbol{s}
is a connected ladder. The main result of this section is the following generalization of (
2.5.3
).
We let
\operatorname{soc}V
and
{\rm hd}V
stand for the socle and head of the module
V
.

Theorem 2.5.1
.

If
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{L}^{l}
are such that
\boldsymbol{s}\rhd\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
, then

(2.5.4)

\operatorname{soc}(V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}))\cong V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}\diamond\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}})\cong{\rm hd}(V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})).

\diamond

It follows from (
2.5.3
) that, if
l=\ell(\boldsymbol{s})
, we have a maps

(2.5.5)

V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{k}\cap s^{\prime}_{k}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{k}\cup s^{\prime}_{k}})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{k}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s^{\prime}_{k}})\quad\text{for all}\quad 1\leq k\leq l.

The most intricate part of the proof of
Theorem

2.5.1
is the following proposition whose proof will be given in
Section

5.1
.

Proposition 2.5.2
.

There exists a non-zero map

V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}\cup\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}\cap\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}).

\diamond

2.6.
A Construction of Imaginary Modules

Let
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{l}
and recall (
2.3.4
). For
0\leq p<l
, we say that
\boldsymbol{s}
is a
p
-cover of
\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
, and write
\boldsymbol{s}\rhd_{p}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}

\boldsymbol{s}(0,l-k)\rhd\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}(k,l)\quad\text{for all}\quad 0\leq k\leq p.

In that case, for each

\boldsymbol{s}\cup_{k}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{s}(0,l-k)\cup\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}(k,l),\ \ \ \boldsymbol{s}\cap_{k}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{s}(0,l-k)\cap\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}(k,l),

\boldsymbol{s}\diamond_{k}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}=(\boldsymbol{s}\cap_{k}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})\vee(\boldsymbol{s}\cup_{k}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}(0,k)}\,\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{k}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}(l-k,l)}\quad\text{with}\quad\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}\diamond_{k}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}.

In particular,
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}\diamond\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}=\boldsymbol{\pi}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{0}

Proposition 2.6.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{L}^{l}
, suppose
\boldsymbol{s}\rhd_{p}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
for some
0\leq p<l
, and set

If
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}^{+}
and
\boldsymbol{\pi}\leq\boldsymbol{\pi}_{p}

\operatorname{Hom}(W(\boldsymbol{\pi}),V)\neq 0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k}:0\leq k\leq p\}.

Moreover, there exist a chain of highest
\ell
-weight submodules

such that the highest
\ell
-weight of

is
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k},0\leq k\leq p
.

\diamond

The first part of

2.6.1

, while the second is proved in

. In the notation of

2.6.1
, we pose the following conjecture.

.

For all

,

(2.6.3)

M_{k}/M_{k-1}\ \ \text{is simple and imaginary.}

\diamond

The following is our main result. The proof is given in

.

Theorem 2.6.3
.

The first claim in (

) is true for

and, moreover, there exists a nonzero module map

for some

s_{2}\cap s_{1}^{\prime}=\emptyset\quad\text{and}\quad{}^{*}\!s_{2}\cap{}^{*}\!s_{1}^{\prime}=\emptyset,

then
\psi
induces a nonzero map

. In particular, the second claim in (

) is also true for

in this case.
\diamond

We remark that (
2.6.4
) is equivalent to

j_{1}^{\prime}<i_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad j_{2}-i_{1}^{\prime}>h.

This theorem was proved in

]

, and

Example 2.6.4
.

\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}

s_{1}\rhd s\rhd s_{1}^{\prime}\rhd s^{\prime},\ \ \ s\rhd s^{\prime},\ \ \ j^{\prime}<i_{1},\ \ \ j_{1}-i^{\prime}>h,

3.
Further Background and Technical Results

3.1.
General Hopf Algebra Results

Given a Hopf algebra
\mathcal{H}
over
\mathbb{F}
, its category
\mathcal{C}
of finite-dimensional representations is an abelian monoidal category and we denote the dual of a module
V
by
V^{*}
. More precisely, the action of
\mathcal{H}
of
V^{*}
is given by

(3.1.1)

(hf)(v)=f(S(h)v)\quad\text{for}\quad h\in\mathcal{H},f\in V^{*},v\in V.

It is well known that

\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbb{F},V\otimes V^{*})\neq 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{H}}(V^{*}\otimes V,\mathbb{F})\neq 0.

If the antipode is invertible, one can define a second notion of dual module by replacing
S
by
S^{-1}
in (
3.1.1
), which will be denoted by
{}^{*}V
. The following are also well-known:

(3.1.2)

^{*}(V^{*})\cong(^{*}V)^{*}\cong V

and, given
\mathcal{H}
-modules
V_{1},V_{2},V_{3}
,

(3.1.3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(V_{1}\otimes V_{2},V_{3})\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(V_{1},V_{3}\otimes V_{2}^{*}),

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(V_{1},V_{2}\otimes V_{3})\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(V_{2}^{*}\otimes V_{1},V_{3}),

and

(3.1.4)

(V_{1}\otimes V_{2})^{*}\cong V_{2}^{*}\otimes V_{1}^{*}.

Lemma 3.1.1

(
[
21
, Lemma 2.6.2]
)
.

Let
V_{1},V_{2},V_{3},L_{1},L_{2}
be
\mathcal{H}
-modules and assume
V_{2}
is simple. If

\varphi_{1}:L_{1}\rightarrow V_{1}\otimes V_{2}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\varphi_{2}:V_{2}\otimes V_{3}\rightarrow L_{2}

are nonzero homomorphisms, the composition

L_{1}\otimes V_{3}\xrightarrow{\varphi_{1}\otimes\operatorname{id}_{V_{3}}}V_{1}\otimes V_{2}\otimes V_{3}\xrightarrow{\operatorname{id}_{V_{1}}\otimes\varphi_{2}}V_{1}\otimes L_{2}

does not vanish. Similarly, if

\varphi_{1}:V_{1}\otimes V_{2}\rightarrow L_{1}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\varphi_{2}:L_{2}\rightarrow V_{2}\otimes V_{3}

are nonzero homomorphisms, the composition

V_{1}\otimes L_{2}\xrightarrow{\operatorname{id}_{V_{1}}\otimes\varphi_{2}}V_{1}\otimes V_{2}\otimes V_{3}\xrightarrow{\varphi_{1}\otimes\operatorname{id}_{V_{3}}}L_{1}\otimes V_{3}

does not vanish.∎

3.3.
Tensoring with Right and Left Subintervals

Given
i\leq k\leq j
with
[i,j]\in\mathbb{S}
, in light of (

), we have

(3.3.1)

V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,k})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k,j})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,j})\quad\text{and}\quad V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,j})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k,j})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,k}).

Suppose
s_{1},s_{2}\in\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
satisfy
s_{2}\rhd s_{1}
or, equivalently
(s_{1},s_{2})
is a connected ladder.
Writing
s_{k}=[i_{k},j_{k}]
, set

(3.3.2)

s_{1}\lrcorner s_{2}=[j_{1},j_{2}]\quad\text{and}\quad s_{1}\llcorner s_{2}=[i_{1},i_{2}].

It follows that
s_{1}\cap s_{2}=[i_{2},j_{1}],s_{1}\cup s_{2}=[i_{1},j_{2}]\in\mathbb{S}
and

(s_{1}\lrcorner s_{2})^{*},{}^{*}(s_{1}\llcorner s_{2})\in\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
.
It then follows from (
3.3.1
) and (
3.1.3
) that there exist nonzero maps

(3.3.3)

\displaystyle V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}\cap s_{2}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}\lrcorner s_{2}})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{2}})

\displaystyle,\quad V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}\lrcorner s_{2}}^{*})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}\cup s_{2}})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}})

\displaystyle V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}\llcorner s_{2}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}\cap s_{2}})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}})

\displaystyle,\quad V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}\cup s_{2}})\otimes V({}^{*}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}\llcorner s_{2}})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{2}}).

We interpret
s_{1}\lrcorner s_{2}
and
s_{1}\llcorner s_{2}
as the right and left subintervals of
s_{1}\cup s_{2}
, respectively.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{L}^{l}
are such that
\boldsymbol{s}\rhd\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
. It follows from the first line of (

) that

(3.3.4)

V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{k}\cap s^{\prime}_{k}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s^{\prime}_{k}\lrcorner s_{k}})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{k}})\quad\text{and}\quad V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s^{\prime}_{k}\lrcorner s_{k}}^{*})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{k}\cup s^{\prime}_{k}})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s^{\prime}_{k}})

for all

\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{s}=(s^{\prime}_{1}\lrcorner s_{1},\dots,s^{\prime}_{l}\lrcorner s_{l})

(3.3.6)

\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{L}^{l}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \boldsymbol{s}\rhd\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{s},\,\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\llcorner\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{L}^{l}.

We note however that they are not necessarily connected ladders even if

4.
Snake Modules and Mukhin-Young Lattice Paths

We recall some results of Mukhin and Young established in
[
22
,
23
]
which explore the combinatorics of certain diagonal rank-2 lattice paths to describe results about the
\ell
-weights of snake modules as well as certain exact sequences arising from tensor products of such modules.

4.1.
MY Paths

We reformulate in the language of intervals the results established in
[
22
]
. Let
\mathbb{P}
be the set
of all functions
p:[0,h]\to\mathbb{Z}
satisfying

(4.1.1)

p(0)\in\mathbb{Z}\quad\text{and}\quad|p(k+1)-p(k)|=1\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq k<h.

In particular,

(4.1.2)

|p(k)-p(k^{\prime})|\leq|k-k^{\prime}|,\ \ k,k^{\prime}\in[0,h],

and
p(k)\pm k\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
k\in[0,h]
.
Given such
p
and
k\in[1,N]
, consider the interval

(4.1.3)

s_{p,k}=[(p(k)-k)/2,(p(k)+k)/2],

which is clearly a segment and, moreover,

(4.1.4)

\operatorname{supp}(s_{p,k})=k\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{spar}(s_{p,k})=p(k).

One can easily check using (
4.1.2
) that

(4.1.5)

\{s_{p,k},s_{p,k^{\prime}}\}\quad\text{is not connected for all}\quad k,k^{\prime}\in[0,h].

Given
s=[i,j]\in\mathbb{S}
, let
\mathbb{P}_{i,j}=\mathbb{P}_{s}
be the subset of
\mathbb{P}
satisfying

(4.1.6)

p(0)=2j\quad\text{and}\quad p(h)=h+2i,

or, equivalently,

(4.1.7)

p(0)=\operatorname{spar}(s)+\operatorname{supp}(s)\quad\text{and}\quad p(h)=\operatorname{spar}(s)+\operatorname{supp}(s)^{*}.

We refer to such a function
p
as an MY path. Letting
k^{\prime}\in\{0,h\}
in (
4.1.2
), one easily checks

(4.1.8)

\max\{2j-k,k+2i\}\leq p(k)\leq\min\{k+2j,2h+2i-k\}\quad\text{for all}\quad k\in[0,h].

In particular,

(4.1.9)

p(k)+k^{*}\geq(k+2i)+(h-k)=p(h)\quad\text{for all}\quad k\in[0,h]

and

(4.1.10)

p(k)+k\geq p(0)\quad\text{for all}\quad k\in[0,h].

Let

E^{-}_{p}=\{k\in[1,N]:p(k\pm 1)=p(k)-1\}\quad\text{and}\quad E^{+}_{p}=\{k\in[1,N]:p(k\pm 1)=p(k)+1\}.

In other words,
E^{\pm}_{p}
are the points of local maxima and minima of
p
in
[1,N]
. Let
E_{p}=E_{p}^{+}\cup E_{p}^{-}
.
It is straightforward to check that

(4.1.11)

p(k)+k^{*}=p(h)\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ E_{p}\subseteq[1,k]\quad\text{and}\quad p(k)+k=p(0)\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ E_{p}\subseteq[k,N].

Given
k\in E^{\pm}_{p}
, let
\tau_{k}p:[0,h]\to\mathbb{Z}
be given by

\tau_{k}p(m)=p(m)\ \ \text{if}\ \ m\neq k\ \ \text{and}\ \ \tau_{k}p(k)=p(k)\pm 2.

One easily checks

(4.1.12)

p\in\mathbb{P}_{s}\ \ \text{and}\ \ k\in E^{\pm}_{p}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\tau_{k}p\in\mathbb{P}_{s}\ \ \text{and}\ \ k\in E^{\mp}_{\tau_{k}p}.

Set also

\mathbf{c}_{p}^{\pm}=\left\{s_{p,k}:1\leq k\leq N,\ \ k\in E^{\pm}_{p}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{c}_{p}=\mathbf{c}^{-}_{p}\cup\mathbf{c}^{+}_{p}.

In light of (
4.1.4
), this can be rewritten as

(4.1.13)

s^{\prime}\in\mathbf{c}^{\pm}_{p}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad p(\operatorname{supp}(s^{\prime})-1)=p(\operatorname{supp}(s^{\prime})+1)=p(\operatorname{supp}(s^{\prime}))\pm 1=\operatorname{spar}(s^{\prime})\pm 1.

Note

\{\operatorname{supp}(s^{\prime}):s^{\prime}\in\mathbf{c}_{p}^{\pm}\}=E_{p}^{\pm}.

Using this and (
4.1.12
), it is easily seen that

(4.1.14)

s^{\prime}\in\mathbf{c}_{p}^{+}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad 1+s^{\prime}\in\mathbf{c}_{\tau_{\operatorname{supp}(s^{\prime})}p}^{-}.

Clearly, the knowledge of either
\mathbf{c}_{p}^{+}
or
\mathbf{c}_{p}^{-}
is sufficient to reconstruct an element
p\in\mathbb{P}_{s}
. One easily checks using (
4.1.8
) that

(4.1.15)

s^{\prime}\in\mathbf{c}^{-}_{p}\text{ and }k=\operatorname{supp}(s^{\prime})\ \Rightarrow\ \max\{2j-k,k+2i\}<p(k)

while

(4.1.16)

s^{\prime}\in\mathbf{c}^{+}_{p}\text{ and }k=\operatorname{supp}(s^{\prime})\ \Rightarrow\ p(k)<\min\{k+2j,2h+2i-k\}.

Lemma 4.1.1
.

If
p
is an MY path and
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{l})\in\mathbb{L}
, then
|\{k:s_{k}\in\mathbf{c}^{\pm}_{p}\}|\leq 1
.

Proof.

Suppose
1\leq k_{1}<k_{2}\leq l
are such that
s_{k_{1}},s_{k_{2}}\in\mathbf{c}_{p}^{\pm}
. This means
s_{k_{j}}=s_{p,m_{j}}
for some
m_{j}\in[1,N],j\in\{1,2\}
, and then (
4.1.4
) implies

p(\operatorname{supp}(s_{k_{j}}))=\operatorname{spar}(s_{k_{j}}),\ \ j\in\{1,2\}.

Since
p
is an MY path, this, together with (
4.1.2
), implies

|\operatorname{spar}(s_{k_{1}})-\operatorname{spar}(s_{k_{2}})|\leq|\operatorname{supp}(s_{k_{1}})-\operatorname{supp}(s_{k_{2}})|,

yielding a contradiction with (
2.4.10
).
∎

Let
p_{i,j},p_{i,j}^{*}\in\mathbb{P}_{i,j}
be defined by

(4.1.17)

p_{i,j}(k)=i+j+|k+i-j|\quad\text{and}\quad p^{*}_{i,j}(k)=i+j+h-|h+i-j-k|.

This can be rewritten as

(4.1.18)

p_{s}(k)=\operatorname{spar}(s)+|k-\operatorname{supp}(s)|\quad\text{and}\quad p^{*}_{s}(k)=\operatorname{spar}(s)+h-|k-\operatorname{supp}(s)^{*}|.

In particular,

(4.1.19)

\mathbf{c}_{p}^{-}=\emptyset\Leftrightarrow p=p_{s}\quad{\rm and}\quad\mathbf{c}_{p}^{+}=\emptyset\Leftrightarrow p=p^{*}_{s}.

Moreover,

(4.1.20)

p_{s}(k)\leq p(k)\leq p^{*}_{s}(k)\quad\text{for all}\quad 1\leq k\leq h,\ \ p\in\mathbb{P}_{s},

and

(4.1.21)

p\in\mathbb{P}_{s}\setminus\{p_{s},p_{s}^{*}\}\ {\rm and}\ s^{\prime}\in\mathbf{c}_{p}^{\pm}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\operatorname{spar}(s)<\operatorname{spar}(s^{\prime})<\operatorname{spar}({}^{*}s).

The elements
p_{s}
and
p_{s}^{*}
are referred to a the highest and lowest path of
\mathbb{P}_{s}
, respectively, in
[
23
]
.
Finally, set

\mathbf{c}_{s}^{\pm}=\bigcup_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{s}}\mathbf{c}_{p}^{\pm}.

One easily checks

\mathbf{c}^{-}_{s}\cup\mathbf{c}^{+}_{s}\neq\emptyset\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad s\in\tilde{\mathbb{S}}.

Lemma 4.1.2
.

Let
p
be an MY path and
s\in\mathbb{S}\setminus\mathbf{c}_{p}
. If
p(\operatorname{supp}(s))\leq\operatorname{spar}(s)
,

(4.1.22)

p(\operatorname{supp}(s))-\operatorname{supp}(s)<p(0),\quad\text{and}\quad p(\operatorname{supp}(s))-\operatorname{supp}(s)^{*}<p(h),

there exists
s^{\prime}\in\mathbf{c}^{+}_{p}
such that
\operatorname{spar}(s^{\prime})<\operatorname{spar}(s)
.

Proof.

To shorten notation, let
i=\operatorname{supp}(s)
. The assumptions in (
4.1.22
) imply
i\in[1,N]
. Since
s\notin\mathbf{c}_{p}
, we have
p(i+1)-p(i-1)=\pm 2
.
Suppose it is
2
and let
1\leq r\leq i
be such that

p(i-r)=p(i)-r.

The assumption
p(i)-i<p(0)
implies
i-r>0
. Hence,
i-r\in E^{+}_{p}
and,
letting
s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}
be determined by
\operatorname{supp}(s^{\prime})=i-r
and
\operatorname{spar}(s^{\prime})=p(i-r)
, it follows that
s^{\prime}\in\mathbf{c}^{+}_{p}
and
\operatorname{spar}(s^{\prime})<p(i)\leq\operatorname{spar}(s)
.

If
p(i+1)-p(i-1)=-2
, let
1\leq r\leq i^{*}
be such that

p(i+r)=p(i)-r.

The assumption
p(i)-i^{*}<p(h)
implies
i+r<h
. Hence,
i+r\in E^{+}_{p}
and,
letting
s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}
be determined by
\operatorname{supp}(s^{\prime})=i+r
and
\operatorname{spar}(s^{\prime})=p(i+r)
, it follows that
s^{\prime}\in\mathbf{c}^{+}_{p}
and
\operatorname{spar}(s^{\prime})<p(i)\leq\operatorname{spar}(s)
.
∎

Lemma 4.1.3

(
[
23
, Section 6.4]
)
.

If
s_{1}\rhd s_{2}
, there exists unique
p=p_{s_{2}}^{s_{1}}\in\mathbb{P}_{s_{2}}
such that
\mathbf{c}_{p}^{-}=\{s_{1}\}
. Moreover, in that case,
\mathbf{c}_{p}^{+}=\{s_{1}\cup s_{2},s_{1}\cap s_{2}\}\cap\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
.∎

Lemma 4.1.4
.

If
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathbb{S}
, then
s_{1}\in\mathbf{c}_{s_{2}}^{-}
if and only if
s_{1}\rhd s_{2}
.

Proof.

If
s_{1}\rhd s_{2}
,
Lemma

4.1.3
implies
s_{1}\in\mathbf{c}_{s_{2}}^{-}
.

For the converse, letting
s=s_{2}=[i_{2},j_{2}]
in (
4.1.8
) and taking

and
s^{\prime}=s_{1}=[i_{1},j_{1}]
in (
4.1.15
), since
\operatorname{supp}(s_{1})=j_{1}-i_{1}

\max\{2j_{2}-j_{1}+i_{1},\ j_{1}-i_{1}+2i_{2}\}<p(j_{1}-i_{1})=i_{1}+j_{1}.

i_{2}<i_{1}
and

and

\{p\in\mathbb{P}_{s}:|\mathbf{c}^{-}_{p}|=1\}=\{p^{s^{\prime}}_{s}:s^{\prime}\rhd s\},

Lemma 4.1.5
.

If
s=[i,j]\in\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
,

, and
s^{\prime}=[m,n]\in\mathbf{c}_{p}^{-}\setminus\{{}^{*}s\}
, there exists
s^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbf{c}^{+}_{p}
such that
\operatorname{spar}(s^{\prime\prime})=\operatorname{spar}(s^{\prime})-|\operatorname{supp}(s^{\prime\prime})-\operatorname{supp}(s^{\prime})|

m<j
,
s^{\prime\prime}=[m,n^{\prime}]
for some
m<n^{\prime}<n

s^{\prime\prime}=[m^{\prime},n]

n-h<m^{\prime}<m

The assumption
s\in\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
implies that either
m<j
or
n<h+i
and, since
[m,n]\in\mathbf{c}^{-}_{p}
, we have
p(\operatorname{supp}([m,n]))=\operatorname{spar}([m,n])
. Suppose
m<j

\operatorname{spar}([m,n])-\operatorname{supp}([m,n])=(m+n)-(n-m)=2m<2j=p(0).

This implies

(4.1.25)

p(a)=p(\operatorname{supp}([m,n]))-(\operatorname{supp}([m,n])-a)=2m+a\quad\text{for all}\quad a\in[0,m-n],

and

satisfies the desired properties.
∎

Lemma 4.1.6
.

If
s_{1}\in\mathbb{S}
and

such that
\mathbf{c}_{p}^{+}=\{s_{2}\}
. Moreover, in that case,
\mathbf{c}_{p}^{-}=\{s_{2}\cap{}^{*}s_{1},s_{2}\cup{}^{*}s_{1}\}\cap\tilde{\mathbb{S}}=\{s_{1}\lrcorner s_{2},{}^{*}(s_{1}\llcorner s_{2})\}\cap\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
.

p
is determined by
\mathbf{c}^{\pm}_{p}
.
Note (

) implies
s_{2}+1\in\mathbf{c}_{s_{1}}^{-}
which, together with

4.1.4
, implies
(s_{2}+1)\rhd s_{1}
. Writing
s_{1}=[i_{1},j_{1}]
and
s_{2}=[i_{2},j_{2}]
, the latter implies
i_{1}\leq i_{2}<j_{1}\leq j_{2}
.
Let
p:[0,h]\to\mathbb{Z}
be defined by

p(k)=\begin{cases}2j_{1}+k,&0\leq k\leq j_{2}-j_{1},\\
2j_{2}-k,&j_{2}-j_{1}<k\leq j_{2}-i_{2},\\
2i_{2}+k,&j_{2}-i_{2}<k\leq h+i_{1}-i_{2},\\
2h+2i_{1}-k,&h+i_{1}-i_{2}<k\leq h.\end{cases}

It is straightforward to check that

. Moreover,
E^{+}_{p}=\{j_{2}-i_{2}\}
and
p(j_{2}-i_{2})=j_{2}+i_{2}
, which implies
\mathbf{c}^{+}_{p}=\{s_{2}\}
. In order to check the claim about
\mathbf{c}^{-}_{p}
, note

E^{-}_{p}=\{j_{2}-j_{1},h+i_{1}-i_{2}\}\cap[1,N],\ \ p(j_{2}-j_{1})=j_{1}+j_{2},\ \ \text{and}\ \ p(h+i_{1}-i_{2})=h+i_{1}+i_{2}.

If

, then
[j_{1},j_{2}]=s_{1}\lrcorner s_{2}\in\mathbf{c}^{-}_{p}
, while
[i_{2},h+i_{1}]={}^{*}(s_{1}\llcorner s_{2})\in\mathbf{c}^{-}_{p}
provided
i_{1}<i_{2}
. Since
(s_{2}+1)\rhd s_{1}
, in particular,
j_{2}+1-i_{1}\leq h
and, hence,

[j_{1},j_{2}]=s_{2}\cap{}^{*}s_{1}\ \ \text{and}\ \ [i_{2},h+i_{1}]=s_{2}\cup{}^{*}s_{1}.

∎

Lemma 4.1.7
.

If
s\rhd s^{\prime}
, then
p_{s}^{*}(\operatorname{supp}({}^{*}\!s^{\prime}))\geq\operatorname{spar}({}^{*}\!s^{\prime})+2
and
p^{*}_{s^{\prime}}(\operatorname{supp}({}^{*}\!s))\leq\operatorname{spar}({}^{*}\!s)-2
.

Proof.

Write
s=[i,j]
and
s^{\prime}=[i^{\prime},j^{\prime}]
, so
{}^{*}s^{\prime}=[j,i+h],\operatorname{supp}({}^{*}\!s^{\prime})=h+i^{\prime}-j^{\prime}
, and
\operatorname{spar}({}^{*}\!s^{\prime})=i^{\prime}+j^{\prime}+h=h+\operatorname{spar}(s^{\prime})
. Then, since
i^{\prime}<i\leq j^{\prime}<j
, we get

\displaystyle p_{s}^{*}(\operatorname{supp}({}^{*}\!s^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{e:pp*mod}}{=}h+i+j-|(h+i-j)-(h+i^{\prime}-j^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\geq h+\min\{2i-i^{\prime}+j^{\prime},2j-j^{\prime}+i^{\prime}\}\geq h+i^{\prime}+j^{\prime}+2,

as desired. Similarly,

\displaystyle p_{s^{\prime}}^{*}(\operatorname{supp}({}^{*}\!s))

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{e:pp*mod}}{=}h+i^{\prime}+j^{\prime}-|(h+i-j)-(h+i^{\prime}-j^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq h+\max\{2i^{\prime}-i+j,2j^{\prime}-j+i\}\leq h+i+j-2=\operatorname{spar}(^{*}\!s)-2.

∎

4.2.
On Tuples of MY Paths

We now collect some combinatorial facts about certain tuples of MY paths. Given
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{l})\in\mathbb{M}
, let
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
be the following subset of
\mathbb{P}_{s_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}_{s_{l}}
. An element
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{l})\in\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
if and only if

(4.2.1)

p_{m}(k)<p_{n}(k)\quad\text{for all}\quad k\in[0,h],\ 1\leq m<n\leq l.

The following is easily established.

Lemma 4.2.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{s}
be a ladder of length
l
and
0\leq m<n\leq l
.

(a)

If
(p_{1},\dots,p_{l})\in\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
, then
(p_{m+1},\dots,p_{n})\in\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{s}(m,n)}
.

(b)

If
(p_{m+1},\cdots,p_{n})\in\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{s}(m,n)}
, then
(p_{s_{1}},\dots,p_{s_{m}},p_{m+1},\dots,p_{n},p_{s_{n+1}}^{*},\dots,p_{s_{l}}^{*})\in\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
. ∎

The next lemma gives a characterization of
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
in terms of local maxima.

Lemma 4.2.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{l})
be a ladder and let
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},\cdots,p_{l})\in\mathbb{P}_{s_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}_{s_{l}}
. Then
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
if and only if

(4.2.2)

p_{m+1}(\operatorname{supp}(s))>p_{m}(\operatorname{supp}(s))\quad\text{for all}\quad s\in\mathbf{c}_{p_{m}}^{-},\ \ 1\leq m<l.

Proof.

If
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
then (
4.2.2
) holds by definition.
Consider

S=\{(m,n):1\leq m<n\leq l\text{ and }\exists\ k\in[0,h]\text{ such that }p_{m}(k)\geq p_{n}(k)\}.

If
\boldsymbol{p}\notin\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
, then
S\neq\emptyset
and we need to show

(4.2.3)

\exists\ 1\leq m<l\ \ \text{and}\ \ s\in\mathbf{c}_{p_{m}}^{-}\ \ \text{with}\ \ p_{m}(\operatorname{supp}(s))\geq p_{m+1}(\operatorname{supp}(s)).

Choose
(m,n)\in S
such that

(4.2.4)

n-m=\min\{n^{\prime}-m^{\prime}:(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})\in S\}.

The minimality of
n-m
implies
(p_{m+1},\dots,p_{n})\in\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{s}(m,n)}
. It then follows from
Lemma

4.2.1
(b) that
p_{s_{m}}(k)<p_{n}(k)
for all
k\in[0,h]
and, since
(m,n)\in S
, we conclude
p_{m}\neq p_{s_{m}}
. An application of (
4.1.19
) then implies
\mathbf{c}_{p_{m}}^{-}\neq\emptyset
. We claim

(4.2.5)

\exists\ s\in\mathbf{c}_{p_{m}}^{-}\ \ \text{such that}\ \ p_{m}(\operatorname{supp}(s))\geq p_{n}(\operatorname{supp}(s)).

If
n=m+1
, this implies (
4.2.3
), thus completing the proof. Let us check we cannot have
n>m+1
. Indeed, in that case, (
4.2.4
) implies
(m,m+1)\notin S
and, hence,
p_{m}(\operatorname{supp}(s))<p_{m+1}(\operatorname{supp}(s))
. But then,

p_{n}(\operatorname{supp}(s))\leq p_{m}(\operatorname{supp}(s))<p_{m+1}(\operatorname{supp}(s)),

showing
(m+1,n)\in S
and yielding a contradiction with (
4.2.4
).

To prove the claim, let
k\in[0,h]
such that
p_{m}(k)\geq p_{n}(k)
. Note that, since
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{L}
,

(4.2.6)

p_{m}(0)<p_{n}(0)\quad\text{and}\quad p_{m}(h)<p_{n}(h),

so
k\in[1,N]
. If
k=\operatorname{supp}(s)
for some
s\in\mathbf{c}_{p_{m}}^{-}
there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, i.e., if
k
is not a point of local maximum for
p_{m}
, there exists
r\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
such that
|r|
is maximal with respect to the following property:

k+r\in[0,h]\quad\text{and}\quad p_{m}(k+r)=p_{m}(k)+|r|.

In particular,
p_{m}(k+r)\geq p_{n}(k+r)
and (
4.2.6
) implies
k+r\in[1,N]
. The maximality of
|r|
then implies

p_{m}(k+r\pm 1)=p_{m}(k+r)-1,

thus showing
s=s_{p_{m},k+r}\in\mathbf{c}_{p_{m}}^{-}
and completing the proof of (
4.2.5
).
∎

Lemma 4.2.3
.

If
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{L}^{l}
and
\boldsymbol{s}\rhd\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
, then
(p_{s^{\prime}_{1}}^{s_{1}},\dots,p_{s^{\prime}_{l}}^{s_{l}})\in\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Write
s_{k}=[i_{k},j_{k}],1\leq k\leq l
and, for ease of notation set,
p_{k}=p_{s^{\prime}_{k}}^{s_{k}}
. By definition of
p_{k}
(
Lemma

4.1.3
),
\mathbf{c}_{p_{k}}^{-}=\{s_{k}\}
and, hence, the last equality of (
4.1.13
) implies

p_{k}(j_{k}-i_{k})=j_{k}+i_{k}\quad\text{for all}\quad 1\leq k\leq l.

By Lemma
4.2.2
, it suffices to check that

p_{k+1}(j_{k}-i_{k})>j_{k}+i_{k}\quad\text{for all}\quad 1\leq k<l.

Suppose this were not the case for some
k
. Since
s_{k+1}\in\mathbf{c}_{p_{k+1}}^{-}
, it would follow that

\displaystyle p_{k+1}(j_{k+1}-i_{k+1})-p_{k+1}(j_{k}-i_{k})

\displaystyle\geq(j_{k+1}+i_{k+1})-(j_{k}+i_{k})

\displaystyle=(j_{k+1}-j_{k})+(i_{k+1}-i_{k})>0,

where the last inequality follows since
\boldsymbol{s}
is a ladder. On the other hand, (
4.1.2
) implies

|p_{k+1}(j_{k+1}-i_{k+1})-p_{k+1}(j_{k}-i_{k})|\leq|(j_{k+1}-i_{k+1})-(j_{k}-i_{k})|.

One easily checks the combination of these two inequalities contradicts the assumption that
\boldsymbol{s}
is a ladder.
∎

Lemma 4.2.4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{S}^{l}
and
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{P}_{s_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}_{s_{l}}
. If one of the following conditions are satisfied, then
\boldsymbol{p}\notin\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
.

(a)

There exist
1\leq k<l
and
s\in\mathbb{S}
such that
p_{k}=p_{s_{k}}^{s}
,
p_{k+1}=p_{s_{k+1}}
and
\operatorname{spar}(s)-\operatorname{spar}(s_{k+1})\geq|\operatorname{supp}(s)-\operatorname{supp}(s_{k+1})|
.

(b)

There exist
1\leq k<m\leq l
and
(s,s^{\prime})\in\mathbb{L}
such that
p_{k}=p_{s_{k}}^{s^{\prime}}
and
p_{m}=p_{s_{m}}^{s}
.

Proof.

For part (a), since
s\in\mathbf{c}^{-}_{p_{k}}
by
Lemma

4.1.3
, we have
\operatorname{spar}(s)\overset{\eqref{e:supspspk}}{=}p_{k}(\operatorname{supp}(s))
. An application of (
4.2.1
) then gives

\displaystyle\operatorname{spar}(s)<p_{k+1}(\operatorname{supp}(s))\overset{\eqref{e:pp*mods}}{=}\operatorname{spar}(s_{k+1})+|\operatorname{supp}(s)-\operatorname{supp}(s_{k+1})|,

yielding a contradiction.

For part (b), (
4.2.1
) would imply
p_{k}(\operatorname{supp}(s))<p_{m}(\operatorname{supp}(s))=\operatorname{spar}(s)
and, hence,

p_{k}(\operatorname{supp}(s^{\prime}))-p_{k}(\operatorname{supp}(s))=\operatorname{spar}(s^{\prime})-p_{k}(\operatorname{supp}(s))>\operatorname{spar}(s^{\prime})-\operatorname{spar}(s).

Since
(s,s^{\prime})\in\mathbb{L}
, it then follows from (
2.4.10
) that

p_{k}(\operatorname{supp}(s^{\prime}))-p_{k}(\operatorname{supp}(s))>|\operatorname{supp}(s^{\prime})-\operatorname{supp}(s)|,

contradicting (
4.1.2
).
∎

4.3.
MY Paths and
\ell
-weights

Given
s\in\mathbb{S}
and
p\in\mathbb{P}_{s}
, define

(4.3.1)

\boldsymbol{\omega}(p)=\left(\prod_{s^{\prime}\in\boldsymbol{\rm c}_{p}^{+}}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s^{\prime}}\right)\left(\prod_{s^{\prime}\in\boldsymbol{\rm c}_{p}^{-}}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s^{\prime}}^{-1}\right)\in\cal P.

In particular,

(4.3.2)

\boldsymbol{\omega}(p_{s})=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s},\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}(p_{s}^{*})=({}^{*}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s})^{-1},\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}(p_{s}^{s^{\prime}})=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s^{\prime}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s\diamond s^{\prime}}.

Given
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{l})\in\mathbb{S}^{l}
and
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{l})\in\mathbb{P}_{s_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}_{s_{l}}

(4.3.3)

\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{p})=\prod_{k=1}^{l}\boldsymbol{\omega}(p_{k}).

Remark 4.3.1
.

If
\boldsymbol{s}
is a ladder and
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
, the conditions in (
4.2.1
) guarantee that the expression for
\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{p})
given by (
4.3.1
) and (
4.3.3
) is a reduced word on the fundamental
\ell
-weights regarded as free generators of
\mathcal{P}
. Moreover, if
{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}}_{i,j}^{a}
occurs in
{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}}(\boldsymbol{p})
for some

, then
a\in\{-1,1\}
.
\diamond

Let
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{L}^{l}
. If
\boldsymbol{s}
is not connected, there exists
1\leq k<l
minimal such
\boldsymbol{s}(k-1,k+1)
is not connected, i.e.,
s_{k+1}\ntriangleright s_{k}
. Let
\boldsymbol{s}_{1}=\boldsymbol{s}(0,k)
, which is a connected ladder, and note
\boldsymbol{s}=\boldsymbol{s}_{1}\vee\boldsymbol{s}(k,l)
. Iterating this process, we obtain a sequence of connected ladders
\boldsymbol{s}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{s}_{m}
, such that

\boldsymbol{s}=\boldsymbol{s}_{1}\vee\cdots\vee\boldsymbol{s}_{m}.

The next result was proved in
[
22
,
23
]
.

Proposition 4.3.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{L}^{l}
.

(a)

For all
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}
, we have
\dim V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\leq 1
. Moreover,
\operatorname{wt}_{\ell}(V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}}))=\{\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{p}):\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\}
,
\operatorname{wt}_{\ell}^{+}(V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}}))=\{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\}
, and
\operatorname{wt}_{\ell}^{-}(V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}}))=\{({}^{*}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})^{-1}\}
.

(b)

If
\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
, then

\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime})\leq\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{p})\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad p_{r}(k)\leq p_{r}^{\prime}(k)\ \ {\rm{for\ all}}\ \ r\in[1,l],\ k\in[0,h].

(c)

For
\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{L}
such that
\boldsymbol{s}\vee\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{L}
and we have that
V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}})
is reducible if and only if
s_{1}^{\prime}\rhd s_{l}
. ∎

Suppose
s_{2}\rhd s_{1}
, let
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},s_{2})
, and recall the definition of
p_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}
from Lemma
4.1.3
. Comparing with (
2.5.3
), one easily checks we have
a short exact sequence

(4.3.4)

0\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}(p_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}})\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{2}})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{2}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{1}})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})\to 0.

More generally, we have the following.

Proposition 4.3.3
.

If
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{L}^{l}
with
l>1
, then
V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{l}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}(0,l-1)})
is reducible if and only if
s_{l}\rhd s_{l-1}
.
Moreover, if
s_{l}\rhd s_{l-1}
, there exists a short exact sequence

(4.3.5)

0\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{l}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}(0,l-1)})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})\to 0,

where
\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{s}(0,l-2)\vee(s_{l}\diamond s_{l-1})

Proof.

The first statement is immediate from
[
25
, Lemma 6.2.1]
. Since
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{L}
,
Proposition

3.2.1
implies the tensor product in (
4.3.5
) is highest-
\ell
-weight. Thus, if
s_{l}\rhd s_{l-1}
, it is also reducible and
[
15
, Proposition 4.7]
implies it has length two. Therefore, there exists a short exact sequence

0\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{l}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}(0,l-1)})\to V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})\to 0,

for some
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\cal P^{+}
and we are left to show
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}
. In particular,

\operatorname{Hom}(W(\boldsymbol{\omega}),V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{l}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}(0,l-1)}))\neq 0

and, hence,
Lemma

3.2.7
, together with
Proposition

4.3.2
(a), implies

\boldsymbol{\omega}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s_{l}}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{g})\ \ {\rm for\ some}\ \ \boldsymbol{g}\in\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{s}(0,l-1)}.

It follows from Remark
4.3.1
and (

) that there exists a unique

such that

and, moreover,

We need to show

, which is clear if

. Otherwise, if it were

and

\operatorname{spar}(s_{l})=p_{s_{l}}(\operatorname{supp}(s_{l}))\overset{\eqref{pbomega}}{>}p_{s_{l-1}}(\operatorname{supp}(s_{l}))=g_{l-1}(\operatorname{supp}(s_{l}))\overset{\eqref{pbomega}}{>}g_{k}(\operatorname{supp}(s_{l}))=\operatorname{spar}(s_{l}),

yielding a contradiction.
∎

\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{J}

We now characterize and count how many

8.1.
General Set Up

For the sake of record keeping, let us start by counting the segments which are covered by a given proper segment. Thus, given
s\in\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
, set

C(s)=\{s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}:s\rhd s^{\prime}\}.

Writing
s=[i,j]
and
s^{\prime}=[i^{\prime},j^{\prime}]
, then
s\rhd s^{\prime}
if and only if
i^{\prime}<i\leq j^{\prime}<j\leq h+i^{\prime}
, or, equivalently,

i\leq j^{\prime}<j\ \ \text{and}\ \ j-h\leq i^{\prime}<i.

Thus, there are
j-i
choices for
j^{\prime}
and
h+i-j
choices for
i^{\prime}
. In other words,

(8.1.1)

|C(s)|=\operatorname{supp}(s)\operatorname{supp}(s^{*}).

In the next section, the following subset of
C(s)
will be relevant:

C_{1}(s)=\{s^{\prime}\in C(s):i\leq j^{\prime}<j-1\text{ and }j-h\leq i^{\prime}<i-1\},

whose cardinality is

(8.1.2)

|C_{1}(s)|=(\operatorname{supp}(s)-1)(\operatorname{supp}(s^{*})-1).

Lemma 8.1.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{L}^{l}
and
0\leq p<l
. Then,

\boldsymbol{s}\rhd_{p}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\boldsymbol{s}(k,p+1+k)\rhd_{p}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}(k,p+1+k)\ \ \text{for all}\ \ 0\leq k<l-p.

Proof.

The
\Rightarrow
part is immediate from the definition of
\rhd_{p}
. Thus, assume the right hand side of
\Leftrightarrow
holds. Note that, for
p=0
, this is exactly the definition of
\boldsymbol{s}\rhd\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
, so there is nothing to do. Also, if
p=l-1
, then
k=0
and the assumption reads
\boldsymbol{s}(0,l)\rhd\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}(0,l)
, which coincides with the left hand side. Hence, we can assume
p\leq l-2
. Let us proceed by induction on
l
, which starts when
l=2
by the case
p=0
. Thus, assume
l>2
and that the claim holds for ladders of length
l-1
.

Let
\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}=\boldsymbol{s}(0,l-1)\in\mathbb{L}^{l-1}
and similarly define
\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\prime}
. By assumption,

\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}(k,p+1+k)\rhd_{p}\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\prime}(k,p+1+k)\ \ \text{for all}\ \ 0\leq k<l-1-p.

The inductive assumption then implies
\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}\rhd_{p}\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\prime}
or, equivalently

(8.1.3)

\boldsymbol{s}(0,l-1)\rhd_{p}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}(0,l-1).

Similarly, it follows that

(8.1.4)

\boldsymbol{s}(1,l)\rhd_{p}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}(1,l).

These two facts together clearly imply
\boldsymbol{s}\rhd_{p}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
, as desired.
∎

This lemma is a step towards reducing the task of characterizing the pairs
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})
such that
\boldsymbol{s}\rhd_{p}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
to the case
p=l-1
.

8.2.
1
-Covers of Length
2

Henceforth, let us focus on the case
p=1
.
Given
s_{1},s_{1}^{\prime}\in\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
such that

, let

We will show

\displaystyle|C_{1}(s_{1},s_{1}^{\prime})|=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}(\operatorname{supp}(s_{1}^{\prime}\lrcorner s_{1})-1)(\operatorname{supp}(s_{1}^{\prime}\llcorner s_{1})-1)\times

\displaystyle\times(\operatorname{supp}(^{*}\!s_{1})+\operatorname{supp}(^{*}\!s_{1}\cap{}^{*}\!s_{1}^{\prime}))(\operatorname{supp}(s_{1})+\operatorname{supp}(s_{1}\cap s_{1}^{\prime})).

Combining this with (

), it follows that, for each

(8.2.3)

and

We shall see below that, if

, then

has a single element.

We want to characterize and count the elements in

. As usual, write
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},s_{2})
,
\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}=(s_{1}^{\prime},s_{2}^{\prime})
,

i_{1}<i_{2}\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq h-i_{1},

and similarly for

Since the latter implies

\operatorname{supp}(s_{1})=2,\quad s_{1}^{\prime}=s_{1}^{*},\quad s_{2}=1+s_{1},\quad\text{and}\quad s_{2}^{\prime}=1+s_{1}^{\prime}=s_{2}^{*}.

In particular,
|C_{1}(s_{1},s_{1}^{\prime})|=1
,

is a KR module and
V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}})\cong V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{s}})^{*}
.

Henceforth, let us assume
s_{1}
and

also satisfy (
8.2.6
). Choose any

such that

(8.2.7)

i_{1}^{\prime}<i_{2}^{\prime}<i_{1}\ \ \text{and}\ \ j_{1}^{\prime}<j_{2}^{\prime}<j_{1}.

There are
(i_{1}-i_{1}^{\prime}-1)(j_{1}-j_{1}^{\prime}-1)

Finally, choose any
s_{2}
such that

Note the inequalities

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{2}^{\prime}=i_{1}^{\prime}+1}^{i_{1}-1}\sum_{j_{2}^{\prime}=j_{1}^{\prime}+1}^{j_{1}-1}(j_{2}^{\prime}-i_{1})(h+i_{2}^{\prime}-j_{1})

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i_{2}^{\prime}=i_{1}^{\prime}+1}^{i_{1}-1}h+i_{2}^{\prime}-j_{1}\right)\left(\sum_{j_{2}^{\prime}=j_{1}^{\prime}+1}^{j_{1}-1}j_{2}^{\prime}-i_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}(2h+i_{1}+i_{1}^{\prime}-2j_{1})(i_{1}-i_{1}^{\prime}-1)\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}(j_{1}+j_{1}^{\prime}-2i_{1})(j_{1}-j_{1}^{\prime}-1)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{4}(\operatorname{supp}(s_{1}^{\prime}\lrcorner s_{1})-1)(\operatorname{supp}(s_{1}^{\prime}\llcorner s_{1})-1)\times

\displaystyle\times(\operatorname{supp}({}^{*}\!s_{1})+\operatorname{supp}({}^{*}\!s_{1}\cap{}^{*}\!s_{1}^{\prime}))(\operatorname{supp}(s_{1})+\operatorname{supp}(s_{1}\cap s_{1}^{\prime})).

i_{1}^{\prime}=j_{1}-h\ \ \text{and}\ \ j_{1}^{\prime}=i_{1}.

Plugging this back in (
