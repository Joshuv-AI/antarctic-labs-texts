Are nurse-led patient consultations acceptable for the general practitioners and practice nurses in Germany? Results from a cross-sectional survey in two federal states
Authors: Julia Morgner, Marcus Heise, Celina Wiens, Felix Bauch, Andreas Wienke, Thomas Frese, Solveig Weise
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDemographic changes, increasing prevalence of chronically ill and multimorbid patients and the ongoing shortage of general practitioners (GPs) collectively present significant challenges for European general practice. Task delegation from GP to practice nurse (PN)-led patient consultations could be a potential solution to tackle those challenges.Therefore, this study aimed to examine the attitudes of GPs and PNs towards PN-led consultations in general and for specific conditions.DesignCross-sectional survey using a self-developed, pretested questionnaire, conducted between September 2023 and November 2024. The questionnaire assessed attitudes towards PN-led consultations, conditions within PN-led consultations, possible benefits and concerns regarding PN-led consultations, sociodemographic characteristics and characteristics on GP offices (GPOs). We performed descriptive statistics, univariable and multivariable regression analyses using SPSS V.27.SettingGPOs located in the German federal states of Saxony-Anhalt and Saxony.ParticipantFrom 2071 contacted GPOs, 437 GPs and 339 PNs participated (GP response rate: 21.1%).ResultsThe majority of GPs (61.7%) and PNs (61.2%) were open towards PN-led consultations. In multivariable analysis, GPs showed greater openness if they had prior positive delegation experiences (OR=5.88, 95% CI (3.01 to 11.48)) or already delegated special tasks (OR= 5.34, 95% CI (2.29 to 12.46)). GPs were less open if they worked in urban GPOs (OR=0.44, 95% CI (0.22 to 0.88)) or owned a single GPO (OR=0.41, 95% CI (0.20 to 0.83)). In multivariable analysis, PNs were more open towards PN-led consultations if they had prior positive delegation experiences (OR=3.03, 95% CI (1.12 to 8.18)) and advanced PN training (OR=3.50, 95% CI (1.44; 8.51)).The three most accepted conditions by GPs and PNs for PN-led consultations were chronic wounds, diabetes mellitus and arterial hypertension.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate broad openness among both GPs and PNs towards PN-led consultations in German GPOs in general, and for various acute and chronic conditions. PN-led consultations are already partially practised. The results indicate considerable potential for further delegation beyond current national agreements.Future pilot studies should further develop PN roles and provide evidence of feasibility and non-inferiority of PN-led consultations compared to GP-led consultations. Conditions and participant characteristics investigated may serve as a foundation for study design and participant recruitment.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This is one of the first quantitative studies in Germany assessing attitudes of both general practitioners (GPs) and practice nurses (PNs) towards PN-led consultations in general and for specific conditions, with this level of scope and detail. There is broad openness—especially among rural GPs, PNs with advanced training and those with prior delegation experience—with particularly strong support for PN-led consultations for diabetes mellitus, arterial hypertension and chronic wounds.

Background

Demographic changes,1 increasing prevalence of chronically ill and multimorbid patients2 and the ongoing shortage of general practitioners (GPs3) collectively present significant challenges for sustainability and functionality of European general practice. Task delegation from GP to practice nurse (PN)-led patient consultations (PNLC) could be a potential solution to tackle those challenges.4

Previous research has shown that task delegation from GPs to PNs is well accepted by patients, GPs and the PNs themselves48 and could lead to equal or even better health outcomes, especially for chronically ill patients.912 For example, conditions as arterial hypertension and diabetes mellitus appear to be slightly better controlled in PN-led care compared with GP-led care,91113 and PN-led care may also reduce mortality associated with these conditions.9 10 Patient satisfaction appears to be higher in PN-led care,9 10 12 14 which may be explained by longer consultation times and providing patients with more information about their diseases in PN-led care.10 15 16 Furthermore, PN-led care could lead to a more attractive job profile for PNs,8 11 17 reduced waiting times for patients and increased accessibility to GP offices (GPOs).7 18 Although cost-effectiveness remains uncertain,19 there is evidence to suggest that PN-led care may help reduce overall costs in general practice.11 20

The use of task delegation and team-based approaches is well established in general practice across Europe and globally.162123 However, the scope of task delegation in general practice differs greatly between countries. In healthcare systems such as Canada, the Netherlands, New Zealand, the United Kingdom and the USA, advanced PNs (APNs) play a significant role in general practice.23 They independently carry out patient consultations, diagnose medical conditions, order diagnostic tests, manage chronic illnesses such as arterial hypertension and diabetes and prescribe or adjust medications.16 23 In Austria, the Czech Republic, France and Germany, the scope of task delegation in general practice is comparatively restricted.23 Here, PNs are not permitted to independently perform tasks such as patient consultations, diagnosing conditions, prescribing medications, adjusting dosages or ordering diagnostic tests.23

In Germany, PNs are crucial for smoothly running a GPO. However, PNs perform less complex medical tasks24 and work under close supervision of the GP. For the purpose of this study, we used the term ‘PN’ to encompass a broad range of medical professions, which currently work in German GPOs, including healthcare assistants, PNs, non-physician practice assistants (NäPa), care assistants in general practice (VERAH) and academically trained health professionals such as physician assistants (PA).

PN training in Germany normally lasts 3 years.25 In recent years, an increasing number of PNs have pursued additional training and qualifications.4 5 These specialised PNs are referred to as VERAHs26 or NäPas (box 127). For simplicity, we further refer to VERAHs/NäPas as APN. German APNs take on a broader range of responsibilities than regular PNs (box 1), but legal accountability remains with GPs.

The role of practice nurses (PNs) and advanced PNs in German general practitioner offices

PNs in German general practitioner offices

PNs are the administrative and operational foundation of the practice, supporting the general practitioner (GP) in both organisational and clinical tasks. However, their duties mainly involve routine tasks, such as patient receptions, coordinating daily operations and performing fundamental medical procedures. These tasks include recording ECGs, taking blood or measuring vital signs (eg, blood pressure and heart rate). Additionally, PNs perform wound care and can inject a limited range of medications, such as vaccinations or vitamin B12 injections, under the direct supervision of the GP. Beyond clinical activities, they manage medication inventories, carry out administrative work and handle billing responsibilities.

GPs in German general practitioner offices

In outpatient care, GPs and specialists operate alongside one another. Patients are not required to register with a specific physician and have the freedom to choose their healthcare provider. Typically, patients first consult their GP when facing health issues, with the GP then serving as a coordinator of their care.

In Germany, a delegation agreement28 defines services eligible for delegation and specifies conditions under which delegation is permitted.

Tasks that may not be delegated include anamnesis, determining indications, patient examinations (including invasive diagnostics), diagnosis, patient education and counselling, therapeutic decision-making as well as the execution of invasive therapies and surgical procedures. In consequence, PNLC remains currently informal and understudied in Germany.

Our previous qualitative research8 found promising acceptance among GPs and PNs towards PNLC. However, views of GPs and PNs on these tasks in German GPO have not yet been quantitatively explored and remain understudied. Therefore, it is unknown how many GPs and PNs are open towards PNLC and which conditions they would consider most suitable for PNLC. Previous quantitative studies conducted in Germany on PN-led care56 24 2931 have predominantly focused on the delegation of less complex tasks, rather than on PNLC in general. Additionally, these studies have not examined the attitudes of GPs and PNs towards specific reasons for encounter and conditions (hereafter referred to as ‘specific conditions’) within PNLC.

While international surveys have addressed these aspects,32 33 their findings are not directly applicable to German general practice due to structural differences in healthcare systems and variations in the implementation of task delegation within general practice.

Therefore, this study seeks to fill this gap by investigating the perspectives of German GPs and PNs on PNLC.

Methods

Study design

This is a cross-sectional survey of GPs, GP trainees and PNs in two federal states in Germany, Saxony-Anhalt and Saxony. It is part of the RESIDENCE Study (nuRse lEd conSultatIon anD pErmaneNt mediCation advicE; DRKS00032386), which investigates the attitudes of the general population, GPs and PNs towards PNLC and PN-led dose adjustments. In this paper, we present our findings on the attitudes of GPs and PNs towards PNLC.

Recruitment and data collection

We invited GPs, GP trainees and PNs of 2071 GPOs to complete our questionnaire either online or via post or fax. In Saxony-Anhalt, we contacted all registered GPOs and selected mode of contact randomly via email, post or fax. In Saxony, we invited all GPOs available by email address to participate in our study. Participants invited per email were asked to complete our survey questionnaire online, those contacted per post or fax were invited to complete a paper-based version. We used Lime Survey34 for data collection of the online survey. We contacted participating GPs directly and personalised, based on data of the register of the corresponding Associations of Statutory Health Insurance Physicians in both federal states. As no PN register exists in Germany, we approached PNs impersonalised and indirectly through the practice contact information, as no dedicated PN register exists in Germany. We included all study documents and the questionnaire in the email, post or fax addressed to the GP.

We conducted our survey in two stages (online supplemental file 1). In the first stage, we distributed the full questionnaire between September 2023 and June 2024, including three reminders. In the second stage, we carried out a supplementary survey including a shortened version of our questionnaire to further increase the initial response rate (16.8%) up to 21.1% and gather a broader range of opinions. Of note, we calculated our response rate based on GP response only, as no register of PNs exists in Germany. We distributed this shortened version of our questionnaire via fax between September and November 2024 to all GPOs initially invited to the survey, excluding those who explicitly declined participation. In this stage, we did not use reminders.

We also developed and included a non-responder questionnaire in both stages of our survey.

Questionnaire and measurements

We used a self-developed, pretested questionnaire. We based the questionnaire’s development on preceding qualitative studies involving semistructured interviews with patients, GPs and PNs.7 8 These studies identified key themes for our questionnaire, such as PNs’ competence, trust in PNs’ medical and communication skills, patient safety and contextual factors. The pretest involved the Think-Aloud Method to ensure clarity and comprehensibility. The questionnaire included items on attitudes towards PNLC, specific conditions within PNLC, possible benefits and concerns regarding PNLC, sociodemographic characteristics and characteristics on GPO (onlinesupplemental files 2 3).

We based our primary study outcome variable on an adapted item originally used by Jedro et al4: ‘Would you be willing to establish (GPs)/perform (PNs) PNLC?’ using a 5-point Likert scale for its assessment. Since PNLC are uncommon in Germany, we introduced participants to a hypothetical care concept. This concept described PNLC conducted by PNs with advanced training and qualifications, comparable to APNs in other countries, operating independently of a GP. In this scenario based on our previous research,7 8 APNs would handle consultations for patients with stable or uncomplicated conditions, while those with complex conditions would remain under the care of the GP. GPs who had already established PNLC, PNs who already performed PNLC and those who expressed willingness to do so, were asked additional questions (figure 1). These included items on the perceived benefits of PNLC, attitudes towards PNLC for specific conditions and current qualifications of PNs. GPs who were unwilling to establish and PNs who were unwilling to perform PNLC were asked further questions on their concerns about PNLC. For concerns, perceived benefits and PN qualifications, we utilised 6-point Likert scales. To quantify acceptable conditions for PNLC, we used a 7-point Likert scale (onlinesupplemental files 2 3).

For the supplementary survey in the second stage of our study, we utilised a shortened version of our original questionnaire. This version included the primary study outcome, further gender, year of graduation, residential region of the GPO and the number of PNs, APNs and GPs employed at the GPO.

Our non-responder questionnaire (online supplemental file 4), which was also pretested, included eight 6-point Likert-scaled items addressing reasons for non-participation, such as, eg, lack of time or rejection of PNLC as well as sociodemographic and GPO-related items. These items comprised gender, age, professional role within the practice, residential region of GPO and the number of GPs employed.

Assessment of data

To ensure accuracy, two independent scientists (JM and FB) verified data consistency and coherence through comparison with the completed paper-based questionnaires. Before data analysis, we checked all data for plausibility and dichotomised all categorical variables in accordance with the specifications set out in tables1 2 and onlinesupplemental files 5 6. We dichotomised our primary study outcome variable into ‘willing to accept/perform PNLC’ (including ‘would be willing’ and ‘would rather be willing’) and ‘not willing to accept/perform PNLC’ (including ‘would not be willing’ and ‘would rather not be willing’) and excluded the ‘already takes place’-option. We dichotomised the variable ‘residential region of GPO’ into ‘rural’ (≤50 000 inhabitants) and ‘urban’ (> 50 000 inhabitants), and the variables concerning professional exhaustion of GPs and PNs into ‘high’ (including always/often) and ‘low’ (including occasionally/rarely/never). We transformed the variables concerning participants’ concerns and benefits and qualification of PNs into three categories: ‘agreement’ (strong/somewhat agreement), ‘undecided’ (undecided) and ‘disagreement’ (strong/somewhat disagreement). We dichotomised the variables concerning trust in PNs’ medical skills and PNs’ skills to communicate empathically as well as PNs adherence to agreements, into two categories: ‘high trust’ (strong agreement) and ‘lower trust’ (somewhat agreement/undecided/somewhat disagreement/strongly disagreement). The ‘I don't know’ option was excluded for all variables.

Statistical analysis

For our primary study outcome, we included both the data from the short questionnaire as well as from the long questionnaire, as the relative frequencies of the two sets of data were found to be nearly identical. We utilised our dichotomised primary study outcome ‘Would you be willing to establish/perform PNLC?’ as the dependent variable for univariable and multivariable analysis.

For descriptive analyses, we calculated absolute and relative frequencies using pairwise deletion, meaning that cases with missing values were excluded only for the respective variable.

To compare GPs and PNs who were willing to delegate or perform PNLC with those who were not, we performed weighted (binomial) logistic regression analysis. In our regression analysis, we chose independent variables based on the criteria outlined by Hosmer et al.35 Initially, we included all variables with an univariable p value <0.25. We selected cases for the regression analysis exclusively from data collected during the first stage of our survey (see onlinesupplemental files 5 6) and performed complete case analysis. Since the correlation between the variable ‘Perceiving the need for PNLC’ and our primary study outcome variable was too strong, we excluded this variable from our model and reported only its univariable effect. Subsequently, we employed a backward stepwise selection approach, excluding independent variables with a Wald statistic p value >0.05 to create the final model. To address missing data, we applied multiple imputations by chained equations (m=10)36 within a sensitivity analysis for all multiple regression models. Implementing fully conditional specifications,37 all model-immanent factors served as auxiliary variables. We set the two-sided significance level at 5%. We validated the results of our sensitivity analysis through a comparison with the findings of the main analysis.

We performed descriptive statistics, univariable and multivariable analyses using SPSS V.27. To conduct sensitivity analysis, we used STATA V.16.1 (Stata Corp., College Station, Texas).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this study.

Results

In total, 437 GPs and 339 PNs from 2071 contacted GPOs participated in our survey (GP’s response rate 21.1%). Of those, 89 GPs and 88 PNs completed the short questionnaire.

Participants’ characteristics and team interactions

The majority of participating GPs (64.7%) and PNs (97.7%) were female (tables1 2). A total of 55.3% of GPs and 59.6% of PNs graduated between 2000 and 2024 and 67.8% of GPs and 69.7% of PNs worked in rural GPOs, and 47.7% of PNs were trained as APN (table 2). The majority of GPs trusted their PN’s skills to communicate empathically (57.4%) and their PN’s medical skills (53.3%; table 1).

From the participating PNs, 52.2% trusted in their own skills to communicate empathically, while 63.8% perceived a high level of trust from their GP in their medical skills (table 2).

Currently implemented task delegation procedures in GPO

Of participating GPs and PNs, 85.0% of GPs (n=260) and 89.8% of PNs (n=177) indicated they already delegate or take on tasks prior performed by the GP (eg, home visits or patient consultations; online supplemental file 7). Of those, 79.1% (n=140) of PNs reported to independently perform home visits and 84.0% (n=218) of GPs reported to delegate home visits. Of GPs, 12.9% (n=34) reported they had delegated patient consultations under GP’s supervision, while 5.3% (n=14) had done so independently to PNs after providing instruction. Similarly, 16.9% (n=30) of PNs reported conducting consultations under GP’s supervision, while 6.2% (n=11) had done so independently.

Attitudes towards PNLC

The majority of GPs (61.7%) were willing to delegate PNLC and 61.2% of PNs were willing to perform PNLC (figure 2). A total of 4.9% of GPs have already implemented PNLC, while 6.8% of PNs have been involved in PNLC.

Initial regression model

Our initial regression model indicated that GPs demonstrated a greater willingness to establish PNLC when they had prior positive delegation experiences (OR=5.23), had already delegated special tasks (eg, home visits, patient consultations or medication recommendations; OR=4.05), had been in their current GPO ≤10 years (OR=1.31), had high trust in their PN’s medical skills (OR=1.14) or the PN’s skills to communicate empathically (OR=1.82) or when ≥1 PN trained as APN was currently employed in their GPO (OR=1.66; online supplemental additional file 8a). Furthermore, the willingness of GPs to establish PNLC was found to be lower among GPs who graduated until 1999 (OR=0.69), owned a single GPO (OR=0.53), worked in an urban GPO (OR=0.41), worked in an GPO with only one GP employed (OR=0.92) or employed a maximum of one PN trained as healthcare assistant (OR=0.59; online supplemental file 8a).

A prior positive delegation experience (OR=2.62), training as APN (OR=4.10) and high trust in own skills to communicate empathically (OR=2.03) were associated with a greater openness of PNs to perform PNLC (online supplemental additional file 8b). The willingness of PNs to PNLC was found to be lower in PNs who graduated until 1999 (OR=0.92), PNs in urban GPOs (OR=0.40) and in PNs who had at least one PN trained as APN working in their current GPO (OR=0.81; online supplemental file 8b).

Final regression model

The final regression model confirmed that GPs with prior positive delegation experiences were more open towards PNLC (OR=5.88, 95% CI (3.01 to 11.48)) as well as GPs who already delegated special tasks (OR=5.34, 95% CI (2.29 to 12.46); figure 3a). We found the willingness to delegate PNLC to be lower among GPs owning a single GPO (OR=0.41, 95% CI (0.20 to 0.83)) and GPs in urban GPOs (OR=0.44, 95% CI (0.22 to 0.88)).

Sensitivity analysis

We did not find any relevant differences between our complete case analysis and sensitivity analysis (online supplemental file 9a–d).

Specific conditions

GPs most frequently accepted arterial hypertension (70.6%), COPD (64.7%) and diabetes mellitus (63.0%) as conditions for PNLC (figure 4). PNs most frequently accepted arterial hypertension (60.3%) and urinary tract infections (59.6%) as conditions for PNLC, whereas 61.4% of PNs would be willing to perform physical examinations, only 29.0% of GPs would be willing to delegate this task. Of participating GPs and PNs, some had already implemented PNLC for chronic wounds (GPs: 30.0%; PNs: 27.9%) and diabetes mellitus (GPs: 24.0%; PNs: 29.3%).

Benefits and concerns

Of the GPs who had already established PNLC or were willing to implement them, 74.1% thought that implementation of PNLC would result in a reduction of workload for GPs (table 3). Additionally, 67.6% of GPs and 69.2% of PNs agreed that PNLC would lead to improved work satisfaction for PNs. Furthermore, 70.9% of GPs and 71.8% of PNs believed PNLC would offer more time for patients’ needs.

Of those participants who were not willing to delegate or perform PNLC, 77.6% of GPs and 78.8% of PNs indicated that patient consultations are only the GP’s responsibility.

Current qualification of PNs

Of participating GPs, 41.0% indicated that they believe their PNs are already enough qualified to perform PNLC independently. Similarly, 46.1% of participating PNs stated that they consider themselves to be adequately qualified to perform PNLC independently.

Non-responder analysis

In our non-responder analysis, 47 GPs and PNs participated, with 82.9% of these being GPs. The majority of non-respondents were female (GPs: 75.9%; PNs: 83.3%), and 33.3% of PNs worked in single-GPOs. Furthermore, no relevant differences were observed between respondents and non-respondents. As main reasons for non-participation, participants reported lack of time (75.0%), general refusal to delegate medical tasks (63.0%) and the length of the questionnaire (75.0%). Some GPs and PNs cited additional reasons for non-participation via survey open questions, either paper-based (n=10) or online (n=17), with the most common being lack of time, professional exhaustion and lack of incentives for participation.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we examined the openness of GPs and PNs towards independent PNLC in two federal states in German GPO. To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first to quantitatively explore the perspectives of GPs and PNs on independent PNLC in German GPO, including a broad range of acceptable conditions for PNLC.

Main findings

Roughly two-third of both GPs and PNs were open towards the implementation of PNLC. GPs were more likely to support the implementation of PNLC if they had prior positive delegation experiences, had already delegated some special tasks, worked in rural GPOs or practised in group/collaborative GPOs. Similarly, PNs demonstrated greater willingness to perform PNLC if they had prior positive delegation experiences and had APN training. The three most commonly accepted conditions in PNLC among GPs and PNs were chronic wounds, diabetes mellitus and arterial hypertension. The perceived primary benefits of PNLC included reduced workload for GPs, increased job satisfaction for PNs and more time for patient’s needs.

Therefore, this study adds important aspects for further research on PN-led care in general practice in Germany and provides valuable insights into the experiences and attitudes of GPs and PNs towards task delegation in a healthcare system currently predominantly naïve to PN-led care.

Comparison with other studies

Most international studies on GP’s and PN’s perspectives were conducted after implementation of PN-led care.3843 In contrast, we explored these perspectives prior to the introduction of PN-led care in GPO, which may limit the comparability of our results. Our findings may be interesting for countries considering the implementation of PN-led care in GPOs, such as Austria, France or the Czech Republic.39

Consistent with the previous studies,24 31 33 our participants displayed a notable willingness to engage in task delegation. Interestingly, some participants had already implemented independent PNLC, suggesting that PNLC are already practised in a small amount in German GPOs. According to current delegation regulations in Germany,28 PNLC are currently explicitly not permitted and would therefore exceed the limitations of what is presently allowed and customary German general practice. Some GPs may not be fully aware of this restriction. Consistent with prior qualitative evidence,8 we interpret these findings as a pragmatic response within routine general practice to ensure continuity of patient care amidst the persistent GP shortage.3 Given that PNLC are still very uncommon in German general practice,16 21 22 the majority of other German studies focused on assessing the willingness to delegate more limited or specialised tasks.5 24 31

Consistent with the previous international studies, we found a strong negative correlation between willingness to delegate and GPs working in single GPOs,30 32 and a strong positive correlation between GPs willingness to delegate and GPs working in rural areas.6 22 30 Furthermore, our study confirmed the assumption that previous experience is strongly correlated with GPs’ willingness to delegate.39

In contrast to other international studies, we identified no univariable correlations between gender and willingness to delegate,6 30 44 or between the number of patients seen per quarter and willingness to delegate.29 However, given the relatively small sample size of our study, we cannot entirely rule out these associations.

As we did not ask our participants about their age, it is not possible to confirm or deny the findings of other international studies concerning the correlation between age and willingness to delegate.6 22 30 33 44 45 However, participants were asked about their year of graduation, revealing a negative univariable correlation between longer work experience and willingness to delegate or take on tasks.

In this view, early-career GPs in rural group GPOs may be a promising target group to initiate pilot projects on PNLC.

A recent German study by Wolf et al31 observed that PNs were more willing to take on additional tasks than GPs were willing to delegate, a result that our study did not confirm. We found the overall willingness to implement PNLC to be nearly identical among GPs and PNs (61.9% vs 61.4%). Furthermore, GPs demonstrated a greater willingness to implement PNLC for chronic conditions such as arterial hypertension (70.6% vs 60.3%) and diabetes mellitus (63.0% vs 54.3%) than PNs. Conversely, our findings indicated that PNs demonstrated a greater willingness to perform PNLC for specific challenging tasks, including physical examinations (61.4% vs 29.0%) and ECG analysis (59.3% vs 28.9%). One possible explanation for this discrepancy may be the belief that PNs should only manage patients with less complex conditions, a view held by some GPs and patients, as described in the literature.11 14 42 Conversely, PNs have demonstrated a willingness to assume more challenging and intricate responsibilities.40

Another possible explanation is that definition of what is a complex task or condition may vary between GPs and PNs. Additionally, when assessing openness towards PNLC for specific conditions, such as, eg, arterial hypertension, our questionnaire described these consultations only in general terms, without specifying the exact tasks involved. Consequently, GPs and PNs may have interpreted this concept differently. Future research should therefore examine whether variations exist in how PNLC for specific conditions are understood and to which aspects of these consultations GPs and PNs would be open.

Consistent with international research, we found that participants were open to implement PNLC for conditions, such as arterial hypertension,40 diabetes mellitus31 and COPD.43 In other countries, PNs already manage patients with these conditions, achieving health outcomes comparable to or even superior to those of GP-led care.12 13 46 Notably, some of our participants had already introduced such consultations, suggesting that this approach could be successfully implemented in German general practice in the future.

In concordance with previous international studies, our participants perceived GP’s workload reduction,33 40 45 47 increased time efficiency for GPs,6 7 24 48 higher work satisfaction for GPs and PNs11 17 24 and having more time for patient’s needs7 17 18 24 33 as potential benefits of PNLC.

In contrast to other studies, participants did not agree that PNLC would lead to earlier appointments at GPOs.7 16 17

Previous international research identified that PNs may lack the requisite qualifications5 39 40 and the burden of time-consuming training and supervision5 7 15 30 39 as potential barriers towards task delegation.

In our study, more than half of the participating GPs and PNs did not believe that PNs were yet sufficiently qualified to independently conduct PNLC. Considering that, continuing education opportunities for PNs should prioritise enhancing their knowledge of symptom management and therapeutic approaches to support the implementation of PNLC for specific conditions in countries naïve to PN-led care.

Furthermore, our findings indicate that PNs with additional training as APN showed greater openness towards PNLC. This aligns with previous evidence suggesting that attitudes towards delegation are strongly influenced by the qualifications of PNs.5 30

Existing evidence also suggests that patient outcomes may be more favourable in APN-led consultations than in PNLC.10 However, our study was not designed to directly compare attitudes towards PNLC versus APN-led consultations. Based on the previous findings, it is possible that APN-led consultations would receive higher levels of acceptance. While studies from Germany have shown that both GPs and PNs are more supportive of delegation when nurses possess advanced clinical training,8 research from France indicates that not all GPs perceive APNs as more beneficial than other PNs.32 As to the best of our knowledge, none of these studies have directly compared agreement to APN-led consultations versus PNLC, future research should address this gap to clarify how advanced training influences the acceptance and implementation of nurse-led care models.

Ensuring that PNs are adequately qualified is essential for successful implementation of PNLC.11 In Germany, PN training normally lasts 3 years25 and PNs with at least 3 years of work experience can pursue additional qualifications, such as VERAH or NäPa, to become German APNs (26 27; box 1).

Internationally, more advanced programmes, such as those for nurse practitioners (NPs) in the USA,49 require a master’s or doctoral degree alongside advanced clinical training. These qualifications, far exceeding those of German APNs and PNs, enable NPs to diagnose and manage acute and chronic conditions, including diabetes and hypertension.21 49

To ensure German APNs and PNs are adequately prepared for PNLC, it may be necessary to provide additional training to align with international standards. Emerging programmes, such as the Primary Care Manager and PA bachelor’s degrees, offer opportunities to implement this approach. However, the cost-effectiveness and economic feasibility of integrating Primary Care Managers and PAs into general practice remain uncertain.44 48

Prior research has highlighted that trust and a positive relationship between GPs and PNs are essential for a favourable attitude towards task delegation.15 29 39 40 47 In our study, we also explored participants’ perceptions of trust but delved deeper than previous studies by categorising trust into three dimensions: medical skills, communication skills and adherence to agreements. Our findings revealed a positive univariable correlation between GPs’ trust in PNs’ medical and communication skills and their willingness to implement PNLC. In light of this, both medical and communication skills should be incorporated into future qualification programmes for PNs to enhance GPs’ trust in their capabilities.

However, it is equally important to consider the risk of intensifying the existing PN shortage,50 by imposing stringent qualification requirements. According to the delegation agreement in Germany, a vocational healthcare qualification is usually sufficient for delegated tasks,28 making additional formal training non-mandatory.

An alternative approach could involve revising the PN training curriculum to better equip healthcare professionals for delegation responsibilities, offering a more sustainable and accessible solution.

Strengths and limitations

This is one of the first quantitative studies conducted in Germany to investigate openness towards independent PNLC for a broad range of conditions with this level of scope and detail. Our study offers a comprehensive range of variables and perspectives on topics that have not been extensively explored in prior research in a country naïve to PN-led care in general practice, including a broad range of acceptable conditions in PNLC and specific benefits and concerns. As one of the few studies, our study focuses on both GPs and PNs’ perspectives on PNLC quantitatively and examines reasons for non-participation.

In contrast to countries where PN-led care is already established,22 23 this study was conducted in Germany prior to such implementation. Surveys conducted prior to the introduction of PN-led care are uncommon, making the findings particularly valuable to an international audience—especially in countries currently considering implementation, such as Austria and the Czech Republic.

Due to the cross-sectional design of our study, we cannot assume direct causality. Selection bias cannot be excluded, as participation was voluntary and may have attracted a higher proportion of GPs and PNs who were already open towards PNLC. Our response rate is moderate, but aligns with those reported in similar studies.293244 45 Our non-responder analysis revealed that GPs who completed our non-responder questionnaire but did not participate in the main survey were more likely to be female, while non-responding PNs were more often male. Therefore, a potential gender effect, as observed in other studies,6 30 cannot be excluded. Furthermore, no relevant discrepancies were identified between respondents and non-respondents. In addition, non-response bias cannot be ruled out: of 2071 contacted GPOs, 437 GPs participated (GP response rate 21.1%), whereas no reliable response rate could be calculated for PNs. Additionally, respondents may have been influenced by social desirability. Our questionnaire was not validated, as no validated questionnaire on this topic exists. However, our interdisciplinary research team developed it carefully and ensured clarity and relevance by pre-testing.

We could not contact PNs directly and instead reached out to them indirectly through the practice contact data. This approach was necessary because no ‘PN register’ exists, and PNs, unlike GPs, could not be contacted individually using, eg, web search or hand search.

Additionally, the generalisability of our findings may be limited due to regional focus, as data were drawn from only two German federal states, and the heterogeneity of PN and APN roles in German general practice could have influenced the results. Future research should, therefore, examine role definitions more systematically and across a broader geographic scope to better reflect attitudes and practices on a national level. As the study was conducted within the German healthcare system, transferability to countries with different structural conditions may be limited too. Nevertheless, our results provide valuable insights and remain highly relevant for informing practice and future research.

Conclusion

Our study reveals that roughly two-thirds of GPs and PNs are willing to implement PNLC in German general practice, despite this being a novel concept in Germany.

Support was particularly strong among GPs in rural and group GPOs, PNs trained as APNs, and both GPs and PNs with prior positive experiences in delegation.

While participants expressed openness to PNLC, particularly for chronic conditions, and recognised benefits such as reduced GP workload and increased time for patients’ needs, some concerns remained regarding PN qualifications.

Although PN-led care is currently limited in scope, both GPs and PNs demonstrate considerable openness to expand its use, highlighting potential for delegation beyond current national frameworks. In practice, this suggests a favourable foundation for broader integration of PNLC in general practice, especially in GPOs where task delegation is already established. In rural settings, PNLC may offer an acceptable and effective approach for maintaining comprehensive care amid GP shortages. The acute and chronic conditions examined in our study offer a practical starting point for pilot studies assessing the feasibility and non-inferiority of PNLC compared with GP-led consultations, with study design and recruitment guided by identified participant and GPO characteristics.

From a policy perspective, investment in advanced nursing education, supportive regulatory frameworks and evidence-based pilot programmes will be crucial to ensure the sustainable, safe and effective implementation of PNLC in general practice.
