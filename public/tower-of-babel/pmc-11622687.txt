A qualitative enquiry into lived experience and coping strategies of undergraduates with sickle cell disease in Nigeria
Authors: Olumide Thomas Adeleke, Yetunde Toyin Olasinde, Olufemi Ebenezer Folaranmi, Yusuf Larry Ayuba, Efeturi Agelebe, Mofoluke Modupe Rufai, Oludamola Victoria Adeleke, Oyelola Eyinade Adeoye, Aderemi Temitayo Olabode, Dolapo Emmanuel Ajala, Titilola Stella Akingbola
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSickle cell disease (SCD) is of serious public health concern. Paucity of literature exists on qualitative experiences of people with SCD, despite substantial research on the clinical signs and management of the disease. This study examined how SCD affects a range of undergraduate students’ experiences, their academic achievements, relationships, mental health and healthcare utilization.MethodsThis was an exploratory qualitative study that interviewed 22 participants selected by thematic saturation between September 2023 and February 2024. The study was conducted in Bowen University, Iwo, Osun State and Bowen University Teaching Hospital, Ogbomoso, Oyo State, annex of Bowen University, Nigeria. Thematic analysis of qualitative data was conducted.ResultsEight distinct themes emerged and were linked to the study objectives. Findings show resilience and determination of the students in navigating the complexities of living with sicknesses while pursuing their academic goals and daily activities. This underscores the need for increased community awareness, support for undergraduates living with SCD, and access to effective healthcare to better assist undergraduates with SCD in managing their health and academic responsibilities effectively.ConclusionHealth professionals and lecturers working with undergraduates with SCD should be aware of diverse coping strategies for them and prioritize integrating such into clinical conversations and support in appropriate contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20927-6.

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a significant public health problem in Nigeria. About 4–6 million of the 50 million people with SCD worldwide live in Nigeria [1]. Despite extensive research on the clinical manifestations and management of SCD, there remains a paucity of literature from the qualitative perspectives that explores the lived experiences of individuals with SCD, particularly among Nigerian undergraduate students. Understanding the unique challenges faced by this demographic group is essential for developing targeted interventions to improve their quality of life and academic outcomes.

One in every four Nigerians carries the sickle cell trait, with Nigeria being the epicenter with 4–6 million people affected. In Nigeria, SCD affects approximately 2–3% of newborns, resulting in a substantial population living with the disease [2–4]. The impact of SCD extends beyond physical health, encompassing psychosocial, emotional, and academic dimensions. Nigerian undergraduates with SCD navigate various challenges related to managing their health conditions while pursuing higher education, including frequent pain crises, hospitalizations, medication adherence, and stigmatization as well as challenges of the expectations for them to attend classes and do well in their academic pursuits [5–7].

While studies from other parts of the world have shed light on the experiences of individuals living with SCD, the Nigerian context presents unique sociocultural factors that may influence the lived experiences of undergraduate students. Cultural and religious beliefs, socioeconomic status, access to healthcare services, and educational opportunities contribute to the complexity of managing SCD in Nigeria [8].

Despite the growing recognition of the importance of patient-reported outcomes in healthcare research, there is limited qualitative exploration of the lived experiences of Nigerian undergraduate students with SCD. By employing qualitative research methods, this study aims to investigate the impact of SCD on various aspects of undergraduate life, including academic performance, religious expectations, social interactions, mental well-being, and healthcare utilization patterns [9–11]. This study seeks to delve into the experiences of Nigerian undergraduate students with SCD and aim to inform tailored interventions to address the needs of undergraduate students with SCD nationwide.

Findings may inform policy and practice aimed at enhancing the holistic care and support services available to this population.

Methods

Study design

This was an exploratory qualitative study through in-depth interview guide conducted among undergraduate students at Bowen University in Iwo, Osun State, and Bowen University Teaching Hospital in Ogbomoso, Oyo State, Nigeria from September, 2023 to February, 2024. This design is suitable for this study because it can investigate the lived experience of those with sickle cell disease. Contextual awareness, open-ended questions, and thorough explanations of the phenomenon under study are among the fundamental ideas used. In keeping with the study’s goal, open-ended questions were created to allow for this flexibility.

Study settings

This study was conducted in two locations. This was aimed at capturing a diverse range of experiences with SCD due to the nature of the research topic and the availability of participants as well as accessibility.

Selection of research setting

Bowen University Hospital in Iwo, Osun State and Bowen University Teaching Hospital in Ogbomoso, Oyo State, an annex of Bowen University served as the study’s locations. Some factors influenced the choice of these settings.

The researcher’s association with Bowen University enabled smooth admittance to these settings, enabling efficient data collection. Conducting the study in close proximity also reduced logistical difficulties.

By making use of already-existing support system and medical services, these settings reduced the possibility of disturbances to the daily schedules of participants and the workflows of healthcare professionals. This made sure that neither the activities of the support group nor the quality of care were harmed by the research.

Study population and sampling

Using purposive sampling method, consenting participants in both study locations were recruited. Those who are undergraduates and living with SCD were included while those living with SCD but not undergraduates are excluded.

Purposive sampling was used to select participants with specific characteristics, relevant to the study’s phenomenon. This deliberate sampling approach enabled targeted selection, without equal probability of selection for all population members.

A total of 22 participants were recruited with data saturation guiding the sampling process. Interviews were conducted until thematic saturation was achieved, which occurred at the 22nd participant, indicating that the sample size was sufficient to explore the research phenomenon.

Data collection

The data collection method for this study involved conducting in-depth interviews with participants. A semi-structured interview guide was developed to ensure consistency while also allowing for flexibility to delve deeper into specific topics arising from participants’ narratives [See Supplementary file]. This interview guide was subjected to expertise consideration for validation.

Each participant was provided with a detailed information as regards the study. They were explicitly informed of their right to withdraw consent at any point, without penalty. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

This study, maintained participant’s confidentiality and anonymity. The interview lasted between 45 and 60 min and the conversation was focused on lived experiences of people living with SCD. All interviews were audio-recorded and field notes were taken to support the audio recordings. These recordings are securely stored on password-protected devices.

Data analysis

This study employed inductive thematic analysis for the identification and exploration of themes directly grounded in the rich narratives shared by participants during the in-depth interviews. The transcripts generated from the audio recordings and field notes were proof-read to ensure accuracy. Codes were generated and assigned to significant segments of text within the transcripts, capturing key concepts, experiences, and emotions expressed by participants. These codes were then systematically analyzed to identify recurring patterns and relationships. Overarching themes that reflected the core elements of participants’ lived experiences with SCD were generated.

Ethical consideration

The Institutional Research Ethics guidelines and the ethical principle pertaining to human participation were followed in the conduct of the study. (i.e., Helsinki Declaration). The researcher obtained formal ethical approval from the Bowen University Research Ethical Board with reference number BUREC/COHES/FMD/004. In addition, consent form was signed by participants and those < 18years grant assent after obtaining parental consent.

Results

The study findings showed the lived experience of the participants with their friends, colleagues, family members and the challenges they have with interpersonal relationships. It also revealed the impact of the illness on the participants’ academic performance and their experience in seeking healthcare support. Despite these challenges, participants shared their experiences on use of various coping strategies. The study shed light on the resilience and determination of these students in navigating the complexities of living with SCD while pursuing their academic goals and daily activities.

Socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents

The result of this qualitative study is based on the twenty-two (22) in-depth interviews conducted among undergraduate students, aged 16 to 25 years. The study collected both male and female undergraduate students at various academic levels, ranging from 100 to 400 level, and those in their clinical posting year at the College of Health Sciences in the university. Participants’ course of study includes medicine and surgery, anatomy, public health, architecture, mass communication, and law. Majority are of Yoruba ethnicity and of Christianity and Islamic religions.

Lived experience of undergraduates living with sickle cell disease

Struggles with daily activities including academic programme demand

The participants highlighted their struggles with daily activities and physical restrictions. They expressed challenges in engaging in daily activities due to the physical and emotional toll of SCD. These struggles extend to academic demands. The unpredictable nature of crisis occurrence often disrupts their academic progress, leading to feelings of frustration and academic setbacks. Besides, being in a faith-based institution, they are expected to attend religious services. Below are some quotes supporting evidence for the theme;

“…Once I have a crisis like this, it affects me personally because I would not be able to do as I like for that moment. I will not be able to do the things I’m meant to do and all these are like personal delays to me. When I was in secondary school I wanted to join some sports, my mom had to tell me that I shouldn’t. If I’m going for NYSC (Like becoming a corps member) and I am asked to submit my medical fitness report and they see it, I will not be able to do all of these interesting things they have to do in camp because of my health.” (Participant 1).

“It makes things harder, you have to try harder as twice as other people to achieve great things. There were times that I’d miss my exam in a whole lot of academic sessions and for that reason, it was a drawback in my life… it was still okay in this school as exams are personalized for us, and sometimes I only had to miss classes.“(Participant 20).

Impact of religio-cultural constructs on their stigmatization experience

In addition, they also expressed the perceived impact of religio-cultural constructs on their stigmatization experience (from people around them) about their health condition. Some participants specifically mentioned how certain religious or cultural beliefs attributed the illness to ancestral punishments or personal faults, leading to social stigma and misconceptions. This stigmatization further exacerbated feelings of shame and isolation among individuals living with the condition. However, some participants do not think religion and culture affect their experiences.

Some religious practitioners will feel that having this disease is a punishment from your forefathers, from your ancestors, they may have done something to offend someone and like, you are the one taking the blame. While some people just neglect and then you know, start nicknaming you…. (Participant 20)

I don’t think religion has any effect on my living condition. (participant 14).

Access to healthcare Services and hospitalization experience

The participants also narrated their experience visiting hospitals and hospitalization. They discussed the challenges of managing pain crises, the need for prompt medical attention, and the impact of hospital visits (by friends, chaplains, fellowship leaders, and medics) on their overall well-being. Hospitalization was described as a necessary but often daunting aspect of living with SCD.

“Okay, there was one, maybe two or three years ago. I was on admission and the nurse thought she had the experience. I told her that I was still having pain. And she just commented that she thinks I’m addicted to pain when they were doing ward round. The doctors knew better, because I had told them and they were like, oh, obviously not. So I think that was sorted”. (Participant 15)

“Most of the doctors and nurses have been amazing people. Even in secondary school, the matrons in the clinic were very, very understanding. Whenever I had any issue, I could always go to them and they knew what to do. So, over the years, hospital visits were not something that they scheduled because somehow we always knew the right place to go”. (Participant 12)

Strained Social and interpersonal relationship

Lastly, they also expressed how their daily experience living with SCD brings about strained social and interpersonal relationships with their friends, families, and acquaintances. They were often misunderstood or neglected by peers, friends, and even healthcare providers. The stigma associated with the illness sometimes led to strained relationships, isolation, and difficulties in seeking support from others.

“I think one of the major challenges is maybe not being able to do some of the things you would want to do. Okay. Like I did when I was a child, and you get to play you know. I didn’t get to play like that as a child because my parents were always very protective, that’s one of the major challenges. .the stigma that still comes with it, the way people view you. Those are part of the challenges”. (Participant 13)

“I have never publicly opened up to anybody about my condition, like friends, or family. The only people who are aware are my parents, my siblings, my immediate family, and then my caregivers here at school. So I don’t get stigmatized, but I listen to other people who have conversations about sickle cell. And honestly, it is widely misunderstood. I attended a country school in Nigeria here with this girl, she had just SCD as well, but luckily for me, it doesn’t show on me. So, my classmates were talking about it, and they were like, she’s an attention seeker. I didn’t experience it because my dad told me, I should not let anybody know. Therefore, I don’t experience the stigmatization, but generally, I feel like this society lacks adequate knowledge”. (Participant 14)

So whenever people find out about this disease that I have, they tend to single me out from what other people do participate in. So they are always giving me the special treatment because of this. And whenever people tend to realize why exactly, then they begin to get angry. They will ask why I am getting this special treatment. Yeah, she’s faking it. She’s not sick. (Participant 20)

Coping strategies

The participants shared their strategies of embracing improved self-care practices, relying on robust social and healthcare support systems, relying on prayers and spirituality, and building resilience and adaptation skills in the face of SCD. These themes highlight the importance of holistic care approaches that encompass physical, emotional, and social well-being, emphasizing the significance of self-empowerment, community support, and personal growth in enhancing the quality of life for individuals living with SCD.

Improved self-care practice

Participants highlighted the importance of implementing and maintaining improved self-care practices to manage their condition effectively. They discussed adopting healthier lifestyle choices, such as regular exercise, balanced nutrition, adequate hydration, and adherence to medication regimens. By prioritizing self-care, participants aimed to minimize the frequency and severity of pain crises, enhance their overall well-being, and take proactive steps to manage their health more effectively.

“I have to like take my drugs. Taking my drugs because this thing is not magic. You just have to do it. So take your drugs and a lot of water. Okay, when it comes to water intake. I’m very good at it.” (Participant 2).

“I drink a lot of water, take care to avoid stress, and also stay away from cold, like too much cold because cold triggers joint pains”. (Participant 16)

“Every day I take my routine daily medications. My mom always tells me when I’m ranting about stress she doesn’t want me to break down. She’s always hammering that in my ears. If I get overwhelmed, I have my painkiller with me although my dad said I should stop consuming that thing. That I should just be drinking water. I take a lot of water. Yes, the water is very helpful. Very effective.” (Participant 14).

Reliance on social and healthcare support systems

Participants emphasized the crucial role of social and healthcare support systems in their journey with SCD. They expressed gratitude for the unwavering support and understanding received from family members, friends, and healthcare providers. The presence of a strong support network provided emotional encouragement, practical assistance during health crises, and a sense of belonging and acceptance, which were instrumental in navigating the challenges posed by the illness.

“I want to thank God for my mom. I’m really grateful to my parents. They know the way they care of me. I’m having crisis with so much love and care and you know, prayers and other kinds of stuff. At home if I’m hospitalized, like they don’t leave me for a night without anybody. That’s how much they actually care about me. Especially my mom runs around and so I feel that they take care of me well. They manage my crisis well at home” (Participant 16).

Build-up resilience and adaptation

Participants shared stories of resilience and adaptation in the face of adversity brought about by SCD. They cultivate positive mindset and embrace proactive approach to managing setbacks and challenges. They engage in self-reflection that fosters a sense of purpose and determination which makes them emerge stronger.

“Don’t make it obvious to people that something is wrong with you. If you make it obvious to people that you are in pain, they think you are helpless. That is just the mentality”. (Participant 10)

“… I think as you grow, you get used to the restrictions, but like as a child, you know you might not understand what you’re living with, so you do not understand what restrictions that you’re placed on. So as you grow older, you understand what you’re living with. And these are the kinds of things you can do the things you can’t do”. (Participant 13)

The role of prayer and spirituality

The study participants utilized religious activities primarily prayers as useful sources of hope and effective strategies to cope with their condition. Some of them even connected their religious beliefs and prayers with the effectiveness of their medications.

“Prayer pays and has been very effective and helpful for me. My drugs have been too but without prayer, they won’t work. Like sometimes, I wouldn’t actually take the drugs but I’ll just pray to God, talk to God, and just sleep”. (Participant 16)

Discussion

Experiences of living with SCD

The study findings reveal the lived experience of these participants which explains their struggles with daily activities and physical restrictions, social stigma, and misconceptions about the disease which lead to challenges in interpersonal relationships. Though the study has revealed that the influence of the illness on the participants’ academic performance is substantial, with missed classes and or exams due to health crises, they have managed to make progress due to determination and support of friends and family members. Despite challenges, participants shared various coping strategies, including staying hydrated, being cautious to prevent crises, and seeking support from family and friends.

The study participants bemoaned restrictions in participating in normal physical activities, including sport and limitations of normal daily activities. This could serve as a painful reminder of their condition. This finding has been earlier reported to aggravate the negative feelings of isolation and frustration of young adults living with SCD [12–14].

Similarly, our study highlighted the negative academic impact of SCD such as missing classes and examinations. This finding confirms the reports of other similar studies [15–17]. However, it is noteworthy that the school system can support this population of undergraduates by allowing them to participate in missed examinations and other academic activities at a more convenient time.

Furthermore, the participants engaged in an excruciating analysis of the impact of leaving with pain and the sociocultural misperceptions of many about their condition. The stigma associated with living with SCD could lead to strained relationships, isolation, and difficulties in seeking support from other friends and family members. The social disadvantages of stigma such as negative reactions from family/friends, co-workers, peers, and community members regarding SCD status have been well-documented in literature [18]. Some undergraduates with this condition chose not to disclose their sickle cell status in a bid to avoid stigma. Some participants believed that stigma is largely due to a lack of adequate knowledge of SCD in society. This further confirms the earlier report that when the general populace is not knowledgeable about SCD, people subsequently form their own negative opinions about people living with SCD [19]. Therefore, there is a need to create more awareness about SCD to reduce stigmatization.

Our study also detailed hospital visits experiences of undergraduates with SCD. Overall, the experience was mixed. While some described the healthcare providers’ attitude as encouraging, others narrated their ordeal of health-related stigma, especially about pain management with opioids. The health-related stigma associated with SCD has been reported in several studies [20–22]. There is an urgent need to continuously educate the health care providers on the need to be aware of this bias and to constantly avoid it.

Coping strategies

Participants emphasized the significance of employing and sustaining better-quality self-care practices to achieve effective management of the ailment. They support lifestyle options, including adequate hydration, regular exercise, adequate nutrition, and cogent observance of medication routines. Dehydration is a well-known trigger for painful crises. It makes blood more viscous and consequently increases the risk of painful vaso-occlusive crisis [23]. The practice of staying well hydrated prevents crises and thus a useful coping strategy.

Participants also embraced religious practice primarily prayers as an effective coping strategy. Religious and spiritual practices have been considered to be effective coping strategies for many adults with chronic illness [24]. This has been particularly found to be helpful in patients living with SCD, according to the findings of a study conducted in Nigeria [25]. Many children, adolescents, adults and caregivers of children with SCD reported using prayer and other religious practices to manage their SCD in several studies [26–28]. This aligns perfectly well with Karl Marx’s interpretation of religion as “the opium of the people.” However, this contrasted with the findings of a study by Cotton et al. in which more than one-third of the study participants questioned God’s love for them [26]. Healthcare professionals, families, friends, and lecturers working with undergraduates with SCD should be conscious of the import of this religious practice as a coping strategy for these individuals and should prioritize integrating it into clinical conversations of coping and support in appropriate contexts.

Importantly, within a religious-cultural context that the university community provides among the participants, empathy encouraged by religious backing appears to be reassuring. Roommates, course mates, fellowship brethren, and staff are readily available as sources of positive and physical presence to serve as sources of inspiration for persons living with SCD. In listing self-care, it is very clear that participants adopt practical personal actions in managing their health efficiently. Given the antecedent and inherent socio-cultural and religious-cultural challenges of wrong ideological perceptions, the actions of the participants appear a complex survival strategy to the pervasive cultural explanations of SCD.

Conclusion and recommendation

This study revealed that the lived experience of undergraduates with SCD ranges from struggles with activities of daily living especially academic demand, experience of stigmatization, frequency in hospitalization and strained social and interpersonal relationship. However, some of the coping strategies employed include improved self-care practices, reliance on social and healthcare support systems, the need to build-up resilience and adapt to the situation and resort to prayers.

Pursuant to the study’s findings, more community awareness, support, and access to quality healthcare are necessary to help undergraduate students with sickle cell disease (SCD) efficiently manage their health and academic obligations.

Strength of the study

The lived experiences of undergraduate students with sickle cell disease (SCD) in a Nigerian university setting are illuminated by this study. It tackles the dearth of studies on SCD experiences among university students in Nigeria. Additionally, it improves comprehension of SCD-afflicted undergraduates’ sickness narratives, coping mechanisms, and resilience. This study also increases knowledge about SCD, its consequences, and the value of support system. Additionally, it provides information about the needs of students with SCD to university administrators and policy makers.

Limitation of the study

This study acknowledges it limitation. There may be selection bias introduced by purposeful sampling. The study’s conclusion might also not apply to other study settings in Nigerian or other countries.

The study was conducted in a predominantly Christian Yoruba community. This makes the voice of other ethnic and religious groups not well represented.
