Exploratory CT-Based Assessment of Emphysema Burden in Smokers with Bipolar Disorder Receiving Lithium Treatment
Authors: Şaban Melih Şimşek, Meltem Hazel Şimşek, Ruhsel Cörüt, Fatma Gül Helvacı Çelik, Gökhan Tonkaz, Abdulkadir Kaya, Selçuk Takır
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeExperimental studies suggest that lithium may modulate emphysema-related tissue injury through activation of the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway. However, clinical data examining the relationship between lithium exposure and emphysema burden remain limited. This study aimed to investigate the association between long-term lithium use and CT-derived emphysema burden in smokers with bipolar disorder.Patients and MethodsThis retrospective observational study included 67 smokers with bipolar disorder who had received lithium treatment for at least 5 years and 71 matched controls with similar demographic and smoking characteristics. Quantitative emphysema measurements were obtained from thoracic computed tomography using low attenuation area percentage (LAA%). Multivariable linear regression analyses were performed adjusting for age, sex, BMI, and smoking exposure.ResultsRight, left, and total lung LAA% values were lower in the lithium-treated group than in controls (7.53% vs 10.11%, p=0.009; 7.73% vs 10.02%, p=0.019; and 7.64% vs 10.07%, p=0.011, respectively). In multivariable analysis, lithium treatment remained independently associated with lower total LAA% after adjustment for age, sex, body mass index, and smoking exposure (B = −2.636, standardized β = −0.236, 95% CI −4.537 to −0.736; p = 0.007). Weak inverse correlations between serum lithium concentrations and emphysema measurements were observed in unadjusted analyses but were no longer significant after multivariable adjustment.ConclusionLong-term lithium exposure was associated with lower CT-derived emphysema burden in smokers with bipolar disorder. Given the observational design and potential influence of inspiratory effort during CT acquisition, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of a protective effect. Prospective longitudinal and mechanistic studies are warranted to clarify the relationship between lithium exposure and smoking-related parenchymal lung injury.

Introduction

Emphysema is defined as the destruction of lung parenchyma due to various exogenous and endogenous exposures, mainly smoking and biomass. Development of emphysema in the lungs is known to be linked to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), a disease characterized by irreversible airflow obstruction and functional loss of lung tissue. COPD is a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide and is associated with substantial economic burden and functional impairment.1

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a mood disorder characterized by manic and depressive episodes.2 Smoking in patients with bipolar disorder is 45–70% and corresponds to 2–3 times that of the general population.3 COPD, for which smoking is the leading risk factor, is more common in individuals with bipolar disorder than in the general population.4,5 A meta-analysis reported that individuals with BD had approximately 55% higher odds of COPD than the general population.4 In addition, BD patients who smoke can be diagnosed with COPD at much more advanced stages.6 These findings underscore the importance of research aimed at detecting lung diseases at an earlier stage, with a particular focus on structural changes in this patient group.

Lithium is used to prevent mania episodes, reduce manic attacks, and reduce the risk of suicide in bipolar disorder.7 In a prospective study of 50,000 patients diagnosed with bipolar disorder, it was shown that those who used lithium had 28% lower respiratory infections.8 Experimental studies have suggested that lithium may modulate pulmonary inflammatory and fibrotic processes.9 In addition, emerging human observational evidence has reported an association between lithium exposure and lower CT-derived emphysema burden; however, independent clinical data remain limited.10

The WNT/ß-catenin pathway is a signaling pathway that regulates tissue development, healing, and remodeling.11,12 Studies have shown that the WNT/ß-catenin pathway is inhibited by smoking in patients diagnosed with COPD.13,14 Experimental studies have shown that lithium may activate the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway.14,15 Experimental studies have suggested that activation of this pathway may attenuate emphysematous changes.14 Additionally, some studies suggest that lithium has anti-inflammatory effects.16,17 Based on these experimental observations and emerging human evidence, we hypothesized that long-term lithium exposure would be associated with a lower CT-derived emphysema burden in smokers with bipolar disorder. Accordingly, this study aimed to investigate the association between chronic lithium use and quantitative CT-derived emphysema measurements in this population.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

This study was designed as a retrospective exploratory observational study. According to calculations made using power analysis (G*Power), when the type 1 error was 5%, the power of the study was 80%, and the effect size was moderate (0.5), the minimum sample size for both groups was determined to be 51 people. Since no similar study was found in the literature, the effect size was considered to be moderate (0.5). For our study, permission was obtained from the scientific research ethics committee of our institution with the decision number “19.03.2025/13”. The present study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The current study is not a clinical trial. According to the Regulation on Clinical Researches (Official Gazette of the Republic of Turkey, No. 28617, 13 April 2013), informed consent is not required for retrospective studies using anonymized data.

The study group consisted of patients with bipolar disorder who had been followed up at the Community Mental Health Center for approximately 10 years and had received lithium treatment for at least 5 years. Early structural and functional changes associated with smoking generally become more pronounced in smokers of 5 pack-years or more.1 To ensure chronic exposure, only patients receiving lithium treatment for at least 5 years were included. All patients received oral lithium carbonate therapy. Data on participants’ age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), place of residence, smoking history (pack-years), and thoracic computed tomography (CT) records were collected from the medical records. Chest CT images were independently evaluated for emphysematous changes by two blinded radiologists. CT scans with severe motion artifacts or inadequate image quality for quantitative assessment were excluded based on radiologist evaluation. The date of the CT examination was used as the reference date for determining cumulative smoking exposure, mean serum lithium concentration during the preceding year, and duration of lithium treatment in order to minimize temporal misclassification. Serum lithium concentrations were routinely monitored during follow-up according to standard clinical practice, and the mean value obtained during the year preceding CT acquisition was used for analysis. Controls were individually selected to achieve comparable distributions of age, sex, BMI, and smoking exposure.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

Being between the ages of 18 and 60, having a smoking history of at least five pack-years, and having undergone a chest CT examination within the previous 2 years in the Hospital Information Management System (HIMS).

Exclusion Criteria

History of pulmonary conditions that may independently cause emphysematous or destructive lung changes, including tuberculosis, bronchiectasis, childhood lower respiratory tract infections, pneumoconiosis, α1-antitrypsin deficiency, or occupational exposure associated with emphysema (eg, pneumoconiosis or other relevant dust exposure). CT scans with severe motion artefacts, inadequate inspiratory effort, or insufficient image quality for quantitative analysis were excluded.

3D Slicer 5.8.1 Software and Computed Tomography Devices

Lung segmentation and quantitative measurements were independently performed by two blinded thoracic radiologists using 3D Slicer version 5.8.1. Lung parenchymal attenuation was categorized according to predefined Hounsfield unit (HU) thresholds.18 Quantitative emphysema burden was expressed as the percentage of lung voxels with attenuation values below −950 HU (LAA%), calculated by dividing the emphysema volume by the total lung volume, a widely accepted approach for CT-based emphysema quantification.18 Computed tomography examinations were carried out using the GE Revolution EVO 128-slice scanner (model year 2020).

Primary, Secondary Outcomes

The primary outcome was the difference in CT-derived emphysema burden (LAA%) between lithium-treated patients and matched controls. Secondary outcomes included lung volume measurements and the association between serum lithium concentrations and emphysema measurements.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Descriptive statistics of numerical data are given as mean±standard deviation (SD) if the data are normally distributed, and median (min-max) if the data are generally not distributed. Descriptive statistics of the obtained categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages (%). When the numerical data were compared between the two independent groups, Student’s t-test was used for the normally distributed data, and the Mann–Whitney U-test was used for the data that were not normally distributed.

Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). Multivariable linear regression analyses were performed to evaluate the independent association between lithium exposure (lithium-treated vs control) and emphysema measurements after adjustment for potential confounders, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and smoking exposure (pack-years). Regression coefficients (B), standardized beta coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p values were reported. The statistical significance level was set at P < 0.05.

The reporting of this study was prepared per the recommendations of the “Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE)” guideline.19

Discussion

This study demonstrated a lower CT-derived emphysema burden (LAA%) in smokers with bipolar disorder receiving long-term lithium treatment than in matched controls. This association remained significant after adjustment for age, sex, body mass index, and smoking exposure. Although weak inverse correlations were observed between serum lithium concentrations and LAA% in unadjusted analyses, these associations did not remain significant after multivariable adjustment.

The findings of our study suggest that lithium exposure may be associated with lower smoking-related parenchymal lung injury. Studies have examined the protective effect of lithium on parenchymal tissue damage in several aspects. One of these is that lithium has been associated with reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, as well as oxidative stressors, and increasing the antioxidant effect.16 Another possible effect of lithium is that it increases the proliferation of mesenchymal stem cells and promotes tissue regeneration.20,21 These experimental findings provide biological plausibility for a possible association between lithium exposure and emphysema burden observed in clinical settings.22,23 In addition, studies have shown that the use of microdose lithium has anti-aging effects.17,24 Additionally, lithium exhibits positive effects on tissue organization and scar formation during wound healing.25 Consequently, these properties may constitute a mechanism of action that limits parenchymal damage in chronic inflammatory diseases such as COPD. Although weak inverse correlations between serum lithium concentrations and left lung and total LAA% were observed in the unadjusted analyses, these associations should be interpreted cautiously because they did not remain significant after multivariable adjustment. In addition, multiple correlation analyses were performed, increasing the possibility of chance findings. Therefore, these results should be considered exploratory rather than supportive of a biological dose–response relationship.

Reduced activation of the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway has been implicated in the development of emphysema. Decreased activity of this pathway contributes to tissue destruction and impaired repair capacity within the lung parenchyma.14,26

In particular, smoking reduces the signaling activity of the WNT/β-catenin pathway, thereby contributing to the increase in inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β) and the development of emphysema. This process is regulated via the PPARδ/p38 MAPK pathway.13,27,28 Experimental studies suggest that lithium may activate the WNT/β-catenin pathway and modulate emphysema-related tissue remodeling processes.14,15,29 Lithium inhibits GSK-3β, which is the key negative regulator of the WNT/β-catenin pathway. This allows β-catenin to accumulate in the cell and activate the target genes. Thus, lithium produces results similar to those typically made by the WNT signal in the cell.30 It has been experimentally demonstrated that agents other than lithium may be protective against emphysema through the WNT/ß-catenin pathway.29,31

Clinical evidence regarding the association between lithium exposure and emphysema remains limited. A recent large-scale exploratory analysis based on the COPDGene cohort also reported lower CT-derived emphysema burden among lithium users.10,32 In the present study, both right and left lung LAA% values were significantly lower in the lithium-treated group, consistent with these previous observations. However, the COPDGene findings are currently available as a conference abstract and an unpublished preprint; therefore, direct comparisons should be interpreted with caution. Overall, our findings provide additional clinical evidence supporting a potential association between lithium exposure and lower CT-derived emphysema burden, although the available evidence remains observational and does not establish a causal or protective effect.

Although weak inverse correlations between serum lithium concentrations and emphysema measurements were observed in the unadjusted analyses, these associations did not remain significant after multivariable adjustment. This finding suggests that serum lithium concentrations measured over a limited period may not adequately reflect cumulative biological exposure. Furthermore, the absence of an independent association between serum lithium concentration or treatment duration and CT-derived emphysema burden argues against a clear dose–response relationship in this cohort. These findings should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of causality. Prospective longitudinal studies with repeated lithium measurements are needed to further clarify this relationship. Although emerging human evidence is now available, clinical data examining the association between lithium exposure and emphysema remain limited. Our findings provide additional independent clinical evidence supporting this evolving body of literature.

Lithium is a molecule with a narrow therapeutic range. Lithium-related adverse effects primarily involve the renal and cardiovascular systems.7 Research on the detection of pulmonary side effects primarily focuses on inhalation-related toxicity studies associated with industrial exposure. In these studies, it was reported that toxicity is attributed mainly to other metals in battery components.33,34 Experimental studies investigating inhaled or high-dose lithium have reported pulmonary toxicity under non-therapeutic exposure conditions.35–37 These studies were mainly conducted to determine the therapeutic range of lithium, and the effects seen were also detected at high doses. Pulmonary toxicity associated with therapeutic lithium use in humans has been reported only rarely, primarily in isolated case reports, and is not supported by robust epidemiological or experimental evidence.38 In our cohort, serum lithium concentrations remained within the therapeutic range, and no clinically apparent pulmonary adverse effects were observed.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. First, because of the retrospective observational design, causal relationships cannot be established. In addition, thoracic CT scans were obtained for clinical purposes, and variability in inspiratory effort and imaging conditions may have limited the standardization of quantitative measurements. Although scans considered unsuitable for evaluation were excluded, differences in inspiratory level during CT acquisition may still have influenced LAA% measurements. Therefore, volume ratios (LAA%) were primarily used to assess emphysema.

The study was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Although the control group was matched for major demographic and smoking-related variables, confounding by indication cannot be excluded, as treatment allocation was not randomized and patients receiving long-term lithium therapy may differ from controls in ways that were not fully captured by the available clinical variables. Furthermore, residual confounding related to psychiatric background, healthcare utilization, changes in smoking behaviour over time, and other unmeasured clinical factors cannot be completely excluded. Additionally, pulmonary function test data were not available for all participants; therefore, functional correlations with CT-derived emphysema measurements could not be evaluated. Finally, serum lithium concentrations were calculated from measurements obtained during the year preceding CT acquisition and may not accurately reflect cumulative long-term lithium exposure.

Conclusions

In this retrospective observational study, long-term lithium use was associated with lower CT-derived emphysema burden in smokers with bipolar disorder. Although weak inverse correlations between serum lithium concentrations and emphysema measurements were observed in unadjusted analyses, these associations did not remain significant after multivariable adjustment.

Experimental studies suggesting anti-inflammatory and WNT/β-catenin–modulating effects of lithium may provide biological plausibility for these findings; however, causal inference remains limited due to the observational design of the study.

Overall, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and support the need for prospective and mechanistic studies investigating the relationship between lithium exposure and smoking-related parenchymal lung injury. They should not be interpreted as evidence supporting the clinical use of lithium for the prevention or treatment of emphysema.
