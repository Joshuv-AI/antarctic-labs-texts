Inflammatory effects of commercial and natural mosquito repellents on respiratory system in male and female mice
Authors: Qurat U Ain, Zilli Huma, Habiba Rashid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study evaluated and compared the inflammatory impacts of different commercial (mosquito coil and liquid vaporizer) and natural (eucalyptus) mosquito repellents on the respiratory systems of male and female mice.MethodsMale and female mice were exposed daily to mosquito coil smoke, liquid vaporizer fumes, or eucalyptus leaf fumes for 6 h in a ventilated closed room over a 45-day period. Lung tissues were collected for histological analysis to assess structural changes. Levels of the inflammatory marker tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) were assessed immunohistochemically, and interleukin 6 (IL-6) concentrations were quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) in lung tissue samples.ResultsExposure to mosquito coils and eucalyptus leaves induced significant inflammatory responses in the lung tissues of both male and female mice, as indicated by elevated TNF-α and IL-6 levels (p < 0.05), and notable histopathological alterations (p < 0.05). By contrast, exposure to liquid vaporizers only resulted in mild inflammatory changes with comparatively lower levels of inflammatory markers. No significant gender differences in inflammatory responses were observed.ConclusionSmoke-producing mosquito repellents, particularly mosquito coils and eucalyptus leaves, produced marked inflammatory effects and histological lung damage in mice, irrespective of sex. Liquid vaporizers had minimal respiratory impacts, suggesting that they are a safer alternative. These findings highlight the urgent need to develop and promote less harmful mosquito repellent options to mitigate the respiratory health risks associated with currently popular products.Keywords: Eucalyptus, Mice, Mosquito repellants, Respiratory epithelium, Respiratory system

Introduction

Due to global warming and changing environmental conditions, the mosquito population is expanding throughout the world.1 Mosquitoes are vectors of various deadly illnesses such as dengue, malaria, yellow fever, and West Nile virus.2 Mosquito breeding is greater in overpopulated regions and a major problem in subtropical regions.3 Commercial and natural mosquito repellents are used for protection against mosquitoes. In many rural regions, the leaves, stem, or seeds of different plants such as Leucaena leucocephala, Gliricidia sepium, and Eucalyptus globulus (eucalyptus) are used to repel mosquitoes.4 In Eucalyptus globulus plants, the insect repellent constituent is eucalyptol and it is released during the combustion of eucalyptus leaves.5

Pyrethroid compounds are used as the major insect repelling chemicals in commercial mosquito repellents such as mosquito coils, sprays, and liquid vaporizers.1,6 These pyrethroid compounds affect the central and peripheral nervous systems of mosquitoes by acting on voltage gated sodium channels, causing paralysis.7

Multiple polyaromatic hydrocarbons and aldehydes are used as binders in mosquito coil disks and chloroform is applied as a solvent in liquid vaporizers.2,8 Burning mosquito coils releases smoke and leads to environmental pollution, thereby yielding indoor pollutants with damaging effects on human health9,10 Contact with chemicals released by burning these mosquito repellents leads to irritation of the eyes, nose, and throat in humans, as well as some behavioral changes.6 Idowu et al. and Abiodun et al., showed that long-term exposure to mosquito repellent chemicals induced apoptosis and necrotic changes in liver tissues.3,11

Some studies have reported gender dimorphism in terms of the pathogenesis of lung inflammation as a consequence of certain insults.12 In particular, younger male children are more susceptible to obstructive disease of the lungs, probably because of the smaller diameter of their airways and relatively smaller lung size compared with female children. However, the pattern of obstructive respiratory disease changes at puberty, where females are then affected more than males. This change in the prevalence of the disease may be related to sex hormones.13

Many studies have shown the toxicological effects of mosquito repellants on various organs, but few have compared inflammatory changes in the lungs after exposure to mosquito coils, aerosolized mosquito repellants, and natural mosquito repellants. Moreover, little is known about gender related variations in inflammatory responses caused by these mosquito repellants. The main aims of this study were to compare the toxicological and gender specific effects of different mosquito repellents on the respiratory systems in male and female mice.

Materials and Methods

This study was approved by the Advanced Study and Research Board (ASRB) of Khyber Medical University (DIR/KMU-AS&RB/CA/160).

Animals

Sixty-four adult male and female Balb/C albino mice aged three months were purchased from the Veterinary Research Institute at Peshawar. Experiments were conducted in the animal house at KMU under strict compliance with the guidelines provided by the National Institutes of Health guide for the care and use of laboratory animals (NIH Publications No. 8023, revised 1978). The mice were acclimatized for two weeks before the experiments started. Male and female mice were kept in separate plastic cages topped with iron gauze (dimensions of 35.5 × 20 ×187 cm). Eight mice were kept in each cage and wood chippings were provided as bedding. Mice were kept at 25 ± 2 °C under a natural light:dark cycle of 14 h:10 h, with free access to food and water.

Chemicals

The chemical repellents tested in this study were: Mortein mosquito coils and Mortein liquid vaporizers purchased from a local market in Peshawar. Eucalyptus globulus leaves were purchased from All Season Nursery farm, Jamrud Road. Peshawar. Anti-tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) antibody (clone 52B83) (ab1793), goat anti-mouse IgG + IgM (horseradish peroxidase (HRP)) antibody (ab47827), and 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kits (ab64238) were purchased from Abcam. Interleukin 6 (IL-6) enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (ELK1157) were purchased from ELK Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Wuhan.

Experimental procedure

This experimental setup was designed to simulate the traditional use of mosquito repellents in certain rural and urban regions. To compare the different types of mosquito repellants, mice were randomly assigned to groups of four males and four females. Mice in the treatment groups were exposed to mosquito repellents, i.e., mosquito coils, liquid vaporizers, and eucalyptus leaves, in a closed room with appropriate ventilation. Animals were exposed for 6 h each day (9:00 am to 3:00 pm) for 45 days. The concentration of particulate matter (PM2.5) emitted by each mosquito repellent was measured using a HAZ dust particulate air monitor (Model: EPAM 5000). The PM2.5 concentrations emitted by the repellents were 201 μg/m3 by mosquito coils and 93 μg/m3 by liquid vaporizers, and 211 μg/m3 after burning eucalyptus leaves for 6 h in a closed room with appropriate ventilation.

Control mice were not exposed to any of the repellents. After completing the experiment, mice were sacrificed under deep anesthesia and both lungs were harvested to assess inflammation based on biochemical and histological analyses.

Histological analysis

Histological sections were prepared with immediate fixation in 10% formalin to preserve cellular structures and prevent degradation, before processing according to a series of steps involving dehydration using increasing concentrations of ethanol, clearing with xylene as a solvent, and infiltration with molten paraffin wax, thereby removing all water and replacing with embedding medium. The tissue was then embedded in a solid wax block while ensuring correct orientation. The hardened block was sectioned using a microtome to prepare thin slices measuring 5 μm, which were floated on a warm water bath to flatten them, before carefully transferring to glass slides.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining

The tray of prepared slides was placed for 2 min in xylene to remove the wax, and this was repeated 2–3 times. The slides were rehydrated by dipping in decreasing concentrations of alcohol (100%, 90%, 80%, and 70%), washed with tap water, and stained with hematoxylin. The slides were washed again with tap water and immersed in 1% acid alcohol, before washing with tap water and passing through 1% ammonia, washing again, and staining with eosin for 1 min. The slides were washed with running tap water and dehydrated by immersing in increasing concentrations of alcohol (80%, 90%, and 100%), passing through xylene to clear them, and drying. The slides were mounted with distyrene, plasticizer, and xylene (DPX), and cover slips, before keeping for 24 h to allow drying and adhesion. Statistical analysis

All results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted followed by Tukey's post hoc test for posttest multiple comparisons using JASP software. A p-value <0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

Mosquito repellent exposure induced histological changes in lung tissues

Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed to detect any structural changes in the lungs of mice after exposure to mosquito repellents (Figure 1). Two-way ANOVA detected a significant main effect of treatment on the hematoxylin and eosin staining scores (F(3,24) = 19.968, p < 0.001; Figure 1A), indicating differences among the treatment groups. However, the main effect of gender (F(1,24) = 0.871, p = 0.360) and the treatment × gender interaction (F(3,24) = 0.355, p = 0.786) were not significant, suggesting comparable responses in males and females.

Tukey's post hoc tests showed that the hematoxylin and eosin staining scores were significantly higher in the eucalyptus and mosquito coil treatment groups compared with the control in both sexes (p < 0.05), whereas the results for the liquid vaporizer group did not differ significantly from the control (p > 0.05). No significant difference was observed in the hematoxylin and eosin staining scores between the eucalyptus and mosquito coil treatment groups. A significant difference in the hematoxylin and eosin staining scores was observed only in males in the liquid vaporizer and mosquito coil treatment groups (p = 0.020). Overall, the hematoxylin and eosin staining scores were highest for the mosquito coil treatment group, followed by the eucalyptus group, but there was only a modest increase in the liquid vaporizer group, where the trends were similar in both genders.

Mosquito repellent exposure increased TNF-α levels in lung tissue

The TNF-α levels in lung tissues were detected by immunohistochemistry (Figure 2). Two-way ANOVA indicated a significant main effect of treatment on the TNF-α levels (F(3,24) = 19.247, p < 0.001; Figure 2A), but the main effects of gender (F(1,24) = 0.275, p = 0.605) and the treatment × gender interaction (F (3,24) = 2.441, p = 0.089) were not statistically significant.

Tukey's post hoc tests demonstrated that the TNF-α levels were significantly higher in the mosquito coil treatment group compared with the control and liquid vaporizer groups (p < 0.05). The levels were also significantly higher in the eucalyptus treatment group compared with control (p < 0.05). The TNF-α levels did not differ significantly between the control and liquid vaporizer groups.

Mosquito repellent exposure increased IL-6 levels

Two-way ANOVA detected a significant main effect of treatment on the IL-6 levels (F (3,24) = 137.12, p < 0.001; Figure 3), but the main effect of gender was not significant (F(1,24) = 0.174, p = 0.680). However, a significant treatment × gender interaction was observed (F(3,24) = 4.852, p = 0.009), indicating sex-dependent variation in the treatment responses.

Effects of exposure to mosquito repellents on IL-6 levels. Graph showing IL-6 concentrations in male mice and female mice. Two-way ANOVA indicated significant effects of treatment and the treatment × gender interaction, but no significant effect of gender. Different letters (a–c) indicate significant differences between groups (Tukey's post hoc test, p < 0.05).

Post hoc Tukey's tests showed that eucalyptus significantly increased the IL-6 levels compared with the control in both females and males (p < 0.001). Liquid vaporizer treatment significantly reduced the IL-6 levels compared with eucalyptus in both sexes (p < 0.001). Mosquito coil treatment resulted in higher IL-6 levels than liquid vaporizer treatment (p ≤ 0.006) and the levels were significantly elevated compared with the control (p < 0.001). Overall, the IL-6 levels were highest in the eucalyptus treatment group, lowest in the control group, and intermediate in the liquid vaporizer and mosquito coil groups, with slightly greater treatment effects observed in males.

Discussion

Mosquito repellents are widely used for protection from mosquito bites. These repellents are mostly based on pyrethroids and are known to be harmful.17 The most common exposure route is via inhalation. Previous studies have shown that exposure to commercial mosquito repellents causes inflammatory changes in lung tissues.12 However, previous studies have not compared the effects of natural combustion-derived smoke from eucalyptus leaves and commercial mosquito repellents on histological changes and inflammation in the lung tissues of mice. Thus, in the present study, we evaluated the respiratory effects of combustion-derived emissions from eucalyptus leaves, a plant material that is traditionally used in some settings as a smoke-based mosquito deterrent.

Mosquito coils usually contain pyrethroids and other chemicals that help the coils to burn. Pyrethroids are released after burning mosquito coils, but also heavy metals such as lead and chromium. These heavy metals and other ultrafine particles tend to accumulate in pulmonary tissues and initiate the inflammatory process.18 Burning eucalyptus leaves produces smoke containing particulate matter, and the smoke released by combustion contains levoglucason, which is the end-product formed by the thermal degradation of cellulose. In addition, some toxic compounds in eucalyptus smoke are responsible for toxic effects on pulmonary tissue.19 The smoke generated by burning mosquito coils and eucalyptus leaves consists of a gas phase and particle phase. The gas phase contains carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, polyaromatic hydrocarbons and volatile organic compounds, and the particle phase consists of particulate matter.20

The damaging effects of smoke on lung tissue are evident in the form of alveolar wall thickening, neutrophilic infiltration, necrosis of the cell linings of alveoli, and congestion of alveolar blood vessels.20 The smoke produced by burning mosquito coils and eucalyptus leaves also induced structural changes in the lung tissues of both male and female mice in the present study. These structural alterations included increased cellular infiltration and alveolar septal wall thickening.

Various types of gases and particulate matter in smoke are responsible for inflammatory reactions in the body. The inhaled gases and particles in smoke reduce the antioxidant defense response of lungs, thereby increasing their ability to induce inflammation.21 These particles directly affect lung epithelial cells.22

Umar et al. stated that estrogen can protect females against inflammation.23 Therefore, in our study, we expected milder inflammation in females due to the presence of estrogen. However, we found increases in the TNF-α and IL-6 levels in both male and female mice after exposure to burning mosquito coils and eucalyptus leaves, whereas exposure to the liquid vaporizer caused only mild inflammatory changes. Our findings agree with those obtained by Blacquière et al. who demonstrated that there was no difference in the airway remodeling caused by macrophages in both genders.24

TNF-α regulates the inflammatory process and is released by macrophages and lymphocytes in response to stimuli such as particles and gases in smoke. TNF-α then binds to its specific receptors, i.e., TNFR1 and TNFR2, leading to the transmission of molecular signals to activate inflammation. The interaction of TNF-α with its receptors results in the activation of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and the mitogen-activated protein pathway.25

NF-κB is an important mediator of pro-inflammatory gene induction. NF-κB is activated by inflammatory stimuli in smoke and it then binds to specific cell membrane receptors, resulting in the activation of signaling pathways.26 After translocation into the nucleus, NF-κB binds to specific DNA elements and induces target genes such as IL-6.27 In pulmonary tissue, IL-6 is known to be involved in the initiation and continuation of the inflammatory process. IL-6 initiates inflammation by converting monocytes into macrophages, which then release further chemokines, leukotrienes, and histamines. In addition, IL-6 can activate mononuclear cells in blood to release monocyte chemoattractant protein. This chemokine further propagates the inflammatory process by increasing the synthesis of reactive oxygen species.28

Our results showed that inhaling smoke produced by burning pyrethroid-containing mosquito repellents and eucalyptus had deleterious local effects on lung tissue, whereas the liquid vaporizer caused only mild changes in inflammatory markers. Our results also showed that males and females were affected almost equally by the toxic chemicals. Absolute dose quantification may be refined in future studies, but the internal validity of the treatment comparisons in the present study remains robust. We compared different household mosquito repellents but the main limitation of the study design was investigating the effects of natural mosquito repellents by directly burning eucalyptus leaves, and thus the health effects of the natural mosquito repellents were masked by the smoke produced. Another limitation was that the mass concentration of PM2.5 was used because detailed aerosol spectrometry was unavailable.

Conclusion

According to our results, we conclude that the chronic use of both commercial and natural mosquito repellents caused inflammatory changes in the lungs of mice. Thus, we suggest that humans should minimize their exposure to mosquito repellents and only apply these repellents in well-ventilated spaces. Future studies should be conducted to explore the toxicological effects of smoke on the respiratory systems of children and antenatal females. These studies will help people to protect themselves from the harmful effects of smoke generated by mosquito repellents.
