Reclassification of the first Bacillus tropicus phage calls for reclassification of other Bacillus temperate phages previously designated as plasmids
Authors: Ridwaan Nazeer Milase, Johnson Lin, Nontobeko E Mvubu, Nokulunga Hlengwa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacillus tropicus is a recently identified subspecies of the Bacillus cereus group of bacteria that have been shown to possess genes associated with antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and identified as the causative agent for anthrax-like disease in Chinese soft-shelled turtles. In addition, B. tropicus has demonstrated great potential in the fields of bioremediation and bioconversion. This article describes the comparative genomics of a Bacillus phage vB_Btc-RBClinn15 (referred to as RBClin15) infecting the recently identified B. tropicus AOA-CPS1. RBClin15 is a temperate phage with a putative parABS partitioning system as well as an arbitrium system, which are presumed to enable extrachromosomal genome maintenance and regulate the lysis/lysogeny switch, respectively. The temperate phage RBClin15 has been sequenced however, was erroneously deposited as a plasmid in the NCBI GenBank database. A BLASTn search against the GenBank database using the whole genome sequence of RBClin15 revealed seven other putative temperate phages that were also deposited as plasmids in the database. Comparative genomic analyses shows that RBClin15 shares between 87 and 92% average nucleotide identity (ANI) with the seven temperate phages from the GenBank database. All together RBClin15 and the seven putative temperate phages share common genome arrangements and < 29% protein homologs with the closest phages, including 0105phi7-2. A phylogenomic tree and proteome-based phylogenetic tree analysis showed that RBClin15 and the seven temperate phages formed a separate branch from the closest phage, 0105phi7-2. In addition, the intergenomic similarity between RBClin15 and its closely related phages ranged between 0.3 and 47.7%. Collectively, based on the phylogenetic, and comparative genomic analyses, we propose three new species which will include RBClin15 and the seven temperate phages in the newly proposed genus Theosmithvirus under Caudoviricetes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10937-4.Keywords: Bacteriophage, Bacillus tropicus, Theosmithvirus, vB_Btc-RBClin15, Genomics, Species

Introduction

The classification of bacteriophages began in the 1970s, and for many years tailed phages were classified based on structural features as well as nucleic acid composition [1]. Following the advancement in next-generation sequencing technology, many bacteriophage genomes have been sequenced, and this has made drastic improvements in the field of bacteriophage taxonomy, which include whole genome sequence comparisons and proteomics, leading to the whole new virus classification system [2, 3]. In August 2022, ICTV revised the phage classification system. To create a primary, genome-based classification that accurately reflects the evolutionary relationships between phage members, for instance, the taxonomy structure of the class Caudoviricetes underwent a significant overhaul [4]. As a result, several essential families from the previous order Caudovirales, such as Siphoviridae, Podoviridae, and Myoviridae, were eliminated [5].

The most studied bacteriophages infect members of the Bacillus sp., and one of the first reports on lysogeny in B. cereus associated with the presence of phage particles observed via electron microscopy during the 1950s [6]. However, interest in Bacillus sp. phages particularly phages infecting B. cereus and B. thuringiensis only caught the public attention after the first electron micrograph which showed phages associated with B. thuringiensis during the 1960s and led to more studies focusing on isolating phages that infect not only B. cereus and B. thuringiensis but members of the Bacillus sp. mainly the cereus-anthracis-mycoides group, now known as the CAM [6]. Currently, phages infecting members of the Bacillus species are popular for their agricultural and medical importance, and similar to most spore-forming bacteria; members of the Bacillus sp. appear widespread; hence, it is no surprise that many phages that infect this species are widespread, and highly represented in the class Caudoviricetes as well as unclassified phages [7].

The Bacillus sp. phages are also popular for their astonishingly diverse lifestyles and lysogenic states such as their common temperate chromosomal state known for phages such as BceA1 and vB_BceM_Bc431v3 [8, 9], their virulent state is known from phages such as Fah and d’Herelle [6, 10], as well as their temperate circular or linear plasmids or an episomal state reported for phages TP21-T, vB_BceS_IEBH, and 250 [6, 11, 12]. Even though reports on prophages that replicate as circular plasmids predate the Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) era, it is noteworthy that prophage P1 of E. coli K12 was the first circular plasmid prophage with an extensively studied genome [13]. Prophage P1 later became a model organism in molecular biology, along with the popular phages lambda and T4 [13, 14]. Extensive research has been conducted on its replication as a low-copy number plasmid, and the key proteins involved in this process have been thoroughly identified [15]. Over the years, there has been an increasing number of Bacillus sp. phages exhibiting this phenomenon which is now recognized as a legitimate variant of the lysogenic cycle, distinct from the traditional scenario that involves the integration of phage genetic material into the host DNA. Thus far, the phages; IEBH, Bp8p-C, and B83 are the only Bacillus sp. phages that have been experimentally confirmed plasmid prophages, and all three phages to use a parM-like protein, and an RHH/HTH domain-containing (adaptor) protein plasmid partitioning system enabling extrachromosomal replication [7, 12, 16]. Of the three Bacillus phages, only B83 has been shown to code for two Xer recombinases which are thought to facilitate the effectiveness of its’ parM-like plasmid partitioning system [7]. Otherwise, the number of Bacillus phage genome sequences available on the NCBI database have increased, and many discoveries have been made regarding the interaction of Bacillus phages with their hosts. For example, the arbitrium signal system which is like a quorum sensing mechanism of the Bacillus sp. has been reported first with Bacillus cereus phages [17]. Therefore, with the increasing number of Bacillus phage genome sequences on the GenBank database thorough investigation of these phage genomes becomes necessary as they are truly full of surprises.

The microbe known as Bacillus tropicus is a Gram-positive, spore-forming, facultative anaerobic Bacillus species that has been isolated in various environments, including wastewater treatment plants, marine environments, arable land, and market areas. The bacterium has shown potential in the degradation of various pollutants and waste materials [18, 19]. Bacillus tropicus strain AOA-CPS1 was found to degrade pentachlorophenol (PCP) and other xenobiotic compounds [18, 19]. Another strain, Gxun-17, demonstrated efficient degradation of feather waste through the production of keratinase enzymes [20]. B. tropicus isolated from dumping site soil was able to biodegrade low-density polyethylene (LDPE) films, leading to changes in the properties of the polymer matrix [21, 22]. Additionally, B. tropicus MCCC 1A01406 showed resistance against multiple heavy metals and had a high capacity for lead (Pb) biosorption [23]. Furthermore, B. tropicus LS27 was found to produce keratinolytic protease, which had applications in dehairing, destaining, and metal recovery processes [23, 24]. These studies highlight the diverse capabilities of B. tropicus in bioremediation and waste management.

Bacillus tropicus is a pathogen that has been underestimated in terms of its geographic distribution and host range. For instance, Bacillus tropicus JMT has been found to cause anthrax-like disease in Chinese soft-shelled turtles, sharing similar phenotypical properties and carrying virulent genes such as protective antigen (PA), edema factor (EF), hyaluronic acid (HA), and exopolysaccharide (Bps) genes on their plasmids [25]. The B. tropicus strain AOA-CPS1 has been identified as a pentachlorophenol-degrading bacterium with the potential for bioremediation of xenobiotic compounds [18]. In addition, Fatima et al. (2022) associated B. tropicus EMB20 with the production of beta-lactamases [26]. Although the pathogenesis of B. tropicus is not explicitly discussed in the literature as of the year 2023, it can be inferred that the presence of virulent genes and the ability to degrade toxic compounds contribute to its pathogenicity.

Thus far, various strains of B. tropicus have been isolated and their potential in bioremediation and bioconversions have been demonstrated. As of 2023, only six plasmids have been associated with six strains of the 38 sequenced B. tropicus genomes however, no bacteriophage has been associated with B. tropicus among the 125 Bacillus sp. phage genomes deposited in the GenBank database on the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) (accessed in December 2023), with genome sizes ranging from 18 to 251 kbps. Characterizing phages that infect B. tropicus could be of great importance for industry, phage biology, and alternative therapeutic approaches such as phage therapy due to the potential importance of B. tropicus in future applications, as well as its association with pathogenesis and antibiotic resistance.

This study aims to characterize the genome of the first putative plasmid prophage vB_Btc_RBClin15 (abbreviated as RBClin15) of Bacillus tropicus, which was erroneously deposited as a plasmid pBClin15 of Bacillus tropicus strain AOA-CPS1 (Accession number CP049020.1) on the NCBI GenBank database. The putative plasmid prophage RBClin15 shows the presence of key proteins that are characteristics of plasmid prophages and has further been used to identify other putative plasmid prophages that may be related to RBClin15.

Materials and methods

Genome sequence rearrangements and re-annotation

The putative phage genome was retrieved from NCBI GenBank database. The putative phage genome was passed through RASTtk v.29 [27] to search for open reading frames (ORFs), and tRNAscan-SE v.2.0 [28] was used to search for tRNA(s). Glimmer3 v3.02 [29], GeneMarkS v4.28 [30], and Prodigal v2.60 [31] were used for the prediction of coding sequences (CDS). The functionality of the CDSs was then predicted using BLASTp [32] and HHpred (E-values cutoff of 1e-5) [33]. The predicted CDSs were further analyzed through DeepTHMMM v.1.0.24 [34] from Python and the SignalP-6.0 server to search for transmembrane domains [35] as well as signal peptides cleavage sites. Putative promoter regions on the re-annotated genome were predicted using phiSITE PromoterHunter [36] and further verified in silico using SnapGene. The presence of putative CRISPR protospacers was assessed using CRISPRCasFinder standalone program v2.0.3 [37]. Temperate markers, virulence genes, and antibiotic resistance genes on the genome of RBClin15 were assessed using the PhageLeads sever [38].

Comparative genomics

A whole-genome BLASTn search against the NCBI GenBank database v.2.15.0 was conducted to find phages related to the putative phage RBClin15. For comparison, all genomes in this study were then manually shifted setting the predicted small terminase subunit (TerS) CDSs as the starting points of the genomes. The Virfam server was used for the recognition of head-neck-tail modules in phage genomes [39]. A plausible RBClin15 endolysin protein structure was predicted using AlphaFold3 [40]. Phage genome sequences predicted as the outcome of the BLASTn search were added to the proteomic tree generated using the ViPTree server v3.7 [41], conducted based on genome-wide sequence similarities calculated by tBLASTx. For phage taxonomy, vConTACT2 v.0.9.19 was used to compare the genome of RBClin15 against the ProkaryoticViralRefSeq v.201 database with default parameters, and the phages predicted to be related to RBClin15 were determined by similarity score > 1. The resulting network was visualised and annotated using Cytoscape v3.10.1 [42]. Amino acid profiles of RBClin15 and its related prophages were submitted to an online resource (VICTOR) (tree building and virus classification bioinformatics tool) for phylogenetic analysis, using the recommended settings of the genome BLAST distance phylogeny (GBDP) method [41]. Genome map alignments and linear diagrams illustrating the level of identity between the putative phage genome RBClin15, and its closely related genomes were also created using the ViPTree server or EasyFig v.2.2.236 [43]. Under the recommended configurations, the Virus Intergenomic Distance Calculator (VIRDIC) was used to plot intergenomic nucleotide sequence similarities as well as the aligned genome fractions within the imported phage genome sequences [44].

Panproteome analyses and phylogeny

Orthologous genes of phages closely related to RBClin15 were predicted using CoreGene 5.0 [45]. The CoreGenes5.0 webserver (https://coregenes.ngrok.io/) was used with the OrthoMCL option with E-value of 1e‐5. CoreGenes5.0 uses the GET_HOMOLOGUES package to implement the ortholog clustering [46]. Signature genes were those with gene products present in all members of the proposed species/genus where there was consensus between two or more of the analyses.

The phylogenetic analyses for the typical proteins used as biomarkers, which include; terminase large subunit protein (TerL), terminase small subunit proteins (TerS), portal proteins and the endolysin proteins were conducted in MEGA X [47]. The available protein sequences were aligned using MUSCLE [48] default parameters. For all optimal trees shown in Fig. S3 – S6, the evolutionary history was inferred using the Neighbor-Joining method [49]. The percentage of replicate trees in which the associated taxa clustered together in the bootstrap test (1000 replicates) are shown next to the branches [50]. The trees are drawn to scale, with branch lengths in the same units as those of the evolutionary distances used to infer the phylogenetic tree. The evolutionary distances were computed using the p-distance method [51] and are in the units of the number of amino acid differences per site.

Results and discussion

Genomic properties of vB_Bt-RBClin15

The DNA molecule with accession number CP049020.1, found in GenBank, is a potential prophage with a length of 58,449 bp and a GC content of 35.43%. After conducting a genome analysis, it was discovered that this molecule encodes multiple (889) open reading frames (ORFs) related to phages. The putative phage is now known as RBClin15 and is predicted to have 85 coding sequences (CDSs), with 36 of them having hypothetical functions (28 of which are unique to this phage) and 48 (57%) with an assigned function based on sequence similarity (E value < 10− 5) to known protein sequences in the NCBInr database. The CDSs have diverse roles such as DNA packaging, structural proteins, cell lysis, and DNA replication/transcription/repair. The list of the putative protein-coding genes has been manually curated and is listed in supplementary material Table S1.

The encoded CDSs of RBClin15 include 74 proteins that begin with ATG codon (88.1%), 6 with TTG codon (7.1%), and 4 with GTG codon (4.8%). No antibiotic resistance genes, CRISPR protospacer, or tRNA were identified on the genome of RBClin15. However, a putative LCl antimicrobial peptide, similar to that of Bacillus subtilis, which has been shown to possess antimicrobial properties against Pseudomonas solanacearum PE1 and Xanthomonas campestris pv oryzea, was discovered [52]. Moreover, the PhageLead server predicted 4 CDSs at regions 1, 59, 44 787, 45 560 as anti-repressor proteins, which may be related to lysogenic phage-related proteins. However, only CDS 57 had a 100% amino acid identity (AAI) hit against the NCBI protein database and a conserved domain that was recognized in the Pfam database.

Genomic and phylogenetic analysis

Genome structural arrangement

Bacteriophage genomes often contain clusters of coding sequences (CDSs) that are related to biological functions. These clusters form modules that can be co-regulated and co-inherited [53]. The genome of RBClin15 has five functional modules, each with its particular function as shown in (Fig. 1). These modules include DNA packaging and morphogenesis, host lysis, prophage maintenance and plasmid replication, DNA replication, repair, and recombination, as well as DNA metabolism and modification, which are all highlighted in (Fig. 1). The genome map of RBClin15, displayed in (Fig. 1), is compared to that of phage 0105phi7-2 which is a close relative and both genome maps shows a modular arrangement similar to that of the phage lambda [54].

Functionality of predicted open reading frames

Virion assembly genes

The genome map presented shows modules on the left arm that are involved in phage DNA packaging, structure morphogenesis, and host lysis. The CDSs linked to the packaging of mature phage genomic DNA concatemers into the head of RBClin15 include CDS1, CDS2, and CDS3 sequences, which are highly similar to the terminase small (TerS) and large subunits (TerL), and a portal protein of a B. tropicus prophage genome in the NCBInr database, respectively. The TerS protein recognizes and binds a specific site on concatemeric phage DNA, enabling the endonuclease and ATPase activity of the TerL protein to initiate the packaging of newly synthesized viral genomic DNA into empty phage capsids through a mechanism facilitated by a portal protein [55]. The (Fig. S4 and S5) of the supplemental material show phylogenetic trees comparing the projected TerS and portal proteins, respectively. Following the importance of the TerL protein during the packaging of the phage genomic DNA, TerL protein phylogeny is vital for predicting possible DNA packaging strategies associated with newly discovered phages. Therefore, based on the TerL phylogenetic tree presented in the supplementary material (Fig. S3), the TerL of phage RBClin15 clusters with the TerL proteins of T5-like however, with a low bootstrap of 21%. The mechanism of T5-like phages generates long exact direct repeat ends [56], suggesting that phage RBClin15 generates long exact terminal direct repeats during its DNA packaging.

Virion particle genes

The proteins involved in the head morphogenesis of RBClin15 were predicted from CDS 4 – CDS 6, including the phage minor head protein (CDS 4), a capsid maturation protease (CDS 5), and a major capsid protein (CDS 6). These proteins share less than 99% amino acid sequence similarities with proteins of PBSX family phages of B. tropicus. Furthermore, downstream of the major capsids encoding CDS lies the subsequent CDSs (CDSs 7 and 8), coding for proteins YqbF and YqbG. According to previous studies, the B. subtilis YqbG protein has structural similarities to connector proteins phage HK97 (gp6) and SPP1 (gp15) [57]. Therefore, it is speculated that the two predicted proteins (YqbF and YqbG) and the downstream CDSs (CDS 9 and 10), which form part of the operon, are critical for the assembly of the head-tail connector [57, 58]. This specialized interface is used for head and tail attachment, as well as the exit point for DNA during infection [58], and therefore may form part of the head morphogenesis proteins. Finally, the Virfam server analysis presented in the supplementary material (Fig. S2) shows that RBClin15’s neck module is somewhat similar to proteins in Neck Type 1-Cluster 5, suggesting a neck structural arrangement that may be similar to that of the Streptomyces phage phiC31 (Fig. S2).

The tail morphogenesis module of phage RBClin15 consists of 11 CDSs, which have no significant sequence homology with previously sequenced lytic bacteriophages on the NCBInr database. However, most proteins that constitute the tail of RBClin15 have significant sequence similarities to tail proteins of the PBSX family phages of B. tropicus or in a few cases the PBSX family phages of B. cereus prophage. The 11 CDSs code for an array of conserved proteins, such as a hypothetical protein, a putative major tail protein, a hypothetical protein containing a complete phage TAC_12 family domain that serves as a phage tail assembly chaperone for this phage [59], a hypothetical protein, two subsequent putative tail length tape measure protein (TMP) (CDS 15 and 16) [59], which may be presumed as possibly one long CDS based on the length of the TMPs of closely related phages. The putative TMP CDS is followed by five CDSs coding for a putative sipho_tail minor superfamily protein, a putative prophage_tail superfamily protein, and three CDSs that seem to form part of the distal tail proteins of phage RBClin15, namely the AbrB protein (CDS 19), a putative receptor-binding protein (CDS 20), and one hypothetical protein (CDS 21). The TMP of many bacteriophages has been shown to determine the length of the phage tail [60]. By contrast, (Fig. 1) suggests that the largest gene on the genome of RBClin15 codes for a TMP, as observed for many siphoviruses [61]. (All predicted structural proteins and their Aclame phage hits are presented in the supplementary material Fig. S1).

Host lysis

As per the genome map (Fig. 1), the host lysis proteins are located on the left side. In phage RBClin15, the host lysis module is believed to be an operon, comprising of a holin-like protein (CDS 22) and an N-acetylmuramoyl-L-alanine amidase or peptidoglycan amidohydrolases (endolysin) (CDS 23). This configuration of host lysis CDSs is similar to the host lysis module discovered in Bacillus sp. bacteriophages 250 and IEBH [6, 11, 12]. Based on the predicted host lysis module, it can be inferred that RBClin15 uses the usual dsDNA phage holin-endolysin system to archive host lysis. A manual BLASTp/tBLASTx search against the database of non-redundant protein sequences showed that the predicted holin-like protein (consisting of 141amino acids (aa)) showed < 99.29% sequence similarity with high query cover to holin proteins found on the genome of seven prophages associated members of the Bacillus sp. including B. tropicus, B. anthracis, B. thuringiensis, and B. cereus. Further analysis of the predicted holin protein for the transmembrane domain(s) revealed three transmembrane helices (TMHs) (7aa – 26aa, 22aa – 54aa, 67aa – 89aa), which are shown in (Fig. 2). The three predicted TMHs suggest that this putative holin may belong to Class I holin proteins as the holin protein of E. coli phage lambda S protein, and S. aureus phage P68 hol15 protein [62] (Fig. 2).

Subsequently, a manual BLASTp/tBLASTx search against the database of non-redundant protein sequences revealed that the predicted endolysin protein of RBClin15 showed 100% sequence similarity to only 1 N-acetylmuramoyl-L-alanine amidase which appears to be encoded on a genome of a Bacillus cereus prophage (Accession number WP_001985751.1), and all other N-acetylmuramoyl-L-alanine amidases which are also associated with members of Bacillus genus including B. anthracis, B. thuringiensis, and B. subtilis share ≥ 92% sequence similarity with endolysin of RBClin15. A phylogenetic tree of the compared endolysin protein sequences is presented in the supplementary material Fig. S6. Therefore, this RBClin15 endolysin is novel with possible properties that may be useful in the current global crisis of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), as previously demonstrated for endolysins such as PlyP123 [63], PlyB [64], M4Lys [65], and LysAB5 [66]. A highly confident 3D model of the predicted RBClin15 endolysin protein is presented in (Fig. 3). It shows that the protein is linear and consists of two domains: a cell-binding domain (CBD) on the N-terminal of the predicted endolysin protein is connected to a catalytic domain on the C-terminal of the protein by a linker protein.

The catalytic domain of the predicted endolysin belongs with Amidase_2 proteins that hydrolyzes the link between N-acetylmuramoyl residues and L-amino acid residues in certain cell-wall glycopeptides [67–69]. However, the predicted CBD appears similar to CBDs of endolysins encoded by other bacteriophages that infect members of the Bacillales, and its specificity can be determined with certainty in vivo.

Host integration and SOS response

Contrary to many bacteriophages that use the bacterial SOS defense mechanism to escape the cell during DNA mutagenesis, irradiation, and other DNA damaging effects, the genome of RBClin15 does not code for specific SOS response proteins. However, immediately after the endolysin operon, there is another operon that appears to be transcribed in the reverse direction. It consists of a hypothetical protein (CDS 24), a bacterial CdiA-CT RNAse A domain-containing protein (CDS 25), a YolD-like family protein (CDS 26), a PolY_Pol_V_umuC protein (CDS 27), and helix-turn-helix xenobiotics response element (HTH_XRE) superfamily protein (CDS 28).

Although members of the YolD-like family protein are functionally uncharacterized, they are thought to function equivalently to UmuD subunit of polymerase V from Gram-negative bacteria [70]. However, the genome of phage RBClin15 appears to code for PolY_Pol_V_umuC protein [71]. In the past, umuC is a subunit of DNA Polymerase V (Pol V) [72], a bacterial translesion synthesis (TLS) polymerase that consists of the heterotrimer of one umuC and two umuD subunits [72, 73]. Translesion synthesis is a process that allows the bypass of a variety of DNA lesions. Translesion synthesis polymerases lack proofreading activity and have low fidelity and low processivity [74]. They use damaged DNA as templates and insert nucleotides opposite the lesions [74]. Pol_V, RecA, single-stranded DNA-binding protein, beta sliding clamp, and gamma clamp loading complex induce the SOS response in bacteria to repair UV-induced DNA damage [72, 73]. Therefore, in this case, it is most likely that RBClin15 relies on this YolD-like family protein-containing operon for SOS response.

Lysis-lysogeny regulation

The CDSs 39, 56, and 57 are XRE-family transcriptional regulators that are similar to lambda cI, CII, and Cro repressors [7, 54], suggesting that these genes may be responsible for regulating the life cycles (lysis-lysogeny) of RBClin15. In lambda-like phages, the cI repressor protein works in conjunction with other proteins that were not predicted on the genome of RBClin15 [75]. However, this DNA-binding helix-turn-helix protein is divergently transcribed from the Cro protein, and the two proteins compete for the operator region. This competition determines whether the life cycle of the phage is lytic or lysogenic. When the operator region is occupied by the Cro protein, transcription of cI repressor is inhibited, and the late phage genes are expressed, leading to the formation and release of new viral particles (lytic cycle is favored). Likewise, when the operator region is occupied by the cI repressor, the transcription mechanism of the Cro protein is inhibited leading to a suppression of the late phage genes, and the lysogenic cycle is favored [75–77].

Towards the end of the right arm on the mapped genome of RBClin15 (Fig. 1), lie CDSs that code for proteins involved in the lysogenization of this phage. These proteins include the resolvase family protein (CDS 82) and the GIY-YIG nuclease family protein (CDS 83) [78]. The predicted resolvase family protein (CDS 82) is a site-specific DNA recombinase that plays a crucial role in DNA replication, recombination, and repair [79]. It is related to the DNA invertase Pin. The GIY-YIG nuclease family protein (CDS 83) is a putative nucleotide excisionase repair endonuclease that may be involved in an excision and integration process of RBClin15 [80]. Therefore, the presence of Cro/cI-type DNA binding protein, a site-specific DNA recombinase, and a nuclease protein in CDSs strongly suggest that RBClin15 is capable of lysogenization. This process appears to be mediated by an antirepressor (CDS 58) and transcriptional regulatory proteins (CDS 53, 56, and 57) encoded further upstream, with a hint of repressor cleavage-mediated induction.

Between the lysogenizing antirepressors (CDSs 53 and 56) are two adjacent CDSs (CDSs 54 and 55) that are presumed to be part of the arbitrium system of RBClin15. The arbitrium systems discovered by Erez et al. (2017) are in abundance amongst temperate phages that infect the Bacillus sp. strains, such as Bacillus phages phi3T, B83, and SPbeta [17]. Arbitrium systems are composed of three components: AimP, a small peptide that acts as a signal molecule; AimX, a non-coding RNA that negatively regulates lysogeny; and AimR, the cellular receptor of AimP and the transcriptional activator of AimX. The CDS 54 in RBClin15 codes for a protein that shares structural similarity with the AimR protein, which can be found in (Table S2). Downstream of CDS 54 is an AimP-like protein (CDS 55), which is a 52-amino acid protein. This AimP-like protein has a predicted signal peptide cleavage site between positions 27–27, resulting in the generation of a pro-peptide EQSKQVAVEKTTEVQRMMVDPGGGGW (C-terminus is Italic).

Stokar-Avihail et al. (2019) discovered that all known arbitrium systems can be classified into nine clades based on the phylogeny of AimR homologs. Each clade has a unique profile of mature AimP sequence. In this study, the AimP C-terminus of RBClin15 was used to browse through the list of recorded AimP homologs and AimP C-termini (Erez et al., 2017 (Table S1). It was apparent that the AimP C-terminus of RBClin15 is identical to the AimP C-terminus of Bacillus cereus tsu1 phage LambdaBa01, with 100% amino acid identity (AAI). However, the total AimP protein sequence shares up to 88% of BLASTp identity, indicating that the AimR-like protein of RBClin15 is novel and belongs to clade 9. Essentially, the AimR homologs in clade 9 are almost exclusive to phages of B. anthracis, B. cereus, and B. thuringiensis.

Phage origin of replication

Phage RBClin15 lacks a common integrase, but it has a tyrosine/serine integrase protein that helps integrate the phage genome into the host chromosome during lysogeny. This means that RBClin15 can be replicated along with the bacterial host genome [81]. Additionally, RBClin15 shares some features with temperate phages like Escherichia coli phage P1, which completely lacks an integration system [82]. Phage P1 has genes that code for components of partitioning and recombination systems, ensuring prophage genome maintenance extrachromosomally [82]. Such plasmidial prophages are relatively uncommon, but there have been some reported for various bacteria, including Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus anthracis, Chlamydia pneumoniae, and Borrelia burgdorferi. Since prophages like RBClin15 are likely maintained extrachromosomally, they are probably underrepresented in NGS projects. Recent studies have discussed how similar phages (phage-plasmids) promote gene flow between different types of mobile genetic elements (MGE) and play a crucial role in the spread of genes like antibiotic resistance and defence systems [83]. These elements drive bacterial evolution by recombining genetic material, sometimes evolving into purely phage-like or plasmid-like entities, which emphasize the importance of reclassifying certain MGEs due to the complexity of their evolutionary trajectories, especially in complex environments such as the human gut [84].

Previous studies found that cluster A temperate mycobacteriophages (such as RedRock and CRB1) contain partitioning systems that are believed to assist in prophage maintenance. The RedRock phage encodes for ParB, a DNA-binding protein that recognizes two parS loci (parS-L and parS-R), each containing eight directly repeated copies of an 8-bp motif [85]. The expression of RedRock’s parA and parB has been confirmed to occur during lysogeny and the parABS cassette plays a significant role in stabilizing extrachromosomally replicating shuttle plasmids, such as those based on oriM from Mycobacterium fortuitum plasmid pAL5000 [85].

The RBClin15 phage has a parABS system consisting of three CDSs (CDS 38–40) that are transcribed in the reverse strand. The system’s replication is regulated by a repA-like protein (CDS 33) located in the middle of the parABS system [82, 85]. Upstream of the repA-like protein, there is an Fts/SpoIIIE-replication-relaxation-caspase operon involved in DNA replication, repair, and recombination in the genome of RBClin15. The FtsK/SpoIIIE protein (CDS 30) of RBClin15 shares 94.5% percentage identity with a DNA segregation ATPase FtsK/SpoIIIE protein found in B. cereus (Accession No. NVH0597-99), as revealed by a BLASTp search against the NCBInr database. The FtsK/SpoIIIE homolog of many prophages is thought to regulate DNA segregation in Bacillus sp. strains and is closely related to proteins that play a role in chromosome partitioning, cell cycle control, and cell division [86]. In phage RBClin15, the CDS coding for the FtsK/SpoIIIE domain-containing protein is transcribed as an operon along with the downstream genes coding for proteins such as the replication-relaxation family protein (CDS 31) and a caspase-like family protein (CDS 32) presumed to have some form of nuclease activity.

Because the phage RBClin15 reads were exclusive of the reads assembled for the bacterial host genome, and code for parABS system similar to that of phage RedRock for extrachromosomal maintenance. We presume that the ParB of RBClin15 also recognizes two parS loci (parS-L and parS-R) which span both directions of the parABS cassette, and in both directions contain direct repeated copies of a 10-bp (5′-AAAGTAGAAA-3′) motif. It may also control the expression of other genes necessary for replication.

RepA is a common replication initiator protein found in many Bacillus plasmids. It binds to the origin of replication (oriC) of the plasmid DNA and initiates the process of plasmid replication [87]. RepA often works in conjunction with other proteins and regulatory elements to ensure the proper replication and maintenance of the plasmid within the bacterial cell [82]. The presence of an initiator protein, RepA, an origin of replication, as well as a parABS system on the genome of RBClin15 strongly suggests the prophage undergoes extrachromosomal genome maintenance. However, there are also other replication and recombination-related proteins such as the DnaD-type, DnaC-type, the RecU-like Holliday junction resolvase, and ArpU-like protein.

As per the ICTV’s previous virus classification standards, viruses belonging to the same genus should have a nucleotide sequence similarity of > 50% or > 40% homologous CDSs [2, 88]. Therefore, based on the presented phylogenetic as well as the comparative genomic analysis, we propose a new genus which will be known as Rimilasevirus for RBClin15 and a new subfamily Theosmithvirinae to include phages vB_Bt-AlH1 (+ 3 phages), and vB_Bt-ArG4 (+ 2 phages), which all fall under the family Caudoviricetes.

Conclusions

Bacteriophages are viruses that infect bacteria [89] and are the most abundant biological entities on Earth [89, 90]. Temperate bacteriophages are able to form prophages that are stably maintained in the host population. They can either integrate into the host genome or replicate as plasmids in the host cytoplasm [7]. Tailed temperate bacteriophages have been found to form circular plasmid prophages in many bacterial species, including those in the taxa Firmicutes, Gammaproteobacteria, and Spirochaetes [6, 91, 92]. However, it is believed that the actual number of such prophages may be underestimated for two main reasons. Firstly, in bacterial whole genome-sequencing assemblies, they are difficult to distinguish from actual plasmids [7, 93]. Secondly, there is a lack of experimental studies to confirm their existence [6, 93, 94]. In Firmicutes, these prophages appear to be particularly numerous. In this study, the temperate phage genome of vB_Bt-RBClin15, the first phage to infect B. tropicus, was characterized using bioinformatics tools. Phage RBClin15 is a putative plasmid prophage related to seven other putative plasmid prophages that were all mistaken for plasmids in the past. Based on the ICTV virus classification standards, we propose that RBClin15 be the only member of a new genus, which we have tentatively named Rimilasevirus, and the seven closely related plasmid prophages to be considered as new members of a new subspecies of the genus Theosmithvirus, which will be known as Theosmithvirinae under Caudoviricetes. This study expands our understanding of the diversity and interactions that infect the Bacillus sp. in wastewater.
