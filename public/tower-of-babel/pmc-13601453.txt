The effect and mechanism of TIPE in promoting M2 polarization through DcR3 in the microenvironment of colorectal cancer
Authors: Shiying Zhang, Chunlin Shen, Zeyang Lin, Qiang Zhu, Yihan Feng, Liwei Sun, Chengjia Zhang, Haole Zhang, Lumin Wang, Shuyu Chen, Jiyi Huang, Yuhan Ye, Xingfeng Qiu, Guohong Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElevated expression of tumor necrosis factor α-induced protein 8 (TIPE) is observed in colorectal cancer (CRC) and is known to regulate decoy receptor 3 (DcR3) expression while promoting M2 polarization of macrophages. However, the precise role of DcR3 in macrophage M2 polarization remains to be elucidated.MethodsWe employed bioinformatic analysis alongside in vitro and in vivo experimental approaches to investigate the regulatory mechanism by which TIPE influences macrophage M2 polarization in CRC.ResultsTIPE expression in CRC tissues positively correlates with M2 macrophage markers (CD206, CD163, IL-10). Functionally, TIPE promotes M2 polarization of macrophages by upregulating DcR3. Mechanistically, DcR3 binds to heparan sulfate proteoglycan 2 (HSPG2) in a heparan sulfate (HS)-dependent manner, leading to activation of the protein kinase Bα1 (AKT1) signaling pathway. AKT1 activation not only directly drives M2 polarization but also enhances macrophage chemotaxis and recruitment toward CRC cells by upregulating the chemokine CXCL8.ConclusionsThis study reveals a novel mechanism through which TIPE regulates M2 macrophage polarization and function via DcR3, offering new perspectives for the diagnosis and therapeutic targeting of CRC.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00262-026-04531-8.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most common malignant tumor worldwide, with its high morbidity and mortality posing a serious threat to human health [1, 2]. The immunosuppressive nature of the tumor microenvironment (TME) is a key driver of CRC progression and treatment resistance [3]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), constituting up to 50% of immune cells within the TME, play a central role in tumor immunomodulation through dynamic phenotypic polarization [4]. While the M1 phenotype exhibits anti‑tumor activity, the M2 phenotype promotes tumor progression [5]. In CRC, infiltration by M2 is significantly associated with poor patient prognosis; however, the specific regulatory mechanisms underlying this process remain incompletely understood [6].

Tumor necrosis factor α‑induced protein 8 (TIPE), an important immunomodulatory molecule, shows a significant positive correlation with M2 macrophage (M2) infiltration in CRC [7], suggesting its involvement in regulating TAM polarization. Decoy receptor 3 (DcR3), a member of the tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily, inhibits apoptotic signaling and promotes immune escape by competitively binding to ligands such as FasL, LIGHT, and TL1A [8]. Notably, TIPE has been shown to significantly enhance DcR3 secretion by CRC cells, which in turn promotes tumor proliferation [9]. Nevertheless, direct evidence indicating whether DcR3 acts as a downstream effector of TIPE in regulating TAM polarization is still lacking.

Heparan sulfate proteoglycan 2 (HSPG2) is predominantly localized in the basement membrane and extracellular matrix. It is also distributed on the cell surface, where it interacts with diverse ligands and participates in cell adhesion, migration, and signal transduction. [10]. Liu et al. demonstrated that DcR3 interacts with HSPG2 to promote metastasis in pancreatic cancer [11]. This raises the question of whether the DcR3/HSPG2 axis mediates TAM polarization in CRC. Consistent with this hypothesis, our preliminary data reveal a significant co‑occurrence of high DcR3 expression and HSPG2 activation.

AKT1, a core component of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, plays crucial roles in regulating cell survival, proliferation, and metabolism [12]. Feng et al. reported that the PI3K/AKT signaling axis is pivotal for macrophage activation in breast cancer [13]. However, whether HSPG2/AKT1 signaling mediates TAM polarization in CRC has not been explored. Intriguingly, our study identified a significant positive correlation between high HSPG2 expression and AKT1 activation.

In this study, we integrated bioinformatics analyses, in vitro cellular assays, and in vivo animal models to systematically elucidate the molecular mechanism through which TIPE‑overexpressing CRC cells regulate M2 polarization. We demonstrate that CRC cells with TIPE overexpression secrete DcR3. The secreted DcR3 interacts with HSPG2 on macrophages in a heparan sulfate (HS)-dependent manner, thereby activating PI3K, which further recruits and activates AKT1. This activation not only directly promotes M2 polarization and migration of macrophages but also upregulates CXCL8 expression in polarized M2, thereby markedly enhancing the proliferation and migration of CRC cells. These findings reveal a novel mechanism of CRC‑macrophage crosstalk within the TME and provide a theoretical foundation for developing combined immunotherapy strategies targeting the TIPE/DcR3/HSPG2/AKT1 axis, highlighting its potential clinical translational value.

To address the aforementioned scientific questions, we integrated bioinformatic analyses, in vitro co-culture systems and in vivo animal models and verified that DcR3 secreted from TIPE-overexpressing CRC cells binds to cell-surface HSPG2 on macrophages in an HS-dependent manner. This binding event triggers PI3K activation, which subsequently recruits and phosphorylates AKT1 to AKT1 to drive macrophage M2-type polarization and migration; in turn, these remodeled tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) further facilitate the proliferation of CRC cells. This finding uncovers a novel mechanism underlying the crosstalk between colorectal cancer cells and macrophages within the TME and provides theoretical support for developing therapeutic targets against the TIPE/DcR3/HSPG2/AKT1 signaling axis, with promising prospects for clinical translation.

Methods

Cell lines and culture

Human colon cancer cells SW480 and HCT116, human monocytic leukemia cells THP-1, human embryonic kidney cell line HEK 293 T, and mouse monocyte macrophage leukemia cells RAW264.7 were kindly provided by the Typical Cultures Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). HCT116, SW480, 293 T, and RAW264.7 cells were maintained in DMEM (Meilunbio, China), while THP-1 was maintained in RPMI-1640 (Meilunbio, China). All cultures were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Meilunbio, China), double antiseptic sterilizing solution (penicillin/streptomycin solution, 100, Meilunbio, China). THP-1 cells were treated with 50 ng/mL phorbol ester (PMA, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 48 h to differentiate them into M0. Cell culture dishes/plates were obtained from NEST Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Wuxi, China). Exogenous DcR3/anti-DcR3 recombinant protein was obtained from Cancer Research Center of Xiamen University.

Induction of M1 macrophage polarization: THP‑1 monocytes were differentiated into unpolarized M0 macrophages via incubation with 100 ng/mL phorbol‑12‑myristate‑13‑acetate (PMA; Sigma‑Aldrich, USA) for 24 h. After medium replacement with complete RPMI‑1640 medium and a subsequent 24 h resting culture, cells were stimulated with 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) plus 20 ng/mL IFN‑γ (Meilunbio, China) for another 24 h to obtain M1‑polarized macrophages.

Induction of M2 macrophage polarization: THP‑1 cells were subjected to identical PMA differentiation and resting procedures as described for M1 induction. Afterward, M0 macrophages were treated with 20 ng/mL IL‑4 and 20 ng/mL IL‑13 (both from Meilunbio, China) to drive M2 polarization [14].

Heparan sulfate (HS) treatment: THP‑1 cells were differentiated into M0 macrophages using 50 ng/mL PMA (Sigma‑Aldrich, USA) over 48 h, followed by 1 h incubation with 100 μg/mL heparan sulfate (HS; GLPbio, China).

Heparinase III treatment: Following 48 h differentiation into M0 macrophages with 50 ng/mL PMA (Sigma‑Aldrich, USA), THP‑1‑derived M0 cells were incubated with 1 U/mL Heparinase III (Yuanye Biotechnology, China) for 1 h.

Lentiviral transduction

Lentiviral vectors encoding TIPE (TIPE/PLNX2) and control vectors, provided by the School of Life Sciences, Xiamen University [15], were packaged in 293 T cells. For this purpose, 293 T cells were plated in 100-mm dishes to ensure 70–80% cell density for transfection on the following day. For stable cell line establishment, SW480 and HCT116 cells were seeded at 50% confluency in culture dishes. Viral infection was performed by adding the corresponding lentiviruses supplemented with Polybrene (Solarbio, China) at a final concentration of 10 μg/mL to improve infection efficiency. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with selection medium containing puromycin (Meilunbio, China) at a final concentration of 10 μg/mL. After 24 h, the puromycin-containing medium was refreshed, and the SW480 cells were screened for 5–7 days to establish the stable TIPE-overexpressing colon cancer cell line SW480-OE and its control SW480-V. Next, the stable TIPE-knockdown cell line HCT116-KD and its control HCT116-V were similarly established in HCT116 cells using the described infection and selection protocol. All stable cell lines (SW480-OE, SW480-V, HCT116-KD, and HCT116-V) were subsequently verified by qPCR and Western blot [7].

siRNA transfection

M0 were seeded in 6-well plates at 2×105–7×105 cells/well 18–24 h before transfection, reaching 70–80% confluency on the transfection day. Then, the medium was refreshed with 2 mL serum-containing complete medium with antibiotics, which exerted no influence on Lipo8000-mediated transfection. For transfection complex preparation, 100 pmol siRNA and 4 μL Lipo8000 were sequentially mixed with 125 μL antibiotic-free DMEM/Opti-MEM, followed by 20 min room-temperature incubation without vortex or centrifugation. Afterward, 125 μL transfection complexes were added evenly to each well, and plates were shaken gently for mixing. Transfection efficiency was detected by fluorescence observation, Western blot, ELISA or reporter gene assay after 48 h culture, and the culture duration could be prolonged to 3–5 days as needed. The sequences of siRNAs used in this study were listed in Supplementary Table S1

Preparation of conditioned medium (CM)

SW480-OE, SW480-V, and M2 cells were inoculated at a density of 5×10^6 cells in 100-mm dishes. After an initial 24-h incubation period, the culture medium was replaced with serum-free medium for an additional 24 h of cultivation. Subsequently, the cell culture supernatant was harvested and subjected to centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min, yielding a clarified supernatant. This clarified supernatant was then mixed 1:1 with fully supplemented medium after passing through a 0.22 μm filter (Merck Millipore, USA) to prepare the conditioned medium.

Quantitative real‑time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using an RNA extraction reagent that does not require chloroform (Takara, China). Subsequently, RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using reverse transcriptase (ABclonal, China) and analyzed by real-time quantitative PCR using 2×Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (ABclonal, China). Data were normalized to the expression level of GAPDH in each sample. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blot

Western blot analysis was performed according to standard procedures [16]. SW480, HCT116, and THP-1 cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (Strong, Beyotime, China). Cell lysis products were subsequently separated on 6%, 10%, and 12% SDS-PAGE gels. Primary antibodies used were Anti-TIPE (Rabbit, 1:1000; Abcam, USA), Anti-DcR3 (Rabbit, 1:1000; Abcam, USA), Anti-HSPG2 (Mouse, 1:1000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), Anti-AKT1(Rabbit,1:1000; Affinity, China), Anti-Phospho-AKT1 (Rabbit, 1:1000; Affinity, China), Anti-PI3K (1:1000, Rabbit, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), Anti-Phospho PI3K (1:1000, Rabbit, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA) Anti-ACTIN (Rabbit, 1:5000; Affinity, China), Anti-GAPDH (Rabbit, 1:5000; Affinity, China), and Anti-Sodium Potassium ATPase (1: 5000,Rabbit,Zenbio,China). Protein bands were visualized using Meilunbio® FG Super Sensitive ECL Luminescence Reagent Solution B (Cat. No. MA0186-1, Dalian Meilun Biotechnology, China), and the band gray value was quantified with Image J software.

Co‑immunoprecipitation (Co‑IP)

M2 cells underwent overnight lysis at 4 °C using RIPA buffer (weak, Beyotime, China). The resulting cell lysate was then collected and incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific antibodies targeting the proteins of interest. For immunoprecipitation, Protein A/G beads (MedChemExpress, USA) were subsequently added to this mixture and incubated for 2 h. Following this, the beads were collected, thoroughly washed, and the bound protein complexes were eluted by boiling. Finally, protein interactions were determined using Western blotting.

Wound healing assay

RAW264.7 cells were seeded into a 6-well plate. Once the cells reached 80% confluence, a straightedge was used as an aid to guide a 200 μL tip, which was then used to vertically scribe a scratch intersecting a pre-drawn marker line, thereby creating multiple crosses that served as fixed detection sites. Following the scratch creation, cells were washed with 1× PBS (Meilunbio, China). Subsequently, SW480 and HCT116 cell conditioned medium and exogenous DcR3 were mixed with complete medium and applied to RAW264.7 cells. Wound healing images were captured microscopically at 0, 6, 12, and 24 h and analyzed using ImageJ (NIH, USA) software.

Transwell migration assay

A 24-well Transwell co-culture plate (Labselect, China) was used to set up two separate experimental groups. Group 1: RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded in the upper chambers at a density of 2×105 cells per well. The lower chambers were filled with complete medium mixed with conditioned medium collected from SW480 or HCT116 cells plus recombinant DcR3 protein. Group 2: SW480-OE cells were seeded in the upper chambers at a density of 2×105 cells per well, and M2 macrophages were seeded in the lower chambers. After cell seeding, all chambers were rinsed with 1× PBS. The upper chambers were supplemented with DMEM containing 2% FBS, whereas the lower chambers were filled with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. The Transwell plates were incubated for 48 h. At the end of incubation, residual cells on the inner side of the upper chamber membrane were gently wiped away. Cells that migrated to the bottom surface of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, China) for 10 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbio, China) for 15 min. After washing three times with PBS, migrated cells were photographed under an optical microscope for quantitative analysis.

CCK‑8 proliferation assay

To assess cell viability, RAW264.7 cells were inoculated into 96-well plates at a density of 2000 cells per well, with 3–5 replicate wells established for each group. After the cells had adhered to the well walls, the culture medium was carefully removed. Experimental groups were then supplemented with fresh medium containing the drug (100 μL/well), whereas control groups received fresh medium without the drug. Following incubation for 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, the medium in each well was replaced with a CCK-8 (Meilunbio, China) mixture (CCK-8:medium = 1:9). The plates were then incubated in the dark for 0.5–4 h. Subsequently, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using an enzyme marker (MAX190, China).

Immunofluorescence

Following co-culture with CRC cell conditioned medium, THP-1 cells were fixed for 30 min using 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, China). Subsequently, cells were incubated overnight with primary antibodies against DcR3 (1:500, rabbit, Abcam, USA), HSPG2 (1:200, mouse, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), and AKT1 (1:500, rabbit, Affinity, China). This was followed by a 1-h incubation in the dark with the corresponding secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor 647 (1:500, Mouse, Abcam, USA) and Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500, Rabbit, Abcam, USA). For nuclear counterstaining and subsequent imaging, samples were mounted for 10 min using a DAPI-containing antifluorescence attenuation sealer (Meilunbio, China) and then observed with an LSM880 confocal microscope (Zeiss, DE).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

For immunohistochemical analysis, tissue specimens were initially fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde. Following fixation, tumor sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including Anti-Phospho-AKT1 (Affinity, China) and Ki-67 (Abcam, USA). After rinsing with PBS, sections were then incubated with the corresponding fluorescent dye-coupled secondary antibodies for subsequent visualization and imaging.

ELISA

ELISA was performed using the DcR3 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (RayBiotech, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. This technique employs enzyme markers to produce a detectable signal, with subsequent absorbance measurement.

Determination of ARG-1 enzymatic activity

ARG-1 enzyme activity was measured using the Arginase Activity Assay Kit (Boxbio, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, and absorbance values were detected via a microplate reader.

Endotoxin quantification

Endotoxin levels were determined with the High‑sensitivity Kinetic Chromogenic LAL Endotoxin Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol, and absorbance was recorded using a microplate reader.

Subcutaneous tumor model in nude mice

To investigate the in vivo tumorigenicity and the effects of DcR3 modulation, xenograft models were established in nude mice. Nude mice were randomly divided into four experimental groups: SW480-OE control, SW480-OE-anti-DcR3, SW480-V control, and SW480-V-DcR3. SW480-OE and SW480-V cells in logarithmic growth phase were collected, counted, and mixed 1:1 (5×106 cells/100 μL) with Matrix Gel Matrigel (Corning, USA). The resulting cell suspensions were kept on ice and then subcutaneously injected into the backs of 4-week-old nude mice (100 μL/each) according to their respective groups. Following tumor establishment, specific interventions were administered: Anti-DcR3 (50 μg/each) was injected into the SW480-OE-anti-DcR3 group, exogenous DcR3 (50 μg/each) into the SW480-V-DcR3 group, and an equal volume of PBS into the SW480-OE control and SW480-V control groups. These interventions were repeated once after 7 days. Mice were monitored for body weight and tumor volume every 2 days. Subsequently, mice were anesthetized and euthanized after 28 days. Tumors, lymph nodes, and spleens were collected for subsequent analysis.

Flow cytometry

Immunofluorescence staining for HSPG2 with Fluor 488-Conjugated Secondary Antibody: Cultured cells from each experimental group were harvested and washed with PBS. The cell pellets were then resuspended in staining buffer (PBS supplemented with 1% BSA) and blocked on ice for 15 min. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with anti-HSPG2 primary antibody (1:200 dilution, rat monoclonal; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA) at 4 °C for 50 min. Following three washes with staining buffer, the cells were incubated with Fluor 488-conjugated AffiniPure rabbit anti-rat IgG (H+L) secondary antibody (1:500 dilution; Boster Bio, China) at 4 °C for an additional 50 min. The cells were then washed thoroughly to remove unbound antibodies prior to subsequent analysis.

Tumors, spleens, and lymph nodes were isolated from nude mice. These tissues were then treated with collagenase D (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and erythrocyte lysis buffer (Meilunbio, China). Following enzymatic treatment, the tissues were filtered through cell filters (70 μm, NEST, China) and subsequently processed into single-cell suspensions.

RNA-seq library construction, sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from all cell samples, and RNA integrity was verified prior to library construction. Each experimental group contained three independent biological replicates (n = 3). Paired-end RNA-seq (150 bp, PE150) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq sequencing platform. The raw sequencing depth of each sample ranged from 37 to 55 million raw reads.

Raw FASTQ data were subjected to quality control using FastQC (v0.10.1). Low-quality bases, adapter sequences and reads containing ambiguous N bases were trimmed with Cutadapt (v1.9.1). Only clean reads with a remaining length ≥75 bp were retained for subsequent analysis. All filtered samples exhibited Q20 > 97% and Q30 > 92%. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome hg38 (Homo_sapiens) using HISAT2 (v2.0.1) under default parameters. The mapping rate of all samples exceeded 92%, with uniquely mapped reads accounting for more than 87% of total clean reads.

Gene-level read counts were generated by HTSeq (v0.6.1). Two normalization approaches were adopted: the median-of-ratios normalization embedded in DESeq2 for differential expression analysis, and FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) for inter-sample expression comparison. Differential expression analysis was carried out with DESeq2 (v1.26.0) based on negative binomial distribution. Significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened by the criteria of adjusted P value (FDR/padj) ≤ 0.05 and |log2(fold change)|≥ 1. The Wald test was applied for statistical evaluation, and the Benjamini–Hochberg method was used for multiple testing correction to obtain FDR values.

Functional enrichment of DEGs against the KEGG database was performed via hypergeometric tests, followed by multiple testing correction to calculate Q-values. Pathways with Q-value ≤ 0.05 were defined as significantly enriched signaling pathways, including the HSPG2/AKT pathway focused in this study. All raw RNA-seq sequencing data have been submitted to the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database; the corresponding GEO accession number will be added to the manuscript upon official data release.

Results

TIPE promotes M2 polarization through DcR3

Previous studies by our group demonstrated that TIPE-overexpressing colorectal cancer (CRC) cells could promote macrophage (Mφ) polarization toward an M2 phenotype [7], though the underlying mechanism remained unclear. Given that interactions between Mφ and CRC cells can be mediated by soluble molecules, and our prior research indicated that TIPE, a cytoplasmic protein, enhances the secretion of DcR3 from CRC cells [9], we hypothesized that DcR3 might serve as a mediator in CRC cell-Mφ interactions, thereby influencing M2 polarization induced by TIPE-overexpression CRC cells.

To investigate the effect of TIPE-overexpression CRC cells in M2 polarization, we utilized the GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/), an online interactive web server for analyzing gene expression in various cancers. Correlation analysis within this database revealed a significant positive correlation between TIPE expression in CRC and the M2 markers CD206, CD163, and IL-10 (Fig. 1A). Consequently, to further elucidate TIPE’s role, we constructed TIPE differentially expressing CRC stable cell lines. We selected SW480 cells, which exhibit low basal TIPE expression, and HCT116 cells, which have high basal TIPE expression, for stable transfection with a TIPE plasmid or control vector. Using the above approach, we established TIPE-overexpressing (SW480-OE), empty vector control (SW480-V), TIPE-knockdown (HCT116-KD) and empty vector control (HCT116-V) cell lines. Subsequently, CM were prepared by collecting supernatants from SW480-OE and SW480-V cells, which were then mixed 1:1 with complete medium. Murine-derived RAW264.7 macrophages were treated with these CMs for 48 h. qPCR assays demonstrated that CM from TIPE-overexpression CRC cells significantly promoted the expression of M2 marker molecules (CD206, CD163, and IL-10) in RAW264.7 cells, exhibiting a significant difference compared to CM from TIPE low-expressing CRC cells (Fig. 1C). However, no significant differences were observed in the expression of M1 markers (CD80, CD86, and IL-1β) when macrophages were exposed to CM from CRC cells with differential TIPE expression (Fig. 1D). Our group has previously generated SW480-OE and HCT116-KD stable cell lines and verified the critical role of TIPE in regulating macrophage M2 polarization, supporting that the TIPE-mediated modulation of macrophage M2 polarization is consistent across different CRC cell lines [ 7].

To determine if DcR3 is involved, we first analyzed the UALCAN database (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/), an online web resource for comprehensive cancer transcriptome analysis, which indicated high expression of DcR3 in CRC in situ tumors (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, tumor and adjacent paracancerous tissues were collected from 30 consecutively enrolled colorectal cancer patients undergoing surgical resection. qPCR analysis revealed significantly higher DcR3 expression in tumor tissues compared to paracancerous tissues, showing a significant positive correlation with TIPE expression (Fig. 1F). Western blot analysis subsequently detected increased DcR3 expression in TIPE-overexpression CRC cells (Fig. 1G). Moreover, ELISA confirmed elevated DcR3 secretion in the supernatants of TIPE-overexpression CRC cells (Fig. 1H), suggesting that DcR3 could function as a crucial soluble molecule in promoting M2 polarization.

To further explore whether DcR3 derived from TIPE-overexpressing CRC cells regulates macrophage M2 polarization, we mined the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. In the CRC dataset GSE110223 (17 normal tissues and 17 cancer tissues), DcR3 expression was consistent with CD163 expression (Fig. 1I). Additionally, GEPIA database analysis further confirmed that DcR3 was significantly positively correlated with CD206, CD163, and IL-10 in CRC (Fig. 1J). To clarify the direct role of DcR3 in M2 polarization, suspended THP-1 monocytes were induced into adherent M0 macrophages (M0). Subsequently, M0 were treated with exogenous DcR3 for 48 h, using standard M1- and M2-polarized macrophages as positive controls, exogenous DcR3 treatment of M0 was found to upregulate ARG-1 expression (Fig. 1K), qPCR assays demonstrated a significant upregulation in the expression of M2 markers including CD206, CD163, ARG-1, and IL-10 (Fig. 1L). Concurrently, flow cytometry results corroborated these findings, showing a significantly higher proportion of CD206, CD163, and ARG-1-positive macrophages, indicative of an increased M2 phenotype, after DcR3 treatment (Fig. 1M). Finally, to confirm DcR3’s mediating role, exogenous DcR3 was added to SW480-V CM, while an anti-DcR3 antibody was added to SW480-OE CM. These modified CMs were then co-cultured with M0 for 48 h. Flow cytometry results demonstrated that exogenous DcR3 significantly increased the M2 ratio, whereas the anti-DcR3 antibody led to a decreased M2 ratio (Fig. 1N).

Collectively, these results strongly suggest that TIPE promotes M2 polarization predominantly through the secretion of DcR3.

DcR3 exerts its function via binding to HSPG2 in an HS-dependent manner

As a soluble molecule, DcR3 drives tumor progression via immunoregulatory mechanisms [17]. As a multifunctional macromolecule, HSPG2 plays an essential role in Mφ [18] Given their respective established roles, we investigated the potential interplay between DcR3 and HSPG2 in the context of tumor-macrophage interactions.

To explore the mechanisms underlying tumor-macrophage communication, M0 were co-cultured with CM from SW480-OE or SW480-V cells for 48 h, followed by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of the cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction pathway in macrophages (Fig. 2A), indicating active signaling between CRC cells and macrophages. Supporting this hypothesis, volcano plot analysis demonstrated a significant upregulation of HSPG2 in M0 co-cultured with SW480-OE cells compared to controls (Fig. 2B). This initial finding suggested a potential role for TIPE-overexpression CRC cells in modulating macrophage HSPG2 expression. To validate this observation in a clinical context, tumor tissues and adjacent non-tumor tissues were collected from 30 CRC patients. qPCR analysis evidenced that HSPG2 expression was significantly higher in tumor tissues than in adjacent non-tumor tissues, and this upregulation was significantly positively correlated with TIPE expression (Fig. 2C). These clinical correlations suggest that increased HSPG2 levels in tumor-associated macrophages might be driven by tumor-derived factors and contribute to the tumor-promoting microenvironment.

Further mechanistic investigations involved co-culturing M0 with CM derived from SW480-OE, SW480-V, HCT116-KD, and HCT116-V cells for 48 h. qPCR analysis revealed a significant upregulation of HSPG2 expression in macrophages following treatment with CM from TIPE-overexpression CRC cells (Fig. 2D). This observation underscores the capacity of TIPE-overexpression CRC cells to modulate macrophage phenotype through secreted factors. To further characterize the time-dependent effect of TIPE-overexpression CM on HSPG2 expression, M0 were treated with CM from SW480-V or SW480-OE cells for various durations (0 min, 30 min, 2 h, or 6 h). Western blot analysis confirmed a significant and time-dependent upregulation of HSPG2 expression in macrophages treated with SW480-OE CM (Fig. 2E). These consistent findings from both genetic and conditioned medium experiments strongly suggest that tumor-derived factors, specifically those related to TIPE-overexpression CRC cells, induce HSPG2 expression in macrophages, potentially altering their functional state.

Exogenous DcR3 was obtained from the Anti-Cancer Research Center, Xiamen University. The endotoxin level of the DcR3 preparation was rigorously assessed, with the OD4₄₀₅nm growth rate being significantly lower than the endotoxin standard of 0.1 EU/mL and substantially below the 1 EU/mL positive control, thereby meeting the biological endotoxin detection criterion (≤ 0.1 EU/mL) (Fig. 2F). To directly validate the relationship between DcR3 and HSPG2, M0 were treated with exogenous DcR3 for 48 h. qPCR analysis indicated a significant upregulation of HSPG2 expression in macrophages (Fig. 2G), establishing a direct link between soluble DcR3 and HSPG2 induction. Subsequently, a series of experiments further explored this interaction: exogenous DcR3 was added to SW480-V CM, and, conversely, a neutralizing anti-DcR3 antibody was added to SW480-OE CM. These modified CMs were then co-cultured with M0 for 48 h. Western blot results confirmed that HSPG2 expression was positively correlated with DcR3 concentration (Fig. 2H), thereby demonstrating that DcR3 promotes HSPG2 expression. We next explored whether DcR3 exerts downstream functions through cell-surface HSPG2. Molecular docking simulation performed using PyMOL predicted potential binding regions between DcR3 and HSPG2 (Fig. 2I).

Flow cytometric analysis of HSPG2 expression on the surface of viable macrophages revealed that compared with the M0 group, exogenous DcR3 treatment significantly upregulated HSPG2 levels on the M0 membrane. Similarly, HSPG2 expression was markedly elevated on M2 relative to the M0 group, with no statistically significant difference observed between M2 and DcR3-treated M0 cells (Fig. 2J).

To explore the binding mode between DcR3 and cell-surface HSPG2, we conducted heparin competition assays and heparinase III digestion experiments. ELISA results revealed significant differences in supernatant DcR3 concentrations among different treatment groups. Compared with the blank control (M0), DcR3 supplementation alone (M0-DcR3) significantly increased supernatant DcR3 levels. Notably, when cells were pretreated with heparan sulfate (HS) prior to DcR3 addition (M0-HS-DcR3), supernatant DcR3 concentrations were further elevated and substantially exceeded those of the M0-DcR3 group. Similarly, heparinase III treatment (M0-Heparinase III-DcR3) also resulted in a marked increase in supernatant DcR3, reaching levels comparable to those of the M0-HS-DcR3 group. Neither HS treatment alone nor heparinase III treatment alone significantly affected baseline endogenous DcR3 levels in the cell supernatant (Fig. 2K).

Subsequently, cellular proteins from the above groups were harvested to evaluate the activation status of signaling pathways. The results showed that HS and heparinase III treatments inhibited the levels of p-AKT and p-PI3K regardless of exogenous DcR3 administration (Fig. 2L). Immunofluorescence assays further revealed that DcR3 colocalizes with cell-surface HSPG2 (Fig. 2M). Taken together, these results demonstrate that DcR3 functionally binds to cell-surface HSPG2 in a heparan sulfate (HS)-dependent manner, and this interaction initiates downstream intracellular signal transduction.

DcR3–HSPG2 complex activates PI3K, followed by the recruitment and activation of AKT1

DcR3 binding to cell-surface HSPG2 in an HS-dependent manner initiates intracellular signal transduction. As a core kinase of the PI3K/AKT pathway, AKT1 is regulated by upstream PI3K and recruited to the cell membrane [19]. Upon activation, AKT1 translocates to the cytoplasm and nucleus, and participates in multiple biological processes including cell polarization, proliferation and metabolism [20, 21]. We therefore hypothesize that AKT1 acts as a key kinase in HSPG2-mediated intracellular signaling.

In the co-culture system of M0 macrophages with SW480-OE or SW480-V cells, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in M0 cells (Fig. 3A). Volcano plot analysis further demonstrated that AKT1 expression was markedly upregulated in M0 macrophages co-cultured with SW480-OE cells (Fig. 3B). We collected CM from SW480-OE and SW480-V cells. After treating M0 macrophages with the collected CM for 0 min, 30 min, 2 h and 6 h, Western blot results showed that CM from TIPE-overexpressing colorectal cancer cells markedly promoted AKT1 phosphorylation, an effect observed as early as 30 min after treatment (Fig. 3C). Additionally, qPCR analysis revealed a distinct increase in AKT1 expression in M0 macrophages continuously treated with exogenous DcR3 for 48 h (Fig. 3D). To clarify the direct effect of DcR3, we added exogenous DcR3 to the CM of SW480-V cells. Meanwhile, DcR3 neutralizing antibody (anti-DcR3) was supplemented into the CM of SW480-OE cells. M0 macrophages were treated with the above media for 6 h. Western blot results verified that DcR3 significantly promoted AKT1 phosphorylation (Fig. 3E). Analysis based on the GEPIA database revealed a significant positive correlation between HSPG2 and AKT1 expression (Fig. 3F). To investigate the regulatory relationship between them, siRNA was utilized to knock down HSPG2 expression in M0 macrophages. qPCR results revealed a significant reduction in AKT1 expression (Fig. 3G). Western blot assays further confirmed that HSPG2 silencing markedly suppressed AKT1 phosphorylation (Fig. 3H). Collectively, these results suggest that DcR3 effectively activates AKT1 after binding to cell-surface HSPG2 in an HS-dependent manner.

To clarify the function of HSPG2 in the DcR3-mediated signaling pathway, we treated HSPG2-knockdown M0 macrophages with exogenous DcR3 and detected the recruitment of AKT1 on the cell membrane. The results showed that HSPG2 knockdown significantly inhibited DcR3-induced membrane localization of AKT1 (Fig. 3I), indicating that HSPG2 is an essential molecule for efficient AKT1 recruitment to the cell membrane upon DcR3 stimulation.

To dissect the upstream and downstream mechanisms underlying HSPG2-regulated AKT1 membrane localization, we examined PI3K, a canonical upstream molecule of AKT [20]. HSPG2 silencing also decreased PI3K activation (Fig. 3H). We further conducted rescue experiments using the PI3K inhibitor LY294002. Preconditioning cells with LY294002 to suppress PI3K activity significantly abrogated DcR3-triggered phosphorylation of the PI3K p85 subunit and Ser473 phosphorylation of AKT1, verifying that AKT1 activation relies on upstream PI3K signaling. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DcR3 drives activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade, and HSPG2-dependent membrane recruitment of AKT1 serves as an essential prerequisite for complete activation of this pathway.

To dissect the molecular mechanism by which HSPG2 participates in the membrane localization of AKT1, we performed molecular docking simulations using PyMOL. The results predicted potential interaction regions between HSPG2 and AKT1 (Fig. 3K). Immunofluorescence colocalization and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays were further carried out for experimental verification. Co-IP results revealed that the AKT1 antibody specifically precipitated HSPG2 in M2-type macrophages, whereas no obvious specific bands were detected in the IgG negative control group (Fig. 3L). Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated prominent colocalization of HSPG2 (red fluorescence) and AKT1 (green fluorescence) on the macrophage membrane (Fig. 3M). Collectively, results from molecular simulations and cellular experiments demonstrate that HSPG2 is an essential molecule for DcR3-mediated PI3K activation and AKT1 membrane recruitment. HSPG2 associates with membrane-bound signaling complexes containing AKT1. As adaptor proteins or co-receptors mediating the transmembrane linkage between HSPG2 and DcR3 have not been identified in this study, direct physical interaction between the two molecules cannot be confirmed. The observed colocalization and co-immunoprecipitation data suggest that HSPG2 and DcR3 reside within the same membrane signaling complex, cooperatively driving downstream PI3K/AKT activation.

Subsequently, we further verified the effects of M2 macrophages on the proliferation and migration of CRC cells. SW480-OE cells were treated with CM derived from M2 macrophages. Given that CXCL8 exerts its biological functions primarily through its cognate receptors CXCR1 and CXCR2, we next sought to investigate whether these two receptors mediate the pro-tumor effects induced by M2 macrophages.

We first explored whether CXCR1 and CXCR2, the receptors for CXCL8, are expressed in CRC cells. qPCR detection showed that the expression of CXCR1 and CXCR2 was markedly higher in SW480-OE cells than in SW480-V cells (Fig. 7I). Moreover, a slight decrease in weight was observed in the SW480-V-DcR3 group relative to the SW480-V group, while the SW480-OE-anti-DcR3 group showed a minor increase in weight compared to the SW480-OE group (Fig. 8B). These observations suggest potential systemic effects or metabolic alterations associated with TIPE expression and DcR3 modulation, though further investigation is warranted.

Following a 4-week experimental period, mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues, paracancerous lymph nodes, and spleens were harvested for subsequent analyses. Assessment of tumor volumes demonstrated that the SW480-OE group developed significantly larger tumors than the SW480-V group. Furthermore, exogenous DcR3 treatment led to a notable increase in tumor volume in the SW480-V-DcR3 group compared to the untreated SW480-V group. Conversely, the SW480-OE-anti-DcR3 group showed significantly smaller tumor volumes compared to both the SW480-OE group and the SW480-V-DcR3 group (Fig. 8C). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for Ki-67, a proliferation marker, within tumor tissues revealed a significant upregulation following the administration of exogenous DcR3. Conversely, Ki-67 expression was markedly reduced after anti-DcR3 application (Fig. 8D). These findings collectively indicate that DcR3 actively promotes tumor growth and proliferation in vivo.

To further delineate DcR3’s impact on M2 polarization in vivo, flow cytometry was employed to quantify the proportion of M2 in tumor tissues, paracancerous lymph nodes, and spleen. The analysis demonstrated a significant elevation in the M2 population in both paracancerous lymph nodes and spleen following DcR3 administration. Conversely, anti-DcR3 treatment resulted in a substantial decrease in M2 in these sites. While the proportions of CD163⁺ and CD206⁺ M2 in the SW480-OE-anti-DcR3 group remained higher than those in the SW480-V-DcR3 group,the proportion of ARG1⁺ M2 macrophages in lymph nodes was higher in the SW480‑V‑DcR3 group than in the SW480‑OE‑anti‑DcR3 group.Meanwhile, no significant difference was observed in the splenic population of ARG1⁺ macrophagesacross the groups (Fig. 8E–G).

Complementary qPCR analysis further elaborated on the molecular changes, indicating that the expression levels of HSPG2, AKT1, and CXCL8 were significantly higher in tumor tissues from the SW480-OE group compared to the SW480-V group. Similarly, the SW480-V-DcR3 group exhibited elevated expression of these genes relative to the SW480-V group. In contrast, the SW480-OE-anti-DcR3 group showed reduced expression of HSPG2, AKT1, and CXCL8 compared to the SW480-OE group (Fig. 8H). Western blot analysis revealed that DcR3 treatment upregulated HSPG2 expression and increased AKT1 phosphorylation in tumor tissues (Fig. 8I). Additionally, IHC staining of tumor sections confirmed that AKT1 phosphorylation levels were enhanced following DcR3 treatment, and conversely, diminished after anti-DcR3 treatment (Fig. 8J).

Taken together, these in vivo experimental results strongly support our hypothesis that TIPE promotes M2 polarization and colorectal cancer proliferation by modulating DcR3.

Discussion

CRC represents one of the most common malignant tumors globally, characterized by high morbidity and mortality. Its occurrence and progression depend not only on the genetic alterations within tumor cells but also significantly on the TME [22, 23].

TME is a complex ecosystem composed of various cellular and acellular components, and plays a vital role in tumor progression, metastasis and therapeutic resistance [24]. As one of the major immune cells in the TME, TAMs can be polarized into M1 or M2 phenotypes in response to alterations in the TME [25]. The M1 and M2 phenotypes of TAMs are not static or mutually exclusive, and the two phenotypes can dynamically interconvert [26]. Specifically, M2, as important immune cells within the TME, exert a critical influence on the initiation, development, and metastasis of CRC through their polarization status and function [27]. This study investigated the regulatory mechanisms of TIPE, DcR3, HSPG2, PI3K, AKT1, and other molecules in M2 polarization and CRC proliferation, thereby elucidating the complex interactions of these molecules within the TME.

TIPE is a vital immunoregulatory protein [28], and numerous unresolved questions about its function in macrophage polarization remain to be investigated. Our previous research demonstrated that CRC cells with TIPE overexpression facilitate M2 macrophage polarization, whereas the exact underlying mechanism has not been fully clarified. To further dissect the detailed mechanism by which TIPE-overexpressing CRC cells drive M2 polarization, we first performed bioinformatic analysis via the GEPIA database. The results confirmed that TIPE expression is significantly positively correlated with the M2 macrophage markers CD206, CD163 and IL-10 in colorectal cancer tissues. Subsequently, we collected CM from SW480-OE and SW480-V cells and treated RAW264.7 macrophages with the collected CM. The cellular experiments further verified that conditioned medium derived from TIPE-overexpressing CRC cells accelerates the progression of M2 polarization in RAW264.7 macrophages.

Cellular interactions are mediated by both membrane-bound and soluble molecules. We therefore questioned which soluble substance in the supernatant of TIPE-overexpression CRC cells mediates M2 polarization. Our previous findings indicated that high TIPE expression in CRC promotes DcR3 secretion [9]. As a soluble molecule with diverse regulatory functions, we hypothesized that DcR3 might play a key role in M2 polarization.

Existing studies report that DcR3, a soluble immunomodulatory protein, significantly affects Mφ polarization and functional status [8, 29]. Research has shown that DcR3 expression in tumors promotes Mφ polarization toward the M2 phenotype, thereby fostering tumor development [30]. Concurrently, DcR3 also inhibits M1 production by reducing reactive oxygen species production and lysosomal rupture, thereby suppressing monosodium urate-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation [11]. In this study, we initially clarified the significant correlation between DcR3 expression and M2 infiltration in CRC through an online database. Following this initial validation, we applied exogenous DcR3 and anti-DcR3 to M0 cells to further confirm that TIPE-overexpression CRC promotes M2 polarization via DcR3.

As a soluble molecule, DcR3 exerts its regulatory effect on Mφ by binding to a corresponding receptor [8]. Through literature retrieval and RNA-Seq screening, we identified HSPG2. In vitro experiments confirmed that conditioned medium from colorectal cancer cells with high DcR3 secretion upregulated HSPG2 expression in macrophages in a time- and concentration-dependent manner. Clinical sample analysis also revealed that HSPG2 expression was markedly higher in CRC tumor tissues than in adjacent normal tissues, and it was positively correlated with TIPE expression. HSPG2 expression is closely correlated with macrophage polarization, and HSPG2 promotes M2 polarization by modulating NF-κB signaling [31]. As a member of the tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily, OPG can bind to cell-surface HSPGs [32]. DcR3 belongs to the same superfamily and shares the highest homology with OPG [8], suggesting possible functional similarities between the two proteins. We therefore hypothesized that DcR3 facilitates M2 macrophage polarization through binding to HSPG2.

Molecular docking and immunofluorescence analyses identified potential interaction regions and validated the functional binding between DcR3 and HSPG2. Flow cytometry further demonstrated that DcR3 treatment significantly upregulated surface HSPG2 expression in macrophages, which phenotypically matched M2 macrophage characteristics. Heparin competition and heparinase III digestion assays revealed that competitive blockade or enzymatic degradation of HSPG2-attached HS glycan chains markedly increased the abundance of free DcR3 in the supernatant. These results indicate that HSPG2 serves as the major HS-dependent anchoring site for DcR3 on the macrophage membrane.

Meanwhile, disruption of this HS-dependent interaction markedly decreased PI3K/AKT phosphorylation, indicating that DcR3 binds to membrane-surface HSPG2 in an HS-dependent manner to activate downstream PI3K/AKT signaling molecules.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of RNA-Seq data revealed significant activation of the PI3K-AKT pathway. Interestingly, AKT1, a key molecule in this pathway, showed significant differential expression in macrophages stimulated for 30 min with CM collected from TIPE differentially expressed CRC cells. We also found that DcR3 activates AKT1. HSPG2, functioning as both a plasma membrane protein and an extracellular matrix protein, plays an important role in intercellular signaling [33]. We thus hypothesized that AKT1 activation in macrophages is mediated by HSPG2. Combining Western blot, qPCR, molecular docking, immunofluorescence and Co-IP assays, our study collectively verified that HSPG2 coexists with membrane-recruited AKT1 in plasma membrane signaling complexes and jointly participates in AKT activation. As a cytosolic protein, the membrane recruitment mechanism of AKT1 was further verified in this study, consistent with previous reports. Briefly, PI3K catalyzes PIP3 production. AKT1 is recruited to the plasma membrane via specific binding of its N-terminal PH domain to PIP3, followed by full activating phosphorylation mediated by PDK1 and mTORC2 [34–36]. Our data showed that exogenous DcR3 treatment increased the protein level of membrane-localized AKT1. Accordingly, we hypothesize that DcR3 functionally interacts with macrophage membrane HSPG2 through heparan sulfate (HS) glycan chains. This process initiates PI3K signaling, facilitates AKT1 membrane recruitment, incorporates AKT1 into HSPG2-containing membrane signaling complexes, and ultimately drives AKT1 activation. This signaling cascade promotes AKT1 recruitment to the plasma membrane, where AKT1 is incorporated into HSPG2-containing membrane signaling complexes for activation. Once activated, AKT1 translocates back into the cytoplasm to exert its kinase activity. To further verify the direct effects of HSPG2 and AKT1 on M2 polarization, silencing HSPG2 and AKT1 expression in Mφ using siRNA significantly decreased the degree of M2 polarization, demonstrating that TIPE-induced M2 polarization is HSPG2/AKT1-dependent.

Following Mφ polarization, specific functional changes occur, with significant implications for CRC biological behavior. To thoroughly investigate these functional changes after M2 polarization, we systematically studied them through a series of in vitro experiments. These experiments confirmed that exogenous DcR3 significantly enhanced Mφ proliferation and migration.

Mechanistically, inhibition of AKT1 activation reduced CXCL8 expression. CXCR1/2, the receptors for CXCL8 [37, 38], were highly expressed in SW480-OE cells. Blockade of CXCR1/2 markedly attenuates the proliferation and migration of CRC cells. Notably, CXCR1 exerts a stronger regulatory effect on CRC biological functions than CXCR2. This discrepancy may be attributed to the distinct ligand affinity and binding spectrum of the two receptors: CXCR1 is a high-affinity receptor specific to CXCL8, while CXCR2 binds not only CXCL8 but also other chemokines including CXCL1 and CXCL2.

The recruitment of M2 macrophages to tumor lesions preliminarily clarifies the potential mechanism underlying the accumulation of M2 macrophages in the CRC microenvironment. Meanwhile, M2 macrophages significantly boost the proliferation and migration of CRC cells, further demonstrating their pro-tumor functions within the TME.

This study only used the SW480 cell line without validation in HCT116 cells. CXCL8 expression was merely detected via AKT1 knockdown and AKT1 inhibitor treatment. Follow-up experiments using recombinant CXCL8, neutralizing antibodies and overexpression rescue assays will be performed to further verify its biological function.

To simulate physiological conditions, we conducted subcutaneous tumor formation experiments in nude mice. Nude mice exhibit functional deficiencies in T and B cells due to congenital thymic aplasia; however, Mφ development and function, as intrinsic immune cells, are less impacted by the thymus [39]. We injected TIPE differentially expressed CRC cells into the dorsal subcutis of nude mice to construct a xenograft tumor model, followed by DcR3 and anti-DcR3 interventions according to the experimental design. Through a complex isolation and extraction process, we obtained single-cell suspensions from transplanted tumors, spleen, and lymph nodes. We then analyzed Mφ content in each tissue by flow cytometry. Results showed an increased M2 population in the graft tumor, spleen, and lymph nodes. We prepared some tumor tissues for subsequent experiments after RNA and protein extraction and immunohistochemical section preparation. Results revealed elevated expression of Ki-67, HSPG2, AKT1, p-AKT1, and CXCL8 in the DcR3-treated group. These subcutaneous tumor formation experiments in nude mice further confirmed that DcR3 promotes Mφ M2 polarization and CRC proliferation.

In this study, we systematically investigated the roles of TIPE, DcR3, HSPG2, and AKT1 in M2 polarization and their effects on the CRC microenvironment. These molecules play important roles in M2 polarization and function, and their aberrant expression associates closely with CRC progression. These findings are expected to offer new strategies for the diagnosis and treatment of CRC. Future studies can further explore the specific mechanisms by which HSPG2 and AKT1 regulate M2 polarization. Additionally, further validation of their feasibility as therapeutic targets for CRC in combination with the analysis of clinical samples is warranted.
