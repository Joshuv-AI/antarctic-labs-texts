Topology-guided dual-masked contrastive learning for weakly supervised cell-type annotation in single-cell multi-omics
Authors: Yi Zhang, Longneng Ran, Yu Liang, Xiaohua Wang, Hanyan Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSingle-cell multi-omics technologies provide complementary views of cellular states, but are challenged by pervasive feature sparsity, pronounced cross-modality heterogeneity, and a critical shortage of labeled cells, hindering accurate cell-type annotation. We propose dual-masked contrastive learning (DMCL), a topology-guided weakly supervised cross-modal representation learning framework that addresses these challenges within a unified architecture. DMCL first constructs modality-specific K-nearest neighbor graphs to capture cell–cell topological relationships within each omics layer. A dual masking strategy combining global column-wise masking and local random masking is then applied to simulate realistic feature missingness and regularize the model against technical noise. Topology-aware latent representations are subsequently derived through a graph autoencoder that faithfully preserves the local cellular neighborhood structure of each modality. Cross-modal integration is achieved via a multi-head self-attention module that dynamically weights modality-specific features according to per-cell informativeness, enabling context-adaptive modality fusion. To further improve representation quality, DMCL incorporates a dual contrastive objective that jointly enforces intra-cell view consistency and inter-cell neighborhood structure, thereby enhancing both cross-modality alignment and structural preservation in the latent space. Extensive experiments on diverse real and simulated datasets demonstrate that DMCL achieves accurate cell-type annotation and superior clustering performance under minimal label supervision, establishing a principled and scalable foundation for weakly supervised single-cell multimodal analysis.Keywords: single-cell multi-omics, cell-type annotation, data augmentation, contrastive learning, weakly supervised learning

Introduction

Recent advances in single-cell sequencing technologies have substantially improved the characterization of cellular heterogeneity at unprecedented resolution [1]. In particular, emerging single-cell multi-omics platforms, such as transcripts, epitopes, and accessibility sequencing (TEA-seq) [2], nuclear protein epitope, chromatin accessibility, and transcriptome sequencing (NEAT-seq) [3], and DOGMA-seq [4], a trimodal method named after the central dogma of molecular biology, enable the simultaneous profiling of chromatin accessibility through single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (scATAC-seq), gene expression through single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), and protein abundance through antibody-derived tags (ADT) within individual cells. Compared with unimodal assays, these technologies provide complementary regulatory information across multiple molecular layers, enabling a more comprehensive characterization of cellular identity and state [5–7].

Cell-type annotation is a fundamental task in single-cell analysis, aiming to assign biologically meaningful identities to individual cells [8]. Accurate annotation is critical for downstream analyses, including reference atlas construction, differential expression analysis, developmental trajectory inference, and cell–cell communication studies [9]. The integration of multi-omics data offers richer biological evidence for resolving subtle cellular states and rare populations, making multimodal cell-type annotation an important research problem [10].

Despite these advantages, accurate annotation of single-cell multi-omics data remains challenging. These datasets are typically high-dimensional and highly sparse, particularly for scATAC-seq, which complicates robust feature extraction [11, 12]. In addition, different omics modalities exhibit heterogeneous statistical distributions, noise profiles, and biological semantics, making effective cross-modal integration difficult [13]. Moreover, cellular relationships are highly nonlinear and modality-dependent, requiring models to jointly preserve modality-specific topology and shared representations [14]. These challenges are further exacerbated under weakly supervised settings with limited labeled cells.

Existing methods only partially address these issues. Traditional approaches, such as reference-based [15] and marker-driven methods [16], rely heavily on prior biological knowledge and are limited in capturing complex nonlinear relationships in multimodal data [17]. Recent computational studies have explored more advanced representation-learning strategies for different types of single-cell data. For scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq, approaches based on deep matrix factorization, graph regularization, contrastive learning, and generative models have been used to alleviate sparsity and learn robust cellular representations [18, 19]. Related graph-based generative frameworks have also been extended to spatial transcriptomics by jointly modeling gene-expression profiles and spatial neighborhood information [20]. Meanwhile, transformer- and large language model-based approaches have emerged as promising tools for genomics and single-cell analysis, with applications ranging from representation learning, batch correction, clustering, cell-type annotation, and perturbation-response prediction [21, 22]. Despite these advances, most existing methods focus on single-modality, spatial transcriptomics, or general-purpose representation learning and few are specifically designed for weakly supervised annotation of paired single-cell multi-omics data.

More directly related deep learning approaches have improved multi-omics representation learning through attention mechanisms [23], graph neural networks [24, 25], and multimodal fusion strategies [26]. However, they still suffer from three key limitations: (i) reliance on fixed or simplistic fusion strategies that fail to adaptively model modality-specific contributions; (ii) insufficient preservation of both modality-specific local topology and cross-modal shared structure; and (iii) degraded performance under weak supervision due to limited labeled data and vulnerability to noise and missing features.

To overcome these limitations, we propose dual-masked contrastive learning (DMCL), a unified, weakly supervised framework for single-cell multi-omics cell-type annotation (Fig. 1). DMCL consists of three tightly integrated modules. First, a dual-masked representation learning strategy applies complementary global column-wise and local stochastic masking to simulate realistic feature sparsity and modality-specific missingness, enabling robust representation learning under noisy and incomplete observations. Second, a topology-aware adaptive fusion module, which combines graph-based representation learning with multi-head self-attention, preserves modality-specific topological structures while dynamically capturing cross-modal dependencies, thereby reconciling heterogeneous feature spaces without compromising intra-modality structural fidelity. Third, a dual-view contrastive objective simultaneously enforces intra-cell cross-view consistency across stochastically augmented views and inter-cell structural consistency across topology-related cells.

Overview of the DMCL framework. DMCL operates in two stages. (Phase I) During unsupervised pre-training, modality-specific KNN graphs are constructed and encoded by a graph autoencoder; the resulting representations are fused via multi-head self-attention and jointly optimized with a dual contrastive objective. (Phase II) During supervised fine-tuning, the pre-trained encoder is adapted with a small subset of labeled cells, and the resulting embeddings are used for cell-type classification.

Data preprocessing

The model inputs comprise three data modalities: scRNA-seq gene expression matrices, scATAC-seq peak count matrices, and ADT cell surface protein abundances. Each modality was preprocessed independently prior to integration. For scRNA-seq data, the raw count matrices were log-normalized, and the top 3000 highly variable genes (HVGs) were selected [27]. Principal component analysis (PCA) [28] was subsequently applied for dimensionality reduction to generate the final transcriptomic inputs. For scATAC-seq data, the peak count matrices underwent term frequency-inverse document frequency [29] transformation, followed by dimensionality reduction using latent semantic indexing (LSI). For ADT data, given the limited feature space of surface proteins, all protein features were retained and log-normalized without further dimensionality reduction [30].

These masking operators are representation-learning augmentations rather than explicit generative models of technical dropout. They are designed to approximate two broad forms of information loss—feature-level and entry-level corruption—but do not reproduce the distinct zero-generation mechanisms of scRNA-seq, scATAC-seq, and ADT assays.

Evaluation metrics

To assess cell-type annotation performance, three widely used classification metrics are adopted, including Accuracy (ACC), weighted F1-score (F1_weighted), and macro F1-score (F1_macro). ACC measures the overall proportion of correctly classified samples, whereas F1_weighted and F1_macro provide complementary evaluations by accounting for class imbalance and the performance on rare cell populations.

To further evaluate the quality of the learned representations, K-Means clustering is performed on the latent embeddings. Clustering performance is assessed using five standard metrics, including Adjusted Rand Index (ARI), Normalized Mutual Information (NMI), Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI), Purity Score (PS), and Homogeneity Score (HS). ARI, NMI, and AMI measure the agreement between clustering assignments and ground-truth labels, while PS and HS evaluate cluster purity and within-cluster label consistency.

The mathematical definitions of all evaluation metrics are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Model performance analysis

Cell-type annotation

Cell-type annotation performance was evaluated under weakly supervised settings using three labeling ratios (2%, 6%, and 10%). To ensure fair evaluation on class-imbalanced datasets, at least one cell from each cell type was guaranteed in the training partition during random splitting. The NEAT-seq dataset, comprising seven closely related CD4 T-cell subtypes, was selected as the primary benchmark for trimodal cell-type annotation. As shown in Fig. 2a, DMCL achieved the highest median ACC, weighted F1-score, and macro F1-score among all ten methods at each labeling ratio. The relatively narrow score distributions across 10 independent runs further indicate robust performance with respect to the selection of labeled cells. Notably, DMCL maintained a clear advantage even with only 2% labeled cells, particularly in macro F1-score, indicating more balanced performance across cell populations. As the labeling ratio increased from 2% to 10%, all three metrics improved steadily, while the performance advantage of DMCL over the competing methods was largely maintained. These results indicate that DMCL can learn effective multimodal representations under extremely limited supervision and benefit further from additional labeled data.

To assess cell-type-level performance, we further analyzed the confusion matrices under the challenging setting of 2% labeled cells (Fig. 2b). DMCL showed a clear diagonal pattern across all seven CD4 T-cell subtypes, indicating balanced recognition of both abundant and more challenging cell populations. Although scMHNN and Matilda also successfully recognized all seven subtypes, they exhibited more off-diagonal confusion than DMCL, while several other methods failed to recognize specific subtypes. For example, MultiKano and scMoAnno failed to correctly identify C2, scMoAnno also missed C7, and MOSGAT, scMoMtF, CellPredX, and scMGAT showed limited recognition of C1. In contrast, DMCL maintained clear class-specific signals across all subtypes and substantially reduced cross-subtype misclassification. This cell-type-level performance is consistent with its superior macro F1-score, highlighting its improved discrimination across heterogeneous cell populations rather than gains limited to dominant classes. DMCL was additionally evaluated using only 0.5% labeled cells (Supplementary Fig. S42 and Supplementary Table S12). Despite using one-quarter of the labeled data available to the competing methods in Supplementary Fig. S42, DMCL remained competitive across the eight real datasets.

Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) [47] visualization was further used to evaluate the quality of learned latent representations under the relatively challenging setting of 2% labeled cells (Fig. 3). Among the 10 methods, DMCL showed the closest agreement between the ground-truth and predicted label distributions (Fig. 3a), largely preserving the spatial organization of the major cell populations and the distinct C2 population. In contrast, several competing methods produced substantial changes in the label composition of individual regions. For example, scMoAnno assigned a large proportion of cells to C3, whereas MOSGAT, scMoMtF, CellPredX, and scMGAT showed evident regional over- or under-representation of specific subtypes. Visualization of correctly and incorrectly annotated cells further confirmed the superior annotation consistency of DMCL (Fig. 3b). DMCL achieved the highest accuracy (77.0%) and showed fewer and less extensively distributed misclassified cells. The remaining errors were mainly located in overlapping regions of closely related CD4 T-cell subtypes. Together with Fig. 2b, these results indicate that DMCL learns more annotation-informative multimodal representations under limited supervision.

Cell clustering

The clustering quality of latent representations learned by different methods was evaluated across all 11 benchmark datasets using K-means clustering. Figure 4a–e summarizes the average values of five clustering metrics over 10 independent runs under the 2% labeling setting, together with the overall score of each method. DMCL achieved the highest overall PS (0.873), HS (0.793), AMI (0.753), and NMI (0.799), and the second-highest ARI (0.656), following scMGAT (0.719). Although scMGAT performed better according to ARI, particularly on several simulated and bimodal datasets, DMCL exhibited a more balanced advantage across the complete set of clustering criteria. Overall, DMCL achieved the best or near-best performance across all five metrics on trimodal, bimodal, and simulated datasets, demonstrating that the learned embeddings effectively capture biologically meaningful cell-type structures across diverse data modalities. This indicates that DMCL preserves biologically meaningful cell-population structures under diverse modality configurations and data characteristics. These results suggest that topology-guided neighborhood modeling and contrastive representation learning jointly produce robust and discriminative latent representations, particularly when supervision is extremely limited. Results obtained under the 6% and 10% labeling settings are provided in Supplementary Figs S31 and S32, respectively, and they show similar overall trends.

To further assess clustering quality, UMAP visualizations were generated for the PBMC dataset (Fig. 5). DMCL produced more compact clusters with clearer separation between cell populations. In contrast, competing methods exhibited varying degrees of cluster overlap or fragmentation. Specifically, MultiKano captured several distinct cluster structures but still showed partial overlap among closely related cell populations. scMoAnno and scMHNN produced more dispersed embeddings with reduced intra-cluster compactness, whereas MOSGAT and Matilda showed less distinct cluster boundaries and increased inter-cell mixing. scMoMtF produced a comparatively fragmented representation with several disconnected population groups. Although CellPredX and scECDA separated some peripheral populations, substantial mixing or fragmentation remained in their main components. scMGAT produced highly separated clusters and achieved the highest ARI on PBMC, whereas DMCL provided a more favorable overall balance across the other clustering criteria, obtaining the highest PS, HS, AMI, and NMI on this dataset (Fig. 4). Collectively, these results demonstrate that DMCL improves both inter-cluster separability and intra-cluster compactness while preserving global structural consistency, yielding latent representations that are well suited for downstream clustering analysis.

Marker genes identification

To characterize the biological properties of the predicted clusters, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified within each cluster using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The top three DEGs ranked by adjusted statistical significance were selected as candidate marker genes for downstream biological interpretation. As shown in Fig. 6, the predicted clusters exhibited distinct transcriptional signatures, with the selected marker genes showing clear differential expression patterns across clusters, indicating high intra-cluster coherence and strong inter-cluster separability at the transcriptomic level.

Marker gene expression profiles across predicted clusters in the PBMC dataset. Stacked violin plots show the expression distributions of the top three differentially expressed marker genes identified for each predicted cluster.

The biological relevance of these genes was first assessed by cross-referencing them with the CellMarker database [48]. A substantial proportion of the DEGs identified by DMCL corresponded to previously reported cell-type markers. Specifically, SLC4A10 and IL2RA were associated with CD4 T-cell populations [49, 50], whereas MS4A1 was specifically enriched in Cluster 5, consistent with its established role as a B-cell marker [51]. In addition, BCL11B, a canonical marker of CD4 and CD8 T cells [52, 53], showed high expression in T-cell-associated clusters, while CLEC4C, a well-established marker of plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) [54], was specifically enriched in Cluster 18. These observations support the biological validity of the predicted cluster assignments.

Beyond established markers, INPP4B and PLXDC2 showed cluster-specific expression but were not documented as corresponding markers in CellMarker. We therefore consider these genes as candidate cluster-associated markers that require validation using independent datasets or targeted experiments.

To further assess the functional coherence of the identified DEGs, we performed GO Biological Process over-representation analysis and pre-ranked KEGG enrichment analysis. The GO analysis revealed cluster-specific programs (Supplementary Fig. S43). The KEGG analysis further identified cluster-specific enrichment patterns across multiple functional pathways, providing complementary support for the biological interpretability of the inferred clusters (Supplementary Fig. S44).

To verify that the identified clusters reflect genuine biological heterogeneity rather than technical artifacts, doublet scores were computed using Scrublet [55] and projected onto the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) embedding derived from the learned multimodal representations (Fig. 7). Most cells exhibited low doublet scores, whereas high-scoring cells (>0.5) constituted only a small fraction and were sparsely distributed across the embedding space rather than concentrated within specific clusters. Cross-referencing cluster assignments (Fig. 7a) with the doublet score distribution (Fig. 7b) revealed no discernible enrichment of high-scoring doublets in any predicted cluster. This indicates that the observed cluster structure is unlikely to be driven by doublet contamination, further supporting the reliability of the differential expression analysis and marker gene interpretation.

Ablation studies

The ablation analysis comprised three complementary components: module ablation, encoder architecture comparison, and modality ablation. The module ablation study included six variants: removal of the complete dual contrastive learning module (w/o DCL), removal of the dual masking strategy (w/o DM), removal of intra-cell contrastive learning (w/o Intra-CL), removal of inter-cell contrastive learning (w/o Inter-CL), removal of global masking (w/o GM), and removal of local masking (w/o LM). The modality ablation study included six variants: removal of RNA (w/o RNA), removal of ATAC (w/o ATAC), removal of ADT (w/o ADT), RNA-only input (RNA-only), ATAC-only input (ATAC-only), and ADT-only input (ADT-only).

Contribution of contrastive learning and masking components

To disentangle the contributions of the two contrastive objectives and two masking strategies, we extended the component-wise ablation study by removing each component separately. Cell-type annotation results for NEAT-seq, DOGMA-seq, and MA-2020-1 are shown in Fig. 8a–c, with results for the remaining five datasets provided in Supplementary Fig. S33. The corresponding clustering results across all eight datasets are reported in Supplementary Fig. S34.

Ablation study results on representative real-world datasets. (a–c) Cell-type annotation performance under module ablation on the NEAT-seq, DOGMA-seq, and MA-2020-1 datasets, respectively. (d–f) Cell-type annotation performance under modality ablation on the NEAT-seq, DOGMA-seq, and MA-2020-1 datasets, respectively. Performance was evaluated using ACC, weighted F1-score, and macro F1-score.

Removing Intra-CL generally resulted in a larger performance reduction than removing Inter-CL, particularly on NEAT-seq (Fig. 8a), BMMC (Supplementary Fig. S33d), PBMC (Supplementary Fig. S33c), and 10X_multiome (Supplementary Fig. S33e). This finding highlights the importance of maintaining representation consistency for the same cell across the global and local masked views. Nevertheless, removing Inter-CL also consistently degraded performance, indicating its complementary role in preserving topology-guided relationships among cells and maintaining cell population structure. Removing both contrastive objectives led to a more pronounced decline, especially on NEAT-seq and BMMC. Similar trends were observed for clustering performance (Supplementary Fig. S34), where w/o DCL showed a markedly smaller radar profile on NEAT-seq and reduced performance across several datasets.

The global and local masking strategies also made complementary contributions. Removing global masking generally caused a larger reduction than removing local masking, particularly on DOGMA-seq (Fig. 8b) and HUMAN (Fig. S33b), suggesting that perturbing common latent dimensions across the cell population provides an effective self-supervised regularization signal. Local masking also contributed to performance by introducing cell-specific perturbations in the preprocessed representation space. Removing the complete dual masking strategy (w/o DM) consistently reduced performance across the evaluation metrics, although the magnitude of degradation was generally smaller than that observed after removing DCL. These results suggest that the two masking schemes expose the encoder to complementary population-shared and cell-specific perturbation patterns, thereby promoting more robust feature representations and improved generalization.

Encoder architecture comparison

To further evaluate the choice of encoder architecture, we compared GAE with two alternative architectures, MLP and GAT, on three representative datasets (Supplementary Fig. S35). Although GAT achieved slightly higher performance on some datasets, it incurred substantially greater training time and GPU memory consumption. In contrast, MLP had lower computational cost but generally weaker and less balanced performance due to its lack of explicit cell–cell topology modeling. Overall, GAE provided a favorable balance between predictive performance and computational efficiency across the evaluated datasets and tasks. These results support the use of GAE as an effective and computationally efficient encoder for topology-aware representation learning in DMCL.

Contribution of multi-omics modalities

The contribution of each omics modality was further assessed by examining performance changes under modality-specific ablations. Cell-type annotation results for three representative datasets are shown in Fig. 8d–f, with results for the remaining five datasets provided in Supplementary Fig. S36. Clustering results across all eight datasets are summarized in Supplementary Fig. S37 using PS, HS, AMI, ARI, and NMI.

The annotation results reveal that modality importance is dataset dependent. Removing RNA caused pronounced performance reductions on HUMAN (Fig. S36b), MA-2020-1 (Fig. 8f), and BMMC (Supplementary Fig. S36d). RNA-only input retained performance close to that of the complete model on DOGMA-seq (Fig. 8e) and TEA-seq (Fig. S36a), further demonstrating the strong discriminative information provided by transcriptomic measurements. However, RNA was not uniformly dominant. On NEAT-seq (Fig. 8d), removing ATAC produced a larger reduction in ACC and weighted F1-score than removing RNA, whereas on PBMC, removing ATAC caused the clearest decrease in macro F1-score. These findings indicate that chromatin-accessibility information can be particularly important for resolving closely related or less abundant populations in specific datasets. ADT-only input generally yielded the weakest annotation performance among the trimodal datasets, especially on NEAT-seq and DOGMA-seq. Nevertheless, this does not imply that ADT is uninformative. On NEAT-seq, removing ADT affected clustering more strongly than annotation performance, demonstrating that protein measurements can contribute to preserving intrinsic population structure even when their effect on classification accuracy is relatively small.

Collectively, these results refine our original interpretation of modality importance. RNA provides the primary discriminative signal in several datasets, but its dominance is not universal. ATAC and ADT contribute complementary regulatory and protein-level information, respectively, and their relative importance varies with dataset composition and downstream task. Integrating all available modalities therefore provides the most consistently robust performance across annotation and clustering, while DMCL remains applicable to datasets with different modality combinations.

Parameter sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the sensitivity of DMCL to key hyperparameters, parameter analyses were conducted on the HUMAN and NEAT-seq dataset. Seven parameters were investigated, including the number of neighbors in the KNN graph (K), the masking ratio, the contrastive learning balance parameter α, the sampling weight, the latent dimension, the number of attention heads, and the contrastive learning temperature τ.

Effect of neighbor number

The influence of the KNN graph neighbor number was examined over . As shown in Fig. 9a and d, both clustering and annotation performance improved as K increased from 10 to 30, with the highest or near-highest values observed across most evaluation metrics at K = 30. Further increases in K led to moderate performance reductions, particularly for macro F1-score. A similar trend was observed for clustering metrics, where the radar area reached its maximum at K = 30. These results suggest that an appropriate neighborhood size facilitates the preservation of informative local cellular relationships, whereas excessively large neighborhoods may introduce redundant or less informative connections and thereby reduce representation quality. Importantly, the observed predictive robustness does not establish the biological correctness of individual KNN edges. Instead, these edges should be interpreted as local similarity priors inferred from the observed feature space, and their quality remains dependent on the underlying modality, feature representation, and preprocessing procedure.

(d–f) Effects of different parameter settings on cell annotation performance measured by ACC, weighted F1-score, and macro F1-score.

Effect of masking ratio

The impact of the masking ratio was evaluated over . As shown in Fig. 9b and e, annotation performance remained relatively stable as the masking ratio increased from 0.05 to 0.15, with the highest performance generally observed at a masking ratio of 0.15. In particular, macro F1-score showed noticeable improvement under moderate masking. When the masking ratio exceeded 0.15, performance declined across most metrics, indicating that excessive masking may remove informative biological signals. Consistent trends were observed for clustering performance. These results suggest that moderate masking provides effective supervision during representation learning while avoiding substantial information loss.

Effect of contrastive loss weight

The influence of the contrastive learning balance parameter α was investigated using values ranging from 0.1 to 0.5. As shown in Fig. 9c and f, clustering and annotation performance improved progressively as α increased from 0.1 to 0.3, with the highest or near-highest values achieved at α = 0.3. Performance declined slightly when α was further increased to 0.4 and 0.5, particularly for macro F1-score. Similar trends were observed across clustering metrics, where the largest radar area was obtained at α = 0.3. These findings suggest that an appropriate balance between inter-cell and intra-cell contrastive objectives facilitates the learning of informative multimodal representations.

Effects of additional parameters

We further examined the sensitivity of DMCL to three architectural and optimization hyperparameters that were not considered in the initial analysis: the number of attention heads, the GAE hidden dimension, and the contrastive temperature τ (Supplementary Fig. S39). On NEAT-seq, DMCL remained relatively stable across different numbers of attention heads and temperature values, whereas the HUMAN dataset showed clearer optima at four attention heads and τ =0.5. Increasing the latent dimension from 32 to 128 consistently improved the four evaluation metrics on both datasets, while further increasing it to 256 produced no additional benefit and generally resulted in a slight decline. These observations suggest that an excessively small latent space may restrict representational capacity, whereas further increasing model complexity does not necessarily improve performance. Overall, four attention heads, a latent dimension of 128, and a contrastive temperature of 0.5 provided the most favorable and consistent balance across the two datasets and were therefore used as the default settings. Importantly, DMCL did not exhibit a severe performance collapse within the examined ranges, although suboptimal configurations, particularly a hidden dimension of 32 or extreme temperature values on HUMAN, resulted in noticeable performance degradation.

Overall, DMCL maintained competitive performance across a broad range of parameter settings, demonstrating limited sensitivity to moderate variations in key hyperparameters. The results further suggest that appropriate neighborhood construction, masking intensity, and contrastive learning balance contribute to improved cell-type annotation and clustering performance.

Computational efficiency and scalability analysis

To improve the computational efficiency of DMCL without altering its original learning objective, we adopted sparse graph representations and exact block-wise similarity computation. Importantly, the original all-pairs contrastive objective is fully preserved.

All efficiency experiments were conducted on the same NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 Laptop GPU. Compared with the original dense implementation, the optimized DMCL achieved 1.36× and 2.09× speedups on the HUMAN and NEAT-seq datasets, respectively, while reducing peak GPU memory consumption by 35.1% and 88.2%. The dense implementation encountered out-of-memory errors on DOGMA-seq and BMMC, whereas the optimized implementation successfully processed both datasets. When compared with nine competing methods under identical hardware and training settings, DMCL exhibited competitive runtime and maintained peak GPU memory consumption below approximately 1.03 GB across the five benchmark datasets (Fig. 11 and Supplementary Fig. S40).

We further evaluated scalability by subsampling the BMMC dataset from 1,000 to 29,486 cells. Peak GPU memory consumption increased approximately linearly from 79.2 MB to 1.80 GB, whereas training time increased from 13.2 to 933.3 s (Supplementary Fig. S41). The superlinear increase in runtime is attributable to the complexity of the exact all-pairs contrastive objective, where d denotes the embedding dimension. In contrast, graph propagation is reduced from dense computation to sparse computation. Nevertheless, all evaluated dataset sizes were processed successfully without out-of-memory errors. These results indicate that the optimized implementation substantially improves the computational scalability of DMCL while preserving its predictive performance.

Conclusion

Cell-type annotation and clustering of single-cell multi-omics data remain challenging because of high dimensionality, severe feature sparsity, and limited availability of reliable labels. To address these challenges, we proposed DMCL, a unified weakly supervised framework that integrates dual-masked representation learning, topology-guided multimodal fusion, and dual contrastive optimization for multimodal cell-type annotation and clustering. Extensive experiments on 11 benchmark datasets spanning trimodal, bimodal, and simulated settings demonstrated that DMCL achieved competitive or superior performance across a range of evaluation metrics under limited supervision. Notably, DMCL maintained useful annotation performance with as little as 0.5% labeled cells, further demonstrating its applicability to highly label-scarce settings. Functional enrichment analysis also provided additional support for the biological coherence of the learned representations, although candidate markers identified by the model still require validation using independent datasets or experimental evidence.

The current framework is primarily designed for fully paired multimodal data and closed-set annotation. Its extension to unpaired or partially matched modalities will require explicit cross-modal alignment, while the recognition of previously unseen cell populations will require an open-set annotation mechanism. Addressing these challenges, together with adaptive graph construction and negative sampling for heterogeneous cell populations and improved computational efficiency for large-scale datasets, will be important directions for future development.

Overall, DMCL provides a unified framework for weakly supervised multimodal single-cell analysis, and it demonstrates the potential of jointly exploiting multimodal topology, complementary data perturbations, and contrastive representation learning for cell-type annotation and clustering.
