Superior dorsal nigral hyperintensity depiction at 7 T MRI using CLEAR-DESS improves diagnosis performance of Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Sai Li, Ruiting Chen, Qing Li, Yicheng Hsu, Jiaqi He, Yihang Su, Dongcui Wang, Hao Zhou, Weiwei Zhang, Li Meng, Shulin Liu, Jingyi Tang, Chenyu Li, Peng Lei, Long Qian, Jianxun Qu, Wu Xing, Harrison X. Bai, Weihua Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to compare the qualitative and quantitative measurement of dorsal nigral hyperintensity (DNH) and its diagnostic efficacy for Parkinson’s disease (PD) between 7 T MRI sequences: the combined liquid and blood vessel attenuation with refined double echo steady state (CLEAR-DESS) and susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI). Following sequence simulation and optimization, a prospective study enrolled 33 PD patients and 52 healthy controls, all undergoing 7 T MRI with CLEAR-DESS and SWI. Four blinded observers qualitatively assessed image quality and DNH visibility, while two independent readers performed quantitative analyses of signal-to-noise (SNR) and contrast-to-noise ratios (CNR). Excellent inter-observer agreement was noted for both qualitative assessments. Diagnostic performance at the DNH level showed CLEAR-DESS (4 observers; AUC: 0.90–0.96; sensitivity: 85%–96%; specificity: 88%–94%) significantly outperformed SWI (4 observers; AUC: 0.73-0.85; sensitivity: 65%–70%; specificity: 74%–89%). Similarly, at the patient level, CLEAR-DESS (4 observers; AUC: 0.91–0.96; sensitivity: 91%–94%; specificity: 88%–100%) surpassed SWI (4 observers; AUC: 0.79–0.87; sensitivity: 80%–89%; specificity: 60%–79%). CLEAR-DESS also showed significantly better DNH visualization slices, SNR, and CNR than SWI (all p < 0.001). The 7T CLEAR-DESS sequence provides superior DNH visualization and PD diagnostic performance compared to SWI, indicating its potential as a preferred choice for DNH evaluation.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disease, which seriously affects the quality of life of patients1. Its pathogenesis mainly involves the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra-striatal system, leading to a decrease in dopamine levels and, in turn, disrupting the motor control function of the basal ganglia2. The crucial role of dopaminergic neurons in motor control and emotional regulation makes them the core of PD pathological research3. However, traditional symptom-based diagnosis lacks specificity in the early stage and is prone to being confused with other Parkinsonian syndromes4.

Neuroimaging techniques have significantly improved the accuracy of PD diagnosis5. The utilization of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to detect the existence of nigrosome-1 (N1) has been regarded as a potential biomarker for the diagnosis of PD. The substantia nigra (SN) is divided into the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc) and the substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNpr). The SNpc is densely populated with dopaminergic cells containing neuromelanin, while the SNpr is formed by the loose aggregation of medium-sized and large γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-ergic neurons6. The largest nigrosome in the SNpc, that is, N1, is lens-shaped and located in the dorsal part of the SN along the rostral/caudal axis, at the caudal and intermediate levels7. Studies have shown that the MRI hyperintensity representing or partially overlapping with N1, termed dorsal nigral hyperintensity (DNH), resembles a swallow’s tail on susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) and neuromelanin imaging, and is thus also known as the “swallow tail sign” (STS)6,8–13. The DNH can be observed in healthy controls in SWI, while in patients with PD, this sign disappears due to the abnormal accumulation of iron6,9,14.

However, DNH in a portion of healthy controls may likewise fail to be distinctly visualized14, and this circumstance consequently gives rise to false positive occurrences regarding the DNH15,16. Therefore, the DNH is currently more suitable as supportive evidence and has not yet become one of the official diagnostic criteria for PD. This sign requires a comprehensive evaluation in combination with clinical symptoms and other examinations.

With the high resolution and enlarged susceptibility effect from SWI, the utilization of 7 T ultra-high field strength MRI has alleviated this issue10–12,17,18. Nevertheless, the visualization of microvessels9, along with the increased artifacts resulting from motion due to long scan time18 within the 7T-SWI sequence, could potentially have an impact on the evaluation of DNH. Since the signal of blood vessels is nulled in the T2-weighting sequence19, high-resolution fast imaging based on the T2-weighting sequence may provide a potential solution.

The double echo steady state (DESS) sequence has been widely employed for imaging cartilage and nerves in clinical practice at both 3 T and 7 T MRI20,21. Traditionally, the two echoes generated by DESS are combined to form the final image. Several studies have utilized the custom-built DESS sequences to enable diffusion weighted imaging or to measure the T2 relaxometry22,23, where the two echoes are used separately, and the second echo provides T2w contrast. To address the limitations associated with prior visualization of the DNH, we developed an optimized DESS sequence by adjusting the flip angle and incorporating an additional spoiler gradient. This optimization effectively suppresses signals from both vessels and fluid, generating a semi-FLAIR-like contrast. We hypothesized that this modification would enhance the visualization of the DNH sign at the second echo by minimizing interference from microvessels and fluid. We designated this sequence as CLEAR-DESS (Combined Liquid and blood vessel Attenuation with Refined DESS).

This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and diagnostic performance of the prototype CLEAR-DESS sequence for assessing DNH at 7 T. We evaluated whether CLEAR-DESS could serve as a viable alternative to the SWI sequence in 7 T-MRI protocols, potentially reducing scan time, enhancing image quality, and preserving diagnostic accuracy for PD diagnosis and monitoring.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that the optimized CLEAR-DESS sequence enables clear visualization of the DNH with reduced interference from microvessels and fluid at 7 T MRI. The results showed that compared with the commonly used SWI sequence in clinical practice, the CLEAR-DESS sequence has a better ability to display DNH and has good diagnostic performance for PD, with the AUC ranging from 0.90 to 0.96.

Unlike iron-sensitive sequences, such as SWI, the CLEAR-DESS sequence does not cause the interference of small blood vessels as observed in previous studies24. Moreover, since the CLEAR-DESS is a steady-state sequence with a short TR, compared with SWI and Flair sequences, it has a relatively shorter scanning time. To some extent, it reduces the motion artifacts caused by patients’ inability to tolerate the scanning process. In addition, the specific absorption rate (SAR) issue is a major challenge in 7 T MRI scanning. An excessively high SAR value not only affects the image quality but also has an impact on the safety of magnetic resonance scanning25. In order to obtain a semi-FLAIR contrast, the FA of CLEAR-DESS is low. Therefore, in 7 T scanning, it has reduced the SAR problem caused by the 180-degree refocus pulse in conventional T2w sequences.

Compared to age-matched patients, PD shows increased iron deposition in deep gray matter nuclei such as the SN26. However, healthy individuals also exhibit age-related iron accumulation in these structures, posing diagnostic challenges as physiological iron deposition may mimic pathological changes27. SWI, an MRI technique highly sensitive to T2* relaxation times, effectively detects cerebral iron deposits28. Yet, under identical TE conditions, its sensitivity may amplify normal age-related iron signals, potentially causing false positives in elderly populations29. In contrast, CLEAR-DESS contrast relies on combined T2 and T2* effects, with minimal T2* weighting30. This property reduces the exaggeration of physiological iron deposition in N1, thereby mitigating false positives in PD diagnosis. The isotropic volumetric acquisition makes the atlas-based anatomy segmentation easy for CLEAR-DESS, thereby making CLEAR-DESS suitable for the automatic PD diagnosis utilizing the vision-language models. This application deserves further exploration in the future.

Different researchers have attempted to observe and quantitatively evaluate the DNH or STS using different sequences, mainly focusing on SWI, quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM), T2*-weighted imaging, and neuromelanin imaging (MT-T1 weighted)11,31. Some researchers use MT-T1 for neuromelanin (NM) imaging of the SN to directly reflect the loss of dopaminergic neurons at 7 T. Lakhani et al.17 reported that on 7 T MRI, by quantitatively analyzing the changes in iron deposition in the region through delineating the region of interest of the SN, they found that the AUC of MT-T1 in differentiating PD from healthy controls could reach 0.94 (bradykinesia-rigidity type). However, current quantitative analysis methods in such studies remain overly complex compared to direct morphological observation of N1, which not only hinders their clinical utility but also imposes additional workload challenges for radiologists and neurologists. A meta-analysis incorporating multiple 3 T and 7 T MRI studies reported that this imaging sign achieved an AUC of 0.98, with sensitivity and specificity of 0.94 (0.93–0.96) and 0.90 (0.88–0.92), respectively, for diagnosing PD31. In our study, the diagnostic performance of CLEAR-DESS was comparable to these prior benchmarks, whereas SWI underperformed relative to earlier reports. Several factors may explain this discrepancy beyond differences in study cohorts and imaging protocols. First, while previous studies typically classified DNH visibility using a binary (normal/abnormal) or three-tier scale (normal, may be normal, abnormal), we employed a more granular four-category system (normal, may be normal, uncertain, abnormal). This finer classification, though clinically nuanced, may have contributed to slightly lower aggregated metrics such as AUC. Second, earlier high-resolution 7 T SWI protocols often required scan times exceeding 10 min per sequence. Although prolonged acquisition can improve signal clarity, it poses practical challenges for PD patients, who frequently experience tremor or motor restlessness, thus compromising scan compliance and clinical feasibility. To balance diagnostic accuracy with patient comfort and real-world applicability, we referenced established methodological adaptations16,32,33. By strategically increasing in-plane resolution alongside slice thickness, we reduced total scan time to approximately 5 min without substantially sacrificing image quality. In summary, although the diagnostic performance of CLEAR-DESS did not exceed the highest reported values in the literature, its integrated advantages remain clear: it delivers diagnostic accuracy comparable or superior to conventional high-resolution SWI, while offering improved DNH conspicuity, higher SNRs and CNRs, and reduced vulnerability to motion artifacts. These attributes provide radiologists with clearer images and greater diagnostic confidence. Moreover, whereas SWI is known to be susceptible to false positives related to age-related iron accumulation and microvascular effects, our findings suggest that CLEAR-DESS may help mitigate these confounders, underscoring its translational potential in clinical practice.

Furthermore, we utilized the Fazekas score to explore the ability of the CLEAR-DESS sequence to assess WMH. The results showed that there was a high degree of consistency between the CLEAR-DESS sequence and the FLAIR sequence. This indicates that the CLEAR-DESS sequence, with its semi-FLAIR technology, can serve the purpose of visualizing WMH to a certain extent. However, since there is no gold standard for the assessment of WMH, this clinical application is worthy of further exploration and optimization in the future. The water suppression effect of CLEAR-DESS is based on the flow velocity of CSF. Compared with the FLAIR sequence, the CLEAR-DESS sequence has a significantly better fluid suppression effect at the base of the skull. This advantage also broadens the scope of clinical application of this sequence, especially in the visualization of lesions at the base of the skull.

This study also has certain limitations. Firstly, as an exploratory, single-center study with a small sample size, it may have higher random error in the study. Additionally, since some of the PD volunteers recruited in the study were not first-time visitors, data on PD-related scales and clinical indicators were lacking, which limited our investigation into the correlation between the research and clinical markers. In the future, we will continue to recruit volunteers to expand the study cohort for verification and explore its significant value in differential diagnosis, such as in multiple system atrophy-Parkinsonism, progressive supranuclear palsy, and vascular Parkinsonism. Moreover, our current study did not conduct quantitative research on the effectiveness of CLEAR-DESS in displaying varying degrees of iron deposition. In subsequent studies, we will further explore this direction to refine the diagnostic application of the CLEAR-DESS sequence in early-stage PD patients. In addition, the diagnosis of PD and the assessment of white matter hyperintensities lack a pathological gold standard, which may have an impact on the actual results.

The CLEAR-DESS sequence based on 7 T MRI demonstrates a superior capacity to visualize DNH compared to the SWI sequence. Correspondingly, its diagnostic performance for PD also outshines that of SWI, suggesting its potential as a preferred choice for DNH evaluation.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective research was carried out in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and obtained the official approval from the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (Ethics approval number: 2025081406). Prior to their inclusion in the study, all participants provided their written informed consent. Participants were recruited between June 2024 and June 2025, and included individuals diagnosed with PD and healthy volunteers. The diagnosis of PD was established in accordance with the Movement Disorder Society Clinical Diagnostic Criteria4 by neurologists. The exclusion criteria encompassed factors such as the presence of contraindications for MRI, including metallic implants and large-area tattoos. Participants who failed to complete the examination were also excluded.

Several co-authors (Q.L., Y.H., L.Q., and J.Q.) are employees of the 7 T MRI equipment manufacturer and provided technical guidance on sequence parameters. To ensure impartiality, all data analyses were performed independently by researchers not affiliated with the company. The images were independently evaluated by four radiologists (Y.S. and J.H. with 5 years of neurological MRI experience; W.X. and W.L. with 20 years of neurological MRI experience) who were blinded to clinical information. Two radiologists (S.L. and R.C., with 6 and 7 years of neurological MRI experience, respectively) independently performed quantitative analyses of the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) for all acquired images. 7T CLEAR-DESS MR pulse sequence simulation and optimization

The DESS sequence with the spoiler gradient (Fig. 7) has been proposed for high-resolution cartilage diffusion-weighted imaging34,35. Two echoes namely S and S are acquired, in which the image contrast from S is T1 weighting and S is a combination of T2 and diffusion weighting. Additionally, S is contributed by the signal from the previous TR, resulting in the signal being lost within moving tissues, i.e., vessel and CSF. Herein, we optimized the DESS sequence to achieve fluid attenuated T2w contrast by adjusting the flip angles (FAs) and the spoiler gradients for the dephasing of moving tissues.+−+−−Fig. 7The sequence diagram of the spoiled CLEAR-DESS.

The sequence diagram of the spoiled CLEAR-DESS.

The extended phase graph (EPG) algorithm (https://github.com/lamyj/sycomore/tree/master) was employed to simulate the impacts of the magnitude of the spoiler gradient and the FA on the signal of brain tissue. The spoiler gradient is positioned in the anterior-posterior direction to lessen the interference of the diffusion effect from the spoiler gradient on the gray and white matter signal. The simulation was based on the following parameters of 7 T brain tissue, including the relaxation times and diffusion coefficients36 white matter (1200 ms, 34 ms, 788um2/s), gray matter (2100 ms, 49 ms, 1100 μm2/s), and cerebrospinal fluid (4000 ms, 1200 ms, 2275 μm2/s). Other simulation sequence parameters: amplitude of the spoiler gradient = 0 ~ 60 mT/m in step of 5 mT/m, flip angle = 4° ~ 40° in step of 2°, slice thickness = 0.64 mm, TR = 8.51 ms, TE1 = 2.42 ms, TE2 = 6.09 ms, and the duration of the spoiler gradient = 1.4 ms.

Image quality assessment

Subjective general image quality was rated on a five-point Likert scale37, as follows: 5 = excellent image quality, interpretable with no artifacts; 4 = good image quality, interpretable with minimal artifacts; 3 = average image quality, interpretation mildly degraded by image artifacts; 2 = below-average image quality, interpretable but moderately degraded; and 1 = poor image quality, uninterpretable images.

Qualitative analysis of DNH assessment

We modified the method mentioned in the aforementioned literature8,15 by classifying “possibly normal” into two grades based on the observer’s diagnostic confidence, namely “may be normal” and “uncertain”. DNH was subjectively rated as “normal” (normal: definite hyperintense region between hypointense SN and medial lemniscus at or below the inferior border of red nuclei), “may be normal” (The observer believes that there is a hyperintense region between the hypointense SN below or at the inferior margin of the red nucleus and the medial lemniscus, but the diagnostic confidence decreases), “uncertain” (The observer cannot determine whether there is a hyperintense region between the hypointense SN below or at the inferior margin of the red nucleus and the medial lemniscus) or “abnormal” (no discernable region of differing signal intensity between hypointense SN and medial lemniscus at or below the inferior border of red nuclei) at the level of the caudal posterior SN. Given that PD patients may exhibit asymmetric nigrosome abnormalities bilaterally13,15, we conducted separate analyses at the DNH level and the patient level (considering single or bilateral absence as positive) for the qualitative assessment of DNH.

Quantitative analysis of N1 assessment

Furthermore, each sequence’s capability in imaging DNH was performed by recording the number of layers where DNH could be visualized for each sequence. The SNR assessment included CLEAR-DESS and SWI images from all volunteers, while the CNR evaluation was restricted to images from normal volunteers. For each image type, measurements were conducted on the same anatomical slice using a standardized formula. Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually delineated in the perirubral white matter and the adjacent air outside the body on the slice where the red nucleus was most clearly visualized, as well as in the DNH and the surrounding air on the slice where DNH was optimally displayed38. The mean signal intensity and standard deviation (SD) were recorded for each ROI to calculate SNR and CNR.

Both the SWI and CLEAR-DESS images used for DNH assessment were reconstructed perpendicular to the midbrain level, with a slice thickness of 1.2 mm. Considering that the SWI resolution (0.2 × 0.2 mm2) is higher than that of CLEAR-DESS (0.8 × 0.8 mm2), the high-resolution 0.2 × 0.2 mm2 images were downsampled to 0.8 × 0.8 mm2 to match the resolution of CLEAR-DESS. However, the original SWI resolution was retained for evaluation to align with clinical practice.

Assessment of WMH

We employed the Fazekas scale as a semi-qualitative method to assess the location (deep white matter) and the extent of pathological WMH spread. For each participant, a rating for deep white matter hyperintensities (DWMH) was assigned. DWMH were rated on a scale from 0 to 3. A higher rating corresponded to greater severity and spread, where 0 indicated no WMH, 1 denoted mild, 2 signified moderate, and 3 represented severe24,39.

Statistical analysis

The assessment of normality was carried out by means of the Shapiro–Wilk test. In the case of continuously distributed data conforming to a normal distribution, the mean values and SD were computed; whereas for data not adhering to a normal distribution, the median values and interquartile ranges were determined. Categorical variables were presented as absolute figures and percentages. For the comparison of continuous variables, paired t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were employed. To assess interobserver and interreader agreement on image quality scores, qualitative assessment of DNH, SNR, and CNR quantification, Bland-Altman plots and the interclass correlation coefficient were used. The inter-rater agreement between the actual diagnostic results and each observer’s assessments was calculated using weighted kappa statistics. The qualitative assessment of DNH visibility was classified into four distinct confidence levels: normal (definitive DNH identification with 100% probability of presence), may be normal (probable DNH identification with 50% probability of presence), uncertain (uncertain DNH identification with 25% probability of presence), and abnormal (definitive DNH absence with 0% probability of presence). Subsequently, we further employed receiver operating characteristic analysis, DeLong’s test, and a confusion matrix to systematically evaluate the predictive performance. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.1.1) and SPSS (version 8.0.2, version 26; IBM). All P values were two-tailed, and the significance level was designated as P < 0.05.
