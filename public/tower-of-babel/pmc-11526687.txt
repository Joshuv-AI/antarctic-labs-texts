Persistent renal dysfunction post-chemotherapy: a diagnostic conundrum in pediatric cancer survivorship – a case report
Authors: Jhao-Jhuang Ding, Shih-Hua Lin, Tai-Wei Wu, Min-Hua Tseng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLate-onset type II Bartter syndrome is an exceedingly rare condition, with only six documented cases presenting symptoms and signs beyond infancy. We report a unique case of late-onset type II Bartter syndrome with an atypical presentation and clinical course following chemotherapy treatment during childhood.Case presentationA 10-year-old boy, diagnosed with hepatoblastoma at age 2 and treated with cisplatin and epirubicin, presented with polyuria, polydipsia, failure to thrive, and electrolyte imbalances. He exhibited hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, and elevated urinary excretion of sodium, chloride, calcium, and magnesium. Whole exome sequencing and Sanger sequencing identified compound heterozygous variants in the KCNJ1 gene, confirming the diagnosis of type II Bartter syndrome. The patient’s clinical presentation was distinct from previously reported cases, with an absence of nephrocalcinosis, unusually small and hyperechoic kidneys, and a substantial decline in kidney function. Treatment included oral potassium supplementation, spironolactone, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors.ConclusionsThis case highlights the importance of considering late-onset Bartter syndrome in patients with a history of chemotherapy presenting with persistent electrolyte imbalances and ongoing renal dysfunction. The atypical features and rapid progression of chronic kidney disease in this patient may be attributed to the deleterious nature of the identified variants and the potential impact of previous chemotherapy on kidney susceptibility to damage. Careful monitoring and management of electrolyte imbalances and renal function are crucial in such cases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-024-05129-8.

Background

Bartter syndrome is a rare inherited renal tubular disorder characterized by hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, and hyperreninemic hyperaldosteronism. Type II Bartter syndrome, caused by variants in the KCNJ1 gene encoding the renal outer medullary potassium channel (ROMK), typically presents during the neonatal period or early infancy. Late-onset type II Bartter syndrome is extremely rare, with only eight cases reported in the literature. This case report describes an atypical presentation of late-onset type II Bartter syndrome in a patient with a history of chemotherapy treatment for hepatoblastoma.

Clinical presentation

A 10-year-old boy was referred due to polyuria, polydipsia, and failure to thrive. He was born at 27 weeks of gestation, without congenital anomalies or polyhydramnios. Family history was insignificant. At the age of 2 years, he was diagnosed with hepatoblastoma and underwent intravenous cisplatin and epirubicin infusions as neoadjuvant chemotherapy, followed by tumor resection. Hypokalemia, 3.0 mEq/L, was noticed incidentally and treated with oral potassium supplement after completion of treatment. Physical examination revealed body height 125 cm (< -2SD), blood pressure of 109/69 mmHg, heart rate of 102 /min, respiratory rate of 24 /min, low body weight, short stature, and depleted volume. Blood tests showed Na+ 133 mmol/L, K+ 2.6 mmol/L, Cl− 105 mmol/L, pH 7.434, HCO3− 32 mEq/L, PCO2 49, total calcium 9.6 mg/dL, inorganic phosphate 4.5 mg/dL, Mg2+ 1.9 mg/dL, blood urea nitrogen 17.4 mg/dL, creatinine 0.72 mg/dL, and osmolarity 299 mOsm/kgH2O. Simultaneous spot urine analysis revealed Na+ 33 mmol/L, K+ 14.2 mmol/L, Cl− 19 mmol/L, Calcium 5.4 mg/dL, inorganic phosphate 3.1 mg/dL, Mg2+ 1.7 mg/dL, urea 23.8 mg/dL, creatinine 23.4 mg/dL, uric acid 8.8 mg/dL, osmolarity of 174.6 mOsm/kgH2O, urine microalbumin-to-creatinine ratio of < 30 mg/g, and urine protein-to-creatinine ratio 3446 mg/mg (Table 1). Renal sonography demonstrated hyperechoic and small kidneys without nephrocalcinosis (Fig. 1). Oral potassium supplement (3 ~ 5 mEq/kg/day), spironolactone, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor were therefore given for his hypokalemia and proteinuria. During follow-up, his renal function deteriorated and proteinuria progressed (Table 1).

Discussion and conclusions

When investigating the causes of hypokalemia, as illustrated in Fig. 2, it’s crucial to distinguish renal potassium wasting from other conditions. This can be accomplished by examining urine potassium-creatinine ratios or transtubular potassium gradients. Low renal K+ excretion can be caused by two primary conditions: extrarenal potassium loss or intracellular potassium shifting. High renal K+ excretion can be further classified into hypertensive and normotensive hypokalemia. High renal K+ excretion with hypertension can result from conditions with hyperaldosteronism or aldosterone-like effects, such as cortisol and its analogs. High renal K+ excretion with normotension can be further divided into those with metabolic acidosis and those with metabolic alkalosis. Common etiologies of high renal K+ excretion with normotension and metabolic acidosis include renal tubular acidosis, profound diarrhea, and toluene abuse. Differentiating the causes of high renal K+ excretion with normotension and metabolic alkalosis necessitates measuring spot urine for Na+ and Cl-. High urine Na+ and Cl- excretion may be due to congenital or drug-induced Gitelman/Bartter-like syndromes. High urine Na+ with low Cl- excretion suggests gastric juice loss or the presence of non-absorbable anions. Low urine Na+ with high Cl- excretion is associated with specific types of diarrhea. Low levels of both urine Na+ and Cl- indicate recovery from previous vomiting or diuretic use, or the presence of chloride-losing diarrhea.

The patient’s clinical presentation, high renal K+ excretion, normal blood pressure, metabolic alkalosis, and elevated urinary excretion of Na+, Cl− were suggestive of inherited or drug-induced Gitelman/Bartter-like nephropathy [1–3]. Given his previous history of cisplatin therapy, a well-known nephrotoxic agent known to mimic Bartter syndrome (BS) [4–6], our initial approach was to manage him for cisplatin-induced kidney tubulopathy. However, the patient’s enduring kidney function deterioration and proteinuria contradicted the usual stabilization of renal function observed in cisplatin-induced kidney tubulopathy cases [7]. This prompted us to consider the possibility of an underlying inherited kidney disease. Considering the patient’s age, the absence of polyhydramnios and nephrocalcinosis, and high renal excretion of Ca2+ and Mg2+, we initially suspected type III classic BS. However, direct CLCNKB sequencing revealed no pathogenic variants. Further investigation involving whole exome sequencing and Sanger sequencing identified compound heterozygous variants, c.562delA (p.K187fs) and c.941 A > C (p.Y314S), in the KCNJ1 gene, which are both novel variants [8]. Further Sanger sequencing of his parents confirmed that his father carried p.K187fs and mother carried p.Y314S variants. According to the ACMG guidelines for the interpretation of sequence variants, the K187fs and Y314S variants in the KCNJ1 gene, which encodes the Kir1.1 potassium channel, are likely pathogenic for Bartter syndrome. The frameshift variant K187fs is predicted to result in a truncated, non-functional protein, consistent with a loss-of-function mechanism for Bartter syndrome (PVS1 criterion). The Y314S missense variant affects a critical residue in the IgLD core of Kir1.1, and computational analysis suggests that it disrupts channel stability similar to the known pathogenic Y314C variant (PP3 criterion). The presence of these two variants together in a patient suspected to have Bartter syndrome provides strong evidence for a molecular diagnosis of the disease (PM2 and PP4 criteria). In conclusion, based on the ACMG guidelines and the available evidence, the K187fs and Y314S variants are classified as likely pathogenic for Bartter syndrome.

Late-onset type II BS is exceedingly rare, with only eight documented cases presenting symptoms and signs beyond infancy, as presented in Table 2. Typical initial symptoms include polyuria and polydipsia, and some patients also present with short stature or general weakness. Only one case had a prenatal history of polyhydramnios. The key laboratory findings across these patients, including ours, comprised of hypokalemia with renal potassium wasting, hypercalciuria, and hyperrenin-hyperaldosteronism. All previous patients exhibited nephrocalcinosis and early-stage chronic kidney disease (CKD stage I-II). Our patient, however, presented differently; he did not exhibit nephrocalcinosis but showed unusually small and hyperechoic kidneys. Moreover, a substantial decline in kidney function (serum Cr 3.6 mg/dL, CKD stage IV) was observed six years after his initial presentation, a departure from the other reported cases. The pathogenic variants, K187fs, might lead to volume depletion and hypokalemia by impaired the function KCNJ1. In addition to the volume depletion, the chronic hypokalemia per se may be contributory to CKD by resulting renal damage through the generation of typical tubulointerstitial nephritis, renal tubule vacuolization, and cyst formation [9]. These distinctive features may potentially be attributed to a more deleterious frameshift variants of the KCNJ1 gene and an increased susceptibility of the kidneys to damage due to previous exposure to chemotherapy.

The mainstay treatment of Bartter syndrome involves correcting volume depletion and electrolyte imbalances, preventing nephrocalcinosis, and avoiding nephrotoxic agents. To maintain fluid status, patients are encouraged to consume adequate amounts of water, and cyclooxygenase-2 inhibitors could be considered for severe polyuria. Potassium supplementation is typically necessary to prevent severe hypokalemia, and potassium-sparing diuretics help raise serum K+ levels and reverse metabolic alkalosis. Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEI) may be used if the presence of proteinuria. We delivered oral potassium supplementation (5 mEq/kg/day) to maintain serum potassium levels, spironolactone to decrease renal K+ excretion and reverse metabolic alkalosis, along with ACEI to control proteinuria. The non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug was discontinued due to concerns regarding advanced chronic kidney disease.

The strengths of this case report lie in its detailed description of a rare presentation of late-onset type II Bartter syndrome in a pediatric patient with a history of chemotherapy, adding to the limited literature on this topic. However, the limitations of this report include its single case nature, which may limit the generalizability of the findings, and the speculative nature of the exact mechanisms by which the identified variants and previous chemotherapy exposure contributed to the atypical presentation and rapid progression of chronic kidney disease.

This case emphasizes the need for close monitoring and management of electrolyte imbalances and renal function in pediatric cancer survivors, particularly those with a history of nephrotoxic chemotherapy. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for rare inherited renal tubular disorders when faced with persistent electrolyte abnormalities and renal dysfunction in this patient population. Early recognition and appropriate management of these conditions are crucial to prevent further kidney damage and optimize patient outcomes.
