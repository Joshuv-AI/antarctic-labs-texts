Airway Anatomy as a Predictor of Tracheostomy in Critically Ill Patients: A Multicampus Analysis of Airway Grade, Admission Diagnosis, and Clinical Trajectory
Authors: Michael Sramek, Dana Eitan, Darby Keirns, Bryant Noss, Hannah Gibbs, Austin Swisher, India Casaday, Payam Entezami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveDetermine whether the Cormack–Lehane airway grade at intubation predicts subsequent tracheostomy in critically ill adults and whether this association persists after controlling for admission diagnosis.MethodsRetrospective multicenter cohort study of 10,234 ICU patients requiring ventilation across three campuses from April 2018 to June 2024. Rates varied by diagnosis, from 28.4% for COVID‐19 respiratory failure to 6.5% for gastrointestinal/hepatic conditions. In the fully adjusted model (N = 4770), airway grade remained independently significant (aOR: 1.13, 95% CI: 1.03–1.24, p = 0.014). The model achieved an AUC of 0.638.ConclusionDifficult airway anatomy predicts tracheostomy risk after controlling for admission diagnosis, comorbidities, and demographics. Diagnosis provides additional discrimination, with COVID‐19, transplant, and cardiac patients at highest risk. Grade worsening across re‐intubation identifies an ultrahigh‐risk subgroup. Introduction

Tracheostomy timing in critically ill patients remains a significant challenge in intensive care medicine, with profound implications for patient outcomes and healthcare resource utilization [1, 2, 3]. Despite decades of research, the optimal approach to identifying patients requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation remains elusive, with current practice largely reactive rather than predictive [4]. Existing prediction models achieve only modest discrimination (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC]: 0.78–0.79), limiting their clinical utility for early risk stratification [5].

The American College of Chest Physicians acknowledges insufficient evidence for timing recommendations, with most institutions deferring tracheostomy until 2–3 weeks after intubation [6]. This approach may delay beneficial interventions. Large trials demonstrate that many patients randomized to “late” tracheostomy ultimately never require the procedure; in the TracMan trial, 55% of patients in the late arm never received tracheostomy, highlighting the need for improved early risk stratification [7, 8].

Recent meta‐analyses suggest potential benefits of early tracheostomy (≤ 7–10 days), including reduced ventilator‐associated pneumonia and shorter ICU stays, underscoring the importance of timely intervention and accurate patient selection [9, 10, 11, 12].

Current predictive models rely on admission diagnoses, comorbidities, and physiological severity scores. These are ultimately variables that capture illness acuity but not airway factors predisposing to prolonged ventilation [13]. Notably absent are direct assessments of airway anatomy, despite compelling biological rationale linking airway characteristics to respiratory management challenges [14].

The Cormack–Lehane classification provides a standardized assessment of laryngoscopic views during intubation, from Grade 1 (complete vocal cord visualization) to Grade 4 (no glottic structures visible) [15]. The Cormack–Lehane scale is widely recognized as the most common definition of a difficult airway in clinical practice, though it identifies difficult laryngoscopic views rather than difficult intubation [16, 17, 18, 19]. Higher grades correlate with increased intubation difficulty and greater risk of airway trauma, factors that may influence subsequent respiratory management.

Difficult airways may reflect underlying anatomical abnormalities or physiological derangements that persist throughout the ICU course. Patients with challenging airway anatomy might experience impaired secretion clearance, increased work of breathing, compromised cough, or heightened aspiration risk factors that could impede successful extubation and necessitate tracheostomy [20, 21]. However, the relationship between initial airway anatomy and long‐term ventilation outcomes has received minimal systematic investigation. Previous studies focused on immediate intubation success rather than downstream respiratory trajectory [22].

Critically, tracheostomy risk varies enormously by admission diagnosis; patients intubated for respiratory failure, trauma, or neurological emergencies face fundamentally different ventilation trajectories. Any airway‐based predictor must demonstrate independence from diagnostic case mix to have clinical utility. We hypothesized that Cormack–Lehane airway grade at intubation would independently predict tracheostomy need after accounting for admission diagnosis and conducted a large multicampus cohort study to test this hypothesis.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from the Mayo Clinic Unified Data Platform across three campuses (Arizona, Minnesota, and Florida) between April 2018 and June 2024. This multicampus design enhances external validity, capturing diverse patient populations, practice patterns, and geographic variations while maintaining data consistency through unified electronic health records. The study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board with waiver of informed consent. This institution does not have formalized early tracheostomy protocols, mitigating indication bias.

Study Population

Inclusion criteria were adult patients (≥ 18 years) admitted to the ICU who required mechanical ventilation via endotracheal intubation. Exclusions included preexisting tracheostomy, emergency tracheostomy within 24 h of admission, and multiple ICU admissions (only first admission included). Emergency tracheostomies within 24 h were excluded, as these reflect acute decompensation unrelated to prolonged ventilation trajectory.

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome was tracheostomy during index hospitalization, defined as surgical or percutaneous tracheostomy documented in medical records, procedure codes, or respiratory therapy databases.

Primary Predictor

The Cormack–Lehane airway grade was the primary predictor, standardized according to established criteria: Grade 1 (complete glottic opening visible), 2A (partial glottic opening visible), 2B (only posterior commissure visible), 3A (only epiglottis visible and liftable), 3B (epiglottis adherent to pharyngeal wall), and 4 (neither glottis nor epiglottis visible) [15, 23]. Grades were extracted from both structured documentation fields and free‐text anesthesia notes. The worst grade documented across all intubation events per encounter was used as the primary predictor, as this captures the most challenging airway anatomy encountered during the patient's ICU course. Grades were converted to ordinal values (1–6) for trend analysis.

Covariates and Diagnostic Classification

Covariates included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score calculated from ICD‐10 coded problem lists according to Deyo's adaptation [24], and intubation method (video vs. direct laryngoscopy).

The principal problem documented for each hospital encounter was used to classify patients into 12 diagnostic categories: respiratory failure/pneumonia, COVID‐19 respiratory failure, neurological, cardiac, sepsis, trauma, malignancy, transplant, gastrointestinal/hepatic, overdose/toxicology, shock, and other. This approach captures the primary indication for ICU admission and ventilation, addressing a key limitation of models that rely solely on comorbidity indices without accounting for admission diagnosis.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as means ± standard deviation for normally distributed continuous variables, medians with interquartile ranges for skewed variables, and frequencies for categorical variables. Univariate associations were assessed using chi‐square tests for categorical variables and t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables.

Primary Analysis

The association between airway grade and tracheostomy was assessed using ordinal logistic regression to estimate the per‐grade odds ratio. Categorical odds ratios were estimated using Grade 1 as the reference category. A binary comparison of Grade ≥ 3 versus Grade 1–2B was also performed.

Multivariable Models

Two multivariable logistic regression models were constructed. Model A adjusted for age, sex, BMI, CCI, and intubation method. Model B additionally incorporated the admission diagnosis category as dummy‐coded covariates (with the aggregate “other” category as reference), testing whether airway grade retained independent predictive value after controlling for the diagnostic case‐mix. A third model (Model C) included all covariates from Model B except airway grade to quantify the incremental discriminative contribution of grade.

Model Performance and Validation

Model discrimination was assessed using AUC. Internal validation was performed using 10‐fold stratified cross‐validation to estimate optimism‐corrected AUC. Model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test. The incremental discriminative value of airway grade was assessed by comparing the AUC of models with and without grade.

Sensitivity Analysis: Initial vs. Worst Grade

To assess whether the observed association was driven by baseline airway anatomy versus the clinical trajectory of repeated airway difficulty, we performed a prespecified sensitivity analysis comparing the initial documented grade (first recorded intubation event, ordered chronologically) to the worst documented grade (maximum across all events). Patients were classified as having stable grade (initial = worst) or grade worsened (worst > initial across re‐intubation events). Tracheostomy rates were compared between these subgroups.

Missing Data

Documented Cormack–Lehane grades were available for 6189 of 10,234 patients (60.5%). The primary analysis was conducted on complete cases with documented airway grades. CCI was available for 4794 patients from ICD‐10 coded problem lists; the multivariable models were restricted to patients with complete data on all covariates (N = 4770).

All analyses were performed using Python 3.13 with NumPy 2.4, Pandas 3.0, Statsmodels 0.14.6 for regression, Scikit‐learn 1.8 for cross‐validation and AUC estimation, and SciPy 1.17 for statistical testing. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

COVID‐19 Sensitivity Analysis

The study period spans the COVID‐19 pandemic, which altered tracheostomy practices. We performed three prespecified sensitivity analyses: (1) exclusion of patients with COVID‐19 as the principal diagnosis (n = 387); (2) exclusion of the peak pandemic period (March 2020–June 2021), representing the pre‐vaccine era when guidelines recommended delayed tracheostomy due to aerosol generation concerns; and (3) restriction to pre‐pandemic admissions (before March 2020). For each analysis, we repeated the unadjusted ordinal trend, the binary Grade 3+ comparison, and the fully adjusted Model B.

Results

Study Population

A total of 10,234 adult ICU patients met inclusion criteria. Documented Cormack–Lehane airway grades were available for 6189 patients (60.5%), who constituted the primary analysis cohort. The mean age was 61.8 ± 15.4 years, 3635 (58.7%) were male, and the mean BMI was 29.5 ± 8.7 kg/m2. Overall, 922 patients (14.9%) underwent tracheostomy.

Airway Grade Distribution and Primary Analysis

The worst documented airway grade distribution was Grade 1 (2786 patients, 45.0%), Grade 2A (2765 patients, 44.7%), Grade 2B (421 patients, 6.8%), Grade 3A (133 patients, 2.1%), Grade 3B (53 patients, 0.9%), and Grade 4 (31 patients, 0.5%) (Table 1). Tracheostomy rates increased across these airway grades (Figure 1): Grade 1 (13.9%, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 12.6%–15.2%), Grade 2A (15.2%, 13.9%–16.6%), Grade 2B (15.7%, 12.5%–19.5%), Grade 3A (22.6%, 16.3%–30.4%), Grade 3B (24.5%, 14.9%–37.6%), and Grade 4 (19.4%, 9.2%–36.3%). Ordinal logistic regression demonstrated a significant trend (OR: 1.14, 95% CI: 1.05–1.23, p = 0.001), with each unit grade increase conferring 14% higher tracheostomy odds. The lower tracheostomy rate for Grade 4 (19.4%) compared to Grades 3A (22.6%) and 3B (24.5%) reflects the small sample size (n = 31), producing a wide 95% CI (9.2%–36.3%) that overlaps substantially with both Grade 3 subgroups. Grade 4 patients also had the highest mortality rate (25.8%), consistent with survivorship bias.

Tracheostomy by Admission Diagnosis

Tracheostomy rates varied markedly by admission diagnosis category (Table 2). The highest tracheostomy rates were observed in COVID‐19 respiratory failure (28.4%, n = 387), transplant (27.9%, n = 136), and cardiac disease (20.8%, n = 667). Intermediate rates characterized respiratory failure/pneumonia (17.3%, n = 1144), neurological conditions (15.1%, n = 694), and malignancy (14.3%, n = 266). Lower tracheostomy rates were seen in trauma (10.5%, n = 220), sepsis (8.9%, n = 359), and gastrointestinal/hepatic conditions (6.5%, n = 727). No patient admitted for overdose/toxicology (n = 46) underwent tracheostomy.

COVID‐19 Sensitivity Analysis

The association between airway grade and tracheostomy was robust across COVID‐19 sensitivity analyses. After excluding 387 COVID‐19 patients (N = 5802), the ordinal trend remained significant (OR: 1.13, 95% CI: 1.04–1.24, p = 0.005; adjusted aOR: 1.13, p = 0.019). After excluding the peak pandemic period (N = 4739), the association was unchanged (OR: 1.15, p = 0.002; adjusted aOR: 1.19, p = 0.002). In the pre‐pandemic subcohort (N = 1164), the ordinal trend was consistent in direction and magnitude (OR: 1.11) but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.24), reflecting reduced power. The binary Grade 3+ comparison remained significant, excluding COVID (OR: 1.70, p = 0.003) and excluding the peak pandemic (OR: 1.71, p = 0.005).

Discussion

This multicampus cohort study demonstrates that Cormack–Lehane airway grade at intubation independently predicts subsequent tracheostomy in critically ill patients, even after controlling for admission diagnosis, comorbidities, and demographics. Each ordinal increase in the worst documented grade conferred 13%–14% higher tracheostomy odds in both unadjusted and diagnosis‐adjusted models. Patients with Grade ≥ 3 airways had 70% higher tracheostomy odds compared to Grade 1–2B, and the 2.9% of patients whose grade worsened across re‐intubation events demonstrated a tracheostomy rate of 38.8%, nearly three times the cohort average.

Comparison With Existing Literature

Current tracheostomy prediction models rely predominantly on illness severity scores, admission diagnoses, and physiological derangements [5, 25]. Nguyen et al. developed machine learning models using 331 features from ICU admission data, identifying pneumonia, sepsis, and chronic respiratory failure as top predictors (AUC: 0.78–0.79) [5]. Parreco et al. achieved AUC 0.830 using severity‐of‐illness scores and comorbidities [25]. Our study extends this literature by introducing airway anatomy as a readily available marker documented at the point of intubation as an independent predictor. While our model's cross‐validated AUC of 0.638 is lower than these post‐admission models, this reflects our intentional restriction to information available at intubation, offering earlier predictive utility before the clinical trajectory fully unfolds.

To our knowledge, this is the first large‐scale study examining Cormack–Lehane grade as a predictor of tracheostomy in critically ill patients. Previous literature has focused on the Cormack–Lehane classification as a predictor of difficult intubation itself, but not as a marker of downstream respiratory trajectory [18, 19]. Our findings suggest that this anatomical assessment carries prognostic significance beyond the immediate intubation procedure.

Admission Diagnosis and Diagnostic Case‐Mix

Our analysis revealed substantial variation in tracheostomy rates by admission diagnosis, ranging from 28.4% for COVID‐19 respiratory failure to 0% for overdose/toxicology. The highest‐risk diagnostic categories, COVID‐19 (aOR: 2.60), transplant (aOR: 2.95), and cardiac disease (aOR: 1.84), represent conditions characterized by prolonged ventilatory support requirements, multi‐organ dysfunction, or complex postoperative recovery. Conversely, gastrointestinal/hepatic conditions (aOR: 0.44) and overdose (0% tracheostomy rate) carry low tracheostomy risk, likely reflecting shorter anticipated ventilation courses.

Crucially, airway grade retained independent significance (aOR: 1.13, p = 0.014) after adjustment for admission diagnosis, demonstrating that the airway anatomy–tracheostomy association is not explained by confounding from diagnostic case mix. This strengthens the biological interpretation: airway anatomical factors contribute to tracheostomy risk through mechanisms independent of the underlying disease process. The addition of diagnostic categories substantially improved model discrimination (cross‐validated AUC: 0.638 vs. 0.589 without diagnosis), indicating that both airway anatomy and clinical context contribute complementary prognostic information.

Biological Plausibility and Mechanistic Insights

Several mechanisms may explain the observed association. Anatomical factors that complicate laryngoscopy, including restricted mouth opening, cervical immobility, airway edema, obesity, and thick neck soft tissue, may also impair subsequent airway management, secretion clearance, and extubation readiness [26]. Heidegger notes that obesity, a predictor of difficult laryngoscopy, is associated with reduced functional residual capacity and decreased apnea tolerance that persist throughout the ICU course [26]. Difficult intubations often require multiple attempts and greater insertion forces, potentially causing airway trauma, mucosal ulceration, and inflammation [27, 28, 29]. Shinn et al. found that 57% of mechanically ventilated patients developed acute laryngeal injury, independently associated with larger endotracheal tube size and body habitus [27].

The sensitivity analysis comparing the initial versus the worst grade provides important mechanistic insight. The initial grade reflecting purely baseline anatomy showed a weaker and nonsignificant ordinal trend (OR: 1.04, p = 0.32), while the worst grade retained significance (OR: 1.14, p = 0.001). Grade worsening likely reflects progressive airway edema from repeated instrumentation, accumulating airway trauma, and the clinical instability necessitating re‐intubation. Elmer et al. demonstrated that reintubation is associated with significantly higher complication rates (13% vs. 5%, p = 0.02) [30]. Tanaka et al. found that reintubation is associated with increased in‐hospital and ICU mortality [31]. The worst grade thus functions as a composite marker capturing both anatomical difficulty and clinical trajectory.

Video Laryngoscopy

The protective effect of video laryngoscopy aligns with recent high‐quality evidence. Prekker et al., in a multicenter randomized trial of 1417 critically ill adults, demonstrated that video laryngoscopy increased first‐pass intubation success (85.1% vs. 70.8%, p < 0.001) [32]. A Cochrane systematic review found that video laryngoscopy likely reduces failed intubation rates and increases first‐pass success [33]. Araújo et al.'s meta‐analysis of 14 RCTs demonstrated significantly increased first‐pass success (RR: 1.12) and reduced esophageal intubations (RR: 0.44) [34]. Our findings support routine documentation of laryngoscopy method and preferred use of video‐assisted techniques in ICU intubations [32, 35].

Tracheostomy Timing and Early Risk Stratification

The optimal timing of tracheostomy remains controversial [6, 36]. Han et al.'s meta‐analysis of 21 RCTs found that early tracheostomy shortened ICU stay (MD: −6.36 days) but did not significantly alter mortality [36]. Kishihara et al.'s network meta‐analysis demonstrated that tracheostomy ≤ 4 days may result in lower short‐term mortality than tracheostomy ≥ 13 days (RR: 0.67), though certainty was very low [37]. A critical challenge is that many patients randomized to late tracheostomy never require the procedure [7]. Our findings suggest that airway grade documentation, integrated with admission diagnosis, could contribute to early risk assessment. While airway grade alone is insufficient for clinical decision‐making, it provides an objective, readily available marker that complements existing prediction models.

Clinical Significance

The most clinically actionable finding is the identification of high‐risk subgroups through the combination of airway anatomy and admission diagnosis. Patients with Grade ≥ 3 airways in high‐risk diagnostic categories (e.g., respiratory failure, neurological emergencies) represent a particularly vulnerable population warranting proactive airway management planning, including consideration of early otolaryngology consultation and heightened surveillance for extubation readiness. The identification of patients whose grade worsens across re‐intubation events, who demonstrate a 38.8% tracheostomy rate, provides an additional early warning signal. Serial documentation of airway grade across re‐intubation events may identify this ultrahigh‐risk subgroup earlier in the clinical course.

Our findings complement rather than replace existing prediction models. Integration of airway grade into models incorporating admission diagnosis and post‐admission variables may improve overall discrimination [5, 38].

Comorbidity Patterns

Problem list analysis revealed that tracheostomy patients had paradoxically lower comorbidity burden (mean CCI: 5.7 vs. 7.0, p < 0.001), consistent with survivorship bias, as patients with the highest comorbidity burden may die before tracheostomy can be performed. Comorbidities enriched in tracheostomy patients included cerebrovascular disease (+5.0%) and hemiplegia/paraplegia (+3.4%), consistent with neurological conditions predisposing to prolonged ventilation. Comorbidities depleted in tracheostomy patients included CHF (−5.3%), liver disease (−5.3%), and peripheral vascular disease (−5.1%), likely reflecting high early mortality in these populations.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the retrospective design precludes causal inference, though the temporal relationship (grade documented before tracheostomy) and biological plausibility support the association. Second, airway grades were available for 60.5% of encounters; patients without documentation may differ systematically. However, the 60.5% documentation rate is comparable to real‐world clinical practice and enhances external validity. Third, Cormack–Lehane grading has known inter‐rater variability, though we used the worst documented grade to capture the most conservative assessment. We were unable to control for the level of training of the airway evaluator; less experienced proceduralists may assign higher grades, potentially influencing observed associations. However, use of the worst grade across all events may partially mitigate this by capturing assessments from multiple providers [23]. Fourth, principal problem classification captures the primary admission indication but may not fully reflect the complexity of clinical decision‐making regarding tracheostomy; specific indications such as failed extubation versus anticipated prolonged ventilation, number of failed extubation attempts, and provider decision‐making factors were not available. Fifth, the study period (2018–2024) spans the COVID‐19 pandemic, which altered tracheostomy practices, patient populations, and airway management approaches; however, this heterogeneity also enhances generalizability across varied clinical contexts. To directly address this concern, we performed three COVID‐19 sensitivity analyses, confirming the robustness of the airway grade‐tracheostomy association. Sixth, the modest effect size (OR: 1.13–1.14 per grade) and incremental AUC (ΔAUC: +0.003 beyond the diagnosis‐adjusted model) indicate that airway grade alone is insufficient for clinical decision‐making and must be interpreted alongside admission diagnosis, comorbidities, and evolving clinical trajectory. Finally, we cannot exclude residual confounding from unmeasured variables such as neurological status or goals‐of‐care preferences.

Future Directions

These findings warrant prospective validation in external cohorts. Future studies should investigate whether early identification of high‐risk patients based on the combination of airway grade and admission diagnosis leads to improved outcomes through earlier tracheostomy or enhanced airway management strategies. Integration of airway grade with machine learning models incorporating post‐admission variables, diagnostic categories, and ventilator data may yield more robust prediction tools. Prospective studies should evaluate the impact of systematic airway grade documentation on clinical practice patterns and patient outcomes. As a preliminary step toward clinical translation, we developed a point‐based tracheostomy likelihood scoring system integrating airway grade with admission diagnosis, age, and comorbidity burden (Figure 4). This bedside tool assigns integer points for each variable, yielding a total score that maps to estimated tracheostomy probability, and is intended to support early goals‐of‐care conversations at the time of intubation. The scoring system is undergoing prospective refinement for a dedicated validation study. Additionally, future work should examine whether the laryngoscopy method modifies the prognostic significance of airway grade, as video laryngoscopy significantly shifts grade distributions and may alter grade interpretation.

Conclusions

The Cormack–Lehane airway grade at intubation independently predicts tracheostomy risk in critically ill patients, retaining significance after adjustment for admission diagnosis, comorbidities, and demographics (aOR: 1.13, p = 0.014). Tracheostomy risk varies substantially by admission diagnosis, with COVID‐19 respiratory failure, transplant, and cardiac patients at highest risk (Figure 5). Sensitivity analysis demonstrates that the worst documented grade outperforms the initial grade as a predictor, capturing both baseline anatomy and the clinical trajectory of repeated airway difficulty. The 2.9% of patients whose grade worsened across re‐intubation events represent an ultrahigh‐risk subgroup with a 38.8% tracheostomy rate. These findings support systematic airway grade documentation integrated with admission diagnosis as part of comprehensive ICU risk assessment and warrant prospective validation.
