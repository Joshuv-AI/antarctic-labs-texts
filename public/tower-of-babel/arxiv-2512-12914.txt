CTIGuardian: A Few-Shot Framework for Mitigating Privacy Leakage in Fine-Tuned LLMs
Shashie Dilhara Batan Arachchige, Benjamin Zi Hao Zhao, Hassan Jameel Asghar, Dinusha Vatsalan, Dali Kaafar

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are often fine-tuned to adapt their general-purpose knowledge to specific tasks and domains such as cyber threat intelligence (CTI). Fine-tuning is mostly done through proprietary datasets that may contain sensitive information. Owners expect their fine-tuned model to not inadvertently leak this information to potentially adversarial end users. Using CTI as a use case, we demonstrate that data-extraction attacks can recover sensitive information from fine-tuned models on CTI reports, underscoring the need for mitigation. Retraining the full model to eliminate this leakage is computationally expensive and impractical. We propose an alternative approach, which we call privacy alignment, inspired by safety alignment in LLMs. Just like safety alignment teaches the model to abide by safety constraints through a few examples, we enforce privacy alignment through few-shot supervision, integrating a privacy classifier and a privacy redactor, both handled by the same underlying LLM. We evaluate our system, called CTIGuardian, using GPT-4o mini and Mistral-7B Instruct models, benchmarking against Presidio, a named entity recognition (NER) baseline. Results show that CTIGuardian provides a better privacy–utility trade-off than NER based models. While we demonstrate its effectiveness on a CTI use case, the framework is generic enough to be applicable to other sensitive domains.

The risks related to a fine-tuned LLM in the CTI domain can be explained via a sample deployment scenario. Consider a collaborative CTI setting where multiple organizations contribute threat reports, logs, and vulnerability data to fine-tune a shared LLM. While such a model enhances collective defense by aggregating intelligence, it also inherits sensitive details unique to each contributor such as internal IP ranges, employee emails, open ports or unpatched software versions. An adversarial user, say an inside attacker, with black-box access could recover sensitive details from the fine-tuned LLM model if it is prone to memorization

Using this dataset, our
second contribution
is to show that LLMs fine-tuned on CTI data are indeed vulnerable to privacy leakage. We show that these models can memorize sensitive information from training data and unintentionally regurgitate it at inference. By adapting a prefix-based data extraction attack
[

,
36
]
to the CTI domain, we demonstrate that carefully crafted prompts can be used elicit memorized details when queried.

Given that fine-tuned CTI models are vulnerable to privacy leakage, it is essential to investigate strategies that can mitigate this risk and safeguard sensitive information.
In practice, most defenses attempt to protect privacy either during or after fine-tuning, but both approaches face limitations. Datasets can be sanitized before fine-tuning, either by redacting sensitive entities with named entity recognition (NER) tools
[
40
]
or by adding noise via Differential Privacy (DP)
[
16
]
.
NER based redaction is lightweight but limited in handling obfuscations and poorly suited for CTI-specific formats
[
61
]
(see
subsection VIII-A
). DP offers formal guarantees, but its noise injection often reduces overall utility
[
31
]
, as the resulting perturbations can disrupt the contextual integrity of CTI data and hinder meaningful interpretation. As a post-training alternative, unlearning techniques aim to selectively remove memorized data
[
20
]
, yet they require access to model internals, incur high retraining cost, and are impractical in collaborative, shared model settings. This highlights the need for a practical alternative that can prevent sensitive data leakage without costly retraining or sacrificing utility.

To address this gap, our
third and main contribution
is the design of CTIGuardian, a privacy-preserving framework for CTI settings that both evaluates the privacy risks of fine-tuned LLMs and protects against sensitive information leakage at inference. Inspired by the broader safety alignment paradigm, CTIGuardian introduces the notion of privacy alignment, where language models are guided through supervisory mechanisms to avoid leaking sensitive information during inference. While safety alignment focuses on ethical boundaries and toxicity
[
17
]
, our approach pivots toward privacy alignment. Instead of preventing unsafe responses, CTIGuardian enforces privacy constraints post-training via few-shot prompting, without modifying the base LLM itself.

This design removes the need for costly retraining or labeled NER datasets. Through experiments, we show that CTIGuardian effectively mitigates privacy leakage while preserving model utility. Evaluations with GPT-4o mini and Mistral-7B Instruct demonstrate that our framework outperforms a named entity redaction baseline, with GPT-4o mini achieving the best privacy–utility balance.

II
Background

II-A

Threat Model

We assume an adversary with black-box access to a fine-tuned LLM. The adversary interacts by providing crafted prefixes and observing the outputs, with the ability to compute full next-token probability distributions and apply decoding strategies such as top-
k
sampling. A token refers to a subword unit from the model’s vocabulary, and for each prefix the model produces a probability distribution over all possible next tokens and top-
k
sampling select one of the most likely tokens from this distribution to extend the sequence. Prior work has shown this to be a realistic attack setting
[
9
,
31
]
. The adversary can obtain up to 256 generated tokens per query, with the objective of extracting memorized sensitive data categories defined in Section
II-C
.

II-B

Definition of Discoverable Memorization

We adopt the notion of discoverable memorization from prior work
[
36
,
8
]
to conduct the data extraction attack and quantify privacy leakage. Our objective is not to measure memorization itself, but to assess how much sensitive information leaks. Formally, if a model
\mathcal{M}
reproduces a suffix
s
when given a corresponding prefix
p
, where
d=[p\,\|\,s]
denotes the concatenation of prefix and suffix originating from the training dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
, then
\mathcal{M}
is said to have memorized
d
.

II-C

Definition of Privacy
Leakage

We quantify privacy leakage as the percentage of exact matches between sensitive items in the training set and those extracted from the model, computed separately for each sensitive category. The categories (
c
) considered in the CTI domain are IP addresses, email addresses, port numbers, domain names, and software versions. For each category
c
, leakage is defined as

L_{c}=\frac{\sum_{x\in D_{\text{extract}}}\mathbb{I}[x\in D_{\text{train}}]}{|D_{\text{train}}(S_{c})|}\times 100\%,

where
D_{\text{extract}}
is the set of extracted items,
D_{\text{train}}
is the training set, and
\mathbb{I}[x\in D_{\text{train}}]
is 1 if
x
appears in both.
|D_{\text{train}}(S_{c})|
is the total number of sensitive items of category
c
in the training set.

Datasets

.

Model Training and Evaluation

V-A

Data-Centric Knowledge Injection

To better adapt the base model to the CTI domain before fine-tuning, we perform data-centric knowledge injection.
We use a Next Token Prediction (NTP) objective
[
57
]
with autoregressive loss:

L(M)=-\sum\log M(t_{i}|t_{1:i-1})

(1)

where
M
is the model,
t_{i}
the predicted token, and
t_{1:i-1}
its context.
For this step, we use a publicly available CVE dataset
[
23

[

]
, which includes CVE IDs, descriptions, CVSS scores, affected products, CWE identifiers, and references.
This dataset is used only for knowledge injection, separate from the APTQA and CTI-MITRE datasets used for fine-tuning and leakage evaluation.
Knowledge injection was applied to Llama-2-7B
[
14
]
for 5 epochs on 2 NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPUs, balancing adaptation and overfitting.

V-B

Fine-Tuning the Model for a Downstream Task

We fine-tuned the knowledge injected Llama model on two curated datasets, APTQA and CTI-MITRE, for 14 epochs each to adapt to CTI downstream tasks while avoiding overfitting. Using a prompt–response format, loss was computed only on response tokens. The resulting models are referred to as the

and the

.
To assess the impact of knowledge injection, we fine-tuned a base Llama-2-7B model on the smaller APTQA dataset without knowledge injection. We only performed this using the APTQA dataset as it is the smaller dataset and performing this on multiple datasets is computationally expensive.

VI
Data Extraction Attack

VI-A

Extracting Training Data

To evaluate whether fine-tuned models memorize and regurgitate sensitive information, we adopt a black-box extraction attack inspired by prior prefix-based methods. Carlini et al.
[
9
]
showed that short, high-likelihood prefixes can trigger verbatim completions in GPT-2. Nasr et al.
[
36
]
extended this idea to larger models such as ChatGPT, using divergence based prompting and sampling prefixes from corpora like Wikipedia and The Pile.

Building on this insight, we use prefixes derived from our fine-tuning datasets. Since access to pretraining data is infeasible, this approach lets us directly evaluate leakage against ground-truth sensitive entities. To simulate an attacker’s perspective, we first sanitize the dataset used for fine-tuning with SpaCy’s
6
6

6

https://spacy.io/models/en#en_core_web_trf
NER model and regular expressions (regex), assuming the attacker only sees this sanitized corpus. Prefixes are then crafted from the remaining non-sensitive text and used to query the models.

These prefixes are provided as inputs to the fine-tuned model, each treated as an independent data point for generation. During inference, tokenized prefixes and their attention masks are processed to produce suffixes. Following prior work
[
9
,
31
,
60
]
, we adopt top-k sampling (
k=40
) with a maximum sequence length of 256 tokens, since wider beam search yields diminishing returns and reduces diversity. We fix the temperature at 0.5 to balance diversity and fidelity. To limit redundancy, we also apply a repetition penalty of 1.3 and enforce a no-repeat 3-gram constraint
[
60
]
. A sample of generated results are reported in Appendix
Table IX
.

VI-B

Quantifying Privacy Leakage

As described in Section
VI-A
, we performed a data extraction attack and analyzed the generated outputs using spaCy NER to detect sensitive entities. Following Section
II-C
, we restrict our analysis to exact matches, as they provide a clear measure of leakage. Approximate matches, while potentially useful, may capture coincidental similarities rather than actual memorization, making it difficult to attribute them to genuine leakage.

Prefix-based extraction successfully revealed sensitive data across all categories, as illustrated in
Figure 5
and
Figure 6
. For the APTQA model, port numbers (19.23%) and software versions (19.35%) leaked more than IPs (13.54%), emails (13.64%), and domains (15.5%). Similarly, for the CTI-MITRE model, leakage for IPs (12.15%) and emails (12.90%) was lower than for ports (18.46%), software versions (20.43%), and domains (21.92%).

Overall, port numbers and software versions show consistently higher leakage. A likely cause is duplication, as Kandpal et al.
[
24
]
show memorization is amplified by repeated data. In our datasets, common ports such as port 80 and software versions appear across many records, making de-duplication impractical without harming utility. This underscores the need for defenses that can handle such issues.

VII
Privacy Defense with CTIGuardian

The extraction results in Section
VI
highlight the risk of privacy leakage in fine-tuned LLMs. Few-shot safety alignment techniques demonstrate that with minimal supervision, models can be guided to avoid harmful outputs
[
4
]
. We argue that privacy leakage presents a parallel risk. Instead of harmful outputs, the threat is the unauthorized disclosure of sensitive information. Inspired by this, we adapt safety alignment principles to privacy by leveraging few-shot prompting. Our defense, CTIGuardian, introduces a supervisory layer that filters or redirects inputs and outputs containing sensitive data. This approach avoids retraining or additional labeled datasets, incurs minimal overhead, and this framework can be applied universally to fine-tuned LLMs.

CTIGuardian consists of two components: a privacy classifier and a privacy redactor, both powered by the same underlying LLM, which in this work are Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 and GPT-4o mini. These components are guided by a small set of handcrafted few-shot examples that specify how sensitive entities should be detected and transformed. Unlike handcrafted rules that hard-code specific patterns, our few-shot examples act as demonstrations that teach the model how to handle sensitive entities rather than what exact strings to match. This design enables CTIGuardian to adapt to unseen or obfuscated formats while avoiding the need for predefined schemas, large annotated corpora, or domain-specific regex engineering. Figure
2
illustrates the overall architecture.

VII-A

Privacy Classifier

The privacy classifier provides the first line of defense by deciding whether a prompt is privacy-intrusive or not, much like safety classifiers determine whether a prompt is harmful. In our setting, the classifier labels each prompt as either harmful, which triggers a refusal message, or harmless, which is then passed to the fine-tuned LLM for response generation.

We implement this as a few-shot classifier using a second LLM, distinct from the fine-tuned model under attack. Specifically, we employ GPT (closed-source) and Mistral (open-source) as representative classifier models, and study their performance independently under identical few-shot initialization. We observe that prompts may trigger sensitive information leakage even without explicitly requesting it. To capture this nuance, we define four categories of prompts: (a) direct requests, (b) indirect requests, (c) disguised requests, and (d) harmless requests. We then manually curated a diverse set of few-shot examples for each category. A representative subset of these examples is shown in

VII-B

Evaluating the Privacy Classifier

We evaluated the privacy classifier by randomly sampling 1,000 prefixes from each dataset (APTQA and CTI-MITRE) and manually labeling them to establish ground truth. Since manually labeling the full prefix sets was infeasible, we restrict evaluation to this representative subset. Each classifier produces both a binary decision (harmful or harmless) and an associated confidence score on a scale of 1–10, reflecting how strongly the model supports its classification.

Using the binary outputs, we report accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, false positive rate (FPR), and false negative rate (FNR), as summarized in
Table II
. GPT outperforms Mistral on both datasets, achieving 94.10% and 92.80% accuracy on APTQA and CTI-MITRE versus 88.20% and 80.30%. It also delivers higher precision, recall, and F1-scores, with lower FPRs (1.57%, 9.98%) and FNRs (10.43%, 4.29%). The observed FPRs and FNRs reflect the inherent ambiguity of CTI prompts, which often appear in short, obfuscated, or fragmented forms. These variations naturally create a trade-off between precision and recall, yet GPT consistently achieves lower error rates than Mistral, demonstrating stronger generalization despite the complexity of the domain.

To analyze trade-offs beyond a single decision threshold, we use the confidence scores to plot Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and compute the Area Under the Curve (AUC). As shown in Figure
3

VII-C

Privacy Redactor

While the classifier blocks harmful prompts, our extraction experiments show that sensitive information can still leak from seemingly benign inputs. An example of such leakage is shown in
Figure 4
. This highlights the limits of input-only filtering and motivates the need for output level protection.

To address this challenge, we introduce a
privacy redactor
that post-processes model outputs to detect and remove sensitive content. The redactor’s goal is twofold: (i) remove sensitive information, and (ii) transform the output to remain coherent and natural, without drawing attention to the redaction.

The privacy redactor is responsible for removing any data belonging to sensitive categories defined in Section
II-C
. Using few-shot learning with GPT and Mistral, the redactor learns to redact categories defined in Section
II-C
without relying on rigid NER rules. Representative few-shots are shown in Table

, with the full set in Appendix

.

While the redactor removes sensitive content from outputs, the

Relying solely on redaction would expose the model to adversarial inputs and burden post-hoc filtering, potentially harming output quality. Together, the classifier and redactor provide defense in depth, combining proactive input filtering with reactive output sanitization to preserve privacy without sacrificing utility.

Utility Preservation Comparison with Presidio

While Presidio achieves strong surface-level utility metrics by leaving sentence structure intact (
Table IV
), it offers only superficial privacy because placeholders explicitly reveal the type of sensitive data removed (
Table V
). This leakage invites reconstruction attacks, as an adversary can easily infer what kind of entity was present and where. In contrast, CTIGuardian not only detects obfuscated entities missed by Presidio but also rewrites the text to blend redactions seamlessly into fluent, semantically coherent sentences. For example, instead of leaving markers such as
<Email_Address>
or
<IP_Address>
, CTIGuardian rewrites the content naturally, concealing both the type and location of redactions. This difference is critical because Presidio masks entities, whereas CTIGuardian obfuscates and rewrites, thereby minimizing semantic leakage while maintaining readability.

The effectiveness of this approach is reflected in quantitative evaluation. As shown in
Table V
, CTIGuardian produces coherent outputs that preserve overall meaning while fully removing sensitive detail. While this results in modest reductions in BLEU and ROUGE-L due to structural changes, CTIGuardian maintains high semantic fidelity, with the best-performing model (GPT-4o mini) achieving 97.6% and 96.07% cosine similarity on APTQA and CTI-MITRE, respectively (
Table IV
). This demonstrates CTIGuardian’s ability to balance privacy and utility, providing robust redaction while preserving coherence and context.

VIII-B

Latency Analysis for CTIGuardian

We evaluate CTIGuardian’s efficiency by measuring latency and cost. GPT-4o mini achieves lower average latency (2.28s) compared to Mistral (3.17s), with classifier and redactor times of 1.05s and 1.23s respectively, versus 0.92s and 2.25s for Mistral. The operational cost per inference for GPT is only 0.008¢, making the approach both cost-effective and efficient while adding minimal overhead.

IX
Limitations and Future Work

Although our focus has been CTI data, CTIGuardian’s methodology is not domain-specific. The same principles of few-shot classification and redaction could extend to other sensitive domains such as healthcare, finance, and law, where models face similar risks of leaking private identifiers. Exploring such cross-domain applications remains an important direction.

Our defense currently relies on a fixed set of handcrafted few-shot examples for GPT-4o mini and Mistral. While effective, these may not be optimal for all model architectures. Future work could explore model-specific or automated prompt construction to further improve classification and redaction.

At present, CTIGuardian removes all detected entities to guarantee privacy. This conservative approach prevents leakage but may reduce utility through over-redaction. More precise strategies such as targeting only memorized entities or anonymizing sensitive details could better balance privacy and utility.

Finally, our evaluation used only Llama-2 7B, as training and knowledge injection across multiple models is computationally expensive. Since CTIGuardian operates at the input and output levels, it remains compatible with any fine-tuned LLM. Future work could extend evaluation to larger models and domains to further validate robustness and scalability.

X
Conclusion

In this work, we analyze the privacy risks of LLMs fine-tuned on CTI data and introduce CTIGuardian, a few-shot-based novel defense framework that mitigates sensitive data leakage during inference. By combining a privacy classifier and redactor, CTIGuardian effectively reduces leakage across multiple sensitive categories, outperforming traditional NER based methods.
Our findings show that strong privacy protection can be achieved without retraining the underlying model. As future work, we aim to refine CTIGuardian by improving few-shot generalization and enabling selective redaction for better privacy–utility trade-off.

XI
Ethics

This work does not involve private user data. All potentially sensitive information, such as IP addresses or email addresses, was sourced from publicly available cyber threat intelligence reports. No personally identifiable information (PII) from private individuals was collected or exposed, and all examples provided are anonymized or redacted to ensure ethical handling.

(b)

The predicted CWE ID from the generated model output is matched against the expected CWE ID for the input.

(c)

CVE to CWE Mapping:
The CVE ID extracted from the actual output is mapped to its corresponding CWE ID. This is then compared to the CWE ID from the generated output.

(d)

CVE to Pillar Mapping:
The CVE ID from the generated output is mapped to its corresponding Pillar, and the CWE ID is also mapped to a Pillar. These mappings are checked for consistency.

(e)

CWE to Pillar Mapping:
The CWE ID from the generated output and the CWE ID from the actual output are both mapped to their respective Pillars, and the mappings are compared.

(f)

Actual CVE to Generated CVE Pillar Mapping:
The CVE ID from the actual output is mapped to its corresponding Pillar. The same is done for the CVE ID from the generated output, and the Pillar mappings are compared.

(g)

CVE to Base Severity Mapping:
The CVE ID from both the actual and generated outputs is mapped to its corresponding Base Severity score, and the results are compared.
