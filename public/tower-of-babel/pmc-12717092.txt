Radiological assessment and dose evaluation from natural radionuclides in common pasta
Authors: Hossein Sadeghi, Reza Pourimani, Mohadese Feyzi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, the specific activities of the radionuclides 226Ra, 232Th, 40K, and 137Cs in popular pasta samples were measured. The risk indices of the annual effective dose received (AED) and the permitted consumption rate (CR) were calculated.12 samples of pasta were collected from the food stores of Arak city. The specific activities of the mentioned radionuclides were measured by gamma-ray spectrometry using a high-purity germanium (HPGe) detector with a relative efficiency of 80%. The specific activities of radionuclides ranged from the minimum detectable activity (MDA) to 2.55 for 226Ra, from MDA to 19.84 for 232Th, and from 44.3 to 88.2 for 40K in Bq/kg. The specific activity of 137Cs for all samples was ≤ MDA (0.88 Bq/kg). The average values for annual effective dose and allowed consumption rate were calculated as 9.31 µSv/y and 491 kg/y. The results indicate that the radiological parameters for the analyzed pasta samples are well below international safety limits, suggesting no significant radiological health risk to consumers.

Introduction

Pasta is a staple food product derived from plants and is widely consumed worldwide. It encompasses a diverse range of food products and is a significant component of diets worldwide1. Italy is the leading producer of pasta, accounting for approximately one-fourth of the world’s total production2. In Iran, pasta production reaches 0.56 million tons annually, with a per capita consumption of approximately 8.5 kg, reflecting its importance in the Iranian diet3. Despite its nutritional value and widespread consumption, the safety of pasta and other food products can be compromised by environmental contaminants, including radioactive materials.

The presence of radioactive elements in the environment is a growing concern because of various human activities and natural processes. Industrial land development, coal combustion waste, petrochemical production, nuclear accidents, nuclear weapons testing, and nuclear waste generation are among the primary sources of radionuclides released into the soil4. These radioactive substances can be absorbed by plants and subsequently enter the food chain. Humans are exposed to these radionuclides either directly through the consumption of plant-based foods or indirectly through animal products fed on contaminated plants. This pathway of exposure raises significant public health concerns because prolonged ingestion of radioactive materials can have harmful effects on human health.

When the radiation received through food consumption exceeds a certain threshold, it can lead to harmful biological effects. These effects may manifest as various diseases, including cancer, or result in genetic abnormalities that can be passed on to future generations. Given the potential health risks associated with radionuclide exposure, monitoring and assessing the levels of radioactive elements in commonly consumed food products, such as pasta, is important. This is particularly important in regions where environmental contamination may be higher because of industrial or nuclear activities. Recently, we examined the concentrations of radioactive isotopes and heavy metals in bottled mineral water5. The results showed that the levels of the radionuclides 232Th, 40K, and 137Cs were below the World Health Organization thresholds, whereas 226Ra was undetectable. The annual effective radiation doses were significantly lower than the international safety limits, and heavy metals such as lead and chromium were not detected. Using machine learning models, the study provided robust predictions, underscoring the minimal health risks of consuming mineral water in the region.

This study aims to evaluate the radiological parameters of pasta consumed in Iran. Specifically, the research focuses on measuring the specific activity of four radioactive elements 226Ra, 232Th, 40K, and 137Cs using gamma-ray spectrometry with a high-purity germanium (HPGe) detector, which has a relative efficiency of 80%6. These elements were selected based on their environmental prevalence and potential health impacts. By quantifying the concentrations of the ingredients in the pasta samples, this study aimed to assess the radiological risks associated with pasta consumption. The findings will contribute to a better understanding of food safety and provide valuable data for regulatory bodies to establish guidelines for acceptable levels of radionuclides in food products. In summary, this research addresses a critical aspect of food safety by investigating the presence of radioactive elements in pasta, a widely consumed food item. By combining advanced analytical techniques with a focus on public health, this study aims to shed light on the potential risks posed by radionuclides in food and contribute to the development of strategies to mitigate these risks. The outcomes of this work will not only benefit consumers but also support efforts to ensure the safety and quality of food products in Iran and beyond.

This paper is structured as follows: Sect. 2 outlines the procedures, equipment, and analytical approaches used for evaluating radioactive isotope activity levels in pasta samples. Section 3 presents and discusses the results of radiological measurements, comparing findings from pasta samples collected in Arak City with international data and established safety standards. Lastly, Sect. 4 summarizes the study’s key findings and their implications for food safety monitoring.

Materials and methods

Sampling and sample Preparation

Twelve different brands of pasta, including Jahan, Mak, Tak, Zar, Mai, Mana, Savin, Zhik, NC, Shahang, Samira, and Ash Novin, were collected from various food stores in Arak City (see Table 1). Sampling was performed in the spring of 1402. Each sample was ground into a fine powder, and 318 g of each sample was placed in specially coded cylindrical containers.

To prevent the escape of radon gas, the lids of the cylindrical containers were sealed with aquarium glue. The date of preparation of each sample was recorded on the containers, which were stored away from direct sunlight and heat in a nuclear laboratory. After allowing enough time for the radon gas to reach equilibrium (50 days), work began on determining the specific activity of the radioactive elements 226Ra, 232Th, 40K, and 137Cs in the samples. For the spectroscopy of the samples collected in this study, a coaxial HPGe detector (model GCD30195 from BSI, with a relative efficiency of approximately 80% and an energy resolution of 1.95 keV for the gamma line of 60Co with an energy of 1332.520 keV) was used. The operating voltage is 3000 V. The detector was placed in two protective layers of lead (10 cm thick) and copper (3 mm thick. Cosmic rays are reduced to a negligible level by a shield made of lead metal with a thickness of 100 mm. To reduce the scattering effect of radiation from the shield, the detector is placed in the center of the shield, and the samples are placed on it for one day and night (86400 s). A standard container containing the sample is placed on the crystal detector in the form of a cap. The spectrum obtained from the samples was collected and analyzed using the Lsrmbsi software7–10. The results are shown in Fig. 1.

To ensure statistical robustness, three independent sub-samples (n = 3) were collected for each pasta brand, representing distinct production batches. Each sub-sample was homogenized and analyzed separately. This approach adheres to the ISO 18589-3:2015 guidelines for environmental radioactivity measurements, ensuring reproducibility (relative standard deviation < 15% for ²²⁶Ra/²³²Th and < 10% for ⁴⁰K). The sample size (N = 12 brands × 3 sub-samples = 36 total) provided 80% statistical power to detect activity variations exceeding ± 20% (α = 0.05, β = 0.2) based on prior pasta matrix studies16.

The activity of 226Ra in the samples was determined using gamma rays belonging to 214Pb with an energy of 351.93 keV and gamma rays of 214Bi with an energy of 609.31 keV. Additionally, the activity of 232Th was determined using two gamma lines belonging to 228Ac, one with an energy of 911.2 keV and an intensity of 26.6%, and the other with an energy of 968.97 keV and an intensity of 17.4%. The specific activities of 40K and 137Cs were determined using the gamma lines of these nuclei at energies of 1460.70 and 661.66 keV, respectively13.

The results of this measurement of the specific activities of the radionuclides 226Ra, 232Th, 40K, and 137Cs are compared with those of similar studies conducted in several other countries (Table 5). The conclusion is that, in general, the specific activity of natural radioactive elements, except for the MF-4 sample, is lower than the values ​​reported in the mentioned countries, such as Italy, Hungary, Turkey, Indonesia, Ukraine, and the United Arab Emirates18,19. In the MF-4 sample, the value of thorium was higher than in other countries, which can be related to the soil structure of the raw materials and is similar to the values ​​reported in Italy. While this could suggest the use of imported ingredients from regions with higher natural thorium background, such as Italy, it could also originate from specific local agricultural regions in Iran with unique geological characteristics. This outlier highlights the importance of traceability in the food supply chain and continuous monitoring, even for products generally considered safe.

The elevated ²³²Th content in sample MF-4 is notable due to its high radiotoxicity and long half-life (1.4 × 10¹⁰ years), highlighting the importance of monitoring this radionuclide in food products [UNSCEAR, 2020]. The absence of ¹³⁷Cs (≤ MDA: 0.12–0.88 Bq/kg) indicates negligible anthropogenic contamination from nuclear incidents, in contrast to the Ukrainian pasta (0.04 ± 0.007 Bq/kg) (Table 5). For context, the AED from MF-4 (45.72 µSv/y) represents 0.02% of the ICRP annual dose limit (1 mSv/y) for the public.

The radiological parameters associated with pasta ingestion included AED and CR. The lowest CR was observed for the Zar pasta (MF-4) at 59 kg/year. The highest AED was also recorded for the Zar pasta (MF-4), with values of 45.72 µSv/y and 34.86 Bq/kg, respectively. These values indicate a higher potential health risk associated with Zar pasta consumption due to its high radionuclide content.

Figure 4 demonstrates the strong inverse correlation between AED and CR across the pasta samples, where samples with elevated AED values consistently exhibit diminished CR values. The majority of brands cluster within a narrow range of low AED (< 10 µSv/y) and high CR (> 300 kg/y), indicating uniformly low radiological risk; however, sample MF-4 (Zar) is a conspicuous outlier, exhibiting the highest AED (45.72 µSv/y) and the lowest CR (59 kg/y), which is directly attributable to its significantly elevated 232Th content. This stark contrast underscores that while the overall radiological safety of the pasta supply is excellent, the identified outlier emphasizes the critical importance of continuous monitoring within the food supply chain to identify and investigate samples that deviate from the norm, even when their calculated doses remain well below international safety limits.

Summary and conclusion

This study presents a systematic radiological evaluation of 12 commercial pasta brands from Arak, Iran, using high-resolution gamma spectrometry. Our investigation was motivated by the critical need to establish baseline radioactivity data for staple foods in the region. The analysis revealed distinct activity concentrations across samples, with ²²⁶Ra ranging from below the detection limits to 2.55 Bq/kg, ²³²Th reaching 19.84 Bq/kg in Zar pasta (MF-4) - potentially due to imported ingredients - and consistent ⁴⁰K levels between 44.3 and 88.2 Bq/kg, while ¹³⁷Cs remained undetectable throughout. The calculated radiological indices AED and CR, for all samples, were found to be well below international safety limits, indicating no significant radiological health risk associated with pasta consumption in the studied region. These findings provide the first systematic dataset on radionuclide levels in Iranian pasta products and establish a valuable baseline for future food safety monitoring. Looking ahead, this research suggests several promising directions for future work. First, expanding the monitoring program to encompass additional staple foods across different geographic regions would provide more comprehensive safety data. Second, investigating the relationship between soil radioactivity in agricultural areas and subsequent crop uptake could yield valuable insights into contamination pathways. The development of standardized protocols for food radiological assessment and examination of seasonal variations in radionuclide concentrations would further enhance monitoring capabilities. Additionally, further research into the origin of elevated ²³²Th levels in specific samples would be valuable for understanding supply chain variations.
