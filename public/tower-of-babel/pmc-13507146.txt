Morphological identification of fungi associated with preharvest spoilage of sweet orange (Citrus sinensis L.) fruit in Bilo Nopha, Southwestern Ethiopia
Authors: Gobena Adeba, Tekalign Kejela, Tesfalem Atnafu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sweet orange (Citrus sinensis L.) is a major fruit crop globally, but fungal pathogens have severely impacted its yield. Bilo Nopha, known for high-quality sweet oranges, has recently faced significant preharvest spoilage. This study aimed to isolate fungal pathogens associated with preharvest spoilage of sweet orange (Citrus sinensis L.) fruit in Bilo Nopha, southwestern Ethiopia. In the present study, a total of 240 symptomatic orange fruit samples were collected from six orchards of study area and fungi pathogens were isolated and characterized to genus level following standard microbiological techniques. A total of 430 fungal isolates were recovered. Pseudocercospora spp. were most prevalent (45.0%), followed by Colletotrichum spp. (42.1%). Alternaria spp. showed moderate prevalence (30.4%) with significant site variation (15.0–37.5%, P < 0.05). Rhizopus spp. were consistent across sites (20–30%, total 27.1%), while Fusarium and Phytophthora spp. had the lowest rates (17.5% each). Pseudocercospora spp., Colletotrichum spp., and Phytophthora spp. were identified as the primary fungal pathogens, whereas Alternaria spp., Rhizopus spp., and Fusarium spp. were involved as secondary or opportunistic pathogens. Species-level identification and targeted management are recommended to reduce losses and sustain production.

Introduction

Sweet orange (Citrus sinensis L.) is an important fruit crop worldwide. It’s valued for its vitamin C, fiber, bioactives, and flavonoids like hesperidin, which support immunity, lower cardiovascular risk, and provide anti-inflammatory and anticancer benefits1,2. Global production exceeds 78 million metric tons, over half from Brazil, India, and China3. Africa’s leading producers are Egypt, South Africa, Algeria, and Morocco, while Ethiopia produces relatively little (41,890 tons in 2021; 25.2 kilotons in 2022) despite favorable conditions4.

In southwestern Ethiopia, smallholder farmers rely on traditional practices, with Bilo Nopha District offering ideal conditions for high-quality oranges. However, production and quality are declining due to disease, notably preharvest fruit rot, causing > 500,000 kg annual losses (Bilo Nopha Agricultural Annual Report, 2022) and prompting crop replacement. Infected fruits are often sold, posing health risks.

Fungal pathogens are the main cause of spoilage of sweet orange fruit across the world, with Colletotrichum gloeosporioides, Pseudocercospora angolensis, Colletotrichum acutatum, Botryodiplodia theobromae, Alternaria citri, and Phomopsis citri causing major preharvest losses6–10. In Africa, Aspergillus niger, Colletotrichum gloeosporioides, and Alternaria alternata are common5,11; in Ethiopia, Colletotrichum gloeosporioides and Colletotrichum boninense often cause fruit and leaf spot9.

In Bilo Nopha, the specific pathogens driving these losses remain unknown, limiting effective control. To this end, this study aimed to identify fungal pathogens linked to preharvest spoilage in the district.

Methods

Description of the study area

Bilo-Nopha Woreda, in Iluu Abba Bora Zone, Oromia Regional State, southwestern Ethiopia (8°02′42″–8°31′18″N; 35°37′48″–36°05′18″E), lies 615 km from Addis Ababa and 18 km from Mettu. Covering 39,000 ha across 16 kebeles, it sits at 1600–1800 m altitude with mainly mountainous terrain (95% mid-highland). Land cover includes coffee woodlands (20,230 ha), cultivated woodlands (9707 ha), and savannah/grazing/marshlands (9063 ha)12. Orange is grown as a garden crop by nearly all farmers.

Sampling and sample collection

Symptomatic sweet orange fruits were purposively collected from six orchards (Kitabir, Kodohiri, Jafti, Abu, Adare, and Karo Mariam) in Bilo Nopha Woreda during peak production (June–July 2022) based on visible rind symptoms. The symptoms include circular brown to black lesions, sunken necrotic spots, raised corky or scabby lesions with cracked surfaces, chlorotic halos around lesions, and advanced stages showing large, irregular coalescing dark patches and rind tissue breakdown (Fig. 1). A total of 240 samples (40 per site) were gathered with farmers’ consent, placed in sterile polyethylene bags, transported to Mattu University’s microbiology laboratory, and processed for fungal isolation within 12 h.

Isolation of fungi

Fungi were isolated from samples using standard morphological procedures13. Fruits were surface-disinfected in 70% ethanol (1 min), 1% sodium hypochlorite (10 min), rinsed thrice with sterile water, and from each symptomatic fruit, multiple tissue sections showing distinct symptoms a segment (3–6 mm) of each sample was cut with a sterile scalpel, placed on PDA (Oxoid) with 0.3% chloramphenicol. Plates were incubated at 27 °C for 3–10 days and examined daily. Colonies of varying colors (brown, black, green, white) were sub cultured to obtain pure isolates. Pure cultures were stored at 4 °C for further experiments. All assays were in triplicate.

Morphological characterization and genus-level identification of fungi

Pure isolates were characterized and identified based on macroscopic (colony color, size, texture, pigment, margin) and microscopic (hyphae, shape, type, color) features, as well as biochemical traits13,14. For macroscopic assessment, spores from 1-week-old cultures were suspended in sterile water and inoculated (triplicate) onto PDA plates, incubated at 25–30 °C for 7–10 days, and colony diameter, margin, color, and texture recorded. Microscopic characterization used the slide culture technique13,15, where PDA blocks on slides were inoculated, covered with coverslips, and incubated for 1–3 days at 25–30 °C. Post-incubation, lactophenol blue: glycerol (1:1) was added, and fungal structures observed under 10× and 40× objectives using Motic Images Plus 2.0. Identification followed taxonomic keys and morphological descriptions16,17.

Physiological characterization of the pathogen

Determination of the optimum temperature and growth rate

To determine optimal growth temperature, 6 mm plugs of representative fungal isolates were inoculated on PDA plates and incubated at 15 °C, 20 °C, 23 °C, 25 °C, 28 °C, 30 °C, 33 °C, or 37 °C for 10 days. Daily radial growth was measured, and the temperature with the highest growth rate was deemed optimal. Experiments were triplicated under controlled pH, humidity, and time. Fungi were classified as fast (> 10 mm/day), moderate (5–10 mm/day), or slow (< 5 mm/day) growers.

Carbon source utilization

Carbon utilization was tested on basal mineral salt media with a single carbon source (2% sugars; 0.5–1% polymers). Growth on glucose, sucrose, and fructose was compared to a no-carbon control after 7–14 days at 25–30 °C; sucrose use indicated invertase activity18. Starch hydrolysis (amylase) was shown by clear zones on starch agar after Gram’s iodine19, and cellulose degradation (cellulase) by yellow halos on CMC agar after Congo red de-staining20,21. All assays were in triplicate.

Nitrogen source utilization

Nitrate utilization was tested on nitrogen-free media (e.g., Czapek-Dox) with 0.3% KNO₃. Cultures were incubated at 25 °C for 7–10 days with positive (ammonium sulfate) and negative (no nitrogen) controls; growth indicated assimilation. Nitrate reduction was assessed via sulfanilic acid and N-naphthyl-ethylenediamine (red/pink = nitrite). If negative, zinc was added: red = unreduced nitrate; no color = complete reduction. Fungi were classified as nitrate utilizers, reducers, or denitrifiers17,22. All assays were in triplicate.

Extracellular enzyme production

Fungal extracellular enzyme production was detected via substrate-specific agar assays, where enzyme activity is visualized by hydrolysis zones or colorimetric changes. For amylases, starch agar plates are flooded with Gram’s iodine; clear zones indicate starch hydrolysis23. Pectinases were tested on pectin agar using CTAB (opaque halos)24. The cellulase test was performed by using CMC agar stained with Congo red, where yellow halos, after destaining, confirmed activity20. Proteases were checked by using skim milk/casein agar, with a clear zone indicating protein degradation23. Lipases were detected on tributyrin agar, where clear zones indicate positive results25. All tests were checked against negative controls. All the experiments were performed in triplicate.

Oxygen requirement

Fungal oxygen requirements were assessed by comparing growth in aerobic (loose-capped thioglycollate broth at 25–30 °C) and anaerobic conditions. Inoculated thioglycollate broth showed surface growth (aerobiosis) or deep growth (anaerobiosis). Growth was monitored for 3–14 days, species-dependent. Results were classified as obligate aerobes (aerobic only), facultative anaerobes (both), microaerophiles (low O₂ only), or obligate anaerobes (anaerobic only)26,27.

Pathogenicity tests

Pathogenicity tests were performed on healthy Citrus sinensis fruits following standard methods28. Fruits were washed, surface-sterilized with 70% ethanol for 1 min, and air-dried inside a laminar airflow hood. A Completely Randomized Design with two factors (six fungal isolates plus control × wounded and unwounded fruits) was used, with three fruits per treatment in triplicate (42 fruits total). Fruits were inoculated with 10⁶ spores mL⁻¹ or mycelial suspension from two-week-old PDA cultures; controls received sterile water. Inoculated fruits were incubated at 25–27 °C for 14 days and checked daily for symptoms. The disease severity was recorded using a five-point scale where 0 indicated no symptoms, 1 represented 1–25% of the fruit surface infected, 2 denoted 26–50%, 3 signified 51–75%, and 4 represented 76–100% infection. Symptomatic tissues were re-isolated on water agar, and recovered cultures were compared to the original isolates. The experiment was repeated twice.

Method of data analysis

Qualitative characteristics of the fungal isolates were grouped based on morphological similarity using Microsoft Excel. Quantitative data (fungal isolation rates) were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0. Differences in isolation rates among the six sweet orange orchard sites were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Fungal growth data were analyzed using a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA, with incubation temperature as the between-subject factor and incubation time (days) as the within-subject factor. The analysis tested the main effects of temperature and time, as well as their interaction. When significant effects were detected, mean comparisons were performed using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test. Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05.

Results

Isolation of pathogenic fungi

From 240 symptomatic orange fruits collected across six sites in Bilo Nopha (40 fruits per site), a total of 430 fungal isolates were recovered, with multiple fungal isolates obtained from most symptomatic fruits in the study area. Kitabir had the highest number (82), followed by Addisalem (78) and Aggata (77), with no significant differences among these three (P > 0.05). The remaining sites yielded 60–67 isolates, with Qaro Mariyam having the lowest (60). No significant differences occurred among Qaro Mariyam, Abbu, and Kodohiri (P > 0.05), but isolates from these sites were significantly fewer than those from Kitabir, Addisalem, and Aggata (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2).

Physiological characterization of the pathogen

Determination of the optimum temperature and growth rate

Rhizopus spp. showed a rapid growth rate, with colonies reaching a mean diameter of 78.33 mm in 4 days on PDA at 25 °C (Fig. 4F). The growth rates of Colletotrichum spp., Phytophthora spp., and Alternaria spp. were moderate, with colonies reaching diameters of 76.33 mm, 69.33 mm and 62 mm, respectively, after 7 days of growth on PDA at 25 °C (Fig. 4A, B, D). Similarly, the growth rate of Fusarium spp. was moderate, with colonies reaching a diameter of 55.67 mm after 7 days of growth at 28 °C on PDA (Fig. 4C). However, Pseudocercospora spp. showed a slow growth rate, with colonies reaching a diameter of 43 mm after 10 days of incubation at 25 °C on PDA (Fig. 4E). All the fungal groups showed optimum growth at 25–30 °C, except for Fusarium spp., which presented an optimum growth temperature of 27–30 °C. The growth of all the isolates was inhibited at 37 °C and above (Fig. 4A-F).

Nutrient utilization, enzyme production, and oxygen requirement tests for the fungal isolates

All isolates utilized simple sugars and nitrates (Table 2). Colletotrichum, Alternaria, Rhizopus and Fusarium showed fast growth on starch agar; while Phytophthora and Pseudocercospora showed slow/no growth. Enzyme profiles varied: Rhizopus produced abundant amylases, pectinases, and lipases; Alternaria and Fusarium showed strong amylase and cellulase activity; Colletotrichum showed strong pectinases and cellulases activity; Phytophthora and Pseudocercospora showed minimum activity of the tested enzyme except pectinases. Rhizopus was facultatively anaerobic; others were strictly aerobic.

Distribution of fungal species associated with preharvest spoilage of sweet orange fruits

Pseudocercospora spp. were most prevalent (45% overall; 55% in Kitabir to 30% in Qaro Mariam, P < 0.05), followed by Colletotrichum spp. (42.1%; 50% in Kodohiri to 30% in Qaro Mariam, significant only between these sites). Alternaria spp. showed moderate prevalence (30.4%; 15–42.5%, P < 0.05). Rhizopus spp. ranked fourth (27.1%), with stable rates except in Qaro Mariam. Fusarium and Phytophthora spp. were least common (17.5% each), with Phytophthora spp. varying significantly (12.5–25%, P < 0.05). Highest fungal loads occurred in Kitabir, Addisalem, and Aggata, driven by high Phytophthora, Colletotrichum, and Alternaria rates, while Qaro Mariam was generally lower except for Alternaria (37.5%) (Table 3).

Pathogenicity test of fungal isolates

Disease severity differed significantly among the tested fungal isolates. Pseudocercospora spp. showed the highest severity (86.5 ± 4.1%, p < 0.05), followed by Phytophthora spp. (74.6 ± 5.1%), Colletotrichum spp. (74.3 ± 5.6%), and Rhizopus spp. (71.4 ± 3.7%) (Table 4). Alternaria spp. caused moderate severity (53.8 ± 4.8%), whereas Fusarium spp. showed low severity (21.7 ± 2.5%). In wounded samples, all isolates grew from day 2, while in nonwounded samples only Colletotrichum spp., Pseudocercospora spp., and Phytophthora spp. showed growth from day 4 to day 14.

Discussions

This study identified the major fungal pathogens responsible for preharvest spoilage of sweet orange (Citrus sinensis L.) across six orchards in Bilo Nopha, southwestern Ethiopia. Multiple fungal species were frequently isolated from individual symptomatic fruits, with a total of 430 isolates recovered from 240 symptomatic fruit samples. The consistent isolation of more than one fungal type from the same fruit indicates frequent co-occurrence of pathogens, underscoring the complex etiology of preharvest sweet orange fruit spoilage under orchard conditions.

Among the isolated fungi, Pseudocercospora spp. were the most prevalent, accounting for 45% (108/240) of the isolates. Species of Pseudocercospora are well known for their negative impact on citrus production, primarily through leaf and fruit spot diseases that reduce photosynthetic capacity, weaken trees, and lower yield. Infected fruits typically develop raised or corky lesions with yellow halos on young fruits; flat to slightly sunken brown lesions on mature fruits; rind cracking at lesion sites resulting in uneven coloration and surface blemishes that severely reduce market acceptability. The morphological characteristics observed in this study, including gray-green colonies and truncated conidia, were consistent with Pseudocercospora angolensis6,29–31, a major and economically important citrus pathogen in Africa30–33.

Colletotrichum spp. were the second most frequently isolated fungi, representing 42.5% (101/240) of the isolates. The isolates exhibited diverse morphological features characteristic of the Colletotrichum species complex34–37, particularly C. gloeosporioides and C. boninense, which are commonly associated with citrus anthracnose in Ethiopia9 and worldwide11,36–39. The presence of appressoria and melanized conidia enhances their ability to penetrate fruit tissues40,41, and their prevalence is likely promoted by favorable climatic conditions typical of the study area36,38.

Phytophthora spp. were also commonly isolated and displayed morphological traits such as white rosette-shaped colonies, coenocytic hyphae, and chlamydospore formation, consistent with previous descriptions10,42 and earlier reports from Ethiopia43. The subtropical climate of the region likely facilitates zoospore production and dispersal. Major species such as P. citrophthora, P. nicotianae, and P. palmivora are known to infect citrus roots, trunks, and fruits, causing gummosis and substantial yield losses worldwide7.

Alternaria spp. accounted for 30.4% of the isolates and exhibited woolly brown to black colonies with septate conidia, consistent with the morphology of Alternaria alternata, the causal agent of citrus black rot44–47. This pathogen produces cell wall–degrading enzymes that contribute to tissue maceration and disease development in citrus fruits globally48–50.

Rhizopus spp. constituted 27.1% of the isolates and showed morphological similarity to Rhizopus stolonifer, including gray colonies, rhizoids, and sporangiophores51,52. Their facultative anaerobic metabolism and production of degradative enzymes likely enhance survival and colonization in humid orchard environments, particularly where management practices are suboptimal.

Fusarium spp. were among the least frequently isolated pathogens (17.5%). Despite their lower incidence in this study, Fusarium spp. are recognized as major threats to global sweet orange production due to their association with citrus wilt, stem-end discoloration, and fruit rot. Notably, F. oxysporum and F. equiseti have been linked to significant yield and economic losses53–55.

The pathogenicity assay showed significant variation in disease severity among fungal isolates, with Pseudocercospora spp. causing the highest severity (86.5%), followed by Phytophthora spp., Colletotrichum spp., and Rhizopus spp., indicating strong pathogenic potential. Earlier studies indicate, the high pathogenicity of Pseudocercospora spp. is underpinned by a suite of molecular virulence factors that facilitate host colonization and suppression of plant defenses56. Pseudocercospora species deploy a large complement of effector proteins, including RXLR, LysM, and Y/F/WxC families, which can interfere with host immune recognition and manipulate host physiology to promote infection57. These secreted effectors, along with small cysteine‑rich proteins homologous to known effectors (e.g., PfAvr4 and PfEcp2), are predicted to suppress host defenses and protect fungal structures such as the cell wall during early infection stages58. Alternaria spp. showed moderate pathogenicity, while Fusarium spp. exhibited weak pathogenicity. Similar results have been reported where Colletotrichum and Phytophthora species were identified as major citrus fruit pathogens causing severe postharvest and field diseases59,60. Alternaria spp. have also been associated with moderate citrus fruit rot, whereas Fusarium spp. are commonly reported as weak or opportunistic pathogens61,62.

Although this study focused on fungal pathogens, preharvest citrus fruit spoilage likely results from complex interactions among fungi, bacteria, environmental conditions, and host-related factors. Therefore, the pathogens identified here should be regarded as major contributors rather than the sole causal agents of fruit deterioration, and future study should identify the role of bacterial or viral pathogens in preharvest citrus fruit spoilage in the study area.

The observed temperature-dependent growth responses of these pathogens further indicate that environmental conditions in citrus-growing regions strongly influence pathogen development, disease severity, and epidemic potential. Temperature governs pathogen growth kinetics and infection efficiency, with the prevalent genera exhibiting maximal activity under warm conditions (approximately 20–30 °C). Within this range, Colletotrichum spp. show enhanced conidial germination and appressorium mediated penetration, while Pseudocercospora spp. display increased sporulation and lesion expansion.

Moisture availability is the primary driver of infection dynamics. High relative humidity and prolonged leaf wetness are essential for conidial germination in both pathogens, with Colletotrichum being particularly dependent on free water for host penetration. Rainfall events further amplify disease pressure by facilitating splash dispersal and extending leaf and fruit wetness duration, thereby accelerating secondary infection cycles.

These findings have important practical implications for orchard management, as periods of favorable temperature may increase infection risk and pathogen proliferation. Orchard management modulates these environmental effects by shaping canopy microclimate and inoculum reservoirs. Dense, poorly ventilated canopies favor humidity retention and disease development, whereas pruning, optimized spacing, and removal of infected tissues reduce inoculum load and disrupt pathogen life cycles. Collectively, this study provides valuable insights to support improved disease management strategies and to help minimize economic losses in citrus production systems.

Limitations

A main limitation of this study was reliance on morphological traits for fungal identification, restricting identification to the genus level. Molecular methods were not used due to budget and facility constraints. Since species-level identification requires multi-locus sequence analysis, the exact pathogen could not be determined. In addition, the current study also not assessed bacterial and viral contributions of pre-harvest spoilage of Citrus sinensis fruit in the study area. Future studies should incorporate molecular approaches for precise species identification.

Conclusions

Multiple fungal species were associated with preharvest fruit spoilage of Citrus sinensis in the study area. Pseudocercospora spp., Colletotrichum spp., and Phytophthora spp. were identified as the primary fungal pathogens, whereas Alternaria spp., Rhizopus spp., and Fusarium spp. were involved as secondary or opportunistic pathogens. Immediate implementation of appropriate disease management measures is essential to reduce yield losses and sustain citrus production. In addition, molecular-based studies are recommended to clarify fungal species diversity, population structure, and spatial distribution. Although this study focused on fungal pathogens, further investigations incorporating bacterial and viral diagnostics would be valuable to fully elucidate the multifactorial nature of preharvest citrus fruit spoilage.
