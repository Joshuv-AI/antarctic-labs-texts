Surface-termination control of nonlinear dynamics in functionalized 2D NEMS
Authors: Massoud Malaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurface functionalization provides an important route for tuning the properties of two-dimensional (2D) materials, yet its implications for nonlinear nanomechanical behavior remain insufficiently understood. Here, we examine how surface termination may influence the mechanical and nonlinear response of functionalized 2D nanoelectromechanical systems (NEMS), with emphasis on MXenes and comparison with graphene oxide and MoS₂. By combining literature-derived structural and elastic data with a reduced-order nonlinear resonator framework, we examine the roles of termination-dependent bonding, stiffness, electrostatic tuning, and parametric excitation. The analysis indicates that surface termination can modify the local bonding environment and thereby influence the effective mechanical response of MXene resonators. Cross-material and termination-dependent comparisons are presented as qualitative trends rather than as a universal quantitative relationship between bond length, stiffness, and nonlinearity. The resulting framework provides a physically motivated basis for relating surface chemistry to resonance behavior and for developing termination-by-design strategies for functionalized 2D NEMS. These results highlight surface termination as a potentially useful material-level design parameter for controlling the mechanical response and functionality of nanoscale resonators.Keywords: MXenes, Nanoelectromechanical systems (NEMS), Surface termination, Nonlinear dynamics, Parametric resonance

Introduction

The continued miniaturization of nanoelectromechanical systems (NEMS) towards atomic-scale dimensions increasingly drives device operation into the nonlinear regime. Although nonlinear behavior has traditionally been regarded as a limitation, nonlinear phenomena such as parametric amplification, bistability, and frequency-comb generation have recently emerged as powerful mechanisms for sensing, signal processing, and frequency control. Understanding the physical origins of nonlinear response has therefore become a central challenge in the development of next-generation NEMS technologies [1–3].

Among two-dimensional (2D) materials, MXenes have attracted considerable attention owing to their combination of metallic conductivity, solution processability, and exceptional mechanical properties. MXenes are a family of two-dimensional transition-metal carbides and nitrides, generally represented as Mn+1XnTx, obtained by selective removal of the A-layer from layered MAX-phase precursors. The Tx groups denote surface terminations such as –O, –OH, and –F, which can substantially modify the structural, electronic, and mechanical properties of the resulting sheets [4–8]. Early studies established the elastic properties of monolayer Ti₃C₂Tₓ and demonstrated its suitability for high-frequency nanomechanical resonators. Lipatov et al. measured the elastic modulus of monolayer Ti₃C₂Tₓ using AFM nanoindentation, reporting a Young’s modulus of approximately 330 GPa [9], while Xu et al. [10] demonstrated electrically tunable MXene resonators operating at very high frequencies. More recently, Ahmed et al. [11] revealed an intriguing coupling between mechanical vibration and surface chemistry through the recovery of oxidized MXenes under high-frequency electromechanical excitation. Despite these advances, the role of surface termination in governing nonlinear mechanical behavior remains poorly understood [12, 13].

Existing theoretical descriptions of nonlinear nanomechanical resonators have focused primarily on idealized systems in which surface chemistry plays no explicit role. Comprehensive nonlinear frameworks have been developed for atomically thin resonators, capturing phenomena including Duffing nonlinearity, nonlinear damping, and frequency scaling [14, 15]. At the same time, theoretical studies have demonstrated that MXene surface terminations strongly influence structural stability, bond characteristics, and elastic properties [13, 16–20]. However, these two research directions have remained largely disconnected. Current nonlinear resonator models cannot predict how bond-level chemical modifications alter nonlinear response, nor can they provide design rules linking surface functionalization to device performance. Addressing this challenge requires a shift from purely mechanical descriptions towards chemistry-aware models capable of connecting atomic-scale interactions with emergent nonlinear dynamics.

Here we develop a reduced-order framework for examining how surface termination may influence the mechanical and nonlinear response of functionalized 2D nanoelectromechanical systems [14, 15]. The framework combines literature-derived structural and elastic information with established nonlinear-resonator descriptions. In this interpretation, surface termination is treated as a material-level variable that can modify local bonding, elastic stiffness, and the anharmonic response of the membrane [13, 18–20]. Importantly, the present analysis does not assume that bond length alone determines stiffness or nonlinearity; rather, bond geometry, termination chemistry, and the resulting interatomic interactions are considered collectively [21, 22]. The cross-material comparison is therefore used to illustrate qualitative trends rather than to establish a universal quantitative bond-length–nonlinearity law.

Results and discussion

We begin by establishing the conceptual link between surface termination and nonlinear mechanical behavior. Figure 1 presents a three-panel framework that encapsulates our central finding.

Conceptual framework: from surface chemistry to nonlinear dynamics

Panel A (Physical mechanism)

The atomic structures of –F, –O, and –OH terminations on Ti₂C are shown schematically. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations by Zha and co-workers reveal systematic variations in metal–termination (M–T) bond lengths: –F exhibits the shortest bond (2.05 Å), –O intermediate (2.10 Å), and –OH the longest (2.23 Å). This bond length variation reflects termination-dependent changes in local bonding geometry. However, bond length alone does not uniquely determine the effective stiffness of the functionalized MXene, which also depends on termination chemistry and the resulting atomic interactions. Consequently, the material’s nonlinear response—which emerges from the anharmonicity of the interatomic potential—is strongly modulated by termination chemistry.

Panel B (Cross-material evidence)

The relationship between stiffness and nonlinearity extends beyond MXene. We compiled data from three distinct 2D material families: MXene (DFT calculations), graphene oxide (experimental nanoindentation by Suk and colleagues), and MoS₂ (DFT literature). The comparison is intended to illustrate the qualitative variation in mechanical stiffness and nonlinear response across these material families, rather than to establish a quantitative cross-material correlation. Because a common quantitative nonlinearity metric is not available across the cited studies, the nonlinearity levels are presented qualitatively as relative low, intermediate, and high response.

Panel C (Design map)

Based on the observed correlation, we construct a termination-by-design map (Fig. 1C). –F termination (green) occupies the high-stiffness, low-nonlinearity region, making it more suitable for sensing-oriented applications where linearity and stability are paramount. –OH termination (red) occupies the low-stiffness, high-nonlinearity region, suitable for oscillator and parametric amplification applications where nonlinearity is exploited. –O termination (blue) provides intermediate behavior for general-purpose use.

Conclusion

This study developed a reduced-order framework for examining the influence of surface termination on the mechanical and nonlinear response of functionalized 2D NEMS. By combining literature-derived structural and elastic properties with an established nonlinear-resonator formulation, we examined how changes in termination chemistry may modify the mechanical response of MXene resonators. The analysis emphasizes that bond length alone does not uniquely determine stiffness or nonlinearity; rather, the response reflects the combined effects of local bonding geometry, termination chemistry, and atomic interactions.

The comparison with Lipatov et al. provides a mechanical benchmark for the force–deflection behavior of MXene, while the parametric and termination-dependent analyses illustrate possible routes for connecting material-level chemistry with device-level functionality. Importantly, the cross-material comparisons and termination-dependent nonlinear classifications are intended as qualitative design guidance rather than as universal quantitative correlations or experimentally validated instability thresholds.

Overall, the results support the use of surface termination as a potentially effective design parameter for tailoring the mechanical and dynamic behavior of functionalized 2D resonators. The proposed framework provides a physically motivated starting point for termination-by-design strategies, while quantitative prediction of nonlinear coefficients, parametric gain, and stability boundaries will require termination-specific experimental or atomistic data in future work.

Calculations and graphical analyses were performed using Python. The termination-dependent resonance-frequency curves were generated from the scaling relationship given in Eq. Material parameters

MXene properties were taken from Lipatov et al. (E = 330 ± 30 GPa, ρ = 3900 kg/m³, ν = 0.227). Termination-dependent bond lengths and elastic moduli were extracted from DFT calculations by Zha et al. and Dahlqvist & Rosen. Graphene oxide data were from Suk et al. (E = 207.6 GPa). MoS₂ data were from DFT literature. The complete set of numerical inputs used for the reported calculations is summarized in Table 1. For the mechanical benchmark, the model response and experimental force–deflection data were compared directly over the reported 10–90 nN force range using nine paired data points. The coefficient of determination (R²) and mean absolute error (MAE) were calculated from these paired values.
