Breaking barriers: transforming healthcare education for transgender patient equity in a South African undergraduate paramedic program
Authors: Llizane McDonald, Simpiwe Sobuwa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite growing global attention to diversity, equity and inclusion in healthcare, transgender health remains insufficiently addressed in undergraduate medical curricula, particularly in prehospital emergency care training. Transgender individuals often experience discrimination and substandard care linked to healthcare providers’ limited knowledge and cultural competence. Emergency medical services (EMS) personnel, as first responders, play a crucial role in shaping early patient experiences, yet EMS education in South Africa has seldom addressed transgender health content in formal curricula.MethodsThis paper presents a qualitative, reflective case study of a curriculum innovation introduced in a South African undergraduate paramedic programme. Between 2019 and 2024, approximately six cohorts of 40 students each participated in a targeted one-hour session. The intervention combined LGBTQAI+ terminology and clinical considerations, a transgender lived-experience guest speaker and structured Q&A, reflective storytelling grounded in EMS practice, and the Genderbread Person as a conceptual scaffold. Evaluation drew on educator reflection/classroom observation and an interpretive thematic review of anonymous post-session questionnaire feedback, focusing on student reactions to curricular content, instructional strategies, the learning environment, and perceived learning.ResultsStudent feedback indicated strong engagement with the session and reported conceptual clarification regarding distinctions between sex, gender identity and expression, and sexual orientation, supported by the shared visual framework. Students described the lived-experience speaker and reflective storytelling as making barriers to care and the consequences of unconscious bias more tangible, and many valued a safe, non-judgmental learning environment that enabled candid questions and correction of misconceptions. Reported learning included perceived shifts in awareness, empathy and preparedness to engage respectfully with transgender patients. However, some expressed discomfort or concerns about infringement of personal cultural or religious beliefs and questioned curricular relevance.ConclusionAlthough the intervention does not measure clinical competence or behaviour change, it offers a feasible, contextually responsive teaching model for introducing transgender health in prehospital education. Its subsequent scaling to other departments within the Faculty of Health and Wellness Sciences suggests institutional transferability beyond a single cohort. To advance equitable, patient-centred emergency care, transgender health content should be embedded within formal curricula and supported by institutional leadership and longitudinal evaluation.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09361-x.

Introduction

Inclusive medical education incorporates equity, diversity and social justice, creating a learning environment that prepares healthcare professionals to serve diverse patient populations [1]. It challenges traditional models of curriculum design that have historically marginalised underrepresented individuals, including ethnic and cultural minorities, people with disabilities, LGBTQAI+ individuals, and those from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds [2].

The World Health Organisation (WHO) has committed to achieving universal health coverage by 2030, which requires an integrated health system that provides a quality and people-centred service to all [3]. The Global Competency Framework for Universal Health Coverage (UHC) classifies the competencies needed to achieve this goal as: people-centredness, decision-making, communication, collaboration, evidence-informed practice and personal conduct.

Undergraduate education represents the entry point into the healthcare system for aspiring practitioners. These formative years profoundly shape how graduates approach patient care and decision-making [4]. However, these undergraduate programmes often prioritise biomedical content over psychosocial dimensions. This leaves limited space for teaching social determinants of health, cultural competence, or inclusive practice [5–8].

While many communities encounter discrimination and inequity in healthcare, transgender patients experience particularly profound barriers. To understand the context of transgender issues in healthcare, it is important to acknowledge the historical pathologisation of gender-variant identities. Until the publication of ICD-11 in 2019, transgender identity was classified within the mental and behavioural disorders chapter of the International Classification of Diseases, and a diagnosis of “gender identity disorder” remained in the DSM until its reclassification as “gender dysphoria” in DSM-5 (2013). The ICD-11’s reclassification of this experience as “gender incongruence” under conditions related to sexual health marked a major shift in global policy, reframing gender diversity as part of human variation rather than mental illness [9]. Despite this shift, discrimination and inadequate training continue to compromise the care transgender individuals receive worldwide.

Recognising this critical gap in existing training, we present an educational intervention, the first of its kind in the undergraduate paramedic curriculum in Cape Town. The aim of this paper is to explore the perceived impact of this intervention by (i) analysing student reactions to the curricular content, instructional strategies, and learning environment, and (ii) describing perceived learning in terms of reported changes in knowledge, attitudes and preparedness. It does not claim causal attribution but rather explores perceived learning and attitudinal shifts arising from participation in the intervention.

Discrimination and inequality

Access to healthcare without fear of harassment or judgment is a fundamental human right [10]. However, transgender individuals frequently encounter stigma, discrimination and barriers to care. These experiences include social exclusion, violence and a pervasive lack of understanding among healthcare professionals [11, 12]. International research has documented routine denial of care, inappropriate clinical interactions and widespread discomfort among providers treating transgender patients [13–22]. A large European survey revealed that more than half of transgender respondents reported discrimination in the previous year [23], while a U.S. national survey found that 23% had been denied care after disclosing their identity [24].

In South Africa, despite constitutional protections against discrimination [25], similar patterns persist for transgender and gender-diverse patients. The Ritshidze report [26] documented widespread privacy violations and breaches of confidentiality in services used by gender-diverse populations, undermining trust in the healthcare system. Müller et al. [27] found that most transgender patients had encountered some form of discrimination from healthcare providers based on their gender identity. Further local studies describe hostile interactions, misgendering and outright refusal of treatment for transgender patients [28, 29].

These acts are often justified by the cultural and religious beliefs that frame homosexuality as “un-African” [30]. This toxic environment is exacerbated by the extremely high prevalence of violence targeting sexual and gender minorities, including frequent, horrific crimes such as the misnamed “corrective rape” [31–33]. These systematic attacks underscore the profound personal safety risks and vulnerability faced by transgender and gender-diverse individuals seeking care.

Compounding these challenges is the lack of institutional support for transgender health.

Only six public hospitals in South Africa offer gender-affirming care, with waiting lists extending up to five years for hormone replacement therapy and 25 years for the first surgery [12]. The lack of accessible, affirming care, combined with social rejection, discriminatory government policy and hostile healthcare environments [11], drives many transgender patients away from seeking healthcare [34]. This leads to reduced health screening, increasing their risk of disease [5]. Adding to the burden, multiple NGOs that run transgender clinics were forced to close after the PEPFAR funding freeze by the United States in 2025, leaving many patients without their hormone and Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) treatments [35]. PrEP is a particularly important biomedical HIV-prevention tool for transgender populations [36, 37]. Transgender women globally face HIV prevalence several times higher than the general adult population, with the burden especially pronounced in sub-Saharan Africa, while structural barriers, including provider discrimination, gender-insensitive services and interactions between feminising hormone therapy and PrEP regimens, further complicate access [36, 37].

This avoidance of care has serious health consequences. Transgender individuals have been shown to experience high rates of chronic illness and psychological stressors, including cardiovascular disease, substance abuse, HIV, sexually transmitted infections, tuberculosis, mental health disorders and suicidality [5, 11–13, 38, 39]. Adding to these challenges, medical insurance exclusions often force transgender people to self-source hormones through unregulated channels, further increasing health risks [13]. Furthermore, patients frequently find that they have to educate their providers about their health needs, undermining trust and placing additional burdens on those already marginalised [12, 13].

In sum, the combination of inadequate medical education, systemic prejudice, and institutional barriers results in profound inequities in healthcare access and outcomes for transgender individuals. Addressing these issues demands not only inclusive policies but also urgent reform of the training and attitudes of healthcare professionals. Out-of-hospital care settings, which often serve as the first point of medical contact, have been largely neglected in this regard, highlighting the need for targeted interventions within emergency medical education.

The gaps in the medical undergraduate curriculum

Given the disparities in healthcare access and outcomes for transgender patients, medical education plays a critical role in addressing these inequities. In 2015, the World Medical Association called “for appropriate expert training for physicians at all stages of their career to enable them to recognise and avoid discriminatory practices, and to provide appropriate and sensitive transgender health care” [40].

Similarly, Volmink [41] emphasises that health professions education should produce knowledgeable, competent, relevant, and socially accountable healthcare professionals who can confidently address the country’s burden of disease across the continuum of care.

This includes equipping graduates to meet the specific health needs of transgender patients by integrating education on gender identity and sexual orientation into the curriculum.

Despite these calls for an all-inclusive medical curriculum, numerous studies have documented significant gaps in undergraduate medical education [42–46]. Medical students frequently report feeling unprepared and lacking confidence in providing care to transgender patient [47]. A common finding across the literature is the absence of structured LGBTQAI+ education in undergraduate medical curricula, which results in unchallenged biases such as homophobia and transphobia that students may bring with them into training. Evidence suggests that these biases are well-documented and may persist across training in the absence of targeted curricular interventions [5, 48, 49]. In addition, reports of heterosexism and anti-LGBTQAI+ discrimination within medical training environments often originate from peers, suggesting that such biases are actively reproduced rather than systematically challenged [5].

South African research echoes these international concerns. Studies have found that curricula rarely include clear learning outcomes related to gender identity and sexual orientation, and that these topics are often taught inconsistently, based on individual educators’ discretion [27, 47]. Educators themselves often report uncertainty about how to address transphobia among students, further hindering progress [11]. For example, a study [5] at one of South Africa’s largest undergraduate health sciences faculties revealed a lack of structured teaching on LGBTQAI+ health. Students were not encouraged to reflect on their own attitudes towards the LGBTQAI+ community, nor were they adequately trained to address the specific health needs of these patients. Although the undergraduate medical curriculum is based on the Primary Health Care approach, which emphasises holistic patient care, it still lacks a systemic focus on gender and sexuality [50]. Contributing factors include an overcrowded curriculum, large class sizes, and a reliance on didactic teaching, which limits opportunities for interactive and reflective learning.

Considering the positive impact LGBTQAI+ inclusive education can have on patient experiences and healthcare providers’ confidence, similar education for EMS students could foster socially conscious practitioners and improve patient care. To achieve this, such education must comprehensively address attitudes, skills and knowledge, empowering students to take histories, create inclusive environments and provide compassionate, competent care to all patients.

Methods: addressing the curriculum gap in a South African undergraduate paramedic programme

This paper adopts a qualitative, reflective case study design to describe and analyse a curriculum innovation introduced within a South African undergraduate paramedic programme. The innovation emerged in response to an identified educational need by the first author to better prepare students to provide affirming and inclusive care for transgender patients.

The account is based on the professional reflections and classroom observations of two educators directly involved in the intervention, combined with analysis of student feedback obtained through post-session questionnaires. The reflection considered the context, rationale, pedagogical choices, and perceived impact on student learning. Although the intervention did not originate as a formally designed research study, this reflective approach enables a critical examination of its development. It situates the lessons learned within broader debates on inclusive medical education and emergency care training. To align with the qualitative design, we did not perform inferential or descriptive statistical analyses of questionnaire items; the analysis focused on the thematic interpretation of student narratives and reflections.

The educational intervention was first introduced in 2019 within the second-year Emergency Medical Care module at a university in Cape Town. From 2019 to 2024, approximately six annual cohorts of 40 undergraduate paramedic students participated in the session. The initial iteration was delivered as a two-hour lecture but was subsequently revised to a one-hour session after feedback revealed declining attention and engagement. The revised format incorporated more visual aids and interactive strategies to sustain participation. Since 2022, the intervention has been extended to other disciplines with the Faculty of Health and Wellness Sciences, including nursing, radiography, ophthalmology and dental assisting.

The session content was designed and delivered by the first author, with the department head’s approval. The learning objectives were explicitly aligned to the instructional strategies used in the session. Objective 1 was to clarify distinctions between sex, gender identity, and sexual orientation. This was achieved through the Genderbread Person visual aid [51] (Fig. 1), to provide a structured framework for positioning these constructs as related but distinct continua. Objective 2 was to raise awareness of healthcare barriers and humanise the patient experience by inviting a transgender speaker to share an authentic, lived-experience dialogue on healthcare encounters and barriers. Objective 3 was to equip students with inclusive communication and professional skills. This was addressed through reflective storytelling in which the lecturer drew on past clinical errors to model self-reflection and illustrate how bias and non-verbal reactions affect patient trust and care.

There was a deliberate attempt to create an environment where participants could feel psychologically secure enough to express uncertainties, ask questions without fear of judgment, and engage in potentially uncomfortable conversations about bias, knowing their contributions would be met with respect and constructive dialogue. We recognise that this was an intention rather than a guaranteed outcome and that, despite these efforts, some students, particularly quieter participants and those with strongly held religious or cultural beliefs, may have remained uncomfortable or disengaged. To reduce discomfort associated with sensitive content, a light-hearted but respectful disclaimer was provided at the outset, making it clear that the session was not intended to change personal beliefs but rather to inform students about communities they may not previously have encountered, whose members are nonetheless deserving of equal treatment. This framing explicitly acknowledged the students’ diverse cultural and religious backgrounds while underscoring their constitutional and professional obligations to treat all patients with dignity. Students were encouraged to ask questions freely, regardless of how challenging or uncomfortable, so that misconceptions could, where possible, be surfaced and addressed. We acknowledge that this approach could not guarantee that all misconceptions would emerge, particularly those held by students who chose not to speak.

At the conclusion of each session, an anonymous post-session questionnaire was distributed to all attendees. The lecturer designed the tool for use in teaching evaluations. It assessed three main areas: the campus climate (safety and inclusion of the LGBTQIA+ community), the perceived impact of the workshop (changes in knowledge, views, and preparedness), and the influencing factors on student perceptions (culture, religion, and the role of the medical professional). The questionnaire included both closed and open questions. Open responses invited reflection and elaboration, and the quotations cited in the results originate from these open-ended comments. The same evaluation form was used across all iterations. Although the evaluation was not designed for formal research, informal thematic grouping of qualitative responses identified recurring themes. Each student’s response was anonymised using an identification code for quotation purposes. Participation was voluntary, and students completed the questionnaire immediately after the workshop, limiting findings to short-term responses.

Results

Clarifying distinctions (curricular content, including the Genderbread Person)

Students’ comments indicated that the session filled baseline knowledge gaps and introduced language many had not previously encountered. Several explicitly positioned the workshop as their first meaningful exposure to transgender identities and related terminology, for example:

“never known of the transgender community” “…The workshop is educational and as someone who comes from rural areas where gay people are regarded as people who are possessed and as a taboo to our culture I benefited a lot from the workshop.” (S13).

However, very few students explicitly named the Genderbread Person in their open-ended feedback. As a result, student reactions to this specific visual tool could only be interpreted indirectly through broader comments about “misconceptions,” new understanding, and clarified distinctions, rather than through direct, tool-specific statements.

At the same time, some responses suggested resistance to the curricular content itself (regardless of content), including perceptions of limited relevance or duplication of prior exposure: “…I am a Christian and I felt I could have used that time on something else- the media shares a lot of info on the LGBTQ+ community so when the lecture started, I felt that I already knew a lot of the things they were saying.” (S22).“…I really feel that I treat all my patients with respect and dignity irrespective of their beliefs or sexual orientation … I could have done something else.” (S31).

Humanising the patient experience and surfacing barriers (guest speaker)

The transgender guest speaker was consistently described (in student questioning and educator observation) as a key catalyst for engagement and for challenging assumptions. Students asked candid, experience-oriented questions, including:“What made you decide to become transgender?”, “When did you decide?”, “Do you feel discriminated against?”, and “How difficult was it to make the transition?

Inclusive communication and professional conduct (reflection & application)

Students responded strongly to teaching moments that linked bias to clinical consequences and professional behaviour. Reflections highlighted the value of a non-judgmental facilitation style and educator modelling:

Students reported that the light-hearted disclaimer and explicit reassurance that the session was not intended to challenge their personal beliefs created an atmosphere conducive to honest discussion.

“…I enjoyed how the lecturer answered questions with no judgment, even if they might have been rhetorical or biased. Learning environment as an enabling condition (psychological safety; culture/religion)

Among students who chose to speak in class and those who submitted open-ended written feedback, many described a willingness to ask questions they might otherwise have suppressed. This openness enabled several misconceptions to be addressed directly. We cannot, however, extrapolate from these vocal and self-selecting participants to the cohort as a whole: quieter students, non-responders and students who disengaged from the session may have experienced the environment very differently, and their data perspectives are under-represented in our data: “…I absolutely loved the session, The lecturer began the session by stating that she did not come to change anyone’s beliefs or values, which I personally feel was a good way to start it off in order to avoid emotional feedback from the audience.”(S2).“…The lecturer created a safe and inclusive space for participants to share their thoughts and experience, which is particularly important when discussing sensitive topics like gender identity.” (S11).

Discussion

This paper reflects on the perceived impact of a brief, structured teaching session within an EMS context. In designing the intervention, we aimed to help students develop a shared conceptual understanding of sex, gender identity and expression, and sexual orientation; recognise how stigma and lived experience shape transgender patients’ healthcare encounters; and translate this into professional communication and self-regulation in clinical interactions.

These aims informed the pedagogical choices, using the Genderbread Person and guided dialogue as a conceptual scaffold, a transgender lived-experience speaker to make barriers concrete, and reflective storytelling grounded in EMS practice to link attitudes to patient trust and observable behaviours (including non-verbal cues). From a faculty perspective, the session appeared to work at the level it was intended to influence: students engaged more openly, shifted toward more precise terminology, and asked more clinically oriented questions as the session progressed. The intervention was also feasible to deliver and was subsequently scaled to other departments in the Faculty, reinforcing our view that the approach was transferable beyond this single cohort and context.

A critical component of this alignment was the integration of the Genderbread Person graphic as a shared conceptual scaffold. This choice is consistent with the broader training literature, which shows that LGBTQAI+ education is most effective when it moves beyond purely didactic information delivery toward structured, multimodal approaches that build usable knowledge and skills [52–54]. In practice, the visual framework appeared to help students differentiate between gender identity, expression, biological sex, and sexual orientation, understanding each as a continuum rather than binary choices.

Creating a safe, non-judgmental space was pivotal to ensuring students could ask questions without fear of judgment. The careful use of humour, balanced with respect, reduced tension and made the session approachable without trivialising the subject matter [55, 56]. While there remains a paucity of literature that explicitly theorises or evaluates how safe space functions pedagogically within transgender health education, the broader psychological safety literature is clear on the underlying mechanism: when learners experience an environment as respectful and non-punitive, they are more willing to speak up, ask clarifying questions, and disclose uncertainty, which in turn makes misunderstandings visible and correctable [57–59].

In this sense, our findings are consistent with psychological safety scholarship on why learning interactions that lower fear of judgement, particularly those that invite open dialogue, can support deeper engagement and more direct correction of misconceptions, even where topic-specific evidence in transgender health education remains limited.

The inclusion of a transgender guest speaker added authenticity and directness that could not be replicated by lecture content alone, while the lecturer’s willingness to share personal clinical experiences reinforced the tangible consequences of unconscious bias in patient encounters. Unlike some initiatives that focus primarily on attitudes, this workshop explicitly integrates knowledge, reflection, and practical application, a combination shown to be more effective for long-term competency development [60].

Nevertheless, this reflection acknowledges several conceptual and methodological limitations. The evaluation measured immediate perceptions (Kirkpatrick’s model of training evaluation levels 1–2) [61]; it did not assess behaviour change in practice (level three) or patient outcomes (level four). Without a pre-test or control group, it is not possible to attribute perceived improvements directly to the intervention or to confirm that awareness translated into competence. Additionally, the post-session questionnaire was designed as a teaching-evaluation instrument rather than a research tool. Voluntary, anonymous written responses are subject to self-selection bias: students who felt most positively engaged are likely to be over-represented.

In contrast, those who felt alienated, distressed or dismissive of the session may be under-represented. On reflection, some questionnaire items may have invited reflection on perceived benefits of the session more readily than on discomfort or disagreement. The full instrument is provided as Supplementary Appendix A for transparency, and we recommend that future iterations use more neutral probes and include items that explicitly invite expressions of discomfort, resistance or distress.

Future work should address these limitations through longitudinal evaluation. A follow-up study of graduates one to two years into practice could explore whether the intervention influenced actual clinical behaviour and interactions with transgender patients. There is also scope to expand this educational model to other patient groups often marginalised in prehospital care, such as individuals with autism or hearing impairments. While students are taught to elicit patient medical history, little attention is given to how this process must be adapted when patients face communication barriers.

For example, obtaining a medical history from a patient who is hard of hearing requires modified strategies such as written communication, visual aids, or ensuring access to interpreters. Without such preparation, students may know the framework in theory but remain ill-equipped to apply it in practice when faced with patients whose needs do not align with standard verbal questioning. Embedding such teaching into the core emergency medical care curriculum, rather than as an isolated intervention, would ensure consistency of exposure and signal institutional commitment to inclusive practice.

Overall, this case study appears to have fostered perceived shifts in empathy, reflection, and preparedness among paramedic students. With further institutional support and longitudinal evaluation, such approaches hold promise for advancing patient-centred and equitable emergency medical care in South Africa and beyond.

Conclusion

This paper addressed the ongoing exclusion of transgender health content from health professions education, with a focus on the largely overlooked field of prehospital emergency care. Despite legal protections in South Africa, transgender individuals continue to face discrimination within healthcare systems, partly due to inadequate training of healthcare providers. In response, an educational intervention was developed and implemented in an undergraduate paramedic programme in Cape Town. Student feedback suggested perceived improvements in awareness, empathy and preparedness to engage respectfully with transgender patients. The findings demonstrate the potential value of contextually relevant, inclusive teaching strategies.

To achieve meaningful and sustained change, such interventions must be integrated into formal curricula, supported by institutional leadership, and adapted across various disciplines.

This model offers a scalable, practical approach to help close systemic education gaps, especially in low- and middle-income countries. For the global medical education community, this case study offers a reflective illustration of how targeted, values-driven initiatives may begin to advance diversity, equity, and inclusion, while encouraging future healthcare professionals to provide respectful, person-centred care to all patients. Continued research and evaluation will be essential to determine how such interventions influence clinical competence and patient experiences over time.
