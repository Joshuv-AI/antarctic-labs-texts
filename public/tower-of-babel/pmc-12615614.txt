Mathematical modeling of heart rate tracking in motion affected PPG signals
Authors: Shahid Ismail, Muhammad Usman Akram, Imran Siddiqi, Norah Saleh Alghamdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Heart rate tracking using Photoplethysmography (PPG) suffers from motion artifacts, which can change signal structure in a way that the spectral peak due to motion artifacts (MAs) can mask the actual peak related to the heart rate. To handle the problem just mentioned, a novel mathematical model for heart rate (HR) tracking is introduced. Our technique is based on a mathematical model for a multichannel PPG. The model uses a fixed-resolution spectrum of Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), Chirplet Z Transform (CZT) spectra at various resolutions, confinement of spectral space, previous heart rate, range of the signal, and a golden seed (GS) algorithm to generate the next heart rate. GS algorithm is a novel technique which is introduced to handle the masking of spectral peaks related to HRs. The GS algorithm utilizes intensity profiling, the Singular Spectrum Analysis (SSA) algorithm, spectral multiplication and subtraction, and proximity clustering to enhance the masked peak. The average time taken by our technique is 21.21ms and a mean average error of 2.12 on the IEEE signal processing Cup 2015 makes it fit for the real-time applications.

Introduction

Photoplethysmography or PPG is a non-invasive optical technology that uses a light emitting diode (LED) as a light source (transmitter) and a photo detector (PD) as a receiver to measure volumetric changes in blood1. PPG along with ECG & PCG is being used to estimate heart rate but unlike other modalities it does not a require specific position or resting state, a definite advantage. However, the advantage is offset by motion artifacts (MAs) that are introduced during motion. Motion artifacts are a type of noise that not only change the baseline (DC value) of the signal but also change the AC or pulsatile component in the PPG signal. The cumulative effect of motion artifacts is to distort the PPG signal to the extent such that the morphology of the signal becomes very complex. Figures 1, 2 and 3 show a clean signal and signals affected by noise and motion artifacts, respectively. For a clean signal, the main peak in both PPG spectra actually corresponds to the actual heart rate. In the case of a signal affected by noise, the third peak in PPG1 and the 2nd peak in PPG2 reflect the actual heart rate peak. In the case of motion artifacts, peaks have become random and none of the main peaks actually correspond to the actual heart rate peak. Due to the modulating effects of ACC on PPG, the output PPG signal becomes very complex. Despite its complexity, PPG is being used extensively in various fields like heart rate variability2, establishing the relationship between heart and respiration rate3, the effect of stimuli on heart rate4, and others. The widespread use of PPG is due to its inexpensive nature, portability, and ease of use. However, a major use of PPG aside from oxygen saturation (SpO2 estimation5) is heart rate detection and tracking. For heart rate tracking, researchers have focused their attention on the use of adaptive filtering, single- and multi-resolution signal processing, signal conditioning, etc. Fu et al6. compared the performance of wavelet decomposition with mean filtering and concluded that a six level wavelet decomposition resulted in the same performance as moving average filters of length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{s}/2,\hspace{0.1cm}F_{s}/4\hspace{0.1cm} and \hspace{0.1cm} F_{s}/8$$\end{document}Fs/2,Fs/4andFs/8. In the beginning, Garde et al7. used empirical mode decomposition (EMD) to decompose the signal. Afterwards, they utilized power spectral density (PSD) and statistical analysis to calculate heart and respiration rate. Another decomposition which is closely related to EMD is variational mode decomposition (VMD) and it was employed by Hasan et al.8. However, authors complemented VMD with adaptive filtering for final estimation of heart rates. Pengfei Wei et al9. focused on the development of a wrist band that could give clean PPG during motion of body part like the arm. The authors concluded that the use of a prototype wrist band along with fast transversal adaptive filtering produced reliable and stable PPG signal under motion. To reduce hardware resource requirements, Park et al10. utilized sign-sign least mean square (LMS) filters. Modified normalized LMS is another LMS filter that was applied in the heart rate estimation by authors11. However, the authors utilized motion artifact intensity classification and removal (MAICR) framework to remove MAs. The focus of Rasoul Yousefi et al12. was also on the reduction of motion artifacts, but they employed adaptive filtering for this purpose instead of developing new hardware. Researchers concluded that the output of adaptive filtering is highly correlated with the reference. Giannetti et al13. employed filtering to cleanse the signal before using the harmonic nature of motion and heuristic analysis to calculate and track heart rate. From this discussion, it is clear that PPG analysis for heart rate is a very difficult task due to the presence of motion artifacts. All works employed private datasets for evaluation of their techniques except Garde et al7., who used the public dataset of Capnobase14.Fig. 3Figure shows an example of PPG affected by motion artifacts. Zhilin Zhang15 introduced a theory composed of a bandpass filtering (BPF), signal decomposition using singular spectrum analysis (SSA), temporal difference (TD), sparse signal reconstruction (SSR) using Bayesian learning, and frequency bin selection for heart rate tracking. Based on this work, Zhang16 introduced a heart rate tracker named TROIKA (BPF, SSA, TD, focal under determined system solver (FOCUSS) and spectral peak tracking) along with the IEEE Signal Processing Cup 2015 dataset17. Work in18is similar to TROIKA, except that instead of using a single spectrum from an acceleration signal, it uses all three acceleration channels, resulting in joint spectrum. Zhang et al19. combined ensemble EMD (EEMD) with spectral subtraction and proved that the correlation between PPG and acceleration could be used for heart rate estimation. Sun et al20. used asymmetrical least squares along with Bayesian theory for heart rate peak selection and verification. In another work, Sun et al21. combined high resolution grid-less spectrum calculation and subtraction followed by SVM classification. In22, non-linear (Volterra) filters, SSA decomposition, and peak tracking were used for heart rate detection and verification.

A number of authors23–28 tried to reduce the computational cost to make their techniques fit for real-time applications, as heart rate detection and estimation are computationally expensive processes. Andriy Temko23provided both an online (Wiener filter+Phase vocoder (WVPV)) and an offline-version (Wiener filter+Phase vocoder+Viterbi algorithm) of the algorithm for real-time and offline processing. Lee et al24. used a finite state machine (FSM) having states (stable, recovery, alert, and uncertain) and previous heart rates for tracking and concluded that a higher crest factor resulted in lower absolute error. In another study27, the authors concluded that gyroscope performance is better for cases where the heart rate was exactly overlapped with the MA spectral peak and small finger related motions. Schak’s26approach was to use all ACC signals as reference NLMS adaptive filter input. All filtered outputs were multiplied together to give an enhanced spectrogram from the which the heart rate was tracked using a high-energy continuous line with highest probability. De Giovanni et al25. tried to reduce computational cost by using FFT-only spectrum and was able to run their equipment for more than 9 days. Zhang et al28. used different sampling frequencies to address the problem related to MAs.

The methods presented above are similar in the sense that they utilize either a single PPG channel or a single wavelength with a multiple-channel PPG. In the present study, the dataset was multichannel, but used LEDs of the same wavelength. In one of the recent studies on heart rate estimation using multi-wavelength PPG, the authors utilized four channels of PPG with varying wavelengths. They claimed that better heart rate estimation was achieved using their setup in comparison to single wavelength-based PPG29. Another study which highlights that a multi-site PPG is better than a single channel PPG was conducted by Chowdhury et al30. Similar to the setups just described, Park et al10., utilized a dual-channel PPG setup with green and red LEDs generating these channels. Their analysis proved that a variable-wavelength setup for PPG channels can reduce motion artifacts, thereby increasing the accuracy of heart rate estimation. Aside from the techniques just discussed, artificial intelligence (AI)-based methods are also in use for heart rate estimation using PPG signals. The systems used can be divided into classical machine learning-based networks like support vector machine, random forest, etc., and deep neural networks like artificial neural networks (DNNs), convolutional neural networks (CNNs), long short-term memory (LSTM), etc. In one of the earlier studies using DNN, Biswas et al31. utilized a network they named as Cornet. They utilized not only CNN and LSTM to calculate heart rates. The same author along with others went on to create another version of Cornet they named binary Cornet, owing to the use of binary weights in this new version32. A recent study utilizing a composite network comprising CNN and LSTM was conducted by Lucas et al33.. In order to collect information in both forward and reverse directions, Mengshan Wu and Xiang Chen utilized BiLSTM (Bidirectional LSTM). By utilizing a BiLSTM inside their DNN, they achieved stable performance.

From the detailed discussion presented above, it is clear that heart rate estimation is dependent on multiple factors like heart rate changes, type of exercise, intensity of exercise, number of PPG and ACC channels employed, noise reference, etc. Major approaches are heuristic-based, adaptive filtering, and multi-resolution decompositions like EMD, wavelet, etc. Multi-resolution and adaptive filtering-based techniques can estimate heart rate with high accuracy comparatively, but suffer from high computational cost. For real-time applications, the technique must be using low computational resources with high estimation accuracy. In this paper, we propose a technique which is using low computational resources and is fit for offline and online applications. Our proposed technique is based on the use of CZT and FFT for PSD calculation, extension of spectral solution space, and peak recovery using golden seed algorithm. Material and methods

IEEE Signal Processing Cup (SPC) is a publicly available dataset which, was used for the evaluation purpose. It is composed of three parts with first training set (12 samples) uploaded in August 2014. Another training set (second chunk) and test sets of 10 were uploaded in January 2015. Training data (12+1) is a multi-channel signal with first being ECG reference, the next two channels are PPG, and finally the three acceleration (ACC) signals (x,y, and z axis). On the other hand, in the test dataset, reference ECG is missing and the dataset is composed of five channels. First two channels are PPG and other three channels are acceleration signals17. Reference ECG and ground truth for both test and training are provided. Signals (ECG, PPG, and ACC) are sampled at a sampling frequency of 125 Hz using green LEDs (515nm). The dataset is composed of two types of exercises and among them are whole body and upper body exercises. Whole body is named as TYPE 1 (TYPE01) and upper body exercises involving hand and fists are called TYPE 2 (TYPE02) exercises. Sequence of motion and rest within these exercises is given below

Analysis of the PPG signal for heart rate estimation is a challenging task as both noise and motion artifacts introduce modulating effects. Modulation shifts the spectral peak related to the heart rate. In this research study, we have introduced a computationally less expensive technique that is able to estimate heart rate with considerable accuracy. Figure 4 shows the detailed diagram of the technique introduced, which can be used for both offline and real-time tracking. During pre-processing, filtering is applied to all channels of the signal which, is then divided into overlapping segments. The golden seed algorithm is the heart of the technique introduced in the paper. Golden seeds are the heart rates that can be used as the reference for heart rate tracking. They are either the same or have a minor difference from the ground truth and are calculated empirically. The golden seed algorithm is always used in offline processing, as all signal segments are available. In contrast to the real-time tracking where, we need buffering to collect data to be used to generate reference heart rates. All heart rates are calculated using spectrum-based such as CZT and FFT. If the signal is free of noise and MAs, we immediately calculate the next heart rate using only CZT-based power spectral density (PSD) spectrum. However, we need to expand the spectral space if signal is noisy, and to handle a signal with MAs along with noise, we need to use the golden seed algorithm again. Pre-processing

The signals under study are composed of 2 PPG and 3 ACC channels. These five channels are first passed through an 11-th order Elliptical IIR bandpass filter with cutoffs in the range [0.4, 5] Hz to remove all frequencies outside the heart rate spectrum. Subsequently, data comprising these five channels are further divided into segments of length 1000 samples, each representing 8 seconds at a sampling frequency of 125 Hz. Golden seed algorithm

Golden seed algorithm is the main contribution introduced and is based on intensity profiling, heart rate estimation, and proximity-based clustering (Appendix A). During intensity profiling, normalized power using the Eigenvector method is calculated. However, samples that are only among top \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{thr}$$\end{document}Ithr are considered for clustering. The second step in golden seed calculation is the generation of heart rates from all channels in the composite signal. For a cluster, candidate heart rates are generated using two methods. In the first method, candidate peaks related to heart rates are localized in a filtered PPG spectrum. Then, SSA decomposition of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{A}}$$\end{document}DSA levels is performed on the same filtered PPG channel and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_{n,p}$$\end{document}Dn,p peaks per sub-levels are localized in all sub-levels resulting in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_{N}$$\end{document}DN candidates peaks. Out of all peaks (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_{N}$$\end{document}DN), only candidate peaks that are in proximity to the peaks from filtered signals will be retained. Generally, a maximum of 5 candidate peaks per PPG channel is finally retained, resulting in a maximum of 10 peaks for both channels. In the second method, spectral boosting is applied to each PPG channel. During spectral boosting, the spectrum of both PPG and their sum is multiplied together to generate a positive spectrum. Similarly, the spectrum of all ACC channels and the difference of PPG channels is multiplied together to create a negative spectrum. Then, the negative spectrum is subtracted from the positive spectrum. From the resultant spectrum, the major peak is localized, reflecting one candidate heart rate. An additional spectral peak is also generated using the same PPG that is now padded with zeros (equal to 8 s) for better localization. Hence, two candidate peaks per PPG channel are generated using spectral boosting for the FFT based spectrum. Similarly, two additional peaks per channel with CZT are also generated, resulting in 8 candidate peaks from both FFT and CZT spectral boosting. In all, we have a maximum of 10 peaks from SSA-based decomposition and 8 peaks from spectral boosting, giving 18 peaks at the end. Once all peaks are available, they are fed into proximity-based clustering which generates the final heart rate, reflecting a peak that is present in both SSA decomposition and spectral boosting and has highest votes. In case of a tie, the peak with the highest magnitude will be considered. However, for a candidate heart rate to be a golden seed, it must be present in a cluster of length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{l,thr}$$\end{document}Cl,thr and cluster variation must be less than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}_{thr}$$\end{document}CPthr.

Golden seed algorithm is used once during the initial phase of offline processing for reference generation. Afterwards, it is used only when noisy and MAs-affected signals are encountered. For real-time tracking, buffering is done first, and once we have samples corresponding to 10 s (5 consecutive HRs (Section 2.1)), golden seeds are used to generate the reference heart rates. However, if current buffer is not able to generate reference heart rates, 250 initial samples are removed, the next 750 samples are shifted to the left, and new 250 samples are added at the trailing end of the buffer. PSD calculation, spectral sampling & heart rate estimation

Generally, power spectral density (PSD) is calculated using CZT for HR estimation. This is due to the fact that a PPG channel with a relatively smaller range is likely to be less affected by exercise and noise as opposed to the channel with a larger range. The frequency corresponding to the largest peak location, when multiplied by 60 is the estimated heart rate. However, since we have samples affected by strong motion, we need strategies that can localize displaced, overlapped, and even suppressed peaks. Expansion of spectral solution space

First technique to deal with noisy signals is the expansion of the spectral solution space which is related to the heart rate. Here, we utilized CZT at various resolutions (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{spect}=4,096;16,384;8,192;32,768$$\end{document}Nspect=4,096;16,384;8,192;32,768). By employing more resolutions, the peaks overlapped between MAs and actual HRs can be highlighted. To further mitigate this effect, three peaks per resolution are used. Hence, we have a total of 3x4= 12 peaks from four CZT resolutions. Additionally, we used a single major peak from the FFT spectrum. Here, peaks that have most votes in CZT and are in proximity to the FFT-based peak are considered as the final heart rate. In case of a tie between two heart rates, the one which has the higher magnitude will be considered as the final peak and the heart rate calculated from this peak would be the final. Limitations of present work and its future extension

Figure 7 presents an example segment that proved challenging for our proposed technique. From the Figure, we can see that the spectral peak corresponding to the heart rate is masked in both PPG1 and PPG2. Since the proposed technique relies on estimating heart rate only from PPG signals without using any additional information from acceleration (ACC) channels, heart rates from such samples are expected to contain errors. The only information our technique uses from ACC is the generation of a range which is related to spectrum which could be used for heart rate estimation. A solution to reduce the error is to switch from CZT to FFT for PSD calculation and vice versa. Conclusion

In this research, we have presented a heart rate estimation technique for multichannel PPG signals. Estimation in signals affected by motion artifacts is a challenging task as MAs can displace or even mask the spectral peak related to heart rate. The technique presented is based on estimation of heart rates from FFT and CZT spectra and spectral peak recovery. Despite being a simple one, as it does not use any complex decomposition technique or filtering techniques like adaptive filtering, it is able to encompass all different types of signals representing different types of exercises like Type-01, Type-02 and even an abnormal test signal. Moreover, the technique is able to generate a local reference to initiate the estimation process. The comparable performance of the novel technique on a benchmark dataset validates its potential in estimation of heart rate from PPG signals in challenging scenarios. However, there appear spurious discrepancies in heart rate estimation related to noisy or dirty patches of signals where changes in estimator direction lead to divergence. Hence, further exploration is required to better sampling and reduce divergence.
