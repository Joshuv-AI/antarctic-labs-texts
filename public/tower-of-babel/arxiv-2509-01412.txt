Vis-CoT: A Human-in-the-Loop Framework for Interactive Visualization and Intervention in LLM Chain-of-Thought Reasoning
Kaviraj Pather, Elena Hadjigeorgiou, Arben Krasniqi, Claire Schmit, Irina Rusu, Marc Pons, Kabir Khan

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable reasoning capabilities through Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting. However, the underlying reasoning process remains an opaque ”black box,” making it difficult to verify, debug, or control, which limits trust and reliability in high-stakes applications. To address this critical gap, we introduce Vis-CoT, a novel human-in-the-loop framework that transforms the linear CoT text into a structured, interactive reasoning graph. Our system allows users to visualize the logical flow of the model’s reasoning, identify flawed steps, and directly intervene by pruning incorrect paths and grafting new, user-defined premises. This transforms the interaction from a passive observation to an active collaboration, enabling users to steer the model toward more accurate and trustworthy conclusions. Through comprehensive experiments on diverse reasoning benchmarks, including GSM8K and StrategyQA, we demonstrate that Vis-CoT significantly improves final answer accuracy by up to 24 percentage points over non-interactive baselines. Furthermore, our user study reveals a substantial increase in perceived usability and user trust, underscoring the value of transparency and control. Our work presents a promising paradigm for creating more reliable, understandable, and collaborative reasoning systems by synergizing the computational power of LLMs with human critical oversight.

III
The Vis-CoT Framework

To address the challenges of opacity and uncontrollability in LLM reasoning, we propose Vis-CoT, a novel human-in-the-loop framework designed to render Chain-of-Thought (CoT) processes transparent, interpretable, and interactive. The framework is built upon a core principle: by transforming the monolithic, textual reasoning trace into a structured, visual, and editable artifact, we can empower users to collaboratively guide the model towards more accurate and trustworthy outcomes. This section details the architectural design and technical mechanisms of the Vis-CoT system, from initial CoT capture to the final, human-validated reasoning product.

III-A

Overall Architecture

The Vis-CoT framework operates as a closed-loop system that integrates a user, an LLM, and an interactive interface. The workflow, as depicted in Figure
1
, proceeds through a cycle of generation, visualization, intervention, and refinement.

Fig. 1
:
Overall architecture of the Vis-CoT framework.

The process begins when a user submits a complex query. The framework first prompts the LLM to generate an initial CoT trace. This raw text is then passed to our **CoT Structuring Module**, which parses it into a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) representation. This structured graph is rendered by the **Visualization Interface**, where the user can inspect the logical flow. If the user identifies a flaw, they can use the tools provided by the **Intervention Module** to modify the graph. These modifications are then processed by the **Model Feedback Module**, which constructs a new, context-aware prompt. This prompt, containing the history of valid reasoning steps and the user’s correction, is sent back to the LLM to generate a revised continuation of the reasoning chain. This iterative refinement loop continues until the user is satisfied with the final answer.

III-B

Chain-of-Thought Capture and Structuring

The foundation of our framework is the ability to reliably capture and structure the LLM’s reasoning process. We employ a carefully designed prompt template that encourages the model to externalize its thinking process in a step-by-step, semi-structured format. Each step is implicitly numbered or delineated, which facilitates subsequent parsing.

Once the raw text
T_{CoT}
is generated, the CoT Structuring Module converts it into a reasoning graph
G=(V,E)
, where
V
is a set of nodes representing individual reasoning steps and
E
is a set of directed edges representing the logical dependencies between them. Each node
v_{i}\in V
corresponds to the
i
-th step in the reasoning chain. An edge
e_{i,j}\in E
from node
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
indicates that step
j
is a direct logical consequence of step
i
. For a standard linear CoT, this graph is a simple chain.

Each node
v_{i}
is a tuple containing several attributes:
v_{i}=(s_{i},c_{i},\sigma_{i},\tau_{i})
, where:

•

s_{i}
: The raw text content of the reasoning step.

•

c_{i}
: A confidence score, typically derived from the model’s log-probabilities for the generated tokens in
s_{i}
.

•

\sigma_{i}
: The state of the node, initialized as ‘VALID‘. This can be changed by user intervention.

•

\tau_{i}
: The type of the node (e.g., ‘PREMISE‘, ‘INFERENCE‘, ‘CONCLUSION‘), which can be heuristically determined through keyword matching or a classifier.

We formalize the reasoning graph
G
as follows:

G=(V,E)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{s.t.}\hskip 10.00002ptV=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}\},\hskip 10.00002ptE=\{(v_{i},v_{i+1})\mid i=1,\dots,n-1\}

(1)

Where
G
is the reasoning graph,
V
is the set of
n
reasoning nodes, and
E
is the set of edges representing a linear dependency for a basic CoT.

To quantify the model’s certainty at each step, we calculate the node confidence score
c_{i}
as the average log-probability of the tokens comprising the step’s text
s_{i}
. Let
s_{i}
consist of
m
tokens
t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{m}
. The confidence is:

c_{i}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\log P(t_{k}\mid t_{1},\dots,t_{k-1},C_{hist})

(2)

Where
c_{i}
is the confidence of node
v_{i}
,
m
is the number of tokens in its text

,

is the conditional probability of token
t_{k}
, and

is the historical context.

Interactive Visualization Interface

The structured reasoning graph
G
is rendered in a web-based interface designed for clarity and intuitive interaction. The interface consists of two main components: a global graph view and a node detail panel.

III-D

User-Driven Intervention Mechanism

The core innovation of Vis-CoT lies in its interactive intervention capabilities, which allow users to actively shape the reasoning process. We define three primary intervention operations: Flagging, Pruning, and Grafting.

1. Flagging:
This is the simplest intervention. If a user identifies a step
v_{i}
as factually incorrect, logically unsound, or irrelevant, they can mark it. This action updates the node’s state
\sigma_{i}
from ‘VALID‘ to ‘FLAGGED‘.

\text{Flag}(v_{i})\rightarrow\sigma_{i}^{\prime}=\text{FLAGGED}

(3)

Where
\text{Flag}(v_{i})
is the user’s flagging operation on node
v_{i}
, and
\sigma_{i}^{\prime}
is the updated state of the node.
Flagged nodes are visually distinguished in the interface (e.g., by changing their color to red) but are not immediately removed, allowing the user to maintain context.

2. Pruning:
This is a more decisive action. When a user determines that a node
v_{i}
and all subsequent reasoning based on it are incorrect, they can prune it. This operation removes not only
v_{i}
but also all nodes and edges in the subgraph reachable from
v_{i}
. Let
V_{sub}(v_{i})
be the set of nodes in the subgraph rooted at
v_{i}
. The pruning operation is:

\text{Prune}(v_{i})\rightarrow G^{\prime}=(V\setminus V_{sub}(v_{i}),E\setminus E_{sub}(v_{i}))

(4)

Where
G^{\prime}
is the new graph state, and
V_{sub}(v_{i})
and
E_{sub}(v_{i})
are the nodes and edges in the subgraph originating from
v_{i}
.

3. Grafting:
This is the most powerful intervention. It allows a user to introduce a new, correct reasoning step into the graph. A user can select a valid node
v_{p}
(the parent) and provide the text for a new reasoning step,
s_{new}
. The system creates a new node
v_{new}
and ”grafts” it onto the graph by adding an edge from
v_{p}
to
v_{new}
.

\text{Graft}(v_{p},s_{new})\rightarrow V^{\prime}=V\cup\{v_{new}\},\hskip 9.24994ptE^{\prime}=E\cup\{(v_{p},v_{new})\}

(5)

Where
v_{p}
is the selected parent node,
s_{new}
is the user-provided text, and
V^{\prime}
and
E^{\prime}
are the updated sets of nodes and edges.
The new node
v_{new}
is marked with a special state
\sigma_{new}=\text{USER\_PROVIDED}
and serves as the new frontier for the LLM to continue its reasoning from.

IV
Experimental Setup

To empirically validate the effectiveness of our Vis-CoT framework, we designed a series of experiments to assess its performance in terms of task accuracy, user efficiency, and overall user experience. This section details the datasets, implementation specifics, evaluation metrics, and baseline models used in our study.

IV-A

Datasets

We selected three datasets that represent diverse and challenging reasoning tasks, requiring multi-step logical deduction where standard few-shot prompting often fails. These datasets are well-suited to evaluate the benefits of interactive course-correction in LLM reasoning.

•

GSM8K (Grade School Math):
A dataset of 8.5K high-quality, linguistically diverse grade school math word problems . These problems require 2 to 8 steps to solve, making them an ideal testbed for evaluating sequential reasoning. A single misstep in calculation or logic leads to an incorrect final answer, highlighting the need for precise intervention.

•

StrategyQA:
A question-answering benchmark where the required reasoning steps are implicit and must be inferred from the question . Questions are typically of a ”yes/no” format but require a valid, multi-step explanation to be considered correct. This dataset tests the model’s ability to construct plausible and factually grounded arguments, a process where human oversight is highly valuable.

•

Multi-Step Planning Task (Custom):
To evaluate performance on practical, real-world problems, we created a custom dataset of 100 multi-step planning scenarios. These tasks involve logistical challenges such as conference scheduling, travel itinerary planning, and event resource allocation. They are characterized by multiple constraints and dependencies, where errors can cascade, making them a strong use case for the iterative refinement capabilities of Vis-CoT.

For our experiments, we used the official test splits for GSM8K and StrategyQA. For our custom planning dataset, we ensured a diverse set of problems with varying complexity.

IV-B

Implementation Details

Language Model:
The core of our system utilizes the
Llama-2-70B-Chat
model , a powerful, publicly available LLM known for its strong reasoning capabilities. All prompts, both for initial generation and for refinement, were formatted according to the model’s specific chat template to ensure optimal performance.

Hardware and Software:
The Vis-CoT backend, including the LLM inference server, was run on a system equipped with four NVIDIA A100 GPUs (80GB VRAM). The interactive web-based interface was built using standard web technologies (React for the frontend, FastAPI for the backend) and communicates with the LLM server via REST APIs. The force-directed graph layout for visualization was implemented using the D3.js library.

User Study Participants:
We recruited 24 participants for our user study from a local university. Participants had backgrounds in computer science or related fields and were familiar with the concept of AI but were not experts in LLM prompting or interpretability. They were randomly assigned to either the experimental group (using Vis-CoT) or one of the control groups.

IV-C

Evaluation Metrics

We evaluated our framework using a combination of quantitative and qualitative metrics to capture a holistic view of its performance.

1. Task Performance:

•

Final Answer Accuracy:
This is the primary quantitative metric. For GSM8K, we measure the percentage of problems where the final numerical answer is correct. For StrategyQA and our custom planning task, we measure the percentage of questions answered correctly and supported by a valid reasoning chain, as judged by human evaluators.

2. Human-in-the-Loop Efficiency:

•

Task Completion Time:
The total time taken by a user to arrive at a satisfactory final answer.

•

Number of Interventions:
The average number of Flagging, Pruning, or Grafting operations a user performed per task. A lower number for a correct outcome suggests a more efficient process.

3. User Experience and Trust (Qualitative):

•

System Usability Scale (SUS):
A standardized 10-item questionnaire providing a reliable, global view of subjective usability assessments.

•

Trust in AI Scale:
We adapted a standard trust-in-automation scale to measure user trust in the system’s outputs, focusing on aspects like reliability, predictability, and faith in the final answer
[
60
]
.

•

Post-Task Interviews:
Semi-structured interviews were conducted to gather qualitative feedback on the system’s strengths, weaknesses, and the user’s thought process during the intervention tasks.

IV-D

Baselines

We compare the performance of our Vis-CoT framework against two strong baseline conditions:

•

Baseline 1: Standard CoT (Non-Interactive):
In this condition, users are presented with the same initial problem. The LLM generates a complete Chain-of-Thought and a final answer. The user can see the entire reasoning trace but cannot interact with or change it. They can only accept the answer or re-generate the entire solution from scratch. This represents the current standard practice for using CoT.

•

Baseline 2: Zero-Shot LLM (No CoT):
In this condition, the LLM is prompted to provide only the final answer without showing any intermediate reasoning steps. This baseline helps to quantify the foundational benefit of CoT reasoning itself, against which the interactive improvements of Vis-CoT can be measured.

By comparing Vis-CoT against these baselines across our defined metrics, we aim to provide robust evidence of its advantages in improving reasoning accuracy, efficiency, and user trust.

V-C

Case Study: Correcting a Flawed Inference

To provide a concrete example of Vis-CoT in action, we analyze a session from the GSM8K dataset. The problem was: ”A farmer has 15 cows and 23 chickens. He sells 6 cows and buys 8 more chickens. How many total legs are on his farm now?”

The LLM’s initial CoT, shown in text form, was:
1. The farmer starts with 15 cows.
2. He sells 6 cows, so he has 15 - 6 = 9 cows left.
3. He starts with 23 chickens.
4. He buys 8 more chickens, so he has 23 + 8 = 31 chickens.
5. Cows have 4 legs each, so the cows have 9 * 4 = 36 legs.
6. Chickens have 2 legs each, so the chickens have 31 * 2 = 62 legs.
7. The total number of legs is 36 + 62 = 98 legs.
8. There are also 2 legs for the farmer.
9. So the total legs on the farm are 98 + 2 = 100.

Here, the model correctly performs the animal calculations but makes a classic ”over-thinking” error in step 8 by including the farmer’s legs, a common issue in commonsense reasoning. In the Standard CoT baseline, a user would see the final answer ”100” and the entire trace, and would have to either accept the flawed result or restart the entire process.

With Vis-CoT, the user saw this reasoning as a 9-node graph. They immediately identified step 8 as irrelevant and illogical. The user performed a **Pruning** operation on node
v_{8}
. This action removed nodes
v_{8}
and
v_{9}
from the graph. The reasoning chain now correctly terminated at node
v_{7}
, which contained the correct intermediate total of 98. The user, satisfied with this result, terminated the session. The entire correction took less than 15 seconds, demonstrating the efficiency of targeted intervention. This interactive debugging process is far more direct than implicit methods that aim to steer models via activation engineering
[
57
]
or representation editing
[
56
]
, as it places the domain expert (the user) directly in control.

V-D

Discussion

Our results strongly suggest that integrating a human-in-the-loop through an interactive visualization and intervention framework is a highly effective strategy for improving the reliability and trustworthiness of LLM reasoning. The Vis-CoT framework successfully transforms the user’s role from a passive observer to an active collaborator, leveraging the strengths of both human intelligence and machine computation.

The significant performance gains across all datasets indicate that many of the current reasoning failures of even powerful LLMs are not catastrophic but are often localized and correctable. By providing the tools to pinpoint and fix these small errors, Vis-CoT prevents them from propagating and corrupting the final outcome. This finding has important implications, suggesting that the path to more reliable AI may not solely lie in scaling up models, but also in designing better human-AI interfaces. The importance of robust, human-centric design is a lesson learned across many technology domains, from creating intuitive gesture recognition systems
[
42
]
to building effective federated learning schemes that account for real-world network heterogeneity
[
4
]
.

The qualitative feedback from our user study is perhaps even more telling. The dramatic increase in reported trust and usability underscores a fundamental point: transparency breeds trust. When users understand
how
a conclusion is reached and have the agency to influence that process, they are far more likely to accept and rely on the technology. This is of paramount importance as we consider deploying LLMs in critical sectors like medicine, law, and finance, where the cost of an unexplainable error is unacceptably high. The need for verifiable, trustworthy systems is a universal requirement for technologies that interact with human well-being, whether it is through affective computing or remote health monitoring
[
19
]
.

However, our work is not without limitations. First, the effectiveness of Vis-CoT is dependent on the user’s ability to identify reasoning errors. While effective for the tasks we tested, more subtle or domain-specific fallacies might still go unnoticed by a non-expert user. Second, our current intervention mechanism, while powerful, could potentially introduce user bias. A user might guide the model towards a preconceived but incorrect answer. Future work could explore methods to mitigate this, such as having the model ”push back” or request justification if a user’s intervention drastically contradicts its internal world model. Finally, the framework currently relies on a single LLM. Future iterations could incorporate a multi-agent debate or polling system
[
51
,
52
]
, visualizing the reasoning processes of multiple models simultaneously to help users identify a consensus path.

In conclusion, our work demonstrates a promising path forward for human-AI collaboration in complex reasoning. By moving from opaque generation to transparent, interactive co-creation, frameworks like Vis-CoT can make LLMs not just more powerful, but also more reliable, understandable, and ultimately, more trustworthy.
