Alignment of registered cell therapy trial protocols with regulatory guidance on preclinical evidence: a systematic, cross-sectional, protocol-level analysis
Authors: Matthew S. Jeffers, Wimonchat Tangamornsuksan, Brian Dorus, Patrick Bedford, Jonathan Kimmelman, Daniel I. McIsaac, Dean A. Fergusson, Manoj M. Lalu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundCell therapies often advance into clinical trials based on supportive preclinical evidence, yet it remains unclear whether such evidence is presented in trial protocols in ways that explicitly justify translation to humans. We assessed whether preclinical efficacy evidence in cell therapy trial protocols aligns with regulatory guidance principles (e.g., mechanism of action, evidence of disease modification, clinically relevant model selection).MethodsWe systematically searched ClinicalTrials.gov for cell therapy trials with available protocols. The primary outcomes were the proportions of protocols explicitly reporting 20 predefined preclinical evidence elements corresponding to regulator-derived translational principles. Secondary outcomes included the types and sources of evidence used to justify trial initiation and differences in reporting across trial contexts.FindingsWe identified 305 eligible trials with protocols, of which 209 (68·5%) incorporated preclinical evidence. Reporting was uneven across domains of translational principles: at least one therapeutic mechanism of action element was explicitly reported in 95% of trials (95% confidence interval [CI], 91–97, n = 198/209), preclinical evidence of disease modification in 70% (95% CI, 63–76, n = 146/209), evidence informing trial intervention parameters in 69% (95% CI, 62–75, n = 144/209), and explicit justification of model relevance in 24% (95% CI, 19–31, n = 51/209). Protocol-level reporting of translational principles was generally higher in trials incorporating large-animal or original preclinical data, and lower in trials citing prior human data or sponsored by industry. Preclinical evidence was more often used to demonstrate biological plausibility than to justify key aspects of trial design.InterpretationAlthough key translational principles are partially reflected in current practice, domains central to translational validity remain underrepresented. While additional preclinical evidence may be available in investigator brochures or regulatory submissions, publicly available protocols are important documents through which investigators formalise and communicate the rationale for trial initiation. Strengthening how preclinical evidence is structured in protocols may improve trial justification, regulatory evaluation, and support more reliable clinical translation.FundingCanadian Stem Cell Network Impact Awards: Ethical, Legal, and Social Impact Stream.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Before undertaking this study, we considered three bodies of evidence relevant to how cell therapy trials are justified: international regulatory guidance on preclinical efficacy; empirical studies examining the presentation of supporting evidence in investigator brochures, trial protocols, and trial reports; and studies of regulatory review experience in advanced therapies.

Across these three sources, a consistent picture emerged. Regulatory guidance broadly agrees that preclinical evidence used to justify human trials should demonstrate mechanism of action, support clinically relevant outcomes, inform key trial parameters such as intervention route and dose, and justify the relevance and limitations of the preclinical models used. At the same time, empirical studies show that supporting evidence in investigator brochures, protocols, and trial publications is often incompletely reported, selectively presented, and insufficiently synthesised to allow independent assessment of trial justification. Regulatory review studies in cell and gene therapy have similarly noted frequent concerns about the adequacy of preclinical evidence. However, no previous study had systematically examined whether cell therapy trial protocols incorporate regulatory translational principles with broad international consensus to help align trial justifications with regulatory expectations.

Added value of this study

This study provides a large-scale, protocol-level evaluation of how preclinical evidence is used to justify human cell therapy trials against regulatory guidance and expectations. By analysing publicly available protocols from 305 registered cell therapy trials, we demonstrate that protocols commonly invoke anticipated therapeutic benefit and frequently combine human and preclinical evidence in doing so. Among trial protocols citing preclinical evidence, reporting was concentrated in domains related to mechanism of action and biological activity, whereas explicit justification of model relevance, intervention parameters, endpoint relevance, and study rigour was much less common. These findings show that preclinical evidence is often present, but that the translational reasoning recommended by regulators to link that evidence to the specific design and rationale of the proposed human trial is poorly formalised.

Implications of all the available evidence

Taken together, existing evidence and our findings suggest that the main challenge is not simply whether preclinical evidence is available, but whether it is clearly structured and explained in ways that allow reviewers to judge why a human trial is justified. For cell therapies, this matters because these products often have elevated risk profiles, complex mechanisms, context-dependent effects, and important dependencies on dose, delivery, and manufacturing conditions that complicate preclinical-to-human translation. More explicit and structured presentation of translational justification in trial protocols could improve transparency, strengthen regulatory review, and help ensure that early human studies are supported by a clearer rationale for why results observed in preclinical models are expected to matter in patients.

Introduction

Clinical translation of cell therapies requires exposing human participants to investigational interventions under conditions of uncertainty, making robust justification of anticipated risks and benefits essential.1, 2, 3, 4 These justifications often rely heavily on evidence derived from preclinical systems, including in vivo animal, in vitro, and in silico models.4, 5, 6 In this context, the grounds for trial initiation depend on whether the rationale linking preclinical findings to expected human outcomes is clearly and transparently articulated.

When this rationale is not made explicit, the transition from preclinical testing to human experimentation becomes difficult to independently evaluate, limiting the ability to assess whether the available evidence adequately supports trial initiation.7, 8, 9, 10 This challenge is particularly pronounced for cell therapies, which are often highly context-dependent, sensitive to manufacturing and delivery conditions, and characterised by complex or incompletely understood mechanisms of action.11, 12, 13, 14 In addition, many cell therapies involve persistent or potentially irreversible biological effects following administration, with limited ability to modulate or withdraw exposure once delivered. These features complicate extrapolation from preclinical systems to human populations and increase the importance of a clearly defined and defensible link between preclinical findings and clinical trial design.12,15, 16, 17

Regulatory agencies address these challenges by providing guidance for the preclinical evidence to support clinical translation, typically through non-binding recommendations.4,5,18 Although formal international harmonisation in cell and gene therapy regulation remains incomplete,19, 20, 21 guidance across jurisdictions is broadly consistent in emphasising key translational principles. Our previous work has synthesised these recommendations into core domains, including demonstration of mechanism of action; evidence of disease modification using clinically relevant outcomes; alignment of preclinical intervention parameters (e.g., dose, route) with intended clinical use; and selection of preclinical model systems that are relevant to human disease and populations.17 Collectively, these principles provide a framework for explicitly linking preclinical evidence to the design and rationale of human trials.

What remains unclear is whether, and how, these regulator-endorsed principles are reflected in clinical trial protocols. Trial protocols are a primary document through which investigators formalise the scientific rationale for trial initiation and translate that rationale into specific design decisions.22, 23, 24 Some preclinical details may reasonably reside in investigator brochures or confidential regulatory submissions; however, current international regulatory standards emphasise that trial protocols should include transparent summaries of preclinical evidence sufficient to justify anticipated benefit and support risk–benefit assessment.1,22 We therefore conducted a systematic, cross-sectional protocol-level evaluation of registered cell therapy clinical trials to assess the extent to which supporting preclinical efficacy evidence aligns with key translational principles derived from regulatory guidance, and how these patterns vary across trial phases and evidence contexts.

Protocol and reporting

A pre-specified protocol for this systematic, cross-sectional, protocol-level analysis was deposited to Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/DS6HZ).25, 26, 27 Results are reported following relevant components of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) and Synthesis Without Meta-Analysis (SWiM) guidelines, reflecting the systematic search and evidence identification components of the study.25,28

Data source

ClinicalTrials.gov was systematically searched for Early Phase I, Phase I, Phase I/II, Phase II, Phase II/III, and Phase III clinical trials of cell therapies that included a protocol attachment. ClinicalTrials.gov was selected because it provides structured access to trial records and protocol attachments through the Aggregate Analysis of ClinicalTrials.gov database, enabling protocol-level extraction at scale.29 Local registries from other jurisdictions with active cell therapy development were not searched because protocol attachments and structured registry data were not consistently available in a comparable format, which would limit consistent protocol-level extraction. Protocols submitted to ClinicalTrials.gov should include all amendments that have been approved by both a human subjects protection review board and the US Food and Drug Administration (if applicable), indicating that the protocols available on ClinicalTrials.gov are intended to reflect both regulator and institutional review board requirements.23,24

Eligibility criteria

All Early Phase I, Phase I, Phase I/II, Phase II, Phase II/III, and Phase III trials of cell therapies for any indication or population, registered in ClinicalTrials.gov, with an attached protocol document were included. “Cell therapies” were identified using a previously published definition, “Therapies containing viable cells of allogeneic or autologous origin undergoing a manufacturing process. These cells may be combined with non-cellular components and/or may be genetically modified.”17 No exclusion criteria based on country, trial status, or date of registration were used.

Search strategy

An up-to-date, archived, copy of all data in ClinicalTrials.gov were obtained from the Aggregate Analysis of ClinicalTrials.gov—Clinical Trials Transformation Initiative (AACT-CTTI; https://aact.ctti-clinicaltrials.org/; archive date March 31, 2025).29 A search strategy and list of relevant keywords were developed in collaboration with an information specialist to identify trials based on the eligibility criteria above. Broadly, .TXT tables of the ClinicalTrials.gov database containing relevant search information were joined and queried using R (v4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) to identify studies based on selected keywords and relevant fields of interest (e.g., study title, study description, interventions, etc.).30 The search strategy consisted of four main components to allow a comprehensive search. Study screening

After using R to filter the complete trial list to only trials that: 1) included an attached protocol; 2) were listed as Early Phase I, Phase I, Phase I/II, Phase II, Phase II/III, and Phase III trials, and; 3) included at least one of the keywords described above and in the Supplementary Methods, the list of potentially eligible studies was exported as a .RIS file for upload to DistillerSR (DistillerSR Inc.), using the “write_bib” function from the “synthesisr” package.33 Two reviewers independently screened studies based on the title, study description, and intervention fields of ClinicalTrials.gov to identify entries meeting the definition of a “cell therapy” (level 1 screening). Studies included at this level were further verified using the full-text protocol (level 2 screening). Conflicts at each level were resolved through consensus discussion, or consultation with a third reviewer, as necessary.

Data extraction

Data were first extracted from elements included in ClinicalTrials.gov, followed by additional extraction from the full-text PDF protocol documents.

Trial characteristics were automatically extracted from structured .TXT tables of the ClinicalTrials.gov database by joining relevant fields of interest to the library of included ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers (NCT#). These variables comprised the first phase of extraction and were used to describe the study sample and define stratification variables for downstream analyses. Extracted items included descriptive information for screening (e.g., title, interventions, trial description) as well as key trial characteristics such as phase, sponsor type, regulatory oversight, trial status, and disease indication. A detailed list of relevant ClinicalTrials.gov tables and variable names is provided in the Supplementary Methods. Automated extraction was limited to structured ClinicalTrials.gov fields available in AACT tables. No automated text-mining, natural language processing, or machine-learning tools were used to classify protocol-reported preclinical evidence elements; these data were extracted manually by two independent reviewers using a predefined extraction form, as described below.

For the second part of extraction, PDF protocol attachments for all included trials were uploaded to DistillerSR. Two reviewers independently extracted protocol-level data across three structured components aligned with the study objectives, as follows. During extraction, reviewers annotated text segments within protocol PDFs with their coding decisions to support consensus discussions with the second reviewer in cases where disagreements arose. The extraction form operationalised each regulator-derived recommendation as a discrete binary item, thereby reducing subjectivity by requiring explicit protocol text to support each coded element (see Supplementary Methods for full extraction framework).

Alignment with regulatory-derived translational principles (primary objective)

We extracted the presence or absence of specific preclinical efficacy elements corresponding to key translational principles derived from regulatory guidance. These principles were operationalised based on our prior systematic synthesis of guidance from 38 regulatory organisations,17 which included all national member and observer agencies in the International Council for Harmonisation. From this work, we identified 20 preclinical evidence recommendations endorsed by ≥50% of regulatory agencies (both the US Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency were mandatory inclusions). This threshold was selected to focus the primary analysis on recommendations endorsed by a majority of regulatory agencies and does not suggest that recommendations endorsed below this level are unimportant (all recommendations below this threshold are provided in the data supplement). Recommendations were grouped into five overarching domains reflecting how regulators expect preclinical evidence to support clinical translation: 1) mechanism of action; 2) intervention parameters (e.g., dose, route, product characteristics); 3) study rigour (e.g., replication, randomisation, blinding); 4) disease modification (e.g., magnitude, precision, and clinical relevance of therapeutic effects); and 5) relevance of preclinical models to human populations and conditions. Each trial protocol was systematically evaluated for explicit reporting of these predefined elements within the justification for trial initiation.

Sources of evidence and types of arguments used in trial justification (secondary objective 1)

We extracted all sources of evidence and types of arguments invoked to justify trial initiation and support anticipated therapeutic benefits. The types of arguments used to justify initiation of the clinical trial included anticipated therapeutic benefit, risk mitigation or safety considerations, disease burden, and limitations of existing treatment options. Categories of evidence sources included both preclinical (in vivo animal, in vitro, in silico systems) and human evidence (e.g., prior clinical trials or observational studies, outcomes from related therapies). This enabled characterisation of how different forms of evidence are combined in translational justifications.

Contextual variables for stratified analyses of alignment and evidence composition (secondary objective 2)

To examine how protocol-level reporting of translational principles and the composition of supporting evidence vary across trial contexts, we extracted a set of contextual and stratification variables. These included trial-level characteristics extracted from ClinicalTrials.gov (trial phase, disease indication, sponsor type, regulatory oversight, and study status), as well as features of the supporting evidence base extracted from the protocols (presence of original preclinical data, inclusion of large-animal studies). These variables were used to stratify analyses of alignment with regulatory-derived translational principles, enabling evaluation of how trial justification practices and reliance on different forms of evidence vary across different development stages and evidence contexts.

Statistics

Inter-rater agreement was summarised for screening and extraction using the overall weighted kappa statistics reported by DistillerSR. Descriptive characteristics of included trials were summarised using frequencies and proportions across key trial-level variables (e.g., phase, indication, sponsor type, regulatory oversight).28 Analyses were conducted in three sequential stages. We first summarised the frequency and co-occurrence of argument types and evidence sources used to justify trial initiation (Secondary objective 1). We then estimated the proportion of trials reporting each of the predefined preclinical evidence recommendations (with two-sided 95% Wilson score confidence intervals (CI)), both individually and aggregated across domains, restricting analyses to trials citing preclinical evidence (Primary objective). Finally, we examined variation in these patterns across trial phases and predefined contextual variables using stratified descriptive analyses (Secondary objective 2). Exploratory analyses were conducted to assess whether descriptive differences were directionally consistent after accounting for clustering. Stratum-specific proportions and absolute differences in proportions were estimated using linear probability models with HC1 cluster-robust sandwich variance estimators, clustering by intervention cell type (17 clusters; mean 12·3 protocols per cluster in the n = 209 primary analytic subset). Cluster-robust 95% confidence intervals were calculated as the estimate ±1·96 times the robust standard error. These analyses were interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Ethics

Ethical approval was not required for this analysis of publicly available protocol documents.

Role of funders

Funders had no role in study design, data collection, data analyses, interpretation, or writing of the manuscript.

Results

Study selection and trial characteristics

From 532,208 clinical trials in ClinicalTrials.gov, we identified 1342 trials that potentially met eligibility criteria at first screening. Of these, 305 cell therapy trials with available protocol documents met full eligibility criteria and were included in data extraction (Fig. 1). Overall weighted kappa values for inter-rater agreement were 0·80 for level 1 screening, 0·63 for level 2 full-text screening/protocol verification, 0·84 for trial-characteristic extraction, 0·83 for preclinical-evidence extraction, and 0·82 for product-approval extraction.Fig. 1Identification and selection of cell therapy trial protocols for analysis.

Identification and selection of cell therapy trial protocols for analysis.

Most trials were Phase I (n = 164/305; 53·8%) or Phase II (n = 117; 38·4%), with 24 (7·9%) Phase III studies included (Table 1). The majority of trials were targeted at cancer indications (64·3%), with all other individual indications each representing less than 5% of the sample. The most common cell types were adoptive T-cell therapies (23·6%) and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapies (21·0%), followed by mesenchymal stromal cells (16·4%), dendritic cells (9·2%), and tumour infiltrating lymphocytes (6·6%), with all other cell types accounting for less than 5% of the sample each. Most trials were regulated by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (88·9%), with additional oversight from other agencies as shown in Table 1. The sample was evenly divided between industry (49·5%) and non-industry (50·5%) sponsors (Table 1). Composition of translational justifications

Among all trials, 94·8% (95% CI, 91·6–96·7; n, 289/305) included an explicit statement justifying initiation of the clinical trial (Supplementary Table S3). Of these, 97·6% (95% CI, 95·1–98·8; 282/289) cited anticipated therapeutic benefit. Multiple justification types were frequently combined, with 67·8% (95% CI, 62·2–72·9; 196/289) referencing therapeutic benefit alongside risk considerations, disease burden, and limitations of existing treatments (Supplementary Figure S1).

Of the 282 trials citing anticipated benefit, 94·0% (95% CI, 90·6–96·2; 265/282) included human evidence (previous trials, observational studies, experiences with the therapy for other indications, experiences with related therapies for the same indication, etc.), and 74·1% (95% CI, 68·7–78·9; 209/282) included at least one form of preclinical evidence (in vitro, in vivo rodent, large animal; Fig. 2). The most common combination of evidence sources included human data, in vivo rodent models, and in vitro studies (37·2%, 95% CI, 31·8–43·0; 105/282; Fig. 2). Across preclinical evidence types, in vivo rodent models were most frequently reported (68·1%, 95% CI, 62·4–73·3; 192/282), followed by in vitro models (53·2%, 95% CI, 47·4–58·9; 150/282), large animal models (12·8%, 95% CI, 9·4–17·2; 36/282), and in silico approaches (1·4%, 95% CI, 0·6–3·6; 4/282). Mechanism of action

Mechanism of action was the most prevalent domain, with nearly all trial protocols including at least one mechanistic element (94·7%, 95% CI 90·8–97·0; 198/209; Supplementary Table S4). Demonstration of functional activity (87·1%, 95% CI, 81·9–91·0) and target modulation (82·8%, 95% CI, 77·1–87·3) were especially common, followed by localisation data (49·8%, 95% CI, 43·0–56·5) and explicit justification that the proposed mechanism is relevant to humans (50·2%, 95% CI, 43·5–57·0; Table 2). These elements were descriptively most frequently reported in Phase I and less frequently reported in later phases. Mechanism of action remained the most prevalent domain of preclinical evidence in both Phase II and Phase III trials.

Intervention parameters

At least one element related to intervention parameters was explicitly reported in 68·9% (95% CI, 62·3–74·8; 144/209) of trial protocols (Supplementary Table S4). 39·2% (95% CI, 32·9–46·0) of trials used preclinical evidence to support any dosing element (Table 2). The similarity of the intervention product between the supporting preclinical evidence and clinical trials was documented in 36·8% (95% CI, 30·6–43·6) of trials overall, and was the only element related to intervention parameters that was not descriptively lower in Phase III trials. Other dosing and delivery justifications were reported at comparable levels in Phases I and II but were rarely included in Phase III trials (e.g., selection of dosing schedule, Phase I: 10·5% (95% CI, 8·7–20·9); Phase II: 10·7% (95% CI, 6·4–21·3); Phase III: 0·0% (95% CI, 0·0–27·8). Phase III estimates should be interpreted cautiously as only 10 Phase III protocols included preclinical evidence, resulting in wide confidence intervals.

Study rigour

Elements reflecting preclinical study rigour were reported in 48·3% of trial protocols (95% CI, 41·6–55·1; 101/209; Table 2). Reporting of replication across experimental conditions or models was the most common element (45·9%; 95% CI, 39·3–52·7; 96/209; Supplementary Table S4). Foundational design elements commonly expected in clinical research, such as randomisation (1·0%, 95% CI, 0·2–3·4), blinding (0·0%, 95% CI, 0·0–1·8), sample-size justification (0·0%, 95% CI, 0·0–1·8), and study registration (0·0%, 95% CI, 0·0–1·8) were rarely reported in the protocol-level summaries of supporting preclinical evidence (Supplementary Table S4). Overall, the rigour of preclinical evidence was framed primarily in terms of replication within experimental programs and triangulation of directionally consistent effects across a variety of experimental conditions and model systems, rather than adherence to formal bias-reduction or confirmatory standards.

Disease modification

Preclinical evidence of disease modification was reported in 69·9% of trial protocols (95% CI, 63·3–75·7; 146/209). Demonstration of effect magnitude at a specific time point was described in 61·2% (95% CI, 54·5–67·6) of trials (Supplementary Table S4), while explicit reporting of clinically relevant outcome measures (23·9%, 95% CI, 18·6–30·1) and duration of effect (27·3%, 95% CI, 21·7–33·7) were less common (Table 2). Measures of preclinical endpoint validity (1·9%, 95% CI, 0·7–4·8) and use of surrogate endpoints with direct trial relevance (1·4%, 95% CI, 0·5–4·1) were rare. Reporting of disease modification elements was similar between Phases I and II, and descriptively lower in Phase III trials (e.g., use of outcome measures relevant to human trials, Phase I: 23·4% (95% CI, 16·8–30·7); Phase II: 28·0% (95% CI, 19·1–39·0); Phase III: 0·0% (95% CI, 0·0–27·8). Overall, while many trials demonstrated that an intervention altered surrogate endpoints in preclinical models (66·0%, 95% CI, 59·4–72·1), few explicitly articulated how those outcomes were clinically relevant to humans, or to the conditions of the proposed trial (Supplementary Table S4).

Relevance of preclinical models

Relevance of preclinical models was the least frequently reported domain overall (24·4%, 95% CI, 19·1–30·7; 51/209; Supplementary Table S4). Explicit justification of model relevance to human populations was reported in 13·9% (95% CI, 9·8–19·2; 29/209) of trials, with fewer trials reporting model limitations (7·7%, 95% CI, 4·8–12·1), describing rationale for expected similarity in pharmacological response between preclinical models and humans (4·3%, 95% CI, 2·3–8·0), or using models where the intervention could be feasibly delivered the same way as in humans (2·4%, 95% CI, 1·0–5·5; Table 2). Justifications for the relevance of preclinical models were most common in Phase I, less common in Phase II (e.g., outline limitations of model systems, Phase I: 11·3% (95% CI, 6·8–18·1); Phase II: 2·7% (95% CI, 0·7–9·2), and not reported in the small subset of Phase III protocols that cited preclinical evidence (0·0% for all elements, 95% CI, 0·0–27·8). Very few studies discussed alignment of preclinical models with other translational elements, such as alignment with human comorbidities (0·5%, 95% CI, 0·1–2·7), population demographics (0·0%, 95% CI, 0·0–1·8), disease pathophysiology (7·7%, 95% CI, 4·8–12·1), or disease time course (0·0%, 95% CI, 0·0–1·8; Supplementary Table S4).

Across domains, reporting of preclinical justification elements was descriptively lower in later-phase trials, suggesting that early evidentiary considerations are not consistently carried forward as development progresses and new evidence accumulates. Across most recommendations, trials that included large animal data or presented original data from preclinical studies demonstrated higher reporting of corresponding elements than trials without these models/data (Fig. 3). For example, using preclinical evidence to inform at least one element of trial dosing was reported in 63·9% (95% CI, 45·8–82·0) of trials with large animal data, and 58·2% (95% CI, 41·3–75·2) of trials providing original preclinical data, compared with 34·1% (95% CI, 24·7–43·5) and 30·3% (95% CI, 19·3–41·3) respectively in trials without these data.

Conversely, trials citing prior human evidence and industry-sponsored trials generally showed lower reporting across many preclinical elements, although confidence intervals were often wide for some contrasts. For example, preclinical evidence to justify starting dose selection was reported in 10·3% (95% CI, 4·6–15·9) of trials with human data compared with 42·9% (95% CI, 16·1–69·6) of trials without human data (Fig. 3). Discussion

This study evaluates how preclinical evidence is reported in trial protocols to justify initiation of human cell therapy trials, and whether those justifications align with regulatory guidance.17 Across a large sample of registered trials, we found that while protocols commonly incorporated preclinical evidence, it was unevenly structured across regulatory guidance domains. Mechanistic evidence and demonstrations of biological activity were highly prevalent, whereas more explicit translational elements, such as justification of model relevance, linkage of preclinical findings to dose and delivery decisions, and reporting of core study-rigour features, were much less frequently articulated. These findings should be interpreted as variability in protocol-level reporting and how translational justification is formalised, not as direct evidence that the preclinical evidence package available to investigators or regulators was inadequate. Overall, preclinical justification in cell therapy protocols is often organised around biological plausibility and proof-of-concept, rather than around an explicit account of why the cited evidence supports important design elements and rationale of the proposed human trial. While it is possible that these preclinical justifications may be communicated to regulators through other confidential channels, omitting this information from protocols decreases the ability to assess whether the available evidence adequately supports trial initiation.

Evidence related to mechanism of action, particularly functional activity of the cell therapy and modulation of target host tissues, was widely reported across phases, suggesting that cell therapy development is strongly anchored in mechanistic claims. Even among the subset of Phase III trials that continued to cite preclinical evidence, mechanistic elements remained more prevalent than other domains. This may indicate that some aspects of preclinically-derived mechanistic understanding continue to be perceived as important for supporting trial credibility, even as human evidence accumulates.12 More broadly, however, reporting across other preclinical justification domains was descriptively lower in later-phase protocols. This may be expected as evidence from preceding clinical studies accumulates and reduces the need to restate earlier preclinical justification. However, preclinical considerations may remain relevant for cell therapies when product characteristics, manufacturing processes, dose, or other trial features change during development. In these settings, limited reporting can make it more difficult to trace how earlier evidence informed, or was reconsidered, in subsequent trial-design decisions.

Beyond demonstrating biological activity, explicit linkage between preclinical findings and human trial design elements was frequently absent. Rationales for intervention parameters, such as dose justification, were often not clearly articulated despite strong regulatory endorsement for using preclinical evidence to inform these decisions. Similarly, while many trials reported disease modification in preclinical models, few explicitly addressed endpoint relevance, persistence of effects at clinically meaningful timepoints, or the validity of surrogate markers. Most notably, explicit justification of preclinical model relevance to humans was rare. These patterns point to a consistent translational reasoning gap: preclinical evidence is often present, but the reasoning that should connect that evidence to specific elements of trial design is incompletely formalised.15 Similarly, the infrequent reporting of preclinical study rigour elements such as randomisation and blinding should not be interpreted as proof that these practices were absent from the underlying preclinical studies; however, because these features are central to judging evidentiary credibility, their omission limits reviewers’ ability to assess the robustness of the preclinical evidence being used to justify trial initiation. If low reporting reflects underlying study conduct, then inadequate use of randomisation, blinding, or related bias-reduction practices may exaggerate estimated preclinical treatment effects.16,34,35 Such overestimation may propagate into clinical trial planning when preclinical effects inform assumptions about anticipated treatment effects in humans.

Trials incorporating large animal data or presenting original preclinical data (often contained in figures or tables showing preclinical results, as opposed to narrative summaries or citations alone) showed more comprehensive reporting across many translational principles. These features may mark settings in which translational uncertainty is being addressed more explicitly, possibly because the intervention is more novel, model limitations are more visible, or additional regulator-facing justification is required. However, the higher reporting observed in protocols using large-animal data should not be interpreted as implying that large-animal models are always necessary or preferable. Future preclinical strategies should be fit-for-purpose and may increasingly rely on advanced in vitro, organoid, organ-on-a-chip, or computational approaches that reduce animal use while improving human relevance. Regardless of model type, protocols should explain why the selected system is appropriate for the intended clinical population, route of administration, biological mechanism, and outcome being evaluated.

In contrast, trials citing prior human evidence and industry-sponsored trials tended to show lower reporting across many preclinical justification elements. This pattern may reflect strategic emphasis on existing clinical experience, rather than on weaker preclinical foundations. Once human data are available, preclinical evidence may be perceived as less central to trial justification. Industry sponsors may also perform trials in therapeutic contexts with more developmental maturity, where prior clinical trials have generated confidence (i.e., de-risking) in the likelihood of downstream success. Also, industry sponsors may operate within environments in which proprietary considerations shape how preclinical data are summarised in publicly available protocols (i.e., for intellectual property or competitive advantage concerns).

These findings have several implications for the clinical development and translation of cell therapies, which have faced distinctive regulatory and translational challenges compared to other types of biological products.13,14 Cell therapy products often have elevated risk profiles, complex context-dependent mechanisms of action, pharmacodynamic effects that vary by disease setting, immunogenicity concerns, and sensitivity to manufacturing and delivery conditions.11,14 These features make extrapolation from preclinical systems especially difficult and increase the importance of carefully justified model selection, dose justification, and endpoint translation.16 Regulatory guidance across jurisdictions reflects this challenge by consistently emphasising the need to generate preclinical evidence under conditions that approximate anticipated clinical use, and to use that evidence to inform concrete aspects of trial design.5,17,18 Our findings suggest only partial operationalisation of these expectations in current protocol practices: biological plausibility is frequently presented, but explicit articulation of why selected models, doses, delivery approaches, and endpoints are expected to predict human outcomes is comparatively underdeveloped. Protocols are the most visible documents through which the rationale for exposing participants to an investigational cell therapy can be scrutinised outside confidential regulatory submissions. When the rationale for dose, route, model relevance, or endpoint selection is not explicit, regulators, ethics committees, investigators, and readers of trial protocols have less ability to determine whether the proposed design logically follows from the supporting evidence. This can weaken protocol review, make early clinical results harder to interpret, and obscure whether disappointing findings reflect an ineffective therapy or a poorly justified translational design.

Similar translational gaps have been described in other areas of biomedical research, where failures in preclinical translation contribute to inefficiencies and attrition in development pipelines.36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41 Prior work in cell therapies for ischaemic stroke has suggested that differences between preclinical and clinical design may contribute to translational loss of efficacy.42 Importantly, these gaps may not reflect an absence of the underlying evidence, but rather the absence of structured synthesis and explicit translational reasoning, which limits the evaluability of trial justification by regulators and ethics committees.7, 8, 9,43, 44, 45, 46 In this sense, the present study extends prior work on the completeness, robustness, and synthesis of supporting evidence in investigator brochures, protocols, and publications by examining how translational justification itself is structured in cell therapy trial protocols, and which types of preclinical claims are carried forward into the formal rationale for trial initiation. Strengthening translational justification may not require generating entirely new categories of evidence, but rather more explicitly and transparently articulating how preclinical findings support human relevance and specific trial design decisions.15

This study has limitations. ClinicalTrials.gov does not capture all global trials, some may be registered elsewhere or remain unregistered, and our search strategy, although systematic and broad, may not have identified every eligible cell therapy trial within the registry. Our findings may reflect the subset of cell therapy trials on ClinicalTrials.gov with publicly available protocol attachments. Trials registered only in other registries, trials without protocol attachments, or trials conducted in jurisdictions with different disclosure practices may differ in how preclinical evidence is documented. Moreover, protocol documents may not fully represent the entirety of preclinical evidence available to investigators or regulators (particularly in confidential documents such as investigator's brochures), and the scope of preclinical evidence described in the protocol may change across amendment versions. However, the AACT-CTTI provides uniquely structured, relational access to protocol-level materials, enabling a level of systematic evaluation not feasible in other registries.29 The present sample of over 300 protocols spans a broad range of cell types and disease indications that exceeds the depth of protocol-level preclinical evidence characterisation currently available in the published literature.47, 48, 49, 50, 51, 52, 53, 54 Importantly, although the supporting preclinical information available in protocols may not capture all evidence available to decision-makers, it does capture the evidence that investigators (a) chose to formalise in justifying trial initiation, and (b) are able to share with study participants and other researchers, making it an informative comparator against regulator-endorsed expectations.17

In summary, cell therapy trial protocols commonly include preclinical evidence demonstrating biological activity and mechanistic plausibility, but less consistently articulate the regulatory translational principles needed to connect that evidence to human trial design. The central gap identified in this study is therefore not simply the availability of preclinical evidence, but the incomplete formalisation of the reasoning that links that evidence to the design and justification of the proposed human trial. Several actions will help address these gaps. We advocate that trial protocols should include structured summaries or tables that identify the specific translational claim being supported; explain why the preclinical model is relevant to the target human disease and population; link preclinical (or other supporting clinical) findings to dose, route, timing, product characteristics, and endpoint choices; and provide sufficient information on supporting study rigour to allow patients, researchers, and other reviewers to assess evidentiary credibility without the need to trust regulator or ethics committee determinations, or rely on investigator interpretations. These summaries need not reproduce the full investigator brochure or confidential regulatory submission but should provide enough transparent reasoning to show why the proposed trial design follows from the available evidence. Development of protocol templates, checklists, or regulator-facing justification summaries or tables for cell therapies could help investigators present this reasoning more consistently. Strengthening this translational bridge from preclinical studies to human trial design through clearer, more explicit, and more structured justification, may improve regulatory evaluation, enhance interpretation of early-phase findings, and support more reliable and efficient development of cell-based therapies. By clarifying not only whether a therapy works in preclinical models, but why those models meaningfully inform human trials, the field can better align scientific innovation with responsible clinical translation.
