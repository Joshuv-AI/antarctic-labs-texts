Epidemiological characteristics of Helicobacter pylori infection in Shizuishan and its association with colorectal cancer and the gut microbiota
Authors: Hailong Qi, Jun Zhu, Jing Yang, Chan Wang, Lei Fu, Xin Chen, Minyuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate the epidemiological characteristics of Helicobacter pylori (Hp) infection in Shizuishan of Ningxia and to analyze the associations of Hp infection and the gut microbiota with the development of colorectal cancer (CRC).MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted. From January 2024 to June 2024, 2,000 community residents from 3 areas of Shizuishan City were recruited for Hp screening using the 13C-urea breath test irrespective of digestive symptoms. From this screened community population, 300 healthy controls with normal colonoscopy findings and 100 patients with pathologically confirmed CRC were selected, and stool samples were collected for 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing to analyze the gut microbiota. Chi-square test, t test, and multivariable logistic regression were used for statistical analysis.ResultsThe overall Hp positivity rate among screened adults in Shizuishan was 58.4%. The Hp positivity rate in the CRC group (78.0%) was significantly higher than that in the healthy control group (52.3%) (P < 0.001). After adjustment for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, and drinking history, Hp infection remained an independent risk factor for CRC (odds ratio (OR) = 2.15, 95% confidence interval (95% CI) 1.42–3.28). Gut microbiota analysis showed that alpha diversity (Shannon index) in the CRC group was significantly lower than that in the healthy control group (P < 0.001) and in the Hp-positive healthy subgroup (P < 0.05). Beta-diversity analysis showed significant separation in microbial community structure among the three groups (Adonis, P < 0.001). Linear discriminant analysis effect size analysis showed enrichment of pathogenic bacteria such as Fusobacterium nucleatum and Escherichia coli in the CRC group, whereas beneficial bacteria that produce short-chain fatty acids, such as Roseburia and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, were reduced. The microbial community structure of Hp-positive healthy individuals had shown a transitional state between that of Hp-negative healthy individuals and CRC patients.ConclusionsThe Hp positivity rate among screened community residents was relatively high in Shizuishan, and Hp infection is an independent risk factor for CRC. Hp infection may increase the risk of CRC by disrupting gut microbiota homeostasis and promoting the formation of a pro-carcinogenic microbial community.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common malignant tumors worldwide. Both its incidence and mortality rank among the highest, imposing a substantial disease burden on society [1]. In recent years, with changes in lifestyle and the westernization of dietary patterns in China, the incidence of CRC has shown a sustained upward trend [2]. Helicobacter pylori (Hp), a Gram-negative bacillus that colonizes the gastric mucosa, is an established causative factor of chronic gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, and gastric cancer [3]. However, the association between Hp infection and CRC risk remains controversial. Although several meta-analyses have suggested that Hp infection may increase CRC risk by 35–50% [4], the specific mechanisms involved have not yet been fully elucidated.

The gut microbiota plays a key role in maintaining human health and regulating immunity and metabolism. Disruption of its homeostasis (dysbiosis) has been confirmed to be closely related to the occurrence and progression of CRC [5]. In recent years, some scholars have proposed the concept of the "gastric-intestinal axis," suggesting that gastric Hp infection may remotely interfere with the composition and function of the distal gut microbiota by altering gastric acid secretion, inducing low-grade systemic inflammation, or affecting host immune regulation, thereby participating in the pathogenesis of CRC [67]. However, this hypothesis still requires validation in specific populations.

Shizuishan City, located in Ningxia in northwestern China, has a relatively high burden of digestive tract tumors [8]. However, there is still a lack of city-level population-based level data on Hp infection in Shizuishan. Previous China-wide analyses reported pooled Hp prevalence estimates of approximately 44.2%, with variation by study period, region, population, and diagnostic method [9]; recent regional surveillance also suggests substantial geographic heterogeneity in Hp positivity [10]. Because Hp infection is often acquired through long-term household and community exposure, regional differences in Hp positivity may be influenced by birth-cohort effects, intrafamilial transmission, shared eating habits, socioeconomic conditions, sanitation, and prior testing and eradication patterns. Therefore, region-specific screening data are needed to determine whether Shizuishan represents a relatively high-burden area for Hp infection and to support localized prevention strategies. There is also a lack of systematic studies on the relationship among Hp infection, the gut microbiota, and CRC in this region. In addition, the role of the gut microbiota in CRC is double-edged [11]. This study aimed to: (1) define the epidemiological characteristics of Hp infection among adults in Shizuishan; (2) investigate the association between Hp infection and CRC risk; and (3) analyze the impact of Hp infection on the structure of the gut microbiota and preliminarily explore its potential mechanism in CRC development in this region, thereby providing a scientific basis for the formulation of localized CRC prevention and control strategies.

Methods

Study design and patients

This study was a cross-sectional study. For the first-stage Hp infection screening, from January 2024 to June 2024, 2,000 community-dwelling residents aged 20–75 years (residence duration ≥ 5 years) were recruited by cluster sampling from Dawukou District, Huinong District, and Pingluo County of Shizuishan City. Participants were recruited from community clusters irrespective of digestive symptoms and were not selected because they sought medical care for suspected Hp infection. Thus, the first-stage denominator represented screened community residents rather than patients tested because of digestive symptoms. Exclusion criteria were: (1) use of antibiotics, proton pump inhibitors, bismuth agents, or H2 receptor antagonists within the previous 4 weeks; (2) history of gastric surgery; (3) pregnancy or lactation; and (4) refusal to sign informed consent.

Between July 2024 and March 2025, 100 patients with CRC were consecutively enrolled at the Second People's Hospital of Shizuishan City. All CRC diagnoses were confirmed via colonoscopy and histopathological examination. During the same period, 300 healthy volunteers were recruited as a control group. These controls underwent Hp screening and colonoscopy, with normal endoscopic findings confirming the absence of polyps, tumors, and inflammatory bowel disease. The control subjects were frequency-matched to the CRC patients by age and sex.

Clinical data were gathered through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained investigators using a standardized questionnaire. The collected information included demographic characteristics (age and sex), lifestyle factors (smoking status and alcohol consumption), dietary patterns, and personal as well as family medical histories for all participants. Hp infection testing

All participants completed the 13C-urea breath test (13C-UBT). After fasting, participants swallowed a reagent containing 75 mg of 13C-urea and remained seated for 30 min, after which breath samples were collected into gas sampling bags. Detection was performed using an infrared spectrometer analyzer from Beijing Huagen Anbang Technology Co., Ltd., and a delta over baseline (DOB) value ≥ 4.0 was defined as Hp positivity.

Stool sample collection and deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) extraction

Approximately 1 g of fresh morning stool was collected from all study subjects and stored in a −80 degrees C ultra-low temperature freezer within 2 h. Fecal microbial genomic DNA was extracted using the OMEGA Soil DNA Kit strictly according to the manufacturer's instructions.

16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene sequencing: The V3-V4 variable region of the bacterial 16S ribosomal RNA gene was amplified using specific barcoded primers (341F: 5'-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3' and 806R: 5'-GGACTACHVGGGTATCTAAT-3'). This PCR amplification was performed for 16S rRNA amplicon library preparation and gut microbiota sequencing, not for diagnosing Hp infection. Hp positivity was determined by the 13C-urea breath test; therefore, no separate Hp diagnostic PCR results were generated. After purification and quantification, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products were subjected to 2 × 250 bp paired-end sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq platform.

Bioinformatics analysis

Bioinformatics analysis was performed using Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology 2 (QIIME 2, version 2023.9) [12]. After quality filtering, denoising, and chimera removal of the raw sequencing data, amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were obtained [13]. Taxonomic annotation was performed based on the SILVA database (v138). Alpha diversity indices (Chao1 and Shannon) were used to evaluate within-community richness and evenness. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed based on the Bray–Curtis distance matrix.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0. Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and comparisons between groups were performed using the independent-samples t test; non-normally distributed variables are expressed as median (interquartile range) [M (IQR)], and comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as number (percentage) [n (%)], and comparisons between groups were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test. A multivariable logistic regression model was used to calculate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI) for the association between Hp infection and CRC, with adjustment for potential confounders including age, sex, BMI, smoking history, and drinking history. Differences in the gut microbiota among groups were identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis, with a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score > 2.0 and P < 0.05 considered statistically significant for differential taxa. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic characteristics of the patients

A total of 2,000 individuals underwent Hp screening; the healthy control group comprised 300 participants and the CRC group comprised 100 participants. After matching, there were no significant differences between the two groups in age or sex distribution (P > 0.05), indicating good comparability. There were also no significant differences between the CRC group and the healthy control group in baseline variables such as BMI, smoking, and drinking (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Epidemiological characteristics of Hp infection in Shizuishan

Among the 2,000 screened individuals, 1,168 were Hp-positive, yielding an overall Hp positivity rate of 58.4%. Among the 300 healthy controls with normal colonoscopy findings, 157 were Hp-positive, corresponding to an Hp positivity rate of 52.3%; this indicates that Hp positivity was also common among apparently healthy community residents in this region. The Hp positivity rate differed across regions (56.1% in Dawukou District, 61.5% in Huinong District, and 57.8% in Pingluo County, P < 0.05) and increased with age (20–39 years: 51.2%, 40–59 years: 59.8%, ≥ 60 years: 63.5%, P < 0.01). The Hp positivity rate was slightly higher in men (60.1%) than in women (56.9%) (P < 0.05).

Association between Hp infection and CRC

The Hp positivity rate in the CRC group (78.0%, 78/100) was significantly higher than that in the healthy control group (52.3%, 157/300), although Hp positivity exceeded 50% in both groups. The absolute difference was 25.7 percentage points, and Pearson chi-square testing confirmed statistical significance (χ2 = 20.68, P < 0.001). Univariate analysis showed that Hp infection was significantly associated with CRC risk (OR = 3.23, 95% CI 1.98–5.28). Analysis of differential taxa among groups

LEfSe analysis revealed microbial biomarkers that differed significantly among the three groups (Fig. 2). Compared with the healthy control group, the CRC group showed significant enrichment of proinflammatory and potentially carcinogenic taxa, such as Fusobacterium nucleatum [14] and Escherichia-Shigella, whereas beneficial bacteria capable of producing short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate and exerting anti-inflammatory effects, including Faecalibacterium prausnitzii [5] and Roseburia, were significantly reduced. In Hp-positive healthy individuals, a decreasing trend in the abundance of these beneficial bacteria and an increase in some opportunistic pathogens had already been observed. Discussion

This study, conducted in Shizuishan, Ningxia, showed that the Hp positivity rate among screened adult community residents was 58.4%, which was higher than the pooled national estimate of 44.2% reported by a recent China-specific meta-analysis [9]. The high Hp positivity observed in healthy controls (52.3%) likely reflects common community exposure rather than the selection of symptomatic patients or misclassification of disease status. Potential contributors include age structure, intrafamilial transmission, shared eating habits, socioeconomic conditions, sanitation, and regional differences in prior Hp testing and eradication. Recent regional surveillance in Guangdong Province, China, also supports substantial geographic heterogeneity in Hp positivity [10]. Nevertheless, this high background positivity did not obscure the association with CRC, as the absolute between-group difference was 25.7 percentage points and the association remained significant after multivariable adjustment. More importantly, this study confirmed that Hp infection is an independent risk factor for CRC in this region, consistent with the conclusions of multiple previous meta-analyses [4], and provides epidemiological evidence from northwestern China that Hp infection is involved in CRC development. Our findings are consistent with Ionescu et al. [11] regarding the double-edged role of the gut microbiota in CRC.

The main novelty of this study lies in analyzing Hp infection, the gut microbiota, and CRC within a unified framework. We found that Hp infection was associated not only with an increased risk of CRC, but also with significant alterations in the structure of the gut microbiota. Specifically: (1) in otherwise healthy individuals with Hp infection, alpha diversity of the gut microbiota was already reduced, and the microbial structure lay between that of Hp-negative healthy individuals and CRC patients, suggesting that Hp infection may act as an initial insult that disrupts the stability of the intestinal microecology [6]; (2) the gut microbiota of CRC patients exhibited a typical "pathogenic ecology" pattern, characterized by enrichment of pro-carcinogenic bacteria (such as Fusobacterium nucleatum) and depletion of beneficial bacteria (such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii) [5141617]; and (3) Hp-positive healthy individuals already showed a trend toward reduced beneficial bacteria, strongly suggesting that Hp infection may, through the gastric-intestinal axis, create in advance an intestinal microenvironment favorable for CRC development. Hp infection may alter the physicochemical properties and immune status of the intestinal environment by inducing gastric mucosal atrophy, hypochlorhydria, or systemic inflammatory responses, thereby facilitating the colonization and proliferation of specific pathogens [671819]. These pathogens and their metabolites may further damage the intestinal mucosal barrier, activate oncogenic signaling pathways, and ultimately promote the development of CRC [2021]. Gut microbial dysbiosis is an important feature of CRC [22], and the influence of Hp infection on the gut microbiota through the gastric-intestinal axis has also been reported in gastric cancer research [23].

A limitation of this study is that cross-sectional design cannot establish causal relationships. Although available clinical and disease-related information for CRC patients was extracted from medical records, some tumor-specific variables and Hp-associated upper gastrointestinal conditions may not have been uniformly assessed in all patients. In particular, routine gastroscopy was not performed in every CRC patient; therefore, subclinical gastritis or peptic ulcer disease could not be completely excluded. Future large-scale prospective cohort studies, combined with metagenomics, metabolomics, and other technologies, are needed to further elucidate the specific molecular mechanisms by which Hp influences gut microbiota and CRC development. In addition, interventional studies, such as those examining the effects of Hp eradication therapy on the gut microbiota and long-term CRC risk, will be critical for validating this association and guiding clinical practice, and the Taipei global consensus may serve as a reference [24]. This study supports the findings of the large population-based study by Boustany et al. [25]. The relationship between Hp infection and inflammatory bowel disease has also been investigated [26], but the present study focused on CRC.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicate that the Hp positivity rate among screened community residents in Shizuishan, Ningxia, was relatively high compared with the reported pooled national estimate and that Hp infection is an independent risk factor for CRC in this region. Hp infection may be closely related to gut microbiota dysbiosis, and the two may act synergistically to participate in the pathogenesis of CRC. Therefore, implementing Hp screening and eradication therapy in high-risk populations in this region, together with attention to regulation of gut microecology, may have important public health implications for the primary prevention of CRC.
