Locus-specific LINE-1 mRNA expression reflects cell-type- and stimulus-specific senescence states
Authors: Minh N Tran, Sihan Liu, Melody Baddoo, Emily C Stow, Sudesh K Srivastav, Victoria Belancio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong Interspersed Element-1 (L1) causes DNA damage and inflammation, which are hallmarks of cellular senescence. To understand the role of endogenous L1 in senescence, accurate detection and measurement of L1 messenger RNA (mRNA) expression changes during this process is crucial, as L1 transcription is the prerequisite step of L1-related downstream effects. We developed an automated bioinformatics pipeline to quantify locus-level L1 mRNA expression in ex vivo and in vitro models of normal and cancer human cells under various senescence-inducing stimuli. Contrary to previous studies, our analyses show that L1 expression does not always increase in senescent cells, and when it does, the changes are moderate and independent of overlapping gene expression. We find that each cell type expresses subsets of L1 loci that are either expressed constitutively (core L1s) or only in proliferating or senescent cells. These subsets can be used to identify cellular state and inform the origin of senescent cells and senescence stimuli. Additionally, we present a comprehensive atlas of L1 splice sites used in proliferating and senescent cells, expanding our knowledge of L1 splicing events beyond the few previously validated sites. These findings establish L1 as an additional indicator for identifying and characterizing senescent cell populations.

Introduction

Long Interspersed Element-1 (LINE-1 or L1) is the only autonomous retroelement in human genomes that continues to copy itself through retrotransposition [1, 2]. L1 sequences make up ∼17% of human genome, which is represented by over 500 000 copies [3]. Of these, ~5000 loci are full-length and have the potential to be expressed, as they contain promoter sequence present in their 5ʹ untranslated region (5ʹ UTR) [3–8]. The remaining 495 000 L1 loci are 5ʹ truncated, lack promoters, and unable to generate L1 messenger RNA (mRNA) [3]. However, they can diversify the transcriptome by forming hybrid transcripts through passive transcription, alternative splicing, and polyadenylation [9–11].

The L1 retrotransposition cycle begins with the transcription of full-length L1 mRNA, which is translated into two proteins, ORF1p and ORF2p, required for retrotransposition [1, 5, 12]. ORF1p serves as a nucleic acid chaperone, while ORF2p contains an endonuclease (EN) and reverse transcriptase (RT) activities [5, 13–17]. These proteins form a ribonucleoprotein complex by binding to the L1 mRNA [12, 18–23]. Once in the nucleus, the ORF2p initiates de novo integration by nicking the target site in the genomic DNA and synthesizing complementary DNA using the L1 mRNA template [5, 14, 24–27]. Although custom antibodies detect ORF1p and ORF2p overexpressed in cultured cells, only endogenous ORF1p is detectable in vivo, exclusively in cancer cells [22, 28–31]. Thus, assessment of L1 mRNA remains a commonly used approach to trace L1 expression in normal cells. Apart from retrotransposition, L1 expression triggers DNA damage responses (DDR) due to ORF2p’s DNA cleavage that generates single- and double-strand breaks [32, 33]. The L1 RT activity is also a potent inducer of DDR through a currently unknown mechanism [34]. It also triggers an inflammatory response through activation of cGAS cascade [35, 36]. Consequently, L1 overexpression in cultured human cells leads to cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, or senescence [32, 33, 37].

Senescence is a cellular state triggered by stress or DNA damage [38–41]. Although the number of senescent cells increases with age and contributes to tissue degeneration and age-related diseases, they are also essential for tissue healing and healthy aging [42–49]. While L1 mRNA expression changes with age in an organ- and sex-specific manner [50] and is linked to senescence [33, 37], how it changes during replicative senescence remains unknown. This gap exists because methods like quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) used in published studies [35, 36, 51–53] do not differentiate between authentic L1 mRNA and passive inclusion of L1 sequences during gene transcription [54–56]. This distinction is critical for identifying L1 transcripts involved in retrotransposition and DDR, as opposed to L1 sequences that can alter cellular transcriptome post-integration.

There are two main types of L1-containing transcripts in cellular transcriptome (Fig. 1A) [54, 55, 57, 58]. The first type includes L1 mRNA, which is transcribed from the L1 5ʹ UTR sense promoter and terminates either at the L1 polyA site (L1 mRNA transcript) or at the polyA site in the flanking genomic sequence (L1 mRNA 3ʹ readthrough transcript) [54, 55, 58–60]. The L1 5ʹ UTR also has an antisense promoter that produces antisense transcripts containing L1 sequence and upstream genomic sequences (5ʹ antisense transcripts) [11, 55]. The second type includes L1 sequences incorporated into cellular genes transcripts (passive L1-related transcript) (Fig. 1A) [54, 55, 57, 58]. Among these two types of transcripts, only full-length L1 mRNA transcripts can encode both ORF1p and ORF2p and support autonomous de novo L1 insertion, making them most directly connected to L1-mediated DNA damage and L1 RT-associated inflammatory responses [2, 5, 12, 32, 33, 54, 61]. While passive and truncated L1-containing transcripts may still exert biological effects, including contributions to innate immune activation [62], they are treated as background for the purposes of this study, which focuses on quantifying autonomous L1 transcription [54, 55, 58]. Passive L1-related transcripts are as much as 100 times more abundant than L1 mRNA and are preferentially detected by methods like RT-qPCR, which cannot distinguish between L1 mRNA and passive transcripts [54, 55, 58, 59, 63] (Fig. 1A, PCR amplicons).

To identify expressed L1 loci annotated in the human genome, many bioinformatics tools were developed [55, 59, 64–68]. However, many of them do not detect locus-level L1 expression or do not discriminate between L1 mRNA and passive co-transcription of L1 sequences, often using non-stranded RNA-seq datasets [55]. In contrast, a user-friendly bioinformatics tool like L1EM accurately detects expressed L1 loci in samples with high L1 expression, such as human cancer samples [59]. However, its accuracy declines when detecting expressed L1 loci in normal cells, which support low L1 mRNA levels [59]. A multi-omics approach (ATLAS-seq, RNA-seq, and ChIP-seq) [58] accurately detects expressed L1 loci with 3′ read-through, including younger unannotated L1HS loci, though it is costly and challenging to use for high-throughput studies. Long-read RNA sequencing can capture full-length L1 transcripts [69], yet it remains costly and, in most datasets, only a small fraction of reads is longer than full-length L1 RNAs [70]. Thus, while no single approach is perfect and all existing methods struggle with detection of the youngest L1HS loci, several methods have valuable individual strengths [55, 71]. However, detection of low expressed L1 loci remains a shared weakness. To address this challenge, our lab reported a strategy for detecting low-level L1 expression in normal tissues ex vivo using short-reads RNA-seq with stringent alignment and manual validation [50, 54, 72], but this method is time-consuming and not scalable. Given the abundance of existing short-read datasets and the continuing use of short-read sequencing, a high-throughput method for accurate detection L1 expression in normal tissue RNA-seq data is still needed.

In addition to accurately detecting L1 mRNA expression, it is equally important to consider the impact of passively transcribed L1s [9–11, 73, 74]. Although they do not generate new inserts, they diversify cellular transcriptome through their functional donor and acceptor splice sites [10, 74, 75]. While transposable elements significantly impact splicing in cancer cells [76, 77], the extent and frequency of their splice site usage in proliferating and senescent cells are unknown. However, alternative splicing is an established mechanism underlying transcriptional changes during replicative senescence, including deregulation of genes involved in senescence [78–81]. Investigating whether L1 sequences participate in alternative splicing in senescent cells would establish L1 involvement and identify functional L1 splice sites involved.

In this study, we developed L1ABA, an automated pipeline for high-throughput, locus-specific detection of expressed L1 loci in RNA-seq datasets generated from cells supporting low L1 mRNA expression levels. Using this tool, we track changes in L1 expression during replicative senescence of human lung fibroblasts (WI-38 and LF-1), CD8+ T cells from young and old donors, and therapy-induced senescent (TIS) MCF7 cells [36, 82–84]. We identify senescence-specific L1 loci unique to cell types and senescence-inducing stimuli. We determine that expression of these loci is independent of gene expression, nominating them as additional senescence markers. Analysis of splicing patterns discovers significant differences in both genome-wide and L1-specific splicing events, including diversity in L1 splice-site usage between proliferating and senescent cells.

Materials and methods

RNA-seq alignment and manual validation to identify expression of annotated human L1 loci

RNA-seq alignment workflow can be found on GitHub. Briefly, we used Bowtie v0.12.8 to concordantly map read pairs (-X 600) with tryhard option (-y) to exhaustively search the entire genome for a single unique, best-matched alignment location (-m 1) [85]. The output BAM file was separated into strand-specific BAM files and then intersected with annotated full-length L1 loci in the same orientation using BEDTools v2.30.0 to generate output text files with full-length L1 loci and the corresponding number of aligned reads [54, 72, 86]. Manual validation of the output was performed using IGV [87]. The hg38 genome annotation was downloaded from Ensembl [88].

Validation of L1ABA

L1ABA (module 1 and module 2) workflows and parameters are detailed in the Supplementary Figs S1 and S2. L1ABA script can be found on GitHub. RNA-seq datasets used for L1ABA validation are MCF-7 (GSM2400231), HeLa (SRR14309746), BJ (GSM7300306 and ERR10312950), and LF-1 (GSE109700) (Supplementary Table S2). To identify optimal parameter values across datasets with varying levels of L1 mRNA expression, we systematically evaluated multiple values for each parameter, generating a comprehensive set of parameter combinations (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Supplementary Table S3). The output of predicted expressed L1 loci generated for each parameter combination was compared against manually validated L1 loci identified by IGV inspection (Supplementary Table S2). L1EM analysis

L1EM was used to detect L1 mRNA expression [59]. L1EM script can be found on GitHub. Briefly, the output Bowtie-aligned BAM files from our RNA-seq alignment mentioned earlier were used as input for L1EM with default parameters and hg38 annotation. The transcripts are assigned to either one of two L1 categories in L1 annotation of L1EM. Category 1 includes annotated L1 elements with 5ʹ UTR, and Category 0 includes 5ʹ UTR-truncated annotated L1 elements. Because L1 requires 5ʹ UTR promoter for transcription, only category 1 was considered in this study. In the L1EM output, any L1 locus in category 1 can have five different types of transcripts: “only,” “3prunon,” “passive sense/antisense,” and “antisense.” Among these five types, we considered L1 loci with reads corresponding to “only” and “3prunon” transcripts and required reads corresponding to “passive sense/antisense.” This is because L1 transcripts meeting these parameters comply with expected transcripts that can be generated from the L1 5ʹ UTR promoter. Specifically, “only” and “3prunon” transcripts are sense relative to L1 transcription and generate reads that fall entirely within L1 full-length unit or both L1 unit and downstream sequence of L1, respectively. Considering these parameters also excludes L1 sequences generated from upstream promoters (“passive sense/antisense”). The full_counts.txt output, which contains raw count estimates for each L1HS/L1PA* element, was used to estimate read counts for each L1 locus with “only” and “3prunon” transcripts. Once the output file containing read counts was generated, we only considered L1 loci to be expressed if they have the sum of “only” and “3prunon” reads greater than four reads (two read pairs).

For L1ABA, L1EM, and multi-omics approach comparison of L1HS expression in MCF-7 cells, expressed L1HS loci were extracted from the L1EM output file filter_L1HS_FPM.txt, retaining loci supported by at least 100 read pairs and reported as fragments per million (FPM). Expressed L1HS loci identified by the multi-omics approach were obtained from Lanciano et al. [89], and L1HS loci identified by L1ABA were extracted from the corresponding L1ABA output. Only full-length L1HS elements were considered in this comparison.

RNA composition quality control

To ensure that transcripts per million (TPM) values could be appropriately compared across samples, we performed comprehensive RNA composition analysis following the guidelines outlined in Zhao et al. [90]. All samples were first verified to meet the prerequisite criteria for cross-sample TPM comparison: (i) identical library preparation protocols, including strandedness, [2] consistent RNA isolation approach [poly(A)+ selection versus ribosomal RNA depletion); and (iii) comparable RNA composition profiles without extreme outliers that could distort the sequencing capacity available for gene quantification.

For RNA composition assessment addressing criterion [3], raw sequencing reads were aligned using Bowtie v0.12.8 to concordantly map read pairs (-X 600) with the tryhard option (-y) for exhaustive genome-wide search of single unique best alignments (-m 1) as described earlier. Gene-level quantification used featureCounts from the subread package v2.1.1 [91, 92] Transcript-level abundance was estimated in parallel using Salmon v1.10.1 [93] (paired-end mode, default settings) against an index built from Ensembl genome annotation [88].

Five metrics were evaluated to assess RNA composition bias: (i) ribosomal RNA content, (ii) mitochondrial gene expression, (iii) globin gene expression, (iv) cumulative expression of the top 20 most highly expressed genes, and (v) total aligned read counts. Outliers were identified using z-score standardization (|z| > 3), with warning thresholds set at >20% ribosomal, >15% mitochondrial, >5% globin, or >60% top 20 genes. All samples passed quality control with no warnings detected and no outliers identified, except for one replicate in the density-induced senescence WI-38 group, which showed a borderline mitochondrial z-score (z = 3.2). This sample was retained because the mitochondrial percentage (5.14%) remained well below the 15% threshold and all other metrics were within normal range (Supplementary Table S6). Library preparation, sequencing, and RNA isolation details are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Transcripts per million normalization for L1 expression

To quantify L1 expression, we calculated TPM values for individual L1 loci. Strand-specific read counts aligned to full-length L1 loci were obtained from featureCounts output for both sense and antisense orientations [94]. Only L1 loci classified as authentically expressed by L1ABA were included in the analysis.

TPM values were calculated using a combined scaling approach that included both authentically expressed L1 loci and all annotated genes (Ensembl hg38 gene annotation) [88]. This approach normalizes for both feature length and sequencing depth, with the scaling factor derived from all features within each sample. The resulting TPM values sum to ~1 000 000 per sample, enabling valid cross-sample comparisons under the conditions verified by our RNA composition quality control analysis.

For sequential passaging experiments, three subsets of expressed L1 with patterns of expression of interest to this study were identified in WI-38 and LF-1 cells using RNA-seq datasets as reported [36, 82]. Specifically, these subsets are core-specific (CS) L1, proliferation-specific (PS) L1, and senescence specific (SS) L1 loci that were identified for each cell line based on the criteria listed below (Table 1). These criteria for these L1 subsets are to ensure that (i) core-specific L1 loci are expressed in all/the majority of passages and experimental groups, (ii) proliferation-specific L1 loci are expressed in proliferating cells and not in senescent cells, (iii)senescence-specific L1 loci are expressed in senescent cells and not in proliferating cells, and (iv) group-specific L1 are exclusive to each experimental group. These criteria also account for some presence of senescent cells during proliferating passages.

Identification of genes containing expressed L1 loci and calculation of expression levels of these genes

Genes containing expressed L1 loci were identified by using BEDTools to intersect the coordinates of expressed L1 loci generated by L1ABA and genome annotation (hg38) downloaded from Ensembl [86, 88]. Gene expression levels of WI-38, LF-1, CD8+ T cells, and TIS MCF-7 datasets were measured by Salmon v0.8.2 [93].

Assessment of splicing junctions within L1 loci and chimeric splicing junctions involving L1 sequence and genomic sequences

RNA-seq samples were aligned to the L1.3 consensus sequence (GenBank accession number L19088.1) using STAR with default parameters to identify splice junctions within the L1 sequence [1, 95]. The number of splice junctions within the L1 sequence of each sample was extracted from the SJ.out.tab, the STAR output file containing high-confidence splice junctions, and then normalized by dividing by the number of reads uniquely mapped to L1.3 sequence.

RNA-seq samples were aligned to the hg38 genome assembly using STAR with default parameters to identify splice junctions containing L1 sequence and genomic sequences. The total number of splice junctions was obtained from the SJ.out.tab STAR output file for each sample [88, 95]. The splice donors and acceptors of these splice junctions were intersected with full-length L1 annotations and truncated L1 annotations (limited to L1PA subfamilies) using BEDTools to identify splice junctions involving full-length or truncated L1 and flanking regions [72, 86]. All these splice junction counts were normalized by dividing total number of splice junctions of interest by the number of reads uniquely mapped to hg38.

Assessment of differential splicing events. STAR output BAM files containing RNA-seq reads aligned to the hg38 genome were used as inputs for rMATS and the reference to identify differential splicing events [96]. Splicing events with P-values and false discovery rate < .05 were considered significant differential splicing events.

Data availability

The RNA-seq datasets analyzed in this study are publicly available under accession numbers GSE175533 (WI-38), GSE109700 (LF-1), GSE169009 (CD8+ T cells), and GSE229264 (TIS MCF-7). In these datasets, the senescence state was validated in the original studies using established markers, including senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining, and transcriptomic confirmation of senescence-associated signatures, such as elevated expression of CDKN2A (p16), CDKN1A (p21), and senescence-associated secretory phenotype genes. We adopted these validated classifications for our analyses.

Statistics

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Comparisons between multiple groups were performed using the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc multiple comparison tests or by non-parametric Aligned Rank Transformation (ART) analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. Pearson’s correlation was used for continuous/sequential experimental design (e.g. WI-38 and LF-1), whereas Spearman’s correlation was applied for data with categorical experimental design (e.g. CD8+ T cells and TIS MCF-7). Chi-square tests were used for comparisons between categorical groups. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism and R.

Results

L1 automated bioinformatics approach accurately detects annotated L1 loci expressed in normal cells

As shown in Fig. 1A, the cellular transcriptome contains different types of L1-related transcripts that cannot be unambiguously distinguished by conventional approaches developed for single-gene expression analyses. To create a workflow for accurate, high-throughput analyses of L1 mRNA expression, especially in tissues with low L1 expression, we automated the downstream manual validation process of our reported RNA-seq pipeline [54, 72] and called this pipeline L1ABA (Supplementary Figs S1 and S2). Our previous manual validation requires visual inspection of each L1 locus in IGV to examine: (i) numbers of reads aligned to an L1 locus, (ii) numbers of reads, which are in the same orientation as L1 transcription, aligned upstream of an L1 locus, and (iii) numbers of reads, which are in the same orientation as L1 transcription, aligned downstream of an L1 locus. An L1 locus is considered (i) authentically expressed if there are no or few upstream reads compared to reads aligned to L1 sequence, (ii) passively transcribed if there are as many or more upstream reads as those aligned to the L1 sequence, and (iii) expressed with 3′ read-through transcription if there are no or few upstream reads compared to reads aligned to L1 sequence, which is accompanied by downstream reads terminating at an adjacent genomic polyA site. L1ABA computationally automates these validation criteria by implementing user-adjustable parameters: L1-aligned read count threshold, upstream and downstream window sizes for read detection, and thresholds for upstream-to-L1 and downstream-to-L1 read ratios. This pipeline identifies expressed L1 loci using two complementary modules (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Figs S1 and S2). Both modules first classify L1 loci as intergenic or intragenic based on gene annotation. For intragenic L1s, they are further stratified by their transcriptional orientation relative to the host genes. This classification is important because L1 loci transcribed in the same orientation as their host genes are at higher risk of false-positive detection due to gene transcription reading through the L1 sequence (Fig. 1A). Therefore, same-orientation intragenic L1s are subjected to different filtering parameters than intragenic L1s in opposite orientations relative to gene transcription or L1s in intergenic regions to account for their higher risk of false-positive detection from gene read-through (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Figs S1 and S2).

L1ABA Module 1 identifies expressed L1 loci from the L1HS to L1PA8 subfamilies by calculating the ratio of reads aligned upstream of an L1 locus to reads aligned to the L1 locus itself (Supplementary Figs S1 and S2). We focused on these subfamilies because older L1 elements have likely accumulated promoter mutations that prevent autonomous expression [3, 97]. Additionally, manual curation of both cancer and normal cell lines (MCF-7, HeLa, BJ, LF-1) revealed that expressed L1s predominantly belong to L1HS-L1PA8 subfamilies, with the majority belonging to L1PA2-4 (Supplementary Table S2). Furthermore, biologically, while L1HS is the only retrotransposition-competent subfamily, expressed L1s from L1PA2–L1PA8 subfamilies may still contribute to DNA damage and inflammatory responses [33–35]. L1 loci with upstream-to-L1 read ratios exceeding a user-defined threshold are discarded as false positives, as high upstream read counts indicate passive transcription by upstream gene promoters rather than authentic L1 expression (Fig. 1A and B; Supplementary Figs S1 and S2).

L1ABA Module 2 identifies expressed L1HS loci that generate 3′ read-through transcripts (Supplementary Figs S1 and S2). We created this module for two reasons: (i) due to the high sequence similarity among L1HS elements, many L1HS loci have few or no reads aligned to the L1 sequence, but reads can align uniquely to the 3′ flanking sequence when read-through transcription occurs, and (ii) we aimed to capture as many expressed L1HS elements as possible since L1Hs loci are capable of active retrotransposition [4, 18]. Module 2 analyzes two categories of L1Hs loci annotated in the human genome assembly those with and without reads aligned to L1 sequence. For L1HS with reads aligned to their sequence, we calculate both upstream and downstream read ratios; L1HS loci are classified as expressed if the upstream ratio is below a user-defined threshold and the downstream ratio exceeds a user-defined threshold, indicating autonomous L1 transcription with readthrough transcription. Some of these L1HS loci identified by Module 2 will also be detected by Module 1; thus, the duplicates removal step is performed to generate L1ABA final output of unique L1 loci. L1HS loci without reads aligned to their sequence are classified as expressed if they have no upstream reads and their downstream reads in user-defined distance exceed a user-defined threshold, which is a pattern consistent with L1HS-specific transcription extending into 3′ flanking sequences.

To establish a range for L1ABA user-adjustable parameters, we systematically evaluated different parameter combinations (upstream/downstream distances from L1 sequence and maximal read ratios) to determine optimal values that perform robustly across cell lines with varying L1 expression levels. We tested different parameter combinations for both modules (Supplementary Fig. S2) in four cell lines with different L1 expression levels: breast cancer cells MCF-7 (high), cervical cancer cells HeLa (moderate), primary fetal lung fibroblasts LF-1 (low), and primary foreskin fibroblasts BJ (low). We tested 64 parameter combinations for L1ABA module 1, 576 parameter combinations for L1ABA Module 2 analyzing L1HS with aligned reads, and 96 parameter combinations for L1ABA Module 2 analyzing L1HS without aligned reads (Supplementary Table S3). To identify the optimal parameter combination(s) for each module, we calculated F1 scores by comparing the L1ABA pipeline output against manual curation results. We chose F1 score as our primary metric for both modules because it balances sensitivity (detecting true positives) and precision (minimizing false positives) while accounting for true negatives [98]. In addition to the F1 score, for Module 2 we evaluated specificity to assess the ability to exclude false positives in cell lines with no detectable L1HS expression.

Using this approach, we found that multiple parameter combinations in L1ABA Module 1 achieved F1 scores exceeding 0.9 in each cell line. The optimal parameters identified for robust cross-cell-line performance were: 6000 bp upstream window for L1s in the antisense orientation relative to gene or intergenic regions, 10 000–20 000 bp upstream window for L1s in the sense orientation within genes, and upstream-to-L1 read ratio thresholds of 0.05–0.2 (Supplementary Fig. S3 and Supplementary Table S3). Similarly, in L1ABA Module 2, multiple parameter combinations achieved F1 scores and/or specificity above 0.85. The optimal parameters for cross-cell-line performance were: 1000–10 000 bp upstream window for L1s in antisense orientation or intergenic regions, 2000 bp upstream window for L1s in sense orientation within genes, and 1000–2000 bp downstream window for all L1HS loci. For parameters specific to L1HS with aligned reads, optimal thresholds were 0.2–0.3 for upstream-to-L1 read ratio and 1.0 for downstream-to-L1 read ratio. For parameters specific to L1HS without aligned reads, the optimal downstream read cutoff was 2 read pairs (Supplementary Fig. S4 and Supplementary Table S3). Based on these analyses, we selected the optimal values for parameters as defaults for all subsequent L1ABA analyses: for Module 1, a 6000 bp upstream distance for L1s in the antisense orientation or intergenic regions, 20 000 bp upstream for L1s in the sense orientation within genes, and upstream-to-L1 read ratio as 0.05; for Module 2, 6000 bp upstream distance for L1HS loci in the antisense orientation or intergenic regions, 2000 bp upstream distance for L1HS in the sense orientation with gene transcription, 1000 bp downstream distance, upstream-to-L1 read ratio as 0.2 and downstream-to-L1 read ratio as 1.0 for L1HS with aligned reads, and downstream reads threshold for 2 read pairs for L1HS with no aligned reads (Supplementary Table S3).

L1ABA benchmarking

To benchmark L1ABA performance in normal somatic cells with low L1 expression, we compared outputs from L1ABA and L1EM (most L1 biology-aware locus-specific bioinformatics tool) using stranded RNA-seq data from proliferating, early senescent, and late senescent primary fetal lung fibroblast LF-1 cells [36, 59]. Output of RT-qPCR (which does not distinguish authentic L1 mRNA from passive L1 transcripts) from the same cells was also used for comparison (Fig. 2A). Manual inspection of detected L1 loci in IGV shows that only 3 (1.3%) of the 224 L1 loci reported by RT-qPCR as expressed passed manual validation as authentic L1 transcripts (Fig. 2A, RT-qPCR, Supplementary Table S4), with the remaining loci representing passively transcribed L1 sequences as shown in Fig. 1A. This finding is consistent with the known limitations of RT-qPCR and previous reports that L1 mRNA is 10–100-fold less abundant than passively transcribed L1 sequences [54, 55]. Consistent with its reported limitation in detecting expressed L1 loci in cells with low L1 mRNA expression [59], L1EM identified 9 L1 loci with >100 aligned read pairs [Fig. 2, L1EM (100 reads cutoff)], which is the cutoff that L1EM developers established as providing accurate results, and met L1EM’s expression criteria (reads mapping to L1 mRNA or L1 mRNA 3′ read-through, excluding passive sense transcripts) (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Table S4). Four of these 9 L1s belong to older L1PA10–L1PA14 subfamilies, which typically contain many promoter mutations.

To enable fair comparison with L1ABA, we applied L1EM using the same minimum read threshold as L1ABA (≥2 read pairs per L1 locus and allow numbers of upstream reads up to 5% of total L1-aligned reads). With this threshold, L1EM detected 837 L1 loci, of which 29 (3.5%) were validated as transcribed from L1 promoters upon manual inspection (Fig. 1C, L1EM, Supplementary Table S4). This output, 837 L1 loci is comparable to the 949 L1 loci with aligned reads identified by RNA-seq alignment to hg38 that serves as input for L1ABA [Fig. 2A, Uncurated L1 (L1ABA input)]. L1ABA application removes 797 passive L1 loci from the input of 949 uncurated L1 loci and detects 152 L1 loci as expressed. Of these, 141 (92.8%) loci are validated as L1 mRNA transcripts as defined in Fig. 1A (Supplementary Tables S2 and S4). These findings establish that L1EM with 100-read cutoffs efficiently removes background L1 sequences but also excludes many expressed L1 loci. In comparison, L1ABA is sensitive to accurately detect expressed L1 loci in cells with low levels of L1 mRNA expression. This allows quantitative comparison of the number of expressed L1 loci and their expression levels, as well as assessment of the repertoire of expressed L1 loci in normal proliferating and senescent cells.

To evaluate L1ABA performance in detecting expressed annotated L1HS loci, we compared L1ABA output to the outputs from L1EM and the multi-omics approach that combines targeted WGS, ChIP-seq, and RNA-seq to identify expressed L1HS through 3′ read-through transcription [58, 59]. All three methods were applied to the same RNA-seq dataset from MCF-7 cells (Supplementary Table S5). Unannotated L1 loci detected by the multi-omics approach were excluded from this analysis because, by design, they cannot be detected by either L1ABA or L1EM, which analyze only annotated L1 loci. L1ABA identified the highest number of expressed L1HS (50 L1 loci), followed by L1EM detecting 29 L1HS loci and the multi-omics approach identifying 13 L1HS loci (Fig. 2B). This outcome is consistent with previous findings that the Bowtie1 RNA-seq alignment strategy is more robust than a 3′-readthrough approach like multi-omics in detecting expressed L1 loci [54]. Seven L1HS loci are detected by all three approaches (Fig. 2B). This set included the well-characterized L1HS element, L1-5219, present in the antisense orientation within the TTC28 gene [54, 58, 61, 89, 99]. In a pairwise comparison, L1ABA identified 8 of the 12 L1HS loci (67%) detected by the multi-omics approach and 16 of the 28 L1HS loci (57%) detected by L1EM (Fig. 2B) (Supplementary Table S5). L1ABA uniquely identified 33 additional L1HS loci not detected by either L1EM or the multi-omics approach, several of which showed clear evidence of 3′ read-through transcription on IGV, supporting the detection by L1ABA as expressed L1HS loci (Supplementary Fig. S5). The observed discrepancies among methods likely reflect differences in their design and alignment strategies. The multi-omics approach is specifically designed to detect both annotated and unannotated L1HS with 3′ read-through transcription, making it highly effective for identifying unannotated L1HS with transduction potential. However, this targeted approach does not capture expressed L1HS without read-through transcription, which can be detected by both L1ABA (21 L1HS loci) and L1EM (13 L1HS loci). Differences between L1ABA and L1EM likely arise from their distinct alignment strategies: L1ABA uses output of Bowtie1 with concordant unique alignment parameters to avoid multimapping, whereas L1EM realigns the unaligned reads using BWA, which permits multimapping.

These results demonstrate that L1ABA addresses a need in the field by enabling reliable, high-throughput analysis of L1 mRNA expression in cells with low and high L1 expression, which is critical for cancer versus normal tissues comparisons, while matching existing approaches in detecting expressed L1HS loci.

Changes in L1 expression levels and numbers of expressed L1 loci in senescent cells are modest and cell-type dependent

Having established that previously reported analysis of L1 expression in senescent cells using RT-qPCR did not reflect authentic L1 mRNA expression, we used L1ABA to analyze L1 expression in proliferating and senescent WI-38 lung fibroblasts, LF-1 lung fibroblasts, CD8+ T cells, and TIS MCF7 cells [36, 55, 82–84]. Specifically, senescent WI-38 and LF-1 cells were established through serial passaging leading to replicative senescence [36, 82]. Non-senescent and senescent CD8+ T cells were isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of young donors (20s) and old donors (60s) and sorted into low Sa-β-gal (non-senescent) and high Sa-β-gal (senescent) pools [83]. MCF-7 cells were treated with palbociclib (CDK4/6 inhibitor), cisplatin (DNA-damaging agent), and doxorubicin (DNA-damaging agent) to induce senescence (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table S6) [84].

Our analysis demonstrated that changes in the number of expressed L1 loci and total L1 expression levels are cell-type dependent (Fig. 3B). Among the cell types examined, only replication-induced senescent LF-1 cells show statistically significant increased L1 mRNA expression. Specifically, late senescent LF-1 cells exhibit increased total L1 expression levels compared to proliferating cells (Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparison tests, P < .05) (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table S6). A similar trend is observed in the number of expressed L1 loci in these cells, which did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 3B). Replication-induced senescent WI-38 fibroblasts and TIS MCF-7 cells show no significant increases in either the number of expressed L1 loci or total L1 expression levels (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table S6). Similarly, there are no significant changes in L1 mRNA expression in radiation-induced and density-induced senescent WI-38 cells (Supplementary Fig. S6 and Supplementary Table S6).

For human CD8+ T cells, there is a significant interaction between donor age and senescence status for both the number of expressed L1 loci (non-parametric ART ANOVA, F(1, 8) = 6.76, P = .03) and L1 expression levels [non-parametric ART ANOVA F(1, 8) = 5.41, P = .048], indicating that the relationship between L1 expression and senescence differs by donor age (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table S6). In young donors, senescent cells (SA-β-gal-high) trended to have 2–3 times more expressed L1 loci compared to non-senescent cells (SA-β-gal-low), though this comparison did not reach statistical significance after correction for multiple comparisons (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table S6). Senescent and non-senescent cells from old donors expressed similar numbers of L1 loci, which were comparable to those observed in senescent cells from young donors (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table S6). There are no significant changes in L1 expression levels between non-senescent and senescent cells in both young and old donors (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table S6).

Our analysis across both in vitro and ex vivo systems, including both cancer and primary cells, demonstrates that L1 upregulation in senescent cells, when present, is moderate and highly context-dependent rather than a universal phenomenon of senescence. (Fig. 3B). This finding contrasts with the previously reported “retrotransposon storm” in senescent cells [35, 36, 51, 100], where massive upregulation of L1 expression was described.

Distinct L1 subsets are expressed: core-, proliferation-, and senescence-specific L1 loci

Having characterized the overall number of expressed L1 loci and total L1 expression levels across different experimental models, we next examined which specific L1 loci are expressed as cells progress from proliferation to senescence, providing additional resolution of L1 expression patterns in WI-38, LF-1, CD8+ T cells, and TIS MCF-7 cells across all passages, ages, or different treatments, respectively (Fig. 4A). Using a binomial readout of L1 expression status (expressed or not expressed) and considering the experimental designs for induction of the senescent state in each cell line, we identified distinct groups of expressed L1 loci (Fig. 4A, see “Materials and methods” section for detailed description of inclusion parameters).

L1ABA identified a total of 639 L1 loci expressed in CD8+ T cells across samples collected from young and old donors (Fig. 4A CD8+ T cells, Supplementary Table S7). Out of these 639 L1 loci 15 (2.3%) loci are identified as CS L1 loci, being expressed in all four cell groups. Only one (0.1%) L1 locus is identified as PS L1, which is uniquely expressed in SA-β-gal low non-senescent cells collected from young and old donors, potentially reflecting mixed male female composition of unrelated individuals in the two groups. Five (0.8%) L1 loci are identified as SS L1, which are expressed in the SA-β-gal high senescent cells collected from young and old donors. L1ABA identified a total of 941 L1 loci expressed in MCF7 cells across four groups (control and TIS MCF-7) (Fig. 4A MCF-7, Supplementary Table S7). Having established the presence of CS, PS, and SS L1 loci in all analyzed cell types, we next examined their expression levels as cells progress to senescence. In WI-38 cells, CS L1 expression levels remain unchanged across passages and PS L1 are exclusively expressed in proliferating passages (PDL 20 and 25) (Supplementary Table S9). SS L1 expression is significantly higher in late senescent passages (PDL 52 and 53) compared to proliferative passages (PDL 20), as expected given their senescence-specific nature (Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparison tests, P < .05) (Supplementary Table S9). The total L1 mRNA expression levels of the remaining non-specific 221 L1 (Fig. 4A, gray) loci that are not categorized into any of these three subsets (CS, PS, or SS) remained unchanged across all passages (Supplementary Table S9). The upregulation of the 15 SS L1 loci did not detectably shift overall L1 expression in WI-38 cells, which remains unchanged during senescence (Fig. 3B). In contrast to WI-38 cells, CS L1 expression levels increased significantly as LF-1 cells progressed from proliferation to late senescence (Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparison tests, P < .05) (Supplementary Table S9). SS L1 expression was also significantly higher in senescent passages, consistent with their senescence-specific expression (Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparison tests, P < .05) (Supplementary Table S9). L1 expression levels of non-specific L1 loci (N = 104) remained unchanged across all passages. The significant upregulation of both CS L1 and SS L1 subsets in LF-1 cells contributes to the overall increase in total L1 expression observed in senescent LF-1 cells (Fig. 3B). In CD8+ T cells, we observed comparable CS L1 expression levels among four groups (Supplementary Table S9). PS and SS L1 expression levels are significantly higher in non-senescent and senescent cell groups, respectively (Supplementary Table S9). Unlike the cultured normal cells undergoing replicative senescence where there are specific L1 subsets showing significant expression changes, expression of CS and uncategorized L1 subsets in TIS MCF-7 cells remain unchanged between control and treatment groups (Supplementary Table S9).

The identification of CS, PS, and SS L1 subsets demonstrates that as cells reach senescence, they express distinct, cellular state-dependent subsets of L1 loci. Locus-level resolution also reveals changes in expression levels of CS and SS L1 subsets that are masked when examining total L1 expression. Together, these results establish that although changes in total L1 expression or in individual L1 subsets are cell-type-dependent, the presence of distinct L1 expression subsets is a consistent feature across all cell types examined.

Core and senescence L1 loci are cell-type- and stimuli-specific

Previous studies demonstrated that L1 expression exhibits strict organ-specific patterns in mice and rats and that different cell types from mouse or human testes express different subsets of L1s [50, 101]. We therefore tested whether core-, proliferation-, and senescence-specific L1s are cell-type and stimulus-specific. Comparison of the identified CS L1 loci across different cell types (WI-38, LF-1, CD8+ T cells, and MCF-7) shows minimal overlap between any two groups with each group having a unique subset of expressed L1 loci (Fig. 5A). Not a single L1 locus was shared by all groups, while all groups shared at least one expressed L1 locus with MCF7 cells (Fig. 5A), likely due to a large number of expressed L1 loci (282) in MCF7 cells. Similarly, SS L1 loci showed strict cell type specificity with no shared L1 loci among cell types of different origins (Fig. 5B, MCF7, CD8+ T cells, and lung fibroblasts). We observed 2 L1 loci shared between senescent WI-38 and LF-1 cells, consistent with their origin from human fetal lung [102]. These results establish that CS and SS L1 subsets are cell-type specific, consistent with previous findings about cell type specificity of L1 expression in mouse and human testis [50, 101].

A comparison of CS and SS L1 loci in cells driven to senescence by different stimuli shows that the majority of CS L1 loci identified in radiation-induced (10 of 12; 83%) and density-induced senescent WI-38 cells (7 of 9; 78%) overlapped with CS L1 loci detected in WI-38 cells undergoing replicative senescence (Supplementary Fig. S7). This overlap confirms the same cell line identity and the same cell culture system. There are four CS L1 loci that are shared among CS L1 of replicative senescent, radiation-induced, and cell density-induced senescent WI-38, suggesting that these four loci are core expressed L1 loci of WI-38 regardless of cellular state or senescent stimuli (Supplementary Fig. S7). In contrast to CS L1s, there is a minimal overlap among SS L1 identified in replication-, radiation-, and cell density-induced senescent WI-38, with only density-induced senescent WI-38 cells sharing 1 expressed SS L1 locus with each of the other two groups (Fig. 5D). Similarly, there is no overlap in expression of SS L1 loci among senescent MCF7 cells induced by different therapeutic treatments (Fig. 5C).

Overall, these results demonstrate that cell type specific expression of CS L1 loci reflects cell identity. SS L1 loci provide information about both cell identity and senescence-inducing stimuli. These findings demonstrate that L1 expression profiles retain memory of cellular origin and, in addition to being specific to senescent state, they can inform about senescence-inducing stimulus that triggered their state.

Changes in expression of core-, proliferation-, and senescence-specific L1s are independent of gene expression

Significant transcriptional changes between proliferating and senescent cells are reported [103–105]. Therefore, we examined a relationship between expression of core-, proliferation-, and senescence-specific L1 loci and their overlapping genes (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table S10).

L1 expression is independent of their encompassing gene expression in WI-38, CD8+ T cells, and MCF-7. For each cell type, left heatmap represents gene expression, and the right heatmap represents L1 expression across passages or experimental groups. Each row in the left heatmap represents a specific gene, each row in the right heatmap corresponds to an L1 locus located within that gene. Gene expression levels remain stable across passages and experimental groups in all three cell types, while L1 loci exhibit locus-specific expression. Similar result was observed in LF-1, see Supplementary Fig. S8.

In WI-38 cells, 14 of 24 CS L1 loci are located within genes, and only 1 of these 14 CS L1 loci showed significant correlation with its host gene (Pearson’s correlation, r = 0.5, adjusted P < .05), while all other L1/gene pairs showed no correlation. Two of 5 PS L1 loci and 5 of 15 SS L1 loci are located within genes. No significant correlations were observed between PS or SS L1 loci and their corresponding host genes (Pearson’s correlation, adjusted P > .05) (Supplementary Table S10). Similar to WI-38, no significant correlations were observed in LF-1 cells for any L1/gene subset among CS, PS, and SS L1 loci (Pearson’s correlation, adjusted P > .05) (Supplementary Fig. S8 and Supplementary Table S10).

In CD8+ T cells, 10 of 15 CS L1 loci are located within genes, and no correlations were observed between L1 and host gene expression. There were also no correlations for any PS or SS L1/gene pairs. For GS L1, while GS L1 loci are exclusively expressed in one group, their host genes are either expressed across all groups or not expressed at all, and no correlation was observed (Spearman’s correlation, adjusted P > .05) (Fig. 6, CD8+ T cells GS L1 panel). Similarly, in MCF-7 cells, no significant correlations were observed for CS, PS, SS, or GS L1/gene pairs (Spearman’s correlation, adjusted P > .05) (Supplementary Table S10).

Our findings show that consistently across different cell types and models, the expression of CS, PS, and SS L1 loci is independent of the expression status of their encompassing genes. Consequently, this independence, combined with the cell-type, stimulus-, and cellular state-specific patterns of SS L1 locus expression, shows that locus-level L1 expression profiles reflect cellular identity, senescent status, and the senescence-inducing stimulus.

Changes in global and L1-specific splicing in senescent cells are cell type specific

Previous studies established presence of several functional and many putative splice sites in L1 sequence [10, 11, 75, 106]. To determine which L1 splice sites are used in normal cells and whether their use may change as cells become senescent, we utilized STAR alignment to hg38 genome assembly to identify splice junctions and rMATS to identify splicing events that are different between proliferating and senescent cells (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Table S11). These approaches determine that the proportions of skipped exons, alternative 5ʹ splice sites, alternative 3ʹ splice sites, mutually exclusive exons, and intron retention differ significantly between proliferating and senescent cells across all cell types (Chi-square test, WI-38 PDL 20 versus PDL 53, LF-1 proliferating versus late senescent cells, non-senescent, SAβGal low versus senescent, SAβGal high CD8+ T cells in both young and old donors, control versus TIS MCF-7, P < .001) (Fig. 7B and Supplementary Fig. S9). Among these events, skipped exons and intron retention contribute most substantially to the observed differences (Fig. 7A and B).

(A) Illustration of the two main splicing events, exon skipping and intron retention, that show the most notable changes between non-senescent and senescent cells. (B) Relative proportions of differential splicing events differ significantly between proliferating and senescent cells across all cell types, particularly in exon skipping and intron retention, as determined by a Chi-square test (P < .05). (C) Diagram of splice donor and acceptor positions in the L1.3 sequence showing splice sites used in CD8+ T cells. Sites used across all experimental groups are in black. Sites used only in senescent cells are in orange. (D) Categorical heatmap of splice junctions detected in CD8+ T cells within L1.3 across various experimental groups. Each row represents a splice junction detected, labeled with splice donor and splice acceptor sites positions in the L1.3 DNA sequence. A purple cell indicates usage of the splice junction, while a white cell indicates lack of use.

The changes in the proportion of these events are cell type specific (Fig. 7B and Supplementary Fig. S9). Late senescent LF-1 and palbociclib-induced senescent MCF-7 cells exhibit a decrease in skipped exon events (higher exon inclusion) and an increase in intron retention events (Fig. 7A). In contrast, WI-38 and SA-β-gal-high CD8+ T cells from young donors exhibit an increase in skipped exon events (fewer exon inclusions) and a decrease in intron retention events (Fig. 7A and B). No significant differences in the proportions of splicing events are observed between senescent, SAβGal high CD8+ T cells from young and old donors, (Chi-square test, SAβGal low-young donors versus SAβGal low-old donors, χ2 = 8.83, P = .06). However, significant differences are observed between non-senescent CD8+ T cells derived from young and old donors (Chi-square test, P < .0001) (Supplementary Fig. S9), which aligns with previous findings that the complexity and dysregulation in alternative splicing increases with age [45, 80, 107–110].

To determine whether L1 splice sites contribute to splicing in proliferating and senescent cells, we first identified chimeric splice junctions involving L1 sequences by intersecting splice donors and splice acceptors coordinates of STAR-derived splice junctions with full-length and truncated L1 loci annotated in hg38 (Supplementary Table S11). No significant difference in the normalized number of splice junctions involving full-length L1 were observed between proliferating and senescent WI-38, LF-1, or CD8+ T cells. However, cisplatin-treated MCF-7 cells exhibited more full-length L1-associated splice junctions than doxorubicin-treated MCF-7 cells (Supplementary Fig. S10). Notably, very few (0–8) of splice junctions involved expressed full-length L1s in WI-38, LF-1, and CD8+ T cells, and none were in the same transcriptional orientation as their corresponding L1 loci (Supplementary Table S11). In contrast, in MCF7 cells, ~12% of splice junctions involved a sense strand sequence of a total of 10 expressed L1s (which is ~0.8% of total expressed L1 loci in MCF-7) (Supplementary Table S11). Proliferating LF-1 cells showed more splice junctions involving truncated L1 loci than senescent LF-1 cells, while WI-38, CD8+ T cells, and MCF-7 cells showed no significant differences in the use of L1 splice sites.

To identify splicing events with splice donor and acceptor sites falling within L1 sequence, RNA-seq reads were aligned to the L1.3 consensus sequence using STAR. This approach identified 570 unique L1 splice junctions across all cell types (Supplementary Table S11). Of these, 194 (34%) splice junctions involved sense strand L1 sequence, indicating that they can be mostly used during cellular gene or L1 transcription, if L1 is expressed. The remaining 370 (66%) splice junctions are in the L1 antisense strand, and thus are expected to be used during passive transcription of L1 sequences. Among the sense-strand junctions, 31 splice junctions produce L1 mRNA splice variants lacking some 5ʹUTR and ORF1 sequences but retaining ORF2 sequence (Supplementary Table S11). Although there are no significant differences in the number of sense- or antisense-strand L1 splice junctions used by senescent compared to non-senescent WI-38, LF-1, CD8+ T cells, and MCF-7 (Supplementary Fig. S11), different splice junctions are used in each cellular state (Fig. 7C and D, Supplementary Fig. S12, and Supplementary Table S11). Chi-square analyses determine that WI-38, LF-1, and CD8+ T senescent cells utilize different sets of splice junctions compared to their corresponding non-senescent cells (Chi-square test results, P < .05) (Fig. 7D and Supplementary Table S7). In contrast, control MCF-7 cells have greater splice junction diversity compared to palbociclib-, cisplatin-, and doxorubicin-induced senescent MCF-7 cells (Chi-square test, P < .05) (Supplementary Fig. S12 and Supplementary Table S11).

Discussion

The development of L1ABA advances high-throughput, locus-specific analysis of endogenous L1 mRNA expression in normal cells. While statistical approaches like L1EM [59] perform well in cancer samples with high L1 expression, their accuracy requires substantial read coverage, limiting performance in normal and pre-cancerous cells with low L1 read counts. L1ABA utilizes a pattern-based framework rooted in directional transcriptional signatures consistent with L1 5′ UTR-driven expression, enabling identification of expressed L1 loci at low read depth (Figs 1 and 2). Although neither L1ABA nor L1EM is designed to detect expression of non-reference L1HS loci, some of which are detectable by the multi-omics approach [58], L1ABA provides a faster, lower-cost, and scalable alternative for high-throughput studies of L1 mRNA expression under physiological conditions, during aging, disease states, or cellular stress. It is worth noting that the Bowtie1 RNA-seq alignment used to generate input files for L1ABA does not allow gapped alignments, meaning L1 exonization events (L1 sequences incorporated into gene transcripts via splicing) could theoretically be misidentified as expressed L1 loci. However, no such false positives were observed in normal WI-38, LF-1, and CD8+ T cells (Figs 3B and 4A), where nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) likely eliminates aberrant chimeric transcripts [111–113]. In cancer cells the NMD pathway is often deregulated [114]. Splice junction analysis revealed that only 0.8% (range: 0%–2.4%) of expressed L1 loci in MCF-7 cells, corresponding to 0–10 of ∼350 expressed loci, are involved in exonization (Figs 3B and 4A), indicating minimal impact on detection accuracy. Furthermore, as aberrant splicing and L1 expression are not mutually exclusive events, these findings indicate that this source of false-positive calls is expected to contribute minimally to the overall detection accuracy of L1ABA. Collectively these features make L1ABA a valuable tool to study L1 mRNA across diverse tissue types, conditions, and mammalian species, as it can be readily adapted to any species with genome annotation of L1 loci. The L1ABA conceptual workflow can also be applied to long-read sequencing data, extending its versatility.

In contrast to reports of massive L1 upregulation in senescent cells, our analyses using L1ABA demonstrate that senescence is not necessarily accompanied by increased L1 mRNA expression (Fig. 3) [36]. Instead, L1 expression changes are context-dependent, varying with cell type and senescence-inducing stimuli. A major reason for the discrepancy between our and previous findings is the use of L1-biology-aware bioinformatics approach instead of RT-qPCR, which accurately detects only 1.2% of expressed L1 loci (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Table S4). The inadequacy of RT-qPCR for detection of L1 expression was first established by Skowronski and Singer [63]. Single-gene-focused bioinformatics pipelines suffer from the same technical issue, which is overcome by custom bioinformatics pipelines measuring L1 mRNA expression [54, 55, 58]. Pabis et al. further showed that the increase in L1 signal detected by RT-qPCR in senescent cells likely reflects a rise in intron retention and transcriptional readthrough of genes adjacent to L1 [56], which we observe in our differential splicing analysis (Fig. 7B). Despite these findings, the recurring use of RT-qPCR continues to produce results which do not reflect L1 biology [35, 36, 51, 100, 115–118]. By accurately identifying expressed L1 loci, L1ABA expands our understanding of both numbers of expressed L1 and L1 expression levels in senescent cells, revealing previously obscured cell-type- and stimulus-specific changes. For L1 mRNA expression levels analyses, we verified that there was no library composition bias, allowing us to use TPM for cross-sample comparisons. However, TPM use has known limitations, and in cases where composition bias is present, alternative normalization methods such as VST or TMM should be used [90, 119, 120]. We recommend that future studies applying L1ABA or other pipelines assess library composition before selecting a normalization approach to analyze their output.

Application of L1ABA identified three major groups of expressed L1 loci core-, proliferation-, and senescence-specific L1 loci in all cell types examined in this study. While this pattern is uniform across all models tested in this study, the L1 loci belonging to each of these groups vary by cell type and stimulus and are expressed independent of their corresponding genes (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S6). The latter strengthens previous findings showing that although expressed L1 loci are often located within expressed genes, L1 expression levels do not correlate with overall gene expression (Fig. 5) [50, 99]. The cell and stimulus specificity points that expressed L1 loci independently inform about cellular identity, senescent status, and the nature of senescence-inducing stress (Figs 4–6 and Supplementary Fig. S7 and S8). This aligns with previous findings showing that L1 expression varies by organ, sex, and cell type in both mice and humans [50, 101]. Given the lack of universal senescence markers [121], senescence-specific L1s can be used as an additional variable to identify senescent cells, senescent subtypes, and triggers. Additionally, L1 expression could reveal cellular origins of senescent cells, enhancing our understanding of tissue-specific senescence in aging and disease and potentially guiding development of targeted senotherapeutics. Established epigenetic requirements for L1 expression [89, 99] suggest that proliferation- and senescence-specific L1 expression may result from significant epigenetic changes that occur in senescent cells, including DNA hypomethylation, loss of heterochromatin, and alterations in histone marks [121, 122]. These changes likely create a permissive environment for L1 expression within certain chromatin domains. Similarly, the cell type- and stimulus-specificity of L1 mRNA expression may be driven by distinct epigenetic and transcriptional changes of specific cell-type and senescence stimuli [104, 105, 123, 124]. Thus, L1 specificity is consistent with broader, context-dependent epigenetic and regulatory changes rather than mirroring expression of overlapping host genes [104, 105]

While increases in the number of expressed L1 loci or L1 expression levels were not universal across cell types in our study, all cell types exhibited a shift in which L1 loci were expressed as cells became senescent (Figs 3 and 4). This qualitative change can be functionally significant, as individual L1 loci differ in their retrotransposition potential and capacity to affect gene expression through splicing or epigenetic regulation [10, 89, 125]. For instance, the upward trend in expressed L1HS loci in CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table S8) may be particularly relevant because L1HS is the only retrotransposition-competent subfamily, and increased L1HS expression could elevate retrotransposition, DNA damage, and inflammatory responses associated with L1 EN and RT [6, 8, 33, 34, 36, 37]. While older subfamilies are retrotranspositionally inactive, expression of previously silent L1 loci of older subfamilies with locus-specific polymorphisms or alternative open reading frames [73, 77, 97, 126, 127] may produce neoantigens, potentially aiding immune elimination of senescent cells in both normal and tumor environments [128].

Neoantigen production can also arise from alternatively spliced L1 mRNA or L1-cellular chimeric transcripts [11, 74, 77, 126, 127]. Senescent cells showed notable shifts in the usage of specific L1 splice donor and acceptor sites (Fig. 7C and D; Supplementary Fig. S12). Except for MCF-7, senescent cells exhibited greater L1 splice site diversity than proliferating cells (Supplementary Fig. S12 and Supplementary Table S11), a pattern also observed in non-senescent, low SAβGal CD8+ T cells from older donors (Fig. 7D and Supplementary Table S11), indicating age-related changes in L1’s contribution to transcriptome diversity. We identified 570 L1 splice junctions across all samples, with only 15 (2.6%) shared among WI-38, LF-1, CD8+ T cells, and MCF-7 (Supplementary Table S11), creating the most comprehensive library of functional L1 splice sites used in proliferating and senescent cells [10, 11, 75, 106]. Consistent with their genomic abundance, truncated L1 sequences contribute ∼10-fold more splice junctions than full-length L1 loci (Supplementary Fig. S10) [3]. Notably, most chimeric splice variants or those occurring within L1 are likely unstable, suggesting L1’s contribution to splicing diversity is underestimated in these cells and could influence gene expression in senescent or aging cells.

While overexpression of exogenous L1 has been linked to senescence in normal and cancer cells [33], use of L1ABA demonstrates that endogenous L1 mRNA expression does not uniformly increase as cells become senescent. Instead, we identified senescence-specific L1 loci that are distinct to each cell type and senescence-inducing stimulus. These changes occur independent of host gene expression, establishing L1 expression patterns as informative indicators of cellular origin and senescence trigger. The cell-type and stimulus-specificity of senescence-specific L1 loci could enhance precision in characterizing senescent cells and retrospectively identifying senescence-inducing stimuli. Thus, independent of whether or not endogenously expressed L1 elements cause cellular senescence, our findings position L1 as a readout for identifying senescent cells. We also created the first comprehensive atlas of L1 splice sites utilized in proliferating and senescent cells, uncovering numerous previously unknown junctions that expand understanding of L1’s contribution to transcriptomic diversity. This resource provides a foundation for exploring functional roles of L1 splice variants in senescence and their potential as therapeutic targets for eliminating senescent cells.
