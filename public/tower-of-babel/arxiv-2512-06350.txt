Why They Disagree: Decoding Differences in Opinions about AI Risk
Özgecan Koçak, Phanish Puranam, Nghi Truong

Abstract
Identifying the reasons for disagreements between influential points of view on issues that affect the public can help produce informed policy responses, even if they do not bring disagreeing parties closer to agreement. We present a methodology for extracting reasoning chains — the sequences of premises that motivate or justify opinions — from natural discourse, and for characterizing the types of premises (facts, forecasts, definitions, causal beliefs, and evaluations) that make up these chains. We demonstrate the utility of this approach for two practical goals: diagnosing specific points of contention and aggregating arguments across speakers. We illustrate the methodology through an analysis of the debates on the nature of risks that AI poses to the public, using a corpus of interviews from the Lex Fridman podcast. We find that differences in perspectives among the podcast’s guests on existential risk and employment risk from AI arise primarily from differences in causal premises and forecasts, whereas in the case of AI’s effects on human social relationships, premises regarding what is valued and definitions about what counts as genuine human connection play a distinctively larger role. Our approach to analyzing reasoning chains at scale, using an ensemble of LLMs to parse textual data, can be applied to facilitate deliberation and aggregation of opinions on any topic.



Significance.
Public debates about AI risk are loud but hard to interpret: opposing experts seem to talk past each other, and it’s unclear whether they disagree about facts, forecasts, mechanisms, terminology, or values. We introduce a method using an ensemble of large language models to extract the reasoning behind expressed opinions from natural discourse at scale. Applying it to hundreds of expert conversations about AI risk, we find opposing sides rarely divide over current facts. Instead, debates diverge on forecasts and causal mechanisms—and, distinctively in the debate over AI companionship, on definitions and values. Identifying which premises a debate rests on shows where new evidence and discussion can help—for observers and policymakers, at least, if not the proponents themselves.

In this paper, we introduce a methodology to analyze a large corpus of naturally occurring text containing a diversity of expressed opinions that (i) decomposes expressed opinions (including content left implicit) into elements of reasoning and diagnoses root causes for divergent opinions, identifying whether these originate in definitions, evidentiary statements of facts and forecasts, causal beliefs, or values, and (ii) compresses the many arguments on each side of a debate into a compact map of the mechanisms that side relies on. Our method is issue-neutral, scalable, and replicable, and can therefore inform observers and guide deliberation and decision making by policy makers across different domains.

We do this through two innovations. First, we introduce a “grammar of premises": we conceptualize an expressed opinion as the conclusion of a (possibly branching) chain built from five kinds of premises linked by three operators. The five premise types are refinements of the kinds of codes on which a reasoning chain can differ
[
16
]

Factual-current and forecast premises are one code in two tenses; both relate to beliefs about states of the world, while causal premises concern transitions between states and evaluative premises supply the standard against which states are judged. The three operators link them: implication (
\Rightarrow

Under assumptions of intended coherence (not necessarily honesty) of the speaker, we can also identify enthymemes (i.e., arguments in which crucial premises have been left implicit). This framework helps us to describe and compare differences in expressed reasoning chains to identify why opinions diverge in terms of a set of mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive factors, extending prior theoretical work on argumentation

We apply this framework to the optimistic and pessimistic positions on AI risk expressed by leading proponents and critics of AI on the Lex Fridman podcast (https://lexfridman.com/podcast/). Focusing on three topics—existential risk, labor-market disruption, and risks to human social relationships—we aggregate the main arguments on each side, identify where they diverge, and surface the underlying question each debate turns on. Our application is descriptive. We do not evaluate the soundness of premises or the accuracy of a reasoning chain, we make no claims about why speakers make the arguments they do, and we analyze expressed reasoning, which need not coincide with the private reasoning that produced an opinion.

Prior work has sought to understand AI-risk disagreement by bringing together opposing experts in intensive adversarial collaboration
[
54
]
. Both our analysis and theirs suggest that disagreements about AI risk are not primarily moral conflicts but differences in causal models, forecasts, and underlying conceptualizations of intelligence and control. Their effort required months of structured interaction among small groups of experts and still yielded little convergence. We show that the dominant contours of expert disagreement—primarily causal and predictive rather than moral—can be recovered algorithmically from naturally occurring public discourse at scale, without requiring access to the disputants themselves. In this sense, PEEL provides the bystander with much of the same map of the debate that adversarial collaboration painstakingly constructs, while addressing a fundamentally different problem: understanding by observers rather than reconciliation among principals.

Results

Structure of Reasoning Chains.
Each reasoning chain consists of a conclusion, a set of supporting premises, and the logical relationships connecting them. Of the 1,943 conclusions in the corpus, most are supported by a single chain; only 118 (6.1%) draw on more than one. For most conclusions (76.0%), the supporting argument contains at least one implicit premise and thus constitute enthymemes; we extract these premises under the coherence assumption rather than leave these chains out of the analysis (Materials and Methods). Chains typically contain 4 to 7 premises (median 5, mean 5.1), with a long tail reaching 15; the full distribution by premise type is in SI Appendix, Fig. S1. We validated the extracted chains for a sample of thirty speakers with two human coders (as described in SI Appendix S6).

Attitude is significantly associated with reasoning structure. We find that pessimistic conclusions are supported by more premises than optimistic or neutral ones (5.85 vs. 5.47 and 5.33; Kruskal–Wallis H = 38.6, P < 0.001), a difference that survives controls for topic, profession, gender, and time (+0.47 premises vs. neutral, P < 0.001). Optimists and pessimists might be expected to base their arguments on different foundations, with optimists relying on forecasts and pessimists on values. However we find that within a topic they do not: once topic is controlled for, the two sides draw on a similar mix of premise types.

Comparing Arguments of

Individual Experts.
Our framework allows us to compare reasoning chains of any two speakers who addressed the same topic, including dyads who never interacted. This does not necessarily reflect what they would say in an actual exchange, where clarification and concession can dissolve some divergences and open others. However, applying our grammar reveals sources of disagreement, the specific premises on which their stated reasoning is logically incompatible. This lets us map a debate across speakers who were never in a room together, at scale and without their participation, complementing adversarial collaboration, which requires the disputants themselves.

For each pair of chains we compare, the ensemble of LLMs identifies divergences: incompatible claims about the same proposition, one from each chain, represented by either a single premise or an explicitly encoded combination (+) or implication (
\Rightarrow
) of premises. It classifies each divergence as factual, forecast, causal, definitional, or evaluative, and as either root or derivative. We define a root divergence as being independent of the others, in contrast to a derivative divergence that would disappear if its root were resolved.

Table 2 presents one comparison per debate (panels A–C). Additional pairs and the full reasoning chains are in SI Appendix, Tables S1–S6. LeCun and Yampolskiy (X-risk) both treat human extinction as unacceptable and agree on the current state of the technology; their disagreement reduces to forecast and causal questions (e.g. how fast capability arrives and whether failures are recoverable (D1, D3)). Kuyda and Huberman (S-risk) are the mirror image: Huberman does not contest Kuyda’s measured reductions in loneliness. He contests whether outcomes reached with an artificial partner count as the good being measured. All of their divergences are rooted in evaluative codes, one of them left implicit by the speaker and recovered by PEEL. The Altman–Dalio pair (E-risk) decomposes a single disagreement into its belief and value components. The two agree that AI will eliminate jobs. What divides them has two causal roots (will gains concentrate; will displacement self-correct without policy) and two evaluative roots (is a transition desirable against the costs of stability; does an on-average-beneficial transition justify policy action). Presumably, the causal divergences can be resolved through evidence whereas the value-based divergence will require negotiation.

Among the 209 conclusions on X-risk, 87 are optimistic, 106 are pessimistic, and 16 are neutral; E-risk has 66 optimistic, 36 pessimistic, and 18 neutral conclusions, and S-risk 36, 29, and 33. For our disagreement analysis, we focus on pairs of reasoning chains that contrast optimistic and pessimistic conclusions. From the full optimistic

PEEL evaluates the remaining 9,182, 2,358, and 1,030 pairs and classifies 7,526 X-risk pairs (82.0%), 869 E-risk pairs (36.9%), and 265 S-risk pairs (25.7%) as exhibiting conclusion-level disagreement under our operational criteria (conclusions that cannot both be held by the same speaker, assessed through a counterfactual test; SI Appendix S8). These rates are themselves informative: nominally opposing conclusions contradict one another far more often in X-risk than in S-risk, where three-quarters of optimist–pessimist pairs prove not to be in direct conclusion-level disagreement at all—an early sign that the S-risk "sides" diverge over what is being claimed rather than over a shared proposition, consistent with the definitional pattern below.

A total of 19,499 divergences (12,761 roots) are identified and classified for X-risk, 1,581 for E-risk (1,187 roots), and 444 (361 roots) for S-risk. Table 2A applies this analysis to the chains in Table 1.

To locate where disagreement concentrates, we compare what the two sides disagree about with what they talk about: for each pair, we assume that divergences arise in proportion to the premise types available, so the expected share of a given divergence type equals that premise type’s share of all premises in the two chains (SI Appendix, Eq. S1). Our comparative claims—how X-, E-, and S-risk differ from one another—do not depend on the absolute baseline, and the baseline discriminates among debates rather than imposing a fixed ordering: S-risk departs from the causal-and-forecast signature that X- and E-risk share (below). Because pairs formed from the same conclusions are not independent, we assess uncertainty with a two-way cluster bootstrap that resamples optimistic and pessimistic conclusions (2,000 replicates; SI Appendix S9, Table S11). We report mean pair-level deviations, which match Fig. 1, with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals; pooled compositions and conventional per-pair tests are reported in SI Table S10. These analyses are computed over the pairs with at least one divergence: 7,477 of the 7,526 disagreeing X-risk pairs, 833 of 869 for E-risk, and 255 of 265 for S-risk. Summarizing

the Mechanisms Underlying Disagreement.
Having identified where individual pairs part ways, we turn to cataloguing all the causal arguments that support optimistic or pessimistic conclusions on the same topic. We focus on causal reasoning because causal divergences are overrepresented in all three debates. For each debate, we use the same ensemble, with identical prompts across the three debates, to group the reasoning chains on each side (optimistic and pessimistic separately) that rely on the same causal mechanism. We then manually align the two sides’ catalogs into the underlying questions of each debate based on our own understanding of the causal mechanisms. Fig. 2 shows each side’s main mechanism, as its causal path, for selected core questions of each debate; the full catalogs—38 and 32 mechanisms for X-risk, 25 and 13 for E-risk, and 21 and 14 for S-risk, organized under all thirteen underlying questions—are in SI Appendix, Tables S12–S14.

We can summarize each debate’s fork along a single axis each. In X-risk the axis is emergence versus design. The pessimistic catalog is dominated by loss of control: a misaligned superintelligence pursues goals with no place for humans (14 chains, 12 speakers); technology outpaces human oversight (11 chains); while the optimistic catalog answers with gradualism and design: no credible near-term path to superintelligence (10 chains, 10 speakers), and AI, being designed rather than evolved, lacks the drives that would motivate existential harm (6 chains). In E-risk the axis is continuity versus rupture. Optimists anchor on historical precedent: routine-task automation shifts work upward (18 chains, 17 speakers), new jobs have historically offset losses (10 chains), while pessimists split between who-gains stories (gains concentrate, widening inequality) and a sharper break: AI reaches cognition itself, so the historical compensation mechanism will not repeat. Discussion

Disagreements about societal risk are commonly explained through cultural priors and moral values: people diverge because they interpret evidence through different worldviews and moral foundations
[
5
,
7
,
10
]
. These accounts ask why people come to hold different beliefs. We ask a different question— where do opposing arguments part ways?—and find a distribution of divergences that these prior accounts would not predict. Across the three debates about AI risk that we analyze, factual divergences register far below the factual content of the arguments, causal divergences are consistently elevated, and evaluative divergences are never the single largest source of conflict. Among the expert and expert-adjacent speakers in this corpus, disagreement surfaces more over what will happen and how the world works than over specific factual claims or the desirability of outcomes.

This claim invites an immediate objection: speakers often leave values implicit, so a text-based analysis of conversational data might simply miss the value conflict that is really there, mistaking silence for agreement. We have two defenses against this objection. First, our pipeline does not discard implicit premises; it recovers them under the coherence assumption (finding 42.5% of evaluative premises to be implicit), so evaluations that speakers assume rather than state still enter the analysis and can register as divergences, as they do in the social-relationship debate. Second, an independent effort that brought opposing experts into direct, months-long adversarial collaboration, where unstated values have every opportunity to surface, reached the same conclusion: these disagreements are driven by causal models and forecasts, not moral conflict
[
54
]
.

Debates about issues that matter for public welfare can attain high visibility and volume without necessarily generating much insight because expressed opinions compress complex reasoning that is difficult to compare reliably. The value of our approach becomes clear when we compare the three debates. Pessimistic and optimistic positions on existential risk diverge mainly on causal mechanisms and forecasts about emergence and control. The debate on employment risk turns primarily on whether the historical pattern of technological job creation will continue. Disagreements regarding social-relationship risk concern whether AI companionship supports or replaces human relationships—a dispute that combines causal, definitional, and evaluative questions. These different structures suggest different paths toward resolution. Forecast disagreements can be updated as evidence accumulates; causal disagreements can be narrowed through research and critical tests; and definitional and evaluative disagreements require translation, reframing, or negotiation. The concern that AI debates may polarize and become politicized
[
3
]
can therefore be understood as a risk that disagreements now rooted mainly in forecasts and causal models may harden into conflicts of identity and value, closing paths toward resolution that remain open today.

When the reasoning on each side is aggregated, a central tension emerges in each debate about AI risk. In the existential-risk debate, the tension concerns Design versus Emergence: is AI an artifact whose behavior its makers can anticipate and control, or a process whose capabilities and failures exceed human foresight? In the employment debate, the tension is between Continuity versus Rupture: does the history of technological change remain a useful guide, or does AI’s reach into cognitive work break the pattern? In the social-relationship debate, the central question is about Augmentation versus Substitution: does artificial interaction add to human relationships, or replace them? The most divisive questions about the future of AI turn out to be timeless questions about ourselves: what we can foresee and control, what our history can teach us, the classic bounds of human rationality
[
55
,
56
]
and what we need from each other.

Our methodology, combining the grammar of premises and PEEL, can be used to analyze any debate. Public discourse is now recorded at a scale no human analyst can read, while the reasoning behind expressed opinions has traditionally been accessible only one argument at a time. A thoughtful reader can locate the origin of disagreement in a single pair of opposing arguments; an ensemble of language models makes it possible to do so across an entire debate and reveal patterns that no single comparison can show. PEEL makes the structure of disagreements visible, showing what must be examined, tested, defined, or reconsidered for opposing conclusions to move closer.

Materials and Methods

Corpus and extraction.
The corpus comprises episodes 1–493 of the Lex Fridman Podcast, released from April 2018 through March 2026. Official transcripts were downloaded from lexfridman.com; episodes without official transcripts were transcribed with TurboScribe/Whisper. Each transcript was split by guest, and each guest’s statements about the risks and impacts of AI were extracted and faithfully summarized. From each speaker summary, the ensemble extracts reasoning chains: conclusions, typed premises (marked explicit or implicit), and the relationships connecting them. A code-based structural validator checks every output and triggers a model repair loop if it detects grammatical errors, for example, if the LLMs propose premise types that do not fit our grammar. Separate processes verify each conclusion for relevance to AI-risk, classify into (one to three) topics, and assign a per-topic attitude (optimistic, neutral, pessimistic). These processes yield 1,943 conclusions from 364 episode-speaker combinations and 3,215 conclusion-topic assignments for this corpus.

The PEEL ensemble.
Every LLM task in the pipeline is executed as a hierarchical ensemble: two worker models—DeepSeek and Anthropic Claude—perform the task independently, and an integrator (GPT-4.1) evaluates both outputs against the source material and synthesizes the final result, making the final call at every step. Structured outputs are checked by programmatic validators that trigger model repair loops; the pairwise pipeline runs at temperature 0. Model versions and all prompts (verbatim) are given in SI Appendix and the deposited code.

Pairwise disagreement pipeline.
For each debate we form all optimistic
\times
pessimistic pairs of reasoning chains, excluding pairs in which both chains belong to the same person, including appearances in different episodes, leaving 9,182 (X-risk), 2,358 (E-risk), and 1,030 (S-risk) pairs. Step 1 classifies conclusion-level disagreement through a counterfactual incompatibility test; step 2 identifies divergences—pairs of units making incompatible claims about the same proposition—localizes each to the most specific premise or compound carrying the conflict, and types it; step 3 classifies each divergence as root or derivative through a simulation test (would this divergence dissolve if a root were reconciled?), allowing multiple roots per pair.

Statistical analysis.
For each pair we compare the observed composition of divergences with the expected composition if divergences arose in proportion to the local prevalence of premise types within that pair (SI Appendix, Eq. S1). Because pairs sharing a conclusion are not independent, headline inference uses a two-way cluster (dyadic) bootstrap that resamples optimistic and pessimistic conclusions (2,000 replicates; percentile 95% confidence intervals; SI Appendix, Table S11). Conventional per-pair tests are reported in SI Appendix, Table S10.

Mechanism aggregation.
Within each debate and side, the same ensemble discovers candidate causal mechanisms in chunks of reasoning chains and merges them into a catalog of mechanisms, each an ordered sequence of causal steps with the chains and speakers that support it. We then manually align the two sides’ catalogs into the underlying questions of each debate based on our own understanding of the causal mechanisms.

Reliability, validation, and data availability.
Ensemble agreement rates at every pipeline stage are reported in SI Appendix. Human validation of the extraction stage (two independent reviewers, 200 reasoning chains) found no hallucinated content, with no reservation from either reviewer on 196 of 200 reasoning chains. All prompts, the complete pipeline and analysis notebooks, per-pair outputs, and analysis datasets are deposited with the paper; podcast transcripts are not redistributed, and the collection notebook downloads the official transcripts and the remaining episodes’ audio from public sources.

S1. Data

The Lex Fridman podcast (https://lexfridman.com/podcast/) presents an excellent empirical context for examining disagreements about AI risk for several reasons. First, as one of the most prominent platforms for discussions about artificial intelligence, it features extensive dialogues with key figures representing diverse perspectives on AI development and risk, from AI safety researchers to leading technologists and technology entrepreneurs. Second, the long-form conversational format (typically 2-3 hours) allows for in-depth exploration of participants' reasoning, making lexical, causal, and evaluative codes more readily observable than in shorter media formats or written statements. Third, Fridman's interviewing approach, which combines technical expertise with philosophical inquiry, consistently probes both the technical foundations of his guests' beliefs (causal codes) and their value judgments (evaluative codes) and attempts disambiguation (arising from fuzzy lexical codes), providing rich data for our theoretical framework. Fourth, the temporal span of these conversations captures the evolution of the debate on AI risk through several significant technological developments. Finally, the semi-structured nature of these conversations, where similar themes are explored with different guests, facilitates systematic comparison across perspectives while maintaining the naturalistic character of the discourse, and with the anchor, Fridman himself, acting as a point of constancy.

We downloaded all episodes of the Lex Fridman Podcast with official transcripts directly from the website. For the remaining episodes, we downloaded the audio files and transcribed them using TurboScribe AI, an AI transcription service powered by the Whisper model, which reports an accuracy of 98%. The corpus covers episodes 1–493 except two episodes (84 and 100) that were unavailable.

S2. The ensemble

We array three LLMs in the structure of a hierarchical team with two parallel, independently operating workers, whose outputs are evaluated and synthesized by a third LLM in the role of integrator. For the results reported in the paper, the workers are DeepSeek (deepseek-chat; deepseek-v4-flash for the pairwise and aggregation stages) and Anthropic Claude (claude-sonnet-4-6; claude-haiku-4-5 for speaker summaries), and the integrator is GPT-4.1; the pairwise pipeline runs at temperature 0. All prompts are deposited verbatim with the code. We use GPT-4.1 as the integrator because it has a longer context window. The final result can be the same as both workers, the same as only one worker, or entirely different from both; the distribution of agreement outcomes at every stage is reported in the reliability table at the end of this document.

We developed the pipeline’s prompts through many cycles of testing. In each cycle, we ran a stage of the pipeline on a sample of inputs, read the cases where the two workers’ outputs differed, asked why, and checked whether the integrator’s judgment resolved the difference correctly. Where a recurring difference or error traced back to the instructions, we rewrote the prompts and tested again. Our prompts, therefore, are tuned to the models we used and to our corpus through this validation-guided iteration. What transfers is the framework: the grammar of premises and PEEL’s architecture — independent workers, an integrator, the counterfactual test, and opt-out options. Applying PEEL to other models, corpora, or debates requires the same iteration; we deposit the prompts, code, and stage-level reliability statistics as its starting point.

S3. Preparing the corpus for analysis: Summarization

The objective of the summarization step was to extract only the AI-relevant content in a clear and faithful way. This step reduces textual length and filters out noise commonly found in long-form conversations—such as back-and-forth dialogue, digressions and fragmented delivery—without altering the speaker's tone or argumentative flow. Summarization involves two sub-steps.

First, we used the two worker models (DeepSeek and Anthropic Claude; claude-haiku-4-5 for this stage) to summarize each speaker’s views related to AI’s risks, concerns and impacts on humans and society, making sure to exclude Lex Fridman’s side of the conversation and focusing on a single speaker in episodes that featured multiple guests. In constructing summaries, LLMs were instructed to faithfully preserve the speaker's original tone and reasoning, and exclude all unrelated content or views on non-AI topics. In order to overcome context length limitations of the worker models, we first had GPT-4.1 divide the long-form conversation transcript of each episode into a small number of large, coherent segments based on major topic shifts or distinct thematic transitions. Each segment covers approximately 10–20 minutes of content and contains a complete discussion around one broad theme, including all main arguments and supporting ideas. Each worker model created segment-level summaries and then combined these for each speaker-episode.

In the second sub-step, we used GPT-4.1 to evaluate and synthesize the summaries. For each speaker in each episode, we provided GPT-4.1 with the full transcript of their conversation and the two workers’ summaries, and instructed it to evaluate each summary according to four criteria: faithfulness, completeness, relevance, and tone. Based on this evaluation, GPT-4.1 produced a final synthesized summary that includes only the speaker’s stated views and supporting arguments related to AI’s risks, concerns or its impacts on humans and society, preserving full reasoning structure and including any relevant background ideas (e.g., philosophical or ethical beliefs) only if they were used to support the speaker’s views on AI.

S4. The grammar of premises

A factual (current) premise asserts an empirical state of affairs, either present or historical, treated as axiomatic within the reasoning chain without further justification. Formally, a factual premise takes the form "X is/has/was the case" (without providing a causal explanation).

A factual (forecast) premise predicts a future state or event, projecting what is expected to happen without providing a causal explanation. Forecast premises describe what is claimed will exist or occur. Formally, a forecast premise takes the form "X will be/do/happen."

A causal premise posits a cause-effect relationship between two variables or states. It specifies how one element influences, produces, prevents, or alters another through some mechanism. Formally, a causal premise takes the form "X causes/influences/prevents Y" or "X leads to a change in Y." It may or may not describe the mechanism through which X causes Y.

An evaluative premise is a propositional statement that assigns normative value or makes a judgment about the desirability, morality, or worth of an action, outcome, or state of affairs. It reflects a value position rather than an empirical claim. Formally, an evaluative premise takes the form "X is good/bad/desirable/undesirable" or "X has positive/negative value according to criterion Y." The pipeline codes evaluative premises in two subtypes—evaluations of actions and evaluations of outcomes—which are reported jointly as "evaluative" throughout the paper.

A definitional premise establishes the semantic boundaries or conceptual scope of a term, entity, or category. It specifies what constitutes membership in a class or the meaning attributed to a concept within the context of the argument. Formally, a definitional premise takes the form "X is defined as Y" or "X constitutes/comprises Y."

Premises are connected to form reasoning chains through three types of linking relationships. The implication operator (=>) establishes a binary connective establishing a consequential relationship between two premises, where the first premise leads to or entails the second. The combination operator (+) joins two or more premises such that they are considered jointly rather than independently in supporting a subsequent premise or conclusion. The evaluation operator (ˆ) indicates an assessment relationship, where the second premise represents a value judgment about the content of the first, marking transitions from "what is" to "what ought to be" or "what is valuable."

S5. Reasoning extraction (PEEL)

The synthesized summaries are used as input for the PEEL architecture to extract reasoning chains structured according to the grammar of premises. Each worker model independently performs a two-stage Chain-of-Thought process. The first stage instructs the model to perform a multi-step reasoning task, including: Conclusion Identification—identify distinct conclusions the speaker makes about AI's risks and impacts, defined as the final endpoints of reasoning; Premise Extraction—extract all explicit and implicit premises used to support each conclusion, classified by type, with a confidence score indicating how clearly each is expressed or implied in the summary; and Reasoning Chain Construction—build complete logical chains linking premises to conclusions using the three formal operators. In the second stage, the same LLM is prompted again using a validation-focused user prompt and asked to validate and if necessary correct the reasoning output: identifying missing premises, correcting misclassifications of premise types, ensuring all logical operators were used correctly, and checking that all conclusions are properly supported and that no unused or invalid premises or chains remain. A programmatic structural validator additionally checks every output (well-formed premise and relationship references, connectivity to the conclusion, type membership) and triggers model repair loops if it detects grammatical errors, for example, if the LLMs propose premise types that do not fit our grammar.

We then used GPT-4.1 to evaluate, compare and finalize the structured reasoning outputs produced by the two workers. This pipeline includes three evaluation stages: Faithfulness check—assess which model's reasoning chains more accurately reflect the speaker's content; Logical validation—check for structural correctness, proper use of logical relationships, and presence of all necessary premises; and Final decision—select the best output or synthesize a better version that synthesizes both chains. Applied to the extended corpus, this yields 382 episode-speaker outputs, each output containing multiple conclusions and chains. Within these 382 outputs, we have 2,451 conclusions and 2,597 reasoning chains (2,317 conclusions have only one chain, 123 have two, 10 have three and 1 has four). Figure S1 shows the distribution of number of premises for this entire set. After a conclusion-level classification (S7), we retain 1,943 AI-risk-relevant conclusions from 364 of these episode–speaker combinations for further analysis."

S7. Topic and attitude assignment

To systematically examine how speakers disagree about AI risks by pairing arguments addressing the same subject matter but expressing opposing attitudes, we first needed to organize their conclusions by topic and attitude. We used the two worker models to independently classify the topics and attitude of each conclusion with respect to AI risks and impacts.

Each conclusion, along with its supporting premises as context, was fed to the two LLMs using the same classification prompt. Topic classification began with an initial list of 30 topics derived from the Stanford HAI 2024 AI Index Report; for each conclusion, the LLM was asked to select the most appropriate topic from the current list, and if none of the existing topics were suitable, the LLM could create a new topic, with the topic list updated for subsequent classifications. This iterative, adaptive approach allowed the topic structure to emerge organically from the conclusions' content and ensured that conclusions within one topic share the same central issue, which is essential for a meaningful pairwise comparison. The dynamically created topics were subsequently consolidated into a consolidated topic dictionary of 107 topics, which we provide in Table S7. We allowed each conclusion to be classified into one to three topics. Of the 1,943 AI-risk-relevant conclusions extracted from 364 episode-speaker combinations, 896 received one topic, 822 two, and 225 three, giving 3,215 conclusion-topic assignments.

Each conclusion-topic pair was also labeled as pessimistic, neutral, or optimistic, based on its orientation toward AI's risks and societal impact. Our coding allows each multi-topic conclusion to carry different attitudes on the different topics because a conclusion can take different orientations toward different risks. An example of one single conclusion with 2 topics and 2 opposite attitudes is the one from Sergey Levine (ep. 108): "Current AI risks are more about systems not being optimized well enough for safety and reliability, and about human misuse, rather than about systems being too capable or inherently dangerous." This is classified into 2 topics: optimistic on X-Risk and pessimistic on AI System Safety, Reliability and Error Prevention. In the entire corpus, 174 of the 1,047 multi-topic conclusions carry different attitudes across their topics. Of the 3,215 conclusion-topic assignments, 1,184 are optimistic, 1,375 pessimistic, and 656 neutral. Table S7 reports the number of assignments and their attitude composition for every topic. Table S8 shows regression estimates from models predicting attitude based on speaker and reasoning chain characteristics, controlling for topic. We find that later episodes are slightly more likely to contain pessimistic conclusions and speakers from industry are less likely to be pessimistic. We also regress attitude on reasoning chain structure. We find that pessimistic conclusions are supported by significantly more premises, while premise-type composition does not differ significantly by attitude once topic is held constant.

S8. Pairwise disagreement analysis

We grouped conclusions by topic and, within each topic, we systematically compared the reasoning chains of speakers who expressed opposing attitudes—optimistic ("boomers") versus pessimistic ("doomers")—toward that specific AI risk. We excluded 40, 18 and 14 pairs in which both chains belong to the same person (including appearances in different episodes). Ultimately, we compared 9,182; 2,358; and 1,030 pairs of chains for X-risk, E-risk and S-risk. The pipeline proceeds in three steps, each run by both workers independently, with the integrator making the final call.

Step 1 (disagreement check).
Two conclusions disagree only if they cannot both be held simultaneously: explicit negation, implicit incompatibility (opposite answers to the same underlying question), or presupposition clash. The operational criterion is a counterfactual test: can the same speaker hold both conclusions? If yes, the pair is not a disagreement. Parallel arguments about different sub-phenomena and differences of emphasis are excluded.

In Step 1, the ensemble classified 7,526 (for X-risk), 869 (for E-risk), and 265 (for S-risk) pairs as disagreeing at the conclusion level.

Step 2 (divergence identification and classification by type).
For each disagreeing pair, the ensemble identifies divergences in premises or operators. Each divergence is localized to the most specific premise or explicitly encoded compound carrying the conflict and typed as factual, forecast, causal, definitional, or evaluative. Claims that are merely co-tenable possibility statements (modal mismatch) or that condition on the same factor (conditional mismatch) are not divergences. The integrator evaluates both workers' candidates against the chains and synthesizes the final divergence list. At the end of this step, the ensemble had identified 21,524 divergences, in 7,477 (X-risk) / 833 (E-risk) / 255 (S-risk) pairs.

In the remaining 49 / 36 / 10 pairs, no divergence could be localized in the chains. This is because we allow for LLMs to decide there is no divergence in Step 2, even if a disagreement had been identified in Step 1. Step 1 is tuned to miss as few disagreements as possible; step 2 applies the strict incompatibility test with an explicit opt-out, so pairs flagged at the conclusion level but with no premise-level collision are recorded as having none rather than being forced to yield a divergence. For example: Paul Krugman has the conclusion: "Current automation is not a qualitatively different threat than past shifts" vs Andrew Ng "AGI_alignment talk distracts from immediate problems like job displacement". The two conclusions are identified as incompatible in Step 1, but Krugman's chain is entirely about whether automation is currently eliminating jobs at an unprecedented scale while Ng's chain is about which problems deserve attention. Similarly for Kuyda (“AI companionship has great potential if developed responsibly") and Hotz ("generative-TikTok engagement systems will cause wireheading harm").

The integrator's divergence-type field was occasionally schema-noncompliant (0.14%, or 31 of 21,524 divergences). The authors inspected and resolved each exception under a fixed rule. Disagreements about whether evidence, probability estimates, or methods provide valid grounds for belief or action were classified as evaluative, while features such as presupposition clash, framing conflict, and meta-level reasoning were treated as structural characteristics rather than separate divergence types. Eleven divergences were accordingly recoded to their underlying substantive type (documented in Table S9 and applied programmatically in the analysis code; pipeline outputs are unmodified); twenty divergences carrying hybrid labels (e.g., factual/forecast) are excluded from the type analyses as ambiguous between two substantive types. These cross-type levels point to a limitation of the premise grammar: it does not separately distinguish first-order disagreements from disagreements about epistemic warrant; a structural second dimension of disagreement (first-order vs. meta-level) may merit its own analysis in future work. To verify that the exclusion is inconsequential, we reran the composition analyses and the dyadic bootstrap under two alternative treatments: assigning each of the twenty to whichever of its two types accounts for the smaller share of divergences in its debate (the assignment least favorable to our findings), and assigning each a single type by our own reading of the conflict. All results hold: every reported deviation changes by less than 0.1 percentage points.

Step 3 (root/derivative classification).
For each divergence, the model assesses whether it depends on any earlier divergence. Divergences that did not depend on others are marked as root divergences, representing the fundamental starting points of the split. The operational criterion is a simulation test: if the speakers reconciled the root, would this divergence dissolve? A disagreement between a pair of conclusions may have several independent roots.

S9. Statistical analysis of disagreement type incidence

Our analysis of the composition of divergence types covers all pairs of disagreements that are identified as having at least one divergence: 7,477 X-risk pairs; 833 E-risk pairs; and 255 S-risk pairs. Dependency-robust inference.
Pairs formed from the same conclusions are not independent: each optimistic and pessimistic conclusion appears in many pairs. Therefore, we use a two-way cluster (dyadic) bootstrap for inference reported in the main text: optimistic conclusions and pessimistic conclusions are independently resampled with replacement (B = 2,000; seed 42), observed pairs are reweighted by the product of their endpoints' resampling multiplicities, and the mean pair-level deviation is recomputed on each replicate; 95% intervals are percentile intervals (Table S11). Conventional per-pair paired t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, and pooled chi-square goodness-of-fit tests, are reported for completeness in Table S10; they treat pairs as independent.

S10. Aggregation of arguments to create a catalog of mechanisms

Having identified the roots of pairwise disagreement, a critical question remains: how can we aggregate these insights to inform decision-making within a multi-stakeholder group? To answer this, we shift our analysis from the pairwise comparison to the entire set of speakers that addressed each topic, again using our ensemble of LLMs. Within each debate (topic) and position (attitude), the ensemble discovers candidate causal mechanisms in chunks of reasoning chains and merges them into a catalog: each mechanism is an ordered sequence of causal steps with the chains and speakers that support it. Chains are grouped by causal mechanism, not by how the speaker evaluates its outcome. We use the same prompts to summarize arguments presented for all three debates. We then manually align the two sides' catalogs into the underlying questions of each debate (Tables S12–S14), based on our own interpretation of the catalogs. This synthesis reflects our summary reading of the extracted chains, illustrating how observers such as policy makers might distill a debate corpus using PEEL.

Table S9. Documented divergence-type recodes

Handling of out-of-category labels.
The integrator's divergence-type field is generated text and is occasionally schema-noncompliant (31 of 21,524 divergences, 0.14%). Such labels are flagged automatically, and each flagged divergence is then inspected and resolved by the authors under a fixed rule: if the label describes the disagreement's discursive level or origin (e.g., epistemic, presuppositional, meta-evaluative, analogical, compound) rather than its substantive nature, the divergence is recoded to the underlying substantive type based on the divergence's content; if the label is a hybrid of two substantive types (e.g., factual/forecast), the divergence is excluded from the type analyses as genuinely ambiguous between the two. Every human decision is recorded in this table with its rationale and applied programmatically in the analysis code, so the pipeline outputs remain unmodified and the recoding is fully reproducible and auditable.

Eleven X-risk divergences whose final ‘type‘ label described the disagreement's discursive level or origin rather than its substantive nature were recoded as follows. The recode is applied programmatically in the analysis notebook; pipeline outputs are unmodified.
