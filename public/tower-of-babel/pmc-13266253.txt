Bacterial microbiome and flavor metabolome shifts in traditional Hinggan league sauerkraut fermentation
Authors: Yang Yang, Hongyi Zhang, Quan Shuang, Ya Nan Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrobial diversity is crucial for the flavor and quality of fermented vegetables. The unique geography and fermentation techniques of Hinggan League, Inner Mongolia, endow local sauerkraut with distinctive characteristics; however, the association between its microbiota and metabolites, as well as the underlying flavor formation mechanism, remains unclear, representing a gap in current research. Therefore, this study systematically investigated the interactions between microbial communities and metabolites during traditional sauerkraut fermentation in Hinggan League, Inner Mongolia, before and after fermentation. Using MiSeq sequencing technology, the core microbiota was identified, mainly including Lactobacillus, Halomonas, and Psychrobacter, with specific strains Azospirillum and Aureimonas observed exclusively in pre-fermentation (VC) samples. Metabolomics analysis detected a total of 335 abundant metabolites, among which 146 upregulated metabolites such as organic acids, amino acids, and fatty acid derivatives significantly accumulated during the late fermentation stage. Notably, 3-phenyllactic acid and 5-aminovaleric acid were the most representative characteristic metabolites. Metabolites pathway enrichment analysis revealed that arginine and proline metabolism, alanine/aspartate/glutamate metabolism, and cyanoamino acid metabolism were the major pathways significantly activated during fermentation, closely associated with energy metabolism, acid-base homeostasis regulation, and flavor generation. Correlation analysis showed a strong association between Lactococcus and 31 flavor compounds, including palmitic acid, L-phenylalanine, and heptadecanoic acid. Functional annotation indicated that Lactococcus-driven protein degradation, amino acid conversion, and lipolytic activities generate key precursors for sour and umami flavor development. Collectively, this study elucidates the microbial community succession characteristics, inter-microbial interaction patterns, and the associated metabolic regulation mechanisms of flavor formation during sauerkraut fermentation, providing a theoretical basis for the targeted modulation of characteristic flavor compound synthesis and the optimization of traditional fermentation processes.Keywords: Sauerkraut, Lactic acid bacteria, High-throughput sequencing, Metabolomics, Flavor compounds

Introduction

Fermented vegetables, particularly sauerkraut, are integral to diverse food cultures across the world—from Northeast China and Germany's Lusatia region to Korea's kimchi tradition—serving not only as staple side dishes but also as carriers of deep-rooted cultural heritage (Swain et al., 2014). These traditional foods rely on natural epiphytic microbes to initiate LAB-driven fermentation under salt and anaerobic conditions, producing characteristic sourness, aroma, and crisp texture (Xiong et al., 2016). Flavor complexity and uniqueness arise from microbial metabolism that converts carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids into compounds such as lactic acid, acetic acid, ethanol, and esters (Zhao et al., 2016). Consequently, unraveling how core microbial communities interact and evolve to regulate flavor formation has become a central theme in modern food microbiology (De Filippis et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2022).

In the sauerkraut fermentation system, the dominant microbial community that drives the fermentation process plays a decisive role in shaping the final product quality and its unique sensory characteristics (Zabat et al., 2018).The microbial community of sauerkraut is dynamic and strongly influenced by multiple environmental and processing factors, including raw material composition, temperature, pH, salt concentration, and fermentation vessel (Xiong et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2023). Metagenomic studies have shown that Firmicutes and Proteobacteria dominate the bacterial consortia throughout fermentation. During early stages, Proteobacteria species are often abundant, contributing proteolytic and lipolytic activities that release amino acids and fatty acids, which serve as substrates for subsequent flavor synthesis. As fermentation progresses, increasing acidity selects for LAB, particularly Lactobacillus, Leuconostoc, and Pediococcus, whose relative abundance may exceed 90% (Jung et al., 2011; Yang, Hu, Xiu, Jiang, Yang, Saren, & Feng, 2020). These LAB not only continue organic acid and ester production but also transform early precursors into volatile compounds that define sauerkraut's distinctive aroma (Liu et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). Thus, sauerkraut flavor formation follows a cooperative mechanism in which early-stage substrate release by Proteobacteria is followed by LAB-mediated biochemical conversion.

Recent research has also emphasized the influence of terroir—the integrated effects of local climate, raw materials, and traditional techniques—on the sensory profiles of fermented vegetables. Geographical specificity manifests as distinct volatile organic compound (VOC) patterns and microbial assemblages. For instance, Northeast Chinese sauerkraut exhibits sharp acidity and a typical “sauerkraut aroma” due to high levels of lactic and acetic acids produced by Leuconostoc and Lactobacillus (Xu, Peng, et al., 2024); Sichuan sauerkraut display more complex fruity and spicy notes arising from terpenoid-rich spices such as chili and pepper (Xiong et al., 2012); whereas German sauerkraut, produced under standardized fermentation, is characterized by milder acidity and a cleaner flavor profile (Plengvidhya et al., 2007). These variations collectively demonstrate that microbial ecology and regional environment jointly shape the sensory diversity of sauerkraut.

Traditional 16S rRNA sequencing only reveals microbial community structure but fails to uncover functional potential or flavor-related metabolic mechanisms, while single metabolomics cannot trace the microbial origins of key metabolites. The integration of metagenomics and metabolomics overcomes these limitations by enabling systematic analysis of community composition, functional genes, and inter-microbial interactions, along with dynamic metabolite profiling. This multi-level approach—linking microbial communities, functional genes, metabolic pathways, and flavor compounds—elucidates the molecular mechanisms of sauerkraut flavor formation and has become an effective strategy for deciphering microecology and metabolic regulation in traditional fermented foods (Wen et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2023).

The microbial community structure of sauerkraut exhibits significant geographical variation, yet existing research has largely focused on temperate/subtropical regions, leaving a limited understanding of the microbial ecology and metabolic mechanisms under low-temperature, short-cycle fermentation conditions in cold-temperate zones such as Hinggan League, Inner Mongolia. This study, taking naturally fermented sauerkraut from Hinggan League as the subject, integrated metagenomics and metabolomics to reveal the core microbiota interaction mechanisms associated with flavor formation in a cold-temperate environment, thereby filling the research gap in this region and providing a theoretical basis for achieving standardized production and quality control of sauerkraut while preserving its regional flavor.

Materials and methods

Materials

Sauerkraut samples were collected in June 2023 from Baiyinwende'er Aili, Harinur Jirigedai Gacha, Horqin Right Wing Middle Banner, Hinggan League, Inner Mongolia, China. Two groups of sauerkraut samples were included in this study: pre-fermentation samples (VC) and post-fermentation samples (PVC). For each group, three representative households with locally high sales volume were selected as sampling units, and three biological replicates were obtained per group. A total of six sauerkraut samples were collected. Existing studies have shown that the physicochemical indicators of naturally fermented Chinese sauerkraut are generally within a stable range: salinity of 2%–5%, pH of 3.2–6.9, titratable acidity of 0.1%–1.8%, and nitrite content of 0.2–2.5 mg/kg. This range is basically consistent with the limits specified in the national food safety standard for preserved vegetables (National Health and Family Planning Commission of the People's Republic of China, 2015) of China.

Fresh Chinese cabbage was mixed with 4% (w/v) NaCl brine at a 1:1 (w/v) ratio and fermented naturally in water-sealed ceramic jars at 22 ± 2 °C for 30 days. Pre-fermentation (VC) and post-fermentation (PVC) samples (∼50 g each) were collected, stored at −80 °C, and used for subsequent analyses.

Microbial diversity and community dynamics analysis

Approximately 0.5 g of homogenized sample was placed into a 2 mL cryogenic tube, to which 500 μL of SA buffer, 100 μL of SC buffer, 0.25 g of zirconium beads, and 10 μL of RNase A (Qiagen) were added. Mechanical lysis (vortex mixing) was performed to disrupt cells, while RNase A was used to remove residual RNA. After vortex mixing for 5 min and incubation at 70 °C for 15 min to facilitate complete cell lysis, the sample was centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 1 min. The supernatant was collected and further treated with 200 μL of pre-chilled SA buffer at 4 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, genomic DNA was extracted using the MEGA Soil DNA Kit (D5625–01, Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA), and its purity and concentration were determined.

DNA integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of bacterial 16S rRNA genes were amplified by PCR using universal primers (forward 5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′, reverse 5′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3′). PCR products were verified by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, purified using an AxyPrep PCR Cleanup Kit (AP-PCR-500G; Corning, NY, USA), and quantified with a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Q33238, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Libraries were prepared using the TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Library Prep Kit (Illumina, USA) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (2 × 250 bp paired-end).

Raw reads were quality-filtered and adapter-trimmed using fastp (Chen et al., 2018), and paired-end reads were merged with FLASH (Magoč & Salzberg, 2011) to obtain high-quality sequences. Denoising and chimera removal were performed using the DADA2 algorithm implemented in QIIME2, generating amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) (Callahan et al., 2016). Representative ASVs were taxonomically annotated against the SILVA 138 database (Quast et al., 2012).

Alpha and beta diversity indices were calculated to evaluate microbial richness and community structure. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) were used to visualize inter-sample variation. Differences in community composition were assessed using PERMANOVA or ANOSIM tests (Anderson, 2001). Predicted functional profiles were inferred using PICRUSt2, and annotations were performed based on the KEGG database (Douglas et al., 2020).

GC–MS-based metabolomic analysis

Metabolite extraction

50.0 mg of sample was accurately weighed into a 2 mL EP tube, and 500 μL pre-cooled methanol/water (3:1, v/v) containing ribitol (Adonitol, CAS 488–81-3, ≥99%, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added as the internal standard for metabolite quantification. Methanol (CAS 67–56-1, HPLC grade, CNW Technologies, Düsseldorf, Germany) and deionized water (Watsons, China) were used for solvent preparation. After vortexing, steel beads were inserted, and the sample was ground at 40 Hz for 4 min, then ultrasonicated in an ice-water bath for 5 min (three replicates). The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm (13,800 ×g) for 15 min at 4 °C, and 200 μL supernatant was collected. Equal 80 μL aliquots from each sample were pooled to prepare QC samples. All extracts were vacuum-dried, reconstituted in 40 μL methoxyamine hydrochloride (CAS 593–56-6, AR grade, TCI, Tokyo, Japan) dissolved in pyridine (CAS 110–86-1, HPLC grade, Adamas, Shanghai, China) at 20 mg/mL, and incubated at 80 °C for 30 min. Afterwards, 60 μL BSTFA (with 1% TMCS, v/v; REGIS Technologies, Inc., Morton Grove, IL, USA) was added for derivatization at 70 °C for 1.5 h. After cooling, 5 μL FAMEs (saturated fatty acid methyl esters in chloroform; Dr. Ehrenstorfer GmbH, Augsburg, Germany) was supplemented for retention index calibration. Chloroform (CAS 67–66-3, HPLC grade, Adamas, Shanghai, China) was used as the solvent for FAMEs. Samples were finally analyzed in random order.

The extracts were vacuum-dried and derivatized with 40 μL of methoxyamine hydrochloride (CAS 593–56-6, AR grade, TCI, Tokyo, Japan) dissolved in pyridine (CAS 110–86-1, HPLC grade, Adamas, Shanghai, China) at a concentration of 20 mg/mL, incubated at 80 °C for 30 min, followed by 60 μL of BSTFA (with 1% TMCS, v/v; REGIS Technologies, Inc., Morton Grove, IL, USA) at 70 °C for 1.5 h. After cooling, 5 μL of FAMEs (saturated fatty acid methyl esters in chloroform; Dr. Ehrenstorfer GmbH, Augsburg, Germany) was added as an internal standard. The chloroform used was of HPLC grade (CAS 67–66-3, Adamas, Shanghai, China). All samples were randomly injected for GC–MS analysis (Dunn & Ellis, 2005).

Instrumental analysis

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis was conducted using a Shimadzu Nexis GC-2030 gas chromatograph (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) coupled with a QP2020 NX mass spectrometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). The system was equipped with a DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) (Fiehn, 2016; Kind et al., 2009). A 1 μL aliquot of the derivatized sample was injected in splitless mode. Helium served as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL·min−1, with the front inlet purge set to 3.0 mL·min−1.

The oven temperature program was as follows: initial temperature of 50 °C held for 1 min, ramped to 310 °C at 8 °C·min−1, and maintained for 11.5 min at the final temperature (Lisec et al., 2006). The injector, transfer line, and ion source temperatures were maintained at 280 °C, 280 °C, and 200 °C, respectively. Electron impact ionization (EI) was performed at −70 eV.

Mass spectra were acquired in full-scan mode across an m/z range of 50–500 using a QP2020 NX mass spectrometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan), with a data acquisition rate of 12.5 spectra·s−1 following a 7.2 min solvent delay (Dunn et al., 2010).

Data processing

Raw GC–MS data were processed using ChromaTOF software (version 4.3×, LECO, St. Joseph, MI, USA) for peak detection, baseline correction, deconvolution, alignment, and integration (Kind et al., 2009). Metabolite identification was performed by comparing both mass spectra and retention indices against the LECO–Fiehn Rtx5 database. To ensure data reliability, features detected in fewer than 50% of quality control (QC) samples or showing a relative standard deviation (RSD) greater than 30% across QC replicates were excluded from further analysis (Dunn et al., 2011).

Correlation analysis between dominant bacterial genera and differential metabolites

Key microbial taxa and metabolites showing significant differences among groups were identified based on both statistical and biological criteria. Core bacterial genera were determined using LEfSe and STAMP analyses, while differential metabolites were selected according to VIP > 1 and p < 0.05 thresholds. Subsequently, Spearman's or Pearson's correlation analyses were performed on the BioinCloud platform(https://www.bioincloud.tech/) to assess the associations between microbial taxa and metabolites. The resulting correlation coefficients were visualized in the form of heatmaps, illustrating the strength and direction of the relationships.

Data processing

Data recording and summarization were performed using Excel 2010 software. Statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS 26 software. All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A pp-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant, and p < 0.01p < 0.01 was considered highly significant. Final results were visualized using Origin 2021 software, with heatmaps generated using the ChiPlot online tool (https://www.chiplot.online/).

Results and analysis

Comparison of bacterial communities before and after sauerkraut fermentation

High-throughput sequencing of six sauerkraut samples collected before and after fermentation was performed using the Illumina MiSeq 6000 platform. For the VC samples, a total of 282,238 raw reads were obtained; after quality filtering, identification, and paired-end merging, 270,644 high-quality reads were retained, and 259,234 effective reads remained after removing chimeras, with an average sequence length of 227 bp. For the PVC samples, 250,105 raw reads were generated, of which 240,597 were high-quality reads after filtering and merging; subsequent chimera removal resulted in 160,223 effective reads, also with an average read length of 227 bp.

Rank abundance curves were constructed based on the relative abundance of features within each sample to evaluate species richness and evenness. A broader curve indicates higher richness, whereas a flatter curve reflects greater evenness (Lundberg et al., 2013). As shown in Fig. 1A, the PVC samples exhibited a wider and flatter curve compared with the VC samples, suggesting that the post-fermentation microbiota possessed higher richness and evenness. Shannon rarefaction curves for both VC and PVC samples (Fig. 1B) approached saturation, indicating sufficient sequencing depth and reliable representation of the microbial communities.

Sample Rank abundance curve (A) and Shannon curve (A).

Alpha diversity was assessed using the Ace, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices to evaluate species richness and diversity. Chao1 and Ace reflect species richness, while Shannon and Simpson measure diversity, with higher values indicating greater diversity (Grice et al., 2009). The alpha diversity indices of VC and PVC samples are presented in Supplementary Table 1. As shown in the table, the Chao1 and ACE indices of PVC samples were higher than those of VC samples, but the differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05), suggesting that the fermentation equipment and environment may have introduced abundant bacterial resources, although the effect did not reach significance due to sample variability. In contrast, the Shannon index of VC samples before fermentation was significantly higher than that of PVC samples after fermentation (p < 0.05), and although the Simpson index was slightly higher in VC samples, the difference was not significant (p > 0.05). These results indicate that the microbial community structure at the early fermentation stage was more complex and evenly distributed, with higher community diversity. As fermentation proceeded, lactic acid bacteria gradually became the dominant group; the lactic acid they produced lowered the pH of the system and inhibited the growth of other non-acid-tolerant bacteria, leading to a decrease in community evenness and diversity. This phenomenon is consistent with the typical “diversity decline and dominant enrichment” pattern of community succession observed in most vegetable fermentation systems. Previous studies have reported that during lactic acid fermentation of vegetables such as sauerkraut and kimchi, various spoilage microorganisms initially participate, but with the rapid proliferation of lactic acid bacteria and the establishment of an acidic environment, the microbial community gradually simplifies and eventually becomes dominated by acid-tolerant genera such as Leuconostoc and Lactobacillus (Yang, Hu, Xiu, Jiang, Yang, Sarengaowa, & Feng, 2020). Therefore, the higher richness but lower diversity observed in PVC samples reflects the successional trend from a complex community to a stable, lactic acid bacteria-centered community during sauerkraut fermentation.

Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities revealed a clear separation trend between VC and PVC samples (Fig. 2A), suggesting substantial fermentation-induced shifts in microbial composition. ANOSIM permutation test (Fig. 2B) yielded an R value of 1 but a p-value of 0.1 (not significant, p > 0.05). This non-significant p-value is likely due to the limited sample size (n = 3 per group), which reduces statistical power. Nevertheless, given the clear PCoA separation and the known succession pattern (enrichment of lactic acid bacteria), we infer that fermentation substantially impacts the microbial community of sauerkraut. These results should be interpreted with caution, and larger-scale studies are needed to validate the observed trends.

Bacterial community structure before and after sauerkraut fermentation

As shown in Fig. 3A, the post-fermentation sauerkraut microbiota was dominated by members of the phyla Proteobacteria and Firmicutes, while the relative abundances of other phyla were lower than 0.01% and therefore categorized as “others.” These two phyla represent the major dominant taxa throughout the sauerkraut fermentation process. During fermentation, the relative abundance of Proteobacteria decreased from 81% to 63%, whereas Firmicutes increased from 14% to 36%, reflecting a typical microbial succession pattern commonly observed in lactic acid–fermented vegetables. In the early fermentation stage, raw vegetables and the surrounding environment harbor large numbers of Gram-negative bacteria, particularly Proteobacteria, which generally exhibit poor acid tolerance and can only persist transiently in neutral or mildly acidic environments. As Firmicutes proliferate and lactic acid accumulates, the system pH rapidly declines, thereby inhibiting most Gram-negative taxa. This finding aligns with the results of Jia et al. (2022), who reported that Proteobacteria and Firmicutes were the predominant bacterial phyla in sauerkraut fermented under different salt concentrations based on MiSeq sequencing. Similarly, Zhou et al. (2018) observed that the proportion of Firmicutes increased progressively with fermentation in naturally fermented northeastern Chinese sauerkraut, while Proteobacteria declined correspondingly, further supporting the limited competitiveness of Gram-negative bacteria in increasingly acidic environments.

As shown in Fig. 3B, eight dominant bacterial genera were detected at the genus level: Halomonas, Lactobacillus, Psychrobacter, Pseudomonas, Methylorubrum, Acinetobacter, and Sphingomonas. After fermentation, Lactobacillus was the most abundant genus (30.46%), followed by Halomonas (28.80%) and Psychrobacter (25.35%). During fermentation, microorganisms with acid and/or salt tolerance rapidly proliferated and occupied ecological niches, while non-tolerant ones were suppressed. Notably, the average relative abundance of Lactobacillus increased from 6.00% to 30.46%, indicating its dominant role in sauerkraut fermentation. Previous studies have demonstrated that Lactobacillus promotes flavor formation in fermented vegetables and inhibits spoilage microorganisms by producing organic acids or bacteriocins, thereby ensuring the quality and safety of the final product (Kõljalg et al., 2013).

In addition to Lactobacillus, the marked enrichment of Halomonas (from 6.00% to 28.80%) and Psychrobacter (from 4.00% to 25.35%) is primarily attributable to the pickling process used in this study. Sauerkraut fermentation was performed using a traditional salting method with an initial brine concentration of 4% (w/v) NaCl. This high-salt environment exerts strong selective pressure favoring halotolerant or halophilic bacteria. Halomonas is a typical halotolerant/halophilic genus commonly found in high-salt habitats (e.g., salted foods and salterns) and adapts to hyperosmotic stress by accumulating compatible solutes. Psychrobacter is also halotolerant and capable of growing under low-temperature and high-salt conditions. Both genera can metabolize glucose to produce various flavor compounds (e.g., esters and alcohols); therefore, their enrichment is likely linked to the salting step during sauerkraut production (Pei et al., 2016). Furthermore, these halotolerant bacteria may coexist with Lactobacillus in the later stages of fermentation, potentially forming mutualistic relationships through metabolic complementarity (e.g., oxygen consumption and local pH modification), thereby jointly contributing to the characteristic flavor of sauerkraut.

Analysis of key differential microbial taxa during sauerkraut fermentation

The Venn diagram provides an intuitive representation of the similarity and overlap of OTU (Operational Taxonomic Unit) composition among environmental samples (Fouts et al., 2012). At a 97% similarity threshold, Venn analysis was performed for the two sauerkraut samples collected before and after fermentation (Fig. 3C). A total of 17 OTUs were shared between the VC (pre-fermentation) and PVC (post-fermentation) samples, whereas 79 and 69 OTUs were uniquely detected in VC and PVC, respectively. These results clearly demonstrate the pronounced microbial succession occurring during fermentation. First, the limited number of shared OTUs—accounting for only 21.5% of the total OTUs in the pre-fermentation community—indicates that the majority of taxa present at the initial stage were unable to adapt to the distinct microenvironment established during fermentation, characterized by low pH, elevated lactic acid concentration, reduced oxygen availability, and intensified nutrient competition. This rapid turnover of microbial members is a hallmark of vegetable lactic fermentation and is jointly shaped by the initial microbiota and fermentation conditions. Second, the slightly reduced OTU richness in PVC relative to VC suggests that the fermentation process acts as a strong environmental filter. Only those microorganisms capable of tolerating and thriving under acidic, low-oxygen, and nutrient-limited conditions persist and become dominant, ultimately resulting in a simplified and specialized community structure. Lastly, the presence of 52 unique OTUs in the PVC samples suggests the emergence of fermentation-adapted taxa, many of which likely represent core functional microorganisms driving the sauerkraut fermentation process.

To further identify taxa contributing significantly to the differentiation of microbial communities between pre- and post-fermentation samples, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was applied (Segata et al., 2011). The cladogram based on phylogenetic relationships (Fig. 3D) revealed a total of 11 discriminative biomarkers between VC and PVC samples, all identified at the genus level. Consistent with the cladogram, taxa with an LDA score > 2 were selected as biomarkers (Fig. 3E), validating the reliability of the observed group-specific microbial signatures.

In the PVC samples, the significantly enriched biomarkers were primarily affiliated with Lactobacillaceae and Bacillaceae. Members of Lactobacillaceae, comprising both homofermentative and heterofermentative lactic acid bacteria, serve as the principal drivers of vegetable fermentation. Their rapid growth and lactic acid production during mid-to-late fermentation phases effectively reduce the pH, suppress spoilage and pathogenic bacteria, and play an essential role in product maturation and preservation (Park et al., 2019). Some Bacillaceae species possess acid tolerance and produce hydrolytic enzymes, suggesting potential involvement in substrate degradation during early stages and an ecological role in the specific microenvironment of late fermentation (Tamang et al., 2016).

LEfSe analysis revealed significant differences in the bacterial community structure between pre- and post-fermentation sauerkraut samples (LDA score > 4). In the pre-fermentation (VC) group, Alphaproteobacteria and its subordinate taxa, including Rhizobiales and Sphingomonadales, were significantly enriched. These taxa are common microorganisms originating from raw material surfaces and the environment(Sui et al., 2024). In contrast, in the post-fermentation (PVC) group, Firmicutes and Bacilli, along with their subordinate taxa Lactobacillales, Lactobacillaceae, and Lactobacillus, became the dominant differential groups. Among these, Lactobacillus was one of the most highly contributive species, indicating that lactic acid bacteria (LAB) became the core microbiota during natural fermentation, dominating the fermentation process and driving the formation of the characteristic flavor of sauerkraut(Xu, Zhang, et al., 2024). Additionally, certain taxa within Gammaproteobacteria were also significantly enriched after fermentation, which may be associated with their adaptation to salt stress and the low-pH environment during fermentation.

Comparison of volatile metabolites in sauerkraut before and after fermentation

To evaluate the metabolic diversity of sauerkraut, GC–MS–based metabolomic profiling was performed to characterize the overall differences in volatile metabolites across samples (Fig. 4A). A total of 335 metabolites were identified and classified into 12 chemical categories, including organic acids and derivatives (32.33%), organic oxygen compounds (18.80%), lipids and lipid-like molecules (12.78%), organoheterocyclic compounds (8.65%), benzenoids (3.01%), organic nitrogen compounds (1.50%), nucleosides, nucleotides, and analogues (1.50%), phenylpropanoids and polyketides (1.13%), homogeneous non-metal compounds (0.75%), lignans and related compounds (0.38%), hydrocarbons (0.38%), and others (18.80%). These results indicate that organic acids and derivatives, followed by organic oxygen compounds, constitute the dominant metabolite classes in sauerkraut.

Principal component analysis (PCA) clearly distinguished the pre-fermented samples (VC) from the post-fermented samples (PVC). As shown in Fig. 4B, the two groups exhibited pronounced separation along PC1 (49.8%) and PC2 (14.7%), which together explained 64.5% of the total variance. The tight clustering within groups and the significant distances between groups highlight the substantial compositional differences in metabolites introduced by fermentation.To further enhance discriminatory power, orthogonal partial least squares–discriminant analysis (OPLS–DA) was conducted. The model extracted a predictive variance of 49.4% and an orthogonal variance of 14.8%, effectively emphasizing group differences (Fig. 4C). High reproducibility among biological replicates and the clear intergroup separation (p < 0.05) jointly demonstrate that fermentation is a major driver shaping the metabolic profiles of sauerkraut. The OPLS–DA model exhibited excellent performance and robustness, with a classification accuracy of 99.9% for known samples (R2Y = 0.999) and a predictive ability of 98.8% for unknown samples (Q2 = 0.988), both surpassing the empirical threshold of 0.90. Furthermore, the Q2 regression line intersected the y-axis at the negative region, confirming the absence of model overfitting and indicating strong model reliability (Fig. 4D–E) (Westerhuis et al., 2008).

Comparative analysis of volatile flavor compounds before and after sauerkraut fermentation

Differential metabolites were identified based on the criteria Fold Change ≥1, VIP ≥ 1, and p < 0.05. A total of 335 differential metabolites were detected between pre-fermentation (VC) and post-fermentation (PVC) sauerkraut samples, of which 146 metabolites were upregulated during fermentation. During vegetable fermentation, microorganisms utilize intrinsic nutrients through multiple metabolic routes—such as lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and carbohydrate metabolism—to generate a wide array of metabolites that ultimately shape the flavor profile of the final product (Liu et al., 2021). Therefore, key amino acids, organic acids, and fatty acids were selected as core indicators for assessing fermentation-driven metabolic transformations. Selected representative differential metabolites between VC and PVC samples are summarized in Table S2.

Given that this study focuses specifically on the metabolic shifts occurring between pre-fermentation and post-fermentation sauerkraut, comparative analyses were performed primarily on VC and PVC samples. Differential metabolites between the two groups were mainly classified into lipids and lipid-like molecules, organic oxygen compounds, organic acids and their derivatives, phenylpropanoids and polyketides, and nucleosides, nucleotides, and analogues. Notably, several metabolites associated with flavor development—including abietic acid 2 (the suffix “2” denotes a peak index or a different adduct/feature of the same metabolite), arachidonic acid, 1,5-anhydroglucitol, N-acetyl-L-glutamic acid 2, lactamide 2, 3-phenyllactic acid, 5-aminovaleric acid 1 (with the suffix “1” similarly indicating a peak index), and sulfuric acid—were significantly elevated in PVC samples. Conversely, metabolites such as adenosine, a cytidine-monophosphate degradation product, and 6-phosphogluconic acid exhibited a marked decrease after fermentation.

During the natural fermentation of sauerkrauts, the succession of microbial communities dominated by lactic acid bacteria drives the restructuring of multi-level metabolic networks involving carbon, nitrogen, and lipids. Metabolomic results revealed that metabolites such as Abietic Acid, arachidonic acid, 1,5-Anhydroglucitol, N-Acetyl-L-glutamic acid, Lactamide, 3-Phenyllactic acid, 5-Aminovaleric acid, and sulfuric acid were significantly upregulated (p < 0.001) after fermentation, with their synthesis-related enzyme genes generally strongly activated. This finding indicates that, as the fermentation environment becomes more acidic and anaerobic, microorganisms adapt to environmental stress and maintain ecological dominance by redistributing metabolic fluxes and activating specific response pathways. Among these, the accumulation of 3-Phenyllactic acid reflects the activity of aromatic amino acid metabolism in lactic acid bacteria. It is generated from phenylalanine via the phenylpyruvate pathway, not only contributing to the unique aroma and antibacterial properties of sauerkrauts but also playing a key role in maintaining microecological homeostasis (Mu et al., 2012). The upregulation of 5-Aminovaleric acid, a lysine metabolite, indicates reprogramming of nitrogen metabolism and enhanced expression of decarboxylase genes, contributing to the formation of umami flavor layers. The significant increase in N-Acetyl-L-glutamic acid suggests activation of the arginine synthesis pathway, reflecting enhanced nitrogen assimilation by microorganisms during the high-density fermentation stage (Zúñiga et al., 2002). Meanwhile, the upregulation of Lactamide and 1,5-Anhydroglucitol indicates the activation of carbon flux restructuring and energy metabolism compensation mechanisms, helping microbial communities maintain energy balance and redox homeostasis (Wang et al., 2021). Additionally, the release of arachidonic acid results from the degradation of raw material cell membrane phospholipids by enzymes such as phospholipase A2. This substance can be further oxidized to generate volatile flavor precursors, playing a critical role in the formation of the characteristic lipid-derived aroma of sauerkrauts (Zhao et al., 2022). The accumulation of sulfuric acid is associated with the degradation of sulfur-containing amino acids and the activation of sulfur metabolism pathways. It not only participates in pH buffering but also collaborates with organic acids to shape the typical acidic stress environment in sauerkrauts (Chen & Narbad, 2018). The significant upregulation of metabolites and the activation of key enzyme genes during sauerkraut fermentation collectively reveal multi-layered synergistic mechanisms of microbial communities in energy metabolism, nitrogen cycling, flavor formation, and environmental stress response. This metabolic restructuring not only supports the ecological dominance of lactic acid bacteria but also provides a molecular basis for the unique flavor and stable quality of sauerkrauts.

As shown in Fig. 5C, the dynamic changes of amino acids, organic acids, and related intermediates during sauerkraut fermentation reflect a lactic acid bacteria (LAB)-driven metabolic reprogramming and adaptive response to environmental stress. Our results demonstrated that N-Methyl-DL-alanine, proline, and succinic acid were significantly upregulated post-fermentation (p < 0.001), whereas asparagine, serine, and D-glyceric acid were significantly downregulated (p < 0.001), indicating that the microbial community reshapes amino acid metabolism to balance energy production and flavor formation.Specifically, N-Methyl-DL-alanine increased approximately 49-fold after fermentation, likely reflecting LAB-enhanced amino acid methylation and osmotic stress adaptation. Methylated amino acids can serve as protective osmolytes, maintaining intracellular protein stability and membrane integrity under stress conditions (Bremer, 2000). Proline accumulation increased ∼4-fold, suggesting that the microbial consortium activated proline biosynthesis and recycling under acidic stress, enhancing intracellular osmotic tolerance and antioxidative capacity (Ganie, 2021). Proline also acts as a flavor precursor, participating in Maillard and esterification reactions that contribute to the unique aroma of fermented sauerkraut (He et al., 2021). Succinic acid exhibited a 2.6-fold increase post-fermentation, indicating enhanced activity of TCA cycle branches and reductive pathways, which are crucial for maintaining intracellular NADH/NAD+ balance, energy supply, and pH regulation (Ahn et al., 2016). Conversely, the observed decrease in asparagine and serine suggests their conversion into succinate or lactate intermediates to support microbial growth and energy metabolism (Hu et al., 2021). Collectively, these findings indicate that during fermentation, the microbial community orchestrates amino acid biosynthesis, methylation modifications, and TCA cycle activity to achieve a dynamic equilibrium between energy provision, acid–base homeostasis, and accumulation of flavor metabolites. This metabolic reprogramming underpins the adaptive capacity of LAB and the development of sauerkrauts characteristic sensory properties.

Differential metabolic pathways in sauerkraut samples before and after fermentation

Enrichment analysis of the significantly differential metabolites between the pre-fermentation (VC) and post-fermentation (PVC) sauerkraut samples, conducted via KEGG pathway annotation, identified 81 metabolic pathways. The top 15 enriched pathways are shown in Fig. 5Dand Fig. 5E. The five pathways with the highest number of enriched differential metabolites were: Metabolic pathways (primarily including 133 significantly differential metabolites such as Pyruvic acid, ribose, and proline); ABC transporters (including 32 significantly differential metabolites such as valine and isoleucine); Biosynthesis of amino acids (including 25 significantly differential metabolites such as asparagine and glycine); Carbon metabolism (including 23 significantly differential metabolites such as D-Glyceric acid and 4-hydroxybutyrate); and D-Amino acid metabolism (including 18 significantly differential metabolites such as glutamic acid and L-cysteine). Among the 81 metabolic pathways identified through the KEGG database enrichment, only three were significantly enriched (p < 0.05): Arginine and proline metabolism, Alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, and Cyanoamino acid metabolism.

During sauerkraut fermentation, Arginine and proline metabolism plays a key role in maintaining microbial community homeostasis and flavor formation. Arginine is decomposed into citrulline and ammonia via the Arginine Deiminase (ADI) pathway, alleviating acid stress and enhancing the metabolic activity of lactic acid bacteria, while simultaneously generating ornithine as a precursor for proline and glutamate synthesis. Proline metabolism can regulate osmotic pressure under acid and salt stress and can be converted to glutamate, promoting energy metabolism and the formation of flavor precursors. The significant changes in the metabolites of this pathway indicate its close association with the flavor development and maturation process of Sauerkraut fermentation (Ali et al., 2025; De Angelis et al., 2002; Gänzle, 2015).

During sauerkraut fermentation, Alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism plays a critical role in energy conversion, acid-base balance, and flavor formation. These three flavor-contributing amino acids are derived from protein degradation and are interconnected with pathways such as arginine, proline, and the TCA cycle. Glutamate not only imparts umami but can also be converted to γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA). Aspartate serves as a precursor for oxaloacetate, participating in energy metabolism. Alanine is generated via the pyruvate pathway, contributing a mild sweetness. The significant increase in their levels reflects the mechanism by which lactic acid bacteria coordinately regulate the fermentation process and flavor construction through amino acid metabolism (Du et al., 2013; Gaudioso et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023).

Cyanoamino acid metabolism involves the degradation of cyanogenic compounds into cyanide ions or thiocyanate, which are further converted into non-toxic amino acids or nitrogen metabolism intermediates, providing nitrogen and carbon sources for microorganisms and promoting microbial adaptation and growth in the high-salt and acidic environment. Concurrently, this metabolic process reduces potential toxicity and contributes to the re-synthesis of amino acids and the formation of flavor compounds through the release of ammonia and α-keto acids. It is therefore a key metabolic pathway for evaluating the safety and flavor metabolic activity of Sauerkraut fermentation (Okoth et al., 2022).

Correlation between Core bacterial genera and volatile flavor compounds in sauerkraut

Integrated correlation analysis (Fig. 6) revealed a functional synergy between core microbial communities and differential metabolites during sauerkraut fermentation. The top 18 bacterial genera ranked by relative abundance at the genus level were subjected to Spearman correlation analysis with the differential metabolites, and the correlation strength was assessed using p-value significance (p < 0.05) to identify potential links between core flavors and dominant bacterial genera. Among which Lactococcus and other dominant bacterial genera exhibited significant positive correlations (p < 0.05) with 31 metabolites, including palmitic acid, L-phenylalanine, heptadecanoic acid, N^G-methyl-L-arginine, lactic acid, and glycine. These results align with Wang et al. (2023), indicating that these dominant bacterial genera promote the formation of acidic and umami flavor compounds by regulating metabolic fluxes. Lactococcus, Methylobacterium–Methylorubrum, Sphingomonas, and Pseudomonas were positively correlated (p < 0.05) with N-acetyl-L-glutamic acid, lactylamide, sulfuric acid, and creatinine, these bacterial genera are closely correlated with the metabolism of amino acids, organic acids and sulfur-containing compounds, and exhibit a significant association with flavor development and quality improvement of pickled mustard greens. Azospirillum and Aureimonas showed significant positive correlations (p < 0.05) with glycine, glutaraldehyde, glycolic acid, α-linolenic acid, β-alanine, uracil, linoleic acid, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), phosphate, oleic acid, and succinic acid. Notably, Azospirillum, a nitrogen-fixing and amino acid-synthesizing genus, may enhance nitrogen cycling and metabolite accumulation (e.g., amino acids and GABA) within the fermentation system (Cruz-Hernández et al., 2022). Whereas Aureimonas is closely associated with the metabolic processes of organic acids such as glycolic acid and succinic acid, as well as unsaturated fatty acids including α-linolenic acid and linoleic acid, and exhibits a potential correlation with bacterial energy metabolism and the formation of flavor lipid substances (Madhaiyan et al., 2013).

Overall, the fermentation of sauerkraut is characterized by a multi-layered metabolic interaction mechanism: dominant LAB drive the synthesis of organic acids, amino acids, and fatty acids; supportive genera such as Lactococcus and Methylobacterium–Methylorubrum enrich flavor complexity through metabolite production; and environment-adapted taxa like Azospirillum and Aureimonas maintain nitrogen cycling and energy balance. This framework reveals the functional division of labor within the microbial community, underpinning the formation of sauerkraut's characteristic flavor profile.

Conclusions

In this study, high-throughput sequencing and metabolomics were used to investigate microbial and metabolic changes during sauerkraut fermentation (Hinggan League, Inner Mongolia). Fermentation drove a typical succession from a diverse microbial community to a stable, lactic acid bacteria (LAB)-dominated one, with increased Firmicutes and decreased Proteobacteria. Lactobacillus, Halomonas, and Psychrobacter became the dominant genera; the halotolerant Halomonas and Psychrobacter were enriched by the 4% NaCl pickling process and showed metabolic complementarity with Lactobacillus. Flavor-related metabolites (e.g., 3-phenyllactic acid, 5-aminovaleric acid, arachidonic acid) were significantly upregulated after fermentation, and three KEGG pathways (arginine/proline, alanine-aspartate-glutamate, and cyanoamino acid metabolism) were activated. Spearman correlation analysis revealed a synergistic network between core bacterial genera and key metabolites, characterized by “dominance of beneficial bacteria, synergy of accessory bacteria, and maintenance of microecological homeostasis”. These findings provide a theoretical basis for understanding sauerkraut flavor formation and optimizing traditional fermentation processes.

Discussion

By integrating high-throughput sequencing and untargeted metabolomics, this study revealed microbial succession and flavor metabolic remodeling during traditional sauerkraut fermentation in Inner Mongolia's Hinggan League. Fermentation drove a typical “diversity decline, dominant enrichment” pattern: Firmicutes (especially Lactobacillus) increased while Proteobacteria decreased. Halotolerant Halomonas and Psychrobacter, enriched by 4% NaCl brining, formed metabolic complementarity with lactobacilli. Major metabolites were organic acids and derivatives, with 3-phenyllactic acid, 5-aminopentanoic acid, and arachidonic acid significantly upregulated. Three KEGG pathways (arginine/proline, alanine/aspartate/glutamate, and cyanoamino acid metabolism) were activated. Spearman correlation uncovered a multi-layer interaction network of “dominant bacteria driving, auxiliary bacteria cooperating, and microecological homeostasis maintenance.” These findings agree with prior reports on sauerkraut, kimchi, and other vegetable fermentations (Park et al., 2019).

The advantages of this study are mainly reflected in four aspects: First, the multi-omics strategy (16S rRNA sequencing plus metabolomics) systematically elucidated the fermentation mechanism from three levels—microbial community structure, metabolic pathways, and flavor compounds; Second, the study focused on actual samples of traditionally homemade sauerkraut from Northeast China, providing good regional representativeness and process authenticity; Third, LEfSe and Spearman analyses clearly identified the succession trajectory from environmental microbiota (Azospirillum, Rhizobiaceae) to functional microbiota (Lactobacillaceae, Bacillaceae), and pinpointed core bacterial genera significantly positively correlated with key flavor metabolites, thereby offering candidate microbial targets for flavor-oriented regulation; Fourth, the established OPLS-DA model (R2Y = 0.999, Q2 = 0.988) was robust and reliable without overfitting (Trygg & Wold, 2002).

However, this study also has several limitations. First, only three biological replicates per group were used, yielding a small sample size. Although the ANOSIM permutation test gave R = 1, the p-value was 0.1 (not significant), a typical sign of low statistical power with small (Anderson & Walsh, 2013). Thus, the PCoA separation trend should be interpreted cautiously, and future studies should use at least six replicates per group. Second, based on 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing, this study cannot precisely infer strain-level metabolic functions; metagenomics or metatranscriptomics could directly identify expression of key metabolic enzyme genes (Franzosa et al., 2018). Third, numeric suffixes on some metabolites (e.g., abietic acid 2, lactamide 2) represent peak numbers or adduct characteristics but were not explained in the Methods, causing potential ambiguity—this should be clarified in future reports. Fourth, the proposed metabolic complementarity among Halomonas, Psychrobacter, and Lactobacillus was inferred only from Spearman correlations, lacking direct evidence from co-culture or metabolic flux analysis. Finally, only the fermentation endpoint and starting point were compared; no intermediate time points were included, so continuous dynamics of microbiota and metabolites could not be captured. Future time-series experiments with multivariate analysis should construct kinetic models (Faust et al., 2015).

Despite these limitations, this study still provides valuable insights into the fermentation mechanism of traditional sauerkraut. At the practical level, the identification of synergistic interactions between the halotolerant genera Halomonas and Psychrobacter and lactobacilli suggests that flavor formation and microbial safety can be balanced by modulating salt concentration. At the theoretical level, three significantly activated KEGG pathways highlight the central role of amino acid metabolism in acid stress responses and flavor precursor generation (An et al., 2023). In summary, this study provides a theoretical basis for elucidating the molecular foundations of flavor formation in traditional sauerkraut and for optimizing traditional fermentation processes.
