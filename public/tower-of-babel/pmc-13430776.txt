Engineering RVG-modified exosomes for targeting TGF-β1 signaling in stroke recovery
Authors: Wang Zhao, Lei Hao, Jiangwei Zhang, Jun Zhu, Jia Kuang, Meibiao Zhang, Yonggang Zhang, Xiaoxing Xiong, Zhao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the role of TGF-β1 signaling in astrocyte-neuron interactions after ischemic stroke and to develop a brain-targeted engineered exosome system, EXO-RVG-SD208, for promoting neural repair.MethodsPublic datasets and transcriptomic analyses were used to characterize the dynamic changes of TGF-β1 after stroke, and key targets were identified through GO, KEGG, and WGCNA analyses. A brain-targeted engineered exosome, EXO-RVG-SD208, was constructed and characterized for its physicochemical properties. Its inhibitory effect on the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 pathway in astrocytes and its neuroregenerative potential were evaluated in oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) and neuron-astrocyte co-culture models. Therapeutic efficacy was further assessed in a middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (MCAO/R) mouse model. Integrated multi-omics analyses were performed to explore the downstream mechanisms involved in neural repair.ResultsTGF-β1 was markedly upregulated after stroke and was predominantly derived from astrocytes, where it was closely associated with neuroinflammation and impaired neuroplasticity. EXO-RVG-SD208 effectively inhibited activation of the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 pathway, promoted astrocyte phenotypic remodeling, enhanced neuronal synaptic activity, and improved functional recovery in MCAO/R mice. Multi-omics analyses further indicated that the therapeutic effects were associated with the regulation of mTOR, BDNF, and MAPK-related pathways.ConclusionEXO-RVG-SD208 effectively delivered TGF-β1 inhibitor to the brain, suppressed astrocytic TGF-β1/Smad2/3 activation, facilitated astrocyte-neuron remodeling, synaptic reconstruction, and neural functional recovery, presenting a promising nanodelivery strategy for stroke rehabilitation.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08271-2.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke (IS) remains a leading cause of death and long-term disability worldwide, substantially reducing quality of life and imposing a major burden on healthcare systems [1, 2]. Although acute interventions such as thrombolysis and vascular recanalization have improved short-term outcomes, long-term neurological recovery after stroke is still limited [3–5]. This gap highlights the incomplete understanding of secondary injury and repair mechanisms following stroke, as well as the lack of effective therapeutic targets for these processes [6]. Recent evidence indicates that non-neuronal cells in the central nervous system (CNS), particularly astrocytes, play pivotal roles in the post-stroke response [7]. Astrocytes are rapidly activated after brain injury and contribute to inflammatory cytokine release, blood–brain barrier (BBB) maintenance, and synaptic homeostasis [8, 9]. However, excessive activation of astrocytes may promote glial scar formation, hinder neuronal regeneration, and disrupt neural network remodeling through persistent inflammatory signaling [10]. Therefore, regulating astrocyte functional polarization after stroke, especially the transition from the neurotoxic A1 phenotype to the neuroprotective A2 phenotype, has emerged as a critical focus in stroke recovery research.

TGF-β1 is a key cytokine involved in the regulation of cell proliferation, differentiation, and immune responses [11]. After stroke, TGF-β1 is persistently upregulated and contributes to multiple pathological processes. Previous studies have shown that TGF-β1 mainly modulates astrocyte activation and neuroinflammatory responses through the Smad2/3 signaling pathway [12, 13]. While TGF-β1 may exert neuroprotective effects in the early phase of stroke [14, 15], its sustained activation in the subacute and chronic phases has been associated with the maintenance of neurotoxic A1 astrocytes, exacerbated inflammation, and impaired synaptic plasticity and axonal regeneration [16, 17]. In addition, TGF-β1 signaling may indirectly affect synaptic connectivity and astrocyte–neuron functional coupling by regulating the expression of cytokines and adhesion molecules [12]. Therefore, targeting the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 pathway to inhibit A1 astrocyte activation and promote a neuro-supportive phenotype may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for post-stroke neural repair.

With the advancement of nanotechnology and bioengineering, exosomes (Exos) have emerged as a promising biological delivery system for precision therapy in neurological diseases [18, 19]. Exos are membrane-bound vesicles approximately 30–150 nm in diameter, derived from various cell types, and exhibit excellent tissue penetration and biocompatibility [20]. Their natural structure facilitates the ability to cross the BBB, enabling targeted delivery within the CNS [21]. Through surface modification (e.g., RVG peptide) and loading of therapeutic agents (e.g., small-molecule inhibitors, siRNA), engineered Exos have shown considerable targeting ability and therapeutic potential in models of Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and brain tumors [22–24]. However, the application of Exo-based strategies for precise modulation of TGF-β1 signaling after stroke remains limited. Most existing studies have focused on Exos as carriers of neurotrophic or protective factors rather than as platforms for targeted pathway inhibition [25, 26]. Therefore, developing an engineered Exo system capable of crossing the BBB, targeting astrocytes, and delivering a TGF-β1 pathway inhibitor may offer important theoretical and translational value.

Based on this rationale, the present study investigated the role of TGF-β1 signaling in astrocyte–neuron interactions after stroke. We developed a brain-targeted engineered exosome modified with RVG and loaded with the transforming growth factor-β receptor I (TGF-βRI) inhibitor SD208 (EXO-RVG-SD208). This platform regulated astrocyte phenotypic transformation and synaptic support functions, thereby promoting neurological recovery after stroke. Comprehensive bioinformatic analyses were first performed to characterize the dynamic changes and cellular sources of TGF-β1 signaling. Cellular and animal experiments were then used to evaluate its role in neuroinflammation and neural network reconstruction. Integrated multi-omics analyses further identified the key regulatory pathways involved. Collectively, these findings provide a novel strategy for astrocyte-targeted intervention after stroke and establish a theoretical and experimental basis for the translational application of TGF-β1 pathway inhibition in neural repair.

Materials and methods

Acquisition of RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets

Brain tissue samples were collected from sham-operated mice and middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (MCAO/R) model mice (n = 3 per group) for RNA extraction and sequencing analysis. Total RNA was extracted from each sample using TRIzol reagent (Cat. No. 16,096,020, Thermo Fisher, New York, USA). RNA concentration, purity, and integrity were assessed using the Qubit® RNA Assay Kit (HKR2106-01, BioGems, Shanghai, China) with a Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer® (Q33216, Life Technologies, CA, USA), a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, CA, USA), and the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit (5067–1511, Agilent) on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system, respectively. Samples meeting the required standards for concentration, purity, and integrity were used for subsequent library preparation, with 3 μg of total RNA per sample as input material. cDNA libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (E7435L, NEB, Beijing, China) for Illumina platforms. Library quality was further evaluated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system. Indexed samples were then clustered on a cBot Cluster Generation System using the TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Cat. No. PE-401–3001, Illumina). Sequencing was subsequently performed on the Illumina HiSeq 550 platform.

Quality control of RNA-seq data

The quality of paired-end raw sequencing reads was initially evaluated using FastQC software (v0.11.8). Raw reads were then preprocessed with Cutadapt (v1.18) to remove Illumina adapter sequences and poly(A) tails. Reads containing more than 5% ambiguous bases (N) were discarded using a Perl script. Low-quality reads were further filtered using FASTX Toolkit (v0.0.13), and only reads in which at least 70% of bases had a quality score greater than 20 were retained. Paired-end reads were subsequently repaired using BBMap software. The resulting high-quality clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome using HISAT2 software.

Differential expression analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the edgeR package (v3.38.4) in the R environment. Raw read count matrices were first filtered to remove low-expression genes, followed by normalization using the trimmed mean of M values (TMM) method. Generalized linear model fitting and quasi-likelihood F-tests were performed using the glmQLFit and glmQLFTest functions. The criteria defined DEGs |log2FC| >2 and p-value < 0.05. DEG visualization, including volcano plots and heatmaps, was conducted using the R packages ggplot2 (v3.4.4) and pheatmap (v1.0.12), respectively.

Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) functional enrichment analysis

DEGs were input into the clusterProfiler package (v4.6.2) for GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. GO terms were analyzed across three categories: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). Gene annotation was standardized using the org.Mm.eg.db or org.Hs.eg.db databases. p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction method, with a significance threshold set at q < 0.05. Significantly enriched neuroplasticity-related pathways, inflammatory responses, and signal transduction were visualized using bubble plots and bar charts.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction

Feature genes were imported into the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) for PPI analysis, with the species restricted to Homo sapiens. The R package “count” was used to analyze the PPI network further. Network nodes were ranked based on their degree values.

Single-cell sample preparation and sequencing

Brain tissues were collected from sham-operated mice and MCAO/R model mice (n = 1 per group) for single-cell RNA sequencing. After dissociation into single-cell suspensions, samples were loaded onto Chromium Single Cell chips. Single-cell capture and library preparation were performed using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ v3 Reagent Kit (10x Genomics), with a target recovery of approximately 10,000 cells per sample. During reverse transcription, individual mRNA transcripts were uniquely barcoded. Sequencing libraries were then generated using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Solution (10x Genomics) for Illumina platforms. Sample indexing enabled multiplexed sequencing within a single flow cell channel. Final libraries were sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform with the following configuration: 28 bp for Read 1, 98 bp for Read 2, and 8 bp for the sample index. Each library achieved a minimum sequencing depth of 25,000 reads per cell.

T-Distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) clustering, cell annotation, pseudotime analysis, and cell–cell communication analysis

scRNA-seq data were processed and analyzed using the Seurat package in R. Quality control was performed using the following criteria: nFeature_RNA > 200, nCount_RNA between 1,000 and 25,000, and mitochondrial gene percentage < 5%. The filtered data were then normalized using the LogNormalize method.

For dimensionality reduction, principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted based on the top 2,000 highly variable genes. The first 30 principal components were used for downstream clustering analysis. Cell subpopulations were identified using Seurat’s FindClusters function with the resolution parameter set to the default value (res = 0.6). Nonlinear dimensionality reduction was subsequently carried out using the UMAP algorithm. Marker genes for each cell subpopulation were identified using the Seurat package. Cell-type annotation was performed with the SingleR package using the MouseRNAseqData reference dataset and was further refined according to established lineage-specific marker genes and the CellMarker database. Pseudotime trajectory analysis was carried out using the monocle package, and cell–cell communication analysis was performed using the CellChat package.

Cell culture and Exo collection

The human astrocyte cell line HA1800 (BFN60804059, ATCC, USA) was cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (11965092, Thermo Fisher, USA) supplemented with 10% Exo-depleted fetal bovine serum (FBS) (EXO-FBS-50A-1, Shanghai Qiran Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (V900929, Sigma, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. When the cell density reached 80%, the medium was replaced with an Exo-depleted medium, and the cells were maintained for an additional 48 h. The culture supernatant was then collected and pre-filtered using a 0.22 μm filter membrane (Millipore, USA) for further processing.

Exo purification and concentration

Exos were purified using a combination of ultracentrifugation and size-exclusion chromatography. First, cell debris and large particles were removed using sequential centrifugation steps in a Beckman Optima XPN-100 ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter, USA): 300 ×g for 10 min, 2,000 ×g for 20 min, and 10,000 ×g for 30 min to remove cells, debris, and large vesicular particles. Exosomes were subsequently pelleted at 100,000 ×g for 70 min. The pellet was resuspended in 1 mL PBS and further purified using a qEVoriginal SEC column (Izon Science, New Zealand), followed by concentration with an Amicon Ultra 10K centrifugal filter (Millipore, USA).

Exo drug loading and brain-targeted modification

The TGF-β1 small-molecule inhibitor SD208 (S7624, Selleck Chemicals, USA) was added to the Exo solution at a final concentration of 20 μM. Drug encapsulation was performed by ultrasound-assisted loading using a Sonics Vibra-Cell VCX130 system (USA) at 20% amplitude with 3-s pulse intervals for 2 min. For brain-targeted surface engineering, DSPE-PEG2000-RVG (Avanti Polar Lipids, USA) was incubated with exosomes at a mass ratio of 500:1 at 37 °C for 30 min to allow membrane insertion. The engineered brain-targeted, SD208-loaded exosome formulation was designated as EXO-RVG-SD208.

Exo morphology and particle size analysis

The morphology of Exos was observed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Hitachi HT7700, Japan). Purified Exos were fixed in 2% (v/v) paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 5 min, placed onto carbon-coated copper grids, and negatively stained with 0.5% phosphotungstic acid (pH 7.0). After excess stain was removed with filter paper, the grids were air-dried and imaged.

Exo particle concentration and size distribution were determined using a NanoSight NS300 system (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Worcestershire, UK) equipped with a 405 nm laser and a high-sensitivity CMOS camera. Exosome pellets were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and diluted to a final concentration of 1 × 107 to 1 × 109 particles/mL, followed by vortexing for 1 min.

Samples were then loaded into the detection chamber and analyzed at room temperature. For each sample, a 60-s video was recorded and processed using NTA software (version 3.2), which tracked particle movement under Brownian motion to calculate particle size and concentration.

Detection of Exo markers

Western blot (WB) was used to detect Exo marker proteins. Exo samples were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail and PMSF. To ensure complete lysis, samples were vortexed on ice every 5 min for 30 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 1 h. The supernatants were collected and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A55860). Equal amounts of protein were separated on 8–16% Tris–HCl polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad) at 4 °C for 90 min. Membranes were blocked in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 and 5% (w/v) non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD63 (Abcam, ab134045, 1:1000), CD9 (Abcam, ab236630, 1:1000), and TSG101 (Abcam, ab30871, 1:1000). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents (Thermo Fisher, USA).

In vivo tracing of Exos

EXO-RVG-SD208 was labeled with the near-infrared fluorescent dye DiR (Invitrogen, D12731) at a molar ratio of 1:100 and incubated in the dark for 15 min. Free dye was removed using a size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) column, followed by PBS washing. To facilitate fluorescence detection, the heads of C57BL/6 female mice (approximately 20 g, n = 5) were shaved before imaging. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and 200 μL of DiR-labeled exosomes were administered via tail vein injection. In vivo fluorescence imaging was performed at 0, 6, 12, and 24 h using an IVIS Spectrum imaging system (PerkinElmer, USA) with excitation and emission wavelengths set at 745 and 800 nm, respectively. Background fluorescence was subtracted, and all animals were imaged under identical instrument settings. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using Living Image software (version 4.2).

Validation of RVG peptide-mediated brain targeting specificity

To verify that RVG modification was responsible for the brain-targeting ability of EXO-RVG-SD208, a competitive inhibition experiment was performed. EXO-RVG-SD208 was incubated with excess free RVG peptide (final concentration: 100 μg/mL) at 37 °C for 30 min to generate a competitive blocking formulation (Competition group). EXO-SD208 and EXO-RVG-SD208 served as control groups. At 24 h after surgery, MCAO/R mice were intravenously injected via the tail vein with DiR-labeled formulations from the three groups at an equivalent dose of 200 μg exosomal protein per mouse. Six hours after injection, whole-body fluorescence imaging was conducted using the IVIS Spectrum imaging system. Brain-region fluorescence intensity was quantified to evaluate the specific contribution of RVG peptide to brain-targeting efficiency [27].

Determination of drug encapsulation efficiency and release profile

The release behavior of SD208 was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Agilent 1260, USA). A C18 column (Waters XBridge BEH C18, 4.6 × 150 mm, 5 μm) was used with a mobile phase of methanol:water = 60:40 (v/v) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min and detection wavelength of 254 nm. The free, unencapsulated drug was removed using an SEC column before sample processing. For the release study, Exo suspensions were incubated at 37 °C in PBS at pH 7.4 or pH 5.5 at 37 °C. Samples were collected at 0, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h, and the cumulative release rate of SD208 was subsequently calculated.

CCK-8 assay for cell viability

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates (353072, Corning, USA), and 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (CK04, Dojindo, Japan) was added to each well. After incubation at 37 °C for 2 h, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to assess cell viability.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay

Cell cytotoxicity was evaluated using the CytoTox 96 Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (G1780, Promega, USA). Briefly, 50 μL of co-culture supernatant was mixed with 50 μL of substrate solution and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 50 μL of stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader. Cytotoxicity was calculated based on LDH release using the formula: (Absorbance of the experimental group - Absorbance of spontaneous release) / (Absorbance of maximum release - Absorbance of spontaneous release) × 100%.

Flow cytometry for apoptosis detection

Cells were washed with PBS and stained using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (556547, BD Biosciences, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Apoptosis was then quantified using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA).

In vivo biocompatibility evaluation of Exos

C57BL/6 mice were randomly assigned to different groups (n = 6 per group) and intravenously injected via the tail vein with EXO-RVG-SD208 at a dose of 200 μg per mouse every 24 h for a total of three administrations. On day 7 after the first injection, blood samples were collected for routine hematological analysis using a Mindray BC-5000 Vet hematology analyzer (China). Serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CRE) were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi 7180, Japan). Brain, liver, spleen, and kidney tissues were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (G1120, Solarbio, China) and examined under a light microscope (Leica DM3000, Germany) for histopathological evaluation. Serum levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β were quantified using ELISA kits (R&D Systems, USA) to assess systemic inflammatory status.

Exo uptake and subcellular localization observation

EXO-RVG-SD208 was labeled with the fluorescent dye DiI (Invitrogen, V22885) at a molar ratio of 1:100 and incubated in the dark for 20 min. Unbound dye was removed using a qEV column (Izon Science, New Zealand). The labeled exosomes were then added to oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R)-treated HA1800 astrocytes at a final concentration of 100 μg/mL and incubated for 0.5, 1, 2, 4, or 8 h. Cells were subsequently collected, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and mounted on slides. Lysosomes were stained with LysoTracker Green (Beyotime, C1045), and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Exosome uptake dynamics and intracellular colocalization were observed using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP8, Germany).

Quantitative analysis of exo uptake

The uptake efficiency of EXO-RVG-SD208 by astrocytes was quantified by flow cytometry. After 4 h of incubation, cells were digested, resuspended, and analyzed using a CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). A total of 10,000 cells were acquired from each sample, and red fluorescence intensity was recorded. Cells in the blank control group were used to correct for background fluorescence. The percentage of fluorescent-positive cells and mean fluorescence intensity were analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1).

Establishment of the stroke model and detection of Exo distribution

Male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China; License No. 219). Animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at 22–25 °C and 60–65% humidity under a 12 h light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water. After 1 week of acclimatization, mice were used for subsequent experiments. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee and complied with local guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Male C57BL/6J mice were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg, intraperitoneally). Body temperature was maintained at 37.0 ± 0.3 °C during surgery using a temperature-controlled heating pad. A midline cervical incision was made to expose the left common carotid artery (CCA), external carotid artery (ECA), and internal carotid artery (ICA). After ligating the ECA, a silicone-coated monofilament (diameter 0.18 ± 0.01 mm, Guangzhou Jialing Biotech Co., China) was inserted through the ECA and advanced via the CCA bifurcation into the intracranial segment of the ICA (10 ± 1 mm from the carotid bifurcation) to occlude the middle cerebral artery (MCA) for 60 minutes. Regional cerebral blood flow (rCBF) in the ischemic hemisphere was continuously monitored using a laser speckle imaging system (RWD, Shenzhen). A ≥ 70% reduction in rCBF compared to the contralateral hemisphere was a successful model. After the ischemic period, the filament was withdrawn to allow reperfusion. Sham-operated mice underwent the same anesthesia and surgical procedures but without filament insertion [28].

At 24 h after surgery, DiR-labeled EXO-RVG-SD208 (200 μg/mouse) was administered via tail vein injection. In vivo fluorescence imaging was conducted at 6, 12, and 24 h post-injection using the IVIS Spectrum system (PerkinElmer, USA) to evaluate brain accumulation of Exos.

Cell grouping and treatment in Vitro experiments

The OGD/R model was established by exposing cells to glucose-free DMEM (Gibco, USA) under 1% O2, 5% CO2, and 94% N₂ for 4 h, followed by reoxygenation in normal glucose-containing medium under normoxic conditions for 24 h. Cells were assigned to the following groups: Control group; OGD/R group: Cells were subjected to OGD treatment (cultured in glucose- and oxygen-deprived conditions for 4 hours), followed by 24 hours of reoxygenation under normoxic and normoglycemic conditions to simulate ischemia/reperfusion injury. No treatment was applied; OGD/R + EXO-RVG group: After OGD/R treatment, cells were immediately incubated with engineered Exos (EXO-RVG, without SD208 loading) for 24 hours; OGD/R + EXO-RVG-SD208 group: After OGD/R treatment, cells were immediately incubated with engineered Exos loaded with the TGF-β1 inhibitor SD208 (EXO-RVG-SD208) for 24 hours; Rescue group (OGD/R + EXO-RVG-SD208 + recombinant TGF-β1 [rTGF-β1]): After OGD/R treatment, cells were simultaneously incubated with EXO-RVG-SD208 and rTGF-β1 for 24 hours.

TGF-β1 response element reporter assay

TGF-β response element luciferase reporter plasmids (Cignal Reporter Assay, Qiagen, USA) were transfected into OGD/R model cells. At 6 h after transfection, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing the indicated treatments. The experimental groups included Control, OGD/R, OGD/R + EXO-RVG, OGD/R + free SD208, OGD/R + EXO-RVG-SD208, and OGD/R + EXO-RVG-SD208 + rTGF-β1 (Rescue). After 48 h of treatment, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA). Relative luminescence intensity was expressed as the ratio of firefly luciferase to Renilla luciferase activity.

siRNA interference assay

TGF-β1 expression was silenced using siRNA synthesized by RiboBio (China). Transfection was performed with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, USA) at a final concentration of 100 nM. After confirming knockdown efficiency, key experiments were repeated to verify pathway specificity.

Construction of neuron-astrocyte co-culture system

Primary cortical neurons were isolated from the brains of P0–P1 neonatal mice and digested with 0.25% trypsin (25200114, Gibco, USA). Cells were then seeded into poly-D-lysine-coated 12-well plates (P6407, Sigma, USA) and maintained in Neurobasal medium supplemented with B27 (10888022, Gibco, USA). Astrocytes were pretreated for 24 h and subsequently added to the upper chamber of a Transwell insert (0.4 μm, Corning, USA) to establish a non-contact neuron-astrocyte co-culture system.

Whole-cell patch-clamp recording

To evaluate the effect of EXO-RVG-SD208 on neuronal synaptic transmission, whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed in the astrocyte-neuron non-contact co-culture system. The experimental groups were the same as those used in previous assays: Control, OGD/R, OGD/R + EXO-RVG, OGD/R + EXO-RVG-SD208, and Rescue (OGD/R + EXO-RVG-SD208 + rTGF-β1). Recordings were carried out 24 h after OGD/R and exosome treatment. Glass pipettes with a resistance of 4–6 MΩ were prepared using a micropipette puller at room temperature (22–24 °C). The pipette solution contained (in mM): 105 K-gluconate, 30 KCl, 10 phosphocreatine, 10 HEPES, 4 ATP-Mg, 0.3 GTP-Tris, and 0.3 EGTA, with the pH adjusted to 7.2 using KOH. Morphologically intact cortical pyramidal neurons were identified under infrared-differential interference contrast (IR-DIC) microscopy. Neurons were voltage-clamped at − 65 mV, near the GABA reversal potential, to record spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs). Only one neuron was recorded from each coverslip. Series resistance was continuously monitored, and recordings were excluded if the resistance changed by more than 20%. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using pCLAMP 10.7 software (Molecular Devices, USA) with the Clampfit module. A threshold of − 20 pA was used for event detection. The frequency and amplitude of sEPSCs were analyzed over a stable 5 min recording period. At least six neurons were recorded in each group. sEPSC frequency and amplitude were normalized to the mean values of the Control group [29].

Detection of neurotrophic factor secretion

Culture supernatants from the co-culture system were collected, and the levels of BDNF (R&D Systems, DY248), GDNF (R&D Systems, DY212), and VEGF (R&D Systems, DY493) were determined using ELISA kits. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm with 570 nm as the reference wavelength.

Experimental grouping and Exo administration

Mice were randomly assigned to five groups: Sham group; MCAO/R group, in which mice underwent MCAO/R surgery with 90 min of occlusion followed by reperfusion, and received an equal volume of PBS via tail vein injection as the model control; MCAO/R + EXO-RVG group, in which mice received engineered Exos (EXO-RVG, without SD208 loading) to evaluate the baseline therapeutic effect of unloaded Exos; MCAO/R + EXO-RVG-SD208 group, in which mice were treated with EXO-RVG-SD208 after MCAO/R to assess its role in promoting post-stroke neural repair; and Rescue group (MCAO/R + EXO-RVG-SD208 + rTGF-β1), in which mice received EXO-RVG-SD208 together with recombinant TGF-β1 (rTGF-β1) to determine whether the protective effects of Exos were dependent on TGF-β1 signaling. EXO-RVG or EXO-RVG-SD208 was administered via tail vein injection at a dose of 200 μg/mouse, with the first injection given 24 h after MCAO/R surgery, followed by injections every 48 h for a total of three doses. In the rescue experiment, rTGF-β1 (Peprotech, USA; 0.5 μg/mouse) was co-administered with Exos [30].

Neurological behavioral assessment

Behavioral assessments were performed on days 1, 3, and 7 after surgery. Neurological deficits were evaluated using the modified neurological severity score (mNSS), which includes motor, sensory, balance, and reflex functions. The total score ranges from 0 to 18, with higher scores indicating more severe neurological impairment. In the balance beam test, mice were placed on a wooden beam (1 m long and 2 cm wide), and the distance of stable walking and latency to fall were recorded. Each rat was tested three times, and the average value was used for analysis. Cognitive function was assessed using the Morris water maze. The training phase lasted for 5 consecutive days, with four trials per day and a maximum duration of 60 s for each trial. On day 6, a spatial probe test was conducted to record the time spent in the target quadrant and the number of platform crossings. All behavioral assessments were performed by investigators blinded to the experimental groups.

Long-term neurological and cognitive assessment

To evaluate the long-term therapeutic effects of EXO-RVG-SD208, behavioral tests were performed on mice in each group (n = 8/group) on postoperative day 28.

For the Grid Walk Test, mice were placed on a wire grid platform (100 cm × 100 cm) with 2.5 cm × 2.5 cm openings, elevated 50 cm above the floor, and allowed to move freely for 4 min. Hindlimb movements were recorded, and the percentage of foot faults, defined as hindlimb slips through the grid, was calculated relative to the total number of steps [31].

For the Nest Building Test, 5 g of standard nesting material was placed in each cage on the evening before testing. After 12 h, nests were scored from 0 to 5 according to the degree of material integration, where 0 indicated untouched material and 5 indicated a well-formed compact nest. This test was used to assess spontaneous survival-related behavior and executive function [32].

For the Y-Maze Spontaneous Alternation Test, mice were placed in the center of a Y-maze with three symmetrical arms and allowed to explore freely for 8 min. The sequence of arm entries was recorded, and spontaneous alternation was calculated as follows: [(number of consecutive entries into three different arms) / (total arm entries − 2)] × 100% [33].

Long-term systemic safety evaluation

On postoperative day 28, blood samples were collected from the retro-orbital venous plexus under anesthesia. One portion was used for complete blood count analysis with an automated hematology analyzer (Alcyon 300, Abbott, USA). The remaining blood was centrifuged to obtain serum for measurement of liver function markers, ALT and AST, as well as renal function markers, including CREA, using an automated biochemical analyzer (Alcyon 300, Abbott, USA).

After behavioral testing, mice were transcardially perfused, and major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, were collected and fixed in 10% formalin. Tissues were then dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, sectioned at 5 μm, and stained with H&E. Histopathological changes, including inflammatory infiltration, necrosis, and fibrosis, were evaluated under light microscopy in a blinded manner by an experienced pathologist [34].

Brain tissue fixation and collection

After behavioral testing, animals were euthanized on postoperative day 7 by overdose anesthesia (1% sodium pentobarbital, 80 mg/kg, i.p.), followed by transcardial perfusion with 0.9% saline (250 mL) and 4% paraformaldehyde (500 mL, Solarbio, China). Brains were harvested and divided into two portions: one was paraffin-embedded and sectioned at 5 μm for histological analysis, and the other was snap-frozen and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent molecular assays.

Nissl staining

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized and stained with Nissl staining solution (Beyotime, C0117). After incubation at 37 °C for 30 min, sections were dehydrated, cleared, and coverslipped. Neuronal morphology and arrangement in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus were examined under a light microscope (Leica DM3000, Germany), and changes in Nissl body integrity and neuronal density were recorded.

Immunohistochemical staining

Paraffin sections were dewaxed, and antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (P0081, Beyotime, China) at 95 °C for 20 minutes. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% H2O2, followed by blocking with 5% BSA (SW3015, Solarbio, China) for 1 hour. Primary antibodies were then applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies included NeuN (Abcam, ab177487, 1:200), GFAP (Abcam, ab7260, 1:500), PSD95 (Abcam, ab18258, 1:250), C3 (Abcam, ab200999, 1:350), p-Smad2/3 (Abcam, ab272332, 1:350), TGF-β1 (Abcam, ab215715, 1:250), and S100A10 (Abcam, ab76472, 1:250). The next day, sections were washed with PBS and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (ZSGB-Bio, China). DAB (P0203, Beyotime, China) was used for chromogenic detection, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining (C0105M, Beyotime, China). Images were captured from the same field of view and analyzed using ImageJ to calculate the percentage of positive staining area.

WB

Proteins were extracted from cells or ischemic cortical tissue using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime) and quantified with a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher, USA). Equal amounts of protein (20 μg per sample) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against TGF-β1 (Abcam, ab92486), Smad2 (CST, 5339), Smad3 (CST, 9523), GFAP (Abcam, ab7260), C3 (Abcam, ab200999), and S100A10 (Abcam, ab76472). Protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL), and band intensity was quantified with ImageJ. Protein expression was normalized to β-actin (Table S1).

Immunofluorescence staining

Primary neurons were seeded onto glass coverslips placed in 24-well plates (3526, Corning, USA). After fixation, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100. Primary antibodies bind specific antigens, followed by detection with FITC-conjugated secondary antibodies (ab6785, Abcam, USA). Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMi8, Leica Microsystems, Germany).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues and cells using TRIzol reagent (A33254, Thermo Fisher, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (RR047A, Takara, Japan). Quantitative PCR was performed using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq II Kit (DRR081, Takara, Japan) on an ABI 7500 real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher, USA). The amplification program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. GAPDH served as the internal control. Each reaction was performed in triplicate and repeated in three independent experiments. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Phagocytosis assay of astrocytes

Phagocytic activity of astrocytes was evaluated using the pHrodo™ Green S. aureus BioParticles® Conjugate (Thermo Fisher, P35361). OGD/R model astrocytes were seeded in 96-well plates. The experimental groups were consistent with previous assays: Control, OGD/R, OGD/R + EXO-RVG, OGD/R + EXO-RVG-SD208, and Rescue (OGD/R + EXO-RVG-SD208 + rTGF-β1). pHrodo™ BioParticles were resuspended in serum-free medium and added to each well at a final concentration of 5 μM, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. Cells were then washed three times with PBS to remove unphagocytosed particles. Cultured cells were sampled and subjected to immunofluorescence staining, and phagocytosis was assessed using fluorescence microscopy. To quantify the phagocytic function of astrocytes, the area of the red fluorescence signal (μm2) was measured, and this value was defined as the phagocytic index [35, 36].

Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) detection

BV2 and B6M7 cells were seeded into XFe 96-well microplates (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA) at a density of 6,000 cells per well. The extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) was measured in real-time using a Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer (Agilent Technologies) with the Glycolysis Stress Test Kit. Final data were normalized to cell numbers determined by the YO-PRO®-1 assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) [37].

Statistical analysis

All data were derived from at least three independent experiments and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). An unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to compare the two groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among three or more groups. If ANOVA indicated significant differences, Tukey’s HSD post hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons. The Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis H test was used for data not meeting the assumptions of normality or homogeneity of variance. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc.) and R version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, whereas p ≥ 0.05 was insignificant.

We performed an intersection analysis between the DEGs and a curated set of IS-related genes from the GeneCards database to identify further functionally relevant genes associated with IS. This analysis identified 15 co-expressed key genes (Fig. 1E), suggesting their potential central regulatory roles during the MCAO/R process. These genes were further submitted to the STRING database to construct a PPI network. Among them, Tgfb1 was located at the center of the network and showed strong interactions with multiple structural and inflammatory molecules, including Vwf, Fga, Apoe, and Gfap (Fig. 1F). Node degree analysis further demonstrated that Tgfb1 had the highest connectivity, with eight direct interactions, ranking first among all candidate genes (Fig. 1G). This finding suggests that Tgfb1 may act as a central hub molecule in post-stroke regulation.

Taken together, these transcriptomic data identify Tgfb1 as a markedly upregulated and highly connected hub gene after stroke, supporting its potential role in astrocyte-associated responses and post-stroke neural repair.

To further explore alterations in intercellular communication networks after IS, we employed CellChat to analyze cell-cell communication patterns between the Sham and MCAO/R groups. The MCAO/R group showed an overall increase in both the number and strength of intercellular interactions, with particularly enhanced communication between immune-related cells, such as monocytes and macrophages, and glial cells, including astrocytes and oligodendrocytes (Fig. 2F–I).

We then focused on astrocyte-centered communication patterns. In the MCAO/R group, interaction strength between astrocytes and multiple surrounding cell types was markedly enhanced (Fig. 2J–K). Among these, astrocyte–endothelial cell signaling showed the most prominent increase, suggesting a potential role for astrocytes in regulating blood-brain barrier function and cerebrovascular remodeling after stroke. In addition, the strengthened communication between astrocytes and immune cells may contribute to inflammatory activation and glial scar formation.

To further define cellular state transitions, we performed pseudotime analysis using Monocle3. The reconstructed developmental trajectory revealed a clear spatial distribution of different cell populations along the pseudotime axis (Fig. 2L–M). Astrocytes, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells were enriched in earlier developmental states, whereas immune-related cells, including monocytes and neutrophils, were mainly located at terminal states, suggesting their involvement in later inflammatory and tissue remodeling processes after stroke.

Analysis of Tgfb1 expression across cell types further showed that Tgfb1 was predominantly expressed in astrocytes and exhibited a broader activation pattern in the MCAO/R group than in the Sham group (Fig. 2N). These data indicate that Tgfb1 is selectively upregulated in astrocytes under ischemic stress.

In conclusion, the cell communication analysis revealed that astrocytes are a central signaling hub following stroke in the MCAO/R model, with enhanced interactions across multiple cell types. These interactions likely contribute to neuroinflammation, vascular responses, and tissue repair processes, providing a mechanistic basis for developing astrocyte-targeted therapeutic strategies.

Successful construction of brain-targeted EXO-RVG-SD208

In this study, we successfully constructed an engineered Exo system capable of brain-targeted delivery of the TGF-β1 inhibitor SD208, designated as EXO-RVG-SD208. The overall construction strategy and schematic mechanism are shown in Fig. 3A. Human astrocyte HA1800 cells were cultured under Exo-depleted conditions, and Exos were isolated from the conditioned medium by ultracentrifugation combined with SEC. TEM showed that the isolated Exos displayed the typical round or cup-shaped morphology with intact membrane structures (Figure S1A). Nanoparticle tracking analysis showed an average particle diameter of approximately 110 nm with a narrow size distribution (Figure S1B). The zeta potential was negative, indicating good colloidal stability (Figure S1C). Subsequently, we employed ultrasound-assisted encapsulation to load the small-molecule inhibitor SD208 into Exos and modified the Exo membrane with DSPE-PEG-RVG via lipid insertion to confer brain-targeting capability, forming the EXO-RVG-SD208 system. TEM imaging showed that the engineered Exos retained a typical round or cup-shaped morphology with an intact structure (Fig. 3B). Compared with unmodified Exos, EXO-RVG-SD208 exhibited a slight increase in particle size (Fig. 3C), whereas zeta potential remained largely unchanged (Fig. 3D), indicating that the modification process had minimal effects on vesicle stability.

We next assessed the cellular uptake and in vivo targeting ability of EXO-RVG-SD208. DiI-labeled EXO-RVG-SD208 was incubated with HA1800 astrocytes, and confocal microscopy showed stronger intracellular red fluorescence in the EXO-RVG-SD208 group than in the unmodified EXO group, indicating enhanced cellular uptake after RVG modification (Fig. 3E). In addition, DiR-labeled Exos were intravenously injected into MCAO/R mice for in vivo imaging. Compared with the EXO group, EXO-RVG-SD208 produced stronger and more concentrated fluorescence signals in the brain, indicating enhanced accumulation in ischemic brain tissue (Fig. 3F).

To determine whether SD208 released from Exos could effectively inhibit its downstream target pathway, we used a TGF-β response element (TRE)-luciferase reporter system to evaluate TGF-β/Smad transcriptional activity in astrocytes under different treatment conditions (Fig. 3G). OGD/R markedly increased TRE luciferase activity compared with the Control group, whereas EXO-RVG alone had no significant effect. Free SD208 partially reduced pathway activity, but the inhibitory effect was limited. In contrast, EXO-RVG-SD208 significantly suppressed TRE luciferase activity and showed a markedly stronger inhibitory effect than free SD208. Importantly, this suppression was substantially reversed by exogenous rTGF-β1, indicating that the regulatory effect of EXO-RVG-SD208 primarily depends on TGF-β1 signaling. Consistently, WB analysis showed that OGD/R markedly increased p-Smad2/3 levels, whereas EXO-RVG-SD208 significantly reduced Smad2/3 phosphorylation. This effect was partially abolished by rTGF-β1 rescue, further confirming that SD208 acts through inhibition of the TGF-β1/Smad pathway (Fig. 3H–I).

To further validate the specific contribution of RVG to brain targeting, we performed a competitive inhibition experiment. In vivo imaging showed that EXO-RVG-SD208 alone generated strong fluorescence signals in the brain, whereas preincubation with excess free RVG peptide (Competition group) markedly reduced brain fluorescence intensity (Fig. 3J). Similarly, non-modified EXO-SD208 exhibited substantially weaker brain accumulation than EXO-RVG-SD208. Quantitative analysis confirmed that EXO-RVG-SD208 showed the highest brain fluorescence intensity, while both the Competition and non-modified groups displayed significantly lower signals, with no significant difference between them (Fig. 3K). These results demonstrate that RVG modification is essential for the efficient and specific brain targeting of engineered Exos after stroke.

These results demonstrate that EXO-RVG-SD208 exhibits favorable particle size distribution, marker expression, cellular uptake efficiency, and stroke-specific brain-targeting ability. The successful construction of this engineered Exo system capable of delivering the TGF-β1 inhibitor lays a solid foundation for subsequent functional intervention experiments.

Material stability, drug release, and in vitro/in vivo biocompatibility evaluation

We first established an experimental workflow to evaluate the material stability, drug release behavior, and in vitro/in vivo biocompatibility of the EXO-RVG-SD208 (Figure S2A).

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis showed that EXO-RVG-SD208 maintained good colloidal stability in PBS, 10% FBS, and complete DMEM, with no obvious change in particle size over 24 h (Figure S2B). Consistently, the zeta potential remained stable between −18 and −20 mV, indicating that the vesicles preserved their structural integrity under different physiological conditions (Figure S2C). HPLC further showed that the encapsulation efficiency of SD208 reached 72.4%, with a drug loading content of 18.9 μg/mg protein, demonstrating efficient incorporation of the inhibitor into Exos. Drug release studies performed under pH 7.4 and pH 5.5 conditions, representing physiological and pathological microenvironments, respectively, revealed that SD208 was released more rapidly at pH 5.5 than at pH 7.4. Fitting of the release curves suggested consistency with the Higuchi diffusion model and biphasic release kinetics, indicating that EXO-RVG-SD208 exhibits pH-responsive release behavior that may favor drug delivery in the lesion microenvironment (Figure S2D).

In the in vitro biocompatibility experiments, we evaluated the cytotoxicity of EXO, EXO-RVG, and EXO-RVG-SD208. CCK-8 viability assays showed that none of the three groups exhibited significant cytotoxicity toward HA1800 astrocytes or primary cortical neurons, with cell viability maintained above 90% (Figure S3A). Similarly, LDH release assays detected no significant differences among groups, indicating that membrane integrity remained intact (Figure S3B). Annexin V/PI double staining further demonstrated that EXO-RVG-SD208 did not significantly increase the proportion of apoptotic cells compared with the Control group, supporting its favorable compatibility with CNS cells at the effective concentration tested (Figure S3C). For in vivo safety evaluation, C57BL/6 mice were administered EXO-RVG-SD208 via tail vein injection and monitored for seven days. Blood routine and hepatic/renal function tests showed no significant abnormalities in leukocyte count, ALT, AST, or creatinine levels. H&E staining of major organs (brain, liver, spleen, and kidneys) revealed intact tissue architecture with no signs of inflammation or necrosis (Figure S3D). These findings collectively support the favorable in vivo biosafety profile of EXO-RVG-SD208.

In the in vitro mechanistic validation experiments, we established an OGD/R-induced astrocyte model to investigate the regulatory effects of the engineered Exo on the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway and astrocytic phenotype (Fig. 5E). Experimental groups included Control, OGD/R, OGD/R + EXO-RVG, OGD/R + EXO-RVG-SD208, and Rescue (EXO-RVG-SD208 + rTGF-β1).

WB analysis showed that TGF-β1 and phosphorylated Smad2/3 levels were significantly increased in the OGD/R group compared with the Control group, indicating activation of the TGF-β1/Smad pathway after ischemic injury. Relative to the OGD/R + EXO-RVG group, EXO-RVG-SD208 markedly suppressed this pathway, whereas reactivation was observed in the Rescue group, suggesting that exogenous TGF-β1 could partially reverse the inhibitory effect of EXO-RVG-SD208 on pathway activation (Fig. 5F).

We next examined astrocyte phenotypic changes by WB and immunofluorescence. Compared with the Control group, OGD/R significantly increased the expression of the A1-type markers GFAP and C3 while reducing the A2-type marker S100A10, indicating a shift toward a pro-inflammatory astrocytic phenotype. Compared with the OGD/R + EXO-RVG group, EXO-RVG-SD208 more effectively decreased GFAP and C3 expression and significantly increased S100A10 expression, suggesting that loading the TGF-β1 inhibitor enhanced the phenotypic regulatory capacity of the engineered Exo. In the Rescue group, GFAP and C3 levels increased again, whereas S100A10 expression declined, further indicating that this phenotypic remodeling depended on inhibition of the TGF-β1 signaling pathway (Fig. 5G–H).

Consistently, the dual-luciferase reporter assay showed that OGD/R markedly increased the activity of TGF-β-responsive elements. This increase was significantly suppressed by EXO-RVG-SD208, with a stronger inhibitory effect than that of EXO-RVG alone, whereas pathway activity was restored in the Rescue group (Fig. 5I).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that EXO-RVG-SD208 suppresses OGD/R-induced activation of the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting the A1 astrocytic phenotype and promoting the transition toward an A2 reparative phenotype. This regulatory effect appears to be pathway-dependent rather than a nonspecific consequence of exosome delivery.

To further verify whether the EXO-RVG-SD208-mediated phenotypic remodeling of astrocytes specifically depended on the TGF-β1/Smad pathway, we established a mechanistic validation model using siRNA to silence TGF-β1 expression (Figure S4A). TGF-β1 expression was examined 48 h after transfection to verify knockdown efficiency. RT-qPCR showed that TGF-β1 mRNA levels were significantly reduced in the si-TGF-β1–1 group compared with the NC-siRNA group (Figure S4B–C). Consistently, WB analysis confirmed a corresponding reduction in TGF-β1 protein expression, indicating effective gene silencing for subsequent mechanistic studies.

WB results further showed that TGF-β1 and p-Smad2/3 levels were markedly increased in the OGD/R group compared with the Control group, confirming activation of the TGF-β1/Smad pathway after ischemic injury. Compared with the OGD/R + EXO-RVG group, EXO-RVG-SD208 significantly reduced the expression of TGF-β1 and p-Smad2/3. This inhibitory effect was even more pronounced in the OGD/R + si-TGF-β1 group, whereas total Smad2/3 levels remained unchanged, indicating specific blockade of the pathway (Figure S4D).

At the phenotypic level, EXO-RVG-SD208 reduced the expression of the A1-type markers GFAP and C3 while increasing the A2-type marker S100A10 compared with the OGD/R group. A similar but stronger trend was observed in the si-TGF-β1 group (Figure S4E), further supporting that the mechanism of EXO-RVG-SD208 is dependent on TGF-β1 signaling inhibition.

Consistent with these findings, dual-luciferase reporter assays showed that OGD/R markedly increased TGF-β response element (CAGA-Luc) activity, whereas EXO-RVG-SD208 significantly suppressed this response compared with EXO-RVG alone. Pathway activity was further reduced in the si-TGF-β1 group (Figure S4F). Together, these results validate at both the signaling and phenotypic levels that EXO-RVG-SD208 remodels astrocyte phenotype through specific inhibition of the TGF-β1/Smad pathway.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that EXO-RVG-SD208 promotes the transition of astrocytes from the A1 phenotype to the A2 phenotype by inhibiting TGF-β1/Smad signaling, thereby facilitating neuronal functional recovery in a pathway-dependent manner.

Discussion

The role of TGF-β1 signaling in CNS disorders has received increasing attention in recent years [16], particularly for its dual role in post-stroke inflammation and neural repair [17]. Previous studies have primarily focused on the neuroprotective effects of TGF-β1 during the acute phase of stroke while overlooking its sustained upregulation in the subacute and chronic phases, which may impede the repair process [12, 38]. In this study, multi-group RNA-seq analyses revealed that TGF-β1 remained highly expressed throughout the stroke, indicating its temporally persistent biological activity and underscoring the need for increased attention. We further confirmed that astrocytes are the primary source of TGF-β1 expression. Combined with pathway inhibition experiments, these findings support a central role for TGF-β1 in sustaining neuroinflammation and impairing neuronal synaptic function after stroke. This observation challenges the traditional view that TGF-β1 acts mainly within a limited temporal window and instead suggests that it may represent an important therapeutic target during the chronic phase of stroke recovery.

Astrocytes exhibit high plasticity, and their reactive states can shift from a neurotrophic A2 phenotype to a neurotoxic A1 phenotype following stroke [39]. Although several pro-inflammatory cytokines have been reported to induce the A1 phenotype, the upstream driving mechanisms remain unclear [40, 41]. This study demonstrated that elevated TGF-β1 significantly activates Smad2/3 signaling, thereby promoting or maintaining the A1 phenotype in astrocytes. In contrast, inhibition of this pathway facilitated the transition toward the A2 phenotype, which was accompanied by reduced inflammation and an improved neuronal microenvironment. These findings not only clarify an upstream mechanism governing A1/A2 phenotype switching but also suggest a potential therapeutic strategy that shifts the focus from simple cytokine neutralization to targeted modulation of intracellular signaling pathways.

Astrocytes play a role in inflammation regulation and contribute critically to synapse formation and the release of neurotrophic factors during neural function reconstruction [42, 43]. Previous studies have often overlooked the direct interaction between astrocytes and neurons and their impact on synaptic function [44, 45]. In the present study, we established a neuron-astrocyte co-culture system and demonstrated that inhibition of the TGF-β1 pathway in astrocytes significantly enhanced neuronal synaptic protein expression and electrophysiological activity, indicating a shift in astrocyte function from a detrimental state to a reparative one. Importantly, these effects were reversed by rTGF-β1 supplementation or Smad3 overexpression, further confirming the pathway specificity of this mechanism. These findings provide both structural and functional evidence that astrocyte-neuron interactions are critical for synaptic remodeling after stroke.

Efficient delivery across the BBB remains a major challenge for stroke therapy [46]. Compared with traditional nanocarriers such as liposomes and polymeric particles, Exos exhibit superior tissue penetration and biocompatibility and have recently emerged as promising vectors for precision therapy in neurological diseases [47, 48]. In this study, we successfully developed a brain-targeted EXO-RVG-SD208 capable of delivering the TGF-β1 inhibitor across the BBB. This system demonstrated excellent physical stability, drug-loading efficiency, and tissue distribution. With its dual advantages of low immunogenicity and high targeting capacity, EXO-RVG-SD208 exhibits strong technological innovation and holds significant potential for clinical application.

A major strength of this study is the integrated application of proteomics, metabolomics, and phosphoproteomics, which together revealed the downstream regulatory network associated with TGF-β1 pathway inhibition. Notably, several pathways related to neural plasticity, including mTOR, MAPK, and BDNF signaling, were significantly activated, suggesting that TGF-β1 may regulate neural repair through multiple interconnected pathways. This multi-omics-based mechanistic analysis not only deepens understanding of the underlying biology but also provides an important resource for identifying additional synergistic therapeutic targets. Compared with conventional single-pathway studies, omics-based approaches are more effective for capturing the complex network changes that occur under pathological conditions and therefore represent an important direction for future neural repair research.

Methodologically, this study combined database mining, engineered vector construction, in vitro and in vivo functional validation, and omics-based mechanistic analysis into a unified framework. This bidirectional strategy, integrating top-down discovery with bottom-up validation, substantially strengthens the reliability of the conclusions compared with studies relying solely on cell-based assays or phenotypic observations in animal models. Moreover, behavioral, immunohistochemical, and histological analyses in the MCAO/R model consistently confirmed the therapeutic efficacy of the intervention in alleviating stroke-induced functional impairment, further supporting its translational potential.

In contrast to the current clinical emphasis on acute thrombolytic therapy for stroke, this study proposes a new intervention strategy based on EXO-RVG-SD208 for post-stroke neural repair. Owing to its nanoscale precision, BBB penetration, and astrocyte-targeting capability, this system may serve as a promising therapeutic platform not only for chronic-stage stroke rehabilitation but also for other CNS injury-related disorders. This potential is particularly meaningful given the limited treatment options and declining neuroplasticity during the chronic phase, highlighting the translational significance of this engineered exosome-based approach.

Although this study functionally validated the specific inhibitory effect of EXO-RVG-SD208 on TGF-β1/Smad signaling through TGF-β response element reporter assays, dynamic changes in p-Smad2/3, and rescue experiments, we did not directly monitor the real-time intracellular release kinetics of SD208 in astrocytes or its molecular interaction with the receptor complex. Future studies incorporating drug-tracing approaches or biophysical analyses will be valuable for further defining the precise mode of action of SD208 within the exosomal delivery system. Nevertheless, given that SD208 has been extensively characterized as a classical inhibitor of the TGF-β type I receptor, the functional and signaling evidence obtained here is sufficient to support that its major biological effects in astrocytes are mediated primarily through the TGF-β1/Smad axis.

In summary, this study systematically elucidates the role of TGF-β1 in astrocyte activation and astrocyte-neuron interactions after stroke. We established a brain-targeted engineered exosome platform, EXO-RVG-SD208, which promoted glial phenotypic transformation and improved functional neural recovery. Our findings demonstrate that TGF-β1 inhibition facilitates neural repair through the regulation of multiple plasticity-related pathways. These results provide both mechanistic insight and a technological framework for post-stroke neural repair, with promising translational potential. Nevertheless, several limitations remain, including the lack of validation in human-derived cells and insufficient long-term safety assessment. Future studies using organoid systems and large-animal models are needed to optimize delivery efficiency and therapeutic outcomes and to further advance this strategy toward clinical application.

Conclusion

Based on the findings of this study, we draw the following preliminary conclusions. EXO-RVG-SD208, through brain-targeted delivery of the TGF-β1 inhibitor SD208, effectively blocked activation of the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway. This inhibition suppressed the shift of astrocytes toward the pro-inflammatory A1 phenotype while promoting the reparative A2 phenotype, thereby enhancing astrocyte-mediated neuronal support, increasing neurotrophic factor expression, improving synaptic function, and ultimately alleviating neuronal injury after IS. The brain-targeting capability, therapeutic efficacy, and pathway dependence of this engineered delivery system were systematically validated at the behavioral, histological, and molecular levels. In addition, siRNA-based experiments further confirmed the specificity of its regulatory mechanism and strengthened the mechanistic basis of the study. By integrating public database mining with experimental validation, this work identifies TGF-β1 as a key regulator of glial activation after stroke and establishes a precision nanodelivery strategy targeting this signaling axis. This approach helps address the current lack of controllable therapeutic strategies for modulating astrocyte-neuron interactions after stroke. Collectively, these findings provide both a potential nanotherapeutic tool and a targeted mechanistic framework for IS repair, supporting its future translational development.
