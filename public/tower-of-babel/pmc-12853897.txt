Replanting alters nitrogen uptake and the expression level of ammonium transporter genes in grapevine
Authors: Yihan Zhang, Xiaotong Fu, Hongyao Jin, Yihan Xu, Kun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractReplanting disease caused stunted grapevines growth, hindering the healthy and sustainable development of the grape industry. Nitrogen metabolism is closely related to plant growth, however the effect of replanting on nitrogen metabolism in grape have seldom been reported. In this study, we used ‘Beta’ grape (Vitis riparia × V. labrusca cv.) cuttings as experimental materials to investigate the nitrogen content, nitrogen uptake behavior, and ammonium transporter genes (AMT) expression levels in grapevines after replanting. The results indicated the total nitrogen content in both the roots and leaves of the replanted grapevines decreased. After replanting, the net influx rate of NO3− into grapevine roots decreased, whereas that of NH4+ increased. The Ndff (Nitrogen derived from fertilizer) of 15N-NH4+ in the roots of replanted grapevines increased, and the 15N-NH4+ distribution ratio in the stems of replanted grapevines decreased compared to that in the CK. In total, 12 AMT genes were identified in the grape genome. The RT-qPCR results revealed that the transcript levels of the 12 VlAMT genes in grapevine roots displayed dynamic changes, and VlAMT2.1 and VlAMT4.1 increased significantly on the 96th d after replanting. Further studies showed that VlAMT4.1 is localized on the plasma membrane and has ammonium transport function. Introduction

Replant diseases results in diminished plant vigor, yield and quality, even under normal management conditions [1, 2]. This phenomenon occurs widely across crops, including forestry [3], field crops [2, 4], medicinal plants [5], vegetables [1] and fruit trees [6–8]. Extensive research has revealed that replant diseases are primarily attributed to microbial imbalance, autotoxicity, and soil nutrient deficiencies [9–12].

Replanting often alters soil physicochemical properties, including total nitrogen and carbon contents, enzyme activities, organic matter, and pH. These parameters interact with each other and regulate soil physicochemical processes and biodiversity, ultimately leading to soil degradation and diminished crop yields [13–15]. For example, Panax notoginseng replanting resulted in decreased soil contents of NH4+-N, NO3−-N, available P, and total N. The survival rate of P. notoginseng was strongly and positively correlated with soil pH, available K, and total N [16]. Strawberry replanting altered the abundance of soil carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus metabolic functional genes, leading to a significant decrease in the carbon fixation gene accA. The nitrogen fixation gene nifH and phosphorus metabolism-related genes (phoD, phoX, and pqqC) exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease. Furthermore, the shifts in fungal community structure induced by strawberry replanting were significantly correlated with the abundance of these carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus functional genes in the soil [17]. Xu et al. [18] reported that long-term continuous cropping of soybean significantly increased soil TN and nitrate N content and significantly decreased soil TP and AP content compared to rotational cropping, which was related to the increased abundance of N metabolism genes, and the abundance of P metabolism genes of soil microorganism.

Several studies have demonstrated that replanting influences the nitrogen metabolism of plants [2, 19]. Wang et al. [20] reported that replanting inhibits the growth and function of roots in Malus hupehensis Rehd. seedlings, thereby reducing nitrogen uptake. Zhang [21] demonstrated that replanting severely reduced nitrogen accumulation in the organs of flue-cured tobacco, and decreased nitrogen fertilizer use efficiency. Furthermore, the autotoxin phenolic acid decreased the contents of nitrate-nitrogen, ammonium-nitrogen, and total nitrogen in poplar leaves as the phenolic acid concentration increased, along with a decline in enzyme activities related to nitrogen metabolism [22]. The autotoxin cinnamic acid inhibited the absorption of N, accompanied by reducing enzyme activities associated with nitrogen metabolism [23].

Grapes are economically important crops that are cultivated worldwide. However, the accelerated pace of variety renewal has led to severe replant diseases in many vineyards during replantation, significantly hindering the healthy development of the grape industry. Our previous research found that grape replanting changed the bacterial and fungal community composition, from a high-fertility ‘bacterial type’ to a low-fertility ‘fungal type’, and the proportion of soil nutrients became imbalanced [24, 25]. However, how the soil environment under replanting stress affected nitrogen metabolism in grape was seldom reported. In the present study, grape cuttings (Vitis riparia×V.labrusca cv. ‘Beta’) were used as experimental materials to study the uptake characteristics of NH4+ and NO3− by replanting grapevine roots. Additionally, the expression levels of VlAMT genes were analyzed and VlAMT 4.1 function was identified. The aim of this study was to elucidate the causes of grapevine growth suppression after replanting from the perspective of nitrogen metabolism.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

Cutting of the grapevine rootstock ‘Beta’ (V.riparia×V.labrusca cv.) were used as materials. The replanted soil was collected from a 4-year-old planting vineyard at Shenyang Agricultural University, Liaoning Province, China. The surface soil was removed, and the soil from the grapevine root zone was collected. Non-replanted soil was obtained from the same vineyard with a scallion rotation. The soil was sieved to remove stones and other debris, thoroughly mixed, and then utilized for the pot experiment. The nutrient content of the tested soil is presented in Table 1.

Experimental treatments and sample collection

The experiment was conducted in an unheated greenhouse of Shenyang Agricultural University. The seedlings of ‘Beta’ planted in nutrient pots (the top diameter of 19 cm, the bottom diameter of 13 cm, and the height of 16 cm) filled with replanted soil and non-replanted soil, designated as replanting treatment (LZ) and blank treatment (CK), respectively. Each pot contained 3 kg of soil, with 48 pots allocated to each treatment, for a total of 96 pots.

After 47 d of planting, 32 pots from each treatment were used for the isotope experiments. Briefly, 16 pots of each treatment were treated with 15NH4NO3 (10 atom%; chemical purity ≥ 98.5%) with 0.28 g and dicyandiamide with 0.0049 g (5% of the nitrogen content of NH4NO3) per pot, while the other 16 pots received NH415NO3 (10 atom%; chemical purity ≥ 98.5%) with 0.28 g and dicyandiamide with 0.0049 g per pot. After 45 d of isotope treatment, the abundance of 15N-NH4 + and 15N-NO3 − in the roots, stems, and leaves was measured. Three seedlings were combined to form a single replicate, with three replicates.

The remaining 16 seedlings pots for the LZ and CK treatments were sampled on d 66, 76, 86 and 96 for measuring the plant height, aboveground and belowground fresh weight, chlorophyll content, and VlAMT4.1 gene expression levels. The ion flux was measured on the 92nd d after treatment. All measured indicators were replicated three times, with each replicate comprising a single seedling, except for height, which was measured for all the remaining seedlings after each sampling.

During sampling, the seedlings were rinsed 2–3 times with tap water, followed by a rinsing with distilled water. The fresh weights of the aboveground and belowground parts of the seedlings were measured separately after the moisture was completely absorbed by the filter paper. Different parts of the seedlings were placed in aluminum foil bags and stored in liquid-nitrogen containers. After all samples were collected, they were stored at − 80℃ for measuring the contents of total nitrogen, nitrate nitrogen and ammonium nitrogen, as well as the expression level of VlAMT4.1 gene.

Determination of plant growth and physiological indices

The plant height was measured from the base of the new shoot to the apical growth point of the stem. The aboveground and belowground fresh weights were assessed using the weighing method.

The total nitrogen content was determined following the method described by Zhang [26] with minor modifications. Briefly, 0.2 g of a dried sample was weighed into a conical flask, followed by the addition of 5 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid. After mixing, the mixture was allowed to stand for 12 h. Then, the flask was placed on a digestion block at 200 °C and digested for 1 h. After slightly cooling, 10 drops of H2O2 were added, and the mixture was shaken thoroughly before digestion was continued for another 20 min. After cooling, an additional 10 drops of H2O2 were added, and the mixture was digested again. This process was repeated until the solution became clear and colorless. After cooling, the digestion solutions were diluted to 50 mL with distilled water, filtered through a filter paper, and then stored for further analysis using a Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (BUCHI Labortechnik AG, Switzerland).

The nitrate nitrogen content was measured according to Patterson et al. [27] following the salicylic acid colorimetric procedure, and the ammonium nitrogen content was measured according to Bräutigam et al. [28], using the indophenol-blue colorimetric method.

Measurement of the ion flow rate on the root surface

The flow rates of NH4+ and NO3− were measured using non-invasive micro-test technology (NMT Physiolyzer, Xuyue Company, Beijing, China) in Zhongguancun NMT Industry Alliance (http://nmtia.org.cn).

The roots were submerged in the corresponding test solutions (CK/LZ treatment: 0.125 mM (NH4)2SO4, 0.06 mM Ca(NO3)2, 0.2 mM MES, pH 5.8) and incubated for 0.5 h. Data were then recorded for 5 min at the root maturation zone (5000 μm from the root tip apex), with eight replicates per group.

Ion flux rates (pmol·cm− 2·s− 1) of NH4+ and NO3− were obtained and processed using the imFluxes V3.0 software. Negative values represent a net influx (from the external medium into the roots), whereas positive values denote efflux (outward movement).

Measurement of isotopic abundance in grapevine

To determine the 15N isotope abundance in the root, stem, and leaf tissues of seedlings, sample pretreatment and measurement methods were as follows: The samples were sequentially rinsed with tap water, detergent, tap water, 1% hydrochloric acid, and deionized water (three times) to thoroughly remove surface contaminants. The cleaned samples were subjected to kill-green treatment at 105 ℃ for 30 min and then dried at 80 ℃ to constant weight. The dried samples were ground using a stainless-steel electric mill and passed through a 0.15 mm sieve to obtain a homogeneous powder.

The 15N isotope abundance was determined using a stable isotope ratio mass spectrometer (Elementar, Germany) coupled with a Vario MICRO cube elemental analyzer (Elementar, Germany).

Identification of AMT gene family members in grape

Using the Arabidopsis thaliana AMT sequences as queries, we performed sequence alignment against grapes (V vinifera cv. ‘Pinot Noir’) proteome using TBtools, with an E-value cutoff of < 1 × 10− 5 and sequence identity ≥ 50%. Candidate AMT sequences from grapevines were further verified by examining for the presence of a conserved AMT domain using the InterPro database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/). The number of amino acids, molecular weight, and theoretical isoelectric point of the identified grapevine AMT proteins were predicted using the ExPASy ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/compute_pi). The genomic locations and conserved domains of the grapevine AMT gene family were obtained from the NCBI for Biotechnology Information database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/taxonomy).

A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining method in the MEGA X software. The analysis included the amino acid sequences of the identified grapevine AMT proteins, along with those of the AMT family members from Arabidopsis and Malus domestica. The AMT sequences of M. domestica were described by Huang et al. [29].

RNA extraction and expression analysis of the VlAMT gene

Total RNA was extracted from grapevine roots at 66, 76, 86, and 96 d after replanting using the CTAB method. RNA quality and concentration were assessed by electrophoresis and Nanodrop ND-2000 spectrophotometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed using the HiScript® III 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). RT-qPCR was performed utilizing the ABI QuantStudio 6 Flex System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The reaction mixture was formulated using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), with three biological replicates for each treatment and three technical replicates for each biological replicate. Gene expression levels were measured using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized with the Ct value of VlActin. To analyze the tissue-specific expression of VlAMT4.1, the roots, stems and leaves were sampled at 66 d under normal conditions, and the primer pairs are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Subcellular localization of VlAMT4

VlAMT4.1 was amplified from the cDNA of ‘Beta’ root. The resulting PCR products were purified and directionally cloned into the pCAMBIA2300-GFP vector using BamHI and SalI restriction enzyme digestion sites to construct the35S:: VlAMT4.1-GFP plasmid, with the 35S: GFP vector serving as a control. The primer sequences and reaction systems used for gene cloning are shown in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3, respectively. The experimental procedure followed the method described by Zang [30].

Yeast functional complementation assay

To construct the pDR196-VlAMT4.1, the coding regions of VlAMT4.1 were connected to the pDR196 vector by SalI and EcoRI sites. The yeast used in the experiment was mutant strain 31019b (Δmep1, Δmep2, Δmep3, and ura3), which cannot grow on the medium containing < 5 mM NH4+ as the sole nitrogen source. The yeast strain was activated using YPDA. When the OD600 of the yeast liquid reached 0.4, it was centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 3 min. The supernatant was discarded, the pellet was resuspended in 40 mL of 1×TE and centrifuged again. The supernatant was discarded, the pellet was resuspended in a mixture of 2 mL of 1×LiAC and 0.5×TE, and transferred to a 2 mL centrifuge tube for storage.

Following the method described by Li et al. [31], the empty vector pDR196 and the recombinant vector pDR196-VlAMT4.1 were separately transformed into the competent 31019b yeast cells. The transformants were spread on YNB solid medium containing 0.17% YNB + 2 mM arginine + 2% D -galactose + 2% agar and incubated at 30 °C for 2–3 d for positive identification.

The successfully identified yeast colonies were cultured in YNB liquid medium at 28 °C overnight with shaking until the OD600 reached approximately 0.5. The cell suspension was washed 3–5 times with sterile water, then adjusted to OD600 = 0.6 with 0.9% NaCl, and centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was diluted 10-, 100-, and 1000-fold with NaCl. Briefly, 5 µL of yeast suspensions at different dilution ratios were inoculated onto selective medium containing different concentrations of ammonium nitrogen and incubated at 30 °C for 3–5 d. The selective medium compositions were as follows: 0.17% YNB (without amino acids and ammonium sulfate) + 2 mM NH4Cl + 2% D-galactose + 2% agar; 0.17% YNB (without amino acids and ammonium sulfate) + 0.2 mM NH4Cl + 2% D-galactose + 2% agar; 0.17% YNB (without amino acids and ammonium sulfate) + 0.02 mM NH4Cl + 2% D-galactose + 2% agar.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were statistically analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 22.0). One-way ANOVA with Duncan’s test was applied for comparisons (p < 0.05 was considered significant), while comparisons between two indicators were performed using the Student’s t-test (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).

Results

The effect of replanting on the growth and nitrogen contents in grape seedlings

According to Fig. 1a, replanting grapevines resulted in reduced growth vigor. After replanting for 66, 76, 86, and 96 d, the seedlings height and fresh weight of the aboveground parts were significantly reduced. Moreover, the root fresh weight of the replanted grapevine was significantly lower than that of the control, except on d 76. Compared with the control, the chlorophyll content of the seedling also showed a decreasing trend, which decreasing by 6.99%, 5.72%, and 8.54% at 76, 86 and 96 d after replanting, respectively. These results demonstrate that replanting impedes the growth of grape seedlings.

AMT gene family identification in grape

To identify AMT family genes in grapes, the Arabidopsis AMT members were used as queries to search the grape genome (Vitis vinifera cv. ‘Pinot Noir’), resulting in the identification of 12 family genes possessing AMT characteristics. Subsequently, a phylogenetic tree was constructed by aligning the protein sequences of these grape AMT members with those of apple (M. domestica Borkh.) and Arabidopsis. Based on the phylogenetic analysis, the grape AMT family was classified into four subfamilies: two members belonged to AMT1, one to AMT2, four to AMT3, and five to AMT4 (Fig. 6).

Following the successful construction of the pDR196-VlAMT4.1 vector, functional validation was performed using yeast 31019b. Complementation assays demonstrated that mutant strain 31019b transformed with the empty vector failed to grow. Discussion

Grape replanting led to an increased uptake of ammonium nitrogen

Nitrogen is an inorganic macronutrient essential for plant growth, development, and biomass production [32]. In most soils, ammonium (NH4+) and nitrate (NO3−) are the predominant inorganic N sources for plants [33]. As concentrations of ammonium (NH4+) are generally very low in well-drained soils, terrestrial plants often take up most of their N as nitrate (NO3−) [34].

Recent studies have shown that stress affects nitrogen uptake. Drury et al. [35]reported that replanting severely impaires nitrogen uptake by corn plants. Zhang et al. [36] found that the net NH4+ flux was higher in the fine roots of Populus. simonii after NaCl treatment compared to the NO3− flux. Huang et al. [37] reported that compared to NO3−, the net influx of NH4+ on the root surface increased in response to drought stress, suggesting that the latter may play a more important role in improving drought tolerance in Malus. prunifolia. Our findings were similar to those of Zhang et al. [36] and Huang et al. [37], the net influx of NH4+ on the root surface increased, while NO3− decreased after grape replanting, and isotopic experiments also revealed an increased NH4+ uptake capacity in grape seedlings after replanting, possibly because ammonium uptake requires less energy [38] and enhanced grapevine tolerance to replanting stress, which may have resulted from the combined effects of nutrient content, autotoxicity and microorganisms, leading to the deterioration of soil function. Studies have indicated that soil organic matter exhibits a highly significant linear correlation with ammonium nitrogen and nitrate nitrogen, and soils rich in organic matter demonstrate stronger gross nitrogen mineralization and nitrification [39, 40].In our study, despite the higher organic matter content in the replanted soils, decreases in available nitrogen, ammonium nitrogen, and nitrate nitrogen were found, implying that the conversion of soil nutrients may be impeded, which is linked to soil microorganisms.

Most of the nitrate absorbed by roots is transported to the aboverground parts and is reduced in mesophyll cells. Most of the free ammonium is catalyzed by the GS/GOGAT cycle in roots [41], and a portion of the ammonium nitrogen can be translocated to the overground and participate in amino acid synthesis [37, 42]. High concentrations of NH4+, partially due to over-uptake by plant cells, are toxic to plant tissues [43].

In the present study, the distribution ratio of 15N-NH4+ in the stems of replanted grapevines decreased significantly, whereas no significant difference was observed in the leaves. This may result from an increased sink strength in the leaves, at the expense of the stems, regulated by replanting seedlings to fulfill their metabolic requirements under stress conditions.

The VlAMT genes likely plays a role in ammonium nitrogen uptake of grapevine

It is evident that the uptake of NH4+-N from the soil by plant is regulated by NH4+ transporter genes (AMT) [44]. AMT family have been identified in a variety of plants, such as A. thaliana, rice, apple, tomato [29, 45–47] and can be divided into two major classes: AMT1 and AMT2, and in some plants, the AMT2 subfamily can be further classified into AMT2, AMT3, AMT4, and other categories based on phylogenetic branching because of their large number of proteins [48]. In Athaliana, the AMT family comprises six isoform: AtAMT1;1-AtAMT1;5 and ATAMT2;1 [45, 49].

Numerous studies have demonstrated the role of AMT genes in abiotic stress responses. Zhang et al. [50] reported that salt treatment significantly up-regulated the expression of all AMT genes in fine roots, with the exception of AMT1.1 and AMT3.2. Notably, AMT1.2 and AMT4.1 expression increased by more than threefold compared to that in the control. Salt stress increased the mRNA expression of AMT1.1 in Solanum lycopersicon, whereas the expression level of AMT1.2 was strongly reduced after 7 d of salt treatment [51]. Overexpression of the Puccinellia tenuiflora gene PutAMT1;1 in Arabidopsis significantly improved salt tolerance during the early root growth stage after seed germination [52], and drought stress affected the expression of genes involved in the uptake of NH4+ (AMT1;2, AMT1;5, and AMT4;2) in M. prunifolia [37]. The expression of two ammonium transporter genes, AMT1;2 and AMT1;6 in Populus simonii was also up-regulated in response to drought stress [53].

However, research on the AMT genes in grapevine roots under replanting stress has rarely been conducted. In our study, 12 AMT genes in the grape genome were identified, among which 2 belong to the AMT1 subtype, 1 belongs to the AMT2 subtype, 4 belong to the AMT3 subtype, and 5 belong to the AMT4 subtype, and RT-qPCR analysis revealed that the expression patterns of 12 VlAMT genes in ‘Beta’ grape varied after grape replanting, and among them, the expression levels of VlAMT2.1 and VlAMT4.1 genes were significantly higher than those in the control group on the 96th day of replanting.The result suggested that grapevine may possess a highly sophisticated AMT regulatory network to precisely control ammonium uptake for adapting replant stress. Cluster analysis revealed a close phylogenetic relationship between VlAMT4.1 and MdAMT4.1 or MdAMT4.2. Under drought or alkaline stress, MdAMT4.2 gene expression was up-regulated in the roots [54, 55].

Under adverse conditions, plants often have a limited energy supply [56], and ammonium assimilation requires less energy than nitrate [38]. The upregulation of AMT genes may help plants acquire nitrogen more economically and efficiently when energy was constrained, thereby maintaining life activities. However, high ammonium concentrations can inhibit plant growth [57].

Studies have shown that the expression levels of plant AMT genes were modulated not only by soil nitrogen availability [58, 59] but also by drought stress [37], salt stress [50], and the function of soil microorganisms [60]. In this study, whether the altered nitrogen uptake behavior of grapevines under replanting stress was influenced by soil nitrogen levels or resulted from changes in soil microecological functions, influenced by multiple factors, requires further in-depth investigation.

Our combined results allow us to propose a possible model for nitrogen uptake in replanted grapevines (Fig. 9). Replanting stress suppressed nitrate uptake and promoted ammonium uptake. However, this enhanced ammonium uptake may be insufficient to meet growth demands, resulting in a lower total nitrogen content. In addition, the excessive absorption of ammonium may cause ammonium toxicity, which further suppresses seedlings growth.

A possible model of nitrogen uptake in replanting grapevine

Conclusion

Replanting stress reprogrammed nitrogen uptake in grapevine roots by suppressing nitrate uptake while favoring ammonium absorption. In the grape genome,12 AMT genes were identified, and the transcript levels of 12 VlAMT genes in grapevine roots displayed dynamic changes. Notably, VlAMT4.1 was localized on the plasma membrane and exhibited ammonium transport function. Although the root exhibited increasing ammonium uptake under replanting stress, the nitrogen accumulation in the seedlings declined, failing to meet the growth requirements.
