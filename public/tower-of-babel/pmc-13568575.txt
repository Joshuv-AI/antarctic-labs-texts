Conserved miRNA regulators of PD-1/PD-L1 in glioblastoma and colorectal cancer
Authors: Alja Zottel, Maša Ždralević, Janja Rašović, Andrijana Radović, Ivana Jovčevska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis have transformed cancer therapy, but their efficacy remains limited in glioblastoma (GBM) and heterogeneous in colorectal cancer (CRC). MicroRNAs (miRNAs) regulate gene expression at the post-transcriptional level, including immune checkpoint molecules, yet conserved regulatory miRNA networks across distinct cancers remain poorly defined. Five conserved miRNAs (miR-106a-5p, miR-106b-5p, miR-20a-5p, miR-20b-5p, miR-138-5p) fulfilled the selection criteria and were consistently dysregulated in GBM and CRC. MiR-106a-5p and miR-106b-5p were upregulated in both cancers and showed favourable prognostic associations, with higher expression correlating with improved survival. miR-20a-5p and miR-20b-5p were preferentially expressed in microsatellite-stable (MSS) CRC and correlated with favourable outcomes in both cancers, whereas miR-138-5p was downregulated in both tumours compared to normal tissue, but showed opposite survival associations, with higher levels linked to worse prognosis. Correlation analysis revealed significant inverse associations between several miRNAs and checkpoint gene expression, including moderate inverse correlations for CD274–miR-106a-5p in GBM, CD274–miR-20a-5p in CRC and PDCD1LG2–miR-20a-5p in both cancers. Pan-cancer profiling demonstrated broad and heterogeneous dysregulation, with expression absent in ovarian cancer for four of the five miRNAs. Pathway enrichment implicated the TGF-β, Hippo, FoxO, and cell cycle pathways, consistent with their known roles in tumour immune evasion. We identified a conserved set of miRNAs that are dysregulated in both GBM and CRC, correlate with survival, and display inverse relationships with PD-1/PD-L1/PD-L2 expression. These miRNAs represent candidate regulators of the PD-1/PD-L1/PD-L2 axis and potential biomarkers of tumour biology that may influence immune checkpoint signalling.

Background

Immunotherapy has revolutionized clinical oncology and has brought substantial success in several cancers, such as melanoma, lung cancer, and renal-cell carcinoma [1–3]. The most common immunotherapy agents are immune-checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), which restore suppressed anti-tumour immunity and induce durable responses in several cancer types [4]. The most common immune checkpoints are programmed death-1 (PD-1), its ligand PD-L1, and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4). However, the efficacy of ICIs varies substantially across malignancies. One of the cancers that is largely resistant to ICIs is glioblastoma, the most common primary brain tumour and one of the deadliest cancers. The resistance to ICIs has been attributed to the highly immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment (TME), which is characterized by low levels of effector T-cell infiltration and the enrichment of immunosuppressive populations, such as regulatory T cells (Tregs), tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [5]. By promoting neutrophil expansion and depleting multiple B-cell populations, accelerating mesenchymal type GBM progression, GBM profoundly reshapes bone and marrow-associated immune environments and negates the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy [6]. In contrast, colorectal cancer (CRC) demonstrates heterogeneous responses: microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) tumours respond favourably to ICIs, while the majority of microsatellite stable (MSS) tumours show little to no benefit [7]. GBM and CRC represent distinct patterns of ICI response. GBM remains almost universally resistant to immunotherapy, whereas CRC demonstrates striking heterogeneity, with MSI-H tumours responding favourably and MSS tumours showing little benefit. One of the mechanisms of ICs regulation is via miRNA.

miRNAs are small, highly evolutionary conserved non-coding RNAs that function as post-transcriptional regulators of gene expression. They bind to complementary sequences in mRNAs and lead to either translational repression or cleavage of the mRNA, resulting in reduced levels of the target protein [8]. miRNAs play crucial roles in cell growth, differentiation, development, and apoptosis [9]. In cancer, dysregulated miRNA expression contributes to several hallmarks of malignancy, including cell cycle progression, proliferation, metastasis, invasion, EMT, apoptosis, DNA repair, angiogenesis, metabolic reprogramming, drug resistance, and immune modulation [10]. In cancer, miRNAs can act as both tumour suppressors and promoters. Increasing evidence indicates that miRNAs are also critical modulators of antitumor immunity, as they shape immune cell differentiation, cytokine signalling, and tumour – immune interactions [11]. Lately, miRNA-seq technologies have enhanced our understanding of gene regulation mechanisms by providing detailed expression profiles and discovering new miRNAs. This has also led to the evolution of tools and algorithms for predicting miRNA targets and interactions, such as miRanda, miRBase, TargetScan, PicTar, TarBase, and miSTAR, to name a few [9]. Most of the current findings of miRNA targeting ICs have been reported in the context of individual tumour types or single miRNA–checkpoint interactions, leaving the open question of whether shared miRNA networks regulate IC expression across diverse malignancies. Addressing this gap is essential, as the discovery of shared regulatory miRNAs could yield broadly applicable biomarkers of response or resistance to ICIs and uncover novel therapeutic targets with pan-cancer relevance.

Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to identify miRNAs that regulate IC genes and to determine whether such regulatory interactions are conserved across GBM and CRC. By investigating these contrasting cancers together, we aim to identify miRNAs that consistently regulate IC pathways, potentially identifying conserved miRNA candidates associated with immune checkpoint pathways across biologically distinct tumour types. Given the exploratory nature of this work, the aim was not to establish direct mechanistic regulation, but rather to identify conserved miRNA candidates associated with immune checkpoint pathways across distinct tumour types and prioritize them for future functional validation. To achieve this, we first investigated candidate miRNAs predicted or experimentally validated to target IC molecules and examined their expression patterns in GBM and CRC, comparing tumour tissues with their normal counterparts and across different disease stages. We then assessed the prognostic significance of these miRNAs and analysed their correlation with the expression of IC genes. Finally, to evaluate the broader relevance of our findings, we extended the analysis to a pan-cancer setting and performed functional enrichment of miRNA target genes to uncover biological pathways potentially contributing to immune evasion and therapeutic resistance.

Results

Selection of candidate miRNAs was performed by searching four relevant databases, in particular miRTarBase, miRWalk, TarBase, miRDB. miRNAs found in at least three of these databases were selected for further analysis. Total of 19 miRNAs were identified as potential regulators of CD274, four of PDCD1, and five5 of PDCD1LG2, with overlaps represented in Figure 1. Five miRNAs – miR-106a-5p, miR-106b-5p, miR-20a-5p, miR-20b-5p and miR-138-5p, emerged as robust candidates, being recurrent across datasets and fulfilling the filtering criteria. miRNA expression across tumors

Pan-cancer profiling revealed broad but heterogeneous expression for all candidate miRNAs.

MiR-106a-5p is expressed across all cancers analysed, except for ovarian cancer and soft tissue sarcoma (Figure 8), with highest expression in CRC and melanoma. MiR-106b-5p is expressed in all cancers, with the highest expression in mature B-cell lymphoma, non-small cell lung cancer, CRC and uterine endometrioid carcinoma. The lowest expression is noted in ovarian cancer. The highest expression of miR-20a-5p is noted in mature B-cell lymphoma, while the expression is absent in ovarian cancer. The highest expression of miR-20b-5p is in melanoma, while it is not expressed in ovarian cancer and soft tissue sarcoma. The highest expression of miR-138-5p is in thyroid cancer. High expression is also observed in melanoma and glioma, while it is absent in various cancers (bladder, breast, endometrial, esophagogastric, hepatobiliary, ovarian, and prostate cancer, as well as mature B-cell lymphoma, soft tissue sarcoma and uterine endometrioid carcinoma). Discussion

In this study, a set of five candidate miRNAs, miR-106a-5p, miR-106b-5p, miR-20a-5p, miR-20b-5p, and miR-138-5p, targeting selected ICs in GBM and CRC were identified using publicly available miRNA databases. As illustrated in Figure 9, multi-cohort transcriptomic datasets were then used to assess differential expression, survival association, gene–miRNA correlations, pan-cancer patterns and pathway enrichment. miR-106a-5p, miR-106b-5p, miR-20a-5p, miR-20b-5p, and miR-138-5p that are predicted to target PD-1/PD-L1/PD-L2 are dysregulated in both GBM and CRC. They showed reproducible differences between tumour and normal tissue, significant associations with OS and/or DFS, and largely inverse correlations with CD274 and PDCD1/PDCDLG2 expressions across multiple independent datasets. Diverse immunological and prognostic roles of miR-17/92 family members in GBM and CRC

A major observation is that miR-106a-5p, miR-106b-5p, miR-20a-5p and miR-20b-5p, all members or paralogs of the miR-17/92 family, showed broadly similar patterns in survival: higher expression correlated with better prognosis in the cohorts. Moreover, miR-106a-5p and miR-106b-5p were both overexpressed in GBM vs normal tissues as well as WHO3 and LGG. There was a discrepancy between CRC and GBM in miR-20b expression, as it was downregulated in CRC and upregulated in GBM.

In CRC, miR-20a-5p is known to exhibit immunomodulatory activity. Ghofrani-Shahpar et al. showed that cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) secrete exosomal miR-20a-5p, which targets PTEN and stimulates interleukin-6 (IL-6) production, thereby activating inflammatory and pro-survival signalling cascades that can reshape immune cell recruitment and suppress antitumor immunity [13]. This enhanced inflammatory signalling could modulate the response to ICs. In addition, miR-20b-5p was shown to contribute to immune evasion and resistance to cetuximab in CRC via HCG18/miR-20b-5p/PD-L1 axis [14]. Since miR-20b-5p levels were significantly elevated in the plasma of CRC patients, it was proposed as a potential diagnostic biomarker [15]. On the other hand, miR-20b-5p was shown to have a tumour-suppressive role, via direct negative regulation of cyclin D expression, thereby inhibiting cell cycle, migration, and invasion in vitro, and tumorigenesis in in vivo models [16]. We have shown that higher miR-20b-5p expression is correlated with the better DFS and OS in CRC, in line with the findings by Ulivi et al. [17].

In our data, miR-106a-5p and miR-106b-5p emerged as favourable prognostic markers: higher expression was associated with improved OS and DFS in both CRC and GBM. Although their prognostic signal derives from a ratio rather than the absolute miR-106a-5p level, studies from Fu et al. and Valladares-Ayerbes et al. converge on the concept that increased relative abundance of miR-106a-5p is associated with more favourable clinical outcome. Our data show significantly higher miR-106a-5p levels in GBM compared with LGG and normal tissues highlight a strong grade-associated pattern that differs from the tumour-suppressive activity described by Zhi et al. in astrocytoma [18]. On the other hand, oncogenic roles of miR-106a-5p have been described [19], including the study by Liang et al., who showed that highly metastatic CRC cells secrete exosomes enriched in miR-106a-5p, which drive macrophage M2 polarization [20]. There were conflicting findings in GBM as well. Zhao et al. found that the higher serum miR-106a-5p predicted poorer OS and DFS [21]. miR-106a-5p was also identified as part of a predicted ceRNA regulatory axis (chr22-38_28785274–29,006,793.1–miR-106a-5p-DDHD1) associated with CD4+ T-cell relevant genes in colon cancer [22]. This suggests that miR-106a-5p may modulate T-cell infiltration or function in the TME, thereby implicating it in immune regulation within that context. This supports a model in which diminished miR-106a-5p activity contributes to tumour progression and may shape the TME by promoting a more aggressive cellular phenotype. In contrast to our findings, lower miR-106a-5p levels were shown to be associated with more advanced clinical staging and poorer survival outcomes [18].

Favourable prognostic role of miR-106b-5p, is in line with its tumour-suppressive role in CRC either by inhibiting its activity by the lncRNA PVT1, which functions as a ceRNA to sequester miR-106b-5p [23] or by direct targeting of SLAIN2, a protein that enhances microtubule dynamics and supports metastatic behaviour [24]. In GBM, miR-106b-5p acts as a key regulator of the immune TME, promoting an immunosuppressive macrophage phenotype that supports tumour progression, via targeting IRF1 and thereby suppressing downstream IFNβ signalling [25]. However, miR-106b-5p displayed oncogenic function in GBM, by directly targeting the tumour-suppressor genes RBL1, RBL2 and CASP8, suppressing their expression and thereby promoting tumour growth and survival [26].

miR-138-5p has consistent tumour-suppressive expression pattern

In contrast to the miR-17/106 family, miR-138-5p displayed a more classic tumour-suppressive expression pattern in both CRC and GBM not only in our study, but also in already published literature. In our cohorts, however, higher expression was associated with worse DFS/OS. Namely, miR-138-5p is frequently down-regulated in CRC tissues compared with adjacent normal tissue, and low miR-138-5p expression correlates with advanced clinical stage, lymph node metastasis and worse overall survival [27]. This downregulation of miR-138-5p levels in both plasma and tissue samples of CRC patients was found to be due to the significant hypermethylation of the promoter region of the miR-138-5p gene [28]. Notably, miR-138-5p directly targets PD-L1, and patients with low miR-138-5p/high PD-L1 had significantly worse survival, establishing that miR-138-5p acts as a tumour-suppressive miRNA in CRC through modulation of the IC gene PD-L1 [27]. Furthermore, the lower plasma miR-138-5p levels were significantly associated with reduced densities of CD8+ tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in CRC tissues, reflecting a potential link between miR-138-5p expression and anti-tumour immune responses [29]. Other miR-138-5p targets include human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT) [30], zinc finger E-box-binding homeobox 2 (ZEB2) [31], nuclear factor IB (NFIB) [32], eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E binding protein 1(4EBP-1) [33], metastasis associated in colon cancer 1 (MACC1) [34], SIN3 transcription regulator family member A (SIN3A) [35], specificity protein 1 (SP1) [36], mitochondrial calcium uniporter (MCU) [37], and semaphoring 4C (SEMA4C) [38].

miR-138-5p was shown to exert its tumour suppressive role in GBM by directly targeting cyclin D3, reducing cell viability, and causing cell cycle arrest [39], as well as targeting the transcription factor NFAT5 [40], cell surface receptor CD44 [41], and cAMP response element‑binding protein 1 (CREB1) [42]. In GBM, miR-138-5p plays a critical tumour-suppressive role and is related to a better response to temozolomide and reduced tumour growth, particularly in the context of chemoresistance [43]. In agreement with lower miR-138-5p expression in GBM tissue compared with normal brain tissue is our previous finding [44], as well as findings by Li et al. [45]. Nevertheless, some clinical datasets, including our own and those of Zottel et al., have reported that higher miR-138-5p in specific GBM subsets (e.g. IDH-mutant) can correlate with worse outcome, suggesting that elevated expression may in some settings reflect a reactive or compensatory response in more aggressive disease [44].

Our data show that miR-106a-5p, miR-20a-5p and miR-20b-5p are more highly expressed in MSS than MSI-H CRC, yet higher expression of these miRNAs is associated with better OS/DFS overall. This apparent paradox is likely resolved by the treatment context: in immunotherapy-naïve, MSS-dominated cohorts, prognosis is driven by tumour-intrinsic and microenvironmental biology under surgery/chemo-based regimens, not by ICI responsiveness. Within MSS disease, higher expression of these miRNAs may mark tumours that are less proliferative, less EMT-like, or have better preserved tumour-suppressive networks, thereby translating into improved survival despite the general ICI resistance of MSS CRC. Whether the same miRNAs predict response to ICIs in MSI-H and MSS patients remains an open question that will require dedicated analysis in ICI-treated cohorts.

miR-106a-5p in GBM and miR-20a-5p in CRC contribute to PD-L1/PD-L2 expression

Correlation analysis across GBM and CRC revealed that most miRNA–checkpoint gene pairs showed weak but significant inverse correlations, with three moderate associations: CD274–miR-106a-5p in GBM, CD274–miR-20a-5p in CRC, and PDCD1LG2–miR-20a-5p in CRC. These findings are consistent with TargetScan-predicted binding sites and support a model in which these miRNAs contribute to, but do not solely determine, PD-L1/PD-L2 expression. The moderate inverse correlations suggest that in a subset of tumours, higher miR-20a-5p and miR-106a-5p levels may constrain PD-L1/PD-L2 expression, potentially shaping the immune phenotype. For example, in CRC, miR-20a-5p could help restrain PD-L1/PD-L2 even within an MSS background, potentially favouring better prognosis under standard therapy but not necessarily predicting benefit from PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. Conversely, in GBM, where the TME is profoundly immunosuppressive, the impact of these miRNAs on checkpoint expression may be dominated by other mechanisms that exclude or inactivate effector T cells.

Candidate miRNAs-enriched pathways intersect with immune modulation

Pan-cancer profiling highlighted that the five selected miRNAs are broadly dysregulated but show striking tissue specificity, with very low or undetectable expression of four of them (miR-106a, miR-106b, miR-20a and miR-20b) in ovarian cancer. This reinforces that even ‘conserved’ regulatory miRNAs are not uniformly used across all tissues. Pathway enrichment analyses showed convergence on TGF-β, FoxO, Hippo, cell cycle and protein-processing pathways – canonical regulators of tumour growth, EMT, stemness and treatment resistance, all of which intersect with immune evasion mechanisms. These data suggest that the miRNAs we identified may influence IC biology both directly, via 3′-UTR targeting of CD274/PDCD1LG2, and indirectly, by modulating signalling axes that shape the immune and TME.

Limitations and future perspective

The main limitations of this study are its reliance on bulk transcriptomic data without functional validation, the heterogeneity of clinical cohorts (including variable treatments and MSI status) and the inability to distinguish direct from indirect regulatory effects. Functional assays such as the luciferase reporter assay or western blotting of GBM/CRC cell lines can be used to confirm the direct miRNA-mediated regulation of PD-L1/PD-L2, as correlation alone does not prove causality. In addition, the weak to moderate correlation coefficients indicate that additional regulatory layers are likely. Moreover, immunotherapy-naïve cohorts were used for the survival analysis. This is why the identified miRNAs should be considered as candidate prognostic biomarkers of tumour biology and not predictors of ICI response. At last, while the PD-1 expression mostly originates from immune cells, bulk transcriptomic datasets used in our study do not distinguish between tumour and immune cell-specific expression. Consequently, the observed miRNA–gene associations should be interpreted in the context of the tumour microenvironment as a whole rather than as evidence of tumour-cell specific regulation. For this purpose, single-cell transcriptomics can define the cellular origin of checkpoint-related signals.

Future work should focus on (i) experimental confirmation of direct binding to checkpoint 3′-UTRs; (ii) validation of checkpoint modulation at the protein level; (iii) mechanistic dissection in immune-competent in vitro and in vivo models; (iv) integration of miRNA profiling with clinical outcomes in ICI-treated GBM and MSI-H/MSS CRC cohorts; and (v) use of single-cell or spatial transcriptomics to resolve cell-type-specific regulatory interactions within the TME. Such efforts will be necessary to determine whether these conserved miRNAs can move from prognostic markers of tumour biology to true predictors and modulators of immunotherapy response.

Conclusion

From a translational perspective, our findings support these miRNAs as candidate pan-cancer biomarkers of tumour biology with potential relevance to immunotherapy, rather than as established predictive markers of ICI response. Importantly, the present study should be interpreted as a comparative bioinformatic and hypothesis-generating analysis. While the identified miRNAs showed consistent associations with immune checkpoint genes across datasets and tumour types, the current results do not establish causal regulatory mechanisms. Their prognostic associations in largely ICI-untreated cohorts, together with inverse correlations with PD-L1/PD-L2 and integration into immune-relevant pathways, justify further evaluation in MSI-stratified, ICI-treated GBM and CRC patient sets. Therapeutically, miRNA mimics or inhibitors targeting this small set of conserved miRNAs might be explored as adjuncts to ICIs, for example, to modulate PD-L1 expression, re-programme the TME, or overcome acquired resistance.

Methods

Venn diagram

MiRNAs targeting PD1 (PDCD1), PDL1 (CD274) and PDL2 (PDCDLG2) genes were retrieved from miRTarBase [46], miRWalk [47], TarBase [48] and miRDB [49]. miRTarBase and TarBase include experimentally supported interactions, whereas miRDB, miRWalk and TargetScan provide computational predictions. To increase confidence in candidate selection, only miRNAs identified in at least three independent miRNA–target interaction databases were retained for downstream analyses. Venn diagrams were made with RStudio and the package VennDiagram.

Datasets

CRC

For CRC, three datasets were included in the analysis. TCGA (Colorectal Adenocarcinoma, TCGA, Nature 2012 [50]) and Colon Cancer (CPTAC-2 Prospective, Cell 2019 [51]) were obtained from cBioPortal. The third dataset was MicroRNA profiling by array of CRC tissue obtained from BioStudies [52]. Supporting data are available in the BioStudies database (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/biostudies) under accession number E-MEXP-3515.

GBM

For GBM, three datasets were included in the analysis. TCGA, Nature 2008, was obtained from cBioPortal. CGGA dataset was obtained from Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas and only GBM samples with IDHwt were included in the study [53]. The third dataset was a genome-wide analysis of microRNA expressions in surgical specimens of primary GBM, obtained from BioStudies [52]. Supporting data are available in the BioStudies database (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/biostudies) under accession number E-GEOD-25631.

Differential expression

Differential expression analysis was performed with R (v 4.5.1) and RStudio (v 2025–05-1). To further characterize the identified miRNAs, subgroup analyses were performed using available clinicopathological and molecular annotations. In colorectal cancer, expression was evaluated according to microsatellite status (MSS/MSI-H) and molecular subtype classification when available. In glioma datasets, expression patterns were assessed across tumour grades and between glioblastoma, lower-grade gliomas, and normal brain tissue. For CPTAC-2 dataset the differential analysis was adjusted for mutation phenotype confounder/MSI-H/MSS) and for TCGA dataset (colorectal cancer) the analysis was adjusted for MSI status. If more than 2 groups were compared, one-way ANOVA statistical test was used with Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test (R package ‘stats’). If two groups were compared, t-test was used (R package ‘stats’). Results were visualized with ggplot2.

Survival analysis

Survival analysis was performed with two different methods. First, the median survival was set as a cut-off and then the most optimal cut-off was calculated using surv_cutpoint function (package ‘survminer’). For CPTAC-2 dataset the differential analysis was adjusted for mutation phenotype confounder/MSI-H/MSS) and for TCGA dataset (colorectal cancer) the analysis was adjusted for MSI status. For both, results were visualized with ggsurvplot (package ‘survminer’).

Correlation analysis

The correlation of miRNA-mRNA expression was determined by the Spearman correlation (package ‘stats’).

Correlation with immune cell infiltration was performed using TIMER 2.0 web tool (http://timer.cistrome.org/) using data provided by CIBERSORT.

miRNA expression across cancers

The expression of candidate miRNAs was performed across several cancers using the dataset pan-cancer analysis of whole genomes (ICHC/TCGA, Nature 2020 [54]), obtained from cBioPortal. Graphs were performed in R (v 4.5.1) and RStudio.

Pathway analysis

The pathway for each of the selected miRNAs was determined by mirPath v.3, using TarBase v7.0 and KEGG pathway [55].
