Impact of the CPS-EG score as a new prognostic biomarker in triple-negative breast cancer patients who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy
Authors: İrem Öner, Alper Türkel, Bediz Kurt İnci, Pınar Kubilay Tolunay, Öztürk Ateş, Cengiz Karaçin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to establish risk groups on the basis of CPS-EG scores, independent of pCR status, in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) to identify the prognostic impact of the CPS-EG score.MethodsPatient characteristics included age, menopausal status, clinical stage, pathological stage, estrogen receptor (ER) expression, nuclear grade, Ki-67 proliferation index, residual cancer burden (RCB) score, HER2 status, and other tumor characteristics. The CPS-EG scoring system included clinical stage, pathological stage, ER status, and grade. Patients were divided into two groups on the basis of their CPS-EG scores ≤ 3 and > 3.ResultsA total of 148 patients who were diagnosed with TNBC and treated with NACT were included in the study. A total of 52.0% of the patients had a CPS-EG score of ≤ 3, whereas 48.0% had a score of > 3. The rate of patients who achieved pCR was 29.7% (n = 44). CPS-EG score (HR: 2.331; 95%CI; 1.179–4.608; p = 0.015), pCR (HR: 0.348; 95%CI; 0.144–0.844; p = 0.019), pre-treatment mKi-67 proliferation index (HR: 0.467; 95%CI; 0.251–0.871; p = 0.017), and RCB score (HR: 0.401; 95%CI; 0.174–0.923; p = 0.032) were identified as significant prognostic factors for 5-year DFS. For 5-year OS, significant prognostic factors were CPS-EG score (HR: 2.30; 95%CI; 1.036–4.799; p = 0.040) and pre-treatment mKi-67 proliferation index (HR: 0.484; 95%CI; 0.246–0.954; p = 0.036).ConclusionsThe CPS-EG score, pre-treatment mKi-67 level, and the pCR and RCB score were practical prognostic markers for long-term survival. Conversely, the prognostic significance of pCR status was diminished, particularly in predicting OS. These findings underscore the importance of not only post-treatment pathological staging but also the initial tumor stage and biological characteristics of the tumor in predicting ultimate survival outcomes following neoadjuvant chemotherapy in TNBC patients.

Background

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a biologically and clinically heterogeneous disease that accounts for approximately 15% of all breast cancers. Breast cancer has the highest recurrence and mortality rates among breast cancers, with limited treatment options. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) is the standard of care for high-risk cancers such as TNBC and HER2-positive disease, facilitating tumor removal and disease control. In early-stage breast cancer, NACT is preferred for enabling breast-conserving surgery, reducing axillary lymph node dissection, and providing prognostic information on the basis of pathological response [1, 2]. Prognosis after NACT is related to treatment response and tumor subtype, with pathological complete response (pCR) in TNBC associated with improved disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) [2–4]. Nevertheless, while significant recurrence rates can be observed in some patients who achieve pCR, the recurrence rates in non-pCR patients are highly variable [5, 6].

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of tumor biology beyond pCR in prognostic evaluation. The CPS-EG staging system, developed for predicting prognosis following NACT, incorporates not only pathological staging but also clinical stage, ER status, and grade, providing a comprehensive assessment [7, 8]. It stratifies patients into scores from 0 to 7, predicting five-year distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) [7, 9]. The CPS-EG system provides more precise prognostic information, particularly in hormone receptor (HR)-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative breast cancer patients, and facilitates the decision-making process regarding adjuvant treatment strategies [8–10]. However, its effectiveness in other breast cancer subtypes remains uncertain.

This study aimed to establish risk groups based on CPS-EG scores, independent of pCR status, in TNBC patients undergoing NACT to identify patients with different survival outcomes and assess their prognostic impact.

Methods

Between 2013 and 2023, 148 TNBC patients who underwent NACT were retrospectively evaluated in our center. Female patients aged 18 years and above who received standard neoadjuvant cyclophosphamide and taxane-based chemotherapy for 4‒6 months were included, regardless of their clinical axillary lymph node status. Approximately 20% of these patients received additional carboplatin. Patients who had undergone palliative or only adjuvant chemotherapy, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, or surgery before receiving NACT were excluded from the study. Additionally, patients who did not complete NACT for any reason or who did not undergo curative surgery were also excluded from the study. Patient characteristics such as age, menopausal status, clinical stage, pathological stage, ER expression, nuclear grade, Ki-67 proliferation index, HER2 status, residual cancer burden (RCB) score, and other tumor features were recorded from patient files, archives, and hospital automation systems.

TNBC was defined according to the ASCO/CAP guidelines as having ER and PR expression < 1% and HER2-negativity by immunohistochemistry (IHC) or negativity by in-situ hybridization (ISH) [11]. HER2 status was categorized into HER2-zero (IHC 0) and HER2-low (IHC 1 + or IHC 2 + and negative ISH). Clinical and pathological staging was performed according to the TNM staging version of the AJCC Cancer Staging Manual at the time of diagnosis. The CPS-EG score, which integrates the clinical stage, pathological stage, ER status, and grade, was calculated via the MD Anderson Cancer Center Neoadjuvant Therapy Response Calculator, with scores ranging from 1‒6 [7]. All patients were ER-negative, so there was no group with a CPS-EG score of 0. Studies evaluating the role of the CPS-EG score in guiding adjuvant therapy decisions have employed a cutoff value of 3 [12–14]. In these studies, a score below 3 was considered low risk. However, our study defined a cutoff value of 3, considering scores of 3 and below as low risk. This decision was based on the small number of patients with scores of 1 and 2 (n = 22) and the absence of patients with a score of 0 due to the TNBC characteristics.

Patients were divided into two groups on the basis of their CPS-EG scores ≤ 3 and > 3. pCR was defined as the absence of residual invasive disease in the breast and no measurable disease in any sampled axillary lymph node (ypT0/is ypN0). Residual in-situ carcinoma was also considered pCR. To account for potential changes in tumor biology following NACT, we evaluated pre-treatment core biopsy Ki-67 levels and post-treatment residual disease Ki-67 levels. Our study stratified patients into two groups based on their pre- and post-treatment Ki-67 proliferation index: Ki-67 ≤ 20% and Ki-67 > 20%. However, given the high proliferative rates inherent to TNBC and the heterogeneous intratumoral Ki-67 expression patterns they often exhibit, we also analyzed the median Ki-67 (mKi-67) values to determine the optimal cutoff for prognostication in our cohort, which displayed elevated Ki-67 levels. Patients were grouped on the basis of pre-treatment and post-treatment Ki-67 levels as ≤ 70% and > 70%, respectively. Furthermore, the difference between the pre-treatment Ki-67 proliferation index and the Ki-67 proliferation index in the residual tumor post-treatment was analyzed. Patients with unavailable pre- or post-treatment Ki-67 assessment and those who achieved pCR were excluded from this analysis. Based on the difference in Ki-67 levels, patients were categorized into groups demonstrating an increase, decrease, or stable.

Our study calculated the RCB score using histopathological parameters defined by Symmans et al. in 2007 [15]. The RCB index allows for the stratification of patients into four categories based on their residual tumor burden: RCB-0 (pCR), RCB-I (minimal residual disease), RCB-II (moderate residual disease), and RCB-III (extensive residual disease).

Descriptive statistical analyses were conducted, with categorical data reported as frequencies (percentages). The independent samples t-test was used to compare continuous variables between two independent groups, whereas the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical data. DFS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to recurrence, metastasis, or death. OS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to death or the last follow-up date. These durations were calculated via the Kaplan‒Meier method, with five-year DFS and OS rates computed for patients for whom the median DFS and OS could not be reached. The median follow-up duration was estimated via the reverse Kaplan‒Meier method. The log-rank test was used to compare DFS between groups. Statistically significant factors identified by Kaplan‒Meier analysis (p < 0.05) were further evaluated to determine independent prognostic factors via Cox regression modeling. All tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed via IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0, developed by IBM Corp.

Results

A total of 148 patients who were diagnosed with TNBC and treated with NACT were included in the study. A total of 77 (52.0%) of the patients had a CPS-EG score of ≤ 3, whereas 71 (48.0%) had a score of > 3. The median age of the patients was 50 years (range: 27‒85). The percentage of patients who achieved pCR was 44 (29.7%), and the percentage of patients who did not achieve pCR was 104 (70.3%). There was a significant difference in the pCR rate and non-pCR rate between patients with CPS-EG scores ≤ 3 and those with CPS-EG scores > 3 (p < 0.001). In our study, nine patients (12.7%) who achieved pCR had a CPS-EG score > 3, whereas 42 patients (54.5%) who did not achieve pCR had a CPS-EG score of 3 or less. Discussion

This study investigated the prognostic impact of the CPS-EG score in patients with TNBC receiving NACT. Our findings indicate that the CPS-EG score, along with pCR, RCB score, and pre-treatment mKi-67 proliferation index, is a strong predictor of DFS. Similarly, the CPS-EG score and pre-treatment ve mKi-67 proliferation index were found to be effective in predicting OS. However, we observed that the prognostic value of achieving pCR, particularly in predicting OS, was diminished when considering the CPS-EG score.

The CPS-EG score is a staging system designed to increase the accuracy of long-term survival predictions beyond AJCC tumor staging. It incorporates tumor characteristics such as HR status and grade [7, 8, 10]. This system has been applied in various prospective studies to categorize patients into risk groups. For example, the OlympiA study investigated the effectiveness of the adjuvant olaparib in patients with HER2-negative early-stage breast cancer and BRCA mutations, defining high-risk individuals as those with a CPS-EG score above three [12]. Similarly, the Penelope-B study assessed palbociclib efficacy in HR-positive, HER2-negative patients with residual disease after NACT, identifying high-risk patients as those with a CPS-EG score ≥ 3 or a CPS-EG score of 2 with ypN1 [13]. The phase 3 randomized SASCIA study is ongoing, evaluating the adjuvant sacituzumab govitecan. Patients with the luminal subtype as well as those with TNBC were included. High-risk participants in this trial were defined as those with a CPS-EG score ≥ 3 or a CPS-EG score of 2 with ypN + status [14].

In a study of 432 breast cancer patients post-NACT, regardless of HR or HER2 status, the CPS-EG score was used to categorize patients into three risk groups: 0‒1 (low risk), 2‒3 (intermediate risk), and 4‒6 (high risk). The 5-year locoregional recurrence-free survival (LRFS) and DMFS rates were 99% and 93%, 93% and 82%, and 86% and 52%, respectively, in the low-risk group, in the intermediate-risk group, and high-risk group (p = 0.03 and p < 0.0001) [16]. In a study evaluating neoadjuvant epirubicin, gemcitabine, and docetaxel in patients with breast cancer irrespective of subtype, the CPS-EG score emerged as the most significant prognostic factor for DFS in both univariate (p = 0.0006) and multivariate analyses (HR for CPS-EG scores ranging from 0 to ≥ 4 were 0.18, 0.11, 0.29, 0.45, and reference, respectively) (p < 0.002) [17]. Additionally, in a study focusing on luminal-type breast cancer after NACT, patients with CPS-EG scores ≥ 3 (high risk) experienced significantly worse outcomes in terms of DFS (HR: 2.95; 95% CI: 2.02‒4.33; p < 0.001) and OS (HR: 3.70; 95% CI: 2.29‒5.99; p < 0.001) than those with CPS-EG scores < 3 (low risk) [18].

In a study of 1795 TNBC patients from eight prospective studies, the 5-year DFS rate was 68.2%, and the OS rate was 77.2%. Patients with CPS-EG scores of 1, 2, and 3 had 5-year DFS rates of 83.1%, 90.3%, and 64%, respectively (p < 0.001). Low-risk patients (CPS-EG scores 1‒2) had a 5-year DFS of 83.4%, whereas high-risk patients (scores 3‒6) had 51.1% (p < 0.001) [19]. In our study, the 5-year DFS rate was 58.0%, and the 5-year OS rate was 60.6%. We established a cutoff point for the CPS-EG score of 3. Accordingly, our results revealed that the 5-year DFS rate was 74.8% for patients with a CPS-EG score ≤ 3, whereas it was 40.6% for those with a score > 3 (p < 0.001). Similarly, the 5-year OS rate was 76.7% for patients with a CPS-EG score ≤ 3, whereas it was 44.1% for those with a score > 3 (p < 0.001). Another study revealed that the CPS-EG score significantly predicts DMFS and OS in TNBC and HER2-positive patients (p < 0.001) but not in luminal B-like patients (p = 0.643) [20]. In a TNBC study using the MD Anderson CPS, low-risk patients had 3-year DFS and LRFS rates of 85% and 93%, respectively, versus 51% and 58%, respectively, in high-risk patients (p = 0.03). OS rates were 93% for low-risk patients and 77% for high-risk patients (p = 0.24). While univariate analysis linked CPS stratification to survival (p = 0.03), it lost significance in multivariate analysis (p = 0.08) [21].

In NACT studies, pCR is a key endpoint, that is strongly correlated with DFS and OS, especially in HR-negative tumors [22, 23]. Although our primary goal was to assess the prognostic value of the CPS-EG score, we also evaluated pCR, as our patients received NACT. In our study, the percentage of patients who achieved pCR was 29.7%. In the literature, the pCR rate in patients with TNBC is reported to be approximately 40‒50% [24]. The lower pCR rate observed in our study may be associated with the smaller number of lymph node-negative patients and the limited use of carboplatin. Patients who achieved a pCR had a 5-year DFS of 81.5% and OS of 83.3%, whereas 48.5% and 51.2% of non-pCR patients did (p = 0.003 and p = 0.008, respectively). Despite the lower pCR rate, the survival benefit for pCR patients in our study aligned with existing data. Univariate analysis indicated that the CPS-EG score and pCR status were prognostic factors for 5-year DFS and 5-year OS. However, multivariate analysis revealed that while pCR maintained its prognostic significance for 5-year DFS, its predictive value for 5-year OS diminished. In contrast, the CPS-EG score remained a statistically significant prognostic factor for both 5-year DFS and 5-year OS. These findings suggest that the CPS-EG score may be as prognostically valuable as pCR and perhaps even superior in predicting OS. Nevertheless, more extensive studies are needed to confirm these findings.

In Marme et al. study, the pCR rate in TNBC patients was 45.8%. The 5-year DFS was 86.4% for those who achieved pCR versus 52.8% for non-pCR patients (p < 0.001) [19]. In this study, the CPS-EG scoring system was suggested to be useful in identifying TNBC patients who do not achieve pCR but have a favorable prognosis or those who have a poor prognosis despite achieving pCR. However, the CPS-EG score did not provide additional prognostic information beyond pathological staging in this patient population. It fails to accurately categorize patients according to their risk of recurrence independently of pCR. In a phase 2 study which sunitinib was added to paclitaxel for NACT in locally advanced breast cancer patients, 27% achieved pCR. The mDFS and OS were greater in patients with CPS-EG scores ≤ 2 than in those with CPS-EG scores ≥ 3 (p = 0.0035 and p = 0.0029) [25]. The MD Anderson CPS score study revealed a 28.2% pCR rate, with 3-year iDFS rates of 74% for pCR patients versus 53% for non-pCR patients (p = 0.18). The CPS provided better prognostic information than did the pCR and suggested identifying TNBC patients for additional adjuvant therapy [21]. In this study, despite not utilizing ER and grade, our findings revealed that the CPS has a more favorable impact on survival than does pCR. In Roussot et al. study, similar to our study, the pCR rate was 23.6% in the ER-negative/HER2-zero group. Among pCR patients, the 5-year DFS was 75% for those with CPS-EG scores of 2‒3 and 50% for those with scores of 4‒5. Although not statistically significant, higher CPS-EG scores were correlated with lower 5-year DFS. Non-pCR patients have poor to inferior prognoses, with 5-year DFS rates ranging from 75 to 40% [22].

According to the CPS-EG system, the 5-year DMFS and DSS rates for early-stage patients with favorable biological markers are 98% and 100%, respectively. These rates are 72% and 63% for more advanced-stage patients with aggressive features, even if pCR is achieved [7]. This finding indicates that long-term survival may be less favorable for some patients who achieve pCR than for those who do not. In a phase I/II study of neoadjuvant epirubicin, gemcitabine, and docetaxel, only 16% of patients had TNBC. Surprisingly, patients who achieved pCR had worse outcomes for DSS (HR: 2.86; 95% CI: 1.2–6.82; p < 0.02) and OS (HR: 3.11; 95% CI: 1.36–7.09; p < 0.007) than those who did not achieve pCR. The 5-year OS was 67% for pCR patients and 89% for non-pCR patients. This difference in OS was significant in the univariate analysis but not in the multivariate analysis. DFS was also shorter in pCR patients (HR: 1.78; 95% CI: 0.89–3.56; p > 0.10). The inverse relationship between pCR and OS was attributed to the inclusion of all breast cancer subtypes, various chemotherapy regimens, small sample sizes, and phase I/II designs. This finding underscores that prognosis post-NACT involves more than just achieving pCR [17].

In a meta-regression analysis of nine randomized prospective studies, pCR explained only 8% of the factors affecting DFS and 9% affecting OS, indicating a weak connection between pCR and survival [26]. The CTNeoBC pooled analysis revealed that while pCR is prognostic for survival at the patient level, its impact varies across treatment groups, making it less reliable for assessing treatment effects on survival [2]. Recent studies have developed biomarkers that incorporate post-treatment pathological staging and tumor biology, which are superior to pCR in prognostic stratification for breast cancer. Thus, while pCR is essential, it is not the sole determinant of treatment efficacy and long-term survival.

Studies have shown a strong association between high Ki-67 expression and adverse prognostic factors in cancer, including increased tumor size, higher histological grade, and the presence of lymph node metastasis [27]. Although Ki-67 staining levels of 10–20% are the most commonly used thresholds for dividing populations, a standardized cutoff value has yet to be established in clinical practice [28]. In our study, a 20% cutoff value for the Ki-67 proliferation index did not provide a prognostic benefit. This was likely attributed to the limited number of patients with Ki-67 values below 20% (n = 5) and the inherently high tumor proliferation rates in our cohort of TNBC patients. In addition to the 20% Ki-67 cutoff point, some studies evaluate higher Ki-67 levels, such as 35%, in node-negative TNBC patients and associate them with different prognostic subgroups [29]. In a different study, it was determined that only 26.7% of TNBC patients exhibited lower Ki-67 expression [30].Therefore, we also evaluated disease progression based on the mKi-67 level of our patient population. The 5-year DFS rates were 43.7% in the group with pre-treatment mKi-67 ≤ 70% and 71.1% in the group with pre-treatment mKi-67 > 70% (p = 0.003). The 5-year OS rates were 48.5% in the group with a pre-treatment mKi-67 score ≤ 70% and 70.7% with a pre-treatment mKi-67 score > 70% (p = 0.009). Studies have shown a positive correlation between Ki-67 expression levels and tumor response to NACT, although this is associated with worse DFS and OS outcomes [31–33]. In a previous study, pre-treatment Ki-67, grade, ER, and HER2 status were significantly associated with survival in univariate analyses. However, in multivariate analyses, the significance of Ki-67 was lost [33]. Additionally, patients with relatively low Ki-67 levels at the time of diagnosis are less likely to benefit from NACT due to the limited potential for tumor suppression. Moreover, given their excellent prognosis, the likelihood of these patients benefiting from additional treatment is also reduced [28]. Our study identified the pre-treatment Ki-67 proliferation index as an independent prognostic factor for DFS and OS in univariate and multivariate analyses.

No differences in 5-year DFS or 5-year OS were observed between the groups with post-treatment mKi-67 scores ≤ 70% and those with mKi-67 scores > 70%. Furthermore, we examined the difference between pre-treatment and post-treatment Ki-67 levels (Ki-67 gap). Five-year DFS rates were 40.3% in patients with an increased Ki-67 gap, 56.1% in those with a decreased gap, and 37.5% in stable levels (p = 0.189). Similarly, 5-year OS rates were 44.2% in patients with an increased Ki-67 gap, 57.7% in those with a decreased gap, and 38.9% in those with stable levels (p = 0.447). Our assessment of Ki-67 levels pre-treatment and post-treatment led us to several conclusions. We demonstrated that the Ki-67 level measured before NACT was a better prognostic indicator than the Ki-67 level measured after treatment and surgery. Additionally, the higher 5-year DFS and 5-year OS rates in patients with pre-treatment mKi-67 levels > 70% suggested that this group benefited more from NACT. Although some retrospective studies contradict our results, showing no independent prognostic value for NACT Ki-67 [34, 35], two large meta-analyses confirmed the consistent prognostic value of Ki-67 despite study heterogeneity and assessment differences [36, 37]. This was further supported by a randomized controlled trial (HR: 1.05‒1.72) [38]. Another study revealed post-treatment Ki-67 as an independent predictor of long-term survival in HR-positive patients, even those who achieved a pCR [39]. In ER-negative disease, a 2.7-fold increase in pre-treatment Ki-67 significantly increased the likelihood of pCR [33]. In a study with 85% triple-positive patients, post-NACT Ki-67 levels in residual disease were found to be prognostic, independent of ER status [40]. A study in China on TNBC patients using CPS-EG and post-NACT Ki-67 with a 14% cutoff revealed no prognostic value for Ki-67. The low proportion of patients (11.2%) with Ki-67 < 14% may have affected reliability [23]. Some studies have used thresholds of 14% and others 20% for classifying luminal A and luminal B tumors. However, these cutoff values are considerably lower in highly proliferative tumors such as TNBC. The cutoff point for Ki-67 to predict prognosis needs to be well-established.

Residual cancer burden (RCB) classification quantifies the extent of residual tumor burden in the breast and axillary lymph nodes after NACT. RCB provides additional prognostic value to the dichotomous pCR and residual disease assessment in predicting long-term survival [6]. International study groups recommend the RCB index to measure residual disease in clinical trials of NACT in breast cancer [41]. Our study investigated the prognostic value of the RCB and CPS-EG scores. Five-year DFS rates were 81.4%, not estimable, 72.0%, and 33.9% for RCB scores of 0, 1, 2, and 3, respectively (p < 0.001). Similarly, 5-year OS rates were 83.3%, NE, 66.7%, and 42.4%, respectively (p = 0.001). In a study evaluating long-term prognostic risk after NACT, 5-year relapse-free survival rates in patients with TNBC were 94%, 89%, 62%, and 26% for RCB scores of 0, 1, 2, and 3, respectively, with similar prognoses for both DMSF and OS. The RCB index was identified as the only prognostic variable in multivariate models, including age, pre-treatment clinical stage, grade, multifocality, and pCR [42]. In another study with 44.5% of patients with TNBC, the RCB index’s prognostic impact was significant for TNBC and HER2-positive cancers but not for luminal cancers (p = 0.07) [43].

Currently, HER2-negative disease is subdivided into the HER2-zero and low subtypes. In our study, when we divided patients into HER2-zero and low subtypes, we found no difference in terms of 5-year DFS and OS rates between the two groups (60.1% vs. 52.6%, p = 0.213; 61.3% vs. 58.0%, p = 0.430, respectively). However, in the HER2-negative group, the 5-year DFS rates were 75.1% for patients with a CPS-EG score ≤ 3 and 43.0% for those with a score > 3 (p < 0.001). In the HER2-low group, the 5-year DFS rates were 74.2% for patients with a CPS-EG score ≤ 3 and 34.9% for those with a score > 3 (p < 0.021). Therefore, being HER2-low or zero did not change survival outcomes, and the CPS-EG score did not alter its independent prognostic value. Similarly, a multicenter study of 2489 cases reported recurrence rates of 46.3% in HER2-zero patients with a CPS-EG score ≥ 3 and 44.0% in HER2-low patients (p = 0.744) [44]. In another study evaluating the prognostic significance of the CPS-EG score in HER2-zero and HER2-low breast cancer [22], 40% of patients had TNBC, and 60% were HR-positive. In the ER-negative/HER2-low patient group, the DFS rates for patients with CPS-EG scores of 1‒5 were 100%, 68.9%, 72.4%, 46.2%, and 40%, respectively (p = 0.055), whereas the OS rates were 100%, 87.5%, 84.2%, 53.8%, and 60%, respectively (p = 0.028). In the ER-negative/HER2-zero patient group, the DFS rates for patients with CPS-EG scores of 1‒5 were 76.8%, 87.3%, 75%, 57.8%, and 40%, respectively (p < 0.0001), whereas the OS rates were 85.6%, 92.7%, 80.5%, 65.4%, and 50%, respectively (p = 0.001). Ultimately, being HER2-low did not affect the prognostic power of the CPS-EG score. They reported results similar to those of our study, demonstrating that the CPS-EG score maintains its long-term prognostic classification ability independent of HER2-low or HER2-zero status across the general population, luminal subtypes, and TNBC. In a different study evaluating NACT in HER2-low breast cancer, 35% of patients had TNBC. Following neoadjuvant anthracycline and taxane-based chemotherapy, pCR was observed in 38.5% of TNBC patients. This study evaluated the CPS-EG score in HR-positive/HER2-low breast cancer patients for whom pCR was not achieved. Patients with a CPS-EG score ≥ 3 had a high recurrence risk of 57.1%. Similar to our study, there was no significant difference in DFS between patients initially categorized as HER2-zero and those categorized as HER2-low [45].

This study has several limitations. These included being single-center studies, having a limited number of patients, having unknown BRCA status, having a retrospective design, and having a relatively short follow-up period. However, the 40-month median follow-up period was considered acceptable since recurrences in TNBC typically occur within the first 2‒3 years. While most patients received neoadjuvant standard cyclophosphamide plus taxane therapy, some received additional platinum, and whether non-pCR patients received adjuvant capecitabine was not evaluated, which were among the other limitations of this study.

Ongoing studies aiming to improve prognosis after NACT in TNBC patients are evaluating factors such as BRCA status, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, and the Ki-67 index, in addition to the CPS-EG [46, 47]. Similarly, in an ongoing phase 1 study comparing neoadjuvant endocrine therapy and NACT in luminal breast cancer patients [48], the PEPI score (which is calculated on the basis of tumor size, lymph node involvement, Ki-67 level, and post-treatment ER positivity) and CPS-EG score are being utilized to standardize the assessment of neoadjuvant treatment response and evaluate prognosis.

As emphasized in our study, particularly in tumors with aggressive biology such as TNBC, incorporating additional factors such as the Ki-67 index, BRCA status, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, molecular prognostic signatures, or ctDNA can increase the prognostic power of markers such as pCR and CPS-EG. This approach enables more effective evaluation of patient prognosis and therapeutic decision-making processes.

Conclusions

In our study of TNBC patients, we demonstrated that the CPS-EG score, pCR status, RCB score and pre-treatment mKi-67 level are prognostic parameters for DFS. Additionally, the CPS-EG score, pCR status, RCB score, pre-treatment mKi-67 level, and clinical stage collectively serve as prognostic parameters for OS. Multivariate analyses revealed that CPS-EG score and pre-treatment mKi-67 level, in addition to pCR and RCB score, were practical prognostic markers for long-term survival; in contrast, the prognostic significance of pCR status was diminished, particularly in predicting OS. These findings underscore the importance of not only post-treatment pathological staging but also the initial tumor stage and biological characteristics of the tumor in predicting ultimate survival outcomes following NACT in TNBC patients.
