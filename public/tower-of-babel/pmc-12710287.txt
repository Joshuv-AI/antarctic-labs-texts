Successful Endovascular Embolization for Hemorrhage from Superficial Inferior Epigastric Artery after Inguinal Hernia Repair: A Case Report
Authors: Sang Ah Cho, Chang Hoon Oh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBleeding from the superficial inferior epigastric artery (SIEA) following inguinal hernia repair is extremely rare. However, if such bleeding leads to hemodynamic instability, surgical or transarterial embolization (TAE) intervention is required. We report the case of a 71-year-old man who, after undergoing laparoscopic repair for a recurrent direct inguinal hernia, was referred to the interventional radiology department following active bleeding in the right inguinal area. Selective external iliac artery and SIEA angiography revealed contrast medium extravasation from the right SIEA branch. The bleeding branch was embolized using a mixture of N-butyl cyanoacrylate and iodized oil. Postembolization angiography demonstrated successful hemostasis without complications.Keywords: Case Report, Embolization, Inguinal Hernia, N-Butyl Cyanoacrylate, Superior Inferior Epigastric Artery

INTRODUCTION

Laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair has various advantages, including reduced postoperative discomfort and pain, shorter recovery time, easier repair of recurrent hernias, concurrent treatment of bilateral hernias, and improved cosmesis (1). However, this approach has certain complications, as do other surgical methods. According to a systematic review, the most commonly reported complication after inguinal hernia repair was bleeding (0.9%) (2). Bleeding from the superficial inferior epigastric artery (SIEA) following inguinal hernia repair is an extremely rare complication. Only one case report has described SIEA hemorrhage following inguinal hernia repair, which was successfully treated with surgical hemostasis (3).

We present a rare case of hemorrhage from an SIEA after inguinal hernia repair, which was successfully treated with embolization using N-butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA) in a coaxial technique employing a 5-F curved catheter and a small-caliber microcatheter.

CASE REPORT

The patient was a 71-year-old man who had a recurrent direct inguinal hernia, despite inguinal hernia repair at another institution 21 months prior to presentation to our facility. He had underlying diseases, including coronary artery occlusion and cerebral infarction, and was taking oral antiplatelet and anticoagulant drugs. Laboratory findings 1 month prior to surgery showed a prothrombin time (PT) of 17.4 seconds, international normalized ratio (INR) of 1.48, and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) of 40.0 seconds, which indicated prolonged clotting times. He underwent laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair again at our institution; antiplatelet and anticoagulants were discontinued 5 and 2 days prior to surgery.

On the 3rd postoperative day, while his overall vital signs were stable, hemoglobin levels decreased (12.8 g/dL) and mild swelling was noted at the operative site in the right inguinal area upon physical examination. The patient also reported a sensation of scrotal swelling. Abdominopelvic CT was performed and revealed extravasation of contrast medium, indicating active bleeding, with an acute hematoma around the right inguinal area (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, the patient was referred to the interventional radiology unit for transarterial embolization (TAE).

The initial right external iliac arteriogram showed contrast medium extravasation; however, the exact vessel involved could not be identified (Fig. 1B). Therefore, the right deep inferior epigastric artery (DIEA) was selected, and angiography was performed using a microcatheter. Contrast medium extravasation was not observed on the right DIEA angiogram. However, prophylactic embolization was performed using gelatin sponge particles (Nexsphere; Next Biomedical, Incheon, Korea) and microcoils (Interlock: Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA, and Concerto: Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA). To identify the injury site, additional selective angiography was performed at the right superficial and deep circumflex iliac arteries (CIA), but no active bleeding was observed.

A subsequent selective angiogram was performed for the small branch arising from the medial aspect of the common femoral artery, the SIEA, which showed contrast extravasation (Fig. 1C). Selective catheterization of the right SIEA orifice was directly performed using a 5-F C2 catheter (Cook, Bloomington, IN, USA). A 1.7-F microcatheter (ASHAI INTECC, Aichi, Japan) was introduced into the proximal portion of the bleeding branch using the coaxial technique (Fig. 1D). Thereafter, the bleeding branches were embolized with a 1:2 mixture of NBCA (Histoacryl; B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany) and iodized oil (Lipiodol ultra fluide, Guerbet, France). Postembolization angiography demonstrated successful hemostasis, with no complications (Fig. 1E). The patient recovered well and was discharged after 3 days.

After 3 weeks, follow-up CT showed decreased size and liquefaction of the hematoma, and the absence of active bleeding (Fig. 1F). No complications were noted at the 3-month follow-up assessment.

This single-institution retrospective report was approved by the relevant Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from the patient (IRB No. EUMC 2024-02-013).

DISCUSSION

In most individuals, the SIEA originates from the common femoral artery, about 1 cm below the inguinal ligament, and ascends in front of the rectus sheath. In 1975, Taylor and Daniel investigated the anatomy of the SIEA by dissecting 100 cadavers. They observed that the SIEA and superficial circumflex iliac artery shared a common origin in 48% and had separate origins in 17% of cases, while the SIEA was absent in 35% of cases (4). Fukaya et al. (5) later reported that 64.7% of 34 expected SIEAs were successfully visualized. The inability to visualize the remaining 35% may have been due to either the actual absence of the SIEA or current limitations in detecting vessels of this size. Among the visualized SIEAs, 36.4% exhibited a common trunk formation with and 63.6% originated independently from the femoral artery. Overall, these findings highlight the significant variability in both the anatomy and the dominance of the SIEA in the abdominal wall vasculature across individuals.

During inguinal hernia repair, an incision is made above the inguinal crease. The SIEA, typically located in the right lower abdominal wall or inguinal area, is thus vulnerable to damage, and hemostasis via manual compression is challenging due to the lack of supporting structures, such as the femoral head. In cases of bleeding from the SIEA, surgical hemostasis or TAE can be performed. Takahashi et al. (3) reported a similar case where surgical hemostasis was performed for SIEA bleeding at 10 hours after inguinal hernia repair. Endovascular therapies, including embolization of injured arteries for hemorrhage control, have emerged as fast and efficient treatment options with reduced morbidity and mortality rates (6).

Although not directly related to the inguinal hernia, rare instances of successful treatment of active SIEA bleeding following trauma by using NBCA treatment via TAE have been reported (7). Similar to our case, the DIEA and CIA were first examined due to the low incidence of SIEA bleeding. However, postembolization angiography showed continued bleeding; thus, the SIEA was targeted, and embolization was successful. Before endovascular treatment, determining the source of bleeding and the presence of a hematoma using preprocedural CT or other imaging modalities is crucial to identify the bleeding vessel. Given the rarity of SIEA bleeding, other vessels, such as the DIEA or CIA are typically examined first, but the SIEA should be considered if active bleeding is present in the lower abdominal wall or inguinal area, as embolization can be effective. Additionally, effort should be made to avoid missing the bleeding focus, such as by obtaining images from various angles using detector rotation during angiography and properly adjusting the field of view to ensure that the suspected bleeding area does not fall outside the imaging range.

Our patient was on antiplatelet and anticoagulant medications due to multiple underlying conditions, which resulted in baseline prolongation of PT, aPTT, and INR. A previous study reported a 23.3% incidence of all bleeding complications, with a 10% incidence of hematoma development, in patients receiving long-term warfarin who underwent inguinal hernia repair using low-molecular-weight heparin bridging (8). Another study reported that, among 47 patients, 6 (13%) experienced surgical-site hematoma, and one patient had major bleeding that required surgical intervention (9). Although data are not definitive to date, patients receiving anticoagulation therapy should be actively monitored for hematoma development after inguinal repair, and their presence, characteristics, and size changes should be evaluated.

In this case, bleeding from the SIEA following recurrent inguinal hernia repair was successfully treated using TAE. This case illustrates that hemostasis can be achieved safely and effectively by selecting the branch leading to the bleeding focus by using a small-caliber microcatheter and performing embolization using NBCA.
